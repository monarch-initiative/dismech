    Chronic social defeat stress as a model of major depressive disorder

    Written 2026-10-19 for issue #14274, area 3 of #13754. It answers the four questions that issue asks about chronic social defeat stress (CSDS) and recommends how a CSDS model record should be linked into kb/disorders/Major_Depressive_Disorder.yaml.

    No model record is added here. AnimalModel cannot yet record a procedure applied to a wild-type animal; that waits on #13768.

    Sources. Two passes were combined: a reading of the leads named in the issue and the standard CSDS papers, and an OpenScientist deep-research report (research/model_validity/Major_Depressive_Disorder-model-validity-chronic_social_defeat_stress-deep-research-openscientist.md). Every quotation below is an exact substring of the cached file references_cache/PMID_<id>.md for the PMID that follows it. Most caches hold only the abstract; the table at the end says which.

    Summary

    • CSDS models stress-induced social avoidance and anhedonia-like behaviour, not depressed mood. Its strongest validity argument is pharmacological: social avoidance reverses with chronic but not acute antidepressants, and within a day with ketamine or psilocybin. Its construct validity is disputed: both susceptible and resilient mice become anxious, several sources say the paradigm models PTSD as much as depression, and one review of 318 reports says the protocol has limited ecological validity.
    • The susceptible/resilient split is presented as a model of human vulnerability, but no source tests it as one in people. What has been tested is narrower: six molecules or programs that mark susceptible mice are also altered in patients with MDD compared with controls.
    • CSDS findings bear on ten of the twelve mechanism nodes, but often in a different direction or form from the node. The monoamine findings do not fit the Monoamine Deficiency node (they are mixed in direction, and VTA dopamine firing increases), the enkephalin finding runs opposite to the PENK+ interneuron node, and the neurogenesis suppression is transient rather than stalled. There is no CSDS evidence for Reference Point Dysregulation or Neurogenic Niche Interferon Signaling Activation.
    • The blood-brain barrier finding is the strongest case for a new node: it is specific to susceptible mice and has the same change in patient postmortem tissue. Circuit findings, including the lateral habenula, are better recorded as a knowledge gap for now.
    • Standard CSDS cannot be run in females, and 95% of 318 reviewed defeat reports studied males. Four female adaptations exist and disagree about whether the female phenotype matches the male one.

    1. What CSDS models, and what it does not

    Protocol. An experimental C57BL/6J mouse is defeated daily by a larger aggressor: "C57BL/6J mice that are repeatedly subjected to bouts of social defeat by a larger and aggressive CD-1 mouse results in the development of a clear depressive-like syndrome, characterized by enduring deficits in social interactions" (PMID:21799487). "This sequence is repeated for 10 days, with a novel opponent each day" (PMID:31584111). The primary readout is a ratio of time spent near a caged novel mouse: "The social interaction ratio (SI ratio) is obtained by dividing the time spent in the interaction zone when the target is present by the time spent in the interaction zone when the target is absent" (PMID:21799487).

    Face validity. Reviews list several readouts that resemble human symptoms: "Defeat produces specific behaviors resembling the signs and symptoms of humans with affective disorders, such as anhedonia, social avoidance, despair and anxiety" (PMID:26475995). One review calls it "an ethologically valid rodent model that exhibits long-term physiological and behavioral phenotypes similar to depression and anxiety" (PMID:33090813).

    Different readouts depend on different mechanisms, so "depression-like" is not one phenotype. Prefrontal NPAS4 "is required in this brain region for CSDS-induced changes in sucrose preference and natural reward motivation in the mice", but "NPAS4 is not required for CSDS-induced social avoidance or anxiety-like behavior" (PMID:36780219).

    Construct validity. The argument is that social stress is a cause of human depression, and that the mouse response depends on reward-circuit plasticity: "BDNF is required for the development of this experience-dependent social aversion" (PMID:16469931). Hammels and colleagues claim "the social defeat paradigm has been shown to have etiological, predictive and face validity" (PMID:26475995). Lyons and colleagues, reviewing 318 reports, dispute the ecological part of that claim: "Elements of limited ecological validity include frequent and repeated exposure to defeat stressors without opportunities to avoid or escape from unfamiliar conspecifics that are pre-screened and selected for aggressive behavior" (PMID:36608919).

    Predictive validity is the model's strongest claim:

    • "Mice experiencing repeated aggression develop a long-lasting aversion to social contact, which can be normalized by chronic, but not acute, administration of antidepressant" (PMID:16469931). The protocol paper calls this "a pharmacological validity not often seen in other stress models that tend to respond equally to both acute and chronic antidepressant treatments" (PMID:21799487).
    • The model separates slow from rapid-acting drugs: "Fluoxetine showed no significant effects 24 h after a single dose or following 7 days of repeated administration; antidepressant-like effects only appeared after 14 days of continuous treatment. In contrast, a single dose of either ketamine or psilocybin significantly reversed social avoidance behavior at 24 h" (PMID:41290975).
    • Ketamine's effect is partial: "High, but not low, doses of ketamine administered after completion of the CSDS regimen attenuated social avoidance in defeated mice, although this effect was transient" (PMID:24495460), and "acute administration of ketamine fails to attenuate CSDS-induced anhedonia despite reducing other depression-related behavioral abnormalities" (PMID:24495460).
    • Only some susceptible mice respond, as in patients: "Each treatment reversed social interaction deficits in approximately 50% of susceptible mice—similar to previous studies" (PMID:27569543).

    What is disputed, and by whom.

    • Anxiety rather than depression. Donahue and colleagues report the competing reading: "it has been proposed that CSDS-triggered behaviors have their basis in anxiety (17,18), and what is often interpreted as anhedonia in sucrose preference tests may actually reflect anxiety-enhanced neophobia" (PMID:24495460). The protocol paper itself reports that anxiety does not separate the groups: "both susceptible and resilient mice show increased anxiety-like behavior, as measured by the elevated plus maze, stress-induced polydipsia and increased corticosterone release in response to a swim stress" (PMID:21799487).
    • PTSD and other disorders. Hammels and colleagues say the defeat model "may mimic cardinal features of a multitude of psychiatric disorders including depression, post-traumatic stress disorder and schizophrenia" (PMID:26475995). Browne and colleagues point out that the drug response used as predictive validity for depression fits PTSD just as well: avoidance "can be reversed by 28 days of treatment with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, the only pharmaceutical class approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for treating post-traumatic stress disorder" (PMID:29020387).
    • Readouts. Donahue and colleagues note that "In many behavioral assays, it can be difficult to determine if these adaptations reflect core signs of depression" (PMID:24495460). CSDS studies often add the forced swim test, which Molendijk and de Kloet reject as a depression readout: "The forced swim test (FST) for rodents does not model despair or helplessness" (PMID:30738104). Commons and colleagues agree independently: "The FST provides little construct- or face-validity to support an interpretation as "depression-like" behavior" (PMID:28287253).
    • Symptoms no rodent model can show. Planchez and colleagues, writing about rodent models in general: "some symptoms observed in humans, such as feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt or recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal ideation or a suicide attempt cannot be observed in animals such as rodents" (PMID:31584111).

    No source found critiques the social interaction ratio itself as a readout. The disputes are about what the behaviour means, not whether it is measured reliably.

    A misattribution risk. Planchez and colleagues write that a model "mimics a trauma, and therefore, it should instead be considered as a model of post-traumatic stress disorder" (PMID:31584111). That sentence is about the predator-exposure model, not CSDS, and should not be cited for CSDS.

    2. Susceptible versus resilient

    Origin and framing. Krishnan and colleagues introduced the split: "an inbred population of mice subjected to social defeat can be separated into susceptible and unsusceptible subpopulations" (PMID:17956738). They frame it through human resilience: "most individuals exposed to adversity maintain normal psychological functioning" (PMID:17956738). Wang and colleagues say CSDS "can imitate individual differences in stress responses in humans" (PMID:33090813).

    So the split is presented as a model of human vulnerability. None of the sources tests it as one. Every human comparison found compares patients who already have MDD with controls. None follows people before illness to ask whether a susceptibility marker predicts who becomes ill.

    Tested in human tissue or blood (patient versus control):

    The transcriptional overlap holds at the network level but is weak gene by gene: Scarpa and colleagues report overlap of "5.2% in CSDS susceptible mice (18/344; 16 common, 2 opposite)" in one comparison (PMID:32169281).     Proposed for humans, tested only in mice:

    • Hodes and colleagues found "individual differences in IL-6 levels from ex vivo stimulated leukocytes that predict susceptibility versus resilience to a subsequent stressor" (PMID:25331895). The prediction before stress was shown in mice only.
    • Hultman and colleagues found an electrical network that "is also enhanced in three independent models of MDD vulnerability" and "is biologically distinct from the networks that encode dysfunction after stress" (PMID:29502969). The cohort is male mice, and no human data were collected.

    Recommendation. Treat the claim that the split models human vulnerability as a proposal. Record it as a HUMAN_MODEL_MISMATCH discussion rather than as fidelity on a model link: the mouse evidence exists, and its translation to people is what is open. A prospective human study that measures one of the markers above before illness would settle it.

    Findings with no existing node:

    • Blood-brain barrier / neurovascular integrity (PMID:29184215). This is the strongest case for a new node: a mouse mechanism, a susceptible-only phenotype, and the same change in patient postmortem tissue. A cross-paradigm meta-analysis of six CSDS and chronic unpredictable stress datasets also finds "downregulation of glia/myelin and vascular pathways" in the prefrontal cortex (PMID:41566898). The MDD entry already names a neurovascular, blood-brain-barrier route in one discussion and a claudin-5 readout in a proposed experiment, so a node would give those an anchor.
    • Circuit-level findings. Three are reported:
    • In Hultman's network, "Activity patterns in this network originate in prefrontal cortex and ventral striatum, relay through amygdala and ventral tegmental area, and converge in ventral hippocampus" (PMID:29502969).
    • Chaudhury and colleagues found that "phasic activation of VTA neurons projecting to the nucleus accumbens (NAc), but not to the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), induced susceptibility" (PMID:23235832).
    • Li and Ning found "elevated spontaneous neuronal activity in the LHb, robust hyperconnectivity between the LHb and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex" in susceptible mice (PMID:42874279).

    The OpenScientist report proposes a lateral habenula node, citing serum ULK1 as human support. This report does not recommend one yet. The habenula fMRI study is a single 2026 study in male mice, and serum ULK1 is a blood marker rather than a measure of habenula activity. Hultman's network is a vulnerability trait that its authors say is distinct from the disease state. A KNOWLEDGE_GAP discussion naming the circuit question fits better. - Glycemic shift (PMID:42633587): "this stress-induced hyperglycemic response was abolished in C57BL/6N and C57BL/6J mice, whereas BALB/c mice showed stress-induced hypoglycemia". This is a single study comparing strains rather than susceptible and resilient mice. It does not warrant a node.

    4. Sex

    Why standard CSDS fails in females. "An important limitation of RSDS studies to date is that they have been conducted exclusively in male mice due to the difficulty of initiating attack behavior directed toward female mice" (PMID:28993631). The protocol paper does not mention females (PMID:21799487). The bias persists: "Most of the 318 reviewed reports (95%) focus on males" (PMID:36608919).

    Adaptations, and how their results compare with males:

    Method Result compared with males Source
    Male odorants applied to females so male residents attack them "This method produces comparable results to those obtained in male mice" PMID:29090682
    Chemogenetic activation of the aggressor's ventromedial hypothalamus so it attacks females "These patterns of behavioral abnormalities closely resemble susceptibility and resilience in male mice after RSDS" PMID:28993631
    Aggressive female residents defeat female intruders "significant features of the defeated female phenotype are distinct from males"; defeated females showed vigilance "though the duration of time spent in a predefined social interaction zone was comparable between groups" PMID:31255250
    Females witness a male being defeated (vicarious stress) Females develop reduced sucrose preference, which "appears to be sex dependent, since previous work suggests that ES-male c57BL/6 mice do not develop a decrease in preference for sucrose 24 h after VDS" PMID:28888327
    Aggressive parous females defeat females (femCSDS) Proteomic study; no male comparison PMID:42614171

    The adaptations disagree. The two that make males attack females report a male-like phenotype. The female-aggressor model finds that time in the interaction zone does not change, so a female cohort scored by the male readout would look resilient. The vicarious model applies emotional rather than physical stress, a different construct. Where the molecular response has been compared, it differs: "there are sex-specific differences in peripheral immune responses and differential gene regulation in the prefrontal cortex to chronic stress and to the treatment" (PMID:32918293).

    The femCSDS authors criticise the first two methods, which "relied on artificial triggers like applying male urine to females or using optogenetic stimulation which risk introducing confounding hormonal and sensory variables" (PMID:42614171). (Takahashi and colleagues used chemogenetic, not optogenetic, activation.) A systematic review argues against defeat for females altogether: "social defeat stress model does not fit the social nature of females" (PMID:30196085).

    Does it limit the model? Yes, and several sources say so. "The risk of developing stress related disorders such as depression is two times higher in women than in men" (PMID:30196085). One review states that "a historical weakness of rodent social stress models has been an under-representation of studies in females" (PMID:34532840), and another that CSDS "is limited to the study of male mice" (PMID:26881133). The human comparison in question 2 relies on male CSDS cohorts: "Only our human and CVS cohorts included females. This may, unfortunately, limit our interpretation of the data" (PMID:32169281). The CSDS cohorts behind the blood-brain barrier, dopamine, network and habenula findings above are male (PMID:29184215, PMID:23235832, PMID:29502969, PMID:42874279).
