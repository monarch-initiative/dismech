# SSTR2 Expression in Olfactory Neuroblastoma: Clinical and Therapeutic Implications.
**Authors:** Cracolici V, Wang EW, Gardner PA, Snyderman C, Gargano SM, Chiosea S, Singhi AD, Seethala RR
**Journal:** Head Neck Pathol (2021)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s12105-021-01329-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12105-021-01329-1)

## Content

Somatostatin receptor 2 (SSTR2) expression has previously been documented in olfactory neuroblastoma (ONB). Here, we fully characterize SSTR2 expression in ONB and correlate staining results with clinicopathologic parameters including Hyams grade. We also assess SSTR2 immunohistochemistry expression in various histologic mimics of ONB to assess its diagnostic functionality. 78 ONBs (51 primary biopsies/excisions and 27 recurrences/metastases) from 58 patients were stained for SSTR2. H-scores based on intensity (0-3 +) and percentage of tumor cells staining were assigned to all cases. 51 histologic mimics were stained and scored in an identical fashion. 77/78 (99%) ONB cases demonstrated SSTR2 staining (mean H-score: 189, range: 0-290). There were no significant differences in staining between primary tumors and recurrences/metastases (mean H-score: 185 vs 198). Primary low-grade ONB had somewhat stronger staining than high-grade tumors (mean H-score: 200 vs 174). SSTR2 expression had no prognostic value when considering disease-free or disease-specific survival. SSTR2 staining is significantly higher in ONB than its histologic mimics (mean H-score: 189 vs 12.9, p < 0.001) suggesting a potential use of the marker in diagnosis of ONB. In conclusion, SSTR2 is consistently expressed in ONB suggesting a role for somatostatin-analog based imaging and therapy in this disease. More generally, SSTR2 may be another marker of neuroendocrine differentiation in ONB.

Keywords: Olfactory neuroblastoma, Esthesioneuroblastoma, Somatostatin receptor, SSTR2

Somatostatin receptor 2 (SSTR2) is a G-protein coupled membrane receptor expressed in a variety of neural and neurosecretory tissue types. While there are at least 2 isotypes, SSTR2A and SSTR2B which differ by their carboxyl terminus [1], the dominant isoform in humans is SSTR2A. In humans, distribution of SSTR2 is fairly wide but prominent in the central nervous system, meningothelium, and in a variety of neural crest derived, neuroendocrine and endocrine tissues, most notably pancreatic islet cells [2]. As expected, SSTR2 expression has been identified in a variety of endocrine-type tumors [3–9] and is a favorable prognostic marker in some tumors [5]. One promising application of SSTR2 expression is the use of radionuclide labeled synthetic somatostatin-analog aided detection and treatment of tumors [10–15]. Specifically, tumors expressing SSTR2 can be radiographically detected using PET-based methodologies and 68-gallium labeled synthetic somatostatin-analogs. Subsequently, tumors with strong uptake can then be targeted with particle emitting somatostatin-analogs such as 177-lutetium for therapy [14–17].

Olfactory neuroblastoma (ONB) is a rare sinonasal tumor which has been shown to express SSTR2 [18, 19]. Somatostatin-analog based scintigraphy has been effective for radiographically detecting ONB [12, 17, 20–25]. Peptide receptor radionuclide therapy has been at least partially effective for some patients with unresectable and/or metastatic ONB [17, 26–28]. However the relevance of SSTR2 expression in diagnosis and prognosis/theranostics of ONB is not clearly established.

We herein characterize SSTR2 expression in a large cohort of ONB via immunohistochemistry (IHC), in both primary and recurrent/metastatic disease along with several non-ONB mimics to determine impact on clinicopathologic parameters including grade and outcome as well as its discriminatory diagnostic capacity when compared to other round blue cell tumors.

This study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh institutional review board (PRO14080496). Seventy-eight ONB (51 primary lesions, 15 recurrences, and 12 metastases) in 58 patients were retrieved from the pathology archives of the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center (1994–2020). Cases were reviewed by three pathologists (SG, VC, and RRS) to verify diagnosis. Minimal diagnostic criteria for inclusion were: round blue cell morphology, lobular growth pattern with variable fibrillary matrix, prominent vascular-capillary components in the interlobular areas, neuroendocrine immunophenotype (synaptophysin, and/or chromogranin reactivity), at least focal S100 positive sustentacular cell components, and exclusion of other round blue cell tumor considerations. Unless tumor volume was scant (roughly less than 2 mm2), primary ONBs were graded using a modified Hyams grading scheme as previously described (Supplemental Table 1) [29]. For analysis, modified Hyams grade I-II was considered low grade, while grade III-IV was considered high grade.

All available primary, recurrent, and metastatic ONBs were stained for SSTR2 (Abcam [Cambridge, MA]: monoclonal rabbit UMB, 1:100 [Ventana BenchMark Ultra platform]) and reviewed by two pathologists (VC and RRS). Cases were assigned a Histo (H)-score based on the intensity (0–3+) and percentage of SSTR2 staining in the tissue section [30]. The expected pattern of reactivity was membranous with variable cytoplasmic staining. When present, internal tissue controls were used to calibrate the SSTR2 scoring (normal meningothelium: 3+ , normal olfactory neuroepithelium: 2+). Pancreatic islet cells served as an external control (3+). Histologic mimics of ONB were stained and scored in an identical fashion.

Clinical, demographic, and pathologic parameters were correlated with SSTR2 H-scores. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS statistical software (ver.25.0, SPSS, Chicago, IL). For group-wise comparison of mean H-scores, either an independent sample or paired sample T test was used depending on the type of comparison. Overall 5 and 10 years disease free survival (DFS) and disease specific survival (DSS) for ONB patients were derived from Kaplan Meier Estimate. A univariate Cox proportional hazards model was used to assess the significance of SSTR2 on DFS and DSS. In all cases, a p value < 0.05 was considered significant. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with area under the curve (AUC) were generated. The optimal cutoff points to maximize sensitivity and specificity of SSTR2 expression were derived manually using the Youden’s index (J) = Sensitivity + Specificity − 1.

Of the 58 tumors from unique patients, 43 primary tumors could be graded, of which there were 20 low-grade (all Hyams grade II) and 23 high-grade tumors (21 Hyams grade III, and 2 Hyams grade IV). Only one of the 43 cases (2%) had received neoadjuvant therapy.

The vast majority of ONB (77/78, 99%) demonstrated SSTR2 expression (mean H-score 189; range: 0–290) with the expected pattern of membranous and variable cytoplasmic reactivity (Fig. 1). There were no significant differences in SSTR2 expression between primary ONB (H-score: 185, range: 0–290) and recurrences/metastases (H-score: 198, range: 80–290; p = 0.35). Comparison between paired primary and recurrences/metastases were limited (n = 6) but similarly demonstrated no significant differences with mean H-scores 189 (range: 125–230) and 191 (range: 90–290) respectively (p = 0.91). Low-grade tumors had a slightly higher mean H-score (200, range: 0–290) than high-grade tumors (174, range: 35–285; p = 0.41) (Table 1).

Overall, this cohort of ONB showed a 5 and 10 year DSS of 94% and 78% respectively and a 5 and 10 year DFS of 69% and 52% respectively. SSTR2 expression did not correlate with disease-free survival (Cox regression univariate analysis hazard ratio: 1.002, p = 0.75) or disease-specific survival (Cox regression univariate analysis hazard ratio: 1.01, p = 0.36) with mean length of follow-up of 74.3 months (range: 0.8–209).

SSTR2 expression was also assessed in 51 histologic mimics of ONB: sinonasal undifferentiated carcinoma (SNUC) (n = 13), sinonasal neuroendocrine carcinoma (SNEC) (n = 11), non-intestinal-type adenocarcinoma (non-ITAC) (n = 10), intestinal-type adenocarcinoma (ITAC) (n = 8), poorly-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma (n = 2), pituitary adenoma (n = 2), NUT carcinoma (n = 1), plasmacytoma (n = 1), germ cell tumor (n = 1), SMARC-B1 deficient carcinoma (n = 1), and brain undifferentiated carcinoma (n = 1) (Fig. 2). The mean SSTR2 H-score for all histologic mimics was 12.9 (Table 2). Compared to all mimics, the mean H-score of ONB is greater (189 vs 12.9, p < 0.001). SNEC demonstrated the greatest average H-score of all mimics (mean H score = 40.4). However, ONB still demonstrated a greater average H-score (189 vs 40.4, p < 0.001). Using an H-score of 105 or greater yielded a sensitivity of 93.5% and specificity of 98% for ONB versus all mimics (AUC: 0.979). Similarly, when restricting the group to SNUC and SNEC the same cutoff point yielded a sensitivity of 93.5% and specificity of 96.4% (AUC: 0.969) (Fig. 3).

With the advent of a combined multimodal approach for treatment of ONB, outcomes for this tumor had dramatically improved since the original description of the tumor by Berger in 1924 [31]. Current 5-year and 10-year DSS in this group of patients are 94% and 78% respectively. Despite improvements, additional therapeutic options are limited. The discovery that ONB is yet another neuroendocrine tumor that expresses SSTR2 now offers a potential therapeutic target. Indeed, we find that SSTR2 is almost ubiquitously expressed in ONB regardless of grade or whether a tumor is primary, recurrent, or metastatic. Expression was slightly diminished in high grade ONB, but not significantly so, and for the first time (albeit not surprisingly), we demonstrate that expression did not correlate with DSS or DFS. However, the size of our cohort may not be adequately powered to detect smaller differences in outcome based on SSTR2 expression. Our study is concordant with a recent publication from Czapiewski et al. [19] demonstrating SSTR2 expression in 30 of 40 (75%) ONB, with no differences based on grade. The frequency of SSTR2 is higher in our cases, which may be attributed to differences in scoring schemes and/or immunohistochemical protocol variability.

These findings thus confirm the opportunity for the use of 68-gallium labeled synthetic somatostatin-analogs for imaging and therapy in ONB (Fig. 4) akin to other SSTR2 expressing neuroendocrine tumor types. Use of somatostatin-analogs to treat ONB has been reported in individual cases [17, 26–28]. In these prior studies, somatostatin-analog radionuclide therapies have been both administered empirically and, in other cases, following confirmation of SSTR2 tissue expression via IHC [17]. However given the rarity of cases treated in this manner with corresponding SSTR2 expression, there is no established threshold for SSTR2 expression levels and prediction of response to therapy. Our study is also not equipped to answer this as only two of our patients had received somatostatin analogs (with H-scores of 270 and 290), and limited current follow-up. Ultimately, if documentation of a certain level of SSTR2 is shown to be predictive of response to treatment, we suggest based on our findings that testing of any specimen: primary, recurrent, or metastasis for a single patient would be adequate given the consistent expression in ONB. While it is not clear whether SSTR2 expression is altered by neoadjuvant chemoradiation, one such case tested showed robust SSTR2 expression.

Furthermore, moderate SSTR2 expression (as defined by an H-score of 105) is far more common in ONB than in other round blue cell mimics raising its potential utility diagnostically as well as theranostically. This is also in line with the study by Czapiewski et al. [19], who found no expression of SSTR2 in ONB mimics. Currently, in the context of sinonasal round blue cell tumors, there are no single markers that are diagnostic of ONB. The diagnosis rests on appropriate location (i.e. nasoethmoid), morphologic features, and the characteristic but somewhat non-specific immunophenotype consisting of essentially absent cytokeratins, neuroendocrine marker positivity, and S100 positive sustentacular cells. More recently, calretinin has enjoyed some use in supporting the diagnosis of ONB [32]. Regarding the use of SSTR2, while this marker performed well in discriminating between mimics in this study, there were some non-ONB cases (particularly sinonasal neuroendocrine carcinomas) that did show considerable SSTR2 expression. Furthermore, comparisons between ONB and specific entities (rather than mimics as a whole) are limited given the limited number in each non-ONB diagnostic category. Additionally, not all entities in the broad differential diagnosis were tested. For instance, while not an exceptionally common dilemma with clinicopathologic correlation and Epstein Barr virus testing, nasopharyngeal carcinomas may enter the differential diagnosis for ONB, but are also now known to express SSTR2 [33].

Thus, it would seem that SSTR2 offers a reliable supportive but not necessarily definitive marker for ONB. Conversely, absent or low SSTR2 expression, while not excluding the diagnosis of ONB, should prompt consideration for the possibility of an ONB mimic. We had one SSTR2 negative ONB, and two cases with an H-score of < 105. These cases had only small specimens available for testing: one case was Hyams grade II, one was Hyams grade III, and one could not be graded. All 3 cases did show appropriate site, morphology (where preserved) and immunophenotype suggesting that low or absent expression could be related to technical issues.

In summary, SSTR2 is highly expressed in ONB. While it has no apparent prognostic value, the expression points to a potential therapeutic value with somatostatin analogues. If SSTR2 immuno-expression is required to initiate treatment, it would seem that any specimen type (primary, recurrence, or metastasis) with adequate tumor would be sufficient for testing. SSTR2 expression is retained in ONB across all grades and is far less common in ONB mimics, suggesting that expression can be a supportive diagnostic finding as well.
