# Random X chromosome inactivation in female Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type X1: insights from sural nerve biopsy analysis.
**Authors:** Bekircan-Kurt CE, Aksu-Menges E, Kumtepe ET, Durmaz CD, Terzi YK, Ergul-Ulger Z, Temucin ÇM, Erdem-Ozdamar S, Tan E, Balci-Hayta B
**Journal:** BMC Neurol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12883-025-04297-z](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-025-04297-z)

## Content

BACKGROUND: X-linked Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease type 1 (CMTX1) is a hereditary neuropathy caused by mutations in the GJB1 gene encoding Connexin 32 (Cx32). Despite its X-linked dominant inheritance, it has been suggested that the variable phenotypic expression of the disease in females may be due to skewed X chromosome inactivation (XCI) in Schwann cells. This pilot study aimed to examine the XCI patterns in archived sural nerve biopsies of female patients diagnosed with CMTX1 for the first time in the literature. METHODS: Two unrelated female CMTX1 patients, initially misdiagnosed with chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP), with motor conduction blocks were included. GJB1 mutations were identified using whole-exome sequencing (WES). XCI patterns were analyzed using the human androgen receptor (HUMARA) assay on archived fresh-frozen sural nerve biopsy samples performed prior the genetic diagnosis and compared with age-matched vasculitic neuropathy controls. RESULTS: The GJB1 c.379 A > C mutation was identified in both CMTX1 patients. XCI analysis revealed a random XCI pattern in affected nerve tissues, with no significant differences between patients and controls. CONCLUSION: Contrary to previous hypotheses, our findings suggest that XCI does not contribute to phenotypic variability in female CMTX1 patients. These results highlight the complexity of CMTX1 pathophysiology and highlight the need for further studies on alternative mechanisms regulating disease severity in females.

X-linked Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease (CMTX) accounts for approximately 7–15% of all cases of Charcot-Marie-Tooth (CMT) disease [1, 2]. CMTX1 represents approximately 90% of all X-linked Charcot-Marie-Tooth (CMTX) cases [3]. It is caused by mutations in the Gap junction beta-1 (GJB1) gene on chromosome Xq13 which encodes the gap junction protein Connexin 32 (Cx32) [4, 5]. Cx32 belongs to a family of homologous integral membrane proteins expressed particularly by Schwann cells and oligodendrocytes that oligomerize to form functional aqueous channels that allow rapid transport of ions and small molecules between cells [6, 7] When the GJB1 gene is mutated, the function of these gap junction channels is impaired, leading to impaired communication between Schwann cells and resulting defective myelination of the nerves [8, 9]. Most GJB1 mutations lead to partial or complete loss of protein function, such as abnormal protein trafficking or stabilization of the closed state of the channel, causing large shifts in the hemichannel conductance-voltage relationship, and cause loss-of-function in myelinating Schwann cells [4, 10]. Occasionally, mutations can also cause a gain-of-function resulting in accumulation of Cx32 in the endoplasmic reticulum or Golgi apparatus [11, 12]. Although the inheritance pattern of the mutations is recognized as X-linked dominant, the clinical presentation of the disease varies between males and females.


The clinical presentation in male patients is typically uniform, with distal extremity weakness usually emerging by the age of 10 [13–16]. Electrophysiological evaluation reveals a combination of axonal and demyelinating features, referred to as ‘intermediate neuropathy’ [14]. CMT1X also affects female carriers. However, females with heterozygous GJB1 mutation have a variable and milder phenotype with later age of onset than males [14, 17].

Previously, the variable phenotype in female was attributed to the pattern of X-inactivation. One study found that only some of the myelinating Schwann cells expressed the mutant GJB1 allele in heterozygous cx32+/- female mice that attributed to skewed X-inactivation in mice [18]. However, X-inactivation analysis of serum samples in females with CMTX1 failed to reveal a skewed inactivation pattern in humans [17, 19]. This finding has led to the hypothesis that the variable phenotype in females may be associated with a skewed X chromosome inactivation (XCI) pattern in the affected human Schwann cells [19]. However, this hypothesis has not yet been confirmed to date due to the lack of routine nerve biopsies in CMTX1 patients. In this study, the XCI pattern was investigated in archived sural nerve samples obtained through nerve biopsy performed for differential diagnosis prior to the genetic diagnosis of CMTX1 in females. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to analyze this hypothesis through the evaluation of XCI pattern in the nerve tissue of CMTX1 patients. The distinct clinical and electrophysiological features of the patients were also reviewed.

This study was approved by local Ethical Review Board (KA-21040 and 21-AKD-94). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, according to the Helsinki declaration. Two unrelated female CMTX patients, who displayed distinct clinical and electrophysiological features, were followed up in our neurology department, and were included in this study. Before genetic diagnosis, these patients were followed up in another center with a diagnosis of chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) based on their clinical and electrophysiological findings. Due to lack of response to treatment, the patients were referred to the Neuromuscular Diseases Research Laboratory for diagnostic nerve biopsy. Neurological examinations, Charcot-Marie-Tooth (CMT) neuropathy scores v.2 [20] and electrophysiological findings were recorded in these patients. Routine nerve biopsies were acquired from the left sural nerve under local anesthesia. A portion of the sample was embedded in plastic and the semi-thin sections were stained with toluidine blue. The remaining part of the specimens were rapidly frozen in isopentane cooled in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until further use. Standard haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was applied to the cryostat tissue sections. Control group samples were composed of the nerve tissues from two female patients of similar age who underwent nerve biopsy with the diagnosis of vasculitic neuropathy.

Genomic DNA was extracted and purified from peripheral blood samples of patients using standard protocols. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Sophia Genetics DNA Library Prep Kit according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, and WES was performed on the Illumina NextSeq2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and analyzed by SOPHiA-DDM-v4™ (Sophia Genetics, SA) software.

The XCI pattern was investigated by analyzing the methylation status of a polymorphic locus in the androgen receptor (AR) gene [21] DNA samples were extracted from fresh frozen nerve biopsy tissues (1–3 mm3) of patients and controls using the DNA QIAmp DNA Micro kit as per manufacturer’s guidelines. (Qiagen Ltd, Manchester, UK). 500 ng of genomic DNA was first digested with 5 U of methylation-sensitive restriction enzyme HpaII (NEB, Beverly, MA) in a 20 µl reaction volume at 37 °C for overnight, and then the 75 ng of digested and undigested DNA template from each tissue was amplified by PCR using primers flanking the polymorphic region of the AR gene. The following primers, as previously described [21], were used: F: 5’-TCCAGAATCTGTTCCAGAGCGTGC-3’; R: 5’-GCTGTGAAGGTTGCTGTTCCTCAT-3’, in which the forward primer was labeled with FAM. Two CCGG sites upstream from the polymorphic ‘CAG’ repeats within exon 1 of the AR gene were methylated on the inactive X chromosome. PCR amplification was obtained only from the inactive allele harboring the methylated HpaII sites. The size and signal intensity of the digested and undigested PCR products were determined by capillary electrophoresis using the Applied Biosystems™ 3500 Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using GeneScan™ 500 ROX™ size standard (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and analyzed by the GeneMapper™ software 5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to quantify the product from each parental allele by peak area. As previously reported by Thouin et al., relative activation/inactivation percentage ratios of AR alleles for each sample were quantified by first identifying the two peaks in the undigested sample, then normalizing the peak intensities to compensate for any unequal amplification of the alleles. This normalization factor was then multiplied by the digested sample peak ratio [22]. The terms short and long allele were used to distinguish between paternal and maternal alleles depending on the size of the CAG repeats. The cut-off defining skewed XCI was defined as ≥ 80% [23–25].

Two female CMTX1 patients were included in this study. The median age of onset was 40.5 years (32–49), and the first symptom in both patients was weakness in the lower extremities. None of the patients showed signs of pes cavus or hammertoes. All were initially diagnosed with CIDP, based on motor conduction blocks (Fig. 1) and lack of family history. Cerebrospinal fluid protein was slightly elevated in all patients (ranging from 51 to 58 mg/dL, with a normal value of < 40 mg/dL). Sural nerve biopsy was performed in these patients due to lack of response to corticosteroid and/or intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Specifically, nerve fibers showed evidence of demyelinating neuropathy with prominent axonal degeneration in these two patients (Fig. 2). As a result of WES analysis, c.379 A > C (p.Ile127Leu) mutation was detected in the GJB1 (NM_000166.6) gene of both patients. This mutation had already been submitted to ClinVar and has been classified as likely pathogenic, associated with the CMTX1 phenotype (information available on ClinVar [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/variation/3731607/]. CMTX1, which is caused by GJB1 mutations, is usually associated with distal extremity weakness, muscle wasting, sensory deficits (loss of proprioception), upper limb areflexia, and pes cavus emerging in the second decade in male patients [14]. The nerve conduction studies show an “intermediate neuropathy” where axonal and demyelinating features are mixed [14, 26]. However, despite its X-linked dominant inheritance, the CMTX1 phenotype varies in female patients. Female CMTX1 patients are generally less affected with a later age of onset; some female mutation carriers are asymptomatic [17]. In addition, nerve conduction studies of female CMTX1 patients show marked variation of velocity within the same motor nerve and inter-nerve heterogeneity of motor nerve conduction velocities [14].

This variable phenotype in CMTX1 females has long been thought to result from skewed X-inactivation, as occurs in some X-linked hereditary diseases such as Rett syndrome [27] and Duchenne’s muscular dystrophy, where non-random X-inactivation preferentially silences either the mutant or wild-type allele in different tissues [28]. However, one study investigated the mechanism of X inactivation using the HUMARA assay in blood samples of 67 women with CMTX1 and 24 controls and found no difference in the XCI pattern between patients and controls [19]. Therefore, the variable phenotype in females has been proposed to be associated with non-random X-inactivation in Schwann cells, which are responsible for myelination in peripheral nerve. A study provided evidence for this hypothesis by showing that in heterozygous cx32+/- female mice have fewer demyelinated axons than age-matched homozygous cx32-/- females and cx32-/Y males, and patchy staining of connexin 32 protein along the axon [18]. With the widespread availability of genetic diagnosis, diagnostic nerve biopsy is no longer used in CMTX1 patients, making it difficult to perform a similar study with human nerve tissue to support the hypothesis to date.

Two female patients were referred to our department in the early 2000 s due to progressive weakness prominently in the lower extremities. Both were initially diagnosed with CIDP with motor conduction blocks and slight elevation of cerebrospinal fluid protein, and both were treated with IVIG. However, apart from minimal improvement in motor strength and resolution of motor conduction blocks, no benefit was obtained from the treatment. The patients underwent left sural nerve biopsy for differential diagnosis and predominant demyelinating neuropathy accompanied by axonal regeneration was detected.


More recently, both patients received a genetic diagnosis of CMTX1, identified by the same c.379 A < C mutation in GJB1 gene, that has been previously reported in Turkish population [29]. A significant finding in these patients with the same mutation was the presence of motor conduction blocks at disease onset, which mimicked an acquired polyneuropathy. Previously, motor conduction blocks outside usual entrapment sites had been reported in only two cases [14], but with a different mutation [30]. Additionally, we observed motor conduction blocks in another CMT1X female patient with 585 T > C (NM_000166.6). This further supports that, as in previous studies, mutation type does not influence clinical or electrophysiological presentation [17].

Archived fresh frozen nerve biopsy samples of female CMTX1 patients were then analyzed for X-inactivation to elucidate the variable phenotype seen in female CMTX1 patients, but both displayed a random XCI pattern. Therefore, in our study, we could not demonstrate skewed X-inactivation in Schwann cells. A key limitation of our study is the small sample size, which restricts the generalizability of our findings. However, the findings of this study are of considerable significance, especially in the context of increasing reliance on genetic diagnostic methods and the declining use of diagnostic nerve biopsies. Moreover, the archival nature of the biopsy material may represent a technical constraint, as prolonged storage may compromise nucleic acid integrity and cellular preservation. These factors may particularly affect the recovery of Schwann cell-specific material in sufficient quantity and quality, thereby reducing the sensitivity and reliability of X-inactivation analyses in this cell type. However, we were able to obtain sufficient and high-quality DNA from nerve biopsies of both patients. Given that nerve biopsies are no longer performed for diagnostic purposes in this disease, obtaining additional samples for further investigation is not feasible. Although our study included only two patients, the presence of the same mutation in both strengthens the interpretative power of our findings.


Our study together with previous large cohort studies of XCI patterns in blood suggests that X inactivation does not explain the phenotypic variation in female CMTX1 patients [17, 19]. This result implies that other factors, such as somatic mosaicism, genetic modifiers, epigenetic regulations or Schwann cell-intrinsic factors, may contribute to the phenotypic variability and play a larger role in disease severity. Somatic mosaicism, for instance, may lead to genetic heterogeneity within Schwann cells due to post-zygotic mutations or differential XCI patterns occurring in a subset of cells during development. Such mosaicism could result in a mixed population of cells with varying levels of functional GJB1 expression, potentially leading to regionally distinct patterns of demyelination and variable clinical severity. Moreover, genetic modifiers—including rare or common variants in genes implicated in peripheral nerve biology, such as those involved in gap junction integrity, myelination, or cellular stress responses—may influence disease expressivity and progression. The presence of such modifiers could modulate the downstream effects of GJB1 mutations in a patient-specific manner. Epigenetic regulatory mechanisms such as aberrant DNA methylation, histone modifications, or dysregulated non-coding RNA expression may alter transcriptional activity of GJB1 or other critical genes in Schwann cells, thereby contributing to functional differences that are not captured by XCI analysis alone. Further research utilizing single-cell transcriptomics, epigenetic profiling, and patient-derived Schwann cell models may be needed to fully elucidate the factors driving phenotypic variation in female CMTX1 patients.
