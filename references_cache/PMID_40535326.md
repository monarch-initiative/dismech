# Tapetal-like sheen as a key phenotypical feature in TTLL5-associated cone dystrophy caused by a novel variant.
**Authors:** Zhai Y, Kodida R, Ballios BG
**Journal:** Am J Ophthalmol Case Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ajoc.2025.102360](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajoc.2025.102360)

## Content

PURPOSE: To report a patient with cone dystrophy and a striking tapetal reflex caused by a novel variant in TTLL5, which was initially missed by a local retinal specialist due to inaccurate phenotyping of macular dystrophy.

OBSERVATION: A 33-year-old male patient presented with Bull's eye maculopathy and a tapetal-like reflex, confirmed via fundus examination and multimodal imaging. Initial genetic testing with a macular dystrophy panel was negative. Electroretinography showed reduced photopic responses and normal scotopic responses, supporting a diagnosis of cone dystrophy, and subsequent evaluation using an extended inherited retinal dystrophy panel identified a homozygous TTLL5 c.1186+2T>C variant classified as "likely pathogenic."

CONCLUSIONS AND IMPORTANCE: This case underscores the critical role of accurate phenotyping in guiding molecular genetic testing, particularly in distinguishing cone dystrophy from other macular dystrophies, and highlights the unique expertise of inherited retinal disease specialists in this process. Additionally, it further validates the possibility that the tapetal reflex is a common but previously underrecognized feature of TTLL5-associated retinal dystrophies and expands the known variant spectrum of TTLL5-associated disease. The presence of a tapetal reflex-previously widely associated with RPGR-associated dystrophies-highlighting the overlapping disease mechanisms and clinical presentation.

Cone and cone-rod dystrophies (CD/CRD) are rare inherited retinal dystrophies characterized by primary cone photoreceptor dysfunction, leading to impaired visual acuity, abnormal color vision, photophobia, and central scotomas. Due to the overlap in anatomical finding, these conditions can sometimes be clinically misdiagnosed as macular dystrophy if there is no supporting electrophysiology. TTLL5, encoding tubulin tyrosine ligase-like protein 5, is a recently identified gene associated with CD/CRD. 1 , 2 TTLL5 is highly expressed in cone photoreceptors and functions as a glutamylase, catalyzing post-translational modifications of RPGRORF15, a critical protein for ciliary function in photoreceptor cells. 3

Here, we present a case of TTLL5-associated cone dystrophy caused by a novel homozygous TTLL5 variant, characterized by a distinct tapetal reflex, further validating a potentially underrecognized feature and expanding the known variant spectrum of TTLL5-associated retinal dystrophies. We also highlight how accurate clinical phenotyping guided the molecular diagnosis.

The proband is a 33-year-old man referred to the inherited retinal dystrophy clinic for a “bullseye maculopathy” with negative genetic testing results. He developed difficulty with central vision at four years of age, which gradually declined over the years. No complaints of peripheral vision loss, color vision deficits, or nyctalopia. The patient reported preserved scotopic function, noting the ability to see stars in a clear night sky. His best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/200, bilaterally. Intraocular pressure was within the normal range, measured 13 mmHg in the right eye and 12 mmHg in the left eye. Fundus examination revealed bilaterally normal optic discs and vessels, with central macular atrophy. A tapetal-like sheen was observed in both the macula and peripheral retina (Fig. 1 A and 1B). Green laser fundus autofluorescence (G-FAF) revealed a distinct hyperautofluorescent ring surrounding a hypoautofluorescent region in the central macula of both eyes (Fig. 1C and D). Peripheral retinal autofluorescence was normal. Spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) revealed significant central macular thinning accompanied by disruption of the outer retinal layers. Peripheral macular OCT at the location of the tapetal sheen demonstrated thinning of the outer nuclear layer, with a preserved external limiting membrane and maintained thickness of the outer retinal layers. However, there was a lack of distinction between the ellipsoid zone (EZ) and the RPE/Bruch's membrane complex, which appeared as a single homogeneous thick band. (Fig. 1E). The family is of Pakistani descent, and the proband's parents are first cousins (Fig. 1G), making an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern more likely, although X-linked and de novo autosomal dominant inheritance remain possible. No other family members are affected. The patient was initially diagnosed with Stargardt maculopathy by his local retina specialist based on clinical features. However, a macular dystrophy panel comprising 28 genes, organized by a clinical geneticist, returned negative results. All full-field electroretinography (ffERG) recordings were performed in accordance with the International Society for Clinical Electrophysiology of Vision (ISCEV) Standard for Full-Field Electroretinography (2015 update). 4 His ffERG recording revealed electrophysiological findings consistent with cone dystrophy that had previously been overlooked. The scotopic responses, including the dark-adapted 0.01 cd⋅s/m2 ERG, showed normal amplitude. In contrast, the photopic responses were markedly abnormal: the light-adapted 3.0 cd⋅s/m2 ERG was absent, and the light-adapted 30 Hz Flicker ERG was significantly reduced and delayed (Fig. 1H). These findings are not consistent with macular dystrophy, where full-field ERG responses are typically normal. Thus, an accurate diagnosis of cone dystrophy was established. A Next Generation Sequencing (NGS)-based inherited retinal dystrophy panel covering 351 IRD-associated genes was performed at a CLIA (Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments)-certified laboratory in Finland (Blueprint Genetics, #99D2092375). He was found to be homozygous variant in TTLL5 c.1186+2T>C. This variant is absent from gnomAD (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org, accessed April 12, 2025), a large reference population database designed to exclude individuals with severe pediatric disease. It alters a nucleotide within a canonical splice site, likely resulting in abnormal splicing and loss of gene function—a well-established disease mechanism for this gene. For these reasons, the variant is classified as 'likely pathogenic' according to ACMG guidelines. Due to high coverage and variant allele frequency, Sanger confirmation was not pursued in accordance with CLIA standards.

Tubulin Tyrosine Ligase-Like 5 (TTLL5) is an enzyme from the TTLL family, which is involved in post-translational modifications of tubulin and other proteins, such as glutamylation. TTLL5 specifically glutamylates RPGRORF15 within its Glu-Gly–rich repetitive region containing specific motifs. 3 Mutations in TTLL5 can impair RPGR glutamylation, resulting in a cone dominant retinal phenotype that resembles the retinal dystrophy caused by distal truncating variants in RPGRORF15 5.

Retinitis Pigmentosa GTPase Regulator (RPGR) is a critical ciliary protein, and mutations in the RPGR gene are a leading cause of inherited retinal dystrophies. RPGR is expressed in a complex manner, generating two primary isoforms: the default isoform (RPGRdefault) and the ORF15 isoform (RPGRORF15). The RPGRdefault isoform spans exons 1–19, whereas RPGRORF15 shares the first 14 exons with RPGRdefault but terminates in a large alternative exon, known as the Open Reading Frame 15 (ORF15) region. 6 This topic is particularly important as recent clinical trials on RPGR gene replacement therapy have shown promising results, 7 , 8 highlighting the importance of understanding the pathogenic changes in the RPGRORF15 and TTLL5 interaction.

When the TTLL5 pathogenic variant was eventually identified in this patient, the initial failure to achieve an accurate molecular diagnosis highlighted the critical importance of an accurate clinical diagnosis based on structure and function study. Although the patient's anatomic features were consistent with macular dystrophy, the ERG findings clearly demonstrated a functional deficiency consistent with cone dystrophy, rather than macular dystrophy. The initial genetic testing organized by the clinical geneticist was based on a diagnosis of macular dystrophy made by the local retina specialist. Genes associated with broader IRD phenotypes, such as cone dystrophy in this case, were excluded from the initial panel, leading to a false negative result. This case underscores the vital role of detailed phenotyping in guiding the selection of appropriate genetic testing strategies. It also highlights the unique expertise of inherited retinal disease specialists in this process, particularly in interpreting ERG findings. Furthermore, this case underscores the need for medical genetics specialists to exercise caution when ordering phenotype-based genetic panels for IRDs, due to some overlap in the structure and function of these conditions. Broad-based IRD panels, when used as a first-tier test, may provide greater clinical utility by reducing the need for additional testing, lowering healthcare costs, and enhancing patient medical management.

Pathogenic variants in TTLL5 are associated with autosomal recessive cone dystrophy (OMIM #615860), first described in 2014. 1 The majority of reported TTLL5-associated cases present with either a cone dystrophy (CD) or cone-rod dystrophy (CRD) phenotype. Unusual ocular phenotypes, including retinitis pigmentosa (RP) 9 and early-onset severe retinal dystrophy (EOSRD), 10 have also been reported. Additionally, TTLL5-associated disorders have been linked to systemic involvements such as high-frequency hearing loss 11 and reduced sperm motility leading to infertility. 2

A tapetal-like sheen is a golden metallic retinal reflection seen on clinical fundus examination, resembling the reflection seen in the fundus of animals with a tapetum. It was initially a well-recognized as a characteristic feature in female RPGR carriers 12 and has been infrequently observed in hemizygous males. 13 Additionally, a tapetal-like retinal sheen has been reported in other inherited retinal disorders, such as Oguchi disease, 14 which features a golden or metallic sheen that disappears after prolonged dark adaptation, and X-linked retinoschisis. 15 Benson et al. recently reported that 25 % of hemizygous males exhibited a tapetal reflex, all with CD or CRD phenotypes, suggesting it may be a common feature of RPGR-associated CRD caused by variants near the 3′ end of ORF15. 16 Cehajic-Kapetanovic et al. found that, unlike RPGR-associated RP, RPGR variants associated with a cone-dominant phenotype are predominantly located in the distal region of ORF15, including the basic domain, disrupting the RPGRORF15/TTLL5 interaction and subsequent glutamylation. 5 As accumulating evidence links the etiology of TTLL5 to the 3′ end of RPGRORF15, the presence of a tapetal reflex in TTLL5-associated cone dystrophy—a distinctive feature previously described in RPGR-associated cone-dominant dystrophy—provides further clinical support for the recently proposed etiology, as detailed in our case report. On our literature review, we identified three reported TTLL5 cases, including the present case, where a tapetal sheen was observed. 16 , 17 These cases represent 13 % of all reported TTLL5 cases, suggesting that a tapetal sheen may be a common feature of TTLL5-associated cone dystrophy.

In summary, our case underscores the importance of accurate phenotyping in guiding molecular diagnosis and highlights the potential diagnostic value of recognizing tapetal sheen as a clinical feature in the evaluation of TTLL5-related retinal dystrophies.
