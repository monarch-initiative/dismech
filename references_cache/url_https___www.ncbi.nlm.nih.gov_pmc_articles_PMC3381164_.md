<p><strong>Background:</strong> A humanized mouse was developed to study elastin assembly and the pathogenesis of cutis laxa.</p>
<p><strong>Results:</strong> Mutant transcripts incorporate into elastic fibers of skin and lung with adverse effects but not aorta.</p>

<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Alternative Splicing, Elastin, Extracellular Matrix, Mouse, Vascular Biology, Cutis Laxa</p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="abstract2"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Elastin is the extracellular matrix protein in vertebrates that provides elastic recoil to blood vessels, the lung, and skin. Because the elastin gene has undergone significant changes in the primate lineage, modeling elastin diseases in non-human animals can be problematic. To investigate the pathophysiology underlying a class of elastin gene mutations leading to autosomal dominant cutis laxa, we engineered a cutis laxa mutation (single base deletion) into the human elastin gene contained in a bacterial artificial chromosome. When expressed as a transgene in mice, mutant elastin was incorporated into elastic fibers in the skin and lung with adverse effects on tissue function. In contrast, only low levels of mutant protein incorporated into aortic elastin, which explains why the vasculature is relatively unaffected in this disease. RNA stability studies found that alternative exon splicing acts as a modifier of disease severity by influencing the spectrum of mutant transcripts that survive nonsense-mediated decay. Our results confirm the critical role of the C-terminal region of tropoelastin in elastic fiber assembly and suggest tissue-specific differences in the elastin assembly pathway.</p></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>The extracellular matrix protein elastin appeared in evolution concurrent with the closed circulatory system and allowed the heart to evolve into an efficient multichambered pump by enabling the arterial tree to support pulsatile flow. In a similar way, elastin enabled the vertebrate lung to become a more efficient gas exchange organ by allowing the respiratory apparatus to store potential energy created by contraction of the diaphragm during inhalation and use that energy to drive lung recoil during exhalation (<a href="#B1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B1">1</a>). The critical role of elastin in vertebrate tissue function is underscored by the fact the mice lacking elastin do not survive past the first few hours of life (<a href="#B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2">2</a>).</p>
<p>There is a single elastin gene in mammals (<em>ELN</em>) that encodes from 34–36 exons (depending on species) with a relatively simple promoter that is regulated mostly in an on-off fashion during development (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>, <a href="#B4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B4">4</a>). Like many other mammalian genes, <em>ELN</em> undergoes extensive alternative splicing, which results in isoform diversity. Although the functional consequences of alternative splicing are unclear, the tight regulation of this process during development suggests an important role for variant transcripts in elastin function (<a href="#B5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B5">5</a>, <a href="#B6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B6">6</a>).</p>
<p>Elastin is secreted as a monomer (tropoelastin) that is cross-linked in the extracellular space to form the functional polymer. The process of tropoelastin secretion and elastic fiber assembly is complicated, and not all steps are understood (<a href="#B3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B3">3</a>). Proteins known to be important in the assembly process include members of the fibulin, fibrillin, and lysyl oxidase families. Functional mapping studies have identified domains in the C-terminal region of the tropoelastin protein, encoded by exons 30 and 36, that are critical for tropoelastin self-association and for interaction with cells and accessory assembly proteins (<a href="#B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7">7</a>). Loss of function mutations that delete this region of the gene transcript (<em>e.g.</em> premature termination, multiexon deletion, etc.) lead to elastin haploinsufficiency and supravalvular aortic stenosis (OMIM 185500), an autosomal dominant disease in humans that predominantly affects the large elastic vessels in the vascular system (<a href="#B8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B8">8</a>–<a href="#B12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B12">12</a>).</p>
<p>A second class of elastin mutation is linked to an autosomal dominant form of cutis laxa (ADCL,<a href="#FN4" class="usa-link"><sup>3</sup></a> OMIM 123700) and arises from nucleotide deletion, insertion, or exon-splicing errors. These mutations produce missense sequence, usually in the 3′ end of the transcript that encodes the sequences important for fiber assembly and elastin function (<a href="#B13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B13">13</a>–<a href="#B16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16">16</a>). In contrast to loss of function associated with deletion of the 3′ region, evidence suggests a dominant-negative mechanism for ADCL where the mutant tropoelastin adversely affects assembly and function of protein from the normal allele (<a href="#B17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17">17</a>, <a href="#B18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B18">18</a>).</p>
<p>The clinical phenotype of ADCL is variable, ranging from loose and sagging skin (the defining phenotype in this disease) to severe pulmonary complication and mild internal organ involvement (<a href="#B15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B15">15</a>, <a href="#B16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16">16</a>, <a href="#B19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B19">19</a>–<a href="#B21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B21">21</a>). An interesting question is why this type of elastin mutation greatly affects skin and lung, whereas the aorta, with a much higher elastin concentration, is relatively spared. This is opposite to what is seen in supravalvular aortic stenosis where elastin loss-of-function mutations severely affect the aorta and other elastic vessels but have little consequence in the skin.</p>
<p>To better understand the molecular pathology of ADCL and to learn more about how elastin gene mutations influence elastic fiber assembly, we used recombineering to introduce a well characterized ADCL mutation (a single base deletion in exon 30 at position 2012 (2012ΔG) (<a href="#B16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16">16</a>)) into the human elastin gene in a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC). The mutant elastin BAC (hBAC<sup>CL</sup>) and a BAC containing the wild-type human gene (hBAC<sup>WT</sup>) were then express as transgenes in mice. It was necessary to use the human gene for this study because the elastin gene has undergone significant changes in the primate lineage (<a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">22</a>). The human elastin gene has two fewer exons than mouse, attributable to the sequential loss of exons 34 and 35 during primate evolution (<a href="#B23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23">23</a>). In addition, although still contained in the human gene, exon 22 is never included in the human elastin transcript but is found in the mouse transcript. Another difference is that the 3′ exons around the CL mutations are frequently spliced out in humans but not in mice (<a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">22</a>), and the presence or absence of these spliced exons has a functional consequence in terms of transcript length and stability. These differences suggest that human mutations introduced into the mouse elastin gene will have a different biological effect and might not be relevant for functional analysis of the human disease. Importantly, the human BAC elastin transgene retains the human alternative splicing pattern when expressed in the mouse (<a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">22</a>), which makes the human BAC mouse a relevant model to study how <em>ELN</em> mutations impact tissue function. Our findings show that mutant transcript is expressed appropriately in all elastin-rich tissues and that alternative splicing of down-stream exons generates multiple transcripts with different lengths of missense sequence and different stabilities. Analysis of tissues from animals expressing the hBAC<sup>CL</sup> transgene found that the mutant protein is incorporated into elastic fibers in skin and lung with adverse effects, but low levels of incorporation were observed in the aorta, which explains why the vasculature is relatively unaffected in this disease as a consequence of this mutation. These results suggest that the process of elastin assembly may be different in the aorta compared with lung and skin, which challenges the current thinking of a common fiber assembly mechanism in all tissues.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES</h2>

<section id="sec5"><h5 class="pmc_sec_title">Engineering Single Base Deletion Synonymous to Known Human Cutis Laxa Mutation into Human BAC Elastin Gene</h5>
<p>Tointroduce the cutis laxa mutation (deletion of nucleotide 2012) into the <em>ELN</em>-BAC gene (clone CTB-51J22, Research Genetics), we used a two-step RedE/RedT recombination technique (<a href="#B24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B24">24</a>) in <em>Escherichia coli</em> strain DY380 that expresses bacteriophage proteins for homologous recombination (kindly provided by Dr. Neal Copeland, National Cancer Institute, Frederick, MD). The overall strategy is illustrated in <a href="http://www.jbc.org/cgi/content/full/M111.327940/DC1" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">supplemental Fig. 1</a>. The first step was recombination between a cloned genomic elastin fragment containing both a single base deletion at position 2012 sequence and a <em>sacB-Kan</em> fusion gene. This construct was prepared by cloning a 1.9-kb genomic DNA fragment containing elastin exons 28, 29, and 30 from hBAC<sup>WT</sup> into a pUC vector. Guanine at BAC position 30,494 (position 2012 in exon 30 of the elastin gene) was deleted using PCR. A <em>sacB-Kan</em> fusion gene, constructed by PCR using the pKOV-Kan vector as a template (kindly provided by Dr. John Heath, Birmingham, UK) (<a href="#B25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B25">25</a>), was inserted into an AccI site in the 1.9-kb elastin construct located in intron 29. The construct was then trimmed with BmgBi and PpuMi, giving a 626-bp construct (nucleotides 30345–30970).</p>
<p>The bacteria were prepared for the first recombination step by electroporating the complete unmodified elastin BAC into DY380 cells using a Gene Pulser® II Electroporation System (Bio-Rad) at 2.5 kV, 25 microfarads, 200 ohms in a 0.2-cm cuvette. Electroporated cells were grown in LB media containing chloramphenicol at 30 °C to prevent the expression of bacteriophage proteins. Recombination was initiated by activating the BAC-containing DY380 cells at 42 °C for 45 min then washing in cold water. The 626-bp genomic fragment containing the mutation and the <em>sacB-Kan</em> cassette were then introduced into the cells by electroporation (1.8 kV, 20 microfarads, 200 ohms in 0.1 cm cuvette). After incubating the electroporated cells at 30 °C for 1.5 h to facilitate recombination, the bacteria were spread onto LB agar plates containing 20 μg/ml chloramphenicol and 20 μg/ml kanamycin. Surviving cells were harvested and used for the second recombination step, which involved electroporation into the cells of the mutant genomic fragment that lacked the <em>sacB-Kan</em> cassette. Selection of recombinants was done by growing the cells on LB agar plates containing 20 μg/ml chloramphenicol and 7% sucrose, which kills cells that still contain the <em>sacB</em> gene.</p>
<p>Appropriate recombination reactions were confirmed by digestion with BglI followed by ethidium bromide staining and Southern blot analysis. BAC DNA, obtained using a mini prep kit (Qiagen), was digested with BglI endonuclease and run on a 0.7% agarose gel. DNA was transferred from the gel onto a Hybond N+ nylon membrane and hybridized with a <sup>32</sup>P-labeled probe specific for elastin intron 27. The probe corresponding to nucleotides 31538–32520 was made by PCR using forward primer 5′-CATTCCAGCCTGGGCGACAGAGCAAG-3′ and reverse primer 5′-GGCCTGGACGACAGAGCGAGACTGTA-3′.</p></section><section id="sec6"><h5 class="pmc_sec_title">hBAC<sup>CL</sup> DNA Purification and Generation of Transgenic Mice</h5>
<p>hBAC<sup>CL</sup> circular DNA was isolated using a Large-construct kit (Qiagen). After the last ethanol precipitation step, DNA was dissolved in injection buffer (10 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 10 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> NaCl, 0.25 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> EDTA) and then dialyzed on a floating 0.1-μm Millipore membrane filter against the injection buffer. Isolated hBAC<sup>CL</sup> DNA was injected at a concentration of 1 ng/ml into fertilized mouse hybrid C57BL/6-C3H/F1 oocytes, which were implanted into the uterus of pseudopregnant foster mothers. After birth, potential founders were screened for the presence of the transgene by PCR on mouse tail DNA using the human elastin specific primers: forward 5′-GATCAGAATCTCTAGGACTGAATAGGCCAG-3′ and reverse 5′-GCTACTAGGGATACTAAGGGGGAATAATGG-3′. PCR reactions were performed in a 25-μl total volume containing 50 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> Tris-HCl (pH 9.2), 16 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> ammonium sulfate, 3.5 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 0.1% Tween 20, 0.2 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> dNTP, 2.5 units of KlentaqLA, and 1 μl of mouse tail DNA. Mouse tail DNA was prepared by digesting 2–3 mm of mouse tail tissue overnight at 55 °C in 200 μl of tail digestion buffer (50 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> CaCl<sub>2</sub>, 1% Tween 20) plus 25 μl of proteinase K (10 mg/ml). Animals positive for the transgene were mated to WT animals (C57BL/6) to stabilize the line. hBAC<sup>CL</sup> mice were also bred into the hBAC<sup>WT</sup> line to generate hBAC<sup>CL</sup>- hBAC<sup>WT</sup> double transgenics.</p>
<p>The generation and characterization of <em>Eln</em><sup>+/−</sup>mice has been previously reported (<a href="#B2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B2">2</a>). The genotype at the mouse <em>Eln</em> locus was assessed by PCR analysis using specific primer sets: primer A (DL1), 5′-GGTTGTTCAGACTACAATCTGACC-3′; primer B (DL2), 5′-CAACTTTGCCCAAATGACTCTCC-3′; primer C (DL5), 5′-GAGAGGTATAGAGGGAAGACTTGCT-3′. Used together, primers B and C are specific to the wild-type mouse <em>Eln</em> locus and yields a 477-bp fragment, whereas primer A is specific to the targeted mouse <em>Eln</em> locus and yields a 250-bp fragment when used with primer B (<a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">22</a>). hBAC<sup>CL</sup>- hBAC<sup>WT</sup> double transgenic mice were genotyped by direct sequencing using the primer 5′-ATCTTGAGCAAAGACGTGCCA-3′ after PCR amplification (see above).</p></section><section id="sec7"><h5 class="pmc_sec_title">Southern Analysis, RNA Isolation, and Assessment of Alternative Splicing</h5>
<p>To characterize the transgene by Southern analysis, 10 mg of genomic DNA was digested with SspI and subjected to electrophoresis in agarose and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes according to standard procedures. Radioactive probes were produced using a random priming kit obtained from Amersham Biosciences, and the blots were hybridized with <sup>32</sup>P-labeled 340-bp human elastin cDNA probe produced by PCR as described (<a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">22</a>). Total RNA was isolated from aorta and lung using a TRIzol reagent kit (Invitrogen). The expression of human tropoelastin mRNA was confirmed by RT-PCR using primers specific for human elastin. Total RNA was reverse-transcribed using a 20-mer oligo(dT) primer and Superscript III reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen). PCR conditions were 95 °C for 5 min, 95 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min for 30 cycles.</p>
<p>Splice variants were assessed by semiquantitative RT-PCR (21 cycles) with human- or mouse-specific primers located in exon 21, 25, and 36. Amplification of mouse β-actin provided an internal control (<a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">22</a>). Primers were: human exon 21 forward, 5′-TGCCGCCAAGGCTGCCAAGTA-3′; human exon 25 reverse, 5′-TGGTGGAGTTGCAGCTTGTGCAGCAAAAT-3′; human exon 25 forward, 5′-ATTTTGCTGCACAAGCTGCAACTCCACCA-3′; human exon 36 reverse, 5′-TTCCGGCCACAAGCTTTCCCCAG-3′.</p></section><section id="sec8"><h5 class="pmc_sec_title">Analysis of Exon 32 Splicing Using Real-time Quantitative PCR</h5>
<p>Six-week old mice were euthanized with carbon dioxide, and tissues were harvested then immediately frozen for later processing. A square centimeter of skin was shaved then excised, and the lungs were removed by simple dissection. The aorta, from the heart to the diaphragm, was removed, and loose connective tissue and fat were removed by careful dissection. RNA was isolated from the tissue using the Neucleospin RNA-XS kit (Macherey-Nagel Inc.). cDNA was reverse-transcribed from 500 ng mRNA using the Superscript II first-strand synthesis system (Invitrogen) with oligo(dT)12–18 primers according to manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative PC was performed on 10 ng of transcribed mRNA using an ABI Vii7 real-time PCR system and a common forward primer located in exon 28 of human elastin, a common reverse primer located in exon 36, and splice site-specific TaqMan probes custom-synthesized by Applied Biosystems Incorporated. The sequences of the primers and probes were: huELN 28F, AGTAGGCATCCCAGGCGGTGT; huELN36R, TCTCCACCAAGCAGTAGCACCAAC; huELN, 31-32 6-carboxyfluorescein (6Fam)-AAGCAGCTAAATACGGTGCTGCTGG-MGB-NFQ; huELN, 31-33 6Fam-AGCAGCTAAATA↓CGGAGTGGCAGCA MGB-NFQ (minor groove binder, nonfluorescent quencher) (the ↓ indicates the position of the splice junction). The primer-probe sets are human-specific and show no amplification from wild-type mouse tissues (data not shown). TaqMan fast universal 2× PCR master mix (without UNG) from Applied Biosytems was used for the reactions with standard PCR conditions. Results of the amplification were normalized to cyclophilin-A, accession number <a href="https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nucleotide/NM_008907.1" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NM_008907.1</a> (ABI, PPIA-Fam Mm02342430_g1), and -fold change between samples was calculated using the ΔΔCT method (<a href="#B26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B26">26</a>).</p></section><section id="sec9"><h5 class="pmc_sec_title">Immunohistochemistry and Electron Microscopic Analyses</h5>
<p>Frozen sections of ascending mouse aorta were stained with antibodies specific for mouse (antibody MRT6–17) or human (antibody HAE) elastin followed by detection with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies. To reduce background autofluorescence from preexisting elastic fibers, sections were incubated with 0.5% pontamine sky blue in PBS for 5 min followed by washing with PBS before viewing (<a href="#B27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B27">27</a>). A Zeiss Axioscope microscope was used for fluorescence microscopy, and the images were captured with an Axiocam digital camera using Axiovision software.</p>
<p>For electron microscopy, specimens were fixed in 3% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 <span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4), cut into small pieces, washed in buffer, and post-fixed sequentially en bloc in 1% osmium tetroxide, 2% tannic acid, and 2% uranyl acetate (<a href="#B28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B28">28</a>). After dehydration through a graded series of methanol and infiltration with Epon, tissues were embedded in pure Epon and polymerized. Sixty-nm sections were counterstained with 7% methanolic uranyl acetate and lead citrate and viewed using a Tecnai 12 transmission electron microscope at 120 kV.</p></section><section id="sec10"><h5 class="pmc_sec_title">Vessel, Lung, and Skin Extensibility</h5>
<p>For vessel extensibility measurements, isolated vessels from 3-month-old mice were cannulated and mounted on a pressure arteriograph (Danish Myotechnology, Copenhagen, Denmark). Vessels were transilluminated under an inverted microscope connected to a charged-coupled device camera and computerized system, allowing a continuous recording of changes in vessel diameter. Experimental details have been previously described in detail (<a href="#B29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29">29</a>, <a href="#B30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B30">30</a>). Assessment of respiratory system compliance was conducted as described (<a href="#B31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B31">31</a>). For mouse skin elasticity measurements, animals were anesthetized with 1% inhaled isoflurane. The abdomen of each mouse was shaved with electric hair clippers, and Nair depilatory cream was applied to remove all remaining hair residue. The DermaLab suction cup apparatus (Cortex Technology) was then applied to the abdomen of each mouse using adhesive stickers. Pressure was applied in increasing increments to the small segment of skin under the suction cup causing the skin to be lifted into the apparatus. When the skin reached 2.5 mm, as detected by interference with a light beam emitted within the suction cup, the pressure was recorded, and the skin was released. This cycle is repeated four subsequent times.</p></section><section id="sec11"><h5 class="pmc_sec_title">Tissue Culture and Protein Synthesis Inhibition</h5>
<p>Cultured fibroblasts were obtained from skin explants of newborn mice. The cells were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's media supplemented with 100 units/ml penicillin, 100 μg/ml streptomycin, 2 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> glutamine, 0.1 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> nonessential amino acid, and 10% fetal bovine serum in a 5% CO<sub>2</sub> incubator at 37 °C. To assess RNA stability, cells were treated with the translation inhibitors puromycin (100 μg/ml) or anisomycin (100 μg/ml) for 4 h before harvesting RNA. RNA levels were determined by semiquantitative PCR as described above.</p></section><section id="sec12"><h5 class="pmc_sec_title">Tissue Extraction, Western Blot Analysis, and Desmosine Analysis</h5>
<p>The aorta was dissected from 2-week-old mice and immediately homogenized in 1 ml of radioimmune precipitation assay buffer (50 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 150 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">m</span> NaCl, 1% Nonidet P-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS). The sample was centrifuged, and total protein concentration of the supernatant was determined using the BCA assay (Pierce). Three μg of total protein were loaded onto a 7.5% polyacrylamide gel. After electrophoresis, the gel was electroblotted to a nitrocellulose membrane followed by incubation with 5% nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween 20 to block antibody nonspecific binding. The blot was incubated with HAE antibody or an antibody to mouse GAPDH (CEA-335, Stressgen) for 1 h at room temperature and then, after washing, with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG or anti-mouse IgG. Reactive bands were detected with ECL detection reagents (GE Healthcare).</p>
<p>Desmosine evaluation in hydrolyzates of aortic tissue was kindly provided by Dr. Barry Starcher, University of Texas Health Science Center, Tyler, TX. Values were normalized to total protein determined by amino acid analysis as described (<a href="#B32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B32">32</a>).</p></section></section></section></section><section id="sec13"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">RESULTS</h2>

<section id="sec16"><h5 class="pmc_sec_title">Nomenclature of Mouse Lines</h5>
<p>In this report mWT refers to a non-transgenic mouse with the wild-type murine elastin gene (<em>Eln</em><sup>+/+</sup>). mHet is a mouse with one functional elastin allele (<em>Eln</em><sup>+/−</sup>). hBAC<sup>WT</sup> refers to the wild-type human elastin gene (<em>ELN</em>) expressed from a BAC (<a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">22</a>). hBAC<sup>CL</sup> refers to the human elastin BAC containing the 2012ΔG cutis laxa mutation. Compound genotypes are indicated by the presence or absence of each allele. For example, hBAC<sup>WT</sup>-mWT represents the hBAC<sup>WT</sup> transgene in the mWT background (<em>ELN</em>+; <em>Eln</em><sup>+/+</sup>), hBAC<sup>WT</sup>-mHet indicates the human wild-type transgene in the mouse heterozygous background (<em>ELN</em>+; <em>Eln</em><sup>+/−</sup>), and hBAC<sup>WT</sup>-mNull is the human wild-type transgene in the null background (<em>ELN</em><sup>+</sup>; <em>Eln</em><sup>−/−</sup>). A similar nomenclature is used for the hBAC<sup>CL</sup> background.</p></section><section id="sec17"><h5 class="pmc_sec_title">Mutation of BAC Human Elastin Gene</h5>
<p>The two-step recombineering procedure used for BAC targeting provided for the introduction of a common ADCL mutation (a single base deletion at position 2012 (2012ΔG) of exon 30 (<a href="#B16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B16">16</a>)) into the human elastin gene without leaving behind a drug-selectable marker at the targeted locus (<a href="#B33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B33">33</a>, <a href="#B34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B34">34</a>) (<a href="http://www.jbc.org/cgi/content/full/M111.327940/DC1" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">supplemental Fig. 1</a>). Targeting this region of the gene using homologous recombination was extremely difficult, most likely due to the enrichment of Alu sequences and repetitive elements in the surround introns (<a href="#B35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B35">35</a>). Correct insertion was confirmed by restriction digestion of hBAC DNA with Bgl1 followed by gel electrophoresis and ethidium bromide staining. <a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a><em>A</em> shows a ladder pattern of fragments in the Bgl1 digest as well as a 3.9-kb fragment that corresponds to elastin base pairs 30115–33981 (exons 28–30, GenBank<sup>TM</sup> accession number <a href="https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nucleotide/AC005056" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">AC005056</a>). This band shifted in size to 7.5 kb in intermediate clones after kanamycin selection, which is the expected size of the fragment containing the <em>sacB-Kan</em> selection cassette. After the second recombination step, the size of the band returned to 3.9 kb, confirming removal of the <em>sacB-Kan</em> cassette. Southern blot analysis of the Bgl1 digests with probes located outside (<a href="#F1" class="usa-link">Fig. 1</a><em>B</em>) and inside (data not shown) of homologous recombination arms confirmed the correct size of the constructs. Moreover, PCR amplification of the final product followed by direct sequencing showed that nucleotide 2012G was deleted in the hBAC<sup>CL</sup> but not hBAC<sup>WT</sup> clone (<a href="http://www.jbc.org/cgi/content/full/M111.327940/DC1" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">supplemental Fig. 2</a>). Together, these data confirm that a human elastin gene was generated that contains the 2012ΔG cutis laxa mutation.</p>

<p>Reduced levels of full-length mutant (hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-ex32<em>in</em>) transcript compared with the full-length transcript from the WT human gene (hBAC<sup>WT</sup>-ex32<em>in</em>) or from the CL transcript with exon 32 spliced out (hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-ex32<em>out</em>) were also found in skin fibroblasts from hBAC<sup>WT</sup> and hBAC<sup>CL</sup> animals using semiquantitative RT-PCR (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a><em>A</em>). It is unlikely that this disparity results from differing levels of gene transcription, as the hBAC<sup>WT</sup> and hBAC<sup>CL</sup> transgenes are identical except for the single base deletion in the hBAC<sup>CL</sup>. Although insertional effects or variation in copy number could be a reason for the differences, analysis of splice variant abundance offers another explanation. <a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a> shows that transcript levels with exon32<em>out</em> from both hBAC<sup>WT</sup> and hBAC<sup>CL</sup> are similar for both alleles, confirming approximately equal expression of both transgenes. However, the amount of transcript with exon32<em>in</em> from the hBAC<sup>CL</sup> (a premature stop in the new reading frame) is considerably less (∼80%) than exon32<em>in</em> from the hBAC<sup>WT</sup>. The lower transcript levels suggest that mRNA from the mutant allele in both lung and skin is less stable when exon 32 is present. The existence of a premature termination codon in exon 32 of the mutant transcript makes it a candidate for degradation through nonsense-mediated decay (NMD), a RNA surveillance pathway that degrades transcripts with premature termination codons (<a href="#B36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B36">36</a>). To determine whether NMD was a factor in reducing hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-ex32<em>in</em> mRNA stability, skin fibroblasts from the hBAC<sup>WT</sup> and hBAC<sup>CL</sup> animals were treated with the translation inhibitors puromycin or anisomycin. Because NMD is a translation-dependent process, these drugs can be used to increase the abundance of transcripts that otherwise would be degraded (<a href="#B36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B36">36</a>). After treatment with either reagent, there were increased levels of the mutant full-length transcript (hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-ex32<em>in</em>) with little change in the full-length transcript from the hBAC<sup>WT</sup> (<a href="#F4" class="usa-link">Fig. 4</a><em>B</em>) or in levels of the mutant transcript with exon 32 spliced out (hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-ex32<em>out</em>). This finding confirms that the hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-ex32<em>in</em> transcript with a premature stop codon in exon 32, but not the hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-ex32<em>ou</em>t transcript with the longer missense sequence, undergoes substantial, but not complete, degradation by NMD.</p>

<p>In a previous study we showed that human elastin from the hBAC<sup>WT</sup> transgene can rescue the perinatal lethality of the mouse null (<em>Eln</em><sup>−/−</sup>) phenotype (<a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">22</a>). Furthermore, the deleterious effect of the mutant protein when combined with WT elastin from the hBAC<sup>WT</sup> gene supports a dominant negative mechanism wherein the mutant protein interferes with normal elastic fiber assembly or function.</p>

<p>Electron microscopic examination of the aorta, lung, and skin in hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-mWT, mHet, and hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-mHet mice showed that elastic fibers did assemble in the correct location in all three tissues in all three genotypes (<a href="#F9" class="usa-link">Fig. 9</a>). In the mHet tissues, as expected due to the production of one-half of the normal amount of elastin, the elastic laminae in the aorta were thinner, and less elastin was observed among the microfibrils in the other tissues, which was especially evident in the skin. For the hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-mHet tissues, the elastic laminae in the aorta appear slightly “ragged” but were essentially normal. Elastic fibers in the skin, however, were more diffuse and less uniformly dense. In the skin and lung of these animals, the elastin within microfibrillar bundles was frequently distributed intermittently within the microfibrils, whereas the elastin in mWT, hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-mWT, and mHet animals formed a normal, continuous structure. Overall, the changes in hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-containing elastic fibers were subtle, consistent with the mild phenotype (described below) in lung and skin tissues.</p>

<p>Elastin loss of function mutations associated with supravalvular aortic stenosis led to marked phenotypes in aorta and lung, as characterized by vascular stenosis, pulmonary, and systemic hypertension, increased vessel stiffness, increased lung compliance, and in mice and perhaps humans, a propensity to develop lung disease when exposed to injurious stimuli (<a href="#B11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B11">11</a>, <a href="#B29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29">29</a>, <a href="#B37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B37">37</a>). Interestingly, these mutations do not result in dramatic skin phenotypes in either humans or mice. To determine how the ADCL exon 30 frameshift mutation influences elastin-rich organ systems, we assessed the mechanical function of vascular, lung, and skin tissue in hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-mWT mice. We also determined whether protein from the hBAC<sup>CL</sup> allele could reverse the adverse vascular effects of elastin haploinsufficiency by supplementing mouse elastin in the mHet background, as has been shown for elastin from the hBAC<sup>WT</sup> (<a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">22</a>).</p></section><section id="sec26"><h5 class="pmc_sec_title">Aorta</h5>
<p><a href="#F10" class="usa-link">Fig. 10</a> compares the mechanical properties of the carotid artery and ascending aorta in hBAC<sup>WT</sup> and hBAC<sup>CL</sup> animals in either the mWT or mHet background. mHet vessels have a smaller diameter at any given pressure and reach maximal distention at lower pressures than similar vessels in mWT animals (<a href="#B29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B29">29</a>). Consistent with our previous results, expression of human elastin in the mHet background (hBAC<sup>WT</sup>-mHet) was efficacious in restoring normal compliance to the vessel wall (<a href="#F10" class="usa-link">Fig. 10</a>). The presence of the hBAC<sup>CL</sup> allele, however, had no such effect, with the pressure-diameter curve for hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-mHet vessels being unchanged from mHet. There was also no adverse effect of the hBAC<sup>CL</sup> allele in the mWT background (<a href="#F10" class="usa-link">Fig. 10</a>). Similarly, vessel compliance of both the carotid and ascending aorta was unchanged when the hBAC<sup>CL</sup> allele was bred into the hBAC<sup>WT</sup>-mHet background, resulting in a hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-hBAC<sup>WT</sup>-mHet genotype. Together, the mechanical studies confirm that WT human and mouse elastin proteins can combine to form a functional fiber. The results also show that the hBAC<sup>CL</sup> protein has neither a positive nor negative effect on vessel compliance, which is consistent with the immunofluorescence findings in <a href="#F7" class="usa-link">Fig. 7</a> and unchanged desmosine levels in <a href="#F8" class="usa-link">Fig. 8</a> showing that only low levels of mutant protein are associated with elastic fibers in the vessel wall.</p>

<p>Mice with elastin haploinsufficiency (mHet) have more compliant lungs than WT animals (<a href="#B37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B37">37</a>). These results confirm that the ratio of mutant to WT protein is an important modifier of lung dysfunction.</p>

<p>Measurements of skin elasticity in mWT, mHet, hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-mHet, and hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-mWT animals were obtained using the DermaLab suction cup (<a href="#B38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B38">38</a>). <a href="#F11" class="usa-link">Fig. 11</a><em>C</em> shows that skin from animals with the hBAC<sup>CL</sup> allele is more lax than mWT animals, in agreement with the lax skin phenotype seen in humans with this mutation. After four conditioning cycles, approximately equivalent force was required to displace skin by 2 mm in mWT and mHet animals, whereas only about half as much force was required to displace skin from hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-mWT and hBAC<sup>CL</sup>-mHet animals. These results show that skin in the CL mutants has different physical properties than animals without the mutant allele even when compared with animals having half the normal elastin levels (mHet).</p></section></section></section></section><section id="sec29"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">DISCUSSION</h2>
<p>As a model organism, the mouse has been valuable for exploring the relationship between gene mutation and human disease. The ability to introduce a human gene mutation into the mouse ortholog using conventional gene targeting is a powerful tool for understanding disease causality. This approach is useful when the mouse and human genes are similar but may not be physiologically relevant if the genes are substantially different. Such is the case for the elastin gene, which has undergone considerable alteration in the primate lineage such that the human gene differs from the mouse gene in both structure and splicing of the gene transcript (<a href="#B23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B23">23</a>). Most human elastin gene mutations that are associated with dominant cutis laxa-type phenotypes are +1 or −1 frameshift mutations that adversely affect exons that encode critical assembly domains (<em>e.g.</em> exons 30 and 36) in the 3′ end of the gene. This 3′ region also undergoes extensive alternative splicing in humans where splicing influences the types and amounts of product that contribute to disease. Splicing out the exon containing the mutation or splicing out downstream exons can have the effect of silencing the mutation or modifying the nature of the RNA products when the mutant allele is transcribed. For example, the impact of transcript splicing on the mutant protein is illustrated by types of missense sequence produced by the Δ2012G mutation described in this study (<a href="#F3" class="usa-link">Fig. 3</a>). In the human gene, missense sequence downstream of the exon 30 mutation ends at a premature termination codon in exon 32. When exon 32 is deleted, which occurs &gt;50% of the time in human skin fibroblasts and in fibroblasts from the transgenic BAC mouse, missense sequence continues into the 3′-UTR until a new stop codon is encountered downstream of the normal translation termination site, resulting in a 25-amino acid extension. Transcripts from the mouse elastin gene, in contrast, show no splicing of these 3′ exons (<a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">22</a>) and, hence, cannot model the variability in missense sequence associated with the human mutations.</p>
<p>In a previous study we showed that the hBAC<sup>WT</sup> transgene in mice has a temporal and tissue expression pattern similar to the endogenous mouse elastin gene. The human transgene also retains the human splice pattern and does not alter splicing of the endogenous mouse gene (<a href="#B22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B22">22</a>). These characteristics are also true for the hBAC<sup>CL</sup> transgene, with the notable difference that transcript levels from hBAC<sup>CL</sup> are ∼50% lower than those from hBAC<sup>WT</sup>. This decrease was correlated with alternative mRNA splicing, with substantial, but not complete, degradation of the hBAC<sup>CL</sup>exon32<em>in</em> transcript through nonsense-mediated decay. It is interesting that nonsense-mediated decay did not degrade the hBAC<sup>CL</sup>exon32<em>out</em> transcript, indicating that this splicing product, with significant missense sequence, is stable and capable of producing mutant protein. How the two protein products of hBAC<sup>CL</sup>exon32<em>in</em> and exon32<em>out</em> combine to alter normal elastic fiber assembly is unknown. It is intriguing to speculate, however, that the variable severity and phenotypic heterogeneity of ADCL may be related to the amount of mutant exon32<em>in</em> transcript that survives NMD.</p>
<p>Functional studies comparing the mechanical properties of skin and lung establishes that the hBAC<sup>CL</sup> protein has a negative effect on mechanical function, providing direct evidence for a dominant-negative mechanism. Electron microscopic analysis of tissues from hBAC<sup>CL</sup> mice showed some suggestion of disorganized bundles of elastin in the lung and skin, but the differences from mWT were subtle. This suggests that the CL protein does not dramatically change the organization or packing of elastin (at least in ways that are obvious by electron microscopy) but changes the functional properties of the fiber as was evident from the tissue mechanical studies.</p>
<p>In contrast to lung and skin, the CL mutant protein incorporates inefficiently in aorta, consistent with the absence of a vascular phenotype in the hBAC<sup>CL</sup> mice and in ADCL individuals with the mutation modeled in this report. Similar results were obtained by Hu <em>et al.</em> (<a href="#B17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B17">17</a>) who expressed a cDNA for tropoelastin containing a 25-bp deletion (2114–2138 del) in exon 30 as a transgene in mice. Like the mutant tropoelastin described in our study, the exon 30(2114–2138 del) protein was found to incorporate into lung but not aorta.</p>
<p>The reason why elastin incorporation is different in the aorta is not known. Because the extent of exon 32 splicing is not substantially different between aorta and lung or skin, the loss of C- terminal “assembly sequences” is more consequential for assembly of elastic fibers in the aorta as compared with skin/lung, suggesting either different mechanisms or a more lax restriction on assembly in skin as compared with aorta. It is important to note, however, that alternative splicing of elastin gene transcripts is developmentally regulated (<a href="#B39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B39">39</a>–<a href="#B41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B41">41</a>) such that the splice pattern we observe in adult animals may be different from what occurs during development when elastin deposition is at its highest level. Developmental changes in the ratio of hBAC<sup>CL</sup>exon32<em>in</em> and exon32<em>out</em> splicing could impact the mutant protein isoforms and be an important modifier of disease pathogenesis.</p>
<p>The C-terminal region of tropoelastin that is altered in ADCL (exons 30–36 in this study) contains important sequences for fiber assembly, and it is easy to envision how modification of this region has negative consequences for proper fiber formation. <em>In vitro</em> studies and studies with transgenic mice showed that tropoelastin with altered or deleted sequences in this region is still capable of interacting with microfibrils to form an elastic fiber, although with abnormal cross-linking (<a href="#B7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B7">7</a>, <a href="#B42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B42">42</a>, <a href="#B43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B43">43</a>). How this region of tropoelastin facilitates fiber assembly is still unknown, although recent studies have identified binding sites for fibulin-4 and fibulin-5-, two proteins that facilitate elastin organization (<a href="#B44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B44">44</a>, <a href="#B45" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B45">45</a>). Inactivation of these two fibulins in mice results in abnormal elastic fiber formation, and particularly relevant to our study, mutations in the human genes for fibulin-4 and fibulin-5 have been linked to an autosomal recessive form of cutis laxa (ARCL Type I) (<a href="#B46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B46">46</a>–<a href="#B50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B50">50</a>). The fact that reduced fibulin-4 levels achieved through gene inactivation (<a href="#B51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B51">51</a>) or RNA knockdown (<a href="#B44" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="B44">44</a>) result in increased desmosine cross-links (similar to our findings in hBAC<sup>CL</sup> mice) suggests that the critical interaction disrupted by the CL-frameshift is likely between tropoelastin and fibulin-4.</p>
<p>In humans, the 2012ΔG mutation results in a phenotype that includes loose skin, redundant mitral and tricuspid valves, mild aortic dilation, and umbilical and inguinal hernias. Pulmonary traits include mild right ventricular hypertrophy and reduced expiratory flow indicative of upper airway obstruction. Although we did not study the heart or ligaments in the hBAC<sup>CL</sup> mice, the phenotypic traits that we did describe show significant overlap with the human phenotype. Thus, the hBAC<sup>CL</sup> mouse provides an excellent model to develop mechanistic insight into the human disease and to advance our understanding of elastic fiber assembly and function <em>in vivo</em>.</p></section><section id="ack1" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>
