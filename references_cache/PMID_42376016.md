# Senescence intensifies bleomycin-induced injury in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis lung epithelial cells.
**Authors:** Read J, Reid AT, Thomson C, Plit M, La QT, Mejia R, Saxena P, Knight DA, Lizé M, Prêle CM, Grainge CL, Stahl H, Schuliga M
**Journal:** ERJ Open Res (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1183/23120541.01212-2025](https://doi.org/10.1183/23120541.01212-2025)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a lethal lung disease involving injury and a dysregulated repair response of an ageing epithelium. Here we investigate the contribution of senescence and telomere shortening to bleomycin-induced injury in alveolar epithelial cells (EpCs) derived from lung parenchymal tissue of patients with pulmonary fibrosis (PF), predominantly IPF, in vitro.

METHODS: Injury responses of EpCs in air-liquid interface (ALI) culture were assessed by ELISA, qPCR and/or immunofluorescence at 48 and 96 h post-bleomycin (30 μg·mL-1) treatment. Telomere length was evaluated by qPCR and corroborated by fluorescence in situ hybridisation.

RESULTS: Bleomycin treatment increased markers of inflammation (i.e. interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-36γ) and senescence (i.e. p16 and p21) in EpCs from PF patients (PF EpCs) (p<0.05); responses markedly higher than control EpCs (Ctrl EpCs). Conversely, the effects of bleomycin on the expression of type I and type II alveolar EpC markers were supressed or downregulated in cultures of PF EpCs (when compared with Ctrl EpCs), whereas the percentage of keratin 17-positive type I and II intermediate EpCs were increased (p<0.05). Telomere length was also significantly shorter in PF than Ctrl EpCs at baseline and further reduced post-bleomycin treatment (p<0.05). Induction of telomere shortening by cell passaging augmented bleomycin-induced PF EpC injury, whereas pharmacological telomerase activation and selected senotherapeutics were protective.

CONCLUSION: This study provides evidence that senescence-associated telomere shortening increases the susceptibility of IPF lung EpCs to injury. Our cell-based lung epithelial injury model has utility as a pre-clinical tool for drug discovery and the evaluation of potential senotherapeutics for IPF treatment.

Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) occurs in interstitial lung diseases (ILDs), including the most common and devastating form, idiopathic PF (IPF) [1]. IPF pathogenesis is thought to originate from repeated unidentified microinjuries to lung parenchymal epithelial cells (EpCs) coupled with a dysregulated wound repair response involving aberrant epithelial–mesenchymal crosstalk and fibroblast activation [2, 3]. This predisposition for injury and disrepair is a result of a complex interplay between age, genetics and environment and is mediated by senescent EpCs [4].

Senescence is a cell fate induced by cellular stress and telomere shortening [4, 5]. At a molecular level, cellular senescence is initiated by double-stranded DNA breaks and a subsequent DNA damage response (DDR) involving p53–p21 signalling; processes that lead to cell cycle arrest and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [4, 6]. Type II alveolar EpCs with features of senescence, including shorter telomeres, accumulate in lung tissue of IPF patients [7–12]. Evidence for a causative role of senescence in IPF includes the linkage of mutations in telomere-related genes (TRGs) that accelerate telomere attrition [13]. Furthermore, the selective induction of senescence in type II alveolar EpCs evokes PF in recombinant mice [9]. In normal physiology, progenitor type II alveolar EpCs undergo cell division and/or differentiate into type I alveolar EpCs in the repair of a damaged epithelium. However, the diminished stemness of senescent type II alveolar EpCs hinders re-epithelialisation following injury [4, 9]. Indeed, keratin 17 (KRT17) expressing, senescent EpCs “stuck” in transition between an intermediate type I–II phenotype are detected in the fibrotic lung [14, 15].

Bleomycin-induced lung injury in mice remains the mainstay experimental model for PF investigations [16–18]. Indeed, bleomycin is a major cause of drug-induced ILD, which accounts for ∼3% of all ILDs [19, 20]. Bleomycin induces DNA breaks, which evokes a DDR and subsequent cellular senescence [21–23]. Intra-tracheal administration of bleomycin in mice induces damage to the alveolar capillary barrier, resulting in a period of acute inflammation and subsequent PF [21–23]. However, this model fails to recapitulate all features of human PF and has poor predictive value in the evaluation of potential therapies [24]. Considering these limitations, in vitro experimental models using PF patient-derived lung cells provide an important alternative tool for discovery research and are recommended to be used in conjunction with in vivo models in pre-clinical drug testing [18]. There have been limited primary cell culture studies with PF patient-derived lung EpCs investigating injury and repair responses to bleomycin; and even fewer with cells maintained in the physiologically relevant, air–liquid interface (ALI) system [25, 26].

Our group has previously shown that lung parenchymal EpCs from PF patients, when compared with controls exhibit features of senescence at ALI [27]. In this study, ALI cultures of EpCs comprising both type I and II alveolar EpCs and treated with bleomycin were used to explore the contribution of senescence and telomere shortening to epithelial injury in IPF.

Lung parenchymal tissue was obtained from ILD patients undergoing lung transplant at St Vincent's Hospital (Sydney, NSW, Australia) or patients undergoing thoracic surgery at the John Hunter Hospital (New Lambton Heights, NSW, Australia). The acquisition and use of tissue for research is under single or multicentre, ethical review approvals from the human research Ethics Committees of Hunter New England NSW Health (2019/ETH03848 and 2019/ETH00956) and the University of Newcastle (H-2016-0325), following guidelines from the National Health and Medical Research Council (Australia). All patients provided written, informed consent for their tissue to be utilised in medical research. Patients were classified as PF, based on diagnosis and/or histological evidence of fibrosis in lung biospecimens. Macroscopically normal lung tissue with no evidence of PF, mainly from patients with cancer undergoing thoracic surgery were used as non-PF controls. Patient demographic data are provided in supplementary table S1.

EpCs were isolated from lung parenchyma and seeded into T75 flasks (0.5 million cells per flask) (passage 0, or P0) in bronchial epithelial cell growth medium at 37 °C (5% CO2) as previously described [27, 28]. At ∼75% confluence, EpCs were collected and transferred to Transwell inserts of 12-well plates at passage 1 (P1) for ALI culture and experimentation (see supplement for details). In select experiments, P0 cells were sub-cultured for an additional 1–2 passages in T25 flasks before transfer to Transwells at P2 or P3 for experimentation.

At day 4 of ALI culture, EpCs were injured by addition of bleomycin (30 μg·mL−1) to the basal medium, which was replenished with fresh medium (containing no bleomycin) 24 h later. In selected experiments, senotherapeutics or dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) as vehicle control were added to the basal medium of cells 10 min before addition of bleomycin and re-added in fresh medium when changed. Pharmacological modulation of telomere length involved exposing cells to the telomerase activator, cycloastragenol (1 μM) during expansion in T25 flasks (0.25 million cells seeded per flask) and after transfer to Transwells at P2 or P3 for further growth before ALI culture and treatment with bleomycin. Cycloastragenol or DMSO vehicle were re-added each time the medium was replenished.

RNA and DNA purification, RNA reverse transcription and qPCR of nucleic acids for gene expression and relative telomere length (RTL) analysis along with relevant PCR primers are described in the supplement.

Immunofluorescence (IF) was conducted on sections of cells grown on Transwell inserts or formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded lung tissue (5-μm thick) from PF patients as described previously [27]. Further details including antibodies used for IF are provided in the supplement.

Telomeres in lung cell or tissue sections were visualised by fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) using telomere Cy3- or Alex488 G-rich telomere PNA probes (Eurogentech, #TG050-005 and TG060-005, respectively). See supplement for details of the hybridisation protocol and subsequent IF labelling and fluorescence microscopy.

Levels of interleukin (IL)-1α, IL-6 and IL-36γ in the basal medium (1.5 mL) were determined by ELISA using commercial kits (DuoSets, RnDSystems, MN, USA). Absorbances of ELISA 96-well plates were measured using the FLUOstar OPTIMA Multi-Detection Plate Reader (BMG Labtech).

Data are graphed as scatterplots with each point representing a separate patient. Comparisons between two groups were analysed by nonparametric Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank or Mann–Whitney U-tests (GraphPad Prism 9.0, GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA) as appropriate.

The effect of bleomycin-induced injury on EpC phenotype was also assessed (figure 1g and supplementary figure S1). The type I alveolar EpC genes, podoplanin (PDPN), aquaporin 5 (AQP5) and receptor for advanced glycation products (RAGE) were downregulated in PF EpCs, whereas they were either upregulated or little changed in Ctrl EpCs. Bleomycin treatment also increased the expression of the type II markers, ATP binding cassette subfamily A member 3 (ABCA3) and surfactant protein C (SFTPC) in Ctrl EpCs, whereas these responses were suppressed in PF EpCs (figure 1g and supplementary figures S1 and S3). Conversely, bleomycin induced an increase in the percentage of KRT17+ cells in cultures of PF EpCs (figure 1h,i). Lower power images of cross sections of ALI cultures show KRT17+ cells were sparsely present in cultures of Ctrl EpCs but more abundant and randomly distributed in epithelial layers of PF EpCs, which varied in thickness from 1 to 5 cells deep (supplementary figure S4). In lung tissue from PF patients, KRT17+ EpCs show a diminished telomere signal when compared with neighbouring KRT17− cells (figure 1j and supplementary figure S5). Similarly, in ALI cultures of PF EpCs, telomere labelling in KRT17+ cells appeared less intense than surrounding KRT17− cells (figure 1k). Finally, the gene expression of the mesenchymal marker, α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA or ACTA2) was also significantly increased in bleomycin-treated PF EpCs, more than controls (supplementary figure S1). Conversely, epithelial cadherin (E-cadherin), the epithelial marker downregulated in epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) was decreased (figure 1h and supplementary figure S6).

ALI cultures of lung parenchymal EpCs from PF patients (predominantly with IPF) were used to evaluate the contribution of senescence to epithelial injury processes in IPF. Markers of senescence (including SASP cytokines), EMT and KRT17+ cell numbers increased in bleomycin-treated cultures of PF EpCs, more so than controls. Conversely, bleomycin-induced changes in type I and II alveolar EpC gene expression were less in PF EpCs than controls. Telomere length was also significantly shorter in PF EpCs at baseline and further reduced post-bleomycin treatment. Telomere attrition induced by cell passaging augmented bleomycin-induced injuries, whereas pharmacological telomerase activation and senotherapeutics were protective. Collectively, these data show that senescence involving telomere abnormalities contributes to heightened injury responses of PF EpCs.

The fibrosis in IPF is heterogeneous, progressive and irreversible, unlike the homogenous, reversible response observed in experimental bleomycin mouse studies [1, 16–18]. Mouse models do not recapitulate the complex interactions between age, environment and genetics that underlie human PF. The use of PF patient-derived cells in a lung epithelial injury model obviates this issue, albeit with the caveat that cell culture comprises an artificial environment. However, the ALI system in which the apical surface of EpCs is exposed to air provides a physiological-relevant setting [29]. Using a previously established protocol, our ALI cultures of lung parenchymal EpCs from both PF patients and controls comprise ∼40% type II alveolar EpCs, with the remaining cells being predominantly type Is or an intermediate type I-II KRT17+ phenotype [27]. In this study, PF EpCs with higher baseline senescence mounted an intensified inflammatory response to bleomycin. The protective effects of senomorphic drugs which target pathways downstream of senescence further suggest a contribution of senescence to this exacerbated inflammatory response. Indeed, the NF-κB inhibitor, iguratimod attenuated bleomycin-induced increases in all cytokines examined. The transcription factor NF-κB is activated downstream of the ATM kinase-driven DDR and is the central mediator of the SASP [4]. Particularly large increases of IL-6 and IL-36γ were detected in the basal lateral compartment of injured PF EpCs. IL-6 has an established role in PF, regulating transforming growth factor (TGF)-β signalling [9, 30–32]. While little is known about the fibrogenic actions of IL-36γ, its detection in senescent type II alveolar EpCs in fibrotic lung provides additional evidence of a role in PF. Increased IL-36γ production is also part of the heightened injury response of virus-infected PF EpCs in vitro [27].

Bleomycin treatment escalated PF EpC senescence, as shown by further increases in p21, p16 and H2AXγ and reductions in telomere length. mTORC1 inhibition attenuated bleomycin-induced p21 expression, albeit with marginal effect on telomere length. mTORC1 is activated downstream of the DDR, which is elicited by bleomycin, contributing to senescence by stimulating anabolism, regulating mitochondrial homeostasis and by inhibiting autophagy [4]. Asides from PF EpCs, mTORC1 inhibition slows the escalation of senescence in lung fibroblasts from IPF patients in vitro [33]. In this study, bleomycin treatment also increased the percentage of KRT17+ cells in PF EpC cultures. KRT17+ cells in lung tissue and isolated EpCs from PF patients had shorter telomeres, providing additional evidence that senescent KRT17+ EpCs contribute to PF. Their accumulation maybe a consequence of injuries that accelerate an otherwise incomplete transition of senescent type II into type I alveolar EpCs. In support, bleomycin-induced changes in type I and II markers were diminished or downregulated in PF EpCs. Type II alveolar EpC proliferation and trans-differentiation into type I cells are crucial processes in alveolar epithelial wound repair. Senescence would perturb these phenotypic changes, increasing the susceptibility of the alveolar epithelium to injury and disrepair.

EMT, comprising the downregulation of epithelial cell characteristics and acquisition of a mesenchymal phenotype may also contribute to IPF [34]. Whether increases in α-SMA gene expression and decreases in E-cadherin in bleomycin-treated EpCs of PF patients signifies an early stage of EMT requires further investigation. Interestingly, iguratimod was the only senomorphic agent assessed that attenuated bleomycin-induced increases in α-SMA. NF-κB has an important regulatory role in EMT by activating transcription factors such as Snail, which promote and repress the expression of mesenchymal and epithelial markers respectively [34]. Increases in α-SMA are also associated with fibroblast senescence, including lung fibroblasts of IPF patients [35, 36].

Telomere shortening contributes to cellular senescence and is implicated in PF [13]. Telomeres, the noncoding TTAGGG tandem repeats at the end of chromosomes, protect and maintain chromosomal stability. These repeats are progressively lost with each mitotic division. When telomeres become critically short, a p53-dependent DDR is induced, leading to “replicative” senescence. Other causes of DNA damage including oxidative stress also contribute to telomere shortening. Certain IPF endotypes are caused by germline mutations in TRGs that affect telomere maintenance and stability with patients exhibiting prematurely shortened telomeres associated with a worse prognosis [13, 37–39]. Telomeres of type II alveolar EpCs in regions of dense fibrosis in IPF lung are shorter than other cell populations, or type II alveolar EpCs in macroscopically normal areas of lung [12, 40]. These observations, corroborated by our FISH analysis of lung sections from PF patients suggest that anomalous telomere shortening in PF occurs primarily in type II alveolar EpCs. In addition, baseline telomere length was shown to be shorter in ALI cultures of lung parenchymal PF than control EpCs. Furthermore, there was a negative correlation between baseline telomere length and bleomycin injury. Exposure to bleomycin accelerated telomerase erosion in both the control and PF cohorts after 48 h. Such effects of bleomycin, a genotoxic agent are likely to be manifested indirectly by the induction of chromosomal and telomeric DNA damage, which lead to genomic and telomere instability, particularly in cells that have existing telomere abnormalities [41]. A prolonged reduction in the telomere length of PF EpCs (i.e. after 96 h) occurred even after single-dose bleomycin, corresponding with increased detection of senescence, inflammatory and EMT markers. Furthermore, when replicative senescence was induced by cell passaging, baseline telomere length was further reduced, and injury responses post-bleomycin challenge were elevated. In alignment, recombinant mice with shortened telomeres in type II alveolar EpCs are more prone to bleomycin-induced lung injury than wild-type mice [42]. Overall, our in vitro observations are further evidence that telomere shortening contributes to PF EpC senescence and an increased susceptibility to injury [4, 27]. We primarily used the qPCR method to quantify RTL, an assay associated with higher inter-assay variability compared with other substantially more time-demanding and lower throughput approaches to measure telomere length (i.e. FISH) [43–45]. However, for key experiments, telomere FISH analysis was conducted in parallel, not only corroborating the quantitative qPCR data but also showing that telomere shortening is more prominent in KRT17+ EpCs.

The histopathological pattern of IPF is highly heterogenous, typically comprising regions of fibrosis interspersed with areas of preserved alveolar parenchyma. On a gross anatomical level, the fibrosis emanates from the base of the lung and gradually moves upwards, to ultimately encompass the whole organ [46]. With this in mind, we compared cultures of EpCs from IPF patients that were isolated separately from the base and apex of the right lung respectively. As expected, telomere lengths were shorter in PF patient-derived EpCs from the base when compared with cells from the apex, corresponding with intensified injury responses post-bleomycin treatment. In IPF, telomere length in EpCs is shown to be inversely related to the degree of fibrosis in the region they reside, suggesting that local, lung-specific factors asides from TRG mutations contribute to telomere abnormalities in PF [12, 47]. Our data corroborate these findings and provide experimental evidence that such telomere abnormalities contribute to heightened injury responses of alveolar EpCs in PF.

Telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC) make up the telomerase ribonucleoprotein complex responsible for telomere maintenance. Loss of function mutations in TERT and TERC genes are specifically linked to IPF [37]. Ongoing exposure of PF EpCs to cycloastragenol, a TERT activator increased cell number and telomere length during expansion and attenuated bleomycin-induced telomere erosion, and other injury responses in ALI culture. The protective effects of cycloastragenol in bleomycin-induced PF in mice involve the selective targeting of telomerase in replicating progenitor EpCs [48, 49]. Unlike somatic cells, progenitor stem cells, including type II alveolar EpCs express TERT, particularly in response to injury to maintain replicative capacity [50]. Interestingly, we found resveratrol also to be protective against bleomycin-induced telomere shortening and cytokine production. Resveratrol has diverse senomorphic properties involving multiple mechanisms including TERT activation [51]. The antioxidant properties of both cycloastragenol and resveratrol may also contribute to their telomere preserving effects [52].

Overall, this study provides evidence that senescence underlies heightened injury responses in lung parenchymal EpCs from PF patients and suggests that senotherapeutics, including telomerase activators, are a potential treatment option for IPF. Delineating the molecular mechanisms that underpin the increased susceptibility of the alveolar epithelium to injury and disrepair will provide immense insights into PF pathology and potentially identify new targets to either prevent or treat ILDs.
