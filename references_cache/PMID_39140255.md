# Perinatal Airway Management Mandibular Anomalies: A National Inpatient Cohort Analysis.
**Authors:** Puricelli MD, Barr SJ, Ellefson JL, Matabele MN, Nuttall EC, Garcia G, Huang SX, Venkatesh M, Lobeck IN
**Journal:** Laryngoscope (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/lary.31699](https://doi.org/10.1002/lary.31699)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: To characterize incidence of mandibular anomalies (MAs) and compare gestational age, airway interventions, and complications among individuals with MA phenotypes (isolated retrognathia, isolated micrognathia, syndromic micrognathia, micrognathia plus cleft palate/cleft lip and palate, agnathia/micrognathia plus cervical auricle/otocephaly, and agnathia/micrognathia plus microstomia) and unaffected individuals.

METHODS: The Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project Kids' Inpatient Database was used to collect data over a 20-year period beginning in 2000. Interventions were classified as perinatal when performed on day of life (DOL) 0 or 1 and subsequent when performed during the birth hospitalization after DOL 1. Hypoxic complications included cardiac arrest, birth asphyxia, hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, anoxic brain damage, intraventricular hemorrhage or cerebral infarction. Descriptive statistics are reported, and the Rao-Scott chi-square test compared groups.

RESULTS: MAs affected 119 per 100,000 birth visits. Preterm delivery was more frequent for all MA phenotypes. Individuals with MA phenotypes are more likely to require medical attention (airway intervention on DOL 0 or 1 OR no airway intervention received but patient sustained hypoxic complication/mortality): 16.2%-70.7% vs. 3.8%, p < 0.01. Despite receipt of airway interventions at a higher rate, collectively individuals with MAs who received an airway intervention on DOL 0 or 1 have a mildly elevated risk of hypoxic complication or mortality (32.4% vs. 26.4%, p < 0.01).

CONCLUSIONS: Preterm birth is more common, however, does not account for the elevated rate of airway intervention. Individuals with MAs require higher rates of medical attention, and current airway management paradigms are insufficient to prevent complications and mortality.

Currently, however, there are no population data on the incidence of mandibular anomalies, frequency of perinatal airway interventions, and the impact of intervention performance (or nonperformance) on the neonate. These are essential to understand the scope of mandibular anomalies and to ascertain if there is an opportunity to selectively escalate treatment to improve airway attributable complications, selectively de‐escalate treatment to mitigate maternal risk, or need for more ideal selection to accomplish both. Further, birth hospitalization duration, subsequent airway improvement procedure performance, and survival can support prognostication and inform families as they weigh difficult decisions regarding the risks and benefits of delivery modification. Due to the infrequency of mandibular anomalies, a large data source with consistent data points is necessary.

Therefore, this study aims to characterize epidemiology and compare gestational age at delivery, perinatal airway procedure performance, subsequent airway interventions, and complications among individuals with differentiable mandibular phenotypes and unaffected birth hospitalization encounters.

The Kids' Inpatient Database (KID) was developed by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) as part of the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP). It is a nationally representative sample of about 3 million pediatric discharges representing all payers. Each discharge record captures diagnosis and procedure codes for a given inpatient admission as defined by the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions (ICD‐9 and ICD‐10, respectively) as well as demographics, hospital characteristics, charges, and length of stay.

We conducted a retrospective cohort study utilizing the KID data from 2000 to 2019 and included all birth hospitalization encounters for live and stillbirths. Out‐of‐hospital births and transfers were excluded. A certified professional coder assisted with code selection and verified that the ICD‐9 to ICD‐10 transition for each diagnostic category was accurate. In some cases, not reportable (NR) was listed in accordance with the HCUP privacy policy. In other cases, data were listed in ranges to illustrate clinically relevant information, while respecting privacy policies. Sampling weights based on survey design allow for calculation of national estimates.
30
 Due to the de‐identified nature of the data in the KID, this study was exempt from institutional review board review.

Patients with birth hospitalization discharge diagnosis (BHDD) along the spectrum of mandibular anomaly were identified using ICD‐9/10 codes and stratified into six phenotypic groups: (1) retrognathia, (2) isolated micrognathia, (3) syndromic micrognathia, (4) micrognathia plus cleft palate or cleft lip and palate, (5) agnathia/micrognathia plus cervical auricle/otocephaly, and (6) agnathia/micrognathia plus microstomia (Supplementary Table I).

The primary outcome was incidence of mandibular anomalies. Additional outcomes included: distribution of gestational age at delivery; frequency, type, necessity, and sufficiency of interventions on day of life (DOL) 0 or 1; rate of airway intervention performed on DOL 0 or 1 according to gestational age; rate and type of complications; frequency of subsequent airway interventions; all patient refined diagnosis related group (APR‐DRG) risk mortality; and APR‐DRG risk severity.

Gestational age was defined according to ICD‐9/10 codes for less than 24 weeks, 24 to 25 weeks, and every 2 weeks from 25 to 37 weeks. These groups facilitated a smooth transition between ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 codes. The gestational age classifications extremely preterm (less than 28 weeks), very preterm (28–32 weeks), and moderate‐to‐late preterm (32–37 weeks) were also utilized.

The receipt of perinatal airway procedure was defined as airway intervention received on DOL 0 or 1 to account for variations in the time of birth. Airway intervention types included intubation, nasopharyngeal airway, bronchoscopy, tracheostomy, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), and cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Hypoxic complication (including cardiac arrest, birth asphyxia, hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy, anoxic brain damage, intraventricular hemorrhage or cerebral infarction) and mortality were used to infer necessity and sufficiency of medical intervention. The population requiring medical attention was defined as the total of those receiving any intervention plus individuals not receiving an intervention but receiving a discharge diagnosis of hypoxic complication or mortality on DOL 0 or 1. Sufficiency of perinatal airway interventions performed was examined by assessment of the frequency of individuals receiving airway interventions on DOL 0 or 1 who were diagnosed with a hypoxic complication or suffered mortality. To assess the relative impact of mandibular anomalies as compared to gestational age, the rate of airway intervention on DOL 0 or 1 for each gestational age group was examined.

In addition to preterm birth and hypoxic complication (as defined above) additional complications (nervous system complication during/following procedure, acute respiratory distress/failure, and failure to thrive) were also assessed for the entire birth hospitalization. Subsequent airway interventions (occurring during the birth hospitalization but after DOL 1) were captured including tongue–lip adhesion, mandibular reconstruction (including distraction osteogenesis), and tracheostomy. To ensure all relevant interventions were included, multiple investigators separately developed the datapoints and integrated findings.

The risk of mortality and severity of illness were measured by the APR‐DRG, which defines and measures a hospital's case mix complexity while accounting for severity of illness. The APR‐DRG risk mortality class descriptions are: class 1 minor likelihood of dying, class 2 moderate likelihood of dying, class 3 major likelihood of dying, and class 4 extreme likelihood of dying. The APR‐DRG risk severity is defined as the extent of organ system loss of function or physiologic decompensation and is categorized as class 1 minor loss of function (includes cases with no comorbidity or complications), class 2 moderate loss of function, class 3 major loss of function, and class 4 extreme loss of function.

Multiple covariates were collected including sex, race, hospital location and setting, region, length of stay, hospital charges, and disposition.

The incidence of mandibular anomalies over the study period was calculated. Sociodemographic characteristics, hospital characteristics, length of stay, median total charges and outcomes were reported for unaffected patients and each type of mandibular anomaly. The percentage of patients receiving a perinatal airway intervention and type of intervention performed was calculated for each mandibular anomaly and unaffected group. The Rao‐Scott chi‐square test was utilized to compare the frequencies between affected and unaffected individuals.

In aggregate, 26,975,562 weighted birth hospitalization discharges were analyzed between 2000 and 2019. Among those, there were 32,185 patients with BHDD of mandibular anomaly. The mean weighted incidence was 119 per 100,000 birth visits (Fig. 1). When compared to unaffected individuals, those with BHDD of mandibular anomaly were more frequently male (59.5% vs. 51.1%, p < 0.01), white (56% vs. 52.8%, p < 0.01) and delivered at urban teaching hospitals (61.1% vs. 51.5%, p < 0.01). The southern region was the most frequent area to encounter individuals with this diagnosis; however, this region also had the most unaffected deliveries. The median length of stay was longer (2.4 vs. 1.6 days, p < 0.01), and the median total charges were greater ($6,066 vs. $2,561, p < 0.01). Survival occurred in 97.8% of neonates with mandibular anomalies as compared to 99.7% of unaffected individuals (p < 0.01), and the majority discharged to home (Table I).

Our analysis identified 32,185 weighted individuals with a BHDD of mandibular anomaly and yielded a mean weighted incidence of 119 per 100,000 birth visits. This incidence is significantly higher than a prior nationwide database analysis of micrognathia from 2000 to 2012 that reported 999 cases out of 19,638,453 (5 per 100,000 births).
31
 Notably, the prior analysis included only live born infants with a single diagnostic code for micrognathia, ICD‐9 code 52404. Patients were segregated into micrognathia with and without cleft palate; however, patients with cleft lip and palate were not utilized in their analysis.
31
 Perinatal airway interventions were not examined.

The present study aimed specifically to differentiate presentations and predictive data. The coding algorithm was designed in collaboration with a certified professional coder to capture the clinical spectrum of mandibular anomalies and accompanying phenotypic features that might be identifiable prenatally. Accordingly, we broadened the coded definition for micrognathia and added retrognathia, syndromic micrognathia, micrognathia plus cleft palate or cleft lip and palate, agnathia/micrognathia plus cervical auricle/otocephaly, and agnathia/micrognathia plus microstomia. After reviewing the data, we advocate for a broader definition of congenital mandibular anomalies given the high rate of perinatal airway interventions and hypoxic complications demonstrated across the BHDD of mandibular anomaly phenotype spectrum.

Preterm delivery was more frequent in each group of individuals with BHDD of mandibular anomaly. The potential for preterm delivery is recognized among patients with potential perinatal airway obstruction, including micrognathia, and is recommended to be accounted for in prenatal planning.
1
, 
3
, 
26
 This study is the first to report the relative frequency of preterm delivery in a large population with mandibular anomalies. Further, the data show variable risk of preterm delivery according to phenotype. Individuals with mandibular anomalies and accompanying features such as cleft palate, cervical auricle/otocephaly, and microstomia have the highest rate of preterm delivery. The KID data does not include linkage to maternal records, and presentation for scheduled delivery dates and presentation in labor were not differentiated. Future investigations should integrate fetal and maternal factors that predict preterm delivery such as presence of polyhydramnios.
3



Airway interventions on DOL 0 or 1 occurred more frequently in individuals with BHDD of mandibular anomaly as compared to unaffected individuals. Prior expert opinion statements recommended availability of resuscitation, video laryngoscopy, operative laryngoscopy, videotelescopic intubation, rigid ventilating bronchoscopy, transnasal fiberoptic intubation, fiberoptic intubation via laryngeal mask airway, and tracheostomy for patients receiving delivery modification (attended delivery or delivery with sustained placental support).
3
 These data support that recommendation by showing that the most frequently utilized interventions included intubation, tracheostomy, and laryngoscopy or bronchoscopy for all groups regardless of phenotypic subgroup. Furthermore, ECMO use was infrequent, occurring in <0.01% of individuals with mandibular anomalies and was restricted to the group with syndromic micrognathia. Recent reports particularly emphasize the utility of technology to support visualization and airway procedures.
32
, 
33
 Advanced techniques should be incorporated where necessary and possible.

Among those with BHDD of mandibular anomaly, differential risk for airway intervention was noted among the phenotypic groups. This ranking is consistent with prior literature reporting high rates of surgical airway for individuals with agnathia spectrum and recommending consideration of delivery with sustained placental support to provide sufficient time to secure an airway.

Therefore, it cannot provide a rate of positive or negative outcomes for all pregnancies with the most severe mandibular anomalies. The data did, however, show that most individuals with pregnancies that progressed to delivery yielded surviving neonates who discharged to home with or without home healthcare. This observation is consistent with prior series and expert opinion that identified cleft palate or cleft lip and palate as a potential risk factor for treatment escalation along with preterm delivery, intrauterine growth restriction, neurologic impairment, positive family history, and aerodigestive tract obstruction including polyhydramnios.

The discrepancy may be dilutional, as the ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 code encompassing micrognathia‐glossoptosis (including Pierre Robin sequence), mandibulofacial dysostosis (Treacher Collins), and oculo‐auriculo‐vertebral (Goldenhar) syndrome also includes other syndromes less likely to impact the airway, while retrospective case series can select a more specific subset of higher risk syndromes.

Currently, the highest level recommendations for delivery modification are based upon expert opinion.
3
 A recent North American Fetal Therapy Network survey showed that otolaryngologists provide highly influential input in determining perinatal airway management in micrognathia.
29
 Identification of additional prenatal differentiating features is valuable given the limited data and conflicting results about the use of imaging parameters. Prior studies show 7 patients with jaw index less than the 5th percentile and polyhydramnios received 4 tracheostomies, one difficult intubation, one easy intubation and one death (otolaryngology was not involved in the case of mortality).

,

 Finally, 10 patients with micrognathia (criteria not defined) and polyhydramnios reported no deaths or cases of hypoxic encephalopathy without EXIT procedures, however, the odds ratio for intervention within 24 h was 3.71.
26
 In this series, among individuals with BHDD of mandibular anomaly who received an airway intervention on DOL 0 or 1, up to 40.5% suffered complications and as high as 45%–55% died. This suggests that performance of an airway intervention alone is insufficient in all cases and subspecialist providers, advanced techniques or delivery modification, such as attended delivery or EXIT, might be necessary in some situations.

Higher rates of airway intervention were noted among individuals born extremely preterm, very preterm, and moderate‐to‐late preterm in the unaffected and BHDD of mandibular anomaly groups. To control for the impact of gestational age on the rate of airway interventions, comparisons were made among the groups at each gestational age. More frequent airway intervention was present among individuals with BHDD of mandibular anomaly across gestational age. This suggests the higher rate of airway intervention is associated with the mandibular anomaly, rather than solely the elevated rate of preterm delivery.

Complications were segregated into two groups: hypoxic complications occurring without performance of an airway intervention on DOL 0 or 1 and all birth hospitalization complications. The most frequent complication was acute respiratory distress/failure. Not surprisingly, it segregated among the diagnostic groups according to the frequency of airway intervention on DOL 0 or 1. Cardiac arrest was noted at a high rate among individuals with agnathia/micrognathia plus microstomia, however, also was noted frequently among individuals diagnosed with retrognathia. Similarly, birth asphyxia was most common among individuals with agnathia/micrognathia plus microstomia; however, the next two most common categories were retrognathia and isolated micrognathia. This highlights the risk of all phenotypes and reinforces the importance of contingency planning and consideration for routine delivery at a more resourced facility when a diagnosis anywhere along the spectrum of mandibular anomalies is made. Prior studies show that the life expectancy, quality of life, and developmental progress of children with isolated Pierre Robin sequence, nonsyndromic Pierre Robin sequence, or a positive family history are favorable.
8
 Further, positive long‐term developmental outcomes have also been reported among individuals with isolated agnathia spectrum.
15
, 
27
 Importantly, among individuals not receiving an airway intervention, hypoxic complications were more frequent in the BHDD of mandibular anomaly group suggesting that higher rates of intervention might be necessary to achieve optimal outcomes for all patients.

Among individuals with BHDD of mandibular anomaly, additional procedures (beyond DOL 1) to improve airway safety were not uncommon. Tracheostomy is the most frequently performed, followed by mandibular reconstruction and less frequently tongue procedures. For individuals with retrognathia, isolated micrognathia, or syndromic micrognathia, the risk of needing an additional procedure is 1%–2%. Among individuals with associated cleft palate or cleft lip and palate or agnathia/micrognathia plus cervical auricle/otocephaly, the risk is approximately 10%. For people with agnathia/micrognathia plus microstomia the risk of a subsequent procedure is approximately 30%. For retrognathia and micrognathia phenotypes, mandibular reconstruction including mandibular distraction osteogenesis is most common. In contrast, for individuals with agnathia spectrum, tracheostomy is more frequent. Other interventions such as feeding tube placement might additionally be required during the birth hospitalization.

This study is subject to several limitations. As a retrospective database review, it is limited by the quality of data entry and datapoints available in the HCUP KID database. We are unable to assess specific case factors that potentially influence patient management and decision for airway intervention. While the 20‐year study period was necessary to obtain larger case counts for rare phenotypes, new technologies, improved techniques, and changes in practice patterns likely occurred during the study period. Precise timing after birth and severity parameters such as prenatal imaging findings as well as details around the perinatal period such as grade of laryngoscopy view, number of attempts, and ability to ventilate between attempts are lacking. We are also unable to link any patient to the pregnant person's chart, thus are unable to determine any comorbidities or diagnoses that might affect decision making such as polyhydramnios. Finally, information about delivery modification to support the airway interventions is lacking, so we cannot determine if any of these individuals were delivered via EXIT or had an attended delivery.

Nevertheless, this study provides the first systematic examination of the perinatal airway needs of individuals with a BHDD of mandibular anomaly and important refinements to the anticipated outcomes for pregnant individuals. Future directions include applying the methodology to other specific etiologies of potential perinatal airway obstruction and creation of a multi‐institutional prospective cohort to link detailed prenatal, perinatal, and postnatal elements to support personalized risk/benefit analysis and guide treatment recommendations.

Newborns with six mandibular anomaly phenotypes (isolated retrognathia, isolated micrognathia, syndromic micrognathia, micrognathia plus cleft palate or cleft lip and palate, agnathia/micrognathia plus cervical auricle/otocephaly, and agnathia/micrognathia plus microstomia) receive higher rates of perinatal airway intervention, yet current treatment paradigms are insufficient to prevent hypoxic complications for any group. Retrognathia and micrognathia phenotypes pose moderate risk. Individuals with agnathia spectrum are at high risk. Preterm birth is more common among individuals with mandibular anomalies; however, preterm birth alone does not account for the elevated rate of airway intervention. Current airway management paradigms fail to prevent hypoxic complications for any group. These data will support clinical counseling and demonstrate a potential opportunity to improve outcomes.
