# Restoration of lysosomal membrane integrity in cell models of Pompe disease depends on fatty acid synthase and its product palmitic acid.
**Authors:** Le Guillou E, Segaloni A, Gadault A, Oliveira Dias C, Henneman NF, Su M, Nemazanyy I, Hifdi N, Nivet-Antoine V, Laemmerhofer M, Hnia K, Caillaud C, Panasyuk G
**Journal:** Cell Mol Biol Lett (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s11658-026-00897-w](https://doi.org/10.1186/s11658-026-00897-w)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Lysosomal storage disorders (LSDs) are rare inherited diseases caused by mutations in genes encoding lysosomal proteins, leading to substrate accumulation and lysosomal dysfunction. Whether this build-up compromises lysosomal membrane integrity remains an open question. In this study, we aimed to investigate the presence of lysosomal membrane damage in LSD cells and to identify mechanisms that could reveal potential strategies for improvement of lysosomal function. METHODS: Primary skin fibroblasts from patients with LSDs were used to evaluate lysosomal integrity and function. Lysosomal immunopurification enabled lipidomic and proteomic profiling, complemented by viral transduction for targeted protein expression at lysosomal membranes. Pharmacologic and metabolite treatments were applied in loss-of-function and rescue analyses. Microscopy-based approaches were used to assess lysosomal activity, membrane damage, and integrity. As a complementary model, we employed conditional mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) lacking class 3 phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K-3), which exhibit lysosomal dysfunction and autophagy defects characteristic of LSDs. RESULTS: In Pompe disease, caused by mutations in the acid alpha-glucosidase gene, glycogen accumulation aggravates persistent lysosomal membrane damage. Moreover, using loss- and gain-of-function models of PI3K-3, we showed that its product, phosphoinositide-3-phosphate (PI3P), was essential for preserving lysosomal membrane integrity. Notably, Pompe patient fibroblasts exhibited chronic lysosomal damage, marked by increased galectin-3 (GAL-3) recruitment and reduced levels of lysosome-associated PI3K-3. Targeted restoration of PI3K-3 at lysosomal membranes rescued this defect and stabilized membrane integrity. Proteomic analyses following genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of PI3K-3 revealed a striking downregulation of fatty acid metabolism, including depletion of fatty acid synthase (FASN). We further demonstrated that PI3K-3 and FASN functionally interacted to maintain lysosomal integrity in Pompe cells. Supplementation with the FASN product palmitate reproduced the protective effect of PI3K-3 targeting, enhancing lysosomal proteolysis and restoring autophagic flux. Importantly, palmitate treatment also improved lysosomal function in fibroblasts from patients with Gaucher disease and type 2 neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis, extending the relevance of this mechanism across LSDs. CONCLUSIONS: These findings in cell models of LSDs uncover a link between PI3K-3, fatty acid metabolism, and lysosomal membrane integrity. They provide a basis for future in vivo validation of PI3K-3 activation and metabolic modulation as adjuvant treatments to improve lysosomal function in LSDs.

Lysosomes are ubiquitous acidic single membrane organelles that degrade macromolecules and function as nutrient sensing signaling hubs [1]. Their physiological importance is highlighted by over 60 reported lysosomal storage disorders (LSDs) that arise from inherited mutations in genes encoding lysosomal proteins [2]. While the frequency of LSDs ranges from 1:40,000 to 1:4,000,000 for individual diseases, collectively, they affect approximately 1 in 7700 live births [3]. Although a specific enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) is available in 11 LSDs, most of them remain incurable, often causing early death [2]. Thus, better understanding of lysosomal dysfunction is a key to uncovering therapeutic targets for treating multiple LSDs. Among LSDs, Pompe disease stands out as one of the most frequent (1:40,000) and best studied in terms of its clinical manifestations, for which ERT is available [4]. It is caused by loss-of-function mutations in the lysosomal enzyme acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA) gene, resulting in intracellular glycogen build-up. Glycogen accumulation in Pompe disease is mostly damaging in skeletal, cardiac, and diaphragm muscles owing to impaired lysosomal degradation by autophagy [5]. To this end, in severe infantile-onset Pompe disease, low GAA activity leads to rapid decline, causing cardiomyopathy, hypotonia, and respiratory failure, while in late-onset Pompe disease, patients experience progressive skeletal muscle atrophy and respiratory decline [6]. Left untreated, Pompe disease leads to severe morbidity and the premature death of patients. While ERT with recombinant GAA is often life-saving for infantile-onset Pompe disease, it has major limitations, such as incomplete glycogen clearance and immune response, ineffectiveness in the central nervous system, and reduced efficacy in advanced disease [7]. Altogether, there is a need to identify targets and treatments in Pompe disease that could improve lysosomal function and enhance ERT efficacy.

Notably, substrate accumulation in several LSDs has been associated with lysosomal membrane damage [8]. Such damage has been reported in skin fibroblasts from patients with Niemann–Pick A (NPA) disease [9, 10], and lysosomal membrane permeabilization was shown to promote cytosolic aggregates in neurons in a mouse model of neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis type 2 (CLN2) [11]. Increased sensitivity to lysosomal membrane damage in response to lysosomotropic compounds has also been observed in fibroblasts from patients with Niemann–Pick C and Gaucher disease [12, 13]. Collectively, these findings implicate lysosomal membrane damage as a feature of LSDs; however, it has not been assessed in Pompe disease or examined as a therapeutic target.

Here, we show that Pompe disease patient fibroblasts exhibit lysosomal membrane damage, which was worsened by acute substrate overload. This phenotype was accompanied by reduced lysosome-associated class 3 phosphoinositide-3-kinase (PI3K-3), a lipid kinase complex essential for lysosomal function [14], consistent with the report that PI3K-3 inactivation in mouse muscle results in glycogen accumulation and Pompe-like myopathy [15]. Mechanistically, targeting PI3K-3 to lysosomes improved their membrane integrity, while proteomic analysis of immunopurified lysosomes from PI3K-3-depleted cells showed downregulation of fatty acid metabolism enzymes, including fatty acid synthase (FASN). Functionally, FASN supports lysosomal membrane integrity, and supplementation with its product palmitate enhanced lysosomal activity and autophagic flux. Similarly, palmitate improved lysosomal readouts in Gaucher and CLN2 fibroblasts, suggesting that targeting fatty acid metabolism may be relevant across multiple LSD cell models.

To test the presence of lysosomal membrane damage in Pompe disease, we used the Galectin-3 (GAL-3) puncta assay in patient primary skin fibroblasts using immunofluorescence microscopy [19]. GAL-3 binds beta-galactoside residues and, given an extensive glycosylation of inner lysosomal membrane proteins, lysosomal membrane damage renders these binding sites accessible for its recruitment. Therefore, the presence of GAL-3 puncta on lysosomes is indicative of compromised lysosomal membrane integrity [19]. Lysosomal membrane damage was quantified by assessing the co-localization of GAL-3 with the lysosomal membrane marker LAMP2 and counting GAL-3-positive, LAMP2-positive puncta per cell (hereafter referred to as GAL-3+ LAMP2+ structures; Fig. 1A). Notably, compared with a cell line of a healthy control, fibroblasts of five Pompe disease patients showed a marked presence of GAL-3+ LAMP2+ structures in untreated basal growing conditions (Fig. 1B). Next, we asked whether Pompe patient cells are more sensitive to chemically induced lysosomal membrane damage. To test this, we used l-Leucyl-l-leucine-O-methyl ester (LLOMe) that induces membrane perforation predominantly in late-endosome and lysosome membranes [20]. Consistently, LLOMe treatment induced the appearance of GAL-3+ LAMP2+ structures in fibroblasts of control subjects (Fig. 1B). Similarly, their number in Pompe patient cells was further increased by LLOMe (Fig. 1B). Moreover, the number of GAL-3+ LAMP2+ structures in Pompe patient cells was higher compared with the controls, suggesting their increased sensitivity to chemically induced lysosomal membrane damage (Fig. 1B). At the population level, LLOMe increased the fraction of healthy control cells positive for GAL-3+ LAMP2+ structures (Supplementary Fig. S1A). In contrast, most Pompe patient cells were already positive at baseline, and LLOMe further increased the proportion of GAL-3+ LAMP2+ cells (Supplementary Fig. S1A). This was corroborated by quantifying the fraction of LAMP2+ lysosomes that were GAL-3+ under the same conditions (Supplementary Fig. S1B), confirming the conclusions in Fig. 1B. We used the number of GAL-3+ LAMP2+ structures per cell as the most appropriate metric of lysosomal membrane damage throughout the manuscript. To test whether glycogen accumulation exacerbates damage in Pompe fibroblasts, we induced glycogen synthesis by overexpressing human glycogen synthase 1 (GYS1) (Fig. 1C). Stable GYS1 overexpression in Pompe patient cells increased glycogen levels as shown by PAS staining (Fig. 1D), and concomitantly was accompanied by an increased number of GAL-3+ LAMP2+ structures (Fig. 1E). To identify the pathways implicated in persistent membrane damage in Pompe patient cells, we explored the involvement of class 3 PI3K (PI3K-3) for two main reasons. First, inactivation of PI3K-3 in muscle cells mimics Pompe disease phenotypes, such as glycogen accumulation, muscle damage, and weakness [15]. Second, in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs), PI3K-3 deletion leads to accumulation of dysfunctional lysosomes and blocks autophagic flux [15]. Importantly, these phenotypes are reminiscent of LSDs including manifestations in Pompe disease. Therefore, we asked if PI3K-3 inactivation in MEFs also induces lysosomal membrane damage as seen in Pompe patient cells. PI3K-3 functions as a complex of the catalytic subunit with the lipid kinase activity, vacuolar protein sorting 34 (Vps34), and its essential regulatory subunit, vacuolar protein sorting 15 (Vps15) protein [14]. In the PI3K-3 complex, depletion of Vps15 results in degradation of Vps34 and is accompanied by a decrease of intracellular PI3P, representing a model of PI3K-3 inactivation [15]. To acutely deplete Vps15, we transduced Vps15f/f MEFs with CRE-expressing adenoviral vectors. As expected, Vps15 depletion in MEFs resulted in a decrease of Vps34 protein and was accompanied by a progressive increase of lysosomal mass and autophagy block, witnessed by Lamp2 and p62 autophagy cargo protein accumulation, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1C). It was also accompanied by increased levels of Gal-3 protein starting from 5 days after expression of CRE recombinase (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Moreover, this genetic depletion of Vps15 induced Gal-3+ Lamp2+ structures, consistent with lysosomal membrane damage (Fig. 2A). This was accompanied by activation of lysosomal biogenesis, evidenced by upregulation of transcription factor EB (TFEB) target transcripts (Supplementary Fig. S1D), and confirmed by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) for selected targets (Supplementary Fig. S1E). Notably, this basal lysosomal membrane damage was further worsened by LLOMe treatment, suggesting enhanced sensitivity of Vps15-depleted cells and their inability to sustain chemically induced lysosomal membrane perforation (Fig. 2A). To test the involvement of lipid kinase activity of PI3K-3, we treated MEFs for 24 h with its specific inhibitor, Vps34-IN1 [21]. Mirroring findings of PI3K-3 genetic inactivation, Vps34-IN1 treatment increased Gal-3 protein levels, promoted appearance of Gal-3+ Lamp2+ structures, and rendered cells more sensitive to LLOMe-induced damage (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Fig. S1F). Next, we asked if protein levels of PI3K-3 subunits were modified in fibroblasts of patients with Pompe disease. Immunoblotting of total cell extracts revealed no significant change in VPS15 or VPS34 protein levels (Fig. 2C). In contrast, VPS15 proximity to lysosomes was reduced in Pompe patient fibroblasts, as indicated by fewer proximity ligation puncta between endogenous VPS15 and LAMP2 (Fig. 2D). Consistent with a sub-pool of PI3K-3 localized to the lysosome, immunofluorescence analyses showed partial Vps15 co-localization with LAMP2 in MEFs at steady state (Supplementary Fig. S2A). These suggest that, in Pompe cells, reduced lysosomal association of PI3K-3, while unchanged total protein levels, may reflect its re-localization to nonlysosomal compartments. Next, given decreased proximity of PI3K-3 to lysosomes in Pompe patient cells, we hypothesized that its targeting to the lysosomal membrane could improve lysosomal membrane integrity. We generated a construct expressing the fusion of VPS15 with lysosomal transmembrane protein TMEM192, hereinafter referred to as Lyso-VPS15 (Fig. 3A). Notably, to identify Lyso-VPS15-infected cells, the vector also expressed GFP. Lyso-VPS15 was detected in the lysosomal fraction purified from MEFs expressing TMEM192-3xHA protein (Lyso-tag) that allows for rapid immunopurification of the lysosome-enriched fraction with anti-HA antibodies (Fig. 3A, B) [17]. Lyso-VPS15 localization was further validated by proximity with lysosomal membrane protein Lamp1 (Supplementary Fig. S2B). The functionality of the Lyso-VPS15 construct was further backed by pull-down of Vps34 lipid kinase subunit with immunoprecipitated Lyso-VPS15 (Supplementary Fig. S2C). To gain mechanistic insight into pathways downstream of lysosomal PI3K-3, we performed proteomic analyses of immunopurified lysosomes from control MEFs and MEFs acutely depleted of Vps15. We identified 220 proteins significantly decreased and 172 increased in lysosomes from Vps15-depleted cells (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table S1). As expected, Vps15 was the top downregulated protein, further validating the proteomics findings (Fig. 4A). Pathway analyses of downregulated proteins using DAVID Gene Ontology (GO), KEGG, and Reactome pointed to interconnected processes of autophagy, endocytosis, endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT), protein transport, and lysosome function, in line with the well-known involvement of PI3K-3 in these processes [22, 23]. In this study, we identify a protective role for de novo fatty acid synthesis in maintaining lysosomal membrane integrity in patient fibroblasts from Pompe, Gaucher, and CLN2 diseases. Lysosomal membrane damage is increasingly implicated in LSD pathophysiology and may reinforce mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired autophagy in a feed-forward manner. Although the core drivers of Pompe disease, including GAA mutations and glycogen accumulation, are well established [33], how glycogen burden affects lysosomal membrane integrity is less clear. In GAA-mutant mice, Gal-3 levels are increased in skeletal muscle, track with autophagy defects, and are normalized by recombinant GAA enzyme replacement therapy, consistent with reversibility [34]. Along the same lines, Atg7/GAA double-knockout mice, which are impaired in glycogen delivery to lysosomes, show reduced Gal-3 levels [35]. These in vivo data align with our Pompe fibroblast findings, where GYS1 overexpression increased glycogen accumulation and exacerbated lysosomal membrane damage. Lysosomal injury has also been reported in other LSDs, as Niemann–Pick A fibroblasts with sphingomyelin accumulation exhibit GAL-3 puncta, reduced cathepsin B activity, lysosomal exocytosis, and defective autophagolysosome clearance [9, 10], and Niemann–Pick C and Gaucher fibroblasts show increased sensitivity to lysosomal damage [12, 13]. Together with our observations in Pompe, CLN2, and Gaucher fibroblasts, these studies support a model in which progressive substrate accumulation compromises lysosomal integrity once a threshold is exceeded, increasing cellular vulnerability to stress.

Our data identify PI3K-3 as a key determinant of lysosomal membrane integrity in Pompe cells, consistent with prior work linking PI3K-3 dysfunction to glycogen storage myopathies and autophagy failure. In mouse skeletal muscle, Vps15/PIK3R4 deletion causes an autophagic vacuolar myopathy (AVM)-like phenotype, whereas PI3K-3 overexpression in Danon patient myoblasts reduces glycogen accumulation [15]. In both Danon and Pompe patient muscles, PI3K-3 subunits are increased and mislocalized in association with disease progression and autophagy impairment [36]. Similarly, Pompe mouse models show elevated autophagy-related proteins, including PI3K-3 subunits, Atg7, p62, and LC3-II, with ERT partially normalizing these profiles [34]. In our Pompe fibroblasts, total PI3K-3 levels were not markedly altered, but its localization to the lysosomal membrane was reduced, supporting the idea that defective recruitment rather than reduced abundance limits PI3P production at lysosomes. This interpretation is reinforced by studies in neurons in which Vps34/PI3K-3 inhibition induces endolysosomal damage marked by increased Gal-3 and impaired LC3 turnover [37]. Together, these results suggest that impaired PI3K-3 association with lysosomes and the resulting disruption of the lysosomal PI3P pool may represent an underappreciated mechanism contributing to lysosomal vulnerability in LSDs. This concept fits with emerging models in which phosphoinositides coordinate lysosomal membrane repair by organizing endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT) recruitment, PI4P-dependent membrane tethering, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) contact sites that enable lipid transfer [38–40]. ESCRT-I supports repair and lysophagy after damage, while PI4K2A-driven PI4P production promotes phosphatidylserine and cholesterol transfer, processes required for survival during sterile injury and infection [39–42]. Phase separation has also been implicated in stabilizing damaged lysosomal membranes through ESCRT-dependent and independent mechanisms [43]. Future work should define how the lysosomal PI3P pool changes across disease stage and if restoring PI3K-3 lysosomal recruitment can improve membrane repair capacity through these distinct mechanisms in LSDs cells.

An unexpected finding of this study is that FASN activity is required to preserve lysosomal membrane integrity, both after chemically induced damage and in LSD patient cells. Although a role for de novo fatty acid synthesis in lysosomal membrane repair has not been examined in LSDs, prior work on exogenous fatty acids and autophagy provides insights. Oleic acid [44] and palmitic acid [45, 46] can promote autophagy, yet palmitate is also widely used to model lipotoxic “steatosis” and can inhibit autophagic flux despite increasing lysosomal activity, with outcomes that depend strongly on dose and exposure time [47]. Reported differences between saturated and unsaturated fatty acids further highlight this context dependence and likely reflect variation in cell type and the autophagy-inducing stimulus [45, 48, 49]. In our hands, exogenous palmitate improved lysosomal membrane integrity in LSD cells, raising the question of whether the source of fatty acids is functionally important. In autophagy settings, fatty acids mobilized from lipid droplets can activate autophagy [44, 50], whereas exogenous free fatty acids may require re-esterification to elicit similar effects [51]. Consistent with our observation that FASN activity supports lysosomal membrane repair, adipocyte-specific Fasn deficiency impairs autophagosome–lysosome fusion through reduced membrane phosphoinositides and is partially rescued by exogenous palmitate [31]. More broadly, lipid metabolism intersects with autophagy through incorporation of newly synthesized choline phospholipids into autophagosomal membranes [52], impaired autophagic flux upon inhibition of phosphatidic acid and phosphatidylinositol synthesis [53, 56], localization of PI synthase at autophagosome biogenesis sites [53], and the requirement of stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 for autophagy in flies and mammals [54, 55]. On the basis of these precedents and our data, we propose that reduced de novo lipogenesis (DNL)-derived palmitate flux could limit renewal of lysosomal membrane phospholipids and/or the availability of palmitoyl-CoA for lipidation reactions that support membrane association of key regulators, thereby sensitizing cells to lysosomal membrane permeabilization. Accordingly, restoring palmitate through supplementation or synthesis may replenish these pools and improve lysosomal membrane integrity. Mechanistically, palmitate could act by supporting phospholipid synthesis and thus membrane packing and resilience, by serving as a substrate for S-palmitoylation that tunes recruitment and retention of lysosome-associated proteins and assembly of repair or trafficking complexes, or by indirectly reshaping lysosomal phosphoinositide composition and organelle contact sites that coordinate effector recruitment during repair. Finally, our results in mouse and human fibroblasts point to a bidirectional relationship between PI3K-3 and FASN, with each required for the other’s proximity to lysosomal membranes. This parallels yeast work showing that Atg14 colocalization with Atg8-positive autophagosomes depends on local acyl-CoA production by the acyl-CoA synthetase Faa1, while Faa1 recruitment in turn requires Atg proteins, including Atg14, and Faa1 preferentially binds PI3P/PI4P-containing membranes [56]. Future studies should determine whether PI3K-3 and FASN interact directly or through intermediates, and whether lysosomal PI3P promotes FASN recruitment and local fatty acid production to facilitate membrane repair, potentially in coordination with ER contact sites and PI4P-dependent pathways.

From a translational standpoint, our early preclinical findings in LSD patient fibroblasts point to fatty acid metabolism as a potential adjunct to existing therapies. In Pompe disease, the clinical benefit of ERT with recombinant GAA often declines after approximately 5 years, with persistent muscle weakness, gait impairment, and skeletal deformities [4]. So far, several adjunct strategies have been explored to enhance ERT by limiting glycogen accumulation and mitigating secondary defects in mitochondria and autophagy. In a Pompe mouse model, pharmacologic inhibition of glycogen synthesis with the GYS1 inhibitor MZ-101 reduced muscle glycogen to a degree comparable to ERT, and the combination further improved glycogen clearance toward normalization [57]. Antioxidants, including N-acetylcysteine, idebenone, resveratrol, and edaravone, also potentiated recombinant GAA in cell and mouse models [58]. Dietary approaches have likewise been tested, with ERT combined with a ketogenic diet, a ketone precursor (1,3-butanediol), or antioxidant diets producing distinct benefits. In particular, a ketogenic diet plus ERT most effectively reduced quadriceps glycogen, whereas 1,3-butanediol plus ERT best improved autophagic blockade [59]. Notably, only the combinations of 1,3-butanediol or antioxidant diet with ERT normalized elevated Gal-3 in GAA-mutant muscle, supporting the idea that restoring lysosomal homeostasis improves ERT efficacy [59]. Despite these encouraging preclinical results, ketogenic regimens are difficult to implement clinically owing to formulation constraints, adherence, and potential weight loss. Although fatty acid supplementation has not been evaluated in Pompe disease, related work supports the broader principle that modulating lipid availability can improve LSD outcomes. In a Krabbe disease model (GALC deficiency), n-3 PUFA treatment delayed onset, reduced oxidative stress, increased resolvin D1, and elevated brain n-3 PUFA levels [60]. In Niemann–Pick A cells, lysosomal membrane integrity can be improved by ApoD, an extracellular lipid transporter that targets damaged lysosomes after uptake, reduces GAL-3 puncta, restores cathepsin B activity, improves autophagic flux, and lowers oxidative stress [10, 61]. Together with our results, these studies underscore a link between lysosomal lipid metabolism and membrane integrity. We therefore propose that restoring fatty acid pools, either by supplementation or by supporting endogenous synthesis, could complement ERT by mitigating lysosomal membrane damage in Pompe disease and potentially other LSDs. As our conclusions are based on fibroblast models, an essential next step is validation in muscle-relevant systems and in vivo, combining established lysosomal damage and function assays (Gal-3 recruitment, lysosomal activity/acidification readouts, and autophagic flux) with physiological endpoints of muscle health, including contractile performance, strength and endurance, and histopathological measures of muscle integrity, under defined lipid- and diet-based interventions.

Our findings identify lysosomal membrane integrity as a shared vulnerability in LSDs and link it mechanistically to PI3K-3 activity and fatty acid metabolism. Across different LSD patient fibroblast lines, palmitate restored lysosomal function, providing proof-of-principle that modulating lipid metabolism can counter lysosomal dysfunction. This work positions lipid-dependent membrane homeostasis as a determinant of LSD pathogenesis and supports metabolic interventions, potentially combined with ERT when available, as a strategy to improve the durability of lysosomal correction.
