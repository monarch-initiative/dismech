# Exposure to Mixtures of Metals and Neurodevelopmental Outcomes: A Multidisciplinary Review Using an Adverse Outcome Pathway Framework.
**Authors:** von Stackelberg K, Guzy E, Chu T, Claus Henn B
**Journal:** Risk Anal (2015)
**DOI:** [10.1111/risa.12425](https://doi.org/10.1111/risa.12425)

## Content

Current risk assessment guidance calls for an individual chemical-by-chemical approach that fails to capture potential interactive effects of exposure to environmental mixtures and genetic variability. We conducted a review of the literature on relationships between prenatal and early life exposure to mixtures of lead (Pb), arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), and manganese (Mn) with neurodevelopmental outcomes. We then used an adverse outcome pathway (AOP) framework to integrate lines of evidence from multiple disciplines based on evolving guidance developed by the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD). Toxicological evidence suggests a greater than additive effect of combined exposures to As-Pb-Cd and to Mn with any other metal, and several epidemiologic studies also suggest synergistic effects from binary combinations of Pb-As, Pb-Cd, and Pb-Mn. The exposure levels reported in these epidemiologic studies largely fall at the high-end (e.g., 95th percentile) of biomonitoring data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), suggesting a small but significant potential for high-end exposures. This review integrates multiple data sources using an AOP framework and provides an initial application of the OECD guidance in the context of potential neurodevelopmental toxicity of several metals, recognizing the evolving nature of regulatory interpretation and acceptance.

The regulatory science of risk assessment informs decision-making at scales ranging from site-specific remediation to broader policy development. Current risk assessment guidance dictates a largely chemical-by-chemical evaluation of exposures and risks, which fails to adequately address potential interactions with other chemicals, non-chemical stressors, and genetic influences reflecting true environmental exposures. As our understanding of complex exposures and interactions continues to grow, synthesis and integration across disciplines and individual studies focused on different aspects of the continuum from environmental fate – exposure – toxicology – health outcome are required to assess the likelihood of adverse effects occurring.

Environmental exposures are virtually always to complex mixtures, and while mathematically characterizing dose additivity is relatively straightforward, potential synergistic or antagonistic interactions present significant statistical challenges due to the large number of predictors and interactions, potential correlations, non-linear dose-response functions, and likely effect modification within the mixture( 1 ). Nonchemical stressors (e.g., nutritional and socioeconomic status) also impact potential responses to chemical mixtures, and genetic polymorphisms and epigenetic changes are likely to influence outcomes. Cumulative risk assessment methods and approaches are evolving to meet regulatory needs( 2- 4 ), but significant challenges remain.

The Superfund Research Program at the Harvard School of Public Health (www.srphsph.harvard.edu) explores these issues in the context of prenatal and perinatal exposures to mixtures of As, Pb, and Mn and developmental outcomes in children. As and Pb rank first and second, respectively, on the ATSDR 2011 substance priority list at National Priorities List (NPL) sites based on a combination of frequency, toxicity, and potential for human exposure at NPL sites, while Cd ranks 7th and Mn 140th. The association between developmental health outcomes in children and exposure individually to Pb( 5 ), Mn( 6 ), As( 7 ) and Cd( 8 ) is increasingly well-established. In general, the assessment of developmental outcomes is relatively non-specific (e.g., poor performance on IQ and other developmental tests) although epidemiologic evidence is growing for associations with specific cognitive outcomes such as autism and lead( 9 ) and Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) and both Mn( 10 ) and Pb( 11 , 12 ). Evidence for the potential impact of combined exposures across two or more metals is less well understood( 13- 15 ). In addition, the role that the genome might play in interacting with exposures to influence metal metabolism and uptake( 16- 21 ) to result in observed health outcomes( 12 , 20 , 21 ) provides additional support for gene-environment interactions contributing to observed variability and potential susceptibility in population responses.

The Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR) has developed “interaction profiles” for several chemicals including Pb, Cd, chromium (Cr), and As( 22 , 23 ) based on a binary weight-of-evidence approach( 24 , 25 ), leading to conclusions regarding the expected direction of interaction for specific health outcomes and the degree of confidence in these conclusions. That assessment found evidence for a greater than additive effect of combined exposures to Pb and As for developmental outcomes, and possible evidence for a greater than additive effect of combined exposures to Pb and Cd. An analysis for Cd and As was not presented. By definition, the binary weight-of-evidence approach only considers potential interactions between two constituents. There are no published studies that extend the analysis to combinations of three and four constituents simultaneously.

Given the potential for exposures to individual metals to result in adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, the purpose of our systematic review is to evaluate the evidence for potential exposures to concentrations of mixtures of metals in conjunction with genetic influences to result in neurodevelopmental health outcomes of greater or lesser magnitude than would be predicted from exposures to individual metals alone. Neurodevelopmental outcomes encompass a range of cognitive and behavioral deficits as evidenced by reduced performance on standard developmental tests specific to a particular age group with established psychometric properties that form the basis of most epidemiologic studies( 26 ). Similarly, there are analogs to developmental tests in humans for rodents that facilitate interpretation of toxicological studies. As shown in Figure 1, the review focuses on the intersection of domains of evidence ranging from outcome-focused epidemiologic studies to mechanistic-based toxicological studies, environmental exposures (environmental exposure concentrations relative to biologically-effective doses), and what is known about cellular events at the subclinical level in relation to neurodevelopmental health outcomes. We evaluate biological plausibility with respect to hypothesized modes-of-action based on the best available understanding of molecular events, cellular targets, and genomic interactions required for neurodevelopmental effects relevant to learning and cognition to occur in the context of normal neuronal development and function using an adverse outcome pathway (AOP) framework. The AOP is a conceptual framework as shown in Figure 2 for organizing and evaluating evidence for predictive and/or causal linkages between observable, and ideally measurable, biological changes starting with a molecular initiating event (MIE) through a series of cellular and organ-level events (referred to as key events; KEs, which are typically supported through evidence from toxicological studies) essential to the progression to an adverse outcome (AO) considered relevant to regulatory decision making. Finally, biomonitoring studies and data together with exposure levels from the epidemiologic and toxicological studies provide an indication of the implications to public health more broadly.

We conducted a literature search to develop a database of studies categorized by exposure, toxicology, epidemiology, gene-environment, and health outcome. The literature search relied on standard search engines including PubMed, Web of Science, Google Scholar, Environmental Index, Scopus, Toxline, and Toxnet. The search strategy included terms related to metal mixtures, individual metals (e.g., arsenic, lead, manganese, cadmium), neurodevelopmental health outcomes, and associated Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms. We reviewed references from individual citations to identify additional studies not captured through the literature search itself.

The Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (available at www.ctdbase.org) was used to identify 1) top interacting genes for each of the four metals, 2) genes in common to all four metals related to learning and cognitive disorders generally, including specific outcomes such as ADHD and autism, as well as non-specific outcomes; and, 3) all genes related to learning and cognition for each metal individually. Genes and pathways were cross-referenced and summarized based on information from the National Center for Biotechnology Information gene database (available at www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/).

To explore potential exposures, we extracted biomonitoring data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, available from http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes.htm) for the general population to compare to exposure levels from the epidemiologic studies for urinary and blood levels of As, Cd, and Pb using the cohorts as described in the epidemiologic studies (e.g., women 16-45, and so on) to explore exposure levels in the United States. Exposure data for Mn, not available through NHANES, were obtained from Rice et al.( 109 ) as described in the results section.

We first categorized each study for inclusion in separate databases (e.g., epidemiologic, toxicological, exposure, outcome-based). Only those studies focused primarily on developmental or neurotoxic endpoints were included; those focused on carcinogenesis or other systemic effects were not included unless there was a particular relevance to a neurotoxic or developmental outcome.

Each study was reviewed by one of the authors and relevant data extracted including study design and number in cohort (e.g., cross-sectional, prospective cohort, etc.), exposure measures and outcomes, tests for interactions, key conclusions, statistical approaches, and novel concepts.

Each study was reviewed by one of the authors and relevant data extracted including study type (e.g., in vivo, in vitro, etc.), study design (e.g., gestational, reproduction etc.), number of individuals, exposure levels, target tissues/organs and responses, dosing vehicle (e.g., gavage, oral), statistical tests, tests for interactions, and key conclusions.

An additional separate database was developed for common genetic targets identified from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database focusing on those genes with documented impacts on learning, development, cognition, and specific developmental outcomes such as ADHD and autism. The database includes information on genetic targets, gene functions (from the National Center for Biotechnology Information gene database), and reported impact of the individual chemical exposure (e.g., activation, inhibition).

We first evaluated toxicological studies using a modified version of the Toxicological data Reliability Assessment Tool (ToxRTool) available from the European Commission Institute for Health and Consumer Protection at http://ihcp.jrc.ec.europa.eu/our_labs/eurl-ecvam/archive-publications/toxrtool to determine data quality for each study. We further evaluated each study for evidence of greater or less than additive effects with respect to the outcome being studied, and as to how and where study results might fit in an adverse outcome pathway based on the outcome (e.g., molecular initiating event, key event at the cellular level, key event at the organ or organism level) as defined by guidance from the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD)( 27- 30 ). Epidemiologic studies were similarly evaluated with respect to data quality and study design, followed by an evaluation of the evidence for or against synergistic effects. Where possible, we compared in vivo doses or body burdens from the epidemiologic studies to NHANES data or other sources.

Finally, we integrated these data using an AOP framework to evaluate the evidence by adapting the modified Bradford Hill considerations as presented in OECD guidance( 30 ). As shown in Figure 2, the AOP identifies a series of key events (KEs) essential to the progression from a molecular initiating event (MIE) to an adverse outcome. Predictive and/or causal linkages between KEs are referred to as key event relationships or KERs. The MIE identifies the initial chemical interaction with a biological target (e.g. DNA binding, protein oxidation etc.) to initiate a sequential series of biological key events (e.g. gene activation, or altered tissue development etc.) to ultimately culminate in an adverse effect relevant to risk assessment (e.g. developmental delays, disrupted reproduction, cancer, etc.)( 28 , 30 ). KEs are modular, and may not be unique to a particular adverse outcome. KERs define upstream KEs (KEup) necessary for subsequent downstream KEs (KEdown) to occur as depicted graphically in the center box of Figure 2. For example, a change in gene expression is required for subsequent histopathological changes. Gene expression would then be defined as the KEup and the histpathological changes associated with the outcome defined as KEdown.

Evaluating the strength of the evidence for key events in AOPs based on evolving OECD guidance( 30 ) relies on answering and interpreting a series of questions as shown in Table I. The OECD guidance 1) prioritizes the modified Bradford Hill considerations (shown in the left-hand column of Table I); 2) identifies the defining questions to be asked relative to each Bradford Hill consideration; and, 3) provides basic criteria for evaluating confidence in the evidence based on responses to the questions( 30 ). As the guidance is written largely in terms of single stressors (or single pathways), we adapted the questions and criteria in the context of exposures to mixtures of metals. For example, the OECD guidance identifies “biological plausibility” (number 1 in Table I) of the hypothesized key events as the most important consideration for evaluation. The key question for that consideration as written in the original OECD guidance is “a) Is there a mechanistic (i.e., structural or functional) relationship between KEup and KEdown consistent with established biological knowledge?” We adapted that question as: “Is there a mechanistic (i.e., structural or functional) relationship between KEup and KEdown for the mixture consistent with established biological knowledge for the individual compounds?” Per OECD guidance, the three key considerations include: biological plausibility, essentiality, and empirical (e.g., documentation or derivation of quantitative relationships across key events).

Similarly, evaluating the weight of evidence (e.g., low, medium, strong) as defined in OECD guidance( 29 , 30 ); requires professional judgment and is developed for each key event using a narrative-based format, again relying on our adaptation of the OECD language and guidance. For example, “Strong” evidence in the original OECD guidance is written as: “Extensive understanding of the key event relationship(s) based on extensive previous documentation and broad acceptance (e.g., mutation leading to tumours) -Established mechanistic basis” which we adapted to read: “Extensive understanding of the key event relationship(s) based on previous documentation and broad acceptance - established mechanistic basis for the mixture: One or more (appropriately designed) studies for the primary mixture; plus several studies of individual constituents or binary combinations if the primary mixture is three or more individual constituents. ” Studies based on exposure to any of the metals individually constituted moderate support – evidence was only considered strong support for biological plausibility if it was based on one or more studies involving mixtures.

The literature search identified 7 in vitro and 37 in vivo toxicological studies as shown in Table III and 22 epidemiologic studies provided in Table III that examined associations of neurodevelopment with exposure to at least one of the metals of interest (Pb, Cd, Mn, As) and/or to other toxicants. Table IV provides a broad overview summarizing the toxicological and epidemiologic studies available for different binary, three-way and four-way combinations of metal mixtures.

Of the 22 epidemiologic studies, seven measured multiple metals, but did not examine interactive effects of metals on neurodevelopmental outcomes. Fourteen of the 22 studies examined interactions between the metals of interest, and one study evaluated interactions between a metal of interest and another toxicant. The majority of epidemiologic studies limited the interaction analyses to binary combinations of metals. Furthermore, there is little consistency in the exposure measures and the statistical approaches used to examine interactions. Despite the relative lack of consistency across studies, at least one trend seems to be present in the existing epidemiologic literature: the toxicity of lead, a well-documented neurotoxicant, appears to increase in the presence of high levels of other metals (e.g., Mn, As, Cd).

The three-way combination studied most frequently thus far is As-Cd-Pb, with 7 in vivo toxicological studies (4 including an in vitro component), and 4 epidemiologic studies (one focused specifically on autism as an outcome and the remaining 3 focused on non-specific developmental outcomes). Although these studies evaluated potential impacts of exposure to 3 metals with the goal of identifying interactions, these interactions were typically described either in general terms relative to the impact of the individual constituent, or as different combinations of binary interactions.

Marlowe et al.( 77 ) investigated associations between concentrations of As, Cd, Pb, mercury (Hg) and aluminum (Al) with children's visual-motor performance, assessed with the Bender Visual-Motor Gestalt Test, in 69 elementary school age children. Various binary combinations of metals were evaluated using partial regression coefficients. Using hair as the exposure index, the combination of Pb and Cd together, among other binary combinations, was found to account for a significant proportion of variance in classroom teachers’ ratings. Another study, Kim et al.( 85 ), evaluated only the combination of Pb and Cd without any other metals. These authors dichotomized blood Cd exposure at the median, and examined effects of blood Pb levels at low versus high Cd levels. Neurodevelopment was assessed at 6 months of age using Bayley Scales of Infant Development. They reported a potential antagonistic interaction between Pb and Cd in maternal blood during early pregnancy for mental development scores. During late pregnancy, however, authors observed synergistic effects, whereby adverse Pb effects on mental and psychomotor development scores were only significant at high Cd levels. In terms of possible mechanisms, both Pb and Cd have been shown to cause oxidative stress in astrocytes( 86 , 92 ), and to interfere with calcium signaling63 and neuroendocrine controls such as thyroid hormone( 93 , 94 ). The antagonistic interaction between Pb and Cd reported may be attributable to a potential protective effect of Cd at low levels, and/or an interference of Cd with Pb uptake, which has been demonstrated in pregnant mice( 48 ). Cd has been shown to stimulate ovarian progesterone biosynthesis at low levels, but to inhibit the same hormone at high levels( 94 ). Cd has also been found to stimulate DNA synthesis and cell proliferation at low levels but to increase apoptosis and chromosomal aberrations at high levels. While confidence in these results would be strengthened through additional studies, the shift in direction of the Pb-Cd interaction observed here suggests that mechanisms for interaction between these metals may depend on stage of pregnancy and/or dose of Cd. However, a cross-sectional study of 10,098 adults in the general population found an association between blood Pb and urinary Cd and oxidative stress markers based on NHANES data( 86 ) from the general population.

There were 12 in vivo toxicological studies focused on Cd-Pb, and 3 of those found antagonistic effects. In a study of adult female Charles Foster rats treated intraperitoneally, Pillai et al.( 46 ) found accumulation of the metals in the pituitary and lowered the membrane fluidity across exposure groups, with the greatest effect seen from Cd exposure alone. A follow-up study( 47 ) demonstrated similar results in the context of effects on the hypothalamic-pituitary axis in pre-estrus rats. Neurotransmitter release as well as levels of luteinizing and follicle stimulating hormones was significantly decreased in the Cd and Cd-Pb exposed groups, but not the Pb-exposed group. Finally, coadministration of Pb and Cd to pregnant mice resulted in Pb uptake mediated by the presence of Cd as reported by Smith et al.( 48 ). Three other studies focused on potential reproductive rather than developmental effects in rats found either no effects, or additive effects( 43- 45 ), and three studies focused on biogenic amines did not test for interactions, although reported changes in biogenic amine levels related to behavioral outcomes( 38- 40 ). Three toxicological studies with outcomes related to mitochondrial disruptions, lipid peroxidation, and divalent metal transporter found greater than additive impacts in rats with combined Cd-Pb exposures( 21 , 41 , 42 ).

A series of 4 in vivo-in vitro toxicological studies focused specifically on As-Cd-Pb exposures and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) expression in rat astrocytes. In a study of rat pups exposed throughout gestation and lactation to a combination of As-Cd-Pb, Rai et al.( 62 ) observed a greater than additive reduction in GFAP expression during brain development and concomitant reductions in learning and memory performance. GFAP-generating astrocytes were greater than additively reduced, and an exploration of MAPK signaling pathways revealed a greater than additive downregulation in viability and an increase in apoptosis of astrocytes, induced by proximal activation of extra cellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling and downstream activation of the Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) pathway. This led to an increase in Ca2+ release and reactive oxygen species (R0S) generation, resulting in astrocyte apoptosis greater in the mixture than was observed for individual exposures. A series of follow-on studies( 59- 61 ) explored the influence of As-Cd-Pb on GFAP isoforms, myelin and axon development, and the role of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARG) downregulation of GFAP and found similar synergism across outcomes.

Results from epidemiologic studies examining Pb-As interactive effects on neurodevelopment are mixed. In a correlation analysis, Moon et al.78 and Marlowe et al.( 77 ) reported interactions between Pb and As, measured in hair, with cognitive function (Wide Range Achievement Test [WRAT] reading and spelling tests, and Bender Visual-Motor Gestalt Test) in 69 elementary school age children, leading ATSDR to interpret the results as providing strong evidence for an interaction( 22 , 23 ). In these studies, decreased reading and spelling achievement were associated with higher hair Pb and As levels, and the combination of As and Pb together was found to account for a significant proportion of the variance in classroom teachers’ ratings on the Bender Visual-Motor Gestalt Test. In contrast, Calderon et al.( 81 ) found no evidence of interactions between urinary As and blood Pb in a cross-sectional study on associations between Pb, As, and the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (assessing full, verbal, performance IQ; long-term memory; linguistic abstraction; attention span; and visuospatial organization). Details about how interaction was assessed, however, were not provided. Rosado et al.( 84 ) reportedly considered As-Pb interaction in regression models, but did not report any results, leading the reader to assume no significant interaction was observed.

Marlowe et al.( 76 ) investigated associations between concentrations of As, Pb, Cd, Hg, and Al, individually and in binary combinations, with children's classroom behavior assessed with the Walker Problem Behavior Identification Checklist (for nonadaptive classroom behavior). Among 80 elementary school age children, a significant increase in explained variance was observed due to Pb-As and Pb-Cd interactions. Two out of 4 in vivo toxicological studies observed synergistic effects of combined Pb-As exposures, including increased Pb uptake and dopamine release in the rat brain( 54 ) as well as a statistically significant increase in apoptosis and oxidative stress in rat brain as compared to exposures to As or Pb individually( 55 ). One study did not evaluate interactions and the remaining study found a decrease in blood As in the presence of Pb as compared to As exposure alone( 56 ), although the study only evaluated Pb-As and As, not Pb.

There is evidence for an interaction between Mn and other metals. In 2009, Kim et al.( 14 ) reported findings from a cross-sectional study of 261 Korean children, aged 8-11 years. Authors reported significant associations between blood Pb levels and full-scale as well as verbal IQ, though only among kids with high blood Mn levels, suggesting significant effect modification of Pb by Mn. Claus Henn et al.( 88 ) reported similar findings in a prospective cohort of 450 Mexican children between one and three years of age. In this study, blood Mn and Pb levels were associated with repeated measures of neurodevelopment, assessed with the Bayley Scales of Infant Development II (BSID-II). The BSID evaluates mental development (sensory/perceptual acuities, discriminations, acquisition of object constancy, memory, learning and problem-solving, vocalisation, early verbal communication and abstract thinking, habituation, mental mapping, complex language and mathematical concept formation) and motor development (degree of body control, coordination of large muscles, fine manipulation skills, dynamic movement, postural imitation and stereognosis). Increased Pb toxicity was observed among children with high blood Mn levels, compared to children with lower blood Mn levels. A third study conducted by Lin et al.( 79 ) also reported interactions between Mn and Pb, whereby significantly lower scores on the Comprehensive Developmental Inventory for Infants and Toddlers (CDIIT) were observed among 230 two-year-old children whose cord blood Mn and Pb levels were both high (>75th percentile), as compared to children with low cord blood Mn and Pb levels. This study additionally adjusted for cord blood As and Hg levels as confounders. In contrast to the three aforementioned studies, Lucchini et al.( 89 ) observed no interactions between blood Pb and either blood, hair, air, or soil Mn in relation to cognitive function. Authors reported significant adverse effects of Pb on IQ, measured using the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC), among 299 adolescents (11-14 years old) residing near a ferro-manganese plant in Italy, but no Mn-Pb interactions were observed.

It has been suggested that Mn and Pb cause synergistic effects on neurodevelopment due to their interactions with similar proteins. Pb disrupts zinc in regulation of NMDA receptor activation, and inhibits Ca2+ dependent acetylcholine and dopamine release( 96 , 97 ). At high levels, Mn affects dopaminergic and cholinergic neurotransmitters and synaptic modulation, inhibits various protein transporters and enzymes, and can increase release of nitric oxide, which is involved in cellular signal transduction( 98 , 99 ). At insufficiently low levels, Mn may also have adverse neurodevelopmental effects( 98 ). Although evidence suggests an inverted U-shaped relationship between Mn and neurological function( 100 ), Claus Henn et al.88 found no change in Pb toxicity among children with low Mn levels, suggesting that mechanisms by which Mn deficiency causes adverse neurological effects may not overlap with those for Pb.

Several toxicological studies suggest that coexposure to Mn with other metals increases uptake of the other metals, for example, coadministration of Pb and Mn increased brain Pb levels by a factor of three as compared to lead alone50 and increased brain Pb in five of seven brain regions( 68 ). Mining waste containing a mixture of Pb, Mn and As orally administered to rats resulted in elevated levels of brain As and Mn( 70 ). Andrade et al.67,68 found increased levels of Pb, Mn, and As in rat brain tissue following coadministration as compared to uptake by the individual metals, and found a correlation with Delta ALA-U levels in urine as well as greater than additive behavioral deficits. The combined administration of Mn and Cd increased the brain contents of Cd as compared to individual uptake in a study of rats exposed intraperitoneally( 58 ).

Mining waste containing a mixture of Pb, Mn and As decreased dopaminergic transmitter following depolarization( 70 ). Similarly, Mejía et al.( 54 ) observed greater than additive changes in monoaminergic neurotransmitter levels in rat brains following administration of Pb and As. Increases in serotonin were observed in the cortex and midbrain, while decreases in norepinephrine were observed in the hippocampus. Leret et al.( 40 ) found similar results in a study of pregnant rats exposed to Pb and Cd, although the design of this study was such that it was not possible to determine whether the interaction was greater than additive or not. In an in vitro study of rat synaptosomes, Chandra et al.( 33 ) found an interaction between Mn and Pb or Cd and dopamine and noradrenaline uptake.

Antonio et al.( 38 ) observed significant inhibition of Na+/K+-ATPase activity in vivo following coadministration of Pb and Cd, while Carfagna et al.32 noted a greater than additive action of Pb or Cd in the presence of Mn, and Hussain et al.( 34 ) noted the same for Pb and Mn in rat synaptosomes in vitro. However, Chandra et al.( 33 ) in an in vitro study of rat synaptosomes noted only an additive interactive effect of Pb, Mn and Cd with no effect of Mn, and Lai et al.( 36 ) noted no effects at all following combined Cd, Mn, and Al exposures in purified synaptosomes from adult male rats.

The genes that consistently emerge across all metals and are associated with neurodevelopmental outcomes include:

The BDNF gene regulates brain-derived neurotrophic factor, essential to the growth, maturation (differentiation), and maintenance of neuronal cells. The BDNF protein is primarily active at the synaptic junctions and is required for cellular communication and signaling and with a direct influence on synaptic plasticity. The effect of BDNF on learning and memory is associated with NMDA and non-NMDA receptor functions as well as the expression of synaptic proteins required for exocytosis( 101 ). Exposure to As, Pb, and Cd down-regulates BDNF, while exposure to Mn is associated with upregulation of this gene. A recent study documents the direct effect of Pb on BDNF( 102 ).

It is the predominant caspase involved in cleavage of a protein associated with neuronal death in Alzheimer's disease and is related to numerous neurotoxic pathways( 104 , 105 ). This gene is upregulated across all four metals and is in the top ten interacting genes for both Mn and Cd.

Exposure to As, Cd, Mn, and Pb has been shown to down-regulate this gene, and it is one of the top ten interacting genes for As and Cd.

Heme oxygenase, an essential enzyme in heme catabolism, cleaves heme to form biliverdin, which is subsequently converted to bilirubin and carbon monoxide, a putative neurotransmitter. Studies have shown expression of this gene by astrocytes correlated with lower cognitive scores in patients with sporadic Alzheimer's Disease and mild cognitive impairment( 106 ).

Mitogen-activated protein (MAP) kinases, also known as extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERKs), act as integration points for multiple biochemical signals. They are involved in proliferation, differentiation, transcription regulation and development and participate in signal transduction pathways that control intracellular events following activation by upstream phosphorylation( 107 ). All 4 metals upregulate these genes, with some evidence of limited down-regulation of MAPK1 by Pb. These genes are amongst the top ten interacting genes for Mn, As, and Cd.

The TH gene encodes Tyrosine hydroxylase, the primary enzyme responsible for the catelcholamine synthesis using molecular oxygen and iron. This gene is down-regulated by As, Mn, and Pb and is upregulated by Cd.

The NHANES database provides data on urinary and blood levels of As, Pb, and Cd for the 2009-2010 sampling timeframe. No NHANES data were available for Mn, so we relied on a study from Maine that reported on measured blood levels in one-to five year olds( 109 ). In an exploratory analysis, we compared individual levels of As, Pb, and Cd from the epidemiologic studies with NHANES data to determine the potential overlap of concentrations associated with effects and observed values in the general population.

The epidemiologic studies are based different exposure metrics, including concentrations of metals in hair, urine (both creatinine and non-creatinine adjusted), maternal or child blood, and cord blood while NHANES data are available for urine and blood. In order to use the hair data, we used regression relationships from the literature to relate blood or urine levels to hair levels( 110- 113 ) and maternal blood levels to cord blood( 114 , 115 ).

Figure 3 shows these results of the comparisons between biomonitoring data for the general population and exposure levels from the epidemiologic studies that evaluated mixtures. This initial screening comparison shows that, on an individual chemical basis, exposures either fall at or exceed the 95th percentile of population exposures, indicating that the majority of the U.S. population is likely not exposed to levels corresponding to those observed in the epidemiologic studies associated with outcomes. Although the 4 toxicological studies supporting a synergistic interaction of As-Cd-Pb were designed to capture environmental exposures( 60- 63 ), these were typical ground water levels as observed in India (As-0.38 mg/kg; Pb-0.22 mg/kg; Cd-0.098 mg/kg), which are higher than what would be found in the United States.

Figure 4 provides a schematic relating potential initiating events with key events, key event relationships, and the outcome of interest. Overall, the strongest evidence for potential interactions is for exposures to As-Cd-Pb as demonstrated through the series of toxicological studies involving rat astrocytes( 60- 63 ), and 4 epidemiologic studies, although the epidemiologic studies only evaluated binary interactions( 9 , 76- 78 ) between pairs of those metals. Both the epidemiologic and toxicological studies provide the most evidence for an interaction between the presence of Mn and either As, Pb, or Cd, related to uptake and distribution of concentrations of these metals in the brain( 32 , 33 , 58 , 59 , 67- 69 ) and for motor activity and biogenic amines in the brain( 50- 52 ). There is more limited evidence for interactions involving As-Cd-Pb, and binary combinations of As-Pb, and the evidence is strongest for downstream key events with little evidence for initiating events. Evidence is strongest for greater than additive retention in the brain for Pb and Cd in the presence of Mn, while there is suggestive evidence of an inhibitory effect between Cd and Pb. Concurrently, there is evidence for the role of genetic polymorphisms for enhancing uptake, particularly for lead( 20 ).

Most of the toxicological studies tended to provide evidence for downstream key events both biologically plausible and essential for neurodevelopment, including apoptosis( 55 , 60- 63 ); oxidative stress( 41 , 42 , 52 , 55 , 64 , 68 , 70 ) and neurotransmitter release( 33 , 38 , 40 , 47 , 50 , 54 , 59 ) with less evidence for initiating events. Each of these key events is discussed next with reference to the citations in Table VI.

One potential initiating event involved the combined action of As-Cd-Pb is activation of the extracellular-signal-regulated kinase (ERK1/2) leading to increased intracellular Ca2+ followed by apoptosis in astrocytes( 60 ). Extracellular-signal-regulated kinases (ERKs) are not only important regulators of cortical development generally but are known to specifically initiate an apoptotic signal via a mitochondria-dependent pathway( 154 , 155 ). Individual exposure to As in a neuroblastoma cell line( 116 ), Cd in the immature rat hippocampus( 117 , 118 , 138 ), Mn in developing rat striatum( 119 ) and microglia( 120- 121 ) and Pb in the immature rat hippocampus( 122 ) has also been shown to activate the ERK1/2 pathway.

Data from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database shows that all four metals independently are documented to increase the expression of MAPK3, one of a set of ERKs, and As, Mn and Cd increase the expression of MAPK1 with a variable response from Pb. In addition, both MAPK1 and MAPK3 are in the top ten interacting genes for As, Mn, and Cd. All four metals (independently) also increase the expression of CASP3, which is associated with the BDNF and MAPK signaling pathways, the caspase cascade in apoptosis and signal transduction, and has been specifically implicated in the development of autism. CASP3 is one of the top ten interacting genes for both Mn and Cd. A study of mechanisms of oxidative stress and apoptosis in primary cultures of rat cerebral cortex neurons found that hydrogen peroxide and nitric oxide act synergistically to induce neuronal death through apoptosis in which activation of p38 MAPK and caspase-3 is involved( 126 ). CASP3 and MAPK1/2 are genetic targets in common across As, Mn, Cd, and Pb (with CASP3 in the top ten interacting genes for Mn and Cd).

The ERK MAP kinase signaling cascade plays critical roles in brain development, learning, memory, and cognition. Samuels et al.( 154 ) discuss the essentiality of this signaling pathway to cortical neurogenesis, cognitive function, and successful neurodevelopment and how this key event leads to developmental syndromes in humans associated with impaired cognitive function and specific developmental disorders such as autism. Moreover, a knockout mouse model has been used to demonstrate essentiality of this pathway( 155 ). Both biological plausibility and essentiality are considered strong for this initiating event.

There are numerous opportunities for exposure to metals to influence synaptic connections as they are being established during brain development. Many of these involve calcium signaling, which directs structural as well as functional adaptation in neurons( 136 , 137 ) and astrocytes( 128 ) to establish synaptic selectivity in the developing brain. While astrocytes have long been known to support neuronal signaling, there is increasing evidence that astrocytes detect synaptic activity and engage in reciprocal signaling with neurons, again based on variations in intracellular Ca2+( 144 , 145 ). Although there is only one study that explicitly observed release of Ca2+ from a mixture of As-Cd-Pb60, there is substantial data for the individual metals, including Mn, as shown in Table VI. Moreover, the essentiality of Ca2+ signaling in neurodevelopment and how perturbations are associated with neurotoxicity and neurodegenerative disorders is well-established, with some key references provided under “essentiality” in Table VI.

A number of toxicological studies have observed increased production of ROS with subsequent impairment of cellular antioxidant defense systems and damage to critical biomolecules such as lipids and proteins correlated with behavioral outcomes in rodents. Rai et al.( 63 ) exposed astrocytes to a mixture of As-Cd-Pb as well as the individual constituents and found the mixture triggered a release of Ca2+ with subsequent ROS generation, resulting in apoptosis of astrocytes treated with the mixture greater than would have been predicted based on exposure to the individual metals. Zhang et al.( 42 ) reported decreased activity of glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and increased activity of maleic dialdehyde (MDA) in rat pups perinatally exposed to a mixture of Cd-Pb as compared to exposures to Cd and Pb individually. They reported synergistic alterations in lipid peroxidation and ultrastructural modifications in the pup brains following exposure to the mixture.

On an individual basis, each of these metals has been shown to increase oxidative stress, ROS, and subsequent neuronal or glial apoptosis via a variety of mechanisms, many of which are not completely understood. In general, evidence for biological plausibility for this key event is strong, but in terms of evidence for a synergistic effect in this specific context, the evidence is considered moderate due to a lack of consistency across tissue types and potential mechanisms. However, evidence for essentiality of oxidative stress as a potential key event in neurodevelopmental deficits is considered strong,

The strongest evidence for synergistic effects is provided based on studies in astrocytes, and astrocytes are also documented targets for individual metals( 124 ). Astrocytes are a source of neurotrophic factors (Table V shows that BDNF is a genetic target across all of these constituents from the comparative toxicogenomics database and in particular, Pb has a documented direct impact on BDNF( 102 ); reduced glial fibrillary acidic protein expression interferes with astrocyte production and modulates synaptic efficiency in the central nervous system( 131 , 132 ) and the As-Cd-Pb combination has been observed to act synergistically specifically on GFAP in toxicological studies with rats( 60- 63 ). Astrocytes control ion homeostasis, glutamate uptake at the synapse and provide metabolic support for neighboring neurons( 143 , 144 ). Astrocyte contact is necessary for the synaptic maturation of developing neurons( 142 , 146 , 148 , 149 ), and the coadministration of As-Cd-Pb revealed a greater than additive effect on apoptosis of astrocytes in rats( 60- 63 ) and As-Pb on apoptotic markers in the cerebral cortex, hippocampus and cerebellum in offspring of maternally exposed rats( 55 ). Toxicological studies show that individual exposure to As, Cd, Mn, and Pb all independently have been associated with apoptosis or apoptotic markers in neuronal and glial tissues as referenced in Table VI, providing strong evidence for biological plausibility of this key event.

A recent study demonstrates that neuroglial signaling involving astrocytes differs significantly in the adult versus developing brain( 157 ), highlighting both the potential vulnerability of perinatal exposures as well as the evolving nature of our understanding of expected neurodevelopment. Further, new evidence suggests that astrocyte synaptic regulation is not restricted to the active tripartite synapse but can be manifested through astrocyte signaling at synapses relatively distant from active synapses, a process termed lateral astrocyte synaptic regulation( 150 ), demonstrating strong evidence for the essentiality of apoptosis as a key event.

Several toxicological studies have explored biogenic amines and neurotransmitter release in mixtures as compared to individual metals and found some evidence of synergistic action or changes relative to the mixture. For example, Mejía et al.( 54 ) assessed levels of three monoamines: dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin (DA, NE, and 5-HT) and of two metabolites: 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid (DOPAC) and 5-hydroxyindole-3-acetic acid (5-HIAA), in specific brain regions following 14 days of oral administration of As, Pb, or the As-Pb mixture. They found exposure to the mixture led to a 38% decrease of NE in the hippocampus and a 100% and 90% increase of 5-HT in the midbrain and frontal cortex over control values, while there were no statistical differences from controls in the individually exposed As or Pb rats. Three studies focused on the Cd-Pb mixture( 38 , 40 , 47 ), but none were designed to test interaction effects from the mixture. Antonio et al.( 38 ) and Leret et al.( 40 ) observed statistically significant increases in DOPAC, 5-HT and 5-HIAA contents in the cerebellum and hippocampus in rat offspring exposed to a Cd-Pb mixture throughout pregnancy and lactation, but without any comparison to individual Cd or Pb exposure. Pillai et al.( 47 ) only evaluated the hypothalamic-pituitary axis of adult rats. Chandra et al.( 50 ) exposed adult rats to Mn, Pb, or a Mn-Pb mixture and observed dose-related decreases in the levels of DA, NE and 5-HT in brain tissue and concomitant behavioral outcomes.

Evidence for biological plausibility for this key event is strong in a general sense given the evidence from studies with individual metals and some combinations, but the lack of concordance across tissue types and life stages for the studies in mixtures provides only moderate evidence of potential interaction. Essentiality for this key event is considered strong based on a putative understanding of the role neurotransmitter release plays in learning and development( 101 , 136 ).

Three epidemiologic studies explored exposure to mixtures of As-Cd-Pb( 76- 78 ) although only evaluated binary combinations with respect to interaction and found interactions between As-Pb and Cd-Pb. This is the same mixture from the series of toxicological studies reporting a greater than additive effect of the mixture on apoptosis in astrocytes via increases in Ca2+ and ROS initiated by ERK1/2( 60- 63 ), providing moderate to strong evidence of a complete pathway from initiating event to adverse outcome.

Both Claus Henn et al.( 88 ) and Kim et al.( 14 ) found an interaction between high levels of Mn and Pb exposures and performance on neurodevelopmental tests in epidemiologic studies of children. Evidence from the toxicological studies is equivocal with respect to key events, although the evidence is stronger for observed increases in brain tissue concentrations of Pb in the presence of Mn( 49- 51 ).

There are a number of molecular initiating events one could hypothesize based on data for individual metals and known neurodevelopmental pathways, particularly those that lead to known downstream events. For example, acetylcholinesterase binding is known to inhibit acetylcholinesterase resulting in oxidative stress, and there is data for individual metals for this potential MIE but not for combinations of metals. As there are no studies of these metals as mixtures and therefore no way to determine at this time whether the combined effect on converging key events could be greater (or less) than additive, these potential MIEs are left unspecified.

Data are not sufficient at this time to develop quantitative estimates of relationships between key events. However, as shown in Figure 3, a preliminary comparison of biomarkers of exposure in the general population and exposures as determined from the epidemiologic studies suggests that the majority of the U.S. population is likely not exposed at levels at which effects have been observed.

As the magnitude of individual epidemiologic and toxicological studies grows, systematic review offers a structured approach for considering specific research or public health questions, and there is great interest in developing guidelines and best practices( 158 ), particularly for identifying and evaluating study quality and reliability( 159 ) and for combining qualitative and quantitative data( 160 ). In the context of environmental exposures, and as risk assessment methodology evolves to capture growing evidence on the ways in which environment, genes, and exposures combine to lead to adverse outcomes, the AOP construct is emerging as an integrative framework for evaluating the evidence supporting or refuting key events across the continuum from molecular initiating events to adverse outcomes in the context of expected physiological function( 161 , 162 ). The AOP framework extends the results of a systematic review by providing a higher level depiction and mechanistic understanding of the sequence of key toxicological events culminating in an adverse outcome( 163 ). Key events are essential, although not necessarily sufficient, measurable changes in biological states in the progression from a defined biological perturbation to a specific adverse outcome( 162 ).

In the context of neurotoxicity, neurodevelopmental pathways in the developing human brain are not fully understood( 164- 165 ), precluding the development of definitive AOPs. However, much is known about individual key events. A workshop organized under the auspices of the European Union Reference Laboratory for Alternatives to Animal Testing resulted in ten proposed, although not fully developed, putative AOPs( 163 ) focused on individual chemicals. Developed independently, the results presented here include many of the key events identified through the workshop( 163 ).

Most examples of AOPs currently focus on individual chemical exposures evaluated in the context of a single pathway from MIE to adverse outcome. There is strong evidence that the key events shown in Figure 4 are both biologically plausible contributors to non-specific learning and developmental deficits, and essential to neurodevelopment, although defining the quantitative relationships between key events still requires further analysis. Exposure to multiple chemicals, as well as non-chemical stressors, will undoubtedly lead to interactions that may result in different mechanisms of toxicity than observed for individual constituents, as well as leading to differences in outcomes (e.g., synergistic, antagonistic) than would be predicted for individual chemicals. A key challenge in developing AOPs is that causation is induced logically, not observed empirically( 166 ), which is complicated enough when considering one chemical-one outcome, let alone the assessment of mixtures and interactions. Ultimately, AOPs will likely be developed in terms of networks to capture these potential overlaps and feedbacks. Bayesian networks, agent-based modeling, and other computational frameworks with common interfaces will be required to support quantitative descriptions of pathways( 161 ). There are many challenges and complexities associated with AOP development, but also many efforts to identify and describe best practices going forward( 161- 163 , 186- 187 ).

Risk assessment requires quantitative estimates of toxicity, which requires additional modeling( 167- 172 ). The evidence suggests that exposure to Mn in the presence of other metals increases uptake into the brain, which impacts the absorbed dose. Physiologically-based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling approaches can be used to inform a better understanding of the relationship between exposed and absorbed dose and target organs, but typically these are developed for individual compounds. For example, PBPK models have been developed for Mn( 173 , 174 ), As( 175 , 176 ), Cd( 177- 179 ), and Pb (the Integrated Exposure UptakeBiokinetic [IEUBK] Model for Lead in Children, available from www.epa.gov/superfund/lead/products.htm). There are few published studies that extend the analysis to mixtures of metals, although these kinds of PBPK approaches are being developed for other kinds of mixtures( 180 , 182 ) and for predicting in vivo exposures associated with in vitro assay results( 182- 184 ), including the specific context of neurodevelopmental effects and neuroprogenitor proliferation( 185 ). For “traditional” epidemiologic and toxicological studies, complex statistical methods are being developed for estimating the effects of exposure to mixtures, including supervised clustering methods (e.g., tree-based methods, random forests), Bayesian approaches, dimension reduction approaches (e.g., principal component analysis), and weighted quartile score regression strategies( 1 , 167- 172 ). These methods also consider potential thresholds, which may be relevant both for individual chemicals (e.g., there is a requirement for some level of Mn as an essential nutrient) as well as mixtures (e.g., physiological pathways and events may involve initiation thresholds). In the context of AOP development informing risk assessment, human dosimetry methods applied to effect levels based on non-testing approaches and in vitro data can be used to compare to exposure levels or develop regulatory values. For example, Patlewicz et al.( 187 ) discuss the use of exposure:activity ratios (EAR) to interpret biological activity based on AOPs for skin sensitization, endocrine disruption, and liver tumor promotion in rodents.

This review provides evidence of synergistic effects related to exposures to mixtures of several metals, notably As-Cd-Pb and Mn in the presence of As, Cd, or Pb, and provides a foundational example for the evaluation of key events and outcomes relevant both to this specific mixture as well as more broadly applicable to other neurotoxicants based on an application of the evolving OECD guidance for developing AOPs. We propose a series of key events in keeping with related efforts to develop putative AOPs for neurodevelopmental pathways with the goal of ultimately estimating quantitative linkages for key event relationships to better inform regulatory decision making in the face of scientific complexity.
