# Molecular Mechanisms Driving Lymphedema and Other Lymphatic Anomalies.
**Authors:** Geng X, Srinivasan RS
**Journal:** Cold Spring Harb Perspect Med (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1101/cshperspect.a041272](https://doi.org/10.1101/cshperspect.a041272)

## Content

Lymphatic vasculature regulates fluid homeostasis by absorbing interstitial fluid and returning it to blood. Lymphatic vasculature is also critical for lipid absorption and inflammatory response. Lymphatic vasculature is composed of lymphatic capillaries, collecting lymphatic vessels, lymphatic valves, and lymphovenous valves. Defects in any of these structures could lead to lymphatic anomalies such as lymphedema, cystic lymphatic malformation, and Gorham-Stout disease. Basic research has led to a deeper understanding of the stepwise development of the lymphatic vasculature. VEGF-C and shear stress signaling pathways have evolved as critical regulators of lymphatic vascular development. Loss-of-function and gain-of-function mutations in genes that are involved in these signaling pathways are associated with lymphatic anomalies. Importantly, drugs that target these molecules are showing outstanding efficacy in treating certain lymphatic anomalies. In this article, we summarize these exciting developments and highlight the future challenges.

The primary function of the lymphatic vasculature is to absorb interstitial fluid and return it to blood (Aspelund et al. 2016; Petrova and Koh 2018). In addition, specialized lymphatic capillaries of the intestine, known as lacteals, absorb digested lipids. Furthermore, lymphatic vessels are the conduits for the migration of immune cells from peripheral tissues to the draining lymph nodes. Recent publications have found that the lymphatic vessels are necessary for the formation and functioning of specific tissues and organs. For example, proper lymphatic vascular function is required to prevent adipogenesis and obesity (Harvey et al. 2005; Ho and Srinivasan 2020); lymphatic vessels of the heart secrete signaling molecules to promote the growth and repair of cardiomyocytes (Liu et al. 2020); meningeal lymphatic vessels clear immune cells and damaged proteins from the brain (Antila et al. 2017; Da Mesquita et al. 2018; Louveau et al. 2018); pulmonary lymphatic vessels clear fetal lung fluid to allow alveolar maturation in the newborn (Jakus et al. 2014); lymphatic vessels in adult lungs remove inflammatory cells and hyaluronan to prevent fibrosis and tertiary lymphoid organ formation (Cui et al. 2017; Courtwright et al. 2019; Reed et al. 2019; Baluk et al. 2020); lymphatic vessels in the skin and aorta perform reverse cholesterol transport and remove salt (Machnik et al. 2009; Lim et al. 2013; Martel et al. 2013); and lymphatic vessels of adipocytes and kidney are necessary to reduce pathological inflammation caused by hypertension (Balasubbramanian et al. 2020a,b). Thus, the lymphatic vasculature is critical for our overall health and well-being.

Accumulation of interstitial fluid is known as edema. Edema of hands and legs that is caused by lymphatic vascular dysfunction is known as lymphedema, and it is the most obvious phenotype that is caused by dysfunctional lymphatic vasculature. Secondary lymphedema is caused by damage to lymphatic vessels, and it is the most prevalent form of lymphedema (Rockson 2018). Ten to twenty-five percent of breast cancer survivors develop secondary lymphedema due to surgery- and radiation-induced damage to lymphatic vessels (Rockson 2018). Hundreds of millions of individuals in tropical countries are afflicted with lymphatic filariasis, a disease that is caused by parasitic worms that infiltrate and block the lymphatic vessels (Hoerauf et al. 2011). In addition to lymphedema, there are other lymphatic disorders that cause defects such as fluid- or blood-filled cysts, chylous ascites, chylothorax and vanishing bones. The goal of this article is to describe the molecular mechanisms behind lymphedema and other lymphatic disorders.

Lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs) are the building blocks of the lymphatic vasculature. Lymphatic capillaries, collecting lymphatic vessels, lymphatic valves (LVs) and lymphovenous valves (LVVs) are made of a heterogeneous population of LECs (Tammela and Alitalo 2010; Oliver et al. 2020; Geng et al. 2021). Lymphatic capillaries are made of LECs that interact with each other through button-like junctions to collect interstitial fluid (Fig. 1A). Lymphatic capillaries lack continuous basement membrane and mural cell coverage for allowing unhindered interstitial fluid flow into the lumen. Lymph collected by lymphatic capillaries is transported upstream to collecting lymphatic vessels in which LVs prevent backflow. Collecting lymphatic vessels contain zipper-like junctions that prevent the leakage of lymph (Fig. 1A). Additionally, specialized mural cells known as lymphatic muscle cells (LMCs) with intrinsic contractile activity are recruited to the collecting lymphatic vessels. LVs and LMCs are critical to transport lymph against gravitational and hydrostatic pressure gradients.

Collecting lymphatic vessels have zipper-like junctions that prevent the leakage of lymph. Lymph flow is propelled by the contractile activity of lymphatic muscle cells (LMCs) that cover the collecting lymphatic vessels. Lymphatic valves (LVs) within the collecting lymphatic vessels regulate the unidirectional flow of fluid. (B) Lymph nodes (LNs) filter the lymph that pass through collecting lymphatic vessels. Collecting lymphatic vessels from throughout the body converge into the thoracic duct (TD) or right lymphatic duct (RLD). TD and RLD drain lymph into blood circulation through lymphovenous valves (LVVs) at the junction of the jugular vein (JV) and subclavian vein (SCV). The blood from the JV/SCV junction flows into the right atrium (RA) of the heart through superior vena cava (SVC). Collecting lymphatic vessels are periodically punctuated by lymph nodes where lymph is filtered and potentially toxic substances such as bacteria and virus are removed (Fig. 1B; Jalkanen and Salmi 2020). In humans, collecting lymphatic vessels from the upper right side of the body (right arm, head, and neck) drain into the junction of the right jugular vein and right subclavian vein (Tammela and Alitalo 2010). Collecting lymphatic vessels from the rest of the body, including the gut, converge into the thoracic duct that runs along the aorta and drains into the junction of the left jugular vein and left subclavian vein (Tammela and Alitalo 2010). LVVs regulate the flow of lymph into blood while preventing the entry of blood into the lymphatic vessels (Srinivasan and Oliver 2011; Geng et al. 2016, 2017).

Defects in any of the structures described above will lead to lymphatic disorders. Genetic disorders, surgery, radiation, or infection could damage the lymphatic vasculature. An increase in lymph production or defects in lymph flow can also cause lymphatic dysfunction. For example, liver cirrhosis, a disease in which lymph production by liver is increased is a common cause of lymphatic dysfunction (Itkin et al. 2020). The heart provides the suctional force that is critical for lymphatic drainage. Consequently, congestive heart failure results in edema and protein-losing enteropathy in which lymph from the thoracic duct is abnormally shunted to the gastrointestinal tract (Itkin et al. 2020; Geng et al. 2021). In the following sections, we will describe lymphatic conditions that are caused by genetic disorders.

Primary lymphatic anomaly is the term used to describe a developmental disorder that affects the lymphatic vasculature. Primary lymphatic anomalies occur in approximately one in 4000 live births. The International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies (ISSVA) and St. George's University of London have provided guidelines for classifying genetically determined lymphatic disorders (Wassef et al. 2015; Gordon et al. 2020; Mäkinen et al. 2021). We will use these classifications with slight modifications for simplicity to show the molecular mechanisms underlying lymphatic disorders (Fig. 2; Table 1).

Lymphatic anomalies are broadly classified as lymphedema and nontruncal lymphatic malformations (Fig. 2; Table 1). Lymph drainage is compromised in lymphedema resulting in swelling. Isolated lymphatic anomalies that do not affect the main drainage pathways are known as nontruncal lymphatic malformations.

Nontruncal lymphatic malformations (LMs) are further classified based on whether the defects are local or diffuse, the tissues that are impacted in addition to the lymphatic vasculature, and whether central lymphatic vessels (pleural and abdominal cavities) are affected.

These LMs are made of cyst-like lymphatic vessels that appear as solitary soft subcutaneous masses (Wassef et al. 2015; Gordon et al. 2020; Mäkinen et al. 2021). They can occur anywhere in the body but are commonly seen in the neck, armpit, or groin areas. Cystic LMs are further classified into macrocystic, microcystic, or mixed-type LMs. Macrocystic LMs are large fluid-filled lymphatic vessels that are commonly seen in the neck. Due to their size and location, macrocystic LMs could be diagnosed prenatally and can complicate vaginal delivery. Macrocystic LMs are treated by aspiration or sclerotherapy. Microcystic LMs can be identified anywhere in the soft tissues, and in the skin they present a blister-like appearance. Somatic hyperactivating mutations in PIK3CA, the catalytic subunit of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K), are associated with cystic LM (Rodriguez-Laguna et al. 2019; Martinez-Corral et al. 2020).

These lymphatic disorders affect multiple visceral organs and are classified further based on disease presentation. Generalized lymphatic anomaly (GLA) is caused by macrocystic LMs in spleen and liver and chylous fluid build-up (chylous ascitis, pleural effusion) (Wassef et al. 2015; Ozeki and Fukao 2019; Gordon et al. 2020; Mäkinen et al. 2021). Additionally, lymphatic vessels could infiltrate the medulla of bones without damaging cortical bones (Rodriguez-Laguna et al. 2019). GLA is associated with somatic hyperactivating mutations in PIK3CA.

Kaposiform lymphangiomatosis (KLA) is an aggressive form of GLA in which spindle-shaped LECs are observed in the lymphatic vessels of the lesions. Thrombocytopenia, tortuous blood vessels, chylous or hemorrhagic effusions, splenic cysts, bone lesions, respiratory symptoms, bleeding, and subcutaneous masses are observed in KLA patients (Croteau et al. 2014; Ozeki and Fukao 2019). Additionally, D-dimers (indicator of coagulopathy), angiopoietin-2, and soluble VEGFR3 are increased in the plasma of KLA patients. GLA is associated with somatic hyperactivating mutations in NRAS and loss-of-function mutation in the E3 ubiquitin ligase CBL (Barclay et al. 2019; Foster et al. 2020).

Gorham–Stout disease (GSD) is also known as vanishing bone disease (Lala et al. 2013; Dellinger et al. 2014; Ozeki and Fukao 2019). The cortical bones of GSD patients undergo progressive osteolysis resulting in fractures and even complete disappearance of bones. Axial bones (cranium, sternum, and ribs) are more commonly affected in GSD patients. Somatic hyperactivating mutations in the proto-oncoprotein KRAS can cause GSD (Homayun-Sepehr et al. 2021).

Central conducting lymphatic anomaly (CCLA) is also known as channel type LM or lymphangiectasia. Thoracic duct and cisterna chyli are affected in this disease resulting in retrograde flow and abnormal lymph drainage. Symptoms of CCLA include pericardial effusions, respiratory distress, lower extremity, and lower abdominal lymphedema. Germline mutations in the serine/threonine-protein kinase ARAF and the receptor tyrosine kinase EPHB4 are associated with CCLA (Li et al. 2018, 2019).

These LMs are associated with other vascular malformations or organ overgrowth.

Klippel–Trenaunay syndrome (KTS) affects blood capillaries, venous, and lymphatic vessels with limb overgrowth (Cohen 2000). Treatment involves surgical debulking, pulse-dye laser ablation, and surgery to arrest growth of bones.

CLOVES syndrome (congenital lipomatous overgrowth, vascular malformations, epidermal nevi and spinal/skeletal anomalies, and/or scoliosis) affects lymphatic vessels, veins, and blood capillaries with arteriovenous malformations and adipose tissue hypertrophy (Osborn et al. 2015). CLOVES patients are treated with embolization for arteriovenous malformations, sclerotherapy for the venous malformations, surgical debulking, liposuction, and pulse-dye laser.

CLAPO syndrome is named after capillary malformation of the lower lip, lymphatic malformation, asymmetry, and partial or generalized overgrowth that are observed in the patients (López-Gutiérrez and Lapunzina 2008). Due to the presence of somatic hyperactivating mutations in PIK3CA KTS, CLOVES, and CLAPO syndromes are also named as PIK3CA-related-overgrowth-syndromes (PROS) (Le Cras et al. 2020).

Parkes Weber syndrome (PWS) is caused by mutations in the Ras GTPase activating protein RAS P21 Protein Activator 1 (RASA1) (Burrows et al. 2013; Lapinski et al. 2018). The symptoms of PWS include lymphedema, capillary malformation, and arteriovenous malformation.

Lymphedema patients present with pitting edema in one or both lower extremities. Swelling could also be observed in arms, trunk, face, or genitalia. An early sign of lymphedema is thickening of skin, which is determined by pinching the toes. Lymphedema patients display “Stemmer's sign”: The skin fold over the second or third toe cannot be pinched due to thickening of the skin (Greene and Goss 2018). The skin is fragile and slow to heal from minor scratches or insect bites. Primary lymphedema can be classified as follows (Gordon et al. 2020).

Primary lymphedema could be associated with syndromes such as Noonan syndrome or Turner syndrome (Atton et al. 2015; Joyce et al. 2016). Mutations in the protein–tyrosine phosphatase PTPN11 are most frequently (>50%) associated with Noonan syndrome. Other common genes include SOS1, RAF, KRAS, HRAS, and BRAF, which are associated with the Ras signaling cascade.

This group of lymphatic anomalies includes lymphatic-related nonimmune hydrops fetalis, lymphedema, intestinal lymphangiectasia, and pulmonary lymphangiectasia. Causative loss-of-function mutations have been identified in the transcription factor SOX18, VEGF-C-activating proteases CCBE1 and ADAMTS3, proto-cadherin FAT4, integrin-α9 and the mechanosensitive ion channel PIEZO1 (Brouillard et al. 2014; Gordon et al. 2020).

Gordon et al. have categorized CCLA under this section due to the common occurrence of lymphedema in patients afflicted with phenotypes such as chylothorax and chylous ascites (Gordon et al. 2020).

Congenital lymphedema is observed before or shortly after birth. Milroy disease or Nonne–Milroy disease is caused by loss-of-function mutation in FLT4/VEGFR3. Loss-of-function mutations in VEGF-C, which is the ligand for VEGFR3, are also associated with congenital lymphedema (Gordon et al. 2020). Recently, loss-of-function mutations in ANGPT2, the gene coding for the signaling molecule angiopoietin-2, were reported in congenital lymphedema patients (Leppänen et al. 2020). Mutations in SOX18 results in hypotrichosis-lymphedema-telangiectasia (HLT) syndrome (Irrthum et al. 2003).

Lymphedema is identified in adolescence in these patients (Gordon et al. 2020). Heterozygous loss-of-function mutations in the transcription factors forkhead box protein C2 (FOXC2) and GATA2 or missense mutations in the gap junction molecule GJC2/Connexin-47 are associated with lymphedema praecox.

This form of lymphedema occurs after the age of 35 years. Mutations in FOXC2 could cause lymphedema tarda (Gordon et al. 2020).

In summary, lymphatic anomalies are classified based on tissues that are affected, age of onset, and gene mutations. Nevertheless, the criteria for classification and the disease nomenclature are not always intuitive as mutations in genes such as PIK3CA, RASA1, and EPHB4 can cause a wide range of phenotypes. We hope that the rapid advances in genomic technologies will simplify the classifications based on the disease-causing mutations. Approximately 30 genes have been linked to lymphedema (Brouillard et al. 2014; Mäkinen et al. 2021). However, these genes account for only 30% of primary lymphedema. Hence, additional genomics work is needed to fully understand the etiology of lymphedema. Basic research has revealed many more molecules that are necessary for lymphatic vascular development (see below). These molecules could be considered as candidates for lymphatic anomalies of unknown etiology.

A brief discussion about the stepwise development of the lymphatic vasculature and the molecules that are involved in these steps is necessary to understand the etiology of lymphatic anomalies (Fig. 3; Table 1).

The homeobox transcription factor PROX1 is considered as the master regulator of LEC identity (Wigle and Oliver 1999). The formation of PROX1+ endothelial cells from other cell types is known as LEC specification. LECs originate predominantly from the anterior cardinal vein, iliac vein, intersomitic veins and the superficial venous plexus (Srinivasan et al. 2007; Yang et al. 2012; Hägerling et al. 2013; Pichol-Thievend et al. 2018). Mesenchymal cells and second heart field cells make minor contributions to the lymphatic vessels of tissues such as the skin, mesentery, and heart (Martinez-Corral et al. 2015; Stanczuk et al. 2015; Maruyama et al. 2019). The mechanisms of LEC specification from the anterior cardinal vein are the best understood. PROX1 expression is initially observed in a polarized manner in the anterior cardinal vein of embryonic day (E) 10 in mice (Fig. 3A; Wigle and Oliver 1999). These PROX1+ cells are the progenitors of most LECs in the anterior section of the body including the skin, lungs, and heart (Srinivasan et al. 2007). The transcription factors COUP-TFII and SOX18 directly activate the expression of PROX1 and specify LEC (François et al. 2008; Srinivasan et al. 2010). As mentioned in the previous section mutations in SOX18 cause HLT syndrome. Thus, it is reasonable to conclude that LEC specification is compromised in patients with mutations in SOX18.

Starting at around E10.5, LECs up-regulate the expression of VEGFR3 and migrate from the anterior cardinal vein. The migrating LECs proliferate and cluster together to form lymph sacs that are precursors of the lymphatic vasculature (Fig. 3B). VEGF-C provides the guidance for LEC migration and promotes LEC proliferation. LEC migration and lymph sac formation does not occur in mice lacking VEGF-C or the VEGF-C processing proteases CCBE1 and ADAMTS3 (Karkkainen et al. 2004; Bui et al. 2016). Mice lacking even one allele of Vegfc have severely reduced numbers of LECs (Srinivasan et al. 2014).

PROX1 and GATA2 are regulators of VEGFR3 expression in LECs (Petrova et al. 2002; Srinivasan et al. 2014; Frye et al. 2018). Migrating LECs experience a drop in ECM stiffness as they exit the anterior cardinal vein (Frye et al. 2018). This change in ECM composition promotes the nuclear translocation of GATA2 and transactivation of VEGFR3 expression. Correspondingly, mice lacking Gata2 in endothelial cells have fewer LECs and smaller lymph sacs. Thus, it is reasonable to suspect a reduction in the number of LECs as one of the disease-initiating defects in patients with mutations in VEGFR3, VEGFC, CCBE1, ADAMTS3, and GATA2.

The developing lymph sacs quickly establish connections with the anterior cardinal vein through two pairs of bilaterally located LVVs at E12.5 in mice. A subset of LEC progenitors in the vein differentiate into LVV-forming endothelial cells (LVV-ECs) by up-regulating the expression of PROX1, FOXC2, and GATA2 (Srinivasan and Oliver 2011; Geng et al. 2016). Wnt/β-catenin signaling is necessary for the differentiation of LVV-ECs (Cha et al. 2016, 2018). LVV-ECs undergo stepwise morphogenesis to become mature LVVs. Prox1+/− and Foxc2−/− embryos do not develop LVVs due to the absence of LVV-EC differentiation (Geng et al. 2016). Furthermore, some Foxc2+/− embryos lack LVVs while others develop only one LVV per side.

GATA2 is not necessary for the differentiation of LVV-ECs (Mahamud et al. 2019). However, GATA2 is necessary for the survival of LVV-ECs and for their morphogenesis into LVVs (Kazenwadel et al. 2015; Mahamud et al. 2019). Therefore, we believe that a defect in LVV development is a lymphedema-initiating defect in human patients with mutations in FOXC2 and GATA2.

Once the lymph sacs are formed, lymphatic vessels sprout from these structures to form the primitive lymphatic plexus. As the embryonic dorsal skin is thin, it has been an ideal tissue to image the developing lymphatic plexus. The lymphatic vessels sprout from the laterally located lymph sacs, branch periodically, and migrate toward the midline (James et al. 2013; Coxam et al. 2014; Martinez-Corral et al. 2015).

VEGF-C/VEGFR3 signaling is critical for the growth of the lymphatic plexus. Vegfc+/− mice have severely hypoplastic dermal lymphatic vessels (Karkkainen et al. 2004). Likewise, Vegfr3+/chy mice that carry a dominant-negative mutation in VEGFR3 have severely hypoplastic lymphatic vessels. An initial defect in LEC migration from the anterior cardinal vein could have contributed to the hypoplastic phenotype in Vegfc+/− and Vegfr3+/chy mice. However, we recently showed that the conditional knockdown of Vegfr3 after the formation of lymph sacs (E12.5) in mice leads to the absence of dermal lymphatic vessels at E17.5 (Cha et al. 2020). Therefore, VEGF-C/VEGFR3 signaling is continuously required for the growth, survival, and patterning of lymphatic vessels.

Molecules that regulate VEGF-C/VEGFR3 signaling, such as PROX1, GATA2, neuropilin-2, PIK3CA, DLL4, angiopoietin-2, YAP/TAZ, TGF-β2, and the G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) polycystic kidney disease 1 (PKD1), and sphingosine-1 phosphate receptor 1 (S1PR1), are also necessary for dermal lymphatic vascular patterning (Harvey et al. 2005; James et al. 2013; Coxam et al. 2014; Kazenwadel et al. 2015; Uchida et al. 2015; Mahamud et al. 2019; Rodriguez-Laguna et al. 2019; Cha et al. 2020; Geng et al. 2020; Martinez-Corral et al. 2020). PROX1, GATA2, and TGF-β2 promote VEGFR3 expression in LECs. Neuropilin-2 directly interacts with VEGF-C and forms a complex with VEGFR3; PIK3CA phosphorylates AKT in response to VEGF-C signaling; VEGF-C promotes DLL4 expression in LECs, which in turn enhances the duration of VEGF-C signaling (Geng et al. 2020); angiopoietin-2 synergizes with VEGF-C signaling through unknown mechanisms (Zheng et al. 2014); YAP/TAZ are activated by VEGF-C to regulate the expression of numerous targets including PROX1, and PKD1 regulates LEC sprouting in response to VEGF-C signaling (Coxam et al. 2014; Outeda et al. 2014). Finally, S1PR1 antagonizes VEGF-C/VEGFR3 signaling. Consequently, mice lacking S1PR1 in LECs have hyperplastic lymphatic vasculature (Geng et al. 2020).

The Hennekam syndrome–associated protocadherin FAT4 is a target of GATA2 that is necessary for LECs to polarize and align in the direction of lymphatic vascular growth (Alders et al. 2014; Betterman et al. 2020). The dermal lymphatic vessels of mouse embryos lacking FAT4 are dilated and mispatterned (Pujol et al. 2017; Betterman et al. 2020). Mice lacking Foxc2 also have dysplastic dermal lymphatic vessels (Petrova et al. 2004). Whether FAT4 and FOXC2 are related to VEGF-C/VEGFR3 signaling is currently unknown.

In summary, the lymphedema-associated molecules VEGFC, VEGFR3, GATA2, FOXC2, FAT4, and angiopoietin-2 are necessary for lymphatic plexus formation.

After populating the tissues and organs the primitive lymphatic plexus undergoes maturation into lymphatic capillaries and collecting lymphatic vessels. The lymphatic plexus is composed of zipper-like junctions with uniform expression of PROX1, VEGFR3, and LYVE1 (Norrmén et al. 2009; Yao et al. 2012). The expression of these LEC markers is maintained in portions of the plexus that differentiate into lymphatic capillaries. However, the zipper-like junctions are converted into button-like junctions to allow for the entry of lymph into the lymphatic capillaries.

Zipper-like junctions are maintained in portions of the lymphatic plexus that become collecting lymphatic vessels. However, PROX1, VEGFR3, and LYVE1 are down-regulated, a thin layer of basement membrane is established, and specialized vascular smooth muscle actin+ cells known as LMCs are recruited to collecting lymphatic vessels. LMCs have contractile properties, and they pump lymph against gravitational and hydrostatic pressure gradients.

FOXC2, GATA2, epsins 1/2, and angiopoietin-2 are some of the molecules that are required for lymphatic vascular maturation (Gale et al. 2002; Norrmén et al. 2009; Liu et al. 2014; Kazenwadel et al. 2015; Frye et al. 2018; Mahamud et al. 2019). Among the molecules FOXC2, GATA2, and angiopoietin-2 are associated with lymphedema.

With few exceptions, LVs are nearly identical to LVVs with respect to their structure and the molecules that are required for their development (Bazigou et al. 2009; Norrmén et al. 2009; Geng et al. 2016). The lymphedema-associated molecules PIEZO1, PROX1, FOXC2, GATA2, FAT4, and ITGA9 are necessary for the development of LVs (Petrova et al. 2004; Norrmén et al. 2009; Cha et al. 2016; Geng et al. 2016; Choi et al. 2019). Wnt/β-catenin signaling, YAP/TAZ, ANGPT2, FOXP2, and BMP9 are additional molecules that are necessary for LV formation (Gale et al. 2002; Dellinger et al. 2008; Levet et al. 2013; Cha et al. 2016, 2018, 2020; Hernández Vásquez et al. 2021). Among these molecules, ANGPT2 and FOXP2 are not necessary for LVV development (Mahamud et al. 2019; Hernández Vásquez et al. 2021). The transcription factor FOXO1 inhibits the expression of FOXC2 and GATA2 to prevent LV formation (Scallan et al. 2021). Deletion of Foxo1 from LEC results in an increase in the number of LVs per vessel.

In summary, numerous molecules are necessary to coordinate the stepwise development of the lymphatic vasculature. Mutations in some of these genes are observed in patients with lymphatic anomalies. While some molecules regulate just one of the developmental events, other molecules are used repeatedly. Additional studies are required to dissect the functions of molecules that are used in an iterative manner.

Signaling pathways are attractive targets for therapeutic intervention. Biochemical assays have revealed the mechanisms of action of many molecules that regulate lymphatic vascular development. These mechanistic studies have revealed receptor tyrosine kinases and shear stress signaling pathways as the most critical for lymphatic vascular development (Figs. 4 and 5).

VEGFR2, VEGFR3, TIE2/TEK, EPHB4, and FGFR1/3 are the receptor tyrosine kinases that are known to operate in LECs. VEGF-C can activate VEGFR2 and VEGFR3. Angiopoietins 1/2, ephrin-B2, and FGF2 are the ligands of TIE2/TEK, EPHB4, and FGFR1/3, respectively. VEGF-C/VEGFR3 signaling is necessary for every step of lymphatic vascular development and maintenance. TIE2 and angiopoietin-2 are necessary for the growth and maturation of lymphatic vessels and for the formation of LVs. FGF signaling regulates LEC metabolism (Yu et al. 2017). EPHB4 is necessary for LVV and LV development and its ligand ephrin-B2 is necessary for the maturation of lymphatic vessels and LV formation (Mäkinen et al. 2005; Martin-Almedina et al. 2016; Frye et al. 2020).

Receptor tyrosine kinases typically activate RAS/MAPK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathways to promote growth, migration, and proliferation of cells. Hyperactivating mutations in RAS and PI3K pathways are commonly observed in various lymphatic anomalies (Figs. 2 and 4). Mechanistically, these mutations likely result in an increase in lymphatic vascular density. RAS and PI3K pathways can also play additional roles. For example, hyperactivation of MAPK signaling in a mouse model of Noonan syndrome (RAF1S259A) resulted in the up-regulation of SOX18 and PROX1 expression in all endothelial cells (Deng et al. 2013). By inhibiting MAPK signaling RASA1 promotes the proper folding and secretion of collagen IV (Chen et al. 2019, 2020). RASA1 could also interact with EPHB4 and inhibit mTOR signaling in endothelial cells (Kawasaki et al. 2014). We speculate that EPHB4/RASA1 complex antagonizes RAS/PIK3CA interaction that is necessary for lymphatic vascular growth and patterning (Gupta et al. 2007). MAPK signaling downstream of FGFR1/3 activates glycolysis in LECs (Yu et al. 2017).

Lymph flow generates a frictional force known as shear stress on the luminal surface of LEC (Geng et al. 2021). Depending on the pattern of lymph flow shear stress can either be laminar or oscillatory. In linear portions of the lymphatic vasculature LECs experience laminar shear stress (LSS) that is characterized by nonturbulent flow in a single direction. In contrast, oscillatory shear stress (OSS) is experienced by LECs that are located at branch points and at valve sinuses where the intensity and direction of lymph flow change periodically. LSS and OSS regulate lymphatic vascular development by activating unique pathways (Fig. 5A,B).

LSS appears to be critical for the growth and patterning of lymphatic plexus. VEGFR3 functions as a LSS sensor in LECs. LSS triggers the autophosphorylation of VEGFR3 and enhances the synthesis and secretion of VEGF-C from LECs (Choi et al. 2017a). Furthermore, LECs that are cultured in the presence of LSS are more sensitive to VEGF-C when compared to statically cultured LECs. Consequently, phosphorylation of ERK and AKT is significantly higher in LECs cultured under LSS. S1PR1 specifically antagonizes the VEGF-C signaling that is enhanced by LSS (Geng et al. 2020). As mentioned previously, deletion of S1PR1 promotes excessive sprouting from the lymphatic plexus.

LSS also inhibits Notch signaling that plays a dual role in lymphatic vascular growth. LSS inhibits and restricts the expression of the Notch ligand DLL4 and therefore Notch signaling to tip cells (Geng et al. 2020). DLL4 sustains VEGF-C signaling in tip cells and promotes lymphatic vascular growth and patterning (Geng et al. 2020). Additionally, LSS promotes the expression of DLX1 and DLX3L, which are E3 ligases that cleave the Notch1 intracellular domain (NICD) (Choi et al. 2017b). Down-regulation of Notch signaling by LSS through this mechanism promotes LEC proliferation as overexpression of NICD results in a reduction in LEC numbers (Murtomaki et al. 2013; Choi et al. 2017b).

OSS appears to be critical for the development of valves (Fig. 5B). PIEZO1 and VE-cadherin are important for sensing OSS (Choi et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2019). The expression of FOXC2 and GATA2 is regulated by OSS in a PROX1-dependent manner (Sabine et al. 2012; Cha et al. 2016, 2018; Choi et al. 2019). HDAC3 is necessary for the expression of GATA2 in response to OSS (Janardhan et al. 2017). Wnt/β-catenin signaling is another critical regulator of FOXC2 and GATA2 that is activated in LECs by OSS or by Wnt ligands secreted by mural cells (Cha et al. 2016, 2018). An exciting new study has identified the transcription factor FOXO1 as an inhibitor of FOXC2 and GATA2 expression (Scallan et al. 2021). OSS promotes the nucleus to cytoplasmic translocation of FOXO1 thereby de-repressing the expression of FOXC2 and GATA2.

Targets of FOXC2 and GATA2 such as connexin-37, miR-126, ephrin-B2, and integrin-α9 are also necessary for valve morphogenesis (Kanady et al. 2011; Sabine et al. 2012; Sweet et al. 2015; Mahamud et al. 2019). Ca2+ ion flow between LECs that is mediated by connexin-37 promotes the nuclear translocation of NFATC1 (Sabine et al. 2012). NFATC1 forms a complex with FOXC2 to regulate the expression of target genes (Norrmén et al. 2009).

Numerous drugs are available to target receptor tyrosine kinase signaling (see below). However, approaches to mimic shear stress signaling, such as chemicals that could enhance the expression of shear stress responsive molecules like FOXC2, are yet to be discovered. When they become available, shear stress mimics could be used to promote lymphatic vessel growth and valve formation.

Surgery, sclerotherapy, and laser ablation are some of the standard approaches for treating nontruncal lymphatic malformations. The identification of activating mutations in RAS and PI3K pathways in patients has led to the development of mouse models and targeted therapies. For example, LEC-specific expression of PIK3CAH1047R, a hyperactive mutant form of PIK3CA that is frequently observed in lymphatic anomalies, resulted in cystic LMs in mice (Rodriguez-Laguna et al. 2019; Martinez-Corral et al. 2020). The lymphatic capillaries of mutant mice were hyperplastic in which the expression of VEGFR3 and NRP2 was increased. Furthermore, the lymphatic capillaries abnormally expressed zipper-like junctions in place of button-like junctions, presumably resulting in a reduction in lymph absorption and drainage. Importantly, VEGF-C trap and mTOR inhibitor rapamycin could dramatically reduce lymphatic vascular hyperplasia and enhance lymphatic drainage. Consistent with these findings, rapamycin provides significant relief of symptoms in human patients with cystic LM and GLA (Mäkinen et al. 2021). However, patients with PROS are less responsive to rapamycin treatment. In a small clinical trial, an FDA-approved AKT inhibitor Alpelisib appears to improve the symptoms of patients with PROS (Mäkinen et al. 2021). It will be important to determine whether additional approaches such as VEGF-C trap, and Angiopoietin-2 trap can be used to improve the clinical outcome.

Expression of KRASG12V in murine LECs recapitulated the phenotype of GSD and resulted in the appearance of lymphatic vessels in the bones (Homayun-Sepehr et al. 2021). Additionally, lymphatic drainage was compromised in these mice due to fewer LVs and LMCs. Consequently, most mutant mice succumbed to chylothorax before 1 month of age. The MAPK inhibitor trametinib prevented LV degeneration in KRASG12V-expressing mice. Consistent with these results trametinib provides dramatic reduction in hyperactivation of RAS signaling in human patients. Specifically, trametinib improved the symptoms of a CCLA patient with germline ARAS (p.S214P) mutation and a KLA patient with a somatic truncating mutation in CBL (Li et al. 2019; Foster et al. 2020).

Unfortunately, the treatments for lymphedema are limited to compression bandages and massages. A deeper understanding of the VEGF-C and shear stress signaling pathways could lead to targeted approaches to treat lymphedema. In line with this expectation, recently, it was reported that the inhibition of PTEN was able to promote the growth of functionally normal lymphatic vessels (Kataru et al. 2021). Similarly, inhibition of FOXO1 resulted in the rescue of LV defects that are normally observed in Foxc2+/− mice (Scallan et al. 2021).

Advances in genomic technology have led to the identification of numerous genes and mutations that are associated with lymphatic anomalies.

VEGF-C and shear stress signaling pathways are critical regulators of lymphatic vascular development.

Inhibition of PI3K and/or MAPK signaling pathways can significantly ameliorate lymphatic anomalies such as cystic LM, GLA, GSD, and CCLA.

Targeted therapies for primary lymphedema are urgently needed.
