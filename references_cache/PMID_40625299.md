BACKGROUND: Intracranial solitary fibrous tumors (SFTs) and meningiomas are 
meningeal tumors with different malignancy levels and prognoses. Their similar 
imaging features make preoperative differentiation difficult, resulting in high 
misdiagnosis rates. Thus, accurately distinguishing SFTs from meningiomas 
preoperatively is vital for surgical planning and treatment strategies.
PATIENTS AND METHODS: A total of 252 patients (56 SFTs and 196 meningiomas) data 
from January 2014 to May 2024 were used to train our models . A VB-Net deep 
learning network was employed to refine automatic segmentation. To identify SFTs 
and meningiomas, the segmented data were analyzed using machine learning to 
construct single-sequence and multi-sequence MRI models and combined with 
clinical/radiological features to develop a fusion index-related model.To 
enhance clinical applicability, we constructed a four-category model using the 
predictive probabilities from secondary classification as input features.
RESULTS: The VB-Net segmentation model performed well in both the tumor cores 
and the whole tumor, with DSCs of 0.87 (±0.17) and 0.79 (±0.26), respectively. 
The integration of clinical and radiological data enhanced the model's AUC to 
0.957. Stratified analysis showed that the weighted AUC value reached 0.846 in 
the validation set.
CONCLUSION: The comprehensive system integrating automatic segmentation with 
diagnostic models can differentiate SFTs from meningiomas precisely.

Intracranial solitary fibrous tumors (SFTs) originating from the perivascular mesenchyme of the meninges are rare and invasive tumors [1,2]. Given their overlapping molecular characteristics, the mixed term ‘solitary fibrous tumor/hemangiopericytoma’ was revised to ‘solitary fibrous tumor’ in the 2021 classification of central nervous system tumors [3]. Due to the high invasiveness, frequent recurrence, and high intraoperative bleeding probability of SFTs, once diagnosed, it requires active preoperative preparation, development of maximal surgical resection plans, and rational postoperative treatment [4,5]. Meningiomas, on the other hand, are common tumors with clinical and radiological features similar to SFTs. In the 5th edition of the WHO classification of central nervous system tumors, meningiomas are divided into three main grade categories (WHO grades 1-3), involving 15 different histological subtypes, and most WHO grade 1 meningiomas are benign and have a good prognosis [3]. Among them, angiomatous meningiomas (AM), with their rich blood supply similar to SFTs, are particularly challenging to differentiate from SFTs both radiologically and histologically [6,7]. However, accurately distinguishing AM, SFTs, and other meningioma subtypes preoperatively is crucial, as they exhibit significant differences in biological behavior, treatment strategies, and prognosis. For instance, compared to meningiomas, SFTs often require more extensive surgical resection and may respond differently to adjuvant therapies such as anti-angiogenic agents [8,9]. Additionally, AM, as a WHO grade 1 meningioma, typically has a lower recurrence risk than SFTs, highlighting the importance of precise diagnosis for tailored follow-up plans. Misdiagnosis could lead to inappropriate treatments, such as insufficient surgical margins for SFTs, which may cause recurrence, or unnecessary radiotherapy for meningiomas, exposing patients to avoidable side effects and risks [6,10], emphasizing the clinical significance of preoperative differentiation in optimizing patient outcomes.

Although studies have reported that conventional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) features, including dural tail sign, peritumoral edema, and bone invasion, have achieved certain effectiveness in distinguishing intracranial SFTs from meningiomas, the selective bias introduced by these qualitative analyses has increased the misdiagnosis rate [11,12]. In recent years, the emergence of multimodal functional MRI techniques, such as the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) value in diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and myoinositol concentration in magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging, have allowed for a more systematic and in-depth assessment of the subtle biological characteristics of tumors and provided quantitative analysis for distinguishing these two brain tumors [13–16]. However, due to small sample sizes, these studies have only demonstrated simple correlations and their clinical utility is limited. Based on conventional MRI, artificial intelligence, such as deep learning and machine learning algorithms, can extract complex visual features directly from the imaging data, thus improving the diagnosis of this rare disease with lower economic costs [17–20]. Chen et al. [21] combined radiological features with deep learning models to distinguish SFTs from meningiomas and achieved a higher area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC), demonstrating the potential of deep learning models in distinguishing these two tumors. Automatic segmentation techniques have been effectively applied to outline brain tumors [22,23]. Rui and colleagues [24] trained a segmentation network using the BraTS dataset, which offered a vast collection of precisely annotated images, achieving satisfactory outcomes. Building on this, our recent research enhanced the pre-trained model with transfer learning, successfully achieving high-precision segmentation for MRI images of pituitary tumors [25]. Yet, delineation of regions of interests (ROI) for SFTs and meningiomas were still relying on manual segmentation in these studies, which is biased and laborious. Therefore, automatic ROI segmentation from MR images is required in clinical practice.

In this study, to achieve precise preoperative discrimination between SFTs and meningiomas, a comprehensive system integrating an automatic segmentation module with an automatic diagnostic module was developed. The potential of deep learning-driven automatic segmentation was assessed as a viable alternative to manual segmentation techniques. Subsequently, a machine learning diagnostic model based on MRI radiomics signatures along with clinical and radiological data was employed to distinguish SFTs from meningiomas. To further align with clinical needs, we extended our differentiation to include SFTs, WHO grade 1 and grade 2 meningiomas, as well as AM. The ultimate goal is to assist clinicians in formulating personalized surgical strategies and evaluating associated surgical risks.

From January 2014 to May 2024, we collected MRI and clinical data from 478 patients with histopathologically confirmed intracranial SFTs or meningiomas from four clinical centers (A, B, C, and D). All patients had MRI of the brain before surgery. The inclusion criteria were given as follows: (1) pathologically confirmed SFTs or meningiomas according to the 2021 WHO classification criteria; (2) a preoperative MRI examination that included conventional T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2-Flair), contrast-enhanced T1WI (CE-T1WI), and DWI sequences; and (3) patients with no history of brain tumors. We excluded 30 patients due to the following reasons: (1) incomplete patient clinical information; (2) low-quality MRI (serious motion, aliasing, or rippling artefacts); and (3) incomplete MRI scan sequences.

In this study, a total of 252 cases were utilized for the training of the classification model, while 196 cases were employed for the fine-tuning of the segmentation network.

MRI data were collected retrospectively from a picture archiving and communication system and performed at different sites using different MRI providers and various acquisition parameters. The detailed parameters of MRI acquisition are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

The overall study design is shown in Figure 1. Considering the inherent attributes of MRI, we initially implemented bias magnetic field correction for T1WI, T2WI, T2-Flair, CE-T1WI, and DWI sequences employing the N4 algorithm built into the ANTs suite. This step was undertaken to mitigate the artifacts attributable to magnetic field inhomogeneity. Subsequently, the brain tissue regions within the above images were isolated utilizing the skull-stripping functionality of the uAI toolkit [26], aimed at minimizing potential confounding effects emanating from the cranial vault. Following these preparatory processes, the gray-scale intensity values of the extracted brain tissue were normalized to a range of [0, 1]. Each image was cropped to a size of [128, 128, 128] voxels. This standardization facilitated uniform input dimensions for our model. These meticulously pre-processed images were then fed into the segmentation network to facilitate the delineation of the ROIs associated with the tumors. The specific process is shown in Figure 2.

The research workflow of image segmentation, feature extraction, and model-building steps. An automatic segmentation model was established based on CE-T1WI and T2WI images to extract the regions of interest (ROIs) in each slice. After three-dimensional reconstruction and repositioning of the ROIs, 1276 features were extracted, and the optimal features were selected using mRMR and LASSO methods. Clinical/radiological index-related model, machine learning index-related models, and fusion index-related model were developed using logistic regression and a five-fold cross-validation algorithm. The performance of all models was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC), as well as calibration and decision curves.

The process of image preprocessing. Based on CE-T1WI and T2WI images, the N4 bias correction technique is applied to the tumor core and the entire tumor area to correct for intensity inhomogeneities in the images. Subsequently, skull stripping is conducted, and the preprocessed images are ultimately input into the segmentation network.

Confronted with the difficulty of securing a large dataset of meningiomas and SFTs with lesion annotations, and considering the morphological similarities among various brain tumors, we initially developed deep learning-based segmentation models using the publicly available BraTS2021 dataset. With the VB-Net framework, we established a pre-trained model for segmenting the tumor core from CE-T1WI images and the whole tumor (including edema areas and tumor core) from T2WI images. Then, we utilized 196 cases of meningiomas from our collection to evaluate the pre-trained model. Upon review by two radiologists, the segmentation masks for the majority of cases demonstrated a relatively accurate localization of the lesions, which attests to the utility of the BraTS2021 dataset. However, it is undeniable that some segmentation masks still exhibited issues with boundary misalignments and misidentifications.

To develop a more accurate and broadly applicable segmentation model for meningiomas and SFTs, we initiated the process by enhancing our dataset. Two radiologists manually addressed segmentation discrepancies, resulting in a refined private dataset with 196 accurately labeled T2WI images and CE-T1WI images. Following the integration with the BraTS2021 dataset, we utilized an adaptive fine-tuning approach based on the pre-trained VB-Net model to develop and validate a novel segmentation model. The segmentation quality was assessed using the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), jaccard coefficient (JC), average surface distance (ASD), and root mean squared volumetrical difference (RSVD). The fine-tuned model will replace the pre-trained model for the automated delineation of regions of interest (ROIs).

In this phase, radiomics feature extraction was conducted utilizing the tumor core and whole tumor delineations derived from structural MRIs (T1WI, CE-T1WI, T2WI, T2-Flair, and DWI). which were aligned to CE-T1WI and T2WI with the rigid mode in ants-toolkit, respectively. Five distinct classes of features from each image-ROI combination were extracted with pyradiomics-package, including (1) First-Order Statistical Features: These features quantitatively describe the pixel intensity distribution within the ROI, providing insights into the radiographic texture of the tumor. (2) Shape Features: This category encompasses metrics that define the geometric contours and dimensions of the ROI, offering a morphological perspective of the tumor architecture. (3) Texture Features: These features, derived from methodologies such as the Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix, Gray Level Dependence Matrix, Gray Level Size Zone Matrix, Gray Level Run Length Matrix, and Neighboring Gray Level Difference Matrix, elucidate the intricate textural and patterned nuances within the image. (4) High-Order Statistics: These features capture complex statistical information beyond basic texture and shape characteristics, enhancing the descriptive power of the feature set. (5) Filter-Based Features: These features represent the image properties after the application of various filters, further characterizing the underlying image structure. Finally, 1276 imaging histology related features were obtained based on each ROI in each modality of the image.

Besides above mentioned radiomics features, we also utilize the general clinical information (age, gender) and the lesion features marked by the radiologist (dural tail sign, whether the tumor boundary is clear, and the presence of bone erosion, etc., as shown in Table 1) to construct machine learning models to discriminate whether it was a common meningioma or SFT. We categorized features into qualitative (clinical/radiological features, assessed by physicians) and quantitative (radiomics features, extracted from MRI images). Details were as follows: (1) Clinical/Radiological index-related model. This model included only qualitative features, combining radiological and clinical features. Ten clinical/radiological factors were analyzed, including age, gender, peritumoral edema, dural tail sign, venous sinus erosion, tumor borders, serpentine signal voids, bone invasion, cystic components, and heterogeneous enhancement [27–29]. All 10 case-report features were normalized by Box-Cox transformer respectively, then composed to map into probality of SFT with logistic regression and selected through a five-fold cross-validation process. (2) Machine learning index-related models. Taking into multiple sequence’s ROI and the number of features, we employed a five-fold cross-validation approach to apply mRMR (maximal relevance and minimal redundancy) and LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) algorithms for key feature selection within each sequence’s ROI. Thereafter, we utilized logistic regression to construct a single-sequence machine learning model for each MRI sequence and built multi-sequence machine learning models across multiple MRI sequences following feature reselection. The radiomics features selected by each model and their proportions are shown in Supplementary Figure S1–S6. (3) Fusion index-related model. Ultimately, we concated the case-report indexes and selected features from all radiomics features to fit logistic regression model parameters. The specific features are displayed in Figure 3. The final selection of 20 features accounted for less than 10% of the total sample size, which is a ratio widely accepted in machine learning to balance the risks of underfitting and overfitting. Despite this, the limited positive samples may still pose overfitting risks. To address this issue, we introduced dimensionality reduction techniques and applied five-fold cross-validation along with the LASSO algorithm to enhance the model’s generalizability and mitigate the risk of overfitting [30].

Student’s t-test was used for variables with a normal distribution, the Mann-Whitney U test for variables with a non-normal distribution, and the chi-square test for qualitative variables to analyze statistically significant differences. Statistical analysis was conducted using Python 3.10.6. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p-values lower than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

In this process, all data were randomly divided into five equal-sized subgroups for five-fold cross-validation. Each subgroup was regarded as a validation set and the remaining four-fifths of the cases as a training set. This process was repeated five times with different subgroups to form five training sets and five corresponding validation sets. When evaluating, all validation sets were statistics together.

Further differentiation of different classes of meningiomas (WHO grade 1, WHO grade 2, AM) from SFTs. We utilize the predicted probabilities from the binary classification as input features to further distinguish among the four types of tumors, including WHO grade 1 meningiomas, WHO grade 2 meningiomas, AM, and SFTs. After reassigning new labels to each sample, we repeat the model construction process as previously mentioned, ultimately achieving the results of the four-level classification.

To address the performance degradation attributable to the imbalance in the sample sizes of SFTs and meningiomas, our study employed the SMOTE (synthetic minority over-sampling technique) to equilibrate the dataset. Furthermore, we monitored the model’s accuracy, recall, and F1 score to ensure that the application of SMOTE did not precipitate overfitting.

Continuous variables with normal distribution were analyzed using Student’s t-test, while those with non-normal distribution were assessed with the Mann-Whitney U test to determine if there were statistically significant differences. Qualitative variables were examined using the chi-square test. The machine learning model’s performance was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, AUC, and decision curve analysis (DCA); and DeLong’s test was used for AUC comparison. The t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) was used to reveal clustering patterns of SFTs and meningiomas. The p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses and visualizations were conducted using the uAI-Research Portal (https://www.uii-ai.com/research.html).

This retrospective study investigates the application of multiparametere MRI-based deep learning segmentation and machine learning diagnostic models in the preoperative differential diagnosis of SFTs and meningiomas. We developed a tool capable of segmenting tumours on MRI, extracting features and performing differential diagnosis. The diagnostic models incorporates clinical/radiological factors and radiomics features to improve the accuracy of differential diagnosis and the efficiency of clinical workup. The AUC in the validation set was as high as 0.96, which can provide a real and consistent help for neuro-oncologists in identifying SFTs and meningiomas, and then, based on the classification results of the model, we established a four-level classification model. This model can effectively identify SFTs and subgroups of meningiomas. Among them, the AUC for both SFTs and WHO grade 1 meningiomas was as high as 0.92 in the validation set. Despite the inherent difficulty in distinguishing AM using conventional radiological characteristics, this model exhibited an AUC of 0.84 within the validation set, indicating robust performance.

In the past few years, clinical/radiological factors have been used to identify SFTs from meningiomas [16,21,31]. However, using demographic characteristics to diagnose SFT is associated with a higher error, and radiologists currently rely extensively on preoperative radiological factors to make the diagnosis [11,12]. In the present study, 10 clinical/radiological factors were analyzed, and clinical/radiological index-related model was developed. The results showed the AUCs of this model were 0.87 in the training cohorts, which was was similar to a previous study conducted by Xiao et al. [11]. On the one hand, due to the more invasiveness and richer blood supply of SFTs, some studies have reported that SFTs have more cystic components as well as enhancement of inhomogeneous enhancement [9,32,33], which reflects their high degree of malignancy and the necessity of surgical treatment. On the other hand, despite both SFTs and meningiomas being dural-based tumors, their differing origins and biological characteristics often result in a varied presentation of the adjacent dura mater. The wide-based dural tail sign is a characteristic feature often associated with meningiomas [11]. This also becomes a key factor in differentiating the two.

This study demonstrates the great potential of integrating multiparametric MRI data to differentiate SFTs from meningiomas by using the machine learning diagnostic models. A previous study developed a radiomic model named HMDT, integrating conventional MRI sequences, achieving an AUC of 0.91 in the validation set [9]. There have also been studies that successfully differentiated SFTs from meningiomas using a single DWI sequence [34,35]. In our model, DWI performed the best among the five individual MRI sequences in the tumor core, possibly due to the feature extraction in the tumor core focusing on its internal structure and molecular properties [36]. The DWI sequence reflects information about the tissue microstructure and is highly sensitive to cell density and compactness in the tumor core region [37–40]. Unlike meningiomas, which are composed of densely packed polygonal cells, SFTs are characterized by loosely arranged spindle-shaped cells and abundant staghorn vessels [41], leading to distinct DWI signal patterns for these two tumor types. Notably, in the tumor core, the model trained by combining DWI with four conventional sequences achieved the best performance among all machine learning models, with an AUC of 0.93. The DeLong test indicated significant differences in AUC between the models (p < 0.05). This suggests that the complementary information provided by different imaging sequences enhances diagnostic accuracy [42,43]. The whole tumor feature extraction focuses on overall morphology and the interface with edema. When extracting features from the whole tumor, the model trained on the T2-Flair sequence alone had the highest AUC value (0.84) compared with other individual sequences. This may be because the T2-Flair sequence enhances soft tissue contrast [36], aiding in the visualization of the tumor and its margins, and is particularly sensitive to edema and tumor boundaries in the whole tumor region. However, given the comparable edema profiles between meningiomas and SFTs [21], incorporating features that include edema and boundary information into the model did not enhance diagnostic efficiency. The DeLong test indicated no significant difference between the models.

To further improve performance of the distinction between SFTs and meningiomas, we built the fusion index-related model by integrating the best-performing clinical/radiological factors and radiomics features, which achieved an AUC of 0.96 (95% CI: 0.94–0.98). In the validation cohort, the fusion index-related model significantly improved the AUC compared with the Clinical/Radiological index-related model, and DCA demonstrated better benefits. Although the integration of clinical and radiological factors did not significantly enhance the AUC when compared with machine learning index-related models, the DeLong test showed no significant difference between the models (p > 0.05), it did indeed increase the accuracy of the results. This indicates that while quantitative machine learning models may play a more critical role in diagnosis, radiomics feature combining with qualitative clinical/radiological factors can lead to higher accuracy and greater clinical utility.

Our study introduced an advanced automatic segmentation model (Fine-tuned model) for all cases to streamline clinical processes and minimize subjective differences among observers. By initializing with pre-trained parameters and refining the model with meningioma data through adaptive fine-tuning, we have developed an automatic segmentation model better suited for meningiomas and SFTs. Similar research have already been initiated and have shown promising results [44–46]. Additionally, the accuracy of the segmentation was evaluated, and the results indicated an improvement in the model’s DSC value after fine-tuning. Since meningiomas and SFTs are blood-rich tumors, which have clearly defined boundaries after enhancement, while edema regions typically have unclear boundaries [2], the segmentation efficacy based on CE-T1WI (DSC = 0.87) was higher than that of T2WI (DSC = 0.79) in the validation set of private data.

While this study has yielded promising early outcomes, there are limitations to acknowledge. First, the SFTs sample size is modest, and to enhance the model’s robustness, it is imperative to broaden the sample size, aggregate more clinical and laboratory data, and utilize external datasets for reliability testing. Second, given the varying subgroups of both tumors that demand distinct preoperative and treatment protocols, our model currently only classifies certain meningioma subtypes. Ultimately, future work should incorporate prospective studies to affirm the efficacy and stability of the model.

Segmentation networks based on deep learning and diagnostic models based on multi-parametric MRI machine learning can accurately distinguish between meningiomas and SFTs. The four-category classification model also demonstrates stable performance for the challenging differentiation of AMs and SFTs, offering vital information for the development of subsequent treatment plans.
