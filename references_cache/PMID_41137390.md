# Development of an AAV-based gene therapy for the ocular phenotype of Friedreich's ataxia.
**Authors:** Tang H, Gupte S, Xu E, Calabro KR, Friend H, Crosson SM, Fajardo D, Kostamo Z, Zhang H, Peterson JJ, Lin F, Kozmik Z, Lutz CM, Boye SL, Boye SE
**Journal:** Mol Ther (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ymthe.2025.10.048](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ymthe.2025.10.048)

## Content

Friedreich's ataxia (FA) is a leading form of hereditary ataxia caused by autosomal recessive mutations in frataxin (FXN). GAA triplet repeat expansions lead to lower levels of FXN expression, abnormal influx of iron into mitochondria, and damage to the nervous system. Patients typically present before the second decade with loss of muscular function, speech impediments, and cardiomyopathy. At later stages, vision loss typically manifests. Work is under way to develop gene therapies that address the cardiac and CNS manifestations, but their routes of administration do not lead to efficient transduction of the retina. The purpose of this study was to develop a more direct approach for treating the ocular phenotype of FA, which includes loss of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer, optic nerve atrophy, and loss of visual field. We generated two novel conditional knockout (KO) models, mRx-Fxn KO and Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice, wherein Fxn is ablated in all retinal cells or RGCs, respectively, and showed that FXN deficiency led to retinal dystrophy in both models. Gene supplementation via intravitreal injection of a novel AAV2-based capsid carrying FXN partially preserved retinal structure and/or function in both models, establishing proof of concept for this therapeutic strategy.

Friedreich’s ataxia (FA) is an autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disorder that affects multiple organ systems, with an average age of onset in the first 2 decades of life. It is the most common hereditary ataxia, representing about 50% of all cases, and affects 1 in 20,000–1 in 50,000 people, predominantly of European descent.1,2,3 FA patients present with various complications, including cardiomyopathy, scoliosis, muscle weakness, speech impairment, and other systemic issues.4 The most frequent cause (accounting for 96% of patients) is homozygous guanine-adenine-adenine (GAA) trinucleotide repeat expansions in intron 1 of the frataxin gene (FXN) on chromosome 9q13-21.1, while the other 4% of patients are compound heterozygotes with one expanded allele and a deactivating point mutation on the other.4,5,6 Healthy individuals typically have fewer than 56 GAA repeats, whereas FA patients may have between 56 and 1,300, which results in reduced levels of FXN protein due to inefficient transcription. The number of GAA repeats is inversely correlated with FXN expression levels (65%–95% reduction compared to unaffected) but positively correlated with disease severity and age of onset.7,8,9,10,11,12,13,14

While the exact mechanisms are not fully understood, it is hypothesized that FXN plays an essential role in the maturation of iron-sulfur (Fe-S) clusters.15,16,17,18 Fe-S clusters are thought to be critical for oxidative phosphorylation and other vital mitochondrial functions supporting energy homeostasis. When FXN expression is reduced, Fe-S cluster assembly is disrupted and mitochondrial function is reduced. This is thought to result in Fe accumulation, elevated reactive oxygen species, and increased oxidative stress. This leads to impairment of adenosine triphosphate production, negatively impacting bioenergetic processes within the cell. While all tissues are affected, the damage is most profound in energy-demanding systems such as cardiac and other muscles, cerebellum, dorsal root ganglia, and the retina.6,19,20,21

A significant proportion (73%) of FA patients exhibit neuro-ophthalmologic abnormalities, including optic nerve atrophy and eye movement disturbance.22 Approximately 30% of FA patients experience retinal dystrophy by their second decade of life.23,24 Retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), which have a high mitochondrial content, are particularly affected by FXN deficiency, and their degeneration compromises optic nerve integrity. Alterations in visually evoked potentials (VEPs) correlate with gradual loss of retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) and are associated with delayed conduction, showing that the visual pathway is impaired by the same extensive process of axonal degeneration found throughout the nervous system of these patients. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) has been used in a limited number of studies to assess RNFL thickness in FA patients, and results show that thinner RNFL correlates with worse Friedreich’s Ataxia Rating Scale scores, further underscoring the link between RGC health and overall disease progression.9,23,24,25,26,27,28,29,30,31

Current treatment strategies for FA, including the only US Food and Drug Administration-approved therapy, omaveloxolone, primarily focus on managing symptoms rather than providing a definitive cure.32,33,34 Efforts are also under way to develop adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based gene therapies.35,36,37,38,39,40 Due to their associated risk of mortality, priority has been given to developing animal models and treatment strategies focused on the cardiac and neuronal symptoms of FA.41,42,43,44,45 For instance, conditional knockout (KO) mouse lines have been created that exhibit FXN deficiency only in the heart or only in proprioceptive neurons.39,40 Existing models and treatment strategies, while informative for understanding the impact of FXN deficiency in critical organs and their treatment, are insufficient for understanding the ocular manifestations of this disease or developing treatments for vision loss in these patients for the following reasons: (1) a retinal phenotype in existing models is either absent or has not been interrogated, and (2) while systemically delivered AAV vectors can target the heart and intrathecal/intracerebral injections can directly target the central nervous system these injection routes do not lead to efficient retinal transduction. Although vision loss is not a cause of morbidity, it significantly reduces patient quality of life.46 Therefore, the development of an animal model that faithfully mimics the ocular phenotype of FA and a more targeted treatment strategy for preventing vision loss in these patients remains a greatly unmet medical need.

In this study, we describe the development of two novel mouse models with retina-wide or RGC-only KO of Fxn, mRx-Fxn KO, and Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice, respectively. Using structural and functional outcome measures, we observed early-onset and progressive retinal dystrophy in both models. In mRx-Fxn KO mice, degeneration and dysfunction were observed in all retinal layers, whereas dystrophy was most pronounced in RGCs of Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice. To evaluate therapeutic intervention, we tested a novel AAV2-based vector carrying FXN for its ability to slow or halt disease progression following intravitreal injection in both models.47 Gene replacement resulted in robust FXN expression in treated retinas. In-life and postmortem analyses demonstrated that AAV-mediated FXN supplementation partially preserved retinal structure and/or function in these models, establishing proof of concept for this therapeutic approach.

Prior to generating retina-wide Fxn KO mice, we asked whether the presence of Cre in all retinal cell types would adversely affect retinal structure and/or function, thereby confounding experimental findings. This ensured that any phenotypic changes observed in the resulting mouse model were the result of FXN deficiency rather than Cre expression. In mice carrying one copy of the mRx-Cre allele (mRx-Cre+/−), Cre recombinase is controlled by regulatory sequences of the mouse Rx gene, one of the earliest determinants of retinal development and is therefore constitutively expressed in all retinal cell types.48 Mice were analyzed via OCT and full-field electroretinogram (ffERG) at postnatal day (P)30 and P180 and compared to wild-type (WT, C57BL/6J) controls. No differences in rod- or cone-mediated function were observed (Figures 1A–1D). Similarly, no changes in outer nuclear layer (ONL) thickness were observed between mouse lines out to P180 (Figure 1E). IHC was performed on retinas isolated from 30-day-old mRx-Fxn KO and control (Fxnflox/null) mice (Figure 2B). We demonstrated that FXN is expressed in the RGCs and photoreceptor inner segments of our controls (Figure 2B). In contrast, little to no FXN expression was observed in the mRx-Fxn KO retinas (Figure 2B). Thinning of all retinal layers, particularly the ganglion cell layer (GCL) and ONL, was also noted. The capillary-based Jess WB apparatus was utilized to evaluate FXN expression in retinas from P14 mRx-Fxn KO mice and age-matched control mice. HEK293 cells transfected with hFXN plasmid were included as a positive control. FXN was detected in all samples except for the mRx-Fxn KO retina. The presence of FXN in the heart but not in the retina of mRx-Fxn KO mice confirmed the specificity of the Fxn KO (Figures 2C and 2D).

Next, we assessed retinal structure and function in mRx-Fxn KO mice and age-matched littermate controls using OCT and ffERG, respectively (Figure 3A). Significant thinning of all retinal layers was observed as early as P14, with a reduction of 10%–30% in mRx-Fxn KO mice relative to the littermate controls. Degeneration progressed rapidly through P60, when thickness was reduced to between 59% and 70% of controls (Figures 3B–3D and S2). Consistent with the loss of structure, a profound loss of both rod- and cone-mediated function was observed (Figures 3E–3H). This loss was evident as early as P21, and by P60 ffERG responses were undetectable (Figures 3I and 3J). Hemagglutinin (HA)-tagged hFXN and GFP were packaged in the AAV.P2V1(YF+TV) capsid under the control of the ubiquitous chicken β-actin (CBA) or the neuronal-specific human synapsin 1 (hSyn1) promoter. We previously demonstrated that this capsid was superior to benchmark AAVs such as AAV7m8 in its ability to transduce the retina following intravitreal injection and escape preexisting neutralizing antibodies in human vitreous samples.47 We injected mRx-Fxn KO mice and littermate controls with AAV-FXN in one eye and AAV-GFP in the contralateral eye at P14 (Table S1).

Eyes injected with AAV.P2V1(YF+TV)-CBA-FXN-HA-WPRE (WPRE, woodchuck hepatitis virus post-transcriptional regulatory element) (AAV-CBA-FXN) (Figures S3A and S3B) and AAV.P2V1(YF+TV)-hSyn1-FXN-HA-WPRE (AAV-hSyn1-FXN) (Figures S3A′ and S3B′) exhibited high levels of FXN expression in the retina. Expression was observed in the cell bodies of all neuronal cell layers (GCL, inner nuclear layer [INL], and ONL). In contrast, AAV-GFP-injected controls showed negligible levels of FXN and exhibited more pronounced deterioration of retinal structure (Figures S3C and S3C′). Since control animals retain endogenous FXN, staining with anti-FXN antibodies resulted in signal emanating from both the RGCs as well as photoreceptor (PR) inner segments of mice treated with AAV-FXN (Figures S3D and S3D′). However, expression was higher in RGCs than PRs, a result attributed to the overexpression of AAV-mediated FXN in the inner retina. Indeed, HA staining of retinas from control mice injected with AAV-FXN showed that HA expression was limited to the RGCs, the inner plexiform layer (IPL), and the INL (Figures S3E and S3E′).

To evaluate whether FXN supplementation was therapeutic, mRx-Fxn KO mice were intravitreally injected with AAV-FXN, AAV-GFP, or vehicle at P14 (Figure S4A; Table S1). Significant preservation of all retinal layers was observed at 1-month post-injection (mpi) with AAV-CBA-FXN compared with eyes injected with AAV-CBA-GFP (p < 0.05). The RNFLs and ONLs in mice treated with AAV-CBA-FXN were 13% and 20% thicker than those treated with AAV-GFP, while the GCL+IPL was 28% thicker (Figure S4B). By 2 mpi, the continued retinal degeneration in this mouse model made it difficult to isolate individual retinal layers. As such, total retinal thickness was measured. AAV-CBA-FXN-treated retinas had significantly thicker (21%) retinas (p < 0.05) compared with AAV-CBA-GFP-injected controls (Figure S4C). Despite the observed improvements in retinal structure following gene replacement, AAV-CBA-FXN treatment did not positively affect either rod- or cone-mediated function in mRx-Fxn KO mice. There was no significant difference in ffERG amplitudes between the eyes treated with AAV-CBA-FXN and those treated with AAV-CBA-GFP (Figures S4D–S4F).

Similar results were observed in animals treated with AAV-hSyn1-FXN. Significant preservation of the RNFLs (14%) and GCL+IPLs (25%) was observed at 1 mpi with AAV-hSyn1-FXN compared to AAV-hSyn1-GFP-injected controls. However, there was no significant difference in the ONL thickness (Figure S4G). At 2 mpi, retinas in AAV-hSyn1-FXN-injected eyes were significantly thicker (42%) than those in controls (Figure S4H). While significant protection of retinal structure was observed in mRx-Fxn KO mice treated with either AAV-CBA-FXN or AAV-hSyn1-FXN vectors, this protection was incomplete, and degeneration continued (albeit at a slower rate) in the treated eyes. Some improvement in rod-mediated function was observed in AAV-hSyn1-FXN-treated mice at 1 mpi, but at no other time point were responses in AAV-hSyn1-FXN-injected eyes higher than controls (Figures S4I–S4K).

Use of the mRx-Fxn KO mouse was justified by the localization of endogenous FXN expression in mouse retina; the highest expression was seen in RGCs and photoreceptor inner segments of control mice and WT mice (Figures 2B and S5A). By driving Cre with mRx, we were able to knock out expression in all of these cells. However, clinical data clearly show that FXN deficiency primarily impacts RGCs in FA patients. Clinical or subclinical optic neuropathy is found in approximately two-thirds of people with FA.49 Alterations in VEPs correlate with the gradual loss of RNFL and are associated with delayed conduction, showing that the visual pathway is impaired by the same extensive process of axonal degeneration found far and wide in the nervous systems of these patients.30,50 In severe cases, with large triplet expansion, the visual disturbance is subacute/acute, resembling Leber’s hereditary optic neuropathy.23,27 This suggests that FXN may be differentially expressed in mouse vs. primate retinas. Additionally, there are anatomical differences in the abundance and organization of retinal cells in the murine and primate retina. The primate retina contains a fovea where the density of retinal cells, including RGCs, is highest. In contrast, the mouse retina has a relatively homogenous distribution. We evaluated FXN expression in the cynomolgus monkey (Macaca fascicularis), a non-human primate (NHP) species whose ocular physiology and morphology closely resembles that of humans.51 Indeed, FXN expression in the NHP was predominantly restricted to RGCs in both the peripheral and central retina (Figure S5A). The pattern of expression in NHPs is consistent with the clinical presentation of ocular disease in FA patients and suggested the need for a mouse model that displays a phenotype that is more restricted to RGCs.

To better model the ocular symptoms of FA patients, we generated a mouse in which Fxn was knocked out preferentially in RGCs, the Pou4f2-Fxn KO mouse (Figure S1C).52 Specificity was enabled by Pou4f2 (POU domain, class 4, transcription factor 2 gene, also known as Brn3b)-mediated Cre expression.52
Pou4f2 is critical for RGC differentiation and is expressed in the majority of RGCs. Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice harbor a global deletion of exon 2 on one Fxn allele and a single allele of floxed Fxn that is preferentially ablated in RGCs. IHC performed on retinal cross-sections isolated from 60-day-old Pou4f2-Cre mice revealed Cre recombinase expression predominantly in the GCL and to a lesser degree in the INL (Figure S5B).

A 6-month natural history study was conducted to characterize the retinal phenotype of Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice (Figure 4A). Degeneration was observed in the RNFL and the GCL+IPLs as early as P30 (Figures 4B and 4C). At P60, these two layers were significantly thinner than those in the controls. At P120, significant thinning of the INL, where the bipolar cells reside, was also observed (Figure 4D). Interestingly, the ONL was significantly thicker in Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice than in controls from P60 onward (Figure 4E). It is likely that this thickening is related to reactive gliosis of Müller glia in response to the underlying disease.53 In addition to the progressive retinal degeneration observed, Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice exhibited significant declines in retinal function. A significant difference in ffERG b-wave amplitudes between Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice and controls did not emerge until after 4 months of age (Figures 4F–4K). This finding is consistent with the preferential KO of Fxn in RGCs and the fact that ffERGs are a measure of photoreceptor and bipolar cell function. At P120, both rod and cone b-wave amplitudes began to decline and, at P180, were significantly reduced (49.5% and 79.5%, respectively) relative to controls (Figures 4G and 4I). These observations align with OCT findings of INL thinning in Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice starting at P120 (Figure 4D). Pattern ERG (pERG) measures electrical responses to contrast-reversing visual patterns and is used to isolate the function of RGCs. The P1 wave is predominantly driven by the ON RGC pathway, and the N2P1 wave is commonly associated with the OFF RGC pathway in mice.54,55 Both P1 and N2P1 amplitudes were significantly reduced in Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice compared to the littermate controls (Figures 4L–4N). The difference was significant for both parameters by P90. At P180, P1 amplitudes in Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice were reduced by 94.87% compared to littermate controls, and the N2P1 amplitude was reduced by 77.30%. These pERG results further confirm FXN deficiency in RGCs of this novel mouse model. In addition to our analysis of retinal structure and function, we evaluated visually guided behavior using optokinetic reflex testing (OKT). The main goals of this study were to (1) generate a mouse model that mimicked the retinal disease progression seen in FA patients and (2) establish proof of concept for an AAV-based gene supplementation approach that preserves retinal structure and function in this model. An ideal mouse model for FA would closely mirror the FA patient phenotype (slowly progressing loss of ganglion cells, RNFL, and vision) and genotype (GAA trinucleotide expansion).42,43,57,58 However, existing GAA repeat knockin/KO mice have been described with gait defects and some mild cardiac and neuronal symptoms, but largely no retinal defects have been reported.59,60,61 The sole FXN knockdown mouse model with mild retinal phenotype lacks reported functional deficits.62 Global FXN KO is embryonic lethal and, while existing conditional KO models have been designed to study various organs/tissues, none have focused on the retina.41,43,63 We aimed to fill this critical gap by establishing two novel, retina-focused, conditional KO mouse models in which to study the ocular manifestations of FA: mRx-Fxn KO and Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice. mRx-Fxn KO have a retina-wide KO of FXN and exhibit rapid and progressive degeneration of retinal structure and a loss of retinal function as measured by ffERG. While this mouse provided, for the first time, the ability to evaluate the impact of complete FXN loss on the retina, it did not replicate the slowly progressing retinal dystrophy seen in FA patients. This difference is likely owed to the complete KO of FXN in mRx-Fxn KO mice vs. its partial reduction in FA patients.

We developed a strategy for delivering functional human FXN to the retinas of these mice using AAV.P2V1(YF+TV), an AAV capsid which we have shown efficiently transduces the inner retina of mice and NHPs following intravitreal injection.47 Despite their aggressive phenotype, mRx-Fxn KO mice treated with AAV-FXN exhibited a slower rate of retinal degeneration compared with those injected with AAV-GFP. However, no significant preservation of retinal function was observed. This lack of functional rescue is likely attributed to the early, retina-wide depletion of FXN in these mice. Indeed, our natural history study revealed a marked decline in retinal function of mRx-Fxn KO mice as early as 21 days of age (the earliest time point assessed), suggesting that treatment at P14 was unlikely to confer a protective effect. Previous work suggests that the retina may be more sensitive to complete FXN deficiency than other tissues. For example, Perdomini et al. demonstrated that AAVrh10-FXN could prevent or reverse the onset of cardiac disease in a conditional mouse model with complete Fxn deletion in cardiac and skeletal muscle.40 Furthermore, Piguet et al. showed that although fully dysfunctional, proprioceptive neurons survive for several weeks in a conditional mouse model with complete Fxn deletion in parvalbumin-positive cells, and that AAV9-FXN treatment completely rescued sensory neuropathy.39 In contrast, complete Fxn deficiency in the retina results in early onset and progressive dysfunction/degeneration in mRx-Fxn KO mice.

The aggressive retinal dystrophy seen in mRx-Fxn KO mice provided a very limited treatment window in which to thoroughly evaluate the effects of gene replacement. To overcome these limitations and more closely mimic the disease progression seen in FA patients, we developed a second mouse model, the Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice. In Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice, Fxn is preferentially ablated in RGCs, allowing us to isolate and study the effects of FXN loss in a more clinically relevant retinal cell population. Indeed, Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice display a milder phenotype than mRx-Fxn KO mice. They exhibit progressive structural loss that is primarily limited to the inner retina until 6 months of age, when photoreceptor degeneration initiates. pERG revealed a loss of RGC function in these mice that became statistically significant at P90, and ffERG revealed later (P180) dysfunction of secondary-order neurons (reduced b-waves). Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice also exhibit a loss of visual guided behavior, as evidenced by a decreased OKT response at P60.

Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice provided a wider treatment window in which to evaluate the impact of AAV-mediated FXN replacement. Intravitreal injection of these mice with AAV.P2V1(YF+TV)-FXN led to significant preservation of RGC function compared to contralateral eyes that was evident by 2 mpi. Additionally, gene therapy led to significant preservation of RGCs as early as 1 mpi. Despite the observation of significant preservation of retinal structure and function in treated mice, no significant improvement was observed in visually guided behavior (data not shown). We speculate that the inability to restore visually guided behavior relates to the fact that RGCs in this mouse never expressed FXN and that our intervention was performed at P30. The absence of RGC signaling to the brain throughout early development may have limited the potential for behavioral recovery. Although pERG, OCT, and histological analyses reveal partial preservation of RGC function and structure, abnormal visual input may have impaired the development or stabilization of central visual pathways. As a result, the higher-order visual processing required for an OKT response could have been irreversibly compromised, despite rescue in the retina.64

The incomplete rescue observed in Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice could be attributed to (1) unsuitable levels of FXN expression and/or (2) the complete KO of FXN in RGCs during development. We plan to explore the first possibility in future studies via the use of alternative promoters and dose-range finding to identify optimal AAV-mediated FXN expression levels in RGCs. These experiments will incorporate dosing of WT or Fxn+/− mice to assess for the potential of overexpression toxicity, an issue that has arisen for gene replacement of FXN targeted to other tissues.65
Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice better mimic the disease progression in patients and therefore are an improvement over the mRx-Fxn KO mice, but there are several drawbacks: (1) FXN expression is completely absent in the majority of RGCs during development, and (2) they do not genotypically mimic the human condition. Thus, our results do not necessarily imply that a treatment effect in patients would be incomplete. In FA patients, a GAA repeat expansion reduces FXN expression in all retinal cells, which primarily affects RGC function, but does not abolish its expression entirely. As such, these patients maintain their ability to signal to higher-order visual processing centers until late in the disease progression. In contrast, Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice have complete KO of FXN in RGCs. A focus of our future studies is to characterize the ocular phenotype of a recently developed repeat expansion model, the YG8-800 mouse. These mice carry a global null allele for mouse Fxn alongside the human FXN YAC transgene that includes over 800 GAA trinucleotide repeats. In comparison to earlier triplet repeat expansion mice that did not display hallmark FA pathology, the YG8-800 mice do.66 However, no studies to date have focused on their retina/vision. This model has the potential to serve as a more clinically relevant system in which to evaluate therapies.

In summary, we generated two novel models, mRx-Fxn KO and Pou4f2-Fxn KO mice, that confirm that FXN deficiency leads to retinal dystrophy and showed that FXN supplementation via intravitreal injection of a novel AAV2-based capsid partially preserved retinal structure and/or function in both models. Our study establishes proof of concept for an intravitreally injected, AAV-based approach for addressing the retinal phenotype of FA and supports continued efforts toward the development of a gene therapy to target FA-associated vision loss.

Visually guided behavior was assessed using optokinetic reflex tracking (OKT) responses using the OptoMotry system (CerebralMechanics, Lethbridge, Canada) with a previously described method.71 In brief, each mouse was placed on a central platform and allowed to acclimate for approximately 1–2 min. A blinded observer manually recorded the presence or absence of head-tracking behavior in response to clockwise or counterclockwise rotation of the gratings. Each eye was tested independently; clockwise rotation was used to stimulate the left eye and counterclockwise for the right eye. The highest spatial frequency that elicited consistent tracking was recorded as the visual acuity threshold for each eye. Average thresholds were determined for each test group, and one-way ANOVA followed by a post hoc Tukey’s range test was used to analyze differences between groups. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

After fixation, the cornea, lens, choroid, and retinal pigment epithelium were carefully removed to reveal the neural retina. The neural retina was then flattened by four equidistant cuts (from the edge of the retina toward the optic nerve). Retinas were treated with VisUCyte antigen retrieval reagent-universal (catalog no. VCRS023, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Retinas were blocked with CytoVista blocking buffer (catalog no. V11307, Invitrogen) for 24 h in 4°C and then transferred directly into the solution containing rabbit anti-RBPMS antibody (GeneTex, catalog no. GTX118619) at 1:100 diluted in blocking buffer and incubated at 4°C for 48 h. After incubation, retinas were washed using PBS and placed into solution containing IgG secondary antibody Alexa Fluor 647 (donkey anti-rabbit, catalog no. A21207, Thermo Fisher) at 1:500 dilution in PBS for 24 h. Retinas were then washed and mounted on a microscope slide using ProLong Diamond Antifade mounting media (catalog no. P36970, Invitrogen) and allowed to dry in the dark for at least 2 h. Whole-mount images were obtained by using All-in-One fluorescence microscope (Keyence) with all exposure and gain settings remaining constant. RBPMS+ RGCs were counted using RGCode by the previously published method.56,73 For each group, the number of RBPMS+ RGCs per retina was averaged. Welch’s t test was used to determine differences between groups, and the significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Mice were sacrificed as described above. Retinal and heart tissues were isolated from mice and flash frozen in protease inhibitor. Radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer was used to perform protein extraction, followed by bicinchoninic acid assay assay to determine the total protein concentration of the samples. Due to the difference in endogenous FXN levels, 1 μg/μL total retinal protein or 0.25 μg/μL total protein from heart and transfected HEK293 cells was used for analysis with the 2- to 40-kDa separation module (catalog no. SM-W012-1, ProteinSimple, San Jose, CA), of the Jess capillary-based Simple Western system (ProteinSimple)., The protein concentrations in the WB were adjusted to allow better visualization of FXN in retina. The protocol was run according to the manufacturer’s recommendation using the RePlex module (catalog no. 043-828, ProteinSimple). FXN was detected using a mouse anti-FXN antibody (clone 4F9, MABN2313, EMD Millipore, Burlington, MA) at 1:1,000. TOM20 was used as a loading control and detected using a rabbit anti-TOM20 antibody (D8T4N, catalog no. 42406S, Cell Signaling Technology) at 1:100. FXN bands were visualized using the anti-mouse chemiluminescent detection system (catalog no. DM-002, ProteinSimple), and TOM20 was visualized using the anti-rabbit near-infrared detection system (catalog no. DM-007, ProteinSimple). The results were analyzed using the ProteinSimple Compass software. FXN expression was quantified and adjusted to TOM20 expression in the WT retina, followed by normalization to FXN.
