# Loss-of-function variants in ERF are associated with a Noonan syndrome-like phenotype with or without craniosynostosis.
**Authors:** Dentici ML, Niceta M, Lepri FR, Mancini C, Priolo M, Bonnard AA, Cappelletti C, Leoni C, Ciolfi A, Pizzi S, Cordeddu V, Rossi C, Ferilli M, Mucciolo M, Colona VL, Fauth C, Bellini M, Biasucci G, Sinibaldi L, Briuglia S, Gazzin A, Carli D, Memo L, Trevisson E, Schiavariello C, Luca M, Novelli A, Michot C, Sweertvaegher A, Germanaud D, Scarano E, De Luca A, Zampino G, Zenker M, Mussa A, Dallapiccola B, Cavé H, Digilio MC, Tartaglia M
**Journal:** Eur J Hum Genet (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41431-024-01642-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41431-024-01642-7)

## Content

Pathogenic, largely truncating variants in the ETS2 repressor factor (ERF) gene, encoding a transcriptional regulator negatively controlling RAS-MAPK signaling, have been associated with syndromic craniosynostosis involving various cranial sutures and Chitayat syndrome, an ultrarare condition with respiratory distress, skeletal anomalies, and facial dysmorphism. Recently, a single patient with craniosynostosis and a phenotype resembling Noonan syndrome (NS), the most common disorder among the RASopathies, was reported to carry a de novo loss-of-function variant in ERF. Here, we clinically profile 26 individuals from 15 unrelated families carrying different germline heterozygous variants in ERF and showing a phenotype reminiscent of NS. The majority of subjects presented with a variable degree of global developmental and/or language delay. Their shared facial features included absolute/relative macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, palpebral ptosis, wide nasal bridge, and low-set/posteriorly angulated ears. Stature was below the 3rd centile in two-third of the individuals, while no subject showed typical NS cardiac involvement. Notably, craniosynostosis was documented only in three unrelated individuals, while a dolichocephalic aspect of the skull in absence of any other evidence supporting a premature closing of sutures was observed in other 10 subjects. Unilateral Wilms tumor was diagnosed in one individual. Most cases were familial, indicating an overall low impact on fitness. Variants were nonsense and frameshift changes, supporting ERF haploinsufficiency. These findings provide evidence that heterozygous loss-of-function variants in ERF cause a "RASopathy" resembling NS with or without craniosynostosis, and allow a first dissection of the molecular circuits contributing to MAPK signaling pleiotropy.

Subject terms: Genetics research, Disease genetics, Clinical genetics

The term “RASopathies” refers to a group of clinically related genetic disorders caused by germline pathogenic variants in genes that encode components and regulators of the RAS-mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway [1, 2]. Noonan syndrome (NS), the most common and clinically variable phenotype among these conditions, is characterized by a distinctive facial gestalt (including relative macrocephaly, high forehead, hypertelorism, ptosis, downslanted palpebral fissures, epicanthal folds, low set/posteriorly angulated ears, short/broad nose with depressed nasal bridge, most commonly), short stature, congenital heart defects (CHD) (e.g., pulmonic stenosis and septal defects), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), variable developmental delay (DD)/intellectual disability (ID), and skeletal, lymphatic and ectodermal features [3, 4]. The disorder is largely transmitted as a dominant trait, and is genetically heterogeneous with more than ten genes implicated thus far [5].

Cranial sutures are essential structures that guarantee the physiological expansion of the cranial vault to accommodate the growing brain. The premature closure of one or more cranial sutures (i.e., craniosynostosis) is generally associated with compensatory cranial growth at other sutures resulting in skull deformation and variable dysmorphic craniofacial features [6]. However, craniosynostosis can also lead to clinically relevant complications, including increased intracranial pressure, impaired cerebral blood flow, airway obstruction, and compromised vision and hearing [7, 8]. Premature fusion of one or more cranial sutures is observed in a number of clinically and genetically heterogeneous syndromic disorders, including a group of diseases that are caused by upregulated function of cell surface receptors with tyrosine kinase activity belonging to the fibroblast growth factor receptor family (i.e., FGFR1, FGFR2, and FGFR3) [9]. In these disorders (i.e., Apert, Crouzon, Pfeiffer, Jackson-Weiss, and Muenke syndromes), dysregulation of intracellular signaling promoted by an altered FGFR function is mediated, at least in part, by the RAS-MAPK transduction pathway [10, 11]. Of note, craniosynostosis has occasionally been reported as an associated feature in individuals with molecularly confirmed NS or a related RASopathy. In these subjects, the premature closure of various sutures has been associated with pathogenic variants involving KRAS more frequently, and SHOC2, BRAF, RAF1, PTPN11, and HRAS less commonly [12–20].

The E26 transformation-specific (ETS) family of transcription factors are proteins functioning as effectors of the RAS-MAPK pathway. These proteins regulate the expression of “early response” genes and other functionally related genes by interacting with other transcription factors and cofactors at specific promoter regions [21]. ETS members can either positively or negatively control gene expression. Among them, ETS2 repressor factor (ERF), encoded by ERF (MIM: 611888), is a ubiquitously expressed transcription factor negatively controlling MAPK signaling. ERF was originally identified as a transcriptional repressor of ETS2 [22], which encodes a major effector of the extracellular signal-related kinases 1 and 2 (ERK2/MAPK1 and ERK1/MAPK3), the downstream tiers of the MAPK cascade. ERF is phosphorylated by ERK1/2, and its phosphorylated status determines the subcellular localization of the protein and its ability to compete with the other ETS proteins that positively modulate gene transcription [23]. ERF acts as a tumor suppressor, and contributes to control multiple developmental processes, including hematopoiesis, osteogenesis, and cranial development [24–26].

Heterozygous loss-of-function (LoF) variants of ERF cause a rare form of autosomal dominant craniosynostosis (CRS4; MIM: 600775) with either single (e.g., lambdoid, sagittal, metopic, and coronal) or multisuture involvement [25, 27–30]. A small number of missense substitutions affecting conserved residues of the protein have also been reported [25, 28]. In mice, craniosynostosis has been proved to be caused by ERF haploinsufficiency [25, 31]. Of note, a recurrent missense variant (c.266A>G, p.(Tyr89Cys)) in the same gene has been reported to underlie Chitayat syndrome (MIM: 617180) [32], a developmental disorder characterized by neonatal respiratory distress, mild facial dysmorphism, bilateral accessory phalanx with shortened index fingers, and ulnar deviation [33]. This specific genotype-phenotype correlation has been reported in 13 affected subjects from 9 unrelated families, none of whom showing craniosynostosis [32, 34–36].

Recently, a de novo truncating variant (p.(Arg183*)) in ERF has been reported in a single subject with craniosynostosis who presented with features suggestive of NS [37]. Here, we provide evidence of a consistent association between heterozygous LoF ERF variants and a phenotype clinically overlapping NS, based on the molecular and clinical data collected on 26 individuals from 15 unrelated families. We systematically assessed the clinical presentation of this previously unappreciated ERF-related phenotype. In the majority of affected individuals, mild DD/ID with speech delay was reported, and a short stature was noted. CHD does not represent a common feature of the disorder, while a facies resembling NS is largely present. Remarkably, craniosynostosis was documented in only 3 individuals, while dolichocephaly was reported in 10 subjects. Our findings support a clinical link between ERF haploinsufficiency and the phenotypic spectrum of RASopathies.

ES raw data were processed using an in-house implemented pipeline [38, 39], based on the GATK Best Practices. The UCSC GRCh37/hg19 genome assembly version was used as reference for reads alignment and variant calling. High-quality variants were annotated using public (dbSNP150 and gnomAD v.4) and in-house databases, and various tools as reported [38, 39]. Sequencing metrics are summarized in Supplemental Table 1.

Trio-based CES was performed on the generated libraries (Twist Bioscience) using a NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina). All coding exons ± 25 nucleotide-long intronic flanking stretches of the target genes (150X mean coverage) (RefSeq database) were scanned for variation. Reads were aligned to the GRCh37/hg19 human reference genome. The Dragen Germline Enrichment application, BaseSpace pipeline (Illumina), TGex software (LifeMap Sciences) and Geneyx Analysis tool (Geneyx Genomex) were used for variant calling/annotation, and phenotype-based prioritization of candidate genes.

Probands from families F8, F9, F11, F13–F15 were analyzed for RASopathy testing by targeted sequencing (SureSelect, Agilent) considering the coding sequence and ± 15 nucleotide-long adjacent intronic regions of an updated list of genes implicated in RASopathies. Based on the identification of pathogenic variants in ERF in families F1, F6, and F7 by ES, these panels were designed to include this gene and other putative candidate disease genes, in the frame of a shared research protocol (NSEuroNet consortium). Libraries were sequenced using a MiSeq/NextSeq550 apparatus (Illumina). Proper coverage of target regions (depth >30 reads) was attained. Variant filtering and prioritization considered their predicted functional impact and frequency using in-house and public databases (gnomAD v.4.0).

The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines were used for variant classification [40], taking into consideration the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD), Leiden Open Variation Database and ClinVar information.

Individuals were clinically assessed by experienced clinical geneticists/pediatricians. Detailed clinical data were collected for all subjects taking into account the major features and signs occurring in NS and related disorders. Evidence of craniosynostosis was also systematically verified based on the clinical records. To guarantee an unbiased and homogeneous clinical assessment of each enrolled subject, the clinical data were thoroughly reanalyzed (M.L.D. and M.P.). Clinical and genetic data were also collected from published papers reporting on subjects with pathogenic/likely pathogenic ERF variants. Presence/absence of features were scored only when clearly stated in the clinical reports or tables, and when representative pictures were available.

In 20 cases, the DeepGestalt algorithm from the Face2Gene tool v.19.1.7 [41] was used to unbiasedly analyze the facial gestalt using the available patients’ pictures (frontal images). We divided the cohort into two subgroups according to the age: subgroup 1 (children aged 0 to 12, 10 individuals) and subgroup 2 (adolescents and adults, 10 subjects).

Twenty-six individuals from 15 unrelated families were identified to carry rare/private ERF variants at the heterozygous state (Supplemental Fig. 1). Nine different variants were found, including 3 nonsense, and 6 frameshift changes (Table 1). In 8 out of the 12 families in which co-segregation analysis was performed, the variant was transmitted from a clinically affected parent to one or two affected children, while the remaining four subjects (#501, #701, #1001 and #1101) were sporadic cases and the variant was confirmed to be a de novo event. All variants but one (c.1551del, p.(Pro518Leufs*13), parental DNA samples not available for segregation analysis) were classified as pathogenic/likely pathogenic according to the ACMG criteria (Table 1). One recurrent out-of-frame deletion (c.1201_1202del, p.(Lys401Glufs*10); NM_006494.4, NP_006485.2) was identified in affected individuals from five unrelated families (individuals #501, #701, #801, #802, #1401 and #1501), and a nonsense substitution (c.697C>T, p.(Arg233*) was identified in three families segregating the variant (F1, F6 and F12). Five variants, including the recurring c.697C>T nonsense change and c.1201_1202del frameshift, had previously been reported in dbSNP and ClinVar, while the other variants (c.11del, p.(Pro4Argfs*73); c.136dup, p.(Ile46Asnfs*16); c.1509del, p.(Phe504Leufs*27); c.1551del, p.(Pro518Leufs*13)) were novel (Table 1). The occurrence of additional functionally relevant variants involving genes implicated in RASopathies (all cases) and other Mendelian disorders based on the expected inheritance model and clinical presentation (families F1-F7, F10 and F12) was excluded.

Twenty-four subjects showed relative or absolute macrocephaly. Among them, 16 individuals had an occipitofrontal circumference within the normal range (within ±2 SD) but yet disproportionately large compared to their stature (relative macrocephaly); 8 individuals were characterized by absolute macrocephaly. The occurrence of craniosynostosis was definitely documented by brain MRI or CT in only 3 unrelated individuals (#402, #601 and #1401), who presented with a premature closure of the lambdoid, sagittal and metopic sutures, respectively, which required surgical correction during infancy. In addition, 10 subjects were dolichocephalic at clinical evaluation, and 1 individual showed positional plagiocephaly. In subject #401, MRI excluded occurrence of craniosynostosis; instrumental imaging was not performed to investigate any possible premature closure of cranial sutures in the other individuals.

Brain MRI was available for 12 individuals. No gross structural defects were documented with the exception of a Chiari type 1 malformation, a relatively common finding in Costello syndrome but also reported in other RASopathies [42], which was reported in two individuals, and other unspecific findings (i.e., mild enlargement of the lateral ventricles, mild ectasia of the perivascular spaces, nonspecific white matter abnormalities and mild hydrocephalus), which were described in five individuals. The neurocognitive phenotype of the subjects was variable in terms of severity. Hypotonia, global DD, speech delay or learning difficulties (dyscalculia, dysgraphia), which resolved over time in most of the cases, were consistently though variably present in 19/26 individuals. Five subjects presented with behavioral problems (hetero-aggressive outbursts, ADHD, anxiety, sleep disorder). ID was reported only in four individuals (mild cognitive impairment in three subjects, and moderate in one subject), though borderline intellectual functioning was appreciated in additional eight individuals. Epilepsy was reported in five subjects.

Ocular abnormalities were present in 11/20 individuals, with astigmatism and low-grade myopia reported in 5 and 2 subjects, respectively.

Minor skeletal defects were common, with mild brachydactyly occurring in 11/25 subjects and chest abnormalities (pectus excavatum/carinatum) reported in 9/23 individuals. Cubitus valgus was described in six subjects, while scoliosis/kyphosis was present in six individuals. Fourteen subjects had large and broad halluces.

Intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) was documented in 6/24 subjects, including one associated with small placental abruption (#701), while postnatal growth deficiency (below or equal the 3rd centile) was observed in approximately half of the affected individuals (15/26). Six individuals reached a stature between the 3rd–25th centile range.

Among the skin and ectodermal derivative features typically occurring in RASopathies, a relatively high prevalence of increased pigmentation/dark skin (10/26), multiple melanocytic nevi (13/24), café au lait spots (6/24), medially sparse eyebrows (6/26), and thin/curly hair (11/26) were reported.

While the overall phenotype of these subjects appeared variable in terms of presentation and severity, it consistently included a facial gestalt suggestive of NS or a related RASopathy. These subjects presented with a high forehead (16/26), hypertelorism (21/26), palpebral ptosis (16/26), downslanted palpebral fissures (16/26), low-set/posteriorly angulated ears (21/26), a wide depressed nasal bridge (17/26), thick lips and/or macrostomia (20/26). The craniofacial appearance and co-occurrence of a short/webbed neck (15/26), low posterior hairline (8/26), and reduced postnatal growth substantiated a clinical suspicion of NS in all subjects. In one affected member of F3 (#301), the overall severe phenotype characterized by moderate ID, coarse face, and skin features (deep palmar and plantar creases, sporadic café-au-lait spots, and keratosis pilaris) was suggestive of cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome (MIM: 115150), while a mitigated phenotype characterizing her affected brother and father was rather suggestive of NS. Of note, subject #301 was the only individual of the cohort who was diagnosed with cancer (unilateral Wilms tumor diagnosed at 4 years of age).

No typical CHD (e.g., pulmonic stenosis and septal defects), HCM, bleeding diathesis, lymphedema, or genitourinary abnormalities were reported with the exception of two individuals presenting with bilateral hydrocele and cryptorchidism, the latter requiring surgical correction.

Facial photographs were available for 20 subjects of the cohort (Fig. 1, frontal images plus photograph of individual #103). Facial analysis by Face2Gene (FDNA, Boston, MA) (version 19.1.7), without providing any additional clinical and molecular information, ranked NS as the top match in 8/10 of subcohort 1 (0–12 years) and 9/10 in subcohort 2 (adolescences and adults) (Supplemental Table 3). In the latter, subject #801, NS scored as the second suggested diagnosis. Matches with NS were scored with “high” confidence in 10 (#101, #201, #202, #601, #602, #603, #802, #902, #1101 and #1201) and ”medium” confidence in 5 (#103, #401, #501, #801 and #1202). The analysis did not offer a “high/medium” confidence diagnosis in individuals #301, #302, #303, #402 and #901 for whom multiple “low” confidence proposed diagnoses were reported.

We systematically compared the main facial and clinical features suggestive of NS in the present series and in 36 previously published individuals carrying ERF LoF variants and reported to be affected by CRS4, whose clinical pictures were publicly available for evaluation (Supplemental Table 4 and Table 2) [25, 28–30]. Notwithstanding the selection bias associated with the two cohorts, our analysis confirmed a similar occurrence of the assessed NS-related features. Among the CRS4 series, a high prevalence of typical facial signs/features was observed (e.g., high forehead, hypertelorism, downslanted palpebral fissures, ptosis, thick lips/macrostomia, and low-set/posteriorly angulated ears). A similar prevalence was also scored for short/webbed neck and low posterior hairline. Some clinical features (e.g., DD/ID, speech delay, and relative macrocephaly) were recorded with different prevalence, which might be related to the biased clinical assessment of the individuals belonging to the CRS4 cohort. We could not perform a comparison for two main NS key signs, short stature and pectus abnormalities, as data on these two items were not systematically investigated in CRS4.

Here we provide evidence that germline-inactivating variants of ERF underlie a clinically variable neurodevelopmental condition characterized by a phenotypic overlap with NS in a significant proportion of affected individuals. The data collected, which refer to a relatively large and well-characterized cohort including 26 individuals from 15 unrelated families and derive from both subjective and objective (Face2Gene) analyses, support an association with facial features resembling NS (e.g., macrocephaly, broad forehead, hypertelorism, palpebral ptosis, wide nasal bridge, low-set/posteriorly angulated ears, thick lips/macrostomia), a variable degree of DD and/or language delay, and short stature. Notably, craniosynostosis was documented only in 3 subjects, and a dolichocephalic aspect of the skull was reported in 10 individuals, indicating that the premature closure of cranial sutures does not represent a constant feature of ERF haploinsufficiency.

Seven of the individuals of the present cohort (three families) were analyzed in the frame of a dedicated research project focused on RASopathies, and were identified among undiagnosed patients with features suggestive of NS in whom pathogenic variants in known RASopathy-associated genes had not been identified, which is indicative of a bias in enrollment. Similarly, 8 (six families) were analyzed using an expanded RASopathy gene panel because of a suspected diagnosis of NS (or a related RASopathy) based on clinical assessment. The remaining 11 individuals (6 families) were referred for diagnostic genetic testing and analyzed by CES. These subjects were enrolled in this study on the basis of the identified gene variants, ruling out any enrollment bias related to their clinical phenotype, which was retrospectively evaluated. Heterozygous truncating variants in ERF had previously been reported to cause CRS4, a form of isolated or syndromic craniosynostosis [25]. Thus far, more than 50 affected subjects have been described [25, 27–30]. These subjects belonged to cohorts of individuals selected based on their clinical features (i.e., genetically undiagnosed craniosynostosis), representing a bias in enrollment, as well. Our systematic assessment of the craniofacial characteristics and clinical features of the publicly available clinical pictures/data documented a significant phenotypic overlap with NS. Specifically, a facial gestalt suggestive of NS (i.e., palpebral ptosis, hypertelorism, downslanted palpebral fissures, low-set ears, and thick lips/macrostomia) was observed. We could not assess relative macrocephaly since height was not reported in the majority of the cases. Absolute macrocephaly prevalence was comparable in the two series (8/26 in present series vs 8/25 in CRS4). These findings, further emphasize the clinical overlap with NS characterizing subjects with LoF ERF variants. Of note, the individual previously reported with the c.1512del frameshift, predicting the same divergent C-terminus (p.Phe504LeufsTer27) identified in family F2, was reported to be associated with a syndromic condition with hypertelorism (no pictures available) and clinically suspected craniosynostosis [25].

In the present cohort, only three individuals were reported with a documented craniosynostosis. In two, the premature closure involved the lambdoid and sagittal sutures, which indeed are the most commonly involved cranial sutures in CRS4 [25]. In ten individuals, physical examination indicated a dolichocephalic aspect, and positional plagiocephaly was described in one subject. In all cases, surgical correction was not required, nor clinical complications were reported. In line with this finding, the single reported individual with craniosynostosis and a clinical diagnosis of NS associated with a de novo LoF variant in ERF showed a subclinical craniosynostosis for which surgical treatment was not required [37]. We hypothesize that an anticipated closure of the sagittal suture resulting in a “subclinical” craniosynostosis with no increased intracranial pressure or other complications might occur as associated feature in these subjects. Of note, craniosynostosis and dolichocephaly have also been reported among patients with NS or clinically related disorders caused by upregulated RAS-MAPK signaling [12–20, 43–45]. Further studies are required to exclude/confirm a causal link between a “clinically silent” craniosynostosis and dolichocephaly in these subjects.

Patients with CRS4 were reported with DD, which is consistent with the variable developmental impairment occurring in NS [3, 4]. Specifically, one third of the subjects had been reported to have behavioral or learning problems, particularly affecting concentration and language acquisition, and functional fine motor skills [25, 28, 46]. DD/ID and speech delay/learning difficulties were more represented in the present series. The majority of these subjects showed a mild neurodevelopmental disorder with speech delay that generally resolved over time. Based on our observations, it might be possible that the neurocognitive profile has not been properly or retrospectively investigated in CRS4. Overall, the collected data indicate that subjects with LoF ERF variants show a relatively mild neurodevelopmental phenotype compared to what is generally observed among RASopathies. In a significant proportion of previously described individuals with heterozygous LoF variants in ERF, short stature was also reported, though height was not reported in the majority of the CRS4 cases. The present cohort documents that a height below the 3rd centile is a major finding of the disorder. Other clinical features recurring in NS, including skin and cardiac involvement, were either not reported or investigated among CRS4 affected subjects. In the present series, typical NS cardiac involvement was not observed in any subject, while dark skin, multiple nevi, cafè-au-lait spots were relatively common findings. Finally, excluding the cranial bones, scanty data had previously been available on the prevalence of skeletal defects. We report that minor skeletal defects typical of NS are also found in subjects with LoF ERF variants. These features include chest abnormalities (pectus excavatum/carinatum), cubitus valgus, and scoliosis. Interestingly, mild brachydactyly and broad halluces, which are not recurrent features in RASopathies, but occur in CRS4 and other syndromes with craniosynostosis caused by activating variants in proteins functionally linked to the RAS-MAPK cascade (e.g., FGFR3 and TWIST), were reported in more than half of the present series, indicating a differential role of ERF in modulating the expression of a the large set of genes that are controlled by the MAPK pathway.

In NS, increased RAS-MAPK signaling can result from the upregulated activity of RAS proteins, enhanced function of upstream signal transducers, downstream effectors belonging to the MAPK cascade, or from the inefficient switch-off by feedback mechanisms [4]. ERF functions as repressor of MAPK signaling [23]. Specifically, in absence of stimulation, ERF shows a nuclear localization and acts as an immediate response factor to negatively modulate the transcription of genes implicated in cell proliferation. Following growth factor stimulation, the protein is phosphorylated by ERK1/2, which promotes its export to the cytoplasm and functional switch-off. Importantly, forced nuclear localization of ERF is able to suppress RAS-mediated cell transformation, indicating that functional inactivation of the repressor is a requirement for proper transduction of MAPK signaling [23]. Consistent with its negative regulatory role on MAPK signaling [47], ERF haploinsufficiency has been linked to MAPK signaling upregulation [25]. Previous studies have demonstrated that a decreased dose of ERF cause craniosynostosis in mice [31], which is partially rescued by MEK inhibitor treatment [48], in line with the well-established role of enhanced activation of the MAPK cascade in favoring the premature closure of cranial sutures [10, 11]. However, as a major signaling cascade controlling cell response to various stimuli, the RAS-MAPK pathway contributes to multiple cellular processes and developmental programs. The deep phenotypic characterization of the present cohort and our re-analysis of the previously published cases with heterozygous LoF ERF variants, consistently indicate that ERF participates in a molecular circuit that contributes to the control of multiple developmental processes mediated by the RAS-MAPK pathway that are altered in RASopathies, including craniofacial development, growth, and cognitive function. On the contrary, notwithstanding its apparently ubiquitous expression (Human Protein Atlas, https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000105722-ERF/tissue; GTEX, https://gtexportal.org/home/gene/ERF), this protein does not seem to contribute to the modulation of RAS-MAPK signaling in cellular processes with relevance in cardiac development and physiology. Of note, most cases were familial (8 families segregating the condition vs 4 families with sporadic occurrence), indicating an overall relatively low impact on fitness.

Considering the whole spectrum of the identified variants and the previously published, ERF LoF variants appear to be scattered throughout the entire coding sequence without obvious genotype-phenotype correlations. Among the novel variants, the frameshift c.1509del (p.(Phe504Leufs*27)) and c.1551del (p.(Pro518Leufs*13)) affect the repressor domain at the C-terminus. A different single base deletion at codon 504 (c.1512del) predicting the same divergent tail had previously been reported as disease-causing [25]. In case of a negligible impact of these variants on transcript/protein stability, this finding would suggest a requirement of this region for proper ERF function in the context of MAPK signaling. In contrast, a single recurrent missense variant in ERF (p.Tyr89Cys) has been described in Chitayat syndrome. The clinical phenotype markedly differs from individuals carrying ERF LoF variants [32–36]. The pathomechanism underlying Chitayat syndrome is still uncharacterized, though this residue is highly conserved among orthologues and paralogs and directly contributes to the intermolecular binding network stabilizing ERF interaction with DNA [49]. The nonconservative substitution is expected to affect proper binding of ERF to DNA. Consistently, a different amino acid substitution of the equivalent residue in the C. elegans LIN-1, resulting in a developmental phenotype associated with upregulated RAS-MAPK signaling, has been reported to affect DNA binding [49]. While different missense variants affecting residues flanking Tyr89 have been reported in subjects with CRS4 [25], and are supposed to result in defective DNA binding [49, 50], further studies are required to dissect the specific qualitative and quantitative perturbations induced by these disease-causing amino acid substitutions on ERF binding behavior to DNA.

In conclusion, the clinical profiling of the individuals carrying ERF LoF variants indicate that, besides the occurrence of isolated forms of craniosynostosis, ERF haploinsufficiency is associated with a developmental condition within the RASopathy phenotypic spectrum, which is suggestive of NS in a significant proportion of cases. This clinical reassessment should be considered by OMIM to accommodate “Noonan-like syndrome, with or without craniosynostosis” among the Mendelian traits causally related by pathogenic ERF variants. Moreover, the low prevalence of CHD and HCM in these subjects provides a first dissection of the molecular circuits that contribute to MAPK signaling pleiotropy.
