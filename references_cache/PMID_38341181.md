# Genetic, immunologic, and clinical features of 830 patients with Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial diseases (MSMD): A systematic review.
**Authors:** Khavandegar A, Mahdaviani SA, Zaki-Dizaji M, Khalili-Moghaddam F, Ansari S, Alijani S, Taherzadeh-Ghahfarrokhi N, Mansouri D, Casanova JL, Bustamante J, Jamee M
**Journal:** J Allergy Clin Immunol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jaci.2024.01.021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2024.01.021)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial diseases (MSMD) is a rare clinical syndrome characterized by vulnerability to weakly virulent mycobacterial species, including Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccines and environmental mycobacteria.

OBJECTIVE: We sought to perform a systematic review of the genetic, immunologic, and clinical findings for reported patients with MSMD.

METHODS: We searched PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus databases for publications in English relating to MSMD. All full texts were evaluated for eligibility for inclusion. Two reviewers independently selected the publications, with a third reviewer consulted in cases of disagreement.

RESULTS: A primary systematic search and searches of other resources identified 16,155 articles. In total, 158 articles from 63 countries were included in qualitative and quantitative analyses. In total, 830 patients-436 males (52.5%), 369 females (44.5%), and 25 patients of unknown sex (3.0%)-from 581 families were evaluated. A positive family history was reported in 347 patients (45.5%). The patients had a mean age of 10.41 ± 0.42 (SEM) years. The frequency of MSMD was highest in Iran, Turkey, and Saudi Arabia. Lymphadenopathy was the most common clinical manifestation of MSMD, reported in 378 (45.5%) cases and multifocal in 35.1%. Fever, organomegaly, and sepsis were the next most frequent findings, reported in 251 (30.2%), 206 (24.8%), and 171 (20.8%) cases, respectively. In total, 299 unique mutations in 21 genes known to be involved in MSMD were reported: 100 missense (34%), 80 indel-frameshift (insertion or deletion, 27%), 53 nonsense (18%), 35 splice site (12%), 10 indel-in frame (2.7%), 6 indel (2%), and 15 large deletion/duplication mutations. Finally, 61% of the reported patients with MSMD had mutations of IL12RB1 (41%) or IFNGR1 (20%). At the time of the report, 177 of the patients (21.3%) were dead and 597 (71.9%) were still alive.

CONCLUSIONS: MSMD is associated with a high mortality rate, mostly due to impaired control of infection. Preexposure strategies, such as changes in vaccination policy in endemic areas, the establishment of a worldwide registry of patients with MSMD, and precise follow-up over generations in affected families, appear to be vital to decrease MSMD-related mortality.

Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial diseases (MSMD) is a rare clinical syndrome, resulting in predisposition to clinical disease caused by weakly virulent mycobacterial species, including Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccines and non-tuberculous environmental mycobacteria (1). Patients are also susceptible to the more virulent Mycobacterium tuberculosis (2). Unlike patients with other immunodeficiency disorders, these patients are selectively vulnerable to intracellular microorganisms, including Salmonella, and related bacteria, fungi, and parasites (3, 4). MSMD-related defects can be inherited in an X-linked recessive (XR) (5), autosomal dominant (AD) (6), or autosomal recessive (AR) manner (1). The first MSMD-associated mutations were identified in 1996 as biallelic null mutations of the interferon gamma receptor-1 gene (IFNGR1). Twenty-one genes are now known to be associated with MSMD: 18 autosomal genes (IL12B, IL12RB1, IL12RB2, IL23R, JAK1, RORC, ISG15, USP18, TYK2, IRF8, SPPL2A, IFNGR1, IFNGR2, IRF1, STAT1, TBX21, IFNG and ZNFX1) and three X-linked genes (CYBB, MCTS1 and NEMO) (2, 7, 8, 9). Between 1996 and 2014, nine recessive MSMD-associated genes — seven autosomal and two X-linked genes —were described. Mutations of the autosomal genes STAT1, encoding signal transducer and activator of transcription-1, IFNGR1, and IFNGR2, encoding two interferon gamma receptor chains, IL12RB1, encoding a subunit of the interleukin-12 receptor, IL12B, encoding the IL-12p40 subunit common to two heterodimeric cytokines, IL-12 and IL-23, IRF8, encoding interferon regulatory factor-8, a transcription factor essential for myeloid linage development, and ISG15, encoding interferon-stimulated gene-15, an IFN-inducing cytokine, were identified. Two X-linked MSMD-associated genes were also identified: NEMO, encoding nuclear factor kappa-B, a regulator of innate immunity, and CYBB, encoding the cytochrome B-245 beta chain, also known as gp91-phox, a subunit of the NADPH oxidase enzyme complex (10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15). IL-12Rβ1 deficiency is the genetic etiology most frequently identified in MSMD patients (16).

Between 2014 and 2020, six new AR genetic diseases involving complete deficiencies were described. The genes concerned were SPPL2A, encoding signal peptide peptidase-like 2, a transmembrane protein that modifies immunity by cleaving TNF in activated dendritic cells (17, 18), IL12RB2, encoding another subunit of IL-12Rβ2 (19), IL23R, encoding the interleukin 23 receptor (19, 20, 21, 22), TYK2, encoding a subunit of Janus kinases, with homozygosity for the P1104A variant associated with disease (23, 24), the Janus kinase-1 (JAK1) genes (25), and the gene encoding RAR-related orphan receptor C (RORC), which is thought to play a crucial role in immunity (26). In 2020 and 2021, another three MSMD-related genes were described. In late 2020, Yang et al. showed that homozygosity for a T-box transcription factor-21 (TBX21) variant could result in MSMD (27). Kerner et al. claimed that IFN-γ deficiency itself can be associated with MSMD (Supplementary Table 1) (28). Then, in 2021, Le Voyer et al. reported that the NFX-1-type zinc finger-containing protein-1 (ZNFX-1) plays a crucial role in human immunity and stress granules, and that defects of the corresponding gene can result in MSMD (29).

Finally, in 2022 and 2023, another two new AR and one XR deficiencies were described. Martin-Fernandez et al. discovered that a partial deficiency of ubiquitin-specific peptidase-18 (USP18) predisposes patients to mycobacterial infections and interferonopathy (with cerebral calcifications) (30). Rosain et al. revealed that interferon regulatory factor-1 (IRF1) is an essential component of the immunity mediated by the IFN-γ. By contrast, IRF1 does not seem to be required for the antiviral immunity mediated by the IFN-α and ß pathways (8). The most recent XR MCTS1 deficiency described in several MSMD patients impairs JAK2 translation in innate-like T lymphocytes decreasing IL-23 dependent IFN-γ production (9). Depending on the type of mutation and its penetrance for mycobacterial disease, clinical manifestations may range from early-onset, disseminated, fatal mycobacterial to late-onset, regional, chronic, and spontaneously improving disorders (31, 32).

We performed a comprehensive literature review, limited to articles written in English, using the PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus databases. A combination of the following keywords was used for the systematic search; “Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease” or “MSMD” or “Primary immunodeficiency” or “PID” or “Inborn error of immunity” or “IL12B” or “ IL12RB1” or “IL23R” or “ ISG15” or “ TYK2” or “IRF8” or “ ZNFX1” or “ IL12RB2” or “ USP18” or “ IFNG “ or “ TBX21” or “ IRF1” or “SPPL2A” or “ CYBB” or “ IFNGR1” or “ IFNGR2” or “ STAT1” or “NEMO” or “RORC” or “JAK1” or “ MCTS1” or “Interferon-gamma” or “ Interleukin-12” and “Mycobacteriosis” or “Tuberculosis” or “Bacillus Calmette-Guerin” or “BCG vaccines” or “Non-tuberculous environmental mycobacteria” or “Typhoidal Salmonella” or “Listeriosis” or “Nocardiosis” or “Klebsiellosis” or “Candidiasis” or “Histoplasmosis” or “Paracoccidioidomycosis” or “Coccidioidomycosis” or “Leishmaniasis” or “Toxoplasmosis” or “Cytomegalovirus” or “Human herpes virus 8” or “Parainfluenza virus type 3” or “Respiratory syncytial virus” or “Varicella, zoster virus” or “Mycobacterium” or “BCG-osis, BCG-itis” or “Disseminated BCG infection” or “Mycobacteria” or “Susceptibility to mycobacterial disease”. We also reviewed the reference lists of the corresponding full-text articles and major reviews and manually added additional studies.

The articles were screened on the basis of title and abstract to eliminate all irrelevant studies. We classified the articles into three major groups: include, exclude, or unclear. The full texts of studies in the “unclear” group were evaluated further to determine whether the articles concerned should be placed in the “include” or “exclude” groups. The eligibility of all full texts for inclusion in the study was analysed according to the following criteria: published in English, studies on humans, reporting at least one MSMD patient with a confirmed genetic origin and a detailed presentation of the epidemiological, clinical, genetic and immunological findings for patients. Articles performed exclusively in animal models, congress abstracts, and studies published in languages other than English were excluded. If necessary, the corresponding author was contacted for additional information.

Two researchers first extracted data from the “included” articles. Data relating to publication year, the number of participants, and genetic, demographic, clinical, and immunological data for the patients were extracted from each article. Furthermore, normal and age-adjusted normal ranges reported in the articles were applied as a baseline for the comparison of data and results. Patients included in more than one study were identified and eliminated from the analysis to prevent duplication. Two researchers independently selected the data and a third reviewer was consulted in cases of disagreement. In this study, BCG-osis and BCG-itis were considered to indicate positivity for M. bovis-BCG.

Central and descriptive statistics were reported for quantitative data. Median and interquartile ranges (IQR) were reported for variables that did not follow a normal distribution. For variables with a normal distribution, we used the mean ± standard deviation (SD) in the data report. Mann-Whitney U tests, Chi-squared tests, or Fisher’s exact tests were used for comparisons between groups. P-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS software (v. 26.0, Chicago, IL).

The primary systematic search and searches of other resources identified 16,155 articles, of which 540 were duplicates and 15,085 were immediately excluded. In total, 530 articles were subjected to a thorough initial assessment; 16 of these articles were excluded because they were congress proceedings or review articles, and 20 were irrelevant (Figure 1). We subjected 491 eligible articles to a second round of assessment, in which 336 were excluded because the associated gene mutation was not identified. Studies describing immunological assessment only, with no genetic confirmation of the deficiency, were excluded from the analysis. After the evaluation, we included 158 articles in which a defect of one of the 21 known MSMD-associated genes was clearly identified in qualitative and quantitative analyses.

In this study, 876 cases were retrieved from the 158 articles included. However, 46 cases were excluded from the final analysis due to the absence of a definitive genetic diagnosis. In total, 830 cases, corresponding to 436 male patients (52.5%), 369 female patients (44.5%), and 25 patients of unknown sex (3%) from 581 families, were included in this study. A positive family history was reported in 347 patients (45.5%). Mean patient age (± the standard error of the mean, SEM) was 10.41 ± 0.42 years and the patients included in the various studies were aged between 14 days and 62 years.

The global distribution of the patients is shown in Figure 2. MSMD patients from 63 countries were identified in the published articles. The countries with the largest numbers of reported MSMD patients were Iran, Turkey, India, Saudi Arabia, and France, with 114, 107, 63, 54, and 51 cases, respectively. Three of these countries are located in the Middle East. At the time of the report, 177 patients (21.3%) were dead, and 597 patients (71.9%) were still alive. Vital status information was unavailable for the remaining patients (n=56, 6.7%). The mean (± SEM) age at death was 8.68 ± 1.00 years. For the patients who died, the cause of death was clearly identified for 153 patients: 45 patients (29.4%) died from mycobacterial infections, 20 patients (13.1%) died from non-mycobacterial sepsis, 13 patients (8.5%) died from respiratory failure unrelated to infectious disease, 11 patients (7.2%) died due to central nervous system (CNS) involvement, five patients (3.3%) died from cancer, five patients (3.3%) died due to gastrointestinal (GI) involvement, three patients (2%) died from graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), and 51 patients (33.3%) died from other causes, including trauma and end-stage renal disease.

The immunological data for the patients with MSMD are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. The median (IQR) absolute lymphocyte count was 2,550 cells/μL (1,020 to 6,350). The median (IQR) number of CD3+ T cells was 2,984 (1,462 to 4,442) cells/μL. The median (IQR) number of CD4+ T cells was 1,410 (706 to 2,437) cells/μL. The median (IQR) number of NK cells was 387 (166 to 861) cells/μL. The median (IQR) levels of IgG, IgA, IgM, and IgE were 1,374 (954 to 1,998) mg/dL, 131 (60 to 225) mg/dL, 151 (87 to 258) mg/dL, and 49.5 (22.62 to 224.5) IU/mL, respectively. High serum IgG, IgA, IgM, and IgE levels were reported in 47.2%, 28.5%, 31.2%, and 17.5% of patients, respectively.

The presentations of MSMD, their frequency (%) within each group and the total number of MSMD cases are documented in Table 1.

Initial misdiagnosis of a disease other than MSMD was reported for 23 (2.8%) patients. The initial diagnosis was a neoplastic disease in five of these 23 cases (21.7%) cases, with more than half of them these individuals initially diagnosed with Langerhans cell histiocytosis (n=3, 13%). Sarcoidosis was the initial diagnosis in four (17.4%) patients, whereas CGD, AIDS, and primary tuberculosis (TB) were the initial diagnosis in three (13%) cases each. Chronic recurrent multifocal osteomyelitis was reported as the initial diagnosis in two (8.7%) cases. The remaining three cases (13%) had other primary diagnoses. Further evaluations revealed that all 23 patients had MSMD.

Among the 417 (50.2% of the total) MSMD patients for whom data were available for initial clinical presentation, lymphadenopathy was the most frequent finding, reported in 195 patients (46.8% cases). The next most frequent clinical presentation was isolated fever in 59 patients (14.1%), followed by BCG infection in the form of BCG-osis (disseminated BCG infection) or BCG-itis (local or regional BCG infection) in 27 patients (6.5%), osteomyelitis in 24 (5.7%), fever with the involvement of at least one organ in 17 (4.1%), and anorexia and cutaneous manifestations in 14 patients (3.4%) each. Figure 3 provides detailed data for the initial presentation in the MSMD patients.

A history of BCG vaccination was noted in 571 (68.8%) cases, most of whom (484, 84.8%) experienced abnormal reactions to the vaccine. The adverse reactions observed were either local (lymphadenitis or BCG-itis) or disseminated (BGC-osis). BCG infection was limited to the injection area in 146 (30.2%) cases, disseminated in 289 (59.7%) cases, and its extent was undetermined in another 49 (10.1%) cases. The mean time from BCG exposure to adverse reaction was 4.54 ± 0.37 months (mean ± SEM). The mean age at development of an adverse reaction to vaccination was 0.97 (0.42–1.52) years (mean, 95% CI). Based on these data, we can conclude that BCG was administered at a mean age of eight months.

Lymphadenopathy was reported in 378 (45.5%) cases. It was isolated and axillary in 106 (28.0%), cervical in 23 (6.1%), peripheral subcutaneous in 19 (5.0%), abdominal in 11 (2.9%), and mediastinal in nine (2.4%) cases. Ten (2.6%) patients had isolated lymphadenopathy at other sites. Finally, 134 (35.4%) patients developed lymphadenopathies at multiple sites. The site of the lymphadenopathy remained undetermined for the others (17.5%).

In total, 117 (14.1%) patients suffered from at least one episode of osteomyelitis, which was multifocal (defined as involvement of at least two bones from different sites) in 67 cases (57.3%); eight (6.8%) had isolated vertebral column osteomyelitis, seven cases (6.0%) had isolated lower limb osteomyelitis, five cases (4.3%) had pure hip osteomyelitis, two (1.7%) had isolated rib involvement, one patient (0.9%) had isolated skull osteomyelitis, and one (0.9%) had isolated upper limb osteomyelitis. The site of osteomyelitis was not determined in 26 cases (22.2%). Mixed bacterial and mycobacterial infection was reported in 38 of 117 cases of osteomyelitis (34.5%), TM infection was reported in 38 (34.5%), and NTM infection in 31 (28.2%) cases. Isolated Salmonella osteomyelitis was reported in one (0.9%) case. Osteomyelitis was aseptic or of unknown cause in nine (7.7%) cases.

Cutaneous manifestations were observed in 118 (14.2%) cases. Maculopapular patch/plaque rashes were the most frequent finding, reported in 21 (17.8%) cases. Candidiasis and granuloma were each reported in 12 (10.2%) cases, skin ulcers were reported in nine cases (7.6%), eczema in eight (6.8%), abscesses and cutaneous vasculitis in six patients each (5.1%), and erythema nodosum was reported in five (4.2%) cases. Other cutaneous manifestations were observed in 39 patients (33.1%).

An abscess developed in at least one episode in 85 (10.2%) patients. Abscesses were most frequently reported at lymph nodes, in 17 (20%) cases, paravertebral sites, including psoas muscles, in 15 (17.6%) cases, at hepatosplenic sites in 13 (15.3%) cases, cutaneous sites in eight (9.4%), and in the chest wall in five (5.9%) cases. Abscesses developed at other sites in 27 (31.8%) cases. Fever was described in 251 (30.2%) patients, and 171 (20.6%) patients experienced at least one episode of sepsis.

Neurologic presentations were reported in 64 (7.7%) patients, 27 (42.2%) of whom developed meningitis; seven (10.9%) had facial or limb paralysis/paresis, six (9.4%) cases experienced a seizure, two (3.1%) developed simultaneous seizures and meningitis, and two (3.1%) had central nervous system (CNS) granulomas. Twenty (31.3%) patients experienced other CNS manifestations, including encephalitis, brain abscesses, and cerebral calcifications. Seven patients developed brain abscesses or infectious brain infiltrations.

Rheumatologic conditions were reported in 23 (2.8%) cases. Eight (34.8%) experienced Henoch–Schönlein purpura (HSP), eight (34.8%) developed vasculitis, classified as leukocytoclastic vasculitis of the skin in three cases. Five (21.7%) cases had arthritis, and two (8.7%) had other manifestations. Cancers were reported in 17 (3.4%) cases, including five cases of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), and three of B-cell lymphoma. At least one allergic reaction was reported in 13 (2.6%) cases.

Weight loss was observed in 51 (6.1%) cases. The mean weight loss was 12 ± 5.01 (mean ± SEM) kilograms. Failure to thrive (FTT) was reported in 31 (3.7%) cases. Organomegaly was reported in 206 patients (24.8%): splenomegaly in 78 (37.9%), hepatomegaly in 66 (32.0%) and hepatosplenomegaly in 62 (30.1%).

Bacterial infection was reported in 727 (87.6%) patients, 209 of whom (28.7%) experienced mixed bacterial infections with at least two different bacteria. In total, 560 (67.5%) patients had TM-complex infections, due to M. bovis-BCG in 491 (87.7%), M. tuberculosis in 44 (7.9%), concomitant M. bovis-BCG and M. tuberculosis infections in 23 (4.1%) and M. africanum in two (0.4%). NTM-complex infections were observed in 188 (22.7%) cases. The frequency of mycobacterial infections is displayed in Table 2. MSMD patients suffered a mean of 1.75 ± 0.16 (mean ± SEM) mycobacterial episodes. Salmonella infections were reported in 152 (18.3%) cases; 13 (8.6%) patients had localized infection, 34 (22.4%) had disseminated infection, and the extent of infection was not determined in the others. The Salmonella species reported were S. enteritis, S. typhimurium, S. paratyphi, S. typhi, and S. dublin, in 39 (25.7%), 14 (9.2%), 8 (5.3%), 3 (2%), and 2 (1.3%) cases, respectively. Concomitant infections with at least two types of Salmonella were noted in 14 (9.2%) patients. The species of Salmonella was not determined in 60 (39.5%) cases. Twelve (7.9%) cases of Salmonella group D infection were reported, but without subtype identification (i.e. typhi or enteritis). Salmonella infection occurred at a mean age of 4.71 ± 0.55 (mean ± SEM) years.

Severe viral infections were reported in 107 (12.9%) cases, including isolated cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection in 22 (20.6%), isolated varicella zoster virus (VZV) infection in 20 (18.7%), isolated herpes simplex virus (HSV) infection in six (5.6%), isolated Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) in six (5.6%), isolated coronavirus infection in three (2.8%) and isolated human herpesvirus-8 (HHV-8), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and influenza/parainfluenza virus infections in two cases (1.9%) each. Six (5.6%) patients had isolated infections with other viruses. Mixed viral infections with at least two viruses were reported in 38 patients (35.5%).

Thirteen (1.6%) patients suffered protozoan infections: toxoplasmosis in six cases, leishmaniasis in four, giardiasis in two and cryptosporidiosis due to C. parvum in one.

Fungal infections were reported in 136 (16.4%) patients: candidiasis in 120 (88.2%), histoplasmosis in 10 (7.4%), aspergillosis in three (2.2%) and coccidioidomycosis, paracoccidioidomycosis, and Pneumocystis jirovecii infection in one case (0.7%) each.

In total, 830 (648 families) of the 876 patients (708 families) had a definitive genetic diagnosis, with a mutation identified in one of 21 genes known to be associated with MSMD. The other 46 patients were diagnosed on the basis of clinical signs and symptoms. For the 830 patients with identified mutations, 61% had mutations of either IL12RB1 (41%) or IFNGR1 (20%). The detailed mutation spectrum of MSMD patients is presented in Table 3. There were 299 unique mutations of these 21 genes: 100 missense (34%), 80 indel-frameshift (insertion or deletion, 27%), 53 nonsense (18%), 35 essential splice site (12%), 10 indel-in frame (2.7%), 6 Indel (2%) and 15 large deletion/duplication mutations. Mostly, the mutations were distributed fairly evenly along the length of the genes, with no particular mutational hot spots identified. However, the c.819_822delTAAT (or classically defined 818del) mutation of the IFNGR1 gene and the c.315_316insT mutation of the IL12B gene were the most prevalent mutations, with frequencies of 40% and 35%, potentially corresponding to a mutational hot spot. MSMD displayed autosomal recessive (AR) inheritance in most patients (79.9%). However, AD and XR inheritance patterns were also seen. AD and XR inheritance patterns were observed in about 15.2% and 4.9%, respectively, of all patients. Dominant-negative mechanisms have been reported in IFN-γR1, STAT1 and IRF8 deficiencies, and haploinsufficiency has been reported for IFNGR2. In an analysis of common symptoms associated with frequently mutated genes, osteomyelitis was found to be common in AD deficiencies of STAT1 (62%) or IFN-γR1 (54%), fever was frequent in deficiencies of STAT1 deficiency (79%) or IFN-γR2 (63%), and lymphadenopathy was observed more rarely, in patients with deficiencies of NEMO (20%) and TYK2 (19%).

Antimycobacterial agents were used in 390 (47.0%) patients, and a response to treatment was noted in 80.3% (261 of 325) of the patients for whom follow-up data were available. IFN-γ and IFN-α were prescribed for 168 (20.2%) and 16 (1.9%) MSMD patients, respectively. The genetic diagnoses of these 183 (one patient received both IFNs) cases treated with IFN are shown in Supplementary Table 3. Most patients (97 of 127, 76.2%) with documented follow-up after IFN-γ treatment had a favourable response, but IFN-γ treatment was available in only a few countries.

Thirty-nine patients (23 males (59%), 14 females (35.9%), and two of unknown sex (5.1%)) underwent hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation (HSCT) once (34; 87.1%) or twice (5; 12.8%) during the course of the disease. Transplantation details for the MSMD patients are provided in Supplementary Table 4. Most of the patients who underwent HSCT had complete deficiencies of the IFN-γR1 (16, 41%) or IFN-γR2 (8, 20.5%). The next most frequent defects leading to transplantation were complete deficiencies of IL-12Rβ1 (5, 12.8%), IRF8 (4, 10.3%), STAT1 (2, 5.1%), IFN-γ (1, 2.6%), and TYK2 (1, 2.6%); and partial XR NEMO deficiency (2, 5.1%). The median (IQR) age at first HSCT was 4 (1.25–5.0) years. The HSCT were from a matched sibling donor (11, 25%), a haploidentical donor (11, 25%), a matched unrelated donor (10, 22.7%), a matched related donor (7, 15.9%), or a mismatched unrelated donor (3, 6.8%). Age at transplantation (p=0.582) and donor type (p=0.527) were not associated with mortality outcome, but the underlying genetic defect contributed to transplant prognosis in survival analysis (log-rank: p<0.001) (Figure S1).

The median (IQR) number of CD34+ cells infused (n=22 patients) was 7.2 (3–16.3) ×103 and the median (IQR) number of total nucleated cells (n=14 patients) infused was 4.5 (3.8–10.4) ×103. The conditioning regimen was described in 26 of 39 transplant procedures (66.7%) and included fludarabine (16, 41%), treosulfan (10, 25.6%), cyclophosphamide (11, 28.2%), anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG) (9, 23.1%), busulfan (10, 25.6%), thiotepa (6, 15.4%), alemtuzumab (5, 12.8%), rituximab (3, 7.7%), melphalan (2, 5.1%), total-body irradiation (TBI) (3, 7.7%), and/or tacrolimus (1, 2.6%). Information about graft-versus-host-disease (GvHD) prophylaxis was available for 21 patients, 16 of whom received prophylactic regimens with cyclosporine (76.2%), mycophenolate mofetil (8, 38%), methotrexate (7, 33.3%), steroid (2, 9.5%), tacrolimus (3, 14.3%), ATG (1, 4.7%), α-IL2R antibody (1, 4.7%), cyclophosphamide (1, 4.7%), and/or alemtuzumab (1, 4.7%).

Polymorphonuclear neutrophil (PMN) cell engraftment occurred after a median (IQR) of 16.5 (12.8–19.8) days. Full chimerism was achieved in 12 of 19 (63.2%) patients. Almost half the patients with available follow-up data developed GvHD (8 of 18, 44.4%). Median post-transplantation follow-up was 360 (180–1460) days. The mortality rate for patients who had undergone transplantation was 33.3% (13 of 39). No statistically significant association was identified between transplantation and life status (p=0.122). The most common causes of death among the 13 patients who died were post-transplantation infection or infection-related complications, including septicemia (4, 30.8%), disseminated mycobacterial infection (3, 23.1%), EBV-associated lymphoproliferative syndromes (1, 7.7%), and CMV pneumonia (1, 7.7%), followed by other causes, such as liver disease (1, 7.7%), multiple-organ failure (1, 7.7%), prolonged convulsions (1, 7.7%), and severe GvHD (1, 7.7%).

Surgery of some kind was performed in 119 (14.3%) patients, 56 (47.1%) of whom underwent lymph node resection. Abscess/empyema drainage was performed in 24 (20.2%) cases and seven (5.9%) patients underwent transplantation-related surgery. In 32 (26.9%) cases, surgical procedures were performed for other reasons.

We performed a systematic review of the genetic, demographic, immunological, and clinical characteristics of MSMD patients. The cohort considered here is the largest cohort of MSMD patients analyzed to date, comprising 830 patients from 581 families. The patients originate from 63 countries in Asia, America, Europe, Africa, and Oceania; they belong to various ethnic groups and display considerable geographic and ethnic heterogeneity. The studies included in this analysis demonstrated that the incidence of MSMD is closely related to consanguineous marriage, which increases the risk of AR etiologies (33). By contrast, the prevalence of AD and XR etiologies does not seem to vary geographically. Middle-Eastern countries, such as Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Syria, Iran, Turkey, and Morocco, have a high frequency of consanguineous marriages, which, together with strong neonatal BCG vaccination policies, results in a higher frequency of MSMD identification during childhood mycobacterial disease (21, 34). The small number of MSMD cases reported in North America and Australia may be attributed to low levels of consanguinity and a more restricted BCG vaccination policy. MSMD diagnosis rates are intermediate in Latin America, with an increase in the number of patients diagnosed in the last decade (35). The very small numbers of patients diagnosed in Africa may be due to underreporting, early death from infectious disease, or other unrecognized factors (36, 37).

Since the discovery of the first genetic etiology of MSMD in 1996 (38, 39), a number of other genes have been implicated in the pathogenesis of MSMD. Genetic studies are required to obtain a definitive diagnosis in patients with MSMD. To date, 21 genes have been shown to be associated with MSMD, all involved in IFN-γ production, the IFN-γ response, or both. We found that the most frequent genetic defects in patients with MSMD concerned IL-12Rß1 (~41%), followed by IFN-γR1 (~20%), IL-12p40 (~9%), STAT1 (~9%), and IFN-γR2 (~6%). Furthermore, a genetic analysis of a large cohort of 220 MSMD patients with defects of the IL-12/23-IFN-γ circuit performed by Casanova et al. ranked the affected genes as follows: IL12RB1 (40%), IFNGR1 (39%), IL12B (9%), STAT1 (5%), IFNGR2 (4%), and NEMO (3%) (40). Data for two Iranian cohorts indicate that genetic defects of IL-12Rß1 and IL-12p40 are the most common in Iranian patients (41, 42). MSMD displayed AR inheritance in most patients; however, AD and XR patterns of inheritance are also seen. In countries with high a prevailing tradition of first-cousin marriages, rates of consanguinity are high and, as for other AR diseases, AR forms of MSMD are significantly more prevalent than AD or XR forms. In such cases, children of healthy parents present complications of BCG vaccination (3, 40).

The clinical severity and penetration of MSMD vary considerably, even within affected kindreds, as they depend on residual IFN-γ activity. Patients with complete IFN-γR1, IFN-γR2, and STAT1 deficiencies have the most severe genetic forms of MSMD, in which IFN-γ activity is completely abolished. These patients require HSCT, as the only curative treatment available (43, 44, 45). Complete IFN-γ and IRF1 deficiencies also cause severe MSMD, but these patients should be treated with IFN-γ in addition to the antibiotics (8). Penetrance varies from 100% in AR complete IFN-γR1 deficiency (46) to 0.5% in AD partial IFN-γR1 and AD partial IFN-γR2 deficiency (47), as reviewed by Das et al. (48). The spectrum of clinical features in MSMD patients can be classified as isolated or syndromic, according to the basic clinical presentation (44, 49). Other genetic defects with complete penetrance for mycobacterial diseases include complete deficiencies of IFN-γR2, IFN-γ, or IRF1. The differences in the underlying genetic defect and cellular phenotype resulted in considerable heterogeneity in terms of the clinical phenotype and severity of disease manifestations in these 830 patients (50, 51). Furthermore, differences in BCG vaccination policy, ethnic background, and differences in social factors, including population density, the incidence of M. tuberculosis in the country, the quality of nutrition and hygiene and local endemicity patterns for a particular infection, can account for the diverse patterns of infection observed in MSMD patients from around the world (51). The national vaccination policy in Iran recommends the injection of a single 0.05 cc dose of BCG vaccine after birth (52). However, in individuals with a family history of MSMD, BCG vaccination is contraindicated due to the risk of mycobacterial infection (21).

Like other inherited genetic IEI, MSMD should be considered in the differential diagnosis of children with clinical infections consistent with MSMD (53). This was the case in 2.8% of patients with an initial diagnosis other than MSMD. According to our results, for MSMD patients with available primary clinical presentation data, lymphadenopathy was the most frequent finding (45.5%), followed by isolated fever, osteomyelitis, BCG infection, either as BCG-osis or BCG-itis, anorexia, cutaneous manifestations, and respiratory symptoms, including cough and respiratory distress. The isolation of microorganisms can provide significant clues to the underlying molecular defects in MSMD patients (51). For example, the combination of mycobacterial and CMV infections indicates the an AR complete deficiency of IFN-γR1/R2 or an AR complete deficiency of STAT1 (54); infections with Salmonella or non-Typhi Salmonella are highly suggestive of complete IL-12Rβ1 or IL-12p40 defect (55); CMC is suggestive of IL-12Rβ1, IL-23R or ROR-γT deficiency (26, 56). The combination of brain calcification and mycobacterial infection suggests an ISG15, USP18 or ZNFX1 deficiency (57). Multifocal bone osteomyelitis is strongly associated with an underlying molecular defect of AD-IFNγR1, XR-MCTS1 or AD-STAT1 deficiency (9, 58). Viral infections have also been reported, including diseases caused by CMV, HHV-8, parainfluenza virus type 3 (PRV-3), RSV, SARS-CoV-2 and VZV (37, 43, 46, 59, 60). Viral infections were observed in 107 (13%) of the cases considered here. Viral infections were frequently described in patients with AR complete IFN-γR1 or IFN-γR2 deficiency, or with AR STAT1 deficiency (37, 43, 45). Our findings highlighted different clinical manifestations, such as lymphadenopathy, osteomyelitis, abscesses, weight loss, neurological and rheumatological presentations related to MSMD, and different genotypes potentially associated with different severities of infection. Furthermore, in 2019, Ying et al. showed, in a cohort of 30 patients diagnosed with MSMD, that the lymph nodes, lungs, skin, bones, and peritoneum were the most common sites of infection (61).

Therapeutic approaches for MSMD include the use of antimycobacterial antibiotics, IFN-γ therapy, surgery to remove lymph nodes, and hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation (HSCT). Gene therapy remains in at the experimental stage but holds promise for the future (62, 63). MSMD treatment should target the particular disease-causing genetic defect and clinical manifestations. The identification of molecular defects and their confirmation in phenotypic and functional immune investigations are, therefore, essential for patients with MSMD, to ensure accurate diagnosis and estimates of disease prognosis and for selection of the optimal mode of treatment (64). Genetic confirmation is also important to guide genetic counseling and genetic testing for the relatives of MSMD patients in countries offering BCG vaccination, such testing being required before immunization (65).

The treatment of mycobacterial infections in MSMD patients depends on early identification of the infecting microorganisms and effective antibiotic treatment based on the results of antibiograms, if possible. However, the early identification of the causal microorganisms in these patients can be difficult because mycobacterial isolation by culture can take weeks and is often unsuccessful (65, 66, 67). After the diagnosis of BCG-osis, even before the underlying genetic defect has been identified, it is recommended to try a treatment including at least three or four antimycobacterial antibiotics for BCG infection (68, 69). Evidence-based therapeutic protocols for infection management are lacking, and it therefore remains challenging to select the most appropriate antimycobacterial regimen for MSMD patients; decisions are made on a case-by-case basis (62). Occasionally, abdominal adenomegaly may require excision surgery. Some milder forms of MSMD require no treatment other than appropriate antibiotics, but adjuvant therapy with recombinant IFN-γ is effective in some types of MSMD involving defects of IFN-γ production (70, 71). IFN-γ is crucial for macrophage activation. The inability of the infected macrophages of MSMD patients to kill intracellular bacteria may play an essential role in the particular susceptibility of these patients to mycobacteria (72, 73, 74, 75). Despite the absence of FDA approval for this indication, IFN-γ therapy should, in practice, be considered as the natural treatment, in conjunction with antibiotics, in all MSMD patients who do not have a complete lack of cellular responses to this cytokine.

HSCT remains the only curative treatment available and has been recommended in several patients with the more severe forms of MSMD, such as AR complete IFN-γR1, IFN-γR2, or STAT1 deficiencies (76, 77, 78, 79, 80). The outcome of transplantation is variable and may be unfavourable in cases of recurrent infection or high serum IFN-γ levels (77), with delayed engraftment or even complete graft rejection rapidly occurring. Nevertheless, the control of mycobacterial infection before transplantation is essential to ensure a successful outcome of HSCT in patients eligible for this treatment (81). IFN-γ levels should also be normalized before transplantation, as high IFN-γ levels have been associated with graft rejection (82, 83).
