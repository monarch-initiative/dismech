# Age-Related Decline in Dendritic Architecture of Hippocampal CA1 Principal Neurons in a Mouse Model of Fragile X Syndrome.
**Authors:** Farooqi NNU, Nyengaard JR, Banke TG
**Journal:** Dev Neurobiol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/dneu.70030](https://doi.org/10.1002/dneu.70030)

## Content

Fragile X syndrome (FXS) is the most common inherited cause of intellectual disability and is associated with attention deficits, hyperactivity, anxiety, impulsivity, and repetitive behaviors. The disorder results from transcriptional silencing of the FMR1 gene, leading to loss of fragile X messenger ribonucleoprotein (FMRP), an RNA-binding protein that regulates local dendritic translation by repressing ribosomal activity. To examine how impaired local protein synthesis affects dendritic organization, we used Golgi-Cox staining to analyze hippocampal CA1 principal neurons across four developmental stages (P14-21, P30-40, P60-80, and P120-150) in an FXS mouse model. We identified a progressive reduction in dendritic complexity, reflected by decreased Sholl intersections and reduced dendritic branch number and length. In contrast, spine density was increased in both apical and basal dendrites during early development but normalized to wild-type levels in adulthood. Collectively, these structural alterations are likely to disrupt neural circuit development, with downstream consequences for cognition and behavior characteristic of FXS.

Fragile X syndrome (FXS) is a genetic disorder characterized by mild‐to‐moderate intellectual disability and represents the most common single‐gene cause of autism (Jacquemont et al. 2007; Wang et al. 2012). FXS results from transcriptional silencing of the FMR1 gene on the X chromosome, leading to loss of fragile X messenger ribonucleoprotein (FMRP). FMRP is a polyribosome‐associated RNA‐binding protein that suppresses the translation of target mRNAs, particularly at synapses (Darnell and Klann 2013). Loss of FMRP results in elevated protein synthesis, disrupting the expression of proteins critical for synaptic function and plasticity (Brown et al. 2001; Schütt et al. 2009). As an X‐linked disorder, FXS is fully expressed in hemizygous males and homozygous females, while heterozygous females are typically carriers.

To investigate disease mechanisms, an Fmr1 knockout (Fmr1KO) mouse was developed (Dutch‐Belgium Consortium et al. 1994) to recapitulate many behavioral features of human FXS, including hyperactivity, repetitive behaviors, learning deficits, and seizures (Verkerk et al. 1991; Pimentel 1999; Bagni and Oostra 2013; Sørensen et al. 2015). Synaptic plasticity is markedly altered in this model, with enhanced mGluR‐dependent long‐term depression and disrupted NMDAR‐dependent long‐term potentiation in hippocampal CA1 (Huber et al. 2002; Lauterborn et al. 2007; Lundbye et al. 2018; Toft et al. 2016).

Dendrites and spines are essential for synaptic integration, and their morphology strongly influences neuronal connectivity. Although FXS does not cause gross brain malformations, Fmr1KO mice exhibit region‐specific alterations in dendritic architecture and increased numbers of elongated, immature spines in cortical and hippocampal neurons (Comery et al. 1997; Irwin et al. 2002; Grossman et al. 2006; Thomas et al. 2008; Qin et al. 2011). Similar abnormalities have been reported in postmortem tissue from individuals with FXS (Rudelli et al. 1985; Hinton et al. 1991; Irwin et al. 2001; Martínez‐Cerdeño 2017).

In this study, we analyzed dendritic arborization and spine morphology in hippocampal CA1 neurons from Fmr1KO and wild‐type (WT) mice across four developmental stages (0.5–5 months of age) using Golgi–Cox staining. Our goals were to characterize developmental trajectories of CA1 neuron morphology and evaluate dendritic metrics with potential relevance for therapeutic assessment.

Male Fmr1KO mice (Fmr‐1 ‐/y, RRID:IMSR_JAX: 003035, The Jackson Laboratory) and C57BL/6J WT mice were used. The Fmr1KO mouse was bred in a C57BL/6J background in a non‐littermate fashion. Four different age groups were examined: pups: P14–21, juveniles: P30–40, young adults: P60–80, and mature mice: P120–150, 10 for each group (a total of 80 animals). All animals were kept on a 12:12 h light/dark cycle with constant room temperature. The mice were housed in groups of 5 and provided with food and water ad libitum. The animal experiments were conducted per the animal care guidelines of Aarhus University and approved by the Danish Animal Experiments Inspectorate.

All animals were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and decapitated. The brains were quickly isolated, immediately rinsed with Milli‐Q water, and transferred into equal amounts of solution A and B (8 mL in total) as provided by the FD Rapid GolgiStainTM Kit (FD NeuroTechnologies, Inc.). The solutions had been prepared 24 h prior to the procedure. Twenty‐four hours after brain isolation, solution A+B was renewed. The brains were kept for a total of 5 days in solution A+ B and then transferred into 8 mL of solution C. After an additional 24 h, solution C was renewed. The brains remained in solution C for a total of 72 h.

The brains were carefully and slowly submerged in precooled (−80°C) isopentane (2‐methylbutane), and the brains were cryostat sectioned using Tissue‐Tek O.C.T. in a Microm HM 560 cryostat, with the chamber temperature set at ‐24°C and the microtome temperature set at −23°C. Coronal hippocampal sections of 120 µm were cut and serially mounted on gelatin‐coated slides.

After sectioning, sections were dehydrated in 50%, 75%, 95%, and 100% (absolute) ethanol, and cleared in xylene before being coverslipped with Permount. A total of 210 slides were prepared from all 80 brains, which were randomly code‐labeled. All further analyses were conducted blinded to the investigator, and genotyping results were not revealed until after the final analysis. A period of up to 6 months passed between cutting, imaging, and analyzing the data.

To assess neuronal integrity in the stained hippocampal CA1 region, an Olympus BX50 light microscope (Olympus, Denmark) equipped with a 4× objective (NA 0.16), a motorized stage (Prior Scientific), a microcator (ND281, Germany), and an Olympus DP73 digital camera was used. Z‐stacks of CA1 hippocampal neurons with a soma located approximately in the middle of the slides with steps of 1 µm were sampled to avoid significant dendrite amputations by newCAST software (Version 4.4.5.0; Visiopharm) using a 60× oil lens (NA 1.35). Sampled neurons were 3D reconstructed, as we have previously described in Al‐Absi et al. (2018) using Imaris software (Imaris version 7.7.2, Switzerland) (Figure 1). Four brain slices per brain were used from each of the 210 slides, each with a right and left hemisphere creating a total of 375 neuronal reconstructions.

Sholl analysis of dendritic arborization was done using the Imaris software (Figure 1B,C). For spine characterization, secondary dendritic apical and basal branches in the range of 100–200 µm distance from soma were selected and analyzed using Imaris and ImageJ software (NIH). Spine density, spine lengths, and spine head diameter were analyzed using the spine analysis tool in Imaris (XTension) on selected branches. For spine density analysis, this was followed by a detailed manual inspection in ImageJ as a control (Figure 1C,D). The length was defined as the distance from the base to the tip of the protrusion; the width was defined as the maximum distance perpendicular to the long axis of the protrusion. To separate spines from immature protrusions, the ratio of width/length of individual spines was calculated and plotted in Figure S1. Spines were defined as protrusions if longer than or equal to 0.5. Filopodia (immature) were defined as protrusions if the ratio was less than 0.5 (Levenga et al. 2011). No further attempts were made to classify spine groups.

Significant differences between groups were tested using one‐way or two‐way ANOVA followed by Fisher's protected least significant difference post hoc test when appropriate, as specified in the text, using GraphPad Prism 10. A non‐parametric Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used in Figures 6 and 7.

To assess the developmental trajectory of hippocampal pyramidal neuron morphology in FXS, we analyzed CA1 neurons from four age groups: P14–21 (juvenile), P30–40 (adolescent), P60–80 (young adult), and P120–150 (mature adult). A total of 380 neurons were reconstructed and analyzed using Sholl analysis to quantify dendritic complexity (Sholl 1953).

Both apical and basal dendrites were examined. In juvenile Fmr1 knockout (Fmr1KO) mice, Sholl analysis revealed a transient increase in apical dendritic branching compared to age‐matchedWT controls. However, this phenotype reversed with maturation, such that apical dendritic complexity was significantly reduced in adolescent, young adult, and mature adult Fmr1KO mice (Figure 2). In contrast, basal dendrites exhibited a consistent reduction in Sholl intersections across all age groups in Fmr1KO mice relative to WT (Figure 2A). The average number of Sholl intersections differed significantly between genotypes across age groups (one‐way ANOVA, p < 0.002; Figure 2B). When normalized to WT, apical dendrites in the juvenile Fmr1KO group showed significantly increased intersections (p < 0.05), whereas mature adult Fmr1KO mice exhibited significantly fewer intersections (p  =  0.003). Normalized basal dendritic intersections were consistently reduced in Fmr1KO mice at all ages examined (Figure 2B, right).

To further characterize dendritic architecture, we performed branch order analyses of apical (Figure 3) and basal (Figure 4) dendrites across all age groups. Significant genotype‐dependent differences were observed in both branch number and branch length for apical and basal dendritic trees (two‐way ANOVA, p = 0.001; Figures 3A and 4A). In apical dendrites, alterations were most prominent at branch order 4, where branch length was increased in juvenile Fmr1KO mice but significantly reduced at all later developmental stages. Basal dendrites exhibited a similar, albeit less pronounced, pattern, with length reductions occurring most frequently at branch orders 2 and 3.

Consistent with Sholl findings, both apical and basal dendritic arbors in juvenile Fmr1KO mice displayed increased total branch number and length, particularly in the apical compartment (Figures 3 and 4, right panels). This pattern was reversed in older age groups, where total dendritic branch number and length were reduced relative to WT. Together, these data indicate an early overgrowth followed by progressive dendritic simplification in Fmr1KO neurons, suggesting impaired extension or maintenance of distal dendritic branches during maturation.

Because dendritic spines are critical for synaptic integration, we quantified spine density in both apical and basal dendrites of CA1 pyramidal neurons. In juvenile Fmr1KO mice (P14–21), spine density was significantly increased compared with age‐matched WT in both dendritic compartments, with the largest effect observed at this early developmental stage (Figure 5B). In contrast, spine density in adolescent, young adult, and mature Fmr1KO mice did not differ significantly from WT (Table 1).

To assess spine maturity, we calculated the ratio of spine head diameter to spine length, defining spines with a ratio ≥ 0.5 as mature (Levenga et al. 2011). Both WT and Fmr1KO mice exhibited mixed populations of mature and immature spines across all age groups (Figure S1). However, relative to WT, juvenile Fmr1KO mice showed a reduced fraction of mature spines, whereas mature adult Fmr1KO mice exhibited an increased proportion of mature spines in both apical and basal dendrites (Figure S1C).

In this study, we used Golgi–Cox staining to examine developmental changes in dendritic architecture and spine morphology of hippocampal CA1 pyramidal neurons in Fmr1 knockout (Fmr1KO) and WT mice across four postnatal stages (P14–21, P30–40, P60–80, and P120–150). This developmental approach enabled a direct comparison of early juvenile through mature adult stages, encompassing critical periods of hippocampal circuit maturation.

Dendritic spines constitute the primary postsynaptic sites of excitatory transmission and are highly dynamic structures whose morphology and density are tightly linked to synaptic integration, learning, and memory. Numerous studies have shown that both Fmr1KO mice and individuals with FXS exhibit abnormal dendritic protrusions across multiple brain regions, consistent with the cognitive impairments observed in this disorder (Hinton et al. 1991; Irwin et al. 2001; Irwin et al. 2002; McKinnon et al. 2005; Nimchinsky et al. 2001). The hippocampus, in which FMRP is highly expressed and where Fmr1 mRNA levels are abundant during development (Abitbop et al. 1993), is particularly vulnerable to the loss of FMRP and plays a central role in learning and memory processes (Sidorov et al. 2013).

Our findings reveal a pronounced developmental shift in the dendritic arborization of CA1 pyramidal neurons in Fmr1KO mice. Across multiple complementary measures including Sholl analysis, total dendritic length, branch number, and branch order, we observed an early overgrowth of dendritic arbors that progressively transitioned into a marked reduction relative to WT neurons. By mature adulthood, both apical and basal dendritic arbors were reduced by up to ∼20% in Fmr1KO mice. This reduction reflected combined decreases in both branch number and branch length, suggesting impaired extension or maintenance of distal dendritic branches.

This developmental trajectory supports a model in which Fmr1KO neurons transition from an early “hyperconnected” state to a later “hypoconnected” state. Both extremes are likely to disrupt local and long‐range circuit integration and neuronal excitability (Forrest et al. 2018; Contractor et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2022), phenomena that have been implicated in FXS pathophysiology. Similar dendritic simplifications have been reported in pyramidal neurons of the visual cortex (Restivo et al. 2005), spinal motor neurons (Thomas et al. 2008), neural stem cell‐derived neurons from Fmr1KO mice and human FXS tissue, and CGG repeat knock‐in models (Qin et al. 2011). Notably, reduced dendritic branching in hippocampal neurons has also been described in children with autism, further underscoring the translational relevance of these findings (Castren et al. 2005; Raymond et al. 1996).

Dendritic spine density has historically been considered a hallmark metric of FXS pathology, particularly in cortical regions (Grossman et al. 2006; Levenga et al. 2011; Jawaid et al. 2018; Irwin et al. 2002). However, results in the hippocampal CA1 subregion have been inconsistent across studies. Our data reconcile several of these discrepancies by demonstrating that increased spine density in Fmr1KO mice is restricted to a narrow early developmental window (P14–21). In agreement with recent reports (Zeitouny et al. 2023), spine density was elevated in juvenile Fmr1KO mice but normalized to WT levels at later ages, despite persistent synaptic plasticity defects reported in older animals.

Interestingly, consistent with clear alterations in both spine density and dendritic arborization, we also observed genotype‐dependent differences in both spine length and spine head diameter at most developmental stages. This suggests that a combination of changes in spine number and gross morphological remodeling of individual spines, dominating the phenotype in CA1 pyramidal neurons.

Consistent with this interpretation, analysis of spine maturity revealed an overrepresentation of immature protrusions in juvenile Fmr1KO mice, likely reflecting an increase in filopodia. This supports the hypothesis that loss of FMRP impairs activity‐dependent synaptic pruning and spine maturation during early development. Intriguingly, this delay appeared to reverse in later life, with mature adult Fmr1KO mice showing a relative increase in mature spines. Such findings suggest a dysregulation—rather than a simple delay—of spine turnover and maturation dynamics across development.

Collectively, our results support a model in which loss of FMRP disrupts early synaptic refinement, leading to abnormal dendritic growth trajectories and long‐term alterations in circuit architecture. While early spine density changes may reflect immature synapse formation, the progressive decline in dendritic arborization likely represents a more stable and functionally relevant phenotype contributing to hippocampal dysfunction in FXS.

For decades, dendritic spine morphology has been considered a central metric for characterizing neuronal pathology in FXS. While this approach remains informative in cortical regions, our data suggest that in hippocampal CA1 pyramidal neurons, dendritic arborization provides a more robust and developmentally informative measure. The complex geometry of CA1 neurons, combined with methodological and age‐dependent variability, likely underlies the inconsistent spine density findings reported across studies.

Using large sample sizes and a developmental framework, we demonstrate that Fmr1KO mice exhibit a transient early increase in spine density; shifts in spine maturity over time; and a pronounced, age‐dependent reduction in both apical and basal dendritic arborization. These findings suggest that dendritic architecture—particularly in mature animals—may represent a more reliable structural substrate for evaluating disease progression and therapeutic efficacy. Importantly, a developmental decline in dendritic arborization is expected to profoundly alter synaptic integration and network connectivity, contributing to the cognitive and behavioral deficits characteristic of FXS.
