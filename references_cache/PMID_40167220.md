# Repurposing With Purpose: Treatment of Bachmann-Bupp Syndrome With Eflornithine and Implications for Other Polyaminopathies.
**Authors:** Bupp CP, VanSickle EA, Michael J, Schultz CR, Nguyen K, Hoefer M, Rajasekaran S, Bachmann AS
**Journal:** Am J Med Genet C Semin Med Genet (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ajmg.c.32138](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajmg.c.32138)

## Content

Rare diseases impact approximately 1 in 10 people worldwide, and yet, less than 5% of all rare diseases currently have an approved treatment option available. This is due to many challenges unique to rare diseases, including small, diverse patient populations, the cost of drug development that is not proportionate to the number of patients who could potentially benefit from treatment, and difficulty with clinical trial design to validate new therapeutics. As a result, drug repurposing has become an increasingly promising option for finding treatment options for rare diseases. First described in 2018, Bachmann-Bupp Syndrome (BABS) is a rare neurodevelopmental disorder that is caused by gain-of-function variants in the ornithine decarboxylase (ODC1) gene and is characterized by developmental delay, hypotonia, and alopecia. Through collaboration and the use of a unique drug repurposing strategy, the first patient identified with BABS was treated with the repurposed drug eflornithine, also known as α-difluoromethylornithine (DFMO), in just 16 months. Currently, five additional patients with BABS are being treated with DFMO. This model of drug repurposing of an FDA-approved drug for use in another indication can serve as an example of what is possible in the scope of other rare diseases, specifically in other polyaminopathies.

Historically, the clinical practice of genetics has been based on observation and pattern recognition. This advanced as various biochemical, cytogenetic, and molecular diagnostic testing became available. Yet most diagnoses took time and were challenging to translate to medical management. Newborn screening is an example of a tool for early diagnosis of conditions with treatments, and the number of conditions included in that testing continues to grow. The increase in availability and speed of molecular testing also allows early diagnoses, yet the number of conditions that have available treatments has not grown in parallel. The possibilities for new diseases that could have treatments remains a tantalizing opportunity as more conditions are discovered, diagnosed, and as more patients could have known, or novel therapies applied early in their disease course.

The 7,000 rare diseases known today impact approximately 1 in 10 people worldwide, with children being primarily impacted by rare diseases. In fact, rare diseases account for over a third of all deaths of children in the first year of life, largely because less than 5% of rare diseases have approved treatment options available (Griggs, 2009; Rare Diseases at FDA, 2022; Rare Diseases, 2023). Rare diseases face many challenges to achieving successful treatment options. Patient populations of rare diseases are inherently small and can be geographically far apart, creating roadblocks for clinical trial design and recruitment. Given that symptoms of rare diseases may be on a spectrum and patients with the same rare disease can present with diverse phenotypes, it can be challenging to develop standardized outcome measurements for clinical trials for new therapies. There is also an exigency with rare diseases as many of the patients present with neurological sequalae that deteriorate if treatment is unavailable or delayed. Therefore, data resulting from clinical trials for new treatments for rare diseases have unique challenges to overcome, particularly in evaluating the safety and efficacy of the proposed treatment (Barrett, 2023; Four Challenges to Successful Rare Disease Drug Development, 2024).

The process of developing a new drug is incredibly time-consuming and costly, taking approximately 10-15 years and costing approximately USD 1 billion from start to finish (How Long Does it Take to Develop a New Drug, 2020; Research and Development in the Pharmaceutical Industry, 2021). Given this sizable investment of time, money, and other resources, along with the inherent risk of failure for a variety of reason such as toxicity, pharmaceutical companies have historically opted to pursue development for drugs that can impact a greater number of patients, thus allowing them to recover money invested in drug development. To overcome these barriers, the Orphan Drug Act of 1983 was passed to encourage research and development of new drugs for the treatment of rare diseases. In the 20 years since its passing, over 6,300 orphan drug designations across just over 1,000 rare diseases have been granted (Fermaglich & Miller, 2023). However, promising treatment options do not exist for the majority of rare diseases today. Drug repurposing presents a promising opportunity for rare diseases. In 2019, the first patient identified with a newly described, ultra-rare disease called Bachmann-Bupp Syndrome (BABS) (Bupp et al., 2018) was treated with repurposed drug eflornithine (α-difluoromethylornithine; DFMO) (Rajasekaran et al., 2021). In the past 5 years, an additional 5 patients around the world with BABS have been treated with DFMO. Bench researchers and clinicians working in tandem accelerate the pace of disease description, treatment identification, and therapy initiation. Using this method, BABS demonstrates a model for new hope in treating rare diseases.

Polyamines are aliphatic molecules that are involved in key cellular processes, including proliferation and embryogenesis, and can be found in all living organisms. Within the polyamine pathway, the three principal polyamines putrescine, spermidine, and spermine are produced from ornithine. Recently, a rare family of neurodevelopmental disorders known as polyaminopathies has been described. These five known syndromes are associated with polyamine metabolism and homeostasis and include Snyder-Robinson Syndrome (SRS), BABS, Faundes-Banka Syndrome (FABAS), DHPS deficiency, and DOHH deficiency (Bachmann et al., 2024). Given the complexity of the polyamine pathway and that 4 of these 5 syndromes have been described in just the past 5 years, we expect that variants in other genes in the polyamine pathway may also be associated with unique human genetic syndromes.

BABS, first described in 2018 is an ultra-rare neurodevelopmental disorder that is characterized by developmental delay, alopecia, hypotonia, macrocephaly, and nonspecific MRI findings. As patients continue to be identified, clinical phenotypes are varying in both severity and type of presentation. This suggests that the phenotypic spectrum of BABS is widening beyond the original description of the syndrome. BABS is caused by pathogenic variants in the 3’-end of the ornithine decarboxylase 1 (ODC1) gene, which causes premature C-terminal truncations in ODC. This truncation prevents the cell from degrading ODC through the proteasome when polyamine levels are sufficient, resulting in the accumulation of enzymatically active ODC. This converts ornithine to putrescine in the polyamine pathway, thereby causing cellular accumulation of putrescine (Bachmann, 2024; Bupp, 2018). Eleven patients with BABS have been described in the literature to date, with another 6 patients identified, but unreported (Bupp, 2018; Michael, 2023; Rodan, 2018; VanSickle, 2021).

To date, 6 patients with BABS are being treated with the repurposed drug, DFMO. Five of those patients are being treated in the United States under FDA-approved single patient investigational new drug (IND) protocols. The 6th patient is being treated outside of the U.S. DFMO dosing for BABS is modeled after pediatric neuroblastoma dosing strategies, where patients begin at a dose of 500mg/m2/BID for 3 months, increase to 750mg/m2/BID for another 3 months, and then finally increase to 1000mg/m2/BID indefinitely (Rajasekaran, 2021; Saulnier Sholler, 2015). Treated patients have consistently shown improvement in muscle tone, developmental milestones, and hair regrowth. As we learn of new cases, the clinical spectrum of BABS patients is also broadening with some more mild and severe symptom manifestations. The age of diagnosis and the onset of treatment has also varied from the neonatal period to adulthood. This adds in the challenge of understanding the natural history of the condition as well as the treatment responses with so many variables.

Rare disease treatment is a delicate balance of clinical management with research study, particularly in the early stages of a condition’s discovery. For BABS patients, treatment has been initiated as quickly as possible to allow maximum clinical impact. This has included patients outside the United States, whereby travel to our center for evaluation was often a necessary step to treatment as procurement of DFMO in other countries was a slower path forward. Patient samples were obtained before and during initiation of treatment, with continued collection to this day. Batched analysis of various methodologies is planned, with publication when sufficient data is available.

DFMO is an irreversible inhibitor of ODC and was initially approved by the FDA in 1990 for the treatment of West African sleeping sickness (trypanosomiasis) through intravenous administration. Then in 2000, topical DFMO (Vaniqa) was approved by the FDA to treat hirsutism. Finally, in December 2023, the FDA approved oral DFMO (Iwilfin) as a maintenance therapy for pediatric and adult patients with high-risk neuroblastoma (Duke, 2024; National Center for Biotechnology Information, 2024). DFMO has a strong safety profile for long-term use in pediatric patients for the treatment of neuroblastoma and West African sleeping sickness (Alirol, 2013; Lewis, 2020; Priotto, 2009; Sholler, 2018).

Safety profiles by themselves are inadequate to initiate treatment with a repurposed drug, however. Fortunately, there existed a transgenic ODC1 mouse model previously developed that displayed many of the human phenotypic features of BABS, including follicular cysts and alopecia (Megosh et al., 1995). When treated with DFMO in the drinking water, these mice demonstrated hair regrowth, as well as resolution of follicular cysts (Soler at al., 1996). We have recently confirmed these findings using the same model for future studies (unpublished data). In 2018, we demonstrated that DFMO treatment of primary BABS patient dermal fibroblasts restored ODC activity and putrescine levels to physiologically normal levels (Schultz et al., 2018). Together with the decades of demonstrated safety of DFMO in pediatric patients, these data provided compelling enough evidence that the first patient identified with BABS could benefit from treatment with DFMO.

As publications and awareness of BABS continue, the clinical phenotype continues to be expanded and defined. Increasing our understanding of the genotypic spectrum is now coming more to the forefront. Using published standards and guidelines for interpretation of sequence variants, some clinical genetic laboratories are now identifying variants not in the 3’-end ofODC1 that are classified as variants of uncertain significance but also may meet criteria for likely pathogenic and pathogenic classification (Richards, 2015). As discussed previously, the initial collection of BABS patients and those reported thus far all have variants in the 3’ end of the gene, escaping nonsense-mediated decay and leading to C-terminal protein truncation, removing a key group of amino acids that are necessary for antizyme-mediated (and ubiquitin independent) proteasomal protein degradation. Mechanistically, this leads to increased enzyme levels, which we have previously shown in red blood cells and primary dermal fibroblasts (Bupp, 2018; Schultz, 2018). What is not currently known is the effect of variants elsewhere in the gene that are not located in the 3’ end.

At the time of reporting, this patient is a 20-month-old female born at 27 weeks gestation. She has a clinical history of microcephaly, hemangioma, umbilical hernia, feeding difficulties, apnea, and an abnormal EEG, indicative of severe and diffuse neurological dysfunction. She also had cardiac findings including mild left atrial enlargement, small patent foramen ovale and PDA which has since been surgically repaired. Additionally, brain MRI revealed abnormal cerebral ventricle morphology and abnormal periventricular white matter morphology. She was originally connected to our team at the age of 5 months, when exome sequencing identified a de novo heterozygous variant in the ODC1 gene, c.1217A > T (NM_001287189.1), which is predicted to result in the amino acid substitution (p.Tyr406Phe). This variant is not observed in the population and was classified as a variant of uncertain significance. These clinical features would not fit the classic phenotype of BABS, but the clinical spectrum has widened with emergence of additional cases and the condition remains rare. This variant was appropriately classified as uncertain as it is present in databases like gnomAD and prediction algorithms would classify it as benign. However, the reporting of this variant, the association of ODC1 with BABS, and published data on treatment of BABS presents a unique challenge as patients, families, and healthcare providers are understandably inquisitive whether DFMO may still benefit a patient. Because of this, ODC activity is a method of determining the variant’s effect to provide clarity for the clinical situation. The patient was consented to the IRB-approved International Center for Polyamine Disorders (ICPD) research protocol. Whole blood was collected after consent was obtained. ODC activity analysis was performed via 14C radioactive ODC activity assay (Schultz et al., 2018). Unlike other patients with BABS who demonstrated elevated baseline ODC activity, the patient’s ODC activity levels were not greater than unaffected control patients’ samples (Figure 1). These findings suggest that this patient may not benefit from DFMO treatment. Furthermore, they also support a growing number of unreported cases that point to BABS being a syndrome with a spectrum of clinical presentations and biochemical features.

Treatments targeting the polyamine pathway are not new. As discussed with the development of DFMO as an anti-cancer, then anti-parasitic, then return to anti-cancer and chemopreventive agent, modulation of the pathway holds promise as therapy for diseases . Consideration and modeling of treatment in the other known polyaminopathies are no exception, though none have made it the level of clinical utilization as BABS.

As the first described polyaminopathy, it is not surprising that the most theorizing about treatment has been given to SRS. Small molecule treatment was postulated and modeled, (Zhang, 2013), and treatment of SRS cells with exogenous spermine was done (Murray-Stewart, 2018). This was followed by testing of dimethylspermine in both patient-derived lymphoblasts and fibroblasts, as well as mice, which led to decreased spermidine levels in both models. Drosophila models of SRS have also been studied for prodrug effects and that of phenylbutyrate (Tanak, 2021; Tao, 2022). DFMO usage in SRS has been considered and further study is ongoing (Gilmour, 2023; Stewart, 2023). Many patients with SRS present with bone anomalies, including osteoporosis, kyphoscoliosis, and recurrent fractures (Koerner, 2024). Therefore, the unique skeletal phenotype of SRS has also been a source of treatment study looking at the impact on osteogenesis (Cressman et al., 2024). Continued animal model development is underway to provide additional insight into treatment possibilities (Akinyele et al., 2024).

Though more recently discovered, a zebrafish model of Faundes-Banka Syndrome has been treated with spermidine supplementation as a potential therapeutic. Zebrafish have also been used to model DHPS deficiency syndrome, providing another route for treatment experimentation (Shojaeinia et al., 2024). With much of the polyamine pathway still undiscovered as it pertains to genetic syndromes, the tight regulation of its metabolism, and its adjacence to robustly treated pathways like the urea cycle, increases the potential for drug repurposing.

For decades, researchers have attempted to elucidate the biological role of diet and polyamines, especially in terms of rebalancing abnormal polyamine levels to physiologically normal levels in certain human diseases. While we know that the principal polyamines are synthesized from ornithine endogenously, we also know that polyamines are also supplied exogenously through dietary intake (Larque et al., 2007). Various studies have utilized gas chromatography, thin-layer chromatography, or high-performance liquid chromatography to measure polyamines in food. These analyses determined that putrescine generally tends to be found more often in fermented food or in food containing microorganisms, whereas spermidine and spermine are typically found from raw plant and animal sources (Muñoz-Esparza, 2019; Muñoz-Esparza, 2021). The impact of physiological polyamine levels from dietary intake is difficult to measure and no recommendations currently exist in the U.S. for daily dietary polyamine intake. Researchers have postulated that altering diet may restore polyamine homeostasis in human diseases, such as polyaminopathies or certain cancers. In SRS, which demonstrates a high spermidine to spermine ratio, dietary supplementation of spermine was attempted in mice. After supplementing the diet for 3 weeks, no increase in spermine was measured in any of the key tissues of the mice (Wang et al., 2009).

General understanding of the polyamine pathway and the ability to exert modification is exemplified by the presence of a Gordon Research Conference on polyamines which began in 1975. The interdisciplinary gathering has helped drive forward collaboration and treatment possibilities for many years, all the more so as the polyaminopathies have been recently described and targeted for pharmacotherapy.

Due to the previously described challenges associated with drug development, drug repurposing continues to gain momentum, especially for the treatment of rare diseases. In fact, repurposed drugs now constitute approximately 30% of all drugs approved by the FDA for new indications (Pillaiyar, 2020; Polamreddy, 2019). Despite the success seen with drug repurposing for rare diseases, barriers to approval of repurposed drugs for other indications still exist. These challenges may include disagreements with intellectual property rights, limited numbers of patients with rare diseases in clinical trials to validate hypothesized repurposing strategies, and lack of resources to support research for more accurate repurposing predictions (Fetro, 2020; Krishnamurthy, 2022; Pillaiyar, 2020). Artificial intelligence (AI) or Machine learning (ML) technologies have been used with increasing frequency to improve the accuracy of therapeutic predictions. The algorithms designed in these models have now expanded beyond their original use in drug discovery and present an exciting new avenue for use in drug repurposing. That said, it is uniquely difficult to make predictions for drug repurposing of rare diseases. This is mainly due to the heterogeneous nature of available datasets because of limited numbers of patients, however novel algorithms and networks have been proposed in recent years that aim to improve the accuracy of predicting drugs for rare diseases (Cai, 2023; Cao, 2021). Drug repurposing remains one of the most promising avenues for identifying therapeutic options for rare diseases. Due to the availability of an existing mouse model, validated biochemical assays to confirm DFMO target efficacy, and regrowth of hair as a measurable phenotypic feature after treatment initiation, BABS is an excellent model for drug repurposing in a novel rare disease.

Thanks to advances in rare disease research and strategies for drug repurposing, there is now more hope than ever for the possibility of finding promising treatments for patients with rare diseases. Challenges remain, however, mainly due to the inherently small patient populations associated with these syndromes. The speed at which BABS was first described, DFMO was identified as a potential therapeutic agent for this indication, and treatment was initiated in the first patient is a remarkable example of the power and potential of drug repurposing in rare disease. Advances in polyaminopathy research and describing the natural history of these rare diseases are strengthened by international collaborations through the ICPD and with patient and family advocacy groups such as the Bachmann-Bupp Family Coalition and the Snyder-Robinson Foundation.The journey of repurposing DFMO for the treatment of BABS can ideally model the pursuit of innovative approaches and new collaborations and demonstrate what is possible in the scope of rare disease treatment.
