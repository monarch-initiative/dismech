that of the Clinical Practice Guideline.<br/> Colors in tables and figures correspond to Class of Recommendations and Level of Evidence tables on pages 92–93.</em></div></div></div><div><div> <h3>2.2 The ACHD Anatomic and Physiological Classification System</h3></div><div><div> <h4>3.1. Care Access and Delivery in the ACHD Population</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>1. <span>ACHD</span> programs and clinicians who care for adults with congenital heart disease should follow policies and procedures that reduce barriers, reduce attrition, and improve access to lifelong <span>ACHD</span> care. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div><div> <h2>General Principles</h2></div><div><div> <h3>3.1.1. Transition Education and Transfer of Care</h3></div><div><div> <div>1. All patients with congenital heart disease should receive structured, patient-centered transition education that is age-, sex-, gender-, and developmentally appropriate</div></div><div><div> <div><div>to improve knowledge <span>( <span>1, A</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>reduce loss to care. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. Congenital heart disease programs and clinicians should have transfer-of-care policies and procedures to ensure effective handoffs of patients from a pediatric to an <span>ACHD</span> cardiologist. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>3.1.2. The ACHD Program and Cardiologists</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>1. Patients in <span>ACHD</span> <span>AP</span> classes IB–D, IIA–D, and IIIA–D should be evaluated and managed by or in collaboration with an <span>ACHD</span> cardiologist and, when indicated and accessible, an <span>ACHD</span> program to improve outcomes. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. Patients in <span>ACHD</span> <span>AP</span> class IA should have at least 1 evaluation by an <span>ACHD</span> cardiologist to develop a plan of care. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>3.2. Expertise for Noncardiac Surgery</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> being considered for noncardiac procedures, preprocedural involvement of an <span>ACHD</span> cardiologist is recommended to risk-stratify the patient, determine appropriate procedure location, guide periprocedural planning and support, and assist in preprocedural clinical optimization to improve outcomes. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> <span>AP</span> classifications IC–D, IIB–D, and IIIA–D who undergo noncardiac procedures, postprocedural care should involve collaboration with an <span>ACHD</span> cardiologist to incorporate expert opinion, provide guideline-based therapy, and improve outcomes. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> <span>AP</span> classifications IC–D, IIC–D, and IIIA–D, anesthesia for noncardiac procedures should be administered by, or in collaboration or consultation with, an anesthesiologist with <span>ACHD</span> expertise to reduce the likelihood of perioperative complications. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>3.3. Mental Health and Neurocognitive Assessment</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>1. Adults with congenital heart disease should be assessed for mental health conditions, including depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic stress disorder, to identify those who may benefit from mental health services. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. For adults with congenital heart disease who have mental health concerns, referral to a mental health clinician can be beneficial to improve psychological symptoms and quality of life. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. For adults with congenital heart disease, neuropsychological testing may be considered to guide therapies that enhance academic, behavioral, psychosocial, and adaptive functioning. <span>( <span>2b, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. For adults with congenital heart disease, evaluation for cognitive decline and frailty may be considered to identify patients who can benefit from additional support. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>3.4. Expertise for Cardiac Surgery and Invasive Cardiac Procedures</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> who undergo invasive cardiac procedures, preprocedural involvement of an <span>ACHD</span> cardiologist is recommended to risk-stratify the patient, determine appropriate procedure location, guide periprocedural planning and support, and assist in preprocedural clinical optimization to improve outcomes. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In patients with <span>ACHD</span>, invasive cardiac procedures involving congenital lesions should be performed by operators with expertise in congenital heart disease procedures to ensure appropriate periprocedural care. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> undergoing invasive cardiac procedures, postprocedural care should involve collaboration with an <span>ACHD</span> cardiologist to incorporate expert opinion, provide guideline-based therapy, and improve outcomes. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> <span>AP</span> classifications IC–D, IIA–D, and IIIA–D, anesthesia for invasive cardiac procedures should be administered by, or in collaboration or consultation with, an anesthesiologist who has congenital heart disease expertise to reduce the likelihood of perioperative complications. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>5. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> who undergo invasive cardiac procedures for acquired cardiovascular disease, it can be beneficial for operators with expertise in procedural treatment of acquired disease to collaborate with <span>ACHD</span> experts. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>3.5. Management of Cyanosis</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>1. Patients with <span>ACHD</span> and cyanosis should receive annual screening for and treatment of iron deficiency to improve exercise capacity and functional status. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> and cyanosis who present with persistent, new, or worsening neurologic deficits, urgent brain imaging to exclude cerebral abscess or stroke should be performed. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> and cyanosis who present with symptoms of hyperviscosity, rehydration with oral or intravenous fluids should be performed to improve symptoms and reduce the risk for vascular complications. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. Pregnant patients with <span>ACHD</span> and cyanosis should be closely followed for the entire duration of the pregnancy by a multidisciplinary cardio-obstetrics team of experts in maternal-fetal medicine, <span>ACHD</span>, and obstetric anesthesia to reduce the risk for pregnancy-related cardiovascular and obstetric complications. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>5. Patients with <span>ACHD</span> and cyanosis who are of childbearing age should receive comprehensive family planning consultation, including recommendations on safe and reliable contraceptive methods, from a multidisciplinary team of experts in family planning and <span>ACHD</span>. <span>( <span>1, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>6. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> and cyanosis who experience recurrent unexplained hypertension or tachycardia, evaluation to detect pheochromocytomas and paragangliomas is reasonable. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>7. In patients with <span>ACHD</span>, cyanosis, and recurrent episodes of joint pain, it is reasonable to check serum uric acid levels and evaluate for gout. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>8. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> and cyanosis, the use of supplemental oxygen during exercise may be considered to improve exercise capacity. <span>( <span>2b, B-R</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>9. In patients with <span>ACHD</span>, hematocrit &gt;65%, and hyperviscosity symptoms that persist despite adequate rehydration and treatment of iron deficiency, phlebotomy may be considered. <span>( <span>2b, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>10. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> and cyanosis, prophylactic phlebotomy is <span>not</span> recommended. <span>( <span>3 - No Benefit, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>3.6.    Heart Failure and Transplantation</h4></div><div><div> <h5>3.6.1. Heart Failure</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> and heart failure symptoms who have systemic left ventricular (<span>LV</span>) dysfunction and biventricular circulation, and who otherwise meet accepted criteria for cardiac resynchronization therapy (<span>CRT</span>), use of <span>CRT</span> is reasonable to improve heart failure symptoms. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> and heart failure symptoms who have systemic <span>LV</span> systolic dysfunction and biventricular circulation, use of <span>GDMT</span> for heart failure is reasonable to improve heart failure symptoms. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> and heart failure symptoms who have systemic <span>LV</span> dysfunction and biventricular circulation, who otherwise meet accepted criteria, use of a primary-prevention implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (<span>ICD</span>) is reasonable to prevent sudden cardiac death (<span>SCD</span>). <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>3.6.2. Heart Transplantation and MCS</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> and heart failure, evaluation by specialists with medical and surgical experience in <span>ACHD</span>, as well as by specialists with expertise in advanced heart failure, is recommended to assess appropriateness for advanced heart failure therapies, such as heart transplantation or <span>MCS</span>. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> who are considered for heart transplantation, multiorgan transplantation (heart–lung, heart–kidney, or heart–liver) is reasonable based on center-specific assessment of the degree of respective lung, kidney, or liver dysfunction. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> and advanced heart failure that is refractory to optimal medical and device-based therapy, the use of durable <span>MCS</span> devices is reasonable to improve quality of life or as a bridge to transplantation. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>3.7. Genetic Syndromes Screening</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>1. For adults with conotruncal defects, screening for 22q11.2 deletion syndrome is reasonable to assess the risk of transmission to offspring and to screen for associated comorbidities. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In adults with congenital heart disease and a family history of congenital heart disease or clinical features associated with a genetic syndrome, referral for genetic evaluation is reasonable to guide management. <span>( <span>2a, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>3.8. Reproductive Health, Pregnancy, and Contraception</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In adults with congenital heart disease of childbearing age, counseling on contraceptive options is recommended to inform pregnancy-related shared decision-making. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. Adults with congenital heart disease interested in pregnancy should receive preconception counseling, in collaboration with an <span>ACHD</span> cardiologist, about the maternal cardiac, obstetric, and fetal risks associated with pregnancy and the potential long-term risks to the pregnant individual. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. Screening fetal echocardiography is indicated to detect congenital heart disease if either parent has <span>ACHD</span> to optimize delivery planning and access to care. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In pregnant patients with <span>ACHD</span> <span>AP</span> classifications IB–D, IIA–D, and IIIA–D, an individualized care plan, including mode of delivery, need for cardiac monitoring, and anesthetic support, should be documented in the medical record and communicated to the patient and care team, to reduce maternal risks for morbidity and mortality. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>5. In pregnant patients with <span>ACHD</span> <span>AP</span> classifications IB–D, IIA–D, and IIIA–D, management should be provided by, or in collaboration with, a cardio-obstetrics team with expertise in <span>ACHD</span> and maternal-fetal medicine, to improve clinical outcomes. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>6. Patients with <span>ACHD</span> who have high risk for maternal morbidity and mortality should be counseled to avoid pregnancy and given guidance on effective contraception to improve outcomes. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>7. For pregnant patients with <span>ACHD</span> who have high risk for morbidity and mortality, the option of terminating pregnancy should be discussed to reduce that risk. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>8. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> who are of childbearing age, referral to a genetic counselor for preconception planning is reasonable to facilitate understanding of the risks for congenital heart disease in their offspring. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>9. For patients with <span>ACHD</span> who are considering assisted reproductive technology, discussions involving an <span>ACHD</span> cardiologist can be beneficial to increase patients’ knowledge about their options and the associated risks. <span>( <span>2a, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>10. Routine cesarean delivery has no benefit and may cause harm in pregnant patients with <span>ACHD</span> in the absence of an obstetric indication or high-risk cardiac condition. <span>( <span>3 - No Benefit, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>11. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> who are at high risk for thromboembolic events, contraceptive agents that contain estrogen are potentially harmful. <span>( <span>3 - Harm, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>3.9. Ionizing Radiation Exposure</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In adults with congenital heart disease, processes to limit radiation exposure during imaging and cardiovascular procedures are recommended to reduce the lifetime risk for cancer. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>3.10. Exercise Testing and Sports</h4></div><div><div> <h5>3.10.1. Exercise Testing</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In patients with <span>ACHD</span>, <span>CPET</span> can be useful for establishing a baseline functional assessment, guiding clinical care, stratifying risk for major adverse cardiovascular events, and ongoing surveillance. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In patients with <span>ACHD</span> and established <span>PAH</span>, or in those unable to perform <span>CPET</span> yielding reliable diagnostic information, a 6-minute walk test can be useful to objectively assess symptom severity, functional capacity, and response to therapy. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>3.10.2. Physical Activity, Exercise, and Sports Participation</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. Patients with <span>ACHD</span> should have their activity levels assessed at regular intervals to receive counseling about the types and intensity of exercise appropriate to their clinical status. <span>( <span>1, B-R</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In patients with <span>ACHD</span>, exercise training and cardiac rehabilitation can be useful to increase exercise capacity. <span>( <span>2a, B-R</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In patients with <span>ACHD</span>, <span>CPET</span> can be useful to guide physical activity recommendations. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In patients with <span>ACHD</span>, participation in competitive sports is reasonable following a comprehensive evaluation by an <span>ACHD</span> specialist that incorporates functional status and individualized assessment of risks and benefits. <span>( <span>2a, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div><div> <h2>Specific Conditions</h2></div><div><div> <h3>4.1. Shunt Lesions</h3></div><div><div> <h4>4.1.1. Atrial Septal Defect</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In adults with an unrepaired <span>ASD</span>, <span>CMR</span> imaging, transesophageal echocardiography, or cardiac <span>CT</span> is recommended to define defect size, morphology, rim anatomy, and pulmonary venous connections. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. Adults with an unrepaired <span>ASD</span> and <span>PAH</span> should undergo risk assessment and management in consultation with pulmonary hypertension specialists to improve outcomes. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. Adults with an unrepaired <span>ASD</span> should be assessed for the presence of <span>PAH</span> to guide medical therapy and determine suitability for repair. <span>( <span>1, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In adults with an unrepaired <span>ASD</span>, balloon test occlusion before closure may be reasonable to assess hemodynamic changes. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>Therapeutic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>5. In adults with an unrepaired <span>ASD</span>, significant left-to-right shunt (<span>Qp:Qs</span> ≥ 1.5) and right ventricular (<span>RV</span>) dilation, in the absence of <span>PAH</span> (<span>PVR</span> ≤2 Wood units) or significant <span>LV</span> disease*, <span>ASD</span> closure is recommended to improve functional class and clinical outcomes. <span>( <span>1, B-R</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>6. In adults with an isolated unrepaired secundum <span>ASD</span>, transcatheter closure is usually preferred to surgical repair to reduce length of stay and recovery time. <span>( <span>1, B-R</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>7. In adults with an unrepaired <span>ASD</span> and evidence of (or strong suspicion for) paradoxical embolism, <span>ASD</span> closure is recommended to prevent recurrent embolism. <span>( <span>1, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>8. In adults with an unrepaired <span>ASD</span>, significant left-to-right shunt (<span>Qp:Qs</span> ≥1.5) and <span>RV</span> dilation, with a <span>PVR</span> &gt;2 to&lt;5 Wood units, and without significant <span>LV</span> disease,* <span>ASD</span> closure is reasonable to improve functional status. <span>( <span>2a, B-R</span> )</span></div></div> <div>* Significant left ventricular disease: any condition resulting in chronically elevated left atrial pressure ≥15 mm Hg.</div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>9. In adults with an unrepaired <span>ASD</span>, significant left-to-right shunt (<span>Qp:Qs</span> ≥1.5), <span>RV</span> dilation, <span>PAH</span> (<span>PVR</span> 5 to 8 Wood units), and without significant <span>LV</span> disease, closure can be beneficial if <span>PVR</span> &lt;5 Wood units can be achieved with targeted <span>PAH</span> therapy, to improve medium-term functional status. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>10. In adults with an unrepaired <span>ASD</span> and <span>PAH</span> for whom closure is indicated, fenestrated repair can be considered to improve functional class and clinical outcomes. <span>( <span>2b, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>11. In adults with unrepaired superior sinus venosus <span>ASD</span> (<span>SVASD</span>), transcatheter closure (when technically feasible) may be a reasonable alternative to surgery to reduce operative morbidity. <span>( <span>2b, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>12. In adults with an unrepaired <span>ASD</span>, significant left-to-right shunt (<span>Qp:Qs</span> ≥1.5), and <span>PAH</span> with <span>PVR</span> of 5 to 8 Wood units while receiving targeted <span>PAH</span> therapy, the usefulness of fenestrated <span>ASD</span> closure to improve functional class and clinical outcomes is uncertain. <span>( <span>2b, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>13. In adults with an unrepaired <span>ASD</span> and Eisenmenger physiology, <span>ASD</span> closure should <strong><span>not</span></strong> be performed to avoid increasing morbidity and mortality. <span>( <span>3 - Harm, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>4.1.2. Ventricular Septal Defect</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. Patients with an unrepaired <span>VSD</span> should be assessed for the presence of <span>PAH</span> to guide decisions about medical therapy and repair. <span>( <span>1, B-R</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In adults with a perimembranous <span>VSD</span> being considered for repair, evaluation to exclude <span>RV</span> outflow tract (<span>RVOT</span>) obstruction or double-chambered right ventricle (<span>DCRV</span>) is recommended to improve management planning. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. Adults with a <span>VSD</span> (repaired or unrepaired) and <span>PAH</span> should be followed by specialists with expertise in <span>ACHD</span> and pulmonary hypertension to reduce morbidity and mortality. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>Therapeutic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In adults with a <span>VSD</span>, a significant left-to-right shunt (<span>Qp:Qs</span> ≥1.5), and significant or progressive <span>LV</span> dilatation with no evidence of <span>PAH</span> (<span>PVR</span> ≤2 Wood units), closure of the defect is recommended to eliminate the shunt and preserve ventricular function. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>5. In adults with a <span>VSD</span>, <span>LV</span> volume overload, <span>Qp:Qs</span> ≥1.5:1, and mild <span>PAH</span> (<span>PVR</span> &gt;2 but &lt;5 Wood units), closure of the <span>VSD</span> is reasonable to eliminate the shunt, in order to reduce the risk for progressive <span>PAH</span> and the burden of chronic volume overload on the <span>LV</span>. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>6. In adults with an outlet or perimembranous <span>VSD</span> causing progressive moderate or greater aortic regurgitation, closure of the <span>VSD</span> is reasonable to preserve aortic valve function. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>7. In adults with a recent history of infective endocarditis involving a <span>VSD</span>, closure of the <span>VSD</span> may be reasonable to reduce the risk for recurrent endocarditis. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>8. In adults with a <span>VSD</span>, <span>Qp:Qs</span> ≥1.5, and <span>PAH</span> (<span>PVR</span> 5 to 8 Wood units) without hypoxemia, if <span>PVR</span> drops to &lt;5 Wood units with <span>PAH</span> therapies, usefulness of closure of the <span>VSD</span> is uncertain to reduce the risk for progressive <span>PAH</span> and the burden of chronic volume overload on the left ventricle. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>9. In adults with a <span>VSD</span>, <span>Qp:Qs</span> &lt;1.5, and no other indications, closure should <span>not</span> be performed to avoid unnecessary perioperative risks. <span>( <span>3 - No Benefit, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>10. In adults with a <span>VSD</span> and Eisenmenger physiology, <span>VSD</span> closure should <span>not</span> be performed to avoid escalating morbidity and mortality. <span>( <span>3 - Harm, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>4.1.3. Atrioventricular Septal Defect</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. Adults with an unrepaired <span>AVSD</span> or those with residual shunts after prior repair should be assessed for the presence of <span>PAH</span> to guide medical therapy and assess suitability for repair. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. Adults with an <span>AVSD</span> (repaired or unrepaired) and <span>PAH</span> should be managed by clinicians with pulmonary hypertension expertise to improve outcomes. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>Therapeutic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In adults with repaired <span>AVSD</span> and <span>LV</span> outflow tract (<span>LVOT</span>) obstruction with symptoms attributable to the obstruction, or <span>LV</span> systolic dysfunction (<span>LV</span> ejection fraction &lt;50%), surgical repair is recommended to improve functional status and preserve ventricular function. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In adults with repaired <span>AVSD</span> with a) symptomatic severe left atrioventricular valve regurgitation or b) asymptomatic severe left atrioventricular valve regurgitation with <span>LV</span> dilation (<span>LV</span> end-systolic diameter ≥40 mm) or <span>LV</span> systolic dysfunction (<span>LV</span> ejection fraction &lt;60%), valve surgery is recommended to improve functional status and prevent worsening ventricular function. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>5. In adults with an unrepaired <span>AVSD</span> or those with residual shunts after previous repair, significant left-to-right shunt (<span>Qp:Qs</span> ≥1.5), and/or significant or progressive atrial/ ventricular dilatation with no <span>PAH</span> (<span>PVR</span> ≤2 Wood units), closure of the <span>AVSD</span> or residual shunt is recommended to improve functional class and clinical outcomes. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>6. In adults with an unrepaired <span>AVSD</span> or those with residual shunts after previous repair, significant left-to-right shunt (<span>Qp:Qs</span>
<br/> ≥1.5), and/or significant or progressive atrial/ventricular dilatation with mild <span>PAH</span> (<span>PVR</span> &gt;2 to &lt;5 Wood units), closure is reasonable to improve functional class and clinical outcomes. <span>( <span>2a, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>7. In asymptomatic adults with <span>AVSD</span>, <span>LVOT</span> obstruction, and progressive greater than mild aortic regurgitation, surgical repair may be reasonable to prevent worsening ventricular function and progressive aortic valve disease. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>8. In adults with an unrepaired <span>AVSD</span> or those with residual shunts after previous repair demonstrating Eisenmenger physiology or <span>PVR</span> &gt;10 Wood units, shunt closure should <span>not</span> be performed to avoid additional morbidity and mortality. <span>( <span>3 - Harm, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>4.1.4. Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Connections</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. For adults with APVCs, cardiovascular magnetic resonance (<span>CMR</span>) imaging is recommended to assess pulmonary venous anatomy, to evaluate <span>RV</span> size and function, and to determine shunt fraction (<span>Qp:Qs</span>). <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. For adults with APVCs who do not have an indication for repair, routine cardiac imaging is recommended for serial evaluation of <span>RV</span> size and function and for assessment of pulmonary hypertension. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. For adults with repaired APVCs and dyspnea on exertion and/or reduced functional capacity, <span>CMR</span> or computed tomography (<span>CT</span>) angiography is recommended to evaluate for venous obstruction or residual shunt. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. For adults with unrepaired APVCs and noninvasive evidence of, and/or risk factors for, <span>PAH</span>, cardiac catheterization can be useful to evaluate hemodynamics and aid in determining indication for repair. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>Therapeutic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>5. For adults with APVCs who have cardiac symptoms and/or reduced functional capacity with <span>RV</span> volume overload, and whose <span>Qp:Qs</span> is ≥1.5, surgical repair is recommended to improve symptoms and clinical outcomes. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>6. For adults with APVCs undergoing surgical repair for an <span>ASD</span>, repair of the <span>APVC</span>(s) is recommended, when technically feasible, to reduce left-to-right shunting and <span>RV</span> volume overload. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>7. For adults with APVCs who are asymptomatic; whose <span>Qp:Qs</span> is ≥1.5; and who have severe or progressive <span>RV</span> volume overload, reduced <span>RV</span> systolic function, <span>PAH</span> by invasive measurement, or objective exercise intolerance, surgical repair* is reasonable in order to improve hemodynamics and/ or stabilize <span>RV</span> size and function. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>8. For adults with APVCs who have a normal to mildly enlarged right ventricle and whose <span>Qp:Qs</span> is &lt;1.5, surgical repair is <span>not<strong> </strong></span>indicated, given the absence of data to support benefit. <span>( <span>3 - No Benefit, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>4.1.5. Patent Ductus Arteriosus</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. Adults with a <span>PDA</span> and <span>PAH</span> should be managed by an <span>ACHD</span> specialist and clinicians with pulmonary hypertension expertise, to minimize morbidity and mortality. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. Patients with an unrepaired <span>PDA</span> should be evaluated for <span>PAH</span> to guide recommendations for treatment. <span>( <span>1, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. Adults with a <span>PDA</span> should undergo assessment of <span>LV</span> function and left-sided chamber size with echocardiography and/or <span>CMR</span> imaging, to guide recommendations for treatment. <span>( <span>1, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In adults with a <span>PDA</span> and <span>PAH</span>, balloon test occlusion of the <span>PDA</span> can be useful to facilitate accurate quantification of <span>PVR</span> and to identify patients who are candidates for closure. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>Therapeutic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>5. In adults with a <span>PDA</span>, left heart enlargement attributable to the <span>PDA</span>, a net left-to-right shunt, and no evidence of <span>PAH</span> (<span>PVR</span> ≤2 Wood units), closure to eliminate the shunt is recommended to improve clinical outcomes. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>6. In adults with a <span>PDA</span>, left heart enlargement attributable to the <span>PDA</span>, a net left-to-right shunt, and <span>PVR</span> &gt;2 but &lt;5 Wood units, closure to eliminate the shunt is reasonable to improve clinical outcomes. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>7. In adults with a <span>PDA</span>, left heart enlargement attributable to the <span>PDA</span>, a net left-to-right shunt, and moderate <span>PAH</span> (<span>PVR</span> 5 to 8 Wood units), closure may be considered if the <span>PVR</span> drops to &lt;5 Wood units with initiation of <span>PAH</span> therapies. <span>( <span>2b, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>8. In asymptomatic adults with a <span>PDA</span>, no evidence of left atrial or <span>LV</span> enlargement, and no prior history of endarteritis, closure has no benefit. <span>( <span>3 - No Benefit, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>9. In adults with a <span>PDA</span> and Eisenmenger syndrome or <span>PVR</span> &gt;10 Wood units, closure to eliminate the shunt should <span>not<strong> </strong></span>be performed to avoid additional morbidity or mortality risk. <span>( <span>3 - Harm, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h3>4.2. Left-Sided Lesions</h3></div><div><div> <h4>4.2.1. Cor Triatriatum Sinister</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In adults with unrepaired cor triatriatum sinister, transesophageal echocardiography and/or cross-sectional imaging can be useful to identify the size and location of the membrane orifice and to assess for the presence of associated congenital cardiac defects. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In adults with repaired cor triatriatum sinister and symptoms compatible with recurrent membrane obstruction, it is reasonable to evaluate for the presence of residual congenital cardiac defects, including pulmonary vein stenosis. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>Therapeutic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In adults with unrepaired cor triatriatum sinister and symptoms attributable to membrane obstruction, surgical resection is recommended to reduce the consequences of left atrial hypertension. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In adults with unrepaired cor triatriatum sinister and atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter, prior stroke, or left atrial thrombus, chronic anticoagulation is recommended to prevent embolic stroke, whether or not conventional thromboembolic risk factors are present. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>5. In asymptomatic adults with unrepaired cor triatriatum sinister and severe membrane obstruction, surgical resection can be useful to prevent the sequelae of left atrial hypertension. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>6. In adults with unrepaired cor triatriatum sinister and atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter, a rhythm control strategy can be beneficial to avoid clinical decompensation. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>7. In highly symptomatic adults with unrepaired cor triatriatum sinister, significant membrane obstruction, and elevated operative risk,* catheter-based intervention may be considered as a bridge to surgical resection. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div> <div>* For example, decompensated heart failure, severe pulmonary hypertension, or pregnancy (see supportive text for details).</div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>4.2.2. Congenital Mitral Stenosis, Including Shone Complex</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>1. Adults with congenital mitral stenosis or a parachute mitral valve should be evaluated for additional left-sided obstructive lesions to guide management. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. Adults with multiple left-sided obstructive lesions (Shone complex) and suspected pulmonary hypertension should undergo invasive hemodynamic assessment to identify and classify pulmonary hypertension. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>4.2.3. Subaortic Stenosis</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. For patients with subaortic stenosis and equivocal symptoms, exercise stress testing may be considered to assess the physiological response to exercise and to determine whether symptoms are attributable to subaortic stenosis. <span>( <span>2b, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>Therapeutic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>2. For patients with symptoms attributable to subaortic stenosis, surgical repair is recommended to alleviate symptoms. <span>( <span>1, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. For asymptomatic patients with <span>LV</span> dysfunction or <span>LV</span> hypertrophy in the setting of subaortic stenosis, surgical repair may be considered to mitigate worsening <span>LV</span> function, hypertrophy, or both. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. For asymptomatic patients with subaortic stenosis and at least mild aortic regurgitation, surgical repair may be considered to prevent progressive aortic regurgitation. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>4.2.4. Congenital Diseases of the Aortic Valve</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. Adults with a <span>BAV</span> should be screened for the presence of COA and aortic aneurysm to obtain early diagnosis and determine a management plan. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. Adults with a <span>BAV</span> who have an aortic diameter &gt;4 cm at the level of the sinuses or the ascending aorta, or who have risk factors for aortic dissection,* should undergo lifelong surveillance for progression of thoracic aortic disease to improve early detection of aortic growth.<sup>†</sup> <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div> <div>* Risk factors: family history of aortic dissection, aortic growth rate ≥0.3 cm/year, COA, “root phenotype” aortopathy.
<br/> <sup>†</sup> Modified from the “2022 <span>ACC</span>/AHA Guideline for the Diagnosis and Management of Aortic Disease.”</div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In first-degree relatives of adults with a <span>BAV</span>, 1-time echocardiographic screening for the presence of a congenitally abnormal aortic valve or aortic aneurysm is reasonable to obtain early diagnosis. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>Therapeutic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In adults with a congenitally abnormal aortic valve who meet indication for valve replacement, surgical and transcatheter options should be reviewed by heart valve and <span>ACHD</span> expert teams in a shared decision-making process to determine a management plan. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>5. In young adults with congenital aortic valve stenosis, a noncalcified valve with no more than mild aortic regurgitation, and an indication for intervention, balloon valvuloplasty may be reasonable to delay the need for aortic valve surgery. <span>( <span>2b, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>4.2.5. Supravalvar Aortic Stenosis</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In adults with suspected supravalvar aortic stenosis or with Williams syndrome, baseline aortic imaging is recommended to define the anatomy and severity of supravalvar aortic stenosis. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In adults with Williams syndrome and supravalvar aortic stenosis, baseline coronary imaging is recommended to evaluate for abnormalities such as coronary ostial stenosis. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>Therapeutic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In adults with supravalvar aortic stenosis who are symptomatic and/or have diminished <span>LV</span> systolic function due to stenosis, surgical repair is recommended to alleviate symptoms and preserve ventricular function. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. Adults with supravalvar aortic stenosis and coronary ostial stenosis with symptoms of ischemia should undergo coronary artery revascularization to alleviate symptoms and preserve ventricular function. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>5. In adults with asymptomatic severe supravalvar aortic stenosis, normal ventricular size and function, and a normal exercise stress test, surgical repair may be considered to avoid worsening of cardiac function and symptoms. <span>( <span>2b, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>4.2.6. Coarctation of the Aorta</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In adults with unrepaired COA, cross-sectional imaging is recommended to determine COA severity and delineate anatomy of the thoracic aorta. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In adults with repaired COA, periodic cross-sectional imaging is recommended to screen for recurrent COA, aortic root/ascending aorta aneurysms, and vascular complications related to COA repair (focal aortic aneurysm/ pseudoaneurysms/dissection, and stent fracture).* <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div> <div>* See Table 24.</div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In adults with COA, blood pressure measurement at rest in the upper and lower extremities is recommended as part of routine clinical assessment to screen for hypertension and residual/recurrent COA. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In adults with COA, ambulatory blood pressure measurement is reasonable to diagnose hypertension and monitor response to antihypertensive therapy. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>5. In adults with COA, exercise testing is reasonable to screen for exercise-induced hypertension. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>6. In adults with COA who present with exertional symptoms, evaluation for coronary artery disease is reasonable to guide management. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>7. In adults with COA, screening for intracranial aneurysms by brain <span>MR</span> or <span>CT</span> angiography may be considered to guide management. <span>( <span>2b, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>Therapeutic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>8. In adults with native or repaired COA, surgical or transcatheter stent therapy is recommended for anatomically and hemodynamically significant COA to prevent irreversible <span>LV</span> dysfunction from chronic pressure overload.<sup>†</sup> <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div> <div>† See Table 25.</div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>9. In adults with native or repaired COA and hypertension without hemodynamically significant COA, <span>GDMT</span> for hypertension is recommended to maintain controlled resting and ambulatory blood pressure. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h3>4.3. Right-Sided Lesions</h3></div><div><div> <h4>4.3.1. Ebstein Anomaly</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In adults with Ebstein anomaly, an electrophysiological study is recommended for patients with symptomatic arrhythmia or asymptomatic ventricular preexcitation to assess for substrates for ablation, including multiple or high-risk accessory pathways. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In adults with Ebstein anomaly, <span>CMR</span> imaging can be useful to determine anatomy, <span>RV</span> dimensions, and systolic function to guide surgical planning and management. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In adults with Ebstein anomaly, electrophysiological study (and catheter ablation, if needed) can be useful before tricuspid valve surgery to improve clinical outcomes. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In adults with Ebstein anomaly, if transthoracic echocardiography is inadequate to guide surgical planning, transesophageal echocardiography can be beneficial to characterize tricuspid valve morphology and function. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>5. In adults with Ebstein anomaly and severe <span>RV</span> dysfunction in whom there is concern that the <span>RV</span> will not support the entire stroke volume, preoperative diagnostic cardiac catheterization may be considered to assess candidacy for a bidirectional superior cavopulmonary (Glenn) anastomosis. <span>( <span>2b, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>Therapeutic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>6. In adults with Ebstein anomaly and at least moderate tricuspid regurgitation in the presence of heart failure, worsening exercise capacity, or progressive <span>RV</span> systolic dysfunction, surgical intervention is recommended to restore valve function and augment cardiac output. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>7. In adults with Ebstein anomaly and high-risk accessory pathway conduction or multiple accessory pathways, catheter ablation is recommended to reduce the risk for <span>SCD</span>. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>8. In asymptomatic adults with Ebstein anomaly and at least moderate tricuspid regurgitation in the presence of progressive <span>RV</span> enlargement, systemic desaturation from a right-to-left atrial shunt, recurrent arrhythmias, and/or paradoxical embolism, surgical intervention can be beneficial to improve clinical outcomes. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>9. In adults with Ebstein anomaly and severe <span>RV</span> dysfunction in whom there is concern that the <span>RV</span> will not tolerate supporting the entire stroke volume, a bidirectional superior cavopulmonary (Glenn) anastomosis at the time of tricuspid valve surgery may be considered in carefully selected individuals to improve cardiac output through ventricular unloading. <span>( <span>2b, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>10. In adults with Ebstein anomaly who have an <span>ASD</span>/patent foramen ovale, and cyanosis or paradoxical embolism, isolated closure may be considered after hemodynamic assessment to improve clinical outcomes. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>4.3.2. Valvular Pulmonary Stenosis</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In symptomatic adults with moderate or severe valvular pulmonary stenosis who have favorable valve anatomy, balloon valvuloplasty is recommended to improve symptoms. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In symptomatic adults with moderate or severe valvular pulmonary stenosis who are ineligible for, or have had suboptimal results after balloon valvuloplasty, pulmonary valve replacement is recommended to improve symptoms. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In asymptomatic adults with moderate or severe valvular pulmonary stenosis and at least moderate tricuspid regurgitation or any <span>RV</span> dysfunction, pulmonary valve intervention is recommended to reduce pressure overload and improve valvular function. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In asymptomatic adults with severe valvular pulmonary stenosis, intervention is reasonable to reduce pressure overload and improve valvular function. <span>( <span>2a, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>4.3.2.1. Isolated Pulmonary Regurgitation After Repair of Pulmonary Stenosis</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In asymptomatic patients who have moderate or greater pulmonary regurgitation resulting from treatment of isolated pulmonary stenosis and at least moderate tricuspid regurgitation and/or progressive <span>RV</span> dilation or progressive decline in exercise capacity,* pulmonary valve replacement may be reasonable to restore valve function, ameliorate <span>RV</span> size, and preserve <span>RV</span> systolic function. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div> <div>* See Figure 5.</div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>4.3.3. Management of Double-Chambered Right Ventricle</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In adults with <span>DCRV</span>, moderate or greater <span>RVOT</span> obstruction, and otherwise unexplained symptoms of heart failure, cyanosis, or exercise limitation, surgical repair is recommended to improve symptoms. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In asymptomatic adults with <span>DCRV</span> and severe <span>RVOT</span> obstruction, surgical repair is reasonable to prevent symptoms and adverse <span>RV</span> remodeling. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>4.3.4. Isolated Branch Pulmonary Artery Stenosis</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In adults with peripheral or branch <span>PA</span> stenosis, echocardiography and cross-sectional imaging (cardiac <span>CT</span> or <span>CMR</span>) are recommended to evaluate <span>RV</span> pressure and systolic function, degree of tricuspid regurgitation, and degree and location of stenosis. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>Therapeutic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>2. Tetralogy of Fallot</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In adults with repaired <span>TOF</span>, echocardiography is recommended for assessment of pulmonary and tricuspid valve dysfunction, right atrial size, ventricular size and function, residual VSDs, and estimation of <span>RV</span> systolic pressure to characterize residual hemodynamic sequelae. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In adults with repaired <span>TOF</span> undergoing right ventricle-to-pulmonary artery (<span>RV</span>-to-<span>PA</span>) conduit stenting or transcatheter pulmonary valve placement with a balloon-expandable platform, assessment of coronary artery compression risk is indicated to ensure absence of coronary artery compression from stent/valve placement. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In adults with repaired <span>TOF</span> and native <span>RVOT</span> anatomy being considered for transcatheter pulmonary valve replacement, cardiac <span>CT</span> is recommended to determine anatomic suitability. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>5. In adults with repaired <span>TOF</span> and moderate risk for future sustained ventricular tachycardia and/or <span>SCD</span>, invasive electrophysiology evaluation is reasonable to inform clinical management. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>6. In adults with repaired <span>TOF</span> in whom adequate data cannot be obtained noninvasively in the setting of an arrhythmia, heart failure, unexplained ventricular dysfunction, suspected pulmonary hypertension, residual hemodynamic lesions, or cyanosis, cardiac catheterization with angiography is reasonable for assessing hemodynamics to direct management and therapies. <span>( <span>2a, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>7. In adults with repaired <span>TOF</span> undergoing pulmonary valve placement (surgical or transcatheter), preprocedural invasive electrophysiology evaluation with programmed ventricular stimulation and 3-dimensional mapping may be considered to identify and treat latent monomorphic ventricular tachycardia substrates. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>8. In adults with repaired <span>TOF</span>, noninvasive evaluation (12-lead electrocardiography, contrast-enhanced <span>MR</span> imaging, and/ or <span>CT</span> angiography) may be considered to characterize ventricular tachycardia substrates. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>Therapeutic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>9. In adults with repaired <span>TOF</span> and high risk (by clinical markers and/or multivariable score†) for <span>SCD</span>, placement of an <span>ICD</span> is reasonable to prevent <span>SCD</span>. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div> <div>† Refer to Table 32 and Table 33 for individual risk scores.</div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>12. In adults with repaired <span>TOF</span> and appropriate <span>ICD</span> therapies for monomorphic ventricular tachycardia, adjunctive catheter ablation is reasonable to reduce ventricular tachyarrhythmia burden. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>13. In adults with repaired <span>TOF</span> with moderate or greater pulmonary valve dysfunction* and progressive ventricular systolic dysfunction, pulmonary valve replacement (surgical or transcatheter) is reasonable to preserve ventricular function. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div> <div>* Pulmonary valve dysfunction defined as moderate <span>PR</span> (<span>CMR</span>-derived <span>RF</span> ≥25%) or <span>RVSP</span> &gt;2/3 systemic pressure due to <span>RVOT</span> obstruction.</div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>14. In adults with repaired <span>TOF</span> with at least moderate pulmonary valve dysfunction* and progressive functional tricuspid regurgitation of a moderate or greater severity associated with <span>RV</span> dilation, pulmonary valve intervention may be reasonable to prevent worsening tricuspid valve dysfunction and <span>RV</span> dilation. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div> <div>* Pulmonary valve dysfunction defined as moderate <span>PR</span> (<span>CMR</span>-derived <span>RF</span> ≥25%) or <span>RVSP</span> &gt;2/3 systemic pressure due to <span>RVOT</span> obstruction.</div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>15. For carefully selected patients with repaired <span>TOF</span> and hemodynamically tolerated monomorphic ventricular tachycardia related to a well-defined anatomical isthmus, ablation monotherapy may be considered in lieu of <span>ICD</span> placement at centers with <span>ACHD</span> electrophysiology expertise. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>16. In adults with repaired <span>TOF</span> and moderate or greater pulmonary regurgitation and ventricular tachyarrhythmia requiring treatment,‡ pulmonary valve replacement (surgical or percutaneous), in addition to electrophysiological intervention, may be considered. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div> <div>‡ Antiarrhythmic classes I/III or amiodarone, catheter ablation, electrical cardioversion, defibrillation, pace-termination, or <span>ICD</span> shock for sustained ventricular arrhythmia.</div></div></div><div><div> <div><span>CMR</span> indicates cardiovascular magnetic resonance; <span>ICD</span>, internal cardioverter-defibrillator; <span>PR</span>, pulmonary regurgitation; <span>RF</span>, regurgitant fraction; <span>RV</span>, right ventricular; <span>RVOT</span>, <span>RV</span> outflow tract; <span>RVSP</span>, <span>RV</span> systolic pressure.</div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h3>4.3.6. Pulmonary Atresia With Intact Ventricular Septum</h3></div><div><div> <h4>4.3.7. Management of Right Ventricle-to-Pulmonary Artery Conduits</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In patients undergoing <span>RV</span>-to-<span>PA</span> conduit stent implantation and/ or transcatheter pulmonary valve implantation, preprocedural assessment of the risk for coronary artery compression should be performed to prevent coronary artery obstruction. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In patients with a stent implanted within an <span>RV</span>-to-<span>PA</span> conduit who have unanticipated progression in conduit dysfunction, imaging should be performed to rule out conduit stent fracture. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In patients with an <span>RV</span>-to-<span>PA</span> conduit, with or without transcatheter pulmonary valve implantation, unexpected conduit dysfunction with worsening pulmonary stenosis or pulmonary regurgitation should prompt an evaluation to rule out infective endocarditis and/or thrombus. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. Adults with dextro-transposition of the great arteries (d-<span>TGA</span>) and atrial switch who have progressive exercise intolerance or heart failure should undergo imaging with transthoracic echocardiogram and <span>CMR</span> imaging to assess ventricular, valve, baffle, and pathway function to detect and identify treatable hemodynamic lesions. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. For adults with d-<span>TGA</span> and atrial switch, biomarkers and validated disease-specific risk scores can be useful to identify patients at high risk for adverse cardiac events, prompting closer follow-up and referral to specialized heart failure transplant centers. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>6. In adults with d-<span>TGA</span> and atrial switch with new or progressive symptoms of heart failure, it is reasonable to obtain a resting electrocardiogram and outpatient ambulatory rhythm monitoring, to ensure timely diagnosis and treatment of tachyarrhythmia or bradyarrhythmia. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>7. In adults with d-<span>TGA</span> and atrial switch who have atrial arrhythmias, rhythm control strategies are preferable to rate control strategies to reduce symptoms and prevent heart failure. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>9. In adults with d-<span>TGA</span> and atrial switch who have symptoms attributable to systemic or pulmonary venous pathway stenosis or liver congestion, intervention to relieve the stenosis is indicated to improve symptoms and prognosis. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>11. Adults with d-<span>TGA</span> and atrial switch who have worsening symptoms of exercise intolerance, heart failure, or arrhythmia refractory to treatment should be referred to a heart failure program for assessment for mechanical support and transplantation in consultation with an <span>ACHD</span> cardiologist, to improve quality of life and prolong survival. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>12. In adults with d-<span>TGA</span> and atrial switch who have heart failure with reduced <span>RV</span> function, it may be reasonable to use <span>GDMT</span> for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction in order to treat the heart failure and improve symptoms, ventricular function, and outcomes. <span>( <span>2b, B-R</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>4.4.1.2. Arterial Switch Operation</h5></div><div><div> <h6>Diagnostic</h6></div><div><div> <div><div>1. Patients With d-TGA and Rastelli Repair</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In symptomatic adults with <span>CCTGA</span> and high-grade atrioventricular block, physiological pacing (<span>CRT</span> or conduction system pacing) is reasonable to relieve symptoms. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>7. In adults with <span>CCTGA</span> and heart failure with <span>RV</span> dysfunction, the use of <span>GDMT</span> may be reasonable to improve ventricular function and functional capacity. <span>( <span>2b, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>8. In symptomatic adults with <span>CCTGA</span> and severe <span>LVOT</span> stenosis, surgical or transcatheter therapy to relieve <span>LVOT</span> obstruction may be considered to improve symptoms and preserve <span>LV</span> systolic function while accounting for the potential risk of worsening the severity of tricuspid regurgitation. <span>( <span>2b, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h3>4.4.5. Fontan Palliation of Single-Ventricle Physiology</h3></div><div><div> <h4>Diagnostic</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In adults with Fontan circulation and evidence of progressive Fontan circulatory failure, formal evaluation by a heart failure/ transplant cardiologist with experience in heart transplantation in the adult Fontan population is recommended. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In adults with Fontan circulation, imaging and laboratory evaluation of the liver should be performed at least annually to screen for hepatocellular carcinoma and for evidence of progressive Fontan-associated liver disease (<span>FALD</span>). <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In adults being considered for Fontan palliation or Fontan conversion, cardiac catheterization should be performed to ensure the presence of acceptable hemodynamics. <span>( <span>1, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In adults with Fontan circulation and new-onset or worsening atrial tachyarrhythmias, evaluation with imaging and follow-up cardiac catheterization as indicated is recommended to assess for potential contributory anatomic or hemodynamic abnormalities and Fontan-pathway thrombosis. <span>( <span>1, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>5. In adults with Fontan circulation and new or progressive symptoms, hypoxemia, declining functional status, or evidence of progressive or new-onset noncardiac organ dysfunction, hemodynamic evaluation with cardiac catheterization is recommended to guide therapy. <span>( <span>1, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>6. In adults with Fontan circulation and newly progressive or severe hypoxemia or hypotension, evaluation with advanced cardiac imaging (<span>MR</span>, <span>CT</span>, transesophageal echocardiography) is recommended to rule out thrombus or emboli in the Fontan or pulmonary vasculature. <span>( <span>1, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>7. In adults with Fontan circulation undergoing <span>CT</span> angiography to rule out thrombus or emboli in the Fontan or pulmonary vasculature, imaging should be performed using protocols to avoid false-negative or false-positive results. <span>( <span>1, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>8. In adults with Fontan circulation, annual laboratory evaluation is recommended to assess for evidence of organ-system dysfunction or hematologic abnormality. <span>( <span>1, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>9. In adults with Fontan circulation, consultation by a hepatologist, in collaboration with an <span>ACHD</span> cardiologist, is reasonable to facilitate interpretation of hepatic testing, diagnose and treat complications related to portal hypertension, and participate in timing and management decisions specific to organ transplantation. <span>( <span>2a, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>10. In adults with Fontan circulation, liver biopsy can be helpful to delineate the degree of hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis before consideration of transplantation. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>11. In adults with Fontan circulation undergoing cardiac catheterization (diagnostic, interventional, or electrophysiological), it may be reasonable to exclude Fontan-related thrombus before catheter placement to avoid pulmonary or systemic thromboembolism. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>Therapeutic</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>12. In adults with Fontan circulation and a known or suspected thrombus, atriopulmonary Fontan, a history of thromboembolism, or a history of sustained atrial flutter or fibrillation, anticoagulation is recommended to reduce the likelihood of thromboembolic disease. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>13. In adults with Fontan circulation and the absence of high-risk features (history of thromboembolism, sustained atrial flutter/fibrillation, or atriopulmonary Fontan) or bleeding contraindications, treatment with either aspirin or anticoagulation is recommended to reduce the probability of thromboembolic disease. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>14. In adults with Fontan circulation and sinus node dysfunction requiring pacemaker placement, atrial-based pacing with programming to minimize ventricular pacing is recommended to improve patient symptoms and to avoid pacing-induced cardiomyopathy. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>15. In adults with Fontan circulation with new-onset atrial flutter or atrial fibrillation, timely cardioversion (pharmacological or electrical) is recommended to prevent clinical decompensation. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>16. Adults with Fontan circulation referred for cardiac transplantation should undergo pretransplantation review by a committee with broad multidisciplinary representation to improve patient selection and posttransplant outcomes. <span>( <span>1, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>17. In adults with Fontan circulation and atrioventricular block whose burden of ventricular pacing is likely to be high (ie, &gt;40%), apical site pacing is preferred over nonapical sites to improve transplant-free survival. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>18. In adults with Fontan circulation, formal exercise programs and/or cardiac rehabilitation (appropriate to the patient’s ability) can be beneficial in improving functional capacity. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>19. In adults with Fontan circulation and recurrent supraventricular tachycardia, catheter ablation performed by an electrophysiologist with experience in <span>ACHD</span> is reasonable to reduce arrhythmia recurrence and associated morbidity. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>20. In adults with Fontan circulation and hemodynamically significant stenosis of the Fontan or PAs, catheter-based stenting of the stenosis to reduce Fontan pressure and improve flow is reasonable to improve hemodynamics. <span>( <span>2a, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>21. In symptomatic adults with Fontan circulation, or those with evidence of systemic ventricular volume overload, closure of aortopulmonary collaterals to improve symptoms and reduce the likelihood of pathologic ventricular remodeling can be useful. <span>( <span>2a, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>22. In adults with Fontan circulation, treatment with pulmonary vasodilators may be considered to improve exercise capacity. <span>( <span>2b, B-R</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>23. In adults with an atriopulmonary Fontan, preserved ventricular function, and acceptable surgical risk with atrial arrhythmias refractory to medical and catheter-based therapies, Fontan conversion surgery may be considered to reduce arrhythmia recurrence. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>24. In adults with Fontan circulation and symptoms or Fontan circulatory failure attributable to significant intracardiac structural or anatomic abnormalities, reoperation may be considered to improve symptoms or reduce the likelihood of progressive Fontan circulatory failure. <span>( <span>2b, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>4.4.5.1. HLHS/Norwood Repair</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In patients with <span>HLHS</span>, routine 3-dimensional imaging of the neoaorta to assess for dilation and obstruction is reasonable. <span>( <span>2a, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h3>4.4.6. Eisenmenger Syndrome</h3></div><div><div> <h4>Diagnostic</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In adults with suspected Eisenmenger syndrome, invasive hemodynamic assessment should be performed to confirm the diagnosis and to exclude concomitant disease that contributes to right-to-left shunting or <span>PAH</span>. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. Adults with Eisenmenger syndrome should be managed by specialists with expertise in both congenital cardiology and <span>PAH</span> to improve outcomes. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. Adults with Eisenmenger syndrome should be advised <span>against</span> pregnancy to decrease the risk associated with excess maternal morbidity and mortality. <span>( <span>3 - Harm, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>Therapeutic</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In adults with Eisenmenger syndrome and an <span>LV</span> ejection fraction &gt;40% who are symptomatic or have reduced exercise capacity, initial monotherapy with <span>PAH</span>-directed therapy* is recommended to improve symptoms, hemodynamics, and overall survival. <span>( <span>1, B-R</span> )</span></div></div> <div>* See Table 43 for evidence and recommendations on specific <span>PAH</span> therapies.</div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>5. In adults with Eisenmenger syndrome and an <span>LV</span> ejection fraction &gt;40% who remain symptomatic or have worsening exercise capacity on a single <span>PAH</span> therapy, dual combination therapy with an endothelin receptor antagonist and a phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor is recommended to improve symptoms, hemodynamics, and overall survival. <span>( <span>1, B-R</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>6. In adults with Eisenmenger syndrome who present with atrial arrhythmias, prompt restoration and maintenance of sinus rhythm is recommended to avoid hemodynamic deterioration. <span>( <span>1, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>7. In adults with Eisenmenger syndrome, a regular exercise program in combination with directed <span>PAH</span> therapy can be effective in improving exercise capacity. <span>( <span>2a, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>8. In adults with Eisenmenger syndrome and <span>LV</span> ejection fraction &gt;40% who are deemed to be at high risk for adverse outcomes† or who have rapidly progressive disease, inhaled or subcutaneous prostacyclin therapy may be considered as initial therapy or as an addition to other <span>PAH</span> therapy to improve symptoms, exercise capacity, and hemodynamics. <span>( <span>2b, C-LD</span> )</span></div></div> <div>† Patients at high risk include patients with World Health Organization functional class 3 or 4, a 6-minute walk distance &lt;165 m, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion &lt;16 mm, and NT-proBNP level &gt;1,400 pg/mL.</div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>9. In adults with Eisenmenger syndrome, anticoagulation therapy may be considered for patients with high-risk features (atrial arrhythmia, new or enlarging pulmonary arterial thrombosis, or prior thromboembolic event) and low bleeding risk to prevent thromboembolic complications. <span>( <span>2b, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>10. Adults with Eisenmenger syndrome should <span>not </span>be routinely prescribed oral anticoagulation given the high bleeding risk and lack of long-term survival benefit. <span>( <span>3 - No Benefit, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>11. In adults with Eisenmenger syndrome, closure of any intracardiac or vascular shunt should <span>not</span> be performed given the increased perioperative risks and risks for short- and long-term morbidity and mortality. <span>( <span>3 - Harm, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>12. In adults with Eisenmenger syndrome and intracardiac shunts who meet indications for permanent pacing or an <span>ICD</span>, endocardial leads may be potentially harmful given the increased risk for systemic thromboembolism. <span>( <span>3 - Harm, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div>Recommendations on managing chronic cyanosis—including, but not limited to, management of iron supplementation, hyperviscosity, and risk and management of central nervous system infections—generally apply to patients with Eisenmenger syndrome and are provided in Section 3.5, “Management of Cyanosis.”</div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h3>4.5. Coronary Artery Anomalies</h3></div><div><div> <h4>4.5.1. Anomalous Aortic Origin of a Coronary Artery</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In adults with suspected anomalous origin of a coronary artery (<span>AAOCA</span>), coronary <span>CT</span> angiography or <span>MR</span> angiography (if <span>CT</span> angiography is contraindicated) is recommended to confirm the anatomic diagnosis and guide management. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In adults with an <span>AAOCA</span> from the opposite sinus, risk stratification with evaluation for physiological and/or ischemic changes should be performed to guide management. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>Therapeutic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In adults with symptomatic <span>AAOCA</span> or diagnostic evidence consistent with myocardial ischemia attributable to the anomalous artery, surgery is recommended. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. In adults with asymptomatic anomalous origin of the left coronary artery, surgery is reasonable in the presence of high-risk anatomy.* <span>( <span>2a, C-EO</span> )</span></div></div> <div>* High-risk findings such as ostial or proximal stenosis, slit-like orifice, acute angle of takeoff, or intramural course.</div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>5. In asymptomatic adults with an <span>AAOCA</span> from the opposite sinus and without evidence of ischemia or evidence of compromised coronary perfusion, the benefit of surgery is not well established, and continued observation or surgery may be reasonable. <span>( <span>2b, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h4>4.5.2. Anomalous Coronary Artery Arising From the Pulmonary Artery</h4></div><div><div> <div><div>1. In adults with an anomalous left coronary artery arising from the <span>PA</span>, surgery is recommended to improve cardiac function and reduce the risk for myocardial ischemia. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. In symptomatic adults with an anomalous right coronary artery arising from the <span>PA</span> and with evidence of ischemia, surgery is recommended to reduce ischemia, to reduce the risk for <span>SCD</span>, and to improve cardiac function. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>3. In asymptomatic adults with an anomalous right coronary artery arising from the <span>PA</span> and without evidence of cardiac dysfunction or ischemia, surgery may be considered to avoid the development of cardiac dysfunction. <span>( <span>2b, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h3>4.5.3. Coronary Artery Fistula</h3></div><div><div> <h4>4.6. Vascular Rings and Pulmonary Artery Slings</h4></div><div><div> <h5>Diagnostic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>1. For adults with unexplained symptoms of airway or esophageal compression that raise concern for vascular ring or <span>PA</span> sling, anatomic assessment with cross-sectional imaging of the aortic arch and PAs is recommended. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>2. For adults with persistent symptoms of airway or esophageal compression after repair of vascular ring or <span>PA</span> sling, cross-sectional advanced imaging is recommended to evaluate postoperative anatomy and indications for additional intervention. <span>( <span>1, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div><div><div> <h5>Therapeutic</h5></div><div><div> <div><div>3. For adults with symptoms of airway or esophageal compression and a vascular ring or <span>PA</span> sling, surgical repair* is reasonable to improve symptoms. <span>( <span>2a, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div> <div>* A cardiothoracic surgeon with expertise in <span>ACHD</span> should perform the repair.</div></div></div><div><div> <div><div>4. For asymptomatic adults with a vascular ring or <span>PA</span> sling, surgical repair is <span>not</span> indicated. <span>( <span>3 - No Benefit, B-NR</span> )</span></div></div></div></div></div></div></div></div></div><div><h2>Class of Recommendations and Level of Evidence</h2><div><div><table><thead><tr><th>Class (Strength) of Recommendation</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><th>CLASS 1 (STRONG)                                                                                                                                                                             <span>Benefit &gt;&gt;&gt; Risk</span></th></tr><tr><td>Suggested phrases for writing recommendations:
