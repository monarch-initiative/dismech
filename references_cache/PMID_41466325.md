Meningiomas are common intracranial tumors with complex behavior that can be 
difficult to predict. Historically, morphology has been used to predict tumor 
aggressiveness and risk of recurrence, but this strategy has limitations as a 
prognostic tool. DNA methylation, transcriptomics, and copy number data are 
valuable for identifying groups of tumors with distinct biological signatures, 
thereby predicting recurrence risk. Multiple risk-stratifying classifiers which 
incorporate methylation data are available, but to date, a clinically validated 
risk-predicting classifier which exclusively uses methylation data has not been 
created. Using samples from 217 patients, we developed, validated, and 
implemented a clinically applicable methylation classifier for prognostic 
stratification of meningiomas based on k-means clustering of methylation data. 
Our classifier is 96% accurate, with 91% of samples receiving high confidence 
scores in the validation cohort (n = 76). This classifier is unique in that it 
includes de novo identification of risk groups by DNA methylation, confidence 
score calculation, internal clinical validation, and public model availability. 
Our newly validated classifier has the potential to aid diagnostic workup, 
improve recurrence risk prediction, and enhance clinical management of 
meningiomas.

Meningiomas are common intracranial tumors, with over 35,000 diagnosed annually in the United States [1]. Many meningiomas are benign and curable with surgical resection [2]. However, some meningiomas recur, causing significant morbidity for patients [3]. A subset behave as malignant tumors and may be fatal, even with aggressive therapy [4, 5]. The extent of resection is a major clinical predictor of recurrence-free survival [6]. Historically, the World Health Organization (WHO) grade has been considered the best pathologic predictor of recurrence [7], and is considered when determining optimal therapy (e.g., surveillance versus radiation). WHO grade 1 meningiomas are considered benign, while WHO grade 2 atypical meningiomas are associated with an increased risk of recurrence. WHO grade 3 meningiomas are anaplastic (i.e., malignant), with a poor prognosis. Grading meningiomas is a complex process, with variable quality of evidence and evolving guidelines. Grading is primarily based on morphologic assessment, with specific histopathologic features or combinations of features carrying different weights. Some features, such as mitotic activity, are supported by high-quality evidence in determining prognosis [4, 8], whereas other features, such as brain invasion, are more controversial. Without other atypical features, data are inconsistent on whether brain invasion alone is associated with an increased risk of recurrence [4, 9, 10]. Although the risk of progression and recurrence is higher in grade 2 and 3 meningiomas, WHO grade 1 ostensibly benign meningiomas can also progress and recur, highlighting the limits of morphology as a prognostic tool [2, 11]. Select tissue sampling from large meningiomas may also limit histopathologic evaluation.

With the increased availability of ancillary testing in neuropathology, integrated molecular diagnostics play an increasing role in meningioma grading. For example, the presence of TERT promoter mutation or bi-allelic loss of CDKN2A/B are associated with poor prognosis [12, 13]. These alterations are now considered sufficient for classifying a meningioma as grade 3 [7]. Certain chromosomal copy number alterations are also associated with an increased risk of recurrence and are now recommended as grading criteria for meningioma [14, 15]. DNA methylation profiling has revolutionized diagnostic neuropathology [16, 17] and proven useful in identifying biological signatures and for predicting recurrence risk in meningioma. Previous work by multiple groups has utilized DNA methylation data, either alone or in combination with additional ancillary data, to identify biologically and prognostically distinct groups of meningiomas [18–21]. For example, the International Consortium on Meningiomas (ICOM) has developed a classifier to predict recurrence risk using sample methylation data to sort meningiomas into one of four groups, which were previously identified through the integration of methylation, DNA sequencing, transcriptomic, and copy number data [19]. Additionally, Choudhury et al. (2022) developed a publicly available meningioma recurrence risk classifier based on methylation clustering that categorizes samples into one of three groups [20]. At Northwestern University, we routinely perform methylation array-based profiling for brain tumor classification [22] on a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) platform and have developed a clinical classifier for recurrence prediction based on DNA methylation data. This classifier uses pre-defined groups identified on de novo methylation clusters, calculates a confidence score for each classification, has been fully validated for clinical testing at Northwestern, and is freely available for public use.

This study was approved by the Northwestern University Institutional Review Board (STU00218648) and consists of 217 meningioma cases diagnosed between January 1993 and January 2024. The tumors were submitted to the Division of Neuropathology at Northwestern Memorial Hospital for genome-wide DNA methylation array analysis and copy number profiling by OncoScan Chromosomal Microarray Analysis. 141 tumors were used for training and 76 for validation. Relevant demographic and clinicopathological data were collected for all study cases. Demographic and clinical data were tabulated, including gender, age at diagnosis, tumor localization, and number of tumor sites. Tumor recurrence status and date of recurrence were known for 203 patients. Tumor samples were reviewed in the Division of Neuropathology for consensus interpretation and classified according to the 2021 WHO classification for CNS tumors, including assessment of TERT and CDKN2A/B.7 Immunohistochemistry (IHC) using Ki-67 was performed in all cases (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Genomic DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections using the Purigen FFPE DNA isolation kit (Purigen Biosystems, Pleasanton, CA). This method includes overnight proteinase digestion followed by automated isotachophoresis for nucleic acid isolation.

CMA was performed for all study cases as part of the clinical diagnostic work-up in the clinical cytogenetics laboratory using OncoScan CNV assay (Affymetrix, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Santa Clara, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The data were analyzed with the Affymetrix Chromosome Analysis Suite (ChAS) software. The advantage of CMA is its ability not only to detect CNVs, but also regions of copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity (CN-LOH). All identified copy number alterations and stretches of CN-LOH were manually reviewed.

The most common TERT promoter mutations (c.-124 C > T alias C228T and c.-146 C > T alias C250T, Chr5:1295228 and Chr5:1295250, respectively) were interrogated using a droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) assay with the limit of detection at 0.1% following standard laboratory protocol as part of clinical diagnostic work-up. Briefly, this assay interrogates these two loci and the wild-type alleles at the c.-124 and c.-146 positions in two separate reactions utilizing the Bio-Rad QX200 instrument (Bio-Rad, Des Plaines, IL, USA) with FAM and HEX fluorescence.

DNA methylation profiling for all study cases was performed using the Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip or MethylationEPICv2 BeadChip array platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) following previously reported protocols [22]. Filtering and preprocessing of data were performed as previously described [22]. To ensure compatibility with all current chip versions, all analyses were performed with only probes found in the 450k, EPIC, and EPICv2 chips. All methylation analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (v4.1.2; R Core Team 2021).

Similar to Choudhury et al., methylation profiles were clustered using the k-means algorithm, which iteratively assigns samples to the closest of k-cluster centroids. k-means clustering was performed using the stats package. The optimal number of clusters in the dataset was determined by varying the number of clusters from 1 to 10 and comparing the within the sum of squares using the elbow method as implemented in the factoextra package (https://github.com/kassambara/factoextra). Clustering used the 10k most variable probes by standard deviation.

Additional unsupervised clustering with the 10k most variable probes was performed to compare groups based on k-means sample assignment to WHO grades. Principal component analysis used the package RSpectra (https://github.com/yixuan/RSpectra) and was visualized using t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE). The t-SNE analysis used the Rtsne package (https://github.com/jkrijthe/Rtsne) with 94 principal components, perplexity = 30, theta = 0, and 2500 iterations. Hierarchical clustering was performed using the hclust function and complete agglomeration.

To examine the methylation probes separating the 3 k-means clusters, differential methylation analysis was performed using methylation M values calculated with the minfi package [23]. Differentially methylated probes were identified using linear models with the package limma [24], retaining those that had adjusted p < 0.05 after correction using the Benjamini Hochberg approach [25]. Significant probes were subsequently used to erect a heatmap using ComplexHeatmap [26]. Chromosome enrichment analysis was performed to determine which chromosomes exhibited significantly more or fewer differentially methylated probes than expected. Enrichment was examined for each chromosome using a Fisher exact test comparing the number of differentially methylated probes to the total number of probes on the chromosome. Significance was assessed using p-values corrected with the Bonferroni method [27].

A machine learning classifier was constructed to classify samples into the 3 k-means clusters using a K nearest neighbors (KNN) approach with the caret package [28]. Samples were first split into training and validation sets. The training set consisted of 75% of the samples from the cluster with the fewest samples, and an equal number of samples from the remaining two clusters. All remaining samples were used for the validation set. The classifier was developed on the training set, performing 5-fold cross-validation to identify the number of neighbors to use for classification. The number of neighbors varied from 0 to 50. The optimal number of neighbors was chosen as the number that produced the lowest errors in the test folds while also providing enough neighbors for accurate probability calculation. Probability calculation was enabled for the classifier by dividing the number of neighbors supporting the highest supported cluster by the total number of neighbors. KNN classifiers were developed and compared for three different sets of methylation probes: the 10k most variable probes across samples, significant differentially methylated probes among the three clusters with estimated fold change >2, and significant differentially methylated probes among the three clusters with estimated fold change >3. Classification metrics, such as specificity, sensitivity, optimal threshold, and area under the curve (AUC) were calculated using the pROC package [29]. Additionally, classifier results were compared to the available Choudhury classifier (https://william-c-chen.shinyapps.io/MeninMethylClassApp/).

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to identify pathologic variables associated with each methylation cluster. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time in months from the time of pathologic diagnosis to the time of disease recurrence. PFS of patients in each group was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method. Cox proportional hazards regression was performed using the survival package [30]. t-test was used to assess the statistical significance of body mass index (BMI) and age. The chi-square test of independence was used to assess the statistical independence between groups for the following variables: gender, multiple sites, convexity of meningioma, grade, recurrence, and history of smoking. Average mitotic counts and Ki-67 proliferation indices between groups were compared via one-way ANOVA with post-hoc Tukey’s test. The results of statistical tests were considered significant with p-values of less than 0.05. All statistical calculations and graphing were performed using R software version 4.1. (https://www.r-project.org/) or Graph Pad Prism version 10.4.1. Figures were plotted with the ggplot2 R package (https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org/), and with Graph Pad Prism.

Clinical validation of the meningioma risk classifier was performed by assessing test accuracy, precision, and limit of detection (LOD) via DNA input and tumor purity. Accuracy was assessed by comparing the classifications of the validation samples to their true k-means cluster assignments. Precision was examined by repeatedly analyzing nine meningioma samples, three from each risk class. Each repetition repeated bisulfite conversion, array hybridization, chip scanning, idat preprocessing, and classification. To assess LOD of DNA input, one sample from each risk class was repeatedly diluted and analyzed with DNA inputs of 500ng, 250ng, 125ng, 100ng, and 50ng. Finally, LOD of tumor purity was performed by analyzing sample repetitions with decreasing tumor purities. One sample from each risk class was assessed with tumor purity values of 0.9, 0.7, 0.6, and 0.5. For both precision and limit of detection, concordance was assessed by comparing repetition classifications in agreement for each sample.

The cohort consisted of 217 meningioma cases from 217 patients who underwent tumor resection between 1993 and 2024. All meningiomas were graded according to the 2021 WHO criteria [31]. Per WHO grading criteria, 98 tumors were grade 1 (45.2%), 103 were grade 2 (47.5%), and 16 were grade 3 (7.4%). A total of 209 samples were from primary tumors (96.3%) and 8 were from recurrent tumors (3.7%). No matched pairs were included. The cohort included 148 female patients (68.2%) and 69 male patients (31.8%) (Table 1).

After filtering, a total of 358,310 methylation probes were selected for analysis. k-means clustering analysis (Fig. 1, Online Resource 1) identified three clusters, hereafter designated as “low-,” “medium-,” and “high-risk.” The low-risk group was composed of 90 samples, the medium-risk group was composed of 64, and the high-risk group was composed of 63. The t-SNE analysis (Fig. 2A, B) and hierarchical clustering (Fig. 2C) of the 10,000 probes with the greatest variability indicated that k-means risk groups produced more consistent clusters than WHO grading, i.e. separate clusters showed mixed WHO grades, but consisted of mostly a single k-means risk group. Additionally, the high-risk cluster showed enrichment for some, but not all recognized high risk genetic markers by hierarchical clustering, including deletions in chromosome 1p and CDKN2A/B.

Methylation analysis identified 88,155 significantly different methylated probes among the 3 k-means clusters. Of these probes, 403 exhibited log-fold changes greater than 2, and 16 exhibited log-fold changes greater than 3 (Fig. 3; Online Resource 2). Chromosome enrichment analysis indicated that chromosomes 11, 16, and 17 exhibited more differentially methylated probes relative to other chromosomes (Online Resource 3. A novel k-nearest neighbors (KNN) classifier was developed for each of three different probe subsets (Table 2): (1) the 10,000 most variable probes (10k); (2) probes with log-fold change > 2.0 (diff_meth_fc2); and (3) probes with log-fold change > 3.0 (diff_meth_fc3). The training set included 141 samples (47 from each risk cluster), and the validation set consisted of the remaining 76 samples. The classifier developed with the fc2 probe set showed the best combination of train error (0.05), test error (0.039), and validation accuracy (0.961). The optimal number of neighbors for the selected model was 10, and confidence scores were calculated by dividing the number of neighbors supporting a particular cluster by the total number of neighbors. Using an optimal threshold score of 0.75, 91% of validation samples received matching classifier scores.

The concordance between k-means clusters and parallel histologic grades was analyzed. In the low-risk cluster, 56/90 samples (62%) showed grade 1 histology, 32/90 (36%) showed grade 2 histology, and 2/90 (2%) showed grade 3 histology. In the medium-risk cluster, 31/64 samples (48%) showed grade 1 histology, 31/64 (48%) showed grade 2 histology, and 2/64 (3%) showed grade 3 histology. In the high-risk cluster, 11/63 samples (17%) showed grade 1 histology, 40/63 (63%) showed grade 2 histology, and 12/63 (19%) showed grade 3 histology. Sequencing and copy number data are summarized in a waterfall plot in Fig. 4A. Spearman correlation analysis of histologic grade with the most frequent sequence and copy number variations showed that polysomy and WHO grade 1 histology were enriched in the “low-risk” cluster. Deletion of 22q was enriched in the “medium-risk” cluster. The “high-risk” cluster was enriched for deletion of 1p, chromothripsis, heterozygous and homozygous deletion of CDKN2A/B, TERT promoter mutation, deletion of 22q, NF2 mutation, and WHO grade 2 and 3 morphology (Fig. 4B; Table 3).

k-means clusters were also analyzed relative to the published Choudhury classification system. In the k-means low-risk cluster, 85/90 samples (95%) corresponded to the “Merlin-intact” class, 2/90 (2%) corresponded to the “immune-enriched” class, and 3/90 (3%) corresponded to the “hypermitotic” class. In the medium-risk cluster, 0/64 samples (0%) corresponded to Merlin-intact, 61/64 (95%) corresponded to immune-enriched, and 3/64 (5%) corresponded to hypermitotic. In the high-risk cluster, 5/63 samples (8%) corresponded to Merlin-intact, 7/63 (11%) corresponded to immune-enriched, and 51/63 (81%) corresponded to hypermitotic. The correspondence of k-means clusters to histologic grade and Choudhury clusters is shown in Fig. 4C.

Among 16 analyzed variables using Cox proportional hazards regression, the “medium-risk” k-means cluster, the “Merlin intact” Choudhury cluster, age at resection, and bone invasion were associated with PFS (Table 4). To benchmark the k-means cluster relative to the standard WHO grade, PFS was used as the endpoint. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that increasing grade was associated with an increased risk of recurrence (Fig. 5A). More specifically, WHO grade 1 had a median PFS of 93.4 months, grade 2 had a median PFS of 47.7 months (p = 0.0014 relative to grade 1), and grade 3 had a median PFS of 35.6 months (p = 0.0005 relative to grade 1). The difference in PFS between grades 2 and 3 was not statistically significant (p = 0.6906). K-means clustering effectively identified tumors at the greatest risk of recurrence (Fig. 5B). PFS was comparable between the low-risk and medium-risk clusters (median of 86.6 vs 96.1 months, p = 0.6431). However, PFS was markedly shorter in the high-risk cluster (median 32.9 months), which was significant relative to the low-risk (p < 0.0001) and medium-risk (p = 0.0007) clusters. The PFS rates were comparable between meningiomas with WHO grade 3 morphology and the high-risk cluster. Comparison of survival analyses between k-means clusters and Choudhury classifications showed few differences in survival curves (Supplemental Fig. 2), with high risk groups exhibiting significantly lower survival (p < 0.001) while no significant differences were observed between low and medium risk groups (p > 0.5).

Next, the association of the k-means clusters with differences in recurrence risk and proliferation markers (mitotic activity and Ki-67 proliferation index) within meningiomas of the same WHO grade was determined. Among grade 1 meningiomas (Fig. 5C), the high-risk cluster (n = 11) had the shortest median PFS (32.9 months) compared to low-risk (n = 47, 86.6 months) and medium-risk (n = 28, 206.0 months), although this was not statistically significant likely due to low power. There was no significant difference in mitotic activity between k-means clusters in grade 1 meningiomas (Fig. 5D). Specifically, the average Ki-67 proliferation index was 7.9% in high-risk grade 1 tumors, while low- and medium-risk tumors both had an average Ki-67 of 3.9% (p = 0.0024). For grade 2 meningiomas (Fig. 5E), the high-risk cluster (n = 35) had shorter PFS (median 27.4 months) compared to both the low-risk (n = 31, median 47.7 months, p = 0.0039) and medium-risk clusters (n = 32, p = 0.0063, median undefined due to censored subjects). Proliferative markers for grade 2 tumors showed trends toward higher mitotic activity and higher Ki-67 proliferation index in higher-risk clusters (Fig. 5F), although these differences were not statistically significant. For analysis of grade 3 meningioma, the low-risk and high-risk clusters were combined for analysis due to low numbers (n = 4) against the high risk-clusters. Even so, no significant differences in survival, mitotic activity, or Ki-67 were noted, likely due to low power (Fig. 5G, H). Cumulatively, these data indicate that the incorporation of the proliferation index as part of our DNA methylation classifier would not add additional prognostic value.

Clinical validation included analyses of accuracy, precision, DNA input, and limit of detection (LOD) (i.e. effect of tumor purity in the classifier performance). The Northwestern DNA methylation classifier achieved 96% accuracy in the validation set. Precision concordance among repetitions of the same samples was 100% (Online Resource 4). While a small number of repetitions differed in score by 0.1, these differences did not change the resulting class or match status. No differences in class or score were observed among differing DNA inputs ranging from 500ng to 50ng (Online Resource 5). LOD was also tested across various tumor purities (Online Resource 6). No differences in class were observed among repetitions with tumor purity of at least 0.7. However, match status did change in the low-risk sample, with the 0.9 tumor purity repetition receiving a non-matching score of 0.7 while the lower tumor purity repetitions received matching scores of 0.8. The only class discordance among repetitions occurred in the high-risk sample, where high tumor purity repetitions (0.9 and 0.7) were accurately classified as high risk, but lower tumor purity repetitions (0.6 and 0.5) were incorrectly classified as medium risk. According to these data, the optimal DNA input for this test is 50ng while tumor purity should be greater than 0.6 to obtain accurate results.

Two example cases with fully illustrated clinical workflows and sample-integrated diagnostic reports are included in Online Resource 7 and 8. Case one represents a tumor identified as WHO grade 3 that was classified as low-risk and case two represents a tumor identified as WHO grade 1 that was classified as high-risk.

Meningiomas are common and tumor recurrence is both a major contributor to morbidity and a challenge to predict. Grading and prognostic stratification of meningiomas is evolving and is a topic of intensive ongoing research. Growing evidence shows that morphologic features alone are insufficient for reliably predicting meningioma recurrence risk. Improved prognostic stratification would enable the identification of patients at the greatest risk of tumor recurrence who need more frequent monitoring and who would benefit from adjuvant therapy. Here, we report the use of methylation array data and k-means clustering analysis to create a clinically validated classifier for prognostic stratification of meningiomas. Three distinct methylation clusters are designated as “low-,” “medium-,” and “high-risk.” These clusters are effective in predicting PFS, with the high-risk cluster having a significantly shorter PFS than the low- and medium-risk clusters when compared via log-rank test. Furthermore, subgroup analysis shows that k-means clustering can differentiate the risk of recurrence within WHO Grade 2. Higher Ki-67 proliferation index with higher-risk clusters suggests that k-means clustering can identify tumors with more rapid growth. Cox proportional hazards regression identified the medium-risk cluster as a significant predictor of tumor recurrence. Correlation analysis shows that our “high-risk” cluster is enriched for multiple features that are known recurrence predictors, including loss of chromosome 1p, homozygous or hemizygous CDKN2A-B loss, 22q deletion, and higher histologic grade [13–15, 32]. Interestingly, Cox proportional hazards regression showed that bone invasion is a strong predictor of recurrence risk in meningioma. Previous studies of bone invasion as a prognostic factor have been limited, and it has been associated with recurrence only in the context of atypical histologic features [33]. The prognostic relevance of bone invasion in meningiomas remains to be determined and merits future investigations.

Our classifier is concordant with previously published work but also has important distinctions. Our 3 clusters showed strong correspondence to the Choudhury group’s previously identified clusters [20]. Because Choudhury et al. also used k-means clustering to identify classes and found that their classes represent different clinical outcomes, concordance between the two classifiers further supports the differences in survival observed among our classes. While Choudhury et al. achieved better separation in PFS between their low and medium risk clusters, our classifier has been fully clinically validated for use in clinical diagnosis and includes confidence scores for each classification, which are helpful for pathologists to interpret classifier results. ICOM recently published a meningioma classifier, which is notable for its validation cohort of over 1500 cases and forthcoming availability for public use [34]. Our classifier has two key distinctions from ICOM. First, our three risk groups were derived exclusively from methylation data, whereas the four groups in the ICOM classifier were originally identified through the integration of DNA methylation, transcriptomic, and copy number data [19]. As the ICOM authors point out, training a classifier to identify a mixture of epigenomic, transcriptomic, and copy number signatures using only methylation data comes with limitations. The methylation data may not be able to capture the full features of each group, as some features were derived from other data types [34]. Our classifier relies on methylation only de novo clustering for class creation on a single CLIA-certified platform, ensuring all data features of the different groups are captured in the classification algorithm. Second, our classifier utilizes a “single layer” algorithm, whereas ICOM uses 3 “layers.” This is because two of the ICOM groups (“hypermetabolic” and “proliferative”) have comparable methylation signatures in the first layer and are more readily distinguished by transcriptomic signatures. A classifier can be trained to distinguish these groups based on methylation data, but doing so requires a second layer of classification after the initial sorting. A third layer uses inferred copy number information from DNA methylation data for further refinement. Incorporating copy number variations as a separate layer may lead to unpredicted effects when estimating a probability score, as each layer of the classifier will have a separate probability. Moreover, the estimation of copy number via methylation is prone to low sensitivity [35, 36], and such uncertainty could be propagated into the classification model. While additional published models have shown promise for predicting meningioma risk [37, 38], our classifier distinguishes itself by calculating confidence scores for each sample, being publicly available, and being CAP/CLIA clinically validated.

While our classifier displays high performance in classifying meningioma risk, it is not without its own limitations. First, even though our methylation data clearly supports different clusters corresponding to our low- and medium-risk groups, the two do not differ significantly in PFS. It is possible that larger differences in recurrence would be observed by adding additional samples with recurrence data. Our study is based on a smaller cohort than others, and usable recurrence data can be sparse due to the long recurrence times of meningioma patients in general. However, PFS in our high-risk group is starkly different from the other two groups, and the high-risk classification may be of the most importance, as high-risk patients will likely need the most aggressive treatment. Moreover, the high accuracy of the classifier indicates that our sample sizes do not impair classifier performance. When survival in our cohort is compared using Choudhury classifier classes, we observe similar survival patterns, suggesting that the lack of survival difference between low and medium risk groups is a general observation of our cohort rather than a limitation of the classifier. Finally, while our methylation data strongly support multiple risk groups, they do not explain the large differences observed in some cases between molecular results and morphology, as seen in WHO grading. Indeed, methylation profiling may not capture the entire signal necessary to predict risk of recurrence. Thus, incorporation of additional molecular data like gene expression profiling by RNA sequencing [39], not yet widely implemented as a clinical diagnostic test, can assist in building an integrated model of meningioma classification [37, 38]. However, the use of only methylation data in the classifier developed here allows for more widespread application, as laboratories do not need to conduct both methylation and RNA profiling. Future studies may find additional success in recurrence prediction by integrating both WHO grades and molecular predictors into a single classifier, as also suggested by recent cIMPACT-NOW recommendations [15].

Methylation profiling can identify biologically and clinically distinct groups of meningiomas and predict their risk of recurrence. Our clinically validated methylation classifier has the potential to improve the accuracy of recurrence risk prediction and improve surveillance and neoadjuvant therapy for patients.
