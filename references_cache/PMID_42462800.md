Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a disease of increasing electrical and structural
heterogeneity, where triggers dominate early, and substrate-anchored drivers
tend to predominate subsequently. Low-voltage substrate regions are a common
feature, and typically correlate with underlying fibrotic scar. As the atrial
milieu remodels and AF progresses from paroxysmal to persistent, longstanding
persistent, and permanent forms, the electrical conduction patterns and
structural topology become more disorganized, more likely to fibrillate because
of unidirectional conduction block and obstructed pathways, and less likely to
terminate spontaneously even with drug, ablative, or surgical intervention.
Ablative targeting of the disorganized areas in addition to standard pulmonary
vein isolation can improve the success rate for treatment of the more advanced
forms of AF. Yet, if AF is perpetuated without satisfactory intervention, it
becomes increasingly reflective of a more complex, spatially heterogeneous, and
recalcitrant milieu arising from electrical uncoupling, fibrosis, adipose
deposits, calcification, and wall thickness gradients. For more efficacious
treatment, better understanding of the mechanisms by which AF is initiated and
perpetuated must be prioritized. Development of techniques to enhance the
analysis of atrial substrate properties and to determine the best areas for
ablative therapy to improve outcome during electrophysiologic study and
treatment are needed. Additionally, attention to lifestyle-based factors, such
as patient diet and exercise, is important for prevention and control of
arrhythmia. Anti-inflammatory interventions may halt or even partially reverse
electrical and structural remodeling and reduce arrhythmia severity. Possible
new modalities include novel drug intervention and advanced pacing methods.
