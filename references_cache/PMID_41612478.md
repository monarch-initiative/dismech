# Pathology of peripheral neuroblastic tumors.
**Authors:** Conces MR, Shibui Y, Shimada H
**Journal:** Diagn Pathol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13000-026-01758-y](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13000-026-01758-y)

## Content

Neuroblastoma is the most prevalent extracranial solid tumor in pediatric age populations worldwide and the most common neoplastic disease diagnosed in the first year of life. The term neuroblastoma often encompasses all peripheral neuroblastic tumors (pNTs including neuroblastoma, ganglioneuroblastoma, and ganglioneuroma) of neural crest origin. Since pNTs demonstrate a wide range of clinical behaviors, including spontaneous regression, tumor differentiation/maturation, and aggressive progression refractory to even intensive treatment modalities, these tumors are believed to be distinguished into different biological/molecular groups. For the practical purpose, risk classification systems have been developed based on combinations of so-called prognostic factors.In this review of pNTs, we describe a history of pathology, summarize epidemiology and clinical features, and outline International Neuroblastoma Pathology Classification (INPC). Then we discuss advantages and limitations of core needle biopsy and conventional biopsy of neuroblastoma. Besides the INPC, we also describe other prognostic factors, such as age at diagnosis, clinical stage, and molecular/genetic factors, since they are included in widely used Risk Classification Systems. Additionally, we discuss further molecular abnormalities closely associated with highly aggressive neruoblastomas. Towards the end, we touch on rapidly advancing technologies for establishing an artificial intelligence-assisted INPC system.

There has been a long history for establishing current concept and guideline of peripheral neuroblastic tumor (pNT) pathology. As expected, the first case of neuroblastoma (NB) was reported in the adrenal medulla by the German pathologist, Rudolf Virchow, in 1864 and he called the tumor “glioma” [1]. In 1891, Marchand suggested that those adrenal tumors were derived from the fetal sympathetic ganglion [2]. In 1910, J H Wright summarized a series of cases, and published the landmark review: [3] He was the first to use the term “neuroblastoma” and provided a detailed description of pseudo-rosette formations by the tumor cells, which we now refer to as Homer Wright rosettes.

Cases of ganglioneuroma (GN) was reported by Loretz in 1870 [4], Key in 1879 [5], and Weichselbaum in 1881 [6]. Ganglioneuroblastoma (GNB) was described as “a transitional tumor of sympathetic cell origin that contained malignant neuroblastomatous and benign ganglioneuromatous element” by Robertson in 1915 [7]. The ability of NB cells to differentiate into mature neurons (or ganglion cells) was also recognized by Percival et al. [8] By comparing the same patient’s specimen at diagnosis and a resected specimen 10 years later, Cushing and Wolbach reported that malignant NB could differentiate into benign GN [9]. In 1962, Willis, in his textbook, described that pNT were embryonal tumors, and all GNs were once NBs in their early stage of tumor development [10].

The modern era of pNT pathology was started by Beckwith, who described ‘in situ neuroblastoma’ [11]. Autopsy specimens from stillborn fetuses and infants revealed the presence of small adrenal/peri-adrenal “neuroblastoma-like” nodules with markedly higher incidence (around 1/150 to 250) than clinically detectable NB cases (1/7,000 newborn babies) [12, 13]. Bolande, in his monograph, mentioned that neuroblastoma in situ particularly over 2000µ could represents precursive or latent form of overt neuroblastoma [14]. Though we do not know how many of them are neoplastic or simple embryonic rest without neoplastic potential, a considerable number of these nodules could spontaneously regress/disappear without making clinically detectable tumor masses. In 1968, together with Martin, Beckwith proposed the first innovative classification by quantitatively assessing the differentiating neuroblasts present in NBs and reported the prognostic significance of his grading system [15]. Subsequently, various histopathological features—such as calcification, mitotic activity, necrosis, and multinucleation, etc.—were proposed for predicting clinical behavior of pNTs [16, 17].

In 1984, Shimada et al. introduced a pathology classification system based on the concept that pNTs did not represent a single disease but were composed of at least two different diseases, biologically favorable and biologically unfavorable, supported by uniquely different genomic/molecular properties [18]. They classified pNTs into Favorable Histology (FH) Group where the tumors had an age-appropriate differentiation/maturation and mitotic/karyorrhectic activities and Unfavorable Histology (UH) Group where tumors had no or limited potential of differentiation/maturation and/or increasing mitotic/karyorrhectic activities. This classification, called Shimada system, evolved into the International Neuroblastoma Pathology Classification (INPC) in 1999 [19] with minor modification in 2003 [20]. World Health Organization now recognizes the pathology diagnosis/evaluation according to the INPC is critical for predicting clinical behavior of individual pNT cases. The International Collaboration on Cancer Reporting (ICCR) also incorporates datasets aligned with the INPC to standardize the handling and reporting of pNT cases globally [21]. ( https://www.iccr-cancer.org/datasets/published-datasets/paediatrics/neuroblastoma/)

pNTs account for approximately 10% of pediatric neoplasms and are the most common extracranial solid tumors diagnosed in the pediatric age group. Many children present with a painless abdominal mass, but symptoms may also result from mass effect of primary tumor or metastatic disease.

Majority of patients are diagnosed before 5 years of age, with the median age at presentation of 19 months [22]. Most cases occur sporadically, but the early onset of this disease in infants and even in the prenatal period suggests a potential role of preconceptual or gestational factors. For instance, a significant association between prenatal pesticide exposure and the development of neuroblastoma has been reported [23]. pNTs are neural crest origin and diagnosed in the adrenal gland or in the paraspinal sympathetic ganglia of the neck, chest, abdomen or pelvis. pNTs can also be found in presacral and ovarian teratomas. However, since most of the neuronal component in teratomas has central nervous system characteristics, developing pNTs (neural crest tumor of peripheral nervous system) in these tumors seems exceptionally rare with only few cases reported [24–27].

A small subset of pNTs arise in the setting of a familial predisposition (1–2%) through an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern with incomplete penetrance. These familial tumors tend to be found in younger children and may have bilateral or multiple primaries [28]. Activating germline pathologic variants (GPVs) involving anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) gene are responsible for approximately 75 to 80% of the familial NB cases [29]. Also reported in familial NB cases are GPVs involving the paired-like homeobox 2B (PHOX2B) gene [30].

Rare paraneoplastic syndromes are known to develop in patients with pNTs. They are (1) Opsoclonus-myoclonus-ataxia syndrome – tumor tissue characteristically having extensive lymphocytic infiltration and lymphoid follicular formation (supporting the presence of immune-mediated mechanism); [31] (2) Verner-Morrison syndrome – tumor typically composed of differentiating neuroblasts producing VIP hormone; [32] (3) Hypertension – either tumor growth causing renal artery stenosis or tumor cells producing adrenalin/noradrenalin; [33] and (4]) Horner syndrome – especially associated with mediastinal primary tumor [34]. Also noted here is a rare and unique combination of congenital central hypoventilation, Hirschsprung’s disease, and neuroblastoma due to non-polyalanine repeat mutations of PHOX2B gene [35].

pNTs in adults are sporadically reported in the literature [36, 37]. Like those in pediatric cases, they are histologically diagnosed as NB, GNB, GN, but there are no reports describing a series of cases with clear distinction morphologically and clinically between GNB, intermixed and GNB, nodular (please see International Neuroblastoma Pathology Classification below). Adult on-set NBs are highly aggressive clinically, often associated with alpha-thalassemia X-lined intellectual disability (ATRX) mutation leading to abnormal telomere maintenance mechanism of alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT) phenotype and commonly not associated with MYCN amplification [36] molecularly, and frequently reported PHOX2B negative immunohistochemically, so that many of them may be biologically different from “pediatric” NBs [38]. In contrast, “adult” GN could have started as “pediatric” NB having potential of differentiation/maturation and eventually become clinically detectable later in adult age [39]. It is interesting that adult thymic NBs, though reported cases are limited in number, are often associated with syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion [40]. It should be noted that since INPC is designed for prognostic distinction among “pediatric” pNTs, it is not applicable to “adult” pNTs even though no clear consensus for separating these two age groups.

INPC defines 4 categories in the pNTs: Neuroblastoma (NB; Schwannian stroma-poor), Ganglioneuroblastoma, intermixed (GNB-i; Schwannian stroma-rich), Ganglioneuroma (GN; Schwannian stroma-dominant), and Ganglioneuroblastoma, nodular (GNB-n; composite, Schwannian stroma-rich/stroma-dominant and stroma-poor) [19]. In order to clarify their histologic characteristics, each category is often followed by its unique feature of Schwannian stromal development in parenthesis. To note, tumors in the biologically favorable pNTs are considered to proceed by age-dependent differentiation/maturational steps from NB to GNB-i, and finally to GN. As mentioned below, tumors in the NB category and NB nodules of GNB-n category, are subject of age-linked evaluation for their grade of neuroblastic differentiation and Mitosis-Karyorrhexis Index (MKI) and classified into FH group or UH group. While all tumors in the GNB-i and GN are always classified into the FH group. The distinction of FH group or UH group according to the INPC, as summarized in Table 1, should only be determined based on the pathology review of primary or metastatic tumors obtained before starting chemotherapy/irradiation therapy. Once FH or UH group classification is assigned, it should not be changed throughout the clinical course of individual cases, since therapy-associated histology changes including neuroblastic cytodifferentiation and even Schwannian stromal development, if present, may not have proven prognostic significance [19, 41].

According to the INPC, mitotic and karyorrhectic activities are also evaluated, and the MKI class (i.e., low, < 100/5000 cells; intermediate, 100–200/5000 cells; or high, ≥ 200/5000 cells) is determined for the individual tumors in NB category. It is reported that MYCN-amplified NB shows the characteristic histology of either the undifferentiated subtype or the poorly differentiated subtype with high MKI (please see Fig. 1B) [42]. Like the grade of neuroblastic differentiation, prognostic effects of MKI are also age dependent. As shown in the Table 1, low MKI tumors diagnosed in patients less than 5 years (1824 days) of age and intermediate MKI tumors in patients less than 18 months (547 days) of age are associated with a favorable prognosis. Whereas low MKI tumor in patients ≥ 5 years (1825 days), intermediate MKI tumor in patients ≥ 18 months (548 days) of age, and high MKI tumors in patients of any age indicate a poor prognosis. As mentioned above, even differentiating NBs with low MKI diagnosed in patients over 5 years of age are classified into UH group.

Tumors in this category represent the most mature form of pNTs. They are elastic and yellowish white, and free surfaces are well-circumscribed and encapsulated. However, infiltrative growth into the surrounding fatty tissue, without prognostic significance, may be observed in deeply seated GN. Tumor cells (ganglion cells) are distributed individually or making small clusters in Schwannian stroma (Fig. 2C). They are either completely mature (mature GN) or a mixture of mature and maturing ganglion cells (maturing GN). Completely mature ganglion cells are covered with satellite cells. Neurites produced by the ganglion cells are immediately incorporated in the cytoplasm of Schwannian stromal cells, so that naked neurites are not identified around the ganglion cells. As mentioned above, cases of both GNB-i and GN tumors are classified into the FH group, and the patients have an excellent prognosis.

Tumors in this category are considered as multi-clonal tumors. In gross appearance, the NB component often forms hemorrhagic and necrotic nodule(s) in a background of elastic and yellowish white component of GNB-i or GN (Fig. 2D and E). The borders of these components are well-demarcated in some cases. However, in other cases, NB component may be invading/infiltrating into the other components of GNB-i or GN. Tumors in this category are classified into either FH group or UH group by applying the same age-linked evaluation of grade of neuroblastic differentiation and MKI to the NB component (see above).

Immunohistochemical staining is important for confirming the pathology diagnosis of pNTs, and even essential for distinguishing undifferentiated neuroblastoma from other pediatric small round blue cell tumors. Two groups of immunohistochemical markers are utilized in routine practice: They are neuronal markers and neural crest markers. Neuronal markers are phenotype-specific and include PGP9.5, Synaptophysin, Chromogranin, and CD56, etc. Neural crest markers include tyrosine hydroxylase and PHOX2B, and the latter is lineage-specific and critical for neuronal differentiation (or pNT development) and neuroendocrine differentiation (or pheochromocytoma development) of the neural crest cells [43]. (Fig. 3) PHOX2B is stable and positively detected in tumor cell nuclei even in metastatic sites in bone/bone marrow after decalcification procedure [44]. Accordingly, it is highly recommended to include PHOX2B along with some of the neuronal markers for confirming the diagnosis by immunohistochemistry. When dealing with a case of small round blue cell tumor showing immunohistochemically negative for PHOX2B, serious consideration of the diagnosis other than pNT would be advised [45].

Historically, pathology diagnoses have been determined based on histological review of the samples secured by surgical resection or incisional biopsy. However, image-guided percutaneous core needle biopsies (PCNB) are increasingly utilized for obtaining pathology specimens due to their less invasive nature to the patients. For performing PCNB on pNTs, it is important to pay attention that obtained samples could be useful not only for diagnostic purpose, but also for INPC application and molecular testing critical for determining risk grouping of the individual patients. Retrospective studies reported acceptable results and demonstrated that multiple cores obtained through PCNB were reasonably effective for these purposes [46–48]. Instances of PCNB being deemed inadequate were typically due to extensive necrosis preventing MKI determination, as well as sparse cellularity and crush artifact [46, 49].

Compared with surgical resection, PCNB seems to have disadvantages when dealing with pNTs having intra-tumoral heterogeneity, such as GNB-n where a NB nodule is co-existing with GNB-i or GN. When the given tumor is large in size, however, PCNB with multiple cores may offer a better chance to obtain samples from a NB nodule, that is often hemorrhagic and/or necrotic and detected by radiology images, than incisional biopsy. For our routine practice, especially when the samples obtained by PCNB show only GNB-i or GN histology, it is advisable to include a note of “Favorable Histology of this case according to the International Neuroblastoma Pathology Classification is determined based on review of limited material” in the surgical pathology report. When tumor samples obtained by PNBC from one anatomic site shows either GNB-i or GN histology and the other anatomic site demonstrates NB histology in the same patient, we should consider (1) GNB-n where NB nodule present in the primary site is not sampled but found in the metastatic site (variant of GNB-n) [20] or (2) an extremely rare example of multiple primaries presumably composed of different clones [50].

There are two well-known risk classification systems for pNTs: (1) The International Neuroblastoma Risk Group (INRG) classification system, developed in 2009, is design to compare the risk-based clinical trials conducted across different regions and countries [51] and (2) the Revised Neuroblastoma Risk Classification (RNRC) system, published in 2021, for the purpose of patient stratification and protocol assignment to support international clinical trials by the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) [52]. According to the INRG, 4 risk groups are distinguished: Very-Low-Risk Group (> 85%, 5-year EFS), Low-Risk Group (> 75% to ≤ 85%, 5-year EFS), Intermediate-Risk Group (≥ 50% to ≤ 75%, 5-year EFS), and High-Risk Group (< 50%, 5-year EFS) [51]. According to the RNRC, 3 Risk groups are distinguished: Low-Risk Group (90.7+/-0.7% 5-year EFS and 97.9+/-0.5% 5-year OS), Intermediate-Risk Group (85.1+/-1.4% 5-year EFS and 95.8+/-0.8% 5-year OS), and High-Risk Group (51.2+/-1.4% 5-year EFS and 62.5+/-1.3% 5-year OS) [52]. Both systems are made by combination of so-called prognostic factors: i.e., age at diagnosis, clinical stage, histopathology, and genomic/molecular characteristics. One of the prognostic factors, histopathology according to the INPC, has already been outlined above and other factors are briefly described below.

While composing this review article, a new system named INPC-Risk Grouping (INPC-RG) is published by analyzing the same cohort of patients involved in establishment of the RNRC [53]. The system starts with the INPC followed by the International Neuroblastoma Risk Group Staging System (INRGSS) and then uses other prognostic factors by survival-tree regression analysis. The INPC-RG system distinguishes Group I (92.0 +/- 1.3%, 5-year EFS; 98.5+/- 0.6% 5-year OS), Group II (83.7 +/- 2,3% 5-year EFS; 95.7 +/- 1.3% 5-year OS), Group III (66.1 +/- 4.7% 5-year EFS; 77.8 +/- 4.0% 5-year OS) and Group IV (42.1 +/- 2.2% 5-year EFS; 52.9 +/- 2.2% 5-year OS). The INPC-RG system identifies highly aggressive tumors of the Group IV simply by a combination of UH according to the INPC and Stage M according to the INRGSS without requiring contribution of other prognostic factors. Whereas tumors starting with FH never end up in the Group Ⅳafter survival-tree regression analysis with other prognostic factors.

Age at diagnosis has been utilized as an important clinical factor in predicting the prognosis of NB patients. Sutow was the first to report that patients diagnosed at 24 months of age or younger had a favorable prognosis [54]. Breslow and McCann subsequently indicated that both age and stage contributed as significant predictive factors in pediatric NB and proposed a practical threshold of 365 days (one year) for distinguishing favorable and unfavorable age groups [55]. Decades later, in 2005, London et al. studied the impact of age on the outcomes of 3666 NB patients [22]. They found that, while age continuously affected prognosis of the patients with this disease and younger children had better survivals than older children at any age cutoff points, statistical support for a new cutoff of 15–19 months was confirmed and was integrated into the NB risk classification system with a cutoff of 547 days (18 months). However, age factor by itself seems to play a role as surrogate for other known or unknown prognostic factors. Furthermore, age factor has already been included in the INPC and in the clinical staging system as well (see below). Additionally, the INPC distinguishing FH and UH groups can clearly add independent prognostic information beyond the prognostic contribution of age factor [56].

Historically, International Neuroblastoma Staging System (INSS), a post-surgical system, had been applied to the pNT cases for years [57, 58]. Currently, tumors are staged using the INRGSS [59]. The INRGSS is a pre-surgical system including Stages L1, L2, M, and MS. Locoregional tumors are classified into either stage L1 or L2 based on the absence or presence of 20 image-defined risk factors (IDRFs). Stage M is applied to the cases with metastatic disease, except for Stage MS. Stage MS is applied to the special cases with metastatic disease limited to the skin, liver and/or bone marrow in children younger than 18 months of age [59].

MYCN amplification is a very strong prognostic factor indicating aggressive clinical behavior, and significantly correlates with higher clinical stage and UH (NB of either undifferentiated subtype or poorly differentiated subtype with high MKI) [42]. The MYCN oncogene is located on the short arm of Chromosome 2 [42, 60–62]. By fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), MYCN amplification is determined when more than 4-fold increase of the MYCN signals are detected compared to the signals of a reference probe located on Chromosome 2 [63, 64]. (Fig. 4) The prognostic significance of MYCN gains (MYCN signal numbers increasing less than 4-fold the number of reference signals on chromosome 2) is still uncertain [65, 66].

A recent study found that ~ 90% of MYCN-amplified tumors made MYCN protein overexpression. It was also reported that the protein overexpression rather than DNA amplification seemed to have a direct association with aggressive tumor behavior [66]. Further analysis on 800 GNB-i and GN tumors, which were classified into to Favorable Histology Group according to the INPC and linked to an excellent prognosis of the patients, 11 (1.4%) had MYCN amplification by FISH but no protein overexpression by immunohistochemistry (unpublished data). Accordingly, COG NB Study stopped performing FISH testing for MYCN oncogene status for their GNB-i and GN cases.

Tumors demonstrating a hyperdiploid pattern (DNA index > 1) with numeric whole chromosomal copy number gains without structural aberrations are reported to have an excellent prognosis in NB. In contrast, tumors having a diploid pattern (DNA index = 1) are associated with a poor clinical outcome [67, 68]. However, COG no longer provides ploidy results determined by flow cytometry analysis to treating institutions through the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (Project: EveryChild)/Molecular Characterization Initiative program. This is because DNA ploidy pattern by itself seems to have limitations in predicting prognosis for patients with this disease.

Various SCAs, partial gains or losses of chromosome regions, are reported to be indicators of poor clinical outcome in NB [69, 70]. SCAs, such as 1p loss, 11q loss, and 17q gain, were once considered as merely “prognostic markers” without linking to any specific biological activities leading to aggressive tumor growth. However, approximately 70% of MYCN-amplified tumors are known to harbor 1p36 deletions [71], and now Arid1a is considered a MYCN collaborator that accelerates NB onset [72]. CHD5 loss may confer most of the MYCN-independent tumor suppressor effects of 1p36 loss of heterozygosity (LOH) tumors [72]. 11q LOH is inversely correlated with MYCN amplification and significantly associated with the expression of MYC signature genes, suggesting elevated MYC protein expression [73, 74]. DLG2 functions as a tumor suppressor gene in NB with 11q LOH, playing a critical role in the differentiation of neural crest lineages [75]. A gain of 17q chromosome arm is the most frequent chromosomal alteration in NB, although its exact function still remains unclear [76]. However, the 17q21 breakpoint involves the IGF2BP1 and NME1 genes, which are believed to be the key oncogenes driving the functional effects of 17q gain [77].

Though ALK gene abnormalities are not included in the current prognostic factors, they are one of the hot topics in management of high-risk NB. Activated ALK, known in various cancers like anaplastic large cell lymphoma, non-small-cell lung cancer, inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor, and rhabdomyosarcoma, is found in NB as well [78–80]. ALK mutations are well documented in familial NB cases, and both ALK mutations and amplifications are reported in sporadic cases. Activated ALK abnormalities seem to drive cell-cycle progression, tumor growth, proliferation, and migration in NB [81, 82]. In mouse and zebrafish models, activated ALK can efficiently induce NB development in combination with MYCN overexpression [83, 84]. However, NB rarely or never develops in those models with activated ALK alone. In addition, there is no correlation between ALK expression levels by mRNA study and known prognostic factors in NB tumors [85]. It has also reported that there is no correlation between ALK protein expression detected by immunohistochemistry and ALK gene abnormalities [86].

Clinical trials targeting activating ALK mutations/amplifications in aggressive NBs especially with MYNC amplification have started using ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors (ALK-TKIs) [87, 88]. During these clinical trials, however, we have encountered two challenging issues: (1) developing resistance to ALK-TKIs after the treatment because of off-target resistance mechanisms, such as the activation of alternative survival pathways [89–91] and (2). increasing rate of newly developing ALK abnormalities in recurrent tumors [92–94]. More effort would be required in this field of NB research biologically and clinically.

pNTs are well known by their heterogeneity and are classified into different risk groups based on the combination of prognostic factors. Recent progress of NB research clearly discloses that highly aggressive NBs are also heterogenous and composed of molecularly distinct subgroups.

About 90% of MYCN oncogene amplified NBs overexpress MYCN protein [66], and MYC-MAX protein heterodimer formation is considered critical for up- or down-regulation of downstream targets leading to aggressive clinical behavior [95]. MYC (c-MYC) protein overexpressing NB is also reported to behave similarly aggressive [96]. Those tumors are grouped in the MYC-driven (MYC family protein overexpressing) NBs and histologically characterized by nucleolar hypertrophy of the tumor cells supporting increased protein synthesis [66]. As mentioned above, MYCN protein overexpressing NB demonstrates high MKI, while c-MYC protein overexpressing NB appears to exhibit various MKI classes [66]. It is also noted that c-MYC protein overexpressing NB rarely have c-MYC oncogene amplification: Focal enhancer amplification, enhancer hijacking, and OCT4-binding in the novel MYC promotor/enhancer sites are reported for the protein overexpression [97, 98]. The median age at diagnosis of the patients in this subgroup is around 2 years old [99].

Abnormal telomere maintenance mechanisms have been reported in various cancers [100]. In this subgroup of NB, telomerase reverse transcription (TERT) activation is caused by gene rearrangements with super-enhancer hijacking [101]. The median age at diagnosis of the patients in this subgroup is around 4 years old [99]. TERT activation is also observed in MYC subgroup, since MYC-family proteins can transcriptionally activate TERT gene [102, 103].

ALT phenotype is telomerase independent and characterized by the presence of accumulation of extrachromosomal telomeric circles [104]. Fifty-five to sixty% of NBs in this subtype are associated with ATRX alterations and rarely with death domain-associated protein (DAXX) alterations [105]. NBs in the ALT subtype are not found in the MYC subtype and TERT subtype. The median age at diagnosis of patients in the subgroup is reported to be over 5 years [99, 106].

Tumors in this subgroup do not exhibit MYC-family protein overexpression, TERT activation, or ATRX phenotype. Hopefully, tumors in this subgroup are expected to have a better response to current high-risk treatment regimens than those in the other subgroups [107]. However, more investigation is needed to identify additional molecular drivers, if any and still unknown, for explaining aggressive clinical behavior of this subtype.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) with innovative strategies has transformed healthcare by significantly impacting medical diagnosis, treatment, clinical outcome, and on-going and future research. Advances in digital whole slide imaging (WSI) have been promoting the growth of computational pathology and enhancing diagnostic capability by integrating AI with data from anatomic and clinical pathology. For example, Ramesh et al. developed a deep learning model for NB using WSI, achieved excellent performance in distinguishing between GNBI and NB, with an Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.94 and 0.92 in the training and validation datasets, respectively, but struggled in evaluation of grade of neuroblastic differentiation (poorly differentiated vs. differentiating), determination of MKI, and MYCN amplification assessment [108]. Similarly, Liu et al. created a model to distinguish prognostic groupings based on nuclear intensity and patient age with AUROC of 0.946 and 0.938 in training and test groups [109]. With close collaboration between pathologists and computer scientists, we also have been working together towards establishing AI-assisted INPC, since this classification is reported to provide very high hazard ratios [HR (95% CI)] in distinguishing FH group and UH group: EFS HR of 4.41 (3.86 to 5.03) and OS HR of 10.50 (8.44 to 13.04) after analyzing more than 4,500 of pNT cases [52]. We believe that AI-assisted model could offer new stage of (1) standardizing and improving histopathological evaluation/classification and (2) advancing collaborative research between morphological features and molecular characteristics for the sake of children with this disease world widely.
