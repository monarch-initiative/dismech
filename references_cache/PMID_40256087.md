# Acrokeratosis verruciformis of Hopf: A rare presentation in a young Indian female-A case report and review.
**Authors:** Singh G, Goswami P, Pathania Y, Pate T
**Journal:** J Family Med Prim Care (2025)
**DOI:** [10.4103/jfmpc.jfmpc_1210_24](https://doi.org/10.4103/jfmpc.jfmpc_1210_24)

## Content

Acrokeratosis verruciformis of Hopf (AVH) is a rare dermatological disorder associated with keratotic lesions primarily on the dorsal surfaces of the hands and feet, caused by a missense mutation in the ATP2A2 gene. Here, we report the case of a 24-year-old woman presenting with multiple wart-like lesions on the hands, feet, and neck over four years. A V-shaped notch on the nails indicated nail dystrophy. The patient had no family history of similar symptoms and an unremarkable medical history. The histopathological examination of a skin biopsy revealed marked hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, papillomatosis, and church spire-like epidermal elevations, confirming the diagnosis of AVH. AVH poses diagnostic challenges due to its similarity to other conditions, such as seborrheic keratosis, lichen planus, and Darier disease. Despite resource limitations precluding genetic testing, thorough clinical and histopathological evaluations were crucial for accurate diagnosis. This case underscores the importance of awareness and documentation of AVH, particularly in nonfamilial cases, to facilitate early recognition and management. Increased vigilance among dermatologists is essential to prevent misdiagnosis and to manage this rare condition effectively.

Acrokeratosis verruciformis of Hopf (AVH) is an uncommon dermatological disorder characterized by the occurrence of keratotic lesions on the dorsal surfaces of the hands and feet. The disorder is associated with a missense mutation in the ATP2A2 gene. It is caused by a genetic mutation and is associated with Darier disease.[12] The initial presentation of this condition is marked by flat-topped, skin-colored papules and wart-like lesions on the back surface of the hands and feet. Lesions are often absent on the forehead, scalp, flexor areas, and oral mucosa, but can occur on the knees, elbows, and forearms.[34] Both men and women are affected, and while most cases start in childhood, there have been a few documented adult cases in the literature[35]

Herewith, we are presenting a case of 24-year-old woman who presented with a wart-like lesion over the dorsal aspect of the hands, feet, and neck region.

A 24-year-old woman presented to the Outpatient Department of Dermatology with chief complaints of multiple wart-like lesions that had developed bilaterally on her hands and feet, as well as on her neck, over the past four years. A V-shaped notch was also reported on the nails of both hands, which could be a sign of nail dystrophy [Figure 1a and b]. She did not report any family history of similar complaints, and her medical history was unremarkable for any systemic diseases.

(a) and (b) show multiple wart-like lesions bilaterally on hands and feet, as well as on the neck, (c) skin biopsy revealed stratified squamous epithelium with marked hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, and papillomatosis, prominent granular layer in the epidermis with the formation of epidermal elevation like church spires [H and E stain, 10x] (d) Perivascular chronic inflammatory infiltrate in superficial dermis [H and E stain, 40x]

To further investigate the nature of these lesions, a skin biopsy was performed, and the sample was sent to the Department of Histopathology for further analysis. The pathology laboratory received a tissue sample, which was then treated with formalin, dehydrating chemicals, a clarifying agent, and paraffin wax to make a block. The section was sliced using a microtome and a microscopic slide was made with H and E staining. The histopathological examination of the biopsy revealed stratified squamous epithelium with marked hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, and papillomatosis, prominent granular layer in the epidermis with the formation of epidermal elevation like church spires at places. The superficial dermis shows perivascular chronic inflammatory cell infiltrate [Figure 1c and d].

Based on the clinical and histopathological findings, a diagnosis of acrokeratosis verruciformis of Hopf (AVH) was made and the patient was prescribed Alitretinoin 10 mg/day and asked for regular follow-up. Due to resource limitations and cost restraints, the mutation study of the ATP2A2 gene in Dowling–Degos disease was not feasible.

The primary cause, leading to AKV, is a persistent, hereditary autosomal dominant mutation. AKV lesions commonly manifest as flat, brown, or skin-colored papules on the dorsal surface of the hands and feet. AKV lesions are less commonly found on the legs, knees, arms, elbows, and other areas of the body. In our situation, the ailment presents itself as hyperkeratotic clustered papules on the dorsum of the right foot.[67]

It can be challenging to diagnose AKV. Seborrheic keratosis, lichen planus, and warts are among the typical ailments that this rare condition may resemble. Darier disease and epidermodysplasia verruciformis are additional conditions that can be considered as potential alternative diagnoses.[6]

AVH is categorized as a variant of Darier disease because of its similar clinical characteristics and pattern of inheritance. Darier’s illness is distinguished by the presence of dyskeratotic cells, a predilection for sebaceous regions, the involvement of the oral mucosa, and an exceedingly low probability of progressing into malignancy [Table 1].[89]

A skin biopsy is required for the definite diagnosis which revealed stratified squamous epithelium with marked hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, and papillomatosis, with the formation of epidermal elevation like church spires, as observed in our patient.[10]

Seborrheic keratosis has several variations, although the majority of them exhibit histological features, such as hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, and papillomatosis. The acanthotic variety primarily exhibits pseudo-horn cysts [Table 1].[11]

Lichen planus may exhibit morphological features similar to acrokeratosis verruciformis, such as hyperkeratosis, papillomatosis, acanthosis, and hypergranulosis. However, lichen planus histopathologically exhibits basal cell vacuolization and a band-like lymphocytic infiltration in the upper dermis [Table 1].[12]

Currently, surface ablation is the sole efficacious therapy option. Other treatment options, such as cryotherapy, laser therapy, and surgical excision, are not recommended due to their tendency to result in repeated recurrences. One should use caution when using keratolytic solutions.[1314]

In conclusion, the rarity of AKV, particularly in the Indian context, highlights the need for increased awareness and documentation of such cases. Dermatology professionals must remain vigilant and educate patients on the potential risks associated with AKV, including its rare progression to squamous cell carcinoma. By presenting this case, we aim to contribute to the literature on nonfamilial AKV and underscore the importance of thorough clinical evaluation and histopathological examination to ensure accurate diagnosis and appropriate management of this often-underestimated condition.
