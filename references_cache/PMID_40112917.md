Main Manuscript

Transferrin plays a key role in erythropoiesis by providing iron to the erythron . [1]
Atransferrinaemia has been described with early diagnosis because of severe anemia , severe
iron overload, liver disease, hypogonadism, and cardiopathy .[2] Herein, we report two sisters
diagnosed with late-onset atransferrinemia. Both showed massive iron overload and effective
erythropoiesis, questioning the exclusive role of transferrin in erythropoiesis.

A 49-year-old female was admitted for ket oacidosis revealing diabetes. CT workup revealed
cirrhosis with ascites. Blood tests revealed increased liver enzyme,  normocytic anemia
(hemoglobin:9.7g/dl) and high ferritin (12080µg/L). HFE hemochromatosis and other causes
of chronic liver disease were ruled out. There was no family history of blood -related diseases
or iron overload. The patient had no follow-up and was readmitted one year later for lethargy.
Anemia remained  stable; m yelogram revealed moderate dysmyelopoiesis.  Ferritin was
9528µg/L, transferrin saturation 163%, and transferrin 0.14g/L(N:2.02-3.64 g/L). MRI revealed
liver iron overload (362µmol/g). Bloodletting was initiated without worsening of anemia or  
need for plasma or purified transferrin supplementation.

Two months later, she was admitted with heart failure  and refractory cardiogenic shock.
Coronary angiography  was normal and h eart biopsy revealed massive iron overload.  She
underwent heart transplantation days later but died of primary graft failure.  Pathological
examination of the explant ed heart  revealed widespread iron overload and fibrosis in the
cardiomyocytes.

The patient’s legal representatives provided informed consent for diagnostic and scientific
research purposes, allowing DNA samples to be  referred for  next-generation sequencing
(coding sequences of TF (NM_001063.3), HFE, HJV, TFR2, BMP6, CP, HAMP, SLC40A1,
FTH1, FTL, SLC11A2, and TMPRSS6). Confirmation of the variant identified in the TF gene
and family screening was conducted using Sanger sequencing.  Genetic analysis revealed
homozygous TF variant NM_001063.3:c.1108G>A , p.(Glu370Lys). No other variants were
found in the gene panel.

Family screening revealed her sister (52yo) was homozygous for the same TF variant. Her
ferritin was 1408µg/L, transferrin saturation 100%,  serum transferrin 0.3 g/L, and NTBI  
1.3µmol/L (N:<0.5). Hemoglobin was 12,6g/dL with an MCV of 94,7µm³. Liver function tests
were normal, and Liver MRI revealed iron overload (219µmol/g). Cardiac MRI findings were
normal, without iron overload.  Bloodletting (300cc month ly w ithout plasma or purified
transferrin supplementation) was well tolerated and efficient ; after one year of treatment, the
ferritin level was 115µg/L without anemia.

The p.(Glu370Lys) variant was described in a heterozygous state in patients with
hypotransferrinaemia. [3] It was predicted to be deleterious by SIFT, MutationTaster, and
PolyPhen-2. Family screening showed seg regation with both sisters having very low serum
transferrin, and 5 relatives with heterozygous  p.(Glu370Lys) variant showing low serum
transferrin (1.41-1.63g/l). Therefore, according to the ACMG guidelines, this variant is Likely
Pathogenic.

Contrary to  previously described cases in young children with anemia, both sisters were
diagnosed late, with iron overload at the forefront , and normal erythropoiesis. We previously
described cases of mild hypotransferrinemia leading to iron overload without abnormal

erythropoiesis.[3] However, these patients developed iron overload only in the presence of
associated variants in iron metabolism genes. In case with severe hypotransferrinemia, massive
iron overload occurred without additional genetic or environmental causes. This is in line with
the impact of serum transferrin level on hepcidin secretion ,[4]. Therefore, in the diagnosis
workup of iron overload clinicians should not only look at transferrin saturation but also the
actual serum transferrin level.

Interestingly, although serum transferrin was similar to previously described cases, anemia was
mild in the index patient, and was absent in her sister.  Furthermore, her sister has been treated
for several years  without developing anemia event in this context of stress -induced
erythropoiesis. Some su ggests that minimal amount of transferrin  might allow normal  
erythropoiesis,[5] however these threshold are disputed. [6]

These observations strengthen interesting hypothesis for other iron routes in erythroid
precursor. [1] Discrepant results suggest their ability to uptake circulating NTBI [7]. CD44
could allow uptake of iron bound hyaluronates.[8] Ferritin uptake was described in vitro,
although its role in vivo remains elusive [9]. It can be hypothesized that  under these
circumstances this mechanism is upregulated and becomes sufficient for erythropoiesis. These
observations emphasize the  importance of defining alternative routes for iron absorption by
erythroid cells.
