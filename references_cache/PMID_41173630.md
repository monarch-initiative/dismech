# Syphilitic Aortitis Presenting With De-Novo Aortic Thrombus and Acute Myocardial Infarction.
**Authors:** Mendez RS, Mohamoud A, Abdelhamid Mahmoud OA, Park BU, Polycarpou A, Punjabi G, Shroff GR, Shaffer AW
**Journal:** JACC Case Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jaccas.2025.105428](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaccas.2025.105428)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Syphilitic aortitis, caused by Treponema pallidum, is a known complication of tertiary or latent syphilis.

CASE SUMMARY: We present a case of syphilitic aortitis in a patient who presented with acute inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and was detected to have a large, mobile aortic thrombus on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and confirmed with coronary computed tomography angiogram. After multidisciplinary discussion, the patient underwent urgent definitive surgical intervention.

DISCUSSION: With syphilis cases surging, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for syphilitic aortitis with atypical clinical presentations. Multimodality cardiovascular imaging is imperative to establish a diagnosis, and surgical intervention may be required to treat this rare disease.

TAKE-HOME MESSAGE: Awareness and timely intervention are essential to address this morbid, re-emerging public health challenge.

A 32-year-old male was brought in by emergency medical services with severe chest pain after a syncopal event. Twelve-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) by emergency medical services was significant for ST-segment elevations in leads II, III, and aVF concerning for acute inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction extending to the lateral leads (Figure 1). The patient had no prior medical history.

Due to his acute onset of symptoms, a broad differential of acute coronary syndrome, spontaneous coronary artery dissection, pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, and anomalous coronary artery were entertained.

High-sensitivity troponin I was markedly elevated at 49,046 ng/L. Due to ECG findings of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, the patient was taken emergently to the cardiac catheterization laboratory for coronary angiogram which showed no thrombotic epicardial coronary disease. Transthoracic echocardiogram showed preserved biventricular function with no diagnostic regional wall motion abnormality or valvular abnormalities. In the absence of an explanation from the coronary angiogram and echocardiogram, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) was ordered for further evaluation of presumed myocardial infarction with no obstructive coronary arteries vs myocarditis. The CMR demonstrated a focal area of near-transmural late-gadolinium hyperenhancement involving the basal inferior septum consistent with embolic infarction in a typical coronary distribution, consistent with the findings on his ECG. Importantly, and unexpectedly, the scout images revealed a highly mobile thrombotic mass on the posterior aspect of the ascending aorta, just superior to the ostium of the right coronary artery (Video 1). A gated coronary computed tomography angiogram confirmed the ascending aortic thrombus measuring 1.5 cm × 0.6 cm (Video 2) and further revealed evidence of thickening of the ascending aortic wall. In addition, renal infarcts were noted on cross-sectional imaging of the abdomen.

Hematology and rheumatology services were consulted for assistance with evaluation for systemic vasculitis and undiagnosed hypercoagulable state. A heparin infusion was initiated for acute management of aortic thrombosis, and considering the large thrombus in the ascending aorta with evidence of systemic embolic events, cardiothoracic surgery was consulted for a heart-team approach. The team considered percutaneous vs surgical intervention. Due to the risk of further embolic events and the patient's excellent surgical candidacy, definitive surgical management was favored.

The patient was expeditiously taken to the operating room for thrombectomy and possible replacement of his ascending aorta. Transesophageal echocardiogram in the operating room confirmed the thrombus adherent to the aortic wall. A median sternotomy was performed, and the aorta was closely evaluated with epiaortic ultrasound. The thrombus was easily identified in the proximal ascending aorta, and a safe place for aortic cannulation was identified (Figure 2). Central cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated. The aorta was cross-clamped at a previously identified safe spot away from the thrombus, and cardiac standstill was achieved using high-potassium cold-blood cardioplegia. The aorta was opened, and the aortic thrombus was identified adherent to an abnormal thickened aorta (Figure 3). The aorta was inspected closely, and there were ecchymotic changes of the adventitia at the site of the thrombus concerning for full-thickness injury and risk of rupture. The abnormal aorta was resected, and an interposition tube graft was sewn in place. The resected aorta was sent to pathology for further evaluation. The patient separated from central cardiopulmonary bypass without difficulty and was transported to the cardiovascular intensive care unit for postoperative recovery. The patient was extubated without event on the first postoperative night. He quickly weaned from vasopressors and was stable for transfer to the floor. Laboratory testing for syphilis with Treponema pallidum antibody and rapid plasma reagin was positive with a highly positive rapid plasma reagin titer of 1:32. Hypercoagulable testing showed a normal lupus anticoagulant and mixing study. Pathology results from the aortic specimen showed dense lymphoplasmacytic inflammation, with rare organisms highlighted by Treponema immunohistochemical stain, findings confirmatory of syphilitic aortitis (Figures 4 and 5). 1 Infectious diseases was consulted, and treatment was initiated following results of the rapid plasma reagin titer with Penicillin G benzathine 2.4 million units once weekly for 3 consecutive weeks. A lumbar puncture was offered for evaluation of possible neurosyphilis, which the patient declined. The remainder of his hospitalization was uneventful, and he discharged home without event. Syphilitic aortitis, though rare today, remains a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge and necessitates a high index of clinical suspicion. This case demonstrates an atypical but serious presentation, a mobile ascending aortic thrombus presenting with acute coronary syndrome. The patient had presumed embolic myocardial and renal infarcts; findings ultimately linked to syphilitic aortitis. Such presentations in young patients without known cardiovascular risk factors highlight the need for clinicians to maintain a broad differential and consider unusual presentations with syphilis, which is often referred to as the “great masquerader.” The pathophysiology of syphilitic aortitis centers on obliterative endarteritis of the vasa vasorum, leading to medial necrosis with weakening and destruction of elastic tissue in the aortic wall. 2 This process can foster the development of ulcerations or plaques that serve as niduses for thrombus formation. When located near the coronary ostia, as in our case, these thrombi may embolize into the coronary circulation, resulting in acute myocardial infarction. 3 , 4 , 5

Diagnosing syphilitic aortitis can be complex due to its prolonged latency, often emerging decades after the initial infection. In this case, multimodality complementary imaging with CMR and computed tomography angiogram played a crucial role in identifying aortic pathology and guiding further intervention. However, definitive diagnosis still required surgical visualization and histopathological confirmation. Maintaining a broad differential, including infectious etiologies, is vital in young patients presenting with thromboembolic events or unexplained aortic abnormalities.

The resurgence of syphilis in the United States has further relevance to this case. In 2023, over 209,000 cases were reported, with a troubling rise in late-stage disease. 6 Disruptions to sexually transmitted infection care during the COVID-19 pandemic are thought to have contributed to delayed diagnoses. While syphilitic aortitis remains rare, it can lead to severe complications, including aortic aneurysm, valvular disease, and thromboembolism. Surgical management may involve grafting, aneurysm repair, or valve replacement, depending on the presentation. Take-Home Messages • This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high-index of suspicion, early detection, multidisciplinary management, and strategic use of multimodality imaging in addressing this life-threatening but treatable condition. • As tertiary syphilis becomes more prevalent again, clinicians, especially cardiologists and cardiothoracic surgeons, must be aware of its protean manifestations and potential for delayed, serious complications.
