# La Crosse Virus Neuroinvasive Disease in Children: A Contemporary Analysis of Clinical/Neurobehavioral Outcomes and Predictors of Disease Severity.
**Authors:** Boutzoukas AE, Freedman DA, Koterba C, Hunt GW, Mack K, Cass J, Yildiz VO, de Los Reyes E, Twanow J, Chung MG, Ouellette CP
**Journal:** Clin Infect Dis (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1093/cid/ciac403](https://doi.org/10.1093/cid/ciac403)

## Content

BACKGROUND: La Crosse virus (LACV) is the most common neuroinvasive arboviral infection in children in the United States. However, data regarding predictors of disease severity and neurologic outcome are limited. Additionally, long-term neurologic and neurobehavioral outcomes remain relatively sparse.

METHODS: This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study, followed by recruitment for a cross-sectional analysis of long-term neurobehavioral outcomes, among children aged 0-18 years with proven or probable LACV neuroinvasive disease (LACV-ND) between January 2009 and December 2018. Case ascertainment was assured by International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification codes cross-referenced with laboratory results detecting LACV. Demographics, diagnostics, radiographs, and outcomes were evaluated. Recruitment of patients with prior diagnosis of LACV-ND occurred from January 2020 to March 2020, with assessment performed by validated pediatric questionnaires.

RESULTS: One-hundred fifty-two children (83 males; median age, 8 years [interquartile range, 5-11.5 years]) were diagnosed with proven (n = 61 [47%]) and probable (n = 91 [60%]) LACV-ND. Sixty-five patients (43%) had severe disease. Altered mental status (AMS) (odds ratio [OR], 6.36 [95% confidence interval {CI}, 2.03-19.95]; P = .0002) and seizures at presentation (OR, 10.31 [95% CI, 3.45-30.86]; P = .0001) were independent predictors of severe disease. Epileptiform discharges on electroencephalogram (EEG) were independently associated with epilepsy diagnosis at follow-up (OR, 13.45 [95% CI, 1.4-128.77]; P = .024). Fifty-four patients were recruited for long-term neurobehavioral follow-up, with frequent abnormal assessments identified (19%-54%) irrespective of disease severity.

CONCLUSIONS: Severe disease was observed frequently among children with LACV-ND. Seizures and AMS at presentation were independent predictors of severe disease. EEG may help determine long-term epilepsy risk. Long-term neurobehavioral issues are frequent and likely underrecognized among children with LACV-ND.

Abstract

Keywords: La Crosse virus, encephalitis, pediatric
Altered mentation and seizures at presentation are reliable predictors of severe La Crosse Virus neuroinvasive disease (LACV-ND). Epileptiform discharges on electroencephalogram during hospitalization is predictive of long-term epilepsy diagnosis. Children with LACV-ND have long-term neurobehavioral problems, which may be underrecognized.
La Crosse virus (LACV) is a member of the California serogroup of Bunyaviridae and remains the most common neuroinvasive arbovirus infection in children in the United States (US) [1–3]. Human disease transmission occurs through bites of infected Aedes triseriatus mosquitoes in the Midwestern, mid-Atlantic, and Southeastern US. Patients with LACV neuroinvasive disease (LACV-ND) present commonly with fevers, headache, vomiting, altered mental status (AMS), and seizures. Additionally, pediatric patients are more likely to have symptomatic disease than adults.
In the United States, an average of 68 cases of LACV-ND were reported annually from 2009 to 2018 to ArboNet, a Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) surveillance program for arboviral infections [4]. Given the incidence and distribution, limited publications examining large cohorts of children exist. In 2001, McJunkin et al published the largest study to date examining clinical presentation and outcomes of 127 children with LACV-ND. This study and others attempted to identify early clinical factors that predict severe outcomes or clinical deterioration during hospitalization. However, sample size has limited the determination of reliable predictors [5, 6]. Additionally, data on long-term clinical and neurobehavioral outcomes are either limited or out of date.
We hypothesized that clinical, laboratory, or radiographic findings evident at presentation or during hospitalization are predictive of disease severity or epilepsy diagnosis at long-term follow-up. Additionally, we suspected that LACV-ND–associated long-term cognitive and behavioral difficulties are underreported. To address these issues, we performed a retrospective analysis of pediatric patients with LACV-ND at a single institution over a 10-year period, with recruitment of patients post–LACV-ND for cross-sectional neurobehavioral assessment.
METHODS
Subjects and Definitions
This retrospective cohort study included children (aged 0–18 years) with a diagnosis of LACV-ND who received care at Nationwide Children’s Hospital (NCH) in Columbus, Ohio, from January 2009 to December 2018. To assess long-term neurobehavioral outcomes, we attempted to recruit any child with prior LACV-ND diagnosis who was <18 years of age as of January 2020 to complete standardized neurobehavioral questionnaires. Children and families were compensated for their participation. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at NCH.
Case ascertainment was assured by use of International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification codes for California La Crosse encephalitis (062.5 and A83.5, respectively) cross-referenced with virologic results detecting California serogroup virus. Inclusion in the study required a compatible clinical illness and serologic detection of LACV, in absence of other acute infectious etiologies. Seropositive results for LACV were defined as serum detection of IgG ≥1:16 to California serogroup (Focus Diagnostics, Cypress, California). Additional virologic testing was performed at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and included LACV-specific immunoglobulin M (IgM) capture by enzyme immunoassay (EIA) and LACV-specific immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody by EIA or plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT). Proven LACV disease required the presence of either (1) LACV-specific IgM antibody by EIA (serum or cerebrospinal fluid [CSF]) with LACV-specific IgG antibody by EIA or PRNT, or (2) ≥4-fold change in LACV-specific serum IgG antibody titer. Probable disease was defined as a compatible clinical illness with an elevated LACV-specific IgG antibody titer in absence of convalescent serology or ≤2-fold change, or LACV-specific IgM by EIA (in serum or CSF) without IgG confirmation.
Demographic, clinical, laboratory, neuroimaging, treatment, and outcome data were recorded. Diagnostic testing data included serologic assessment (serum and CSF), neuroimaging results (computed tomography [CT], magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]), and electroencephalographic (EEG) findings. A subject with any 1 of the following during acute illness was classified as having severe disease: clinical or electrographic status epilepticus, syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) [5], new need for parenteral/tube feeding, intensive care admission, mechanical ventilation, intracranial pressure monitoring, need for inpatient rehabilitation, or inpatient death.
Long-term neurologic outcome follow-up data were collected on patients who had ≥1 documented encounter(s) within our electronic health record (EHR) related to LACV-ND, at least 1 month after primary diagnosis. Outcome variables included diagnosis of epilepsy at follow-up, continued antiseizure medication management, and need for surgical intervention related to intractable epilepsy. Independent review and determination of status epilepticus, EEG reports, and epilepsy diagnosis [7] after discharge from initial hospitalization was independently made by an epileptologist and author of this paper (D. A. F.).
To assess long-term neurobehavioral outcomes, standardized parent-report questionnaires were sent to eligible families to assess long-term neurobehavioral outcomes. Five forms were included: an intake form (Supplementary Methods 1) and 4 validated neurobehavioral assessments to evaluate different domains of function, including the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) 4.0 Generic Core Scales [8], the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) [9], the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) [10], and the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Functioning, second edition (BRIEF-2) (Supplementary Methods 2). Survey-specific scoring protocols and normative data were applied to interpret the results of the questionnaires. For subjects identified with severe impairments and without known neurobehavioral assessments, study staff contacted the subject and family to offer formal neuropsychological evaluation.
Statistical Analysis
To analyze the data, descriptive and nonparametric statistics were used. To assess for differences between severe and nonsevere cohorts and presence or absence of epilepsy at follow-up, categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher exact test and continuous variables were analyzed with Mann–Whitney U test. Multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed for prediction of disease severity and epilepsy diagnosis using clinically relevant variables. For both multivariate analyses, correction for multiple comparisons was performed by controlling the false discovery rate at 5%. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. For all neurobehavioral measures, cutoff scores (defined as 1.5 standard deviations below the normative mean) were used to dichotomize normal vs abnormal results. χ2 analyses were performed to evaluate associations between abnormal scores and disease severity or age at admission. Exploratory analyses were conducted to examine the difference between the cohort sample means compared to normative population means on 2 of the standardized measures (CBCL and BRIEF-2). We chose to examine composite scores from the measure of executive functions (BRIEF-2) and the measure of overall emotional and behavioral functioning (CBCL). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad, La Jolla, California) or SPSS (IBM, Armonk, New York) software. P values ≤ .05 were considered statistically significant.
RESULTS
Patient Characteristics, Diagnostic Evaluation, and Clinical Outcomes
From January 2009 to December 2018, 152 patients diagnosed with LACV-ND at NCH met inclusion criteria. Eighty-three (55%) patients were male, and the median age at diagnosis was 8 years (interquartile range [IQR], 5–11.5 years). A median of 12.5 (IQR, 8–20.5) patients per year were diagnosed with LACV-ND, presenting between May and October, most frequently in late summer months (Figure 1A and 1B). Epidemiologic history frequently identified rural residence (n = 129 [85%]) and mosquito exposure (n = 110 [72%]). Clinical findings are summarized in Table 1. Sixty-one (40%) cases were proven and 91 (60%) were probable; 65 (43%) patients had severe disease. Median time to presentation was 4 days (IQR, 3–5 days) after symptom onset and 2 days (IQR, 1–4 days) after fever.

Laboratory findings are described in Table 1. CSF analysis was notable for an elevated white blood cell count (median, 132.5 cells/µL [IQR, 59–230 cells/µL]), consistent with aseptic meningitis. Initial serologic assessment by LACV serum IgG was positive in 125 (82%) patients. LACV serum IgM, while performed infrequently, was positive on all tested samples (n = 28). Head CT, performed on 114 (75%) patients, was abnormal in 16 (14%). MRI of the head, performed on 91 (60%) patients (90/91 performed with intravenous contrast), was abnormal in 69 (76%), primarily demonstrating focal T2 hyperintensities or restricted diffusion (n = 49 [71%]). EEG was performed in 74 (49%) patients and was abnormal in 71 (96%), demonstrating predominantly slowing (n = 69 [93%]) with a minority (n = 18 [25%]) noting epileptiform discharges.
The median length of hospital stay was 5 days (IQR, 3–6 days). In total, 67 (44%) patients had an acute symptomatic seizure at any point during their acute illness, with seizures occurring during the hospital admission in 37 (24%) patients. Sixty-five (43%) patients met criteria for severe disease. Among these, 46 (71%) patients required pediatric intensive care unit admission, and 41 (63%) had status epilepticus. Seventeen (26%) patients required intubation and mechanical ventilation, 14 (22%) received tube feedings (1 required parental nutrition), and 4 (6%) were diagnosed with SIADH. Twelve (18%) patients required inpatient rehabilitation. No inpatient deaths were observed.
Follow-up data after hospital discharge were available for 104 (68%) patients. Fifteen (14%) patients had a diagnosis of epilepsy at follow-up. A diagnosis of epilepsy was observed more frequently in patients who had seizure activity (60% vs 28%, P = .03) or epileptiform discharges on EEG (54% vs 20%, P = .03) during hospitalization (Table 2). On multivariate analysis, epileptiform discharges observed on EEG during hospitalization were independently associated with an epilepsy diagnosis at long-term follow-up (OR, 13.45 [95% CI, 1.4–128.77]; P = .024) (Supplementary Table 1).

Evaluating for Predictors of Severe Disease
In our cohort, severe disease was observed more frequently in those with a shorter duration of symptoms at presentation (3 vs 4 days, P = .0005; Table 1). Younger age (≤5 years vs >5 years, P = .008), AMS (85% vs 44%, P < .0001) and seizure activity (72% vs 16%, P < .0001) at presentation were associated with severe disease (Table 1). Initial negative serologic assessment was observed more frequently in those with severe disease (31% vs 8%, P = .0003). On multivariate analysis, AMS (OR, 6.36 [95% CI, 2–20]; P = .002) and seizures (OR, 10.3 [95% CI, 3.5–30.9]; P = .001) at presentation were independently associated with severe disease (Table 3).

Evaluation of Long-term Neurobehavioral Sequelae
One-hundred thirteen (74%) patients were recruited for long-term follow-up via standardized questionnaires mailed to their homes; 54 (48%) patient questionnaires were returned. Of those who returned packets, we observed no differences in sex, disease severity, or length of stay when compared to recruited patients who did not participate (Supplementary Table 2). The time from discharge to recruitment was shorter for those who returned packets compared to those who did not (median, 4.3 years [IQR, 1.4–7.4 years] vs 7.6 years [IQR, 6.5–10.6 years]; P < .0001). Results from the intake form are reported in Supplementary Figure 1. Eighteen (35%) children had Individualized Education Plans or Section 504 plans, and 9 (17%) were receiving mental health services or psychotherapy. On the PedsQL, mean scores for emotional, social, school functioning, and total score were all below clinical cutoffs (Table 4). Frequencies of patients with scores below the clinical cutoff ranged from 44% to 56% for each domain on the PedsQL. Scores across all major domains evaluated on the SDQ, BRIEF-2, and CBCL were within the average range, though frequencies of individual scores greater than the cutoff ranged from 19% to 30%. In comparing our sample scores to normative population data on the BRIEF-2 and CBCL, our sample differed significantly on the BRIEF-2 Emotional Regulation Index and the CBCL Internalizing Problems Index (P < .05). Severe disease and age on admission (≤5 vs >5 years) were not associated with differences in scores on any standardized assessment or parental reported diagnoses.

DISCUSSION
LACV is the most common cause of pediatric arboviral encephalitis in the United States [2, 3]. However, there is a paucity of data on predictors of disease severity and long-term neurologic and neurobehavioral outcomes in pediatric LACV-ND. To address this, we assessed the largest cohort to date of pediatric patients with proven or probable LACV-ND. In our cohort, AMS and seizure activity at presentation were independently associated with severe disease, and epileptiform discharges observed on EEG during hospitalization were suggestive of a long-term epilepsy diagnosis. Last, a substantial portion of children demonstrated neurobehavioral abnormalities on standardized assessments, suggesting an underrecognized long-term neurologic impact of LACV disease.
As expected with the pathophysiology of LACV infection, most patients in our cohort presented with fever and neurologic complaints. Prior studies have described a similar epidemiology of LACV-ND including demographics, age at presentation, and clinical findings including high seizure rates at presentation (46%–61%) [5, 6, 11]. Within our cohort, children with severe LACV-ND tended to be younger and presented to the hospital earlier in their clinical course. The suggestion of earlier time to presentation is supported by other temporally dependent processes, such as a general finding of neutrophilic predominance and higher proportion of initial IgG seronegative testing results. This insight raises questions regarding the host–pathogen interaction, specifically the importance of the innate immune response. Arboviruses encode nonstructural proteins that interfere with type I interferon (IFN) responses, for which nonstructural proteins directly antagonize type I IFN signaling in LACV infection [12, 13]. Mouse models of LACV infection have noted an age-dependent myeloid dendritic cell response, where older mice produced a more robust type I IFN response protective of neuroinvasive disease [14]. However, the role of type I IFN responses within the central nervous system (CNS) requires further elucidation as recent murine models suggest a potential pathogenic contribution of innate immune responses during LACV infection [15, 16]. Interestingly, no patient in our cohort had known immunocompromise, despite a recent pediatric study noting an increased likelihood of infectious causes of encephalitis among immunocompromised patients [17]. Our findings may lend support to the apparent dichotomy of the murine model results, though assessment of peripheral and CNS immune responses among symptomatic children is needed to clarify host susceptibility to LACV-ND.
Identification of predictors of severe LACV-ND is of clinical importance, allowing for improved risk assessment and timely provision of interventions to higher-risk subgroups. McJunkin et al evaluated a cohort of 127 pediatric LACV cases and noted that patients with a history of vomiting and a Glasgow Coma Scale score <12 were at increased risk of in-hospital deterioration [5]. Miller et al described a cohort of 47 patients and noted that seizure activity on admission was associated with an increased length of stay [6]. Unfortunately, sample sizes in prior studies have limited assessment of potential variables, and low overall case numbers have made reproducibility of studies challenging. Despite each study using varying definitions of disease severity, a similar trend is evident with respect to neurologic symptoms at presentation and complicated LACV-ND. In our cohort, AMS and seizure activity at presentation were independently associated with severe disease. As seizures and status epilepticus are not mutually exclusive, we repeated multivariate analysis excluding status epilepticus from our severe disease definition and found similar results. Importantly, the clinical factors identified are readily assessable by providers, thus useful in initial management and anticipatory guidance. Last, the association of AMS and seizure activity at presentation with severe disease, specifically with concerns of status epilepticus, raises further questions regarding whether empiric prophylaxis with antiseizure medications or routine prolonged EEG monitoring may be beneficial in those presenting with severe LACV-ND.
Given the significant potential for neurologic compromise in patients with LACV-ND, describing long-term epilepsy outcomes and identifying predictors of epilepsy is of value. Previous studies have noted that 6%–13% of patients experience a recurrent seizure 1–8 years following LACV-ND, though lack of follow-up data has been a limiting factor in calculating the rate of epilepsy in this population [11, 18, 19]. In our cohort, 15 patients with follow-up assessments (representing 10% of the entire cohort) were later diagnosed with epilepsy. Lateralized periodic discharges, the EEG finding previously referred to as periodic lateralized epileptiform discharges, were reported more frequently in patients with severe LACV-ND and in those who would develop epilepsy after LACV-ND diagnosis [18]. In our cohort, epileptiform discharges observed on EEG during admission were independently associated with an increased likelihood of epilepsy diagnosis at follow-up assessment. This finding further supports the value of EEG as both a diagnostic and prognostic tool for patients with LACV-ND.
Despite the known acute neurologic complications of LACV-ND, long-term neurobehavioral outcomes have been relatively understudied. Previous research has been mixed; some studies documented significant cognitive and behavioral deficits in children with severe LACV-ND while others noted relatively limited neurobehavioral sequelae [5, 20]. We conducted a cross-sectional assessment of neurobehavioral outcomes in 54 patients with prior LACV-ND. The PedsQL has previously been used to assess neurobehavioral outcomes among children with encephalitis [21]; in our cohort, a substantial portion of patients had abnormal total scores (48%). Additionally, our sample population performed worse on measures of emotional regulation and internalization when compared to the normative population. This suggests that children with LACV-ND may be at greater risk for emotional difficulties as compared to their healthy peers. Although beyond the scope of this paper, a more thorough analysis of our data may clarify the nature of the emotional difficulties reported in our sample. Interestingly, neither disease severity nor age at LACV-ND diagnosis was associated with increased rates of neurobehavioral difficulties on the intake form or questionnaires. The economic and social impacts of LACV-ND have been reported previously [22], and our data add to these findings given the neurobehavioral impairments observed in those with and without severe disease. In total, our data argue for routine long-term neurobehavioral follow-up care for children following a diagnosis and hospitalization for LACV-ND regardless of disease severity.
Though this is the largest evaluation of LACV to date, the retrospective design in a single institution, lack of systematic testing and evaluation, and possible selection bias are limitations of the study. Patients with LACV infection and mild symptomatology may have not sought care or had repeat serologic assessment if initially seronegative, and thus are not included in our analysis. However, the clinical, laboratory, and neurologic findings among patients within our cohort resembles prior reports [5, 6], suggesting that our study population serves as a representative cohort of symptomatic LACV-ND. Diagnostic evaluation was limited in that convalescent sera were performed infrequently, and not all sera were confirmed by PRNT for LACV. Additionally, our definition of probable disease differs from the CDC case definition, notably with use of serum IgG as opposed to IgM for LACV diagnostic testing. This decision is supported by prior data noting the high positive predictive value of serum IgG in LACV diagnosis among a symptomatic, pediatric population living within an LACV-endemic region [23]. Consistent with these data, all patients in our cohort were acutely symptomatic with findings consistent of LACV-ND, and review of virologic records noted that all patients with confirmatory CDC testing in our cohort had detectible IgM, as well as PRNT positive for LACV (data not shown). Classification of EEG findings was limited due to reliance on EEG reports and changes in EEG terminology during the study period [24]. Long-term neurologic outcomes were limited to records in our EHR, potentially representing a selection bias in downstream analyses. For cognitive and behavioral outcomes, cross-sectional analysis limits our ability to account for influences other than LACV-ND since discharge. Finally, the neurobehavioral assessment was done as a parent proxy report for all children regardless of age, and thus may differ from self-report or direct observation/neurocognitive assessment.
In summary, our contemporary cohort of children with LACV-ND presented most frequently with fever and neurologic symptoms at diagnosis. AMS and seizures at presentation independently predicted severe disease, and the presence of epileptiform discharges on EEG during admission was an independent predictor of epilepsy diagnosis at long-term follow-up. Finally, long-term neurobehavioral difficulties were identified frequently in our cohort, likely reflecting an underreporting of cognitive and emotional/behavioral sequelae after LACV infection. Our data underscore the need for further assessment of immunologic responses in children with LACV-ND and prospective studies of neurologic and neurobehavioral assessment in children after LACV infection, to better define outcomes and guide interventions.
