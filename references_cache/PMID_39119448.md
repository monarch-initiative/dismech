# Two Sibling Cases of Spastic Paraplegia-45 with a Novel Pathogenic Variant in NT5C2 Gene: Concomitant RYR1 Gene in One Sibling.
**Authors:** İpek R, Kömür M, Direk MÇ, Bozdoğan ST, Okuyaz Ç
**Journal:** Mol Syndromol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1159/000536183](https://doi.org/10.1159/000536183)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Hereditary spastic paraplegia (SPG) is a genetically and clinically heterogeneous group of rare neurodegenerative disorders. SPG45 is the AR inherited type of complicated SPG, which is due to a mutation in the NT5C2 gene.

CASE PRESENTATION: Two sisters, aged 8 and 4, exhibited delayed motor development since early childhood. They also experienced learning difficulties, dysarthric speech, ataxia, nystagmus, strabismus, and spasticity in their extremities. Additionally, brisk deep tendon reflexes were observed in their upper and lower limbs, and they exhibited positive pathological reflexes. Whole-exome sequencing identified a previously unidentified homozygous mutation in the NT5C2 gene, leading to the diagnosis of SPG45 in both siblings. A mutation in the RYR1 gene associated with malignant hyperthermia was also detected in one of the siblings, necessitating ongoing monitoring.

DISCUSSION/CONCLUSION: To the best of our knowledge, we report the first case of a patient with coexistence of the NT5C2 gene and the RYR1 gene.

Hereditary spastic paraplegia (SPG) is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by spasticity, reduced muscle strength, gait disturbance, brisk deep tendon reflexes (DTRs), and extensor plantar response. It can be inherited in various ways (autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X-linked recessive, or mitochondrial). SPGs are divided into complicated and uncomplicated types. The complicated type is typically inherited in an autosomal recessive manner and includes additional features such as mental retardation, epilepsy, optic atrophy, hearing loss, peripheral neuropathies, ataxia, and skin abnormalities [1, 2].

SPG45, first reported in 2009 by Dursun et al. [3], is an autosomal recessive form of complicated SPG caused by a mutation in the cytosolic 5′-nucleotidase II (NT5C2) gene located at chromosomal locus 10q24.3-q25.1. Unlike many other SPGs, individuals with SPG45 exhibit clinical features, including spasticity, delayed gait, brisk DTRs, positive pathological reflexes, dysarthria, and mental retardation. These symptoms typically appear in infancy, with early onset noticed within the first year of life. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) images of SPG45 patients often reveal a thin corpus callosum (TCC) and hyperintense lesions in the periventricular white matter [1–7].

The ryanodine receptor 1 (RYR1) gene, located at chromosomal locus 19q13.2, is responsible for encoding calcium release from the skeletal muscle sarcoplasmic reticulum, the source of intracellular calcium. Mutations in this gene can give rise to various conditions, such as malignant hyperthermia (MH), central core disease, and minicore myopathy. MH is a rare but potentially life-threatening condition characterized by skeletal muscle dysfunction triggered by specific anaesthetics. Abnormal calcium release from the muscle sarcoplasmic reticulum, caused by the RYR1 gene mutation, leads to sustained muscle contraction, hyperthermia, and muscle rigidity [8].

This report highlights the case of two siblings diagnosed with SPG45 with a novel mutation in the NT5C2 gene. Additionally, it was revealed that one of the siblings and their father had a mutation in the RYR1 gene. To our knowledge, this is the first documented instance of simultaneous mutations in the NT5C2 and RYR1 genes.

An 8-year-old girl presented at our hospital with complaints of difficulty walking, initially diagnosed with cerebral palsy. Over the past few years, she has undergone physiotherapy, received botulinum toxin injections, and underwent bilateral hamstring release surgery. There were no significant perinatal complications. The patient exhibited notable motor delay, not being able to walk until the age of three. Laboratory investigations were within normal range, including haemogram, blood biochemistry, creatine kinase, ammonia, lactate, tandem mass, and urinary organic acid. The patient displayed dysmorphic facial features and an intellectual disability. She also experienced learning difficulties, dysarthric speech, ataxia, nystagmus, strabismus, and spasticity in the upper and lower limbs. DTRs were brisk, particularly in the lower extremities. Positive signs of Babinski and clonus were observed, and residual urine was present in the bladder. Brain MRI revealed thinning of the corpus callosum and hyperintense signal changes in the frontal periventricular white matter (Fig. 1). Spinal MRI and ENMG findings were normal.

A four-year-old sister of the patient presented with similar complaints and clinical signs. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on the 8-year-old girl, who had a preliminary diagnosis of SPG. The analysis identified a homozygous frameshift mutation (c.591_594dup p.(Phe199Profs17)) in exon 9 of the NT5C2 gene (Fig. 2). During the family screening, this mutation was also found in a homozygous state in the 4-year-old sister and a heterozygous state in their parents. The mutation was not previously reported and was considered a potential cause after in silico analysis (https://varsome.com/variant/hg19/NT5C2(NM_001351177.1)%3Ac.591_594dup). Additionally, the older sister was found to be heterozygous for a frameshift mutation (c.2084del p.(Pro695Leufs35)) in exon 18 of the RYR1 gene, which is considered potentially pathogenic. This mutation was also detected in the father. Both siblings were diagnosed with SPG type 45, inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. All variants were sequenced via NGS; therefore, variants need not be validated with Sanger. Furthermore, the older sister and the father were monitored for inherited MH but did not exhibit any related symptoms.

This paper presents the case of 2 siblings who were initially misdiagnosed with cerebral palsy due to motor developmental delay, spasticity, and brisk DTRs, especially in the lower extremities during infancy. Subsequently, they were diagnosed with SPG45 after identifying a novel mutation in the NT5C2 gene. Additionally, WES revealed the presence of a heterozygous RYR1 gene mutation in the older sister. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case in the literature of concurrent SPG45 disease and RYR1 mutation.

A total of 26 reported cases of SPG45 have been documented thus far. Table 1 provides an overview of the clinical features of these cases as reported in studies [1–7]. The clinical findings observed in our two sisters were highly consistent with those reported in the literature. Additionally, the MRI findings of SPG45 patients typically demonstrate TCC and periventricular white matter lesions, as noted in studies by Novarino et al. [1], Darvish et al. [4], and Straussberg et al. [5]. Interestingly, our study found similar MRI findings in both siblings. However, it is worth mentioning that Darvish et al. [4] reported normal brain MRI findings in their study.

A novel homozygous variant in the ninth exon of the NT5C2 gene was identified through WES of our patient. This variant, located at the 199th amino acid position, results in an early stop codon and the production of a truncated protein (NM_001351170.1) c.591_594dup p.(Phe199Profs*17) after 17 additional amino acids. Notably, this variant was not found in the HGMD and ClinVar variant databases, and in silico analysis programs (CADD, PolyPhen, SIFT, MutationTaster, BLOSUM, PhyloP, MaxEntScan, GeneSplicer, B-SIFT, QCI Inferred Activation) categorized it as “possibly pathogenic” based on the ACMG standards and guidelines. Family screening and segregation analysis confirmed the presence of the same mutation.

The underlying mechanism by which mutations in the NT5C2 gene, which encodes the protein “cytosolic purine 5′-nucleotidase II,” lead to the clinical features and MRI findings observed in SPG. While studies on knockout mice suggest that NT5C2 deletion may not have an impact on muscle contraction, the segregation analysis results and in silico perspective indicate that the NT5C2 mutation may still have a phenotypic effect [9]. This protein plays a critical role in maintaining nucleotide and nucleoside balance in the brain and spinal cord by being involved in purine metabolism [10]. In addition to their involvement in nucleic acid synthesis, purines also participate in various metabolic pathways, cell growth control, and energy production [11]. Some purines have specific functions in brain development and neuronal protection against ischaemia [10, 11]. Thus, alterations in purine levels may explain the described clinical and radiological findings associated with homozygous mutations in the NT5C2 gene as they potentially render neurons more susceptible to stress [5].

The older sister and her father were found to have a p.(Pro695Leufs*35) mutation in the RYR1 gene. MH, central core disease, and minicore myopathy have been defined depending on this variant. However, it seems that this variant in the RYR1 gene does not contribute to the development of clinical features in the older sister. As a precaution, they were monitored for MH and advised to avoid trigger agents such as inhalational anaesthetics or succinylcholine. We believe that our patient’s co-occurrence of the RYR1 gene variant and the NT5C2 gene mutation is coincidental. This previously undefined coincidental association was detected for the first time in our study.

In conclusion, SPG45 should be considered in the differential diagnosis of motor developmental delay, especially in the absence of risk factors for cerebral palsy during infancy. Furthermore, ophthalmologic examinations and evaluation of MRI findings, including TCC and periventricular white matter lesions, are essential for diagnosing SPG45. Notably, NT5C2 and RYR1 gene mutations can coexist in the same patient. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case documenting the coexistence of these mutations.
