Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most frequent histological subtype 
of non-Hodgkin lymphoma and the paradigm for the management of aggressive 
lymphoma. An excisional or incisional lymph node biopsy evaluated by an 
experienced hematopathologist is recommended to establish the diagnosis. Twenty 
years following its introduction, the combination of rituximab, 
cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (R-CHOP) remains the 
standard first-line treatment. No modification of this scheme (increased 
chemotherapy dose intensity, new monoclonal antibodies, or the addition of 
immunomodulators or anti-target agents) has significantly improved the clinical 
outcomes, whereas therapy for recurrence or progression is evolving rapidly. The 
introduction of chimeric antigen receptor T cells, polatuzumab vedotin, 
tafasitamab, and cluster of differentiation (CD)20/CD3 bispecific antibodies is 
changing the natural history of patients with relapsed DLBCL and will challenge 
R-CHOP as the benchmark for newly diagnosed patients. These updated guidelines 
(2025) summarize current evidence and available therapies for the medical 
management of DLBCL.

Introduction
This article presents the 2025 update to the clinical practice 
guidelines for the management of diffuse large B-cell lym-
phoma (DLBCL), developed jointly by the Spanish Society 
of Medical Oncology (Sociedad Española de Oncología 
Médica; SEOM) and the Oncology Group for the Treat-
ment and Study of Lymphomas (Grupo Oncológico para 
el Tratamiento y Estudio de los Linfomas; GOTEL). The 
previous edition was published in 2022 [1 ]. Since then, 
new clinical trial data and regulatory developments have 
emerged, prompting a review and revision of selected rec-
ommendations (Table  1). The current update incorporates 
these changes, refines the wording of prior guidance where 
needed, and maintains alignment with evolving international 
standards. It is intended as a practical reference for oncolo-
gists managing patients with DLBCL in Spain and reflects 
expert consensus on evidence-based strategies across the 
diagnostic, therapeutic, and post-treatment continuum.
Methodology
This guideline is based on relevant published studies and 
the consensus of ten GOTEL and SEOM oncologists with 
expertise in lymphoma treatment. The updated recommen-
dations in these guidelines reflect the results of clinical tri-
als released since the publication of the previous version 
in 2022, as well as minor corrections to the wording of the 
original text. The updated version has been reviewed and 
approved by all the authors. The Infectious Diseases Soci-
ety of America–United States (US) Public Health Service 
Grading System for Ranking Recommendations in Clinical 
Guidelines [2] has been used to assign levels of evidence and 
grades of recommendation (Supplementary Table).
Incidence and epidemiology
In Spain, non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) is the seventh 
most common tumor in men and the eighth most common 
in women [3 ]. As in other Westernized countries, its inci-
dence increased by 6% per year between 1975 and 1995 for 
unknown reasons and, since then, has remained stable [4 ].
DLBCL is the most common histological subtype of NHL 
(35%) in Spain, followed by follicular lymphoma (27%) 
and marginal cell lymphoma (13%), and is the paradigm of 
aggressive lymphoma [1]. The estimated number of cases of 
DLBCL diagnosed in Spain in 2024 was 4473, with a crude 
incidence rate of 9.3 cases/100,000 inhabitants/year [5]. The 
median age at diagnosis was 64 years and, as with most 
hematological neoplasms, it was more frequent in men (sex 
ratio 1.1) [6 , 7]. During the 2002–2013 period, the 5-year 
relative survival of DLBCL in Spain was 55.6% [8].

clinical presentation (Table 2). The differential diagnosis of 
DLBCL includes other entities that can result in lymphad-
enopathy and similar pathologic features. Patients usually 
present with a growing mass in a lymph node area or with 
symptoms of infiltration of extralymphatic organs. One-third 
have systemic B symptoms (i.e., fever, weight loss, sweats), 
and 40% have extranodal disease.
When available, a lymph node is generally the preferred 
tissue for diagnosis. Biopsy specimens should include both 
fresh and formalin-fixed material and should be evaluated 
by an experienced hematopathologist. If nodal involvement 
is not identified, diagnosis can be made using extranodal 
tissue. The tissue sample should be obtained urgently if 
aggressive NHL is suspected and, ideally, prior to steroid 
treatment.
An excisional or incisional lymph node biopsy is recom-
mended to establish the diagnosis of DLBCL. A core nee-
dle biopsy is not optimal but can be used in certain clinical 
circumstances. Importantly, fine-needle aspiration alone is 
not an acceptable substitute for an initial diagnosis of lym-
phoma, although it may suffice to diagnose recurrence [9 ].
In addition to morphological classification, pathology 
assessment must necessarily include immunohistochemis-
try (IHC), flow cytometry, fluorescent in situ hybridization 
(FISH), and molecular tests. Three main morphologic sub-
types of DLBCL (centroblastic, immunoblastic, and anaplas-
tic) have been recognized, albeit with poor reproducibility 
among pathologists and scant clinical relevance. The IHC 
core panel for DLBCL diagnosis includes cluster of differen-
tiation (CD)20, CD3, CD5, CD10, CD45, B-cell lymphoma 
(BCL)2, BCL6, Ki-67, interferon regulatory factor 4 (IRF4)/
multiple myeloma oncogene 1 (MUM1), and MYC. CD30, 
human herpesvirus (HHV)-8, and Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) 
may be needed for classification.
Gene expression profiling (GEP) classifies DLBCL into 
three biological subtypes that arise from the developmental 
stages of normal B cells or their so-called ‘cell of origin’ 
(COO): germinal center B-cell-like (GCB, 56%), activated 
B-cell-like (ABC, 32%), and type 3 (11%). Retrospective 
studies have revealed significantly better outcomes for GCB 
compared with ABC [10–12]. However, prospective studies 
have failed to detect significant differences in terms of sur -
vival. Furthermore, COO alone does not explain the clinical 
heterogeneity across patients, and up to 20% of DLBCL can-
not be classified using this genetic signature [13].
Hans’ immunohistochemical algorithm based on CD10, 
BCL6, and IRF4/MUM1 differentiates the COO more 
quickly and cost-effectively, but does not identify the entire 
ABC subgroup, and there is insufficient scientific evidence 
to be of use for treatment decisions [14].
Genomic sequencing platforms classify DLBCL into 
groups with different genetic signatures that are related to 
treatment outcomes. However, a significant proportion of 
cases remain unclassified by these genetic models, and they 
are not currently available in routine clinical practice [15].
FISH analysis of aggressive B-cell lymphomas is needed 
to detect MYC and BCL2/BCL6 translocations, as they 
define a new entity in the latest World Health Organiza-
tion (WHO) classification (“High-grade B-cell lymphoma 
with MYC and BCL2 and/or BCL6 rearrangements”), which 
more often than not belongs to the GCB. These patients have 
particularly poor outcomes with rituximab, cyclophospha-
mide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (R-CHOP), 
making them good candidates for inclusion in clinical trials. 
DLBCL overexpressing MYC and BCL2 (double expres-
sors) tend to be included in the ABC group and should not 
be confused with double-hit or triple-hit, high-grade lym-
phomas that carry genetic translocations [16].
Staging and risk assessment
The staging process should include a complete medical his-
tory, physical examination, laboratory tests, and imaging 
studies according to the recommendations for initial evalu-
ation, staging, and response assessment of Hodgkin lym-
phoma and NHL of the Lugano classification (Table 2) [17]:
• Anamnesis with special attention to the presence of B 
symptoms (fever, profuse night sweats, and loss of more 
than 10% of body weight the last 6 months) and signs 
suggestive of extralymphatic involvement, in addition to 
Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status 
(ECOG PS).
• Complete physical examination should be performed, 
focusing on peripheral lymph node regions, Waldeyer’s 
ring, and skin.
• Routine laboratory studies including complete blood 
count, electrolytes, kidney and liver function tests, those 
necessary to determine prognostic indices (serum lactate 
dehydrogenase [LDH] and β2-microglobulin), as well as 
serologies for hepatitis B and C, and human immunode-
ficiency virus (HIV).
• Positron emission tomography (PET) and computed 
tomography (CT) with iodinated contrast medium is the 
imaging test par excellence for staging aggressive lym-
phomas and will serve as a baseline reference for treat-
ment response.
• Since PET-CT can detect bone marrow involvement in 
DLBCL, bone marrow biopsy is not routinely recom-
mended [18].
• Cerebrospinal fluid cytology for patients at risk of central 
nervous system (CNS) involvement (high-risk Interna-
tional Prognostic Index [IPI], kidney or adrenal involve-
ment, or primary testicular or breast DLBCL).

For staging, the Lugano modification of the Ann Arbor 
classification system is used (Table 3). Note that the suffixes 
A and B are no longer applied in this staging system. There 
is no consensus regarding the cut-off point to define bulky 
disease, with thresholds ranging from 7.5 to 10 cm.
The IPI, with five variables (including stage), improves 
prognostic accuracy over stage alone (Table 4). Despite the 
original IPI [19] being conceived in the pre-rituximab era, 
more recent studies have validated this score for patients 
treated with chemotherapy plus rituximab. For those treated 
with CHOP or CHOP-like chemotherapy plus rituximab, 
3-year overall survival (OS) for low, low-intermediate, high-
intermediate, and high-risk groups was 91%, 81%, 65%, and 
59%, respectively [20].
Patients with limited-stage DLBCL have a markedly bet-
ter prognosis, and the IPI has been modified for this sub-
group (see below) [21, 22].
Treatment of early‑stage DLBCL
Limited-stage DLBCL is commonly defined as Ann Arbor 
stages I and II, usually anatomically located in a potential 
radiation field. Standard treatment consists of chemoim-
munotherapy with R-CHOP, alone or in combination with 
involved-site radiation therapy (ISRT; Table 2, Fig. 1).
Radiotherapy alone is not an acceptable treatment for lim-
ited-stage DLBCL, with the exception of a selected group of 
patients with stage I disease and comorbidities, in whom the 
use of chemotherapy may be contraindicated.
To select the most appropriate treatment, patients 
with limited-stage DLBCL can be stratified based on the 
presence of adverse prognostic factors according to the 
stage-modified version of IPI (mIPI) [22]. This version 
includes the same prognostic factors described by Shipp 
and colleagues [19], except that for limited-stage disease, 
stage II is considered an adverse risk factor instead of 
stages III and IV (Table  5):
• Stage II.
• Serum LDH above normal limits.
• Age > 60 years.
• ECOG PS ≥ 2.
Moreover, the presence or absence of bulky disease 
(defined as a tumor >7.5–10 cm) must be taken into 
account.
Patients with stage I DLBCL without any adverse prog-
nostic factors have a 5-year median OS of >90%, and those 
with stages I and II and some adverse prognostic factors 
have a 5-year OS of 70% [22]. However, in bulky dis-
ease, OS decreases to 50% at 5 years (similar to advanced 
stages) [22].
For practical purposes, early stages of DLBCL can be 
divided into three groups:
• Group I: 0–1 adverse prognostic factors, non-bulky.
• Group II: ≥2 adverse prognostic factors, non-bulky.

For group I patients, treatment with 4 cycles of R-CHOP 
is usually enough. There are two trials that endorse this strat-
egy. The phase III, non-inferiority FLYER trial included 592 
patients aged ≤60 years with stage I–II DLBCL without 
adverse prognostic factors. Four cycles of R-CHOP followed 
by 2 doses of rituximab alone were compared with 6 cycles 
of R-CHOP. No differences were found in terms of 5-year 
OS, progression-free survival (PFS), or event-free survival 
(EFS); toxicity was lower with 4 cycles of R-CHOP [23]. In 
the LYSA trial, 334 patients without bulky disease received 
4 cycles of R-CHOP every 14 days (R-CHOP-14) if they 
had mIPI of 0 and 6 cycles if they had mIPI of ≥1, with or 
without 40 Gy ISRT. No differences were detected in EFS or 
OS and, similarly, no benefit was derived from the addition 
of radiotherapy [24].
In group II, two options are considered acceptable, 
depending on whether a risk-adapted therapeutic approach 
based on interim PET imaging is used. If such an approach 
is not used, both 3 cycles of R-CHOP followed by radiation 
therapy (30 Gy ISRT) and 6 cycles of R-CHOP are suitable 
treatment alternatives [21, 25]. Although no head-to-head 
studies have compared risk-adapted therapy versus conven-
tional treatment in any stage of DLBCL [26], some authors 
have proposed the following recommendations:
• If PET-negative after 3 cycles of R-CHOP, complete

Treatment of advanced‑stage DLBCL
Approximately 70% of patients have advanced-stage disease 
at diagnosis. The standard of care in first-line therapy of 
advanced-stage DLBCL remains R-CHOP every 21 days 
(R-CHOP-21; Table  2). This is based on the results of the 
phase III study by the GELA group, in which 8 cycles of 
R-CHOP in patients >60 years of age significantly improved 
survival compared with 8 cycles of CHOP [30]. This sur -
vival benefit has been upheld in subsequent trial updates: 
5-year OS for patients treated with CHOP and R-CHOP 
was 45% and 58%, respectively (p  = 0.0073) [31]. In the 
MiNT trial, individuals aged <60 years were randomized to 
receive either 6 cycles of CHOP-like therapy or 6 cycles of 
R-CHOP-like therapy; 6-year EFS was 55.8% versus 74.3% 
(p < 0.0001) [28].
The RICOVER-60 trial demonstrated that increasing 
dose density, by administering R-CHOP-14, significantly 
improved survival compared with CHOP-14 [32]. Sub-
sequently, the two large, randomized trials comparing 
R-CHOP-21 with R-CHOP-14 revealed no survival benefit 
[33, 34].
Several studies have evaluated whether increasing the 
dose intensity of chemotherapy can improve outcomes with 
R-CHOP, and results have been mixed. In a randomized 
study conducted in France in patients aged 18–59 years 
with untreated DLBCL and an age-adjusted IPI score (aaIPI) 
of 1, a dose-intensive regimen of rituximab, doxorubicin, 
cyclophosphamide, vindesine, bleomycin, and prednisone 
(R-ACVBP), with subsequent consolidation using metho-
trexate, rituximab–ifosfamide–etoposide, and cytarabine, 
significantly improved survival outcomes compared with 
those who received R-CHOP. The 3-year OS rate was 92% 
in the R-ACVBP group versus 84% in the R-CHOP group 
(p = 0.0071), but this benefit came at the expense of much 
greater toxicity [35]. In a phase II study, patients with 
high-risk large B-cell lymphoma (IPI 3–5, aaIPI 2–3) were 
treated with dose-adjusted etoposide, prednisone, vincris -
tine, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and rituximab (DA-
EPOCH-R), achieving 10-year EFS and OS rates of 47.8% 
and 63.6%, respectively [36]. However, in the randomized 
ALLIANCE/CALGB50303 trial that compared R-CHOP 
with DA-EPOCH-R, the latter was associated with greater 
toxic effects and no benefit in PFS or OS [37].
Another strategy in first-line treatment of advanced-stage 
DLBCL is to change the type of monoclonal antibody (mAb) 
associated with CHOP. The randomized, phase III GOYA 
study compared R-CHOP with obinutuzumab-CHOP and 
detected no differences in PFS [38]. Polatuzumab is an 
anti-CD79b antibody conjugated to monomethyl aurista-
tin, an anti-tubulin agent. In the randomized, phase III 
POLARIX clinical trial, 879 treatment-naive patients with 
intermediate-risk or high-risk DLBCL were randomized to 
receive 6 cycles of either R-CHOP or polatuzumab vedotin, 
rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and prednisone 
(R-CHP) [39]. Two-year PFS was 76.7% in the experimental 
arm and 70.2% in the control arm (hazard ratio [HR] 0.73; 
95% confidence interval [CI] 0.57–0.95; p = 0.02). OS was 
similar in both arms, although more patients in the control 
arm required salvage treatment. Toxicity was similar, and 
a potential benefit in the activated B-cell-like subtype was 
suggested in the subgroup analysis (HR 0.4), although these 
results must be viewed with caution as patients were not 
stratified by this characteristic.
Various randomized trials have evaluated the addition of 
new agents to R-CHOP, including bortezomib [40], ibrutinib 
[41], venetoclax [42], and lenalidomide [43]. However, none 
demonstrated statistically significant differences. Similarly, 
several phase III trials have failed to demonstrate a survival 
benefit of post-R-CHOP maintenance therapy, with agents 
such as rituximab [44], enzastaurin [45], everolimus [46], 
or lenalidomide [47].
Treatment of relapsed or refractory DLBCL
Unfortunately, 10–15% of patients do not respond to ini -
tial treatment (primary refractory disease) and 20–30% will 
relapse after achieving a complete response (CR).
Salvage regimens are recommended with rituximab and 
chemotherapy, to be followed by high-dose chemotherapy 
(HDC) and autologous hematopoietic cell transplantation 
(HCT) in patients who respond (Table  2, Fig.  2). The use 
of rituximab, dexamethasone, cytarabine, and cisplatin 
(R-DHAP) or rituximab, ifosfamide, carboplatin, and etopo-
side (R-ICE) combinations as salvage regimens appears to 
result in similar outcomes [48]. However, in the LY.12 trial, 
the combination of rituximab, cisplatin, gemcitabine, and 
dexamethasone (R-GDP) demonstrated similar efficacy, but 
lower toxicity compared with R-DHAP [49]. For selected 
cases with documented CNS involvement, high-dose cyta-
rabine-based salvage regimens (rituximab, etoposide, meth-
ylprednisolone, cytarabine, cisplatin [R-ESHAP], R-DHAP) 
can be considered, but no studies have directly compared 
high-dose cytarabine-based salvage therapy with other inten-
sive regimens for relapsed/refractory DLBCL. Carmustine, 
etoposide, cytarabine, and melphalan (BEAM) are the most 
commonly used high-dose regimen, although no randomized 
studies have demonstrated its superiority over other condi-
tioning regimens. Response should be evaluated by fluoro-
deoxyglucose PET (FDG-PET) scan after 3–4 cycles of the

salvage regimen (before HDC) and after completion of all 
therapy.
Results have varied when chimeric antigen receptor 
T-cell (CAR-T) therapy was used as a second-line treatment 
in relapsed/refractory DLBCL and compared with standard 
treatment with salvage chemotherapy followed by HDC. 
In three published phase III studies conducted in patients 
with primary refractory DLBCL or DLBCL progressing 
within 12 months of first-line therapy [50– 52], axicabta-
gene ciloleucel (ZUMA-7 trial) and lisocabtagene maraleu-
cel (TRANSFORM trial) demonstrated superior outcomes 
compared with HDC, whereas no such benefit was observed 
with tisagenlecleucel (BELINDA trial). These results may 
be due to differences in time to CAR-T infusion (29 days 
with axicabtagene ciloleucel vs. 52 days with tisagenlecleu-
cel). In addition, the fact that bridging therapy was permitted 
in the trial of tisagenlecleucel may have led to the inclusion 
of patients with clinically more aggressive disease and worse 
prognosis.
Long-term follow-up of the ZUMA-7 and TRANSFORM 
trials has since confirmed the durability of CAR-T responses 
with axicabtagene ciloleucel and lisocabtagene maraleucel 
[53, 54]. In addition, real-world studies have validated the 
efficacy of CAR-T in early relapse outside of clinical trial 
settings [55, 56]. In recognition of these findings, both the 
European Medicines Agency (EMA) and US Food and Drug 
Administration (FDA) have extended approval of CAR-T 
therapies to the second-line setting for patients who relapse 
within 12 months of initial therapy [57, 58]. Accordingly, 
these updated guidelines recommend CAR-T therapy as a 
second-line option for eligible patients with early relapsed 
or refractory DLBCL.
Patients who are not candidates for high-dose therapy and 
autologous HCT may be treated with the aforementioned 
salvage regimens or others, such as rituximab, gemcitabine, 
and oxaliplatin (R-GEMOX). Nevertheless, they should pref-
erably be enrolled in clinical trials. Newer treatment options 
include combinations of anti-CD20 and anti-CD79b mAbs, 
such as polatuzumab vedotin, rituximab, and bendamustine, 
as well as combinations of anti-CD19 mAbs, such as tafasi-
tamab plus lenalidomide. These therapies have shown prom-
ising activity, even in patients whose disease was refractory 
to previous CD20-targeted immunochemotherapies [59, 60].

are refractory to ≥2 lines of therapy are also refractory or 
relapse following CAR-T therapy. In these cases, there is no 
optimal standard of care; therefore, it is advisable that they 
be enrolled in clinical trials. Preliminary data from ongo-
ing, early-phase clinical trials with bispecific anti-CD20/
CD3 mAb (mosunetuzumab, glofitamab, epcoritamab, and

CAR-T therapy. These findings have since been confirmed in 
pivotal phase II trials. Epcoritamab has demonstrated dura-
ble remissions in a substantial proportion of patients with 
poor prognostic features, with many responses maintained at 
2 years [64]. Mosunetuzumab has shown consistent activity 
across multiple studies, with durable responses in patients 
previously treated with CAR-T therapy [65– 67]. Odronex-
tamab has also demonstrated sustained clinical benefit and 
minimal residual disease clearance in a significant subset of 
patients, with a generally manageable toxicity profile [68]. 
Glofitamab, evaluated in a fixed-duration regimen following 
obinutuzumab pre-treatment, has shown high CR rates with 
durable remissions and an acceptable safety profile, includ-
ing among patients with prior CAR-T therapy [69]. At pre-
sent, and in view of the activity of new drugs (e.g., bispecific 
antibodies, polatuzumab vedotin, tafasitamab), allogeneic 
transplant is only an experimental alternative for selected 
cases after multiple relapses. Individuals who are not can-
didates for combined treatment can be treated with mono-
therapy agents (pixantrone, rituximab, gemcitabine, oxali-
platin, etc.) with poor response rates (<20%), albeit with 
some symptomatic benefit, or with palliative radiotherapy.
Follow‑up, long‑term implications, 
and survivorship
Patients who achieve CR after first-line treatment should 
undergo follow-up based primarily on history and clinical 
examination (Table 2). Periodic blood tests are also recom-
mended and may be helpful when recurrence is suspected 
and to detect possible late treatment toxicities. In asympto-
matic cases, routine imaging techniques, such as CT, have 
not been shown to be useful in terms of improving survival 
[70, 71], although many oncological and hematological 
teams perform them for the first 2–3 years.
In women who have received mediastinal radiotherapy, 
especially before the age of 25, a breast magnetic resonance 
imaging (MRI) scan can be considered from 8 to 10 years 
after treatment. Modern radiotherapy techniques using small 
fields (such as ISRT) appear to reduce the risk of radiation-
induced breast cancer. Smoking cessation counseling is 
important for all patients, especially those who have received 
radiation therapy to the head, neck, and chest.
Conclusions
These updated guidelines provide evidence-based recom-
mendations for the diagnosis, staging, treatment, and follow-
up of patients with DLBCL, incorporating the most recent 
clinical data and expert consensus available as of June 2025. 
While the standard of care remains largely unchanged in 
many areas, emerging therapies, including CAR-T treat-
ments, bispecific antibodies, and novel antibody–drug con-
jugates, are beginning to reshape the therapeutic landscape, 
particularly in relapsed or refractory settings. Continued 
integration of new evidence into clinical practice and access 
to innovative treatments through clinical trials remain criti-
cal to improving outcomes for patients with DLBCL.
