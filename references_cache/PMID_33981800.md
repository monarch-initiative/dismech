# Broadening the Clinical Spectrum of Very Low Density Lipoprotein Receptor Associated Dysequilibrium Syndrome.
**Authors:** Wali GM, Wali G
**Journal:** Mov Disord Clin Pract (2021)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mdc3.13184](https://doi.org/10.1002/mdc3.13184)

## Content

Dysequilibrium syndrome (DES) is an autosomal recessive genetically heterogeneous condition characterized clinically by congenital onset of non‐progressive cerebellar ataxia, disturbed equilibrium and mental retardation associated with cerebellar hypoplasia.
1
 Mutation in the very low density lipoprotein receptor (VLDLR) gene on chromosome 9p24 is a common cause of this syndrome. VLDLR is a member of the low‐density lipoprotein receptor gene family and part of the reelin signaling pathway. Reelin exerts several important functions in the brain including the regulation of neuronal migration, synaptogenesis, and synaptic plasticity.
2
 Reelin signaling pathway has been associated with several human brain disorders such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and autistic spectrum disorder.
3
 It is hypothesized that reelin pathway may be associated with VLDLR DES pathogenesis as well, but this needs to be evaluated. Approximately 50 genetically proven cases have been published to date. They belong to descendants of the Hutterite sect of North America and isolated families with Turkish, Iranian, Iraqi, and Caucasian background. Here, we report a novel frameshift VLDLR variant (NM_003383.3: c.658_743del p.[Asp220Trpfs*6]) in 10 members of a consanguineous Indian family. Clinical examination with video documentation, genetic analysis, and neuroimaging was performed for the affected cases.

Clinical phenotype characterization of the cases showed a wide range of unusual patterns of locomotion (refer to Table 1). These included habitual quadrupedal locomotion, adaptive quadrupedal locomotion, hand‐knee crawling, bottom shuffling, and switch pattern from 1 type to another (1, 2, 3, 4). Neuroimaging confirmed cerebellar hypoplasia and decrease in the cortical sulci in all cases where it was performed (Table 1 and Figure S1A,B). History of truncal hypotonia in infancy was reported in 6 cases belonging to the fourth generation (Table 1). Truncal hypotonia and tongue protrusion dystonia continue to be salient clinical features in one 5‐month‐old case (case IV‐5 in Table 1 and Figure S1C). Elements of focal dystonia were present in all cases (Table 1, Video 4, and Figure S1C). Hitherto undescribed feature of ocular telangiectasia was present in 5 cases (Table 1, Figure S1D), further broadening the clinical spectrum of VLDLR‐DES.

Clinical exome sequencing (CentoDX) by Centogene first detected the novel frameshift variant (c.658_743del p.[Asp220Trpfs*6]) in case III‐5, the index case (Figure S2). This variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and it is classified as likely pathogenic (class 2) according to the recommendations of Centogene and American College of Medical Genetics (ACGM) guidelines. In the other 9 family members, the variant was tested and confirmed by Sanger sequencing, Medgenome. In 9 of the 10 cases, the mutation was homozygous (Table 1) and heterozygous in 1 case (case III‐7 in Table 1 and Figure S2).

Certain clinical differences were noticeable between the previously described Hutterites and our cases. Although seizures were reported in almost 40% of the cases in the former, they were absent in the latter. Hutterites use ataxic bipedal gait for locomotion whereas patients from Turkey and India use habitual quadrupedal locomotion. This difference may be a consequence of homozygous large deletion of the chromosomal region containing the whole VLDLR gene in case of Hutterites leading to more severe disease with poor motor skills to attempt quadrupedal locomotion.
4
 However, this hypothesis warrants further investigation. Elements of focal dystonia were present in all the cases suggesting possible involvement of the cerebello–ganglionic–cortical motor circuitry in the pathogenesis.
5
 Truncal hypotonia was noticed in members of the 4th generation but not in the 3rd generation. This may be an outcome of increased homozygosity because of inbreeding. Polio diagnosis was suspected in case III‐7 based on history provided by the family (ie, flaccid paralysis at the age of 10 months) but this diagnosis remains unconfirmed. Case III‐7 is a heterozygous carrier of the pathogenic VLDLR variation c.658_743del detected here. Genotype–phenotype correlation in case III‐7 remains controversial. We hypothesized that both the presence of the reported mutation in the heterozygous state and possible polio outcomes might contribute to his phenotype. Presence of ocular telangiectasia in 5 cases is a novel finding not documented in previous reports of VLDLR gene mutation. The pathogenesis of this feature is unclear. Description of retinal neovascularization in mice with VLDLR mutation lends support to its relationship to this disorder.
6
