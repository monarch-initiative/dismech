# Disruption of the human cystin-1 myristoyl-electrostatic switch causes polycystic kidney disease that phenocopies autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease.
**Authors:** Yang C, Harafuji N, Watts JA, Tao B, Moran C, Clements J, Price K, Laucevicius A, Burrill N, Gebb J, Soni S, Oliver E, Savla JJ, Christ L, Moldenhauer J, Hartung EA, Didier R, Santani A, Sandford RN, Selkirk L, Radley JA, Mann K, Simonicova I, Karl R, Kariat Ashraf AP, Wachten D, Wilson L, Bebok Z, Caldovic L, Guay-Woodford LM
**Journal:** Kidney Int (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.kint.2026.01.023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.kint.2026.01.023)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease (ARPKD) is caused primarily by pathogenic variants in PKHD1, encoding fibrocystin/polyductin. In Cys1cpk/cpk (cpk) mice, the kidney and liver lesions closely phenocopy ARPKD. Cys1 encodes cystin, a myristoylated protein that traffics to the primary cilium and nucleus. We recently reported the first patient with ARPKD due to a homozygous CYS1 splicing variant.

METHODS: To study this, we utilized high-resolution ultrasound, array Comparative Genomic Hybridization and exon sequencing to identify an ARPKD-like kidney lesion and CYS1 defects in two families. Bioinformatic analyses compared cystin sequence conservation across mammals. Immunofluorescence, optogenetic studies, and 3D culture comparatively evaluated subcellular localization, ciliary trafficking, and cyst formation under cAMP stimulation of wild-type and mutant cystin. Tandem affinity purification/mass spectrometry identified cystin-binding partners. Serine-17 phosphorylation was evaluated functionally.

RESULTS: The Family 1 proband carried a homozygous deletion about 24-kb in size encompassing the CYS1 regulatory region and exon 1. In Family 2, two affected siblings had a homozygous CYS1 c.4G>A (p.Gly2Ser) variant affecting Gly2 within the N-terminal myristoylation motif. This region, together with an adjacent arginine-rich stretch flanked by serines-8 (S8) and -17 (S17) residues, comprises a conserved myristoyl-electrostatic switch that can regulate reversible protein-membrane binding. S17 phosphorylation modulates cystin membrane association and intracellular trafficking. In 3D culture, CRISPR/Cas9-generated cystin-null cells or null cells expressing cystin-1G2S showed enhanced cyst formation versus their respective controls. cAMP activation by cilia-specific optogenetic stimulation in cultured cells or pharmacologic treatment of ex vivo embryonic kidneys reduced cystin ciliary localization in a protein kinase A-dependent manner. Mass spectrometry identified the phosphatase PPM1A as a cystin-interacting partner, and pharmacologic inhibition reduced cystin S17 dephosphorylation.

CONCLUSIONS: Cystin-1 intracellular trafficking and function are regulated by a myristoyl-electrostatic switch mechanism. Cystic kidneys in three individuals with homozygous CYS1 pathogenic variants provide further support that cystin-1 deficiency causes polycystic kidney disease that phenocopies ARPKD.

Autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease (ARPKD; MIM #263200) is an inherited hepato-renal fibrocystic disorder with an estimated incidence of 1 in 26,500 live births1. In most patients, ARPKD is characterized by the cystic dilatation of renal collecting ducts, causing progressive functional decline and ultimately end stage kidney disease2, 3. In the liver, the ductal plate malformation gives rise to congenital hepatic fibrosis, which often leads to portal hypertension2, 3. The majority of cases are caused by pathogenic variants in the PKHD1 gene, which encodes the protein fibrocystin/polyductin (FPC)4, 5. Genetic defects in the DZIP1L and CYS1 genes also result in ARPKD in a small subset of patients6, 7.

The human CYS1 gene encodes cystin-1, a 158 amino acid protein. Mouse cystin is a 145 amino acid protein that is expressed in the ductal epithelia of the kidney and liver and localized in the primary cilia8. In the Cys1cpk/cpk (cpk) mouse, loss of cystin results in a renal cystic lesion that closely phenocopies human ARPKD9.

Mouse cystin is N-myristoylated at the Gly2, which is required for its membrane association and a ciliary targeting sequence is necessary for its ciliary localization8. In other N-myristoylated proteins, reversible plasma membrane association can be regulated through a myristoyl-electrostatic switch mechanism10. The myristoyl-electrostatic switch motif consists of a serine residue within a cluster of positively charged amino acids adjacent to the N-myristoylated Gly210. Positively charged amino acids promote electrostatic interactions between myristoylated proteins and negatively charged phospholipid heads of the membrane lipid bilayer. Within the switch motif, phosphorylation of the serine residue reduces the electrostatic interactions enabling membrane dissociation of N-myristoylated proteins and trafficking into other cellular compartments10.

Primary cilia are 2-4 μm microtubule-based structures that play a crucial role in vertebrate development, as well as in human genetic diseases11. In renal epithelial cells, primary cilia are located at the apical surface and extend into the fluid-filled lumen12. Defects in a wide range of ciliary proteins are associated with polycystic kidney disease (PKD) in humans and mice13-15.

Chronic stimulation of the ciliary cAMP signalosome can transform kidney epithelia from tubules into cysts16, an effect that may be mediated by one or more of three major downstream signaling pathways: protein kinase A (PKA), exchange proteins directly activated by cAMP (EPAC), and cyclic-nucleotide-gated (CNG) ion channels17. Of note, the expression of the PKA regulatory subunit 1b and the EPAC proteins is increased in Pkhd1-deficient (pck) rat cholangiocytes and activation of PKA and EPAC results in cysts growth in normal and PCK cholangiocytes18.

In the current study, we present two independent families with children affected by polycystic kidney disease with ARPKD-like features. The first proband carries a homozygous ~24-kb deletion that includes the CYS1 exon 1. In the second family, the affected siblings are homozygous for a CYS1 c.4G>A (p.Gly2Ser) variant, which disrupts the Gly2 myristoylation site of cystin-1. These new human data extend our previous findings that site-directed mutagenesis of Gly2 in mouse cystin abrogates its ciliary localization8 and suggest that myristoylation is necessary for its function. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms that determine how disruption of cystin membrane association and ciliary trafficking contribute to polycystic kidney disease with ARPKD-like features in patients with CYS1 deficiency, we employed bioinformatic, molecular, cellular, ex vivo as well as functional assays. Optogenetics determined that regulated phosphorylation/dephosphorylation of the putative cystin myristoyl-electrostatic switch directs its intracellular trafficking. These data provide new mechanistic insights regarding cystin intracellular trafficking and how cystin-1 deficiency contributes to disease pathogenesis.

While the cpk mouse recapitulates important clinical features of ARPKD, only one human case of the disease has been linked to variants in the CYS1 gene7.

Here, we present children from two additional families with polycystic kidney disease with ARPKD-like features and CYS1 defects. In Family #1, the proband was born at 40+1 weeks gestation to consanguineous Bangladeshi parents (Figure 1A) by caesarean section due to an abnormal cardiotocography. A routine antenatal scan had revealed enlarged, echogenic kidneys with normal amniotic fluid volume. No other anomalies were detected throughout the pregnancy. Ventilatory support for less than 24 hours was required at birth due to poor respiratory effort and high oxygen requirements. Postnatal sonography on day 1 of life confirmed the antenatal scan renal findings, revealing enlarged (right kidney 6.9 cm, left kidney 7.0 cm), echogenic kidneys with poor corticomedullary differentiation (Figure 1B, i). The liver was anatomically normal (Figure 1B, ii). An echocardiogram demonstrated mildly dysplastic heart valves and mild ventricular hypertrophy. Renal function was initially impaired but normalized by day 14, at which point the infant developed systemic hypertension that was medically managed. In view of the congenital renal and cardiac structural anomalies, array CGH was performed and revealed a homozygous ~24-kb copy number loss at 2p25. The loss, arr[GRCh38]2p25.1(10073370_10097737)x0, included the CYS1 regulatory region and exon 1 (Figure 1C). No other microdeletions were noted, and no additional genomic investigations have been performed on the affected child. The parents were each hemizygous for the deletion.

In Family #2, we evaluated two successive fetuses of a 28-year-old woman of Pakistani descent who was referred to the CHOP Richard D. Wood Jr. Center for Fetal Diagnosis and Treatment (CFDT). The family history was significant for consanguinity, as the patient and the father of the baby were first cousins (Figure 1D).

In sibling #1, a detailed high-resolution ultrasound and fetal echocardiogram performed at gestational age (GA) 22 weeks 6 days revealed a structurally normal heart and echogenic, enlarged kidneys with discrete cysts (Figure 1E, i and ii). A cycling urinary bladder was identified, and the amniotic fluid index was normal. However, at 29 weeks GA, severe oligohydramnios was noted, which rapidly progressed to complete anhydramnios a week later. Preterm labor occurred at 35 weeks 6 days. The fetus was delivered by cesarean section due to breech presentation and potential abdominal dystocia. Neonatal respiratory distress prompted immediate intubation and mechanical ventilation, which was complicated by bilateral pneumothoraces. The neonate expired on the first day of life due to complications of severe pulmonary hypoplasia.

The patient presented to the CFDT with her next pregnancy (sibling #2). A detailed high-resolution ultrasound and fetal echocardiogram performed at 20 weeks 6 days revealed enlarged, echogenic kidneys with multiple scattered tiny cysts (Figure 1E, iii and iv). In addition, there was mild right pelviectasis, a normal urinary bladder, and low-normal amniotic fluid. Fetal echocardiogram revealed hypoplastic left heart syndrome with mitral hypoplasia and aortic atresia in the fetus (data not shown). The pregnancy was electively terminated and an autopsy was declined.

No imaging abnormalities of the liver were evident in either case (Figure 1E, v and vi). While the ductal plate malformation is an invariant histopathological finding in patients with ARPKD and can be observed in a subset of patients with early onset ADPKD, sonographic imaging abnormalities associated with the ductal plate malformation are typically not evident until after birth19.

Genetic analysis in sibling #1 was performed following an amniocentesis using a single nucleotide polymorphism microarray and a cystic kidney disease gene panel (Hereditary Cystic Kidney Diseases Sequencing Panel with CNV Detection; Prevention Genetics, Marshfield, WI). The microarray identified normal dosage for a female fetus with multiple regions of homozygosity that in total combined to 234 Mb.

In sibling #2, an amniocentesis was performed prior to termination. Exome sequencing analysis with DNA from both affected fetuses and their parents (QUAD-ES), revealed a homozygous variant in CYS1 at c.4G>A, p.Gly2Ser (G2S) in both fetuses (Figure 1F), which is predicted to disrupt the highly conserved MGxxxSx N-myristoylation motif. This variant is absent from gnomAD, but was classified as a variant of unknown significance (VUS)20. The patient, her husband, and the couple’s 5-year-old daughter were shown to be carriers of a single c.4G>A CYS1 variant. In addition, the patient’s mother and the father of the pregnancy’s father, who are siblings, were also found to be unaffected carriers. No other causative variants were identified in >100 cystic kidney disease genes including PKHD1 and DZIP1L.

To evaluate the functional significance of the cystin-1G2S variant, we collected and analyzed 131 cystin protein sequences from mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibia, and fish species (Table S1). Alignment of 97 mammalian cystin sequences revealed conservation of the N-myristoyl transferase (NMT) recognition motif, MGxxxSx (Figure 2A, purple highlights with black box), and an arginine-rich stretch of amino acids flanked by S8 and S17 residues (Figure 2A, red highlights), consistent with a myristoyl-electrostatic switch. Two additional regions of conservation were identified in the multiple cystin sequence alignment (Figure 2A, blue highlights), which corresponded to predicted α-helical regions in the otherwise unstructured and poorly conserved sequences of human, mouse, and rat cystin proteins in the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database21, 22 (Figure S1).

We then used the NetPhos3.1 phosphorylation prediction tool23 and identified 16 serine residues in mouse cystin with predicted phosphorylation scores above the 0.5 threshold (Figure 2B and Table S2). Among these residues, S17 (Figure 2B, red oval) is located in the stretch of arginine residues adjacent to the NMT recognition site, had the highest predicted phosphorylation site score, and was predicted to be phosphorylated by PKA (Figure 2C). These data suggest that cystin membrane association could be regulated by a myristoyl-electrostatic switch mechanism through S17 phosphorylation/dephosphorylation.

To determine the role of S17 phosphorylation (pS17) in the regulation of mouse cystin membrane association, we generated a phospho-protein specific mouse monoclonal antibody against a 15 amino acid peptide harboring pS17 (mAb-pS17, Figure 2D, underlined). The specificity of mAb-pS17 was validated by immunoblotting ovalbumin conjugated with unphosphorylated and phosphorylated S17 peptides (Figure 2E). We then investigated the cell membrane localization of wild-type (WT) cystin and S17 mutants (S17A, S17D and S17K that alter the charge at S17) by generating mIMCD-3 cell lines that stably expressed either WT, S17A, S17D, or S17K mutant cystin proteins fused to green fluorescent protein (GFP) (cystinWT::GFP, cystinS17A::GFP, cystinS17D::GFP, cystinS17K::GFP, respectively) which allowed us to visualize differences in membrane association among WT and mutant S17 cystin proteins. Immunoblotting of total protein lysates from the four cell lines showed that mAb-pS17 recognized only cystinWT::GFP fusion protein while anti-GFP antibody detected all four fusion proteins. We did not observe protein expression differences among S17 mutant cystin::GFP compared to WT cells. (Figure 2F).

Confocal imaging revealed that all four GFP fusion proteins localized to primary cilia (Figure 3A). We then quantified the membrane association of the four fusion proteins using a cell fractionation assay followed by immunoblotting (Figure 3B). E-Cadherin, a membrane fraction marker, was used for the normalization of cystin::GFP band intensities (Figure 3B and C). The abundance of cystinWT::GFP, cystinS17A::GFP and cystinS17D::GFP in the membrane fraction were similar (Figure 3C), which could reflect the fusion protein overexpression and saturation of the system that regulates membrane association of N-myristoylated proteins. CystinS17K::GFP, with an extra positive charge in the putative electrostatic switch motif, was more abundant in the membrane than cystinWT::GFP (Figure 3C). These data suggest that increasing the positive charge of cystin S17 to mimic cystin dephosphorylation enhances the membrane association of the protein.

We then evaluated the intracellular localization of endogenous cystin-pS17 using immunofluorescence (IF) staining in mIMCD-3 cells and acetylated Tubulin as a cilia marker. Endogenous cystin-pS17 localized primarily in the nucleus and was excluded from the cilia (Figure 3D and Figure S2). Using cystin- (red) and pS17- (green) specific antibodies to compare the subcellular distribution of total cystin and cystin-pS17, we found that both antibodies stained cystin in the nucleus (Figure 3E, asterisks), while only the antibody against total cystin stained the cilia (Figure 3E left panels, arrowheads) and decorated on the membranes (Figure 3E right panels). Quantification of the cystin-pS17 intracellular distribution using fluorescent intensity ratios between cystin and cystin-pS17 in the cilia and nucleus confirmed that cystin-pS17 was markedly reduced in cilia (Figure 3F). These data are consistent with our hypothesis that phosphorylation of cystin at S17 results in its dissociation from the ciliary membrane.

We then examined the intracellular localization of human cystin-1WT and cystin-1G2S in stably transfected hTERT-immortalized human renal epithelial cells (hTERT-HRE; a heterogeneous population of renal epithelial cells) and mouse IMCD-3 cells (mIMCD-3; collecting-duct-derived). Immunoblotting confirmed expression of both proteins (Figure 4A). Confocal imaging showed that cysin-1WT mainly localized to the cytoplasm and plasma membrane (Figure 4B and C), while cysin-1G2S primarily localized to nucleus (Figure 4B and C). Subcellular fractionation followed by immunoblotting confirmed nuclear localization of cysin-1G2S (Figure S3A). IF staining revealed both WT mouse cystin and human cystin-1 localize to cilia while cystinG2A or cystin-1G2S were excluded from the cilium (Figure 4D, E and Figure S3B, C, D).

We evaluated the N-terminal myristoylation of human cystin-1 using click chemistry-based metabolic labeling and observed that cystin-1WT but not cystin-1G2S is myristoylated (Figure S3E, arrow). These data are consistent with our previous demonstration that mouse cystin is N-myristoylated8 and demonstrate that the N-myristoylation motif in both human cystin-1 and mouse cystin is necessary for their cilia and membrane association.

To assess the role of the Gly2Ser substitution on cyst formation in vitro, we used CRISPR/Cas9 homology-directed repair (HDR) to generate Cys1 (c.4G>A) cell lines. Our initial effort was confounded by two factors: (i) the high G-C content in Cys1 exon 1 sequence flanking the target site, and (ii) the fact that most of the mIMCD-3 cells were triploid (based on karyotyping test done by WiCell Research Institute). As an alternative, we generated a series of mIMCD-3 cell clones with various Cys1 deletions, confirmed the genotype of each clone using Sanger sequencing (Figure 5A and Figure S4A and B) as well as evaluated cystin expression in each clone by IF and immunoblotting (Figure S4C). The C1 clone expressed two CRISPR-modified alleles (designated CRSP1 and CRSP2) and no WT allele. The CRSP1 allele contained a 2-bp deletion that disrupts the Kozak consensus sequence before the translation initiation codon (ATG, underlined), likely abolishing Cys1 expression24, 25. The CRSP2 allele has a 7-bp deletion that includes the ATG codon. IF (Figure 5B) and immunoblotting (Figure S4C) confirmed that the C1 cells did not express cystin, thus establishing C1 as a cystin-null cell line. Using this line, we generated C1 cells stably expressing either human cystin-1WT or cystin-1G2S. IF staining showed that cystin-1WT localized to the cell and ciliary membrane, while cystin-1G2S mainly localized to cytoplasm and nucleus (Figure 5C).

We then used 3D cell culture to comparatively evaluate cAMP-stimulated cyst formation in the mIMCD-3, C1, cystin-1WT and cystin-1G2S cell lines. As previously described, mIMCD-3 cells form cysts in response to 10 μM forskolin treatment26. The same treatment induced more and larger cysts in the C1 (null) and cystin-1G2S cells as compared to their respective controls, mIMCD-3 and cystin-1WT cells (Figure 5D and E). These data demonstrate that either disruption of cystin-1 N-terminal myristoylation or complete loss of functional cystin increases the cystogenic potential in this in vitro system.

Signaling through the second messenger cAMP is mediated by three major kinase pathways: PKA, EPAC, and CNG17. We sought to determine which effector kinase pathway was involved in cystin phosphorylation. Previous studies have demonstrated association of cystin8 and PKA27 with lipid raft microdomains at the plasma membrane. Using antibodies recognizing the PKA regulatory subunit 1 (PKA-RI), we confirmed that PKA is lipid raft-associated (Figure 6A), while the presence of cystinWT::GFP in lipid rafts has been shown previously by the same method8. Using sequential co-immunofluorescence staining on both folded filter and en face images with anti-PKA-RI and anti-cystin antibodies, we demonstrated that PKA-RI and cystin colocalized in the cilia of mIMCD-3 cells (Figure 6B and Figure S5A and B).

In mIMCD-3 cells, cystin co-colocalized with acetylated Tubulin in primary cilia and was present in the cytoplasm and in the nucleus (Figure 6C, top). When we treated mIMCD-3 cells with 8-Br-cAMP to activate PKA, we observed a reduction in ciliary cystin localization (Figure 6C bottom and Figure S6A), exhibited by a reduction of the ratio of the ciliary versus cytoplasmic cystin labeling (Figure 6D).

We then employed optogenetic tools to analyze the role of cAMP-mediated phosphorylation of cystin in the primary cilium. Specifically, we evaluated the localization of cystin in mIMCD-3 cell lines expressing either mNphp3(1-201)-bPAC-mCherry (cilia-bPAC), which localizes photoactivated adenylyl cyclase (bPAC) to the primary cilium, or bPAC-mCherry (cyto-bPAC), which localizes bPAC to the cytoplasm16. We assessed the abundance of ciliary and cytoplasmic cystin at baseline (dark conditions) or following light stimulation, which selectively increases the cAMP concentration either in the cilium or the cytoplasm28. Light-stimulation of cilia-bPAC cells decreased the cystin labeling in the cilium, but not in the cytoplasm (Figure 7A, C, and D). In turn, the ratio of ciliary to cytoplasmic cystin decreased upon light stimulation in cilia-bPAC, but not in cyto-bPAC or WT control cells (Figure 7A, C, and D). We hypothesize that the ciliary cAMP-induced reduction in ciliary cystin reflects the cystin-pS17 fraction that traffics into the cytoplasm following release from the ciliary membrane. Indeed, we observed an increase in cytoplasmic cystin-pS17 upon increasing ciliary cAMP levels in cilia-bPAC cells, but not after increasing cytoplasmic cAMP levels in cyto-bPAC cells or after light illumination in control cells (Figure 7B, E). Of note, a similar tendency was also observed in the nuclear fraction (Figure 7E). To investigate whether cAMP-dependent activation of PKA activity in the cilium underlies the reduction of ciliary cystin levels, we specifically inhibited ciliary PKA activity using a PKA-inhibiting peptide localized to cilia (cilia-PKI)29. Indeed, the ciliary cAMP-induced reduction of ciliary cystin was abolished (Figure 7F). In addition, PKA-RI co-localized with cystin and trafficked out of the cilia when cAMP levels in the cilium increased (Figure 7G and 7H). Moreover, changes in cAMP levels did not result in changes of the total cystin-pS17 abundance (Figure S6B and C). These data strongly suggest that PKA phosphorylates cystin S17 in the cilium.

To verify whether ciliary cAMP signaling not only controls cystin localization in mIMCD-3 cells but also in the kidney tissue context, we employed ex vivo embryonic kidney cultures. Ex vivo stimulation of embryonic kidneys with 8-Br-cAMP resulted in profound cyst formation after 5 days in culture (Figure 8A), consistent with previously reported studies30. Immunofluorescent, whole-mount labeling of the tissue with antibodies against ARL13B (cilia) and cystin demonstrated that cystin was localized in primary cilia of tubular epithelial cells under control conditions, which was reduced after stimulation with 8-Br-cAMP (Figure 8, B-D). Our data demonstrate that cAMP signaling determines the ciliary cystin localization in vitro and in vivo.

Given our data indicating that ciliary PKA regulates cystin release from the ciliary membrane, we were interested in identifying cytoplasmic/nuclear proteins that interact with cystin-pS17, as the first step in deciphering the pathways involved in the nuclear trafficking of this cystoprotein. Therefore, we applied TAP/mass spectrometry to mIMCD-3 cells expressing TAP-tagged cystinG2A (Figure S7A), a cystin variant that is functionally equivalent to cystin-pS17 in that both are excluded from the cilia8.

First, we verified that the TAP protocol was capable of purifying and retaining the cystin protein, while reducing the complexity of the initial lysate (Figure S7B, upper panel). SDS-PAGE was used to resolve proteins in samples from each step of the protocol and gels stained either with silver or SYPRO Ruby showed that TAP-tag purification markedly reduced the complexity of the sample in the final eluate. Immunoblotting (Figure S7B, lower panel) confirmed that cystinG2A enrichment through the TAP procedures. The putative cystin-interacting partners enriched in the final elution were identified by mass spectrometry.

Between 245 and 821 proteins co-purified with cystin in four independent TAP/mass spectrometry experiments (Figure S7C). False positives were identified by performing the TAP protocol with mIMCD-3 cells stably transfected with an empty TAP vector and removed from the dataset. Eighty proteins (including cystin) remained as potential cystin interacting partners after removal of common sticky proteins (http://www.dkfz.de/gpcf/251.html) from the dataset (Table S3).

Protein phosphatases 1A and 1B (PPM1A and PPM1B, respectively) were among 80 proteins that reproducibly co-purified with cystin. PPM1A and PPM1B phosphatases are Ser/Thr protein phosphatases31. We used co-immunoprecipitation of WT and cystinG2A with PPM1A to assess the interaction detected in TAP/mass spectroscopy experiments but found no evidence of protein-protein interaction (Figure S8), most likely due to lower sensitivity of immunoblotting. Therefore, we examined the functional interaction between PPM1A and cystin using sanguinarine, a specific PPM1A inhibitor32. Specifically, we evaluated whether PPM1A inhibition with sanguinarine results in increased levels of endogenous cystin-pS17 in mTERT-CCD cells33. Confluent mTERT-CCD cells were treated with increasing concentrations of sanguinarine. Levels of endogenous cystin-pS17 and total cystin were determined using immunoblotting with mAb-pS17 and anti-cystin antibodies. Changes in the PPM1A-regulated phosphorylation levels of p38, as well as pSrc-Y416 and pSrc-Y529 residues, were used as positive controls34, 35. Increased levels of cystin-pS17 (Figure 9A) were observed in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 9B and Tables S4 and S5), with the highest level at 30 μM sanguinarine. As expected, inhibition of PPM1A with sanguinarine resulted in the activation of Src, exhibited by increased phosphorylation of pSrc-Y416 and decreased phosphorylation of pSrc-Y529 (Figure 9A). Given that sanguinarine increased p38 phosphorylation, which can be associated with apoptosis36, we evaluated mTERT-CCD cells treated up to 30 μM sanguinarine for cleaved caspase-3 and found no detectable fragments, indicating that these cells were not apoptotic (Figure 9A). These data suggest that PP1MA phosphatase specifically dephosphorylates cystin-pS17 and that this step may be necessary for regulating cystin function following nuclear entry.

The pathogenesis of ARPKD remains largely undefined, despite the identification of several disease-causing genes. Based on recent cohort studies, 80% or more of patients with typical ARPKD have pathogenic variants in PKHD1 gene37-41. Variants in the DZIP1L gene account for less than 1% of affected patients6, while variants in other hepato-renal fibrocystic disease genes, e.g. HNF1B, PKD1, NPHP2, NPHP3, and NPHP13, can phenocopy ARPKD42.

Mice with a targeted disruption of Pkhd1 exhibit little or no kidney disease43-50. In the absence of a Pkhd1 mutant mouse model that accurately recapitulates the human disease phenotype, the cpk mouse, which lacks functional cystin, has been the most widely studied mouse model of ARPKD51, 52. We previously demonstrated that cystin is an N-myristoylated, lipid raft-associated protein with a unique cilia targeting motif and a functional nuclear localization signal8. Cystin forms a complex with necdin that downregulates the expression of Myc in mouse renal collecting duct epithelia53. We recently reported the first human patient with an ARPKD phenotype due to homozygosity for a deleterious splicing variant in CYS17.

In recent studies, we have shown a functional interaction between mouse cystin and FPC54. FPC levels were markedly (~80%) reduced in both cystin-deficient cpk mouse kidneys and renal epithelial cells while Pkhd1 mRNA levels were unchanged, indicating posttranslational FPC loss. The converse was not observed, that is in FPC deficient Pkhd1 mutants that do not express renal cysts, levels of Cys1 mRNA and cystin protein did not differ from wild-type controls. While this study does not definitively demonstrate that downregulation of FPC expression in cpk kidneys causally contributes to the cystic kidney phenotype, it is an intriguing consideration that merits further investigation.

The current study adds to the relevance of cystin deficiency for polycystic kidney disease with ARPKD-like features. We describe two independent families with children with renal imaging evidence consistent with ARPKD. The first proband carries a homozygous ~24-kb deletion that includes the CYS1 5’ regulatory region and exon 1. In the second family, the affected siblings are homozygous for a CYS1 c.4G>A (p.Gly2Ser) variant, which disrupts the Gly2 myristoylation site of cystin-1. This Gly2 residue is part of a highly conserved signature that includes the NMT recognition motif, MGxxxSx followed by a predicted myristoyl-electrostatic switch sequence. In our previous work, replacement of Gly2 residue with alanine in mouse cystin abrogated its ciliary localization8. In the current study, we show that the human Gly2Ser variant is also excluded from the cilium and localizes primarily in the nucleus. To establish the cystogenic potential of this variant, we used CRISPR/Cas9 to generate a cystin-null cell line from which we created stable lines expressing cystin-1WT and cystin-1G2S. Under cAMP stimulation in 3D culture, cystin-null and cystin-1G2S cells had enhanced cyst formation as compared to their respective control cell lines, demonstrating that N-myristoylation is required for cystin-1 function.

A related study provides further insight into the mechanistic impact of the cystin-1G2S substitution. Tandem affinity purification using WT cystin as bait identified the myristoyl-binding protein UNC119b as putative cystin-binding partner55, leading the investigators to propose a functional model in which UNC119b binds to myristoylated (myr) cystin in the cytoplasm and the UNC119b-myr-cystin complex is targeted to the cilium. In the process, the ciliary small GTPase ARL3 is activated by ARL13B, a guanine-nucleotide exchange factor. Once in the cilium, membrane-bound ARL3-GTP binds to UNC119b, releasing myr-cystin into lipid raft microdomains within the ciliary membrane.

Lipid rafts are highly dynamic membrane microdomains, serving as platforms for targeted protein traffic as well as cellular signaling processes56. In the current study, we examined whether the ciliary membrane association of myr-cystin is regulated by its predicted myristoyl-electrostatic switch. Using site-directed mutagenesis, complemented by immunostaining with a specific anti-pS17 antibody, we demonstrated that changes in the amino acid charge at the S17 position, either by phosphorylation or amino acid substitution, modulates the myr-cystin membrane association and its ciliary localization. In its phosphorylated state, S17 carries 3 negative charges. In contrast, the aspartate carboxyl group (S17D) has one negative charge. Therefore, the aspartate in place of phospho-serine mimics, but does not completely replicate, the biophysical properties of phospho-serine57.

These findings begged the question: does cAMP signaling modulate the cystin myristoyl-electrostatic switch and release myr-cystin from the ciliary membrane? Treatment with 8-Br-cAMP caused a reduction in cystin ciliary localization in mIMCD-3 cells and in 3D cultures from WT embryonic kidneys. To further examine cAMP signaling in specific subcellular compartments, we performed optogenetic studies with photoactivated adenylyl cyclase constructs that were specifically targeted to the cilia (cilia-bPAC) and cytoplasm (cyto-bPAC) and observed that light-stimulation of cilia-bPAC cells, which selectively increased ciliary cAMP, decreased the ciliary localization of cystin. Furthermore, ciliary-specific PKA inhibition abolished the loss of ciliary cystin localization. These data indicate that the ciliary cAMP signaling regulates cystin trafficking out of the cilia via PKA-dependent phosphorylation of cystin S17, which regulates the myristoyl-electrostatic switch.

Finally, we sought to identify the pathways involved in the cytoplasmic-nuclear trafficking of cystin. We applied TAP/mass spectrometry to cells expressing a cystinG2A variant that is functionally equivalent to cystin-pS17 in that it primarily localizes to the cytoplasm and nucleus. Given that the cystinG2A variant did not traffic to the cilium, as expected, PKA was not detected our TAP assays. In contrast, the protein phosphatases 1A and 1B (PPM1A and PPM1B, respectively) were among 80 proteins that were reproducibly co-purified with the cystinG2A construct. Previous immunofluorescence studies have localized PPM1A primarily to the nucleus with a small fraction in cytoplasm58. Using sanguinarine, a specific inhibitor for PPM1A, we demonstrated that PP1MA specifically dephosphorylates cystin-pS17. Interestingly, an unbiased phosphatome screen identified PPM1A as the physiological phosphatase for the Hippo pathway transcriptional co-activator, Yes-associated protein-1 (YAP)59.

Our previous data indicating that cystin is a transcriptional co-regulator of Myc53 and our current study identifying PPM1A and PPM1B and the 14-3-3 proteins (Table S3) in the cystinG2A interactome suggest parallels to the Hippo-signaling pathway60. Therefore, we propose an initial functional model for myr-cystin and the cystinG2A/G2S variants as depicted in Figure 10. In brief, myr-cystin localizes to the primary cilium. Activation of ciliary PKA by cAMP releases cystin from the cilial membrane via the myristoyl-electrostatic switch. Phosphorylated cystin translocates to the nucleus and is dephosphorylated by PPM1A. Based on our previous data, cystin binds to necdin and regulates Myc P1 activation53. Disruption of cystin N-myristoylation (G2A/G2S variants) abolishes cystin ciliary trafficking and lipid microdomain localization. We propose that cystin functions in the nucleus as a part of a regulatory complex that is initially assembled in ciliary lipid microdomains. This cystin-containing complex undergoes PKA-regulated release from cilia and traffics to the nucleus. A missense variant that disrupts cystin ciliary localization would be predicted to disrupt the assembly of this putative functional complex and nuclear trafficking of cystin alone would not have the appropriate transcriptional functions. Future experiments will test this hypothesis and also examine whether pathogenic CYS1 variants affect FPC expression in order to gain a fuller understanding of the mechanisms underlying ARPKD.

Taken together, our data strongly implicate the pathogenicity of the CYS1 c.4G>A, p.Gly2Ser variant. The missense change disrupts N-terminal myristoylation which impedes cystin-1 association with lipid raft microdomains and ciliary localization. Without ciliary processing, cystin likely does not associate appropriately within a putative transcriptional regulatory complex that binds to the P1 promoter and regulates MYC expression. The resulting dysregulated MYC expression would be a driver of renal cystogenesis.

Our study describes two independent families with children affected by polycystic kidney disease with ARPKD-like features. In the first, a microdeletion disrupts the CYS1 regulatory region and exon 1 and likely eliminates cystin-1 expression. In the second family, a missense variant abrogates cystin-1 N-myristoylation, disrupting the myristoyl-electrostatic switch that regulates its ciliary trafficking and membrane association. Cell lines in which this switch was disrupted exhibited enhanced cyst formation in 3D culture as compared to their respective control cell lines. Therefore, this report further supports CYS1 as a disease-causing gene for polycystic kidney disease with ARPKD-like features and provides the first mechanistic insights for disease pathogenesis. Confirmation of CYS1 as a new ARPKD locus will require histopathological confirmation of renal collecting duct dilatation and congenital hepatic fibrosis in patients with CYS1 deficiency.
