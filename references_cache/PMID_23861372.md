# Precocious puberty and Leydig cell hyperplasia in male mice with a gain of function mutation in the LH receptor gene.
**Authors:** McGee SR, Narayan P
**Journal:** Endocrinology (2013)
**DOI:** [10.1210/en.2012-2179](https://doi.org/10.1210/en.2012-2179)

## Content

The LH receptor (LHR) is critical for steroidogenesis and gametogenesis. Its essential role is underscored by the developmental and reproductive abnormalities that occur due to genetic mutations identified in the human LHR. In males, activating mutations are associated with precocious puberty and Leydig cell hyperplasia. To generate a mouse model for the human disease, we have introduced an aspartic acid to glycine mutation in amino acid residue 582 (D582G) of the mouse LHR gene corresponding to the most common D578G mutation found in boys with familial male-limited precocious puberty (FMPP). In transfected cells, mouse D582G mLHR exhibited constitutive activity with a 23-fold increase in basal cAMP levels compared with the wild-type receptor. A temporal study of male mice from 7 days to 24 weeks indicated that the knock-in mice with the mutated receptor (KiLHR(D582G)) exhibited precocious puberty with elevated testosterone levels as early as 7 days of age and through adulthood. Leydig cell-specific genes encoding LHR and several steroidogenic enzymes were up-regulated in KiLHR(D582G) testis. Leydig cell hyperplasia was detected at all ages, whereas Sertoli and germ cell development appeared normal. A novel finding from our studies, not previously reported in the FMPP cases, is that extensive hyperplasia is commonly found around the periphery of the testis. We further demonstrate that the hyperplasia is due to premature proliferation and precocious differentiation of adult Leydig cells in the KiLHR(D582G) testis. The KiLHR(D582G) mice provide a mouse model for FMPP, and we suggest that it is a useful model for studying pathologies associated with altered LHR signaling.

The LH receptor (LHR) is critical for mammalian male sexual development and reproductive function. In humans, LHR binds with high affinity to placental chorionic gonadotropin (CG) and pituitary LH in the Leydig cells of the testis. During fetal development, human CG (hCG) induces the differentiation of fetal Leydig cells (FLCs) and stimulates testosterone production, essential for male sexual differentiation (1, 2). In mice, proliferation and differentiation of FLCs does not require LH (3). The number of FLCs declines after birth due to degeneration, or they may persist and become outnumbered by a second population of Leydig cells, the adult Leydig cells (ALCs). These cells develop postnatally around day 7 in mice, and their proliferation and differentiation is highly dependent on LH (4). The spindle-shaped progenitor Leydig cells (PLCs) proliferate around 10–14 days, differentiate into immature and then to ALCs by day 56. Activation of LHR in ALCs by pituitary LH at puberty produces testosterone and is essential for male secondary sexual characteristics and spermatogenesis.

LHR is a member of the family of G protein-coupled receptors characterized by a large extracellular domain that is sufficient for hormone binding (5). LHR has 11 exons with the first 10 exons encoding the extracellular domain and exon 11 encoding a small portion of the extracellular domain, the transmembrane helices with the connecting extra- and intracellular loops and the cytoplasmic tail (5, 6). The primary signaling pathway mediated by LHR that is responsible for its function in Leydig cells is the Gαs/cAMP protein kinase A pathway leading to androgen production. LHR can also activate the inositol trisphosphate pathway (5), and recent studies have demonstrated that LHR activation of the ERK1/2 cascade modulates androgen synthesis as well as proliferation/survival of Leydig cells (7–11).

Naturally occurring mutations in human (h) LHR have been linked to various reproductive disorders. Severe inactivating mutations result in the failure of testicular Leydig cell differentiation and a disorder called Leydig cell hypoplasia that may be accompanied by male pseudohermaphroditism with female external genitalia. Milder mutations that permit partial LHR function result in micropenis and hypospadia (12, 13). Females with inactivating mutations exhibit amenorrhea and infertility. Activating or gain-of-function mutations in LHR appear limited to exon 11. Most of the mutations are clustered in transmembrane helix 6 and the third intracellular loop with aspartic acid at position 578 most commonly mutated to glycine (D578G) (2, 13). These mutations are heterozygous and inherited in an autosomal dominant male-limited pattern, because no phenotype is detected in female carriers. Therefore, boys with activating mutations present with familial male-limited precocious puberty (FMPP), Leydig cell hyperplasia and high testosterone levels in the context of prepubertal LH levels (13). Affected males generally show signs of puberty by age 4. Testicular biopsies in early studies of gonadotropin-independent sexual precocity showed that although Leydig cells exhibited cellular features of fully differentiated steroidogenic cells, they lacked Reinke crystals that are characteristic of ALCs but not present in FLCs (14–16). Later reports of patients with LHR-activating mutations did not determine whether the hyperplastic Leydig cells were of fetal or adult origin. The D578G mutation accounts for about 62% of all FMPP cases and 29% of all cases of sporadic cases of male-limited precocious puberty (17). Mutation of aspartic acid 578 to histidine (D578H) is a somatic mutation found in Leydig cells of boys with precocious puberty and Leydig cell adenomas (18–20), and this mutation has not been identified in boys with FMPP. When expressed in heterologous cells, these mutant receptors display varying levels of constitutive activity as measured by intracellular basal levels of cAMP and may or may not respond to additional stimulation with LH or hCG.

Inactivating LHR mutations have been modeled by LHR knockout mice. However, no genetic model of activating LHR mutations exist. Here, we describe the generation and characterization of knock-in mice expressing the D582G mutation (analogous to the D578G mutation in hLHR). We demonstrate that the knock-in LHR mouse (KiLHRD582G) is an accurate phenocopy of patients with FMPP. Our results with this model suggest that Leydig cell hyperplasia seen in FMPP patients is most likely due to the proliferation and premature differentiation of ALCs rather than proliferation or persistence of FLCs.

Mouse (m)LHR cDNA cloned in pcDNA3 was kindly provided by Dr Lutz Birnbaumer (National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences, Research Triangle Park, North Carolina). The aspartic acid residue at codon 582 was mutated to glycine (D582G). Two nucleotides were modified (GAC to GGA) to generate a unique Hinf1 restriction enzyme site. Mutagenesis was performed using the QuikChange Site-Directed Mutagenesis kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California). Mutant clones were identified by sequencing (Sequenase version 2.0 DNA sequencing kit; GE Healthcare, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania) and plasmid DNA purified using a QIAGEN kit (Valencia, California).

Transient transfection of HEK 293 cells, competitive binding, and cAMP assays were performed as described previously (21). Nonlinear regression analysis of the competitive binding data was performed to determine the receptor affinity (IC50, the concentration of unlabeled hCG that displaces 50% of specifically bound labeled hCG) using the 1-site competitive binding model. To determine the signaling efficacy (EC50, concentration of hCG required to produce 50% of the maximal signaling response), the hCG-induced increases in cAMP were fitted to a sigmoidal dose-response equation. Prism 5 software program (GraphPad Software, Inc, San Diego, California) was used to perform both analyses. The experimental and mathematical description of hormone receptor interactions and their analyses has been described in detail (22, 23).

All animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Southern Illinois University. Male KiLHRD582G mice were bred with female B6129SF1/J hybrid mice (The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, Maine). Mice were maintained in a conventional colony with a 12-hour light, 12-hour dark cycle and fed a standard laboratory chow (Purina Labdiet 5008; Gray Summit, Missouri) and tap water ad libitum. For bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) incorporation assays, mice were injected ip with 150-mg/kg body weight BrdU 2 hours before killing. Mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation and blood immediately collected by cardiac puncture. Desired tissues were harvested, and serum was collected by centrifugation.

Starting at postnatal day 12, mice were examined daily for the detection of preputial separation. The first day that preputial separation was detected was recorded. Urine was collected from wild-type (WT) and KiLHRD582G mice at ages before and after the detection of preputial separation and frozen until ready to use. Samples were analyzed by 10% SDS-PAGE as described previously (24).

RNA isolation and cDNA synthesis were performed as described in Supplemental Materials and Methods, published on The Endocrine Society's Journals Online web site at http://endo.endojournals.org, except that the RNA was not treated with deoxyribonuclease. The primers for Q-PCR were designed such that 1 of the primers spanned an exon-exon junction ensuring amplification of cDNA and not genomic DNA. The sequence of the primers used is listed in Supplemental Table 1. Ribosomal protein S2 (Rps2) was used as the endogenous control because its levels did not change significantly in the testis at different ages. A template negative control and a calibrator sample, prepared by mixing equal amounts of cDNA from all the samples, were also analyzed. Real-time PCRs were performed in a 25-μL volume with 0.4μM of each primer and 1× GoTaq qPCR Master Mix (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin). Amplification was performed by initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 minutes followed by 40 cycles for 15 seconds at 95°C and 61°C annealing for 1 minute. The difference in the cycle threshold (CT) value between the gene of interest and Rps2 was defined as ΔCT. The difference in the ΔCT between the gene of interest and the calibrator was defined as ΔΔCT. The relative quantity was determined by 2−ΔΔCT (25).

Testicular extracts were prepared by homogenization in PBS and extraction with diethyl ether as described previously (26). Serum samples were not extracted. Testicular and serum testosterone levels were measured in duplicate in a single assay using an enzyme immunoassay kit (Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, Michigan) following the manufacturer's instructions. The reportable range of the assay was 3.9–500 pg/mL. The intraassay coefficient of variation was 19.1, 14.0, 9.9, 6.6, 6.0, 6.1, 4.6, and 44%, whereas the interassay coefficient of variation was 14.2, 10.7, 8.5, 7.5, 7.2, 4.7, 2.8, and 7.7% at 3.9, 7.8, 15.6, 31.3, 62.5, 125, 250, and 500 pg/mL, respectively. Serum LH and FSH levels were measured by the University of Virginia Center for Research in Reproduction Ligand Assay and Analysis Core (Charlottesville, Virginia). The reportable range for LH was 0.08–37.4 ng/mL and FSH was 1.8–65.4 ng/mL.

Testis were fixed in Bouin's fixative and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5μM) were stained with periodic acid Schiff-hematoxylin (PASH). Immunohistochemistry was performed by incubation of tissue sections with 10% normal goat serum overnight at 4°C followed by incubation for 1 hour at room temperature with 1:500 dilution of SOX9 antibody, 1:2000 dilution (for testis sections from 1- and 2-wk-old mice), or 1:5000 dilution (for sections from 12-wk-old mice) of estrogen sulfotransferase (EST) antibody. Antibody-antigen complex were visualized using the Vectastain Elite ABC-DAB kit (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, California) and counterstained with hematoxylin.

Double immunofluorescence was performed by blocking testis sections with 10% normal goat serum for 1 hour at room temperature followed by overnight incubation with 1:4000 dilution of 3β hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (3βHSD)1 antibody at 4°C. Sections were then incubated with a goat antirabbit biotinylated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 hour and Alexa Fluor 594 conjugated streptavidin (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, Pennsylvania) for 30 minutes at room temperature. Sections were blocked with mouse-on-mouse blocker (MOM kit; Vector Laboratories) for 3 hours at room temperature followed by incubation with 1:200 dilution of anti-BrdU antibody for 1 hour at room temperature. BrdU positive cells were visualized using Alexa Fluor 488 conjugated donkey antimouse secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch). Coverslips were mounted with HardSet containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Vector Laboratories). Primary antibodies were obtained from the following sources: SOX9 (AB5535; Millipore Corp, Billerica, Massachusetts), EST (generated by Wen-Chao Song, University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, and kindly provided by Mario Ascoli, University of Iowa, Iowa City, Iowa, with permission from Dr Song), 3βHSD1 (kindly provided by the late Anita Payne), and BrdU (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California).

One epididymis was trimmed of fat and placed in 500 μL of M2 medium (Sigma, St. Louis, Missouri) at room temperature and sperm released from the cauda and counted as described (11), except that a hemocytometer was used instead of a Makler chamber. The edges of the coverslip were sealed with mineral oil to prevent evaporation. The immotile sperm were counted first and again after incubation at 55°C for 15 minutes. The number of motile sperm was calculated by subtracting the immotile sperm before heating from the immotile sperm after heating.

Statistical analysis was performed using the Prism 5 software program (GraphPad Software, Inc). Outliers were identified with the Grubbs' test using the online GraphPad software (http://www.graphpad.com/quickcalcs/Grubbs1.cfm). Comparisons of genotype and age effects for each experimental endpoint were determined by 2-way ANOVA, and Fisher's post hoc test was used for comparisons between genotype at each age. Comparison of the age of detection of balanopreputial separation was determined by the Kaplan-Meier survival analysis using the SAS 9.1 software. Survival curves were compared by the log-rank χ2 test. P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

To determine whether the D582G mutation in mLHR was constitutively active, HEK 293 cells were transfected with WT or D582G expression vectors. Competitive binding assays (n = 2) showed that the binding affinity of D582G LHR (IC50, 60.2 ± 1.6 ng/mL) was similar to WT (IC50, 129.1 ± 1.8 ng/mL), although receptor density was 60% of WT levels (Figure 1A). The basal levels of cAMP in cells expressing D582G LHR was 66.7 ± 92 pmol/mL compared with 4.7 ± 0.7 pmol/mL in cells expressing WT LHR (n = 3) (Figure 1B). When normalized for receptor density, the basal level of cAMP in D582G transfected cells was 23-fold higher than that detected in WT cells indicating that the D582G LHR is constitutively active. Maximal cAMP (normalized for receptor density) and EC50 values (WT, 14.8 ± 0.7 ng/mL; D582LHR, 25.1 ± 1.7 ng/mL) were similar between WT and mutant receptors.

The targeting strategy used to introduce the D582G mutation in the exon 11 of mouse Lhcgr gene is shown in Supplemental Fig. 1A. Screening for homologous recombination in the ES cells was performed by Southern blotting using 5′ and 3′ probes located outside the homologous arms (positions and expected sizes of WT and targeted alleles are shown in Supplemental Figure 1A). Two correctly targeted clones, 8142 and 8144 (Supplemental Figure 1B), which were euploid, were injected into blastocysts and implanted into foster mothers. Twelve male chimeras were bred with ACT-FLPe transgenic mice expressing the FLP recombinase, and germ line transmission was obtained with one of them. This mouse was the founder for the KiLHRD582G line. The F1 progeny were genotyped by PCR for Lhcgr, the neomycin cassette, and FLP recombinase (Supplemental Figure 1C). The Lhcgr-specific primers hybridize to sequences flanking the mutated site to generate a 600-bp product. Lanes 1–4 represent an example of heterozygous mice (+/D582G) showing the presence of the neomycin cassette and absence of FLP recombinase sequence. Lanes 5–8 represent an example of heterozygous mice showing the absence of the neomycin cassette and presence of FLP recombinase sequence. Lanes 9–11 are an example of a WT mouse. The mutant allele was detected by digestion of the PCR product with Hinf1 at the mutation-specific site to generate 2 bands of 485 and 115 bp (lanes 2 and 6). Tissue-specific expression of the mutated allele was confirmed by RT-PCR using primers flanking the mutation site in exon 11 (Supplemental Figure 2A). The presence of the 485- and 115-bp bands after HinfI digestion indicated expression of the mutant allele. Expression of WT and mutant alleles was detected in the testis and at significantly lower levels in the brain. No expression was detected in the seminal vesicles, epididymis, prostate, pituitary, adrenal, or liver, although Gapdh mRNA was readily detected in these tissues demonstrating RNA integrity (Supplemental Figure 2B). The primers used in the RT-PCR should detect the major transcript of 2.6 kb that encodes the full-length receptor as well as additional transcripts resulting from utilization of alternate splice and polyadenylation sites (27–29). Lack of a PCR product in controls without reverse transcriptase indicated that the RNA was free of contaminating DNA (data not shown).

Pubertal development was determined by the age of balanopreputial separation and detection of mouse urinary proteins (MUPs) in the urine. Preputial separation is androgen dependent in rodents (30), and high levels of MUPs are excreted in the urine of male mice at the onset of puberty (24, 31, 32). Mice were examined daily starting at 12 days of age, and the average age of preputial separation in KiLHRD582G mice was 14.5 ± 0.2 days (n = 9) compared with 22.4 ± 0.3 days (n = 8) in WT mice. A Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed on the data, and comparison of the survival curves for WT and KiLHRD582G using the log-rank χ2 test showed that the age of preputial separation in KiLHRD582G mice was significantly earlier than in WT mice (P < .0001). MUPs were undetectable in KiLHRD582G mice at 10 days but clearly detectable at 15 days (Figure 2B). In contrast, WT mice did not excrete detectable levels of MUPs until 22–23 days. These data are consistent with precocious puberty in KiLHRD582G mice.

Examination of the reproductive tract indicated that KiLHRD582G mice showed larger seminal vesicles and prostate, indicating precocious development of the androgen-responsive organs (Figure 3A). Body and tissue weights increased during development from neonatal to adult animals (2-way ANOVA, P < .05). Body weights of WT and KiLHRD582G mice were similar except for a window between 4 and 8 weeks of age, when surprisingly, the weights of the KiLHRD582G mice were slightly, but significantly, lower (Figure 3B). Testes were significantly smaller at all ages, except at 7 days (Figure 3C). Seminal vesicle weights of KiLHRD582G mice were significantly greater from 3 to 24 weeks compared with WT mice, and prostate weights were greater at all ages (Figure 3, D and E). Epididymides weights were greater in 10-day-old to 3-week-old KiLHRD582G mice compared with WT mice but not in adult animals (Figure 3F). These data suggest elevated levels of testosterone in the KiLHRD582G mice, which and was confirmed by measuring testicular and serum testosterone levels. Serum levels of testosterone in some or all of the 10-day-old and 2- and 3-week-old WT mice were below the limits of detection of the assay (3.9 pg/mL), and the lowest detectable value of 3.9 pg/mL was assigned for these animals. Serum and testicular testosterone were significantly increased in KiLHRD582G compared with WT mice, at ages from 3 to 24 weeks (Figure 4, A and B). Testicular testosterone was also significantly increased in 7- and 10-day-old KiLHRD582G mice compared with the WT counterparts (Figure 4B). Serum levels of LH and FSH were suppressed in KiLHRD582G mice due to testosterone feedback and were below the detection limits of the assay at all ages (Figure 4, C and D).

Expression of genes involved in the steroidogenic pathway in Leydig cells, starting with Lhcgr, was examined using total testicular RNA (Figure 5). Steroid acute regulatory protein (Star), cytochrome P450 side-chain cleavage (Cyp11a1), P450 17α-hydroxylase/c17–20 lyase (Cyp17a1), and 3β hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type V1 (Hsd3b6) showed a temporal increase in expression corresponding to pubertal development (2-way ANOVA, P < .05). Lhcgr, Star, Cyp11a1, and Cyp17a1 were up-regulated at most ages in KiLHRD582G mice compared with the WT mice. Expression of Hsd3b6 was significantly lower in adult KiLHRD582G compared with WT mice. Expression of 5α-reductase 1 (Srd5a1) peaks at 3 weeks in WT mice, consistent with previous studies showing a peak of Srd5a1 expression and activity around puberty (33). However, compared with WT animals, levels of Hsd3b1 mRNA in KiLHRD582G mice were up-regulated at neonatal and prepubertal but not adult ages.

Examination of testicular sections stained with PASH showed Leydig cell hyperplasia in the KiLHRD582G mice as early as 7 days of age (Figure 6) and persisted through adulthood. The hyperplasia was patchy, and larger areas were most often seen at the periphery of the testis under the capsule rather than in the central area. Leydig cell hypertrophy was also observed in some but not all cells. The seminiferous tubules appeared normal, and spermatogenesis was complete. At 24 weeks of age, 1 out of 7 mice examined displayed extensive hyperplasia (Figure 6) with degeneration of the surrounding seminiferous tubules. The area of this Leydig cell mass is greater than that of the surrounding seminiferous tubules and, therefore, may be classified as an adenoma based on the criteria established in rodents (36, 37).

To examine Sertoli cell development and function, testis sections were stained with the Sertoli cell-specific marker, SOX9 (Figure 7A). SOX9 staining was similar between WT and KiLHRD582G mice when Sertoli cells are proliferating (1 and 2 wk) or differentiated (3 and 12 wk). Expression levels of the FSH receptor (Fshr), another Sertoli cell marker, was also examined (Figure 7B), and the temporal pattern of Fshr expression was similar between the 2 genotypes. The KiLHRD582G mice are fertile, because the line is maintained by breeding the males. Although we have not performed a detailed breeding study, we found that the KiLHRD582G mice became progressively infertile and were unable to produce litter after an average age of 5–6 months. Analysis of total and motile sperm from the cauda epididymis from 24-week-old mice did not show a significant difference in sperm count or motility (Figure 7C), indicating that sperm loss was not the reason for the infertility. Additionally, about 25% of the mice that were mated did not produce litter, and plugs were usually not detected in these matings.

To determine whether the Leydig cell hyperplasia seen in the neonatal mice was due to precocious differentiation of ALC or persistence of FLC, we examined the expression of FLC markers, patched (Ptch1) and thrombospondin (Thbs2) and the ALC marker estrogen sulfotransferase (EST) (Sult1e1) (33). Q-PCR showed that expression of Ptch1 significantly decreased (2-way ANOVA, P < .05) during postnatal development, but there was no significant difference between the WT and KiLHRD582G mice. The Thbs2 RNA levels also decreased postnatally in WT and KiLHRD582G mice (2-way ANOVA, P < .0.05), and levels in KiLHRD582G mice are significantly lower at 2 weeks of age (Figure 8A). Expression of Sult1e1 was very low at 7-day-old, 10-day-old, and 2-week-old WT mice (CT values > 30) but was detectable in KiLHRD582G mice. RNA levels increased during development (2-way ANOVA, P < .05) and were expressed as significantly higher levels in KiLHRD582G mice than in WT mice at 6 and 12 weeks of age (Figure 8A). This result was confirmed by examining EST protein expression by immunohistochemistry (Figure 8B). The interstitial cells in the KiLHRD582G mice clearly stained with the EST antibody at 1 and 2 weeks of age. No staining was detected in the WT mouse testis. At 12 weeks, the interstitial cells of both WT and KiLHRD582G mice stained, although the intensity of staining is much greater in the KiLHRD582G mice demonstrating both increased expression of EST and increased numbers of ALC. Together, these data provide strong evidence for precocious development of ALC in the KiLHRD582G testis. To determine whether precocious proliferation of Leydig cells could be detected in the KiLHRD582G mice, 7-day-old mice were injected with BrdU, and testis sections were stained with antibodies against BrdU and the Leydig cell marker, 3βHSD1 (Figure 8C). Proliferating (BrdU positive) cells were present within the tubules but not in the interstitium of WT mice. No colocalization of BrdU and 3βHSD1 staining was detected in WT mice. In contrast, BrdU positive cells were present in both the tubules and interstitial cells of KiLHRD582G mice, and BrdU/3βHSD1 colocalization showed a few proliferating Leydig cells. These proliferating cells appear to be ALCs, rather than PLCs, based on their location within the interstitium and EST positive staining (seen in Figure 8B).

In this study, we present the first mouse model (KiLHRD582G) for FMPP that confirms that the constitutively active D578G mutation in hLHR causes this disorder in boys. Similar to humans with this mutation, KiLHRD582G mice exhibit precocious puberty, Leydig cell hyperplasia, and high testosterone in the context of prepubertal levels of LH. Two new findings from our study, not previously noted or confirmed in the FMPP cases, are that the extent of Leydig cell hyperplasia is not uniform throughout the testis and that the hyperplasia is due to the precocious development and perhaps continued proliferation of ALCs. Compared with the hLHR mutation, which results in a 3- to 5-fold increase in basal levels of cAMP (38, 39), the corresponding D582G mutation in mLHR is more potent, eliciting a 23-fold increase in basal cAMP in cell culture. This level of constitutive activity is similar to 28-fold increase exhibited by the somatic D578H mutation found in Leydig cell adenomas, but unlike the D578H LHR, the human D578G and the mouse D582G LHR can be further stimulated by hCG (19, 38, 39).

Expression of Lhcgr has been reported in a variety of extragonadal tissues, including epididymis, seminal vesicles, prostate, pituitary, adrenal, and brain (40, 41). With the exception of the brain, we did not detect Lhcgr expression in any of these tissues. Therefore, the enlarged seminal vesicles, prostate, and epididymis in the KiLHRD582G mice is due to androgen excess rather than any direct effect of LHR activity in these tissues.

The WT and mutant Lhcgr alleles are expressed at high levels in the testis, and phenotype of the KiLHR male mice suggests that the effects of the mutation are specific for LHR and Leydig cells, because FSHR expression and Sertoli cell development and function are qualitatively normal. An early study that examined testicular changes by electron microscopy in 7 patients between 3 and 5 years of age with gonadotropin-independent familial male precocity found early differentiation of Sertoli cells and formation of tight junctional complexes preceding the onset of spermatogenesis (14). Premature onset of spermatogenesis with all stages of germ cells was observed in the testicular biopsies. However, degenerating germ cells and spermatid abnormalities were also found. Although KiLHRD582G mice exhibit precocious puberty, progression of spermatogenesis was not advanced. This is probably due to the fact that the time (14 d) before preputial separation in the KiLHRD582G mice is shorter than the first seminiferous cycle of 35 days. In contrast, humans have a prepubertal period of about 10 years with a seminiferous cycle of about 64 days (42). A recent study has shown that premature expression of the androgen receptor (AR) in Sertoli cells of mice can accelerate Sertoli cell maturation and spermatogenic development (43). All stages of spermatogenesis appear qualitatively normal in testicular sections of KiLHRD582G mice, in spite of reduced testis size most likely due to nondetectable levels of FSH. These results are consistent with the testicular phenotype seen in FSHβ and FSH receptor knockout mice as well as FSH and FSHR transgenic mice in the hypogonadal background (hpg mice), which suggest that FSH is required for Sertoli cell proliferation and quantitative spermatogenesis but is not obligatory for fertility in mice (44–47). We have not performed a quantitative or detailed analysis of Sertoli cells or testicular germ cells. The normal levels of total and motile epididymal sperm seen in 24-week-old KiLHRD582G mice suggest that germ cell development is quantitatively normal and cannot explain the progressive infertility observed in KiLHRD582G mice. Perhaps abnormal function of the accessory sex organs (seminal vesicles and prostate) and/or lower urinary tract plays a role (48). Mice with AR inactivation in the prostate, seminal vesicles, and epididymis have quantitatively normal sperm but exhibit in vivo subfertility (49), whereas mice with AR inactivation in the proximal epididymis caused obstructive azoospermia (50). It is unlikely that AR function is impaired in KiLHRD582G mice, because the weights of the accessory glands in these mice are increased compared with WT animals. However, a detailed histological examination of the accessory glands in the KiLHRD582G mice needs to be performed.

In FMPP patients, Leydig cell hyperplasia is apparent at the time of evaluation for precocious puberty. However, as noted earlier, it is unclear whether these Leydig cells are of fetal or adult origin (14–16). KiLHRD582G mice exhibit Leydig cell hyperplasia at 7 days of age, the earliest time point examined and before external signs of precocious puberty (14 d). Using the KiLHRD582G mouse model, we now show that although FLCs are still present at this age, positive staining with EST and up-regulation of Sult1e1 mRNA indicates that the hyperplasia is a consequence of precocious ALC development. Our BrdU/3βHSD colocalization experiments indicate persistent proliferation of some of the ALCs. This was surprising, because ALCs represent the differentiated population and PLCs are proliferative (4). Presumably the PLCs have completed proliferation by 7 days in KiLHRD582G mice. Additional studies are required to determine the mechanism of the accelerated proliferation and development of Leydig cells in the KiLHRD582G mice. A novel finding from our mouse model, not previously reported in the FMPP case studies, is the prevalence of large areas of hyperplasia around the periphery rather than homogenous distribution throughout the testis. Additionally, our studies reveal Leydig cell hypertrophy in some of the cells of the hyperplastic interstitium.

It is interesting that detectable levels of serum and testicular testosterone can be measured in neonatal WT mice at 7 days of age, when FLCs are present but ALCs cannot be detected. In humans, testosterone synthesis by Leydig cells of the fetal testis is required for sexual differentiation and is dependent on placental hCG (1, 2). In contrast, studies in hpg mice lacking gonadotropins and mice with deletion of the common α-subunit (Cga) or Lhcgr genes suggest that testosterone synthesis by FLCs is gonadotropin independent (51–54). It has been suggested that androstenedione is the major androgen produced by the FLCs in the neonatal testis and is converted to testosterone by 17βHSD3 expressed in the tubules, and its levels decline before puberty and increase again in the ALC (55, 56). A recent study has also shown that FLCs can synthesize only androstenedione, because they do not express Hsd17b3 and fetal Sertoli cells convert androstenedione to testosterone, because they express Hsd17b3 (57). Consistent with these studies, the decline in testosterone to nondetectable levels in 10-day-old to 3-week-old WT mice is accompanied by a decrease in Hsd17b3 expression as well as diminished FLC numbers as determined by decreased expression of the FLC markers, Ptch1 and Thbs2. At 3–6 weeks of age, the increase in testosterone is accompanied by increased expression of Hsd17b3, the ALC marker Sult1e1, as well as the steroidogenic enzymes. The exception is Hsd3b1, which is not regulated by LHR and is expressed constitutively in Leydig cells at all ages (34, 35). A similar decrease in neonatal testosterone levels is seen in KiLHRD582G mice between 7 days and 2 weeks, although hormone levels are elevated compared with the WT mice. The elevated testosterone levels in 7-day-old to 2-week-old neonatal KiLHRD582G mice could be due to constitutive LHR activity in FLC, increased FLC number, or precocious development of ALC. Similar expression levels between WT and KiLHRD582G mice of the FLC marker, Ptch1, suggests a parallel decline in FLC numbers between the genotypes during postnatal development. Expression of the second FLC marker, Thbs2, also declines during development in both genotypes. However, the levels are significantly lower in 2-week-old KiLHRD582G mice compared with WT, and this is likely due to the negative regulation of Thbs2 mRNA by LHR activation (58) rather than an accelerated decline in FLC in KiLHRD582G mice. The detection of EST protein and Sult1e1 mRNA in the neonatal KiLHRD582G testis and up-regulation of several steroidogenic enzymes (Star, Cyp11a1, and Cyp17a1) as well as Lhcgr support the conclusion that precocious development of ALC is the major contributor to the increased testosterone in the neonatal ages.

Testosterone levels remain elevated in KiLHRD582G compared with WT mice through puberty and adulthood. KiLHRD582G mice are heterozygous and LH can activate the WT receptor and further activate the mutant receptor at puberty. However, because LH levels are greatly suppressed in the KiLHRD582G mice, it is likely that the high testosterone levels are mediated by the constitutive activation of the mutant receptor rather than additional activation of the WT or mutant receptors. Expression of Star, Cyp11a1, Cyp17a1, and Hsd3b1 remains up-regulated in the adult KiLHRD582G testis. Because the KiLHRD582G mice exhibit Leydig cell hyperplasia, the increased RNA levels may be due to increased cell number without much change in expression or a combination of higher cell number and up-regulation.

The temporal expression of the steroidogenic enzyme genes, Hsd3b6, Hsd17b3, and Srd5a1, in WT mice is consistent with previous reports (33), although none of the genes were up-regulated in the KiLHRD582G testis. Studies in hpg mice suggest that expression of Hsd3b6 is directly dependent on by LH stimulation (35), and it was surprising that its expression was not up-regulated in the KiLHRD582G testis. Its temporal expression in WT mice suggests that it is a marker for ALC (33, 59). Testosterone negatively regulates expression of Hsd3b1 (60). However, it is not known whether testosterone produced as a result of LHR activation in KiLHRD582G mice can also down-regulate Hsd3b6 mRNA. Although testosterone levels are increased at all ages and ALCs are present as early as 7 days in KiLHRD582G mice, we do not see an increase in the mRNA levels of Hsd17b3. The only exception is at 3 weeks of age, when testosterone levels increase again in KiLHRD582G after the neonatal decline. Furthermore, mRNA levels are similar in adult WT and KiLHRD582G mice in spite of increased numbers of Leydig cells in the KiLHRD582G mice. Expression of Hsd17b3 was inhibited by a single dose of hCG in vivo, and this agonist-induced down-regulation resulted from changes at the transcriptional level (61). Although LH levels are low, constitutive LHR activity may explain the lack of up-regulation of Hd17b3 mRNA in the KiLHRD582G mice. Srd5a1 encodes steroid 5α-reductase 1, which converts testosterone to dihydrotestosterone and is the predominant form in the rodent testis (62). Expression levels of Srd5a1 in WT and KiLHRD582G mice are similar and peak around 3 weeks followed by a decline to a constant level in adult mice. This pattern of expression is consistent with previous reports (33, 63). We expected that Srd5a1 mRNA levels would be higher in KiLHRD582G mice because of increased numbers of Leydig cells. Previous studies have shown that testosterone negatively regulates Srd5a1 mRNA and enzyme activity and FSH positively regulates Srd5a1 mRNA (64). Therefore, the high testosterone and low FSH levels in KiLHRD582G mice likely down-regulate Srd5a1 mRNA levels and enzyme activity. This decrease in Srd5a1 mRNA could contribute partly to the increased levels of testosterone.

Previously, transgenic mouse models have been generated to mimic the LHR-activating mutation by precocious activation of the receptor. These include transgenic mice expressing a single chain hCG-lutropin receptor complex (yoked hormone receptor) under the control of the inhibin α-subunit promoter (65) and 2 models of ectopic hCG overexpression using the metallothionein (66) and ubiquitin promoters (48). However, none of these models were an accurate phenocopy of patients with FMPP. All models showed increase in testosterone but no evidence of precocious puberty. Leydig cell hyperplasia was not detected in yoked hormone receptor-expressing mice (65), presumably because the inhibin α-subunit promoter used to drive the transgene does not mimic the cell-specific and temporal expression of LHR. The metallothionein promoter-driven hCG overexpressing mice showed Leydig cell hyperplasia in adult but not neonatal animals (66). In the ubiquitin promoter-driven hCG overexpression mice, Leydig cell adenomas were found in prepubertal but not adult mice (48, 58), and it suggested that the adenomas were derived from FLCs. These models, in which hCG is overproduced in a number of tissues, did not mimic the more subtle functional changes brought about by genetic mutations.

Of all the activating LHR mutations that have been identified, only the D578H mutation has been associated with Leydig cell adenomas (18–20, 67). The adenomas were detected at the time of diagnosis of precocious puberty. The mutation is limited to the tumor tissue and cannot be detected in the adjacent normal tissue or blood leukocytes. Although the D582G mLHR is as potent as D578H hLHR in increasing the basal cAMP levels, we did not detect adenomas in the young KiLHRD582G mice. Studies comparing functional properties of D578H LHR with germ line LHR mutants associated with hyperplasia (D578Y and L457R) showed that all mutants activated the same G proteins and caused constitutive activation of cAMP, inositol phosphate, and ERK1/2 phosphorylation (68). A recent study (69) suggests that the high basal production of inositol phosphate by D578H compared with D578G LHR may be responsible for adenoma formation. There are very few reports of long-term follow up of FMPP patients to determine whether these patients develop tumors later in life. One patient with the D578G mutation who was diagnosed with precocious puberty at 27 months developed a seminoma at 35 years of age (70). A second FMPP patient with a D564G mutation was diagnosed with precocious puberty at 4 years of age and developed nodular hyperplasia at 10 years of age (71). Therefore, it will be of interest to determine whether KiLHRD582G mice develop tumors as they age beyond 6 months. Conversely, because the KiLHRD582G mice become progressively infertile, it is also of interest to determine whether boys with FMPP develop infertility/subfertility later in life and whether testosterone levels remain elevated above normal. One patient with precocious puberty at 3 years who was revaluated at 23 due to infertility has been reported (14, 72). Mice with premature AR activation and precocious testicular development have smaller testis and decreased postmeiotic germ cell development in the adult (43), suggesting that subfertility may be a long-term consequence of premature testosterone exposure.

In summary, KiLHRD582G mice exhibit precocious puberty, elevated testosterone, and Leydig cell hyperplasia similar to boys with FMPP. Our studies suggest that accelerated differentiation and continued proliferation of ALCs contributes to the hyperplasia. We propose that KiLHRD582G mice are a useful model to study signaling mechanisms leading to altered Leydig cell development and function and the consequence of premature and sustained increased production of testosterone on fertility.
