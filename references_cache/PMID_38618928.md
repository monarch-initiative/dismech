# Auriculocondylar syndrome 2 caused by a novel PLCB4 variant in a male Chinese neonate: A case report and review of the literature.

BACKGROUND: Auriculocondylar syndrome (ARCND) is a rare congenital craniofacial 
developmental malformation syndrome of the first and second pharyngeal arches 
with external ear malformation at the junction between the lobe and helix, 
micromaxillary malformation, and mandibular condylar hypoplasia. Four subtypes 
of ARCND have been described so far, that is, ARCND1 (OMIM # 602483), ARCND2 
(ARCND2A, OMIM # 614669; ARCND2B, OMIM # 620458), ARCND3 (OMIM # 615706), and 
ARCND4 (OMIM # 620457).
METHODS: This study reports a case of ARCND2 resulting from a novel pathogenic 
variant in the PLCB4 gene, and summarizes PLCB4 gene mutation sites and 
phenotypes of ARCND2.
RESULTS: The proband, a 5-day-old male neonate, was referred to our hospital for 
respiratory distress. Micrognathia, microstomia, distinctive question mark ears, 
as well as mandibular condyle hypoplasia were identified. Trio-based whole-exome 
sequencing identified a novel missense variant of NM_001377142.1:c.1928C>T 
(NP_001364071.1:p.Ser643Phe) in the PLCB4 gene, which was predicted to impair 
the local structural stability with a result that the protein function might be 
affected. From a review of the literature, only 36 patients with PLCB4 gene 
mutations were retrieved.
CONCLUSION: As with other studies examining familial cases of ARCND2, incomplete 
penetrance and variable expressivity were observed within different families' 
heterozygous mutations in PLCB4 gene. Although, motor and intellectual 
development are in the normal range in the vast majority of patients with 
ARCND2, long-term follow-up and assessment are still required.

Auriculocondylar syndrome (ARCND) is an ultra‐rare disorder with a prevalence of under 1/10,00,000 (Orphanet; http://www.orpha.net/consor/cgi‐bin/index.php) which was first reported in 1978 (Uusp, 1978), also known as dysgnathia complex or question mark ear (QME) syndrome, belonging to a group of malformations arising from the developmental defects of the first and second pharyngeal arches (Passos‐Bueno et al., 2009). The core features of ARCND are micrognathia, mandibular condyle hypoplasia, and distinctive QME with a cleft or notching between the lower helix and the lobule. In addition, other less common features, such as microstomia, glossoptosis, postauricular tags, prominent cheeks, and facial asymmetry may manifest (Gordon, Vuillot, et al., 2013; Vegas et al., 2022).

Four subtypes of ARCND have been described so far, that is, ARCND 1 (ARCND1, OMIM # 602483) which is due to mutation in GNAI3 gene on chromosome 1p13.3, ARCND2 which is due to mutation in PLCB4 gene on chromosome 20p12.3‐p12.2, divided into two types based on different inheritance patterns: ARCND2A, autosomal‐dominant inheritance, OMIM # 614669; ARCND2B, autosomal‐recessive inheritance, OMIM # 620458, ARCND3 (OMIM # 615706) which is due to mutation in EDN1 gene on chromosome 6p24.1, and ARCND4 (OMIM # 620457) is due to heterozygosity for a tandem duplication within the HDAC9 gene on chromosome 7p21.1. (Gordon, Petit, et al., 2013; Kido et al., 2013; Leoni et al., 2016; Masotti et al., 2007; Rieder et al., 2012; Romanelli et al., 2015). The most commonly mutated gene in individuals with ARCND is PLCB4 gene (Li et al., 2023; Vegas et al., 2022) which was reported in only about 36 cases worldwide.

Here, we report a case of ARCND2A initially presenting with feeding difficulties and respiratory distress, which resulted from a mutation in the PLCB4 (OMIM # 600810, NM_001377142.1:c.1928C>T) gene, and the mutation has not been reported. PLCB4 gene mutations were also reviewed in order to summarize the molecular and phenotypic spectrum of the syndrome.

The clinical data of a patient with ARCND2A caused by PLCB4 gene mutation was retrospectively analyzed. His parents provided informed consent for the participant under the age of 18 years and agreed to disclose clinical, imaging, and genetic data. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Anhui Provincial Children's Hospital (EYLL‐2022‐036).

We report a case of ARCND2A in a 5‐day‐old male neonate. The patient is the third child of non‐consanguineous parents with an unremarkable family history. He was born at 39‐week gestation by spontaneous vaginal delivery with normal Apgar scores. The patient presented core phenotypes of ARCND, including decreased mouth opening, micrognathia, microstomia, prominent cheeks (Figure 1), glossoptosis, and postauricular tags.

Phenotypic presentation of the patient. (a) Anterior posterior view of the face showing prominent cheeks, microstomia, and micrognathia. (b and c) The notch within the helix, giving the appearance of question mark ears.

The patient suffered from respiratory distress within 4 days after birth, requiring referral to our hospital. Clinical examination showed normal heart and limb function, but poor feeding. His bloodwork, liver/kidney function, electrolytes, cardiomyopathy markers, and urine/stool analysis were unremarkable.

Computed tomography (CT) revealed a narrowed oropharynx airway and absent condylar fossae, shortened mandibular rami, and obtuse mandibular angles (Figure 2). Fibrobronchoscopy showed a sagging root tongue, enlarged lingual tubercle, and left bronchomalacia.

A 3D reconstruction of a computational tomography indicating micrognathia. Craniofacial 3D CT imaging demonstrated bilateral mandibular condyle hypoplasia, absence of condylar fossa, foreshortened vertical mandibular ramus, and obtuse mandibular angle, and obvious appearance of micrognathia was presented.

The patient underwent bilateral mandibular osteotomy and distraction osteogenesis at 17 days after birth. A second procedure at 16 months old improved mandibular growth. At 10 months old, his primary teeth erupted but were crowded with fragile enamel. At the age of 2 years, the patient had normal developmental milestones; however, his speech articulation difficulties persisted and required gesturing or pointing for communication. Hearing was normal in the patient.

Prior to conducting this study, ethical approval was obtained from the hospital's ethical committee, and the parents provided informed consent. This study employed trio whole‐exome sequencing (WES) to identify the molecular etiology of the child's medical condition. Peripheral blood was collected from the child and his parents into EDTA tubes, and leukocyte DNA was extracted using a genome extraction kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. The genome library was constructed, and the MiSeq sequencer (Illumina, USA) was used for high‐throughput sequencing. The resulting sequenced reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (NCBI Reference Sequence: NC_000020.10, GRCh37.p13 [GCF_000001405.25]) using the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner, and the initial comparison results of sequence alignment/map format were obtained, which were subsequently converted into binary alignment/map files using the SAMtools software package. The candidate variants were verified by Sanger sequencing using an ABI 3500 analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA).

To predict potentially harmful biological variants, various online analysis tools, including Provean, SIFT, Polyphen2, Mutationtaster, M‐Cap, and Revel, were utilized. Public single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) databases, such as the 1000Genomes Project, dbSNP databases, and Genome Aggregation Database, were searched for annotation of variants, and minor allele frequency (MAF) of each variant was annotated. The output was curated based on the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) Genetic Variation Classification Standards and Guidelines (Richards et al., 2008) rating for the variants. Additionally, three softwares, GERP++, PhyloP, and PhastCons, were used to conduct conservative analysis of the variant sites, respectively. Finally, we used the crystal structure of human PLCB4 (AF‐A0A7P0MRI8‐F1) from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database to predict the structures of the wild‐type (WT) and mutated PLCB4. The predicted structures of the WT and variant were visualized using the ChimeraX software.

We conducted a literature search of PubMed and the Human Gene Mutation Database for reports of ARCND with search terms “auriculocondylar syndrome” and “PLCB4”, from January 1978 to October 2023. Abstracts and full texts were screened to identify cases describing PLCB4‐associated disorders, and to summarize the phenotypic and genotypic spectrum.

The results of genetic testing showed that the PLCB4 gene in the patient contained a heterozygous variant (NM_001377142.1:c.1928C>T, NP_001364071.1:p.Ser643Phe), while this variant was not detected in the patient's parents (Figure 3), who had normal phenotypes. The variant was determined to be a de novo heterozygous mutation, in line with the inheritance pattern of autosomal‐dominant disease. Analysis softwares (Provean, SIFT, Polyphen2, Mutationtaster, M‐Cap, and Revel) predicted that the variant would affect protein structure (Table 1). The positive results of three conservation tools (GERP++, PhyloP, and PhastCons) are defined as follows: GERP++ >3 (Davydov et al., 2010), PhyloP >2.5 (Pollard et al., 2010), and PhastCons >0.6 (Siepel et al., 2005). Our scores of GERP++, PhyloP, and PhastCons were 6.04, 1.026, and 0.998, respectively. According to the above standards, two of the three conservation tools were positive, we identified that the PLCB4 gene mutation, c.1928C>T, detected in this study was species conserved. A search of the relevant public databases (1000Genomes Project, dbSNP databases, and Genome Aggregation Database) showed that the variant was not included. According to the ACMG guidelines, c.1928C>T was rated as having likely pathogenic, and the rating evidences were PS2 (Strong pathogenic evidence) + PM2 (moderate pathogenicity evidence) + PP3 (supporting pathogenic evidence).

The PLCB4 gene mutation c.1928C>T (p.Ser643Phe), detected in this study, was present in phosphatidylinositol‐specific phospholipase C, Y (PI‐PLC‐Y) domain (Figure 4). Hydrogen bonds play a significant role in maintaining the normal spatial conformation and stability of proteins. Ser643 naturally forms four hydrogen bonds with Arg640 and Thr665 in the PI‐PLC‐Y domain, as well as with Gly334 and Gln336 in the phosphatidylinositol‐specific phospholipase C, X (PI‐PLC‐X) domain. The p.Ser643Phe variant replaces a hydrophilic serine with a hydrophobic phenylalanine, thereby disrupting two hydrogen bonds with Gly334 and Gln336 in the PI‐PLC‐X domain. This suggests that the mutation may have contributed to an abnormal spatial structure and reduced stability, ultimately affecting catalytic activity.

From the review of the literature, 36 patients with PLCB4 gene mutations were retrieved, but only 32 cases were reported with complete clinical phenotype and genotype, and our report increases the number to 33 (Table 2).

In terms of appearance characteristics, the most common signs were QMEs in 97.0% (32/33), while only 7/33 patients had auricular tags, followed by micrognathia/retrognathia (27/33), prominent cheeks (16/33), and microstomia (15/33). Some patients had other rare signs such as crowded teeth (6/33), absence of facial hair (6/33), cleft palate (3/33), and macropenis (3/33). In addition, 14/33 patients were reported with mandibular and condylar dysplasia. In terms of functional characteristics, the most common symptoms were feeding difficulties (12/33), followed by speech articulation difficulties (11/33), which were often considered to be related to difficulty in temporomandibular joint activity, then sleep apnea (10/33), psychomotor developmental delay (6/33). Hypotonia, growth delay, and constipation were all only 3/33, while the rarer symptoms were hearing loss (2/33), intellectual disability (1/33), and autistic features (1/33).

Compared to patients with heterozygous missense mutations, patients with heterozygous compound alteration and homozygous alterations presented with a more severe phenotype, such as QMEs, auricular tags, micrognathia/retrognathia, prominent cheeks, feeding difficulties and sleep apnea, and additional atypical symptoms, such as macropenis and constipation (Table 2).

In 37 cases, 17 heterozygous missense mutations in the PLCB4 gene, inheriting in an autosomal‐dominant manner, have been found so far. All those point variants occurred in the highly conserved catalytical X and Y domains of phospholipase C beta 4 protein (Figure 4a). The hot spot mutation sites of heterozygous missense mutations of PLCB4 gene were c.1862G>A (11/32), followed by c.1861C>T (4/32), and all of these occurred in Y domain of phospholipase C beta 4 protein. However, five exceptional recessively inherited cases have been reported, one heterozygous compound alteration (c.854‐1G>A, c.1238+1G>C) and three homozygous alterations (c.624delG, c.1612‐279_2015+1546del4997, and c.1620dup) (Table 3).

ARCND is an extremely rare genetic disease, and is a congenital craniofacial developmental malformation syndrome of the first and second pharyngeal arches. ARCND2 caused by PLCB4 gene mutation has been reported in only about 36 cases in the world. In this study, we describe a 5‐day‐old male neonate from a Chinese family presenting with ARCND2 due to a PLCB4 gene variant, and summarize the molecular and phenotypic spectrum of the syndrome through reviewing the literatures about ARCND and PLCB4 gene.

The patient had the full‐blown picture of ARCND: the typical QME, micrognathia, microstomia, and prominent cheeks, feeding difficulty, glossoptosis, postauricular tags, respiratory distress, and crowded teeth. The craniofacial 3D CT imaging in our patient indicated that the patient had condylar hypoplasia, and temporomandibular joint anomalies. Trio‐based whole‐exome sequencing was performed on the child and his parents, and the data, we have obtained, pointed to the heterozygosity of the c.1928C>T (p.Ser643Phe) variant in the patient's PLCB4 gene, which was further confirmed through Sanger sequencing (Figure 3). Interestingly, the novel missense variant of c.1928C>T in the PLCB4 gene has not been reported.

We summarize clinical features and variants of patients with ARCND2 in this case and reported in the literature, and summarize the frequency of the major clinical features based on 33 ARCND2 cases (Table 2). As mentioned above, incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity were observed within different families' heterozygous mutations in PLCB4 gene. It should be emphasized that only one female who was heterozygous for the PLCB4 gene mutation c.1862G>A (p.Arg621His) had normal clinical appearance, all the other 32 people had QME. Moreover, five exceptional recessively inherited cases, presenting with more severe phenotypes and additional atypical symptoms, such as neurodevelopmental, genital, digestive, and skeletal anomalies (Gordon, Vuillot, et al., 2013; Kido et al., 2013; Leoni et al., 2016; Peart et al., 2022; Vegas et al., 2022), have been reported in the medical literatures. The good news is that motor and intellectual development are in the normal range in the vast majority of patients with ARCND2, as well as the patient in our study.

Our research indicate that the novel missense variant of c.1928C>T in the PLCB4 gene is species conserved and is predicted that the variant would affect protein structure (Table 1), which could cause an amino acid substitution of p.Ser643Phe in PI‐PLC‐Y domain, and the amino acid substitution is predicted to destroy the hydrophobic interaction between amino acids (Figure 4b). This suggests that the mutation may have contributed to an abnormal spatial structure and reduced stability, ultimately affecting catalytic activity. Our literature review indicate that 17 heterozygous missense mutations in the PLCB4 gene have been found so far. An interesting finding is that all those point variants occur in the highly conserved catalytical X and Y domains of phospholipase C beta 4 protein.

The requirement of EDNRA signaling in lower jaw development appears conserved in all jawed vertebrates, including humans (Clouthier et al., 2013; Pritchard et al., 2020), illustrating that it is a fundamental mechanism that drives facial development. PLCB4 is a direct signaling effector of the endothelin receptor type A (EDNRA)‐Gq/11 pathway (Ellis et al., 1998; Gresset et al., 2012), which establishes the identity of neural crest cells (NCCs) that form lower jaw and middle ear structure and contributes to craniofacial development through induction, migration, and maintenance of NCCs (Bonano et al., 2008). Missense mutations of PLCB4 gene were predicted to act via a dominant‐negative mechanism to interfere in EDNRA signaling pathway (Bukowska‐Olech et al., 2021; Staus et al., 2016; Vegas et al., 2022). It was supported by the latest research which showed that PLCB4 gene variants interfere with EDNRA‐Gq/11‐mediated activity of wild‐type PLCB in a partially dominant‐negative manner, with this interference blocked by disrupting the Gq/11‐PLCB4 binding interface (Kanai et al., 2022). It should be noted that previous studies have suggested that heterozygous myocyte enhancer factor 2C (MEF2C) is essential for the development of the craniofacial skeleton in mice and zebrafish (Miller et al., 2007; Verzi et al., 2007), and is a transcriptional target of endothelin signaling in cranial NCCs (Hu et al., 2015), that suggests that loss‐of‐function mutations of MEF2C should be considered as a possible cause of QME associated with severe intellectual deficiency and/or epilepsy (Gordon et al., 2018). Therefore, even if other developmental milestones are within the normal range, including motor and intellectual development, long‐term follow‐up and assessment of nervous system function for patients with ARCND are also worthwhile.

To conclude, we describe molecularly confirmed ARCND2 with a novel PLCB4 mutation and review the literature on PLCB4‐ARCND2 cases. ARCND exhibits marked phenotypic variability that impedes accurate clinical diagnosis; hence, genetic test is essential for diagnosis. Although motor and cognitive development approximates normal in the vast majority of ARCND2 patients, long‐term monitoring and reevaluation remain warranted. Expanding our knowledge of such rare genetic disorders is imperative.
