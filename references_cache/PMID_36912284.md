Fanconi anemia (FA) is a heritable malformation, bone marrow failure and cancer 
predisposition syndrome that confers an exceptionally high risk of squamous 
carcinomas. These carcinomas originate in epithelia lining the mouth, proximal 
esophagus, vulva and anus: their origins are not understood, and no effective 
ways have been identified to prevent or delay their appearance. Many 
FA-associated carcinomas are also therapeutically challenging: they may be 
multi-focal and stage-advanced at diagnosis, and most individuals with FA cannot 
tolerate standard-of-care systemic therapies such as DNA cross-linking drugs or 
ionizing radiation due to constitutional DNA damage hypersensitivity. We 
developed the Fanconi Anemia Cancer Cell Line Resource (FA-CCLR) to foster new 
work on the origins, treatment and prevention of FA-associated carcinomas. The 
FA-CCLR consists of Fanconi-isogenic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma 
(HNSCC) cell line pairs generated from five individuals with FA-associated 
HNSCC, and five individuals with sporadic HNSCC. Sporadic, isogenic HNSCC cell 
line pairs were generated in parallel with FA patient-derived isogenic cell line 
pairs to provide comparable experimental material to use to identify cell and 
molecular phenotypes driven by germline or somatic loss of Fanconi pathway 
function, and the subset of these FA-dependent phenotypes that can be modified, 
complemented or suppressed. All 10 FANC-isogenic cell line pairs are available 
to academic, non-profit and industry investigators via the "Fanconi Anemia 
Research Materials" Resource and Repository at Oregon Health & Sciences 
University, Portland OR.

Fanconi anemia (FA) is a heritable bone marrow failure, malformation and cancer predisposition syndrome that results from loss of function of any of the 23 functionally-linked Fanconi (FANC) genes 1,2. The cancer predisposition first recognized in association with FA was leukemia, often in conjunction with bone marrow failure. This risk of leukemia and bone marrow failure were substantially reduced by the development of highly effective bone marrow transplantation protocols. These protocols substantially changed the natural history of Fanconi anemia, and there are now more adults living with FA than newly diagnosed younger individuals.

An unanticipated consequence of the success in treating FA-associated bone marrow failure has been the emergence of an extraordinarily high lifetime risk of squamous cell carcinomas (SCC) of the epithelial lining the upper aerodigestive tract (oropharynx, neck and proximal esophagus), the anus and vulvar skin. Multiple oral cavity carcinomas are distressingly common in conjunction with an elevated risk of cutaneous squamous carcinomas with a persistent elevated risk of leukemia 3,4. SCC may be the first clinical presentation of FA at any age 1,5. Many other cancer types have been reported in individuals with FA, but only a small subset of rare cancers - liver and brain tumors, lymphomas, and embryonal tumors such as Wilms tumor and neuroblastoma - are observed at higher-than-expected frequencies versus population controls 3, though none with an elevated risk estimate approaching squamous mucosal carcinomas.

The origins of FA-associated cancers are not well-understood. The loss of function of any of the 23 genes and proteins that constitute the FA functional pathway promotes constitutional genomic instability while perturbing other cellular pathways that may potentiate cancer risk 2,4,6. Smoking and excessive alcohol consumption together with human papillomavirus (HPV) infection appear to be less prominent drivers of HNSCC risk in individuals with FA than in the general population though may contribute together with prior bone marrow transplantation to counter marrow failure 3,7. Effective therapies for FA-associated SCC remain elusive: many cancers are detected too late for surgical cure, and FA patients cannot tolerate effective doses of standard-of-care therapies including DNA cross-linking drugs and ionizing radiation 5. These clinical features have made SCC the leading cause of premature morbidity and mortality in adults with FA.

In order to foster new work on the origins, treatment and prevention of FA-associated SCC, we generated FANC-isogenic cell line pairs from HNSCC cell lines isolated from individuals with FA or with sporadic, non-FA-associated HNSCC. FA patient-derived cell lines were from individuals of FA complementation groups FA-A, FA-C and FA-L. Groups FA-A and FA-C encompass ~75% of FA patients, whereas the FA-L-associated FANCL gene is altered in <1% of FA patients. New isogenic sporadic HNSCC cell line pairs were generated in parallel to provide comparable experimental material by inactivating the dominant FANCA gene, followed by FANCA transgene complementation. The generation and distribution of the FANC-isogenic HNSCC cell line pairs to academic, non-profit and industry investigators has been generously supported by the Fanconi Anemia Research Fund, Eugene, OR.

Eleven existing FA patient-derived or sporadic HNSCC cell lines were used to develop the FA-CCLR. All were given systematic names following International Cell Line Authentication Committee (ICLAC) guidelines 8 for unambiguous identification, together with shortened abbreviations that we used to improve manuscript text, Figure and Table readability (Table 1). Cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) DNA fingerprinting and repeatedly verified to be free of Mycoplasma ssp. Infection or interspecies contaminants (CellCheck9 Plus, IDEXX BioAnalytics Westbrook, ME USA). The human osteosarcoma-derived cell line U-2 OS was purchased from ATCC and used as a FA+ control cell line for methods development. Cell line sources including any commercial suppliers are detailed in the Extended Methods section.

FA-patient derived OHSU-SCC-974 and sporadic cell line JHU-SCC-FaDu were grown in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Media (MEM) supplemented with 10% v/v FBS (Hyclone Laboratories) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco #10378016). Sporadic cell line SFCI-SCC-9 was grown in a 1:1 mixture of Dulbecco-Modified Eagle’s Medium (D-MEM) and Nutrient Mixture F12 supplemented with 10% FBS, 0.5 mM sodium pyruvate, 400 ng/mL hydrocorti-sone, and 1% penicillin- streptomycin. All other cell lines were grown in D-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% non-essential amino acids (Gibco #11140050) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin unless otherwise noted. Cell growth was at 37°C in a 5% CO2/ambient (20%) oxygen, water vapor-saturated incubator.

Prior reports and more recent, comprehensive genomic profiling data were assembled to identify cell line-specific single nucleotide (SNV), copy number (CNV) and indel (insertion-deletion) variants 9–14. Somatic SNV/indel and copy-number data for FA patient-derived cell lines OHSU-SCC-974, CCH-SCC-FA1 and CCH-SCC-FA2 were from Webster et al.
14. In brief, somatic SNV/indel calls were made using CaVEMAN 15 and Pindel 16 using tumor cell line and patient matched normal control whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data. Copy-number amplification and deletion in OHSU-SCC-974, CCH-SCC-FA1 and CCH-SCC-FA2 were called using CNVkit 17 to compare tumor cell line and patient-matched WGS data. SNV/indel and copy-number amplification and deletion were called for FA patient-derived cell lines VU-SCC-1604, VU-SCC-1365 and VU-SCC1131 using, respectively, Mutect2 18 and CNVkit to compare tumor cell line and patient-matched fibroblast whole-exome sequencing (WES) data 12. Our focus was on variant calling in significantly mutated genes considered to be HNSCC drivers 9,19,20. Somatic mutation data for sporadic HNSCC cell lines JHU-SCC-FaDu, CAL-SCC-27, CAL-SCC-33, SFCI-SCC-09 and UM-SCC-01 were extracted from the Broad Institute Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE)10, employing Mutect2 and Absolute 21 for SNV/indel and copy-number analyses. For all cell lines, the reporting threshold for copy-number amplifications and deletions was set at log2(CN)>0.5 or log2(CN)<−0.5 respectively.

FANCA-mutant sublines were generated from sporadic HNSCC cell lines by Cas9-mediated gene editing using dual guide RNA-targeted Cas9 deletion of a 5’ portion of FANCA, or single guide-targeted FANCA exon 4 cleavage to promote mutagenic end joining 22. Clonally-derived FANCA-mutant sublines were identified and characterized by deletion-specific colony PCR screening and/or PCR and Sanger sequencing prior to transgene complementation 22. FANC transgene complementations were performed by Cas9/Cas12a/CpfI-gRNA-targeted FANC transgene insertion into chromosome 4q Safe Harbor Site 231 (SHS231; 23), and/or lentiviral or retroviral transduction. Lentiviral transductions were performed at low multiplicities-of-infection using 3rd generation HIV-1-based FANCA, FANCC, FANCF or FANCL transgene vectors that had been CsCl gradient-purified and packaged according to Addgene-archived pLKO.1 protocols. Retroviral transduction of the FANCA transgene vector S11FAIN was performed as previously described 22. Chromosomal SHS231 safe harbor site transgene cassettes were electroporated into FANC-deficient cells with expression plasmids encoding SHS231-specific gRNAs and Cas9 or Cas12a/Cpf1 nuclease, then grown for 3 days prior to the addition of hygromycin B (400μg/mL; Millipore Sigma, Catalog #400050) or puromycin (1µg/ml, Invivogen) followed by an additional 10 – 14 days growth followed by dilution cloning and PCR screening to identify transgene-positive colonies.

Cell population doubling times were quantified in different growth media to identify potential metabolic constraints on proliferation as previously described 24. Cell cycle phase distributions were determined by 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI)/ethidium bromide staining of exponentially growing control or mitomycin-C-treated cultures, followed by DNA content profiling on a BD Biosciences LSR II Flow Cytometer using chicken erythrocytes as a DNA standard. Cell cycle phase fractions were extracted from these FACS data using FlowJo™ (FlowJo, Ashland, OR) as previously described 25.

Drug and small molecule dose-sensitivities were determined for the chemotherapeutic drugs mitomycin-C (MMC), cis-Pt, carboplatin, and oxaliplatin; for formaldehyde; for the kinase inhibitors gefitinib and afatinib; inhibitors of the ATR kinase (M6620, VX-970/VE822) and WEE1 (AZD-1775); and for metformin and rapamycin. All cell lines and derivatives were profiled for MMC sensitivity, a canonical FA function-dependent cellular phenotype, whereas additional exploratory analyses used in most instances two representative FA patient-derived and two sporadic isogenic cell line pairs.

In brief, triplicate wells containing 2,500 cells/well were seeded in 96-well plates in 150 µL of complete growth medium, followed by the addition of two- or three-fold serial dilutions of drugs or small molecules in 50 μl of medium, followed by 4 days of growth in the presence of drug/small molecule. Cell survival was then quantified versus a DMSO/vehicle control by WST-1 (Takara Bio, Catalog #MK400) or AlamarBlue (ThermoFisher, Catalog #DAL1025) assay. Data were normalized versus control (mock-treated/vehicle-only treated) wells, then plotted using GraphPad PRISM (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) to determine dose-dependent growth suppression and estimate drug/small molecule IC50 values.

Western blot analyses of FANC protein expression were performed using total cell protein extracts (~40 μg from ~1–2.5 × 106 cells) prepared in RIPA Lysis Buffer (Boster Biological Technology, Catalog #AR0105) containing protease inhibitors as previously described 26. Proteins were size-fractionated on precast 10 cm 4–12% Bis-Tris or Tris-acetate gels (Thermo Fisher Catalog #NP0336BOX), then electroblotted onto nitrocellulose membranes (Thermo Fisher Catalog #88025). Membranes were blocked in Tris-buffered saline solution containing 5% non-fat dry milk for 1 hr at room temperature or overnight at 4°C, then incubated with FANC protein-specific rabbit polyclonal or mouse monoclonal primary antibodies that were detected by horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG goat or anti-mouse antibodies. All transgenes apart from FANCA could also be detected by an anti-HA epitope tag-specific mouse monoclonal antibody, as each had an in-frame, N-terminal HA epitope tag. All antibodies, suppliers, dilutions and blot protocols are detailed in Extended Methods.

Peptide immunoenrichment coupled with targeted multiple reaction monitoring mass spectrometry (immuno-MRM) was used as a complementary approach to detect and quantify expression and modification status of 66 DNA damage response (DDR) proteins including 10 FANC proteins. In brief, triplicate 10 cm dishes were seeded with 2 – 5 × 106 cells prior to the addition of 0.2 µM mitomycin C for 24 hrs. Whole cell lysates were prepared and processed in a blinded fashion as previously described, using two antibody panels that targeted 126 peptides and 59 post-translational modification sites 27–29. MRM-MS data were analyzed using Skyline 30, with manual review of peak integrations to confirm and align peptide transitions of endogenous and stable mass isotope-labeled peptide standards. Means and standard deviations of peak area ratios were calculated from three complete biological replicates of cell line ± treatment, then combined and reported in a single list of analytes.

Three-dimensional (3D) ‘tumoroid’ cultures were grown in vitro from FANC-isogenic cell line pairs JHU-SCC-FaDu and VU-SCC-1131 by modifying a previously reported protocol 31. In brief, single cell suspensions were plated in Matrigel (Corning Life Sciences) onto plastic culture dishes to form 40µl ‘domes’ that each containing ~1000 cells. Plates were flooded with media once domes had solidified and grown for 7 days before being mechanically dissociated to allow further growth in suspension. MMC responsiveness of tumoroids was assessed using Day 40 tumoroids that were grown for 3 additional days in medium supplemented with 10, 50 or 100 nM MMC. Tumoroid samples were then fixed for paraffin embedding, H&E staining and immunostaining to detect cytokeratin 5 (CK5), the p40 fragment of p63 and the functional state markers Ki67, Ser139-phosphorylated histone H2AX (γ-H2AX) and cleaved caspase 3 (CC3).

Cell proliferation and dose-dependent survival analysis are displayed with mean ± the standard error of the mean (SEM) values. The statistical significance of differences in cell proliferation rate as a function of genotype was determined by one-way ANOVA. Cell cycle phase analyses were extracted from a minimum of 30,000 gated and flow-analyzed single cells/culture.

HNSCC isogenic cell line pairs generated as part of this project were expanded, re-authenticated by DNA STR fingerprinting and verified to be Mycoplasma-free prior to distribution via the Fanconi Anemia Research Fund-supported ‘Fanconi Anemia Research Materials (FARM) Resource’ (https://apps.ohsu.edu/research/fanconi-anemia/cell_line/). All of the cell line pairs are available to academic, non-profit and industry investigators upon approval of a materials transfer and use agreement with the Fanconi Anemia Research Fund. Additional data generated or used as part of this project that are not in the public domain can be requested from the corresponding author.

A systematic workflow was used to generate isogenic cell line pairs from FA patient-derived and sporadic HNSCC cell lines (Figure 1). The five FA patient-derived HNSCC cell lines were from FA patients with causative mutations in FANCA, FANCC or FANCL. Sporadic HNSCC cell line pairs were generated by inactivating FANCA followed by FANCA transgene complementation (Table 1). All cell line identities were confirmed by DNA fingerprinting, and verified to be free of cross-species contamination or Mycoplasma infection (Table S1).

Both FA patient-derived and sporadic HNSCC cell lines harbored genomic alterations that have been previously reported 7,9,12,13,32,33. Figure 2 summarizes cell line-specific single nucleotide (SNV) and copy number (CNV) variant data for 3 FA patient-derived and 5 sporadic HNSCC cell lines where full data access allowed a comparison with each other and HPV-negative HNSCC samples included in TCGA data (n = 426; 20). We had more limited data for three FA patient-derived cell lines though were able to call variants in the same genes identified as potential drives of HNSCC 19,20. TP53 variants were present in nearly all cell lines, together with different combinations of deleterious variants in significantly-mutated and/or putative HNSCC driver genes (Figures 2 and S1). Of note, none of the FA patient-derived cell lines contained identifiable HPV-derived DNA sequences. These results together with the recent observation that the vast majority of gene alterations in FA patient-derived HNSCC cell lines are gene deletion/amplification/rearrangement events provide a more complete picture of genetic alterations in FA patient-derived cancer cell lines 14.

Five independent sporadic HNSCC cell lines were used to generate FANCA-mutant and complemented isogenic cell line pairs to provide directly comparable experimental material for comparison with FA patient-derived isogenic cell lines. The initial FANCA-mutant sporadic sublines were generated by Cas9 dual guide RNA-targeted deletion of FANCA exon 2 and a portion of exon 3, or by Cas9 single guide RNA-targeted cleavage of FANCA exon 4 to promote mutagenic end-joining. FANCA mutant clones were verified by a combination of PCR deletion or mismatch cleavage screening, DNA sequencing and Western blot analysis, to confirm the presence of allelic mutations and the loss of FANCA protein expression (Figure 3A and 3B, 22).

FANC-complemented derivatives of FA patient-derived and newly-generated FANCA-mutant sporadic cell lines were generated by chromosome 4 safe harbor site SHS231 transgene insertion and/or lentiviral or retroviral transduction (Figure 3B–D). Viral complementation led to consistently higher transgene protein expression than did safe harbor site targeting, and was required to fully restore MMC resistance in FA patient-derived - but not newly generated sporadic, FANCA-mutant - HNSCC cell lines. Mutant/deleted and transgene-complemented cell line clones were comparable to parental cell lines or FANC transgene-complemented cell pools, indicating their functional equivalence as assessed by MMC dose-survival. Figure S2 provides an example, in which MMC dose-dependent survival of patient-derived FANCA-mutant OHSU-SCC-974 cells was compared with the patent cell line, 11 independent transgene-complemented clonal derivatives and a pool of lentiviral transgene-complemented cells. The functional equivalence of clones and pools of transgene-complemented FANC-deficient cells led to the decision to generate and distribute complemented pools of cells with clonal complexities of ≥200 as the most versatile starting point for end users. We were unable to generate a fully complemented isogenic cell line pair from the FA patient-derived cell line CCH-SCC-FA2. None of the protocols we tried - sequential or simultaneous safe harbor site insertion or viral transduction of FANCA and FANCF transgenes - fully restored MMC resistance. For this reason we chose not to include CCH-SCC-FA2 along with the 5 FA patient- derived cell lines that were successfully completed for distribution (Table 1).

Key FANC gene- and pathway activity-dependent phenotypes were characterized in isogenic cell line pairs to establish causal relationships between FANC genotype, FANC protein and canonical FA-associated cellular phenotypes. The phenotypes we investigated were cell proliferation rate; FANC transgene expression; MMC dose-dependent cell killing and the induction of FANCD2 monoubiquitylation detected by Western blot and/or mass spectrometry; and MMC induction of a late S/G2 cell cycle arrest.

A proliferative advantage of FANC-proficient cells has long been recognized in specific cell lineages, and this observation provided the strong rationale for bone marrow or hematopoietic stem cell therapy to prevent FA-associated bone marrow failure 34. Although we observed clear cell line- and growth medium-specific proliferative differences among FANC-isogenic cell line pairs, these differences did not clearly segregate with FANC genotype. These results parallel observations reported by Errazquin et al. for the FANCA-mutant CAL-SCC-27 and CAL-SCC-33 isogenic cells included in this Resource 22. Figure 4A provides a representative example of the proliferation of two FANC-isogenic patient-derived cell line pairs in different growth media at 37°C in ambient (20%) oxygen. Proliferation rates varied by over 2-fold between cell lines and ranged from 0.47 – 1.2 doublings/day, with FA patient-derived cell line VU-SCC-1131 displaying the fastest and FA patient-derived cell line VU-SCC-1365 the slowest doubling rates. We did not observe genotype-specific proliferation rate differences between cells grown in ambient (20%) versus low (5%) oxygen (additional results not shown).

In contrast to cell proliferation, FANC genotype modified MMC sensitivity in a highly reproducible and specific way in all isogenic cell line pairs, though again we observed substantial cell line-specific differences (Table S2). FA patient-derived cell lines were predictably MMC-sensitized, with MMC IC50 values of 2.8 – 13.4 nM (mean 6.5 nM), ~ 4-fold lower than FA-proficient sporadic HNSCC cell lines (range 4.4 – 36.8 nM, mean 22.9 nM). FANC transgene complementation of FA patient-derived cell lines conferred MMC resistance, with 3-to-10-fold higher MMC IC50 values. In contrast, FANCA deletion in sporadic HNSCC cell lines sensitized cells to MMC, with 14-to-44-fold lower MMC IC50 values (Table S2).

In addition to MMC sensitivity, FA-deficient cells display a reproducible MMC-induced late S/G2 cell cycle arrest. These two cellular phenotypes are so consistently robust that they remain cornerstones in FA diagnosis and mechanistic analyses 1,2,6,35. FA patient-derived cell lines displayed strong MMC dose-dependent increases in late S/G2 cell cycle fractions after treatment with 10 nM or 40 nM MMC. This arrest phenotype, most easily quantified in flow cytometric profiles as an elevated G2/M fraction, ranged from 16.4% (range 13.1% - 25.2%) in untreated cells, up to 41.3% (range 23.6 – 65.7%) in cells treated with 40 nM MMC. FANC transgene complementation had little effect on G2/M fractions in untreated cells, though substantially reduced G2/M fractions in MMC-treated cells. Comparable results were observed by Errazquin et al. who used diepoxybutane (DEB) as opposed to MMC to treat FANCA-mutant derivatives of CAL-SCC-27 and CAL-SCC-33 cells 22.

A third FA-dependent phenotype induced by MMC treatment or other types of DNA damages is the monoubiquitylation of FANCD2, a key regulatory post-translational modification and biomarker of FA pathway activity 2,6,36. This post-translational modification, mediated by the FA core complex protein and E3 ubiquitin ligase FANCL, is lost in ~ 90% of FA patients. We used peptide immuno-enrichment coupled with targeted multiple reaction monitoring mass spectrometry (immuno-MRM) to detect and quantify FANCD2 expression and monoubiquitylation status, together with the abundance and post-translational modification status of peptides located in 65 additional DNA damage response proteins including 9 other FANC proteins 27–29.

We were able to quantify 98 of 129 potential target peptides by this approach in FA-proficient and deficient cell line pairs using experimentally-defined lower limits for quantification (LOQ)(Figure 5A–C, Table S3). FANCA protein was readily detected in parental JHU-SCC-FaDu and control U-2 OS cells, but not in FANCA-mutant isogenic cell line derivatives. FANCD2 protein expression was unchanged in FANCA-proficient or mutant cells (Figure 5B), whereas FANCD2 ubiquitylation, detected as a ubK561-modified peptide, was induced in parental - but lost in both FANCA-mutant - cell line backgrounds (Figure 5B). Our ability to detect FANCD2 ubiquitylation by immuno-MRM, though not consistently by Western blot, in nominally FANCA-proficient JHU-SCC-FaDu cells, may reflect in part homozygous FANCM nonsense mutations in these cells 37 that could blunt a MMC-induced DNA damage response without conferring a clear FA-deficient cellular phenotype.

Other functionally important DNA damage-dependent post-translational modifications could be detected following FANCA loss including ATRpT1989, H2AXpS139 (γH2AX) and CHEK1pS317, together with additional cell line-specific protein expression and/or modification differences that drove prominent sample clusters of MMC treatment-responsive peptides in both cell line backgrounds (Figure 5C, Table S3). These results demonstrate the ability of immuno-MRM, in contrast to Western blot analysis, to detect and quantify the expression and the DNA damage-dependent modification of many proteins in parallel after DNA damage or other perturbations.

We performed exploratory experiments using a subset of FA patient-derived and sporadic HNSCC isogenic cell line pairs to determine the ability of nine drugs and other small molecules to suppress HNSCC proliferation and viability in a dose- and FANC genotype-dependent manner. These additional agents were chosen based on their mechanism of action and prior reports of FANC genotype-dependent activity.

The loss of FA pathway activity did not consistently sensitize FA-deficient patient- or sporadic, FANCA-mutant HNSCC lines to formaldehyde dose-dependent cell killing over 4 days of continuous low dose exposure (Figure S4). This may reflect cell line-specific differences that collectively determine formaldehyde toxicity 38,39. In contrast, loss of FA pathway function sensitized cells regardless of origin to platinum-based chemotherapeutic drugs with cis-Pt displaying the strongest - and oxaliplatin the least – dose-dependent reductions in survival of FA-deficient cells. The dose-dependent sensitizations were most reliably suppressed by FANC transgenes in sporadic FANCA-mutant HNSCC cell lines, and in the FA patient-derived cell line VU-SCC-1365 (Figure S5A,B). Comparable cis-Pt results were observed in FANCA-mutant sporadic HNSCC cell lines SCC-CAL-27 and SCC-CAL-33 by Errazquin and colleagues 22.

The kinase inhibitors gefitinib and afatinib have been reported to modify the growth of FA-deficient HNSCC cell lines in culture and in xenograft experiments 40. These inhibitors gave equivocal results with growth suppression in two FA patient-derived and one sporadic FANCA-deficient HNSCC cell line, though little change in dose-dependent growth inhibition following FANC transgene complementation (Figure S5C,D). Similar results were observed for the WEE-1 kinase inhibitor AZD-1775 (Figure S5E), for the ATR inhibitor VE-822 and for metformin and rapamycin, two small molecules being explored to prevent, delay or treat bone marrow failure or head and neck cancer in FA (additional results not shown).

Many HNSCC cell lines can form mouse xenografts, and tumor tissue as well as some HNSCC cell lines have been used to generate HNSCC-derived organoid cultures 31,41,42. We used two isogenic HNSCC cell line pairs, from FA patient-derived VU-SCC-1131 and sporadic JHU-SCC-FaDu and modified a previously reported protocol 31 to generate three-dimensional (3D) FA-proficient and -deficient in vitro ‘tumoroids’. FANC-isogenic cell line pairs readily formed tumoroids after single cell seeding in Matrigel, followed by growth in suspension where tumoroid cell numbers and sizes loosely correlated with cell line proliferation rates, time in culture and the extent of central necrosis (Figure 6).

JHU-SCC-FaDu-derived tumoroids regardless of FANC genotype displayed diffuse squamous differentiation with strong cytokeratin 5 cytoplasmic (CK5) and p40 antigen nuclear staining (Figure 6B). Continuous cell proliferation and death were observed in tumoroids as robust Ki67 and cleaved caspase 3 (CC3) immunostaining (Figure 6C) up to 6 weeks in culture. Both parental and FANCA-deleted JHU-SCC-FaDu Day 40 tumoroids also displayed a DNA damage response after 3 days of continuous low dose MMC treatment with both CC3 and γH2AX immunostaining (Figure 6C). These experiments establish the feasibility of generating and using FANC isogenic tumoroid pairs to further explore 3D growth, gene expression and therapeutic responses as a function of FANC genotype.

Fanconi anemia confers a remarkably high risk of developing carcinomas in the squamous mucosal epithelium lining the upper aero-digestive tract (oropharynx and proximal esophagus), vulva and anus 3,43,44 and to a lesser extent cutaneous squamous carcinomas 4. A better understanding of this cancer risk has been hampered by the lack of tractable, well- characterized disease models. In order to address this challenge, we have generated ten new FANC-isogenic cell line pairs from existing FA patient-derived or sporadic HNSCC cell lines to foster new research on the origins, treatment, and prevention of FA-associated cancers.

FA patient-derived isogenic cell line pairs were developed for three FA complementation groups including FA-A and FA-C that encompass ~75% of all clinically-ascertained FA patients 1,2. We also generated comparable isogenic pairs from sporadic HNSCC cell lines by Cas9-targeted inactivation of FANCA followed by FANCA transgene complementation. FA-A is the dominant FA complementation group that is mutated in approximately two thirds of FA patients and in three of our five FA patient-derived HNSCC cell lines (Table 1). The FANCA protein plays a central role in the heteromeric FA ‘core complex’ that orchestrates FA-dependent DNA damage responses including FANCD2 monoubiquitylation mediated by the FANCL protein36.

Isogenic derivatives of patient-derived or newly generated FANCA-mutant sporadic HNSCC cell lines were generated by FANC transgene insertion into a chromosome 4 safe harbor site as well as by viral transduction. Both approaches restored FANC protein expression, though only viral transgene expression with higher levels of transgene protein expression fully restored MMC resistance and other FA-associated cellular phenotypes in FA patient-derived HNSCC cell lines. The apparent requirement for higher levels of transgene protein expression to suppress FA-associated phenotypes in FA patient-derived cell lines may reflect the many additional genomic and other differences between FA-patient-derived and newly generated, FANCA-mutant sporadic HNSCC cell lines 9,14. The related decision to distribute complemented pools of FA patient-derived or sporadic FANCA mutant cells was informed by early experiments that revealed little evidence of clonal heterogeneity in either FA patient-derived or sporadic HNSCC cell lines.

We observed considerable heterogeneity between cell lines as a function of origin and genotype. However, apart from MMC and cis-platinum, we did not see substantial, reproducible FANC genotype-dependent differences in drug or small molecule-mediated dose-dependent survivals. This heterogeneity was anticipated in light of the highly variable drug response profiling data for HNSCC and other cancer types in CCLE 10,45, GDSC 46–48 and DepMap (depmap.org/portal/)) data. Oxaliplatin may be the most interesting of the additional drugs we tested. Despite displaying little FANC genotype-dependent cell killing, oxaliplatin shows little cross-resistance with cis-Pt in HNSCC cell lines 49 and displays the ability to potentiate anti-PD-1 immunotherapy in preclinical models 50. These differences may reflect a different mechanism of action and the potential utility of oxaliplatin versus other platinum-based therapeutics as a chemotherapeutic agent 51,52.

Cell lines continue to play a central role in cancer research 53–55. They are experimentally tractable disease models that can capture and propagate many cell-autonomous features of neoplasia, and by virtue of simplicity and low cost have enabled many high-throughput drug, small molecule and genomic screens among other experimental analyses. The resulting publicly available data have, in turn, become a widely used resource and cancer discovery tool (see, e.g., CCLE 10,45; GDSC 46–48; DepMap resources (depmap.org/portal/)). The limitations of cancer cell line models are also well-recognized, most notably the lack of mmune/inflammatory cells and a vascularized tumor microenvironment. These limitations are now being effectively addressed by combining xenograft, tissue engineering, and organoid protocols. The FANC-isogenic cell line pairs generated here can be used in many of these approaches, and can provide highly reproducible results when care is taken to minimize passage-associated phenotypic drift or genomic evolution 53,55,56. Our hope is that these FA-CCLR isogenic cell line pairs will support many promising new research directions to benefit individuals with FA and their families, as well as individuals with sporadic HNSCC.
