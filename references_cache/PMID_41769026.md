INTRODUCTION: Pathogenic variants in COQ8A cause a rare form of primary coenzyme
Q10 (CoQ10) deficiency that can lead to childhood-onset cerebellar ataxia and
developmental delay. However, reports of pediatric cases remain limited, and
evidence regarding therapeutic response to CoQ10 supplementation in children is
still scarce.
CASE PRESENTATION: We report a 7-year-old boy with compound heterozygous COQ8A
variants who presented with progressive cerebellar ataxia and intellectual
disability. Oral CoQ10 supplementation was initiated at a dose of 10 mg/kg/day
after institutional ethics approval. During 1 year of treatment, the Scale for
the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia (SARA) score improved from 17 to 9, and
serum CoQ10 concentration increased from 622 to 9.100 ng/mL. Mild cognitive
improvement was also observed, with the intelligence quotient increasing from 53
to 64. Brain MRI demonstrated radiological stabilization of cerebellar atrophy.
No adverse effects related to CoQ10 supplementation were observed throughout the
treatment period.
CONCLUSION: This case demonstrates the clinical benefit and safety of CoQ10
supplementation in pediatric-onset COQ8A-related ataxia. Early genetic diagnosis
and timely initiation of CoQ10 therapy may lead to meaningful neurological
improvement and stabilization of disease progression in affected children.

Primary coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) deficiency is a rare mitochondrial disorder caused by pathogenic variants in genes involved in CoQ10 biosynthesis [1, 2]. Among them, COQ8A variants are recognized as a cause of autosomal recessive cerebellar ataxia [2–4]. Patients typically present with progressive cerebellar ataxia, developmental delay, and mild cognitive impairment, while systemic manifestations are usually absent [3, 4].

CoQ10 plays a dual role as an essential electron carrier in the mitochondrial respiratory chain and as a lipid-soluble antioxidant within cellular membranes [1, 5]. Its deficiency impairs ATP production and promotes oxidative stress, leading to neuronal vulnerability, particularly within Purkinje cells of the cerebellum [2, 3]. Furthermore, impaired redox cycling of CoQ10 disrupts calcium buffering and neurotransmitter release, contributing to the combination of motor incoordination and cognitive dysfunction observed in affected individuals [3, 5].

Clinical evaluation of disease severity and therapeutic response in COQ8A-related ataxia commonly employs the Scale for the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia (SARA), a validated quantitative measure of cerebellar dysfunction [6]. Although CoQ10 supplementation is considered a standard therapeutic approach [2, 5, 7], clinical responses and optimal treatment regimens remain variable and incompletely characterized [2, 4, 8]. Pediatric-onset COQ8A cases are particularly rare [2, 3, 8].

Given the rarity of pediatric cases, our report provides additional evidence for the therapeutic potential of early CoQ10 replacement in COQ8A-related disorders. Furthermore, this case contributes novel insights by presenting a previously unreported COQ8A variant (p.Arg464Trp) and quantitatively documenting therapeutic response through serial SARA scoring, intelligence quotient (IQ) assessment, and serum CoQ10 monitoring. Such integrated evaluation across clinical, biochemical, and neuroimaging domains remains uncommon in pediatric-onset COQ8A ataxia.

A 7-year-old boy, born to non-consanguineous parents, had an unremarkable perinatal history. Developmental delay was first noted at age 4. By age 5, he developed gait instability and frequent falls, prompting referral to our hospital. On examination, he exhibited at follow-up hyperactivity and marked truncal ataxia. He was unable to perform the finger-to-nose test, and his SARA score was 17. Routine laboratory studies revealed no abnormalities; both plasma lactate and pyruvate levels were within normal limits. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis also showed normal cell counts and protein concentrations, with lactate and pyruvate remaining normal. Testing for common spinocerebellar ataxia repeat expansions was negative, and peripheral nerve conduction studies demonstrated normal sensory and motor velocities, excluding peripheral neuropathy.

Brain MRI at age 6 revealed cerebellar vermian and hemispheric atrophy, which showed mild progression over 1 year (Fig. 1, before treatment). Cognitive assessment using the Tanaka-Binet Intelligence Scale, Fifth Edition, demonstrated an IQ of 53. TruSightOne panel sequencing (Illumina) identified compound heterozygous variants in COQ8A (NM_020247.5):c.1189G>A (p.Val397Met), maternally inherited and previously reported in ClinVar (Variation ID: 861520), and (NM_020247.5):c.1390C>T (p.Arg464Trp), paternally inherited and unreported, considered a novel variant. The diagnosis of COQ8A-related primary CoQ10 deficiency (OMIM #615567) was confirmed. After approval by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Yokohama City University Medical Center, M240010), oral CoQ10 supplementation was initiated at 10 mg/kg/day. Baseline serum CoQ10 level was 622 ng/mL. After 2 weeks, the level rose to 6,521 ng/mL with a SARA score of 16; at 3 months, CoQ10 was 8,347 ng/mL with SARA 13; at 6 months, 5,480 ng/mL with SARA 11; and at 12 months, 9.100 ng/mL with SARA 9 (Fig. 2). IQ improved modestly from 53 to 64 after 1 year. Hyperactivity diminished, and his ability to participate in group activities improved. No adverse effects occurred. MRI after 1 year showed cerebellar atrophy remained radiologically stable (Fig. 1, after treatment). The patient’s parents reported noticeable improvement in motor coordination and social participation during the course of treatment, and expressed satisfaction with the therapeutic outcome.

This case illustrates a favorable neurological response to CoQ10 supplementation in a child harboring compound heterozygous variants in COQ8A, including a novel missense variant (p.Arg464Trp). The treatment resulted in marked improvement of cerebellar ataxia, radiologic stabilization of cerebellar atrophy, and modest cognitive and behavioral gains. Collectively, these findings broaden the phenotypic and molecular spectrum of COQ8A-related primary CoQ10 deficiency and emphasize the therapeutic potential of early, sustained CoQ10 replacement in pediatric patients.


COQ8A encodes a mitochondrial matrix protein of the UbiB family whose kinase-like domain performs ATP hydrolysis coupled to CoQ biosynthesis rather than canonical phosphorylation. Structural studies revealed that human COQ8A (also known as ADCK3) possesses an atypical protein-kinase-like fold containing a KxGQ motif that occludes a conventional substrate pocket and an alanine-rich loop favoring ADP binding [1]. These features explain its role as a membrane-activated ATPase facilitating assembly and stabilization of the multienzyme CoQ-synthetic complex (the “CoQ-synthome”) [2, 3].

Our patient carried p.Val397Met (maternal) and p.Arg464Trp (paternal) variants. The latter, unregistered in ClinVar, resides in the nucleotide-binding core, where substitution of a charged arginine by a bulky aromatic residue likely disrupts local electrostatics and weakens ATP engagement, creating a hypomorphic state. Such residual activity may explain the treatment-responsive, childhood-onset phenotype. Consistent with prior reports, missense variants within the kinase-like domain tend to produce slowly progressive ataxia amenable to therapy, whereas truncating or splice-disrupting alleles are associated with early, multisystemic disease [2, 3].

Evidence from multiple series in patients with COQ8A-related ataxia supports early and continuous CoQ10 supplementation as critical for favorable outcomes [2–4]. In pediatric COQ8A ataxia, daily doses between 5 and 50 mg/kg (typically 10–30 mg/kg) are employed [3, 4]. Our patient received 10 mg/kg/day, achieving a ninefold rise in serum CoQ10 concentration (from 622 to >9,000 ng/mL) and a SARA score improvement from 17 to 9 within one year – aligning with previous observations that ≥12 months of therapy is required for durable benefit [2, 3]. Accumulating evidence suggests that early initiation of CoQ10 supplementation is crucial for achieving optimal neurological improvement, whereas delayed treatment may lead to suboptimal or incomplete response in COQ8A-related disorders. In adult-onset COQ8A-related ataxia, long-term CoQ10 supplementation often yields only modest or limited clinical benefit, as reported in adult case series such as that by Horvath et al. [7] (2012). These differences highlight that the therapeutic window may differ by age of onset and underscore the need to consider both genotype and disease stage when tailoring therapy.

Pharmacokinetics strongly influence response because CoQ10 is highly lipophilic and absorbed via micellar solubilization and chylomicron transport. Co-administration with dietary fat enhances uptake, while formulation differences (ubiquinone vs. ubiquinol, nano- or liposomal carriers) variably affect bioavailability [5]. Inter-individual variability warrants therapeutic drug monitoring: serum trough levels of ≥3–5 µg/mL (≈3,000–5,000 ng/mL) correlate with clinical improvement [5]. Our patient exceeded this threshold without adverse events. Given relapse risk after discontinuation and favorable safety data, continued administration beyond 12 months with 3–6-month monitoring of SARA/ICARS scores and serum levels is advisable [2–4, 8]. Long-term continuation appears justified while measurable benefit persists since rebound deterioration after withdrawal has been reported [2, 4, 8].

Beyond motor coordination, CoQ10 repletion may enhance cognitive and behavioral domains. Cerebellar-thalamo-cortical circuits contribute to executive and attentional control; mitochondrial dysfunction within these pathways can manifest as hyperactivity or reduced processing speed. In this case, IQ increased from 53 to 64 and hyperactivity lessened after 1 year, paralleling motor gains. Experimental studies suggest that CoQ10 stabilizes mitochondrial membranes, attenuates oxidative and excitotoxic stress, and promotes PGC-1α–dependent biogenesis and BDNF signaling – mechanisms consistent with improved neuronal resilience [4, 5]. Although these findings derive mainly from model systems, they provide a plausible explanation for the modest but reproducible cognitive benefits observed in responsive COQ8A patients [2, 3].

Clinical practice remains heterogeneous regarding dose, formulation, and monitoring. Standardized prospective protocols employing quantitative scales (SARA/ICARS) and imaging biomarkers such as volumetric MRI or 31P-MRS are needed [3, 4]. Routine pharmacokinetic surveillance will facilitate inter-study comparability and optimization of exposure-response relationships.

Integration of variant type, biochemical data, and clinical trajectory may enable personalized therapy. Patients with residual enzymatic activity – typically missense variants in the kinase-like domain – respond to moderate-dose, long-term supplementation, while truncating alleles often require intensified or adjunctive strategies [2, 3, 7]. Expanding genotype-response registries will refine prognostic counseling and trial stratification.

Several novel approaches are under exploration. First, mitochondria-targeted analogs such as idebenone or MitoQ aim to enhance delivery to neuronal mitochondria, though efficacy in COQ8A disease remains unproven [5]. Second, recent co-crystal structures of COQ8A with small-molecule ligands have revealed potential druggable pockets, suggesting feasibility of pharmacologic chaperones that stabilize residual ATPase activity [1]. Third, cryo-EM analysis of the COQ7–COQ9 complex provides a blueprint for interventions that reinforce CoQ-synthome integrity [3]. Finally, adeno-associated-virus-based gene replacement – successful in other mitochondrial defects – may ultimately enable causal therapy, though vector distribution and immune constraints remain obstacles [5]. Parallel advances in AI-assisted neuroimaging, including machine-learning-based cerebellar volumetry and spectroscopic metabolite profiling, may supply sensitive biomarkers for early therapeutic response assessment.

As a single-case experience, this report cannot define generalizable pharmacokinetic targets or predict inter-individual variability. Nonetheless, the concordance of clinical, biochemical, and radiologic improvements supports a causal link between CoQ10 supplementation and neurological recovery. Future priorities include multicenter pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic modeling across age ranges, genotype-stratified natural-history cohorts with harmonized endpoints, and combination trials pairing CoQ10 with synthome-stabilizing or biosynthesis-enhancing agents [2, 3, 5]. Because the follow-up period in this case was limited to 1 year, the long-term durability of the therapeutic response remains uncertain. In addition, although conventional MRI showed radiological stabilization, quantitative brain volumetry was not performed. Volumetric MRI typically requires high-resolution contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images and three-dimensional reconstruction and segmentation software to objectively quantify cerebellar volume.

Early identification of COQ8A variants and timely initiation of optimized CoQ10 therapy can achieve clinically meaningful gains in motor and cognitive function with radiologic stabilization over at least 1 year. Ongoing integration of structural biology, molecular genetics, and systems pharmacology provides a roadmap toward precision treatment and evidence-based standards of care for this treatable mitochondrial ataxia.
