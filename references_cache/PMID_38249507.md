# ADAMTS18-related anterior segment dysgenesis mistaken as Axenfeld-Rieger syndrome.
**Authors:** Khan AO
**Journal:** Taiwan J Ophthalmol (2023)
**DOI:** [10.4103/tjo.TJO-D-23-00034](https://doi.org/10.4103/tjo.TJO-D-23-00034)

## Content

Axenfeld-Rieger spectrum is a range of anterior segment dysgenesis (ASD) phenotypes often related to heterozygous pathogenic variants in the ocular transcription factor genes FOXC1 or PITX2. Microcornea with myopic chorioretinal atrophy, a less common ASD, is distinct, recognizable, and secondary to biallelic pathogenic variants in the metalloproteinase gene ADAMTS18. This report describes the case of a boy with ADAMTS18-related ASD that was mistaken for Axenfeld-Rieger syndrome and highlights distinguishing features.

Anterior segment dysgeneses (ASDs) are a group of nonacquired developmental anterior segment abnormalities (defects in neuroectodermal derivatives such as iris, cornea, and iridocorneal angle tissue) that confer a risk for glaucoma. Axenfeld–Rieger spectrum is a range of ASDs that is classically related to heterozygous pathogenic variants in the ocular transcription factor genes FOXC1 (forkhead box C1) or PITX2 (paired-like homeodomain 2) and includes a risk for extraocular disease.[ 1 ] Findings range from mild (e.g., posterior embryotoxon) to severe (e.g., childhood glaucoma in the setting of significant iris hypoplasia or corneal opacity). The most commonly associated features of the phenotype include iris corectopia, iris atrophy, and posterior embryotoxon with adherent iris strands. When there are coexistent extraocular features related to abnormal neural crest migration, the term Axenfeld–Rieger syndrome is used. Such extraocular features can include craniofacial abnormalities (telecanthus/hypertelorism, broad nasal bridge, maxillary hypoplasia, thin upper lip, dental abnormalities, and low-set ears), redundant umbilical skin, and extraocular diseases such as cardiac defects and hearing loss.[ 1 2 ]

A less common but distinct ASD is microcornea with myopic chorioretinal atrophy, a recognizable phenotype that is specific for biallelic pathogenic variants in the metalloproteinase gene ADAMTS18 (a disintegrin-like and metalloprotease with thrombospondin type 1 motif 18).[ 3 4 ] As a protein group, ADAMTSs play a role in extracellular matrix assembly and degradation. Unlike ASD related to typical Axenfeld–Rieger spectrum, ADAMTS18-related ASD is autosomal recessive and does not include a risk for extraocular disease.

This report describes the case of a boy with ADAMTS18-related ASD that was initially diagnosed as Axenfeld–Rieger syndrome and highlights distinguishing features.

An eight-month-old boy was referred because of eye turn in the setting of presumed Axenfeld–Rieger syndrome. General health was unremarkable. Pregnancy was unremarkable, and birth was natural full term. On examination, the child was very visually alert and playful. The vision was central, steady, and maintained in either eye. External examination was significant for low-set ears, telecanthus, and a prominent philtrum [Figure 1]. He had a pseudoexotropic appearance with no true ocular misalignment or ocular motility defects. The pupils were small and inferiorly displaced but reacted normally. The corneas were small (approximately 10 mm horizontal corneal diameter) with temporal posterior embryotoxon either eye [Figure 2]. Intraocular pressure (rebound tonometry) was 20 mmHg either eye. Cycloplegic refraction (cyclopentolate 0.5% and tropicamide 1%) was approximately −4.00 either eye. Fundus examination either eye was significant for chorioretinal atrophy surrounding the optic nerve head, which otherwise appeared normal.

Genetic testing was performed (whole-exome sequencing). The only notable variant of interest was an apparently homozygous novel ADAMTS18 variant – NM_199355.3: c.2058C>A; p.(Cys686*). Targeted sequencing of the child’s parents showed each to be a carrier for the variant and thus confirmed homozygosity for the variant in the child.

At the last examination, at two years old, there was a true small angle left exotropia. Vision, anterior segment findings, intraocular pressures, and retinal appearance were stable. Cycloplegic refraction was -2.00 -1.25 x180 and -3.25 -2.00 x170, and glasses with this refraction were dispensed.

The first description of microcornea with myopic chorioretinal degeneration was in 2012.[ 3 ] Five boys from four families were noted to have an unusual combination of small corneas with myopia. Examination revealed that all had microcornea, myopic chorioretinal degeneration, telecanthus, and posteriorly-rotated ears. The recurrence of these distinct ocular and facial features in four different inbred families from Saudi Arabia suggested a distinct autosomal recessive syndrome caused by pathogenic variants in a gene involved in ocular and facial development. Additional features included retinal dysfunction by electroretinography, lens opacities, and mild lens subluxation. Glaucoma was not reported but can uncommonly occur.

The genetic cause for microcornea with myopic chorioretinal degeneration was uncovered in 2013.[ 4 ] Homozygosity mapping for the original four families and an additional affected family revealed different homozygous pathogenic variants in ADAMTS18 as responsible for the phenotype. The following year, three non-Saudi families with ADAMTS18-related ASD were reported.[ 5 ]

ADAMTS18 belongs to the ADAMTS family of extracellular multidomain enzymes that have multiple functions in the extracellular matrix. The ADAMTS family differs from the ADAM family in that ADAMTS proteins lack transmembrane and epidermal growth factor domains and have thrombospondin motifs in the C-terminus.[ 6 ] The murine equivalent of ADAMTS18 is highly expressed in the anterior and posterior structures of the developing and adult mouse eye.[ 7 ] Although biallelic pathogenic variants in ADAMT18 were initially thought to be linked to Knobloch syndrome, this was incorrect.[ 4 7 ] Biallelic ADAMTS18 pathogenic variants are specifically associated with microcornea with myopic chorioretinal atrophy.

While there is some overlap between the Axenfeld–Rieger spectrum and microcornea with myopic chorioretinal degeneration, the two conditions are distinct. Features that can be common to both microcornea with myopic chorioretinal degeneration and Axenfeld–Rieger spectrum include posterior embryotoxon, mild iris abnormalities, telecanthus, and glaucoma (although glaucoma is less common in microcornea with myopic chorioretinal atrophy). Findings more classic for Axenfeld–Rieger syndrome include corectopia/polycoria, iris strands to a posterior embryotoxon, micrognathia, dental abnormalities, and, more rarely, hypospadias, anal stenosis, pituitary abnormalities, and cardiac valvular abnormalities.[ 8 ] Findings more typical for microcornea with myopic chorioretinal degeneration include the small corneas, posteriorly-rotated ears, myopic chorioretinal atrophy, and lens subluxation.

In summary, the ADAMTS18-related phenotype is a distinct recognizable syndrome that should be distinguished from other forms of ASD such as the Axenfeld–Rieger spectrum. Relatively small corneas in the setting of myopic chorioretinal degeneration should raise suspicion for the ADAMTS18-related phenotype, particularly when in the context of telecanthus and posteriorly-rotated ears. Unlike ASD related to typical Axenfeld–Rieger spectrum, ADAMTS18-related ASD is autosomal recessive and does not include a risk for extraocular disease.
