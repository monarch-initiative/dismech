# TFRC Germline Variants and Inborn Error of Immunity: Mechanistic Insights into Iron-Immune Crosstalk.
**Authors:** Aljohani AH
**Journal:** J Clin Immunol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10875-026-01999-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-026-01999-y)

## Content

Mutations in TFRC, which encodes transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1/CD71), have emerged as a rare but significant cause of inborn error of immunity (IEI). TfR1 is a crucial membrane glycoprotein responsible for receptor-mediated iron uptake, playing fundamental roles in erythropoiesis and immune function, and linking impaired iron homeostasis to profound immune dysfunction. Notably, TFRC deficiency provides a biologically instructive human model demonstrating that iron uptake via TfR1 is a non-redundant metabolic and signaling checkpoint required for antigen receptor–driven immune activation, clonal expansion, and immune homeostasis. This review summarizes the molecular mechanisms of TfR1, including its interactions with transferrin, and explores how pathogenic TFRC mutations impair iron uptake, affect immune cells, lymphocyte activation, and clonal diversity. We also examine TfR1 in the context of immunodeficiency, linking its roles in iron metabolism and immune regulation to clinical outcomes. By integrating current knowledge on TfR1 biology, molecular mechanisms with patients’ clinical presentation, observations, variants, and therapeutic approaches, this review highlights knowledge gaps and unresolved questions, providing a framework for future research toward elucidating TfR1 mechanisms, improving patient diagnosis, and ultimately enabling the development of targeted therapies for TfR1-mediated immunodeficiency.

The TFRC gene encodes transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1/CD71), a cell-surface type II membrane protein required for cellular iron uptake. This receptor is essential for metabolically active and proliferating cells in all vertebrates, including immune cells, erythropoiesis and neurologic development [1, 2]. In addition, TfR1 is essential for the control of iron metabolism [3].

The 180 kDa TfR1 homodimer interacts with diferric transferrin (TF) and mediates its receptor-mediated endocytosis into specialized endosomes. Endosomal acidification releases iron, which is transported to the cytosol primarily via DMT1(divalentmet al. transporter 1); the apo–transferrin–TfR1 complex is then recycled to the plasma membrane [2, 3]. The TF–TfR complex is then recycled to the cell surface, where the neutral pH leads to a loss of binding affinity between TF and its receptor. Transferrin (TF) is the primary ligand of transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1), mediating cellular iron uptake, whereas the hemochromatosis protein (HFE) functions as a regulatory molecule that modulates TfR1–transferrin interactions and intracellular iron homeostasis rather than serving as a primary ligand. HFE, which is a nonclassical MHC I molecule, regulates intracellular iron loading by modulating TfR and ferritin activity, and its expression decreases the level of ferritin and increases the level of TfR expression, similar to the effects of mutations in TFRC and STEAP (which encodes transmembrane epithelial antigen protein-3) [4, 5]. HFE forms a complex with TfR, thereby preventing TfR degradation. Therefore, TfR1 plays key roles in both iron metabolism and immune function.

Homozygous missense mutations in TFRC (e.g., p.Y20H and p.R22W) impair TfR1 internalization, leading to defective cellular iron uptake. In lymphocytes, reduced iron availability thereby impairs lymphocyte activation, proliferation, and clonal diversity, resulting in a rare autosomal recessive combined T- and B-cell immunodeficiency. These defects are associated with reduced cytokine production and contraction of specific immune cell subsets, including natural killer (NK) and regulatory T (Treg). In TFRC c.64 C > T (p.R22W) germline variants, there is a defect in mucosa-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells, as well as expansion of low-density neutrophils. Due to defective TfR1-mediated iron uptake, patients become vulnerable to infections, cytopenias, and immune dysfunction observed in affected individuals [1, 4, 6, 7].

Patients with the TFRC variant exhibit an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern. Immunologically, affected individuals present with a primary combined immunodeficiency characterized by recurrent infections, hypogammaglobulinemia, decreased memory B-cell numbers, defective immunoglobulin class switching, impaired proliferative responses of T cells, and intermittent neutropenia, although absolute lymphocyte counts are typically normal. The condition usually manifests in early childhood and, in severe cases, can lead to early mortality [4, 6, 7]. Hematologic features include mild anemia and intermittent thrombocytopenia. Disease severity varies, and some patients may develop severe complications, such as sepsis or hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis-like symptoms (HLH) [6]. To date, more than 20 patients worldwide have been reported with the TFRC germline variants c.58T > C (p.Y20H). Only two pathogenic TFRC variants have been identified: the homozygous c.58T > C (p.Y20H) variant, which accounts for most cases and is mainly seen in patients of Arab origin, and the c.64 C > T (p.R22W) variant, reported in a single patient. This clustering suggests a founder effect for the p.Y20H variant, highlighting the extreme genetic rarity of TFRC deficiency. A mouse model of TFRC mutation has been developed, which simulates some of the clinical features observed in patients [4]. It showed that transgenic mice homozygous for the human TFRC variant Y20H were viable, indicating that the mutation resulted in a hypomorphic allele. Mutant mice had decreased serum IgG, hemoglobin, and MCV levels compared to controls, but normal percentages of splenic T and B cells, as well as naive and memory T cells, and NK cells. However, mutant T cells showed impaired proliferative responses, and B cells showed impaired immunoglobulin secretion in response to stimulation. TfR1 surface expression on mutant T and B cells was significantly increased, reflecting impaired internalization. In addition, iron supplementation or wild-type TfR1 expression can rescue cellular defects associated with TFRC mutations in vitro [4].

Mutations in the TFRC gene have profound effects on immune cell populations in both humans and animal models, as described in the previous section. The most consistent and severe effects are observed in T cells, B cells, and natural killer (NK) cells, with additional effects on innate immune cells, as illustrated in Fig. 1. These combined effects result in defects in T- and B-cell function, impairing adaptive immunity and making such patients highly susceptible to severe infections.

TfR1 is essential for T-cell maturation, activation, differentiation, and signaling by facilitating the uptake of transferrin-bound iron, which is critical for cell cycle progression, protein synthesis, and mitochondrial function [4, 8]. As outlined in the core TfR1 iron-uptake mechanism, this process depends on proper receptor internalization mediated by the aromatic residue Tyr20 and is essential for iron transport in lymphocytes [4]. During T-cell activation, interleukin-2 (IL-2) induces TFRC transcription and stabilizes TfR1 mRNA, thereby ensuring sufficient intracellular iron availability to support T-cell proliferation and cytotoxic function, particularly in CD8⁺ T cells [9]. Accordingly, reduced TfR1 expression is associated with impaired immune responses, whereas TfR1 inactivation suppresses T-cell proliferation and cytotoxicity,underscoring its central role in T-cell biology.

Experimental studies demonstrate that defects in TfR1 function lead to intracellular iron deficiency, resulting in reduced peripheral T-cell numbers, thymic atrophy, increased apoptosis, and diminished clonal diversity [8]. In this context, regulatory T cells (Tregs) and mucosa-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells—subsets with high metabolic demands—are also significantly reduced [4, 6, 7]. Consistently, TFRC knockout mice exhibit arrest of T-cell development at early stages, supporting a critical requirement for TfR1-mediated iron uptake during T-cell ontogeny [8].

In patients harboring the homozygous TFRC c.58T > C (p.Y20H) variant, defective TfR1 internalization impairs T-cell proliferation despite markedly increased surface expression of TfR1 on T and B cells. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells from affected individuals show significantly reduced proliferative responses to phytohemagglutinin (PHA), anti-CD3 antibody, and PMA plus ionomycin, and these defects are not rescued by T-cell costimulation with anti-CD28 or exogenous IL-2 [4]. Clinically, patients exhibit normal or reduced total T-cell counts, including naïve CD3⁺CD45RA⁺ and memory CD3⁺CD45RO⁺ populations, with disproportionately reduced CD8⁺ T cells and an elevated CD4:CD8 ratio, reflecting impaired clonal expansion rather than defective thymic output [8, 10]. The impaired lymphocyte proliferation in response to PHA can be restored in vitro by iron citrate supplementation, indicating that defective iron uptake underlies the observed T-cell dysfunction [4, 11].

Beyond its role in iron uptake, TfR1 also acts as a signaling molecule in T cells by physically interacting with parts of the T-cell receptor (TCR) complex, including the CD3ζ chain and the ζ-associated kinase ZAP70 [10]. Engagement of TfR1 triggers tyrosine phosphorylation and enhances antigen-dependent TCR signaling, and direct interactions among TfR1, ZAP70, and CD3ζ indicate a dual function for TfR1 in iron metabolism and TCR-mediated signal transduction. This mechanistic connection offers insight into how TFRC variants impair TCR signaling and contribute to T-cell dysfunction and immune dysregulation [10, 11].

TfR1 plays a distinct role in B-cell biology, different from its function in T cells. Experimental studies indicate that TfR1 is required primarily for the proliferation of early B-cell precursors rather than differentiation [8]. In TfR-deficient mouse models, B-cell development proceeds to at least the IgM stage, although the number of IgM⁺ B cells in peripheral lymphoid organs is reduced, suggesting that early differentiation is partially preserved despite impaired expansion [8]. These findings indicate that TfR1-dependent iron uptake is more critical for B-cell proliferative capacity than for lineage commitment.

Although some B cells can mature, B-cell proliferation and clonal expansion are markedly impaired under conditions of TfR1 deficiency. The reduced number of mature B cells observed in TfR-null settings highlights the importance of TfR1 in meeting the iron requirements of rapidly dividing B-cell precursors. Consistent with this, B cells derived from patients with TFRC variants exhibit significantly reduced proliferative responses to stimulation [4].

Beyond impaired expansion, TFRC variants also disrupt the functional maturation of B cells by affecting immunoglobulin class switching. Patient-derived B cells show decreased secretion of IgG and IgE following stimulation with anti-CD40 and anti-IL-4 antibodies, and mature Iµ–Cε transcripts required for IgE switching are undetectable, indicating defective class-switch recombination rather than a complete block in B-cell differentiation [4, 9].​.

Clinically, these cellular defects manifest as hypogammaglobulinemia in all reported patients with TFRC variants, with low levels of IgG, IgA, and IgE, while some individuals exhibit normal or elevated IgM levels [6]. Together, these findings reflect impaired B-cell proliferative capacity and defective class-switch recombination, rather than an absence of B-cell development, as the principal drivers of humoral immunodeficiency in TFRC-related inborn errors of immunity.

The number of circulating NK cells is significantly reduced, contributing to combined immunodeficiency [4, 6]. Transferrin receptors may serve as targets for NK cells [10, 11]. In mice, TfR forms a complex with a fraction of KLRG1 (killer cell lectin-like receptor G1) receptors, which are primarily expressed by natural killer (NK) cells and differentiated T cells at the late stage of cellular maturation or differentiation and are detected in IL-2-activated natural killer (NK) cells but not in resting NK cells. KLRG1 plays a role in cancer immunosurveillance and the prevention of autoimmunity. KLRG1 may also modulate lymphocyte activation, proliferation, and responsiveness. High levels of TfR1s correlate with decreased KLRG1-mediated inhibition, and vice versa. In other innate immune cells, TfR1 expression can impair the ability of these cells to respond to pathogens [12].

Recurrent thrombocytopenia is a prominent hematologic feature in several patients with TFRC variants and can be severe, occasionally requiring platelet transfusions [6]. Available clinical and laboratory data argue against an autoimmune etiology, as platelet-specific antibodies are absent, bone marrow examination reveals hypocellularity with reduced megakaryocytes, and responses to immunosuppressive therapy are poor [6]. Instead, the favorable response to thrombopoietin suggests an intrinsic defect in platelet production or maturation rather than immune-mediated destruction [6]. During normal megakaryopoiesis, TfR1 is expressed on early megakaryocyte precursors and progressively downregulated with differentiation, indicating a role for iron availability during platelet development [13].

The relationship between TfR1 and platelet biology remains incompletely defined, as TfR1 expression on platelets from patients with TFRC variants has not been directly characterized. Nevertheless, platelets use transferrin-dependent iron for protein synthesis and mitochondrial function, and severe iron deficiency has been associated with thrombocytopenia. Together, these observations suggest that impaired intracellular iron availability resulting from defective TfR1 internalization in megakaryocyte precursors may contribute to abnormal platelet production or function in TFRC-related disease. However, direct evidence for this mechanism is currently lacking [14–17].

Patients with the TFRC c.58T > C (p.Y20H) variant typically exhibit mild microcytic anemia with slightly reduced hemoglobin levels, a phenotype thought to reflect compensatory mechanisms rather than profound defects in erythropoiesis [4]. One such mechanism may involve STEAP3, a metalloreductase highly expressed in erythroid precursor cells (EPCs), which has been identified as a potential accessory factor facilitating TfR1 endocytosis and intracellular iron handling. This interaction may partially mitigate the effects of defective TfR1 internalization, thereby protecting affected individuals from severe anemia. Consistent with this interpretation, anemia in these patients is associated with a reduced mean corpuscular volume (MCV) that does not respond to iron supplementation, indicating impaired cellular iron utilization rather than systemic iron deficiency (Fig. 1). Notably, surface TfR1 expression on EPCs is increased by only 2–2.5-fold compared with controls, a substantially smaller increase than that observed in lymphocytes, suggesting relative preservation of iron uptake in the erythroid lineage [4].

Under physiological conditions, TFRC upregulation enhances iron uptake and contributes to maintaining intracellular iron homeostasis, processes essential for erythropoiesis. Increased TFRC expression has also been linked to iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, a regulated form of cell death driven by reactive oxygen species generated in the presence of excess intracellular iron. Conversely, reduced TFRC expression limits ferroptosis, underscoring the central role of TfR1 in balancing iron availability and cellular survival [18, 19]. Although ferroptosis has not been directly implicated in the erythroid phenotype of TFRC-deficient patients, these observations may highlight the broader importance of tightly regulated TfR1 activity in red blood cell biology.

In addition to transferrin receptor–mediated iron uptake, many non‑hematopoietic cells utilize TfR‑independent pathways, including ferritin uptake, non‑transferrin‑bound iron (NTBI), and other mechanisms of iron acquisition [8]. The relative contribution of these pathways varies by cell type and iron status.

In the central nervous system (CNS), TfR1 is expressed in endothelial cells of the blood–brain barrier, neurons, and oligodendrocytes, reflecting the high iron demand of metabolically active neural cells. Conditional neural TfR1 deficiency in mouse models leads to impaired iron uptake, developmental defects, increased apoptosis, and functional deficits in the CNS, underscoring the importance of TfR1 for neural iron supply [1]. Neuronal reliance on iron reflects its essential role in mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, where iron–sulfur clusters and heme cofactors support the electron transport chain. Rare pathogenic TFRC variants in humans have been associated with neurological features such as developmental delay and optic atrophy, possibly due to disrupted mitochondrial iron utilization and iron homeostasis in neural tissue [6, 7, 17, 20–24]. 

Beyond its established roles in hematopoietic and lymphoid cells, transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) also contributes to liver function and antiviral immunity. TfR1 has been shown to mediate the uptake and presentation of hepatitis B envelope antigen (HBenvAg) by activated T cells and was proposed in early studies as a potential entry factor for hepatitis B virus (HBV) particles [25, 26]. These findings link TfR1 not only to antigen processing but also to immune modulation in chronic HBV infection. Although these observations stem from studies of viral pathogenesis rather than TFRC deficiency, they raise compelling questions: mutations that impair TfR1 function could plausibly affect viral entry, antigen presentation, or immune responses to HBV and related pathogens. While such interactions have not been previously documented, the presence of chronic hepatitis B infection in two individuals with TFRC variants [6] highlights a potential avenue for future investigation [10, 25, 27, 28].

Building on the discussion of TfR1 function across different cell types, this section addresses diagnostic strategies for TFRC variants, a critical step in linking cellular dysfunction to clinical features and advancing the goals of this review. Clinically, affected patients often present with recurrent bacterial and viral infections, poor growth, and anemia, sometimes within the context of a family history of immunodeficiency or consanguinity. A patient with a TFRC variant is in the differential diagnosis for common variable immunodeficiency (CVID), Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome (WAS), and hyper-IgM syndrome (HIGM). The initial laboratory workup includes a complete blood count, which may reveal anemia or lymphopenia. Additionally, the measurement of lymphocyte subsets by flow cytometry is used to analyze the cell surface expression of TfR1 on T and B cells, as well as to determine the percentage of CD19 + CD27+ memory B cells. Immunoglobulin levels should also be assessed. Following these tests, functional assays are conducted to assess the proliferation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) in response to various stimuli (PHA, anti-CD3, and PMA + ionomycin). The results show that proliferation in response to PHA is significantly decreased compared to controls, indicating a global defect in T cell proliferation. The immunoglobulin levels showed a significant reduction in IgG, IgA, and IgE. IgM level can be low or high. Iron metabolism studies, including normal serum iron levels, ferritin levels, and transferrin saturation, may support this diagnosis.

A definitive diagnosis is established through genetic testing, which identifies variants in the TFRC gene. Two mutations have been detected: a c.58T > C substitution, resulting in a p.Tyr20His (Tyrosine to Histidine) change in the transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) protein, and a c.64 C > T mutation, leading to a p.R22W change (referred to as TfR1R22W). The latter disrupts the YTRF motif in the cytoplasmic tail of TfR1, impairing receptor internalization and iron uptake. These variants confirm the diagnosis in patients with CID. This is typically achieved using targeted primary immunodeficiency panels, whole-exome sequencing, or whole-genome sequencing, with segregation analysis performed in family members. The elevated TfR1 cell surface expression on lymphocytes can be used for the diagnosis of patients at birth [4, 6, 7, 29]. Together, these approaches allow clinicians to identify TFRC variants and their immunological consequences, ensuring accurate diagnosis and guiding appropriate management. Management of TFRC-related inborn error of immunity primarily involves supportive care, including monthly intravenous immunoglobulin therapy and prophylactic antibiotics for nontransplant patients, to manage infections and immune deficiencies. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is the most effective treatment, as it resolves clinical and laboratory abnormalities, including correction of TfR1 expression and restoration of lymphocyte function [29]. Early HSCT is advised for severe cases to prevent complications and boost survival. HSCT has been successfully performed in numerous reported patients with TFRC germline variants c.58T > C (p.Y20H), resulting in the resolution of recurrent infections, normalization of immunoglobulin levels, correction of lymphocyte proliferation defects, and restoration of normal TfR1 expression and immune function. These clinical outcomes demonstrate that HSCT can be a curative therapy for severe presentation and support early consideration of HSCT during the disease course for patients with significant clinical symptoms [4, 6].

Thrombocytopenia management in patients with TFRC variants includes thromboplastin, which yields favorable responses, particularly in severe cases. Platelet transfusions can be administered for severe thrombocytopenia. However, other treatments, such as intravenous immunoglobulins and anti-CD20 therapy, are not indicated, as no autoimmune cause has been identified, and these treatments are ineffective in these patients. Some patients may experience improvement in thrombocytopenia as they reach adulthood. Iron supplementation is typically ineffective for anemia in patients with TFRC variants. Many individuals with mild microcytic anemia have normal iron profiles, suggesting that the anemia results from impaired iron uptake due to defective endocytosis of TfR1 rather than iron deficiency [4, 6].

The prognosis for IEI caused by TFRC variants without HSCT varies, as disease severity differs among reported cases [6]. Early deaths and severe disease have been reported, particularly in early childhood, while some individuals have survived into adulthood despite ongoing health challenges [4, 6, 29].

Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is the most effective treatment option for patients with severe disease, offering long-term survival and resolution of clinical manifestations [4, 6, 29]. Patients who do not undergo HSCT are managed with supportive therapies, including antibiotics and immunoglobulin replacement, but remain at significant risk for serious complications such as recurrent infections and progressive organ damage [4, 6].

To date, no malignancies have been reported in patients with TFRC variants. However, one report suggests that TFRC deficiency may predispose patients to hematologic malignancies, as three individuals exhibited dysmyelopoiesis before HSCT. One patient developed a clonal cytogenetic abnormality concerning for myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), although no malignancy developed following HSCT [29]. Nevertheless, long-term follow-up remains warranted. Early diagnosis and timely intervention are critical for improving outcomes and preventing life-threatening complications [4, 6, 29].

Despite growing recognition of TFRC germline variants as a cause of inborn errors of immunity, several key questions remain unresolved. The full genetic and clinical spectrum of TFRC-related disease remains undefined, as only two pathogenic variants have been reported in a limited number of patients, raising the possibility that additional variants or milder phenotypes remain undetected. Moreover, the mechanisms underlying the selective immune cell subsets, particularly CD8⁺ T cells, regulatory T cells, MAIT cells, and NK cells, to impaired TfR1-mediated iron uptake are poorly understood. Although TfR1 has been shown to associate with components of the T-cell receptor signaling complex, the precise mechanistic link between iron availability, metabolic fitness, and antigen receptor signaling remains incompletely elucidated.

The primary limitation of this review is the scarcity of reported cases and genetic variants, which restricts the ability to draw definitive genotype–phenotype correlations or fully characterize disease variability. Most available data derive from a small number of patients and experimental models, and long-term clinical outcomes—particularly following hematopoietic stem cell transplantation—remain limited. In addition, the mechanistic insights discussed here rely largely on indirect evidence linking iron metabolism to immune signaling, highlighting the need for further functional and translational studies. Expanded patient registries and multicenter collaborations will be essential to address these limitations and advance understanding of TFRC-related inborn errors of immunity.

Mutations in the TFRC gene, which encodes TfR1, link defective iron uptake to CID and highlight the crucial role of iron in affecting both adaptive and innate immunity. These mutations disrupt lymphocyte function and hematopoiesis, leading to recurrent infections, anemia, and other systemic complications. Diagnosis of patients with these mutations involves a combination of clinical, immunological, and genetic evaluations. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a curative option. Overall, understanding the role of TFRC in bridging iron metabolism and immune function highlights areas where further research could improve diagnosis and treatment. Expanded registries and multicenter studies are needed to identify and characterize additional TFRC cases. Functional and therapeutic research targeting iron homeostasis and immune modulation may enhance understanding and improve patient outcomes.
