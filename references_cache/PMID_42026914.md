# The Genetics of Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia - Advances and Limitations.
**Authors:** Horani A
**Journal:** Pediatr Pulmonol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ppul.71635](https://doi.org/10.1002/ppul.71635)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a genetic condition that is caused by variants in genes associated with motile cilia. Most individuals with PCD present early in life with symptoms of chronic cough, nasal congestion, and recurrent upper and lower respiratory tract infections. About 50% of patients have laterality defects and cardiac malformation including heterotaxy syndrome, and most individuals have sub-infertility.

METHODS: This narrative review synthesizes available evidence on the genes associated with PCD with emphasis on the differences in the effects on cilia structure and function.

RESULTS: Pathogenic variants in over 60 genes cause PCD. Many of these genes encode structural components within the ciliary axoneme, while others encode cytoplasmic assembly and regulatory proteins crucial for the normal function of cilia. Recent identification of variants in genes that are associated with both motile and sensory cilia function have expanded our understanding of the clinical manifestations of ciliopathies. Genetic confirmation is encouraged by the recent European Respiratory and American Thoracic societies diagnostic guidelines. The expanded use of genetic approaches and identification of variants of unknown significant in many genes associated with PCD emphasize the need for better diagnostic tools for PCD.

CONCLUSION: PCD is a complex genetic condition. Recognizing the clinical symptoms associated with PCD and establishing a genetic diagnosis is crucial for early initiation of proper treatment and for differentiating PCD from other conditions with similar symptoms.

Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) and mucociliary clearance disorders are rare inherited disorders caused by dysfunction of motile cilia. It is estimated to affect 1:7500 live births and is likely more prevalent amongst populations with high levels of consanguinity1. Prevalence and causative genes associated with PCD vary by region and ethnicity2. For instance, variants in the gene DNAAF5 are relatively rare in the general population, but common in individuals belonging to the Amish and Mennonite Communities2,3. Individuals with PCD often present early in live with symptoms of cough and recurrent upper and lower airway infections4, which can lead to significant morbidity including progressive lung disease.

Cilia are divided into two major types: motile and sensory (primary cilia) that differe in their distribution and function. Motile cilia are hair-like organelles that line the surface of epithelial cells in the airways, brain ventricles, fallopian tubes, testes efferent ducts, as well as form the tail of sperm. Motile cilia beat rhythmically propelling surface fluid across the epithelium, and their dysfunction can lead to impaired airway mucociliary clearance, development of brain ventriculomegally, and infertility5,6. A closely related structure is the nodal motile cilia which emerges during development. These mono-cilia have a rotational movement that results in a leftward flow of extra-cellular fluid across the embryonic node’s surface7. This flow triggers a subset of sensory cilia at the rim of the node starting a signaling cascade that determines body-sidedness8. Lack of proper signaling in the node results in laterality defects and conditions such as situs inversus totalis and heterotaxy syndromes9.

Primary (sensory) cilia, are non-motile organelles that emerge on most non-dividing cells. Primary cilia are essential signaling hubs involved in many developmental pathways (such as the sonic hedghog and WNT pathways)10. They also perform specialized sensory functions inclduing mechosensing in the cartlige11, chemosensing as occurs in the olfactory cilia12, or photo-sensing as occurs in the outer segment of the cones and rodes in the retina13. Genetic defects in primary cilia are assocated with diverse neurocognitive and developmental conditions that are collectively called primary ciliopathies14,15. These include conditions such as the polycystic kidney diseases caused by pathogenic variants in PKD1 and PKD2 encoding mechno-sensors in the renal tubular cilia, usher syndrome cuased by variants in several genes encoding proteins that are essential for the function of eye photoreceptors and ears hair-bundle16, or Jeune syndrome caused by dysfunction of ciliary transport porteins causing skeletal dysplasia17,18.

The impact of cilia dysfunction on disease presentation depends on the type of cilia affected. Genetic variants affecting the function of primary cilia often cause multiorgan involvement and developmental abnormalities19. Symptoms associated with PCD and motile cilia dysfunction often cause significant sinopulmonary disease, neonatal respiratory distress, recurrent otitis media, laterality defects, and subfertility (Table). It should be noted that disease presentation in PCD is affected by the causative genes. For instance, not all PCD related genes are associated with laterality defects, particularly if the gene variant cause a cilia rotational movement. Moreover, although it is estimated that about 80% of males and 60% of females with PCD are subfertile20, not all PCD variants are associated with subfertility, and some genes may be associated with subfertility without significant respiratory symptoms (Table).

Motile cilia are complex organelles composed of hundreds of proteins21. A cilium is made of a microtubular skeleton extending from the apical surface of the cell and anchored by a modified centriole called a basal body22. Each cilium is composed of 9 microtubular doublets and an additional central microbular pair (central apparatus) (Figure)23,24. Attached to the microtubular outer 9 doublets are outer and inner dynein arm complexes (ODA and IDA respectively), which are ATP-dependent molecular motors composed of several heavy, intermidiate, and light chain dyneins25. The ODA generate the force required for cilia motility. The IDA together with the radial spokes and dynein regulatory complex proteins modulate that force26,27. Nodal cilia within the embryonic node, which is a transient structures that emerges during development, lack the central apparatus and thus have a rotational waveform7. Primary cilia have a similar ultrastructure but lacks the ODA and IDA, as well as the central pair. Primary cilia may also express specialized receptors and proteins that are improtant for their unique tissue specific function10.

Motile cilia ultrastructural organization is tightly controlled with many structures repeating every 96 nm and 24 nm intervals28. This repetition is dictated by specialized proteins, including the heterodimers CCDC39 and CCDC40, which function as addresses for a cilia connectome of over 90 axonemal structures within the cilia axoneme29. This periodicity may be dictated by other address proteins as occurs for microtubular inner proteins28.

There may be tissue unique differences in motile cilia. Multiciliated cilia in the airways for instance may differ in their length, density, and beat frequency between the nasal, tracheal, and lower airways. These differences are in part driven by differences in the expression of regulatory factors between the proximal and distal airways30,31. Similarly, ependymal multiciliated cells in the brain ventricles have longer cilia and a lower cilia density than those in the airways, which likely reflect the unique cilia functional requirements in these different organs32,33. Moreover, the sperm tail which is a form of motile cilia has unique proteins that are not expressed in airway cilia, including the heavy dynein chains DNAH8 and DNAH1734. Therefore, pathogenic variants affecting DNAH8 and DNAH17 may cause male infertility without respiratory symptoms. While those affecting genes not expressed in the sperm, such as CCDC114, may cause PCD without male infertility20,35.

PCD is often inherited as an autosomal recessive condition, though cases of x-linked and autosomal dominant inheritance have been described36,37. Pathogenic variants in over 60 genes have been linked to PCD. Many of these genes encode proteins involved in cilia biogenesis, assembly, or structure (Figure). It is estimated that motile cilia are composed of more than 500 proteins21,38, therefore it is likely that the number of genes associated with PCD will increase as more individuals are screened and better genetic tools are employed.

Expert opinion estimates that 70 to 80% of clinically diagnosed cases of PCD can be identified with a genetic tool39. Those with clinical symptoms highly suggestive of PCD, yet no identifiable genetic variant, likely harbor a change in intronic regions, in genes encoding uncharacterized proteins, or in proteins that have not been linked to cilia. It is important to note however, that individuals with other conditions such as immunodeficiency can have symptoms overlapping with PCD and therefore it is of great importance to rule out these conditions in any patient with PCD symptoms but no clear genetic diagnosis40,41.

Variants in genes associated with a cilia structural protein often result in specific ultrastructural defect that can be identified using electron microscopy or cilia dysmotility that is evident on high-speed video microscopy (Table). This includes genes associated with the ODA and IDA, radial spokes, dynein regulator complex, address proteins or central pair apparatus. However, increasingly, disease-causing variants in cilia structural components are identified that do not cause clear ultrastructural defect and result only in a subtle change in the waveform which reflects the limitation of some of the diagnostic tools used in PCD.

DNAH5 encodes a heavy dynein arm protein within the ODA, and was the first gene identified as causative of PCD42,43. Pathogenic variants in DNAH5 often cause absence or truncation of the ODA and immotile or flickering cilia. Similarly, variants in DNAI1 and DNAI2 which encode intermediate dynein chains in the ODA are associated with a similar phenotype44,45. It is estimated that variants in DNAH5 and DNAI1 account for 30% of PCD cases and collectively may be the largest subgroup of PCD2. However, not all variants in ODA related genes cause similar phenotypes.

Variants in DNAH11 and DNAH9, encoding ODA dynein heavy chains, have near normal cilia ultrastructure46–49. DNAH11 pathogenic variants often result in hyperkinetic cilia beat patterns, while DNAH9 variants are often associated with near normal beat patterns. These differences stem from the distribution of these proteins within the ciliary axoneme and their role in generating force49,50. While DNAH5 is expressed along the entire cilia length and is the major force generating dynein within the cilia, DNAH11 and DNAH9 are expressed only in the proximal and distal parts of the cilia respectively and help in establishing the proper waveform pattern. Moreover, within this group of ODA defects, the clinical phenotype can vary. Whereas variants in DNAH5, DNAI1 and DNAH11 are associated with classical forms of PCD, variants in DNAH9 are often associate with milder disease expression48.

Variants in genes associated with radial spoke proteins or the central pair apparatus are not associated with laterality defects51,52. Pathogenic variants in this category of genes are often associated with a rotational waveform patten. Nodal cilia do not have a central pair and thus these variants do not affect their function. Individuals with variants in radial spoke proteins, especially RSPH1, may have milder lung disease and less middle ear involvement53,54.

HYDIN encodes a key component of the cilia central apparatus. Pathogenic variants in HYDIN result in loss of the c2b projection and impaired cilia motility. These structural abnormalities are subtle and are often missed on electron microscopy evaluation of cilia cross sections55. Genetic diagnosis is often challenging as during evolution, the HYDIN gene was duplicated, resulting in HYDIN 2, with over 98% homology to HYDIN56. Therefore, traditional genetic tools including short-read next-generation sequencing (NGS) or PCR based sequencing often are unable to distinguish between variants in HYDIN and HYDIN 2, leading to many clinical genetic labs not reporting these variants. Identifying variants in HYDIN 2 may require the use of advanced tools such as long-read sequencing or non-genetic tools such as immunofluorescent analysis57,58. It is estimated that variants in HYDIN account for 7% of PCD cases, and possibly higher in specific populations58,59.

The ciliary axoneme is highly organized with specific periodicity along the axoneme including 96nm for the outer doublets, 48n for the microtubular inner proteins, and 32nm for the central pair28. This periodicity is essential for maintaining cilia function. The CCDC39 and CCDC40 genes encode a heterodimer that form a coiled-coil repeat along the length of the axoneme and function as addresses and recognition sites for over 90 cilia structural protein29. Pathogenic variants in these genes cause a unique structural phenotype of microtubular disorganization and absence of the inner dynein arms60,61. PCD patients due to variants in these genes are often described as having more pronounced disease manifestations and faster progression in lung disease compared to other PCD patients2,62. This is presumed to be caused by the significant non-motility dysfunction in the cilia and airway epithelium, including increased expression of mucus cells in the airways, loss of the protective periciliary layer, and increased mucus plugging29.

Cilia is a complex organelle and studies performed in model organisms revealed than many of the structures within motile cilia are assembled in the cytoplasm prior to transport into the axoneme28,63–65. Variants in at least 15 genes that encode cytoplasmic proteins have been identified in individuals with PCD (Figure). These proteins are required for the assembly of the dynein arm complexes in the cytoplasm and variants in them result in absence of both the outer and inner dynein arms. The exact function of these cytoplasmic assembly proteins during the assembly process continues to be investigated, however several interact with each other and localize within specialized structures that are called dynein assembly particles (DyNAPs)66,67. These cytoplasmic DyNAPs possess liquid-phase separated properties67,68. The assembly of the ODA and IDA complexes may occur in separate partitions of the DyNAPs or even separate organelles68.

Ciliogenesis requires a tightly regulated process by which progenitor cells undergo fate determination and differentiation into ciliated cells. This program is controlled by several transcription factors including TP73, FOXJ1, RFX1, and RFX269,70. Many of which have been identified in individuals with PCD. Patients with variants in transcription factors often have few or no motile cilia resulting in a sub-type of PCD that is often termed ciliary aplasia or reduced generation of multiple motile cilia (RGMC) disease71,72. Similarly, RGMC is linked to pathogenic variants in CCNO which encodes a cyclin protein that is essential for the generation of multiple cilia73. These patients often present with a severe form of PCD and may develop hydrocephalus due to the role of motile cilia in regulating cerebrospinal fluid flow6.

Motile cilia are anchored to the apical cell surface by a basal body. In multiciliated cells, basal bodies and cilia maintain directionality through a series of proteins at the base of cilia and around the basal bodies which link these structures to the cell cytoskeleton74,75. Polarity within the cells is influenced by expression of unique planer polarity proteins at opposite ends of the cell which in turn aid in remodeling the cytoskeleton76.

GAS2L2 encodes a protein that anchors the basal bodies to the cytoskeleton. PCD patients with variants in GAS2L2, have disorganized cilia with normal structure on electron microscopy and minimal changes in the cilia waveform, making diagnosis challenging77. This loss of orientation causes abnormal mucociliary clearance and PCD77.

It should be emphasized that not all cases of PCD are inherited as autosomal recessive conditions. PIH1D3 which encodes a cilia assembly factor, is located on the X chromosome78. Variants in RPGR which are associated with X-linked retinitis pigmentosa can also cause PCD36,79.

De novo variants in the transcription factor FOXJ1 have been implicated with autosomal dominant forms of PCD, often associated with hydrocephalus72. Recognizing these inheritance modes as associated with PCD is important, as it has implications for genetic counseling and for segregation studies.

Ciliopathies are often divided into primary ciliopathy, due to dysfunction of the primary (Sensory) cilia, and motile ciliopathies (such as PCD) caused by dysfunction of motile cilia. However, these two organelles have many structural and functional similarities80.

Several patients with PCD have been identified as having symptoms associated with primary ciliopathy, often due to variants in genes that are essential for both structures. These conditions are often termed combined ciliopathies. An example of which are variants in the gene TUBB4B, which encodes a βtubulin isotype crucial for microtubule formation in both primary and motile cilia. Variants in TUBB4B were first identified in individuals with inherited forms of blindness and deafness due to dysfunction of the cones and rods (sensory cilia) and sensory cilia in the hearing apparatus81. Some variants however also affect the stability of motile cilia causing reduced numbers of functioning motile cilia, while other variants cause a combined ciliopathy with patients developing both PCD and early-onset sensorineural hearing loss and retinal degeneration82.

Variants in the intraflagellar transport protein IFT74, are associated with several primary ciliopathies including Joubert syndrome and skeletal dysplasia83,84. Some variants also affect motile cilia causing sinopulmonary disease in addition to skeletal dysplasia83. Recognizing these overlapping syndromes is important as it can have an impact on the clinical management of these patients.

Motile cilia dysfunction was also suggested in other primary ciliopathies including the polycystic kidney diseases, due to variants in PKD1 and PKD285, retinitis pigmentosa due to variants in the RPGR gene36, and in oral-facial-digital syndrome type 1 and Joubert syndrome caused by variants in OFD186. It is likely that other overlap syndromes will be identified as features of ciliopathies are better characterized.

PCD diagnosis requires the use of clinical criteria and several diagnostic tools87. Recent European Respiratory Society (ERS)/American Thoracic Society (ATS) guidelines outline the general approach to PCD diagnosis87. In short, patients suspected of PCD based on clinical symptoms (including two or more of the following: laterality defects, bronchiectasis, neonatal respiratory distress, lifelong chronic cough, upper and lower airway symptoms), or as part of idiopathic bronchiectasis evaluation88, or based on ATS and ERS clinical criteria89,90 should be referred for evaluation. Several tools are available to aid in the diagnosis of PCD, including genetic testing, high-speed video microscopy, immunofluorescent staining, nasal nitric oxide measurement, and electron microscopy87. To confirm the diagnosis, identification of the genetic variants or if unavailable, confirmation of a class-I structural defect on electron microscopy is required87. It is important to know that electron microscopy can miss up to 30% of genetically confirmed PCD cases91. A genetic diagnosis is of great value as it has significant implications on management, prognosis, and potential eligibility for novel therapy. Moreover, PCD symptoms can overlap with other conditions, including immunodeficiency, making a genetic diagnosis critical to provide the proper treatment.

The choice of the genetic tool is important. Gene Panel based testing may miss PCD diagnosis if the variant is in a gene not included on the panel. A comprehensive approach such as whole exome sequencing or whole genome sequencing is more likely to identify pathogenic variants in individuals with clinical symptoms highly suspected for PCD. However, even comprehensive tools have limitations. For instance, analysis performed using long-read sequencing is more likely to identify splice variants and differentiate variants in HYDIN from HYDIN 2 pseudogene compared to traditional next-gen methods58. Furthermore, although more than 60 genes are associated with PCD, not all genes causative of disease have been identified, and individuals may have a variant in an uncharacterized gene.

Here it should be emphasized that not all variants identified in a gene associated with PCD are disease causing. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) classifies variants as benign (or likely benign) and pathogenic (or likely pathogenic) based on a set of molecular and supportive criteria92. Variants that do not fit these classifications are termed variants of unknown significance (VUS) and require further evidence for pathogenicity.

VUS are increasingly being identified in patients evaluated for PCD. This is often the case in genes related to the dynein heavy chains, due to the relatively large size of these genes. These VUS may complicate the diagnostic pathway for PCD, since identifying two VUS or 1 VUS and 1 pathogenic (or likely pathogenic) variant in an individual does not confirm the diagnosis of PCD. In these cases, ancillary studies including the use electron microscopy, nasal nitric oxide measurements, immunofluorescent staining, or high-speed video microscopy are essential to substantiate the diagnosis. The recent diagnostic guidelines for PCD propose the use of the term PCD highly likely for those individuals with symptoms suggestive of PCD as well as ancillary studies supporting PCD diagnosis, but VUS on genetic testing87.

Working groups like ClinGen (https://clinicalgenome.org/) continuously evaluate additional supportive data that emerges over time for variants pathogenicity, which allow reclassification of VUS to likely pathogenic or pathogenic as new evidence emerges. Once the VUS is reclassified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic, then a formal PCD diagnosis can be given87. An individual who is classified as PCD highly likely or suspected PCD should be managed as PCD pending further diagnostic evaluation as delaying treatment may cause long term morbidity.

Primary ciliary dyskinesia is a rare inherited disease of motile cilia. PCD is caused by over 60 genes, some of which are rare founder variants that are only common in specific ethnic groups. Recognizing symptoms associated with PCD and establishing a genetic diagnosis leads to early initiation of proper treatment and help differentiate PCD from other conditions including immunodeficiency.
