INTRODUCTION: Dieulafoy's lesion, a rare but life-threatening condition 
accounting for a small percentage of acute gastrointestinal bleeding cases, has 
historically posed diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.
CASE PRESENTATION: In this article, we present two cases that required surgical 
intervention due to unsuccessful attempts with endoscopy. Case 1 involved a 
40-year-old patient with a history of treated duodenal ulcers, while Case 2 
featured a 74-year-old woman with no notable medical history. Both patients 
exhibited severe bleeding, necessitating urgent surgical procedures. The 
surgical approach involved wide gastrotomy, careful inspection, and successful 
suturing of the bleeding vessel.
DISCUSSION: Dieulafoy lesions, discovered by French surgeon Georges Dieulafoy in 
1885, constitute 1-2 % of acute gastrointestinal bleeding cases. These anomalies 
involve enlarged submucosal arteries, predominantly in the stomach, but 
occurrences in other sites are documented. Endoscopic methods, surpassing 
surgical intervention, are preferred for treatment, boasting success rates over 
90 %. Surgical measures become a last resort for uncontrolled bleeding, with 
laparoscopic surgery emerging as a minimally invasive alternative, facilitated 
by various intra-operative localization techniques. Laparoscopic wedge 
resection, in particular, exhibits lower re-bleeding rates than traditional 
oversewing methods, although feasibility depends on lesion location.
CONCLUSION: While endoscopic methods are preferred, surgery remains a vital 
option when bleeding persists or endoscopic intervention fails. This report 
highlights the significance of surgical management in selected cases of 
Dieulafoy's lesion.

Over time, the management approach for Dieulafoy's lesion has undergone significant advancements. In the past, when endoscopy was not yet available, radical surgeries like subtotal or total gastrectomies were commonly employed, despite their associated high morbidity rates [1]. However, with the advent of endoscopy, the management of bleeding from Dieulafoy's lesion has predominantly shifted towards this less invasive procedure, yielding superior outcomes. Consequently, the role of surgery has been considerably diminished [2], although it still retains its significance in cases of refractory bleeding and uncontrollable massive bleeding. In this report, we present two cases of Dieulafoy's lesion that necessitated surgical intervention due to unsuccessful attempts with endoscopy.

This work was reported in line with the SCARE guidelines [3].

A 40-year-old patient was admitted to the intensive care unit for severe upper gastrointestinal bleeding manifested as hematemesis. He had a history of a treated duodenal ulcer and had been taking acetylsalicylic acid for joint pain for a few days.

Upon admission, the patient was pale and tachycardic, with a blood pressure of 80/50, necessitating the use of catecholamines. The hemoglobin level was 5 g/L. Given the patient's medical history, a recurrent bleeding episode from the duodenal ulcer was suspected. Endoscopic hemostasis was not feasible because there was massive bleeding and the source of hemorrhage couldn't be identified despite the presence of a scarred ulcer in the duodenum with no signs of hemorrhage.

A wide gastrotomy was performed. After removing multiple blood clots, careful inspection of the gastric mucosa revealed a visibly protruding vessel in the fundus with diffuse bleeding. The postoperative period was uneventful, and the patient was discharged from the hospital on the 8th day after surgery. He was doing well at 1-year follow-up.

A 74-year-old woman was admitted to the hospital due to melena accompanied by severe anemia. He had no notable medical history and denies the use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.

Upon admission, the clinical examination revealed paleness of the skin and mucous membranes, stable hemodynamic status, a soft abdomen, and the presence of dark-colored stool during the rectal examination. Laboratory tests showed microcytic hypochromic anemia with a hemoglobin level of 7 g/dL, a platelet count of 150,000/mm3, a prothrombin level of 85 %, and normal liver and kidney function.

Upper endoscopy was conducted revealing no source of hemorrhage in the stomach and the duodenum. 3 units of packed red blood cells were administered. The hemoglobin level was 9.5 g/dL.

24 h later, the patient presented massive hematemesis. And hemoglobin level dropped to 6 g/dL.

Upper endoscopy revealed a visible vessel in the subcardial region with a blood clot treated with a hemoclip. The patient received two additional units of packed red blood cells. However, the patient had a second episode of hematemesis a few hours later along with signs of hypovolemic shock, including a blood pressure of 70/40 mmHg, a heart rate of 150 bpm, sweating, and a decrease in hemoglobin level to 4.5 g/dL. This required a transfusion of 4 units of packed red blood cells, a macromolecule infusion, and the administration of catecholamines.

An urgent surgery was decided, and during the operation, after gastrotomy, the extraction of several blood clots revealed active bleeding from a small erosion in the subcardial region, suggestive of a diagnosis of Dieulafoy's ulcer (Fig. 2) treated by suturing. The Dieulafoy lesion is a critical condition that poses a significant risk to life, resulting in substantial morbidity and mortality. It accounts for approximately 1 % to 2 % of cases of acute gastrointestinal bleeding [4]. Discovered by the esteemed French surgeon Georges Dieulafoy in 1885 [5], this lesion consists of an enlarged, anomalous submucosal artery that breaches the epithelium, leading to severe and recurrent bleeding. Although these lesions are typically found in the stomach, there have been documented instances in the medical literature of extra-gastric Dieulafoy lesions [6]. Notably, one case report highlighted the presence of two synchronous lesions in the stomach and jejunum of a 15-year-old patient, which is an uncommon presentation given that these lesions predominantly affect the elderly population [7].

Dieulafoy lesions are commonly situated in the proximal lesser curvature of the stomach, accounting for at least 71 % of reported cases [4]. However, they have also been observed in other locations such as the esophagus, duodenum, jejunum, ileum, colon, rectum, and anal canal. Among these alternative locations, the duodenum and colon rank as the second and third most frequent sites, respectively [8]. While bleeding from Dieulafoy lesions is typically self-limiting, it often recurs and can be severe enough to endanger the patient's life. Diagnostic challenges during endoscopy arise due to the small size of the lesion, its relatively inaccessible location, the absence of ulcers, and the presence of blood and blood clots.

The typical morphological characteristics of a Dieulafoy lesion involve a large-diameter vascular stump (generally 1 to 3 mm) protruding through a small erosion (2 to 5 mm) in the mucosa, which is usually covered in clots. This erosion may exhibit pulsatile bleeding or present as a jet or intermittent bleeding. In the past, surgical resection, such as gastrotomy and wide-wedge resection or gastrectomy, was the primary treatment for Dieulafoy's lesions. However, advancements in endoscopic procedures have now superseded surgical intervention.

Endoscopic methods are the preferred treatment for easily accessible lesions, with reported success rates exceeding 90 % [4,8,9]. Endoscopic hemostatic procedures can be categorized into three groups: 1) thermal-electrocoagulation, heat probe coagulation, and argon plasma coagulation; 2) regional injection, including local epinephrine injection and sclerotherapy; and 3) mechanical techniques like banding and hemoclip application. Each technique has its own advantages and disadvantages in terms of hemostatic efficacy and technical aspects, leading to varying success rates. Evidence in the literature suggests that mechanical hemostatic methods during endoscopy are more effective compared to injection or thermal treatments [10]. Additionally, combined endoscopic therapies have shown lower re-bleeding rates compared to endoscopic monotherapy [2].

Surgery is considered a last resort for patients with uncontrolled bleeding from Dieulafoy lesions or when the bleeding site cannot be identified via endoscopy, or when bleeding persists despite multiple endoscopic treatments. Salvage surgery has been required in 3 % to 16 % of patients in the published series [2]. Surgical procedures currently employed include oversewing of the lesion or wedge resection of the affected section. In our cases, suturing effectively controlled the bleeding, with no recurrence in the long term.

Some authors suggest that surgical resection remains preferable. Surgical oversewing of the vessel is associated with a higher risk of recurrent bleeding, and wedge resection of the affected segment may be a better surgical option for patients with refractory bleeding [1,11]. Wide gastrotomy is recommended to remove clots and provide a clear view of the gastric mucosa. In our cases, a large gastroduodenotomy was made for better exposure of the mucosa and extraction of blood clots. If the bleeding point is not evident, wiping the mucosa with a dry gauze may induce hemorrhage and reveal the lesion. Careful palpation of the stomach can sometimes detect the thickened blood vessels and clots associated with the Dieulafoy lesion. It is important to note that vagotomy is not necessary, and “blind” resection or subtotal gastrectomy is not recommended, as the lesion may not be included in the resected part, and massive recurrent bleeding has been reported after such surgeries [1]. The reported mortality rate following surgical intervention remains high, up to 33 % [11].

While most patients requiring surgery have traditionally undergone laparotomy, laparoscopic surgery has recently been described for the removal of these lesions [12]. Laparoscopic surgery presents an attractive option for treating Dieulafoy lesions, as it offers a curative approach with minimal invasiveness for the patient. However, successful laparoscopic resection relies on accurate localization of the bleeding site [13]. Several case reports in the literature describe successful laparoscopic wedge resection of bleeding Dieulafoy lesions in the stomach following pre-operative or intra-operative localization [12,14].

Accurate intra-operative localization of these lesions can be challenging. Various methods have been proposed to ensure precise localization. One approach involves intra-operative endoscopy, which allows real-time visualization and localization of the bleeding lesion. After pneumoperitoneum induction, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy is performed. The laparoscopic light is dimmed or turned off, and the gastroscope is used to identify and mark the bleeding lesion with clips or sutures. Subsequently, resection is performed [12]. Mixter et al. [15] described a combined endoscopy and laparoscopy approach to identify and ligate the artery supplying the bleeding Dieulafoy lesion, without the need for resection.

Laparoscopic wedge resection has been reported to have a lower re-bleeding rate compared to oversewing the Dieulafoy lesion (36). However, wedge resection may not be feasible for lesions located within 6 cm of the gastroesophageal junction. In such cases, oversewing the lesion or laparoscopic clip placement on the vessel supplying the lesion may be a better option. Alva et al. [12] described pre-operative localization of the lesion using tattooing with India ink and clips, which allows for accurate localization and obviates the need for intra-operative endoscopy. Concerns about clip displacement before surgical intervention have not been reported, and the use of clips for pre-operative localization of the lesion has not been associated with interference with stapling devices or disruption of the staple line during wedge resection.

Dieulafoy's lesion should be included in the differential diagnosis of obscure gastrointestinal bleeding even though it accounts for less than 2 % of all cases of acute GI bleeding. Full awareness of this disease and careful endoscopy are essential for diagnosis. Repeat endoscopies may be necessary to establish the diagnosis. There is no doubt that the primary management of Dieulafoy's lesion is endoscopic. However, surgery is the only option for patients who fail endoscopic management.
