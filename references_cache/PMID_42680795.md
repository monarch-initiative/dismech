# Musculoskeletal symptoms and misdiagnoses among children with neuroblastoma.
**Authors:** Bregnhøj SN, Amstrup J, Jensen CF, Henriksen KA, Nissen KB, Harideep C, Brix N
**Journal:** Pediatr Res (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41390-026-05268-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41390-026-05268-x)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Neuroblastoma often presents with non-specific signs and symptoms, including musculoskeletal (MS) symptoms, potentially leading to misdiagnoses. This study aimed to identify, characterise, and compare children with neuroblastoma who had prior MS misdiagnoses to those without, analysing clinical presentation, diagnostic interval, and outcome.

METHODS: We performed a nationwide, historic cohort including all children under 15 years of age diagnosed with neuroblastoma in Denmark from 1996 to 2018. The patients were identified using the Danish Childhood Cancer Registry, and data were collected through a medical chart review.

RESULTS: Out of 143 children diagnosed with neuroblastoma included in this study, MS symptoms occurred in 30% and misdiagnoses in 12%, mainly as reactive arthritis. The group with MS misdiagnosis more commonly presented with back pain, arthralgia, and more advanced disease with a higher rate of stage 4 at diagnosis, bone metastases, relapse, and increased mortality. The group with MS misdiagnosis had an increased diagnostic interval, primarily due to hospital delays. After adjusting for age and disease stage, no significant difference in survival was observed between the children with or without MS misdiagnosis.

CONCLUSION: MS misdiagnoses occur in 12% of childhood neuroblastoma with back pain and arthralgia as potential red flags.

IMPACT: This study is the first nationwide, population-based cohort to quantify musculoskeletal misdiagnoses in children with neuroblastoma. We show that 12% of patients were initially misdiagnosed, most often with reactive arthritis, leading to longer diagnostic delays and more advanced disease at presentation. The findings add to existing literature by identifying specific clinical red flags-back pain and arthralgia-that should prompt consideration of malignancy. Early use of abdominal ultrasound in children with unexplained musculoskeletal symptoms may help reduce diagnostic delay and improve care.

INTRODUCTION
Neuroblastoma is the predominant extracranial solid tumour in
children, accounting for 8 –10% of all paediatric cancers. 1–4
Neuroblastoma can originate from anywhere along the sympa-
thetic nervous system, leading to a diverse range of clinical
manifestations depending on the primary tumour location, the
extent of metastatic spread, and associated paraneoplastic
syndromes. The most common presentations include an abdom-
inal mass and musculoskeletal (MS) pain but may also include a
broad spectrum of clinical manifestations such as Horner ’s
syndrome, raccoon eyes, or opsoclonus.
1,2,5,6
Approximately 50% of patients with neuroblastoma present
with localised disease, with most cases being asymptomatic and
often identi ﬁed during evaluations for unrelated medical
conditions.2 The remaining 50% of patients show signs of
metastases, most commonly affecting the MS system, particularly
the bone or bone marrow. 2,6 Consequently, there is a potential risk
of misdiagnosis, as metastatic neuroblastoma-related MS symp-
toms can be mistaken for common rheumatic disorders, such as
juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA), osteomyelitis, reactive arthritis, or
spondyloarthropathies.
7–10 In the absence of MS symptoms,
diagnosis becomes even more challenging, with nonspeci ﬁc
presentations limited to fever and ambiguous laboratory
ﬁndings.11
Despite notable improvements in survival rates for paediatric
cancer patients, neuroblastoma remains a major contributor to
childhood cancer mortality, accounting for 15% of these cases.
2

Although neuroblastoma presenting with MS symptoms is well-

Our group recently conducted a nationwide registry-based

The aim of this national historical cohort study was to identify
and characterise the subgroup of children with neuroblastoma
presenting with MS misdiagnosis and evaluate any patterns or
“red ﬂags”. Additionally, we aimed to compare the clinical
presentation, diagnostic interval, and survival between those with
and without MS misdiagnoses.
METHODS
We performed a nationwide historical cohort study including all
consecutive paediatric patients below 15 years of age diagnosed with a
neuroblastoma between January 1, 1996, and December 31, 2018. The
cohort was identi ﬁed from a nationwide (population of 5.8 million)
registry-based cohort study, previously described in detail.
7 The capture of
clinical data and registry coverage was complete for children under the
age of 15. However, during most of the study period, registration of
adolescents aged 15 –17 was not captured consistently. As such, inclusion
was restricted to children aged 0 –14 to ensure comparability in the cohort.
The cohort was identi ﬁed using The Danish Childhood Cancer Registry
(DCCR).
20 The DCCR includes all cases classi ﬁed as neoplasms according to
the International Classi ﬁcation of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD-10). This
included the following neuroblastoma diagnoses: malignant neoplasm of
peripheral nerves and autonomic nervous system (C47.0 –C47.9), malignant
neoplasm in medulla of adrenal gland (C74.1), malignant neoplasm in
adrenal gland, unspeci ﬁed (C74.9), malignant neoplasm in aortic body and
other paraganglia (C75.5). Three additional ICD-10 codes were included to
identify potentially misclassi ﬁed neuroblastoma cases: malignant
neoplasm in scapula and long bones of upper limb (C40.0), benign
neoplasm of peripheral nerves and autonomic nervous system (D36.1),
neoplasm of uncertain or unknown behaviour in liver, gallbladder, and bile
ducts (D37.6).
The DCCR is linked to the Danish National Patient Registry, the National
Pathology Registry, and the Danish Cancer Registry using the unique
national identi ﬁcation number assigned to all permanent residents in
Denmark at birth or immigration.
The inclusion criteria were all children in Denmark with a neuroblastoma
diagnosis from 1996 to 2018. The exclusion criteria were the following:
children above 15 years of age at diagnosis, primary treatment abroad,
severe comorbidities, and very rare subtypes of neuroblastoma that differ
substantially regarding treatment or presentation, when compared to the
remaining cohort.
Data were collected through systematic chart reviews, and in addition to
MS misdiagnoses, the following information was collected: age, sex, date
of diagnosis, neuroblastoma staging (according to the International
Neuroblastoma Staging System, prognostic markers (N-MYC ampli ﬁcation,
1p36 deletion, and 17q21 gain), treatment (radiotherapy, chemotherapy,
surgery, bone marrow transplant, etc.), comorbidities, non-MS misdiag-
noses (misdiagnoses not related to MS), mistreatment (any treatment not
recognised as treatment for neuroblastoma), sequelae related to cancer or
its treatment (hearing loss, dental issues, etc.), recurrence, and outcome.
The patient’s medical history leading up to their cancer diagnosis was also
collected with an emphasis on MS symptoms, other presenting symptoms,
objective ﬁndings, and diagnostic intervals. Additionally, imaging, and
laboratory ﬁndings were also collected. A standardised de ﬁnition of the
diagnostic delay, also referred to as time intervals, was applied.
21 These
included the period from the initial symptom until the start of treatment,
illustrated in Fig. 1.
In most cases, the date of diagnosis was recorded as the day microscopy
results from a biopsy con ﬁrmed neuroblastoma. If no biopsy was
performed, or if the patient ’s condition required immediate intervention,
the date of diagnosis was noted as the ﬁrst time either an expert physician
conﬁrmed the diagnosis based on auxiliary investigations, or the date on
which speci ﬁc neuroblastoma treatment began, whichever occurred ﬁrst.

(CI) and illustrated with mean and standard deviation (SD). Time intervals
were non-normally distributed and compared using Mann Whitney U-tests
and presented as median with interquartile range (IQR), and range.

RESULTS
A total of 192 patients below the age of 15 were diagnosed with
neuroblastoma in Denmark from January 1, 1996, to December 31,
2018, according to data from DCCR. Of these, 24 patients were
excluded due to misclassi ﬁcation, as they were not diagnosed
with neuroblastoma. An additional 25 patients were excluded
based on other exclusion criteria (Fig. 2). Therefore, a total of 143
paediatric patients were included in the ﬁnal analysis.
Among the 143 included patients, 43 (30%) presented with MS
symptoms prior to diagnosis, and 17 (12%) had at least one MS
misdiagnosis. Two children had two MS misdiagnoses, for a total
of 19 misdiagnoses. The most common were reactive arthritis,
accounting for 11/19 (58%) and osteomyelitis accounting for 3/19
(16%). The remaining MS misdiagnoses occurred only once each
and included MS pain, infectious myositis, pyogenic arthritis,
discitis, and spondylitis.
The demographic and cancer characteristics of the cohort are
summarised in Table 1, comparing children with neuroblastoma
who had at least one MS misdiagnosis (MS group) and those
without (no-MS group). The MS group was signi ﬁcantly older
(mean 46.2 months, SD 29.6) compared to the no-MS group (mean
23.2 months, SD 30.1, MVR 2.2, 95% CI: 1.2 –3.2). Stage 4 disease
was the most common stage in both groups, and signi ﬁcantly
more prevalent in the MS group (88% vs 47%, PR = 7.0 (95% CI
1.7–29.6)). Bone metastases were the most common metastasis
and were also more prevalent in the MS group (88% vs. 67%, PR
8.5 (2.0 –36.0)).
Treatment regimens were largely similar between the groups,
except for radical surgery. In cases where surgery was performed,
radical surgery was signi ﬁcantly more likely in the MS group (92%
vs 55%, PR = 7.8, 95% CI: 1.0 –58.8). Cancer relapse occurred more
frequently in the MS group (65% vs. 25%, PR = 4.3, 95% CI
1.7–10.9), as did cancer-related deaths (65% vs 26%, PR = 4.1, 95%
CI: 1.6 –10.5). No signi ﬁcant differences were found between the
groups in terms of sex, histological prognostic markers ( MYCN
ampliﬁcation, 1p deletion, 17q gain), comorbidities, non-MS
misdiagnosis, or sequelae.

Diagnostic time intervals
The total diagnostic interval, de ﬁned as the time from the ﬁrst
primary care visit to diagnosis, was signi ﬁcantly longer in the MS
group, with a median of 45 days (IQR 18;79) compared to 20 days
(IQR 9;37) in the no-MS group ( p = 0.02), as shown in Table 3 and
in Fig. 3. This difference was primarily driven by a prolonged
secondary care interval and ﬁrst doctor interval in the MS group,
with a median of 32 days (IQR 17;49) and 16 days (IQR 10;36),
respectively, compared to 15 days (IQR 9;33) and 2 days (IQR 0;12)
in the no-MS group (both p < 0.01). The parental, primary care,
specialist, and treatment intervals did not differ signi ﬁcantly
between the two groups (Table 3, Fig. 3).
Survival
Among the 17 patients with an MS misdiagnosis, 65% (11/17) died
due to cancer or its treatment, compared to 26% (33/126) in the
no-MS group. The MS group had a lower 5-year survival rate of 47
(95% CI:23.0 –68.0) compared to 76% (95% CI:67.482.5) in the no-
MS group (log rank, p = 0.003). Initially, overall survival was
signiﬁcantly lower in the MS group (hazard ratio = 2.8, 95% CI:
1.4–5.4). However, after adjusting for age at diagnosis and cancer
stage, the survival difference was no longer signi ﬁcant (hazard
ratio = 1.1, 95% CI: 0.6 –2.3).

Comparison of the patients with MS-symptoms versus those
without
Finally, we compared the group with MS symptoms ( n = 43) to
those without any MS symptoms ( n = 100) regarding both
demographics, clinical presentation, diagnostic time intervals,
and survival. The observed trends were largely consistent with
those found when comparing MS misdiagnoses versus no
misdiagnoses, though often less pronounced. Patients with MS
symptoms were signi ﬁcantly more likely to present with stage 4
disease, bone metastases, cancer recurrence, and cancer-related
death. Additionally, they more frequently exhibited general
symptoms (72% vs. 52%, PR = 1.9, 95% CI: 1.1 –3.3), and CNS
symptoms 30% vs. 11%, PR = 2.2, 95% CI: 1.4 –3.5) compared to
those without MS symptoms. No signi ﬁcant differences were
observed in the prevalence of abnormal laboratory values
between the groups.
Diagnostic time intervals
Consistent with the ﬁndings in the MS misdiagnosis comparison,
the MS symptom group had a longer total diagnostic interval, with
a median of 35 days (IQR 17.5;49.5) compared to 18 days (IQR
8;37.5) in the no-MS symptom group ( p = 0.03). This difference
was primarily driven by a prolonged ﬁrst hospital doctor interval,
with a median of 11 days (IQR 2;26)) in the MS symptom group
compared to one day (IQR 0;9) in the no-MS symptom group
(p < 0.01).
Survival
The group with MS symptoms had signi ﬁcantly lower survival
rates, with one- and ﬁve-year survival of 88% (95% CI: 74 –95) and
60% (95% CI: 44 –73), respectively, compared to 95% (95% CI:
88–98) and 77% (95% CI: 68 –85) in the group with no MS
symptoms (hazard ratio = 2.4, 95% CI: 1.3 –4.4). However, after
adjusting for age and stage, this difference was no longer
statistically signi ﬁcant (hazard ratio = 1.3, 95% CI: 0.7 –2.3).
DISCUSSION
Misdiagnosis of neuroblastoma as MS conditions occurred in a
meaningful proportion of children and was associated with
prolonged diagnostic intervals and more advanced disease at
presentation. Back pain and arthralgia emerged as potential red
ﬂags that may contribute to diagnostic delay. Although children
initially misdiagnosed had worse unadjusted survival, this
difference was no longer evident after accounting for age and
stage, suggesting that advanced disease rather than the
misdiagnosis itself may explain the observed survival disparity.
These ﬁndings highlight the need for heightened clinical vigilance
when evaluating persistent MS symptoms in children.
Back pain was the most strongly associated symptom with
misdiagnoses. This ﬁnding is particularly relevant, as previous
studies have shown that back pain — especially in prepubescent
children— is highly correlated with malignancies.
10,15,18 Civino
et al. 10 conducted a multicentre, cross-sectional study comparing
various childhood malignancies, including 109 children diagnosed
with neuroblastoma, and found that arthralgia was signi ﬁcantly
associated with malignancy, with neuroblastoma identi ﬁed as the
malignancy most likely to be misdiagnosed. Our ﬁndings are
consistent with these results, underscoring the importance of
cautious evaluation by clinicians when diagnosing arthralgia or
back pain in children.
Although VMA, HVA, and LDH are commonly used biomarkers
in the evaluation of neuroblastoma,
10,22 none distinguished
children with MS misdiagnoses from those without in our cohort.
Interpretation of these results must be cautious, as laboratory data
were incomplete for a substantial proportion of patients. Missing
values may have reduced statistical power and introduced
information bias, particularly as abnormal results seemed to be
more consistently documented than normal ﬁndings. Conse-
quently, the absence of signi ﬁcant associations should not be
interpreted as evidence that laboratory markers lack clinical
relevance, but rather that their role in the context of misdiagnosis
could not be robustly evaluated within this dataset.
The median total diagnostic interval of 1 –1.4 months aligned
with previous studies.
12–14 The prolonged diagnostic interval was
primarily driven by extended hospital-based evaluation, suggest-
ing that diagnostic uncertainty persisted even after referral to
secondary care. A similar but less pronounced pattern was
observed among children presenting with MS symptoms overall.
Importantly, after adjusting for confounding factors, MS
misdiagnosis was not associated with increased mortality. This
suggests that while the diagnostic delays were signi ﬁcant, they
did not independently affect survival — a ﬁnding consistent with
previous research on acute leukaemia with MS involvement.
23–25
However, prolonged diagnostics have been associated with a
higher prevalence of stage 4 disease in neuroblastoma as reported
by Parise et al. 19 Regardless of the impact on survival, timely
diagnosis remains critical for ensuring optimal clinical conditions
at treatment initiation, reducing psychological distress, and
maintaining trust between families and healthcare providers.
25
Notably, many of the children misdiagnosed with inﬂammatory MS
conditions lacked radiological ﬁndings consistent with such diag-
noses, such as intra-articular ﬂuid accumulation,26 raising concern
about over-reliance on clinical impression without imaging conﬁrma-
tion. Previous studies 16–18 have demonstrated that children later
diagnosed with neuroblastoma are frequently misclassiﬁed as having
MS conditions despite lacking radiological conﬁrmation. This pattern
suggests that diagnostic anchoring may occur even when imaging
does not support an MS aetiology. Ourﬁndings reinforce this concern

A major strength of this study is its nationwide, population-
based design, which includes all children diagnosed with
neuroblastoma in Denmark over a 23-year period. The use of
the DCCR, a robust and well-regarded database, further enhances
data reliability.
20 Additionally, Denmark ’s universal healthcare
system minimises socioeconomic disparities, ensuring more
equitable access to healthcare. However, limitations must be
acknowledged. The retrospective nature of the study introduces
potential information bias, as data were extracted from medical
records with no possibility of clarifying missing information.
Symptom duration and parental recall may be prone to error,
particular in cases with long diagnostic intervals. There is also a
potential for selection bias, as medical records were more likely to
be complete for patients who lived longer, given digitalisation
trends in hospital documentation. Furthermore, our study lacks
data from primary care settings, likely underestimating the
number of preliminary MS diagnoses and missing insights into
the parental and primary care intervals.

CONCLUSION
MS misdiagnoses occurred in 12% of children with neuroblastoma,
most commonly as reactive arthritis. This subgroup frequently had
advanced disease and experienced a diagnostic delay more than
twice as long as those without MS misdiagnosis. Clinicians should
maintain a high index of suspicion when evaluating children with
MS symptoms, particularly those with back pain and arthralgia. In
ambiguous cases, when initial imaging does not support an MS
diagnosis, further investigation, including abdominal ultrasound, is
essential to rule out malignancy.
