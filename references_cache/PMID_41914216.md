# Novel Variants in KMT2C Further Support a Neurodevelopmental Disorder Distinct From Kleefstra and Kabuki Syndromes.
**Authors:** Sedláčková L, Brožková DŠ, Havlovicová M, Laššuthová P
**Journal:** Mol Genet Genomic Med (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mgg3.70209](https://doi.org/10.1002/mgg3.70209)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Heterozygous loss-of-function variants in the KMT2C gene were only recently recognized as a cause of neurodevelopmental disorder distinct from Kleefstra and Kabuki 1 syndromes. KMT2C-related neurodevelopmental disorder is characterized by developmental delay, intellectual disability, behavioral and psychiatric problems, seizures, craniofacial dysmorphism, and other comorbidities. Here we describe detailed phenotypes of three additional patients with novel variants in the KMT2C gene.

METHODS AND RESULTS: Using exome sequencing, we found two heterozygous variants c.3212G>A, p.(Trp1071*) and c.13204_13205delinsA, p.(Cys4402Ilefs*11) leading to premature stop codon and one heterozygous missense variant c.6517C>T, p.(Pro2173Ser) in the KMT2C gene (NM_170606.3) in patients with unrecognized neurodevelopmental disorder. Our patients suffer from developmental delay, speech delay, autism spectrum disorder, cognitive impairment, seizures, and/or craniofacial dysmorphism.

CONCLUSION: After detailed phenotype analysis and by comparison with previous reports, we found that our patients have very similar phenotypes as described before and thus further extend the list of KMT2C causal variants. Therefore, we believe that they fit into these previously described phenotypes of KMT2C-related Kleefstra syndrome 2 as well.

Recently, the clinical phenotype associated with de novo variants in the KMT2C gene (OMIM *606833) has been defined as intellectual disability (ID), language and motor delay, and autism spectrum disorder (ASD). The condition was defined as Kleefstra syndrome 2 (OMIM # 617768). These individuals showed clinical overlap with Kleefstra syndrome 1 (OMIM # 610253) and other related neurodevelopmental disorders (NDD) (Koemans et al. 2017). Kleefstra 1, Kleefstra 2, and Kabuki 1 syndromes were described as three different autosomal dominant disorders caused by variants in the genes EHMT1, KMT2C, and KMT2D, respectively. Kleefstra syndrome is a clinically recognizable syndrome characterized by microcephaly, ID, hypotonia, and seizures. Facial features include a dish‐shaped midfacial profile, arched eyebrows, synophrys, hypertelorism, a short nose, anteverted nostrils, a cap mouth, a protruding tongue, and micrognathia (Firth and Hurst 2017). Other frequent anomalies are cardiac abnormalities, cryptorchidism, and hypospadias (Iwakoshi et al. 2004). Kabuki syndrome is characterized by facial dysmorphism with long palpebral fissures, eversion of the lateral one‐third of the lower lid, and arched eyebrows that are sparser laterally. Postnatal growth retardation, skeletal anomalies with joint laxity, mild to moderate ID, and persistent fetal finger pads are present in most individuals. Diagnosis is not usually made until the characteristic facial appearance at about 12 months (Firth and Hurst 2017) but otherwise, it is also easily recognizable in most cases.

Despite some clinical overlap, it is currently impossible to objectively compare the frequencies of specific features among these conditions (Rots et al. 2024). Kleefstra 1 and Kabuki 1 syndromes are clinically well known and well recognized, and most of the published individuals are diagnosed through a phenotype‐first approach (Willemsen et al. 2012; Schott et al. 2016). However, only the most typical affected individuals can be described by clinical examination, and identifying non‐specific and/or mildly affected individuals is only possible after a genetic examination and determination of the causal gene variant. On the other hand, KMT2C‐related Kleefstra syndrome 2 is not clinically recognizable, and the so far‐described patients were diagnosed through a genotype‐first approach (Rots et al. 2024). Rots et al. (2024) further confirmed that heterozygous loss‐of‐function KMT2C variants and rare missense variants in functional domains result in a disorder characterized by variable combinations of developmental delay, intellectual disability, short stature, congenital anomalies, craniofacial dysmorphism, and recurrent infections, and they indicated haploinsufficiency as a likely mechanism of the disorder. They also demonstrated that the KMT2C‐related NDD (Kleefstra syndrome 2) is clinically and epigenetically distinct from EHMT1‐related Kleefstra syndrome 1 and KMT2D‐related Kabuki syndrome 1 (OMIM # 147920).

In our work, we describe three patients with novel variants in the KMT2C gene with very similar phenotypes of NDD as described previously, thus further extending the list of causal KMT2C variants.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital Motol and Second Faculty of Medicine, Charles University in Prague, ID: EK‐151.24/25. The legal representatives of the patients received genetic counseling as part of the clinical care provided before and after genetic testing.

Patients were referred to our laboratory from the Department of Biology and Medical Genetics, Second Faculty of Medicine, Charles University in Prague and Motol University Hospital, where they were consulted by the specialist in clinical genetics. In our laboratory, patients were screened for ASD or epilepsy by exome sequencing (ES) and evaluation of a virtual panel of genes associated with ASD or monogenic epilepsy (EPI) as requested by the clinical geneticist.

A DNA library was prepared from the patient's DNA sample and sequenced in an outsourced laboratory (Macrogen Inc., Korea) using SureSelect XT v.6 on Illumina's HiSeq6000 or prepared in the laboratory using the TWIST Human Comprehensive Exome Panel (Twist Bioscience HQ, San Francisco, CA, USA) and sequenced on Illumina's NovaSeq 6000 System (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Data were processed from the provided FASTQ files in our laboratory following the GATK‐DRAGEN workflow for calling germline SNVs and indels and the GATK‐SV workflow for CNV variants. The list of single nucleotide variants and indels was then filtered. Priority was given to rare variants [i.e., variants with a frequency of less than 1% in public databases such as gnomAD (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org)], fitted to a de novo or autosomal recessive inheritance model, and located in genes previously associated with ASD, epilepsy or neurological phenotypes. The virtual panels for the diagnosis of ASD and EPI were analyzed first, prior to the analysis of the ES data. The list of genes in each panel is shown in Table S1. In addition, other genetic examinations were performed prior to ES, based on the decision of the clinical geneticist. These included conventional karyotyping, molecular karyotyping with arrayCGH, fragile X syndrome testing, or metabolic screening. If the cause of the disorder remained undetermined after all of these tests, the patients were eligible for performing ES. The variants of interest were validated by Sanger sequencing in the patient and both parents as well. The variant nomenclature is consistent with the HGVS nomenclature guidelines (den Dunnen et al. 2016).

As it was not possible to clearly identify a known clinical syndrome in our patients, and there was only a suspicion of syndromic disease based on the features of the current phenotype, genetic testing was necessary. The first two patients were referred for genetic testing on the basis of a diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder, developmental delay, and speech delay. The third patient was referred on the basis of a diagnosis of epilepsy and developmental delay. Subsequently, based on the results of the genetic examination and comparison with previously described patients in the literature, we were able to determine a genetic diagnosis.

After detailed data analysis of the ASD/EPI virtual panel/ES, two pathogenic premature stop variants and one missense variant of uncertain significance in the KMT2C gene (NM_170606.3, GenBank ID: 58508) were found to be suspicious in our patients and were taken into further consideration. The variants in the first two patients, c.3212G>A, p.(Trp1071*) and c.13204_13205delinsA, p.(Cys4402Ilefs*11), respectively, were confirmed in the heterozygous state in the patient by Sanger sequencing and were not present in the healthy parents, so they probably arose de novo. Correct parentity was tested using six microsatellite markers from three different chromosomes. The variant c.6517C>T, p.(Pro2173Ser) described in the third patient was inherited from the healthy parent. The variant was confirmed to be heterozygous in both of them by Sanger sequencing.

In silico tool predicts a pathogenic outcome for the variant p.(Trp1071*) according to GeneBe database (Stawinski and Ploski 2024) and the variation shows a highly conserved nucleotide (PhyloP score 7.90). Variant p.(Cys4402Ilefs*11) causes a frameshift, missense change involving the alteration of a conserved nucleotide (PhyloP score 9.32). In silico tool for the missense variant p.(Pro2173Ser) predicts a benign outcome according to GeneBe database and the variation shows a moderately conserved nucleotide (PhyloP score 4.75). AlphaMissense in silico prediction tool classifies it as likely benign (0.117). Both of the stop variants were classified according to ACMG classification as pathogenic (used criteria PVS1 and PM2 according to GeneBe database). The missense variant is classified as uncertain significance (used criteria PM2 and BP4 according to GeneBe database); however, if the variant segregation is added to the evaluation, the classification changes to likely benign.

All three variants were not present in the gnomAD v.4.1.0 population database, and were not present in the HGMD Professional database v.2025.4 (www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk). To the best of our knowledge, the variants have not been previously published. The criteria used to classify the variants and determine the pathogenicity class were in accordance with the ACMG classification (Richards et al. 2015). None of the other variants from the ES evaluation were found to completely resolve the patients' phenotype.

We could only determine the cause of the disorder on the basis of genetic diagnosis since none of the patients exhibited symptoms typical of the individual syndromes described above.

No suspicion of any of the aforementioned syndromes was expressed at first sight. For completeness, the comparison of all three syndromes is summarized in Table S2 using Clinical synopsis data from the OMIM database (https://www.omim.org).

Our first patient with de novo p.(Trp1071*) variant, a 3‐year‐old boy, was born from high‐risk pregnancy by cesarean section. The high‐risk pregnancy was due to precollapsed maternal conditions, fetal tachycardia repeatedly detected on the tococardiogram (around 210 at 7th month) and diabetes, which was resolved by adjusting the diet. The early neonatal period was unremarkable, but there was feeding difficulties, which were resolved by artificial nutrition. Early development was normal until 9 months of age, then there was an apparent gross motor delay, he also had an atypical feet posture when standing or walking. He started to walk without support at almost 3 years of age. He also had a speech delay with the ability to say only a few simple words at 14 months. After this period, speech regression was observed. He was diagnosed with ASD at the age of 18 months. MRI scan showed ventriculomegaly. He has subtle craniofacial dysmorphism—down‐slanted palpebral fissures, deep‐set eyes, wider nasal bridge, long and prominent eyelashes a thin vermillion of upper lips. He has increased weakness in the muscles around his mouth, and he does not chew his food, but eats mashed pieces. Follow‐up outcome, education, therapy response, daily functioning: Follow‐up is limited due to a young age of the patient. With regular occupational therapy he is beginning to imitate activities. He repeats syllables. Episodes of sound hypersensitivity and tearfulness occur intermittently. Social interactions during therapy and with unfamiliar individuals are adequate, whereas interactions with certain family members trigger negative reactions (e.g., asking his grandparents to leave). According to his mother, the most disturbing symptoms of autism are that he cannot stand it when his mother wears a different jacket or has a different hairstyle. He sleeps relatively well. He is curious and can play by himself—he draws.

The second patient with de novo p.(Cys4402Ilefs*11), a 9‐year‐old boy, was born by cesarean section because of the previous macrosomic fetus. After birth, artificial respiratory support was necessary for 24 h. Later there was a suspicion of intracranial bleeding. Early development was normal; he had no gross motor delay. He started to use first words between 12 and 18 months; however, he stopped speaking at all at around 2.5 years of age. Nowadays, he uses some simple words but prefers to show. He repeats some words after his parents without understanding the meaning. He has been diagnosed with ASD, having the classical triad of impairment (social interaction impairments, communication impairments, and restricted and repetitive behaviors). He is also likely to have cognitive impairment. He has subtle craniofacial dysmorphism (hypertelorism, anteverted nares, short philtrum, wide root of the nose, narrow palpebral fissures).

Follow‐up outcome, education, therapy response, daily functioning: Now he repeats words more often, exhibits echolalia, and sings simple songs. A care plan was established as follows: due to the clarification of the diagnosis to Kleefstra, we requested the following additions: heart echocardiography, abdominal ultrasound, including kidney congenital developmental defects, hearing test, and ophthalmologic examination. Results of those requested examinations are not yet available.

The third patient with the inherited p.(Pro2173Ser) variant, a 20‐year‐old woman, had no pre‐ or perinatal risks, her neonatal period was unremarkable. Development was delayed from the beginning of unsupported walking at 19 months, first words at 12 months and short sentences at 3 years. She has been followed‐up from the age of 7.5 years for atypical autism, moderate intellectual disability with behavioral disorder and focal epilepsy. At the age of 5, she started to suffer from absences without EEG findings. At the age of 8, she got antiseizure therapy after increased seizure frequency that correlated with EEG findings. She had some seizures over the next few years and after the withdrawal of the treatment at 18 years of age, focal bilateral tonic–clonic seizures occurred. The patient also has eufunctional autoimmune thyroiditis, currently untreated. Her facies was described as full cheeks, larger mouth, fleshy lips, smaller chin and teeth requiring orthodontic correction. Follow‐up outcome, education, therapy response, daily functioning: She attends a special school. She knows the alphabet, writes in block letters, can read five‐letter words, perform simple arithmetic operations up to 10, and count to 20. She speaks in phrases, often repeating what she has heard, but her comprehension is very limited. She has not been seriously ill in the last 3 years, but has been hospitalized three times in connection with seizures. Her current antiseizure medications include lamotrigine, cenobamate, and brivaracetam. Cardiological and sonographic examinations of the abdomen were without pathological findings.

Several neurodevelopmental phenotypes in the majority of the individuals were described including developmental delay, ID, ASD and craniofacial dysmorphism and defined as KMT2C‐related Kleefstra syndrome 2 (Koemans et al. 2017; Rots et al. 2024; Faundes et al. 2018). Here we present three additional cases of patients with neurodevelopmental disorders who have developmental delay and ASD as their main features and therefore we believe that they fit into this previously described phenotype of Kleefstra syndrome 2. However, the dysmorphic features described in 90% of so far published cases were observed in only one of our patients. Nevertheless, the dysmorphic features were described as subtle and non‐specific; it is also possible that they were not yet fully expressed in our first patient. As haploinsufficiency of the KMT2C gene was only recently recognized as a cause of neurodevelopmental disorder (Koemans et al. 2017), the pathogenic premature stop variants found in our patients strongly match this loss‐of‐function mechanism model. However, caution should be taken into account in the case of the third patient with epilepsy who inherited a variant classified as likely benign on the basis of ACMG classification, variant segregation as well as in silico prediction tools. Moreover, the variant is not located in any of the functional KMT2C domains (UniProt ID Q8NEZ4). Although Rots et al. (2024) also described inherited missense variants, the parents in that study were mildly affected, unlike the healthy parent of our patient. They also reported that missense variants should be taken with caution as the clinical classification of KMT2C non‐truncating variants is challenging. Many of the described missense or splice site variants were classified as benign and were confirmed negative on the DNA methylation signature even including some de novo variants (Rots et al. 2024). In addition, protein‐altering variants that disrupt functional domains are more likely to be pathogenic or likely pathogenic, which this variant does not fulfill. Based on the above characteristics, despite the patient having a similar phenotype to others, the cause may lie in a gene that has not yet been linked to the disease or in unexamined regions of known genes, such as non‐coding or regulatory regions. Therefore, it would be appropriate to continue searching for another cause of the disease in this case.

Despite some clinical and molecular overlap, KMT2C‐related NDD, Kleefstra and Kabuki 1 syndromes are clearly distinct conditions (Rots et al. 2024). Some of our patients exhibit phenotype characteristics identical to those of Kleefstra 1 and Kabuki 1 syndromes, such as ID and developmental delay (individual 1, 2, and 3) or behavioral abnormalities and obsessive‐compulsive disorder (individual 3). However, they have no distinctive facial dysmorphism except for individual 3. On the contrary, autistic features described in all three individuals do not occur in Kleefstra 1 or Kabuki 1 syndromes. Therefore, it is not possible to assign these syndromes to our patients based solely on phenotype. Moreover, most of the symptoms found in our patients also occur in other neurodevelopmental disorders.

Such similar patients will apparently increase over time with the identification of non‐specific and/or mildly affected individuals using a genotype‐first approach as in our report. The study by Rots et al. (2024) also showed lower penetrance and highly variable expressivity in their cohort of patients and so the individuals with few manifestations can be expected. Our report showed two patients obviously carrying a KMT2C causal variant with subtle craniofacial dysmorphism or other common features such as short stature, congenital anomalies, or recurrent infection. Their findings also indicate haploinsufficiency as the likely mechanism underlying KMT2C‐related NDD. As two of our patients have a stop variant and a frameshift indel variant with a premature stop codon, respectively, and moreover fit the defined phenotype, these variants are likely to be causal. However, the missense variant in our third patient is likely benign and exome data should be used to search for another genetic explanation for the patient's condition.

To the best of our knowledge and, in comparison with published phenotypes and genotypes, we add two novel causal variants with detailed phenotypic descriptions to the spectrum of KMT2C‐related NDD patients. As it was shown in the current and already published reports, Kleefstra syndrome 2 is not clinically recognizable and a diagnosis can only be made after genetic testing. Moreover, the KMT2C patient cohort can also include patients with variable non‐specific neurodevelopmental features and with subtle/non‐specific craniofacial dysmorphism.
