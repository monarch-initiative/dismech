Primary ciliary dyskinesia is a rare monogenic syndrome that is associated with
chronic respiratory disease, infertility, and laterality defects. Although more
than 50 genes causative of primary ciliary dyskinesia have been identified,
variants in the genes encoding coiled-coil domain-containing 39 (CCDC39) and
CCDC40 in particular cause severe disease that is not explained by loss of
ciliary motility alone. Here, we sought to understand the consequences of these
variants on cellular functions beyond impaired motility. We used human cells
with pathogenic variants in CCDC39 and CCDC40, Chlamydomonas reinhardtii
genetics, cryo-electron microscopy, and proteomics to define perturbations in
ciliary assembly and cilia stability, as well as multiple motility-independent
pathways. Analysis of proteomics of cilia from patient cells identified that the
absence of the axonemal CCDC39/CCDC40 heterodimer resulted in the loss of a
network of more than 90 ciliary structural proteins, including 14 that were
defined as ciliary address recognition proteins, which provide docking for the
missing structures. The absence of the network impaired microtubule
architecture, activated cell quality control pathways, switched multiciliated
cell fate to mucus-producing cells and resulted in a defective periciliary
barrier. In CCDC39 variant cells, these phenotypes were reversed through
expression of a normal CCDC39 transgene. These findings indicate that the
CCDC39/CCDC40 heterodimer functions as a scaffold to support the assembly of an
extensive network of ciliary proteins, whose loss results in both
motility-dependent and motility-independent phenotypes that may explain the
severity of disease. Gene therapy might be a potential treatment option to be
explored in future studies.

Fluid propulsion by motile cilia is an essential function during development at the embryonic node and for fluid flow in the brain, reproductive, and respiratory tracts (1). These tasks are accomplished by molecular motors with companion regulatory complexes docked along the length of the cilia’s microtubule skeleton, the axoneme (2). Insufficient cilia motor function in humans results in respiratory tract infection, male infertility, and organ laterality alterations, which are hallmarks of the genetic disease primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) (3). Pathogenic variants in over 50 genes cause PCD (4, 5), and their phenotypes provide powerful tools for understanding cilia biology (6). Cilia genes with PCD variants encode proteins of the dynein arms, the radial spokes (RS), nexin dynein regulatory complex (N-DRC) or central pair apparatus (7, 8). About 25% of patients with PCD have pathogenic variants in Coiled-coil domain-containing 39 (CCDC39) and CCDC40 (5, 9, 10). These patients exhibit some of the most severe disease phenotypes as indicated by decreased lung function and increased mucus plugging of airways, as compared to individuals with variants in other PCD genes (5, 11, 12). Current dogma suggests that PCD is caused by ciliary dysmotility, however this concept does not explain the range of patient phenotypes. Thus, a major gap exists in our understanding how variants in different genes impact multiciliated cells.

PCD variants link ciliary structures with genes, but do not inform the mechanisms of ciliary assembly of the major structures. Seminal studies from Chlamydomonas reinhardtii using freeze-fracture electron microscopy (EM) showed a 96-nm repeating pattern of these structures along the ciliary axoneme (13). Recent single particle cryo-EM of cilia provided exquisite detail that allowed the identification of proteins and their position along the doublet microtubules (DMTs) (14–16). In parallel, advances in proteomics and genomics provided a catalog of motile ciliary components and pathogenic gene variants (17). These complementary discovery tools moved the understanding of cilia biology and human disease forward.

CCDC39 and CCDC40 form a heterodimer (CCDC39/40) that repeats every 96-nm along the length of the DMT (14). The heterodimer directly contacts most of the structures within the 96-nm repeating unit (15, 18). CCDC39/40 was suggested to be a molecular ruler for the positioning of the nexin dynein regulatory complex and inner dynein arms (19). The ruler model proposes that CCDC39/40 provides a measure of specific distances along the microtubule to direct the placement of these structures. An alternative model is that CCDC39/40 provides specific sites or “addresses” to dock ciliary complexes.

In this study, we aimed to identify how loss of CCDC39/40 leads to severe lung disease. A proteomic analysis of primary airway epithelial cells from a patient with a pathogenic CCDC39 variant revealed the loss of over 90 ciliary proteins that formed a large network that was anchored at addresses on CCDC39/40. The loss of the ciliary protein network resulted in the absence of major structures including the inner dynein arms, radial spokes, and nexin dynein regulatory complex. This loss caused an unexpected cytoplasmic burden of unanchored proteins that led to short cilia, altered motility, loss of the periciliary barrier, cell stress, and increased numbers of mucous cells. Lentivirus-mediated delivery of a CCDC39 transgene to basal cells with a CCDC39 disease-causing variant rescued these phenotypes. These findings provide insights into the motility-dependent and -independent impacts of pathogenic CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants on patients with PCD.

To understand the function of the human CCDC39/40 heterodimer and its impact on ciliary structure (Fig. 1, A and B), we obtained freshly isolated, primary airway epithelial cells from the nasal upper and lower respiratory tracts of individuals without lung disease (normal, n=30) and from patients with biallelic pathogenic variants in CCDC39 (n=5) or CCDC40 (n=4). Most variants were frameshift mutations that likely result in nonsense-mediated decay of the RNA (table S1). To avoid secondary insults on cilia due to an inflamed airway environment in PCD, we isolated and expanded the airway basal progenitor cells in culture with medium supplemented with antimicrobial drugs, then differentiated cells using the air-liquid interface (ALI) condition to generate multiciliated cells (Fig. 1C). The onset of CCDC39 and CCDC40 expression during ALI differentiation coincides with the expression of the cilia transcription factor Forkhead box protein J1 (FOXJ1) (Fig. 1D). The specificity of antibodies to CCDC39 and CCDC40 was validated in cultured cells obtained from individuals with pathogenic CCDC39 or CCDC40 variants by immunofluorescence and immunoblot analysis (Fig. 1, E and F). In the variants studied, we found that the unaffected partner protein was markedly reduced in cell lysates, although it could be detected by immunoblot analysis following long exposure (Fig. 1F, fig. S1, A and B). CCDC39 protein was absent in the cytoplasm of the Chlamydomonas reinhardtii ccdc40 mutant (Fig. 1G), suggesting that formation of the heterodimer enhances stability in this mutant. Functional analysis of cilia in human CCDC39 and CCDC40 variant cultures showed that most were immotile or flickered (Fig. 1H and Movies S1 and S2). The surface transport of microbeads by variant cilia was markedly decreased, suggestive of deficient in vivo mucociliary clearance (Fig. 1I; fig. S1, C and D; Movies S3 to S5.).

We previously observed that cilia of Chlamydomonas with null alleles in ccdc39 and ccdc40 were short with ciliary splaying (20). Cilia with human CCDC39 or CCDC40 variants were about half the length of normal, when measured intact on the surface of cultured cells or when scraped from cultures and streaked on a glass slide. Immunostaining using an anti-tubulin antibody revealed splaying in some cilia isolated from variants ( Fig. 1, J and K). Previous TEM imaging of human variant axonemes in cross-section showed the loss of organization of the nine DMTs (9, 10). TEM imaging of the length of the cilia showed splayed microtubules (Fig. 1L), which were not previously described in PCD patient samples. Analysis of TEM images of Chlamydomonas with null alleles in ccdc39 and ccdc40 revealed other ultrastructural defects including misplaced DMTs and open B-tubules at the inner junction (Fig. 1, M and N), indicating the extensive loss of DMT integrity.

To determine how loss of CCDC39 or CCDC40 may lead to ciliary splaying and potentially instability, we used single particle cryo-EM of cilia from wild-type Chlamydomonas cells. We previously reported that CCDC39/40 was restricted to the groove between protofilaments A02 and A03, adjacent to inner dynein arms, radial spokes, and the nexin dynein regulatory complex (Fig. 1O) (14) but we were unable to resolve the C-terminus. With improved cryo-EM data processing and 3D classification, we have now resolved the last ~30 residues of the C-termini of CCDC39/40 near the inner junction (Fig. 1P). CCDC39/40 extends as a hook from the A- to the B-tubule, forming a 4-helices bundle that agrees well with the AlphaFold prediction (Fig. 1Q). The importance of the A-to-B tubule extension was illustrated by analysis of a CCDC39 disease-causing frameshift variant (WU152) that is predicted to truncate the last 32 amino acids and shows persistence of CCDC39 and CCDC40 protein (fig. S1B). The individual carrying this mutation has clinically severe PCD; cilia beat frequency was reduced and immunostaining showed reduced expression of CCD39 and CCDC40 (fig. S1, B, E and F), suggesting an essential role for the CCDC39 C-terminal structure. A temperature-sensitive mutation in ccdc39 (L845P) is also within this hook region (Fig. 1Q) and causes loss of the heterodimer at the restrictive temperature but reduced function at intermediate temperatures (20). Taken together, these results suggest that the loss of CCDC39/40 function accounts for a unique defect in ciliary structure.

Previous analyses of Chlamydomonas cilia in ccdc39 and ccdc40 (null) mutants identified deficiencies in inner dynein arm, nexin regulatory complex and tektin (19, 20). Other proteins were preserved, although studies were limited by antibody availability (20). To take an unbiased approach to axonemal content in the absence of CCDC39/40, we isolated cilia from human ALI cultures from healthy donors and a patient with a CCDC39 variant. Cilia obtained from ALI preparations of cells from three normal donors identified 440 proteins, which were compared to proteomes of cilia from a CCDC39 variant individual (Fig. 2, A and B).

Proteomics of cilia from the individual carrying the CCDC39 variant showed loss of more than 90 proteins (Fig. 2C, table S2). Outer dynein arms were retained, but many known structural proteins were lost or present at reduced abundance. All the inner dynein arm proteins were missing (Fig 2B; fig. S2A). Nine of the 12 nexin dynein regulatory complex proteins were missing, although Dynein regulatory complex 5, 6, 12 (DRC5, DRC6, DRC12) proteins were also not detected in samples of normal cilia (Fig 2C; fig. S2B). Cilia and flagella associated protein 73 (CFAP73 ) and Cilia and flagella associated protein 100 (CFAP100), which regulate the normal waveform (21), were missing. Many radial spoke proteins were missing or reduced in abundance, as were several proteins within the central apparatus, consistent with the loss of this structure in cross-sections of the ccdc39 or ccdc40 variants (Fig. 1, L to N). Intraflagellar transport proteins were the only ciliary complex that is increased (Fig. 2C). There were 22 proteins with unknown ciliary locations and roles that were missing or reduced in abundance in cilia from the individual carrying the CCDC39 variant. Most of these proteins with unknown roles were transcribed primarily in multiciliated tissues or testes (22).

Inspection of single particle cryo-EM reconstructions showed that 13 of the missing proteins were in direct contact with the CCDC39/40 heterodimer (Fig. 2C) (14–16, 18, 23–34). Each of the 13 missing proteins, as well as MORN3 (MORN repeat containing protein 3), which was not present in the cilia of our control samples, is linked to a major cilia complex (Fig. 2D). We refer to these 14 proteins as ciliary address recognition proteins (CARPs). The CARPs are schematically represented to indicate their position relative to ciliary structures (Fig. 2E).

Radial spokes act as mechanotransducers to control the ciliary waveform (18). Chlamydomonas has two complete radial spokes (RS1, RS2) and a shorter third radial spoke (RS3) structure, whereas humans have three complete radial spokes within each 96-nm repeat unit (Fig. 3A) (15, 18, 35). Each radial spoke contains unique proteins, as well as shared head and stalk proteins.

We found that radial spokes proteins were differentially depleted in the CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants. Cryo-EM analysis shows that each of the three radial spokes is anchored to CCDC39/40 by different CARPs (15). The loss of several RS2 and RS3 proteins in cilia from individuals carrying CCDC39 or CCDC40 variants was investigated by immunofluorescence, based on the availability of antibodies (Fig. 3B, fig. S2C). The previous proteomic analysis revealed that the RS2 and RS3 CARP, Cilia and flagella associated protein 91 ( CFAP91) was absent. The RS3-specific CARP, Coiled-coil containing domain 96 (CCDC96), was also absent and this was confirmed by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 3B, fig. S2C). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed the reduction of RS2 stalk protein Leucine rich repeat containing protein 23 (LRRC23) and RS3-specific proteins Cilia and flagella associated protein 61 (CFAP61) and Leucine rich repeat containing protein 34 (LRRC34). Radial spoke head protein 3 (RSPH3), a protein shared among the radial spokes, was present in the cilia of normal and variant cells by immunostaining, consistent with the assembly of some radial spokes. We depleted CCDC96 with shRNA to assess its role for RS3 (Fig. 3C) CCDC96 shRNA resulted in loss of CCDC96 and a decrease in the surface transport of beads (fig. S2, C to F). CCDC39 and RSPH3 remained present (Fig. 3D). There was loss of both LRRC34 and LRRC23, suggesting that CCDC96 played a role in both RS2 and RS3 (Fig. 3D).

The RS1 CARP, IQ motif and ubiquitin domaining containing, (IQUB) was also diminished (Fig. 2C). Chlamydomonas was used to test the retention of RS1 and loss of RS2 in the ccdc39 null mutant. Analysis of longitudinal TEM images of ccdc39 showed loss of the periodicity and rigidity of the radial spokes (Fig. 3, E and F), as was suggested to occur in CCDC40 variants (15). Radial spoke protein 16 (RSP16), a radial spokehead protein in both radial spokes, was present in cilia isolated from both ccdc39 and the IQUB ortholog, flagella associated protein 253 (fap253), by immunoblot analysis, confirming that some radial spokes remained (Fig. 3G). However, immunoblots of cilia from the ccdc39; fap253 double mutant lacked RSP16 (Fig. 3G), indicating the complete loss of radial spokes and highlighting the important role of a CARP for radial spoke docking. Together, our observations suggest that CCDC39/40 provides unique addresses for the attachment of the radial spokes via designated CARPs.

We found that some microtubule inner proteins (MIPs) were decreased in variant cilia. MIPs are proteins within the lumen of the DMT and variants in some MIPs show sperm motility defects but only mild PCD phenotypes (36, 37). The proteomics of CCDC39 variant cilia showed that two of the four tektins (TEKT2 and TEKT4) were decreased compared to normal cilia (fig. S3A). Although it does not contact CCDC39/40, TEKT3 was absent in variant cilia, which was verified by immunofluorescence (fig. S3B). An inability to form TEKT3-TEKT2 and TETK3-TEKT4 heterodimers may result in the loss of TEKT2 and TEKT4 (15). In addition, two MIP proteins that might affect cilia architecture were missing from CCDC39 variant cilia (fig. S3A). Cilia and flagella associated protein 90 (CFAP90), localized at the inner junction (14) near the hook of CCDC39/40 (Fig. 1Q) and Cilia and flagella associated protein 141 (CFAP141), at the outer junction of DMT. Our Chlamydomonas cryo-EM analysis of the ccdc39 mutant cilia showed missing and misplaced MIPs (fig. 3SC), in contrast with previous structural analysis that found only loss of tektin in human CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants (15). The presence of other MIPs was verified by immunofluorescence staining (fig. S3D). Loss of tektin proteins could potentially contribute to abnormal ciliary function.

Our data suggested that the CCDC39/40 heterodimer creates a set of addresses for anchoring the CARPs on the ciliary axoneme. By this hypothesis, we predicted a temporal pattern of trafficking, whereby the heterodimer would enter the cilia early, followed by the CARPs, and lastly by those complexes that preassemble in the cytoplasm (38). Guided by stages of ciliogenesis (Fig. 4A), we examined trafficking of CCDC39/40 in normal and variant cells. During early stages of multiciliated cell differentiation marked by centriole amplification, CCDC39 and CCDC40 were present in the cytoplasm, but did not colocalize with basal bodies (Fig. 4B). Just before cilia assembly, CCDC39 and CCDC40 were enriched in the apical domain but infrequently colocalized (Fig. 4C). Expansion microscopy showed that CCDC39/40 began to colocalize at basal bodies after docking in newly assembled cilia (Fig. 4, C and D).

During normal cilia assembly, the RS3 CARP CCDC96 and the IDA CARP Cilia and flagella associated protein (CFAP57) were localized with CCDC40 in very short cilia (Fig. 4E). In contrast, non-CARPs (CFAP100, CFAP61) remained within the cytoplasm, near the basal bodies (Fig. 4, F and G). Localization of these proteins in normal multiciliated cells during early cilia assembly may identify a staging zone for later transport into the cilia. In later stages of ciliogenesis, network proteins (CFAP73, CFAP100, Myeloid leukemia factor 1 (MLF1)) were in cilia (Fig. 4, H). In normal cells, immunoprecipitation with CCDC40 pulled down network proteins CFAP100 and CFAP73 to a greater degree at late compared to early stages of cilia growth (Fig. 4I). In CCDC39 and CCDC40 variant cells, network proteins remained within the apical domain (Fig. 4H), a location similar to the assembly staging zone in normal early cilia (Fig. 4F). However, the unbound members were degraded (Fig. 4J).

To interrogate multiciliated cell dysfunction resulting from CCDC39/40 loss, we applied single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to identify differentially expressed genes and pathways. Transcriptomes were compared from normal (n=6) and variant (n=2 CCDC39, n=1 CCDC40) human cell cultures. Cell types were identified using unsupervised clustering and annotated with known airway epithelial markers (Fig. 5A) (39). All previously identified clusters of airway epithelial cells were identified in both normal and variant datasets including basal, secretory, ciliated, and ionocytes. Because CCDC39 and CCDC40 were primarily expressed in the cluster of mature multiciliated cells (Cil3), we have focused on this class (fig. S4A). In this cluster, over 1800 genes were differentially expressed in the variants compared to normal (Data file S1). Enrichment analysis of differential gene expression in multiciliated cells (Cil3) showed increases in ciliary, proteostasis, cell stress, oxidative phosphorylation, Notch signaling pathways (Fig. 5B, fig. S4, B to F). Genes coding for cilia proteins, as well as cilia assembly factors and FOXJ1, were upregulated (table S3, Data file S1), perhaps as a compensatory response. Genes upregulated by enrichment analysis included: (i) components of the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, proteasome subunits, and heat shock chaperone proteins that handle unfolded proteins and aggregates (fig. S4, B and E); (ii) cell stress response genes Serum amyloid A1, A2, and A4 (SAA1, SAA2, and SAA4), which encode secreted acute phase reactants in sterile inflammation (40) (Fig. 5B), (iii) mitochondrial complex I subunits of NADH ubiquinone oxidoreductase and complex V subunits of ATP synthase, indicating upregulation of ATP production (fig. S4C); and (iv) HES1 (Hairy and enhancer of split-1 transcription factor), a canonical target of Notch signaling that increases secretory cell differentiation and airway secretory cell proteins, was unique among the Notch pathway genes (41) (Fig. 5B). Together, these data indicated the presence of cellular stress in variant cells, possibly suggesting the need to degrade the large burden of undocked network proteins. We thus examined the proteasome components in variant cells (Fig. 5C). PSMB6 (Proteosome 20S subunit beta 6), a component of the 20S core subunit is normally located throughout the cytoplasm but was localized just beneath the cilia in variant cells (Fig. 5C). The 11S immunoproteasome lid proteins, PSME1 (proteasome activator subunit 1) and PSME2 (proteasome activator subunit 2), were similarly localized (Fig. 5C). The locations of the three proteins were similar to the location of CFAP100 in variant cells (Fig. 4G), suggesting a site-specific requirement for proteostasis (42).

In vivo, chronic airway inflammation is associated with increased numbers of Mucin 5AC (MUC5AC)-expressing mucous cells (also called goblet cells) in patients with asthma, cystic fibrosis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (43–45). Patients with PCD caused by CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants also have airway obstruction with mucus (12). Single cell RNA-seq of cultured variant cells revealed increased secretory (Sec1 and Sec2) populations compared to normal cells (Fig. 5D). In support of this finding, there is an abundance of mucous cells in tissue samples obtained from the airway of a patient with CCDC39 variants who underwent lung transplantation (Fig. 5E). There was also an increase in the Notch target gene, HES1 (Fig. 5B), which controls the balance of secretory and multiciliated cells in the airway (46). Therefore, we examined the production of MUC5AC in ALI-cultured cells.

Control cultures showed a high percentage of multiciliated cells, with few MUC5AC-expressing cells. In contrast, CCDC39 and CCDC40 variant cultures displayed significantly (P<0.001) more MUC5AC-expressing cells but reduced multiciliated cells (Fig. 5, F and G). Cultured cells from patients with PCD due to variants in DNAAF5 (Dynein axonemal assembly factor 5), DNAI1 (Dynein axonemal intermediate chain 1), or DNAH5 (Dynein axonemal heavy chain 5) had no greater number of MUC5AC-staining cells than cultures from normal cells (Fig. 5, F and G). A small number of cells expressing both MUC5AC and cilia proteins were present in the CCDC39 and CCDC40 variant cultures and may represent a transition state in cell differentiation (43) (Fig. 5H).

It is possible that the chronic inflammatory environment in the airways of patients with PCD from CCDC39 or CCDC40 variants may account for the increase in mucous cells in vitro, as has been observed in cultures of cells from patients with COPD (47). To test this, we depleted CCDC39 or CCDC40 from normal donor airway cells using shRNA (fig. S5, A to E). CCDC39- or CCDC40-depleted cultures also showed increased numbers of MUC5AC-expressing cells and fewer multiciliated cells than controls (Fig. 5, I to K)suggesting that the shift in the differentiation phenotype was not attributable to the effects of a chronically inflamed PCD airway. We also assayed cultures of inducible pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) that underwent CCDC40 or DNAH5 deletion by Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats and CRISPR-associated protein 9 (CRISPR-CAS9) editing in the parental cells and were then differentiated into airway basal cells (48), then cultured using ALI conditions. CCDC40-deficient cells showed more MUC5AC-expressing cells compared to those with DNAH5 deletion or the control iPSCs (Fig. 5, L and M; fig. S5F). Together these findings suggested that CCDC39 or CCDC40 patient cells or CRISPR-edited IPSCs were not altered by the environment. Thus, the shift in cell fate likely occurs by the transition of multiciliated to secretory cells via a genetic loss of CCDC39 or CCDC40. The resulting increase in mucous-producing cells is consistent with increased mucus plugging of airways observed by computed tomography imaging of patients with CCDC39/40 variants compared to other PCD variants (12).

To ask if the shift in differentiation from multiciliated to mucous cells in the CCDC39 and CCDC40 variant cultures was mediated by Notch signaling as suggested by upregulation of HES1, we inhibited Notch signaling with the γ-secretase inhibitor N-[N-(3,5-Difluorophenacetyl)-l-alanyl]-S-phenylglycine t-butyl ester (DAPT) (49). We delayed treatment of the CCDC39 and CCDC40 variant cultures until after ALI day 28, when MUC5AC-expressing secretory cells were increased. In cultures treated with DAPT for one week, the numbers of MUC5AC-expressing cells were markedly decreased and acetylated α-tubulin-expressing cells were increased compared to MUC5AC-expressing cells in vehicle-treated variant cultures (Fig. 5, N and O). These findings suggest that Notch signaling is required to shift the differentiation of the multiciliated variant cells to mucous cells and that continuous inhibition of Notch signaling blocks the switch from multiciliated to mucous cells and prevents the establishment of new mucous cells. We found no effect of treatment with an anti-inflammatory agent (dexamethasone), or an antioxidant (catalase) (fig. S5, G and H). As expected, the CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants remained immotile after treatment (fig. S5I). Taken together, CCDC39/40 deficiency resulted in loss of ciliary proteins and activated Notch activity that altered the maintenance of multiciliated cell differentiation.

The cilia are also an intrinsic component of the periciliary barrier, which is defined as containing a brush-like polymer of tethered mucins (Mucins 1, 4, 16; MUC1, MUC4, MUC16) that surround the cilia and act as a selective filter to particle entry (44, 50). One feature of the CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants that we identified was ciliary splaying (Fig. 1, L to N). To better visualize DMT splaying, we used scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Cells were either untreated or treated with detergent-containing buffer that disrupts the ciliary membrane (Fig. 6A). Normal cilia exhibited intact DMTs in both conditions. Cilia from CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants showed DMT splaying, particularly at the distal ends in the untreated condition and further unraveling of the DMT with detergent treatment (Fig. 6, A to C). Cells were then scraped from cultures and streaked on a glass slide. Normal cilia showed little splaying regardless of treatment, but at least 15% of variant cilia showed splaying with no treatment, and about 50% showed splaying when the membrane was removed.

To determine the effect of the respiratory tract environment, we examined the extent of splaying of free cilia present in freshly obtained nasal brushings (Fig. 6D). Although the median number of splayed cilia in freshly obtained and cultured samples was similar (Fig. 6, C and E), untreated, fresh samples from individuals with variants were significantly more variable in their splaying phenotype (F=2.70, P=0.03) (Fig. 6, D and E), suggesting that the respiratory tract environment may destabilize the ciliary membrane.

Lastly, we investigated how the abnormal ciliary architecture in variant cells affects the periciliary barrier. The periciliary barrier relies on both intact cilia and inter-ciliary tethered mucins (44, 50). MUC4 is present within the ciliary region of normal and DNAH5 variant cells but absent in the periciliary layer of cells with CCDC39 variants (Fig. 6, F and G). The mucins of the periciliary layer are bound with glycosaminoglycan keratan sulfate, which was also absent in the variant cells (Fig. 6, H and I). Although it is difficult to visualize the polymer-like structure of this barrier, it can be functionally defined as being impervious to particles that are greater or equal to 40 nm in diameter (50). To test the barrier function of variant multiciliated cells, we labeled live cells using a fluorescent probe, SPY-tubulin, that is incorporated into cilia. Fluorescent-coated spheres (100 nm or 40 nm) were added to the apical surface (Fig. 6J). The particles penetrated the periciliary space of the CCDC39 and CCDC40 variant cells, but not that of control cells (Fig. 6, K and M). Likewise, there was no detectable entry of microparticles in cells from individuals with PCD due to DNAI1 or DNAAF5 variants, which also have non-beating cilia. These findings suggested that CCDC39 and CCDC40 variant cells assembled an incompetent periciliary barrier that may contribute to severe lung disease in affected patients.

There are no specific therapies that restore cilia movement to PCD patient cells. We assessed whether normal CCDC39 cDNA could rescue both the motility-dependent and -independent phenotypes of CCDC39 variant cells. We used lentivirus-mediated delivery of a transgene expressing CCDC39 under the control of the FOXJ1 promotor (Transgene, TG) to basal cells with two different variant genotypes. The control (Cont) was a YFP-containing lentivirus (Fig 7A; fig. S6, A to E). We determined that the motility-dependent phenotypes of cilia beat frequency (Fig. 7B; Movies S6 to S9) and cilia length (Fig. 7, C and D) were rescued by the CCDC39 transgene. The splaying phenotype was partially rescued compared to normal cells (Fig. 7E). Motility-independent phenotypes were also improved, including structure of the periciliary layer as detected by presence of MUC4 and keratan sulfate immunostaining (Fig. 7, F to I.). Periciliary barrier function was markedly improved, with marked reduction in penetration by nanoparticles (Fig. 7, J and K). As expected, expression of the transgene impeded cell fate switching, resulting in a decreased percentage of MUC5AC cells and an increased percentage of ciliated cells (Fig. 7, L and M).

By cryo-EM the CCDC39/40 heterodimer appears as a ribbon-like structure that repeats every 96-nm along the length of the outer DMTs (Fig 1B) (14, 15). We propose that the heterodimer guides multiple structural complexes to their correct positions by providing addresses for their docking, rather than measuring distances along a molecular ruler (19). CCDC39/40 anchors an extensive set of proteins that is much greater than suggested by earlier studies (9, 10, 19, 20). The severe lung disease in patients with PCD from CCDC39 or CCDC40 variants may be explained by the large burden of the undocked proteins. We suggest that this proteotoxic load causes alterations in the transcriptional and differentiation programs of multiciliated cells. In addition to the intracellular effects, we suggest that the microtubular disorganization results in a change in the periciliary barrier, contributing to motility-independent impairments.

Cilia of a patient carrying the CCDC39 variant were missing 57 proteins while 36 further proteins were significantly decreased. Thirteen of the missing or reduced proteins normally directly contact CCD39/40 and we propose that these function as CARPs for IDA, the N-DRC, and the radial spokes. CARPs attach to specific amino acid sequences of CCDC39/40 (the addresses) (14–16, 18, 23, 24, 28–30). During assembly, CARPs enter cilia coincident with CCDC39/40. The IDA and radial spokes are prefabricated in the cytoplasm into megadalton complexes that do not include the CARPs (38). The entry and docking of these structures follow the CARPs. Therefore, the loss of CCDC39/40, results in loss of addresses for CARPs followed by the failure to dock entire complexes. Our findings are relevant because variants in genes encoding CARPs (CFAP57, Dynein regulatory complex, 1, 2, 4) (DRC1, DRC2 and DRC4) result in PCD associated with the loss of the IDA or N-DRC complexes (8, 29). Variants in CARPs Coiled coil containing domain 146 (CCDC146) result in human male sterility (27). The extent of ciliary protein loss in these variants, or their impact on multiciliated cell function is not yet known.

Two striking features of CCDC39 and CCDC40 variant cilia was their short length and ciliary splaying due the loss of N-DRC proteins, although loss of other proteins does cause splaying in Chlamydomonas (51, 52). Loss of N-DRC proteins also causes microtubule misalignment in patients with PCD due to DRC4 variants, but splaying was not investigated (8). SEM of CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants in cultured cells showed that removal the ciliary membranes increased splaying substantially. Interestingly, the degree of cilia splaying observed in fresh nasal biopsies of individuals with CCDC39/40 variants was more variable even without membrane removal. We suggest that this variability in splaying occurs from inflammatory environmental insults on the ciliary membrane, which normally functions to support ciliary integrity.

We found that the tethered mucin MUC4 was decreased in the periciliary layer, suggesting a role of the ciliary membrane in establishing or maintaining the interconnected polymer structure. The relationship between ciliary integrity and a functional periciliary barrier is unexplored. The periciliary space plays an important role in separating the mucus layer and its contents from the airway epithelia (50). We observed that fluorescent-labeled microbeads enter the periciliary space of variant cells but not in normal cells, which suggests that pathogens and foreign particles may more easily perturb multiciliary cells in the variants. Short cilia could also impact the function of the periciliary barrier. Of note, patients with PCD variants Cyclin O (CCNO), Tumor protein 73 (TP73), FOXJ1, and Multicilate differentiation and DNA synthesis associated cell cycle protein (MCIDAS) have short and sparse cilia and severe lung disease (5, 53, 54) however, their barrier structure and function was not reported. Disruption of the periciliary layer could further impair airway host defense and contribute to severe lung disease in these patients.

We were surprised to find that in vitro differentiation of CCDC39 and CCDC40 variant cells led to mucous cell metaplasia. These findings were consistent with increased airway mucus plugging observed by X-ray imaging of the lungs of patients with CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants (12). Cultures of airway cells from individuals with COPD and cystic fibrosis display a similar phenotype that is proposed to arise from altered basal cell differentiation in response to a chronically inflamed environment (43, 49). Four lines of evidence suggest that the altered cell fate switching observed in CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants is associated with the genotype and not the environment. First, no increase in mucous cells was observed in cultures of cells with other PCD variants with immotile cilia that were isolated from an inflamed environment. Second, mucous cell metaplasia was observed in cells from normal individuals after depleting CCDC39 and CCDC40 by shRNA. Third, an increased number of mucous cells was present in cultures derived from CCDC40 knockout iPSC derived from peripheral blood cells of a healthy donor. Fourth, transgene rescue with a normal CCDC39 gene in the CCDC39 variant cells reversed the mucous cell phenotype. These lines of evidence strongly suggest that the loss of CCDC39/40 causes the shift in airway cell fate.

Single cell transcriptomics showed alterations in multiple pathways that may contribute to the mucous cell phenotype. Oxidant stress is reported to increase the number of mucous cells and expression of MUC5AC in airway epithelial cell via intracellular ER stress, neutrophilic inflammation, and cigarette smoke (42). SAA1, SAA2, and SAA4 were upregulated in the variant multiciliated cells. These secreted proteins respond to injury, and may activate inflammatory pathways in neighboring cells, possibly increasing oxidants and providing Notch signals to multiciliated cells (40).

We suggest that cilia assembly proceeds by an orderly sequence with CCDC39/40 binding the DMTs, followed by CARP binding to the heterodimer, and subsequently, the remaining network proteins binding (fig. S7A). However, in the absence of the CCDC39/40 heterodimer, the proteins remain in the staging zone or are degraded (fig. S7B). We estimate that the proteostatic stress due to the unassembled proteins in the CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants is substantial. We and others have previously suggested that proteostatic stress occurs in PCD cells with DNAAF5 and ZMYND10 variants (55, 56). In these variants and the CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants, proteins missing from the six inner dynein arms alone, each ~6 μm long cilium, from an average of 200 cilia per cell would result in one billion kilodaltons of protein that must be continually degraded in each cell. Our transcriptomic data shows that five of nine upregulated proteasome genes are in the immunoproteasome (PMSD8, PMSD9, PMSD10, PSME1, PSME2) (57).

Although the immunoproteasome plays a key role in generating peptides for antigen presentation, it is implicated in neurodegenerative diseases, epilepsy, and aging (57). Pro-inflammatory cytokines and redox imbalance induce the expression of PSMD8, 9, and 10 (Proteasome 26S subunit, non-ATPase 8, 9, and 10). In this context, CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants share transcriptional pathways with Parkinson’s disease (57). The re-localization of the proteasome proteins to just below the cilia is coincident with accumulation of unbound proteins at what we term the “assembly staging zone”. This localization of the proteasome and unbound network proteins may indicate a demand for local quality control.

The identification of biomarkers in the CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants and the ability to rescue variant phenotypes is particularly important for the burgeoning development of therapies for PCD (58). Our findings suggest that motility-dependent and motility-independent phenotypes may be approached with different therapies. First, the observation that Notch inhibitors decreased mucous cell numbers in the variant cultures suggests an adjunctive therapy for patients with CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants that show increased mucus plugging (12, 46, 49). Second, many CCDC39 variant-associated cellular phenotypes could be ameliorated by expression of a transgene, which may provide an opening for future gene therapies. For future studies, inhibitors of the immunoproteasome-specific subunits rescue cognitive defects in neurodegenerative diseases (59).

There are several limitations to this study. Although we report extensive disruption to the ciliary proteome from the CCDCC39 variant, we have not examined the proteome from the CCDC40 variant. However, there is interdependence between CCDC39 and CCDC40 proteins as described in Chlamydomonas (22), and we validated the loss of selected proteins in both CCDC39 and CCDC40 variant cells. Also, although our proposal that CCDC39/40 provides amino acid sequences as addresses for attachment of CARPs to CCDC39/40 is supported by multiple cryo-EM models and experimental data, we have not yet performed mutagenesis studies of these addresses. This will be the focus of future work. Lastly, we do not yet know if other PCD-causing genes or variants lead to similar proteostatic stress or ciliary barrier impairment.

In conclusion, we have shown that CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants have both motility-dependent and -independent ciliary defects, including cell fate switching. We found that these phenotypes were similar in CCDC39 and CCDC40 variant cells, independent of specific genotypes. Combined, these phenotypes may account for the previously unexplained severe lung disease seen in patients with PCD who carry these variants. Our findings suggest that Notch inhibition may be helpful for patients with PCD due to CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants who suffer from significant mucus airway plugging. In addition, this work highlights the possibility that future gene therapy could restore cilia function, although further research is required before this approach is translated to humans.

The goal of these studies was to determine the cilia motility-dependent and -independent effects of pathogenic variants in CCDC39 and CCDC40 in human multiciliated airway epithelial cells. Airway epithelial cells from normal individuals without lung disease were used as controls. All individuals provided consent for biopsies and tissue use for research. Other cells were obtained from surgical excess or lungs donated from deceased individuals for research and therefore did not meet criteria for human studies. Cells were isolated from biopsy or tissue samples and cultured using air-liquid interface conditions to avoid secondary effects due to the in vivo environment. Where possible, cells were procured from multiple individuals with different pathogenic variants in CCDC39 or CCDC40. All samples were assigned a code number for anonymization, and clinical information related to patients was available only to the investigator who obtained the samples. At least two unique replicate culture preparations from variant and control cells were evaluated for all assays. Technical replicates of each unique cell preparation were also obtained for measures of cilia function, morphology, gene expression, and multiciliated cell phenotypes. Because these studies were exploratory, there were insufficient studies for power calculations. Studies were guided by the unbiased analyses of proteomics and scRNA-seq of cell culture samples. Chlamydomonas reinhardtii was used as an evolutionarily conserved organism to validate structural and functional data when appropriate.

The Institutional Review Board of Washington University in Saint Louis approved these studies (IRB# 201705095). Consent was obtained from adults. For minors, a parent or guardian provided consent, and assent was obtained from older children. Diagnostic assessment and genetic testing for PCD was performed during clinical evaluation. All subjects or their parents or guardians were informed of their diagnoses of PCD and the gene containing the pathogenic variant. Human nasal epithelial cells (HNEC) were obtained by brushing the inferior nasal turbinate (Medical Packaging CytoSoft Cytology brush,#CYB-1). Other cells were isolated from lungs surgically removed during lung transplantation, from surgical excess of lungs donated for lung transplantation (human tracheobronchial epithelial cells), or those donated for research, as previously described (43, 55).

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 10). Heatmaps were generated using the group data function in GraphPad. Differences between genotypes were compared using chi square. Differences between two groups were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Multiple medians were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s Multiple Comparison. The variance between the percent splaying of cilia in the cultured cells versus freshly obtained cells were described by the F statistic. The unpaired t-test was used to compare groups in Fig 5D and fig. S2E. P <0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.
