# Encephalocraniocutaneous lipomatosis phenotype associated with mosaic biallelic pathogenic variants in the <i>NF1</i> gene
**Authors:** Steven Smeijers, Hilde Brems, Alexander Verhaeghe, Wim van Paesschen, Johannes van Loon, Seppe Van der Auweraer, Raf Sciot, Dietmar Rudolf Thal, Lieven Lagae, Eric Legius, Tom Theys
**Journal:** Journal of Medical Genetics (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1136/jmg-2023-109785](https://doi.org/10.1136/jmg-2023-109785)

## Content

Encephalocraniocutaneous lipomatosis (ECCL) is a sporadic congenital condition characterised by ocular, cutaneous and central nervous system involvement. Mosaic activating variants in FGFR1 and KRAS have been reported in several individuals with this syndrome. We report on a patient with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) with a germline pathogenic variant in the NF1 gene and an ECCL phenotype, suggesting ECCL to be part of a spectrum of malformations associated with NF1 pathogenic variants. An anatomical hemispherectomy was performed for intractable epilepsy. Through genetic analysis of blood, cerebral tissue and giant cell lesions in both jaws, we identified the germline NF1 pathogenic variant in all samples and a second-hit pathogenic NF1 variant in cerebral tissue and both giant cell lesions. Both NF1 variants were located on different alleles resulting in somatic mosaicism for a biallelic NF1 inactivation originating in early embryogenesis (second-hit mosaicism or Happle type 2 mosaicism). The biallelic deficit in NF1 in the left hemicranium explains the severe localised, congenital abnormality in this patient. Identical first and second-hit variants in a giant cell lesion of both upper and lower jaws provide confirmatory evidence for an early embryonic second hit involving at least the neural crest. We suggest that the ECCL phenotype may be part of a spectrum of congenital problems associated with mosaic NF1 nullisomy originating during early embryogenesis. The biallelic NF1 inactivation during early embryogenesis mimics the severe activation of the RAS-MAPK pathway seen in ECCL caused by embryonic mosaic activating FGFR1 and KRAS variants in the cranial region. We propose that distinct mechanisms of mosaicism can cause the ECCL phenotype through convergence on the RAS-MAPK pathway.

 BACKGROUND AND IMPORTANCE Encephalocraniocutaneous lipomatosis (ECCL) is a sporadic congenital condition characterized by ocular, cutaneous and central nervous system (CNS) involvement. It belongs to the spectrum of neurocutaneous syndromes and shows considerable phenotypical overlap with disorders with pathogenic variants in genes coding for proteins of the RAS-MAPK and PI3K-AKT pathways.[1] ECCL can be associated with early embryonic mosaic activating variants in FGFR1 and KRAS [1,2] explaining the patchy nature of the abnormalities.[3,4] We previously reported on the first and only description of an infant with ECCL and a pathogenic germline neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) gene variant, thereby suggesting ECCL to be part of a spectrum of congenital malformations associated with NF1 variants.[5]  This patient was under further medical surveillance at our institution. An anatomical hemispherectomy was performed because of intractable epilepsy. Through genetic analysis of cerebral tissue and giant cell granulomas of the upper and lower jaw, we now provide evidence for an additional mosaic somatic NF1 inactivation (mosaicism for a biallelic inactivation of the NF1 gene) originating during early embryogenesis and severely affecting the cranial neural crest, thereby confirming our earlier hypothesis.[5] Apart from the mosaic activating variants in FGFR1 and KRAS, we now propose that the ECCL phenotype can also result from a different mechanism with mosaicism for a biallelic NF1 inactivation during early embryonic development compatible with the Happle type 2 form of mosaicism.  CLINICAL PRESENTATION Clinical findings This patient was previously reported as a 2-year-old child with a germline NF1 pathogenic nonsense variant and clinical diagnosis of ECCL in 1995 (for a complete patient description, we refer to Legius et al.[5]). From birth, the patient presented with left-sided skin abnormalities and subsequently

developed left-sided malformations (corneal opacity, soft subcutaneous swellings, clavicular hyperostosis,  a giant cell granuloma in both jaws (for which debulking was performed), pectus excavatum and a slowly growing plexiform neurofibroma in the craniofacial area (without cutaneous neurofibromas), severe intellectual disability (without language development) and hemiplegia, epilepsy and multiple CALMs (café-au-lait macules) (>6). Brain MRI showed diffuse left-sided abnormalities (Figure 1A). The patient fulfilled the revised ECCL criteria of Moog et al.[3] (‘definite case: 3 systems involved with major criteria in ³ 2’) and fulfilled the clinical diagnostic criteria of NF1, the latter only became apparent during follow-up.[6]   Surgical findings Because of intractable epilepsy with concordant noninvasive workup and progressive growth of the central cystic lesion (Figure 1B) (potential tumoral etiology), an anatomical hemispherectomy was performed (Figure 1C, for intraoperative findings see Figure 1D). Postoperatively, global functioning improved with increased alertness, communication, and seizure reduction (from 3 seizures/week to once monthly, Engel IIb). Pathological analysis confirmed focal cortical dysplasia IIIa (with hippocampal sclerosis), diffuse gliosis and a hamartomatous lesion (Figure 1E).   Results of molecular analysis In 1995, we identified a heterozygous, de novo germline NF1 nonsense variant in exon 29 (traditional numbering, current numbering NM_000267.3 coding exon 38) of the NF1 gene (c.5234C>G; p.(Ser1745*)) in DNA from peripheral white blood cells.[5] After hemispherectomy, DNA from both the hamartomatous lesion and other cerebral regions such as the hippocampus was extracted from formalin fixed paraffin embedded tissue (FFPE). The same germline NF1 nonsense variant was confirmed in all specimens by Sanger sequencing. Furthermore, we identified an additional somatic second hit nonsense NF1 variant in exon 23.1 (traditional numbering, current numbering NM_000267.3 exon 29) (c.3916C>T; p.(Arg1306*)) in all cerebral specimens in different proportions

(Table 1). Analysis of previously resected giant cell lesions of both jaws showed an identical first and second hit pathogenic NF1 variant (Table 1).[7] The second hit variant could not be detected in DNA extracted from FFPE tissue of the craniofacial plexiform neurofibroma (nor other variants), suggesting that the analyzed FFPE extracted DNA was of insufficient quality or did not contain sufficient Schwann cells with a second hit to be detected by the technique of Sanger sequencing or next generation sequencing (NGS). Alternatively, we cannot exclude that the neurofibroma developed independently from the mosaicism caused by the early second hit in the NF1 gene. We carried out additional experiments to investigate if both NF1 variants were located on different alleles. Cells from the giant cell lesion of the maxilla were cultured and treated with puromycin to prevent nonsense mediated decay. RNA was extracted and cDNA synthesized. A PCR was designed to amplify a large fragment containing both variants. These PCR products were cloned in a vector and transformed in bacteria. Different bacterial clones were selected and Sanger sequenced. We identified clones containing the first hit in NF1 and other clones containing the second hit in NF1. We did not identify any colonies containing both pathogenic NF1 variants. This experiment proves that both pathogenic NF1 variants were located on two different alleles.  DISCUSSION ECCL (otherwise referred to as Haberland syndrome and sometimes Fishman syndrome) has been described in around 100 patients.[3,8] It was hypothesized to represent a mesenchymal disorder due to post-zygotic somatic mosaicism (either due to ianta mosaic heterozygous var or a heterozygous variant with a somatic second hit leading to a mosaic biallelic variant).[3] Since our description of a patient with ECCL phenotype and a pathogenic germline NF1 variant, the question has risen whether ECCL could be etiologically related to NF1.[3,5] Indeed, several NF1 characteristics such as CALMs and sphenoid wing dysplasia have been described in ECCL patients.[3] In 2016, somatic mosaicism for activating variants in FGFR1 and KRAS has been associated with ECCL, followed by more recent publications confirming these original findings.[1,2,4,9,10] Diagnosis is now based on clinical criteria 

7  
or a molecular diagnosis (so far a postzygotic, activating variant in FGFR1 or KRAS) and suggestive clinical findings.[11] Through genetic analysis of cerebral tissue after hemispherectomy, we now provide evidence of how a pathogenic germline NF1 variant can cause the ECCL phenotype through somatic mosaicism via an additional somatic second hit mutation in the normal NF1 allele during early embryogenesis (mosaic biallelic mutation). The early embryonic biallelic inactivation of NF1 in cells from the left hemicranium can explain the severe localized abnormality in this patient. Furthermore, the presence of the same germline and second hit NF1 variants in the giant cell lesions of the jaw and in brain cells are evidence of a very early embryonic second hit involving at least the left cranial neural crest. We propose that several distinct mechanisms of mosaicisms (pathogenic germline NF1 variant with somatic mosaicism resulting in a biallelic NF1 deficit, or somatic mosaicism for pathogenic FGFR1 - or KRAS variants) can cause the ECCL phenotype. NF1 results from loss-of-function pathogenic variants in the NF1 gene, a very large gene (consisting of more than 8000 coding nucleotides) with one of the highest spontaneous mutation rates across single-gene genetic disorders.[12,13] In a cohort of 8000 unrelated NF1 individuals, more than 3000 different variants have been found (with only 31 variants having a prevalence of ≥ 0.4%), which makes it very likely that our first hit variant has a very low allelic frequency.[13] Furthermore, the observed high percentage of the first hit variant during cDNA sequencing in our subject (see Figure 3 original report of Legius et al.[5]) excludes significant mosaicism for the first hit variant.[5] Second hit variants can be seen in NF1 (e.g., development of plexiform neurofibromas), but very early post-zygotic second hits resulting in more severe phenotypes as in our case, are much rarer. The second hit c.3916C>T p.(Arg1306*) NF1 variant has been described multiple times, germline and somatic, in different databases (ClinVar[14], LOVD[15] and COSMIC[16]). This variant was two times reported in central nervous system tissue, more particular in glioma. The mosaic FGFR1 and KRAS pathogenic variants are examples of type 1 mosaicism of Happle.[17] Type 1 mosaicism is frequently caused by strongly activating heterozygous variants that are embryologically 

8  
lethal if present in all cells of the embryo. This type of segmental developmental mosaicism was recently classified as D1 type mosaicism.[18] The variants in FGFR1 and KRAS identified in ECCL patients strongly activate their respective proteins and the same variants are also seen as somatic variants in tumors contributing to the oncogenic process by activating the RAS-MAPK pathway. The two FGFR1 variants reported in ECCL patients are frequently seen in gliomas.[19] It has been suggested that these variants are lethal if present in all cells of the embryo and only viable in a mosaic condition. If mosaicism affects the cranial neural crest it could result in an ECCL phenotype. Depending on the timing and involved cranial regions mosaic activating KRAS variants might result in ECCL, oculoectodermal syndrome or Schimmelpenning-Feuerstein-Mims syndrome (linear nevus sebaceous syndrome) phenotype.[10] Type 2 mosaicism defined by Happle or D2 type of developmental mosaicism is reported in individuals heterozygous for an inactivating pathogenic variant of a gene in all cells causing an autosomal dominant disease in association with segmental mosaicism for an inactivating variant on the other allele of the same gene causing segmental mosaicism for biallelic pathogenic variants of the involved gene.[18] These individuals show the typical features of the autosomal dominantly inherited disease in combination with a very severe phenotype in the region of the body affected by biallelic inactivation of the gene. This mechanism has been shown for the PTEN gene resulting in a severe segmental phenotype resembling Proteus syndrome.[20] Proteus syndrome results from mosaic activating variants in AKT1.[21] Both the Proteus associated activating AKT variants and the mosaic biallelic PTEN variants result in overactivation of the AKT pathway with a similar developmental phenotype. The same severe segmental phenotype resulting from type 2 mosaicism has been reported in Gorlin syndrome (nevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome) caused by heterozygous inactivating variants in PTCH1 in combination with a segmental mosaicism for biallelic inactivation of PTCH1.[22] In NF1, type 2 segmental mosaicism due to a second hit has been previously reported in plexiform neurofibromas.[23,24] As such, the combination of a pronounced plexiform neurofibroma in a segmental distribution superimposed on the classic skin lesions of NF1 can be explained by 

9  
superimposed mosaicism.[24] Depending on the timing and involved regions (early post-zygotic neural crest), second hit variants with early embryonic mosaicism can result in an ECCL phenotype. In this report the developmental type 2 mosaicism at the level of the left cranial neural crest results in the complete inactivation of neurofibromin, a negative regulator of RAS. Loss of RAS inactivation results in an increase in active RAS (GTP-bound RAS), which stimulates its downstream effectors such as the MAP-kinase pathway resulting in cell proliferation and differentiation. The activating KRAS variants and FGFR1 variants also result in an  increased signaling through the MAP-kinase pathway.[1,2] Convergence on the RAS-MAPK pathway with increased RAS signaling could therefore represent the common underlying molecular mechanism resulting in the phenotypical appearance of ECCL.[1] However, an early embryonic mosaicism for an NF1 nullisomy is very rare and as such, these severe NF1 phenotypes are rarely seen.   CONCLUSION We found supporting evidence that the ECCL phenotype is part of the spectrum of congenital malformations associated with NF1 variants. This report provides molecular evidence for the underlying mechanism of a pathogenic germline NF1 variant with an additional somatic second hit in the remaining NF1 allele in the early embryonic neural crest, resulting in mosaic biallelic inactivation (type 2 mosaicism according to Happle). We propose that distinct mechanisms of mosaicism can cause the ECCL phenotype through convergence on the RAS-MAPK pathway.
