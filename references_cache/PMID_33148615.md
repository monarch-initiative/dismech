More than a half of urinary tract infections in infants spread from the bladder to the kidneys and cause acute pyelonephritis, which may lead to renal scarring, hypertension, and subsequent loss of kidney function. However, the cellular mechanisms underlying permanent renal damage after an acute pyelonephritis episode are unknown. In this study, the authors used a preclinical rodent model of acute pyelonephritis–mediated renal scarring to evaluate the contribution of immune phagocytes to resolution of the condition. The data demonstrated that neutrophils are required to control bacterial ascent and to prevent aberrant macrophage-dependent inflammatory responses in the infected kidney. These findings suggest a delicate balance between neutrophil and macrophage immune responses is required to effectively control acute pyelonephritis and prevent impairment of kidney function.

In children, the acute pyelonephritis that can result from urinary tract infections (UTIs), which commonly ascend from the bladder to the kidney, is a growing concern because it poses a risk of renal scarring and irreversible loss of kidney function. To date, the cellular mechanisms underlying acute pyelonephritis–driven renal scarring remain unknown.

We used a preclinical model of uropathogenicEscherichia coli–induced acute pyelonephritis to determine the contribution of neutrophils and monocytes to resolution of the condition and the subsequent development of kidney fibrosis. We used cell-specific monoclonal antibodies to eliminate neutrophils, monocytes, or both. Bacterial ascent and the cell dynamics of phagocytic cells were assessed by biophotonic imaging and flow cytometry, respectively. We used quantitative RT-PCR and histopathologic analyses to evaluate inflammation and renal scarring.

We found that neutrophils are critical to control bacterial ascent, which is in line with previous studies suggesting a protective role for neutrophils during a UTI, whereas monocyte-derived macrophages orchestrate a strong, but ineffective, inflammatory response against uropathogenic,E. coli–induced, acute pyelonephritis. Experimental neutropenia during acute pyelonephritis resulted in a compensatory increase in the number of monocytes and heightened macrophage-dependent inflammation in the kidney. Exacerbated macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses promoted renal scarring and compromised renal function, as indicated by elevated serum creatinine, BUN, and potassium.

These findings reveal a previously unappreciated outcome for neutrophil-macrophage imbalance in promoting host susceptibility to acute pyelonephritis and the development of permanent renal damage. This suggests targeting dysregulated macrophage responses might be a therapeutic tool to prevent renal scarring during acute pyelonephritis.

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most severe causes of morbidity in childhood. Pediatric UTIs account for >1 million clinician visits and 50,000 hospital admissions in the United States alone.1UropathogenicEscherichia coli(UPEC) is the primary etiologic pathogen responsible for >80% of UTIs.2Ascension of UPEC from the bladder (cystitis) to the kidney leads to acute pyelonephritis (APN). Over a half of infants with UTIs develop APN.3Despite prompt diagnosis and treatment with appropriate antibiotics, a population of children with APN will develop permanent renal scars, which are associated with hypertension, CKD, and, in the worst cases, ESKD.3–6Evidence from our laboratory, using a novel preclinical model of UPEC-mediated APN,7indicates dysregulated host inflammation strongly correlates with the development of renal scarring during APN. These findings suggest the inflammatory response elicited during APN largely contributes to kidney fibrosis. However, due to lack of mechanistic insight, identification of children at risk of developing renal scars and therapies to promote renal function after APN are not currently available.

Neutrophils and macrophages are innate immune phagocytes that constitute the first line of defense against bacterial infections. The immune response elicited during UPEC-induced cystitis is characterized by rapid infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages in the bladder,8,9which leads to the resolution of cystitis.10However, the contribution of these phagocytic immune cells to the control of APN and the development of subsequent renal pathology remains to be elucidated.

Previous studies suggest neutrophils and macrophages are required for the host’s resistance to APN and may prevent the development of subsequent long-term sequelae.11–17In this study, we focused on elucidating the individual contribution of neutrophils and monocyte-derived macrophages to bacterial ascent and establishing the cellular mechanism that triggers the development of kidney fibrosis during experimental UPEC-mediated APN.

Wild-type (WT) C3H/HeOuJ, C57BL/6 mice, transgenic B6.129P-Cx3cr1tm1Litt/J (Cx3cr1gfp/gfp), and B6.129S4-Ccr2tm1Ifc/J (Ccr2−/−) mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratories (Bay Harbor, ME). The animals were maintained in a pathogen-free environment and established as breeding colonies in the transgenic mouse facility at the animal facility of the Abigail Wexner Research Institute at Nationwide Children’s Hospital. All mice were females aged 6–8 weeks, weighing 20–22 g, and were group housed. Littermates of the same sex were randomly assigned to different experimental groups. All procedures followed Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee regulations at Nationwide Children’s Hospital (Columbus, OH).

UPEC (CFT073 strain) was isolated from the blood and urine of a woman with APN.18The CFT073 is a type-1 fimbriated, hlyA, cnf1 UPEC strain with serotype O6:H1.19Bioluminescent UPEC (CFT073 strain, Pem7-lux reporter) was generated as described previously.20These bacterial strains were used for induction of experimental APN.

Six-week-old, female, C3H/HeOuJ mice (immunocompetent mice with a high incidence of vesicoureteral reflux)7were acclimated in our facility for 1 week and then inoculated transurethrally with 108CFUs of UPEC (CFT073 strain, WT, or Pem7-lux reporter) in 50µl PBS. Inoculation was performed using a soft polyethylene catheter (outer diameter 0.6 mm; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) under isoflurane anesthesia as described previously.7Control animals underwent intravesical injection of PBS alone. Infected C3H/HeOuJ mice were treated daily from −1 to 7 days postinfection (dpi), intraperitoneally, with one of the following depleting or isotype antibodies (Abs) diluted in 100µl of InVivo Pure Buffer (Bio X Cell): anti-Ly6G (BP0075-1, 1A8, 250 ng), anti-CD115 (BP0213, AFS98, 1 mg), anti-GR1 (BP0075, RB6-8C5, 500 ng), isotype IgG2b (BP0090, LTF-2, 1 mg), or isotype IgG2a (BP0089, 2A3, 500 ng) (all from Bio X cell, Lebanon, NH). Uninfected, PBS-treated mice that were treated with depleting or isotype Abs were used as negative controls. Animals were observed daily, and body weight loss was monitored during the course of APN. The mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation at 2 and 7 dpi. Also, C3H/HeOuJ mice were transurethrally infected with bioluminescent UPEC and treated daily intraperitoneally with clodronate liposomes in PBS (100μl [20 mg/ml]; FormuMax) from −2 to 1 dpi. Furthermore, groups of WT,Cx3cr1gfp/gfp, andCcr2−/−mice (C57BL/6 background) were transurethrally infected with UPEC, as mentioned earlier, and euthanized at 16 and 48 hours postinfection (hpi). Uninfected WT and transgenic mice treated with PBS were used as negative controls. To assess the development of renal scarring after APN, groups of infected C3H/HeOuJ mice were treated with depleting Abs from −1 to 6 dpi. This strategy allowed the natural cell reconstitution of monocyte-derived macrophages and/or neutrophils in the kidney and bladder at 8 dpi, which prolonged the survival rate of infected neutropenic mice. The animals were euthanized at 21 dpi for histopathologic, transcriptome, and flow-cytometry analyses.

The number of bacteria was quantified by scoring colony-forming UPECs on plates containing Luria broth agar (Becton and Dickinson) after overnight culturing (37°C) of mechanically homogenized bladders or kidneys. The ascension of UTI in anesthetized, infected mice was tracked with bioluminescent UPEC by using the Xenogen IVIS Imaging System (PerkinElmer). Photons were measured during a 1-minute exposure by keeping the animals in the ventral or dorsal position. After the procedure, mice were euthanized and bladders and kidneys were sterilely dissected. Bacterial burden within the infected organs was measured by a 30-second exposure on the Xenogen IVIS system. Photon emissions were quantified with Living Image software (Caliper Life Science).

Harvested bladders and kidneys were minced and digested in Hank balanced salt solution with 1 mg/ml collagenase type 4, 20µg/ml DNase I, and 1 mg/ml BSA/fraction V (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY). After enzymatic dissociation, mononuclear and polymorphonuclear cells were enriched using a double gradient formed by layering an equal volume of Histopaque-1077 (Sigma, St. Louis, MO) over Histopaque-1119 (Sigma). Leukocytes from EDTA-treated blood were also enriched with this double gradient. Single-cell suspensions were incubated in 1μg/ml of anti-mouse Fc receptor Ab (anti-mouse anti-CD16/32, clone 93) in 100 ml PBS containing 0.5% BSA plus 0.02% sodium (Na) azide (FACS buffer) for 15 minutes on ice to block nonspecific binding of Abs. Cells were then stained with blue-fluorescent reactive dye (L23105; Life Technologies) for 20 minutes at room temperature to remove dead cells from the analysis. After washing, 1×106–3×106cells were stained for cell-surface receptors in FACS buffer, for 15 minutes at 4°C, with various fluorescent mAb combinations (see the list below). Flow-cytometry studies used Abs at 1:100 dilutions. Intracellular staining was performed using the Cytofix/Cytoperm intracellular staining kit (eBiosciences). Stained cells were further collected on a LSR II cytofluorometer (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star, Ashland, OR). Absolute cell numbers were calculated using CountBright Absolute Counting Beads (Thermo Fisher, Wilmington, DE).

The following fluorochrome-conjugated Abs were purchased from BioLegend: Brilliant Violet 650 anti-mouse I-A/I-E Ab (107641, M5/114.15.2), Brilliant Violet 421 anti-mouse I-A/I-E Ab (107632, M5/114.15.2), APC anti-mouse F4/80 Ab (123116, BM8), Brilliant Violet 605 anti-mouse F4/80 Ab (123133, BM8), Brilliant Violet 785 anti-mouse CD45 Ab (103149, 30-F11), Alexa Fluor 700 anti-mouse/human CD11b Ab (101222, M1/70), PerCP/Cy5.5 anti-mouse Ly-6G Ab (127616, 1A8), Brilliant Violet 711 anti-human CD64 Ab (305042, 10.1), Brilliant Violet 650 anti-mouse TNF-αAb (506333, MP6-XT22), and PE/Cy7 anti-mouse Ly-6C Ab (clone 128018, HK1.4). The following Abs were purchased from eBioscience: PE CD115 mAb (12-1152-82, AFS98), APC inducible nitric-oxide synthase (iNOS) mAb (17-5920-82, CXNFT), PE iNOS mAb (12-5920-82, CXNFT), and PE anti-mouse IL-1αAb (503203, ALF-161).

Kidneys and urinary bladders were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned serially at 4µm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin or Picro Sirius Red. Inflammation and fibrosis in the kidney were blindly evaluated by two veterinary pathologists (C.Y. and B.B.) using established histopathologic analyses.21–24Severe pyelonephritis was defined as acute tubular necrosis and interstitial inflammation involving the renal cortex. Histopathologic features in the urinary bladder were also evaluated by a veterinary anatomic pathologist (C.Y.) using established grading scores.25,26

Kidney sections, frozen at −80°C with liquid nitrogen embedded into Tissue-Tek (Sakura), were analyzed using immunostaining. Frozen blocks were cut into 4-μm sections and mounted on poly-L-lysine-coated glass slides (Menzel) and fixed with acetone. The sections were stained with the following primary Abs: rat anti-mouse F480 (MCA497GA, A3-1; Bio-Rad), rabbit anti–E. coli(NB200-579, polyclonal; Novus Biologicals), and goat anti-NOS2 (clone M-19; Santa Cruz Biotechnology). The following secondary Abs were used: goat anti-rabbit 647 (ab150079, polyclonal; Abcam), goat anti-rat AF594 (ab150160, polyclonal; Abcam), and Alexa Fluor 568 donkey anti-goat IgG (A-11057; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The slides were prepared with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Abcam) and wheat germ agglutinin AF488 conjugate (W11261; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for visualization of the nucleus and plasma membrane, respectively. Micrographs were captured a using a LSM800 confocal microscope (Zeiss). Images were analyzed using the software ImageJ (National Institutes of Health).

Total RNA was extracted and purified from single-cell suspensions of harvested kidneys using the miRNeasy kit protocol (Qiagen). RNA concentration and integrity were determined using a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100, respectively. cDNA was prepared with SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For quantitative PCR, TaqMan gene expression arrays of 96-well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used with a customized TaqMan design. Data were normalized using 18s RNA as a housekeeping gene, and the fold changes were calculated by comparing noninfected with infected samples. Fold expression changes were determined by the 2−ΔΔCTmethod.27

Blood samples from infected mice were collected and placed in i-STAT CHEM8+ cartridges (Abbot), following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The cartridges were analyzed in the portable clinical analyzer (i-STAT Handheld; Abbott) to determine the levels of Na, potassium (K+), chloride (Cl−), total carbon dioxide (TCO2), ionized calcium (iCa2+), BUN, and creatinine (Cr).

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 7, GraphPad Software (La Jolla, CA). Differences between the means of two groups were analyzed using the Mann–WhitneyUtest. Multiple groups were analyzed using Fisher exact test, one-way ANOVA with Tukey post-test analysis, or two-way ANOVA followed by the Bonferroni test. All experiments were subject to at least three technical replicates per experimental parameter, and all data shown are representative of at least two individual experiments, unless otherwise indicated. Results are expressed as mean±SEM. Differences are noted as significant when aPvalue is ≤0.05.

We first determined changes in body weight, a critical sign of a severe infection, in the different experimental groups. Neutrophil depletion in infected mice resulted in drastic weight loss compared with infected mice treated with an isotype Ab or PBS (Figure 1B). Interestingly, the dual depletion of neutrophils and monocytes in infected mice resulted in significantly attenuated weight loss compared with neutrophil depletion alone (Figure 1B). Also, insignificant changes in body weight were observed in mice receiving monocyte-depleting Abs compared with isotype-treated or PBS-treated mice (Figure 1B). Uninfected mice treated with depleting Abs, or isotype Ab, experienced no significant weight change compared with their vehicle-treated counterparts (Supplemental Figure 1A).

To evaluate the specific contribution of neutrophils and monocytes to bacterial clearance during UTI, we measured CFUs in bladders and kidneys harvested from the different cohorts at 2 and 7 dpi. Infected, neutropenic mice showed significantly more viable bacteria in the bladder (Figure 1C) and kidney (Figure 1D) compared with isotype-treated controls. Unexpectedly, monocyte depletion resulted in significantly less bacterial burden 2 dpi compared with isotype-treated mice (approximately 15-fold;Figure 1D). Additionally, the dual depletion of monocytes and neutrophils resulted in reduced bacterial burden as compared with neutropenic mice (approximately eight-fold;Figure 1D). These findings suggest neutrophils, not monocytes, are required for bacterial clearance during APN. Because our data diverge from previous studies suggesting a bactericidal role for mononuclear phagocytes in the resistance to UTI,15,28–30we sought to confirm our results by using three additional experimental approaches. First, we demonstrated that UPEC-infected, C3H/HeOuJ mice treated with a low dose of clodronate liposomes (100μl, 20 mg/kg), which depletes circulating monocytes but not kidney resident macrophages (Supplemental Figure 1K), display a similar bacterial load compared with those treated with liposomes containing PBS at 2 dpi (Supplemental Figure 1, B and C). Also, we infectedCcr2−/−knockout (C57BL/6 background) andCx3cr1gfp/gfptransgenic mice (reporter, null/knockout, C57BL/6 background) with UPEC. The CCR2 and CX3CR1 are essential for the mobilization of monocytes into the infected/inflamed tissues and the accumulation of macrophages in the infected bladder and kidney.31–35We found a similar bacterial burden inCcr2−/−(Supplemental Figure 1, D and E) andCx3cr1gfp/gfpmice (Supplemental Figure 1, F and G) relative to infected WT counterparts at 16 and 48 hpi. Next, we evaluated bacterial disseminationviabiophotonic imaging in C3H/HeOuJ mice infected with bioluminescent UPEC (CFT073, Pem7-luxreporter),20as illustrated inSupplemental Figure 1L. As expected, neutropenic mice showed a marked increase in bioluminescence within the bladder and kidneys compared with isotype-treated mice at 12, 24, and 36 hpi (Figure 1, E and F). Reduced bacterial ascent was observed in infected mice deficient in both neutrophils and monocytes compared with infected mice treated with neutrophil-depleting Abs (Figure 1, E and F). Insignificant differences in bacterial ascent were observed between monocyte-depleted and isotype-treated mice (Figure 1, E and F). Theex vivoanalysis of bioluminescence in bladders and kidneys collected from the different groups at 48 hpi (Figure 1, G and H) showed similar trends as the analysis of viable bacteriain vivo(Figure 1, C and D).

To associate the cell dynamics and specific cell depletion of neutrophils and/or monocytes with the susceptibility to APN, we evaluated the recruitment kinetics of these phagocytic cells, at 2 and 7 dpi, in the kidney, bladder, and blood (see the gating strategy inSupplemental Figure 2) from the different experimental groups (Figure 1A).

Ly6C+macrophages (black squares inFigure 2, A and C), Ly6C−macrophages (blue squares inFigure 2, B and C), and Ly6G+neutrophils (red circles inFigure 2, A and C) increased in the kidney from infected mice treated with the isotype Ab compared with uninfected mice.

The treatment with anti-Ly6G efficiently eliminate neutrophils in the blood (Figure 3, B and C) and prevented neutrophil infiltration in the kidney from infected mice (Figure 2, A and C). However, Ly6C+(Figure 2, A and C) and Ly6C−(Figure 2, B and C) macrophages were significantly increased in this group compared with isotype-treated mice at 2 and 7 dpi. The accumulation of macrophages in the kidney from infected neutrophil-deficient mice was associated with an increase in Ly6C+hi(black squares inFigure 3, A and C) and Ly6C+low(green squares inFigure 3, A and C) monocytes in the blood. This compensatory monocytosis was not present in uninfected mice treated with anti-Ly6G (Figure 3, A and C), indicating monocytosis emerged only in the context of a UTI. The efficiency of cell depletion in the blood from infected mice was confirmed by flow cytometry, using distinct neutrophil and monocyte markers (Supplemental Figure 2, C and D).

Furthermore, the treatment with anti-CD115 or anti-GR1 successfully prevented the accumulation of both Ly6C+and Ly6C−macrophages in the kidney from infected mice (Figure 2, A–C). These data indicate blood-derived monocytes infiltrate the urinary tract and differentiate into Ly6C+and resident-like Ly6C−macrophages during APN. However, Ly6C−resident macrophages in the kidney from uninfected mice were unaffected by anti-CD115 or anti-GR1 treatments (Figure 2, B and C), confirming this macrophage subset derives from embryonic progenitors, not monocytes, as indicated earlier.36Similar results were observed in the bladder as compared with the kidney (Figure 2, A, B, and D).

Additionally, we observed that UPEC-infectedCcr2−/−orCx3cr1gfp/gfpmice had a reduced accumulation of Ly6C+macrophages, but not Ly6C−macrophages, in the bladder (Supplemental Figure 1, H and J) and the kidney (Supplemental Figure 1, H and I). These findings are in line with previous studies indicating a crucial role for CCR2 and CX3CR1 in the recruitment of monocytes in the infected human kidney.15

To determine the specific contributions of innate phagocytic cells to kidney damage during UTI, we performed a histopathologic analysis in kidneys from the different experimental groups at 7 dpi. The incidence of renal pyelitis was similar among the groups of infected mice (Figure 4, A and B,Supplemental Figure 3A). In marked contrast, kidneys from neutropenic mice showed a higher incidence of severe APN, consisting of tubular necrosis and interstitial nephritis involving the renal cortex, compared with isotype-treated mice (75% versus 7%;Figure 4, A and C). In addition, neutropenia in infected mice led to an increased risk of papillary necrosis (50% versus 0%;Figure 4D,Supplemental Figure 3B), renal abscesses (66% versus 0%;Figure 4E,Supplemental Figure 3C), and venous thrombus formation (66% versus 0%;Figure 4F,Supplemental Figure 3D) relative to isotype-treated mice. These data indicate a critical role for neutrophils in preventing severe renal pathology during APN.

Of note, the dual cell depletion of monocytes and neutrophils in infected mice led to significantly less incidence of severe APN (33% versus 75%;Figure 4C), papillary necrosis (16% versus 50%;Figure 4D), renal abscesses (16% versus 66%;Figure 4E), and renal thrombus (0% versus 66%;Figure 4F) compared with selective neutrophil depletion. No significant differences were observed in the renal pathology between infected mice treated with anti-CD115 and isotype Ab (Figure 4). These findings indicate the exacerbated infiltration of monocyte-derived macrophages in the kidney from neutrophil-depleted mice promotes kidney damage during APN.

Increased incidence of cystitis (Supplemental Figure 3, E and F), bladder edema (Supplemental Figure 3, E and G), and bladder hemorrhage (Supplemental Figure 3H) were observed in infected mice treated with anti-Ly6G or anti-GR1, relative to isotype-treated mice at 7 dpi. However, the treatments with anti-Ly6G and anti-GR1 induced similar bladder pathology in infected mice. These findings indicate the accumulation of macrophages in the kidney, but not in the bladder, has a detrimental role during UTI.

We next determined the mRNA expression of various inflammatory factors (Figure 5A) in kidneys harvested from the different groups of infected mice at 2 and 7 dpi. We found a significant increase in the expression of chemokines (Figure 5B), growth factors (Figure 5C), kidney injury markers (Figure 5D), inflammatory cytokines (Figure 5E), and proteins associated with the signaling pathway triggered by UPEC (Figure 5F) in kidneys from neutrophil-depleted mice compared with kidneys from isotype-treated animals at 2 dpi. In contrast, monocytopenia in infected mice by anti-GR1 or anti-CD115 Abs resulted in a significant decrease in the expression of various inflammatory mediators compared with the treatment with anti-Ly6G or isotype Ab, respectively (Figure 5, A–F). Similar trends in gene expression were observed at 7 dpi (Supplemental Figure 4, A–F). These findings indicate monocyte-derived macrophages promote the early expression of inflammatory products during APN.

We then confirmed the proinflammatory (M1) status of macrophages during APN by flow cytometry. Increased Ly6C+(Figure 6, A and C) and Ly6C−(Figure 6, B and D) macrophages producing iNOS+, TNF-α+, and IL-1β+were detected in kidneys from infected neutropenic mice at 7 dpi, compared with isotype-treated controls. Immunostaining of F4/80+macrophages (red inFigure 6E) and UPEC (white inFigure 6E) in infected kidneys revealed that macrophages colocalized with bacteria in the renal pelvis, but not in the renal cortex of infected mice treated with the isotype Ab. In contrast, infected mice treated with anti-Ly6G showed macrophages colocalizing with UPEC in the renal pelvis and cortex, particularly in the tubulointerstitum (Figure 6E), compared with isotype-treated controls. Similar results were observed for iNOS+F480+macrophages in the renal pelvis and cortex from infected isotype-treated and anti-Ly6G-treated mice (Supplemental Figure 5). These findings indicate UPEC-induced M1 macrophages have a crucial role in orchestrating the early inflammatory response elicited during APN. However, the fact that kidneys from infected neutropenic mice displayed increased bacterial burden compared with infected mice, deficient in both neutrophils and monocytes (Figure 1, C–H), suggests that inflammation evoked by the accumulation of M1 macrophages in the kidney potentially contributes to bacterial invasion.

Previously, we demonstrated that dysregulated host inflammation during APN correlates with the development of kidney fibrosis.7However, the specific contribution of neutrophils and monocyte-derived macrophages to the development of renal scarring during APN remains unknown.

Our gene-expression analysis indicates that kidneys from neutropenic, infected mice expressed more profibrotic mediators at 2 (Figure 5G) and 7 dpi (Supplemental Figure 4F), compared with infected mice treated with the isotype Ab. Notably, kidneys from mice subjected to anti-GR1 or anti-CD115 treatments expressed significantly less profibrotic factors compared with kidneys from infected mice treated with anti-Ly6G or isotype Ab, respectively (Figure 5G,Supplemental Figure 4F). These data indicate a role for monocyte-derived macrophages in promoting the early development of kidney fibrosis during APN.

Next, we evaluated renal scarring by determining collagen deposition in kidneys from the different experimental groups at 7 dpi. Unexpectedly, we found insignificant changes in collagen deposition among groups at this time (data not shown). To better assess the development of renal scarring in our model of APN, we collected kidneys from infected mice at 21 dpi (Figure 7A). Because infected mice treated with anti-Ly6G or anti-GR1 were highly susceptible to UPEC infection (Figure 1, B–H) and died at 9 dpi (data not shown), we discontinued the treatment with Abs at 6 dpi (Figure 7A). This strategy allowed the natural cell reconstitution of monocyte-derived macrophages and/or neutrophils in the kidney (Supplemental Figure 6, A and C) and bladder (Supplemental Figure 6, B and D) at 8 dpi, and prolonged the survival rate of infected mice treated with anti-Ly6G or anti-GR1 until termination of the experiment.

Our results demonstrate that infected mice treated with anti-Ly6G or anti-GR1 (−1 to 6 dpi) fully regained their initial weight by 21 dpi (Figure 7B). Ab-induced neutropenia in infected mice during the first 7 dpi resulted in increased incidence of renal scarring (75% versus 14%;Figure 7, C and D), cortical abscesses (83% versus 14%;Figure 7E), cortical necrosis (83% versus 21%;Figure 7F), and increased bacterial burden (Figure 7F) at 21 dpi compared with isotype-treated mice. Interestingly, the dual cell depletion in infected mice resulted in reduced renal fibrosis (40% versus 75%;Figure 7D) and lower incidence of cortical abscesses (40% versus 83%;Figure 7E) relative to infected neutropenic mice. Monocyte-depleted mice exhibited similar renal pathology as compared with those treated with isotype Ab during APN (Figure 7, C–G).

Furthermore, increased collagen deposition in infected neutropenic mice was associated with aggravated renal pyelitis (Figure 8, A and B), severe APN (Figure 8, A and C), increased expression of inflammatory and profibrotic mediators (Supplemental Figure 7, A–F), and higher numbers of macrophages and neutrophils in the kidney (Supplemental Figure 8, A and B) compared with all of the groups at 21 dpi. These results indicate a functional role for neutrophils in preventing long-term repercussions in the kidney caused by uncontrolled APN and aberrant M1 macrophage–dependent inflammation.

To associate the incidence of renal scarring with the development of deteriorating kidney function in the experimental groups described inFigure 7A, we measured blood levels of serum Cr, K+, Cl−, Na+, TCO2, iCa2+, and BUN at 7 and 21 dpi.

Experimental neutropenia led to increased BUN levels in infected mice compared with all of the groups at 7 dpi (Figure 8D). At 21 dpi, infected mice treated with anti-Ly6G or anti-GR1 showed elevated Cr and BUN levels compared with isotype-treated mice (Figure 8E). Reduced BUN levels at 21 dpi in infected mice treated with anti-GR1, compared with those treated with anti-Ly6G (Figure 8E), suggest that increased monocyte-derived macrophages contribute to renal functional impairment during APN.

Also, increased K+levels were detected in the blood from neutropenic mice compared with all of the groups at 21 dpi (Figure 8E), suggesting dysregulated K+homeostasis (hyperkalemia) in this group. The levels of Cl−, Na+, TCO2, and iCa2+remained unchanged among the groups at 7 and 21 dpi (Supplemental Figure 8C).

Overall, our results indicate, for the first time, a crucial role for neutrophils in controlling APN and preventing permanent renal damage by impeding bacterial dissemination and regulating aberrant macrophage-dependent inflammatory responses in the kidney.

Our results provide experimental evidence for the specific contributions of neutrophils and monocyte-derived macrophages to bacterial clearance, renal scarring, and loss of kidney function in a preclinical model of APN (summarized inSupplemental Figure 9).

To date, the beneficial role of neutrophils in inducing immunity to APN is still controversial. Although neutrophil-mediated oxidative stress has been largely associated with kidney damage during APN,37–40recent studies suggest that impaired neutrophil responses can lead to an increased susceptibility in UPEC-infected mice.12,17,41This discrepancy may be due, in part, to the lack of studies explicitly targeting circulating neutrophils in experimental models UTI.

In this study, prior experimental limitations were circumvented with the use of the monoclonal Ab anti-Ly6G, which depletes exclusively neutrophils in mice.16Our results indicate experimental neutropenia in infected mice resulted in increased bacterial load in the bladder and kidney, compared with infected mice treated with an isotype-matched Ab.

Additionally, we found the absence of neutrophils resulted in a compensatory monocytosis and increased accumulation of macrophages in the kidney from infected mice. Monocytosis and aberrant macrophage responses in neutropenic mice were likely due to an infection-mediated increase in the expression of growth factors, such as GM-CSF, and proinflammatory chemokines. These findings are in line with clinical observations in patients with neutropenia who maintain high susceptibility to invasive bacterial infections despite compensatory monocytosis.42,43

Our results also demonstrate that monocyte-derived M1 macrophages (Ly6C+hiand Ly6C−) orchestrate the early inflammatory response elicited during APN. The accumulation of M1 macrophages in infectedneutropenic mice was associated with increased renal abscesses and tubulointerstitial nephritis. These detrimental outcomes were significantly reduced in infected mice depleted of neutrophils and monocytes, compared with infected neutropenic mice, suggesting exacerbated macrophage-dependent inflammation in the kidney contributes to the development of severe APN and, possibly, bacterial dissemination.

Here, we also demonstrate that neutrophil depletion led to an increased incidence of renal scarring in the kidney compared with isotype-treated mice. Collagen deposition in kidneys from infected neutropenic mice was found primarily in the circumjacent areas of the renal abscesses, contiguous to necrotic tissue, which suggests functional renal tissue was partially replaced by connective tissue (collagen fibers) in an attempt to contain the bacterial dissemination.

Reduced interstitial fibrosis and fewer renal abscesses in kidneys from infected mice treated with anti-GR1, compared with those treated with anti-Ly6G, indicates that dysregulated macrophage responses in the kidney contribute to bacterial ascent and the development of renal scarring during APN.

Furthermore, our study demonstrates that increased renal pathology in infected neutropenic mice was associated with elevated circulating BUN, Cr, and K+levels. Because the kidneys are essential in removing waste products and maintaining electrolyte homeostasis in the blood, our findings suggest this experimental cohort has an impaired kidney function.

As expected, decreased incidence of renal scarring and reduced BUN and K+levels were detected in infected mice deficient of both neutrophils and monocytes compared with infected neutropenic animals at 21 dpi. These data indicate that, in the absence of neutrophils, exaggerated macrophage-dependent inflammation in the infected kidney likely contributed to decreased renal function. In addition, these findings suggest that regulating the excessive recruitment of inflammatory monocytes in the kidney during APN may be a novel approach to reduce the development of long-term sequelae in children with a UTI. This strategy may be feasible by targeting essential pathways driving chemotaxis or myelopoiesis of mononuclear phagocytes, such as CCL2/CCR2 and CSF1/CSF-1R axes, respectively.

Our findings may be also clinically relevant for the treatment of UTIs in children with congenital or acquired neutropenia (neutrophil counts of <500 cells/mm3). Previous research estimated that the prevalence of UTI in pediatric patients with cancer showing fever and neutropenia is 8.6%,44whereas the prevalence of UTI in normal infants ranges from 3.3% to 5.3%.45,46Therefore, our results may translate into advanced therapies to reduce the risk of kidney fibrosis in susceptible infants.

In summary, our preclinical research demonstrates that a delicate balance between antimicrobial and inflammatory responses orchestrated by neutrophils and monocytes, respectively, is required to effectively control APN and prevent deteriorating kidney function.
