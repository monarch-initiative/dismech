# Targeting KIT prevents brain arteriovenous malformations driven by ALK1-deficient angiogenic endothelial cells.
**Authors:** Drapé E, Carrier L, Cagnone G, Fuad AR, Bizou M, Sanchez DA, Anquetil T, Wang J, Touzani Walali HD, Gopinadhan A, van Vliet PP, Howard JP, Ibnat M, Andelfinger G, Winkler E, Larrivée B, Dubrac A
**Journal:** J Clin Invest (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1172/JCI197587](https://doi.org/10.1172/JCI197587)

## Content

Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia type 2 (HHT2), caused by mutations in ACVRL1 ( also known as ALK1), is characterized by brain arteriovenous malformations (bAVMs), abnormal artery-vein connections for which treatment options remain limited. Despite evidence of endothelial cell (EC) heterogeneity, its role in bAVM pathogenesis remains poorly defined. Using endothelial-specific inducible Alk1 knockout mice (Alk1iECKO) and regionally resolved single-cell RNA sequencing, we show that loss of ALK1 signaling induces bAVMs predominantly in the perineural vascular plexus (PNVP). This process is driven by the emergence of a KIT+ angiogenic EC population with human AVM-like transcriptional features, including tip-cell markers and activation of PI3K and KRAS signaling pathways. Cross-species analyses and validation in human samples demonstrate that KIT expression is conserved in endothelial cells from both sporadic and HHT2 brain AVMs. Drug repurposing analysis identified KIT as a top actionable target, and we show that Kit is directly repressed by BMP9-ALK1-SMAD4 signaling. Pharmacological inhibition of KIT reduced angiogenic reprogramming and vascular malformations in vivo without affecting normal vasculature. These findings identify a pathogenic angiogenic EC state and position KIT signaling as a therapeutically actionable pathway in brain AVMs.

Introduction 44 Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia (HHT), or Rendu-Osler syndrome, is a rare genetic 45 disorder affecting 1 in 5,000 to 8,000 individuals worldwide (1, 2). It is caused by 46 mutations in components of the BMP9/ALK1 signaling pathway, which regulates vascular 47 development and homeostasis. Mutations in the ENG and ACVRL1 genes, encoding 48 ENDOGLIN and ALK1, respectively, account for the majority of HHT cases (HHT1 and 49 HHT2) (3–5). Rare mutations in GDF2 or SMAD4 result in HHT5 and juvenile polyposis-50 HHT (JP-HHT) (6, 7). HHT patients develop recurrent epistaxis, telangiectasia, and 51 arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) in various organs, with brain AVMs (bAVMs) 52 occurring in 10–20% of cases (8, 9). AVMs are vascular anomalies characterized by the 53 direct connection of arteries and veins without an intervening capillary bed. These lesions 54 are associated with severe complications, including epilepsy and cerebral hemorrhage (10, 55 11). Current management of bAVMs relies on invasive procedures, including 56 microsurgical resection, endovascular embolization, and stereotactic radiosurgery, which 57 are associated with substantial risks. Despite ongoing efforts to develop pharmacological 58 approaches targeting pathways such as VEGF, BMP9/ALK1 signaling, tyrosine kinases, 59 and PI3K/mTOR, no medical therapy is currently approved for bAVMs, underscoring the 60 need for new therapeutic strategies (12–17).  61 The pathogenesis of bAVM remains poorly understood. BMP9 and BMP10 bind ALK1 62 and its co-receptors, including BMPRII and ENDOGLIN, activating SMAD1/5/8 and 63 SMAD4 transcription factors. Studies using different HHT mouse models have highlighted 64 that deletions of ALK1, ENDOGLIN, SMAD1/5, or SMAD4 in ECs are sufficient to 65 induce bAVMs (18–21). Moreover, recent studies have demonstrated that the deletion of 66

ALK1 and SMAD4, specifically in venous ECs induces AVM formations, whereas similar 67 deletions in arterial ECs do not, suggesting that AVMs originate primarily from venous 68 ECs (22, 23). Loss of BMP9/ALK1/SMAD4 signaling modulates the response of ECs to 69 shear stress, activating VEGF-induced PI3K-AKT signaling and the integrin/YAP/TAZ 70 pathways (14, 20, 22–24). As a result, mutant ECs display hyperproliferation, loss of 71 arteriovenous identity, and impaired migration against blood flow (20, 22, 23, 25, 26). 72 However, most mechanistic insights have been derived from postnatal retinal models, 73 while the specific cellular and molecular mechanisms in bAVMs remain poorly 74 understood. Moreover, these findings suggest the BMP9/ALK1/SMAD4 signaling 75 disruptions alter the transcriptomic landscape of ECs, driving cellular heterogeneity and 76 contributing to the diverse phenotypes observed in AVMs. 77 In addition to these molecular mechanisms, EC heterogeneity has emerged as a defining 78 feature of AVMs, including those in the brain. This heterogeneity encompasses differences 79 in cellular identity, metabolic activity, and proliferative states, all of which contribute to 80 the distinct structural and functional abnormalities of AVMs. Pathological endothelial 81 plasticity, characterized by proliferative ECs with mixed arteriovenous characteristics, 82 disrupts physiological arteriovenous zonation. Recent studies utilizing single-cell 83 transcriptomics have provided critical insights into this heterogeneity. For instance, 84 Winkler and colleagues (27) identified a distinct subset of ECs in bAVMs exhibiting 85 heightened angiogenic and immunogenic potential and originating from the AVM nidus. 86 Moreover, this pathological remodeling involves alterations in CNS-specific vascular 87 properties coupled with the reactivation of developmental pathways (28). Similarly, a 88 recent study in retinal Alk1 mutant mice has shown that some ECs within AVMs exhibit 89 

6  
enhanced proliferation and mixed arteriovenous characteristics (29). Yang et al (30) further 90 described arterial-lymphatic-like ECs arising from arterial ECs after Alk1 deletion, 91 implicating aberrant plasticity in lung AVM development. However, the specific EC 92 populations driving HHT AVM pathogenesis in the brain and retina remain undefined. 93 Identifying and characterizing EC driving AVM is essential for understanding AVM 94 pathogenesis and developing effective therapeutic strategies. 95 Here, we investigate the contribution of EC heterogeneity to brain AVM pathogenesis in 96 HHT2 using an endothelial-specific Alk1 knockout mouse model combined with single-97 cell transcriptomics. We identify distinct angiogenic EC populations, including a 98 previously uncharacterized cluster that emerges specifically in the brain surface vasculature 99 and exhibits transcriptomic similarity to human bAVM ECs. Among the defining features 100 of this population, we identify KIT as a direct downstream target of BMP9-ALK1 101 signaling. Moreover, pharmacological KIT signaling inhibition limits angiogenic 102 reprogramming and vascular malformations in vivo. Our findings highlight a distinct 103 angiogenic EC population as a key driver of AVM formation in HHT2 and identify KIT as 104 a candidate therapeutic target for bAVMs.  105

Results 106 Brain endothelial Alk1 deletion induces region-specific AVMs in the postnatal brain. 107 To investigate bAVM formation in a model of HHT2, we used endothelial cell-specific and 108 inducible Alk1 knockout mice (Alk1iECKO). Tamoxifen was injected at P6, and the 109 vascular phenotypes were assessed at P8 to circumvent early lethality observed in this 110 model (29), which we quantified and found to occur approximately 48–50 hours after 111 tamoxifen injection (Figure 1A, Supplemental Figure 1A). This time point corresponds 112 to an active phase of postnatal brain angiogenesis, arteriovenous maturation, and vascular 113 remodeling (31, 32), allowing efficient induction of AVM formation upon Alk1 deletion. 114 We analyzed the perineural (PNVP) and intraneural (INVP) vascular plexuses as distinct 115 vascular compartments due to their different vessel compositions and hemodynamic 116 environments (33) (Figure 1A). The PNVP, located at the brain surface, is primarily 117 composed of veins, venules, and arteries, and undergoes active remodeling during early 118 postnatal development, including a progressive decrease in vascular density during the first 119 two postnatal weeks. It exhibits higher flow patterns compared to the INVP, which is 120 largely composed of capillary beds within the brain parenchyma. Given that AVMs arise 121 from aberrant arteriovenous (AV) connections and are strongly influenced by flow, we 122 hypothesized that the PNVP would be more susceptible to bAVM formation upon loss of 123 ALK1 signaling. 124 To examine the vascular network, we performed immunostaining for the endothelial 125 marker CD31 on whole-mount brain preparations to assess the PNVP, and on coronal brain 126 sections to examine the INVP (Figure 1A, Supplemental Figure 1B). To distinguish 127 arteries within the vascular network, we co-stained with an antibody against α-smooth 128

muscle actin (α-SMA), a marker of vascular smooth muscle cells (34) (Figure 1A, 129 Supplemental Figure 1B). We observed that loss of ALK1 signaling induced vascular 130 malformations in the PNVP compared to control littermates at P8 (Figure 1B). 131 Specifically, we detected a significant increase in the overall vascular area (Figure 1C). 132 This change is associated with a significant increase in the diameter of arteries, veins, and 133 venules (Figure 1D), along with a marked reduction in the proportion of CD31+ vessels 134 smaller than 10 μm and a corresponding shift toward larger vessel diameters (>10 μm), 135 although these changes did not reach statistical significance (Figure 1E).  136 In line with previous reports that identified abnormal vein filling with blue latex in the 137 presence of AV shunts (24), we also observed direct AV connections in the mutant PNVP, 138 indicative of early bAVM formation (Figure 1F). This phenotype was accompanied by a 139 significant increase in both the number and diameter of PNVP AV connections (Figure 140 1G, H). While we confirmed efficient Alk1 deletion in ECs isolated from both the PNVP 141 and INVP compartments and validated loss of ALK1 protein in INVP vessels 142 (Supplemental Figure 1C, D), we did not observe marked vascular alterations in the 143 INVP. Specifically, vessel diameter and vascular area remained unchanged in the mutant 144 INVP compared to controls (Figure 1I-K). These results indicate that a 48-hour loss of 145 endothelial ALK1 signaling predominantly induces vascular malformations and AVMs in 146 the PNVP. 147  148 Single-Cell RNA sequencing reveals that Alk1 deficiency induces EC transcriptomic 149 aberrancy in PNVP and INVP ECs. 150

Discussion 359 Vascular heterogeneity has emerged as a defining feature of brain AVMs, yet the 360 transcriptional programs underlying lesion formation remain poorly understood. Here, we 361 used single-cell transcriptomics to characterize EC heterogeneity and transcriptional 362 reprogramming in a mouse model of HHT2 bAVMs. We found that AVMs form at the 363 brain surface (PNVP) within 48 hours of Alk1 inactivation, driven by the emergence of a 364 distinct angiogenic EC cluster enriched for Kit expression. We further demonstrated that 365 BMP9/ALK1/SMAD4 signaling regulates Kit transcription in brain ECs. Finally, we 366 showed that pharmacological inhibition of KIT signaling limits angiogenic reprogramming 367 and vascular malformations in this model. 368 Our data reveal that the PNVP is more susceptible to AVM formation than the INVP, likely 369 due to its arteriovenous architecture and associated hemodynamic forces. This regional 370 vulnerability is consistent with the venous origin of AVMs and their predilection for high-371 flow regions observed in both mouse models and human brain pathology (39, 49). 372 Trajectory analysis further supports this idea, showing that the angiogenic and proliferative 373 EC clusters driving AVM formation originate from venous ECs. Notably, AVMs emerge 374 in an angiogenic context, yet the PNVP at this developmental stage is undergoing 375 physiological regression, in contrast to the actively expanding INVP (33). It is therefore 376 surprising that loss of ALK1 signaling not only prevents PNVP regression but also induces 377 endothelial proliferation and AVM formation within this normally regressing vascular bed. 378 While the molecular mechanisms governing PNVP regression during postnatal 379 development remain poorly understood, our findings point to ALK1 as a critical regulator 380 of this process. 381 

20  
Moreover, the apparent confinement of AVMs to the PNVP at P8 may reflect a temporal 382 constraint, and in models with longer survival, these malformations are likely to extend 383 into the INVP. In patients, brain AVMs are most frequently located in superficial cortical 384 regions but can also occur in deeper structures, including the basal ganglia and thalamus 385 (50). Identifying regional phenotypic differences could lead to the discovery of 386 newmolecular drivers involved in brain AVM development. 387 Using a regional PNVP and INVP EC isolation strategy, our study reveals a transcriptional 388 reprogramming of AVM ECs characterized by both proliferative and angiogenic clusters. 389 Genet et al. showed that CDK (cyclin-dependent kinase) 4/6 inhibitor (CDK4/6i) 390 palbociclib treatment reduces retinal Alk1iECKO AVM by limiting EC proliferation (29). 391 BMP9/ALK1 signaling plays a central role in regulating angiogenic sprouting and tip cell 392 specification (36), and accordingly, Alk1 deletion in the brain induced the emergence of 393 two transcriptionally distinct angiogenic endothelial clusters. Angiogenic 1 ECs, enriched 394 in the INVP, displayed strong transcriptomic similarity to canonical tip cells and appeared 395 to emerge early in the AVM trajectory. RNA velocity analysis suggests that angiogenic 1 396 ECs may give rise either to sprouting tip cells or to angiogenic 2 ECs associated with AVM 397 formation. The molecular mechanisms governing this fate decision remain unknown but 398 may depend on contextual cues such as hemodynamic forces. As AVMs arise in high-flow 399 environments, elevated shear stress may bias angiogenic 1 ECs toward an angiogenic 2 400 state, thereby promoting AVM onset. 401 We identified an angiogenic 2 population characterized by both angiogenic and 402 inflammatory signatures, as previously described by Winkler et al. in human bAVM 403 samples (27). Despite differences between HHT2 mouse models and sporadic human 404 

21  
AVMs, this convergence suggests conserved molecular mechanisms. This is particularly 405 striking given the distinct developmental origins and genetic backgrounds involved. 406 Interestingly, established human brain AVMs did not exhibit a proliferative EC cluster, 407 suggesting that the angiogenic EC cluster may represent a more relevant target for treating 408 established AVMs. 409 We found the angiogenic 2 cluster is enriched for Kit expression. In mutant ECs, its 410 expression extends beyond tip cells and is enriched in angiogenic populations. Moreover, 411 SMAD4 knockdown significantly increased KIT levels, consistent with RNA-seq data 412 showing increased Kit expression in Alk1 and Smad4 mutant brain ECs (46). Therefore, 413 ALK1–SMAD4 signaling negatively regulates Kit and this repression is uncoupled from 414 ENG. These findings suggest that Kit upregulation is a feature of ALK1/SMAD4-deficient 415 AVMs, but may not be a marker of HHT1-associated lesions. 416 Importantly, we validated KIT protein expression in human HHT2 and sporadic brain 417 AVM endothelium by immunostaining. Together, these findings support the translational 418 relevance of our work and identify KIT as a conserved feature of AVM-associated 419 endothelial reprogramming across species and AVM etiologies, raising the possibility that 420 KIT signaling may represent a shared therapeutic vulnerability across both HHT-associated 421 and sporadic bAVMs. 422 To prioritize potential therapeutic targets, we applied a single-cell drug repurposing 423 analysis. This approach revealed that the angiogenic 2 cluster displayed high inhibition 424 weights and sensitivity scores for several KIT-targeting agents such as imatinib and 425 dasatinib. Pharmacological inhibition of KIT signaling reduced endothelial proliferation 426 and angiogenic reprogramming and improved vascular morphology, but did not affect 427 

22  
survival or established AV shunts, highlighting a stage-specific and partial role of KIT 428 signaling to AVM pathogenesis. 429 In addition to its well-known function in angiogenesis (51–53), KIT is a potent activator 430 of PI3K/AKT/mTOR and RAS signaling pathways (54), which are central regulators of 431 endothelial metabolism, proliferation, and migration. Consistent with this, we observed a 432 reduction in PI3K–AKT–mTOR and KRAS signaling signatures following KIT inhibition, 433 aligning with the transcriptional programs enriched in angiogenic 2 ECs. KIT signaling 434 also promotes HIF1a expression (55), an important regulator of glycolysis (56, 57), which 435 we found strongly upregulated in angiogenic 2 ECs. KIT may therefore act as an upstream 436 integrator of multiple pathways implicated in AVM pathophysiology, including EC 437 migration, polarity, and flow-responsive signaling (24). Therefore, future studies should 438 evaluate how KIT signaling interacts with other pathways involved in AVM formation. 439 Finally, KIT signaling inhibition does not rescue survival, indicating a partial functional 440 contribution of this pathway. While brain AVMs can lead to severe neurological 441 complications and potentially life-threatening hemorrhage, this dissociation suggests that 442 KIT signaling primarily contributes to brain-specific vascular pathology but is unlikely to 443 underlie the systemic lethality observed in this model. Consistent with this, we did not 444 detect changes in Kit expression in lung endothelial cells, where hemorrhagic 445 complications have been proposed to drive mortality. 446 Overall, this study identifies a brain AVM–specific endothelial cell population in an HHT 447 mouse model using single-cell RNA sequencing and provides a valuable resource for 448 uncoveringmolecular targets and developing therapeutic strategies for bAVMs. 449  450

Limitations 451 We identified EC transcriptomic reprogramming at an early stage of brain AVM formation, 452 restricting our ability to capture dynamic changes in EC states. A longitudinal analysis at 453 later stages will be critical for defining the ontogeny of AVM EC populations and 454 identifying progressive reprogramming events that could contribute to neurovascular 455 complications. Moreover, although we validated KIT expression in human HHT2 brain 456 AVM endothelium, this observation is currently limited by the small number of available 457 samples due to their rarity, and will require confirmation in larger patient cohorts. 458 Finally, although pharmacological inhibition of KIT signaling reduced angiogenic 459 reprogramming and vascular malformations in Alk1iECKO mice, we did not assess its 460 efficacy in reversing established AVMs, as treatments were initiated at the onset of lesion 461 formation. Addressing these limitations will be essential for refining our understanding of 462 AVM initiation and progression and for developing targeted therapeutic strategies. In 463 addition, the dosing regimens used in this study were based on preclinical murine models 464 and may not directly translate to human patients. Further dose–response and 465 pharmacokinetic studies will be required to define clinically relevant dosing strategies for 466 brain AVMs. 467  468   469
