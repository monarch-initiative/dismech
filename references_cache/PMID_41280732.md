Waterborne diseases affect millions of people annually, challenging public 
health worldwide. Despite the advancements in water management, high-income 
countries remain at risk, mainly because of faulty or ageing water 
infrastructure. Giardia and Campylobacter are two key waterborne pathogens and 
leading agents of gastrointestinal illnesses. Both can be transmitted through 
contaminated water under similar environmental and sanitary conditions, with 
co-infections worsening symptoms and complicating diagnosis and treatment 
efficacy. This review examines waterborne outbreaks in Europe, over the last 
15 years (2010-2024), focusing on Giardia while adding an additional perspective 
on Campylobacter. Over 30 outbreaks were reported during the study period, 
primarily in Ireland and Nordic countries. Recreational and drinking water 
sources were the most commonly implicated, with contamination events often 
associated with specific incidents or treatment failures, and heavy rainfall 
suggested as contributing factor to pathogen entry. In addition, climate change 
is discussed as a major driver exacerbating waterborne disease risks through 
extreme weather events. The review highlights persistent vulnerabilities in 
water safety across Europe, and emphasizes the importance of mitigation 
strategies, which include risk assessment, early detection and continuous 
surveillance, improved water treatment and climate-resilient infrastructures, 
along with interdisciplinary collaboration with different stakeholders.

Waterborne diseases (WBDs) represent a significant public health concern: almost two billion people worldwide lack safely managed water (WHO and UNICEF, 2021) and over two million deaths from waterborne diarrheal diseases are reported annually (Efstratiou et al., 2017). Additionally, WBDs cause severe economic losses related to healthcare, work absences, legal actions, and food safety (Lee et al., 2023). WBDs are usually caused by gastrointestinal pathogens transmitted via the faecal-oral route, mainly bacteria, protozoa and viruses (e.g., Campylobacter, Escherichia, Salmonella, Shigella, Vibrio, Cryptosporidium, Giardia, norovirus and rotavirus) (Farrell et al., 2021; LeChevallier et al., 2024). These pathogens can contaminate various water sources at different stages of use (Fig. 1), leading to both sporadic cases and large-scale outbreaks (Coly et al., 2017). WBDs are frequent in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), due to inadequate sanitation and limited access to safe water (Cissé, 2019). Nevertheless, high-income countries (HIC) also face risks related to failures in water treatment, cross-connections between sewage and drinking water systems, ageing infrastructure and breaches in the distribution networks (Lee et al., 2023). Climate change is expected to significantly impact the epidemiology of WBDs in future years, because of the increased frequency of extreme weather events (floods, drought, heavy precipitation, etc.) that will facilitate water contamination by enhancing pathogen concentration and transport (Cann et al., 2013). Mitigating WBD risks and ensuring access to safe water sources require integrated and multi-faceted approaches (Clasen et al., 2015; World Health Organization, 2022, World Health Organization, 2025). Within this context, the SPRINGS project “Supporting Policy Regulations and Interventions to Negate aggravated Global diarrheal disease due to future climate Shocks”, funded by the EU, investigates climate-driven waterborne diarrheal diseases in Europe (study sites in Italy and Romania) and Africa (study sites in Ghana and Tanzania). The SPRINGS project adopts an interdisciplinary approach to support the development of adaptive policies and infrastructure improvements, by integrating epidemiological studies, socio-demographic data and climate projections (www.springsproject.eu).

This review explores the epidemiology of WBDs in Europe, with particular attention to Giardia as main parasitic agent, while including a bacterial pathogen such as Campylobacter, to provide a broader perspective. These agents are two index pathogens for WBDs within SPRINGS, and were selected as key examples for their prevalence, resilience in aquatic environments, and public health significance (Moreira and Bondelind, 2017). Giardia causes annually 184 million clinical cases and 171,100 disability adjusted life years (Kirk et al., 2015). The presence of Giardia cysts in water without information on species, assemblage, or cyst viability does not necessarily indicate a risk of human infection (Cacciò et al., 2003; Rousseau et al., 2018). However, waterborne transmission is likely the most common source of giardiasis in low-resource settings with limited access to safe water and sanitation (Karanis et al., 2007; Lalle and Cacciò, 2023), whereas in HIC, cases are mainly associated with travel to or immigration from endemic areas (Krumrie et al., 2022). Campylobacter is annually responsible for 400–500 million gastroenteritis cases worldwide and significant economic burdens (Kaakoush et al., 2015; Khairullah et al., 2024). Although unable to multiply in aquatic environments, Campylobacter presence in water usually suggests recent faecal contamination (Mughini-Gras et al., 2016; Pitkänen, 2013), highlighting the role of water as both a source of infections and a reservoir of resistant strains (Bell et al., 2021; Kalu et al., 2024). Giardia and Campylobacter are leading agents of waterborne gastroenteritis in HIC (Sideroglou et al., 2024) and occur under similar environmental and sanitary conditions. Co-infections are common and associated with more severe symptoms (Potgieter et al., 2023). Due to potential underdiagnosis, both pathogens should be included in routine laboratory procedures to ensure effective treatment (Hayman et al., 2023). Therefore, a wider understanding of shared transmission pathways and risk factors within different water sources is essential. This review aims to provide a comprehensive and detailed assessment on this topic, identify potential knowledge gaps and propose strategies to mitigate WBD risks, to be prepared for the expected climate change scenario.

A literature search was conducted in January 2025 using the PubMed and Scopus databases with the following keywords: Campylobacter / Giardia, water and Europe. EFSA and ECDC annual reports were also reviewed to identify relevant outbreaks. For publications, titles and abstracts were screened, followed by full-text evaluation of selected studies. Only reports of waterborne outbreaks in Europe between 2010 and 2024 involving the target pathogens were included. Publication reference lists were also examined for additional outbreak reports. A comprehensive overview of the disease burden in Europe is challenged by differences among countries in terms of surveillance systems, case definitions, diagnostic methods, and notification practices, resulting in substantial variations in outbreak reporting (Curtis et al., 2014; EFSA and ECDC, 2023).

Between 2010 and 2024, 36 waterborne outbreaks involving Giardia and Campylobacter were reported across European countries (Table 1). Specifically, 16 of them involved Giardia and 18 Campylobacter, whereas 2 outbreaks involved both pathogens (Fig. 2A). Outbreak numbers varied annually, with 2010 and 2011 accounting for approximately 30 % of all the outbreaks reported in the fifteen-year period. Contrarily, no outbreak was reported in the period 2021–2024 (Fig. 2B). The highest number of reports was recorded in Ireland for Giardia (Fig. 3A), in Finland and other Nordic Countries (Denmark, Norway and Sweden) for Campylobacter (Fig. 3B), consistently with previous reports (Baldursson and Karanis, 2011; Pitkänen, 2013). This could reflect extensive efforts in outbreak detection and notification in these countries, but also differences in drinking water sources (i.e., surface versus groundwater) and their management, such as type and extent of treatments (Guzman-Herrador et al., 2015; Pouey et al., 2021). Outbreaks ranged from 2 to over 1800 cases, with Finland (2012) and Norway (2019) reporting the largest outbreaks of Campylobacter in drinking water (Hyllestad et al., 2020; Jalava et al., 2014). However, the true extent of the outbreaks might be underestimated, due to underreporting of mild cases and subsequent lack of notification. Additionally, many reports relied on retrospective cohort studies with questionnaires to the exposed population, which is often the only available approach for outbreak reporting (Braeye et al., 2015; Hall et al., 2017; Jalava et al., 2014; Mouly et al., 2016; Resi et al., 2021; Tzani et al., 2019).Fig. 3Distribution of waterborne outbreaks in Europe reported between 2010 and 2024 involving Giardia (A) and Campylobacter (B). Overall, drinking water was the main implicated water source, responsible for 15 outbreaks: two involving Giardia, 12 Campylobacter, and one involving co-infection with both pathogens (Fig. 3). Contamination often resulted from faulty infrastructure, treatment failures, and incidents. In two Campylobacter outbreaks (France and Norway), heavy precipitation and subsequent flooding were considered contributing factors to the contamination of the water reservoirs (Hyllestad et al., 2020; Mouly et al., 2016). Recreational waters were implicated in six reports, three involving Giardia, two Campylobacter and one involving both pathogens (Fig. 3). No specific reason was linked to these outbreaks, except in the case involving both pathogens, where heavy rainfall led to flooding and sewer overflow the day before a triathlon competition in Copenhagen, Denmark (Harder-Lauridsen et al., 2013). Similarly, heavy rain preceded a swimming event in the River Thames (London, UK) associated with a waterborne outbreak involving Giardia. However, no safety concerns due to rainfall were raised by the day of the competition, and Giardia was not considered the main causative agent, despite it was detected in a few tested cases (Hall et al., 2017). For 15 outbreaks (11 involving Giardia and 4 Campylobacter), the exact water source or the suspected contamination route could not be determined (Fig. 3) (Guzman-Herrador et al., 2015; Hall et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2022; Pouey et al., 2021; Suominen et al., 2024).

Several outbreaks involved multiple pathogens, with Giardia and Campylobacter frequently detected alongside viral agents (e.g., norovirus, adenovirus), enteropathogenic and enterohaemorrhagic E. coli, suggesting faecal contamination of water sources (Braeye et al., 2015; Hall et al., 2017; Hyllestad et al., 2020; Jalava et al., 2014; Pouey et al., 2021; Tzani et al., 2019). Water quality was monitored in ten outbreaks, although results were non-compliant with regulatory frameworks in only half of the cases, in Belgium, France and Nordic countries (Braeye et al., 2015; Harder-Lauridsen et al., 2013; Hyllestad et al., 2020; Jalava et al., 2014; Mouly et al., 2016). These findings reinforce the importance of pathogen-specific investigations to identify the causative agent of the outbreak. According to the EU Directives 2020/2184 and 2006/7/EC and European Union Directive 2006/7/EC of the European Parliament and of the Council concerning the management of bathing water quality and repealing Directive 76/160/EEC, 2006, traditional water monitoring relies on culture-based methods, such as heterotrophic plate count and enumeration of faecal indicator bacteria, overlooking non-culturable or unrelated pathogens, such as Giardia and Campylobacter (Holcomb and Stewart, 2020; Korajkic et al., 2018). Detection methods were reported in only six outbreaks, with Campylobacter detection mainly relying on culture and molecular methods (Braeye et al., 2015; Gubbels et al., 2012; Jalava et al., 2014; Hyllestad et al., 2020; Tzani et al., 2019), whereas Giardia investigations combined microscopy, immunochromatographic assay, and molecular techniques (Braeye et al., 2015; Jalava et al., 2014; Resi et al., 2021). Additional epidemiological tools (e.g., whole-genome sequencing, multilocus sequence typing and microbial source tracking) were utilized in a few cases to help identifying the outbreak origin (Braeye et al., 2015; Davies et al., 2020; Gubbels et al., 2012; Jalava et al., 2014; Paruch et al., 2020; Tzani et al., 2019). To our knowledge, only these latter studies conducted extensive epidemiological analyses to confirm the contamination source by investigating the pathogens present in both clinical and environmental samples. Indeed, molecular methods (e.g., qPCR, digital PCR, next generation sequencing) improved pathogen detection and characterization, helping pinpoint contamination sources (Bruno et al., 2022; Mahajna et al., 2022). However, only two studies obtained a partial correspondence between the pathogens detected in stool and water samples, confirming the waterborne origin (Hyllestad et al., 2020; Jalava et al., 2014). Detection of responsible pathogens in water samples might fail due to the delay between contamination and outbreak notification, likely prolonged by incubation/prepatence period and lengthy diagnostic procedures. Furthermore, public health interventions, such as chlorination or flushing of the distribution system, rightfully take precedence over environmental sampling, often eliminating the pathogens before their detection (Hall et al., 2017; Jalava et al., 2014; Resi et al., 2021; Tzani et al., 2019). Ultimately, it is worth mentioning that two studies were not included in the present review either because the waterborne origin could not be determined for each campylobacteriosis outbreak (Kuhn et al., 2018) or because the involved pathogens could not be determined for each waterborne outbreak (Coly et al., 2017).

The data summarized in the present review highlight persistent vulnerabilities in water safety across Europe, mainly involving drinking and recreational water sources. Drinking water represents a major exposure pathway for giardiasis and campylobacteriosis, particularly when water source protection, treatment or distribution systems are inadequate (Moreira and Bondelind, 2017). Surface waters are vulnerable to microbial contamination from animal faeces, runoff, wastewater effluents, and combined sewer overflows (Fregonesi et al., 2022; Vernile et al., 2009). Groundwater is generally considered a protected source, leading to less rigorous management in some HIC (Andrade et al., 2018). Nevertheless, its contamination may occur through infiltration of polluted floodwaters, groundwater recharge, or contaminant migration, especially in karst regions or in proximity to surface water (Daniels et al., 2016; Mooney et al., 2021). Pathogen intrusion into drinking water distribution system is mainly associated with ageing infrastructures, pipe breaks, cross-connections, and backflow (LeChevallier et al., 2024). Premise plumbing and intermittent water supply system are additionally vulnerable to microbial persistence and regrowth, favoured by stagnation, fluctuations in temperature and pressure, and biofilm formation (Bautista-de Los Santos et al., 2019; Furst et al., 2024). Recreational waters, including swimming pools (Xiao et al., 2017), playgrounds (Sales-Ortells and Medema, 2015) and natural bathing sites (Ehsan et al., 2015), represent another important exposure pathway. These settings are susceptible to contamination mainly due to inadequate design, maintenance or disinfection (de Man et al., 2014a), as well as overcrowding and accidental faecal contamination (Papadopoulou et al., 2008), particularly in case of chlorine resistant protozoa such as Giardia (Yin et al., 2022). Other water sources, such as wastewater and stormwater, can serve as reservoirs for waterborne pathogens (Ahmed et al., 2019; Cacciò et al., 2003), contributing to environmental contamination and posing occupational risks (Stobnicka-Kupiec et al., 2024). Reclamation and reuse of these water sources are increasingly important in HIC subjected to water scarcity, but these systems require careful management to ensure their safe use (Levantesi et al., 2010; Szelag et al., 2025), especially in agricultural settings, which represent a critical link among contaminated water, animal health and foodborne diseases (Bell et al., 2021; Banting et al., 2016).

Although most of the examined studies did not determine the exact contamination event, few reports suggested heavy rainfall and flooding as contributing factors. Indeed, altered precipitation patterns increase water system vulnerability in HIC and climate-susceptible regions, undermining the progress achieved in mitigating diarrheal diseases (Chhetri et al., 2017; Young et al., 2015). According to the “concentration-dilution hypothesis”, droughts concentrate pathogens in the environment, whereas heavy rainfall and flooding mobilize them, enhancing contamination of surface and groundwater (Kraay et al., 2020). Furthermore, heavy precipitation might overwhelm water treatment plants and sewer systems, resulting in release of untreated effluents into surface water (Lo Iacono et al., 2017; Mohammed and Seidu, 2019), or direct exposure risks to individuals involved in flood emergency (Andrade et al., 2018; de Man et al., 2014b). Conversely, droughts force reliance on unsafe water sources and increase the risk of groundwater contamination (Cann et al., 2013; Nijhawan and Howard, 2022). In addition, rising temperatures promote microbial replication and survival (Sterk et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2023), accelerate disinfectant decay and biofilm formation (Furst et al., 2024; Levy et al., 2016), and increase water demand with resulting intermittent supply (Levy et al., 2016, Levy et al., 2018). Although climatic shifts are considered to disproportionately affect populations with inadequate water, sanitation and hygiene infrastructures, climate-related WBD outbreaks have been documented in both LMIC and HIC, demonstrating that no country is exempt from these risks (Colston et al., 2022; Mora et al., 2022). Understanding the interplay among water sources and climate change is essential for improving water management strategies and preventing WBD outbreaks.

Ensuring safe drinking water and protecting public health are key targets within the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SGDs), specifically Goal 6 (clean water and sanitation) and Goal 3 (good health and well-being), as outlined in the 2030 Agenda (United Nation General Assembly, 2015). Achieving these goals requires a comprehensive and multidisciplinary approach that integrates risk assessment, water treatment, surveillance, and climate adaptation strategies (Abkar et al., 2024; Geremew et al., 2024). Water safety planning (WSP), as recommended by the World Health Organization, promotes a proactive approach to risk management along the drinking water supply chain, emphasizing contamination prevention (World Health Organization, 2023). Water safety is further enhanced by infrastructure improvements and proper maintenance, including optimized piping materials, adequate disinfectant residuals, regular flushing, and sediment removal (Hemdan et al., 2021; LeChevallier et al., 2024). Collaboration among researchers, policymakers, and water utility professionals is strongly needed to translate scientific progress into regulatory frameworks and reinforce WSP at global level (Abkar et al., 2024; Moreira and Bondelind, 2017). Several methods have proved effective in reducing microbial loads in water, including ultrafiltration, chlorination, ozonation, and ultraviolet irradiation (Clasen et al., 2015; Moreno-Mesonero et al., 2024). However, the excessive use of disinfectants might promote the emergence of resistant bacteria and the formation of harmful by-products, highlighting the need for a balanced approach that integrates multiple treatment methods (Castaño-Henao et al., 2024). WBD outbreaks are often underreported and are frequently subject to delays in source identification and public health response. These limitations might be overcome by continuous surveillance and early warning systems, combined with advanced detection techniques and microbial source tracking (Fu et al., 2023; Holcomb and Stewart, 2020). Additionally, rapid notification and enhanced collaboration between laboratories and authorities are indispensable for an efficient outbreak management (Andrade et al., 2018; Hintaran et al., 2018). In this regard, machine-learning (ML) represents a promising approach to water resource management to improve monitoring and prediction of waterborne pathogens (Ahmed et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2022). Recently, ML and eXplainable Artificial Intelligence have been developed to predict and estimate water contamination by Giardia based on environmental and microbiological data (Ligda et al., 2020, Ligda et al., 2024). Similarly, the combination of ML with spectrophotometry has been proposed as a rapid method for Campylobacter detection and quantification in different water matrices (Zhang et al., 2024). These approaches have the potential to inform risk-based sampling strategies and support early warning systems to prevent WBD outbreaks.

As climate change reshapes water systems and the water demand increases, investments in climate-resilient infrastructures are urgently needed to mitigate contamination risks and increase preparedness to extreme weather events (Cann et al., 2013; Levy et al., 2018). Targeted water management strategies, including reliance on sustainable resources and strict separation between drinking and wastewater systems, are particularly important for water safety in climate-vulnerable regions (Farrell et al., 2021; Mooney et al., 2021). Public health interventions and clear risk communication, such as awareness campaigns and promotion of adequate practices, will further support water source protection and equitable access to water, sanitation, and hygiene, ultimately reducing the burden of WBDs (Cissé, 2019; Geremew et al., 2024).

The increasing complexity of waterborne disease risks, exacerbated by climate change and urbanization, requires an interdisciplinary approach that integrates scientific research, infrastructure improvements, public awareness, and policy interventions. In high-income countries, access to safe water can obscure existing vulnerabilities, with limited surveillance reinforcing the misconception that waterborne diseases mainly affect low- and middle-income nations. This perception contributes to underinvestment in resilient water systems. Outbreaks in Europe are likely underestimated due to underreporting, limited surveillance, and non-mandatory notification. Moreover, current regulations often neglect key pathogens like Giardia, emphasizing the need to update water quality standards across various uses. Adopting advanced detection technologies and expanding research into environmental and societal actions are crucial for effective risk assessment, outbreak response, and climate-resilient public health strategies.
