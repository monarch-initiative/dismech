# Non-Isolated Dandy-Walker Malformation: Exome Sequencing Efficacy and Phenotypic Expansions.
**Authors:** Araji S, Zhao X, Rosenfeld JA, Lalani SR, Scott DA
**Journal:** Clin Genet (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/cge.70155](https://doi.org/10.1111/cge.70155)

## Content

Dandy-Walker malformation (DWM) is a rare congenital abnormality of the posterior fossa and the cerebellum and has an incidence of 1 in 10 000 to 30 000 births. Although DWM can present in isolation, it is often associated with other central nervous system (CNS) abnormalities or extra-CNS anomalies (DWM+). A molecular cause is not identified in the majority of individuals with DWM+. This is due, in part, to uncertainty regarding optimal testing strategies and an incomplete understanding of the genetic causes of DWM+. In this study, we analyzed clinical exome sequencing (cES) data from 91 individuals with DWM+ to determine its diagnostic efficacy. A definitive or probable diagnosis was made for 32 individuals, yielding a diagnostic rate of 35.2% (32/91). Commercially available brain malformation panels would have detected only 24.2% (8/33) to 54.5% (18/33) of the diagnoses made by cES. We then used data from our cohort, published cases, and mouse models to identify nine phenotypic expansions involving DWM. Our results suggest that cES should be considered for all individuals with DWM+ for whom a molecular diagnosis has not been established and that additional testing to identify a genetic cause of DWM is likely not warranted in individuals with genetic syndromes caused by variants in ANKRD11, C2CD3, COL4A1, KMT2D, KRAS, OPHN1, SHOC2, SMARCB1, and WDR73.

In humans, 80% of all brain neurons are located in the cerebellum, which plays a role in physical movement, higher‐order cognitive functions, and the regulation of emotional responses [1, 2, 3, 4]. Cerebellar anomalies are seen in 1:5000 live births [5]. Among these, Dandy‐Walker malformation (DWM) is the most commonly diagnosed anomaly with a prevalence of 1 in 10 000 to 30 000 live births [6, 7, 8]. DWM is diagnosed by neuroimaging and is defined by a complete or partial agenesis of the cerebellar vermis, cystic dilation of the fourth ventricle, and enlargement of the posterior fossa with elevation of the torcula [7]. Moreover, approximately 80% of DWM patients have hydrocephalus [6, 7, 9].

The consequences of DWM can vary widely, from asymptomatic to severe clinical presentations that include hypotonia, respiratory distress, hydrocephalus, and seizures [7, 10, 11]. In 50% to 70% of cases, DWM is associated with other central nervous system (CNS) abnormalities or extra‐CNS anomalies (DWM+) [6, 12]. These associations significantly impact the disorder's outcome and the severity of clinical symptoms [13].

In some cases, isolated DWM and DWM+ have identifiable genetic etiologies. Genes that are known to cause isolated DWM include FOXC1, ZIC1, and ZIC4 [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. DWM is also a known phenotype for individuals with many genetic disorders, including those associated with Meckel‐Gruber syndrome (MIM series PS249000) and Walker‐Warburg syndrome (MIM series PS236670) [6, 19, 20, 21, 22]. There are also reported associations between DWM and maternal alcohol use, maternal diabetes, and in utero infections [6].

Establishing a molecular cause for DWM+ can impact genetic counseling and prognostication and may guide medical management. However, a molecular cause is not identified in the majority of individuals with DWM+. This is due, in part, to uncertainty regarding optimal testing strategies and an incomplete understanding of the genetic causes of DWM+. Here we use data from 91 individuals with DWM+ to determine the diagnostic yield of clinical exome sequencing (cES), compare the efficacy of brain malformation gene panels to cES, and identify phenotypic expansions involving DWM.

This work was approved by the institutional review board of Baylor College of Medicine (Protocol H‐47546) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of this institution's committee on human research and international standards. This study uses only coded data from a clinical database. Hence, no individual consents were obtained.

We identified a cohort of 91 individuals with DWM+ who were referred to Baylor Genetics (BG) for cES between January 2012 and March 2025. BG is a CLIA/Department of Health and Human Services and College of American Pathologists accredited clinical laboratory. In this cohort, cES was performed as proband‐only in 63.7% (58/91), duo‐based in 1.1% (1/91), and trio‐based in 35.2% (32/91). Individuals who received a molecular diagnosis based on tests other than cES (i.e., chromosome analysis, chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA), gene panel testing, or genome sequencing) were excluded from this cohort.

All of the variants reported here were identified at BG and reported back to the referring physician. Variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing, specific PCR, Multiplex Ligation‐dependent Probe Amplification (MLPA), or another methodology if the sequencing or copy number variants detected by next‐generation sequencing did not meet internal quality standards or were within highly homologous regions. In a singleton analysis, Sanger sequencing was also performed for segregation analysis if parental samples were available but did not undergo exome sequencing analysis.

The classification of all variants reported was confirmed or updated by an ABMGG board certified clinical laboratory geneticist as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, a variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign, or benign, based on the 2015 American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) standards for the interpretation of sequence variants (completed in October 2025) [23]. Variables taken into consideration included allele frequencies reported in gnomAD, and the results of computational prediction tools including CADD and REVEL scores [24, 25, 26]. These values, as identified with the help of the GeneBe portal (https://genebe.net/), and the ACMG criteria that were applied to each variant are shown in Table S1 [27].

Over time, BG has adapted its cES pipeline to take advantage of new technologies and resources. In the most recent iteration of the pipeline, is described in the Supplemental Methods.

The molecular and clinical data from individuals (S1–S45) were reviewed to determine the diagnostic certainty associated with the variants reported back to physicians. All diagnoses were classified as “definitive,” “probable,” or “provisional” based on criteria previously outlined by Scott et al. [28]. Briefly, a definitive diagnosis was made if the individual carried a pathogenic variant(s) in a gene whose inheritance was consistent with a disorder associated with that gene, and the phenotypic data provided in the indication were suggestive of the disorder.

A probable diagnosis was made if one of the following criteria were met: (1) the individual carried a likely pathogenic variant in a gene associated with an autosomal dominant or X‐linked gene whose inheritance was consistent with a disorder associated with that gene, and the phenotypic data provided in the indication were suggestive of the disorder, (2) the individual carried a pathogenic variant and a likely pathogenic variant or VUS in trans, or two likely pathogenic variants in trans, in a gene associated with an autosomal recessive disorder, and the phenotypic data provided in the indication were suggestive of the disorder, (3) the individual was mosaic for a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant in an autosomal dominant gene, or an X‐linked gene in a male, and the phenotypic data provided in the indication were suggestive of the disorder.

A provisional diagnosis was made if one of the following criteria were met: (1) the individual carried only a single pathogenic variant in a gene associated with an autosomal recessive disease, and the phenotypic data provided in the indication were suggestive of the disorder, (2) the individual carried two VUSs in trans, or a likely pathogenic variant and a VUS in trans, in a gene associated with an autosomal recessive disease, and the phenotypic data provided in the indication were suggestive of the disorder, (3) the individual was a female who carried a heterozygous pathogenic variant in a gene associated with an X‐linked disorder, and the phenotypic data provided in the indication were suggestive of the disorder, (4) the individual carried VUSs in a gene whose inheritance was consistent with a disorder associated with that gene, and the phenotypic data provided in the indication were suggestive of the disorder [28].

Comparisons of the distribution of cES tests types between sub‐cohorts were performed using a Chi‐square test for independence performed using the online LibreTexts Statistics calculator found at https://stats.libretexts.org/Learning_Objects/02%3A_Interactive_Statistics/37%3A__Chi‐Square_Test_For_Independence_Calculator.

We determined the efficacy of cES by summing the number of individuals in our DWM+ cohort with a definitive or probable diagnosis and dividing by the total number of individuals in the cohort. In cases with multiple variants, and therefore more than one possible diagnosis, we used each individual's variant of highest diagnostic certainty when calculating efficacy.

To assess the coverage of commercially available brain malformation gene panels, we evaluated the genes included in three commercially available tests (October 2025; Table S2). One was titled “Brain Malformation Panel,” and two were titled “Comprehensive Brain Malformations Panel.” We then compared the gene coverage of these panels to the genes affected in our cES cohort.

We searched the OMIM database (https://www.omim.org/) and the scientific literature for reports in which DWM candidate genes, or their associated genetic disorder(s), were associated with DWM [29]. We searched the Mouse Genome Informatics database (MGI; http://www.informatics.jax.org/) and the scientific literature to determine if the mouse homologs of DWM candidate genes were associated with development of DWM [30].

Using data from the Mouse Genome Informatics (MGI) database, we determined whether the mouse homolog of each DWM candidate gene was expressed in the central nervous system (CNS) between Theiler stage (TS) 14 and TS 28, which corresponds to embryonic day (E) 8.5–E9.75 and postnatal day (P) 4‐adult [31, 32].

Of the 91 individuals in our cohort, 45 (S1–S45) had a sequence variant(s) that was thought to be associated with one or more of the phenotypes listed in their indication for testing. In this positive sub‐cohort of 45 individuals, cES was performed as proband‐only in 64.4% (29/45), duo‐based in 2.2% (1/45), and trio‐based in 33.3% (15/45). Molecular and clinical data for these individuals are summarized in Table S1. The distribution of cES test types was not different between the positive sub‐cohort and the negative sub‐cohort for which no variants were returned to physicians—proband‐only in 63% (29/46) and trio‐based in 37% (17/46) (p = 0.57; Chi‐Square Test for Independence).

In the 91 individuals in our DWM+ cohort, cES provided a definitive diagnosis for 27.5% (25/91) and a probable diagnosis for 7.7% (7/91), yielding a molecular diagnostic efficacy for cES of 35.2% (32/91). One individual (S21) received two definitive diagnoses. A provisional diagnosis was made in an additional 14.3% (13/91) of our cohort.

Of the 33 definitive or probable diagnoses made in our DWM+ cohort, 24.2% (8/33) to 54.5% (18/33) would have been detected using one of three commercially available brain malformation gene panels based on gene coverage. These diagnoses involved 31 unique genes. Between 25.8% (8/31) and 58.1% (18/31) of these genes were covered in one of these panels (Table S2).

Genes carrying sequence variants that resulted in a definitive or probable diagnosis, and had not been clearly shown to cause DWM, were designated as DWM candidate genes. To evaluate which of the DWM candidate genes were likely to be contributing to the development of DWM, we considered (1) the number of individuals in our cohort who carried a variant(s) in the gene, (2) the number of previously published cases in which DWM was associated with variants affecting the gene, (3) whether the candidate gene's mouse homolog has been shown to be expressed in the brain, and (4) whether DWM or other brain anomalies have been observed in mouse models involving the gene's homolog.

Among DWM candidate genes associated with a definitive or probable diagnosis, our analysis revealed nine genes for which there was sufficient evidence to support a phenotypic expansion involving DWM: ANKRD11, C2CD3, COL4A1, KMT2D, KRAS, OPHN1, SHOC2, SMARCB1, and WDR73 (Table 1). Detailed descriptions of the evidence in support of the association of each of these genes with DWM are presented in the Discussion.

The identification of a molecular diagnosis is essential for accurate genetic counseling and prognostication and provides the foundation from which individualized care plans can be formulated [49, 50]. Although cES is commonly used as a diagnostic tool in individuals with congenital malformations, it is not universally employed among individuals with DWM+. Assessing the diagnostic efficacy of cES is a prerequisite to determining its role in optimal genetic testing for individuals with DWM+.

In our DWM+ cohort, cES provided a definitive or probable diagnosis in 35.2% (32/91) of cases. In addition, a provisional diagnosis was made in 14.3% (13/91) of our cohort. In most cases, these provisional diagnoses were based on the presence of one or more variants of uncertain significance (VUSs). It is likely that a subset of these variants will be reclassified in the future as pathogenic or likely pathogenic, leading to a higher cES diagnostic efficacy.

Chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) has been reported to have a diagnostic efficacy of 14.6% in individuals with posterior fossa defects and 25% in DWM specifically [51, 52]. Since cES may fail to identify some copy number variants, it would be reasonable to request a CMA before, after, or in conjunction with cES to maximize diagnostic efficacy. Alternatively, clinical genome sequencing (cGS), with its ability to detect both sequence and copy number variants, could be used as a stand‐alone genetic test.

The ability of cES to interrogate all disease‐relevant genes, regardless of their known association with a specific phenotype, leads to an increased ability to make a molecular diagnosis in individuals with DWM+ when compared to a gene panel. Specifically, we found that of the 33 definitive or probable diagnoses made in our DWM+ cohort, 24.2% (8/33) to 54.5% (18/33) could have been detected using one of three commercially available brain malformation gene panels. This suggests that cES, or cGS, should be considered in individuals with DWM+ who have had a negative brain malformation gene panel.

Heterozygous pathogenic variants in ANKRD11 are associated with KBG syndrome (MIM# 148050). KBG syndrome is characterized by intellectual disability, behavioral issues, epilepsy, brain malformation, macrodontia, hearing loss, skeletal anomalies, short stature, feeding difficulties, and distinctive facial features. In our cohort, S5 had a definitive diagnosis of KBG syndrome. Two other individuals with DWM and KBG syndrome have been described: one with a heterozygous c.1903_1907del, p.(Lys635Glnfs) ANKRD11 frameshift variant and the other with a de novo heterozygous c.2240C>A, p.(Ser747*) ANKRD11 stop‐gain variant [33]. Ankrd11 is expressed in the developing mouse CNS from TS14‐TS28 [31, 32]. This suggests that individuals can have DWM in the setting of KBG syndrome.

Homozygous or compound heterozygous variants in C2CD3 are associated with orofaciodigital syndrome XIV (OFD14; MIM# 615948). This syndrome is characterized by intellectual disability, severe microcephaly, brain abnormalities, as well as facial, oral, and digital abnormalities. In our cohort, S40 had a definitive diagnosis of OFD14 and was previously described by Boczek et al. along with a second individual with DWM who was compound heterozygous for a likely pathogenic c.128T>G, p.(Leu43Arg) missense variant and a pathogenic c.5227G>T, p.(Gly1743Cys) missense variant in the C2CD3 gene [34]. Cortés et al. also described two siblings with DWM who were compound heterozygous for a paternally inherited likely pathogenic c.195G>C, p.(Trp65Cys) missense variant and a maternally inherited likely pathogenic c.1429delA, p.(Ile477*) frameshift variant [35]. C2CD3 is expressed in the developing mouse CNS from TS14‐TS28 [31, 32]. This suggests that individuals with OFD14 can present with DWM.

Heterozygous pathogenic variants in COL4A1 are associated with small‐vessel brain disease of varying severity, variably associated with eye defects and systemic findings (MIM# 120130) [53]. In our cohort, S4 had DWM+ and a provisional diagnosis, and S28 had DWM+ and a probable diagnosis of small‐vessel brain disease. A total of 3 additional individuals have been previously reported to have DWM and COL4A1 pathogenic variants: two with pathogenic variants that were not individually reported, and an individual with a pathogenic c.3046A>G, p.(Met1016Val) missense variant [36, 37]. Col4a1 is expressed in the developing mouse CNS from TS14‐TS28, and mice that are heterozygous for a variant that leads to skipping of exon 40 have cerebral cortical malformations ranging from subtle lamination defects to large structural abnormalities [31, 32, 37]. This suggests that individuals with COL4A1‐related disorders can present with DWM.

Heterozygous pathogenic variants in KMT2D are associated with Kabuki syndrome 1 (KABUK1; MIM# 147920). KABUK1 is characterized by mild‐to‐moderate intellectual disability, seizures, cleft lip, widely spaced teeth and hypodontia, hearing loss, congenital heart defects, persistence of fetal fingertip pads, skeletal anomalies genitourinary anomalies, postnatal growth deficiency, susceptibility to infections and autoimmune disorders and distinctive facial features. In our cohort, S12 had a definitive diagnosis of KABUK1. Three additional individuals with DWM and KABUK1 have been described; one with a heterozygous de novo c.96C>G, p.(Asp32Glu) missense variant of uncertain significance, one with a heterozygous pathogenic de novo c.12165del, p.(Glu4056Serfs*10) frameshift variant, and one with a heterozygous de novo pathogenic c.7048G>A, p.(Q2350*) stop‐gain variant [38, 39, 40]. KMT2D is expressed in the developing mouse CNS from TS14‐TS28 [31, 32]. Mice that are heterozygous for a gene trapped allele resulting in a 50% reduction of Kmt2D mRNA have decreased dentate gyrus volume and defective neurogenesis [54]. This suggests that DWM is a low‐penetrance phenotype of KABUK1.

Noonan syndrome and Noonan syndrome‐like disorders are caused by pathogenic variants that affect genes in the Ras/MAPK cell‐signaling pathway and are characterized by neurodevelopmental phenotypes, cardiac abnormalities, short stature, and dysmorphic facial features (MIM series PS163950). Noonan syndrome 3 (NS3; MIM # 609942) is caused by heterozygous variants in KRAS, and Noonan syndrome‐like with loose anagen hair 1 (NSLH1; MIM #607721) is caused by heterozygous variants in SHOC2. In our cohort, S7 had a definitive diagnosis of NS3 and S8 had a definitive diagnosis of NSLH1. Three other individuals with DWM and pathogenic variants causing Noonan syndrome have been previously described: one with a de novo heterozygous c.468C>G, p.(Phe156Leu) pathogenic variant in KRAS and two with pathogenic variants in the PTPN11 gene—c.417G>C (p.Glu139Asp) and c.5C>T (p.Thr2Ile) [41, 55, 56]. KRAS, PTPN11, and SHOC2 are expressed in the developing mouse CNS from TS14‐TS28 [31, 32]. Ptpn11‐null embryos have kinked and open neural tubes, and Kras

+/G12D embryos expressing an oncogenic form of KRAS have abnormal brain development [57, 58]. This suggests that DWM is a low‐penetrance phenotype that can be seen in individuals with pathogenic variants affecting the Ras/MAPK cell‐signaling pathway, specifically KRAS, PTPN11, and SHOC2.

Hemizygous pathogenic variants in OPHN1 are associated with intellectual developmental disorder, X‐linked Billuart‐type (MRXSBL; MIM# 300486). This syndrome is characterized by moderately to severely impaired intellectual development, cerebellar hypoplasia, seizures, and dysmorphic facial features. In our cohort, S16 had a definitive diagnosis of MRXSBL. Three individuals have been previously described to have DWM and MRXSBL caused by maternally inherited pathogenic variants: a frameshifting 13.5‐kb deletion encompassing exons 11–15, a frameshifting duplication of exons 7–15, and deletion involving exons 20 and 21 [NM_002547.3] [42, 43, 44]. Ophn1 is expressed in the developing mouse CNS from TS14‐TS28 [31, 32]. Mice that are hemizygous for an Ophn1 null allele have abnormal dendrite morphology, hydrocephaly with dilated lateral and third ventricles, and a thin cerebral cortex [59]. This suggests that individuals with MRXSBL can present with DWM.

Heterozygous pathogenic variants in SMARCB1 are associated with Coffin‐Siris syndrome 3 (CSS3; MIM# 614608). CSS3 is characterized by intellectual disability, developmental delay, brain malformations, scoliosis, feeding difficulties, hypoplastic or absent fifth fingernails and fifth distal phalanges, and coarse facial features. In our cohort, S22 had a definitive diagnosis of CSS3. Previously, three individuals with DWM who were heterozygous for a c.1091_1093del, p.(Lys364del) pathogenic variant in SMARCB1 have been described [45, 46]. Filatova et al. were the first to report on a mouse model that demonstrates brain abnormalities seen in SMARCB1‐related CSS based on a genetic alteration in Smarcb1 mutant animals (Smarcb1
+/inv:NesCre+/−, monoallelic reversible disruption of exon 1 in Nestin‐expressing cells), and the fact that Smarcb1 mRNA levels were decreased by about 30% in embryonic brain tissue, making this phenotype a result of a partial loss of function [46]. Their study showed that DWM is seen in 95% of Smarcb1 mutant mice. Smarcb1 has been shown to be expressed in the developing mouse CNS at TS14 and from TS17–TS28 [31, 32]. Mice that are homozygous for a c.1148del variant in Smarcb1, which leads to the synthesis of elongated SMARCB1 proteins, appear to have structurally normal brains at birth but frequently develop hydrocephalus with enlarged lateral ventricles and a thinned cortex during adolescence [60]. This suggests that DWM is a low‐penetrance feature of CSS3.

Homozygous pathogenic variants in the WDR73 gene are associated with Galloway‐Mowat syndrome 1 (GAMOS1; MIM# 251300). The syndrome is characterized by infantile onset of microcephaly and central nervous system abnormalities leading to severely delayed psychomotor development. Other features include optic atrophy, seizures, movement disorders, and nephrotic syndrome. In our cohort, S38 had a probable diagnosis of GAMOS1. Three other individuals with DWM and GAMOS1 were previously described: two siblings homozygous for a WDR73 c.703C>T, p.(Gln235*) stopgain variant and an individual homozygous for a likely pathogenic c.1111T>G, p.(Trp371Gly) variant [47, 48]. Wdr73 is expressed in the developing mouse CNS from TS14‐TS28 [31, 32]. This suggests that DWM can be seen in individuals with GAMOS1.

This is a retrospective study performed on coded data available from a clinical database of a CLIA/CAP approved genetic laboratory. As such, selection bias may have positively or negatively affected our estimates of cES efficacy. It is possible that the individuals with DWM+ who were referred for cES represent a group with a higher likelihood of having an underlying genetic diagnosis. This type of selection bias would tend to increase the diagnostic yield. Alternatively, individuals with DWM+ for whom a genetic diagnosis could have been made by clinical ES may not have been referred since a molecular diagnosis was already made based on other genetic tests. The exclusion of these individuals from our cohort would lower the diagnostic yield. We are also unable to report, individually or in aggregate, whether members of our cohort underwent chromosome analysis, array‐based CNV analysis, or targeted molecular testing prior to cES.

Other inherent limitation of our study are our reliance on information provided by referring centers and an inability to access additional clinical and molecular information, or to perform additional studies, that could be used to increase the accuracy of variant interpretation and confirm or refute a diagnosis. This absence of information has minimal impact on individuals who received a definitive diagnosis based on their cES results but may have allowed us to reclassify individuals who received a probable or provisional diagnosis.

We also note that 63.7% (58/91) of the individuals in our cohort had cES performed on a proband‐only basis, which is expected to result in a lower diagnostic yield when compared to trio‐based testing [61]. Advances in cES technology and clinical genetics that took place over the time interval in which the cES tests were performed for our cohort would also be expected to yield higher diagnostic rates.

These biases and limitations could also be minimized in a well‐conducted prospective study in which researchers have full access to the medical record and all individuals with DWM+ undergo genetic testing that includes trio cES.

We conclude that cES combined with CMA, or cGS as stand‐alone test, is a reasonable approach to genetic testing in individuals with DWM+. We also conclude that ordering cES or cGS should be considered in individuals with DWM+ even if panel testing has been negative. Our data also suggest that ANKRD11, C2CD3, COL4A1, KMT2D, KRAS, OPHN1, SHOC2, SMARCB1, and WDR73‐related disorders can present with DWM. Hence, in patients with these diagnoses, additional testing to identify an independent cause of DWM is likely not warranted.
