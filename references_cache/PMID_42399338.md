# Distinct sensory and autonomic involvement in hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome compared with idiopathic small fiber neuropathy: a multimodal study.
**Authors:** Dell'Aversana D, Provitera V, Trinchillo A, Masciarelli F, Tozza S, Caporaso G, Vitale F, Borreca I, Areniello AR, Ciccarelli G, Esposito G, Iodice R, Dubbioso R, Manganelli F, Santoro L, Castori M, Nolano M
**Journal:** Sci Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41598-026-60461-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-60461-6)

## Content

Hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (hEDS), frequently presents with pain and autonomic symptoms suggestive of small fiber neuropathy (SFN). However, systematic comparisons between hEDS and idiopathic SFN (iSFN) using combined clinical, functional, and morphological approaches are lacking. We prospectively studied a population of SFN patients who also fulfilled the 2017 criteria for hEDS (hEDS/SFN) and compared them with a group of iSFN patients of similar age. All underwent SFN-Symptoms Inventory Questionnaire (SFN-SIQ), Douleur Neuropathique 4 (DN4), and the Composite Autonomic Symptom Score-31 (COMPASS-31) questionnaires, quantitative sensory testing (QST), autonomic testing (cardiovascular reflexes, sympathetic skin response, dynamic sweat test), and skin biopsy from leg, thigh, and fingertip. Clinical, morphological and functional data were compared with our normative dataset and between the two patient groups. 35 hEDS/SFN and 38 iSFN patients were included in the study. hEDS/SFN patients had earlier symptom onset (19.5 ± 5.9 years vs. 35.2 ± 8.7 years, p < 0.001), more generalized distribution, and higher COMPASS-31 scores (54.3 ± 16.9 vs. 33.9 ± 19.4 p < 0.01), particularly in orthostatic intolerance, gastrointestinal, and urinary domains. Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome (POTS) was present in half of hEDS/SFN patients while it was not found in iSFN (51.5% vs. 0.0%). Skin biopsy revealed similar intraepidermal nerve fiber loss in both groups, but hEDS had greater autonomic fiber loss (p < 0.05). Small fiber involvement in hEDS is characterized by earlier onset, more generalized pain and severe autonomic symptoms, and higher autonomic morpho-functional impairment compared with iSFN. Systematic autonomic assessment and targeted management should be considered in this population.

Introduction
Hypermobile Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (hEDS) is a heritable connective
tissue disorder with unknown molecular basis. According to the 2017
International Ehlers–Danlos syndrome classification, the diagnosis remains
clinical, requiring generalized joint hypermobility (JHM), additional
systemic or musculoskeletal features, and exclusion of other heritable and
acquired connective tissue disorders, including autoimmune rheumatologic
conditions [1-3].
Chronic pain is a protean manifestation in hEDS with nociceptive,
neuropathic and nociplastic features [4]. While joint instability remains an
early contributor of pain it is clear enough that additional mechanisms
participate in pain variability and related disability. Among them, an early
overlap between hEDS and small fiber neuropathy (SFN) hs been earlier
pointed out [5,6]. More recently, Igharo et al. [7] reported SFN in 19 out of
31 individuals with hEDS and compared their data with matched healthy
controls and Novak in a larger study comparing 290 hEDS individuals with
29 healthy controls found morphological evidence of SFN in 64% of them
[8]. Current evidence highlights that hEDS and SFN often run together at
least in adults and that, although the nature of their relationship remains
obscure, they collaborate in deteriorating patients’ life.
In the present work we tested the hypothesis that small fiber neuropathy in
patients with hEDS presents a distinct clinical/instrumental phenotype
compared to idiopathic SFN (iSFN) that may suggest different
pathophysiological mechanisms.
The characterization of pain and dysautonomia in hEDS could contribute to
a better clinical management and to an improvement of patients’ quality of
life.
Methods
Patients
Between January 2021 and December 2024, we prospectively enrolled 35
patients that fulfilled the 2017 hEDS diagnostic criteria [1] from 318

outpatients referred to our institute for suspected SFN (pain and/or
autonomic symptoms). They were selected from ninety one (28.6%) who
presented only a positive Beighton score (BS) for generalized JHM. The 35
patients fulfilled diagnostic criteria for hEDS and SFN (hEDS/SFN) for the
presence of symptoms and signs, normal neurography and abnormal QST
and or skin biopsy [9,10]. Clinical evaluation, sensory and autonomic
functional and morphological assessment and results were compared to 38
patients of similar age that met criteria for iSFN [9,10] with negative BS
and after the exclusion of all known secondary causes of SFN. Morphologic
findings were compared with those obtained from a group of healthy
controls selected from a large sex-and age-stratified normative dataset
available in our laboratory [11-13]. Functional sensory and autonomic
findings were compared with those obtained from 40 healthy subjects
comparable for sex and age extracted from ICS Maugeri, Institute of Telese
Terme database [13,14].
All patients were screened to rule out metabolic, toxic, infective,
dysimmune, paraneoplastic or genetic disorders [15].
Clinical evaluation
All patients underwent neurologic examination, clinical assessment of
sensory and autonomic symptoms through the SFN-Symptoms Inventory
Questionnaire (SFN-SIQ) [16], Douleur Neuropathique 4 (DN4) [17], and
the Composite Autonomic Symptom Score-31 (COMPASS-31) [18]. SFN-SIQ
evaluated the frequency of SFN typical symptoms with a score from 0 to 39.
DN4 assessed the characteristics of sensory impairment and a score >4
indicated a neuropathic pain. COMPASS-31 evaluated the severity and the
frequency of six autonomic domains dysfunction: Orthostatic hypotension
(OH), Vasomotor (VM), Secretomotor (SS), Gastrointestinal (GI), Urinary
(U), Pupillomotor (PM) with a corrected score for each domain and a
corrected total score from 0 to 100.
Sensory and autonomic functional assessment

Quantitative sensory testing (QST) was assessed using a thermal sensory
analyzer (TSA2001, Medoc, Ramat Yishai, Israel) [19] and the method of
limits at four sites (dorsum region of foot, leg, thigh and dorsum region of
hand) to evaluate cold detection threshold (CDT), warm detection threshold
(WDT), cold pain (CP) and heat pain (HP) thresholds. Tactile threshold was
assessed using calibrated monofilaments (Semmes-Weinstein) [20].
Cardiovascular reflexes study (CVRs), sympathetic skin response (SSR) and
dynamic sweat test (DST) were performed to assess autonomic function.
Before testing, patients were asked to avoid coffee, tea and nicotine intake
for 2h. Drugs potentially interfering with autonomic function were withheld
24h (beta-blockers) or 48h (antidepressants) before testing. The
measurements were recorded in a quiet room with constant ambient
temperature. CVRs included heart rate variability at rest and during deep
breathing, Valsalva ratio, 30/15 ratio, blood pressure and heart rate
changes to standing and handgrip [21].
SSR evoked by a random electrical stimulation of the right median nerve at
wrist [22], was recorded at hands and feet using surface electrodes.
Postganglionic sudomotor function was assessed using dynamic sweat test
(DST) [23] after stimulation with 1% pilocarpine by iontophoresis on the
lateral aspect of distal leg and volar region of forearm. Sweating was
visualized using a colorimetric method and recorded by a digital video
camera through a cornstarch-powdered transparent tape used as a contrast
enhancing device. Sweat gland density, sweat volume per gland and per
cm2 were calculated using a dedicated software.
Sensory and autonomic morphological assessment
Cutaneous sensory and autonomic innervation was analyzed on 3 mm punch
skin biopsies from distal leg, thigh and fingertip applying indirect
immunofluorescence techniques as previously described [11,12,24]. Briefly,
free-floating 50 μm-thick sections were incubated with a panel of primary
antibodies to mark nerve fibers (panneuronal marker protein gene product
9.5[PGP]), myelinated fibers (myelin basic protein [MBP]), cholinergic

(vasoactive-intestinal-peptide [VIP]) and noradrenergic (dopamine-
betahydroxylase [DβH]) nerve fibers, vascular bed and basal membranes
(collagen type IV [COLIV]). Endothelia and epidermis were labeled with
Ulex europaeus agglutinin1. Species-specific secondary antibodies coupled

semiquantitative evaluation of the autonomic nerve fibers (scoring:
1=marked loss, 2=moderate loss, 3=mild loss, 4=normal) were performed
according to previously described procedures [25].
Statistical analysis
Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD).
Normal distribution was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical
variables were reported as absolute counts and percentages. Between-
group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test for normally
distributed variables or the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally
distributed data. Proportions were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s
exact test, as appropriate. Differences among groups more than two were
evaluated by the analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni correction.
To account for the unequal sex distribution between groups, additional
multivariate analyses were performed using linear regression models for
continuous outcomes and logistic regression models for binary outcomes,
including diagnosis, age, and sex as independent variables. Correlations
between clinical, functional, and morphological measures were assessed
using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.
All tests were two-tailed and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically
significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS and Stata/SE
14.1.

Results
Clinical and demographic data are reported in table 1. The two groups did
not differ for age: 35 hEDS/SFN (36.4±13.5 years, F/M= 32/3) and 38 iSFN
(39.1 ± 7.5 years, F/M= 27/11). Women represented the 91.4% of
hEDS/SFN and 71.1% of iSFN groups (p<0.05). Patients with hEDS/SFN
compared to subjects with iSFN had an earlier disease onset (19.5 years ±
5.9 years vs 35.2 years ± 8.7; p<0.01) and longer disease duration (16.9
years ± 10.1 years vs 3.8 years ± 2.9 years; p<0.01). Moreover, individuals
with hEDS/SFN reported more frequently generalized musculoskeletal pain
(65.7% vs 9.4%, p<0,01), migraine (48.6% vs 15.6%, p< 0.01), food or
pharmacological allergies and urticaria (28.6% vs 6.3%, p< 0.01) and
Hashimoto's thyroiditis (34.3% vs 9.4%, p< 0.01) than patients with iSFN.
Concerning reported pain characteristics, individuals with hEDS/SFN
described more commonly generalized burning sensations (90.6% vs 68.4%;
p<0.05), pinprick pain (62.5% vs 36.8%; p<0.05), itching (25.0% vs 7.9%;
p<0.05) and pain localized to the perineal region (34% vs 0% p<0.05)
compared to those with iSFN. The rate of tingling (40.6% vs 39.5%; p>0.05)
and allodynia (37.5% vs 21.1%; p>0.05) were comparable between the two
patients’ groups.
On neurological examination, hEDS/SFN patients more frequently exhibited
stocking (90.6% vs 57.9%; p<0.01) and glove (68.8% vs 26.3%; p<0.01)
patterns of punctate hypoesthesia, as well as stocking (68.8% vs 39.5%;
p<0.05) and glove (50.0% vs 23.7%; p<0.05) patterns of tactile
hypoesthesia, compared to iSFN patients.

According to the SFN-SIQ score, sensory symptoms were more prevalent in
daily life among hEDS/SFN patients than in those with iSFN (25.2 ± 7.4 vs
15.8 ± 8.5; p<0.01).
Autonomic symptoms across all domains (Table 1) were also more severe in
the hEDS/SFN group, as reflected by a significantly higher COMPASS-31
corrected total score (54.3 ± 16.9 vs 33.9 ± 19.4; p<0.01). Specifically,
hEDS/SFN patients reported more severe orthostatic intolerance
characterized by palpitations, dizziness, and neck/shoulder pain while
standing, as well as gastroesophageal reflux, slow digestion, early satiety,
alternating constipation and diarrhea, dry skin, heat intolerance, urinary
urgency, and retention.
Regression analyses adjusted for age and sex demonstrated that hEDS/SFN
diagnosis remained independently associated with higher COMPASS-31
scores (β=17.7, 95% CI 9.0–26.5, p<0.001) and higher SFN-SIQ scores
(β=8.0, 95% CI 4.1–11.8, p<0.001) whereas age was not significantly
associated with either score.
Sensory and autonomic functional assessment
Our control group for functional sensory and autonomic assessment was
composed of 40 healty controls (F/M=30/10; age 35.7±9.5). Mean values
are reported in supplementary Table 1.
Compared to controls both hEDS/SFN and iSFN showed a significant
increase in thermal and tactile thresholds at distal sites (foot and leg), while
only hEDS/SFN showed also a proximal impairment with a significant
increase in CDT, CP and WDT at the thigh and CDT and WDT at the hand
(Figure 1).
CVRs revealed autonomic dysfunction (defined as ≥2 abnormal tests) in
54.5% of hEDS/SFN patients versus 24.0% of iSFN patients (p<0.02).
Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome (POTS) was diagnosed in 51.5%
of hEDS patients, but in none (0.0%) of the iSFN group (p<0.01) (Table 2).
Regarding sudomotor function, SSR amplitude and latency did not differ
significantly between the two groups. A postganglionic sudomotor

Skin Innervation Analysis
Based on sex and age stratified cutoff [11,12] in hEDS/SFN individuals,
IENF density analysis revealed a loss in 73.5% of the patients at the leg and
75.7% at the thigh, with a non-length-dependent pattern (defined by a
leg/thigh IENF ratio >0.48,) in 85.3% of cases (Figure 2). No significant
differences in overall IENF density were observed between the two patient
groups. MC density did not differ significantly between groups, with mildly
reduced density observed in 33.3% of hEDS (Figure 4) and 52.8% of iSFN
patients (p>0.05). Similarly, the proportion of patients with reduced
intrapapillary myelinated fiber density in glabrous skin was comparable
between groups (24.2% in hEDS/SFN vs 19.4% in iSFN; p>0.05).
Autonomic nerve loss was frequently observed in hEDS/SFN as well as in
iSFN involving pilomotor, sudomotor and vasomotor nerve fibers (Figure 3
and 4). Sudomotor denervation at the thigh (defined by a semi-quantitative
score ≤2) was significantly more frequent in the hEDS/SFN compared to
iSFN (90.9% vs 37.1%; p<0.01) (Table 2, Figure 3).
Logistic regression analysis adjusted for age and sex confirmed that
hEDS/SFN patients had a significantly increased likelihood of abnormal
sudomotor innervation score at the thigh compared with iSFN patients
(OR=9.89, 95% CI 3.02–32.44, p<0.001), whereas neither age nor sex
showed significant effects.

Discussion
This study offers a comprehensive sensory and autonomic characterization
of patients with hEDS, and reveals, in this condition, a distinct profile of
small fiber pathology when compared to individuals with iSFN. To avoid to
replicate previous studies pointing out a high rate of SFN in hEDS [7,8,26],
we selected individuals with SFN that fulfilled criteria for hEDS
(hEDS/SFN) and compared their profile of small fiber morpho-functional
impairment with that of subjects with a diagnosis of iSFN.
Through an integrated, multimodal approach, we demonstrated that, in
hEDS/SFN, SFN has a non–length-dependent distribution affecting both
somatic and autonomic fibers, and mildly large fiber endings. Patients with
hEDS/SFN exhibited earlier symptom onset, longer disease duration, and a
greater impact on daily functioning, as reflected by significantly higher
SFN-SIQ and COMPASS-31 scores, compared to iSFN. Neuropathic pain in
hEDS/SFN was more widespread than in iSFN, with notable involvement of
the perineal region, a feature absent in our cohort of iSFN.
Consistent with prior studies [5,6,26], we observed a moderate loss of
IENFs in most hEDS/SFN patients. Mixed signs of nerve degeneration and
regeneration with abnormal spatial distribution of epidermal nerves were

frequently observed in hEDS/SFN compared to iSFN suggesting a chronic
slow pathological process able to activate compensatory regenerative
attempts. Such regenerative processes may contribute to the severity of
pain in hEDS/SFN, although this does not exclude a role of central
sensitization in pain symptomatology [27]. Accordingly, the congenital and
slow-progressing nature of hEDS well fits with a complex pathogenesis of
pain, in which peripheral (i.e. SFN) and central (i.e. central sensitization)
factors coexist and amplify each other over the years.
In our study, the application of the leg/thigh ratio cut-off previously
obtained on a large population of healthy controls [12] allowed us to
confirm and reinforce the finding of a predominance of a non–length-
dependent pattern for SFN in hEDS/SFN, as originally proposed by Igharo
et al. [7]. However, this evidence is of little prognostic and discriminatory
value because we had similar results also in the iSFN group likely reflecting
the exclusion of metabolic conditions.
The widespread distribution of pain, so commonly observed in hEDS/SFN
patients, and the common involvement of the perineal regions cannot be
explained exclusively with the predominance of non-length dependent
sensory nerve loss and very likely other features as nerve displacement due
to visceral ptosis may contribute to the development of this type of pain in
these patients.
In agreement with the pathological findings, QST revealed a generalized
sensory impairment in hEDS/SFN patients also involving the proximal sites
usually spared in iSFN individuals. Moreover, we demonstrated for the first
time a moderate loss of the MC and intrapapillary myelinated endings in
hEDS/SFN that however was not different than in iSFN suggesting that the
unmyelinated and small myelinated fibers are primarily affected in both
conditions. However, the loss of large nerve fiber endings involved in
mechanoception and tactile sensitivity might contribute with the
polyarticular joint instability secondary to generalize JHM in determining
alterations in balance and gait, and an increased fear of fall in hEDS [28].

Autonomic involvement was undoubtedly more pronounced in hEDS/SFN
than in iSFN for all domains investigated by the COMPASS-31. This was
corroborated instrumentally, as cardiovascular and sudomotor autonomic
tests revealed higher functional impairment in hEDS/SFN. For example,
instrumental diagnosis of POTS occurred in 51.5% of hEDS/SFN individuals
and in none of those with iSFN. While the high rate of POTS in hEDS is a
consolidated finding in the literature [29-32], many authors speculated that
the primary mechanism for such an association is an abnormal venous blood
pooling due to an intrinsic laxity of the peripheral vessels [33]. Our findings
suggest the existence of a primary dysfunction of the peripheral autonomic
nervous system as a complementary mechanism contributing to POTS in
hEDS.
A primary and pronounced peripheral autonomic involvement was also
demonstrated in hEDS/SFN by means of DST, which revealed a
postganglionic sudomotor damage, and of skin biopsy, that showed severe
autonomic loss involving sudomotor, pilomotor and vasomotor nerves,
compared to iSFN. DST showed a generalized hypohidrosis with more
pronounced reduction in sweat output, decreased sweat gland activation,
and reduced per-gland secretion in hEDS. Although the sex imbalance, such
differences between the 2 patient groups remained significant after
adjustment for age and sex.
The lower sweat output in hEDS/SFN can be due, in addition to the loss of
sudomotor nerves, to other features such as a possible glandular
dysfunction that may be attributed to collagen abnormalities causing
impaired transudation from capillaries to the sweat gland epithelium.
Clinically, hypohidrosis may amplify POTS-related symptoms such as fatigue
and exercise intolerance. Such symptoms are frequent in patients with
POTS [34] and can be aggravated by the coexistence of a sudomotor
impairment due to SFN [35-37]. Sudomotor dysfunction may explain other
symptoms reported by hEDS patients such as heat intolerance and dry skin.

Regardless of the underlying mechanisms connecting findings to symptoms
and disease evolution, the presence of cutaneous nerve remodeling,
alongside with diffuse loss of sensory and autonomic nerves, supports the
hypothesis that small fiber involvement may be an intrinsic feature, rather
than a secondary phenomenon of hEDS, as recently proposed [8]. However,
the mechanisms underlying small fiber pathology in hEDS remain
incompletely understood. Beside a presumed pleiotropic mutation that
remains unknown, other factors, such as the continuous tissue damage due
to joint instability, physical deconditioning, dietary restriction, immune
dysregulation, and low-grade systemic inflammation, may contribute to
ongoing axonal dysfunction in hEDS. The early onset of SFN-related
symptoms and instrumental findings suggest that the process leading to
small fiber pathology in hEDS starts early in the patients’ lives.
Further studies on larger patient cohorts including younger subjects as well
as longitudinal studies are warrant to further characterize the small fiber
pathological process and its rate of progression in hEDS. Although
regression analysis adjusted for sex demonstrated that differences between
groups were not gender related, the imbalance between male and female
patients in our study group can be a limitation.
Our study identifies a non–length-dependent small fiber neuropathy in
hEDS, characterized by widespread sensory and autonomic symptoms, and
converging functional and structural evidence of both somatic and
autonomic fiber pathology. Compared to iSFN, SFN in hEDS exhibits a
distinct profile including earlier onset, higher prevalence of POTS,
generalized hypohidrosis, and a broader distribution of neuropathic pain,
particularly in the perineal region. These findings support the notion that
small fiber involvement is a core feature of hEDS, rather than a secondary
manifestation. They underscore the diagnostic value of skin biopsy and
autonomic testing in the clinical evaluation of hEDS, and further reinforce
the emerging concept of hEDS as a neuroconnective disorder. On the other
hand, the above depicted SFN profile in hEDS could help the neurologist to
