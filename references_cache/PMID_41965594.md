BACKGROUND: Neurolathyrism is an upper motor neuron disorder affecting the 
corticospinal tracts and causing spastic paraparesis. Linked to consuming 
Lathyrus sativus, it remains a global public health challenge, particularly in 
parts of Ethiopia. This research explored the prevalence, associated factors, 
and social impact of neurolathyrism among patients in the Delanta district, 
Ethiopia.
OBJECTIVE: To assess the prevalence, associated factors, and social impact of 
neurolathyrism among patients in the Delanta district, Ethiopia.
METHODS: A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from February to 
March 2023 among 470 randomly selected individuals in two purposively selected 
kebeles of Delanta district, Amhara region. A multistage sampling technique was 
employed. The data were collected using a structured questionnaire after written 
informed consent was obtained from each participant. The collected data were 
cleaned and analyzed using Epi Info version 7.2 and SPSS version 26.0, 
respectively. Descriptive statistics and multivariable logistic regression 
models were used. Statistical significance was determined using P value < 0.05. 
Associations were measured by adjusted odds ratios (AORs). Factors with a P 
value of 0.2 or less in the bivariable analysis were selected for further 
multivariable analysis and entered a logistic regression model.
RESULTS: Of 470 participants, 56 (11.9%) reported neurolathyrism. At the 
population level, prevalence in two purposively selected kebeles of the Delanta 
district was 6.6%. Significant individual-level variables were older age 
(adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 2.69; 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.06-9.34), 
male sex (AOR = 3.57; 95% CI = 1.79-7.10), and inability to read (AOR = 3.13; 
95% CI = 1.22-7.99). Household-level variables included family size (AOR = 2.33; 
95% CI = 1.16-4.69) and farmland lease (AOR = 2.73; 95% CI = 1.23-6.06). Among 
neurolathyrism patients, 73% discontinued school due to lack of support, and 
21.4% were divorced after disease onset.
CONCLUSION: The prevalence of neurolathyrism is notably high in the study 
population. Older age, male sex, educational status, family size, and farmland 
lease were significantly associated with neurolathyrism.

Neurolathyrism is an upper motor neuron disorder that affects the corticospinal tracts, resulting in spastic paraparesis [1]. This condition represents a significant public health concern in countries where grass pea is commonly cultivated and consumed [2]. The condition is a neurotoxic disorder caused by excessive consumption of grass pea (Lathyrus sativus) [3], a legume known to contain the neurotoxin β-ODAP [4, 5]. The term lathyrism is a broader term historically used to refer to diseases caused by Lathyrus species, including neurolathyrism (neurological) and osteolathyrism (skeletal). In Ethiopia, particularly in the Amhara region, the condition is commonly referred to as Yeguya Bisheta, meaning “the disease that bends the legs.” In this manuscript, the term neurolathyrism will be used consistently to refer to the specific neurological condition caused by grass pea consumption [1, 4, 6].

Clinically, neurolathyrism is characterized by irreversible spastic paraparesis that can result in long-term disability [1], which changes the general appearance of bones and joints, particularly at the knees and feet [7]. Beyond physical impairment, the disease imposes substantial psychological, cultural, and socioeconomic burdens [2, 8, 9]. Cases have been reported across a wide age range, though many reports describe a higher burden among young adult males [9, 10].

Neurolathyrism has been known since the eras of ancient Hindus and Hippocrates (460–377 BC). Large outbreaks were reported across parts of Europe, North Africa, the Middle East, and Asia, including Afghanistan, Russia, China, and India [11–13]. In more recent decades, reports have largely been limited to South Asia and Ethiopia, with published community-level estimates commonly ranging from about 1.7% to 6% [14].

Neurolathyrism remains a significant public health concern in Ethiopia, with both historical outbreaks and ongoing cases reported in certain regions [14]. Grass pea commonly replaces cereal-based staple foods during periods of food insecurity in drought-prone areas of northern and central Ethiopia [2, 15], increasing population-level risk. Although neurolathyrism has been known in Ethiopia for many years, well-documented and comprehensive historical records are lacking, and only limited estimates from past decades are available. Major outbreaks occurred in the northwest following the mid-1970s drought. An epidemiological study in northwestern Ethiopia estimated prevalence rates between 0.6% and 3%. Additional peaks were observed during the 1984–1985 famine and again in 1994 [2].

An additional outbreak occurred in the Amhara region, with approximately 2,000 cases documented in a single district during 1997 to 1998. A community-based study conducted in 2019 determined the prevalence of lathyrism to be 5.5% [2].

Building on these findings, it is clear that despite its public health relevance, neurolathyrism continues to be overlooked by the health, agriculture, and policy sectors in Ethiopia, where poverty and recurrent food insecurity directly shape exposure risk.

 [2, 12, 14, 16, 17]. The reasons for this major neglect and the peak occurrence of neurolathyrism cases remain unknown.

One Ethiopian study identified household-level risk indicators including lower educational attainment of the household head, grass pea cultivation, and the exclusive preparation of grass pea dishes in traditional clay cooking pots [11].

Identified prevention strategies include implementing policy restrictions on grass pea production or sale, employing household processing methods to reduce toxin exposure, such as washing, soaking, and fermentation, blending grass pea with other staple foods, developing lower-toxin varieties through breeding or genetic techniques, and promoting the cultivation of alternative drought-tolerant crops [17, 18]. In Ethiopia, prohibiting the production or consumption of grass peas is impractical because frequent weather fluctuations force farmers to rely on grass peas as an alternative crop, often leading to overconsumption [2, 14]. This community-based cross-sectional study aims to assess the prevalence, associated factors, and social impact of neurolathyrism in Delanta. The findings will inform healthcare practitioners, policymakers, and researchers about the specific challenges faced in this community and expand knowledge of neurolathyrism. Moreover, understanding neurolathyrism’s social impact highlights its broader implications. This knowledge lays the groundwork for reducing the burden in Delanta and similar regions.

In conclusion, this research aims to assess the prevalence, associated factors, and social impact of neurolathyrism in Delanta, Amhara Region, Ethiopia. By identifying these elements, the study seeks to bridge gaps in understanding and inform strategies to enhance healthcare delivery, promote community well-being, and address neurological health disparities in the region and beyond.

This community-based study was conducted from February 01 to March 30, 2023, to assess the prevalence, associated factors, and social impact of neurolathyrism among patients in two purposively selected kebeles of Delanta district, Ethiopia. The Amhara Region is one of the most populous regions in Ethiopia. As of the last available census in 2007, the region had a population of more than 17 million people. The estimated population size of the district is 139,618, with 31 rural and 2 urban kebeles covering an area of 126,879 square kilometers [14]. The district is divided into 33 kebeles (the smallest administrative unit) and has an estimated population of 139,618.

The study units were residents of two purposely selected rural kebeles affected by a neurolathyrism epidemic. Inclusion criteria were individuals who had resided in the kebeles for at least six months and consented to participate. Exclusion criteria included temporary residents, individuals unable to respond, and households that declined participation. The sample size was calculated using the single population proportion formula [11], considering a prevalence (p) of 5.5%, a 95% confidence interval (CI), and a 3% margin of error [11]. By adding a 10% nonresponse rate, the final sample included 499 inhabitants of households. The sample size was also calculated using the double population proportion formula, with a power of 80%, a confidence level of 95%, educational status (p1 = 0.04, p2 = 0.12), and availability of grass pea farms (p1 = 0.04, p2 = 0.11). The largest calculated sample size was 221, and after adding a 10% nonresponse rate and adjusting for the design effect, the final sample size became 499 inhabitants of households. The research received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Amhara Public Health Institute (Ethics approval number: APHI-DB/RTTD-020/2015), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Structured questionnaires were used to collect the primary quantitative data from all the selected households. The data collection tool was prepared in English and translated into Amharic, the local language. The data collection tool was pretested to assess its completeness in capturing all variables required to meet the specific objectives of the study. Quality assurance audits were conducted during data collection by field supervisors and research team members. All hindering the collected data, documents, and reports were subject to regulatory audits and inspections without compromising confidentiality. The researchers provided three days of training to both the data collectors (three general practitioners and six BSc nurses) and the supervisors (two MPH). The training included a discussion of the study objectives, the contents of the data collection tools, data collection techniques, the clinical presentation of neurolathyrism, and maintaining the confidentiality of responses. Moreover, supervisors checked the completeness of the questionnaires daily.

Neurolathyrism was defined as symmetrical spastic paralysis of the lower extremities, with normal sensory function and a scissoring gait-walking on the balls of the feet. Onset of symptoms occurred during or after grass pea consumption and was not explained by other diseases [2, 16, 18]. Physicians experienced in the field examined and diagnosed all cases.

The collected quantitative data were entered and cleaned in Epi Info version 7.2, then exported to SPSS version 26.0 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, means, and standard deviations (SDs), and binary logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with neuropathy.

Variables with a p-value ≤ 0.2 in the bivariable analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model. However, certain variables, such as cattle ownership and the availability of grass pea farms, were excluded from the final model due to collinearity with other agricultural and socioeconomic variables, including land ownership and land-leasing status [19].These variables also lacked an independent association after adjustment.

More than half (56.8%) were aware that grass pea contains harmful substances. Two-fifths (40%) of them reported that consuming grass pea causes health problems. A high level of awareness of Yeguaya Beshita, the local term for neurolathyrism, was observed among participants, with 97.9% indicating prior knowledge of the term. In addition, 68.1% of respondents reported knowledge of the disease’s cause. This suggests a moderate level of understanding within the community regarding etiology and contributing factors of neurolathyrism.

Only a small percentage (5.5%) of respondents believed that Yeguaya Beshita was curable, reflecting a common perception of the disease as chronic or a limited awareness of available treatment options. In contrast, the vast majority (86%) believed that neurolathyrism is preventable. This highlights a positive attitude toward reducing the incidence of neurolathyrism through preventive measures and interventions.

The findings reveal significant insights into dietary habits and food preparation methods among individuals in the Delanta area of the Amhara Region, Ethiopia. 100% of participants reported using grass pea as their primary food source, indicating widespread reliance on it as a staple in the region. The majority of participants, 434 individuals (96.3%), reported consuming grass pea in the form of sauce. This indicates that preparing grass pea as a sauce is the most common cooking method among the population. Only 10 individuals (2.1%) reported consuming grass pea as pancakes; three (0.6%) reported consuming it roasted; and two individuals (0.4%) reported consuming it raw. Similarly, only three individuals (0.6%) reported consuming it boiled, and a total of eight individuals (1.8%) reported consuming it in bread form. 

Among the participants, 11.9% were diagnosed with neurolathyrism. This indicates that a significant portion of the population is affected by this condition. The median age of the patients was 41 years, with an interquartile range of 16–103 years. Eighteen of the patients were aged 31 to 40. The majority were male (69.6%). The median household family size is 4, with an interquartile range of [1–8].

Among those diagnosed with neurolathyrism, 16% reported experiencing stiffness, a symptom that can significantly impair mobility and quality of life. All patients diagnosed with neurolathyrism (100%) reported experiencing walking difficulty. This underscores the debilitating nature of the disease and its impact on mobility. A majority of patients (62.5%) reported experiencing tingling or numbness sensations. These sensory symptoms are common manifestations of neurological damage associated with neurolathyrism. A significant proportion (57%) of patients exhibited a spastic gait characterized by stiff, jerky movements.

Nearly one-fifth of patients (19.6%) reported toe walking, which may indicate lower-limb muscle weakness or contractures. All patients with neurolathyrism (100%) exhibited spinal flexion. Spinal deformities are common in advanced stages of the disease and can further exacerbate mobility issues. A vast majority (96.4%) of patients relied on a walking stick for mobility assistance. Walking aids are essential for improving stability and independence in individuals with neurolathyrism. Among those using walking sticks, the majority (78.6%) used one stick, while a smaller proportion (17.8%) used two sticks. This reflects varying degrees of mobility impairment among patients. A small percentage (3.6%) of patients were bedridden due to complete loss of leg use and contracture. Severe cases of neurolathyrism can lead to permanent disability and loss of functional independence. 

The findings revealed that 21.4% of the participants experienced divorce following the onset of the disease. Among those who divorced, 50% attributed the separation to stigma and conflict related to the disease. Furthermore, 73.2% of the participants did not attend school or classes, indicating a significant disruption in educational activities. Nearly half of the participants (46.4%) reported a lack of support from their relatives and society.

The multivariable analysis revealed several significant findings regarding the associations between various individual and household-level variables and neurolathyrism. Participants aged 46 years and older had higher odds of neurolathyrism than those aged 7–25 years (AOR = 2.69; 95% CI: 1.06–9.34). Compared with females, males had an AOR of 3.57 (95% CI: 1.79–7.10), signifying that they were more likely to be associated with neurolathyrism. Individuals from households with fewer than five members had higher odds of neurolathyrism than those from households with five or more members (AOR = 2.33; 95% CI: 1.16–4.69). In addition, participants from households that leased farmland were more likely to have neurolathyrism than those from households that did not lease farmland (AOR = 2.73; 95% CI: 1.23–6.06) (Tables 3 and 4).

These findings suggest that age, sex, educational status, family size, and farmland lease are all significantly associated with neurolathyrism in the multivariable analysis after adjusting for potential confounding variables. These results may help in understanding the associated factors and developing targeted interventions for the prevention and management of neurolathyrism. Only variables that remained independently associated with neurolathyrism after adjustment were retained in the final multivariable model. 

This study on the prevalence, associated factors, and social impact of neurolathyrism among patients in Delanta, Ethiopia, sheds light on the significant health burden posed by this condition and its wider societal implications. The findings revealed a considerable prevalence of neurolathyrism among the study participants, with 11.9% reporting having the disease. At the population level, the prevalence of neurolathyrism in two purposely selected kebeles of the Delanta district was estimated to be 6.6%.

The identified prevalence rates underscore the importance of addressing neurolathyrism as a public health priority in the region. Neurolathyrism, primarily caused by the consumption of the legume Lathyrus sativus, can result in severe neurological impairment, significantly impacting the quality of life of affected individuals. The higher household prevalence compared to population prevalence suggests that the condition may disproportionately affect certain households or communities. This highlights the importance of targeting interventions at the household level to address specific associated factors and mitigate the impact of neurolathyrism on affected households. The findings also highlight the urgent need for targeted interventions to prevent, detect early, and manage neurolathyrism within the community.

The above findings regarding the prevalence, associated factors, and social impact of neurolathyrism were supported by reports from previous studies. Several studies conducted in different countries reported that one of the consequences of Lathyrus sativus is severe neurological impairment, which significantly impacts the quality of life of affected individuals [2, 14, 20].

The reported household-level prevalence of 11.9% and population-level prevalence of 6.6% are markedly higher than those found in Dawunt District, where household-level prevalence was 9.2% and population-level prevalence 2.4% [21]. These figures are also higher than the 5.5% reported in the Begemeder region of Ethiopia [2]. This difference may reflect higher dependence on Lathyrus sativus, greater food insecurity, or cultural dietary practices specific to Delanta.

The clustering of cases within particular households parallels findings from Dawunt, where shared dietary patterns such as exclusive grass pea consumption and feeding immature seeds were significantly associated factors [21]. At the individual level, age, male sex, and low educational status were significantly associated with neurolathyrism in Delanta. This is consistent with findings from Dawunt: older age (AOR ≈ 7.4), male sex (AOR ≈ 7.8), and marital status were important associated factors. In contrast, some studies have reported no gender differences, suggesting that cultural food-role norms may modulate risk [21].

Low educational status was associated with neurolathyrism, consistent with patterns observed in other parts of Ethiopia and Bangladesh, where poor health literacy and limited awareness about grass pea toxicity contribute to higher disease rates [11].

At the household level, smaller family size and farmland leasing were significant in Delanta. In rural settings, a small family size may have a reduced labour force, limiting their capacity to diversify livelihood activities, cultivate multiple crops, or generate additional income [22, 23]. As a result, smaller households may depend more heavily on labour-efficient and drought-resilient crops such as grass pea, particularly during periods of food shortage. Labour constraints, rather than land, have been shown to limit production in many smallholder farming systems [24]. Further, smaller households may have fewer working-age adults to migrate or engage in wage labour, narrowing their resilience strategies during drought years and reinforcing dependence on grass pea as a primary staple. This reliance may increase exposure to grass pea and, consequently, neurolathyrism [22, 23]. This mirrors Dawunt’s findings, where household characteristics such as large size (AOR ≈ 12.6), exclusive grass pea feeding (AOR ≈ 8.8), feeding immature seeds (AOR ≈ 6.3), and high grass pea-to-cereal ratio (> 3:1) (AOR ≈ 6.1) were all strongly associated with neurolathyrism [18].

This study further explored the factors associated with the incidence of neurolathyrism. At the individual level, age, sex, and educational status emerged as significant variables. Old age was found to be associated with neurolathyrism, possibly due to prolonged exposure to associated factors or reduced adaptive capacity. Additionally, male sex was associated with increased odds of neurolathyrism, indicating potential gender-specific vulnerabilities. The biological underpinnings of male vulnerability to neurolathyrism warrant careful examination. Hormonal differences between sexes, genetic predispositions, and variations in metabolic pathways are among the factors that may contribute to this phenomenon. Additionally, sociocultural factors influencing dietary habits and access to alternative food sources may exacerbate this disparity.

Educational status was a significant factor: individuals with lower levels of education had higher odds of developing the disease. This finding underscores the importance of health literacy and disease prevention awareness.

At the household level, family size and farmland leases were identified as significant determinants of neurolathyrism. Farmland leases, reflecting socioeconomic status and access to resources, emerged as a significant factor, underscoring the socioeconomic determinants of health.

The study also highlights the profound social consequences experienced by individuals affected by neurolathyrism. A significant proportion of patients (73.2%) discontinued their education due to lack of support, while a notable percentage (21.4%) experienced marital dissolution following the onset of the disease. These findings underscore the broader socioeconomic impact of neurolathyrism, including educational disruption and social isolation, further emphasizing the need for comprehensive support mechanisms for affected individuals and their families.

In conclusion, this study provides valuable insights into the prevalence, associated factors, and social impact of neurolathyrism among patients in Delanta, Ethiopia. By identifying key determinants at both the individual and household levels, the research informs the development of targeted interventions to prevent and manage neurolathyrism. Furthermore, the findings underscore the need to address the broader socioeconomic consequences of the disease to promote the well-being and inclusion of affected individuals within the community. Coordinated efforts among healthcare professionals, policymakers, and community stakeholders are crucial to addressing the complex challenges posed by neurolathyrism and to improving health outcomes for affected populations.

This cross-sectional study on the prevalence, associated factors, and social impact of neurolathyrism in Delanta, Ethiopia, provides insights into the epidemiology of this condition. However, it is essential to acknowledge the methodological limitations when interpreting the findings.

First, cross-sectional studies provide a snapshot of a population, making it challenging to establish temporal relationships or determine causality between factors and neurolathyrism due to a lack of longitudinal data. Although associated factors were identified, causality cannot be established. Second, the study’s findings may not be generalizable to the broader population of the Delanta district or the Amhara Region, given the sampling method employed. The prevalence rates and associated factors identified may not accurately reflect the characteristics of the entire population. Third, all identified cases reported walking difficulty (100%); this may reflect selection bias, as individuals with mobility impairment were more likely to be recognized in this community-based study. Self-reported symptoms may also introduce reporting bias, as respondents with known neurolathyrism may overreport mobility difficulties.

Moreover, the study may not have accounted for all potential confounding variables that could influence the association between these factors and neurolathyrism. Unmeasured confounders could distort the observed relationships, leading to spurious conclusions. Even participants may underreport or overreport certain information due to social desirability bias or recall bias. This could affect the validity of the data collected, particularly regarding sensitive topics or behaviors associated with neurolathyrism. Nevertheless, the findings should be interpreted in light of these limitations. Future research employing longitudinal designs, probability sampling methods, and standardized diagnostic criteria can address some of these limitations and provide a more comprehensive understanding of neurolathyrism and its determinants.

The findings in this study show a high prevalence of neurolathyrism. Older age, male sex, educational status, family size, and farmland lease are significantly associated with neurolathyrism. This study provides valuable insights into community awareness, perceptions, and beliefs regarding neurolathyrism, as well as their knowledge of preventive measures. Addressing misconceptions and promoting an accurate understanding of the disease, its causes, and preventive strategies are essential for effective public health interventions aimed at reducing the burden of neurolathyrism within the population.

These findings also highlight the diverse array of physical symptoms experienced by patients with neurolathyrism, ranging from mobility difficulties to sensory and motor impairments. Understanding these symptoms is crucial for healthcare professionals to diagnose and manage the condition effectively, as well as to develop targeted interventions to improve the quality of life for affected individuals. Moreover, these findings underscore the multifaceted challenges individuals face with the disease, including social, emotional, and educational ramifications. Further research and interventions are warranted to address these complex issues and provide adequate support for affected individuals.
