# Clinical Significance of TERT Promoter Mutations in Neuroblastoma.
**Authors:** Persaud N, Gundem G, Kushner BH, Ladanyi M, Shukla N, Modak S
**Journal:** JCO Precis Oncol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1200/PO-25-00074](https://doi.org/10.1200/PO-25-00074)

## Content

PURPOSE: Telomere maintenance mechanisms including MYCN amplification (MYCN-A) (via upregulated telomerase reverse transcriptase [TERT] expression), TERT rearrangements (TERT-RA), and ATRX mutations confer neoplastic immortality in neuroblastoma (NB) cells. Clinical characterization of patients with NB harboring activating somatic TERT promoter point mutations (TERT-PM) in NB may improve stratification.

METHODS: To identify TERT-PM and TERT-RA, tumors were profiled by the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center-Integrated Mutation Profiling of Actionable Cancer Targets targeted next-generation sequencing platform and whole-genome sequencing, respectively. Associated clinical outcomes were studied.

RESULTS: TERT-PM and TERT-RA were detected in 17 of 603 (2.8%) and 31of 168 (18.4%) tumors from individual patients, respectively. The median age at diagnosis was 32 (range, 15-79) and 48 (range, 3-168) months for TERT-PM and TERT-RA, respectively. TERT-PM were located at canonical hotspots (C228T [16/17] and C250T [1/17]) and were concurrent with MYCN-A in 9 of 17 (53%). By contrast, MYCN-A occurred in 1 of 31 TERT-RA tumors. Most patients with TERT-PM had stage M (94%) and high-risk NB (HR-NB) (81%) at diagnosis. Twenty-eight patients with TERT-RA had HR-NB (90%). After risk appropriate therapy, complete response was achieved in 7 of 17 (41%) and 17 of 31 (55%) patients with TERT-PM and TERT-RA, respectively. The median progression-free (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were 9.7 ± 1.2 and 16.5 ± 2 months for patients with TERT-PM. Corresponding PFS/OS for patients with TERT-RA were 20 ± 6.4/56.8 ± 8.6 months (P = .015 for OS). Excluding MYCN-A status produced no significant survival difference (P > .1) between the two groups. CNS relapse occurred in 11 of 31 (35%) patients with TERT-RA versus 1 of 17 (6%) patients with TERT-PM.

CONCLUSION: TERT-PM is rarer than TERT-RA but both are associated with HR-NB and poor prognosis. MYCN-A frequently co-occurs with TERT-PM and might represent an ultra-high-risk subset of patients.

Neuroblastoma, the most common extracranial solid tumor of childhood, has a highly heterogenous clinical behavior presenting as locoregional or widely metastatic disease with spontaneous regression, cure with little or no cytotoxic therapy, or fatal outcomes despite aggressive multimodality treatment.

Advances in molecular techniques have highlighted the critical role of telomere maintenance mechanisms that stabilize telomeres for unlimited replicative capacity and immortality in the pathogenesis of high-risk neuroblastoma (HR-NB)1–4. Telomere maintenance is mediated by telomerase-dependent (classical) and independent (alternate) pathways. The former includes activation of the telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) gene. Reported mechanisms for TERT activation in neuroblastoma include MYCN amplification (MYCN-A)5 (via upregulation of TERT, a direct transcriptional target of MYCN) or rearrangement of the TERT locus6. Whereas MYCN-A in NB was discovered decades ago, TERT-rearrangements (TERT-RA) have only recently been described in NB and are associated with tumors of aggressive phenotype in NB despite not co-occurring with MYCN-A5–7. Genomic aberrations of the ATRX gene are most frequently associated with activation of the alternate lengthening of telomeres (ALT) pathway8. Among HR-NB, the most common telomeric maintenance mechanisms – TERT-RA, ATRX mutation, and MYCN-A appear to be mutually exclusive.

Activating somatic TERT promoter mutations (TERT-PM) have been described in several adult tumors including melanoma, glioblastoma, bladder cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, thyroid and non-small cell lung cancer9, typically involve two hotspot mutations: C228T and C250T9, and are associated with a poor prognosis in several tumor types.10,11 These mutations appear to be functionally redundant in that they both generate a new consensus binding motifs for E-twenty-six (ETS) transcription factors and the subsequent constitutional activation and overexpression of TERT12–15. In NB, TERT-PM have been anecdotally investigated in cell lines and have previously reported to be absent in NB16 . We first noted TERT-PM in 9 patients in an investigation into clonal evolution of NB,17 but clinical impact was not described. We have identified additional patients with NB with TERT-PM from an expanded pool of NB tumor samples and we now report on clinical findings in comparison with those associated with TERT-RA.

After obtaining approval from the Institutional Review Board of Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSK) records of patients with neuroblastoma who underwent targeted exome sequencing and/or whole genome sequencing between 1996–2023 were reviewed. No patients were excluded from analysis based on race, age, gender or ethnicity. Patients were enrolled on an institutional profiling protocol and informed consent was obtained (clinicaltrials.gov
NCT01775072). Patients harboring tumors with TERT-PM or TERT-RA were identified and included in analysis of clinical and biological data. Demographics are provided in Table 1. Staging was recorded using International Neuroblastoma Risk Group staging system and response using the International Neuroblastoma Response Criteria18. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as time from diagnosis to detection of progressive disease (PD). Overall survival (OS) time was censored at date of last contact. Survival was analyzed by the Kaplan-Meier method. Differences between groups were analyzed using chi-squared test.

Targeted tumor sequencing was performed using the CLIA-certified MSK-IMPACT platform that utilizes established pipelines followed by manual review were to characterize germline and somatic mutations, CN variants, and if targeted, genomic rearrangements18. WGS analysis was performed using the Isabl platform19 and included data quality control, variant calling for somatically acquired mutations for each variant class, and variant classification. Tumor DNA was extracted from fresh frozen or OCT-embedded tissue biopsies and matched normal DNA from buffy coat using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen). FFPE tissues were deparaffinized and DNA eluted. Genomic DNA was sheared, and sequencing libraries were prepared using the KAPA Hyper Prep Kit (Kapa Biosystems KK8504) with modifications. Libraries were subjected to a 0.5X selection using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter) after post-ligation cleanup. Libraries were not amplified by PCR and were pooled equivolume for sequencing. Samples were run on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina; RRID:SCR_016387). Tumors were covered to an average of 80–90X, and normal covered at 42–52X20. Briefly, upon completion of each sequencing run, Isabl imports paired tumor-normal FASTQ files, executes alignment using BWA-mem21 (RRID:SCR_010910) as implemented by pcap-core v2.18.2 wrapper [https://github.com/cancerit/PCAP-core] and quality control using MultiQC (v1.9) (https://github.com/ewels/MultiQC) (RRID:SCR_014982), FastQC (v0.11.5) (https://github.com/s-andrews/FastQC) (RRID:SCR_014583) and Picard (v2.25.6) (https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard) (RRID:SCR_006525). Genome-wide median coverage was calculated using Mosdepth (RRID:SCR_018929)22. Estimation of tumor purity/ploidy and identification of copy number aberrations were performed using the Battenberg algorithm23. High-confidence mutations for single nucleotide variants (SNVs), insertions and deletions (indels) and structural variations were identified as those mutations called by at least two out of three algorithms run: 1) Caveman24 (RRID:SCR_017089), Mutect25 (RRID:SCR_000559) and Strelka26 (RRID:SCR_005109) for SNVs 2) cgpPindel27 (RRID:SCR_0107090), Mutect and Stelka for Indels 3) SvABA28 (RRID:SCR_022998), GRIDSS29 and BRASS (RRID:SCR_017091) [https://github.com/cancerit/BRASS] for SVs. High-confidence somatic SNV/indels were annotated for functional consequence using Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (v92)30 (RRID:SCR_007931) and VAGrENT (v3.3.0) (RRID:SCR_005180). Mutations were further classified with regards to their putative role in cancer pathogenesis by assessing their clinical relevance using OncoKb (v1)31 (RRID:SCR_014782), COSMIC (v92)32 and ClinVar (2022_03_20)33 databases. Structural variants were annotated using a modified version [https://github.com/papaemmelab/AnnotSV] of AnnotSV [https://github.com/lgmgeo/AnnotSV]. Mutational signature analysis was performed with the MutationalPatterns package (v1.6.1)34 (RRID:SCR_024247) and using the COSMIC Mutational Signatures (v3.1) with the addition of Temozolomide signature from Kucab et al.35. Microsatellite-instability status was assessed using MSISensor36 (RRID:SCR_006418). Further details of the analysis pipeline have been reported previously37.

TERT-PM were assessed using either IMPACT or WGS. However, since the former is not sensitive for detection of rearrangements, TERT-RA were evaluated only by WGS37.

Clonal structure was analyzed using the union of high-confidence SNVs called in all WGS samples for a patient. DPClust (v0.2.2, https://github.com/Wedge-Oxford/dpclust) was used for calculation of cancer cell fraction corrected for purity and local CN, as well as clustering and assignment of mutations across samples with the exception of the Gibbs Sampling Dirichlet Process step that was optimized internally23. Clonal ordering was deduced using clonevol38 (v0.99.11, https://github.com/hdng/clonevol). Mutational signatures were computed in each cluster independently. Figures were generated with matplotlib (v3.1.0, https://matplotlib.org/).

Tumor tissue was homogenized in 1mL TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) followed by phase separation with 200 μL chloroform. RNA was extracted from the aqueous phase using the miRNeasy Micro Kit on the QIAcube Connect (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol with 350 μL input. Samples were eluted in 15 μL of RNase-free water. After RiboGreen quantification (Thermo Fischer Scientific) and quality control by Agilent BioAnalyzer (RRID:SCR_018043), 18ng- 1μg of total RNA with an RNA integrity number varying from 1 to 0.9 underwent ribosome depletion and library preparation using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA LT Kit (Illumina) according to instructions provided by the manufacturer with 8 cycles of PCR. Samples were barcoded and run on a HiSeq 2500 in Rapid Mode or HiSeq4000 at PE100 or on a NovaSeq 6000 et PE150, using the HiSeq SBS Kit v2, HiSeq 3000/4000 SBS Kit or NovaSeq 6000 SP, S1 or S4 Reagent Kit (300 cycles (Illumina)). Sequencing was performed to achieve a median of 80 million plated reads per sample. Processing of RNA sequencing was performed as described previously37.

MSK-IMPACT sequencing data is deposited to AACR Project GENIE (https://genie.cbioportal.org). All additional data generated in this study are not publicly available to protect patient privacy but are available upon reasonable request from the corresponding author.

Of 603 NB tumors from individual patients profiled by MSK-IMPACT, 17 (2.8%) had TERT-PM. Of these 17 samples with TERT-PM, 13 (77%) were identified prior to relapse (at diagnosis or after neoadjuvant chemotherapy). At diagnosis, patients with TERT-PM were 16–79 (median 30) months old; 16 (94%) had metastatic disease, and 15 (88%) had HR-NB (Table 1). One patient with MYCN-non-amplified (MYCN-NA) stage L2 diagnosed at age 16 months was considered to have low-risk NB. One patient with intermediate-risk NB had MYCN-NA stage M disease diagnosed at age 15 months. Nine patients, all with stage M at diagnosis, had MYCN-A.

All 15 patients with stage M HR-NB received appropriate neoadjuvant induction chemotherapy. The stage L2 patient presented with an incidentally discovered left adrenal mass and was followed without intervention. But 16 months after diagnosis, he had widespread PD received HR-NB therapy. However, 7 months after achieving remission PD occurred again. Salvage therapy achieved another remission but 22 months after diagnosis he developed myeloid neoplasm post-cytotoxic therapy which was successfully treated. He remains disease-free 56+ months post-diagnosis of NB and 30+ months post-diagnosis of leukemia. (Table 2) The patient with intermediate-risk NB at diagnosis received risk-appropriate chemotherapy plus crizotinib (for ALK mutation R1245C) but developed rapid osteomedullary PD and died of disease 7 months post-diagnosis.

Of 168 tumors from individual patients with NB with tumors profiled by WGS, 31 (18.4%) had TERT-RA. At diagnosis, patients with TERT-RA were 3–168 (mean 48) months old and 28 (90%) had Stage M HR-NB. Three patients had intermediate-risk locoregional NB (all stage L2). Two of the latter, respectively diagnosed at 1 month and 4 years, developed metastatic PD, and died of disease 48 months and 14 months post-diagnosis. The third stage L2 patient was diagnosed at age 4 years, had PD which was treated with aggressive multi-modality HR-NB therapy, and remains disease-free 40+ months post-diagnosis (Supplemental Table 1).

In tumors with TERT-PM, C288T and C250T hotspot mutations were observed in 94% (16/17) and 6% (1/17) of patients, respectively. MYCN-A was co-expressed in 9/17 (53%) and ALK mutations in 5/17 (29%) (Table 1). As expected, no tumor co-expressed TERT-PM and ATRX aberrations or TERT-RA. No patient had oncogenic germline aberrations in TERT, although 1 patient each with TERT-PM carried germline mutations in CHEK2 and PHOX2B. Of the 31 tumors with TERT-RA, only 1 co-expressed MYCN-A. No aberrations were noted in TERT-PM, ATRX or ALK in tumors with TERT-RA.

TERT mRNA expression was assessed in 184 NB tumor samples. patients. TERT mRNA expression was significantly higher in MYCN-A tumors ( n=25) when compared to MYCN-NA (n=159). In the MYCN-NA cohort, mRNA expression was significantly higher in patients with TERT-RA (n=27) compared to wild type TERT (n=126) (p=0.0000006). There was no statistically significant difference in expression in TERT-PM compared to wild type TERT or TERT-RA though there appeared to be a trend towards higher expression in tumors harboring TERT-PM (Supplemental Figure 1).

Five patients with TERT-PM had ≥2 separate tumors available for analysis. In 4 patients TERT-PM was shared clonally across all tumors while in 1 patient (H132374) it was present at 5% in the diagnostic tumor and became fully clonal in a lesion metastatic to the brain. Seven TERT-RA patients had ≥2 tumors evaluable. In 3 patients TERT-RA was shared by all tumors tested. In all 7 patients there were additional TERT-RA in later subclones. In 5/6 patients with diagnostic and relapse tumors the subclonal TERT-RA was specific to the relapse. (Supplemental Figure 2) These relapse-specific rearrangements result in increased copy number and expression of TERT consistent with an augmented addiction to TERT signaling17.

Twelve patients of 17 with TERT-PM underwent attempted resection of primary tumor after induction chemotherapy. Gross total (R1) resection was possible in 11 (90%). Resection was not attempted in 4 due to disease progression while receiving induction chemotherapy. Initial management in 1 patient was watchful observation without attempt at resection. For the 17 patients with TERT-PM, responses to induction were CR in 7 (41.2%), PR in 3 (17.6%), SD in 2 (11.7%) and PD in 5 (29.4%). One patient died of sepsis.

Of 31 patients with TERT-RA, 1 patient had PD while receiving induction chemotherapy, and resection was not attempted after partial response in another. Resection was attempted in the other 29 with gross total resection possible in 23 (79%).

For the 31 patients with TERT-RA, responses to induction were CR in 17 (55%), PR in 10 (34%), SD in 2 (7%) and PD in 2 (3%).

There was no significant difference in major responses (CR+PR) to induction between the two groups (p>0.05) though PD while receiving therapy was significantly higher in the TERT-PM group (p=0.03).

Median PFS and OS of patients with TERT-PM was 9.7 ± 1.2 and 20 ± 6.4 months, respectively (Table 3). Corresponding survival probabilities for the TERT-RA group were 16.5 ± 1.1 and 56.8 ± 8.6 months (Figure 1). There was no difference in PFS between the 2 groups. OS was significantly better for TERT-RA than TERT-PM (p=0.01). When patients with MYCN-A were removed from the analysis, there was no statistically significant difference in survival between the 2 groups (p>0.1 for PFS and OS). PFS and OS were similar in patients with and without MYCN-A in the TERT-PM group (n=8 and 9, respectively). Patterns of relapse were similar among patients with TERT-PM and TERT-RA with one exception: central nervous system relapse occurred in 11/31 (35%) patients with TERT-RA (median follow-up 48 months) versus 1/17 (6%) patients with TERT-PM (median follow-up 16.5 months). Five patients with TERT-PM survive at a median follow up of 21 months. All received similar multimodality therapy to those who relapsed (Table 2). However, non-relapsed patients appeared to have highly chemosensitive disease, 4/5 achieving CR compared to only 2/15 patients who relapsed. Of the 603 patients with tumors undergoing testing by MSK-IMPACT, PFS and OS could be determined in a total of 520 including 17 with TERT-PM. For the 503 patients whose tumors did not harbor TERT-PM, median PFS and OS was 32.6 ± 1.2 and 145 ± 15.3 months respectively (p<0.001 for both when compared to those with TERT-PM).

TERT-RA is one of the more common genetic aberrations in NB with some investigators reporting an incidence of 20–30% of HR-NB16. A similar incidence (16.7%) was encountered in our cohort of patients. Both MYCN, a known transcriptional activator of TERT and TERT-RA independently activate TERT gene transcription and converge on telomerase-dependent telomere maintenance mechanisms5–7,39. The presence of TERT-RA is associated with a poor prognosis in patients with NB with initial observation in small numbers of patients indicating survival of 20–40%6,7.

TERT-PM first described in patients with melanoma40,41 have since been discovered in several other malignancies and in general are associated with a poor prognosis. However, little is known about TERT-PM in NB. In reports on tumor screens for presence of TERT-PM, one report observed their presence in 3/16 NB cell lines tested42 and another in 2/22 primary tumors43. A prior study reported on the absence of TERT-PM in NB tumors from 131 patients. The lack of TERT-PM in that study could be attributed to the relatively small number of samples tested for an aberration that is rare (2.8% in our cohort) and inclusion of a relatively few HR patients in the published study16. On the other hand, our study surveyed tumors from > 500 patients. In our cohort, TERT-PM mutations in NB occured in the canonical hotspots at a relative frequency typical for that reported in other malignancies i.e. C228T being far more frequent than C250Y (93% vs 7% in our cohort) and were mutually exclusive, i.e. no tumor harbored both mutations. As expected, ATRX aberrations were not observed in any tumor with TERT-PM. TERT-PM mutations were less common than TERT-RA (3% vs 18%) and there was mutual exclusivity between the two aberrations. In NB, TERT-RA has been reported to be mutually exclusive with MYCN-A6,7. Indeed, we encountered co-expression of MYCN-A and TERT-RA in only 1/31 tumors tested. On the other hand, TERT-PM were both observed in MYCN-A and MYCN-NA tumors. 50% of our patients had co-expression of TERT-PM and MYCN-A. TERT-PM appear to arise early in the development of the disease; all 4 patients in whom multiple samples were available, had TERT-PM noted before and after relapse.

We first observed TERT-PM in 9 patients17 but did not previously study their clinical implications. In this current expanded cohort that includes samples from the prior study, we noted TERT-PM in a small proportion of NB tumors with an incidence of 2.8%. However, with the increasing use of NGS in assessment of NB tumors, it is possible that further tumors with TERT-PM will be discovered. TERT-PM, similar to TERT-RA appear to confer an aggressive clinical phenotype. In our cohort of 17 patients, TERT-PM were invariably associated with HR-NB (15/17). Furthermore, the 2 patients that were deemed to have low or intermediate risk disease at diagnosis, had rapid disease progression. Response to induction therapy appeared to be similar in both TERT-RA and TERT-PM, but patients with the latter had a higher rate of PD while receiving chemotherapy (even without concurrent MYCN-A). Both TERT-PM and TERT-RA were associated with a dismal prognosis with median long-term PFS and OS < 5 years. The prognosis appeared to be even worse in those patients with tumors co-expressing TERT-PM and MYCN-A, though the individual contribution of each genetic aberration could not be determined. However in patients harboring tumors with TERT-PM, robust response to induction chemotherapy appeared to imbue patients with a better prognosis. Four of five patients who did not experience relapse had CR to induction chemotherapy compared to 2/15 who relapsed.

CNS relapse is rare in NB occuring in 5–10% of patients with NB44,45 with a higher incidence in MYCN-A tumors compared to MYCN-NA (11% of MYCN-A vs 3%)46,47. Our group previously reported that MYCN-NA CNS metastases show focal gains at 5p15.33, a region corresponding to the TERT locus and have higher higher TERT mRNA expression, suggesting a role of TERT in CNS relapse of NB. However, we had not hitherto performed genomic TERT studies48. It is therefore not surprising that TERT aberrations would be associated with CNS metastases: we report CNS metastases in 38% of patients with TERT-RA tumors. Although CNS metastases occurred in only 1/16 patients with TERT-PM, the median follow up of this cohort is relatively short. TERT-PM have been detected at increased frequency in thyroid malignancies metastatic to the CNS, 14/15 in one study49.

NB is a disease well known for its biologic and clinical heterogeneity, and patients have benefited from improved understanding of the genetic and/or molecular properties of individual tumors. Despite the reported independent prognostic impact of genetic aberrations impacting telomere-maintenance mechanisms, neither ATRX nor TERT aberrations are included in the current stratification and management strategy of HR-NB. Although in published reports, TERT-RA have been reported exclusively in patients deemed to have high-risk NB, TERT-PM can be observed even in those patients considered to have low or intermediate risk disease as in 2 of the patients in our cohort. In one of these patients, administration of high-dose multimodality therapy resulted in remission after relapse and he continues to be in remission 44 months after initiation of salvage therapy. Finally, attempts are currently being made to target TERT. The FDA has recently approved a telomerase inhibitor, imetelstat, for MDS50 which has already undergone phase I evaluation in children51. Another telomerase-interacting compound, 6-thio-2’-deoxyguanosine (6-thio-dG)52, has been tested preclinically in NB models. Several other therapeutic approaches targeting TERT and related molecules are being investigated for a variety of cancers53. Therefore, routine analysis for TERT-PM and TERT-RA may arguably help refine risk stratification and identify patients with NB who may be eligible for trials of novel TERT-targeted agents.
