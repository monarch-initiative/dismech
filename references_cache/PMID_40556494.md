# Structural Alterations Associated With Cardiovascular Autonomic Failure in Multiple System Atrophy.
**Authors:** Georges C, Arribarat G, Kermorgant M, Bonneville F, Fabbri M, Foubert A, Meissner W, Rascol O, Traon AP, Péran P
**Journal:** Eur J Neurol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1111/ene.70212](https://doi.org/10.1111/ene.70212)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Early severe autonomic failure (AF) in multiple system atrophy (MSA) is a risk factor for poor survival. Postmortem studies suggested that AF is related to the degeneration of preganglionic autonomic neurons of the brainstem and spinal cord.

OBJECTIVES: Characterize cerebral alterations on brain imaging associated with cardiovascular AF.

METHODS: Cardiovascular sympathetic failure was evaluated through orthostatic hypotension (OH) based on changes in systolic and diastolic blood pressure during tilt-test (ΔSBP and ΔDBP). Reduced heart rate (HR) variability reflecting cardio-vagal impairment was assessed with a composite score formed by the root-mean square differences of successive R-R intervals (RMSSD) and HR changes during deep breathing. Voxel-based morphometry (SPM12), volumetry, and cortical thickness measurements (FreeSurfer 7.0) of T1-weighted anatomical images were used to assess gray matter (GM) atrophy in sub-tentorial structures. Multivariate analysis included age, disease severity (UMSARS), and total intracranial volume as confounding factors.

RESULTS: A total of 62 MSA patients followed at the French Reference Center were retrospectively included, aged 67.3 ± 8.6 years, 69.4% MSA-P, disease duration 4.2 ± 2.1 years. Medulla atrophy was correlated to OH (p < 0.006). Decrease in GM volume in the left anterior cerebellum (lobule V) was correlated to ΔDBP (pFWEc = 0.017). GM loss in the left interposed nucleus was correlated to ΔSBP (p < 0.003), whereas atrophy of the right dentate was associated with decreased HR variability (p < 0.003).

CONCLUSION: Medulla volume was strongly correlated with OH. Cerebellar degeneration was associated with the severity of cardiovascular AF.

Multiple system atrophy (MSA) is a rare, rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disease, characterized by a variable combination of motor signs including parkinsonism poorly responsive to Levodopa (MSA‐P) or cerebellar ataxia (MSA‐C), possible pyramidal signs, and autonomic failure [1]. The latter is a key feature in MSA that often begins before the first motor sign and progressively worsens to involve all spheres of the autonomic nervous system [2].

Cardiovascular autonomic failure is particularly disabling as it can lead to impairment of baroreflex‐mediated vasoconstriction resulting in orthostatic hypotension (OH) and to decreased heart rate variability (HRV) [3]. Normal autonomic cardiovascular responses are coordinated by several brainstem nuclei including the nucleus of the solitary tract, the caudal and rostral regions of the ventrolateral medulla, the ambiguous nucleus, and the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus. These receive inputs from higher‐level structures grouped under the term central autonomic network, including the insula, thalamus, and cerebellum [4]. The exact neural mechanisms responsible for cardiovascular autonomic failure in MSA are not well known. The degeneration of preganglionic sympathetic and parasympathetic neurons is believed to be key [5]. Postmortem studies have shown neuronal loss in some brainstem nuclei involved in autonomic cardiovascular responses such as the rostral ventrolateral medulla, the ambiguous nucleus, and the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus [3, 5, 6]. Whilst current MRI techniques make it possible to explore brainstem anatomy in living individuals, only one published study has investigated the relationship between cardiovascular autonomic failure and brainstem morphology in MSA [7]. A limitation of this work was that changes in brainstem anatomy were assessed using anteroposterior diameters manually measured on T1‐weighted images from 1.5 and 3 T MRI. The small size of the brainstem requires consideration of the spatial resolution of the technique used. For instance, macroscopic brainstem atrophy can be efficiently and accurately measured using automated segmentation approaches [8]. On a smaller scale, patterns of local gray matter (GM) atrophy can be examined within the entire brain using voxel‐based morphometry (VBM) [9].

Our aim was therefore to use T1‐weighted anatomical images from 3 T MRI to determine GM atrophy associated with baroreflex dysfunction (i.e., OH) and reduced HRV in patients with MSA. Based on previous postmortem studies, we hypothesized that these structural alterations would be located in the brainstem with a particular involvement of small nuclei within the medulla. Given the evidence that the degenerative process particularly affects the cerebellum and the putamen, we hypothesized that architectural changes in these structures might also be associated with autonomic dysfunction.

MSA‐P and MSA‐C patients followed at the French Reference center for MSA (Toulouse site) were retrospectively included. To be included, patients had to: (i) meet former consensus diagnostic criteria for probable or possible MSA [10], (ii) have performed a cardiovascular autonomic evaluation and a brain MRI within 3 months of each other. This restrictive time window was chosen because of possible rapid worsening of structural alterations in MSA. Patients were classified by an experienced neurologist into MSA‐P and MSA‐C subtypes based on their predominant motor symptoms. Exclusion criteria consisted of pathological MRI findings other than mild white matter (WM) hyperintensity and significant MRI movement artifacts.

All patients provided written informed consent and the study was carried out in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. All patients are part of a prospective longitudinal natural history cohort that has been registered with the CNIL (Commission Nationale Informatique et Liberté no. 1338780; CCTIRS, no. 10.065).

Clinical assessment was performed using a standardized scale for MSA (Unified Multiple System Atrophy Rating Scale, UMSARS [11]). To assess the severity of the disease, UMSARS I and II were summed up (UMSARS I + II) to form a score ranging from 0 to 104, with higher scores indicating a more severe illness.

Blood pressure (BP) was measured with a sphygmomanometer in the supine position every minute during 10 min and in a 70°–80° head‐up tilt to detect OH [12]. We calculated the maximum difference between supine and tilting systolic and diastolic BP (SBP, DBP), following the same procedure as in previously published studies [13]. These changes assess the sympathetic response of the baroreflex. BP changes during the Valsalva maneuver were not used in this study, as no significant association was found in previous exploratory analyses in a subset of 56 patients (data not shown).

In healthy individuals, the time intervals between consecutive heartbeats are constantly changing in order to allow a rapid adaptation to variation in homeostasis. These variations are called HRV and reflect the shifts in cardiac autonomic regulation. Continuous beat‐to‐beat digital BP (Nexfin) and ECG were recorded and computerized (LabChart 7.0) during several standardized cardiovascular reflex tests. The deep breathing test (six cycles/min) induces fluctuations in HR and allows evaluating respiratory sinus arrhythmia, that is, a relative bradycardia during expiration and a relative tachycardia during inspiration. The magnitude of this variability is assessed by subtracting the maximum HR during inspiration from the minimum HR during expiration. This is a direct measure of parasympathetic cardiac control [14]. HRV was assessed at rest from a continuous ECG stable signal, free of noise and artifact [15]. The root‐mean square differences of successive R‐R intervals (RMSSD) are time‐domain indices that estimate changes in HRV induced by the vagus nerve [16]. A composite score was calculated, formed by the sum of RMSSD and HR changes during deep breathing, each divided by its mean value in the population. This score was estimated in 56 patients included in this study and allows a comprehensive evaluation of cardio‐vagal modulation in a spontaneous and reflex manner.

All MRI data were acquired with 3T scanners. 57 brain MRI were performed between 2013 and 2021 at the neuroradiology department (Toulouse University Hospital). Scanning protocols included 3‐dimensional T1‐weighted images acquired in the sagittal plane with a fitting 32‐channel head coil. Three different scanners were used with various acquisition parameters (see Data S1).

In five patients (8.1%), a high‐resolution T1‐weighted anatomical image was obtained during participation in the MultiPAMS and SYMPATH studies that were conducted between 2013 and 2017 at the Inserm‐UPS UMR1214 Technical Platform (Philips Achieva dStream 3 T 32‐channel coils) [17, 18].

First, we performed an unbiased analysis using WBM to determine which patterns of regional atrophy (i.e., decreased local GM density) were associated with cardiovascular dysfunction. Using volumetry, we investigated in the second section the association of global brainstem, cerebellar, and putaminal atrophy with cardiovascular dysfunction. We also sought to study the impact of neuronal loss within specific nuclei using region‐based volumetry (Figure 1).

To minimize differences induced by the heterogeneous MRI acquisition sources, the following steps were performed before image analysis: (i) interpolation, (ii) denoising using the AONLM (Adaptive Optimized Nonlocal Means [19]) program running under MATLAB (The MathWorks, Massachusetts, US), and (iii) intensity bias correction included in Statistical Parametric Mapping version 12 software (SPM12, Welcome Trust Centre for Neuroimage, University College London, UK).

Using the automated FreeSurfer software (v.7.0), the following steps were performed: removal of non‐brain tissue [20], automated Talairach transformation, segmentation of the subcortical WM and deep GM volumetric structures [21], intensity normalization [22], tessellation of the GM/WM boundary, automated topology correction [23], and surface deformation following intensity gradients [24]. The output of each step was visually inspected to ensure accurate preprocessing. Subcortical volumes (medulla oblongata, pons, mesencephalon, cerebellar WM, and of the right and left putamen) were obtained with automated subcortical segmentation. Parcellation of the cerebellar cortex was used to measure cortical thickness of the right and left cerebellar hemispheres [25]. The volume of each structure was divided by the estimated total intracranial volume (TIV) to correct for inter‐individual differences in brain sizes (i.e., normalized volume).

To study specific brainstem nuclei, we used the Brainstem Navigator toolkit [26, 27, 28, 29]. This collection includes atlas labels and probabilistic labels (range: 0–1) of in vivo brainstem nuclei. It is built in two stereotaxic spaces: (i) Illinois Institute of Technology: IIT Human Brain Atlas (v.5.0), matrix: 256 × 256 × 256, or (ii) Montreal Neurological Institute: MNI, matrix: 182 × 218 × 182. It includes GM regions of the brainstem involved in wakefulness/sleep, autonomic, motor, sensory, and limbic functions. To segment our T1‐weighted MRI data, we used the 3 GM, WM, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) tissue probability maps (TPM) from the IIT Human Brain Atlas stereotaxic space and the in vivo brainstem probabilistic nuclei labels from the Brainstem Navigator toolkit. The probabilistic labels were added as additional TPMs in the SPM software.

To correct for size and shape variations between brains of different subjects, we used the DARTEL method [30] to create common GM and WM reference images. Previously obtained GM and WM probability maps were iteratively aligned (7 iterations with a degressive Gaussian filter) to form a study‐specific model that includes 10 brainstem nuclei for which current knowledge indicates a contribution to ANS regulation. The GM and WM probability maps were then “warped” into the specific model obtained with the DARTEL method. The spatial transformation from normalization to standard MNI152 space was calculated and then applied using the “preserve” option. An isotropic Gaussian smoothing of 4 mm width at half maximum (FWHM) was applied. This kernel size value was chosen to maintain a spatial resolution comparable to the average size of brainstem nuclei.

Regions of interest covered the three deep cerebellar nuclei and nine regions in the medulla, including (i) the superior medullary reticular formation that hosts the rostral ventrolateral medulla, (ii) the inferior medullary reticular formation that hosts the caudal ventrolateral medulla, and (iii) a viscero‐sensori‐motor nuclei complex encompassing the nucleus of the solitary tract, the vagus, and the hypoglossal nerves. Atlas labels incorporating these nuclei have been used with the following: Brainstem Navigator [31], Neuromorphometrics [32], and SUIT [33]. The GM concentration value at each voxel bounded by the mask of the nucleus of interest was extracted and then averaged over the entire cluster using SPM12. These values were then used for the analysis of the regions of interest.

Data are shown as mean ± standard deviations or n (frequencies), except when otherwise indicated. Group differences between MSA‐P and MSA‐C were compared for demographic, clinical, and autonomic variables by chi‐squared or Mann–Whitney tests.

Correlations between local GM density and autonomic variables were performed using a voxel‐to‐voxel general linear model provided by SPM12. The analysis was repeated separately for each autonomic variable. The model included GM density as a dependent factor, the autonomic variable as an independent factor, and TIV as a nuisance factor. TIV is commonly used as a nuisance variable in the literature using VBM. An explicit mask [34] was used to restrict the analysis to sub‐tentorial voxels and thus limit the number of multiple comparisons. Significant clusters were assessed by a two‐tailed t‐test (p
FWEc ≤ 0.05, family wise error corrected at a peak level, minimum cluster size 20 contiguous voxels) and then overlaid to the study‐specific GM template for visualization. The anatomical identification of these clusters was performed using a SPM plugin with probabilistic cytoarchitectonic maps (JuBrain Anatomy Toolbox 3.0) [35].

Normalized volumes of the brainstem and cerebellum were plotted to determine the association between gross anatomy and cardiovascular autonomic variables. Mean values of GM concentration in each nucleus of interest were also plotted to study the specific influence of candidate nuclei. Correlations with autonomic variables were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (r
s). Multivariate linear regressions were then performed to include TIV, age, and disease severity (UMSARS I + II) as covariates. Normalization based on TIV and age has been proven to significantly reduce the inter‐individual variability of total brainstem volumes [8]. Disease severity is closely linked to neuronal degeneration in MSA [36]. The Bonferroni correction was used to correct for multiple comparisons. Exploratory univariate analysis using Spearman's rank was conducted in both MSA‐P and MSA‐C subgroups.

Statistical analyses were performed by XLSTAT 2020 (Addinsoft, France) and RStudio (2022.02.2). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, unless otherwise specified.

We included 62 patients (age: 67.3 ± 8.6 years, 43 MSA‐P and 19 MSA‐C). Table 1 summarizes demographic, clinical, and cardiovascular characteristics of the patients. MSA subtypes were comparable regarding age, disease duration, and severity. MSA‐P patients had higher levels of levodopa equivalent daily dose compared to MSA‐C patients. HR changes induced by deep breathing were lower in MSA‐P. However, we found no significant difference in other parameters assessing cardio‐vagal function (RMSSD, HRV composite score). Forty patients (64.5%) had OH, with no difference between MSA‐P and MSA‐C patients.

The non‐a priori analysis using VBM revealed that a specific pattern of cerebellar GM atrophy was associated with OH (Table 2, Figure 2). A significant reduction in GM density in the left anterior cerebellar cortex (lobule V) was associated with a greater fall in DBP during head‐up tilt (T = 5.11, p
FWEc = 0.017). This association was also observed in a supplementary VBM analysis restricted to the MSA‐P group (T = 5.46, p
FWEc = 0.016).

The link between cerebellar alterations and sympathetic cardiovascular impairment was confirmed in the region of interest approach. A significant reduction in GM density in cerebellar deep nuclei (i.e., dentate, fastigial, interposed) was associated with a more pronounced fall in SBP and DBP during head‐up tilt (Table 3a). The association between decreased GM density in the left interposed nucleus and the fall in SBP during head‐up tilt persisted significantly after Bonferroni correction (p
B = 0.002).

When analyzing the impact of gross atrophy using volumetry, we did not find any relationship between the volume of cerebellar WM or the cortical thickness of the cerebellar hemispheres and OH. Global atrophy of the medulla was, however, associated with a more pronounced fall of BP during head‐up tilt (p

B
 = 0.002) (Table 3b). This association was also observed in a supplementary univariate analysis restricted to the MSA‐P group (Data S1).

Global atrophy of the putamen was significantly associated with OH (Table 3b). Atrophy of the putamen was correlated with impaired sympathetic responses in the form of a more severe SBP and DBP drop during head‐up tilt test. However, this correlation did not survive correction for multiple comparisons.

The non a priori analysis using VBM revealed an association between local cerebellar atrophy and reduced HRV. A greater reduction in GM density in the region of the right dentate nucleus was associated with a lower HRV composite score, that is to a more severe cardio‐vagal impairment (anterior part: T = 5, p
FWEc = 0.032; posterior part: T = 6.52, p
FWEc < 0.001).

Regarding the specific role of structural changes in the deep nuclei of the cerebellum, reduced GM density in bilateral dentate nuclei and right interposed nucleus was correlated with lower HRV composite score. These associations were also observed in a supplementary analysis restricted to the MSA‐P group (Data S1). Among these, only the right dentate GM survived correction for multiple comparisons (p
B < 0.001).

Volumetric analysis did not reveal any relationship between the global macroscopic structure of the cerebellum or the brainstem and reduced HRV. There was an association between global atrophy of the putamen and reduced HRV, but this association did not survive correction for multiple comparisons.

Our results showed that structural alterations in several sub‐tentorial brain regions are associated with cardiovascular autonomic dysfunction in MSA. Local depletion of GM in the cerebellar cortex and in the deep cerebellar nuclei was linked with OH severity and reduced HRV. Medulla atrophy was also associated with OH but appeared to have less influence on the decrease in HRV.

It has been considered that the degeneration of spinal intermediolateral preganglionic sympathetic neurons is correlated with the severity of OH in patients with MSA [37], in contrast to the peripheral sympathetic innervation which is relatively preserved [3]. Evidence from postmortem studies has suggested that impairment of supraspinal control may also contribute to the pathogenesis of this altered baroreflex efferent control of BP in MSA. In our work, global atrophy of the medulla was associated with an impaired sympathetic baroreflex response. This result is in line with previous studies suggesting this impaired regulation may result from the loss of the C1 group of epinephrine‐synthesizing neurons of the rostral region of the ventrolateral medulla, a component of the intermediate reticular zone of the medulla [3, 5, 6]. Although the exact mechanism behind the impaired HRV in patients with MSA remains unclear, it has been proposed that loss of cholinergic neurons in the ambiguous nucleus and dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus might be involved [3]. We did not find, however, any correlation between the global volume of the medulla and reduced HRV. In addition, we failed to identify any association between the specific volume of several nuclei of the medulla and cardiovascular autonomic failure. We can speculate that the neuronal damage may not have been significant enough to be detectable. The study of brainstem nuclei is very challenging when using T1‐weighted structural images due to low contrast between these small structures and the surrounding WM parenchyma. The size of these structures was at the limit of the spatial resolution since the average volume of the medulla was 4.1 mL and the nuclei are no more than a few millimeters in diameter. Thus, despite several methodological precautions, we cannot rule out an imperfect segmentation of the GM in brainstem nuclei or a defect in spatial resolution that compromised their precise localization.

Our work showed an association between cerebellar cell loss and the severity of autonomic failure in MSA. We have identified a correlation between the atrophy of the anterior cerebellar cortex and OH severity. However, this association was not reproduced when considering the overall cortical thickness of the cerebellar hemispheres. Our results also suggest that neuronal loss within the deep cerebellar nuclei might be linked with cardiovascular sympathetic and parasympathetic failure.

We did not perform subgroup analysis on MSA‐C patients due to the small number of subjects. However, almost all these associations were also observed in subgroup analyses restricted to MSA‐P patients. Cerebellar cell loss is in fact common in both MSA subtypes, as shown by numerous anatomopathological and MRI studies. The degenerative process in the Cerebellum is known to affect the cell bodies of Purkinje cells [38, 39, 40, 41], white matter fibers [41, 42], and cerebellar nuclei such as the dentate nucleus [38, 43]. That said, our results suggest that severe autonomic failure reflects a more widespread neurodegeneration, regardless of patients' motor phenotype (cerebellar of parkinsonian). Yet, we cannot rule out the hypothesis of a role of the cerebellum in the functioning of the baroreflex. Some electrophysiological [44] and fMRI [45, 46, 47] studies have provided evidence supporting this idea, and few authors have proposed that the cerebellum contributes to compensating for large BP variation through the vestibulo‐sympathetic reflex and the baroreflex [44, 48].

Putamen atrophy was closely related to the severity of cardiovascular autonomic dysfunction independently of age or disease severity. Increasing evidence suggests that putamen participates in autonomic control [49, 50]. A recent study using DAT‐SPECT in untreated early‐stage patients with Parkinson's disease has shown a correlation between OH and striatal dopamine depletion, thus suggesting that dopaminergic deficiency could impact autonomic nervous system function [51].

The strengths of our study were the large sample size of MSA patients and the accurate laboratory assessment of the autonomic system temporally coupled with the MRI evaluations. This study has also limitations. First, the lack of a healthy control group. Second, the lack of comparison between MSA‐P and C patients, as these motor subtypes have distinct degeneration patterns. These results might not have been reproduced in MSA‐C patients due to a lack of statistical power, though statistical comparisons continued to be significant even when restricted only to MSA‐P patients. We acknowledge that these results must be taken with caution, and prospective studies comparing MSA‐P and C patients are needed. A longitudinal study design would allow for a more thorough evaluation of the effect of disease progression on structural alterations and severity of autonomic failure. Finally, data were collected retrospectively, and brain MRI acquisition protocols were heterogeneous, but we did carry out a three‐step preliminary analysis to minimize differences related to the scanners and increase internal validity. Morphometric values are also limited in the brainstem context, and other MRI modalities could be used to analyze microstructural integrity (e.g., diffusion imaging) [52].

Overall, the main contribution of our approach was a comprehensive study of the sub‐tentorial structural alterations associated with cardiovascular autonomic failure in MSA. Medulla volume was strongly correlated to sympathetic baroreflex response, suggesting that neuronal loss in brainstem nuclei could contribute to OH. Cardiovascular dysautonomia was associated with cerebellar atrophy even in MSA‐P patients, suggesting that severe autonomic failure is a marker of a particularly widespread neuronal degeneration.
