# Gene augmentation therapy successfully treats mice with complete congenital stationary night blindness (cCSNB), improving retinal function and visual acuity.
**Authors:** Hasan N, Attaway CA, Di Paolo M, McCall MA, Gregg RG
**Journal:** Gene Ther (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41434-026-00633-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41434-026-00633-1)

## Content

Recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) mediated gene therapy is an effective approach for targeting therapeutic genes to retinal photoreceptors. Complete congenital stationary night blindness (cCSNB) is a genetically heterogeneous inherited retinal disease caused by mutations in one of several genes that are part of a large, interdependent depolarizing bipolar cell (DBC) signalplex required for normal synaptic signaling with photoreceptors. These genes include NYX, GRM6, TRPM1, GPR179, and LRIT3, and the resulting cCSNB phenotype is characterized by abnormally low-light vision, myopia, and nystagmus, but does not include retinal degeneration. Because of the non-progressive and recessive nature of cCSNB, we investigated the potential of a gene augmentation approach in the mature retina to improve retinal function and cortical visual acuity. We used a mouse model of cCSNB caused by LRIT3 loss to evaluate the efficacy of a single subretinal injection of an rAAV expressing LRIT3 in either rods or cones, and the extent of restoration of retinal function and visual acuity. We show that gene augmentation by expressing LRIT3 in Lrit3-/- retinas of mature mice restores the DBC synaptic signaling complex and is sufficient to rescue retinal function and improve visual acuity.

Complete congenital stationary night blindness (cCSNB) is a genetically heterogeneous group of retinal disorders that cause poor night vision and are associated with high myopia and nystagmus [1]. To date, mutations in NYX, GRM6, TRPM1, GPR179, and LRIT3 have been identified in mice and humans [2–17]. Mutations in each eliminate normal signaling from photoreceptors to depolarizing bipolar cells (DBCs) by altering the expression of the postsynaptic signalplex that detects changes in glutamate release.

Many current therapeutic approaches are under investigation to restore vision loss, by using gene therapy to replace a missing protein or to modify the causative gene mutation (see review [18]). The only clinically approved gene therapy is an rAAV-mediated augmentation therapy, Luxturna (voretigene neparvovec-rzyl), to treat a form of recessive Leber’s congenital amaurosis (LCA2) [19]. Luxturna restores RPE65 expression in the RPE, restores the visual cycle, and prevents retinal degeneration.

cCSNB is an attractive target for gene augmentation because, in many cases, it is a recessive condition that results from absent protein expression, and the retina does not degenerate, providing intact tissue for potential therapeutic manipulation. For non-null mutations, additional testing or alternative strategies would be required to ensure that mutant proteins do not interfere with the treatment. Gene augmentation utilizing rAAVs has been used to treat several cCSNB mouse models to restore function in Nyx, Grm6, and Lrit3 knockout (Lrit3–/–) mice [17, 20–25], and in a dog Lrit3–/– model [26]. Here, we focus on LRIT3 gene augmentation in mature mice to examine whether gene therapy can restore cortical visual acuity, as assessed using a visual water task. We show that subretinal delivery of LRIT3 by rAAV8 into rods or cones of Lrit3–/– mice improves both retinal function and cortical visual acuity.

Animals were housed in the University of Louisville AAALAC-approved facility under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, and both males and females were included in all experiments and approved by the university committee for animal welfare. The generation of the Lrit3–/– mouse line has been described previously [17]. Because there are no antibodies to Nyctalopin, staining for Nyctalopin used antibodies to GFP after crossing a transgenic line into the Lrit3–/– background that expresses an EYFP-Nyctalopin fusion gene [27]. We refer to C57Bl6/J as Wildtype, and C57Bl6/J treated with rAAV8 GRK1::GFP as Controls. For subretinal injection and ERG recording, mice were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine solution (118/11 mg/kg, respectively) diluted in normal mouse Ringer’s solution. Mice were euthanized using CO2 followed by cervical dislocation, according to American Veterinary Medical Association guidelines.

Antibodies used to label LRIT3, TRPM1, and mGluR6 have been described previously [17, 24]. Others were anti-GFP (1:1000, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA Cat# A10262), Alexa Fluor 488 Donkey anti-goat (1:1000, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA Cat# A32814), Alexa Fluor 488 Donkey anti-rabbit (1:1000, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA Cat# A21206), Alexa Fluor 488 Donkey anti-chicken (1:1000, Jackson ImmunoResearch Inc., West Grove PA Cat# 703545155), and Donkey anti-guinea pig Cy3 conjugated (1:1000, Millipore, Burlington, MA Cat# AP193C).

To control expression of LRIT3 using rAAVs, we used a human rhodopsin (RHO) promoter (for rods) or a mouse GNAT2 promoter (for cones) [17, 21, 24]. For control injections, we used the GRK1 promoter, which is expressed in both rods and cones [28], and its expression is shown in Supplementary Fig. 5. The expression constructs were packaged in the rAAV8 capsid by Vigene Biosciences (now Charles River Laboratories, Wilmington, MA). rAAVs (1.0 µl of 1 ×1013 vg/ml) were introduced into mature retinas (postnatal day 35-40 (P35-P40)) using subretinal injections with a custom-built injector based on a Hamilton syringe with 33 gauge needle (Hamilton, PN:6609071-01). A subretinal bleb can be seen forming during successful injections. Eyes in which a bleb was not seen, or there was obvious retinal damage, were excluded from the study. Retinal function and structure were assessed four to eight weeks after treatment.

Retinal functions were assessed by using full-field electroretinogram (ffERG) recording methods, as described previously [13, 29]. Mice were dark-adapted overnight, anesthetized using ketamine/xylazine solution (118/11 mg/kg, respectively), and all procedures to prepare the mice for ERGs were performed under dim red light. Pupils were dilated by applying 0.5% phenylephrine hydrochloride or 0.1% Tropicamide solutions to the corneal surface. Body temperature was maintained by using a feedback-controlled electric heating pad (TC1000 Temperature control, CWE Inc.). Artificial tears (Tears Again, OCuSOFT, Gaithersburg, MD) were applied to the cornea, and a contact lens with a gold electrode (LKC Technologies, Gaithersburg, MD) was placed. Reference and ground needle electrodes were placed on the forehead’s midline and tail, respectively. Scotopic responses were recorded using an increasing intensity series of dim flashes (from -3.6 to 1.4 log cd s/m2) presented on a dark background. Photopic responses were measured by presenting a series of increasing-intensity flashes (from -0.8 to 1.4 log cd s/m²) after 5 min of light adaptation (20 cd/m²).

Retina wholemounts or transverse cryosections were prepared as described previously [21, 30]. Briefly, mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation, eyes were enucleated, and the cornea and lens were removed. Retinas were dissected in phosphate buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and fixed for 15-60 min in 4% paraformaldehyde diluted in 0.1 M PB, washed 3X with PBS 10 min each, and cryoprotected in a graded series of sucrose solutions (5, 10, 15 and 20% in PB) and finally in OCT:20% sucrose (2:1). Cryoprotected retinas were frozen and transverse retinal sections (18μm) were cut on a cryostat (Leica Biosystems, Buffalo Grove, IL), mounted on Superfrost Plus slides (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), and stored at -80oC. Immunohistochemistry methods have been described previously [21]. Retina sections were imaged on an Olympus FV-4000 Confocal Microscope, and contrast and brightness were adjusted using Fluoview Software (Olympus, Waltham, MA).

Visual acuity was measured using a two-alternative forced-choice method [31], modified also to measure scotopic visual acuity [32] and depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1. There are three phases: molding, training, and testing. Molding acclimatizes the mice to the water, and they learn they can escape to a hidden platform. Training involves mice learning that the grating on the monitor is associated with the hidden platform and successfully completing the task in 8/10 trials. The testing phase consists of changing the grating frequency and determining at what spatial frequency testing is successful (8/10 successes). Below this threshold, the mouse can no longer discern the grating from the equiluminant side, indicating a failure (going to the correct side) in 5/10 trials. We train mice under photopic conditions (screens 100 cd/m2), then dark adapt them for 1 h at 0.01 cd/m2 and repeat the task with both screens at 0.025 cd/m2 to obtain scotopic visual acuity.

The percentage of rod LRIT3-expressing synapses in rAAV RHO::Lrit3-treated Lrit3–/– retinas was determined from wholemount retinas after staining for mGluR6 and LRIT3. Confocal images using a 40x (NA = 1.4) objective on an Olympus FV4000 were collected from 5 areas (central and 4 quadrants) that included the bleb, central retina, and 3 additional quadrants as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. In each retina, counts from the 5 regions were averaged. Counts were made from maximum projections of z-stacks of wholemount images using ImageJ. The total number of rod synapses (mGlur6 positive) and those positive for LRIT3 were counted.

Prism 10.5.0 (774) (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA) was used for statistical analyses (see text and figure legends). Šídák’s multiple comparison test was used to adjust for multiple testing when appropriate, and the padj is reported. Statistical significance, padj ≤ 0.05. Variance was similar in groups where comparisons were made. Sample size for animals was based on pilot data and was designed to achieve 80% power, assuming a difference of 0.5 SD. Blinding of the data was not possible because a single investigator conducted all experiments.

Four to six weeks after subretinal injection of rAAV RHO::Lrit3 in mature Lrit3–/– mice, we measured scotopic and photopic full-field electroretinograms (ffERGs) to assess rod- and cone-driven function, respectively. Compared to Lrit3–/– mice, Lrit3–/– mice treated with rAAV RHO::Lrit3 had increased rod-isolated ffERG b-wave amplitudes at all tested flash intensities (padj<0.001), although they remained significantly below response amplitudes in controls (Wildtype treated with rAAV GRK1::GFP; padj < 0.001 for all flash intensities; Fig. 1B, C). This rescued scotopic b-wave was maintained through 28 weeks post-injection (WPI, Fig. 1D). In contrast, rAAV RHO::Lrit3 treatment of Lrit3–/– retinas did not restore a photopic b-wave, which remained like that in Lrit3–/– mice (padj>0.05 for all flash intensities; see Supplementary Fig. 3 for photopic responses). This reinforces the specificity of the RHO promoter, which restricts expression to rods, as reported previously [21]. Lrit3–/– mice treated with rAAV GNAT2::Lrit3 had increased photopic ffERG b-wave amplitudes at all tested flash intensities (padj<0.001 for all other flash intensities), although they remained significantly below response amplitudes in controls (padj<0.001 for all flash intensities; Fig. 1E, F), data consistent with our previous report [25]. We also examined the durability of the treatment of cones for 40 WPI, and the photopic ERG b-wave was maintained throughout (Fig. 1G).

To determine if there is a relationship between the scotopic ERG b-wave amplitude and the number of rods whose expression of LRIT3, Nyctalopin, and TRPM1 was rescued, we counted rods with rescued LRIT3 expression in a subset of Lrit3–/– treated wholemount retinas, after staining for mGluR6 and LRIT3 (Fig. 3). All the rod to rod BCs synapses in wildtype retinas double label with mGluR6 and LRIT3 (Fig. 3Ai, Bi), whereas in Lrit3–/– rod-rod BC synapses only mGluR6 is present (Fig. 3Aii, Bii). In Lrit3–/– retinas treated with rAAV RHO::Lrit3, infected rods show punta double labeled with mGluR6 and LRIT3, whereas uninfected rod puncta show only staining by mGluR6 (Fig. 3Aiii, Biii). Quantification of these data shows that the infection percentage across rAAV RHO::Lrit3 treated retinas ranged from 22 to 47% (Fig. 3C). To determine if there was a correlation between structure and function, we plotted the percentage of rods expressing LRIT3 against the dark-adapted, rod isolated ffERG (flash intensity –3.6 log cd s/m2, Fig. 3C) for treated and untreated Lrit3–/– and wildtype retinas (Fig. 3C). What is notable is that treatment rescued the ffERG b-wave in all treated Lrit3–/– retinas, and the amplitudes fall above the line connecting the Lrit3–/– and wildtype retinas (Fig. 3C), suggesting that the relationship between the ffERG b-wave amplitude and the number of infected rods expressing LRIT3 is not linear.

Similar to the above results in restoring function in rods of mature mice, we previously showed that photopic ERG function and cone signalplex protein expression are rescued when LRIT3 is expressed in cones of mature Lrit3–/– mice [25]. A key question in any therapy that treats the mature retina is whether it improves overall vision. To address this question, we used a two-alternative forced-choice visual discrimination task to measure visual acuity mediated by rescued Lrit3–/– cones [31], and we adapted this test to evaluate visual acuity mediated by rescued rods.

In summary, these data show that rAAV-mediated expression of LRIT3 in either rods or cones restores the postsynaptic signaling complex on DBCs, and this translates to improved ffERG b-waves and dark-adapted visual acuity. When cones are treated, both photopic and scotopic visual acuity are restored to near wildtype acuity in many animals.

Gene therapy for IRDs (inherited retinal diseases) is a proven approach, with FDA approval in 2017 of Luxturna, an rAAV approach to treat mature and juvenile patients with a form of Leber’s congenital amaurosis (LCA2), caused by biallelic null mutations in RPE65, and better outcomes are reported when younger patients are treated [35]. This indicates that the developmental stage of the retina may predict the outcome; however, this interpretation is clouded by differences in the state of photoreceptor degeneration. In contrast, cCSNB is a stationary disease characterized by poor night vision from birth. The recessive and non-progressive nature of cCSNB allows us to interpret our rescue results as a function of retinal maturity alone.

Normal vision requires photoreceptors to communicate with inner retinal neurons by altering glutamate release from their axon terminals. The changes in glutamate release are detected by the DBCs as well as the hyperpolarizing BCs (HBCs) that form the ON and OFF retinal pathways, respectively. However, there is crosstalk between the ON and OFF pathways, and this is reflected by defects in ON pathway signaling that result in defects in the OFF pathway in mouse models of cCSNB [17].

rAAV-mediated gene augmentation in mouse models of cCSNB caused by mutations in Grm6, Nyx, and Lrit3 has produced varying degrees of rescue [17, 20–23, 25, 36]. Some of the variability in the results can be attributed to the size of the gene (e.g., Grm6 is near the packaging limit of rAAV), the cell type being targeted (DBCs are more difficult to infect than photoreceptors), and the route of delivery, subretinal versus intravitreal. Treatment of Grm6 and Nyx mutants in younger animals, P15 and P5, respectively [20, 22], showed only small improvements in the scotopic ERG b-wave amplitude. Intravitreal delivery of LRIT3 by rAAVs to DBCs, photoreceptors, or both, at P30, showed a small improvement in the scotopic ffERG b-wave, no improvement in the photopic ffERG b-wave, and improved optomotor responses, suggesting improved vision [23]. We found that intravitreal delivery of LRIT3 using AAVmax to express LRIT3 in rods of Lrit3–/– retinas at P5 was more effective than delivery at P30 for restoring the b-wave of the scotopic ERG [21]. Finally, in a dog model of LRIT3-mediated cCSNB, investigators delivered an rAAV optimized to infect dog DBCs between 1.3 and 2.8 years of age, resulting in a small improvement in the scotopic ffERG b-wave and an improvement in rod-mediated visual behavior that was durable [26]. Taken together, these studies suggest that gene augmentation for LRIT3-mediated cCSNB can improve some functional measures, and we now extend those studies by showing that subretinal injection of adult Lrit3–/– mice with rAAV8 vectors improves visual acuity.

While the primary phenotype of cCSNB is poor scotopic vision, many patients and cCSNB mouse models have nystagmus [37]. In the mouse models, this has been shown to result from abnormal retinal activity, which manifests as retinal oscillations, first described in the Nyxnob mouse [38, 39] and now demonstrated in LRIT3 and mGluR6-deficient lines [40]. We also found that this oscillation was decreased in BCs from Lrit3–/– mice after expressing LRIT3 in some rods [21]. Therefore, it is possible that the visual acuity improvements we document here could be a combination of restoring normal signaling between photoreceptors and DBCs and a decrease in the abnormal retinal activity that may be driving nystagmus. The improvement in photopic visual acuity when LRIT3 is expressed in the cones of Lrit3–/– mice is not surprising, but the improvement under scotopic conditions was unexpected. Furthermore, these data suggest that the treatment of cones provides some animals with near wildtype scotopic visual acuity, despite a lack of signaling through the rod pathway. This variability is not due to differences in the number of rescued cones, as those expressing LRIT3 are similar across all treated retinas. Another possibility is that the sensitivity of the rescued cones extends to the scotopic levels used in the experiments. Another possibility is that there are ectopic connections between rod BCs and cones, which could improve scotopic cone signal transmission. Resolving the underlying mechanism will require additional studies. These results suggest that therapies for cCSNB should focus on both the functional restoration of cones and rods.

The improvement in both scotopic and photopic visual acuity demonstrated in this mouse model of cCSNB sets the stage for additional studies on other cCSNB models and a related disorder, incomplete CSNB (iCSNB), which is also caused by disruption in rod and cone synaptic function [41]. As gene therapy for IRDs progresses, both complete and incomplete CSNB patients may be treated, improving scotopic and photopic visual acuity and decreasing nystagmus.
