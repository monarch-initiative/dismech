# Undetectable NK Cells due to the FCGR3A Variant, L66H, Which May Not Be Directly Disease-Causing.
**Authors:** Izadi N, Sun M, Mace EM, O'Gorman MRG, Church JA
**Journal:** J Clin Immunol (2021)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10875-021-01122-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-021-01122-3)

## Content

A baby girl born at 38 weeks of gestation with no perinatal complications was brought to our attention for possible severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) because of low T cell receptor excision circles (TRECs) (11/mcL, reference range > 18) on newborn screen. Blood was collected for confirmatory flow cytometry and the absolute number of T cells was borderline low (2116/mcL, reference range 2500–5500) but sufficient to rule out SCID. However, her NK cell enumeration showed absent (< 1%) NK cells (CD3− CD56+ CD16+ by BD Biosciences 6-color TBNK, San Jose, CA). Subsequent testing at a local laboratory identified normal numbers of NK cells (26%), but at this laboratory, NK cells were defined as (CD3− CD56+ by locally derived reagents). Complete laboratory values can be found in Supplementary Table E1. We therefore pursued further testing to determine if CD16 was absent from the patient’s cells or if there was a lack of targeting by the specific anti-CD16 monoclonal antibody used.

NK cells are innate lymphoid cells characterized by expression of CD56 and/or CD16 in the absence of CD3. Almost all NK effector cells express CD16, a key NK cell activating receptor (FcγRIIIA) encoded by FCGR3A. Therefore, NK cells are commonly identified as CD3− CD56+ CD16+. CD16 is generally identified by flow cytometry using anti-CD16 antibodies derived from either the 3G8 or the B73.1 clone which bind the proximal or distal immunoglobulin domains of CD16, respectively [1]. For our patient, flow cytometry showed normal CD16 expression with the 3G8 antibody and absent expression with the B73.1 antibody (Fig. 1). However, next-generation sequencing (NGS) did not identify any rare variants in FCGR3A (< 1% population frequency). As this seemed incongruent with the flow cytometry results, we performed Sanger sequencing of the FCGR3A gene which identified a homozygous missense variant c.197 T > A (p.L66H) in FCGR3A. This missense variant occurs in the Ig-like C2-type 1 domain of CD16 and was reported in multiple instances in association with isolated functional NK cell deficiency [1–3]. Additional NK studies of our proband showed appropriate distribution of CD56bright and CD56dim NK cells, expression of NK cell maturation markers including CD94, CD62L, and CD57 (Supplemental Figure E1), and normal NK cell lytic function by killing assay compared to controls (Supplementary Table E2, Cincinnati Children’s Diagnostic Immunology Lab). Furthermore, lymphocyte antigen and mitogen proliferation panel (ARUP Labs, Salt Lake City UT) demonstrated absent response to candida, low-normal response to poke-weed mitogen and normal responses to tetanus, phytohemagglutinin, and concanavalin A.

Our patient was found to have a previously reported variant resulting from a leucine to histidine change at amino acid position 66 (L66H) in FCGR3A protein. The L66H variant results in CD16 that fails to express the epitope identified by the B73.1 antibody clone (Fig. 1). Therefore, using the B73.1 antibody clone to identify CD16, particularly if CD56 is not examined, has the potential for demonstrating falsely absent NK cells. A second pitfall in the evaluation of this patient emerged when next-generation sequencing (NGS) analysis likely missed the L66H variant due to the analysis strategy designed to filter out relatively common genomic variants (> 1% of population frequency). NGS is usually set to report “rare” variants occurring in < 1% of the general population to prevent over-reporting of clinically irrelevant variants for rare genetic conditions [4]. Therefore, NGS may not report the L66H variant because it is observed in 7.1% of alleles of European ancestry including 68 homozygotes, and a population frequency of about 5.0% including 100 homozygotes in overall populations (gnomAD Database v3.1.1; https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org [5]). However, the study of a cohort of 346 children found that only 12 had loss of the B73.1 epitope, a lower frequency than reported by gnomAD [6]. Similarly, both the Human Genome Database and 1000 Genomes Project report lower allele frequencies than gnomAD (3.5% and 2.5%, respectively), and all databases report much lower allele frequencies in East Asian, African, and South Asian populations than European, Middle Eastern, and Latino populations. The higher frequency in some populations suggests that the L66H variant alone is not sufficient to cause disease directly but instead may act as a risk factor with low disease penetrance. However, studies have suggested that the L66H substitution can result in defective NK cell function [2]. The L66H substitution was first identified in a young boy with recurrent viral respiratory infections, abscess after Bacille Calmette-Guerin vaccination, and a difficult clinical course following both EBV and Varicella infection [1]. Patients with the L66H substitution have also demonstrated decreased NK cell spontaneous cytotoxicity but without impairment of antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity [1, 3]. It should also be noted that absent B73.1 epitope detection can occur independently of the L66H transversion (which is annotated in some reports as L48H [6]). Therefore, loss of B73.1 epitope detection should always be combined with Sanger sequencing to confirm the presence of the L66H allele if it is suspected.

Finally, our patient’s low TRECs and mild T-lymphopenia were found to be due to DiGeorge syndrome confirmed by a 2.8-megabase deletion of 22q11.2. Our patient was also found to have a copy number gain of chromosome 3q28 of unknown clinical significance. A focused exome panel for primary immunodeficiencies did not demonstrate any additional clinically relevant variants. Currently, at 1 year of age, the patient has been doing well with no clinical manifestations consistent with previously reported damaging effects of the L66H variant. She has only had one brief upper respiratory tract infection after her sibling had a similar illness. Serum calcium was normal, and echocardiogram demonstrated small atrial and ventricular septal defects that did not require intervention. We will continue to monitor this patient closely and counseled the family and the primary care provider on the increased risk of infections for patients with DiGeorge syndrome and need for periodic monitoring of NK cell function due to the L66H variant. However, in light of recent population genetics data, this case report highlights that the L66H variant is unlikely a direct genetic cause of NK cell dysfunction.

This case highlights two very important considerations in NK cell evaluation. Firstly, it may be prudent to prioritize the 3G8 clone when evaluating NK cells given the potential loss of the B73.1 epitope in L66H and other reported polymorphisms such as L48R and L48H [6]. Secondly, due to the important limitation of filtering out reportedly common variants during NGS analysis, the minor allele frequency criteria may need to be relaxed to identify the L66H variant. Another option is to use Sanger sequencing to confirm the L66H variant.
