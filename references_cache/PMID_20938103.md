Several missense mutations in the protein kinase Cγ (γPKC) gene have been found 
to cause spinocerebellar ataxia type 14 (SCA14), an autosomal dominant 
neurodegenerative disease. We previously demonstrated that the mutant γPKC found 
in SCA14 is susceptible to aggregation that induces apoptotic cell death. Congo 
red is widely used as a histological dye for amyloid detection. Recent evidence 
has revealed that Congo red has the property to inhibit amyloid oligomers and 
fibril formation of misfolded proteins. In the present study, we examine whether 
Congo red inhibits aggregate formation and cytotoxicity of mutant γPKC. Congo 
red likely inhibits aggregate formation of mutant γPKC – green fluorescent 
protein (GFP) without affecting its expression level in SH-SY5Y cells. Congo red 
counteracts the insolubilization of recombinant mutant γPKC, suggesting that the 
dye inhibits aggregation of mutant γPKC by a direct mechanism. Congo red also 
inhibits aggregation and oligomerization of mutant γPKC-GFP in primary cultured 
cerebellar Purkinje cells. Moreover, the dye reverses the improper development 
of dendrites and inhibits apoptotic cell death in Purkinje cells that express 
mutant γPKC-GFP. These results indicate that amyloid-inhibiting compounds like 
Congo red may be novel therapeutics for SCA14.

Introduction
Spinocerebellar ataxia type 14 (SCA14) is an auto-
somal dominant spinocerebellar ataxia that is clinically 
characterized by symptoms of cerebellar dysfunction and 
is caused by missense or deletion mutations in the 
PRKCG gene encoding protein kinase C γ ( γPKC) 
(1 – 15). γPKC is a member of the PKC family that is 
specifically present in the central nervous system and is 
especially abundant in cerebellar Purkinje cells (PCs) 
(16). γPKC-knockout mice show mildly impaired motor 
coordination and incomplete elimination of synapses 
between PCs and climbing fibers during development 
(17, 18). However, the ataxic symptoms in γPKC-
knockout mice are milder than those found in SCA14 
patients. Moreover, SCA14 is inherited in an autosomal 
dominant fashion, raising the possibility that a toxic gain 
of function for mutant γPKC, rather than a loss of func-
tion, underlies the pathogenesis of SCA14.
We have previously demonstrated that mutant versions 
of γPKC form aggregates in cultured cells (19) and mouse 
primary cultured PCs (20), leading to apoptotic cell death 
(21). Furthermore, mutant γPKC forms soluble oligomers 
that have amyloid structure and induce improper devel-
opment of PC dendrites (20). Amyloid oligomers and 
fibrils caused by mutant or misfolded proteins are also

Materials and Methods
Materials
Congo red, Hoechst 33342, Ham’s F-12 medium, and 
Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) were 
 obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). 
Sumitomo Nerve-Cell Culture System (Neuron culture 
medium and dissociation solutions) was from Sumitomo 
Bakelite (Tokyo). The anti-green fluorescent protein 
(GFP) mouse monoclonal antibody and penicillin/strep-
tomycin solution were from Nacalai Tesque (Kyoto). 
The anti-calbindin D28k mouse monoclonal antibody 
was from Swant (Bellinzona, Switzerland). The anti-
γPKC rabbit polyclonal antibody was from Santa Cruz 
Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA, USA). Horseradish 
peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse and anti-
rabbit antibodies were from Jackson ImmunoResearch 
Laboratories (West Grove, PA, USA). AlexaFluor 546 
(Alexa546)-conjugated goat anti-mouse antibody, normal 
goat serum (NGS), and Hanks’ balanced salt solution 
were from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA). The glass-
bottomed culture dishes (35-mm diameter) were from 
MatTek (Ashland, MA, USA).
Cell culture
SH-SY5Y cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 (1:1) 
medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum 
(FBS), 100 units/ml of penicillin, and 100 μg/ml of 
streptomycin in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% 
CO
2 at 37°C.
Mouse cerebellar primary culture was prepared as 
described previously (20). Briefly, E14 embryos from 
pregnant ICR mice were dissociated with dissociation 
solutions from the Sumitomo Nerve-Cell Culture System, 
according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Dissociated 
cerebellar cells were suspended in the neuron culture 
medium of the Sumitomo Nerve-Cell Culture System 
and plated at 2 × 10
5 cells/100 μl in the center of a 35-
mm diameter glass-bottomed culture dish. Cells were 
cultured for 28 days in vitro (DIV) in a humidified atmo-
sphere containing 5% CO
2 at 37°C. Half of the medium 
was changed every 3 – 4 days.
Construction of adenoviral vectors to express γPKC-
GFP using a tetracycline (Tet)-regulated system
We constructed two types of adenoviral vectors to 
express γPKC-GFP, which was regulated by Tet (Supple-
mentary Fig. 1: available in the online version only) (30). 
The first vector expresses the Tet transactivator (tTA) 
under the control of the cytomegalovirus (CMV) pro-
moter for SH-SY5Y cells (Ad-CMV-tTA) or the L7 
promoter for primary cultured PCs (Ad-L7-tTA). The 
second vector, Ad-TetOp- γPKC-GFP, expresses wild-
type (WT) or mutant γPKC-GFP under the control of the 
TetOp minimal promoter, which is activated by tTA.
Ad-L7-tTA and Ad-TetOp- γPKC-GFP were con-
structed as previously described (20). Ad-CMV-tTA was 
constructed by using the AdEasy adenoviral vector sys-
tem (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA, USA) according to the 
manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, tTA cDNA excised 
from a pTet-tTAk plasmid (Invitrogen) was subcloned 
into pShuttle-CMV (Stratagene), downstream of the 
CMV promoter. Homologous recombination was per-
formed between the shuttle vector and pAdEasy-1, an 
adenoviral backbone cosmid vector, in the E. coli strain 
BJ5183. The recombinant adenoviral genome was re-
moved from the cosmid vector using Pac I and transfected 
into HEK293 cells. Adenoviral vectors that had prolifer-
ated in HEK293 cells were extracted and concentrated by 
cesium chloride ultracentrifugation.

After 24 h of cultivation, cells were infected with two

GFP, at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 and cul-
tured for another two days. Various concentrations of 
Congo red (1 – 100 μM) were added at the time of adeno-

On DIV21, primary cultured PCs were infected with 
Ad-L7-tTA (MOI of 20) and Ad-TetOp- γPKC-GFP

The GFP fluorescence in living cells on glass-bottom

From a total of 100 – 200 PCs expressing γPKC-GFP,

Immunoblotting
SH-SY5Y cells on 6-cm dishes were analyzed by im-
munoblotting using an anti-GFP antibody as described 
previously (31). Briefly, following 2-day cultivation after 
adenoviral infection and Congo red treatment, cells were 
lysed in RIPA buffer [1% nonidet P40, 0.1% sodium 
deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 
20 μg/ml of leupeptin, 1 mM phenylmethanesulfonyl 
fluoride (PMSF), 1 mM sodium orthovanadate, 1 mM 
NaF, 100 nM Calyculin A, and 10 mM Tris/HCl, pH 7.4] 
by sonication (UR-20P; Tomy Seiko, Tokyo; output 4, 
duty 50%). Protein concentration in the cell lysate was 
quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Pierce 
 Biotechnology, Rockford, IL, USA). Protein samples 
were subjected to SDS-PAGE, and separated proteins 
were electrophoretically transferred onto polyvinylidine 
difluoride (PVDF) filters (Millipore, Bedford, MA, 
USA). Nonspecific binding sites were blocked by incu-
bating in 5% skim milk in PBS-T (0.01 M phosphate-
buffered saline containing 0.03% Triton X-100) for >1 h 
at room temperature (RT). After the filters were washed 
with PBS-T, they were incubated with PBS-T containing 
anti-GFP mouse monoclonal antibody (diluted 1:2000) 
and 1% NGS for >1 h at RT. After further washing, the 
filters were incubated in PBS-T containing HRP-conju-
gated anti-mouse IgG antibody (diluted 1:10000) for >30 
min at RT. After three more washes, immunoreactive 
bands were visualized with a chemiluminescence detec-
tion kit (ECLTM Western Blotting Detection Reagents; 
GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences, Uppsala, Sweden) and de-
tected with an LAS-1000plus luminescent image analyzer 
(Fuji Photo Film, Tokyo).
Purification of recombinant mutant γPKC protein and 
analysis of its solubility
Recombinant baculoviruses encoding glutathione S-
transferase (GST)-tagged S119P mutant γPKC (GST-
S119P) were prepared according to the manufacturer’s 
instructions (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Approxi-
mately 3 – 4 days after infection, Sf9 cells were collected 
and washed once with ice-cold PBS and resuspended in 
ice-cold TBS-T (150 mM NaCl, 0.5% Triton X-100, and 
50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4) containing 20 μg/ml leupeptin 
and 1 mM PMSF. After homogenization using a sonica-
tor, cell lysates were cleared by centrifugation for 15 min 
at 15,000 × g at 4°C. Supernatants were incubated for 4 
h at 4°C with glutathione-Sepharose 4B resin (GE 
Healthcare Bio-Sciences). After being washed six times 
with ice-cold TBS-T, bound proteins were eluted with 50 
mM Tris-HCl and 20 mM reduced glutathione at pH 8.0. 
Recombinant proteins were dialyzed three times against 
50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 
and 1 mM DTT and stored at −80°C until use. The 
 concentration of purified GST-S119P was estimated to 
be 3 ng/ μl by SDS-PAGE and Coomassie brilliant blue 
staining.
Recombinant GST-S119P (10 μl) was mixed with an 
equal volume of PBS containing vehicle [0.8% dimethyl 
sulfoxide (DMSO)] or Congo red (20 or 200 μM), fol-
lowed by incubation at 37°C for 1 h. The incubated 
samples were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C 
and supernatants were harvested as soluble (S) fractions. 
Pellets were resuspended in RIPA buffer, sonicated, and 
then used as the insoluble (I) fractions. Half of each frac-
tion was subjected to 7.5% SDS-PAGE; the amount of 
mutant GST-S119P in the S and I fractions was quanti-
fied by immunoblotting with anti-γPKC antibody (diluted 
in 1:2000) as described above.
Fluorescent recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) for 
γPKC-GFP
Seven days after adenoviral infection and Congo red 
treatment of primary cultured PCs (DIV28), fluorescent 
images of γPKC-GFP in somata were obtained using a 
confocal scanning fluorescence microscope. To photo-
bleach γPKC-GFP, repetitive irradiation with the maxi-
mal excitation laser was applied to a small circular region 
of the somata and dendrites of PCs. Sequential fluores-
cent images were recorded every 0.1 s for 1 min before 
and after photobleaching. Changes in fluorescence in the 
bleached area were normalized to the total fluorescence 
of the cell and analyzed using GraphPad Prism  (GraphPad 
Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). In these experi-
ments, we observed PCs without any aggregates of 
γPKC-GFP. All experiments were performed at room 
temperature.

Immunostaining and nuclear staining for detection of 
apoptotic cells
The extent of apoptotic cell death triggered by mutant 
γPKC-GFP was evaluated using nuclear fluorescent 
Hoechst 33342 staining as described previously (32). 
Briefly, 7 days after adenoviral infection and Congo red 
treatment of primary cultured PCs (DIV28), cells were 
fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS, stained with 
0.5 μg/ml Hoechst 33342, and subsequently stained with 
anti-calbindin D28k mouse monoclonal antibody (diluted 
1:1000) and Alexa546 anti-mouse IgG antibody (diluted 
1:500). The fluorescence of Hoechst 33342 and Alexa546 
was monitored with a confocal laser scanning fluores-
cence microscope using a 364-nm ultraviolet laser exci-
tation and a 546-nm HeNe laser, respectively, and a 
385 – 470-nm band pass barrier filter or 560-nm long 
pass barrier filter, respectively. Cells with condensed or 
fragmented nuclei were considered apoptotic.

Congo red prevents insolubilization of recombinant 
GST-tagged mutant γPKC
We assumed that Congo red would inhibit aggregation 
with an amyloid structure by directly binding to mutant 
γPKC and stabilizing its conformation. Therefore, we 
examined whether Congo red affects the solubility of 
recombinant mutant γPKC in vitro. Recombinant GST-
fused mutant S119P γPKC (GST-S119P) was constructed 
using the baculovirus system. Although most recombi-
nant GST-S119P was detected in the soluble fraction 
before incubation, a 1-h incubation at 37°C with vehicle 
(0.4% DMSO) led to accumulation of GST-S119P in the 
insoluble fraction, likely due to aggregation (Fig. 2A). 
However, incubation with Congo red (10 or 100 μM) 
significantly improved the solubility of recombinant 
GST-S119P (Fig. 2: A and B). These results indicate that 
Congo red directly stabilizes mutant γPKC and inhibits 
its aggregation with an amyloid structure.
Congo red inhibits aggregation and oligomerization of 
mutant γPKC-GFP in primary cultured PCs
We recently demonstrated that mutant γPKC-GFP 
frequently forms aggregates and oligomers, thereby in-
ducing incorrect development of dendrites and apoptosis 
in primary cultured PCs (20). We first examined whether 
Congo red inhibits aggregation of S119P-GFP in primary 
cultured PCs. PC-specific expression of WT γPKC-GFP 
(WT-GFP) and S119P-GFP was achieved by adenoviral 
infection of Ad-L7-tTA and Ad-TetOP- γPKC-GFP, 
which induced the expression of γPKC-GFP in a PC-
specific manner (Supplementary Fig. 1: available in the 
online version only). We added Congo red at the time of 
adenoviral infection on DIV21 and observed GFP fluo-
rescence in living PCs after 7-day cultivation (DIV28). 
While 100 μM Congo red itself induced an aberrant 
morphology in PC dendrites expressing WT-GFP, 10 μM 
Congo red did not affect the morphology of PC dendrites 
(Supplementary Fig. 2: available in the online version 
only). Therefore, we examined the effect of 10 μM Congo 
red on properties of mutant γPKC-GFP after 7-day culti-
vation. Aggregation of S119P-GFP was significantly 
reduced in the Congo red–treated group compared with 
the vehicle-treated group, while aggregation of WT-GFP 
was not affected (Fig. 3: A and B). This indicates that 
Congo red inhibits aggregation of mutant γPKC-GFP in 
primary cultured PCs.

Congo red on the mobility of mutant γPKC-GFP. Mobili-
ties of γPKC-GFP were assessed by FRAP analysis in 
γPKC-GFP–expressing PC somata without aggregates.

apoptosis induced by mutant γPKC-GFP. Apoptosis of 
PCs was assessed by chromatin condensation in calbin-

since Congo red reduced the percentage of cells contain-
ing aggregates for PCs expressing S119P-GFP (Fig. 3: A 
and B). These results suggest that Congo red inhibits 
mutant γPKC-mediated apoptosis in primary cultured 
PCs by preventing aggregation.
Discussion
Since missense mutations in γPKC were first identified 
as the genetic cause of SCA14 (1), several researchers 
have focused on the molecular mechanism by which this 
mutant γPKC triggers the neurodegeneration of cerebel-
lar PCs and cerebellar dysfunction. Verbeek et al. re-
ported that certain mutations (G118D and C150F) 
 increase the kinase activity of γPKC (5). In contrast, Lin 
et al. reported that other mutant γPKCs (H101Y, S119P, 
and G128D) have reduced kinase activities and inhibited 
hydrogen peroxide–induced activation of endogenous 
γPKC, rendering the expressing cells vulnerable to oxi-
dative stress (33). The kinase activities of mutant γPKCs 
(G118D, V138E, and C142S) at the plasma membrane 
are lower when activated by a phorbol ester, leading to 
aberrant MAPK signaling (34). More recently, Asai et al. 
have reported that a mutant γPKC with higher activity 
induces aberrant phosphorylation and localization of 
aprataxin, a DNA repair protein (35). While we have 
shown that most SCA14 mutant γPKCs have increased 
basal kinase activities, several mutants displayed lower 
or unchanged activities (36). In contrast to controversial 
results concerning the kinase activity of mutant γPKC, 
we have confirmed that most mutant γPKCs tend to form 
cytoplasmic aggregates and that this property is related 
to the cytotoxicity of mutant γPKC (20, 31). Therefore, 
we consider that neurodegeneration of cerebellar PCs in 
SCA14 patients is mostly due to the aggregation-prone 
property of mutant γPKC. Our present study revealed 
that Congo red inhibits aggregation of mutant γPKC in 
SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 1: A and B) and primary cultured 
PCs (Fig. 3: A and B), suggesting that mutant γPKC 
forms aggregates with an amyloid structure. This is 
consistent with our previous findings that PCs expressing 
mutant γPKC stain with an anti-amyloid oligomer anti-
body (20). This amyloidogenic property is commonly 
observed in mutant proteins causing neurodegenerative 
disease, including proteins with expanded polyglutamine 
(23). Since many types of SCAs (SCA1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 12, 
17) are caused by polyglutamine expansion (37), amyloid 
formation of mutant protein might be a common patho-
genic pathway between SCA14 and other SCAs.
Several reports have demonstrated that Congo red di-
rectly interacts with recombinant mutant proteins, stabi-
lizes their conformations and inhibits fibril formations 
(26, 29, 38). Consistent with these findings, this study 
revealed that Congo red significantly inhibits rapid in-
solubilization of recombinant mutant γPKC (Fig. 2). 
Taken together with the findings in Figs. 1 and 3, Congo 
red that is taken up by cells directly stabilizes mutant 
γPKC and may thereby reduce aggregate formation. In 
the present study, we did not clarify whether Congo red 
is taken up into cells. However, similar to our present 
study (Fig. 1: A and B) it has been reported that extracel-
lular treatment with Congo red inhibits intracellular amy-
loid aggregation of proteins with expanded polyglutamine 
(26 – 28), indicating that Congo red is incorporated into 
cells.
Our previous study demonstrated that mutant γPKC 
forms oligomers in cultured cells (20). In the present 
study, FRAP analysis revealed that Congo red improves 
the mobility of mutant γPKC in primary cultured PCs, 
likely by inhibiting its oligomerization (Fig. 3: C and D). 
Similar to our studies, other groups have demonstrated 
by different methods that Congo red inhibits oligomer-
ization of mutant proteins with expanded polyglutamine 
using fluorescence resonance energy transfer and fluo-
rescent correlation spectroscopy (28, 39). These findings 
strengthen the hypothesis that improved mobility of 
mutant γPKC by Congo red is caused by inhibition of 
oligomerization. γPKC is translocated from the cytosol

channel-coupled receptors (40). In PCs, trans-synaptic 
stimulation induces propagation of γPKC translocation, 
which has a physiological significance for PC function 
through PKC-mediated signaling (41). Our previous 
studies have revealed that the reduced mobility of mutant 
γPKC results in attenuation of its translocation within 
PCs (20). These aberrant properties of mutant γPKC 
cause improper development of PC dendrites (20). In the 
present study, Congo red reversed the improper develop-
ment of primary cultured PC dendrites triggered by mu-
tant γPKC (Fig. 4: A – C). This is the first demonstration 
that Congo red prevents the aberrant neuronal morphol-
ogy triggered by a mutant protein that causes neurode-
generative disease. Since Congo red improves mobility 
of mutant γPKC, restoration of dendritic development by 
Congo red might be due to the improvement of γPKC-
mediated signal transduction. Moreover, it is possible 
that monomeric, as well as oligometric, mutant γPKC 
might cause improper development of PC dendrites. In 
addition to the higher basal activity as described above, 
mutant γPKC has a reduced ability to bind diacylglycerol. 
Indeed, single molecule fluorescent observation has re-
vealed that mutant γPKC was retained at the plasma 
membrane for less period than WT when cells are treated 
with phorbol ester or stimulated via Gq-coupled recep-
tors (36), leading to reduced phosphorylation of PKC 
substrate at the plasma membrane (34, 36). It is possible 
that this aberrant property of monomeric mutant γPKC is 
one of causes for improper development of PC dendrites. 
Although we did not examine the effect of Congo red on 
this property of monomeric mutant γPKC, this dye might 
improve this dysfunction through directly interacting 
with mutant γPKC and stabilizing its conformation (Fig. 
2).
Congo red has been demonstrated to inhibit apoptosis 
by amyloid-prone mutant proteins (28, 42, 43). Consis-
tently, Congo red inhibited apoptosis triggered by mutant 
γPKC in primary cultured PCs (Fig. 4: D and E). As 
previously reported (20), apoptotic PCs with condensed 
nuclei were exclusively observed in PCs displaying ag-
gregates of mutant γPKC (Fig. 4D). Since Congo red 
inhibited aggregation of mutant γPKC, the percentage of 
PCs having aggregates among total PCs expressing mu-
tant γPKC was reduced, resulting in an insignificant ef-
fect of Congo red on apoptotic cells in PCs with aggre-
gates. This finding strongly suggests that apoptotic 
suppression by Congo red is mainly due to inhibition of 
aggregation itself. However, Congo red almost com-
pletely inhibited apoptosis triggered by mutant γPKC, in 
spite of the partial inhibition of its aggregation and oli-
gomerization (Fig. 3: B and D). In our previous report, 
we revealed two types aggregation of mutant γPKC, large 
massive and small dot-like types, and we have demon-
strated that massive aggregation preferably inhibits 
ubiquitin-proteasome–related protein degradation and 
causes apoptosis (21). The massive aggregation of mutant 
γPKC proceeded through the accumulation of small dot-
like aggregates (21). Therefore, Congo red might inhibit 
this progression of aggregate size from dot-like to the 
massive type, leading to almost complete inhibition of 
apoptosis. In addition, Congo red might inhibit apoptosis 
of PCs, independently of aggregation inhibition because 
this chemical is reported to disturb cellular redox activity 
and cell energy metabolism (24).
Our previous and present study revealed that mutant 
γPKC formed aggregates in primary cultured PCs (20). 
However, previous autopsy reports have shown no ag-
gregation in SCA14 patient brains (1, 44). The discrep-
ancy might be due to the excessive expression of mutant 
γPKC in our experiments. However, these two autopsy 
reports are from SCA14 patients having the same mu-
taion (H101Y). Patients with other mutations might have 
aggregates of mutant γPKC in their cerebellar PCs. Fur-
ther autopsy studies are necessary to reveal the impor-
tance of mutant γPKC aggregation in the pathogenesis of 
SCA14. Moreover, we have recently revealed that mutant 
γPKC is more rapidly degraded than WT γPKC (45). It is 
generally accepted that decline of protein degradation 
with age results in accumulation of unfolded proteins 
(46, 47). In the case of SCA14 mutant γPKC, age-related 
decline of proteolysis would cause its accumulation. A 
longer period might be needed for mutant γPKC 
 molecules to form aggregates in the brain of SCA14 
patients.
In summary, our study suggests that Congo red may be 
useful for the treatment of SCA14. However, the effect 
of Congo red in vivo on mouse neurodegenerative models 
is controversial; Sanchez et al. demonstrated that intra-
peritoneal treatment with Congo red ameliorated the 
weight loss and motor dysfunction in the Huntington’s 
disease mouse model (28), whereas Wood et al. found 
completely opposite effects using the same mice (48). 
The latter supposed that systemically administered 
Congo red does not reach the brain parenchyma through 
the blood brain barrier (BBB). Moreover, it has been re-
ported that this dye reduces serum proteins and becomes 
highly carcinogenic when it is metabolized in liver (24). 
From these findings, Congo red is not suitable for the 
treatment for SCA14 by oral or systemic administration. 
Instead, intrathecal treatment of this dye might be effec-
tive for SCA14 treatment. However, since 100 μM Congo 
red itself affects the dendritic development of primary 
cultured PCs in our present study (Supplementary Fig. 2: 
available in the online version only), it could have a po-
tential to damage neuronal cells at higher concentration.

Taken together, Congo red is not an ideal compound for 
treating SCA14 at present. However, our present study 
raises the possibility that chemicals inhibiting amyloid 
formation may be useful for the treatment of SCA14. To 
date, there are several Congo red derivatives such as 
Chrysamine G, BSB (1-bromo-2,5-bis(3-carboxy-4- 
hydroxystyryl)benzene), and FSB (1-fluoro-2,5-bis(3-
carboxy-4-hydroxystyryl)benzene), which have improved 
ability to cross the BBB (24). It is also possible that other 
compounds inhibiting amyloid production, including 
thioflavin T, thioflavin S, and curcumin (26, 49), may 
also inhibit the aggregation and cytotoxic effects of mu-
tant γPKC. It is worth examining whether these com-
pounds are candidates for SCA14 treatment, especially 
in vivo.
