# A novel homozygous missense variant in LRP4 causing Cenani-Lenz syndactyly syndrome and literature review.
**Authors:** Fu Y, Zhou Y, Zhang Q, Dong J, Zheng J, Li M, Liu J
**Journal:** Mol Genet Genomic Med (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mgg3.2319](https://doi.org/10.1002/mgg3.2319)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Cenani-Lenzsyndactyly syndrome (CLSS; OMIM 212780) is a rare autosomal recessive acral deformity, which is mainly manifested in the fusion of fingers or toes, disordered phalangeal structure, shortening or fusion of the radius and ulna, and renal hypoplasia.

CASE PRESENTATION: Our report described an individual with mild phenotypes from China. His parents were not consanguineous. The affected individual was non-dysmorphic. Standard X-ray showed that the both hands have only four metacarpal bones. The distal end of the first metacarpal bone on the right was relatively slender, and the distal phalanx was absent. Multiple phalanges and some soft tissues of both hands were fused. Exome sequencing revealed a novel biallelic c.282C⟩Avariant in low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 4 (LRP4; OMIM604270; NM_002334.4) causing p. (Asn94Lys) change in the encoded protein. This variant is predicted to be potentially pathogenic, affecting protein structure and function.

CONCLUSION: We report a novel missense variant present in homozygosity in LRP4 to broaden the pathogenic spectrum of LRP4 in syndactyly, and exome sequencing technology is a powerful tool for genetic analysis in prenatal diagnosis and medical research, as a preferred method for the diagnosis of syndactyly and related phenotypes.

Syndactyly deformity (SD) is a congenital abnormality of hand and foot development that affects more men than women (Jordan et al., 2012). Syndactyly can manifest alone or as a feature of a complex genetic disorder or syndrome. According to the specific phenotype, syndactyly deformities are divided into nine categories, Cenani‐Lenz syndactyly syndrome (CLSS; OMIM 212780) is type VII syndactyly and is also the most severe type in SD (Zaib et al., 2022).

CLSS is an autosomal recessive disorder and is the first reported by Cenani A. and Lenz W. in 1967 (Cenani & Lenz, 1967). CLSS is very rare (Afzal et al., 2017; Kariminejad et al., 2013; Khan et al., 2013; Li et al., 2010; Lindy et al., 2014). The typical features of affected individuals include shortening and fusion of the fingers, metacarpals, and toes. There might also be fusion of the skin tissue surrounding the phalanges. Often the impact on foot structure is not as severe. It is one of the most severe of syndactyly syndromes known to date. This is not only reflected in the development of deformities of the phalangeal and metacarpal bones but also affects the carpal bones and the distal ends of the upper and lower extremities, making the carpal bones misplaced. The ulna and radius are fused together, and severe cases show spinal dysplasia and short stature. Some affected individuals also have mild facial deformities, prominent forehead, ptosis of palpebral fissure, and micrognathia. In addition, most affected individuals have symptoms of renal hypoplasia and dysfunction (Harpf et al., 2005).

The causative gene of CLSS has been determined to be located in the chromosome 11p11.2‐q13.1 region, namely low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 4 (LRP4; OMIM 604270; NM_002334.4) (Jarbhou et al., 2008). LRP4 belongs to the low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) receptor family and comprises 38 exons, encoding 1905 amino acids (Tian et al., 2006). During limb development, LRP4 can antagonize the WNT/β‐catenin signaling pathway by competing with LRP5/LRP6 for binding to WNT ligands. Loss of LRP4 function results in increased WNT/β‐catenin pathway activity and abnormal extremity development (Leupin et al., 2011; Winkler et al., 2003). Previous studies have shown that missense mutations of LRP4 confer loss of function. Proteins encoded by some missense mutations of LRP4 can neither antagonize the canonical WNT signaling pathway nor be transported to the cell membrane normally. (Li et al., 2010). Here, we report a novel missense variant in LRP4 that results in a p. (Arg94Lys) substitution in the encoded protein, and we predict this variant to be likely pathogenic.

First, exon capture (Exome V6) using the Agilent SureSelect method was used, and high‐throughput sequencing was performed on the Illumina sequencing platform. After the sequencing data were matched and analyzed by NextGENe® (State College, PA) software, the variants were screened and interpreted by the Ingenuity online software system, and the candidate variants were verified by Sanger sequencing.

The variant c.282C>A; p. (Asn94Lys) in LRP4 (OMIM 604270; NM_002334.4) is not included in databases such as HGMD (The Human Gene Mutation Database, https://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk/ac/index.php) and gnomAD (Genome Aggregation Database, https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/). This variant is classified as “likely pathogenic” according to the ACMG (American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics, https://www.acmg.net/) variant classification criteria (ACMG evidences: PM2, PM3, PP3, PP4). The variant is predicted as “probably damaging” by Polyphen‐2 software. And its CADD score is 22.7.

The distal phalanx was absent, and the structure of the remaining phalanges was disordered. The hands were asymmetrical syndactyly and had missing fingers. Syndactyly deformity (SD) is a congenital digital malformation, and it is one of the most common hereditary limb disorders. In 2012, Malik put forward a nine‐type classification system (Malik, 2012). In 2022, Zaib et al. summarized the classification of non‐syndromic syndactyly (Zaib et al., 2022). According to the specific phenotype, syndactyly deformities are divided into nine categories, most SD are autosomal dominant (AD), only types VII and IX are autosomal recessive (AR). Type VII SD is CLSS, the most serious type in SD and very rare (Zaib et al., 2022), with syndactyly, shortening and fusion of radius and ulna, and abnormal development of metacarpal and metatarsal bones as typical clinical features. Most CLSS is caused by missense mutations in LRP4 (Afzal et al., 2017; Alrayes et al., 2020; Hettiaracchchi et al., 2018; Steel et al., 2020; Zaib et al., 2022). It is one of the most severe types of syndactyly known to date (Harpf et al., 2005; Malik, 2012).

We find that CLSS is usually caused by missense variants in highly conserved regions of LRP4 that affect extracellular domains of the encoded protein. A few variants are truncating mutations, and the clinical phenotype is more serious or even lethal. These changes may lead to structural alterations in the extracellular domains of LRP4 that interfere with normal protein folding and thus prevent efficient protein output through the secretory pathway. Among the 14 clinically different CLS families studied in 2010, mutations in LRP4 were found in 12 families (Li et al., 2010). In these 12 cases, the investigators observed that most affected individuals of CLSS had mild facial deformities in addition to typical limb deformities, including forehead prominence, overdevelopment of the eyeball, palpebral fissure oblique, and micrognathia. They also found that half of CLSS families had symptoms of kidney hypoplasia. In 2010, a mouse model of CLSS with Lrp4 deletion was constructed, which showed that Lrp4 deletion caused delayed ureteral bud formation and failed cell differentiation in mice, resulting in renal hypoplasia in mice (Karner et al., 2010).

A severe case of CLSS due to loss of LRP4 function was reported in 2013 (Kariminejad et al., 2013). The homozygous mutation c.289G > T led to premature termination of translation. Compared with the classic CLSS phenotype, this individual exhibited a more severe phenotype, especially short limbs and severe humeral deformities that have not been previously reported. In addition, the affected individual had a rare clinical manifestation: excessive nipples. The face also had mild deformities, including a prominent forehead, simple ears, thin nares, and a depressed nasal tip.

In the large Pakistani family with nine members having c.2858T>C (p.L953P) homozygous mutations, the clinical manifestations of the affected individuals were mild, except for the typical upper limb deformities, and the foot deformities were relatively mild. Three of these individuals presented with hypoplasia of the left kidney, confirming a close link between CLSS and renal abnormalities (Khan et al., 2013). In 2018, two individuals with mild clinical manifestations of CLSS were also reported, and both of them were homozygous mutations inLRP4 c.1348A > G (p.I450V), which showed bilateral postaxial oligodactyly limited to the upper limbs (Hettiaracchchi et al., 2018). Other than that, no descriptive features of CLSS were observed. However, there are a small number of LRP4 variants with different clinical manifestations of non‐CLSS. In 2000 Seven et al. (2000) reported an affected individual who presented with scoliosis of the thoracic spine and mixed hearing loss in addition to the typical CLSS. Temtamy et al. (2003) reported two cases with CLSS and mild facial dysmorphism in 2003. In one of the families, an affected sibling had genital anomalies and cleft palate. In 2008, Jarbhou et al. (2008) described an affected individual with CLSS with facial dysmorphism, hypoplasia of the kidney, hyperthyroidism, laryngomalacia, and congenital dislocation of the hips. In 2017, Afzal et al. (2017) reported three affected individuals in a family who all had severe CLSS phenotypes caused by the novel splice mutation c.316+1G>A in LRP4 with orofacial dysmorphism (cleft‐lip), hypoplastic shoulder joint and mildly short stature. In 2020, Steel et al. (2020) reported two affected individuals with the novel homozygous missense mutation c.4910G>A (p.C1637Y), both of whom had recurrent ketotic hypoglycemia in addition to more severe complex syndactyly dysmorphism.

Cases of prenatal lethal CLSS due to truncating mutations in LRP4 were also reported in 2014 (Lindy et al., 2014) and 2021 (Yesodharan et al., 2021). In 2014, two fetuses in the first and second pregnancies of healthy parents were reported (Lindy et al., 2014), both of whom died prenatally due to the compound heterozygous truncating mutation of LRP4. The father was a carrier of c.2401A>T (p.K801X), and the mother was a carrier of c.3062delC (p.S1020Qfs*27). Both fetus II‐1and fetus II‐2 were compound heterozygous, not only with c.2401A>T (p.K801X) heterozygous truncating mutation but also c.3062delC (p.S1020Qfs*27) heterozygous frameshift mutation. Fetus II‐1 showed a hypoplastic nose, anteverted nares, prominent zygomata, micrognathia, short malformed limbs, and bilateral renal agenesis. Fetus II‐2 was small for gestational age, collapsed cranium, short limbs, and four‐limb oligodactyly. fetus II‐2 had no obvious bilateral renal agenesis. The variant c.2710 del T (p.Trp904GlyfsTer5) discovered by Yesodharan et al. (2021) in 2021 led to frameshift mutations and predicted that this variant caused premature termination of the LRP4 protein. The truncating protein is predicted to have a length of 907 amino acids (aa)instead of the original length of 1905 aa. The truncating protein may lack function. The fetal phenotype was similar to that of fetus II‐1 reported by Amanda in 2014: bilateral renal agenesis, transverse defects of all four limbs, flipper‐like (tetraphocomelia) and the presence of obvious facial deformities and micrognathia. The difference was that this case also presented with polycystic enlargement, prominent forehead, extremely flat nasal bridge, short nose, and everted upper and lower lips.

The individual we present exhibited typical clinical features of CLSS, with more severe upper extremity deformities than lower extremities, and no significant renal abnormalities. His parents and sister did not have a corresponding phenotype.

This report not only discovers a new missense mutation in the causative gene LRP4 of CLSS, which broadens the pathogenic spectrum of LRP4 gene in syndactyly but also shows that exome sequencing technology is a powerful tool for genetic analysis in prenatal diagnosis and medical research, as a preferred method for the diagnosis of syndactyly and related phenotypes.
