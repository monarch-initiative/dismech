# Predictors for Mortality in Children with Scorpion Envenomation Admitted to Pediatric Intensive Care Unit, Qena Governorate, Egypt.
**Authors:** Baseer KA, Naser MAA
**Journal:** Am J Trop Med Hyg (2019)
**DOI:** [10.4269/ajtmh.19-0319](https://doi.org/10.4269/ajtmh.19-0319)

## Content

This study aimed to identify the clinical and laboratory manifestations that affect outcome of scorpion envenomation in children. It included 154 children admitted with scorpion sting envenomation over a period of 2 years. The epidemiological, clinical, and laboratory findings of patients were recorded, and grading of severity was performed based on local and systemic involvement. Organ failure was determined according to diagnostic criterion of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, and severity of illness was assessed by the Pediatric Risk of Mortality (PRISM III) score. Of studied children, 58.4% were males and 41.6% were females. Children aged > 5 years suffered more scorpion stings (79.9%) than others did. The place of residence was rural more than urban, outdoor stings more than indoors, nocturnal more than diurnal, and most stings were on the exposed areas of the limbs. Based on clinical evaluation, 37.7% of patients were classified as class I severity followed by class II (48.7%) and class III (13.6%). Among studied cases, 21 deaths (13.6%) were registered; all of them belonged to class III severity. Mortality was significantly higher in children with agitation, coma, convulsions, arrhythmia, heart failure, pulmonary edema, and priapism. There were significantly higher values of leukocytes, platelets, creatinine, liver enzymes, glucose, and creatine phosphokinase in non-survivors than in survivors. The presence of organ failure was associated with mortality. In addition, the need for mechanical ventilation and inotropic support were at increased risk of mortality. Moreover, a significant association was found between PRISM score and the number of failed organs with fatal outcome.

Scorpion envenomation is a broad public health problem all over the world, particularly in both tropical and subtropical areas, not only because of its high incidence but also because of the ability of some species to induce severe and sometimes fatal clinical manifestations.1 There are more than 2,000 of scorpion species; among them, only 50 species are found to be harmful to human being.2 Most stings produce local manifestations only, but on occasion can be a life-threatening indigence, especially in children. Most deaths due to scorpion envenomation are assigned to cardiopulmonary complications as toxic myocarditis and acute pulmonary edema.3

The scorpion species that are of valuable medical importance belong to the Buthidae family. Buthidae encompass four genera (Androctonus, Leiurus, Mesobuthus, and Hottentotta) and are known to cause variable public health problems in North Africa, Asia, the Middle East, India, and Central and South America.1 The Leiurus quinquestriatus scorpion is considered as one of the most serious and significant of many scorpion envenomation to humans, especially in children.4 It is known by several names, including the Egyptian scorpion, five-keeled gold scorpion, and Arabian death-stalker.5

The venom contains mucopolysaccharides, enzymes such as hyaluronidase and phospholipase, serotonin, histamine, enzyme inhibitors, and neurotoxins.6 Several complex toxins may be present in the venom of a single scorpion species and produce potent interactive effects in a victim. Clinical effects of envenomation depend on the species of scorpion, amount of injected toxin at the time of sting, size of the scorpion, age of the victim, season of the sting, and time consumed between sting and hospitalization.7 Scorpionidae species trigger severe agonizing pain, which radiates along conforming dermatomes, whereas Buthidae species envenomation can result in fatal systemic effects.8

The real incidence, morbidity, and lethality of scorpion stings are likely to be underappreciated because scorpion envenomation is not one of the notifiable diseases, but since the introduction of therapeutic approaches like vasodilators as prazosin and hydralazine, scorpion antivenom, and intensive care management, the fatality decreased.9 Multiple organ failure among scorpion-envenomed children has been documented by many researchers, and appreciation of envenomation severity primarily in children is pivotal for establishing the prognosis and developing adequate treatment.10

This retrospective descriptive study analyzed the epidemiological and clinical features of a total of 154 patients who presented to the emergency department of General Qena Hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) of South Valley University Hospital in Qena Governorate with scorpion sting between October 2016 and September 2018. The diagnosis of scorpion envenomation was established on a history of scorpion sting documented by direct visualization of scorpion by the child or one of his family. Data were obtained from parents and recorded in a questionnaire, and written consent was taken. It included the age and gender of scorpion-envenomed victims child; residence of patients, whether urban or rural; place of sting occurrence, whether out- or indoor; time of sting, whether day or night; the anatomical sting site; time between sting and hospitalization; time of antivenom injection; and the clinical manifestations. The laboratory investigations included serum glucose, sodium, potassium, calcium, complete blood count, prothrombin time and prothrombin concentration, bilirubin level, urea and creatinine, liver enzymes, and creatine phosphokinase (CPK) levels. The severity of envenomation was evaluated according to Scorpion Consensus Expert Group11 to include three classes; class I including local manifestations, class II including systemic involvement, and class III including one or more of the following: cardiac failure, hypotension, arrhythmias, bradycardia, cardiovascular collapse, respiratory failure, cyanosis, dyspnea, pulmonary edema, and neurological failure (Glasgow score < 6 in the absence of sedation). Organ failure was determined according to diagnostic measure of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome12 and severity of illness was assessed by Pediatric Risk of Mortality (PRISM III) score.13 The frequencies of clinical and epidemiological parameters were tabulated and expressed as percentage.

Analysis of data was evaluated using SPSS version 20 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD, and categorical variables were expressed as frequency. Pearson’s chi-square was used for the comparison of categorical data between groups and t-test used for comparison of means. Finally, the univariate linear regression Pearson correlation was used to study the association between PRISM score and length of hospital stay. For all tests, a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Scorpion envenomation is a life-threatening emergency event in many parts of the world.1 Although the incidence of scorpion envenoming is higher in adults, the severity of stings is significantly more important in children, in whom the mortality rate approximates more than 10 times compared with adults. This may be due to the fact that for the same amount of venom injected, children will have higher serum toxin levels than adult victims. Also, it is possible that the heart and other organs are particularly vulnerable to higher venom uptake.14

The severity of scorpion envenomation depends on the scorpion and the victim. Factors related to the scorpions include the involved species, its size, the amount and content inoculated by the venom glands, and the sequence of stings whether single or multiple. The victim-related factors are age, part of the body attacked by the sting, body weight, health status, sensitivity of the child to venom, and time consumed between sting and treatment including support of vital organs.15 A smaller child, a lower body mass, and a larger ratio of venom to body mass lead to a more severe reaction. In untreated babies, a mortality rate of 20% is reported compared with 10% in untreated school-aged children and 1% in untreated adults.16

In our study, the older age-group most affected, but fatality was more in younger age-group. Mallanagouda et al.17 found that age was one of the important risk factors of scorpion envenomation and that younger age-group had earlier symptoms after the sting and more delayed recovery than older. The detected higher frequency of envenomation in males more than in females, in outdoors more than in indoors, and in rural than in urban areas is supported by several studies.1,14 Most of our patients lived in rural areas and had typically played outside of their homes. Also, walking barefooted is widespread in our regions, especially in agricultural areas where children help their parents.17 Delayed hospital admission and antivenom serotherapy were associated with mortality, and this was in concordance with Çağlar et al.’s study.18 In addition, Bahloul et al.19 found a lower rate of complications in victims with rapid antivenom injection time and explained this by the rapid absorption and distribution of venom to different body organs on delayed antivenom therapy.

Similar to other investigators, agitation, coma, and convulsions were the most common neurologic manifestation among our patients and hypertensive encephalopathy, hypoxia, and a direct effect of the venom were suggested as the major pathways for its occurence.20 Bahloul et al.19 detected that coma and convulsions were clearly correlated with required ICU admission and mortality. Association of tachypnea and pulmonary edema with higher mortality is supported by Prasad et al.’s study.21 Acute pulmonary edema has been explained by increased pulmonary capillary permeability in response to massive release of catecholamines and toxic myocarditis induced by the scorpion toxins.22 Respiratory disturbances have also been observed in the absence of pulmonary edema or myocardial damage as a result of acidosis, excessive secretions, or electrolyte imbalance.23 Scorpion venom may affect voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels, causing myocardial strain and damage,18 and this may be responsible for arrhythmia and heart failure detected in our study. Soomro et al.24 reported major cardiovascular complications, such as blood pressure changes, electrocardiographic findings simulating myocardial strain or infarction, echocardiographic abnormalities of left ventricular dysfunction, congestive cardiac failure, and pulmonary edema in 18.5% of cases of scorpion stings. Hyperglycemia and hypokalemia increase the risk of disturbed cardiac rhythm, and an abnormal electrocardiographic changes.25 As the scorpion venom contains cardiotoxins, it can cause toxic myocarditis through reduction of Na-K-ATPase and release catecholamines from neurons, and adrenal glands, thereby increasing myocardial oxygen demands.26

Regarding gastrointestinal tract manifestations, acute pancreatitis may explain the detected abdominal pain in our study.27 In fact, gastrointestinal manifestations are the consequence of massive release of neurotransmitters by neurotoxin components of scorpion venom, leading to excessive acid and pepsin secretion and change in gastrointestinal peristalsis, leading to nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. On the other hand, it may be related to compromised gastrointestinal blood flow resulting from the effects of catecholamines and the arterial constrictive effect induced by shock.28

Disturbed renal functions detected in some of our cases is supported by an Iranian study that reported cases with acute kidney tubular necrosis (ATN) following scorpion envenomation.29 A systemic inflammatory response syndrome, hemoglobinuria-related nephropathy, ATN, hemolytic uremic syndrome, and interstitial nephritis have been estimated to be responsible for renal failure in scorpion envenomation.30 Priapism like Parsad et al.’s study21 had significantly higher mortality. The nitrergic nerve stimulation of supplying penile smooth muscle as a part of autonomic storm may explain the priapism observed in severe scorpion envenomation.8

In the present study, leukocyte levels were markedly increased in non-survivors and induction of a venom-mediated systemic inflammatory response-like syndrome was suggested as the cause.31 In addition, thrombocytosis may reflect the stimulatory effect of released catecholamines, especially adrenaline, on platelet synthesis.32 In agreement with previous studies, we documented hyperglycemia as a poor prognostic sign in cases of severe envenomation.33 It was explained by the opposing effect of released catecholamines, and corticosteroids against insulin.19

Similar to Bahloul et al.’s study,20 the presence of organs failure was positively correlated with poor outcome. Moreover, the need for ventilation mechanical support and inotropes use may reflect more occurrences of left ventricular failure, cardiogenic shock, and pulmonary edema. A significant association was found between PRISM score and the number of failed organs with lethal outcome, and this was comparable to other studies.34,35

The strengths of this study include the use of predefined assessment parameters for case classification and study plan to evaluate epidemiological and clinic–laboratorial risk factors of severity. The most important limitation of this study is the small sample size of studied scorpion-envenomed children who presented to our largest two hospitals in Qena Governorate. Because the study is a retrospective descriptive study, only the patients who presented to the hospital are considered in the study and many cases were missed in local centers because of unawareness of the danger of envenomation. Another important limitation of this study is that we use general nonspecific antivenom for scorpion-envenomed victims as we have no specific antivenom in our localities; so we inject it routinely, regardless of the scorpion type, and cannot determine exactly who benefits more from it.

This study permitted us to appreciate the epidemiological profile of hospitalized stung children in our governorate and to underline some aggravating factors that may alert toward possible deterioration. Agitation, coma, convulsions, arrhythmia, heart failure, pulmonary edema, and priapism were major contributing factors predicting outcome in children with scorpion envenomation. In addition, non-survivors showed higher values of leukocytes, platelets, glucose, liver enzymes, and CPK than survivors did. Moreover, higher PRISM score and number of failed organs appeared to be associated with fatal outcome. Our study recommends that patients with severe systemic symptoms must be admitted to ICU setting because of the probable deterioration, the risks of organ failure occurrence, and the possibility of need for airway and blood pressure support. Also, we recommend inclusion of scorpion envenomation topic in a regular medical training and teaching at our localities. Future studies should be directed toward new modalities of therapy plus specific antivenom aiming for minimizing organs affection.
