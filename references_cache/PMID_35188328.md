# LMOD2-related dilated cardiomyopathy presenting in late infancy.
**Authors:** Lay E, Azamian MS, Denfield SW, Dreyer W, Spinner JA, Kearney D, Zhang L, Worley KC, Bi W, Lalani SR
**Journal:** Am J Med Genet A (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ajmg.a.62699](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajmg.a.62699)

## Content

Leiomodin-2 (LMOD2) is an important regulator of the thin filament length, known to promote elongation of actin through polymerization at pointed ends. Mice with Lmod2 deficiency die around 3 weeks of age due to severe dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), resulting from decreased heart contractility due to shorter thin filaments. To date, there have been three infants from two families reported with biallelic variants in LMOD2, presenting with perinatal onset DCM. Here, we describe a third family with a child harboring a previously described homozygous frameshift variant, c.1243_1244delCT (p.L415Vfs*108) with DCM, presenting later in infancy at 9 months of age. Family history was relevant for a sibling who died suddenly at 1 year of age after being diagnosed with cardiomegaly. LMOD2-related cardiomyopathy is a rare form of inherited cardiomyopathy resulting from thin filament length dysregulation and should be considered in genetic evaluation of newborns and infants with suspected autosomal recessive inheritance or sporadic early onset cardiomyopathy.

Pediatric cardiomyopathy affects about one in every 100,000 children in the United States and remains the primary reason for cardiac transplantation in those older than one year of age (Webber, 2008). Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is the most common type of cardiomyopathy, accounting for ~50% of all pediatric cardiomyopathies, of which one-third are attributed to an identifiable etiology (Lipshultz et al., 2013; Towbin et al., 2006; Wilkinson et al., 2015). More recent studies of pediatric patients with idiopathic DCM who had molecular genetic testing reported diagnostic yields between 10% to 50%--highlighting that some of these previously labeled idiopathic cases have a genetic etiology (Herkert et al., 2018; Quiat et al., 2020; Ramchand et al., 2020).

There are over 30 known causative genes responsible for DCM, with many encoding for proteins within the sarcomere unit. Some of the sarcomeric genes relevant for pediatric cardiomyopathy include TTN, MYH7, MYH6, MYPN, TNNT2, TNNC1, TNNI3, and TPM1, which tend to show autosomal dominant inheritance pattern in families. Alterations in these genes affect interaction of actin and myosin within the sarcomere, leading to decreased cardiomyocyte contractility. Assembly of actin is distinctively orchestrated through processes of nucleation of new filaments, elongation at filament ends, and capping of filament ends to terminate elongation. Leiomodin (Lmod) is a homolog of tropomodulin (Tmod), a capping protein. Both are important players in maintaining uniform length of thin filaments which is crucial for cardiac function. LMOD2 is an actin-binding protein, expressed in cardiac and skeletal muscles and known to regulate thin filament lengths by enhancing actin assembly in vitro (Chereau et al., 2008). Lmod2 and Tmod1 have been shown to have opposite effects in maintaining thin filament lengths (Tsukada et al., 2010). While Tmods play a role in capping of filament ends to terminate elongation, Lmods act as powerful nucleators of actin polymerization in vitro to lengthen thin filament. Lmod2 competes for binding with Tmod1 and displaces it, allowing for actin polymerization/thin filament elongation (Tolkatchev et al., 2020). Overexpression of Lmod2 in cardiomyocytes leads to elongation of actin-thin filaments (Tsukada et al., 2010), and reduces Tmod1 assembly at the slow-growing (pointed) end of the actin filament.

In 2015 and 2018, Pappas et al. reported early postnatal cardiomyopathy and lethality around three weeks of age in Lmod2 knockout mice resulting from short thin filaments, decreased force generation, and decreased cardiac contractility. In 2019, the first infant with severe dilated cardiomyopathy due to a homozygous nonsense variant in LMOD2 was described by Ahrens et al. The onset of DCM was detected shortly after birth with rapid progression to heart failure, and she ultimately received orthotopic heart transplant (OHT) at 10 months old. Further analysis of the explanted heart showed short thin filaments and reduced myocyte contractility, similar to the Lmod2 knockout mice. Most recently, a second family of two siblings born one year apart was published by Greenway et al. (2021). Both had compound heterozygous variants in LMOD2 with neonatal dilated cardiomyopathy found after delivery, and both died around one month of age without OHT. Here we report the third family with severe DCM due to a homozygous loss of function variant in LMOD2 detected by whole exome sequencing. Our patient highlights consideration of this rare autosomal recessive cardiomyopathy in evaluation of pediatric cardiomyopathy, particularly in those presenting in the first year of life with relevant family history.

The study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Institutional Review Board of Baylor College of Medicine and informed consent was obtained for the patient. Peripheral blood samples from the patient and his parents were submitted to Baylor Genetics. DNA was extracted from whole blood using the Puregene DNA Blood Kit (Gentra, Minneapolis, MN, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For trio exome sequencing, the average depth of coverage was 154X with 97.5% of the targeted regions (exonic regions of all nuclear genes plus +/− 5bp of exon-intron boundaries) sequenced at 20X and higher. The sample was concurrently analyzed by HumanExome-12 v1 array for quality-control and detecting large copy-number variants (CNVs), absence of heterozygosity (AOH), and uniparental disomy (UPD). Exome data were interpreted according to ACMG guidelines and variant interpretation guidelines of Baylor Genetics as previously described (Yang et al., 2014).

Proband was a male born at 39 weeks gestation by spontaneous vaginal delivery to a 27 year old G5P4 mother and a 31 year old father. Pregnancy and delivery were uncomplicated. He was developmentally appropriate for 9 months of age at the time of presentation when he was evaluated for emesis, diarrhea, and dehydration—raising concern for an acute gastroenteritis. After he received a total of 40 mL/kg of IV fluid bolus, a gallop was auscultated and hepatomegaly was palpated on follow-up physical exam. This prompted a chest x-ray to be obtained, which demonstrated cardiomegaly and interstitial edema (Fig. S1A). The initial B-natriuretic peptide was significantly elevated at 2960 pg/mL (reference range 0-100 pg/mL). The initial electrocardiogram (ECG) was notable for right ventricular (RV) hypertrophy, left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy, and a nonspecific T-wave abnormality (Fig. S1B). The initial echocardiogram demonstrated dilated cardiomyopathy (LV diastolic dimension of 3.82 cm, Z-score 5.06) with severely depressed LV systolic function (ejection fraction or EF of 15%). He also had moderate mitral valve regurgitation, moderate tricuspid valve regurgitation, and a mildly dilated RV with severely depressed RV systolic function (Fig. S1C, D & E). He was admitted to the intensive care unit and was started on an intravenous milrinone infusion. Due to impending cardiorespiratory failure, he was intubated and mechanically ventilated.

His clinical course during the hospitalization was complicated by supraventricular tachycardia for which he was started on amiodarone. He was weaned off intravenous milrinone and discharged home on oral heart failure therapies after a two-month hospitalization. At discharge, his RV systolic function had improved to normal, and his LV systolic function had improved to mildly depressed (ejection fraction 45-50%). He was re-admitted four days later for persistent emesis. An upper gastrointestinal (GI) series showed midgut malrotation without volvulus, requiring a Ladd’s procedure. One week later, while still hospitalized recovering from abdominal surgery, he was noted to be tachycardic with a new gallop. A follow-up echocardiogram demonstrated a decline in LV systolic function. He clinically deteriorated, and because of impending cardiogenic shock, he was intubated and placed on inotropic support. Due to an inability to be weaned from inotropic support, he was listed for cardiac transplantation at 10 months old. He underwent orthotopic heart transplantation (OHT) at 14 months of age. After transplantation, the proband had seven admissions over the following year for infectious-related indications—some secondary to immunosuppression-induced leukopenia. He required physical therapy for approximately one year to build endurance and optimize motor milestone attainment.

His family history was significant for a sister who died about one decade prior at one year of age within hours of presenting to an emergency room with cardiomegaly (Fig. 1A). The proband’s parents and three other older siblings were healthy. Parents were from a small town in Mexico, and parental consanguinity status was unknown. On physical exam at nine months of age, there was no evidence of dysmorphic features or skeletal myopathy. Subsequent follow up over five years after transplant revealed normal strength and normal development.

Etiologic work-up for the patient’s dilated cardiomyopathy due to acquired causes showed evidence of CMV infection (Table S1). Metabolic testing, including plasma amino acids, urine organic acids, pyruvic acid, and acylcarnitine profile, was unremarkable. Creatine kinase (CK) was also normal. Gross inspection of the explanted heart showed a moderately dilated right ventricle with 0.2 cm wall thickness while the left ventricle was more dilated than the right—moderately to markedly dilated with 0.5 cm to 0.6 cm wall thickness (Fig. 2A). Microscopic examination with H&E staining of both ventricles showed moderately hypertrophied myocytes with focal areas of mild interstitial fibrosis but no significant endocardial fibrosis or myocardial disarray. Electron microscopy showed multifocal mild degenerative changes of the sarcomeres with Z-band streaming. (Fig. 2B, 2C & 2D). Actin filament lengths could not be clearly assessed. SNP array did not show any copy number changes but did show absence of heterozygosity (AOH, regions greater than 5 Mb) in chromosomes 3, 7, and 14 of approximately 28 Mb in total, suggesting identity by descent. Mitochondrial DNA next generation sequencing did not reveal any pathogenic variants (Table S1). Trio exome sequencing revealed a novel homozygous frameshift variant, c.1243_1244delCT (p.L415Vfs*108) in LMOD2 located within the region of AOH on chromosome 7 (Fig. S2A & B). This frameshift variant is classified as pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) standards and guidelines for the interpretation of sequence variants (Richards et al., 2015). The allele frequency in population databases such as gnomAD is low at 0.0008% without any homozygotes observed. Each parent was found to be heterozygous for the variant by Sanger sequencing with no known cardiac dysfunction (Fig. 1B). Further molecular evaluation by Sanger sequencing of the unaffected living siblings showed that two older siblings were heterozygous for the LMOD2 variant, and one sibling had normal sequencing results. Evaluation by ECG and echocardiogram have not been performed on parents or carrier siblings. The deceased sibling could not be tested for the variant due to unavailability of any DNA samples.

To our knowledge, this is the fourth case of an infant with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) due to biallelic variants in LMOD2. The first identified patient with a homozygous nonsense variant (c.1193G > A, p.Trp398*) in LMOD2 presented with neonatal DCM and ejection fraction (EF) of 14%, requiring OHT at 10 months of age (Ahrens-Nicklas et al., 2019). A recent publication describes a family of two female non-twin neonates with compound heterozygous variants in LMOD2 presenting with cardiomyopathy after delivery (lowest EF of 15% in older sister and 22% in younger sister), and both died at or before one month of age (Greenway et al., 2021). The variants found were c.1537C>T (p.R513*) and c.1243_1244CTdel (p.L415Vfs*108), which is the same variant observed in our patient. Our case further supports pathogenicity of the c.1243_1244CTdel variant. Similar to the parents of previous cases who were heterozygous for a LMOD2 variant, the first degree carrier relatives of our patient were clinically asymptomatic. None of the carrier family members of our proband have had echocardiogram to rule out less severe cardiac phenotypes. The c.1243_1244CTdel variant is at very low frequencies in control population databases (less than 0.001%) and not observed in a homozygous state. Our case provides further evidence that biallelic loss of function variants in LMOD2 lead to dilated cardiomyopathy in humans.

Difference in histological findings between our patient and previous cases may correlate with the differences in disease onset and severity between the four cases (Table S2). Our case had a period of some cardiac function recovery with mild interstitial fibrosis and without myofibril disarray at time of transplant while marked fibrosis was seen in at least two of the three published cases. Like the first published case, our patient did not have signs of generalized myopathy or skeletal involvement and had normal CK levels. He also had typical development before and after OHT. Our case was diagnosed with intestinal malrotation, which is not reported in the previous studies. Future ascertainment of additional cases will elucidate the full spectrum of extracardiac effects, if any, of LMOD2-related cardiomyopathy.

Considering this is the fourth known case of LMOD2-related cardiomyopathy, cases of dilated cardiomyopathy due to LMOD2 biallelic variants are likely underdiagnosed with a limited number of clinically available cardiomyopathy gene panels that include LMOD2. Though this patient’s DCM was severe, his age of presentation may indicate varying age of onset of symptoms—not just within the neonatal period. LMOD2-associated DCM should be a consideration for familial DCM that shows an autosomal recessive pattern since most other forms of DCM show autosomal dominant inheritance. Further identification of LMOD2 variants associated with DCM may provide insight on genotype-phenotype correlation, accurate recurrence risk to families, and may impact management, including candidacy for orthotopic heart transplant.
