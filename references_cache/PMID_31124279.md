# Genetic variants in the KDM6B gene are associated with neurodevelopmental delays and dysmorphic features.
**Authors:** Stolerman ES, Francisco E, Stallworth JL, Jones JR, Monaghan KG, Keller-Ramey J, Person R, Wentzensen IM, McWalter K, Keren B, Heron B, Nava C, Heron D, Kim K, Burton B, Al-Musafri F, O'Grady L, Sahai I, Escobar LF, Meuwissen M, Reyniers E, Kooy F, Lacassie Y, Gunay-Aygun M, Schatz KS, Hochstenbach R, Zwijnenburg PJG, Waisfisz Q, van Slegtenhorst M, Mancini GMS, Louie RJ

## Content

Lysine-specific demethylase 6B (KDM6B) demethylates trimethylated lysine-27 on histone H3. The methylation and demethylation of histone proteins affects gene expression during development. Pathogenic alterations in histone lysine methylation and demethylation genes have been associated with multiple neurodevelopmental disorders. We have identified a number of de novo alterations in the KDM6B gene via whole exome sequencing (WES) in a cohort of 12 unrelated patients with developmental delay, intellectual disability, dysmorphic facial features, and other clinical findings. Our findings will allow for further investigation in to the role of the KDM6B gene in human neurodevelopmental disorders.

Abstract
Lysine-specific demethylase 6B ( KDM6B) demethylates trimethylated lysine-27 on
histone H3. The methylation and demethylation of histone proteins affects gene
expression during development. Pathogenic alterations in histone lysine methylation
and demethylation genes have been associated with multiple neurodevelopmental
disorders. We have identified a number of de novo alterations in the KDM6B gene via
whole exome sequencing (WES) in a cohort of 12 unrelated patients with develop-
mental delay, intellectual disability, dysmorphic facial features, and other clinical find-
ings. Our findings will allow for further investigation in to the role of the KDM6B
gene in human neurodevelopmental disorders.

1 | INTRODUCTION
Neurodevelopmental disorders are a heterogeneous group of conditions
usually diagnosed in early childhood. The molecular, cellular, neurophysio-
logic, and environmental factors involved in neurodevelopmental disorders
are varied and complex. Neurodevelopmental disorders are characterized
by motor, speech, cognitive, and social developmental deficits that pro-
duce impairments of personal, social, academic or occupational function-
ing (“Neurodevelopmental Disorders”).
Post-translational alterations of histone proteins are known to be
involved in the regulation of gene expression (Kim, Lee, Lee, Lee, & Cho,
2017). A variety of different histone methyltransferases, histone methyl-
ases, and methylated histone binding proteins have been identified (Kim
et al., 2017). The methylation and dem ethylation of histone proteins at
specific residues regulates gene expression during developmental pro-
cesses (Swigut & Wysocka, 2007). H istone methylation can occur on
arginine or lysine residues (Xiang et al., 2007). Lysine methyltransferases
(KMTs) and lysine demethylases (KDMs) are involved in the regulation of
lysine methylation of histone tails by the addition or removal of methyl
groups (Backe et al., 2018). A number of pathogenic alterations in his-
tone lysine methylation-related genesare associated with intellectual dis-
ability, many of which have been recently identified by exome
sequencing (Faundes et al., 2018; Ki m et al., 2017). Well known neu-
rodevelopmental disorders caused bymutations in histone methylation-
related genes include Kabuki syndrome 1 ( KMT2D, OMIM#147920; Ng
et al., 2010), Kabuki syndrome 2 ( KDM6A, OMIM#300867; Lederer
et al., 2012), cleft palate, psychomotor retardation, and distinctive facial
features syndrome (CPRF; KDM1A, OMIM#616728; Tunovic, 2014),
Kleefstra syndrome ( EHMT1, OMIM#610253; Kleefstra et al., 2005),
Sotos syndrome (NSD1, OMIM#117550; Kurotaki et al., 2002), Weaver
syndrome ( EZH2, OMIM#277590; Tatton-Brown et al., 2011),
Wiedemann-Steiner syndrome ( KMT2A, OMIM#605130; Jones
et al., 2012), Autosomal dominant mental retardation 51 ( KMT5B,
OMIM#617788), Autosomal dominant mental retardation 52 ( ASH1L,
OMIM#617796), and Autosomal recessive mental retardation 65
(KDM5B, OMIM#618109; Faundes et al., 2018). KMT2B mutations
cause childhood onset dystronia (OMIM #617284) (Meyer et al., 2016;
Zech et al., 2016), and KMT2C mutations cause Kleefstra syndrome
Type 2 (OMIM #617768; Faundes et al., 2018; Koemans et al., 2017).
KDM6B, located at 17p13.1, (previously named JMJD3)i sah i s t o n e
H3K27 demethylase that specifically demethylates di- or tri-methylated
lysine 27 of histone H3 (Lan et al., 2007; Xiang et al., 2007). The KDM6B
protein consists of the JmjC catalytic domain and C-terminal helix and
zinc motifs which are important in cofactor binding and protein stability
(Kruidenier et al., 2012). Najmabadi et al. identified homozygosity for a
C-to-T transition in theKDM6B gene, resulting in a proline to serine sub-
stitution at amino acid 888 (p.Pro888Ser) in an individual with intellectual
disability, facial dysmorphisms, sy ndactyly, and short toes (Najmabadi
et al., 2011). However, a systematic case series of patients with variants
in the KDM6B gene has not published to date.
In this study, we report a case series of 12 patients presenting
with neurodevelopmental delays in speech and motor development,
dysmorphic facial features including a prominent nasal bridge or nose,
coarse features, as well as widened hands and syndactyly with de
novo variants in the KDM6B gene. These changes were detected by
exome sequencing and were subsequently classified as variants of
uncertain significance (VUS) due to their novel nature and an unclear
link between alterations in the KDM6B gene and neurodevelopmental
delay. We propose that the phenotypic and genotypic findings in
these patients that involve genetic variants in the KDM6B gene repre-
sent a novel neurodevelopmental disorder.
2 | MATERIALS AND METHODS
This cohort of patients was collected through the use of the website Gene
Matcher (Sobreira, Schiettecatte, Valle, & Hamosh, 2015). Patients with
variants in the KDM6B gene that were typically found through exome
sequencing were included through international collaboration. History and
phenotypic information was obtained from the patient's medical records,
patient's photographs with family consent, and correspondences with the
collaborating clinicians. Informed consent was obtained for photographs
when appropriate. All procedures employed were reviewed and approved
by the appropriate institutional review committee.
Standard laboratory conditions were used for exome analysis and
were performed in diagnostic or research laboratories. In general, all ana-
lyses followed a similar work flow f or DNA sequencing, bioinformatic
analysis, and variant interpretation.All molecular findings were reported
using transcript NM_001080424.1 and confirmed by Sanger sequencing.
The variants in the KDM6B gene that are described in this report are
publically available in the ClinVar database under submission name
SUB4879206. Specific detailed meth odologies and reagents used for
each sample are described in an appendices Supporting Information.

4 | DISCUSSION
In this case report, we describe 12 patients with variants in the
KDM6B gene that presented with variable neurodevelopmental delays
as well as dysmorphic facial features. All patients demonstrated delays
in speech and motor development often requiring specific therapies.
Neuromuscular hypotonia was also often present. There was usually
some degree of intellectual disability evident in the older patients
who had been evaluated although this was variable in severity. In
addition, some patients also met the criteria for a diagnosis of autism
spectrum disorder.

Dysmorphic facial features that were reported included coarse
features, a prominent forehead, broad mouth, large and prominent
ears, a round face, prognathism, and epicanthal folds. Musculoskeletal
features that were present in some but not all cases included some-

that were reported were hypoplastic nipples, pectus excavatum,
laryngomalacia, and cryptorchidism.
Histone methylation and demethylation is involved in heterochro-
matin formation and the regulation of promoter activity (Greer & Shi,
2012). Histone methylation is known to influence biological processes
particularly in development and cellular responses (Greer & Shi, 2012).
A number of pathogenic alterations in histone lysine methylation-
related genes have been identified as being associated with intellec-
tual disability and neurodevelopmental disorders (Kim et al., 2017).
For example, Kabuki syndrome 1 (OMIM 147920) is associated with
alterations in KMT2D which is a lysine specific methyltransferase (Kim
et al., 2017). Kabuki syndrome 2 (OMIM 300867) is associated with
alterations in KDM6A. Overlapping phenotypic features between
KDM6A and KDM6B include prominent ears, abnormal dentition, con-
genital heart disease, feeding difficulties, cryptorchidism, joint hyper-
mobility, developmental delay, hypotonia, and behavioral difficulties.
Agger et al. showed that lysine specific demethylase 6A UTX (alter-
natively known as KDM6A)a n d JMJD3 (the alternative name for
KDM6B) demethylate tri-methylated lysine 27 on histone H3 (Agger
et al., 2007). They suggested that H3K27me3 demethylation regulated
by UTX/JMJD3 proteins is essential for proper development (Agger
et al., 2007). Shpargel et al. demonstrated that male mouse embryos
devoid of all KDM6 demethylation remarkably survive to term and
appear phenotypically normal at mid-gestation (Shpargel, Starmer, Yee,
Pohlers, & Magnuson, 2014). Naruse et al found that in mice,
demethylase activity of Jmjd3, but not Utx, affects mouse axial pattern-
ing (Naruse et al., 2017).
The alterations in the KDM6B gene in 10 of the 12 patients
described in this cohort are predicted to cause loss of protein function.
Additionally, querying of the DatabasE of genomiC varIation and Phe-
notype in Humans using Ensembl Resources (DECIPHER)_database
identified seven submissions of de novo deletions involving the KDM6B
gene, and overlapping clinical features include developmental delay,
intellectual disability, and speech delay. However, it is important to note
that all seven of these deletions also encompass neighboring genes the
effects of which are uncertain. Finally, the observed/ expected (o/e)
constraint score generated by gnomAD is 0.06 with a 90% confidence
interval of 0.03 –0.14 indicating that haploinsufficiency of the KDM6B
gene is not tolerated (Lek et al., 2016).
Two patients of the 12 individuals in this cohort have de novo mis-
sense alterations in the KDM6B gene. Although the gene constraint
metrics indicate that missense alterations are tolerated in this gene
(missense o/e = 0.86 with confidence interval of 0.81–0.91), these indi-
vidual's missense alterations are located in the JmjC catalytic domain of
the protein, and located near residues involved in peptide and
cofactor binding, which suggests that these missense alterations
may be perturbing important electrostatic interactions. Further
molecular studies are required to understand the function effect of
all 12 alterations, however, these data suggest that the molecular
mechanism of disease is by loss of protein function, and not domi-
nant negative or gain of protein function. As for the rate of de novo
alterations in the KDM6B gene, the GeneDx database was queried
and of the 10,619 exomes tested through April of 2017 for which
intellectual disability and/or developmental delay were ordering
indications, 12 patients, or approximately 0.12%, have de novo
alterations in the KDM6B gene. This finding indicates that this gene
may be a small but significant contributor to neurodevelopmental
delay. Previous publications have associated variants in KDM6B
with autism and or developmental delay but did not have detailed
phenotypic information presen ted (Yuen et al., 2017; Stessman
et al., 2017).
In summary, we describe 12 patients with de novo variants in the
KDM6B gene who have variable neurodevelopmental delays and dys-
morphic features. Further functional in vitro and in vivo studies are
required to fully validate the gene-disease association for the KDM6B
gene (Strande et al., 2017).
