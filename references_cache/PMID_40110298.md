INTRODUCTION: Scimitar syndrome, or congenital venolobar syndrome, is a rare 
congenital heart condition that typically involves partial anomalous pulmonary 
venous drainage (PAPVD) of the right lung, right lung hypoplasia, and 
dextroposition of the heart. The incidence is estimated to be between 1 and 3 
cases per 100 000 live births. Most patients present with cardiopulmonary 
symptoms, though rare cases may involve gastrointestinal (GI) complications. We 
report a unique case of Scimitar syndrome in a 10-year-old boy presenting with 
hematemesis due to esophageal varices.
CASE PRESENTATION: A 10-year-old boy presented with massive hematemesis, losing 
approximately 1 liter of fresh blood. After stabilization, an upper GI endoscopy 
revealed grade IV esophageal varices, which were ligated successfully. Abdominal 
ultrasound showed mild portal hypertension, and a unique vessel, identified as 
the Scimitar vein, was found draining into the left portal vein. Subsequent 
imaging confirmed right lung hypoplasia, dextroposition of the heart, and PAPVD, 
leading to the diagnosis of Scimitar syndrome. The patient was transferred 
abroad for specialized care due to limited medical facilities in Sudan.
CLINICAL DISCUSSION: Scimitar syndrome is often associated with severe symptoms 
in children, such as respiratory failure and pulmonary hypertension. Upper GI 
bleeding in this syndrome is rare, likely resulting from portal hypertension 
caused by the anomalous drainage of the Scimitar vein into the left portal vein.
CONCLUSION: This case underscores the importance of recognizing the diverse 
complications of Scimitar syndrome, including its rare association with upper GI 
bleeding. Timely diagnosis and intervention, especially in resource-limited 
settings, are critical to managing complex congenital conditions.

Variceal bleeding occurs due to elevated portal pressure exceeding 12 mm Hg, leading to dilation of portosystemic collaterals, increased variceal wall tension, and rupture, driven by factors like vessel radius, wall thickness, and pressure gradients.[4].

Here, we present the case of a 10-year-old boy who was diagnosed with Scimitar syndrome following a unique presentation of hematemesis due to esophageal varices.

A 10-year-old boy with a BMI of a height of 137 cm and a weight of 24 kg presented for follow-up after an episode of massive hematemesis, involving approximately 1 liter of fresh blood with clots that developed over 1 day. The episode was managed, and he was discharged in good condition. At follow-up, the patient denied experiencing shortness of breath, orthopnea, wheezing, or any history of known cardiopulmonary or gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms. He had a history of recurrent chest infections, which had been diagnosed as allergies, for which he received oral antibiotics and bronchodilators. Additionally, the patient had undergone orchidopexy at 6 years of age for an undescended testicle, with no complications reported post-operatively. There was no family history of congenital heart disease or vascular anomalies.

On examination, the patient’s vital signs were stable, though he appeared pale. Physical examination was unremarkable. Laboratory results showed white blood cells at 10.5 × 109 cells/L, hemoglobin at 7.9 g/dL, and a platelet count of 290 × 109/L. The mean corpuscular volume was 79.1 fL, and the mean corpuscular hemoglobin was 26.7 pg. The patient’s liver function tests are as follows: alkaline phosphatase at 219 IU/L, alanine aminotransferase at 16.4 IU/L, aspartate aminotransferase at 55.7 IU/L, total protein at 7.01 g/dL, serum albumin at 4.1 g/dL, total serum bilirubin at 0.68 mg/dL, and direct serum bilirubin at 0.2 mg/dL. The International Normalized Ratio is 1.17, and the platelet count is 290 000/µL, both within normal ranges. There is no clinical or radiological evidence of ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, portal hypertension, or any other signs of liver decompensation.

Due to the recent hematemesis and abnormal laboratory findings, an upper GI endoscopy was performed, revealing six columns of grade IV esophageal varices. A total of 10 bands were applied for ligation, successfully achieving hemostasis. Mild portal hypertensive gastropathy was observed in the stomach, but there were no gastric varices or signs of active bleeding. The duodenum was unremarkable.

An abdominal ultrasound revealed a slightly enlarged liver with a coarse texture. The inferior vena cava, hepatic vein, and portal vein were all patent, and centrifugal flow within the portal vein (diameter = 8 mm) suggested portal hypertension. No periportal fibrosis was detected. A unique vessel, identified as the Scimitar vein, was observed draining into the left main portal vein near its junction with the main portal vein. This finding was consistent with PAPVD. The spleen was also slightly enlarged.

A pediatric cardiology department was consulted based on these findings. Chest X-ray revealed apparent dextrocardia with right lung hypoplasia, enlargement of the right heart chambers, and a curvilinear opacity extending below the right hemidiaphragm (Fig. 1). Echocardiography confirmed a hypoplastic right pulmonary artery measuring 7 mm in diameter compared to the left pulmonary artery at 16 mm. Based on the imaging findings, a diagnosis of Scimitar syndrome was established. Due to the lack of advanced medical facilities in Sudan, the patient was transferred abroad for further assessment and management.

Scimitar syndrome is a rare and complex type of anomalous pulmonary venous return, first described in 1836 by Cooper and Chassinat[5]. The precise mechanism underlying the development of Scimitar syndrome remains unclear, though it is thought to arise from an embryological error during lung bud formation in early development[2]. Patients with Scimitar syndrome often present with a variety of congenital anomalies, including congenital heart defects, pulmonary abnormalities, and genitourinary malformations[6-8]. In our case, the patient had a history of an undescended testicle, which was identified early and surgically corrected.

Scimitar syndrome is associated with a poorer prognosis when identified in children, as it is more likely to cause severe symptoms such as respiratory failure, cyanosis, or recurrent respiratory infections. Heart failure and pulmonary hypertension are common complications, especially if aortopulmonary collaterals are present in the sequestered lung. In contrast, Scimitar syndrome in adults typically presents with milder symptoms, such as progressive dyspnea, and is often discovered incidentally during imaging studies[9].

Upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB) is a rare but potentially fatal condition, with an incidence of 6.4% and a mortality rate that can reach up to 15%[10]. In Sudan, where schistosomiasis is a common cause of hematemesis[11], the patient’s condition was initially misdiagnosed as schistosomiasis. The abnormal collateral vessels in Scimitar syndrome usually cause hemorrhagic manifestations like hemoptysis[12]. However, variceal bleeding, as seen in this patient, is exceptionally rare and likely resulted from portal hypertension due to the Scimitar vein draining into the left main portal vein. This case is particularly noteworthy because of this rare complication. An aberrant right subclavian artery-esophageal fistula was also found to be a cause of UGIB[13].

Several imaging modalities, including CT, magnetic resonance imaging, and echocardiography, are essential for diagnosing Scimitar syndrome. In resource-limited settings, a chest X-ray may suffice if the characteristic “scimitar sign” is present[14].

Management generally involves surgical intervention in symptomatic patients, those with aberrant stenosis of the right pulmonary venous trunk, significant volume overload of the right heart chambers, or the presence of an atrial septal defect associated with the Scimitar vein[9,15]. Due to limited medical resources, our patient was transferred abroad for further specialized care.

In conclusion, this case highlights the rare presentation of Scimitar syndrome with UGIB caused by esophageal varices due to portal hypertension. The complex nature of this congenital condition, combined with its unusual complications, underscores the importance of accurate diagnosis and timely intervention. Given the resource limitations in some regions, early identification and transfer of patients to specialized centers for further management are crucial for improving outcomes in cases like this.
