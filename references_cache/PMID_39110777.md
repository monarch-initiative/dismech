The recent emergence and rapid response to severe acute respiratory syndrome
coronavirus 2 was enabled by prototype pathogen and vaccine platform approaches,
driven by the preemptive application of RNA vaccine technology to the related
Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus. Recently, the National Institutes
of Allergy and Infectious Diseases identified nine virus families of concern,
eight enveloped virus families and one nonenveloped virus family, for which
vaccine generation is a priority. Although RNA vaccines have been described for
a variety of enveloped viruses, a roadmap for their use against nonenveloped
viruses is lacking. Enterovirus D68 was recently designated a prototype pathogen
within the family Picornaviridae of nonenveloped viruses because of its rapid
evolution and respiratory route of transmission, coupled with a lack of diverse
anti-enterovirus vaccine approaches in development. Here, we describe a
proof-of-concept approach using a clinical stage RNA vaccine platform that
induced robust enterovirus D68-neutralizing antibody responses in mice and
nonhuman primates and prevented upper and lower respiratory tract infections and
neurological disease in mice. In addition, we used our platform to rapidly
characterize the antigenic diversity within the six genotypes of enterovirus
D68, providing the necessary data to inform multivalent vaccine compositions
that can elicit optimal breadth of neutralizing responses. These results
demonstrate that RNA vaccines can be used as tools in our pandemic-preparedness
toolbox for nonenveloped viruses.

Every year in the United States, enteroviruses cause 10 million to 15 million symptomatic infections, particularly in young children and neonates (1, 2). Symptoms range from mild febrile illness to endocarditis, hepatitis, meningitis, and acute flaccid myelitis (AFM). Enterovirus D68 (EV-D68) species/serotype, a virus within the Enterovirus genus that is transmitted through respiratory droplets, is becoming increasingly associated with AFM (2–5). This paralytic syndrome has been documented in more than 746 children in the United States since 2014, and mounting evidence, including the presence of virus in the central nervous system as well as viral RNA and antibodies in cerebral spinal fluid, points to EV-D68 as the main causative agent (5, 6). The Enterovirus genus, within the family Picornaviridae, includes others such as poliovirus, coxsackievirus, enterovirus A71, and rhinovirus, which, along with EV-D68, are all small nonenveloped positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses encoding a single open reading frame comprising both structural and nonstructural polyproteins. The former polyprotein, termed P1, is cleaved by host and viral proteases, including the 3CD nonstructural protein, to form the capsid subunits VP1, VP2, VP3, and VP4. Extensive studies on the evolution of this family of viruses have revealed a remarkable capacity for rapid mutation and niche adaptation because of the presence of a highly error-prone RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, encoded in their genome. Given (i) the alarming increase in EV-D68–associated AFM in young children, (ii) the propensity for these viruses to change antigenically (7, 8), and (iii) the potential role of antigenic drift combined with waning population immunity (9, 10) on the biennial pattern of AFM emergence, there is an urgent need to develop effective vaccine platform approaches that can be rapidly adapted to existing and emerging enteroviruses.

Now, there are two major approaches being pursued in the development of vaccines against EV-D68 infection: recombinant virus-like particles (VLPs) produced in yeast (11), insect cells (12), or mammalian cells (13) and an inactivated virus vaccine, the latter entering clinical development this year in the Netherlands (14). However, variants of EV-D68 may emerge to escape immunity provided by such vaccines, and the timeline to producing an updated product using these approaches is on the order of years. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the potential of mRNA vaccine technologies to revolutionize vaccine development. We witnessed this as the development, evaluation, and licensure of two mRNA vaccines against COVID-19 that occurred in less than 1 year. Alternative approaches such as these are needed in the EV-D68 vaccine pipeline. In addition, given some of the shortcomings associated with first-generation mRNA vaccines, including poor transmission-blocking characteristics, second-generation approaches that can limit virus replication in upper respiratory tracts are needed to limit spread of respiratory viruses. To date, nucleic acid vaccine approaches for nonenveloped viruses, including enteroviruses, have focused on the expression of capsid subunits, including VP1, from DNA vaccine platforms (15–17). Recombinant protein approaches have established that coexpression of enterovirus P1 and 3CD polyproteins in yeast (11, 18–20) or insect cells (12, 21–23) can yield recombinant VLPs and that these antigens induce neutralizing antibody responses in animals. Subsequently, it was demonstrated that P1–3CD coexpression in vivo, achieved by an adenoviral vector, could similarly achieve neutralizing responses in mice (24). Recently, an alternative, 3CD-independent approach was described, whereby a ribosomal skipping motif was inserted between capsid subunits of the P1 polyprotein, successfully yielding VLPs in vitro; however, the capacity for these VLPs to induce neutralizing antibody responses in vivo was not reported (24). Here, we build on these findings to develop a self-amplifying replicon RNA (repRNA) vaccine encoding EV-D68 antigens delivered by our recently described formulation, called LION (25–30), a cationic nanoemulsion distinct from lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) that complexes with the nucleic acid payload at the nanoparticle surface. We additionally use our platform to characterize the antigenic relationships between EV-D68 genotypes.

We designed a series of repRNA constructs on the basis of the B1 subclade virus, US/MO/14–18947, to optimize neutralizing antibody responses in mice. First, to optimize coexpression of the P1 and 3CD polyproteins, we compared the encephalomyocarditis virus internal ribosomal entry site (IRES) to a ribosomal skipping peptide sequence derived from Thosea asigna virus (T2A) to drive the production of 3CD downstream of P1. We identified that IRES-mediated, rather than T2A-mediated, coexpression of P1 and 3CD resulted in more efficient processing of P1 into its subunits and yielded better responses in vivo (fig. S1). Next, we designed additional constructs to determine whether 3CD coexpression was required to induce neutralizing responses in mice. To do so, we compared constructs encoding (i) the optimized P1IRES-3CD that coexpresses P1 and 3CD, (ii) P1 alone (P1Δ3CD), (iii) P1 with each capsid subunit separated by the T2A ribosomal skipping peptide sequence (P1T2A), and (iv) VP1 fused to the HA2 domain of influenza hemagglutinin (HA) to enable secretion and tethering of VP1 to the cell surface (VP1HA2) (Fig. 1A). To confirm the differential processing of VP1 from each of these constructs in vitro, baby hamster kidney (BHK) cells were transfected with each repRNA, and cell lysates were analyzed by anti-VP1 Western blot the next day. P1IRES-3CD resulted in the anticipated molecular weight of the VP1 subunit (~34 kDa) (Fig. 1B). The other three constructs resulted in differential VP1 migration, likely because of lack of subunit cleavage in P1Δ3CD, glycosylation and fusion to HA2 in VP1HA2, and residual N-terminal T2A residues in P1T2A.

Next, we asked whether the correct processing of P1 by 3CD is necessary for inducing protective antibody responses in immunized mice. Female C57BL/6 mice, 6 to 8 weeks old, were immunized with 1 μg of each of the LION-formulated repRNA vaccines (Fig. 1A). Mice were immunized intramuscularly in the hind leg on day 0, bled, and boosted on day 28, followed by euthanasia and blood collection on day 42 (Fig. 1C). Serum samples collected on day 42 were tested for neutralizing activity using a cell impedance–based real-time cell analysis (RTCA) assay against homologous EV-D68 US/MO/14–18947 (Fig. 1D). Whereas P1Δ3CD, P1T2A, and VP1HA2 repRNA vaccines (not containing 3CD) were unable to induce neutralizing antibody responses in mice, the 3CD-containing P1IRES-3CD repRNA induced a geometric mean 50% neutralizing antibody titer (NT50) of 1:430 against homologous virus (Fig. 1E). Last, to determine whether the immunity induced by these four repRNA vaccines could provide protection from respiratory infection, the same groups of mice were challenged intranasally with 2 × 107 plaque-forming units (PFU) of a mouse-adapted version of the B1 subclade virus, US/MO/14–18949. Lungs were harvested 24 hours later, homogenized, and assayed for live virus by plaque assay (Fig. 1F). Correlating with the NT50 results, only the P1IRES-3CD repRNA vaccine induced sufficient protective immunity to prevent detection of any live virus, whereas ~1 × 106 PFU/gram of lung tissue was detected in the other three groups, similar to values observed in the unvaccinated control group. From these studies, we concluded that coexpression of 3CD is required to drive protective immunity in mice when delivered by LION-formulated repRNA.

Although the genotypic relationships between EV-D68 strains have been extensively characterized (7, 8, 31, 32), little is known about the antigenic relationships or serotype classifications of EV-D68 genotypes. To remedy this, we sought to exploit the flexibility of the repRNA technology and produced a library of subclade-targeted repRNAs that would allow us to rapidly generate antiserum for mapping the antigenic relationships among EV-D68 genotypes. Having determined that the P1IRES-3CD strategy was sufficient for induction of neutralizing antibody responses in mice, likely through in vivo production of VLPs displaying native EV-D68 antigenic features, we further expanded our vaccine candidates to include representative P1IRES-3CD amino acid sequences of the five remaining subclades of EV-D68. One isolate from each subclade was chosen for repRNA sequences, as well as a corresponding live virus isolate available through the Biodefense and Emerging Infections Research Resources Repository (BEI Resources) (Table 1 and fig. S2). Whereas vaccine and virus isolate were 100% matched in P1–3CD amino acid identity for the contemporary subclades A2, B1, B2, and B3, we selected slightly mismatched vaccine and virus isolates for the A1 (two amino acid differences between P1 polyproteins) and C (five amino acid differences between P1 polyproteins) subclade viruses to evaluate the importance of P1–3CD identity within genotypes in defining antigenic subtypes (fig. S2A). To confirm that adequate proteolytic processing of VP1 occurred for each subclade-targeted repRNA, anti-VP1 Western blot was performed on BHK cell lysates 24 hours after transfection, alongside a P1Δ3CD-negative control (fig. S2B). Although staining of the A2-repRNA was diminished relative to the other groups, the anticipated molecular weight of VP1 was observed for all six candidates. Next, C57BL/6 mice (n = 5 per group) were immunized on the same schedule and method as preceding studies (Fig. 1C) with 1 μg of LION/repRNA vaccine, and serum was harvested on day 42 for evaluation of homologous and heterologous neutralization against A1, A2, B1, mouse-adapted B1 (B1MA), B2, B3, and C viruses by RTCA neutralization assay (Fig. 2A). Each of the subclade-targeted repRNA vaccines exhibited varying degrees of immunogenicity and a capacity to neutralize heterologous EV-D68 viruses. In terms of relative immunogenicity, the A2-specific repRNA vaccine was poorly immunogenic, eliciting a geometric mean NT50 of 1:61 against homologous virus and peak NT50 of 1:207 against the heterologous B3 virus isolate. This observed poor immunogenicity correlated with relatively poor VP1 expression observed by Western blot (fig. S2B). In contrast, the remaining repRNA vaccines elicited greater than 1:400 NT50 against at least one virus, with B1 and B2 repRNAs eliciting the highest NT50 titers against homologous viruses with geometric mean NT50 values of 1:1339 and 1:2048, respectively. In terms of breadth of neutralizing antibody responses, the B1-repRNA vaccine was best against contemporary strains, eliciting geometric mean NT50 values greater than 1:700 against A2, B1, B2, and B3 viruses and 1:140 and 1:134 against A1 and C, respectively. The B2-repRNA vaccine was similarly immunogenic against heterologous strains, except for the A2 strain, eliciting a geometric mean NT50 of 1:66 against A2 virus. The A1, B3, and C repRNA vaccines appeared to elicit more subclade-specific neutralizing antibody responses, with the highest NT50 responses observed against C (1:1549 mean NT50), B3 (1:550 mean NT50), and A1 (1:476 mean NT50), respectively. For the immunogenic vaccines that were 100% matched to virus isolate (B1, B2, and B3), robust homologous neutralization was observed. In contrast, immunogenic vaccines that were mismatched to virus isolate (A1 and C) did not elicit robust homologous neutralization. A1 repRNA, which differed from A1 virus by two amino acid substitutions, elicited a geometric mean NT50 of 1:44 against A1 virus, with a peak NT50 of 1:1550 against C virus. Similarly, C repRNA, which differed from C virus by five amino acid substitutions, elicited a geometric mean NT50 of 1:48 against C virus, with a peak NT50 of 1:476 against A1 virus. To determine the plausibility that such minimal P1 amino acid divergence between repRNA and virus could be responsible for the observed lack of robust homologous neutralization, we mapped the amino acid differences between vaccine and virus onto the structure of the EV-D68 capsid (fig. S3). Two of the five amino acid differences between C repRNA and C virus, T698A and K834R, are in the established antigenic epitopes known as the DE loop and VP1 C terminus (33, 34), respectively. However, the remaining five changes between C or A1 repRNAs and their matched viruses (S346N, V416A, G551R, D741E, and I759N) are located in sites yet to be described as antigenic epitopes in EV-D68. Of these five previously uncharacterized sites, VP3 G551R, like VP1 K834R, appears in a position that could interfere with binding of the potently neutralizing, clade–cross-reactive human monoclonal antibody EV68-228 (35) to VP1, VP2, and VP3 (fig. S3).

Overall, these data show that, within the B clade, minimal escape was observed between viruses despite a wide range in amino acid differences among B1, B2, and B3 subclades (13 to 20 amino acid differences; fig. S2, C and D). In contrast, for A1 and C clades, minimal differences (two and five amino acid differences, respectively) resulted in large variations in neutralizing responses.

To test whether repRNA/LION vaccines could protect mice against EV-D68 infection of the upper and lower respiratory tract, a subset of EV-D68 vaccines targeting the B clade were evaluated in a series of murine studies. First, using the matched B1-repRNA/B1MA challenge model, we evaluated protection against both upper and lower respiratory tract infection (Fig. 2B). Mice were primed and boosted 28 days apart with 1 μg of B1-repRNA/LION or mock vaccine by the intramuscular route and, 41 days after prime, infected with 3 × 105 PFU of B1MA. Twenty-four hours later, mice were euthanized, and nasal cavities and lungs were harvested for titration of infectious virus by plaque assay. A significant (>2 log10-fold, P < 0.05) reduction in viral load was detected in nasal turbinates between mock- and B1 repRNA–vaccinated mice (Fig. 2C). In addition, no virus was detectable in lungs of B1 repRNA–vaccinated mice (Fig. 2D), consistent with what we previously observed (Fig. 1F). Next, we evaluated durability of protection against homologous and heterologous challenge with B3 and B1MA, respectively, after prime/boost with 1- and 0.1-μ g doses of a B3-repRNA/LION vaccine (Fig. 2E). One hundred eighty-three days after prime, mice were challenged intranasally with 6.9 × 105 PFU of either B3 or B1MA virus. Lungs and nasal cavities were harvested 24 hours later for titration of infectious virus by plaque assay. In nasal turbinates, no differences were detected between groups (Fig. 2F), indicating that increasing the dose to 1 μg does not improve control of virus in the upper airway ~6 months after vaccination, regardless of vaccine composition. Although no differences in lung viral loads were observed between the 1- and 0.1-μg dose groups that received heterologous vaccine relative to challenge virus (Fig. 2F), a significant (P < 0.05) difference was observed between the 1- and 0.1-μg dose groups that received homologous vaccine relative to challenge virus, suggesting a dose- and composition-dependent effect on long-term protective outcomes in the lower airway (Fig. 2G). Together, these data suggest that optimal and long-term protection is dependent on both dose and composition, with higher doses and genotype-matched vaccines affording better protection in both upper and lower airways.

We next evaluated heterologous protection in the context of B1MA virus challenge, comparing protective outcomes between C and A1 vaccination to homologous B1 vaccination. Mice were left untreated or immunized intramuscularly on days 0 and 55 with 2 μg of repRNA/LION vaccine [A1, B1, or C repRNAs, or a repRNA encoding respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) fusion antigen as a negative control] (Fig. 3A). Neutralizing antibody responses to B1MA were measured on days 27 and 95 (Fig. 3B), and, consistent with data in Fig. 2A, only homologous immunization induced a significant (P < 0.05) neutralizing antibody response, with heterologous C immunization inducing low cross-reactive responses and A1 immunization inducing no detectable neutralizing responses. On day 102, animals were infected intranasally with B1MA virus, and, 24 hours later, lungs and nasal cavities were collected for measurement of viral load (Fig. 3C) and host response to infection (Fig. 3, D to G). Consistent with neutralizing antibody responses, only homologous vaccination provided significant (P < 0.05) protection from infection (Fig. 3C). Given these observations combined with limitations on the number of samples that we could evaluate using NanoString, we analyzed three random samples per group for the uninfected naïve B1-, C-, and RSV-vaccinated groups. RNA was extracted from homogenized lung samples collected 1 day after infection, and host response mRNA transcripts were quantified by NanoString (nCounter mouse host response panel). In contrast with the differential ability of B1 or C vaccination to protect against B1MA virus infection of the lung, B1- and C-vaccinated groups were indistinguishable from each other when evaluating the host response to B1MA virus infection (Fig. 3D) with up-regulation of 56 and 57 host response transcripts, respectively, compared with the RSV-vaccinated animals that demonstrated up-regulation of 85 host transcripts in response to B1MA infection of the lung. The identity of the RSV-vaccinated group’s up-regulated host mRNAs was consistent with infiltrating immune cells, including macrophages (Fig. 3E) and neutrophils (Fig. 3F), as determined by a cell abundance score calculated by NanoString nCounter analyses, with both B1- and C-vaccinated groups showing significant (P < 0.05) reductions in macrophage and neutrophil cell abundance scores compared with the RSV-vaccinated group. Again, no differences were observed between the B1 and C vaccine. Last, when comparing the host response to B1MA virus infection of the lung in the RSV vaccine group with the combined responses in the B1- and C-vaccinated groups, we identified nine transcripts that were up-regulated in the RSV vaccine group, including chemokine (C-C motif) receptor 10 (CCR10), interferon-γ 2/3 (IFNL2/3), radical S-adenosyl methionine domain containing 2 (RSAD2), interferon-induced protein with tetratricopeptide repeats 2 (IFIT2), CC ligand 4 (CCL4), MX dynamin-like GTPase 1 (MX1), interleukin-6 (IL6), 2′−5′ oligoadenylate synthetase-like 1 (OASL1), and IFIT1. The normalized expression for all these analytes was up-regulated in the RSV-vaccinated group compared with that in the naïve, B1, and C groups; no differences in expression of these genes were observed between the B1 and C groups (fig. S4). Many of these cytokine, chemokine, and interferon-stimulated genes have been implicated in pathogenesis of respiratory viral diseases (36, 37), including that caused by EV-D68 (38). Collectively, these data suggest that, although heterologous immunization of mice provides incomplete protection from EV-D68 infection of upper and lower respiratory tracts, protection from host inflammatory responses to infection, particularly those responses associated with lung pathology, can be achieved with either homologous or heterologous immunization.

Having determined that long-term (Fig. 2G) and short-term (Fig. 3C) heterologous protection from infection was not observed at lower doses, we next sought to evaluate short-term heterologous protection from infection in a B2 vaccination/B1MA virus challenge model, including a higher vaccine dose, to see whether heterologous protection is dose dependent. In addition, given our previous observations in the context of our COVID-19 vaccine, where we have observed enhanced control of virus in upper airways of infected hamsters (27, 28) and nonhuman primates (NHPs) (39) compared with published studies evaluating LNP-formulated mRNA vaccines (40–42), we included LNP-formulated repRNA groups in this study. This allowed us to directly compare between formulation modalities within the same study and challenge model. C57BL/6 mice were mock vaccinated or primed and boosted 35 days apart with either 1 or 10 μg of B2-repRNA formulated with either LION or LNP. Serum was collected on days 28 and 49 to evaluate postprime and postboost neutralizing antibody responses against homologous virus. Then, 49 days after prime, mice were challenged with 4.2 × 106 PFU of B1MA EV-D68 by the intranasal route and, 24 hours later, euthanized so that lungs and nasal turbinates could be collected for titration of challenge virus by plaque assay (Fig. 4A). In terms of neutralizing antibody responses, whereas LION-formulated repRNA mediated a significant (P < 0.05) increase in postprime responses between 1- and 10-μ g doses, no differences were observed between (i) doses within the LNP-formulated groups, (ii) formulations within each dose, or (iii) postprime and postboost responses within each formulation (Fig. 4B). These data suggest that boosting may be dose dependent, with lower doses benefiting from a second immunization, particularly when LION is used as the formulation. In addition, both LION and LNP can mediate similar serum neutralizing antibody responses to EV-D68. However, in terms of protective outcomes, differences were observed between LION and LNP formulations in the context of the upper airway, with significant (P < 0.05) reductions in nasal turbinate viral loads observed in both the 1- and 10-μg LION-formulated groups compared with those of their respective LNP groups (Fig. 4C). This observation is in line with our COVID-19 vaccine observations in hamsters and NHPs. In the lower airway, all doses and formulations mediated significant (P < 0.05) reductions in lung viral loads relative to mock-vaccinated mice (Fig. 4D). Together, these data suggest that serum neutralizing antibody titers appear to correlate with control of viral loads in the lower airway and that LION appears to offer better control of virus in the upper airway compared with LNP.

To determine whether the antibody response induced by repRNA/LION is sufficient to protect against EV-D68–induced neurological disease outcomes in vivo, we next used a paralytic disease model similar to one previously described (43). First, immune-competent adult C57BL/6 mice were vaccinated with LION-formulated B2 repRNA or a negative control repRNA encoding influenza virus HA (flu). Pooled terminal serum from each group collected 2 weeks after the boost immunization was heat-inactivated and passively transferred to 7-day-old type I interferon receptor knockout (Ifnar1−/−) mice (Fig. 5A) along with a group that received phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). A sample of each of the two serum pools was assayed for anti-B2 neutralizing activity by RTCA (Fig. 5B), indicating a titer induced by B2 vaccination similar to that observed in Fig. 4B and low to no neutralization induced by flu vaccination. One day after passive transfer, mice were challenged intramuscularly with B2 virus (US/IL/14–18952) and monitored for clinical signs of disease. We observed complete protection from mortality (Fig. 5C), weight loss (Fig. 5D), and any neurological deficits (Fig. 5E) in mice that received B2-repRNA/LION serum. However, all control mice that were inoculated with PBS and all but one mouse that received serum from control-repRNA/LION–vaccinated animals succumbed to disease within 8 days of challenge (Fig. 5B). Before death or euthanasia, motor impairment in PBS- and flu-repRNA/LION–treated animals ranged from bilateral flaccid hindlimb paralysis to complete quadriplegia. The only surviving flu-repRNA/LION–treated mouse exhibited substantial motor deficits, characterized by flaccid paralysis in both hindlimbs that persisted throughout the 21-day observation period (Fig. 5F). Therefore, humoral immunity elicited by B2-RepRNA/LION vaccination was effective at preventing flaccid paralysis and mortality in an in vivo model of EV-D68–induced AFM, and this immunity was not elicited by a mock repRNA/LION vaccine.

Given the immunogenicity and breadth of neutralizing antibody response of the B1-repRNA/LION vaccine, as well as the contemporary nature of the B1 subclade of EV-D68, we next evaluated the B1-repRNA/LION vaccine in rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta) with ages ranging from 2 to 12 years old (table S1). Animals were divided, with roughly equal distributions in age and sex, between two groups. On days 0 and 42, NHPs were vaccinated intramuscularly with 25 μg of either B1 repRNA/LION or an irrelevant repRNA/LION encoding a Crimean Congo hemorrhagic fever virus antigen (44). Serum was collected on days 0, 14, 28, 42, 49, and 63 for evaluation of neutralizing antibody responses (Fig. 6A). All six time points were evaluated for kinetics of response against homologous B1 virus (Fig. 6B). In all animals, seroconversion was detected by day 42 after prime, and, after the boost, all NHPs responded with NT50 values ranging from 1:41 to 1:2124.

To determine the breadth of neutralizing antibody responses, the day 63 serum samples were assayed against all six representative viruses by RTCA (Fig. 6C). In contrast with postboost responses in mice, which exhibited a maximum 10-fold decrease in geometric mean NT50 titer between homologous and A1 heterologous virus, only an approximately five fold decrease in geometric mean NT50 values was observed in NHPs, with geometric mean NT50 values against A1, A2, B1, B2, B3, and C viruses of 1:100, 1:362, 1:1501, 1:209, 1:1506, and 1:130, respectively. Given the spread in age between animals, we performed a correlation analysis between age and serum neutralizing antibody response (fig. S5). Although the study was underpowered, no differences were observed (Spearman ρ = 0.06 for B1-, B2-, and B3-neutralizing antibody titer). These data suggest that B1-targeted vaccines can elicit broad neutralizing antibody responses in NHPs. However, we cannot rule out the effect of any preexisting immunity that could not be detected by our neutralization assay on day 0 samples.

To date, advanced development of vaccines for nonenveloped viruses has focused on live-attenuated, inactivated, or recombinant protein–based approaches. Common to all three of these approaches is the successful elicitation of neutralizing antibody responses against conformational epitopes present on the surface of the vaccine virus (live-attenuated or inactivated) or on the surface of VLPs (recombinant protein). Although neutralizing antibody responses have been elicited after vaccination with capsid subunits, such as VP1, and neutralizing monoclonal antibodies detected against linear epitopes, more robust and broadly reactive polyclonal responses are induced by antigens harboring conformational epitopes present on the surface of the virus. To incorporate these findings in the design of a nucleic acid vaccine against the nonenveloped virus EV-D68, we aimed to express VLPs in situ from repRNA.

Enveloped viruses have evolved mechanisms of structural protein production and particle release through their infected host cell’s secretory pathway (45). This simplifies mRNA or DNA vaccine strategies that co-opt a vaccinee’s cell secretory pathway to produce vaccine antigens in situ. All mRNA vaccines now in clinical development against viruses target those that are enveloped (46). In contrast, nonenveloped viruses have evolved alternative pathways for release of infectious virions from infected cells, often requiring cooperativity among viral structural, nonstructural, and host proteins for successful release from infected cells (47). Although it has been established that coexpression of enterovirus structural polyprotein P1 and nonstructural polyprotein 3CD leads to the successful production of virions in vitro (20, 48–50) and protective immune responses in vivo (12, 21–24, 51), the necessity of this coexpression strategy had yet to be tested in the context of mRNA vaccines. We evaluated three alternative strategies, including secretion of VP1 and cytosolic expression of P1 polyprotein or P1 subunits, the latter mediated by ribosomal skipping motifs and recently described to produce VLPs in vitro (52). Although the intended protein products were detected in vitro, we did not observe any neutralizing antibody responses in mice. However, as expected, robust neutralizing responses were observed when P1 was coexpressed with 3CD.

This apparent dependence of repRNA vaccine-mediated protective immune response on 3CD expression in vivo warrants further evaluation of vaccine safety. Recently, for example, derivatives of EV-D68 3CD have been proposed to contribute to pathogenesis of AFM by cleavage of host soluble N-ethylmaleimide–sensitive factor attachment receptors in neurons (53–56), implicating picornavirus protease-host interactions in disease pathogenesis. However, given the established safety profile of the live-attenuated polio vaccine, a virus that encodes 3CD, it is likely that tissue tropism and biodistribution of 3CD-encoding vaccines will contribute to vaccine safety. The oral polio vaccine’s attenuation is due to loss of neurotropism with replication restricted to gut-associated tissues, and vaccine-derived polio disease is linked to reversion of neurotropism (57, 58). Therefore, restricting the biodistribution of mRNA vaccines encoding 3CD will likely contribute to enhanced safety. Although LNP-formulated mRNA vaccines exhibit broad biodistribution patterns after intramuscular injection, including in the brain and heart (59), LION-formulated mRNA vaccines exhibit local muscle tissue retention (60). In addition to reducing unwanted systemic reactogenicity, this would also alleviate unwanted expression of 3CD in off-target tissues.

In addition to providing an important function in the assembly of VLPs, 3CD also provides a conserved antigen target for cross-protective immune responses. This was recently demonstrated when an adenovirus vaccine encoding EV71 P1 and 3CD mediated cross-protection against coxsackievirus A16 challenge in mice (24). Although EV71-neutralizing antibody responses were readily detected, only 3C-specific T cell responses were detected against coxsackievirus A16, suggesting a potential role for cross-reactive T cells in protection against enterovirus infections. Given the high conservation of 3CD between picornaviruses, the inclusion of this antigen alongside neutralizing antibody targets would likely improve the breadth of protection. Given the role of vaccine-induced antibodies in protection from neurological disease in our passive transfer study, we did not investigate the T cell response to EV-D68–repRNA/LION vaccination. However, we have previously demonstrated that robust T cell responses are elicited against other antigens encoded in the repRNA/LION platform (25, 26, 29, 60–63).

In contrast with 3CD, the P1 polyprotein is likely under a stronger immune pressure because increased diversity of the P1 subunits compared with that of 3CD has been observed (64, 65). Given the receptor binding strategy used by enteroviruses (hiding their receptor binding site in canyon-like depressions on the surface of the virion), antibody escape can be mediated by changing a few residues to alter the composition of the canyon rims, the major neutralizing antibody epitopes, while maintaining receptor binding activity (8, 33, 34, 66). We observed almost complete escape of neutralizing responses for viruses that were mismatched to their vaccine by two or five amino acids for A1 and C isolates, respectively (33–35).

Given the observation that genotype poorly predicts serotype, it is likely that an EV-D68 vaccine would require either a closely matched composition to circulating strain(s) or a multivalent approach aimed at driving enhanced breadth of immunity. Although RNA vaccines can be easily combined to drive multivalent responses, as recently demonstrated for influenza (67), dose-limiting reactogenicity may limit their application in humans. We recently determined that LION is able to overcome that dose limitation by retaining the RNA payload locally in the injected muscle tissue and by avoiding systemic inflammation; in contrast, LNP formulations drained from the injected site and activated innate signaling pathways in distal organs, including the liver (60). When we compared LION and LNP in the context of multivalent immunization combining an RSV vaccine, an influenza virus vaccine, and an EV-D68 vaccine in a trivalent dose, we demonstrated that the systemic inflammatory response to LNP/repRNA correlated with a reduction in the neutralizing antibody response to EV-D68 compared with the neutralizing antibody response induced by a monovalent EV-D68 LNP/repRNA control. The binding antibody response to the other two targets of the multivalent composition, RSV and influenza virus, was not affected. This suggests that LION could be uniquely suited for multivalent EV-D68 vaccines and that multivalent LNP-specific responses could potentially interfere with EV-D68 vaccine compositions; more work is needed to understand this phenomenon.

Last, our data also highlight a formulation-dependent difference in protection against upper respiratory tract infection. Although first-generation mRNA/LNP vaccines have enabled robust protection against severe disease in the context of COVID-19, their ability to reduce virus infection in the upper airway and subsequent transmission events has been limited (68–70). We previously observed reduction of virus replication in the upper airways of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2–infected hamsters (27, 28) and NHPs (39) vaccinated with spike protein–encoding LION/repRNA; however, we were unable to directly compare to LNP-formulated counterparts. Here, we reproduced that phenotype in an EV-D68 challenge model with a direct comparison to LNP formulation. Despite the induction of similar titers of serum neutralizing antibodies between LNP- and LION-formulated groups and similar reduction of virus replication in the lower airway after challenge, we observed distinct abilities of LNP-or LION-formulated vaccines to drive protection in the upper airway after intramuscular administration. Additional studies are needed to determine what components of the immune response are responsible for this observation and whether this could contribute to reduced transmission between animals.

Our study has several limitations. First, although we have previously characterized the T cell responses induced by our platform, we only evaluated antibody response and function in these studies. As we discussed above, it is known that T cell responses can contribute to protection from enterovirus infections, and additional efforts are needed to characterize those responses in the context of EV-D68 repRNA/LION vaccination. Second, we did not evaluate protection from virus pathology in infected tissues after challenge of vaccinated animals; therefore, we cannot conclude that repRNA/LION vaccination can provide protection from virus-induced tissue pathology. Last, how these findings will translate into humans is limited to a preliminary understanding of neutralizing antibody responses elicited in a small sample size of healthy NHPs at a single dose. Additional dose-finding safety and immunogenicity studies are warranted in support of continued clinical development of a repRNA/LION vaccine for the prevention of EV-D68 infection.

Overall, our studies provide a starting point for addressing nonenveloped viruses of public health concern using RNA vaccine technology. First, coexpression of P1 and 3CD polyproteins from repRNA in vivo can be used to mount neutralizing antibody responses against enteroviruses. Second, this approach can be parallelized across genotypes to rapidly characterize antigenic relationships. Third, genotypes were not particularly predictive of serotype, with few amino acid substitutions required for substantial escape from neutralization. Fourth, formulation composition can be modulated to improve protection from virus infection in other tissue compartments. Additional studies are warranted to further characterize the immunogenicity in terms of binding antibody and T cell responses, the contribution of those responses to protection, the safety and general applicability to other nonenveloped viruses, and the use of multivalent compositions to drive broadly protective immunity.

The primary objective of these studies was to evaluate the immunogenicity and efficacy of various repRNA vaccines against EV-D68 in murine models and immunogenicity in NHP models. Mouse group sizes were determined on the basis of power calculations derived from previous studies using similar repRNA constructs. Randomization was used to allocate mice into different treatment groups. For the NHP study, the number of animals included was constrained by availability and resources. Six rhesus macaques were stratified by age and weight to minimize variability. Blinding was used in the passive transfer study (Fig. 5) given the subjectivity of clinical scoring. The remaining studies were not blinded because of practical constraints. Predefined end points were established before the commencement of each study, focusing on assessing immunogenicity and protective efficacy against EV-D68 infection. Experimental replication and the specifics on biological and technical replicates are detailed in the figure legends corresponding to each experiment.

Rhabdomyosarcoma (RD) cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (CCL-136) and grown in complete medium containing Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with high glucose (Gibco, 11995) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (pen/strep; Gibco, 15140). Cells were maintained at 37°C and 5% CO2. One representative isolate from each clade of EV-D68 was obtained from BEI Resources. The isolates include subclade A1, USA/WI/2009–23230 (BEI, NR-51995); subclade A2, US/KY/14–18953 (NR-49132); subclade B1, US/MO/14–18947 (NR-49129); subclade B2, US/IL/14–18952 (NR-49131); subclade B3, USA/2018–23087 (NR51996); and subclade C, USA/2003–23226 (NR-51990). Virus stocks were grown in RD cells at 33°C with 5% CO2. Confluent flasks of RD cells were infected at a multiplicity of infection of 0.1 with EV-D68 stocks from BEI Resources and incubated until a cytopathic effect was observed, about 2 days after infection. These master stocks were then titrated by plaque assay and used to propagate working stocks used for subsequent experiments.

Murine experiments were approved by the University of Washington (protocol 4538–01) or University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Mice were housed in a temperature and humidity-controlled animal care facility with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and were provided access to food and water ad libitum. Six- to 8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were purchased through the Jackson Laboratory and acclimated for no less than 2 days before immunizations. Mice were immunized intramuscularly in the hind leg with 50 μl of formulated vaccine on day 0 and boosted on day 28 as outlined in the figure legends. Animals were bled by retro-orbital or submental bleed on day 28. Mice were euthanized on day 42 after prime, and blood was collected by cardiac puncture. Serum was isolated from whole blood and used for subsequent testing. Serum was heat inactivated at 56°C for 30 min when used in xCELLigence assays or passive transfer studies.

For murine infection studies, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 150 μl of a ketamine (13 mg/ml) and xylazine (0.88 mg/ml) stock solution. Once animals were not responsive to toe pinch, mice were then challenged with 30 μl of EV-D68 stocks. Viral stocks were diluted in PBS if necessary to meet appropriate concentrations for administration. Using a 200-μl pipette, 30 μl of virus stock was administered intranasally, equally dividing between the two nostrils (~15 μl per nostril). Mice were observed until they awoke from ketamine/xylazine treatment. Twenty-four hours after intranasal infection, mice were euthanized using a CO2 chamber, and nasal cavity cartilage and lungs were collected in screw-top tubes containing infection medium.

For the mouse paralytic disease model study, mice from three mixed litters of 7-day-old pups that were Ifnar1−/− on a C57BL/6 background were randomly assigned to receive 75 μl of PBS (n = 4) or pooled serum from mice vaccinated as described above with either B2 repRNA/LION (n = 5) or influenza repRNA/LION (n = 7) passively transferred by the intraperitoneal route. The next day, mice were inoculated with a 5 × 105 cell culture 50% infectious dose of the B2 clade EV-D68 isolate US/IL/14–18952 in 10 μl of PBS administered intramuscularly. Body weight and neurological scores were recorded daily for 21 days. Total daily neurological score for each animal was calculated as the total sum of the neurological scores for each limb. Animals with neurological scores above 8 were considered to have reached a humane end point and euthanized. The individual who scored the animals was blinded to the treatment groups, and at least one mouse from each treatment group was present in every litter. The scores ranged as follows: 0, no impairment; 1, abnormal hindlimb splay, ataxia; 2, partially collapsed hindlimb, limited movement of limb; and 3, severe impairment, no movement of limb, and limb is non–weight bearing [modified from (43)].

For NHP studies, all experiments were approved by the Rocky Mountain Laboratories IACUC (protocol no. 2021–052). Male and female rhesus macaques ranging from 2.1 to 12.2 years of age were randomly assigned to vaccine groups. On day 0, six animals were immunized by the intramuscular route with 25 μg in a 0.5-ml injection of EV-D68 vaccine against the B1 clade US/MO/14–18947, and six animals were immunized with a control, irrelevant repRNA vaccine encoding Crimean Congo hemorrhagic fever virus antigen. Animals were boosted on day 42 after prime, and serum samples from immunized NHPs were collected at days 0, 14, 28, 42, 49, and 63 after prime. Serum samples were heat-inactivated at 56°C for 30 min when used in RTCA neutralization assays.

Nasal turbinates and lungs were harvested from euthanized animals and collected in 1.75-ml screw-cap tubes with a rubber O-ring containing 500 μl of infection medium and a metal homogenizer bead, and tissue weights were recorded. Tissues were homogenized using the QIAGEN TissueLyser LT for 5 min at the maximum speed. Tissues were then centrifuged at the maximum speed for 5 min to pellet tissue debris. Supernatants were collected and used for plaque assay to determine viral load as described above.

To titer virus stocks and tissue homogenates, RD cells were seeded in 12-well tissue culture plates at 500,000 cells per well the day before usage. Virus stocks were diluted in infection medium (DMEM with high glucose, containing 2% FBS and 1% pen/strep). Complete cell culture medium was removed from monolayers of RD cells, and 100 μl of 10-fold dilutions of virus stock or tissue homogenate was added to each well. Plates were rocked every 15 min for 1 hour at 33°C. After 1 hour, 1 ml of a 1% agarose overlay in infection medium was placed in each well. Plates were incubated at 33°C for 3 days. Plates were fixed with a 10% formaldehyde solution for 20 min. Agarose and formaldehyde were removed, and 200 μl of crystal violet was added to each well for 2 to 5 min. Plates were washed in water, and plaques were counted manually without blinding to group treatments.

Double-stranded DNA fragments encoding EV-D68 antigens described in the figure legends were synthesized and cloned into a plasmid backbone between PFl FI and Sac II restriction enzyme sites, harboring the 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions and nonstructural open reading frame of Venezeulan equine encephalitis virus, strain TC-83, as well as a T7 DNA-dependent RNA polymerase promoter and kanamycin resistance gene cassette. Plasmids were sequence-verified and linearized by digestion with Not I enzyme. RNA was then transcribed and capped from purified linear template DNA by T7 polymerase and vaccinia capping enzyme reactions as previously described (25). RNA quality was assessed by fragment analysis (Agilent). RNA was then complexed with LION as previously described (25) or encapsulated in LNPs as described below.

LION was prepared as previously described (25, 71). Briefly, the oil phase, containing squalene (Sigma-Aldrich), Span 60 (Sigma-Aldrich), and 1,2-dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium propane (DOTAP) (Lipoid), was sonicated for 30 min in a 65°C water bath. Separately, the aqueous phase, containing Tween 80 and sodium citrate dihydrate solution in water, was prepared with continuous stirring until all components were dissolved. The oil and aqueous phases were then mixed and emulsified using a high-shear mixer (Silverson L5M-A). The crude colloid was subsequently processed by passaging through a microfluidizer at 206,843 kPa with an M110-P microfluidizer (Microfluidics) equipped with a 75-μm Y-geometry diamond primary interaction chamber and an H30Z 200-μm ceramic auxiliary interaction chamber until the Z-average hydrodynamic diameter, measured by dynamic light scattering (Malvern Zetasizer Nano S), was ~60 nm with a polydispersity index of 0.25 or lower. The microfluidized LION was terminally filtered with a 200-nm–pore size polyethersulfone filter and stored at 2° to 8°C.

RNA was encapsulated into LNPs using methods described previously (72). Briefly, lipid components comprising the ionizable lipid SM-102 (Cayman Chemical), helper lipid 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC) (Lipoid), cholesterol (MP Biomedical), and 1,2-dimyristoyl-rac-glycero-3-methoxypolyethylene glycol-2000 (DMG–PEG-2000) (Avanti) were dissolved in ethanol at a molar ratio of 50:10:38:2 (SM-102:DSPC:cholesterol:DMG–PEG-2000) and mixed at an nitrogen-to-phosphate (N:P) molar ratio of 5.67 with RNA dissolved in a sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.5) using a glass micromixer chip (Dolomite Microfluidics). After mixing, the formulations were dialyzed against PBS (pH 7.4) for 16 to 24 hours in a 10,000-Da molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) dialysis cassette (Thermo Fisher Scientific). LNPs were concentrated using Amicon 100,000 MWCO ultra centrifugal filter devices (EMD Millipore) and stored at 5°C. RNA encapsulation was quantified using a Ribogreen assay using Triton X-100 to disrupt formulated LNPs. Particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were measured using a Malvern Zetasizer Ultra.

To measure the neutralizing antibody activity of polyclonal serum samples from immunized mice, the Agilent xCELLigence RTCA Multiple Plate instrument was used to quantify cell monolayer integrity. RD cells were plated on 96-well electronic microplates (E-Plate 96) at 60,000 cells per well. Cell impedance was measured every 15 min and recorded on the RTCA-MP instrument. The following day, individual mouse serum samples were diluted fourfold starting at 1:10 in infection medium containing DMEM with high glucose, 2% FBS, and 1% pen/strep. One hundred microliters of EV-D68 (1 × 106 PFU/ml) was added to diluted serum. Serum-virus mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 30 min to allow antibody-virus interaction. Plates were then removed from the RTCA, and medium was removed from each well and replaced with serum-virus mixtures. Plates were put back on the RTCA, and impedance was measured every 15 min for 160 hours.

To calculate the 50% neutralization titer, the area under the normalized cell index curve over time was determined using the RTCA software for each serum dilution as well as control wells containing virus alone or no virus. Then, the percent neutralization at each serum dilution was calculated, normalized between virus-alone and no-virus conditions, and plotted for regression analysis in GraphPad Prism 10. Last, the 50% neutralizing serum dilution was interpolated.

For RNA isolation and preparation, total RNA was extracted from lung tissue of three groups of mice: B1-, C-, and RSV-repRNA–immunized; EV-D68–infected; and naïve control. The extraction was performed using the RNeasy Fibrous Tissue Mini Kit (QIAGEN). For each sample, 75 ng of total RNA was hybridized with the Mm Host Response Panel CodeSet (NanoString) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After overnight hybridization, the reaction mixtures were loaded onto a cartridge for analysis with the nCounter SPRINT system (NanoString Technologies). Gene expression data were normalized, and cell-type profiling was analyzed using the nSolver 4.0 Advanced Analysis software (version 2.0.134). Differential gene expression was analyzed by using the Rosalind platform. Data were visualized by GraphPad Prism 10 software. NanoString data are available at https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.73n5tb34p.

Strain US/MO/14–18947 [Protein Data Bank (PDB): 7T9P] was selected for EV-D68 structural visualization (73). To visualize the effect of mutations of single amino acids, we assumed constant backbone coordinates and inserted the most common rotamer of the previously unidentified antigenic epitope residue using Coot 0.8.9.2 without repacking. Antibody costructures (35, 74, 75), such as antibody EV68–228 (PDB: 6WDT), were overlaid by superimposing the capsid proteins in UCSF Chimera.

All statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10. All enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, neutralization, and plaque assay data were log-transformed. Normally distributed data (determined by Shapiro-Wilk test) were analyzed by Student’s t or analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests, and non-normally distributed data were analyzed by Kruskal-Wallis test with multiple comparisons tests used as needed according to the figure legends.
