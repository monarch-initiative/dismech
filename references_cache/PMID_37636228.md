# Unmistakable Truncal Dystonia Mistaken as Psychogenic: A Case Report of VAC14-Related Neurodegeneration.
**Authors:** Bhowmick SS, Mahajan SK, Shah V, Kashyap K
**Journal:** Mov Disord Clin Pract (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mdc3.13776](https://doi.org/10.1002/mdc3.13776)

## Content

VAC14‐related neurodegeneration is characterized by childhood‐onset dystonia, parkinsonism, pyramidal signs, dysarthria, and poor response to dopaminergic and anticholinergic therapy.
1
 The lipid kinase PIKfyve and the lipid phosphatase Fig4 interact through the pentameric scaffold protein VAC14 to regulate synthesis of phosphatidylinositol 3,5‐bisphosphate (PI(3,5)P2), which has diverse functions such as trafficking and acidification of the endo‐lysosomes, autophagy, stress‐induced signaling, and ion‐channel activity.
2
,
3
 PI(3,5)P2 plays an important role in the generation of intracellular transport vesicles through promotion and inhibition of membrane fission and fusion, respectively.
4
 Neurodegeneration is observed in mouse models lacking VAC14; affected neurons have enlarged vacuoles arising from swelling of the endo‐lysosomes.
5
 Likewise enlarged vacuoles are found in the cultured skin fibroblasts of patients carrying pathogenic variants in the VAC14 gene.
1
,
6
 Perhaps these variants alter the protein's ability to form multimers, thereby affecting assembly of the PIKfyve complex and synthesis of PI(3,5)P2.
7

We report a case of VAC14‐related neurodegeneration presenting with marked truncal dystonia that was erroneously labeled psychogenic at first.

A 14‐year‐old girl, born of non‐consanguineous parents from Gujarat, India started complaining that her trunk arched backward when she attempted walking (Video 1). The awkward gait and apparently normal neuroimaging prompted clinicians to diagnose her with a functional neurological disorder. Treatment commenced with counseling and medications such as fluoxetine 20 mg/day, clonazepam 1 mg/day, trifluoperazine 5 mg/day, and trihexyphenidyl 2 mg/day (trihexyphenidyl was supposedly prescribed along with trifluoperazine to prevent neuroleptic‐related dystonic reaction). The girl's parents decided to discontinue therapy after 3 weeks because there was no improvement in her gait. Over the next 2 years, she avoided walking because of fear of falls. She faced difficulty in speaking and performing fine motor activities with hands, such as writing (micrographia) and weaving. There was no history of oculogyric crisis, tremor, myoclonus, seizures, cognitive decline, dysautonomia, and sleep disorders. She had age‐appropriate developmental milestones and an unremarkable childhood. There was no family history of relevant neurological disorders. Examination at the age of 16 years (Video 2) showed normal cognition, normal eye movements, normal fundus, parkinsonism (mild hypomimia, hypophonic speech, mild neck and limb rigidity, slight left hand rest tremor, and bradykinesia), brisk deep tendon reflexes, extensor plantar responses, and normal sensations. She had generalized dystonia with prominent involvement of the trunk and legs (opisthotonus and excessive right plantar flexion while walking, and splaying of the toes on the right) and subtle involvement of the hands (more on the right). Systemic examination was normal. Dexterity of her hands and speech improved mildly with trihexyphenidyl 6 mg/day and levodopa 450 mg/day. Unfortunately, surgical options could not be explored as the cost was prohibitive.

Gait at age 14 years. Segment 1 shows backward arching of the girl's trunk (truncal dystonia) when she walks. Segment 2 shows improvement in her gait while pushing a chair (sensory trick).

Neurological examination at 16 years. Segment 1 (seated neurological examination) shows normal eye movements, hypomimia, bradykinesia (left slower than right), subtle dystonia of extremities (posturing of the right hand, splayed toes on the right), and slight left hand rest tremor. Segment 2 (gait examination) shows backward arching of the trunk, dystonic right plantar‐flexion, laterally deviated left foot, slight scissoring of lower limbs, and pirouetting while turning. There is improvement in the girl's gait when she assumes a slightly flexed posture with arms down.

Hemogram, blood glucose, liver and renal function tests, serum electrolytes, uric acid, ceruloplasmin, plasma lactate, and urinalysis were normal. Serum thyroid stimulating hormone was high (25.5 mIU/mL), but thyroxine replacement did not improve her motor functions. Susceptibility‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain showed hypointense globus pallidus and substantia nigra (Fig. 1). A dystonia gene panel (next generation sequencing) did not yield any abnormality. Whole exome sequencing revealed compound heterozygous variants in the VAC14 gene [(NM_018052.5): c.1744G > T (p.Ala582Ser) and c.2005G > A (p.Val669Met)]. Ala582Ser was maternally inherited, whereas Val669Met was absent in either parent (Sanger sequence profile, Fig. 2), implying either de novo occurrence or gonadal mosaicism in the father for Val669Met. The variants were considered likely pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics recommendations (see Data S1 for in silico analysis).

Sequence chromatogram and VAC14 exons: alignment to the reference sequence shows that the variant p.Val669Met was detected in heterozygous condition in the proband (A), but not in mother (C) as well as father (E); the variant p.Ala582Ser was detected in heterozygous condition in the index patient (B) and mother (D), but not father (F); a schematic representation of the VAC14 exons shows reported pathogenic variants.

Individuals with biallelic pathogenic variants in the VAC14 gene have a variable period of normal development after birth. They manifest motor symptoms in infancy, childhood, or adolescence, often abruptly. Infantile‐onset is characterized by axial hypotonia, regression in motor skills, and subsequently, progressive dystonia and spasticity.
8
 Older children and adolescents manifest focal‐onset hand or foot dystonia initially, thus presenting with writer's cramp or difficulty in walking. Over the next few years, truncal, cervical, and cranial muscles are involved. There may be rapid progression or exacerbation of dystonia with febrile illness.
1
,
9
 Because of severe dystonia, hand and foot movements seem slow and give the impression of bradykinesia.
10
 Some patients may have true bradykinesia and additional features indicative of parkinsonism, such as hypomimia, rigidity, and freezing of gait.
6
 Dysarthria is pronounced. Pyramidal signs (hyperreflexia and extensor plantar responses) are commonly elicited.
11
,

 Susceptibility weighted MRI of the brain shows hypointense globus pallidus and substantia nigra.

 In patients who manifest symptoms in infancy or early childhood, conspicuous hyperintensity of the striatum on T2‐weighted and diffusion‐weighted MRI may indicate profound neurodegeneration.

,

 There is no meaningful improvement with levodopa and anticholinergic drugs. Deep brain stimulation of the globus pallidus internus may alleviate dystonia and improve gait; long‐term benefit is not known.
6

Clinical and radiological features are useful in narrowing myriad acquired and genetic disorders that cause dystonia‐parkinsonism.
14
 Opisthotonus occurs in several infectious, drug‐induced, metabolic, autoimmune, and neurodegenerative disorders
15
,
16
; it should be differentiated from “arc de cercle” of psychogenic origin.
17
,
18
 Although dystonic opisthotonus may occur spontaneously, it usually occurs when the patient stands up and starts walking (action‐induced). Impaired postural reflexes due to involvement of the mesencephalic centers for maintaining body posture may cause backward leaning during gait.
19
 Our patient likely had truncal dystonia because, not only there was a clear arching of the back that was alleviated by slightly flexed posture (geste antagoniste), but also involvement of other body parts (extremities).

Dystonic opisthotonus is a red‐flag sign for neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation.
16
 VAC14‐related neurodegeneration may present with similar clinicoradiological features (see Table 1 and Data S2 for details). Brain histopathology of two affected siblings showed degeneration and vacuolation in the putamen, caudate, and globus pallidus. Contrary to expectation, nigral abnormalities and iron deposition were not found.
9
 Certain genetic panels (eg, dystonia gene panel or neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation gene panel) may not include the VAC14 gene; therefore, the diagnosis can be missed if one is not aware of this condition.
