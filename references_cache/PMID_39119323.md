Amyloidosis cutis dyschromica is a very rare form of primary cutaneous amyloidosis characterized by prepubertal onset of hyper and hypopigmented spots and amyloid deposits in the papillary dermis. We report two cases of this condition highlighting its dermoscopic findings.

Amyloidosis cutis dyschromica (ACD) is a rare form of primary cutaneous amyloidosis and showing reticulate hyperpigmentation interspersed with hypopigmented and hyperpigmented asymptomatic macules diffusely spread all over the body with prepubertal onset and deposition of amyloid in the papillary dermis.[1]Differential diagnosis include dyschromatosis universalis hereditaria, generalised Dowling-Degos disease, xeroderma pigmentosum, and poikilodermatous amyloidosis.[2]Genetic factors have been found out to be one of the causative factors.[3]There is no satisfactory treatment modality of ACD.[1]There are very few case reports highlighting the dermoscopic findings of ACD which would help in diagnosing and differentiating with similar conditions.

Two females of 23 years old and 12 years old came with chief complaints of asymptomatic discolored lesions all over the body since the age of 5 years which was accidentally noticed [Figure 1]. There was a history of use of plastic scrub in the second patient. There was no history of any local application. There is no history of similar complaints in the family, consanguinity, or photosensitivity. On examination, there were multiple well defined hypopigmented and hyperpigmented macules involving the trunk and extremities sparing the face, palms and soles, and mucosae. A Provisional diagnosis of Dyschromic amyloidosis, Dyschromatosis universalis hereditaria, and pigmented xerodermoid was made. Dermoscopy of hyperpigmented lesion in nonpolarised mode showed irregular hyperpigmented areas with tiny pits and exaggerated skin markings in a few lesions. In polarised mode, hyperpigmented macules showed multiple dark brown dots and globules which on high power showed a honeycomb pattern. Dermoscopy of the hypopigmented lesion in nonpolarised mode showed a pebbled appearance and radial furrows at the periphery. In polarised mode, reddish brown and grayish globules arranged in mosaic or honeycomb pattern corresponding to the pebbled pattern of non polarised mode was seen. On higher magnification, globules showed reddish dots with brownish pigmentation [Figures 2and3]. On histopathology from both hyperpigmented and hypopigmented macules, clefted homogenous eosinophilic material in the papillary dermis suggestive of amyloid deposits was seen which stained positive with congo red stain [Figure 4]. Also dilated capillaries and perivascular infiltrate with melanophages were seen. A final diagnosis of dyschromic amyloidosis was made.

Amyloidosis cutis dyschromica is a form of primary cutaneous amyloidosis causing hyperpigmentation and hypopigmentation all over the body sparing palms, soles, and face and mucosae. Japanese dermatologist Morishima first described the lesion in 1970.[2]Men and women are equally affected. Such cases are seen in people of South East Asian countries.[4]The majority of patients have a family history of similar lesions. Most patients develop skin pigment changes in early childhood not associated with pruritis. The lesions run a benign course. There is no spontaneous regression. Genetic factors have been found to be a causative factor as they occur before puberty and shows a familial history.[4]UVB- and UVC-induced damaged keratinocytes due to delayed DNA repair undergo apoptosis. The cytokeratin following keratinocyte apoptosis is phagocytosed by histiocytes and fibroblasts and that is responsible for the amyloid formation.[1]Amyloidosis cutis dyschromica is diagnosed by recognising the typical clinical features and confirmed by the biopsy of a hyperpigmented or hypopigmented macule showing amyloid deposits that stains positive with congo red. Amyloidosis cutis dyschromica may closely resemble a number of other rare dyschromatoses; these include Dyschromatosis universalis hereditaria, Cutis tricolor, and idiopathic guttate hypomelanosis.[5]There are very few case reports highlighting the dermoscopy finding of dyschromic amyloidosis. Dermoscopy of our patients showed pebbling corresponding to the amyloid deposits, brown dots and globules corresponding to the melanophages and amyloid deposits, reddish dots corresponding to the dilated vessels, white spots with ill-defined margins which reflect hyperkeratosis, due to the incident light that cannot pass through the compact hyperkeratosis. Multiple topical treatments, including 10% urea cream and tazarotene, topical steroids, keratolytics, capsaicin, dimethyl sulfoxide, and CO2laser have been prescribed to patients showing mild to no improvement. Oral acitretin owing to its ability to induce apoptosis reduces the available keratinocytes, the most probable source of amyloid. Photoprotection is strongly recommended in all cases.[3]
