# Splicing Alterations Associated with Multiple Homozygous TNXB Variants in a Patient with Suspected Classical-Like Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome.
**Authors:** Imanaka B, Hasegawa Y, Kawato K, Kimoto Y, Oda I, Okamoto N, Saigoh K, Nakama M
**Journal:** Biochem Genet (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10528-026-11416-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10528-026-11416-z)

## Content

Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS) comprises a heterogeneous group of heritable connective tissue disorders characterized by joint hypermobility, skin hyperextensibility, and tissue fragility. Classical-like EDS (clEDS) is typically associated with pathogenic biallelic variants in TNXB. The patient showed generalized joint hypermobility involving the knees, fingers, elbows, and feet, along with soft, pliable skin. She had a history of three dislocations of the right elbow and one dislocation of the left elbow. She also reported orthostatic dizziness and chronic constipation, suggesting possible autonomic nervous system involvement. Cardiovascular assessment detected mild mitral valve regurgitation. Based on these findings, hypermobility syndrome or EDS was suspected, and gene panel testing was conducted for classical and pseudo-classical EDS. The patient was found to be homozygous for multiple TNXB (NM_001365276.2) variants, including c.11,925 + 6 C > G, c.12,156 C > G (p. Arg4052=), c.12,210 + 5G > A, c.12,210 + 39dup, and c.12,210 + 43T > G, and also carried a mosaic variant c.8012 C > T (p. Ala2671Val). To investigate the potential effects of these variants on splicing, RT-PCR using peripheral blood RNA and in vitro minigene splicing assays were performed. Analysis of the patient-derived cDNA and cDNA derived from mutant minigenes revealed alternative splicing leading to intron 40 retention alongside the normal transcript. Intron 40 retention was more pronounced with multiple variants and was most prominently associated with c.12,210 + 5G > A in the minigene assays. These results suggest that c.12,210 + 5G > A can influence TNXB splicing, generating in-frame transcripts. The aberrant transcripts are predicted to affect the fibrinogen C-terminal domain. Although the clinical significance of these variants remains uncertain, these findings highlight the importance of integrating population frequency data, clinical assessment, and functional analyses when interpreting uncertain TNXB variants in patients with suspected clEDS.

Introduction
The Ehlers–Danlos syndromes (EDSs) are a group of heritable connective tissue dis-
orders (HCTDs) marked by skin hyperextensibility, joint hypermobility, and tissue 
fragility. According to the 2017 International Classification, they are categorized into 
13 subtypes based on clinical features and linked to 20 causative genes. Classical-
like EDS (clEDS) is classified under disorders involving the structure and function 
of the myomatrix (Malfait et al. 2020). The clinical features of TNXB-related clEDS 
resemble those of classical EDS (cEDS). clEDS is defined by skin hyperextensibility 
with a velvety texture but without atrophic scarring, generalized joint hypermobility 
with or without repeated dislocations (typically affecting the shoulder and ankle), and 
easily bruised skin or spontaneous ecchymoses as primary diagnostic criteria (Mal -
fait et al. 2017). clEDS resulting from a deletion and a point mutation in the TNXB 
(OMIM#600985) has been described (Schalkwijk et al. 2001). Tenascin-X (TNX), 
encoded by TNXB, is an extracellular matrix protein that can influence connective 
tissue in various forms. Biallelic loss-of-function mutations in TNXB cause clEDS, 
which follows an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern (Malfait et al. 2020). TNX 
deficiency was first identified in a patient who presented with combined features of 
congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) and EDS (Burch et al. 1997). CAH is due 
to 21-hydroxylase deficiency caused by pathogenetic variants in its encoding gene, 
CYP21A2. TNXB is located adjacent to CYP21A2, the gene responsible for CAH, 
with both situated within the human leukocyte antigen complex region (Merke et 
al. 2013). Up to 10% of individuals with CAH also exhibit EDS, a condition termed 
CAH-X (Marino et al. 2022).

Because genetic analysis of TNXB is complicated by the presence of the nearby 
TNXA pseudogene, TNX-deficient EDS often goes undiagnosed (Marino et al. 2021). 
Next-generation sequencing (NGS) using a panel-based approach is useful for evalu-
ating EDS subtypes and other HCTDs with overlapping clinical features (Marino 
et al. 2022; Yamaguchi et al. 2022). In the present study, gene panel analysis was 
performed in a patient with suspected clEDS or an EDS-related hypermobility phe -
notype, and multiple TNXB variants of uncertain clinical significance were identified. 
To investigate their potential effects on splicing, we performed RT-PCR using periph-
eral blood RNA and in vitro minigene splicing assays. This study aimed to evaluate 
whether these variants, individually or in combination, could influence TNXB splic-
ing, while recognizing that their clinical significance requires cautious interpretation.
Case
The patient is a 9-year-old girl and the first child of unrelated parents. The parents 
were non-consanguineous, and no known familial relationship was reported. Pedi -
gree tree illustrating the family structure was shown in Fig. 1. She was delivered at 39 
weeks and 2 days of gestation, with a birth weight of 2,540 g, a height of 49.0 cm, and 
a head circumference of 31.4 cm, with no signs of asphyxia. Her growth and develop-
ment were normal, and she began walking independently at 11 months. Around the 
age of 3, she started to complain of pain in both knees without any clear cause. An 
initial X-ray performed by a local orthopedic specialist showed no abnormalities. 
However, as the episodes of pain became more frequent with age, she was referred to 
our orthopedic department at age 7. She demonstrated bilateral joint hypermobility 
in the knees, fingers, elbows, and feet, along with soft skin (Fig. 2). By that time, she 
had experienced three right elbow dislocations and one on the left. She also reported

KF-4109) used as a healthy control, following standard protocols for genetic test -
ing. Initially, gene analysis of the patient was conducted using NGS at the Kazusa

DNA Research Institute. Short-read NGS, which covered the protein-coding regions 
and splice sites (exon/intron junctions), was used to analyze TNXB, COL5A1, and 
COL5A2, which are known to cause cEDS, as well as COL1A1, COL1A2, PLOD1, 
ADAMTS2, and CHST14 as differential diagnoses. Because TNXB contains homolo-
gous regions in certain exons that share identical sequences with TNXA (Demirdas et 
al. 2017), TNXB was specifically amplified only in these homologous regions using 
long-range PCR to distinguish it from TNXA.
To detect variants in the genomic DNA of the patient and her parents, a two-step 
PCR strategy was employed. In the first round, TNXB-specific primers that do not 
amplify the TNXA pseudogene (primers no. 1 and no. 2 in Supplementary Table S1) 
were used to selectively amplify the TNXB locus. The first-round PCR products were 
then diluted with water 1:20 and used directly as templates for the second-round PCR 
using primers specific for the target region (primers no. 3 and no. 2). PCR products 
were analyzed by a 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and subsequently subjected to 
Sanger sequencing for variant identification.
To determine whether the five variants were located in cis or in trans, genomic DNA 
from the father was amplified using primers spanning the relevant TNXB region. The 
resulting PCR products were cloned into a plasmid vector, and individual clones were 
subjected to Sanger sequencing. All five variants were consistently detected within 
the same cloned PCR fragment, demonstrating that they reside on the same allele.
RT-PCR
Total RNA was isolated from the peripheral blood samples of the patient and her 
parents using standard protocols with Isogen kit (Nippon Gene, Japan). First-strand 
cDNA synthesis was performed with 3 µg of total RNA using the M-MLV (Invitro-
gen; Thermo Fisher Scientific) with oligo (dT) primer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). 
For amplification, a first PCR was carried out using TNXB-specific primers (primer 
no. 4 and no. 5), followed by a second PCR using target region primers (primer no. 5 
and no. 7) to analyze RNA splicing around intron 40 while avoiding amplification of 
TNXA. Following RT-PCR, the amplified products were analyzed by polyacrylamide 
gel electrophoresis. RT-PCR products were directly subjected to Sanger sequencing 
to assess exon–intron structures. In cases where multiple splice isoforms were pres -
ent, overlapping sequencing signals were observed; therefore, chromatograms flank-
ing the retained intron were used for interpretation.
Minigene Splicing Assay
A minigene for the patient’s allele and a wild-type (WT) allele minigene were prepared 
using genomic DNA from the patient and the father’s normal allele, respectively. To 
examine the effects of single and multiple variants on intron 40 RNA splicing, seven 
different minigenes were generated by site-directed mutagenesis (TOYOBO, Japan) 
using the WT minigene vector as a template. The minigene splicing assays used the 
pCAGGS vector (Supplementary Fig. S2), which includes a multicloning site for 
inserting the target sequence between two coding exons (exons 40 and 42, Supple -
mentary Table S3a, b). After amplification, the target sequence was cloned into the

minigene vector with the In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit (Takara-Bio, Japan). The accu -
racy of the minigene constructs was confirmed by direct sequencing. HEK293T cells 
were cultured in 10-cm dishes in DMEM supplemented with 10% bovine serum at 
37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. When the cells reached about 80% confluency (cor -
responding to ~ 2.0–2.5 × 10⁶ cells per well), they were transfected with 1 µg of plas-

Using 1 µg of total RNA, cDNA synthesis was carried out with a vector-specific 
primer (primer no. 16), followed by RT-PCR using primers (primer no. 17 and no. 
18). The PCR products were separated on a 5% polyacrylamide gel. Band intensities 
corresponding to each splice isoform were measured using ImageJ software, and the 
percentage of intron 40 retention was calculated as the ratio of the corresponding 
band intensity to the total signal. Because the same primer pair was used to amplify 
all splice isoforms within a single reaction, the resulting PCR products competed 
for amplification, allowing relative comparison of isoform abundance within each 
sample. This approach provided a semi-quantitative assessment of splicing patterns.

Results
NGS analysis identified multiple homozygous variants in the patient’s TNXB gene 
(NM_001365276.2): #1 c.11,925 + 6 C > G in intron 38, #2 c.12,156 C > G in exon 40, 
#3 c.12,210 + 5G > A in intron 40, #4 c.12,210 + 39dup in intron 40, and #5 c.12,210 + 
43 T > G in intron 40. TNXB variants #1–#5, all of which showed low allele fre -
quencies (< of 1%) in East Asian population (Table 1). For the proband, targeted 
short-read next-generation sequencing showed no evidence of copy number loss or 
allelic imbalance at the TNXB locus, arguing against loss of heterozygosity. Although 
Sanger sequencing alone cannot formally exclude LOH, chromatograms showed no 
features suggestive of allelic dropout (Supplementary Fig. S4). The patient’s mother 
was also homozygote for variants #1–#5, while the father was heterozygous for these 
variants. Paternal genomic analysis confirmed that these variants were located on the 
same allele. The father was heterozygous for the TNXB variants and displayed trunk 
hypermobility. The mother, who was homozygous for the same variants, showed 
more pronounced manifestations than the father, including wrist joint deformity and 
hyperextensible skin. The proband, who was also homozygous, exhibited the most 
prominent clinical features. Moreover, long PCR sequencing showed that the patient 
did not have a TNXB/TNXA chimeric gene.
Among the detected variants, the CADD score for variant #3 was 24.30, indicating 
relatively high predicted pathogenicity. Furthermore, splicing prediction using Splice

Discussion
Multiple TNXB variants were identified in this patient, who was clinically suspected 
to have clEDS. The finding that those variants were homozygous state in the pro -
band is consistent with an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern. Given that clEDS 
often presents relatively very mild or variable clinical manifestations, it is conceiv -
able that carriers of such variants may exist within the healthy population. In this

was the only individual who required clinical evaluation. The mother, who was also 
homozygous for the TNXB variants, showed milder but clearly detectable connec -
tive tissue features, whereas the father, who was heterozygous, exhibited only subtle 
manifestations. These observations may be compatible with variable expressivity of 
TNXB-associated connective tissue phenotypes; however, the clinical features are 
not specific to clEDS and may overlap with a broader spectrum of hypermobility-
related conditions. Therefore, the apparent phenotypic gradient in this family should 
be interpreted cautiously and cannot, by itself, establish a gene dosage effect. Tissue-
specific expression patterns, additional genetic modifiers, and non-genetic factors 
may also influence phenotypic severity. Previous reports have suggested that TNXB-
associated phenotypes may show variable expressivity depending on the number of 
affected alleles (Zweers et al. 2003), and that carriers may show milder connective-
tissue manifestations in families with TNXB haploinsufficiency (Merke et al. 2013). 
However, further studies are required to determine whether a similar model applies 
to the variants identified in the present family.
None of the variants detected in our patient are classified as clearly pathogenic in 
the ClinVar database. Population frequency analysis using gnomAD v4.1.0 demon -
strated that variant #3 (c.12210 + 5G > A) shows a relatively higher allele frequency 
in East Asian populations compared with the other identified variants (Table 1). In 
addition, the presence of several these variants in East Asian populations, together 
with the familial inheritance pattern, raises the possibility that at least a subset of 
these variants may occur on population-specific haplotypic backgrounds, potentially 
related to TNXA-derived sequences. Given the relatively high population frequency 
of variant #3 compared with the rarity of clEDS, this variant should not be interpreted 
as a definitive disease-causing variant, and its clinical significance remains uncertain. 
Variant #3 has previously been reported in patients with CAH (Zhang et al. 2024). 
Although aberrant splicing was predicted for this variant in that report, experimental 
validation and functional evidence have not previously been reported.
While ClinVar is designed to evaluate the clinical significance of individual 
variants, it has limitations in assessing the potential effects of multiple variants in 
combination. In our patient, each variant individually showed limited evidence for 
pathogenicity; however, the possible combined effects of these variants on splicing 
regulation remain uncertain. In the present study, the minigene assay demonstrated 
abnormal intron 40 splicing associated with specific combinations of TNXB variants 
at the RNA level. In particular, variant #3 was associated with increased intron 40 
retention resulting in an in-frame insertion in this experimental system. The inserted 
intronic sequence is located within the FBG domain, and the high degree of evolu -
tionary conservation of the affected region suggests that this insertion may have func-
tional relevance. However, the functional consequences of this in-frame insertion at 
the protein level remain unknown. In addition, although intron-retained transcripts 
were detected at lower levels than the normally spliced transcript, the relationship 
between transcript abundance and functional impact on TNXB protein or extracel -
lular matrix organization remains unclear. These molecular findings therefore support 
a possible effect of the identified variants on TNXB splicing, but do not establish their 
pathogenicity according to current ACMG/AMP-based variant interpretation criteria

to clarify the biological and clinical significance of these splicing alterations. Nev -

TNXB variants can alter splicing patterns in vitro.
Long-range PCR revealed that the patient did not carry a TNXB/TNXA chimera, 
although she harbored three variants (#2, #4, and #5) identical to TNXA-specific 
sequences. As reported by Yamaguchi et al. ( 2023), such variants likely represent 
TNXA-derived sequences that have recombined into TNXB through gene conversion. 
Based on this model, we speculate that the #3 variant may originally have arisen 
within the TNXA region and subsequently been transferred, together with the sur -
rounding sequence including exon 40, into TNXB via partial gene conversion. How-
ever, this hypothesis remains speculative and requires further genomic analysis for 
confirmation.
Since TNXB is normally highly expressed in connective tissues (Burch et al. 
1997), patient-derived fibroblasts would have been the preferred material for mRNA 
expression analysis. However, a skin biopsy was not performed because there was no 
clinical indication for an invasive procedure beyond genetic testing. Because TNXB 
expression in peripheral blood is very low, only nested PCR yielded sufficient ampli-
fication, and in vitro minigene assays were therefore employed to supplement the 
analysis. Nevertheless, the splicing patterns observed in the minigene assays may 
not fully reflect endogenous TNXB splicing regulation in disease-relevant tissues 
such as fibroblasts. In addition, the relatively high proportion of wild-type transcripts 
observed in the minigene experiments limits the ability to determine whether the 
degree of aberrant splicing is sufficient to cause disease in vivo. Therefore, the func-
tional and clinical significance of the observed splicing alterations should be inter -
preted cautiously.
Although the splicing patterns observed in the minigene assays cannot fully reca-
pitulate endogenous splicing regulation in disease-relevant tissues, they provide 
mechanistic insight into the potential effects of multiple TNXB variants on gene 
expression. In this study, intron 40 retention was observed in association with specific 
combinations of TNXB variants in family members showing connective tissue mani-
festations. However, because the clinical phenotype was not specific to clEDS and 
the proportion of aberrant transcripts was relatively low, the biological and clinical 
significance of these splicing alterations remains uncertain. Nevertheless, these find-
ings suggest that intron retention may represent one possible mechanism contributing 
to abnormal TNXB splicing in individuals with suspected clEDS-like phenotypes. 
Further studies using disease-relevant tissues and larger cohorts will be necessary 
to clarify the relationship between these variants, splicing alterations, and clinical 
manifestations.
Conclusions
In conclusion, we identified multiple homozygous TNXB variants, including 
c.12,210 + 5G > A, that may influence TNXB splicing and may represent part of a 
population-specific haplotype in East Asian populations. Although their clinical sig-

plexity of genotype–phenotype relationships in suspected clEDS. Our findings also
