# Patients with MMAF induced by novel biallelic CFAP43 mutations have good fertility outcomes after intracytoplasmic sperm injection.
**Authors:** Ma J, Long SH, Yu HB, Xiang YZ, Tang XR, Li JX, Liu WW, Han W, Jin R, Huang GN, Lin TT
**Journal:** Asian J Androl (2023)
**DOI:** [10.4103/aja2022118](https://doi.org/10.4103/aja2022118)

## Content

As a specific type of asthenoteratozoospermia, multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella (MMAF) is characterized by composite abnormalities, including absent, short, coiled, angulation, and irregular-caliber flagella. Mutations in cilia- and flagella-associated protein 43 ( CFAP43 ) are one of the main causative factors of MMAF established to date. To identify whether there are other CFAP43 mutations related to MMAF and to determine the clinical outcomes of assisted reproductive technology for patients with MMAF harboring different mutations, we recruited and screened 30 MMAF-affected Chinese men using a 22-gene next-generation sequencing panel. After systematic analysis, seven mutations in CFAP43 , including five novel mutations and two previously reported mutations, were identified from four families and related to MMAF in an autosomal recessive pattern. Papanicolaou staining, immunofluorescence, and electronic microscopy further clarified the semen characteristics and abnormal sperm morphologies, including disorganized axonemal and peri-axonemal structures, of the CFAP43 -deficient men. The female partners of two patients were pregnant after undergoing assisted reproductive technology through intracytoplasmic sperm injection, and one of them successfully gave birth to a healthy boy. This study significantly expands the mutant spectrum of CFAP43 , and together with the available information regarding male infertility and MMAF, provides new information for the genetic diagnosis and counseling of MMAF in the future.

Infertility is a global health issue that affects 10.0%–15.0% of couples at reproductive age worldwide.1,2 Male factors, including abnormal sperm count, reduced sperm motility, and/or abnormal sperm morphology, account for approximately 50.0% of infertile cases generally.3,4 Multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella (MMAF) is a special form of asthenoteratozoospermia that presents with severely impaired sperm motility and mosaic patterns of abnormal spermatozoa with absent, short, coiled, angulation, and irregular-caliber flagella.5,6 The flagellar defects observed by light microscopy in MMAF-affected patients are usually induced by ultrastructural abnormalities of axonemal and peri-axonemal structures, such as the absence of central pair (CP) resulting in a “9 + 0” arrangement of dynein microtubules.5,7

MMAF is primarily caused by genetic mutations, and an increasing number of investigations into the genetic etiology of MMAF have used high-throughput genetic testing via gene panels or whole-exome sequencing (WES).7–9 To date, recurrent mutations have been identified in more than 20 MMAF-associated genes, including the cilia- and flagella-associated genes (CFAPs; e.g., CFAP43, CFAP44, CFAP58, CFAP65, CFAP69, CFAP70, CFAP74, and CFAP251), the dynein axonemal heavy chain family of genes (DNAHs; e.g., DNAH1, DNAH2, DNAH6, DNAH8, and DNAH17), and other genes expressed in sperm axonemal and peri-axonemal structures, such as armadillo repeat containing 2 (ARMC2), adenylate kinase 7 (AK7), tetratricopeptide repeat domain 21A (TTC21A), fibrous sheath-interacting protein 2 (FSIP2), glutamine-rich 2 (QRICH2), tetratricopeptide repeat domain 29 (TTC29), sperm flagellar 2 (SPEF2), DAZ-interacting zinc finger protein 1 (DZIP1), and ciliogenesis-associated TTC17-interacting protein (CATIP).5,7–12 As an evolutionarily conserved organelle, sperm flagella contains more than 1000 types of proteins, resulting in a highly heterogeneous etiology of MMAF.13 The currently identified mutations are responsible for approximately 60.0%–75.0% of MMAF-affected cases, while the remaining cases (25.0%–40.0% of MMAF patients) have unknown genetic factors.7,9,14

The CFAP43 gene (also known as WD repeat domain 96 [WDR96], Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man [OMIM]: 617558), located in chromosome 10q25.1, has 38 coding exons for an inferred 1665-amino acid protein that belongs to CFAPs with an N-terminal WD40 domain and C-terminal structural maintenance of chromosomes (SMC)_N domain.10 The CFAP43 protein is mainly expressed in human testis and is associated with the inner dynein arm complex tether/tether head.10,15,16 Animal model studies demonstrated that male Cfap43-null mice are sterile and exhibit peri-axonemal and axonemal defects in sperm flagella.10,17,18 Biallelic mutations of CFAP43 were found to account for approximately 3.7%–30.8% of studied human MMAF cohorts.10,12,15–17,19,20 Previous studies also found that patients with MMAF and CFAP43 mutations have a good prognosis for fertility following intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI).9,21,22 However, whether there are other mutations in CFAP43 associated with MMAF, and the ICSI outcomes of patients carrying different mutations need to be further explored in larger studies.

The present study recruited 30 Chinese families with infertile men exhibiting MMAF and receiving treatment in the Center for Reproductive Medicine, Women and Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Chongqing, China). Through genetic screening with a 22-gene next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel and Sanger sequencing, four loss-of-function mutations (c.585_735del, p.R195Sfs*33; c.944del, p.G315Afs*22; c.4579dup, p.S1527Ffs*10; and c.3768+1G>A) and three missense mutations (c.386C>A, p.S129Y; c.1418C>T, p.S473L; and c.2546T>C, p.L849P) in CFAP43, including five novel mutations and two previously reported recurrent mutations, were identified in four unrelated families. In addition, we examined abnormalities in semen parameters and sperm morphology and the outcomes of assisted reproductive technology (ART) with ICSI. These findings greatly extend the mutant spectrum of CFAP43 and provide new knowledge for the genetic counseling of infertile men with MMAF in the future.

The 30 patients with MMAF were recruited from the Center for Reproductive Medicine, Women and Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, from August 2019 to June 2022. They were diagnosed with primary male infertility and abnormalities in sperm flagella morphology. The chromosomal karyotypes of all patients were normal (46,XY), and no Y chromosome deletion was detected. Before collecting peripheral blood and seminal fluid, each participant provided signed informed consent, and blood samples were obtained from family members when possible. This study was approved by the Clinical Application and Ethics Committee of Human Assisted Reproductive Technology of Chongqing Health Center for Women and Children (Chongqing, China; Approval No. 2022-RGI-05).

A QIAamp® DNA blood mini kit (69504, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used to extract genomic DNA from peripheral blood samples. An NGS panel of 22 genes was performed for genetic testing. The list of genes was as follows: CFAP44 (OMIM: 617559), CFAP69 (OMIM: 617949), ARMC2 (OMIM: 618424), CFAP43 (OMIM: 617558), AK7 (OMIM: 615364), TTC21A (OMIM: 611430), CFAP251 (OMIM: 618146), FSIP2 (OMIM: 615796), DNAH1 (OMIM: 603332), QRICH2 (OMIM: 618304), CFAP65 (OMIM: 614270), DNAH8 (OMIM: 603337), CFAP47 (OMIM: 301057), TTC29 (OMIM: 618735), CFAP91 (OMIM: 609910), SPEF2 (OMIM: 610172), CFAP70 (OMIM: 618661), CFAP58 (OMIM: 619129), DZIP1 (OMIM: 608671), DNAH2 (OMIM: 603333), DNAH17 (OMIM: 610063), and CATIP (OMIM: 619387). The entire DNA sequence of these genes, and the exon–intron regions, were captured and sequenced with a mean depth of more than 200×.

The CFAP43 mutations in the patients with MMAF were validated by Sanger sequencing and pedigree analysis. The primers for PCR are shown in Supplementary Table 1. After purification on 2.0% agarose gels, the amplification products were sequenced with ABI 3500 (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) and analyzed with Chromas 2.6.5 (Technelysium Pvt. Ltd., South Brisbane, Australia). The VarSome (https://varsome.com/),23 Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD; http://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk/ac/index.php), and the gnomAD database (http://gnomad-sg.org/) were searched for previously reported mutations and the frequencies of mutations in different populations. The potential pathogenicity of all identified mutations was evaluated by pedigree analysis and in silico analysis using VarCards (http://varcards.biols.ac.cn/).24 The guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) were followed in annotating the clinical significance of mutations.25

The amino acid sequence and name of mutant CFAP43 proteins were analyzed with Name Checker (https://mutalyzer.nl/name-checker). CFAP43 amino acid sequences from different species were compared using the Clustal Omega (http://www.clustal.org/omega/) and analyzed with ESPript 3.0 (https://espript.ibcp.fr/ESPript/cgi-bin/ESPript.cgi). Diagram images of CFAP43 protein were modeled by Illustrator for Biological Sequences (IBS; http://ibs.biocuckoo.org/online.php). Wild-type and CFAP43 mutant proteins were assessed using the SWISS-MODEL software (https://swissmodel.expasy.org) and mapped to the atomic model using PyMOL (http://www.pymol.org) based on Q8NDM7.

Total RNA was extracted from blood and semen samples using the QIAamp RNA blood mini kit (52304, Qiagen), and then reverse transcription and amplification were performed through the PrimeScript RT-PCR kit (RR014A, Takara, Kyoto, Japan), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The forward primer (5´-AAACCTGATGCTGTGTGGACT-3´) used for cDNA amplification was located at exon 27, while the reverse primer (5´-GAAATCCTTGGTCGGCGTTT-3´) was located at exon 31. Then, the products were purified on 2.0% agarose gels, ligated with T-vector, and analyzed on ABI 3500.

After 2–7 days of abstinence, seminal fluid was collected via masturbation into a sterile container and analyzed in the source laboratories following the World Health Organization guidelines (the 5th edition published in 201026 and the 6th edition published in 202127). In brief, within 1 h of ejaculation, the liquefied samples were analyzed for semen volume, sperm concentration, round cells, normal morphology, and sperm motility via a computer-aided sperm analysis system (Jiangsu Rich Life Science Instrument Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Asthenozoospermia was defined as reduced motility or absent sperm motility (progressive motility <32.0%) in the fresh ejaculation. Teratospermia referred specifically to the condition in which the frequency of sperm with normal morphology was below the lower reference limit of 4.0%. Asthenozoospermia and teratospermia may exist at the same time, which is collectively called asthenoteratozoospermia.

The semen samples were stained with modified Papanicolaou staining (Cariad Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Zhuhai, China). Briefly, the semen slides were first air-dried at room temperature and then fixed with 95.0% ethanol for 3 min. Afterward, the slides were submerged in hematoxylin for 3 min, acidic ethanol for 5 s, and eosin and bright green for 3 min. Washing was performed between each step of staining. After thorough drying, more than 200 stained sperm were assessed for the morphological statistical analysis by light microscopy (TCS SP8, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) according to the World Health Organization guidelines.26,27 The morphology of sperm flagellum was then divided into normal, absent, short, coiled, angulation, and irregular caliber. The observations and evaluations were repeated twice.

Human spermatozoa were smeared onto slides and fixed with 4.0% paraformaldehyde. Then, the slides were treated with 0.1% Triton X-100 (X100, Sigma, Shanghai, China) for 30 min, incubated with a monoclonal antibody for α-TUBULIN (1:500 dilution; F2168, Sigma) at 4°C overnight, and stained with 4´-6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; P0131, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, samples were observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (TCS SP8, Leica).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were performed as previously reported.20,28 Briefly, sperm from control participants and patients were collected and fixed in phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) comprising 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4°C overnight. The prepared samples for SEM were sputter coated by an ionic sprayer meter (ACE200, Leica) and observed under the SEM (Nova NanoSEM 450, FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA) at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. For TEM, the samples were dehydrated in graded alcohol and embedded in Epon 812 (90529-77-4, SPI, West Chester, PA, USA). Ultrathin (70 nm) sections were stained with lead citrate and uranyl acetate and then observed with the TEM (TECNAI-10, Philips, Eindhoven, The Netherlands) at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV.

Of the four patients with CFAP43 mutations confirmed by molecular diagnosis, two progressed with ART through ICSI to conceive offspring at our reproductive center. Ovarian stimulation was conducted based on the ovarian reserve of female partners as previously reported.29 After insemination, embryos were cultured in a time-lapse monitoring system (Embryoscope Plus, Vitrolife, Kungsbacka, Sweden) for limiting the embryos’ exposure to sub-optimal conditions. The embryos were cultured in a G1 medium (509776, Vitrolife) from day 1 to day 3 and then cultured in a G2 medium (509698, Vitrolife) from day 3 to day 5 or day 6 for blastocyst formation. The embryos were assessed on day 3 based on the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) consensus.30 Blastocyst quality was evaluated on day 5 and day 6 according to previously reported criteria.31

Four Chinese male patients with MMAF (Family 1-II-1, Family 2-II-1, Family 3-II-1, and Family 4-II-1 are abbreviated as F1-II-1, F2-II-1, F3-II-1, and F4-II-1, respectively) were identified to have homozygous or compound heterozygous CFAP43 (ENST00000357060.8) mutations with an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern (Figure 1a). No causative mutations were found in other tested genes. A total of seven different mutations in CFAP43 were identified, including four loss-of-function mutations (c.585_735del, p.R195Sfs*33; c.944del, p.G315Afs*22; c.4579dup, p.S1527Ffs*10; and c.3768+1G>A) and three missense mutations (c.386C>A, p.S129Y; c.1418C>T, p.S473L; and c.2546T>C, p.L849P), as shown in Figure 1b. Among these seven mutations, one (c.4579dup, p.S1527Ffs*10) was homozygous from a consanguineous family, while others were heterozygous. The mutation of c.585_735del was induced by the deletion of exon 5. In silico analysis suggested that all the mutations disrupted the function of CFAP43, as predicted by Polymorphism Phenotyping (PolyPhen-2), Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant (SIFT), MutationTaster, and Combined Annotation-Dependent Depletion (CADD), and that they all have extremely rare frequencies (<10−4) or were absent in the gnomAD database (Table 1). Moreover, five mutations were novel and had not been previously reported in patients with MMAF.

A detailed description of physical characteristics and semen parameters of the CFAP43-deficient patients is presented in Table 2. The four CFAP43-deficient patients were aged between 26 years and 32 years, and the semen volumes and pH were all within the normal ranges published by the World Health Organization guidelines (the 5th edition published in 201026 and the 6th edition published in 202127). However, two patients (F3-II-1 and F4-II-1) had severe oligozoospermia (sperm concentration ≤5 × 106 ml−1) with sperm concentrations significantly lower than the normal range. A severe loss of sperm motility was observed in all patients, especially progressive motility (PR < 5.0%). As an atypical patient with MMAF, the F3-II-1 exhibited 13.0% nonprogressive motility.

Several abnormalities of sperm flagella, including absent, short, coiled, angulation, and irregular caliber, were observed during sperm morphological analysis, representing typical characteristics of MMAF (Figure 1c). The main abnormal types were absent (59.0%, 37.4%, and 42.0% for F1-II-1, F3-II-1, and F4-II-1, respectively) and short (26.0%, 23.2%, and 23.9% for F1-II-1, F3-II-1, and F4-II-1, respectively). Moreover, the abnormalities of sperm flagella were heterogeneous, and F3-II-1 and F4-II-1 showed several elongated sperm flagella visible by light microscopy, while spermatozoa with normal-appearing flagella were absent in patient F1-II-1 (Figure 1d and Table 2).

To further investigate the effect of c.3768+1G>A localizing in intron 29–30 in F4-II-1, we performed RT-PCR using primers specific to exon 27 and exon 31 for alternative splicing analysis. The results of Sanger sequencing showed that compared with the normal splicing between exons 28, 29, 30, and 31, c.3768+1G>A induced two kinds of alternative splicing in F4-II-1, including the skip of exon 29 or the concurrent skip of exons 29 and 30 (Figure 2a and Supplementary Figure 1). The two kinds of alternative splicing produced the mutant CFAP43 proteins of p.E1221_K1256del and p.E1221_K1285del, respectively.

The missense mutations identified in this study were all localized at highly conserved protein domains (Figure 2b). In addition, the effect of missense mutations was assessed via the three-dimensional structure of the CFAP43 protein, which was displayed by the PyMOL program (Figure 2b). The mutations of p.S129Y and p.S473L occurred in the loop region, while p.L849P was found in the α-helix region. These three mutations were predicted to lead to changes in hydrogen bonding that affected the structure and stability of the CFAP43 protein.

Moreover, we reviewed all CFAP43 mutations reported previously and in the present study (Figure 2c and Supplementary Table 2).10,12,15–17,19,20 More than 70.0% were loss-of-function mutations, including frameshift, splice-site loss, and nonsense mutations. The altered amino acids were distributed along the full length of CFAP43 protein without a mutational hotspot.

SEM and immunofluorescence were performed for additional phenotypic characterization. The spermatozoa from the control showed long, smooth tails and normally condensed heads, while spermatozoa obtained from men carrying biallelic CFAP43 mutations frequently exhibited clear and typical MMAF phenotypes (Figure 3a). Immunostaining of spermatozoa with α-TUBULIN antibody showed that α-TUBULIN was localized along the spermatozoa flagella from the control participant but was remarkably reduced in spermatozoa from men with biallelic CFAP43 mutations (F1-II-1 and F4-II-1; Figure 3b).

To further study the ultrastructure of spermatozoa flagella in men harboring biallelic CFAP43 mutations, samples from two CFAP43-deficient patients and control participants were processed for TEM analysis. The normal sperm had a symmetrical mid-piece with regular arrangement of mitochondria around a smooth axoneme (Figure 3c). Comparatively, a large cytoplasmic bag with unassembled axonemal and peri-axonemal components was presented in CFAP43-deficient sperm. In the control spermatozoa, a typical microtubule structure was observed with a “9 + 2” axonemal arrangement of nine microtubule doublets and the CP, surrounded by a fibrous sheath and outer dense fibers, as indicated in Figure 3d. Compared with the control spermatozoa, the axonemal cross-sections of CFAP43-deficient spermatozoa were often characterized by the loss of CP (“9 + 0” conformation) or severe disorganization of the fibrous sheath, outer dense fibers, and axonemal disassembly.

Two of the four patients (F3-II-1 and F4-II-1) harboring biallelic CFAP43 mutations underwent ART with ICSI. The numbers of metaphase II oocytes, fertilized oocytes (two pronuclei), cleaved embryos, and blastocysts are listed in Table 3. The rate of fertilization was 66.7% and 75.0%, the rate of embryo cleavage was 100.0% and 86.7%, and the rate of blastocyst formation was 41.7% and 46.2%. Following frozen-thawed embryo transfer, the female partner of F3-II-1 proband became pregnant and gave birth to a full-term healthy boy, while the partner of F4-II-1 proband was in a good state of clinical pregnancy.

The present study identified four families with MMAF-harboring biallelic mutations in CFAP43. Genetic screening with a panel of 22 MMAF-associated genes identified seven different mutations in CFAP43, including the compound heterozygous state of c.386C>A and c.944del in F1, c.1418C>T and c.585_735del in F2, and c.2546T>C and c.3768+1G>A in F4 and the homozygous state of c.4579dup in F3. Pedigree analysis, Sanger sequencing, and in silico analysis further confirmed the pathogenicity or likely pathogenicity of these mutations in a recessive pattern. The results of Papanicolaou staining, SEM, immunofluorescence, and TEM showed heterogeneity in the abnormalities of sperm flagella between different patients and severe disorganization of the axoneme. We also found that patients with MMAF and CFAP43 mutations had a favorable prospect for fertility after ICSI.

To date, 84 mutations have been found in the CFAP43 gene, of which 38 were pathogenic (22 pathogenic and 16 likely pathogenic; the data described were collected on VarSome as of July 11, 2022). More than 70.0% of CFAP43 mutations both previously reported and identified in this study were loss-of-function, which include frameshift (n=13), nonsense (n=7), and splice-site loss mutations (n=5).10,12,15–17,19,20 The splice-site loss mutations might cause exons skipping and induce frameshift of the CFAP43 protein. Coutton et al.19 first reported two splice-site loss mutations (c.2141+5G>A and c.3541-2A>C) in infertile patients with MMAF. Then, Li et al.32 demonstrated that the c.3661-2delA mutation might cause the skipping of exon 29 and generate the p.E1221_K1256del protein. In this study, the mutation c.3768+1G>A was found to cause the skipping of exon 29 or exons 29 and 30 in patient F4-II-1. Furthermore, 27 splice-site loss mutations of CFAP43 have been found in the gnomAD database. These findings may demonstrate that aberrant splicing is a frequently mutational mechanism in CFAP43-induced MMAF.

The identified mutations are distributed throughout CFAP43, indicating that the entirety of CFAP43 protein is essential for preserving its function. Previous studies provided support for the existence of a genotype–phenotype correlation where missense mutations present a potentially milder effect compared with the truncating mutations.19 In contrast, our patient with homozygous mutation of p.S1527Ffs*10 (F3-II-1) had the highest percentage of spermatozoa with morphologically normal flagella (22.9% versus 0.9% for F1-II-1 and 2.2% for F4-II-1). Moreover, typical MMAF characteristics were present with an increase in immotile spermatozoa count (98.0%–100.0%). In the present study, three of the four patients (F1-II-1, F2-II-1, and F4-II-1) exhibiting MMAF and harboring biallelic CFAP43 mutations showed no significant differences in semen parameters compared with previously reported patients.20 However, 13.0% of the spermatozoa obtained from F3-II-1 exhibited nonprogressive motility, even though the progressive motility was zero. This indicates that F3-II-1 is an atypical patient with MMAF with high heterogeneity in clinical manifestations.

In recent years, genomic investigation of the MMAF phenotype with NGS technology has advanced understanding in the genetic etiology and discovery of new pathogenic genes in patients with MMAF. According to all relevant studies to date, CFAP43 mutations were found to account for 3.7%–30.8% of patients with MMAF from different study cohorts.10,12,15–17,19,20 In this study, 4 (13.3%) out of 30 patients with MMAF were carrying CFAP43 mutations. Previous reports suggested that patients exhibiting MMAF and CFAP43 mutations have a favorable ICSI prognosis. Sha et al.21 reported that the rates of fertilization, pregnancy, and delivery were 83.3%, 100.0%, and 100.0%, respectively, for such patients. Our study further confirmed the good prospect of fertility following ICSI for patients with CFAP43 mutations and MMAF. So far, the reported findings account for approximately 60.0% to 75.0% of all MMAF-affected individuals, indicating the potential involvement of unknown genetic factors in approximately 25.0%–40.0% of patients.33,34

In conclusion, our study identified seven different mutations of pathogenicity or likely pathogenicity in CFAP43 related to MMAF phenomena. The results of our study indicated that patients with MMAF harboring CFAP43 mutations have a good prospect of fertility after ICSI. This study significantly extends the mutant spectrum of CFAP43 and provides researchers and clinicians with an improved understanding of the genetic etiology, thus improving the counseling of infertile men with MMAF in the future.
