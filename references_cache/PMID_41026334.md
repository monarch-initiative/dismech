# Novel Compound Heterozygous Mutations in HOIP Result in Autoinflammation and Immunodeficiency.
**Authors:** Wang L, Xiao J, Gan R, Tang X, Wu J
**Journal:** J Clin Immunol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10875-025-01939-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-025-01939-2)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: Mutations in the HOIP gene, encoding Heme-oxidized IRP2 ubiquitin ligase-1 (HOIL-1) interacting protein, a key component of the linear ubiquitination chain assembly complex (LUBAC), affect the activation of the NF-κB pathway and result in autoinflammation and immunodeficiency. To date, only three patients with HOIP mutations have been reported in the English literature. This study aimed to identify the genetic cause in a 1-year-6-month-old boy with early-onset autoinflammation and immunodeficiency.

METHODS: Clinical manifestations and immunological phenotypes were assessed in a Chinese patient with novel compound heterozygous mutations in HOIP. Swiss-PdbViewer was used to predict the pathogenicity of the mutations. HOIP and LUBAC protein levels were evaluated by western blot. Immunological phenotypes and intracellular NF-κB phosphorylation in T and B cells were analyzed by flow cytometry, and the interferon (IFN) signature was assessed using quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR).

RESULTS: The patient experienced recurrent fever, multiple site infections, and chronic diarrhea from the neonatal period, requiring repeated hospitalizations. Targeted gene sequencing identified novel compound heterozygous mutations in HOIP (c.1654 C > T, p.Gln552Ter; c.3038 A > C, p.His1013Pro). These mutations significantly reduced HOIP and LUBAC protein expression and altered the HOIP protein structure. Immunophenotyping revealed a reduction in CD8 + T cells, central memory (CD8 CM) and effector memory (CD8 EM) T cells, and CD4 + T cells. Subset analysis of CD4 + T cells showed decreased T follicular helper cells (Tfh) and increased IL-17-producing T helper (Th17) cells. The patient also exhibited a higher percentage of naïve B cells and a lower percentage of memory B cells, alongside impaired NF-κB phosphorylation in T and B cells upon LPS stimulation. The expression of the patient's interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) was markedly higher than that of healthy controls.

CONCLUSION: HOIP mutations may lead to multiple immune abnormalities, impaired NF-κB activation, and activation of the type I interferon pathway. These findings highlight the diversity of HOIP mutations and expand the clinical spectrum of associated diseases.

Nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) is pivotal in adaptive and innate immunity, inflammatory responses, and cell survival [1]. Its signaling pathway is tightly regulated by post-translational modifications, including ubiquitination [2]. HOIL-1 interacting protein (HOIP), also known as RING finger protein 31 (RNF31), is the catalytic subunit of the linear ubiquitination chain assembly complex (LUBAC) [3]. LUBAC, comprising HOIL-1, HOIP, and SHANK-associated RH domain interactor (SHARPIN) [4, 5]is the only known ubiquitin ligase responsible for forming linear (Met1-linked) ubiquitin chains. This complex is essential for NF-κB activation and regulation of inflammation and immunity [3, 6]. LUBAC facilitates linear polyubiquitination of IKBKG and RIPK1, contributing to the activation of the canonical NF-κB and JNK signaling pathways [3, 7].

Mutations in HOIP lead to decreased expression of HOIP and LUBAC proteins, destabilizing the entire complex. Patients with HOIP mutations present with systemic autoinflammation, recurrent viral and bacterial infections, and amylopectinosis [8]. To date, only three cases have been reported in the English literature [9–11]. The first case, reported in 2015, described a 19-year-old Kuwaiti woman with multiorgan autoinflammation, combined immunodeficiency, subclinical amylopectinosis, and systemic lymphangiectasia [9]. The second case, reported in 2019, involved an 8-year-old girl with early-onset immune deficiency and autoinflammation [10]. The third case, a 12-year-old Chinese boy diagnosed with common variable immunodeficiency (CVID), exhibited infectious mononucleosis, necrotizing lymphadenitis, sepsis, and recurrent bacterial infections [11].

Here, we present the clinical and immunological features of a Chinese boy with novel compound heterozygous HOIP mutations, the fourth reported case globally. Similar to the previous reported cases, the patient exhibited multiorgan infection and systemic inflammatory features but lacked amylopectinosis and lymphangiectasia. However, this patient presented with earlier and more severe symptoms and passed away before the age of two, despite intensive treatment. These findings further highlight the diversity of HOIP mutations, expanding the clinical spectrum of the disease and emphasizing the severity of the condition that can lead to early mortality.

The patient, a 1-year-6-month-old boy from a non-consanguineous family, was evaluated at the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University in June 2021. Informed consent was obtained from the guardians, and the study was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No.2021–138).

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed, and HOIP mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Conservation analysis was conducted using WebLogo.

The crystal structure of HOIP (PDB ID: 4LJQ) served as the template [12]and mutations were analyzed using the Swiss-model and DynaMut to predict the structural impact.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were analyzed by flow cytometry to assess T and B cell subsets under unstimulated condition. CD4 + T cell subsets, including circulating follicular regulatory T (Tfr) cells (CD3 + CD4 + CXCR5 + CD25 + CD127-), circulating follicular helper T (cTfh) cells (CD3 + CD4 + CXCR5 + CD45RO+), Th1 (CD3 + CD4 + CD45RA-CXCR5-CXCR3 + CCR6-), Th2 (CD3 + CD4 + CD45RA-CXCR5-CXCR3-CCR6-), Th17 (CD3 + CD4 + CD45RA-CXCR5-CXCR3-CCR6+), and regulatory T cells (Tregs) (CD4 + CD25 + Foxp3+) were also evaluated. Foxp3 was stained intracellularly using eBioscience™ Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer (Cat. 00-5523-00) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Protein lysates from patient and healthy donor PBMCs were subjected to SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting, as reported previously [13]. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-I-kappa-B alpha (IkBα) Polyclonal Antibody (Proteintech, USA), anti-SHARPIN Polyclonal Antibody (Proteintech, USA), anti-HOIL-1 Polyclonal Antibody (Proteintech, USA), anti-HOIP Polyclonal Antibody (Proteintech, USA) and anti-Vinculin Monoclonal Antibody (Proteintech, USA).

The intracellular phosphorylation of NF-κB was evaluated in T and B cells from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) using flow cytometry. First, T cells and B cells were stained for surface markers for 30 min. Cells were then stimulated with prepared stimulants (anti-human IgG and IgM at 10 µg/ml or LPS at 0.1 mg/L) at 37 °C for 0, 5, and 15 min. Following stimulation, cells were immediately fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After fixation, 90% methanol was applied for 10 min on ice to permeabilize the cells. The cells were then incubated with anti-human phospho-NF-κB antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

The IFN signature was measured using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) as described previously [14]. Briefly, Total RNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using the Blood Total RNA Miniprep Kit (Axygen). The expression of six interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs)—IFI27, ISG15, SIGLEC1, IFI44L, IFIT1, and RSAD2—was quantified by RT-qPCR. cDNA was synthesized from 40 ng of total RNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara). RT-qPCR was performed on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Touch machine, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method, with results expressed as fold induction relative to control samples. β-actin was used as the housekeeping gene, and five sex- and age-matched healthy controls were included for comparison.

All statistical analyses were performed by t-test using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

The proband (Fig. 1A, II2) was a male born to a healthy, non-consanguineous Chinese couple. He was delivered by Cesarean section at full term, weighing 2.8 kg, and was vaccinated with BCG at birth without adverse reactions. He initially presented with fever at 8 days of life, accompanied by elevated inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein(CRP) 57 mg/L). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analyses showed mild abnormalities: white blood cell count 10–28 × 10⁶/L (0–15), lymphocytes 87–92%, protein 0.66–0.79 g/L (0.15–0.45), and glucose 2.20–2.87 mmol/L (2.5–4.5). Blood and CSF cultures were negative for pathogens. Brain MRI revealed parenchymal inflammatory lesions near the frontal horns of bilateral lateral ventricles. Following a month of sequential antibiotic therapy (cefoperazone/sulbactam, meropenem + oxacillin, ceftriaxone, meropenem + vancomycin), the patient showed improved mental status with a 9-day afebrile period. However, recurrent fever persisted after discharge, primarily moderate-to-high grade with an irregular pattern, and the longest fever-free interval was 10 days. At 3 months of age, he developed gastrointestinal symptoms including abdominal distension and feeding difficulties. Given the early onset of symptoms, recurrent infections, failure to thrive, and elevated inflammatory markers, a primary immunodeficiency was suspected. Whole-exome sequencing revealed novel compound heterozygous mutations in the HOIP gene: a heterozygous nonsense mutation in exon 9 (c.1654 C > T, p.Gln552Ter) and a missense mutation in exon 20 (c.3038 A > C, p.His1013Pro). These mutations were absent in population databases such as ExAC, 1000 Genomes, HGMD, and gnomAD. Both mutations were confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Fig. 1H). The p.Gln552Ter mutation was predicted to be pathogenic, while the p.His1013Pro mutation was classified as a variant of uncertain significance by predictive tools like SIFT, PolyPhen-2, and MutationTaster. Both residues are highly conserved across species (Fig. 2A). Structural analysis suggested that the p.Gln552Ter mutation could truncate the protein, impacting its structure and function, while the p.His1013Pro mutation might introduce steric hindrance, potentially disrupting protein activity (Fig. 2B, C). Since LUBAC is a critical regulator of immune responses, we assessed NF-κB phosphorylation in T and B cells following stimulation with anti-human IgG and IgM (Fig. 4C) or LPS (Fig. 4D). In the patient’s cells, phosphorylation of NF-κB was significantly reduced compared to the healthy control (Fig. 4C–E).

Clinically diagnosed with HOIP deficiency at 15 months, the patient started a regimen of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (400-600 mg/kg monthly) and sulfamethoxazole (SMZ) for infection prophylaxis. At 19 months, following functional confirmation of HOIP deficiency, subcutaneous adalimumab (recommended10mg every 2 weeks) was started, though the pandemic disrupted regular dosing.The first adalimumab injection on October 9, 2021, sustained 25 days of afebrile status before recurrent fevers. A second dose on November 23 led to rapid defervescence and a significant drop in CRP(Fig. 1G). However, fever returned 24 days later, prompting a third injection on December 18 with similar response. After the fourth injection on January 5, 2022, the patient remained afebrile for 15 days until fever relapsed, the condition progressed rapidly this time, accompanied by seizures, vomiting, tachycardia, and tachypnea. Laboratory tests showed elevated inflammatory markers (WBC 17.42 × 10⁹/L, CRP 192.8 mg/L, PCT 77.9 ng/mL). Despite aggressive intervention, the patient’s condition worsened rapidly. Following the family’s withdrawal of resuscitation consent, the patient died within 24 h of admission. Two days later, blood culture results indicate the presence of Streptococcus pneumoniae.

Ubiquitin is a highly conserved, low molecular weight protein (comprising 76 amino acids) that is widely expressed in cells and plays a key role in multiple cellular functions via its attachment to various substrate proteins through the ubiquitin conjugation system [15]. Ubiquitin is covalently attached to its substrates or itself through the action of three enzymes: a ubiquitin-activating enzyme (E1), a ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (E2), and a ubiquitin ligase (E3) [16]. The linear ubiquitin chain assembly complex (LUBAC) is a multiprotein E3 ligase complex that assembles a distinct ubiquitin signal, referred to as ‘linear’ or ‘M1-linked’ ubiquitin chains [3]. These linear chains play a crucial role in regulating inflammation, immune responses, infection, and cell death signaling pathways [3, 17, 18]. LUBAC-mediated canonical NF-κB signaling regulates the expression of genes critical for cell survival, proliferation, pro-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic pathways [19, 20]. Consequently, mutations in LUBAC components, such as HOIP, can lead to various disease states.

In this study, we report a patient with novel compound heterozygous mutations in HOIP. The patient presented with early-onset recurrent high fever, multiple site infections, failure to thrive, and elevated inflammatory markers. Targeted gene sequencing identified two novel mutations in the HOIP gene: c.1654 C > T (p.Gln552Ter) and c.3038 A > C (p.His1013Pro). Both Gln552 and His1013 residues are highly conserved. Structural analysis indicated that the p.His1013Pro mutation results in the substitution of a hydrophilic residue (His1013) with a hydrophobic one (Pro1013), likely causing steric hindrance with the nearby Leu1009 residue, potentially affecting HOIP function. Our data also revealed reduced expression of HOIP, HOIL-1, and SHARPIN proteins in the patient’s peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) [9–11]. Similar findings were observed in animal models, where HOIP deficiency led to reduced HOIL-1 and SHARPIN levels [21, 22]. Together, these results suggest that HOIP mutations destabilize the LUBAC complex.

HOIP deficiency presents with a wide range of clinical symptoms, often including immunodeficiency and autoinflammation, though specific manifestations vary across patients. We conducted a literature review of HOIP cases reported through January 2025, systematically summarizing their genetic profiles, clinical manifestations, and treatment strategies in Table 2. When compared with the three previously reported patients with HOIP deficiency, the patient in this case had the earliest onset (fever started 8 days after birth), more severe inflammatory manifestations, and more extensive and severe infections. Although the first reported patient(case 1) also developed symptoms in the neonatal period with recurrent fever, his CRP level was only mildly elevated. In contrast, our patient experienced persistent recurrent fever with the longest afebrile interval of 10 days and significantly elevated CRP from the onset until the use of subcutaneous adalimumab at 19 months (Fig. 1G). Similar to cases 2 and 3, our patient had repeated bacterial infections presenting as CVID, but with more affected sites (respiratory, nervous, gastrointestinal, urinary tracts, suppurative lymphadenitis, etc.) and more severe infections requiring multiple ICU admissions. Compared with previously reported cases, this patient did not exhibit arthritis, lymphangiectasia, myasthenia, etc., possibly due to the young age.Differences in clinical severity and manifestations may be linked to the location and nature of the HOIP mutations. This underscores the need for further exploration into the genotype-phenotype correlation of HOIP gene mutations, while highlighting the importance of early diagnosis and timely intervention in managing such cases.

LUBAC consists of three components: SHARPIN, HOIL-1, and HOIP, all of which are involved in immune regulation. HOIP and HOIL-1, both members of the RBR ubiquitin ligase family, function by attaching ubiquitin to substrates [23, 24]. Most patients with LUBAC deficiency display clinical features of immune dysregulation [25, 26]with severe bacterial and viral infections being common due to low memory B cell counts. In this case, the patient experienced recurrent infections involving multiple sites and pathogens. This susceptibility pattern resembles classical innate immune deficiencies (e.g., MyD88, IRAK-4, or TLR defects), in which impaired TLR/IL-1R-NF-κB signaling compromises defense against diverse pathogens [27, 28].Impaired NF-κB signaling has been documented in patients with HOIP deficiency. Previous studies have shown that TNF-α-stimulated PBMCs from HOIP-deficient patients exhibit reduced phosphorylation and degradation of IκBα [9]. Our data are consistent with these findings, as LPS-stimulated B and T cells from our patient displayed reduced NF-κB phosphorylation. In animal studies, LUBAC-mediated linear ubiquitination has been shown to be crucial for TLR4-mediated NF-κB activation [21].Mechanistically, delayed IκBα degradation (Fig. 2E) and reduced NF-κB phosphorylation disrupt cytokine production (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) and neutrophil recruitment—critical processes for clearing diverse pathogens [29]. Thus, HOIP deficiency may represent a novel etiology of TLR-NF-κB pathway defects, partially explaining the patient’s recurrent infections.

Notably, during the patient’s final hospitalization, the disease progressed rapidly to death. Although blood culture ultimately identified Streptococcus pneumoniae, the patient’s manifestations of hyperinflammatory response syndrome (explosive elevation of inflammatory markers, multi-system dysfunction, etc.) indicate that infection-triggered hyperinflammation, rather than the infection itself, was likely the primary cause of death. Linear ubiquitination is a key regulator of inflammatory signaling pathways, yet its substrates and biological significance remain incompletely understood. The second reported HOIP-deficient patient reported increased expression of type I interferon-regulated genes and exhibited elevated expression of phosphorylated STAT1 in T cells after IFN-α stimulation, instead of monocyte cells [10]. In our patient, we also observed an increased type I interferon signature, but the exact mechanisms by which HOIP mutations affect interferon production remain to be elucidated and more importantly the specific mechanisms by which HOIP mutations induce autoinflammation urgently warrant further investigation.

In our patient, detailed immunophenotyping revealed a significant reduction in CD8 + T cells, particularly in effector and central memory T cell subsets (Fig. 4A), which may impair cytotoxic responses, increasing vulnerability to viral/fungal infections. Further analysis of CD4 + T cell subsets showed a decrease in T follicular helper (Tfh) cells and an increase in Th17 cells. Tfh cells are essential for humoral immunity, facilitating high-affinity antibody production via interactions with B cells [30, 31]. Decreased cTfh cells compromise germinal center function, while expanded Th17 cells may drive mucosal inflammation (e.g., chronic diarrhea). However, it remains unclear whether the deficiency in circulating Tfh cells in our patient was directly caused by HOIP mutations. Besides, naïve B cell accumulation and memory B cell depletion (Table 1) limit antibody diversity, consistent with hypogammaglobulinemia in prior cases 15.Previous research suggests that LUBAC is not involved in the B cell receptor-mediated pathway or the noncanonical NF-κB pathway [32]. Thus, whether LUBAC contributes to BCR-mediated signaling remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

In conclusion, we describe a Chinese patient with novel compound heterozygous HOIP mutations, who presented with early-onset severe infection, recurrent fever, and autoinflammation, ultimately leading to early death. This case expands the clinical spectrum associated with HOIP mutations and underscores the need for early diagnosis and treatment. Comprehensive immunological assessments suggest that HOIP mutations can affect multiple lymphocyte subsets, although the pathogenesis of immunodeficiency in such patients remains poorly understood and requires further research.
