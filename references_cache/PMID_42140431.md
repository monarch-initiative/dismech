# Disease associated missense substitutions disrupt structural stability and catalytic function of 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase.
**Authors:** Huang CW, Ni MY, Huang WM, Huang HC, Hsu YC, Huang KL, Chen CH, Hwang CC, Chang YL, Lee HJ
**Journal:** J Biol Chem (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jbc.2026.113158](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbc.2026.113158)

## Content

4-Hydroxylphenylpyruvate dioxygenase (HPPD) is a crucial enzyme in the tyrosine catabolic pathway, catalyzing the conversion of 4-hydroxylphenylpyruvate (HPP) to homogentisate (HG). Missense substitutions in HPPD are associated with type III tyrosinemia and hawkinsinuria. This study investigated disease-related variants in terms of their roles in HPPD structure stability and function. Our whole-cell assay showed a loss of soluble protein expression for G154S, Y160C, and I267F variants, suggesting that the three locations at the domain interface can be critical for proper protein folding. The A33T, A268V, and I335M variants exhibited low soluble protein expression and reduced bioactivity, indicating the three locations at their specific structural motifs affect protein folding but can be less effective. The biochemical analysis found that the N241S variant underwent an uncoupled reaction, forming an oxepinone intermediate that reacts with cysteine and forms the hawkinsin adduct. The A33T and V212M variants, which produced no intermediate product, exhibited similar substrate binding affinity as WT enzymes, but they had decreased structural stability and HG production (aligned with bioassay findings). The reduced structural stability and HG production, and the loss of substrate binding with HPPD-Co(II) complex for the A268V variant suggested that its location is related to the stable conformation of the metal binding motif. The reduced substrate binding affinity and catalytic efficiency for V340L variant suggested the effect at the active site entrance. This study showed the molecular underpinnings of how disease-related substitutions at specific structural locations affect the structural stability and function of HPPD.

Inherited mutations in genes responsible for tyrosine catabolism can result in metabolic disorders, such as tyrosinemia, hawkinsinuria, and alkaptonuria (1, 2, 3). Among them, mutations in the HPD gene, which encodes HPP dioxygenase (HPPD, EC 1.13.11.27), may cause tyrosinemia type III and hawkinsinuria (3). HPPD catalyzes the oxidation of HPP to homogentisate (HG), marking the first step in the tyrosine catabolic pathway (Fig. 1) (4, 5). Tyrosinemia type III is an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by an increased blood tyrosine level, leading to the urinary excretion of tyrosine metabolites, such as HPP, 4-hydroxyphenyllactatic acid, and 4-hydroxyphenylacetic acid (HPA) (6). Hawkinsinuria is an autosomal dominant metabolic disorder (7, 8). This condition is characterized by the urinary excretion of hawkinsin [(2-cystein-S-yl-1,4-dihydroxycyclohex-5-en-1-yl)-acetic acid] and cis- and trans-hydroxycyclohexylacetic acid (9, 10). Both disorders can lead to metabolic acidosis, mental retardation, and growth failure during infancy; however, these symptoms are transient and can be alleviated through a diet low in phenylalanine and tyrosine (8, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15).Figure 1Tyrosine catabolic pathway. The human HPPD protein is dimeric, consisting of two domains in each monomer (Fig. 2) (16). The active site is located in the C-terminal domain. Although the N-terminal domain is known to be associated with the stability of the dimer interface, its entire function in HPPD remains unclear (4, 5, 17). HPPD belongs to the nonheme Fe(II)/2-oxoacid-dependent oxygenase family (18, 19). Structurally, the ferrous ion in HPPD is coordinated by a 2-His-1-carboxylate facial triad, which is a conserved motif buried in a β-barrel structure at the active site (16, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25). The respective coordination sites of these ligands with the ferrous ion indicate their individual roles in the function of HPPD (26). The resolved crystal structure and simulated model of HPPD have revealed different substrate binding modes at the active site that are present either in an open or closed conformation (28, 29, 30). In both modes, the carboxyl and α-keto groups of HPP form bidentate coordination with the ferrous ion. However, the orientation of the aromatic side chain varies. This difference affects the interaction of the 4-hydroxyl group of HPP with the side chains of Gln251 and Gln265 and Asn363 on the C-terminal helix, as observed in the closed and opened conformation of the active site, respectively (27, 29, 30). In the closed conformation of the active site, the bound substrate is stabilized in an optimal position by an H-bond network (formed by Gln334, Glu349, and Asn363) and interacts with Gln251, resulting in a nucleophilic reaction by the oxygen activated at the keto group of HPP (27, 30, 31, 32, 33). Oxidative decarboxylation leads to the generation of HPA and a highly reactive Fe(IV)-oxo intermediate (5, 32, 34, 35). HPA is stabilized by the H-bond network, preparing it for an electrophilic reaction with the Fe(IV)-oxo intermediate (27, 36). Subsequently, the aromatic ring of HPA is hydroxylated, followed by the migration of the acetyl group to form the HG product (32, 34, 37). Site-directed mutagenesis, steady or pre-steady state kinetic analysis, and computational studies have proposed intermediates involved in the catalytic cycle and elucidated structural and functional relationships observed during HPPD catalysis (26, 27, 28, 30, 31, 36, 38, 39, 40).

Genetic variants in clinical patients with type III tyrosinemia and hawkinsinurea have been identified, demonstrating either truncation due to stop substitution or missense substitution in the HPD gene (3, 13, 15, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46). Although proteins resulting from truncation are assumed to be inactive, the effects of missense substitutions on the structure and function of HPPD remain largely unclear (28, 43). Using various computational tools, a study examined the disease susceptibility of all missense single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) in the HPD gene by employing data retrieved from the Ensembl and OMIM databases (47). The study identified 30 out of 152 missense SNVs that were consistently predicted to be the most deleterious. Among them, A33T, Y160C, I335M, and A268V have been discovered to be associated with hawkinsinurea or type III tyrosinemia (3, 13, 15, 41, 42, 43, 44). Although these highly detrimental SNVs are believed to alter the structural stability and function of the proteins, experimental studies are yet to determine the effects of these substitutions (47).

Studies have analyzed the in vitro function of the heterozygous missense variant N241S using the equivalent mutation in the Streptomyces avermitilis and rat genes, which is believed to be responsible for hawkinsinurea (15, 48). The purified enzyme variant displayed the ability to produce the quinolacetic acid (QAA) intermediate instead of the HG product. This process involves an uncoupled reaction in which HPP is converted into the QAA intermediate, which subsequently reacts with cellular thiols through Michael addition to form hawkinsin (Fig. 1) (48, 49). The activity of the N241S variant can be inhibited by the herbicide 2-(2-nitro-4-trifluoromethylbenzoyl)-1,3-cyclohexanedione, indicating its potential as a therapeutic agent for hawkinsinurea (48, 50). However, the molecular basis of other identified disease-related variants and their effects on the structure and function of HPPD remain largely unexplored.

Clinically reported missense mutations resulting in amino acid substitutions, including the variants A33T, G154S, Y160C, V212M, N241S, I267F, A268V, I335M, and V340L, have been identified to be either homozygous or heterozygous in affected patients (Table 1) (3, 15, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 51). Among them, A33T, G154S, Y160C, V212M, N241S, I335M, and V340L were defined as SNVs (52). However, I267F and A268V variants were not included in the SNP database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp). This study examined the R113Q variant, which is a polymorphic mutation without any clinically reported cases (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp/rs1833399). Despite only approximately 19% similarity, these substituted residues are highly conserved in amino acid sequences across diverse species (Fig. S1). In the structural model, these residues are located in the N-terminal domain facing a structural motif (Ala33), domain interface (Arg113), or a long loop connecting the N- to C-terminal domain (Gly154 and Tyr160); in the C-terminal domain facing the domain interface (Ile267 and Val340) or flanking α-helix (Ile335); or in the active site (Val212, Asn241, and Ala268) (Fig. 2) (3, 13, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45). This study examined the effects of these disease-related substitutions on the structure and function of HPPD by performing whole-cell assays in Escherichia coli cells and human cell lines, and biochemical analyses of recombinant protein variants. Our results revealed that these substitutions at specific structural locations affect bioactivity by reducing soluble protein expression and structural stability. Computational methods involving the use of the online tools of PolyPen-2, SIFT, Alphamissense and ThermoMPNN, and the Discovery Studio software were used to evaluate the effects of the functions and structural stability of polymorphic substitutions on HPPD (53, 54, 55, 56). The results of different prediction methods revealed a consistent unfavorable effect on the function and structural stability for V212M, N241S and A268V variants (Table 1) (3, 15, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45). However, conflicting results between different prediction methods were obtained for A33T, R113 Q, G154S, Y160C, I267F, I335M, and V340L variants. Although computational methods predicted the effects of polymorphic substitutions, molecular studies are necessary to determine the effects of disease-related variants on the structural stability and function of HPPD.

To determine the expression level of HPPD and the bioactivity of its genetic variant in vivo, the mutants of A33T, R113Q, G154S, Y160C, V212M, N241S, I267F, A268V, I335M, and V340L were constructed through site-directed mutagenesis and expressed in E. coli cells (Table S1). We performed a whole-cell colorimetric assay to examine the formation of a brownish pigment in the culture medium of E. coli cells harboring cloned WT and mutant HPPD (57, 58). The brownish pigment observed for the WT enzyme in the culture medium disappeared in the presence of sulcotrione, indicating the bioactivity of HPPD (Fig. 3A). Quantification of the brownish pigment by measuring absorbance at 410 nm indicated that the bioactivities of the mutants A33T, R113Q, G154S, Y160C, V212M, N241S, I267F, A268V, I335M, and V340L were decreased by approximately 4-, 2-, 60-, 27-, 3-, 10-, 52-, 44-, 5-, and 2-fold to that of the WT enzyme, respectively (Fig. 3B). The differences were computed by subtracting the absorbance of the WT enzyme in the presence of the inhibitor. The HPPD protein expression level in cultured cells was analyzed through Western blotting. According to the observed band intensity, the HPPD protein expression levels of the mutants R113Q and V340L were similar to that of the WT enzyme (Figs. 3C and S2A). Compared with the WT enzyme, the mutants A33T V212M, N241S, A268V, and I335M exhibited reductions of approximately 80%, 10%, 30%, 70%, and 90%, respectively, in the HPPD protein expression level. No significant soluble protein was detected for G154S, Y160C, and I267F variants under the same culture conditions, supporting the bioactivity lost (Fig. 3B). The results suggest that the substitution at the domain interface (G154S, Y160C and I267F) or specific structural motif (A33T, A268V and I335M) might impact protein folding process, potentially reducing soluble protein production (Fig. 2).

To confirm the effect of substitution on protein expression level, the genes of human HPPD WT and variants were transfected into human HEK293T cells. The protein expression level was analyzed using the Western blotting. It showed the similar protein expression level of V212M and N241S variants as WT protein, and no detectable soluble protein for A33T, Y160C, and A268V variants (Figs. 3D and S2B). The result is consistent with that observed for the same variant genes expressed in E. coli cultured cells (Fig. 3C). The mRNA level of these variant genes was analyzed by RT-PCR (Figs. 3E and S2C). The similar expression level for these variants as WT indicated that the mRNA expression of HPPD was not affected by substitution. It further demonstrated the effect of substitution on the protein expression level of the enzyme.

The findings of the whole-cell assay demonstrated the effect of substitution on the bioactivities and soluble protein expression levels of HPPD in vivo (Fig. 3). However, the loss of bioactivity might due to the loss of soluble protein expression, the loss of HG product formation or alternative reaction product formation by the variant HPPD. To further elucidate the effects of residue substitutions in vitro, both recombinant WT and mutant HPPD that showed available soluble protein expression level with bioactivity in whole cell assay were overexpressed and purified to near homogenous levels except for the I335M mutant, as indicated by SDS–PAGE results (Fig. S3). The yield for the purified WT protein was approximately 2 mg per gram of cell pellet. The yields of A33T, R113Q, V212M, N241S, A268V, I335M, and V340L variants were approximately 15%, 87%, 32%, 25%, 20%, 4%, and 100%, respectively, relative to that of the WT protein.

The far-UV circular dichroism (CD) spectrum and estimated secondary structure contents of purified A33T, R113Q, V212M, N241S, A268V, and V340L mutant proteins were similar to those of the WT protein, suggesting that the secondary structure of the proteins was not perturbed by residue substitutions (Fig. S4 and Table S2). The CD spectrum of the I335M variant was not available because of its low yield and purity.

The formation of the HG product in the reaction was analyzed using HPLC to determine the activity of HPPD (Figs. 4 and S6). Compared with the WT enzyme, the specific activities of A33T, V212M, N241S, A268V, and I335M mutants were decreased by approximately 3-, 6-, 10-, 3-, and 5-fold, respectively (Table 2). Furthermore, HG production for the N241S variant was approximately 15-fold less than that measured using the oxygraph assay, indicating an uncoupled reaction. The HPLC elution profiles of the reaction mixture from the human N241S mutant displayed two new peaks at approximately 9.2 and 9.4 ml, respectively, along with the HG product peak (∼10.2 ml), suggesting the formation of new products (Figs. 4 and S6).

The new products were isolated for subsequent analysis. ESI ionization LC–MS revealed the same mass signal of m/z 167.03 (M + H)+ for the two isolated products (S1 and S2) and the fragment signal of m/z 123.045 (M + H)+, which resulted from the decarboxylation of the new product (Fig. S7). Both isolated products exhibited similar 1H NMR spectra, with chemical shifts for the first product being δ7.002 (1H, d, J = 10 Hz), δ6.796 (1H, d, J = 10.4 Hz), δ6.116 (1H, d, J = 10.4 Hz), δ6.055 (1H, d, J = 10.4 Hz), δ4.888 (1H, td, J = 6.4, 0.8 Hz), δ2.74 (1H, d, J = 6 Hz), and δ2.697 (1H, d, J = 6.4 Hz; Fig. S8, A and B). The results indicated that the two new products were similar. However, whether they represent different conformations must be determined in future studies. The chemical shift signals in 1H NMR are similar to those in oxepinone, as identified in the uncoupled reaction of the P214T mutant of S. avermitilis HPPD (59). This finding suggests that oxepinone is the newly isolated product from the reaction involving the human N241S variant.

The optimized geometries, derived from QMM/MM calculations for both WT and variant HPPD-Fe(III)-O2•--HPP complexes, exhibited a similar bidentate coordination of HPP using its carboxyl and keto groups with the ferric ion (Fig. 6). This reflects an oxygen activation state within the six-coordinated complex, as inferred from the spin population, bond length, and charges on the iron and dioxygen (Table S3) (31, 61, 62). For the WT and A33T and V340L mutants, the 4-hydroxyl group of HPP interacted with both Gln251 and Gln265 residues (Fig. 6, A, B, and G). However, R113Q, V212M, and A268V mutants interacted with either Gln251 or Gln265 (Fig. 6, C, D, and F). Furthermore, the N241S variant displayed a distinct conformation for the aromatic side chain of HPP, causing the 4-hydroxyl group to interact with Ser226. This alternative binding suggests a potential mode for the uncoupled reaction observed in this mutant (Fig. 4). The distances between the activated oxygen atom of the peroxide anion and the carbonyl carbon of HPP were approximately 2.4, 2.6, 2.7, 2.4, 2.6, 2.5 and 2.4 Å for the WT and A33T, R113Q, V212M, N241S, A268V, and V340L variants, respectively (Fig. 6). These findings demonstrated the potential nucleophilic attack mode by the activated oxygen on the HPP substrate in the catalytic reactions of both the WT and HPPD variants (31, 34).

Missense variants of HPPD observed in clinical patients result in amino acid substitutions and were identified in either homozygous or heterozygous forms: Y160C, I267F, and A268V variants or A33T, G154S, V212M, N241S, I335M, and V340L variants, respectively (Table S1) (3, 15, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45). Although low or no detectable enzyme activity was observed in the liver biopsy samples of affected patients, the effects of mutations on the structure and function of HPPD remain unclear (6, 43, 63). Whole-cell colorimetric assays, human cell line analysis and biochemical studies of these missense variants revealed that amino acid substitutions at specific structural locations affect soluble protein expression, structural stability, and catalytic efficiency and even cause uncoupling during catalytic reactions. The findings offer a molecular perspective on the effects of these disease-associated missense variants on the function of HPPD.

No significant soluble protein was detected for G154S, Y160C, and I267F variants after expression, indicating their critical roles in the protein folding process (Fig. 3, C and D). The result suggested that the activity deficiency from patient’s liver biopsies might due to available soluble protein (43). Tyr160 and Ile267, but not Gly154, are conserved residues across different species (Fig. S1). Gly154 and Tyr160 are located in a long loop connecting the N-terminal domain to the C-terminal domain (Fig. 2). Mutations in these sites may affect the stability of the domain interface, leading to improper protein folding and eventually protein aggregation; these findings align with those of previous N-terminal truncation studies (17, 64). Ile267 is situated near the iron binding motif facing the domain interface, in which His266 is a metal-binding ligand. The bulky phenylalanine replacement to affect soluble protein expression suggests its critical role in stabilizing the metal binding motif and ensuring appropriate domain arrangement during protein folding. Therefore, patients with type III tyrosinemia carrying the G154S, Y160C, and I267F variants of HPPD might experience this condition due to the loss of soluble protein expression, leading to the accumulation of HPP and its derivatives (Table 1) (43, 45, 51).

We observed an approximately 80% to 90% reduction in the soluble protein expression level for A33T and I335M variants in E. coli cells, indicating that these substitutions disrupted the protein folding process (Fig. 3C). However, even with a low amount of soluble protein, both still exhibited approximately 20% bioactivity (Fig. 3B). Moreover, despite their low yield, the purified A33T and I335M variants displayed catalytic activity and coupled reaction for HG production, indicating that the biological activity reduction was not due to the low expression level (Table 2). This observation is consistent with the results of the bioactivity assay and with activity measurements derived from the liver biopsy samples of clinical patients (Fig. S3, Table 2 and Fig. 3) (43). Ile335 is a highly conserved residue, and it is located on the back side of the β-barrel of the active site. It faces the flanking α-helix and stabilizes the interface through hydrophobic interactions (Figs. S1 and S2). Loss of soluble protein expression resulting from the substitution of bulky methionine can lead to type III tyrosinemia, suggesting the critical role of Ile335 in structural stabilization (Table 1) (15, 43).

Ala33 is a conserved residue across different species (Fig. S1). This residue is located at the first α-helix of a motif near the domain and dimer interfaces. It faces the inside of the motif and interacts with His60, Ile62, and Leu71 through hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 6H). The lost interactions resulting from its replacement with threonine indicates its critical role in maintaining protein folding/assembly and structural stability; this speculation is supported by the observed reduction in soluble protein expression and structural stability (Fig. 3 and Table 4) (15). The destabilized protein conformation by residue substitution increases the possibility for improper protein folding and assembly, and the propensity for protein aggregation (3, 15). Although the substrate binding affinity of this variant was similar to that of the WT enzyme, the catalytic activity of this variant for HG production and catalytic efficiency exhibited a decline (Table 2, Table 3, Table 4). The functional performance of the A33T mutant was supported by the simulation model, which demonstrated the nucleophilic reaction initiated by the activated peroxide anion (Fig. 6B). The A33T variant has been associated with hawkinsinuria, a condition caused by HPPD deficiency (Table 1) (3, 41, 42, 43, 44). In patients with impaired HPPD, hawkinsin, an adduct product, emerges from an epoxide intermediate with cellular cysteine or glutathione used for detoxification (9, 49). The epoxide intermediate was identified as QAA during the uncoupled reaction of the N241S mutant of the S. avermitilis and rat HPPD in vitro (48). However, the study revealed that the A33T variant undergoes a coupled reaction, with no QAA intermediate detected in the reaction, as evidenced by the findings of HPLC (Table 2 and Fig. S6). Some patients carrying the A33T variant excreted the metabolites of HPP, 4-hydroxyphenyllactatic acid, HPA, or 4-hydroxycyclohexylacetic acid (HCAA) except for hawkinsin; thus, HCAA is used as a marker for the clinical diagnosis of hawkinsinuria (3, 42, 43, 44). Our findings suggest that the detrimental effects of the A33T mutation on the structural stability and function of HPPD might lead to the accumulation of HPP metabolites, excluding the QAA intermediate, in patients with hawkinsinuria.

Val212 is located in the active site, forming hydrophobic interactions with Trp210, Val217, Ala361, and Phe364. However, upon being replaced by methionine, Val212 interacts with Val217, Leu224, and Phe364 (Fig. 6H). Phe364 is a critical residue that forms π–π interaction with the aromatic side chain of HPP to maintain the substrate in an optimal position for catalysis (30, 59). The reduction in structural stability suggested a change in the conformation of the active site due to this substitution (Table 5). Although the substrate binding affinity and catalytic efficiency of this variant was similar to those of the WT enzyme, its Km and kcat values were significantly reduced (Table 3). This resulted in decreased HG production, which is consistent with the results of the bioactivity assay in E. coli cells (Table 2, Fig. 3, A and B). The V212M variant is associated with hawkinsinuria; however, no QAA intermediate was identified in the reaction (Table 1 and Fig. S6). The heterozygous A33T-V212M variant was detected in patients with elevated tyrosine levels and positive HCAA marker findings; however, hawkinsin remained undetected (41, 44). Patients exhibiting this mixed HPPD mutation may exhibit decreased soluble protein expression, structural stability, and catalytic function. The synergistic effect of these mutations can exacerbate the conditions for patients with the combined variant (41, 44).

Asn241, located in the substrate binding pocket, forms H-bond interactions with Gln265 and Ser226. These interactions stabilize the conformation of the active site for HPP binding and catalysis (Fig. 6H). However, the substitution with serine disrupts these interactions, likely altering the conformation of the substrate binding pocket and thereby resulting in a different binding orientation of the aromatic side chain of HPP (Fig. 6E). The binding mode of HPP is stabilized through interactions between its 4-hydroxyl group and the side chain of Ser226, setting up the substrate for a nucleophilic reaction by the activated dioxygen (Fig. 6E). This distinct binding mode implies a potential alternate reaction pathway. Although the substrate binding affinity of the N241S variant was similar to that of the WT enzyme, its catalytic efficiency was better than the WT enzyme (Tables 3 and 4). However, an uncoupled reaction occurred, leading to the formation of the oxepinone intermediate without the rearrangement step for HG production (Figs. 4, S6, and S7) (48, 59). This result revealed the cause of reduction of the biological activity of N241S in the whole-cell assay (Fig. 3).

The oxepinone intermediate formation for N241S variant suggests the possible binding mode of HPA with the 4-hydroxyl group to interact with Ser226 as that reported in the structure of 4-hydroxymandelate synthase (HMS) with bound 4-hydroxymandelate (HMA) (65). HMS is the homologues protein of HPPD, which catalyzes HPP substrate through oxidative decarboxylation and hydroxylation to form HMA. The conformational difference in the active site of the two enzymes leads to the different binding mode of the HPA, which locates the benzylic carbon or the phenol C1 in close proximity to react with the highly reactive iron-oxo intermediate for hydroxylation or arene oxidation in HMS or HPPD, respectively (4). Engineering the different residues in the active site between HPPD and HMS resulted in three products, the HMA, HG and oxepinone intermediate production in F337I mutated S. avermitilis HPPD (59). It suggests the involvement of the benzene-oxide intermediate in the hydroxylation reaction before side-chain migration for HG production. The result implies that the environment in the active site of N241S variant might favor the epoxidation at the aromatic ring by the iron-oxo intermediate to form stable oxepinone intermediate.

The same N241S mutant in S. avermitilis and rat HPPD resulted in the formation of the QAA intermediate (48). However, oxepinone was identified from the P214T mutant of S. avermitilis HPPD (it is relative to P239T in human HPPD) (59). The generation of QAA or oxepinone in the uncoupled reaction is believed to involve either a benzylic cation or epoxide intermediate following decarboxylation and oxidation steps during HPP’s reaction with HPPD (48, 59). Similar to QAA, the dienone of oxepinone can form an adduct with cysteine through a Michael addition reaction (Figs. 4 and S8) (48). The presence of hawkinsin in the urine of patients with hawkinsinuria is assumed to be a detoxification process in which glutathione or cysteine reacts with an epoxide intermediate of defective HPPD to form the adduct product (8, 15, 49). However, the extraction conditions for urine samples or reaction conditions for adduct formation might affect the conversion of the epoxide intermediate into QAA or oxepinone (49). The detection of the oxepinone intermediate in the human N241S variant and its adduct with cysteine suggest the occurrence of hawkinsin formation from the epoxide intermediate in the urine of patients with hawkinsinurea (3, 9).

Ala268 is located near the metal binding motif, forming hydrophobic interactions with His183 and Ile351, in which H183 and His266 are metal-binding ligands (Fig. 6H). Substitution with bulky valine reduced structural stability. The destabilized protein conformation of A268V variant increases the propensity for protein aggregation, indicating its critical role in protein folding and structural stability (Fig. 3, C and D and Table 5). This variant showed the similar iron content as WT enzyme (∼7 and 3 atoms/monomer, respectively). However, no substrate binding signal was observed from ITC analysis, suggesting that the conformation of the metal binding motif in the A268V variant might be changed in the presence of cobalt ion (Fig. 5). Differential scanning fluorometry analysis was performed to examine the structural stability of HPPD bound with different metal ions. The Tm values for WT and A268V variant proteins were 52.2 °C ± 0.3 °C and 46.3 °C ± 0.2 °C or 47.2 °C ± 0.5 °C and 43.9 °C ± 0.2 °C in the presence of Fe2+ or Co2+ ion, respectively (Fig. S10B). The structural stability of HPPD bound with cobalt ion was decreased. For the WT enzyme, the use of the cobalt ion as a cofactor resulted in a specific activity of 0.5 ± 0.02 and 0.6 ± 0.1 nmol/min/μg, respectively, as determined from oxygen consumption and HG production assays, indicating the occurrence of a coupled reaction. However, no activity was detectable for the A268V variant in the presence of the cobalt ion, which is consistent with the failure of substrate binding observed in ITC analysis (Fig. 5). These results distinguished the conformation of the metal binding motif in the A268V variant from that of the WT protein. The perturbed conformation might uncouple the reaction, reducing catalytic efficiency for HG product formation (Table 2). It elucidated the possible cause of reduction of the biological activity of A268V variant rather than the low expression level as shown in the whole-cell assay (Fig. 3). Therefore, the A268V variant HPPD reduced soluble protein expression levels, structural stability, and HG production, possibly affecting the normal metabolism of tyrosine and thereby causing type III tyrosinemia (Table 1) (3).

The protein expression levels and structural stability of R113Q and V340L variants were similar to those of the WT enzyme (Fig. 3 and Table 5). The two residues were located at the domain interface in which Arg113 is situated in the N-terminal domain at a distance from the active site and Val340 is located at a loop connecting β15 and β16 strands in the β-barrel of active site and near the active site entrance (Fig. 3). The substrate binding affinity and catalytic efficiency of R113Q were similar to those of the WT enzyme, suggesting that interactions at the domain interface were not significant for the function of HPPD (Fig. 6, Tables 3 and 4). However, the increased hydrophobic interaction at the domain interface of the V340L variant appeared to change the conformation of the active site, resulting in lower substrate binding affinity and catalytic efficiency (Tables 3 and 4, Fig. 6). Both variants exhibited similar HG production activity and biological function as the WT enzyme, suggesting that the effects of these substitutions on the disease might be tolerable (Table 1).

In conclusion, missense substitutions in the HPD gene were associated with type III tyrosinemia and hawkinsinuria. These mutations can result in the reduction of soluble protein expression for A33T, G154S, Y160C, I267F, A268V, and I335M variants by decreasing the stability at the domain interface, metal binding motif and the flanking α-helix to affect the efficient protein folding. Despite their low soluble expression levels, A33T, A268V, and I335M variants still displayed biological activity. However, their structural stability and catalytic efficiency for HG production were lower than average. Similar characteristics were observed in the V212M variant, supporting its decreased biological activity. The N241S variant demonstrated a different HPP binding mode in the altered active site with similar substrate binding affinity and better catalytic efficiency for the nucleophilic reaction. However, an uncoupled reaction occurred, leading to oxepinone intermediate formation, which could react with cysteine to form an adduct. The V340L mutation at the active site entrance exhibited the reduction of substrate binding affinity and catalytic efficiency.

This study reveals the molecular mechanisms of HPPD missense variants which affect the function of HPPD by changes in protein folding and structural stability to reduce soluble protein expression, or alter the active site to reduce catalytic efficiency and coupled reaction. Notably, due to the structural critical positions in HPPD, most variants are highly susceptible to destabilization by missense mutations. Decreased protein stability may impact native protein folding leading to aggregation as known for a broad range of pathogenic loss of function variants (66, 67). The recent reported strategy to develop or repurpose existing drugs to act as pharmacochaperones provides a potential therapy for stabilizing the non-native state of variants to restore the expression and activity (68, 69).

Protein samples (0.2 mg/ml) were diluted 1000-folds by 1% HNO3 solution (v/v). The buffer of protein samples was measured for correction. Standard solutions were prepared using high purity standard elements in 1% HNO3 solution to different concentrations for quantification.

The model of human HPPD (PDB code: 3ISQ) (16) in complex with HPP and an Fe(III)-O2•- species in a previous study was used for simulations of MD in Discovery Studio 2021 software (Biovia) (27). This model was also used as the template to generate HPPD mutants by using the Built Mutants protocol followed by minimization in the CHARMm force field. An explicit periodic boundary solvation model was applied to the HPPD-Fe(III)-O2•--HPP model. It included water and counter ions in an orthorhombic shape with a minimum distance of 10 Å from the boundary. The system was set to harmonic restraint for all atoms in the protein complex followed by energy minimization in the algorithms of the steepest descent and conjugate gradient for 1000 and 2000 steps, respectively. Then, the system was heated from 50 to 300 K for 4 ps and equilibration was conducted for 10 ps at 300 K. Production simulation was performed with no constraints at constant Tm and pressure conditions. A distance of at most 14 Å was used for counting nonbonded interaction pairs. Long-range electrostatic calculations were conducted using the particle mesh ewald method, and the shake constraint was applied for all hydrogen-related bonds in the simulation.

QM/MM calculations were performed for stable conformations that were selected from the trajectories of MD simulations. The Fe(III)-O2•-, HPP, and the side chains of His183, His266, Glu349, Gln251, Gln265, Gln334, Asn241, and Ser226 were defined as the quantum region. Regions that were located outside 5 Å of Fe(III)-O2•- and HPP were fixed by atom constraints. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof function and the quintet spin state were used in quantum calculations. The other parameters were set as reported previously (27).
