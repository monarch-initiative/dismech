Systemic immunoglobulin light chain amyloidosis is a protein misfolding disease 
caused by the conversion of immunoglobulin light chains from their soluble 
functional states into highly organized amyloid fibrillar aggregates that lead 
to organ dysfunction. The disease is progressive and, accordingly, early 
diagnosis is vital to prevent irreversible organ damage, of which cardiac damage 
and renal damage predominate. The development of novel sensitive biomarkers and 
imaging technologies for the detection and quantification of organ involvement 
and damage is facilitating earlier diagnosis and improved evaluation of the 
efficacy of new and existing therapies. Treatment is guided by risk assessment, 
which is based on levels of cardiac biomarkers; close monitoring of clonal and 
organ responses guides duration of therapy and changes in regimen. Several new 
classes of drugs, such as proteasome inhibitors and immunomodulatory drugs, 
along with high-dose chemotherapy and autologous haematopoietic stem cell 
transplantation, have led to rapid and deep suppression of amyloid light chain 
production in the majority of patients. However, effective therapies for 
patients with advanced cardiac involvement are an unmet need. Passive 
immunotherapies targeting clonal plasma cells and directly accelerating removal 
of amyloid deposits promise to further improve the overall outlook of this 
increasingly treatable disease.

is caused by the conversion of immunoglobulin light chains from their soluble functional 25 
states into highly organized amyloid fibrillar aggregates that lead to organ dysfunction. The 26 
disease is progressive and, accordingly, early diagnosis is vital to prevent irreversible 27

novel sensitive biomarkers and imaging technologies for the detection and quantification of 29

 [H1] Introduction  42 
 Amyloidosis is a group of complex diseases that are caused by protein misfolding and 43 
aggregation into highly ordered amyloid fibrils that deposit in tissues, resulting in 44 
progressive organ damage. These amyloid fibrils are characterized by a cross-β-sheet 45 
quaternary structure1. Over time, protein misfolding and amyloid accumulation can result in 46 
severe organ dysfunction. Protein aggregates, or preceding intermediaries, may induce 47 
cell dysfunction and death, a process termed proteotoxicity. In addition, the distortion of 48 
tissue architecture caused by amyloid deposits contributes to organ dysfunction 2; 49 
however, these mechanisms are poorly characterized.  50 
To date, 36 proteins that can form extracellular amyloid fibrils in humans have been 51 
identified: some form localized deposits, such as β-amyloid in Alzheimer disease, leading 52 
to localized amyloidosis, and others accumulate throughout the tissues of the body (known 53 
as systemic amyloidosis)3. At least 17 proteins can cause systemic amyloidosis; 54 

 
3 
 
immunoglobulin heavy or light chains are notable in being able to form both systemic 55 
amyloid deposits or localized amyloid deposits, for example those restricted particularly to 56 
urothelial tissue and the larynx4. Systemic amyloidosis can be hereditary or acquired; the 57 
two most common forms of systemic amyloidosis — monoclonal immunoglobulin light 58 
chain (AL) amyloidosis and wild-type transthyretin (ATTR) amyloidosis— are acquired. AL 59 
amyloidosis is typically found in individuals with monoclonal gammopathy, and is caused 60 
by the increased production of immunoglobulin light chains owing to the proliferation of 61 
clonal plasma cells that characterize these disorders; these light chains aggregate into 62 
amyloid fibrils, leading to organ damage. Wild-type ATTR amyloidosis is caused by the 63 
aggregation of transthyretin, is age related and predominantly affects men >70 years of 64 
age. Another form of non-hereditary systemic amyloidosis is caused by persistent high 65 
concentrations of serum amyloid A protein (an acute phase reactant) associated with 66 
chronic inflammation caused by chronic inflammatory disorders such as rheumatoid 67 
arthritis, persistent infections, or hereditary autoinflammatory diseases (familial 68 
Mediterranean fever, cryopyrin associated periodic syndrome and many others)5. Systemic 69 
amyloidosis caused by leukocyte chemotactic factor 2, mainly presents with nephropathy 70 
and is gaining greater recognition in the United States6. 71 
Systemic amyloidosis can also be caused by genetic mutations inherited in an 72 
autosomal dominant manner. More than 120 point mutations in TTR (encoding 73 
transthyretin) can cause systemic amyloidosis that mainly affects the peripheral nervous 74 
system and the heart. Genetic variants of APOA1, APOA2, APOC2 and APOC3 (encoding 75 
apolipoprotein AI, AII, CII and CIII, respectively), as well as FGA (encoding fibrinogen 76 
alpha chain), GSN (encoding gelsolin), CST3 (encoding cystatin C) and LYZ (encoding 77 
lysozyme) can also cause hereditary systemic amyloidosis 3 (Table 1).  78 
 Despite the biochemical and aetiological heterogeneity of systemic amyloidosis, 79 
the clinical manifestations of the different forms largely overlap and essentially depend 80 
upon the affected organs. Predominantly affected organs include the kidney and heart, 81 
followed by the peripheral nervous system (including the autonomic nervous system), liver, 82 
gastrointestinal tract and soft tissues. Cardiac damage is a major determinant of survival 83 
and, therefore, a major goal of therapy is to improve cardiac function. A rapid and 84 
profound decrease of the amyloid precursor protein can reverse organ dysfunction, and is 85 
the aim of therapy. In AL amyloidosis, therapy should target the B cell clone responsible 86 
for producing the aberrant clonal immunoglobulin protein. The type and intensity of 87 

 
4 
 
treatment targeting the B-cell disease is based on risk assessment, which is based on the 88 
characteristics of the patient and the biology of the clone. Immunotherapies targeting the 89 
amyloid clone or the amyloid deposits are now in development; hopefully, these new 90 
agents will enter clinical practice, and will be combined with therapies to suppress the 91 
amyloid protein, which might improve quality of life (QOL) and improve survival.  92 
This primer focuses on systemic AL amyloidosis, highlighting the disease 93 
mechanisms and basis for effective treatment, owing to advance in deciphering the 94 
molecular mechanisms of this form of amyloidosis and in developing novel, effective 95 
therapies that have improved quality of life and survival7, 8. 96

 [H1] Epidemiology  98 
Limited data on the epidemiology of AL amyloidosis are available due to the lack of large 99 
population databases to assess incidence. The prevalence of the disease rises with 100 
increasing age. The prevalence doubles from age 35-54 compared to 65+ with reported 101 
mean age of 63, and 55% of patients are men 9. There are two known risk factors for AL 102 
amyloidosis. The first is a pre-existing monoclonal gammopathy. Among patients with 103 
MGUS, the relative risk is 8.810. In one series of 1384 MGUS patients followed for up to 50 104 
years, 14 developed AL amyloidosis (1%). As many as 10-15% of patients with myeloma 105 
have overt, coexisting AL amyloidosis, and in another series as many as 38% of myeloma 106 
patients were found to have covert co-existing AL amyloidosis11.  Approximately 1% of 107 
patients with pre-existing myeloma, who are not diagnosed with AL amyloidosis 108 
simultaneously, will go onto to develop AL amyloidosis12. Antecedent viral infection does 109 
not appear to be a predisposing factor. The other identified risk factor is the existence of 110 
particular single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Associations were found in a genome-111 
wide association study (GWAS) on  1229 AL amyloidosis patients. SNPs at 10 loci 112 
showed evidence of an association at P<10−5. Rs9344 at the splice site of cyclin D1, 113 
which promotes translocation (11;14), reached the highest signi ficance, P=7.80 × 10−11 . 114 
The SNP rs79419269, which is close to gene SMARCD3 which is involved in chromatin 115 
remodeling was also significant ( P=5.2 × 10−8). These data provide evidence for 116 
common genetic susceptibility to AL amyloidosis 13. 117

The prevalence rates of systemic AL amyloidosis have increased due to improved 155

[H1] Mechanisms/pathophysiology  162 
[H2] Amyloid fibril formation  163 
As previously mentioned, the process underlying amyloidosis is the conversion of 164 
globular, soluble proteins into insoluble amyloid fibrils that deposit in vital organs and 165 
damage their function1. This complex process can be favored by several factors, such as 166 
mutations that destabilize the native protein structure and expose hydrophobic and 167 
protease-sensitive regions, increased protein concentrations, due to either greater protein 168 
synthesis or reduced clearance, or the intrinsic propensity of certain proteins to form 169 
amyloid fibrils that becomes apparent with aging. Typically, protein aggregation is 170 
countered by protein homeostasis (proteostasis), that functions to maintain the proteome, 171 
both intracellularly and extracellularly, in a native conformation, in the correct location and 172 
at the right concentration21, 22. Overall, ~ 1,600 molecules have a role in proteostasis, the 173 
efficiency of which declines with age23. When intracellular and/or extracellular proteostasis 174 
fail, protein aggregation might occur. Proteins with diverse structures and functions 175 
aggregate to form amyloid fibrils which have a highly ordered cross-β fibre structures and 176 
are characterized by antiparallel β-strands that are arranged perpendicular to the fibre, as 177 
demonstrated by x-ray diffraction24. Amyloid fibrils have a distinct diameter of 7.5–10.0 nm 178 
as determined using electron microscopy25.  179 
[H3] AL amyloidosis fibril formation.  180 
AL amyloidosis is usually caused by the low-level expansion of an indolent B cell clone26 181 
that produces an immunoglobulin light chain λ (referred to as light chain in this Primer) in 182 
75–80% of cases and κ light chains in the remaining cases (Figure 1). A high frequency 183 
(∼40–60%) of the chromosomal translocation t(11;14), which juxtaposes the 184 

 
7 
 
immunoglobulin heavy chain locus (IgH) to the oncogene cyclin D1, characterizes this 185 
amyloid B cell clone27. Somatic mutations in IGLV (encoding the light chain variable 186 
region) reduce the fold stability of the native protein and increase protein dynamics, which 187 
favors endoproteolysis and the production of variable light chain domains that can cause 188 
amyloidosis28. Indeed, amyloidogenic light chains have a low fold stability and high protein 189 
dynamics compared with the light chains produced in multiple myeloma 29, 30. In 190 
proteostasis, extracellular chaperones favor appropriate light chain folding and inhibit 191 
protein aggregation31. The aggregation of amyloidogenic light chains can occur owing to 192 
disruption to, or overwhelming of extracellular proteostasis (Figure 1). Other factors can 193 
facilitate protein aggregation and oligomer formation, such as the interactions of 194 
amyloidogenic light chains with the tissue microenvironment, such as with extracellular 195 
matrix components (including glycosaminoglycans, collagen and lipids)32, shear forces, 196 
proteases, and metals (copper in particular)33. In addition, cell membrane surfaces have 197 
been hypothesised to facilitate fibril attachment, by acting as anchors for a cell-mediated 198 
seeding mechanism34. Once formed, oligomers of light chains are on the pathway to form 199 
highly organized amyloid fibrils. The pentraxin serum amyloid P component (SAP) is a 200 
circulating plasma protein that is universally present in amyloid deposits owing to its 201 
calcium-dependent binding to amyloid fibrils35. SAP has been reported to protect amyloid 202 
fibrils from degradation36, making this protein an excellent candidate for amyloid 203 
scintigraphy and as a target for amyloid-directed immunotherapy. The accumulation of 204 
amyloid deposits in parenchymal tissue leads to tissue damage, which causes dysfunction 205 
of vital organs2. In addition, amyloid fibrils cause cytotoxicity and promote the misfolding of 206 
light chains and further oligomer formation34. Soluble prefibrillar species, mainly oligomers, 207 
also contribute to organ damage through proteotoxicity, increased cellular oxidative stress 208 
resulting in mitochondrial damage and reduced cell viability37-39 (Figure 1).  209 
The kinetics of fibril formation offers important clues for clinical management (Figure 2). 210 
The formation of amyloid fibrils begins from a solution of the monomeric native protein, 211 
which might misfold and assume an amyloidogenic, partially folded conformation. When 212 
the amount of partially folded proteins reaches a specific concentration, a critical fibrillar 213 
nucleus forms, which catalyses protein aggregation and fibril development. The critical 214 
concentration required for nucleation varies, and can be very low for very unstable light 215 
chains or high for more stable light chains. Initially, conditions do not favor aggregation, 216 
which corresponds to the ‘lag phase’ that precedes fibril formation. The kinetics of 217 

 
8 
 
aggregation dramatically changes after formation of the fibrillar nuclei owing to their 218 
catalytic role. The concentration of partially folded proteins that is necessary to elongate 219 
the amyloid fibrils is 10-20 fold lower than the concentration required for forming the first 220 
fibrillar nucleus, depending on the protein species40. Thus, early diagnosis of AL 221 
amyloidosis and administration of a rapidly acting therapy that produces a swift and deep 222 
reduction of the amyloid precursor is critical to halt fibril growth and disease progression. 223 
Amyloid deposits are in general persistent and unusually resistant to degradation. 224 
However,  slow natural clearance of amyloid deposits, by endogenous immunological 225 
mechanisms in which macrophages play an important role, does occur41. Clearance of 226 
amyloid deposits may contribute considerably to recovery of organ function2. 227

[H2] Organ involvement  229 
The heart and the kidneys are the two most frequently affected organs in systemic AL 230 
amyloidosis, although all organs can be involved, except the brain (Figure 3). The precise 231 
molecular mechanisms underlying amyloid organ targeting remain elusive. Several 232 
investigators have shown that certain structural features related to the light chain variable 233 
region gene and gene family confer a higher risk of involvement of specific organs, 234 
possibly through interactions with resident cells. For example, the germ line gene 235 
(unarranged DNA segments inherited through the germline, before it is modified by 236 
rearrangement and somatic hypermutation) IGLV6-57  is more common in patients with AL 237 
systemic amyloidosis than in the normal B cell repertoire and is associated with renal 238 
involvement42. Mesangial cells of the kidney have a propensity to form amyloid fibrils when 239 
incubated with light chain derived from IGLV6-57 42. Cardiac tropism has been related to 240 
the IGLV1-44 germ line gene, which confers a 5-fold increase in the chance of dominant 241 
heart involvement43, 44. Although λ light chains are responsible of most cases of systemic 242 
AL amyloidosis, κ light chain of the IGKV1-33 germ line preferentially targets the liver44. 243 
 244 
Cardiac involvement is a key determinant of patient survival, therefore several 245 
investigators have studied the mechanisms of cardiac damage caused by misfolded light 246 
chain37-39. Cardiac dysfunction can result from amyloid deposits causing widespread 247 
disruption of tissue architecture and from proteotoxicity of the light chains7. Other 248 
speculated mechanisms of organ dysfunction include the perturbation of cellular 249 
membranes by amyloid fibrils, cell toxicity owing to fibril growth and the formation of 250 

 
9 
 
soluble light chain oligomers by amyloid fibrils, although these mechanisms require further 251 
study. In addition, AL amyloid fibrils are cytotoxic at low concentrations, whereas soluble 252 
amyloid light chains induce apoptosis, suggesting that the mechanisms of cytotoxicity 253 
differ between soluble protein and amyloid fibrils34. Exposing cardiac cells to light chains 254 
purified from patients with cardiac AL amyloidosis led to the increased production of 255 
reactive oxygen species (ROS) compared with control LC proteins isolated from patients 256 
without cardiac involvement33, 38. Amyloidogenic light chains from patients with AL amyloid 257 
cardiomyopathy can induce p38 MAPK signaling resulting in increased production of ROS, 258 
impaired calcium homeostasis, cell dysfunction and eventually cell death in isolated adult 259 
cardiomyocytes37. This p38 MAPK pathway mediates also the transcription of type B 260 
natriuretic peptide (BNP), a serum biomarker of cardiac stretch and damage45, supporting 261 
a possible connection between cardiotoxic effects of light chain with induced MAPK 262 
signaling and BNP. This pathogenetic link is the basis of the use of the serum biomarker 263 
NT-proBNP (the amino-terminal fragment of BNP) in the management of patients with AL, 264 
from early detection of cardiac involvement, to the risk classification and in monitoring 265 
cardiac response to therapy46  266

[H1] Diagnosis, screening and prevention  268 
A diagnosis of amyloidosis should be considered in any patient presenting with heart 269 
failure with preserved ejection fraction, nephrotic range proteinuria, a mixed axonal 270 
demyelinating peripheral neuropathy with autonomic features or carpal tunnel syndrome, 271 
hepatomegaly without imaging abnormalities, or any patient with a monoclonal 272 
gammopathy or atypical multiple myeloma. Taste alterations are common 7, 47 (Figure 3). 273 
In any patient with these clinical signs and symptoms, at a minimum, immunofixation 274 
electrophoresis of the serum and urine and an immunoglobulin free light chain assay 275 
(which assesses the concentration of κ and λ free light chains and their ratio in the serum) 276 
should be carried out to assess for a precursor light chain protein. Where available, 277 
imaging with radio-iodinated SAP can identify amyloid deposits in individuals with these 278 
syndromes48 but this test has limited availability and is limited to certain specialized 279 
amyloidosis treatment centers. Tissue biopsy and histopathological analysis to confirm 280 
diagnosis is warranted in patients with an immunoglobulin light chain abnormality (Figure 281 
4). The ordered ultrastructure of amyloid fibres allows the regular intercalation of Congo 282 
red dye, which shows green birefringence under polarized light microscopy; the diagnosis 283 

 
10 
 
of AL amyloidosis requires this histological observation (Figure 5). Although the direct 284 
biopsy of an affected organ will yield the diagnosis, it is generally not necessary as less-285 
invasive investigations such as the aspiration of subcutaneous fat, a bone marrow biopsy, 286 
or lip biopsy can lead to diagnosis in 50–85% of patients49, 50. When amyloid deposits are 287 
detected in biopsies, accurate identifying the precursor protein is crucial to guide 288 
treatment. This is feasible using immunohistochemistry, in highly specialized 289 
laboratories51, 52, and using immune-electronmicroscopy53. However, a mass 290 
spectrometry-based analysis of the amyloid-containing tissues is now considered the best 291 
approach with a reported sensitivity of 88% and specificity of 96% higher than 292 
immunochemical techniques and does not require a large panel of antisera to identify non 293 
AL amyloidosis54. Although not widely available, reference laboratories exist that will 294 
unequivocally confirm the protein subunit composing the amyloid fibril. This is particularly 295 
important in the black population with its high prevalence of V122I mutant ATTR 296 
amyloidosis can clinically resemble AL amyloidosis55.  297

[H3] Differential diagnosis. 299 
Diagnosing AL amyloidosis  based on the presence of a serum and/or urine light chain 300 
abnormality and Congo red-positive tissue is insufficient. As many as 23% of patients with 301 
wild-type ATTR cardiac amyloidosis have a clonal immunoglobulin abnormality, which 302 
could result in misdiagnosis and inappropriate administration of chemotherapy56. Nuclear 303 
scintigraphy using 99mTc-labeled pyrophosphate (PYP) or 99mTc-labeled 3,3-diphosphono-304 
1,2-propanodicarboxylic acid (DPD) can be useful in differentiating cardiac AL amyloidosis 305 
from ATTR type 57, 58 . The mechanism by which these bone tracers bind to amyloid 306 
deposits is unclear, but substantial cardiac uptake occurs in virtually all patients with ATTR 307 
amyloidosis and in only about 40% of those with cardiac AL type; among the latter one-308 
quarter do demonstrate substantial ATTR-like grade 2 or 3 uptake, which is associated 309 
with poor outcome 59. 310 
 When an amyloid deposit is detected as AL using mass spectrosc opy, systemic 311 
amyloidosis should be distinguished from localized disease, as this determines 312 
management strategies. Localized deposits of AL that do not require systemic 313 
chemotherapy can be observed in the bladder, larynx, stomach, colon, skin, eyelids, lung, 314 
and urinary tract. Only systemic amyloidosis requires systemic therapy, as localized 315 

 
11 
 
disease usually has a very good prognosis4. A thorough evaluation for other areas of 316 
organ dysfunction can usually distinguish systemic from localized AL amyloidosis.  317

[H2] Screening and prevention 319 
A substantial delay to diagnosis until after advanced organ damage has already ensued is 320 
still common for AL amyloidosis and, although therapeutic advances have been made, this 321 
results in high rates of death due to cardiac involvement and progression to end-stage 322 
renal disease in the first few months after diagnosis8, 60. The clinical manifestations of 323 
systemic AL amyloidosis resemble symptoms of more common conditions found in elderly 324 
individuals, therefore, appropriate diagnostic testing is usually initiated only several months 325 
after the onset of symptoms. Indeed, AL amyloidosis was diagnosed >1 year after the 326 
onset of symptoms in 40% of patients in one study61. Delays in diagnosis of AL 327 
amyloidosis are also common in patients with diagnosed monoclonal gammopathy of 328 
undetermined significance (MGUS) despite the appearance of amyloid-related 329 
symptoms62. Indeed, a monoclonal component with increased free light chain ratio can be 330 
consistently detected in the sera of patients with MGUS who eventually develop AL 331 
amyloidosis at least 4 years before the diagnosis63. Thus, patients with asymptomatic 332 
monoclonal gammopathy, MGUS or smoldering multiple myeloma, with an abnormal free 333 
light chain ratio are at risk of developing AL amyloidosis, and should be the target of 334 
screening programs. The heart and / or the kidneys are involved in >95% of patients with 335 
systemic AL amyloidosis, and biomarkers with 100% sensitivity are available to detect the 336 
presence of cardiac and renal involvement. For example, increased levels of NT-proBNP 337 
in serum can detect cardiac involvement in systemic AL amyloidosis before symptoms 338 
manifest, with 100% diagnostic sensitivity64, 65. When glomerular filtration rate (a marker of 339 
kidney function) is preserved, albuminuria can detect renal involvement at earlier disease 340 
stages when progression to end-stage renal disease can be almost invariably prevented60. 341 
Accordingly, assessment of NT-proBNP levels and for albuminuria should be integrated 342 
into the regular follow-up panel of patients with MGUS and an abnormal free light chain 343 
ratio66, 67. More than 95% of patients with AL amyloidosis have elevated NT-proBNP or 344 
albuminuria, and this approach can lead to the detection of pre-symptomatic systemic AL 345 
amyloidosis, that can be effectively treated with very good outcomes68. 346

[H2] Patient risk stratification  348 
The survival of patients with systemic AL amyloidosis is heterogeneous, depending on the 349 
severity of cardiac dysfunction at the time of diagnosis. Patients with diagnosis late in the 350 
clinical course (when heart damage is often irreversible) have a median survival of 3-6 351 
months69, whereas patients without cardiac involvement can survive for many years even 352 
if they fail to respond to first-line therapy. Similarly, the early diagnosis and effective 353 
treatment of patients with renal involvement almost abolishes the risk of progression to 354 
end-stage kidney disease and dialysis, whereas late diagnosis during the advanced 355 
stages of disease is associated with a higher risk of progression, despite treatment60. This 356 
heterogeneity requires accurate prognostic stratification to establish the best therapeutic 357 
approach, balancing treatment intensity and rapidity of action with patient frailty. Moreover, 358 
patient stratification is necessary for comparing results of clinical trials.  359 
The current staging systems for systemic AL amyloidosis are based on the levels of 360 
circulating markers of cardiac, renal and B cell clonal disease. One cardiac staging system 361 
is based on the levels of NT-proBNP and cardiac troponins and was devised by the Mayo 362 
Clinic and modified by European investigators to improve the discrimination of very high 363 
risk patients (Figure 6A)69-72. This cardiac staging system is the most widely used for 364 
clinical trial design and to determine patient management. This staging system was 365 
modified to include clonal burden, assessed by bone marrow plasma cell infiltration and 366 
dFLC (difference between involved and uninvolved circulating free light chain) 367 
concentration, that have independent ability to predict survival. Patients with AL 368 
amyloidosis and a bone marrow plasma cell infiltrate of >10% have poorer survival that is 369 
comparable to patients with concomitant overt multiple myeloma73. Individuals with a very 370 
low (<50 mg/L) dFLC level have a significantly better outcome irrespective of cardiac 371 
stage74-76. The Mayo Clinic group incorporated the dFLC level (with a cutoff value of 180 372 
mg/L) in their revised staging system (Figure 6B)77, 78. A renal staging system predicting 373 
the progression to dialysis has also been proposed and validated by European 374 
investigators (Figure 6C)60, 79 Although the severity of renal involvement does not directly 375

[H1] Management  383 
The aims of therapy are rapid elimination of the amyloid precursor and enhanced 384 
reabsorption of amyloid deposits, to rapidly ameliorate cardiac function to improve 385 
patients’ quality of life and enhance survival. The suppression of amyloid light chain 386 
synthesis is effectively achieved using chemotherapy (both conventional and high dose) in 387 
combination with peripheral blood autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, 388 
and, more recently, with immunotherapy targeting the B cell clone. Immunotherapies 389 
promoting the reabsorption of amyloid deposits are in clinical development7. Ultimately, the 390 
types of therapy used depend on the patient’s risk classification. 391 
Changes in levels of dFLC, NT-proBNP, proteinuria or GFR are used to assess 392 
treatment efficacy; indeed, an international effort established and validated hematologic 393 
and organ response criteria in AL amyloidosis (Table 3). The aim of chemotherapy should 394 
be the rapid achievement of very low absolute values (rather than percent reductions) of 395 
dFLC, which are associated with improvements in organ dysfunction and prolonged 396 
survival82, 83 Emerging data indicate that minimal residual disease (MRD) may be 397 
responsible for residual organ disease despite what is generally considered good-quality 398 
hematologic response. If the available preliminary data are confirmed, the coexistence of 399 
persistent organ dysfunction in the absence of other causes and MRD could prompt further 400 
chemotherapy to obtain MRD negativity and improving the likelihood of organ response.84, 401 
85. Assessment of treatment response should be frequent, and should be carried out at 402 
least every 2 cycles of chemotherapy or 3 months after stem cell transplantation, and more 403 
frequently for patients with severe cardiac involvement. Patients failing to achieve a good 404 
response should be rapidly shifted to alternate rescue regimens. Organ response usually 405 
closely follows hematologic response, and can be assessed as early as 3 months after 406 
treatment initiation, but can continue to improve afterwards. 407

[H3] Treatment of low risk patients.  409 
The goal of treatment for AL amyloidosis is targeting the underlying clonal plasma cell 410 
dyscrasia, aiming for rapid and deep hematologic responses. High-dose intravenous 411 

 
14 
 
melphalan conditioning followed by autologous peripheral blood stem cell transplantation 412 
(HDM/SCT) has been used as treatment for highly selected patients with AL amyloidosis 413 
since the first reports in the mid 1990’s86. The results of studies of HDM/SCT at single 414 
center and multiple centers are reported in Supplementary Table 1. The risk of major 415 
complications, including death, during stem cell mobilization and collection is ~15%,87 and 416 
early treatment-related mortality is 2-15% after transplantation;88 appropriate patient 417 
selection is the key to reduction in morbidity and mortality. Eligibility criteria for HDM/SCT 418 
vary between centers, but broadly require a confirmed tissue diagnosis of amyloidosis, 419

hepatic function (total bilirubin level <2 mg/dL) and hemodynamic stability (baseline 424

not excluded at some centers if other eligibility criteria are met.89 426 
Several studies have evaluated the efficacy and morbidity associated with SCT. 427 
One multicenter randomized controlled trial demonstrated similar clonal hematologic 428

melphalan and dexamethasone compared to HDM/SCT. However, this trial had major 430

failed to show superiority of overall survival in this randomized trial. Another report from 434 
the Center for International Blood and Marrow Transplant Research registry showed 435 
improvement in overall survival and a reduction in early mortality with excellent 5-year 436 
survival after HDM/SCT.91 Dose adapted melphalan strategy, with dose reductions 437 
depending on renal, cardiac parameters and age, increases the potentially suitable patient 438 
population for SCT and can lead to prolonged survival especially if a hematologic CR is 439 
achieved 92 93. Lower doses of melphalan could reduce treatment related toxicity, but also 440 
lower hematologic responses.94 441

[H3] Treatment of intermediate-risk and high-risk patients. 478 
Patients at intermediate risk (that is, those with stage II or IIIa disease, Figure 6a) or high-479 
risk (stage IIIb, Figure 6a) have increasing treatment options; however, the benefit of 480 
treatment for high-risk patients is considerably less compared with other patients109. A 481 
small proportion of patients with intermediate risk can safely undergo upfront autologous 482 
peripheral blood stem cell transplantation (HDM/SCT) as they have partially preserved 483 
organ, particularly cardiac, function. However, the best-suited therapy for those with 484 
intermediate-risk disease is unclear and practice patterns vary between amyloidosis 485 
centers.  486 
A major breakthrough for patients with systemic AL amyloidosis was the 487 
introduction of oral chemotherapy with melphalan and dexamethasone (MDex) 488 
(Supplementary Table 2)110; this treatment was the standard for patients not undergoing 489 
HDM/SCT for more than a decade. MDex is very well tolerated in intermediate risk patients 490 
and a hematologic response is reached in up to 76% of patients, with very good partial 491 
response or complete response in 60% of cases when full dose dexamethasone can be 492 
given78. Regimens using bortezomib (a proteasome inhibitor) are now considered the 493 
upfront standard of care in most patients with AL amyloidosis. In the largest retrospective 494 
study of first-line treatment with cyclophosphamide, bortezomib and dexamethasone 495 
(CyBorD), the overall hematologic response rate was 66% in patients with stage II or IIIa 496 
disease, with a very good partial response or complete response in 47% of patients109. In 497 
studies comparing bortezomib-based combinations with previous standards of care, MDex 498 
and cyclophosphamide / thalidomide / dexamethasone response rates were higher with 499 
bortezomib treatment when combined with alkylating agents and dexamethasone, 500 
although this did not translate into a survival advantage111, 112. In an international phase III 501 
study, bortezomib plus MDex demonstrated a higher hematologic response rate  502 
compared with MDex alone (81% vs. 57%, P=0.005)113.  503 
Based on these data, intermediate-risk patients who are not eligible for HDM/SCT, 504 
should be treated with bortezomib-based regimens provided they do not have 505 
contraindications, such as peripheral neuropathy or fibrotic lung disease. Clonal and 506 
patient characteristics should be considered when choosing the most appropriate 507 
combinations; for example, treatment with bortezomib plus MDex can overcome the 508 
effects of both gain 1q21 (which confers a poorer outcome with oral melphalan) and 509 
t(11;14) (which confers a poorer outcome with bortezomib)114-117. Cyclophosphamide and 510 

 
17

dexamethasone alone or in combination with cyclophosphamide is preferred in patients 513 
with potentially reversible contraindications to ASCT as these treatments are stem cell 514 
sparing, as well as in patients with renal failure, in whom melphalan dose usually requires 515 
adjustments. Intermediate risk patients in whom bortezomib is contraindicated due to pre-516

toxicity has been reported with thalidomide in patients with AL amyloidosis120, 121. 522 
Combining lenalidomide and MDex for frontline therapy led to hematologic response rates 523

High-risk patients represent ~20% of all individuals with AL amyloidosis, and 528 
represent a challenge owing to advanced cardiac stage (IIIb) or severe heart failure 529 
(NYHA class III or IV). So far, no treatment regimen can significantly alter the course of the 530 
disease in these patients, with median survival not exceeding 7 months128. Nevertheless, 531 
the few patients (approximately 20%) who survive long enough (1-3 months) to take 532 
advantage of response to chemotherapy can enjoy prolonged survival129. High-risk 533 
patients can be treated with low-dose combinations of the drugs used in intermediate-risk 534 
subjects, with weekly dose escalation based on tolerability with close monitoring130. 535 
Although high risk patients are typically excluded from clinical trials, there is interest in 536 
whether therapies directed at the amyloid itself may offer better hope.  537

[H3] Treatment of relapse. 539 
Patients with relapsed disease have a good prognosis, with remarkably longer survival 540 
than patients with refractory disease131, 132. A few studies report the rate of relapse after 541 
initial treatment. The Boston University group reported a 38.5% rate of relapse after 542 
complete response following stem cell transplant.96  A study by the Mayo Clinic 543 

 
18 
 
investigators revealed hematologic relapse or progression in 36% of patients who 544

non-transplant upfront therapy needed second-line therapy after a median follow-up of 41 546 
months.132 No consensus has been reached on the criteria to commence rescue therapy 547 
in patients with relapsing disease133. Cardiac progression as assessed by increase in NT-548 
proBNP should not be awaited, because it is associated with shorter survival132. Patients 549 
with relapsed disease can be treated by repeating upfront therapy, if possible, although 550 
this is associated with shorter time to retreatment without reduction in overall survival 551 
compared to patients who are treated with a different regimen at relapse134. For patients 552 
who have relapsed after autologous stem cell transplantation, treatment using a 553 
proteasome inhibitor is indicated. If the patient maintains eligibility and stem cells are 554 
available, a second autologous stem cell transplant may also be considered131. Although 555 
immunomodulatory drugs are less often considered as the first choice for patients with 556 
newly-diagnosed disease, they are often the backbone of treatment of refractory patients 557 
(Supplementary Table 3 and Figure 7). Lenalidomide can overcome resistance to 558 
alkylating agents, proteasome inhibitors, and thalidomide inducing a hematologic response 559 
also in patients refractory to these agents135-140. However, lenalidomide can worsen renal 560 
failure in patients with substantial proteinuria141. Pomalidomide is one of the most powerful 561

Hematologic response to pomalidomide is obtained rapidly, in a median time of 1–2 564

III trial in patients with relapsed and/or refractory disease (NCT01659658)145. The 571 
humanized anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody daratumumab, is one of the most promising 572

[H2] Amyloid Directed Immunotherapy 578 
Although chemotherapy reduces the plasma cell burden and ultimately the production of 579 
the amyloidogenic light chain protein, this therapy does not degrade amyloid deposited in 580 
tissues, although amyloid does slowly resorb from the body once the amyloid precursor 581 
has been suppressed. To this end, three monoclonal antibodies have been developed to 582 
target existing amyloid deposits: NEOD001, 11-1F4 and anti-SAP antibody.  583 
In a phase I–II study, patients with AL amyloidosis who had completed at least one 584 
previous anti-plasma cell-directed therapy, had a partial hematologic response or better, 585 
and persistent organ dysfunction received NEOD001, which targets amyloid fibrils149. No 586 
drug-related serious adverse events or discontinuations were observed among the 27 587 
treated patients, and of the 14 patients who could undergo cardiac evaluation, 8 588 
responded and 6 were stable, whereas of the 15 patients who could go renal evaluation, 9 589 
responded and 6 were stable. However, despite these results, a phase III trial and a phase 590 
IIb placebo-controlled trial failed to confirm the positive effects of NEOD001 on cardiac 591 
involvement, and the development of this antibody was discontinued. This emphasizes the 592 
need of controlled studies based on robust endpoints to introduce novel therapies for AL 593 
amyloidosis. 594 
 The murine monoclonal antibody 11-1F4 recognizes an amyloid-as sociated 595 
conformational epitope in human light chain-related fibrils150. In studies of mice with human 596 
amyloidomas (soft tissue tumors of AL composition created in the hindquarters of mice) 597 
11-1F4 induced a rapid reduction of the masses without toxicity151. In an open-label, dose-598 
escalation, phase 1 trial, 11-1F4 was well tolerated by all treated patients without dose-599 
limited adverse events and promising organ responses after completion of phase 1a. 600 
Overall, 27 patients were treated with 11-1F4 in this study (8 patients during phase 1a and 601 
19 patients during phase 1b).  Of the evaluable patients, 63% demonstrated an organ 602 
response after infusion of 11-1F4 in phase 1a, and 61%demonstrated an organ response 603 
in phase 1b, with a median time to response of 2 weeks after the start of treatment152. No 604 
grade 4 or 5 adverse events were reported, and further clinical trials of 11-1F4 are 605 
currently being planned. 606 
A third antibody approach is potentially applicable to all types of amyloidosis targeted 607 
SAP. Depletion of circulating SAP using miridesap allows the subsequent administration of 608 
the humanized anti-SAP antibody, dezamizumab, which binds to residual SAP in amyloid 609 
deposits and induces a macrophage response that triggers their rapid removal153. In an 610 

 
20 
 
open label, dose–escalation phase 1 clinical trial (NCT01777243), 16 patients with AA 611 
(amyloidosis A; formerly known as secondary amyloidosis), AL and AApoAI (Amyloid 612 
ApoplipoproteinA1) amyloidosis received a single dose of antibody. Mild infusion reactions 613 
and rashes were observed in some patients whom received larger doses, but no serious 614 
adverse events were reported. 123 I-SAP scintigraphy confirmed amyloid removal from the 615

Further evaluation of the safety, pharmacokinetics, and dose-response effects of up to 617

[H3] Cardiac disease. 630 
Patients with cardiac dysfunction due to AL amyloidosis should be managed differently 631 
from those with cardiac dysfunction caused by other factors. Patients with AL 632 
cardiomyopathy do not typically tolerate β-blockers, calcium channel blockers, angiotensin 633 
converting enzyme inhibitors, or angiotensin receptor blockers. The sinus tachycardia for 634 
many of these patients is physiological and necessary to maintain adequate cardiac 635 
output. Careful diuresis, avoiding over-diuresis, and avoidance of drugs that may reduce 636 
cardiac output are the best strategies for the treatment of cardiac dysfunction owing to AL 637 
amyloidosis. Loop diuretics are most commonly used, of which, torsemide has a better 638 
bioavailability than furosemide. Spironolactone and metolazone can be used as adjunctive 639 
diuretics. Superimposed nephrotic syndrome or autonomic dysfunction makes the 640 
management of amyloid cardiomyopathy even more challenging since both hamper 641 
diuresis. In patients with atrial fibrillation or flutter, amiodarone is the best tolerated drug. 642 
Atrial ablation and atrioventricular nodal ablation can also be of value155. Careful use of 643 
digoxin in patients with atrial fibrillation or flutter and low blood pressure should not be 644 

 
21 
 
discounted156. Ventricular arrhythmias, especially premature ventricular contractions, are 645 
common and the presence of couplets and complex arrhythmias are prognostic;157 646 
however, defibrillators are less effective in patients with AL amyloidosis in part because 647 
pulseless electrical activity is one of the more common pre-terminal events158-161. Patients 648 
on β-blockers might have increased risk of bradycardia owing to complete atrioventricular 649 
block, and in one series, the pre-cardiac arrest rhythm was bradycardia in all 8 patients, 650 
including a complete heart block in 6 patients162. Which patients with AL amyloidosis might 651 
benefit from cardiac defibrillations is unclear. Pacemakers can be useful with patients with 652 
chronotropic incompetence.  653 
Doxycycline has demonstrated anti-amyloid activities in vitro and in vivo. 654 
Doxycycline interferes with amyloid formation in a mouse model of AL amyloidosis163 and 655 
abrogates light chain toxicity in vitro164. Indeed, the addition of doxycycline to standard 656 
chemotherapy reduced early mortality in cardiac AL amyloidosis in a retrospective case 657 
matched study165. An international phase III trial is ongoing in newly diagnosed patients 658 
with advanced cardiac involvement comparing standard of care (that is, bortezomib-based 659 
therapies) versus standard of care plus doxycycline (NCT03474458). 660 
Orthotopic heart transplantation might be used in selected patients.166 167 Key 661 
determinants for the best outcomes following transplantation include limiting candidates to 662 
those who have lower tumor burden, accepting candidates who have clinical organ 663 
involvement limited to the heart and administering chemotherapy that is effective against 664 
the clone. However, most patients do not satisfy these criteria. Many patients awaiting 665 
transplant do not survive long enough to receive an orthotopic heart. For patients who do 666 
receive transplantation, five-year overall survival ranges from 18% to 66%168. Some of the 667 
best results have been in patients who have ASCT after their cardiac transplant169, 170, but 668 
with improving therapies directed at the plasma cell clone, one could consider other non-669 
ASCT options.  670

[H3] Other symptoms. 689 
Autonomic neuropathy alone or with other organ involvement is very difficult to manage. In 690

[H1] Outlook  742 
Great advances have been made during the last decade in understanding the 743 
mechanisms of AL amyloidosis and in treatment, which have translated into better QOL 744 
and improved survival. However, less than one-quarter of patients achieve a complete and 745 
long-lasting hematologic remission and survive for more than 10 years7, 47. Production of 746 
AL amyloid light chain precursors by clonal plasma cells still cannot be adequately 747 
suppressed in most cases, and the function of vital organs impaired by amyloid improves 748 
in only one quarter of patients on an intention to treat basis7. Furthermore, ~20% of 749 
patients are diagnosed at a late stage when cardiac damage is very advanced and 750 
survival can be measured in weeks. At the present time, therapy directed towards the 751 
underlying clonal disease improves the outcome in approximately 30% of these patients, 752 
but anti-amyloid therapies might increase survival in additional patients. Improved 753 
awareness of AL amyloidosis and diagnostic methods to enable earlier diagnosis before 754 
cardiac damage has become irreversible is one of the major aims of current research. 755 
In addition, searching for new drugs is ongoing. For instance, the proteotoxicity exerted by 756 
amyloid, misfolded, LCs, could be harnessed for therapy. The distinctive perinuclear 757 
distribution of mitochondria in plasma cells from patients with amyloidosis as compared 758 
with plasma cells from patients with multiple myeloma and individuals with MGUS is 759 
indicative of oxidative stress, which was further supported by the abundance of transcripts 760 
encoding organelle-resident redox sensors177. Moreover, the expression of amyloidogenic 761 
light chains in myeloma cells altered cell growth and proteostasis through proteotoxicity, 762 
and conferred sensitivity to bortezomib; accordingly, proteasome inhibitors are targeted 763 
therapy in AL amyloidosis, and might direct future anti-clone drug development177. Drugs 764 
targeting the ubiquitin/proteasome system are under development for multiple myeloma in 765 
order to overcome resistance to proteasome inhibitors178 and preliminary data obtained in 766 
primary amyloidogenic plasma cells indicate potential activity for AL amyloidosis. 767 
Researchers are focusing their efforts on investigation of the biological characteristics of 768 
amyloidogenic B-cell clones and on development of novel agents and their optimal use in 769 
combinations to provide high rates of eradication. Sensitive technologies based on mass 770 
spectrometry are being developed to detect trace amounts of the amyloid LC, along with 771 
next generation flow cytometry and sequencing for the detection of minimal residual 772 
disease. Complete eradication of the clonal disease is expected to be associated with 773 
higher recovery of organ dysfunction and long-lasting remission and might become the 774 

 
25 
 
next therapeutic goal. Other open questions are the heterogeneity of organ involvement in 775 
a single patient and the mechanisms underlying the vital organ dysfunction caused by 776 
amyloidosis, particularly in the heart. Pathogenetic mechanisms being studied include 777 
direct disruption by amyloid of the myocardial architecture, coronary vasculature and 778 
autonomic nerves and cytotoxicity of amyloid fibrils and their soluble precursors. Although 779 
some ancestral models of amyloidosis (such as in C. elegans and zebrafish) have been 780 
used to investigate the mechanism of amyloid cardiac damage, other animal models of AL 781 
amyloidosis are urgently needed. The development of novel sensitive biomarkers and 782 
imaging technologies, notably including tissue characterization with MRI, for the detection 783 
and quantification of organ involvement and damage are already facilitating earlier 784 
diagnosis and enhanced evaluation of the efficacy of new and existing therapies. The 785
