# Decreased TXNRD1 is associated with resistance to tagraxofusp in blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasms, as seen in phase II.
**Authors:** Beird HC, Kannan S, Liu J, Chen J, Olguin A, Desai P, Cai T, Han L, Cotton JL, Singh S, Gupta SK, Little L, Estecio MR, Song X, Abbas HA, Zhang J, Gumbs C, Brooks C, Kantarjian H, Andreeff M, Konopleva M, Futreal PA, Pemmaraju N
**Journal:** Leukemia (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41375-026-03022-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41375-026-03022-0)

## Content

Tagraxofusp is a CD123-targeted therapy comprised of a recombinant human interleukin-3 (IL-3) fused to a truncated diphtheria toxin payload. It is the first approved treatment specifically for patients with blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm (BPDCN). To identify biomarkers of response, bone marrow samples from 12 BPDCN patients who were treated with tagraxofusp in the pivotal phase II trial (NCT02113982) were profiled longitudinally using a gene panel and single-cell RNA sequencing. Residual tumor cells following tagraxofusp expressed lower levels of TXNRD1 that would reduce the efficacy of tagraxofusp. In support of this, enzymatic inhibition of TXNRD1 resulted in higher viability of CAL-1 BPDCN cells following tagraxofusp. Responders had either wild-type or missense TET2 mutations, while transient and non-responders had at least one truncating TET2 mutation. Examples of these mutations within the catalytic domain of TET2 were constructed and transduced into cells. Missense and truncating mutants displayed reduced sensitivities to hypomethylating agents and prolonged S-phase stasis. These results suggest that the levels of TXNRD1 interact with intrinsic TET2 truncating mutations within the bone marrow to modulate patient response to tagraxofusp.

Blastic Plasmacytoid Dendritic-Cell Neoplasm (BPDCN) is a hematologic malignancy with a non-viral origin that occurs predominantly in older males (3:1–5:1) [1, 2]. The disease is heterogeneous in both presentation and in patterns of progression, with involvement in multiple tissue sites [3, 4]. Diagnosis is confounded by the presence of dendritic, lymphoid, and myeloid markers [5] and of concurrent myeloid and lymphoid malignancies [6–8]. Currently, positivity in CD4 + CD56 + CD123+ by flow cytometry and immunohistochemistry is used to confirm diagnosis. At the molecular level, multiple co-existing truncating and missense mutations in TET2 occur in a majority of these patients that are often associated with clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) [9–11]. Divergent evolution of the founding TET2 mutant clone would produce a mixed phenotype and co-incidence of other leukemias [7].

Aberrant immune activity in BPDCN patients may contribute to its pathobiology. High levels of pro-inflammatory molecules RANTES (CCL5) and eotaxin, along with high expression of PTPRS that prevents hyperactivation of plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC), suggest that pDC cell functions are abnormal in BPDCN patients [12, 13]. One route to impairment is through ZRSR2 mutations (in 16% of BPDCN patients [9]), which induce intron retention in pro-inflammatory transcription factor IRF7 that prevents dendritic cell activation and apoptosis [14]. Recent or chronic inflammation may also be a feature of BPDCN, as denoted by high expression levels of LAMP5 [12, 14], IFNA-related genes, and exhausted T-cells [15].

Based on the overexpression of IL3RA/CD123 in BPDCN patients, tagraxofusp was developed and approved [16]. Tagraxofusp is a CD123-targeted therapy comprised of a recombinant human interleukin-3 (IL-3) fused to the catalytic and translocation domains of diphtheria toxin payload. It is the first targeted agent approved for the treatment of BPDCN with improved outcomes, prolonged survival, and facilitated successful bridging to stem cell transplantation, particularly in the frontline treatment setting and older patients [17]. Longer-term follow-up has confirmed these clinical results with upfront tagraxofusp administration [17]. Here, we have conducted molecular profiling on pre- and post-treatment bone marrow cells from BPDCN patients enrolled in the pivotal Phase II trial of tagraxofusp (NCT02113982) to identify molecular markers of response [16].

Mononuclear cells were cryopreserved from bone marrow aspirates at screening, at the end of cycles 1, 2, and any subsequent cycles 4 and 6 of treatment if the patient was still on study. For profiling, cells were thawed, centrifuged (1500 rpm, 5 min), and 500,000 cells were placed in PBS for gene panel sequencing. The remaining cells were submitted for hCD45 live cell sorting, and 200,000 cells in 200 µL of PBS were submitted for scRNAseq (Supplementary Methods).

Single-cell separation, cDNA amplification, and library construction were performed using reagents and protocols from 10X Genomics, and MINSEQE guidelines were followed (Pleasanton, CA). Cellular suspensions from CD45-sorted bone marrow cells were placed into bead-in-emulsions (10x Chromium Single Cell Controller) and libraries were constructed (Chromium Single Cell 3ʹ Library & Gel Bead Kit v.3.1), qualified (Supplementary Methods) and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq4000 (150 bp paired-end reads). The sequenced raw data were preprocessed using the 10X Genomics Cell Ranger Single Cell Software Suite version 5.0 [18] and cleaned (Supplementary Methods). Seurat 4.1.0 [19] was used for cell clustering and differential analysis. Cluster annotation was done manually using canonical markers. Copy number calling was done with erythrocyte cells as reference (inferCNV [20]). Based on annotations and copy number calls, four tumor clusters were identified: clusters 10, 22, 28, and 29.

Genomic DNA was extracted from bone marrow specimens (Frozen Tissue protocol 389 from the QIAamp DNA Mini kit, Qiagen, Inc., Valencia, CA). Libraries were prepared for the upgraded in-house liquid tumor panel (T300) (Agilent SureSelect Custom DNA Target Enrichment Probes) (Supplementary Table 1) and sequenced with Illumina HiSeq. An in-house virtual normal control was used to identify somatic alterations. We annotated variants with the Mastermind Genomic Search Engine [21].

We used the MigR1 retroviral MSCV mammalian expression system containing GFP marker for cloning the catalytic domain (CATDO) full length of TET2, a missense mutation p.Arg1359Cys, and two frameshift mutations p.Met1456fsTer41 and p.Met1456delTer22 (See Supplementary Methods). TET2 CATDO variant constructs and control MigR1 were transduced in immortalized human dendritic cells (ImmoDC) (Cat. No. T0525, Applied Biological Materials Inc., Richmond, Canada), CAL-1 BPDCN cell line (RRID: CVCL_5G46), and AML lines OCIAML3 (RRID: CVCL_1844) and MOLM13 (RRID: CVCL_2110) (Supplementary Methods). TET2 loss of function is reported for CAL-1 and WT in OCIAML3 and MOLM13 (https://depmap.org/portal).

Cells were treated with the indicated concentrations of agents and analyzed using the CellTiter-Glo® Luminescent Cell Viability Assay. Viability was determined by the percentage of DAPI (4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) positive dead cells as described previously [22].

For 40,000 cells/swell in triplicate, 100 nM of azacytidine (AZA) and 5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine (DAC) were added on alternate days up to four days. The TET2 inhibitor Bobcat339 hydrochloride (Cat. No. HY-111558A, MedChemExpress, NJ, USA) that targets gene methylation and TRi-1, irreversible inhibitor of TXNRD1 (HY-125006, MedChemExpress, NJ, USA) were given (Supplementary Methods). CAL1 and immoDC cell lines overexpressing TET2CatDo wildtype and mutants were treated with HMA at 100 nM, and samples were harvested at 12 and 24 h for CpG sequencing. Results were compared with control DMSO-treated samples. Viable cell numbers of GFP expression were normalized to viable parental cells. TET2 CATDO constructs were normalized to Mig-GFP control.

One million CAL-1 cells were plated into a flat-bottom 96-well tissue culture plate. TRi-1 was given at 5 and 10 μM for 5 h in a CO2 incubator. Cells were washed once with complete RPMI medium, resuspended in complete RPMI, and distributed at 50k/100 μL/well. Cells were then treated with increasing doses of SL-401 (10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 ng/mL) in quadruplicate (n = 4). After 38 h, viability was assessed with CellTiter-Glo.

One million cells (ImmoDC, CAL-1, MOLM13, OCIAML3) were plated in 10 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) dissolved in PBS for 6 h. The media was washed and replaced after 48 h and 96 h. Statistical analysis was evaluated by 2-way ANOVA with Tukey multiple comparison tests.

One microgram of gDNA was treated with sodium bisulfite (EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit, Zymo Research, Irvine, CA). Both bisulfite conversion and subsequent pyrosequencing analysis were done at the DNA Methylation Analysis Core. PCR primers for pyrosequencing methylation analysis of H19 and LINE-1 were designed using the Pyromark Assay Design Software 2.0 (Qiagen, Germany) (Supplementary Methods). Sequencing was performed on a PyroMark Q96 ID instrument with the PyroMark Gold Q96 Reagents (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The degree of methylation for each individual CpG site was calculated using the PyroMark Q96 software. The average methylation of all sites and duplicates was reported for each sample.

RNA was extracted from viral vector-induced cells and converted to cDNA (Supplementary Methods). Triplicate wells of each cell type and condition were run using the Applied Biosystems™ reagents on the QuantStudio 6 Flex machine (Life Technologies, Inc.). The FAM-MGB-labeled Taqman Gene Expression Assays: CCL3 (Hs00234142_m1), CCL4 (Hs99999148_m1), CCL5 (Hs00982282_m1), and LAMP5 (Hs00202136_m1) were normalized to the housekeeping gene GAPDH (Hs02786624_g1) (ThermoFisher).

T cells were subset based on the expression of CD3D, CD3E, and CD3G. Seurat 4.3.0 was used for cell clustering and differential gene expression analyses. To avoid bias in clustering, TCR genes were removed from the variable features. Cluster annotation was done manually based on canonical gene expression patterns. Single-sample gene set variation analysis using Hallmark pathways was used to calculate pathway scores [23, 24].

The levels of TXNRD1 in cluster 22 against all other clusters were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test. In the heatmaps, the top 10 differentially expressed genes (P < 0.01) and the largest log-fold change difference among response groups were displayed. The Student’s t-test with unpaired samples, populations with equal variance, was used to compare each vector-induced condition or inhibitor-drug-treated condition against parental cells. For the differentially expressed pathways, the normalized enrichment scores that account for the total number of genes within a given pathway were used for comparison across pathways. The gene expression levels of HBA1 and HBB in CD4 + FOXP3 + T cells were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test. The Wilcoxon rank sum was used for comparing each pair of response groups.

Patients included were participants in the pivotal tagraxofusp clinical trial [16, 17]. Bone marrow specimens were taken for gene panel DNA sequencing and scRNAseq ( > 70% viable cells) in parallel (Fig. 1A). There were 6 responders (R), 4 transient responders (TR), and 2 nonresponders (NR). Best overall response was complete response with no progression for R (N = 6), partial or complete response with progression for TR (N = 4), and no objective response at any timepoint (NR) (N = 2) (Fig. 1B).

We screened bone marrow specimens for recurring somatic mutations using an in-house panel related to hematologic malignancies at an average sequencing depth of 637X to accurately detect mutations in samples with 1% blasts [25]. This panel includes genes that are frequently mutated in BPDCN (Supplementary Table 1).

TET2 mutations were confirmed longitudinally in six of twelve patients (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 1A–F), with five having two co-occurring, coding TET2 mutations. In the R group, samples either had missense or no TET2 mutations. Truncating TET2 mutations were more prevalent in the TR and NR groups. For B-102, the variant allele frequencies for these TET2 mutations at screening (70% blasts) were between 0.3 and 0.4, consistent with heterozygous mutations. In contrast, those from patients B-105, B-109 and B-111 were much higher than the per cent blasts detected. Since TET2 mutations have not been detected in the germline of BPDCN patients, CHIP is the more likely explanation [9]. For B-102 and B-105, these missense mutations were located within the oxygenase domain of TET2 (p.Phe1321Cys in B-102 and p.Pro1744Ser in B-105). B-108 had one truncating TET2 mutation, p.Pro989fs, upstream of the oxygenase domain (VAF = 0.464), while B-109 and B-110 each had one truncating and one missense TET2 mutation. In the NR group, B-111 had one truncating and one missense TET2 mutation, whereas B-112 had no TET2 mutations. Losses or large deletions within or including TET2 were not detected. Other truncating point mutations within genes previously reported as altered in BPDCN were observed in responders B-101 (ZRSR2 p.Ser434fs) and B-102 (ARID1A p.Gln658X and CDKN2A p.Arg58X) and in non-responders B-110 (NF1 p.Trp784X) and B-111 (EZH2 p.Gln251X) (Supplementary Fig. 1G). To understand the impact of TET2 mutations, we generated viral-mediated transgene constructs containing mutations within the TET2 catalytic domain (TET2 CATDO) (Fig. 2A). The first contains the missense mutation (p.Arg1359Cys) that was reported in a BPDCN patient [9]. Next, we engineered the p.Met1456fsTer41 variant that has been observed in BPDCN [12, 26], in AML as a germline variant [27], in CMML as a somatic mutation [28] and contains a foreign peptide (see Supplementary Methods for details). We also generated a p.Met1456delTer22 mutation where the Met1456 is deleted and truncated 22 amino acids later. These constructs were transduced into Immortalized dendritic cells (ImmoDC), CAL-1 BPDCN cells, and AML lines OCIAML3 and MOLM13 (see “Methods”). First, ImmoDC accommodated both truncating mutants, but the vector alone, WT, and the missense mutation failed to expand (Fig. 2B). In contrast, all transduced CAL-1, OCIAML3, and MOLM13 cells could be passaged. ImmoDC and CAL-1 cells expressing the truncating mutants acquired enhanced viability and growth advantage (Fig. 2B). Similar viability trends were observed in MOLM13, but not OCIAML3 (Supplementary Fig. 2). To assess the effect of TET2 CATDO in a clinical setting, we treated CAL-1 cells with the hypomethylating agents AZA and DAC at subtoxic doses (Fig. 2D). Viability was decreased in TET2 WT upon HMA treatment, whereas this decrease was less pronounced in missense and p.Met1456fsTer41 overexpressing cells.

TET2 is the primary enzyme responsible for the oxidation of 5-methylcytosine (5mC) into 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) on both DNA and chromatin-associated RNAs [29, 30]. Therefore, we used pyrosequencing to assess the levels of 5mC in four well-established CpG regions in transduced cells (Fig. 2E, F). Overexpression of TET2 WT and mutant constructs resulted in demethylation of H19 and LINE-1 CpG islands as compared to parental and empty vector transduced cells. This effect was more pronounced in the LINE-1 CpG elements for p.Met1456fsTer22 expressing CAL-1 cells as compared to WT and missense constructs. TET2 missense and truncating mutations were less sensitive to hypomethylating agents after 12 h of treatment, with some exhibiting higher sensitivity at 24 h (Fig. 2E, F). This suggests that these mutants reduce the effectiveness of the demethylating agent as compared to WT.

From the 98,762 cells sequenced, 29 minimal clusters were identified (Fig. 3). We used reported markers of BPDCN and inferred copy number variation to identify clusters that were BPDCN cells (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Tables 2–4). Three of these clusters had high expression of the 45-gene signature for BPDCN (clusters 10, 28, and 29) (Supplementary Fig. 3) [31]. Loss of chr3p in cluster 28 and loss of chr7p in cluster 10 were also consistent with previous reports [5, 31] and karyotyping confirmed other copy number calls (Supplementary Figs. 4–5). The proportions of tumor clusters were not associated with overall response. Therefore, we examined the changes in tumor populations over time. Clusters 28 and 29 were eliminated over the course of treatment. For every patient except for B-107, cluster 22 remained (Fig. 4A–C). This cluster was characterized by lower levels of TCL1A and GLUL, a four-fold increase in GZMB, which may indicate a highly inflamed context [32], and >2-fold higher levels of dendritic cell maturation regulators CST3 [33], LILRA4 [34] (Supplementary Table 4). Thioredoxin reductase (TXNRD1) was significantly lower in cluster 22 as compared to all other tumor clusters (Kruskal-Wallis test, p < 1e-15) (Fig. 4D). Since the absence of TXNRD1 would prevent translocation of the diphtheria toxin payload from the endosome into the cytosol, thereby preventing its ability to inhibit EF2, this absence is a potential mechanism of resistance [35]. To test this, CAL-1 BPDCN cells were pre-incubated with the inhibitor TRi-1 that inhibits TXNRD1 irreversibly in an NADPH-dependent manner [36] and treated with tagraxofusp. Inhibition of TXNRD1 resulted in enhanced viability of CAL-1, which indicated resistance to tagraxofusp in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4E). The single-cell data showed that TET2 and TXNRD1 expression levels follow similar increase and decrease patterns over time (Supplementary Fig. 8). To test whether there is any codependency between TET2 and TXNRD1, we treated CAL-1 cells with both a TET2 inhibitor, Bobcat339 (TET2i) and the TXNRD1 inhibitor TRi-1. Dose-dependent increases in viability occurred with TET2i, which recapitulated the effects of our TET2 CATDO truncating mutants (Fig. 4F). TRi-1 also achieved a similar effect, which is expected to impair the cleavage of diphtheria toxin and cell survival. When combining TET2i and TRi-1, steady inhibition of TET2 enhanced the TXNRD1-mediated effects of higher viability. These increases in viability were accompanied by decreases in apoptosis (Fig. 4F). This postulates a codependency between TET2 and TXNRD1. Next, we compared the sensitivities of the TET2 mutation-expressing constructs in ImmoDC and CAL-1 in single and combination treatments of the TET2i, Tri-1, and hypomethylating agent DAC (Fig. 4G). There, the M1456delTer22 mutant in CAL-1 was the most resistant to any treatment configuration as compared to all other constructs.

Given that clusters 10 and 22 were the most numerous tumor cells across patients, we identified differentially expressed pathways between them. The top significantly enriched pathway in cluster 22 was HALLMARK_ALLOGRAFT_REJECTION, which included GZMB and chemokines CCL3, CCL4, and CCL5. KRAS signaling was also elevated in cluster 22, which may indicate the downstream effect of GAS6 [37], one of the top- expressed genes in cluster 22 (Supplementary Fig. 9).

To identify genes associated with response, we compared R and NR (Supplementary Fig. 10; Supplementary Table 6). Chemokines CCL3 and CCL4 were enriched in the monocyte populations (clusters 7, 10, and 17) of TR and NR and were also among the top 10 markers of T-cells in TR than in R (STAT3-expressing cytotoxic CD8 + T-cells) (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Table 7). A related chemokine, CCL5, was predominantly expressed in CD4 + T-cells (Supplementary Table 8), NK cells, and tumor cells of TR and NR (Supplementary Fig. 11). Higher gene expression of these chemokines can be induced following challenge with lipopolysaccharide to a greater degree in tumor cells expressing TET2 truncating mutants as compared to TET2 WT (Supplementary Fig. 12). Significantly higher proportions of CD4 + T-cell numbers, lower proportions of CD8 + T-cell numbers, and enriched TNFα signaling occurred in R as compared with TR and NR (Supplementary Fig. 13; Supplementary Table 9).

We conducted molecular profiling of bone marrow specimens before and during various cycles of tagraxofusp treatment from the pivotal phase II trial for BPDCN. At least four tumor populations (clusters 10, 22, 28, and 29) were shared among all patients examined, underscoring the phenotypic heterogeneity of BPDCN. Tumor clusters 28 and 29 were eliminated, while cluster 22 comprised the largest proportion of residual tumor cells post-treatment regardless of overall response to tagraxofusp. Out of the CHIP genes that we profiled, only TET2 was altered in more than half of BPDCN patients. Responders were either WT or harbored missense TET2 mutations, while TR and NR had at least one truncating mutation, supporting our previous study in BPDCN, suggesting that patients with TET2 truncating mutations tend to have a worse prognosis (30/57, 52.6%) [10, 38]. ImmoDC and CAL-1 cells expressing TET2CatDo truncating mutations were more viable than with WT and missense R1359C and may mimic the effects of TET2 KO in the decreased methylation of H19 and LINE-1 CpG islands. Viability also increased when CAL-1 cells were treated with TET2i. Conversely, overexpressing TET2 WT protein results in decreased viability and increased demethylation of CpG islands in H19 and LINE-1 elements. This suggests that having the TET2 WT protein might rescue the tumor suppressor effect of the loss-of-function mutant TET2 in parental CAL-1 [39]. In addition, TET2 mutations are associated with defects of the G1-S and/or intra-S transition that may show intra-S phase checkpoint [40, 41], promoting cell proliferation in myeloid neoplasms [42], and chromosomal and centrosomal abnormalities [41]. Our data also support this delay in the S-phase in TET2 mutants.

Truncating mutations may be heterogeneous in their functions - some more accommodating and adaptive for cell proliferation and survival (e.g., ImmoDC cells expressing M1456delTer22 that could be passaged long-term), while others may not be tolerated and become eliminated (e.g., ImmoDC cells with M1456fsTer41). CAL-1 cells with M1456fsTer41 were delayed in their hypomethylation of LINE-1 CpG elements after treatment with a demethylating agent, while those with M1456delTer22 were still resistant after 24 h of treatment. These heterogeneous functions could explain the transient response of some patients with truncating mutations and no response of others to tagraxofusp. While we focused on H19 and LINE-1 to validate the epigenetic deregulation secondary to TET2 mutations, it is anticipated that multiple loci are differentially methylated in BPDCN with potential correlation to response to tagraxofusp. Previous studies have demonstrated that BPDCN are epigenetically distinct from other hematological malignancies (PMID 38600314, PMID 30381297) [43, 44], further supporting the need for future evaluation of the methylome of BPDCN in the context of response to therapies. TET2 mutation status is most likely a prognostic, but not a predictive biomarker of response to tagraxofusp.

The IL3 portion of tagraxofusp binds to IL3RA/CD123, and the entire drug is internalized within the cell. The rate-determining step in diphtheria toxin-mediated cell killing is the breakage of the disulfide bond between the catalytic and translocation domains of diphtheria toxin upon exposure to the reducing environment of the cytosol [45]. This catalytic portion is then released from the endosome into the cytosol by TXNRD1 and heat shock protein 90. Once in the cytosol, this domain inhibits the activity of the diphthamide-modified version of elongation factor EF2, which blocks protein translation and causes aberrant mitosis [46, 47]. Previous work has shown that BPDCN cells can become resistant to tagraxofusp by expressing lower levels of DPH1, the enzyme that forms diphthamide, and that resistance can be reversed with AZA that increases DPH1 expression [48]. Unlike that study, we found reduced expression of TXNRD1, a key enzyme in the cytosolic release of diphtheria toxin, in cluster 22. To support this, inhibition of TXNRD1 with TRi1 in CAL-1 cells favored viability with decreased apoptosis. Downregulation of TXNRD1 would reduce the cleavage of the disulfide bonds between the catalytic and translocation domains in the diphtheria toxin region of tagraxofusp and result in resistance to tagraxofusp-mediated killing. Alternatively, as an indirect consequence of TXNRD1-downregulation due to acquired resistance to tagraxofusp, TXNRD1-specific functions related to apoptosis that are likely independent of the mechanism of action of tagraxofusp may also be suppressed, thereby explaining the survival advantage observed in the cells in these preclinical experiments with inhibition of TXNRD1 in the absence of tagraxofusp exposure. Induced rather than naturally occurring mechanisms of resistance may explain the difference in findings from Togami et al. and our study here, although both mechanisms indicate that successful processing of diphtheria toxin contributes to effective treatment. Co-suppression of TET2 (non-functional TET2 condition) using TET2i and TXNRD1 potentiated the survival of cells, indicating a synergy between these genes that tagraxofusp likely exploits. The enrichment of KRAS signaling in this cluster also suggests increased aggressiveness since KRAS is a negative marker of survival in diploid AML and is associated with lower rates of complete response following chemotherapy in AML [49–51].

Chemokines CCL3, CCL4, and CCL5 were present at higher levels in TR and NR than in the R group in various non-tumor cell types. These chemokines induce other inflammatory cytokines, TNFα, IL1B, and IL6, which are chemoattractants for T and B lymphocytes [52]. TET2 deficiency has also been associated with chemokine release after immune challenges. Exposure to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in TET2 knockout mice on a high-fat diet or TET2 knockout preleukemia cells induces CCL3 and CCL4 [53, 54]. CCL5 is released inTET2 knockout mice given uric acid crystals and inTET2 knockout reticulum cell sarcoma cells after LPS stimulation [55, 56]. These chemokines may be the mechanism by which hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells with TET2 knockout can enhance the expansion of myeloid cells after LPS challenge more efficiently than TET2 WT cells [54]. Since UV light induces immunomodulatory effects [57], future exploration to determine whether UV light exposure affects chemokine release and immunomodulation in BPDCN patients with TET2 truncating mutants is warranted.

Here, we have identified TXNRD1 as a potential biomarker of response for further investigation. If successful, TXNRD1 could be monitored by flow cytometry on biopsies. As the understanding of BPDCN pathobiology improves, combinations with tagraxofusp are anticipated as the gold standard backbone in both frontline and relapsed-refractory settings. Indeed, several international BPDCN consortia are investigating CD123-targeted therapy as the backbone with novel combinations across pediatric and adult patients with BPDCN and AML [58, 59]. Patient-derived xenograft models show improved survival when tagraxofusp and AZA are combined [48]. The combination of tagraxofusp/AZA/venetoclax is safe and effective in patients with AML, MDS, and BPDCN (NCT0311364) [60].
