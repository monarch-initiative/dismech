Propionic acidemia (PA), arising from PCCA or PCCB variants, manifests as 
life-threatening cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias, with unclear pathophysiology. 
In this work, propionyl-CoA metabolism in rodent hearts and human pluripotent 
stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes was investigated with stable isotope tracing 
analysis. Surprisingly, gut microbiome-derived propionate rather than the 
propiogenic amino acids (valine, isoleucine, threonine, and methionine) or 
odd-chain fatty acids was found to be the primary cardiac propionyl-CoA source. 
In a Pcca-/-(A138T) mouse model and PA patients, accumulated propionyl-CoA and 
diminished acyl-CoA synthetase short-chain family member 3 impede hepatic 
propionate disposal, elevating circulating propionate. Prolonged propionate 
exposure induced significant oxidative stress in PCCA knockdown HL-1 cells and 
the hearts of Pcca-/-(A138T) mice. Additionally, Pcca-/-(A138T) mice exhibited 
mild diastolic dysfunction after the propionate challenge. These findings 
suggest that elevated circulating propionate may cause oxidative damage and 
functional impairment in the hearts of patients with PA.

Propionic acidemia (PA) is an autosomal recessive inherited metabolic disorder caused by defective functioning in a mitochondrial enzyme, propionyl-CoA carboxylase (PCC) [12, 21, 23, 24, 29, 40, 41, 51, 53, 61, 66, 67]. This condition presents with a spectrum of complications, encompassing hypotonia, intellectual disability, epilepsy, growth failure, abnormal feeding behavior, constipation, pancreatitis, cardiomyopathy, and prolonged QTc and ventricular dysrhythmia, among other manifestations [1, 10, 22, 32, 45, 59, 62]. Notably, recent advancements in cardiac diagnosis reveal a high prevalence of life-threatening cardiac complications in individuals with PA [5, 18, 19, 25, 33, 34, 36, 37, 44, 46, 58].

The life-threatening cardiac manifestations of PA have garnered increased attention. Identifying risk factors for cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias in PA is a prioritized research area for the Propionic Acidemia Foundation. Many pathological models have been proposed to understand the pathological mechanism of cardiac dysfunction associated with PA. The elevated propionate could inhibit the potassium channel [7] and trigger the increase in miRNA expression [14, 48]. Propionyl-CoA or its metabolite not only inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase [52], 2-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase [36], and glutamate dehydrogenase [3], but also disrupts calcium homeostasis and induces reactive oxygen species (ROS) according to published reports [15–17, 49]. All of these factors might contribute to the development of PA-associated cardiomyopathy.

The overwhelming data suggests that the accumulation of propionyl-CoA and its metabolites leads to various complications including cardiac dysfunction in patients with PA [42, 70]. This is also strongly supported by the effectiveness of treatment with a protein-restricted diet low in propiogenic amino acids, valine (Val), isoleucine (Ile), threonine (Thr), and methionine (Met). Poor diet management is a major risk factor for various PA-associated complications including cardiomyopathy [11]. However, despite adherence to dietary therapy, the risk of developmental delay and cardiomyopathy remains elevated [28, 58], underscoring that the restriction of propiogenic amino acids alone is not sufficient to prevent PA-associated cardiac complications.

Organs share common metabolic activities but also exhibit organ specificity. Understanding heart-specific propionyl-CoA metabolism is essential to unravel the pathogenic mechanism of cardiac complications in PA. In this study, we utilized a comprehensive toolset for a systematic investigation into propionyl-CoA turnover and its metabolic sources in the heart and human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (h-iPSC-CMs). Surprisingly, propiogenic amino acids (Ile, Val, Thr, and Met) and odd-chain fatty acids contribute minimally to propionyl-CoA synthesis in the heart. Circulating propionate emerges as the major metabolic source of cardiac propionyl-CoA. In normal healthy conditions, the liver efficiently removes 99% of propionate derived from the gut microbiome, and circulating propionate remains at low levels after liver clearance. However, in PA patients and Pcca−/−(A138T) mice, impaired propionate clearance by the liver results in elevated plasma propionate, overwhelming cardiac propionyl-CoA metabolism and leading to its accumulation and oxidative stress due to defective propionyl-CoA carboxylase (PCC) activity. Cardiomyocytes are particularly vulnerable to elevated propionate levels when PCC activity is impaired, highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of cardiomyocyte-specific propionyl-CoA metabolism to develop targeted therapies for cardiac complications in PA.

All animal procedures were ethically approved by the IACUC Committee of Duke University. Rats weighing approximately 250 g were subjected to anesthesia using 5% isoflurane in an induction chamber, with maintenance at 2% via a facial mask during the surgical procedure. A median laparotomy was performed, and a heparin bolus (500 U/kg) was administered via the inferior vena cava. Following the diaphragm incision, the thorax was filled with 100 ml of ice-cold Krebs–Henseleit buffer to induce cardioplegia. The heart was swiftly excised and immersed in ice-cold Krebs–Henseleit buffer until complete cardioplegia was achieved. Subsequently, the aortic arch was meticulously dissected and cannulated using an 11-gauge stainless steel cannula connected to a modified Langendorff apparatus [30, 64].

The hearts were perfused retrogradely (Langendorff) with gassed (95% O2 and 5% CO2) modified Krebs–Henseleit buffer (pH 7.4), comprising 119 mM NaCl, 4.8 mM KCl, 2.6 mM CaCl2, 1.2 mM KH2PO4, 1.2 mM MgSO4, 25 mM NaHCO3, 11 mM glucose, and 0.05 mM carnitine. Perfusion was maintained at a constant flow rate (12 ml/min) using a RAININRabbit-Plus peristaltic pump (Woburn, MA) in a nonrecirculating mode, equipped with an inline 0.22-mm pore size filter (Millipore, Billerica, MA). Temperature control was achieved through a temperature-controlled cabinet and a water-jacketed chamber, ensuring a constant heart temperature of 37 °C. Spontaneous heartbeating was allowed throughout the perfusion process. The hearts were initially perfused with Krebs–Henseleit buffer for 15 minutes, followed by a switch to perfusate containing labeled tracers for the final 30 minutes. At the conclusion of each perfusion, the hearts were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

The perfusions were conducted in seven distinct groups with [13C3]propionate as follows: (i) Control group: Hearts were perfused with a composition containing 11 mM glucose, 0.8 mM octanoate, 100 μU insulin, 50 μM L-carnitine, and 80 μM [13C3]propionate. (ii) Ile group: In addition to the components of the control group, hearts in this group received an extra 71 μM Ile. (iii) Thr group: Hearts in this group received the control group components along with an additional 166 μM Thr. (iv) Met group: Hearts in this group received the control group components supplemented with 23 μM Met. (v) Val group: Hearts in this group received the control group components supplemented with 230 μM Val. (vi) BSA + AAs group: Hearts in this group were perfused with the control group components plus 3% BSA and a mixture of amino acids at physiological concentrations, including 370 μM alanine, 82 μM arginine, 88 μM asparagine, 3 μM aspartic acid, 48 μM cysteine, 58 μM glutamate, 250 μM glycine, 520 μM glutamine, 73 μM histidine, 71 μM Ile, 118 μM leucine, 185 μM lysine, 23 μM Met, 55 μM phenylalanine, 235 μM proline, 98 μM serine, 166 μM Thr, 55 μM tryptophan, 58 μM tyrosine, and 230 μM Val. (vii) BSA group: Hearts in this group were perfused with the control group components supplemented with 3% BSA. Each group consisted of five rats. Octanoate was chosen as the fatty acid in all perfusion groups, as long-chain fatty acids are insoluble in the control group without BSA.

To trace the direct metabolic contribution to propionyl-CoA from odd-chain fatty acids and propiogenic amino acids (Val, Ile, Thr, and Met) in the heart, we replicated the conditions of group vi. In these perfusions, we replaced the unlabeled amino acids with the same concentration of uniformly labeled 13C-propiogenic amino acids (Val, Ile, Thr, and Met) or added 80 μM D33 heptadecanoic acid (D33 C17 fatty acid).

The detailed method of measuring propionyl-CoA turnover in the perfused heart can be referred to as our acetyl-CoA turnover assay in the perfused heart [38]. The uptake of [13C3]propionate (μmol/(min.g)) was assessed by measuring [13C3]propionate concentration difference between inflow perfusate and outflow perfusate. M3 Propionyl-CoA labeling in the perfused hearts was measured by our published LC-MS/MS method [39]. Propionyl-CoA turnover can be calculated with the following equation.

Propionyl-CoA turnover (μmol.min−1.g−1) = [13C3]propionate uptake / M3 propionyl-CoA labeling.

Following the differentiation of a healthy control individual h-iPSC into h-iPSC-CMs, as described in the published work [2], one million cells were cultured in RPMI 1640/B27 containing 0.1 mM or 1 mM propionate, 382 μM Ile, 101 μM Met, 168 μM Thr, and 171 μM Val for 4 days. The concentrations of propionate, Ile, Met, Thr, and Val in the media were assessed both before and after the culturing period.

Treatment regimens in PA aim to reduce propionyl-CoA either by accelerating propionyl-CoA metabolic disposal (e.g. enzyme replacement therapy or L-carnitine supplement) or by inhibiting its production (protein restriction diet or antibiotics). In order to understand the pathogenic mechanism of cardiac complications in PA, identifying heart-specific propionyl-CoA sources and catabolism is crucial for informing precise treatment approaches. Similar to our previous work on acetyl-CoA turnover in the perfused heart [38], we measured propionyl-CoA turnover in the perfused rat heart using [13C3]propionate as a tracer (Fig. 1A).

The measured propionyl-CoA turnover ranged from 40 to 100 nmol/(min*g dry w), which is 47 – 173 fold lower than acetyl-CoA turnover (4,700 to 6,900 nmol/(min*g dry w)) in the perfused rat hearts [38]. The contribution of potential metabolic substrates to cardiac propionyl-CoA was investigated by perfusing rat hearts with various amino acid substrates together with the tracer ([13C3]propionate) (Fig. 1B). Interestingly, propionyl-CoA labeling derived from [13C3]propionate was not diluted when hearts were perfused with [13C3]propionate and with or without BSA + mixed amino acids (20 amino acids at physiological concentrations), Ile, Thr, Met, Val, or BSA (Fig. 1C). Perfusing hearts with Ile alone diluted propionyl-CoA labeling by 16.3% but not when Ile was mixed with other amino acids (Fig. 1C). Consequently, the major source of propionyl-CoA in the heart is propionate (~74%, Fig. 1D), with a small contribution (~16%) from Ile under Ile alone conditions. In presence of mixed amino acids or with BSA supplementation, the contribution of propionyl-CoA from known amino acids (Thr, Ile, Met, and Val) was not detectable (Fig. 1D).

PA patients are advised to adhere to a protein-restricted diet to reduce the intake of Thr, Ile, Met, and Val that are known metabolic precursors of propionyl-CoA. Intriguingly, none of these amino acids or proteins (BSA) has any pronounced contribution to cardiac propionyl-CoA synthesis (Figs 1 C and 1D). To investigate this, we assessed amino acid uptake and their concentrations across various perfusion groups (Figs 1B, 1E, and Supplemental Fig. 1).

Perfusing rat hearts with individual amino acids (Thr, Ile, Met, and Val) resulted in significantly higher levels (≥3.3 fold) compared to control, BSA, and mixed amino acids perfusion groups (Fig. 1E and Supplemental Fig. 1). Met and Thr were not significantly higher in perfused hearts with BSA+AAs than control hearts without AAs (Supplemental Figs 1A and 1B), although two branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), Val and Ile, were significantly higher in hearts with BSA+AAs than control hearts without AAs (Supplemental Figs 1C and 1D). This data suggests the competition in amino acids’ uptake when hearts were perfused with mixed amino acids, particularly for Thr and Met. However, the less competition in the uptake of Val and Ile in mixed amino acids perfusion did not lead to significant synthesis of propionyl-CoA (Figs. 1C and 1D). We therefore profiled other metabolites of these amino acids (Supplemental Fig. 2A) in the perfused hearts. The metabolites of Ile (tigylyl-CoA, tigylycarnitine, 2-methylbutanoyl-CoA, and 2-methyl-3-OH-butylryl-CoA) and Val (isobutyryl-CoA and 3HIB) were accumulated in the hearts perfused with Ile and Val (Supplemental Figs 2B–G) without notable changes in their downstream metabolites (propionyl-CoA and propionylcarnitine) (Supplemental Figs 2H–2I). This also confirmed the high uptake of Ile/Val when hearts were perfused with Ile or Val alone compared to other groups. The difference in concentration changes between upstream metabolites of Ile/Val and propionyl-CoA in the heart perfused with Ile/Val suggests the presence of rate-limiting steps in catabolism of Ile/Val to propionyl-CoA in the heart (Supplemental Fig. 2A). The interplay between amino acid uptake competition and rate-limiting steps in catabolism aligns with the minimal contribution of four amino acids (Val, Ile, Thr, and Met) to propionyl-CoA production in the heart (Figs. 1C and 1D).

Experiments using13C-labeled propionate suggest that sources other than propionate contribute minimally to propionyl-CoA. To further confirm this, we perfused rat hearts directly with stable isotope-labeled C17 fatty acids and propiogenic amino acids (Val, Ile, Thr, and Met) (Figs. 2A–2E). The 13C incorporation into metabolic intermediates, including propionylcarnitine, from labeled substrates is shown in Figs. 2A–2E, and the labeling of metabolites was measured (Figs. 2F–2J). The labeling of C17 fatty acid and branched-chain amino acids (Val and Ile) was higher than 79%, while the labeling of Thr and Met in the heart was 47% and 65%, respectively. This is consistent with the relatively low uptake of Thr and Met by the heart. However, the labeling of all downstream metabolites decreased significantly. Specifically, the labeling of propionylcarnitine from stable isotope-labeled C17 fatty acids and propiogenic amino acids was lower than 3.6% (Figs. 2F–2J). Propionylcarnitine labeling was used as an index of propionyl-CoA labeling because propionylcarnitine labeling equilibrates well with propionyl-CoA (Supplemental Fig. 3A). In summary, a comparison of propionylcarnitine labeling from various substrates confirms that propionate is the major substrate for propionyl-CoA in the heart (Supplemental Fig. 3B).

To validate the observations made in perfused hearts, we exposed cultured h-iPSC-CMs to a 4-day regimen of 0.1 mM propionate, 1mM propionate, 0.382 mM Ile, 0.101 mM Met, 0.168 mM Thr, or 0.171 mM Val (Fig. 3A). Following the exposure, we measured propionyl-CoA substrate levels in both pre-cultured and post-cultured media. The post-cultured media substrate levels were then normalized to the pre-cultured levels, as illustrated in Fig. 3B. The corresponding calculated substrate consumptions are depicted in Fig. 3C. After the 4 days, h-iPSC-CMs metabolized 95% of 0.1 mM propionate and 71.6% of 1 mM propionate, whereas propiogenic amino acids exhibited less than 30% consumption (Fig. 3C). These findings are consistent with the data obtained from the heart perfusion experiment above. Notably, the negative consumption of Thr may be attributed to a higher release from protein breakdown compared to uptake.

Next, we investigated the source of free propionate in plasma. We collected the plasma samples from 3 distinct locations within the circulatory system, portal vein (PV), after the hepatic vein (HV), and the renal vein (RV) (Fig. 4A) in both control and germ-free (GF) mice. In control mice, propionate level was significantly higher in PV compared to the HV (~ 50-fold) and RV (~ 26-fold) (Fig. 4B). However, propionate levels in HV, RV, and PV were consistently low and identical in GF mice (Fig. 4B). Similar trends were observed for other short-chain fatty acids, such as acetate, butyrate, and pentanoate (Supplemental Figs 4A–D). Medium-chain fatty acids, like hexanoate (Supplemental Fig. 4D), did not originate from the gut microbiome and showed no significant variation among HV, RV, and PV.

These findings indicate that propionate and other short-chain fatty acids are predominantly generated by the gut microbiome. Short-chain fatty acids are absorbed by the intestine and transported to the liver through PV. The liver efficiently metabolizes most short-chain fatty acids, especially propionate, butyrate, and pentanoate (Supplemental Fig. 4E). In GF mice, there was no significant production of short-chain fatty acids from the gut microbiome, nor positive uptake by the liver (Supplemental Fig. 4E).

Analysis of short-chain acylcarnitines in plasma from the PV of control and GF mice revealed that only propionylcarnitine was significantly lower in GF mice compared to control mice (Figs 4C and 4D). Microbiome contribution to circulating propionylcarnitine was estimated at 52.9%, based on the difference in propionylcarnitine levels found in PV from GF and control mice.

The liver plays a crucial role in metabolically processing propionate absorbed from the intestine [47]. Normally, only a minimal amount of propionate bypasses the liver to reach the heart and other organs under physiological conditions. However, in Pcca−/−(A138T) mice, both propionate and propionylcarnitine in plasma exhibited a remarkable increase of 23- and 29-fold, respectively (Figs 5A and 5B). Similarly, PA patients showed elevated levels of propionate (approximately 30-fold) and propionylcarnitine (approximately 106-fold) in their plasma (Figs 5C and 5D). Propionate and propionylcarnitine emerged as the most significantly elevated short-chain fatty acids and short-chain acylnitines in both Pcca−/−(A138T) mice and PA patients (Supplemental Fig. 5).

To investigate whether the rise in circulating propionate resulted from impaired propionate disposal, we conducted a 13C labeled propionate tracing experiment. Both wild-type (WT) and Pcca−/−(A138T) mice received a high-dose bolus injection of [13C3]propionate, and plasma propionate levels were measured at 10 and 20 minutes (Figs. 5E and 5F). In Pcca−/−(A138T) mice, both unlabeled and M3 propionate plasma concentrations were significantly higher compared to controls, indicating a reduced disposal rate of propionate in these mice. Propionate serves as a gluconeogenic substrate, and 13C tracing from [13C3]propionate to glucose was depicted in Fig. 5G. As anticipated, glucose labeling from [13C3]propionate was impeded in Pcca−/−(A138T) mice (Fig. 5H).

Moreover, we assessed the activity of liver acyl-CoA synthetase short-chain family member 3 (ACSS3), as ACSS3 is responsible for activating propionate to propionyl-CoA, the initial step in propionate metabolism [68]. Hepatic ACSS3 activity showed a notable decrease of 37% in the Pcca−/−(A138T) liver (Fig. 5I).

Our metabolic flux data unequivocally demonstrate that cardiac propionyl-CoA primarily originates from circulating propionate. Additionally, the Pcca−/−(A138T) liver catabolizes approximately 90% of propionate from the gut microbiome, maintaining propionate at a modest level (~20 μM). This level is not anticipated to induce metabolic disturbances in the heart, especially considering Pcca−/−(A138T) mice maintain relatively high cardiac PCC activity [27] under a regular diet.

To investigate the potential association of elevated circulating propionate with cardiomyopathy in Pcca−/−(A138T) mice, we administered 0.25 M propionate in drinking water of both WT and Pcca−/−(A138T) mice, monitoring heart functions before and after supplementing propionate for 2 or 16 weeks. Following the propionate challenge, plasma propionate levels were 12.1 ± 5.1 μM and 132 ± 69.9 μM in WT and Pcca−/−(A138T) mice, respectively. Fig. 6 and Supplemental Figure 6 are echocardiography assessments before and after the propionate challenge. No discernible differences in cardiac function were observed between WT and Pcca−/−(A138T) mice before the propionate challenge, consistent with metabolic data under a chow diet. However, after the propionate challenge, late diastolic mitral inflow velocity (A) in Pcca−/−(A138T) mice progressively increased, while early diastolic mitral inflow velocity (E) remained unchanged (Figs 6A and 6B). This increase in A led to a reduction in the E/A ratio in Pcca−/−(A138T) mice after propionate feeding (Fig. 6C). Propionate feeding also prolonged the early diastolic mitral inflow velocity deceleration time (DT) and consequently decreased the early diastolic mitral inflow velocity slope of deceleration (Decel Slope) (Figs 6D and 6E). All these changes indicate a mild diastolic dysfunction without alterations in systolic functions, fraction shortening (Supplemental Fig. 6), or calculated ejection fraction (data not shown) in Pcca−/−(A138T) mice. Given the small changes in diastolic parameters, the role of propionate in mediating cardiac diastolic dysfunction in Pcca−/−(A138T) mice requires further investigation using more sensitive techniques or additional stress in combination with propionate challenge.

The lack of drastic cardiac phenotype associated with Pcca−/−(A138T) mice was attributed to relatively high cardiac PCC activity in our prior study [27]. To explore the impact of reduced cardiac PCC activity and propionate challenge on cardiac function, we utilized siRNA to knock down PCCA in HL-1 cells. Confirmation of PCCA knockdown in HL-1 cells was achieved through the assessment of PCCA expression by Western blotting (Fig. 7 A). Notably, propionate exposure induced significantly higher oxidative stress in PCCA KD HL-1 cells compared to the parent HL-1 cells (Figs. 7B and 7C).

4-Hydroxynonenal (HNE), a reactive byproduct of lipid peroxidation, can modify proteins by forming Michael adducts and Schiff bases through reactions with cysteine, histidine, lysine, and to some extent arginine and other amino acid residues in proteins. Consequently, HNE-modified proteins serve as markers of oxidative stress [8]. Our comprehensive assay of HNE-modified proteins clearly indicates that propionate exposure induces oxidative stress when PCCA is knocked down, particularly evident at higher propionate concentrations (Figs. 8A and 8B).

To explore whether the observed oxidative stress could occur in vivo by propionate challenge in Pcca−/−(A138T) mice, akin to the in vitro findings, we assessed oxidative stress biomarkers in the hearts of WT and Pcca−/−(A138T) mice following propionate administration. The ratio of reduced and oxidized glutathione (GSH/GSSG) significantly decreased in the hearts of Pcca−/−(A138T) mice (Figs. 8C). Additionally, methionine sulfoxide levels markedly increased in the hearts of Pcca−/−(A138T) mice (Fig. 8D). The changes in GSH/GSSG and methionine sulfoxide levels reflected the increased oxidative stress in the heart of Pcca−/−(A138T) mice after administration of propionate [35, 43].

Mutations of the PCCA or PCCB gene in PA patients impair PCC activity and result in the accumulation of propionyl-CoA and its related metabolites. Cardiac disease is one of the life-threatening complications associated with PA. Importantly, the pathogenicity of propionyl-CoA in the cardiomyopathy of PA remains poorly understood. Therefore, understanding the cardiac-specific propionyl-CoA metabolism including its synthesis and catabolism in the heart will enhance the understanding of the mechanism of cardiomyopathy in PA patients.

In comparison to the rapid acetyl-CoA turnover in the perfused heart, propionyl-CoA turnover is approximately 100-fold slower. The fast turnover rate of acetyl-CoA is expected to meet the heart’s energy needs [56]. The slow turnover of propionyl-CoA could be due to the low reliance on anaplerotic flux from propionyl-CoA in the heart, in contrast to the liver where the anaplerosis from propionyl-CoA is important for gluconeogenesis as shown in Fig. 5H. Such tissue-specific metabolic variations imply different pathophysiological mechanisms of PA complications in these tissues.

A protein restriction diet for PA patients is to reduce the propiogenic amino acids (Ile, Thr, Met, and Val). Surprisingly, the heart perfusion experiments with stable isotope labeled propionate and propiogenic amino acids and the h-iPSC-CM cell culture experiment (Figs. 1–3) demonstrated that propiogenic amino acids and proteins are not the major sources of propionyl-CoA in the heart. The negligible metabolism of these four amino acids to propionyl-CoA in the heart was explained by the following observations: (1) the limited uptake of amino acids by the heart and h-iPSC-CMs (Figs 1 and 3 and Supplemental Fig. 1) and (2) the presence of rate-limiting catabolic steps of BCAAs to propionyl-CoA in perfused heart (Supplemental Fig. 2). The low metabolism of amino acids or BCAAs in heart is consistent with recent reports [9, 31, 63].

Odd-chain fatty acids are considered another significant source of propionyl-CoA. In our experiment, we perfused hearts with equivalent quantities of stable isotope-labeled propionate and C17 fatty acid to assess their respective contributions to propionyl-CoA production. The dosage of C17 fatty acid was determined based on values reported in patients with PA [65]. Our data unequivocally demonstrate that the conversion of C17 fatty acids to propionyl-CoA is negligible compared to propionate metabolism at matched levels (Fig. 2A and Supplemental Fig. 3B). This finding aligns with prior research indicating that in the heart, metabolism of long-chain fatty acids is competitively inhibited by short-chain fatty acids [6].

The fast activation of propionate to propionyl-CoA with relatively low anaplerosis from propionyl-CoA to TCA cycle results in the accumulation of propionyl-CoA with consequent CoA trapping even in normal rat hearts [64]. This effect can be further amplified in cases where PCC activity is diminished, as seen in patients with PA [54, 55]. Circulating propionate primarily originates from the gut microbiome (Fig. 4B), supported by two key findings in our study: (1) the high levels of propionate (~200 μM) in the PV of control mice, and (2) the low levels of propionate in the portal vein of GF mice, indicating that the gut microbiome releases short-chain fatty acids, including propionate, to the host.

The liver acts as a natural barrier for propionate, efficiently removing it through first-pass metabolism (Fig. 4B and Supplemental Fig. 4E). Consequently, propionate from PV scarcely bypasses the liver and reaches other organs, such as the heart, in healthy humans or animals. However, in PA patients or Pcca−/−(A138T) mice, the accumulation of hepatic propionyl-CoA due to low PCC activity diminishes propionate disposal and increases its release into the circulating system. Moreover, the attenuated hepatic ACSS3 in Pcca−/−(A138T) mice further hampers propionate metabolism in the liver. Notably, PA patients exhibited significantly elevated propionate levels (506 μM in one patient) in the plasma (Fig. 5D), confirming the reduced metabolic disposal of propionate in the livers of PA patients. The wide variation in propionate levels in plasma among six PA patients (ranging from 38 μM to 506 μM) might explain why not all PA patients are equally at risk of developing cardiomyopathy. Those with chronically high circulating propionate levels could face an increased risk of cardiac diseases. This observation is consistent with the reported reversal of cardiomyopathy through liver transplantation in a PA case, underscoring the restoration of hepatic propionate metabolism as a critical factor [50].

While the Pcca−/− mice succumbed within 36 hours of birth, the transgenic Pcca−/−(A138T) mouse exhibited better survival, rendering it a valuable model for PA [20, 26, 27]. Propionate levels were elevated to ~ 20 μM in the plasma of Pcca−/−(A138T) mice (Fig. 5A). This moderate increase of plasma propionate is not expected to cause severe cardiac metabolic crisis, especially considering that the residual PCC activity in the heart of Pcca−/−(A138T) mice is not significantly reduced [27]. This is supported by the data that unchanged or even slightly increased free carnitine and acetylcarnitine in the heart of Pcca−/−(A138T) mice [27].

Further echocardiographic testing revealed no noticeable difference in cardiac function between WT mice and Pcca−/−(A138T) mice under a standard chow diet (Fig. 6 and Supplemental Fig. 6). Despite administering a substantial dose of propionate through drinking water, Pcca−/−(A138T) mice did not exhibit any adverse effects on systolic function, including fraction shortening (Supplemental Fig. 6), consistent with our previous findings [14]. However, if there were any effects of propionate challenge, Pcca−/−(A138T) mice might have displayed early signs of mild diastolic dysfunction (Fig. 6). The mild phenotype could be attributed to the elevated residual PCC activity in the cardiac muscle of Pcca−/−(A138T) mice [27]. The heightened expression of mutant human PCCA in the hearts of Pcca−/−(A138T) mice complicates the investigation of the pathological mechanisms of PA associated with cardiac diseases in this mouse model. To address this experimental challenge, we knocked down PCCA in HL-1 cells and exposed PCCA knockdown HL-1 cells to propionate. The propionate challenge induced oxidative stress, resulting in a deterioration in the survival of PCCA knockdown HL-1 cells [57]. Elevated oxidative stress was also observed in the hearts of Pcca−/−(A138T) mice challenged with propionate. This data unequivocally suggests that elevated propionate is the major culprit of cardiac complications in patients with PA.

In summary, this is the first work investigating propionyl-CoA turnover in the perfused heart which revealed that circulating propionate, rather than amino acids or odd-chain fatty acids, predominates as the primary source of propionyl-CoA in cardiac metabolism. The diminished removal of propionate from the gut microbiome by the liver observed in PA underscores elevated circulating propionate as a significant risk factor for cardiac complications, especially in the presence of impaired PCC activity in the heart. Reduction of propionate, rather than propiogenic amino acids and odd-chain fatty acids, may be pursued as a therapy for PA-associated cardiomyopathy.

Firstly, all the data were derived from rat, mouse, human-induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (h-iPSC-CMs), and HL-1 cell models, despite confirming elevated propionate levels in PA patients. The findings necessitate thorough evaluation in humans. Secondly, this study demonstrated that propiogenic amino acids do not directly contribute to propionyl-CoA in the perfused heart. However, these amino acids could undergo metabolism in other organs, releasing intermediates to the heart for further conversion to propionyl-CoA. However, it is noteworthy that, based on our previous work, branched-chain keto acids are still preferentially reaminated and activate protein synthesis in the heart rather than undergoing complete catabolism to propionyl-CoA [63]. Thirdly, the significance of the in vitro findings is limited due to the non-physiological conditions of the study.
