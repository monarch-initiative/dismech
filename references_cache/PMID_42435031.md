# Catecholaminergic Polymorphic Ventricular Tachycardia Type 2 Presenting as Seizure in a Child: Diagnostic Pitfalls.
**Authors:** Rahimpour F, Moghaddam ES, Fallahi NA
**Journal:** JACC Case Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jaccas.2026.109233](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaccas.2026.109233)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia is a rare inherited arrhythmogenic disorder. In children, it is often misdiagnosed as epilepsy due to convulsive syncope triggered by exercise.

CASE SUMMARY: A 6-year-old boy presented with exercise-induced seizure-like episodes despite normal neuroimaging. Resting electrocardiogram and Holter were unremarkable, but exercise stress testing revealed bidirectional ventricular tachycardia. Genetic testing confirmed a homozygous CASQ2 mutation, consistent with catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia type 2. The patient was successfully managed with propranolol and flecainide, avoiding implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement.

DISCUSSION: Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia type 2 often presents earlier and more severely than type 1. This case highlights the importance of exercise testing in pediatric syncope with normal baseline findings and emphasizes genotype-phenotype correlation when managing incidental findings.

TAKE-HOME MESSAGES: Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia type 2 should be considered in any child with exertional seizures despite normal baseline evaluations. Combination therapy provides an effective alternative to early implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation.

Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT) is a lethal inherited calcium channelopathy characterized by stress-induced ventricular arrhythmias in the absence of structural heart disease.1 One of the greatest clinical challenges in CPVT is its misleading neurological presentation; due to tonic-clonic movements and loss of consciousness during activity, it is frequently misdiagnosed as epilepsy.2 The underlying mechanism is "transient global cerebral ischemia" following reduced cardiac output from ventricular arrhythmia,3 leading to convulsive syncope. Studies show that up to 30% of patients with CPVT are mismanaged with antiseizure medications for years before a definitive cardiac diagnosis.2 Type 2 CPVT, caused by CASQ2 mutations, is autosomal recessive and typically more aggressive than type 1.4 Because resting electrocardiograms (ECG) are often normal, diagnosis relies on exercise stress testing or genetic analysis.1 Given the risks of implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) in children, such as electrical storms, optimizing combination pharmacotherapy is vital.5

A 6-year-old boy (21 kg) presented with recurrent episodes of body stiffness and upward gaze triggered by intense physical exertion. Because of the convulsive nature of these episodes, he was initially evaluated for refractory epilepsy.

The patient had no prior history of heart disease or surgical interventions. Family history was negative for sudden cardiac death or inherited arrhythmias. The patient had no siblings at the time of evaluation. Both parents were asymptomatic and had no known history of syncope, ventricular arrhythmias, or sudden cardiac death.

The patient's presentation of exercise-induced seizures with normal neuroimaging necessitated a broad diagnostic approach. Initial differentials included idiopathic epilepsy, long QT syndrome (specifically type 1), and CPVT. Although the tonic-clonic movements initially pointed toward a neurological origin, the normal results of an electroencephalogram and brain magnetic resonance imaging shifted the focus toward a cardiac etiology.

Cardiac evaluation began with a resting ECG, which showed a normal sinus rhythm and a QTc of 440 ms (Figure 1A), which remained within normal limits. Transthoracic echocardiography confirmed a structurally normal heart with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%. To unmask a potential exertional arrhythmia, an exercise stress test was performed using the Modified Bruce Protocol. As the heart rate reached the range of 100 to 130 beats/min, the patient developed isolated premature ventricular contractions (Figure 1B), followed by couplets, and finally sustained bidirectional ventricular tachycardia (Figure 1C). The test was immediately terminated because of the high risk of cardiac arrest.

Subsequent genetic testing via whole exome sequencing identified a homozygous likely pathogenic CASQ2 variant (NM_001232.4:c.840C>T; p.Gln280∗), confirming the diagnosis of autosomal recessive CPVT type 2. An incidental homozygous likely pathogenic ACAD8 variant was also identified but was considered unrelated to the patient's clinical phenotype.

Given the autosomal recessive inheritance pattern of CASQ2-associated CPVT, family screening and genetic counseling were recommended. Both parents were presumed obligate heterozygous carriers of the CASQ2 pathogenic variant. At the 6-month follow-up, the patient remains asymptomatic and has experienced no further events. A repeat exercise test confirmed significant reduction in arrhythmic burden with no inducible ventricular tachycardia.

The diagnosis of CPVT in young children remains particularly challenging because baseline cardiac investigations, including resting ECG and ambulatory Holter monitoring, are frequently normal. Consequently, exertion-related syncope with convulsive features is often misattributed to primary neurological disorders, most notably epilepsy.2 In this case, the absence of epileptiform discharges on electroencephalogram and a normal brain magnetic resonance imaging did not establish the diagnosis but appropriately redirected clinical suspicion toward a cardiac etiology,3 highlighting the importance of considering arrhythmogenic causes in children presenting with exercise-induced seizures.

Importantly, children with exertional or convulsive syncope, frequently misdiagnosed as seizures, represent a high-risk group for CPVT, even with normal baseline investigations. Early recognition is critical, as delayed diagnosis increases the risk of life-threatening arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death.

A key diagnostic insight from this case is the central role of exercise stress testing as an early, phenotype-driven diagnostic tool, rather than a merely confirmatory investigation. The induction of bidirectional ventricular tachycardia during exertion represents a pathognomonic finding for CPVT and, in this patient, strongly supported the diagnosis even before genetic results became available.1,4 This underscores that in suspected CPVT, exercise testing should be prioritized early in the diagnostic pathway, as reliance on resting ECGs or Holter monitoring alone may result in false reassurance and delayed diagnosis. Furthermore, it is important to recognize that standard graded exercise protocols, such as the Modified Bruce Protocol, may not unmask arrhythmias in all patients with suspected CPVT. Emerging evidence suggests that burst or sprint exercise testing, which involves abrupt high-intensity exertion, may provide greater sensitivity for provoking catecholamine-mediated arrhythmias and should be considered when clinical suspicion remains high despite a negative standard exercise test.5

The identification of a homozygous pathogenic CASQ2 variant alongside an incidental ACAD8 variant further illustrates the limitations of a genotype-first diagnostic strategy. With the expanding use of whole exome sequencing, incidental findings are increasingly common and may complicate clinical decision-making if interpreted outside the appropriate phenotypic context. In this case, the clear electrophysiological phenotype allowed confident attribution of causality to the CASQ2 mutation, emphasizing the critical importance of genotype-phenotype correlation in contemporary genomic medicine.

Because CASQ2-associated CPVT is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner, both parents of an affected child are expected to be heterozygous carriers. Each future offspring therefore carries a 25% risk of being affected and a 50% risk of being a carrier.6 Accordingly, family screening and genetic counseling are important components of management.

From a therapeutic standpoint, this case provides clinically meaningful evidence supporting an optimized pharmacological approach in pediatric CPVT type 2. Although ICDs are effective in preventing sudden cardiac death, their use in young children is associated with a high risk of inappropriate shocks and adrenergic-driven electrical storms.7,8 In this patient, the initiation of combined propranolol and flecainide therapy resulted in a marked reduction of arrhythmic burden on follow-up exercise testing, with complete suppression of inducible ventricular tachycardia.9 This objective improvement reinforces the therapeutic potency of flecainide, not only through its molecular stabilization of abnormal calcium release at the ryanodine receptor but also through demonstrable clinical efficacy, supporting dual medical therapy as a viable and less invasive alternative to early ICD insertion in selected patients.

In children with exercise-induced seizures, cardiac evaluation including exercise testing is mandatory. CPVT2 management with dual therapy (propranolol and flecainide) can effectively control life-threatening arrhythmias without early ICD intervention.

The early implementation of dual medical therapy, combining beta-blockers with flecainide, serves as a critical strategy to stabilize cardiac calcium handling. By optimizing this pharmacological approach, it is possible to effectively manage severe CPVT2 in young children while avoiding the significant risks of electrical storms and inappropriate shocks associated with early ICD placement.
