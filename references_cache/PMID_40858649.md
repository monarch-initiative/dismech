# Higher proportion of coagulative necrosis and PD-L1(+) immune cells in splenic tuberculosis.
**Authors:** Dai J, Liu L, Liu J, Liu Y, Wu R, Zhou J, Chen W
**Journal:** Sci Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41598-025-15754-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-15754-7)

## Content

Due to its low incidence and non-specific clinical manifestations, early diagnosis of splenic tuberculosis (STB) is extremely challenging. Pathology is the gold standard for disease diagnosis. The spleen's unique structural and functional characteristics may confer distinct pathological features and immune microenvironment in STB. However, no relevant study has been reported to date. Here, we collected seven cases of STB and compared their clinical and pathological characteristics with those of pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB). CT scans revealed that STB primarily manifests as significant enlargement of the spleen, with multiple round-shaped low-density shadows visible within. Compared to the PTB group, the positive rates for molecular detection and acid-fast staining were significantly lower in the STB group, while the proportion of coagulative necrosis was substantially higher. Granulomas, caseous necrosis, abscesses, fibrous proliferation, collagen degeneration, and granulation tissue formation did not show significant differences between the two groups. We further compared infiltrating immune cells and found that the numbers of T cells, CD8+ T cells, and macrophages were significantly higher in the STB group than those in the PTB group, but there was no statistical difference compared to normal spleen tissue, suggesting that tuberculosis infection may not have a significant impact on the immune response in STB cases. The number of PD-L1+ immune cells in the STB group was significantly higher than that in the PTB group and normal spleen tissue, while the number of PD-1+ immune cells did not differ significantly among the three groups. In summary, STB has unique pathological features and immune microenvironment, with a higher incidence of coagulative necrosis and an extreme immune suppression state.

Tuberculosis (TB) poses a significant threat to global health, with an estimated 10.8 million people infected worldwide in 2023, a slight increase from 10.6 million in 2022 1– 3 . Globally, approximately 15–20% of TB infections are extrapulmonary TB 4 , and the clinical and pathological characteristics of different types of extrapulmonary TB vary greatly. The spleen, as the largest lymphatic organ in the human body, has the functions of blood storage, haematopoiesis, removal of senescent red blood cells, and immune responses 5 , and exhibits strong resistance to Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) infection. Splenic tuberculosis (STB) is extremely rare in clinical practice and is prone to be misdiagnosed or diagnosed late 6 , 7 . Importantly, acute MTB infection may lead to extensive necrosis of the spleen tissue or the formation of abscesses, significantly increasing the risk of splenic rupture (SR) 8 . Long-term infection of MTB can induce chronic inflammation and fibrosis of splenic tissue, impairing splenic function and systemic immunity 9 . The low diagnostic rate and incidence of STB make it difficult for patients to receive timely treatment, often resulting in poor outcomes 6 . Therefore, early accurate diagnosis as well as personalised treatment are of vital importance for patients.

At present, the diagnosis of STB relies on clinical manifestations, imaging studies, and histopathological examination. Patients typically present with persistent fatigue, weight loss, and other nonspecific symptoms. Imaging findings are also non-specific, and clinicians with limited experience may find it difficult to distinguish STB from splenic lymphoma or metastatic tumors in the spleen 10 . Pathology remains the gold standard for disease diagnosis. The typical pathological change of TB is chronic granulomatous inflammation, with caseous necrosis 11 . The pathological changes of extra-pulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) differ from those of PTB. Our previous studies demonstrated a higher incidence of caseous necrosis, abscesses, and granulation tissue formation in spinal tuberculosis 12 . Due to the rarity of STB, the pathological characteristics of STB and its distinctions from PTB have not been systematically described to date.

The STB is predominantly caused by the hematogenous dissemination of MTB from other parts, and in some cases, it may be primary 6 . Lesions disseminated through the bloodstream typically present as tiny nodules but can also manifest as large nodules, although the latter are relatively rare 13 . Structurally, the spleen is soft, rich in blood vessels, and stores a large amount of blood. Functionally, the spleen serves as an important immune organ; it harbors vast numbers of lymphocytes and macrophages. These structural and functional differences may result in organ-specific pathological features in STB. Lanjewar et al. observed that STB lesions more frequently exhibit caseous necrosis, with atypical or absent granulomatous morphology 9 , which markedly differs from the typical pathological features of TB. In this study, we collected seven cases of STB, analysed their clinicopathological characteristics and immune microenvironment, and compared them with PTB, providing important data for early diagnosis and precise treatment.

Patients who visited the 8th Medical Centre of Chinese PLA General Hospital between January 2011 and January 2025 and underwent needle biopsy or surgical resection were included in this study. The inclusion criteria: age > 18 years, clinically and pathologically diagnosed with TB. Exclusion criteria: concomitant malignant tumours, severe haematological disorders, HIV/AIDS, or viral hepatitis, and a history of splenic surgery. In this study, 7 patients were definitively diagnosed with STB, and 10 cases of PTB during the same period were selected as the PTB group. To investigate the effects of MTB infection on the spleen, we selected 10 cases of emergency splenectomy due to SR. Within this group, the affected tissue was designated as the SR/lesion group, and the adjacent unaffected tissue was designated as the SR/normal group.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 software. Comparisons between multiple groups were performed using a one-way analysis of variance, while comparisons between two groups were performed using a chi-square test or t-test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The CT scan of STB revealed significant splenomegaly, with multiple round hypodense lesions. Heterogeneous densities, including both hyperdense and hypodense areas, were observed within the spleen (Fig. 3A). In PTB, CT imaging demonstrated a pulmonary mass with ill-defined margins, internal cavitation, and short spiculated edges. Additionally, patchy and nodular opacities with indistinct borders were observed in the apical segment of the right upper lobe, the apicoposterior segment of the left upper lobe, and the anterior segment of the left upper lobe (Fig. 3B). The CT scan of SR/normal revealed splenic heterogeneity, with irregular internal density, a subcapsular hematoma, and marginal discontinuity.CT imaging of patients with STB revealed dense pulmonary opacities, whereas patients with PTB exhibited normal splenic findings. In the STB group, both molecular detection methods showed a positive rate of 28.6% versus 14.3% for acid-fast staining. The PTB group exhibited 100% and 80% positivity rates with molecular methods and acid-fast staining respectively. Statistical analysis demonstrated significant intergroup differences (P < 0.05). This significant difference may be attributed to splenectomies in STB are usually performed after prolonged anti-TB treatment when MTB load in the spleen has been either eradicated or diminished below detection thresholds.

Typical granulomas were observed in all cases of STB (100%). Caseous necrosis was present in 3 cases (42.9%), coagulative necrosis in 6 cases (85.7%), acute inflammation in 4 cases (57.1%), and inflammatory necrosis in 6 cases (85.7%). Fibrous proliferation in 6 cases (85.7%), collagen degeneration in 1 case (14.3%), and exudation in 4 cases (57.1%). No abscesses, granulation tissue formation, or bleeding were observed. In the PTB group, 8 cases (80%) showed typical granulomas, 8 cases (80%) showed caseous necrosis, 4 cases (40%) showed acute inflammation, 2 cases (20%) showed abscesses, 4 cases (40%) showed inflammatory necrosis, 4 cases (40%) showed fibrous proliferation, and 2 cases (20%) showed exudation. No cases of coagulative necrosis, collagen degeneration, or granulation tissue formation were observed. In the SR/lesion group, 10 cases (100%) showed acute inflammation, 8 cases (80%) showed inflammatory necrosis, 7 cases (70%) showed exudation, and 10 cases (100%) showed bleeding. No cases of granuloma, caseous necrosis, abscess, coagulative necrosis, fibrous proliferation, collagen degeneration, or granulation tissue formation. In the SR/normal group, only vascular dilation and congestion were observed. Statistical analysis revealed significant differences in the proportion of coagulative necrosis between the STB group and the PTB group (P < 0.05), as well as significant differences in the proportions of granulomas, coagulative necrosis, and fibrous proliferation between the STB group and the SR/lesion group (P < 0.05). The splenic blood supply derives primarily from the splenic artery, which exhibits a segmental distribution with relatively sparse collateral circulation, contrasting markedly with the pulmonary vasculature. During TB infection, granuloma formation, fibrosis, and macrophage proliferation all affect local blood circulation, thereby predisposing to coagulative necrosis (Fig. 4, Table 2).

The number of T cells, CD8+T cells, and macrophages in the STB group was significantly higher than those in the PTB group (P < 0.05). However, no statistically significant differences were observed in B cells, CD4+ T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, or plasma cell populations (P > 0.05). We further compared the STB group with the SR/ normal group and found that there were no statistically significant differences in the numbers of T cells, CD8+T cells, and macrophages between the two groups (P > 0.05). These results indicate that the unique tissue structure and function of the spleen are important factors causing the differences in immune cells between STB and PTB. TB infection may not activate the immune response of these patients and has a relatively small impact on the subtypes and quantities of immune cells in the spleen (Fig. 5, Table 3).

The PD-1 and PD-L1 are critical immune checkpoint pathways, wherein elevated expression significantly acceleration the apoptosis of activated immune cells and fosters immune tolerance. Our prior research demonstrated significant PD-L1 upregulation within tuberculous granulomas, which was associated with an enhanced degree of malignancy in lung adenocarcinoma 14 . Current immunohistochemical analysis revealed a marked increase in PD-L1+ immune cell infiltration in STB specimens compared to both PTB and SR/normal groups (P < 0.05). However, there was no statistically significant difference in the number of PD-1+ immune cells among the three groups (P > 0.05). These results indicate that TB infection in the STB group may not activate the immune response in the spleen, but the number of PD-L1+ immune cells was significantly increased. Severely impaired immunity, particularly the increase in PD-L1+ immune cells, may be an important cause of STB (Fig. 5, Table 3).

STB is a relatively rare form of EPTB, primarily occurring in immunocompromised patients 15 . The clinical manifestations of STB are non-specific, commonly including fever, fatigue, weight loss, and splenomegaly 16 . Among these, unexplained fever is the most common clinical manifestation of STB. In systemic tuberculosis, STB predominantly arises from haematogenous dissemination, with lymphatic spread being rare 6 . The clinical presentation of PTB is insidious and nonspecific, commonly comprising persistent cough, sputum production, chest pain, haemoptysis, fatigue, weight loss, low-grade pyrexia, and night sweats 17 . PTB is primarily transmitted through droplets. When PTB patients are immunocompromised, MTB disseminates systemically via the bloodstream and the lymphatic system 18 . The diagnosis of PTB is conventionally established by bacteriological confirmation and supported by radiographic assessment (chest X-ray or CT). In contrast, STB is a rare clinical entity whose nonspecific presentation and imaging findings may resemble those of splenic lymphoma or splenic abscesses, thus complicating diagnosis 19 . Therefore, understanding the differences between STB and PTB facilitates early diagnosis, particularly in high-tuberculosis-burden regions.

Like PTB, STB typically presents with chronic granulomatous inflammation and caseous necrosis. Notably, our study found that the prevalence of coagulative necrosis was significantly higher in STB than in PTB, likely due to differences in tissue structure and vascular supply between the spleen and lungs. The spleen receives its blood supply mainly from the splenic artery, which branches segmentally with limited collateral circulation. Moreover, the spleen’s dense tissue structure and lack of natural drainage pathways hinder the removal of necrotic material 20 . There was no statistical difference observed between the groups in other pathological features, including granulomas, caseous necrosis, acute inflammation, inflammatory necrosis, fibrous hyperplasia, or exudation. Molecular testing has become increasingly vital for species-level identification of Mycobacterium, differential diagnostic confirmation, and assessment of drug resistance. However, acid-fast staining and molecular testing had significantly lower positivity rates in STB than in PTB, complicating histopathological confirmation. Most STB specimens in our study were obtained from surgical resections. Given the spleen’s critical role in immune and circulatory homeostasis, splenectomy is considered only after careful evaluation. Prolonged anti-tuberculosis therapy may eliminate or reduce splenic Mycobacterium to undetectable levels. However, the pathological changes associated with STB remain unresolved and continue to pose a serious threat to patients’ health. Since the remaining normal spleen is also unable to perform its normal biological functions, splenectomy may be the optimal intervention. These findings suggest that, while tuberculosis lesions trigger similar pathological responses across different tissues, the unique anatomical structure and immune properties of the spleen contribute to its distinct pathological features.

Following MTB infection, antigen-presenting cells (APCs) process and present MTB antigens, activating CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes to mount an immune response that controls intracellular MTB. These activated T cells secrete cytokines that activate resting monocytes/macrophages, promoting granuloma formation and effectively confining MTB within the granuloma 18 . Immunohistochemical results showed that the STB group had significantly higher numbers of T cells, CD8+ T cells, and macrophages compared to the PTB group. However, no statistically significant differences were observed compared to normal spleens. We speculate that this result may primarily be attributed to the unique microenvironment of the affected organ. As a lymphoid organ rich in vascular sinuses 21 , the spleen harbours a high quantity of immune cells, far exceeding those in the lungs. As a barrier organ 22 , the lungs require a longer process for immune cells to respond, which may be an important reason for the low incidence of TB in the spleen. As a key immune checkpoint, PD-L1 inhibits lymphocyte activation, proliferation, and cytokine secretion, thereby weakening T cell-mediated killing of MTB and facilitating its immune escape 23 . Our study showed that PD-L1+ cell counts were significantly higher in the STB group than in the PTB and normal spleen groups. Compared to normal spleens, total immune cell counts in STB were unchanged, whereas PD-L1+ cell counts were significantly increased. This marked increase in PD-L1+ cells, indicative of impaired immunity, may be a key factor in STB development. The PD-L1-driven immunosuppressive state likely plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of STB.

STB is typically a localized manifestation of systemic tuberculosis. Therefore, the decision to perform splenectomy should be guided by the principles of systemic tuberculosis management. If surgery is deemed necessary, it is crucial to administer standard antituberculosis treatment both preoperatively and postoperatively 24 . There is evidence indicating that anti-tuberculosis treatment can alter immune cell infiltration in the peripheral blood of tuberculosis patients 25 . However, research on histological immune cell infiltration and MTB detection in affected tissues remains limited. Future studies should investigate changes in histological immune cell infiltration and MTB detection before and after anti-tuberculosis treatment, incorporating larger sample sizes to validate these findings. Due to the limited sample size, this study did not conduct a stratified analysis based on the history of anti-tuberculosis treatment. We acknowledge this limitation and recommend that future studies with larger cohorts comprehensively assess the impact of anti-tuberculosis therapy on immune cell infiltration in STB patients. Although we employed multiple immune cell markers and antibodies, other potential molecular and immune mechanisms remain unexplored. We employed rigorous statistical methods to ensure data robustness, thereby supporting the reliability of our findings. Finally, given the association between tuberculosis and PD-L1, future studies should explore PD-L1 blockade in combination with anti-tuberculosis therapy as a potential treatment strategy, which may provide new insights for clinical management. Additionally, as reported in recent literature 26 , the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) with digital pathology is emerging as a key driver for companion diagnostics in immunotherapy. In the future, AI-based tools may significantly enhance the quantification of immune cell distributions, such as PD-L1+immune cells.

Owing to the specificity of tissue structure and function, STB demonstrates unique pathological characteristics and immune microenvironment. The incidence of coagulative necrosis in STB is significantly higher than that in PTB. The spleen of patients with STB shows a markedly immunosuppressive state. TB infection does not substantially alter the subtypes and quantities of immune cells, while the quantities of PD-L1+ immune cells are significantly higher than that of PTB. Our research provides important references for both the clinical diagnosis and treatment as well as basic research of STB.
