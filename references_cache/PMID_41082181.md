# Decoding GNE Myopathy: From Molecular Basis to Therapeutic Advances.
**Authors:** Yoshioka W, Noguchi S, Nishino I
**Journal:** Ann Indian Acad Neurol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.4103/aian.aian_837_25](https://doi.org/10.4103/aian.aian_837_25)

## Content

GNE myopathy is a rare, adult-onset, autosomal recessive muscle disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in the GNE gene, which encodes a key enzyme in the biosynthesis of sialic acid. Deficient GNE enzyme activity results in decreased production of sialic acid and subsequent hyposialylation of muscle glycoproteins, ultimately leading to progressive muscle degeneration and characteristic histopathological changes. The disease typically presents with progressive distal muscle weakness while sparing the quadriceps until advanced stages. Advances in molecular biology have significantly clarified the underlying disease mechanisms and revealed genotype-phenotype correlations. Natural history studies, supported by patient registries, have detailed the disease course and identified extra-muscular manifestations such as thrombocytopenia and sleep apnea. Multiple therapeutic strategies are under active investigation, including sialic acid supplementation, antioxidant therapy, and gene therapy. Several clinical trials targeting sialic acid biosynthetic pathways, such as oral N-acetylneuraminic acid, ManNAc, and 6'- sialyllactose, have advanced to late-stage development, culminating in the approval of the N-acetyl-neuraminic acid extended-release tablet in Japan in 2024. Other emerging therapeutic approaches, including antioxidant- and gene-based interventions, are also currently being explored. This review synthesizes the current understanding of the molecular pathogenesis of GNE myopathy and highlights recent advances and future prospects in targeted molecular and therapeutic approaches. The ultimate aim is to foster international collaboration toward the development of innovative and effective treatments for GNE myopathy.

GNE myopathy is a rare, adult-onset autosomal recessive muscle disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in theGNEgene, which encodes a bifunctional enzyme involved in the first step of sialic acid biosynthesis [Figure 1]. Over recent decades, advances in molecular genetics have significantly enhanced our understanding of the clinical spectrum, diagnostic approaches, and emerging therapeutic strategies for this disease.[1]Studies using mice model have provided insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the disorder[2,3]and facilitated multiple clinical trials. In parallel, natural history studies and patient registries have expanded knowledge of the disease course,[4,5]including extramuscular manifestations.[6,7]This review, based on a systematic PubMed search up to July 2025 focusing on “GNE myopathy,” aims to provide an updated overview of recent advances in clinical presentation, genetics, pathogenesis, and current and emerging therapeutic approaches to support clinicians and researchers in this rapidly evolving field.

Biosynthesis pathway of sialic acid leading to low sialylation in GNE myopathy. The GNE gene encodes the bifunctional UDPGlcNAc 2epimerase/Nacetylmannosamine kinase enzyme (GNE/MNK), which drives the first two committed and ratelimiting steps of Neu5Ac biosynthesis in the cytoplasm: converting UDPGlcNAc to ManNAc and then phosphorylating it to ManNAc6P. Pathogenic GNE variants impair these activities, resulting in hyposialylation. Oral supplementation with Neu5Ac, ManNAc, or 6′sialyllactose restores sialylation and reduces muscle weakness, with these compounds classified into two groups based on their targeted sialic acid biosynthesis pathways: the salvage pathway (NeuAc, and sialyllactose) and the de novo pathway (ManNAc). NeuAc: N-acetylneuraminic acid, ManNAc: N-acetylmannosamine, UDP-GlcNAc: Uridine 5′-diphospho-N acetylglucosamine, CMP-NeuAc: Cytidine monophosphate-N-acetylneuraminic acid

GNE myopathy was first described in Japan in 1981 as distal myopathy with rimmed vacuoles (DMRV)[8]and was independently reported in Persian Jewish populations as hereditary inclusion body myopathy (HIBM).[9]Initially, these conditions were considered distinct disorders. In 2001, theGNEgene was identified as the causative gene for HIBM,[10]and subsequent studies revealed that variants in the same gene also cause DMRV.[11]This discovery unified these previously separate entities under a single molecular diagnosis, resolving longstanding confusion. Consequently, the term “GNE myopathy” has become widely accepted to describe this condition.[12]

GNE myopathy is an ultra-rare muscle disease, with a global prevalence estimated at 1–9 per million based on clinical diagnoses—likely an underestimate due to undiagnosed cases and limited genetic testing (Orphanet;https://www.orpha.net/consor/cgi-bin/index.php). By contrast, estimates from population variant frequencies range from 11 to 87 per million[13]but tend to overestimate the true prevalence because of regional differences in variant distribution and the rarity of individuals carrying founder mutations from multiple populations. The disease is notably more common in populations with founder mutations, such as Persian Jews (p.M743T), Japanese and Chinese individuals (p.D207V), Japanese and Korean individuals (p.V603L), and Indian or Southeast Asian groups (p.V727M).[4,14-16]Additional founder variants have been identified in specific populations, including p.I618T in Roma Gypsies and p.A662V in certain UK communities[14][Figure 2]. These ethnic and geographic patterns have played a critical role in identifying patient cohorts, advancing natural history studies, and supporting clinical trial development.

TheGNEgene, located on chromosome 9, consists of 13 exons and encodes multiple isoforms. Among them, hGNE1 (NM_005476) and hGNE2 are the most relevant in skeletal muscle, with hGNE2 (NM_001128227), encoding 753 amino acids, recommended as the reference transcript for variant annotation.[12]All isoforms are derived from the same genomic structure, and exon numbering should be based on the full 13-exon gene to ensure consistency [Figure 2]. As of January 2025, over 320 pathogenic variants have been reported in the Human Gene Mutation Database (https://portal.biobase-international.com/hgmd/pro/start.php), most of which are missense mutations. These mutations are typically located outside the allosteric site and are associated with GNE myopathy, whereas allosteric site variants are linked to sialuria, a distinct clinical entity.[17]Other types of pathogenic variants include nonsense mutations, frameshifts, small insertions/deletions, copy number variations (CNVs), and deep intronic variants, although these are less frequent.[18-20]The growing use of next-generation sequencing and RNA-based diagnostics has expanded the detectable mutational spectrum, enabling the identification of complex and regulatory-region variants that were previously missed.[21]

GNE myopathy results from impaired sialic acid biosynthesis due to reduced activity of the bifunctional enzyme UDP-N-acetylglucosamine 2-epimerase/N-acetylmannosamine kinase (GNE/MNK), encoded by theGNEgene. This reduction in enzyme activity leads to systemic hyposialylation of glycoproteins and glycolipids[22,23][Figure 1]. Although the precise mechanism linking hyposialylation to muscle degeneration remains unclear, animal studies strongly suggest that hyposialylation is one of the central pathogenic factors. Transgenic mice carrying the humanGNEp.D207V variant exhibit systemic hyposialylation, progressive motor deficits, β-amyloid accumulation in myofibers, and rimmed vacuole formation.[24]Notably, oral supplementation with sialic acid precursors in these mice prevents muscle atrophy[2]and can reverse muscle pathology even after symptom onset.[3]The disease is thought to result from partial loss of GNE function, as mutant proteins retain some enzyme activity.[25]Complete knockout of GNE in mice is embryonically lethal,[26]and no biallelic null mutations have been identified in humans.

Accumulating evidence from patient registries has revealed notable genotype–phenotype associations in GNE myopathy. Certain GNE variants correlate with distinct clinical trajectories. For instance, in Japanese cohorts, individuals homozygous for the p.V603L variant often exhibit early-onset, rapidly progressive disease, losing ambulation typically within a decade of symptom onset and showing a progressive decline in respiratory function over time.[4]By contrast, compound heterozygotes carrying the p.V603L and p.D207V variants exhibit much slower progression, often remaining ambulatory for more than 20 years and showing no significant decline in respiratory function.[4]Similar genotype-based differences are observed in Indian and Southeast Asian populations: the p.V727M variant, frequently found in compound heterozygotes, is associated with a milder phenotype, whereas the p.A662V variant appears to be linked to a more severe clinical course and earlier functional decline.[14]

Notably, recent analyses suggest that homozygosity for certain variants may result in a markedly attenuated or even asymptomatic phenotype. While the Japanese founder variant p.D207V is clearly pathogenic in compound heterozygotes, individuals homozygous for p.D207V are rarely affected. Although it has been estimated that 238 homozygotes exist in Japan, only three have been clinically diagnosed with GNE myopathy, and at least one asymptomatic elderly homozygote has been documented.[27]A similar pattern was observed for the Indian founder variant p.V727M, where the number of clinically diagnosed homozygous patients is disproportionately low compared to its expected frequency.[16,28]In addition, compound heterozygotes carrying p.V727M often exhibit a mild phenotype, suggesting that homozygosity for this variant may be associated with a very mild or even subclinical presentation.[14,16]This is further supported by the detection of homozygous individuals in population databases without corresponding clinical reports, indicating that p.V727M homozygosity may confer minimal disease burden. These observations underscore the importance of incorporating genotype information into prognosis, patient management, genetic counseling, and patient stratification in clinical trials.

Moreover, for c.620A>T encoding p.D207V, a pathomechanism has been proposed. Biochemical studies indicate that this variant interferes with GNE oligomerization. Structural modeling of a full-length GNE dimer reveals that the position of p.D207V is located away from the enzyme’s catalytic sites and near the dimerization interface [Figure 3]. The model suggests that the variant also interferes with domain communication, resulting in impaired transport of enzymatic products between the two catalytic domains within the mutated GNE protein. This leads to only a mild decrease in N-acetylmannosamine (ManNAc) 6-phosphate production, which may explain why individuals homozygous for c.620A>T rarely develop myopathy.[27]However, the dynamics of GNE enzyme oligomerizationin vivoremain to be clarified. Because oligomerization occurs within a complex and dynamic cellular environment and is strongly influenced by numerous factors, it is difficult to accurately capture its true nature using the simplified conditions ofin vitroassays. Furthermore, because thein vivoenvironment varies among individuals, differences in oligomerization states may arise, which may contribute to variations in disease severity. In addition, oligomerization itself may serve as a potential therapeutic target, as it is influenced by factors such as the cellular environment and aging, including alterations in redox balance, oxidative stress, and protein stability.[29]Therapies that specifically modulate oligomerization hold promise for the future in patients with this variant.

A similar consideration may apply to the p.V727M variant. Although its biochemical impact has not yet been fully understood, the residue is located on the surface of the ManNAc kinase domain, distant from the catalytic site [Figure 4]. Its spatial positioning suggests that this variant may affect post-translational modifications or interactions with other components, but it is unlikely to directly affect catalytic function. Similar to the p.D207V variant, this substitution could contribute to the relatively mild phenotype observed in homozygous individuals. However, this hypothesis remains speculative. Further studies are needed to investigate potential haplotype–phenotype correlations, exclude the presence of pathogenic variants in other genes involved in the sialic acid biosynthesis pathway, and explore possible epigenetic changes or regulatory disturbances that may affect GNE expression and sialylation levels.

GNE myopathy typically presents between the ages of 20 and 40 years, though onset can occur from adolescence to late adulthood.[1,4,14,16]The earliest symptom is usually foot drop caused by weakness of the tibialis anterior, leading to a steppage gait and frequent tripping. As the disease progresses, weakness extends to the hamstrings, distal upper limb muscles, and eventually to the proximal muscles of both the upper and lower extremities.[4,14,16,30,31]However, data from a Japanese patient registry (n= 220) demonstrated a broader range of initial manifestations: gait abnormalities were reported as the first symptom in 54.5% of patients, frequent tripping in 44.1%, foot drop in 30.0%, difficulty climbing stairs in 16.8%, inability to run in 6.4%, upper limb symptoms in 5.9%, difficulty standing on toes in 1.8%, and neck muscle weakness in 0.9%.[4]Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies showed that certain lower limb muscles—such as the short head of the biceps femoris, gluteus minimus, tibialis anterior, extensor digitorum longus, extensor hallucis longus, soleus, and medial gastrocnemius—are often affected even in atypical cases.[30-33]Clinically, patients often exhibit a characteristic gait pattern featuring foot drop combined with lumbar lordosis. A distinctive and diagnostically helpful feature of GNE myopathy is the relative preservation of quadriceps strength until the advanced stages of the disease.[34]Recently, “Beevor sign”—an upward movement of the umbilicus upon neck flexion due to selective weakness of the lower abdominal and hip flexor muscles—has also been reported as a supportive clinical finding in some patients.[35]Disease severity varies considerably among individuals: while many patients become wheelchair-dependent within 10–15 years of onset, others retain ambulation for decades.[4,14]Although many patients eventually lose ambulation, the appropriate use of orthotic support can help delay this decline and enhance quality of life.

Although GNE myopathy is primarily characterized by progressive skeletal muscle weakness, recent studies have highlighted several extra-muscular features. These manifestations, often underrecognized, are important considerations in comprehensive patient care.

Thrombocytopenia is the most frequently reported extra-muscular manifestation associated with GNE myopathy. Several studies suggest that impaired sialylation of platelets due to GNE dysfunction may lead to their premature clearance from circulation, resulting in reduced platelet counts.[36]This hypothesis is further supported by reports of platelet-associated IgG (PA-IgG) positivity in a considerable proportion of Japanese patients.[7]In a survey conducted through the Japanese national registry, 4.1% of patients reported a diagnosis of idiopathic thrombocytopenia—a prevalence higher than that observed in the general population.[6]While thrombocytopenia is not a common clinical feature, these findings suggest that routine monitoring of platelet counts may be advisable. Furthermore, there are reports of thrombocytopenia being identified before the onset of muscle symptoms.[22]When accompanied by other clinical findings, the presence of thrombocytopenia may prompt genetic testing for GNE variants and potentially facilitate earlier diagnosis.

Respiratory involvement in GNE myopathy is generally considered uncommon, especially in the early to mid-stages of the disease. However, findings from Japanese cohorts have revealed that a subset of patients, particularly those who are wheelchair-dependent, may experience a decline in respiratory function over time, with some requiring nocturnal non-invasive ventilation.[4]This decline may correlate with earlier disease onset and more severe clinical progression. Genotype may contribute to this variability. For instance, respiratory function was preserved in patients with the homozygous p.I618T variant[37]as well as in those with the compound heterozygous p.D207V/p.V603L genotype,[4]even over extended follow-up periods. By contrast, reduced respiratory capacity was more frequently observed in individuals homozygous for p.V603L.[4]These observations suggest that respiratory dysfunction in GNE myopathy may be associated with both ambulatory status and specific GNE variants. Accordingly, regular respiratory assessment is advisable, particularly for non-ambulatory patients and those with genotypes linked to more severe disease progression.

Sleep apnea syndrome (SAS) appears to be more prevalent in patients with GNE myopathy than in the general population. A survey of the Japanese patient registry found SAS diagnosed in 16.3% of males and 6.6% of females.[6]Notably, the occurrence of SAS showed no correlation with body weight, disease severity, or respiratory function.[6,7]The underlying mechanism remains unclear. However, factors such as craniofacial skeletal architecture and reduced muscle strength required to maintain upper airway patency—resulting in a predisposition for tongue root descent and pharyngeal obstruction—may contribute to its development. Future investigations assessing craniofacial parameters such as the facial axis angle, as well as functional evaluations of upper airway muscles (e.g., genioglossus, soft palate, mylohyoid), which have not yet been performed in patients with GNE myopathy, are warranted. Although current evidence linking GNE myopathy and SAS is limited to Japanese patients, the potentially life-threatening nature of untreated SAS, including cardiovascular risks[38], warrants clinical vigilance. To clarify this association across diverse populations, international data are needed. Meanwhile, sleep screening should be considered for prevention and to support global evidence building.

The onset of GNE myopathy frequently coincides with reproductive age, prompting questions about pregnancy safety and disease progression. Overall, available data suggest that pregnancy and delivery are generally well tolerated. A nationwide Japanese survey found no significant worsening of disease symptoms in the majority of cases and reported no serious maternal or neonatal complications. While the incidence of threatened abortion was significantly higher among women who were symptomatic before delivery, the rate of full-term deliveries was comparable to that in the general population. Six patients experienced disease onset, and 19.0% of those with pre-existing symptoms reported worsening within a year postpartum.[39]However, postpartum disease worsening was based on self-reported evaluations, and lifestyle changes during this period should be considered as influence factors in patients’ perceptions of disease progression. Future prospective studies with objective assessments are needed to better understand the clinical course during pregnancy and postpartum.

Studies based on patient registries found no clear disease-specific abnormalities in cardiac function or structure.[4]Mild arrhythmias or structural changes were occasionally reported but were typically linked to other comorbidities.[37]Although cardiomyopathy was observed in some family members, a direct connection to GNE myopathy remains unconfirmed. Overall, cardiac complications are generally not associated with GNE myopathy.

Diagnosis is confirmed by identifying biallelic pathogenic variants in theGNEgene. While muscle biopsy was previously considered essential, increasing disease awareness and the availability of next-generation sequencing have enabled diagnosis in many typical cases without performing a muscle biopsy. Distal muscle weakness with quadriceps sparing and marked fat replacement of the hamstrings in Computed tomography (CT)/MRI imaging are characteristic features that strongly suggest the diagnosis. Routine laboratory tests can provide supportive information, although they are not specific to GNE myopathy. Serum creatine kinase levels are typically normal or only mildly elevated (usually <1000 U/L),[4]which may help distinguish GNE myopathy from muscular dystrophies associated with markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK). Electromyography often reveals nonspecific myopathic changes, such as short-duration, low-amplitude, polyphasic motor unit potentials with early recruitment.[22]In atypical cases, muscle biopsy remains informative; it typically shows rimmed vacuoles [Figure 5] and the accumulation of protein aggregates such as β-amyloid and phosphorylated tau. Electron microscopy can also reveal tubulofilamentous inclusions in the nucleus and cytoplasm.[40]These findings may resemble those seen in sporadic inclusion body myositis. However, GNE myopathy lacks the endomysial CD8-positive T cell infiltration and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II upregulation characteristic of sIBM, and these differences can aid in distinguishing the two conditions. These pathological findings support clinical suspicion and guide genetic analysis.

Various biomarkers have been used to assess disease progression and treatment efficacy in GNE myopathy, but more sensitive and reliable markers are still needed. Functional tests like the 6-minute walk test (6MWT) show high variability and limited usefulness, while the 2-minute walk test (2MWT) and upper/lower extremity composite (UEC/LEC) scores appear more promising, though they have limitations, especially in late-stage disease.[1]The GNE Myopathy Functional Activity Scale (GNEM-FAS), a disease-specific questionnaire assessing daily activities, has demonstrated reliability in monitoring progression.[1]Imaging biomarkers such as thigh muscle volume measured by MRI and spectroscopy correlate well with clinical status.[30]Biochemical biomarkers focused on sialylation, including quantitative fluorescence on muscle biopsies such as lectin histochemistry[41]and serum markers like KL-6 and PA-IgG, show potential but have limitations, particularly regarding invasiveness and sensitivity to therapeutic response.[1,42]Overall, further research is needed to develop better biomarkers that accurately reflect disease status and treatment effects.

Over the past two decades, progress in disease models has clarified the role of hyposialylation in GNE myopathy, prompting a series of clinical trials focused on sialic acid supplementation. In addition, research into alternative therapeutic strategies is ongoing, aiming to expand future treatment options.

The primary therapeutic strategy for GNE myopathy has focused on restoring sialic acid levels to compensate for impaired biosynthesis caused by pathogenic variants in the GNE gene [Figure 1]. Among the agents developed, aceneuramic acid extended-release (Ace-ER, brand name Acenobel) has demonstrated the most clinical progress [Table 1]. Ace-ER was designed to improve systemic availability of free sialic acid by addressing its rapid metabolism and urinary excretion.[43]Early-phase studies confirmed its safety and showed increased serum sialic acid levels. A phase 2 trial and a subsequent placebo-controlled phase 3 trial conducted in Japan demonstrated that 6 g/day of Ace-ER slowed the decline in upper extremity strength, particularly in ambulatory patients.[44,45]By contrast, an international phase 3 trial failed to demonstrate significant efficacy.[46]While various factors are under consideration, differences in genetic background and disease severity across participants may have contributed.

Summary of Clinical Trials for GNE Myopathy

Based on the consistent findings in Japanese patients, Ace-ER was approved in Japan in March 2024, becoming the first and only approved treatment for GNE myopathy worldwide. However, the therapy is currently distributed under a limited supply program, and domestic availability remains insufficient. Despite this, Acenobel has emerged as a major therapeutic milestone, representing significant hope for affected individuals.

Other agents targeting the sialic acid pathway are under clinical investigation. N-acetyl-D-mannosamine (ManNAc), a precursor in the de novo biosynthetic pathway, bypasses the feedback inhibition characteristic of Neu5Ac and is efficiently taken up by cells. In preclinical models, ManNAc restored protein sialylation and improved muscle pathology.[45]Clinical studies confirmed safety, increased plasma sialic acid levels, and enhanced sarcolemmal sialylation.[47]A single-center phase 2 study showed slower functional decline,[48]and a larger multicenter phase 2 trial is currently ongoing (NCT04231266), with results expected in 2025.

Another promising agent is 6’-sialyllactose (6SL), a sialylated oligosaccharide with prolonged retention in the bloodstream. In GNE myopathy mouse models, 6SL demonstrated greater efficacy in reducing muscle degeneration compared to Neu5Ac.[47]Initial pilot studies in humans confirmed safety and showed dose-dependent improvements in proximal strength and reduced muscle fat infiltration on MRI.[49]Randomized controlled trials further supported its safety and suggested that long-term use may slow disease progression, particularly in early-stage patients.[50]

These clinical trials demonstrated the effectiveness of sialic acid supplementation therapy. However, continued research may be needed to develop more effective approaches, such as those for sustained high-dose administration, to further improve treatment outcomes.

GNE myopathy is a monogenic disorder caused by loss-of-function variants in the GNE gene, making gene replacement a rational therapeutic target. Early gene therapy approaches included liposomal delivery of wild-type GNE cDNA, which demonstrated transient biochemical correction in a patient with advanced disease.[51]More recently, adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based vectors were investigated for their ability to deliver functional GNE into muscle tissue. In preclinical studies, AAV8 vectors carrying human wild-type GNE successfully transduced both murine and patient-derived muscle cells, resulting in the production of functional GNE transcripts and increased sialylation.[52]

Despite the theoretical advantages, several challenges remain. Approximately 50% of patients with GNE myopathy had pre-existing neutralizing antibodies against AAV,[53]which potentially limited the effectiveness of systemic delivery. Moreover, concerns have been raised regarding acute toxicity and long-term safety, including immune reactions, apoptosis, and oncogenic risks, particularly with high-dose administration to skeletal muscle.[54]Ongoing studies aim to develop low-dose, muscle-specific gene delivery systems to address these issues and enable broader clinical application.

Oxidative stress was implicated as a secondary pathomechanism in GNE myopathy, particularly in the setting of protein hyposialylation. In mouse models, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) were observed in muscle tissue, and treatment with the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC) led to notable improvements in muscle pathology and function.[55]Although clinical trials are still lacking, adding antioxidant therapy to sialic acid replacement may improve its effectiveness. Human trials are expected to provide more insight into its potential efficacy.

In recent years, significant progress has been made in elucidating the pathophysiology and clarifying the clinical phenotype of GNE myopathy, alongside encouraging advancements in therapy development. To deepen our understanding of this disorder, expanding existing patient registries and enhancing global collaboration are essential. Notably, patient registries have provided valuable insights into extra-skeletal muscle manifestations and pregnancy outcomes, as well as long-term ambulatory prognosis. Beyond patient recruitment for clinical studies, registries play a critically important role by enabling the collection of long-term, wide-ranging clinical data that would otherwise be difficult to obtain. Furthermore, while improving the efficacy of sialic acid supplementation continues to be a promising avenue, the development of additional therapeutic strategies such as gene-based treatments and antioxidant approaches—is anticipated as future interventions.
