# COCH-related autosomal dominant nonsyndromic hearing loss: a phenotype-genotype study.
**Authors:** Oh KS, Walls D, Joo SY, Kim JA, Yoo JE, Koh YI, Kim DH, Rim JH, Choi HJ, Kim HY, Yu S, Smith RJ, Choi JY, Gee HY, Jung J
**Journal:** Hum Genet (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00439-021-02368-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00439-021-02368-y)

## Content

This phenotype-genotype study aimed to investigate the extent of audioprofile variability related to cochlin major domains and to identify potential ethnic-specific differences associated with COCH-related hearing loss. Eight Korean families (26 cases) were diagnosed with COCH-related hearing loss by exome sequencing. Audiometric test results were combined with those from nine published East Asian families (20 cases) and compared with those from 38 European-descent families (277 cases). Audioprofiles were created by grouping audiometric test results into age ranges by age at testing and then averaging hearing loss thresholds by frequency within age ranges. The functional impact of the identified variants was assessed in vitro by examining the intracellular trafficking, secretion, and cleavage of cochlin. In both East Asian and European-descent families segregating COCH-related hearing loss, deafness-associated variants in non-LCCL domains of cochlin were associated with hearing loss that was more severe earlier in life than hearing loss caused by variants in the LCCL domain. Consistent with this phenotypic difference, functional studies demonstrated distinct pathogenic mechanisms for COCH variants in a domain-dependent manner; specifically, a cytotoxic effect was observed for the p.Phe230Leu variant, which is located in the vWFA1 domain. No ethnic-specific differences in hearing loss progression were observed, except for those attributable to an overrepresentation of presymptomatic cases in the European-descent cohort.

Hearing loss is the most common sensory disorder, being diagnosed in 1 in 500 newborns and increasing in prevalence with age to reach up to approximately half of all octogenarians (Fortnum et al. 2001; Kral and O'Donoghue 2010; Morton and Nance 2006). Its causality includes both environmental and genetic factors. To date, more than 125 genes have been implicated in nonsyndromic hearing loss (NSHL) (https://hereditaryhearingloss.org/), approximately 30% of which are associated with autosomal dominant NSHL (ADNSHL) (Azaiez et al. 2018; Cunningham and Tucci 2017).

COCH is a frequently mutated gene in ADNSHL (Hilgert et al. 2009). In 1997, genetic variants of this gene were linked to ADNSHL at the DFNA9 locus (MIM 601369) (N. G. Robertson et al. 1997) and, in 2018, to autosomal recessive nonsyndromic hearing loss (ARNSHL) at the DFNB110 locus (MIM 603196) (S. P. F. JanssensdeVarebeke et al. 2018). COCH-related ADNSHL typically affects the high frequencies, usually in the 3rd decade of life, and ultimately progresses to severe-to-profound hearing loss across all frequencies by the 6th decade (Nahid G. Robertson et al. 1998). Vestibular dysfunction can occur and ranges from minimal unsteadiness to severe balance disturbances and vertigo (S.-H. Bae et al. 2014a; Nahid G. Robertson et al. 1998).

COCH encodes cochlin, the major noncollagenous protein in the extracellular matrix of the cochlea and vestibule (Ikezono et al. 2001; Jung et al. 2019; N. G. Robertson et al. 1997; Verdoodt et al. 2021). It is composed of an N-terminal LCCL domain (Limulus factor C, cochlin, and late gestation lung protein Lgl1) followed by two von Willebrand factor A-like domains (vWFA1 and vWFA2). Variants in the LCCL domain typically cause misfolding and defective multimerization of cochlin (S.-H. Bae et al. 2014a; Jianhua Yao et al. 2010a), and are associated with late-onset hearing loss accompanied by vestibular dysfunction (S.-H. Bae et al. 2014a). In comparison, variants in the vWFA domains result in secretion failure and aggregate formation (S.-H. Bae et al. 2014a), and are associated with early-onset hearing loss without vestibular dysfunction (S.-H. Bae et al. 2014a).

This study aimed to confirm these genotype–phenotype associations and identify potential ethnic-specific differences in COCH-related hearing loss.

Detailed materials and methods are further described in Supplementary methods.

A cohort of COCH-related hearing loss individuals enrolled in the Yonsei University Hearing Loss (YUHL) study was identified by whole-exome sequencing (WES) and evaluation of pathogenic variants for hearing loss, as described previously (Fig. 1a) (Jung et al. 2017). All patients in the YUHL cohort had hearing loss and were referred to Severance Hospital, Yonsei University Health System, for further evaluation and treatment. Pure-tone audiometry (250 to 8000 Hz) and speech audiometry were performed for all patients and their affected and unaffected family members.

Audiometric data and diagnosed causative COCH variants in the YUHL cohort (Supplementary Figs. S1-S3) were combined with those from COCH-related hearing loss individuals in AudioGene 4.0 (https://audiogene.eng.uiowa.edu) and previous publications (Supplementary Table S1); a total of 13 publications were utilized for audiometric data. Among them, East Asian families were combined with the YUHL cohort, whereas European-descent families were combined with those in the AudioGene 4.0 (Supplementary Tables S2, S3). Ethnicity of the included individuals was either recorded as reported or inferred from their country of origin.

All procedures were approved by the human research institutional review boards at the Yonsei University College of Medicine, Seoul, Korea (IRB #4-2015-0659) and the University of Iowa, Iowa City, Iowa, USA. Informed written consent was obtained from all YUHL cohort individuals for participation in this study and publication of their clinical data.

In the YUHL cohort, several distinct clinical features were observed according to the location of COCH variants. For instance, asymmetric hearing thresholds was exclusively identified in patients carrying variants in the LCCL domain (noted as stars in Supplementary Figs. S1 and S3). In addition, vestibular hypofunction was observed only in families segregating variants in the LCCL domain. Although recurrent and episodic vertigo symptoms were reported in YUHL195-21, YUHL195-22, YUHL265-21, YUHL612-21 and YUHL612-12, only YUHL195-21 and YUHL195-22 showed bilateral hyporeflexive caloric responses by videonystagmography and bilateral reduced gain in video-head impulse and rotary chair testing.

The genetic variants were classified according to the hearing loss-specific American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) criteria (Oza et al. 2018; Richards et al. 2015). COCH variants causative of DFNA9 were detected in eight families, including YUHL5 (Jung et al. 2015), YUHL102, YUHL104, YUHL195, YUHL265, YUHL522, YUHL590, and YUHL612 (Table 1). The suspected inheritance pattern was autosomal dominant (Fig. 1a). In these families, the affected individuals showed progressive hearing loss with onset age of approximately late 10s to 40s. Subsequently, the hearing function deteriorated further, with severe-to-moderate hearing loss throughout all frequencies being observed in their 60s (Supplementary Figs. S1 and S3).

In exome analyses, two reported and four novel variants of COCH (NM_001135058.1) were identified (Table 1). The previously reported variant c.113G>A (p.Gly38Asp) was found in YUHL195 and c.1580T>G (p.Phe527Cys) was identified in both YUHL265 and YUHL522 (Cho et al. 2012; Kim et al. 2016). In YUHL102, YUHL104, YUHL590, and YUHL612, the variants in COCH, including c.1207G>T (p.Gly403Cys), c.226G>A (p.Ala76Thr), c.690G>T (p.Phe230Leu), and c.1345G>A (p.Gly447Asp), respectively, were newly designated as causative variants associated with DFNA9. The protein of these novel variants was predicted to have deleterious effects as per in silico analyses (Table 1). These variants have not been reported in ClinVar, HGMD, or the Deafness Variation Database. The novel variants were not found or were extremely rare in public databases, such as gnomAD. Furthermore, segregation analysis by Sanger sequencing revealed the variants in seven families to cosegregate with the progressive hearing-loss phenotype (Supplementary Fig. S2). The four novel variants herein identified were diversely located in LCCL (p.Ala76Thr), vWFA1 (p.Phe230Leu), and vWFA2 (p.Gly403Cys and p.Gly447Asp) domains, and were all missense (Fig. 1b). The amino acid residues affected by these variants were evolutionarily well conserved down to Danio rerio (Fig. 1c).

Audioprofiling was used to characterize and compare the hearing loss of the different groups of individuals included in the study. Audioprofiles were generated by binning audiometric test results into age ranges by age at testing and then averaging hearing loss thresholds by frequency within the age ranges. When thresholds were available for both ears, the minimum threshold at each frequency was used. Since the individuals had varying numbers of audiometric test results, a repeated random sampling technique was used to equally weight the contributions of individuals to the audioprofiles. A random audiometric test was sampled for each individual and an audioprofile was generated for the sample. This procedure was repeated 10,000 times (empirically chosen) and the resulting sample audioprofiles were averaged.

Human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 and HeLa cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified essential medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and penicillin (50 IU/mL)/streptomycin (50 μg/mL) (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) NIH3T3 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% bovine calf serum and antibiotics. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere.

The variant clones of COCH were generated by polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based site-directed mutagenesis of a wild-type COCH-bearing plasmid previously described (Jung et al. 2015), using the QuickChange II XL site-directed mutagenesis kit (Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. HEK293 cells were transfected with wild-type or mutant COCH plasmids in serum-free media using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) or Transporter 5 (Polysciences, Warrington, PA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To detect the secreted cochlin, culture media were collected 72 h after transfection and concentrated using Amicon Ultra-4 filters (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Concentrated media were partly used for immunoblotting.

Experiments were performed as previously described (Gee et al. 2010). The following antibodies were used: Anti-BiP (ab21685), anti-β-actin (ab6276) from Abcam (Cambridge, UK); anti-Myc (sc-40), anti-aldolase A1 (sc-12059) from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA), anti-GM130 (610822) from BD Biosciences (Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), and anti-COCH (LCCL-specific, NBP1-69141) form Novus Biologicals (Centennial, CO, USA). Immunoblotting was performed using primary antibodies at 1:1,000 dilution, followed by corresponding anti-isotype secondary antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at 1:2,000 dilution. Signals were visualized using the SuperSignal West Pico kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/).

HEK293 cells transfected with wild-type or variant COCH plasmids were cultured for 36 h, after which the culture medium was collected, concentrated using Amicon Ultra-4 filters (Millipore), and diluted with serum-free medium. Approximately 1.5 × 104 NIH3T3 cells (murine fibroblasts) in 100 μL medium per well were seeded into a 96-well plate and incubated for 24 h. Thereafter, culture medium was changed by 100 μL of conditioned medium containing cochlin from HEK293 cells, and the cells were incubated for another 36 h. Next, cell viability of NIH3T3 cells was evaluated by the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay (Invitrogen). Briefly, MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL) in culture medium was added to the wells and incubated for 3 h. The medium was then removed, and purple formazan crystals were solubilized with 50 μL dimethyl sulfoxide. Absorbance of each sample was measured at 540 nm.

Hearing loss was compared statistically between groups by multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) using R (version 4.0.5; https://www.r-project.org/). A repeated random sampling technique was used, where one audiometric test result was randomly sampled for each individual and MANCOVA performed. The analysis was repeated 10,000 times (empirically chosen) and the median p value from all repetitions was reported. Fisher’s exact test was performed using R to compare variant distributions across the populations.

All quantitative experimental data were obtained from at least three independent experiments. Statistical differences between three or more groups were examined using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni corrections for multiple comparisons using Prism 7.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Two-sided p values < 0.05 were considered significant.

The study data set comprised 983 audiograms (927 European-descent, 56 East Asian) from 317 individuals (277, 40) belonging to 55 families (38, 17) (Table S2). Each audiogram included hearing loss thresholds at up to six frequencies (250, 500, 1,000, 2,000, 4,000, and 8,000 Hz) for each ear and age at testing. Most of the audiograms were associated with variants in the LCCL domain (926 audiograms, 272 individuals, 41 families), followed by vWFA2 (27, 25, 9), and vWFA1 (3, 3, 1). The remaining audiograms were associated with interdomain variants (27, 17, 4).

Audioprofiles for COCH and its two major domains—the LCCL domain and the vWFA domains—were generated (Fig. 2a-c), along with an audioprofile for COCH interdomain variants (Fig. 2d). Hearing loss appeared to be greater following onset in vWFA-associated cases vs. LCCL-associated cases; from ages 20 to 39 it was moderate in high frequencies for vWFA but still mild for LCCL. Hearing loss associated with interdomain variants was even greater following onset, which occurred earlier, with moderate-to-severe hearing loss in high frequencies before age 20. MANCOVA was performed to determine the significance of the differences observed between the audioprofiles. Since there were relatively few audiograms for variants outside the LCCL domain, non-LCCL audiograms were combined into a single group for comparison with LCCL audiograms. Hearing loss differed significantly between the LCCL and non-LCCL groups (p = 5.5 × 10−5) (Table 2).

MANCOVA was also performed to investigate whether ethnicity could be a contributing factor for COCH-related hearing loss, revealing a significant difference between East Asian and European-descent populations (p = 5.3 × 10−4) (Table 2). Mutational spectrum of the populations differed significantly by domain (Fisher’s exact test, p = 5.4 × 10−14), potentially explaining the observed difference between populations. To control for variant domain in the population comparison, the combined data set was divided into LCCL and non-LCCL cohorts, and the population analyses were repeated, revealing a moderately significant difference by population for the LCCL cohort (p = 0.016), whereas no significant difference was observed for the non-LCCL cohort.

An examination of the variant composition of the LCCL cohort showed that p.Pro51Ser, a founder variant identified in Belgian and Dutch families (Fransen et al. 2001), was the most common variant among European-descent cases (167 out of 255 cases) with no p.Pro51Ser carriers identified in the East Asian group. Therefore, hearing loss was compared between p.Pro51Ser vs. non-p.Pro51Ser cases, which showed a significant difference between groups (p = 1.9 × 10−7), suggesting that p.Pro51Ser cases may account for the observed population difference in the LCCL-based hearing loss phenotype (Fig. 2e,f). Exclusion of p.Pro51Ser cases resolved the audioprofile differences previously observed between the populations (p = 0.20).

To elucidate the molecular basis of different audioprofiles according to the affected domains and variants, intracellular trafficking, secretion, and cleavage of mutant cochlin proteins were investigated next. Specifically, the newly identified variant cochlins from the YUHL cohort were analyzed (Fig. 1) and their characteristic pathogenic profiles were compared to those of previously reported COCH variants.

Subcellular localization of wild-type and mutated cochlin proteins were compared in HeLa cells by coimmunofluorescence with endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi markers (BiP and GM130, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. S4). Wild-type cochlin colocalized with BiP and GM130, since it is released through the ER and Golgi into the extracellular space via a conventional trafficking pathway. The p.Gly38Asp, p.Ala76Thr, p.Phe230Leu, p.Gly403Cys, p.Gly447Asp, and p.Phe527Cys variants showed no difference in localization compared to the wild-type cochlin.

Secretion and cleavage were examined by western blotting using the p.Cys162Tyr mutant cochlin as a positive control for defective secretion and decreased cleavage (S.-H. Bae et al. 2014a; Gao et al. 2013). The p.Gly38Asp, p.Ala76Thr, p.Phe230Leu, p.Gly403Cys, p.Gly447Asp, p.Phe527Cys, and p.Cys162Tyr mutant cochlins were identified in cell lysates, indicating that its expression was not affected by these variants (Fig. 3a, b). However, the variants in vWFA2 domain prominently increased aggregated proteins including dimers and multimers, comparable with the previous data for p.Ala487Pro in the nonreducing condition (Fig. 3c, d) (S.-H. Bae et al. 2014a; Cho et al. 2012).

Secreted and cleaved cochlin in culture medium differed among variants, with the C-terminal fragment being almost undetectable with p.Gly403Cys, p.Gly447Asp, and p.Phe527Cys (vWFA2 domain), indicating impaired cleavage of these variants by aggrecanase (Fig. 3e,f) (Jung et al. 2015; Jung et al. 2019). Notably, the variants in the LCCL domain (p.Gly38Asp and p.Ala76Thr) distinctly formed a dimer even in the reducing condition (Fig. 3e), which is consistent with the previous data (J. Yao et al. 2010b). These findings indicate that the dimer formation is characteristic of the variants in the LCCL domain, whereas the aggregate formation and impaired post-translational cleavage is dominant in the variants in the vWFA2 domain. However, not only wild-type LCCL peptide, but also LCCL peptides harboring a variant in the LCCL domain did not dimerize (Supplementary Fig. S5). p.Phe230Leu in vWFA1 domain neither formed a dimer nor decreased C-terminal cleaved cochlin (Fig. 3c-f). In addition, the protein stability of p.Phe230Leu was not decreased when compared to that of WT (Supplementary Fig. S6).

To examine possible cytotoxicity, as previously shown for p.Gly90Glu mutant cochlin (Jianhua Yao et al. 2010a), NIH3T3 cells were incubated with conditioned medium from HEK293 cells expressing wild-type or mutant cochlins for 72 h, and cell viability was assessed. Two other benign variants in the vWFA1 domain—p.Asp281 Asn and p.Arg324Gln—were included as negative controls; they were normal in expression, secretion, and post-translational cleavage (Supplementary Fig. S7). As a result, only p.Phe230Leu significantly decreased cell survival (Fig. 3g), indicating a third mechanism of action and suggesting that pathogenic variants in the vWFA1 domain can be cytotoxic.

DFNA9 presents as late-onset progressive hearing loss with or without vestibular symptoms (Nahid G. Robertson et al. 1998; Tsukada et al. 2015). In this study, audioprofiles of all cases in the present COCH-related hearing loss YUHL data set showed mild or mild-to-moderate down-sloping hearing loss in the first four decades of life, followed by moderate-to-severe hearing loss from ages 40 to 59 and severe-to-profound deafness at later ages (Table 1, and Supplementary Figs. S1 and S3).

Audioprofiling of individuals by affected major protein domain (LCCL, vWFA, or none, i.e., interdomain) suggested differences in the progression of hearing loss by variant location. Hearing loss in LCCL-associated cases appeared to be milder following onset vs. vWFA-associated cases, which in turn appeared to be milder following onset vs. interdomain-associated cases. MANCOVA confirmed the apparent differences by protein domain when the hearing loss of LCCL and non-LCCL cases was compared (p = 5.5 × 10−5). vWFA-associated hearing loss appeared milder from ages 60 to 99 vs. LCCL- and interdomain-associated hearing loss, but may be attributable to differences in age distribution within this age group—the average age of vWFA-associated cases (64.3) is lowest.

A previous study with combined AudioGene data for other genes identified an ethnicity-based difference in hearing function (Walls et al. 2020). Therefore, herein, potential ethnicity-based differences were also evaluated. A moderate difference in audioprofiles was observed between East Asian or European-descent populations among the LCCL cases; however, it was attributable to the predominance of the p.Pro51Ser founder variant in the European-descent cohort. The p.Pro51Ser variant was associated with milder hearing loss in high frequencies at younger ages compared to other LCCL variants. This difference is presumed to be due to relatively more presymptomatic cases among the p.Pro51Ser cases: p.Pro51Ser audiometric data in AudioGene v4.0 were collected extensively for many members of affected families, thereby collecting data for many younger individuals, whereas this extensive collection was not done for families affected by non-p.Pro51Ser variants. Since onset of DFNA9 hearing loss is typically in the 4th decade, young, presymptomatic cases tend to be underrepresented in studies (S. JanssensdeVarebeke et al. 2019). Presuming more underrepresentation of young non-p.Pro51Ser LCCL cases has occurred vs. p.Pro51Ser cases, the p.Pro51Ser audioprofile may be more representative of the natural history of hearing loss associated with the LCCL domain.

Underrepresentation of presymptomatic cases to varying degrees is a limitation of our audiometric data analysis. Another important limitation is that the asymmetric component of COCH-associated hearing loss is not a factor in the analysis—we consider only the symmetric component by choosing the better hearing threshold between ears, in attempt to remove the effects of non-genetic causes of asymmetry.

Having identified domain-associated differences in COCH hearing loss by audiometric data analysis, we sought to elucidate the underlying functional impact of different COCH variants. Indeed, significant mechanistic variability was suggested in previous reports. For example, p.Pro51Ser, p.Val66Gly, and p.Gly88Glu results in misfolding of the LCCL domain, which in turn leads to cytotoxicity and inner ear damage (Grabski et al. 2003; Liepinsh et al. 2001). Multimer formation and intracellular accumulation of p.Cys162Tyr and p.Ala487Pro were also previously reported (S. H. Bae et al. 2014b). Impaired post-translational cleavage of the LCCL domain has been designated as the pathogenic mechanism for the variants in the vicinity of the interdomain region, including p.Val123Glu (Jung et al. 2015). However, owing to the paucity of audiograms and variants in each domain of COCH, whether variant location is associated with different an underlying mechanism of action had remained unclear.

In this study, we confirmed that underlying pathomechanism differed by the variant locations in COCH and determined distinct phenotypes. Dimerization of LCCL domain variants p.Gly38Asp and p.Ala76Thr was observed, while vWFA2 variants p.Gly403Cys, p.Gly447Asp, and p.Phe527Cys showed impaired post-translational cleavage. These findings suggest that variants in the LCCL domain cause protein misfolding and dimerization without any defect in cellular trafficking and secretion. Since the LCCL domain is soluble and secreted, it is reasonable to hypothesize that its nonfunctional dimer may circulate in the perilymph and provoke an autoimmune response, as suggested previously (Baek et al. 2006; Pathak et al. 2013), which is consistent with the fluctuating hearing loss and vertigo-like symptoms that are seen with variants of the LCCL domain (Baek et al. 2006).

Cho et al. (Cho et al. 2012) demonstrated that patients with DFNA9 carrying the vWFA2 variant p.Phe527Cys have unstable mutant cochlin that forms aggregates in the cochlea. The novel variants p.Gly403Cys and p.Gly447Asp are also located in the vWFA2 domain and more aggregated than the other variants in the LCCL domain (Fig. 3c). These findings suggest that aggregation is vWFA2 domain-specific. However, it could be also variant-specific. For instance, p.Cys162Tyr (in the interdomain between LCCL and vWFA1) changes a cysteine residue, which might disturb protein conformation, thereby leading to destabilization and aggregation (S.-H. Bae et al. 2014a). Chronic accumulation of such aggregates may be cytotoxic and lead to cochlear degeneration.

In addition, p.Gly403Cys, p.Gly447Asp, and p.Phe527Cys were found to cause impaired cleavage of the LCCL domain, suggesting that the C-terminal domain of cochlin, including vWFA2, normally lies close to the LCCL domain, and that these variants hinder the cleavage between LCCL and vWFA1 by aggrecanases (Jung et al. 2019). We also consider that the tendency toward aggregate formation in the variants in vWFA2 domain (Fig. 3c) may hide the target site of aggrecanase, and subsequently lead to the impaired cleavage of the variants in the vWFA2 domain. These multilayered and detrimental pathomechanisms caused by the variants in the vWFA2 domain may account for the early onset (beginning from ages 20 to 39) of mild-to-moderate high-frequency hearing loss. In contrast, the variants in the LCCL domain may cause a dimer or a non-functional LCCL peptide, which only results in the deficiency of functional LCCL peptide, but without cleavage impairment as occurred with variants in the vWFA2 domain. This difference may explain why variants in the LCCL domain induce only mild high-frequency hearing loss from ages 20 to 39.

We also identified a novel pathogenic mechanism associated with the variant in the vWFA1 domain (p.Phe230Leu), namely, a cytotoxic effect of mutant cochlin (Fig. 3g). Of the two previously reported variants in the vWFA1 domain reported in ClinVar, c.841G>A (p.Asp281Asn) is a known single nucleotide polymorphism (rs28362775) with a minor allele frequency of 0.5% for African chromosomes (gnomAD, 145/24032), making it benign. The second, c.971G>A (p.Arg324Gln) is also a known single nucleotide polymorphism (rs748731866) and is classified as likely benign. It has been identified in 0.04% (5/10024) of Ashkenazi Jewish and 0.04% (5/112076) of European chromosomes by gnomAD (Fig. 1b). Thus p.Phe230Leu is the first identified DFNA9-causing variant in the vWFA1 domain. While its secretion and cleavage are comparable to wild-type cochlin, p.Phe230Leu is cytotoxic. Further investigation is required to know whether the cytotoxicity of p.Phe230Leu is vWFA1 domain-specific.

In conclusion, although no ethnic-specific differences in COCH-related ADNSHL were identified between East Asian and European-descent populations, the presence of domain-specific differences in phenotype that appear to be associated with domain-driven mechanistic differences in variant effect were confirmed. Thus, dimerization of cochlin appears to be caused by LCCL variants, which results in mild asymmetric hearing loss at onset with vestibular hypofunction. In comparison, conformational changes, aggregated formations, and cytotoxicity due to vWFA variants lead to initially mild-to-moderate high-frequency hearing loss without hearing fluctuation and vestibular dysfunction. Altogether, multilayered pathogenic mechanisms result in phenotypic diversity in DFNA9.
