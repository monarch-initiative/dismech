# A case report of SPONASTRIME dysplasia with novel TONSL mutation: genetic analysis, clinical manifestations, and the effect of growth hormone treatment.
**Authors:** Yao M, Zhang C, Li S, Tian A, Liang F, Luo X
**Journal:** Hum Mol Genet (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1093/hmg/ddaf128](https://doi.org/10.1093/hmg/ddaf128)

## Content

SPONASTRIME dysplasia is a rare genetic disorder characterized by short stature, facial abnormalities, vertebral issues, and bone striations, caused by recessive mutations in the TONSL gene. We reported a 6-year-old boy with characteristic clinical features of SPONASTRIME dysplasia, accompanied by neutropenia. Genetic analysis revealed biallelic variants in TONSL: mother-inherited c.1289del (p.Gln430ArgfsTer13) and father-inherited c.1961G > C (p.Arg654Pro), both of which were previously unreported. We predicted the c.1289del (p.Gln430ArgfsTer13) variant as likely pathogenic in silico analysis, while the c.1961G > C (p.Arg654Pro) variant as uncertain pathogenicity in silico analysis, and the pathogenicity was confirmed by functional studies in transfected HEK 293 T cells. Six-month growth hormone therapy was administered to the patient after confirmed diagnosis, with limited improvement. These findings have important implications for the diagnosis and treatment of the disease.

SPONASTRIME dysplasia is a type of spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia, with an incidence of less than 1 in 1 000 000 [1]. It was firstly reported by Fanconi et al. [2] in 1983, with its name derived from ‘SPOndylar and NAsal changes, with STRIations of the MEtaphyses’. Typical physical features of the disease include disproportionate short stature, short limbs, and abnormal facial characteristics (prominent forehead, midface hypoplasia with depressed and short nasal bridge as well as crowded upper jaw teeth). Some patients may also exhibit immunological abnormalities such as hypogammaglobulinemia, neutropenia, dental anomalies, cataracts, cubitus valgus, and hip dysplasia. Sufferers may present with considerably heterogeneous clinical manifestations, and some of the resulted complications may have a great impact on patients’ quality of life. Skeletal X-ray appearance is the predominant diagnostically significant finding, characterized by lumbar vertebral body deformity and metaphyseal striations involving the distal femur and proximal tibia mostly [3–5].

Subsequent studies confirmed that the biallelic variation of the TONSL gene was the pathogenic molecular basis of SPONASTRIME dysplasia [3, 6]. The TONSL gene, located at 8q24.3, encodes the Tonsoku-like DNA repair protein, serving as a multi-domain scaffold that interacts with various DNA replication and repair factors [7, 8]. As a genomic protector, TONSL is essential in counteracting replication stress and preserving genome integrity, and participates in key developmental processes. The deficiency of TONSL disrupts DNA replication and homologous recombination repair, resulting in diverse skeletal dysplasia phenotypes and additional extra-skeletal features [9–11]. Existing functions studies gave documented increased spontaneous replication fork stalling in fibroblast cell lines derived from SPONASTRIME dysplasia patients. These cells also exhibited defective RAD51 foci formation following CPT-induced DNA damage, which is crucial for homologous recombination repair. These defects were effectively rescued by introducing wild-type TONSL via lentiviral transduction. In addition, tonsl−/− zebrafish embryos were observed with shortened body length, spinal abnormalities, and neutropenia. All embryos in the tonsl−/− mouse and zebrafish models were found to be deceased at pregnancy termination [3, 6].

This study reported a 6-year-old boy with SPONASTRIME dysplasia, and identified two novel variations in TONSL. Moreover, we investigated the effect of growth hormone (GH) therapy in the patient, with literature review on the efficacy of GH treatment [6, 12, 13].

The proband was a 6-year-old boy with short stature. He was also prone to respiratory infections, which occurred roughly once a month. His birth weight and length were 2400 g (−2.17 SDS) and 48 cm (−1.33 SDS), respectively, with the gestational age of 38 weeks. The patient showed no abnormality in developmental milestones and cognitive abilities. His parents were non-consanguineous, and the body heights of his father and mother were 166 cm and 154 cm, respectively. Moreover, there was no family history of similar conditions (Fig. 1a).

This patient had a body height of 88 cm (−5.67 SDS) at 6 years old, with a sitting height ratio of 0.61 (+5 SDS), indicating severe disproportionate short stature. He exhibited distinctive facial features, including a short and flat nose bridge, crowded teeth and a short neck (Fig. 1b). In addition, this patient also has skeletal abnormalities, including lumbar lordosis, limited elbow extension and genu valgum (Fig. 1c). No abnormalities in other endocrine hormones were detected.

Laboratory tests revealed neutropenia, with the neutrophil counts fluctuating in the range of 0.62–0.76 × 109/L (ref: 1.5–8.5 × 109/L). The immunoglobulin levels were detected to be within the normal range. His GH stimulation test peak was 13.1 ng/ml, exceeding 10 ng/ml, indicating no GH deficiency (Table S3). No abnormalities were found in other endocrine hormones. The patient was also examined with normal urinalysis, as well as normal functions of liver, kidney, thyroid, and adrenal glands. He showed normal electrolytes, 25OH-D and PTH levels (Table S4). Additionally, no abnormality revealed in echocardiogram and pituitary magnetic resonance imaging.

X-ray imaging revealed flattened and biconcave-shaped spinal vertebrae, particularly in the lumbar region (Fig. 1f). Longitudinal metaphyseal striations were visible in the distal femur (Fig. 1d). The femoral head at the hip joint showed significant developmental abnormalities, with shortening of the femoral neck (Fig. 1e). His spine curvature was normal at age of 6. However, the patient developed right-sided mild scoliosis at age of 7.5, with the apex at the 10th thoracic vertebra and a curvature of 10° (Fig. 1g, h).

This study detected two compound heterozygous variants of TONSL in the patient: a mother-inherited frameshift variant of c.1289del (p.Gln430ArgfsTer13) (Fig. 2a), and a father-inherited missense variant of c.1961G > C (p.Arg654Pro) (Fig. 2b). The variant of c.1289del was located in exon 10 and was predicted to cause a change from glutamine to arginine at amino acid 430. It resulted in a new reading frame that terminated at the 13th codon downstream of amino acid 430. Meanwhile, variant of c.1961G > C, located in exon 16, led to the substitution of arginine with proline at amino acid 645.

According to the criteria reported in ACMG guidelines, c.1289del was a null variant that may induce the loss of gene function (PVS1), and it was not found in literature or population databases (PM2). Therefore, the c.1289del variant of TONSL was classified as a likely pathogenic variant (PVS1 + PM2_Supporting).

In terms of c.1961G > C (p.Arg654Pro) variant, comparative analysis of mutation sites across species supported a relatively conservative site (Fig. 2c). The c.1961G > C (p.Arg654Pro) residue variant model constructed by AlphaFold [14] showed disrupted hydrogen bonds in the side chain (Fig. 2d). However, the predicted protein function analyzed by SIFT, PolyPhen_2, MutationTaster, and GERP+ provided conflicting results of harmful, harmful, benign, and harmful, respectively (BP4_Moderate). The disease was inherited in an autosomal recessive manner, with the paternal c.1961G > C variant occurring in trans with the maternal likely pathogenic variant c.1289del (on different copies of the gene), serving as moderate evidence of pathogenicity for the variant c.1961G > C (PM3(Trans)). Thus, c.1961G > C was preliminarily classified as of uncertain significance (BP4_Moderate +PM3(Trans)). Functional studies were needed to clarify the pathogenicity of the variant.

This study constructed plasmids with wild-type TONSL, c.1961G > C variant TONSL, and an empty vector control to further investigate the pathogenicity of c.1961G > C variant in TONSL. The function of the variant was characterized after transfection of these plasmids into HEK293T. In cells transfected with the mutant plasmid, the TONSL protein level was significantly reduced compared to the wild-type plasmid (Fig. 2e), while the mRNA level remained unchanged (Fig. 2f). Overall, the c.1961G > C (p.Arg654Pro) variant in TONSL might affect the expression of the protein after transcription, and it was classified as a likely pathogenic variant (PS3 + BP4_Moderate +PM3 (Trans)).

The patient was diagnosed with SPONASTRIME dysplasia in view of the clinical presentation, genetic sequencing, and functional analysis. With parental consent, the patient started a 6-month treatment of weekly PEGlyated recombinant GH (rhGH) with a dose of 0.2 mg/kg at 6 years and 2 months of age. During the treatment, the patient’s height increased from 88.5 cm (−5.71 SD) to 90.7 cm (−5.67 SD), with a growth velocity of 4.4 cm/year. It was barely improved compared to the pre-treatment growth velocity of 4.1 cm/year and significantly below the average growth velocity of 6.4 cm/year [15] (Fig. 3). In addition, there was a notable increase in IGF-1 levels, with normal glucose metabolism and thyroid function during treatment.

Through literature review, this study identified 16 case reports with detailed clinical information [3, 4, 6, 12, 16–27]. Therefore, the total number of cases was 45, including our own. Among these, 32 cases underwent genetic testing, revealing 24 cases with bi-allelic TONSL mutations, 5 with a single TONSL mutation, and 3 with no identified mutations through whole exome sequencing.

In aspect of clinical manifestations, all of the patients exhibited significantly short stature, with height standard deviation score (SDS) ranging from −0.9 to −10.8. Remarkably, an overwhelming 93.3% of patients exhibited extremely severe short stature with the height SDS below −3. A majority of these patients presented with disproportionate body proportions (80.0%), characterized by a larger trunk-to-limb ratio. Key facial features included a prominent forehead (82.2%) and midfacial hypoplasia (100%) (Table 1).

Radiologically, metaphyseal striations, a diagnostic finding of the disease, was present in 81.4% of these cases. We collected the minimum age at which metaphyseal striations were observed for each patient and the maximum age at which they were not detected. On this basis, two subgroups were established based on age for comparison. It revealed that patients without detectable striations were generally younger (Fig. 4). However, even in the absence of striations, young patients still exhibited other significant metaphyseal abnormalities, such as dense bands. Vertebral deformity was found in all cases (100%). In addition, spinal deformities primarily included scoliosis and excessive lumbar hyperlordosis, occurring in 40.9% and 38.6% of patients, respectively (Table 1).

This study reported a case of SPONASTRIME dysplasia caused by two novel TONSL variants. The patient presented with typical clinical symptoms and imaging features. He had severely short, asymmetrical stature and midfacial dysplasia, along with lumbar hyperlordosis, limited elbow extension, and valgus knees. Imaging features of this patient included flat and biconvex vertebrae, metaphyseal striations, hip dysplasia, and progressive scoliosis that increased with age. The patient was also detected with neutropenia. All of these are consistent with the multi-system abnormalities caused by the functional defects of the TONSL protein. Genetic sequencing identified compound heterozygous mutations of c.1289del (p.Gln430ArgfsTer13) and c.1961G > C (p.Arg654Pro). The c.1289del variant was predicted to be likely pathogenic, while the potential pathogenicity of the c.1961G > C variant was confirmed by bioinformatic and functional analyses. Moreover, the patient was given GH treatment with unsatisfactory outcome.


TONSL mutation was the cause of SPONASTRIME dysplasia. As of July 2024, the HGMD recorded 54 mutations in TONSL, 38 of which were associated with SPONASTRIME dysplasia. These mutations included 26 missense/nonsense mutations, 1 splice mutation, 2 small deletions, 3 small insertions, 5 small insertions/deletions, and 1 large deletion. These mutations were distributed throughout the protein without significant hotspot regions.

The TONSL protein, consisting of 1378 amino acids, includes eight tetratricopeptide repeats (TPR), three ankyrin repeats (ANK), one ubiquitin-like domain (UBL), and seven leucine-rich repeats (LRR) [7] (Fig. 2g). In functional analysis of the c.1961G > C (p.Arg654Pro) variant, cells transfected with the mutant plasmid showed significantly lower TONSL protein levels compared to those with wild-type plasmid, with no difference in mRNA levels. While western blot analysis confirmed reduced p.Arg654Pro protein expression, we acknowledge this does not directly measure DNA repair activity. However, prior studies demonstrate that TONSL mutations causing SPONASTRIME dysplasia disrupt RAD51 foci formation and replication fork stability [3, 6], processes requiring intact TONSL protein levels. The observed reduction in p.Arg654Pro expression (Fig. 2e) is therefore biologically consistent with impaired DNA repair, as rescue of these defects requires functional TONSL protein [28, 29]. This suggests that the mutation may destabilize the protein structure. The structural model of TONSL predicted that the c.1961G > C (p.Arg654Pro) variant would disrupt seven side-chain hydrogen bonds, potentially altering the protein’s charge, size, and antigenicity, which could affect its migration rate. Therefore, the above results may be explained by the altered migration rate, where TONSL protein levels were significantly lower in cells transfected with the mutant plasmid, even resembling those in cells transfected with an empty vector.

SPONASTRIME dysplasia, a type of spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia, is primarily diagnosed based on vertebral body deformities and metaphyseal striations, which are the most specific and critical diagnostic features. As early as 1996, Langer noted that all SPONASTRIME dysplasia patients presented with vertebral shape abnormalities, while metaphyseal striations might not be evident in younger individuals and typically developed with age [12]. Similarly, our literature review indicated a younger age at which metaphyseal striations were not apparent; however, this manifestation would still be observed in some younger patients. Despite the lack of metaphyseal striations, these younger individuals were also observed with abnormal changes such as dense bands in their metaphyses, highlighting the need for thorough and continuous evaluation of metaphyseal morphology. Besides, the condition cannot be overlooked clinically simply considering the absence of metaphyseal striations.

SPONASTRIME dysplasia patients are usually discovered with heterogeneous clinical manifestations and disease severity. In addition to the typical skeletal changes, some cases presented with abnormalities beyond skeletal system, such as severe microcephaly, congenital aphakia, tracheal stenosis, hypogammaglobulinemia, and congenital neutropenia [3, 4]. Neutrophils are essential for the immune response, and neutropenia may increase the frequency and severity of infections [30]. The patient we reported experienced respiratory infections approximately once a month, which may be related to neutropenia. Neutropenia is rare in patients with SPONASTRIME developmental disorder. Among individuals with known TONSL biallelic mutations, six cases of neutropenia have been reported (including a pair of monozygotic twins), involving five distinct TONSL biallelic mutation types [3, 27]. Burrage et al. [3] also observed neutropenia in tonsl−/− zebrafish embryos. All these data suggest potential associations of neutropenia with DNA replication and repair defects in the immune hematopoietic system caused by TONSL mutations, possibly involving specific mutation sites. It highlights the importance of neutrophil count assessment during the diagnosis and treatment of SPONASTRIME developmental disorder. It may play a critical role in clinical management and is valuable for further understanding of the relationship between neutropenia and TONSL mutations.

The patient in this case report was provided with GH therapy for six months, without significant effect. Skeletal growth in children is primarily regulated by the growth plate, which is influenced by endocrine signals such as GH. TONSL plays a crucial role in DNA replication and stability. Skeletal dysplasia caused by TONSL mutations may be associated with abnormal proliferation and differentiation of chondrocytes in the growth plate [31]. Therefore, GH therapy may partially compensate for defective growth plate function. In certain spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasias, such as ACAN-related spondyloepimetaphyseal dysplasia, patients exhibit a positive response to GH therapy even in the absence of GH deficiency [32]. Thus, despite no deficiency in the patient considering the peak GH level of 13.1 ng/ml in stimulation test (Table 2), the parents requested a trial of GH therapy. Following thorough communication and evaluation, we decided to proceed with a trial period while closely monitoring the patient. Currently, there is no effective treatment for SPONASTRIME, and our case report contributes to the existing literature by documenting the real-world outcomes of growth hormone use in patients without growth hormone deficiency.

Among the 45 patients summarized in our literature review, 8 cases received GH treatment [6, 12, 13]. The median age of these patients was 5 years and 2 months, with an average age of 6 years and 5 months. Post-treatment growth velocity ranged from no significant improvement to slightly above average levels, yet without the achievement of catch-up growth. Overall, the treatment did not significantly promote growth (Table 2). Of the 8 patients treated with GH, 6 cases exhibited GH deficiency (Table 2). At this stage, there was still a poor understanding of the relationship between TONSL mutations and GH deficiency. The 6 cases of GH deficiency were observed with no abnormalities in thyroid, adrenal, or sex hormones, nor any pituitary abnormalities. Additionally, clinical diagnosis of GH deficiency requires a comprehensive approach, including growth assessment, GH-IGF-1 axis evaluation, and imaging studies. Due to the lack of a gold standard, all tests are supportive and prone to false positives or negatives [33]. Thus, further investigation is needed to determine whether SPONASTRIME dysplasia leads to GH deficiency and to uncover its underlying etiology. Furthermore, GH treatment outcomes differed significantly between Cases 6 and 7, who were siblings with the same TONSL gene mutations and started treatment at nearly the same age. However, Case 6 showed a markedly better response than Case 7 [13] (Table 2). Therefore, additional factors other than the mutation may influence the efficacy of GH therapy, warranting further research.

In summary, this report described a case of SPONASTRIME dysplasia with comprehensive clinical evaluation and imaging findings. Two previously unreported TONSL variants were discovered by genetic sequencing, and their pathogenicity were confirmed through bioinformatics analysis and plasmid studies, thus expanding the genetic spectrum of the disease. This study also reported the application of GH treatment in this case, and summarizes outcomes for eight patients with TONSL mutations via literature review. In fact, this study has certain limitations. Future research should directly evaluate the functions of the TONSL protein in vivo and in vitro, including its specific mechanism of action in the process of DNA replication and repair.

In conclusion, this study reports a case of SPONASTRIME dysplasia in a child caused by TONSL gene variation, expanding the genetic variation spectrum of SPONASTRIME dysplasia and providing a theoretical basis for the design of new therapeutic targets. Furthermore, GH appears to exhibit clinical heterogeneity in treating SPONASTRIME dysplasia, with no evidence of catch-up growth.

Prior to initiation, the risks (e.g. glucose dysregulation, hypothyroidism, accelerated bone age closure) and benefits of GH therapy were discussed extensively with the family, including literature-derived growth projections [6]. Informed consent emphasized the exploratory nature of the intervention and lack of guaranteed benefit. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College, Tongji Hospital, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (No. TJ-IRB20180703).

The test was performed using intravenous insulin (0.1 units/kg) combined with clonidine (5 μg/kg). Blood samples were taken for GH and glucose measurements at baseline and 15, 30, 60, and 90 min after administration, with GH peak < 10 ng/ml indicating GH deficiency.

The patient was followed up every 3 months during GH treatment. At each follow-up visit, the patient was subjected to the monitoring of height and weight, as well as assessment of serum Insulin-like Growth Factor 1 (IGF-1) levels, fasting glucose, insulin levels, and thyroid function.

After obtaining informed consent, peripheral blood was collected from the patient and his parents using EDTA-containing tubes. Genomic DNA was extracted with the Qiagen DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Shanghai, China) and DNA libraries were prepared using the library preparation kit (MyGenostics, Beijing, China). The interrupted DNA libraries were captured and enriched using the SPONASTRIME dysplasia-related Gene Capture Kit (MyGenostics, Beijing, China). Sequencing was performed using the DNBSEQ-T7 sequencing platform. Then, primers were designed based on the suspected pathogenic sites obtained from the target sequence capture sequencing, and the target sequences of the children and their parents were verified by Sanger sequencing. Finally, the sequencing results were analyzed using DNASTAR (Madison, WI, USA). Raw data were saved in FASTQ format for further bioinformatics analysis.

After quality control, clean reads were mapped to the UCS hg19 human reference genome using Sentieon. Duplicates were removed and base corrections were made. SNP and InDel variants were detected using Sentieon’s parameter-driven variant calling. The data were then converted to VCF format; and variants were further annotated using ANNOVAR software.

Variants were cross-referenced with multiple databases, including the 1000 Genomes Project, ESP6500, dbSNP, ExAC, an internal database (MyGenostics), Clinical Variants (ClinVar), the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD) and Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD). REVEL, SIFT, PolyPhen_2, MutationTaster, and GERP+ were used to predict the harm of variants. Clustal Omega was used to perform multi-species sequence alignment analysis of mutated amino acids. AlphaFold was used to predict the 3D structure of the mutant protein and the hydrogen bond distribution. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria was referenced for determining the classification of variants to identify the pathogenicity or otherwise [34].

The full-length cDNA of the human TONSL gene (NM_013433.5) was cloned into the FLAG-tagged PCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-copGFP-T2A-Puro vector. Point mutations were introduced into TONSL using PCR-based mutagenesis to construct the mutant TONSL (C.1961G > C). This vector was designed to independently express GFP to assess plasmid transfection efficiency and to serve as an internal reference protein for qualitative assessment of TONSL protein expression. Additionally, empty vector was designed as a control for comparison with the wild-type and mutant genes. DNA sequencing was employed for validation prior to functional analysis. Human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin in an incubator humidified with 5% CO2 at 37°C. Plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and cells were harvested 24 h later.

Total RNA extraction from transfected cells was completed using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Tokyo, Japan). The extracted RNA was quantified by NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). cDNA synthesis was carried out using PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan). Amplification was performed on the CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using TaKaRa Ex Taq PCR Kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan). The data were analyzed using the 2 − ΔΔCt method, with normalization to GFP expression. qPCR primers for GFP and TONSL are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

HEK293T cells washed with PBS were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer for the extraction of total protein. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected for the measurement of protein concentration. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, which were then blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 2 h. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, washed, and then incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, protein bands were detected using a chemiluminescence system (ChemiDox XRS+, Bio-Rad, CA, USA) and analyzed with Image Lab software. Specific details of the primary antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Literature search was performed with keyword ‘SPONASTRIME’ on PubMed and the Wanfang Data Knowledge Service Platform until October 2024. This study included case series and case reports related to SPONASTRIME dysplasia. We excluded irrelevant articles, reviews, duplicates, and those with inaccessible full texts (due to being from earlier years and not included in online databases). After inclusion, data analysis was conducted following the extraction and summary of the basic information, clinical features, and GH treatment details from the selected cases.

Results were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was assessed with Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons and one-way ANOVA for comparisons involving more than two groups in GraphPad Prism version 8. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered significant.
