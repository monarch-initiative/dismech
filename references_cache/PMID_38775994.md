# Identification of a novel CFAP61 homozygous splicing variant associated with multiple morphological abnormalities of the flagella.
**Authors:** Barbotin AL, Boursier A, Jourdain AS, Moerman A, Rabat B, Chehimi M, Thuillier C, Ghoumid J, Smol T
**Journal:** J Assist Reprod Genet (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10815-024-03139-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10815-024-03139-0)

## Content

In this study, we investigated the role of a newly identified homozygous variant (c.1245 + 6T > C) in the CFAP61 gene in the development of multiple morphologically abnormal flagella (MMAF) in an infertile patient. Using exome sequencing, we identified this variant, which led to exon 12 skipping and the production of a truncated CFAP61 protein. Transmission electron microscopy analysis of the patient's spermatozoa revealed various flagellar abnormalities, including defective nuclear chromatin condensation, axoneme disorganization, and mitochondria embedded in residual cytoplasmic droplets. Despite a fertilization rate of 83.3% through ICSI, there was no successful pregnancy due to poor embryo quality.Our findings suggest a link between the identified CFAP61 variant and MMAF, indicating potential disruption in radial spokes' assembly or function crucial for normal ciliary motility. Furthermore, nearly half of the observed sperm heads displayed chromatin condensation defects, possibly contributing to the low blastulation rate. This case underscores the significance of genetic counseling and testing, particularly for couples dealing with infertility and MMAF. Early identification of such genetic variants can guide appropriate interventions and improve reproductive outcomes.

Keywords: CFAP61, MMAF, Exome sequencing, Genetics of male infertility

Multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagellum (MMAF) represent a complex array of morphological defects in sperm flagella, including absent, short, irregular, bent, or coiled structures. Recent studies have highlighted a significant genetic component to MMAF, with pathogenic variants identified in over 42 genes, accounting for 35–60% of affected patients [1, 2].

The axoneme, consisting of 9 peripheral doublets surrounding a central pair, forms the axial skeleton of the flagellum and is essential for sperm motility. Dynein arms, both external and internal, located on peripheral doublet, play a pivotal role in hydrolyzing ATP provided by the mitochondria, and contribute to sperm motility through coordination with radial spokes surrounding the central pair. The cilia- and flagella-associated protein (CFAP) family emerges as critical for human and mouse sperm flagellum formation. Specifically, CFAP61 plays a role in axoneme stabilization by interacting with the calmodulin and radial spoke-associated complex (CSC), as well as the heads and stalks forming the radial spokes [3, 4]. Pathogenic variants of the CFAP61 gene have been identified in patients with asthenoteratozoospermia and the MMAF phenotype [3–5].

We investigated the genetic basis of the MMAF phenotype in an infertile patient and identified a novel CFAP61 biallelic variant, expanding the mutational spectrum of the CFAP61 phenotype.

The infertile patient, born to first cousins, had normal erectile and ejaculatory functions and no symptoms of primary ciliary dyskinesia. Clinical examination revealed normal bilateral testicular sizes, external genitalia development, and secondary sexual features. Urogenital ultrasound was performed, and the right and left testis volumes were measured to be 15 and 12 cc, respectively, and the integrity of the epididymis and vas deferens was confirmed. The absence of cystic pathology at the insertion of the ejaculatory duct was also confirmed.

The patient had a normal 46,XY karyotype, and no Y-chromosomal microdeletions by AZF region analysis. Basal hormone levels, including total testosterone, FSH, and inhibin B, were within normal ranges. A semen analysis was performed following current WHO guidelines. except for sperm morphology, assessed on Shorr-stained smears using David’s modified criteria [6].

Genomic DNA was extracted and purified from peripheral blood using a Qiagen Mini Blood Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples were prepared using Illumina DNA Prep Enrichment (Illumina, San Diego, US-CA). 150 bp paired-end exome sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform and analyzed using GATK best practices, with alignment onto the Genome Reference Consortium human genome build 38. Variant annotation was performed using Variant Effect Predictor [7]. The cDNA coordinates followed MANE transcript NM_015585.4 of CFAP61. The variant was classified according to ACMG recommendations [8]. Specific PCR primers were designed to target the candidate CFAP61 variant. Designed primers are available upon request.

Wild-type and CFAP61 mutant variant splicing patterns were compared using pcDNA3.1 splicing reporter minigene vector [9]. Genomic fragments containing exon 12 and flanking intronic sequences were amplified from wild-type and mutant genomic DNA, and inserted into vectors, producing pcDNA3.1-CFAP61 hybrid minigenes. The wild-type and mutant minigenes were transfected into HeLa cells using Effecten Transfection Reagent (Qiagen) and harvested 24 h post-transfection. mRNA was isolated using Nucleospin RNA (Macherey Nagel). cDNA synthesis was performed using SuperScript VILO (ThermoFisher). The resulting cDNA from wild-type and mutant conditions was visualized on agarose gels, and splicing changes were identified by direct sequencing. All designed primers are available upon request.

Predictions of splicing effect for near-coding variants were performed using SpliceAI, a deep learning tool that produces prediction scores and putative localizations of the effects on predetermined analysis windows [10]. Protein consequences based on plasmid sequences were performed using the Expasy online tool [11].

Semen sample was fixed with glutaraldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, St-Louis, US-MO) in phosphate buffer, washed for 15 min in fresh buffer containing 4% sucrose, and embedded in 2% agar. Post-fixation was performed in 1% w/v osmic acid in phosphate buffer for 1 h. After dehydration in a graded series of ethanol, small agar pieces containing spermatozoa were embedded in Epon resin (Polysciences, Hirschberg-an-der-Bergstraße, Germany). Sections were cut using a diamond knife on a Reichert OmU2 ultramicrotome (Reichert-Jung AG). Nickel grids were used to collect 70 nm sections, stained with uranyl acetate (2% in 70% ethanol) and Reynolds lead citrate, and examined with a Zeiss transmission electron microscope 902 (Leo). Images were captured with a Gatan Orius SC1000 CCD camera (Gatan France).

Controlled ovarian hyperstimulation was performed as described [12]. On the day of intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), a semen sample was collected and processed using a density gradient technique (Puresperm, JCD, la Mulatière, France). ICSI was performed by preferentially selecting progressive motile spermatozoa or, alternatively, non-progressive motile spermatozoa. Fertilization was assessed 16–18 h post-injection, and normally fertilized oocytes were cultured until day 5. Blastocyst quality was assessed before transfer, which was performed under ultrasound guidance.

Semen analysis revealed oligoasthenoteratozoospermia with severe asthenozoospermia despite normal vitality (Table 1). Sperm morphology analysis revealed teratozoospermia with numerous flagellar defects. Transmission electron microscopy analysis of spermatozoa revealed defective nuclear chromatin condensation in half of the heads (Fig. 1). Severe axoneme disorganization was observed, with the absence of one or more peripheral doublets in 40% of the cross-sections examined (20/50), and the absence of the central pair (“9 + 0”) associated with missing radial bridges or supernumerary outer microtubule doublets in 10% (5/50) of the cross-sections (Fig. 1A–C). This analysis suggested a flagellar elongation defect, with mitochondria embedded in cytoplasmic droplets in most of the observed longitudinal midpiece sections. Midpiece defects included a shortened and misshapen mitochondrial sheath (Fig. 1D, E) and excess residual cytoplasm with nuclear membrane accumulation (Fig. 1F). Both arms of dynein were present (Fig. 1G).

We identified a homozygous splice variant NM_015585.4:c.1245 + 6T > C located in the intron 12 of the CFAP61 gene, close to the canonical donor splice site of the exon 12 (Fig. 2A). The variant has been described in the gnomAD database at heterozygous state (AF = 6.56e−6 ; AC = 1) [13]. In silico prediction of the splicing effect was performed using SpliceAI, which indicated the elimination of the consensus donor splice site of exon 12 (Fig. 2B).

Post-transfections, RT-PCR analysis of the minigenes revealed that the wild-type minigene produced the expected single 320 bp fragment, whereas the mutant minigene produced a single 280 bp fragment (Fig. 2C). Sequencing analysis of both conditions revealed that the larger fragment contained the exon 12, whereas the smaller fragment in the mutant minigene lacked exon 12 (Fig. 2D). The c.1245 + 6T > C variant was predicted to induce a splicing change, leading to exon 12 skipping, resulting in a frameshift p.(Ser403Ilefs*5).

The couple underwent one ICSI attempt at our clinic, detailed in Table 2. The woman’s infertility evaluation revealed polycystic ovarian syndrome and an incidental finding of sarcoidosis, during the ICSI procedure. After controlled ovarian hyperstimulation, 21 oocytes were retrieved, with 18 in metaphase 2 suitable for ICSI. An 83.3% fertilization rate was observed. Unfortunately, the usable blastocyst rate was low, resulting in only two usable blastocysts (B3AB and B5AA). The low blastulation rate was attributed to poor embryo quality on day 3, with all cleaved embryos showing high fragmentation rates (> 30%). Due to the woman’s experiencing hyperstimulation ovary syndrome, fresh embryo transfer was not possible. Therefore, the two blastocysts were frozen for a subsequent frozen-thawed embryo transfer. The couple underwent two single frozen embryo transfers, but neither resulted in an ongoing pregnancy.

We report the identification of a novel homozygous variant in the CFAP61 gene, c.1245 + 6T > C, causing MMAF. This variant induces exon skipping, resulting in a frameshift transcript. Eight variants in CFAP61 have been reported causing MMAF in homozygous or compound heterozygous states suggesting an autosomal recessive mode of transmission as observed in our study (Fig. 2H) [4, 5, 14].

The CFAP61 gene, which is highly expressed in testes, muscle, and heart, encodes a member of the CSC, consisting of CFAP61, CFAP91, and CFAP251, which is essential for the stability and assembly of radial spokes RS3, thereby contributing to ciliary motility [4, 15]. CFAP61 is highly expressed in ciliated tissues and is localized along the full length of mature spermatozoa [3, 4]. According to the recommendation for the assessment of gene-disease associations, the gene-disease relationship of CFAP61, which was not classified in the 2020 update of validated monogenic causes of human male infertility, would be considered strong, with an upper score of 13 (Supplemental Table 1) [16, 17].

None of the reported pathogenic variants has been previously described in the homozygous state in large genetic variation databases such as gnomAD [13]. Of the 8 reported variants causing MMAF, five are frameshift, nonsense, or splice variants, resulting in a truncated protein lacking the FAD/NAD(P)-binding domain or causing complete protein absence (Fig. 2E) [4, 5, 14]. The three previously described missense variants are predicted to alter the CFAP61 protein structure, potentially destabilizing it or increasing its solubility, suggesting a putative loss-of-function effect similar to truncating variants [5].

Studies have investigated the relationship between CFAP61 pathogenic variants and the specific morphological features of MMAF. Ultrastructural analysis of spermatozoa has revealed a severely disorganized axoneme structure, abnormal mitochondrial sheath, and cytoplasmic excess residues containing unassembled flagellar components [4, 5, 14]. These features, also observed in our patient, reinforce the role of CFAP61 in the CSC. Functional evaluations of CFAP61 pathogenic variants have shown both the absence of the full-length CFAP61 protein and a complete absence of the CFAP251 protein in flagella, underscoring the critical role of CFAP61 in the CSC [14].

We observed an abnormal mitochondrial sheath in the ultrastructural examination of our patient’s flagellar, consistent with findings in a CFAP61 knockout mouse model displaying mitochondrial hypertrophy and hyperplasia [3]. However, mitochondria disorganization has not been consistently reported in humans with CFAP61 mutations. The homozygous truncating variants c.451_452del and c.847 C > T, located in or near the N-terminal domain, did not show obvious defects in the mitochondria [14]. In contrast, compound heterozygous variants c.[1654 C > T]; [2911G > C] have been associated with mitochondrial sheath malformation [5]. Despite the unclear association between CFAP61 and mitochondrial formation, immunofluorescence analysis of flagellum from patients with pathogenic CFAP251 variants revealed that CFAP251 loss alters mitochondrial sheath formation [18].

Defects in other CSC genes have been described in patients with MMAF. Our patient showed defects like those seen in patients carrying pathogenic variants in other CSC members. Biallelic pathogenic CFAP91 variants have been reported in MMAF patients with severe defects in the central pair complex and radial spokes, resulting in severe flagellar malformations and primary male infertility [19]. Alterations of CFAP251, involved in the assembly and function of motile cilia, have been associated with asthenozoospermia and MMAF [20, 21].

The management of patients with severe /MMAF remains challenging. Here, despite a ICSI procedure, pregnancy did not occur due to poor embryo quality at cleavage stage. Conversely, success was previously reported in a couple with CFAP61 pathogenic variants and oligoasthenoteratozoospermia, suggesting that ICSI should be considered for patients with CFAP61 variants [5]. Additionally, successful ICSI has been proposed for patients with pathogenic variants in other CSC genes, such as CFAP251 [21].

Our report of a novel homozygous CFAP61 variant in a patient with MMAF underscores the importance of genetic studies in MMAF and confirms that CFAP61 alterations are involved in /MMAF. The detection of pathogenic variants in components of the calmodulin- and spoke-associated complex of cilia provides important insights for genetic and reproductive counseling of patients with MMAF.
