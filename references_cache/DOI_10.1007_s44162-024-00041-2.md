# Cystinosis — a review of disease pathogenesis, management, and future treatment options
**Authors:** Lauren Devitt
**Journal:** Journal of Rare Diseases (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s44162-024-00041-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44162-024-00041-2)

## Content

AbstractCystinosis is a rare autosomal recessive disease characterised by an accumulation of cystine in the lysosomes. It is caused by pathogenic variants of the cystinosin gene (CTNS), which interrupts the transport of cystine from the lysosomes into the cytosol. Intra-lysosomal cystine accumulation leads to subsequent cellular dysfunction. Cystinosis has an incidence of 0.5–1/100,000 live births. There are three forms of cystinosis: nephropathic cystinosis, juvenile cystinosis, and ocular cystinosis, with nephropathic cystinosis being the most prevalent disease subtype. Renal impairment is the most common manifestation of disease. Extrarenal manifestations of cystinosis include hypothyroidism, diabetes, and hypogonadism. The current treatment for cystinosis is cysteamine, a cystine-depleting agent. This is not a curative treatment and only aims to slow the progression of disease. A total of 90% of cystinosis patients progress to kidney failure within the first 20 years of life. Kidney transplantation is the only option available to patients once the disease has progressed to this stage. This review highlights the pathogenesis and clinical manifestations of cystinosis, as well as potential future treatment options.

Cystinosis
Cystinosis is a rare monogenic autosomal recessive dis -
ease. It is classified as a rare disease, with a worldwide 
incidence of 0.5–1/100,000 live births [31]. Cystinosis is 
a lysosomal storage disorder. It is caused by pathogenic 
variants of the CTNS gene which encodes for the cysti -
nosin protein that transports cystine from the lysosome 
to the cytoplasm [93]. The cystinosin protein is a lysoso -
mal seven-transmembrane protein which exports intra-
lysosomal cystine and is driven by a H+ electrochemical 
gradient [54].
The dysfunctional cystinosin protein results in the 
accumulation of cystine in lysosomes [93]. This promotes 
the production of pathogenic cystine crystals which can 
lead to downstream organ damage [53]. The organs most 
affected include the kidney, cornea, thyroid, liver, and 
spleen. Additionally, cystinosis affects bones (cystinosis-
associated metabolic bone disease), muscles, and periph -
eral nerves.  Kidney impairment is the most common 
manifestation of cystinosis [90]. The three subtypes of 
cystinosis include the following: nephropathic cystinosis, 
juvenile cystinosis, and ocular cystinosis.
The incidence of cystinosis is highest in the French-
Canadian region (Quebec) (1:62,500) and in North-West-
ern France (Brittany) (1:26,000) [9, 22]. Countries with 
high levels of consanguinity are also expected to have 
higher rates of disease, but there is a lack of data in these 
areas [50]. Furthermore, there is a lack of diagnostic facil-
ities and cystinosis awareness in developing countries, 
also possibly leading to falsely low incidence rate in these

Genetics
The gene encoding for the cystinosin transporter is the 
CTNS gene, located on chromosome 17p13 [93]. More 
than 140 pathogenic variants of the CTNS gene have 
been described since its discovery in 1998 [21]. The 
CTNS gene consists of 12 exons, with the first two being 
noncoding exons. A genotype-phenotype correlation 
has been described, with the majority of pathogenic 
variants resulting in nephropathic cystinosis, and only 
15 and 4 variants related to juvenile and ocular cystino -
sis respectively [21].
The pathogenic variants of cystinosis have a large geo -
graphical distribution. The most common cystinosis vari-
ant in North Europe and North America is a large 57-kb 
deletion. The first 9 exons and part of the  10th exon of the 
CTNS gene are involved in this deletion. Two upstream 
genes named CARKL and TRPV1 are also deleted in 
this variant [34]. This genetic variant is correlated with 
severe nephropathic cystinosis [85]. This 57-kb deletion 
is present in 50–70% of nephropathic cystinosis patients 
in North Europe and North America [32]. This variant is 
not as common in other parts of the world accounting 
for 17% and 0% of cystinosis patients in Italy and Turkey 
respectively [68, 92].
A total of 15% of cystinosis patients have a CTNS  non-
sense variant [85]. The most common nonsense variant is 
p.W138x (753G > A). This variant is responsible for driv -
ing the disease in 50% of cystinosis patients in the French-
Canadian population. It is thought to have originated in 
Ireland and moved to the French-Canadian region in the 
mid-1800s. Although this variant is most prevalent in the 
French-Canadian cystinosis population, it is also found 
at lower levels in Europe and North America [85]. The 
W138X variant results in a premature termination codon 
(PTC) located in exon 7 of the CTNS gene, producing a 
nonfunctional allele [69]. Other common variants preva -
lent in certain regions include the c.681G > A variant in 
Egypt [86] and the Middle East (Saudi Arabia, Turkey, 
and Iran) [2, 83, 92] and the c.971-12G > A variant in the 
black South African community [73].
Molecular pathogenesis of cystinosis
The dysfunctional cystinosin transporter leads to 
impaired cystine transport out of the lysosome, result -
ing in increased intra-lysosomal cystine levels [93]. 
Lysosomes play a key role in protein degradation, with 
cystinosis leading to disruptions in this process [75]. 
The use of small Rab GTPases which improve cellu -
lar function by upregulating lysosomal trafficking fur -
ther supports the role of dysfunctional lysosomes in the 
pathogenesis of cystinosis [52].
Increased lysosomal cystine causes multiple down -
stream effects which all play a role in the molecular 
pathogenesis of cystinosis. The pathogenesis of cys -
tinosis is multifactorial with proximal tubular epithelial 
cell (PTEC) and podocyte dysfunction, inflammation, 
autophagy, oxidative stress, altered cellular energy metab-
olism, modified calcium signalling, and apoptosis all con -
tributing to disease progression.
PTEC and podocyte dysfunction
PTEC and podocyte dysfunction are characterised in the 
pathogenesis of nephropathic cystinosis. Cystinosin is 
pivotal for the function of PTECs and podocytes, with 
ctns −/− zebrafish displaying partial podocyte disappear -
ance, slit membrane stenosis, and enlarged lysosomes in 
proximal renal tubular cells [27].
Cystinosis can cause podocyte injury resulting in mult -
inucleation (due to lack of cytokinesis) [14] and giant cell 
transformation [84]. Podocyte injury itself is likely to be 
multifactorial, resulting from changes to the cytoskel -
eton, multinucleation, and enhanced podocyte motil -
ity [46]. AKT kinase is demonstrated to lie at the centre 
of the complex signalling cascade governing podocyte 
motility and adhesion, with AKT kinase inhibition result-
ing in the normalisation of the hypermobile podocyte 
seen in cystinosis [47, 49].
The presence of podocytes and PTEC’s in the urine of 
cystinosis patients is increased in comparison to controls. 
However, no correlation between podocyturia and esti -
mated glomerular filtration rate, age, or proteinuria has 
been established [47, 49]. Podocytes in cystinosis exhibit 
a hypermobile phenotype with impaired cell adhesion 
sites and an altered cytoskeleton, promoting their loss 
in the urine [47, 49]. The loss of PTECs in the urine is 
explained in part by the abnormal trafficking of endo-lys-
osomal vesicles. This is attributed to a decrease in surface 
expression of multiligand receptors and delayed lysoso -
mal cargo processing [48].
Inflammation
Inflammation plays a large role in the pathogenesis of 
cystinosis. The NLRP2/3 inflammasome, galectin 3, and 
chitotriosidase enzyme are amongst the principle inflam -
matory mediators involved in this process.
The accumulation of cystine can lead to NLRP3 inflam-
masome activation, with cystinosis patients having 
higher serum levels of IL-1β and IL-18 in comparison to 
controls. These cytokines are secreted in a dose-depend -
ant manner in response to cystine crystal stimula -
tion [74]. The NLRP3 inflammasome is simultaneously 
involved in the production of reactive oxygen species, 
which independently contributes to the pathogenesis of 
cystinosis [74]. Additionally, NLRP2 is highly expressed

in cystinotic PTECs and plays an important proinflam -
matory and profibrotic role in cystinosis [79]. In addition 
to IL-1β and IL-18, IL-6 is also involved in this inflam -
matory process, with IL-6 acting as an independent pre -
dictor of leukocyte cystine levels [99]. Furthermore, IL-1 
inhibition using anakinra demonstrates the ability to 
attenuate cachexia in cystinosis patients [17].
There are increased levels of chitotriosidase enzyme 
activity in cystinosis [28]. Chitotriosidase is an enzyme 
involved in the degradation of chitin, which is expressed 
by activated macrophages, thus acting as a marker of 
macrophage activity [28]. Chitotriosidase activity is also 
elevated in other lysosomal disorders such as Gaucher 
disease [41]. Notably, a prospective multicentre study 
reported a significant correlation between chitotriosi -
dase activity and the number of extrarenal complications 
[99]. Chitotriosidase activity has the potential to serve as 
a novel biomarker for cystinosis severity and therapeutic 
monitoring [28, 99].
Galectin 3 is known to be involved in the inflammatory 
pathway of chronic kidney disease progression [64]. Galec-
tin 3 is overexpressed in Ctns − / − mice, with Ctns −/− 
Gal3 −/− mice demonstrating improved renal function 
and reduced macrophage infiltration into the kidney in 
comparison to Ctns −/− mice [64].
Autophagy and defective mTOR signalling
Aberrant lysosomal autophagy and mTOR signalling are 
also involved in the pathogenesis of cystinosis. mTORC1 
acts a nutrient sensor which is activated in the presence 
of amnio acids, triggering protein synthesis and inhibit -
ing autophagy. Thus, amnio acid starvation inactivates 
mTORC1 and activates autophagy. While baseline mTOR 
activity is not altered in cystinotic cells [71], studies sug -
gest a change in mTOR reactivation and subcellular 
localisation after starvation in cystinosis [3]. mTOR path-
way inhibition by everolimus activates autophagy and 
decreases apoptosis [45].
Transcription factor EB (TFEB) is involved in the 
regulation of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy [82]. 
While TFEB nuclear translocation is increased in cysti -
notic cells, levels of endogenous TFEB are reduced [76]. 
The decreased levels of TFEB cannot be normalised with 
cysteamine treatment [76]. TFEB upregulation has been 
shown to increase cystinosin expression, rescuing some 
of the lysosomal abnormalities in cystinosis [76, 107].
LAMP2A is the main receptor for chaperone-mediated 
autophagy (CMA). Decreased LAMP2A expression, mis -
localisation, and dysfunctional CMA has been observed 
in cystinosis mice fibroblasts [71]. Additionally, the fac -
tors tightly regulating LAMP2A trafficking and distribu -
tion such as Rab 11 and Rab 7 are downregulated and 
defective in cystinosis [107].
Oxidative stress
Altered glutathione metabolism and increased oxidative 
stress have been proposed to play a role in the pathogene-
sis of renal Fanconi syndrome in cystinosis [16]. Cystinotic 
cells have a reduced glutathione content and increased 
production of superoxide dismutase, promoting oxidative 
stress [19]. Additionally, elevated levels of cellular oxidative 
stress can been seen in urinary shed PTECs from cystino-
sis patients [102]. Studies suggest that cystinotic cells are 
more vulnerable to oxidative stress as a result of impaired 
glutathione synthesis and a compromised gamma glutamyl 
cycle, possibly due to altered mitochondrial function and 
altered ATP levels [67, 103, 104].
Altered cellular energy metabolism
Altered cellular energy homeostasis is seen in cys -
tinosis, with reduced intracellular ATP levels widely 
reported in cystinosis [61]. Studies suggest the intracel -
lular decrease in ATP may be attributed to a reduction 
in apical reabsorption of phosphate [89] or a decrease in 
cellular glutathione [61], as Na+ K+ ATPase pump activ-
ity is maintained [61, 89]. Furthermore, a conditionally 
immortalised PTEC line has demonstrated significantly 
reduced cAMP levels, mitochondrial potential, and activ-
ity of mitochondrial complex I and IV [5]. The presence 
of an energy imbalance is further supported by the acti -
vation of the cytosolic fuel sensor AMPK in rabbit renal 
proximal tubule cells with a cystinosin knockdown [88].
Calcium signalling
Altered calcium signalling has been postulated to play 
a role in cystinosis, as calcium signalling is disturbed 
in other lysosomal disorders such as Gaucher and Nie -
mann-Pick type C disease [56]. Additionally, the calcium 
sensor UNC13D has been demonstrated to regulate 
endolysosomal trafficking and autophagy in a cellular 
model of cystinosis [105, 106]. Although no major dys -
regulation of intracellular calcium dynamics have been 
found to be disturbed in cystinosis, ATP-induced cal -
cium release appears slightly sensitised in cystinosis cells 
in comparison to control [47, 49]. A link between lysoso -
mal calcium signalling and autophagy has been demon -
strated as lysosomal calcium release causes downstream 
activation of calcineurin and dephosphorylation of TFEB, 
resulting in the expression of lysosomal and autophagy 
genes [70]. This may have a potential link to the role 
autophagy plays in the pathogenesis of cystinosis.
Increased apoptosis
Increased rates of apoptosis have been observed in the 
PTECs of cystinosis patients [80]. Apoptosis is likely to 
be a common endpoint of disease which results in ‘swan 
neck lesions’ (SNL), rather than the primary driver of

disease itself [16]. SNL describe the apoptotic cell death 
and tubular atrophy seen in the proximal tubule of indi -
viduals with cystinosis. SNL are a result of the shedding 
of individual proximal tubule cells through apoptosis, 
atrophy from dedifferentiation, and the sliding meta -
plasia hypothesis which involves the replacement of 
dead PTECs by the flat epithelium of the Bowman’s 
capsule [18]. SNLs can be seen in children as young 
as 6–12  months [66]. Mitochondrial target antioxi -
dants have been shown to delay SNL in Ctns −/− mice 
strengthening the link between apoptosis and oxidative 
stress [39].
Ctns −/− mice are initially capable of adapting to SNL 
and maintaining nephron integrity through the loss of 
mitochondria, thickening of the basement membrane, 
and upregulation of transgelin. However, this adaption 
ultimately fails in adulthood, leading to proximal tubular 
disruption, atubular glomeruli, and kidney failure [39].
Clinical manifestation
There are three clinical forms of cystinosis: nephro -
pathic cystinosis, juvenile cystinosis, and ocular cys -
tinosis. They are characterised by the severity of kidney 
involvement and the age of disease onset. Nephropathic 
cystinosis (also known as infantile nephropathic cystino -
sis) is the most common form of cystinosis, accounting 
for up to 95% of cases [30]. It is the most severe form of 
disease, usually leading to renal Fanconi syndrome and 
kidney failure within the first decade of life without treat-
ment [37]. Juvenile cystinosis (also known as late-onset 
cystinosis) accounts for 5% of cases. Juvenile cystinosis 
normally develops between the ages of 10 and 12. It is 
considered less severe than nephropathic cystinosis with 
individuals likely to develop a milder form of renal Fan -
coni syndrome, as well as having pronounced glomerular 
involvement and proteinuria [30]. Ocular cystinosis is the 
non-nephropathic form of cystinosis. This usually pre -
sents as cystine accumulations within the cornea and is 
rarely diagnosed before adulthood [37, 90].
Children with nephropathic cystinosis begin showing 
symptoms of renal Fanconi syndrome at 1  year of age. 
This includes polyuria, polydipsia, dehydration, vomit -
ing, failure to thrive, growth retardation, rickets, and 
constipation. Laboratory findings may reveal acidosis, 
hypokalemia, proteinuria, hypophosphatemia, and ami -
noaciduria [90]. Although these symptoms appear at 
1 year of age, physical alterations in the kidney structure 
do not manifest until the second year of life. This indi -
cates a possible window of opportunity to provide future 
disease-curing therapy before irreversible physical altera-
tions occur in the proximal tubules of the kidney [13, 
16]. When patients progress to stage 5 kidney failure, the 
most appropriate treatment is kidney transplantation. A 
large European cohort study showed that kidney trans -
plantation in nephropathic cystinosis patients is associ -
ated with improved patient survival and improved renal 
function [97].
There are many extrarenal consequences of cystino -
sis. Endocrine complications include retarded growth, 
hypothyroidism, and diabetes in later life [90]. Cystinosis 
metabolic bone disease is related to the kidney impair -
ments associated with cystinosis. Dysfunctional bone 
metabolism predisposes patients to hypophosphatemic 
rickets and chronic kidney disease-mineral and bone dis -
order (CKD-MBD), which can result in skeletal deformi -
ties such as scoliosis and short stature [42]. CKD-MBD 
is defined as a systemic disorder of bone and mineral 
metabolism as a consequence of chronic kidney disease. 
It is characterised by one or a combination of abnormal 
calcium, phosphorus, parathyroid hormone or vitamin 
D metabolism, abnormalities in bone turnover and min -
eralisation, or abnormalities in vascular or soft tissue 
calcification [101]. A 20-year longitudinal study showed 
that early treatment with cysteamine improves the linear 
growth of cystinosis patients [40].
Ocular cystinosis presents as cystine crystal deposits in 
the cornea. These cystine crystals are diagnostic of cys -
tinosis and can be seen in the cornea after 16 months of 
age [36]. Without treatment, progressive retinopathy and 
band keratopathy can occur in later life [30], with retin -
opathy leading to blindness in 10–15% of patients [36].
Diagnosis
There are three main diagnostic methods for cystino -
sis. This includes leukocyte cystine level measurement, 
genetic analysis, and the detection of corneal cystine 
crystals [91]. Leukocyte cystine level measurement is 
the gold standard. It is carried out by liquid chromatog -
raphy-tandem mass spectrometry or high-performance 
liquid chromatography [37]. Cystinosis is the most com -
mon cause of renal Fanconi syndrome during infancy 
and thus should always be considered as a differential 
diagnosis [30].
Treatment
The current treatment options for cystinosis are limited 
and are not curative. Early diagnosis and treatment are 
crucial to minimise renal damage and the progression of 
disease (Fig. 1) [34].
Cystinosis treatment can be split into lifestyle modifica-
tions, alleviation of symptoms, and cystine-targeted treat-
ment. Lifestyle modifications include avoiding prolonged 
sun and heat exposure to minimise the risk of heatstroke 
from impaired sweating [35], as well as ensuring free 
access to toilets and water due to the significant polyuria 
and polydipsia that patients experience [103, 104].

Symptomatic therapy is aimed mainly at controlling the 
side effects of renal impairment. This involves maintaining 
adequate fluid and electrolyte balance, healthy balanced 
nutrition, and prevention of rickets. The electrolytes of 
most importance are potassium, bicarbonate, and sodium, 
with these being monitored regularly [103, 104]. Due to 
the large loss of fluids and electrolytes from impaired 
renal function, the unrestricted intake of water and salt is 
essential [37]. Vitamin D supplementation is often given to 
patients as rickets prevention. 25(OH)D3 demonstrated an 
advantage over 1,25(OH)2D3 by preventing muscle atrophy 
and adipose tissue browning in an experimental study with 
Ctns −/− mice [108]. Furthermore, an experimental study 
has demonstrated the involvement of IL 1 in cystinosis 
metabolic bone disease, potentially giving a role to immu-
nosuppressive therapy in the cystinosis management [42].
Severe failure to thrive is commonly seen in paediatric 
patients [26]. Nasogastric tubes and gastrostomy tube 
feeding are recommended in patients unable to maintain 
healthy nutrition [31]. Furthermore, proton-pump inhib -
itors may be useful for those suffering with reflux and fre-
quent vomiting [25].
Growth retardation can be a clinical complication of 
cystinosis. Cysteamine treatment itself will improve stat -
ural growth [96], while growth hormone treatment can 
also be initiated improving final height [8, 40].
Less common cystinosis complications including dia -
betes, hypothyroidism, and hypogonadism are treated 
with insulin, levothyroxine, and testosterone respectively 
[103, 104].
Cystinosis progresses to kidney failure in 90% of 
patients before the age of 20 [20]. At this point, the only 
treatment options left are kidney transplant and dialysis 
[50]. Post-transplant, the donor kidney is unaffected by 
the original disease. Although kidney transplants have 
a favourable prognosis for cystinosis patients, it is not 
without risk [57]. Lifelong immunotherapy, surgery com-
plications, and major rehabilitation are amongst the chal-
lenges facing transplant recipients.
Oral delivery of cysteamine does not alter ocular cys -
tine levels [94]. Ocular cystinosis is managed by topical 
0.5% cysteamine eye drops, used 10–12 times a day. Topi-
cal cysteamine has been proven to significantly relieve 
eye pain and photophobia, improving quality of life [95]. 
Multiple studies have investigated the use of sustained 
release cysteamine eyedrops, improving drug stability, 
allowing for a once daily dosing regimen, and reduc -
ing disease burden for patients [51, 78]. Additionally, a 
drugless ocular cystinosis treatment consisting of gold 
nanoparticle contact lenses has been synthesised and 
tested in  vitro, with the gold nanoparticles withdrawing 
cystine from the local area [63]. Patients not treated with

Cysteamine
Cysteamine is the mainstay of treatment for cystino -
sis patients. Cysteamine is a cystine-depleting drug. 
Cysteamine lowers levels of intra-lysosomal cystine by 
driving a disulphide exchange reaction which results in 
the formation of cysteine and cystine-cysteamine mixed 
disulphide. This enables cystine in the form of cysteine-
cysteamine-mixed disulphide to leave the lysosome 
through a ‘system c’ transporter, and the remaining cys -
tine can leave through the cysteine carrier [103, 104]. 
Cysteamine improves overall prognosis and delays the 
progression to kidney failure, the need for kidney trans -
plantation, and the presence of extrarenal complications 
[34]. In the event of a patient receiving a kidney trans -
plant, cysteamine treatment should be continued to pre -
vent extrarenal organ damage [103, 104]. The main side 
effect of cysteamine is gastrointestinal disturbance and 
discomfort, which can be reduced by the use of proton 
pump inhibitors [25].
Cysteamine was originally taken four times daily, 
constantly interrupting the patient’s quality of life. The 
immediate release formulation of cysteamine has a short 
half-life, restricting the individual to a dosing schedule of 
once every 6 h, including throughout the night [103, 104].
Recent efforts have been made to prolong the half-life 
of cysteamine. A delayed release formulation of cysteam -
ine was approved for use in the USA in 2013 and in 
Europe in 2014 [4]. This allows twice daily dosing, every 
12  h [60]. Delayed release cysteamine is designed to 
bypass the stomach and prevent severe gastrointestinal 
symptoms, reducing the need for concurrent gastro-pro -
tection [31]. It has been shown to improve renal function, 
leukocyte cystine levels, and quality of life, in comparison 
to immediate release cysteamine [58, 96].
There have been efforts looking at a cysteamine pro-
drugs such as esterified gamma-glutamyl-cysteamine 
which can keep cysteamine levels above the threshold of 
efficacy for at least 24  h, which provides hope for once 
daily dosing [33]. More research is needed in looking at 
prodrugs/cysteamine analogues which would decrease 
the burden of disease for patients.
Early diagnosis and treatment are key to managing 
this disease [34]. A multicentre sibling cohort study by 
Veys et  al. identified presymptomatic treatment with 
cysteamine as beneficial, in comparison to treatment 
initiated at symptom onset [98]. Moreover, kidney trans -
plantation is required at a significantly later age when 
diagnosed younger than 18 months [72]. This highlights 
the importance of early diagnosis and treatment and 
strengthens the enrolment of cystinosis in new-born 
screening programmes.
While the management of cystinosis has improved 
over recent years, a large disparity still exists, with 7% of 
patients in the developing world dying at 5 years of age, 
in comparison to 0% in developed economies [7]. Nota -
bly, the availability of cysteamine differs greatly around 
the world, with delayed release cysteamine only being 
available for 7% vs 74% of patients in developing and 
developed economies respectively. Additionally, diagnos-
tic capabilities are reduced in third world countries, with 
genetic testing and intra-lysosomal cystine level meas -
urements available in 63% and 30% of developing econo -
mies respectively, while availability is 100% and 94% in 
developed economies [77]. Expectantly, developing coun-
tries have a younger mean age of morbidity and mortality 
[7]. This highlights a massive gap and need for improve -
ment in global access to diagnostic equipment and facili -
ties, as well as treatment.
Future treatment options
In recent years, there has been lots of research into devel-
oping a more effective treatment for cystinosis. There 
has been an interest in bone marrow and haematopoi -
etic stem/progenitor cell (HSPC) transplant, which have 
proved efficacious in Ctns −/− mice. The transplantation 
of syngeneic wild-type murine haematopoietic stem and 
progenitor cells in Ctns −/− mice resulted in up to 97% 
clearance of intra-lysosomal cystine, also reducing sys -
temic manifestations of the disease [87]. Following this, 
an allogenic HSPC transplantation was carried out on 
a 16-year-old male with cystinosis. However, unfortu -
nately, the patient died 35-month post-transplant due to 
graft-versus-host disease [29].
Later, a phase 1/2 clinical trial was developed for 
autologous HSPC transplantation [15], with autologous 
HSPC transplantation carrying reduced risks of graft-
versus-host disease and immune rejection. HSPCs are 
modified ex  vivo using a self-inactivated lentiviral vec -
tor to introduce wild-type CTNS  cDNA (product name: 
CTNS-RD-04). The results of this trial have not yet been 
released [15].
Furthermore, there has been research focusing on 
downstream molecular targets that may be beneficial in 
cystinosis. Such targets include mTOR and LAMP2A. 
Inhibition of mTOR/mTORC1 in preclinical models 
has demonstrated the ability to correct the downstream 
effects of intra-lysosomal cystine accumulation on cell 
differentiation and lysosomal function, highlighting 
an important potential therapeutic target [65]. mTOR 
inhibition in combination with cysteamine treat -
ment [45] and dietary protein restriction [6 ] represent

further options to improve cystinosis-driven cellular 
dysfunction. Chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) is 
also disrupted in cystinosis, and this represents a pos -
sible intervention point to improve disease. In  vitro 
activation of CMA promotes increased LAMP2A 
localisation, increasing murine Ctns −/− cell survival 
[107]. CMA upregulation has also been shown to res -
cue proximal tubule cell dysfunction in a CRISPR-Cas9 
developed CTNS KO human proximal tubule cell line 
[105, 106].
Genistein and luteolin, members of the flavonoid fam -
ily, carry potential therapeutic capabilities. In vitro stud -
ies demonstrated the ability of genistein (an isoflavone 
enriched in soy) to rescue the cystinotic cellular pheno -
type in a cystinosin independent pathway, through acti -
vation of transcription factor EB (TFEB) [76]. Further 
studies showed the ability of genistein to improve kidney 
disease in mouse models of nephropathic cystinosis [24]. 
Genistein also has an additional bone protective effect 
[24]. Additionally, luteolin has demonstrated the ability 
to target aspects of the disease insensitive to cysteamine 
treatment [23]. Taken together, this points towards the 
possibility of a combined genistein/luteolin and cysteam -
ine treatment targeting different pathways in the cystino-
sis pathogenesis for improved clinical benefit.
Targeting the inflammatory pathway is another point 
of interest. This includes anti-inflammatory agents 
which do not target the cyclooxygenase/prostaglandin 
pathway (this can promote further acute and chronic 
kidney injury), such as anakinra and N-acetyl-D-lac -
tosamine. The treatment of Ctns −/− mice with anak -
inra has resulted in attenuated muscle atrophy and 
promoted the browning of adipose tissue [17]. N-acetyl-
D-lactosamine inhibits the involvement of galectin 3 
in the inflammatory pathway. With galectin 3 being 
increased in cystinosis, this presents another interesting 
therapeutic target [64].
Translational readthrough drugs also represent a prom-
ising new treatment option. Brasell et  al. have shown 
that aminoglycosides and aminoglycoside derivatives are 
capable of inducing premature termination codon (PTC) 
readthrough [11, 12]. These studies showed a correlation 
between PTC readthrough and clinical benefit in cys -
tinosis murine models with the nonsense W138X vari -
ant. The modified aminoglycoside geneticin (G418) was 
shown to restore the full-length CTNS protein, decreas -
ing lysosomal cystine accumulation in patients with the 
W138X variant. However, the nephrotoxic and ototoxic 
effects of G418 are too severe and thus removes the abil -
ity for it to be used in humans [11].
In a similar study to above, Brasell et  al. use a modi -
fied aminoglycoside named ELX-02 to promote the pro -
duction of a functional CTNS protein. ELX-02 showed a 
more favourable safety profile with in vivo murine stud -
ies reporting no nephrotoxic or ototoxic effects. Addi -
tionally, no cytotoxicity was observed in in vitro human 
fibroblasts treated with ELX-02. ELX-02 administered to 
cystinotic mice and human fibroblasts with the W138X 
variant demonstrated the ability to permit readthrough 
of the PTC. The reduction in cystine accumulation sug -
gested the readthrough was successful, allowing for a 
fully functional CTNS protein to be produced. The cys -
tine reduction seen in this experiment was similar to that 
of pharmacological treatment with cysteamine. Com -
bination therapy showed a further reduction in cystine 
accumulation [12]. The results of the phase 1 trial of 
ELX-02 demonstrated a good safety profile for the drug 
with no serious or severe adverse effects reported [43]. 
However, the phase 2 study on ELX-02 was terminated in 
2020 due to study design limitations.
Importantly, these studies show the potential clinical 
benefit of aminoglycoside-induced PTC readthrough in 
nonsense cystinosis variants, by allowing the transcrip -
tion of a functional cystinosin transporter. Anticodon-
engineered suppressor tRNAs (ACE-tRNA) represent 
another way of targeting PTC, without the systemic side 
effects of antibiotics. ACE-tRNA’s are capable of driving 
mRNA readthrough and inhibiting nonsense-mediated 
mRNA decay, permitting the translation of a functional 
protein [1].
ACE-tRNA’s have an altered anticodon sequence spe -
cific for the nonsense variants. Endogenous aminoacyl 
tRNA synthetases supply the ACE-tRNA with the correct 
amino acid. This allows for full-length translation of the 
protein. While ACE-tRNA’s have mostly only been tested 
on yeasts and prokaryotes, recent studies by Ko et  al. 
open the possibility for ACE-tRNA use in humans [59]. 
Wang et  al. demonstrated that suppressor tRNAs in an 
AAV vector are safe and efficacious for up to 6  months 
after a single treatment (in a mouse model of a nonsense 
variant-mediated disease) [100]. Ko et  al. have demon -
strated ACE-tRNAs can work efficiently when delivered 
as cDNA or RNA, adeno-associated virus, or nanoparti -
cle delivery system [59].
For example, in the W138X nonsense cystinosis variant 
the ACE-tRNA should be modified to fit the premature 
termination codon TGA. This variant is the result of a 
one nucleotide change from TGG to TGA [85]. This leads 
to the TGG codon for tryptophan being substituted for a 
stop codon. ACE-tRNA tryp will be engineered to have 
an anticodon sequence to TGA, which is ACT.

Conclusion
Cystinosis is a rare autosomal recessive genetic disease 
which is driven by the intra-lysosomal accumulation of 
cystine. It has limited treatment options, none of which 
are curative. Treatment is focused on symptom reduc -
tion, delaying kidney failure, and slowing disease pro -
gression. Significant advancements have been made in 
the management of cystinosis, with most patients who 
are receiving cysteamine treatment now reaching adult -
hood. Early intervention and continued lifelong cysteam -
ine therapy are key to improved morbidity and mortality 
rates [44]. There is lots of exciting research going on in 
this field, with many new promising potential therapeutic 
options for cystinosis patients.
