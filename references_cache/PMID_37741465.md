# Therapeutic implications of impaired nuclear receptor function and dysregulated metabolism in Wilson's disease.

Copper is an essential trace element that is required for the activity of many 
enzymes and cellular processes, including energy homeostasis and 
neurotransmitter biosynthesis; however, excess copper accumulation results in 
significant cellular toxicity. The liver is the major organ for maintaining 
copper homeostasis. Inactivating mutations of the copper-transporting P-type 
ATPase, ATP7B, result in Wilson's disease, an autosomal recessive disorder that 
requires life-long medicinal therapy or liver transplantation. Current treatment 
protocols are limited to either sequestration of copper via chelation or 
reduction of copper absorption in the gut (zinc therapy). The goal of these 
strategies is to reduce free copper, redox stress, and cellular toxicity. 
Several lines of evidence in Wilson's disease animal models and patients have 
revealed altered hepatic metabolism and impaired hepatic nuclear receptor 
activity. Nuclear receptors are transcription factors that coordinate hepatic 
metabolism in normal and diseased livers, and several hepatic nuclear receptors 
have decreased activity in Wilson's disease and Atp7b-/- models. In this review, 
we summarize the basic physiology that underlies Wilson's disease pathology, 
Wilson's disease animal models, and the possibility of targeting nuclear 
receptor activity in Wilson's disease patients.

Wilson’s disease is an autosomal recessive disorder with an occurrence of approximately 1:30,000 [1], and over 500 mutations in the ATP7B gene have been identified in Wilson’s disease patients [2]. S.A. Wilson’s 1912 description of “progressive lenticular degeneration” was the seminal report to describe what is currently known as “Wilson’s disease” [3]. Wilson’s work described an array of neurological symptoms, including involuntary movements, tremors, muscle rigidity, dystonia, and spastic movement of the limbs and face [3]. Post-mortem analysis identified significant liver cirrhosis, which was surprising considering the age of patients (10 – 26 years old) and lack of hepatic symptoms [3].

Wilson’s disease is often difficult to diagnose due to the heterogenous presentation of symptoms. It is currently understood that Wilson’s disease can present with hepatic, neurological, or mixed (both hepatic and neurological) symptoms. The presentation of hepatic symptoms includes liver steatosis, cholestasis, jaundice, hepatitis, and liver failure [4, 5]. Neurological symptoms may also include sleep disorders, psychiatric disturbances, depression, and bipolar disorder [4]. In addition to genetic testing for ATP7B variants, diagnostic criteria include hepatic copper of >250 μg/g dry liver weight, low serum ceruloplasmin, increased urinary copper, and/or the presence of Kayser-Fleisher rings [6].

Copper homeostasis is predominately regulated in the liver. Dietary copper is absorbed in the duodenum, delivered to the liver by portal circulation, and transported into hepatocytes by the membrane copper transporter 1 (Ctr1) [5] (Figure 1). Cytosolic copper binds to chaperone proteins, such as ATOX1, and is transported into the trans-Golgi network via the transmembrane Cu-transporting P-type ATPase, ATP7b, and is incorporated into the ferroxidase, ceruloplasmin, which is secreted into blood [1]. Most circulating copper is bound to ceruloplasmin, with a lesser fraction bound to albumin [5]. In steady state or low hepatocellular copper conditions, ATP7b is localized to the trans-Golgi compartment and transports copper into the secretory pathway. Elevation of hepatocellular copper results in canalicular excretion of copper into bile, followed by fecal excretion, which is the primary route of copper elimination from the body [7, 8] (Figure 1). Loss of Atp7b function prevents translocation of copper into the trans-Golgi network and excretion into bile, leading to free copper concentrations that exceed the capacity of protective copper-binding proteins. Copper-mediated induction of reactive oxygen species and anti-oxidants including metallothionein and glutathione are initial events that follow excessive copper exposure [9, 10]. Continued copper exposure results in oxidative stress, inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction, as well as fibrosis, cirrhosis, and liver failure in Wilson’s disease patients [11].

Hepatocellular copper enrichment in the cytosol, nucleus, and mitochondria has been demonstrated in Wilson’s disease animal models and Wilson’s disease patients [12–17]. Mechanistic studies in Atp7b−/− mice demonstrated an increase in nuclear size and increased Ki-67 staining that preceded hepatocellular pathology, indicating that copper may drive hepatocellular proliferation [12]. It is well known that copper homeostasis is critical for activity of cytochrome c oxidase and mitochondria respiratory function [18]. Several reports in Wilson’s disease animal models [12–15] and Wilson’s disease patients [16] have described misshapen and dysfunctional mitochondria prior to the onset of Wilson’s disease symptoms. Mechanistic evidence has demonstrated decreased mitochondrial membrane fluidity and stability as well as decreased respiratory function and oxidative phosphorylation in Atp7b−/− animals and Wilson’s disease patients [19, 20]. Currently, it is unclear why these dramatic cellular organelle changes precede significant liver pathology. It is plausible that excess copper accumulation induces cytoprotective glutathione and metallothionein, which would initially provide a buffer against increased free intracellular copper.

Wilson’s disease patients and knockout animal models also display copper misbalance in extrahepatic tissues, which has been extensively reviewed elsewhere [5].

Several animal models have been developed for the study of Wilson’s disease. Although these models develop pathological levels of hepatic copper, species differences have been noted between the mouse and rat Wilson’s disease models. Additionally, canine models for copper toxicosis have been well-described in the literature. We will provide a basic overview of the most frequently utilized models.

Zebrafish exhibit a high degree of copper sensitivity. The impact of Atp7b−/− in zebrafish is dependent upon developmental stage, with Atp7b−/− embryos displaying lower copper accumulation than 5- or 45-day-old juveniles relative to wild-type zebrafish [21, 22]. Copper-treated Atp7b−/− 5-day-old larvae zebrafish display phenotypic similarities with Wilson’s disease patients such as liver fibrosis and steatosis and have transcriptomic enrichment in metabolic pathways, including carbon metabolism, PPAR signaling, glycolysis/glucogenesis, relative to copper-treated wild-type zebrafish [22]. Unlike other fish and animals, Atp7a, rather than Atp7b, is predominately expressed in the liver, and Atp7b expression is predominant in the intestine of zebrafish [23]. Whole-mount in situ hybridization (WISH) analysis demonstrated changes in lipid metabolism genes, such as Elovl2 and Cel.2 in embryos 24 and 96 hours post fertilization of either copper treated Atp7a−/− or Atp7b−/− zebrafish relative to the respective knockout non-copper treated mutant [24].

The Atp7b−/− mouse was originally developed in C57BL/6x129S6/ScEv mixed strain mice [23] and has hepatic copper overload by 6 weeks of age, which precedes the onset of Wilson’s disease symptoms [12]. Atp7b−/− mice in the C57BL/6 background [24, 25] produce similar hepatic results as the original strain. Newborn Atp7b−/− pups have significantly reduced hepatic copper levels, likely due to decreased placenta copper transport; however, surviving pups have dramatically increased hepatic copper levels relative to wild-type mice by one month of age [25]. Copper content of milk produced by Atp7b−/− dams is significantly reduced relative to wild-type dams, which likely exacerbates the copper misbalance of nursing pups [23].

Analysis of hepatocellular copper distribution in Atp7b−/− mice showed copper enrichment in cytosolic, nuclear, and mitochondria fractions [12]. suggesting that excess copper may have a direct impact on nuclear function and transcription. Similar cellular distribution has been demonstrated in Wilson’s disease rat models and Wilson’s disease patients [12–16, 26, 27]. Transcriptional profiling at 1.5 months of age revealed a surprising abundance of differentially expressed genes associated with lipid and cholesterol metabolism [12, 28], which was confirmed in subsequent studies and established that copper-mediated changes in cellular signaling pathways precede significant hepatocellular pathology [24].

The onset of hepatocellular and gross pathology in Atp7b−/− mice has chronological strain differences. Hepatocellular features such as hepatitis, hepatomegaly, and karyomegaly are present by 3 months of age in Atp7b−/− C57BL/6x129S6/ScEv mice but not until 5 months of age in Atp7b−/− C57BL/6 mice [23, 24, 29]. Further, Atp7b−/− C57BL/6x129S6/ScEv mice develop regenerative liver nodules beginning at 7 months of age [23]. However, comparable development of regenerative nodules in the two strains was not found until 10 months of age [23, 29]. The progressive development of cellular and gross liver pathology is similar to the phenotype of “hepatic” Wilson’s disease, and further studies will be necessary to clarify whether the regenerative nodules in Atp7b−/− mice are similar to the cirrhotic nodules observed in the advanced stages of Wilson’s disease.

The recently developed hepatocyte specific Atp7b knockout mice, Atp7bΔhep, revealed surprising roles for non-parenchymal liver cells in maintaining copper homeostasis. As expected, Atp7bΔhep mice developed excess copper concentrations in hepatocytes, unlike non-parenchymal cell populations, which had normal copper levels [25, 26]. Interestingly, total liver copper content was lower in the Atp7bΔhep mice relative to the whole body Atp7b−/− mice at 20 weeks of age, due to increased concentrations of copper in non-parenchymal cells in the Atp7b−/− whole body knockout mice [25].

Atp7bΔhep mice do not develop many of the hallmarks of liver pathology observed in whole body Atp7b−/− mice or Wilson’s disease patients until 7 months of age [25, 26]. Atp7bΔhep mice had reduced expression of CD24a and infiltration of immune cells and increased expression of the copper binding metallothionein 1 and 2 mechanism relative to Atp7b−/− mice, which may explain the delayed onset of liver pathology in Atp7bΔhep [25].

The spontaneous occurrence of mutations in ATP7B have been identified in two different mouse strains, DL and C3H/HeJ, resulting in the “toxic milk” mouse model (Tx and Tx-j). Tx mice arose from a single amino acid substitution in the eighth transmembrane domain of the ATP7B gene in the DL mouse strain [27]. The Tx-j strain arose from a spontaneous G712D missense mutation in the second transmembrane domain of C3H/HeJ mice at Jackson Laboratories, and the Tx-j mice had a phenotype similar to that of the original Tx strain [28]. Both Tx and Tx-j strains die by 2 weeks of age unless they are cross-fostered by healthy dams, and surviving Tx pups develop rapid accumulation of copper in the liver, kidney, brain, spleen, and blood [27, 29]. These mice have many overlapping features with Wilson’s disease patients, which have been previously reviewed [30].

Long Evans Cinnamon (LEC) rats were originally identified as offspring of the Long-Evans inbred rat strain that developed a light “cinnamon” coat color and rapid onset of fulminant hepatitis and death [31, 32]. The LEC rats arose from a large deletion in the 3’ end of the rat Atp7b gene [33]. Unlike Atp7b−/− mice, LEC rats have rapid onset of liver pathology and death by approximately 4 months of age, and surviving LEC rats develop hepatocellular carcinoma and cholangiocarcinoma [34, 35].

The pineal night-specific ATPase (PINA) gene was identified in an intronic region of the rat Atp7b promoter, and the PINA protein was not expressed in the pineal glands of LEC rats [36, 37]. LEC rats were also shown to contain mutations in the melatonin producing enzyme serotonin n-acetyltransferase (NAT) gene in the pineal gland [37]. Breeding strategies isolated a set of rats containing mutations in Atp7b/PINA but not the NAT gene [37], which were designated as the LPP strain. LPP rats also develop liver failure and untreated animals die between 90 and 120 d of age [14]. Currently, it is not clear why there is such dramatic species variation in Atp7b knockout rodent models. It is plausible that mutations in PINA cause disruptions in the peripheral metabolic circadian circuits of LPP rats, resulting in exacerbated liver pathology in LPP rats.

Copper toxicity disorders resulting from mutations in the Atp7b, Atp7a (Menkes related), and copper metabolism murr1 domain-containing protein 1 (COMMD1, chaperone protein for Atp7b) have also been identified in canines [38]. Naturally occurring mutations in the Atp7b (Labrador retrievers) and COMMD1 (Bedlington terriers) genes result in excessive liver copper concentrations and cirrhosis [39–42]. The chronic presentation of liver disorders associated with these mutations and the longevity of canine models has been suggested as reasons for using these models for preclinical Wilson’s disease therapeutic trials [38]. Mutations in COMMD1 have also been identified in Wilson’s disease patients presenting with “atypical phenotypes”; however, data is limiting regarding the co-occurrence of COMMD1 and Atp7b dysfunction and necessity for screening for COMMD1 in Wilson’s disease patients [43–48].

Hepatic steatosis is a complication in Wilson’s disease patients, and recent reports in animal models and Wilson’s disease patients have provided mechanistic insight into this pathology. Transcriptome analysis in Atp7b−/− mice at 1.5 to 3 months of age showed significant alterations in genes associated with metabolic pathways involved in lipid and glucose metabolism [12, 49, 50]. In agreement with decreased lipid gene expression, Atp7b−/− mice have lower hepatic and serum LDL, triglyceride, and VLDL levels and have reduced visceral fat mass and body weight by 3.5 months of age [26, 51, 52]. Several reports have studied the impact of a Western-type and high fat diet (HFD) in Wilson’s disease animal models. Wild-type animals had greater increases in body weight, adiposity, and hepatic steatosis in comparison with Atp7b−/− mice on a Western diet [49, 52], which was similar to the attenuated lipid accumulation shown in Tx-j mice on a HFD [53]. Interestingly, AMPK signaling was activated in both Atp7b−/− and Tx-j mice, suggesting a mechanistic link between chronic copper-induced redox stress and decreased glucogenic and lipogenic gene expression [49, 53]. Conversely, hepatocyte specific knockout mice (Atp7bΔhep) have increased susceptibility to hepatic steatosis, possibly due to changes in cellular signaling pathways in nonparenchymal cells [25], Atp7b−/−, but not Atp7bΔHep, mice have reduced concentrations of enteric copper, abnormal enterocyte morphology, and disrupted chylomicron assembly [54]. This important finding highlights the role of copper misbalance in the intestine and the implications for global metabolism in Wilson’s disease.

Additional extra-hepatic mechanisms likely promote metabolic adaptations in Wilson’s disease. Pre-symptomatic (3-month-old) Atp7b−/− mice were more active and had decreased food consumption in the dark cycle [49]. Atp7b deficiency in the choroid plexus results in transient copper deficiency in the brains of young Atp7b−/− mice that is reversed by 5 months of age [55]. Additional studies in Wilson’s disease animal models should clarify the chronological metabolic changes in central vs. peripheral systems and inform metabolic mechanisms that drive both altered whole body metabolism as well as the neuropathology that develops in some Wilson’s disease patients.

Despite overlapping liver pathology in Atp7b−/− mouse and rat models, LPP rats exhibit different lipid metabolism in response to high caloric challenges. LPP rats administered a high calorie diet (HCD, 45% fat and fructose supplemented drinking water) have increased visceral fat mass and liver triglycerides, despite no changes in body weight on HCD [56]. LPP rats also had increased hepatocellular damage and NAFLD scores relative to Atp7b+/− rats (control group) and exacerbated mitochondria dysfunction and decreased ATP production [56]. Activation of AMPK phosphorylation in chow-fed Atp7b−/− mice and Tx mice [49, 53] suggests similar mitochondria pathology in both species. The genetic differences between the Atp7b−/− mice and LPP rat models possibly underlie the different metabolic responses to dietary challenges, which highlights a potentially interesting area for future study.

The dramatic changes in cholesterol metabolism observed in Wilson’s disease animal models are suggestive of alterations in bile acid metabolism. Atp7b−/− mice [12, 51] and LEC rats [57] have decreased expression of cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase (Cyp7a1), the rate-limiting enzyme in the bile acid synthetic pathway, which is the major route for cholesterol elimination from the body. Decreased mRNA expression of the bile salt export pump (BSEP, Abcb11), the dominant determinant of bile flow, was shown in LEC rats and Atp7b−/− mice [57, 58]. Also, Atp7b−/− mice have decreased mRNA expression of multidrug resistance-associated protein 2 (Mrp2, Abcc2) [51], which secretes glutathione and glucuronidated endobiotics and xenobiotics into bile. Pre-symptomatic Atp7b−/− mice have elevated serum bile acids, and Wilson’s disease patients demonstrated increased serum glycocholic acid (GCA), glycoursodeoxycholic acid (GUDCA), and 7-α-OH-3-oxo-cholest-4-en-26-oic acid [59]. It is plausible that disrupted bile acid metabolism exacerbates the hepatocellular pathology in Wilson’s disease patients.

Cirrhosis and poor nutritional status, such as abnormal glucose metabolism and hypocholesterolemia, are associated with Wilson’s disease patients [60]. A recent study compared Wilson’s disease patients with healthy subjects and identified unique metabolites in the Wilson’s disease cohort, including enrichment of TCA cycle components, amino acids, and bile acid intermediates [59]. Similar robust metabolic changes were identified in a Wilson’s disease rat model of chronic copper feeding [61] and Atp7b−/− mice [49]. The consequences of differential enrichment of metabolites on liver pathology are unclear, and the literature is inconsistent regarding glucose tolerance in Wilson’s disease patients [62, 63]. For example, one case study in a 23-year-old female Wilson’s disease patient reported hypoglycemia, which resolved with zinc therapy [63].

Recent studies in Atp7b−/− mice support the dramatic changes in metabolic profiles reported in Wilson’s disease patients. Atp7b−/− mice have decreased hepatic glucose, pyruvate, and lactate levels by 6-months of age in Atp7b−/− livers; however, several glycolytic and TCA cycle intermediates were increased [49]. The metabolic enrichment patterns suggest altered metabolic flux and possibly a Warburg-like metabolic state in Atp7b−/− mice. Glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity were improved, but Atp7b−/− mice became hypoglycemic during insulin tolerance testing, which suggested a defective counter regulatory response rather than a component of normal/healthy metabolism [49]. Recent studies revealed a link between excessive cellular copper and cell death mediated by copper binding of lipolylated TCA cycle intermediates termed “cuproptosis” [64], which may link increased accumulation of TCA cycle intermediates and hepatocellular pathology in Wilson’s disease patients and mice.

Nuclear receptors are ligand inducible transcription factors that consist of an N-terminal domain, conserved DNA-binding domain, hinge region, and ligand binding domain [65]. Class I steroid hormone nuclear receptors (such as estrogen receptor) are highly expressed in steroidogenic tissues and regulate steroid production, such as cortisol, aldosterone, and sex hormones. Class II (RXR heterodimers), Class III (homodimeric orphan receptors), or Class IV (monomeric orphan receptors) nuclear receptors regulate metabolic target gene expression [66]. Metabolic nuclear receptors are primarily localized in the nucleus, and agonist binding results in conformational changes and loss of co-repressor complexes (SMRT and NCoR) and the recruitment of co-activators, such histone acetylases, SRC-1, CARM-1, PGC-1α, and CBP. Nuclear receptors discussed below include: FXR (NR1H4), LXR (NR1H3), PPAR (NR1C1) or orphan nuclear receptors (LRH-1 (NR5A2) and HNF4α (NR2A1)).

Increased hepatic bile acid levels (main endogenous ligand - chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA)) results in FXR activation that in turn increases expression of short heterodimer partner (SHP, NR0B2), an atypical nuclear receptor that lacks a DNA-binding domain [67–69]. Negative feedback regulation of bile acid synthesis and basolateral bile acid uptake into the hepatocyte is mediated by SHP-induced recruitment of co-repressor complexes to LRH-1 on the promoters of Cyp7a1, Cyp8b1, and Ntcp, resulting in decreased bile acid synthesis and uptake [70–72].

FXR also enhances biliary excretion of bile salts and conjugated bile acids via induction of the BSEP and MRP2 [73–75]. Enterohepatic regulation of FXR-signaling is mediated via induction of the fibroblast growth factor 15 (mice)/19(humans) mRNA expression in the ileum [76]. Fgf15/19 subsequently binds to FGFR4/β-Klotho receptors in the hepatocyte, leading to repression of Cyp7a1 expression and reduced bile acid synthesis [76]. This regulatory bile acid feedback loop mitigates bile acid toxicity by reducing bile acid synthesis.

Metabolite catabolism provides endogenous ligands for FXR and PPARα that in turn coordinate the expression of energy conserving and consuming pathways to ensure energy homeostasis. In general, FXR is activated in the fed state (via increased bile acids) and PPARα is activated in the fasting state by free fatty acids. FXR and PPARα repress de novo lipogenesis (Srepb-1c), and ligand specific activation lowers plasma triglyceride levels [77–79]. Conversely, FXR represses (G6Pase) [79] and PPARα upregulates (Pcx, Fbp1, Gpd1) gluconeogenesis [80]. PPARα also upregulates mitochondrial (CPT1 and CPT2), peroxisomal (ACOX1), and microsomal (cytochrome P450 4a, Cyp4a) β-oxidation [80]. These complementary roles of FXR and PPARα activity maintain energy substrate availability.

LXR coordinates cholesterol homeostasis, fatty acid metabolism, and immune function. Oxysterols (24(s), 25-epoxycholesterol) are cholesterol metabolites that are potent endogenous LXR ligands. In contrast with FXR and PPARα, LXR induces fatty acid synthesis (Srebp1c, FASN, SCD1) [81]. LXR regulates cholesterol homeostasis via induction of bile acid synthesis (Cyp7a1, primarily in rodents) and biliary and intestinal cholesterol excretion (Abcg5, and Abcg8), reverse cholesterol transport (Abca1 and Abcg1), and repression of NPC1L1 in the intestine [81]. LXR also regulates immune and inflammatory responses in macrophages, which reduces inflammation induced hepatic fibrosis [81].

The direct impact of copper on nuclear receptor function was demonstrated with the estrogen receptor (ER). Replacement of zinc with other transition metals in the DNA binding domain (DBD) impacted ER binding to target estrogen response elements (ERE). Reconstitution of an apo-polypeptide of the ER-DBD with zinc, cadmium, or cobalt resulted in functional binding to an ERE hexamer probe, but no binding was observed with copper or nickel [82]. Moreover, competition assays demonstrated that copper bound the ER-DBD with higher affinity than zinc [83]. The authors proposed that copper forms a complex with the sulfhydral groups in the zinc finger of the DNA binding domain and copper, like zinc, forms 4-coordinate, tetrahedral structures but may also form 4-coordinate, square planar conformations that disrupt folding of the DNA binding domain [83], resulting in a disordered structure that does not bind an ERE probe [82, 83]. Given the conservation of the nuclear receptor DNA binding domain among nuclear receptors, those studies implicated a role for nuclear receptor dysfunction in the pathology of Wilson’s disease. Since disruption of nuclear receptors results in liver toxicity and aberrant metabolism [70, 84–86], several groups have studied metabolic nuclear receptor activity in Wilson’s disease models and patients.

The negative impact of copper on FXR signaling was demonstrated in vitro and in vivo [49, 58, 87, 88]. Like previous findings with the ER-DBD, addition of copper to in vitro synthetic reactions for FXR or RXR resulted in loss of FXR:RXR binding to FXR-response elements (FXRE) on the Bsep and Shp promoters [58]. Further, FXR:RXR recruitment to FXR target genes was reduced in both HepG2 cells treated with excess copper and Atp7b−/− mice [58]. Recruitment of LRH-1 and HNF4α to response elements on target genes involved in bile acid synthesis displayed similar dysfunction [58]. Indirect effects of copper may also influence nuclear receptor binding. Whole genome sequencing in Wilson’s disease patients revealed hyper-methylated regions within liver-specific enhancer regions for lipogeneic genes, including HNF4α and RXRα [89]. Dysregulation of genes involved in bile acid metabolism agrees with the observed changes in serum bile acids found in animals and Wilson’s disease patients [58, 59].

In agreement with the collective dysfunction of nuclear receptors in Wilson’s disease, LXR regulated gene expression was disrupted in Atp7b−/− mice. LXR dysfunction in Atp7b−/− mice focused on the availability of the potent endogenous LXR ligands, oxysterols, rather than direct copper-mediated effects. Atp7b−/− mice had decreased mRNA expression of oxysterol generating enzymes (Cyp7b1 and Cyp46a1) and related oxysterol levels [90]. Further, Atp7b−/− mice had increased expression of sulfotransferases (Sult1e1) and oxysterol-sulfates (LXR-antagonists) relative to control mice [90]. Atp7b−/− mice treated with the Sult1e1 inhibitor (triclosan) had restoration of decreased oxysterol-sulfate and increased oxysterol levels, as well as decreased mRNA expression of inflammatory genes (TNFα, iNOS, COX2) relative to control mice [90]. Administration of the potent synthetic LXR ligand T0901317 to Atp7b−/− mice normalized hepatic lipid and triglyceride levels, decreased inflammation and fibrosis, and improved liver function, despite no significant changes in hepatic copper levels [50, 90]. T0901317 also decreased stellate cell activation and TGFβ and associated signaling pathways in Atp7b−/− mice [90], which provides therapeutic insight into the reduction of liver pathology in Wilson’s disease.

Basal activity of PPARα may also contribute to the metabolic pathology observed in Wilson’s disease. PPARα protein expression was measured in Wilson’s disease patients with various degrees of hepatic pathology (mild, moderate, and fulminant liver failure) and compared with that in healthy asymptomatic Wilson’s disease carriers [91]. Although patients with mild disease had increased PPARα protein expression relative to healthy controls, PPARα protein expression was lower in patients with advanced liver pathology [91]. It is plausible that variations in PPARα activity contribute to the heterogeneity of clinical symptoms in Wilson’s disease patients. Transcriptomic analysis of Atp7b−/− mice demonstrated an abundance of differentially expressed genes involved in PPARα signaling and decreased expression of hepatic PPARα target genes (Fgf21, G6Pase) [49, 58].

Taken together, all nuclear receptors tested in Atp7b−/− models exhibited reduced activity. It is currently unclear if current therapeutic strategies for Wilson’s disease impact nuclear receptor function, or whether the addition of ligand-specific drugs would be a safe and effective approach for Wilson’s disease patients. Current data suggests that restoration of nuclear receptor activity in Wilson’s disease is plausible, although copper disposition would likely remain unchanged with ligand-specific therapy. It is unclear if altered metabolism in Wilson’s disease also impacts indirect mechanisms, such as decreased endogenous ligand availability for nuclear receptors. Further, profound, and extended, copper in the liver complicates isolation of specific and direct copper effects on nuclear receptor biology (Figure 2). For example, extensive hepatocellular stress may result in activation of hepatic stellate cells, in turn promoting SUMOylation of FXR and reduction of ligand inducibility [92]. The global disruption of nuclear receptors in the liver also impairs our interpretation of metabolic profiling in Wilson’s disease, because it is unclear if regulatory loops, such as FXR-SHP-LRH-1, are maintained.

The current therapeutic strategy for Wilson’s disease patients is the reduction of free-unbound copper in affected tissues, which requires lifelong medical management [93]. Chelation (D-penicillamine, tetrathiomolybdate, and trientine) is a common first line therapy for Wilson’s disease; however, there are many well-documented side effects associated with long-term therapy, such as worsening of neurological symptoms [93]. Tetrathiomolybdate is a newer chelation therapy that is in clinical trials and associated with fewer neurological side effects [94]. Alternatively, oral zinc administration (typically given as zinc acetate) is a therapy for Wilson’s disease [95–97]. Zinc treatment decreases intestinal absorption of copper and induction of hepatic metallothionein [95, 96, 98]. Zinc therapy is typically given as maintenance therapy following prolonged chelation therapy or when chelation therapy is not well tolerated. Zinc has been shown to restore nuclear receptor function in NAFLD and Wilson’s disease mouse models. Dietary zinc supplementation restored DNA binding activity of HNF4α, PPAR-α, FXR, and RXR in NAFLD [58, 99], and Wilson’s disease models, which is in agreement with previous in vitro studies with ERα [58, 82]

A lack of structural analysis limits the precise mechanisms for copper:zinc balance in nuclear receptor dysfunction. The impact of chelation therapy on nuclear receptor function has not been tested. The development of a new metal chelator, ARBM101, a bacteria-originated peptide that is an isoform of methanobactin, displayed exciting long-term reductions of copper levels and significantly extended the lifespan of LPP rats [100]. Usage of ARBM101 and other classes of chelators in Wilson’s disease models will extend our knowledge of the direct impact of copper on nuclear receptor function and liver pathology.

Synthetic nuclear receptor agonists are available, but the clinical appropriateness of these drugs in Wilson’s disease patients has not been assessed. Therapeutic targeting of nuclear receptors in this patient population is complicated by the heterogeneity of the disease and the lack of data regarding metabolic changes in Wilson’s disease patients. Current data in Atp7b−/− mice predicts that nuclear receptors maintain between 40 and 60% basal activity, and ligand induction of hepatic target genes is obtainable [58]. Brief treatment of Atp7b−/− mice with potent synthetic ligands for either FXR (GW4064) or LXR (T0901317) resulted in normalization of target gene expression [50, 58, 90], indicating that a pool of nuclear receptors maintained the capacity for ligand-mediated activation (Figure 3). Currently, it is unknown whether chronic and excess hepatocellular copper results in increased endogenous ligands for nuclear receptors, and thus increased nuclear target gene expression. Further, it is unclear if different WD phenotypes (hepatic, neurological, mixed) will have different responses to ligand therapies.

Enterohepatic regulation of FXR signaling is critical to maintaining metabolic homeostasis. The intestinal FXR target Fgf15 mRNA maintained a strong induction with GW4064 in pre-symptomatic Atp7b−/− mice [58], which reveals a possible mechanism for restoration of FXR signaling in Wilson’s disease. To date, little is known about bile acid pool size and composition in Wilson’s disease patients, which limits our ability to predict the impact of targeting FXR in Wilson’s disease patients. The currently available data predicts that bile acid metabolism is altered in Wilson’s disease patients, but it is not clear if this is of clinical significance.

Wilson’s disease is a devastating disease that presents many therapeutic challenges due to the variety of symptoms present and limited comprehensive metabolic data. Life-long therapeutic management is required to prevent the need for liver transplantation, and these therapies may result in side-effects that necessitate complex medical management. Recent data presents a new understanding of metabolic changes in Wilson’s disease patients, and animal models reveal nuclear receptors as potential targets in the treatment of Wilson’s disease and provide additional tools for clinical management of symptoms, which would be of great utility in complex cases. Further study of copper-metabolome interactions should clarify which nuclear receptors are the most viable targets in Wilson’s disease patients.
