# Relative Diagnostic Yield of Tests for Disseminated Tuberculosis Among Inpatients With Human Immunodeficiency Virus.
**Authors:** Namale PE, Colquhoun C, Zinyakatira N, Lee-Jones S, Boloko L, Vermeulen M, Haigh K, Bagula M, Maseko A, Msomi Y, Sossen B, Barr D, Maartens G, Schutz C, Meintjes G
**Journal:** Open Forum Infect Dis (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1093/ofid/ofag728](https://doi.org/10.1093/ofid/ofag728)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Disseminated tuberculosis (TB) is a major cause of morbidity and mortality among people with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV-1). Early diagnosis is critical, yet the optimal diagnostic strategy remains uncertain. We aimed to describe the relative diagnostic yield of tests for disseminated TB and to explore associations between test results and patient characteristics.

METHODS: We conducted a cross-sectional study nested within the screening population of a clinical trial of participants with HIV-associated disseminated TB, who were identified by at least 1 positive result from urine lipoarabinomannan (uLAM), urine Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra (uXPU), TB blood culture, or blood Xpert Ultra (blXPU). Diagnostic yield was defined as the proportion positive by that test among all participants diagnosed with disseminated TB.

RESULTS: Among the 426 participants, 56% were female, median age was 35 years, and median CD4 count was 39 cells/μL. Advanced HIV (defined as CD4 count <200 cells/μL) was present in 385 (90%), and 36 (8%) participants had an HIV RNA measurement <50 copies/mL. Diagnostic yield was higher for uXPU (82%) than uLAM (68%), blXPU (30%), and TB blood culture (24%). The incremental diagnostic yield from performing uXPU following uLAM was 29.6%. There was minimal incremental yield from performing either TB blood culture or blXPU after both uLAM and uXPU. Diagnostic yield declined with increasing CD4 count for all assays except uXPU.

CONCLUSIONS: Urine LAM and uXPU provide complementary diagnostic yields for disseminated TB. The high yield of uXPU supports its inclusion alongside uLAM in TB diagnostic algorithms among inpatients with HIV.

Disseminated tuberculosis (TB) is an important cause of morbidity and mortality among inpatients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV-1) [ 1–4 ]. Postmortem studies reveal that about half of HIV-associated TB cases go undiagnosed before death, with almost 90% having disseminated TB [ 4 ], highlighting the need for improved diagnostic strategies. Although no universal definition is available, disseminated TB has historically been considered as presence of TB disease at 2 noncontiguous sites and/or a positive TB blood culture (BC). More recently, the application of molecular diagnostics to urine and blood, particularly in HIV-associated TB, has permitted more systematic ascertainment of TB dissemination, and revealed substantial covariance between TB nucleic acid or antigen detection in blood and urine [ 5–8 ]. Blood and urine samples are easier to obtain than sputum for TB diagnosis among inpatients with HIV as many are unable to produce sputum [ 9 ]. Urine tests have higher sensitivity than blood tests in people with HIV (PWH) among inpatients [ 10 ]. Two rapid diagnostic tests for TB in urine are more widely available: a lateral flow test for urine lipoarabinomannan (uLAM), the Determine TB-LAM Ag Lateral Flow Strip Test (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL, USA), which detects a cell wall component of mycobacteria; and a nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT), Xpert Ultra (uXPU; Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). Other NAATs in urine have limited evidence and less availability [ 11 ].

The yield of uLAM is lower than that of uXPU in comparative studies [ 10 , 12 , 13 ]. Furthermore, the specificity of uXPU is higher than that of uLAM [ 13 , 14 ]. However, it is unclear what the relative yield of urine-based tests for TB is specifically among patients with disseminated TB as the comparative studies were conducted among a broader group of inpatients and outpatients with HIV being investigated for TB [ 10 , 12 , 13 ].

Demonstration of mycobacteremia (positive TB BC) is the gold standard for disseminated TB diagnosis; however, contamination in up to 10% [ 8 ] and the long time to culture positivity limit its utility. The prevalence of mycobacteremia from 21 studies in a systematic review of adults with HIV was 15.5% [ 15 ]. There are limited data on the use of blood Xpert Ultra (blXPU; Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) for TB diagnosis. The yield of blXPU among inpatients being investigated for TB was 37% [ 6 ].

We described the relative diagnostic yield of tests indicating disseminated TB in a population of inpatients with HIV with confirmed disseminated TB. We also explored associations between positive disseminated TB tests and patient characteristics.

We conducted a cross-sectional study nested within the NewStrat-TB trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04951986) at 3 secondary level public hospitals in Cape Town, South Africa: Khayelitsha, Mitchells Plain, and New Somerset. NewStrat-TB is a randomized controlled trial testing 2 interventions (intensified TB treatment vs standard TB treatment and prednisone vs placebo), among inpatients with disseminated HIV-associated TB. The NewStrat-TB trial enrolled patients ≥18 years old with HIV and disseminated TB before their third dose of TB treatment. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the published protocol [ 16 ]. The population screened for NewStrat-TB was hospitalized patients with HIV, with clinical symptoms of TB and being investigated for TB.

The diagnostic yield study reported here included all patients who were screened for the NewStrat-TB trial who had at least 1 of 4 tests that were used for defining disseminated TB positive, these being uLAM, uXPU, blXPU, and TB BC. This study includes some patients who met a NewStrat-TB trial exclusion criteria but still met the diagnostic criteria for disseminated TB (at least 1 test positive).

Tests for disseminated TB were collected for trial screening by study staff, but TB BCs were not done among participants with hemoglobin concentrations <6 g/dL or with symptomatic anemia. Urine was collected as soon as participants could provide the sample with no specified timing. Sputum and additional extrapulmonary samples were not collected as part of the study, but were collected as part of routine care in some patients.

All blood and urine specimens required by the trial were processed using standard operating procedures. Disseminated TB tests, apart from the uLAM, were performed in designated and certified trial laboratories by trained laboratory staff. Other laboratory tests including screening blood tests were done by an accredited laboratory, the National Health Laboratory Service.

The uLAM was performed at the bedside by the trial staff on neat urine. As per manufacturer guidelines, approximately 60 μL of urine was applied to the sample pad of the test strip. The test was read 25–35 minutes after sample application. The manufacturer's reference scale card was used to indicate the 4 grades of positivity. The test was read as invalid if no control band was visible and negative if the patient line was not visible or visible below grade 1+ intensity.

Urine was collected in a Falcon tube and sent to the trial laboratory. Study procedures required a minimum of 10 mL of urine for Xpert Ultra. The sample was centrifuged at 3000g for 25 minutes before the supernatant was discarded. The concentrated urine or pellet was then mixed with 1.5 mL of Xpert Ultra buffer and transferred to the cartridge for Xpert Ultra testing.

Approximately 4 mL of blood was collected in an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tube. To optimize the yield of blXPU, blood was treated with red blood cell lysis buffer and centrifuged. The processing steps were similar to those used by Boloko and colleagues [ 6 ] with modifications (see Supplementary Materials and Methods).

We collected 4 mL of blood in a sodium heparin tube. Processing for the TB BC was done under aseptic conditions in a hood, before it was inoculated into a Myco/F lytic bottle. We followed similar processing steps as in a previous study [ 17 ], with modifications (see Supplementary Materials and Methods). Blood was not inoculated directly into the TB culture bottle for 2 reasons. First, we obtained plasma to store from the sample. Second, we optimized the culture yield by wash steps to remove TB medication in blood if present as TB BCs were also performed longitudinally on TB treatment in the trial (not reported here).

Cultures were incubated for 6 weeks. Positive cultures were identified by antigen test, MPT64 antigen (BD MGIT TBc Identification Test) and HAIN MDRplus (Hain Lifescience, GenoType MTBDRplus version 2.0) for identification of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex and its resistance to rifampicin and isoniazid. For nontuberculous mycobacteria (MTBDRplus negative), HAIN CM (Hain Lifescience, GenoType Mycobacterium CM version 2.0) was used for species identification.

Data were analyzed using Stata version 17 and R version 4.3.2 software. Participant characteristics were summarized with medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) as many variables were not normally distributed. We defined diagnostic yield as the proportion of participants with disseminated TB who were positive by that disseminated TB test with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), calculated using binomial distributions. All trace Xpert Ultra results were considered positive. We determined the incremental yield of additional tests performed following an initial uLAM or uXPU test. Xpert Ultra cycle threshold (Ct) value was defined as the minimum Ct value from the 4 rpoB probes. A Ct value for trace-positive samples was imputed using a cubic spline model predicting minimum rpoB Ct from IS1081-IS6110 values, fitted using non-trace-positive samples [ 18 ].

We described the number of tests that yielded a rifampicin-resistant result on any of the 3 disseminated TB tests that ascertain this. Among participants with rifampicin resistance demonstrated on uXPU, we described how many had concordant or discordant rifampicin resistance when compared with rifampicin susceptibility from all other TB tests done in the study and from samples taken by routine care staff, for example sputum XPU, additional TB BCs, lymph node aspirate or gastric lavage TB cultures and XPU. Among participants with rifampicin resistance on uXPU, we defined concordance as a uXPU result with rifampicin resistance and no other sample showing susceptibility to rifampicin.

To investigate how diagnostic performance varied by patent characteristics, we fitted Loess plots to visually assess the relationship between yield of the individual TB tests and biomarkers including CD4 count, creatinine, venous lactate, C-reactive protein (CRP), and hemoglobin. These biomarkers analyzed were chosen due to association with mortality in prior studies [ 6 ]

Participants provided both screening and enrollment informed consent for the main trial. The trial had an approved deferred consent process, which allowed patients without capacity to consent to be screened and enrolled into the trial when a next of kin was not contactable. The main trial and this nested diagnostic yield substudy received permission from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of Cape Town (HREC 001/2021).

Participants for this diagnostic yield substudy were enrolled between 10 August 2021 and 14 August 2023. Participants were severely immunosuppressed with 385 (90%) having a CD4 count <200 cells/μL. Only 36 (8%) participants had HIV virologic suppression (viral load <50 copies/mL).

Blood cultures for TB were not done in 8 participants with anemia, and these were excluded from the denominator for calculating TB BC diagnostic yield. Seven participants did not have their BCs processed due to technical omission; these were regarded as negative in analyses. We did not exclude these participants, as they had the other 3 diagnostic tests performed, and we wanted to include them in yield calculation for these tests. We wanted to reflect a real-life situation where technical problems occur.

The highest diagnostic yield was from uXPU with 82% (95% CI, 77%–85%). The median Ct value for all uXPU results was 26.7 (IQR, 23–29.7), whereas the median Ct value for blXPU results was 29.4 (IQR, 28.2–30.6). Among the positive uLAM results, the commonest uLAM grade was 1+ (40.6%), followed by 2+ (27.2%), 4+ (17.9%), and 3+ (14.4%).

The overlap of diagnostic yield of the tests is shown in an Upset plot (Figure 2, Supplementary Figure S1A and S1B). Fifty-two percent of participants were positive on both uXPU and uLAM, 27% on uXPU and blXPU, and 20% on uXPU and TB BC. Forty-five participants (11%) were positive on all 4 tests (Supplementary Figure S2), 96 (22.5%) were positive on uXPU alone, and 53 (12%) were positive on uLAM alone. Four participants (0.9%) were positive on blXPU alone (Figure 2).

Fifty-six participants (13%) had a trace result on uXPU and 38 (8.9%) on blXPU (Supplementary Table S1). Twenty-five (5.9%) participants were trace on uXPU and positive on uLAM. Thirty-one participants (7.3%) were trace on uXPU and negative on uLAM. Urine XPU gave a trace result in 28 (6.6%) participants who were negative on the other 3 disseminated TB tests. All participants with a trace result on blXPU were positive on at least 1 other disseminated TB test. There was a correlation between uXPU and uLAM, with higher grades of uLAM more likely to have lower Ct values on uXPU (Spearman rho = −0.26, P < .001) (Supplementary Figure S3A and S3B).

Ninety-nine participants (24%) had a positive TB BC, of whom 76 were also positive on uLAM, 87 on uXPU, and 57 on blXPU. The incremental yield of additional tests performed following an initial uLAM or uXPU is shown in Table 3. For each subsequent test, we excluded participants diagnosed by prior tests. The incremental yield of uXPU was 29.6% when the initial test was uLAM, while that of uLAM was 16.2% when the initial test was uXPU.

Among the 426 participants included in this diagnostic study, data on prior TB treatment received before screening and enrollment were available for 375 (88%) participants (these data were collected only for patients enrolled in the trial). Participants were eligible for enrollment if they had had <3 doses of TB treatment. Of these 375 participants, 167 (44.5%) had at least 1 dose of TB treatment. TB BC was positive among 32 (19.2%) participants who had prior TB treatment compared to 52 (25%) who had not received any TB treatment.

We explored variables associated with the diagnostic yield of disseminated TB tests. There was a reduction in yield as CD4 count increased for all tests except uXPU (Supplementary Figure S4). For blXPU, uLAM, and TB BC, yield increased with rise in creatinine, whereas there was a drop in yield of uXPU with increasing creatinine (Supplementary Figure S5). There was no effect of hemoglobin concentrations on the yield of TB tests except for TB BC, which was more likely to be positive the lower the hemoglobin concentration (Supplementary Figure S6). The higher the lactate and CRP concentrations, the higher the diagnostic yield from all TB tests (Supplementary Figures S7A, S7B, and S8). This trend with CRP was seen between a CRP of 10 and 100 mg/L for both XPU tests; above 100 mg/L, no further rise in yield was noted (Supplementary Figure S7A and S7B).

Twenty participants had rifampicin resistance reported on at least 1 of the disseminated TB tests (TB BC, blXPU, or uXPU) (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). Eighteen participants had rifampicin resistance demonstrated on uXPU. Fifteen participants showed concordance, with 11 demonstrating rifampicin resistance on another test. Four participants demonstrated rifampicin resistance only on uXPU, with no other valid drug susceptibility results to allow comparison. Three participants had rifampicin resistance demonstrated on uXPU but another specimen result showed rifampicin susceptibility (Supplementary Table S2). There were 7 participants diagnosed with disseminated nontuberculous mycobacterial infections: 6 with Mycobacterium avium-intracellulare complex on TB BC and 1 with Mycobacterium kansasii. These participants had a positive uLAM and negative blXPU and uXPU.

Among the 4 TB diagnostic tests evaluated, uXPU demonstrated the highest diagnostic yield in this highly selected population. Urine-based tests had higher diagnostic yields than blood tests. The combined diagnostic yield of uLAM and uXPU approached 100%. These findings suggest that uXPU should be more widely used in the rapid diagnosis of disseminated TB in inpatients with HIV. The role of TB BC and blXPU appears to be limited in high-burden settings.

The higher diagnostic yield of uXPU compared to uLAM is not a consistent finding. In a study among PWH being investigated for TB, uXPU had a higher yield compared to uLAM (68% vs 45%, respectively) [ 10 ]. Similarly, another study reported a higher diagnostic yield of uXPU compared to uLAM, with participants with or at high risk for disseminated TB having an even higher yield [ 12 ]. In that study, participants with elevated lactate, low hemoglobin, low CD4 count, and mycobacteremia had a higher diagnostic yield by uXPU compared to uLAM, against a microbiological reference standard [ 12 ]. These findings align with our observation that both uXPU and uLAM yields increase in patients with features of a sepsis syndrome or severe immunodeficiency, consistent with the biology of disseminated TB. However, in a multicenter diagnostic accuracy study, uXPU and uLAM diagnostic yield was similar among inpatients [ 13 ]. The yield for both these tests was lower than in our study; however, among subgroups at high risk for disseminated TB, such as those with CD4 count ≤200 cells/μL, those with mycobacteremia, and those who died within 10 weeks, the yield was higher [ 4 ]. The difference between our study and this study could be explained by the difference in population in terms of immune status. The participants in that study had less immunosuppression generally, with median CD4 count of 374 cells/μL compared to median CD4 count of 40 cells/μL in our study, making participants in that study at lower risk for disseminated TB.

We did not find a relationship between uXPU diagnostic yield and hemoglobin concentration, in contrast to other studies, which reported that lower hemoglobin was associated with increased diagnostic yield [ 2 ]. In the current study, 83% of total participants had hemoglobin of ≤10 g/dL (47% below 8 g/dL); discrepancies with other studies may therefore be because they were able to observe reduction in diagnostic yield at higher hemoglobin levels.

TB BC was the only test in our study to show increased yield with decreasing hemoglobin. One study reported an increased yield for both uLAM and blXPU with anemia in patients with severe HIV-associated TB [ 6 ]. We observed an inverse relationship between renal function and the performance of the 2 urine-based tests: uLAM yield increased with worsening renal function, while uXPU yield decreased. We hypothesize that reduced urine output in renal impairment may lower the concentration of M tuberculosis DNA shed into urine, affecting uXPU yield, while the cell wall component detected by uLAM continues to be shed despite reduction in glomerular filtration rate, increasing the diagnostic yield in patients with severe disease and organ dysfunction.

The yield of blXPU in our study was similar to that of TB BC, suggesting that blXPU could replace TB BC as the latter often takes weeks before a result. However, TB BC is able to diagnose nontuberculous mycobacteria, which was found on BC in 7 participants (1.6%) in our cohort. The overall TB BC yield was low; exposure to 1 or 2 doses of TB treatment may have contributed to this, as the proportion positive was lower in the 45% of participants who received prior TB treatment compared to those who did not.

Implementing uXPU testing in patients with presumed disseminated TB would incur higher diagnostic costs. However, its high diagnostic yield, higher specificity than uLAM, and ability to detect rifampicin resistance could justify this expense. In resource-limited settings, uLAM could be performed first, followed by uXPU if the uLAM is negative. Modeling studies suggest that addition of uXPU to other TB diagnostics including uLAM and sputum microscopy is cost effective and increases life expectancy in resource-limited settings [ 19 ]. In addition, this would enable diagnosis and appropriate treatment of rifampicin-resistant TB and reduce deaths [ 20 ] in areas with high burden of rifampicin resistance. In our study, uXPU had a similar diagnostic yield with increasing CD4 counts up to 200 cells/μL, while the diagnostic yield of uLAM decreased as CD4 counts increased in this range; this finding suggests that uXPU might perform better than uLAM in less profoundly immunosuppressed people. Because few participants in our study had CD4 counts >200 cells/μL, we are not able to make conclusions about the yield of the disseminated TB tests in such patients, but few patients with HIV and disseminated TB have CD4 counts >200 cells/μL.

A key limitation of our study is that it was not designed as a diagnostic accuracy study; therefore, we are unable to assess sensitivity and specificity and our findings may not be generalizable to all PWH who are inpatients with suspected TB. Another limitation is that there was not systematic sputum collection for TB testing performed in this cohort and therefore we did not include sputum results in our analyses. A strength of our study is its large sample size of a well-defined population of PWH with disseminated TB.

While the utility of uXPU in routine TB screening remains unclear [ 21 ], our findings support its diagnostic value in inpatients with HIV and presumed disseminated TB. Currently, the World Health Organization recommends the use of uLAM for TB diagnosis in seriously ill patients with advanced HIV [ 22 , 23 ]. Our findings suggest that uXPU should be incorporated into diagnostic algorithms for this high-risk group and underscores the potential of combined urine-based testing to improve timely disseminated TB diagnosis and treatment initiation. An important advantage of uXPU over uLAM is that it can detect rifampicin resistance, which has direct treatment implications. There is a need for health economic studies of uXPU, particularly among inpatients being investigated for TB. Future studies should also determine if uXPU shortens time to TB treatment and reduces mortality in inpatients with HIV.
