Abstract
Purpose
To evaluate whether combined cilostazol and rosuvastatin therapy improves health-related quality of life,
walking performance, and lipid proﬁles compared with rosuvastatin alone in patients with symptomatic
lower extremity peripheral artery disease (PAD).
Methods
This multicenter, double-blind, randomized placebo-controlled trial enrolled 260 patients with chronic
lower extremity PAD (Rutherford category 2 or 3) at 52 sites in Korea. Participants were randomly
assigned (1:1) to receive cilostazol 200 mg plus rosuvastatin or a matching placebo plus rosuvastatin
for 24 weeks. The primary endpoint was the change in the Korean Peripheral Artery Questionnaire
(KPAQ) summary score from baseline to week 24.
Results
A total of 232 patients completed 24-week follow-up. The combination therapy group showed
signiﬁcantly greater improvement in the KPAQ summary score than in the rosuvastatin monotherapy
group (least-squares mean change: 15.5 vs. 10.2 points; between-group difference 5.3 points, 95%
conﬁdence interval 1.8–8.9; p = 0.003). The treatment effect became statistically signiﬁcant at week 12
and continued to increase through week 24. Treadmill walking distances improved in both groups;
although between-group differences at week 24 were not signiﬁcant, functional and absolute
claudication distances were signiﬁcantly greater in the combination therapy group at week 12.
Combination therapy resulted in greater reductions in triglycerides and apolipoprotein B, and greater
increases in HDL cholesterol, compared with rosuvastatin alone. No unexpected safety concerns were
identiﬁed.
Conclusion
In patients with symptomatic PAD, the addition of cilostazol to rosuvastatin signiﬁcantly improved
patient-reported health status and provided additional favorable effects on atherogenic lipid proﬁles
beyond rosuvastatin therapy alone. 
Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT07600385
Introduction
Peripheral artery disease (PAD) affects approximately 236 million individuals worldwide and is
associated with a two- to four-fold increased risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular
death compared with age-matched controls [1, 2]. In South Korea, the burden of PAD continues to
increase because of population aging, increasing prevalence of diabetes mellitus, and widespread
Page 3/27

dyslipidemia [3, 4]. Despite its growing prevalence and adverse prognosis, pharmacological treatment
options for symptomatic PAD remain limited. Current international guidelines, including the 2024
American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) and 2024 European Society for
Vascular Surgery (ESVS) guidelines, strongly recommend statin therapy for cardiovascular risk reduction
in all patients with PAD and cilostazol for improving claudication symptoms and walking performance in
patients with chronic symptomatic lower extremity PAD [5, 6].
Rosuvastatin, one of the most potent 3-Hydroxy-3-Methylglutaryl-Coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase
inhibitors, provides both robust low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) reduction and pleiotropic
vascular beneﬁts, including anti-inﬂammatory activity, endothelial protection, and plaque stabilization [7,
8]. Cilostazol, a selective phosphodiesterase type-3 (PDE-3) inhibitor, has been shown to improve
claudication symptoms and walking distance in patients with PAD, with evidence suggesting potential
beneﬁts on health-related quality of life [9–11]. Experimental studies have suggested potentially
complementary vascular effects between cilostazol and rosuvastatin, supporting the rationale for ﬁxed-
dose combination therapy (12). In addition, ﬁxed-dose combination therapy has been associated with
improved medication adherence and treatment persistence in patients requiring long-term multidrug
therapy [13–15].
However, no randomized controlled trial has directly evaluated whether adding cilostazol to background
rosuvastatin therapy provides incremental clinical beneﬁt over rosuvastatin alone in patients with
symptomatic PAD. Previous cilostazol trials were largely conducted in statin-undertreated populations
and primarily compared cilostazol with placebo, limiting their applicability to contemporary clinical
practice. Therefore, we conducted the KUP-UI022-301 trial, a multicenter, double-blind, randomized
controlled study comparing ﬁxed-dose combination of cilostazol/rosuvastatin combination therapy
versus rosuvastatin monotherapy in patients with chronic lower extremity PAD.
Methods
Study Design
This study was designed as a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, parallel-group, clinical trial to
evaluate the eﬃcacy and safety of a ﬁxed-dose cilostazol/rosuvastatin combination therapy compared
with rosuvastatin monotherapy in patients with chronic lower extremity PAD. The trial was conducted in
accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, International Council for Harmonisation Guidelines, and
Korean Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review
Board of each participating center and by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety. Written informed
consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment and before any study-speciﬁc procedures
were performed. The clinical trial was approved by the Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety and
registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT number: NCT07600385)

Study population
Eligible participants were adults aged ≥ 19 years with symptomatic chronic lower-extremity PAD and
Rutherford category 2 or 3 intermittent claudication. Key inclusion criteria required lower-extremity
ischemic symptoms for at least 24 weeks, stable guideline-directed statin therapy for ≥ 12 weeks before
screening, stable claudication symptoms without clinically signiﬁcant improvement during the preceding
12 weeks, and an ankle-brachial index (ABI) of ≤ 0.90 at screening or ABI between 0.91 and 1.00 with
imaging-conﬁrmed ≥ 50% lower extremity arterial stenosis. Additional criteria for randomization included
stable Korean Peripheral Artery Questionnaire (KPAQ) scores between the screening and baseline visits
with a score difference of ≤ 10%, conﬁrmed willingness to maintain the rosuvastatin dose throughout the
study period, and run-in medication adherence of ≥ 70%.
Key exclusion criteria included lower extremity revascularization within 24 weeks before screening or
anticipated need for revascularization during the study period; acute cardiovascular events within 12
weeks, including myocardial infarction, unstable angina, stroke, transient ischemic attack, or coronary
revascularization; deep vein thrombosis within 12 weeks; statin intolerance including myopathy or
rhabdomyolysis; heart failure; signiﬁcant hepatic or renal dysfunction; hypersensitivity to either study
drug; or current use of other phosphodiesterase inhibitors.
Run-in Period and Randomization
After providing written informed consent, eligible participants entered a single-blind run-in phase lasting
28 ± 5 days. During this period, all patients received rosuvastatin (either 10 mg or 20 mg once daily)
together with matching placebo for cilostazol. The rosuvastatin dose was selected according to prior
statin therapy and lipid proﬁle.
Participants who successfully completed the run-in phase and met all randomization criteria were
assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the test or control group using stratiﬁed block randomization according to
rosuvastatin dose (10 mg vs. 20 mg) and presence of diabetes mellitus. Randomization codes were
generated by an independent statistician and implemented through an interactive web- response system.
Study medication were identical in appearance to maintain blinding until database lock.
Interventions
Participants in the test group received a ﬁxed-dose combination tablet containing cilostazol 200 mg and
rosuvastatin (either 10 mg or 20 mg according to the run-in dose) once daily. Participants in the control
group received rosuvastatin monotherapy at the corresponding dose together with matching placebo for
cilostazol. The treatment period lasted 24 weeks with study visits scheduled at weeks 4, 8, 12, and 24.
Stable antiplatelet therapy (up to two agents) and non-vitamin K oral anticoagulants were permitted if
maintained before enrolment. However, concomitant use of both antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy
Page 5/27

was prohibited. Analgesics, including non-steroidal anti-inﬂammatory drugs, were allowed for conditions
unrelated to PAD symptoms.
Study endpoints
The primary endpoint was the change in KPAQ summary score from baseline to week 24. The KPAQ is a
cross-culturally adapted and validated Korean version of the Peripheral Artery Questionnaire, a disease-
speciﬁc, patient-reported health status instrument originally developed by Spertus et al. for patients with
peripheral artery disease [16, 17]. The KPAQ assesses the impact of PAD on patients symptoms,
functional status, and health-related quality of life through six domains: physical limitation, symptoms,
symptom stability, treatment satisfaction, social functioning, and quality of life [16]. Each domain score
ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better health status and less functional limitations.
The KPAQ summary score was calculated as the mean of the scores from four domains, physical
limitation, symptoms, functioning, and quality of life. KPAQ assessments were performed at screening,
baseline, and each scheduled follow-up visit.
Secondary endpoints included changes in KPAQ domain scores from baseline at week 4, 8, 12, and 24;
change in lower extremity pain intensity assessed using a 100-mm visual analogue scale (VAS); and the
proportion of patients achieving a ≥ 10-mm reduction in VAS score. Changes in treadmill walking
performance, including initial claudication distance (ICD), functional claudication distance (FCD), and
absolute claudication distance (ACD), were assessed at weeks 12 and 24 using a constant-load treadmill
protocol (3.2 km/h; 12% grade). Treadmill walking performance was assessed in a predeﬁned subgroup
of patients (n = 113) who completed reproducible treadmill testing at both the screening and baseline
visits. To minimize measurement variability, only patients with ≤ 25% difference in absolute claudication
distance (ACD) between the two baseline assessments were eligible for treadmill analyses. Patients who
were unable or unwilling to perform treadmill testing or for whom testing was considered unsafe were
excluded from the treadmill analysis population. The baseline claudication distances were calculated as
the mean of measurements from Visit 1 and Visit 2. Additional secondary endpoints included changes in
ABI and percent changes in lipid parameters, including total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides, non-
HDL-C, apolipoprotein A-1 (Apo A-1), and apolipoprotein B (Apo B) at weeks 12 and 24.
Safety endpoints included treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAE), serious adverse events (SAE), and
changes in laboratory and vital sign parameters, assessed in the safety population deﬁned as all
randomized patients who received at least one dose of study medication.
Exploratory analyses evaluated the consistency of the primary endpoint across prespeciﬁed subgroups,
including rosuvastatin dose (10 mg vs. 20 mg), age (< 70 vs. ≥70 years), diabetes mellitus status,
antiplatelet use, and baseline KPAQ severity.
Statistical Analysis
Page 6/27

The planned sample size was 260 patients (130 per group), providing 80% power to detect an 8-point
between-group difference in change in KPAQ summary score, assuming a standard deviation of 20.5, a
two-sided alpha level of 0.05, and a 20% dropout rate. The primary eﬃcacy analysis was performed in
the study completer population, deﬁned as all randomized patients completed the full 24-week treatment
period and had a valid KPAQ assessment at week 24 (n = 232; combination therapy, n = 114; rosuvastatin
monotherapy, n = 118). Safety analyses included all randomized patients who received study medication.
The primary endpoint was analyzed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with treatment group and
rosuvastatin dose as ﬁxed effects and baseline KPAQ score as a covariate. Treatment effects were
reported as least-squares (LS) mean differences with 95% conﬁdence intervals. Secondary endpoints
were analyzed using ANCOVA or rank-based ANCOVA methods, as appropriate according to data
distribution.
Prespeciﬁed subgroup analyses of the primary endpoint were performed according to rosuvastatin dose,
age, diabetes mellitus status, antiplatelet use, and baseline KPAQ severity, with interaction p-values
reported. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically signiﬁcant. All statistical analyses were
performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).
Results
Baseline characteristics
Between March 2019 and May 2022, 537 patients were screened for eligibility at 52 hospitals in South
Korea. Of these, 315 entered the placebo run-in period, and 260 were randomized to the combination
group (cilostazol plus rosuvastatin, n = 130) or the rosuvastatin monotherapy group (placebo plus
rosuvastatin, n = 130). Of the 260 randomized patients, 232 (89.2%) completed the full 24-week
treatment period and constituted the primary analysis population (combination therapy, n = 114;
monotherapy, n = 118). In the combination therapy group, the most common reason for discontinuation
was adverse events (n = 8), whereas in the monotherapy group it was withdrawal of consent (n = 7).
Participant ﬂow is summarized in Fig. 1.
Baseline characteristics of the study completer population were generally well balanced between groups
(Table 1). The mean age was 69.6 ± 8.6 years in the combination therapy group and 68.8 ± 9.6 years in
the rosuvastatin monotherapy group, and most participants were male (91.2% vs. 87.3%, respectively).
The prevalence of diabetes mellitus was similar between groups (54.4% and 55.1%), as was the
distribution of Rutherford category 2 (47.4% vs. 52.5%). Baseline characteristics of all randomized
patients (n = 260) are presented in Supplementary Table 1 and were similarly well balanced between
groups.

Primary Endpoint
The improvement in KPAQ summary score from baseline to week 24 was signiﬁcantly greater in the
combination therapy group than in the rosuvastatin monotherapy group (Table 2, Fig. 2). The LS mean
change was 15.5 ± 1.3 points in the combination therapy group and 10.2 ± 1.3 points in the rosuvastatin
monotherapy group, corresponding to a between-group difference of 5.3 points (95% conﬁdence interval
[CI] 1.8 to 8.9; p = 0.003). The treatment effect emerged progressively over time (Supplementary Table 3).
At week 4, the between-group difference was not statistically signiﬁcant (+ 6.8 vs. +4.6 points; p = 0.169).
By week 12, the difference became signiﬁcant (+ 12.6 vs. +8.1 points; p = 0.017) and further increased at
week 24 (+ 15.5 vs. 10.2 points; p = 0.003). At week 24, a ≥15-point improvement in KPAQ summary
score was observed in 50.9% of patients in the combination therapy group and 35.6% in the rosuvastatin
monotherapy group (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Subgroup Analysis
Subgroup analyses of the primary endpoint are shown in Fig. 5. The treatment effect on the KPAQ
summary score consistently favored the combination therapy group across all prespeciﬁed subgroups.
No signiﬁcant treatment-by-subgroup interactions were observed. Numerically greater improvements in
KPAQ summary scores were observed in patients without diabetes mellitus and in those receiving
rosuvastatin 20 mg; however, these ﬁndings should be considered exploratory.
Safety
Safety analyses included all 260 randomized patients (Supplementary Table 4). Treatment-emergent
adverse events occurred in 62 patients (47.7%) in the combination therapy group and 48 patients (36.9%)
in the rosuvastatin monotherapy group. Serious adverse events occurred in 8 patients (6.2%) in the
combination therapy group and 7 patients (5.4%) in the rosuvastatin monotherapy group; none were
considered deﬁnitely or probably related to study medications. One patient in the rosuvastatin
monotherapy group died of myocardial infarction, whereas no deaths occurred in the combination
Page 15/27

therapy group. This death was not considered related to study medications. Adverse events leading to
treatment discontinuation occurred in 8 patients (6.2%) and 5 patients (3.8%) in the combination therapy
and rosuvastatin monotherapy groups, respectively. No signiﬁcant between-group differences were
observed in laboratory ﬁndings, vital signs, or electrocardiographic parameters.
Discussion
This multicenter, randomized, double-blind trial demonstrated that the combination therapy of cilostazol
and rosuvastatin signiﬁcantly improved patient-reported health status and showed favorable effects on
walking performance in patients with symptomatic lower extremity PAD over 24 weeks compared with
rosuvastatin therapy alone. Furthermore, add-on cilostazol to rosuvastatin provided comprehensive anti-
atherogenic lipid beneﬁts—speciﬁcally lowering TG and ApoB and raising HDL-C—without altering LDL-C
levels. To our knowledge, this is the ﬁrst randomized controlled trial to directly evaluate the incremental
clinical beneﬁt of cilostazol added to contemporary statin therapy in patients with PAD.
Because cilostazol primarily improves symptoms, functional capacity, and quality of life rather than
inducing substantial anatomical changes, the KPAQ was selected as the primary eﬃcacy measure to
capture patient-centered treatment beneﬁts. Objective functional outcomes assessed by treadmill
testing were evaluated as complementary measures in a predeﬁned subgroup. Combination therapy
signiﬁcantly improved the KPAQ summary score at week 24, with the treatment effect mainly driven by
improvements in the physical limitation and quality-of-life domains. Notably, the treatment effect
emerged progressively over time, becoming signiﬁcant at week 12 and further increasing at week 24,
suggesting sustained symptomatic beneﬁt with continued therapy.
Combination therapy also signiﬁcantly increased functional and absolute claudication distances at week
12, although these differences were no longer statistically signiﬁcant at week 24. This may be explained
by the reduced sample size resulting from the predeﬁned reproducibility criterion, substantial variability
in treadmill performance, and the modest correlation between treadmill-based exercise measures and
disease-speciﬁc patient-reported outcomes reported in prior PAD studies, including the CLEVER trial [18,
19]. Unlike treadmill testing, KPAQ captures broader aspects of functional status and quality of life
relevant to daily activities. Nevertheless, changes in KPAQ summary score correlated with changes in
treadmill walking distance within the treadmill-evaluable subset (Supplementary Fig. 1), suggesting that
patient-reported improvement was accompanied by objective functional gain among treatment
responders. These ﬁndings are consistent with previous data demonstrating improvements in
claudication symptoms and walking performance with cilostazol therapy [10, 11, 20] and support the
value of disease-speciﬁc patient-reported outcomes for evaluating clinically signiﬁcant treatment
beneﬁts in PAD [16, 17].
The favorable effects of cilostazol on lipid parameters represent another important ﬁnding of this study.
Compared with rosuvastatin monotherapy, the addition of cilostazol resulted in greater reductions in
triglycerides and apolipoprotein B, along with a greater increase in HDL-C. Previous studies have
Page 16/27

reported similar lipid-modifying effects of cilostazol, including reductions in triglyceride-rich lipoproteins
and apolipoprotein B levels [7, 21]. In addition, both cilostazol and rosuvastatin have been shown to
enhance endothelial nitric oxide signaling and improve endothelial function [22]. Experimental studies
suggest that the combination of cilostazol and rosuvastatin may exert complimentary or synergistic
effects on vascular function, providing a plausible biological basis for the clinical beneﬁts [23]. Although
the present study was not designed to evaluate cardiovascular outcomes, these ﬁndings suggest that
combined cilostazol and rosuvastatin therapy may provide metabolic and vascular beneﬁts beyond
symptomatic improvement alone.
Prespeciﬁed subgroup analyses demonstrated a directionally consistent beneﬁt of cilostazol across all
examined subgroups without signiﬁcant treatment-by-subgroup interactions. Although the treatment
effect appeared smaller in patients with diabetes mellitus, these ﬁndings should be interpreted
cautiously because the study was not powered for subgroup analysis comparisons. Nevertheless,
diabetic PAD is frequently accompanied by microvascular dysfunction, impaired endothelial function,
and more advanced systemic atherosclerosis, which may attenuate symptomatic improvement despite
pharmacological therapy [24].
The ﬁxed-dose combination of cilostazol and rosuvastatin was generally well-tolerated, with adverse
events consistent with established safety proﬁle of cilostazol. The higher incidence of diarrhea,
headache, and palpitations in the combination therapy group was comparable to previous reports and
rarely resulted in treatment discontinuation [9, 20, 25]. No unexpected safety signals were identiﬁed, and
no serious adverse events were considered related to study medication.
This study has several limitations. First, the primary eﬃcacy analysis was conducted in study completers
rather than all randomized patients, which may introduce selection bias, as patients who discontinued
more commonly due to adverse events in the combination group were excluded from the primary
analysis. Second, the follow-up duration was limited to 24 weeks, precluding assessment of long-term
cardiovascular and limb outcomes. Third, treadmill analyses were conducted in a smaller subset of
patients because of the predeﬁned reproducibility criteria, which may have limited statistical power for
functional walking endpoints. Fourth, the study population consisted exclusively of Korean patients,
potentially limiting generalizability to other ethnic populations. Finally, the trial was not powered to
evaluate cardiovascular clinical events or differential treatment effects according to PAD subtype.
Conclusion
In patients with symptomatic lower extremity PAD receiving background rosuvastatin therapy, the
addition of cilostazol signiﬁcantly improved patient-reported health status and favorably modiﬁed
atherogenic lipid parameters compared with rosuvastatin monotherapy. These ﬁndings support the role
of cilostazol as an effective adjunctive therapy for improving health status and symptom burden in
contemporary statin-treated PAD patients with intermittent claudication.
