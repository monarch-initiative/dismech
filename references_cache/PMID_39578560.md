# Molecular and genetic insights into human ovarian aging from single-nuclei multi-omics analyses.
**Authors:** Jin C, Wang X, Yang J, Kim S, Hudgins AD, Gamliel A, Pei M, Contreras D, Devos M, Guo Q, Vijg J, Conti M, Hoeijmakers J, Campisi J, Lobo R, Williams Z, Rosenfeld MG, Suh Y
**Journal:** Nat Aging (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s43587-024-00762-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43587-024-00762-5)

## Content

The ovary is the first organ to age in the human body, affecting both fertility and overall health. However, the biological mechanisms underlying human ovarian aging remain poorly understood. Here we present a comprehensive single-nuclei multi-omics atlas of four young (ages 23-29 years) and four reproductively aged (ages 49-54 years) human ovaries. Our analyses reveal coordinated changes in transcriptomes and chromatin accessibilities across cell types in the ovary during aging, notably mTOR signaling being a prominent ovary-specific aging pathway. Cell-type-specific regulatory networks reveal enhanced activity of the transcription factor CEBPD across cell types in the aged ovary. Integration of our multi-omics data with genetic variants associated with age at natural menopause demonstrates a global impact of functional variants on gene regulatory networks across ovarian cell types. We nominate functional non-coding regulatory variants, their target genes and ovarian cell types and regulatory mechanisms. This atlas provides a valuable resource for understanding the cellular, molecular and genetic basis of human ovarian aging.

The ovary is the primary female reproductive organ, and the first tissue to undergo profound age-associated loss of function in humans, characterized by a progressive decline in follicle number and oocyte quality1. This decline accelerates around age 37 years2, leading to increased risk of infertility, aneuploidy and congenital disabilities in offspring3. Female reproductive aging also influences lifespan and diverse health outcomes4–6. Despite its importance, little is known about the basic biological mechanisms that underlie human ovarian aging.

Age at natural menopause (ANM) has profound implications for health and disease risk4,5. Family and twin studies have demonstrated a strong relationship between genetics and ANM7. Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have identified hundreds of genetic loci associated with ANM8, with the great majority (~94%) residing in non-coding regions of the genome. Recent studies show that non-coding GWAS variants are significantly enriched in cell-type-specific transcriptional regulatory elements, such as enhancers9–11. Enhancers integrate associated intracellular and extracellular signals, resulting in context-specific transcriptional outputs driven by combinatorial actions of transcription factors (TFs)12. Genetic variation affecting regulatory function is considered a major determinant of differences in cell-specific gene expression and disease risk between individuals12. To understand the biological processes underlying ovarian aging, it is crucial to identify functional ANM-associated non-coding regulatory variants, their target genes and the cell types under their influence. However, an atlas of transcriptional regulatory elements active in ovarian cell types during aging is currently lacking.

Here we present a single-nuclei multi-omics atlas of human ovarian aging, unveiling coordinated transcriptomic and epigenomic changes, particularly highlighting the ovary-specific role of mTOR signaling. By integrating these data with ANM-associated GWAS risk variants, we report the identification of the functional regulatory elements, and functional non-coding variants and their target genes associated with ANM, across most cell types in the ovary. This atlas provides a valuable resource for exploring the cellular, molecular and genetic drivers of ovarian aging.

We performed single-nuclei RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) and single-nuclei assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (snATAC-seq) on the same flash-frozen human ovarian tissues from young (n = 4; ages 23–29 years) and reproductively aged (n = 4; ages 49–54 years) donors, all displaying normal ovarian histology (Fig. 1a, Extended Data Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). Assessment of post-mortem interval (PMI)-related genes showed minimal expression across all samples (Extended Data Fig. 1b), indicating negligible PMI effects on the ovarian transcriptome. After stringent quality control, we retained 42,568 nuclei for snRNA-seq and 41,550 nuclei for snATAC-seq (Methods). Unsupervised clustering on snRNA-seq revealed eight distinct clusters (Methods, Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 1c), identifying all major somatic cell types in the ovary based on well-defined cell-type-specific markers (Fig. 1b,c). For snATAC-seq, unsupervised clustering revealed seven distinct clusters (Methods, Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 1d). We annotated snATAC-seq clusters by transferring cell type labels from snRNA-seq (Methods), confirming the presence of all major cell types (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 2a). Cell types were further validated by examining chromatin accessibility at known marker promoter regions and calculating gene activity scores (Fig. 1e and Extended Data Fig. 2b). We observed high inter-individual consistency within both single-cell RNA-seq and ATAC-seq datasets (Extended Data Fig. 2c,d). To investigate the dynamic changes in cell type composition during human ovarian aging, we compared the cell type proportions of aged and young ovaries in the snRNA-seq data. We found significant changes in several cell types during aging (Fig. 2a). The abundance of granulosa and theca cells, critical components of ovarian follicles, was significantly decreased in aged compared to young ovaries (Fig. 2a), aligning with the known decrease in follicle number with age13. Blood vessel and lymphatic endothelial cells also markedly decreased in proportion in aged ovaries (Fig. 2a), consistent with the observed negative correlation between ovarian vascularity and age14. Interestingly, epithelial cells were the only cell type that increased in proportion in aged ovaries (Fig. 2a), potentially reflecting lifetime ovulation-induced rupture and repair15. Aged epithelial cells exhibited significantly elevated expression of cell-cycle-associated genes, whereas aged granulosa cells, theca cells and endothelial cells showed increased expression of apoptosis-associated genes and/or decreased expression of cell-cycle-associated genes compared to young counterparts (Extended Data Fig. 3a). In agreement with the snRNA-seq results, we observed almost identical age-related changes in the cellular composition estimated from the snATAC-seq data (Fig. 2b). To investigate gene expression changes during human ovarian aging, we identified aging-associated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cell type. We identified 3,455 ovarian aging-associated DEGs (Supplementary Table 4), with granulosa cells showing the largest number (Extended Data Fig. 3b). Most aging-associated DEGs are shared among cell types (Extended Data Fig. 3c) and show congruent expression changes (Fig. 2c). Among ‘common DEGs’ differentially expressed in at least four cell types, 218 were upregulated and 182 were downregulated in aged ovaries (Extended Data Fig. 3d). Common DEGs include known aging-related genes such as RICTOR, IGF1R, MAP3K5 and APOE (Extended Data Fig. 3e,f). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis showed that common DEGs were enriched in aging hallmarks16, including nutrient sensing, cellular senescence, proteostasis, cellular communication and mitochondrial function (Extended Data Fig. 3g). We also identified cell-type-specific aging-associated DEGs (Extended Data Fig. 3h,i).

To investigate whether the coordinated transcriptomic changes observed in ovarian cells during aging also occur in other human tissues, we analyzed single-cell RNA-seq data from eight age-matched human tissues (Methods). We calculated pairwise Pearson correlations based on gene expression fold changes (FCs) between young and aged samples. Ovarian cells exhibited moderate to strong correlations, indicating high coordination in aging-related transcriptomic changes (Extended Data Fig. 4a). In contrast, cells from other tissues showed only negligible or weak correlations (Extended Data Fig. 4b–i). This uniquely high coordination in ovarian cells highlights the distinctive nature of ovarian aging (Fig. 2d).

To gain insight into the dynamic changes in aging hallmarks during ovarian aging, we estimated the pathway activity score for 21 representative pathways derived from ‘the hallmarks of aging’16 (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 5a). Gene expression in histone modification, cellular senescence and nutrient-sensing signaling pathways, including mTOR and insulin pathways, increased in aged ovaries across cell types (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 5b). Conversely, expression in pathways involved in proteasome, extracellular matrix (ECM), oxidative phosphorylation and base excision repair decreased (Fig. 2e and Extended Data Fig. 5c). We validated these age-related changes using in situ hybridization assays, confirming increased expression of the mTOR signaling gene RICTOR and decreased expression of the oxidative phosphorylation gene MT-ATP6 in aged ovaries (Fig. 2f–i).

To delineate the pathways essential for ovarian aging, beyond the cellular level, we assigned each pathway an amplitude index and tissue prevalence ratio (Methods). Those with high values in both metrics are likely important in tissue aging. We pinpointed 10 critical pathways with a prevalence ratio of at least 0.7 (Extended Data Fig. 6a). Applying these analyses to eight human tissue datasets revealed tissue-specific patterns in these aging hallmark pathways (Extended Data Fig. 6b–i). Notably, mTOR signaling emerged as an ovary-specific aging-prevalent pathway (Fig. 2j), highlighting its unique and central role in ovarian aging. This finding is particularly intriguing given the evidence that mTOR inhibition extends lifespan more strongly in female mice than in male mice17 and that it can delay mouse ovarian aging18. In addition, by excluding all aging-associated genes from other tissues (Supplementary Table 5), as identified in the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database19, from our list of ovarian aging-associated DEGs, we identified 2,301 ovarian aging-specific genes (Extended Data Fig. 6j and Supplementary Table 6). GO analysis indicated that those genes were predominantly enriched in cytoplasmic translation (Extended Data Fig. 6k), a process centrally regulated by mTOR signaling. These results reinforce the distinctive and pivotal role of mTOR signaling in ovarian aging. Moreover, oxidative phosphorylation was identified as a prevalent aging pathway across tissues, with the ovary displaying the highest activity change (Fig. 2k), suggesting accelerated cellular aging in the human ovary.

In conclusion, our comprehensive analysis reveals the distinct and accelerated nature of ovarian aging, characterized by high transcriptomic coordination and distinct pathway activity changes. Notably, we identify mTOR signaling as a unique factor in ovarian aging, offering potential insights into sex-specific longevity responses.

Senescent cell burden increases with age in various tissues20,21. To test if cellular senescence increases during ovarian aging, we examined expressions of senescence markers CDKN1A (p21) and CDKN2A (p16). Few ovarian cells expressed CDKN2A (~0.43%), whereas a higher proportion expressed CDKN1A in young (~9.49%) and aged (~15.56%) ovaries (Extended Data Fig. 7a,b). We found significant increases in CDKN1A+ cells with age in several cell types (Fig. 3a). Using in situ hybridization, we observed approximately threefold increases in both the proportion of CDKN1A+ cells and CDKN1A expression in aged ovaries (Fig. 3b,c). In addition, a subset of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) genes was upregulated in most CDKN1A+ cells, albeit with some exceptions (Extended Data Fig. 7c). Transcriptional analysis of CDKN1A+ stromal cells showed upregulation of hypoxia response and HIF-1 signaling genes (Extended Data Fig. 7d). Comparing young and aged CDKN1A+ stromal cells revealed further upregulation of HIF-1 and nutrient-sensing pathways with age (Extended Data Fig. 7e). We computed HIF-1 pathway scores based on DEGs upregulated in CDKN1A+ cells (Supplementary Table 7). The HIF-1 pathway was enhanced in CDKN1A+ cells and further elevated during aging in most cell types (Fig. 3d). Altered intercellular communication is a hallmark of aging16. We used CellChat22 to explore age-related changes in ovarian cellular communication, integrating our snRNA-seq data with published human oocyte transcriptomes from young and aged ovaries23. Aging slightly reduced both the total number and strength of intercellular interactions (Extended Data Fig. 7f,g). Notably, aged granulosa and theca cells received fewer signals from other cell types, as did blood/lymphatic endothelial cells and immune cells (Fig. 3e). Of particular relevance to fertility, the number of signals received by oocytes from granulosa and theca cells profoundly decreased with age (Fig. 3e). In contrast, the interaction number and strength from all cell types to epithelial cells increased (Fig. 3e and Extended Data Fig. 7h).

We further identified the signaling pathways that exhibited a significant difference in communication probability between young and aged ovaries. In total, we identified 46 pathways with significant differential communication probability with age (Fig. 3f). The COLLAGEN and FN1 (fibronectin) pathways, core components of ECM biology, showed higher communication probability in most cell types in young ovaries (Fig. 3f), suggesting an essential role of the ECM in maintaining ovarian function. Interestingly, epithelial cells were the only type with higher COLLAGEN and FN1 signaling probability in aged ovaries (Fig. 3f). As these pathways promote epithelial cell proliferation24,25, this may explain the increased proportion of epithelial cells in aged ovaries (Fig. 2a,b). Conversely, cell adhesion pathways (JAM, PARs and NCAM) showed higher communication probability in most cell types in aged ovaries (Fig. 3f). The PDGF pathway, involved in fibroinflammatory processes, was significantly enriched in all cell types in aged ovaries (Fig. 3f), aligning with elevated fibroinflammatory cytokines found in aged human ovarian follicular fluid26.

Communication between granulosa cells and oocytes is crucial for oocyte maturation and ovulation. FSH and GDF signaling, central to follicle development and growth27, was abundant in young granulosa cells and oocytes but depleted in aged cells (Fig. 3f–h). Consistently, the expression of related receptors and key targets also declined in aged granulosa cells (Fig. 3i,j). The loss of FSHR and the concomitant decrease in steroidogenic enzyme expression suggest reduced ovarian responsiveness to gonadotropins. Our results show that human ovarian aging involves significant changes in cellular communication among oocytes and somatic cells, potentially contributing to age-related loss of follicular function, tissue fibrosis and epithelial hyperplasia.

Master TFs largely determine cell identity. As loss of cell identity with age has been implicated in age-related tissue dysfunction, we identified cell identity-associated TFs and investigated for age-related changes in their activity (Methods) using snATAC-seq data. As expected, individual cell types were distinguishable by predicted motif activity (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Table 8). Motifs of folliculogenesis-related TFs, mainly AP-1 family members (FOS, FOSL1, FOSL2, JUNB and JUND) and RUNX TFs (RUNX2 and RUNX3)28, were predominantly enriched in granulosa cells (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 8a). Steroidogenesis-related TFs29 were mainly enriched in granulosa cells and theca cells (Extended Data Fig. 8b), confirmed by TF footprinting analysis (Extended Data Fig. 8c). ETS family TFs, central regulators of endothelial and immune cells30, were enriched in these cell types (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 8d). To identify TFs governing human ovarian aging, we compared the predicted motif activity between young and aged cells in each cell type (Supplementary Table 9). Surprisingly, CCAAT/enhancer binding protein (C/EBP) motif activities significantly increased in most cell types except immune and epithelial cells (Fig. 4c). Among this family, CEBPD was highly expressed across cell types, with expression changes aligning with motif activity changes during ovarian aging for granulosa, immune and theca cells (Fig. 4c,d). Most cell identity-associated TFs exhibited significantly decreased motif activity, whereas epithelial cell identity-associated TFs exhibited significantly enhanced motif activity during ovarian aging (Fig. 4c). We calculated cell identity scores in each cell type by examining the expression level of the top 100 cell-type-specific genes (Methods). Young ovarian cells exhibited high expression of their corresponding cell-type-specific genes and minimal expression of others, except in smooth muscle and epithelial cells (Extended Data Fig. 8f). Surprisingly, aged granulosa, immune and theca cells expressed low levels of their cell-type-specific genes and high levels of stromal-cell-specific genes (Extended Data Fig. 8f), suggesting widespread loss of cell identity in aged ovaries.

To build cell-type-specific TF regulatory networks for human ovarian aging, we first constructed cis co-accessibility networks (CCANs) in each cell type (Methods). We defined the putative enhancers and promoters by overlaying the CCAN peaks with human ovary tissue enhancer and promoter annotations from the ENCODE database31. Genes with CCAN peaks in their putative promoters were defined as CCAN linked (Extended Data Fig. 9a). We identified varying numbers of CCANs and CCAN-linked genes for each cell type (Extended Data Fig. 9b). Most cell-type-associated DEGs and aging-associated DEGs significantly overlapped with CCAN-linked genes in all cell types (Extended Data Fig. 9c), suggesting that CCANs play essential roles in determining cell identity and ovarian aging regulation. For each cell type, we built the aging-associated TF regulatory networks governed by the top TFs (n = 3–4) based on predicted motif activity. Aging-associated DEGs with accessible peaks and motifs of these TFs in their promoters or enhancers were defined as candidate targets (Extended Data Fig. 9a). The resulting networks revealed a critical role for CEBPD in human ovarian aging (Fig. 4e–k). CEBPD target genes were enriched in key aging processes, including mTOR signaling, MAPK signaling and cellular senescence, across multiple cell types (Extended Data Fig. 9d).

The most recent comprehensive ANM GWAS identified 290 ANM-associated genetic risk loci8. Using public gene expression datasets, Ruth et al.8 reported significant enrichment of ANM-associated variants in genes expressed in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells. To further dissect specific cell types in the ovary enriched with ANM-associated variants, we performed MAGMA32 analysis using our snRNA-seq datasets. For comparison, we also included GWAS data from ovarian epithelial cancer (OEC) and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). We found that ANM-associated variants were significantly enriched in almost all cell types (Fig. 5a), indicating a systemic effect of aging on the ovary. In contrast, OEC-associated and PCOS-associated variants were enriched in epithelial and granulosa cells, respectively (Fig. 5a). To investigate cell-type-specific chromatin accessibility regions enriched for ANM-associated variants, we performed cell-type-specific linkage disequilibrium (LD) score regression33 using our snATAC-seq dataset. Consistently, ANM-associated variants showed significant enrichment in multiple cell types (Fig. 5b). To gain insights into how ANM-associated variants contribute to ovarian aging, we performed post-GWAS analyses to identify functional regulatory variants and affected target genes. Using 290 ANM-associated GWAS lead variants from Ruth et al.8, we compiled co-inherited variants based on LD (R2 ≥ 0.8) calculated from 1000 Genomes European ancestry genotypes (Methods), identifying 5,555 ANM-associated variants (Supplementary Table 10). To identify the functional variants that may affect transcriptional regulatory activity in each cell type, we overlapped ANM-associated variants with the putative enhancers and promoters that we identified in each cell type. This revealed 101 candidate functional variants belonging to 62 lead variants (Supplementary Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 11) with many shared across cell types (Fig. 5c). We repeated the analysis using TOPMed34 as an alternative annotation, thus identifying 9,687 ANM-associated variants (Supplementary Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 12) and 231 candidate functional variants (90 lead variants) (Supplementary Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 13). Consistently, many TOPMed variants were also shared across cell types (Supplementary Fig. 1b).

Next, we focused on putative functional variants in the 1000 Genomes dataset that were shared in at least four cell types, among which six variants occurred in DNA damage response (DDR)-related gene loci (Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 10a–e). The DDR is the major pathway linked to ovarian aging as detected by ANM GWAS8, and our results highlight the functional role of ANM-associated variants in DDR regulation across major ovarian cell types. For example, the rs3741605-T allele, associated with delayed ANM (beta > 0; Supplementary Table 14), is located in the putative promoter of the HELB gene. This gene encodes DNA helicase B, which negatively regulates homologous recombination35 and is active in most cell types (Fig. 5d). Previously, multiple missense variants predicted to be deleterious were identified in HELB that are associated with later ANM8. To explore the potential mechanisms underlying the influence of variants on gene expression, we employed a gapped k-mer support vector machine (SVM)-based approach (Methods) to predict the effect of candidate functional variants on TF binding activity. We found that the delayed ANM-associated T allele of rs3741605 could diminish TF binding activity (Fig. 5e). Remarkably, expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) analysis from the GTEx database19 indicated that rs3741605-T was significantly correlated with decreased expression of HELB in the human ovary (Fig. 5f). This suggests that rs3741605-T may contribute to delayed ANM by downregulating the expression of a critical DNA repair regulator, thereby conferring improved genome maintenance.

We also identified functional regulatory variants with predicted deleterious effects on ANM. The variant rs13263296 is located in the DEPTOR locus, a key gene involved in mTOR signaling (Fig. 5g). The T allele of rs13263296 is associated with early ANM (beta < 0; Supplementary Table 16) and occurs in the putative transcription start site (TSS)-proximal enhancer of the DEPTOR gene in most cell types (Fig. 5g). Mechanistically, rs13263296-T may affect DEPTOR expression by enhancing TF binding activity (Fig. 5h). We observed significant upregulation of DEPTOR expression in aged granulosa and epithelial cells (Fig. 5i). Of note, rs13263296-T was not significantly associated with increased DEPTOR expression in the human ovary (Fig. 5j), perhaps due to the use of bulk tissue in the eQTL analysis. We also identified several functional regulatory variants located in mTOR signaling and oxidative phosphorylation-related gene loci (Extended Data Fig. 10f,g).

We further performed functional analysis using the human stem cell model (Fig. 6a) to establish the causality of identified HELB variant rs3741605 in regulating HELB expression. We identified a coding variant, rs4430553, in HELB exon 2 that was ideally linked (R2 = 0.99) to rs3741605 in the European population (Fig. 6a). Theoretically, HELB mRNAs carrying uracil (U) at the rs4430553 locus were transcribed from the rs3741605-T allele, whereas mRNAs carrying cytosine (C) at the rs4430553 locus were transcribed from rs3741605-C allele, which allowed quantification of allele-specific HELB expression in heterozygous cell lines using digital polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (Fig. 6a). We first identified a human embryonic stem cell (hESC) line (H9-hESCs) and an induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) line derived from IMR90 fibroblasts (IMR90-iPSCs) that were HELB-heterozygous carriers as the amounts of two alleles at the rs3741605 or rs4430553 locus were identical in genomic DNA (Fig. 6b,c). We then differentiated H9-hESCs and IMR90-iPSCs into diverse ovarian cell types, including endothelial, smooth muscle, stromal and granulosa cells and analyzed allele-specific HELB expression. Strikingly, we found significantly less mRNA with T than mRNA with C at the rs4430553 locus in all cell types (Fig. 6b,c), indicating lower HELB transcriptional activity for the rs3741605-T allele. Consistently, cumulus granulosa cells derived from four donors carrying the heterozygous alleles in the rs3741605 and rs4430553 loci exhibited lower expression of the T allele compared to the C allele (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Table 15). GWAS variants can affect the gene expression regulatory networks by altering the DNA methylation level of regulatory elements. We incorporated the human ovary methylation quantitative trait loci (mQTL) dataset36 into our ovary-centric prioritization to assess whether prioritized variants are associated with mQTL in human ovary (Supplementary Fig. 2a). We found that 52 variants are associated with mQTL (Supplementary Table 17), further supporting their regulatory role. Additionally, we performed a comprehensive post-GWAS annotation using public epigenomic datasets, including single-cell ATAC-seq data from diverse human tissues. This generic approach identified 1,260 candidate causal regulatory variants, with 513 top candidates (Supplementary Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 17). In comparison, our ovary-centric approach yielded 101 high-confidence regulatory variants associated with ANM, demonstrating the value of our tissue-specific methodology in accurately identifying and prioritizing causal variants and regulatory mechanisms underlying ANM genetic risk.

Our integrated analyses revealed the global effects of ANM-associated non-coding variants on gene expression across ovarian cell types and nominated functional regulatory ANM risk variants that may dysregulate genes involved in pathways of relevance to the canonical hallmarks of aging, such as mTOR and genome maintenance.

In summary, our single-nuclei multi-omics analysis of young and reproductively aged ovaries provides a comprehensive, high-resolution atlas of the transcriptional and epigenetic regulatory landscape in human ovarian aging at single-cell resolution. This resource uncovers unique features of ovarian aging, such as the hyperactivity of mTOR observed across most ovarian somatic cell types with age. Our dataset and analyses offer valuable insights into potential interventions, suggesting that geroprotectors targeting the basic biology of aging, such as mTOR signaling, may be promising avenues to delay female reproductive aging. Furthermore, our integrative post-GWAS analyses of ANM generate a rich resource of candidate functional variants and their putative target genes, providing biological insights into the role of inherited non-coding variants in human ovarian aging. This comprehensive atlas expands understanding of genetic variation influencing ANM and serves as a valuable resource for future functional studies of non-coding genetic variation affecting ovarian aging. Ultimately, this multi-omics resource points toward potential therapeutic targets for improving female reproductive health and longevity.

This study provides a comprehensive single-nuclei multi-omics atlas of young and reproductively aged human ovaries, offering valuable insights into the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying ovarian aging. In our study, we used normal ovarian tissue from healthy donors, providing a unique reference atlas of human ovarian aging. Nevertheless, given the relatively small sample size, a larger study with more tissue samples across the reproductive lifespan and various menstrual cycle stages is needed to provide more comprehensive datasets. For clarity, we termed donors in their 20s as ‘young’, whereas ‘reproductively aged’ refers to donors in peri-menopausal and menopausal stages. Our analysis reveals coordinated transcriptomic and epigenomic changes across ovarian cell types during aging, with mTOR signaling emerging as a prominent ovary-specific aging pathway.

Our findings align with and expand upon previous studies. We observed reduced granulosa cell proportions in aged ovaries, consistent with earlier reports of decreased proliferation and increased apoptosis in these cells37. This depletion appears conserved between human and mouse ovarian aging38. Although oocytes were not detected in our datasets due to technical limitations39,40, previous single-cell RNA-seq studies showed decreased expression of DNA repair and mitochondrial function-related genes in aged human oocytes23,41, aligning with our observations in ovarian somatic cells. Previous gene expression studies in human granulosa cells noted decreased mitochondrial ATP synthesis genes42, mirroring our pathway activity analysis results. Existing data have also shown increased oxidative stress in aged primate and human granulosa cells43.

Our analysis revealed an increase in CDKN1A+ cells in aged ovaries across multiple cell types, with upregulation of HIF-1 signaling in these cells. However, it is important to note that the presence of cellular senescence in the human ovary requires further experimental validation44. Interestingly, a recent mouse study reported increased senescence markers in only a small subpopulation of cells, specifically type 17 lymphoid cells38. This difference highlights potential species-specific variations in ovarian aging mechanisms. The relationship between hypoxia and cellular senescence is complex. Although in vitro study suggests that hypoxic conditions could extend lifespan by inhibiting senescence45, this is conducted at oxygen levels not representative of in vivo conditions. Conversely, patients with chronic hypoxia-related diseases show increased cellular senescence and elevated HIF-1 levels46,47, indicating potential promotion of senescence by chronic hypoxia in vivo through HIF-1 signaling. Further research is needed to clarify this complex relationship in the context of ovarian aging. In our analyses, CDKN1A+ cells showed upregulation of several SASP genes across multiple cell types, although this was not consistent across all cell types, aligning with a previous report of cell-type-specific SASP expression48.

We identified CEBPD as a potential key regulator of aging-associated gene expression across ovarian cell types. Interestingly, CEBPD activity showed divergent patterns between cell types, possibly reflecting differences in cellular microenvironments and stress responses. CEBPD can be induced by cellular stress, such as hypoxia49. We observed decreased vascularity in aged ovaries, suggesting a hypoxic environment. The anatomical positioning of immune cells and epithelial cells in the ovary makes them less likely to be in hypoxic regions, potentially explaining the unique pattern of CEBPD motif activity in these cells during ovarian aging.

Our integration of genetic variants associated with ANM with our multi-omics data provides insights into potential regulatory mechanisms. We nominated over 100 candidate functional regulatory variants likely affecting ovarian aging. Furthermore, we prioritized variants based on coordinated effects across cell types and involvement in ovarian aging pathways. Using this biologically guided approach, we identified ANM-associated variants situated in DDR gene loci, including rs3741605. Through allele-specific expression analysis in ovarian cell models, we experimentally validated the predicted regulatory effects and directionality of rs3741605-T in reducing HELB expression. However, we acknowledge that establishing causality requires further investigation. Variant knock-in approaches and consideration of other variants in strong LD are needed to definitively prove the causal relationship.

In conclusion, our study provides a valuable resource for understanding the cellular, molecular and genetic basis of human ovarian aging. We identified upstream factors (for example, CEBPD) and downstream effectors (for example, mTOR signaling) contributing to ovarian aging. Furthermore, we provide experimental evidence supporting the rs3741605 variant in regulating HELB expression. The coordinated changes that we observed across cell types and the identification of mTOR as an ovary-specific aging pathway offer potential avenues for therapeutic interventions to promote ovarian health and longevity. However, we emphasize that our findings represent associations, and further mechanistic studies with larger sample sizes, protein-level validations and functional assays are required to confirm causality and translate these insights into clinical applications. As research in this field progresses, we anticipate that our multi-omics atlas will serve as a foundation for developing strategies to extend female reproductive lifespan and improve overall health outcomes for aging cis-gender women and people with ovaries.

We collected publicly available human single-cell RNA-seq data from the Chan Zuckerberg CELLxGENE Discover database and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), focusing on normal tissues from women in two age groups: 20–30 years (young) and 45–55 years (aged), which are similar to the age ranges in our ovarian study. To ensure a fair comparison and minimize potential batch effects, we selected datasets for each tissue type that originated from the same study having single-cell transcriptome profiles of both young and aged samples. This approach yielded eight single-cell RNA-seq datasets from human tissues, including adipose (https://cellxgene.cziscience.com/collections/6b701826-37bb-4356-9792-ff41fc4c3161), blood (https://cellxgene.cziscience.com/collections/ced320a1-29f3-47c1-a735-513c7084d508), brain (https://cellxgene.cziscience.com/collections/cec4ef8e-1e70-49a2-ae43-1e6bf1fd5978), breast (https://cellxgene.cziscience.com/collections/48259aa8-f168-4bf5-b797-af8e88da6637), liver (https://cellxgene.cziscience.com/collections/74e10dc4-cbb2-4605-a189-8a1cd8e44d8c), lung (https://cellxgene.cziscience.com/collections/38833785-fac5-48fd-944a-0f62a4c23ed1) and esophagus (https://cellxgene.cziscience.com/collections/a18474f4-ff1e-4864-af69-270b956cee5b) from the CELLxGENE database and bone marrow from the GEO (GSE120221).

For each human tissue, we assessed the coordination of transcriptomic changes during aging across cell types by calculating pairwise Pearson correlations based on the FCs in gene expression between young and aged samples. The Pearson correlation coefficient was computed using the ‘cor’ function in R (version 4.3.1).

To estimate pathway activity scores for individual cells, we used the AddModuleScore function in Seurat51, based on 21 aging hallmark-associated pathways from the Molecular Signatures Database57 (version 7.4.1). The module scores were normalized using the z-score method and used to indicate pathway activity. To compare pathway activity between young and old tissues in each cell type, we used the limma package58 (version 3.48.3) to contrast the activity scores. Bonferroni-adjusted P values were used to determine significance at Padj < 0.05. The log2(FC) was used to quantify the magnitude of change in pathway activity during aging.

To prioritize the aging hallmark pathways that may govern the tissue aging, two metrics were assigned for each pathway: (1) an amplitude index, defined as the mean absolute change in pathway activity across cell types, and (2) a tissue prevalence ratio, defined as the proportion of cell types exhibiting significant change in pathway activity. Pathways demonstrating both high amplitude and high prevalence ratio are likely to exert significant influence on the process of tissue aging, as their effects extend beyond cellular aging to impact the tissue as a whole. We identified pathways with a prevalence ratio of at least 0.7 as tissue aging prevalent pathways.

To identify aging-associated genes, we used the RNA-seq datasets from GTEx version 8 for all female tissues, excluding the ovary. For each tissue, we performed linear regression using the limma package58 (version 3.48.3) to identify genes whose expression was significantly associated with age. Multiple testing correction was applied using the Bonferroni method, and genes with Padj < 0.05 were considered significant.

We applied Cicero59 (version 1.3.4.11) to generate CCANs for each cell type. In brief, the Signac object for each cell type was converted to the CellDataSet format and then made into a Cicero object. The algorithm assigned the cells into many groups, each group comprising 50 cells similarly positioned in clustering space. Graphical LASSO was used to calculate the correlations in adjusted accessibilities between all pairs of ATAC peaks within 500 kb. Finally, CCANs were identified through community detection.

For a given TF, the ovarian aging-associated DEGs whose promoters or putative enhancers contained both accessible peaks and motifs of the certain aging-associated TFs within the peaks were defined as candidate targets of the selected TFs. We used this information to construct a directed TF regulatory network using Gephi60 (version 0.9.2).

For snRNA-seq data, we estimated the association of gene-level GWAS trait association statistics with gene expression specificity in a given cell type using EWCE61 (version 1.0.0) to calculate gene expression specificity. MAGMA.Celltyping62 (version 1.0.0) was used to calculate quantile groups for each cell type. GWAS variants were annotated onto their neighboring genes (extended 10 kb upstream and 1.5 kb downstream). Finally, MAGMA32 (version 1.08) was used to test for a positive association between cell type specificity and gene-level associations (one-sided test, P < 0.05).

For the snATAC-seq data, we used ldsc33 (version 1.0.1) to annotate variants overlapping ATAC peaks per cell type. Partitioned LD scores were estimated using annotated files, HapMap single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and 1000 Genomes phase 3 PLINK data. Stratified LD score regression assessed annotation contributions to GWAS trait heritability (P < 0.05).

GWAS summary statistics for age at menopause8,63,64, PCOS65,66 and OEC67–69 were downloaded from the GWAS catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/) or ReproGen (https://www.reprogen.org/) and re-formatted with MungeSumstats (version 1.3.5) or munge_sumstats.py in the ldsc package.

ANM-associated lead variants were obtained from the GWAS by Ruth et al.8. LD proxies (R2 ≥ 0.8) were identified using 1000 Genomes phase 1 (ref. 70) or TOPMed34 European ancestry data. LD was calculated using PLINK71 version 1.90 (1000 Genomes) or TopLD72 (TOPMed). Proxies were merged with lead variants.

Putative functional variants were identified by overlapping ANM variants with cell-type-specific ovarian enhancer and promoter annotations, defined by intersecting CCANs with enhancer and promoter regions from ENCODE31. Variants were filtered to those shared across at least four cell types and ranked based on localization in regions controlling ovarian aging-related genes/pathways.

To incorporate DNA methylation data, regulatory variants were intersected with human ovarian mQTL from Oliva et al.36, providing insights into variants influencing gene regulation by altered methylation.

ANM-associated lead variants were obtained from Ruth et al.8. LD proxies (R2 ≥ 0.8) were identified in 1000 Genomes European ancestry data and merged with lead variants.

To prioritize functional variants, ANM variants were overlapped with predicted cis-regulatory elements from public databases, including DNase I hypersensitivity regions from the ENCODE database31, annotated promoters and enhancers in EpiMap73, ENCODE candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCREs) of the SCREEN database (https://screen.encodeproject.org) and open chromatin peaks from CATlas74. Variants were further filtered by predicted TF binding alterations (RegulomeDB75 (ranking ≥ 2b), ENCODE31 TF ChIP-seq).

Putative functional variants were intersected with GTEx version 8 eQTL (49 tissues, P < 5 × 10−8) to identify variants associated with altered gene expression.

To predict TF binding activity score, we overlapped ovary ATAC peaks with human ovary tissue enhancer and promoter annotations from the ENCODE database, yielding 71,470 putative regulatory regions as the positive set. Random length and GC–matched genome sequences served as the negative set. We used LS-GKM (version 0.1.1)76 to train the TF binding model with ‘gkmrbf’ kernel. For variants of interest, we retrieved ±25-bp reference sequences and generated alternative sequences by replacing the 25th position with the effect allele. DeltaSVM scores were computed using all possible 11-mers scored by the trained model. GkmExplain77 was used on reference and alternative sequences, with scores visualized using logomaker (version 0.8) (https://github.com/jbkinney/logomaker).

To build cell–cell interactions between somatic cells and oocytes in young and aged human ovaries, we used CellChat22 (version 1.1.3) to infer the cell–cell interactions based on the expression of known ligand–receptor pairs in different cell types with a combination of our snRNA-seq datasets and publicly available human oocyte single-cell RNA-seq datasets23 from reproductive young and aged females. For data integration, we used the merged raw count data from our somatic cell snRNA-seq and the external oocyte scRNA-seq, using this as input for CellChat. We employed CellChat’s internal normalization method to standardize the data before inferring cell–cell interactions specific to the young and aged ovarian samples. Subsequently, we applied the ‘rankNet’ function in CellChat to identify key outgoing and incoming signaling pathways significantly enriched in the young or aged ovaries, respectively.

No statistical methods were used to pre-determine sample sizes, but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications38,43. No randomization method was used. Data collection and analysis were not performed blinded to the conditions of the experiments. Low-quality nuclei were excluded from both snRNA-seq and snATAC-seq analyses based on pre-defined quality control metrics to ensure data reliability. Data distribution was assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.1) or GraphPad Prism 9.5 with the corresponding statistical test. Specific details of statistical methods are provided in the relevant subsections of the Results and in figure legends.
