INTRODUCTION: Lennox Gastaut syndrome (LGS) can be conceptualised as a 
"secondary network epilepsy", in which the shared electroclinical manifestations 
reflect epileptic recruitment of a common brain network, despite a range of 
underlying aetiologies. We aimed to identify the key networks recruited by the 
epileptic process of LGS using interictal 2-deoxy-2-(18F)fluoro-D-glucose 
positron emission tomography (18F-FDG-PET).
METHODS: Group analysis of cerebral 18F-FDG-PET, comparing 21 patients with LGS 
(mean age = 15 years) and 18 pseudo-controls (mean age = 19 years), studied at 
Austin Health Melbourne, between 2004 and 2015. To minimise the influence of 
individual patient lesions in the LGS group, we only studied brain hemispheres 
without structural MRI abnormalities. The pseudo-control group consisted of age- 
and sex-matched patients with unilateral temporal lobe epilepsy, using only the 
hemispheres contralateral to the side of epilepsy. Voxel-wise permutation 
testing compared 18F-FDG-PET uptake between groups. Associations were explored 
between areas of altered metabolism and clinical variables (age of seizure 
onset, proportion of life with epilepsy, and verbal/nonverbal ability). 
Penetrance maps were calculated to explore spatial consistency of altered 
metabolic patterns across individual patients with LGS.
RESULTS: Although not always readily apparent on visual inspection of individual 
patient scans, group analysis revealed hypometabolism in a network of regions 
including prefrontal and premotor cortex, anterior and posterior cingulate, 
inferior parietal lobule, and precuneus (p < 0.05, corrected for family-wise 
error). These brain regions tended to show a greater reduction in metabolism in 
non-verbal compared to verbal LGS patients, although this difference was not 
statistically significant. No areas of hypermetabolism were detected on group 
analysis, although ∼25 % of individual patients showed increased metabolism 
(relative to pseudo-controls) in the brainstem, putamen, thalamus, cerebellum, 
and pericentral cortex.
DISCUSSION: Interictal hypometabolism in frontoparietal cortex in LGS is 
compatible with our previous EEG-fMRI and SPECT studies showing that interictal 
bursts of generalised paroxysmal fast activity and tonic seizures recruit 
similar cortical regions. This study provides further evidence that these 
regions are central to the electroclinical expression of LGS.

1. Introduction 
Lennox-Gastaut syndrome (LGS) is a severe, childhood-onset epi -
lepsy phenotype characterised by frequent, treatment-resistant seizures 
and generalised interictal epileptiform discharges ( Gastaut et al., 1966 ). 
The EEG shows runs of 1.5 – 2.5 Hz diffuse slow spike-and-wave (SSW) 
and bursts of generalised paroxysmal fast activity (GPFA), particularly 
during non-REM sleep ( Arzimanoglou et al., 2009 ). Multiple seizure 
types can be present, but tonic seizures, which cause patients to sud -
denly stiffen and drop to the ground, are a prominent feature. Onset is 
usually in early life (peak ~3 – 5 years) and > 90 % of patients continue 
to have seizures into adulthood ( Arzimanoglou et al., 2009 ). LGS is 
classified as a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy, as it appears 
that both the epileptic process (i.e., seizures and interictal epileptiform 
discharges) and the underlying patient-specific aetiologies of LGS can 
pervasively impair cognition and cognitive development ( Scheffer et al., 
2017 ). Children with LGS frequently show cognitive stagnation or 
regression at seizure onset, whilst older patients almost always show 
moderate to severe degrees of cognitive impairment ( Camfield, 2011 ). 
A variety of pathogenic processes can cause LGS. ~25 – 50 % of pa -
tients have epileptogenic lesions on structural MRI, including focal, 
multifocal, or diffuse abnormalities, both congential and acquired 
( Arzimanoglou et al., 2009 ). Various genetic abnormalities, particularly 
de novo mutations, are increasingly being identified ( Project and Con -
sortium, 2013 ), but a significant proportion of patients have no known 
cause. Despite these diverse aetiologies, patients with LGS show similar 
electroclinical features, suggesting the epileptic process is driven by 
common brain areas ( Archer et al., 2014a ). Our EEG-fMRI studies have 
shown that generalised paroxysmal fast activity, a characteristic inter -
ictal epileptiform discharge of LGS, recruits widespread areas of frontal 
and parietal “ association cortex ” , including the frontoparietal control 
network (FPCN) ( Archer et al., 2014b; Warren et al., 2019 ). These 
studies have led to the conceptualisation of LGS as a ‘secondary network 
epilepsy ’ , in which the electroclinical features reflect expression of 
epileptic activity through shared brain networks, rather than reflecting 
individuals ’ specific genetic, lesional, or other aetiologies. 
2-deoxy-2-(
18
F)fluoro-D-glucose positron emission tomography (
18
F- 
FDG-PET) can reveal pervasive metabolic abnormalities in brain regions 
involved in the epileptic process ( Engel et al., 1982 ). Tracer uptake 
occurs over approximately 30 min, capturing average brain glucose 
metabolism over an interictal period, usually showing hypometabolism 
of epileptogenic brain regions. For example, focal hypometabolism is 
often seen in patients with focal cortical dysplasia ( Harvey et al., 2015 ), 
and can guide focal surgical resection ( Macdonald-Laurs et al., 2021 ). In 
patients with temporal lobe epilepsy, lobar hypometabolism lateralises 
to the side of the epileptic focus and predicts response to temporal lo -
bectomy ( Wong et al., 2010, 1996 ). In occipital lobe epilepsy, the extent 
of 
18
F-FDG-PET hypometabolism in the adjacent temporal lobe is greater 
in patients with electroclinical evidence of temporal lobe involvement 
during seizures ( Wong et al., 2014 ). In summary, 
18
F-FDG-PET hypo -
metabolism can reflect both the seizure onset zone and the wider cere -
bral networks through which seizures are expressed. 
Previous 
18
F-FDG-PET studies in LGS have used visual inspection or 
semi-quantitative methods to identify focal, regional, or diffuse patterns 
of hypometabolism ( Ferrie et al., 1997a, 1996; Chugani et al., 1987; 
Ferrie et al., 1997b ). This approach can identify major metabolic 
changes, including focal drivers of the epileptic process. However, it 
may be less effective at identifying subtle changes in metabolism, 
particularly if these are diffuse or symmetrically distributed. Such 
changes can be more readily visualised by whole-brain statistical anal -
ysis, comparing scans from a group of patients to those from a group of 
controls, to reveal altered metabolic patterns shared across the patient 
group. 
Here, we aimed to build upon our previous EEG-fMRI findings 
( Archer et al., 2014b; Warren et al., 2019 ) by testing whether LGS in -
volves altered glucose metabolism within shared ‘secondary network ’ 
brain areas hypothesised to drive epilepsy and cognitive impairment in 
this syndrome. We retrospectively compared 
18
F-FDG-PET scans be -
tween LGS patients and an age- and sex-matched group of ‘pseudo- -
controls ’ (patients with unilateral temporal lobe epilepsy). We 
hypothesised that LGS patients would show altered 
18
F-FDG-PET uptake 
maximally affecting frontoparietal cortex. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Study design and ethics 
This was a single-site retrospective study of patients who underwent 
18
F-FDG-PET between 2004 and 2015, at Austin Health Melbourne, 
Australia. Potential subjects were identified through the database of the 
Austin Health comprehensive epilepsy programme, with the cohort 
refined according to inclusion and exclusion criteria listed below. The 
Human Research Ethics Committee at Austin Health approved this study 
(HREC-47410-Austin-2018) and waived the requirement for informed 
consent due to the study ’ s retrospective nature. 
2.2. Patients with Lennox-Gastaut syndrome 
We included patients with an electroclinical diagnosis of LGS defined 
by: (i) tonic seizures captured on prior video-EEG monitoring, or clearly 
reported by a reliable eyewitness; (ii) slow spike-and-wave (SSW) dis -
charges on routine interictal scalp EEG; and (iii) generalised paroxysmal 
fast activity (GPFA) on routine interictal scalp EEG recorded during 
sleep. All LGS patients fulfilling these criteria and who received FDG- 
PET over the study period at our institution were included. To avoid 
imaging results being contaminated by the effects of structural abnor -
malities, we only included patients in whom at least one structurally 
‘normal ’ brain side was available for analysis. As such, patients with 
bilateral structural abnormalities (e.g., tuberous sclerosis, double cor -
tex) or bilateral prior surgeries were excluded and, in those with a focal 
brain lesion, only the structurally normal hemisphere was studied. A 
total of 21 patients were included (mean age = 15 years, range = 4 – 45 
years; 9 females and 12 males; mean seizure onset age = 3.2 years, 
range = 0.25 – 12 years; mean duration of epilepsy = 12 years, 
range = 0.8 – 38 years). Table 1 contains detailed demographic and elec -
troclinical characteristics for each patient. 
Cognitive and behavioural functioning was variable across patients, 
although the majority (17/21) showed evidence of moderate or severe 
global impairment ( Table 1 ). Formal neuropsychological assessment 
results were available in 5/21 patients, revealing full-scale intelligence 
quotient (FSIQ) scores ranging from 40 to 69. In the remaining patients, 
detailed assessments were either unavailable or not performed due to 
the severity of impairment. 
To facilitate a post-hoc exploratory association of 
18
F-FDG-PET 
metabolism and cognitive functioning, we used clinical notes and de -
scriptions to categorise patients into either “ nonverbal ” (i.e., unable to 
verbally communicate using any language) or “ verbal ” (i.e., able to 
verbally communicate using at least some words or sentences) sub -
groups. Although a crude index of cognitive functioning, verbal ability is 
a key adaptive domain commonly measured by standardised neuro -
psychological assessment tools tailored to patients with intellectual 
disability ( Thompson et al., 2018 ). In summary, 7/21 (33 %) patients 
with LGS were classified as nonverbal, whereas the remaining 14/21 (66 
%) were able to communicate at least some verbal language.

2.5. Structural MRI 
A whole-brain T1-weighted structural MRI scan was acquired for 
each participant, at 1.5 T in 29/39 participants and at 3.0 T in the 
remaining 10/39. All participants ’ MRI scans were resampled to a 
common spatial resolution of 0.9 mm × 0.9 mm × 0.9 mm prior to 
analysis. These scans were used for clinical characterisation (e.g., 
identification of structural abnormalities) and to guide spatial normal -
isation of 
18
F-FDG-PET scans to template space. 
2.6. Pre-processing of imaging data 
Image pre-processing was performed using a custom pipeline 
employing FMRIB Software Library version 6.0.1 ( Jenkinson et al., 
2012 ), Advanced Normalisation Tools version 2.3.2 ( Avants et al., 
2011 ), MRtrix software version 3.0, and Freesurfer software version 
7.1.1 ( Dale et al., 1999 ). Code to reproduce all image pre-processing 
steps is available: https://github.com/aaewarren/LGS-PET. 
Each participant ’ s 
18
F-FDG-PET scan was spatially normalised to a 
symmetric brain template (Montreal Neurological Institute [MNI]   152 
6th-generation symmetric brain template; 2 mm voxel resolution) using 
a rigid-body alignment to their T1-weighted MRI scan and a non-linear 
warp to template space ( Avants et al., 2011 ). A symmetric brain tem -
plate was used to facilitate the hemisphere flipping procedure described 
below, yielding a common unilateral analysis space. 
To avoid bias due to structural MRI abnormalities in 12/21 patients 
with LGS (e.g., cortical lesions or prior surgical resections; see Table 1 ), 
and due to the use of unilateral TLE patients as pseudo-controls, we 
included only structurally “ normal ” brain hemispheres in our analysis. 
This involved defining a common unilateral analysis space for both 
groups. For the TLE pseudo-controls, we deleted the hemisphere ipsi -
lateral to the side of their epilepsy and then flipped the contralateral 
hemisphere to appear on the same image side for all subjects. This was 
also performed for the 12/21 LGS patients with structural MRI abnor -
malities (i.e., the abnormal hemisphere was deleted, and the contralat -
eral hemisphere flipped). For the 9/21 LGS patients without 
abnormalities on either brain side, we performed this hemisphere 
deletion/flipping procedure in a randomised fashion to yield approxi -
mately equal proportions of participants in the LGS and pseudo-control 
groups for whom the unilateral analysis space represented an “ originally 
right ” hemisphere or an “ originally left ” hemisphere. 
Intensity normalisation was applied to each resulting unilateral 
18
F- 
FDG-PET image by dividing each voxel by the mean uptake within a 
white matter mask ( Borghammer et al., 2008 ). These masks were 
created by (i) segmenting cerebral white matter from the T1-weighted 
MRI ( Dale et al., 1999 ), (ii) applying the deletion/flipping procedure 
described above to this segmentation, and (iii) eroding using a 5 mm 
radius sphere to avoid non-white matter voxels. After intensity nor -
malisation, spatial smoothing was applied using a Gaussian kernel with 
full-width-at-half-maximum of 8 mm. 
2.7. Between-group comparison 
A two-sample t- test design was used to assess differences in 
18
F-FDG- 
PET metabolism between LGS and pseudo-control groups, restricted to 
the unilateral analysis space described above. The analysis was per -
formed using voxel-wise non-parametric permutation testing (10,000 
permutations) implemented within Permutation Analysis of Linear 
Models (PALM) software version alpha117 ( Winkler et al., 2016 ). A 
nuisance covariate for participant age (in years at time of PET) was 
included in the design matrix submitted to PALM. Two contrasts were 
examined: LGS > pseudo-controls (i.e., “ hyper-metabolism ” in LGS) and 
LGS < pseudo-controls (i.e., “ hypo-metabolism ” in LGS). Threshold-free 
cluster enhancement ( Smith and Nichols, 2009 ) was employed for sta -
tistical inference, with significance assessed at a voxel-wise threshold of 
p < 0.05, corrected for family-wise error (FWE) across the two contrasts 
using the -corrcon option in PALM ( Winkler et al., 2016 ). 
Using a similar approach, we additionally tested for differences in 
18
F-FDG-PET metabolism between the left and right TLE pseudo-control 
participants, restricted to the unilateral analysis space involving only 
hemispheres contralateral to the TLE side. No differences were observed 
( p > 0.05, FWE-corrected), and thus this TLE sub-group comparison is 
not described further.

3. Results 
3.1. Individual-level analyses 
Visual inspection - whole brain: Routine visual inspection of LGS 
patients ’ scans revealed patterns similar to Chugani ’ s ( Chugani et al., 
1987 ) four metabolic subtypes ( Fig. 1 and Table 1 ), at times reflecting 
locations of known structural MRI abnormalities. Unilateral focal 
hypometabolism was seen in 1/21 (5 %) LGS patient, in the same 
location as a focal cortical dysplasia. A more diffuse but unilateral 
hypometabolism was reported in 6/21 (28 %) patients; in two of these, 
the hypometabolism included locations of cortical lesions. Bilateral 
diffuse hypometabolism was the most prevalent pattern, seen in 12/21 
(57 %) of patients and most commonly involving frontal, parietal, and 
temporal lobes. In 2/21 (10%) patients, unremarkable/normal meta -
bolic patterns were reported. 
3.2. Quantitative analysis – “ Structurally normal hemisphere ” 
Comparing individual LGS patients ’ structurally “ normal ” 
hemispheres to the mean metabolic pattern of the pseudo-control group 
( Fig. 2 ) revealed that ≥ 50% of patients had relative hypometabolism 
involving much of the midline and lateral frontal cortex, anterior insula, 
lateral posterior temporal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, 
and lateral parietal cortex. Interestingly, a similar proportion of patients 
showed relative subcortical hyper metabolism in the brainstem and pu -
tamen, a pattern which was not detected in the group-level comparison 
described below. A smaller proportion, approximately 25 %, also 
showed relative hypermetabolism in the dorsomedial cerebellum, 
lateral nuclei of the thalamus, and para-central lobule; again, these in -
creases were not detected in the group-level analysis. We repeated the 
individual-level quantitative analysis in the two patients with LGS who 
had a focal structural abnormality as their only known aetiology (pa -
tients #5 and #7 in Table 1 ). This revealed similar patterns of hypo -
metabolism in the patients ’ non-lesional hemispheres (relative to 
pseudo-controls) to that seen in the penetrance map described above 
( Supplementary Fig. 1 ).

4. Discussion 
Patients with LGS display 
18
F-FDG-PET hypometabolism in a com -
mon set of brain regions, maximally involving frontal and parietal cor -
tex. These regions spatially overlap with key locations of the 
frontoparietal control network (FPCN), a major cerebral network nor -
mally responsible for higher-order cognitive functions including goal- 
directed behaviour ( Marek and Dosenbach, 2018 ). These metabolic al -
terations were seen at the group level despite individual patients having 
a wide variety of underlying causes, suggesting the shared electro -
clinical features of LGS are integrally related to this network. The lo -
cations of hypometabolism show similarity to cortical regions we 
previously found to be active in LGS patients during bursts of general -
ised paroxysmal fast activity studied with EEG-fMRI ( Warren et al., 
2019 ) and during tonic seizures studied with ictal-interictal subtraction 
SPECT ( Intusoma et al., 2013 ). Furthermore, we previously found 
abnormal resting-state fMRI connectivity between nodes of the FPCN, 
the FPCN and other cortical networks ( Warren et al., 2016 ), and be -
tween the thalamus and FPCN ( Warren et al., 2017a ). Hence, this study 
adds to a convergent body of evidence that the FPCN may be a final 
common pathway through which LGS is expressed. As neuromodulation 
becomes an increasingly important therapeutic option, accurate defini -
tion of the networks maximally recruited by an epilepsy syndrome takes 
on new significance, so that therapy can be targeted for maximal effect 
( Warren et al., 2022; Dalic et al., 2022 ). 
Previous 
18
F-FDG-PET studies in LGS classified patients into broad 
metabolic ‘subtypes ’ ( Ferrie et al., 1996; Chugani et al., 1987 ). These 
subtypes were visually defined by the spatial distribution of metabolic 
defects. Such a classification scheme can be helpful in selecting patients 
who may benefit from targeted surgical interventions, for example when 
a pattern of focal hypometabolism may aid in identifying a resectable 
cortical lesion ( You et al., 2007 ) or when diffuse unilateral hypo -
metabolism may indicate a role for corpus callosotomy or hemispher -
ectomy ( Chugani et al., 1987; Kang et al., 2018 ). Consistent with 
previous reports, routine visual inspection of our patients ’ scans 
revealed a variety of patterns similar to Chugani ’ s four subtypes ( Fig. 1 ) 
( Chugani et al., 1987 ), at times reflecting the locations of known lesions. 
In contrast to previous studies which have sought to identify patient- 
specific aetiologies, here we sought to identify areas of altered meta -
bolism that are common across LGS patients, and hence which are likely 
to be central to the electroclinical expression of LGS. To achieve this, we 
excluded patients ’ structurally abnormal hemispheres from our analysis 
and performed a group-level comparison against pseudo-controls, 
enhancing our sensitivity to subtle or diffuse metabolic alterations 
that may not be readily appreciated on routine visual inspection alone. 
This analysis revealed a shared pattern of hypometabolism in the FPCN, 
a core network underpinning human cognition. Importantly, FPCN 
hypometabolism involved the structurally ‘normal ’ brain hemisphere in 
LGS patients with unilateral lesions, consistent with this functional 
disturbance reflecting an extended network alteration, rather than an 
effect of overlapping lesion locations. 
Hypometabolism extending beyond the location of epileptogenic 
lesions is often seen in patients with focal lesional epilepsy, where it is 
interpreted to reflect epileptic recruitment of focal networks ( Casse 
et al., 2002 ). The pathophysiology of this functional-structural 
mismatch is unclear, with suggested structural mechanisms including 
seizure-induced neuronal loss or impaired neurogenesis ( Hattiangady 
and Shetty, 2008 ); deafferentation of otherwise structurally intact brain 
areas connected to the lesion; and damage to pathways involved in 
seizure propagation, reducing synaptic density ( Henry et al., 1994 ). 
However, the observation that these remote areas of altered metabolism 
can sometimes improve following removal of the epileptogenic lesion 
( Spanaki et al., 2000 ) suggests at least a partial role of functional 
mechanisms. Possibilities include seizure-induced disruptions of brain

of network dynamics would be consistent with our previous finding of 
abnormal fMRI connectivity patterns in LGS ( Warren et al., 2016 ) which 
can improve after successful epilepsy surgery in some individuals 
( Warren et al., 2017b ). 
The FPCN is an intrinsic network in the human brain that underpins 
key cognitive functions including executive control ( Marek and Dos -
enbach, 2018 ). Hence, diffuse hypometabolism of this network might be 
expected to show some relationship with the cognitive deficits seen in 
LGS. Indeed, a previous study ( Ferrie et al., 1997b ) in 32 children with 
various forms of developmental and epileptic encephalopathy 
(including 16 with LGS) found an inverse relationship between patients ’ 
adaptive behaviour scores and 
18
F-FDG-PET metabolism within the 
frontal lobes. Similarly, our results suggested that non-verbal patients 
with LGS tended to have lower FPCN metabolism than verbal patients 
( Fig. 4 C), although this difference was not statistically significant. The 
lack of significance may reflect our small sample size and/or potential 
confounding factors impacting cognition such as patient-specific aeti -
ologies of LGS or medications and prior treatment (e.g., surgery). 
Furthermore, we only examined metabolism in patients ’ non-lesional 
hemispheres, whereas cognitive profiles are dependent on the integ -
rity of both hemispheres. 
We did not observe significant areas of interictal hyper metabolism in 
LGS at the group level. However, on individual patient analysis, 
increased metabolism (relative to pseudo-controls) was seen in 
~25 – 50% of patients with LGS in the brainstem, cerebellum, putamen, 
thalamus, and pericentral cortex ( Fig. 2 ). As EEG was not routinely 
recorded during scanning, we cannot exclude the possibility that some 
of the metabolic changes we observe reflect subclinical seizure activity 
or very frequent interictal discharges. However, a key advantage of the 
group analysis approach is that the impact of any one individual (eg: 
with frequent discharges) on the group level result is minimised. Inter -
ictal focal cortical hypermetabolism is occasionally reported in children 
with lesional focal epilepsy ( Schur et al., 2018 ), atypical focal 
self-limited epilepsy ( Curnow et al., 2020 ) and epileptic encephalopathy 
with continuous spike and waves during sleep (EE-CSWS) ( Jenkinson 
et al., 2012 ), possibly reflecting sustained interictal epileptiform activ -
ity, subclinical seizures, or “ active ” inhibitory processes around the time 
of 
18
F-FDG uptake. Additionally, brainstem and lentiform nucleus hy -
permetabolism has been reported in infants with Infantile Spasms 
( Chugani et al., 1992 ), who commonly develop LGS in childhood, 
possibly reflecting brainstem-striatal involvement in epileptic spasms. 
Diffuse cortical hypometabolism (maximal in frontal and parietal asso -
ciation cortex) with preserved/increased subcortical metabolism can 
also be seen in Alzheimer ’ s disease ( Herholz et al., 2002 ). However, in 
the absence of absolute quantitation, apparent hypermetabolism may 
occur as a consequence of image intensity normalisation, where PET 
values are scaled by a reference region that is itself hypometabolic 
( Chugani, 2021 ). We attempted to mitigate this issue by normalising 
patients ’ scans using the mean signal within a mask of white matter, 
which did not significantly differ between the LGS and pseudo-control 
groups. Nevertheless, it is possible that in this sub-group of LGS pa -
tients, metabolism in subcortical and pericentral cortex may merely 
have been ‘relatively preserved ’ , rather than absolutely increased.
