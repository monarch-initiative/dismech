Abstract  28 
Successful approaches for eradication or cure of HIV -1 infection are likely to include 29 
immunological mechanisms, but remarkably little is known about how human imm une responses 30 
can recognize and interact with the few HIV-1 infected cells that harbor genome-intact viral DNA, 31 
persist long-term despite antiretroviral therapy and represent the main barrier to a cure. For a long 32 
time regarded as being completely shielded from host immune responses due to viral latency, these 33 
cells do, on closer examination by single -cell analytic techniques, display discrete footprints of 34 
immune selection, implying that human immune responses may be able to effectively engage and 35 
target at least some of these cells. The failure to eliminate rebound-competent virally-infected cells 36 
in the majority of persons may reflect the evolution of a highly selected pool of reservoir cells that 37 
are effectively camouflaged from immune recognition or rely on sophisticated approaches for 38 
resisting immune-mediated killing. Understanding the fine -tuned interplay between host immune 39 
responses and viral reservoir cells will help to design improved interventions exploiting the 40 
immunological vulnerabilities of HIV-1 reservoir cells. 41

[H1] Introduction 44 
Despite more than 35 years of active research, HIV -1 infection remain s one of the very few 45 
infectious diseases for which no curative t herapy is available; clinical care for the approximately 46 
39 million persons living with HIV-1 today1, therefore, relies on chronic disease management with 47 
indefinite antiretroviral suppression treatment  (ART). Li fe-long persistence of replication -48 
competent HIV -1 is  mostly related to a small pool of infected CD4 T cells that harbor a 49 
chromosomally-integrated, genome-intact version of HIV -1 DNA as a “provirus”  and are 50 
unresponsive to currently -available antiretroviral agen ts. The frequency of these infected cells , 51 
commonly also referred to as “viral reservoir cells”, is very small, typically ranging from 1:10,000 52 
to 1:100,000 CD4 T cells 2-4; they seem to be seeded early in the disease process, likely within the 53 
first days after infection 5-7, and predominantly reside in lymphoid tissues 8, although they can be 54 
readily detected in the peripheral blood, wh ere they can be most easily accessed  for analytic 55 
purposes. Notably, only a small proportion (typically <10%) of all HIV -1-infected CD4 T cells 56 
harbor a genome -intact and replication -competent copy of HIV -1 DNA; the vast majority of 57 
proviruses detectable in ART-treated persons harbor lethal genetic defects that are most frequently 58 
acquired during viral reverse transcription and preclude viral replication 4,9-11. HIV-1-infected cells 59 
from ART-treated persons typically express limited or undetectable levels of viral transcripts. Such 60 
a condition, frequently termed “viral latency ”, has been  attributed to the reversion of activated 61 
virally-infected CD4 T cells to a resting state during which viral gene transcription is ineffective ; 62 
moreover, direct infection of resting CD4 T cells may also occur 12,13. However, low-level viral 63 
transcription, frequently involving short, abortive viral transcripts, remains detectable in a notable 64 
proportion of cells , even after years of viral suppression  with ART14-18. Immune cells other than 65 
CD4 T cells, in particular infected macrophages 19,20 and microglia cells 21, may also contribute to 66 
viral persistence, at least in a subset of persons living with HIV -1; however, the biology of such 67 
infected cells is not as well characterized. 68 
Most approaches for targeting and eliminating  HIV-1 reservoir cells focus on immunological 69 
mechanisms, based on the premise that in  very rare individuals, the frequency of cells harboring 70 
intact HIV-1 DNA is extremely low (sometimes below 1 in a billion peripheral blood mononuclear 71 
cells22,23 (PBMC)), presumably due to host immune mechanisms that have effectively eliminate 72 
many or most HIV-1 reservoir cells. Such a condition can be associated with sustained drug -free 73 
control of HIV -1 infection  in specific individuals described as “elite controllers”  (EC)22, “ elite 74 
suppressors” (ES)24 or post-treatment controllers” (PTC) 25. These profound outliers of the ordinary 75 
HIV-1 disease course can be viewed as evidence that the human immune system is, at least in 76 
principle, able to  effectively control and/or eliminate HIV -1 reservoir cel ls. However, the 77

[H1] Longitudinal dynamics of HIV-1 reservoir cells 94 
Longitudinal studies  to evaluate the evolution of HIV -1 reservoir cells under physiological 95 
conditions in ART-treated persons represent one key approach to clarify their biological behavior, 96 
and to understand their  possible susceptibilities and vulnerabilities to host immune effects. Over 97 
the recent years, PCR technologies became available that  permit to quantify genome-intact, and 98 
likely replication-competent, HIV-1 proviruses in single-molecule resolution; these assays rely on 99 
near full -length individual proviral next-generation sequencing, which allows to 100 
biocomputationally distinguish genome-intact HIV-1 DNA from the much larger pool of proviruses 101 
that display lethal genetic defects  4,10,11,30. As a simpler version of full -genome sequencing, the 102 
intact proviral DNA assay (IPDA) has been developed, which focuses on the simultaneous 103 
detection of two small proviral DNA fragments as surrogate markers  permitting to infer genome-104 
intactness, frequently with appropriate accuracy31. Using these PCR -based assays  for the 105 
quantitative enumeration of intact proviruses , recent stu dies suggest pronounced differences 106 
between the longitudinal evolution of intact versus defective proviruses 2,32-34 (Figure 2A ). In 107 
particular, intact proviruses seem to undergo a multi -phasic kinetic change during suppressive 108 
antiretroviral therapy 35, beginning with  a short initial phase of relatively rapid decline  (with a 109 
proviral half-life of several weeks) after ART initiation. Subsequently, there is a second, somewhat 110 
longer phase of more limited decline (with a proviral half-life of approximately 19 months), which 111 

5 
 
is succeeded by a third more durable phase of slow proviral decline (proviral half-life of 3-4 years) 112 
and a fourth phase that becomes detectable after extended periods of time and is variably associated 113 
with an intact proviral reservoir frequency that either continues to slowly decline, plateaus,  or 114 
increases36-39. In contrast, defective proviruses display a small monophasic decline early after ART 115 
initiation but subsequently remain remarkably stable 35. For both intact and defective proviruses, 116 
these longitudinal patterns are influenced by clonal proliferation of infected T cells, a process that 117 
occurs in response to T cell stimulation with cognate antigens40,41 or cytokine signals42 and during 118 
which identical viral copies are passed on to daughter cells , frequently leading to large clusters of 119 
sequence-identical proviruses originating from the same parental cell. Proviruses in such clonally-120 
expanded T cells can be readily detected in almost all persons livi ng with HIV-14,34,43-45, and 121 
typically increase during prolonged durations of antiretroviral therapy, resulting in a proviral 122 
landscape increasingly dominated by large proviral clones and characterized by lower overall 123 
complexity and diversity. Such a pattern is particularly obvious after long-term ART (>20 years) 124 
and may explain paradoxical increases of intact proviruses described during very long ART 38,39. 125 
Together, t hese experimental results support the following  conclusions: (i) The more profound 126 
decline of intact proviruses , relative to defective proviruses , likely reflects active immunological 127 
elimination of cells harboring intact HIV -1, and does not simply represent a passive consequence 128 
of natural cell death that would invariably affect cells harboring intact or defective proviruses35; (ii) 129 
viral genome-intactness enhances the susceptibility and vulnerability  of infected cells  to these 130 
immune effects, likely because cells with intact proviruses can be better recognized  and targeted 131 
by immune cells  or are more susceptible to viral cytopathic effects ; (iii) clonal proliferation  of 132 
infected cells can antagonize the longitudinal decline of reservoir cells , likely because high-level 133 
cell turnover may outcompete host immune activity against infected cell s; (iv) during chronic 134 
steady-state antiretroviral therapy , virally-infected cells seem poorly-susceptible to host immune 135 
activity or actively resist immune elimination;  (v) susceptibility of individual proviruses to host 136 
immune effect s seem to vary considerably, with some infected cell clones able to persist and 137 
expand, while others appear to be eliminated; and (vi) the true dynamics of the reservoir cells can 138 
be best assessed by assays able to deconstruct the clonal composition of the reservoir . Of note, 139 
some persons living with HIV -1 display disproportionately strong decreases in the frequency of 140 
intact proviruses after ART initiation , resulting in extremely low frequencies of viral reservoir 141 
cells46,47; such i ndividuals are o f particular interest for studying mechanisms influencing viral 142 
reservoir cell decline. The types of immune responses influencing viral reservoir cell dynamics 143 
represent an active area of research and will be discussed later in this manuscript.  144

[H1] Proviral integration sites as biomarkers of immune selection  146 
Beyond the qua ntitative changes in viral reservoir dynamics outline d above, a remarkable 147 
longitudinal evolution of  the viral reservoir cell pool became evident when the specific 148 
chromosomal locations of proviruses were taken into consideration. Analysis of s uch qualitative 149 
changes in the reservoir profile has been greatly facilitated by the introduction o f techniques 150 
permitting to investigate HIV-1 integration sites in conjunction with the corresponding near full-151 
length proviral sequence s in ex vivo assessments of participant-derived cells  (Box 2). This 152 
approach, termed “matched integration site and proviral sequencing”  (MIP-Seq)48 by us and 153 
“multiple-displacement-amplification-single-genome sequencing” (MDA -SGS)49 by others , 154 
catalyzed efforts to selectively map the chromosomal locations of genome-intact proviruses and to 155 
evaluate their specific positions relative to complex genetic, structural, architectural and epigenetic 156 
features of the host genome. Such studies can deconvolute the clonal complexity of HIV-1 reservoir 157 
cells in great detail and have led to the following observations:  158

[H2] Accumulation of intact proviruses in heterochromatin regions: While HIV-1 strongly prefers 160 
integration into euchromatin during acute infection50,51, typically within introns of highly-expressed 161 
genes, detailed cross-sectional and longitudinal investigations conducted in our lab  demonstrated 162 
that intact proviruses persisting during prolonged ART tend to accumulate in chromosomal regions 163 
associated with heterochromatin features38,48 (Figure 2B). Such evolution was selectively observed 164 
for genome-intact proviruses and most likely occurs as a result of  host immune selection pressure 165 
that may preferentially target and eliminate intact proviruses in more accessible chromatin, 166 
presumably due to higher residual proviral transcriptional activity that exposes these cells to host 167 
immune recognition. Indeed, multi-dimensional single-cell assays14 designed to interrogate proviral 168 
transcription in conjunction with the respective chromosomal integration site demonstrated that 169 
proviruses in accessible chromatin, and in linear or three -dimensional proximity to activating 170 
histone modifications (such as H3K4me1, H3K4me3 and H3K27ac) frequently remained 171 
transcriptionally active, although they predominantly express short, abortive viral RNA transcripts; 172 
expression of these transcripts may increase the immunological vulnerability of viral reservoir cells 173 
and expose these cells to immunological selection pressure. In contrast, our work demonstrated that 174 
proviruses integrated into heterochromatin r egions typically failed to produce any type of viral 175 
RNA, a state that we referred to as “deep latency” and that may allow proviruses to remain 176 
effectively camouflaged from host immune recognition, conferring a longitudinal selection 177 
advantage14. Proviral transcriptional r epression and signs of “deep latency” were also noted for 178

[H2] Chromosomal “sanctuary” sites for the persistence of intact HIV-1: A closer investigation of 182 
the HIV-1 integration site landscape in our studies identified specific genomic hotspots which seem 183 
to be particularly well adjusted to promote and support persistence of intact HIV -1 (Figure 2C). 184 
Such regions include centromeric and peri-centromeric DNA, which are typically characterized as 185 
condensed heterochromatin regions and encompass large segments of non -coding satellite DNA 186 
that is strongly enriched for epigenetic transcriptional repressors 56,57. Moreover, so-called “gene 187 
deserts”, DNA segments located in far distance from accessible chromatin and with poor access to 188 
host transcriptional machinery, can also serve as preferred sites for persistence of intact HIV-1. In 189 
addition, members of the zinc finger (ZNF) family, including the Krüppel-associated box (KRAB) 190 
domain–containing ZNF (KRAB-ZNF) genes, emerged as frequent sites for persistence  of intact 191 
HIV-1 DNA22,38,48,58. These genes are enriched with repressive H3K9me3 histone marks over their 192 
gene bodies , deposited through histone methyltransferase recruitment via the heterochromatin 193 
protein-1 ( HP1)59, the KRAB -associated protein -1 ( KAP1)60 and the Human Silencing Hub 194 
(HUSH) complex61, which plays an important role in the silencing of endogenous and exogenous 195 
retroviruses62-64. These ZNF genes are frequently located in “lamina-associated domains” that touch 196 
the inner side of the nuclear membrane and typically display heterochromatin features65. However, 197 
not all ZNF genes demonstrate heterochromatin features, and the exact mechanisms facilitating 198 
viral persistence at these sanctuary sites are still actively investigated.  199

[H2] Deep latency and “blocked and locked” reservoir profiles in drug-free controllers of HIV-1: 201 
Studies from our lab suggested that s election of intact proviruses in heterochromatin regions and 202 
other “sanctuary” sites is arguably most frequent in elite controllers, a rare subgroup of people who 203 
durably control HIV-1 to undetectable levels in the absence of antiretroviral therapy22,66. Moreover, 204 
an enrichment of intact proviruses in heterochromatin has recently been demonstrated in post-205 
treatment controllers38, individuals who maintain drug-free control of HIV-1 after discontinuation 206 
of ART. Thes e results are best explained by highly-functional immune responses that can drive 207 
effective selection of proviruses in heterochromatin. Most likely, the high proportion of proviruses 208 
in “blocked and lock ed” positions  limits the ability of proviruses to drive viral rebound and  209 
contributes to the ability to maintain drug-free control of HIV-1.  210

[H2] Persistence of highly transcriptionally-active proviruses. In apparent violation of the immune 212 
selection mechanisms descr ibed before , numerous investigators have reported proviruses that 213 
remain highly transcriptionally active , but persist long-term and seem to resist immune-mediated 214 
elimination, despite strong production of viral RNA that putatively increases their visibility to host 215 
immune recognition 14,67. Such transcriptionally -active proviruses are typically integrated in 216 
proximity to activating chromatin features and can, in some cases, fuel low-level plasma viremia 217 
that cannot be suppressed by antiretroviral agents 68-70. While these proviruses may simply 218 
outcompete negative host selection mechanisms through elevated cell -intrinsic proliferation that 219 
repopulates the clonal pool size , they may also display other cellular  or viral features that reduce 220 
their susceptibility to host immune activity.  221

[H2] Functional assessments of the depth of proviral latency: Of note, intact proviruses integrated 223 
in heterochromatin locations can be reactivated in v iral outgrowth assays  (VOA)22,38, which 224 
typically use maximal T cell stimulation over 2-3 weeks of in vitro culture; this indicates that intact 225 
proviruses in tegrated in  heterochromatin are not in an irreversible state of latency, and that 226 
epigenetic blocks to proviral gene expression can likely be overcome with maximal cellular in vitro 227 
activation. However, in vitro viral outgrowth is a highly stochastic process9,13,71,72, partially because 228 
cells in which viral reactivation has been successfully induced are more likely to die than to fuel 229 
exponential viral growth 73, an observation supported  by the repeated observation that only 230 
approximately 1 in 50-100 intact proviruses (identified by molecular techniques) are detectable in 231 
in vitro VOAs9,39. Therefore, differences in “depth of latency” and  susceptibility to physiological 232 
reactivation signals that occur in vivo may be difficult to evaluate with in vitro tissue culture assays. 233 
Accordingly, qualitative changes in the viral reservoir profile during long -term ART remained 234 
undetectable when in vitro  viral outgrowth assays were used 39. While providing a quantitative 235 
assessment of the minimum number of replication -competent viruses that are present in a given 236 
person, we argue that in vitro viral outgrowth assays may mask qualitative immune selection effects 237 
on viral reservoir cells and be less informative for identifying their immunological vulnerabilities.  238

Together, these complex assessments of the proviral  integration site profiles unraveled evidence 240 
for a dynamic evolutionary process in the viral reservoir cell pool that remained largely inapparent 241 
when purely quantitative markers of viral reservoir cells were analyzed. In particular, chromosomal 242 
integration site analysis suggested that, while being able to integrate almost anywhere in the human 243 
genome, intact proviruses can only persist at highly selected heterochromatin regions during long-244 
term suppressive ART, strongly suggesting that intact HIV-1 proviruses are surprisingly vulnerable 245 

9 
 
to host immune activity and subject to p reviously-unrecognized immune selection pressure. We 246 
propose that longitudinal changes in proviral integration sites can be interpreted as high-resolution 247 
biomarkers of host immune effects against the viral reservoir cell pool and may, in the future, be 248 
considered as additional virological endpoints in clinical trials aiming at HIV -1 eradication and 249 
cure74. That said, we acknowledge that current technologies for analyzing integration sites of intact 250 
proviruses can only capture a small subset of all proviruses in each study participant, implying that 251 
minority clones of intact proviruses can frequently not b e sufficiently identified. Future 252 
technological improvements in next -generation sequencing techniques may permit higher 253 
throughput analysis of HIV -1 chromosomal locations  and facilitate further advances in our 254 
understanding of the complex dynamic changes occurring within the HIV-1 reservoir cell pool.  255

[H1] Immune responses associated with HIV-1 reservoir cell evolution 257 
What immune responses are driving selection of H IV-1 reservoir cells  that became apparent 258 
through proviral integration site profiling ? Although we currently seem far away from 259 
understanding the mechanisms underlying this immune selection process , we here provide a brief 260 
summary of host immune responses that may influence HIV -1 reservoir cell dynamics . We 261 
acknowledge that our discussion is biased towards antiviral immun e responses in the peripheral 262 
blood, reflecting the fact that most currently-available studies focused on circulating immune cells; 263 
recent progress in analyzing antiviral immune activity in situ in specific anatomic compartments75-264 
77 will help to characterize tissue-immune responses in greater detail in the future.   265 
 266 
[H2] Innate and cell-intrinsic immune responses: Several studies suggest that NK cells, the main 267 
effector cells of the innate immune system, can modulate HIV-1 reservoir cell dynamics, and be of 268 
possible relevance for immune targeting of these cells. Statistical associations between NK  cells 269 
and a decrease in the frequency of HIV -1 reservoir cells were documented both in ART -treated 270 
adults78 and children79,80. Moreover, NK cells were associated with changes in the HIV-1 reservoir 271 
cell pool during treatment with interferon- (IFN-81,82 and with the histone deacetylase inhibitor 272 
panobinostat83. Correspondingly, specific activating phenotypic NK cell markers were identified 273 
that correlate with biomarkers of HIV-1 persistence in ART-treated persons84. A role of NK cells 274 
for targeting HIV/SIV reservoir cells is also supported by animal studies; for instance, allogeneic 275 
NK cells seemed to reduce the viral reservoir size when administered in combination with latency-276 
reversing agents to humanized mice 85, and NK cells were associated with reductions of SIV -277 
infected cells in ART-suppressed non-human primates during treatment with IFN - and IL-2186. 278

The ability of NK cells to target HIV-1 reservoir cells in in vitro cultures following latency reversal 279 
has also been demonstrated 87. In addition to NK cells, prior pre -clinical and clinical studies 280 
suggested that HIV-1 reservoir cells are sensitive to type I interferon responses, and some degree 281 
of HIV-1 DNA reduction was observed during treatment with exogenous I FN-; in part, this 282 
may be explained through latency-reversing effects of IFN-90 and by direct stimulation of innate 283 
effector cell responses 81. Correspondingly, rebound viremia after ART interruption seems 284 
frequently resistant to IFN -, which indirectly suggest s that autologous type I interferon may 285 
contribute to restriction of viral outgr owth when ART is stopped. Cell -intrinsic type I interferon 286 
responses may also contribute to  selective induction of cell death in infected cells, possibly 287 
following recognition of viral RNA transcript by the cytoplasmic sensor RIG-I92. In addition, recent 288 
results suggest that the CARD8 inflammasome can sense HIV -1 protease, initiating selective 289 
activation of CARD8 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis in infected cells93.  290

[H2] Adaptive cellular immune responses: HIV-1-specific T cell responses have , for a long time, 292 
dominated the analysis of protective immune responses against HIV -1, mostly due to their 293 
presumed role as the main mediators of natural drug -free control in elite controllers 94. However, 294 
whether HIV -1-specific T cells from EC can indeed drive selection of intact proviruses in 295 
heterochromatin positions in EC or other PLHIV remains to be determined. Of note, induction of 296 
virus-specific cytotoxic T cells  (CTL) with a CMV-vectored vaccine is one of the very few 297 
interventions that can induce clearance of virally-infected cells in a subset of non-human primates 298 
inoculated with SIV, the simian immunodeficiency virus closely related to HIV 95. Further 299 
supporting a possible activity of cytotoxic T cells against viral reservoir cells, correlative studies in 300 
humans have demonstrated inverse associations between cytotoxic T cell  responses and HIV-1 301 
DNA levels in two studies 96,97, both of which analyzed samples from acute infection.  In contrast, 302 
efforts to identify associations between stronger HIV -specific T cells and a more pronounced 303 
decline of HIV-1 reservoir cells in PLHIV initiating ART during chronic infection have failed so 304 
far; in fact, two studies found positive associations between the frequencies of nef-specific CD8 T 305 
cell responses and the markers of reservoir cells98,99, possibly suggesting that ongoing viral antigen 306 
expression from viral reservoir cells  during ART may stimulate a subset of T cell responses. 307 
Experimental depletion  of CTL in acute infection did not affect SIV reservoir seeding in non-308 
human primates started on ART in early infection 100, raising further doubts that CD8 T cells can 309 
effectively reduce viral reservoir cells. Reasons for their inability to efficiently target viral reservoir 310 
cells may relate to insufficient antigen presentation – short abortive viral transcripts that have been 311 
repeatedly observed in PLHIV on suppressive ART  are unlikely to be effectiv ely translated and 312 

11 
 
presented via the MHC class I complex . Moreover, while some tissue culture experiments 313 
suggested that HIV-1-specific T cells can restrict HIV -1 reservoir cells101,102, more recent studies 314 
have emphasized that HIV -1 reservoir cells actually express molecular features that increase 315 
resistance to CTL -mediated killing 103,104. Finally, provocative investigations suggest that CD8 T 316 
cells can mediate an MHC class I-independent suppressive effects on HIV-1 gene transcription105, 317 
which may help to camouflage viral reservoir cells from immune recognition and stabilize viral 318 
latency106,107. Together, these data suggest that the role of HIV-1-specific T cells as effective drivers 319 
of viral reservoir cell selection is at least questionable.  320

[H2] HIV-1-specific antibodies: Generally, autologous HIV-1 antibody responses are positively 322 
associated with HIV-1 DNA levels, both in pediatric study persons108 and in adults109,110, suggesting 323 
that they have no activity against viral reservoir cells. However, neutralizing antibodies may restrict 324

In conclusion, currently available experimental data are insufficiently conclusive for identifying 330 
immune mechanisms that drive HIV-1 reservoir cell selection, and the types of immune responses 331 
that can effectively engage and target viral reservoir cell s remain to be identified. Moreover, it is 332 
still unclear how HIV -1 reservoir cells are immunologically recognized, and why evidence for 333 
immune selection occurs predominantly in cells harboring intact proviruses – does this reflect an 334 
immune sensing mechanism that can actively distinguish intact from defective proviruses? 335 
Identifying immunological drivers of HIV-1 reservoir cell selection represents an important future 336 
research priority, as it may ultimately allow to desig n specific interventions for accelerating and 337 
improving immune targeting of HIV-1-infected cells during ART.  338

[H1] Resistance and immune evasion in the reservoir cell pool 340 
Immune-mediated killing of HIV-1-infected cells is a bi -directional process between effector and 341 
target cells, but this immunological interplay has so far been mostly studied fr om the perspective 342 
of antiviral immune responses. A direct ex vivo analysis of HIV-1 reservoir cells as th e opposite 343 
side of the immunological synapse may be highly informative, but investigating physiologic 344 
properties of virally-infected target cells in their unperturbed state was hampered in the past by the 345 
lack of appropriate analysis technologies able to capture the very rare virally-infected cells directly 346 

12 
 
ex vivo. Recent methodological progress in single -cell, high resolution evaluations of participant -347 
derived HIV-1 reservoir cells has enabled such studies, and permitted to gain rare insight into their 348 
transcriptional and phenotypic characteristics (Box 3). 349

[H2] Transcriptional profiling of HIV-1-infected cells: Pioneering studies to profile the single-cell 351 
transcriptional signatures in ex vivo isolated HIV-1-infected cells suggested viral persistence in 352 
Th1-polarized cytotoxic CD4 T cells, characterized by expression of granzyme  B114. Moreover, 353 
these cells tended to express higher levels of SERPINB9 and of the anti-apoptosis marker BCL-2, 354 
which may increase resistance of viral reservoir cells to granzyme B-mediated cytotoxic effects of 355 
CD8 T cells. Corresponding to these findings, related single-cell transcriptional profiling studies115 356 
detected intact HIV -1 primarily in effector-memory cells, which displayed higher numbers of 357 
transcripts related to the MHC class II  complex and the granzyme family of genes . Physical 358 
isolation of bulk CD4 T cells containing HIV-1 DNA through complex cell sorting ex periments 359 
enabled transcriptional profiling of HIV -1-infected cells from ART -treated persons in a separate 360 
study116; here, the authors found that HIV-1-infected cells were characterized by higher expression 361 
of transcripts associated with transcriptional repression of HIV-1, and a reduction in the number of 362 
transcripts involved in viral transcriptional activation . Furthermore, there was some evi dence for 363 
downregulation of transcripts related to death receptor signaling and anti -proliferative molecular 364 
pathways. Together, these studies suggested persistence of HIV -1 in cells displaying intrinsic 365 
features of survival and resistance to immune-mediated killing. 366

by innate and adaptive cellular immune responses and  translate into a selection advantage 383 
promoting the long -term persistence of HIV -1 reservoir cells with poor susceptibility to host 384 
immune activity. In lymph nodes, PheP-Seq experiments demonstrated persistence of intact HIV-385 
1 in follicular T helper cells and in resident-memory CD4 T cells, consistent with prior studies119,120; 386 
expression of markers associated with cell survival, in particular CD44, CD127 and IL-21 receptor 387 
appeared upregulated on cells harboring intact HIV-1 in this tissue compartment. Importantly, this 388 
study did not claim that these specific phenotypic characteristics are exclusively present on HIV-1 389 
reservoir cells, rather , these results suggested selective  viral persistence of intact HIV -1 in 390 
physiological cell subsets best adjusted to resist host immune activity  in the ir respective 391 
microenvironments in the peripheral blood or in lymphoid tissues. Of note, upregulation of specific 392 
immune checkpoint and activation markers were also observed when the cellular surface phenotype 393 
was evaluated after in vitro  induction of viral transcription  with latency re versing agents 121,122; 394 
however, these studies were frequently insufficiently powered to distinguish cells harboring intact 395 
or defective proviruses, and may not reflect the phenotypic profile of infected cells in vivo.  396

While these data suggest complex transcriptional and phenotypic signatures of viral reservoir cells, 398 
they also provide clues to understan ding why the immune system, in the vast majority of people 399 
living with HIV-1, fails to effectively eliminate and target viral reservoir cells: Instead of simply 400 
reflecting a passive consequence of viral latency and insufficient target cell visibility to  host 401 
immune recognition, viral persistence during ART may be related to a highly selected pool of viral 402 
reservoir cells that are optimally adjusted to host immune responses and display features conferring 403 
cell-intrinsic resistance to immune -mediated killing  (Figure 2C ). Paired with the described 404 
chromosomal integration site analysis, these studies provide technologically independent, yet 405 
complementary, evidence that viral reservoir cells are vulnerable to host immune activity and are 406 
subject to immune-mediated selection pressure. Consistent with such a view, elegant tissue culture 407 
experiments suggested resistance of HIV -1-infected cells from ART-treated people to immune -408 
mediated killing 103,104. We expect that the technical expansion of single -cell analytic approaches 409 
may, in the future , be helpful to better characterize immunological pathways contributing to the 410 
ability of HIV-1 reservoir cells to withstand and resist immune-mediated elimination.  411

[H1] Targeting immunological vulnerabilities of HIV-1 reservoir cells 413 

14 
 
A number of immunological interventions has now been tested in clinical trials for their ability to 414 
reduce HIV -1 reservoir cells, however, with rare exceptions 123,124, such in vestigations failed to 415 
leave a detectable footprint in the reservoir cell pool. Nevertheless, early-stage exploratory clinical 416 
trials are among the most informative studies to explore possible weaknesses and vulnerabilities of 417 
HIV-1 reservoir cells  in humans, and we expect that the future analysis of clinical trial samples 418 
using novel single -cell analysis techniques may permit to obtain deeper insight into the  immune 419 
susceptibilities of HIV-1 reservoir cells. Here, we outline the recent experience with immune-based 420 
clinical approaches for targeting HIV -1 reservoir cells and identify opportunities for next-421 
generation clinical interventions (Figure 3A).  422 
 423 
[H2] Latency-reversing agents  (LRAs). A whole pipeline of pharmacological agents have 424 
developed over these recent years in an effort to increase viral gene transcription and augment the 425 
immunological vulnerability of HIV -1-infected cells; the se therapeutics can loosely be grouped 426 
into agents that enhance transcriptional activity through epigenetic mechanisms (mainly including 427 
histone-deacetylase inhibitors and cytosine de -methylation agents) and pharmacological 428 
compounds that target specific host factors promoting viral transcription; the latter include protein 429 
kinase C agonists, SMAC mimetics, TLR agonists, bromodomain inhibitors  and STAT5 430 
SUMOylation inhibitors. Their specific pharmacological features have been well -characterized in 431 
an excellent recent review125. Some of these agents have been tested in clinical trials, frequently as 432 
part of “shock and kill” interventions designed to eliminate viral reservoir cells through  immune-433 
mediated mechanisms following latency reversal 26,27,29,126-131. However, the vast majority of these 434 
studies failed to demonstrate notable effects o n viral reservoir sizes, with the exception of a non -435 
randomized clinical study with recombinant IL -15 that r eported induction of viral RNA 436 
transcription, followed by a small but significant reduction of viral reservoir cells124. Limited effects 437 
of LRAs have been attributed to insufficient induction of viral transcription /translation or to the 438 
failure of immune mechanisms to effectively target cells in which successful viral recognition 439 
occurred. Despite such setbacks, the use of LRAs remains conceptually attractive to us , although 440 
we suggest to recalibrate expectations of what th ese agents are likely to be able to accomplish: In 441 
particular, we propose that the susceptibilities of individual viral reservoir cells to LRAs are likely 442 
to differ profoundly, and are probably modified by the chromosomal location of proviruses . 443 
Therefore, specific LRAs may only be able to influence a subset of proviruses located in chromatin 444 
locations more conducive to a given LRA mechanism  of action ; in parti cular, the subset  of 445 
proviruses in accessible euchromatin may be easiest to reactivate and most responsive to LRAs . 446 
Effects of LRA s may, therefore, become more evident when changes in proviral frequenc ies are 447 

15 
 
analyzed in subgroups of proviruses that share specific integration site features, or when 448 
chromosomal integration site positioning relative to defined genetic or epigenetic features are being 449 
considered as qualitative viral reservoir characteristics. In addition, the development of next-450 
generation LRAs that are specifically designed for clinical use in the context of HIV-1 cure research 451 
(as opposed to repurposing existing agents mostly developed for oncologic indications), have more 452 
limited toxicity and can be dosed repetitively over prolonged perio ds of time will likely be 453 
necessary. Such challenges would be best addressable by pharmaceutical companies with resources 454 
for large-scale compound screening, although some reagents evaluated in academic labs appear 455 
promising132.  456 
 457 
[H2] Inhibition of immune checkpoints and immunoregulatory pathways: Pharmacological 458 
targeting of immune checkpoint markers, successfully performed in certain oncologic diseases, has 459 
been considered as a strategy to target viral reservoir cells, based on the hypotheses that such 460 
immune checkpoint s may stabilize viral latency , inhibit antiviral cellular immune  responses, or 461 
increase resistance of viral reservoir cells to immune-mediated killing. However, such agents can 462 
be associated with considerable risks for immune -mediated adverse events ; therefore, clinical 463 
evaluations of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) have mostly been limited to PLHIV with 464 
malignant diseases  who received treatment with PD -1/PD-L1-blocking molecules, the most 465 
frequently used ICI, for oncologic indications. In such studies, mild-to-moderate increases of HIV-466 
1 RNA levels were reported, consistent with some degree of viral latency reversal133. Combination 467 
of PD -1 and CTLA -4 inhibiting agents appear ed to be more effective  for inducing HIV -1 468 
transcription, both in humans134 and in animal models135, and there is a strong rationale for testing 469 
alternative combinations of immune modulators,  particularly those  including inhibitors of IL -470 
10136,137, in animal models . Nevertheless, all human studies  involving ICI have failed to 471 
demonstrate consistent changes in the quantity of HIV-1 reservoir cells. In part, these results could 472 
be due to cellular immune deficiencies in the study participants who frequently had received 473 
extensive prior chemotherapy for oncologic indications; a study that tested PD -1 inhibiting agents 474 
in person s without concomitant oncologic disease s had to be premature ly terminated due to 475 
unacceptable toxicity 138. In the future, we env ision that the use of ICI and immune modulators 476 
could be more directly informed by immunophenotyping studies of markers that are expressed on 477 
autologous HIV-1 reservoir cells harboring intact HIV -1 proviruses; measurements of immune 478 
checkpoint surface expression on cancer cells are routinely used for precision medicine approaches 479 
in oncologic patients. Moreover, we expect that immunomodulatory targets other than PD-1 will 480 
be increasingly considered in HIV-1 cure research, including agents that induce more subtle effects 481 

16 
 
on unleashing immune activity and may possibly be associated with more limited side effects. In 482 
addition, focusing on pharmacological modulators of activating or inhibitory NK cell receptors may 483 
represent a promising opportunity in future studies.  484 
 485 
[H2] Modifications of cell-intrinsic immune activity and cell death pathways: Production of viral 486 
RNA in some infected cells may represent one  key vulnerability of viral reservoir cells , as these 487 
viral transcripts may be visible to cell-intrinsic immune surveillance. Yet, limited clinical activities 488 
are currently focused on developing strategies for enhancing cell -intrinsic immune recognition of 489 
HIV-1 RNA transcripts. Whether induction of type I interferon responses through pharmacological 490 
administration of recombinant IFN- or TLR7 /9 agonists can increase such immune responses 491 
remains to be determined; samples from recently conducted clinical trials 139 may allow to address 492 
this. Recent efforts focus on the development of pharmacological agents that increase  cell-intrinsic 493 
death pathways  in virally -infected cells , specifically those related to CARD8 -dependent 494 
inflammasome activation ; this process can be facilitated by modified non-nucleoside reverse 495 
transcriptase inhibitors that can activate CARD8 through accelerated viral protease dimerization140, 496 
or through chemical inhibition of DDP9 141, a negat ive chemical regulator of CARD8.  497 
Pharmacological modulation of cell survival pathways may also represent a promising strategy for 498 
destabilizing viral reservoir cell persistence, given that HIV -1-infected cells may rely on anti -499 
apoptotic molecules for surviving long-term; initial studies using in vitro and small animal models 500 
evaluating this approach appear encouraging 142,143. Attacking HIV-1-infected cells through 501 
CRISPR-Cas-based therapeutics 144 that may recognize viral DNA through specifically-designed 502 
guide RNAs represents a possible avenue for cell-intrinsic immune targeting of virally-infected 503 
cells, independently of their transcriptional activity.  504 
 505 
[H2] Modulating adaptive immun ity: Three different approaches are considered for targeting 506 
reservoir cells through adaptive immune responses: induction of HIV-specific T cell responses with 507 
therapeutic vaccines, development of  broadly-neutralizing antibodies , and T cells designed to 508 
incorporate a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR-T cells).  509 
[H3] Therapeutic vaccines: Several clinical trials have been conducted to target HIV -1 reservoir 510 
cells with therapeutic vaccines designed to induce HIV -specific T cell responses, either as single 511 
agents145-147 or in conjunction with latency -reversing agents28,148. However, non e of these studies 512 
resulted in detectable changes in the viral reservoir cell pool, suggesting that , in ART -treated 513 
persons, vaccine-induced HIV-1-specific T cells have limited abilities to mount significant immune 514 
activity against latently-infected CD4 T cells . However, some encouraging results were reported 515 

17 
 
with therapeutic vaccines targeting SIV reservoir cells in non -human primates 149,150. F uture 516 
modifications in HIV -1 vaccine de velopment, including the design of personalized vaccines 517 
involving autologous HIV-1 sequences, the specific targeting of “networked” CTL epitopes 151, or 518 
the induction of T cell responses restricted by non -classical HLA class I alleles152, may lead to 519 
improved ou tcomes; however, fundamental obstacles that limit the efficacy of T cell  immune 520 
responses, such as the  putatively low visibility of reservoir cells to T cells and the possible 521 
resistance of reservoir cells to T cell-mediated killing, will need to be addressed.  522 
[H3] Broadly-neutralizing antibodies: Exciting progress has been made over the recent years in 523 
evaluating combinations of broadly -neutralizing antibodies  (bnAb) as therapeutic agents for 524 
substituting standard small-molecule antiretroviral therapy123,153. Although pre-existing or de-novo 525 
evolution of escape mutations represent s a challenge for more widespread introduction of bnAbs, 526 
such treatment regimens may be attractive for specific sub groups of individuals, including young 527 
infants with difficulties adhering to oral treatment options 154. Effects of bnAbs on viral reservoir 528 
cells are actively being studied. Of particular note is one recent (non-randomized) study in which 529 
a significant reduction of intact proviruses during bn Ab treatment was reported 123; this effect is 530 
currently further explored in randomized-controlled clinical trials, involving bnAbs in combination 531 
with LRAs.  Mechanistically, b nAbs may be able to target infected cells through Fc -receptor-532 
mediated immune cell recruitment; such effects are presumably most effective against infected cells 533 
with ongoing or readily inducible viral envelope transcription/translation. Moreover, immune 534 
complexes between bnAbs and viral antigens may act as immunogens that stimulate dendritic cells 535 
and translate into an improved antiviral T cell response. This approach, first explored in elegant 536 
animal model studies 155 and later pursued in human clinical trials 156, suggests that collaboration 537 
between different component s of the innate and adaptive immune system may be necessary to 538 
improve targeting or controlling of viral reservoir cells.  539 
[H3] Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells: T cell s designed to express chimeric antigen 540 
receptors, typically composed of a bnAb-derived viral antigen recognition domain linked to  the 541 
intracellular zeta signaling chain  of the CD3 T cell receptor, represent an additional investigative 542 
treatment modality for attacking viral reservoir cells. Ideally, CAR-T cells  combine the strong 543 
cytotoxic effects of T cells with the broad antigen -recognition activities of bn Abs; however, 544 
successful engagement of reservoir cells with such CAR-T cells will likely be contingent upon 545 
effective expression of viral envelope antigens . Clinical studies with CAR-T cells were first 546 
pioneered as early as 20 years ago 157, but marked technical improvements over the recent decades 547 
have now enabled their routine use in patients with specific oncologic diseases. A clinical trial with 548 
an optimized CAR -T cell product for targeting HIV-1 reservoir cells  has recently been launched  549 

18 
 
(NCT04648046); nevertheless, considerable concern remains regarding their ability to effectively 550 
recognize and eliminate HIV-1-infected cells during suppressive ART.  551 
 552 
[H2] Sequential combinatorial approaches: To induce a sustained virological response in HIV-1 553 
cure studies, a combination of interventions engaging multiple components of the immune system 554 
may offer the highest chances of success (Figure 3B). We propose a sequential approach that would 555 
include an initial induction phase primarily designed to reduce the frequency of rebound-competent 556 
viral reservoir cells; we hypothesize that this treatment step would  predominantly focus on 557 
manipulating innate or cell-intrinsic immune mechanisms, and ideally result into a heavily depleted 558 
pool of virally -infected cells that is strongly biased towards proviruses integrated in 559 
transcriptionally-repressed heterochromatin locations. This induction phase could then be 560 
complemented by interventions aiming to induce a long -lasting, highly-functional adaptive 561 
memory cell response consisting of HIV-1-specific memory T cells (and possibly memory B cells) 562 
able to cross-recognize autologous viral variants. Such a stepwise interventional approach may lead 563 
to a poorly rebound -competent vir al reservoir that may then be durably controlled through a 564 
functional adaptive immune response  prepared to  effectively extinguish breakthrough viremia 565 
originating from rare persisting viral reservoir cell clones. In postulating such a staggered sequence 566 
of interventions, we propose that curative interventions  would capitalize on the innate immune 567 
system’s ability to clear infected cells while leveraging the adaptive immune system’s strength for 568 
maintaining long-term antiviral immune surveillance . P romising data supporting such a 569 
combinatorial interventional approach were recently reported in the exploratory “kitchen sink”  570 
clinical trial  (NCT04357821) focusing on joined administration of innate immune activators, 571 
broadly-neutralizing antibodies and therapeutic T cell vaccines.  572

Conclusion and future perspectives 574 
Are there reasons to be optimistic  about developing future immunologic strategies for finding a 575 
cure for HIV -1? While taking a cautious position, we feel that the available evidence, as 576 
summarized in this manuscript, does suggest that HIV -1 reservoir cells can be vulnerable to host 577 
immune mechanisms; in fact, while signs of immune selection within the reservoir cell pool may 578 
be subtle, they are consistently detectable across multiple study participants, multiple laboratories, 579 
and multiple investigative technologies. The  lack of  more definitive signs of immune activity 580 
against reservoir cells does, from our perspective, not reflect an intrinsic inefficacy of the immune 581 
system, but may rather indicate elaborate mechanisms of viral reservoir cells  to adapt to, escape 582 
from, and resist host immune function.  This ability to resist  immune elimination  seems deeply 583 

19 
 
rooted in the enormous diversity and heterogeneity of virally-infected cells that differ in terms of 584 
viral sequences, phenotypic properties, chromosomal integration sites , viral transcriptional 585 
activities, and host transcriptional programs; in simple probabilistic terms, it is highly likely that 586 
some infected cells possess intrinsic features that protect them from host immune attacks and enable 587 
their lifelong persistence. Targeting such a  highly selected  and increasingly-resistant pool of 588 
virally-infected cells with optimal adaptation to host immune surveillance remains one of the 589 
central challenges to finding a cure.  590 
Moving forward, we propose that detailed molecular ex vivo investigations of HIV-1 reservoir cells 591 
from people living with HIV -1, in the setting of observational or interventional clinical studies, 592 
represents one of the most informative approaches for advancing HIV-1 cure research. Variations 593 
of study participants in age, sex, ethnicity and immunogenetic background will be particularly 594 
valuable for such investigations  (Box 4 ), as will be a specific focus on individuals who act as 595 
outliers of ordinary viral reservoir cell dynamics and display unusual instability or vulnerability of 596 
viral reservoir cells. When embarking on such studies, it will be important to remain cognizant of 597 
the fact that current technologies, as powerful as the y are in deciphering single -cell features of 598 
reservoir cells, still capture only small proportions of HIV-1-infected cells, and that assessments of 599 
peripheral blood reservoir cells may not adequately reflect the biological behavior of tissue-bound 600 
reservoir cells. Optimized, high-throughput technologies that can profile large numbers of single 601 
cells from study participants and take advantage of methodological advancements in the fields of 602 
immunology, genomics, epigenetics, and single-cell analysis will, therefore, be critical for future 603 
progress in understanding the vulnerabilities  and the “Achilles heel”  of the reservoir cell pool. 604 
Insight gained from such investigations may permit future efforts  to target and exploit 605 
immunological weaknesses of HIV-1 reservoir cells, and to develop more targeted, and, possibly , 606 
more personalized therapeutic approaches that are specifically tailored towards the unique immune 607 
responses and the specific reservoir cell characteristics of a given person.  As emphasized in this  608 
article, such a research agenda will depend on interdisciplinary collaborations between researchers, 609 
clinicians, and , most importantly,  people living with HIV -1, and  will have to integrate ethical 610 
considerations for maintaining a balance between risks and possible benefits of cure-related studies. 611 
While we remain enthusiastic about the possibility to  develop the scientific basis for reaching a 612 
cure for millions of people who live with HIV -1, we acknowledge that despite years of research, 613 
the quest for a cure remains full of uncertainties and daunting challenges, and yet, like many others 614 
in the HIV-1 cure research community, we “will not stop until HIV is cured!”158.  615
