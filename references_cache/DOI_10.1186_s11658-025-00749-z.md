# Immune cell aberrations in Systemic Lupus Erythematosus: navigating the targeted therapies toward precision management

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune disease characterized by multilayered dysregulation of immune cell homeostasis, spanning B cell effector dysfunction, T follicular helper (Tfh) cell hyperactivity, and plasmacytoid dendritic cell (pDC) tolerance breakdown. Advances in high-parameter immunophenotyping, single-cell multiomics profiling, and spatial multiomics have redefined SLE pathogenesis, revealing stage-specific immune network perturbations. These discoveries have propelled mechanism-driven therapeutic strategies, including CD19-targeted chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy for B cell depletion, disruption of T–B cell synaptic signaling (CD40L inhibitors), and restoration of pDC tolerance (anti-BDCA2 antibodies). While patient heterogeneity poses challenges for universal therapeutic efficacy, emerging strategies integrating molecular endotyping and cellular biomarkers hold promise for overcoming these limitations. By aligning targeted therapies with the immunophenotypic signatures of individual patients, precision medicine approaches are expected to optimize treatment efficacy, minimize off-target effects, and ultimately enhance long-term clinical outcomes in SLE. This review synthesizes current insights into how immune cell perturbations contribute to SLE pathogenesis, modulate disease flares, and determine therapeutic refractoriness, with a critical synthesis of recent clinical trial outcomes.

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a highly heterogeneous autoimmune disease that predominantly affects women of childbearing age [1,2]. Its pathogenesis is driven by dysregulated immune mechanisms, including aberrant activation of immune cells, altered immune metabolism, and heightened type I interferon (IFN-I) signaling [3,4]. The imbalance between innate and adaptive immune responses is central to SLE development. Dysfunctional immune cells not only shape clinical phenotypes and disease progression but also critically influence the therapeutic outcomes. Moreover, immune dysregulation leads to the aberrant production of cytokines, complement components, and immunoglobulins, further exacerbating disease pathology [5]. In this review, we comprehensively examined the pathological roles of key immune cell subsets—T cells, B cells, dendritic cells (DCs), natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils—in SLE. Current therapeutic approaches, such as corticosteroids and immunosuppressants, have alleviated symptoms to some extent but exhibit significant limitations, particularly in long-term disease management [6]. This highlights the urgent need for more targeted and personalized treatment strategies. A pivotal milestone was the approval of belimumab in 2011, which was the first targeted therapy for SLE in the past decades. Subsequently, agents targeting cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4), CD20, and IFN-α receptor (IFNαR) have advanced into clinical trials, demonstrating promising results. Most notably, chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy has emerged as a groundbreaking approach for the precision treatment of lupus.

Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), spatial transcriptomics, and high-resolution flow cytometry have revolutionized our understanding of immune dysregulation in SLE. These cutting-edge technologies enable precise identification of immune phenotypes and have accelerated the development and application of targeted therapies based on immune phenotyping. Such progress has not only deepened our understanding of SLE pathogenesis but also transformed clinical approaches to diagnosis and treatment.

In this review, we explore recent insights into the contribution of immune cell abnormalities to SLE pathogenesis and discuss how these findings inform the identification of novel and precise therapeutic targets. CD4+T cells are pivotal players in the pathogenesis of SLE, orchestrating immune dysregulation through a complex interplay of pro-inflammatory and regulatory subsets, including T helper (Th) 1, Th2, Th17, T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, and regulatory T cell (Treg). Recent advances in single-cell technologies have further unveiled novel CD4+T cell subsets, refining our understanding of their dynamic roles in SLE pathogenesis and their potential as biomarkers for disease activity. These discoveries highlight the heterogeneity and functional complexity of CD4+T cells in SLE, offering new insights into their contribution to disease progression and therapeutic targeting.

Th1 cells predominantly produce cytokines including interferon-γ (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and interleukin (IL)-2. Compared with healthy controls (HCs), the percentage of Th1 cells is elevated in patients with SLE [7]. The primary function of Th1 cells is mediated by IFN-γ secretion. In the early stages of lupus, enhanced type II interferon (IFN) pathway allows for autoantibody accumulation and increased IFN-α levels, potentially through its ability to augment antibody-forming cell (AFC) responses and germinal center (GC) reactions (8,9). Compared with HCs, the expression of IFN-γ-inducible genes was significantly increased in both adult patients with SLE and those with childhood-onset SLE (cSLE). Increased IFN-γ levels are positively correlated with SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) scores [10,11,12]. Single-cell sequencing of urine samples from patients with lupus nephritis (LN) revealed a sustained IFN-γ-driven immune response in the kidneys of these patients [13]. Moreover, IFN-γ is associated with neuropsychiatric symptoms. In patients with cSLE, depressive symptoms and the ratio ofN-acetylaspartate (NAA) to creatine (Cr) has been found positively correlated with IFN-γ levels [14]. IFN-γ signaling has also been demonstrated to lead to Bcl-6 overexpression in Tfh cells, excessive GC response, and autoantibody production, suggesting its role in the humoral immune reaction (15).

Th2 cells are essential components of the type 2 immune response and play a role in various inflammatory processes [16]. Notably, patients with SLE demonstrate a preferential expansion of histone-specific CD4+T cells particularly Th2-polarized subsets—during disease quiescence compared with active phases [17]. By secreting cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, IL-10, and IL-13, Th2 promote B cell proliferation, differentiation, and antibody production. In SLE, Th2 cells primarily contribute to disease progression by secreting IL-4. IL-4 is a pleiotropic cytokine that mainly promotes humoral immunity by maintaining B cell survival and regulating differentiation [18]. The role of IL-4 in patients with SLE is markedly heterogeneous and is capable of either promoting autoimmune responses or exerting protective effects. Most studies have shown that IL-4 drives lupus progression by promoting B cell proliferation and activation, inducing plasma cell differentiation, and facilitating class switching to IgG1 and IgE [19,20]. In addition, IL-4 can reverse the anergic state of autoreactive B cells and promote SLE progression [21]. In Ets1−/−mice, which develop SLE-like symptoms, neutralization of IL-4 reduces Tfh2 cell frequency and ameliorates disease parameters. Conversely, other studies have reported that IL-4 may play a protective role by downregulating NPA-IgG antibody levels [22].

IL-10 is recognized as an anti-inflammatory cytokine; however, in patients with SLE, elevated serum levels of IL-10 are associated with increased disease activity and a higher risk of testing positive for anti-SSA/Ro antibodies [23]. Additionally, IL-5 is overexpressed in affected skin tissues of patients with SLE, contributing to skin inflammation [24]. In summary, more biomarkers are needed to identify the subtypes of SLE with prominent type 2 immune characteristics, making Th2-target therapy more precise and personalized.

Th17 cells drive SLE pathogenesis through multifaceted proinflammatory mechanisms, including vascular inflammation, neutrophil recruitment, and B cell activation with subsequent autoantibody overproduction [25]. Integrated chromatin accessibility (ATAC-seq) and RNA sequencing analyses in patients with SLE further revealed significant enrichment of transcription factors (TFs) governing Th17 differentiation pathways [26].

Th17 cells primarily mediate immunopathology in SLE through IL-17 secretion. Emerging evidence indicates that IL-17 receptor-expressing plasma cells contribute to disease progression via dual mechanisms: producing anti-double DNA (dsDNA) that exacerbate renal injury and enhancing autoreactive plasma cell survival via IL-17-mediated Bcl-xL stabilization [27].

IL-23 is a critical cytokine that promotes the amplification and differentiation of Th17 cells and mediates IL-17 secretion [28]. Compared with HCs, patients with SLE have elevated serum levels of IL-23 [29]. Elevated IL-23 level enhances RORγt expression, which regulates IL-17 production by Th17 cells, leading to tissue damage [30].

Treg are a subset of immune cells that play a crucial role in preventing aberrant activation of the immune system and in modulating immune tolerance [31]. In lupus, there is an ongoing debate regarding the modifications in Treg cells, which may be ascribed to their inherent plasticity. The classical CD4+CD25highFOXP3+Tregs were generally decreased in patients with SLE and inversely correlated with disease activity [32]. Through scRNA-seq, new Treg subsets, CCR7lowCD74hiand CCR7lowCD74low, have been identified and found to be increased in SLE. Of note, the CCR7lowCD74hiTregs, which highly express the T cell exhaustion-associated genes PDCD1 and LAG3, play a critical role in mediating immune dysregulation [33]. The observed expansion of specific Treg subsets may potentially result from two distinct yet interconnected mechanisms: impaired stability under inflammatory microenvironmental conditions or compensatory activation of immune regulatory feedback pathways.

IL-2 is essential for Treg differentiation, immunosuppressive function, and survival maintenance [34]. In individuals with SLE, diminished expression of the interleukin-2 receptor alpha chain (IL-2Rα) and impaired responsiveness to IL-2 have been identified as contributing factors to Treg dysfunction, which play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of the disease [34,35]. The deficiency of IL-2 in patients with SLE may be related to CD38-induced calcium overload in CD4+T cells, which occurs through the regulation of the cell membrane lipid composition [36]. Signaling lymphocytic activation molecule family 3 (SLAMF3) enhances IL-2 sensitivity in CD4+T cells through Smad3-dependent upregulation of CD25, facilitating Treg differentiation, and ameliorating immune dysregulation in SLE [37].

Tfh cells express B cell lymphoma 6 (BCL6) and facilitate B cell differentiation, antibody production, and GC formation through the secretion of IL-21 and contact-dependent molecules, such as CD40 ligand (CD40L)-CD40 and inducible T cell costimulator (ICOS)—ICOS ligand (ICOS-L). An increased percentage of circulating Tfh cells has been reported in patients with cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE) [38]. IFN-mediated aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR)/jun proto-oncogene (JUN) inhibition may drive CXCL13+Tfh cell elevation in SLE, promoting memory B cell to plasmablast conversion and disease progression [39].

Tfh cells are classified into three subsets: Tfh1, Tfh2, and Tfh17. Tfh1 cells exhibit contrasting dynamics; while their overall frequency is reduced in active SLE compared with inactive SLE and HCs [40], a subset of PD-L1+Tfh1 cells is expanded and positively correlates with plasma cell proportions and IL-21 levels, suggesting enhanced B cell differentiation capacity that may drive disease progression [41]. Notably, active SLE is characterized by significant expansion and activation of circulating Tfh1 and Tfh17 cells, with Tfh1 displaying a central memory (CM) phenotype and Tfh17 exhibiting an effector memory (EM) phenotype. Tfh17 activation is linked to the expansion of atypical naive (aNAV) and double-negative 2 (DN2) B cells, implicating IL-21-mediated signaling in autoantibody production and SLE pathogenesis [42,43]. Tfh2 cells, which primarily secrete IL-4-driven Th2-type cytokines, promote class switching to IgG4 antibodies and plasma cell differentiation [44]. In SLE, the frequency of Tfh2 cells correlates positively with circulating plasmablasts and anti-dsDNA antibody levels, potentially driven by upregulated PD-L1 and IL-4 expression in basophils [40,45].

CD8+T cells, also known as cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), have surface T cell receptors (TCRs) that recognize antigens presented by major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC I) molecules. Cytotoxic molecules, such as perforin and granzymes, produced by CD8+T cells can induce apoptosis in target cells. Studies have shown that the expression of granzyme B (GZMB) and perforin is significantly lower in CD8+T cells from patients with SLE than in HCs [46]. In patients with SLE experiencing infectious episodes, an expansion of CD8+CD38+T cells was observed compared with those without a history of infection. Nevertheless, their cytotoxic function seems to be exhausted, as manifested by diminished degranulation and reduced expression of granzyme A (GZMA), GZMB, and perforin. Additionally, critical TFs involved in cytotoxic activity, including T-bet, RUNX3, and Eomes, were also decreased [47].

Recent scRNA-seq analyses of peripheral blood from patients with SLE have demonstrated an expansion of GZMH+CD8+T cells in the peripheral circulation [48]. However, the precise role of these expanded GZMH+CD8+T cells in SLE pathogenesis remains unclear.

The costimulatory signals provided by Th cells are essential for B cell activation. Therapeutic strategies targeting T cells can be employed by disrupting T-B cell interactions, thereby inhibiting B cell activation. Blocking T cell-associated cytokines, such as IFN-γ, IL-17, IL-23, and IL-6, may help reduce inflammation in SLE. Conversely, the administration of IL-2 has the potential to expand Tregs, thereby promoting immune tolerance in SLE.

Dapirolizumab is a monoclonal antibody that targets CD40L, a member of the TNF superfamily, which is predominantly expressed on the surface of activated CD4+T cells. The dysregulated activation of the CD40L–CD40 axis may play a role in the production of autoantibodies by B cells [49].

In a phase I clinical trial, among 16 patients treated with dapirolizumab, 5 demonstrated a response (31%) according to the British Isles Lupus Assessment Group (BILAG)-based Composite Lupus Assessment (BICLA). It is noteworthy that of the 12 patients evaluable for the SLE Responder Index (SRI)-4, 5 (42%) exhibited a response by week 12 [50]. Conversely, in a phase II randomized, placebo-controlled study, the rate of BICLA response in the dapirolizumab treatment group did not meet the prespecified dose–response model [51].

AMG557 specifically targets ICOS-L, thereby inhibiting the interaction between ICOS and ICOS-L. ICOS is integral to the regulation of T cell activation, maintenance of- Tfh cells and Treg homeostasis and facilitation of interactions with B cells. The inhibition of ICOS/ICOS-L signaling may disrupt T–B cell interactions and modulate autoantibody production in autoimmune conditions such as SLE. A phase I clinical trial indicated that patients with SLE receiving AMG557 treatment exhibited improvements in clinical global BILAG index scores, SLEDAI scores, and reductions in the number of painful and swollen joints compared with HCs [52]. These results support further evaluation of ICOS-L blockade in the clinical treatment of patients with SLE. Moreover, another clinical trial probing into the effects of an anti-ICOS monoclonal antibody (MEDI-570) in patients with SLE is currently in progress (NCT01127321).

Abatacept is a selective T cell costimulation modulator comprising the extracellular domain of CTLA-4 fused to the Fc region of human immunoglobulin G1. CTLA-4 functions by inhibiting T cell activation through competitive binding to B7 family molecules, such as CD80 and CD86, which also act as ligands for CD28 [53]. Inadequate or dysregulated CTLA-4 function may exacerbate autoimmune responses [54]. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved abatacept in 2005 for the treatment of RA. Nevertheless, in a phase IIb randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, abatacept failed to exhibit superior efficacy in the treatment of SLE and was linked to an increased incidence of serious adverse events (SAEs) [55]. Therefore, the clinical evidence of abatacept in the treatment of SLE warrants further investigation and discussion.

AMG 811, a monoclonal antibody targeting IFN-γ, demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction in CXCL10 levels in patients with mild-to-moderate SLE during a phase I clinical study [56]. CXCL10, a chemokine whose receptor is CXCR3, is upregulated in the skin of patients with lupus and is positively correlated with IFN levels [57]. Importantly, CXCL10 is significantly associated with disease activity in patients with LN [58]. Thus, AMG 811 has emerged as a potential targeted therapy for patients with SLE exhibiting elevated levels of IFN-γ.

Secukinumab, an IL-17A antibody, was first approved by the FDA in 2015 for the treatment of psoriasis. However, two phase III studies were halted, as preliminary data revealed that the secukinumab treatment group failed to exhibit superior efficacy when compared with the placebo group (NCT05232864, NCT04181762).

Ustekinumab, an antibody targeting IL-12 and IL-23, which are crucial for the maintenance and expansion of Th17 cells, was evaluated in a phase II, randomized, double-blind, multicenter, placebo-controlled study. In this study, patients with SLE were allocated at a 3:2 ratio to receive either ustekinumab or the standard treatment regimen. The findings indicated a higher SRI-4 response rate at week 24 in the ustekinumab cohort relative to the standard treatment group [59]. Further analysis of the data from this clinical study revealed that serum IFN-γ levels may serve as a potential indicator for assessing patient responsiveness to ustekinumab treatment [60]. Nevertheless, in a phase III, multicenter, placebo-controlled trial, ustekinumab did not exhibit a superior SRI-4 response rate compared with placebo at week 52 [61].

Clinical trials have explored the therapeutic potential of low-dose IL-2 in patients with SLE. In a phase II, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial, although the primary endpoint at week 12 was not attained, patients with LN who received low-dose IL-2 demonstrated a higher rate of complete remission than the placebo group [62]. Similarly, in another phase II trial, the primary endpoint was not achieved at week 12. However, after adjusting for outliers, the analysis revealed that low-dose IL-2 is beneficial for active SLE [62,63]. Additionally, compared with patients treated with prednisone alone, low-dose IL-2 combined with prednisone therapy is more effective in alleviating the patient’s condition [64]. Mechanistically, low-dose IL-2 modulates Treg subsets by promoting tissue-specific homing and regulating B cell populations. Furthermore, biomarkers including CD122 (IL-2Rβ) expression, PD-1 levels, CXCR3+Tregs, thymic emigrant (RTE) Tregs, and IFN-α levels may serve as potential predictors of IL-2 therapeutic efficacy [65].

NKTR-358, a polyethylene glycol-IL-2 conjugate that selectively induces Tregs, was assessed in a phase I study. NKTR-358 demonstrated a dose-dependent augmentation in the proportion and absolute quantity of total CD4+Tregs and CD25brightTregs. Furthermore, in a subgroup of patients with CLASI-A scores ≥ 4, NKTR-358 treatment resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in CLASI-A scores [66].

Sirukumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that targets IL-6, a pivotal inflammatory cytokine involved in the regulation of immune responses and tolerance. A phase I, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study is the first to demonstrate the tolerability of sirukumab in SLE treatment. Although adverse events (AEs) were more frequent in patients receiving sirukumab compared with those in the placebo group, they remained within an acceptable range overall. Notably, the study indicated that sirukumab could partially improve C-reactive protein (CRP) and serum amyloid A levels [67].

Tocilizumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody targeting the IL-6 receptor (IL-6R). It has been approved for the treatment of various conditions including RA, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, and giant cell arteritis. In a phase I, open-label, dose-escalation study, treatment with 4 and 8 mg/kg tocilizumab significantly improved disease activity in patients with SLE. However, tocilizumab can cause dose-dependent neutropenia, necessitating careful monitoring and dose management during treatment [68].

The Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) pathway plays a critical role in the proliferation, differentiation, and activation of immune cells [69]. In patients with SLE, aberrant activation of the JAK/STAT pathway may contribute to the dysregulation of T cell differentiation, thereby initiating autoimmune responses (Fig.4) [70].

Tofacitinib is a targeted agent against the JAK family, primarily inhibiting JAK1 and JAK3. In a phase I, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial, 30 patients with SLE were randomly assigned to either the tofacitinib or placebo group in a 2:1 ratio. Tofacitinib was found to be safe in patients with SLE who met the study’s primary endpoint. They reported that tofacitinib improved cardiometabolic and immunological parameters associated with premature atherosclerosis in patients with SLE. In addition, some of these improvements are more robust in subjects with STAT4 risk allele [71]. An ongoing phase Ib/II open-label study is examining tofacitinib for treating moderate-to-severe cutaneous lesions in juvenile-onset systemic lupus erythematosus (jSLE) (NCT03288324).

Baricitinib primarily targets JAK1 and JAK2. The I4V-MC-JAHH study aimed to evaluate the effect of baricitinib on serological biomarkers in patients with SLE. Although no significant difference in SRI-4 response was observed between baricitinib and placebo groups, baricitinib treatment led to a rapid and sustained reduction in anti-dsDNA antibodies in patients with SLE [72]. Subsequent analysis of these gene expression profiles and cytokines of these patients reveal reductions inSTAT1, STAT2, STAT4target genes, IFN response genes, IL-12p40, and IL-6 following baricitinib treatment [73]. The SLE-BRAVE-I and SLE-BRAVE-II studies are phase III, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials. SLE-BRAVE-I results indicated a higher SRI-4 response rate at week 52 for the baricitinib 4 mg group compared with placebo; however, SLE-BRAVE-II did not replicate this result [74,75].

Upadacitinib primarily targets JAK1. A phase II randomized controlled study explored the therapeutic effects of upadacitinib and elsubrutinib (a Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitor) in patients with SLE. Results indicated that the upadacitinib 30 mg group and the elsubrutinib 60 mg with upadacitinib 30 mg group exhibited superior treatment responses, significantly reducing SLE disease activity, decreasing flare-ups, and demonstrating favorable tolerability [76].

Given that the proportion and function of Tregs are compromised in patients with SLE, the infusion of Treg cells may potentially restore immune function and ameliorate immune dysregulation. A case report suggested that after undergoing Treg adoptive transfer therapy, an patient with SLE presented with only a transient existence of Treg cells in the skin and peripheral blood. Notably, this resulted in a significant decrease in IFN-γ and an increase in IL-17, leading to a reduction in skin inflammation. A clinical study investigating Treg therapy for SLE is currently underway (NCT02428309).

Notably, three of the five trials (60%) failing to meet primary endpoints involved IL-2-based therapies. While IL-2 demonstrates theoretical immunomodulatory potential through Treg cell activation, its clinical translation appears limited by nonselective activation of IL-2α receptors (CD25). This promiscuous activation may induce paradoxical hyperactivation of effector T cells (e.g., Th1/Th17 subsets), resulting in a narrow therapeutic window and disruption of immune homeostasis. Of particular interest, JAK inhibitors accounted for 45.5% of trials achieving primary endpoints. Their clinical superiority stems from targeting the pivotal JAK-STAT pathway—a convergence point for multiple cytokines (e.g., IL-4, IFN-γ, IL-6 and IL-17) that coordinates Th cell differentiation and effector functions through STAT protein phosphorylation. This “multi-pronged modulation through a single target” strategy enables synergistic control of T cell-mediated hyperimmune responses, potentially explaining their enhanced clinical response rates compared with single-cytokine targeting approaches.

B cells play a pivotal role in SLE pathogenesis. Recent scRNA-seq analyses have identified a subset of patients with SLE with early B cell developmental defects (EBlo), which are associated with higher disease activity, nephritis, and IFN-I-driven inflammation. This contrasts sharply with patients exhibiting normal early B cell development (EBnor). Longitudinal data show that patients with SLE-EBlocan achieve remission and corrected B cell development after treatment, highlighting distinct disease states for personalized therapy [77]. Moreover, naive, plasma, and memory B cells significantly contribute to SLE immunopathology.

Newly activated naive B cells (aNAV B cells) with considerable clonality have been identified in patients with active SLE. These clonal naive B cells have the capability to generate antibody secreting cells (ASCs) and can persist in the circulation for several months, closely associated with disease activity [78]. In SLE, pathogenic aNAV B cells demonstrate a bidirectional immunoregulatory mechanism: these cells not only drive the pathological differentiation of Th2/Th17 subsets through cytokine-mediated crosstalk but also reciprocally undergo terminal differentiation into autoreactive ASCs under CD4+T cell-derived activation signals, ultimately generating characteristic anti-dsDNA autoantibodies that exacerbate lupus-specific humoral immune responses [79]. Moreover, aNAV B cells are positively correlated with the ratio of double-negative (DN) B cells and plasmablasts, as well as the disease activity [80]. Upon activation by stimuli such as Toll-like receptor (TLR) 7, immature naïve B cells may undergo differentiation into memory B cells or plasma cells/plasmablasts, thereby acquiring autoreactive antibody specificity and contributing to disease pathogenesis [81].

Anergic autoreactive B cells (BND) represent a distinctive subset of naïve B cells characterized by the expression of surface IgD and lack of surface IgM [82,83]. In lupus-prone mice, anergic B cells exhibit upregulation of the costimulatory molecule CD86 on their surface and significantly higher intracellular calcium influx upon BCR stimulation compared with wild-type (WT) mice [84]. Consistent with these findings, IL-4 has been shown to reverse B cell anergy by modulating surface IgM expression via STAT6 signaling in patients with SLE. This mechanism underscores the therapeutic potential of inducing B cell anergy as an alternative strategy to B cell depletion [21].

Recent advances have implicated memory B cells as central orchestrators of SLE progression via maladaptive activation mechanisms. In SLE, memory B cells and bystander T cells are jointly activated via TLR and IFN signaling, forming a positive feedforward loop that evades immune tolerance mechanisms and drives autoantibody expansion [85]. Importantly, a CXCR5−CD19lowmemory B cell subset is significantly expanded in patients with SLE and has been confirmed as a precursor of pathogenic autoantibody-producing plasmablasts [86].

Basically, memory B cells are primarily categorized into three subsets: CD27+IgD−switched memory B cells (SM), CD27+IgD+non-switched memory B cells (NSM), and CD27−IgD−double-negative B cells, which maintain a dynamic equilibrium in HCs [87]. In patients with SLE, however, the disruption of immune homeostasis often manifests as a reduction in NSM and an elevation in SM and DN B cells [88,89,90]. In patients with SLE, the frequency of DN B cells in peripheral blood increases and is significantly correlates with a higher disease activity index and autoantibodies [91]. Furthermore, in patients with LN, a reduction in DN B cells following treatment has been associated with improved remission rates [92].

Age-associated B cells (ABCs) are a heterogeneous subset of B cells that typically expand in older individuals and are characterized by the expression markers CD19+CD21−CD11c+T-bet+. These cells are also observed accumulate prematurely in patients with autoimmune or infectious diseases [93]. Patients with SLE frequently demonstrate an abnormal early accumulation of ABCs, with the differentiation of these cells being dependent on the zinc finger E-box binding homeobox 2 (ZEB2) for their maintenance [94,95]. Furthermore, a study by Wang et al. described a population of CD11chiT-bet+ABC-like cells in patients with SLE that exhibit many key characteristics of ABCs. These ABC-like cells differentiate into autoreactive antibody-secreting cells in an IL-21 dependent manner, driving the progression of SLE [96].

Regulatory B cells (Bregs), characterized by IL-10 production, are essential for maintaining immune tolerance and suppressing systemic inflammation [97]. Several studies have confirmed that in SLE patients, the number and function of Bregs are often impaired, potentially leading to disease exacerbation and uncontrolled autoimmune responses [98,99]. Specifically, newly diagnosed patients with SLE exhibit a marked reduction in the proportion of IL-10+CD24hiCD27+CD19+Bregs in peripheral blood. Longitudinal analyses of these patients have revealed that this decline in Breg populations is closely associated with disease relapse and elevated SLEDAI scores [100]. This phenomenon extends to LN, where circulating Bregs are markedly reduced at diagnosis but recover following 6 months of immunosuppressive therapy in treatment-responsive individuals [101,102]. Paradoxically, patients with SLE exhibit expansion of a distinct CD24hiCD27−CD38−CD69+/hiBreg population that cosecretes IL-10 and proinflammatory cytokines. These dual-functional Bregs enhance Th17/Th22 differentiation via MAPK/ERK/p38-dependent glycolytic reprogramming, thereby exacerbating immunopathology [103].

Plasma cells are considered as the end-stage differentiation products of mature B lymphocytes and are capable of continuously secreting large amounts of antibodies [104]. In patients with SLE, the overactivation of plasma cells is considered one of the key factors leading to multiorgan dysfunction [105,106]. Interferon stimulated gene (ISG)hiB cells represent a distinct B lymphocyte subpopulation characterized by elevated expression of ISG. These evolutionarily conserved genes are primarily activated through interferon signaling pathways—fundamental mechanisms of antiviral defense mediated by cytokine networks. Notably, emerging evidence implicates ISGhiB cells in the pathogenesis of cutaneous inflammation observed in discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE), with recent clinical studies demonstrating their heightened activation status correlating with disease severity. This pathophysiological involvement positions ISGhiB cells as promising cellular targets for developing novel therapeutic strategies in DLE management [107]. In addition, different types of autoantibodies may originate from different plasma cells. The levels of anti-Sm and anti-RNP antibodies are relatively stable and may be associated with long-lived plasma cells, while the levels of anti-dsDNA antibodies fluctuate with disease activity and may be related to short-lived plasma cells [3].

The survival and function of plasma cells are regulated by various cytokines, among which B cell activating factor (BAFF) and a proliferation-inducing ligand (APRIL) are crucial for maintaining the survival of memory plasma cells. These cytokines bind to receptors on the surface of plasma cells, providing survival signals that prevent apoptosis, allowing plasma cells to survive long-term in the bone marrow (BM) [108].

As B cells are the main source of pathogenic autoantibodies in SLE, targeting B cells has become a popular area of precise medicine in SLE in recent years. Fundamentally, current SLE B cell-targeted therapies include B cell depletion, inhibition of B cell function or signaling, and blocking of related cytokines or ligands. With the advancement of biotechnology, the intervention methods of targeted therapy have expanded from monoclonal antibodies and small molecule drugs to CAR-T and bispecific antibody.

Obexelimab is an experimental, bifunctional, noncytolytic monoclonal antibody that targets CD19 and FcyRIIb to suppress B cells, plasmablasts, and plasma cells. In a phase II clinical trial, the endpoint did not reach statistical significance: obexelimab-treated patients (42.0%) versus placebo-treated patients (28.6%). Nevertheless, secondary analysis showed time to loss of improvement (LOI) was significantly increased in obexelimab-treated patients, and the gene expression with high B cell pathway modules at baseline suggests a responding subpopulation [109]. Therefore, the efficacy, safety, and applicability of obexelimab still need further exploration.

Rituximab is currently the most widely used anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody in clinical practice. Small-scale clinical trials have shown that rituximab can reduce anti-dsDNA antibody levels [110,111]. Dose-escalation studies further revealed that, although rituximab does not significantly alter anti-dsDNA antibody or complement levels, it effectively depletes B cells and improves Systemic Lupus Activity Measure (SLAM) scores in patients [112,113], albeit with individual variability. However, the EXPLORER trial, a phase II/III randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study, found no significant differences in primary or secondary endpoints between the rituximab and placebo groups [114]. Similarly, the LUNAR trial, a phase III randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study in patients with LN, reported no statistically significant difference in renal response between the two groups, with the rituximab group experiencing higher rates of adverse events, including neutropenia, leukopenia, and hypotension [115]. By contrast, observational studies and case reports have demonstrated significant efficacy of rituximab in certain patients with SLE, particularly those refractory to conventional immunosuppressive therapies [116,117]. As a result, rituximab is frequently used off-label in many countries, especially for patients unresponsive to standard treatments [118]. These findings underscore the importance of identifying subgroups of patients with SLE who are most likely to benefit from rituximab therapy.

Ocrelizumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that targets CD20+B cells. It has been approved by the FDA for the treatment of multiple sclerosis. A phase III randomized, double-blind trial assessed the efficacy of ocrelizumab in patients with type III/IV LN, with all participants receiving either mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) or Euro-Lupus Nephritis Trial (ELNT) treatment. The clinical study had to be prematurely terminated due to a significantly elevated incidence of infections, predominantly observed in patients undergoing treatment with MMF. Based on the data before the study was halted, patients receiving ocrelizumab showed a much higher proportion of a ≥ 50% reduction in the urine protein-to-creatinine ratio at 48 weeks, demonstrating some therapeutic potential for ocrelizumab, but its safety is still a matter of discussion [119].

Ofatumumab, a type II monoclonal antibody, targets the CD20 antigen and is distinct from the type I monoclonal antibody rituximab. Ofatumumab exhibit enhanced cytotoxic effects and improved tolerability [120]. A retrospective analysis involving 16 patients with SLE who were intolerant to rituximab demonstrated B cell depletion, as well as improvements in antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti-dsDNA antibodies, complement levels, and renal function following ofatumumab treatment. There were five reported instances of grade III infections; however, no cases of malignancy or mortality were observed [121]. Ofatumumab may therefore be a better option for patients who do not respond well to rituximab.

Obinutuzumab is also a type II monoclonal antibody targeting CD20. In patients with SLE who were nonresponsive to rituximab, treatment with obinutuzumab resulted in a significant reduction in SLEDAI scores and overall British Isles Lupus Assessment Group (BILAG)-2004 scores, alongside notable improvements in C3 and dsDNA titers. Furthermore, out of eight patients who were previously administered steroids at doses exceeding 10 mg/day, five experienced a reduction in their steroid dosage following 5 months of obinutuzumab therapy [122]. The NOBILITY study, a phase II randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter trial, assessed the safety and efficacy of obinutuzumab in patients with LN. The findings indicated that the proportion of patients achieving a complete renal response (CRR) at week 52 was greater in the obinutuzumab treatment group compared with the placebo control group (35% versus 23%); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance. Importantly, obinutuzumab treatment did not result in an increased incidence of serious adverse events (SAEs), severe infections, or mortality compared with placebo [123]. In the pivotal phase III trial, obinutuzumab demonstrated statistically significant and clinically meaningful superiority over standard treatment in patients with active LN, achieving a 46.4% CCR rate versus 33.1% at week 76. These findings confirm obinutuzumab as a B cell-targeting biologic agent that represents a novel immunomodulatory strategy for LN management [124].

While anti-CD20 therapy demonstrates potential in the management of SLE, further research is necessary to elucidate the immunological profiles of patients who respond favorably to anti-CD20 therapy to optimize therapeutic outcomes. Other CD20-targeted drugs, such as mosunetuzumab, are being investigated in related clinical studies (NCT05155345).

Epratuzumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that targets the CD22 molecule on the surface of B cells. Some in vitro studies have shown that epratuzumab can modulate the function of B cells in patients with SLE. In vitro treatment of B cells from SLE patients with epratuzumab inhibits the production of proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α but does not affect IL-10 [125]. Furthermore, epratuzumab predominantly targets CD27−B cells in patients with SLE, resulting in decreased cell surface expression of CD62L and β7 integrin, while concurrently augmenting the expression of β1 integrin and the chemokine CXCL12 [126]. These immunophenotypic modifications, particularly the modulation of adhesion molecules and chemokine receptors, suggest that patients with SLE exhibiting CD27−B cell expansion may represent a distinct clinical subgroup with enhanced therapeutic responsiveness to epratuzumab treatment.

ALLEVIATE and SL0006 were two clinical trials evaluating the efficacy and safety of epratuzumab in the treatment of SLE. Both trials were discontinued due to supply disruptions; however, pretermination data indicated a reduction in the total BILAG score in the epratuzumab group relative to the placebo control group [127]. EMBLEM was a phase IIb randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study aimed at determining the dosing regimen of epratuzumab suitable for adult patients with moderate to severe active SLE. This study demonstrated that epratuzumab treatment at a dose of 2400 mg is well-tolerated in patients with SLE and significantly improves disease activity, thus paving the way for subsequent phase III clinical trials [128]. EMBODY 1 and EMBODY 2 were phase III randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trials designed to assess the efficacy and safety of epratuzumab in patients with moderate-to-severe active SLE. The findings from both studies did not demonstrate a statistically significant improvement in the British Isles Lupus Assessment Group-based Composite Lupus Assessment (BICLA) response rate at week 48 for the epratuzumab group compared with the placebo group, thereby failing to establish the superiority of epratuzumab treatment [129].

Daratumumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting CD38, can be administered via intravenous or subcutaneous routes. In vitro studies have demonstrated that daratumumab induces a dose-dependent depletion of plasma cells and plasmablasts in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) derived from patients with SLE [130]. Moreover, in a B cell-NK cell coculture model involving patients with SLE, daratumumab enhances NK cell degranulation and facilitates the clearance of circulating plasma cells [131]. These findings suggest that daratumumab holds promise as a therapeutic agent. Currently, a phase II clinical trial is being conducted to evaluate the efficacy of daratumumab in the treatment of SLE (NCT04810754).

Mezagitamab is a fully human IgG1 monoclonal antibody that targets CD38. In a phase Ib, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter study, no significant differences were observed in the Cutaneous Lupus Erythematosus Disease Area and Severity Index (CLASI) or the SLEDAI between the mezagitamab treatment group and the placebo control group. Notably, further analysis revealed a more pronounced skin response in the mezagitamab-treated patients with higher baseline CLASI scores (> 10) [132]. Consequently, mezagitamab exhibits promising therapeutic potential in patients with elevated baseline CLASI scores.

BI 655064 is an anti-CD40 monoclonal antibody. In a multiple-dose study involving HCs, no dose-dependent relationship or significant indicators of an acute immune response were observed, thereby affirming the safety profile of this antibody and supporting its potential application in autoimmune diseases [133]. A phase II randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial was conducted to assess the therapeutic effects of 120 mg, 180 mg, and 240 mg doses of BI 655064 in patients with LN. This study did not establish a dose–response relationship for the primary endpoint of CRR at 52 weeks. Notably, both the placebo and BI 655064 groups exhibited a high incidence of AEs, with infections being the most common. The 240 mg BI 655064 treatment group had the highest incidence of severe infections compared with other groups [134]. Given these findings, the efficacy and safety of BI 655064 in the treatment of SLE still need further evaluation. Other CD40 antibodies, such as iscalimab (NCT03610516), are being investigated in ongoing clinical studies related to lupus.

BAFF mainly binds to the receptors TACI, BCMA, and BAFF-R on B cells. BAFF-R is the primary BAFF receptor expressed on human and murine peripheral B cells. The expression pattern of other BAFF receptors is more limited, with BCMA expressed by GC B cells and TACI primarily expressed by splenic transitional type 2 and marginal zone (MZ) B cells as well as activated B cells [135].

Ianalumab is a monoclonal antibody targeting BAFF-R that has not yet been formally approved for clinical use. Nevertheless, in early clinical studies, it has shown promising therapeutic effects for autoimmune diseases. A phase II, multicenter, randomized, parallel-group, double-blind trial demonstrated the drug’s efficacy in the treatment of SLE, evidenced by a higher SRI-4 response rate at 28 weeks (ianalumab group: 70.6%, placebo group: 20.4%) [136]. Building on these findings, a separate clinical study is currently investigating the efficacy of Ianalumab in patients with LN (NCT05126277). Together, these studies are anticipated to offer critical insights into the therapeutic application of ianalumab for SLE.

Teclistimab is a bispecific antibody that concurrently targets CD3 and BCMA. It functions by activating T cells and promoting their interaction with BCMA-expressing B cells, thereby facilitating the destruction of these target cells. Teclistimab has been administered to four patients with autoimmune diseases (Primary Sjögren’s syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis (RA), systemic sclerosis, and idiopathic inflammatory myositis), demonstrating significant therapeutic efficacy [137]. Subsequently, a case report documented the application of teclistimab in a patient with refractory SLE. Following treatment with teclistimab, the patient exhibited rapid depletion of B cells, a reduction in anti-dsDNA antibody levels, normalization of complement levels and IFN-I activity, significant improvement in renal function, and complete resolution of symptoms in the oral cavity, skin, and musculoskeletal system [138]. These findings suggest that teclistimab holds considerable therapeutic potential for patients with refractory SLE. Additionally, other bispecific antibodies, such as blinatumomab, are currently being investigated in related clinical trials (NCT06570798) (Fig.3A).

Belimumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody targeting BAFF. Initial phase I and II clinical studies did not demonstrate significant therapeutic efficacy for belimumab [139,140]. By contrast, in subsequent large-scale phase III clinical trials, namely BLISS-52 and BLISS-76, the treatment groups receiving belimumab exhibited significantly higher SRI rates at 52 weeks compared with the placebo groups [141,142]. Moreover, a pooled analysis of data from these phase III trials revealed that belimumab treatment significantly enhanced overall disease activity, particularly in the musculoskeletal and mucocutaneous domains, among patients with SLE [143]. A subgroup analysis, predicated on the clinical characteristics of participants in these phase III clinical trials further demonstrated that belimumab demonstrated enhanced efficacy in patients exhibiting elevated disease activity, positive anti-dsDNA antibodies, reduced complement levels, or a baseline history of corticosteroid use [144].

The BLISS-LN trial showed that a higher percentage of patients treated with belimumab alongside standard therapy achieved a major renal response compared with those on standard therapy alone [145]. Furthermore, the efficacy and safety of belimumab in cSLE have been confirmed have been substantiated, leading to its approval by the FDA in May 2019 for use in patients aged 5 years and older [146]. Subsequent research has led to the expanded application of belimumab. The BLISS-SC trial provided evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of subcutaneous administration of belimumab when used alongside standard therapy [147].

In recent years, research into the biological effects of belimumab has become increasingly in-depth. Recent studies have shown that belimumab significantly downregulates gene expression associated with B cells, type III IFN, the IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathway, and neutrophil activation. Notably, a group of genes (includingCCL4L2,CARD10,MMP15, andKLRC2) has been identified through machine learning models that can predict treatment response to belimumab with 84% specificity [148].

Tabalumab is a human IgG4 monoclonal antibody capable of binding to BAFF. The ILLUMINATE-1 trial, a phase III multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled study, evaluated the efficacy of standard therapy in combination with placebo versus standard therapy in combination with tabalumab. The findings indicated that tabalumab progressively reduced levels of anti-dsDNA antibodies, total B cells, and immunoglobulins, while simultaneously restoring complement components C3 and C4, beginning from the first week of treatment. Nevertheless, there was no significant increase in the SRI-5 response rate observed at week 52 [149].

Atacicept is a dual inhibitor targeting both BAFF and APRIL. The APRIL-SLE trial, a double-blind, placebo-controlled study targeting moderate to severe patients with SLE, did not meet the efficacy and safety endpoints. The 150 mg atacicept cohort demonstrated a significant reduction in flare rate and an extension in the time to first flare; however, this dosage was discontinued following the occurrence of two fatalities. Conversely, the 75 mg atacicept cohort did not achieve a reduction in flare rate nor a delay in the time to first flare, despite the absence of reported serious AEs [150]. A post hoc analysis of the APRIL-SLE trial data demonstrated a dose-dependent correlation between atacicept concentration and both immunoglobulin levels and flare rates. Importantly, patients exhibiting elevated baseline levels of BAFF and APRIL showed more significant therapeutic benefits [151]. The phase IIb ADDRESS II trial revealed an improved SRI-4 response rate in patients with SLE who received 75 mg or 150 mg doses of atacicept, without a concomitant rise in AEs. This effect was especially notable in patients with higher baseline disease activity [152].

Telitacicept is a fully human TACI-Fc fusion protein that can target both BAFF and APRIL. Results from a phase IIb clinical study indicated that a higher proportion of patients achieved SRI-4 with telitacicept treatment compared with the placebo group, accompanied by a reduction in immunoglobulins and B cells, as well as a recovery in complement levels [153]. Based on this, a phase III clinical study for severe patients with SLE is currently underway (NCT05306574).

Bisibimod is a soluble receptor fusion protein capable of binding both BAFF and APRIL. In a phase IIb, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial, the cohort receiving bisibimod demonstrated a superior SRI-5 response rate at week 24. This was accompanied by improvements in anti-dsDNA antibody levels, complement components C3 and C4, and a reduction in B cell counts [154]. In a phase III randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, bisibimod treatment was found to reduce steroid usage, urinary protein excretion, and levels of autoantibodies, while increasing complement C3 and C4 levels. Despite these benefits, no significant improvement in the SRI-6 response rate was observed in the bisibimod treatment group at week 52 [155]. Consequently, further clinical trials are warranted to validate the therapeutic application and optimal dosing of bisibimod.

Ikaros, encoded by the Ikaros family zine finger 1 (IKZF1) gene, is a transcription factor crucial for lymphocyte development and hematopoiesis. Its role in regulating B cell anergy and its association with SLE susceptibility have been well documented across diverse populations (Fig.4) [156,157,158,159,160,161,162,163]. Loss of Ikaros function disrupts B cell tolerance, contributing to autoimmunity and the pathogenesis of SLE [164]. Iberdomide is a novel immunomodulatory drug that primarily targets cereblon (CRBN), a component of the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex. By targeting cereblon, Iberdomide degrades Ikaros and Aiolos, thereby modulating immune responses. In a phase II clinical study, patients with SLE were randomized to receive iberdomide at doses of 0.45, 0.30, or 0.15 mg, or a placebo. Results indicated that patients taking 0.45 mg iberdomide achieved a higher percentage of SRI-4 response compared with placebo [165]. This efficacy is attributed to the ability of Iberdomide to significantly reduce the activity of IFN-I and B cell pathways, while increasing IL-2 and Tregs, thereby restoring immune balance in patients with SLE [166].

Fenebrutinib is a Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitor. In a phase II, multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled study, the fenebrutinib treatment group exhibited reductions in anti-dsDNA autoantibodies and total IgG and IgM levels, accompanied by an increase in complement C4 levels. Nevertheless, the primary treatment endpoint, SRI-4 response, was not achieved [167]. Likewise, evobrutinib, another BTK inhibitor, demonstrated favorable safety profiles in phase II clinical trials but did not meet the primary treatment endpoints (Fig.4) [168].

CAR-T cell therapy demonstrates significant potential in refractory SLE. The standardized protocol comprises autologous T cell isolation, lentiviral-mediated CD19-specific CAR transduction, and adoptive transfer post expansion (Fig.3B) [169]. Clinically, rapid reductions in disease activity, anti-dsDNA antibodies, and proteinuria were observed within 3 days of infusion [170]. Longitudinal studies (12-month follow-up,n= 5) reveal that CAR-T therapy achieved sustained drug-free remission, even after the recurrence of B cell proliferation. The reemerging B cells were found to be immature and displayed nonclonally converted B cell receptors [171]. Extended monitoring (29 months) confirms that patients with SLE who underwent CAR-T therapy achieved remission, as defined by the Definition of Remission in SLE (DORIS), without experiencing SAEs [172]. Notably, SLE-derived CAR-T cells maintained functional expansion capacity equivalent to healthy counterparts, supporting clinical applicability [173].

BCMA-CD19 compound chimeric antigen receptor T cells (cCAR) therapy has demonstrated efficacy and safety in SLE. In the first reported case of a patient with SLE and diffuse large B cell lymphoma, cCAR therapy led to rapid B cell depletion, normalization of complement levels, and sustained remission over 23 months [174]. A phase I trial in 12 refractory patients with SLE showed anemia in all cases and mild infections in four, with no severe adverse events, confirming tolerability [175]. In a phase I open-label study of SLE with LN, cCAR therapy achieved autoantibody seronegativity, significant SLEDAI score reduction, and improved renal function in most patients [176].

Recent advances have highlighted the emergence of CAR-NK cells as a key focus in immunotherapy research, owing to their unique biological characteristics and potential therapeutic advantages. Compared with CAR-T cells, CAR-NK cells have a lower risk of cytokine release syndrome and can be produced using existing cell lines or allogeneic NK cells, offering broader applicability and reduced toxicity. In humanized lupus mouse models, it was initially demonstrated that CAR-NK cells can selectively target and deplete PD-1highTfh, thereby achieving therapeutic efficacy [177]. At the 2024 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) Annual Meeting, groundbreaking advancements in CAR-NK cell therapy for SLE were unveiled. A clinical trial evaluating allogeneic CD19 CAR-NK cells (KN5501) in 22 patients with refractory or relapsed SLE demonstrated remarkable clinical efficacy and an excellent safety profile. These findings highlight the potential of CAR-NK therapy as a transformative treatment for SLE.

Currently, the data on the efficacy and side effects of CAR T therapy are still quite limited. This is mainly because most of the existing studies have been preliminary explorations conducted with small sample populations, which restricts the generalizability and statistical power of their results. Therefore, it is necessary to expand the sample size and conduct more rigorous and larger-scale randomized controlled clinical trials to ensure the robustness and reliability of the data.

Recent technological advancements reveal two key trends: (1) CAR-T breakthroughs: the first CD19 CAR-T therapy for lupus induced 18-month drug-free remission, potentially through profound B-cell depletion, immune memory reset, and concurrent modulation of Tfh cells. NK cells interact with various immune cells to establish a complex immune network that modulates the overall immune milieu. Notable alterations in both the quantity and functionality of NK cells are evident in patients with SLE, characterized by a reduction in both the percentage and absolute number of NK cells in peripheral blood compared with HCs [178,179]. Further investigation indicates that patients exhibiting high disease activity demonstrate a decreased frequency of mature NK cells, coupled with a relatively increased frequency of immature NK cells, whereas an inverse pattern is observed in patients with low disease activity [179].

The underlying mechanism may involve impaired clearance of apoptotic cells due to the compromised quality and functional capacity of NK cells in SLE. When apoptotic cells fail to undergo timely clearance, they may undergo secondary necrosis, releasing intracellular components that function as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). These DAMPs trigger a cascade of inflammatory responses, potentially initiating and perpetuating the autoimmune responses [180]. Notably, the interplay between NK cells and IFN signaling pathways appears to be bidirectional: on one hand, NK cell deficiencies are closely associated with IFN-α-mediated activation-induced cell death [181]; on the other hand, some NK cells could also enhance IFN-α production by pDC [182].

Recent advances in research have identified novel functional NK cell subsets, such as TIM-3+PD-1+NK cells and AALlowSiglec-1lowNK cells [183,184]. The expansion of TIM-3+PD-1+NK cells is associated with clinical parameters such as Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate, CRP levels, anti-dsDNA antibodies, anti-ribosomal P antibodies, disease activity index, and cardiovascular manifestations. TIM-3 and PD-1, as coinhibitory immune checkpoint molecules, exhibit synergistic effects in mediating NK cell dysfunction in SLE. Their coexpression on NK cells is associated with significant functional impairment, characterized by diminished cytotoxic activity and reduced cytokine production capacity [183]. Furthermore, recent studies have identified a distinct NK cell subset characterized by low expression of AAL and Siglec-1 (AALlowSiglec-1lowNK cells), whose expansion shows a positive correlation with disease activity as measured by SLEDAI scores. The biological significance of this observation lies in the functional role of AAL as a lectin recognizes core fucosylation—a critical factor involved in TCR activation. The reduction of core fucosylation on NK cell surface molecules may impair their capacity for pathogen recognition and cytotoxic targeting, thereby contributing to the dysregulation of immune responses and disease progression in SLE [184].

DCs are pivotal antigen-presenting cells that play crucial roles in both initiating pathogenic autoimmune responses and maintaining immune tolerance [185]. Recent scRNA-seq analyses have defined distinct DC subpopulations named DC2 (HLA-DQ+FcεRIα+CD5+) and DC3 (CD14+), and the DC3s are detected expanded, showing a proinflammatory phenotype correlated with lupus disease activity [186]. Further scRNA-seq studies in kidneys from patients with SLE have revealed a significant accumulation of CD163+DC3s, which display a highly proinflammatory state, with their abundance closely linked to higher LN severity [187].

Among the various subsets of DCs, inflammatory dendritic cells (inf DCs), myeloid dendritic cells (mDCs), and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) are particularly significant in the SLE. Inf DCs, which are found in inflammatory tissues, could produce IL-6 and IL-23 to induce Th17 cell differentiation [188]. In patients with LN, inf DCs are highly expressed in the kidneys and are capable of interacting closely with CD3+T cells to promote local inflammation [189]. Myeloid DCs, derived from bone marrow myeloid precursors, are recognized for their critical role in mediating central and peripheral immune tolerance. Most studies suggest a decrease in circulating mDCs in patients with SLE, with an accumulation in the kidneys correlating with renal involvement [190]. Nevertheless, some studies have indicated no significant changes in circulating mDCs, highlighting the need for further investigation into their role in SLE [191]. Plasmacytoid DCs are closely linked with the pathophysiology of SLE. While DCs are decreased in the bloodstream of patients with SLE, they tend to accumulate in tissues such as the kidneys and skin, with this accumulation being especially pronounced in individuals with class III/IV LN [107,192,193]. Tissue-resident pDCs play a central role in driving pathophysiological changes, primarily through the secretion of IFN-α, a cytokine closely associated with SLE.

IFN-I, particularly IFN-α, constitute a pathogenic axis in SLE through pleiotropic immunomodulatory effects. Mechanistically, IFN-α orchestrates DC-mediated T cell priming, B cell differentiation, and myeloid cell activation, fueling the immunopathological cascade [194]. Clinically, IFN-α hyperproduction correlates with disease severity, relapse frequency, and multi-organ involvement—from CLE to neuropsychiatric manifestations. In CLE lesions, IFN-α-driven overexpression of myxovirus resistance protein A (MxA) and IFN-induced proteins 1 and 2 (IFIT1/2) promotes keratinocyte apoptosis and autoantigen liberation, establishing self-perpetuating inflammation [195,196]. Paradoxically, IFN-α demonstrates compartment-specific effects: within the central nervous system, it disrupts neuroglial signaling pathways to potentiate behavioral abnormalities in neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE) [197], while in the cardiovascular system, it exhibits dual associations—correlating with reduced mucocutaneous inflammation activity yet promoting early vascular damage through IFN-I-inducible mediators [198,199]. Notably, circulating IFN-α levels outperform conventional biomarkers (anti-dsDNA, complement, SLEDAI) in predicting in-hospital mortality, positioning them as both therapeutic targets and prognostic indicators for severe SLE [200].

DCs constitute pivotal mediators in SLE pathogenesis through their capacity to dysregulate antigen processing, prime autoreactive T/B lymphocyte responses, and promote pathogenic autoantibody secretion. Pharmacological targeting of DC functional pathways represents a promising strategy to reestablish peripheral immune tolerance and mitigate SLE progression.

Anifrolumab is a monoclonal antibody targeting the IFN-I receptor (IFNAR), designed to alleviate autoimmune symptoms by blocking IFN-I signaling. Approved by the FDA in 2021 for SLE treatment, anifrolumab demonstrated significantly higher achievement of primary endpoints in patients with SLE compared with placebo in the phase IIb MUSE and phase III TULIP-2 trials [201,202]. This represents a significant milestone in SLE treatment. Nevertheless, it failed to meet the primary endpoint at week 52 in the phase III TULIP-1 trial [203]. Comparative examinations of the clinical data from the MUSE, TULIP-1, and TULIP-2 trials imply that baseline disease activity and fluctuations in glucocorticoid taper primarily caused the inconsistencies in patients’ responses to anifrolumab as evaluated by BICLA/SRI [204]. Beyond these factors, the immune and genetic profile of patients with SLE crucially influences their treatment responses. A post hoc analysis of the combined data from TULIP-1 and TULIP-2 disclosed that anifrolumab was more effective in patients with elevated IFN gene signatures and was correlated with an earlier, more frequent, and sustained low disease activity state [205,206,207].

The mechanisms of anifrolumab in the treatment of SLE are diverse and include: (1) downregulating the expression of IFN-I-induced genes (such asIFI27,IFI44, etc.), affecting type II and type III IFN signaling pathways and reducing IFN-λ protein levels; (2) regulating apoptosis and NETosis to improve the dysregulated cell death processes in SLE; (3) modulating the composition of immune cells, such as increasing the number of MAIT and γδ T cells while decreasing monocyte numbers; (4) affecting the activity of B cells and T cells, as well as regulating the inflammatory response by reducing levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines [208].

Sifalimumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting IFN-α, demonstrated significant efficacy in improving disease activity indices, restoring complement levels, and reducing the use of corticosteroids and other immunosuppressants, with a favorable safety profile in two phase I, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, dose-escalation studies [209,210]. In a larger phase II randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study, sifalimumab similarly exhibited strong therapeutic effects. By week 52, more patients in the sifalimumab group achieved the SLE response index, with reduced skin lesion area and severity, decreased swollen and painful joints, and improved disease activity [211]. Further studies have shown that higher baseline IFN-I-inducible mRNA expression levels in patients with SLE correlate with better responses to sifalimumab treatment [212]. The classification of patients with SLE based on IFN-I-inducible mRNA expression holds the potential for achieving precision medicine by means of sifalimumab.

Rontalizumab, another monoclonal antibody targeting IFN-α, demonstrated safety for SLE treatment in phase I placebo-controlled, double-blind, and dose-escalation studies. However, subsequent phase II clinical trials did not show the expected significant improvement across all active patients with SLE. Stratification of patients with SLE using the IFN signature metric (ISM) revealed that those with low ISM responded better to rontalizumab, showing significant disease improvement, reduced relapse rates, and decreased steroid use [213]. Therefore, ISM may be an important consideration in predicting the response to rontalizumab treatment.

Litifilimab, a monoclonal antibody targeting blood dendritic cell antigen 2 (BDCA2), modulates immune responses by binding to BDCA2 and subsequently inhibiting IFN-I production. In a phase II clinical trial, 132 participants were randomly assigned to receive litifilimab at doses of 50, 150, or 450 mg or a placebo. Treatment with litifilimab at all tested doses significantly ameliorated cutaneous involvement in patients with SLE, without serious adverse events reported [214].

Enpatoran is a novel, highly selective, and potent dual inhibitor of TLR7 and TLR8. A phase I, open-label, continuous-dose study demonstrated that the pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) profiles of enpatoran are similar in both Asian and non-Asian populations, with consistent disease progression trajectories post treatment. Additionally, no adverse events related to enpatoran were observed [215]. This study provides a rationale for dose selection and safety assessment in subsequent phase II clinical trials. Currently, a phase II clinical study of enpatoran for the treatment of SLE is underway (NCT05162586) (Fig.4).

This analysis included 5 DC-targeted agents evaluated in nine trials, with only one trial (11.1%) failing to meet primary endpoints—significantly lower failure rates than those targeting T cells (26.3%) or B cells (51.9%). Anifrolumab, the first fully human monoclonal antibody against IFNAR, received FDA approval in 2021, marking a pivotal transition from theoretical validation to clinical implementation in DC-targeted therapy. The therapeutic superiority of DC-targeted strategies stems from their “upstream immunomodulation” of autoimmune cascades: (1) pDC–IFN axis: pDCs, the primary IFN-α producers, trigger type I interferon storms via TLR7/9-mediated self-nucleic acid recognition. Notably, 60–80% of patients with SLE exhibit elevated IFN signature gene expression. (2) Immune synapse regulation: pDCs infiltrate target tissues through CXCR3–CXCL10 chemotaxis and enhance Tfh–B cell synaptic interactions via CD80/CD86-CD28 costimulatory signals. By blocking IFNAR signaling, anifrolumab concurrently suppresses pDC hyperactivation and downregulates 300 ISGs, achieving central immunomodulation at the immune response initiation phase. These findings position the DC–interferon axis as a core pathogenic driver in SLE, suggesting that targeted intervention at this nodal point may enable endogenous immune homeostasis restoration more effectively than the T/B cell strategies, particularly in IFN-high SLE subtypes.

Monocytes, pivotal in immune surveillance, are dysregulated in SLE, leading to aberrant activation [216]. They are classified into classical (CD14++CD16−), intermediate (CD14++CD16+), and nonclassical (CD14+CD16++) subsets. In SLE, the classical subset becomes proinflammatory, while the nonclassical subset activates T cells via abnormal antigen presentation [217]. It is noteworthy that IFN-induced transmembrane protein 3 (IFITM3), a key effector molecule in the IFN-I signaling pathway, is closely associated with the progression of SLE. The CD14+IFITM3+monocyte subset, associated with IFN-I signaling, expands in SLE and correlates with renal injury, inflammation, and disease activity, suggesting its potential as a biomarker [218].

Monocyte extracellular traps (METs) have recently been recognized as a novel pathological mechanism. METs are composed of DNA scaffolds, histones, and intracellular antimicrobial proteins. Although they share structural similarities with neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), METs have unique protein composition characteristics [219]. Studies have shown that METs contribute to the pathogenesis of SLE by mediating the production of autoantibodies and amplifying the inflammatory cascade. Their aberrant release is closely related to the formation of antinuclear antibodies, complement activation, and organ damage [220]. These findings provide new insights into the precise classification and targeted therapy of SLE.

Macrophage polarization is a dynamic process in which macrophages adopt distinct functional phenotypes in response to microenvironmental cues. Traditionally, this process is dichotomized into classically activated M1 macrophages, which drive proinflammatory responses, and alternatively activated M2 macrophages, which promote anti-inflammatory functions and tissue repair [221]. In SLE, macrophage polarization is dysregulated, with a predominant shift toward the proinflammatory M1 phenotype [216]. This M1 dominance perpetuates autoimmune responses by sustaining inflammation and enhancing autoantigen presentation [222,223].

By contrast, M2 macrophages are typically associated with inflammation resolution, tissue repair, and immune suppression [224]. However, their role in SLE is complex and context dependent. For instance, persistent M2 activity in organs such as the kidneys may contribute to fibrosis and functional impairment [225]. Recent single-nucleus RNA sequencing studies have further elucidated the pathogenic role of macrophages in LN, revealing interactions with mesangial cells via the CXCL12–DPP4 axis, which exacerbates disease progression [226].

Low-density granulocytes (LDGs), identified in the PBMC fraction after density-gradient centrifugation, exhibit reduced density due to altered surface protein expression, including CD66b and annexin A2. In SLE, LDGs display heightened proinflammatory activity, characterized by increased formation of NETs and release of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which contribute to sustained inflammation and tissue damage [227,228]. Additionally, LDGs in patients with SLE show elevated expression of IFN-inducible genes [229]. Clinically, LDG levels correlate with disease severity and specific manifestations, including coronary artery disease [230] and may serve as promising biomarkers for monitoring SLE disease activity [231].

NETs are web-like structures composed of DNA, histones, and granule proteins, which neutrophils release to capture and neutralize pathogens [232]. NETs components, such as DNA, histones, and citrullinated proteins, become autoantibody targets, driving chronic inflammation and atherosclerosis. Furthermore, NETs activate the complement system upon interaction with endothelial cells, exacerbating vascular damage [233,234]. In the kidneys, neutrophils interact with resident cells, including mesangial cells and podocytes, triggering inflammatory responses and promoting tissue injury. The release of ROS and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) from neutrophils further exacerbates renal dysfunction. Preclinical and clinical evidence strongly implicates neutrophil activity, particularly NET formation, in the progression of LN [235].

SLE is a chronic autoimmune disease driven by immune dysregulation, characterized by the aberrant activation of multiple immune cell populations. DCs initiate autoimmune responses by activating T cells, which subsequently stimulate B cells to produce autoantibodies. The overactivation of B cells, T cells, and dendritic cells, along with the contributions of NK cells, macrophages, and neutrophils, exacerbates immune damage through cytokine secretion and immune complex formation. Monocytes, as precursors of dendritic cells and macrophages, further amplify immune responses through their activation and functional abnormalities. Collectively, these interactions highlight the imbalance in immune cell networks as a central feature of SLE pathogenesis [236]. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms provides a foundation for developing targeted therapies aimed at restoring immune homeostasis.

Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of immune cell subset regulation in achieving precision management of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). The persistent imbalance between proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses drives chronic immune activation, underscoring the necessity for therapeutic strategies that target the dynamic interplay among immune cells rather than isolated cell types. For instance, B cell-targeted therapies must account for the heterogeneity of B cell subsets and their complex interactions with T cells and cytokine networks to optimize therapeutic efficacy.

Advances in single-cell sequencing, multiomics, and gene-editing technologies have significantly enhanced our understanding of SLE heterogeneity, enabling patient stratification based on disease activity, organ involvement, and treatment response. Precision medicine, guided by immune cell subset analysis and genetic profiling, holds significant promise for tailoring therapies to individual patients. Despite progress in reducing mortality and improving quality of life, unmet clinical needs remain. Future research should focus on identifying treatment-responsive subgroups and optimizing therapies that modulate immune cell networks holistically. Personalized approaches, informed by immunophenotyping and advanced biomarkers, will be critical in shaping the next generation of SLE treatment.

The development of immunotherapeutic strategies must consider the dynamic plasticity of the immune system. Biologic therapies drive remodeling of the immune microenvironment, which poses two critical challenges: one is the progressive attenuation of baseline immunophenotypes, where treatment interventions may obscure original immune signatures; the other is the temporal coupling between therapeutic response and immune reprogramming, necessitating dynamic monitoring systems. Consequently, immune phenotypes should function as evolving therapeutic coordinates rather than static classification criteria. Clinically, this underscores the need for real-time immune monitoring technologies to map spatiotemporal evolutionary patterns.

Current limitations of single-target strategies stem from compensatory network remodeling mechanisms: (1) target inhibition may activate compensatory signaling pathways. (2) Overblockade risks disrupting immune homeostasis. This necessitates transcending linear therapeutic paradigms to develop: (1) multitarget synergistic modulators. (2) Spatiotemporally precise delivery systems for network-level interventions. Future therapeutic paradigms should focus on reconstructing immune network homeostasis through multidimensional interventions at critical regulatory nodes. This paradigm shift from “singular blockade” to “system-level regulation” will propel precision medicine into an era of topological network intervention.

This review systematically categorizes immune dysregulation patterns in patients with SLE and evaluates current targeted therapies, aiming to inform future therapeutic strategies and drug development.
