# GATA1 silencing restrains granulosa cell ferroptosis through downregulation of TFRC in polycystic ovary syndrome.
**Authors:** Wang Y, Zhang Q, Liu J, Kuang H, Feng X
**Journal:** Sci Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41598-026-61608-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-61608-1)

## Content

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most prevalent endocrine disorder among women of childbearing age and the leading cause of anovulatory infertility worldwide, with the death of granulosa cells implicated in its pathogenesis. However, the mechanisms underlying granulosa cell death remain unclear. Limited research has explored the association between GATA binding factor 1 (GATA1) and adverse pregnancy outcomes, yet its role in PCOS is still elusive. This study established an in vivo PCOS model via dehydroepiandrosterone administration in mice and used ovarian-like KGN cells to explore granulosa cell function. Results showed elevated ovarian GATA1 expression in PCOS mice. Functionally, GATA1 overexpression in KGN cells increased lipid reactive oxygen species, malondialdehyde, and iron, while reducing glutathione. Notably, all these effects of GATA1 were mitigated by a ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1. Furthermore, GATA1 knockdown alleviated lipid peroxidation and iron overload in KGN cells induced by a ferroptosis inducer Erastin. Mechanistically, GATA1 directly bound the transferrin receptor (TFRC) promoter to enhance its transcription. Additionally, TFRC overexpression counteracted GATA1 knockdown's protective effects against Erastin-induced ferroptosis. Collectively, GATA1 promotes granulosa cell ferroptosis via TFRC transcriptional activation, contributing to PCOS pathogenesis. GATA1 regulatory pathways may serve as potential therapeutic targets for PCOS.

Abstract
Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most prevalent endocrine disorder among women
of childbearing age and the leading cause of anovulatory infertility worldwide, with the death
of granulosa cells implicated in its pathogenesis. However, the mechanisms underlying
granulosa cell death remain unclear. Limited research has explored the association between
GATA binding factor 1 (GATA1) and adverse pregnancy outcomes, yet its role in PCOS is still
elusive. This study established an in vivo  PCOS model via dehydroepiandrosterone
administration in mice and used ovarian-like KGN cells to explore granulosa cell function.
Results showed elevated ovarian GATA1 expression in PCOS mice. Functionally, GATA1
overexpression in KGN cells increased lipid reactive oxygen species, malondialdehyde, and
iron, while reducing glutathione. Notably, all these effects of GATA1 were mitigated by a
ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1. Furthermore, GATA1 knockdown alleviated lipid
peroxidation and iron overload in KGN cells induced by a ferroptosis inducer Erastin.

its transcription. Additionally, TFRC overexpression counteracted GATA1 knockdown’s
protective effects against Erastin-induced ferroptosis. Collectively, GATA1 promotes
granulosa cell ferroptosis via TFRC transcriptional activation, contributing to PCOS
pathogenesis. GATA1 regulatory pathways may serve as potential therapeutic targets for
PCOS.

Introduction
Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most prevalent endocrine disorder affecting
women of childbearing age, characterized primarily by hyperandrogenemia, anovulation,
and the presence of polycystic ovaries. It is recognized as the leading cause of anovulatory
infertility globally 1. In addition to reproductive challenges, individuals with PCOS frequently
experience metabolic dysfunctions, including obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and
metabolic syndrome 2. Moreover, numerous studies have demonstrated that women with
PCOS face an elevated risk of developing type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and

endometrial cancer 3-5. The precise etiology of PCOS remains unidentified, underscoring the
necessity for a more profound comprehension of its pathogenesis to facilitate the
development of effective therapeutic strategies. Although women with PCOS possess a
greater number of follicles compared to their counterparts without the condition, none
progress to become dominant follicles 6. The granulosa cell layer surrounding these follicles
exhibits signs of atresia, degeneration, and hypertrophy, suggesting abnormal proliferation
and/or apoptosis 7. Granulosa cells play a vital role in supporting oocyte development by
supplying essential nutrients and growth regulators during the maturation process. The
diminished proliferation and increased apoptosis of granulosa cells result in delayed
follicular growth, which is considered a significant factor in the pathogenesis of PCOS 8,9.
GATA1 is a member of the GATA transcription factor protein family, characterized by two
highly conserved zinc finger domains: the N-terminal finger and the C-terminal finger 10, and
regulates the expression of downstream target genes through binding to specific DNA
sequences. Discovered in 2005, GATA1 is essential for the formation of early eosinophil
precursors, directional erythroid precursors, and megakaryocyte differentiation 11.
Literature reports indicate that GATA1 is involved in various metabolic processes, including
lipid metabolism, glycolysis, and oxidative metabolism 12. Notably, the mRNA profiles of
patients with PCOS show remarkable similarity to those of ovarian cancer patients,
suggesting that similar molecular mechanisms may underlie both conditions 13,14. GATA1 has
been implicated in promoting ovarian cancer progression by activating the Notch signaling
pathway 15, with Notch signaling being activated in granulosa cells in PCOS, contributing to
its pathophysiology 16. Recent research has also indicated a correlation between GATA1 and
adverse pregnancy outcomes 17. Furthermore, baicalin has been shown to inhibit the
recruitment of GATA1 to the HSD3B2 promoter and to reverse hyperandrogenism in PCOS
18. However, the specific mechanisms by which GATA1 operates in PCOS remain largely
unexplored.
Ferroptosis is a newly identified form of iron-dependent cell death, characterized by iron
accumulation and lipid peroxidation 19. Extensive research has indicated that ferroptosis is
implicated in the pathogenesis of various human diseases, making it a burgeoning field for
the development of targeted treatment methods and intervention strategies. In recent years,
the role of ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of PCOS has garnered significant attention. The
treatment with metformin ameliorates PCOS in mice by inhibiting ovarian ferroptosis 20,
while the exacerbation of granulosa cell ferroptosis contributes to the development of PCOS
21. The transferrin receptor protein 1 (TFR1), encoded by the TFRC gene in humans,
regulates intracellular iron levels. TFR1 facilitates the import of iron from the extracellular
environment into cells, thereby enriching the cellular iron pool and enhancing sensitivity to
ferroptosis 22,23. Knockdown of TFRC has been shown to effectively inhibit granulosa cell

ferroptosis and alleviate the progression of PCOS 24. In CD8 T immune cells, programmed
death-1 signaling induces GATA1 binding to the promoter region of phospholipid
phosphatase 1 (PLPP1) and suppress its transcriptional activation, thereby promoting
ferroptosis 25. In K562 myeloid leukemia cells, GATA1 has been shown to induce lipid
peroxidation 26. GATA1 is also identified as a key gene in iron metabolism among patients
with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma 27. Interestingly, potential GATA1 binding sites have been
predicted in the promoter of TFRC using the JASPAR database, suggesting that GATA1 may
be involved in the ferroptosis of granulosa cells through TFRC.
By integrating both in vivo and in vitro research methodologies, we aimed to elucidate the
role of GATA1-related ferroptosis in the etiology of PCOS. Specifically, we investigated the
interaction between the transcription factor GATA1 and the key iron uptake protein TFRC,
as well as their effects on the ferroptosis of granulosa cells. Utilizing PCOS-like mouse
models and in vitro  experiments, we confirmed that GATA1 expression was significantly
upregulated in PCOS mice, and that GATA1 could transcriptionally activate TFRC, thereby
influencing the ferroptosis of granulosa cells and contributing to the pathogenesis of PCOS.
Our findings underscore the potential of GATA1 as a diagnostic marker and therapeutic
target for PCOS, which may enhance both diagnosis and treatment.
Results
Increased ferroptosis levels in the PCOS-like mice
Following 20 consecutive days of treatment with dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), the
PCOS mouse model was successfully established. As presented in Figure 1A-1C, the mice
exposed to DHEA exhibited significantly elevated serum concentrations of gonadotrophic
hormones, including testosterone, E2, LH and LH / FSH levels, compared to those in the
control group. The PCOS animals displayed polycystic ovarian tissue, whereas the Control
group exhibited a normal ovarian structure. In the PCOS group, numerous cystic dilating
follicles, a reduced number of corpora lutea, and a thinner granulosa cell layer were observed,
indicating ovarian dysfunction akin to the clinical presentation of PCOS (Figure 1D).
Furthermore, additional experiments revealed that levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS)
(Figure 1E) and malondialdehyde (MDA) (Figure 1F) were notably elevated, alongside
decreased glutathione (GSH) levels (Figure 1G) in the ovarian tissues of PCOS mice, which
aligns with the characteristics of ferroptosis, suggesting its occurrence in these tissues.
Additionally, we assessed iron levels in the ovarian tissues of both mouse groups, revealing
elevated iron concentrations in the ovarian tissues of PCOS mice compared to the Control
group (Figure 1H). Collectively, these findings provide compelling evidence that ferroptosis
occurs in the ovaries of the PCOS mouse model.
GATA1 is highly expressed in the ovarian tissues of PCOS-like mice
Given that several studies have indicated a correlation between GATA1 and adverse

pregnancy outcomes 17 as well as ferroptosis 25, we investigated the differences in GATA1
expression between the ovaries of control and PCOS mouse models. In this study, we found
that the mRNA and protein levels of GATA1 were significantly elevated in the ovarian tissues
of PCOS mice (Figure 2A-2B). Building on these findings, we further assessed the expression
and localization of GATA1 in the ovarian tissues of mice through IHC (Figure 2C). As depicted
in Figure 2C, GATA1 expression was markedly increased in the ovarian tissues of PCOS mice
compared to control mice, with localization in the granular cell layer. Therefore, we conclude
that GATA1 may serve as a potential ferroptosis-related gene, exhibiting high expression in
the granular cell layer of the PCOS mouse model.
GATA1 overexpression promotes granulosa cell ferroptosis
To further investigate the impact of GATA1 on ferroptosis in granulosa cells, we
constructed KGN cell lines that overexpressing GATA1. The successful overexpression of
GATA1 was validated through RT-qPCR and western blot experiments (Figure 3A-3B).
Subsequently, we observed that GATA1 overexpression suppressed KGN cell viability.
Treatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 markedly rescued this viability suppression,
whereas inhibitors targeting other forms of programmed cell death (necroptosis inhibitor
Nec-1 or apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK) could not reverse the GATA1-mediated growth
defect (Figure 3C). We next examined mitochondrial morphological changes using
transmission electron microscopy. Mitochondrial shrinkage was observed in GATA1-
overexpressing cells, and this change was abrogated after Fer-1 treatment (Figure 3D).
GATA1 overexpression also reduced iron deposition in KGN cells, as visualized via Prussian
blue staining (Figure 3E). A flow assay using the C11-BODIPY probe was conducted to assess
lipid ROS content, and the results demonstrated higher ROS levels in KGN cells from the LV-
oeGATA1 group compared to the LV-vector group, while Fer-1 treatment resulted in a
decrease in lipid ROS levels (Figure 3F). Moreover, KGN cells overexpressing GATA1
exhibited significantly higher levels of MDA, increased levels of Fe2+ and ACSL4 expression,
accompanied by lower levels of GSH and downregulated expression of solute carrier family
7 member 11 (SLC7A11), and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) compared to the LV-vector
group (Figure 3G-3J). Nevertheless, treatment with Fer-1 reversed this trend by decreasing
cellular MDA, Fe 2+, and ACSL4 expression, while reducing GSH level and upregulating
SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression (Figure 3G-3J). In summary, our results suggest that GATA1
may induce ferroptosis in granulosa cells.
GATA1 knockdown inhibits granulosa cell ferroptosis
To further investigate the role of GATA1 in granulosa cells, we established GATA1
knockdown KGN cell lines (Figure 4A-4B). In contrast, the knockdown of GATA1 in KGN cells
resulted in phenomena that were opposite to those observed in overexpressed cells.
Compared to the mock cells, a reduction in cell viability was noted following treatment with

the ferroptosis agonist Erastin (Figure 4C). However, GATA1 knockdown led to an increase
in cell viability, effectively counteracting the negative effects of Erastin (Figure 4C).
Furthermore, Erastin-treated KGN cells displayed mitochondrial shrinkage (Figure 4D), iron

TFRC is the direct target of GATA1
Previous studies have demonstrated that the downregulation of TFRC can mitigate
ferroptosis in KGN cells induced by iron overload 24. We examined the levels of TFRC in
transfected KGN cells using RT-qPCR and western blot analysis. The results indicated that
the overexpression of GATA1 significantly increased the expression level of TFRC (Figure
5A-5B), whereas the knockdown of GATA1 resulted in a decrease in TFRC expression in KGN
cells (Figure 5C-5D). To further investigate the specific relationship between GATA1 and
TFRC, we employed the JASPAR web tool to predict whether GATA1, as a transcription factor,
binds to the promoter region of TFRC. The prediction results identified a binding site for
GATA1 on the TFRC promoter (Figure 5E). To validate this prediction, we conducted a ChIP-
PCR assay, which confirmed that GATA1 binds to the TFRC promoter (Figure 5F). Dual-
luciferase reporter assays further demonstrated that GATA1 overexpression markedly
activates TFRC transcription (Figure 5G). Notably, this transcriptional induction was
completely abolished when the GATA1 binding motif on the TFRC promoter was mutated
(Figure 5H). These results provide evidence for the specific relationship between GATA1 and
TFRC at the transcriptional level.
TFRC overexpression reverses the inhibitory effect of GATA1 silencing on
ferroptosis
Based on the aforementioned results, we hypothesized that TFRC may serve as a
downstream target gene of GATA1, mediating its role in granulosa cell ferroptosis. We
regulated the expression of TFRC in KGN cells by infecting them with the LV-oeTRFC
lentivirus. Compared to the LV-vector group, the mRNA and protein levels of TFRC were
significantly elevated in the LV-oeTRFC group, indicating effective overexpression following
transfection with the LV-oeTRFC lentivirus (Figure 6A-6B). In contrast to the control cells,
GATA1 knockdown enhanced cell viability, counteracting the detrimental effects of Erastin
(Figure 6C). Interestingly, TFRC overexpression diminished the protective effect of GATA1
silencing on cell viability (Figure 6C). Furthermore, GATA1 knockdown inhibited
mitochondrial shrinkage (Figure 6D), decreased levels of lipid ROS (Figure 6E), and reduced
MDA content (Figure 6F), which was exacerbated by TFRC overexpression. Additionally, the

GATA1 knockdown alleviates ovarian ferroptosis and improves follicular quality in
PCOS-like mice
We aimed to explore whether GATA1 knockdown mitigates ovarian damage in PCOS-like
mice. Lentivirus carrying GATA1 shRNA was administered subcapsularly, and successful
GATA1 silencing was verified by RT-qPCR and western blotting (Figure 7A-7B). GATA1
depletion rescued follicle depletion and granulosa cell layer atrophy, mitigates aberrant
cystic follicle accumulation, and restores corpus luteum formation in PCOS ovaries (Figure
7C). Meanwhile, ovarian ROS, MDA, and iron accumulation were all significantly lowered
upon GATA1 knockdown (Figure 7D-7F), demonstrating the therapeutic potential of GATA1
inhibition in alleviating ovarian lesions in PCOS-like mice.
Discussion
Previous studies have elucidated that the proliferation and apoptosis of granulosa cells are
abnormal in the ovaries of patients with PCOS 28, suggesting that the dysregulation of
granulosa cells may be a contributing factor to PCOS. Recent research has indicated that
ferroptosis of granulosa cells may play a significant role in the pathogenesis of PCOS 29, yet
information regarding the ferroptosis pathway in this context remains limited. Therefore, our
findings help to bridge this knowledge gap and provide valuable insights into the mechanism
of ferroptosis in PCOS. This study is the first to demonstrate that GATA1 regulates ferroptosis
in granulosa cells and is involved in the development of PCOS. Through in vivo and in vitro
experiments, we showed that elevated expression of GATA1 in PCOS leads to ferroptosis,
thereby promoting the progression of the syndrome. Mechanistically, GATA1 enhances
granulosa cell ferroptosis in PCOS by binding to the promoter region of TFRC. Our findings
contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying granulosa
cell ferroptosis in PCOS.
Investigating the underlying causes of PCOS and acquiring ovarian tissue samples from
patients pose considerable hurdles. For this reason, a DHEA-induced mouse model is widely
adopted alternative for preclinical exploration of PCOS pathogenesis 30. Mice with DHEA-
induced PCOS core reproductive hallmarks consistent with clinical PCOS patients, including
sustained hyperandrogenism and abundant ovarian cystic follicles 31,32. Consistent with these
established observations, our research showed that 20 days of DHEA administration raised
serum testosterone concentrations and increased the number of cystic follicles in the
ovaries—confirming that this PCOS mouse model effectively recapitulates the key clinical
manifestations of human PCOS. In normal ovarian physiology, LH promotes the synthesis of
androgens (e.g., testosterone). These androgens are then converted to E2 in granulosa cells

under the stimulation of FSH 33. In women with PCOS, aberrant gonadotropin regulation
leads to elevated serum LH levels. The resulting increase in the ovarian LH/FSH ratio further
boosts circulating androgen levels, which in turn disrupts normal follicular development 34.
Our study revealed that DHEA-induced PCOS mice also exhibited elevated serum E2 and LH
levels, coupled with an increased LH/FSH ratio—indicating a disruption in the balance of sex
hormones consistent with the PCOS phenotype.
Mounting evidence indicates that the pathogenesis of PCOS is linked to ferroptosis in
ovarian tissue 35. Ferroptosis involves multiple underlying mechanisms, such as intracellular
iron ion accumulation and lipid peroxidation 36. Elevated ROS levels induce oxidative stress,
which in turn initiates lipid peroxidation and the production of MDA 37. As a terminal
metabolite of lipid peroxidation, MDA is commonly used as a key biomarker to assess
oxidative damage. GSH, a critical antioxidant molecule, functions to scavenge excess ROS
and regulate the oxidative stress response. In PCOS-related research, DHEA has been shown
to cause substantial mitochondrial impairment in ovarian tissues, accompanied by a marked
surge in ROS and MDA levels 38. Moreover, significant iron deposition has been documented
in ovarian tissues from both PCOS patients and animal models of the disease 39. Consistent
with these prior observations, our study found that DHEA administration significantly
enhanced iron accumulation in ovarian tissues of PCOS mice, concurrent with increased ROS
and MDA levels and a reduction in GSH content—collectively confirming that ferroptosis
occurs in the ovarian tissue of this PCOS model. Furthermore, our experiments revealed
notably high expression of GATA1 in ovarian tissues of PCOS-like mice. IHC staining localized
GATA1 expression primarily to the granulosa cell layer. Despite being a well-characterized
transcription factor, GATA1 has been rarely investigated in the context of PCOS. Emerging
evidence suggests a potential association between GATA1 and hyperandrogenism, a core
feature of PCOS 18. Excess androgens directly trigger oxidative stress within granulosa cells
40. As validated by He et al., H₂O₂-driven ROS accumulation directly upregulates GATA1
mRNA and protein expression 41. Collectively, these findings uncover a plausible upstream
regulatory axis underlying ovarian GATA1 overexpression in PCOS: chronic
hyperandrogenemia elicits sustained oxidative stress in granulosa cells, and the resultant
ROS may act as a positive transcriptional trigger to drive excessive GATA1 expression.
Beyond androgen-mediated signaling, elevated anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), a classic
marker of aberrant folliculogenesis in PCOS, may also act as a paracrine upstream modulator.
A recent study demonstrates that excessive follicular AMH exacerbates intrafollicular
oxidative stress 42. This observation provides a potential mechanistic connection between
AMH-associated follicular redox imbalance and our identified GATA1-dependent granulosa
cell ferroptosis pathway, hinting that AMH could act as an auxiliary biomarker reflecting
follicular iron-mediated injury and In Vitro Fertilization clinical outcomes.

While GATA1 has been implicated in ferroptosis across various disease settings, its role
appears to be tissue- and cell-type dependent, driven by divergent transcriptional target
profiles and distinct cellular redox/iron metabolic backgrounds. For example, He et al.
reports that GATA1 acts as a protective factor against oxidative stress-triggered ferroptosis
in aortic vascular smooth muscle cells under aneurysm pathology 41. Mechanistically, 5-
aminolevulinate synthase 2 (ALAS2)-induced GATA1 upregulates antioxidant and iron-
storage genes (Nrf2, SLC7A11, FTH1) to clear ROS and chelate labile iron, restraining lipid
peroxidation. By contrast, pro-ferroptotic functions of GATA1 have been documented across
immune, hematopoietic and renal tissues. Ping et al. demonstrates that GATA1 activation
promotes ferroptosis in CD8⁺ T cells 25, and Trombetti et al. further validates that GATA1
induces lipid peroxidation in K562 myeloid leukemia cells 26. In renal tubular epithelial cells,
Zhang et al. additionally reported that GATA1 transcriptionally activates LIM and cysteine-
rich domain 1 (LMCD1) to exacerbate ferroptosis in sepsis-associated acute kidney injury via
activating the Hippo/YAP pathway and facilitating CUL3-mediated Nrf2 ubiquitination
degradation 43. Our findings in KGN cells align with the reports by Ping et al., Trombetti et
al., and Zhang et al. Specifically, GATA1 overexpression in KGN cells led to decreased cell
viability, along with increased intracellular Fe²⁺ levels, lipid ROS production, MDA content,
and ACSL4 expression, as well as reduced GSH levels. Importantly, these ferroptosis-related
changes were reversed by treatment with Fer-1, indicating that GATA1 promotes ferroptosis
in KGN cells. Additionally, GATA1 silencing attenuated Erastin-induced ferroptosis in KGN
cells. The contradictory roles of GATA1 in ferroptosis across different studies may stem from
tissue-specific differences in GATA1 expression patterns and its interaction with distinct
cellular signaling pathways.
TFRC, a key protein mediating iron uptake, plays a pivotal role in regulating iron
metabolism and boosting intracellular iron levels. Increased iron concentrations have been
shown to trigger the production of ROS and induce mitochondrial impairment 44. Persistent
high ROS further suppresses SLC7A11 expression via transcriptional inhibition and
proteasomal breakdown 45,46. Meanwhile, iron uptake-driven upregulation of ACSL4 is
essential for the degradation of GPX4 24. Together, these observations underscore TFRC’s
critical involvement in the ferroptosis pathway. Notably, previous research has indicated that
TFRC promotes ferroptosis in KGN cells and may serve as a promising therapeutic target for
PCOS 24. In the present study, we verify that GATA1 acts as a transcription factor that
directly binds to the TFRC promoter region. This finding suggests that GATA1 may enhance
TFRC expression at the transcriptional level, thereby facilitating ferroptosis in granulosa
cells. Importantly, our work is the first to confirm this regulatory mechanism in KGN cells.
Additionally, we find that the inhibitory effect of GATA1 silencing on Erastin-induced
ferroptosis in KGN cells could be abrogated by TFRC overexpression. Collectively, this newly

defined GATA1-TFRC ferroptosis cascade mechanistically explains the aberrant ovarian
redox and iron homeostasis in PCOS, which provides tangible translational prospects for
targeted therapeutic strategies and disease biomarker screening in clinical practice.
Nevertheless, it is crucial to recognize the limitations inherent to this study. First, although
the DHEA-induced mouse model recapitulates key reproductive hallmarks of PCOS, it cannot
spontaneously replicate human metabolic disturbances and exhibits fundamentally divergent
hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis hormone profiles relative to patients 47,48. Second, this
work lacks validation using ovarian samples or primary granulosa cells from PCOS and
healthy women, which may create translational gaps between our mouse-derived
observations and human ovarian pathology. Future studies with human clinical specimens
are therefore required to conduct clinical correlation analyses of GATA1 expression and
further evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting the GATA1-TFRC-ferroptosis pathway.
Collectively, these constraints demand prudent interpretation when extending our murine
mechanistic conclusions to clinical PCOS pathogenesis.
In conclusion, our study identified GATA1 as a ferroptosis-related gene in PCOS through
in vitro and in vivo experimental validation. We demonstrated that GATA1 upregulates iron
deposition and lipid peroxidation in granulosa cells by enhancing the transcription and
expression of TFRC, subsequently accelerating granulosa cell ferroptosis. These findings
provide valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms of PCOS and offer potential
avenues for the development of targeted therapies for PCOS.
Materials and methods
Cell culture and transfection
The human granulosa cell line KGN was obtained from Cellverse Co., Ltd, and cultured in
DMEM/F12 medium (#BL305A, Biosharp; Hefei, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine
serum (#11011-8611, Tianhang; Hangzhou, China) in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at
37°C. KGN cells were infected with lentivirus harboring short hairpin RNA targeting GATA1
(LV-shGATA1) or a lentiviral overexpression vector for GATA1 (LV-oeGATA1). Quantitative
real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) and western blotting analyses were conducted 72 hours post-
transfection to assess transfection efficiency. Following infection, KGN cells were treated
with 100 μM Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1; #F864515, Macklin; Shanghai, China) or 100 μM Erastin
(#E872563, Macklin) for 24 h. The sequences for shRNA#1 and shRNA#2 against GATA1
were ACCAGCTTCCTGGAGACTTTG and CCATGCGGAAGGATGGTATTC, respectively.
Animal
C57BL/6 female mice, aged 21 days and weighing between 9 and 15 grams, were obtained
from Jiangsu Huachuang Sino Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. The animal trials
conducted in this study adhered to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals
and complied with Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.

All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Heilongjiang University of
Chinese Medicine. The mice were provided with a standard diet and water ad libitum, and
they were maintained under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle at a temperature of 21-23°C with a
humidity level of 45-55%. After a one-week acclimatization period, the mice were divided

established by administering DHEA (60 mg/kg body weight in 100 μL sesame oil; Aladdin

the control group received an equivalent volume of solvent.
For in vivo GATA1 knockdown experiment, the C57BL/6 female mice were divided into four

(#10009218, Guoyao; Shanghai, China) dehydration protocol, followed by a rinse in xylene

and mounting were performed. The morphological alterations of the ovarian tissues were
observed using a BX53 light microscope equipped with a DP73 digital microscope camera
(Olympus; Tokyo, Japan).

at 1000 g for 10 min, the cell precipitates were resuspended in PBS, and the intensity of DCF

their corresponding absorbance values (y, standard ΔA). The Fe 2+ content was calculated
