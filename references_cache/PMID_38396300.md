# Treatment and outcomes of clear cell sarcoma of the kidney: A report from the Children's Oncology Group studies AREN0321 and AREN03B2.
**Authors:** Benedetti DJ, Renfro LA, Tfirn I, Daw NC, Kalapurakal JA, Ehrlich PF, Khanna G, Perlman E, Warwick A, Gow KW, Paulino AC, Seibel NL, Grundy P, Fernandez CV, Geller JI, Mullen EA, Dome JS
**Journal:** Cancer (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1002/cncr.35266](https://doi.org/10.1002/cncr.35266)

## Content

BACKGROUND: On the fifth National Wilms Tumor Study, treatment for clear cell sarcoma of the kidney (CCSK) included combined vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, and etoposide (regimen I) plus radiation therapy (RT), yielding 5-year event-free survival (EFS) rates of 100%, 88%, 73%, and 29% for patients who had with stage I, II, III, and IV disease, respectively. In the Children's Oncology Group study AREN0321 of risk-adapted therapy, RT was omitted for stage I disease if lymph nodes were sampled, and carboplatin was added for stage IV disease (regimen UH-1). Patients who had stage II/III disease received regimen I with RT.

METHODS: Four-year EFS was analyzed for patients enrolled on AREN0321 and on those enrolled on AREN03B2 who received AREN0321 stage-appropriate chemotherapy.

RESULTS: Eighty-two patients with CCSK enrolled on AREN0321, 50 enrolled on AREN03B2 only. The 4-year EFS rate was 82.7% (95% confidence interval [CI], 74.8%-91.4%) for AREN0321 and 89.6% (95% CI, 81.3%-98.7%) for AREN03B2 only (p = .28). When combining studies, the 4-year EFS rates for patients who had stage I (n = 10), II (n = 47), III (n = 65), and IV (n = 10) disease were 90% (95% CI, 73.2%-100.0%), 93.4% (95% CI, 86.4%-100.0%), 82.8% (95% CI, 74.1%-92.6%), and 58.3% (95% CI, 34%-100.0%), respectively. There were no local recurrences among seven patients with stage I disease who were treated without RT. One stage I recurrence occurred in the brain, which was the most common site of relapse overall. Among patients with local stage III tumors, neither initial procedure type, margin status, nor lymph node involvement were prognostic.

CONCLUSIONS: Patients with stage I CCSK had excellent outcomes without local recurrences when treated without RT. Patients with stage IV disease appeared to benefit from a carboplatin-containing regimen, although their outcomes remained unsatisfactory. Further research is needed to improve outcomes for patients with advanced-stage disease (ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers NCT00335556 and NCT00898365).

Clear cell sarcoma of the kidney (CCSK) is a rare childhood kidney cancer, with a different metastatic pattern and inferior outcomes when compared to Wilms tumor.1 Outcomes for patients with CCSK have improved over time.1–4 The fifth National Wilms Tumor Study (NWTS-5) studied a 6-month regimen of alternating cycles of vincristine/doxorubicin/cyclophosphamide with cyclophosphamide/etoposide (Regimen I) with radiation therapy (RT), which yielded a 5-year event-free survival (EFS) of 79% and overall survival (OS) of 90%, similar to the longer NWTS-4 regimen (15 months of vincristine/dactinomycin/doxorubicin) with lower cumulative doxorubicin dosage (225 mg/m2 vs 300 mg/m2).5 Outcomes varied by stage, with a suggestion of improved outcomes for stage I and II disease with Regimen I. Patients with stage I disease on NWTS-5 had 5-year EFS and OS of 100%, confirming the findings of an earlier analysis from NWTS 1–4.6 The second Japanese Wilms Tumor Study (JWiTS-2) further reinforced the excellent outcomes in patients with stage I CCSK, reporting 100% EFS and OS in 11 patients treated identically to NWTS-5.7 By contrast, patients with stage IV disease on NWTS-5 had unsatisfactory outcomes, with 5-year EFS and OS of 29% (95% CI, 0%, 76%) and 36% (95% CI, 0%, 75%), respectively.5

In AREN0321, the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) “Treatment of High-Risk Renal Tumors” study, therapy for children with CCSK was stratified based on disease stage, with modifications from NWTS-5 therapy. A primary objective was to maintain the excellent EFS of patients with stage I CCSK using Regimen I, but without abdominal RT. A secondary objective was to describe the outcomes of patients with stage IV CCSK treated with Regimen UH-1, a regimen intensified over regimen I with the addition of carboplatin. Patients with stages II and III CCSK continued to receive Regimen I, given acceptable outcomes on NWTS-5, and benefits of shorter duration and lower cumulative doxorubicin dosage compared to the NWTS-4 regimen.

Here we report the outcomes and characteristics of patients with CCSK treated on AREN0321. To increase knowledge of this rare patient group, we also analyzed outcomes of patients who enrolled only on AREN03B2 (Renal Tumor Biology, Banking and Classification Study). Although AREN03B2 is not a therapeutic clinical trial and did not prescribe treatment regimens, data on treatment and outcomes were submitted for a subset of patients. Patients with available outcome data who were treated the same as those enrolled on AREN0321 were included in this analysis.

The National Cancer Institute Central Institutional Review Board (CIRB) and local IRBs approved the protocols. Local research ethics boards provided approval for institutions without CIRB agreements. All participants or their legally authorized guardians provided written consent at the time of enrollment.

Subjects enrolled on AREN03B2 underwent central review of diagnostic imaging, and pathology and surgery from the required upfront diagnostic procedure (biopsy or resection), as previously described.8 These required elements yielded an initial risk assignment, which determined eligibility for four therapeutic renal tumor studies that were concurrently available, including AREN0321 for patients with CCSK and other ‘high-risk’ renal tumors. For subjects enrolled onto AREN03B2 between March 2006 and August 2019 and not subsequently enrolled onto a therapeutic study, enrolling sites were required to submit data on treatment received and long-term outcomes upon activation of Amendment 5 on June 3, 2013. Before this, enrolling sites had the option but were not required to submit these data.

Patients enrolled on AREN0321 between June 2006 to December 2012, after undergoing required central review and risk assignment on AREN03B2. Patients with CCSK were required to undergo bone scan and MRI of the brain for staging at diagnosis and end of therapy, just like they were on NWTS-5. Patients with stages I-III CCSK were assigned to treatment with Regimen I, and those with stage IV CCSK were assigned to treatment with Regimen UH-1, modified in October 2008 due to greater-than-expected toxicity to reduce doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, and etoposide doses (Regimen “Revised UH-1”). Regimen I on AREN0321 included minor modifications to that administered on NWTS-5, with addition of a dose of vincristine and cyclophosphamide to the first week of chemotherapy (NWTS-5 administered doxorubicin alone), and dosing by age (NWTS-5 dosed by weight regardless of age, AREN0321 dosed by weight for infants under 1 year of age (vincristine under 3 years of age) and by m2 for children ≥ 1 year of age (vincristine ≥ 3 years)), with dose-reductions for infants under 1 year of age (supplemental file, as previously described8,9). RT was not indicated for stage I disease with confirmed lymph node sampling. Patients with stage I disease without LN sampling were still categorized as stage I but assigned to receive RT. Patients with local stages II-III disease received RT (10.8 Gy) to the flank or whole abdomen. RT was also prescribed for metastatic sites: lungs (12 Gy), liver (19.8 Gy), bone (25.2 Gy), brain (30 Gy).

All patients with CCSK treated on AREN0321 were included in these analyses. Inclusion of “AREN03B2-only” patients confirmed by central review to have CCSK in pooled outcome analyses were restricted to those for whom outcome data were available, and whose reporting forms indicated treatment with Regimen I for Stages I-III or Regimen UH-1 or Revised UH-1 for Stage IV.

The primary endpoint was EFS, defined as the time from diagnosis to the earliest of relapse, progression, second malignancy, or death due to any cause, with patients not experiencing an event of interest being right-censored at their last disease assessment. OS is also reported, defined as the time from diagnosis to death due to any cause, with surviving patients right-censored at their last known survival status.

Descriptive statistics for demographics, disease, and treatment features were calculated overall and between studies (AREN0321 and AREN03B2-only), with differences between studies tested using Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and chi-square or fisher exact tests for categorical variables. EFS and OS were plotted using Kaplan-Meier estimates with 95% confidence bands, and 4-year rates of EFS and OS with 95% confidence intervals were reported overall and by disease stage within each study. Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the prognostic impact of age at diagnosis in the entire study-pooled cohort, while the prognostic effects of lymph node (LN) involvement, initial procedure type, and nephrectomy margin status were assessed in local stage III patients from both studies combined. Rates of adverse events and receipt of RT specific to AREN0321 were also described. All analyses were performed using R.10

One-hundred ninety-nine patients enrolled on AREN03B2 and had CCSK by central pathology review. Among them, 82 enrolled on AREN0321, while 117 were followed exclusively on AREN03B2. Of those on AREN03B2-only, 85 had treatment and outcome data available; the remaining 32 enrolled on AREN03B2-only before submission of these data became required. Among 85 AREN03B2-only patients with treatment and outcome data, 50 were treated as per AREN0321. The other 35 patients were treated with Regimen UH-1 (n=16, central review stages I-III), Regimen I (n=1, central review stage IV), alternative regimens (17), or no chemo (1).

One-hundred thirty-two patients with CCSK treated as per AREN0321 (82 on AREN0321, 50 from AREN03B2-only) were included in the analyses. Their descriptive statistics are shown in Table 1. No demographic, disease, or treatment factors differed significantly between AREN0321 and AREN03B2-only cohorts. Overall, 92.4% were stages I-III, and 7.6% had metastases (bone (20%), liver (20%), lung only (30%) or lung and bone together (30%)). Eighty-nine percent had LN sampled at nephrectomy, of whom 28% had LN involvement. All patients with stage I disease on both AREN0321 and AREN03B2-only had LN sampled. RT was documented as given in 87% of patients.

Overall and disease stage-specific 4-year EFS and OS for AREN0321 and AREN03B2-only cohorts are shown in Table 2. In general, outcomes did not differ significantly between studies, either overall or by stage. For all stages combined, with a median follow-up of 5.7 years, 4-year EFS was 82.7% (95% CI: 74.8%, 91.4%) for AREN0321 and 89.6% (95% CI: 81.3%, 98.7%) for AREN03B2-only (Figure 1, Supplemental Figure 1). Four-year OS was 95.1% (95% CI: 90.5%, 99.9%) for AREN0321 and 93.9% (95% CI: 87.5%, 100%) for AREN03B2-only (Figure 2, Supplemental Figure 2). Within disease stages, 4-year EFS ranged from 87.5% (stage I) to 75% (stage IV) on AREN0321, and 100% (stage I) to 44.4% (stage IV) on AREN03B2-only (Figure 3, Supplemental Figure 3). Four-year OS ranged from 100% (stage I) to 75% (stage IV) on AREN0321 and 100% (stage I) to 80% (stage IV) on AREN03B2-only (Figure 4, Supplemental Figure 4).

In both studies combined, 19 patients experienced an EFS event, consisting of 15 relapses/progressions and four deaths. All deaths as first event occurred during Regimen I; 1 each from COVID-19, cytomegalovirus, fungemia, and smoke inhalation/burns. Sites of relapse were brain/CNS (n=8), bone (2), lung (1), kidney bed (1), leg soft tissue (1), combined brain and abdominal wall (1), and combined kidney bed and bone (1). Fourteen relapses (93.3%) occurred within 3 years of diagnosis; 10 within two years, and 4 between years 2 and 3. Median time to relapse was 1.6 years, and the latest relapse occurred at 3.5 years. Among patients with local stage III tumors, neither initial procedure type (HR 0.88 for biopsy versus nephrectomy (95% CI: 0.2, 3.88), p = 0.861), margin status (HR 1.01 (95% CI: 0.31, 3.3), p = 0.993) nor LN involvement (HR 1.66 (95% CI: 0.5, 5.53), p = 0.407) were prognostic (Supplemental Table 1). Among stage IV patients, no difference in likelihood of relapse was apparent between patients who could be distinguished to have received original Regimen UH-1 vs. revised Regimen UH-1 (data not shown).

Specific sites and doses of RT were reported on AREN0321 only; for patients followed on AREN03B2-only, radiation details were only collected as a “Yes” to whether radiation was received during frontline therapy. Among 35 patients with left-sided tumors, 31 (89%) received left flank (n=28) or whole abdominal (3) RT. Among 47 with right-sided tumors, 44 (93.6%) received right flank (n=40) or whole abdominal (4) RT. Of the 12 patients who experienced relapse or progression, none had reported prior RT to the relapse sites. Of the four patients with metastatic disease at diagnosis, two (both with lung and bone metastases) received RT to both metastatic sites, while the other two (one with fully resected liver metastases, one with bone metastases) did not receive RT to these sites. One of the eight stage I patients received RT to the left flank against protocol therapy and did not relapse. Another Stage I patient (who did not receive RT) relapsed to the CNS. Both stage I patients enrolled on AREN03B2-only received RT. RT was not reported for ten patients with stages II-IV disease (all on AREN03B2-only); of these, one relapsed.

For Regimen I, AREN0321 required reporting of only Grade 5 and Grade 4 unexpected and possibly related toxicities. Among 78 patients, two grade 5 toxicities were reported, one not otherwise specified and one from lung infection. For Regimen UH-1, routine reporting was required for grade 3 and 4 non-hematologic toxicities and for grade 5 toxicities. No grade 5 toxicities were reported in the patients with CCSK. The most prevalent grade 3 and 4 toxicities were febrile neutropenia, infection, anorexia, colitis, dehydration, and pneumonia/pulmonary infiltrates (Supplemental Table 2).

Our study sought to examine whether AREN0321’s stage-based treatment approach impacted outcomes of patients with CCSK. Outcomes for stage I CCSK remained excellent without flank RT, with no local recurrences among patients who did not receive RT. The only event was a distant (CNS) recurrence, which local RT likely would not have prevented.

SIOP’s Umbrella Protocol also omits RT from patients with stage I CCSK,11 however outcomes for patients with stage I CCSK on previous SIOP studies appear to be inferior to those seen in our study or recent NWTSG and JWiTS studies.5,7,12 Among 80 patients with stage I CCSK treated on studies SIOP-93–01 (etoposide, carboplatin, ifosfamide, doxorubicin or epirubicin) and 2001 (vincristine, dactinomycin, doxorubicin or epirubicin), 5-year EFS and OS were 79.0% (95% CI: 70.4%, 88.7%) and 87.1% (95% CI: 79.5%, 95.3%), respectively.12 SIOP 2001 protocol did include a change in chemotherapy, omitting ifosfamide and carboplatin, which yielded poorer OS (79.6% vs 90.1% on SIOP 93–01, p=0.07), and a trend toward inferior EFS (71.5% vs 82.6%, p=0.23). The seemingly superior outcomes for patients with stage I CCSK treated on the COG regimens suggests that stage I CCSK based on upfront nephrectomy is more favorable than stage I CCSK after neoadjuvant chemotherapy. This observation makes intuitive sense given that staging differences between COG and SIOP have resulted in COG selecting a very small and highly favorable subgroup of patients with CCSK for omission of RT. In the SIOP studies 39.5% (75/191) of patients with CCSK had stage I, compared to only 7.6% (10/132) in our study using COG staging. A patient who has a COG stage II or III tumor (if resected upfront) could have a SIOP stage I tumor after preoperative chemotherapy. But patients like this should not be directly compared to a COG stage I patient, because COG stages II and III patients are known to have worse outcomes. Among 69 patients with stage I CCSK who did not receive RT on the SIOP studies, there were two local and 10 distant recurrences. The low prevalence of local recurrences in the SIOP setting and absence of local recurrences in the COG setting indicate that RT can safely be omitted for stage I CCSK provided that LN sampling is performed.

In addition to the lack of local relapses amongst stage I patients, patients with stage II CCSK had excellent EFS, with no local recurrences among 47 patients. All three local recurrences occurred in patients with local stage III disease. Omission of RT for patients with stage II CCSK could be investigated in a future study, particularly as the late effects of abdominal RT continue to become more clear.13

Patients with metastatic CCSK at diagnosis remain rare, with just four patients treated on AREN0321, a similar proportion as prior studies.4,5,12 This represents too few patients to determine whether Regimen UH-1 improved outcomes for such patients as compared to NWTS-5. Pooling with patients followed only on AREN03B2 reveals outcomes that suggest that chemotherapy intensification may be beneficial (4-year EFS of 58.3% (95% CI: 34%, 100%) on the AREN0321/AREN03B2 cohorts compared to 5-year EFS of 29% (95% CI: 0%, 76%) on NWTS-5. Similar to the AREN cohorts, patients with stage IV CCSK treated on the SIOP studies had 5-year EFS of 58.6% (95% CI: 36.2%, 95.0%) using carboplatin/etoposide/doxorubicin and ifosfamide or cyclophosphamide, suggesting value in adding carboplatin.12 The OS of patients with stage IV CCSK (77.8%; 95% CI 54.9%, 100%) was noticeably superior to the EFS (58.3%; 95% CI: 34%, 100%), which is of potential future interest given patients who relapsed did so after already intensive treatment. The small numbers of metastatic patients, combined with the fact that later line treatment data is not required to be submitted on COG studies, limits conclusions that can be made about optimal treatment of relapse, or about how we might further modify upfront treatment to prevent relapse.

Most patients with CCSK present with stages II or III disease and were treated on AREN0321 with Regimen I and abdominal or flank RT, nearly identically to their treatment on NWTS-5. One difference was that Regimen I on AREN0321 included an additional course of cyclophosphamide (1320 mg/m2) and vincristine as compared to Regimen I on NWTS-5. The outcomes of the stages II and III patients on AREN0321 appear similar to those from NWTS-5; 4-year EFS for stages II and III were 88.4% and 78.6% vs 5-year EFS of 88% and 73% on NWTS-5, respectively.5 While no formal analysis compared these groups, it is unlikely that the additional chemotherapy influenced outcomes. Given risk of infertility and secondary leukemia associated with cumulative cyclophosphamide exposure, it would be reasonable to omit the additional doses.

Pooling AREN0321 and AREN03B2 cohorts to examine prognostic factors in patients with stage III CCSK did not identify any differences in outcomes based on timing of nephrectomy (upfront vs post-chemotherapy), margin status, or LN involvement. Since patients with stage III CCSK had favorable outcomes, the study population may be too small, with too few relapses, to elucidate prognostic factors. Alternatively, different prognostic factors, such as biology, may be more relevant to CCSK.

The pattern of relapse of CCSK continues to be of interest. On AREN0321 and AREN03B2, the site of first relapse included brain or CNS in 9 of 15 patients, bone (including spine) in only 3 patients, and lungs in just a single patient. This indicates an ongoing shift toward more relapses occurring in the brain rather than the bone or lungs, as seen on NWTS-55 and in SIOP studies.12,14 These findings underscore the importance of obtaining brain imaging at diagnosis and during post-treatment surveillance, and suggest that routine interval nuclear medicine imaging for bone recurrences as required on AREN0321 may not be needed. The cause of this shift remains unclear, but inclusion of more CNS-penetrating therapy should be considered for future studies of upfront treatment of CCSK. Ifosfamide is a good candidate for consideration, as it has excellent CNS penetration,15,16 has been proposed to prevent CNS metastases in other malignancies,17 and recent evidence suggests it is well tolerated in children with solitary kidneys.18

Additionally, data from SIOP 93–01 and 2001 studies suggested improved outcomes for patients with CCSK treated with ifosfamide as compared to cyclophosphamide.12 While the brain was the most common site of relapse reported in that SIOP analysis, it does not report what proportion of those patients received ifosfamide versus cyclophosphamide or neither alkylator. Furthermore, CNS screening at diagnosis was not required or routinely performed on SIOP 93–01, such that CNS disease categorized as a relapse might instead have been asymptomatic CNS metastases present at diagnoses. One series of patients with relapsed CCSK involving the brain treated with ifosfamide/carboplatin/etoposide (ICE) showed durable survival in six of eight patients reported.19 Among five Austrian patients with CNS relapses of CCSK, three were long-term survivors with treatment with ICE.20 One of these patients also received intrathecal mafosphamide, highlighting an alternative consideration of adding intrathecal chemotherapy for CCSK. Because this patient also received ICE, the role of the intrathecal mafosphamide is unclear, and there are no data on the efficacy of other common intrathecally-administered chemotherapeutics (e.g. methotrexate, cytarabine) for CCSK.

Given the shift toward earlier recurrences of CCSK seen on NWTS-4 and -5 versus prior reports, the present study examined the timing of recurrences. Nearly all relapses (14 of 15) occurred within 3 years, consistent with NWTS-5 in which 20 of 23 recurrences occurred within 3 years.5 The latest relapse among our cohort was 3.5 years, similar to SIOP’s latest relapse occurring at 4.1 years (with median age 2.0 years).12 These consistent observations suggest that time to recurrence may have shortened with our current treatment approach, and that the duration of post-treatment imaging surveillance should be shortened for patients with CCSK.

Our findings are limited by the small number of patients with stages I and IV disease. As these groups represent the patients for whom AREN0321 changed treatment, the small sample size limits the conclusions that can be made about the effects of the treatment changes. An additional limitation is that patients followed on AREN03B2-only were required to report outcomes of relapse, progression or death, however some outcomes and treatment data are missing, including radiotherapy details for some who received chemotherapy as per AREN0321. Additionally, our study did not include data on distinct biological types of CCSK, as these analyses were not performed on these patients. While most (85–90%) CCSK harbor internal tandem duplications of BCOR,21,22 another distinct subset (5–10%) have YWHAE-NUTM2 fusions,23 as well as a small minority with BCOR-CCNB3 fusions.24 It is unknown whether these biological differences influence disease patterns or treatment response.25 Future study using banked samples from AREN03B2 may provide valuable insight.

AREN0321 studied reduction of therapy for patients with stage I CCSK and augmentation of therapy for patients with stage IV CCSK. Although firm conclusions are limited by the rarity of these subgroups, patients with stage I CCSK who had LN sampling had excellent outcomes when treated without radiation, suggesting a new standard of care in the COG-treatment context. Patients with stage IV CCSK appeared to benefit from a carboplatin-containing regimen. Further work is needed to identify therapeutic targets and optimal treatment for these rare patients and to elucidate clinical and biologic prognostic factors that would refine risk stratification approaches. Strategies to prevent CNS relapse and reduce cumulative therapy toxicity should be considered.
