# A panoramic view of cell population dynamics in mammalian aging.
**Authors:** Zhang Z, Schaefer C, Jiang W, Lu Z, Lee J, Sziraki A, Abdulraouf A, Wick B, Haeussler M, Li Z, Molla G, Satija R, Zhou W, Cao J
**Journal:** Science (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1126/science.adn3949](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adn3949)

## Content

To elucidate aging-associated cellular population dynamics, we present PanSci, a single-cell transcriptome atlas profiling >20 million cells from 623 mouse tissues across different life stages, sexes, and genotypes. This comprehensive dataset reveals >3000 different cellular states and >200 aging-associated cell populations. Our panoramic analysis uncovered organ-, lineage-, and sex-specific shifts in cellular dynamics during life-span progression. Moreover, we identify both systematic and organ-specific alterations in immune cell populations associated with aging. We further explored the regulatory roles of the immune system on aging and pinpointed specific age-related cell population expansions that are lymphocyte dependent. Our "cell-omics" strategy enhances comprehension of cellular aging and lays the groundwork for exploring the complex cellular regulatory networks in aging and aging-associated diseases.

The adage “a chain is only as strong as its weakest link” aptly applies to the aging process. Within the diverse cellular landscape of different organs, certain cell types undergo profound alterations in their states or populations as we age (1–3). These changes not only affect the overall function of the organism but also play a critical role in the onset of age-associated diseases (4). Therefore, cataloging these vulnerable cell types is critical for understanding the cellular basis of aging-related pathologies and identifying potential interventions to mitigate the dysregulated cell population changes associated with aging.

However, comprehensively characterizing these aging-related cellular changes presents considerable challenges. The primary barrier is the inherent heterogeneity within cell populations, which can obscure the identification of less common but crucial cell types involved in aging. Although advancements in single-cell genomic profiling offer a powerful route in characterizing cell state heterogeneity (5–8), current studies are still limited by the throughput and cost of conventional single-cell techniques and thus mainly focus on examining the abundant cell types, neglecting the intricate dynamics of rare cell states or subtypes as well as their variations across different individuals or conditions (e.g., sexes, genotypes). In addition, current large-scale single-cell atlas efforts, which mostly rely on integrating multiple datasets generated using various methodologies or by different laboratories, face the challenge of batch effects that hinder the identification of rare cell types and complicate cross-organ comparisons of broadly distributed cell types, such as immune or endothelial cells (9–11).

Addressing these challenges, we present PanSci, a panoramic view of cell population dynamics in mammalian aging. Building on our previous profiling of the aging brain (12), this study examines the transcriptional states of >20 million cells sourced from 623 mouse samples across 13 additional organs and tissues, including lung, heart, liver, kidney, muscle, stomach, duodenum, jejunum, ileum, colon, brown adipose tissue (BAT), inguinal adipose tissue (iWAT), and perigonadal adipose tissue (gWAT) (Fig. 1, A and B, and fig. S1). These samples were collected from a cohort representing various ages, sexes, and genotypes (fig. S1, A to C, and table S1). Specifically, we included eight sex-balanced wild-type C57BL/6 mice in three age groups (6, 12, and 23 months). Meanwhile, we profiled two immunodeficient mutants, B6.129S7-Rag1tm1Mom/J (13) and B6.Cg-Prkdcscid/SzJ (14), and their age-matched wild-type control at 3 and 16 months old, with four sex-balanced replicates each (Fig. 1A, top). These mutants, characterized by the depletion of mature lymphocytes, allow us to explore the regulatory role of immune cells on other cell types during the aging process. Moreover, the inclusion of multiple age groups and genotypes in our study enables cross-validation of the identified cell population changes in aging.

Single-cell transcriptome profiles were generated using EasySci, an optimized single-cell combinatorial indexing method for organism-scale cell population analysis (12, 15–18). EasySci features full-gene-body coverage, high scalability, and low cost, facilitating the profiling of >20 million cells by a single person. We extensively optimized the cell lysis conditions to extract high-quality nuclei from frozen tissues across diverse mammalian organs, effectively reducing the batch effects commonly associated with conventional tissue digestion and cell isolation approaches (fig. S2). The extracted nuclei were sorted and barcoded through indexed reverse transcription, indexed ligation, and indexed amplification (Fig. 1A, bottom). Sequencing was performed through 25 S4 runs with the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system, yielding >200 billion raw reads. After filtering out low-quality cells and doublets, we recovered 21,786,931 single-nucleus gene expression profiles [including the 1,469,111 brain cells profiled in (12)] (fig. S1G). An average of 1613 different transcripts [unique molecular identifiers (UMIs)] were detected per cell (median = 1034 UMIs) (fig. S1D), and an average of 1,562,909 cells was recovered per organ [Fig. 1B; maximum, 3,767,262 cells (from kidney); minimum, 696,410 cells (from muscle)]. Of note, the number of transcripts detected per nucleus in our dataset is lower than in Tabula Muris Senis (TMS) (6) (a median of 5930 UMIs), likely because of differences in sequencing depth [8950 reads per cell in PanSci versus 850,000 reads per cell in TMS fluorescence-activated cell sorting data (5)] and assay type (nuclei in PanSci versus whole cell in TMS). Nevertheless, the signal per cell in our study is comparable to or exceeds that observed in other single-cell atlases such as the Mouse Cell Atlas (19).

To identify heterogeneous cellular states across various organs, we adopted a two-step approach similar to our prior work (12). We first subjected the gene expression profiles from each organ or tissue to dimension reduction and Leiden clustering (20). A total of 239 organ-specific main cell types were characterized across 13 different organs or tissues, in addition to the 31 cell types previously identified in the brain (12) (Fig. 1C and table S2). Each main cell type was identified across multiple replicates (a median of 48 replicates per cell type; fig. S3A) and represented by a median of 15,922 cells, ranging from 2,465,275 cells from proximal tubule cells in the kidney to only six cells from osteoblasts in muscle (fig. S3C). On average, 56 different marker genes were identified per main cell type (table S3). Marker genes were defined by a minimum fivefold difference in expression between the top-ranked and second-ranked cell types and a minimum expression of 50 transcripts per million (TPM) in the targeted cell type. The identity of these cell types was confirmed by cell type–specific gene markers from published single-cell datasets (10, 21–33) (figs. S4 to S16) and validated by integration analysis with the published mouse cell atlas (6) (fig. S17). Notably, the scalability of our platform has effectively minimized the batch effects that typically arise during the integration of single-cell datasets generated in multiple laboratories in conventional consortium-level studies (6, 34). For example, the same cell type (e.g., type II myonuclei) from different individuals exhibited consistent clustering patterns in the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) space without batch correction (fig. S18, B and C). Further validation, incorporating all cells from wild-type animals from various organs and tissues, confirmed that cells of the same lineage (e.g., immune or endothelial lineages) from different organs and tissues also clustered coherently (Fig. 1C), demonstrating the robustness of our approach against batch effects.

Exploring cellular heterogeneity and dynamics at a higher resolution, we then performed subclustering analysis on each main cell type using an integrated matrix that combines both gene count and exon count information. This analysis leveraged a distinctive feature of EasySci to cocapture both poly-A and non-poly-A transcripts, allowing for extensive coverage of entire gene regions. Similar to our previous study (12), the combined information increased the clustering resolution (fig. S18C). In addition to 359 subclusters identified in the brain (12), we detected 3925 subclusters across 13 other organs and tissues, with each subcluster observed in multiple replicates (a median of 45 replicates per subcluster), represented by a median of 1035 cells (fig. S3, D to F). More than 90% of these subclusters (3535 out of 3925) can be distinguished by specific gene markers (table S4). To validate the transcriptomic signature of each subcluster, we trained a support vector machine classifier using 80% of our single-cell gene expression data. When applied to the remaining 20% of data, the classifier demonstrated high accuracy in identifying different subclusters, thereby confirming their distinct transcriptomic profiles (fig. S18, D to G).

Understanding the molecular and cellular differences between sexes across various tissues is essential for gaining insight into the progression of sex-biased diseases and age-related conditions (35–39). By incorporating equal sex representation in all age and genotype groups, our study provides an in-depth view of the molecular and cellular basis of sex differences across different organs. Analyzing gene expression for each of the 239 organ-specific main cell types, we identified cell type–specific genes that are male (n = 4209) or female (n = 2963) specific (fig. S19). These genes include those expressed from sex chromosomes—Uty and Ddx3y for males, and Xist, Kdm5c, and Kdm6a for females—as well as autosomal genes, such as male-specific expression of Gphn [a gene involved in the inhibitory synapse regulation pathways (40)] and female-specific long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) Gm47283, a gene related to myocardial infarction (41) (fig. S19, A and B).

Despite these sex-specific molecular differences, cells from most cell types in male and female animals still coembedded closely in low-dimensional space (fig. S20A), suggesting that the overall cellular identity programs are largely conserved. However, in a subset of main cell types, sex differences dominate molecular programs and lead to the emergence of distinct sex-specific cellular states. Notable examples include hepatocytes from the liver, proximal tubule cells from the kidney, and adipose stem and progenitor cells (ASPCs) from perigonadal adipose tissues (fig. S20A). Moreover, the large number of differentially expressed genes between sexes observed in these cell types further validate their distinct molecular profiles (fig. S20B). We also observed a global sex dimorphic pattern in adipocytes from multiple organs and tissues, which is masked in previous single-cell characterization of white adipose tissue during integration and in brown adipocytes from BAT (fig. S20). This aligns with bulk gene expression analysis in humans (42) and differential metabolic adaptations of females and males in adipose tissue (43). These observed sex differences in specific cell types are unlikely to stem from experimental batch effects, as our unbiased profiling of all cell types in each organ revealed minimal sex differences in other cell types from the same organs (fig. S20).

Most of the sex-specific genes identified are highly cell type specific and closely linked to the distinctive functions of these cells (fig. S21, A to C). For example, female ASPCs prominently up-regulate genes associated with mammary gland development (44), including Esr1, Tgfbr2, and Vegfa (fig. S21, D and G). Meanwhile, female proximal tubule cells elevated expression of organic anion and acid transporters such as Slc7a12, Slc22a29, and Slc22a19 (fig. S21, E and H), whereas female hepatocytes show significantly enriched expression of genes involved in fatty acid and xenobiotic metabolism processes, such as Cyp2b9, Cyp2b13, Cyp2c69, Cyp2c40, Sult2a1, and Cyp3a41b (fig. S21, F and I). This aligns with the increased demands for cholesterol metabolism in females (45, 46). These sex-associated changes in specific cell types are confirmed across independent samples from different ages (fig. S21, G to L). Furthermore, we noted sex-specific gene expression changes associated with aging. For instance, female hepatocytes exhibit up-regulation in the CYP3A family genes (e.g., cyp3a16, cyp3a44, and cyp3a41a), potentially because of changes in estrogen levels with aging (47) (fig. S21I). Conversely, hepatocytes in males at later life stages shows a significant increase in genes from the acute-phase protein family (e.g., Saa1 and Saa2) involved in the inflammatory response (48) (fig. S21L), suggesting a more pronounced inflammatory signaling compared with females.

These sex-specific effects extend down to the subcluster level, as demonstrated by the identification of 73 subclusters displaying significant differential abundance between males and females across all age groups (fig. S22). Notably, our analysis not only reaffirmed the presence of conserved sexually dimorphic cell types in the liver and kidney (37) but also brought to light underreported cell types in other organs. Taking ASPCs from gWAT as an example, our clustering analysis of 112,515 cells revealed two main ASPCs subtypes: Ly6C− CD9− PDGFRβ+ cells (adipocyte precursor cells, APCs) and Ly6C+ PDGFRβ+ cells (fibroinflammatory progenitors, FIPs) (fig. S23A), consistent with recent publications (49, 50). These subtypes display divergent molecular states between sexes (fig. S23B). Female-specific APCs are characterized by genes linked to the negative regulation of cell growth [e.g., Fgf12 (51)] and inhibition of brown fat–related gene expression [e.g., Dlgap1 (52)]. Male-specific APCs exhibit genes related to metabolic pathways, including cyclic nucleotide signaling (e.g., Pde11a) and calcium signaling (e.g., Hpca). Furthermore, female-specific FIPs showed high expression of Fam155a and Aif1l, with female-specific Aif1l protein expression validated in WAT by recent studies (53). Male-specific FIPs expressed genes associated with insulin sensitivity [e.g., Bmp7 (54)] and immune suppression [e.g., Sema3e (55)]. This finding was further substantiated by integrating our data with a recently published white adipose tissue atlas (26) (fig. S23, C and D). These discoveries highlight how sex influences cellular diversity and specification across subtypes, emphasizing the necessity of considering sex as a fundamental factor in biological studies.

To obtain a global view of aging-associated gene signatures and population dynamics, we first performed differential gene expression analysis across 239 main cell types from various organs and identified cell type–specific genes that are up-regulated (n = 8178) or down-regulated (n = 7208) with aging. Immune cells such as B cells and T cells showed significant numbers of differentially expressed genes, indicating their heightened vulnerability to aging (fig. S24A). Up-regulated aging-associated genes shared across >10 cell types are predominantly involved in immune response (e.g., B2m, C4b, Trim30a), stress response (e.g., Cdk8, Prkn, Mid1, Marchf3), and DNA repair and chromatin remodeling (e.g., Hdac9, Runx2, Lrmda) (fig. S24B). In contrast, down-regulated genes are enriched in pathways implicated in circadian rhythm regulation, such as Arntl and Npas2 (fig. S24C). This pattern highlights altered circadian dynamics in aged tissues, consistent with previous studies demonstrating a loss of rhythmicity in circulating hormones during aging (56).

To investigate aging-associated cell population dynamics, we quantified cell type–specific proportions in both main cell types and subclusters within individual replicates across various age groups, followed by differential abundance analysis controlling for sex and cell number per replicates. We detected 23 main cell types and 374 subclusters undergoing significant population changes in both age intervals: 3 versus 16 months and 6 versus 23 months (Fig. 2A and fig. S26). These changes were consistently observed across both sexes (Fig. 2, B to E), apart from a few outliers that potentially correspond to sex-specific cellular states. Reassuringly, most of these main cell types (21 out of 23) and subclusters (280 out of 374) demonstrated robust and consistent changes during both intervals and are referred to as “aging-associated cell populations” in our subsequent analysis. Also, we manually examined the specificity of marker genes for each aging-associated cell population to ensure that the selected markers were both statistically significant and biologically relevant to the identified subclusters (figs. S27 to S30).

These aging-associated cell populations exhibited defined dynamic patterns across distinct life stages (Fig. 3A). For instance, we observed a significant age-associated expansion in immune cells, including lymphocytes and myeloid cells, across multiple organs, aligning with findings from previous studies (57). Certain age-associated cell types also exhibited a sex difference in population dynamics. For example, although Mirg+ cells declined in skeletal muscle during aging in both males and females, the reduction was more pronounced in females, possibly owing to their higher baseline levels in youth (Fig. 3B). These cells correspond to a rare subset of muscle myonuclear populations, marked by an elevated expression of several lncRNAs—Meg3, Rian, Gm37899, and Mirg—all stemming from the Dlk1-Dio3 locus, containing the largest microRNA megacluster in mammals (58) (Fig. 3, C and D). The observed decline can be attributed to the aging-associated down-regulation of miRNAs from the Dlk1-Dio3 locus, critical for mitochondrial biogenesis and reactive oxidative species protection, suggesting diminished cellular resilience against aging-induced stress (59).

To delve deeper into the evolving landscape of cell populations across the life span, we next clustered all age-associated cell subclusters on the basis of their cell population dynamics across five age stages. A substantial proportion of these subclusters underwent consistent alterations throughout life. Specifically, we identified 174 subclusters that expanded, 56 that became depleted, and 50 with transient dynamics (Fig. 3E and fig. S31). Using our multitimepoint dataset, we discerned the varied pace at which different cellular states altered across cell lineages and organs (Fig. 3, F and G). To further investigate these aging-associated cell populations and their specific molecular markers, we integrated all cells from consistently expanding or depleting subclusters for clustering and dimension reduction (Fig. 3H). This analysis led to the characterization of distinct cellular dynamic patterns at different life stages, accompanied by both organ- and lineage-specific cellular populations, as discussed below.

The initial two cell population dynamic patterns predominantly indicate cell loss (Fig. 3I). The first dynamic pattern, spanning 3 to 6 months, is characterized by a decline in Gmpr+ activated brown adipocytes in BAT, and Bmpr1b+Dkk2+ tenocytes in muscle (Fig. 3K and fig. S27). The subsequent pattern, extending from 6 to 12 months, is notable for the marked decrease in CD4+ naïve T cells across various organs and Dlc1+Spock1+ intestinal macrophages (fig. S32D). As a continuum of muscle degeneration, we witnessed a reduction of Flt1+Shank3+ tenocytes from muscle and Ttn+Neb+ mural cells from gWAT. The age-related adipose decline is further observed in Akap13+ adipocytes from gWAT. Additionally, this period featured a decrease in functional epithelial cells across several organs, including Mirg+ cells from muscle and colon, Cybrd1+ gastric mucous cells, and a range of epithelial cells in the kidney [e.g., Zfp207+ connecting tubule cells, Sgcd+ type B intercalated cells, Rbfox1+ descending thin limb of loop of Henle (LOH) cells, Opcml+ podocytes, and Tspan18+ principal cells] (Fig. 3L and figs. S27 and S28).

The next two cell population dynamic patterns represent expansions in which most of the cells involved are immune cells (Fig. 3J). The third pattern, initiating from 12 months, is dominated by an expansive growth in T cell subtypes (e.g., CD8+Gzmk+ cytotoxic T cells and γδ T cells), IgM+ plasma cells (IgM, immunoglobulin M), Tbx21+ aging-associated B cells (ABCs), and various subtypes of cells from myeloid lineages [e.g., Blnk+Ccr5+ alveolar macrophages, Cxcr1+Fstl1+ alveolar macrophages, Ccr7+ migratory dendritic cells, Col14a1+ macrophages from kidney, Colq+ macrophages from intestine, Mcpt1+Mcpt2+ mucosal mast cells (fig. S32E)]. Also, Pcdh15+ lung fibroblasts, Cyp7b1+ lung lymphatic endothelial cells, Col2a1+ neuromuscular junction myonuclei, and Cdc14a+ myotendinous junction myonuclei and certain epithelial cells in the kidney (e.g., Dock10+ proximal tubule cells, Sntg1+ thick ascending limb of LOH cells, Slco1a5+ urothelial cells, Fgf14+ podocytes, Itga8+ juxtaglomerular cells, and Nlgn1+ distal convoluted tubule cells) significantly surged as well (Fig. 3M and figs. S29 and S30). The later expanding pattern, starting from 16 months, is characterized by a major expansion in immune populations, such as Pstpip2+ ABCs and patrolling monocytes (Fig. 3N and fig. S32E).

In summary, these sequential aging population dynamics delineate a trajectory where cellular depletion precedes expansion, with minimal temporal overlap, suggesting distinct mechanisms governing cell population dynamics at different life stages. From 3 to 12 months, declines are predominantly observed in cell lineages such as adipose, muscle, and epithelial lineages. In contrast, the later life stage, from 12 to 23 months, is marked by a substantial and sequential increase in various immune cells. This progression aligns with recent research documenting sequential alterations of molecular profiles throughout the aging process (60–63), thereby reflecting the nonlinear shifts of the internal cell populations at different life stages.

Our pan-organ dataset provides an opportunity for systematically exploring organ-specific aging changes in broadly distributed cell types, particularly immune cells. To investigate the aging-related alteration of T cells and innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), we first isolated 957,975 cells representing these cell populations across all organs for clustering and dimension reduction (Fig. 4A). A total of 18 cell clusters were recovered, each with highly cell type–specific gene markers (Fig. 4B). Although most cell clusters were broadly distributed across various organs, some immune cells exhibited organ-specific localization (Fig. 4C). For instance, cytotoxic natural killer cells expressing perforin and granzyme were predominantly found in the lung and white adipose tissues, whereas type 2 ILCs were enriched in barrier tissues, including lung, and multiple regions of the gastrointestinal tract. As a key immune responder of sex hormone regulation (64), type 2 ILCs are also enriched in white adipose tissue surrounding the gonads. Type 3 ILCs, the central regulator of mucosal immunity (65), were predominantly detected in the intestine.

When investigating aging-associated dynamics in T cell subsets, nearly all T cell clusters that diminished with age could be traced back to the CD4+ naïve T cells (Fig. 4D). This trend was consistent across different organs and aligned with a previous report (57) (Fig. 4E, left). In contrast, aging-associated expansion of T cells spanned various distinct cell states, including CD4+ T follicular helper cells (Dgki+Hs6st3+), CD4+ T helper cells (Ccr9+Cd6+), CD8+Gzmk+ cytotoxic T cells, CD8+Pde2a+Ly6c2+ T cells, and γδ T cells (Fig. 4D). Although the CD8+Pde2a+
Ly6c2+ T cells were not well characterized in prior studies, their age-related expansion aligns with observations of an age-associated surge in Ly6C-expressing immune cells in both bone marrow and spleen (66, 67). This increase may be attributed to a phenomenon wherein proliferating naïve CD8+ T cells, in the absence of specific antigen recognition, adopt a Ly6C-expressing state as part of a generalized immune response to age-related inflammation (68). Also, we observed a broad expansion of CD8+Gzmk+ cytotoxic T cells across various organs (Fig. 4E, right), in line with prior studies reporting age-related production of proinflammatory molecules (e.g., granzyme K) involved in tissue remodeling in aged mice (69) and higher abundance of granzyme K (GZMK)–expressing CD8+ T cells in aged human blood (70). While the expansion of this CD8+Gzmk+ T cell subset occurs at various locations (e.g., kidney, lung, and adipose tissue), its presence is minimal in the intestine, which is dominated by the CD8+Gzmb+ T cell subset, suggesting a distinctive immune environment in intestinal aging.

Analyzing 1,072,614 pan-organ B cells and plasma cells, we detected nine distinct cell clusters, each marked by different gene markers and organ-specific distributions (Fig. 4, F to H). IgA+ plasma cells (IgA, immunoglobulin A) were predominantly found in the digestive system (Fig. 4H), aligning with their pivotal role in producing IgA, a primary defense for the mucosal epithelium against pathogens and toxins (71). Similar organ-specific distribution was seen in subsets of memory B cells with high expression of Fcgbp and Ceacam1 and germinal center B cells expressing Lgr6 and Mid1 (Fig. 4H). IgGFc-binding protein (FCGBP) underpins mucosal immunity in the intestinal lining (72). In contrast, other B cells and IgM+ plasma cells were dispersed across multiple organs, such as lung, and adipose tissue (Fig. 4H).

In parallel with age-associated T cell expansion, various B cell subsets were substantially expanded during the aging process (Fig. 4I). Aligned with classic hallmarks of B cell aging, we identified ABCs with increased Zbtb32 and Apoe expression, which were enriched in lung and adipose tissues. Through the integration of B cells from multiple organs, we discovered two distinct ABC subtypes with different molecular features. The first subtype, characterized by increased Tbx21 expression, resembles a previously reported B cell subset associated with lupus-like autoimmunity in mice (57, 73). The second ABC subset displays elevated Pstpip2 expression, a factor linked to macrophage activation, neutrophil migration, and autoinflammatory diseases (74) (Fig. 4J and fig. S33, A and B). The age-related expansion of B cell subtypes (e.g., Pstpip2+ ABCs) occurs in a sex-specific manner, with a more pronounced increase observed in female individuals (fig. S33C).

Meanwhile, we detected an age-associated expansion of an IgM+ plasma cell type (Fig. 4J), marked by Xbp1, Dgkg, and Igbm expression. This subtype was widespread in aged mice tissues, including the liver and the adipose tissue (Fig. 4H). Given that IgM is the initial antibody produced in response to infections, this suggests a less specific immune response in aging, consistent with aging-associated low-grade inflammation (75). Additionally, the aged ileum is uniquely featured with a notable rise in Mki67+Mybl1+ germinal center B cells. The age-associated proliferation of distinct B and T cell subtypes in the intestine highlights its differential aging process compared with other solid nonlymphoid tissues, potentially owing to the distinct immune environments within the lamina propria of the gastrointestinal tract. We further delved into the molecular programs underlying these expanded B cell populations. Despite varying cellular characteristics and originating organs, they shared the same gene markers, including Sox5 and Cdk14 (Fig. 4K), which are both involved in the cell cycle and proliferation (76, 77), suggesting their crucial roles in driving the remodeling process that shapes immune aging.

As a further validation, we integrated our data with previous efforts in characterizing the aging immune system across four mouse organs (78), which allowed us to confirm the presence of aging-associated immune cell types across both datasets (fig. S34, A and E). Despite differences in the time windows studied and the techniques used, we observed consistent cell population dynamics (fig. S34), including the expansion of CD8+Gzmk+ cytotoxic T cells, γδ T cells, and Pstpip2+ aging-associated B cells, and the depletion of CD4+ naïve T cells in both lung and liver across different datasets.

To understand the impact of lymphocytes on cell population dynamics in aging, we used a “cell-knockdown” approach, targeting lymphocytes throughout the mammalian body using two specific immunodeficient genotypes: B6.129S7-Rag1tm1Mom/J and B6.Cg-Prkdcscid/SzJ. These models are recognized for lacking functional or mature lymphocytes (13, 79). To validate the lymphocyte deficiency in these models, we compared the main cell populations of the two mutants with age-matched wild-type controls (3 months old). As anticipated, the majority of the diminished cell populations were lymphocytes, including B cells, T cells, and plasma cells, across various organs (Fig. 5A).

In addition to lymphocytes, we observed a marked decrease in intestine-specific Mfge8+ follicular dendritic cells (FDCs) (80) across two immunodeficient mutant models. This decline was consistent across anatomical sites (i.e., duodenum and jejunum) and various age stages (Fig. 5B). FDCs, recognized for their crucial roles in B cell activation and antibody maturation, are the primary producers of chemokine Cxc13 in primary follicles and germinal centers of the intestine (81). This chemokine, in synergy with the B cell–specific receptor Cxcr5 (Fig. 5C), plays a vital role in B cell positioning within follicles and is essential in defining the secondary lymphoid tissue architecture, including lymph nodes and Peyer’s patches (82). The observed reduction of FDCs in immunodeficient models, which lack mature lymphocytes, suggests the cross-dependent relationship between FDCs and mature B cells, where lymphocyte-derived signals support FDC maintenance, and in turn, FDCs sustain B cell survival and maturation within the intestinal stroma.

Extending down to the subcluster level, we identified 289 subclusters exhibiting significant population changes across two immunodeficient models (fig. S35). As expected, the depleted subclusters are primarily associated with lymphocytes across various organs. Notably, however, several subclusters significantly increased upon lymphocyte knockdown (e.g., Rnf213+Ddx60+ intestinal epithelial cells in duodenum and jejunum), suggesting that lymphocytes might play a role in regulating the growth of these intestinal epithelial cells. Meanwhile, specific adipocyte subtype (e.g., Serpine1+Jun+ adipocytes in the lung) were depleted in both immunodeficient mutant models, hinting at potential adipose-immune cross-talk that warrants future exploration. The observed changes in nonlymphocyte subclusters are not due to relative ratio shifts in the wake of lymphocyte depletion, as the results are consistent when normalizing the subcluster cell number with total cell numbers of only nonlymphocyte cells (fig. S36).

We next clustered aging-associated cell subtypes on the basis of their dynamics in two immunodeficient models (Fig. 5D and fig. S37), focusing on subtypes that were detected and displayed consistent alterations in both mutants. Fifteen subtypes with age-associated depletion and 33 subtypes with age-associated expansion exhibited consistent patterns in both wild-type and mutant models, suggesting their population change is not directly caused by lymphocyte involvement (Fig. 5, E and F). Representative examples include the aging-associated depletion of Tspan18+ kidney principal cells (Fig. 5, I and J, and fig. S38A), expansion of Pcdh15+ lung fibroblasts (Fig. 5, K and L, and fig. S38B), and Nlgn1+ kidney connecting tubule (CNT) cells (Fig. 5, M and N, and fig. S38C). Molecular analysis of these cells offers insights into aging-linked organ dysfunction. For example, the aging-depleted renal principal cells correspond to a group of progenitor cells [e.g., Dach1, Tfap2b, Tspan18 (83)] (Fig. 5I) and show elevated expression of genes crucial for calcium homeostasis [e.g., Cacnb4, Atp2b2 (84)]. This indicates a susceptibility to calcium-induced cellular stress, potentially predisposing them to age-related damage and depletion. These findings highlight the complex interplay of cellular changes in aging and suggest mechanisms beyond direct lymphocyte interactions.

In contrast, other aging-associated subtypes presented distinct population dynamics between wild-type and mutants, indicative of immune-dependent regulation (Fig. 5D). A considerable proportion (128 out of 138) of these subtypes were lymphocytes, affected by their depletion in the mutants (Fig. 5, G and H). For instance, naïve T cells are depleted in young mutants, indicative of a developmental defect. Furthermore, the expansion of most mature lymphocytes is absent in aged mutants, as expected. In addition, we observed several nonlymphocyte cell populations that displayed altered dynamics in both mutants. A notable rescued cell type–specific expansion is a Slco1a5+ kidney urothelial subtype (i.e., urothelial cells–14) featured with an enriched expression of genes indicative of immune stimulation [e.g., Csf2rb (85); Fig. 5, O and P, and fig. S38D]. Likewise, the expansion of a specific subtype of Colq+ lung interstitial macrophage (i.e., interstitial macrophage–3) was halted in both mutants (Fig. 5, Q and R, and fig. S38E). This macrophage subtype is characterized by genes associated with lymphocyte interaction (e.g., Cxcl13, Cxcl10), illuminating the critical role of lymphocytes in driving its expansion during aging. These observations underscore the instrumental role of lymphocytes in regulating the dynamics of these cell populations. Consequently, targeted ablation of lymphocytes could be a viable strategy for the in-depth functional analysis of cellular interactions throughout the organism.

In this study, we present a comprehensive organismal single-cell atlas that highlights the intricate dynamics of cell population changes with aging. This was achieved through high-throughput single-cell transcriptome analysis of >20 million cells from 623 tissue samples spanning various life stages (3, 6, 12, 16, and 23 months), sexes, and genotypes. Our work adds to the collective efforts of the aging research community to characterize aging process and identify aging features at both the molecular and cellular levels (6, 7, 60, 86–89). Building on the foundational insights offered by the existing mouse aging atlas, PanSci offers several notable improvements, including a balanced representation of replicates across sexes and a broader age range that covers middle-aged groups as well as an order of magnitude more cells for capturing a broader spectrum of cellular diversity (fig. S39). Our research uniquely catalogs sex differences at both main cell type and subtype levels across various organs or tissues, highlighting how sex diversifies molecular states in ways that may influence the progression and treatment of age-related conditions. We identified a complex and dynamic landscape of aging at the cellular level, uncovering >10 main cell types and >200 subtypes undergoing significant age-associated depletion or expansion. Although some cell types, such as lymphocytes, have previously been documented to expand with age, our study has uncovered a range of rare cellular states that remain underexplored, such as the depletion of Tspan18+ principal cells and the expansion of Nlgn1+ connecting tubule cells within renal tissues. These aging-associated cell types were consistently observed across sexes and even genotypes, underscoring their potential as antiaging targets for further therapeutic exploration.

Moreover, our data suggest that aging at the cellular level unfolds through a series of dynamic changes rather than following a simple linear trajectory akin to the patterns observed with the DNA methylation clock (90). Early adulthood (3 to 12 months, approximately corresponding to human ages of 20 to 40 years) is primarily characterized by the depletion of specific cell types within the adipose, muscle, and epithelial lineages. In contrast, advanced adulthood (12 to 23 months, roughly equivalent to human ages of 40 to 70 years) is dominated by a pronounced expansion of various immune cell populations. This observation, alongside accumulating evidence of the nonlinear nature of aging (60–63), points to a complex array of intra- and intercellular regulations that orchestrate a series of coordinated cell population transitions throughout the aging process, varying distinctly between each examined age interval. Our findings, which identify certain essential cell types that begin to deplete early in life, align with previous reports (91) and underscore the benefits of initiating antiaging interventions at an early stage.

Furthermore, our study has uncovered organ-specific changes within broadly distributed cell types (e.g., immune cells) through a comprehensive analysis of cell population shifts across various organs. For example, we observed a consistent decrease in CD4+ naïve T cells and an increase in CD8+Gzmk+ cytotoxic T cells and age-related B cell subsets. These consistent patterns suggest a universal regulatory mechanism governing immune aging throughout the body. Specific organ systems displayed distinct aging dynamics, with early immune expansion occurring predominantly in the kidney and lung, whereas later expansions were observed in the liver and adipose tissues, potentially linked to the onset of organ-specific aging-associated diseases. The intestinal environment, in particular, displayed a specific cellular milieu, with an expansion of Mki67+Mybl1+ germinal center B cells during aging and sustained abundance of CD8+Gzmb+ T cells, highlighting a distinctive aspect of immune regulation in gut aging compared with other nonlymphoid tissues.

Using a “cell-knockdown” strategy analogous to “gene knockdown” in functional genomics, we targeted and suppressed the functional lymphocytes in vivo to investigate their role in the aging-related population dynamics of other cell types. This strategy was instrumental in delineating the complex interplay between lymphocytes and other cell types, evidenced by the halted increase of Slco1a5+ kidney urothelial cells and Colq+ lung interstitial macrophages upon lymphocyte reduction. However, efforts to restore most depleted cell populations through immune knockdown proved largely unsuccessful, reinforcing that the temporal hierarchy of cellular depletion preceding expansion is irreversible. This underscores the intricate and multilayered regulatory mechanisms governing cell population changes throughout aging.

Our current approach has several limitations. First, although the batch effects in the data are minimal, we observed individual variations when comparing the original dataset to the integrated result (fig. S40). These variations likely stem from intrinsic biological differences (e.g., sex) or individual-specific factors (e.g., environmental influences on the mice) (fig. S40). Second, while the cost of single-cell library preparation has decreased, the sequencing cost remains a limiting factor, resulting in a relatively shallow sequencing depth that may hinder the detection of lowly expressed genes. Third, a more refined temporal sampling (rather than the five age groups used in this study) could reveal subtle cell population transitions that are currently obscured by the broader sampling intervals. Additionally, the use of immunodeficient mutants, which lack functional lymphocytes from early development, may introduce developmental defects and compensatory mechanisms that could skew cell abundance comparisons with wild-type mice. Future studies should explore alternative cell-knockdown models, including drug- or antibody-mediated ablation within a confined time window. These approaches would allow more precise delineation of cellular regulatory networks throughout organismal aging.

In summary, our work has charted an extensive spectrum of >3000 different cellular states in the mammalian system, identifying >200 that exhibit significant aging-related changes in a tightly coordinated manner. We uncovered lymphocyte-dependent cellular population shifts associated with aging by harnessing scalable single-cell genomic techniques alongside mutant strain analysis. This “cell-omics” strategy—mirroring the progress made in high-throughput genomic sequencing—sets the stage for identifying key cellular targets and their regulatory network in various aging-related conditions, which holds the potential to spur therapeutic innovations aimed at restoring cellular functions and rejuvenating the systemic biological processes of organisms in aging and diseases.

C57BL/6 wild-type mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory and the National Institute on Aging colony at Charles River. The immunodeficient strains, B6.129S7-Rag1tm1Mom/J (homozygous for Rag1tm1Mom, JAX #002216) and B6.Cg-Prkdcscid/SzJ (homozygous for Prkdcscid, JAX #001913), were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. All strains were housed according to standard protocols under specific-pathogen-free conditions at the Comparative Bioscience Center (CBC) at the Rockefeller University, an Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AALAC)–accredited facility. All animal procedures were by institutional, state, and government regulations and approved under the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) protocol 21049.

We collected tissue samples from a sex-balanced cohort of mice from C57BL/6 wild-type at 3, 6, 12, 16, and 23 months old, and the immunodeficient strains at 3 and 16 months old. Each age group included two to four replicates per sex. Controlling for circadian rhythm and dissection artifacts, animals of the same age and sex were euthanized, and the organ or tissue collection for each batch was carried out by the same person on the same day, with ~1-hour intervals between each euthanasia, to ensure temporal consistency. This scheduling was designed to reduce the potential effect of circadian rhythm on transcriptomic data across different sexes and age groups. For each mouse, whole organs and tissues were then dissected in the following order: inguinal adipose tissue (with inguinal lymph nodes), stomach, small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum, with Peyer’s patches), colon, perigonadal adipose tissue, kidney, liver, heart, lung, hindlimb muscle, and brown adipose tissue. For the immunodeficient strains, the duodenum was exclusively profiled to represent the small intestine. Comprehensive metadata for each animal, including mouse individual ID, sex, age, birth and euthanasia dates, and body and organ and tissue weights, are detailed in table S1A.

We comprehensively optimized nuclei extraction conditions for each organ or tissue to maximize the recovered transcript signal and nuclei yield. For library preparation of each organ and tissue, dry tissue powder stored in liquid nitrogen was quickly lysed into fresh lysis solution and sorted for 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) singlet. The sequencing libraries were prepared according to a modified EasySci protocol (12) to scale for higher throughput. Library quality was verified with Agilent TapeStation, and sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 System with 25 S4 flow cells. Additionally, we introduced a control step to assess batch effects during library preparation and sequencing. Specifically, control kidney nuclei, extracted from pooled mouse kidney samples using the previously described methods, were spiked into each library at the reverse transcription stage.

Read alignment and gene or exon count matrix generation were performed using the EasySci pipeline (92). Cells from each NovaSeq run were split into an organ- or tissue-specific count matrix for data cleaning and doublet removal with Scrublet (93). Initial annotation of main cell types was crafted on the basis of published cell type markers (10, 21–33) and validated with intra- and interdataset cross-validation. Previously unidentified cell types were annotated with unique cell type markers, identified using the differentialGeneTest() function in Monocle2 (94). Subclusters from each main cell type were identified by leveraging the information from both gene count matrix and exon count matrix to increase the clustering resolution. Enriched genes for each subcluster were calculated with the aggregated gene expression per subcluster then prioritized the prominently expressed subclusters for each gene. The specificity of each enriched gene for each subcluster was further assessed to filter the nonvariable genes.

To access the sex-specific and aging-associated molecular and cellular changes, we performed differential expression (DE) and differential abundance (DA) analysis. Gene count matrix (gene × cell, each gene expression was normalized by the total read counts per cell) and cell count matrix (cell type × replicate, cell count for each cell type was normalized by total cell count of each organ or tissue from each replicate) were constructed as an input for Monocle2 differentialGeneTest(), which uses the negative binomial model and the likelihood ratio test given the fact that both count matrices are overdispersed. We considered the sex or age group as covariates when conducting corresponding analyses to control for confounding effects. We then proposed molecular or cellular features with statistical significance and biological relevance on the basis of the false discovery rate and fold change for filtering. For differentially expressed genes, we used each gene’s TPM count as an additional filtering criterion. Aging-associated cellular dynamics were identified with hierarchical clustering of aggregated cell proportion from all replicates at each time point.
