<p><strong>Background:</strong> Homer proteins bind multiple Ca<sup>2+</sup>-signaling proteins to shape the Ca<sup>2+</sup> signal by poorly understood mechanisms.</p>
<p><strong>Results:</strong> Homer2 regulates PMCA expression and activity in parotid acinar cells.</p>
<p><strong>Conclusion:</strong> Homer2 acts as a regulator of PMCA-mediated Ca<sup>2+</sup> clearance.</p>
<p><strong>Significance:</strong> Inhibition of Ca<sup>2+</sup> clearance by Homer2 further clarifies its role in Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling.</p>
<section><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Calcium ATPase, Calcium Transport, Cell Signaling, Protein-Protein Interaction, Scaffold Protein, Homer Proteins, Plasma Membrane Ca<sup>2+</sup>-ATPase, Parotid Gland, Proline-rich Motif</p></section></section><section><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Homer proteins are scaffold molecules with a domain structure consisting of an N-terminal Ena/VASP homology 1 protein-binding domain and a C-terminal leucine zipper/coiled-coil domain. The Ena/VASP homology 1 domain recognizes proline-rich motifs and binds multiple Ca<sup>2+</sup>-signaling proteins, including G protein-coupled receptors, inositol 1,4,5-triphosphate receptors, ryanodine receptors, and transient receptor potential channels. However, their role in Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling in nonexcitable cells is not well understood. In this study, we investigated the role of Homer2 on Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling in parotid gland acinar cells using <em>Homer2</em>-deficient (<em>Homer2</em><sup>−/−</sup>) mice. Homer2 is localized at the apical pole in acinar cells. Deletion of <em>Homer2</em> did not affect inositol 1,4,5-triphosphate receptor localization or channel activity and did not affect the expression and activity of sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca<sup>2+</sup>-ATPase pumps. In contrast, <em>Homer2</em> deletion markedly increased expression of plasma membrane Ca<sup>2+</sup>-ATPase (PMCA) pumps, in particular PMCA4, at the apical pole. Accordingly, <em>Homer2</em> deficiency increased Ca<sup>2+</sup> extrusion by acinar cells. These findings were supported by co-immunoprecipitation of Homer2 and PMCA in wild-type parotid cells and transfected human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK293) cells. We identified a Homer-binding PP<em>XX</em>F-like motif in the N terminus of PMCA that is required for interaction with Homer2. Mutation of the PP<em>XX</em>F-like motif did not affect the interaction of PMCA with Homer1 but inhibited its interaction with Homer2 and increased Ca<sup>2+</sup> clearance by PMCA. These findings reveal an important regulation of PMCA by Homer2 that has a central role on PMCA-mediated Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling in parotid acinar cells.</p></section><section><h2>Introduction</h2>
<p>Ca<sup>2+</sup> is a common second messenger with roles in fertilization, muscle contraction, neurotransmitter release, exocytosis, learning, and memory. In addition, it regulates critical functions of polarized secretory gland cells (<a>1</a>, <a>2</a>). Secretory cell Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling is evoked primarily by activation of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs),<a><sup>2</sup></a> which are coupled to G<sub>q</sub> or G<sub>i</sub>. These cells are an excellent model system to study Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling and the role of scaffolding proteins in the function of various components of the Ca<sup>2+</sup> signal, including plasma membrane Ca<sup>2+</sup>-ATPase (PMCA) and sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca<sup>2+</sup>-ATPase (SERCA) pumps, the inositol 1,4,5-triphosphate (IP<sub>3</sub>) receptors (IP<sub>3</sub>Rs), Ca<sup>2+</sup> release channels in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx channels in the plasma membrane (<a>3</a>).</p>
<p>Several GPCRs possess binding motifs that associate with members of the Homer family of scaffolding proteins. The Homer family, which includes Homer1, Homer2, and Homer3, as well as several splice variants, was discovered when <em>homer1a</em> was cloned as an immediate early gene product expressed in neurons upon prolonged stimulation (<a>4</a><a>–</a><a>7</a>). With the exception of Homer1a, all Homer proteins (<em>i.e.</em> Homer1b/c, 2, and 3; referred to as “long Homers”) are expressed constitutively throughout the central nervous system (CNS). These proteins are composed of an N-terminal Ena/VASP homology 1 protein-binding domain and a C terminus that folds into a coiled-coil multimerization domain and leucine zipper (<a>7</a>, <a>8</a>). The Ena/VASP homology 1 domain is a protein-protein binding module that recognizes the proline-rich motifs PP<em>XX</em>F, PP<em>X</em>F, and LPSSP (<a>9</a><a>–</a><a>12</a>) and binds to several GPCRs, canonical transient receptor potential channels, IP<sub>3</sub>Rs, ryanodine receptors, and the Shank family of scaffolding proteins (<a>10</a>, <a>12</a><a>–</a><a>16</a>). Homer proteins play a central role in Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling via regulation of neuronal transcription activity and thereby regulate dendritic spine morphogenesis, synapse remodeling, and synaptic clustering of CNS neurons (<a>9</a>, <a>17</a><a>–</a><a>19</a>). Previous research found that Homer1 regulates Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx by associating IP<sub>3</sub>Rs with transient receptor potential channels (<a>12</a>). Homer2 tunes GPCR stimulus intensity by regulating the regulator of G protein signaling proteins and phospholipase Cβ-promoting guanosine triphosphatase by Gα in pancreatic acinar cells. Moreover, Homer2 and Homer3 bind nuclear factor of activated T cells by competing with calcineurin in T lymphocytes (<a>14</a>, <a>20</a>). However, the role of Homer proteins in Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling in nonexcitable cells remains poorly characterized.</p>
<p>In this study, the role of Homer2 on Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling in parotid gland acinar cells was investigated using <em>Homer2</em> knock-out (<em>Homer2</em><sup>−/−</sup>) mice. We report that Homer2 interacts with PMCA (particularly PMCA isoform 4) in model systems and <em>in vivo</em> and that this interaction regulates PMCA activity. The findings suggest a mechanism by which Homer proteins can regulate PMCA expression and PMCA-mediated Ca<sup>2+</sup> efflux in parotid acinar cells. We suggest that by inhibiting transient receptor potential channel-mediated Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx (<a>12</a>) and by differentially modulating Ca<sup>2+</sup> extrusion by PMCA (present data), the Homers serve to protect the cells from Ca<sup>2+</sup> toxicity by facilitating cytosolic Ca<sup>2+</sup> clearance to limit the Ca<sup>2+</sup> signal duration.</p></section><section><h2>EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES</h2>
<section><h3></h3>
<section><h4></h4>
<section><h5>Antibodies and DNA Constructs</h5>
<p>Anti-Homer1a (M-13), anti-Homer1 (D-3), anti-Homer2, and anti-PMCA (5F10) antibodies were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc. (Santa Cruz, CA). Anti-IP<sub>3</sub>R3 antibodies were purchased from BD Transduction Laboratories (San Jose, CA). Anti-PMCA4 (JA9) antibodies were obtained from Affinity BioReagents (Golden, CO). Anti-FLAG M2 antibodies were obtained from Sigma. PMM2-hPMCA1b and PMM2-hPMCA4b were generous gifts from Dr. Emanuel E. Strehler (Mayo Clinic College of Medicine, Rochester, MN). pRK5-HA-Homer1a, pRK5-HA-Homer1c, and pRK5-HA-Homer2 were generously provided by Dr. Paul Worley (The Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine, Baltimore, MD).</p></section><section><h5>Animals and Preparation of Parotid Acinar Cells</h5>
<p>Wild-type (WT) and <em>Homer2</em><sup>−/−</sup> mice have been described previously (<a>14</a>). The life span of <em>Homer2</em><sup>−/−</sup> mice is similar to that of WT littermates. All animal protocols were performed according to institution guidelines. Mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation. The cells were prepared from the parotids of WT and <em>Homer2</em><sup>−/−</sup> mice by limited collagenase digestion as described previously (<a>21</a>). After isolation, the acinar cells were resuspended in an extracellular physiologic salt solution, the composition of which was as follows (in m<span>m</span>): 140 NaCl, 5 KCl, 1 MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 1 CaCl<sub>2</sub>, 10 HEPES, and 10 glucose, adjusted to pH 7.4 and 310 mos<span>m</span>.</p></section><section><h5>Cell Culture and DNA Transfection</h5>
<p>HEK293 cells (Korean Cell Line Bank, South Korea) were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (Invitrogen) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Invitrogen) and 100 units/ml penicillin and streptomycin in a 5% CO<sub>2</sub> incubator. Approximately 1–5 × 10<sup>5</sup> cells were seeded onto coverslips in 60-mm dishes and then incubated in antibiotic-free medium. The next day, DNA was mixed with Lipofectamine reagent (Invitrogen) and Opti-MEM, incubated for 20 min at room temperature, and then added to the cell culture media. The transfected cells were assayed at 38–48 h after post-transfection.</p></section><section><h5>Measurement of Intracellular Ca<sup>2+</sup> Concentration ([Ca<sup>2+</sup>]<sub>i</sub>)</h5>
<p>Parotid acinar cells from WT and Homer2<sup>−/−</sup> mice were loaded with 5 μ<span>m</span> Fura-2/AM (Teflabs Inc., Austin, TX) and 0.05% pluronic F-127 for 60 min in physiologic salt solution. Fura-2 fluorescence was measured at the appropriate excitation wavelengths (340/380 nm) and emission at 510 nm wavelengths (ratio = <em>F</em><sub>340</sub>/<em>F</em><sub>380</sub>) using a Molecular Devices (Downingtown, PA) imaging system. The emitted fluorescence was monitored with a charge-coupled device camera (Photometrics, Tucson, AZ) attached to an inverted microscope. Fluorescence images were obtained at 2-s intervals. All data were analyzed using MetaFluor software (Molecular Devices).</p></section><section><h5>Immunocytochemistry</h5>
<p>The immunostaining procedure was described previously (<a>14</a>). In brief, parotid acinar cells from WT and Homer2<sup>−/−</sup> mice were stained with antibodies against Homer2 (1:50); IP<sub>3</sub>R1, -2, or -3 (1:100); SERCA2, PMCA, or PMCA4 (1:200). Staining was detected using goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG conjugated to fluorescein or rhodamine. Images were collected with confocal LSM 510 and LSM 710 laser scanning microscopes (Zeiss, Göttingen, Germany).</p></section><section><h5>Western Blotting</h5>
<p>Protein extracts were prepared from parotid, submandibular gland, and pancreatic acini from WT and Homer2<sup>−/−</sup> mice. Acinar cells were lysed in a buffer containing 150 m<span>m</span> NaCl, 10 m<span>m</span> Tris (pH 7.8 with HCl), 1 m<span>m</span> EDTA, 1% Nonidet P-40, 0.1% SDS, and protease inhibitors (<em>i.e.</em> 2 m<span>m</span> Na<sub>3</sub>VO<sub>4</sub>, 10 m<span>m</span> NaF, 10 μg/ml aprotinin, 10 μg/ml leupeptin, and 10 μg/ml PMSF). The extracts were spun at 13,000 × <em>g</em> for 15 min, and protein concentration was measured using a Bio-Rad protein assay. The samples (50 μg of protein/well) were separated to 6–8% SDS-polyacrylamide gel. Proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, blocked with 5% skimmed milk, and incubated overnight with the specific primary antibody, an anti-SERCA2b (1:500), anti-PMCA4 (JA9, 1:500), and anti-PMCA (5F10, 1:500), at 4 °C. The blots were exposed to horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h and detected by chemiluminescence (Amersham Biosciences).</p></section><section><h5>Immunoprecipitation</h5>
<p>The immunoprecipitation procedure was modified from Shin <em>et al.</em> (<a>22</a>) and Kim <em>et al.</em> (<a>23</a>). In brief, parotid and HEK293 microsomes were prepared by homogenizing a minced parotid and harvested HEK293 in a buffer containing 20 m<span>m</span> Mops (pH 6.7 with KOH), 250 m<span>m</span> sucrose, 1 m<span>m</span> EDTA, 1 m<span>m</span> MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 10 m<span>m</span> benzamidine, and 0.2 m<span>m</span> PMSF. The homogenized samples were centrifuged at 400 × <em>g</em> for 10 min. The supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 900 × <em>g</em> for 10 min at 4 °C. To avoid protein degradation by digestive enzymes, immunoprecipitation was initiated immediately after completion of microsomal preparation. Microsomes were lysed in a buffer containing 50 m<span>m</span> Tris (pH 6.8 with HCl), 150 m<span>m</span> NaCl, 3 m<span>m</span> EDTA, 2 m<span>m</span> EGTA, and 0.5% Triton X-100 supplemented with protease inhibitors. The lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 14,000 × <em>g</em> for 15 min. About 150 μl of the extract (300 μg of protein/sample) was incubated with 10 μl of anti-PMCA (5F10) or 10 μl of anti-FLAG antibodies for 2 h by rocking at 4 °C. Protein A/G-agarose (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA) was added to each mixture, and rocking was continued overnight at 4 °C. Protein A/G-agarose was pelleted at 1,000 × <em>g</em> for 10 s, and the beads were quickly washed with cold PBS. The immunoprecipitated proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and probed with anti-FLAG (M2, 1:1,000) by overnight incubation at 4 °C.</p></section><section><h5>Measurement of [Ca<sup>2+</sup>] Efflux</h5>
<p>To measure directly the rate of Ca<sup>2+</sup> efflux by PMCA, we measured the appearance of Ca<sup>2+</sup> in the external medium using the procedure published by Zhao <em>et al.</em> (<a>24</a>) with slight modifications. Intact parotid acini from WT and Homer2<sup>−/−</sup> mice were washed once and then suspended in 1 ml of medium containing 140 m<span>m</span> NaCl, 5 m<span>m</span> KCl, 10 m<span>m</span> glucose, and 10 m<span>m</span> HEPES, pH 7.4, with NaOH. 100 μl of cell suspension were added to 1.5 ml of a similar medium containing 2 μ<span>m</span> free acid Fura-2 in a cuvette. After initiation of fluorescence recording, 10 μ<span>m</span> EGTA was added to reduce the extracellular Ca<sup>2+</sup> concentration ([Ca<sup>2+</sup>]<em><sub>o</sub></em>) to ∼100 n<span>m</span>. After establishing a baseline leak for ∼1 min, the cells were stimulated with 1 m<span>m</span> carbachol. At the end of the experiment, the signals were calibrated by adding 1 m<span>m</span> CaCl<sub>2</sub> and then 1 m<span>m</span> MnCl<sub>2</sub> to the medium as described previously (<a>24</a>). The design of experiments for PMCA stimulation while measuring cytoplasmic Ca<sup>2+</sup> was as follows. The cells were stimulated with 1 m<span>m</span> carbachol and 100 μ<span>m</span> CPA for about 15–20 s to release most of the Ca<sup>2+</sup> from internal stores and inhibit the SERCA Ca<sup>2+</sup> pumps. Efflux by PMCA was initiated by inhibition of the muscarinic receptors with the antagonist 10 μ<span>m</span> atropine and removal of external Ca<sup>2+</sup> to prevent Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx. CPA was maintained to inhibit the SERCA pumps.</p></section><section><h5>Measurement of Ca<sup>2+</sup> Uptake and Release from Internal Stores</h5>
<p>IP<sub>3</sub>-mediated Ca<sup>2+</sup> release from internal stores was measured in SLO-permeabilized cells as described previously (<a>24</a>). Cells were washed with a high K<sup>+</sup> (120 m<span>m</span> KCl, 20 m<span>m</span> NaCl, 10 m<span>m</span> glucose, and 10 m<span>m</span> HEPES, pH 7.4, with NaOH), Chelex-treated medium and added to Chelex-treated medium containing an ATP regeneration system (composed of 3 m<span>m</span> ATP, 5 m<span>m</span> MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 10 m<span>m</span> creatine phosphate, and 5 units/ml creatine kinase), a mixture of mitochondrial inhibitors, 2 μ<span>m</span> Fluo-3, and 3 mg/ml SLO (Difco). After the addition of cells, the concentration of free Ca<sup>2+</sup> in this medium was ∼350–400 n<span>m</span>. In this medium, the cells were permeabilized almost instantaneously so that Ca<sup>2+</sup> uptake into the ER could be measured immediately. Uptake of Ca<sup>2+</sup> into the ER was allowed to continue until [Ca<sup>2+</sup>] in the medium was stabilized. Then IP<sub>3</sub> was added in increasing concentrations to measure the extent of Ca<sup>2+</sup> release and the potency of IP<sub>3</sub> in mobilizing Ca<sup>2+</sup> from the ER. Subsequent addition of 1 m<span>m</span> carbachol was used to monitor the receptor-evoked Ca<sup>2+</sup> release.</p></section><section><h5>Data Analysis and Statistics</h5>

<p>In this study, we demonstrate a novel interaction between Homer2 and PMCA in native parotid acinar cells. Furthermore, we provide evidence in support of a critical role for Homer2 in modulating PMCA activity and thus Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling. Previous reports that focused on the molecular structure of Homer proteins in the CNS indicated that these proteins bind GPCRs, such as mGluR1/5, and IP<sub>3</sub>Rs, as well as act as scaffolding proteins for assembling Ca<sup>2+</sup>-signaling complexes in cellular microdomains (<a>8</a><a>–</a><a>10</a>, <a>13</a>, <a>32</a>). The apical region of exocrine cells is equivalent to the CNS synaptic region, where signaling complexes are clustered to form a “trigger zone” from which all forms of Ca<sup>2+</sup> signals, including Ca<sup>2+</sup> waves, are initiated (<a>33</a>, <a>34</a>). Consistently, immunocytochemical studies have demonstrated that all IP<sub>3</sub>R types are highly enriched in the apical region (<a>14</a>, <a>35</a>, <a>36</a>). Accordingly, we found that Homer2 and IP<sub>3</sub>Rs are expressed in the apical pole of parotid acinar cells. However, <em>Homer2</em> deletion had no effect on the expression or function of any of the IP<sub>3</sub>R isoforms, suggesting that Homer2 may have another role on Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling in parotid acinar cells.</p>
<p>Further analyses revealed that Homer2 affected PMCA expression in the apical membranes of parotid acinar cells. Hence, the most interesting finding from our study is the adaptive increase in PMCA protein expression in <em>Homer2</em>-deficient parotid and submandibular gland acinar cells. Like SERCA, the PMCAs have a crucial role in maintaining Ca<sup>2+</sup> homeostasis (<a>37</a>). Previous studies suggested that PMCAs are expressed in both the lateral and apical regions of pancreatic, submandibular gland, and parotid cells, as well as in the brain (<a>31</a>, <a>38</a><a>–</a><a>41</a>). In addition, PMCAs co-localize with mGluR1, IP<sub>3</sub>R1, and Homer proteins, including Homer1a, Homer1b/c, and Homer3, in neurons (<a>31</a>, <a>40</a>, <a>41</a>). Here, we discovered that Homer2 interacts with PMCA and decreases the rate of [Ca<sup>2+</sup>]<em><sub>i</sub></em> clearance in native parotid acinar cells, which is absent in <em>Homer2</em>-deficient mice. These results are similar to previous findings showing up-regulation of specific PMCA isoforms due to adaptations in Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling and Ca<sup>2+</sup>-dependent cellular functions in pancreatic and submandibular gland acinar cells from <em>serca2</em>-deficient mice (<a>24</a>). Therefore, it appears that PMCA expression and function is particularly sensitive to perturbations in Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling, and cells modify PMCA activity to adjusting the Ca<sup>2+</sup> signal.</p>
<p>Adaptations of PMCA expression may be regulated by Ca<sup>2+</sup> itself. Several studies have shown that Ca<sup>2+</sup> can alter the expression levels of Ca<sup>2+</sup>-signaling components, such as pumps and channels, thereby maintaining flexibility in Ca<sup>2+</sup>-signaling remodeling systems (<a>3</a>). In primary cultured cerebellar granule cells, PMCA2, PMCA3, and IP<sub>3</sub>R1 expression is up-regulated during the activation of Ca<sup>2+</sup>-dependent events. In contrast, type 2 Na<sup>+</sup>/Ca<sup>2+</sup> exchanger and PMCA4 are rapidly down-regulated (<a>1</a>, <a>42</a>, <a>43</a>). An interesting aspect of our findings is that <em>Homer2</em>-deficient parotid cells exhibit increased expression and activity of PMCA but not of SERCA2. However, <em>Homer2</em>-deficient pancreatic acinar cells increase SERCA2 but not PMCA expression, suggesting that PMCA adaptation occurs in a tissue-specific manner, perhaps reflecting specific cellular functions and localization.</p>
<p>PMCAs interact with partner molecules through their PDZ-binding motif in the C terminus (<a>28</a><a>–</a><a>30</a>). In this study, we characterized an additional novel binding region (PP<em>XX</em>F-like motif) of PMCA isoforms that participate in the interaction with, and inhibition of, PMCA by Homer2. Thus, mutation of the PP<em>XX</em>F-like motif reduced the interaction of PMCA1 and PMCA4 with Homer2 (<a>Fig. 4</a><em>C</em>) and prevented inhibition of ectopically expressed PMCA by Homer2 (<a>Fig. 5</a><em>E</em>). In contrast, the PMCA4 PDZ motif does not appear to be essential for the interaction with Homer2 because its deletion did not prevent the interaction of PMCA4 with Homer2 (<a>Fig. 4</a><em>C</em>).</p>
<p>Interestingly, the role of the PP<em>XX</em>F-like motif appears to be specific for the interaction of PMCAs with Homer2. Thus, mutation of the PP<em>XX</em>F-like motif does not affect the interaction of PMCAs with a short or long Homer1 (<a>Fig. 4</a><em>C</em>) or activation of PMCA activity by Homer1a in co-transfected HEK293 cells (<a>Fig. 5</a>, <em>D</em> and <em>E</em>). These results were unexpected in view of the reports that Homer1a (Ania-3) retains binding to the PDZ-binding motif in the C terminus of PMCA isoforms and activates [Ca<sup>2+</sup>]<em><sub>i</sub></em> clearance by PMCA when expressed with long Homer1 or the N terminus of Homer2 as an analog of Homer1a in neuronal cells (<a>31</a>, <a>44</a>). Using expression in HEK293 cells, we confirmed interaction of Homer1 with the PDZ motif of PMCA. We found that this interaction is eliminated by deletion of the PDZ motif on PMCA4, as was reported previously (<a>31</a>). However, unlike a previous report, the Ca<sup>2+</sup> clearance rate by native PMCA was increased by overexpression of short Homer1a but not by overexpression of long Homers (<a>Fig. 5</a><em>A</em>) (<a>44</a>). These experiments suggest that the level of long Homers in HEK293 cells (and perhaps other nonexcitable cells) is saturating with respect to PMCA, and thus additional expression of the long Homers caused no further inhibition. A prediction of this interpretation is the deletion of the long Homers, and their inhibition by the short Homer1a should accelerate PMCA activity and Ca<sup>2+</sup> clearance. This was indeed the case, in which deletion of Homer2 in mice or expression of Homer1a in HEK293 cells increased the native PMCA activity.</p>
<p>In summary, the key findings of the our study are as follows: 1) Homer2 binding to the PP<em>XX</em>F-like motif of PMCAs inhibited PMCA activity, and 2) expression and binding of Homer1 to the PDZ domain of PMCAs increased their activity. Thus, PMCAs undergo dual regulation by Homer proteins, <em>i.e.</em> inhibition by the Homer2 and stimulation by Homer1. In this manner, Homer proteins can regulate the duration of the Ca<sup>2+</sup> signal in parotid acinar cells to either extend or shorten the signal by inhibition or stimulation of PMCA activity, respectively.</p></section><section><h2>Acknowledgments</h2>
