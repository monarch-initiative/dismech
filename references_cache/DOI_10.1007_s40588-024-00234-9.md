# Myeloid Cell Reservoirs: Role in HIV-Host Interplay and Strategies for Myeloid Reservoir Elimination
**Authors:** Amber A. Castillo, Corbin McElrath, Grace Marshall, Mario Stevenson
**Journal:** Current Clinical Microbiology Reports (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s40588-024-00234-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40588-024-00234-9)

## Content

Abstract
Purpose of Review
Despite host antiviral responses and antiretroviral therapy, HIV-1 continues to persist in myeloid cell reservoirs. Hence, strategies that promote the elimination of myeloid reservoirs are critically needed. Insight into host-HIV interactions is key to achieving a cure.

Recent Findings
Host antiviral factors are often antagonized by HIV proteins that help establish infection while promoting chronic inflammation and disease in the host. Currently, several methods to eliminate the virus are under investigation including broadly neutralizing antibodies, latency reversal agents, CRISPR platforms, and immune modulation. Compounds that can penetrate the blood brain barrier are also being developed for reservoir cell clearance.

Summary
Here, we will outline features of myeloid cell biology and host-virus interactions that facilitate HIV persistence. We will also review current therapeutic strategies and potential drug candidates to cure HIV infection of myeloid cells even in difficult-to-treat tissues such as the central nervous system.

Introduction
Achieving a cure for human immunodeficiency virus 
(HIV)-1 must first clear the obstacle posed by long-lived 
cellular reservoirs of the virus. Clearing this obstacle will 
require a fuller understanding of the nature of the reservoirs 
in which HIV-1 is able to persist as well as practical strate-
gies that promote their elimination. Antiretroviral therapy 
(ART) is the recommended treatment for achieving viral 
suppression below detectable levels and preventing trans-
mission, yet it does not eliminate viral reservoirs. Most 
studies focus on CD4 + T cells, but myeloid cells are also 
susceptible to HIV infection and are reservoirs [1 , 2]. For 
instance, alveolar macrophages and monocytes contain rep-
lication competent HIV [3 , 4], and urethral macrophages 
and penile tissue of ART-suppressed individuals harbor HIV 
RNA, DNA, and virions [5 ]. Furthermore, HIV is shed in 
the semen of ART-suppressed humans, and these seminal 
virions appear to be macrophage-derived [6 ].
Urethral macrophages can be infected via contact with 
seminal fluid containing HIV-infected T cells. T cells can 
generate virological synapses with mucosal epithelial cells 
allowing for HIV to be transmitted to stromal macrophages 
via transcytosis [7 ]. Macrophages can be infected by cell-
free virus, and perhaps by virions in macrophage vesicles [8] 
and cell-to-cell fusion with infected CD4 + T cells. Infected 
macrophages contribute to virion dissemination and can 
develop into multinucleated giant cells [9 –11]. Ladinsky 
et al. used a BLT humanized mice model to show that bone 
marrow monocytes produce virions within 5–10 days post-
infection. These infected monocytes then spread and differ-
entiate into tissue resident macrophages to become viral res-
ervoirs [10]. ART-suppressed SIV-infected rhesus macaques 
also harbor replication competent virus in monocytes and 
tissue macrophages [12, 13].
We will discuss elements that enable viral persistence as 
well as host factors that prevent viral infection in myeloid 
cells. Furthermore, we will discuss developments of HIV 
infection in the CNS and methods for viral elimination.

Myeloid Cell Biology & Factors Contributing 
to Viral Persistence
Myeloid cells are a subset of leukocytes derived from 
common myeloid progenitor cells in the bone marrow that 
differentiate primarily into monocytes, macrophages and 
dendritic cells (DCs). Within the macrophage population, 
tissue resident forms exist in the CNS, mucosal tissue, and 
gastrointestinal tract. Like CD4 + T lymphocytes, cells of 
the myeloid lineage express CD4 and chemokine recep-
tors CCR5 and CXCR4 [14, 15]. However, myeloid cells 
are reported to have 20-fold less CD4 on the cell surface 
compared to CD4 + T cells [16]. HIV strains capable of 
infecting myeloid cells are termed macrophage (M)-tropic. 
To overcome low density CD4 on myeloid cells, M-tropic 
HIV has evolved a higher binding affinity between enve -
lope (Env) gp120 and the CD4 receptor compared to T 
cell-tropic HIV, which may be determined by Env-CD4 
binding site mutations. Furthermore, M-tropic HIV 
tend to be more sensitive to CD4-binding site antibod-
ies indicating dependence on Env-CD4 affinity. M-tropic 
HIV is characterized by its unique ability to infect and 
replicate in cells with low CD4 density and are approxi-
mately 30-fold more efficient at infecting macrophages 
than T-tropic virus. Similarly, M-tropic viruses may use 
CCR5 more efficiently than T-tropic HIV. However, the 
term “M-tropic” is often misunderstood as these viruses 
can infect both CD4 + T cells and macrophages whereas 
T-tropic viruses infect CD4 + T cells, but not macrophages 
[14, 15]. Recently, Borrajo et al.  described the difference 
in coreceptor usage in HIV infection that may determine 
the status of latency, or lack thereof, in infected cells. 
This group reports that monocyte-derived macrophages 
are more susceptible to apoptosis depending on the HIV 
strain and the coreceptor used for cell entry. CXCR4-tropic 
strains modulate transcriptional programming associated 
with apoptosis and downregulate genes related to cell sur -
vival. Thus, these cellular reservoirs are unable to exist if 
there is rapid cell death and suggests that reservoirs are 
likely composed of CCR5-tropic strains [17].
After binding to the CD4 receptor and coreceptor, the 
HIV membrane and myeloid cell membrane fuse together. 
This allows the HIV-1 capsid (CA), which encases the 
viral genome, to be released into the cell cytoplasm and 
trafficked to the nucleus. In macrophages, host protein 
cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factor 6 (CPSF6) 
is primarily expressed inside the nucleus where it regu-
lates nuclear import. The HIV CA can enter the host cell 
nucleus by crossing a nuclear pore. The CA must first bind 
to nucleoporin Nup153 in the nuclear pore complex. Then, 
CPSF6 binds to the CA lattice and removes Nup153 due 
to competitive binding. This ultimately enables CPSF6 
to pull the CA through the pore and into the nucleus. If 
CPSF6 is absent, the CA will remain trapped inside the 
pore and HIV infectivity decreases [18– 20]. After inte-
gration into the host genome and replication of the HIV 
genome, new viral particles must assemble and exit the 
cell. Macrophages maintain Virus-Containing Compart-
ments (VCC) that may provide access to the extracellular 
space [21, 22]. However, an HIV-infected BLT humanized 
mouse model illustrated the presence of enclosed com-
partments without access to the extracellular space [10]. 
In some cases, viral budding into enclosed compartments 
is more frequent than budding from the cell membrane. 
While this may not be the most efficient way to generate 
viral progeny, it may provide a space for viral storage and 
escape from host anti-HIV antibodies [8 , 10].
Castellano et al.  revealed that upon initial infection 
there is an abundant apoptosis event in which the major -
ity of HIV-infected CD4 + T cells and macrophages do not 
survive. However, macrophages that survive 3 weeks post-
infection can use glutamine as a major energy source via 
oxidative phosphorylation without compromising the gly -
colytic pathway. Inhibition of glutamine with benzylserine 
in HIV-infected surviving macrophages results in signifi-
cantly reduced numbers of infected cells illustrating their 
reliance on glutamine for survival. Infected macrophages 
are metabolically altered and characterized by mitochon-
drial fusion, lipid accumulation and reduced ATP produc-
tion. Uninfected and infected macrophages use fatty acids 
and glucose as primary energy sources for oxidative phos-
phorylation. However, latently infected macrophages can 
also use glutamine and glutamate and can switch between 
energy sources depending on availability (Fig. 1a) [23]. Real 
et al. argued that CD68 + S100A8 + MMP7 + macrophages, 
which characterize the “M4” macrophage subset, rely on 
glycolysis to produce replication competent virus via the 
S100A8 alarmin-mediated pathway. In male genital mucosa 
of ART-suppressed individuals, M4 macrophages are a tran-
scriptionally active HIV reservoir. This macrophage sub-
set can use glycolysis as well as fatty acid oxidation. M4 
macrophages express inflammatory alarmin S100A8, which 
initiates a metabolic shift towards glycolysis. S100A8 targets 
the toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signal transduction pathway 
to activate NF-κB. This ultimately induces HIV long termi-
nal repeat (LTR) promoters and activates HIV replication 
to produce replication competent viral particles. M4 mac -
rophages also harbor HIV in VCCs that can undergo latency 
reversal via S100A8 (Fig. 1b) [24].
Antibody Dependent Cellular Cytotoxicity (ADCC) 
mediated by Natural Killer (NK) cells have been shown to 
be effective in helping eliminate virally infected CD4 + T 
cells, but not myeloid cells. Clayton et al.  recently demon-
strated that NK cells have ineffective ADCC and cytolytic 
responses to HIV-infected macrophages with preferential

tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α production. This occurs 
despite sufficient HIV Env presentation on the cell surface 
and equal recognition by HIV-specific chimeric antigen 
receptor (CAR) T cells. Thus, poor cytolytic output from 
the NK cell population allows myeloid cell reservoirs to 
persist even while maintaining productive proinflammatory 
responses [25]. A CD4-mimetic compound, BNM-III-170, 
allows for exposure of vulnerable Env epitopes in conjunc-
tion with HIV + plasma. This causes HIV-infected mac-
rophages to become significantly more susceptible to ADCC 
[26]. Despite poor recognition and clearance of HIV-infected 
myeloid cell reservoirs by the immune system, myeloid cells 
themselves present a variety of intrinsic defenses to prevent 
infection.
Myeloid Cell Antiviral Responses Versus Viral 
Pathogenesis
To infect myeloid cells, HIV must overcome numerous 
barriers. Wang et al.  discovered host antiviral factor neu-
ropilin-1 (NRP-1) is packaged into myeloid-derived virions 
and expressed on the virion surface. NRP-1 has a heavily 
glycosylated extracellular domain that sterically restricts 
HIV-1 infectivity by blocking attachment to host cells [27]. 
Cell membrane protein otoferlin (OTOF), which is a type II 
ferlin, is present in myeloid cells, but not in CD4 + T cells. 
OTOF acts as a type I interferon (IFN)-induced effector to 
inhibit HIV fusion with the cell membrane. Although there 
is currently no detailed mechanism for OTOF, SNARE pro-
teins involved in membrane fusion may play a role. Simi-
larly, IFN-induced transmembrane protein 3 (IFITM3) also 
block HIV fusion at the cell membrane but is not associated 
with OTOF expression [28]. If an M-tropic virion fuses with 
the myeloid cell, then blocking CA entry is vital. Host factor 
Cyclophilin A (CypA) is expressed in the cytoplasm and 
nucleus of macrophages. CypA protein is incorporated into 
HIV-virions to potentially prevent premature uncoating of 
the CA, facilitate nuclear import of the CA, and improve 
reverse transcription of the viral genome [19, 29]. CypA 
interacts with the CA and shields it from host antiviral fac-
tors [19, 30]. Kim et al. demonstrated that the disruption of 
CA-CypA interactions enables endogenous restriction fac-
tor tripartite-containing motif 5α (TRIM5α) to restrict the 
CA before reverse transcription thereby decreasing HIV-1 
infectivity in myeloid cells. TRIM5α can restrict both 
cell-free viral infection and cell–cell spreading infection 
between primary macrophages [30]. TRIM34 (a TRIM5α 
paralog) restriction has also been reported in myeloid cells 
in a TRIM5α-dependent manner [31]. Alvarez et al. showed 
that pandemic causing HIV-1 from group M are less sus-
ceptible to innate immune responses in myeloid cells com-
pared to non-pandemic HIV-2 and HIV-1 group O. Non-pan-
demic HIV trigger cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) and 
TRIM5α, which activate interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) 
and cytokines like interleukin (IL)-1β. HIV-1(M) can evade 
host restriction and innate immune responses in myeloid 
cells due to its dynamic CA. HIV-1(M) CA has glutamine 
instead of tyrosine at position 50 and a deletion of arginine 
at position 120 enabling efficient infection and replication in 
myeloid cells [32]. Tripartite motif protein 69 (Trim69) has 
also been identified as a negative regulator of early HIV-1 
infection in IFN-stimulated myeloid cells. Trim69 associates 
with microtubules to promote stability, which is important 
for IFN-I responses. This limits viral spread and implicates 
the cytoskeleton in antiviral defenses [33].

which induce ISG expression [28, 34, 35]. When TLR4 is 
activated in macrophages, it engages with TIR domain-
containing adaptor inducing interferon-β (TRIF) to trigger 
sterile α-motif and histidine-aspartate domain-containing 
protein 1 (SAMHD1) via Myd88- and IFN-independent 
pathways. SAMHD1 is a host factor with antiviral activ -
ity [36]. SAMHD1 cleaves deoxynucleoside triphosphates 
(dNTPs) and depletes intracellular nucleotides that are 
essential for HIV-1 replication [11, 37]. During HIV infec-
tion type I IFNs are activated and induce ISG15, tumor 
suppressor p53, and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p21. 
ISG15 regulates transactivation functions of p53 via post-
translational modifications [35]. P21 is induced by p53 
and acts as a downstream effector that inhibits HIV repli-
cation by phosphorylating SAMHD1 and inactivating the 
dNTP biosynthesis pathway [35, 38]. HIV may overcome 
SAMHD1 restriction to efficiently infect DCs, macrophages, 
and osteoclasts via cell-to-cell fusion [11]. HIV accessory 
protein negative factor (Nef) has been shown to modulate 
p53 expression and promote viral replication [38]. Ubiquitin 
specific peptidase 18 (USP18) is an ISG15-specific isopepti-
dase that degrades misfolded p53, and downregulates IFN 
signaling and p21 expression in myeloid cells [35].
HIV viral protein R (Vpr) increases RUNX family 
transcription factor 1 (RUNX1 ) and IFN regulatory fac -
tor 1 (IRF1), which modulate immune genes like trigger -
ing receptor expression on myeloid cells 1  (TREM1) and 
ISG15. Vpr is responsible for decreasing IFN responses, but 
upregulates IL1B. Viral protein U (Vpu) and Nef upregu-
late IL-1, IL-7, and TNF-α. Vpu, Vpr and Nef also increase 
receptor interacting serine/threonine kinase 2 (RIPK2) and 
interleukin 1 receptor associated kinase 2 (IRAK2), which 
activate NF-κB [34 ]. Conversely, Langer et al.  reported 
that HIV accessory protein Vpu suppresses proinflamma-
tory immune responses by inhibiting NF-κB signaling at 
the transcriptional level. Inhibited cytokines include IFN-β 
and -γ, CXCL10, IL-6, TNF-α and -β, MIP-1β/CCL4, and 
RANTES/CCL5 [39]. Furthermore, HIV accessory protein 
Virion infectivity factor (Vif) is primarily responsible for 
the upregulation of cell cycle genes in macrophages. For 
example, Vif and Vpu induce cytokine genes that activate 
transcription factor FOXM1, while Vif and Vpr upregulate 
E2F4 [34]. Macrophages exhibit resistance to many cyto-
pathic effects that are associated with HIV infection. In a 
study by Campbell et al., it was observed that HIV promotes 
macrophage survival via upregulation of TREM1. HIV 
infected macrophages exhibited a 95% survival rate for over 
28 days. TREM1 increases expression of antiapoptotic genes 
BCL-2 and BCL-XL, and mitochondrial regulating genes 
mitofusin 1 ( MFN1) and MFN2, which inhibit BCL2L11. 
Cellular apoptosis occurs as BCL2L11 is translocated to the 
mitochondria where it disrupts the membrane potential and 
initiates the release of cytochrome c and caspase 9 [40]. 
However, TREM1 is also upregulated in liver Kupffer cells 
following HIV infection where it initiates antiviral responses 
via NF-κB pathways [41].
Upon differentiation of myeloid cells, there is selective 
induction of mRNA expression of serine incorporator 3 
(SERINC3) and SERINC5 [42]. SERINC5 is a host restric-
tion factor that inhibits fusion and promotes proinflamma-
tory cytokine production in differentiated cells from the 
myeloid lineage [42, 43]. HIV accessory protein Nef and 
Env glycoprotein counteract SERINC5. However, SER -
INC5 regulates HIV gene expression, and a Nef-dependent 
defect is incorporated into virions [42– 44]. Virions with 
SERINC5 induce ribosomal protein L35 (RPL35) and 
DR1-associated corepressor (DRAP1) expression in mac-
rophages. HIV-1 Trans-activator of transcription (Tat) is 
consequently blocked from binding mammalian capping 
enzyme 1 (MCE1) to the HIV transcriptional complex, 
which incorporate caps to viral transcripts. Transcripts 
lacking caps produce defective virions that are incapable of 
viral protein synthesis [44]. Apolipoprotein B mRNA edit-
ing enzyme polypeptide-like 3 (APOBEC3) family of pro-
teins has been shown to prevent HIV-1 infection in CD4 + T 
cells. Ikeda et al. demonstrated that APOBEC3 also restricts 
HIV infection and replication in myeloid cells. However, Vif 
counteracts APOBEC3 by degrading its associated enzymes 
[45]. HIV requires programmed-1 ribosomal frameshifting 
(-1PRF) to express its Gag-Pol protein. When IFN produc-
tion increases, it stimulates gene expression of C19orf66  
(Shiftless). Shiftless is a restriction factor that inhibits -1PRF 
and targets HIV mRNA causing its premature termination 
at the frameshifting site [46]. Finally, cellular transcription 
factor, Krugel-associated box domain-associated protein 1 
(KAP1), represses HIV-1 gene expression in myeloid cells 
by associating with Tat in the nucleoplasm and initiating 
Tat’s degradation via the proteasome pathway [47].
Lysosomal-associated transmembrane protein 5 
(LAPTM5) is highly expressed in macrophages compared 
to CD4 + T cells and inhibits HIV-1 by transporting the 
Env glycoproteins to lysosomes for degradation. Vpr coun-
teracts LAPTM5 activity by recruiting DDB1 and CUL4 
associated factor 1 (DCAF1), which causes the polyubiq-
uitination and degradation of LAPTM5 via proteasomes 
[48]. Interestingly, Vpu promotes macrophage infection 
by increasing phagocytosis of infected CD4 + T cells. 
Vpu downregulates the expression of cell surface protein 
CD47 on CD4 + T cells, which interacts with myeloid cell 
inhibitory receptor signal regulatory protein-α (SIRPα) to 
send a “don’t eat me” signal thereby preventing phago-
cytosis by myeloid cells. Cong et al. demonstrated that

this mechanism allows for myeloid cells to be successfully 
infected by transmitter/founder HIV viruses that may be 
incapable of cell-free infection of myeloid cells [49 ]. Vpu 
has also been shown to counteract the host restriction fac-
tor, tetherin, which prevents virion budding in myeloid 
cells [39].
Human mannose receptor 1 (hMRC1) is a macrophage-
specific host restriction factor that inhibits detachment of 
HIV virions from host cell membrane. hMRC1 is a cell 
surface glycoprotein receptor that is also involved in innate 
immunity [50, 51]. Host transcription activator, PU.1, 
upregulates hMRC1 expression in myeloid cells [51]. HIV-
infected macrophages exhibit downmodulated hMRC1 due 
to HIV protein Tat. Tat interferes with the transcription of 
hMRC1 by binding and depleting various transcriptional 
cofactors that are involved in the formation of active PU.1 
transcriptional complexes. This ultimately prevents PU.1 
protein from binding to the hMRC1 promoter [50, 51]. 
hMRC1 also targets mannose present on Env reducing its 
expression and decreasing HIV infectivity. Lubow et al. 
found that HIV Nef and Vpr, which interacts with DCAF1, 
cooperate to reduce mannose receptor expression on mac-
rophages (Table 1). Interestingly, YU-2, a M-tropic HIV-1 
strain isolated from brain tissue, lacks mannose on its Env 
enabling the virus to spread efficiently in myeloid cells in 
the absence of Vpr and despite the presence of mannose 
receptors [52].
Persistence of Myeloid Reservoirs 
in the Central Nervous System
The central nervous system (CNS) is an “immune privileged” 
site with a selective, semi-permeable blood brain barrier 
(BBB). The CNS contains two primary myeloid cell types 
that vary depending on the location: parenchymal and extra-
parenchymal. Cell types within these groups include paren-
chymal macrophages, perivascular macrophages, meningeal 
macrophages, choroid plexus, granulocytes, monocytes and 
DCs. Innate parenchymal macrophages and microglia are the 
primary target of HIV and have been identified as reservoirs 
capable of producing replication-competent viral progeny [53, 
54]. Dissemination of HIV to various biological sites depends 
on the circulatory system and lymphoid tissues, which spread 
the virus from the site of infection to distal tissues like the CNS 
[55]. It has been documented that HIV localization and repli-
cation occurs in the CNS shortly after infection [56–58]. Stud-
ies indicate that HIV-1 RNA in the cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) 
are equivalent to plasma RNA levels during acute infection 
indicating that brain tissue may harbor HIV during the early 
stages of infection [58, 59]. Despite ART, a significant portion 
of HIV-infected individuals experience HIV-associated neu-
rocognitive disorders (HAND) suggesting HIV persists in the 
CNS [60, 61]. Cochrane et al. demonstrated that CNS myeloid 
cells contain HIV provirus amongst virally suppressed patients 
[61]. A rhesus macaque model developed by Podgorski et al.

Microglial cells are infected by M-tropic HIV strains; 
however, IFN-α production limits infection in the CNS 
[63]. Microglia are induced to support HIV infection by Tat, 
which increases expression of TREM1 and prevents apopto-

associating with chicken ovalbumin upstream promoter tran-

is also associated with chromatin-modifying enzymes to 
silence HIV-1 genes in latently infected cells. Forouzanfar 
et al. show that Vpr depletes CTIP2 in microglia by associat-

degrade CTIP2 via proteasomes [65]. Nerve Growth Factor

repressor, also suppresses HIV transcription in microglia by 
recruiting histone modifying machinery. Nurr1 expression 
is associated with downregulating pro-inflammatory genes, 
maintaining microglial homeostasis, and promoting HIV 
latency [66]. Using a human brain organoid model, Kong 
et al. observed that HIV infection of microglia increased 
CCL2 and CXCL10 expression and ISGs including MX1, 
ISG15, ISG20, IFI27, and IFITM3. They also found that 
S100 family protein inflammatory genes increased, while 
TREM2 expression decreased lowering phagocytosis and 
antiviral responses. Adding ART to the organoid model 
reduced viral replication, but did not fully mediate the pro-
inflammatory microenvironment eliciting neuron dysfunc-
tion and death [67]. TREM2 and colony-stimulating factor 
1 receptor (CSF1R) are present in perivascular macrophages 
and parenchymal microglia, and are essential in promoting 
cell survival, proliferation, and immune functions [68]. Peri-
cytes, another myeloid cell in the BBB, also harbor replica-
tion competent HIV [69, 70]. HIV replication in pericytes 
is partially inhibited by the membrane protein occludin, 
which regulates 2’-5’-oligoadenylate synthetase ( OAS) 
expression. OAS family genes activate ISGs and endoribo-
nuclease RNaseL, which degrades viral RNA. Occludin also 
prevents HIV-1 budding by inducing the STAT1 pathway, 
which increases expression of IFNs, class-III histone dea-
cetylase SIRT-1, and NF-κB [70] (Table 1). There are many 
steps in the HIV lifecycle that can be targeted to block infec-
tion, therefore, current therapeutic strategies for eliminating 
myeloid cell reservoirs will be outlined.
Future Directions in HIV Cures
Many host and viral factors mentioned thus far have been 
identified as potential drug targets for eliminating myeloid 
reservoirs; however, therapeutics that can penetrate the 
BBB are of particular interest. Agents like baricitinib, a 
JAK inhibitor, have been shown to cross the BBB and safely 
lower neuroinflammation in mice brains while improving 
cognitive defects. In human macrophages, baricitinib also 
decreased markers of HIV persistence, reservoir size and 
infection [71]. In myeloid cells, cannabidiol (CBD) has been 
associated with decreasing extracellular vesicle release, 
which is responsible for the transfer of viruses, proteins, 
and inflammatory molecules into the CNS [72, 73]. Williams 
et al. indicated that a similar synthetic molecule, HU308, 
decreases production of IL-1β and TNF-α, and viral RNA 
in macrophages when used with combination ART (cART) 
[73]. Gong et al. created a poloxamer-PGLA nanoformu-
lation containing the commonly used antiretroviral drug 
elvitegravir, which is an integrase inhibitor. When adminis-
tered in an in vitro model, the nanoformulation elevated drug 
uptake, increased viral suppression in microglia, and proved 
successful at crossing the BBB [74]. Individuals on cART 
harbor integrated HIV, viral mRNA, and viral proteins in 
monocytes that transmigrate across the BBB towards CCL2. 
Specifically, HIV-1 CD14 + CD16 + ART-treated monocytes 
preferentially transmigrate across the BBB. However, cenic-
riviroc and anti-JAM-A and anti-ALCAM antibodies reduce 
transmigration of HIV-1 CD14 + CD16 + ART-treated 
monocytes [75].
Multiple small molecules have been identified as potential 
drug candidates. ONC201 has been shown to inhibit HIV-1 
infection in vivo. ONC201 is an activator of FOXO3a, which 
induces TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) in 
macrophages. ONC201 was also shown to cross the BBB 
and reduce HIV replication, p24, viral RNA, and integrated 
DNA in macrophages [76]. ZL0580 is a BRD4-selective 
small molecule modulator that induces epigenetic suppres-
sion of HIV in microglia and macrophages. ZL0580 inhib-
its HIV transcription elongation by binding Tat to cyclin-
dependent kinase 9 (CDK9) forming a repressive chromatin 
structure at the HIV LTR [ 77]. Another interesting small 
molecule is GS-6207, which targets CA protein–protein 
interactions. GS-6207 is effective against M-tropic HIV and 
strains that exhibit ART resistance. It is administered via 
subcutaneous injection and is long acting with low toxicity 
to many cell types including macrophages [78].
Boliar et al.  screened long noncoding RNAs (lncRNA) 
in HIV-infected macrophages and discovered upregulated 
FAS antisense RNA 1 (SAF/FAS-AS1), which is involved 
in apoptosis resistance. Downregulating SAF with siRNA 
activates effector casepase-3/7 in infected macrophages 
without affecting uninfected cells, thereby decreasing HIV 
infection [79]. LncRNA metastasis-associated lung ade-
nocarcinoma transcript 1 (MALAT1) upregulates micro -
RNA-155, downregulates microRNA-150-5p, and induces 
suppressor of cytokine signaling 1 (SOCS1) in mac -
rophages. During HIV infection, MALAT1 expression is 
increased and is dependent on NF-κB p50. This results in

an increase in TREM1, cold inducible RNA binding pro-
tein (CIRP), and host factor cyclin T1 (CycT1) expression. 
These proteins are involved in promoting macrophage sur -
vival and increasing HIV replication and reactivation from 
reservoir cells. Yuan et al . observed that peptide inhibitor 
SN50 and antisense oligonucleotides silence MALAT1 
expression in macrophages decreasing HIV infection [ 80]. 
Using a pooled shRNA-based genome-wide screen, Dong 
et al.  identified that Cox7a2 could be targeted to initiate 
apoptosis in infected macrophages without impacting 
bystander cells by disrupting the respiratory chain com-
plex II and IV genes and increasing ROS production [81].
A possible avenue to cure HIV and eliminate reservoirs 
is the shock-and-kill strategy, which utilizes latency rever -
sal agents (LRAs) to ignite viral synthesis and promote 
killing of HIV-infected cells. Hany et al.  examined the 
effects of bryostatin-1 (a protein kinase C agonist) and 
romidepsin (HDACi) in macrophages as LRAs. They 
found that bryostatin-1 decreased CD4 receptor expres -
sion thus lowering HIV infection and the production of 
HIV group-specific antigen (Gag) proteins, CAp24 and 
MAp17, which are vital for virion structure and matu-
ration. Romidepsin decreased CCR5 coreceptor expres-
sion and increased SAMHD1 ultimately decreasing HIV 
infection [82]. Rai et al.  examined repurposing the FDA-
approved drug edelfosine as an LRA. This anti-neoplastic 
drug reactivates latent HIV from myeloid cells in a dose 
and time dependent manner by activating NF-κB and AP-1 
pathways. Activated virus was shown to be non-infectious 
and increased stabilization of APOBEC3G (A3G) [83]. 
Human endogenous protein, galectin-9 (Gal-9), was also 
tested in ART-suppressed humanized BLT mice as an 
LRA. Gal-9 reactivated the virus in latently infected mye-
loid cells. However, it did not induce T cell activation and 
inflammation resulting in increased viral RNA and DNA 
in lung, liver, and spleen tissues. This indicates that Gal-9 
may potentially expand tissue reservoirs [84].
Alternatively, Correia da Costa et al. identified single 
guide RNA (sgRNA) LTR5 that bound to the LTR NF-κB 
binding site of the HIV genome and developed a block and 
lock method. Using a CRISPR/dCad9-KRAB platform, HIV 
latency reactivation was effectively prevented in myeloid 
cells exposed to IngB, SAHA, PMA, and Panobinostat [85]. 
Other groups have also investigated CRISPR as a curative 
strategy. Dash et al.  designed two CRISPR-Cas9’s to tar -
get CCR5 and cleave HIV-1 LTR-Gag, which are delivered 
via injection using adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors. 
In combination with ART, replication-competent HIV was 
eliminated in over half of infected CD34 + NSG-humanized 
mice [86]. CRISPR editing of mannosyl oligosaccharide glu-
cosidase (MOGS) also inhibits viral entry in T lymphocytes 
and myeloid cells. MOGS is important for precursor glycan 
trimming for HIV-1 Env glycosylation. CRISPR targeting of 
MOGS resulted in the production if non-infectious virions 
following activation with LRAs [87].

instance, Vif induces A3G degradation by forming a ubiq-

Elongin C (EloC) [88, 89]. Numerous compounds have been

Conclusion
Elimination of the myeloid cell reservoir is pivotal to pro-
duce a sterilizing cure for HIV-1 infection. It is important to 
define the structural, metabolic, and transcriptomic adapta-
tions of latently infected cells to develop novel therapeutics 
and vaccines. The biggest challenges faced are the low-level 
transcription and dissemination of HIV in ART-suppressed 
cells that may continue to release infectious virions. Myeloid 
cells are well equipped with antiviral responses and restric-
tion factors; however, HIV has also developed intricate 
methods to escape host immunity. HIV accessory proteins 
are practical therapeutic targets as they counteract many 
myeloid cell restriction factors to ensure viral persistence. 
By identifying the role of HIV-host interplay in myeloid 
cells, we can obtain a more complete understanding of HIV 
biology and pathological processes that are essential in HIV 
therapeutic discovery.
