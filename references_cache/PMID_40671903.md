# Human infections with the raccoon roundworm, Baylisascaris procyonis: A scoping review.
**Authors:** Weese JS, Stull JW
**Journal:** Can Vet J (2025)

## Content

BACKGROUND: The raccoon roundworm, Baylisascaris procyonis, is a gastrointestinal parasite with zoonotic implications.

OBJECTIVE: To identify and describe available evidence pertaining to clinical B. procyonis infections in humans.

PROCEDURE: A scoping review of the MEDLINE (via OVID), Web of Science, and CAB Abstracts databases.

RESULTS: Sixty infections were identified. Most (38, 75%) were in males and the median age was 2.9 y (range: 9 mo to 73 y). Forty-seven (78%) individuals had neurological disease (9 with concurrent ocular disease), 11 had ocular disease alone, 1 had eosinophilic cardiac pseudotumor, and 1 was an incidental autopsy finding. Clinical signs of neurological disease were highly variable and progressive, with vague initial signs and symptoms. Ocular disease was most often manifested as diffuse unilateral subacute neuroretinitis, retinal lesions, and painless loss of vision and visual acuity. Outcomes were reported for 42 individuals with neurological disease: 8 (19%) died, 4 (10%) fully recovered, and the remaining 30 (71%) had mild to significant neurological residual deficits. Most (7/10, 70%) individuals with solely ocular disease had permanent vision loss.

CONCLUSION AND CLINICAL RELEVANCE: The severity of the disease, poor response to treatment, and widespread presence of the parasite in areas where raccoons are endemic indicate a need for awareness among the public, healthcare providers, and veterinarians.

INTRODUCTION
The raccoon roundworm, Baylisascaris procyonis, is a well-disseminated gastrointestinal parasite with zoonotic implications. The main reservoir is the raccoon (Procyon lotor), in which patent gastrointestinal B. procyonis infection is common and the prevalence of fecal shedding of B. procyonis eggs tends to be high. Dogs may also harbor patent intestinal infections, but this appears to be rare (1). Although gastrointestinal infection of the reservoir host usually causes mild to no disease, ingestion of infectious eggs in some non-reservoir species (e.g., humans) can also result in larva migrans, in which larvae migrate throughout the body, with disease developing in response to damage from the migrating larvae and resulting immune response. The 2 main clinical concerns in humans are neural and ocular larva migrans, both of which can be severe.
Although B. procyonis infections in humans appear to be rare, the severity and widespread risk of exposure through environments that raccoons may have contaminated means that the importance of this disease should not be dismissed. Discussion of risk reduction and contextualization of risk of infection is hampered by limited information about zoonotic transmission. A better understanding of clinical disease in humans is critical for risk assessment, risk mitigation, and counselling about infection prevention and post-exposure prophylaxis.
The objective of this scoping review was to identify and describe available evidence pertaining to clinical infections of humans by the raccoon roundworm, B. procyonis.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
This scoping review was designed based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) reporting guidelines (2). The protocol is published at the University of Guelph’s (Guelph, Ontario) Atrium Institutional Repository (https://hdl.handle.net/10214/28517).
Peer-reviewed articles and abstracts were included, with no language or time limitations. All study designs, including case reports, were included. Reviews were included for manual searching of reference lists. Using a population, concept, and context approach, this study involved humans with clinical disease caused by infection with B. procyonis, in all settings.
The CAB Abstract, MEDLINE (via OVID) and Web of Science databases were searched. A broad search consisting of the terms “Baylisascaris” or “raccoon roundworm” was used for all databases. We manually searched reference lists of publications that were included in full-text screening, as well as identified reviews (e.g., narrative reviews), to identify additional relevant citations. Results were imported into EndNote 20 software (Clarivate, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, USA) and duplicates were removed. The final citation set was imported into Covidence (Covidence systematic review software; Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for Title and Abstract, then Full Text, screening. Both authors conducted Level 1 and Level 2 screening. Only citations for which both reviewers agreed the citation met inclusion criteria advanced to the next step (full-text screening or data extraction). A calibration step was completed after ~10% of the title/abstract search was completed by the 2 reviewers, with a plan to review the inclusion and exclusion criteria if < 90% agreement was achieved.
Because this was a scoping review, critical appraisal of the individual sources of evidence was not undertaken. Full details about the search strategy and selection process are available in the deposited protocol.
Data were extracted and described (median, range, count, proportions). Categorical comparisons were made using the Pearson X2 test or, if any cell value was ≤ 5, Fisher exact test. Normality of continuous data was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and nonparametric data were analyzed using Kruskal-Wallis tests.
RESULTS
After de-duplication, 697 papers were screened (Figure 1). Forty-four papers fit all screening criteria (Table 1). Some cases appeared to be reported in multiple publications. When the age, location, and other metadata suggested duplication, the 1st report was retained unless it was an abstract that was subsequently followed up with a peer-reviewed journal publication. In some situations, additional details about a previously reported case were provided in a subsequent publication. These data were merged to maximize the amount of case-level data but avoid duplication of cases.

A total of 60 confirmed or suspected infections were identified in the 44 papers. These included 8 case series and 36 single case reports. Case series reported a median of 2 infections (range: 2 to 7). Publications from between 1984 and 2023 were identified (Figure 2). Gender was reported for 51 (85%) cases; 38 (75%) in males and 13 (25%) in females. Ages ranged from 9 mo to 73 y (median: 2.9 y). Most (52, 87%) of cases were from the United States, followed by Canada (5, 8.3%), Germany (2, 3.3%), and Brazil (1, 1,7%).

Clinical presentations
Forty-seven (78%) individuals had neurological disease, either alone (n = 38) or in combination with ocular disease (n = 9). Ocular disease alone was reported in 11/60 (18%) individuals. One individual was a child with an eosinophilic cardiac pseudotumor; and infection was suspected to have been an incidental finding in 1 individual, based on detection of a single nematode larva in the brain of a person with Alzheimer’s disease who had died suddenly with no clinical suspicion of neurological disease beyond Alzheimer’s disease.
Five individuals were reported to have had preexisting developmental delay, and 1 had Down’s syndrome. Pica or geophagia were reported to have been observed before the onset of disease for 12 individuals, 8 (75%) of whom were under the age of 5. All individuals with a history of pica had neurological disease.

Serological testing
Serological testing results were reported for 36 individuals, 32 (89%) with neurological disease (with or without concurrent ocular disease) and 4 (11%) with solely ocular disease. All were positive. A variety of tests were reported, and specific details were often lacking. Tests included rBpRAG1 immunoblot (10, 28%) completed at the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC; n = 9) and Canadian National Reference Centre for Parasitology (n = 1), immunofluorescent assay completed at Purdue University (n = 8, 22%), unspecified testing completed at Purdue University (n = 3, 8.3%), EIA and immunoblot completed at Purdue University (n = 2, 5.6%), ELISA completed at Purdue University (n = 2, 5.6%), indirect IFA completed at CDC (n = 2, 5.6%), unspecified ELISA (n = 2, 5.6%), unspecified testing at CDC (n = 2, 5.6%), unspecified Western blot at CDC at a time when rBbRAG1 immunoblot was being done (n = 2, 5.6%), and unspecified ELISA at CDC (n = 1, 2.8%). No details were provided for 2 individuals. Concurrent Toxocara serology was carried out for 28 individuals and was negative for 25 (89%). Two of the 3 samples that were also Toxocara positive were tested at CDC using an unspecified test, but at a time when rBpRAG1 immunoblot testing (with a reported specificity of 98%) (12) was undertaken. The other sample was tested at Purdue University with an unreported test result.
Neurological disease
Age was reported for 41/47 (87%) individuals with a neurological component to their disease. One additional individual was reported as “child.” The median age was 1.5 y (range: 0.75 to 63 y). Twenty-three (56%) individuals were < 2 y of age, whereas 29 (71%) were < 5 y of age, and 36 (88%) were < 18 y of age. Gender was reported for 39; 33 (85%) were male and 6 (15%) were female. Among children < 18 y of age, 32/36 (89%) were male and 4/36 (11%) were female. Ofzthose under the age of 5 y, 24/28 (86%) were male (gender was not reported for 1).
When specifically described, clinical signs of neurological disease were highly variable and progressive. Vague initial signs and symptoms such as increased irritability, fatigue, lethargy, and fever were often noted initially. These were followed by progression of CNS disease with clinical signs such as ataxia, hypotonia, hyperreflexia, inability to stand, confusion, decreased awareness, seizures, cortical blindness, and in some situations, coma. Initial presenting signs and symptoms were described for 36 (77%) individuals. Those that were reported for at least 2 individuals are listed in Table 3.

The remaining individual had a normal CBC at presentation but developed a profound eosinophilia later, with the timeframe not reported. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cytology results were reported for 37 individuals. Eosinophilia in the CSF was identified in 36 (97%) individuals. Reporting of MRI results was variable; however, marked changes in white matter were reported in 24/24 cases that provided detailed descriptions, whereas more vague findings of “consistent with meningitis” and “large enhancing brain lesions” were reported for the others.

Ocular disease
Ocular disease alone was reported in 11 individuals, whereas ocular and neurological disease was reported in 9. Gender was reported for 18, and 12 (67%) were male.
The median age of individuals with ocular disease (sole ocular disease or ocular and neurological disease) was 8 y (range: 11 mo to 48 y). Peripheral blood eosinophilia was reported for 8/11 tested individuals. Seven of 8 (88%) had combined neurological and ocular disease. Only 1/4 (25%) of individuals with solely ocular disease had eosinophilia.
All 10 individuals tested for serum antibodies were seropositive, including 6 individuals with both neurological and ocular disease and 4 with only ocular disease. Eight of those, 4 with neurological and ocular disease and 4 with only ocular disease, were also tested for Toxocara serology, and all were negative. Antibodies against B. procyonis were detected in the CSF of 4/4 tested individuals, all of whom also had neurological disease. In 2 of those, CSF was also tested for anti-Toxocara antibodies and was negative.
Solely ocular disease
The median age of individuals with only ocular disease was 15 y (range: 7 to 48 y). Six (55%) were under the age of 18 y. Four (40%) were male, 6 (60%) were female, and gender was not reported for 1. Ophthalmological examination findings were variably reported. No details beyond progressive vision loss were provided for 1 infection. For the others, diffuse unilateral subacute neuroretinitis (DUSN) (10/10, 100%), retinal lesions (10/10, 100%), optic disk abnormalities (5/10, 50%), painless loss of vision and visual acuity (9/10, 90%), flashes and floaters (2/10, 20%), and eye pain and loss of visual acuity (1/10, 10%) were reported. Worms of a size consistent with B. procyonis were visualized in 9/11 (82%) individuals, whereas the diagnosis was made in 1 case based of the presence of DUSN and positive B. procyonis serology and was suggested in another based on the presence of DUSN and negative Toxocara serology.
Eight (73%) individuals were treated with laser photocoagulation. Two of those also received albendazole and corticosteroids, and 1 received only adjunctive corticosteroids. Of the 3 not treated with photocoagulation, 1 (9%) individual was treated with albendazole and vitrectomy, 1 (9%) with albendazole and corticosteroids, and 1 (9%) was lost to follow-up before any treatment was administered. Comparison of neurological and ocular cases
When individuals with solely ocular or solely neurological disease were compared, those with neurological disease were significantly younger (median age: 1.5 versus 15 y; P = 0.002), more likely to be male (26/32, 81% versus 4/10, 40%; P = 0.02), and more likely to have peripheral blood eosinophilia (33/33, 100% versus 1/4, 25%; P < 0.0001).
DISCUSSION
Baylisascaris procyonis larva migrans was predominantly associated with neurological and ocular manifestations, as expected. Whether this was because those are the main clinical manifestations or because they are the most recognizable or are most often the focuses for diagnosis was unclear. Only 1 non-neurological and non-ocular disease case was reported — a cardiac pseudotumor that caused sudden death.
Neurological disease was most commonly diagnosed. This is of concern because of the potential for fatal infections or profound long-term neurological sequelae; however, neurological infections results from accidental invasion of the CNS, as B. procyonis is not a neurotropic parasite. A small percentage of ingested larvae (e.g., 5 to 7%) reach the CNS (13). This probably necessitates a large inoculum and could explain the apparent rarity of disease despite a very high prevalence in raccoons (14–16) and abundant potential for inadvertent exposure to infectious eggs. However, once larvae reach the CNS, they can cause significant damage as, unlike Toxocara neural larva migrans, Baylisascaris continue to grow as they migrate, resulting in more inflammation and damage and a greater likelihood of severe neurological disease (17). This also likely accounts for the overrepresentation of young children, particularly those with a history of pica, as that would increase the risk of high-level exposure. Ocular disease was most common in adults without a history of pica, potentially reflecting a lower infectious burden to cause ocular disease, which is often associated with a single worm.
Baylisascaris procyonis is considered the primary cause of the large nematode variant (1500 to 2000 μm) of DUSN. It can also be caused by other migrating larvae but can be differentiated from those based on the sizes of the other main causes, Toxocara spp. (400 μm) and hookworms (e.g., Ancylostoma caninum, 500 to 700 μm). Visualization of larvae of the appropriate size was the main diagnostic criterion in ocular cases. Diagnosis of ocular disease when larvae are not visible can be more complicated, as peripheral blood eosinophilia appears to be less common than neurological disease. Serological testing could play an important role in cases with DUSN where larvae are not visible. The sample size here was small but, since all 4 tested individuals with solely ocular disease were seropositive, there may be a role of this noninvasive test for diagnosis of B. procyonis-associated DUSN (18). Whether that would have a clinical effect is unclear, given the typically poor response to treatment and presumed poor efficacy of anthelmintics alone when larvae cannot be visualized and treated directly. However, it is reasonable to suspect that earlier intervention could reduce the risk of long-term, severe visual sequelae. Improved understanding of and access to serological testing could be important for both ocular and neurological disease.
Although visualization of larvae can provide a quick and accurate diagnosis in individuals with ocular disease, diagnosis of neural larva migrans is more complicated because of a vast array of potential bacterial, viral, fungal, and noninfectious (e.g., neoplasia, inflammatory diseases, metabolic diseases) causes. The commonness of peripheral eosinophilia in individuals with neurological disease is important to note, as it may be an important early marker for B. procyonis (and other causes of larva migrans). Lack of blood eosinophilia does not completely rule out B. procyonis larva migrans, as 1 individual developed eosinophilia after initial presentation; however, lack of eosinophilia, particularly with advanced disease, would suggest that B. procyonis larva migrans is very unlikely. Similarly, CSF eosinophilia was almost ubiquitous. Serological testing and detection of antibodies in CSF, ideally paired with testing for other common causes of larva migrans (e.g., Toxoplasma), can play an important role in diagnosis, and so good understanding of and access to these tests is important.
Initial clinical signs of neural larva migrans were often vague and nonspecific, complicating early diagnosis. Baylisascaris procyonis testing was often not undertaken until late in the disease course, in response to continued neurological deterioration, lack of response to empirical treatment for more common causes, or negative test results for other causes. Diagnosis of neural larva migrans was commonly made, at least in part, through serological testing. Specificity of serology must be considered when interpreting these results. Anti-Toxocara antibodies were evaluated for a large subset of individuals, with 12% testing positive. As specific details about the serological tests were not typically available, test performance could not be evaluated. Yet the recombinant B. procyonis RAG1 protein-based Western blot assay performed by the U.S. CDC and Canadian National Reference Centre for Parasitology, which was used for some of these individuals, is reported to have a specificity of 98% (12). Of the 3 Toxocara seropositive cases, 1 was assumed to be a false positive Toxocara result from cross-reaction (19). Another was assumed to be a false positive Toxocara result because MRI findings were more typical for B. procyonis (20). One was suggested to have been coinfection with Toxocara and B. procyonis (21); however, a false positive Toxocara result may have been more likely.
Suspected or potential exposure sources were reported for a subset of individuals, but definitive links were difficult to make given the timeframe from exposure to diagnosis and the potential for widespread environmental exposure to raccoon feces in some environments. Pica is of particular concern because of the potential for ingestion of large egg burdens, something that likely increases disease risk. The presence of raccoon latrines on the property was linked to 4 infections. Raccoon latrines can be highly contaminated with raccoon feces and B. procyonis eggs, which can remain infectious in the environment for many months, and these data support the need to reduce raccoon latrines near human habitats and to use good hygiene measures during raccoon feces removal. Interestingly, pet raccoons were suspected exposure sources in 4 individuals. The risk of B. procyonis shedding in pet raccoons has not been reported and could be lower than wild raccoons because of lower ongoing risk of exposure; however, close, direct contact and potential contamination of the household environment could create ample risk. Although the risk of pet raccoons could not be determined by this review, the presumed rarity of pet raccoons and relatively high incidence of pet raccoons in this study population support concerns about contact with pet raccoons. Despite a variety of situations that would logically pose higher risks of exposure, rarely was a clear, specific incident identified. Lack of identification of specific high-risk situations (e.g., observing a child ingesting old raccoon feces) precludes targeted prophylaxis approaches which, though unproven, may reduce the risk of disease (22).
Treatment of neurological or ocular B. procyonis larva migrans has not been well investigated, and the present data could not be used to assess optimal treatment regimens. Typically, albendazole and corticosteroids were used for treatment of neurological disease, but full recovery was uncommon, likely reflecting the severity of disease by the time treatment was initiated. Similarly, although photocoagulation can effectively eliminate larvae, permanent vision deficits typically remained. Whether early diagnosis improves the outcome is not known, but often, diagnosis is made late in the disease course, after more common causes are excluded.
Although overall recovery was poor for both ocular and neurological disease, it is important to highlight the small percentage of near or full recoveries. One (9%) individual with ocular disease had near-full resolution. Four (9.3%) patients with neurological disease had full recovery, whereas 3 (7.0%) had good recovery with only mild neurological deficits. Based on these data, the prognosis for full recovery was poor and most patients (58%) with neurological involvement had severe neurological sequelae. Yet it is important to note that the prognosis is not hopeless. There were inadequate data to evaluate predictors for death or good recovery in this review.
A scoping review such does have limitations. It cannot be used to determine the incidence of disease or relative occurrence of ocular and neurological disease. Publication biases can be profound, and it is unclear what proportion of diagnosed cases are published. Once a disease is well described in the literature, there is less impetus to publish further cases. There can also be biases related to the types of disease reported over time, if there is a tendency to publish atypical cases (e.g., different clinical presentations, more severe disease). Findings from the cited studies are summarized, but there is no way to assess whether all the data or interpretations are correct. In particular for this review, potential sources of exposure were often suggested but not investigated, and retrospective identification of a source of exposure is challenging. Therefore, the data represent a synopsis or what was reported, with the inherent limitations of those reports. The small number of reported cases should not be taken as an indication that this is an unimportant issue. Rather, the likelihood of underdiagnosis and underreporting, along with the severity of disease and potential life-threating or life-altering consequences highlighted here indicate a need for more awareness and action directed at prevention and early diagnosis.
This review focused on disease caused by B. procyonis, but disease is not the only response to exposure. Subclinical infection can occur and may be common in individuals with abundant exposure to raccoon feces, as shown by seroprevalences of 7% in healthy wildlife rehabilitators and 8% in a convenience sample of healthy adults (23,24). Seroprevalence studies of different groups could help elucidate exposure rates and risks.
Baylisascaris procyonis is presumably a truly rare cause of disease in humans; however, the common severity of disease, poor response to treatment, and widespread presence of the parasite in areas where raccoons are endemic indicate a need for good awareness among healthcare providers (for early diagnosis) and the general public (for exposure-risk reduction). Veterinarians also have a role in educating the public, both about exposure from raccoons and about rare spillover into dogs. The data from this review provide important information, about the clinical and laboratory manifestations and outcomes of disease and about exposure sources, that can be used for targeted education campaigns to reduce the incidence and impact of B. procyonis infections in humans.
