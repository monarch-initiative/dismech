# Single-Cell RNA Sequencing of Ewing Sarcoma Tumors Demonstrates Transcriptional Heterogeneity and Clonal Evolution.
**Authors:** Goodspeed A, Bodlak A, Duffy AB, Nelson-Taylor S, Oike N, Porfilio T, Shirai R, Walker D, Treece A, Black J, Donaldson N, Cost C, Garrington T, Greffe B, Luna-Fineman S, Demedis J, Lake J, Danis E, Verneris MR, Adams DL, Hayashi M
**Journal:** Clin Cancer Res (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-24-2040](https://doi.org/10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-24-2040)

## Content

PURPOSE: Ewing sarcoma is the second most common bone cancer in children, accounting for 2% of pediatric cancer diagnoses. Patients who present with metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis have a dismal prognosis compared with the >70% 5-year survival of those with localized disease. Novel therapeutic approaches that can impact metastatic disease are desperately needed, as well as a deeper understanding of the heterogeneity of Ewing sarcoma tumors.

EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN: In this study, we utilized single-cell RNA sequencing to characterize the transcriptional landscape of primary Ewing sarcoma tumors and the surrounding tumor microenvironment in a cohort of seven untreated patients with Ewing sarcoma, as well as in circulating tumor cells (CTC). A potential CTC therapeutic target was evaluated through immunofluorescence of fixed CTCs from a separate cohort.

RESULTS: Primary tumor samples demonstrate a heterogeneous transcriptional landscape with several conserved gene expression programs, including those composed of genes related to proliferation and Ewing sarcoma gene targets, which were found to correlate with overall survival. Copy-number analysis identified subclonal evolution within patients prior to treatment. Analyses of the immune microenvironment reveal an immunosuppressive microenvironment with complex intercellular communication among the tumor and immune cells. Single-cell RNA sequencing and immunofluorescence of CTCs at the time of diagnosis identified TSPAN8 as a potential therapeutic target.

CONCLUSIONS: Ewing sarcoma tumors demonstrate significant transcriptional heterogeneity as well as a complex immunosuppressive microenvironment. This work evaluates several proposed targets that warrant further exploration as novel therapeutic strategies.

While the intensification of conventional multi-modal therapy has significantly improved the survival rate for localized Ewing sarcoma (EwS) from less than 10% in the surgery-only era to approximately 70%, many patients still die from metastatic disease(1). Even those with initial metastatic disease can achieve a radiographic remission; however, metastatic relapse after a remission frequently occurs demonstrating an initial good response to conventional therapy does not guarantee a cure.

While the EwS genomic landscape involves few mutations, the pathognomonic t(11;22) EWS-FLI1 translocation drives 90% of the disease (2–5). By studying EWS-FLI1 transcriptional regulation, over 1,000 genes have been identified to be directly or indirectly induced or repressed (6–9). While these results have spurred a significant effort focused on EWS-FLI1-induced genes that might be targeted to inhibit tumor progression, clinical translation has been difficult (10,11). EWS-FLI1 expression is inarguably essential to the survival of EwS cells but emerging experimental and clinical evidence suggests that EwS tumors have significant intra-tumoral EWS-FLI1 transcriptional heterogeneity (12,13). A major unmet need is to better understand tumor heterogeneity and how it potentially contributes to poor prognosis, with the goal of identifying therapeutic targets that are essential to metastasis and recurrence.

The understanding of the EwS tumor microenvironment (TME) is still evolving. Previous work in this cancer type has suggested the TME is infiltrated by immunosuppressive myeloid cells, functionally impaired antigen presenting cells, and T cells (14,15). EwS tumor cells have been shown to lack expression of HLA class I molecules, potentially leading to decreased T cell recognition (14,16).

Despite the advances in the investigation of EwS tumor biology, clinical support and translation has been poor. For instance, the knowledge of intra-tumoral transcriptional activity in untreated patient tumors is limited, and we know very little about how this may drive treatment resistance or clonal evolution. Here, we used single cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) to investigate the transcriptional heterogeneity of untreated EwS patient tumors, as well as circulating tumor cells (CTCs). We found that patient tumors contain heterogenous clusters of tumor cells with distinct genetic expression programs, which correlate with patient outcome. Evaluation of primary tumors, CTCs, and the TME revealed several potential therapeutic targets.

This study was approved by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board (COMIRB) and was conducted in accordance with the Ethical Principles and Guidelines for the Protection of Human Subjects of Research (Belmont report). Patients with newly diagnosed EwS patients at Children’s Hospital Colorado were included, and written informed consent and assent (as appropriate) was obtained from the patient or the guardian of the patient to the COMIRB-approved protocol, (COMIRB 00–206) where tumor and blood samples were collected. Age, sex, disease status were collected from all patients. Tumor analysis was performed under COMIRB-approved protocol, COMIRB 22–1456. Untreated primary tumor samples from biopsies were minced and immediately cryopreserved viably using DMSO:FBS (10:90) medium in a Corning CoolCell LX container chilled −1°C/minute until reaching − 80°C, followed by archival storage at −150°C, according to 10x Genomics protocol and published methods (17–19). Frozen tumor tissue was thawed at 37°C and gently dissociated using an optimized collagenase-IV/DNaseI/RPMI digestion protocol. Tumor-digest suspensions were strained through a 70μm filter membrane & washed. Red blood cells were depleted using the ErythroClear™ Red Blood Cell Depletion Kit (STEMCELL Technologies). The resultant single cell suspension was sorted for viable cells using DAPI staining and FACS (Beckman Coulter - MoFlo™ XDP). The enriched-tumor single cell suspensions were loaded into the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ platform (10x Genomics) with a target cell recovery for 4,000 cells maximum at the Genomics Shared Resource of the University of Colorado Cancer Center. Following GEM capture and barcoded cDNA library construction, libraries were sequenced at a targeted depth of 50,000 reads per cell using an Illumina NextSeq2000.

At time of diagnosis, peripheral whole blood was collected using EDTA (K2) BCTs and immediately passed through an 8μm-pore microfilter membrane (CellSieve) at a rate of 5mL/minute on a KD Scientific LEGATO 110 syringe pump. Captured cells were washed from the membrane using RPMI1640–10%FBS and immediately preserved with DMSO:FBS (10:90) medium in a Corning CoolCell LX container chilled −1°C/minute until reaching ™ 80°C, followed by archival storage at −150°C (17). Frozen cells were thawed at 37°C and treated using the ErythroClear™ Red Blood Cell Depletion Kit (STEMCELL Technologies). Cells were constructed into barcoded single-cell cDNA libraries and sequenced as described above.

Cell Ranger (v6.0.1) (20) was used to process fastq files to cell and gene count tables using unique molecule identifiers (UMIs) aligning to GRCh38 (compiled by 10X Genomics as 2020-A). Downstream quality control and analysis utilized the Seurat (v4.3.0) (21) pipeline. Cell Ranger-filtered data was further processed by removing genes identified in fewer than 10 cells. Cell barcodes were removed if they had greater than 50,000 UMIs, greater than 7,000 genes, greater than 20% of UMIs from mitochondria, or fewer than 200 genes. Cell doublets were identified and removed using the scDblFinder R package (v1.6.0) (22). This filtering criteria resulted in 13,367 cells across all samples totaling 22,639 genes.

The primary and CTC-enriched blood samples were further processed independently. The gene counts were normalized using Seurat by dividing each cell’s total counts and multiplying by 10,000 followed by natural-log transformation. The top 2,000 most variable genes were scaled with total UMIs and percentage mitochondria regressed out. Clusters were identified as cell types through multiple methods. The top 100 enriched genes by cluster were used as input for over-representation analysis (23) with gene sets from the MSigDb C8 collection (24), the PanglaoDb (25), and select EwS-related gene sets. Similar enrichment analysis was performed for further indicated comparisons. The SCEVAN R package (v1.0.1) was used to confirm the identification of tumor cells (26). Canonical cell type markers were plotted in UMAP space and using dot plots. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed on fold-change using the fgsea R package (v1.18.0) (bioRxiv 2021.02.01.060012). Myeloid and T cells in the primary samples were subset and analyzed separately.

Copy-number alterations were estimated in primary and CTC-enriched blood samples using the inferCNV R package (v1.11.2; RRID:SCR_021140). In each data subset, cells defined as Ewing were input as the tumor while all other cells were identified as normal. Following analysis on the dataset of primary samples, two samples were analyzed further as they appeared to have subclusters. Discrete subclusters were identified using random trees whose branches were manually trimmed to fewer clusters according to the hierarchy. Phylogenetic trees were produced with the uphyloplot2 python package (v2.3) with major chromosomal alterations labeled (27).

Intratumor gene expression programs (GEPs) were identified using consensus non-negative matrix factorization (cNMF) (28). The EwS cells of each primary tumor were processed separately while including only genes expressed in all samples (12000 genes). cNMF was run per sample with the 2000 most variant genes using raw counts first with k ranging from 2–10 and 50 iterations to select a k per sample with high stability and a low error rate. After k selection, each sample was run a final time with 200 iterations.

Sample-level GEPs were defined as the top 50 correlated genes per program. Ewing cells from all samples were scored using AddModuleScore (21) followed by z-score transformation of each program’s score and labeling the top program per cell as the program with the highest score. Merged GEPs were identified by correlating sample-level programs using the cell scores. Each merged GEP was defined by the aggregate of genes from the appropriate sample-level programs. Genes spanning multiple merged GEPs were removed. Merged GEPs were defined by over-representation analysis as described above (23). Cells were scored by merged GEPs as described above. UMAP plots were visualized after integrating the samples with the RPCA method (21).

VIPER (v1.26.0) (29) was run on the cell-level merged GEP labels to identify transcriptional associations from the high confidence transcription factors (A, B, and C) in the DoRothEA database (v1.4.2) (30). Top regulators were identified by those with the highest difference in mean enrichment between a merged GEP and the rest of the cells. Cells were also scored using top independent components identified in EwS xenografts (12). These scores were compared to those from merged GEPs using the Pearson correlation test.

Two publicly available EwS microarray datasets were analyzed using the merged GEPs. GSE17679 cohort B contains 44 samples and served as the discovery cohort while GSE63157 served as the validation cohort containing 85 samples (31,32). Both datasets were normalized with rma (33). The most variable probeset was chosen for each gene. The genes for each merged GEP were used to subset the GSE17679 dataset. Z-score transformation across each gene was performed and the samples were scored by the mean expression of the transformed values. Samples were divided into two groups using the median score for each merged GEP. Kaplan-Meier plots and log-rank tests were performed using the survminer (v0.4.9; RRID:SCR_021094) and survival R packages (v3.2–13; RRID:SCR_0211237). Heatmaps were generated with the ComplexHeatmap R package (v2.8.0; RRID:SCR_017270). The merged GEPs demonstrating a correlation with survival were tested in a similar manner in GSE63157.

Cell communication between ligands and receptors in the cell types of each primary sample were predicted using the CellChat R package (v1.6.1; RRID:SCR_021946). Select interesting interactions were highlighted based on the significant interactions across multiple samples. Ligand and receptor expression was plotted across cell type by patient sample.

Publicly available FLI1 ChIP-seq data in EwS cell lines (GSE176400) were analyzed for binding near the gene TSPAN8(34). Illumina adapters and low-quality reads were filtered out using BBDuk (v.38.87) (http://jgi.doe.gov/data-and-tools/bb-tools). Bowtie2 (v.2.3.4.3) (35) was used to align the sequencing reads to the hg38 reference human genome. Samtools (v.1.11) (36) was used to select the mapped reads (samtools view -b - q 30) and sort the bam files. PCR duplicates were removed using Picard MarkDuplicates tool (RRID:SCR_006525). The normalization ratio for each sample was calculated by dividing the number of uniquely mapped human reads of the sample with the lowest number of reads by the number of uniquely mapped human reads of each sample. These normalization ratios were used to randomly sub-sample reads to obtain the same number of reads for each sample using samtools view -s. Bedtools genomecov (RRID:SCR_006646) was used to create bedgraph files from the bam files (37). Normalized Bigwig files were created using deepTools bamCoverage (38) and visualized using the trackplot R package (v1.4.00) (39).

Whole-genome sequencing was performed on the 7 primary tumor samples. Fastq files were processed with the nf-core sarek pipeline (40). Mutations were evaluated using mutect2 and annotated using Ensembl VEP (41). Genes and mutations were filtered to those previously identified in multiple EwS samples (3,4) and those that pass mutect2 and VEP filters (42). EWSR1 translocations were identified using TIDDIT (43) followed by annotation with SnpEff (RRID:SCR_005191)(44). An oncoprint was generated using the ComplexHeatmap R package (45).

Archival fixed CTC slides for TSPAN8 re-staining were obtained from the National Pediatric Cancer Foundation study MCC20320. Previously stained and imaged CTC slides from this study was screened for slides with >20 CTCs identified. The identified six CTC slides then underwent the QUAR-R re-staining technique as previously described (46). Briefly, slides were soaked in PBS and filters were demounted. Filters were washed, incubated with borohydride solution, washed, and then incubated with Tris (46,47). After incubation, the filters were washed and stained with anti-TSPAN8 tagged with Alexa Fluor 488 at 2μg/mL (R&D Systems clone 458811). The stain was applied to the filters for 1 hour. Filters were washed and mounted on a fresh microscope slide with DAPI. CTCs were then re-read utilizing an Olympus BX54WI fluorescent microscope with Carl Zeiss AxioCam. Zen2011 Blue software was used to measure the TSPAN8 expression on each cell as previously described (46,47).

Breast cancer cell line MCF7 (RRID:CVCL_0031) and colon cancer cell line HT-29 (RRID:CVCL_0320) were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), and used as positive and negative control for TSPAN8 immunofluorescent staining. Cell lines were cultured between 4–10 passages from date of thaw, and tested for mycoplasma by the ATCC Mycoplasma PCR testing service prior to all experiments. Due to the short culture period following receipt from the ATCC, cell line re-authentication was not performed.

Due to specific patient privacy concerns, the University of Colorado Regulatory Compliance does not permit the public sharing of these raw fastq files. However, we are committed to collaborative data analysis, and more information and mechanisms for data access can be obtained by contacting the corresponding authors. Cell Ranger unfiltered- and filtered-barcode counts as well as filtered gene counts and cell labels are available on GEO as GSE277083. Code used for the bulk of the scRNA-seq analysis are available on GitHub: https://github.com/Aeg22/ews_scrnaseq.

To characterize the intratumoral landscape of EwS, we performed droplet-based scRNA-seq and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) on biopsies of primary tumor samples from seven patients with newly diagnosed and untreated EwS (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Six of the tumors had an EWS-FLI1 translocation with the other containing EWS-ERG (Table 1). Additionally, 3 tumors had mutations in previously associated EwS-altered genes (3,4), including 1 with the most frequently mutated gene, STAG2. The WGS also identified several common copy number alterations (CNAs), demonstrating clinical relevance of these samples (Supplementary Fig. 1B–C). After doublet removal and filtering of low-quality cells, the scRNA-seq dataset consisted of 6,228 cells with 13,791 median Unique Molecular Identifiers (UMIs) per cell and 3,058 median genes per cell (Supplementary Fig. 1D–E, Supplementary Table 1).

Single cells were visualized using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) followed by unbiased clustering. We classified nine major cell types (Fig. 1A–D, Supplementary Fig. 2A–B, Supplementary Table 2). In addition to EwS, we found the tumor microenvironment (TME) to consist of myeloid cells, endothelial cells, T cells, B cells, osteoclasts, and matrix and inflammatory cancer associated fibroblasts (CAFs). Many of these cell types were present in all tumor samples, although inflammatory CAFs were only observed in patient 001.

EwS cells were identified through several means. These cells expressed high levels of canonical EwS-associated genes such as such as MAPT, KDSR, NKX2–2, NR0B1, STEAP1, and PRKCB (Fig 1C–D, Supplementary Fig. 2B) (48–53). The classification of these cells as malignant was further supported by single cell variational aneuploidy analysis (SCEVAN) and by the enrichment of several EWS-FLI1 signature gene sets (Supplementary Fig. 2C–D). Among the EwS cells, a proliferating population was identified by the expression of MKI67 and PCNA (Fig. 1C–D).

A benefit of scRNA-seq is the ability to characterize several forms of tumor heterogeneity. Here, we utilized inferCNV to estimate genome-wide CNAs across the seven primary tumors (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Fig. 2E) (19). Previous EwS studies and our WGS have identified several common CNAs, including gains in chr 1q, chr 2, chr 5, chr 7, chr 8, chr 12, chr 20, and loss of chr 9p and chr 16q (4,54–59). We identified several of these alterations such as gains of chr1q, chr2, chr5, chr8, chr12, and chr20. These findings closely corroborated with the clinical karyotyping of these primary tumors when available (Table 1) and the findings from the WGS (Supplementary Fig. 1C).

Finally, we explored the expression of previously reported preclinical and clinical therapeutic targets for EwS in our data set (Fig. 1F). TME-, immunotherapeutic-, and cell signaling-related targets were plotted across cell type and within tumor cells by sample. Genes comprising the surfaceome of EwS as proposed immunotherapeutic targets were also evaluated (Supplementary Fig. 2F) (60). We found that compared to other cell types in the primary tumor, EwS cells across all samples express moderate to high levels of PAPPA but comparatively low levels of most HLA genes. We show that several proposed immunotherapeutic targets such as ADGRG2 and KDSR are uniquely and highly expressed in EwS while others such as CD99 and STEAP1 are also highly expressed in CAFs (Fig. 1F, Supplementary Fig. 2F) (60,61). However, mRNA expression of these targets is not always ubiquitous across all tumors, as demonstrated by IL1RAP and LINGO1. Several of the proposed cell-signaling targets are also expressed at varying intertumoral levels, including CDK4, PARP1, and DDX3X, demonstrating a potential explanation for heterogenous responses to treatments with single agents (62).

Next, we sought to define heterogeneity of transcriptional programs in EwS tumors. The tumor cells identified in Fig. 1 were isolated, integrated across patients, and then subjected to non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) analysis in a sample-specific manner to identify intratumor gene expression programs (GEPs) (Supplementary Fig. 3A) (28). This revealed a total of 25 GEPs (Supplementary Table 3), which were grouped into six main meta-programs through hierarchical clustering (Supplementary Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table 4). Most of the GEPs were represented in multiple tumors, suggesting that they represent diverse functions shared among patients. Based on the gene expression and gene set over-representation analysis within each merged GEP, we termed the 6 merged GEPs MAPK, EWS, Proliferation, UPR (unfolded protein response), Ribosome, and OxPhos (oxidative phosphorylation) (Fig. 2A–B, Supplementary Fig. 3C–E).

Potential regulators and associations with previous gene programs were further investigated. Transcriptional drivers of each program were explored with VIPER (29). Several drivers were as expected such as JUNB, FOXP1, E2F2, EPAS1, and ATF6 associating with merged GEP MAPK, OxPhos, Proliferation, Ribosome, and UPR, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3F). The top drivers of the merged EWS GEP were identified as ONCECUT1, MEIS1, and POU5F1. The lack of enrichment of FLI1 and ERG in the program is not surprising, considering a major role of EWS-FLI1-mediated transcriptional repression through the displacement of wild-type ETS transcription factors (6). Meanwhile, MEIS1 has been shown to cooperate with EWS-FLI1 to drive its transcriptional profile (63). We also compared the merged GEPs to previously identified programs in scRNA-seq from EwS xenografts (12). Most merged GEPs closely correlated with programs in xenografts, including between merged EWS GEP and the EwS program from Aynaud et al. (12), demonstrating the similarity in programs between EwS primary tumors and model systems (Supplementary Fig. 3G).

Next, we investigated whether genes enriched in each merged GEP correlate with overall survival in previously published datasets (31,32). The two microarray data sets used in this analysis consisted of 44 EwS tumors, which included 32 primary tumors, five recurrences, and seven metastases (GSE17679) (32), and 85 primary EwS tumors which were collected from patients enrolled on COG and EuroEwing trials (GSE63157) (31). GSE17679 was used as a discovery cohort where we found that patients with low merged GEP EWS scores or high proliferation scores had significantly poorer survival (Fig. 2C–F). The other four merged GEPs did not correlate with overall survival (Supplementary Fig. 4A–D).

We further evaluated merged GEP EWS and Proliferation in the validation cohort, GSE63157. Similar to GSE17679, patients with low merged GEP EWS scores had significantly poorer survival. While high merged GEP Proliferation scores correlated with poorer survival in GSE17679, it did not reach statistical significance in GSE63157 (Fig. 2G–H).

As mentioned previously, we identified that most patient tumors were found to have distinct large chromosomal gains and losses that largely agreed with clinical findings as well as the WGS. However, other than a report utilizing molecular inversion probes, there has been little published on CNA subclones within EwS (64). We leveraged scRNA-seq to analyze individual cells to identify and investigate EwS subclones, which were found in tumors from patients 005 and 061.

In patient 005, two subclones were found to diverge following an early chr8 gain (Fig. 3A–B). One subclone, termed Subcluster.1.1, emerged with gains in chr1 and chr5 and a loss of chr11p while a smaller cluster emerged with loss of chr1p and chr16q and gains in chr2 and chr 9, suggesting both chromosomal gains and losses contribute to tumor evolution. These two subclones demonstrate significant transcriptional heterogeneity (Fig. 3C). Similarly, three subclones were identified within the tumor from patient 061 (Fig. 3D). Early alterations were defined by loss of chr1 and gains of chr2, chr8, and chr20. Subclones were identified by the additional gain of chr5 and heterogenous loss and gain of chr12 (Fig. 3E). A slight gain of chr12 was observed in the 061 WGS (Supplementary 1C), consistent with the two subclones identified in the scRNA-seq. Additionally, the clinical karyotyping performed on this tumor also identified a subclone with gains of chr5, chr12, and chr13, consistent with the aggregate of subclusters 1.2.1 and 1.2.2 (Table 1). Similar to patient 005, the patient 061 subclones also demonstrate transcriptional heterogeneity (Fig. 3F).

In EwS, the TME has been suggested to be pro-inflammatory with high chemokine expression (65). We investigated the composition and characteristics of the EwS TME, in the relatively abundant T and myeloid cells (Fig. 4A–C). We were able to distinguish four T cell subtypes within the TME, consisting of tissue resident memory, CD8+ effector, regulatory, and CD4+IL7R+ T cells (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Table 5) (66,67). While the majority of the T cell subtypes did not express exhaustion markers such as CTLA4, TIGIT, LAG3, or PDCD1 (gene encoding PD1) (15), there are several factors to suggest an immunocompromised TME. The T regulatory cells expressed the inhibitory molecules CTLA4 and TIGIT, which can contribute to inactive antigen presenting cells (Fig. 4B) (68). Ligands of CD161 (encoded by KRB1), LLT1 (encoded by CLEC2D) and CD69 (also known as CLEC2C) are expressed in the majority of the T cell subtypes in the scRNA-seq data (Fig. 4B). Most studies in immune cells suggest LLT1-CD161 interactions lead to immunosuppressive environments, although the specific relationship in memory T cells remains to be seen (69). Additionally, CD69 has been shown to inhibit terminal differentiation of CD8+ T cells, which is consistent with observed low TOX expression (Fig. 4B) (70). MIF expression, along with expression of the receptors CD74 and CXCR4, were also identified in the EwS TME (Fig. 4B). While these interactions can lead to chemotaxis, MIF binding to CD74 in T cells has been shown to reduce activation and proliferation (71–74).

The myeloid population was characterized into non-inflammatory CD1C+ dendritic cells, IL1B+ inflammatory monocytes, FCN1+ inflammatory monocytes, and CD14+complement macrophages (Fig. 4D–F, Supplementary Table 6), some of which have been identified in the TME of EwS and other cancer types (15,75–79). Interestingly, the CD14+ complement macrophages express APOE, PLTP, CD163, which along with C1QC indicate a strong anti-inflammatory phenotype. which could oppose the other tumor infiltrating inflammatory myeloid cells (Fig. 4E). The myeloid cells described here express several markers which work in conjunction with T cells to contribute to an immunosuppressed environment. TIM3 (encoded by HAVCR2), which is moderately expressed in each subpopulation, has been shown to reduce the response to tumors (80). CD80, a ligand of the T cell stimulatory receptor, CD28, is lowly expressed in the myeloid cells of our study, as well as those from another EwS study, which suggests dysfunctional myeloid cells may reduce the T cell response (14). Finally, high MIF expression is similar to that in T cells which can reduce T cell activation and proliferation (74). Overall, characterization of the myeloid and T cells in the EwS TME identifies several mechanisms of immunosuppression.

Following the determination of specific cell types in TME, we next sought to identify potential cellular interactions using CellChat (81). We identified multiple ligand-receptor interactions involving EwS tumor cells (Supplementary Fig. 5A–B, Supplementary Table 7). Of the tumor-intrinsic interactions, the most commonly occurring across patient tumors were WNT5A-FZD1, NPY-NPY1R/NPY5R, and MDK-NCL. WNT5A, has been previously reported as highly expressed in metastatic tumors, and as a potential driver of ROR1 signaling in EwS cells (48,82–84). MDK (midkine) has been found to be part of the EwS secretome, however the mechanism of action, and whether NCL (Nucleolin) is a signaling receptor for midkine in EwS is unknown (85).

Additionally, there are several tumor-immune interactions possible in multiple tumor samples (Supplementary Fig. 5A–B). The most common across samples were interactions between MIF, expressed in EwS tumor cells, and CD44, CD74, and CXCR4 expressed in several different infiltrating immune cells. As noted above, MIF was commonly expressed in myeloid and T cells but has not been reported in EwS cells, despite being highly expressed in several cancer types (86–91). In mouse models of soft tissue sarcoma and melanoma, MIF has been reported to interact with CD74-expressing myeloid cells in the TME causing remodeling to a pro-tumor TME (92,93).

In terms of immune-intrinsic interactions, only 3 interactions were identified as probable in at least 3 patient tumor samples, all occurring within tissue resident memory T cells (Supplementary Fig. 5C–D). As noted earlier, the interactions involving the ligands MIF, LLT1, CD69 can promote a pro-tumor and immunosuppressed TME (69,70,92,93).

To further investigate mechanisms of metastatic progression, we next sought to identify and interrogate EwS CTCs using scRNA-seq. Eight patients had peripheral blood collected, including four patients where primary tumors were also analyzed. After collection, blood samples were enriched for CTCs using Cellsieve microfiltration membranes as previously described (Supplementary Fig. 6A) (94). Following similar filtering to the primary tumors, 7,139 cells were identified in the blood samples with 1,055 median UMIs per cell and 489 median genes per cell (Supplementary Table 1). Unbiased clustering revealed ten distinct cell types, which were identified by expression of canonical cell type markers (Fig. 5A–C, Supplementary Fig. 6B, Supplementary Table 8).

A cluster of cells were identified as EwS CTCs in the blood of patient 005 through several mechanisms. These cells expressed higher levels of canonical genes KDSR, NKX2–2, STEAP1, and NPW (Fig. 5B). The primary EwS and CTCs from patient 005 expressed similar genes more highly compared to other cell types (Fig. 5D) (34). Finally, the EwS CTCs were also enriched in EwS-associated gene sets when compared to other blood cells (Supplementary Fig. 6C).

Since we had identified subclones within the primary tumor from patient 005 (Fig. 3A), we next assessed whether the CTCs represented either of those subclones. We were able to identify what appeared to be gains in chr8, chr9, and chr12 suggesting that the CTCs potentially represent both subclones found in the primary tumor but the technical quality of the CTCs was not as high as in the primary, preventing a definitive conclusion (Supplementary Fig. 1E, Supplementary Fig. 6D).

When comparing patient 005 primary EwS cells to CTCs, we found the primary cells to be more enriched with the merged GEP EWS program, which would be consistent with a previous finding in cell lines that a reduction of EWS activity enhances metastatic potential (Supplementary Fig. 6E) (95). Interestingly, one of the most highly expressed genes in the CTCs compared to primary tumor cells was TSPAN8, a member of the tetraspanin superfamily, which has been found to be overexpressed in multiple cancers, and has been indicated to drive metastasis (Fig. 5E, Supplementary Fig. 6F) (96–100). Notably, TSPAN8 was also highly and specifically expressed in six of the seven primary EwS tumors (Supplementary Fig. 2F, Supplementary Fig. 6G). We examined publicly available ChIP-seq data from the ESCLA database (34) and found a FLI1 binding peak proximal to TSPAN8 in all 14 cell lines (Fig. 5F, Supplementary Fig. 7), suggesting that TSPAN8 may be regulated by EWS-FLI1. Finally, to test whether TSPAN8 expression is found in an independent cohort of CTCs from other EwS patients, we performed immunofluorescent staining of CTC slides from the multi-center study, MCC20320. Here, we found positive surface protein expression of TSPAN8 on all six patient samples, in addition to an archival slide from patient 005 in our scRNA-seq cohort, further corroborating the scRNA-seq results (Fig. 5G).

In this work, we performed scRNA-seq on untreated EwS patient tumor and CTC samples to generate a dataset detailing the heterogenic copy-number and transcriptional profiles of tumor cells, as well as the composition of the TME. Previous cell line and xenograft studies have identified transcriptional programs driven by EWS-FLI1 or cell proliferation, but these phenomena have not been confirmed in EwS patient samples (12,13,95). In our scRNA-seq dataset, we were able to identify six unique GEPs. Notably, GEP-EWS and GEP-Proliferation were found to correlate with overall survival in an existing microarray dataset. While the GEP-EWS was significant in an additional validation cohort, GEP-proliferation only trended towards significance. Together, these findings underscore the clinical impact of transcriptional heterogeneity and could inform prognosis and treatment strategies.

In this study, we were also able to characterize the broad and heterogenous expression of potential therapeutic targets in EwS. Priming of T cells against PAX3, STEAP1, and PAPPA have shown preclinical effect against EwS cells (101–104). STEAP1 and PAPPA, but not PAX3, were highly expressed in tumor cells, demonstrating these could be a potential therapeutic target.

Much of what is known about the EwS TME is that it is often highly immunosuppressive and this study corroborates many of those findings. We found tumor cells to express only low levels of HLA genes, consistent with previous work showing a lack of HLA expression by EwS cells, potentially leading to decreased T cell recognition (16). We also found evidence that the myeloid cells of the EwS TME may limit T cell activation through expression of TIM3 and the low expression of CD80 (14,80). The T cell subtypes themselves also seem to support immunosuppression through potential LLT1-CD161, CD69-CD161, and MIF-CD74/CXCR4 interactions which all have previous evidence of supporting pro-tumor environments (69,70,74). We identified inflammatory CAFs, which can secrete CXCL12 to recruit regulatory T cells (105). Taken in aggregate, our scRNA-seq analysis of the TME largely agrees with previous studies that the EwS TME is immunosuppressed which represents an area of need for future clinical development.

Detecting and analyzing circulating EwS cells has been challenging because of low expression of epithelial markers used in typical carcinoma CTC detection (106–113). We have previously reported that CellSieve can detect CTCs in many patients with high-grade sarcoma, which also includes EwS patients (94,114). In this study, we identified a population of circulating cells that expressed many of the EwS markers from the primary cells as well as consistent gene set enrichment. While not all canonical markers and copy number alterations were identified, this was attributed to poorer sequencing depth in these samples, and we labeled this population as EwS CTCs. We were able to molecularly characterize the transcriptome of a population of EwS CTCs for the first time. We identified that these CTCs in our study have less enrichment of GEP-EWS compared to matched primary cells, supporting the finding in cell lines and xenografts that low EWS-FLI1 activity leads to more metastasis (95). Finally, we found that TSPAN8, a trans membranous protein that has been investigated as a potential cell surface target in other cancer types (115,116), was highly enriched in the CTCs. Consistent with previous reports that investigated the surfaceome of EwS (60), we also found TSPAN8 expression to not be restricted to the CTCs but also highly and broadly expressed in primary EwS tumors. Future studies should explore TSPAN8 as an antibody- or CAR T cell-based therapeutic target, as well as investigate the role ofTSPAN8 expression in tumor metastasis.
