# Timothy Syndrome and CACNA1C-Related Disorder: First International Language and Management Guidelines Consensus Statement
**Authors:** Jack F. G. Underwood, Katherine W. Timothy, Holly Tyroll, Rebecca J. Levy, Ivy E. Dick, Geoffrey S. Pitt, Elizabeth M. Tunbridge, Anwar Baban, Gemma Wilkinson, Nicola A. L. Hall, Georgia Sarquella Brugada, Rosemary Bauer, Dominic Abrams, Jeremy Hall, Timothy Syndrome Foundation, Timothy Syndrome Alliance (TSA)

## Content

Abstract

                  Timothy Syndrome is a multisystemic genetic disorder, classically characterised by prolonged QT interval and subsequent cardiac arrhythmias, neurodevelopmental disorders including developmental delay and autism, and syndactyly or hip dysplasia. It is caused by variants in the
                  CACNA1C
                  gene, which encodes the widely expressed Ca
                  v
                  1.2 voltage-gated calcium channel. Since it’s characterisation in 2004, the spread of variants in
                  CACNA1C
                  associated with Timothy Syndrome has expanded. With advances in sequencing and the inclusion of
                  CACNA1C
                  in genomic screening, further variants have been identified presenting with incomplete features of Timothy Syndrome or further aligned phenotypes which are inconsistent with the original description. In the absence of a formal nomenclature, these presentations have been reported in a proliferation of ill-defined terms, e.g. Atypical Timothy Syndrome. At the same time, advances in knowledge and therapeutics have improved morbidity and life expectancy for these individuals when appropriately identified and managed. Here, we present guidelines for the diagnosis of individuals presenting with variants in
                  CACNA1C
                  , developed by an international panel of experts through Delphi consensus with the involvement of the
                  CACNA1C
                  community. We formalise the language around syndromic presentations linked to
                  CACNA1C
                  variants, reassert and demarcate the classical Timothy Syndrome phenotype, and define a new syndrome, CACNA1C-Related Disorder. Finally, we present minimum expected standards of clinical care for individuals with CACNA1C-Related Disorder or Timothy Syndrome, with implications for long-term management and improved outcomes for affected individuals.

INTRODUCTION
Timothy syndrome (TS) (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man
(OMIM) entry #601005) is a multisystemic disorder incorporating
physical, neurodevelopmental and psychiatric features. It was ﬁrst
described in 1992, through cases of a novel prolonged QT
arrhythmia syndrome associated with syndactyly ( ﬁnger and toe
webbing) [ 1, 2]. It was formally characterised in 2004 through the
work of the Keating and Splawski labs [ 3] and named after
Katherine Wilson Timothy (KWT), Clinical Coordinator and key
force behind the search and triangulation of the genetic variant
underlying TS.
The causative variant for TS was isolated to a missense p.G406R
change in the CACNA1C gene at 12p13.33, encoding exon 8A of
the Ca
V1.2 L-type voltage-gated calcium channel [ 3]. Its ultra-rare
presentation was coupled with signi ﬁcant early-life mortality,
which has been reduced with the uptake of screening for
prolonged QT in infants born with syndactyly. Population
prevalence remains unknown, with current estimates of <1/
1,000,000, classifying presentations as ultra-rare [ 4]. KWT ’s 2024
Natural History Study of TS included 87 cases [ 5], whilst the
Timothy Syndrome Alliance ’s (TSA ’s) global CACNA1C Community
Registry includes 104 individuals as of October 2025. Efforts by
KWT, the Timothy Syndrome Foundation (TSF) and the Timothy
Syndrome Alliance (TSA) to raise awareness continue, with the
inclusion of CACNA1C in a range of gene-screening panels and
clinical implementation of comprehensive non-targeted next-
generation (exome or genome) sequencing leading to a rapid
increase in individuals joining the patient community.

Since the ﬁrst description of Timothy syndrome, the spectrum
of phenotypes has expanded, as further variants and affected
organ systems have been identi ﬁed. An early advance was the
recognition that the thirteen children in whom TS was ﬁrst
identiﬁed all had an identical p.G406R variant in exon 8A [ 3]. Exons
8 and 8A can be alternatively spliced in a mutually exclusive
manner and in 2005, a further individual with TS features, most
notably an arrhythmia syndrome associated with marked QT
prolongation but no syndactyly, was identi ﬁed [ 5, 6]. This
individual, with a p.G406R variant present in exon 8 and
congenital hip dysplasia, was the ﬁrst of a small number that
led to the de ﬁnition of TS Type II (TS2), differentiated from TS Type
I (TS1) in exon 8A. An additional case in the same study, of an
individual with a p.G402S variant in exon 8 and with a similar
phenotype, was also included in TS2, extending the molecular
diagnostic criteria to other variants within CACNA1C for the ﬁrst
time [ 6]. It should be noted that the exon 8/8A nomenclature is
inconsistent in the literature, with many early papers referring
imprecisely to Timothy syndrome across exons 8 and 8A. Here, we
utilise the nomenclature originally described in Splawski et al, with
TS1 comprising a phenotype including syndactyly and TS2
featuring hip dysplasia [ 6].
The initial descriptions of TS invariably included prolongation of
the QT interval, a feature which was soon classi ﬁed as Long QT
syndrome type 8 (LQT8). Subsequent work focused on individuals
with isolated prolonged QT intervals, without any extracardiac TS
features [7] such as cognitive impairment or facial dysmorphology
[7, 8]. Some reported cases of novel CACNA1C rare variants have
included symptoms that mirror or physiologically oppose TS or
LQT8, such as shortened QT duration or Brugada ECG patterns.
These studies, however, are predominantly single cases, lack
familial genetic testing pedigrees and often the variants presented
occur at relatively common population frequencies. For example,
in 2018, the Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen) re-examined the
gene-disease relationship for CACNA1C and Brugada Syndrome
and classi ﬁed the evidence supporting a causal role for CACNA1C
in Brugada Syndrome as limited [ 9].
Beyond cardiac presentations, rare variants in the CACNA1C
gene have also been associated with syndromic presentations in
sporadic case series and reports (Fig. 1)[ 10–14]. These symptoms
overlap with the original phenotype ascribed to TS, including
some, but not all, features. Neurodevelopmental disorders are
frequently observed: developmental delay, intellectual disability,
epilepsy, autism, attention-de ﬁcit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)
and hypotonia [ 10, 11, 13, 14]. Efforts establishing this phenotype
over the past ﬁve years have led it to be added to multiple
reference sources, including OMIM (#620029), Orphanet, Wikipe-
dia and Gene Reviews [ 15]. The largest and most rigorous case
series and literature reviews now strongly suggest that rare
variants in the CACNA1C gene cause a severe multisystemic
disorder, but no single review examines all potentially implicated
phenotypic features (Table 1).
As the literature on rare variants in CACNA1C has expanded, the
language and nomenclature used to describe those presentations
have become chaotic and confusing. Beyond TS and LQT8, a range
of other terms can be found across published literature and
public-facing media, including Atypical Timothy Syndrome,
Timothy Syndrome Type 3, Timothy Syndrome Variant, Timothy
Syndrome-like CACNA1C Disorder, Cardiac-only Timothy Syn-
drome, CACNA1C-Related Disorders and CACNA1C-Associated
Neurological Disorders. These terms have no clear, established
or de ﬁned clinical meaning. The number of identi ﬁed individuals
with rare CACNA1C variants is increasing, support groups and
communities are growing and therapeutic treatments are in
development [ 16]. There is therefore a need for clarity to guide
families, researchers and clinicians. In this work, we set out to
achieve cohesion through convening an international network of
experts and engaging with the leading TS and CACNA1C charities.
This resulting co-produced consensus document outlines a
standard language de ﬁnition for individuals presenting with
variants suspected to be pathogenic in CACNA1C, along with the
guidelines for their diagnosis and management.
METHODS
Development of the CACNA1C consensus guideline was modelled
on the Delphi Process [ 17], incorporating multiple rounds of
expert discussion, followed by engagement and co-production
with the TS and CACNA1C community. The CACNA1C language
consensus group consisted of twelve participants from ten
institutions across the USA, UK and Europe, comprising clinicians,
clinical scientists and researchers. Discussions occurred via video
conference calls, e-mail communications and draft ﬁle outcomes
exchanges.

Key issues for discussion were initially identi ﬁed following the
Connect CACNA1C Global Network Conference held by the Timothy
Syndrome Alliance (TSA) in June 2023. The language consensus
group was convened and after three rounds of discussions from
January to August 2024 (Fig. 2), language options were presented
to the TS and CACNA1C community for input, along with
identiﬁcation of any further relevant issues. Individuals with
CACNA1C variants and their caregivers were contacted through
social media, support groups and the Timothy Syndrome Alliance
(TSA) community mailing lists, reaching >200 families. The
community voted on language options, which were then
discussed along with further outcomes to generate consensus
recommendations at a further two working group meeting
rounds.
Clinical diagnostic criteria
Clinical features of Timothy syndrome . As noted, TS Type 1 was
deﬁned through cardinal features of prolonged QT interval,
cardiac arrhythmia and syndactyly in individuals with a p.G406R
gene variant in exon 8A of CACNA1C [3]. Further features included
autism, developmental delay, seizures, baldness at birth, char-
acteristic facies ( ﬂattened nasal bridge, low-set ears, small upper
jaw, thin upper lip), small teeth, hypoglycaemia, hypothermia and
hypotonia [3, 18]. Inclusion of individuals with p.G406R variants in
exon 8 associated with a similar phenotype but featuring hip
dysplasia and lacking syndactyly led to the de ﬁnition of TS Type 2
[6]. These remain the classical signs of TS Type 1 (hereafter TS1)
and Type 2 (TS2) and our consensus reaf ﬁrmed the importance of
these diagnostic entities. The p.G406R gene variant has been
recurrently identi ﬁed among unrelated affected individuals, with
the only known unaffected individual ascertained to be mosaic
after the p.G406R variant was inherited and identi ﬁed in their
affected child and we therefore expect individuals with p.G406R
variants to present with symptoms of TS1 or TS2, dependent on
whether the p.G406R variant is present in exon 8 or 8A [ 12, 15].
In Splawski et al ’s work de ﬁning TS2 they included p.G402S
variants [ 6] and further case reports and series have presented
individuals with the classical phenotypic features of TS but novel
de novo gene variants, notably p.E407G [ 19], p.E407A [ 20],
p.C1021R [ 21, 22], p.I1166T [ 7, 20, 23] and p.A1473G [ 24]. On this
syndromic basis, TS1 and TS2 diagnoses should be assigned based
on phenotypic syndromic features and not be dependent upon
the speci ﬁc p.G406R variant. Any individual with a rare single-
nucleotide variant within the CACNA1C gene presenting with the
classic dyad of prolonged QT interval and a neurodevelopmental
disorder (e.g. developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism)
should be diagnosed as TS following appropriate clinical genetic
medical evaluation. In Fig. 3 below, we outline a ﬂow chart for
diagnostic interpretation of CACNA1C pathogenic rare variants
based upon assessment and examination of all potential
syndromic features. In order to aid clarity, we recommend
providing the Timothy syndrome diagnosis along with the exact
protein and genomic change, for example, Timothy syndrome,
p.G406R (HG38: g.2504944 G > A). The rationale for the inclusion of
genomic coordinates is discussed as part of the Molecular
Diagnostic Criteria below.
These individuals should be further assessed across all
potentially affected organ systems, with ongoing follow-up on
an annual basis to monitor for further symptoms driven by
developmental changes. Of note , individuals with pathogenic
variants in CACNA1C may present solely with bradycardia whilst in
utero and in the ﬁrst year of life with functional 2:1 atrioven-
tricular (AV) block (whereby alternate supraventricular impulses
cannot conduct as the ventricle remains refractory secondary to
QT prolongation) or hypotonia, prior to the development of other
features. Neonates or infants may show prolonged QT intervals
on electrocardiography, with or without functional 2:1 atrioven-
tricular block. All individuals identi ﬁed in utero or in the ﬁrst year
of life displaying these features should be treated as suspected TS
and followed up closely with proactive intervention and
management.

Within the CACNA1C-Related Disorders (CRDs) umbrella, syn-

multiple organ system involvement ﬁtting published literature but
inconsistent with TS can be diagnosed with CACNA1C-Related

CACNA1C variants to syndromic outcomes remains unclear.

Cardiac presentations of CACNA1C-Related Disorders . Individuals
may initially present with isolated cardiac features, encompassing
a range of phenotypes featuring isolated prolonged QT interval,
arrhythmias, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, congenital heart dis-
ease, sinus node disease and congenital structural cardiac
abnormalities (Fig. 3). The symptomatic presentation of Long QT
syndrome type 8 in individuals with rare CACNA1C variants has
been robustly demonstrated [ 5, 12, 29–31]. LQT8 has been
observed with pathogenic variants distinct from TS, for example,
p.P857R in Boczek et al ’s study of 15 members of a multi-
generational family [ 7] and p.R858H in Gardner et al ’s study of 26
individuals from a family across ﬁve generations [ 8]. Systematic
screening of individuals in long QT databases yielded further case
series and novel variants amongst cohorts internationally [ 23, 32].
The Long QT syndrome type 8 term originates during a period
when Long QT syndromes were being sequentially numbered
based upon chronological identi ﬁcation. As the understanding of
physiology and underlying genomics has improved, other
numbered Long QT syndromes are being superseded with
gene-based nomenclature. We would suggest that in future
consideration be given for the use of Long QT syndrome 8 within
the structured format given for CACNA1C-Related Disorders,
where diagnosis may be given as CACNA1C-Related Disorder,
Long QT Syndrome (LQTS). For consistency, LQT8 will continue to
be used through this work.
Many existing case reports on individuals with LQT8 are limited
to only cardiovascular symptom pro ﬁling and do not include
long-term follow-up evaluation, c omplicating the assessment of a
cardiac-speci ﬁc phenotype. Such studies reporting extra-cardiac
features were further complicated by cardiac arrest or hypoxic
brain injuries, causing neurological injury secondary to the
primary arrhythmias. Concern was raised throughout the
consensus group meetings that this may represent under-
reporting or under-assessment of non-cardiac phenotypic fea-
tures. These single organ-system presentations may be due to
splice variation, mosaicism or decreased penetrance, but all
individuals presenting in such a manner require a full, holistic,
multisystemic medical work-up. Individuals presenting with
single organ system presentations of a pathogenic CACNA1C
rare variant (Fig. 3), for example, LQT8, fall within the CACNA1C-
Related Disorders umbrella term. Individuals identi ﬁed via single
organ-system presentations early in childhood may present later
in development with further symptoms as CACNA1C expression
regulation changes [ 5] and therefore ongoing observation and
follow-up are advised. In those with CACNA1C variants, each
individual ’s QT intervals can be highly variable with wide ranges
and cardiac risk does not clearly correlate with observed QT
duration [ 8, 21]. The emergence of symptoms dependent on
developmental stage further supports the umbrella term of
CACNA1C-Related Disorders for all patients presenting with a
pathogenic CACNA1C variant and the need for regular monitoring
as standardised baselines of care.
Further cardiac phenotypes have previously been linked with
CACNA1C rare variants, including Brugada Syndrome (BrS) [
33–35]
and short QT interval or syndrome (SQT) [ 33, 36–38]. Shortened
QT intervals (SQT) and Brugada Syndrome (BrS) were ﬁrst
reported in individuals with CACNA1C p.A39V and p.G490R
variants in 2007, not long after the de ﬁnition of TS. The evidence
for such variants as a genetic aetiology of SQT and BrS is disputed
[9, 36, 39]. As with LQT8, subsequent case series and reports
drawn from clinical cohorts of individuals with established BrS or
early repolarisation syndromes have proposed a number of
variants across the CACNA1C gene linked to a multisystemic
phenotype [ 33, 37, 40]. Reviews of these genes dispute the causal
relationship [ 39], as further evidenced by ClinGen ’s re-evaluation
of the literature [ 9]. Subsequent international meetings held on
topics of ultra-rare cardiac diseases have supported this opinion,
based on insuf ﬁcient case-level data and lack of functional
validation evidencing an association between CACNA1C and
Brugada Syndrome. We therefore endorse that Brugada Syn-
drome is not associated with CACNA1C variants.
Congenital heart disease and cardiomyopathy presentations
are increasingly reported to be associated with CACNA1C variants
[41–43]. Published cases report cardiomyopathy (hypertrophic or
noncompaction) [ 31, 42–45], congenital heart disease and sinus
node disease [ 7, 42, 46], frequently overlapping with existing
documented cardiac features. Where features extend beyond
prolonged QT to include other cardiac disease but do not involve
other organ systems, the description cardiac-only TS, which was
initially an alternative to LQT8, is now also used [ 29, 30, 43]. We
recognise that there is no consensus on this evidence, with a
need for further in-depth assessments and functional analyses of
posited variants.
Molecular diagnostic criteria
Functional interpretation . The initial novel missense p.G406R
variant ﬁrst identi ﬁed in exon 8A results is a gain-of-function
(increased Ca
2+ inﬂux during an action potential), manifesting as a
lower threshold for channel opening, reduced voltage-dependent
inactivation (VDI) and reduced calcium-dependent inactivation
(CDI) [3, 6, 47, 48]. Electrophysiological studies have demonstrated
that this combination of effects is not isolated to the p.G406R
variant [ 12, 48]. The TS phenotype appears associated pathophy-
siologically with speci ﬁc changes in the electrophysiological
properties and functions of the Ca
v1.2 channel, as well as upon
how broadly the variant is expressed due to variable mosaicism
and/or tissue-speci ﬁc expression of alternatively spliced exons, as
recently reviewed by Bauer et al.[ 12]. Subsequent work has shown
that these variants have further effects in neuronal models,
including decreased synaptic inhibition, defects in cortical
differentiation, altered long-term potentiation and altered cortical
interneuron migration [ 49–51]. Variant function can therefore be
understood to be more complex than simple ‘gain- or loss-of-
function’and many variants result in changes of mixed effects at
the channel, cellular and system level [ 7, 12, 23, 25, 48]. Because of
this complexity, the current understanding of how variants
causative of Timothy syndrome result in clinical pathology is
incomplete. Timothy syndrome cannot be adequately explained
or deﬁned by known Ca
v1.2 channel gating changes, as the extant
literature covering such an approach does not capture the
variability of clinical presentations and therefore Timothy syn-
drome should be identi ﬁed through syndromic phenotype.
Further complicating this picture is evidence of both non-linear
relationships between CACNA1C variants and Ca V1.2 expression
proﬁles [ 47]. Variants may show increased cell surface expression
due to decreased degradation [ 7, 32], while some variants (notably
p.G406R) increase basal transcription through excitation-
transcription coupling [ 52] or alter neuronal gene expression [ 53],
with enhanced activation of the Ca v1.2 channel driving multiple
pathological mechanisms at both the cellular and network level
[47, 54, 55]. Expression of Ca
v1.2 and development of symptoms at a
system or organ level are subsequently dependent on transcription
of the pathological variant. Ca v1.2 is widely expressed in almost all
tissues and is critical for development [3, 5, 56]. Evidence from long-
read sequencing suggests that CACNA1C incorporates at least 47
constituent exons, with >240 novel transcripts and those transcripts
demonstrate developmental stage and tissue-speci ﬁc splicing,
which are predicted to alter channel function [ 57]. These mechan-
isms, in combination with germline mosaicism, are hypothesised to
drive the irregular penetrance and phenotypic variability seen
across CRDs [ 5, 12, 13, 18, 21].

Due to this complexity, clinical pathogenicity should not be
extrapolated based upon inferred gain- or loss-of-function. Variants
have historically been reported against different CACNA1C tran-
scripts, with no single reference transcript existing. This can result in
the reporting of alternative coding DNA (c.) and protein (p.) level
changes for the same genomic co-ordinates, complicating cross-
comparison and interpretation. We therefore suggest that all
variants are reported with both the transcript used (for coding
DNA and protein changes) and the genomic coordinates. We
recommend supporting clinical variant interpretation with func-
tional testing of the variant in established model systems. Tools and
literature supporting variant interpretation are predicted to evolve
and therefore we advise periodic re-testing and re-evaluation
utilising updated evidence. This guidance supports the recommen-
dation that diagnoses should be made on a syndromic basis and
pathogenicity cannot yet be inferred from gain- or loss-of-function
descriptors alone.
Guidelines for clinical care
Due to the widespread but variable expression and mixed effects
of CACNA1C gene variants, presentations are individual-speci ﬁc
but frequently multisystemic. All individuals with a CACNA1C
pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant require a full multi-
disciplinary team clinical work-up, to include, at a minimum,
cardiac, neurodevelopmental, musculoskeletal and endocrine
reviews. We expect individuals to show development of new
syndromic features and associated risk over time, secondary to
changes in expression of CACNA1C and the Ca
v1.2 channel over
the lifespan, necessitating consistent follow-up. For individuals
with variants of unknown or uncertain signi ﬁcance (VUS) in the
CACNA1C gene, it is reasonable to clinically screen for other organ
involvement to determine if there is additional clinical evidence
of a CRD.
Cardiac features of CRDs, including TS, as the most reported and
historically most lethal phenotypic presentation, have received
signiﬁcant therapeutic study [ 5, 12]. Recent reviews and case
series have demonstrated that implantable cardioverter-
deﬁbrillators (ICDs) are the most effective current treatments for
Timothy syndrome and Long QT presentations [ 18, 21, 29, 58].
Beta-blocker medications have previously been used for cardiac
management, but may not prevent arrhythmias or sudden cardiac
death and therefore are not recommended as standalone therapy
[8, 18, 21]. Surgical interventions, through left cardiac sympathetic
denervation to attenuate heterogeneous sympathetic myocardial
innervation, have been trialed with some mixed evidence in those
with symptoms refractory to other treatments [ 5, 21, 59]. In
individuals whose age- and sex-adjusted QT interval borders on
prolonged, some evidence suggests exercise stress testing may
precipitate the functional 2:1 AV block and support diagnosis
[32, 60]. Calcium-channel blockers, perhaps counterintuitively, are
not effective in the management of TS due to the complex gating
changes in Ca
v1.2 [5, 48, 61]. Implantation of an ICD early in life in
TS children decreases mortality risk and this change in practice
has led to a dramatic increase in life expectancy of TS children
since the syndrome was ﬁrst described [ 5, 18, 21, 61].
Very few organ-level reviews have been undertaken in TS or
more broadly across CRDs. Timothy et al ’s 2024 Natural History
Study of TS covers a broad range of phenotypic features in depth,
including discussion of key areas for clinical concern [ 5]. As TS and
CRD children survive longer, there is evidence of emerging
mortality risks from further syndromic features [ 5, 14, 15]. Epilepsy
is prevalent across CRDs and seizures may be refractory to
standard treatments [ 10, 11, 13, 14, 25]. Individuals with CRDs
where there is suspicion of seizure activity should have neurologic
evaluation and seizure rescue medication available in their home,
work or school environments. Hypoglycaemia has been noted in
TS and CRDs [ 3, 5, 6, 21] and recent work has demonstrated that
this is through dysregulated glucose homeostasis, absent
hyperinsulinism and defects in glucagon secretion [ 62]. Individuals
with CACNA1C variants are at risk of hypoglycaemia, particularly
when in states of physiological stress, including viral illnesses,
which may be compounded by arrhythmias and lower seizure
thresholds to increase the risk of sudden death [ 10, 21]. Those
with Timothy syndrome and more severe presentations of CRD
should therefore monitor blood glucose levels during periods of
physiological stress or illness and have rescue kits (e.g. glucose
tablets or gel, fruit juice or sugary drinks/candy) available for use
in episodes of low blood sugar.

interventions and are treated symptomatically and individually.
Responses to anaesthesia are reportedly abnormal and care
should be taken with all sedation [ 5, 21]. Many children with CRDs

CONCLUSIONS
In the thirty years since Keating et al ﬁrst described a ‘heart-hand’
syndrome and twenty years since this was formalised as Timothy
syndrome, the landscape of phenotypic features associated with
rare pathogenic variants in the CACNA1C gene has changed
substantially. TS remains the most severe presentation, but
genomic variants linked to this syndromic phenotype have
extended beyond the classical p.G406R description. Meanwhile,
a range of cases have been reported with overlapping or
contrasting phenotypic features and novel rare CACNA1C variants.
Here, through expert consensus and community engagement, we
summarise updated phenotypes and features for TS and its
associated presentations. We provide a formal de ﬁnition for
CACNA1C-Related Disorder, a syndromic phenotype capturing
non-classical and newly recognised presentations of rare CACNA1C
variants. Finally, we present the ﬁrst guidelines on standards of
diagnostic and clinical care for individuals presenting with a rare
CACNA1C gene variant, driven by the evidence that, with
appropriate interventions, these individuals can live longer,
fulﬁlling lives.
