# PAX6 Deficiency Compromises the Ability of Limbal Epithelial Stem Cells to Properly Differentiate Into Mature Corneal Epithelial Cells.
**Authors:** Foroozandeh P, Kaplan N, Qi X, Yang W, Chen X, Lee SK, Ren Z, Lavker RM, Kume T, Peng H
**Journal:** Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1167/iovs.67.1.56](https://doi.org/10.1167/iovs.67.1.56)

## Content

PURPOSE: Aniridia, driven by PAX6 mutations, causes aniridia-associated keratopathy (AAK), a progressive condition linked to limbal stem cell deficiency. A major hurdle to developing targeted therapies for AAK is the incomplete understanding of the molecular abnormalities in affected corneas. To address this, we leveraged Pax6± (Pax6 het) mice, a model of AAK, and applied single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to profile the transcriptomic changes at a single-cell resolution.

METHODS: ScRNA-seq of corneal/limbal tissues of wild type (WT) and Pax6 het mice were conducted. Immunostaining was performed to examine the expression of specific markers for stem cells.

RESULTS: ScRNA-seq identified a quiescent limbal epithelial stem cell (LESC)-like cell cluster and an early transient amplifying cell (eTAC)-like cluster. An increase in the cell numbers in these two clusters in the Pax6 het mouse corneas was observed. Immunostaining detected a marked increase in markers for these two clusters including Tmem176b, Apoe, and Krt15 in the corneal epithelium of Pax6 het mice, suggesting an increase of these LESC/eTA-like cells into the corneal epithelium. The Pax6 deficiency inhibited the expression of genes involved in cell proliferation in the eTAC-like cluster as well as the expression of genes related to corneal epithelial cell fate and differentiation compared with WT mice.

CONCLUSIONS: Our single cell transcriptome of the limbus and cornea of Pax6 het mice indicates that AAK may be due to the increase of dysfunctional stem/eTACs with defects in committing to a corneal epithelial cell fate and differentiation.

Corneal epithelial stem cells reside in the basal layer of the limbal epithelium at the edge of the cornea and thus are called limbal epithelial stem cells (LESCs).1 The LESCs are responsible for corneal epithelial homeostasis by generating their progeny transient amplifying cells (TACs), which centripetally migrate toward and differentiate into corneal epithelial cells. Failure of these TACs to differentiate into mature corneal epithelial cells can result in vision loss. During corneal epithelial wound healing, quiescent LESCs are activated to rapidly proliferate, which leads to expansion of their immediate progeny, the early TACs (eTACs).2–5 It is generally accepted that stem cells and eTACs are phenotypically and morphologically indistinguishable.6 Such LESC activation and subsequent orderly expansion of eTACs repair the corneal epithelium,2–5 which is crucial for maintaining corneal epithelial homeostasis and tissue transparency, features essential for clear vision. Loss or dysfunction of LESCs or an alteration in the stem cell niche cause limbal stem cell deficiency (LSCD),7,8 which can lead to severe visual impairment, and ultimately blindness.

Aniridia is most widely recognized for its hallmark absence or severe hypoplasia of the iris. Patients with aniridia frequently develop other ocular abnormalities. Among these abnormalities, patients with aniridia have a high prevalence to develop aniridia-associated keratopathy (AAK),9 which is largely due to LSCD.10,11 The LSCD can lead to impaired corneal epithelial homeostasis and wound healing, chronic inflammation, corneal stromal scarring, neovascularization, and conjunctivalization.7,8 Consequently, patients with AAK often experience ocular surface instability manifesting as recurrent epithelial erosions, ulceration, stromal scarring, and persistent dry eye, all of which contribute to poor visual quality.

The majority of congenital aniridia are attributed to mutations in the PAX6 gene,12,13 which encodes the PAX6 transcription factor. The mouse Pax6 gene is homologous to the human PAX6 gene, and both are widely expressed in developing ocular tissues, including the iris, lens, corneal epithelium, ciliary body, and all layers of the retina. The heterozygous Pax6 het mice share many of the ocular features of human aniridia and have been widely accepted as an animal model for AAK.14 Consistent with this idea, Pax6 het mice display corneal neovascularization and conjunctivalization14 and these aberrations are consistent with an LSCD phenotype.7,8

A major hurdle to finding novel specific treatments to reverse the progress of AAK is that underlying molecular abnormalities in corneas with AAK are not fully appreciated. Thus, in the present study, we took advantage of single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to characterize the abnormalities in Pax6 het mouse corneas at the single cell level. ScRNA-seq identified LESC and eTAC-like clusters and the cell numbers of these two clusters were markedly increased in Pax6 het mice. To validate this observation, immunostaining demonstrated an increased number of cells expressing markers of LESC and eTAC-like clusters in Pax6 het mice.

Animal procedures comply with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and was approved by Northwestern University Animal Care and Use Committee. Pax6 het female (C57BL/6 background) and wild type (WT) female (C57BL/6 from Jax lab) mice were housed with free access to food and water in a barrier facility. Mice at 8 weeks of age were used. In accordance with NU-IACUC approved Euthanasia Policy and the recommendations of the Panel on Euthanasia of the American Veterinary Medical Association, the adult mice were euthanized by CO2. To evaluate clinically for corneal clarity, a slit lamp examination was performed to visualize the corneal haze.

Corneal/limbal buttons were dissected from two female Pax6 het (4 corneas) and two female WT littermate (4 corneas) mice. Briefly, following euthanasia, mouse eyeballs were proptosed and removed using forceps. Corneal/limbal buttons were excised while keeping a portion of the sclera/conjunctiva. The corneal buttons were placed in PBS with the epithelium facing downward. The iris and ciliary body were removed and the conjunctiva behind the ciliary body was trimmed, although a small portion of conjunctiva was retained. The corneal/limbal buttons were incubated in 0.7 mg/mL liberase TM (from Roche) in RPMI at 37°C for 1 hour and then with 0.25% Trypsin for 15 minutes, as previously described.5,15–18 Pooled single cell suspensions from 2 mice were used to generate the library using a 10 × Genomics Chromium single cell universal 3' gene expression platform. After sequencing, raw sequencing data were processed using CellRanger (10 × Genomics), followed by data normalization, integration, and clustering with the R Seurat package 5.2, as previously described.5,15–18 Regarding the parameter tuning in the analysis, we used all default parameters. To exclude dead cells or debris, cells were filtered out if the levels of mitochondrial or ribosomal gene expression exceeded 15% of total counts, or if fewer than 1000 RNAs were detected. To exclude potential doublets, cells with more than 8000 detected genes were also removed. A list of 2000 highly variable genes was generated. The top 10 significant principal components (PCs) were used for clustering because we can observe an “elbow” around PC10, suggesting that the majority of true signal is captured in the first 10 PCs. Seurat’s FindClusters() function was used to generate clusters for the data, with a resolution of 0.35. Conserved cell type markers were obtained by the Seurat FindConservedMarkers() function and visualized by Dot Plot that shows both the expression level and the percentage of cells in a cluster expressing those marker genes. These marker genes for each cluster were used to annotate these clusters as specific cell types. DimPlot() was used to visualize the highlighted LESC/eTAC-like clusters. VlnPlot was used to visualize gene expressions by each cluster in each sample. FeatureScatter() and FeaturePlot() were used to visualize the cells co-expressing Krt12 and Krt14. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between each sample in each cluster were identified by “FindMarkers.” Gene ontology assay was performed in DAVID Functional Annotation Bioinformatics Resources. AddModuleScore analysis was conducted using gene sets related to apoptosis19 (Supplementary Table S2) and quiescence20 (Supplementary Table S3). The gene set Hallmark_Apoptosis includes genes with critical roles in regulating apoptosis from the MSigDB Collections. Hallmark gene sets summarize and represent specific well-defined key biological states or processes and display coherent expressions. AddModuleScore analysis is more robust than individual gene expression as such overall activity of a gene set relies on multiple genes. Cell-cycle analysis was conducted using function CellCycleScoring() in Seurat. Briefly, G2/M and S phase markers (Supplementary Table S4) were used for calculating an enrichment score for G2/M and S phases. Higher scores indicate high expression of genes associated with these cell cycle phases. The scRNA-seq data that support the findings of this study are submitted to NCBI GEO database (GSE298538).

Cell-cell communications (ligand-receptor interactions) were analyzed using an R software package CellChat. In CellChat, the strength of interaction is a quantitative measure of the predicted communication probability between a sender cell group and a receiver cell group for a given ligand-receptor (L-R) signaling pathway. Higher values indicate stronger predicted signaling potential, driven by both high ligand abundance in the sender and high receptor abundance in the receiver, weighted by biological plausibility. The score of the strength of interaction gets larger when: (1) the sending cell type expresses more of the ligand gene(s); (2) the receiving cell type expresses more of the matching receptor gene(s); and (3) the ligand-receptor pair is known to be biologically relevant (from CellChat's curated database).

Optimum cutting temperature (O.C.T.) frozen sections with mouse eye tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 minutes in room temperature. These sections were incubated with primary antibodies: Tmem176b (rabbit polyclonal antibody, 19825-1-AP; Proteintech), Krt15 (mouse antibody, MA5-11344; Invitrogen), and Apoe (rabbit antibody, 49285S; Cell Signaling), Krt12 (rabbit polyclonal antibody, PA5-71744; Invitrogen), antibodies at 1:50 dilution at 4°C overnight as previously described.16 Slides were visualized with a Nikon Ti-2 (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) microscope (Northwestern CAM core).15 ImageJ software was used for cell counting, relative fluorescence intensity analysis, and measuring the thickness of epithelial layer. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 10).

Unpaired t-test and paired t-test were performed to determine statistical significance. One-way ANOVA were performed to determine statistical significance in immunostaining assays. The data are shown as means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05.

At 8 weeks of age, Pax6 het mouse eyes had microphthalmia and ocular opacity (Supplementary Fig. S1A). In the Pax6 het mice, histological analysis revealed that the expression of Keratin 12 (Krt12) protein and corneal epithelial thickness were dramatically reduced compared to WT (Supplementary Figs. S1B–D).16 These findings indicated a severe impairment in epithelial homeostasis. To understand how Pax6 affects limbal/corneal epithelial homeostasis, we conducted an scRNA-seq experiment in cells isolated from the corneal/limbal buttons from two female WT and two female Pax6 het mice (8 weeks old). The scRNA-seq yielded 9406 cells from WT mice and 8962 cells from Pax6 het mice. Analysis of the scRNA-seq data using Seurat demonstrated 15 clusters (Fig. 1A). The expression patterns of marker genes across 15 cell clusters were visualized using a dot plot (Fig. 1B). Six of these clusters expressed the mesenchymal marker Vimentin (Vim) and thus were identified as mesenchymal cells. Among these mesenchymal cells, Acta2 was detected in smooth muscle cells, whereas Lumican (Lum) and Keratocan (Kera) were expressed in keratocytes (see Fig. 1B). The myeloid cell cluster expressed markers for monocyte/macrophage (Cd68, Cd14, Cd86, and Tnf) and was negative for the neutrophile marker (Ly6g; see Fig. 1B). Because the myeloid cell cluster also expressed Mrc1 (Supplementary Fig. S2A), a resident macrophage marker,21 this cluster was classified as resident macrophages. The resident macrophages were increased in Pax6 het mouse corneas (1.3%) compared to WT (0.3%; Supplementary Table S1). Two vascular endothelial cell clusters expressed Pecam1 (see Fig. 1B). Interestingly, the vascular endothelial cell 2 cluster expressed Lyve1 (see Supplementary Fig. S2A), a maker for lymphatic vessels and thus, was identified as lymphatic endothelial cells. It has been realized that WT mouse corneas are devoid of Lyve1+ cells whereas in Pax6 het mouse corneas, persistent Lyve1+ lymph vessels are accompanied by stromal infiltration of macrophages.22 This is consistent with our scRNA-seq data showing an increased cell number in the Lyve1+ vascular endothelial cells 2 cluster (see Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Fig. S2B). The scRNA-seq identified a cluster of corneal Schwann cells expressing Plp1 and Cdh19 (see Fig. 1B). We conducted a cell-cell communication assay using the CellChat package in R software and found a significant reduction in the interactions between Schwann cells and all corneal/limbal epithelial cell clusters (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Interestingly, a UMAP plot showed that the cell numbers of the LESC and eTAC-like cell clusters were markedly increased in corneas of Pax6 het mice compared with WT littermates (Fig. 3A). The scRNA-seq revealed that Tmem176b, Apoe, and Krt15 were markers for both clusters (Fig. 3B). Krt15 and Apoe are putative LESC markers.2,31,32 Depletion of Tmem176b results in cell cycle arrest33 and Tmem176b also has an inhibitory role in pulmonary fibrosis.34 In WT mice, Tmem176b expression was significantly higher in LESC and eTAC-like cell clusters than corneal epithelial cells (see Fig. 3B). To validate whether Pax6 deficiency results in an increase of LESC and eTAC-like cell clusters, we conducted immunostaining for these specific markers in Pax6 het mouse eyes. Immunostaining revealed that in WT mice, Tmem176b, Apoe, and Krt15 were highly expressed in the limbal epithelium (Fig. 4), which supports the idea that Tmem176b, Apoe, and Krt15 are putative LESC markers. In contrast, these proteins in Pax6 het mice were detected in both the limbal and corneal epithelium (see Fig. 4). More interestingly, between WT and Pax6 het mice, the expression of Tmem176b, Apoe, and Krt15 showed no significant difference in expression in the limbal epithelium. In contrast, the expression of these proteins were dramatically increased in the corneal epithelium of Pax6 het mice compared with WT mice. Because Tmem176b, Apoe, and Krt15 were markers for the LESC and eTAC-like cell clusters, these observations suggest that a decrease of Pax6 gene dosage results in the increase of LESC and eTAC-like cells in the corneal epithelium.

Because only two WT and Pax6 het mice were used in the scRNA-seq experiment, we questioned whether increasing the number of mice will alter the single cell RNA-seq results. We integrated an additional dataset of WT mouse corneal/limbal button from a previous study (4 corneas). UMAP showed that the integration of additional WT replicate (wt2; Supplementary Fig. S4) reproduced the clustering result. In addition, with the same parameters, the LESC/eTAC-like cell clusters were identified (see Supplementary Fig. S4) and the cell numbers of these two clusters were consistently markedly increased in Pax6 het mice compared with WT mice (see Supplementary Fig. S5). These observations suggest that the inclusion of this additional WT sample replicates the findings from our original scRNA-seq dataset with two WT and Pax6 het mice.

To characterize these LESC and eTAC-like cells, the DEGs in LESC and eTAC-like cell clusters were subjected to GO assay using the DAVID program. Interestingly, in both clusters, stress-related genes were upregulated, including “unfolded protein binding,” “autolysosome,” “ubiquitin protein ligase binding,” and “Ferroptosis” (Supplementary Fig. S6A). This observation indicates that cells are undergoing stress and damage, which leads to the activation of the apoptotic pathway and attempts to repair the damage. We conducted the AddModuleScore analysis30 using the Hallmark gene sets from the GSEA MSigDB collections. Among Hallmark gene sets, we detected an increase in the overall activity of the gene set related to apoptosis in LESC and eTAC-like cell clusters of Pax6 het mice compared with WT mice (Supplementary Fig. S6B), which supports the idea that LESC and eTAC-like cells are under stress and dysregulated.

In LESC-like cells, genes downregulated in Pax6 het mice were enriched in the GO terms “cell-cell adhesion,” “basement membrane,” and “extracellular matrix structural constituent” (see Supplementary Fig. S6A), indicating that LESC-like cells may contribute to the abnormalities in the basement membrane in human aniridia and Pax6 het mice.14,35

We examined further the expression of genes (Krt12, Dsg1a, and Dsg1b)44–46 related to corneal epithelial cell fate and differentiation and found that the expression of those genes was significantly reduced in all limbal/corneal epithelial clusters (Fig. 5B), indicating a defect in corneal epithelial differentiation and maturation. Klf4 and Zfp750, which are key transcription factors regulating epithelial cell differentiation,47,48 were also markedly reduced in these clusters (see Fig. 5B). Furthermore, an increase in Krt4 expression was detected in LESC/eTAC-like clusters as well as TACs (see Fig. 5B). Because Krt4 is primarily expressed in healthy esophageal epithelium,49 the increase in Krt4 suggests that LESC-like cells in Pax6 het mice fail to commit a corneal epithelial cell fate.

To characterize further the failure in differentiation and cell fate commitment, we examined the expression of Krt8/Krt18, which is believed to be expressed in corneal epithelial cells before E14.50 Not surprisingly, the expression of Krt8/Krt18 was not detectible (Fig. 6A). Interestingly, we found an increase in Krt14 (basal layer marker) and a decrease in Krt12 (differentiation marker; see Fig. 6A), which is consistent with previous immunostaining observations that showed a reduction in Krt12 protein in Pax6 het mouse corneas.16 It has been shown that at birth (P0), corneal epithelial cells are Krt5+/Krt14+ whereas Krt12 is negative.50 In adult mice, Krt5+/Krt14+ cells persist in the basal layer of corneal epithelium and Krt12 is expressed in suprabasal and superficial layers, suggesting that Krt14+/Krt12– cells are less differentiated, whereas Krt14–/Krt12+ cells are more differentiated.50 Interestingly, some basal cells express both Krt14 and Krt12, suggesting that Krt12+/Krt14+ cells are committed corneal epithelial cells, and the differentiation state of Krt12+/Krt14+ cells is between Krt14+/Krt12– and Krt14–/Krt12+ cells.50 Our scRNA-seq analysis showed that both Krt12+/Krt14+ and Krt12+/Krt14– cells were markedly reduced in Pax6 het mice (Fig. 6B), which further supports the idea that differentiation and corneal epithelial cell commitment are retarded.

LSCD can be a result of loss or dysfunction of LESCs and/or disruption of the stem cell niche. Here, we show that instead of loss of LESCs, Pax6 het results in an accumulation of dysfunctional LESCs and that such dysfunctional stem cells are not only located in the limbal basal layer, the normal niche for LESCs. The LESC-like cells are also found in the cornea (see Fig. 4). This suggests that PAX6-related AAK is likely due to dysfunction of LESCs and disruption of the stem cell niche. The dysfunction of these stem cells is also supported by our observation that proliferation-related gene expression is inhibited in these LESC/eTAC clusters, which may result in the reduced efficiency of producing the progenies (corneal epithelial cells) of stem cells in Pax6 het mice compared with WT mice.51 It has been indicated that Ace2/Lcn2 pathway is a negative regulator of limbal epithelial cell proliferation and plays a role in activation of LESCs to rapidly generate the progenies.16 Thus, the defect in producing the progenies of stem cells in Pax6 deficient mouse corneas may also be attributed to the increase of Lcn2 expression in the eTAC cluster (see Fig. 5A). A compensatory effect causes the increase of these dysfunctional LESCs in Pax6 deficient mouse corneas. The increase of LESC-like cells is also supported by the observation that depletion of PAX6 significantly upregulated ABCG2 expression,52 a putative LESC marker. However, the increase in the LESC-like cluster may reflect a response to external factors such as a chronic wound healing.

In humans, all cell layers become Krt14+ in both the limbal and corneal epithelia before birth.50 Later, the superficial limbal and suprabasal corneal epithelial cells become Krt14− because they have differentiated and now express Krt12. Consistently, in mice, at P0, Krt12 expression is hardly detected. After P4, Krt12 expression is gradually upregulated and primarily limited to suprabasal and superficial layers.50 Our scRNA-seq analysis shows that the corneal epithelium of Pax6 het mice shows a dominance of Krt14+/Krt12– cells, which suggests that the corneal epithelium of Pax6 het mice maintains a neonatal phenotype and fails to further differentiate into Krt12+ cells during postnatal development. This is consistent with the observations in patients with aniridia in which, at birth, the corneal epithelium is clinically normal and AAK manifests itself from mid to late childhood and onward.53 Many studies have confirmed the dysregulation of limbal/corneal epithelial differentiation in patients with AAK as well as mouse models.16 Interestingly, a recent study for the first time profiles microRNA expression in limbal epithelium of patients with aniridia and shows that the most enriched GO term for the direct targets of the differentially expressed microRNAs includes regulation of cell differentiation.54 Among these differentially expressed microRNAs, miR-138-5p was significantly upregulated in the limbal epithelium of patients with AAK. FOXC1, a predicted direct target gene of miR-138-5p, was markedly reduced in aniridia samples.54 Mutations in the FOXC1 gene has been associated with aniridia.55,56 We have shown that an adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector to topically deliver human FOXC1 proteins into Pax6 het mouse limbal epithelium increases the expression of Krt12 in corneal epithelium, suggesting that the dysregulation of miR-138-5p/FOXC1 in Pax6 het mice may contribute to the compromised differentiation. It has also been shown that microRNA-31 is downregulated in impression cytology samples of patients with congenital aniridia.57 We have demonstrated that reduced expression of microRNA-31 inhibits differentiation of corneal epithelial cells and epidermal keratinocytes via its direct target gene FIH-1,58 an endogenous inhibitor of HIF1. These observations suggest that microRNAs play important roles in the pathologies of AAK, particularly compromised epithelial differentiation.

Autophagy is a cellular recycling process in response to stress. There is little evidence that autophagy itself is a primary feature or a known direct cause of aniridia. Interestingly, we have demonstrated that autophagy is critical for the activation of limbal epithelial cell proliferation in response to corneal injury.59 Our scRNA-seq data suggest that in Pax6 het mice, autolysosome-related genes are upregulated in the eTAC-like cell cluster (see Supplementary Fig. S6A), in which the genes associated with proliferation are downregulated. The induction of autophagy may be a compensatory effect that attempts to reverse the inhibitory effect on proliferation of eTAC-like cells.

It has been suggested that a disruption of the neuro-epithelium homeostasis contributes to loss of the nerve spiral pattern and projection of stromal nerves into the epithelium in Pax6 het mouse corneas and patients with aniridia.10,60–62 Consistently, the cell-cell communication assay found a significant reduction in the interactions between Schwann cells and all corneal/limbal epithelial cell clusters (see Supplementary Fig. S3), suggesting a compromised nerve-epithelial interaction. Such compromised interaction may contribute to the declining neurotrophic status in both mouse models and human AAK patients.62,63 Although our mouse model for AAK has many of the phenotypes that are seen in patients, there are some limitations to the model. For example, in patients with AAK, the phenotype that we reported does not appear in mild situations and represents a more advanced stage.

Our routine procedure for isolating single cell suspension yields cells from corneal/limbal epithelia, corneal/limbal stroma, and a small fraction of conjunctiva. Thus, a common limitation of our scRNA-seq analysis is that the single cell suspension only includes the very low abundance of stem/eTACs, which only resolves one LESC/eTAC population without additional re-clustering. It is noted that the number of LESC/eTAC is dramatically increased in Pax6 het mice, which enables us to distinguish and analyze the LESC/eTAC populations without re-clustering, thus reducing analytical arbitrariness. Recently, two studies indicate that two stem cell populations exist in the limbal basal epithelium. Based on their anatomic localization, they are named as outer LESCs, which are relatively quiescent, and inner LESCs.2,64 Our analysis confirms that the LESC-like cluster highly expresses outer LESC markers (Cd63, Ccnd2, and Gpha2; see Supplementary Fig. S2E)2 as well as genes related to quiescence. The eTAC-like cluster highly expresses inner LESC markers (Atf3, Socs3, Zfp36, Btg2, and Klf6; see Supplementary Fig. S2E)2 as well as proliferation related genes (e.g. Mki67). Because inner LESCs are proliferatively active,2 this suggests that eTAC-like cells could be the inner LESCs. However, there is not yet evidence showing the existence of these outer/inner LESC populations in the human limbus.

Our scRNA-seq study has limitations because only two WT and Pax6 het mice were used. To assess whether additional WT replicates would alter the results, we integrated an independent dataset of WT mouse corneal/limbal button (from another batch of scRNA-seq) into our data and repeated the analysis. The inclusion of this additional WT sample replicated the findings from our original scRNA-seq dataset with two WT and Pax6 het mice (see Supplementary Figs. S4, S5). Along with our scRNA-seq analysis, we performed additional external validation experiments to further strengthen our conclusions. We acknowledge the inherent limitations of scRNA-seq because all statistics are designed to be done on each individual cell level. In this case, the thousands of cells provide the sufficient statistical power for the analysis.
