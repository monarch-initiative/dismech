Enterovirus D68 (EV-D68) is a plus-strand RNA virus that primarily causes
respiratory infections in infants but, in rare cases, has been associated with
the pediatric paralytic disease acute flaccid myelitis. We previously
demonstrated that EV-D68 induces nonselective autophagy for its benefit. Here,
we demonstrate that the 3C protease of EV-D68 cleaves the mitochondrial fusion
protein Mitofusin 2 near its C-terminal HR2 domain, inducing fragmentation of
the mitochondrial network. This, in turn, triggers the formation of
mitophagosomes, a hallmark of mitophagy, a selective form of autophagy that
recycles mitochondria. Multiple hallmarks of mitophagy are observed during
infection, including loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and Parkin
translocation to the mitochondria, but mitochondrial degradation is blocked
during infection. While autophagy plays multiple roles in enterovirus infection,
depleting Mitofusin 2 or transiently overexpressing Mitofusin 2, particularly
the cleavage-resistant mutant, specifically reduces EV-D68 release from cells
without affecting intracellular titers. Our results show that enteroviruses
induce mitophagosomes as vectors for nonlytic release of virions from cells.

Enterovirus-D68 (EV-D68), a member of the Picornaviridae family, was first identified in 1962 in California (1). Since its discovery, the virus has caused seasonal clusters of respiratory illnesses, particularly in children, and cases have risen in recent decades (2). In the past decade, infection with EV-D68 has been associated with acute flaccid myelitis, a polio-like neurological condition that leads to asymmetric paralysis in infants and is a major public health concern (3, 4). To date, there are no prophylactic vaccines or antivirals against EV-D68 infection. Therefore, dissecting the mechanisms by which EV-D68 hijacks cellular machineries to aid its reproduction could open opportunities for therapeutic interventions against enteroviruses.

EV-D68 is a positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus with a genome size of 7.5 kb. The genome is encoded as a single polyprotein, which is processed by viral proteases into structural and nonstructural proteins. The structural proteins encapsidate the viral genome and play essential roles in viral binding and entry into the host cells. The nonstructural proteins play various roles during infection, including rearranging cellular membranes to form viral replication complexes. Picornavirus replication occurs on single- or double-membrane vesicles (5). These vesicles resemble autophagosomes, and numerous studies suggest that picornaviruses use autophagosomes for RNA replication, maturation, and nonlytic release (6–8). However, there are several subtypes of autophagy, including xenophagy, microautophagy, lipophagy, and mitophagy (9–11). The specific type or types of autophagy elicited during picornavirus infections, and their roles in viral infections, are beginning to be studied.

Mitophagy is one subset of autophagy that has been investigated previously for its role in enterovirus reproduction. It has been shown that Coxsackievirus B3 induces mitophagy to block interferon signals (12). A recent publication showed that the related enterovirus EV71 induces mitophagy to promote virus replication (13). However, it is unclear how mitophagic signaling is induced by enteroviruses, or what specific role mitophagy in particular plays in the enterovirus life cycle. Here, we provide mechanistic models to answer both of these outstanding questions.

Mitophagy is a selective type of autophagy that targets damaged and dysfunctional mitochondria for degradation, thereby maintaining mitochondrial quality control (14). Mitophagy is a subset of autophagy; both processes involve the formation of double-membrane vesicles called autophagosomes, which enclose cellular cargo and deliver it to lysosomes for degradation. However, unlike nonselective autophagy, mitophagy involves additional molecular players, including Parkin, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, and phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN)-induced kinase 1 (PINK1), a mitochondrial health sensor, neither of which is implicated in bulk nonselective autophagy. In polarized mitochondria, PINK1 is constitutively imported to the inner mitochondrial membrane, but it is rapidly degraded by the inner mitochondrial membrane protease presenilin associated rhomboid like (PARL) (15). However, in depolarized mitochondria, PINK1 import into the inner mitochondrial membrane is inhibited. This leads to the accumulation of PINK1 in the outer mitochondrial membrane, where it phosphorylates and recruits Parkin to the mitochondria. Parkin then ubiquitinates multiple mitochondrial membrane proteins, leading to the autophagic degradation of the mitochondria (16, 17). Parkin translocation to mitochondria is a hallmark of Parkin/PINK1-dependent mitophagy and is often used to determine mitophagy induction in cell culture.

Mitochondria are dynamic organelles that undergo fusion and fission to facilitate mitochondrial redistribution and maintain a healthy mitochondrial network (18, 19). While mitochondrial fusion, orchestrated for the most part by Mitofusin 1 (MFN-1) and MFN-2, is known to inhibit mitophagy, mitochondrial fission, facilitated by Dynamin-related protein-1 (Drp-1), among others, promotes mitophagy (20).

Besides Parkin/PINK1-dependent mitophagy, other forms of mitophagy, orchestrated by specific mitophagy receptors, exist (21). For instance, recently, Bcl-2-like protein 13 (BCL2L13), the mammalian homolog of yeast ATG32, which is vital for mitophagy induction in yeast, was identified as a mitophagy receptor in mammalian cells (22). BCL2L13 binds directly to LC3 through its LC3-interacting region and induces mitochondrial fragmentation and mitophagy in Drp1- and Parkin-depleted cells, indicating that mitophagy induced by BCL2L13 does not require Drp1 and Parkin.

We demonstrate for the first time, to our knowledge, that the EV-D68 3C protease cleaves MFN-2, inducing the formation of mitophagosomes. These mitophagosomes are used for EV-D68 nonlytic release from cells. Knockdown of components of mitophagy signaling, for both receptor-mediated and Parkin/PINK1-dependent mitophagy, decreased viral release without significantly affecting intracellular viral titers. Our results suggest that mitophagosomes are the specific subset of autophagosomes responsible for the nonlytic release of enteroviruses.

EV-D68, like several other picornaviruses, is known to induce and hijack the autophagic machinery for its benefit. However, whether EV-D68 induces specific organellar autophagy is unclear. CVB3, a related picornavirus and a known cause of myocarditis, is known to induce mitophagy and uses mitophagosomes for viral replication and egress (23). Since mitophagy is typically preceded by mitochondrial damage/depolarization, we asked whether EV-D68 infection affects mitochondrial dynamics. For this purpose, we mock-infected or infected H1HeLa cells and measured the mitochondrial membrane potential. As a positive control, we included carbonyl cyanide 3-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP), an inhibitor of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, which is widely used to disrupt mitochondrial activity and structure, as well as induce mitophagy (12, 24). As shown in Fig. 1A, similar to the CCCP control, EV-D68 infection causes mitochondrial depolarization. To understand how EV-D68 causes mitochondrial depolarization, we examined the effect of EV-D68 infection on intracellular calcium levels. An increase in intracellular calcium levels increases mitochondrial calcium uptake, leading to loss of mitochondrial membrane potential. We observed an increase in intracellular calcium levels in EV-D68–infected and CCCP-treated cells compared to the control, suggesting that an increase in intracellular calcium levels may contribute to mitochondrial depolarization during EV-D68 infection (Fig. 1B).

Next, we examined the effect of EV-D68 infection on the morphology of the mitochondrial network by immunofluorescence assay (IFA). The mock control showed a normal tubular mitochondrial network, as expected. However, in EV-D68–infected cells, we observed extensive fragmentation of the mitochondrial network (Fig. 1D). We then examined the effect of EV-D68 infection on Parkin translocation to the mitochondria, a hallmark of mitophagy. While there were no significant changes in Parkin levels between the mock-infected and EV-D68–infected cells in the nonmitochondrial fraction, EV-D68 infection increased Parkin translocation to the mitochondria to a similar degree as the CCCP control (Fig. 1C). To monitor active mitochondrial degradation, we monitored levels of cytochrome c oxidase subunit IV (Cox4), a mitochondrial subunit of cytochrome C oxidase. Despite a comparable level of Parkin translocation between CCCP and infection, Cox4 is degraded by CCCP induction, but not during infection (Fig. 1C). This may indicate induction of mitophagy by EV-D68, but not completion of the pathway. Another hallmark of mitophagy is the detection of mitophagosomes (mitochondria enveloped in autophagosomes) by electron microscopy (EM). To further confirm mitophagy induction during EV-D68 infection, we mock-infected or infected H1HeLa cells with EV-D68 [multiplicity of infection (MOI) = 25] for 4 hours for EM. As shown in Fig. 1E, unlike the mock infection control, which showed normal cellular morphology and organelle structures, EV-D68 infection rearranges membranes, resulting in the formation of replication organelles characteristic of picornavirus infection (25). Zooming in on the EM images, we observed mitochondria with intact cristae in the mock-infected control, in contrast to EV-D68–infected cells, in which loss of mitochondrial cristae was evident (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, we observed mitophagosomes in EV-D68–infected cells (Fig. 1G). Together, these data suggest that EV-D68 disrupts mitochondrial dynamics and induces mitophagosome formation.

CVB3 has previously been shown to induce mitochondrial fragmentation and mitophagy by inducing Drp-1 phosphorylation. To understand how EV-D68 induces mitochondrial fragmentation and mitophagy, we examined the expression levels of proteins that regulate mitochondrial dynamics, including MFN-2 and Drp-1, upon EV-D68 infection. While EV-D68 infection did not significantly alter Drp-1 levels, we observed, in addition to the full-length MFN-2 band, a smaller band beginning at 4 hours post infection (hpi), suggesting a potential cleavage fragment (Fig. 2A and fig. S1A). In contrast, we did not detect any potential cleavage fragments for MFN-1 (Fig. 2A). The picornavirus 3C protease is known to cleave several host proteins, including the autophagosomal SNAP receptor protein, SNAP29 (7, 26). To test whether the 3C protease is responsible for MFN-2 cleavage during EV-D68 infection, we incubated cell lysate with and without EV-D68 3C protease in vitro. Our in vitro cleavage assay showed that 3C protease cleaves MFN-2, displaying a similar band pattern as in the viral infection (Fig. 2C). On the basis of the size of the cleavage fragment, we hypothesized that the cleavage may occur closer to the C-terminal end, which contains the Heptad repeat 2 (HR2) domain essential for mitochondrial dimerization.

To test this hypothesis, we overexpressed a C-terminally hemagglutinin (HA)–tagged MFN-2 plasmid in H1HeLa cells and mock-infected or infected the cells with EV-D68, followed by a Western blot against HA and MFN-2. As expected, we detected an ~65-kDa cleavage fragment with an anti–MFN-2 antibody (Fig. 2D). In contrast, the anti-HA antibody failed to detect the 65-kDa cleavage fragment but instead stained a smaller fragment of ~17 kDa (Fig. 2E), indicating that EV-D68 3C protease cleaves MFN-2 nearer its C terminus. MFN-2 has a single putative 3C cleavage site at the C terminus. Cleavage at this site, glutamine-591 (Q591), would be expected to produce a 17-kDa cleavage fragment. We used site-directed mutagenesis to generate a point mutation in which glutamine at position 591 was replaced with alanine (Q591A). We then transfected H1HeLa cells with the Q591A construct for 24 hours, followed by EV-D68 infection. As shown in Fig. 2F, infection of wild-type (WT) MFN-2 expressing cells produced the 17-kDa cleavage fragment, as expected. However, we did not detect a cleavage fragment in lysates expressing the Q591A mutant, indicating that the 3C protease of EV-D68 cleaves MFN-2 at Q591.We wanted to confirm whether MFN-2 cleavage is conserved among enteroviruses. We infected cells with different enteroviruses, including EV-D68, as a control. As shown in Fig. 2B, all the enteroviruses tested cleaved MFN-2 at similar levels, suggesting that MFN-2 cleavage may be conserved among enteroviruses.

Our data show that MFN-2 is cleaved during EV-D68 infection by the viral 3C protease, suggesting that full-length MFN-2 may be detrimental to EV-D68 release. We depleted MFN-2 in H1HeLa and A549 cells and infected them with EV-D68 to measure the viral titers. Unexpectedly, the depletion of MFN-2 reduced EV-D68 extracellular titers in both H1HeLa (Fig. 3A) and A549 cells (Fig. 3B), without significantly affecting viral intracellular titers. To understand how MFN-2 depletion attenuates EV-D68 release, we knocked down MFN-2 in H1HeLa and A549 cells for a Western blot against the translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 20 (TOMM20), an outer mitochondrial membrane protein. TOMM20 degradation is often used as an indicator of active mitophagy. As demonstrated in Fig. 3C, the knockdown of MFN-2 leads to a loss of TOMM20 staining in both H1HeLa and A549 cells. In contrast, EV-D68 infection of these cells did not reduce TOMM20 staining (Fig. 3D). These findings suggest that the depletion of MFN-2 causes mitochondrial degradation, degrading the mitophagosomes necessary for viral egress and consequently restricting viral release. To further understand how MFN-2 regulates EV-D68 infection, we overexpressed the WT and mutant MFN-2 constructs in H1HeLa cells for 24 hours (Fig. 3G) and then infected these transfected cells with EV-D68. Overexpression of neither construct significantly alters viral intracellular titers (Fig. 3E). Unexpectedly, overexpression of both MFN-2 constructs reduced viral extracellular titers (Fig. 3F), with the mutant exhibiting a stronger effect on diminishing viral release, despite having a lower transfection efficiency (Fig. 3G).

The results in Fig. 1D demonstrate that EV-D68 infection causes a fragmentation of the mitochondrial network. We wanted to understand the timing of mitochondrial fragmentation during infection and whether the mitochondrial membranes and mitophagosomes are used as sites of viral RNA replication. We mock-infected or infected cells with EV-D68 and performed IFA against TOMM20 and double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), a viral RNA replication intermediate commonly used to mark viral RNA replication sites. As shown in Fig. 4, mock-infected cells showed a normal tubular mitochondrial signal. In contrast, EV-D68 infection causes fragmentation of the mitochondrial network, which starts early in the infection (~3 hpi) and becomes more apparent as infection progresses (~5 hpi). While the signals did not largely colocalize at 3 hpi, we observed an increase in colocalization between these signals at 5 hpi.

Our finding that the dsRNA signal colocalizes with TOMM20 at 5 hpi, but not 3 hpi (Fig. 4), suggested that mitophagy may be more important for the later stages of the viral life cycle, post-RNA replication. To test this hypothesis, we knocked down Parkin in H1HeLa cells (fig. S2A), infected the cells, and performed a plaque assay to measure intracellular and extracellular viruses. Knockdown of Parkin had no significant effect on viral intracellular titers but markedly reduced extracellular titers (Fig. 5A). To further confirm the effect of mitophagy on EV-D68 titers, we knocked down Parkin and PINK1 (fig. S2C) in A549 lung cancer cells. Like H1HeLa cells, knockdown of mitophagy specifically reduced extracellular viral titers (Fig. 5B). We observed similar results for BCL2L13 knockdown in H1HeLa (fig. S2B) and A549 cells, wherein knockdown of the mitophagy receptor reduced extracellular viral titers, without affecting viral intracellular titers (Fig. 5, C and F). Next, we tested whether simultaneous knockdown of Parkin/PINK1 mitophagy and receptor-mediated mitophagy through BCL2L13 knockdown would have additive effects on the viral titers. As shown in Fig. 5D, simultaneous depletion of BCL2L13 and Parkin or BCL2L13 and PINK1 in A549 cells did not further reduce EV-D68 titers compared to the individual knockdowns.

Together, these results indicate that mitophagy specifically regulates EV-D68 release. To gauge whether Parkin is crucial for EV-D68–induced mitophagy, we infected Parkin-depleted cells with EV-D68 and subsequently performed IFA against TOMM20 and endogenous LC3. Endogenous LC3 has a nucleocytoplasmic localization, with a strong nuclear pool and weak cytosolic pool (27). EV-D68 infection induced LC3 puncta formation, and these puncta partially colocalized with TOMM20 in the scramble control group. However, knockdown of Parkin attenuated both LC3 puncta formation and colocalization of the puncta with TOMM20 (Fig. 5E). Quantitative colocalization analysis of these data can be found in fig. S4. Furthermore, while knocking down Parkin and LC3B individually (Fig. 5G) reduced viral extracellular titers (Fig. 5H), knocking down LC3B and Parkin simultaneously did not further reduce EV-D68 release (Fig. 5H). These results suggest that Parkin is essential for EV-D68–induced mitophagy and that mitophagy signaling specifically regulates EV-D68 release.

Depleting components of mitophagy signaling attenuated EV-D68 release. To further substantiate how modulation of mitophagy regulates EV-D68 infection, we examined the impact of mCherry-Parkin (mCh-Parkin) overexpression on EV-D68 replication. We included CCCP as a positive control. We observed that mCh-Parkin, which appears diffuse in the cytoplasm, colocalized with the TOMM20 signal (Fig. 6A, top row). CCCP treatment, as expected, induced extensive mitochondrial network fragmentation and mCh-Parkin–mitochondria colocalization (Fig. 6A, middle row). Similar to CCCP treatment, EV-D68 infection, as heretofore shown, fragmented the mitochondrial network (Fig. 6A). However, we consistently found that most mCh-Parkin–expressing cells did not express the viral protein VP3, suggesting that mCh-Parkin inhibits EV-D68 infection (Fig. 6A, bottom row). To confirm this hypothesis, we examined the impact of mCh-Parkin expression on EV-D68 titers. Overexpression of mCh-Parkin reduced both intracellular and extracellular viral titers, with overexpression having a greater effect on extracellular than cell-associated EV-D68 titers (Fig. 6B).

To decipher the mechanism of how mCh-Parkin attenuates EV-D68 release, we examined the effect of mCh-Parkin expression on TOMM20 levels by Western blot. As shown in Fig. 6C, mCh-Parkin overexpression reduced TOMM20 levels compared to the pcDNA3.1 control, indicating that active mitophagy is being induced. We hypothesized that mCh-Parkin expression reduces EV-D68 egress by inducing degradative mitophagy; therefore, pharmacological induction of mitophagy through CCCP should also reduce EV-D68 egress from cells. As shown in Fig. 6 (D and E), CCCP treatment reduced EV-D68 release (Fig. 6D) and decreased TOMM20 levels (Fig. 6E) in WT-H1HeLa cells, but not in LC3B-depleted H1HeLa cells (fig. S3). These results suggest that mCh-Parkin attenuates EV-D68 release by inducing degradative mitophagy. Last, to confirm that mCh-Parkin impairs EV-D68 release by inducing mitophagy, we overexpressed mCh-Parkin in ATG5 knockout (ATG5 KO) HeLa cells (Fig. 6F) and infected the cells with EV-D68. ATG5 is an essential autophagy gene, acting as part of the ATG12-ATG5-ATG16L E1-like complex, crucial for LC3 lipidation. Deletion of ATG5 causes defects in both autophagy and mitophagy. While the knockout of ATG5 reduces EV-D68 release, expression of mCh-Parkin did not further reduce EV-D68 titers in knockout of ATG5 cells (Fig. 6G), indicating that the autophagic machinery is required for the effect of mCh-Parkin overexpression on virus yield.

Although we have previously demonstrated that EV-D68 subverts the autophagy pathway to promote viral replication, whether the virus induces organelle-specific autophagy was unclear. We show here for the first time, to our knowledge, that EV-D68 infection induces mitophagy by cleaving MFN-2 and destabilizing the mitochondrial network. This is in agreement with published data on CVB3 and EV71 (12, 13). However, we show, as evidenced by TOMM20 levels in these cells (Figs. 3C and 6C), that EV-D68 infection induces incomplete mitophagy, meaning that although mitophagosomes are induced by infection, they do not result in the degradation of mitochondria. Instead, the virus usurps the mitophagosomes for nonlytic viral egress. Our data suggest that mitophagosomes constitute the specific form of autophagosomes that regulate the autophagosome-mediated exit without lysis of enteroviruses, a phenomenon first described 20 years ago (28–30).

Our EM data show a loss of mitochondrial cristae, the main site of eukaryotes’ biological energy conversion, in EV-D68–infected cells, suggesting that EV-D68 infection causes mitochondrial damage and dysfunction (Fig. 1F) (31). Consistent with this, we found that EV-D68 infection causes mitochondrial membrane potential loss and mitochondrial fragmentation (Figs. 1, A and D, and 4). As damaged mitochondria are significant sources of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are harmful to macromolecules, their timely removal by mitophagy is critical for cell viability (32, 33). We and others have previously shown that EV-D68 induces autophagosome formation while preventing autophagic degradation (7, 26). The data here show that it is specifically the induction of mitophagosomes during EV-D68 infection, without mitochondrial degradation, that enhances virus production.

EV-D68 induces fragmentation of the cellular mitochondrial network by cleaving MFN-2, a vital mitochondrial fusion protein (Fig. 2). MFN-2 cleavage begins around 4 hpi, which coincides with the earliest time point we observed mitochondrial network fragmentation (Fig. 2A). We demonstrated that the viral 3C protease cleaves MFN-2 near the C-terminal HR2 domain (Fig. 2, C to E). The HR2 domain is essential for tethering MFN from opposing outer mitochondrial membranes, mediating mitochondrial fusion. Cleaving MFN-2 near the HR2 domain separates the HR2 domain from the guanosine triphosphatase domain at the N terminus, which is also important for mitochondrial fusion (34). Therefore, by cleaving MFN-2 at or near the HR2 domain, EV-D68 effectively blocks mitochondrial fusion, potentially leading to mitochondrial fragmentation.

Although our data demonstrate that EV-D68 infection causes mitochondrial damage, it is unclear whether MFN-2 cleavage and fragmentation of the mitochondrial network are sufficient to induce damage to the organelles and, subsequently, mitophagy. Hepatitis C virus (HCV), a member of the Flaviviridae family of viruses and an important cause of end-stage liver disease, was previously shown to cause mitochondrial damage by inducing ROS production. The viral multifunctional structural (core) and nonstructural (NS5A) proteins were identified as the essential inducers of ROS during HCV infection (35–39). HCV was also shown to cause mitochondrial fragmentation and mitophagy to dampen the type I interferon response by inducing Drp-1 protein expression and phosphorylation (40). CVB3 similarly induces Drp-1–dependent mitochondrial fragmentation and mitophagy. However, unlike HCV, CVB3 uses mitophagosomes for release, akin to the autophagosome-mediated exit without lysis model during poliovirus infection (8, 23). Unlike these viruses, however, EV-D68–mediated mitochondrial fragmentation does not appear to depend on Drp-1, as we did not observe any significant change in Drp-1 protein levels during infection.

For EV-D68, we also found that depleting MFN-2 phenocopies knockdown of mitophagy, reducing viral release without affecting intracellular viral titers (Fig. 3, A and B). This was an unexpected result, because we reasoned that the knockdown of MFN-2, known to induce fragmentation of mitochondrial networks, would induce mitophagosomes and promote EV-D68 release. An explanation for these data is shown in Fig. 3C, in which the knockdown of MFN-2 causes degradative mitophagy, indicated by the loss of TOMM20 staining in MFN-2–depleted cells. This finding suggests that by promoting mitophagy flux before the initiation of viral infection, MFN-2 knockdown deprives the virus of these crucial vesicles, directing them to lysosomes instead of a role in EV-D68 release.

Equally unexpected was our observation that overexpression of MFN-2 also reduces EV-D68 release (Fig. 3F). How MFN-2 overexpression attenuates EV-D68 release is unclear. However, in light of the finding that mitochondrial endoplasmic reticulum (ER) coupling orchestrated by MFN-2 inhibits mitophagy initiation (41), we posit that overexpression of MFN-2 enhances ER-mitochondria tethering, preventing mitophagosome formation during EV-D68 infection and thereby reducing viral release.

The 2B protein of EV-A71 was reported to trigger ROS production by interacting with the outer mitochondrial membrane protein, voltage-dependent anion channel 3, which is essential for viral RNA replication (42). We also detected EV-D68 2B protein in the mitochondrial fraction (Fig. 1C), suggesting a possible interaction between the viral protein and mitochondria. A future direction of our work will determine whether the 2B protein of EV-D68 is involved in ROS generation and EV-D68–induced mitochondrial damage and mitophagy.

While this study demonstrates that MFN-2 cleavage is conserved among enteroviruses, a limitation is that it does not establish whether these viruses cleave MFN-2 at the same site as EV-D68 or clarify the functional significance of this cleavage. Furthermore, although MFN-2 cleavage is shown to be essential for EV-D68 nonlytic release, as indicated by reduced viral release in cells overexpressing the Q591A cleavage-resistant mutant, the necessity of the cleavage product for mitophagosome-mediated viral nonlytic release remains undetermined.

In contrast to bulk nonselective autophagy, which is known to promote various stages of the enterovirus life cycle, including RNA replication, our results show that mitophagy is not crucial for EV-D68 RNA replication or production of infectious virions. Knockdown of Parkin/PINK1- and receptor-mediated mitophagy did not affect EV-D68 intracellular titers (Fig. 5, A, B, C, and F). Instead, knockdown of the cellular mitochondrial quality control process specifically impaired EV-D68 nonlytic release, suggesting that the mitophagosomes generated during EV-D68 infection are used to transport virions out of cells in a nonlytic fashion. Consistent with this hypothesis, induction of complete mitophagy through CCCP treatment or mCh-Parkin overexpression severely impairs EV-D68 release from WT-HeLa cells and not from mitophagy-incompetent ATG5 KO cells (Fig. 6, B to G).

Therapeutics to promote complete, degradative mitophagy, as we observed during MFN-2 knockdown, make an attractive possibility as a mechanism to restrict the release of the virus from infected cells. By targeting intracellular virions for degradation instead of release, mitophagy-promoting compounds could make exciting and postexposure prophylaxis treatments, markedly slowing the spread of the virus from cell to cell within a patient and allowing the immune system more time to clear the pathogen.
