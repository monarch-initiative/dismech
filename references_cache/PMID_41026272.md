# To the Editor, "CEBPE-Related Immunodeficiency Mimicking Acute Myeloid Leukemia: A Diagnostic Pitfall in Pediatric Autoinflammatory Disease".
**Authors:** Dwivedi P, Deshpande K, Dhabale T, Kapse A, Pathak A
**Journal:** J Clin Immunol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10875-025-01928-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-025-01928-5)

## Content

Immunodeficiency with autoinflammation (OMIM#260570) is a rare genetic disorder caused by mutations in the CEBPE gene that impair neutrophil development and immune balance. An 8-year-old patient initially suspected of having AML was found to harbor a homozygous CEBPE mutation. This case highlights diagnostic challenges in rare disorders mimicking malignancies, emphasizing multidisciplinary approaches for precise diagnosis and treatment.

An 8-year-old girl, the first child of non-consanguineous parents, presented with a two-month history of intermittent fever, osteomyelitis of the right proximal humerus, and multiple bony lesions. Initial treatment at an external hospital for suspected tubercular osteomyelitis failed to improve her condition. Upon referral, her white blood cell count was observed to be significantly elevated, prompting further investigations for potential hematologic malignancy.

On the basis of initial findings, the diagnosis of AML was considered.

The patient’s family history was unremarkable for similar conditions. Her younger sibling was healthy, and her mother carried a heterozygous CEBPE mutation (c.631 C > T; p.Arg211Trp). Clinical exome sequencing revealed that the patient had a homozygous CEBPE mutation.

Despite initial improvement, inconclusive reports regarding AML and the persistence of abnormal cells prompted a reevaluation of the diagnosis.

As the patient’s symptoms persisted, concerns about an underlying immune disorder arose. Elevated CRP levels, normal procalcitonin levels, and persistent leukocytosis suggested an immune-mediated process rather than active infection.

A trial of corticosteroids (prednisolone) yielded temporary symptom relief, but fever recurred upon tapering. Persistent high-grade fever, negative blood cultures, and increased inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and TNF-α) further supported the diagnosis of an autoinflammatory condition. Peripheral blood was sent for clinical exome sequencing. A homozygous missense variant in exon 2 of the CEBPE gene (chr14:g.23117702G > A; depth: 223x) that results in the amino acid substitution of tryptophan for arginine at codon 211 (p.Arg211Trp; ENST00000206513.6) was detected.

Treatment with the JAK inhibitor baricitinib resulted in significant clinical improvement, with normalization of cytokine levels over the following months.

This case highlights the complex nature of immunodeficiency with autoinflammation (OMIM#260570), particularly when it presents with symptoms similar to AML. In view of the deranged neutrophil function and the molecular findings of a CEBPE mutation, a diagnosis of an autoinflammatory condition was made [1]. The homozygous mutation in the CEBPE gene (p.Arg211Trp) (supplement-1) seems to be central to the patient’s immune dysfunction, causing recurrent infections and systemic autoinflammatory symptoms.

The CEBPE gene encodes a transcription factor involved in the differentiation and function of myeloid and lymphoid lineage cells, particularly those of late myeloid lineages. Previous cases, including those reported by Helka Goos et al. [2]underlined the association between homozygous CEBPE mutations and the emergence of immune deficiency and autoinflammation, underscoring the genetic and clinical parallels in this case.

Their report described a novel autoinflammatory disease that has defective neutrophil function due to a homozygous Arg219His mutation in the transcription factor CEBPε. CEBPε acts as a regulator of both the inflammasome and interferon, and the Arg219His mutation causes monogenic anamorphic and noncanonical inflammasomopathy/immunodeficiency.

Moreover, this condition may resemble CEBPE-Mutant Specific Granule Deficiency (SGD), as observed in two cases documented by Nina K. Sarwas et al. [3]where patients exhibited heterozygous mutations in the CEBPE gene. These mutations resulted in an amino acid substitution (p.Val218Ala) and had a dominant effect. In contrast, our patient harbored a homozygous missense variant in exon 2 of the CEBPE gene, leading to a different substitution (p.Arg211Trp). This particular mutation suggests an autosomal recessive variant of SGD, which has been described in previous studies by Adrian F. Gombart [4]who established a link between CEBPE mutations and SGD.

The initial presentation of abnormal monocytes and promonocytes via baseline flow cytometry led to the suspicion of AML with monocytic differentiation, as 90% of the cells displayed abnormal expression patterns. Following treatment with cytarabine, venetoclax, and azacitidine, residual abnormal cells with the same antigenic profile persisted, suggesting minimal residual disease. However, next-generation sequencing (NGS) of an AML panel was negative for AML-associated abnormalities.

Further complicating the clinical picture, after several months of anti-inflammatory therapy with baricitinib, a repeat bone marrow examination revealed no abnormal cells. However, granulocyte maturation was markedly restricted, with cells appearing to correspond to the promyelocyte stage (supplement-2). Nina K. Serwas et al. [3] reported that polymorphonuclear cells in SGD patients expressed more CD14 and CD64 than did healthy controls. This observation suggests that what was initially thought to be abnormal monocytes could have been immature granulocytes, incorrectly identified as promonocytes owing to their atypical marker expression. Additionally, Halene et al. [5] reported that CEBPE deficiency leads to incomplete differentiation of granulocytes, as well as impaired hematopoietic function in the bone marrow, leading to an intermediate type of cell with characteristics of monocytes and granulocytes. On the basis of these findings, we concluded that the leukocytosis in this patient was likely due to infection and the inflammatory response rather than acute leukemia, with the abnormal cells being immature granulocytes rather than promonocytes, as initially suspected.

The effectiveness of JAK inhibitors (baricitinib) in treating autoinflammatory diseases has been demonstrated in a few studies.

This case illustrates the challenges in distinguishing between overlapping clinical features of immunodeficiency, autoinflammation, and hematologic malignancies, emphasizing the need for a multidisciplinary and personalized approach to diagnosis and treatment. The clinical and laboratory findings initially led to a diagnosis of AML, suggesting the need for AML directed therapy. However, the absence of AML-specific genetic mutations, coupled with evolving clinical features and the identification of a homozygous CEBPE mutation, clarified the diagnosis of an autoinflammatory syndrome.

This case contributes to the limited literature on CEBPE-related immunodeficiency and highlights the need for heightened awareness of such rare conditions in the differential diagnosis of hematologic abnormalities.
