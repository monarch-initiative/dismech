Major depressive disorder (MDD) is associated with reduced hippocampal volume, altered connectivity and negative memory bias, suggesting disrupted hippocampal plasticity. Dysregulated adult hippocampal neurogenesis is a potential contributor, but its relevance in humans and role in MDD remain unclear. Here we investigated the molecular basis of hippocampal dysfunction in nonmedicated individuals with MDD by integrating analyses of neurogenic trajectories, cell-type- and subfield-specific gene expression, chromatin accessibility and protein expression. We identify a neurogenic lineage in the adult human hippocampal subgranular zone and provide evidence for a stalled neurogenic process in MDD, associated with transcriptional regulation, stress-related reprogramming and interferon signaling across developmental stages. Excitatory and inhibitory neurons show dysregulation of transcription factor networks affecting cell states. Cellular stress, excitatory–inhibitory imbalance, impaired synaptic plasticity, reduced metabolic capacity and immune activation, underlie impaired neurogenesis and reduced hippocampus circuit plasticity. Findings indicate genetic and epigenetic regulation of gene expression in MDD, and overlapping pathogenetic mechanisms with autoimmune, neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative diseases. This work provides a new understanding of the pathogenesis of hippocampus-dependent cognitive symptoms in MDD and suggests potential therapeutic targets.

MDD is a major global health burden presenting with mood, neurovegetative, and cognitive symptoms, including memory recall bias toward negative content, a cognitive marker of depression severity1. Pattern separation is dependent on adult hippocampal neurogenesis (AHN) in rodents and was shown to become transiently deficient in patients undergoing focal hippocampus irradiation (likely depleting AHN) to treat benign cavernous meningioma2. Despite increasing evidence that AHN might occur in human adult brain3,4, its clinical relevance remains debated and a molecular atlas of hippocampus circuit cell functional states in MDD is missing5.

To resolve neurogenic trajectories, hippocampal cell states and circuit function in MDD, we integrated single-nucleus transcriptomic and chromatin accessibility sequencing, spatial transcriptomics and regional proteomics in nonmedicated individuals with MDD and neurotypical nonpsychiatric controls (CTRLs; Fig. 1a, Extended Data Table 1 and Methods). This multimodal characterization of the largest human hippocampus dataset (Extended Data Table 2) identified an adult human hippocampal neurogenic lineage and evidence of stalled neurogenesis in MDD. The hippocampal trisynaptic memory circuit, involving dentate gyrus (DG) and cornu ammonis (CA) regions, showed disrupted excitatory–inhibitory balance, synaptic plasticity and neurotransmission driven by altered transcriptional and epigenetic regulation, while all cell types exhibited dysregulated long noncoding RNAs, cellular stress, impaired intracellular trafficking, serotonergic and glutamatergic dysfunction, and neuroinflammation.

Using single-nucleus RNA and assay for transposase-accessible chromatin (ATAC) sequencing (snMultiome-seq, 10x Genomics), we profiled ~500,000 nuclei from the DG and CA regions of MDD and CTRL hippocampi (Extended Data Fig. 1a–v). We identified 31 cell clusters, including granule cells (GCs), GABAergic neurons (InNs), excitatory neurons (ExNs) and astrocytes (Astro; Fig. 1b–f and Extended Data Fig. 2a,b).

Cell clusters were anatomically mapped on the hippocampus subfields by integrating snMultiome data with Visium and single-cell Xenium spatial transcriptomics (10x Genomics; Fig. 1g–j) performed on adjacent tissue sections. Two excitatory neuron subtypes localized to CA fields and five to the subiculum (Fig. 1g–h). Astro1 mapped onto the DG molecular layer and CA regions, whereas Astro2 was prevalent on neurogenic subventricular and subgranular zone (SGZ) niches and CA4 (Fig. 1i). Inhibitory neuron clusters showed layer-specific distributions and included a PENK+ medium-spiny neuron-like cluster (InN5.PENK) enriched in caudate and lentiform nuclei (Fig. 1j and Extended Data Fig. 2c).

Among the five GC clusters, GC2 was enriched with an immature transcriptional profile, including BHLHE22 (ref. 6), encoding for a transcription factor (TF) required for neuron differentiation, which has a variant linked to severe depression in veterans6; POSTN7, which encodes an extracellular matrix (ECM) protein involved in cell adhesion, migration and glioma progression7; PTGFR8, encoding for an oncogene which enhances proliferation and migration8; and FST9, necessary for neurogenesis, learning, memory and hippocampus long-term potentiation9.

The Astro2 cluster that mapped on the SGZ expressed genes involved in cell proliferation (AEBP1)10, differentiation (ACKR3)11, cancer progression (ADGRG1)12, invasion and metastasis (ACTL6A13), ECM remodeling and niche organization (ADAMTS family proteinases14), and neurodevelopment via glutamate NMDA receptors and mTOR signaling (RELN)15.

Cluster identities were validated by data integration with available hippocampus datasets (Methods, Extended Data Table 2 and Extended Data Fig. 3a–m).

Given the immature features of GC2 and Astro2, the known transcriptional similarity between neural stem cells (NSCs) and astrocytes, and the discovery of putative immature GCs within GC clusters3,4, we investigated the existence of a neurogenic progeny by subsetting and re-clustering GC and Astro clusters (Methods). This revealed a putative neurogenic lineage expressing canonical and primate-specific neurogenesis markers3,4, such as quiescent NSCs enriched with SOX2 (ref. 16) and PAX6 (NSCa), and activated NSCs expressing NES16 and ASCL1 (NSCb). Intermediate neural progenitors (INPs) expressed NEUROD6, TUBB3 (ref. 17) and STMN2 (ref. 18). Neuroblasts (NBs) expressed DCX16, RELN15 and CALB2, with neuroblastoma-associated NELL1 and ST8SIA2 expressed during neuroplasticity and maturation16. Two immature GC (ImGC) clusters expressed PROX1, NEUROD2 and FABP7, one was enriched with known ImGC markers NEUROD1, TUBB3, STMN1 and STMN2 (ref. 18) (ImGC1), whereas the other (ImGC2) expressed BHLHE22 (ref. 6), POSTN7 and SOX11 (ref. 19), a marker of neuronally committed precursors involved in schizophrenia19 (Fig. 2a,b).

Further stage-specific gene enrichment analysis (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 4a) showed that NSCa highly expressed RMST (which interacts with SOX2 to regulate neurogenesis), LAMA2 and HEY2 (another BHLH6 family TF), both implicated in neurodevelopment. NSCb was enriched with ID3 and HSBP8, which are essential for cell proliferation. ImGC2 was enriched with FEZF2, which orchestrates neuronal subtype identity during development, and EPHA3, a treatment target for medulloblastoma that regulates NCAM polysialylation and is involved in neuroplasticity and neurogenesis.

Validation of the neurogenesis trajectory by rank-based signature scoring and trajectory inference showed a developmental progression from NSCs through INPs and NBs toward ImGC1, followed by two bifurcated pathways: one toward mature GCs and the other toward ImGC2 (Methods and Fig. 2c–e). A potential path from mature GCs to ImGC2 suggests GC dematuration, a phenomenon described in the marmoset20. Additionally, some pathways from ImGC1 and GCs lead to GC4, a cluster with microglia engulfment signatures, possibly reflecting apoptosis and/or neurogenesis-related pruning21.

Along pseudotime, progenitor markers decreased, whereas neuronal differentiation and maturation markers increased (Fig. 2f). Complementary trajectory inference approaches (Methods) confirmed two cell-fate possibilities: one toward mature GCs and one leading to ImGC2, which, at the latest pseudotime maintained elevated cell growth and neuroplasticity markers FST9, BHLHE22 (ref. 6) and POSTN7. Conversely, the trajectory ending with mature GC highly expressed mature neuronal markers3,4 (RBFOX3 and SYT1; Fig. 2g).

We assessed regulatory programs along the neurogenic trajectory with TF footprinting analysis from ATAC data. Stage-specific TF-binding patterns spanned NSC maintenance through neuronal differentiation (Fig. 2h).

NSCs showed increased binding activity of canonical stemness regulators SOX2, PAX6 and TLX/NR2E1 (ref. 22), a receptor that binding to oleic acid triggers neurogenesis. NSCa showed preferential binding of neurodevelopment-associated Krüppel-like factors23 (KLF4 and KLF9), whereas NSCb showed binding of BHLH neuronal TF ASCL1, notch-associated TFs that maintain an undifferentiated proliferative state (HES1, HES5 and HEY1) and immediate-early genes (FOS and JUN).

In INPs, TF binding shifted toward lineage-specification through developmental homeobox families (HOX, DLX, LHX, EMX and GBX), whereas NBs showed binding of TFs promoting neuronal differentiation (TFAP2, SP8, GLIS2 and NHLH2).

Both ImGC clusters showed increased binding of TFs increasing dendritic branching, spines and synapses (CUX1 and CUX2), with ImGC1 showing activity of differentiation-associated TFs (SMAD3 and NEUROG2), and ImGC2 enrichment with motifs for BHLHE22, with a rare variant linked to severe depression6, and ZBTB7B, a permissive cancer regulator. Finally, in GCs, TF binding converged on KLF15 (ref. 24), encoding a glucocorticoid-regulated TF involved in synaptic plasticity and neuronal differentiation.

TF regulon analysis (PySCENIC; Methods and Fig. 2i) identified dynamic TF regulon activity across cell clusters, showing SOX2 active in astrocytes and more intensely in NSCa, PAX6 active in NSCa and NSCb, and ASCL1 more active in NSCb, whereas KLF9, which regulates dendritic spines and neurogenesis, and is responsive to stress exposure23, was more active at the INP–NB transition. ELK1, which functionally cooperates with the autism risk factor CHD8 to regulate gene expression and chromatin states at MAPK/ERK target genes, was most active in ImGC1, and BHLHE22 (ref. 6), with gene variants associated with depression, was most active at the ImGC2 stage.

We further validated the putative adult neurogenic clusters by integrating their transcriptional profiles with those of a fetal hippocampus dataset (Methods and Extended Data Table 2). In the fetal hippocampus, we identified neural progenitor populations expressing ASCL1 along with proliferation markers PCNA, MCM2, MKI67 (P1), astroglia-like markers AQP4 and GFAP (P2), and INP and ImGC-like markers NEUROD1, NEUROD2 and NELL1 (P3). The adult putative neurogenic clusters co-embedded with fetal progenitors in Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) space, indicating transcriptional similarities by showing adult NBs between fetal P1 and P3 populations, adult ImGC1 and ImGC2 localized with fetal GCs, and fetal astroglia-like P2 population localized between adult NSCs and INP (Extended Data Fig. 4b–d).

Unsupervised clustering of Visium v1 and v2 identified spatial clusters (Extended Data Fig. 4e,f and Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2), including a GC layer (GCL) cluster (gcl), an SGZ-molecular layer cluster (sgz.ml), an SGZ-polymorphic layer cluster (sgz.pl) and spatial clusters associated with CA regions (ca.1, ca.2, ca.3–4, ca.1–4a and ca.1–4b), hippocampus strata (oriens, lucidum and radiatum), subiculum, axons, dendrites, choroid plexus and clusters enriched with markers for inhibitory neurons (InNs), vasculature and ECM.

We applied RNA Velocity to the Visium v1 spatial data, to query a trajectory between SGZ and GCL spatial clusters, which could reflect a migrating neurogenic progeny. From sgz.pl and sgz.ml, we found trajectories going to gcl, supported by partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) graph abstraction displaying the connectivity among these clusters, and latent time going from sgz.ml to sgz.pl, with latest time at the gcl (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 4g,h).

Neurogenic-lineage marker expression was validated in human adult hippocampus tissue. Using Xenium spatial transcriptomics, we detected NSCa (ASCL1/NES/PAX6/SOX2), NSCb (MKI67/NES/PCNA/RELN/TUBB3) and NB marker RNAs (CALB2/DCX/NELL1/RELN) in the SGZ. Conversely, ImGC2 marker RNAs (BHLHE22/FEZF2/FST/POSTN) were expressed in cells located in the GCL (Extended Data Fig. 5a,b), supporting the notion that matured GCs that have already migrated into the GCL can re-express immaturity markers20.

As previously shown16, nestin, Ki67, and doublecortin protein expression was detected in SGZ cells (Extended Data Fig. 5c–e). In comparison to its protein, doublecortin RNA was expressed by tenfold more cells, suggesting post-transcriptional regulation and reduced cell-type specificity of RNA versus protein expression.

Pseudotime analysis of the neurogenic trajectory comparing MDD and CTRL showed a shift in cell distribution in MDD marked by more NSCa and fewer NBs (Fig. 3a–c), suggesting impaired neurogenic progression rather than depletion of stem-like cells. Moreover, MDD stage-associated gene signatures were delayed in NSCs and lower in NBs (Fig. 3d and Methods).

To investigate the molecular underpinnings of halted AHN in MDD, we examined gene expression profiles across neurogenic populations in MDD and CTRL. At early neurogenic stages (NSCa, NSCb and INP) MDD had higher expression of a gene module enriched with interferon-related pathways (Methods and Fig. 3e–g) and containing genes implicated in intellectual disability (EIF2AK2)25, Alzheimer’s disease (IFIT3)26 and neuroinflammatory diseases systemic lupus erythematosus (HERC6 (ref. 27) and NRIR28) and COVID-19 (NRIR29), which can present with mood and cognitive symptoms (Fig. 3f).

Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis by cluster comparing MDD to CTRL (Fig. 3h,i) revealed eight different interferon-related genes disrupted in MDD NSCa, NSCb, INP and ImGC1, including elevated Alzheimer’s-associated SEMA6D30, which regulates microglia function, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis-linked ITM2A31. Downregulated genes in NSCa and INP in MDD had variants implicated in intellectual disability (AFF3 (ref. 32) and KCND2 (ref. 33)) and inflammatory bowel diseases (XKR6)34. Downregulated in INP, NB, ImGC1 and ImGC2 was SCART1, an immune-surveillance gene regulating interleukin (IL)-17, with pathogenetic roles in psoriatic arthritis, which is also associated with mood symptoms35.

Consistent with an enhanced stress response, heat shock proteins involved in cellular apoptosis and senescence were elevated in MDD NSCa and INP. Immediate-early genes JUN, EGR1 and FOS, which are essential for synaptic plasticity, learning, memory, neuronal response to injury, cell proliferation, differentiation, transformation and apoptosis, were upregulated in MDD NSCa and NSCb.

Neurogenesis drivers were disrupted at the intermediate stages of the trajectory in MDD, with INP showing lower expression of NELL1, POSTN7 and ADAMT14 metalloproteinases, important for niche functions and cell growth and implicated in neurodegenerative and psychiatric diseases14. INP had higher expression of SOX9 (refs. 3,4,36), which favors glial fate by promoting the neuron-to-glia switch and suppressing neurogenesis. Additionally, INP had higher progenitor cell marker ETNPPL3,4, suggesting lagging neurogenesis. Moreover, NBs, which were fewer in MDD, had reduced DCX and trophic factor BDNF, indicating deficient trophism. Catenins, involved in Wnt signaling and cell adhesion, were higher in NSCa and lower in ImGC2, ImGC1 in MDD.

Integration of single-nuclei and spatial transcriptomic data of neurogenesis trajectory cell clusters and SGZ niche clusters (sgz.ml and sgz.pl) highlighted concordant upregulated interferon and innate immune response signaling (IFITM3 (ref. 26), MX1 and COL5A3) and blocked cell growth (MT1M37 and APCDD1 (ref. 38)). Niche-associated DEGs included elevated HPCAL1 (ref. 39), involved in calcium binding, neurotransmission and Wnt pathway. Conversely, GC marker CALB1, a mature neuron marker regulating calcium homeostasis, synaptic plasticity and memory, was downregulated in multiple spatial subfields.

Few DEGs downregulated in cell and spatial clusters had genome-wide association studies (GWAS) hits for MDD5. These included oncogene SAMD5 (ref. 40), previously found altered in MDD brain tissue, synaptogenesis-linked CSMD3, calcium-dependent cell-adhesion CDH9 (ref. 41), linked to autism and Alzheimer’s disease41 and thyroid-associated TRHDE (Fig. 3j). Moreover, thyroid function-related TTR42, downregulated in NSCb, NB and ImGC2, encodes for transthyretin, which transports thyroid hormones (thyroxine) and vitamin A (retinol) through the bloodstream and cerebrospinal fluid and has mutations that result in transthyretin amyloidosis (a condition where misfolded proteins build up in the heart and nervous system). Transthyretin was reported to be decreased in MDD cerebrospinal fluid and correlating with serotonin turnover and suicidal ideation42.

Shared dysregulated pathways between MDD neurogenic cells, mature cell clusters and spatial clusters, involved serotonergic pathways, intracellular trafficking, glutamatergic signaling, apolipoproteins, long noncoding RNAs and neuroinflammation (Figs. 3h,i and 4a–c and Extended Data Fig. 7a,b).

Supporting serotonergic system involvement in MDD, INPs had lower neurogenesis-associated HTR4 and suicide-associated HTR1E43, ImGC1 had lower HTR7, which promotes neurogenesis, neurite outgrowth and dendritic spine maturation, whereas spatial subfields had lower HTR2A and HTR2C, and InN.PENK had elevated HTR1D.

Fourteen solute-carrier family (SLC) genes, suggested as biomarkers of MDD44, were downregulated across NSCb, INP, NB, ImGC1 and ImGC2, and ExN1.CA1-4.FIBCD1, whereas nine were upregulated in INPs and NBs. RAB6B45, an SLC member, encoding a protein involved with retrograde transport from the Golgi to the endoplasmic reticulum, was upregulated in InN5.PENK, while CLTCL1 (ref. 46), necessary for endocytosis and neurodevelopment, and associated with 22q11.2-linked autism46, was increased in ExN1.CA1-4.FIBCD1. Meanwhile, DISC1 (ref. 47), coordinating intracellular trafficking and microtubule dynamics, and disrupted in schizophrenia, was downregulated in GC1.

Glutamate ionotropic (AMPA and NMDA) and metabotropic receptors were downregulated in MDD NSCa, NSCb, INP, ImGC1, NB and GC1 and in several spatial clusters. Glutamatergic signaling is essential for synaptic plasticity and excitatory–inhibitory balance and has been strongly implicated in MDD48.

Among apolipoprotein genes, APOE, involved in lipid homeostasis and neuronal repair, was increased in NSCa and INP but reduced in ImGC2. In various MDD cell and spatial clusters, there were elevated APOL2 and APOD, linked to cellular senescence, and in GC1 there was upregulated APOL6, which induces mitochondria-mediated apoptosis by ferroptosis, whereas ExN1.CA1-4.FIBCD1 exhibited decreased anti-apoptotic BCL2.

Long noncoding RNAs were dysregulated in most cell clusters, including INP, NB, ImGC.1, ImGC.2, InN.PENK, GC1 and ExN1.Ca1-4.FIBCD1. Notably, LINC01515 and LINC02315, both upregulated in MDD, were found to be GWAS hits for autism49, psychiatric disorders (in the Japanese population)50 and suicide (in the Indian population)51.

Consistent with the neurogenic clusters, interferon signaling was upregulated in GC1 and ExN1.CA1-4.FIBCD1, including ISG15, MX1, CXCL12 (previously implicated in MDD neuroinflammation52) and MAT2B53, involved in blood–brain barrier damage and apoptosis. While immune surveillance by SCART1 (ref. 35) was reduced in neurogenic clusters, InN5.PENK and GC1, together with Sushi Domain Containing anti-inflammatory signaling, and SEMA6D30, which regulates microglia function in Alzheimer’s disease30, was also reduced in NSCb, INP and ExN1.CA1-4.FIBCD1 (Fig. 4b).

Unique to InN5.PENK was overexpression of proenkephalin, a precursor of enkephalins, which modulate pain perception, stress responses and emotional regulation, as well as immediate-early genes ARC, FOS, JUNB, EGRs, and JAG1, upregulated with neuronal activity and cell development. Consistently, neurodevelopment-associated genes were upregulated in InN5.PENK, including NNAT3,4, CAMKV54, microtubule-associated proteins with variants linked to microcephaly, intellectual disability and epilepsy17, CYFIP2 implicated in epileptic encephalopathy55, NPTN linked to autism56 and neurofilament heavy, medium and light chains, which are biomarkers of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and neuronal damage57. Conversely, RELN15, which regulates neuroplasticity and dendritic growth, was downregulated in InN5.PENK.

Of note, KRAS58, which is involved in long-term potentiation and hippocampus-dependent learning and memory, was upregulated in GC1 but downregulated in several spatial clusters (Fig. 4b,c), potentially reflecting nuclear versus cytoplasmic expression.

HOMER1, a postsynaptic scaffolding protein involved in synaptic plasticity, dendritic structure and bipolar disorder59, had downregulated RNA in ExN1.CA1-4.FIBCD1 (Fig. 4b).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using DEGs from both single-nucleus and spatial transcriptomics highlighted disrupted cellular development, stress and death, signaling and synaptic function, immune response, metabolism, ECM and gene expression regulation in the MDD hippocampus circuit (Methods and Fig. 4d).

To characterize gene regulatory networks, high-dimensional weighted gene coexpression network analysis (hdWGCNA) was performed (Fig. 4e–g). The top co-regulated hdWGCNA gene module associated with MDD in GC1 highlighted dysregulated translation, cell migration, axon pathfinding (ROBO) and response to cellular starvation, with central genes in the network regulating ribosome assembly and translation (for example, RPL, RPS, EIF4A2 and EEF1A1), intracellular trafficking and structural plasticity (TUBA4A, TUBB2A and ACTB) and cellular stress (heat shock proteins; Fig. 4e).

In ExN1.CA1-4.FIBCD1, top MDD-associated gene module involved NMDA receptors, dopamine and serotonin release, long-term potentiation, cell–cell junction and communication, and central genes in the coexpression network were HTR2C and GRM1 receptors, calcium voltage-gated channels (CACNA2D3) and synaptic function genes (Fig. 4f).

In InN.5.PENK, top MDD-associated module confirmed altered glutamatergic and GABAergic neurotransmission, synaptic function, long-term potentiation and cell–cell junction, with central genes in the network being GABRB1 and GRIK2 (Fig. 4g).

Integrating proteome and single-nucleus transcriptome data (Fig. 5d,e), we identified genes dysregulated at the RNA and protein level, including upregulated intracellular trafficking and Golgi-associated RAB6B45, solute-carrier family member SLC25A44, biomarker of treatment response in MDD, CLTCL1 (ref. 46), involved in 22q11.2-associated autism46, NPTN56, linked to calcium homeostasis, synaptic plasticity, neurodevelopment, neurotransmission and autism56, CAMKV54, maintaining dendritic spines54, CYFIP2 (ref. 55), controlling cytoskeletal dynamics and early cortical development, HPCAL1 (ref. 39), regulating calcium signaling, synaptic transmission, and cell differentiation, mitochondria-related PSAP and MDH1, important for oxidative phosphorylation, and MAT2B53, driving neuroinflammation through methylation.

Downregulation of ACYP2 (ref. 60) RNA and protein, involved in cellular development and differentiation, links ATP metabolism, telomere length and aging-associated cellular changes to MDD.

HOMER1 (ref. 59), with a single nucleotide polymorphism linked to antidepressant response in bipolar disorder, and regulating synaptic plasticity, had lower RNA in NSCa, ExN1.CA1-4.FIBCD1 and several spatial clusters, whereas the protein was elevated in MDD bulk hippocampus proper tissue (Figs. 4b and 6b,e), suggesting either compensatory downregulation of transcription in response to elevated protein, or cell-type specific downregulation of the RNA, while the protein was elevated across hippocampus subfields and cell types.

Of note, many of the identified differentially regulated proteins and RNAs (including GSK3β, STMN1 (ref. 18), NPTN56, CALB1, SYN3 and FABP7) had been previously found disrupted not only in MDD but also with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and addictions (PsyGeNET; Fig. 5f,g and Extended Data Fig. 7c–e), suggesting that neuroplasticity- and neurodevelopment-associated pathways are dysfunctional across psychiatric disorders.

A subset of MDD DARs overlapped with DEGs, identifying genomic locations where chromatin accessibility directly corresponds to transcriptional changes of nearby genes, suggesting functional regulatory elements. Those DEGs included downregulated SCART1 (ref. 35) (in GC1, ImGC1, ImGC2, InN5.PENK, INP and NB) responsible for immune surveillance and linked to psoriasis35.

Regulatory programs were characterized analyzing TF footprinting and binding score driving gene expression. This revealed a central role of zinc fingers, KLF15 and other stress-responsive Krüppel-like factors highly regulated by corticosteroids23 as transcription drivers in GC1, InN5.PENK and ExN1.CA1-4.FIBCD1 (Fig. 6b). Of note, stress exposure in the last 6 months before death was higher in individuals with MDD than in CTRLs (Extended Data Table 1, Source Data Fig. 1 and Methods).

In GC1, the footprinting activity of TF with motif similarity (Fig. 6c) induced higher HPCAL1 (ref. 39) (Wnt and β-catenin), KRAS58 (long-term potentiation), MAT2B53 (methylation and methylation-induced inflammation) and CYFIP2 (ref. 55) (cytoskeletal dynamics and cell development). KLF15 binding correlated with changes in gene expression of HPCAL1 (ref. 39) and KRAS58, and TF occupancy at target loci revealed the association between chromatin accessibility and footprint scores in MDD versus CTRL (Fig. 6d).

In InN.5.PENK, footprinting activity of TFs with motif similarity (Fig. 6e) increased expression of CAMKV54 (spine maintenance), RAB6B45 and SLC25A6 (ref. 44) (vesicle trafficking), CYFIP2 (ref. 55) and MDH1 (redox-balance, metabolism), NPTN56 (cell-adhesion and long-term potentiation), while reducing ACYP2 (ref. 60) (maintaining telomeres and protecting against cellular aging). There was a correlation between KLF15 binding and gene expression of CAMKV54, RAB6B45 and SLC25A6 (ref. 44) and TF-binding profiles showed the relationship between chromatin accessibility and footprint scores in MDD versus CTRL (Fig. 6f).

In ExN1.CA1-4.FIBCD1, TFs reduced HOMER1 (ref. 59) RNA, whereas the protein was increased, which is a possible compensatory response aimed at regulating glutamatergic synapse function, calcium signaling, dendritic spine growth and synaptic stability. KLF15 binding correlated negatively with gene expression of HOMER1 (ref. 59) in MDD versus CTRL, and TF-binding profiles showed a chromatin accessibility signal relationship with footprint scores in MDD versus CTRL, illustrating condition-specific TF occupancy at target loci (Fig. 6f).

This study provides a multiomic molecular atlas of the adult human hippocampus and its neurogenic niche, identifying dysregulated functional pathways affecting the stem cell progeny and the MDD trisynaptic circuit. Findings support the presence of sustained AHN in adult human brain3,4,36, and the potential for GCs to maintain long-term immature features and undergoing dematuration20. The MDD hippocampus shows altered TF activity affecting chromatin accessibility and expression of genes involved in intracellular trafficking, spines, cell adhesion, telomeres and cellular aging. Conversely, some DEGs were GWAS hits for MDD5, suggesting genetic factors. These were involved in neurodevelopment, synaptogenesis, cell-adhesion and thyroid function, whereas other DEGs had variants linked to neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental disorders.

The presence of a neurogenic cell lineage was supported by gene expression and stage-specific TF regulation, replicating recent findings4. Moreover, the existence of a stem cell progeny3,4 was supported by computational trajectory inference strategies both in single-cell and DG niche spatial clusters. Immaturity markers retained in ImGC2, and trajectory inference indicating a path re-entry from GC to ImGC2, suggested extended immature neuronal states3,36, and dematuration potential of human GCs supporting marmoset findings20. The fact that immaturity-associated genes expressed in ImGC2 were found in cell located in the GCL rather than the SGZ, further supports de-differentiation of mature GCs. In MDD, there seemed to be halted AHN, with more quiescent progenitors and fewer neuroblasts.

TFs regulated neurogenesis and the trisynaptic circuit in individuals with MDD, who had higher severity of stress exposure in the last 6 months before death compared to CTRL individuals. Notable TFs altered in MDD include the primate-specific progenitor cell marker ETNPPL3,4 and ADAMT14 metalloproteinases, which affect the niche and are disrupted in neuropsychiatric and neurodegenerative diseases14. Corticosteroid- and stress-responsive KLF23 footprinting, driving chromatin accessibility together with gene and protein expression, support the previous findings of an association between stress exposure and KLF9 RNA levels in depressed women and female mice, where KLF9 regulated dendritic spines and neuroplastiticy23.

The dysregulation of long noncoding RNAs in MDD neurogenic trajectory cells, SGZ niche spatial clusters and GCs, including some that have been linked to autism49, psychiatric disorders50 and suicide51, further implicate epigenetic mechanisms, as the expression of long noncoding RNAs is regulated by DNA methylation and histone modifications.

Conversely, neurogenic progeny DEGs that were GWAS MDD hits5, suggests that genetic mechanisms affect cell growth (SAMD5 (ref. 40)), adhesion (CDH9 (ref. 41)), synaptogenesis (CSMD3) and thyroid function (TRHDE). Another thyroid-related gene disrupted in the neurogenic trajectory has been linked to suicidal ideation and serotonergic function in MDD (TTR42), supporting a link between thyroid function and mood regulation.

As variants of calcium-dependent cell–cell adhesion molecule CDH9 were not only linked to MDD, but also to autism and Alzheimer’s disease41, shared pathogenetic mechanisms between these diseases are possible. This is further supported by the finding that interferon-related genes affecting the neurogenic progeny in MDD have variants associated with intellectual disability (EIF2AK2 (ref. 25), AFF3 (ref. 32) and KCND2 (ref. 33)), Alzheimer’s disease (IFIT3 (ref. 26) and SEMA6D30) and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ITM2A31). The other common theme of dysregulation of apolipoproteins and amyloid-related pathways across cell clusters, further supports the hypothesis that MDD is a risk factor for dementia.

Additional trans-diagnostic aspects of MDD pathogenesis are shared mechanisms with autoimmune and infectious diseases that can present with neuropsychiatric symptoms, including inflammatory bowel diseases (XKR6)34, psoriasis (SCART1)35, systemic lupus erythematosus (HERC6 (ref. 27) and NRIR28) and COVID-19 (NRIR29). These autoimmune programs were disrupted in the neurogenic cell trajectory, GCs, excitatory neurons and in medium-spiny-neuron-like GABAergic neurons, suggesting a hippocampus-wide effect, which could be the cause or a consequence of the cellular alterations observed in MDD.

Previously uncharacterized in the human hippocampus, the medium-spiny-neuron-like GABAergic population was the most dysregulated and had elevated proenkephalin production, and immediate-early genes, suggesting an activated state, possibly underlying the link between MDD, pain, stress response, excitatory–inhibitory balance and emotional regulation. InN.PENK had altered vesicle, spine and adhesion genes, supporting an activated state. At the same time, glutamate48 ionotropic (AMPA and NMDA) and metabotropic receptors were downregulated in the DG neurogenic trajectory and GCs, which could happen in response to cellular stress, as neurogenic cells had elevated immediate-early genes and heat shock proteins, and GCs had increased long-term potentiation (KRAS58) and translational regulation. Therefore, InN.PENK might be attempting to compensate for a stress-hyperactivated DG.

Disrupted intracellular trafficking in MDD was shared across cell types and driven by genes already associated with treatment response in MDD (SLC25A44), involved with 22q11.2-linked autism (CLTCL1)46 and with schizophrenia (DISC1)47, whereas tubulins17 and neurofilaments57 have been linked to neurodevelopment and neurodegeneration, suggesting shared pathogenetic pathways in these diseases. Disrupted intracellular transport may affect structural plasticity, (NPTN56, with a variant linked to autism), mitochondria function (CYFIP2 (ref. 55), involved in epileptic encephalopathy), neurotransmission and ultimately memory functions, which are altered in these diseases.

The serotonergic system role in neurogenesis and hippocampus neurotransmission in MDD, is supported by lower neurogenesis-associated HTR4 and suicide-associated HTR1E43 in INPs, lower HTR7 in ImGC1, which controls neurogenesis, neurite outgrowth and dendritic spines, lower HTR2A and HTR2C in spatial subfields, and elevated HTR1D in InN.PENK. Additionally, HTR2C was a central hub gene in the ExN1.CA1-4.FIBCD1 gene-regulatory network identified by hdWGCNA.

Altered CA excitatory neurons NMDA receptors and HOMER1, a key postsynaptic scaffolding protein regulating synaptic plasticity and glutamatergic communication, supports disruption of neuroplasticity in this important part of the hippocampal memory circuitry in MDD. Notably, a HOMER1 variant has been associated with lower MRI gray matter volume, higher BOLD functional MRI neural responses to emotional stimuli, lower white matter fractional anisotropy, increased axial diffusivity after lithium treatment, and worse antidepressant response in patients with mood disorders59.

Limitations of this study include the cross-sectional nature of postmortem studies, limited ability to separate MDD from suicide pathology because most of the individuals with MDD died by suicide, modest power to investigate adversity-related gene expression changes, and potential effects of cause of death or unrecognized medical illness.

This work provides the largest multimodal characterization of the human adult hippocampus, and for the first time identifies MDD-associated changes in cell-type-specific chromatin accessibility and gene expression, subfield-specific transcriptomic profiles and the regional proteome, in a deeply phenotyped cohort, avoiding confounders due to pharmacological treatment or drug and alcohol use. These findings provide a molecular framework for understanding the regulation of human adult human hippocampal neurogenesis and reveal disrupted neurogenic programs, which may underlie halted AHN in MDD. In the hippocampus trisynaptic circuit, altered transcriptional regulation, intracellular trafficking, excitatory–inhibitory balance, neuroplasticity, neurotransmission, cellular stress and neuroinflammation may work in concert or represent different pathogenetic mechanisms of MDD subtypes. Shared pathogenetic mechanisms are revealed between MDD and neurodevelopmental, neurodegenerative and autoimmune disorders, and some involve genes regulating thyroid function, which are conditions that can all present with neuropsychiatric symptoms. The dysregulation of proenkephalin-producing GABAergic neurons underscores the link between chronic pain, stress, reward and depression.

Collectively, these findings establish a brain-based framework for understanding MDD and provide a foundation for precision psychiatry through improved disease phenotyping, biologically informed diagnostic subtyping, biomarker discovery and the identification of new therapeutic targets.

For immunofluorescence, antibodies (anti-doublecortin guinea pig, 1:1,000 dilution, Millipore cat. no. AB2253; anti-NeuN rabbit monoclonal, Sigma-Aldrich cat. no. MABN140) were incubated in blocking solution overnight at 4 °C, and with secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-guinea pig and Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit (1:500 dilution, Jackson ImmunoResearch cat. no. 106-585-003 and 111-545-003, respectively) and counterstained with DAPI.

For both immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence primary antibodies were omitted to exclude unspecific staining16,61.

Cell numbers were estimated in the DG region of interest using unbiased stereology software (Stereo Investigator, MBF)16,61.

GEX count matrices and ATAC fragment files were generated using 10x Cell Ranger v.2.0.0 with Single Cell Multiome ATAC + GEX v1 chemistry. Ambient RNA was removed from GEX matrices using SoupX (autoEstCont and adjustCounts, default parameters). Cells were retained if they contained 500–150,000 UMIs, >300 genes and <10% mitochondrial RNA, consistent with published filtering strategies for high-sensitivity 10x datasets63 and rare neurogenic cell populations3. Low-quality cells (<300 genes and >10% mitochondrial RNA; Supplementary Fig. 1a) and doublets identified using scDblFinder were removed.

ATAC peak BED files were converted to GenomicRanges objects and merged into a unified peak set. Peaks on nonstandard chromosomes, within hg38 blacklist regions, or >10 kb or <20 bp were removed. Cells with transcription start site enrichment <1, nucleosome signal >3 or 200–100,000 ATAC reads were excluded (Supplementary Fig. 1a).

All 74 samples were merged in both modalities. Cells that passed quality control were retained for further analysis.

GEX data were processed with Seurat. After normalization (NormalizeData), the top 2,000 highly variable genes were identified (FindVariableFeatures) and, together with 93 curated neurogenesis-associated genes, scaled using ScaleData. Principal-component analysis (PCA) (RunPCA) was performed, and the top 40 principal components (PCs) were batch-corrected with Harmony (RunHarmony) using sample, donor and batch as covariates (Supplementary Fig. 1b–e).

ATAC data were processed with BPCells and Signac. BPCells enabled disk-backed storage of the peak matrix. Peaks were TF-IDF normalized, reduced by latent semantic indexing (LSI), and batch-corrected with Harmony using LSI components 2–40 and donor, sample and batch as covariates (Supplementary Fig. 1f,g).

WNN analysis was performed with FindMultiModalNeighbors using the top 30 Harmony-corrected PCs and LSI embeddings (prune.SNN = 1/20). The resulting graphs were used for UMAP and Leiden clustering (FindClusters, algorithm = 4, resolution = 0.8).

Differential gene expression across the 31 clusters was assessed by pseudobulk linear mixed-effects modeling with SpatialLIBD, including sample, batch, sex, scaled age and winsorized RIN and post-mortem interval (PMI, time from demise to brain collection, measured in hours) as fixed effects and donor as a random effect. Clusters were annotated using canonical gene expression per human hippocampus snRNA-seq studies3,4,36,65,66 (Fig. 1e and Extended Data Fig. 2a).

Data were integrated with published snRNA-seq datasets3,36,65,66 (Extended Data Table 2) using reciprocal PCA integration. External datasets were preprocessed with Seurat67 and Harmony, and cluster correspondence was assessed by Pearson correlation of average expression across the top 5,000 variable genes (Extended Data Fig. 3a–m).

Because one astrocyte cluster (Astro2) and one GC cluster (GC2) expressed immaturity-associated genes3,36,65,66, we hypothesized that GC and astrocyte populations contained neurogenic cells. We therefore subset these clusters and repeated the Seurat workflow (normalization, dimensionality reduction, Harmony integration, WNN analysis and clustering), identifying 17 unsupervised clusters. Among the 17 clusters obtained, we selected cells enriched with known early, intermediate and late neurogenesis markers3,4,36, and then applied FindSubClusters at 0.5 resolution.

We annotated cell clusters based on their enrichment with canonical neurodevelopmental and neurogenesis-associated markers and, for visualization purposes, the remaining mature GC and astrocyte clusters were merged together as mGC and mAstro, whereas GC4 was maintained separately as it expressed immune and GC markers, suggesting microglia engulfment (Fig. 2a,b). Cells were annotated as NSCa when expressing neuronal-lineage-associated ASCL1 with SOX2 (ref. 16), RMST, a long noncoding RNA regulating neurogenesis by interacting with SOX2 (ref. 16), PAX6 (ref. 68) and LAMA2 (ref. 69), encoding an ECM protein that maintains NSC niches, promotes progenitor survival and dictates the transition from stem cells, and RSPO2, essential for neurogenesis. Cell defined as NSCb expressed NES16, FABP5 and FABP7, regulators of NSC proliferation and survival, ID3 (ref. 70), which inhibits early differentiation, SFRP2, a modulator of the Wnt signaling pathway and TPPP3, encoding a microtubule-associated protein that regulates microtubule dynamics, bundling, and cell mitosis. Cells were annotated as INPs when enriched with neurogenic STMN1 and STMN2 (ref. 18), TUBB3 (ref. 17), NNAT3,4 and NEUROD6. NBs were identified by expression of classic neurogenic marker DCX16, with ST8SIA2 (ref. 16), which drives polysialylation, CALB2 (refs. 3,4), RELN15 and NELL1 found in neuroblasts and neuroblastoma cells. Two clusters expressing PROX1 were annotated as immature GCs: ImGC1, expressing INSM1, encoding for zinc-finger TF involved in embryonic neurogenesis, and NEUROD1 (refs. 3,4), necessary for neuronal differentiation, whereas ImGC2 expressed developmental genes BHLHE22 (ref. 6), FST9, OTOF71, POSTN7 and SMAD7 (ref. 24).

Clusters were validated by applying gene signature scoring with ScoreSignatures_UCell using canonical markers for NSCs, INPs, NBs and ImGCs3,4,18 as features (Fig. 2c).

To compare transcriptional profiles of the putative adult neurogenic trajectory with embryonic neurogenic cells, we integrated NSCa, NSCb, INP, NB, ImGC1 and ImGC2 data with a fetal hippocampus snRNA-seq dataset68 (in Seurat using reciprocal principal component analysis (PCA) with SCTransform (SCT) normalization and 3,000 integration features), including fetal PROX1+ GCs, AQP4+ astrocytes and three types of ASCL1+ progenitors, while OPCs were excluded (Extended Data Fig. 4b–d).

Neurogenic trajectories were inferred with Monocle72 and Palantir73, using NSCa as the root population. Pseudotime, terminal state probabilities and entropy were computed, and gene expression was denoised with MAGIC. Gene expression dynamics and modules were analyzed, and the neurogenic subtrajectory was isolated for downstream analyses. For visualization and analysis, neurogenic populations were randomly downsampled to 10,000 cells each. Trajectory-dependent differential gene expression was assessed with Monocle3 using the model pseudotime × diagnosis.

TF regulation along the neurogenic trajectory was analyzed using TOBIAS74 and pySCENIC (Fig. 2h). Neurogenic ATAC peaks were called with Signac75 CallPeaks from MACS3, corrected for Tn5 bias, footprinted and analyzed with BINDetect to infer TF-binding dynamics using JASPAR76 motifs, excluding ENCODE blacklist regions. Peaks were annotated with UROPA, and TF activity visualized using motif binding z-scores. Gene regulatory networks were inferred from GEX data with PySCENIC72 (GRNBoost2; Fig. 2i), pruned, and repeated 50 times to retain robust regulons (>20% of runs; Fig. 2i). AUCell scores were calculated for each cluster, and regulon activity was compared to TF-binding dynamics to identify stage-specific regulatory programs.

Visium v1 and v2 data were loaded into Seurat (Load10X_Spatial), normalized, variable features identified and data scaled using standard Seurat workflows. Replicates were merged and PCA, UMAP, neighbor graph construction and clustering were performed using the top 30 PCs; spots with fewer than 500 UMIs were excluded from downstream analyses.

Unsupervised spatial clusters were identified with Seurat (FindClusters, resolution of 0.34) and annotated by marker gene expression and hippocampal anatomy. Low-abundance clusters were removed, yielding 13 clusters in Visium v1 and 17 in Visium v2 (Extended Data Fig. 4e,f and Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3), each represented across all samples. Shared clusters included the granule cell layer, SGZ (with molecular and polymorphic layers), choroid plexus, inhibitory neurons, axons, dendrites and vasculature. Visium v1 contained a combined stratum lucidum/radiatum cluster, whereas v2 resolved these into separate lucidum and radiatum clusters and identified a distinct CA2 cluster.

Differential gene enrichment analysis was performed on each cluster by pseudobulked linear mixed-effects modeling, including sample, batch, sex, scaled age and winsorized RIN and PMI as fixed effects, and donor as a random effect, implementing the SpatialLIBD package77.

To determine how snMultiome-seq cell clusters anatomically map onto hippocampus subfields, anchor-based integration using snMultiome-seq as reference and Visium v1 and v2 and Xenium as query datasets was performed by FindTransferAnchors and TransferData in Seurat67, assigning spatial spots probability scores based on transcriptional congruence (Fig. 1g–j).

To probe the existence of a trajectory in the DG neurogenic niche, gene expression dynamics across hippocampus spatial clusters were analyzed, focusing on sgz.pl, sgz.ml and gcl, which expressed proliferation, differentiation, maturation and neurogenesis markers among their top 100 (Extended Data Fig. 4e,f).

RNA velocity was computed on Visium v1 (55 × 55μm spots, poly(A)-based) using velocyto78 and scVelo following BAM sorting with SAMtools. Velocity was estimated using the dynamical model after PCA on the top 5,000 variable genes plus 55 neurogenesis-associated genes and construction of a 30-nearest-neighbor graph, PAGA was used to infer connectivity and branching between spatial spots, and latent time to estimate where each spots lay between the beginning and end of a trajectory (Extended Data Fig. 4g,h).

Pairwise comparisons were performed using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for age, PMI, brain pH, RIN and snMultiome RNA/ATAC quality metrics and chi-squared tests were used to compare sex distribution between MDD (n = 55) and CTRL (n = 68) participants (Extended Data Fig. 1c–l). Resulting P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure and an FDR-adjusted P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Linear regression analyses were performed within diagnostic groups to assess relationships between RIN and age, PMI and brain pH in the full cohort with available RIN values (n = 76 participants), and in cohort selected for the omics studies (n = 38 participants and 74 samples; after removing 3 samples after quality control; Extended Data Fig. 1m–v and Supplementary Table 1) with a P value < 0.05 considered as statistically significant.

Differential gene expression and chromatin accessibility between MDD and CTRL were analyzed on cluster-level pseudobulk data using limma. Count and peak matrices were aggregated by biological sample and cluster without merging samples from the same donor processed in different batches. Pseudobulks with fewer than five cells or spots were excluded. Models included cell/spot number, winsorized RIN and PMI, age, sex, sample and batch as fixed effects, with within-donor correlation estimated using duplicateCorrelation. Lowly expressed genes were filtered with filterByExpr (edgeR), normalized using TMM (calcNormFactors), and analyzed with voomWithQualityWeights, lmFit and eBayes to account for variable sample quality and repeated donor measurements. For NSCs, INPs and NBs, DEG significance was defined as P < 0.05 log2FC > 1 to reduce type II error due to low cell numbers (Fig. 3h); for visualization only, these findings were shown in a single volcano plot. For ImGC1, ImGC2 (Fig. 3i), all other snMultiome clusters (Fig. 4a), and all Visium v1/v2 clusters (Extended Data Fig. 7a,b), DEGs were defined as FDR < 0.05 and log2FC ≥ 0.5.

Proteomic data were analyzed with the Rosetta Elucidator Protein Expression Analysis System, and peptide identifications were assigned with Mascot against the UniProt/SwissProt human canonical protein database with isoforms. DEPs were defined as those with >2 peptides and P < 0.05. Duplicate protein accessions were collapsed before Reactome pathway enrichment using ReactomePA and clusterProfiler (Benjamini–Hochberg correction; minGSSize = 10, maxGSSize = 500), and related pathways were grouped into parent categories (Fig. 5a,b).

Following differential chromatin accessibility analysis, TF-binding activity between MDD and CTRL was inferred across all snMultiome cell clusters using TOBIAS74 (Fig. 6b). Cluster-specific peaks were called with MACS375 via Signac75, corrected for Tn5 bias (ATACorrect), footprinted (ScoreBigWig), and analyzed with BINDetect to identify differential TF binding between MDD and CTRL (Fig. 6d,f,h). ENCODE blacklist regions were excluded, peaks were annotated with UROPA, and vertebrate TF motifs were obtained from JASPAR76. BINDetect results were filtered to TF motifs with differential binding (P ≤ 0.01) and only predicted target DEGs retained in the corresponding cluster and overlapped with DEPs (Fig. 6c,e,g). These TF–target pairs were used to construct regulatory networks linking TF binding, gene expression and protein expression, with edge weights representing log2 differential binding scores and gene nodes colored by DEG logFC (Fig. 6c,e,g). KLF15 binding profiles at target loci were visualized using PyBigWig and Matplotlib (Fig. 6d,f,h).

Differences in neurogenic cell proportions between MDD and CTRL were tested using β-binomial generalized linear mixed models (glmmTMB) in each neurogenic cluster using the same covariates as for the differential gene expression and accessible chromatin analyses (Fig. 3c).

Duplex RNAscope, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence data were compared between MDD and controls using Welch’s t-test in Prism (Extended Data Fig. 6d,e).

For Multiplex RNAscope, linear models in R (using the same covariates as for the differential gene expression and accessible chromatin analyses) tested the effect of MDD on the abundance of GCL and SGZ cells coexpressing PROX1 with POSTN or BHLHE22, or all three markers (Extended Data Fig. 6g,h).

The hdWGCNA79 was performed in Seurat67 on all unsupervised clusters (Fig. 4e–g). After normalization, scaling and Harmony batch correction, metacells were generated within each cluster-sample group (≥100 cells; 20 Harmony dimensions, k = 25). Signed coexpression networks were constructed, gene modules were identified and module eigengene connectivity (kME) was calculated. Reactome pathway enrichment was assessed using Enrichr via the hdWGCNA pipeline. Module–diagnosis associations were tested with linear mixed-effects models, including diagnosis as a fixed effect, sample and batch as random effects, and age, PMI and RIN as covariates, with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction.

Pre-ranked GSEA was performed with clusterProfiler using genes ranked by logFC × −log10 (Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P value). Differentially regulated KEGG and Reactome pathways were identified (minGSSize = 10, maxGSSize = 400, P < 0.05, including Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction) and related pathways were collapsed into parent categories.

Neuropsychiatric disease enrichment was assessed using psygenet2r (PsyGeNET80; database = ‘ALL’). Cell cluster significant DEGs (Extended Data Fig. 7c,d) and DEPs (Fig. 6f,g) were analyzed with psygenetGene to identify associated psychiatric disorders.
