Hypothalamic obesity (HO) is a rare and complex disorder that confers
substantial morbidity and excess mortality. HO is a unique subtype of obesity
characterized by impairment in the key brain pathways that regulate energy
intake and expenditure, autonomic nervous system function, and peripheral
hormonal signalling. HO often occurs in the context of hypothalamic syndrome, a
constellation of symptoms that follow from disruption of hypothalamic functions,
for example, temperature regulation, sleep-wake circadian control, and energy
balance. Genetic forms of HO, including the monogenic obesity syndromes, often
impact central leptin-melanocortin pathways. Acquired forms of HO occur as a
result of tumours impacting the hypothalamus, such as craniopharyngioma, surgery
or radiation to treat those tumours, or other forms of hypothalamic damage, such
as brain injury impacting the region. Risk for severe obesity following
hypothalamic injury is increased with larger extent of hypothalamic damage or
lesions that contain the medial and posterior hypothalamic nuclei that support
melanocortin signalling pathways. Structural damage in these hypothalamic nuclei
often leads to hyperphagia, central insulin and leptin resistance, decreased
sympathetic activity, low energy expenditure, and increased energy storage in
adipose tissue, the collective effect of which is rapid weight gain. Individuals
with hyperphagia are perpetually hungry. They do not experience fullness at the
end of a meal, nor do they feel satiated after meals, leading them to consume
larger and more frequent meals. To date, most efforts to treat HO have been
disappointing and met with limited, if any, long-term success. However, new
treatments based on the distinct pathophysiology of disturbed energy homeostasis
in acquired HO may hold promise for the future.

The hypothalamus contains key brain areas that govern homeostatic body weight
regulation. Nuclei are located in the anterior (paraventricular nucleus [PVN]),
middle (arcuate nucleus [ARC] and ventromedial nucleus [VMN]) and posterior
(dorsomedial nucleus and dorsal hypothalamic area) hypothalamic regions. The ARC
contains two well-studied neuronal populations.1 One expresses proopiomelanocortin, a precursor of the
hormone α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH) that inhibits food
intake and stimulates energy expenditure. The other co-expresses agouti-related
peptide and neuropeptide-Y, both of which promote food intake and decrease energy
expenditure. An overview of central hunger-stimulating and inhibiting regulators is
provided in Table 1.

Together, these neuronal populations form part of the melanocortin (MC)
pathway, which plays a central role in the regulation of energy
homeostasis.2 Activation of
MC signalling reduces food intake, increases energy expenditure, and regulates
secretion of gut hormones such as the potent anorexigenic hormone peptide YY
(PYY3-36) via enteroendocrine cells.3 One critical MC receptor, melanocortin 4
receptor (MC4R), is expressed in the PVN of the hypothalamus, the dorsal motor
nucleus of the hindbrain, the preganglionic cholinergic sympathetic neurons of the
spinal cord and the vagal afferent nerves.4 Administration of pharmaceutical MC4R agonists causes weight
loss by both suppressing energy intake and stimulating energy expenditure, whereas
genetic or pharmacological inhibition of MC4R produces hyperphagia and
obesity.5 While most of the
effects of α-MSH, a neuropeptide in the MC family, are mediated by the brain,
α-MSH is also released into the circulation from the pituitary
gland,6-8 and can act on peripheral tissues, such as
the enteric nervous system,9 and
brown adipose tissue.10,11 Systemic α-MSH increases muscle
thermogenesis and glucose clearance by increasing muscle glucose uptake.3,8 Additional evidence suggests that the MC pathway is important in
the regulation of glucose metabolism beyond its effects on energy balance.8,12

Neurons in the ARC express leptin receptors, as do those in the VMN and
PVN.11 ARC neurons thus
sense nutrient availability and project to several brain areas that regulate
systemic metabolism, including the PVN, lateral hypothalamus, and dorsal
hypothalamic nuclei.13 The PVN, in
turn, contains a variety of appetite-suppressing peptides, such as
corticotropin-releasing factor, thyrotropin-releasing hormone, and oxytocin (OXT),
that promote negative energy balance. Appetite-suppressing peptides in the PVN
convey input from the ARC to other key brain areas such as the appetite-related
sites in the hindbrain (nucleus of the solitary tract, area postrema). Like the ARC
of the medial hypothalamus, sites in the hindbrain also receive metabolic signals
from the periphery such as satiety-related gut hormones.14,15 An
overview of major hunger-stimulating and inhibiting peripheral peptides and
metabolites is provided in Table 2. In
addition, medial hypothalamic areas contain key nuclei for locomotion and
thermoregulation, mediating leptin-induced sympathetic activation of brown adipose
tissue and energy expenditure.16
Like MC4R ligands, OXT is an anorexic downstream target of leptin action. Leptin
activates OXT through a MC4R-dependent mechanism; in the setting of leptin
resistance, downstream release of OXT is impaired.17-19 As
reviewed before,20 central OXT is
produced mainly in the parvocellular neurons of the PVN and the magnocellular
neurons of the PVN and supraoptic nucleus. Hypothalamic OXT exerts effects within
the central nervous system (including cortical areas, the VMN in the hypothalamus,
the ventral tegmental area in the midbrain, the nucleus tractus solitarius in the
brainstem, and the spinal cord) and is also released into the periphery. Centrally,
OXT-producing neurons respond to peripheral nutrient signals by decreasing
homeostatic and reward-driven eating, and increasing sympathetic nervous system
activity and associated energy expenditure. In the periphery, centrally derived OXT
leads to decreased gastric emptying, muscle and bone anabolism, and lipolysis. In
addition, OXT produced by the peripheral nervous system also regulates
lipolysis.21

The hypothalamus is also linked through direct synaptic connections to limbic
systems that mediate the motivation to eat, the decision to eat, and anticipation of
food-related (and other) rewards.22,23 Limbic and reward
circuitry are densely innervated by neurocircuits originating in the hypothalamus
and are directly responsive to ghrelin, leptin and insulin.24

In summary, specific populations of nuclei in the hypothalamus integrate
metabolic signals from the other brain regions and the periphery, and in turn,
facilitate the coordinated response to energy status, modulating various eating
behaviours and means of energy expenditure. The most compelling evidence of how
vital the hypothalamus is for metabolic homeostasis comes from our clinical
experience of what happens when hypothalamic function is disrupted, as occurs with
brain tumours or other injury in this region.

While genetic forms of hypothalamic obesity (HO), including the monogenic
obesity syndromes, often impact central leptin–MC pathways, acquired
forms of HO occur as a result of tumours impacting the hypothalamus, such as
craniopharyngioma, surgery or radiation to treat those tumours, or other forms
of hypothalamic damage, such as brain injury impacting the region. The present
review focuses on the epidemiology, natural history, and treatment of acquired
HO.

In one registry study in the United States, a variety of aetiologies
causing HO were reported. Eighty-six percent of patients reported having
developed HO as a result of a brain tumour or tumour treated by surgery, with or
without associated cranial radiation. Among those with tumours, 72% had had a
craniopharyngioma; other tumour types included astrocytoma (7%) and pituitary
macroadenoma (7%).25
Craniopharyngiomas are histologically benign (World Health Organization Grade
I), and are the most common suprasellar tumours of nonglial origin in
children.26 There are
two peak age ranges for craniopharyngioma diagnosis, one in adolescence
(10–19 years) and another in older adults (65–74 years), with
30%–50% of all craniopharyngioma cases occurring in childhood.27,28 The US data show that 5.4% of all childhood (ages
0–19 years) brain and central nervous system tumours from 2006 to 2010
were craniopharyngiomas.29

While advances in modern treatments mean that most individuals with
intrasellar or suprasellar tumours have excellent likelihood of survival,
quality of life is substantially adversely impacted by comorbidities, especially
psychosocial and metabolic.30-32 Despite optimal endocrine
management of multiple pituitary hormone deficiencies, severe obesity occurs in
50% of craniopharyngioma survivors33,34 and is a
major risk factor for craniopharyngioma-related morbidity and
mortality.35
Furthermore, metabolic derangements, such as type 2 diabetes and
metabolic-associated fatty liver disease, are common complications of HO in
craniopharyngioma survivors.36,37

Disruption of feeding circuits by damage particularly to medial
hypothalamic nuclei due to tumour, surgery or irradiation, leads to unopposed
activation of appetite-stimulating orexigens in the lateral hypothalamus and
deficient activation of appetite-inhibiting anorexigenic leptin/MC/OXT pathways
of the medial hypothalamus (Table 1), the
net effect of which is excess food intake. Damage to the medial hypothalamus can
also lead to disruption of vagal tone, resulting in excess stimulation of
pancreatic β cells, hyperinsulinaemia, and obesity.38 Sympathetic nervous output might also be
reduced, leading to both decreased resting energy expenditure and less voluntary
physical activity.25,39,40 Individuals with hypothalamic injury have other signs of
autonomic nervous system disruption. One way that the hypothalamus impacts
energy balance is by regulating the autonomic nervous system activity.
Specifically, the hypothalamus affects the balance between the two components of
the autonomic nervous system, the parasympathetic and sympathetic nervous
systems. Reduced heart rate variability reflects an imbalance between
parasympathetic and sympathetic nervous system effects on the heart, and has
been observed in individuals with hypothalamic damage, including from
craniopharyngioma.41
As the hypothalamic damage in HO frequently includes the ARC, which is the main
hypothalamic binding site for hormones from the periphery (Table 2), the hypothalamus might be rendered unable
to respond normally to adiposity and satiety signals such as insulin, leptin and
gut hormones, including PYY and ghrelin.42,43

In recent studies, individuals with craniopharyngioma and HO had
significantly lower serum α-MSH levels,44,45 higher baseline and post-meal insulin levels, and higher
circulating leptin levels as compared to individuals with a similar degree of
obesity but without hypothalamic tumours.46 Those with HO also had dampened post-meal gut hormone
excursions,47-51 consistent with altered
secretion of satiety-regulating hormones. Reduced serum α-MSH are
suggestive of deficient MC pathway activation, which might explain lower energy
expenditure in peripheral tissues via reduced fat and muscle fatty acid
oxidation.44,45,52 All of these findings underscore the concept of disturbed
satiety signalling in individuals with HO, and that effective leptin resistance
and a deficiency of downstream mediators of leptin signalling are key features
of HO pathogenesis.

In clinical practice, extensive anatomical damage to the hypothalamus,
whether related to the tumour itself or to surgical resection, is a now
well-established biomarker associated with future excessive weight
gain53 and lower
overall survival.54 Rapid
postoperative weight gain is also a predictor for severe long-term
obesity.53,55,56 While some individuals report hyperphagia, some recent
studies have actually reported an overall decrease in energy intake in patients
with HO compared with controls.40,57,58 As with any study of nutrient intake,
self-report in these investigations may be inaccurate.

The most substantial weight gain in HO typically occurs during the first
12 months after surgery affecting the hypothalamus.53 In several studies using structural
neuroimaging, large structural defects affecting several medial hypothalamic
nuclei were associated with the highest risk of obesity and often cause
treatment-resistant uncontrolled appetite and rapid weight gain.47,53,56,59-65 Assessment of hypothalamic damage, whether pre- or
post-surgery, can help to predict risk for development of HO.53,66,67 Compared with
pre-surgical scoring, post-surgery lesion scoring was more closely associated
with obesity outcomes.

There is strong evidence that hypothalamus-sparing surgery, potentially
in combination with radiotherapy, decreases the occurrence of severe obesity
without increasing local recurrence rate.61,63 However,
development of severe obesity following craniopharyngioma is still a major
problem, requiring identification of risk factors for obesity development which
would permit initiation of early interventions.

In summary, HO is a complex and rare disorder that is characterized by
hyperphagia and excessive weight gain caused by disruptions in the hypothalamic
regulation of metabolism. Especially once HO is established, individuals with
this condition have considerable difficulty with weight loss.

Physical activity is a critical component of any comprehensive weight
management programme, including for HO. In diet-induced obesity, physical
activity interventions are less likely to promote substantial weight loss, and
instead play a role in weight loss maintenance and prevention of obesity-related
comorbidities. Unfortunately, children with the hypothalamic and pituitary
tumours that predispose to HO often have compromised fitness and muscle
strength. For example, over half of children with craniopharyngioma had impaired
knee extension strength at diagnosis, and the proportion with this deficit
worsened over time.68 As
reviewed previously,69 there
have been only a few interventional trials testing ‘lifestyle’
interventions, mostly involving counselling related to nutrition and exercise.
The results of these typically small studies demonstrate need for other
strategies to address recalcitrant weight gain in HO. Low levels of voluntary
physical activity50 are
prevalent in individuals with HO, but highly effective interventions have not
yet been developed. Nutritional interventions are mostly limited to case reports
and observational studies, again with insufficient evidence regarding efficacy
for weight loss, weight loss maintenance, or comorbidity prevention. In a recent
survey, individuals with hypothalamic dysfunction have identified help with
exercise and diet as unmet needs and relevant priority for future
research.70

Acquired HO, including from hypothalamic/pituitary brain tumours, has
historically been both uniquely refractory to usual treatment approaches and
also particularly problematic in producing severe atherosclerotic cardiovascular
disease, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome.71-73 The goal of most weight loss interventions is to mitigate
the morbidity and mortality of adiposity-based chronic diseases
(ABCDs).74 For
example, in individuals with type 2 diabetes, weight loss of >5% or more
yields improvements in glycated haemoglobin, blood pressure,
metabolic-associated fatty liver disease and lipids.75 However, at least 10% weight loss is
required to achieve therapeutic goals for some ABCDs.76-78 Unfortunately, to date, most trials in individuals with HO
have not achieved these clinically relevant efficacy targets, and there is
heterogeneity in responsiveness. Factors contributing to treatment non-response
are not well understood. In addition, previous trials have been limited by too
few participants to meaningfully discern efficacy,79 inconsistent reporting of side effects
or more substantive side effects,80,81 and/or only
moderate clinical efficacy.80-87 Most studies have focused on
pharmacological interventions because nutrition and exercise changes alone have
rarely been adequate to produce meaningful weight loss in HO (reviewed in van
Iersel et al.69). However,
studies of how best to incorporate changes in eating and activity will be
critical for other health benefits, including preventing comorbidities,
maintaining muscle and bone integrity during pharmacological interventions, and
sustaining long-term weight loss. Finally, metabolic surgery is also a potential
option for HO; detailed discussion is beyond the scope of this review, but
accumulating experience suggests reasonable efficacy and safety in centres with
expertise.88-90 An overview of the different

Historically, treatment of hyperphagia and HO with the central nervous
stimulant dextroamphetamine is reported to result in stabilization of weight
gain and may improve body mass index (BMI) and hunger.85,86,91 A pilot study
of caffeine and ephedrine given to three individuals with HO resulted in a mean
weight loss of 14%, with two sustaining weight loss for at least 2 years;
however, long-term safety, particularly in the paediatric population, is
unknown.79 As
individuals with HO typically also have multiple pituitary hormone deficiencies
including central hypothyroidism, practitioners often target free thyroxine
(FT4) levels in the upper part of the reference range when using
levothyroxine.92
Tri-iodothyronine (T3) treatment aiming for supraphysiological T3 levels has
been considered by different groups.93,94 Van Santen et
al.95 assessed whether
switching treatment for central hypothyroidism to tri-iodothyronine (T3)
monotherapy in one individual may be beneficial in increasing energy expenditure
(thyroid hormone-induced thermogenesis). In this individual, T3 monotherapy did
not produce a change in BMI, nor was there a change in energy expenditure as
assessed by sympathetic activity and brown adipose tissue thermogenesis.

Sibutramine, a centrally stimulating serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake
inhibitor, showed some promise in HO but was withdrawn from the market in 2010
due to increased cardiovascular risk.84,96 Similarly,
beloranib, an inhibitor of the enzyme methionine amino peptidase 2 (MetAP2)
showed efficacy in HO, likely attributable to inhibition of food intake and
stimulation of fatty acid oxidation.97,98 However,
beloranib was withdrawn from clinical trials due to potential increases in
thromboembolic events.97,98

Octreotide, a somatostatin analogue, was reported to reduce both
hyperinsulinaemia and BMI in a double-blind placebo-controlled study in HO;
however, octreotide produced weight stabilization only in individuals with
profound hyperinsulinaemia, and its use was limited by significant side
effects.81 Another
attempt to treat hyperinsulinaemia used combined diazoxide-metformin therapy.
Treatment was associated with reduced weight gain, but significant side effects
limited enthusiasm for this agent after the initial pilot study.80

The rationale for using glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists
(GLP-1RAs) in HO is that they can bind to glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1)
receptors located in the hindbrain, triggering satiety signalling pathways in an
area which is still intact even in individuals with severe hypothalamic damage.
Induction of weight loss by GLP-1RAs is believed to be related to multiple
mechanisms involving the gastrointestinal tract, vagus nerve, and the brain, all
leading to increased satiety.99-101 Previous
promising results of studies in pre-clinical models102 and humans103-105 provided a rationale for a trial testing a GLP-1RA
(exenatide) in HO.87 The
exenatide trial was a randomized, multicentre, double-blind, parallel-group,
placebo-controlled Phase III clinical trial that enrolled 10- to 25-year-old
individuals with HO (ECHO, NCT02664441). Forty-two participants were randomized to weekly
GLP-1RA exenatide (n = 23) or placebo (n =
19). Over 36 weeks, BMI increased by 3.5% (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.7% to
5.9%) with placebo, compared with 1.7% (95% CI −0.1% to 3.5%) with
exenatide, a treatment difference of −1.7% (95% CI −4.1% to 0.6%;
p = 0.4), which was not statistically significant. However,
exenatide significantly decreased total body fat mass (estimated treatment
difference −3.1 ± 1.4 kg, 95% CI −5.7 to −0.4 kg;
p = 0.02) and waist circumference (p =
0.004).87 With respect
to energy balance, after 36 weeks, exenatide significantly decreased energy
intake during a buffet meal compared to placebo.106 In this study, doubly labelled water
was used to assess total energy expenditure in the context of disturbed
hypothalamic signalling and drug intervention, which is the gold standard
measurement of free-living energy expenditure.106-108 Exenatide treatment was associated with a decrease in
total energy expenditure, which was not explained by treatment group differences
in physical activity. The exenatide-related decrease in total energy expenditure
also occurred despite a treatment-related increase in lean mass and fat
mass.106 Overall,
while almost half of exenatide-treated participants had a decrease in BMI, the
other half did not respond to treatment, highlighting the need for
alternatives.

While there are no other randomized clinical trials in the literature
testing the effects of GLP-1RAs in HO, there are promising results testing newer
long-acting GLP-1RAs, such as semaglutide, in people with exogenous obesity. In
these non-HO trials, mean placebo-subtracted weight loss averaged 12.3%, and
consistently more than half of patients lost ≥15% of baseline
weight.109-111 However, there are no results from
trials testing these more efficacious GLP-1RAs in HO. Interestingly, in a most
recent case report, Sciacovelli et al.112 demonstrated a 25% reduction of body weight after 6
months of treatment with semaglutide in a patient with HO. Furthermore,
incretin-based multiple agonists, such as the dual GLP-1/glucose-dependent
insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor agonist tirzepatide, or the triple
GLP-1, GIP, and glucagon agonist retatrutide, target several weight-regulatory
pathways simultaneously, and represent additional promising agents for sustained
improvements in body weight and metabolic functions in HO. Of note, nausea,
which has occurred in up to 40%–45% of those treated, often leads to
discontinuation of treatment, and is a potential limitation of this class of
drugs.113,114

In summary, to date, the evidence is limited as to the most efficacious
interventions for individuals with HO. Partly related to logistical challenges
in conducting studies, including the rarity of the condition, and heterogeneity
among individuals, most studies for treatment of HO have consisted of small case
reports or cases series with fewer than 10 subjects. In most studies, even
positive treatment effects have been reported as only moderate reductions in
hunger and/or body weight. Much of the ongoing clinical treatment for HO is with
treatments that are either for exogenous obesity and/or off-label uses of
medications with other indications. New clinical trials, however, are underway
to investigate the use of promising antiobesity pharmacotherapies specifically
for the treatment of HO.

Oxytocin is a nine-amino-acid peptide hormone with roles in metabolism and
energy balance in addition to its well established functions regulating lactation
and parturition.20 Since OXT is
synthesized in the hypothalamic PVN and supraoptic nucleus, OXT deficiency in
individuals with HO is plausible. Since OXT also acts outside the hypothalamus,
particularly in the brainstem, pharmacological replacement of OXT has been proposed
to address features of HO, in particular hyperphagia, as well as some of the
psychosocial features of hypothalamic syndrome. In a pilot randomized,
placebo-controlled, crossover study of intranasal OXT (NCT02849743), no significant change in body weight was detected
after 8 weeks of OXT, although a modest benefit for anxiety and impulsivity was
found.115 Multiple
experimental considerations, including choice of OXT formulation, route of delivery,
and technique for OXT measurement in relevant tissues add complexity to the study of
this potential treatment.

Another agent that has undergone recent testing is tesofensine, a
centrally acting triple monoamine reuptake inhibitor, which addresses low
sympathetic tone in individuals with HO. Treatment with tesofensine inhibits the
presynaptic reuptake of dopamine, serotonin, and noradrenaline, and also
inhibits the dopamine active transporter, leading to a decrease in caloric
intake and weight loss.116
‘Tesomet’ refers to the combination of tesofensine with the
β-blocker metoprolol. The addition of the β-blocker is intended to
decrease potential adverse effects due to adrenergic stimulation. In a recent
randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase 2 trial testing Tesomet
versus placebo in 21 adults with HO, Tesomet produced significant reductions in
body weight, waist circumference and glucose levels as compared to
placebo.117 Tesomet
was overall safe and well tolerated; drug-related adverse events included mild
sleep disturbances, dry mouth, and headache. There were no significant
differences in heart rate or blood pressure between Tesomet and
placebo.117 A larger
clinical trial has not yet been performed testing Tesomet in HO, but planning
may be underway.

The MC4R is one of five human MC receptor subtypes (MC1–5R) that
share Class A G-protein-coupled receptors.118 These receptors regulate energy homeostasis,
pigmentation, as well as cardiovascular and sexual function.118 The MC4R plays a specific
role in energy balance, as reviewed in the preceding sections.

Synthetic MC receptor agonists are promising for the treatment of
genetic and surgery-induced HO affecting the MC4R pathway119 as there is clear evidence that central
MC signalling is deficient8,45,46,65,120,121 in individuals with these conditions. Setmelanotide, a
MC4R agonist, is approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for the
treatment of monogenic obesity related to proopiomelanocortin deficiency, leptin
receptor deficiency,122,123 and Bardet Biedl
syndrome.124 Most
recently, setmelanotide has also been shown to be effective in reducing hunger
and BMI in patients with HO due to hypothalamic injury (NCT04725240),125,126 and so
could represent a new treatment opportunity. Side effects included nausea,
vomiting, and hyperpigmentation, the latter of which comes from off-target
melanocortin 1-receptor binding. A large international multicentre Phase 3
clinical trial of setmelanotide in HO is currently underway (NCT05774756).

Whereas historically there have been no approved therapies with clear
efficacy in HO, as reviewed in previous sections, multiple promising pharmacological
agents are either approved or in development. Rigorous clinical trial data are
beginning to supplant case reports as the main source of evidence in this condition.
One important insight emerges, namely, that even though acquired HO is itself a
distinct subtype of obesity, each individual with HO is unique, and response to
treatment is not uniform. In future, we envision identifying tailoring variables,
clinical characteristics that modify treatment effects, such that individuals can be
prescribed the treatment(s) most likely to work. One likely tailoring variable is
the extent of hypothalamic injury. Already, in the previously mentioned study of
exenatide in HO, individuals with bilateral mamillary body damage had greater
treatment-related reductions in % body fat.127 Systematic collection of data in clinical trials should
evaluate other candidate tailoring variables, including sex, age, duration of HO,
presence/absence of hyperphagia, and/or background genetic tendency for exogenous
obesity. As is true for individuals with exogenous obesity, to maximize efficacy and
tolerability in those with HO may require combination therapies. Testing systematic
approaches for selecting combination treatments is another valuable future endeavour
in HO. Finally, as novel long-term targeted chemotherapies become more widely used
for all forms of cancer, including brain tumours, possible metabolic effects and
medication interactions of these agents in individuals with HO will also require
investigation.
