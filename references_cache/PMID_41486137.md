# A novel pathogenic mutation in TSPEAR associated with sensorineural hearing loss: a case report and review of the literature.
**Authors:** Ahmadkhani A, Taherifard E, Zoghi S, Jafari Khamirani H, Ahmadkhani M, Dastgheib SA
**Journal:** J Med Case Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13256-025-05761-7](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13256-025-05761-7)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Thrombospondin type laminin G domain and epilepsy associated repeats is a protein involved in the expression of genes associated with the Notch signaling pathway that have major roles in ectodermal differentiation and neural tissue development; variants in thrombospondin type laminin G domain and epilepsy associated repeats have been shown to be associated with a variety of clinical presentations including dysplasia in the skin, nail, sweat glands, hair, or teeth and hearing abnormalities.

CASE PRESENTATION: Herein we report a patient presenting with bilateral profound sensorineural hearing loss. The patient was a 6-year-old Iranian girl of Fars ethnicity, born to a consanguineous marriage, who had flat audiogram in pure tone audiometry obtained from both ears and absence of any response in auditory brain response. We examined the patient for any form of ectodermal dysplasia or malformation in teeth, skin, hair, and nail and they were in normal figuration. In this study, a novel homozygous pathogenic variant in thrombospondin type laminin G domain and epilepsy associated repeats was identified (NM_144991.3: c.668C > T, p. Ser223Leu) using whole exome sequencing.

CONCLUSION: Thrombospondin type laminin G domain and epilepsy associated repeats mutation presenting solely with hearing issues and lack of any ectodermal dysplasia was rare based on the review of previously reported cases. Given the high importance of these genetic disorders and the burden associated with them, the family members of these patients should pursue molecular genetic tests to identify the carriers and eliminate the risk of future occurrence of these phenotypes.

Thrombospondin type laminin G domain and epilepsy associated repeats (TSPEAR) is a protein encoded by TSPEAR (OMIM #612,920). Although the full spectrum of TSPEAR’s functions is not yet comprehensively understood, it has been demonstrated to play a role in the regulation of genes associated with the Notch signaling pathway. The Notch signaling pathway is a highly conserved cell communication system that influences cell fate determination, including proliferation, differentiation, or apoptosis, for both the proper development of various tissues and the maintenance of homeostasis in these developed tissues [1, 2]. Notch signaling operates through the interaction of ligands and Notch receptors on adjacent cells, leading to the proteolytic cleavage of this single-pass transmembrane protein into its extracellular and intracellular domains [2, 3]. The intracellular domain then moves to the nucleus, where it modulates gene expression. The relationship between TSPEAR and the Notch signaling pathway has been specifically assessed in keratinocytes and hair follicles [4]. Knockdown of TSPEAR has been shown to result in significant changes in the whole Notch-associated regulatory network, that is, the downregulation of NOTCH1 and abnormal expression of numerous genes associated with the Notch signaling pathway. These findings suggest that functional TSPEAR is crucial for proper Notch signaling, and consequently, for the appropriate development and maintenance of ectodermal and neural tissues.

Mutations in TSPEAR could, therefore, be associated with defects in neuroectodermal structures through the downregulation of the Notch pathway. These defects manifest in a variety of phenotypes with varying severities and typically follow an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern. Recent studies have provided deeper insights into the variability of these phenotypes. Additionally, it has been observed that phenotypic manifestations can differ significantly even among individuals with the same genetic mutation, suggesting variable penetrance and expressivity. The phenotypes manifested due to TSPEAR variants can be categorized into four major groups. The first group includes ectodermal dysplasia 14, hair/tooth type, with or without hypohidrosis (OMIM #618,180), which primarily affects the scalp skin and hair, leading to issues such as dysmorphic features and sparse hair growth [4, 5]. The second group encompasses deafness, autosomal recessive 98 (OMIM #614,861), where individuals experience sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL), which may range from mild to severe [6]. The third group involves selective tooth agenesis 10 (OMIM #620,173), characterized by agenesis of the permanent teeth, varying from the absence of a few teeth to complete tooth agenesis in addition to abnormally shaped teeth [7, 8]. The fourth category includes combined phenotypes, where individuals exhibit a mixture of symptoms from the aforementioned groups, reflecting the diverse impacts that TSPEAR mutations can have on ectodermal and neural tissues. The relationship between deafness, autosomal recessive 98 and TSPEAR variants is, however, provisional, and a recent report on TSPEAR variants questioned its relationship with hearing loss [7]. Suggesting otherwise, herein, we reported a patient with a novel pathogenic variant in TSPEAR presented solely with SNHL. We also have provided a comprehensive review of the reported cases of TSPEAR mutations to clarify the genetic and phenotypic spectrum associated with TSPEAR mutations and to highlight the need for comprehensive genetic testing and analysis in patients with related symptoms.

The patient was a 6-year-old Iranian girl of Fars ethnicity with bilateral severe SNHL suspected first by her indifference to the environment. After the occurrence of this prelingual form of profound hearing loss, she had also speech disorders interfering with normal school performance. We examined the patient for any form of ectodermal dysplasia or malformation in teeth, skin, hair, and nail and they were in normal figuration. The patient had no other clinical features previously reported to be associated with ectodermal dysplasia 14, hair/tooth type with or without hypohidrosis (ECTD14; OMIM# 618,180) including any abnormality in teeth, skin, hair, nail, and so on. In addition, a thorough past medical history was taken to rule out environmental etiologic causes such as trauma or infantile infections. Moreover, the parents and the siblings of the proband were examined for any problems related to the concerns of this study. The patient was born to a consanguineous marriage. She has two siblings, one sister and one brother; all were older than her and they had no complaints regarding hearing problems or other clinical phenotypes of TSPEAR mutations. Pure tone audiometry obtained from both ears of the patient showed a flat audiogram for both sides, indicative of severe bilateral hearing loss. Auditory brain response was also recorded for her and no response was obtained from either of her ears.

We extracted genomic deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) using a QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit from the proband’s peripheral white blood cells according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Genomic DNA obtained from the white blood cells was put through whole exome sequencing (WES) using a HiSeq 3000/4000 SBS Kit (singleton-WES). Burrows-Wheeler Aligner was used to filter and line up the raw data, converted by HiSeq X, against the human reference genome (hg19) [9]. The single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were called by the Genome Analysis ToolKit (GATK) software and the variants were annotated using ANNOVAR [10]. Five categories, namely pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variants of unknown clinical significance (VUS), likely benign, and benign, were used to classify the variants on the basis of the standards for the interpretation of sequence variations recommended by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) [11]. The proband’s phenotype was evaluated against the candidate genes’ phenotypic features. Core phenotypes of the genes and variants were extracted from the OMIM database and utilized to acquire a gene list of the virtual panel (OMIM # 614,861 and # 618,180).

Sanger sequencing was further conducted to confirm the presence of the variants. To amplify the DNA fragments covering the mutilations in the genome, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was carried out. PCR utilized the Hot start mix Ampliqon (25 µL), 70 ng of DNA, 1 µL of forward primer (GATGGGCCAAGCTCAGTG), 1 µL of reverse primer (AGGGAAGGGCAGGATGAC), and 21 µL deionized water. Oligo Primer Designer was utilized to design the primers [12]. The DNA was amplified using the following thermocycling steps: 95 °C for 15 minutes; 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 seconds, 57 °C for 30 seconds, and 72 °C for 5 seconds; 72 °C for 5 minutes. We analyzed the results of the Sanger sequencing using Chromas Lite v2.01.

WES conducted on the proband’s blood sample identified a novel homozygous variant in TSPEAR (NM_144991.3: c.668C > T, p. Ser223Leu). The variant is categorized as likely pathogenic on the basis of the standards for the interpretation of sequence variations recommended by ACMG 2015 criteria (PM2 and PP3 criteria) [11]. A summary of the pathogenicity prediction of this variant is presented in Supplementary Table 1. The presence of the variant was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and determined as the causative agent of the phenotype observed in the proband. Variants in TSPEAR have previously been shown to be associated with a variety of clinical presentations with different levels of severity, including dysplasia in the skin, nails, sweat glands, hair, or teeth, and SNHL [6, 14, 15]. The exact functions of the TSPEAR protein and the underlying mechanisms involved in the pathophysiology of diseases resulting from mutations of its encoding gene are not completely understood; however, the presentations and the defects seem to mainly be developed from disruption of Notch signaling pathway, which have an important role in neuroectodermal development [16–18]. TSPEAR is expressed in the organ of Corti, cochlear ganglion, stria vascularis of the cochlear duct, vestibular end organs, inferior colliculus, and auditory nuclei in mice [6]. Peled and colleagues reported that ribonucleic acids of this gene were also detected in dental organ and major portions of hair follicles of mice, including their matric keratinocytes, both the outer and inner root sheaths, shaft, and infundibulum [4]. In addition, the research group induced the downregulation of TSPEAR transcription by the use of small interfering nucleic acids, which gave rise to apoptosis of the epithelial cells lining the hair follicle and therefore, a decrease in the width of the bulb, and a statistically significant decrease in the expression of Notch1 was also observed.

In this study, we reviewed the existing literature systematically for previously reported cases with different TSPEAR variants, which yielded ten studies. A total of 44 patients from 33 families with manifestations related to variants of TSPEAR have been reported thus far in the literature. There was a wide age range, from infancy to early adulthood, and 16 patients were female and 27 patients were male. With consideration of our case, it was found that the majority of these patients (22 subjects out of 27, 81.5%, with mentioned type of parental marriage) were born of a consanguineous marriage, which is in line with reports from other rare diseases with autosomal recessive inheritance [19]. Manifestations in the patients with TSPEAR variants were mainly related to ectodermal organs (35 patients) and the auditory system (11 patients; Table 1); however, several individuals with ectodermal organ involvement did not fulfill the criteria required to be defined as ectodermal dysplasia and they had only hypotrichosis, oligodontia, conical teeth, and so on. Among the ectodermal derivatives, the most commonly involved tissue was teeth, which were reported to be involved in nearly all those with ectodermal issues (34 patients out of 35); the defects in the teeth of these patients were in their size, shape, or number, presenting with microdontia, conical-shaped teeth, and from total agenesis of all teeth to oligodontia and hypodontia. Other ectodermal involvements were a wide phenotypic spectrum including thinning, pitting, or other dysplastic changes of nails; sparse, wiry hair or hypopigmentation of the hairs; and decreased sweating. Among all the included individuals, only one patient reported by Bowles et al. [7] had presentations of both the auditory system, SNHL, and the ectodermal tissue derivatives such as conical-shaped teeth, nail dysplasia, sparse hair, and eyebrows; however, the patients had two other heterozygous variants in genes previously shown to be in association with deafness, in addition to biallelic mutations in TSPEAR: one in GJB2 and the other in GJB6, which all were identified to be pathogenic on the basis of the guidelines of ACMG 2015 [11, 28].

There exists a debate regarding the association of mutations in TSPEAR and hearing loss. There is a wide range of etiologies that could predispose an individual to or contribute to the development of SNHL, both syndromic and nonsyndromic forms such as genetic backgrounds, systemic disorders, infections, traumatic injuries, use of ototoxic drugs, and so on. [29, 30]. These etiologies should be thoroughly investigated, first with a detailed history taking and meticulously conducted physical examinations, then with the use of different paraclinical workups, if needed. The patient we reported in this paper had no previous history of trauma; congenital, neonatal, or childhood infections; hospitalization; receiving of any ototoxic medications; or diseases such as autoimmune disorders, diabetes, or meningitis. In other words, we could not find any underlying factor responsible for the SNHL other than the variant in TSPEAR. Bowles et al. argued that we should revisit the concept of the relationship between TSPEAR variants and hearing abnormalities [7]; they have justified their argument by pointing to rather fewer reports on the occurrence of SNHL in patients with variants in this gene in comparison with the occurrence of ectodermal tissue manifestations and limitations of WES or hearing loss genetic panel in detection of some variants. However, it may be quite unlikely to attribute presentations of all these individuals with SNHL to these issues; three different variants in TSPEAR without any additional variants in hearing loss-associated genes reported by Sloan‑Heggen et al. (NM_001272037: c.1472_1473delAT and c.1362G > A, p. Pro454Pro) [31], Pavlenkova et al. (NM_144991.3: c.1870G > T, p. Glu624*) [32], and our study (NM_144991.3: c.668C > T, p. Ser223Leu) were found in patients with sole SNHL, which could question the argument, even if we do not consider the variant detected by Delmaghani et al., [6] which was associated with ectodermal dysplasia instead of SNHL in studies by Peled et al. [4] and Du et al. [8]. In addition, presence of SNHL in about one-quarter of all detected patients and expression of TSPEAR in the auditory system, as mentioned before, suggest that it is more than just an accidental concomitancy between these two conditions. However, more studies are required to explore the effects of TSPEAR knockout on the morphology and function of the auditory system and to shed more light on the role of TSPEAR variants in the physiopathology of hearing loss.

Different phenotypes of TSPEAR variants, including ectodermal dysplasia 14, hair/tooth type, with or without hypohidrosis, deafness, autosomal recessive 98, and selective tooth agenesis 10, could be attributed to variable penetrance in different patients. This variable penetrance regarding similar phenotypes has also been shown for other genes; for instance, FGF3 variants have been associated with varying severities of tooth agenesis [33]. Likewise, in the study by Rabie et al., two sisters were reported to have similar TSPEAR genotypes; however, they did not have similar clinical features in teeth: one had total agenesis of all teeth, while the other had oligodontia. Additionally, a compound heterozygous variant in the same study manifested as oligodontia and hypodontia in two other siblings [5]. The study also found a statistically significant association between the ethnicity of patients and the major clinical features of TSPEAR variants, suggesting that there are factors such as ethnic background that may affect the penetrance and expression of these variants. This observation points to the need for a more nuanced understanding of role of TSPEAR variations as a genetic contributor to hearing loss and ectodermal dysplasia. It is reasonable to reevaluate the assertion whether TSPEAR could be a monogenic cause of hearing loss and investigate the putative reduced penetrance of the phenotypes [7].

Additionally, it is important to note that we used WES for the detection of the genetic variation in this study. WES has proven to be highly effective for diagnostic purposes and is often considered the main diagnostic tool in many settings in clinical genetics due to its superiority in terms of time and cost compared with more comprehensive techniques such as whole genome sequencing [34]. However, WES does have limitations, particularly in detecting variations in noncoding regions and structural changes such as copy number variants, inversions, and translocations, where WGS excels in accuracy and inclusiveness [35]. These noncoding regions are vital for the regulation of gene transcription, translation, and posttranslational modifications, and their alterations can result in various genetic disorders [36, 37]. Moreover, studies have indicated that whole genome sequencing offer more thorough coverage of the exome than WES as it may not capture all exons, including some that are significant for clinical diagnosis, resulting in pathogenic variants in these uncaptured exons potentially remaining undetected [38, 39]. In a study on 54 participants at the Vanderbilt Undiagnosed Diseases Network clinical site, it was revealed that up to 30% of them were not diagnosed with sequencing of the exomes and required additional testing, such as genome sequencing, to achieve a diagnosis [40]. We were not able to use whole genome sequencing in the current study due to limited availability and affordability of this approach. While WES successfully identified a mutation in TSPEAR in our patient, a gene whose mutations have previously been shown to be associated with SNHL, as discussed above, it might not have uncovered potential additional mutations. Exploring the presence of other mutations through whole genome sequencing could offer deeper insights into hereditary auditory failure. Therefore, we recommend considering full genome sequencing in future research endeavors to enhance our understanding of the genetic underpinnings of these conditions.

Given the high importance of these genetic disorders and the burden associated with them, the family members of these patients should pursue molecular genetic tests to identify the carriers and eliminate the risk of future occurrence of these phenotypes. As the presentations of TSPEAR variants could be associated with SNHL, school performance, and consequently, social, occupational, or other areas of functioning could be impaired; therefore, age-appropriate counseling for affected patients is highly recommended.
