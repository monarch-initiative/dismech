The branched chainα-amino acids (BCAAs) leucine, valine, and isoleucine provide a substantial share of dietary essential amino acid intake (≈35%) [1]. BCAAs are also overrepresented in proteins relative to their share of total encoded amino acids, underscoring their importance for protein synthesis [2]. BCAAs also regulate nutrient sensitive signaling pathways, including the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) and the integrated stress response (ISR), which coordinate anabolic and catabolic programs according to nutrient availability [3–7].

In addition to serving as building blocks of proteins, BCAAs contribute to neurotransmitter synthesis and are metabolized via transamination to branched chain keto acids (BCKAs). The BCKAs are then irreversibly oxidized by the branched chainαketo acid dehydrogenase (BCKDH) complex to generate acyl CoA derivatives that feed the tricarboxylic acid cycle and support energy production [8]. Flux through this pathway is actively tuned by branched chain keto acid dehydrogenase kinase (BCKDK), which phosphorylates and inhibits BCKDH, thereby constraining BCAA oxidation to maintain systemic balance [1,7,9]. Pharmacologic or genetic reduction of BCKDK activity increases apparent clearance without saturating downstream steps [10]. Whole body tracer studies indicate that skeletal muscle, brown adipose tissue, liver, kidney, and heart serve as major oxidative sinks for circulating BCAA, whereas brain contributes comparatively little to systemic BCAA oxidation [11]. Together, these observations imply that changes in peripheral oxidation can secondarily limit BCAA availability to the brain.

In the central nervous system, BCAAs and especially leucine account for roughly one third of glutamate synthesis, the most abundant excitatory neurotransmitter in brain [12–15]. Cerebral access to BCAAs depends on transporter-mediated delivery across the blood–brain barrier, notably viaSlc7a5/LAT1, directly linking systemic availability to brain metabolism [16,17]. In humans, BCKDK deficiency is characterized by severely reduced circulating BCAAs with relatively preserved or elevated levels of other amino acids and is associated with microcephaly, developmental delay, epilepsy, and autism spectrum disorder [18]. Animal models recapitulate systemic and neurological features.Bckdkknockout (KO) rats display impaired growth, shortened limbs, muscle weakness, and neuromuscular junction abnormalities, andBckdkKO mice are smaller thanBckdkintact wild type (WT) mice, show growth delay from weaning, and exhibit neurological signs including epilepsy and hindlimb clasping [19,20].

Previously we reported that inBckdkKO mice, brain BCAA levels fell while most other amino acids rose, except glutamate which was maintained steadily [5]. Here we asked why this amino acid profile emerged and how it relates to motor behavior and to nutrient stress responses in brain and skeletal muscle. To address this, we studied whole bodyBckdkKO mice and first assessed motor function in response to nutrient stress, followed by a comparison of cellular changes in skeletal muscle and brain. In our analyses, we integrated energy expenditure and muscle strength and coordination tests alongside molecular readouts of translation and stress signaling. We further applied nitrogen-labeled BCAA tracers to quantify circulating nitrogen flux (Fcirc) and estimate [15N] glutamate labeling across the brain. This approach showed that systemic loss ofBckdkreduced BCAA nitrogen Fcircand delivery of BCAA-derived nitrogen to all regions of the brain. We also noted persistent vulnerability in brains of non-growing adultBckdkKO mice, revealed as reduced protein synthesis alongside ISR activation during fasting. In contrast, nutrient sensing and signaling responses in skeletal muscle during fasting and refeeding were similar to WT mice, confirming the priority of skeletal muscle in BCAA use. In conclusion, the basis for motor deficits in non-growing adult mice lackingBckdkare not due to dysregulated protein homeostasis in skeletal muscle but rather to altered AA transporter expression in the brain, likely to sustain production of the neurotransmitter glutamate especially during nutrient scarcity.

Adult (approximately 20–40 weeks old) WT andBckdkKO males were used in this study. In the experiment, WT andBckdkKO mice were housed singly and then subjected to either a 7 h fast (fasted) or refed for 2 h following a 7 h fast (refed).

Young (4–6 weeks-old) WT andBckdkKO males were overnight (12 h) fasted and then assigned to one of two study protocols. Experiment 1: Mice were administered 0, 25% or 50% of their average daily leucine intake (0, 0.34 and 0.68 mg·kg−1body weight respectively; general approach described in [23,24]) by oral gavage and killed 30 min later. Experiment 2: Mice were provided a ~17% protein by kcal beverage (Boost, Nestle Health Science, Bridgewater, NJ, USA) or water as vehicle control through their water bottles and killed 1 h later.

Adult (8–12 weeks) WT andBckdkKO males received infusions and injections into their pre-catheterized right jugular veins. The jugular vein catheterizations for these procedures were performed on 6–8-week-old male mice (both WT andBckdkKO) as previously described [10]). One week prior to infusions and injections, WT andBckdkKO mice were fed a control diet or low protein diet. On the 7th day, mice were transferred to new cages without food at 9 AM. Drinking water was provided as hydrogel. After a short period of fasting (5–6 h), isotope-labeled metabolites (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Tewksbury, MA, USA) were infused in sterile saline for 4 h. All three BCAA were always delivered together, in a ratio of 5:6:3 valine: leucine: isoleucine, with the indicated metabolite in labeled form and the others unlabeled. The infusion rates used were 2, 5, and 10 nmol/g body weight/min. In low protein diet group, oneBckdkKO mouse became moribund before the end of the experiment and was removed from the study, resulting in a finaln=3 in this experimental group. At the end of the infusion, tail blood was collected by tail snipping, incubated on ice for 20 mins and centrifuged at 16,000 xgfor 10 mins at 4°C to obtain serum. Serum samples were kept at −80°C until LC-MS analysis. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and tissues were harvested immediately, snap-frozen in liquid-nitrogen and stored at −80°C. Frozen brains were stored intact at −80°C until MALDI MSI analysis. About 10-μm thick sections were collected on a cryostat (Leica, Welzlar, Germany). Sections for MALDI MSI were thaw-mounted on indium tin oxide (ITO)-coated glass slides (Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA, USA) and desiccated under vacuum for 10 min. Alternating serial sections for IHC and immunofluorescence were collected on standard glass slides. Matrix coating on ITO glass slides was performed as previously described [25].

Metabolite extraction were carried out as previously described [10]. Briefly, Serum metabolites were extracted from 5 μL samples using 100 μL of a 40:40:20 methanol:acetonitrile:water mixture. 15N-labeled metabolite standards were added to the extraction solvent for measuring absolute metabolite concentrations. Samples were vortexed for 5 s and centrifuged at 16,000 xgfor 20 mins at 4°C. The supernatant was then analyzed using LC-MS. For tissue metabolite extraction, frozen tissues were ground with a cryomill for 60 s. Metabolites were extracted from approximately 15 mg of tissue powder using the same extraction solvent, with the solvent volume being 40 times the tissue weight in mg. Samples were vortexed for 30 s and centrifuged at 16,000x gfor 30 mins at 4°C. The supernatant was then analyzed using LC-MS. A quadrupole-orbitrap mass spectrometer (Q Exactive, Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA), operating in negative ion mode, was coupled to hydrophilic interaction chromatography via electrospray ionization and used to scan from m/z 70 to 1,000 at 1 Hz and 140,000 resolution. LC separation was performed on a XBridge BEH Amide column (2.1 mm x 150 mm, 2.5 μm particle size, 130 Å pore size; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) using a gradient of solvent A (20 mM ammonium acetate and 20 mM ammonium hydroxide in 95:5 water: acetonitrile, pH 9.45) and solvent B (acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 150 μL/min. The LC gradient was as previously described [10]. The autosampler temperature was set to 5°C, and the injection volume was 3 μL. Data were analyzed using the EI-MAVEN software with natural isotope correction.

Circulating nitrogen flux (Fcirc) of branched-chain amino acids was calculated under pseudo–steady-state infusion conditions using isotope dilution principles [26,27]. Briefly, for each BCAA, Fcircwas computed from the tracer infusion rate (Rinf, nmol g−1·min−1) and the plateau fractional enrichment (L) of circulating labeled BCAA according to: Fcirc=Rinf• ((1-L)/L), where L was defined as the fraction of circulating BCAA molecules containing15N, calculated at 4 h of constant infusion as the sum of the fully labeled [U-13C,15N] isotopologue and the15N-only isotopologue.

MALDI -MSI was performed essentially as described previously [25] using a solariX XR FT-ICR mass spectrometer with a 9.4 T magnet (Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA, USA). Matrix-coated brain sections were loaded in a slide adaptor and analyzed at 120,000 resolving power (at m/z 500). External calibration with 1 mg mL−1arginine achieved ≤1 ppm mass accuracy; taurine (m/z 124.0068), malate (m/z 133.0136), and glutamate (m/z 145.0611) served as lock masses during acquisition. Spectra were acquired with Smartbeam-II optics (laser focus “small”, 200 shots per pixel at 1,000 Hz) on a 20–50 μm raster. Ions were accumulated using the cumulative accumulation of selected ions (CASI) over m/z 70–300 before transfer to the ICR cell. The absolute minimum intensity for peak picking was set to 5×103. Data were collected in FlexImaging (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) and processed with IsoScope (MAT-LAB, Natick, MA, USA) to visualize and quantify isotope labeling as in [25].

Mice were placed within OxyMax-CLAMS Columbus Instruments International (CLMAS, Columbus, OH, USA). The CLAMS provides mice a temperature and light-controlled environment with real-time monitoring of energy expenditure, respiratory exchange ratio (O2and CO2in and out), food and water intake, locomotor activity (X, Y, and Z axis) and running wheel usage. The initial 24 h were excluded from the subsequent data analysis. CLAMS data were analyzed using the CalR application [28] where the data were compiled into hourly measurements for further calculations and statistical analyses.

Given the documented neuromuscular abnormalities and disrupted neuromuscular junctions associated withBckdkdeficiency in rats [19], we first assessed motor function and physical activity inBckdkKO mice. Grip strength was evaluated by measuring the hanging time of mice suspended from a wire grid.BckdkKO mice performed similarly to WT across trials, suggesting that muscular strength remains intact (Fig. 1A). Motor coordination was then assessed using a balance beam test, where both crossing time and number of slips were recorded.BckdkKO mice displayed significantly longer crossing times and a higher number of foot slips than WT (Figs. 1BandC), indicating impaired motor coordination. Video recordings showedBckdkKO mice relied heavily on their tails for stabilization during movement (Supplementary Video). To examine voluntary physical activity, wheel-running behavior of mice was recorded over time in a CLAMS, the study timeline is shown inFigure 1D. Under a control diet (20% protein), cumulative running activity was comparable between genotypes. However, upon switching to a low protein diet (5% protein),BckdkKO mice showed a marked reduction in running activity relative to WT (Fig. 1E), as reflected by a significantly flatter slope in cumulative distance (Fig. 1F). Interestingly, under control diet feeding,BckdkKO mice exhibited higher energy expenditure as compared to WT. This difference was not observed under low protein diet feeding, where energy expenditure levels between genotypes converged (Fig. 1G). Notably, food intake increased inBckdkKO mice: both food and water consumption were higher than WT under both diets (Supplementary Figs. S1A–D). At the end of the experiment,BckdkKO mice weighed less than WT mice (Fig. 1H). Interestingly,BckdkKO mice lost a greater proportion of lean mass, whereas WT mice primarily lost fat mass during low protein diet (Figs. 1IandJ). These data suggest that the loss ofBckdkimpairs muscle coordination and leads to decreased physical activity and protein wasting under a nutrient-stress condition.

To determine whether feeding status influences nutrient stress signaling in the skeletal muscle of adultBckdkKO mice, we conducted a short-term fasting and refeeding experiment as outlined inFigure 2A. Consistent with previous findings [6], we observed that age-matchedBckdkKO males weighed significantly less than WT (Fig. 2B), and skeletal muscle mass also trended downward as compared to WT (Fig. 2C). Using HPLC, we measured serum BCAA concentrations under fasted and refed conditions. In the fasted state, we observedBckdkKO mice showed the lowest circulating BCAA levels across all conditions, with isoleucine showing statistically lower values than all other groups (Fig. 2D). To evaluate protein synthesis, we used puromycin incorporation as a marker [31]. Both WT andBckdkKO mice exhibited reduced puromycin incorporation in the fasted state, with no significant differences between genotypes (Fig. 2E). We next examined cellular signaling pathways related to nutrient stress. Phosphorylation of mTOR (p-mTOR) directionally trended downward in both WT andBckdkKO mice in fasted as compared to the refed condition (Fig. 2F). This decline aligns with a reduction in protein synthesis as part of the adaptive response to nutrient scarcity. Likewise, in a leucine refeeding study conducted in a cohort of young, growing males (Study 3, Experiment 1), phosphorylation states of 4E-BP1 and S6K1, two well-recognized markers of mTORC1 activity, were similarly increased in the skeletal muscle of WT andBckdkKO mice 30 min after oral administration of increasing amounts of L-leucine (Supplementary Figs. S2AandS2B). In agreement, meal feeding (Study 3, Experiment 2) also showed increased phosphorylation states of 4E-BP1 and S6K1 in the skeletal muscle of both WT andBckdkKO mice (Supplementary Fig. S2CandS2D). Together, these results suggest that mTORC1 signaling and protein synthesis in skeletal muscle are largely resilient to genetic loss ofBckdkand the accompanying reductions in circulating BCAAs.

In skeletal muscle, activation of the ISR, indicated by phosphorylation of eukaryotic initiation factor 2 alpha (p-eIF2α), remained unchanged in both genotypes during fasting (Fig. 2G). No changes to ISR gene targets were observed in the skeletal muscle of WT but several ISR target genes, includingAtf4, Atf5, Trib3, andChop (Ddit3), were downregulated upon fasting inBckdkKO mice (Fig. 2H). This pattern suggests adaptation to BCAA scarcity rather than a canonical stress response.

Because protein homeostasis is regulated on both synthesis and degradation, to further investigate the molecular response to fasting, we examined protein degradation pathways. Autophagy-related genesSqstm1andp21were reduced inBckdkKO mice during fasting (Fig. 2H), indicating a coordinated suppression of stress response and autophagy pathways. On the other hand, we observed elevated protein levels of the autophagy marker LC3II in fasted mice compared to fed mice with no difference seen between genotypes (Supplementary Fig. S3A), aligning with previous reports that fasting induces autophagy. No significant differences were detected in p62 protein levels (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Further analysis of ubiquitin-related gene targets revealed thatTrim63andFbox32expression were also decreased inBckdkKO mice, though total ubiquitin-protein levels remained unchanged (Supplementary Figs. S3C–E). These results suggest the presence of adaptive and homeostatic processes in an attempt to preserve muscle mass inBckdkKO during fasting.

To further investigate the molecular adaptations in skeletal muscle, we performed RNA-seq analysis on freely fedBckdkKO and WT non-growing adult males. We identified only a few altered genes, withBckdkbeing the most substantially suppressed (Supplementary Fig. S4andSupplementary Table S4). These findings align with our results above, emphasizing that skeletal muscle is resilient to the loss ofBckdkwith minimal transcriptomic changes.

We next turned our attention to the brain, focusing on ISR activation and its downstream effects inBckdkKO mice based on the observations we previously reported [5,32]. We observed that meanBckdkKO brain weight was ~10% below WT (Fig. 3A). We then assessed p-eIF2αlevels in whole brain under fasting and refed conditions (Fig. 3B). During fasting,BckdkKO mice exhibited significantly higher p-eIF2αlevels compared to WT mice. Interestingly, in refedBckdkKO mice, p-eIF2αlevels decreased relative to the fastedBckdkKO group, suggesting that refeeding alleviates ISR activation in the brain. To further confirm the ISR was activated by fasting and rescued by refeeding, we measured the mRNA abundance of ISR target genes (Fig. 3C). These genes showed increased expression in fastedBckdkKO mice compared to WT mice, aligning with the elevated p-eIF2αlevels. Refeeding significantly reduced the expression of ISR target genes inBckdkKO mice, consistent with the pattern in p-eIF2α(Fig. 3B) and circulating BCAAs inBckdkKO mice (Fig. 2D). One of the known effects of ISR activation is a reduction in global protein synthesis. To assess this, we used polysome profiling, focusing on the fasted groups where ISR activation was observed (Supplementary Fig. S5A). In theBckdkKO mice, we observed a pronounced monosome peak and a lower polysome-to-monosome ratio compared to WT mice, directly indicating reduced protein synthesis in the brain of fastedBckdkKO mice (Supplementary Fig. S5B), in alignment with observed ISR activation (Fig. 3). Together, these results suggest that nutrient scarcity activates the ISR in the brains ofBckdkKO mice and this is rescued during the prandial state.

To determine how loss ofBckdkaffects brain delivery and utilization of BCAAs, we performed primed continuous infusions of uniformly [13C] and [15N] labeled leucine, isoleucine, and valine in WT andBckdkKO mice fed control diet and low protein diet (Fig. 4). In this labeling scheme, all carbon atoms carry [13C] and the amino group carries [15N]. Forward transamination transfers [15N] from BCAAs toα-ketoglutarate to generate [15N] glutamate, while the [13C] labeled carbon chains are converted to the corresponding branched-chainα-keto acids. Reverse transamination can return [15N] from [15N] glutamate to the unlabeled keto acids, regenerating [15N] BCAAs.

Using LC-MS, we quantified enrichment of [15N] BCAAs in serum as a readout of reverse transamination under identical infusion conditions. Across both diets,BckdkKO mice showed significantly lower [15N] BCAA enrichment than WT for leucine, isoleucine, and valine (Fig. 4A). Because reverse BCAT labeling of BCAAs requires the transfer of labeled amino nitrogen from glutamate, reduced [15N]-BCAA enrichment is consistent with diminished recycling of BCAA-derived nitrogen through the freely reversible transamination reaction. In parallel, the fully labeled [U-13C,15N] BCAA fraction rose rapidly to near steady state and was consistently higher in KO than WT from 1 to 4 h (Supplementary Fig. S6). The higher [U-13C,15N] enrichment demonstrates that KO mice carry a smaller unlabeled endogenous BCAA pool, reducing dilution of the infused tracer and consistent with accelerated irreversible oxidation via constitutively active BCKDH. The circulating nitrogen fluxes of all BCAAs were decreased under low-protein diet and were further reduced in theBckdkKO mice (Figs. 4BandC). This interpretation matches the HPLC data showing lower serum BCAA concentrations inBckdkKO mice (Fig. 2D). Taken together, these data indicate thatBckdkKO mice have a smaller circulating BCAA pool and reduced recycling of BCAA-derived nitrogen via reversible transamination, which may limit delivery of BCAA-derived nitrogen to the brain.

We next assessed incorporation of BCAA-derived nitrogen into brain glutamate using MALDI MS imaging. [15N] glutamate labeling was significantly reduced inBckdkKO mice relative to WT, with decreases observed across all analyzed brain regions (Fig. 4D). This reduction reflects diminished incorporation of BCAA-derived amino nitrogen into the brain glutamate pool inBckdkKO mice. Considering that in humans and mice lackingBckdktotal brain glutamate concentrations are unchanged [5,18], these findings suggest that alternative nitrogen sources are being utilized to maintain the bulk glutamate pool when BCAA-derived nitrogen input declines.

Our findings demonstrate thatBckdkKO mice exhibit tissue-specific outcomes to BCAA depletion, with contrasting physiological responses in skeletal muscle and brain. In skeletal muscle,BckdkKO mice respond similar to WT mice during fasting and protein refeeding, indicating adaptive resilience to a lower supply of circulating BCAAs. Conversely, the brains of adultBckdkKO mice become vulnerable during nutrient scarcity: fasting activated the ISR and refeeding relieved it. We interpret ISR engagement under natural fasting inBckdkKO brains as a signature of supply limitation, in which reduced delivery of BCAA-derived nitrogen to the brain may alter amino acid balance, impairing normal neural function and providing a plausible route to the development of impaired motor control.

Prior work showed that geneticBckdkdeletion in mice reduces circulating branched-chainα-keto acids and glutamate, reflecting enhanced BCAA oxidation [11]. Consistent with this, flux mapping also shows that skeletal muscle serves as the dominant tissue for BCAA oxidation, whereas brain contributes comparatively little [11]. Li et al. reported that for many circulating metabolites, including essential amino acids, consumption rises roughly in step with the circulating concentration, and that inhibiting BCKDK increases clearance efficiency without raising maximal pathway capacity [10]. Applied here, constitutive BCKDH activation inBckdkKO mice would increase peripheral clearance at a given concentration and draw down the circulating pool before it reaches the brain [7]. In this scenario, reduced recycling via reverse transamination would lower the labeled fraction of circulating [15N] BCAAs while disappearance rises, thereby limiting BCAA delivery to the brain. Consistent with this sequence, primed continuous infusion of uniformly [13C] and [15N] labeled BCAAs showed lower serum enrichment of [15N] BCAAs inBckdkKOs across diets and lower [15N] glutamate labeling across brain regions, consistent with reduced incorporation of BCAA-derived amino nitrogen into the glutamate pool. Taken together, these data support a supply constrained state in which accelerated peripheral oxidation outpaces delivery of BCAA-derived nitrogen to the brain.

Existing literature shows that BCAAs provide one third of the amino nitrogen used for glutamate synthesis in brain [13–15,17]. Because BCAA-derived nitrogen to the brain is substantial [33], reduced incorporation of BCAA-derived nitrogen as shown by MALDI-MS imaging could influence neural function even though total brain glutamate is maintained. Several of the amino acids that are elevated inBckdkKO brain [5,18] are established contributors to nitrogen metabolism and therefore represent plausible compensatory sources for maintaining the glutamate pool when BCAA-derived nitrogen input is limited. For example, alanine is an established aminotransferase substrate in brain nitrogen metabolism and may contribute to glutamate synthesis under conditions of altered amino acid availability [34,35]. In addition, several elevated large neutral amino acids (methionine, phenylalanine, tyrosine, threonine, and histidine) shareSlc7a5/LAT1 at the blood-brain barrier and can participate in aminotransferase reactions after entry, while also competing with BCAAs for transport [18,36,37]. Glycine and arginine enter via y+transporters [38], and arginine can feed the ornithine/ornithine-aminotransferase node that generates glutamate in mitochondria [39]. We and others previously showed that in the brains ofBckdkKO mice thatSlc7a5expression increased concurrently with elevations in non-BCAASlc7a5/LAT1 substrates [16,17,37,40]. Those observations are supported by our current findings and suggest altered patterns of amino acid availability at the blood–brain barrier. Together, the isotope-tracing and labelling data in brain indicate a supply limited state in which accelerated peripheral BCAA oxidation limits delivery of BCAA-derived nitrogen to the brain which may influence neural function and subsequently motor control. Additional studies are required to directly quantify flux through these alternative pathways and define their relative contributions.

Human BCKDK deficiency presents with low circulating BCAAs and neurodevelopmental features including microcephaly, epilepsy, and autism spectrum disorder; several reports describe clinical improvement, particularly seizure control and developmental gains, with dietary BCAA or high-protein supplementation when started early [18,41,42]. Our data supports a precursor supply-limited state in theBckdkKO brain (lower serum [15N] BCAAs, lower brain [15N] glutamate with preserved total glutamate) and an adaptive rise inSlc7a5and other LAT1 substrates. This aligns with human studies implicating LAT1/SLC7A5 in autism spectrum disorder risk and pathophysiology [16,43], as well as reports of amino acid dysregulation “metabotypes” in autism spectrum disorder cohorts [44]. Tissue-specificBckdkKO models reinforce this interpretation. Muscle-specificBckdkdeletion does not produce neurological signs or motor coordination deficits at baseline; instead, it sensitizes skeletal muscle to protein undernutrition and amplifies myofibrillar loss while leaving neuromuscular behavior intact [45]. In contrast, cortex/neuronalBckdkdeletion lowers brain BCAA content and causes neurological abnormalities, including hindlimb clasping and reduced endurance capacity, worsened by protein restriction [46]. These observations suggest practical implications: raising circulating BCAAs or avoiding states that accelerate peripheral oxidation (e.g., prolonged fasting) may help protect brain supply in susceptible contexts, whereas interventions that might constrain BCAA supply across the blood-brain barrier may warrant neurological caution [16].

This study used stable isotope tracing to assess BCAA-derived nitrogen delivery to the brain but did not directly measure plasma15N-glutamate, circulating branched-chainα-keto acids, or flux through specific alternative nitrogen pathways. Although preservation of total brain glutamate despite reduced incorporation of BCAA-derived nitrogen suggests compensatory input from non-BCAA sources, the relative contributions of individual pathways were not quantified and warrant future investigation. Accordingly, the model proposed here is hypothesis-generating and will require additional experiments to directly test specific pathways, fluxes, and mechanisms underlying compensatory nitrogen handling in brain. In addition, skeletal muscle analyses were conducted in both young and non-growing adult mice, whereas brain studies were limited to non-growing adults. Developmental stage–specific effects ofBckdkloss on brain metabolism remain to be determined. Finally, increased expression of amino acid transporters such asSlc7a5is consistent with an adaptive response to amino acid limitation, but the functional consequences of this response will require direct experimental testing.

Overall, whole-bodyBckdkloss increases peripheral oxidation and reduces the contribution of BCAAs to brain glutamate nitrogen. Neurological sensitivity in adulthood, in contrast to skeletal muscle resilience, is consistent with the heightened vulnerability of the brain to unregulated BCAA oxidation. Brain activation of the ISR is accompanied by increased expression of amino acid transporters such asSlc7a5, reflecting an adaptive response to BCAA limitation. Rather than selectively restoring BCAA supply, this response may contribute to amino acid imbalance in brain. Taken together, these findings link systemic BCAA depletion to impaired neuromuscular function inBckdkKO mice, highlighting a contrast between the relative resilience of skeletal muscle and the heightened vulnerability of the brain, particularly under conditions of nutrient scarcity.
