The GA1 <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> can vary widely among untreated family members with the same <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genotype/">genotype</a>, primarily as a function of the timing and severity of acute basal ganglia injury. Acute early-onset injury may occur from age three months to three years in infantile-onset GA1 (rarely, up to age six years); more insidious neurologic manifestations may occur after age six years in late-onset GA1. Acute injury manifests as acute bilateral striatal injury and subsequent complex movement disorders. Affected individuals may also develop insidious-onset basal ganglia injury in the absence of an identified acute encephalopathic crisis. Infants identified by newborn screening (NBS) and started on treatment may not develop basal ganglia injury or subsequent movement disorder. Extrastriatal symptoms may occur after age six years including neurologic sequelae such as epilepsy, stroke, and cerebral palsy-like manifestations.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Diagnosis/testing.</h4><p>The diagnosis of GA1 can be established in a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> with suggestive findings and <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> pathogenic variants in <i>GCDH</i> identified by <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a> or significantly reduced glutaryl-CoA dehydrogenase enzyme activity in cultured fibroblasts or leukocytes.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Management.</h4><p><i>Targeted therapies:</i> When GA1 is suspected during the diagnostic evaluation of a newborn with an elevated concentration of 3-hydroxyglutaric acid (3-OH-GA) in plasma or urine, metabolic treatment should be initiated immediately, including avoiding catabolism, a low-lysine diet, and carnitine supplementation.</p><p><i>Supportive care:</i> Feeding tube as needed; standard therapy for dystonic movement disorders; physical therapy and aggressive rehabilitation therapy for gross motor delay; developmental and educational support; anti-seizure medication per neurologist; education, emergency letter, medical alert bracelet, and dietary supplies to avoid acute encephalopathic crisis; communication prior to surgeries or procedures; transitional care plan. Emergency outpatient treatment includes dietary intervention, antipyretics, and antiemetics. Acute inpatient treatment includes high-energy fluids with insulin as needed, intravenous lipid emulsion, dietary adjustments, seizure medication as needed, and treatment of metabolic acidosis.</p><p><i>Surveillance:</i> Regular evaluations by a metabolic specialist and metabolic dietician with quantitative analysis of plasma amino acids; assessment of growth, developmental progress, and educational needs at each visit; neuropsychological testing as needed; assessment for movement disorders at each visit; assessment for nutritional deficiencies if clinically indicated; plasma creatinine and/or cystatin C levels periodically in adolescents and adults; complete blood count, ferritin, alanine transaminase, aspartate transaminase, and albumin if clinically indicated; consider head MRI if clinically indicated; assessment of quality of life and family needs as needed.</p><p><i>Agents/circumstances to avoid:</i> Excessive dietary protein or protein malnutrition inducing catabolic state; prolonged fasting; catabolic illness (intercurrent infection; brief febrile illness post vaccination); inadequate caloric provision during other stressors (e.g., surgery or procedure requiring fasting/anesthesia); avoidance or extreme caution with contact sports and physical activity that increase the risk for head injury.</p><p><i>Evaluation of relatives at risk:</i> If <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/prenatal-testing/">prenatal testing</a> has not been performed, a newborn with an older sib with GA1 should be treated at birth pending results of the definitive glutaryl-CoA dehydrogenase enzyme activity assay and/or <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a> (if the <i>GCDH</i> pathogenic variants in the family are known). The genetic status of older sibs (even if asymptomatic) of a child with GA1 should be clarified by assay of glutaryl-CoA dehydrogenase enzyme activity or molecular genetic testing so that treatment can be instituted in a timely manner.</p><p><i>Pregnancy management:</i> It is recommended that care for a pregnant woman with GA1 be provided by a multidisciplinary team including the treating obstetrician, a metabolic physician, and a specialist metabolic dietician.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Genetic counseling.</h4><p>GA1 is inherited in an <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-recessive/">autosomal recessive</a> manner. If both parents are known to be <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for a <i>GCDH</i> <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>, each sib of an affected individual has at conception a 25% chance of being affected, a 50% chance of being an asymptomatic <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier/">carrier</a>, and a 25% chance of being unaffected and not a carrier. Once the <i>GCDH</i> pathogenic variants have been identified in an affected family member, <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier-testing/">carrier testing</a> for at-risk relatives and prenatal/<a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a> are possible.</p></div></div><div id="glutaric-a1.Diagnosis"><h2 id="_glutaric-a1_Diagnosis_">Diagnosis</h2><p>Guidelines for the diagnosis of glutaric acidemia type 1 (GA1) have been published [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.boy.2023.482">Boy et al 2023</a>].</p><div id="glutaric-a1.Suggestive_Findings"><h3>Suggestive Findings</h3><p>GA1 <b>should be suspected</b> in an infant with an <a href="#glutaric-a1.Infant_with_OutofRange_NBS_R">out-of-range newborn screening</a> (NBS) result or a <a href="#glutaric-a1.Proband_with_Suggestive_Find">proband with suggestive</a> clinical, imaging, and laboratory findings and family history.</p><p>Note: A symptomatic individual can have (1) onset of clinical findings prior to availability of NBS results or (2) untreated GA1 due to NBS not performed or false negative NBS. Approximately 25% of treated individuals develop manifestations of GA1, including a movement disorder, and this risk is higher when caregivers are not adherent to recommended treatment after a positive NBS result.</p><div id="glutaric-a1.Infant_with_OutofRange_NBS_R"><h4>Infant with Out-of-Range NBS Result</h4><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">NBS for GA1 is primarily based on identification of increased glutarylcarnitine (C5DC) on dried blood spots, which has been shown to have 96% <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/sensitivity/">sensitivity</a> [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.boy.2018.970">Boy et al 2018</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">C5DC values above the cutoff reported by the screening laboratory require follow-up biochemical testing with <b>either</b> urine organic acid analysis <b>or</b> quantitative glutaric and 3-hydroxyglutaric acid studies, with preference for quantitative studies if available.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">If <b>either</b> urine organic acid analysis <b>or</b> quantitative glutaric acid and 3-hydroxyglutaric acid is abnormal, treatment (see <a href="#glutaric-a1.Targeted_Therapies">Targeted Therapies</a>) and testing to establish a definitive diagnosis (see <a href="#glutaric-a1.Establishing_the_Diagnosis">Establishing the Diagnosis</a>) should be initiated immediately and concurrently [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.boy.2023.482">Boy et al 2023</a>].</div><div class="half_rhythm">For more information on false positive and false negative results for NBS for glutaric acidemia type 1 click <a href="/books/NBK546575/bin/glutaric-a1-false_results.pdf">here</a> (pdf).</div></li></ul></div><div id="glutaric-a1.Proband_with_Suggestive_Find"><h4>Proband with Suggestive Findings</h4><p>
<b>Clinical findings</b>

The GA1 <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> can vary widely among untreated family members with the same <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genotype/">genotype</a>, primarily as a function of the timing and severity of acute basal ganglia injury. Acute early-onset injury may occur from age three months to three years in infantile-onset GA1, and more insidious neurologic manifestations may occur after age six years in late-onset GA1 [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.l_pezlaso.2007.979">L&#x000f3;pez-Laso et al 2007</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.wang.2014.813">Wang et al 2014</a>]. Acute injury characteristically manifests as acute bilateral striatal injury and subsequent complex movement disorders. Affected individuals may also develop insidious-onset basal ganglia injury in the absence of an identified acute encephalopathic crisis. Infants identified by newborn screening (NBS) and started on treatment may remain asymptomatic and not develop basal ganglia injury or subsequent movement disorder. Extrastriatal symptoms may occur after age six years including neurologic sequela such as epilepsy, stroke, and cerebral palsy-like manifestations [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.boy.2017.77">Boy et al 2017</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.healy.2022.379">Healy et al 2022</a>].</p><p><b>Infantile-onset GA1.</b> Progressive macrocephaly is observed in 75% of affected individuals and may be present prenatally [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.bjugstad.2000.681">Bjugstad et al 2000</a>]. If untreated, 80%-90% of children with infantile-onset GA1 will experience an acute encephalopathic crisis, 95% of which occur in the first 24 months of life. These crises can be precipitated by acute illness, fever, or fasting and catabolic stressors associated with anesthesia and surgical procedures [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.k_lker.2006.840">K&#x000f6;lker et al 2006</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.boy.2023.482">Boy et al 2023</a>]. Approximately 50% of symptomatic individuals have no apparent acute crisis (insidious onset). Disability and mortality are high after acute crises [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.kyllerman.2004.121">Kyllerman et al 2004</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.k_lker.2006.840">K&#x000f6;lker et al 2006</a>].</p><p>Dietary treatment and aggressive treatment during intercurrent illness (see <a href="#glutaric-a1.Management">Management</a>) have reduced the frequency of acute encephalopathic crises and movement disorders to 10%-30%.</p><p>Brain MRI findings in 18 Dutch individuals ages 11 months to 33 years with GA1 (most of whom were diagnosed prior to universal GA1 NBS) include open opercula (n=15), widening of cerebrospinal fluid spaces&#x000a0;/ ventriculomegaly (9), attenuated signal from basal ganglia (8), white matter abnormalities (5), and subdural hemorrhage (1) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.vester.2016.1001">Vester et al 2016</a>].</p><p>Subdural hemorrhage, a rare manifestation of GA1, is probably due to stretching of bridging veins in the enlarged extra-axial fluid spaces in the setting of frontotemporal hypoplasia and macrocrania. Subdural hemorrhages may develop even in individuals diagnosed on NBS, managed appropriately, and without macrocephaly [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.zielonka.2015.1065">Zielonka et al 2015</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.ishige.2017.46">Ishige et al 2017</a>]. Subdural hemorrhages may appear spontaneously or following mild head trauma in GA1; they can also resolve spontaneously. Isolated subdural hemorrhage without other features of GA1 on brain MRI is extremely uncommon [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.vester.2015.405">Vester et al 2015</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.vester.2016.1001">Vester et al 2016</a>].</p><p>Seizures are reported in 7% of individuals with GA1 [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.k_lker.2015a.1041">K&#x000f6;lker et al 2015a</a>]. While self-limited seizures may accompany the acute encephalopathic crisis, in other instances they may be the presenting manifestation [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.mcclelland.2009.235">McClelland et al 2009</a>]. Infantile spasms have been reported in rare instances [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.younglin.2013.e182">Young-Lin et al 2013</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.liu.2015.61">Liu et al 2015</a>].</p><p>When GA1 is diagnosed after the onset of severe neurologic manifestations, outcome is poor and the therapeutic effect of the usual interventions is more limited [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.hoffmann.1996.115">Hoffmann et al 1996</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.bjugstad.2000.681">Bjugstad et al 2000</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.busquets.2000a.315">Busquets et al 2000a</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.kyllerman.2004.121">Kyllerman et al 2004</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.k_lker.2006.840">K&#x000f6;lker et al 2006</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.kamate.2012.31">Kamate et al 2012</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.wang.2014.813">Wang et al 2014</a>]. Nonetheless, therapeutic intervention may prevent additional progressive neurologic deterioration in some [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.hoffmann.1996.115">Hoffmann et al 1996</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.bjugstad.2000.681">Bjugstad et al 2000</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.k_lker.2006.840">K&#x000f6;lker et al 2006</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.badve.2015.61">Badve et al 2015</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.fraidakis.2015.85">Fraidakis et al 2015</a>].</p><p>With early diagnosis and adherence to treatment, 70%-90% of individuals with GA1 remain largely asymptomatic [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.strauss.2011.93">Strauss et al 2011</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.viau.2012.430">Viau et al 2012</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.couce.2013.383">Couce et al 2013</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.lee.2013.61">Lee et al 2013</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.boy.2021.13">Boy et al 2021</a>].</p><p>Insidious onset of manifestations was previously seen in an estimated 10%-20% of symptomatic individuals [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.k_lker.2006.840">K&#x000f6;lker et al 2006</a>]; it now appears to be more common because early diagnosis and treatment of GA1 have reduced the incidence of acute encephalopathic crises [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.boy.2018.970">Boy et al 2018</a>].</p><p>Individuals who adhere to maintenance and emergency treatments are less likely to develop dystonia; those who do not adhere to treatment are at high risk of developing a movement disorder [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.k_lker.2007.357">K&#x000f6;lker et al 2007</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.heringer.2010.743">Heringer et al 2010</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.strauss.2011.93">Strauss et al 2011</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.k_lker.2012.72">K&#x000f6;lker et al 2012</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.boy.2018.970">Boy et al 2018</a>]. Those who have insidious onset generally have less severe movement disorders and less extensive lesions on brain MRI than those with acute encephalopathic crisis [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.boy.2019.117">Boy et al 2019</a>]. The insidious <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> may correlate with lack of adherence to chronic dietary treatment [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.boy.2018.970">Boy et al 2018</a>].</p><p><b>Late-onset GA1.</b> Late-onset GA1 is defined as onset of manifestations after age six years. Some individuals with late-onset GA1 (e.g., mothers diagnosed due to the birth of a child with an abnormal NBS result) are entirely asymptomatic. A number of individuals with late-onset GA1 have been reported with a range of neurologic findings, including individuals with chronic headaches, tremors, cerebral palsy, spastic diplegia, peripheral neuropathy, epilepsy, and dementia [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.herskovitz.2013.849">Herskovitz et al 2013</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.boy.2017.77">Boy et al 2017</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.gelener.2020.179">Gelener et al 2020</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.healy.2022.379">Healy et al 2022</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.baskar.2025.100036">Baskar et al 2025</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.wei.2025.e70281">Wei et al 2025</a>]. The majority of individuals with late-onset GA1 have MRI evidence of frontotemporal hypoplasia and white matter findings, and a few have had subependymal nodules [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.boy.2017.77">Boy et al 2017</a>]. Others have reported clinical and neuroimaging findings [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.k_lkens.2005.2142">K&#x000fc;lkens et al 2005</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.pierson.2015.325">Pierson et al 2015</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.zhang.2017.560">Zhang &#x00026; Luo 2017</a>]. Brain neoplasms have also been reported in several adults and children [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.korman.2007.81">Korman et al 2007</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.burlina.2012.s58">Burlina et al 2012</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.herskovitz.2013.849">Herskovitz et al 2013</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.pierson.2015.325">Pierson et al 2015</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.serrano_russi.2018.276">Serrano Russi et al 2018</a>]. This finding has led to an as-yet-unsubstantiated speculation about possible increased susceptibility to brain neoplasms in adults.</p><p><b>Non-neurologic disease manifestations observed in individuals in GA1 regardless of age of onset.</b> Chronic kidney disease (CKD) may occur in those with GA1, even with adherence to treatment, and may be an extracerebral manifestation in adults with GA1 [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.k_lker.2015b.1059">K&#x000f6;lker et al 2015b</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.baskar.2025.100036">Baskar et al 2025</a>]. CKD has been identified in individuals diagnosed by NBS with late-onset GA1 [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.boy.2018.970">Boy et al 2018</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.baskar.2025.100036">Baskar et al 2025</a>]. A few individuals have had acute kidney failure [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.sauer.2017.52">Sauer et al 2017</a>], although the majority with CKD have not required dialysis, and the clinical relevance of the kidney dysfunction remains unclear.</p><p><b>Note:</b> Infants with biochemical findings consistent with GA1 on NBS but normal blood levels of glutaric acid and 3-hydroxyglutaric acid and only one identifiable <i>GCDH</i> <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> may warrant close clinical follow up. However, given the high <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/sensitivity/">sensitivity</a> of <i>GCDH</i> <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a>, the chances that an infant with these findings is affected and at risk of developing acute striatal necrosis are low.</p></div><div id="glutaric-a1.GenotypePhenotype_Correlatio"><h3>Genotype-Phenotype Correlations</h3><p>Most <i>GCDH</i> variants reported to date are <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/missense/">missense</a> variants [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.schuurmans.2023.371">Schuurmans et al 2023</a>].</p><p><b>GA1 biochemical (excreter) subtypes.</b> GA1 was originally divided into two arbitrarily defined biochemical subtypes: high excreters of urinary glutaric acid and low excreters of urinary glutaric acid [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.baric.1999.867">Baric et al 1999</a>]. High excreters and low excreters are at similar risk for striatal injury [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.christensen.2004.861">Christensen et al 2004</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.k_lker.2006.840">K&#x000f6;lker et al 2006</a>]. While excreter status has no clear correlation with the clinical <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> in childhood, evidence suggests that high excreters have higher concentrations of glutaric acid and 3-hydroxyglutaric acid in the central nervous system and have increased prevalence of progressive white matter lesions on MRI [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.yuan.2023.391">Yuan et al 2023</a>].</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>High excreters have no or very low glutaryl-CoA dehydrogenase activity (0%-3%) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.goodman.1998.141">Goodman et al 1998</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.baric.1999.867">Baric et al 1999</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.busquets.2000b.535">Busquets et al 2000b</a>]. NBS <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/sensitivity/">sensitivity</a> for the high excreter biochemical <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> is 100% [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.boy.2018.970">Boy et al 2018</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Low excreters have up to 30% residual glutaryl-CoA dehydrogenase activity [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.goodman.1998.141">Goodman et al 1998</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.busquets.2000b.535">Busquets et al 2000b</a>], although they may also have very low activity [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.schuurmans.2023.371">Schuurmans et al 2023</a>]. At least one of their <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> <i>GCDH</i> pathogenic variants is a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/hypomorphic/">hypomorphic</a> <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/missense/">missense</a> variant. NBS <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/sensitivity/">sensitivity</a> for the low excreter biochemical <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> is 84% [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.boy.2018.970">Boy et al 2018</a>]. See <a href="/books/NBK546575/table/glutaric-a1.T.gcdh_pathogenic_variants_r/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobglutarica1Tgcdhpathogenicvariantsr">Table 10</a> for details on variants associated with low excreter status.</div></li></ul></div><div id="glutaric-a1.Prevalence"><h3>Prevalence</h3><p>More than 600 individuals with GA1 have been reported to date [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.boy.2021.13">Boy et al 2021</a>]. Prevalence estimates for GA1 vary between 1:30,000 and 1:100,000-110,000 [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.kyllerman.1980.279">Kyllerman &#x00026; Steen 1980</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.lindner.2004.851">Lindner et al 2004</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.tsai.2017.253">Tsai et al 2017</a>].</p><p>Details on founder variants reported in Ojibway-Cree First Nation Canadians of Manitoba and Ontario, South African Xhosa peoples, Pennsylvania Amish, Lumbee Native Americans of North Carolina, and Irish Traveler communities in the Republic of Ireland are included in <a href="/books/NBK546575/table/glutaric-a1.T.gcdh_pathogenic_variants_r/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobglutarica1Tgcdhpathogenicvariantsr">Table 10</a>.</p></div></div><div id="glutaric-a1.Genetically_Related_Allelic"><h2 id="_glutaric-a1_Genetically_Related_Allelic_">Genetically Related (Allelic) Disorders</h2><p>No phenotypes other than <b>Developmental Delay / Intellectual Disability Management Issues</b>
</h5><p>The following information represents typical management recommendations for individuals with developmental delay&#x000a0;/ intellectual disability in the United States; standard recommendations may vary from country to country.</p><p><b>Ages 0-3 years.</b> Referral to an early intervention program is recommended for access to occupational, physical, speech, and feeding therapy as well as infant mental health services, special educators, and sensory impairment specialists. In the US, early intervention is a federally funded program available in all states that provides in-home services to target individual therapy needs.</p><p><b>Ages 3-5 years.</b> In the US, developmental preschool through the local public school district is recommended. Before placement, an evaluation is made to determine needed services and therapies and an individualized education plan (IEP) is developed for those who qualify based on established motor, language, social, or cognitive delay. The early intervention program typically assists with this transition. Developmental preschool is center based; for children too medically unstable to attend, home-based services are provided.</p><p><b>All ages.</b> Consultation with a developmental pediatrician is recommended to ensure the involvement of appropriate community, state, and educational agencies (US) and to support parents in maximizing quality of life. Some issues to consider:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>IEP services:</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>An IEP provides specially designed instruction and related services to children who qualify.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>IEP services will be reviewed annually to determine whether any changes are needed.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Special education law requires that children participating in an IEP be in the least restrictive environment feasible at school and included in general education as much as possible, when and where appropriate.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Physical therapy, occupational therapy, and speech services will be provided in the IEP to the extent that the need affects the child's access to academic material. Beyond that, private supportive therapies based on the affected individual's needs may be considered. Specific recommendations regarding type of therapy can be made by a developmental pediatrician.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>As a child enters the teen years, a transition plan should be discussed and incorporated in the IEP. For those receiving IEP services, the public school district is required to provide services until age 21.</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>A 504 plan (Section 504: a US federal statute that prohibits discrimination based on disability) can be considered for those who require accommodations or modifications such as front-of-class seating, assistive technology devices, classroom scribes, extra time between classes, modified assignments, and enlarged text.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Developmental Disabilities Administration (DDA) enrollment is recommended. DDA is a US public agency that provides services and support to qualified individuals. Eligibility differs by state but is typically determined by diagnosis and/or associated cognitive/adaptive disabilities.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Families with limited income and resources may also qualify for supplemental security income (SSI) for their child with a disability.</div></li></ul></div><div id="glutaric-a1.Motor_Dysfunction"><h5>
<b>Motor Dysfunction</b>
</h5><p>
<b>Gross motor dysfunction</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Physical therapy is recommended to maximize mobility and to reduce the risk for later-onset orthopedic complications (e.g., contractures, scoliosis, hip dislocation).</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Consider use of durable medical equipment and positioning devices as needed (e.g., wheelchairs, walkers, bath chairs, orthotics, adaptive strollers).</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>For muscle tone abnormalities including hypertonia or dystonia, consider involving appropriate specialists to aid in management of baclofen, tizanidine, botulinum toxin, anti-parkinsonian medications, or orthopedic procedures.</div></li></ul><p><b>Fine motor dysfunction.</b> Occupational therapy is recommended for difficulty with fine motor skills that affect adaptive function such as feeding, grooming, dressing, and writing.</p><p><b>Oral motor dysfunction</b> should be assessed at each visit and clinical feeding evaluations and/or radiographic swallowing studies should be obtained for choking/gagging during feeds, poor weight gain, frequent respiratory illnesses, or feeding refusal that is not otherwise explained. Assuming that the child is safe to eat by mouth, feeding therapy (typically from an occupational or speech therapist) is recommended to help improve coordination or sensory-related feeding issues. Feeds can be thickened or chilled for safety. When feeding dysfunction is severe, an NG-tube or G-tube may be necessary.</p><p><b>Speech, language, and communication issues.</b> Speech-language evaluation should be considered early in development for children who have delayed communication milestones or who are not yet talking. Evaluation for alternative means of communication (e.g., <a href="https://www.asha.org/NJC/AAC/" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">augmentative and alternative communication</a> [AAC]) is appropriate for individuals who have speech or receptive and expressive language difficulties. An AAC evaluation should be completed by a speech-language pathologist who has expertise in the area. This evaluation typically takes into account cognitive abilities, sensory impairments, and motor skills to determine the most appropriate form of communication. AAC devices can range from low-tech, such as picture exchange communication, to high-tech, such as voice-generating devices. Contrary to popular belief, AAC devices do not hinder verbal development of speech, but rather support optimal speech and language development. Many children will continue to require AAC into later childhood and adulthood, while some may use their AAC for a shorter time to help aid speech and language development.</p></div><div id="glutaric-a1.NeurobehavioralPsychiatric_C"><h5>

formal published recommendations for dietary or medical management for pregnant women with GA1, it is recommended that care be provided by a multidisciplinary team including the treating obstetrician, a metabolic physician, and a specialist metabolic dietician. Because the perinatal period is a time of high catabolic stress for women with GA1, most metabolic physicians would agree that emergency management and close observation are required; however, evidence and/or sufficient clinical data regarding efficacy or necessity of emergency treatment for GA1 during the peripartum period are not available. Uneventful clinical courses for affected mothers (and their babies) have been reported for women receiving emergency treatment during the peripartum period [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.ituk.2013.141">Ituk et al 2013</a>], as well as for women who did not receive any specific therapy [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.garcia.2008.569">Garcia et al 2008</a>].</p><p>While to date no specific guidelines are available for surgical procedures and other perinatal stressors, usual perioperative/perianesthetic precautions are likely to be clinically relevant (see <a href="/books/NBK546575/table/glutaric-a1.T.glutaric_acidemia_type_1_o/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobglutarica1Tglutaricacidemiatype1o">Table 6</a>).</p></div><div id="glutaric-a1.Therapies_Under_Investigatio"><h3>Therapies Under Investigation</h3><p>Search <a href="https://clinicaltrials.gov/" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">ClinicalTrials.gov</a> in the US and <a href="https://www.clinicaltrialsregister.eu/ctr-search/search" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">EU Clinical Trials Register</a> in Europe for access to information on clinical studies for a wide range of diseases and conditions. </p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If both parents are known to be <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for a <i>GCDH</i> <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>, each sib of an affected individual has at conception a 25% chance of being affected, a 50% chance of being an asymptomatic <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier/">carrier</a>, and a 25% chance of being unaffected and not a carrier.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> of GA1 can vary widely among untreated sibs who have the same <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genotype/">genotype</a>.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Heterozygotes (carriers) are asymptomatic and are not at risk of developing the clinical features of the disorder.</div></li></ul><p><b>Offspring of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>.</b> Unless an individual's reproductive partner also has GA1 or is a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier/">carrier</a>, offspring will be obligate heterozygotes (carriers) for a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> in <i>GCDH</i>.</p><p><b>Other family members.</b> Each sib of the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>'s parents is at a 50% risk of being a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier/">carrier</a> of a <i>GCDH</i> <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>.</p></div><div id="glutaric-a1.Carrier_Detection"><h3>Carrier Detection</h3><p>Carrier testing for at-risk relatives requires prior identification of the <i>GCDH</i> pathogenic variants in the family.</p><p>Quantification of glutaryl-CoA dehydrogenase enzyme activity in fibroblasts or leukocytes is not useful in determining <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier/">carrier</a> status.</p></div><div id="glutaric-a1.Related_Genetic_Counseling_I"><h3>Related Genetic Counseling treatment.</p><p>
<b>Family planning</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The optimal time for determination of genetic risk and discussion of the availability of prenatal/<a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a> is before pregnancy.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>It is appropriate to offer <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genetic-counseling/">genetic counseling</a> (including discussion of potential risks to offspring and reproductive options) to young adults who are affected, are carriers, or are at risk of being carriers.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Carrier testing should be considered for the reproductive partners of known carriers and for the reproductive partners of individuals affected with GA1, particularly if both partners are of the same ancestry. Glutaric acidemia type 1 (GA1) is caused by insufficiency or absence of functional GCDH, resulting from <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> <i>GCDH</i> pathogenic variants. Enzymatic insufficiency or absence results in the accumulation of upstream byproducts of L-lysine, L-hydroxylysine, and L-tryptophan degradation: glutaric acid, 3-hydroxyglutaric acid, glutarylcarnitine (C5DC), and glutaconic acid.</p><p>Accumulation of glutaric acid and 3-hydroxyglutaric acid causes neurotoxicity (especially striatal injury). Glutaric acid probably derives from hydrolysis of the accumulated enzyme substrate (glutaryl-CoA), but the origin of 3-hydroxyglutaric acid remains unknown. It is unclear whether putative toxins cross the blood-brain barrier or are synthesized within the brain from accumulated glutaryl-CoA. The reasons why one or both preferentially affect the striatum and why there is a period of heightened striatal vulnerability in infancy and early childhood remain a mystery.</p><p>More than 200 (confirmed or likely) pathogenic <i>GCDH</i> variants have been reported to date [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.stenson.2020.1197">Stenson et al 2020</a>]. Most <i>GCDH</i> variants reported to date are <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/missense/">missense</a> variants. It is possible that many of these pathogenic variants affect stability and heteromeric GCDH enzyme complex formation, and are disruptive to mitochondrial architecture.</p><p>Note: The Ojibway-Cree First Nation <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/founder-variant/">founder variant</a> c.91+5G&#x0003e;T as well as p.Arg227Pro, p.Val400Met, and p.Met405Val are associated with a low-excreter <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> and may be more difficult to detect conclusively with biochemical testing (and on NBS utilizing C5DC). Homozygous p.Arg227Pro and p.Val400Met variants are both associated with 8%-10% residual enzyme activity [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.christensen.2004.861">Christensen et al 2004</a>].</p><p>The c.553_570del18 (p.Gly185_Ser190del) <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/deletion/">deletion</a>, a suspected <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/dominant-negative/">dominant-negative</a> <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/allele/">allele</a>, is associated with enzyme activity much lower than 50% [<a class="bk_pop" href="#glutaric-a1.REF.bross.2012.787">Bross et al 2012</a>]. An individual <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for the deletion did not manifest any clinical features of GA1 [A Larson, personal observation].</p><p><b>Mechanism of disease causation.</b> Loss of function</p><div id="glutaric-a1.T.gcdh_pathogenic_variants_r" class="table"><h3><span class="label">Table 10. </span></h3><div class="caption"><p><i>GCDH</i> Pathogenic Variants Referenced in This <i>GeneReview</i></p></div><p class="large-table-link" style="display:none"><span class="right"><a href="/books/NBK546575/table/glutaric-a1.T.gcdh_pathogenic_variants_r/?report=objectonly" target="object">View in own window</a></span></p><div class="large_tbl" id="__glutaric-a1.T.gcdh_pathogenic_variants_r_lrgtbl__"><table class="no_bottom_margin"><thead><tr><th id="hd_h_glutaric-a1.T.gcdh_pathogenic_variants_r_1_1_1_1" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">Reference<br />Sequence</th><th id="hd_h_glutaric-a1.T.gcdh_pathogenic_variants_r_1_1_1_2" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">DNA Nucleotide<br />Change</th><th id="hd_h_glutaric-a1.T.gcdh_pathogenic_variants_r_1_1_1_3" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">Predicted Protein<br />Change</th><th id="hd_h_glutaric-a1.T.gcdh_pathogenic_variants_r_1_1_1_4" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">Comment [Reference]</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td headers="hd_h_glutaric-a1.T.gcdh_pathogenic_variants_r_1_1_1_1" scope="row" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">
