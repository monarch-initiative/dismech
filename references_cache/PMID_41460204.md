PURPOSE: Mutant IDH1 (mIDH1) defines a therapeutically targetable subtype of 
intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), with the mIDH1 inhibitor ivosidenib 
approved for advanced disease. A subset of patients experiences prolonged 
disease stabilization; however, the molecular basis for eventual progression 
remains poorly defined.
EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN: We performed molecular profiling of matched baseline and 
postprogression circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) samples from patients with mIDH1 
ICC enrolled in the ClarIDHy phase III trial. Functional studies were conducted 
to characterize candidate resistance mechanisms.
RESULTS: Longitudinal ctDNA analysis of 18 patients treated with ivosidenib for 
>6 months revealed emergent genomic alterations in multiple cases. Acquired 
mutations in mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway genes (KRAS, NRAS, 
MAP2K1, NF1) were identified in five cases, with instances of concurrent 
alterations and/or high variant allele fractions (VAF). Additional candidate 
resistance events included a secondary IDH1 mutation and a hotspot IDH2 
mutation, detected at low VAF in the same patient. Functional studies showed 
that these IDH mutations conferred sustained 2-hydroxyglutarate production and 
ivosidenib resistance, whereas MAPK activation blunted gene expression induced 
by ivosidenib plus IFNγ, a key therapeutic output of mIDH1 inhibition. In 
parallel, baseline ctDNA profiling of 81 patients revealed that ARID1A mutations 
and elevated mIDH1 VAF were associated with reduced clinical benefit.
CONCLUSIONS: MAPK pathway alterations represent a recurrent mechanism of 
resistance to mIDH1 inhibition in ICC, whereas emergent IDH1/IDH2 mutations 
appear infrequent. Functional data suggest that MAPK-mediated resistance may 
involve impaired IFN signaling. These results support MAPK-directed combination 
strategies and highlight the utility of ctDNA profiling to identify predictive 
and resistance biomarkers in mIDH1-driven ICC.

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is an aggressive malignancy of the bile ducts within the liver (1). Although relatively rare, with an estimated 0.3 to 2 cases per 100,000 population annually, its incidence has been increasing steadily over the past several decades (2, 3). Most patients present with advanced disease, where there are few therapeutic options and the prognosis is poor. Although the addition of immunotherapy to first-line chemotherapy (TOPAZ-1) has led to modest improvements in survival, durable responses remain elusive, and effective second-line treatment options are limited (4, 5).

Despite these clinical challenges, targeted therapies have shown promise in molecularly defined subsets of ICC (6). Up to 25% of patients harbor gain-of-function somatic mutations in the metabolic enzymes IDH1 or IDH2, most commonly affecting the hotspot residues R132 (IDH1) and R172 (IDH2; refs. 7–9). These alterations typically arise early in tumor evolution as a clonal event defining a distinct molecular subset of ICC (10–16). IDH1/2 mutations are found across multiple additional tumor types—including acute myeloid leukemia (AML), low-grade glioma, and chondrosarcoma (17)—and drive tumorigenesis through a unique metabolic mechanism. The neomorphic activity of the mutant IDH1 (mIDH1) enzyme converts alpha-ketoglutarate (αKG) to the oncometabolite D-2-hydroxyglutarate (2HG; ref. 18). Accumulation of 2HG competitively inhibits αKG-dependent enzymes, including key regulators of epigenetics and cell metabolism (19–21), leading to epigenetic dysregulation, impaired differentiation, and immune evasion (21–25).

Ivosidenib is a first-in-class, oral small-molecule inhibitor of mIDH1, approved for patients with previously treated IDH1-mutant cholangiocarcinoma (CCA). In the ClarIDHy phase III study, ivosidenib prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) compared with placebo [median PFS, 2.7 vs. 1.4 months; HR, 0.37 (95% CI, 0.25–0.54); one-sided P value < 0.001] and median OS, 10.3 vs. 5.1 months after adjusting for the 70% crossover rate; adjusted HR, 0.49 (95% CI, 0.34–0.70), one-sided P value < 0.001; refs. 26, 27). Ivosidenib also reduced plasma 2HG concentrations by up to 98%, restoring concentrations comparable with those seen in healthy individuals (28).

Although these findings establish the clinical activity of ivosidenib in IDH1-mutant CCA, all patients eventually relapse. In AML, where complete or partial responses (PR) to mIDH1 or mIDH2 inhibitors are frequently observed (29–31), resistance mechanisms have been extensively characterized. These include second-site mutations that impair drug binding as well as isoform switching—emergence of IDH2 mutations in patients treated with mIDH1 inhibitors and vice versa (32–34). Other resistance-associated alterations affect chromatin regulators, hematopoietic cell fate transcription factors, and receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)–mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling genes (35, 36). By contrast, the molecular underpinnings of acquired resistance in CCA remain poorly understood. Although secondary IDH1 and IDH2 mutations have been reported in a small number of patients with CCA (37–39), their overall frequency and spectrum are not well defined. These gaps underscore the need for a deeper understanding of acquired resistance in IDH1-mutant ICC to inform further therapeutic development.

In this study, we investigated the dynamics of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) in patients with IDH1-mutant CCA enrolled in the ClarIDHy trial to characterize the landscape of genetic alterations that emerge with acquired resistance to ivosidenib. We further evaluated the potential functional relevance of de novo acquired alterations. In addition, we examined whether baseline comutations and ctDNA abundance were associated with clinical outcomes, aiming to identify predictors of ivosidenib response and resistance.

This study was conducted according to the International Council for Harmonisation of Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval from the Institutional Review Board and independent ethics committee was obtained by all study investigators. Informed consent was obtained from all patients included in the study.

The ClarIDHy trial was a phase III, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study of ivosidenib in previously treated patients with nonresectable or metastatic cholangiocarcinoma with an IDH1 mutation (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT02989857). The study was conducted from February 2017 to June 2021 across institutes in France, Italy, South Korea, Spain, the UK, and the United States; the study design and methodology have been previously published (26, 27). These publications include full details on attrition and subject dropout, sex as a biological variable, age and weight distributions, blinding and bias-reduction strategies, and statistical power analysis. The full protocol, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and study design are available at https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02989857.

Enrolled patients were adults with unresectable or metastatic, histopathologically confirmed diagnoses of cholangiocarcinoma with a documented IDH1 mutation based on a fresh or recently banked tumor biopsy [Oncomine Focus Assay for IDH1R132 (C/H/G/L/S) in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue (Thermo Fisher Scientific)]. Other eligibility criteria included an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score of 0 or 1, a measurable lesion as defined by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST v1.1), documented disease progression following at least one and no more than two prior systemic regimens for advanced disease, including at least one gemcitabine or 5-FU–containing regimen, and an expected survival of ≥3 months.

Eligible patients were randomly assigned 2:1 to receive ivosidenib 500-mg oral tablets or a matched placebo once daily in continuous 28-day cycles. Randomization was stratified by the number of prior systemic treatment regimens for advanced disease (one or two). Treatment continued until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, confirmed pregnancy, withdrawal of consent, death, loss to follow-up, study unblinding, or the end of the study. The placebo subjects who continued to be eligible for the study after disease progression were allowed to cross over to take ivosidenib.

Blood samples for the evaluation of IDH1 mutations and other genes in ctDNA were drawn at screening, on days 1 and 15 of cycles 1 to 3, on day 1 of subsequent cycles, and at the end-of-treatment (EOT) visit. Blood samples were drawn predose for the measurement of 2HG on days 1 and 15 of cycle 1 and on day 1 of each subsequent cycle (28).

The primary endpoint was PFS assessed by independent radiology review and was defined as the time from randomization to the first documentation of disease progression or death. Responses were categorized as complete response, PR, stable disease (SD), or progression according to RECIST v1.1 criteria (40). Exploratory objectives addressed by the current analyses were to evaluate levels of mIDH1 and other genes in ctDNA obtained from plasma at baseline and over the course of the treatment (Supplementary Table S1). The representativeness details of this study are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Genomic analyses of ctDNA were conducted on plasma samples from baseline and at the EOT (if these were available) using the PGDx elio plasma complete assay (Personal Genome Diagnostics Inc.). This is a hybrid capture, next-generation sequencing (NGS)–based assay with a 521-gene panel designed to detect single-nucleotide variants, insertions and deletions, and selected amplifications and translocations (41). It has a sensitivity of 0.1% to 0.3% for the detection of targeted IDH1 mutations.

Longitudinal analysis of ctDNA for the IDH1 gene was conducted by Digital Droplet PCR (ddPCR) using the BEAMing digital PCR assay (refs. 29, 42; OncoBEAM, Sysmex Inostics), which has a sensitivity of 0.02% to 0.04% for the detection of mIDH1 alleles. Results are expressed as the fraction of mutant to wild-type (WT) DNA alleles present in a particular sample. This fraction is calculated by dividing the number of beads containing the mutated DNA by the total number of beads with PCR product. IDH1 mutation clearance was defined as the reduction in mIDH1 variant allele fraction (VAF) to below the limit of detection of the ddPCR assay for at least one on-study time point.

For longitudinal analysis (Fig. 1B–E), cfDNA was extracted from plasma using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (QIAGEN) with 60 minutes of proteinase K incubation at 60°C. All other steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA template (up to 10 μL, with a total of 20 ng for each specimen) was added to 10 μL of ddPCR Supermix for Probes (Bio-Rad) and 2 μL of the custom primer/probe mixture. This reaction mix was added to a DG8 cartridge together with 60 μL of Droplet Generation Oil for Probes (Bio-Rad) and used for droplet generation. Droplets were then transferred to a 96-well plate (Eppendorf) and thermal cycled with the following conditions: 5 minutes at 95°C, 40 cycles of 94°C for 30 seconds, 55°C for 1 minute, followed by 98°C for 10 minutes (ramp rate 2°C/second). Droplets were analyzed with the QX200 Droplet Reader (Bio-Rad) for fluorescent measurement of FAM and HEX probes. Gating was performed on the basis of positive and negative controls, and mutant populations were identified. The ddPCR data were analyzed with QuantaSoft analysis software (Bio-Rad, RRID:SCR_025321) to obtain the fractional abundance of the mutant DNA alleles in the WT/normal background. The quantification of the target molecule was presented as the number of total copies (mutant plus WT) per sample in each reaction. Fractional abundance (FA) is calculated as follows: FA % = [Nmut/(Nmut + Nwt) × 100], where Nmut is the number of mutant events and Nwt is the number of WT events per reaction. ddPCR analysis of normal control plasma DNA (from cell lines) and no DNA template controls were always included. ddPCR was performed on samples from patients 1, 6, 7, 14, 15, and 17. Samples from patients 6 and 7 had low DNA quantity and did not show amplification. Probe and primer sequences are available upon request.

Baseline plasma samples were available from 81 of 126 patients treated with ivosidenib and 40 of 61 patients randomized to placebo from the ClarlDHy trial (Supplementary Table S1). All patients had preexisting IDH1 mutations confirmed in tumor tissue (see Supplementary Methods for full study details). To investigate potential genetic alterations associated with acquired resistance to ivosidenib, we analyzed 18 patients who received ivosidenib for at least 6 months and had matched baseline and EOT plasma samples (Fig. 1A). Four of these patients experienced clinical or radiographic progression before 6 months but continued to derive clinical benefit and remained on ivosidenib treatment beyond that time point. For the remaining 14 patients, posttreatment samples were collected within 60 days of clinical or radiological progression (mean, 15 days). Among this cohort, three patients had a best response of PR and 15 had SD (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Table S1).

To characterize acquired gene mutations and copy-number alterations, we performed NGS of ctDNA from paired baseline and EOT plasma samples using a commercial 521-gene panel (see Materials and Methods). Pathogenic variant alleles that were not detected at baseline but were observed at EOT were identified in 13/18 patients (Fig. 1A, see Materials and Methods). Among these 13 patients, the IDH1R132X driver mutation was detected in baseline plasma in seven cases, increasing confidence that the co-occurring resistance-associated mutations in those patients were truly acquired during treatment. In the remaining cases, the absence of baseline detection likely reflects either limited NGS assay sensitivity or low tumor DNA shedding, making it more difficult to distinguish acquired from preexisting alterations.

Oncogenic alterations affecting components of the MAPK signaling pathway were common, occurring in 6/18 patients at EOT (33%). These alterations included activating mutations in KRAS, NRAS, and MAP2K1, as well as loss-of-function mutations in the tumor suppressor NF1 (Fig. 1A). Multiple MAPK pathway mutations were detected in two patients.

Pathogenic TP53 mutations were observed in four patients, with the IDH1 driver mutation detected in plasma at baseline in three of these cases. In addition, one patient acquired both a hotspot IDH2R172T mutation and a secondary IDH1G131V mutation (present in cis with the IDH1R132C driver mutation; Fig. 1A). Moreover, inactivating mutations in lineage regulators—including APC, the transcription factors WT1 and SMAD4, and the histone methyltransferase KMT2C—were each observed in one patient, as was an activating mutation in PIK3CA (Fig. 1A). Notably, MAPK pathway alterations exhibited higher overall VAF—with four of eight mutations exceeding 0.9% VAF (range, 0.34%–6.42%)—compared with other mutation types, none of which exceeded 0.7% VAF (0 of 11; range, 0.15%–0.64%).

To better define the temporal dynamics of candidate resistance mutations and address the sensitivity limitations of the NGS assay, we next performed ddPCR on longitudinal plasma samples from selected patients. This approach enabled the quantification of resistance-associated mutations in ctDNA relative to the IDH1 driver mutation and the evaluation of their emergence in relation to disease burden and clinical progression. Calculating the VAF ratio—that is, the VAF of the resistance-associated mutation relative to the IDH1 driver mutation—provided insight into the size of resistant subclones and their potential role in driving progression. Because IDH1R132X mutations are typically early, clonal events in ICC, their persistence provides a stable molecular reference for overall tumor burden, allowing emerging variants to be interpreted as subclonal expansions within the IDH1-mutant background (10–16). We focused on MAPK pathway alterations based on their frequent emergence across the cohort.

We analyzed serial samples from six patients in whom MAPK pathway mutations were detected by NGS at EOT but not at baseline. Evaluable ddPCR data were obtained from four patients; the remaining two lacked sufficient ctDNA quantity or quality. In three of these four patients, MAPK variants were either undetectable (patients 1 and 15) or present at very low VAF ratios at earlier time points (patient 17, VAF ratio <0.1) but became robustly detectable before EOT (Fig. 1B–D). In patient 1, NRASQ61R emerged during treatment with a VAF ratio of 0.44 (0.74% VAF vs. 1.67% VAF for the IDH1 driver mutation, Fig. 1B). In patient 15, NF1V2431* was first detectable before radiological progression and reached a VAF ratio of 0.68 at the final on-treatment time point (Fig. 1C). In patient 17, KRASG12D and NRASQ61K were detected with VAF ratios of 0.74 and 0.07, respectively, in the final on-treatment sample (Fig. 1D). In contrast, the remaining patient (patient 6) harbored a KRASG12C mutation detectable by ddPCR at baseline, indicating a preexisting alteration rather than one that emerged during treatment.

We also examined the dynamics of de novo IDH1 and IDH2 mutations, which were detected less often than we initially expected in the plasma NGS assay. ddPCR analysis validated the emergence of both the secondary IDH1G131V mutation and the IDH2R172T mutation observed by NGS in patient 14 at the time of disease progression (Fig. 1E), although both remained at low VAF ratios (<0.012). The limited contribution of these mutations to resistance was further supported by the finding that plasma 2HG levels at EOT were reduced compared with baseline levels in this patient (Fig. 1E, heatmap below).

Collectively, these data indicate that MAPK pathway alterations can emerge during treatment in patients progressing on ivosidenib and may reach high VAF ratios by EOT, suggesting that they contribute substantially to the resistant disease burden in a subset of cases. However, because MAPK alterations are present at baseline in ∼20% of patients with IDH1-mutant ICC—including some who derived clinical benefit from ivosidenib in the present study and prior reports (38, 39)—these mutations likely enhance clonal fitness in a context-specific manner rather than uniformly driving resistance. By contrast, acquired IDH1 or IDH2 mutations were rare (1/18 patients) and at low VAF, suggesting a limited role in ivosidenib resistance. Likewise, TP53, WT1, APC, SMAD4, KMT2C, and PIK3CA mutations showed low VAF by NGS; given their low abundance and limited sample material, they were not prioritized for ddPCR validation and are unlikely to represent major resistance drivers.

We next asked whether baseline genomic alterations detected in ctDNA were associated with clinical outcomes among the 81 ivosidenib-treated patients with available plasma NGS data (Fig. 2A). The most common alterations identified by the plasma NGS assay included inactivation of TP53 (n = 16, 20%), ARID1A (n = 10, 12%), and PBRM1 (n = 9, 11%) and activation of PIK3CA (n = 9, 11%) and KRAS (n = 6, 7%). MAPK alterations as a group (affecting KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, MAP2K1, and ERRFI1) were present in 17 patients (21%). Among these alterations, baseline ARID1A mutations were associated with a significantly shorter PFS (P value = 0.0056; Fig. 2B). In addition, although baseline MAPK pathway mutations were not significantly associated with overall PFS, none of the 17 patients with these alterations remained on treatment at 1 year, compared with 12 of 81 patients (15%) without such mutations (P value = 0.061, Fisher’s exact test), consistent with a trend toward inferior long-term disease control and in line with findings with the dual mIDH1/2 inhibitor LY3410738 (44).

The level of IDH1 mutations detected in plasma may reflect tumor burden and tumor cell shedding—both of which can be influenced by stage and metastatic pattern—and has the potential to serve as a prognostic marker as well as a dynamic indicator of treatment efficacy. To quantitatively assess the relationship between baseline plasma mIDH1 VAF and clinical outcomes, we conducted mIDH1-specific ddPCR analysis on all available samples. This ddPCR assay has a sensitivity of 0.02% to 0.04% for the detection of mIDH1 alleles. Baseline mIDH1 VAF ranged from undetected to 9.5%. Among patients in the ivosidenib arm, baseline mIDH1 VAF lower than the median mIDH1 VAF of 1.039% were associated with longer PFS (P value = 0.0035; Fig. 2C). In contrast, there was no association between baseline mIDH1 VAF and PFS in the placebo arm (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Additionally, baseline mIDH1 VAF correlated with plasma 2HG levels (Supplementary Fig. S1B).

We next evaluated the dynamics of mIDH1 VAF in serial plasma samples relative to treatment response. Clearance of mIDH1—defined as the absence of detectable IDH1 mutation at one or more time points during treatment—was observed in 8/60 patients (13%) in the ivosidenib arm and was associated with improved PFS (P value = 0.021; Fig. 2D). mIDH1 clearance occurred as early as 28 days after treatment initiation [Fig. 2E (upper/middle); Supplementary Fig. S1C] and was only seen in patients with low baseline VAF (Supplementary Fig. S1D). Among the eight patients with clearance, four showed reemergence of the mIDH1 clone by the time of progression [two of whom (patients 1 and 3) demonstrated earlier transient reappearance]. Four patients lacked detectable mIDH1 at progression despite radiographic disease, potentially reflecting low tumor shedding or sampling limitations. No cases of mIDH1 clearance were observed in the placebo group [Fig. 2E (lower)]. Collectively, these data, as well as prior findings (45), demonstrate that plasma mIDH1 VAF levels—at baseline and during treatment—have prognostic relevance in patients with mIDH1 ICC.

We next sought to functionally test the two classes of candidate resistance mutations: (i) IDH1 second-site and IDH2R172T hotspot mutations and (ii) mutations in the MAPK pathway.

In preclinical models of ICC and other types of solid tumors, targeting mIDH1/2 does not result in proliferative arrest or cell death in vitro, consistent with evidence that mIDH1 is not a conventional mitogenic or antiapoptotic oncogene (25, 46). By contrast, mIDH1 inhibition demonstrates preclinical efficacy in a GEMM of IDH1R132C-driven ICC [Albumin-Cre; KrasG12D; Idh1R132C (CKI model)] and in associated allografts, in part due to promoting antitumor immunity (25, 43). To test whether Idh1 second-site and Idh2 R172 mutations can abrogate this effect, we introduced the IDH1G131V/R132C and IDH2R172T alleles into a GEMM-derived 2205 cell line (Fig. 3A shows a schematic of the study). In control cells, 1 μmol/L ivosidenib reduced intracellular 2HG to background levels; in contrast, 2HG levels remained high in CKI cells ectopically expressing Idh1G131V/R132C or Idh2R172T, regardless of ivosidenib treatment (Fig. 3B). The baseline 2HG levels resulting from the expression of Idh1G131V/R132C were comparable with those observed upon the expression of Idh1R132C (Supplementary Fig. S2), in contrast to the reduced 2HG accumulation reported in the context of the IDH1R132H/D279N resistance allele (39).

Ivosidenib treatment suppressed the growth and proliferation of control 2205 tumors, whereas both 2205-Idh1G131V/R132C and 2205-Idh2R172T tumors were unresponsive (Fig. 3C–E). The promotion of antitumor immunity by mIDH1 inhibition involves tumor cell–intrinsic mechanisms, including the restoration of response to the T-cell–derived cytokine IFNγ (25, 43). This effect is mediated by the reactivation of the TET2 DNA demethylating enzyme, a 2HG target that is required for the induction of IFNγ-stimulated genes. MAPK signaling has also been linked to the suppression of the IFNγ response by multiple mechanisms (47–50). As our murine model is driven by combined Kras and Idh1 mutations, we first turned to the human ICC cell line SNU1079 (IDH1R132C)—which lacks comutations in MAPK pathway genes—to investigate the impact of newly introduced MAPK alterations on IFNγ-induced gene expression. These cells were transduced with doxycycline-inducible lentiviral vectors expressing mutant KRAS alleles, WT KRAS, and an empty vector [Fig. 4A (top) ]. SNU1079-EV and SNU1079-KRASG12D cells were cultured in doxycycline-supplemented media and treated with vehicle or ivosidenib plus IFNγ (see Materials and Methods). We then performed qRT-PCR for a series of genes previously found to be induced by this combined treatment in parental SNU1079 cells (25), including factors involved in antiviral and immune signaling processes (e.g., IFIT1/2, OAS1/2, CXCL9/10). Notably, whereas baseline expression was comparable between SNU1079 derivatives, the induction of these genes by ivosidenib plus IFNγ was markedly attenuated in SNU1079-KRASG12D cells (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Fig. S3B).

To test an alternative mechanism of MAPK pathway activation, we evaluated the ivosidenib/IFNγ response in the context of NF1 inactivation. SNU1079 cells were engineered by CRISPR/Cas9 to disrupt NF1, and experiments were performed using the resulting pooled, nonclonal population, which showed a ∼70% reduction of NF1 protein compared with control. Similar to the effects observed with KRASG12D, NF1 loss suppressed the induction of ivosidenib/IFNγ target genes (Supplementary Fig. S4A–S4C). Collectively, these data show that MAPK activation blunts ivosidenib/IFN-stimulated gene expression in mIDH1 ICC cells, supporting a mechanism by which MAPK gene mutations may confer resistance.

Finally, we asked whether pharmacologic MAPK pathway inhibition could restore the ivosidenib/IFN response in mIDH1 cell lines harboring endogenous activating MAPK mutations. To this end, we used KRAS-mutant human and murine ICC cell lines—RBE (IDH1R132S; KRASG12V) and 4305 (Idh1R132C; KrasG12D). Notably, KRAS inhibition—using the pan-RAS inhibitor RMC-7977 in RBE cells and the KRASG12D inhibitor MRTX1133 in 4305 cells (Supplementary Fig. S5A shows signaling)—markedly enhanced the transcriptional induction of target genes in response to ivosidenib plus IFNγ treatment (Fig. 4B and C; Supplementary Fig. S5B and S5C). These findings suggest that MAPK signaling blockade may help restore IFN-related therapeutic responses in mIDH1 ICC harboring genomic alterations in MAPK pathway genes.

Understanding acquired resistance to ivosidenib in IDH1-mutant ICC has been largely limited to case reports of secondary IDH1 mutations or the emergence of IDH2 mutations. Through integrated analysis of serial patient samples and functional studies in ICC models, our findings highlight the broader relevance of acquired genomic alterations that activate MAPK signaling, which emerged more frequently and reached higher VAF ratios than IDH1/2 mutations. MAPK activation suppressed a key therapeutic response program—IFN-stimulated gene expression—suggesting a distinct mechanistic contribution to resistance. Collectively, these data provide new insight into the genomic basis for acquired resistance in IDH1-mutant ICC and support the exploration of therapeutic combinations targeting MAPK pathway alterations to delay or overcome resistance to ivosidenib.

For several classes of small-molecule targeted therapies, acquired resistance commonly arises through on-target mutations or amplifications or through bypass alterations within the same signaling pathway. Similarly, second-site IDH1 mutations and hotspot IDH2R172X mutations have been observed in a subset of patients with ICC treated with ivosidenib, sometimes at high VAF ratios. Functional studies presented here and in prior work (37, 39) show that these alterations sustain elevated 2HG levels in preclinical models. However, our findings suggest that these events are uncommon clinically, arising in only 1/18 patients with a VAF ratio <0.012. The reasons for the rarity of these on-target resistance mechanisms remain unclear but are unlikely to reflect incomplete target suppression, as pharmacodynamic analyses demonstrated that ivosidenib reduced plasma 2HG to levels observed in healthy individuals. The scarcity of acquired IDH1/IDH2 resistance mutations has important implications for drug development strategies. Rather than motivating the iterative development of mIDH1 or dual IDH1/2 inhibitors with broader coverage of such variants, our findings suggest that combination strategies should be prioritized to prevent or overcome resistance through alternative mechanisms.

In this regard, our study implicates genomic alterations in the RTK-MAPK pathway as a key mechanism of acquired ivosidenib resistance. Mutations in this pathway emerged in five out of 18 patients, with high VAF ratios in three cases (VAF ratios of 0.44–0.74 relative to the IDH1 driver mutation). Similar RTK-MAPK pathway alterations have been recurrently observed in patients with mutant-IDH inhibitor-resistant AML (32, 35, 36). As the MAPK pathway has pleiotropic cell biological functions, its role in mIDH1 inhibitor resistance is likely multifaceted. In ICC models, we show that MAPK activation dampens the transcriptional program induced by ivosidenib plus IFNγ treatment, which we have previously shown to be central to the drug’s mechanism of action (25, 43). RTK-MAPK mutations have been shown to suppress tumor cell-intrinsic IFN responses in other cancer types through several mechanisms, including inhibition of JAK-STAT signaling and transcriptional downregulation of IFN pathway components (47–52). In addition to suppressing IFN signaling, MAPK activation can reprogram cellular metabolism and stress response pathways that are coupled to the mIDH1 state—such as redox balance, glutamine utilization, and mitochondrial function. Further studies will be needed to define how RTK-MAPK activation suppresses the IFN response in mIDH ICC and to establish the functional contribution of impaired IFN signaling and other RTK-MAPK–mediated processes to therapeutic resistance.

RTK-MAPK alterations are not absolute determinants of resistance, as some patients with ICC harboring baseline RTK-MAPK alterations have been observed to experience clinical benefit from mIDH inhibitor treatment (38, 39). Understanding the basis for the context-specific impact of RTK-MAPK signaling on mIDH inhibitor efficacy will require further investigation. Overall, these findings suggest that combined ivosidenib and RTK-MAPK targeting could improve outcomes in patients with mIDH1 ICC harboring baseline or acquired RTK-MAPK alterations. Notably, ivosidenib is well tolerated and seems particularly suitable for use in combination treatment strategies.

Our data also suggest additional mechanisms of acquired resistance to ivosidenib, including mutations in TP53 and PIK3CA and in lineage-regulating factors (WT1, APC, SMAD4, and KMT2C). Although the VAF for these events were low in each case, the loss-of-function mutations in the lineage regulators are reminiscent of AML, in which the loss of developmental transcription factors is a major mechanism of resistance. In ICC, the induction of a hepatocyte-like transcriptional signature in on-treatment versus pretreatment biopsies correlates with ivosidenib treatment efficacy. Thus, it will be important to explore the functional impact of the loss of these lineage regulators on treatment responses.

Although the above findings highlight convergent resistance mechanisms between AML and ICC, another mechanism described in AML—the expansion of non-IDH–mutant clones—warrants consideration in ICC. In AML, this feature reflects the clonal architecture, in which IDH mutations can arise later in disease progression (35, 36, 53). In ICC, IDH1 mutations are early, clonal events in most tumors (10–16), but in the minority (∼10 to 20%) where they are subclonal or later acquired, the expansion of WT IDH clones under selective therapeutic pressure remains a plausible resistance route.

The clinical benefit rate for ivosidenib in previously treated IDH1-mutant CCA is ∼50% (2.4% PR, 50.8% SD), underscoring the need to identify biomarkers of primary resistance and response. Our data (Fig. 2), together with other studies (44, 45), highlight several candidates. Baseline ARID1A and MAPK pathway mutations are associated with inferior outcomes, emphasizing the importance of systematically assessing the tumor biological effects of concurrent genomic alterations in mIDH1 CCA. However, because clinical benefit from ivosidenib has also been observed in some patients harboring ARID1A or MAPK pathway alterations, these genotypes should not be regarded as absolute exclusion criteria but rather as potential modifiers of response (38). In addition, both low baseline mIDH1 VAF and on-treatment mIDH1 ctDNA clearance correlate with improved outcomes. These features likely reflect lower tumor burden, although additional studies will be required to confirm the relationship among VAF, disease burden, and tumor localization.

The limitations of our study include the small sample size. Among 80 patients treated with ivosidenib, we selected 18 who received therapy for >6 months and were therefore considered most likely to have acquired resistance changes. Larger cohorts will be required to definitively establish the frequency and spectrum of resistance-associated alterations. In addition, more sensitive ctDNA assays may further refine the resistance landscape. On the other hand, the low VAF and/or failure to detect acquired mutations in a subset of patients could suggest that clinical features of response to mIDH1 inhibition (infrequent frank tumor regressions) might reduce the selective pressure for the development of genetic drivers of resistance. Finally, further functional studies are needed to understand the key outputs of RTK-MAPK signaling contributing to resistance. Nevertheless, the present data advance our understanding of clinical resistance to ivosidenib in ICC, highlighting the prominence of acquired RTK-MAPK pathway alterations while suggesting a more limited role for the emergence of IDH mutations.
