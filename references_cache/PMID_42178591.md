Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a highly heterogeneous endocrine-metabolic 
disorder whose core pathological features-follicular atresia and ovulatory 
dysfunction-are attributed to functional failure of ovarian granulosa cells 
(GCs). Although autophagy (a cellular quality-control mechanism) and ferroptosis 
(iron-dependent lipid peroxidation-driven cell death) have been shown to 
independently contribute to the pathophysiology of PCOS, their interactive 
networks and regulatory mechanisms within specific pathological 
microenvironments remain to be systematically elucidated. This review aims to 
systematically unravel this overlooked micro-pathogenic network. The dysfunction 
of GCs in PCOS is not an isolated event involving a single mode of cell death, 
but rather a pathological crosstalk arising from the disruption of the dynamic 
equilibrium between autophagy and ferroptosis. This paper elucidates the 
pathological mechanisms driving this cascade of disturbances through four 
distinct pathways: the intracellular stress hub formed by the synergistic action 
of oxidative stress (OS) and endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) serves as the 
initiating factor. This is compounded by the gut-ovarian axis's long-range 
amplification effect, mediated by gut microbiome (GM) dysregulation and 
low-grade chronic inflammation (LGI), collectively disrupting cellular 
homeostasis. while circadian rhythm disruption and epigenetic reprogramming, 
respectively, cement this imbalance at the temporal and molecular memory levels. 
Moreover, we have elucidated the specific execution pathways of 
autophagy-dependent ferroptosis, namely how selective autophagy (e.g., 
ferritinophagy and mitophagy) acts as a key amplifier of ferroptosis by 
exacerbating iron overload and energy collapse. On this basis, this paper 
explores therapeutic strategies ranging from upstream restoration of signal 
homeostasis to downstream precise inhibition of autophagy-ferroptosis 
interaction nodes. This review aims to provide novel theoretical perspectives 
for fundamentally reversing follicular atresia in PCOS and improving patients' 
reproductive outcomes by re-examining these lethal interactions at the 
microscopic level.

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a complex endocrine disorder affecting reproductive, metabolic and psychological health, impacting approximately 6% to 12% of women of reproductive age worldwide, with specific prevalence rates varying according to diagnostic criteria [1–4]. The clinical phenotype of PCOS exhibits considerable heterogeneity, typically characterised by hyperandrogenemia (HA), ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM) [5, 6]. In the International Evidence-Based Guidelines for the Assessment and Management of Polycystic Ovary Syndrome, the diagnostic pathway for adults has been optimised: anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) has been incorporated as a serum surrogate marker for PCOS [7]. It is noteworthy that AMH is only applicable as a diagnostic substitute in adults and is not suitable for use in adolescents. Furthermore, AMH and ultrasound examinations should not be employed concurrently to define PCOS. However, given the limited specificity due to physiological fluctuations during adolescence, the diagnostic criteria for adolescents are restricted to the concurrent presence of HA and ovulatory dysfunction. In addition to reproductive system abnormalities, PCOS frequently co-occurs with metabolic disorders such as insulin resistance (IR), obesity, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), significantly increasing the long-term risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) and endometrial cancer [8–12]. Moreover, the high incidence of psychological disorders such as anxiety and depression further exacerbates the deterioration of the ovarian microenvironment [1, 13]. Although current clinical management can alleviate symptoms to some extent, the dysfunction of ovarian GCs-recognised as the pathological core driving follicular arrest and atresia-remains fundamentally unresolved [14]. GCs, as primary regulators of the follicular microenvironment, provide essential energy and steroid hormone support to oocytes. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying the collapse of GCs' survival homeostasis is crucial for unravelling the pathogenesis of PCOS.

Recent studies indicate that the fate of GCs depends upon the intricate regulation of intracellular programmed cell death networks. Autophagy, as a cellular protective mechanism, eliminates damaged organelles via the lysosomal pathway to maintain energy homeostasis [15–17]. However, within the pathological context of PCOS, it frequently undergoes maladaptive transformation, shifting from a survival signal to a driver of cell death [18–21]. Excessive autophagic flux not only directly induces cell death but may also act as an accomplice to other forms of cell death. Concurrently, ferroptosis-a form of regulatory cell death driven by iron dependency and lipid peroxidation (LPO)-has also been demonstrated to be a significant contributor to ovarian damage in PCOS [22–24]. However, a critical theoretical gap currently exists: clinical and experimental evidence indicate that simply inhibiting autophagy or blocking ferroptosis often fails to fully reverse ovarian damage in PCOS. This limitation suggests that within the complex pathological microenvironment of PCOS, these two forms of cell death do not operate in isolation but may instead be integrated into a more lethal synergistic process.

Increasing evidence supports the hypothesis that within the PCOS microenvironment, autophagy and ferroptosis form a tightly coordinated lethal network through shared key signalling hubs. On the one hand, upstream stress signals simultaneously activate excessive autophagy and increased ferroptosis susceptibility. On the other hand, selective autophagy-such as ferritinophagy, also termed autophagy-dependent ferroptosis-directly contributes to the abnormal activation of ferroptosis [25, 26]. Therefore, the dynamic imbalance in the autophagy-ferroptosis axis is likely the key molecular switch determining the fate of PCOS GCs. This review will systematically analyse the crosstalk between autophagy and ferroptosis and elucidate its upstream mechanisms across four dimensions: intracellular stress, systemic inflammation, chronobiology, and epigenetics. Finally, based on this profound understanding of the underlying mechanism network, we explore potential therapeutic strategies targeting this crosstalk axis. By re-examining these lethal interactions at the microscopic level, we aim to provide a novel theoretical perspective for deepening our understanding of the molecular origins of follicular atresia in PCOS.

Autophagy, a highly conserved lysosomal degradation pathway in eukaryotic cells, maintains ovarian homeostasis by sequestering, degrading, and recycling cellular components. Autophagy in mammals primarily encompasses microautophagy, macroautophagy, and chaperone-mediated autophagy [27]. Among these, macroautophagy (hereafter referred to as autophagy) may be categorised into non-selective autophagy and selective autophagy (e.g., ferritinophagy, clockophagy, and mitophagy) [28]. To elucidate the mechanism of autophagy imbalance in PCOS GCs, we have categorised the dynamic process into three successive stages, which also serve as the structural basis for assessing autophagy flux. The unc-51-like autophagy-activating kinase 1 (ULK1) complex responds to upstream signals (e.g., nutritional status or stress) and becomes activated. It subsequently phosphorylates Beclin-1, recruiting PI3K complexes, including VPS34, to the initiation sites of autophagic membranes, thereby initiating vesicle nucleation [29]. Subsequently, mediated by the ATG5-ATG12-ATG16L1 complex, LC3-I binds to phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) and is converted into the membrane-associated form LC3-II [15, 30]. LC3-II drives autophagosome membrane extension and closure, serving as a key biomarker for detecting autophagic activity [31]. Autophagosomes fuse with lysosomes via proteins such as lysosomal membrane-associated protein 2 (LAMP2), forming autophagolysosomes. Their contents (including the cargo receptor SQSTM1/p62) are then degraded by hydrolases and recycled [20, 32, 33].

Throughout the ovarian physiological cycle, a finely regulated autophagy flux permeates the entire process, encompassing primordial follicle activation, dominant follicle selection, and corpus luteum formation [19, 34]. However, within the pathological microenvironment of PCOS, autophagy no longer functions solely as a protective mechanism but undergoes a non-adaptive transformation exhibiting marked cell-type specificity (Fig. 1) [20, 28]. This differential remodelling of the autophagic flux constitutes the dual pathological basis for ovarian dysfunction in PCOS. In ovarian GCs, autophagy is pathologically hyperactivated. Both clinical samples and animal models confirm that autophagy flux markers (LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, Beclin-1) are significantly upregulated in PCOS GCs, whilst substrate proteins SQSTM1/p62 are excessively depleted [35]. This excessive autophagic flux, exceeding the cellular adaptation threshold, no longer serves energy recycling but instead transforms into a lethal signal, accelerating apoptosis and loss of germinal centres. This process directly impairs the metabolic support GCs provide to oocytes, leading to arrest of follicular development at the small follicular stage. This ultimately results in characteristic ovulatory dysfunction and PCOM [18, 36, 37]. In stark contrast, the autophagic flux in theca cells (TCs) is blocked at a late stage or defective [38]. The high-insulin and high-lipotoxicity environment impairs the fusion and degradation functions of autophagolysosomes in TCs, leading to the pathological accumulation of damaged proteins and organelles. This autophagic defect drives the key molecular mechanism of androgen overproduction by promoting the expression and stability of cytochrome P450-17α-hydroxylase (CYP17A1) through activation of the reactive oxygen species (ROS)/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway [39, 40]. Moreover, impaired autophagy activity in the endometrium and skeletal muscle has been demonstrated to correlate closely with reduced endometrial receptivity and local IR [41, 42]. The transition of autophagy from homeostasis to a pathological state in PCOS is governed by a complex regulatory network shaped by metabolic dysregulation and microenvironmental stress. Centred on the PI3K/protein kinase B (AKT)/mammalian target of rapamycin(mTOR) pathway as its molecular hub, this network integrates input signals from multiple regulatory factors, including PTEN/p53 and MAPK, collectively determining the fate of GCs.

The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway serves as the pivotal hub linking insulin signalling to autophagy regulation. Under physiological conditions, insulin binds to receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) on the cell membrane, inducing tyrosine phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1). This process specifically recruits and activates PI3K [43]. Activated PI3K catalyzes the conversion of phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) into phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate (PIP3), which subsequently activates AKT. This cascade ultimately activates the mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), which directly inhibits autophagy initiation by phosphorylating ULK1, thereby maintaining autophagy at a low basal level [44, 45]. However, in the insulin-resistant state of PCOS, activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is diminished, contributing to metabolic and hormonal dysregulation [35, 46, 47]. The mechanism lies in the fact that HA and free fatty acids (FFAs) induce serine phosphorylation of IRS-1, thereby blocking normal PI3K/AKT signalling. This IR-induced signal conduction blockage relieves mTORC1's negative regulation of autophagy, leading to GCs initiating abnormally high levels of autophagy even under compensatory hyperinsulinemia (HINS) conditions [35, 48]. More insidiously, HA further exacerbates IR by downregulating IRS-1 expression, thereby establishing a vicious cycle involving HA, IR, and autophagy [49].

Beyond the metabolic axis, stress signalling networks further amplify this imbalance. As a negative regulator of the PI3K pathway, phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) exhibits abnormally high expression in PCOS GCs [50–52]. Its lipid phosphatase activity directly antagonises AKT activation by dephosphorylating PIP3 to generate PIP2 [53]. Overexpression of PTEN blocks survival signals and, by inhibiting mTORC1 activity, elevates autophagy flux in an insulin-independent manner. This elevation accelerates autophagic cell death in GCs [50, 54]. Concurrently, OS and mitochondrial damage activate the MAPK pathway, which, in turn, leads to a significant accumulation of the transcription factor p53 in the nucleus [18, 55, 56]. Nuclear p53 directly activates autophagy-promoting genes and inhibits mTOR activity. It also increases PTEN expression through positive feedback, thereby forming a p53-PTEN-autophagy feedforward loop [18, 31, 57–59]. Increased expression of the mitochondrial damage-sensing molecule PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1) is also observed in PCOS GCs, and PINK1 mediates excessive mitophagy via the PINK1/Parkin pathway [60]. As a result, this dysregulation of selective autophagy fails to eliminate damaged mitochondria, leading to cellular energy collapse and redox imbalance.

Multiple studies have consistently shown that dysregulation of autophagy in PCOS is not attributable to a single molecular anomaly, but rather results from the combined effects of impaired PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathways and stress-induced activation of PTEN/p53/MAPK pathways. The interwoven interactions of these signalling hubs translate systemic metabolic disorder and local microenvironmental stress into persistent and lethal autophagic signals within GCs.

Ferroptosis is a regulated form of cell death driven by iron-dependent phospholipid peroxidation, arising from an imbalance in the interplay among iron metabolism, lipid metabolism, and antioxidant systems. Iron is an essential trace element for cellular life processes, with its homeostasis maintained through a dynamic equilibrium of uptake, storage, and efflux [23]. Under physiological conditions, circulating trivalent iron (Fe3+) binds to transferrin (TF) and is subsequently recognised by the transferrin receptor complex (TFRC/TFR1) before undergoing endocytosis [61, 62]. Within the nucleolus, Fe3+ is first reduced to ferrous iron (Fe2+) by the six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of prostate 3 (STEAP3). Next, Fe2+ is transported into the cytoplasm by the divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1), where it participates in various metabolic reactions. Once in the cytoplasm, DMT1 may either release Fe2+ to form the labile iron pool (LIP) or export it from the cell via ferroportin (FPN/SLC40A1) [63]. Finally, ferritin (Fn), comprising ferritin light chain (FTL) and ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), is responsible for oxidising and storing excess Fe2+, thereby limiting the toxicity of free iron [64].

However, this iron metabolic equilibrium is disrupted within the pathological microenvironment of PCOS, leading to iron overload in GCs. Mechanistically, this manifests as a metabolic profile characterised by increased uptake and reduced efflux within the GCs: upregulation of TFRC and DMT1 enhances iron uptake, whilst downregulation of FPN limits iron efflux [65–67]. It is noteworthy that elevated Fn levels are commonly observed in patients with PCOS, particularly in those with concomitant obesity or IR. Regarding whether elevated Fn levels exist independently of body mass index (BMI), current research findings remain inconclusive. Elevated Fn levels correlate positively with the severity of ovarian dysfunction and may further promote ferroptosis and the activation of associated ferritinophagy processes [24, 68]. Potential mechanisms underlying this phenomenon include reduced iron loss due to prolonged menstrual cycles, hypomenorrhoea, or amenorrhoea; increased iron retentio; and diminished ferritin secretion, which in turn enhances intestinal iron absorption [66, 69]. This iron overload environment is further exacerbated by HA: HA activates ferritinophagy mediated by Nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4). Ferritinophagy, as a form of selective autophagy, is crucial for regulating ferroptosis [24]. NCOA4, acting as an autophagy receptor, is significantly upregulated in PCOS GCs. Its specific targeting of fibronectin for degradation within lysosomes results in substantial release of stored iron into the lysosomal membrane [23, 67, 70, 71]. This redox-active iron pool catalyzes the generation of lipid ROS through the Fenton reaction, where Fe2+ reacts with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) to produce hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and hydroxyl ion (OH−), initiating peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in membrane phospholipids [66, 72]. Moreover, iron overload in PCOS patients may further exacerbate IR-associated hepatic iron overload, thereby stimulating iron absorption and ferritin synthesis whilst simultaneously inhibiting ferroportin-mediated iron efflux, ultimately forming a mutually reinforcing pathological cycle [67]. Research has identified additional regulators of iron metabolism. Heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1), regulated by Nrf2 and HIF-1α, undergoes excessive activation that degrades haem into Fe2+, thereby inducing ferroptosis [73, 74]. HO-1 exerts a dual role in ferroptosis, potentially dependent on the extent of ferritinophagy and Nrf2 levels [75]. Heat shock family B member 1 (HSPB1), Iron response element-binding protein 2 (IREB2), and Solute carrier family 40 member 1 (SLC40A1) also participate in regulating iron metabolism and ferroptosis susceptibility [76, 77]. This systemic iron metabolism disorder not only directly impairs follicular development but is also closely associated with pregnancy complications and adverse pregnancy outcomes [78, 79].

Disrupted lipid metabolism and its derivative LPO represent a pivotal pathway in ferroptosis, centred upon the abnormal accumulation and oxidative modification of PUFAs within cellular phospholipids [80, 81]. Membrane phospholipids are rich in PUFAs, such as arachidonic acid (AA), which serve as key substrates for LPO. In PCOS ovarian GCs, the abnormal metabolism and oxidative modification of PUFAs are significantly amplified through both enzymatic and non-enzymatic pathways.

Within the enzymatic pathway, long-chain acyl-coenzyme A synthetase family member 4 (ACSL4) acts as the key rate-limiting enzyme, catalysing the conversion of PUFAs into PUFA-CoA [8]. Subsequently, lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3) conjugates it with PE to form PUFA-PE, providing oxidisable substrates for ferroptosis. These substrates undergo peroxidation catalysed by lipoxygenases (LOXs) or cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase (POR) [82, 83]. This persistent and uncontrolled oxidative process not only depletes PUFAs within membrane phospholipids but also alters the structural integrity of cell membranes, constituting a pivotal step in triggering ferroptosis. In PCOS, LOXs family activity is enhanced through multiple mechanisms. Fe2+-containing 5-LOX promotes PUFA peroxidation [63, 67]. Overexpression of the circadian gene PER1 suppresses sterol regulatory element binding transcription factor 2 (SREBF2) and activates arachidonate 15-lipoxygenase (ALOX15) [84]. Moreover, p53 activation further exacerbates PUFAs' peroxidation in the cell membrane by inhibiting SLC7A11 and activating arachidonate 12-lipoxygenase (ALOX12) [85]. Conversely, the substantial release of AA from ferroptotic cells can further activate LOXs in neighbouring cells, thereby establishing a vicious cycle [86].

In non-enzymatic pathways, iron overload within GCs drives Fenton reactions that generate substantial amounts of •OH. These radicals directly attack the side chains of PUFAs, producing toxic byproducts such as malondialdehyde (MDA) and thereby promoting ferroptosis [63]. These byproducts, in turn, facilitate iron release, establishing a self-perpetuating cycle of iron overload and LPO. Interestingly, monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) exert protective effects by competitively occupying membrane sites. However, the enzymes responsible for their synthesis-acyl-CoA synthetase 3 (ACSL3) and stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD)-are frequently downregulated in PCOS, further diminishing the cells' lipid defence capabilities [80].

During iron-induced cell death, the antioxidant defence system of GCs exerts a decisive protective role. Its core component is the cystine/glutamic acid reverse transporter (System Xc−)/glutathione (GSH)/glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) pathway, alongside non-GSH-dependent replenishment pathways.

System Xc− is a heterodimeric amino acid antiporter composed of the catalytic subunit SLC7A11 and the regulatory subunit SLC3A2. It is responsible for the endocytic uptake of extracellular cystine (Cys) into the cell, exchanging it for an equivalent amount of glutamate [87]. The ingested Cys is rapidly reduced to cysteine within the cell, providing a key precursor for GSH synthesis [8]. As the most important direct antioxidant within the cell, GSH serves as an essential cofactor for GPX4. Under the catalysis of GPX4, GSH provides reducing power to convert lethal cytotoxic lipid hydroperoxides (L-OOH) into non-toxic lipid alcohols (L-OH), whilst simultaneously oxidising itself into glutathione disulfide (GSSG), thereby blocking the ferroptosis pathway [88, 89]. GPX4 is the sole enzyme in mammalian cells capable of directly reducing membrane phospholipid peroxides, and its activity is highly dependent on GSH homeostasis maintained by System Xc− [8, 62]. Consequently, System Xc−, GSH synthesis, and GPX4 collectively form a crucial defence against OS-induced damage [90]. In addition to the core factor GPX4, non-GSH-dependent antioxidant systems also play a crucial role in counteracting ferroptosis. Among these, ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) is a nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate(NADPH)-dependent oxidoreductase that translocates to the plasma membrane via N-myristoylation and continuously produces reduced coenzyme Q10 (ubiquinol). CoQ10 is a lipophilic radical-trapping antioxidant that directly sequesters lipid peroxyl radicals, therefore preventing their propagation along phospholipids containing PUFAs [91]. Dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH), localised to the inner mitochondrial membrane, specifically blocks mitochondrial ferroptosis by regulating the synthesis of CoQ10H2 [92].

However, in PCOS, the aforementioned antioxidant defence systems-particularly the core System Xc−/GSH/GPX4 axis-exhibit significant functional disruption. This disruption constitutes a pivotal mechanism driving GCs towards ferroptosis and forms the focal point of this section's discussion. SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression is markedly downregulated in PCOS GCs [93]. Specifically, inhibition of SLC7A11 directly leads to restricted cysteine uptake and impaired GSH synthesis, causing GPX4 to become inactivated due to substrate deficiency [72]. The ensuing ROS accumulation further suppresses SLC7A11 transcription, creating a vicious cycle [8, 87]. The complex signalling network of PCOS exacerbates disruption of this metabolic axis: research reveals that overexpression of neural precursor cell-expressed developmentally down-regulated 4-like (NEDD4L) promotes GPX4 ubiquitination and degradation via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway [94].

The transcription factor nuclear factor-erythroid-2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is frequently impaired in PCOS, leading to reduced transcription of its target genes (SLC7A11 and GPX4) [95–97]. A study found that downregulation of miR-128-3p in GCs of PCOS mice mediates the overexpression of CSF1, thereby activating the MAPK/p38/JNK pathway to suppress Nrf2 expression and, consequently, inhibit SLC7A11 transcription [93]. Moreover, the pro-apoptotic factor p53 directly inhibits SLC7A11 transcription, establishing an antagonistic relationship with Nrf2 [30, 98]. PCOS GCs face multiple disruptions to their core and auxiliary antioxidant defence systems. In particular, systemic failure of the System Xc−/GSH/GPX4 axis results in cells losing their final line of defence against LPO.

In summary, iron overload, LPO, and impaired antioxidant systems form a synergistic regulatory axis that collectively drives ferroptosis in PCOS ovarian GCs [90]. Within PCOS-a highly heterogeneous disorder-abnormally activated ferroptosis not only leads to follicular atresia and ovulatory dysfunction but may also induce local immune inflammatory responses by releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), thereby exacerbating HA and PCOM [93, 99].

The intricate pathological crosstalk between autophagy and ferroptosis constitutes the core driver of functional impairment in GCs within PCOS ovaries. This lethal cascade unfolds within a multidimensional pathological microenvironment. We shall elaborate how intracellular stress hubs, the gut-ovarian inflammatory axis, circadian rhythm disruption, and epigenetic reprogramming synergistically disrupt the autophagy-ferroptosis equilibrium. This concerted action establishes a self-amplifying vicious cycle that ultimately propels GCs beyond their survival threshold towards irreversible exhaustion.

Within the pathological microenvironment of PCOS, the survival fate of granulosa cells ultimately hinges upon their capacity to withstand internal stress. OS and ERS don’t represent two parallel pathological trajectories but rather converge through complex disruption of calcium homeostasis and metabolic disturbance to form an intracellular stress hub. It is precisely the synergistic drive of this hub that breaches the homeostasis threshold of GCs, triggering lethal crosstalk between autophagy and ferroptosis.

In PCOS patients, HAs, IR, and lipotoxicity constitute a multi-pronged assault on the stress response hub. HAs and IR trigger explosive ROS production by activating NADPH oxidases (NOX) and disrupting the mitochondrial electron transport chain [71, 100, 101]. Moreover, excessive FFA levels, accumulation of oxidised low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL), and haemoglobin (Hb) release due to menstrual irregularities further amplify the cascade of oxidative damage [102]. Clinical evidence indicates that ROS levels in GCs are nearly three times higher in PCOS patients than in healthy controls [103, 104]. This persistent oxidative microenvironment not only directly leads to the accumulation of lipid peroxidation products such as malondialdehyde (MDA), but also acts as a positive feedback regulator exacerbating PCOS pathology: ROS promote androgen synthesis by upregulating CYP17 activity and suppressing hepatocyte nuclear factor-4α (HNF-4α) expression, while simultaneously impairing insulin signalling pathways to worsen IR [8, 105]. ROS induces calcium overload by oxidatively modifying endoplasmic reticulum (ER) calcium channels, disrupting mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), and leading to excessive activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR) [106–109]. Conversely, sustained ERS leads to excessive Ca2⁺ influx into mitochondria, triggering irreversible opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (MPTP) and increasing ROS leakage [110–114]. Moreover, sustained activation of the inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1α) pathway further exacerbates ROS production, establishing a bidirectional feedback loop between ROS and the ERS [115, 116]. This vicious cycle between OS and ERS, mediated by IRE1α and Ca2⁺ influx, marks the critical transition point for GCs from adaptive survival to irreversible death programmes.

Driven by this stress hub, the signalling networks within GCs are remodelled. This collective change leads to the synchronous activation of autophagy and ferroptosis (as shown in Fig. 3). Among these, the Protein kinase R (PKR)-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK)/eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α)/activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4)/C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) signalling axis plays a central regulatory role in this crosstalk. Under sustained stress, PERK dissociates from its molecular chaperone, the 78-kDa glucose-regulated protein (GRP78), undergoes autophosphorylation, and activates eIF2α. This process inhibits global protein translation and induces high expression of the transcription factor ATF4 [117–119]. On the one hand, ATF4 upregulates CHOP, driving non-adaptive, excessive autophagy that depletes organelles [120–122]. On the other hand, ATF4 has been demonstrated to be a transcriptional activator of the ferroptosis-critical enzyme ACSL4; its upregulation significantly elevates ACSL4 expression while inhibiting GPX4 activity, thereby promoting the incorporation of PUFAs into membrane lipids and providing abundant substrates for LPO [123, 124]. Moreover, whilst PERK aims to exert antioxidant effects by promoting Nrf2 nuclear translocation and HO-1 overexpression, it may paradoxically enhance the Fenton reaction under specific conditions by increasing LIP via haem degradation [109, 125]. This intricate regulatory network ultimately triggers ferroptosis in GCs, thereby exacerbating PCOS symptoms in rats [14]. Concurrently, the stress hub synergistically dismantles the cell's antioxidant defences. Under physiological conditions, p62 competitively binds Keap1, releasing Nrf2 and initiating the transcription of antioxidant genes, such as GPX4 [101, 102, 126]. However, in PCOS, this protective mechanism is disrupted. OH generated by iron metabolism disorders impair the Keap1-Nrf2 interaction, while ERS inhibits p62-mediated Keap1 degradation [127]. Research confirms that, despite enhanced autophagy, p62-mediated degradation of Keap1 is impaired, thereby obstructing Nrf2 nuclear translocation [128]. Concurrently, iron metabolism disorders trigger Fenton reactions producing substantial amounts of •OH, which further oxidatively modify Keap1 and disrupt the Keap1-Nrf2 interaction [129]. This dual impairment compromises Nrf2-driven antioxidant defences, and p62 deficiency synergistically disengages protection against ferroptosis. Under such extreme stress, specific forms of selective autophagy become the direct executors of the ferroptosis programme. Both ROS and ERS synergistically upregulate NCOA4 expression. Multiple studies indicate that ROS can upregulate the autophagy marker LC3-II and activate NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy [26]. ERS further amplifies this process via the PERK/eIF2α/ATF4/CHOP pathway [130]. Within the hyperandrogenic microenvironment of PCOS, overexpressed NCOA4 specifically targets FTH1 for lysosomal degradation, resulting in substantial release of free iron [71]. This iron overload directly catalyses LPO via the Fenton reaction, triggering ferroptosis [14]. Moreover, mitochondria serve not only as the primary site for ROS production but also as the foremost target of oxidative damage. The mitochondrial injury induced by HA and IR forms a vicious cycle with OS [100]. T They disrupt UPR function and increase protein aggregation, leading to greater accumulation of the UPR in mitochondria and excessive activation of the ERS [40, 131]. This process induces excessive activation of the PINK1/Parkin pathway, leading to large-scale mitochondrial degradation (mitophagy) and further releasing mitochondrial iron stores [60]. The synergistic effect of lysosomal and mitochondrial iron release rapidly triggers the Fenton reaction within ROS-rich microenvironments [77, 100]. Consequently, under OS-ERS conditions, selective autophagy effectively supplies both the essential iron source and lethal lipid substrates for ferroptosis, forming a tightly interdependent network.

In summary, the intracellular stress hub established by OS and ERS in PCOS GCs serves as an upstream driver of the synergistic induction of autophagy and ferroptosis. This lethal cascade, progressing from co-activated signalling to mechanistic interdependence, not only directly leads to GCs depletion and follicular atresia, but its accumulated toxic lipid peroxides may also be transmitted across cells via gap junctions, further compromising oocyte quality. This microscopic mechanism explains the clinical phenomena of reduced fertilisation rates and impaired embryonic developmental potential observed in PCOS patients. Consequently, targeting the upstream vicious cycle of OS-ERS may represent a critical window of opportunity to deconstruct the downstream autophagy-ferroptosis vicious cycle.

The hallmark features of PCOS-HA, IR, and lipotoxicity (FFAs/ox-LDL)-constitute an upstream multifaceted assault that synergistically induces substantial ROS accumulation within GCs. Excessive ROS generated by compromised mitochondria attack the endoplasmic reticulum, triggering the UPR; conversely, Ca2⁺ leakage from the ERS leads to its overload within mitochondria, further exacerbating mitochondrial damage and ROS surges. The stress network jointly directs the activation of autophagy and ferroptosis through two key pathways. ERS activates the PERK/eIF2α/ATF4 signalling axis, which, on the one hand, upregulates ACSL4 to promote membrane lipid reprogramming of PUFAs, and on the other hand, upregulates Beclin-1 and LC3-II to drive maladaptive excessive autophagy. Impaired function of the P62/Keap1/Nrf2 axis, with blocked nuclear translocation of Nrf2, leads to suppression of anti-iron death gene transcription and compromised antioxidant defence systems. Moreover, under signal-driven conditions, NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy synergises with PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy to transport ferritin and damaged mitochondria to lysosomes for degradation. This leads to the release of substantial free Fe2⁺ from lysosomes, which undergoes Fenton reactions with accumulated ROS, triggering LPO and subsequent ferroptosis.

The fate of PCOS GCs is governed not only by local ovarian stress but also profoundly influenced by the systemic immune microenvironment. Gut microbiota dysbiosis is recognised as a key amplifier driving this pathological cascade [133]. Characteristic alterations in the GM of PCOS patients include: reduced microbial α/β diversity, decreased beneficial microbes (e.g., Bacteroidetes), increased pro-inflammatory taxa (e.g., Proteobacteria), reduced levels of butyrate-producing bacteria (e.g., Roseburia) and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and abnormal bile acid metabolism [134–136]. This imbalance in the microbial community structure directly compromises the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier (mucus layer depletion, degradation of tight junction proteins, activation of the lectin pathway), leading to increased intestinal permeability and translocation of lipopolysaccharides (LPS) into the bloodstream [137, 138]. This triggers metabolic endotoxemia and LGI, manifested by persistently elevated levels of pro-inflammatory factors such as tumour necrosis factor α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [137]. Research has confirmed that the vast majority of PCOS patients exhibit markedly elevated inflammatory markers in both local ovarian and peripheral circulation [139, 140]. The severity of this pro-inflammatory microenvironment shows a significant positive correlation with HA and IR, obesity, and psychological disorders [141–149].

When systemic inflammatory signals reach the ovarian microenvironment, they converge upon Toll-like receptor (TLR) and NOD-like receptor (NLR) hubs within GCs, resulting in a severe imbalance between pro-inflammatory pathways and cell-protective pathways. They are capable of recognising specific pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) such as LPS, as well as danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1). LPS, as a PAMP, activates the TLR4 pathway via the lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP) [150, 151]. TLR4 recruits the adaptor protein MyD88, thereby activating multiple downstream pathways. On the one hand, it activates the Iκβ kinase complex (IKK), leading to the phosphorylation and degradation of the Iκβ protein, thereby facilitating the nuclear localisation of Nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB). Subsequently, the liberated NF-κB rapidly translocates to the nucleus, where it binds to specific κβ sequences. This not only initiates the transcription of pro-inflammatory factors but, crucially, promotes the assembly and activation of the NOD-like receptor 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome (comprising NLRP3, ASC, and pro-caspase 1) [152–154]. On the other hand, TLR4 activates the MAPK pathway, thereby activating the aforementioned pathway [155]. NLRP3 activation mediates the induction of caspase-1, thereby promoting the maturation and secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α [156–159]. Crucially, NLRP3 is regulated bidirectionally by autophagy and ferroptosis (Fig. 4). Although autophagy degrades NLRP3 under physiological conditions to maintain homeostasis, this equilibrium is disrupted in the pathological context of PCOS. Research indicates that NLRP3 overexpression acts as a positive regulator of autophagy, upregulating ULK1 to drive excessive autophagy [36, 160]. This inflammation-driven, non-adaptive hyperautophagy is not a protective mechanism; rather, it accelerates cell death by degrading essential organelles and proteins. Concurrently, the accumulation of ROS serves as a key trigger for the activation of both NF-κB and NLRP3 [161, 162]. NLRP3 activation promotes LPO by upregulating ACSL4, thereby directly driving ferroptosis [163–165]. Furthermore, characteristic dysbiosis-such as the enrichment of Bacteroides vulgatus-weakens IL-22's negative regulation of NLRP3 by inhibiting glycodeoxycholic acid synthesis, subsequently exacerbating ferroptosis via the ROS-ACSL4-LPO axis [161, 166]. This process synergises with dysautophagy to collectively exacerbate intestinal epithelial barrier damage. Ferroptosis is not merely an endpoint but an amplifier of inflammation. Ferroptotic cells release substantial quantities of DAMPs (such as HMGB1), directly exacerbating inflammatory responses. HMGB1 exhibits significantly elevated expression in PCOS GCs, correlating positively with IR and inflammatory severity [8, 168, 169]. As a potent TLR4 ligand, extracellular HMGB1 reactivates the TLR4/MAPK/NF-κB pathway, perpetuating a vicious cycle [170, 171]. Moreover, HMGB1 accumulation further exacerbates excessive autophagy in GCs. By impairing insulin signalling pathways through modulation of NF-κB or AKT phosphorylation, it promotes IR development, thereby tightly linking metabolic dysfunction to cell death at the molecular level1 [21, 160]. Concurrently, elevated HMGB1 levels may trigger GCs death through dysregulated autophagy. This impaired autophagy fails to clear lipid peroxides generated by ferroptosis, instead amplifying inflammation via the NF-κB pathway. Consequently, HMGB1 accumulation in PCOS forms a vicious cycle with GCs autophagy and ferroptosis, further exacerbating the disease progression.

Whilst the pro-inflammatory pathway is excessively activated, the silent mating type information regulation 2 homolog 1 (SIRT1) signalling axis-acting as a cellular protective mechanism within PCOS goblet cells-is markedly suppressed [172–174]. SIRT1, an NAD+-dependent deacetylase, plays a pivotal role in maintaining intestinal barrier function and goblet cell homeostasis. Under physiological conditions, it suppresses inflammation by deacetylating NF-κB and maintains mitochondrial function and antioxidant homeostasis by activating the Adenosine monophosphate (AMP) activated protein kinase(AMPK) and Nrf2 [133, 174, 175]. Xuan et al. discovered that the inflammation-associated transcriptional regulator cytoplasmic polyadenylation element binding protein-1 (CPEB1) is overexpressed in PCOS ovarian GCs, thereby inhibiting SIRT1 activity [176]. Reduced SIRT1 expression leads to loss of a series of protective functions. Low SIRT1 expression is closely associated with increased NF-κB acetylation and transcriptional activity, leading to uncontrolled inflammatory cascades [177]. As a negative regulator of autophagy, the absence of SIRT1 fails to effectively deacetylate autophagy-related proteins such as LC3 or to inhibit AMPK activity, further exacerbating excessive autophagy in gastric cancer cells [172, 175, 176]. Beyond this, SIRT1 plays a pivotal role in regulating ferroptosis by deacetylating key proteins involved in the process, thereby enhancing GCs' resistance to ferroptosis. Low SIRT1 expression not only exacerbates inflammatory responses by activating the NF-κB-p65/NLRP3/TNF-α axis but also suppresses ferroptosis induced via the Nrf2/SLC7A11/GPX4 axis [178, 179]. Recent studies demonstrate that knocking down Nrf2 eliminates the inhibitory effect of SIRT1 overexpression on ferroptosis, indicating that inactivation of the SIRT1-Nrf2 axis is a key mechanism underpinning GCs' susceptibility to ferroptosis [180].

In summary, LGI and GM dysregulation constitute a dual-pronged assault driving PCOS GCs damage. By activating the TLR4/NF-κB/NLRP3 axis and suppressing SIRT1, this pathogenic microenvironment establishes a convergence point for pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic signals. It not only induces maladaptive excessive autophagy through transcriptional upregulation and direct NLRP3-mediated activation, but also significantly increases GCs susceptibility to ferroptosis by disrupting antioxidant defence systems. Crucially, DAMPs released during ferroptosis (such as HMGB1) and oxidised lipid mediators further entrench the vicious cycle of inflammation-autophagy-ferroptosis. Ultimately, this pathological superposition, spanning systemic stress and intracellular molecular crosstalk, leads to irreversible functional failure of GCs, accelerating follicular atresia in PCOS.

Circadian rhythms are an endogenous oscillatory system driven by the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) core biological clock in the hypothalamus, which maintains a 24-h physiological cycle through a transcription-translation feedback loop (TTFL) [181]. In addition to the SCN, peripheral tissues, including the ovaries, exhibit tissue-specific circadian rhythms (referred to as peripheral clocks) [182]. At the core of the TTFL mechanism, brain and muscle ARNT-like 1 (BMAL1) and circadian locomotor output cycles kaput (CLOCK) form a heterodimer. This complex binds to the Enhancer (E)-box element within the promoter regions of circadian rhythm regulatory genes, driving the transcription of period (PER1/2), cryptochrome (CRY1/2), and regulatory elements such as REV-ERBs [183, 184]. Following the accumulation of PER and CRY gene products in the cytoplasm to form complexes, these return to the nucleus where they negatively feedback-inhibit the transcriptional activity of BMAL1/CLOCK, thereby establishing a self-sustaining oscillatory network [184, 185]. This network not only regulates the sleep–wake cycle but also coordinates metabolism, hormone secretion, and cell fate decisions through the circadian-metabolic axis. Ovarian GCs possess an autonomous peripheral biological clock, whose rhythmic oscillations serve as a crucial temporal gating mechanism for maintaining follicular microenvironment homeostasis. In PCOS, circadian rhythm disruption manifests not only as systemic abnormalities-including impaired expression of circadian rhythm factors, compromised pineal melatonin synthesis, and prevalent sleep disorders-but also directly drives granulosa cell dysfunction as an upstream pathological factor [186, 187]. Transcriptomic studies confirm that circadian oscillations across the entire genome are significantly impaired in PCOS GCs, exhibiting abnormal upregulation of repressive factors (PER1/2 and CRY1/2) alongside marked downregulation of positive regulators (CLOCK, BMAL1) and homeostasis-maintaining factors (REV-ERBs) [183, 188, 189]. This disruption may further exacerbate reproductive dysfunction and endocrine-metabolic disorders by bidirectionally dysregulating the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis-the body's biological clock [181, 187, 190]. Mechanistically, HA via histone H3 methylation inhibits the BMAL1/nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (Nampt)/NAD+/SIRT1 pathway, thereby downregulating downstream glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) expression and inducing IR [183, 191, 192]. Concurrently, circadian disruption-induced mitochondrial dysfunction promotes ROS accumulation, activating the NF-κB/NLRP3 pathway and exacerbating ovarian microenvironmental LGI. This vicious cycle involving HA, IR and LGI further suppresses the expression of core clock genes, disrupting circadian oscillations in glycolipid metabolism. Thus, core circadian genes serve not only as timekeepers but also as molecular switches determining cellular fate. In PCOS, alterations in their expression patterns directly precipitate the collapse of protective barriers and the activation of lethal programmes, synergistically driving autophagy and ferroptosis.

Beyond defensive deficiencies, abnormalities in specific clock genes precisely trigger ferroptosis by mediating distinct forms of selective autophagy. CLOCK plays a pivotal role in regulating mitochondrial dynamics. In PCOS GCs, reduced CLOCK expression not only impacts steroidogenesis genes but also disrupts transcriptional regulation of genes associated with mitochondrial fission, fusion, and autophag. This disruption induces abnormal mitophagy, manifesting as either abnormal accumulation or excessive clearance of damaged mitochondria, leading to a surge in ROS and undermining mitochondrial homeostasis [184]. Furthermore, CRY1 abnormalities are closely linked to iron metabolism disorders. Research reveals that abnormally expressed CRY1 in PCOS influences NCOA4 stability via the ubiquitin–proteasome system [185]. This process enhances NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy, accelerating lysosomal degradation of Fn and triggering explosive release of free iron. This iron overload directly catalyses LPO through the Fenton reaction, serving as the detonator for ferroptosis.

In contrast to the previously mentioned downregulation of genes, PER1 is significantly upregulated in PCOS GCs. Acting as an active pro-apoptotic factor, PER1 accelerates cell death through a dual mechanism. Firstly, PER1 directly promotes LPO. It achieves this by specifically inhibiting the transcription of the lipid metabolism transcription factor SREBF2, thereby releasing the regulatory constraints on the lipoxygenase ALOX15 [84]. The abnormal upregulation of ALOX15 directly catalyses the peroxidation of membrane phospholipids, a pivotal step in driving ferroptosis. Secondly, PER1 expression levels correlate positively with autophagy activity, and its overexpression further elevates autophagy flux in GCs. Consequently, elevated PER1 expression simultaneously accelerates both high autophagy and LPO levels, acting as a key disruptor in the pathological progression of PCOS. SIRT1 is transcriptionally regulated by core clock genes (BMAL1/CLOCK/PER/CRY), serving as a central hub connecting these pathways [19, 192, 198]. On one hand, SIRT1 inhibits autophagosome formation and maturation by deacetylating LC3 or suppressing the AMPK pathway [60, 173, 175, 176]. On the other hand, it inhibits ferroptosis by promoting Nrf2 nuclear translocation and GPX4 expression [195, 199]. However, in PCOS, impaired SIRT1 function (reduced expression or decreased activity) results in the loss of this pivotal role. This not only exacerbates circadian rhythm disruption but also leads to a vicious cycle characterised by uncontrolled autophagy and compromised ferroptosis defence, thereby further amplifying the aforementioned pathological alterations.

In summary, core circadian genes such as BMAL1, CLOCK, REV-ERBs, and PER1 form a pivotal molecular network that determines the fate of GCs in PCOS ovaries. These genes function not merely as regulators but as direct effectors. Each core gene has been shown to have dual regulatory potential for autophagy and ferroptosis. The abnormal elevation of PER1 and deficiency of REV-ERBs synergistically drive excessive autophagy, iron metabolism disorders, and LPO; whereas CLOCK and CRY1 precisely trigger ferroptosis by mediating the specific degradation of mitochondria and Fn. However, the persistent low expression of BMAL1 may represent the most critical component within this network, though the precise mechanism remains unclear. Previous studies have proposed the concept of clockophagy, in which autophagy mediates the specific recognition and selective degradation of ARNTL/BMAL1 via the autophagy receptor SQSTM1/p62 [190, 200]. Degradation of BMAL1 blocks HIF-1α-dependent fatty acid uptake by upregulating EGLN2, thereby further promoting LPO and ferroptosis. Therefore, we hypothesise that the marked downregulation of BMAL1 in PCOS GCs may not represent simple transcriptional repression, but rather post-transcriptional degradation induced by clockophagy following hyperactivated autophagy. Future studies elucidating the intricate interplay between BMAL1, clockophagy, and ferroptosis would profoundly advance our understanding of LPO regulation in PCOS. Targeting the restoration of circadian rhythm homeostasis-whether through SIRT1 activators or chronotherapy-to rebalance autophagy-ferroptosis dynamics holds promise as an innovative strategy for improving the ovarian microenvironment in PCOS.

Epigenetics serves as the molecular bridge linking environmental signals to gene expression, enabling the transgenerational transmission of traits without altering DNA sequences [183, 201]. This reversible and heritable regulatory mechanism provides crucial evidence for explaining PCOS's high heterogeneity, phenotypic diversity, and its association with genetic susceptibility, environmental factors, and metabolic disorders. Epigenetic alterations constitute a vital molecular bridge linking metabolic abnormalities to reproductive dysfunction in PCOS. PCOS, being a highly heritable condition (with a heritability of approximately 70%), carries a fivefold increased risk of occurrence in offspring. This suggests that epigenetic mechanisms and genetic variation play a crucial synergistic role in the development of the disease [202, 203]. Research confirms that factors such as an intrauterine hyperandrogenic microenvironment, excessive AMH exposure, and adverse lifestyle habits can trigger significant epigenetic alterations in PCOS patients [204, 205]. These alterations reshape processes including follicular development, steroid synthesis, and oocyte maturation by influencing gene expression in GCs and oocytes [202]. This not only mediates contemporary metabolic and reproductive disorders but may also facilitate transgenerational transmission of disease through epigenetic memory. This genomic reprogramming involves a synergistic network of DNA methylation (DNAm), non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), and histone modifications. Unlike transient signal transduction, these epigenetic modifications function as stable molecular locks, sustaining lethal programmes even in the absence of acute stressors. Specifically, within PCOS GCs, genome-wide epigenetic alterations establish a unique pro-apoptotic phenomenon. DNAm serves as a pivotal regulator of gene expression. Within PCOS GCs, persistent OS disrupts the activity of DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) and compromises the availability of methyl donors, leading to an abnormal genome-wide remodelling of DNAm patterns [173, 204]. In particular, hypomethylation within the promoter regions of key genes acts as a de-repression switch for death-driving factors, thereby initiating lethal molecular programmes. Gao et al. employed whole-exome sequencing to identify transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) as a susceptibility gene for PCOS. Hypomethylation within its promoter region-specifically at CpG4 and CpG7 sites-leads to its overexpression, thereby exacerbating hyperandrogenism and IR [206]. Activated TGF-β1 not only promotes fibrosis by activating the downstream decapentaplegic 2/3 (Smad2/3) phosphorylation cascade, but also upregulates autophagy proteins and inhibits GPX4, thereby accelerating ferroptosis [203, 207, 208]. Moreover, DNAm abnormalities also directly drive excessive autophagy and ferroptosis. Map3k19 (mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase 1), a key enzyme in the MAPK pathway, is significantly upregulated in GCs of PCOS model mice [31]. Hypomethylation of the Map3k1 promoter region leads to sustained activation of the MAPK/p38 pathway. This simultaneously promotes autophagy (by upregulating autophagy-related proteins such as LC3-II) while inhibiting Nrf2-mediated transcription of SLC7A11, thereby establishing a state of high autophagy coupled with low antioxidant defence [31, 93]. Moreover, the autophagy core gene microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (Map1lc3a), which encodes LC3-II itself, also exhibits hypomethylation. This indicates that epigenetic modifications directly enhance the autophagy potential of GCs at the transcriptional level [209]. DNAm abnormalities synergistically drive the loss of autophagy control and ferroptosis by activating key pathogenic genes such as TGF-β1 and MAPK, offering a novel epigenetic perspective to elucidate ovarian fibrosis and ovulatory dysfunction in PCOS.

Non-coding small RNAs (miRNAs), acting as precise post-transcriptional regulators, play a pivotal role in monitoring gene expression networks governing follicular development and ovulation by specifically silencing target mRNA [210]. In PCOS GCs, specific miRNAs are abnormally upregulated, executing a dual-pronged assault that both releases the inhibition of autophagy and dismantles the defensive shield against ferroptosis [36, 211, 212]. miR-30a-5p is a prime example of this synergistic lethality. Research by Huang et al. confirmed that miR-30a-5p acts as an inhibitor of survival signalling. It achieves this by specifically targeting and silencing the downstream SOCS3, leading to a significant reduction in the phosphorylation levels of mTOR and its effector molecule p70S6K [36]. This inhibition of the canonical mTOR signalling axis relieves the negative regulation of autophagy, thereby driving an increase in non-adaptive autophagic flux. Furthermore, its targeted modification of the core autophagic molecule Beclin-1 has been demonstrated to be closely associated with abnormal steroid synthesis and ROS accumulation [213]. More crucially, Wang et al. further revealed that miR-30a-5p post-transcriptionally blocks SIRT1. The downregulation of SIRT1 directly impeded the nuclear translocation of the transcription factor Nrf2, thereby preventing the activation of downstream antioxidant systems. This concurrent suppression of survival signalling and impairment of antioxidant defences left GCs entirely exposed to LPO damage, sealing their fate of cell death [199, 212].

Histone modifications (particularly acetylation) link cellular metabolic states to chromatin architecture. The sirtuin family (SIRT1/3), acting as NAD+-dependent histone deacetylases, serves as a pivotal metabolic sensor, typically responsible for coupling energy availability with cellular survival [60, 173]. Recent research demonstrates that SIRT1-mediated deacetylation occurs at lysine residues K588 and K591 of Nrf2. Through Nrf2 deacetylation, it regulates multiple components of the antioxidant system, including HO-1, GSH, catalase, superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), and SOD2 [180, 214]. In PCOS GCs, the downregulation of SIRT1 and SIRT3 signifies the failure of this induction mechanism [60, 215]. The absence of SIRT1 leads to a state of high acetylation in chromatin and non-histone proteins. Functionally, this impairs the deacetylative activation of Nrf2 and obstructs the proper nuclear-cytoplasmic shuttling of LC3 (disrupting autophagic flux) [199, 212, 216]. Further studies indicate that reduced SIRT1 expression promotes excessive activation of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, inducing ferroptosis in GCs [60]. This results in a pathological state of metabolic arrest: GCs fail to initiate effective antioxidant responses (downregulated GPX4) and cannot regulate autophagy turnover, leading to the accumulation of damaged organelles and lipid peroxides [217]. SIRT3 primarily functions within mitochondria, maintaining oxidative phosphorylation and reducing OS. Its absence further exacerbates this process by inhibiting the AMPK-mTOR axis, thereby increasing autophagy and inducing ferroptosis. The loss of mitochondrial quality control leads to a surge in ROS, which directly triggers ferroptosis [215]. The resulting energy depletion further activates a non-functional compensatory autophagy, paradoxically accelerating the cell death process.

In summary, epigenetic reprogramming within ovarian GCs in PCOS is not merely a passive product of the pathological microenvironment, but rather an active driver of the autophagy-ferroptosis imbalance. Transcriptional de-repression achieved through hypomethylation of death-inducing factor promoters (e.g., Map3k1, TGF-β1), post-transcriptional blockade of survival pathways by ncRNA networks (e.g., miR-30a-5p), and metabolic sensing failure mediated by the SIRT family (SIRT1/3) collectively constitute multidimensional regulatory impairment. This genome-wide epigenetic memory explains why PCOS GCs struggle to regain homeostasis even after acute stress resolution, providing a molecular rationale for the clinical phenomenon of metabolic memory. Strategies targeting epigenetic modification enzymes-such as DNMT inhibitors or SIRT activators-hold promise for fundamentally reversing ovarian dysfunction by erasing this pathological memory and restoring genomic homeostasis, thereby offering novel therapeutic pathways.

As a cornerstone first-line therapeutic for PCOS clinical management, MET's mechanism of action extends beyond regulating metabolic homeostasis to include precise control over the fate determination of ovarian GCs. MET primarily targets intracellular metabolic sensing networks, simultaneously exerting dual effects of correcting autophagy and inhibiting ferroptosis. In PCOS models, MET effectively reverses suppressed PI3K and AMPK signalling, thereby restoring activity in the downstream AKT/mTOR pathway [218–220]. This restoration of survival signals not only protects ovarian reserve by limiting autophagy-dependent cell death through the inhibition of Beclin-1-mediated excessive autophagy; more significantly, MET has also been demonstrated to be an effective inhibitor of ferroptosis. Zhang et al.'s research reveals that MET effectively clears accumulated LPO and ROS by upregulating the core antioxidant molecules GPX4 and System Xc−, thereby reversing typical ferroptosis morphological features such as mitochondrial atrophy in PCOS GCs [215]. Recent evidence further indicates that under HA conditions, excessive activation of chaperone-mediated autophagy leads to abnormal degradation of FTH1 and iron overload [221]. By improving the systemic metabolic environment, MET holds promise in blocking such autophagy-dependent ferroptotic processes. Moreover, MET can exert synergistic effects with probiotics or lifestyle interventions by modulating gut microbiota and SCFAs production, thereby improving reproductive outcomes in PCOS [222].

BBR is a natural plant alkaloid originally described in the Chinese herbal medicine Huanglian. It demonstrates unique dual epigenetic-metabolic regulatory potential in PCOS treatments. At the metabolic level, BBR exhibits significant synergistic effects with MET, restoring disrupted autophagy flux in PCOS GCs to physiological homeostasis by reactivating the energy-sensing hub AMPK and inhibiting excessive mTOR phosphorylation [219]. At the post-transcriptional level, BBR has been demonstrated to specifically block ferroptosis by remodelling the ncRNA network. In a dihydrotestosterone-induced GCs injury model, BBR acts as a molecular sponge by upregulating circ_0097636, capturing miR-186-5p and thereby releasing its post-transcriptional suppression of SIRT3 [216]. The restored abundance of SIRT3 not only sustained mitochondrial biogenesis but also effectively suppressed LPO and Fe2+ accumulation. Despite its complex ubiquitin-modification mechanisms, BBR's synchronous improvement of mitochondrial function and its modulation of autophagy-ferroptosis crosstalk via the AMPK-SIRT3 axis establishes it as a paradigm for precise botanical chemical intervention.

MT, a neuroendocrine hormone possessing both antioxidant and circadian rhythm-regulating functions, is being redefined as a multi-functional therapeutic agent capable of restoring cellular fate imbalances in PCOS [223]. By activating the core deacetylase SIRT1, MT weaves a protective network against GCs' death programme. On one hand, MT significantly upregulates SIRT1 expression via the SIRT1-mitochondrial axis, thereby inhibiting the PINK1/Parkin pathway [60]. This blocks non-adaptive excessive mitophagy in GCs, preventing depletion of functional mitochondria. Concurrently, MT directly downregulates Beclin-1 and LC3-II via the PI3K/AKT and Keap1-Nrf2 pathways, restoring macroautophagic flux homeostasis and redox balance at the macroscopic level [128, 224]. Moreover, MT has been demonstrated to be a potent inhibitor of ferroptosis within the follicular microenvironment [91]. It promotes Nrf2 nuclear translocation via a SIRT1-dependent pathway, thereby initiating the transcription of GPX4 and SLC7A11 to directly counteract ferroptosis-induced LPO accumulation [225]. The deacetylase activity of SIRT1 itself is crucial for maintaining Nrf2's transcriptional function, reinforcing MT's therapeutic value in ameliorating ferroptosis within GCs [180]. More significantly, MT exerts a unique chronotherapeutic effect targeting circadian rhythm disruption specific to PCOS. It ameliorates rhythm-disordered-induced mitochondrial dysfunction upstream by restoring circadian expression of the core clock gene CLOCK [184]. This upstream rhythmic reset, combined with downstream SIRT1-mediated molecular interventions, positions MT as an ideal drug candidate capable of fundamentally remodelling the ovarian granulosa cell microenvironment and homeostasis.

The therapeutic value of vitamin D in PCOS treatment primarily lies in its transcriptional regulatory function as a nuclear receptor ligand. Its active form, 1,25(OH)2D3, has been demonstrated to act as a molecular shield directly blocking androgen-induced ferroptosis in GCs. By binding to the nuclear receptor VDR, vitamin D directly activates transcriptional reprogramming of the antioxidant defence system [226]. VDR activation significantly upregulates GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression while suppressing transcription of ACSL4, a key pro-ferroptotic enzyme, thereby scavenging ROS and preserving mitochondrial integrity. Vitamin D deficiency exacerbates IR in PCOS, closely associated with impaired autophagy flux reflected by reduced LC3-II levels in tissues such as the ovary and liver [227]. Consequently, vitamin D supplementation not only reduces ferroptosis susceptibility in ovarian GCs via the VDR/GPX4 axis but also ameliorates the pathological environment of PCOS by correcting the autophagy-metabolic imbalance induced by vitamin D deficiency.

In recent years, the GM has been recognised as a key remote control centre regulating the local immune and metabolic environment of PCOS GCs. Targeting the gut-ovary axis offers a novel non-invasive strategy for intervening in the GCs' death programme. Luo et al.'s research revealed that colonisation with the probiotic EcN significantly remodels the PCOS-associated GM, promoting secretion of the intestinal immune factor IL-22 [228]. Circulating IL-22, acting as a crucial protective signal, influences ovarian GCs, effectively suppressing ROS-induced excessive mitophagy and preserving MMP and functional integrity. This discovery establishes a novel therapeutic approach to remotely block local ovarian mitochondrial damage and ferroptosis cascades by modulating the GM.

At the level of intracellular gene regulation, epigenetic editing can erase pathological memory at its root. As a core m6A regulator, METTL3 is overexpressed in PCOS GCs, acting as an epigenetic switch that drives the cell death programme. Recent evidence indicates that METTL3 knockdown releases transcriptional repression on GPX4 via m6A modification, significantly upregulating its levels and thereby blocking LPO and subsequent ovarian fibrosis progression [229]. Furthermore, METTL3 stabilises PINK1 mRNA via m6A modification, driving destructive excessive mitophagy. Intervention using umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles effectively downregulates METTL3 expression, thereby inhibiting the PINK1/Parkin pathway and restoring mitochondrial homeostasis [230].

Given that the excessive degradation of specific organelles (selective autophagy) constitutes the direct executory step driving ferroptosis, the direct maintenance of organelle integrity represents a precise salvage strategy [60, 184, 230]. The iron chelator deferoxamine mesylate (DFOM) precisely chelates free iron released by NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy, significantly salvaging GCs' survival by eliminating substrates for the Fenton reaction [71]. For non-adaptive autophagic overactivation triggered by environmental toxins (e.g., cadmium), the autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) precisely disrupts the environmental stress-driven autophagy-dependent ferroptosis axis by inhibiting lysosomal acidification and autophagosome-lysosome fusion, markedly reducing membrane LPO levels [26]. This confirms that inhibiting excessive autophagy represents an effective strategy for blocking ferroptosis in GCs under specific pathological conditions.

PCOS is characterised by its high phenotypic heterogeneity, representing not merely a macro-level endocrine disorder but fundamentally signifying the collapse of micro-level fate determination mechanisms within ovarian GCs Although autophagy and ferroptosis function as distinct forms of cell death in maintaining cellular homeostasis, This review synthesises existing evidence to propose that, when GCs confront multiple pathological insults, the disruption of the autophagy flux and ferroptosis form a synergistic lethal network. This critical transition from adaptive survival to irreversible death constitutes the primary force driving follicular decline. Why do autophagy and ferroptosis, seemingly disparate processes, form such a close association in PCOS? We elucidate its intrinsic logic across two hierarchical levels: firstly, at the upstream signalling level, this crosstalk is driven by the synergistic convergence of multidimensional stressors. The characteristic metabolic stress, systemic inflammation, circadian rhythm disruption, and epigenetic memory of PCOS do not act in isolation but rather constitute a multifaceted assault that compels GCs beyond the critical threshold of adaptive survival. These stressors converge upon shared molecular hubs, establishing a dual-constraint scenario: triggering dysregulated signalling that induces excessive autophagy while simultaneously lowering the susceptibility threshold for ferroptosis. This upstream synergistic drive establishes the pathological foundation for irreversible cellular damage. Secondly, at the downstream execution level, a functional dependency exists wherein selective autophagy serves as an essential amplifier of ferroptosis. The lethality of ferroptosis in PCOS is fundamentally autophagy-dependent. Specifically, ferritinophagy triggers explosive release of free iron through degradation of FTH1, whilst excessive mitophagy accelerates energy collapse and mitochondrial ROS surges. This autophagy-dependent ferroptosis not only accounts for the characteristic follicular atresia in PCOS but also reveals why single inhibition of any individual death pathway often fails to fully rescue ovarian function.

Re-examining PCOS through the crosstalk lens of autophagy-ferroptosis offers novel insights into its pathophysiological network. This framework unifies multiple aetiological factors-metabolic stress, inflammation, chronobiology, and epigenetics-into a central molecular hub underlying GCs' functional decline. Consequently, this understanding provides clear guidance for clinical treatment: future therapeutic targets should shift from merely lowering blood glucose or reducing androgens towards precisely decoupling the autophagy-ferroptosis axis through multi-target strategies. This approach blocks the pathological amplification of lethal signals while preserving the physiological turnover of organelles. Through multi-targeted interventions-restoring intracellular redox and protein homeostasis, remodelling the GM-immune environment, synchronising circadian rhythms, and erasing pathological epigenetic memory-we may ultimately dismantle this lethal network. This approach holds promise for reversing follicular atresia at its source and restoring reproductive health in PCOS patients.
