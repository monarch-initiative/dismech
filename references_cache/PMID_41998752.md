# Airborne pollutants and risk of neurodegenerative diseases: a global systematic review and meta-analysis.
**Authors:** Zhang Z, Xu C
**Journal:** Alzheimers Res Ther (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13195-026-02050-3](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13195-026-02050-3)

## Content

BACKGROUND: The relationship between airborne pollutants and subtypes of neurodegenerative disorders remains poorly understood. This study aims to assess the associations between major air pollutants and the risk of multiple neurodegenerative disease outcomes. METHODS: We searched three databases (PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus), with the most recent update on February 21st, 2026. We included studies involving adults (without the disease at baseline) with exposure to air pollutants such as PM2.5, PM10, NO2, NOx, and O3, and outcomes related to all-cause dementia, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and vascular dementia (VaD). Data extracted included study details, population characteristics, and risk of bias, assessed using a modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were pooled using random-effects models. RESULTS: Among 24,196 identified records, 49 studies met the inclusion criteria. The included studies covered populations from North America, Europe, Asia and Oceania, providing a broad global perspective. A per-increment relationship was found between air pollution and incidence of all-cause dementia. For each 1 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5, the pooled HR for all-cause dementia was 1.07 (95% CI: 1.02–1.12), with weaker but positive associations for AD (HR: 1.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.05), PD (HR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00-1.04) and VaD (HR: 1.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.05). PM10 exposure was positively associated with AD risk (HR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00-1.03, per 1 µg/m³ increase). In addition, NO2 was associated with increased risks of all-cause dementia (HR: 1.05, 95% CI: 1.00-1.11) and VaD (HR: 1.14, 95% CI: 1.01–1.28) per 10 µg/m³ increase. CONCLUSIONS: Our findings indicate that pollutants, particularly PM2.5, PM10, and NO2, are associated with an increased risk of neurodegenerative diseases. These results provide strong evidence supporting the role of air quality in achieving Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 3 and 13, aiming to reduce the dementia burden and improve air quality globally.

Air pollution remains a central concern in global health policy, contributing to approximately 7 million premature deaths annually [ 1 ]. It is also recognized as one of the twelve modifiable risk factors for dementia [ 2 ]. Despite decades of research highlighting the association between air pollution and dementia, no study has yet simultaneously quantified the impact of major air pollutants on the morbidity of different neurodegenerative disorders subtypes on a global scale. Understanding how increased air pollution influences dementia morbidity across diverse regions and populations is essential for addressing health inequities and advancing the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).

Air pollution has been linked to a range of neurodegenerative diseases, as demonstrated in animal model studies [ 3 ]. Several meta-analyses have investigated the relationship between various air pollutants and neurodegenerative conditions (Table S1). However, discrepancies exist regarding the specific pollutants and different types of neurodegenerative disorders involved. While studies consistently suggest that PM2.5 (particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter ≤ 2.5 micrometers) has a detrimental effect on dementias [ 4– 7 ], the relationship between ozone (O3) and dementia remains unclear, with several studies reporting no significant effect [ 4 , 6 , 8 , 9 ]. Furthermore, research has identified PM2.5 as a risk factor for Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) and Vascular Dementia (VaD) [ 7 , 10 ], while PM10 (particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter ≤ 10 micrometers) may specifically increase the risk of VaD [ 7 ]. Nitrogen dioxide (NO2) has also been linked to dementia risk in several studies [ 11 , 12 ]. By contrast, nitrogen oxides (NOx)—primarily emitted from traffic and industrial combustion sources [ 13 ]—is often used as an indicator of traffic-related air pollution. Some previous studies have treated NOx and NO2 as distinct exposures, and reported separate effect estimates for NOx in relation to neurodegenerative disorders [ 4 , 6 , 9 , 14 , 15 ]. Previous meta-analyses have either focused on the link between PM2.5 and neurodegenerative disorders subtypes [ 10 , 16 ] or assessed the effects of various pollutants on all-cause dementia [ 4 , 6 , 9 ], however, none have systematically synthesized the associations between a wide range of air pollutants and different neurodegenerative disorders subtypes, presenting challenges in making cross-context comparisons and accounting for variations in effect sizes across studies.

This study aims to fill this gap by investigating the global relationship between multiple air pollutants—specifically PM2.5, PM10, NO2, NOₓ, and O3—and the morbidity of specific neurodegenerative disorders subtypes, including all-cause dementia, AD, PD, and VaD. By conducting a comprehensive analysis with broader geographical coverage, we seek to provide a more holistic understanding of the links between air pollution and dementia, advancing previous research in both scale and depth.

Methods.

This meta-analysis was performed in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [ 17 ] (Table S2).We searched through three main literature databases (PubMed, Web of Science and Scopus) dated up to February 21st, 2026.

A searching strategy of “(diseases AND pollution) NOT irrelevant words” was employed to locate pertinent archives as broadly as possible and to narrow down the literature scope. The detailed retrieval keywords of the databases are listed in the appendix (Table S3). In addition, all the studies should have an abstract and a full-text English version. The following items stipulate the inclusion criteria: (1) study population is ordinary adults (without any type of neurodegenerative disorders at baseline); (2) exposure is air pollution, including PM2.5, PM10, NOx, NO2, O3; (3) effect size is the hazard ratio (HR), with 95% confidence intervals; (4) can find the corresponding linear increment of pollutant concentration to the effect size; (5) outcome is incidence of at least one type of neurodegenerative disorders, including all-cause dementia, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD) and vascular dementia (VaD).

We excluded studies whose outcome is cognitive impairment or exposure is solid fuel use (an indicator of indoor air pollution) because the number of these studies is not enough to conduct a meta-analysis. In cases where multiple studies utilized repetitive cohorts, only the most recent study with the largest sample size would be included in the analysis.

We extracted fundamental information by browsing full text, including authors, publication date, study area, data sources, sample size, population characteristics, the definitions of exposure and outcome, the increments and units of pollutant concentrations, adjusted confounding factors, the effect size and 95% confident intervals.

We standardized the units of all air pollutants to µg/m3, with transformation rules of ppb and µg/m3 described elsewhere [ 4 ]. To ensure comparability across studies while respecting pollutant-specific characteristics, effect estimates were harmonized using pollutant-specific exposure increments. Specifically, we applied a 1 µg/m3 increment for PM2.5 and PM10, and a 10 µg/m3 increment for NOx, NO2, O3. The effect sizes reported in the included studies included hazard ratios (HRs), relative risks (RRs), and incidence rate ratios (IRRs). We converted effect sizes and their 95% confidence intervals according to the formula above, standardizing units and concentrations. The studies without reporting the linear concentration increments of pollutants were excluded before data extraction.

In order to ensure the quality of included articles, we conducted quality assessments to all studies that met the inclusion criteria, using Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [ 18 ]. The NOS evaluates a cohort study in three aspects: selection, comparability and outcome. The highest score is 9, and a study would be excluded as low-quality if it received a score of 6 or less.

We employed the random-effect model to calculate pooled relative risks and 95% confidence intervals by the appropriate model, to analyze the association of air pollution and all types of neurodegenerative disorders. Forest plots were utilized to visualize the results of meta-analysis synthesis. Furthermore, for investigating feasible origins of heterogeneity, we performed subgroup analysis, grouping studies by sex and study area. Subgroup analyses were performed only when at least two subgroups were available and each subgroup contained three or more studies, to ensure statistical reliability. Heterogeneity was assessed using both the I² statistic and the corresponding p-value. A factor was considered a potential source of heterogeneity if the I² value decreased within all subgroups and the p-values for heterogeneity exceeded 0.05. We only conducted two types of subgroups due to the inadequacy of subgroup data and the variety of definitions and classifications for a same factor in different studies.

We performed some sensitivity analysis to guarantee robustness and accessibility of the model. Considering specific sources of pollutants (e.g. traffic-related NOx), and the studies whose populations are single sex, we defined the following three conditions: (1) only common sources and only both sexes; the main result was calculated in this condition, (2) all sources (including specific sources) and only both sexes, (3) only common sources and all kinds of sexes (including single sex). We conducted meta-analysis in all these conditions as sensitivity analysis.

We performed a leave-one-out analysis to evaluate the stability and robustness of our findings. To assess publication bias, we performed Egger’s tests and generated funnel plots for each of the three conditions, provided that the number of included studies was 10 or more [ 19 ]. When the p-value of Egger test is lower than 0.05, this association can be thought to have publication bias. Trim and fill method will be used if there is any bias, with a report of adjusted pooled estimates.

All analyses were conducted by R version 4.2.1. This systematic review and meta-analysis followed Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. This study is registered with PROSPERO, CRD420251011200.

A total of 24,196 records were identified across three databases: PubMed (7,730), Web of Science (10,193), and Scopus (6,273). After applying exclusion keywords to filter irrelevant articles (Table S3), we removed 13,375 studies, leaving 10,821 results (2,790 from PubMed, 4,265 from Web of Science, and 3,766 from Scopus). Following the removal of 5,674 duplicate entries, 5,147 unique articles remained. After screening titles and abstracts, 220 studies met the inclusion criteria. Following rigorous quality assessment of the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), two studies [ 20 , 21 ] were excluded. Ultimately, 49 studies were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1). Among these, 39 studies scored 8 or 9 points, indicating moderate to high methodological quality (Figure S1). Reasons for excluding 171 studies are listed in Table S4.

The studies were geographically diverse (Fig. 2), with twenty from North America (16 from the U.S. and 4 from Canada). Eighteen studies were from Europe, including 7 from the U.K., 3 from Sweden, 3 from Italy, 2 from France, 1 from the Netherlands, 1 from Denmark and 1 from Ireland. Nine studies were from Asia (3 from Korea and 6 from China), and two study was from Oceania (Australia). The pollutants studied included PM2.5 (41 studies), followed by PM10 (20 studies), NO2 (33 studies), NOx (9 studies), and O3 (12 studies) (Table S5).

We conducted a meta-analysis to investigate associations between the major air pollutants and incidence of diseases including all-cause dementia, AD, PD, and VaD. The associations examined were: (1) PM2.5 with all-cause dementia, AD, PD, and VaD; (2) PM10 with all-cause dementia, AD, PD, and VaD; (3) NO2 with all-cause dementia, AD, PD and VaD; (4) NOx with all-cause dementia and AD; (5) O3 with all-cause dementia and PD.

For PM2.5, the pooled hazard ratio (HR) for all-cause dementia was 1.07 (95% CI: 1.02–1.12) per 1 µg/m³ increase. A weaker but still significant association was observed for AD (HR: 1.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.05), PD (HR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00–1.04) and VaD (HR: 1.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.05) (Fig. 3). Similarly, for PM10, a 1 µg/m³ increase was positively associated with AD (HR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00–1.03), PD (HR: 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00–1.02) and VaD (HR: 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00–1.01) (Fig. 4). For NO2, the estimated HR for a 10 µg/m³ increase was 1.05 (95% CI: 1.00–1.11) for dementia and 1.14 (95% CI: 1.01–1.28) for VaD (Fig. 5).

Forest plots for NOx and O3 are presented in Figure S2. NOx showed a modest positive association with dementia (HR: 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00–1.01), and the result of O3 was not statistically significant with dementia (HR: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.95–1.04).

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses stratified by geographic region and sex were conducted (Figures S3-S4). Only subgroups with at least two strata, each containing three or more studies, were reported. For the sex-stratified analyses, sufficient data were available only for PM2.5 with dementia, AD and PD, PM10 with PD, and NO2 with dementia.

The results indicated that geographic region was not a major contributor to heterogeneity, although a decreased I2 and a p-value > 0.05 were observed in the US subgroup for several associations (PM2.5 with AD, NO2 with dementia and O3 with dementia). In the sex-stratified analyses, heterogeneity decreased in both male and female subgroups for PM10 with PD (male: I2 = 55.6%, p = 0.11; female: I2 = 0%, p = 0.59), suggesting that sex may represent a potential source of heterogeneity for this relationship.

To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed sensitivity analyses and leave-one-out analyses (Figure S5). The results were consistent with the main analysis.

To examine publication bias, we conducted Egger’s tests and drew funnel plots for the condition that the number of included studies was 10 or more (Figure S6). Publication bias was not found in any association.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to systematically and comprehensively quantify the global impact of various airborne pollutants on different neurodegenerative disorders subtypes. Our findings provide robust statistical support for the association between air pollutants, particularly PM2.5, PM10, and NO2, and an increased risk of neurodegenerative disorders. These results underscore the importance of air quality as a modifiable risk factor for neurodegenerative disorders and emphasize the urgency of addressing air pollution in public health strategies aimed at reducing the global burden of neurodegenerative diseases.

Our analysis revealed a statistically significant association between PM2.5 exposure and main types of neurodegenerative disorders. The previous studies also support this finding. For example, a meta-analysis by Wilker et al. reported a hazard ratio of 1.42 (95% CI: 1.00–2.02) for a 2 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5[ 4 ]. By incorporating more recent evidence published between 2023 and 2025, our updated meta-analysis yields a more precise and modest estimate (HR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.02–1.12, per 1 µg/m3 increase), further reinforcing the positive association between PM2.5 and neurodegenerative outcomes while reflecting the accumulation of evidence over time. A cohort study of 12 million participants also found a significant association between PM2.5 and dementia, with a hazard ratio of 1.06 (95% CI: 1.05–1.07) per 3.2 µg/m³ increase [ 22 ].

PM2.5, particularly fine particulate matter, is known to penetrate deep into the lungs and enter the bloodstream, potentially reaching the brain via systemic inflammation and oxidative stress [ 23– 27 ]. Upon entering the central nervous system (CNS), PM2.5 can directly introduce and promote the accumulation of neurotoxic substances, including metals such as lead, arsenic, and iron [ 28 ]. Long-term exposure to high levels of PM2.5 may exacerbate neuroinflammation and accelerate cognitive decline [ 29 , 30 ], possibly through amyloidosis [ 31 , 32 ], further contributing to dementia progression.

Similarly, we observed a weaker but still significant association between PM10 and AD (HR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00–1.03). Although primarily deposited in the upper respiratory tract and absorbed via the digestive tract [ 28 ], PM10 can enter systemic circulation and trigger neurotoxic mechanisms similar to those of PM2.5[ 33 ]. A key pathway is the induction of oxidative stress, which contributes to CNS damage and neurodegenerative diseases by causing mitochondrial dysfunction, synaptic impairment, and β-amyloid (Aβ) production [ 34 ]. Consequently, chronic exposure to particulate matter not only contributes to the onset of Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases but also significantly accelerates their progression [ 35 ]. In a South Korean cohort of over 5.2 million participants, each 1 µg/m3 increment in PM10 was associated with an increased risk of Alzheimer’s disease (HR: 1.021; 95% CI: 1.018–1.023) [ 36 ]. Social and environmental factors (e.g., temperature) can also increase susceptibility to PM10 exposure [ 11 , 37 , 38 ].

Positive correlations with dementia were observed for both NOx and NO2. NOx primarily originates from anthropogenic sources such as industrial processes and traffic [ 13 ], while NO2—the dominant form of NOx in ambient air—is mainly derived from vehicular emissions [ 39 ]. In line with common practice in the literature [ 4 , 6 , 9 ], this study analyzed NOx and NO2 separately. The results showed that NO2 exposure was associated with a more significant risk of dementia (HR: 1.05, 95% CI: 1.00–1.11). Inhaled NO2 reaches the brain via respiratory and systemic circulation [ 39 ], where it induces neurotoxicity—as evidenced by memory and cognitive deficits in exposed mice [ 40 ]. As a member of the reactive nitrogen species (RNS) [ 41 ], NO2 contributes to oxidative stress and inflammation through diverse chemical reactions [ 42 , 43 ], thereby promoting the development of neurodegenerative diseases. Given the rising levels of urbanization and vehicle emissions globally, these findings highlight the public health implications of long-term NO2 exposure, particularly in individuals genetically predisposed to dementia or those already at risk due to other environmental factors.

Interestingly, our study found no significant associations between ozone (O3) and neurodegenerative disorders. Most previous research on O3 has indicated no significant associations with dementia, which is consistent with our result [ 4 , 6 , 8 , 9 ]. However, some animal studies demonstrate that O3 causes oxidative stress in rat brains, which may trigger neurodegenerative changes, neuronal loss, and cognitive deficits [ 44 , 45 , 46 ]. Future high-quality research is needed to investigate whether O3 exposure is associated with neurodegenerative diseases.

In the sex subgroup analysis between PM10 and PD, heterogeneity was substantially reduced, suggesting that sex may be a source of heterogeneity in this relationship. This could reflect sex-specific differences in inhalation rates, particle deposition, or systemic inflammatory responses to particulate matter. However, due to the limited number of included studies (three for both male and female), these findings should be interpreted with caution. Our results advocate for routine sex-stratified analyses in future environmental epidemiological research and to move towards more personalized risk assessment.

Our findings have important public health implications. With neurodegenerative disorders posing a substantial burden on individuals, families, and healthcare systems worldwide, our results emphasize the need to reduce air pollution as a preventive measure. By mitigating exposure to PM2.5, PM10, and NO2, it may be possible to reduce the incidence of neurodegenerative diseases, particularly in urban and industrial regions where pollution levels are highest. Policies aimed at improving air quality—such as stricter emissions standards, promoting clean energy, and expanding green spaces—could have far-reaching benefits, not only in reducing the burden of dementia but also in enhancing overall public health and quality of life.

Our study has several strengths. First, it comprehensively analyzes the impact of five major airborne pollutants on neurodegenerative disorders subtypes, enabling comparisons across different contexts. Second, by including only cohort studies, we minimized the potential for reverse causation and enhanced the robustness of our conclusions. Finally, quality assessments using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale indicated that the included studies were of moderate to high quality, further strengthening the validity of our results.

However, our study also has limitations. There was heterogeneity across studies in exposure assessment methods, sample populations, and control for confounders. To address this, we performed subgroup analyses by study area and sex, which may help explain some of the observed heterogeneity. In the sex subgroup analysis of PD and PM10, the I² decreased and p-value for heterogeneity > 0.05, suggesting that sex may contribute to heterogeneity. Second, although we followed recommended practices for publication bias assessment, the small number of studies (< 10) in some analyses may still affect the reliability of the pooled estimates. Future updates with more accumulating evidence are needed to confirm these findings. Third, it is difficult to determine whether the observed associations for NOx reflect its own neurotoxicity or its role as a proxy for traffic-related pollutant mixtures, as most included studies did not adjust for other traffic-related pollutants. Thus, our findings should be interpreted with caution, and future research using multipollutant models is warranted. Lastly, cognitive impairment and cook fuel use (a marker for indoor air pollution) were excluded from the meta-analysis due to insufficient research. Further studies should explore how other environmental factors, such as diet, physical activity, and socioeconomic status, interact with air pollution to affect dementia risk. Given the growing body of research in this area, future studies with longer follow-up periods are necessary to confirm these findings.

This study systematically assessed the relationship between various airborne pollutants and neurodegenerative disorders through a comprehensive meta-analysis, including sensitivity and subgroup analyses. Our findings reinforce the growing body of evidence linking exposure to air pollution, particularly PM2.5, PM10, and NO2, with an increased risk of neurodegenerative diseases. These results emphasize the urgent need to prioritize air pollution reduction as a public health issue. Interventions aimed at decreasing exposure to these pollutants could play a crucial role in alleviating the global burden of neurodegenerative disorders.
