BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES: Cockayne syndrome (CS) is an ultra-rare, autosomal 
recessive, premature aging disorder characterized by impaired growth, 
neurodevelopmental delays, neurodegeneration, polyneuropathy, and other 
multiorgan system complications. The anatomic aspects of CS neurodegeneration 
have long been known from postmortem examinations and MRI studies, but the 
clinical features of this neurodegeneration are not well characterized, 
especially at later stages of the disease.
METHODS: This was a retrospective observational study in which individuals with 
CS who survived beyond 18 years were ascertained at 3 centers in the United 
States, France, and the United Kingdom. Medical records were examined to 
determine the frequencies and features of the following neurologic 
complications: neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric decline (8 symptoms), tremors, 
neuropathy, seizures, and strokes.
RESULTS: Among 18 individuals who met inclusion criteria, all but one (94.4%) 
experienced at least one symptom of neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric decline, 
with most individuals experiencing at least half of those symptoms. Most 
participants experienced tremors and peripheral neuropathy, with a few 
experiencing seizures and strokes. For individuals with available data, 100.0% 
were reported to have gait ataxia and neuroimaging showed that 85.7% had 
generalized cerebral atrophy on MRI while 78.6% had white matter changes.
DISCUSSION: Symptoms of neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric decline are nearly 
universal in our cohort of adults with CS, suggesting that these individuals are 
at risk of developing neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric decline, with symptoms 
related to but not specific to dementia. Considering the prominent role of DNA 
repair defects in CS disease mechanisms and emerging evidence for increased DNA 
damage in neurodegenerative disease, impaired genome maintenance may be a shared 
pathway underlying multiple forms of neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric decline. 
Components of the DNA damage response mechanism may bear further study as 
potential therapeutic targets that could alleviate 
neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric symptoms in CS and other neurodegenerative 
disorders.

Abstract 
Cockayne syndrome (CS) is an ultra-rare, autosomal recessive, premature aging disorder characterized by 
impaired growth, neurodevelopmental delays, neurodegeneration, polyneuropathy, and other multi -organ 
system complications.  The anatomic aspects of CS neurodegeneration have long been known from post -
mortem examinations and MRI studies, but the clinical features of  this neurodegeneration are not  well 
characterized, especially at later stages of the disease.  This was a retrospective observational study in which 
individuals with CS who survived beyond 18 years were ascertained at three centers in the United States, 
France, and the United Kingdom.  Medical records were examined to determine the frequencies and features 
of the following neurological complications: neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric decline (8 symptoms) , 
tremors, neuropathy, seizures, and strokes.  Among 18 individuals who met inclusion criteria, all but one 
(94.4%) experienced at least one symptom of neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric decline , with most 
individuals experiencing at least half of those symptoms.  The majority of participants experienced tremors 
and peripheral neuropathy, with a few experiencing seizures and strokes .  Symptoms of 
neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric decline are nearly universal in our cohort of adults with CS , suggesting 
that these individuals may develop components of dementia later in the disease course .  Considering the 
prominent role of DNA repair defects in CS disease mechanisms and emerging evidence for increased DNA 
damage in neurodegenerative disease, impaired genome maintenance may be a shared pathway underlying 
multiple forms of dementia.  Components of the DNA damage response mechanism may bear further study 
as potential therapeutic targets that could alleviate dementia associated  symptoms in CS a nd other 
neurodegenerative disorders. 

Introduction 
Cockayne Syndrome (CS) is a n ultra-rare, autosomal recessive premature aging disorder 
characterized by impaired growth, microcephaly, and severe neurological dysfunction1.  Since the 
disease was first described in 1936 by Edward Cockayne2, several studies have defined diagnostic 
criteria for CS  and characterized key aspects of the disease phenotype3.  Notably, the landmark 
paper published by Nance and Berry 3 reviewed 140 CS cases and noted common features of the 
disease in addition to those mentioned above,  including photosensitivity, cataracts, hearing loss, 
motor loss/gait ataxia, hepatic dysfunction, and renal dysfunction .  There are 3 clinical subtypes 
of CS : CS  Type I , the classic form, with onset typically around 2 years and estimated life 
expectancy into adolescence or early adulthood; CS Type II, the severe form, with infantile onset 
and an estimated life expectancy into childhood; and CS Type III, the late onset milder form with 
typical survival into adulthood 3.  These subtypes were described and numbered sequentially as 
they were recognized and not in order of severity4,5. 
Most cases of CS are associated with biallelic pathogenic variants in one of two genes: 
ERCC66 (also known as CSB, accounting for ~62 -68% of cases 7,8) and ERCC89 (also known as 
CSA, accounting for ~32-38% of cases7,8).  While a clear genotype-phenotype correlation has yet 
to be established, it has been noted that a significant number of ERCC6/CSB patients have more 
severe forms of the disease 7.  Additionally, ERCC8/CSA mutations have only been implicated in 
CS1 and CS3 cases thus far, and not in CS2 (the most severe form of the disease) 7.  Some 
individuals who meet clinical criteria for CS do not have pathogenic variants in either ERCC6 or 
ERCC8; recently, it bec ame apparent that some of these individuals have dominant pathogenic 
variants in MORC210.  

Anatomic neurodegeneration was documented  as a cardinal featur e of CS starting in the 
1960s, based initially on post-mortem examinations showing widespread cortical and cerebellar 
atrophy with loss of white matter 11,12,13, supplemented more recently by imaging studies 14. Early 
clinical neurological complications of CS have long been recognized , including ataxia, tremors, 
and neurodevelopmental delay15,16.  There are some comments in the literature regarding cognitive 
deterioration, but without details regarding specific symptomatic changes12,17. 
As more CS patients survive into adolescence and adulthood, it is important to understand 
what, if any, clinical aspects of neurodegeneration develop in the later stages of the disease in order 
to optimize care and ultimately quality of life and longevity for CS patients. This retrospective 
study compares the frequency of different neurologic complications that occur during early versus 
late stages of CS.  
 
Methods 
Study design and p articipant ascertainment . This was a retrospective observational study.  
Participants were identified through biorepositories and pediatric neurology clinics at two 
participating institutions (University of Minnesota and University of Strasbourg), along with a 
multidisciplinary DNA repair disorder clinic at a third institution (Guy’s and St. Thomas’ NHS 
Foundation Trust).  Data collection and analysis occurred from June 2022 to April 2023. 
Data collection.  We used a standardized form to collect data from medical records and targeted 
interviews of participating families.   We sought i nformation relat ed to demographics and the 
following cardinal features of CS: age at last follow-up or death, microcephaly, photosensitivity, 
vision loss, cataracts, hearing loss, and joint contractures.  For neurologic complications, we 
recorded information regarding the age of onset for tremors, neuropathy, seizures, strokes 

(including transient ischemic attacks (TIAs)) , and neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric symptoms 
when available.  We defined early neurologic complications as those with onset typically before 
age 18 years, and late neurologic complications as those with onset typically at age 18 years or 
older. Neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric symptoms included language decline, anxiety/behavioral 
decline, depression, sleep changes, memory decline, confusion/difficulty with commands or time 
management, and appetite changes.  We obtained information from brain MRI and head CT reports 
of interpreting neuroradiologists.  In particular, we sought information relating to cerebral atrophy, 
white matter changes, and deep gray matter changes including calcifications.  We obtained data 
on p athogenic variants from genetic test reports , when available, or alternatively from other 
documents in the medical record. 
Nanopore genomic long read sequencing and variant confirmation.   Nanopore long read 
sequencing was performed on a genomic DNA sample from individual 113 to identify the 
causative variants, following previously described protocols 18. A combination of PCR 
amplification and Sanger sequencing was used to confirm  segregation of the  variants among 
available informative family members. 
Potential sources of bias.  Due to the ultra-rare nature of CS, our cohort was a convenience sample 
accompanied by a risk of ascertainment bias.  The involvement of distinct teams and centers in 3 
different countries helps to mitigate this potential bias, especially as the participants were enrolled 
due to the presence of the primary diagnosis of CS and not for any specific complications.  
Protocol approvals, registrations, and consents.  This study was conducted under protocols 
approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Minnesota, and the Ethics 
Committees of the University of Strasbourg and Guy’s and St. Thomas’ NHS Foundation Trust.  
Informed consent was obtained from all participants or their guardians. 

Data Availability.  De-identified data collected for this study that were not presented in this article 
will be made available upon reasonable request from other investigators. 
 
Results 
Demographics and General Clinical Features.  We identified 34 individuals with CS at the 
University of Minnesota, 160 at th e University of Strasbourg, and 40 at Guys and St. Thomas’ 
NHS Foundation Trust.  Among these 234 individuals with CS, 18 met the inclusion criterion of 
surviving past age 18 years  with sufficient information for retrospective assessment .  Their ages 
ranged from 18 to 51 years at last follow-up or death (Table 1).  Most of these individuals harbored 
multiple common features of CS, including microcephaly, photosensitivity, vision loss, hearing 
loss, and joint contractures.  Only 3 participants – 957, 139, and 15 (16.7%) – in this sample had 
known cataracts, fewer than expected for a diagnosis of CS.  Participant 139 had numerous visual 
complications, including glaucoma, macular degeneration, and retinal dystrophy, but did not have 
cataracts until approximately 27 years (and lived to 32 years).  
Early Neurologic Complications .  These are listed in Table 2 .  All 18 participants had 
neurodevelopmental delay.  Fifteen of 18  participants (83.3%) had tremors: 8 had intention 
tremors, 1 had intermittent tremors (requiring hospitalization), 1 had postural tremor, 3 had resting 
and intention tremors, and for 2 participants the exact type of tremor was unknown.  The tremors 
were generally described as becoming more frequent/intense with age and fatigue and were often 
severe enough to interfere with fine motor activities such as eating, drinking, and writing.  Two of 
our participants (21, 25) have taken carbidopa-levodopa for tremors.  One of these individuals (25) 
continued to have bothersome episodes of tremors once or twice a week despite experiencing 
overall relief from the carbidopa-levodopa therapy.  Thirteen of our 18 participants (72.2%) had 

neuropathy: 8 had demyelinating polyneuropathy, 1 had axonal polyneuropathy, 1 had a mixed 
axonal and demyelinating polyneuropathy, 1 had an unspecified neuropathy, and 2 had suspected 
neuropathy based on absent deep tendon reflexes on physical examination. 
Neurocognitive/Neuropsychiatric and Other Late -Stage Neurologic Complications .  All but one 
individual with CS in our cohort  experienced at least one symptom of 
neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric decline (Table 3).  Progressive language decline was the most 
common of these symptoms, noted in 15 out of 17 participants (88.2%) with available information.  
For 3 individuals (5, 21, 139), this manifested as reduced vocabulary and/or diminished length and 
complexity of sentences and/or speaking only single words or speaking with incomplete sentences, 
all of which were changes from their baseline language abilities.  Two other individuals progressed 
to incoherent speech or paucity of speech (25, 113).   Among the 1 5 individuals with language 
decline, 7 had symptom onset after age 18, 5 had symptom onset in adolescence (ages 12  to 17 
years), and 3 had onset in childhood (ages 2 to 8 years). 
Eleven out of 16 participants with available information  (68.8%) experienced increased 
anxiety and behavioral issues, including mood swings and irritability.  This marks a clear contrast 
to their childhood baseline ; indeed, earlier clinical notes from the medical records of these 
individuals often indicate that they were friendly and interactive  during childhood .  For the 8 
participants with known age of onset  for the behavioral changes, 5 had onset after age 18 years 
and the other 3 had onset between the ages of 15-17 years. 
Other common symptoms of neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric decline  appearing in our 
participants include sleep changes (often increased fatigue and napping) in 11 of 18 individuals 
(61.1%), with onset above age 15 years for all 11.  Mental status decline, increased support with 
activities of daily living (ADLs), and hallucinations were each noted  in 1 1 of 18 individuals 

(61.1%) with age of onset at 15 years or older  for all 1 1.  D epression was noted  in 9 of 18 
participants (50.0%), with age of onset at 16 years and above for all 9.  Individuals 543 (age 20), 
895 (age 22), and 823 (age 32) experienced all 8 neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric symptoms that 
we assessed. 
Seizures occurred in 5 out of 18 participants (27.8%) (Table 2).  Individual 21 had multiple 
seizures at age 23, but no other seizures reported throughout their lifetime and patient 113 had two 
focal seizures at ages 10 and 20  years.  Only 1 patient, 25, had  recurrent staring spells requiring 
antiseizure therapy, with topiramate.  For patients 341 and 115, the type of seizure was unspecified. 
Regarding cerebrovascular events , 4 out of 18 individuals (22.2%) had either a stroke , 
presumed stroke, or presumed transient ischemic attacks (TIAs).  Participant 21 (age 25 years) had 
a left frontal stroke at age 23 years and participant 25 (age 24 years) had recurrent episodes of face 
and hand weakness, most recently reported at age 23 years.  Participant 805 (age 51 years) has had 
chronic right -sided weakness since her mid -thirties that was attributed to a suspected stroke.   
Finally, individual 139 (age 32 years) had encephalomalacia suggesting a prior infarct on MRI at 
age 19 years, though the timing of the stroke is unclear.  Furthermore, this participant exhibited 
hemiparesis for most of her lifetime, with her right side being significantly weaker than  the left.  
Note that these 4 participants in our cohort are on the older end of our sample. 
  Hemiplegic migraine occurred in 2 (11.1%) individuals, both of whom were 24 years old 
at last evaluation and had migraines starting after age 18 years. All 18 individuals were able to 
walk at some point in their lives, though all the ones with available details exhibited gait ataxia 
and/or other gait abnormalities.  In the medical records, our participants’ gaits were most 
commonly described as “stiff,” “scissoring,” “spastic,” “narrow-based,” “with intoeing,” and 
“dragging toes and feet”.  Most affected individuals used a wheelchair, a walker, or both.  All 

participants experienced gross motor delay. Gait deteriorates with age in our cohort: many have 
experienced increased reliance on walkers and/or wheelchairs with age or increased ataxia, and 
eight participants (44.4%) lost ambulation entirely.  For these 8 participants, age of loss of 
ambulation ranged from childhood (ages 4 and 8) to adolescence (ages 14 and 15) to adulthood 
(age 30). 
Neuroimaging.  The three most common imaging findings were diffuse brain atrophy, white matter 
signal changes, and basal ganglia calcifications, seen in 13 of 15 (86.7%), 12 out of 15 (80.0%), 
and 11 of 15 (73.3%) participants, respectively, with records available (Table 4).  These changes 
were seen in participants as young as 14 months, and up to age 43.  White matter changes include 
hypomyelination and demyelination, often persisting from childhood into adulthood , and brain 
atrophy in these patients often encompasses the cerebrum and cerebellum, and sometimes the 
brainstem as well.  For participant 25, serial imaging at ages 4, 6, 8, 18, and 19 showed consistent 
diffuse demyelination. 
Variant identification.  While genotypes for most participants were obtained from medical records, 
the genotypes of two of ou r participants (113 and 139) were  unknown at enrollment.  Nanopore 
long read sequencing on genomic DNA from participant 113 identified 2 compound heterozygous 
variants (NM_000124.4(ERCC6):c.2143G>T (p.Gly715Ter) and c.3536del (p.Tyr1179fs)).  PCR 
amplification confirmed that the c.2143 G>T variant wa s maternally inherited and the c.3536 del 
variant was paternally inherited .  DNA samples were not available for 139 , who was diagnosed 
with CS before genetic testing was widely available.  
Genotype-Phenotype Associations.  Of our 18 participants, 10 had pathogenic variants in ERCC6 
(CSB); 7 participants had pathogenic variants in ERCC8 (CSA); and for one (139), the genotype 
was unknown (Figures 1-2).  The 3 individuals who experienced all 8 

neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric symptoms (543, 895, and 823) had ERCC6 variants. Patient 543 
had 2 compound heterozygous frameshift variants (c.1913A>G, p.Arg637Serfs*34; c.2247delT, 
p.Asp749Glufs*4); patient 895 had a homozygous nonsense variant (c.2203C>T, p.Arg735*); and 
patient 823 had the same nonsense variant ( c.2203C>T, p.Arg735*) in the heterozygous state 
paired with a heterozygous splice variant (c.3778+2T>G, p.Ser1240_Val1260delinsIle).  None of 
these patients had seizures or strokes.  Notably, the c.2203C>T, p.Arg735* variant appears in 1 
other pa rticipant (5), who also experienced a high burden of neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric 
symptoms.  Of the 5 patients who had seizures, 3 had the ERCC6 genotype and 2 had ERCC8.  Of 
the 4 patients who had strokes, 3 had the ERCC6 genotype and the genotype was unknown for the 
fourth.   
 
Discussion 
All but one of the 18 participants in this cohort experienced at least one symptom of 
neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric decline, with language decline, anxiety/behavioral decline, and 
sleep changes being the most common symptoms.  These findings suggest that individuals with 
CS develop aspects of dementia later in the disease course.  While tremors and neuropathy were 
found in the majority of our participants, with onset at younger age s consistent with prior 
reports1,15, neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric decline, strokes, and seizures tend to occur later in the 
disease course.  Pathogenic variants in ERCC6 have generally been considered to be associated 
with more severe CS phenotypes 7, in line with our findings of a higher 
neurocognitive/neuropsychiatric burden and higher rates of seizures and strokes in individuals with 
pathogenic variants in ERCC6 compared to ERCC8.  Two participants have taken carbidopa -
levodopa for tremors15.  It is likely that the presence of neuropathy is higher than what we recorded, 

as not all individuals had electrodiagnostic studies (EDX) and peripheral neuropathy is known to 
be an early feature of CS 19,20.  In contrast to a prior report indicating that seizures tended to be 
ongoing17, 2 of the 5 in our cohort had only rare seizures.  There are only a few other reports of 
cerebrovascular events in individuals with CS 21,22,23.  Notably, one of our participants had early 
cataracts and has survived past 26 years , despite a previously reported correlation between early 
cataracts and shorter life expectancies in CS patients 17. Three participants in our cohort reported 
hemiplegic migraines, a symptom that was only recently described in the literature in conjunction 
with CS24.  
CSA (encoded by ERCC8) and CSB (encoded by ERCC6) play important roles in different 
types of DNA repair.  CSA has no known  enzymatic function and is part of a multi -subunit E3 
ubiquitin ligase complex25.  Stalled RNA polymerases at sites of DNA damage activate CSA in a 
CSB-dependent manner, which in turn targets several proteins for ubiquitination, including CSB 
and RNA polymerase II (RNAPII) 26,27.  CSB is a DNA -dependent ATPase, a member of the 
SWI2/SNF2-family that plays an important role in DNA repair6.  CSB is recruited to double strand 
breaks (DSBs)  and transcription coupled -nucleotide excision repair ( TC-NER) through it s 
ubiquitin binding domain 28,29.  Also, CSB interacts directly with CSA to recruit it to RNAPII 
stalled at sites of DNA damage.  Once recruited, CSA promotes association of the DNA repair 
protein UVSSA with stalled RNAPII through ubiquitination as well as recruits the transcription 
initiation factor IIH (TFIIH)9.  In addition to its role in the repair of double-strand breaks and TC-
NER, CSB has roles in base excision repair (BER), transcription, and chromatin remodeling30.   
Inadequate DNA repair due to deficiencies in CSA and CSB results in the accumulation of 
DNA damage that may cause mitochondrial dysfunction , impaired mitophagy , and reduced 
cellular NAD+ levels 31.  Mitochondrial dysfunction has strong associations with aging and 

neurodegeneration, particularly in the context of Alzheimer’s Disease (AD)32.  Accumulation of 
DNA damage and mitochondrial dysfunction may account for many of the premature aging and 
neurodegeneration symptoms seen in CS patients31.  In addition to DNA repair, CSB appears to be 
crucial for the expression of neuronal genes and the differentiation of neurons 33.  Neurons 
differentiated from human CS induced pluripotent stem cells have reduced synaptic density and 
reduced network synchrony34. 
These cumulative effects of DNA  repair deficits, mitochondrial damage, and neuronal 
dysfunction may explain why we have found a distinct set of later-onset neurological 
complications in individuals with  CS, accompanied by progression of some earlier onset 
complications.  For example, the reduced synaptic network synchrony seen in CSB depleted 
neurons is also seen in seizures, which are characterized by altered neuronal synchrony35.  Recent 
studies implicated mitochondrial damage and impaired base excision repair (BER) with 
epileptogenesis36.  Thus, reduced neuronal synchrony and mitochondrial damage may help explain 
the presence of seizures in the later stages of CS. 
There are several potential explanations for the later onset cerebrovascular events in some 
individuals with CS in addition to differences in function among variant forms of CSA and CSB .  
Defective DNA repair is implicated in neuroinflammation and vascular endothelial dysfunction37.  
Both neuroinflammation and vascular dysfunction have been noted in CS mouse models and in 
humans with CS 37,38.  Furthermore, hypertension is documented in individuals with CS3, and 
atherosclerosis is observed in post-mortem brain of affected individuals39 – both are known risk 
factors for stroke. Recently, mitochondrial dysfunction has been  linked with atherosclerosis: 
damage of mtDNA can result in hyperlipidemia, inflammation, and other pro -atherogenic 
phenomena40.  In mitochondrial encephalopathy with lactic acidosis and stroke-like episodes 

(MELAS), mitochondrial dysfunction leads to vasculopathy, stroke -like events, and neuronal 
death41.  
Regarding a possible link between CS and dementia, there are  several intriguing parallels 
between the disease mechanisms of CS and AD .  Genomic instability and deficient DNA repair 
have been increasingly shown to have major roles in AD pathogenesis32.  Specifically, DNA repair 
pathway proteins are downregulated in AD brains , resulting in oxidative damage, mitochondrial 
dysfunction, and depleted NAD+ levels – all of which are also seen in CS 32.  Mitochondrial 
dysfunction due to accumulation of DNA repair deficits is a known driver of the premature aging 
seen in individuals with CS, and of the neurodegeneration seen in AD 31,32,42,43.  Furthermore, 
reduced synaptic density is a hallmark of dementia 43 and CS; alterations in calcium homeostasis 
have been noted in both diseases33,43.  
 Given that aging is the greatest risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease, an important question 
is whether CS , a syndrome of premature aging , is associated with the neuropathology of 
Alzheimer’s disease or other age -related neurodegenerative conditions.  Amyloid plaques have 
never, to our knowledge, been reported in  CS39,44,45. Neurofibrillary tangles, the other hallmark 
neuropathology of AD, have been inconsistently reported in individuals with CS39,44,46,47, but their 
presence in some cases is nonetheless noteworthy given their rarity in early life.  TDP-43, another 
late-life neurodegeneration related protein, 48 was detected  in a case report o f a  32-year-old 
individual with CS 49.  Given possible emergence of late -life neuropathologies in CS patients at 
young ages, future neurodegenerative-focused neuropathological studies in CS are warranted. 
 A limitation of our study was the retrospective design, which prevented us from 
systematically assessing individuals with CS for the late-onset complications longitudinally.  Our 
lack of access to the brain MR images prevented us from uniformly screening for specific 

neuroanatomic features of CS, some of which may have been overlooked when the studies were 
performed prior to the establishment of the CS diagnosis. 
 This study draws attention to the complex longitudinal course of CS, which may be 
relatively static during childhood for many affected  individuals, but then evolve s into a more 
clearly neurodegenerative phase later in the course.  Monitoring, treatment, and prevention of these 
complications will be important to pursue in the years to come.
