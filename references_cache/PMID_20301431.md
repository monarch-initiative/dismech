CLINICAL CHARACTERISTICS: Carnitine palmitoyltransferase (CPT) II deficiency is
a disorder of long-chain fatty-acid oxidation. Three clinical phenotypes are
typically described: lethal neonatal, severe infantile hepatocardiomuscular, and
myopathic. However, a spectrum exists across these phenotypes, leading to a
continuum of disease. Lethal neonatal and severe infantile hepatocardiomuscular
phenotypes are severe multisystemic diseases characterized by liver failure with
hypoketotic hypoglycemia, cardiomyopathy, seizures, and early death. The
myopathic phenotype is usually mild and can manifest from infancy to adulthood
with exercise-induced muscle pain and weakness, often progressing to episodic
rhabdomyolysis and myoglobinuria. The CPT2-related myopathic phenotype is the
most common disorder of lipid metabolism affecting skeletal muscle and the most
frequent cause of hereditary myoglobinuria. Males are more likely to be
symptomatic than females.
DIAGNOSIS/TESTING: The diagnosis of CPT II deficiency is established in a
proband with biallelic pathogenic variants in CPT2 identified by molecular
genetic testing or reduced CPT enzyme activity in muscle or fibroblasts.
MANAGEMENT: Targeted therapies: Low-fat/high-carbohydrate diet as prescribed by
metabolic dietician; triheptanoin for one third of daily calories. Medium-chain
triglyceride (MCT) oil can be substituted in areas where triheptanoin is not
available. Supportive care: In addition to dietary management, frequent meals
and avoidance of extended fasting, treatment of non-hypoglycemic seizures with
anti-seizure medication, ongoing care coordination in planning for surgeries or
procedures, education of caregivers and educators, and ensure a plan for
transition to adult care. Emergency outpatient treatment includes oral feeds if
not vomiting, carbohydrate and triheptanoin supplementation, antiemetics for
occasional vomiting, antipyretics for fever, and treatment of underlying
infections. Acute inpatient treatment includes D10-containing intravenous fluids
with electrolytes, laboratory assessment for liver, kidney, and muscle disease,
dietary management as tolerated, management of liver disease per hepatologist,
management of arrhythmia or cardiomyopathy per cardiologist, intravenous
hydration and/or dialysis for rhabdomyolysis and myoglobinuria, neurology
evaluation for new or evolving neurologic findings, and anti-seizure medication
per neurologist. Surveillance: Evaluation by a metabolic specialist and
metabolic dietician and laboratory measurement of plasma acylcarnitine profile,
creatine kinase, comprehensive metabolic panel, liver function panel, and plasma
total and free carnitine levels; measurement of growth parameters at each visit;
follow-up liver imaging per hepatologist; follow-up electrocardiogram and
echocardiogram per cardiologist; follow-up kidney imaging per nephrologist;
assess for episodes of rhabdomyolysis and myoglobinuria at each visit; follow-up
EEG as needed; assess developmental progress and educational needs at each
visit; quality of life assessment for affected individuals and
parents/caregivers at each visit. Agents/circumstances to avoid: Avoid extended
fasting and prolonged exercise. General anesthesia should proceed with caution
due to possible risk of malignant hyperthermia or rhabdomyolysis. Evaluation of
relatives at risk: Evaluate apparently asymptomatic at-risk sibs so that
morbidity and mortality can be reduced by early diagnosis and treatment. For
at-risk newborn sibs when prenatal testing was not performed, molecular genetic
testing for the familial CPT2 pathogenic variants can be done in parallel with
NBS. Genetic testing of at-risk asymptomatic sibs is advisable before general
anesthesia, as complications of general anesthesia have been observed.
GENETIC COUNSELING: CPT II deficiency is inherited in an autosomal recessive
manner. If both parents are known to be heterozygous for a CPT2 pathogenic
variant, each sib of an affected individual has at conception a 25% chance of
inheriting biallelic pathogenic variants and being affected, a 50% chance of
being heterozygous, and a 25% chance of inheriting neither of the familial
pathogenic variants. Heterozygous sibs of a proband with CPT II deficiency can
have a biochemically intermediate phenotype but generally do not display
clinical manifestations. If the CPT2 pathogenic variants have been identified in
an affected family member, carrier testing for at-risk relatives and
prenatal/preimplantation genetic testing for CPT II deficiency are possible.
