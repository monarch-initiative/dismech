# Isolated splenic tuberculosis presenting with spontaneous rupture in an immunocompetent adolescent: a case report from an endemic area.
**Authors:** Umer K, Biri P, Abrar F, Dagafa D, Bushira H, Musema A, W/Yohannis A, Ahmed M, Ali S, Abamecha J, Mahfuz M, Fayisa S
**Journal:** Ann Med Surg (Lond) (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1097/MS9.0000000000004052](https://doi.org/10.1097/MS9.0000000000004052)

## Content

INTRODUCTION AND IMPORTANCE: Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global health concern, particularly in developing countries, where its extrapulmonary forms present diagnostic challenges. Splenic involvement is rare and can lead to life-threatening complications if not promptly recognized and managed.

CASE PRESENTATION: We describe the case of an 18-year-old Ethiopian male with a history of childhood TB who presented with severe left upper quadrant pain, progressive weight loss, fever, night sweats, and recurrent syncopal episodes. Despite initial intravenous antibiotic therapy, symptoms persisted. Laboratory and imaging studies revealed severe anemia, splenic hematoma, hemoperitoneum, and a splenic artery pseudoaneurysm with active bleeding. The patient underwent emergency splenectomy. Histopathological analysis confirmed necrotizing granulomatous inflammation consistent with TB. He was started on anti-TB therapy postoperatively and showed marked clinical improvement.

CLINICAL DISCUSSION: Splenic TB is a rare manifestation of extrapulmonary TB and may mimic other splenic pathologies. In this case, the presentation with splenic hematoma and pseudoaneurysm posed significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Failure to respond to broad-spectrum antibiotics and the presence of systemic symptoms emphasized the importance of considering TB in the differential diagnosis. Prompt surgical intervention combined with anti-TB therapy was critical in preventing fatal outcomes.

CONCLUSION: This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for TB in patients presenting with unexplained abdominal symptoms in endemic regions. Early recognition and timely intervention are essential to prevent serious complications such as splenic rupture and the need for emergency surgical management.

Tuberculosis (TB) continues to be a significant public health concern globally, particularly in developing countries where prevalence rates are alarmingly high. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), TB ranks among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality, with millions affected each year[ 1 ]. While pulmonary TB and disseminated is the most recognized form, extrapulmonary manifestations, including splenic tuberculosis, are increasingly acknowledged in clinical practice and research. Isolated splenic involvement is notably rare, constituting only a small percentage of extrapulmonary cases, and can lead to serious complications, including spontaneous rupture of the spleen[ 2 ]. HIGHLIGHTS Recognizing splenic tuberculosis as a rare but serious form of extrapulmonary TB is crucial in endemic areas. The nonspecific symptoms of splenic TB, such as abdominal pain, fever, and weight loss, can easily mimic other conditions, complicating timely diagnosis and intervention. While splenic rupture is typically trauma-related, TB can induce spontaneous rupture and bleeding, leading to life-threatening situations. In cases of unexplained splenic rupture, histopathological examination of the removed spleen can uncover underlying causes, as seen in our case where splenic TB was diagnosed post-rupture. Effective treatment of splenic tuberculosis requires coordination among surgeons, pathologists, and infectious disease specialists to ensure optimal care and monitoring.

Splenic tuberculosis typically occurs in the context of disseminated TB, often affecting immunocompromised individuals or those with a prior history of the disease[ 3 ]. The spleen plays a critical role in the immune system by filtering blood and mounting immune responses, and its involvement in TB can result in pathologies such as abscess formation, infarction, and, in severe cases, rupture. The clinical presentation of splenic TB may be nonspecific, often mimicking other abdominal conditions, complicating diagnosis and timely intervention[ 3 ].

This case report discusses an 18-year-old male patient who developed splenic tuberculosis that culminated in splenic rupture. This case underscores the necessity for a high index of suspicion when evaluating patients with a history of TB who present with abdominal pain, particularly in regions with a high burden of the disease.

Early recognition and appropriate treatment of splenic TB are paramount in preventing life-threatening complications such as splenic rupture, which necessitates urgent surgical intervention. By highlighting this case, we aim to emphasize the critical importance of early diagnosis and effective management strategies in mitigating the risks associated with this rare but severe manifestation of tuberculosis. These case report has reported in line with SCARE guideline[ 4 ].

An 18-year-old male presented to our hospital in April 2024 with severe left upper quadrant (LUQ) abdominal pain persisting for 1 month. His symptoms were accompanied by alarming systemic manifestations, including low-grade fever, night sweats, significant loss of appetite, and profound fatigue. Notably, he had experienced a dramatic weight loss of approximately 25 kg, dropping from 67 kg to 42 kg within this period. Additionally, he reported frequent syncopal episodes, raising concerns about his overall health status.

His medical history revealed a past diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) during childhood, for which he completed a six-month course of anti-TB therapy ten years earlier. Before his admission to our hospital, he was evaluated at a nearby hospital, where he was diagnosed with a suspected splenic abscess or infarction. TB was not considered in this case, as it is an uncommon presentation, particularly since the patient did not exhibit cough, which is typically a key symptom prompting consideration of TB in the differential diagnosis. Despite receiving intravenous antibiotics and analgesics, his condition did not improve.

Upon admission, the patient appeared severely cachectic and physically debilitated. His vital signs indicated significant distress: blood pressure of 90/60 mmHg, pulse rate of 100 bpm, temperature of 37.8°C, and respiratory rate of 24 breaths per minute. A focused abdominal examination revealed marked tenderness and guarding, particularly in the LUQ.

The patient’s lab results showed leukocytosis (WBC: 10.75 × 103/µL) with neutrophilia (7.77 × 103/µL), anemia (HGB: 10.4 g/dL, HCT: 31.1%), and thrombocytosis (PLT: 666 × 103/µL). Inflammatory markers were elevated, with CRP at 48 mg/L. Liver and renal function tests were mostly normal, though direct bilirubin was slightly high (0.27 mg/dL). Pancreatic enzymes showed mild elevations in amylase (104.3 U/mL) and lipase (31.3 U/mL). Coagulation studies were within range, with a PT of 13.9s, APTT of 26.6s, and INR of 1.18. Viral serology for hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and HIV was negative.

A chest X-ray was unremarkable; however, an abdominal CT scan with contrast revealed a non-enhancing hyperdense splenic parenchyma suggestive of hematoma extending into the peri-splenic and left subhepatic spaces. Additional findings included hemoperitoneum in the interloop and pelvic peritoneum, a splenic artery pseudoaneurysm with active bleeding, hepatosplenomegaly, and minimal left pleural effusion (Fig 1). A presplenic collection is noted, accompanied by architectural loss of the spleen (D).

Following admission, the patient received four units of cross-matched blood transfusion and was started on broad-spectrum antibiotics. An exploratory laparotomy was subsequently performed, revealing a ruptured, enlarged spleen filled with clotted blood, though no active bleeding was observed. Approximately 2500 mL of clotted blood was evacuated from the peritoneal cavity. Inflammatory adhesions were noted between the splenic capsule and the diaphragm, along with a 1 cm accessory spleen at the splenorenal ligament (Fig. 2). Figure 2. (A) Illustrates intraoperative images of the exploration and resection of a ruptured spleen. (B) Revealing a fractured necrotic enlarged spleen after removal.

The hematoma was carefully evacuated, and a splenectomy was performed with meticulous dissection and ligation of the vessels at the splenic hilum. The abdominal cavity was thoroughly irrigated with warm saline, and tissue samples were collected for histopathological analysis. During the intraoperative and postoperative periods, the patient received additional transfusions of packed red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, and platelets as required. His postoperative course was uneventful, and he was discharged after 5 days of hospitalization. Before discharge, he received the PCV13, Haemophilus influenzae, and meningococcal vaccines, along with a prescription for amoxicillin 250 mg daily for 2 years as part of his post-splenectomy prophylaxis, in accordance with the IDSA 2023 Guidelines[ 5 ].Regular follow-up appointments are scheduled to assess vaccination status and adherence to prophylactic measures.

One week later, histopathological analysis confirmed necrotizing granulomatous inflammation consistent with tuberculosis (Fig. 3). Figure 3. (A) Cross-section of the spleen showing whitish nodules. (D) Necrotizing granuloma with Langhans giant cells (magnification ×40).

The patient was subsequently initiated on anti-TB therapy (isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide for 2 months, followed by isoniazid and rifampicin for 4 months).At his 1-month follow-up, he had regained 7 kg and demonstrated significant clinical improvement with ongoing nutritional support, including ready-to-use therapeutic food (RUTF). After completing anti-TB treatment, he was in much better condition, weighing 65 kg. He also received additional doses of prophylaxis. Tuberculosis (TB) was diagnosed over other granulomatous diseases, such as fungal infections, lymphoma, and sarcoidosis, based on several factors: the clinical presentation, a history of past TB treatment, and the absence of fungal hyphae on the Grocott stain. Additionally, the epidemiology and distinct histopathologic features of TB, along with the patient’s rapid improvement following the initiation of anti-TB treatment, further support the diagnosis.

This case report contributes to the expanding literature on splenic tuberculosis (TB) as a rare yet significant manifestation of extrapulmonary TB. The patient presented with classic symptoms of splenic involvement, including severe left upper quadrant pain, fever, night sweats, and noticeable weight loss. Given the patient’s history of childhood TB, these symptoms warranted a thorough evaluation, which ultimately revealed a ruptured spleen due to necrotizing granulomatous inflammation.

Splenic tuberculosis, though increasingly recognized, remains a rare form of extrapulmonary TB. It typically develops in the context of disseminated TB in immunocompromised but can also occur in immunocompetent individuals. The isolated involvement of the spleen can lead to serious clinical challenges and complications, such as abscesses, infarction, and rupture[ 6 ].

Globally, TB is a leading infectious disease killer, with approximately 10.8 million new cases reported in 2023. While splenic involvement is uncommon, its recognition is crucial due to the potential for severe outcomes. Splenic TB often occurs alongside other systemic infections or in individuals with underlying conditions, such as HIV/AIDS, malignancies, or diabetes mellitus[ 7 , 8 ].

Patients with splenic TB frequently exhibit nonspecific symptoms, including fever, abdominal pain, weight loss, and night sweats. These manifestations can easily mimic other abdominal disorders, complicating the diagnostic process. Imaging techniques, such as ultrasound and CT scans, may reveal splenic lesions; however, definitive diagnosis usually requires histopathological examination of biopsy or surgically obtained tissue[ 6 , 9 ].

The clinical presentation is consistent with prior studies indicating that splenic TB often presents with nonspecific symptoms, which can lead to misdiagnosis. This nonspecificity can further delay diagnosis and treatment, a concern highlighted in the existing literature[ 6 ]. The patient’s background of childhood TB was pivotal in prompting further investigation, ultimately leading to the diagnosis of splenic rupture.

Comparative analysis with previous case reports sheds light on the challenges of diagnosing splenic TB. For instance, Sharma et al documented solitary splenic tuberculosis in a 64-year-old woman who presented with abdominal discomfort and was diagnosed following splenectomy, underscoring the diagnostic difficulties associated with the nonspecific nature of splenic TB[ 9 ]. Similarly, Prieto-Nieto et al detailed an inflammatory pseudotumor of the spleen linked to splenic tuberculosis in a 50-year-old female patient, emphasizing the necessity of histopathological examination for diagnosis confirmation[ 10 ]. Research by Ali & Khan identified several risk factors for splenic tuberculosis, including immunosuppression and a history of TB, significantly increasing the likelihood of splenic involvement. Additional factors include diabetes mellitus, malignancies, and HIV/AIDS, which can compromise the immune system and facilitate TB dissemination[ 8 ]. The WHO estimates that about 37% of all new TB cases are extrapulmonary, with splenic involvement being particularly rare[ 7 ].

A case series by Gupta et al reported multiple instances of splenic TB, noting that delayed diagnosis often results in severe complications, such as hemorrhagic shock. Our case supports this observation, as the patient required urgent surgical intervention due to a ruptured spleen. This situation highlights the critical need for a high index of suspicion for TB in patients presenting with unexplained abdominal symptoms, especially in regions with a high burden of the disease[ 11 ].

Imaging studies in our case indicated a splenic hematoma and active bleeding, prompting urgent exploratory laparotomy. Surgical findings were consistent with literature reports, where splenic rupture typically presents with significant hematomas and inflammatory changes. While ultrasound and CT scans are essential for identifying splenic lesions, they have limitations in differentiating between malignancies and infectious processes[ 12 ]. CT imaging usually reveals hypodense lesions, which can be misinterpreted as tumors or abscesses. Therefore, histopathological confirmation is vital for a definitive diagnosis, generally relying on the examination of excised tissue.

The patient’s postoperative recovery was uneventful, and his positive response to anti-TB therapy aligns with findings from other studies highlighting that timely initiation of treatment can significantly enhance outcomes. The standard treatment regimen for splenic tuberculosis typically consists of a combination of anti-TB medications – isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol – for at least 6 months[ 13 ]. Recent research indicates that early initiation of therapy can reduce mortality and morbidity associated with extrapulmonary TB, especially in immunocompromised patients.

This case underscores the necessity for a multidisciplinary approach in managing splenic tuberculosis. Collaboration among surgeons, pathologists, and infectious disease specialists is essential for optimal patient care. The involvement of infectious disease specialists ensures appropriate antibiotic coverage and monitoring for potential complications. As TB continues to pose significant health threats, particularly in resource-limited settings, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for atypical presentations, including splenic involvement, to mitigate the risks of severe complications.

The global burden of TB remains a critical public health issue, resulting in substantial morbidity and mortality worldwide. In 2021, the WHO reported an estimated 10.6 million new TB cases and 1.6 million deaths due to TB, highlighting the ongoing challenges in managing this infectious disease[ 7 ]. Increased awareness and training for healthcare providers to recognize atypical presentations of TB are vital for improving outcomes. This case emphasizes the complexities and challenges in diagnosing and managing splenic tuberculosis, underscoring the need for heightened clinical awareness and a collaborative approach to treating this rare but serious condition. Continued research into splenic TB and its management is essential for enhancing diagnosis, treatment, and patient outcomes.

Splenic tuberculosis, though rare, can lead to severe complications such as splenic rupture, requiring urgent intervention. Early recognition, imaging, and histopathological confirmation are essential for timely diagnosis, especially in TB-endemic regions. Prompt anti-TB therapy improves outcomes and prevents life-threatening complications. Increased clinical awareness and a high index of suspicion are vital to ensure early detection and management of extrapulmonary TB in endemic regions. Additionally, larger studies or comprehensive case series are needed to evaluate the role of conservative treatment versus surgical intervention in similar cases.
