# Pulmonary manifestations of STAC3 disorder: A case series.
**Authors:** Williams SP, Hernández Rodríguez MY, Cass B, Ali-Dinar T, ElMallah MK
**Journal:** Pediatr Pulmonol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ppul.26815](https://doi.org/10.1002/ppul.26815)

## Content

STAC3 disorder, formerly known as Native American myopathy, was first described by Bailey and Bloch in 1987 when a 3mo infant of Lumbee descent had an episode of malignant hyperthermia during general anesthesia1. The infant was noted to have several congenital anomalies including cleft palate, micrognathia, clubbed feet, and arthrogryposis. Several cases have been diagnosed and described since, but with such phenotypic complexity the clinical course is still unclear especially from a pulmonary standpoint.

STAC3, a muscle-specific adaptor protein, is a component of the excitation-contraction (EC) coupling machinery which is essential for normal muscle function2. STAC3 is specifically expressed by skeletal muscle as early as embryogenesis, and defects in this machinery are associated with numerous congenital myopathies1. Formal diagnostic criteria is not established; thus, diagnosis is made based on clinical suspicion which leads to molecular genetic testing. Genetic testing reveals biallelic pathogenic variants in STAC3 on chromosome 12q13–141,3. The high rate of consanguinity of the Lumbee population increases the frequency of this autosomal recessive disorder. However, although this myopathic disorder was originally described in individuals from the Lumbee Native American tribe of south-central North Carolina, STAC3 disorder has now been identified in numerous other populations including African, Middle Eastern, Puerto Rican, Turkish, Afro-Caribbean, Comoro Islands, and South American3.

STAC3 disorder is characterized by congenital myopathy involving both weakness and contractures that affect muscles of the trunk and extremities3. In addition, buccal muscle involvement results in upper airway and feeding difficulties3. Patients also have abnormalities of the palate, micrognathia, scoliosis, kyphosis, short stature, delayed motor milestones, and an increased risk of malignant hyperthermia3. Respiratory impairments have been identified in approximately 43% of patients with molecularly confirmed STAC33. Respiratory insufficiencies may present at any stage, even in adulthood, and may include pulmonary hypoplasia, nocturnal hypoventilation, restrictive lung disease, chronic respiratory failure, and ventilatory support dependence among other complications3. In this manuscript, we describe the respiratory findings and pulmonary clinical course of five patients with STAC3 disorder in our pediatric neuromuscular center with the overall goal of highlighting the pulmonary course for pediatric pulmonologists and primary pediatricians caring for patients with STAC3 disorders (Table 1). The Duke Institutional Review Board approved this retrospective chart review, and permitted waiver of consent for this study.

All five patients had genetic testing which confirmed that they all had the C.851G>C missense variant, one of the five pathogenic variants identified to date for molecularly confirmed STAC3 disorder. This variant leads to decreases in the quantity and quality of Ca2+ channels in the excitation-contraction coupling machinery of skeletal muscle which translates to an insufficiency in the frequency and strength of muscular contraction, including muscles involved in respiration2,3. In pre-clinical studies, mice with a stac3 knock-out mutation have smaller, more translucent diaphragms as well as abnormalities of the ribs, sternum, and costal cartilage when compared to control mice2. Further, zebrafish with the C.851G>C missense variant (the same mutation seen in our patients) have decreased calcium signaling in fast muscle twitching which decreases excitation-contraction coupling2. These pre-clinical findings could explain the progressive respiratory muscle weakness and restrictive lung disease seen in our patients.

Since this STAC3 disorder is progressive, respiratory function testing should be measured on a regular basis, i.e every six months despite initial normal findings. The variability seen in average FVC, FEV1, cough peak flow, and MIP/MEP as well as the changes over time highlight the need for individualized plans with continued monitoring. There is no trend or correlation identified at this time with specific pathogenic variations leading to poorer respiratory function or decline in function sooner than another pathogenic variant. Many of the complications that these patients face arise in the setting of respiratory infections or difficulties with aspiration4. Thus, as respiratory weakness progresses, the addition of an assisted cough device using positive and negative pressurewill promote airway clearance4. Further, with progressive restrictive lung disease, nocturnal support with positive airway pressure can alleviate nocturnal hypoventilation4.

It is imperative to have up to date medical history, documentation, and clear communication when preparing these patients for procedures as they are at an increased risk of malignant hyperthermia. Although the genetics of MH are not fully understood the reactions generally occurs in people with RYR1, CACNA1S, or STAC3 mutations, with most cases being due to a defect in the ryanodine receptor.5 Symptoms of MH include hyperthermia, tachycardia, tachypnea, increased CO2 production, increased O2 consumption, acidosis, hyperkalemia, muscle rigidity, and rhabdomyolysis,5 however MH-like reactions have been described with similar symptoms but the absence of biochemical findings.1 Special precautions should be taken to avoid the use of inciting agents including volatile anesthetic gases such as halothane and the depolarizing muscle relaxant succinylcholine, but close monitoring is also advised after procedures as the stress itself can precipitate MH.5 Though non-anesthetic malignant hyperthermia, or stress-induced malignant hyperthermia, are very rare, it is unclear if patients with STAC3 myopathy have an increased risk of this version of MH in addition to their increased risk of anesthetic-induced MH.

Respiratory insufficiency at birth, the need for invasive ventilation, and difficulty weaning respiratory support may be a few of the signs that may lead to further genetic investigation to diagnose STAC3 myopathy. As demonstrated above, the clinical manifestations and respiratory progression of this myopathic condition may vary and can present at different stages of life. Care for STAC3 disorder patients should be multidisciplinary based on symptomology. Once a diagnosis is established it is important for these patients to have regular pulmonary monitoring of their respiratory function, cough strength, ability to manage secretions, and sleep symptoms.
