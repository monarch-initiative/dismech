# Lung function and secondhand smoke exposure among children with cystic fibrosis: A Bayesian meta-analysis.
**Authors:** Reifenberg J, Gecili E, Pestian T, Andrinopoulou ER, Ryan PH, Brokamp C, Collaco JM, Szczesniak RD
**Journal:** J Cyst Fibros (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jcf.2023.04.020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcf.2023.04.020)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Secondhand smoke exposure, an important environmental health factor in cystic fibrosis (CF), remains uniquely challenging to children with CF as they strive to maintain pulmonary function during early stages of growth and throughout adolescence. Despite various epidemiologic studies among CF populations, little has been done to coalesce estimates of the association between secondhand smoke exposure and lung function decline.

METHODS: A systematic review was performed using PRISMA guidelines. A Bayesian random-effects model was employed to estimate the association between secondhand smoke exposure and change in lung function (measured as FEV1% predicted).

RESULTS: Quantitative synthesis of study estimates indicated that second-hand smoke exposure corresponded to a significant drop in FEV1 (estimated decrease: -5.11% predicted; 95% CI: -7.20, -3.47). The estimate of between-study heterogeneity was 1.32% predicted (95% CI: 0.05, 4.26). There was moderate heterogeneity between the 6 analyzed studies that met review criteria (degree of heterogeneity: I2=61.9% [95% CI: 7.3-84.4%] and p = 0.022 from the frequentist method.) CONCLUSIONS: Our results quantify the impact at the pediatric population level and corroborate the assertion that secondhand smoke exposure negatively affects pulmonary function in children with CF. Findings highlight challenges and opportunities for future environmental health interventions in pediatric CF care.

Children with secondhand tobacco smoke exposure (SHSe) display worse pulmonary function than those without SHSe (1), and it is an important modifier and exacerbator in cystic fibrosis (CF) lung disease (2, 3). According to the US Cystic Fibrosis Foundation Patient Registry (CFFPR) Annual Report, the estimated prevalence of monthly, daily or weekly SHSe in individuals with CF aged < 18 years in the US is about 17% among those who report it; however, smoke exposure is unknown for 38.4% of people with CF who are followed in the registry (4). Characterized by declining lung function and recurrent infections (5), clinical evaluation and monitoring of CF lung disease progression requires regular pulmonary function testing particularly during adolescence and young adulthood when individuals are at highest risk of rapid lung function decline (6). Despite differences in how SHSe data are collected (e.g.: self-report as in the US CF Registry (4) versus biomarker measurements like urine cotinine concentration (7)), the emphasis is largely on lung function, measured as forced expiratory volume in 1 second of % predicted (hereafter, FEV1% predicted), as the outcome.

Among children with CF, SHSe has been linked to a decrease in FEV1% predicted through a myriad of epidemiologic investigations, but pooled estimates of the effect of SHSe on lung function are lacking, and the heterogeneity between such studies has not been characterized. While studies have examined SHSe and FEV1 in children with and without CF, it is important to provide accurate information for parents, practitioners, and families to inform them of complications and harm reduction efforts. The purpose of the present study was to perform a systematic literature review and meta-analysis of FEV1 % predicted in children with CF with SHSe, with the goal of identifying gaps to bolster future research agendas for CF environmental health interventions.

Databases employed in the literature search (1990-present) included PUBMED, SCOPUS and MedLine. Recent issues of relevant journals were also searched via the University of Cincinnati Medical Center Libraries. Inclusion criteria were as follows: Studies must be peer-reviewed human subjects research reported in English, results presented with median or mean with appropriate standard deviation (SD) and FEV1 % predicted and must have a comparison group. Exclusion criteria specified no animal studies. Recommendations put forth in the ‚ÄòPreferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)‚Äô guidelines were undertaken for reporting findings and preparing this review article (8).

Data were initially extracted by one coauthor (TP) and ultimately verified for relevant data quantities by coauthors (JMC, EG, JR, RDS), which included direct correspondence with authors of three prior studies to obtain proper data. Relevant data quantities included study year, design, age, standard deviation (SD) of age, sample size, and mean (SD) of FEV1 % predicted.

A Bayesian random-effects model was employed to perform Bayesian meta-analysis (9, 10). As we anticipated considerable between-study heterogeneity, a random-effects model was used to pool effect sizes across studies. All analyses were conducted in R software (version 4.1.0) using the ‚Äòmeta‚Äô (version 5.1‚Äì1) and ‚Äòbrms‚Äô (version 2.15.0) packages. The Bayesian random-effects model was implemented via Markov Chain Monte-Carlo (MCMC) in the ‚Äòbrms‚Äô package. In our Bayesian meta-analysis, we used prior distributions informed by previous research (10, 11). The rationale for using the Bayesian meta-analysis approach was that it provides complete information about the credible parameter values and allows direct modeling of the uncertainty in estimates of pooled effect sizes and heterogeneity parameters. Particularly when the number of included studies is small, which is very common in practice, the Bayesian approach is superior in estimating pooled effects (10, 12). Prior assumptions on the parameter of interest were incorporated by assigning weakly informative priors on model parameters for meta-analysis (Section A of the supplement). Results are expressed as the pooled mean difference between SHSe and non-SHSe groups, which represent the effect size (since the outcome variable has the same unit of measurement for all analyzed studies and groups). Corresponding 95% credible intervals (denoted as CI) are reported. Estimates with a 95% CI that did not overlap zero were considered statistically significant. We performed additional analyses in which the results are expressed as the pooled standardized mean difference (Cohen‚Äôs d), to provide results in statistical units. R code to produce the results presented in this article is provided with detailed steps (Section E of the supplement).

Sensitivity analyses of the Bayesian approach were performed by changing distributional assumptions in the model through specifying prior distributions on model parameters. Although the Bayesian approach was our primary method in this work given the aforementioned advantages over traditional methods (10, 13), we examined sensitivity of results by performing a frequentist meta-analysis. Specifically, we implemented a traditional random-effects model using the ‚Äòmeta‚Äô package to compare the findings of both meta-analysis methods.

We conducted influence analysis by using a leave-one-out paradigm internally and produced data for different types of influence diagnostics for both the Bayesian and frequentist meta-analyses. Influence diagnostics and Baujat diagnostic plots were constructed. For leave-one-out analyses, we performed the meta-analysis without a specific study and compared the findings with the main results in terms of estimated effect size and between-study heterogeneity. These diagnostics were used to determine which study or effect size may excessively influence overall results of the meta-analysis and/or contribute substantially to the between-study heterogeneity in an analysis.

All databases were initially searched by the keywords ‚Äòsecondhand smoke‚Äô, ‚Äòpassive smoke‚Äô, ‚Äòcystic fibrosis‚Äô, and ‚Äòchildren‚Äô. This search yielded 138 results. Duplicate studies were removed resulting in 75 studies for screening. Studies that were before 1990 (n=1) and not human subjects research (n=17) were excluded. This search yielded 57 studies for further inclusion assessment. Of these, 6 studies met inclusion/exclusion criteria. The 51 studies were excluded because they were not related to CF or SHSe, did not have a comparison group, did not include FEV1 measures, or included infants or adults (incorrect patient population). One study was excluded because it was found to have a repeated cohort from a study that was included. Additional searches were conducted in recent issues of relevant journals in CF, tobacco research, and health promotion using the University of Cincinnati Medical Center Libraries. No additional studies were identified (Figure 1).
