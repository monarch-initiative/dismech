Myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA) is a 
working diagnosis encompassing a wide spectrum of ischemic and non-ischemic 
conditions. We report the case of a 57-year-old male admitted for exertional 
chest pain and elevated troponin levels, initially managed as suspected MINOCA. 
Coronary angiography excluded obstructive coronary artery disease, while 
subsequent multimodality imaging unexpectedly revealed Scimitar syndrome, a rare 
congenital heart disease. This case underscores the diagnostic complexity of 
MINOCA and the crucial role of advanced imaging in uncovering alternative, 
non-ischemic etiologies.

Myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA) poses a significant diagnostic challenge for physicians due to its varied underlying causes. According to the European Society of Cardiology[1] and the American Heart Association[2], the term MINOCA refers to ischemic conditions such as epicardial vasospasm and nonobstructive atherosclerotic plaque instability. However, several nonischemic diseases have similar presentations to MINOCA. Therefore, the term MINOCA is frequently used as a descriptive diagnosis until further evaluation confirms an ischemic mechanism or reveals an alternate diagnosis. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) is a crucial tool in diagnosing these patients, and it can uncover unexpected findings.

A 57-year-old male with no known cardiovascular risk factors, who regularly participated in sports, presented to the emergency room with several episodes of typical chest pain during exertion and palpitations. On a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG), the patient showed subtle, nonspecific ST-segment changes [Figure 1] and his highly sensitive troponin-I was elevated (peak value 211 ng/l, normal value 57 ng/L). Upon admission, the patient was asymptomatic and his physical examination was unremarkable: Vital parameters, including blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation, were all within normal limits. Laboratory tests showed normal values for both blood cell counts and C-reactive protein. A nasopharyngeal swab was obtained, resulting in a negative test for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 infection. Transthoracic echocardiography showed normal morphology and function of the left cardiac chambers and excluded valvular and pericardial diseases. However, difficulty in visualizing the right chambers was noted [Figure 2]. Coronary angiography revealed normal coronary arteries [Figure 3], which raised the suspicion of MINOCA. To investigate the etiology of myocardial damage, the patient underwent CMR imaging. The CMR showed no evidence of myocardial edema in the T2 mapping sequences and no late gadolinium enhancement. Nonetheless, CMR uncovered right chamber enlargement (right ventricular end diastolic volume 118 ml/m2), along with the ectasia of inferior vena cava (IVC) and pulmonary trunk, attributed to a left-to-right shunt arising from anomalous pulmonary venous return of the right lung in IVC [Figure 4]. Chest computed tomography (CT) was performed to better define the anatomy of the pulmonary venous return, which revealed a hypoplastic right lung, dextroposition of the heart, right ventricular dilatation, and total anomalous venous return of the right lung draining vertically through a large venous collector (the scimitar vein) into the suprahepatic IVC [Figure 5]. Abnormal blood vessels from the descending aorta and right inferior diaphragmatic artery were distributed within the right lung base, causing pulmonary sequestration. A retrospective review of the chest X-ray obtained in the Emergency Department revealed a reduced right lung area with a partial shift of cardio mediastinal structures to the right and a curvilinear structure extending to the cardiophrenic angle overlying the right heart border [Figure 6]. All of these findings were consistent with the diagnosis of Scimitar Syndrome.[3] The patient experienced no further episodes of chest pain or palpitations during hospitalization and was discharged on the 6th day. After hospitalization, the patient was referred to an adult congenital heart disease tertiary care center for further evaluation. The center excluded the need for surgical intervention due to the absence of symptoms and instead recommended a cardiopulmonary exercise test and regular follow-up.

The diagnosis of MINOCA remains a diagnostic challenge. This clinical case demonstrates how an initial suspicion of MINOCA, through diagnostic investigations such as CT and magnetic resonance imaging, incidentally revealed the presence of a previously unrecognized congenital heart condition: the Scimitar syndrome. Scimitar syndrome is a rare congenital heart defect that is a variant of partial anomalous pulmonary venous return, resulting in a left-to-right shunt, with a characteristic anatomical feature resembling a Turkish sword known as “scimitar.”[4] The estimated incidence is 1–3 per 100,000 live births, with adult-onset presentation being rare and generally characterized by exertional dyspnea, exercise intolerance, or abnormal heart rhythms. A thorough evaluation by a specialist in congenital heart disease is often necessary to establish an accurate diagnosis and develop an appropriate treatment plan. In this case, the abnormal drainage of blood into the right side of the heart caused volume overload, right heart dilation, and increased blood flow to the lungs. These hemodynamic changes may explain the patient’s symptoms, including palpitations and chest pain, which could be related to right atrial enlargement – a known factor that predisposes to arrhythmias. Therefore, the elevated cardiac enzymes and chest pain are unlikely to have a coronary origin and are more consistent with a type II myocardial infarction mechanism. Although the patient reported palpitations, no arrhythmias were documented during hospitalization. Nonetheless, ambulatory follow-up with ECG Holter monitoring has been prescribed.

our case report emphasizes the diagnostic complexity of MINOCA and underscores the significance of advanced imaging techniques for detecting underlying cardiovascular conditions. In this case, a suspicion of MINOCA led to the incidental diagnosis of a previously unknown congenital heart defect in adulthood, highlighting the importance of a thorough diagnostic workup in such cases.
