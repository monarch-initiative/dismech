# Stratified Medicine Pediatrics: Cell-Free DNA and Serial Tumor Sequencing Identifies Subtype-Specific Cancer Evolution and Epigenetic States.
**Authors:** George SL, Lynn C, Stankunaite R, Hughes D, Sauer CM, Chalker J, Waqar Ahmed S, Oostveen M, Proszek PZ, Yuan L, Shaikh R, Jamal S, Brew A, Tall J, Rogers T, Clifford SC, Vormoor J, Shipley JM, Tweddle DA, Jones C, Willis C, Burke GAA, Vedi A, Howell L, Johnston R, Rees H, Adams M, Jesudason A, Ronghe M, Elliott M, Ross E, Makin G, Campbell-Hewson Q, Grundy RG, Turnbull J, Wilson S, Lee V, Gray JC, Stoneham S, Gatz SA, Marshall LV, Angelini P, Anderson J, Cresswell GD, Graham TA, Al-Lazikani B, Cortés-Ciriano I, Kearns P, Hutchinson JC, Hargrave D, Jacques TS, Hubank M, Sottoriva A, Chesler L
**Journal:** Cancer Discov (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1158/2159-8290.CD-24-0916](https://doi.org/10.1158/2159-8290.CD-24-0916)

## Content

In tumors of childhood, we identify mutations in epigenetic genes as drivers of relapse, with matched cfDNA sequencing showing significant intratumor genetic heterogeneity and cell-state specific patterns of chromatin accessibility. This highlights the power of cfDNA analysis to identify both genetic and epigenetic drivers of aggressive disease in pediatric cancers.

Cancer remains the leading cause of childhood death due to disease in Europe and North America (1, 2). Cancers of childhood are often treated with complex, multimodal treatment regimens including combinations of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery. Unfortunately, despite these intensive therapies, some pediatric solid tumors are either refractory to treatment or relapse rapidly following an initial response (3, 4). In recent years, toward developing more effective therapies, large precision medicine programs have been developed with the goal of identifying targetable genetic alterations in tumor tissue from patients with either treatment-refractory or relapsed cancers of childhood (5–9). However, there is limited understanding of the evolution of tumors from diagnosis to relapse and the representativeness of the tumor biopsy with regard to the most aggressive subclones of disease.

Stratified Medicine Pediatrics (SMPaeds) is the UK precision medicine program offering rapid turnaround clinical-grade sequencing with clinical reporting for patients undergoing biopsy at the time of relapse or treatment-refractory disease. A defined aim of SMPaeds was to identify patients likely to respond to molecularly targeted therapeutic agents and to discover genomic and epigenetic determinants of cancer relapse. Alongside the clinical program, an archival tissue sample and a temporally matched research plasma sample were obtained.

Here, we report results of the retrospective sequencing of matched diagnostic relapse tumor tissues and the largest study of paired tumor cell-free DNA (cfDNA) sequencing of patients with relapsed childhood cancer to date. Across this heterogeneous cohort of poor-outcome tumors of childhood, we identify both common and cancer-specific patterns of tumor evolution and clinically significant rare genetic alterations predictive of very poor outcome disease. In certain cancer subtypes, we highlight the utility of cfDNA to identify clinically actionable mutations and tumor heterogeneity at relapse. Finally, given the limited number of driver alterations identified in relapsed pediatric tumors (10) and the increasing recognition that transcription factor–associated epigenetic dysregulation is a prominent driver of therapy resistance (11, 12), we establish the utility and practicality of cfDNA nucleosome positioning analysis to identify cancer-specific DNA fragmentation patterns using low cost, clinical grade, low-coverage whole-genome sequencing (lcWGS).

Patients with clinically relapsed, treatment-refractory or progressive pediatric-type solid tumors (including lymphomas) who underwent a biopsy as part of the standard of care were eligible for enrollment into SMPaeds, which recruited patients from February 2019 to January 2024. The results of the main program, which returned clinical results on tumor tissue and germline sequencing to practitioners in rapid turnaround at the time of relapse, will be reported separately. The study mandated collection of an archival primary tumor sample from the time of diagnosis and a plasma sample in parallel to tissue biopsy at SMPaeds enrollment. Here, we present the results of paired next-generation sequencing (NGS) targeted panel sequencing (Panel-Seq) and lcWGS in primary diagnosis–relapse tumor pairs and matched tumor–cfDNA pairs from the time of relapse (Fig. 1A). Archival tissue and cfDNA samples were sequenced retrospectively, and results were not reported back to the treating clinicians. Matched relapse tissue and cfDNA sequencing were performed in 401 patients and matched relapse and archival tissue sequencing was performed in 277 patients (Fig. 1B). The most frequent diagnoses in the overall study and in the subset analyzed were central nervous system (CNS) tumors, sarcomas, and neuroblastoma (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Table S1), reflecting a diagnostic distribution typical of this study population.

Around 41% of sequence alterations were present in both the primary and relapse samples, whereas 37% were found only at relapse (23% were found only in the primary). The proportions of patients who acquired genetic alterations at relapse are summarized in Supplementary Fig. S1A–S1C, with all sequence variants presented in Supplementary Table S2 and fusions in Supplementary Table S3. In the whole cohort, and as expected, the most frequently altered gene at the time of relapse was TP53 (Fig. 2B), with a high frequency of TP53 mutations seen in patients with sarcoma (Fig. 2C). In neuroblastoma, the largest single disease type in the study, the most frequently mutated genes that were enriched at relapse were ALK and NF1, mirroring findings of previous small, paired sequencing studies (Fig. 2D; refs. 13, 14). In addition to the acquisition of ALK mutations at relapse, ALK was also the most frequently mutated gene at diagnosis, and ALK mutations were only found in neuroblastoma (Fig. 2D), highlighting the prominent role of this gene in both neuroblastoma oncogenesis and disease progression. Using Panel-Seq data, the ratio of synonymous to nonsynonymous mutations (dN/dS) was calculated to identify nonsynonymous and truncating variants that are putative drivers of relapse (Fig. 3F; ref. 15). TP53 mutations and truncating CDKN2A mutations were positively selected at both diagnosis and relapse (Fig. 3G). Across the cohort, there was a strong positive selection at relapse for missense and truncating mutations in CHEK2 (Fig. 3G). Mutations in genes responsible for epigenetic modification of DNA were also highly enriched at relapse including ATRX, SETD2, ARID1B, and CREBBP (Fig. 3G), likely consistent with the role of epigenetic remodeling as a driver of therapy resistance in childhood cancers.

To systematically evaluate the ability of cfDNA sequencing to detect somatic mutations at relapse in pediatric tumors, Panel-Seq was performed on all available time-matched tissue and cfDNA samples (Supplementary Fig. S6; Supplementary Table S5). We used lcWGS when available to estimate the purity of plasma cfDNA–defined as the fraction of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) in total cfDNA. Of 397 patients with cfDNA lcWGS and Panel-Seq data available, 86 had high-purity (>10%) ctDNA. Patients with neuroblastoma had higher ctDNA levels, than those with all other cancer types, and patients with CNS tumors had lower ctDNA levels (Fig. 4A). In the cohort as a whole (n = 402 patients), 43% of alterations expected from tissue sequencing were detected in cfDNA, with 89% expected alterations detected in high-purity samples and 25% expected alterations detected in low-purity samples (Fig. 4B). Of note, although the overlap between tissue and cfDNA sequencing was lower in low-purity samples, cfDNA-specific variants could still be identified.

The most common cfDNA-unique variants were in ARID1A, NF1, PPM1D, TP53, and CREBBP (Fig. 4C). TP53 and PPM1D mutations are commonly reported in clonal hematopoiesis; therefore, the hematopoietic origin of these variants was excluded by inspecting Panel-Seq data from the matching blood cell pellet for each patient. Matched tissue was inspected for all cfDNA-unique variants. Of a total of 98 cfDNA-unique variants, only six were detected in the time-matched tissue at extremely low, subclonal levels [below the variant allele frequency (VAF) limit for reporting for tissue Panel-Seq of 5%]. The average VAF of the cfDNA-unique variants was significantly lower than the VAF of variants overlapping between tissue and cfDNA (Fig. 4D), likely reflecting the subclonal nature of cfDNA-unique variants. The use of unique molecular identifiers (UMI) in the sequencing library preparation, stringent variant filtering, and further manual curation to only include cancer-related likely pathogenic variants minimize the risk of these cfDNA-unique variants being technical artefacts.

The highest number of cfDNA-unique variants was detected in neuroblastoma, the tumor type with the highest purity cfDNA (Fig. 4E). In neuroblastoma, a high frequency of the cfDNA-unique variants were found in epigenetic genes known to be important in neuroblastoma biology (ARID1A, ATRX, and SMARCA4; refs. 16–18) and genes associated with ultra–high risk (UHR) disease (ALK, NF1, PTPN11, FGFR1, TP53, ATM, and CREBBP; ref. 19). cfDNA-unique variants in NF1 were detected in 5/72 patients. Taken together with the enrichment of NF1 mutations that we also identified in tumor sequencing at relapse (Fig. 2D), this identifies NF1 as a major potential target in relapsed and treatment-resistant neuroblastoma. In comparison, low cfDNA purity and low concordance between cfDNA and tissue sequencing was seen in patients with brain tumors (Fig. 4F).

Further cancer type–specific comparison of cfDNA with tissue sequencing was limited by patient numbers. cfDNA-specific variants were detected in some sarcoma subgroups and in some patients with hepatoblastoma but not in any patients with Wilms tumor (Supplementary Figs. S6 and S7A–S7D). Although the numbers of patients with lymphoma were limited, cfDNA sequencing seemed to be of particular utility in these patients. For example, in Hodgkin lymphoma, four of six patients had cfDNA-unique variants detected (Fig. 4G). This warrants further investigation given the challenges in producing reliable sequencing data from Hodgkin tumor samples because of the high levels of stroma and paucity of tumor cells.

Overall, these data show a good correlation between variant detection in high-purity ctDNA samples and tissue and strongly support the added value of cfDNA profiling in detecting tumor heterogeneity at the time of relapse/tumor progression in certain pediatric cancer subtypes.

Genome-wide copy number profiles were analyzed for tissue and cfDNA pairs from 350 patients. A good concordance between analytes was observed in high-purity pairs (n = 80; Supplementary Fig. S8A–S8C). Although the majority of cfDNA samples were of low purity, 56 tissue samples were also of low purity. In eight of these patients for whom no CNVs were detected in the tissue, lcWGS was successful in identifying CNVs in plasma. In one patient with neuroblastoma, the relapse biopsy taken from the adrenal tumor did not show clear CCND1 amplification although it showed some CNVs indicative of the presence of tumor tissue. However, the copy number profile of cfDNA at the time of relapse showed a clear CCND1 amplification more in keeping with the time-matched biopsy of liver metastases (Fig. 5A and B). Given that only the primary tumor results would have been considered in the clinical arm of this study, this demonstrates the added value of matched cfDNA sequencing at the time of relapse to better reflect genomic changes in other sites of disease. Taken together, our data demonstrate the utility of cfDNA to detect neuroblastoma heterogeneity, including the detection of genetic changes in metastatic sites.

Next, we investigated whether cfDNA could better detect the mutations that are known to be the most clinically relevant drivers of relapse than tissue biopsy. Here, we focused on TP53 and RAS pathway mutations, given the data showing that they are associated with UHR neuroblastoma at diagnosis (19) and are also further enriched at relapse (13). Around 36 of 72 patients with neuroblastoma had at least one UHR gene variant detected in either tissue or plasma at relapse (50 variants detected in total). Seven variants were tissue specific and 17 were cfDNA specific. For all seven tissue-specific variants, cfDNA purity was zero, whereas most tissue biopsies that did not detect cfDNA variants were of high purity (Fig. 5C). Taken together, this shows that all UHR variants can be detected by sequencing of tumor-derived cfDNA, whereas sequencing of a single biopsy does not fully capture all UHR mutations present in the tumor at relapse. The majority of mutations in UHR genes detected in cfDNA were in TP53 and NF1. Three patients had FGFR1 hotspot mutations detected at relapse, all of which were also detected in cfDNA. (Fig. 5D).

Two patients with ALK-mutant neuroblastoma were serially monitored by cfDNA analysis while receiving the ALK inhibitor lorlatinib. One patient with a pathogenic ALKF1174L mutation detected in tissue and cfDNA at relapse had an almost complete response to lorlatinib and concordantly the ALK-mutant fraction was undetectable after 1 month of therapy (also as seen in ref. 20). The same pathogenic ALK mutation re-merged at low levels (0.4%–2.7% VAF) and remained detectable for a year before the clinical relapse was detected by MRI (Fig. 5E). The second patient with metastatic neuroblastoma was commenced on lorlatinib based on the presence of a pathogenic ALKF1174L mutation in cfDNA. A partial response to lorlatinib was mirrored by a reduction in mutant ALK levels (Fig. 5F).

Given that the overall mutational frequency in childhood cancers is low, we investigated whether cfDNA analysis could be used to infer additional epigenetic characteristics relating to the tissue of origin and cancer-specific expression signatures. For this purpose, we focused on cfDNA fragmentation patterns derived from the analysis of lcWGS.

DNA fragments derived from open chromatin are more sensitive to degradation than nucleosome-bound cfDNA fragments, resulting in differences in cfDNA sequencing coverage between open and closed chromatin (Fig. 6A; ref. 21). To identify fragmentomic patterns in pediatric tumor–derived cfDNA, we applied the Griffin methodology combined with the Gene Transcription Regulation Database containing consensus binding sites for 425 transcription factors (TFs) with loss of coverage relating to assumed sites of transcription factor binding (21). We used Wilcoxon tests and hierarchical clustering to identify transcription factor binding sites that were differentially accessible in one tumor type compared with those in others. This approach clearly identified cancer-specific transcription factor expression signature clusters (Fig. 6B; Supplementary Fig. S9A–S9D; Supplementary Table S6). Similarity of the transcription factor binding sites (TFBSs) for TFs within clusters was not explanatory (Supplementary Fig. S10). Gene ontology enrichment analysis of the four main clusters identified gene sets relating to the cell of origin including neural crest–derived pathways in neuroblastoma, muscle development in rhabdomyosarcoma, immune pathways in lymphoma, and hepatocyte differentiation in the hepatoblastoma cluster (Fig. 6C).

The largest cluster, associated with non-Hodgkin lymphoma, contained a large number of transcription factors known to play a role in non–Hodgkin lymphoma pathogenesis such as BCL6, IRF4, BATF3, FOXO1, and BCL11A (Fig. 6C–E; refs. 22–25). In patients with Wilms tumor, the binding sites of only one transcription factor were identified as highly specific and highly accessible: SIX2, a known poor prognostic biomarker and stem cell/blastemal subtype–associated transcription factor (Fig. 6F; refs. 26, 27). Two of the three transcription factors known to be hepatocyte specific (28) were identified in the hepatocyte cluster (Fig. 6G; Supplementary Fig. S11A and S11B).

For the embryonal tumors neuroblastoma and rhabdomyosarcoma, core regulatory circuits (CRC) related to developmentally derived cell states have been described (29–31). We therefore evaluated known transcription factors that are activated as part of these defined CRCs. The pan-rhabdomyosarcoma CRC constituents MYOD1 and MYOG (32, 33) showed significant loss of coverage in rhabdomyosarcoma samples (Fig. 6H and I). In neuroblastoma samples, we identified the accessibility of the noradrenergic CRC and its key constituent transcription factors HAND2, ISL1, TBX2, GATA3, and MYCN (Fig. 6J–L; refs. 30, 34, 35). Some of these transcription factor binding sites, such as HAND2, TBX2, and ISL1, were highly specific for neuroblastoma (Fig. 6J; Supplementary Fig. S11C and S11D), whereas others such as MYCN, although accessible in neuroblastoma, were not specific to neuroblastoma samples only (Fig. 6K). This is consistent with the major role of MYCN in neuroblastoma oncogenesis, alongside the fact that dysregulation of MYCN has also been associated with other aggressive pediatric malignancies (36). We did not identify differential accessibility for Ewing sarcoma–specific CRC transcription factors or the EWS:FLI fusion transcription factors (Supplementary Fig. S12A–S12C).

Taken together, we show that fragmentomic analysis of cfDNA identifies distinct tumor-specific clusters related to the cell of origin and epigenetic plasticity, with potential application in identifying transcription signatures associated with treatment resistance and relapse.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest pan-pediatric cancer analysis of tumor–cfDNA and diagnosis–relapse pairs to date. Our diagnosis–relapse tumor comparison identifies putative drivers of relapse across the whole cohort. These are predominantly mutations in genes involved in epigenetic regulation in addition to the DNA damage response gene CHEK2. Pan-cancer analyses have identified that CHEK2 mutations occur only at very low frequencies (<2%) in individual poor outcome pediatric tumors such as medulloblastoma, osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, and neuroblastoma (37). However, we identify a strong selection for both missense and truncating mutations in CHEK2 between diagnosis and relapse across our cohort. CHEK2 mutations have been associated with standard therapy resistance in multiple adult cancers (38–40) but may also associate with sensitivity to novel targeted agents (41). Taken together with our data, this identifies CHEK2 as a likely driver of therapy resistance in pediatric cancers and also identifies CHEK2 as an ideal target for arms of tumor-agnostic pediatric “basket” early phase clinical trials such as ESMART (5) and NCI-MATCH (8).

In neuroblastoma, the largest single tumor entity in our cohort, we identified an enrichment of both ALK and NF1 mutations at the time of relapse. The enrichment in ALK mutations at relapse has previously been described (13, 14). The sequencing of primary–relapse neuroblastoma pairs has also identified the acquisition of NF1 mutations in 2/23 relapses (13). This is concordant with our data, identifying relapse-specific mutations in 4/59 neuroblastomas. In addition to this, we also identify further five cfDNA-unique NF1 mutations at the time of relapse. Taken together, this identifies clonal selection for NF1 mutations as a potential driver of therapy resistance in neuroblastoma.

Our paired diagnosis–relapse tumor comparison was performed using a targeted sequencing panel which has been designed to detect mutations that are known to be of importance in pediatric cancers (42). This approach was chosen to maximize the chances of successful sequencing in archival diagnostic samples obtained as part of routine clinical care. This is a limitation of our study, in which the lack of genome-wide sequencing limits the potential for novel discovery. However, given that our panel is already curated to identify pediatric cancer driver genes, this also increases the probability that the genes identified as being enriched at relapse are likely to be of high biological relevance. Within this context, it is important to note that only a limited number of tumors acquired additional driver mutations at the time of relapse.

Using a high-depth cfDNA panel sequencing approach, we show that we are able to detect tumor mutations in the majority of patients, if enough tumor-derived DNA is present in the circulation. In patients with brain tumors in whom ctDNA levels are limited, there is extremely limited utility of cfDNA analysis from plasma for the detection of tumor-associated variants. Here, cfDNA analysis from cerebrospinal fluid is likely to be more informative (43). Conversely, in patients with neuroblastoma, there are high amounts of ctDNA, and there exists a high concordance between tumor tissue and cfDNA sequencing. In addition, we see a high number of cfDNA-unique variants in patients with neuroblastoma demonstrating the heterogeneity of this disease and limitations of performing a single biopsy at relapse. For patients with ALK-mutant neuroblastoma who receive ALK inhibitors, our limited data on serial monitoring add to the increasing body of evidence to support prospective evaluation of cfDNA sequencing as a biomarker of therapy response (44–46).

Further subgroup comparisons are limited by sample size because of the heterogeneous nature of our study population. However, we identify a number of pediatric tumors in which cfDNA sequencing for somatic variant detection shows promise, adding to the literature supporting systematic evaluation within disease-specific clinical trials (5, 20, 47).

Taken together, our data show that plasma-based cfDNA sequencing can provide additional information to tumor sequencing at the time of relapse in extracranial tumors of childhood. These data are supportive of cfDNA sequencing as a complimentary technique to tissue-based sequencing at the time of relapse to detect tumor-associated mutations. However, given the rarity of pediatric cancers, further large-scale analyses will be required to accurately define the specificity and sensitivity of cfDNA-based approaches in individual tumor types.

Despite the added value of cfDNA sequencing demonstrated in this study, it is important to note that for a significant proportion of patients, no genetic alterations were detected in either tumor tissue or cfDNA at the time of relapse. This is in keeping with other studies (5) and highlights the importance of identifying nongenetic biomarkers of aggressive disease in pediatric tumors. Within this context, the development of clinical-grade technologies to accurately profile both epigenetic states and the tumor immune microenvironment is a defined goal of SMPaeds2. We also note the homogeneous nature of primary and relapse samples and no enrichment of new genomic alterations at relapse due to treatment as striking. One would have predicted genetic alterations in recurrent driver genes to be the driving force of Darwinian evolution during cancer progression. However, this is not the case in this cohort, and indeed drivers of chemotherapy resistance have not been robustly documented in adult solid tumors so far. Relapse may be driven by a large variety of genetic mechanisms, potentially even multiple ones in the same tumor, with each one being relatively rare and hence difficult to detect without an enormous cohort. A much more parsimonious explanation instead is that epigenetic alterations may be responsible for relapse. We argue that our study strongly suggests that the pursuit of genetic alterations explaining relapse will likely be unfruitful and that we should concentrate on prospective collection of samples that will allow the epigenetic profiling of matched primary and relapse cancers.

Importantly, here, we show that the utility of cfDNA sequencing extends beyond the detection of genetic alterations and that epigenetic states relating to the developmental origins of pediatric cancers can also be identified by lcWGS of cfDNA. Epigenetic dysregulation is increasingly being recognized as the predominant driver of aggressive childhood cancers (48), and the transcriptional circuits that drive aggressive disease also show specific dependencies that are of high interest for the development of novel therapeutics (35, 49). Therefore, new tools are urgently needed that can effectively characterize the epigenetic landscape of childhood tumors. Nucleosome profiling of cfDNA not only has the potential to identify disease subtype in pediatric cancers, but also to identify epigenetic changes associated with aggressive biology. Additionally, this information is obtained from lcWGS of cfDNA which is minimally invasive, relatively low cost, and has the potential to be applied serially and at scale. This offers multiple potential applications including supporting diagnosis, providing prognostic information, identifying epigenetic targets for novel therapeutics, and the potential for serial sampling to understand how specific therapies alter epigenetic programming. Furthermore, this approach has potential to be integrated with other data types such as methylation analysis to aid more precise tumor subgroup classification. Further refinement of our methodology and independent validation in large cohorts will be needed to realize the potential of this tool for pediatric cancers.

In conclusion, we show specific patterns of evolution between diagnosis and relapse in pediatric tumors, including the identification of both tumor-specific and pan-cohort drivers of relapse. In addition, we show that cfDNA analysis has potential to identify genetic drivers in aggressive tumors of childhood. Finally, we show that in patients with relapsed and refractory cancers, standard clinical-grade lcWGS of cfDNA can be used to infer the epigenetic state of cancer cells, relating to the cell of origin.

The SMPaeds study (IRAS: 246557/264925, doi: 10.1186/ISRCTN21731605) received ethical approval from the UK National Research Ethics Service. It opened in 2019 and recruited patients from 20 pediatric oncology centers across England, Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland. Children, teenagers, and young adults with pediatric-type tumors were eligible to enroll at the time of relapse, refractory, or progressive disease. Tumor biopsy was performed as part of the standard of care and was submitted for sequencing. Alongside this, a blood sample was collected at the time of relapse. The blood cell pellet was used for germline sequencing, and plasma was stored for retrospective cfDNA analysis. Archival tumor tissue from the time of diagnosis was requested from the pathologist at the referring center. Serial cfDNA sample analysis was performed as part of our local ethically approved protocol (IRAS: 281408).

Either frozen or formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor samples were accepted in this study. Tumor biopsy specimens were assessed by the study pathologist and analyzed for tumor content. Tumor tissue DNA/RNA and buffy coat germline DNA extractions were performed at Great Ormond Street Hospital using Promega Maxwell machines. The formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue was extracted using the Maxwell RSC FFPE Plus DNA Kit (AS1720) and the Maxwell 16 LEV RNA FFPE Kit. Whole blood was collected into cfDNA blood collection tubes (Streck). Frozen tissue and buffy coat samples were extracted using the Maxwell RSC Blood DNA Kit (AS1400) and the Maxwell 16 LEV simplyRNA Cells Kit.

The Royal Marsden Hospital lab separated plasma from blood within 7 days of venepuncture. Separation was done by double centrifugation. Blood was spun at 1600 g for 10 minutes and the plasma fraction was collected and spun again at 1600 g for 10 minutes, aliquoted into cryovials, and stored at −80°C. cfDNA was extracted from plasma using QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (55114, Qiagen) and the QIAvac 24 Plus Vacuum Manifold system. Cases for which the kit was not available, QIAsymphony Circulating DNA Kit (1091063, Qiagen) was used with the QIAsymphony SP automated extraction system instead. Samples with cfDNA above 10 ng/μL were analyzed using Agilent TapeStation (RRID: SCR_018435) with the genomic DNA (5067–5365) or cfDNA ScreenTape assay (5067–5630) to evaluate the presence and fraction of high–molecular weight DNA.

Targeted sequencing panels were constructed to capture driver mutations in pediatric solid tumors (Supplementary Tables S7–S9). The pediatric-specific panel of 92 genes (42) or a solid tumor panel of 233 genes (Royal Marsden Hospital, United Kingdom) was used to construct libraries for tissue samples depending on availability. A marginally smaller pediatric panel of 67 genes (20) was run for cfDNA.

DNA from tissue and buffy coat was processed by previously established methods (42) using the KAPA HyperPlus Kit and SeqCap EZ adapters (Roche, NimbleGen) with dual-SPRI size selection of the libraries (250–450 bp). Around 200 ng of DNA (samples yielding DNA with a median fragment length >1,000 bp) or 400 ng of DNA if there was sufficient DNA (for samples with a DNA fragment length <1,000 bp) was used for library preparation. Around 1 μg of the pooled library DNA was hybridized to custom panels as described and sequenced to 100× coverage (germline) and 400× coverage (tumor) on the NextSeq system (RRID: SCR_016381; 100 bp paired end reads, mid-output kit or high-output kit depending on the sample number). cfDNA sequencing libraries were generated using Cell3 Target (Nonacus) library preparation kit with Dual Index UMI adapters as per the manufacturer’s protocol (input cfDNA, 5–250 ng). cfDNA was sequenced using NovaSeq 6000 (RRID: SCR_016387; 200 cycle kit, 2 × 100 bp) at ∼1500 × UMI coverage. For tissue from relapse tumors only, RNA fusion panel sequencing libraries were generated using the TruSight RNA Pan-Cancer Panel (Illumina) and sequenced on the Nextseq system (2 × 75 bp).

For lcWGS, the pre-capture amplified libraries prepared for Panel-Seq were pooled and sequenced separately on the the NextSeq system (2 × 75 bp) on a different flow cell at a coverage between 0.5 and 4×. cfDNA lcWGS was ran on the NovaSeq system (200 cycle kit, 100 bp paired end reads). There were fewer samples with material available for lcWGS than for Panel-Seq as Panel-Seq was prioritized. All samples used are detailed in Supplementary Tables S10 and S11.

The initial processing of all NGS data was performed using the Molecular Diagnostics Information Management System v4.0 (50) based on genome build GRCh37 at Clinical Genomics, The Royal Marsden Hospital. To summarize, raw data were converted to FASTQ files using bcl2fastq2 (RRID: SCR_015058). FASTQs were then aligned to the GRCh37 reference genome using BWA-MEM (RRID: SCR_022192), and quality metrics were generated using Picard Tools (RRID: SCR_006525). Somatic nucleotide variants for Panel-Seq of tumor tissue collected at the time of relapse were called using GATK Mutect2 (RRID: SCR_001876; ref. 51) and were annotated using PCGR (52). Germline Panel-Seq results were reported back to the molecular tumor board as part of the main clinical sequencing program; however, for the purposes of this retrospective study, only matched germline samples were used for the purpose of calling somatic variants.

The custom pipeline for the analysis of cfDNA Panel-Seq with UMIs was previously described in (43). Following demultiplexing including UMI extraction, fastqs were converted to an unmapped BAM file with annotated UMI sequences with fgbio (https://github.com/fulcrumgenomics/fgbio). Reads were then aligned to the GRCh37 genome with BWA-MEM and grouped by template and UMI to create consensus reads which arise from the same molecule. Reads were filtered using fgbio FilterConsensusReads with the following options: min-reads 3, max-read-error-rate 0.05, max-base-error-rate 0.1, min-base-quality 30, and max-no-call-fraction 0.1. Filtered reads were once again aligned to the genome with BWA-MEM, followed by clipping of overlapping reads with fgbio ClipBam. Variant calling was performed with VarDict (RRID: SCR_023658) with the minimum frequency set to 0.0001, and variants were filtered using an in-house blacklist and whitelist. The results of relapse tissue and cfDNA Panel-Seq were manually inspected using Integrated Genomics Viewer (RRID: SCR_011793) and reported by the Clinical Genomics team at the Royal Marsden Hospital.

To ensure the greatest sensitivity for longitudinal comparisons, somatic variants occurring at the time of diagnosis were called jointly with relapse tissue and cfDNA using GATK Mutect2 v4.2.3.0 in multisample mode. Following calling, variants were annotated using vep101.0 (RRID: SCR_007931), and GATK FilterMutectCalls v4.2.3.0 was used to mark variants for downstream filtering. Variants were imported into R with VcfR v1.13.0 (RRID: SCR_023453; ref. 53) and filtered with R scripts as described. Blacklisted, synonymous, and noncoding variants were removed, except for upstream TERT mutations and whitelisted variants. Variants required “MODERATE” or “HIGH” severity, VAF ≥5%, allele depth (AD) ≥5, depth passing quality filter (DP) ≥10, and frequency <0.0002 in Gnomad. Ultimately, variants that had not previously been reported in relapse were manually inspected and likely artefacts were removed. FilterMutectCalls flags were used as follows: “normal_artifact” removed if germline allele fraction was greater than 15% of the allele fraction in the tumor; “slippage” removed if AD <20 and VAF <0.3%; and for “strand_bias” up to AD = 10, variants with at least one read on the opposing strand were kept and for AD >10, variants were removed if the count difference between strands exceeded 150%. Variants are displayed using the OncoPrint function from ComplexHeatmap v2.6.2 (RRID: SCR_017270; ref. 54) and ggplot2 v2.3.4.4 (RRID: SCR_014601; ref. 55). Clinically defined hypermutator SMP0342 was excluded from comparative analysis. For all comparative analyses, genes that were not covered by the panel for all samples were excluded.

RNA fusion panel sequencing was analyzed using the BaseSpace Sequencing hub from Illumina (RRID: SCR_011881) using the RNA-Seq Alignment App v2.0.1 with STAR to hg19 and Manta for fusion calling with default parameters. Results were inspected and reported by the Clinical Genomics team at the Royal Marsden Hospital.

BAM files used for copy number analysis (CNA) were processed as described in the previous section, and ichorCNA v0.3.2 (RRID: SCR_024768) was then used to generate normalized relative coverage [log2 ratio = log2(tumor/normal)] in 500 kb bins. Data normalization was performed in accordance with the ichorCNA workflow using an in-house panel of normals, including sex chromosomes. Bins were required to have a minimum mappability of 85% and 95% non-N bases. The log2 ratio was further normalized by subtracting the median log2 ratio of all bins. A selection of bins were removed as they were seen to be recurrently aberrant.

To call the absolute copy number, we used a modified version of the ASCATlp script (https://github.com/cresswell-lab/ASCATlp) to fit a range of purities from 0.1 to 1 and ploidies from 1.5 to 4.5. Seeking to leverage multiple sampling for optimized segmentation, we used the multipcf function from copynumber v1.30.0 (56). For patients with only a single sample, the singular pcf function was used. For both functions, a penalty of 20 was used. ASCATlp is based on the approach of the ASCAT tool (RRID: SCR_016868; ref. 57). In short, it calculates the continuous copy number state and the sum of squared differences to the nearest integer for each combination of parameters. A 3 × 3 search is applied to identify local minima, and the solution with the lowest sum of squares is selected. For each sample, the pipeline was run multiple times constraining the search space to find the most feasible solution. For samples where no fit was produced for the patient ploidy, the purity was set to zero.

The estimated purity values of both tumor and ctDNA content were taken from the lcWGS analysis. Copy number events were defined as follows: deletion = 0 copies; loss = 1; amplification ≥ ploidy +2; and gain = ploidy +1. Runs of matching copy number states were extracted from the segments and split by chromosome arm. Chromosomal copy number change was called for runs longer than 75% of the chromosome. Arms were called if the run was under 75% of the chromosome and greater than 75% of the arm. Focal CNVs (genes or chromosome bands) were called when they were shorter than half the length of the chromosome arm. The FGA was calculated as the fraction of bins with an estimated copy number differing from the median ploidy. Comparative CNV analyses (Fig. 3E; Supplementary Fig. S4) were performed by subtracting the copy number state of segments at diagnosis from the copy number state at relapse. Amplifications were capped at ploidy = 2+ to avoid over-reporting relapse-acquired amplifications (Fig. 3E; Supplementary Fig. S4A–S4D). MEDICC2 v1.0.2 (58) was used to generate copy number phylogenies.

dN/dS analysis was performed using dNdScv v.0.0.1.0 (RRID: SCR_023123; ref. 59) for 278 primary/relapse pairs. dN/dS was considered significantly greater than one (neutral) when the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval was greater than one. SMP0342 and SMP0448 were excluded from dN/dS analysis as they had more than three times as many synonymous mutations as any other sample (272 and 284 synonymous mutations, respectively).

Transcription factor binding sites were obtained following published methods from the Griffin framework (21). The Gene Transcription Regulation Database v21.12 (60) was filtered for bona fide transcription factors in the CIS-BP database v2.00 (61). The database was then filtered for TFs with at least 10,000 binding sites, leaving 425 TFs. For each TF, the top 10,000 sites with the greatest confidence were picked. BAM files were converted to GRCh38 using UCSC liftOver (RRID: SCR_018160). The Griffin v0.2.0 (21) package was used to retrieve GC content–corrected composite coverage for all 425 TFs in 429 cfDNA lcWGS samples. Samples were filtered for ctDNA content >0.2 for further analysis (n = 85). For disease groups with more than three members, two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann–Whitney) tests were performed to compare the composite coverage at the center of TFBSs within a disease with all other samples. TFs with a P value <0.05 (n = 207) in any comparison were selected for hierarchical clustering. Clustering was performed using the Heatmap function in ComplexHeatmap v2.6.2 (54). To inspect potential bias in clustering due the similarity of binding sites between TFs, the Jaccard coefficient was computed across TFBSs to create a similarity matrix. Gene interaction network and gene ontology enrichment analyses were performed with StringDB (62) for clusters of TFs showing disease specificity.
