# Fumarate Hydratase Deficiency.
**Authors:** Adam MP, Bick S, Mirzaa GM, Wallace SE, Amemiya A, Coman D, Kranc KR, Christodoulou J

## Content

CLINICAL CHARACTERISTICS: Fumarate hydratase (FH) deficiency most often results in severe neonatal and early infantile encephalopathy that is characterized by poor feeding, poor weight gain, growth deficiency, hypotonia, lethargy, and seizures. Dysmorphic facial features include frontal bossing, depressed nasal bridge, and widely spaced eyes. Many affected individuals are microcephalic. Brain imaging findings include cerebral atrophy, enlarged ventricles and generous extra-axial cerebrospinal fluid spaces, bilateral polymicrogyria, delayed myelination for age, abnormal corpus callosum (thin or absent), and an abnormally small brain stem. Development is severely delayed and most affected individuals are nonverbal and nonambulatory. Prognosis is poor for severely affected infants and many die during early childhood. Less severely affected individuals with moderate cognitive impairment and long-term survival have been reported.

DIAGNOSIS/TESTING: Isolated increased fumaric acid and alpha-ketoglutarate on urine organic acid analysis combined with increased succinyladenosine on urine purines and pyrimidines is highly suggestive of FH deficiency. The diagnosis of FH deficiency is established in a proband with biallelic pathogenic variants in FH identified by molecular genetic testing or reduced fumarate hydratase enzyme activity in fibroblasts or leukocytes.

MANAGEMENT: Treatment of manifestations: Evaluation and management by a pediatric neurologist to treat seizures; gastrostomy tube to optimize nutrition and prevent aspiration in hypotonic or lethargic children; feeding therapy as needed; special needs services to address developmental deficits; physical therapy to minimize contractures; wheelchair and/or other mobility devices; management of scoliosis by orthopedist; transitional care plan in long-term survivors; family and social work support. Surveillance: At least annual evaluations by pediatric neurologist and physical medicine and rehabilitation specialist; measure growth parameters, assess nutritional status and safety of oral intake, assess developmental progress, and assess educational, family, and social work needs at each visit; periodic evaluation by orthopedist to monitor contractures and scoliosis; ophthalmology assessment with frequency per ophthalmologist. Evaluation of relatives at risk: If the FH pathogenic variants in the family are known, it is appropriate to offer molecular genetic testing to relatives who may be at risk for hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell cancer.

GENETIC COUNSELING: FH deficiency is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. If both parents are known to be heterozygous for an FH pathogenic variant, each sib of an affected individual has at conception a 25% chance of being affected, a 50% chance of being heterozygous, and a 25% chance of inheriting neither of the familial pathogenic variants. Most heterozygous sibs are healthy. However, several reports raise the possibility of increased risk for hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell cancer in the heterozygous relatives of children with FH deficiency. Once the FH pathogenic variants have been identified in an affected family member, heterozygote detection for at-risk relatives and prenatal/preimplantation genetic testing are possible.
