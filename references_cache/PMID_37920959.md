BACKGROUND: Persistent cough is one of the most frequent complications following 
lung cancer surgery. To promote optimal recovery, we conducted a study to 
investigate the trajectories of coughing symptoms and their impact on quality of 
life (QOL), as well as to identify potential risk factors of persistent cough 
after pulmonary resection (CAP).
METHODS: This prospective observational study assessed patients who underwent 
pulmonary resection for lung tumor at two medical centers in China. Persistent 
CAP was evaluated before surgery, at discharge, and 1, 3, and 6 months following 
surgery using visual analog scale (VAS), cough symptom score (CSS), and 
Leicester Cough Questionnaire in Mandarin Chinese (LCQ-MC). Univariate and 
multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to explore independent 
risk factors for persistent CAP.
RESULTS: Of the 506 enrolled patients, 130 patients were diagnosed with 
persistent CAP with an incidence of 25.69%. Compared to the noncough group, 
patients with persistent CAP reported significantly higher VAS (p < 0.001) and 
CSS scores (p < 0.001) and experienced worse QOL (p < 0.001) for up to 6 months, 
particularly at 1 month following surgery. Multivariable regression analysis 
revealed that a duration of anesthesia exceeding 156 min (odds ratio [OR]: 
1.847, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.156-2.951, p = 0.010) and 
gastroesophageal acid reflux (GER) (OR: 3.870, 95% CI: 2.376-6.304, p < 0.001) 
were independent risk factors of persistent CAP.
CONCLUSION: Patients who suffer from persistent CAP face a substantial burden 
and diminished QOL for an extended period compared to noncough patients. 
Moreover, prolonged duration of anesthesia and postoperative GER are potential 
risk factors of persistent CAP.

Pulmonary resection remains the primary treatment for resectable lung cancer.
1
, 
2
, 
3
 Historically, surgeons have prioritized the safety, efficacy, and minimally invasive nature of surgical procedures, while occasionally neglecting the potential complications and postoperative recovery of patients. Persistent cough after pulmonary resection (CAP), with an incidence of 24.4%–55.0%, is one of the most frequent complications following pulmonary surgery.
4
, 
5
, 
6
, 
7
 Unfortunately, this clinical issue has not received sufficient attention. Patients find coping with a postoperative cough to be challenging especially after discharge, due to the lack of guidance from healthcare providers. Persistent CAP negatively affects patients' recovery and quality of life (QOL), yet there is currently insufficient evidence on the underlying mechanisms and effective intervention for this condition.
8
, 
9
 Therefore, identifying the patients at risk and determining the optimal timing for treatment are of pivotal importance for achieving desirable recovery outcomes.

The aim of this study was to profile the trajectories of persistent CAP symptoms and its impact on QOL in patients undergoing pulmonary surgery. Additionally, we analyzed the risk factors for persistent CAP to provide tentative evidence that may help guide proper treatment targeting those factors.

This prospective, observational study was conducted in two tertiary hospitals in China. The research protocol received authorization from the Institutional Review Board at each participating center and has been registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05586035). Prior to enrollment, all participants provided their written informed consent. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revised edition).

Patients who underwent pulmonary resection for lung tumor from October 2021 to December 2022 at the Guangdong Provincial People's Hospital and the Shenzhen People's Hospital were enrolled in the prospective study.

Eligible participants were at least 18 years old, underwent surgery via video‐assisted thoracoscopic pulmonary resection, had a clinical diagnosis of benign lung tumor or lung cancer with stage I–IIIA (8th edition),
10
 and were willing and able to fill out the repeated questionnaire. The exclusion criteria were previous pulmonary surgery, preoperative chronic cough history, multiple lobes operation, conversions to open surgery, inability to comprehend the contents of the research (Figure 1).

Flow chart outlining the selection of the study cohort. CAP, cough after pulmonary resection.

All procedures were carried out with the patient under general anesthesia through double‐lumen endobronchial tube (DLT) intubation. The decision to perform lobectomy or sublobar resection was made at the discretion of the surgeon based on preoperative imaging and intraoperative rapid pathological results. Systematic lymph node dissection or selective lymph node sampling was executed in every case. At the conclusion of the operation, all patients underwent extubation and subsequent transferred back to the ward following a short recovery room stay. The duration of anesthesia was defined as the time elapsed from the start of endotracheal intubation to extubation.

All participants received standardized care postoperatively, including stool softeners administration when needed, pain management with intravenous or oral analgesics, the prompt extraction of the urinary catheter within the initial 48 h subsequent to the surgical intervention, initiating early ambulation commencing on postoperative day one or two, and cessation of chest tube placement when absence of air leakage was evident, lung expansion was adequate on chest radiography, and drainage volume was less than 200 mL per day.

The diagnostic criteria were: (1) nonproductive cough occurring after pulmonary resection that is usually exacerbated when speaking or taking a deep breath but is relieved at rest; (2) cough symptoms lasting for ≥2 weeks; (3) cough not caused by pulmonary infection, postnasal drip syndrome, asthma, angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitor administration or other medical diseases.

The assessment of cough via VAS, cough symptom score (CSS), and Leicester Cough Questionnaire in Mandarin Chinese (LCQ‐MC) was scheduled at enrollment (presurgery baseline), at discharge, and 1, 3, and 6 months following surgery.

The LCQ‐MC is an assessment tool utilized to measure the effect of cough on the QOL. It comprises 19 items that are categorized into three domains: physical (8 items), psychological (7 items), and social (4 items) The individual domains were scored using a 7‐point Likert scale. The overall score, which ranged from 3 to 21, portrayed the respondent's health status, with a higher score implying superior health. The CSS comprises daytime and nighttime symptom scores, with each choice scored from 0 to 3 points based on symptom severity. The scoring system reveals both the frequency and intensity of coughing.

In the present study, the Reflux Diagnostic Questionnaire (RDQ) was administered to evaluate the frequency and intensity of symptoms related to gastroesophageal acid reflux (GER) in all patients after surgery. Reflux symptoms, as assessed by the RDQ, encompassed acid reflux, heartburn, retrosternal pain, and regurgitation. The total score of RDQ was derived from the aggregation of the two subscale scores, and a score exceeding 12 points was considered indicative of a positive diagnostic threshold. The RDQ has been proved to have moderate and comparable accuracy for diagnosis of GER compared with family practitioners and gastroenterologists.
11



Continuous variables are reported as either mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range depending on the distribution. Categorical variables are displayed as frequencies and percentages. The comparison of continuous variables was performed through either student's t test or Mann–Whitney U test, contingent upon the normality of the data. Categorical variables were compared using either the chi‐square or Fisher's exact test. Linear mixed‐effects models were employed to analyze the VAS, CSS, and component score of the LCQ‐MC between the persistent‐CAP and noncough groups over time. The patient group, time, and their interaction were designated as fixed effects, while subject and time were specified as random effects. Maximum likelihood estimation was employed to model the data. To explore independent risk factors of persistent‐CAP, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed. Variables that showed a significant univariate relationship with the outcome with a p < 0.10, or were deemed clinically relevant, were included in the multivariate model using the backward stepwise method. Odds ratios (ORs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was utilized to establish the optimal cutoff value for each variable.

Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. IBM SPSS software version 26.0 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, Chicago, IL, USA) were utilized for data analysis.

The present study included 506 patients that underwent pulmonary resection for benign or malignant lung tumor and completed the cough assessment. Table 1 provides a summary of the demographic and clinical features of the study population. The average age of the subjects was 50.02 ± 13.06 years, and 67.79% were female, and 82.81% had no smoking history. The majority of patients exhibited early‐stage malignancy, with TNM stage 0/I being predominant (84.98%). Sublobar resection and lobectomy was performed on 396 and 110 patients, respectively. All enrolled patients were stratified into two groups, comprising the persistent CAP group (n = 130) and the noncough group (n = 376), with an incidence of 25.69%. The mean duration of persistent CAP was 65.88 days.

The clinical courses of persistent CAP are shown in Figure 2. Prior to surgery, there were no statistically significant differences observed in VAS scores (10.77 ± 15.38 vs. 8.22 ± 13.89, p = 0.096) or CSS (0.45 ± 0.53 vs. 0.37 ± 0.53, p = 0.120) between the persistent CAP and noncough groups. Upon discharge, the patients with persistent CAP exhibited significantly higher VAS scores (37.62 ± 25.78 vs. 19.34 ± 15.45, p < 0.001) and CSS (1.35 ± 0.80 vs. 0.80 ± 0.51, p < 0.001) compared to the noncough group. The mean scores of the cough group reached a peak (VAS 58.77 ± 16.90; CSS 1.94 ± 0.51) one month following surgical treatment and declined subsequently. Six months after surgery, the average VAS and CSS score was 15.73 ± 16.10 and 0.75 ± 0.53 points, which nearly returned to the preoperative level.

The trajectories of cough‐related QOL differed significantly between the persistent CAP and noncough groups (F = 68.47, p < 0.001), as illustrated in Figure 3. Prior to the surgical procedure, there was no discernible statistical distinction between the persistent CAP group and the noncough group in terms of their physical, psychological, social, and overall scores as related to LCQ‐MC. The average physical, psychological, social and total scores (3.90 ± 0.99, 3.85 ± 1.15, 4.10 ± 1.22, and 11.85 ± 3.11) fell to the lowest point one month after surgery in the persistent CAP group. The LCQ‐MC scores of the persistent CAP group showed an upward trend subsequently. Six months after surgery, the LCQ‐MC scores was 17.89 ± 2.76 points, which nearly returned to the preoperative level.

Univariate analysis was conducted to identify potential risk factors associated with persistent CAP (Table 1). Although the prevalence of cough was higher in patients whose tumors located in the right upper lobe (28.8%, p = 0.328), the discrepancy was not statistically significant. However, patients who underwent lobectomy (34.5% vs. 23.2%, p = 0.016) or systematic lymph node dissection (48.6% vs. 24.0%, p = 0.004) had significantly higher incidence of persistent CAP. Compared to the noncough group, the cough group exhibited notably increased lymph node dissection stations (4 [0–8] vs. 3 [0–7], p = 0.009) and counts (4 [0–26] vs. 3 [0–25], p = 0.009), extended durations of anesthesia (175.2 ± 55.7 min vs. 158.1 ± 46.9 min, p = 0.002), and a heightened proportion of GER (36.9% vs. 12.2%, p < 0.001). In the subgroup analysis regarding lobectomy, a comparison between the cough and noncough groups revealed significant disparities only in anesthesia duration and the incidence of gastroesophageal reflux. No significant differences were observed in lymphadenectomy regarding excision type and extent (Table S1).

Variables that were deemed clinically relevant or that revealed a univariate relationship with outcome at a significance level of p < 0.05 were selected as candidates for inclusion in the multivariate model (Figure 4). The cutoff point for anesthesia duration was ascertained to be 156 min based on examination of the ROC curve. In multivariable analysis, the duration of anesthesia (OR: 1.847, 95% CI: 1.156–2.951, p = 0.010) and GER after surgery (OR: 3.870, 95% CI: 2.376–6.304, p < 0.001) were found to be independent predictors of persistent CAP.

Persistent CAP is one of the most frequent complications of lung cancer surgery that adversely affects patients' QOL and hinders their postoperative recovery. Despite its clinical relevance, the underlying mechanisms remain elusive. Therefore, elucidating the trajectory of persistent CAP and identifying its risk factors are crucial to gain a better understanding of this issue and to facilitate targeted interventions for the most susceptible patients at the optimal time. This study delineated the trajectory patterns of persistent CAP symptom and related QOL. Additionally, the duration of anesthesia and GER were indicated as independent predictors of persistent CAP.

In this study, we longitudinally depicted the features of persistent CAP in patients underwent pulmonary resection by analyzing the trajectory patterns of cough symptom severity and related QOL. Patients that present with persistent CAP tend to suffer from cough symptom and impairment of QOL most severely between discharge and one month after surgery. Cough symptom showed a downward trend subsequently while remain a hindrance to the patient's QOL. Patients may not recover their cough to preoperative levels until six months after surgery. Prior studies have revealed that persistent cough imposes a substantial burden on patients in terms of occupational and daily activities, not only directly affecting the physical domain, but also indirectly affecting the psychological and social life, resulting in impaired work productivity and decreased QOL.
12
, 
13
, 
14
 In the absence of standardized management for postoperative cough, patients may experience disruptive symptom over a prolonged period. The present study underscored the significant burden and diminished QOL experienced by patients with persistent CAP compared to noncough patients, thus highlighting the unmet need for more effective treatments to alleviate this burden. Patients who exhibit an over‐threshold cough score and display potential risk factors would benefit from more frequent monitoring and individualized treatment plan to promote optimal recovery.

The present study differed from previous studies of postoperative cough by several strengths. This study implemented cough assessment with multiple time‐points and a longer follow‐up period for up to six months post‐surgery. Postoperative cough and its negative impact on QOL tend to persist for an extended period; therefore, some dynamic changes in persistent CAP data could not be captured in studies that measured outcomes for a shorter period of time. Additionally, this study was prospectively conducted at two medical centers, which added an additional layer of rigor and improved the generalizability of the findings. The findings of the present study provided a more in‐depth understanding of the trajectories of cough severity and its impact on patients' QOL over time. It helps identify the appropriate timing for treatment, especially after discharge when patients find dealing with persistent CAP to be challenging due to the lack of guidance from healthcare providers.

Anesthesia and surgical factors have been proven to be related to persistent CAP in earlier studies.
4
, 
5
, 
6
, 
7
, 
15
 The multivariate analysis conducted in our study revealed that the anesthesia duration (exceeding 156 min) was identified as an independent risk factor related to persistent CAP. This result corroborates the findings of previous studies, which demonstrated that prolonged duration of anesthesia was related to higher incidence of postoperative cough.
6
, 
7
 DLT intubation general anesthesia is a traditional and widely utilized anesthetic procedure among pulmonary surgery. The high incidence of persistent cough in patients with prolonged anesthesia duration can be attributed to several factors. First, DLT intubation can cause stress, congestion, and inflammation of the airway mucosa,
16
 which is an important pathological mechanism that mediates chronic cough.
17
 Second, tracheal intubation can result in airway epithelial cell impairment.
18
 The exposure of cough receptors to external stimuli increases their susceptibility and results in hyperresponsiveness.
19
 Third, one‐lung ventilation and lung recruitment during DLT intubation can induce oxidative stress and exacerbate ischemia–reperfusion injury.
20
 Prolonged intubation can result in increased compression of the tracheal mucosa by the catheter and airbag, subsequently leading to a higher risk of persistent cough.

Pulmonary resection may give rise to an array of unfavorable consequences, including diminished lung volume and diaphragm elevation,
21
 which can engender a decline in intrathoracic pressure and consequent restriction of diaphragm function. Such conditions cause postoperative GER in patients who have undergone pulmonary resection. Postoperative GER was found to be a potential risk factor in the present study, in accordance with the findings of earlier studies.
5
, 
6
, 
22
 Additionally, Sawabata and colleagues utilized a 24‐h esophageal pH monitoring to examine acid regurgitation in patients with persistent CAP, and observed a connection between GER and postoperative cough.
23
 Furthermore, therapeutic interventions with proton pump inhibitors and prokinetic agents were found to alleviate cough symptoms in patients with both GER and postoperative cough.
24
 It is conceivable that the resolution of GER may contribute to the alleviation of persistent CAP. Initial univariate analysis indicated a significant difference in incidence of CAP between patients who underwent lobectomy and sublobar resection, whereas multivariate analysis did not identify lobectomy as an independent risk factor. This suggests that the effect of lobectomy on CAP incidence appears to be mediated by GER, since lobectomy renders more diminished lung volume and diaphragm elevation that contribute to development of GER.

One of the contributing factors for persistent CAP may be excision of pulmonary C‐fibers from the tracheobronchial tract, which is responsible for cough stimulation,
25
, 
26
, 
27
, 
28
 followed by regular afferent signals regarding coughing. Resection of mediastinal lymph nodes in patients with lung cancer enables accurate pathological staging and facilitates the administration of appropriate postoperative treatment. However, during mediastinal lymph node resection, it presents a challenge to circumvent injuring the vast network of vagal nerve branches, which can culminate in the unfortunate loss of segments of the tracheobronchial tract, including the pulmonary C‐fibers. Thus, lymph node resection may increase the risk of postoperative cough,
5
, 
6
, 
7
 but surprisingly, it was not found to be a significant independent factor in the multivariate analysis. This observed inconsistency may be ascribed to the fact that 455 (89.9%) of the patients underwent subcarinal lymph node resection in the present study.

Several limitations in this study must be acknowledged. First, cough assessment data were collected before surgery, at discharge, and 1, 3, 6 months following operation; therefore, some dynamic changes in cough severity and its impact might have been omitted. Nevertheless, there is currently a dearth of evidence regarding the optimal frequency for assessing these outcomes. Second, GER was not definitively monitored by objective measurement, which may have resulted in an incomplete assessment of postoperative acid reflux. Third, this study provided insights into the symptom trajectory and risk factors of persistent CAP. However, we did not focus on the therapeutic intervention for persistent CAP. Further investigation into effective treatment for this condition is warranted to improve patients' recovery and QOL.

In conclusion, this study provides valuable insights into the longitudinal trajectory of persistent CAP and its impact on patients' QOL. Patients with persistent CAP experienced significant burden and worse QOL for an extended period compared with noncough patients. Additionally, we identified the duration of anesthesia (exceeding 156 min) and postoperative GER as the potential risk factors for persistent CAP. Further studies are required to investigate the underlying mechanisms and effective interventions for persistent CAP.
