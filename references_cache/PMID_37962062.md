# Severe Zellweger spectrum disorder due to a novel missense variant in the PEX13 gene: A case report and the literature review.
**Authors:** Su L, Peng MZ, Chen XD, Wu S, Liu L
**Journal:** Mol Genet Genomic Med (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mgg3.2315](https://doi.org/10.1002/mgg3.2315)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Peroxisome biogenesis disorders (PBDs) are caused by variants in PEX genes that impair peroxisome function. Zellweger spectrum disorders (ZSDs) are the most severe and common subtype of PBDs, affecting multiple organ systems due to peroxisomal involvement in various metabolic functions. PEX13 gene variants are rare causes of ZSDs, with only 21 cases reported worldwide and none in China.

METHODS: We describe an infant with biochemically and molecularly confirmed ZSDs due to variants in the PEX13 gene, identified by whole exome sequencing and validated by Sanger sequencing. The patient's treatment and prognosis were followed up. We also reviewed the literature on previously reported cases with PEX13 variants.

RESULTS: The patient had severe hypotonia, seizures, hepatic dysfunction, failure to thrive, and dysmorphic features. Serum analysis revealed elevated levels of very long-chain fatty acids (VLCFA), phytanic acid, and pipecolic acid. We detected a novel homozygous missense variant c.493G>C (p. Ala165Pro) in the PEX13 gene (NM_002618.3), which caused severe clinical manifestations and was inherited from the consanguineous parents. The patient died at the age of 14 months.

CONCLUSION: We report the first case of ZSDs due to the PEX13 variant in China. Our findings broaden the mutational spectrum of the PEX13 gene and indicate that missense variants can lead to severe ZSDs phenotypes, which has implications for genotype-phenotype correlations and genetic counseling.

The peroxisome biogenesis disorders (PBDs) are a variety of autosomal recessively inherited disorders due to defects in peroxisome biogenesis, and Zellweger spectrum disorders (ZSDs) represent the most severe and common phenotype of PBDs (Shimozawa, 2007; Waterham et al., 2016). ZSDs are caused by variants in at least 13 PEX genes coding PEX proteins (peroxins), which are involved in the biogenesis of peroxisomal membrane proteins, the import of peroxisomal matrix proteins, and complete degradation of any peroxisomal remnants (Waterham et al., 2016). Peroxisomes are membrane‐bound organelles that participate in various metabolic pathways, such as the synthesis of ether phospholipids and bile acids, the α‐ and β‐oxidation of fatty acids, and the removal of glyoxylate and reactive oxygen species. As the peroxisome plays a crucial role in the proper functioning of several organs, ZSDs can affect morphology, neurological function, the liver, the kidney, and other organs, either severely or mildly (Waterham & Ebberink, 2012).

Variants in the PEX13 (OMIM:601789) gene only affect the import of peroxisomal matrix proteins and are relatively rare genetic aberrations in ZSDs, with less than 20 variants reported worldwide to date (Borgia et al., 2022).

The aim of this report is to present a comprehensive characterization of a patient with Zellweger spectrum disorders (ZSDs), who was diagnosed based on biochemical and molecular evidence. The patient carried a novel pathogenic variant in the PEX13 gene, inherited from a consanguineous family. A literature review revealed that such cases are seldom reported in China. We hope that by describing the patient's PEX13 gene variant, we can contribute to the understanding of the phenotype–genotype correlation in ZSDs.

Informed consent was obtained from the parents of the proband before this study was conducted. The Institutional Review Board of Guangzhou Women and Children’ Hospital approved the study protocol.

In order to confirm the diagnosis, the child underwent routine biochemical tests, plasma VLCFA analysis, full‐spectrum amino acid analysis, bile acid profiling and adrenal function tests, and other related examinations in our hospital. At the same time, brainstem auditory evoked potential and brain MRI examinations were performed.

Peripheral blood samples were collected from the proband and his family members. Genomic DNA was extracted using a Blood Genomic DNA Mini kit. Clinical whole exome sequencing was performed by the Berry Genomics to detect pathogenic variants in a local hospital. The variant was verified by Sanger sequencing in the proband and his parents in our hospital. The sequencing data were aligned with reference sequences from GenBank (NM_002618.3) using Chromas software. The effect of genetic variants on the function of PEX13 was predicted by the online program InterVar according to the ACMG (American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics) guidelines and ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation Recommendation for in trans Criterion (PM3)—Version 1.0.

PubMed, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and Wanfang Database were searched for articles published before May 2023. The selection criteria for articles were: (1) case reports of ZSD caused by PEX13 variants, (2) individuals with both defined variants and clinical manifestations, and (3) language restricted to English and Chinese.

A 7‐month‐old male infant was brought to our hospital's Genetic Counseling Clinic due to severe hypotonia. The patient had been hospitalized twice previously in a local hospital. He was born to consanguineous parents (first‐degree cousins) in the southwest of China at 40 weeks of gestation with a normal delivery and a birth weight of 2810 g. His Apgar score was seven at 1 min and eight at 5 min, and he was hospitalized due to dyspnea, bluish skin, and an SPO2 below 80% despite receiving oxygen. He exhibited a poor response and hypotonia. He was diagnosed with meconium aspiration syndrome and received treatment, including an invasive ventilator, antibiotics, and supportive and symptomatic therapies, before being discharged ten days later.

At 3 months of age, he was still unable to raise his head and presented with hypotonia, leading to his readmission to the hospital due to convulsions without fever. Laboratory tests revealed abnormal liver enzymes (ALT of 126 U/L (8–71), AST of 221.9 U/L (21–80)). Cranial MRI showed normal results, electroencephalography (EEG) revealed spike slow waves and spike waves in the bilateral central and mesial temporal regions. Renal ultrasound did not show multiple cysts.

At the age of 7 months, the patient was admitted to our hospital with hypotonia, developmental delay, and muscle weakness. His weight was 6.5 kg (Z score −2.2) and his height was 70 cm (Z score 0.38) at 7 months. His head circumference was 44 cm, and the size of his anterior fontanel was 2 × 2 cm. He was unable to lift his head or turnover and exhibited a prominent forehead, visual impairment, and hearing problems (Figure 1). Physical examination revealed hypotonia and a liver 2 cm below the coastal area.

In the laboratory tests, plasma VLCFA analysis showed significantly elevated levels of C26:0 (9.68 μmol/L, normal range: <0.89 μmol/L), and an elevated C26:0/C22:0 (2.124, normal range: <1.04), with a C26:0/C22:0 ratio 0.716 (normal range: <0.013). Phytanic acid was found to be high at 31.98 (normal range: <5.7 μmol/L) and pristanic acid was elevated at 16.60 μmol/L (normal range: <0.60). Full‐spectrum amino acid analysis indicated a significantly increased level of pipemidic acid (221.45 μmol/L, normal range: 0.5–2.5 μmol/L). Routine biochemistry test showed an elevated ALT of 184 U/L (8–71), AST of 299 U/L (21–80), and a total bile acid level of 73.1 μmol/L (0.5–10), while bilirubin and GGT levels were within the normal range. The coagulation function was normal. Bile acid profiling suggested increased level of 3α, 7α‐dihydroxy‐5β‐cholestanoic acid and 3α,7α,12α‐trihydroxy‐5β‐cholestanoic acid. ACTH values were minimally altered, and adrenal hormone levels were within the normal range.

Brainstem auditory evoked potential (BAEP) testing revealed damage to the bilateral auditory pathway, a brain MRI at 9 months of age showed widened outer cerebral space and suspicious abnormal signal in the bilateral basal ganglia (Figure 2).

Cranial MRI results. They show widened extra‐axial spaces, slight enlargement of the third and bilateral ventricles, and suspected abnormal signals in bilateral basal ganglia. (a) T2‐weighted sequence, (b) FLAIR sequence.

The patient was prescribed levetiracetam for convulsions and started treatment with ursodeoxycholic acid and liver‐protecting agents. Despite treatment, the patient has not shown any psychomotor progress and has started to suffer from temporary apnea. The patient died at the age of 14 months.

Using DNA from the patient's peripheral blood, a whole exome sequencing (WES) was conducted at a local hospital and detected a homozygous missense variant c.493G>C (p.Ala165Pro) in the PEX13 gene (NM_002618.3). This variant was verified by Sanger sequencing in the proband and found to be heterozygous in both parents in our hospital. This site is located in the hotspot mutation region, the N‐terminal domain of PEX13 (PM1) and is not present in the 1000G, gnomAD, and ExAC databases (PM2). It was predicted to be pathogenic by multiple tools (PP3). The patient presents homozygous genotype in a consanguineous family (PM3‐supporting) (Figure 3). We classified this variant as likely pathogenic and novel according to the ACMG criteria and ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation Recommendation for in trans Criterion (PM3)—Version 1.0.

Human peroxisomes play a crucial role in both catabolic and anabolic metabolic pathways (Klouwer et al., 2015; Waterham et al., 2016). The primary main catabolic pathways in peroxisomes involve alpha‐ and beta‐oxidation pathway of very long‐chain fatty acids (VLFCA), branched‐chain fatty acids, and bile acid intermediates, while the main anabolic metabolic pathways include the synthesis of ether phospholipids, bile acids, and so on (Klouwer et al., 2015; Wanders & Waterham, 2006). Our patient presented elevated levels of VLFCA, phytanic acid, and pristanic acid in the serum, along with high levels of serum pipemidic acid. The increased total bile acid concentration suggested a defect in peroxisomal anabolic metabolic pathways, which were all consistent with the biochemical manifestations of ZSDs. As peroxisomes are abundant in the liver and kidney, defects in these organelles can lead to the accumulation of toxic metabolites, liver dysfunction, and renal cyst formation, which are commonly observed (Klouwer et al., 2015). Our patient also presented with an enlarged liver and elevated transaminase levels, although renal cysts were not detected, indicating the heterogeneity of clinical presentations (Klouwer et al., 2018).

The elevation of serum VLCFA and phytanic acid are considered effective biochemical markers of peroxisomes dysfunction (Wanders et al., 2017). It has been theoretically proposed that patients with severe ZSDs may exhibit higher levels of VLCFAs than those with milder forms of the disorder (Berendse et al., 2016). However, it has been found that normal or mildly elevated VLCFA levels have been observed not only in patients with mild ZSDs (Lipinski et al., 2020; Rydzanicz et al., 2017), but also in patients with severe forms of the disorder (Borgia et al., 2022). These observations suggested that diagnosis of ZSDs can be challenging.

Classically, severe ZSDs present with hypotonia, epileptic seizures, and failure to thrive (Klouwer et al., 2015), which are thought to result from the accumulation of VLFCA and pristanic acid mainly in the nervous system (Klouwer et al., 2018). However, the specific pathogenic mechanism of these symptoms is still poorly understood. Our patient also experienced severe hypotonia, developmental delay, and muscle weakness since birth. The homozygous missense variant we identified might cause the severe form of ZSDs. Borgia et al. reported that a patient with another homozygous missense PEX13 p.R294W variant from a consanguineous family also showed severe clinical symptoms (Borgia et al., 2022). This patient was born into a first‐cousin Pakistani‐Canadian family. She exhibited profound hypotonia, feeding difficulties, and failure to thrive at birth. At four years of age, she was diagnosed with severe developmental delay. Although the brain MRI of our patient was normal at three months, it revealed a suspicious abnormal signal in the bilateral basal ganglia at nine months. In contrast, the MRI of the other three reported patients involved the cerebellar white matter and parietal lobes (Borgia et al., 2022). While polymicrogyria and pachygyria have been reported to be more common in patients with severe ZSDs (Weller et al., 2008), the brain MRI results in this study showed varied and less severe abnormalities than clinical presentation. This indicates that the clinical features of ZSDs may not correspond to the severity of brain abnormalities detected by MRI. Furthermore, we noticed abnormal signal in the bilateral basal ganglia, which was suspicious. This is remarkable because basal ganglia abnormalities are uncommon in PBDs, which are the main subgroup of ZSDs. PBDs primarily affect the brain white matter, causing demyelination and leukodystrophy. Thus, our finding expands the diversity of brain MRI characteristics in ZSDs and emphasizes the need for careful interpretation of MRI.

The PEX13 gene, which encodes Peroxin 13, is located on chromosome 2 and consists of 4 exons. A literature review (Table 1) revealed that variants in PEX13 were rare, with only 22 cases reported worldwide (including the case in this study) and most cases were homozygous variants (4/22), with relatively severe clinical manifestations. Twenty types of variants have been reported, including missense, nonsense, frameshift, intronic, and deletion variants, which are mostly distributed in exons 1, 2, and 4, especially exons 2 and 4, which may be related to the key structural domains of Peroxin‐13_N domain and SH3_PEX13_eumet domain located in exons 2 and 4, respectively (Figure 3). The number of cases is relatively small; therefore, the relation between genotype and phenotype requires further analysis. While some researchers suggest that loss‐of‐function variants such as nonsense variants or frameshift variants often result in severe ZSD phenotype (Al‐Dirbashi et al., 2009; Fujiki, 2016; Krause et al., 2006), missense homozygote variants such as p.Arg294Trp and p.Gly324Arg have also been reported to cause severe neurological impairment (Borgia et al., 2022). Interestingly, the clinical phenotypes caused by homozygous missense variants in the PEX13 gene varied, with some cases reported as infantile‐onset and severe clinical manifestations, and some cases reported as onset after 1 year of age, but with typical symptoms and rapid progression (Borgia et al., 2022; Shimozawa et al., 1999).In our study, a patient with early onset and serious clinical manifestations was found to harbor a missense homozygote p.Ala165Pro variant, indicating a complex relationship between phenotype and genotype. The Ala165 residue is located in the N‐terminal region of Peroxin 13, and both terminal parts are oriented towards the cytosol (Girzalsky et al., 1999). PEX13 is one of the two core components of the docking/translocation module (DTM) with PEX14, and its transmembrane hydrophilic channel is utilized to import peroxisomal matrix proteins (Barros‐Barbosa et al., 2019). The p.Ala165Pro variant may disrupt the terminal structure of Peroxin 13, resulting in a deficiency of the peroxisomal matrix proteins importation. The absence of functional experiments is a possible limitation that our study faces.

ZSDs are a group of genetic disorders for which curative therapies have yet to be developed. As such, patients are typically managed using supportive and symptomatic therapies, including the use of antiepileptic drugs to manage epilepsy (Klouwer et al., 2015). Given the hereditary nature of these conditions, it is also essential to provide affected families with genetic counseling and prenatal diagnosis (Cheillan, 2020).

In summary, we present a case report of a patient with severe ZSDs carrying a novel missense variant c.493G>C (p.Ala165Pro) in the PEX13 gene. The elevated levels of VLFCA, phytanic acid, and pipemidic acid in the patient's serum were consistent with the diagnosis of ZSDs. The patient exhibited typical clinical manifestations, including severe hypotonia, developmental delay, sensorineural deafness, and ocular abnormalities. Given these findings, we recommend genetic counseling to the patient's family with regard to future pregnancies.
