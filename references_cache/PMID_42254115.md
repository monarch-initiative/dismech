# High-altitude pulmonary edema: a case series of four patients from Nepal.
**Authors:** Paudyal A, Bhatta S, Dhungana S, Karki S, Gautam M, Paudel S, Regmi SM, Khadka S, Thapa A, Dhital S
**Journal:** Ann Med Surg (Lond) (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1097/MS9.0000000000004976](https://doi.org/10.1097/MS9.0000000000004976)

## Content

BACKGROUND AND IMPORTANCE: High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) is a potentially fatal, non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema that develops after rapid ascent above 2500 m. Its incidence is influenced by ascent rate, altitude, and individual susceptibility, with risk factors including overexertion, cold exposure, respiratory infection, and genetic predisposition. Despite Nepal's extensive high-altitude trekking activity, published HAPE reports remain limited. This case series describes the clinical presentation, diagnostic features, and management outcomes of HAPE in Nepalese trekkers to promote early recognition and guide timely interventions.

METHODS: This retrospective case series included patients with HAPE managed at a tertiary care center in Nepal. Cases were identified from hospital records, and relevant literature was narratively reviewed to contextualize findings.

CASE PRESENTATION: Four previously healthy adults developed acute HAPE after rapid ascent above 4000 m in Nepal. They presented with dyspnea, cough, hypoxemia (SpO2 55-60%), and bilateral crackles. Imaging confirmed non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema. All patients received supplemental oxygen, nifedipine, and supportive care, resulting in gradual clinical improvement and discharge within 3-5 days.

CLINICAL DISCUSSION: This series highlights the clinical variability of HAPE and emphasizes early diagnosis, descent, and oxygen therapy as mainstays of treatment. Rapid ascent, excessive exertion, cold exposure, and preceding respiratory infection were identified as common triggers. Diagnosis relied on clinical assessment supported by imaging, including bedside ultrasound. All patients recovered with conservative management.

CONCLUSION: HAPE is life-threatening but reversible with prompt recognition and management. Strengthening clinician awareness, training, and early diagnostic capacity in high-altitude regions is essential to reduce associated morbidity and mortality.

Each year, millions of individuals journey to high-altitude destinations, yet many may not be fully aware of the associated health concerns. This highlights an important opportunity for healthcare providers to support these travelers by developing proficiency in recognizing, managing, and preventing the complications of high-altitude exposure[1]. High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) is a potentially fatal form of altitude sickness marked by the abnormal accumulation of fluid in the lungs. Clinical manifestations typically emerge within 2–4 days following ascent to altitudes exceeding 2500 m[2]. Although rapid ascent to high altitude is a well-established risk factor, the likelihood of developing HAPE is significantly amplified by physical overexertion and the presence of a concurrent respiratory tract infection[3,4].

The reported incidence of HAPE varies widely across the global population, ranging from less than 0.01% to as high as 31%, depending on the rate of ascent, individual susceptibility, and the final altitude achieved[5]. Among trekkers and mountaineers in the Himalayas and Alps who ascend at a rate exceeding 600 m per day, the incidence of HAPE is approximately 4%. This non-cardiogenic form of pulmonary edema is highly lethal, with an estimated mortality rate of 50% in the absence of timely intervention[6]. The etiology of HAPE is multifactorial, driven primarily by hypobaric hypoxia and a rapid rate of ascent. Additional significant risk modifiers include strenuous exertion, cold ambient temperatures, recent respiratory infections, and male sex[5]. A genetic predisposition is also recognized, linked to specific mutations such as those in the JAK2 gene, P1B1 cytochrome genes, and a deletion in the histidine-rich glycoprotein gene, the latter of which is associated with an earlier onset of the condition[7,8]. HAPE has two primary onset patterns. The first and most common form affects non-acclimatized lowlanders following a rapid ascent above 2500–3000 m. The second form, referred to as re-entry HAPE, is observed in highland dwellers who reascend after a period of time at sea level. The pathophysiological mechanism is likely identical for both types[9]. The underlying pathophysiology of HAPE is driven by a pronounced increase in pulmonary artery pressure, secondary to hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction. This elevated pressure induces a permeability defect in the pulmonary capillaries, culminating in the leakage of a high-protein, sanguinous exudate into the alveolar spaces. This hallmark finding is consistent with its classification as a non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema[8].

A clinical diagnosis of HAPE requires the presence of a minimum of two symptoms, such as dyspnea at rest, cough, weakness, reduced exertional tolerance, or chest tightness, concurrent with at least two physical signs, including inspiratory crackles, central cyanosis, tachypnea, or tachycardia[6]. The diagnosis of HAPE is challenging due to the absence of a single gold standard test and its symptomatic overlap with other conditions. It remains primarily a clinical diagnosis. First-line treatment involves immediate descent, supplemental oxygen, and, if available, the use of a portable hyperbaric (Gamow) bag. Pharmacological therapy with nifedipine is also a cornerstone of treatment for susceptible individuals. While positive pressure ventilation [continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP)] can be a highly effective adjunctive therapy by improving oxygenation and reducing preload, it is not the primary or initial treatment modality and is often unavailable in remote settings[2,3].

Although Nepal is one of the global epicenters for high-altitude activity, there is a striking lack of documented cases of HAPE, a known fatal condition[10–13]. Therefore, this case series aims to detail the clinical presentation, diagnostic features, including radiological and ultrasonographic findings, and treatment outcomes of HAPE in the Nepalese population. By reporting these cases, we seek to provide valuable insights and practical management strategies for clinicians operating in Nepal and similar high-altitude settings.

This case series has been reported in accordance with the PROCESS guideline[14]. In this case series, we report four previously healthy patients developing HAPE.

This retrospective hospital-based case series was conducted at a tertiary care center in Nepal. Patients diagnosed with HAPE between 1 July 2024 and 31 July 2025 were identified through a systematic review of medical records. All consecutive patients meeting predefined diagnostic criteria during this 13-month period were included. No cases were prospectively enrolled.

Case identification was performed using two complementary strategies: First, electronic search of hospital discharge diagnoses for the terms “high-altitude pulmonary edema” and “HAPE”; and second, manual review of radiology reports for chest imaging (radiograph and ultrasonography) containing descriptors consistent with pulmonary edema including “bilateral opacities,” “airspace disease,” “alveolar infiltrates,” “confluent opacities,” and “B-lines” in patients with documented recent ascent above 2500 m. No cases were excluded due to incomplete records or alternative diagnoses during the study period.

recent high-altitude exposure (>2500 m),acute onset of dyspnea and hypoxemia,radiologic findings consistent with non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema, anddocumented clinical improvement following oxygen therapy and/or descent.

A 29-year-old previously healthy male trekker from Germany presented with progressive shortness of breath and fatigue after ascending rapidly to 4200 m in the Annapurna region over 2 days without proper acclimatization. On the second night, he developed exertional dyspnea, a nonproductive cough, headache, and malaise. Symptoms worsened the following morning, with dyspnea at rest and the production of pink, frothy sputum.

At the high-altitude base camp, limited resources prevented a full clinical evaluation, but pulse oximetry revealed severe hypoxemia (SpO2 60% on room air). Due to the severity, he was evacuated by helicopter to a tertiary care hospital at a lower altitude, where he received oxygen supplementation.

On admission, he was alert and oriented but in obvious respiratory distress. Vital signs were notable for SpO2 65% on room air, heart rate 120 bpm, respiratory rate 34/min, blood pressure 120/80 mmHg, and temperature 98.4°F. Cardiovascular examination was unremarkable, with normal heart sounds and no signs of right heart strain. Pulmonary auscultation revealed coarse inspiratory crackles in the mid and lower lung zones bilaterally. Chest imaging revealed features consistent with non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema. The initial chest radiograph showed asymmetric patchy and confluent infiltrates in the bilateral mid and lower lung zones, as shown in Figure 1A, while lung ultrasonography demonstrated multiple confluent B-lines replacing the normal A-lines, as shown in Figure 1C. Follow-up chest radiograph after 2 days showed marked resolution of the pulmonary edema, as shown in Figure 1B. Transthoracic echocardiography confirmed normal left ventricular function, effectively ruling out a cardiogenic cause.

A 24-year-old male medical student from Nepal experienced acute dyspnea, chest tightness, severe headache, ataxia, nausea, and vomiting during a rapid ascent to 4100 m. He had no history of cardiopulmonary disease and reported no fever, hemoptysis, or wheezing. Due to the severity, he was evacuated by helicopter to a tertiary care hospital at a lower altitude.

On examination, he appeared fatigued with peripheral cyanosis. Vital signs included SpO2 60% on room air, respiratory rate 28/min, heart rate 105 bpm, blood pressure 110/70 mmHg, and temperature 98.2°F. There were no clinical signs of right heart failure. Cardiac auscultation was normal, while pulmonary examination revealed fine inspiratory crackles throughout both lung fields. Chest imaging demonstrated findings consistent with HAPE, as per Figure 2. The initial chest radiograph showed confluent opacities predominantly involving the lower lung zones bilaterally, as shown in Figure 2A, and lung ultrasonography revealed a few confluent B-lines suggestive of interstitial edema, as per Figure 2C. A repeat chest radiograph obtained after 48 h showed near-complete resolution of the opacities, as shown in Figure 2B. Echocardiography confirmed normal ventricular function, excluding pulmonary hypertension.

A 39-year-old male from the USA began experiencing sudden shortness of breath and reduced exercise capacity while ascending toward a high-altitude lodge at 4200 m in the Manaslu region. This case series highlights the clinical spectrum of HAPE observed among trekkers in the Nepalese Himalayas. The cases demonstrate that early recognition, prompt descent, and oxygen therapy remain the cornerstone of management, even in resource-limited settings. These observations underscore the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for HAPE in individuals presenting with acute respiratory symptoms following rapid ascent. These findings are discussed in relation to previously reported cases, with emphasis on diagnostic challenges, management approaches, and preventive implications in high-altitude regions.

The clinical features observed in our cases align closely with those described in earlier reports, where HAPE typically develops within 2–4 days of rapid ascent above 2500–3500 m[10]. Dyspnea, dry cough, and reduced exercise tolerance were common early symptoms, while inspiratory crackles and hypoxemia served as key diagnostic clues. Similar to previous studies, all patients in our series responded favorably to conservative measures, including descent, oxygen supplementation, and rest, without the need for mechanical ventilation or advanced pharmacologic therapy[15]. However, none of the patients reported the use of pharmacological prophylaxis such as acetazolamide, nifedipine, or other preventive strategies prior to ascent.

The primary and most urgent intervention for HAPE is prompt descent to a lower altitude, ideally by a minimum of 1000 m, while ensuring the patient exerts themselves as little as possible. For milder cases, this descent alone can be enough to alleviate symptoms. Since physical activity can worsen the condition, patients often require assistance during evacuation. Once in a hospital setting, administration of supplemental oxygen is essential. If oxygen is accessible before descent begins, or if descent is not immediately feasible, it should be administered with a target oxygen saturation (SpO2) of greater than 90%. While descent and oxygen form the foundation of HAPE management, pharmacological interventions are indicated for patients who cannot maintain an SpO2 above 90% with oxygen therapy alone or when immediate descent is delayed. Additionally, CPAP can be a useful adjunct if available and if oxygen supplementation by itself is insufficient to maintain adequate saturation[16].

Nifedipine is the established first-line pharmacological therapy for HAPE, administered as a 20 mg SR dose every 8 h or a 30 mg SR dose every 12 h[16,17]. However, initial investigations into phosphodiesterase inhibitors, namely sildenafil and tadalafil, for both treating and preventing the condition have yielded promising results. These agents appear to be effective while demonstrating a reduced impact on systemic blood pressure compared to nifedipine[18,19].

Case 2 in this series received dexamethasone due to suspected concomitant HACE, presenting with severe headache, nausea, vomiting, and ataxia in addition to respiratory symptoms. The rapid resolution of both neurological and pulmonary symptoms in this case precludes definitive attribution of benefit to steroid therapy, and we do not suggest that dexamethasone contributed to HAPE resolution. This case highlights the importance of assessing for overlapping altitude illnesses, as HAPE and HACE frequently coexist.

Of the four patients, two were Nepali nationals, one of whom was a high-altitude porter, while the remaining two patients were foreign nationals from the USA and Germany. The occurrence of HAPE in a high-altitude porter highlights that even individuals with occupational exposure may be susceptible, particularly following rapid ascent or inadequate re-acclimatization.

In contrast to reports from other regions where delayed presentation and diagnostic uncertainty contribute to higher morbidity, early recognition and timely evacuation in our cases likely contributed to positive outcomes.

Diagnosing HAPE in remote, high-altitude environments poses a considerable challenge, as its symptoms can overlap with pneumonia, asthma, or acute respiratory infections. The symptoms of HAPE, including cough, difficulty breathing, a mild fever, and a slightly high white blood cell count, are very similar to those of pneumonia. A key factor in distinguishing them is the patient’s history; if they have recently been at a high altitude, HAPE is the more probable diagnosis.

Radiologic evaluation remains a diagnostic cornerstone in the confirmation and clinical management of HAPE. Practitioners should be aware that a degree of clinical–radiological discordance can be present in these cases. Lung ultrasonography has emerged as a critical diagnostic modality, demonstrating utility in the earlier detection of pulmonary edema. Its inherent portability also positions it as a potentially invaluable tool for point-of-care diagnosis in pre-hospital and resource-limited high-altitude environments[20].

Differentiating HAPE from pneumonia at altitude is a common diagnostic challenge, as both present with dyspnea, cough, hypoxemia, and crackles, and Low-grade fever may occur in HAPE, further complicating the distinction. In our series, prompt recognition of characteristic features, particularly exertional dyspnea and hypoxemia disproportionate to auscultatory findings, temporal relationship to rapid ascent, and absence of high fever or purulent sputum was crucial in differentiating HAPE from infectious causes. Where available, chest radiography and lung ultrasound further supported the diagnosis, revealing bilateral B-lines and patchy alveolar interstitial patterns in a non-lobar distribution, favoring hydrostatic edema over consolidation. The use of bedside ultrasound proved particularly valuable in resource-limited mountain settings. All four patients recovered without antibiotics, demonstrating the critical distinction that HAPE improves with descent and oxygen alone, pneumonia does not. These findings reinforce the growing role of clinical judgment and portable diagnostic tools in early identification of HAPE, ultimately facilitating timely descent and oxygen therapy before the onset of respiratory failure.

Several contributing factors may have influenced the development of HAPE in our patients. Rapid ascent without adequate acclimatization was a common precipitating event, consistent with previous findings that emphasize ascent rate as the most significant modifiable risk factor. In addition, strenuous physical exertion and exposure to cold ambient temperatures likely exacerbated hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, accelerating disease onset. One patient had a recent upper respiratory infection, which was resolved before ascent, which may have increased pulmonary vascular permeability and susceptibility to edema formation[9]. These observations highlight the importance of gradual ascent profiles, sufficient rest days during trekking, and early recognition of prodromal symptoms such as fatigue and exertional dyspnea. Pre-ascent education and awareness programs targeting trekkers, guides, and expedition organizers remain essential strategies to prevent severe altitude illness in Nepal’s popular trekking regions.

This case series has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting findings. First, the retrospective design is inherently subject to selection bias. All cases were identified following helicopter evacuation to a tertiary hospital; therefore, milder HAPE cases successfully managed at high-altitude aid posts or with descent alone are not represented. This limits generalizability to the full clinical spectrum of HAPE in community settings. Second, the small sample size (n = 4) precludes statistical analysis and may not capture the variability in presentation, risk factors, or treatment response across broader HAPE populations. Third, invasive hemodynamic monitoring (e.g., pulmonary artery catheterization) was not performed. While echocardiography excluded cardiogenic causes and pulmonary hypertension in all cases, direct measurement of pulmonary artery pressure – the diagnostic gold standard – was not available. Fourth, diagnosis relied on clinical and radiographic criteria rather than invasive confirmation, which, while standard in clinical practice, may introduce diagnostic uncertainty. Fifth, no long-term follow-up was conducted; therefore, we cannot comment on recurrence risk, persistent pulmonary vascular dysfunction, or long-term outcomes. Sixth, all patients received nifedipine; we cannot determine whether descent and oxygen alone would have achieved similar outcomes, nor can we assess the comparative effectiveness of pharmacological interventions. Finally, the small number and heterogeneous nationality of patients preclude any meaningful conclusions regarding genetic or ethnic predisposition to HAPE. Despite these constraints, the consistent presentation, diagnostic approach, and favorable outcomes across cases provide clinically actionable insights for HAPE recognition and management in resource-limited, high-altitude environments.

HAPE remains a life-threatening yet fully reversible form of altitude illness when identified early and managed appropriately. The experiences described in this series underscore the importance of clinical vigilance among healthcare workers in high-altitude regions and reaffirm the effectiveness of simple measures, such as descent, oxygen therapy, and rest, in achieving recovery. Strengthening awareness, training, and early-diagnosis capabilities among frontline providers can substantially reduce morbidity and mortality associated with HAPE in Nepal and similar mountainous settings.
