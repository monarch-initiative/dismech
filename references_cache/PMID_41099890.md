# A Multi-omics Exploration Revealing SLIT2 as a Prime Therapeutic Target for Peripheral Facial Paralysis: Integrating Single-Cell Transcriptomics and Plasma Proteome Data.
**Authors:** Liu Y, Li C, Yao L, Tian M, Tan Y, Shi L, Chen P
**Journal:** Cell Mol Neurobiol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10571-025-01607-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10571-025-01607-4)

## Content

Peripheral facial paralysis (PFP) is a common neurological disorder characterized by facial-nerve dysfunction. Identifying therapeutic targets and understanding the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying PFP are crucial for developing effective treatment strategies. This study combined Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to explore potential therapeutic candidates and their roles in PFP pathophysiology. The MR analysis included 1925 publicly available plasma protein cis-heritability instruments. Instrumental variables were selected for MR analysis to identify plasma proteins associated with PFP, followed by colocalization analysis to evaluate shared genetic variants between the identified proteins and PFP. After the initial identification of plasma proteins associated with Bell's palsy using MR analysis, a rat model of facial-nerve injury was established to further dissect underlying mechanisms at cellular and molecular levels. Using scRNA-seq technology, we delved deeply into cellular Heterogeneity and dynamic changes in gene expression in the facial-nerve nucleus tissues under both injured and control conditions, thereby achieving a systematic study ranging from macroscopic genetic associations to microscopic cellular functions. Finally, expression patterns were preliminarily validated by performing in vitro immunofluorescence analysis on the facial-nerve nucleus samples of SD rats. The MR analysis results identified 30 plasma proteins significantly associated with PFP, with nine target genes showing differential expression in the scRNA-seq data. Colocalization analysis demonstrated that slit guidance Ligand 2 (SLIT2), semaphorin 4D (SEMA4D), EGF containing fibulin extracellular matrix protein 1 (EFEMP1), and sprouty related EVH1 domain containing 2 (SPRED2) shared causal variants with PFP. SLIT2 was highly expressed in the microglia and inhibitory neurons in the experimental group, whereas SEMA4D showed elevated expression across multiple glial cell types in the same group. In contrast, EFEMP1 and SPRED2 showed distinct expression patterns in fibroblasts and oligodendrocytes. The role of SLIT2 has been previously well-documented in many central nervous system diseases. However, for the first time, this study detected SLIT2 alteration after facial-nerve injury. Altered intercellular signaling, particularly enhanced SLIT2-ROBO signaling between neurons and glial cells, was observed in the PFP group. Pseudotime analysis revealed dynamic SLIT2 expression during microglia and inhibitory neuron differentiation, mirroring changes in ROBO1 expression. Immunofluorescence analysis of rat facial-nerve nucleus samples verified that SLIT2 protein levels were significantly increased in the facial-nerve nuclei of injured samples. In conclusion, despite the fact that this study is primarily founded on animal models and despite notable differences existing between animals and humans in terms of the facial motor nucleus, this study successfully identified SLIT2 as potential therapeutic targets for PFP. The SLIT2-ROBO axis stands out as a particularly promising candidate. SLIT2 may play a role in modulating neuroimmune interactions and promoting nerve repair. These findings provide a foundation for future clinical studies and targeted interventions to enhance recovery from PFP. Future research should focus on human sample validation to enhance clinical translation.

Peripheral facial paralysis (PFP), also known as Bell’s palsy, is characterized by unilateral facial-nerve dysfunction leading to facial asymmetry. In severe cases, patients may experience debilitating sequelae such as synkinesis, contractures, or hemifacial spasms (Nakano et al. 2024). PFP affects 20–30 individuals per 100,000 annually, significantly impairing patient quality of life (Ali et al. 2020). After facial-nerve injury, the facial motor nucleus actively participates in the repair process by receiving, transmitting, and integrating afferent and efferent signals (Chen et al. 2009; Ohki and Takeuchi 2014). Injury signals trigger alterations in the expression of protein-encoding genes, inducing changes in the central nervous system (CNS) through various mechanisms. These changes involve the remodeling of closely interconnected glial cells and neurons within the facial motor nucleus (Lee et al. 2023). Glial cells and neurons surrounding the injured motor neurons exhibit functional heterogeneity and play distinct roles in nerve injury repair (Chen et al. 2016). For instance, depending on the pathological or injury state, microglia may exhibit neuroprotective or detrimental characteristics (Nakajima and Ishijima 2022). Astrocytes are also activated in response to injury, providing physical support to the injured tissue and promoting nerve repair through the release of energy substrates and neurotrophic factors (Müller et al. 1995; Rohlmann et al. 1994). Certain inhibitory neurons surrounding the injured motor neurons are also affected, temporally suppressing functional molecules (Roth and Draguhn 2012). These changes result in alterations in various biomolecules, including cytoskeletal molecules, metabolic enzymes, neuropeptides, and cytokines, further complicating the pathology of peripheral facial-nerve injuries (Ichimiya et al. 2013).

Proteins in the plasma proteome are actively secreted, shed, or leaked into the bloodstream and play critical roles in various biological processes, such as signaling, transport, growth, repair, and immune defense. Dysregulation of these proteins is commonly observed in a wide range of diseases, making them key targets for drug development (Gudjonsson et al. 2022). Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have revealed specific single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) on chromosomes that regulate protein expression and may be associated with dysregulation. These SNPs, which are proportional to the quantitative traits of protein abundance, are referred to as protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) (Fauman and Hyde 2022). Mendelian randomization (MR) leverages these pQTLs as instrumental variables to explore the potential causal relationships between exposure and outcomes, facilitating the screening of drug targets and biomarkers (Mälarstig et al. 2023). Compared with observational studies, MR helps mitigate the impact of confounding factors, thereby enhancing the reliability of causal inference (Walker et al. 2022).

To complement genetic data, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a powerful tool for mapping cellular heterogeneity and identifying cell type-specific expression changes under disease conditions (Zheng et al. 2017). It also enables the characterization of intercellular communication within tissues, particularly in the CNS (Huang et al. 2022). By examining cellular populations within the CNS, scRNA-seq has revealed differential expression patterns and associated pathways of specific genes in models of neuronal injury, offering molecular insights into the cell type-specific dynamics and intercellular communication processes involved in the pathogenesis of PFP (Mathys et al. 2019). Advances in high-throughput genomics and proteomics have facilitated the identification of potential therapeutic targets for neurological disorders such as multiple sclerosis and ischemic stroke (Lin et al. 2023; Chong et al. 2019). However, potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets of PFP remain unclear. In this study, we performed scRNA-seq analysis of the facial motor nuclei of rats following facial-nerve injury and conducted MR analysis on pQTL data from the UK Biobank. This approach led to the identification of potential therapeutic targets for PFP at the cellular and molecular levels.

Adult male Sprague–Dawley rats (250–350 g, 6–8 weeks of age) were used for the experiment. The animals were maintained under a 12-h light–dark cycle with free access to standard food and water. A total of 24 Sprague–Dawley rats were randomly assigned to the sham-operated control and facial-nerve injury groups, with 12 rats in each group. Rats were anesthetized using a routine intraperitoneal injection of 10% chloral hydrate (0.3 mL/100 g). Once anesthetized, a sterile Surgical field was prepared. Using a sterile scalpel, an incision was made near the stylomastoid foramen to expose the left facial nerve. A custom-made stainless-steel peripheral nerve clamp was used to damage the main trunk of the facial nerve. The clamp was applied with a pressure of 50 g for 90 s (Yandong et al. 2024). Thereafter, the muscle and skin were Sutured in layers using sterile sutures, and the incision was disinfected. In the sham-operated control group, rats were anesthetized in the same manner. The skin and subcutaneous tissues were incised, and the facial-nerve trunk was exposed and isolated using sterile forceps. Subsequently, the incision was immediately sutured. Upon recovery from anesthesia, rats in the facial-nerve injury group showed a loss of whisker movement on the left side, with the nose deviating toward the Healthy side and eyelid drooping. Fine tremors in the left whiskers were observed, often accompanied by sniffing motions, indicating the Successful establishment of the facial-paralysis model. Post-operatively, rats were placed in a warm, clean recovery cage with soft bedding material. Their body temperature was maintained at 36–37 °C using a heating lamp, and they were monitored closely for signs of distress. Analgesics were not administered to avoid potential interference with the study outcomes.

Thirty days post-surgery, three rats from each group were randomly selected, anesthetized, and decapitated. The brains were quickly removed and placed on ice. To accurately extract the cerebrum, cerebellum, brainstem, and medulla, specific anatomical landmarks were used. The brainstem was separated from the spinal cord at the level of the foramen magnum, and the cerebrum was carefully dissected away from the cerebellum along the natural fissure.. The tissues were then washed with ice-cold saline. The pia mater was removed under stereotactic microscopy and the brainstem was sectioned horizontally at the level of the cerebellar peduncle. A segment containing the facial-nerve nucleus was cut from the pons (3 mm posterior) and extending from the pontine tegmentum inferior to the inferior colliculus to the pontine basilar area. Both sides of the pyramidal tract were excised centrally. The left-side tissue was then placed in a 2 ml centrifuge tube and immediately processed for cell suspension preparation and quality control. After reverse transcription of the cDNA fragments, a standard sequencing library was constructed, and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed using the Illumina platform.

First, Cell Ranger was used to perform quality control of the sequencing data. Reads with a Phred quality score of less than 20 in more than 50% of their bases were removed as low-quality reads. The number of reads and the sequencing quality of each sample were then statistically analyzed. Based on unique molecular identifier (UMI) correction and effective cell identification, gene expression was quantified using the number of UMIs, generating a cell-gene expression matrix.

The expression matrix was then imported into the R package “Seurat” for dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis. Typical marker genes of neurons and non-neuronal cells were used to annotate each cell cluster. To identify key genes, the FindMarkers function in Seurat was used to further filter genes with differential expression in each cluster, applying selection criteria of P < 0.05 and |log2 (FC)|> 1.

Subsequently, the R package “CellChat” was used to analyze cell–cell communication. This package analyzes ligand-receptor pairs and their expression patterns in cell subpopulations, enabling robust quantitative inference, analysis, and visualization of intercellular communication networks based on single-cell gene expression data (Jin et al. 2024). Pseudotime analysis was performed using the R package ‘monocle2.’ This package orders individual cells along trajectories based on the expression patterns of key genes and places them along biological processes (such as cell differentiation) that correspond to specific developmental stages. This strategy simulates dynamic changes over time. Pseudotime analysis was used to infer the evolution trajectory of cell subtypes during disease progression. Visualization was performed using the R packages “Seurat,” “CellChat,” “ggplot2,” “monocle2,” “plot1cell,” and “ComplexHeatmap.”

We utilized plasma protein-level genome-wide association study (GWAS) data from the UK Biobank Pharmaceutical Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP). This project offers data on 2940 plasma proteomic features from 54,219 UK Biobank participants (https://www.synapse.org/#!Synapse:syn51365303) (Sun et al. 2023). However, pQTL data from the UK Biobank might be limited in fully capturing the intricate interactions among plasma proteins across diverse populations. To improve the research’s generalizability, we incorporated the deCODE GWAS study data, which generated 4907 protein GWAS data from 35,559 Icelanders. Notably, both datasets adhered to consistent data standards and quality control procedures.

The genomic locations of pQTLs relative to those of specific proteins are typically very close to those of their corresponding genes. Specifically, pQTLs near homologous genes are referred to as “cis-pQTLs,” with the assumption that pQTLs exert their effects through the homologous gene. In contrast, pQTLs located far from the homologous gene (or on different chromosomes), termed “trans-pQTLs,” are hypothesized to exert their effects via intermediate genes (Mälarstig et al. 2023). Various studies have employed different distance thresholds to differentiate between cis- and trans-pQTLs, with common thresholds set at 500 or 1000 kb (Pietzner et al. 2021).

In the MR studies focused on the proteome for drug target identification, we selected pQTLs as instrumental variables and applied the following specific selection criteria: (1) SNPs within ± 1 Mb of the gene region (cis-effect pQTLs); (2) a genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5 × 10–8 for identifying SNPs highly correlated with plasma proteins; (3) to ensure independent SNPs and reduce the impact of linkage disequilibrium (LD) on the results, we set an LD parameter threshold (r2) of 0.001 and a genetic distance of 10,000 kb; and (4) F-values < 10 to exclude weak instrument bias.

Additionally, in this study, Bell’s palsy was used as a proxy for PFP. Genetic association data for Bell’s palsy were obtained from the R11 version of the Finnish Biobank Database (https://r11.finngen.fi/). This dataset contains information from 4270 patients and 395,832 controls of European descent.

Two-sample MR analysis was performed using the R package “TwoSampleMR,” with plasma proteins as the exposure and PFP as the outcome. The selection criteria for pQTLs were as described above. The Wald ratio method was used when only one SNP was available for a specific protein. If two or more SNPs were available, the inverse variance-weighted (IVW) method was applied (Deng et al. 2022). P < 0.01 was considered statistically significant. Odds ratios (ORs) for disease risk were expressed as the per standard deviation (SD) increase in plasma protein levels. Visualization was performed using the R packages “ggplot2” and “ggVolcano.”

Colocalization analysis was performed to confirm the presence of shared causal genetic variants between exposure and outcome. For the differentially expressed genes identified by scRNA-seq and the corresponding plasma proteins that were positive in the MR analysis, we examined the colocalization relationship between these proteins and facial paralysis. Colocalization analysis involves five hypotheses: H0 (the selected SNP in the locus is not associated with protein A and disease B); H1 (the SNP in the selected locus is associated with protein A but not with disease B); H2 (the SNP in the selected locus is associated with disease B but not with protein A); H3 (the SNP in the selected locus is associated with either protein A or disease B, but the two associations are independent of each other); and H4 (the SNP in the selected locus is a shared SNP and associated with protein A and disease B). Due to the limited power of colocalization analysis, we focused on genes with a combined posterior probability (PPH3 + PPH4) ≥ 0.8 (Su et al. 2023). Bayesian colocalization analysis was performed using the R package ‘coloc’.

In each group, six rats were randomly selected and anesthetized, followed by perfusion through the left ventricle with 2% paraformaldehyde (pH 7.4). After removal, the brainstem containing the facial-nerve nucleus was fixed in the same fixative at 4 °C for 4–6 h. The brainstem was then placed overnight in 20% Sucrose in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 4 °C.

Using a cryostat with a precision of ± 1 μm, continuous coronal Sects. (20 μm) of the brainstem from the oral to the caudal direction were prepared. Two slices from every five slices were mounted on poly L-lysine-coated slides. Each set consisted of approximately 18–22 slices (i.e., the length of the facial-nerve nucleus cluster was approximately 1800–2200 μm) (Schwarz et al. 2006). Three of the five slices were selected for immunofluorescence staining.

The slides were sequentially placed in eco-friendly deparaffinized solutions I, II, and III for 10 min each. The slides were then washed in absolute ethanol I, II, and III for 5 min each, followed by washing with distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the slides in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) buffer (pH 8.0) in a microwave oven at medium power for 10 min followed by additional low-to-medium power Heating for further antigen retrieval. After antigen retrieval, the slides were treated with fluorescence quenching agent A for 30 min, washed with distilled water, and then washed three times in PBS (pH 7.4). Blocking was performed with bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min. The primary Anti-Slit2 Rabbit polyclonal antibody (1:200, Servicebio, Cat No. GB114837) was added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, the secondary antibody was added and incubated at room temperature for 50 min in the dark. After three washes with PBS, 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining solution was added and incubated for 10 min in the dark. The slides were then treated with fluorescence-quenching agent B for 5 min, followed by a 10 min wash in running water. Finally, the slides were mounted and imaged.

As depicted in Fig. 3C, significant differences in the proportion of cell subtypes were observed between the control (CO) and operated (OP) groups. In the OP group, the proportion of microglia, which are key immune cells in the central nervous system, increased significantly, indicating an enhanced immune response in the facial-nerve injury area. The proportion of oligodendrocytes, which myelinate axons in the central nervous system, also increased, potentially representing a compensatory myelination effort for nerve regeneration post-injury. The proportion of inhibitory neurons, which regulate neural circuit excitability, also increased, helping maintain the balance of the facial nerve-related neural network by counteracting over-excitability. Conversely, the proportion of astrocytes, which support neurons in various ways including nutrient provision and extracellular environment maintenance, decreased in the OP group. This reduction may disrupt the normal neuronal microenvironment and affect neuronal function.

We then identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) within each cell cluster (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Table 3). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that these DEGs were primarily enriched in the cytoplasm, intracellular anatomical structures, protein binding, nervous system development, and neuronal projections (Supplementary Fig. 2). These findings suggested that the identified DEGs were involved in fundamental cellular processes and played significant roles in the development and function of the nervous system. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis revealed that these genes were primarily enriched in cancer, infectious viral diseases, neurodegenerative diseases, endocrine system, immune system, and signal transduction pathways (Supplementary Fig. 3). These findings indicated that the identified DEGs were involved in diverse biological pathways, including those relevant to disease mechanisms and key cellular signaling processes.

Therefore, in addition to exploring characteristics of changes in the cell–cell communication network within the facial motor nucleus tissue under facial-paralysis conditions, we focused on analyzing whether there were ligand-receptor interactions and signaling pathways related to these four plasma proteins involved in the cooperative regulation among cells and the disease process. Global cell–cell communication maps were constructed for the control and facial-paralysis groups. Using the same instrumental variable selection criteria as in the UKB-PPP, we identified 1616 cis-heritability instrumental variables for plasma proteins from the deCODE GWAS data. The relevant SNP information are presented in Supplementary Table 5. Based on the IVW or Wald ratio method (P < 0.01), MR analysis revealed a positive causal relationship between 20 plasma proteins and facial paralysis (Supplementary Fig. 5). Supplementary Table 6 provides detailed information. Among proteins obtained from the UKB-PPP data and colocalization analysis, only SLIT2 (OR 0.56; 95% CI 0.36–0.87; P = 0.0098) also showed a significant causal relationship with Bell’s palsy in the deCODE GWAS data and, consistent with the UKB results, was potentially protective with increasing levels.

Mendelian randomization analysis, based on two independent datasets, has firmly established a significant causal relationship between SLIT2 and facial paralysis. Additionally, single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) data revealed a remarkable enhancement in the ligand-receptor interactions of the SLIT2-ROBO axis among different cell types. To further explore the underlying mechanisms, we integrated the scRNA-seq data and employed pseudotime analysis.

This approach was used to simulate the differentiation trajectory of cells from their initial state to a disease-related state. We placed a particular emphasis on expression patterns of genes such as SLIT2 during this process and their correlations with cell-function transitions. Evidence shows that SLIT2 may play a crucial role in communication between neurons and glial cells. Moreover, SLIT2 expression differed among microglia, astrocytes, and inhibitory neuronal subpopulations. To further explore the evolutionary patterns of these cells and investigate changes in SLIT2 expression during cellular development, we performed pseudotime analysis on the microglia, astrocytes, and inhibitory neuron subpopulations. The developmental timeline for the microglial subpopulation is shown in Fig. 6A. SLIT2 expression in microglia gradually increased along the developmental trajectory (Fig. 6B). The developmental timeline for the astrocyte subpopulation is shown in Fig. 6C, in which SLIT2 expression was relatively low (Fig. 6D). The developmental timeline for inhibitory neurons is shown in Fig. 6E, with SLIT2 expression showing an initial increase, followed by a decrease with cell development (Fig. 6F). As SLIT2 may primarily exert its biological functions by binding to ROBO receptors, we explored the potential mechanism of the SLIT2-ROBO axis in the pathological process of facial paralysis. By integrating scRNA-seq data, we analyzed the expression patterns and temporal trends of ROBO1 and ROBO2. In the facial-paralysis group, ROBO1 was widely expressed across various cell types, whereas ROBO2 was predominantly expressed in microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and inhibitory neurons (Fig. 6G). ROBO1 exhibited an expression pattern similar to that of SLIT2 in microglia, whereas ROBO2 showed relatively low expression in microglia (Fig. 6H). Additionally, although SLIT2 expression in astrocytes was low, ROBO1 and ROBO2 were highly expressed in astrocytes, with the expression levels increasing progressively along the developmental stages and peaking at later differentiation stages (Fig. 6I). Finally, ROBO1 and ROBO2 in the inhibitory neurons exhibited an expression pattern similar to that of SLIT2 (Fig. 6J).

Immunofluorescence experiments further confirmed that SLIT2 protein expression in facial-nerve nucleus samples from the facial-nerve injury group was significantly higher than that in the control group (Fig. 6K). Consistent with previous MR results, SLIT2 protein expression was positively correlated with facial-nerve paralysis.

This study provides evidence of potential therapeutic targets for PFP. MR analysis identified 30 plasma proteins significantly associated with PFP. Subsequent sc-RNA-seq data revealed the differential expression of nine proteins, including SLIT2, across distinct cell types. Colocalization analysis identified key plasma proteins (SLIT2, SEMA4D, SPRED2, and EFEMP1) that share pathogenic variants with PFP, supporting their potential as therapeutic targets. Importantly, SLIT2 showed a significant causal relationship with facial paralysis in different populations as well as differential expression in specific neuronal cell types and alterations in intercellular communication networks in PFP, particularly involving the SLIT2-ROBO signaling pathway, underscoring its role in neuron–glia interactions. These findings highlight the potential mechanistic contributions of SLIT2 to nerve injury and repair, providing novel insights into the molecular mechanisms of PFP and guiding targeted therapeutic development.

Our MR findings align with previous reports, underscoring SLIT2’s critical role in nerve regeneration. Traditionally recognized for its role in axonal guidance, SLIT2 has recently been implicated in various protective processes in CNS pathology, including cell migration, immune responses, vascular permeability, and angiogenesis (Sherchan et al. 2020). SLIT2 regulates neuronal migration and differentiation through its interaction with its receptor, ROBO1, which are key processes in nerve repair following injury, including facial-nerve transection, highlighting the critical role of the Slit-Robo pathway in axonal regeneration and repair (Guan and Rao 2003; Madura et al. 2004). Similarly, injecting SLIT2 into a rat germinal matrix hemorrhage (GMH) model activated the ROBO1-srGAP1 pathway, significantly reducing peripheral immune cell infiltration into damaged brain tissue, decreasing inflammatory markers, alleviating neuroinflammation, and mitigating brain tissue damage caused by GMH, which may have contributed to improved neural recovery (Li et al. 2023). Furthermore, injecting SLIT2 into injured spinal cords increases synaptic density and significantly improves motor function, emphasizing the critical role of the SLIT2/ROBO1 signaling pathway in promoting neural repair and functional recovery following spinal cord injury (Li et al. 2017). Our findings expand this understanding by demonstrating the association of elevated SLIT2 levels with reduced PFP risk, suggesting a potential protective role of SLIT2 in peripheral nerve injury. The causal relationship between SLIT2 and PFP risk, as identified by MR, strengthens the evidence for SLIT2-targeted therapeutic interventions for PFP.

scRNA-seq analysis in this study further underscored the cell-type-specific functions of SLIT2 within the facial motor nucleus, with high expression observed in inhibitory neurons, astrocytes, and microglia. Inhibitory neurons play a critical role in regulating excitotoxicity, and their dysfunction may adversely affect neural repair. SLIT2 overexpression significantly reduces neuronal excitotoxicity, accelerates glymphatic clearance, and improves cognition in multiple microinfarcts (He et al. 2020). Microglia play roles in neuroinflammation and repair; their morphological and functional changes following neural injury are essential for creating an environment conducive to axonal regeneration (Block et al. 2007). Although SLIT2 is generally expressed by neurons in the normal brain, it is also expressed by glial cells, particularly astrocytes, in response to injury (Hagino et al. 2003; Park et al. 2016). Our finding of elevated SLIT2 expression in microglia is novel, suggesting that SLIT2 may regulate inflammatory responses via microglia, mitigate chronic inflammation, and potentially promote regenerative pathways. A previous study also demonstrated SLIT2’s anti-inflammatory effects, showing that SLIT2, through PI3K-γ activation mediated by its receptors ROBO1 and ROBO2, facilitates microglia and macrophage chemotaxis and tumor-supportive polarization. SLIT2 knockdown inhibits macrophage invasion, promotes cytotoxic gene expression, improves tumor vascular function, and enhances chemotherapy and immunotherapy efficacy (Geraldo et al. 2021). Thus, SLIT2 may have therapeutic potential against PFP by modulating the activation of different cell types and cytokine production.

Our findings add to a growing body of evidence supporting SLIT2’s involvement in neuroimmune interactions. In the context of injury, SLIT2 inhibits the migration of various cell types, including neurons, pericytes, endothelial cells, and peripheral leukocytes. For example, the microglial and astrocytic responses reportedly modulate inflammation. Altay et al. reported that the systemic administration of human SLIT protein attenuated leukocyte-endothelial adhesion in cerebral microvessels following tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα)-induced inflammatory responses and global cerebral ischemia in mice. SLIT proteins reduce cerebrovascular inflammation by inhibiting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-1β and TNF-α, thereby decreasing microglia and astrocyte activation (Altay et al. 2007). Similarly, glioma cells treated with recombinant SLIT2 or stably transfected with SLIT2 exhibit reduced migratory and invasive capabilities compared with controls (Yiin et al. 2009). Recombinant SLIT2 inhibits the Cdc42-mediated infiltration of peripheral immune cells into the brain via the Robo1-srGAP1 pathway, thereby reducing neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis (Li et al. 2023). Consistent with these findings, our scRNA-seq results demonstrated upregulated SLIT2 expression in neurons, astrocytes, and microglia within injured facial-nerve tissues, suggesting its regulatory role in neuroimmune crosstalk within the facial motor nucleus. As neuroinflammation is a key component of PFP pathology, SLIT2’s role in modulating inflammatory responses by dampening detrimental immune reactions while promoting repair supports its potential as a therapeutic target for PFP.

The function of SLIT2 is mediated by intracellular signaling pathways activated by ROBO receptors and depends on the type of ROBO receptors expressed by the cells. The SLIT2-ROBO pathway influences multiple aspects of neuronal behavior, including neurite outgrowth and synapse formation, making it a pivotal axis for post-injury recovery (López-Bendito et al. 2007). A study on sciatic-nerve injury demonstrated a significant correlation between the activation of SLIT2-ROBO signaling and enhanced axonal regeneration and functional recovery (Chen et al. 2020). Our intercellular communication analysis revealed enhanced interactions of the SLIT2-ROBO axis in the facial motor nucleus under the condition of PFP. Given that the SLIT2-ROBO signaling is activated after peripheral nerve injury and may contribute to the axonal guidance and synaptic plasticity required for effective recovery (Delpech et al. 2024; Kaur et al. 2019), modulating this axis could potentially rectify disrupted intercellular communication in PFP, promoting axonal regeneration and functional recovery. For SLIT2, pseudotime analysis showed distinct expression patterns in different cell types. Specifically, in microglia, SLIT2 expression gradually increased along the developmental trajectory; in inhibitory neurons, it initially increased and then decreased with cell development. These findings suggest that the role of SLIT2 may vary depending on the cell type and the stage of cell development during PFP and that SLIT2 plays a key role in regulating neuroimmune interactions and nerve repair processes. Therefore, SLIT2 may act as a central molecular regulator; intervening at specific time points during cell differentiation to modulate the SLIT2-ROBO signaling could represent a novel therapeutic strategy to promote facial-nerve repair.

The roles of the other identified proteins, including SEMA4D, SPRED2, and EFEMP1, in the pathogenesis of facial paralysis, warrant further exploration. SEMA4D is a member of the semaphorin family with a sema domain and is primarily secreted by oligodendrocytes in the CNS. It acts as an axon guidance molecule by binding to its receptor, Plexin-B1, inducing growth cone collapse and regulating neuronal growth and connectivity (Ito et al. 2006; Zhang et al. 2014). SEMA4D also plays critical roles in CNS development, synapse formation (Kuzirian et al. 2013), neurodegenerative diseases (Evans et al. 2022), and neuroinflammation (Okuno et al. 2010). In the present study, SEMA4D was highly expressed in various glial cell types and neurons in the facial-paralysis group. Cell communication network analysis indicated enhanced SEMA4D-Plexin signaling in this group, suggesting the role of SEMA4D in modulating the inflammatory environment around the facial nerve and contributing to nerve repair after injury.

SPRED2 is involved in inhibiting the Ras/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)-mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway and is critical for nervous system development and function. SPRED2 negatively regulates the Ras/ERK-MAPK pathway and its deficiency leads to increased activation of tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB), a key molecule involved in neuronal growth, survival, and synaptic plasticity. This dysregulation results in circuit dysfunction in specific brain regions and obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD)-like behaviors (Ullrich et al. 2018). In our experiments, SPRED2 was differentially expressed in oligodendrocytes, suggesting that it may influence cellular responses during injury and repair and contribute to the pathogenesis of facial paralysis.

EFEMP1, an extracellular matrix protein, is closely associated with tissue remodeling and the regulation of cellular functions. Elevated plasma EFEMP1 levels have been linked to reduced brain volume, cognitive decline, and an increased risk of dementia, including Alzheimer’s disease (McGrath et al. 2022; Duggan et al. 2024). In our study, EFEMP1 was differentially expressed in fibroblasts, suggesting its potential involvement in facial paralysis through mechanisms including neuroinflammation, vascular dysfunction, and pathological remodeling of the extracellular matrix.

Despite the promising findings, this study has some limitations. First, although we identified potential therapeutic targets, this study was based on an animal model, and the results may not be fully applicable in human patients given the microenvironmental differences between human and rodent facial motor nuclei. In the future, we will explore gene-edited animal models closer to human conditions or use clinical samples for further validation to improve the reliability and transformation value of the results. Furthermore, we shall actively collect human specimens like blood and facial motor nucleus tissues; we plan to use immunohistochemistry and western blotting to validate differences in SLIT2 and SEMA4D expression in patients compared to healthy controls; to analyze their correlations with clinical symptoms, disease severity, and prognosis to assess biomarker potential; and to explore the feasibility of target-based therapies in patient-derived models. Regarding SLIT2, we aim to expand animal model samples, study their expression changes and cell-function relationships. We will use CRISPR-Cas9 for gene-editing at the cellular level; use virus-mediated gene transduction to observe facial-nerve function recovery in animal models; and detect expression and clinical correlations in clinical samples. Because we studied Bell’s palsy, future work will collect samples from different-etiology peripheral facial-paralysis patients and simulate various etiologies in animal models. Second, although MR is a powerful approach, its inherent limitations include potential biases from population stratification and pleiotropy, which may have affected the findings. Third, PFP is characterized by complex pathophysiology involving multifactorial interactions between genetic and environmental factors. Therefore, the role of each identified protein must be considered in the broader context of disease etiology.

In summary, the identified proteins are potential biomarkers for disease progression and therapeutic targets, offering insights into the mechanisms and treatment strategies for facial paralysis.
