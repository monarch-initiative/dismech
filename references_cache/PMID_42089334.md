# mRNA therapy improves the composition and motility in CCDC40-deficient cilia in vitro and in vivo.
**Authors:** Wohlgemuth K, Rasteiro M, Aneja M, Bota C, Cindric S, Freischem S, George S, Günes Günsel G, Ishola S, Koenig J, Kubisch-Dohmen R, Langenickel T, Loges NT, Lopes M, Mummert V, Olbrich H, Pennekamp P, Pereira T, Pinto AL, Raidt J, Rudolph C, Dorißen Ter Steege A, Valecha D, Lopes SS, Omran H
**Journal:** Am J Respir Cell Mol Biol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1093/ajrcmb/aanag034](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajrcmb/aanag034)

## Content

Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a genetically heterogeneous disorder leading to destructive airway disease with severe bronchiectasis and chronic lung failure in adulthood. Pathogenic variants in CCDC40 are associated with a more severe reduction of lung function compared to most other PCD types. Currently, no therapies correcting the underlying disease mechanism are available. Here, we investigate the efficacy of lipidoid nanoparticle-formulated mRNA encoding human CCDC40 (LNP-CCDC40-mRNA) as a corrective measure for structural and functional defects in vitro (human cells) and in vivo (zebrafish). Human nasal respiratory epithelial cells cultured at an air-liquid interface from 5 CCDC40-deficient individuals and a newly generated vertebrate animal model (ccdc40-/- zebrafish) were treated with LNP-CCDC40-mRNA. CCDC40-deficient cells were analyzed by high-speed video microscopy and immunofluorescence microscopy. ccdc40-/- zebrafish olfactory pit cilia were analyzed by high-speed video microscopy and fluid flow assays. Topical application of exogenous LNP-CCDC40-mRNA to CCDC40-deficient cells results in endogenous CCDC40 expression (10%-74% of ciliated cells), enabling axonemal integration of CCDC40-associated proteins (CCDC39, GAS8/DRC4, DNALI1). Consistently, ciliary beat frequencies were significantly increased in treated CCDC40-deficient cells and were comparable to those of healthy control cells. Further, we showed improved ciliary transport of fluorescent particles. Injection or topical application of human LNP-CCDC40-mRNA to ccdc40-/- zebrafish significantly increased ciliary motility and established directional flow in olfactory pits. We provide structural and functional evidence in vitro and in vivo for the biological efficacy of LNP-CCDC40-mRNA in CCDC40-deficient respiratory cells and zebrafish. Based on our results, an in vivo human study (Phase 1 trial) is planned in individuals with pathogenic variants in CCDC40.

Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a severe inherited airway disease for which no curative therapies are currently available. Our findings demonstrate that lipidoid nanoparticle–mediated delivery of mRNA encoding CCDC40 can restore ciliary structure and function in preclinical models, supporting mRNA replacement as a potential therapeutic strategy for genetically defined forms of PCD. In the next step clinical trials have to evaluate safety and efficacy in human PCD individuals.

Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a rare congenital disease group with onset in early childhood, estimated to affect 1:8000 live births.1,2 Ciliary dysfunction caused by loss of major structural components leads to impaired mucociliary clearance, resulting in recurrent upper and lower airways infections that cause bronchiectasis and chronic lung failure (Figure 1A-D).3-7 Standard of care is to treat symptoms with antibiotic therapies, routine immunizations, physiotherapy and the use of expectorants; currently, no therapies correcting the underlying disease mechanism are available.2

Biallelic pathogenic variants in CCDC40 are found in up to 9% of PCD individuals and are associated with a severe clinical phenotype, including earlier onset, worse lung function outcomes, rapid pulmonary function decline, more lobes of consolidation, and impaired growth parameters compared to PCD individuals with pathogenic variants in other genes.8-11 Thus, individuals with CCDC40 variants might especially benefit from novel therapies aiming to improve ciliary structure and function. Recent studies show that the CCDC39/CCDC40 heterodimer functions as a scaffold to support the assembly of an extensive network of more than 90 ciliary proteins, including ciliary address recognition proteins, which dock additional structural elements.12 CCDC40 deficiency results in disorganized microtubules and rigid cilia with impaired ciliary movement. Previous studies have shown axonemal loss of CCDC40 and CCDC40-associated proteins CCDC39, GAS8/DRC4, and DNALI1 (Figure 1B).8,12,13 The aim of this research project was to find an inhalative treatment option that corrects this underlying molecular defect in PCD individuals with pathogenic variants in CCDC40. First, we investigated whether an optimized human CCDC40-mRNA and a novel lipidoid nanoparticle (LNP) formulation (Figure 1E) is able to improve ciliary composition and function in vitro in CCDC40-deficient human nasal respiratory epithelial cells (CCDC40-hnRECs) from 5 PCD individuals cultured at air-liquid interface (ALI) conditions. Based on encouraging in vitro results, we next wanted to perform in vivo experiments in animals mimicking the human PCD disease. However, Ccdc40-deficient mice are not suitable for in vivo treatment due to premature lethality from severe hydrocephalus,8 which is a typical finding in PCD mouse models.14 Therefore, we generated a novel vertebrate model for PCD utilizing the CRISPR/Cas9 technology in zebrafish. We generated a ccdc40−/− zebrafish mimicking the human axonemal structural defects and dysmotility phenotype. We used this model to test whether human LNP-CCDC40-mRNA could correct the ciliary dysmotility phenotype in vivo.

Signed and informed consent was received from healthy controls and PCD individuals with biallelic pathogenic variants in CCDC40 according to ACMG criteria, using protocols approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Board at the University of Muenster (Muenster, Germany). Zebrafish studies were approved by the Direção Geral de Alimentação e Veterinária (DGAV) (Lisbon, Portugal). Codon-optimized chemically modified mRNAs (CCDC40-mRNA and tdTomato-mRNA)15-18 were provided by Ethris GmbH and formulated using the proprietary LNP platform (LNP-mRNA).19

Nasal brush biopsies were collected from healthy controls and CCDC40-PCD individuals, and hnRECs were cultured at air-liquid-interface (ALI) conditions as previously described.20 Normal human bronchial epithelial (NHBE) cells were cultured at ALI as recommended by the supplier. LNP-mRNA was applied to the apical side of ALI-cultures (resembling topical treatment using inhalation) and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 prior to analysis. Efficiency and cytotoxicity of the transfection were evaluated through single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), fluorescence measurements of tdTomato expression, cytotoxicity assays, and ciliary beat frequency (CBF) measurements using High-Speed Video Microscopy (HSVM) and Sisson-Ammons Video Analysis (SAVA).21 Additionally, in vitro ciliary transport assays (CTAs) were performed to evaluate the transport of fluorescent particles on the apical side of the inserts, as previously described with minor changes.20 Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis was performed as described22 using antibodies targeting acetylated α-tubulin, CCDC40 and the CCDC40-associated proteins CCDC39, GAS8 and DNALI1.8,13,23


ccdc40−/− zebrafish were generated using CRISPR-Cas9 technology as described.24 mRNA delivery to the zebrafish embryos/larvae followed 2 distinct approaches: (1) injection at one-cell stage or (2) olfactory pit (OP) topical application at 30 hours post fertilization (hpf) followed by ciliary assessment at 5 days post fertilization (dpf). High-Speed Video Microscopy, ciliary beat frequency, fluid flow assay and fraction of ciliated area beating was assessed as described.25,26 The ImageJ plugin TPIV (https://signaling.riken.jp/en/en-tools/imagej/639/) was used for particle image velocimetry analysis.

To evaluate the suitability of the ALI cell culture model for mRNA-based protein replacement therapy, we first assessed transfection efficiency and targeting of cell types using LNP-tdTomato-mRNA. Healthy control hnRECs (HC-hnRECs) cultured at ALI were transfected from the apical side with LNP-tdTomato-mRNA every other day over a 13-day period (day 1-14). The percentage of tdTomato-expressing cells was analyzed daily via fluorescence microscopy, starting 24 h after the initial transfection. HC-hnRECs treated with a vehicle were used as a control. Representative images of transfections at day 14 are shown in Figure SE1A (see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure), with the total cell count per growth area of transwell filter inserts normalized to 100% (Figure SE1B—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). The transfection efficiency of LNP-tdTomato-mRNA increased with repeated transfections, peaking at 22.9% of tdTomato-positive cells on day 10 (Figure SE1B—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). Simultaneously, we assessed potential transfection-induced cytotoxic effects using NucGreen staining. NucGreen analysis revealed the highest level of cytotoxicity after the first transfection, with 5.6% positive cells in the vehicle group and 14.3% in the LNP-tdTomato-mRNA transfected group (Figure SE1C—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). After the second transfection, cytotoxicity decreased to 2.4% in the vehicle group and to 2.18% in the LNP-tdTomato-mRNA-treated group. Cytotoxicity remained consistently low and comparable between groups throughout the experiment. After the final transfection, we performed IF analysis to assess the cell types targeted by LNP-tdTomato-mRNA transfection using antibodies directed against the mucus protein MUC5AC and the axonemal radial spoke protein RSPH4A. tdTomato expression was observed in MUC5AC-labeled mucus-producing goblet cells, RSPH4A-labeled ciliated cells, and cells negative for both MUC5AC and RSPH4A (Figure SE1D—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). These findings indicate that LNP-tdTomato-mRNA transfects distinct cell types in ALI cultures derived from hnRECs.

To further characterize levels of exogenous CCDC40-mRNA in different airway cell types, we transfected NHBE cells grown at ALI from the apical side with LNP-CCDC40-mRNA and performed scRNA-seq. Among the treatment types in NHBE ALI cultures (vehicle treated, untransfected control and LNP-CCDC40-mRNA), distinct human bronchial cell types were identified with a similar distribution (Figure 2A). As expected, endogenous CCDC40-mRNA is expressed mainly in the multiciliated cell population in all groups analyzed, ranging from 1.4% to 4.5% (vehicle: 4.5%; untreated: 0.6%; LNP-CCDC40-mRNA: 1.4%) (Figure 2B). In contrast, high levels of exogenous CCDC40-mRNA were exclusively detected in LNP-CCDC40-transfected inserts (vehicle: 0%; untreated: 0.02%; LNP-CCDC40-mRNA: 92.7%), with expression observed in more than 90% of cells across distinct cell populations. These included suprabasal cells (45%), submucosal gland duct cells (15.5%), club (14.1%), goblet cells (6.8%), basal resting cells (4.8%) and multiciliated cells (4.4%) (Figure 2C and D).

Next, we selected 5 individuals (1 male and 4 females, age range 13-46 years) harboring biallelic pathogenic variants in CCDC40 (Table 1) for CCDC40-mRNA protein replacement therapy. As reported previously, IF of CCDC40-hnRECs revealed the absence or severe reduction of CCDC40 and CCDC40-associated proteins CCDC39, GAS8/DRC4, and DNALI1 from the ciliary axonemes.8,14 We cultured these CCDC40-hnRECs at ALI and transfected them with LNP-tdTomato-mRNA or LNP-CCDC40-mRNA once a week for 6 weeks from the apical side of the culture, starting 20 days after airlift. HC-hnRECs cultured at ALI transfected with LNP-tdTomato-mRNA served as a control group.

We monitored transfection-induced cytotoxicity through lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) measurements. Continuous LDH monitoring revealed stable LDH release, with values comparable to untreated ALI. LDH release ranged from 5.04 ± 0.73% after the first to 4.08 ± 0.74% after the sixth LNP-tdTomato-mRNA transfection and 4.610 ± 0.636% after the first to 5.14 ± 2.66% after the sixth LNP-CCDC40-mRNA transfection (Figure SE2—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). Thus, we found no evidence of increased LNP-mRNA related cytotoxicity.

To evaluate the improvement in ciliary motility following LNP-CCDC40-mRNA administration, we measured ciliary beat frequencies as a functional readout by High-Speed Video Microscopy. Ciliary beat frequencies were compared between HC-hnRECs and CCDC40-hRECs after the fourth (Figure 3A and B) and sixth (Figure 3C and D) weekly transfections. After the fourth transfection, multiciliated HC-hnRECs (n = 6) treated with LNP-tdTomato-mRNA exhibited ciliary beat frequencies of 8.98 ± 0.08 Hz, while CCDC40-hnRECs (n = 5) treated with LNP-tdTomato-mRNA showed significantly reduced ciliary beat frequencies of 3.25 ± 0.08 Hz (P < .0001). In contrast, hnRECs of the same CCDC40-individuals (n = 5) treated with LNP-CCDC40-mRNA demonstrated a significant increase in ciliary beat frequencies to 9.48 ± 0.22 Hz (P < .0001), comparable to HC-hnRECs (P = .4931) (Figure 3A and B). Following the sixth transfection, ciliary beat frequency measurements remained consistent, with HC-hnRECs (n = 6) treated with LNP-tdTomato-mRNA showing 8.92 ± 0.10 Hz, CCDC40-hnRECs (n = 5) treated with LNP-tdTomato-mRNA showing 3.11 ± 0.09 Hz, and CCDC40-hnRECs treated with LNP-CCDC40-mRNA reaching 10.28 ± 0.20 Hz (Figure 3C and D). These findings confirm that LNP-CCDC40-mRNA transfections significantly increase ciliary beat frequencies in CCDC40-hnRECs.

To evaluate the ciliary transport capacity important for the clearance of airways, we conducted in vitro ciliary transport assays to analyze the transport of 0.5 µm fluorescent particles, providing an additional functional readout. After the sixth transfection, multiciliated HC-hnRECs (n = 6) treated with LNP-tdTomato-mRNA showed local transport in defined field of views (FOVs) of the insert. In contrast, CCDC40-hnRECs (n = 5) treated with LNP-tdTomato-mRNA only showed background flow of fluorescent particles. In contrast, hnRECs of the same CCDC40-individuals (n = 5) treated with LNP-CCDC40-mRNA showed local transport of fluorescent particles in a defined field of views of the insert, indicating improved ciliary transport capacity (Video S1). These findings confirm that LNP-CCDC40-mRNA transfections not only increase ciliary beat frequencies but can also improve ciliary clearance.

Next, we asked whether the functional improvement observed following LNP-CCDC40-mRNA treatment results from correction of the underlying disease mechanism in CCDC40-hnRECs. Immunofluorescence analyses performed after the final transfection in 4 of the 5 donors demonstrated that exogenous CCDC40-mRNA indeed results in endogenous CCDC40 protein expression in a subset of respiratory cells (Figure 4A-C). In one individual (OP-1072 II1), we were unable to retrieve enough material in order to perform immunofluorescence analyses. In addition, we found that the CCDC40 protein is functionally active and facilitates the integration of CCDC40-associated proteins (CCDC39, GAS8/DRC4, and DNALI1) into the ciliary axonemes of CCDC40-hnRECs: In HC-hnRECs, CCDC40 and CCDC40-associated proteins colocalize with the axonemal marker acetylated α-tubulin along the ciliary axonemes (Figure 4A). As expected, in all CCDC40-hnRECs transfected with LNP-tdTomato-mRNA, CCDC40, and CCDC40-associated proteins were absent or severely reduced from the ciliary axonemes (Figure 4B). In CCDC40-hnRECs transfected with LNP-CCDC40-mRNA, CCDC40 and CCDC40-associated proteins colocalized with the axonemal marker acetylated α-tubulin along the ciliary axonemes in a substantial proportion of treated cells (Figure 4C).

To quantify the efficacy of exogenous CCDC40-mRNA, we evaluated the presence of ciliated cells and the axonemal localization of CCDC40 and CCDC40-associated proteins. Incorporation levels varied among treated CCDC40-hnRECs ALI cultures of the 4 donors (Figure 5): We observed axonemal CCDC40 expression, albeit with some variability, in 10%-74% (mean 31.5%) of multiciliated cells. Axonemal localization of CCDC39, interacting directly with CCDC40 (heterodimer), also showed notable improvements (32%-56%, mean 38.75%) within a similar range. In contrast, axonemal localization of the N-DRC protein GAS8/DRC4 (3%-46%, mean 20.5%) and the inner dynein arm protein DNALI1 (4%-34%, mean 18%) was slightly lower when compared to the axonemal protein expression of the heterodimer composed of CCDC40 and CCDC39. These findings demonstrate that LNP-CCDC40-mRNA treatment could correct the molecular defect caused by pathogenic variants in CCDC40, in a subset of respiratory cells, consistent with the observed increase in ciliary beat frequencies.

Based on these in vitro results, we therefore evaluated the efficacy of human CCDC40-mRNA (39% identity with zebrafish ccdc40 protein) in vivo. First, we generated a novel zebrafish ccdc40-mutant line (ccdc40−/−) through CRISPR/Cas9, introducing a 2-base insertion (c.512dupCA) (Figure SE4B—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). ccdc40−/− larvae exhibited a curly tail phenotype close to the expected Mendelian ratio (82 of 353 larvae, 23.2%) (Figure SE4C—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure) and displayed immotile or hyperkinetic non-functional cilia in the olfactory pit at 5dpf. Overall, ccdc40−/− zebrafish recapitulate the ciliary phenotype observed in humans with pathogenic CCDC40 variants including the ultrastructural defect comprising microtubular disorganization (Figure SE4D and E—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure),8,27 enabling further experiments in a vertebrate PCD model organism.

To evaluate the ability of human LNP-CCDC40-mRNA to improve ciliary motility in olfactory pits, we treated ccdc40−/− larvae using 2 different approaches: injection of LNP-CCDC40-mRNA at the one-cell stage and bathing of ccdc40−/− larvae in embryo medium supplemented with LNP-CCDC40-mRNA at 30 hpf (Figure SE5—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). This topical application—by exposing larvae to LNP-mRNA in embryo medium—was developed as an alternative to injection and mimics the topical (inhalation) route of administration intended for human therapy, despite not being specific to the olfactory pit. However, initial experiments showed that the topical application of 1 µg/µL LNP-tdTomato-mRNA resulted in the expression of tdTomato in a subset of foxj1a: GFP-positive multiciliated cells in the olfactory pit (Figure SE6—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure).

To evaluate the injection approach, we selected 8 slightly curved larvae, all confirmed as ccdc40−/− larvae. These larvae exhibited a partially rescued tail phenotype compared to the characteristic curly tail of untreated ccdc40−/− larvae (Figure 6A). We performed High-Speed Video Microscopy to evaluate ciliary beat frequencies and active areas, and conducted fluorescent bead flow analysis to assess functional improvements (Figures 6 and 7) Wildtype larvae (n = 26) showed mean ciliary beat frequencies of 23.41 Hz and a mean bead velocity of 3.46 µm/s (n = 4), while most ccdc40−/− olfactory pits were either completely immotile (n = 33) or hyperkinetic with high ciliary beat frequencies (mean 44.96 Hz; n = 18), resulting in a significantly reduced mean bead velocity of 1.73 µm/s (n = 5) (Figures 6A-C and 7B). In contrast, ccdc40−/− larvae (n = 9) injected with LNP-CCDC40-mRNA showed significantly improved mean ciliary beat frequencies of 20.60 Hz and a mean bead velocity of 3.75 µm/s (n = 3), comparable to wildtype (Figures 6A-C and 7B).

To evaluate the topical application, we incubated ccdc40−/− larvae in medium supplemented with LNP-CCDC40-mRNA (10 ng/µL, 160 ng/µL), starting 30 hpf for a duration of 4 days. High-Speed Video Microscopy analysis and fluorescent bead flow analysis were conducted at 5 dpf. Larvae grown in LNP-CCDC40-mRNA-supplemented medium exhibited improved ciliary movement in the olfactory pits compared to untreated ccdc40−/− larvae (Figure 6B), with a significant improvement in beating area observed at the higher dose of 160 ng/µL LNP-CCDC40-mRNA (Figure 7A). However, while the percentage of ciliary movement increased in ccdc40−/− larvae upon incubation with LNP-CCDC40-mRNA supplemented medium (Figure 6A), no significant difference in ciliary beat frequencies was observed between untreated ccdc40−/− larvae and larvae incubated with either 10 or 160 ng/µL of LNP-CCDC40-mRNA (Figure 6C). However, mean bead velocity was increased at 160 ng/µL treatment, showing no significant difference from wildtype larvae denoting rescue (n = 4; P = .7619), in contrast to the 10 ng/µL treatment, which remained significantly reduced (n = 4; P = .0043) (Figure 7B).

To further evaluate flow directionality and bead velocity, we performed particle image velocimetry (TPIV) analysis and presented the results as heatmaps and rose plots (Figure 7C and D). In wildtype larvae, olfactory pit cilia pull fluid into the medial olfactory pit regions and expel it laterally,28 with most vectors pointing towards the fish’s nose (270°) or outwards, evidencing the expelled fluid (210°, 330°) (Figure 7C and D; Video S2—see online supplementary material). In untreated ccdc40−/− larvae, the direction of the vectors was randomized (Figure 7D; Video S2—see online supplementary material) while in LNP-CCDC40-mRNA-injected ccdc40−/− larvae, the main flow directionality and velocity were improved, similar to wildtype (Figure 7C and D; Video S2—see online supplementary material). Furthermore, the topical application of 160 ng/µL LNP-CCDC40-mRNA to ccdc40−/− larvae partially improved olfactory pit flow patterns in both the 270° and outward directions (Figure 7C and D; Video S2—see online supplementary material).

Overall, our findings demonstrate that human CCDC40-mRNA ameliorates the characteristic phenotype in ccdc40−/− zebrafish larvae, leading to a partially rescued tail phenotype, improved ciliary movement and function in olfactory pits.

In this study, we performed an mRNA-based proof-of-concept study aiming to improve the composition and motility of CCDC40-deficient cilia in CCDC40-hnRECs and in a novel ccdc40−/− zebrafish model. The mRNA used in this proof-of-concept was codon-optimized and chemically modified with 2′-thiouridine and 5′-methylcytosine, which results in stable and non-immunogenic mRNA.15 The mRNA was encapsulated in LNPs optimized for lung delivery, ensuring potency, tolerability, thermostability, resistance to shaking, and nebulization without undesired particle aggregation. Previous in vivo studies confirmed the high transfection efficiency of these LNPs after both systemic and aerosol administration.19 We utilized 3-dimensional ALI cultures of hnRECs from healthy controls and PCD individuals with biallelic pathogenic CCDC40 variants. These primary cell cultures closely mimic the in vivo respiratory tract environment, incorporating distinct cell types and preserving the disease phenotype, making them ideal for evaluating potential therapeutics.29 In ALI cultures, 2 routes of administration are possible: basal administration via supplementation in the medium or apical administration. Basal administration primarily targets basal cells, which can differentiate into various epithelial cell types, both under steady-state conditions and following acute injury.30 However, this approach does not reflect the therapeutic impact in vivo, as LNP-CCDC40-mRNA treatment is intended for inhalation and therefore has to penetrate the apical airway mucus layer. Therefore, we applied LNP-CCDC40-mRNA from the apical side to better mimic the in vivo situation in humans. We found that LNP-mRNA effectively targeted distinct cell populations, including ciliated cells, improving ciliary motility and transport of fluorescent particles, and axonemal composition in CCDC40-deficient nasal airway cells. These findings demonstrate that neither the mucus barrier nor the epithelial surface of the ALI cultures impede the expression of LNP-mRNA in a relevant cell type. Of note, we found a variable (depending on the donor) and partial correction.

However, there are some limitations to this system, including a small quantity and scarce availability of human tissue, and donor-to-donor variability.31 As an additional limitation of our study, transmission electron microscopy to assess therapeutic effects on axonemal ultrastructure has shown to be impractible in our system. A class 1 defect by transmission electron microscopy due to loss of CCDC40 from the ciliary axoneme is characterized by microtubular disorganization in more than 25% and inner dynein arm defects in more than 50% of axonemal cross-sections.32 To rule out defects caused by secondary effects, at least 50 cross-sections of ciliary axonemes from several distinct cells with clear ultrastructural features and an intact ciliary membrane have to be analyzed. Thus, a high number of cilia from distinct cells have to be analyzed to evaluate therapeutic effects accurately. Further reducing the probability to identify enough rescued cells is the incomplete rescue after mRNA-treatment (18%-38.75% in ciliated cells as assessed by immunofluorescence analyses) and reduced cell differentiation into multiciliated cells of CCDC40-hnRECs.12 Considering these factors and given the limited availability of ALI inserts, we chose not to use transmission electron microscopy as an outcome measure.

To address these limitations, we used immunofluorescence microscopy to confirm that exogenous CCDC40-mRNA successfully generates endogenous CCDC40 in a subset of respiratory cells (mean 31.5%), which is incorporated into the ciliary axoneme of treated CCDC40-hnRECs. We also demonstrate that this CCDC40 is functionally active, facilitating the integration of CCDC40-associated proteins—CCDC39 (mean 38.75%), GAS8/DRC4 (mean 20.5%), and DNALI1 (mean 18%)—into the ciliary axoneme of treated CCDC40-hnRECs. Despite marked variability between donors in the recruitment of CCDC40-associated proteins into the ciliary axoneme, all treated CCDC40-hnRECs showed significantly increased ciliary beat frequencies following LNP-CCDC40-mRNA administration. Furthermore, treated CCDC40-hnRECs were able to transport fluorescent particles in ciliary transport assays, indicating an improved ciliary clearance in vitro. As we used MediumG as culture medium to increase transfection efficiency, ALI-cultured hnRECs more closely resembled the bronchial epithelium displaying fewer multiciliated cells. Consequently, even in HC-hnRECs, the ciliary transport of fluorescent particles was only observed as local transport within defined field of views (Video S1), rather than global transport as observed in previous publications using StemCell PneumaCult ALI-Medium.20 However, we have previously demonstrated that a range of 30%-62% of functioning multiciliated airway cells is still sufficient to maintain ciliary airway clearance.33 Thus, our in vitro findings indicate that the achieved expression of CCDC40 and associated proteins in a subset of patient cells might be of clinical relevance. Our findings are consistent with a recent study by Hennig et al. also analyzing the feasibility of mRNA-based therapy for PCD.34 Here, aerosolized LNP-DNAI1-mRNA was successfully delivered in vitro to primary human bronchial epithelial cells with DNAI1 knockdown and to non-human primates in vivo. Here, we analyzed patient-derived cells. Of note, the mRNA used in our study (CCDC40, 4.2 kb) is considerably larger than the mRNA used in the DNAI1 study (DNAI1, 2.5 kb), indicating that mRNAs encoding larger proteins can also be used for mRNA therapy. Similar to our study, the DNAI1 study employed analysis of ciliary beat frequencies and immunofluorescence microscopy as outcome parameters. However, in addition, we demonstrate improved ciliary transport.

Encouraged by these findings, we aimed to provide an in vivo proof-of-concept in a novel ccdc40−/− zebrafish model using human LNP-CCDC40-mRNA. CCDC40 is evolutionarily conserved (39% identity and 60% similarity at the protein level) between zebrafish and humans and plays a crucial role in the development and function of cilia, making zebrafish an ideal vertebrate model organism for studying human biology.8 Pathogenic variants in CCDC40 lead to similar ciliary dysfunctions in both species, underscoring the gene’s critical role in ciliary motility across vertebrates (Figure SE4—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure).

Microinjection of mRNA at the one-cell stage is a widely used technique for functional gene analysis, often used to verify a loss-of-function phenotype by rescue,8,35 though it has been infrequently used to study mRNA-based therapies in monogenic diseases.36 At this stage, a single blastomere takes up the mRNA through cytoplasmic flow, subsequently undergoing rapid division, leading to ubiquitous expression in almost all embryonic cells. By applying this classical in vivo delivery route with human LNP-CCDC40-mRNA, we observed significantly improved ciliary motility in ccdc40−/− zebrafish, thus confirming the evolutionary conservation of CCDC40 function (Figures 6 and 7).

In humans, however, local administration (inhalation) to the respiratory tract, rather than systemic delivery, is anticipated. Thus, microinjection of LNP-mRNA into zebrafish embryos does not provide sufficient information on whether topical application can also rescue ciliary defects. To address this, we developed a novel experimental approach by bathing zebrafish larvae in LNP-mRNA-supplemented medium, targeting the olfactory pit. The olfactory pit contains motile multiciliated cells that generate a flow field around the nose through asymmetric, whip-like strokes.37 Moreover, the olfactory pit epithelium serves as a barrier to efficient uptake of drug candidates or LNP-mRNA into cells, resembling the challenge posed by the human respiratory epithelium.28,38 As a result, we anticipated a less robust rescue of ciliary defects compared to the one-cell stage embryo injection. Encouragingly, we observed a dose-dependent improvement in ciliary motility and directional flow in zebrafish olfactory pit following topical application of LNP-CCDC40-mRNA. These findings provide compelling evidence for the biological efficacy of this mRNA-based therapy for both systemic and topical applications. Because there is only a 39% identity of the orthologous human and fish CCDC40 proteins, possibly human CCDC40 has some limitations in completely rescuing the fish phenotype. Furthermore, it is conceivable that topical application of the zebrafish ccdc40 mRNA instead of the human CCDC40-mRNA might have resulted in an even greater improvement in ciliary motility in fish. However, we demonstrated that even a high dose of LNP-tdTomato-mRNA only transfected a small subset of multiciliated cells in the olfactory pits (Figure SE6—see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure) and thus it is likely that the partial rescue of LNP-CCDC40-mRNA probably results from a low transfection capacity of mRNA using this study design.

However, our data provide both structural and functional evidence for the biological efficacy of mRNA-based protein replacement therapy for CCDC40 in vitro and in vivo. However, over 50 genes are implicated in PCD,2 and a separate LNP-formulation must be established for each of these genes. CCDC40-mRNA is 4.2 kb in size. Here, we did not encounter challenges typically associated with the delivery of larger mRNA molecules. This suggests that our LNP-mRNA approach could also be extended to numerous other smaller genes implicated in PCD (eg, DNAAFs). However, the size of the mRNA of each gene should be considered, as larger mRNA molecules (eg, DNAH5, 15.8 kb; HYDIN, 21.0 kb) may present unique challenges related to encapsulation efficiency, stability, and delivery compared to smaller mRNA molecules.39

In summary, we provide proof-of-concept of this novel mRNA therapy’s efficacy. To support clinical transition, we utilized advanced mRNA and delivery technologies, addressing common challenges related to mRNA stability, immunogenicity, and delivery.15,40 Recently, mRNA therapy by inhalation has emerged as an alternative to treat cystic fibrosis, where only moderate reactions to the treatment were noted,41 making this approach now feasible for hereditary chronic airway disease. Thus, based on our results, first in vivo human studies (Phase 1 trial) are planned in individuals with pathogenic variants in CCDC40.
