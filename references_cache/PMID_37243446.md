Methylmalonic Acidemia (MMA) is a heterogenous group of inborn errors of 
metabolism caused by a defect in the methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (MMUT) enzyme or 
the synthesis and transport of its cofactor, 5'-deoxy-adenosylcobalamin. It is 
characterized by life-threatening episodes of ketoacidosis, chronic kidney 
disease, and other multiorgan complications. Liver transplantation can improve 
patient stability and survival and thus provides clinical and biochemical 
benchmarks for the development of hepatocyte-targeted genomic therapies. Data 
are presented from a US natural history protocol that evaluated subjects with 
different types of MMA including mut-type (N = 91), cblB-type (15), and 
cblA-type MMA (17), as well as from an Italian cohort of mut-type (N = 19) and 
cblB-type MMA (N = 2) subjects, including data before and after organ 
transplantation in both cohorts. Canonical metabolic markers, such as serum 
methylmalonic acid and propionylcarnitine, are variable and affected by dietary 
intake and renal function. We have therefore explored the use of the 1-13 
C-propionate oxidation breath test (POBT) to measure metabolic capacity and the 
changes in circulating proteins to assess mitochondrial dysfunction (fibroblast 
growth factor 21 [FGF21] and growth differentiation factor 15 [GDF15]) and 
kidney injury (lipocalin-2 [LCN2]). Biomarker concentrations are higher in 
patients with the severe mut0 -type and cblB-type MMA, correlate with a 
decreased POBT, and show a significant response postliver transplant. Additional 
circulating and imaging markers to assess disease burden are necessary to 
monitor disease progression. A combination of biomarkers reflecting disease 
severity and multisystem involvement will be needed to help stratify patients 
for clinical trials and assess the efficacy of new therapies for MMA.

Recent advances in basic, clinical and translational research on isolated methylmalonic acidemia (MMA)1, and specifically the more common and severe mut-type, caused by biallelic variants in MMUT (OMIM #251000), have allowed improved understanding of the molecular pathways involved in disease pathophysiology2–9 and the development of several small molecule8,10,11 and genomic therapies, including mRNA, nuclease-free AAV-mediated editing, and AAV gene addition12–16 that are being tested in phase I/II clinical trials as alternative approaches to organ transplantation. Liver transplantation is currently offered as an elective surgical therapeutic approach to increasingly younger age-groups, aiming at normalizing liver methylmalonyl-CoA mutase enzyme activity, providing metabolic stability, improving neurological outcome and/or delaying the onset of multisystemic complications of the disease17–23. On the other hand, kidney or liver-kidney transplantation are offered to patients with advanced kidney disease in need of renal replacement therapy depending on the degree of associated metabolic instability and disease severity. Isolated kidney transplant as an elective surgery does not provide sufficient enzymatic activity to alleviate disease burden and avoid progression for patients at the severe end of the disease spectrum24–26. Reports from large transplant centers on the outcomes of isolated liver or kidney and combined organ transplantations have been invaluable in providing benchmarks for the development of new therapeutics, especially liver-targeted approaches.

The design of early phase clinical trials is particularly challenging for rare disorders like MMA, where the molecular, biochemical, and clinical phenotype is heterogenous, the approach to dietary and pharmaceutical management variable, and morbidity and disease progression highly individualized. Over the past 5 decades, case reports and small patient series27–30 have been fortified by focused natural history studies (clinicaltrials.gov identifier: NCT00078078)31–35 and large international collaborative efforts (E-IMD)25,36–44 that together have been instrumental in documenting different aspects of the natural history of the disease, improving understanding of phenotype-genotype correlations8,45, identifying biomarkers reflecting disease severity and progression3,5,46–48, and developing informed consensus treatment guidelines in order to standardize the widely different management approaches implemented across various metabolic centers25,36,49–51. These are critical pre-requisites for assessing the efficacy of various therapies in current and future interventional trials.

Clinical trials for new drugs and therapeutics, especially beyond the dose finding/safety phase I studies, require clinical endpoints that reflect clinically meaningful change in how the patient feels, functions, or survives (FFS). Finding such endpoints is very challenging in slowly progressive diseases with highly individualized course, and especially difficult in rare diseases where large numbers cannot be employed to account for the high variability, if classic power analyses were to be utilized. Numerous adaptive trial designs (Bayesian approaches) and new regulatory avenues (for example in the US, sections 526–528 of the FD&C Act (21 U.S.C. 360aa-360ee): Orphan Drug Designation of 1983, Pediatric Research Equity Act, Best Pharmaceuticals for Children Act, Advancing the Development of Pediatric Therapeutics (ADEPT), among others) have been proposed to optimize the chance of demonstrating drug efficacy and safety in rare and ultra-rare/orphan pediatric diseases52–54. Collaborations involving global trial design and between international regulatory agencies are essential for pediatric rare disease drug development55–60, while natural history studies, use of external controls, multiple endpoint strategies, careful consideration of bridging biomarkers and intermediate clinical outcomes as surrogate endpoints, have been strongly encouraged.

A biomarker must undergo a stringent validation process by regulatory agencies to reach the evidentiary requirement to be used as a replacement/surrogate endpoint of providing a measure of how a patient feels, functions or survives (FFS) (see CDER Biomarker Qualification Program, per Section 507 of the FD&C Act (21 U.S.C. 357), section 3011(a) of the Cures Act (Public Law 114–255), the FDA-NIH Biomarkers, Endpoints and Other Tools (BEST) resource61, and similar procedures are utilized by the European Medicines Agency (EMA)62. Such clinical and analytical biomarker qualification is rarely achieved, and in most applications biomarkers are used as supportive measures in registrational trials or as secondary endpoints in early proof-of-concept trials. Inborn errors of metabolism (IEMs) are a unique group of disorders associated with a range of disease-specific biochemical abnormalities easily and accurately measured in samples that can be obtained with minimally-invasive procedures including blood, urine, saliva, and cerebrospinal fluid sampling. Biomarkers employed as surrogate endpoints that contributed to drug approval and licensure in IEMs include: plasma ammonia for the approval of Carglumic acid (Carbaglu) for methylmalonic and propionic acidemia; plasma phenylalanine for approval of sapropterin dihydrochloride (Kuvan) and Pegvaliase (Palynziq); urinary oxalate for primary hyperoxaluria type 1 for Lumasiran (Oxlumo), among others.

This review will present an overview of candidate surrogate biomarkers that might be employed in clinical trials for new therapies in methylmalonic acidemia (Figure 1), with the disclaimer that none of them yet satisfy the conditions for clinically or analytical validity of a qualified biomarker for regulatory purposes, as has been highlighted previously63. Data from a large US natural history study and an Italian patient cohort are summarized to support the correlation of circulating biomarkers with genotype and enzymatic activity, including the response to organ transplantation as a benchmark for liver-directed therapies. Insights into the clinical utility of non-canonical biomarkers to assess disease progression in MMA, and aid with the stratification and selection of severely affected patients for higher-risk interventions, are discussed.

The identification of the causative genes and characterization of the molecular landscape underlying each of the enzymatic defects associated with isolated MMA allowed improved understanding of the genotype-phenotype correlations45,64–69. Despite the significantly improved understanding of the underlying genetics, and the functional effects of variants in each of the causative genes (MMUT, MMAA, MMAB, MMADHC, MCEE), genotype-phenotype correlations remain challenging because of a) the large number of private mutations, b) compound heterozygosity and c) interallelic complementation 70. The use of protein expression, stability and functional enzymatic studies in primary patient cell lines (fibroblasts) will continue to help characterize the patients but are technically demanding, and have limited accessibility 1.

Diagnostic testing based on enzyme activity assays in vitro has historically relied upon fibroblast cultures derived from a patient’s skin biopsy to measure the fixation of radiolabeled 14C-propionate to cellular macromolecules in affected compared to control cell lines, with and without exposure to vitamin B12 in the culture media. The results of 14C-propionate incorporation were used to designate a patient as mut0 vs mut− subtype, based on a partial response to B12 in the latter. The diagnosis of additional biochemical defects in the synthesis or processing of 5’-deoxyadenosyl-cobalamin (cobalamin A, B, D defects) is achieved by the quantification of [57Co]-adenosyl and methylcobalamin from [57Co]-cyanocobalamin and somatic complementation studies with cell lines of known cobalamin biosynthesis defects71–75. These studies require laborious and highly specialized testing involving radiolabels and are currently only available in two expert centers world-wide, Dr Matthias Baumgartner’s lab at the University of Zurich, Switzerland, and Dr David S. Rosenblatt’s laboratory at McGill University Health Center, in Montreal, Canada. Although they have been largely replaced by molecular genetic testing for diagnostic purposes, they remain the gold-standard for functional characterization of novel genes or variants of uncertain significance in the loci causing isolated MMA.

From the first descriptions of methylmalonyl-CoA mutase deficiency as the cause of methylmalonic acidemia, it was evident that patients who show a biochemical response to daily parenteral administration of vitamin B12 had an improved prognosis76–79. The first extensive description of the natural history of isolated methylmalonic acidemia demonstrated that patients in the mut0 group had early age of symptom onset, mostly in the first week of life, showed no response to B12 supplementation, had high mortality (60% by 2 months of age), and suffered frequent hospitalizations, recurrent acidosis, growth restriction and cognitive impairment. Mut− and cblB patients had intermediate phenotypes, while B12-responsive cblA subjects had the best long-term prognosis31. Large patient registries have confirmed the earlier observations on the importance of the underlying genetic and enzymatic defect on disease mortality, morbidity and multisystem complications40,44,46 and have helped evaluate older and newer biomarkers against molecular, biochemical, and clinical severity scores5,8,39,47,51,80.

Multi-omics profiling of over 200 patient fibroblast lines allowed a powerful integrated analysis of whole genome and transcriptome sequencing, data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry proteotyping and metabolomics that enabled novel associations of various biomarkers with cell phenotypes and clinical severity scores8. Propionate incorporation (PI) as absolute values or ratio (+OHCbl/-OHCbl), and MUT enzymatic activity with and without adenosylcobalamin are robust assays of enzymatic in vitro function that, combined with molecular characterization and clinical severity scores allowed powerful associations between the different omics profiles that helped identify pathway disturbances in MMA, importantly TCA cycle anaplerosis as a novel therapeutic target for the disease8.

Given the strong association of disease severity and outcomes with enzymatic function, a simple, non-invasive, non-radioactive, stable isotope breath test (1-13C-propionate oxidation breath test, POBT) was developed to measure propionate oxidative capacity at the whole-body level51,81,82. The POBT using a single, small oral/enteral dose of the isotope provides a versatile estimate of in vivo propionate pathway activity, can be easily administered in the outpatient setting and in patients of all ages and cognitive abilities, theoretically even in newborns. Previous studies using intravenous 14C or 13C-propionate supported that in vivo propionate oxidation was a better predictor of clinical severity than in vitro enzymatic activity measurements, serum methylmalonic acid concentrations or urine methylmalonic acid excretion, while oral administration was comparable with the intravenous method82–84. Furthermore, observations in disease-specific knockout and transgenic murine models demonstrated a durable increase in 1-13C-propionate oxidation in response to liver-directed MMUT genomic therapies, even in the setting of an expanded circulating methylmalonic acid pool5,12,13,15. Critically, the murine observations were replicated in human studies that showed satisfactory inter- and intraperson repeatability of the single oral/enteral bolus POBT, both in control subjects and MMA patients of different subtypes, despite highly variable serum methylmalonic acid levels51. Other limitations intrinsic to oral isotopomer dosing, like variable gastric emptying, microbiome status, renal clearance, and bicarbonate kinetics in the TCA cycle, did not appear to influence the very strong biological signal of hepatic oxidation when MUT activity was present in the liver. After liver transplantation patients had completely normal 1-13C-propionate oxidation despite manifesting serum methylmalonic acid concentrations in the hundreds of uM range, close to the levels seen in patients before transplantation with preserved renal function. In addition, 1-13C-propionate oxidative capacity showed strong correlation with disease specific biomarkers and clinical outcomes, including growth parameters, bone density, neurocognitive function, and age of reaching chronic kidney disease stage 3, while it was restored to control values after liver transplantation51.

The POBT test can clearly distinguish severe mut0 and cblB subjects from those with cblA, mut− and normal controls, and may help select the most severely affected subjects for clinical trials or earlier referral to liver transplantation, especially in patients when the genotype is not predictive (e.g. absence of two nonsense variants or known severe pathogenic variants and absence of in vitro activity studies) or the clinical phenotype is confounded (e.g. patient is young, and has infrequent metabolic crises, and no advanced multiorgan complications). It can also be easily employed for repeat measures over time as a pharmacodynamic response biomarker in studies aiming to restore hepatic MMUT function, especially novel genomic therapies, like mRNA, systemic gene therapy, gene editing and/or cell therapy trials51 to assess the degree of enzymatic correction achieved and possibly, the sustainability of the response. This could be particularly valuable in gene therapy trials, in the event of immunological reactions to the AAV vector (cytotoxic T-cell immunity against AAV vector components) or transgene in CRIM (cross reactive immunologic material/MMUT protein)- negative subjects (two null or deleterious variants resulting in absence of MMUT protein) where a CTL response against the transduced cells can cause hepatic inflammation that may result in loss of activity. Being able to assess hepatic MMUT enzymatic activity at the bedside in real time using a POBT assay may help guide immunosuppressive treatment dose and duration.

Methylmalonic acid is the pathognomonic organic acid accumulating in bodily fluids (blood, CSF, urine) in all forms of MMA, due to the enzymatic defect in the methylmalonyl-CoA mutase reaction that catalyzes the isomerization of L-methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA in the propionate oxidation pathway. It was shown to derive from the D-methylmalonyl-CoA isomer through a hydrolase reaction (KEGG: EC 3.1.2.17) with no associated gene identified to date85,86. Urine organic acid analysis was historically the test that allowed the identification of various organic acidemias, including MMA, and is more readily available in clinics around the world-wide27,44. Spot urine methylmalonic acid concentrations are highly variable and rarely used to guide dietary prescription and long-term patient monitoring87 but methylmalonic acid 24hour urine output (measured in millimoles of MMA in the urine/kg body weight/day) can be a better measure of whole-body propionate metabolism. The kinetics of methylmalonic acid was demonstrated in elegant clinical studies estimating individual patient protein requirements based on serial nitrogen/propiogenic precursor retention and MMA and urea excretion measurements87,88. Nevertheless, it is rarely utilized because it requires complete 24-hour urine collection, which can be cumbersome in the setting of clinical trials or home monitoring and difficult in incontinent and cognitively impaired individuals.

Studies in liver vs. muscle-MMUT expressing transgenic murine models and mice treated with various liver-targeted or constitutive genomic therapies, combined with observations in patients who received liver as opposed to kidney-only transplant procedures, provide important insights about the serum MMA (sMMA) concentrations, renal excretion, tissue distribution and compartmentalization, or toxic effects3,5,17–19,89,90. Although primarily affected by the severity of the enzymatic defect and response to B12 supplementation, sMMA concentration also reflects the everyday balance between the propionate influx (from dietary protein, propiogenic bacteria in the gastrointestinal tract or proteolysis during an acute metabolic crisis), and renal clearance. Patients with severe enzymatic defects (mut0 and cblB) patients have the highest concentrations of sMMA as opposed to patients with more MMUT enzymatic activity or B12 responsive forms of the disorder. 1 (Figure 2A and 3A for the NIH and OPBG patient cohorts, respectively). Baseline sMMA concentrations appear to increase with age, with lower values observed in the rapidly growing infants until 2–3 years of age (100–300μmol/L), which then slowly increase in later childhood and adolescence when growth is slower and renal disease progresses to chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage 2–3 93 (Supplemental Figure 1A, C and E, G).

Decreased renal filtration directly influences sMMA concentrations, with mut0 patients typically exceeding 1mM levels after kidney function declines below an estimated GFR (glomerular filtration rate) of 50%34,48,51. When reaching advanced stages of CKD3–4, sMMA typically exceed 3mM and cannot be managed with lowering protein intake in the diet. CKD stage is therefore a major factor affecting the utility of sMMA as a surrogate biomarker in treatment trials for MMA. The ability for therapeutic interventions to lower sMMA concentrations can be limited by decreased clearance by the kidneys, unless the kidneys are specifically targeted by the treatment. This has been clearly illustrated in the transplant literature, with maximum MMA reduction documented in combined liver-kidney transplant recipients, but not in isolated liver or kidney only transplant cohorts17–19,22,26. Even in liver and kidney transplant recipients, sMMA remains in the 150–500 μmol/L range due to generation of MMA in extrahepatic organs, mainly in the skeletal muscle 89. Mean concentrations of sMMA in patients with the mut0 subgroup, per transplant type and before/after transplantation in patients enrolled in the NIH natural history study are provided in Figures 4A and 5A, while data from the OPBG patient series are depicted in Figure 5G–I.

Furthermore, circulating methylmalonic acid is not an accurate reflection of the concentrations in other tissue compartments, especially that of the CNS27,91, as has been illustrated in a small number of careful clinical studies reporting simultaneous serum and CSF MMA measurements before and after liver/kidney transplantation23,90,92. Higher concentrations in the CSF, with CSF/serum MMA ratio of 3 to 8-fold, have been demonstrated in liver transplant recipients, especially during episodes of acute neurological complications (metabolic stroke, seizures, white matter/pons injury) post-transplant (900–2,000 μmol/L), despite low circulating sMMA (130–400 μmol/L), supporting a de novo production of toxic metabolites in the brain compartment despite the LT or LKT procedure23,90,92.

These are also disease specific metabolites formed by the condensation of propionyl-CoA with oxaloacetic acid in the TCA cycle via the action of citrate synthase. They accumulate in both methylmalonic and propionic acidemia, where higher concentrations are typically observed48,93. This makes them attractive candidates for drug development targeting both disorders and simultaneous determination of 2MCA, 3OHPA, MMA absolute values or ratios of 2MCA/citrate have been proposed as biomarkers for diagnostic and monitoring purposes in both disorders of propionyl-CoA metabolism94–98. Testing for either is not as readily available as sMMA, since fewer laboratories offer testing outside as part of a second-tier test during newborn screening. Furthermore, their dynamic concentration range is much lower compared to sMMA, which makes it more challenging to reach statistical significance for smaller degree changes, unless many measurements are performed. Similar to sMMA, they are strongly correlated with dietary protein intake and renal clearance, while 2-MCA was shown to predict disease burden and the impact of liver transplantation in MMA similar to FGF2148.

Mean concentrations of sMCA in patients with different MMA subgroups and in the mut0 subgroup before/after transplantation in the OPBG series are provided in Figure 5,panels J and K.

Elevated plasma concentration of propionylcarnitine (C3) and variable elevations in C4-dicarboxylic or methylmalonic/succinylcarnitine (C4DC), and the ratios of C3/C2 or C0, C16, glycine or methionine, on a plasma acylcarnitine profile measured by tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS), form the basis of newborn screening for MMA. First tier newborn screening can be followed by serum MMA, 2-MCA and total homocysteine measurements in the dried blood spot, as mentioned earlier94,99 to differentiate isolated MMA from propionic acidemia and defects in the cobalamin biosynthesis pathways causing combined methylmalonic acidemia and homocystinuria, as well as to reduce the high false positive C3 detection rates.

After the original diagnosis, acylcarnitine profiles have limited utility in the day-to-day monitoring and treatment of methylmalonic acidemia patients, since patients receive moderate to high-dose carnitine supplementation causing elevations in all carnitine species, while renal disease also impacts their renal clearance. The ratio of esterified to free carnitine remains a valuable biomarker (Figure 2B, 4B and 5B), likely reflecting the acetyl-species burden/CoA trapping that results in the post-translational modifications and secondary mitochondrial dysfunction that are integral parts of disease pathophysiology7,100–102. Acyl/free carnitine ratio shows a strong correlation with enzymatic activity measured by the POBT test51 and can be monitored to assess the functional or relative carnitine deficiency and guide supplementation and dietary monitoring in the management of MMA patients and response to liver transplantation or other therapies103.

Hyperglycinemia is a cardinal feature of MMA and PA, that led to their original description as ketotic hyperglycinemias104–107, with a decrease in the activity of H-protein in the glycine cleavage system measured in liver tissues of both MMA and PA patients108. Observations in propionic acidemia patients showed normal CSF/plasma glycine concentration ratios suggesting a secondary inhibition of the hepatic, but not brain glycine cleavage system109. The exact mechanism leading to elevations in glycine in MMA was not well understood until the recent demonstration that non-enzymatic methylmalonylation blocks the lipoylation of protein H of the mitochondrial glycine cleavage system (GCSH) that is necessary for GCSH function7. Glycine concentrations are not particularly helpful for clinical monitoring of MMA patients other than as a marker of poor dietary caloric intake110, despite being significantly decreased after liver transplant, as previously reported19,103. We have observed weak correlations of plasma glycine and FGF215, dietary caloric deficits and bone marrow suppression (low white cell count, unpublished observations) in MMA subjects, but they will need to be confirmed in larger cohorts. In PA, glycine concentrations show a poor correlation with markers of acute metabolic decompensation, like ammonia111, and are not ranked highly among disease biomarkers correlating with disease severity when assessed using a machine learning modeling81. However, there remains an intriguing positive correlation between increased glycine and sodium bicarbonate/intact acid-base balance112. Glycine concentrations per disease subtype and organ transplant status are provided in Figure 2C, 4C and 5C for NIH cohort and 3C for OPBG cohort).

Studies on mitochondrial myopathies and the mitochondrial integrated stress response have revealed FGF21 and GDF15 as important mediators of respiratory chain dysfunction associated with mtDNA maintenance or translation, as well as OXPHOS defects113,114. FGF21 is a systemic peptide hormone regulating whole body adaptation to multiple challenges including nutrient restriction, cold exposure, carbohydrate intake and stress. It is synthesized in several organs, including the liver, white adipose tissue, pancreas and has pleiotropic metabolic effects (autocrine, paracrine and endocrine functions). The potent antihyperglycemic, antihyperlipidemic, and thermogenic properties of FGF21 have made its signaling a promising anti-obesity target with a broad range of therapeutic agents in development115. GDF15 is a member of the transforming growth factor β (TGFβ) superfamily and can be activated by the tumor suppressor p53 or master regulator ATF4 in response to mitochondrial/oxidative stress, DNA damage, and metabolic stress. It is expressed in nearly all tissues and has key roles in cardiovascular disease, cancer, obesity, renal and mitochondrial diseases116–118.

FGF21 and GDF15 have been proposed as diagnostic and prognostic markers in patients with a broad spectrum of mitochondrial disorders, with FGF21 performing better at differentiating mitochondrial from other neuromuscular disorders than other traditional markers of mitochondrial disease, like lactate, pyruvate and lactate/pyruvate ratio, alanine, and creatine kinase. Both have been introduced in diagnostic pipelines to guide decisions to proceed with a muscle biopsy119–121. Recent data in pediatric mitochondrial disease cohorts suggested FGF21 outperformed GDF15 as a diagnostic marker114.

Studies in clinical cohorts and murine models of MMA have established FGF21 as a significantly dysregulated marker of hepatic mitochondrial dysfunction in isolated methylmalonic acidemia5,47,51. Concentrations correlate with enzymatic subgroup and disease severity (Figure 2D–E, and 3D–E), increase with age and the accumulation of complications from different organ systems attributed to mitochondrial dysfunction (renal failure, optic neuropathy, cardiomyopathy), and show a significant response to liver-targeted therapies in mice and liver transplantation in patients (Figure 4D, E and 5D, E for the NIH, and 5L for OPBG cohort). Furthermore, concentrations show a positive correlation with oxidative stress markers (plasma alanine, glycine and urine F2-isoprostanes), anti-correlate with essential amino acid deficiencies (valine, methionine), and growth parameters (height and OFC z-scores), and show a weak correlation to renal markers and glomerular filtration rate compared to serum MMA, 2MC, C3 (Supplemental figure 1 B, D and E, F)5,47,48.

Obtaining periodic assessments especially of FGF21 concentrations in the well state in MMA patients can help guide the selection of patients for liver vs kidney transplantation in patients with intermediate MMUT genotype/phenotype severity and might be used to stratify patients for clinical trial design. Moreover, given the significantly increased dynamic range of FGF21 and weaker dependence on renal filtration compared to canonical metabolites, it can be utilized to monitor short term efficacy in liver-directed genomic therapy trials, as has been demonstrated in preclinical AAV gene therapy studies and in transplanted MMA patients.

All individuals with isolated MMA are at risk for chronic kidney disease and progressive renal failure, including the B12 responsive cblA subjects, and those who received an elective liver transplant. Age-appropriate equations to calculate estimated glomerular filtration rate (GFR) by creatinine, BUN and height for “bedside Schwartz”122 and cystatin-C concentrations for the CKiD (Chronic kidney disease in children) study equations123,124, and, preferably, actually measurement of GFR by iohexol clearance or other methods –should be employed for clinical monitoring starting in early childhood125,126. Monitoring cystatin-C concentrations regularly is important, since creatinine is a late marker of renal dysfunction in individuals with low muscle mass, a frequent finding especially in patients of the severe mut0 or cblB subtypes (Figure 2G, 4G, and 5F), due to protein restriction, chronic renal disease and low levels of physical activity. In addition to serum markers, renal ultrasound and bone mineral density (DEXA) should be performed periodically to monitor kidney growth and bone health34,35,51. Referral to nephrology for early implementation of renoprotective measures, including monitoring and treatment of chronic acidosis, hyperuricemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism/ osteopenia, and hypertension, in addition to early planning of transplant options, is strongly recommended (Figure 6)1.

The relationship of creatinine concentration to skeletal muscle mass is relevant for clinical trial design, since it is expected to increase with improvements in body composition and growth achieved with genomic or other experimental therapies. This can be interpreted as renal function decline unless additional measures, such as cystatin-C-based or iohexol GFR, are assessed. This has been discussed widely in the literature in the setting of the effects of growth hormone in children with cystinosis127,128 and chronic kidney disease in general129,130.

Studies in MMA murine models and patients showed strong correlations of another kidney injury marker, lipocalin-2 (LCN2) (Figure 2H and 4H), with markers of oxidative stress and proximal tubular mitochondrial dysfunction3. In the murine model, Lcn2 was an early and more sensitive marker compared to creatinine or serum MMA concentrations to assess the response to the antioxidant therapy, suggesting LCN2 as an additional biomarker to track renal injury in MMA clinical trials.

Hepatomegaly and liver function test elevations are common in patients with severe forms of isolated MMA, especially during acute metabolic crises due to viral illnesses, and often associated with hyperammonemia. Liver ultrasound often reveals hyperechoic liver due to steatosis, which may be accentuated by a diet with a high percent of calories from carbohydrates and fat32. Liver pathology and electron microscopy show lipidosis, and abnormal mitochondrial ultrastructure, which is associated with decreased cytochrome c oxidase activity5. Patients have not been documented to develop chronic liver failure with jaundice, portal hypertension, edema, and decreased synthetic capacity of the liver. Nevertheless, chronic liver disease with progression to cirrhosis, fibrosis and liver cancer is very rare42,43,131,132. Given these case reports, it is prudent to monitor for chronic liver disease by measuring AST, ALT, GGT and alpha-fetoprotein concentrations (AFP).25,43

Monitoring of additional organ systems is an integral part of patient care for IEMs with multisystemic complications, including cardiac, optic nerve, hearing, CNS (Figure 6), but will not be covered in more detail in this review that is focused on circulating biomarkers.

A combination of circulating and urine biomarkers along with imaging and clinical assessments reflecting disease severity and multisystem involvement will be necessary to help select candidate patients and serve as surrogate pharmacodynamic endpoints in clinical trials of genomic or small molecule therapies for isolated MMA. Long term follow-up will be necessary to establish if a novel intervention affects how an MMA patient feels, functions, or survives, for example, to demonstrate metabolic stability (fewer admissions for metabolic decompensation with keto, lactic acidosis/hyperammonemia), improved protein tolerance and growth/bone mineralization, stabilization of kidney, liver, cardiac function, and better cognitive outcomes if implemented in early infancy before multiorgan disease complications accumulate.
