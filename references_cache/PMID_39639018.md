Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) is an obsessive-compulsive disorder-related 
psychiatric condition characterized by an intense preoccupation with perceived 
physical flaws that are not observable by others. BDD affects ~2% of the adult 
population but is underdiagnosed, partly owing to limited clinician awareness, 
and undertreated, partly due to limited access to treatment. Research on the 
aetiology of BDD is scarce but likely involves an interplay between genetic and 
environmental factors. A few studies suggest functional and structural brain 
differences (compared with controls) in the regions involved in visual and 
emotional processing, although firm conclusions about the pathophysiology of the 
disorder cannot be made at this stage. Diagnosis requires the presence of 
repetitive behaviours or mental acts typically aimed at checking, correcting or 
concealing perceived flaws. The disorder typically has its onset before 18 years 
of age, with a female preponderance in youth but no major gender disparity in 
adults. Quality of life is markedly impaired across multiple domains and suicide 
risk is considerable. Evidence-based treatments include cognitive behavioural 
therapy and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors. Future research should 
focus on understanding the biological and environmental factors that increase 
the risk of BDD, and on improving access to effective treatments, thereby 
addressing a critical gap in care for this often misunderstood and overlooked 
disorder.

[H1] Introduction 40 
Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) is a psychiatric condition characterized by a persistent 41 
preoccupation with perceived defects in physical appearance, often fixed on facial features 42 
but can involve any part of one’s appearance 1. Individuals with BDD may spend several 43 
hours daily fixating on these perceived flaws, leading to substantial distress and impairment 44 
across multiple domains, including social, educational and occupational functioning 2.  45 
Many people with BDD experience their perceived flaws as real and often seek 46 
correction, for example, through cosmetic procedures, and may not consult a mental health 47 
professional. Since the flaws are not observable to others, people around them may confuse 48 
their intense preoccupation with vanity, further contributing to the psychosocial burden of the 49 
disorder. In addition, BDD is associated with a 3-fold increased risk of death by suicide and 50 
intentional self-harm 3. 51 
Although BDD was first described in the late 1800s, BDD is a relatively new addition 52 
to the diagnostic nomenclature. The label ‘body dysmorphic disorder’ was introduced as a 53 
diagnosis in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 3rd edition (DSM III-54 
R) in 1987 4 and since 2018, BDD was also included as a separate diagnostic entity in the 55 
WHO’s eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) 5. BDD has 56 
many similarities with obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) 6 and, for this reason, BDD is 57 
classified as an OCD-related disorder in both the DSM-5 and the ICD-11 5,7. In specialist 58 
care, patients are often seen by clinicians specializing in OCD-related disorders as 59 
psychological and pharmacological treatment options for BDD are similar to those used for 60 
OCD.  61 
The lack of awareness of BDD and its management among clinicians have contributed 62 
to BDD being often misunderstood, misdiagnosed or underdiagnosed, which is particularly 63 
concerning as BDD typically follows a chronic course if left untreated 8. The neural and 64 

4 
 
molecular mechanisms involved in BDD are poorly understood and much remains to be 65 
delineated. The recommended evidence-based treatments for BDD are cognitive behavioural 66 
therapy (CBT) and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs). However, access to CBT 67 
is limited; barriers to CBT access include lack of trained therapists and the costs associated 68 
with treatment 9,10. Despite its prevalence and severity, BDD is an often-overlooked disorder 69 
in both clinical and public health contexts. Further research, for example, on neurocognition 70 
or on emotional processing is needed to understand its aetiology. In addition, larger clinical 71 
trials, as well as research on understudied populations, for example, in low and middle-72 
income countries should be prioritized to bridge the treatment access gap by enabling digital 73 
treatment options 1.  74 
In this Primer, we review the epidemiology, risk factors, current knowledge on 75 
pathophysiology, diagnosis, and the management of individuals with BDD. In addition, we 76 
highlight the effect of BDD on patient quality of life and discuss future directions in BDD 77 
research.  78

[H1] Epidemiology  80 
[H2] Prevalence  81 
The weighted prevalence of BDD in community-based samples of adults (>18 years 82 
of age) is ~2% in high-income countries 11. Studies have estimated that approximately two 83 
thirds of individuals have an onset of BDD before 18 years of age12. The prevalence of BDD 84 
may be rare (0.1 %) in children <12 years of age, but the prevalence increases substantially 85 
during adolescence (1.9%) 13. BDD is more prevalent in adolescent girls (3.4%) than in 86 
adolescent boys (0.4%) 13; however, the reasons for such discrepancies are unknown. The 87 
differences in prevalence might reduce in early adulthood; for instance, in adult samples, the 88 
prevalence is similar or only slightly higher in women than in men 11,14,15. The prevalence of 89 

5 
 
BDD is highest in clinical settings; for example, a meta-analysis found the weighted 90 
prevalence of BDD to be ~7% in inpatient psychiatric clinics, 13% in general cosmetic 91 
surgery settings and 11% in dermatology clinics 11. 92 
Most of the prevalence studies to date have been conducted in high-income countries, 93 
so little is known regarding the prevalence of BDD in other parts of the world 11. Although 94 
few transcultural studies have been undertaken, the clinical phenomenology of BDD seems to 95 
be similar across cultures 6. To our knowledge, no long-term data on the natural course of 96 
BDD from untreated individuals exists, but the expert clinical consensus is that the disorder is 97 
likely to follow a chronic course if left untreated 8. 98

[H2] Comorbidities and consequences 100 
In clinical settings, most individuals with BDD present with comorbidities that further 101 
contribute to a diminished quality of life (Figure 1). In adult clinical samples, common 102 
comorbidities of BDD include major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders (such as, social 103 
anxiety disorder), OCD, eating disorders and substance use or substance dependence disorder 104 
9,16,17. Studies have described similar comorbidity patterns in clinical samples of children and 105 
adolescents 18,19. Neurodevelopmental disorders (for example, autism and attention-106 
deficit/hyperactivity disorder) are more commonly diagnosed in young people with BDD 107 
than in adults with BDD19, as these disorders are probably rarely formally assessed in adult 108 
services. The proportion of individuals with BDD with psychiatric comorbidities tends to be 109 
less in epidemiological (that is, non-clinical) samples 13,20.  110 
Knowledge of medical (that is, non-psychiatric) comorbidities in individuals with 111 
BDD is limited. Data on common conditions, such as metabolic or cardiovascular diseases, 112 
are lacking. The risk of all-cause and cause-specific mortality has not been formally 113 
investigated, except for suicide risk, which is thought to be considerable. Estimates of 114 

6 
 
mortality vary, but studies show that between 10 and 35% of individuals with BDD have 115 
attempted suicide at least once 19,21,22. Despite these findings, data on deaths by suicide in 116 
relation to BDD are very scarce 23–25. A Swedish population-based study including 2,833 117 
individuals with BDD found that the risk of death by suicide was >3 times higher among 118 
individuals with BDD than among matched unaffected individuals (hazard ratio=3.47; 95% 119 
CI, 1.76–6.85) 3, which is in line with risk estimates reported from studies investigating other 120 
psychiatric disorders in the OCD spectrum 26–28.  121

[H2] Risk factors  123 
BDD is thought to arise as a result of a complex interplay between genetic liability 124 
and environmental stressors. A handful of clinic-based studies suggest that BDD likely runs 125 
in families 29,30. Total population, register-based family studies are yet to be conducted. Twin 126 
studies of individuals self-reporting symptoms have estimated that the heritability of body 127 
dysmorphic concerns ranges between 37 and 49% (Table 1). Child-specific (that is, non-128 
shared) environmental factors, which could include experiences such as peer victimization, 129 
may explain the remaining variance. Environmental factors shared by twins growing up in the 130 
same family (for example, parental socioeconomic status) do not seem to contribute to the 131 
liability of dysmorphic concerns. Clinical experience suggests that this information could be 132 
reassuring to patients as they may occasionally attribute their (or their children’s) BDD to bad 133 
parenting. As it is possible that heritability estimates based on self-report data might differ 134 
from estimates derived from clinician diagnoses, these findings await confirmation in 135 
extended family pedigrees of clinically diagnosed individuals with BDD and genomic 136 
studies. 137 
To date, no genome-wide association studies of BDD diagnoses or symptoms have 138 
been conducted and therefore, the specific genetic variants that may underpin BDD 139 

7 
 
symptoms remain unknown. Several putative environmental risk factors have been suggested, 140 
including peer victimization (bullying) and childhood trauma (for example, abuse and 141 
neglect) 31. However, studies to date have major limitations, including the use of cross-142 
sectional designs and failure to control for familial or genetic confounding. The latter is 143 
crucial as individuals with a genetic susceptibility to BDD may also be more vulnerable to 144 
experiencing various adverse life events (for example, gene-environment correlations) 32. 145 
Prospective and genetically informative studies are needed to determine if adverse life-events 146 
and other environmental factors are causally associated with BDD.  147

[H1] Mechanisms/pathophysiology  149 
Studies on the pathophysiology of BDD provide early evidence of potential 150 
mechanisms underlying symptom formation and maintenance, although these findings are yet 151 
not conclusive. These studies, conducted thus far in adults, include those examining 152 
cognitive-affective dysfunction, neurocognition, eye gaze behaviours and brain structure and 153 
function. Mechanistically, disturbances in emotional face processing and attentional biases, 154 
alterations in executive functioning, visual perception and visual-emotional information 155 
processing may partly or collectively contribute to BDD symptoms.  156 
 157 
[H2] Cognitive-affective dysfunction  158 
Cognitive and behavioural theories have provided potential explanations for the development 159 
and maintenance of BDD symptoms 33–35. These models describe negative cognitive 160 
appraisals of aspects of one's appearance and repeated thoughts and rumination 161 
(preoccupations), which influence a person's emotions and mood, leading to behaviours such 162 
as repeatedly checking their appearance, attempting to alter or conceal the perceived flaw, or 163 
avoiding circumstances where the perceived flaw might be noticed. Furthermore, the theories 164 

8 
 
incorporate evidence for disturbances in visual perception and heightened allocation of 165 
attention to misperceived flaws (see below). Thus, these theories conceptualise how 166 
individual interpretation of experiences, thought patterns and sense of self, shape emotions 167 
and behaviour, and how visual perceptual and attentional biases towards misperceived 168 
appearance features or minor imperfections also influence their emotions and behaviours36.  169 
Research on neurocognition and emotional processing in BDD has uncovered 170 
potential deficits, which could influence symptom onset and persistence. For instance, studies 171 
using emotional facial stimuli found that individuals with BDD misinterpret others’ neutral 172 
faces as angry, and angry faces as disgusted compared with healthy controls 37. Furthermore, 173 
those with BDD show attentional biases to faces with disgust expressions, specifically 174 
manifesting as interference with how fast they respond during a task involving viewing 175 
neutral or disgust expression faces, compared with healthy controls 38. Moreover, individuals 176 
with BDD were also less accurate in recognizing angry, sad and neutral expressions, and 177 
slower in recognizing angry, happy, and neutral expressions 39 than healthy controls, and 178 
struggled to match the same faces across different emotions 40. Individuals with BDD might 179 
have difficulties in identifying emotions correctly, or even recognizing the identity of the 180 
same face with different emotional expressions, owing to different eye gaze patterns that 181 
might cause impairment in visual information processing, compared with healthy controls 182 
and those with obsessive–compulsive symptoms but not BDD. These impairments that may 183 
affect the accuracy of emotion identification in others’ faces include reduced visual attention 184 
to specific parts of the face that convey emotions, reduced number of fixations, increased 185 
blinking, and greater distance travelled by the eye during scanning than healthy controls 186 
(increased distance during eye scanning was also observed in individuals with OCD 187 
compared with healthy controls)41.  188 

9 
 
Other studies using eye-tracking have found attentional biases in individuals with 189 
BDD. Examples of such biases includes increased focus towards own unattractive features 42 190 
and ‘imagined’ (perceived) defects 43 and focus away from attractive features compared with 191 
healthy controls 44. In addition, they show extensive scanpaths, fewer fixations and reduced 192 
dwell time for their face, regardless of having a primary face concern, compared with healthy 193 
controls 45. These findings may have treatment implications. For example, situations in which 194 
an individual avoids viewing certain areas of concern, or engages in excessive fixation, could 195 
be targeted for remediation of gaze patterns to reduce negative biases. 196 
Neurocognitive batteries (a series of tests that assess cognitive functioning in a variety 197 
of domains) have suggested (although with inconsistencies) impaired executive functioning 198 
including planning, inhibition, and organization 46,47 compared with healthy controls. In 199 
addition, those with BDD may exhibit impaired decision-making, response inhibition and 200 
motor impulsivity 48 compared with healthy controls. Furthermore, individuals with BDD 201 
show deficits in set-shifting (ability to effectively and efficiently shift attention from one task 202 
or stimulus to another) 49, including less flexible shifting between global and local visual 203 
stimuli 50 compared with healthy controls. Studies have identified deficits relating to visual 204 
working memory, verbal auditory and logical memory, and verbal memory (although 205 
inconsistently), in individuals with BDD and in individuals with OCD compared with healthy 206 
controls, although with generally poorer performance in those with BDD than healthy 207 
controls versus performance in those with OCD compared with healthy controls. 51 52. Those 208 
with BDD also showed poorer performance on visuospatial tasks and impaired attention than 209 
healthy controls 51,52.  210 
Studies have uncovered poor visuospatial organization strategies in individuals with 211 
BDD using the Rey Osterrieth Complex Figure task 49,53 (except see 51) compared with 212 
healthy controls. This task tests one’s ability to visually perceive, reproduce and remember 213 

10 
 
visual details of a complex geometric figure, as well as one’s visuospatial organizational 214 
skills related to this. Individuals with BDD reproduced details more than the overall structural 215 
features, suggesting an imbalance in processing global versus local visual information. To 216 
further understand global and local information processing in BDD, several studies have 217 
employed the face inversion effect task, which requires participants to identify and 218 
differentiate faces that are either upright or upside-down (inverted). Identifying inverted faces 219 
is a more difficult and slower process than identifying upright images as the brain must 220 
engage in detailed rather than holistic processing. The ‘inversion effect’ quantifies this 221 
difference in accuracy or speed in identifying inverted versus upright faces. Several studies 222 
have demonstrated a diminished inversion effect in individuals with BDD compared with 223 
healthy controls 54–56 (except see 57), and individuals with OCD 54, suggesting a heightened 224 
engagement in detail processing (Figure 2). This propensity may be connected to specific 225 
attentional biases and patterns of gaze behaviours.  226 
Although most studies in BDD have been conducted in comparison only with healthy 227 
controls, the studies on visual attention and neurocognition that have also included 228 
individuals with OCD show some evidence of both overlapping and specific deficits 41,51,52,54. 229 
This suggests that the two disorders share some, but not all, patterns of information 230 
processing, which supports conceptualization of BDD and OCD as related but distinct 231 
disorders.  232 
Although concerns and preoccupations related to facial features and head appearance 233 
are common in those with BDD, those involving the body are also highly prevalent 25,58. Most 234 
research studies to date, as described above, have examined face processing or tasks related 235 
to face processing. However, a study involving body processing found that those with BDD 236 
mis-estimated certain body part sizes, and showed greater dissatisfaction for most body parts, 237 

11 
 
than healthy controls 59. This finding suggests the possibility of both cognitive and affective 238 
as well as perceptual components of body image disturbance.  239

[H2] Neural systems  241 
[H3] Structural findings. Whether BDD is associated with different brain structure in 242 
comparison to healthy controls is still undetermined. Most studies had small sample sizes, 243 
and the results have generally been inconsistent or have not been replicated.  244 
Neuroimaging brain morphology studies in BDD have focused on gray matter 245 
volumes and cortical thickness. Early studies showed differences in caudate symmetry in 246 
women with BDD (n=8) compared with controls (n=8) 60 and reduced gray matter in the 247 
anterior cingulate cortex and orbitofrontal cortex in men with BDD (n=12) compared with 248 
controls (n=12) 61. A subsequent study similarly found smaller right orbitofrontal cortex and 249 
left anterior cingulate cortex in men and women with BDD (n=20) compared with healthy 250 
controls (n=20) 62. Another study (n=20 BDD; n=20 controls) found thinner cortex in middle 251 
temporal and inferior parietal regions 63, although these anomalies were not observed in two 252 
studies involving unmedicated participants with BDD (n=12 BDD and n=12 controls; n=49 253 
BDD and n=44 controls) , suggesting the possibility of medication effects on brain 254 
morphology. Yet another study in unmedicated individuals with BDD (n=65 BDD; n=68 255 
controls) found enlarged cytoarchitectonically-defined visual cortical regions, which were 256 
previously found to show abnormal activity when processing faces, bodies and houses 64. 257 
Overall, discrepant brain morphology findings across studies may have been due to gender 258 
differences, medication status, and/or sample sizes.  259 
Studies have also examined white matter structure in BDD and found increased white 260 
matter volume in men and women with BDD compared with controls 60, 61. This finding 261 
could reflect a greater volume of myelin and/or a higher proportion of neurons or glia, which 262 

12 
 
might occur, for example, as a result of disruptions in neurodevelopment. An early diffusion 263 
imaging study found a link between poor insight (degree that one is convinced that their 264 
belief, for example, that a body part is deformed, is accurate) in BDD and decreased white 265 
matter integrity  in visual-emotional pathways 65, whereas another study found reduced white 266 
matter integrity across many brain regions66. A white matter network analysis indicated 267 
denser local connections and abnormal visual-emotional connectivity in BDD than controls, 268 
as well as symptom severity associations with global efficiency 67, although no differences in 269 
brain modularity 68. These white matter analyses provide early evidence to suggest 270 
irregularities in visual-emotional information transfer in individuals with BDD. 271

[H3] Functional findings. Much remains to be learned regarding differences in brain 273 
function in individuals with BDD compared with healthy controls, as most studies to date 274 
have had small sample sizes and most findings have not been attempted to be replicated. 275 
However, several studies have suggested altered brain activity when viewing self or others’ 276 
appearance and objects in individuals with BDD compared with healthy controls 69–77. The 277 
first functional MRI (fMRI) study conducted in BDD used spatial frequency-filtered stimuli 278 
to examine facial processing. Those with BDD showed increased left hemisphere activation 279 
for low-detail images, suggesting a bias towards detailed holistic processing69. Consistent 280 
with this finding, subsequent studies observed reduced activity in the early extrastriate cortex, 281 
specifically, regions associated with processing visual patterns, orientation, colour, shapes, 282 
object representation and visual attention 78,79 for low-detail images of own or others' faces 70 283 
and non-appearance-related objects (such as, houses) 71,72. Furthermore, while viewing their 284 
own faces, those with worse BDD symptoms had lower effective connectivity in the dorsal 285 
visual stream than healthy controls73, a tendency for the same was replicated in a separate 286 
cohort 74. Individuals with BDD also displayed reduced activation in dorsal visual 80 and 287 

13 
 
parietal networks while viewing others’ bodies, patterns that were similar although not 288 
identical to those with anorexia nervosa 75, suggesting partially shared disturbances across 289 
these two disorders in visual processing of bodies that may underlie distortions of perception 290 
of appearance. Thus, several studies have converged to point to abnormal functional 291 
activation and connectivity when processing holistic visual elements of self and others’ 292 
appearance and objects in individuals with BDD. Reduced brain activation and connectivity 293 
in these regions and systems could be linked with reduced ability to integrate visual details 294 
into a whole, which could result in visual perceptual distortions. 295 
Neuroimaging research has also shown different emotion-related neural responses in 296 
individuals with BDD compared with controls, although this is a nascent area of research. 297 
One study found reduced connectivity from the amygdala to medial prefrontal cortex when 298 
self-labelling their emotions while viewing fearful faces, which could indicate impaired 299 
communication between these regions to facilitate modulation of emotions 81. Another study 300 
observed increased connectivity from amygdala and fusiform gyrus 80,82 to frontal, striatal 301 
and temporal regions while viewing faces showing negative expression83. In addition, a 302 
resting state study found higher connectivity between the left amygdala and left middle and 303 
inferior temporal cortices and superior frontal gyrus in individuals with BDD than in 304 
controls84. As the amygdala receives visual signals from inferior or ventral temporal areas 85, 305 
increased connectivity in BDD may indicate stronger salience and emotional processing of 306 
visual imagery. Additionally, heightened connectivity during emotional arousal from 307 
amygdala to ventral temporal areas can enhance detail extraction as part of threat responses, 308 
which promote hypervigilance to the environment 86,87. This increased connectivity could 309 
additionally contribute to visual perceptual distortions by increasing the prominence of visual 310 
details.  311 

14 
 
Functional neuroimaging in BDD has also revealed atypical activity and connectivity 312 
in frontostriatal regions70,76. One study observed hyperactivity in the orbitofrontal cortex and 313 
caudate when viewing one’s face (specifically, lateral regions involved in evaluating 314 
punishment value of stimuli and accordingly adapting behaviour 88, which correlates with 315 
BDD-related obsessions and compulsions (which include intrusive and persistent thoughts 316 
about a perceived physical defect, and repetitive behaviours such as mirror checking) 70. 317 
Similar patterns of orbitofrontal connectivity associated with obsessions and compulsions 318 
were also evident while viewing others' faces, alongside abnormal local amygdala 319 
connectivity with other limbic and striatal regions, possibly reflecting differences in 320 
emotional processing of others’ faces compared with healthy controls 76. The frontostriatal 321 
and orbitofrontal findings, particularly in response to emotionally-evocative stimuli, parallel 322 
those observed in individuals with OCD 89. Similar brain-behaviour relationships between 323 
BDD and OCD from these small studies provide some support for BDD’s nosologic 324 
classification as an obsessive-compulsive-related disorder in the ICD-11 5 and the DSM-5 7. 325 
Another study found correlation between BDD-related preoccupations and repetitive 326 
behaviours and a dynamic brain state involving medial orbitofrontal regions (whose functions 327 
include aesthetic judgements and reinforcement processing 88,90–93 while viewing one’s face 328 
77). Furthermore, the probability of this dynamic state (fraction of time in which this state 329 
occurred, across the task) decreased when eye gaze was experimentally manipulated by 330 
having participants hold their gaze constant on a crosshair in the middle of the image (Figure 331 
2) 77, preventing visual scanning and fixation on perceived flaws (Table 2). Whether these 332 
neural system patterns are developmental precursors to BDD or consequences of the disorder 333 
remains uncertain. In addition, the small sample sizes in all studies temper definitive 334 
conclusions at this point. 335 

15 
 
The research to date on neural molecular mechanisms of BDD has examined 336 
serotonin, dopamine and oxytocin, providing early evidence for potential differences in 337 
serotonergic (lower platelet serotonin binding density) and dopaminergic systems (lower 338 
dopamine D2/D3 receptor levels in the caudate and putamen), which may be similar to those 339 
found in OCD 94–98. Yet, studies have been sparse and sample sizes small. 340 
Overall, BDD is a nascent field. Much is yet to be uncovered regarding systems-level 341 
functional neural mechanisms, structural neural mechanisms and molecular mechanisms in 342 
BDD, as well as potential biomarkers and treatment targets (Table 2). 343

[H1] Diagnosis, screening and prevention 345 
[H2] Diagnostic criteria 346 
Owing to its similarities with other disorders in the OCD spectrum 99, BDD is 347 
currently classified under the obsessive-compulsive and related disorders chapter in both the 348 
DSM-5 and the ICD-115,7. Diagnostic criteria for BDD in both manuals are largely similar. 349 
The first core characteristic of BDD is the preoccupation with one or more perceived defects 350 
or flaws in physical appearance that are not observable to others. Slight flaws may be present, 351 
but the degree of preoccupation is clearly disproportionate (for example, worrying 352 
excessively because of a pimple or a small scar). People with BDD are usually profoundly 353 
concerned with several aspects of their appearance, often facial features, although any body 354 
part can be the focus of preoccupation 9,18,19,29,100. For some individuals, the preoccupation is 355 
not focused on a specific feature, but can be a general feeling of being ugly, unattractive or 356 
disfigured. This general feeling of ugliness is uncommon in eating disorders such as anorexia 357 
nervosa, where the preoccupation is specifically focused on body shape and size. People with 358 
BDD often demonstrate excessive self-focused attention, which may present with ideas of 359 
reference, such as thinking that others are judging them, laughing at them or talking about 360 

16 
 
their appearance. This latter characteristic is included in the ICD-11 as part of the description 361 
of the disorder, but not in the DSM-5. The second core characteristic of the disorder is the 362 
presence of repetitive behaviors (for example, mirror checking, camouflaging) or mental acts 363 
(such as, comparing with others) performed in response to the appearance-related 364 
preoccupations. Individuals with BDD tend to perform several of these repetitive behaviours 365 
to cope with the negative feelings caused by their envisaged appearance concerns 18,19,29,100,101 366 
(Table 3). The ICD-11 also mentions avoidance, particularly of social situations and triggers 367 
(for example, reflective surfaces, changing rooms), as a common behavior in individuals 368 
BDD. 369 
Both the ICD-11 and the DSM-5 describe that the disorder causes substantial distress 370 
and impairment; for example, people with BDD may ruminate about their appearance several 371 
hours a day, have a limited number of social interactions and relationships or be isolated, and 372 
sometimes even become housebound. Children and adolescents have high rates of school 373 
dropout and adults may stop working (or never start working) because of their incapacitating 374 
symptoms 19,29. The ICD-11 additionally mentions that if functioning is maintained, it is only 375 
through substantial additional effort. BDD diagnosis should be ruled out if the symptoms can 376 
be better explained by an eating disorder (DSM-5) 7 or another medical condition or if the 377 
symptoms are the effects of a substance or medication on the central nervous system (ICD-378 
11) 5. 379 
In the DSM-5 and the ICD-11, the level of insight (that is, the extent to which the 380 
person believes that their BDD beliefs are true) can be described on a continuum from good 381 
to absent 5,7. Some people with BDD readily recognize that their beliefs about their 382 
appearance are definitely or probably not true (that is, good insight), whereas the majority are 383 
ambivalent or even convinced that the beliefs are true (that is, poor or absent insight). Lack of 384 
insight can be a challenge as it may undermine motivation to see a mental health professional. 385 

17 
 
Additionally, the DSM-5 also allows specifying the presence of muscle dysmorphia within 386 
the diagnosis of BDD. In this group of individuals with BDD with muscle dysmorphia, 387 
predominantly men, the appearance preoccupation focuses on the idea that the body build is 388 
too small or not muscular enough 7,102. 389 
 390 
The features of BDD are similar in adolescents and adults and girls/women and boys/men 391 
19,21,103 but research on BDD symptoms across different cultures and ethnic groups is scarce 392 
and of limited methodological quality 19,21,103. Population surveys in non-clinical samples 393 
report some differences in appearance concerns and number of preoccupations between 394 
different ethnic groups 104,105. Although, taken together, the results from quantitative studies 395 
and qualitative observations from around the world suggest more similarities than differences 396 
between ethnic groups 25. Nonetheless, it is important to note that the traits that are 397 
considered to be more attractive or less attractive may differ across cultures 6. 398

[H2] Differential diagnoses 400 
Worries about one’s own appearance are common and most often do not generate 401 
impairment. These normal concerns are particularly common in certain life periods, for 402 
example during adolescence 106 and such developmentally typical concerns should not be 403 
mistaken with BDD. However, BDD should be differentiated from other psychiatric 404 
disorders. The most common differential diagnoses for BDD are eating disorders and other 405 
obsessive-compulsive and related disorders (Table 4). Importantly, all these disorders can co-406 
exist with BDD in the same individual 12,13.  407 
Individuals that present with actual, observable flaws for example, facial 408 
disfigurement, burn scars etc., will not meet criteria for BDD, despite having a clinical 409 
presentation similar to that of BDD, with certain repetitive behaviours (for example, 410 

18 
 
camouflaging), avoidance and impairment. Nevertheless, these individuals do not tend to 411 
have repetitive checking behaviours, as there is no uncertainty about their appearance 107. 412 
These patients can still receive help and improve the way they feel and how they function in 413 
their lives. In most cases, the treatment will be similar to that offered for individuals with 414 
BDD, but with a greater psychosocial emphasis 108. For example, as people with visible 415 
differences in appearance are often faced with curiosity and questions and/or judgement from 416 
others, treatment may incorporate strategies to cope with these challenges.  417

[H2] Diagnosis 419 
A crucial aspect in the assessment of BDD is that individuals may be reluctant to 420 
disclose their symptoms due to poor insight, a desire for treatments other than mental health 421 
interventions (for example, cosmetic procedures) and shame or embarrassment. Individuals 422 
with BDD may first contact mental health professionals because of low mood, social anxiety 423 
or impairments in their day-to-day life, rather than their concerns with their appearance. 424 
Questions should be non-judgmental and motivational interviewing techniques modified for 425 
BDD may be helpful in facilitating engaging with the assessment and evaluating motivation 426 
for change 109.  427 
An open question such as “have you worried a lot about your appearance or a body 428 
feature and wish you could think about it less?” could be a good icebreaker to explore 429 
dissatisfaction with one’s own appearance before proceeding to assess the rest of diagnostic 430 
criteria. Screening measures such as the Body Image Questionnaire (also known as Cosmetic 431 
Procedure Screening Questionnaire (COPS))110 or the Body Dysmorphic Disorder 432 
Questionnaire (BDDQ) 25, as well as the screening question included in the Structured 433 
Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Disorders (SCID-5) —“In the past month, have you been very 434 
concerned that there is something wrong with your physical appearance or the way one or 435 

19 
 
more parts of your body look?” 111 — could also be of help. However, these screening tools 436 
should be followed by a more extensive, guided assessment to establish whether diagnostic 437 
criteria are met.  438 
A number of instruments can assist in the diagnostic process, quantify symptom 439 
severity and track symptom improvement with treatment. A limited number of semi-440 
structured clinical interviews that focus specifically on BDD are available and can, therefore, 441 
guide this diagnostic process. These interviews include the Body Dysmorphic Disorder 442 
Diagnostic Module (BDD Module) 112 and the Diagnostic Interview for Anxiety, Mood and 443 
OCD and Related Neuropsychiatric Disorders (DIAMOND) 113. To measure BDD symptom 444 
severity, the Yale-Brown Obsessive–Compulsive Scale modified for BDD (BDD-YBOCS), 445 
and its adolescent version (BDD-YBOCS-A) are used in specialist services and clinical trials. 446 
Both instruments are validated and have good psychometric properties 114,115. These scales 447 
are clinician-rated and a certain level of training is needed to use them. A reduction of 30% or 448 
more on the BDD-YBOCS or BDD-YBOCS-A scale is typically used to denote treatment 449 
response, whereas a score of 16 or below would indicate partial or full remission 116. Other 450 
validated self-reported measures that can assess the severity of the dysmorphic concerns and 451 
can be used to monitor treatment effects include the above-mentioned COPS, the Dysmorphic 452 
Concerns Questionnaire (DCQ) 117, the Appearance Anxiety Inventory 118 and the Body 453 
Dysmorphic Disorder Symptom Scale 119,120.  454 
A diagnosis of BDD sometimes requires direct visual observation by the clinician 455 
assessing the symptoms of the person’s perceived defects in appearance, to establish whether 456 
their preoccupation and distress is disproportionate to any visible difference in appearance. 457 
The diagnosis of BDD is generally made by a specialist in mental health (for example, a 458 
psychiatrist or a psychologist). However, if the area of concern is hidden or not easily 459 
assessed (for example, concerns about genitalia), a formal physical examination with a third 460 

20 
 
party (such as, with their general practitioner) can be helpful to inform the diagnosis. The 461 
assessment process should also include a risk assessment for self-harm, suicidality and 462 
unwarranted or unsafe cosmetic procedures. 463

[H2] Prevention 465 
Strategies to prevent BDD symptoms are currently lacking. Some risk factors for 466 
BDD have been suggested (for example, bullying and childhood trauma; see Risk factors 467 
section), which may be susceptible to modification, but research in this area is still in its 468 
infancy. Early detection and improvement and dissemination of evidence-based treatments 469 
should be prioritized as they may prevent chronification of cases and the development of 470 
adverse long-term consequences. 471 
 472 
[H1] Management  473 
Clinical guidelines suggest the management of BDD should be determined by level of 474 
functional impairment (Figure 3), with increasingly intensive and specialist treatment offered 475 
for more severe presentations 121. Consistent with the evidence-base, international consensus 476 
exists regarding the recommended treatments for BDD, which are cognitive-behavioural 477 
therapy (CBT) specifically tailored for BDD and SSRIs121,122. For those with higher levels of 478 
functional impairment, additional support may be required to promote psychosocial 479 
functioning (for example, employment or educational advice for those who have withdrawn 480 
from work or school). In brief, the goals of BDD treatment are to reduce distress and 481 
impairment, improve body image and enhance quality of life of patients. 482 
Engaging patients with BDD in mental health treatment can be challenging because 483 
most have limited or absent insight, viewing themselves as having a physical problem rather 484 
than a psychological one. A key focus early in treatment, therefore, needs to be on 485 

21 
 
engagement with an alternative (psychological) understanding of the problem. Importantly, 486 
clinicians must refrain from polarising physical and psychological explanations but promote 487 
engagement by focusing on ‘common ground’, emphasising that the goal of treatment is to 488 
reduce distress, improve quality of life and build self-confidence. Motivational interviewing 489 
techniques are often used at the start of therapy to increase patient engagement. Many 490 
individuals with BDD seek surgical and non-surgical cosmetic treatments; these are not 491 
recommended as outcomes and are typically unpredictable or poor in most types of cosmetic 492 
procedures 123. In cases where an individual with BDD is intent on seeking cosmetic 493 
procedure, being transparent about the fact that this is unlikely to be an effective solution is 494 
advisable; however, one should refrain from trying to dissuade the patients. For instance, 495 
asking the patient if they would be willing to delay seeking cosmetic procedure while they try 496 
BDD treatment, that is, taking a ‘what is there to lose?’ approach, is found to be more 497 
productive. 498 
Careful and regular risk assessment is a crucial component of BDD management. As 499 
described above, self-harm and suicidal thoughts and behaviours are common in those with 500 
BDD. Furthermore, some individuals with BDD engage in risky behaviours ( such as ‘do it 501 
yourself’ (DIY) cosmetic procedures) aimed at altering their physical appearance 124. These 502 
phenomena should be carefully considered within the assessment, formulation and 503 
management of BDD. 504 
 505 
[H2] Psychotherapy 506 
CBT is the most evidence-based psychological treatment for BDD 125. Core 507 
components of CBT include psychoeducation and formulation, engagement in an alternative 508 
understanding of the problem, exposure with response prevention and behavioural 509 
experiments, cognitive restructuring (for example, reappraising exaggerated beliefs about the 510 

22 
 
importance of appearance), and relapse prevention. Of note, attempting to directly challenge 511 
an individual’s appearance concerns is rarely helpful and can jeopardise engagement. 512 
Exposure with response prevention is a key focus of treatment 121,122 and involves confronting 513 
feared or avoided situations (for example, social situations, bright lights etc.,) at the same 514 
time refraining from engaging in BDD-related repetitive behaviours, such as , camouflaging 515 
or checking perceived flaws. Additional techniques that are used in CBT for BDD include 516 
motivational interviewing, attention retraining, mirror retraining, developing self-517 
compassion, strategies to address low self-esteem (for example, using cognitive techniques to 518 
target core beliefs), and imagery rescripting for aversive memories 126,127. Specific modules 519 
can be incorporated into CBT for BDD as indicated, for example, to target muscle 520 
dysmorphia, skin-picking and hair-pulling. 521 
Ten randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have shown CBT to be efficacious for BDD 522 
compared with waitlist (that is, no treatment) and credible controls (that is, alternative 523 
psychological treatments) . In a meta-analysis of seven RCTs, CBT was associated with 524 
greater improvement in BDD symptoms at post-treatment than waitlist or control treatment (d 525 
= − 1.22) 130. Rates of CBT response (defined as a ≥30% reduction in BDD-YBOCS score)116 526 
range from 40% 131 to 82% 132. Importantly, CBT has also been associated with 527 
improvements on a range of secondary outcomes including insight, depressive symptoms and 528 
quality of life 130 and progress has been shown to be maintained for up to 4 years 133. Little is 529 
known regarding the effectiveness of CBT for BDD in real-world, non-trial settings. 530 
Preliminary data indicate promising outcomes in specialist treatment centres 134 but further 531 
research is needed to establish the acceptability, feasibility (including adherence to treatment) 532 
and effectiveness of CBT for BDD in other settings. Moreover, future RCTs comparing CBT 533 
with active psychological controls are required to determine the specificity of the effect of 534 

23 
 
CBT in the treatment of BDD, although existing studies indicate superiority of CBT over 535 
anxiety management 129 and supportive psychotherapy 9 at least in some contexts 128. 536 
Published trials investigating CBT for BDD have involved a varying number of CBT 537 
sessions, ranging from 8 to 24 132,128. Empirical findings suggest that most individuals with 538 
BDD require 11 sessions or more to achieve a meaningful response to CBT 135, and additional 539 
gains are often observed after session 12 129.  540 
Notably, majority of CBT studies to date have focused on adult populations and only 541 
one RCT has been conducted with adolescents >12 years of age and over with BDD 131. In 542 
this trial, CBT outperformed brief psychoeducation and weekly risk monitoring at post-543 
treatment in adolescents with BDD (d = 1.2). Some adaptations to CBT are required when 544 
working with young people including parental involvement in sessions. Parents or carers are 545 
usually included during the psychoeducation phase to ensure a shared understanding of BDD 546 
131,136. Parental involvement thereafter is dependent on a range of factors including the extent 547 
to which parents are involved in BDD-related avoidance and safety behaviours, such as 548 
providing reassurance, which may inadvertently fuel BDD 137.  549 
Despite its efficacy, the majority of individuals with BDD fail to access CBT 138. This 550 
treatment gap is underpinned by multiple factors, including poor insight, a desire to seek 551 
cosmetic interventions as opposed to psychological treatment, skepticism about the potential 552 
benefits of psychological treatment and stigma 138. An additional major barrier is the scarcity 553 
of adequately trained therapists 139. To address this, digitalized CBT tailored to BDD have 554 
been developed and studies have reported efficacy in mild to moderate BDD compared with 555 
waitlist control (d = 1.44) 140 and supportive therapy comprising general counselling 556 
techniques such as reflecting and empathising (d =.95) 9. These programs involve low levels 557 
of therapist input provided by individuals with minimal psychotherapy training, highlighting 558 

24 
 
the potential scalability and cost-effectiveness of this approach 141,142. Similar programs have 559 
recently been developed for adolescents with BDD, with encouraging preliminary results 143.  560 
 561 
[H2] Pharmacotherapy  562 
SSRIs, including fluoxetine, sertraline, and escitalopram, are the first-line 563 
pharmacological treatment for BDD 121,122. Among adults with BDD, two RCTs have shown 564 
fluoxetine and clomipramine (a tricyclic antidepressant) to be effective against placebo and 565 
desipramine, respectively 144,145. In these trials, response rates (defined as ≥30% reduction in 566 
BDD-YBOCS score) ranged from 53–65% in the SSRI group versus 18–35% in the control 567 
group. SSRIs are also associated with substantial improvements in secondary outcomes 568 
including insight, depressive symptoms, psychosocial functioning and quality of life 146.  569 
No formal dose-finding studies have been performed in BDD, but guidelines suggest 570 
that BDD usually requires relatively high doses of S/SRIs, like OCD 147. An adequate trial of 571 
an SSRI should be a minimum of 12–16 weeks, with at least 4 weeks at the maximum 572 
approved and maximum tolerable dose 122. Continuation of SSRIs over longer periods may be 573 
associated with better outcomes and can minimize the risk of relapse 146. One RCT found that 574 
among patients with BDD who had received escitalopram for 14 weeks, 18% of responders 575 
who received continued escitalopram relapsed over the following 6 months versus 40% of 576 
those who received placebo 146. 577 
Clinical trials investigating SSRIs in youth with BDD are not available. Nevertheless, 578 
in keeping with clinical guidelines for OCD and research in adults, SSRI are commonly 579 
prescribed to adolescents with BDD, with case reports and observational studies providing 580 
preliminary evidence that SSRIs may be beneficial and safe in this population 19,100,148.  581

[H2] When treatment does not work 583 

25 
 
Although most individuals with BDD respond to adequate trials of CBT and/or SSRI 584 
medication, a subset of patients experience persistent symptoms. A number of studies have 585 
sought to establish predictors of BDD treatment response, but findings have been strikingly 586 
inconsistent 149, which may in part reflect over-reliance on secondary analyses of existing 587 
datasets (for example, from clinical trials), which are typically underpowered.  588 
For psychotherapy, clinical guidelines 121,122 recommend that individuals who have 589 
not responded to an initial course of CBT tailored for BDD should be offered more intensive 590 
and specialist CBT, which may include consideration of treatment in a residential or inpatient 591 
setting.  592 
For pharmacotherapy, the usual clinical practice would be to first increase the SSRI 593 
dose, as tolerated, to the maximum dose recommended for OCD 150. If BDD symptoms do 594 
not improve, a second SSRI can be attempted, again up to the maximum tolerated dose. 595 
Clomipramine can be tried if at least two SSRIs at maximum dose have not been effective. 596 
Expert opinion is that S/SRI augmentation with second-generation antipsychotic drugs may 597 
be beneficial 122, but the evidence-base is limited. One RCT that evaluated the efficacy of 598 
augmentation strategies in adults with BDD found that pimozide augmentation of fluoxetine 599 
was no more effective than placebo (response rates of 18.2 versus 17.6%, respectively) 147.  600

[H1] Quality of life  602 
Individuals with BDD experience substantial impairment in quality of life across 603 
multiple domains, which generally correlate with BDD symptom severity. The most 604 
frequently reported measures of quality of life in individuals with BDD are general measures, 605 
such as the Medical Outcomes Study 36-Item, Short-Form Health Survey (SF-36) 151, the 606 
Quality of Life Enjoyment and Satisfaction Questionnaire (Q-LES-Q) 152, the Sheehan 607 
Disability Scale (SDS) 153, and the Body Image Quality of Life Inventory (BIQLI) 154. People 608 

26 
 
with BDD may have lower mental health–related quality of life than the general population 609 
patients with type II diabetes mellitus or myocardial infarction, or patients with psychiatric 610 
disorders, such as depression, OCD, anxiety disorders, eating disorders, bipolar disorder, or 611 
schizophrenia 155. Similarly, one study 156 reported a more negative impact of BDD on quality 612 
of life than anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa, measured with the BIQLIinstrument 154. A 613 
high proportion of people with BDD are not currently working (36%) or not in school (32%) 614 
155. Several factors have been associated with poor quality of life and functional disability, 615 
after controlling for BDD symptom severity 157, including older age, being female, having no 616 
medical insurance, more depressive symptoms and more body parts of concern 157. Several 617 
RCTs reported CBT to be associated with marked improvements in quality of life compared 618 
with control conditions 125. This finding suggests that including quality of life as an outcome 619 
measure in the treatment of BDD likely adds clinically valuable information.  620

[H1] Outlook  622 
Despite the relatively high prevalence and functional impairment associated with 623 
BDD, the disorder frequently goes unrecognized in both clinical and public health settings, 624 
largely owing to misconceptions about the condition. Even when diagnosed, patients seldom 625 
receive evidence-based treatment for BDD 14. Hence, there is an urgent need for increased 626 
awareness and treatment access and an expansion of treatment options.  627 
 628 
[H2] Emerging treatments  629 
Since 2016, several new psychotherapies and pharmacotherapies have received 630 
empirical attention for the management of BDD. In relation to psychological therapy, several 631 
studies have examined the efficacy of acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) in 632 
reducing BDD symptoms, including in one RCT 158, with promising results. Moreover, a pilot 633 

27 
 
study which evaluated ACT with Compassion (ACTwC), targeting shame in BDD, showed 634 
promising preliminary results 159. One study tested a transdiagnostic, emotion-focused 635 
therapy in the treatment of BDD and demonstrated superiority to hybrid waitlist or treatment-636 
as-usual 160. Lastly, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy has been found to be effective 637 
compared with treatment-as-usual in the management of BDD 161. All these therapies require 638 
further evaluation in larger trials, particularly in relation to more established treatments for 639 
BDD, such as exposure-based CBT.  640 
With respect to pharmacological agents, an open-label trial found promising outcomes  641 
for single-dose psilocybin delivered with psychological support to adults with SSRI-resistant 642 
BDD 162, but controlled trials have not been undertaken yet. Intranasal oxytocin (single dose) 643 
versus placebo has also been investigated in BDD using a within-subject cross-over design, 644 
but was not associated with the hypothesized improvement in social cognition.  645

[H2] Future research 647 
Future research into the aetiology and treatment of BDD should address some critical 648 
limitations of previous BDD studies, including retrospective and cross-sectional designs and 649 
the oversight of potential familial and genetic confounding. Very large samples of clinically 650 
diagnosed individuals with BDD will be needed to identify risk genes, and such sample sizes 651 
can only be amassed through international collaboration. Research into environmental risk 652 
factors will be at least as important and should ideally be conducted using genetically 653 
informative designs, such as family or twin designs. Research into biological-environment 654 
interactions is also important to better understand, for example, why some individuals who 655 
experience certain stressful events potentially may be more at risk of developing BDD than 656 
others, and at what stages of psychosocial and neural development are certain environmental 657 
influences more profoundly influential (Table 2). 658 

28 
 
Another topic that needs further exploration is to understand the potential effects of 659 
social media on the development, exacerbation and/or maintenance of BDD. Social media 660 
may worsen the negative beliefs about one’s appearance, in general, by promoting a narrow 661 
range of features as universally beautiful 163–166. Early research in this area found that BDD 662 
symptoms were associated with an increased number of hours of use of Snapchat and 663 
Instagram 167,168 and that the motivation for using social media was appearance-related 169. 664 
However, from these studies, the causal direction of these relationships remains unclear. 665 
Evolutionary biology suggests that concerns about appearance are rooted in sexual 666 
selection, social competition and fitness signalling 170–174. This likely underlies the nearly 667 
ubiquitous grooming behaviours found in mammals, birds and insects. Thus, animal models 668 
of aberrant grooming behaviours may help understand repetitive grooming (hair, skin, etc.) 669 
behaviours, observed in those with BDD 175 (Table 2); the relevance of these animal models 670 
to BDD, however, needs further exploration. 671 
The precise association between the perceptual distortions and the brain and the 672 
symptoms of BDD remains to be clarified. Future research should, therefore, prioritize 673 
studies on perceptual processing to enhance our understanding of the contributions of lower-674 
level visual disturbances, selective attention biases, emotional arousal, and their interplay in 675 
visual perceptual distortions. This could involve, for example, eye-tracking experiments with 676 
appearance-related and non-appearance-related stimuli, employing computational modelling 677 
of eye gaze behaviours to predict selective attention biases and implementing multimodal 678 
integration analyses to capture dynamic changes over time. Research is also warranted to 679 
investigate the clinical effects of manipulation of visual attention such as holding eye gaze 680 
constant when viewing one’s appearance 77. 681 
Addressing the challenges in BDD management is vital for improving healthcare 682 
provision for this disorder. More adequately powered RCTs, particularly in understudied 683 

29 
 
populations (for example, youth and low- and medium-income countries), should be 684 
conducted to investigate the clinical efficacy of various treatments for BDD. Since most 685 
individuals with BDD are unable to access evidence-based treatment, most notably CBT, a 686 
primary objective must be to address this treatment gap. Research on digitalized CBT for 687 
BDD and other forms of accessible treatment options is crucial to develop scalable treatment 688 
options, which is key to develop treatments aimed at improving the quality of life for this 689 
struggling, often misunderstood, patient population. 690
