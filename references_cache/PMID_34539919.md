# Growth Hormone Dynamics among Children with Mixed Gonadal Dysgenesis (45,X/46,XY).
**Authors:** Das D
**Journal:** Acta Endocrinol (Buchar) (2021)
**DOI:** [10.4183/aeb.2021.117](https://doi.org/10.4183/aeb.2021.117)

## Content

CONTEXT: Learn the growth hormone dynamics and discuss the issues of growth hormone therapy in subjects with 45,X/46XY.

OBJECTIVE: To study the growth hormone dynamics in children with 45,X/46,XY karyotyping and mixed gonadal dysgenesis (MGD).

DESIGN: Descriptive clinical study.

PARTICIPANTS: Five subjects with karyotype 45,X / 46,XY with or without genital ambiguity and somatic features of SHOX haploinsufficiency.

INTERVENTIONS: Growth hormone dynamic study and gonadectomy.

MAIN OUTCOME: IGF-1, peak GH levels, Turner's stigmata and histology of gonadal tissue.

RESULTS: Five cases of MGD with both male and female phenotype were studies. IGF-1 levels and GH levels showed both features of growth hormone deficiency and growth hormone insensitivity. One study subject has gonadal germ cell tumour (dysgerminoma). We discuss here the issues regarding the GH therapy in MGD subjects.

CONCLUSION: Growth deceleration in MGD subjects is partly due to defective growth hormone secretion and partly due to growth hormone insensitivity. MGD subjects are at high risk for occurrence of gonadal tumours. Gonadectomy or biopsy of underlying dysgenetic gonads is essential prior GH therapy. Close surveillance for second neoplasm is to be considered in subjects with history of gonadal tumors prior starting GH for short stature.

Mixed gonadal dysgenesis (MGD) or 45,X/46,XY mosaicism is a rare condition which presents as a spectrum of phenotype ranging from women with or without Turner’s stigmata to individuals with ambiguous genitals, and rarely normal males (1). The clinical presentation includes ambiguous genitals at birth, delayed puberty, virilisation at puberty in women or infertility. Turner’s stigmata include linear growth failure, neck webbing, lymphedema, early sensorineural hearing loss, distinctive congenital cardiovascular, skeletal, digital, autoimmune disease and renal anomalies (2). Internal ducts may range from the presence of Mullerian ducts or/and wolffian ducts and gonads show normal testis to streak gonads. MGD arises due to late anaphase lag during mitosis in the zygote (3). Y chromosomal abnormalities such as ring chromosomes may also lead to mosaicism due to meiotic instability. The 45,X/46,XY mosaic has a worldwide incidence rate ranging 1.5 – 1.7/10,000 conceptions (1,4). Growth hormone dynamics has been studied by a few authors, yet no concrete evidence regarding GH-IGF-1 levels among MGD has been elaborated and no specific guidelines to manage growth issues in these subjects are available. Here we present a case series on growth hormone dynamics among MGD and its management issues.

The study was conducted in the Department of Endocrinology, Government Medical College, Thiruvananthapuram, India between October 2015 and April 2020 after gathering consent from study subjects and approval from the ethics committee of the institute. Five children with MGD were available during the study period. MGD was diagnosed based on genital phenotype, gonadal phenotype and mosaic karyotype. Genital phenotype ranged from female external genitalia or mild clitoromegaly through all the stages of ambiguous genitalia to hypospadias or a normal penis. Ambiguity in external genitalia was scored by external masculinization score (EMS). EMS is an objective score to measure the degree of virilisation of genitals and takes into account features such as presence or absence of scrotal fusion, micropenis and the location of each gonad and urethral meatus (5). Gonadal phenotype included streak gonads through dysgenetic testis to normal testis and was confirmed following gonadectomy and histological examination. All subjects had gonadectomy done within one year following the diagnosis of MGD. All gonadal specimens were tissue fixed with 10% formalin for 24 hours, followed by paraffin wax embedding and preparation of slides of 4 micrometre thickness. Hematoxylin- Eosin stainings of the samples were done and classified as normal testis, dysgenetic testis, ovary, streak, combination of these or gonadal germ cell tumours. All specimens were subjected to immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining. IHC staining used to stain GCT included octamer binding transcription factor 3/4 (Oct 4), placental alkaline phosphatase (PLAP), alpha-fetoprotein when gonadal tumour was confirmed histologically (6). Karyotype included were 45,X/46,XY or 45,X/47,XXY or 45,X/47,XYY. Karyotyping was studied from the 30 cells of peripheral blood lymphocyte. 100 cells karyotyping was done if the 30 cells study was negative for the Y chromosome despite having a strong clinical suspicion of the presence of the Y chromosome. In our case series, all cases had a karyotype 45,X/46,XY. There were no specific Y chromosomal abnormalities detected in this case series. Presence of Y chromosomal abnormalities is associated with infertility (7).

Growth hormone dynamic study.All the five cases underwent a growth hormone dynamic study. The growth hormone dynamics study was indicated as the children had clinical significant severe short stature (as per the IAP 2015 growth reference charts). All study subjects underwent an anterior pituitary functional assessment which includes T4, TSH, 8 am cortisol, FSH and LH levels. IGF-1 levels and GH stimulation were done on two separate occasions with clonidine (0.15mg/m2) and glucagon (0.03mg/kg). The stimulation tests are done after an overnight fast and blood samples for GH are taken at the basal (zero) hour and at 30, 60, 90 and 120 minutes. Growth hormone deficiency is defined when IGF-1 is below the normal range for age and pubertal status and a two peak provocative GH test done on two separate days is below 10 ng/mL in an individual with growth failure after exclusion of other causes. Growth hormone insensitivity is characterized by low serum IGF-1 levels for age and pubertal status in the presence of normal or increased production of GH (basal GH >5 ng/mL or stimulated GH >15 ng/mL) in the presence of growth failure. hCG stimulation test.This was done to find the presence of an underlying functioning testicular tissue. Secondly, the test also helps us to distinguish the close differential diagnosis of androgen biosynthetic defects. The test includes a baseline blood sample collection in fasting for total testosterone. hCG is given deep intramuscular for three consecutive days in a dose based on the age of the patient (age < 1 year 500 IU per day hCG, age 1-12 years 1000 IU per day and age >12 years 1500 IU per day). Blood samples are collected 24 hours after the third dose for total testosterone, dihydrotestosterone and androstenedione. A value of two to eight times the basal value suggests the presence of underlying testicular tissue (8). Ratio of dihydrotestosterone: testosterone <10 and testosterone to androstenedione <0.8 is suggestive of 5 alpha reductase enzyme deficiency and 17 β hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase enzyme deficiency respectively.

Here we present five cases of MGD where all patients presented with Turner stigmata (like short stature, mesomelia, Madelung deformity, wide carrying angle). Karyotyping of all five individuals had a predominant 45 X cell population as seen in the peripheral blood lymphocytes (Case 1: 90%, Case 2: 80%, Case 3: 90%, Case 4:90% and Case 5 60%). Growth failure was documented in most cases at an age of 5 to 6 years, though it may occur earlier. The primary cited causation being SHOX haploinsufficiency. SHOX gene is located in pseudoautosomal region (PAR1) of sex chromosomes (both X and Y) and is one of the major growth regulating genes in humans. SHOX protein controls chondrocytes apoptosis and proliferation by regulating multiple target genes, including BNP, Fgfr3, Agc1 and ctgf (9). SHOX abnormalities have been associated with short stature and various skeletal abnormalities. Growth failure is evident from the first year of life among SHOX haploinsufficiency (10). The most characteristic clinical finding of SHOX haploinsufficiency is madelung deformity and mesomelic short stature (11). There are no studies highlighting the frequency of SHOX haploinsufficiency among MGD. We believe the effects of 45, X related SHOX haploinsufficiency do contribute to short stature which is well documented in Turner’s syndrome and non-Turner’s SHOX haploinsufficiency.

Short stature has been an integral part of patients with MGD as discussed above. GH and IGF-1 levels estimations have been done in a few studies and found to be similar to patients with TS showing GH deficiency in some subjects and in other subsets GH insensitivity (12). Subject with 45,X/46,XY treated with GH has shown improvement in linear growth. However, there have been no concrete studies which have studied the GH-IGF-1 levels in depth. Growth hormone studies among these subjects in our case series showed both growth hormones deficiency and insensitivity (other causes of short stature were excluded)Table 1. Short stature despite low IGF-1 and GH levels (both the basal and peak GH in all cases) were suggestive of growth hormone insensitivity or resistance. There have been no previous studies that have documented completely the growth hormone axis function among MGD. A few authors have shown normal to low normal IGF-1 levels among 45,X/46,XY mosaicism though in these study baseline and stimulated GH levels were not well documented (13,14). These studies which have shown a normal to low IGF-1 levels were demonstrated in MGD with male phenotypes. We found that growth hormone dynamics among MGD was similar to that of TS subjects. Studies in TS subjects have shown reduced GH secretions, normal GH levels, reduced free IGF-1, increased proteolysis of IGFBP-3 and IGF-1 resistance (15,16). Early growth failure and biochemical data suggesting both growth hormone deficiency and insensitivity among MGD makes it necessary for growth hormone therapy.

The GH dose required for MGD subjects is yet another question here. As already known the dose required for GH therapy among TS patients is higher than in the GH deficient population (17). Supraphysiologic doses are advised in TS patients to overcome the subtle defects of GH-IGF1 axis seen. Considering the presence of SHOX haploinsufficiency and GH resistance in MGD subjects as evident from our series, we advise to give a supraphysiological dose of GH to patients with MGD similar to TS to have an adequate growth response. Previous studies on the effect of GH therapy among MGD also had a significant growth response to higher doses (0.33-0.5 mg/kg per week) (18,19). It is also worth mentioning that certain patients with MGD, who have growth hormone deficiency in GH stimulation, perhaps benefit with a lower GH dose. One study done in 2004 showed an improvement in height among MGD subjects on physiological GH dose (0.19-0.33 mg/kg per week) (20). Hence it would be prudent to confirm the GH status and decide the dose of GH therapy among MGD subjects. The postulate made has a limitation that our study has not learned the effects of growth with GH therapy in precise doses and these study subjects are currently under follow-up. The time of starting GH therapy in MGD subjects would be soon as growth failure becomes evident clinically (20).

Two cases had autoimmune hypothyroidism in our series. Autoimmune disease is associated with TS and SHOX haploinsufficiency (21). Individuals with TS have an increased rate of development of several autoimmune disorders, including thyroiditis, hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism, celiac disease and type 1 diabetes mellitus, alopecia areata, juvenile rheumatoid arthritis, uveitis and inflammatory bowel disease (22). While the genetic basis for the predisposition is not known, there is a decrease in the CD4–CD8 lymphocyte ratio, suggesting an immune alteration that may predispose to autoimmunity. Girls and women with TS face a lifelong heavy burden of congenital and acquired cardiovascular disease, with increased mortality and morbidity. Congenital heart disease occurs in approximately 50% of girls with TS, including a high incidence of bicuspid aortic valve, coarctation of the aorta and an aortopathy that can lead to rare but often fatal dissection or rupture of the thoracic aorta (20). One patient (case 3) had bicuspid aortic valve. Left sided heart disease known to exist with TS and similar picture is seen in MGD cases. Case 1 also had a unilateral renal aplasia which is also described in TS. One patient had dysgerminoma (Case 5) and underwent complete resection. There was no metastasis evident. The issue we faced here was the concern for a second neoplasm (22). It is known that GH therapy can rarely lead to a second neoplasm due to its mitogenic and antiapoptotic effect of GH and IGF-1 (23). The concept of secondary neoplasm is also still a controversy. However, in our case 5 we started GH after making sure that the patient was in remission and by explaining to him the need for intensive surveillance for a second neoplasm. We also emphasize on the GH therapy to be considered only after gonadectomy in MGD subjects as the tumour risk is as high as 15-18 %. All study subjects underwent an IHC staining for the presence of an underlying germ cell tumour arising from the gonad. The routine IHC staining done was to rule out the presence of asymptomatic germ cell tumour.

In conclusion,GH dynamics in MGD includes both GH deficient and resistance state. GH dynamics should be considered in cases of MGD with short stature, or at the point of any clinical evidence of growth failure. GH dosage should be based on the GH dynamics results. Early gonadectomy followed by GH therapy in MGD subject is what we advise on managing short stature considering the possibility of underlying gonadal tumours in a dysgenetic gonad. Intensive surveillance is needed for a second neoplasm in MGD subjects who had a prior history of gonadal tumours and are on growth hormone therapy.
