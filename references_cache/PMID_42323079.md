# Skeletal muscle-specific deficiency of Rab geranylgeranyl transferase beta subunit induces myopathy and exacerbates the symptoms caused by HMG-CoA reductase deficiency in mice.
**Authors:** Osaki Y, Mizunoe Y, Nakagawa Y, Miyamoto T, Fujita R, Han SI, Araki M, Tomoda M, Kainoh K, Ohno H, Iwasaki H, Matsuzaka T, Sone H, Ohashi K, Ishibashi S, Sekiya M, Shimano H
**Journal:** Mol Metab (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.molmet.2026.102406](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molmet.2026.102406)

## Content

OBJECTIVES: Statins (HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors) are associated with myopathy, yet the precise in vivo mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear. Emerging evidence implicates a deficiency of geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGPP), a key downstream isoprenoid metabolite of the mevalonate pathway. We employed novel muscle-specific genetic mouse models to elucidate the roles of GGPP and Rab geranylgeranyl transferase β (RabGGT-β) in the development of myopathy.

METHODS: Using doxycycline-inducible Cre-LoxP technology, we generated three skeletal muscle-specific knockout (KO) models: Hmgcr-DimKO, Rabggtb-DimKO, and combined Hmgcr/Rabggtb-DimKO mice. The severity of myopathy was evaluated based on serum creatine kinase levels and histological examination. Mitochondrial mass and function were rigorously quantified. Prenylation deficit in Rabggtb-DimKO mice was confirmed via subcellular fractionation. To validate GGPP's involvement, rescue experiments were conducted using its precursor, geranylgeraniol (GGOH).

RESULTS: Hmgcr KO resulted in pronounced myopathy, marked by an early reduction in mitochondria-rich myosin heavy chain (MyHC) type I and IIa muscle fibers, followed by a later reduction in mitochondria-poor glycolytic MyHC type IIb muscle fibers, and these changes were reversed by GGOH administration. Rabggtb-DimKO mice developed myopathy later than Hmgcr-DimKO mice; however, in Hmgcr/Rabggtb-DimKO mice, myopathy was dramatically accelerated and more severe. Across all models, mitochondrial dysfunction emerged early-preceding clinical signs of myopathy-consistent with a causal relationship.

CONCLUSIONS: Our findings demonstrate that myopathy induced by HMGCR deficiency is primarily driven by GGPP depletion in a mouse model. Furthermore, impaired RabGGT-β-mediated protein geranylgeranylation represents a critical downstream mechanism that aggravates the myopathic phenotype. Early mitochondrial abnormalities may contribute to the pathogenesis of myopathy due to disruption of the mevalonate pathway.

HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR), the rate-limiting enzyme in the mevalonate pathway that finally synthesizes cholesterol [1], catalyzes the conversion of HMG-CoA to mevalonic acid (MVA). Statins inhibit HMGCR and are widely prescribed as cholesterol-lowering drugs for preventing cardiovascular and atherosclerotic diseases. However, myalgia or myopathy occurs in approximately 1–5% of patients prescribed statins, with rhabdomyolysis occurring in a minority of these patients [2].

Over the past decade, research has reported various mechanisms of statin-induced myopathy in mice, including the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway via Atrogin-1/MAFbx [3], autophagy clearance pathway via Lipin-1 [4], ligand activation of estrogen-related receptor alpha (ERRα) via cholesterol [5], direct effects of statins on mitochondrial complex III (causing mitochondrial dysfunction) [6], apoptosis via AKT/mTOR [7], endoplasmic reticulum stress via CHOP [8], myostatin activation via Foxo1 [9], and mitochondrial dysfunction due to a decrease in coenzyme Q levels [10]. Another reported mechanism, not demonstrated in mouse experiments, is impaired prenylation of Rab small G protein due to a deficiency in geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGPP) [11]. As of today, the complex mechanisms underlying statin-induced myopathy have not been fully elucidated.

Loss-of-function germline mutations in geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate synthase (GGPS1), which encodes an enzyme that synthesizes GGPP (an isoprenoid that functions as an important intermediate metabolite of the mevalonate pathway), have been reported to cause dystrophy in humans [12]. Germline mutations in HMGCR cause various degrees of limb-girdle muscular dystrophy [[13], [14], [15]]. In addition to statin-induced myopathy, disruptions of the mevalonate pathway, in which HMGCR is the rate-limiting enzyme, cause skeletal muscle disorders in humans. We have previously generated skeletal muscle-specific Hmgcr-knockout (KO) mice as a model of statin-induced myopathy [16]. In this study, we generated doxycycline (Dox)-induced skeletal muscle-specific Hmgcr KO mice and the first skeletal muscle-specific KO mouse model for the Rab geranylgeranyl transferase (GGT) β subunit, a common β subunit of both GGT-2 (also known as RabGGT) and GGT-3, to investigate whether impaired prenylation of the Rab small G protein induces myopathy. The currently known GGPP transferases are depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1A [17]. These mouse models provide a basis for in-depth analyses of the mechanisms underlying statin-induced myopathy and skeletal muscle damage caused by metabolic insufficiency of the mevalonate pathway.

Hmgcr-floxed mice [18] were kindly provided by Shun Ishibashi (Jichi Medical University, Tochigi, Japan). RabGGT-β subunit (Rabggtb)-floxed mice were generated on a C57BL/6J (The Jackson Laboratory) background at the Laboratory Animal Resource Center (University of Tsukuba, Ibaraki, Japan) using the CRISPR/Cas9 system [19]. Two sgRNAs were used to introduce loxP sites into introns 3 and 5 of the Rabggtb gene. The sgRNA sequences were as follows: intron 3, 5′-TCCCTGAAGGTAAAGACCTG-3'; intron 5, 5′-CCAGCACGTAGCAAGCCCCG-3'. B6; Cg-Tg (Acta1-cre)79Jme/J mice (stock #006149) overexpressing Cre recombinase under the control of the human alpha skeletal actin (HSA) promoter [20] along with B6; C3-Tg (ACTA1-rtTA; tetO-Cre) mice (stock #012433) overexpressing Cre recombinase under the control of the tetracycline-responsive regulatory element (tetO/TRE) and a reverse tetracycline-controlled transactivator (rtTA) under the control of the HSA promoter [21] were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. Skeletal muscle-specific Hmgcr KO (Hmgcr-mKO) mice were obtained by crossing Hmgcr-floxed mice with HSA-Cre mice. Dox-induced skeletal muscle-specific Hmgcr KO (Hmgcr-DimKO) or Rabggtb KO (Rabggtb-DimKO) mice were obtained by crossing Hmgcr-floxed or Rabggtb-floxed mice with Dox-induced HSA-Cre mice. Dox-induced skeletal muscle-specific Hmgcr and Rabggtb double-KO (Hmgcr/Rabggtb-DimKO) mice were obtained by crossing Hmgcr and Rabggtb double-floxed mice with Dox-induced HSA-Cre mice. All mice had a C57B/6J genetic background. The control/wild-type (Ctrl) mice were homozygous floxed for Hmgcr and/or Rabggtb, Cre recombinase-negative littermates. Only male mice were used for the experiments.

The following primer sequences were used to detect the presence of the Hmgcr-floxed allele and Rabggtb-floxed allele: Hmgcr-flox forward, 5′-GTC GAC GTT GAA TCC TCT TGT CAG AC-3′ and Hmgcr-flox reverse, 5′-CAA AGC AGA CAT GAG ACT ATT C-3'; Rabggtb-flox forward, 5′-ACG CTG GGC AGG TAA TTT TG-3′ and Rabggtb-flox reverse, 5′-CAA TGC GAC AGT GGA ATG CT-3'.

Four-week-old mice were administered the tetracycline analog Dox (D9891; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) to induce transgene expression. Dox was dissolved in drinking water at a concentration of 2 mg/mL supplemented with 5% sucrose and was provided ad libitum in light-proofed water bottles on the indicated days.

GGOH (G3278; Merck), a precursor to GGPP [22], was administered to evaluate its effects on myopathy in Hmgcr-mKO mice. Five-week-old mice were orally administered 300 mg/kg GGOH or saline through oral gavage twice daily for 2 weeks. Body weight was measured and blood samples were collected on the 15th experimental day.

Following euthanasia, muscle tissues from the anterior tibialis were dissected and frozen in liquid nitrogen-cooled isopentane. Muscles were cut into 10-μm cross-sections, which were subsequently stained with hematoxylin–eosin (HE). For immunohistochemical analysis, the frozen muscle sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min on ice. After pretreatment in 1.3% H2O2, the specimens were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. DAB staining was performed following incubation with horseradish peroxidase-linked secondary antibodies using the Histofine MOUSESTAIN KIT (414321; NICHIREI BIOSCIENCES Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and Histofine Simple DAB solution (415171; NICHIREI BIOSCIENCES Inc.) according to the manufacturer's instructions. To conduct immunofluorescence staining of mouse IgG within muscle fibers, frozen muscle sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min on ice, permeabilized with ice-cold methanol for 10 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 30 min. The specimens were then incubated with biotin-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG antibody for 1 h, washed three times for 5 min each with PBS containing 1% BSA, and incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated streptavidin for 1 h. The images were captured using a Nikon ECLIPSE Ts2R microscope equipped with a Nikon DS-Ri2 digital camera (Nikon Instruments Inc., Tokyo, Japan). The antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Tissue lysates were prepared as described previously [[23], [24], [25]]. Protein concentrations in the anterior tibialis or gastrocnemius muscle were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (06385-00; Nacalai, Kyoto, Japan). Equal amounts of protein were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were probed with the appropriate primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and were then incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, the membranes were incubated with Clarity Western ECL Substrate (#1705061; Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and visualized using ChemiDoc XRS Plus (170–8265J1PC; Bio-Rad). The antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

CS activity was measured as described previously [26] using 20 mg of gastrocnemius muscle.

To measure ATP levels in skeletal muscle, 10 mg of gastrocnemius muscle was used. The levels of ATP in the specimens were measured using an ATP Assay Kit (ab83355; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

This assay was performed as reported previously [27]. In brief, total DNA was isolated from gastrocnemius muscle samples using DNAiso Reagent (9770A; Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Mitochondrial DNA and nuDNA amplification via reverse-transcription (RT)-PCR was conducted using previously described primers [27]. The reaction was performed using a Thermal Cycler Dice Real Time System (Takara Bio Inc.) and the SYBR Green method with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (RR820A; TaKaRa). Relative differences in copy number were quantified based on the difference in amplification threshold between mtDNA and nuDNA using the comparative Ct (ΔΔCt) method [28].

RNA purification and qRT-PCR were performed based on previously reported protocols [16]. In brief, total RNA was extracted from gastrocnemius muscle samples using Sepasol-RNA I Super G (09379-55; Nacalai) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Purified RNA was subjected to reverse transcription using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (RR036A; TaKaRa). Quantitative RT-PCR was then performed using the SYBR Green method with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (RR820A; TaKaRa). The levels of mRNA expression were quantified using the comparative Ct (ΔΔCt) method [26]. Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH expression. The primers used for PCR were previously described [18].

After collecting mouse blood samples, plasma was isolated via centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Serum CK levels were measured using the L-type Wako CK Kit (468-88301; Wako, Osaka, Japan), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

To prepare cytosolic and membranous fractions for immunoblotting analysis, 100 mg of frozen gastrocnemius muscle was homogenized in 2 mL of buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl at pH 7.8, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.25 M sucrose, 10 mM sodium EDTA, and 10 mM sodium EGTA) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (11697498001; Merck). The muscle homogenates were then centrifuged at 7,000×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed and used to prepare membranous and cytosolic fractions via ultracentrifugation as described previously [29]. Aliquots of the cytosolic and membranous fractions were used to measure protein concentrations via the BCA Protein Assay Kit (06385-00; Nacalai). Then, 40 μg of protein from each fraction was subjected to SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting.

We have previously described myopathy in Hmgcr-mKO mice [16]. As shown in Figure 1A, in addition to muscle atrophy, Hmgcr-mKO mice exhibited paler soleus (SOL) muscles than Ctrl mice, suggesting a reduction in mitochondrial content in skeletal muscle. The results of histological staining showed fewer slow-twitch muscle fibers in Hmgcr-mKO mice than in Ctrl mice (Fig. 1B), consistent with previous reports that statin-induced myopathy in rats primarily impairs slow-twitch muscle [30]. As shown in Figure 1C, the weights of the SOL, extensor digitorum longus (EDL), tibialis anterior (TA), and gastrocnemius (GAS) muscles were all lower in Hmgcr-mKO mice than in Ctrl mice, indicating impairment of both slow-twitch and fast-twitch muscle fibers. In addition, muscle weights normalized to total body weight were markedly reduced in the GAS muscles, suggesting that they were the most severely affected among the muscles examined in Hmgcr-mKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 1B). As shown in Figure 1D, immunoblotting of GAS muscle protein revealed a decrease in MyHC type I, IIa, and IIb fibers in Hmgcr-mKO mice compared with Ctrl mice. Since the number of mitochondria is higher in oxidative MyHC type I and IIa fibers than in glycolytic type IIx and IIb fibers [31,32] and was reported to be reduced in patients with statin-induced myopathy [33], we evaluated mitochondrial function and quantity in skeletal muscle from Hmgcr-mKO mice. As shown in Figure 1E–H, the levels of mitochondrial membrane proteins Tom20 and VDAC, mitochondrial complex V (Fig. 1E), citrate synthase activity (Fig. 1F), mtDNA amount (Fig. 1G), and ATP content (Fig. 1H) were all significantly lower in the skeletal muscle of Hmgcr-mKO mice than in that of Ctrl mice. We have previously reported that the oral administration of MVA can rescue myopathy in Hmgcr-mKO mice [16]. Loss-of-function mutations in HMGCR or GGPS1 cause myopathy or dystrophy, indicating that GGPP is a crucial preventive factor [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Accordingly, we investigated whether the administration of GGOH, a precursor of GGPP, can rescue myopathy in Hmgcr-mKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 1C). As shown in Figure 1I and J, GGOH administration restored body weight gain as well as serum CK levels in Hmgcr-mKO mice to the levels observed in Ctrl mice. Furthermore, the levels of VDAC and mitochondrial complex V in Hmgcr-mKO mice were also restored to the Ctrl levels (Fig. 1K). These findings imply that deficiency of GGPP, a downstream metabolite of the mevalonate pathway, is the main cause of myopathy in Hmgcr-mKO mice.

We generated Hmgcr-DimKO mice to explore the time course of the myopathic phenotype. The expression level of Hmgcr mRNA was significantly lower in Hmgcr-DimKO mice than in Ctrl mice (Figure 2A). Serum CK levels in Hmgcr-DimKO mice were significantly higher than those in Ctrl mice at 16 days after Dox induction (Fig. 2B). HE staining revealed no abnormalities at this stage, and myopathy findings based on histological staining became evident at 20 days (Fig. 2C). From days 17–19, myopathy findings through histological staining in Hmgcr-DimKO mice varied from clearly observable to nearly undetectable. As shown in Fig. 2D, IgG accumulation within muscle fibers, an indicator of necrosis or membrane disruption [34], was observed in Hmgcr-DimKO mice at 20 days. As shown in Figure 2E, compared with Ctrl mice, Hmgcr-DimKO mice exhibited a reduction in highly oxidative MyHC type I and IIa fibers at 20 days after Dox induction. In contrast to Hmgcr-mKO mice (Fig. 1D), Hmgcr-DimKO mice did not show a decrease in MyHC type IIb fibers. These findings indicate that HMGCR deficiency in mouse skeletal muscle initially impairs highly oxidative MyHC type I and IIa muscle fibers.

We next examined whether the mitochondrial abnormalities observed in Hmgcr-mKO mice precede the appearance of myopathy in Hmgcr-DimKO mice. Immunoblotting analysis showed that levels of proteins Tom20 and VDAC (indicators of mitochondrial mass) in GAS muscle in Hmgcr-DimKO mice were significantly lower than those in Ctrl mice at 15 days after Dox induction (Fig. 2F). However, in contrast to Hmgcr-mKO mice (Fig. 1E), Hmgcr-DimKO mice did not show a significant reduction in mitochondrial complex V, suggesting that this alteration may emerge at a later stage as myopathy progresses. Additionally, the amount of mtDNA (Fig. 2G) and ATP levels (Fig. 2H) (indicators of mitochondrial function) were significantly lower in Hmgcr-DimKO mice than in Ctrl mice at the stage of 15 days after Dox induction. These results suggest that mitochondrial abnormalities could be a cause rather than a consequence of Hmgcr KO myopathy.

There is evidence that statin-induced myopathy might be caused by a failure in the geranylgeranylation of small G proteins that use GGPP as a substrate, which occurs due to the inhibition of the RabGGT-β subunit [11]. Therefore, we generated the first skeletal muscle-specific Rabggtb KO mice. As shown in Figure 3A, immunoblotting of skeletal muscle samples from Rabggtb-DimKO mice at 20 days after Dox induction showed that RabGGT-β subunit protein expression was almost completely abolished, confirming successful KO of the Rabggtb gene. Consistent with this, immunoblotting of skeletal muscle from Rabggtb-DimKO mice at 28 days revealed a marked decrease in Rab7, a representative endosomal small GTPase and known target of geranylgeranylation, in the membrane fraction and a corresponding increase in the cytosolic fraction. Therefore, Rab7 failed to localize to membranes in Rabggtb-DimKO mice compared to Ctrl mice (Fig. 3B). These findings confirmed that the transition of the Rab small G protein to the membrane fraction was impaired in the skeletal muscle of Rabggtb-DimKO mice. The membranous and cytoplasmic fractions were verified based on the marker proteins Na/K-ATPase and GAPDH, respectively. As shown in Figure 3C, serum CK levels in Rabggtb-DimKO mice did not differ from those in Ctrl mice at 28 days after Dox induction but were significantly higher than those in Ctrl mice at 60 days. HE staining showed no difference between Rabggtb-DimKO and Ctrl mice even at 45 days; however, myopathy was observed only in Rabggtb-DimKO mice at 60 days (Fig. 3D). In contrast to Hmgcr-DimKO mice (Fig. 2D), Rabggtb-DimKO mice did not show IgG accumulation (a marker of necrosis) within muscle fibers at 60 days (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, in contrast to Hmgcr-DimKO mice (Fig. 2E), Rabggtb-DimKO mice did not differ significantly from Ctrl mice in terms of the relative abundance of individual MyHC fiber types (Fig. 3F). Immunoblotting analysis revealed that the levels of Tom20 and VDAC, but not those of mitochondrial complexes, in GAS muscle from Rabggtb-DimKO mice were significantly lower than those in Ctrl mice at 28 days (Fig. 3G). The amount of mtDNA (Fig. 3H) and ATP levels (Fig. 3I) were also significantly lower in Rabggtb-DimKO mice than in Ctrl mice at 28 days. These data confirm that mitochondrial abnormalities precede the onset of myopathy also in Rabggtb-DimKO mice, although they are less severe than those observed in Hmgcr-DimKO mice.

Our findings demonstrated that skeletal muscle-specific KO of the Hmgcr or Rabggtb genes results in myopathy in mice; accordingly, we generated double KO mice of these two genes for further analyses. As shown in Figure 4A,B, Hmgcr/Rabggtb-DimKO mice exhibited a more severe myopathic phenotype than Hmgcr-DimKO mice, both in terms of serum CK levels and signs observed via HE staining, at 17 days after Dox induction. Furthermore, Hmgcr/Rabggtb-DimKO mice exhibited IgG accumulation within muscle fibers as early as day 17, whereas Hmgcr-DimKO mice showed no detectable IgG deposition on the same day (Fig. 4C). As shown in Figure 4D, immunoblotting of GAS muscle protein showed a decrease in MyHC type I, IIa, and IIb fibers in Hmgcr/Rabggtb-DimKO mice at 17 days after Dox induction that was similar to the reduction observed in Hmgcr-mKO mice (Fig. 1D). Additionally, the relative abundance of individual MyHC fiber types did not differ significantly between Hmgcr-DimKO mice and Ctrl mice at 17 days (Fig. 4D), whereas differences were observed at 20 days (Fig. 2E) as mentioned previously. These findings suggest that impaired Rab prenylation exacerbates HMGCR deficiency-induced MyHC fiber-type switching or myopathy, promoting an earlier and more severe myopathic phenotype that extends beyond oxidative fibers.

Immunoblotting analysis showed that VDAC levels in GAS muscle were significantly lower in both Hmgcr-DimKO and Hmgcr/Rabggtb-DimKO mice than in Ctrl mice at 17 days after Dox induction (Fig. 4E). In contrast, neither mouse model showed a significant reduction in the mitochondrial complexes at 17 days. The levels of ATP (Fig. 4F), an indicator of mitochondrial function, were significantly lower in Hmgcr/Rabggtb-DimKO mice than in Hmgcr-DimKO mice at 17 days. These data suggest that mitochondrial abnormalities could be a preceding factor that could be superimposed to Hmgcr-DimKO mice by additional Rabggtb KO.

Various mechanisms of statin-induced myopathy have been identified [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]], many of which involve disruptions of the mevalonate pathway. A summary of key mechanistic studies—predominantly of murine models—is provided in Supplementary Table 2. In these studies, mitochondrial dysfunction is frequently cited as a central driver of muscle toxicity [6,10,33]. Our findings align with this observation, as Hmgcr-DimKO mice exhibited a severe reduction in mitochondria-rich MyHC type I and IIa muscle fibers. Importantly, in both Hmgcr-DimKO and Rabggtb-DimKO mice, mitochondrial defects were detectable before clinical evidence of myopathy emerged, suggesting that mitochondrial impairment is a causal event rather than a secondary response. This closely matches previous observations that statin exposure perturbs mitochondrial function, leading to reduced ATP generation and impaired muscle maintenance [6,10,33].

An increase in MyHC type IIx fibers was observed in the Hmgcr-mKO (Fig. 1D), Hmgcr-DimKO (Fig. 2E), and Rabggtb/Hmgcr-DimKO (Fig. 4D) mice relative to the respective Ctrl groups, suggesting either MyHC fiber-type switching or an early expansion of type IIx fibers during the onset of muscle damage [35]. Alternatively, since HMGCR depletion has been reported to inhibit myoblast differentiation and muscle fiber formation [36], we speculate that impaired transition from MyHC type IIx to IIb fibers may have contributed to the increase in MyHC type IIx fibers. Furthermore, Hmgcr-DimKO mice, which were used to assess the early stages of myopathy development, did not exhibit a reduction in MyHC type IIb fibers, as Hmgcr-mKO did (Figure 1, Figure 2E). This suggests that the decrease in type IIb fibers observed in Hmgcr-mKO mice may be attributable to prolonged HMGCR depletion and/or delayed regeneration and maturation of skeletal muscle fibers, possibly reflecting the known transition from MyHC type IIx to type IIb fibers during skeletal muscle maturation in mice [37]. To our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate that RabGGT-β deficiency in mouse skeletal muscle causes myopathy. However, the proportion of individual MyHC isoforms in Rabggtb-DimKO mice did not differ from that in the Ctrl group (Fig. 3F), and mouse IgG staining revealed no intrafiber IgG accumulation in this KO group (Fig. 3E). These findings suggest that myopathy in Rabggtb-DimKO mice is driven predominantly by fiber degeneration rather than necrosis [34] and does not fully recapitulate the myopathic phenotype caused by HMGCR depletion.

Oral supplementation with geranylgeraniol (GGOH)—a precursor of GGPP—completely rescued the myopathic phenotype in Hmgcr-mKO mice, supporting the conclusion (previously demonstrated in vitro) that GGPP depletion is the primary trigger of myopathy caused by statins or HMGCR deficiency [9,38,39]. Intriguingly, while muscle-specific GGPS1 (GGPP synthase) KO in mice reportedly does not induce myopathy [40], germline mutations in GGPS1 cause human congenital muscular dystrophy with hearing loss and ovarian insufficiency syndromes [12]. This discrepancy may reflect species differences or compensatory systemic mechanisms in mice, as opposed to humans. Nevertheless, our murine model strongly supports the role of GGPP deficiency in precipitating skeletal muscle pathology.

Bi-allelic loss-of-function variants in HMGCR were identified in individuals with limb girdle muscular dystrophy of variable severity [[13], [14], [15]]. Notably, these human cases predominantly involved skeletal muscles, including the diaphragm, but not the cardiac and central nervous systems, reinforcing the concept that muscle tissue is especially susceptible to dysfunction of the mevalonate pathway. This concept, together with our prior findings in Hmgcr-mKO mice [16], supports the conclusion that statin-induced myopathy does not arise from direct mitochondrial toxicity but from inhibition of HMGCR enzyme activity, leading to decreased levels of downstream metabolites required for muscle integrity.

GGPP (via GGOH) is a key substrate for small G protein geranylgeranylation (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Specifically, Rho, Rac, and Rap are substrates for GGT-1, Rab proteins require GGT-2, and FXBL-2 is processed by GGT-3, a recently discovered enzyme whose β-subunit is shared with GGT-2 [17]. In vitro paradigms confirm that statin-induced cytotoxicity is ameliorated by GGPP supplementation, particularly in recovering deficient Rab prenylation [9,38,39]. Wagner et al. further demonstrated that while inhibition of GGT-1 did not injure cultured muscle cells, inhibition of the β-subunit of GGT-2 (RabGGT) did cause damage, implicating Rab prenylation in pathogenesis [11]. Conversely, Detter et al. observed no muscle phenotype in gunmetal mice lacking functional RabGGT-α subunit [41], raising possibilities about whether GGT-2 or GGT-3 contributions are critical factors. The later discovery of GGT-3 (sharing the β-subunit with GGT-2) [42] provides a potential explanation, as the loss of RabGGT-β subunit might impair both GGT-2 and GGT-3 activities.

In this study, we generated the first skeletal muscle-specific RabGGT-β subunit KO model. Compared to Hmgcr-DimKO mice, Rabggtb-DimKO mice displayed a later onset of myopathy and less severe symptoms. Since both GGT-2 and GGT-3 were shown to be functionally compromised, it remains unclear whether impaired Rab prenylation alone is entirely responsible for the phenotype. In addition, GGOH-GGPP is also involved in the biosynthesis of ubiquinone (CoQ10) and vitamin K (menaquinone) [43], and the reported relationship between CoQ10 and statin-induced myopathy remains controversial [[44], [45], [46], [47]]. However, our data are congruent with earlier in vitro and in vivo findings by Wagner et al. [11] and Detter et al. [41]. Future studies using targeted KO of RabGGT-α or PTAR-1 subunit should decisively delineate the distinct contributions of GGT-2 versus GGT-3. Crucially, double KO (Hmgcr/Rabggtb-DimKO) mice exhibited pronounced and accelerated myopathy by 17 days after Dox induction—whereas Rabggtb-DimKO mice remained subclinical at that stage—implying that impaired Rab prenylation potentiates the effects of GGPP deficiency in skeletal muscle. Additionally, our observation that mitochondrial anomalies precede an increase in serum CK levels in both Hmgcr- and Rabggtb-DimKO mice underscores mitochondrial dysfunction as a pathogenesis of mevalonate pathway disruption.

In the present study, myopathy of both slow-twitch and fast-twitch muscle fibers were observed in Hmgcr-mKO mice, a mouse model of statin-induced myopathy, and the administration of GGOH (a precursor of GGPP) rescued this myopathy. In addition, mitochondrial abnormalities were observed prior to the onset of myopathy in Hmgcr or Rabggtb KO mice, suggesting that they contribute to the onset of statin-induced myopathy. Hmgcr/Rabggtb-DimKO mice exhibited significantly more severe symptoms compared with Hmgcr-DimKO mice, indicating that impaired prenylation (geranylgeranylation) via GGT-2 and GGT-3, which use GGPP as a substrate, is partly responsible for the development of myopathy in Hmgcr KO mice.
