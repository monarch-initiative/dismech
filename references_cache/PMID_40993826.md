BACKGROUND: Lenz-Majewski Syndrome (LMS) is a rare multisystem genetic disorder 
characterized by intellectual disability, cutis laxa, craniofacial 
abnormalities, and distinct dental features. It is caused by gain-of-function 
mutations in the Phosphatidylserine Synthase 1 (PTDSS1) gene, which plays a key 
role in phospholipid biosynthesis. Among the craniofacial and dental 
manifestations, a progeroid facial appearance with prognathism, midface 
hypoplasia, large auricles, enamel dysplasia, delayed tooth eruption, and 
malocclusion are commonly observed. Due to the rarity and phenotypic variability 
of LMS, individual case reports remain essential for expanding the clinical and 
molecular understanding of the syndrome.
CASE PRESENTATION: We report a 13-year-old male diagnosed with Lenz-Majewski 
Syndrome (LMS) through whole exome sequencing, which identified a heterozygous 
de novo PTDSS1 variant (c.284G > A; p.R95Q), not previously documented in LMS 
cases. Clinical evaluation revealed moderate intellectual disability, cutis 
laxa, a progeroid facial appearance, and pronounced prognathism. Intraoral and 
radiographic examinations demonstrated enamel hypoplasia, taurodontism, delayed 
eruption of permanent teeth, a horizontally positioned upper lateral incisor, 
and a large follicular cyst in the maxilla. Orthodontic intervention using 
Hawley appliances was employed to stimulate the oral mucosa and support the 
eruption of delayed permanent teeth.
CONCLUSION: This case expands the phenotypic and genotypic understanding of LMS 
and documents novel dental findings—particularly taurodontism—in association 
with a previously unreported PTDSS1 variant. It underscores the importance of 
early dental assessment and multidisciplinary care in managing LMS. Recognizing 
oral manifestations can facilitate timely diagnosis and personalized treatment 
strategies, contributing to improved quality of life in affected individuals.

Lenz-Majewski Syndrome (LMS), also referred to as Lenz-Majewski hyperostotic dwarfism, is a rare genetic disorder characterized by a constellation of clinical features, including skeletal dysplasia, craniofacial abnormalities, and intellectual disability. The syndrome is primarily caused by gain-of-function mutations in the Phosphatidylserine Synthase 1 (PTDSS1) gene, which encodes phosphatidylserine synthase 1, an enzyme involved in phospholipid biosynthesis. The clinical presentation of LMS is diverse, with skeletal abnormalities being one of its most prominent features. Patients typically exhibit generalized craniotubular hyperostosis, which leads to progressive skeletal sclerosis and dwarfism [1–3].

The dysplastic changes in the bones are often accompanied by distinctive craniofacial features such as brachycephaly, midface hypoplasia, and a prominent forehead [4]. Oral and dental manifestations in LMS patients include dental enamel dysplasia [4–6], and delayed dental eruption [2, 4]. Dental enamel dysplasia, a common finding, is characterized by abnormal enamel formation that increases susceptibility to caries and other dental complications [4]. Additionally, delayed tooth eruption is commonly reported, often necessitating early dental interventions to manage potential complications, such as malocclusion or spacing issues [7]. The pathophysiology underlying these dental anomalies is likely linked to the broader developmental disruptions caused by PTDSS1 mutations. The enzyme encoded by PTDSS1 plays a crucial role in lipid metabolism, and its dysregulation can affect cellular processes involved in the development and maintenance of dental tissues [1–3].

LMS is characterized by progressive hyperostosis of craniofacial bones, particularly the skull base and jawbones, which contributes to prognathism and may result in misaligned dentition [1–4, 8]. In addition to dental enamel dysplasia and delayed dental eruption other oral manifestations may include malocclusion and periodontal issues. The delayed eruption may result in misaligned teeth, necessitating orthodontic treatment to address functional and aesthetic concerns [9]. Moreover, hypoplastic alveolar processes, noted in some patients, can further complicate dental health by compromising the stability and support of teeth [4, 10].

The literature indicates that documented cases of LMS remain extremely limited, with approximately 16 sporadic cases reported between its initial description in 1974 and 2018 [2, 4]. This rarity underscores the challenges associated with its diagnosis and management. The present case report consolidates clinical and radiological observations of oral and dental features associated with LMS, emphasizing the syndrome’s phenotypic variability. By integrating recent dentistry advancements in the field particularly in diagnostic imaging, and multidisciplinary treatment planning. This report aims to provide a comprehensive framework to enhance the diagnosis and management of this complex condition.

The patient, a 13-year-old boy, was born as the first and only child of non-consanguineous parents. The mother was 30 years old and the father 36 years old at the time of delivery. Both parents were of normal height, measuring 155 cm (mother) and 175 cm (father). The patient was delivered via cesarean section at 38 weeks of gestation following an uneventful pregnancy through in vitro fertilization (IVF). At birth, his weight was 2,700 g, his length was 47 cm, and his head circumference was 33 cm.

At 2 months of age, clinical examination revealed an enlarged anterior fontanel, which is a normal finding as it is reported to close after 2 years of age. Developmental milestones were delayed: head control was achieved at 3 months, independent walking at 19 months, and meaningful speech at 4.5 years with the support of special education. His mental development was moderately impaired, with a total intelligence quotient of 48. The patient communicates effectively in familiar environments but has delayed fine motor skills, such as writing. He also displays repetitive stereotypical movements, including knocking, shaking, and hand play.

At the follow-up at 5.5 years of age, it was noted that the nipples were widely spaced, pectus excavatum was present, no organomegaly or cardiac murmur was detected, and global developmental delay was observed, with reported deficits in verbal expression skills, fine motor abilities, general knowledge, comprehension, perception, numerical cognition, visual attention, immediate auditory attention, hand-eye coordination, auditory perception, vocabulary, auditory processing abilities, and visual discrimination skills. During the same follow-up, it was also noted that the patient was uncooperative during the back examination to exclude scoliosis.

The patient had abnormal facies characterized by large and prominent auricles, prominent nose with long nasal tip, relative short philtrum, macrostomia with downturned corners, thin lips where the upper lip is almost inverted, and prognathism (Fig. 1a and b). The short philtrum relative to the wide mouth and the prognathism contributed to the progeroid phenotype of the patient reminiscent of the short vertical of occlusion seen in old edentulous patients. Subcutaneous veins were prominent on the face, forehead, temporal region, and trunk.

Lateral cephalometric analysis (Fig. 2b) revealed a decreased anterior cranial base length compared to normal values [Anterior cranial base length: 46 mm]. Since the nasomaxillary complex is attached to the cranial base, a reduction in anterior cranial base length may result in a retrusive maxillary position. Although the maxillary position values appear within normal limits [Maxillary depth: 90 degrees], considering the reduced cranial base length [SNA angle: 79 degrees], the maxilla can be assessed as being in a retrusive position. In contrast, the mandibular length falls within normal limits [Mandibular corpus length: 63 mm]. The increased values observed in the mandibular sagittal position [SNB angle: 88 degrees] are likely related to the shortened cranial base length. Additionally, the three-dimensional developmental deficiency of the nasomaxillary complex contributes to a horizontal facial growth pattern [FMA angle: 19 degrees]. In conclusion, the severe skeletal Class III relationship [ANB angle: −9 degrees] can be attributed to deficiencies in the cranial base and nasomaxillary complex.

At 13 years, the failure of eruption of certain permanent teeth in the maxilla prompted the patient’s mother to seek further evaluation at the Department of Pedodontics. The maxilla exhibited only four erupted permanent teeth—specifically, the central incisors and first premolars—and the palate appeared notably shallow with limited vertical depth and a relatively flat contour (Fig. 3a). Enamel opacities ranging from white to yellow-brown were identified on the upper right central incisor, while white enamel opacities were observed on the upper left central incisor (Fig. 3d). The white opacities were consistent with enamel hypocalcification, whereas the yellow-brown opacities were indicative of enamel hypoplasia. The eruption of the first deciduous tooth was documented at the age of one year. The teeth exhibited a yellowish discoloration, and a significant number were subsequently extracted due to extensive dental caries, as reported in the patient’s historical dental records. A review of the records revealed that primary teeth were treated under general anaesthesia by one of the authors (B.B.A.) in 2013. Additionally, delayed eruption of the permanent dentition was observed, with the first permanent mandibular incisors not appearing until at the age of nine. At the chronological age of 10 years, the patient exhibited significant delays in dental development. Clinical and radiographic evaluations revealed that the maxillary central incisors had not yet erupted, while the mandibular central incisors had only recently initiated eruption. Additionally, the first permanent molars were retained beneath the oral mucosa and were not clinically visible. Based on these findings, the patient’s dental age was estimated to be approximately 6 years, indicating a developmental lag of approximately 4 years relative to chronological age. In 2021, a follicular cyst (Fig. 4b) measuring 2 × 2.5 × 3 cm in the upper left maxilla, along with the associated tooth (tooth number 23), was enucleated under general anaesthesia. Radiographic examination revealed a horizontally impacted left lateral incisor in the anterior maxilla and taurodontism on tooth number 16, 24, 26 (Fig. 4c).

The patient was referred to the Department of Orthodontics to accelerate the eruption of delayed teeth. Removable orthodontic appliances, specifically Hawley appliances, were used to stimulate the oral mucosa and promote tooth eruption. These appliances were intentionally extended to cover the impacted anterior and posterior teeth in both the upper and lower jaws, aiming to stimulate eruption through a pressure effect generated by chewing. To achieve this, the patient was instructed to wear both appliances continuously, including during meals. A follow-up radiograph (Fig. 4d) obtained one year later revealed accelerated tooth eruption, with the previously horizontally positioned upper left lateral incisor aligned with the expected eruption path.

Whole exome sequencing identified a mutation in the PTDSS1 gene, confirming a diagnosis of Lenz-Majewski Syndrome (LMS). Molecular analysis revealed a heterozygous de novo mutation at Chr8:g.97296349G > A, corresponding to c.284G > A (p.R95Q) at the nucleotide and amino acid levels, respectively. The detected variant is classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar database. Sanger sequencing analysis confirmed the absence of this variant in both parents, supporting its de novo origin.

LMS was initially reported by Braham in 1969 under the designation Camuratti-Angelmann’s syndrome [13]. Subsequently, in 1974, Lenz and Majewski characterized the condition in a patient presenting with hyperostotic dwarfism and proposed the eponym Lenz-Majewski syndrome to describe this distinct clinical entity [14]. Subsequent reports [6, 10, 11, 15] have confirmed the presence of similar manifestations in additional patients, though the total number of documented cases remains low.

The patient’s moderate intellectual disability and stereotypical repetitive movements align with previously reported neurological and developmental phenotypic characteristics of LMS. While intellectual disabilities of varying severity are commonly observed, the patient’s IQ of 48 and his ability to communicate effectively in familiar environments are consistent with findings in other mild LMS cases [4, 9]. However, the patient’s cooperation during clinical procedures—particularly in the orthodontic phase—was limited. This non-compliance in wearing both upper and lower removable appliances may have influenced the full effectiveness of the orthodontic intervention, and should be considered when interpreting treatment outcomes.

Follow-up examinations confirmed delayed closure of the anterior fontanelle, which remained open beyond the expected timeframe. Delayed closure of the anterior fontanel by 2 years in this patient is consistent with earlier reports describing delayed or large fontanels as typical LMS features [11, 12]. Similarly, the presence of cutis laxa and prominent subcutaneous veins on the face and body reflects the common dermatological and connective tissue abnormalities seen in LMS [4, 12]. These findings are in agreement with previously documented cases reporting similar progeroid facial appearances, cutis laxa, and visible veins [11, 12].

Skeletal anomalies, such as phalangeal shortening and bilateral simian lines, are recurrent findings in LMS. Hypoplastic or absent phalanges and brachydactyly have been described in earlier studies [9, 12].

LMS is also characterized by craniofacial abnormalities and dental anomalies that can significantly impact an individual’s quality of life. Previous reports have detailed dental enamel dysplasia, and delayed tooth eruption as common dental manifestations of LMS [11]. In the current case, delayed eruption of permanent teeth and enamel dysplasia were observed, with findings aligning closely with a Japanese case of LMS [4]. The enamel opacities and horizontally impacted teeth in the maxilla further resonate with descriptions of hypocalcification and enamel hypoplasia reported in the literature [9, 12]. Additionally, the formation of follicular cysts around anterior teeth and their subsequent surgical extirpation aligns with findings in previous report [4]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report suggesting that taurodontism in some permanent teeth may be observed as an oro-dental feature in patients diagnosed with LMS.

Prior research emphasizes the importance of recognizing craniofacial and dental anomalies as significant components of LMS. While studies such as those by Piard et al. focused on the genetic aspects of LMS [2], others like Sousa et al. have linked PTDSS1 mutations to the craniofacial and dental manifestations of the syndrome, further stressing the need for dental care and monitoring [1]. Multidisciplinary care involving pediatricians, geneticists, and dental professionals is essential for the early detection and management of dental issues, particularly given the increased risk of caries and periodontal disease associated with enamel dysplasia [4, 7, 20]. Preventive strategies, including fluoride treatments and dietary counseling, can help mitigate the impact of dental anomalies on overall health.

To date, only a limited number of pathogenic variants in the PTDSS1 gene have been associated with LMS [1, 11, 12]. In the present case, molecular analysis revealed a heterozygous de novo PTDSS1 variant (c.284G > A; p.R95Q), which has not been previously reported in individuals diagnosed with LMS, to our knowledge. Although this variant is listed in the ClinVar database (Variation ID: 429519) with a classification of “likely pathogenic,” there are currently no published case reports linking it directly to LMS. The absence of this variant in both parents, as confirmed by Sanger sequencing, supports its de novo origin. Given the patient’s clinical presentation and the pathogenic potential of the identified variant, this case may contribute novel insights into the expanding genotypic spectrum of LMS and further support emerging genotype-phenotype correlations. Ongoing research into the molecular mechanisms underlying LMS may pave the way for targeted therapies that address some of the syndrome’s clinical manifestations. Additionally, genetic counseling for families affected by LMS is crucial, as the identification of PTDSS1 mutations provides critical insights into inheritance patterns and future reproductive risks [1, 7, 18].

As with all single-case reports, the findings presented here should be interpreted with caution. Although the identified PTDSS1 variant is classified as likely pathogenic, functional studies were not performed to confirm its role in the disease mechanism. Limited cooperation during certain examinations—such as the orthodontic treatment process—posed challenges due to the patient’s difficulty in wearing both upper and lower appliances continuously. This also created limitations for the supervising clinician in ensuring consistent appliance use and effective treatment monitoring.

In conclusion, this case report contributes to the evolving clinical and genetic understanding of LMS by presenting a previously unreported PTDSS1 variant (c.284G > A; p.R95Q) alongside novel dental findings. The observation of taurodontism may expand the recognized dental phenotype in LMS. The case also underscores the importance of multidisciplinary management—including pediatric, genetic, and dental specialists—for addressing the diverse needs of affected individuals. As new cases are documented, comprehensive clinical characterization and molecular analysis will remain essential for refining genotype–phenotype correlations and guiding individualized care strategies.
