# Prevalence and risk factors for chalazion in an older veteran population.
**Authors:** Patel S, Tohme N, Gorrin E, Kumar N, Goldhagen B, Galor A
**Journal:** Br J Ophthalmol (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1136/bjophthalmol-2020-318420](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjophthalmol-2020-318420)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Chalazia are common inflammatory eyelid lesions, but their epidemiology remains understudied. This retrospective case-control study examined the prevalence, risk factors and geographic distribution of chalazia in a large veteran population.

METHODS: Data on all individuals seen at a Veterans Affairs (VA) clinic between October 2010 and October 2015 were extracted from the VA health database. Subjects were grouped based on International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) code for chalazion. Univariable logistic regression modelling was used to identify clinical and demographic factors associated with chalazion presence, followed by multivariable modelling to examine which factors predicted risk concomitantly. All cases were mapped across the continental US using geographic information systems modelling to examine how prevalence rates varied geographically.

RESULTS: Overall, 208 720 of 3 453 944 (6.04%) subjects were diagnosed with chalazion during the study period. Prevalence was highest in coastal regions. The mean age of the population was 69.32±13.9 years and most patients were male (93.47%), white (77.13%) and non-Hispanic (93.72%). Factors associated with chalazion risk included smoking (OR=1.12, p<0.0005), conditions of the tear film (blepharitis (OR=4.84, p<0.0005), conjunctivitis (OR=2.78, p<0.0005), dry eye (OR=3.0, p<0.0005)), conditions affecting periocular skin (eyelid dermatitis (OR=2.95, p<0.0005), rosacea (OR=2.50, p<0.0005)), allergic conditions (history of allergies (OR=1.56, p<0.0005)) and systemic disorders (gastritis (OR=1.54, p<0.0005), irritable bowel syndrome (OR=1.45, p<0.0005), depression (OR=1.35, p<0.0005), anxiety (OR=1.31, p<0.0005)). These factors remained associated with chalazion risk when examined concomitantly.

CONCLUSION: Periocular skin, eyelid margin and tear film abnormalities were most strongly associated with risk for chalazion. The impact of environmental conditions on risk for chalazion represents an area in need of further study.

Chalazia are inflammatory nodules that develop on the eyelid surface as a result of lipogranulomatous inflammation secondary to tarsal plate Meibomian gland duct obstruction. 1 Typically slow-growing, non-tender, and nodular, chalazia are considered to be the most common inflammatory eyelid lesion in the general population today. 2 Chalazia tend to be self-limited, with conservative measures including warm compresses leading to spontaneous resolution with favorable outcomes. 3 When conservative measures fail, or for very large lesions that obstruct vision, intralesional corticosteroid injections or excision may be indicated. 4 , 5

Only a small handful of studies have thus far examined the epidemiology of chalazia. For example, a retrospective Indian study of 1,982,058 patients who presented for an eye examination at a hospital network between 2010 and 2019 found a prevalence of 0.6% (11,270 individuals) at a rate comparable across sexes [50.9% male (n=5,738) vs. 49.1% female (n=5,532], and with higher frequency in children (1.0%; 2,656 of 280,034) than adults (0.5%; 8,614 of 1,702,024). Distribution of chalazion cases increased from the first decade of life (12.7%), peaked in the third decade (30.8%), and then declined thereafter from the fourth decade (15.1%) with the lowest case rates observed in elderly individuals (5.5% and 3.4% in fifth and sixth decades, respectively). 6

Similarly, only a few studies have examined associated risk factors for chalazion. One Israeli study retrospectively compared 9,119 individuals with chalazion to 9,119 healthy controls, all of who were seen at a health maintenance organization in Israel between 2000 and 2008. In univariable analyses, eyelid abnormalities were the strongest associated variables, including blepharitis (odds ratio (OR) = 6.20, p<0.0001) and rosacea (OR=2.90, p<0.0001). Other associated factors included gastritis (OR=1.40), p<0.0001), inflammatory bowel syndrome (OR=1.70, p<0.0001), anxiety (OR=1.50, p<0.0001), and smoking (OR=1.20, p<0.0001)). 7 Studies have also reported links between nutritional deficiencies and chalazion. For example, an Iranian study on 107 subjects (52 with chalazion and 55 controls) noted that individuals with chalazion had lower Vitamin A levels than controls (0.27 ± 0.10 g/ml and 0.31 ± 0.07 g/ml respectively, p=0.02), and subjects with recurrent chalazia were had lower Vitamin A levels than those with primary chalazion (0.24 ± 0.09 g/ml vs. 0.29 ± 0.13 g/ml; p=0.03). 8 Low Vitamin A levels have been similarly linked to chalazia in other studies. 9 , 10 Other studies have focused on the association between serum cholesterol levels and risk for chalazia, 11 , 12 with one Egyptian study reporting that individuals with recurrent multiple chalazia had significantly higher serum cholesterol compared to healthy controls. 12

Due to limited existing information on chalazion epidemiology and associated risk factors, we used the national Veterans Affairs (VA) database to extract data on all individuals seen in a VA eye clinic across the continental United States (US). Our goal was to evaluate the geographic distribution of chalazion cases and to identify associated risk factors to chalazion presence among documented demographics and underlying co-morbidities.

This study was approved by the Miami VA Institutional Review Board (IRB) - 3011.01.

The study population consisted of all individuals seen at a Veterans Affairs (VA) eye clinic across the continental US between October 1st, 2010 and October 1st, 2015. The VA serves a diverse population of patients seeking treatment for ocular disorders, including those with refractive errors, medical conditions that require monitoring (e.g. diabetes), and ocular conditions. Individuals were split into two groups based on the presence or absence of chalazion, which was determined by International Classification of Diseases-9 (ICD-9) codes (373.2-chalazion, 373.12-internal hordeolum, and 373.11-external hordeolum). The Miami VA institutional review board (IRB) approved this retrospective study, and the methods described in this study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

For each subject, information on demographics (age, sex, race, ethnicity, body mass index [categorized as overweight, obese, morbidly obese]) and clinical characteristics (medical history and medication usage) were collected. Co-morbid conditions of interest assessed by ICD-9 codes included ocular diseases (e.g. blepharitis, conjunctivitis, dry eye disease (DED), ocular hypertension, glaucoma), periocular eyelid conditions (e.g. rosacea, seborrheic dermatitis, eyelid dermatitis, eczema), allergic conditions (e.g. allergic rhinitis, asthma, allergic urticaria, personal history of asthma), and non-ocular diseases (e.g. diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hypothyroidism, gastritis, irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), anxiety, depression). Medication information was also extracted and aggregated by class including oral agents (statins, thyroid hormone replacement, beta blockers, diuretics, angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs)) and topical medications (beta-blockers, prostaglandin analogs, α2 agonists, miotics, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors). ICD-9 codes that were used for the purposes of this analysis are presented in Table 1.

Geographic information system mapping via ArcGIS 10.8 was used to chart and examine the distribution of chalazion cases across the country. 13 Cases were geo-coded using latitude and longitude coordinates of each patient’s treatment facility, which were determined using each facility’s ZIP code. From the entire database, a total of 130 unique ZIP codes were documented and used in our analyses.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0. 14 First, descriptive statistics were utilized in order to summarize demographic and clinical data of the entire study population. Binary logistic regressions were run to identify independent univariable relationships between demographic and clinical variables and risk for chalazion presence. A forward stepwise multivariable logistic regression model, which included all significant variables under univariable analyses and variables linked to chalazion risk in prior studies, was developed to examine these relationships concomitantly.

The study population consisted of 3,453,944 subjects seeking care at a VA eye clinic within the continental US. Overall, the prevalence of chalazion in the population was 6.04% (208,720/3,453,944). The mean age of the population was 69.32 ± 13.9 years (range 21-108 years), with case rates varying across age groups (Figure 1). The majority of patients were male (93.50% of patients), white (71.80%), and non-Hispanic (89.60%). The prevalence of ocular and periocular eyelid diseases was higher in individuals with chalazion compared to those without (Table 1).

By geo-mapping each case by ZIP code, we examined how case frequencies varied across the US. Geographic distribution indicated variation in prevalence across the US, with higher prevalence observed in cities near large bodies of water (e.g. Pacific Coast, Atlantic Coast, Great Lakes region) (Figure 2). Among all cities, the highest case rates were observed in Detroit, Michigan (36.95%) followed by Fresno, California (21.84%), Portland, Oregon (19.93%), Reno, Nevada (18.09%), Cincinnati, Ohio (17.91%), White City, Oregon (17.60%), and Los Angeles, California (15.73%).

Univariable logistic regression was used to assess how each demographic and clinical variable associated with chalazion risk (Table 2). For demographics, chalazion risk was associated with being a white (OR=1.12, p<0.0005), Hispanic (OR=1.10, p<0.0005) male (OR=1.02, p<0.0005). Obesity (OR=1.32, p<0.0005) and smoking (OR=1.12, p<0.0005) were also associated with chalazion risk. The strongest magnitude relationships were with ocular surface abnormalities, including blepharitis (OR=4.84, p<0.0005), conjunctivitis (OR=2.78, p<0.0005), and DED (OR=3.0, p<0.0005). This was followed by disorders of the periocular skin, including eyelid dermatitis (OR=2.95, p<0.0005), rosacea (OR=2.50, p<0.0005), eczema (OR=1.70, p<0.0005), and seborrheic dermatitis (OR=1.80, p<0.0005). Chalazion presence was also associated with allergic diseases, including allergic rhinitis (OR=1.49, p<0.0005) and personal history of allergies (OR=1.56, p<0.0005). Systemic diseases like gastritis (OR=1.54, p<0.0005), irritable bowel syndrome (OR=1.45, p<0.0005), and hypertension were also associated with lesion presence, as were mental health disorders like depression (OR=1.35, p<0.0005) and anxiety (OR=1.31, p<0.0005). Most medications were positively associated with chalazion presence, such as statins (OR=1.11, p<0.0005). However, topical beta-blockers were associated with a decreased risk (OR=0.62, p=0.02).

Multivariable regression modeling was used to understand how variables identified as significantly linked to chalazion presence under univariable analyses modulated risk when considered concomitantly (Table 2). Variables were included in the model if they were significantly associated with chalazion on univariable analysis, or if prior studies linked them to chalazion. Results indicated that smoking, obesity, ocular surface pathologies, periocular eyelid pathologies, allergic diseases, gastrointestinal diseases, and mental health disorders remained associated with chalazion presence (OR>1) (Figure 3). Conversely, three classes of medications (statins, topical CA inhibitors, topical beta blockers) were found to be protective (OR<1).

We found a chalazion prevalence of 6.04% in individuals seen in VA eye clinics across the US between October 2010-2015. This frequency is higher than that reported in prior studies. For example, the retrospective Indian study of 1,982,058 patients reported a prevalence of 0.57% (11,270/1,982,058) over a 9 year period (2010-2019). 6 Similarly, a Nigerian study of all patients (n=13,605) seen at a university eye clinic from 1997 to 2007 reported an overall chalazion prevalence of 0.70% (n=97) in the population during the study period. 15 Importantly, there were notable differences in the subject populations examined in these studies compared to ours. Ours consisted of predominantly older, white males with a large burden of underlying conditions, while the Indian and Nigerian populations were younger and non-white (Indian study: mean age 27.48 ± 15.64 years 6 ; Nigerian study: mean age 25.30 ± 10.50 years 15 ). Beyond demographic differences, other potential contributors to the range of chalazion frequencies noted across studies include geographic variation, ease of access to health care, and motivation for seeking treatment based on cosmetic needs.

Similar to previous studies, we found that chalazion presence was associated with ocular surface and periocular skin disorders. The magnitude of our associations were similar to those reported in previous studies. For example, an Israeli study on 9,119 subjects diagnosed with chalazion seen at an health maintenance organization (HMO) from January 2000 to December 2008 reported that chalazion presence was significantly associated with increased risk for blepharitis (OR=6.2, p<0.05) and rosacea (OR=2.9, p<0.05). 7 Similarly, a British study that examined records of 53,927 patients with a rosacea diagnosis between 1995 and 2009 reported that rosacea was associated with increased risk for chalazion (OR=2.15, p<0.05), blepharitis (OR=3.57, p<0.05), DED (OR=1.78, p<0.05), and conjunctivitis (OR=1.75, p<0.05). 16 The interconnections between chalazia, ocular surface disorders, and periocular skin conditions are not surprising, as one of the proposed pathophysiologic mechanisms for chalazion formation is narrowing or blockage of the distal Meibomian gland duct. Thus, any disease that impacts Meibomian gland function, like blepharitis, 17 , 18 DED, 19 conjunctivitis, 20 rosacea, 21 or seborrheic dermatitis, 22 , 23 may predispose for chalazia. Another proposed contributor to chalazion formation is the presence of Demodex folliculorum in the lash follicles, 23 , 24 which has been reported to be more frequent in individuals with a history of rosacea. 25

Interestingly, we also identified non-ocular factors associated with chalazion, such as the gastrointestinal (GI) disorders of gastritis and IBS. Only one study has reported such associations with chalazia – the Israeli study above reported the same associations at similar magnitudes (gastritis OR=1.40 and IBS OR=1.70, p<0.05 each). 7 Plausibility does exist for this connection, with the presence of inflammation in multiple compartments has been postulated as a link between GI and ocular conditions. 7 Interestingly, H. pylori gastritis has been linked to rosacea, with one study reporting that treatment against H. pylori reduced rosacea severity. 26 Other conditions linked to H. pylori include coronary artery disease 27 and fibromyalgia, 28 and as such, it is plausible that chalazion represents yet another local manifestation of a GI disorder. We further found that anxiety and depression were linked to risk for chalazion, and these associations were also reported across literature, including in the Israeli study. 7 Again, inflammation is a potential connection between these disorders, with studies having implicated a pro-inflammatory state via changes in hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis function 29 , 30 and serum inflammatory cell levels 31 , 32 in depressive states. Interestingly, studies have also found that Meibomian glands express sex hormone receptors, which represents another link between the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, Meibomian gland dysfunction, and chalazia. 33 , 34

We further found that oral statins were slightly protective towards chalazion presence. One prior study linked higher serum cholesterol levels to recurrent and multiple chalazia. 12 Statins function to decrease serum free cholesterol, but it is unknown if they have local effects, such as on the function of Meibomian glands. However, chalazia are partially composed of free cholesterol, triacylglycerols, and free fatty acids 35 and as such, further studies are thus needed to investigate whether statins can decrease the risk of chalazion in individuals with a history of multiple or recurrent chalazia.

Geographically, we found that case distribution rates were highest in cities situated near bodies of water, with the highest rates observed in Detroit, Michigan (36.95%) and Fresno, California (21.84%). Densely populated coastal cities have elevated humidity 36 and airborne pollutants 37 levels compared to non-coastal cities. While no studies have examined the effects of these environmental variables on chalazion risk, these factors have been implicated in risk for blepharitis, 38 conjunctivitis, 39 , 40 and DED. 41 , 42 Interestingly, the frequency of internet searches for chalazion (“stye”) increases during hotter months, also linking environmental exposure to chalazion. 43 As a corollary, acne has a similar pathogenesis to chalazia, characterized by excess sebum production and modified lipid composition leading to pilosebaceous duct blockage, bacterial colonization, and inflammation. 44 Studies have linked acne flare-ups to environmental exposures, including higher temperature and humidity. 45 For example, an Indian study found that in 111 male subjects with season-related acne, most subjects experienced symptomatic flares during summer months with elevated ambient temperature and relative humidity (n = 80; 72.07%), while fewer experienced no change (n = 12; 10.81%) or improvement in symptoms (n = 19; 17.12%) during this time (p<0.01). 46 Similarly, air pollution has been associated with poor skin quality. 47 , 48 Chalazion has been further linked to environmental conditions and widespread use of facemasks during the Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) era. 49 , 50 Literature suggests that elevations in local oculofacial temperature and humidity levels occur with facemask use, 51 which may explain the observed connections to both acne and chalazia.

Our findings must be considered bearing in mind both the study’s strengths and limitations. A strength of our study was the availability of a large dataset from which to examine chalazion risk. However, limitations of using a database design are a reliance on ICD-9 codes for classification which could not be verified against clinical notes and the lack of information on subsequent chalazion treatment. Furthermore, the veteran population is not representative of the general US population as it consists predominantly of elderly white males. Fortunately, despite demographic differences, our findings replicated those of prior chalazion studies, identifying ocular surface and periocular skin disorders as the strongest risk factors for chalazion. This suggests that despite demographic differences, similar risk factors are found across varied populations. Another important strength of our study was the availability of geographic location which allowed us to evaluate novel chalazion risk factors, specifically the role of environmental exposures on chalazion risk.

Overall, our study defined the prevalence and associated risk factors for chalazion in the largest US population studied to date. Our findings highlight that ocular, periocular, and systemic conditions should be considered when examining an individual with chalazion. Further data is needed, however, to understand whether modulating these conditions will reduce the burden of recurrent or severe disease. Spatially, most cases occurred in densely populated regions located by bodies of water, which tend to have higher relative humidity and ambient air pollutant levels. Moreover, elevated humidity has potential to retain airborne particles for a longer period of time, and elongated exposure to airborne particles can further increase the potential for Meibomian duct blockage. These factors may explain the anecdotal association between facemask use and chalazion in the COVID19 era, where eyelid humidity is locally elevated by the flow of air upwards (as opposed to outwards) while wearing a facemask.
