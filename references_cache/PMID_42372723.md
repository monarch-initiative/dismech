Tau pathology spreads cell to cell, but the mechanisms of intercellular tau 
transmission remain unclear. We find that the neuronal gene Arc is critical for 
the release of tau in neuronal extracellular vesicles (EVs) via a direct 
protein-protein interaction. Brain EVs purified from transgenic rTg4510 mutant 
tau mice (rTgWT) crossed with Arc knockout mice (rTgArc KO) contain less tau and 
reduced tau seeding potential. Both Arc and tau are co-packaged in mouse and 
human brain-derived EVs. Moreover, Arc levels in brain-derived EVs isolated from 
human Alzheimer's disease (AD) brains show a strong positive correlation with 
phosphorylated EV-tau levels. rTgArc KO mice have increased accumulation of 
intracellular tau and a modest increase in cell toxicity early in disease 
progression. Strikingly, intercellular tau transmission is almost absent in Arc 
KO mice. These results show that Arc is critical for the packaging of tau in 
EVs, which plays a significant role in intercellular tau transmission.

Neurodegenerative diseases are characterized by protein aggregation in specific brain regions that spread across the brain as the disease progresses. A major histopathological hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and tauopathies, such as frontotemporal dementia (FTD) and chronic traumatic encephalopathy, is intracellular neurofibrillary tangles that consist of misfolded tau protein1,2. During aging, tau becomes hyperphosphorylated, resulting in misfolding and a decreased affinity for microtubules3. Tau pathology is transmitted cell-to-cell4,5, and the spread and levels of pathological tau strongly correlate with the degree of cognitive decline in AD patients6. In AD, tau spreads through synaptically connected neuronal circuits; starting in the locus coeruleus or entorhinal cortex but eventually spreading to the hippocampus and neocortical areas7. Neuronal activity regulates tau release from neurons8,9 and high neuronal activity levels exacerbates tau pathology10, although the mechanisms of activity-dependent tau release and transmission are unclear. Tau is secreted as free protein11 or in extracellular vesicles (EVs)12–14. Healthy neurons take-up extracellular free tau by mechanisms that include LRP (low density lipoprotein receptor-related protein) mediated uptake15 and EV-tau uptake occurs by poorly delineated endocytic pathways16. When misfolded tau is taken up, it can corrupt the conformation of physiological tau through a seeding process that ultimately leads to the formation of intracellular aggregates4. However, the evidence for prion-like propagation of tau comes from animal or cellular models and clinical confirmation in humans is currently being tested17. Some of this newly misfolded tau is then released, possibly to protect cells from intracellular toxicity, continuing the cycle of cell-to-cell transmission. Released tau can be taken up by neurons or microglia, which break down toxic proteins18. However, as the disease progresses, elimination and proteostasis pathways become overloaded and contribute to the spread of tau pathology18,19. Thus, interrupting the spread of tau may be a promising therapeutic strategy for AD and other tauopathies, but the mechanisms of tau release and intercellular transmission remain poorly delineated.

Packaging of toxic proteins in EVs has been implicated in the pathology of various neurodegenerative disorders20. Exosomes, small (~30-150nm) EVs usually derived from endosomal/multi-vesicular body compartments, may be involved in the development and propagation of AD pathology18,21,22. Tau is released from neurons in EVs10 and levels of tau in neuron-derived plasma EVs strongly correlate with cognitive decline in AD patients and may serve as potential biomarkers for disease progression23–25. However, the exact type of EV, whether exosome or ectosomes26 (EVs released directly from the plasma membrane that can also be ~100nm), is unclear. EVs isolated from AD patient brains or transgenic mice expressing mutant human tau contain phosphorylated misfolded tau in EVs12,14,27–29 and can form filaments in the EV lumen13. These brain-derived human EVs can seed tau aggregation in cultured cells and in mouse brains12,16,27,28,30. EV-tau may be more potent at spreading tau pathology, as transmission may be more efficient than free tau protein12, and interfering with EV release reduces tau pathology in mice12,18,31. Nonetheless, the precise role of EV-tau in intercellular transmission of tau pathology remains to be determined. Understanding the balance and regulation of tau release has implications for therapeutic interventions using anti-tau antibodies32. If EV-tau is critical for cell-to-cell transmission, tau antibodies may be unable to target this pool of extracellular tau due to inaccessibility.

The neuronal gene Arc, a master regulator of synaptic plasticity and memory consolidation33, has been implicated in AD pathology34, 35. Early in disease pathogenesis, increased seizure activity associated with elevated Arc protein levels have been observed in AD patients35, 36 and in AD mouse models37 that include mutant tau transgenic mice38. Arc protein spontaneously forms virus-like capsids that encapsulate RNA, a property derived from its retrotransposon origins39,40. Arc is released in EVs that mediate intercellular transmission of proteins and RNAs39,40. Arc EVs are released from dendrites by neuronal activity via the I-BAR protein IRSp5341, which was recently found to also interact with tau42. Hyper-phosphorylated tau is mislocalized in dendrites/post synaptic compartments, which leads to synaptic dysfunction43–45. The Drosophila Arc (dArc) homologs, which originated independently from distinct lineages of Ty3 retrotransposons, also form virus-like capsids39,46 that are packaged in EVs that transmit RNA from the neuromuscular junction synapse into muscle cells47. dArc1 protein levels, including high-molecular-weight species indicative of capsids, are increased in a tauopathy Drosophila model (tauR406W) and dArc1 modulates tau-induced neurodegeneration48. Together, these data suggest that Arc may play an important role in regulating tau release and transmission. Here, we show that Arc plays a critical role in intercellular tau transmission by packaging and facilitating the release of seed-competent tau into neuronal EVs.

Tau and Arc are both released in membrane encapsulated EVs that are regulated by neuronal activity10,31,41. Thus, we hypothesized that Arc may play a role in the release of tau in EVs. To directly test if Arc facilitates the release of EV-tau from neurons, we transduced WT and Arc KO primary rat cortical neuron cultures41 that contain little glia with hSyn-eGFP-2A-2N4R-human-tau(P301L) after 10 days in vitro (DIV). Cell lysates and media were collected from transduced neurons at DIV 17, 48 hours after a full media change. We isolated EVs using size exclusion chromatography (SEC) and measured human tau (hTau) levels in cell lysates and EVs using a hTau specific ELISA. Total hTau levels in WT and Arc KO cell lysates are comparable, while the release of EV-hTau is significantly reduced in Arc KO primary neurons (Figure 1A).

Arc EV biogenesis and release is mediated by the I-BAR protein IRSp5341. To test whether EV-hTau release is regulated by IRSp53, we transfected Neuro2a cells with P301L hTau and Arc or IRSP53 or both. EV-hTau release was significantly enhanced when both IRSp53 and Arc are expressed (Figure 1B), but not when only Arc or IRSp53 is expressed, despite lower hTau levels in the cell lysates. Thus, the ratio of released Arc is even greater when Arc and IRSp53 are co-expressed. These data show that Arc EVs package hTau and that EV-hTau is released from neurons.

To test whether Arc regulates tau release in vivo, we isolated EVs from mouse brain tissue using ultracentrifugation, followed by SEC (Figure 1C and S1A). This protocol circumvents bulk homogenization of cells by using a razor blade to chop the brain, followed by enzymatic digestion of brain tissue, which reduces intracellular contaminants. Brain derived EVs were positive for common EV markers49, such as tetraspanins (CD9), syntenin, and Alix, but were negative for the nuclear histone protein H3 (Figure S1B). Nanoparticle tracking showed an enrichment for EVs with an average size of ~130 nm and negative-stain transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images showed similar sized vesicles (Figure S1C). Using this protocol, we isolated brain EVs from 4-month-old rTg4510 (rTgWT) mice. These mice express multiple copies of the mutant (P301L) hTau transgene in forebrain excitatory neurons, driven by the CaMKII promoter, and develop tau aggregates50. To identify whether hTau is packaged in brain-derived EVs, we first assessed the specificity of tau antibodies for hTau (Tau 13– tau amino acids 2-1851; Tau 22– putative oligomeric tau52; AT8 – phosphorylated 202/205 tau53; Tau Y9– phosphorylated tau at tyrosine 1854; Tau HT7 – tau amino acids aa 15-16355) using brain homogenates containing endogenous mouse tau or both mouse and hTau (Figure S2A). All antibodies detected hTau, with Tau22 and AT8 also detecting endogenous mouse tau (Figure S2A). We detected hTau in brain-derived EVs, including full-length (molecular weight of ~55kDa, which corresponds to the 0N4R isoform expressed in rTg4510 mice) and phosphorylated forms of hTau (Figure 1C and S2B). We also detected Arc, hTau, and syntenin in brain-derived EVs isolated from 4-month-old rTgWT mice that were protected from proteinase K degradation in the absence of detergent (Figure 1D), indicating they are packaged inside EVs.

To determine if Arc facilitates the release of hTau in brain EVs, we crossed rTgWT mice with germline Arc knock-out mice56 (rTgArc KO). rTgWT and rTgArc KO mice express comparable amounts of total hTau in brain homogenates (Figure S2C). Strikingly, hTau levels in EVs isolated from rTgArc KO are dramatically decreased, as assessed by western blot and ELISA (Figure 1E–F). However, overall brain EV production is comparable between rTgWT and rTgArc KO mice, as assessed by nanoparticle tracking analysis (Figure 1G) and EV marker levels (Figure S2D), indicating that the lack of Arc does not affect general brain EV production in vivo.

To assess whether hTau and Arc are co-packaged within brain-derived EVs, which are a mix of neuron and glia-derived EVs, we performed immunogold EM and observed brain EVs that contain Arc alone, hTau alone, or both proteins (Figure 1H and S2E). When EVs were labeled for either Arc or hTau using 6 nm gold particles, ~12% of all EVs were positive for each protein (Figure 1G). Conversely, when EVs were double immunogold labeled using 6 nm gold particles for Arc and 15 nm gold particles for hTau, ~3.5% of EVs were double immunogold labeled (Figure 1H). However, the large 15 nm gold particles only label ~4% of hTau containing EVs, and thus the true frequency of Arc-hTau EVs is an underestimate. This is most likely due to poor penetrance of bigger 15 nm gold particles through the EV membrane, as detergents create pores that can be < 15nm57. Together, these data show that Arc is critical for packaging tau into brain EVs in vivo.

Neurons take up extracellular misfolded tau, which may then corrupt physiological tau and seed tau aggregates4. Moreover, EVs isolated from AD patients can seed tau pathology12. To determine the seeding potential of EV-hTau, we conducted a FRET-based tau seeding assay in HEK293 tau biosensor cells58. Tau biosensor HEK cells stably express hTau(P301S)RD-CFP/YFP. The hTau P301S repeat domain (RD) only aggregates in the presence of hTau competent seeds, generating a FRET signal, as we observe when transfected with purified recombinant hTau (Figure 2A). To determine the seeding capability of brain EVs, we transfected 10μg of rTgWT or rTgArc KO brain EVs into HEK biosensor cells and quantified the percentage and intensity of FRET positive cells by flow cytometry, as a readout of hTau aggregation. rTgWT brain-derived EVs robustly induced FRET positive cells (Figure 2A–B). In contrast, rTgArc KO EVs induced very few FRET positive cells and the total amount of aggregated hTau (FRET density) was significantly reduced in biosensor HEK cells transfected with rTgArc KO EVs (Figure 2A–B). These observations show that brain-derived rTgWT EV-hTau can seed hTau aggregation, while rTgArc KO brain EVs do not have significant hTau seeding potential, most likely due to the low levels of EV-hTau. This suggests that Arc is involved in the packaging of seed competent EV-tau.

Mouse rTgWT brain-derived EVs contain Arc and hTau. However, mutant hTau is highly over-expressed in this mouse line. To determine whether human brain-derived EVs contain Arc and hTau, we obtained flash-frozen postmortem Brodmann area 8/9 prefrontal cortex tissue from 6 age-matched non-AD controls, and Braak stage six AD patients (see Table S1 for patient details). We isolated EVs from flash-frozen post-mortem human brain tissue as previously decribed12, which show characteristic EV morphology (Figure 3A) and are smaller than 200nm (Figure 3B). There were no detectable differences in overall EV morphology or size between EVs isolated from control and Braak stage six brains (Figure 3B). We found no significant differences between non-AD and AD brains in the total levels of syntenin and Arc in brain EVs (Figure 3C) or in the post-mortem brain tissue homogenates (Figure S3A). We also observed no significant difference in total or phosphorylated hTau (AT8) levels in brain EVs (Figure 3C) but did observe an increase in total phosphorylated hTau in AD patient brain homogenates (Figure S3A). We noticed that Arc and phosphorylated hTau levels in EVs are positively correlated and found that brain EVs that had high levels of Arc also had high levels of phosphorylated hTau in both non-AD (r=0.74, p=0.08) and AD samples (r=0.89, p=0.01) (Figure 3D). Corroborating findings from mouse derived brain EVs, we found that human brain EVs from Braak six patient brains contain Arc-hTau double labeled EVs (Figure 3E and S3B). The percentage of Arc only (~10%), hTau only (~15%), and Arc-hTau EVs (~4%) were comparable to mouse EVs (Figure 3E). These data show that Arc and hTau are co-packaged into EVs in human brains, similar to hTau in rTgWT mouse EVs. These data support the hypothesis that Arc and hTau are co-packaged in human brain EVs and that Arc drives the release of phosphorylated hTau in EVs.

Given the specificity of Arc-dependent EV-hTau release, we posited that Arc packages hTau into EVs via a direct protein interaction. We purified GST, GST-Arc, or GST-Endophilin from E. coli as described previously39 and immobilized the purified proteins on beads, followed by incubation with HEK293 cell lysates from untransfected cells (UT), or cells transfected with WT or P301L hTau. WT and P301L hTau bind purified Arc protein, but not GST or GST-Endophilin (Figure 4A). We also observed that purified recombinant mutant hTau protein binds purified Arc protein (Figure 4B), indicating a specific and direct interaction.

Since Arc EV release is mediated by IRSp5341, we also determined whether Arc, hTau, and IRSp53 interact in vivo. We conducted immunoprecipitation experiments from mouse cortex. To induce Arc expression, 4-month-old rTgWT mice were placed in an enriched environment (toys and novel objects in the home cage) for 6 hours41. Arc was immunoprecipitated from cortical lysates and the elutes were blotted for Arc, hTau, and IRSp53. Both hTau and IRSp53 co-immunoprecipitated with Arc (Figure 4C), showing that Arc, hTau, and IRSp53 interact in the mouse brain in vivo. To determine whether both Arc and IRSp53 also interact with hTau in human brains, we immunoprecipitated hTau from flash-frozen post-mortem Brodmann area 8/9 prefrontal cortical tissue of a non-AD control, a Braak stage two AD patient, and a Braak stage six AD patient (Table 1). Both Arc and IRSp53 co-immunoprecipitated with hTau in control and AD patient brains (Figure 4D). To determine if the phosphorylation of hTau influences Arc-hTau binding, we incubated GST-Arc with purified SF9 phosphorylated hTau or purified SF9 hTau that had been incubated with phosphatases. Purified Arc binds phosphorylated hTau with much higher affinity than non-phosphorylated hTau (Figure 4E). Together, these data show that Arc directly binds hTau, with higher affinity for phosphorylated hTau, and forms a complex with hTau in both mouse and human cortex.

To gain insight into how hTau interacts with Arc, we conducted all-atom molecular dynamics simulations using full-length human and mouse Arc with hTau 2N4R. The simulations indicate that hTau remains largely disordered in complex with both human and mouse Arc, forming multivalent, dynamic interactions (Figure S4A–B). Despite the lack of regular secondary structures, the aggregation-prone hexapeptide motifs of hTau, which are critical for tau seeding59,60, transiently adopt a β-strand conformation in complex with Arc (Figure S4C). The interactions of the hexapeptide motifs are distinguished in interactions with Arc and appear to be more stable than those by the other parts of the hTau sequence (Figure S4D–E). The QIVY motif of hTau (residues 307-310, PHF6) in particular, contacts both the Arc coiled-coil region (residues 24-128) and the N-terminal part of the Gag domain and can form a parallel β-sheet with the Arc motif TQIF (residues 211-214) (Figure S4A). The mutant P301L hTau expressed in rTg mice, also forms mixed anti-parallel β-sheets with short β-elements of mouse Arc (Figure S4B and E). Overall, the hTau interactions with the Arc N-terminal coiled-coiled region appear to be more stable than those with the Arc Gag domain, while contacts with the C-terminal region are transient (Figure S4F–G). The P301L hTau appears to form more stable contacts with mouse Arc as compared to WT hTau, in particular through the PHF6 and PHF6* motifs, which are aggregation prone regions59,60 (Figure S4D–E). Interestingly, phosphorylated hTau (S262, Y310, S316, S324, S356) also exhibits preference for the N-terminal region of mouse Arc and forms more persistent contacts as compared to unphosphorylated Tau (Figure S4F), which is consistent with the experimental data showing stronger binding (Figure 4E). In addition to its binding with monomeric hTau, Arc may also interact with fibrillar hTau through polyelectrolyte interactions between the Arc N-terminal domain on the outside of the capsid and the hTau fuzzy coat61 (Figure S4G–I). Fuzzy interactions between oppositely charged blocks that lead to a mesoscale, dynamic protein organisation have been previously described62,63. Consistent with this model, we found that deleting the Arc N-terminal coiled-coiled domain (amino acids 21-65) abolished hTau binding (Figure S4J) and hTau binds both WT and an Arc capsid mutant (113-119aa deletion64) (Figure S4K). These results suggest that Arc binds tau in a complicated manner reminiscent of tau-microtubule interactions65.

Arc-dependent release of hTau in EVs may play a role in the elimination of intracellular toxic hTau. To test this hypothesis, we collected brains from male and female rTgWT and rTgArc KO littermates at 4-months of age, the same age where we observed significantly less hTau in rTgArc KO EVs. We assessed total hTau levels in the CA1 region of the dorsal hippocampus and observed that rTgArc KO mice have significantly increased intracellular hTau levels in individual neurons and dendrites, as compared with rTgWT mice (Figure 5A, C and S5A). We also observed fewer neurons in the CA1 cell body layer of rTgArc KO mice (Figure 5B and D). To ensure the neuronal loss observed in rTgArc KO mice is not caused by genetic deletion of Arc alone, we examined the CA1 cell layer in 2-month-old WT and Arc KO mice. NeuN immunostaining revealed no differences in NeuN-positive area within the CA1 cell layer between WT and Arc KO mice (Figure S5B). This suggests that hTau accumulates in neurons in the absence of Arc, which may ultimately cause higher levels of intracellular toxicity. To assess if intracellular hTau accumulation is associated with neuronal toxicity, we compared TUNEL staining (a measure of DNA damage associated with cell death66) in the dorsal CA1 of 4-month-old rTgWT and rTgArc KO mice. We observed higher TUNEL staining in rTgArc KO mice (Figure 5E) as determined by the size and total area of TUNEL puncta (Figure 5F–G). We did not observe significant differences in the number or intensity of Iba1 positive cells in rTgArc KO mice and no overt changes in morphology (Figure S6A–E), suggesting that microglia are not altered in rTgArc KO mice. To determine whether the intracellular total hTau accumulation and cellular toxicity observed in rTgArc KO neurons correlates with phosphorylated hTau, we measured AT8 positive hTau in the dorsal hippocampus. In contrast to total hTau, no significant differences were detected in AT8 staining between rTgWT and rTgArc KO mice (Figure S5C). To determine whether tau pathology accelerates in rTgArc KO mice at later ages, we examined hTau expression and cell death in 8-month-old rTgWT and rTgArc KO mice (Figure S7A). There were no significant differences in cell number, and either total hTau (Figure S7A–D) or AT8 positive hTau levels (Figure S7E–F) in the dorsal hippocampus between 8-month-old rTgWT and rTgArc KO mice. The levels of TUNEL staining were also not significantly different (Figure S7G–I). To further determine levels of aggregated hTau, we stained slices with ThioS and found no significant difference between genotypes at either 4- or 8-months-of-age (Figure S8). These data suggest that lack of Arc in rTg mice does not significantly accelerate tau pathology in later stages of pathogenesis.

hTau expression is high in rTg mice and it is possible that the phenotypes we observed may be due to the transgene or mouse line. To further investigate whether Arc regulates intracellular levels of hTau, we crossed 3xTg AD (3xTgWT) mice with Arc KO mice to generate 3xTgArc KO mice. 3xTg AD mice express a mutant human APP (swe) and hTau (P301L) transgene driven by the thy1 promoter, and a knock-in of mutant (M146V) PS167. 3xTg AD mice develop amyloid plaques at 6 months and significant tau pathology by 12 months67. We evaluated intracellular hTau levels in 12-month-old 3xTgWT and 3xTgArc KO mice. We imaged the CA1 region of the dorsal hippocampus and quantified hTau levels in the cell body and dendritic layer. As previously reported68, we observed sex differences at this age, with females exhibiting more severe tau pathology. There were no significant differences in intracellular hTau levels between 3xTgWT and 3xTgArc KO male mice at this age (Figure S9A–E) and males generally had low levels of tau pathology. However, 3xTgArc KO females have significantly higher hTau levels in the cell body and dendritic layers than 3xTgWT females (Figure S9F–H). 3xTgArc KO females also exhibit higher intracellular hTau levels in individual neurons, but fewer hTau positive neurons (Figure S9I–J).

Together, these data indicate that intracellular hTau accumulates in neurons in the absence of Arc early in disease progression, which exacerbates cell death but does not significantly accelerate pathology in older animals.

Intercellular transmission of tau is dependent on the efficiency of release and uptake69. While most tau is released in a free form, EV-tau may be taken up more efficiently70. To determine whether Arc plays a role in intercellular hTau transmission, we employed an hTau transmission assay that uses an AAV construct, hSyn-eGFP-2A-2N4R hTau(P301L), which produces equimolar ratio of eGFP and hTau protein from a single mRNA71 (Figure 6A). The 2A peptide sequence self-cleaves and separates eGFP and hTau during translation. As a result, transduced neurons express both eGFP and hTau as individual proteins in equal amounts. In contrast, neurons that receive hTau via intercellular transmission express hTau but not GFP. Thus, donor and recipient neurons can be identified by co-immunofluorescence of eGFP and hTau71. To determine whether Arc is necessary for intercellular Tau transmission in vitro, we sparsely transduced WT and Arc KO primary hippocampal neuron cultures at DIV 7 with AAV2/5: hSyn-eGFP-2A-2N4R hTau(P301L). 7 days post transduction only GFP/hTau neurons and no hTau transfer could be observed in either genotype (Figure S10). However, 14 days post-transduction we found that the percentage of transduced donor neurons (GFP+, hTau+) is the same in WT and Arc KO neurons, but the percentage of hTau recipient neurons (GFP−, hTau+) is significantly reduced in Arc KO neurons (Figure 6B–D).

To test if Arc is important for intercellular hTau transmission in vivo, we injected hSyn-eGFP-2A-2N4R hTau(P301L) in the medial entorhinal cortex of 6-month-old WT and Arc KO mice (Figure 6E) and collected the brains 10 weeks after injections, which was previously shown to be optimal for transmission71. We imaged serial sections of the entorhinal cortex contralateral to the injection site where transduction was sparse (Figure 6F), which allowed us to measure hTau transmission. The number of donor neurons in the imaging area was comparable between WT and Arc KO mice (Figure 6G–H). However, intercellular hTau transmission was significantly reduced in Arc KO mice in vivo (Figure 6G, I). To exclude the possibility that altered neuronal activity accounts for differences in intercellular hTau transmission in Arc KO neurons and mice, we assessed baseline spontaneous neuronal activity and excitability in both primary hippocampal cultures and acute slices. Spontaneous activity and excitability were comparable between Arc KO and WT primary neurons (Figure S11A–B). Excitatory neurons in the CA1 from Arc KO mice slices have significantly elevated excitability as compared to WT CA1 neurons (Figure S11C). Previous studies showed that increased neuronal activity leads to an increase in tau release8,9 and pathology10, thus these changes in excitability are unlikely to explain the dramatic decrease in intercellular hTau transmission observed in Arc KO mice. These data show that Arc is critical for neuron-to-neuron transmission of hTau.

The molecular mechanisms of intercellular tau transmission, despite the importance to understanding AD and tauopathy pathogenesis, are still unclear. Here, we describe a specific molecular role for Arc in packaging hTau into EVs through a direct protein-protein interaction (Figure S12). The profound deficits we observed in EV-hTau levels in rTgArc KO mice and intercellular hTau transmission in Arc KO neurons suggest that EV-hTau is critical for efficient cell-to-cell hTau transmission. Our data from human brains shows that Arc and hTau are co-packaged in brain EVs and Arc levels in EVs correlate with the amount of phosphorylated EV-hTau. Together, these data support the conclusion that Arc mediates EV-hTau release and cell-to-cell transmission in AD.

EV intercellular signaling has emerged as an important component of the spread of pathology in neurodegenerative disorders72. However, the function and specific type of neuronal EVs involved in the spread of pathology is poorly described, and their precise role in disease pathogenesis is unclear73. This is partly due to a lack of mechanistic understanding of the biogenesis and cargo of specific EVs, which are varied and cell-type specific73. hTau is released from neurons in small EVs (<200nm)10 and levels of hTau in neuron-derived plasma EVs strongly correlate with cognitive decline in AD patients23–25. EV-hTau release is modulated by neuronal activity and high neuronal activity exacerbates tau pathology8,10, but the exact type of EV critical for hTau release has remained ill-defined. Arc is also released in small EVs that may be generated via a classic exosome pathway derived from multi-vesicular bodies74. However, we found that Arc is released from dendrites directly from the cell surface, through membrane budding that requires the I-BAR protein IRSp5341 that is normally found predominately in post-synaptic synapses75. Here we show that hTau is specifically released in Arc EVs, providing a molecular mechanism for hTau packaging into neuronal EVs. Moreover, Arc EV levels are highly correlated with phosphorylated hTau EV levels from AD patient brains, suggesting that Arc drives the release of pathological hTau in human AD brain EVs.

Hyperphosphorylated hTau is found at synapses in human AD tissue, both at pre- and post-synaptic compartments44,76. Similar to Arc33, emerging data also shows that physiological tau is locally translated in dendrites77 and localized to the post-synaptic density55. We find that hTau binds Arc and IRSp53 in both mouse and human cortex, suggesting hTau release from neurons most likely occurs at dendrites. Thus, Arc-dependent EV-hTau release may result in post- to pre-synaptic or post- to post-synaptic hTau transmission. Interestingly, we also observed Arc-hTau interactions in non-AD patient tissue. This interaction may be age-dependent but suggests the Arc-hTau interaction plays a normal synaptic function78 that warrants further study. Thus, Arc-tau binding may also interfere with Arc’s normal role in synaptic plasticity and memory formation41,79, contributing to the cognitive deficits in AD.

Arc has been implicated in the generation of β-amyloid peptide, by regulating the endocytosis of APP and PS1 in dendrites34. APP/PS1 transgenic mice crossed with Arc KO mice show a reduction in amyloid plaques34. Thus, Arc may play dual roles in amyloid and tau pathology. The former via an intracellular endocytosis pathway and the latter via an intercellular EV pathway. Data in human AD patients and AD mouse models suggest that neuronal circuits are hyperexcitable early in AD pathogenesis35,36, but become hypoexcitable as pathology accumulates and cells are lost80. Arc levels are abnormally high in young AD mice but eventually decrease as pathology and neurodegeneration cause severe disease35,81,82. Since Arc expression is highly dynamic and Arc release is activity-dependent, it remains to be determined precisely how Arc levels modulate EV-hTau release in vivo as the disease progresses. In a Drosophila tauopathy model, dArc1 expression is increased and reducing dArc1 levels led to decreased neurodegeneration48. How dArc1 modulates tau-induced neurodegeneration in flies is unclear and it remains to be determined whether dArc1 capsid-dependent intercellular signaling or EV biogenesis regulates tau pathology.

Our data supports a model where Arc eliminates intracellular hTau from neurons early in disease progression. A mild increase in cell death and toxicity observed in 4-month-old rTgArc KO mice is consistent with this model. However, we did not observe a significant increase in AT8 hTau staining in 4-month-old rTgArc KO mice, despite evidence of increased toxicity, possibly due to changes in hTau phosphorylation or conformation not detected by the AT8 antibody. Indeed, the dynamic interactions between monomeric hTau and Arc may limit the exposure of aggregation prone hTau motifs59. Precisely which species of hTau is regulated by Arc is a topic of future studies. By 8-months-of-age, hTau levels and pathology are similar between rTgWT and rTgArc KO mice. Thus, lack of Arc does not overtly accelerate tau pathology as the animals age. A caveat of using hTau transgenic mice, however, is that hTau expression is extremely high and total hTau levels in whole brain homogenates are comparable between rTgWT and rTgArc KO. Thus, the potential differences in the transmission of tau pathology in this transgenic model may be masked by the extremely high cell-autonomous expression of mutant hTau. There may also be other mechanisms of EV-hTau release that do not depend on Arc, for example release from axons that is SNARE mediated83 or transfer may also occur through tunneling nanotubes84. These mechanisms may be upregulated in Arc KO neurons to compensate for the loss of Arc-dependent elimination, but do not significantly contribute to intercellular hTau transmission in our studies.

Our molecular modeling suggests that binding of monomeric hTau to Arc through multivalent contacts is similar to the physiological interactions of tau with microtubules65. In addition, Arc capsids may also interact with hTau fibrils through polyelectrolyte interactions between the fuzzy coat61 of hTau fibrils and the N-terminal coiled-coil region of Arc, which most likely form “spikes” on the outside of the capsid39,46. This may explain our observations that EV-hTau is still capable of seeding aggregation and is consistent with earlier reports that intercellular tau transmission does not depend on its aggregation propensity85. How Arc interacts with tau inside the cell prior to EV biogenesis to regulate tau aggregation, and whether this occurs in a liquid-liquid phase separated compartment86, should be addressed in future studies.

Intriguingly, tau pathology promotes the reactivation of endogenous retroviruses (ERVs)87, which may also play a role in the spread of TDP43 and tau pathology88,89. We speculate that this may be mediated through ERV Gag capsid interactions that form virus-like particles90. Recent studies have also implicated acute viral infections in AD and tau pathology; tau interacts with the HSV-1 viral capsid, which promotes the release of phosphorylated tau91. Arc has also been shown to bind and play a role in HSV-1 infection92,93, which further suggests that tau may interact with Arc due to its viral-like biology. Recent epidemiology data has shown that people vaccinated for Shingles have much lower incidence of dementia94,95. Shingles is caused by the Varicella-Zoster virus, an alpha-herpesvirus closely related to HSV-1. Together, these data suggest a strong connection between virus biology and AD that requires more in-depth mechanistic investigation.

While Arc-dependent release of hTau in EVs may be initially beneficial in eliminating intracellular toxic hTau, EV-hTau release contributes to intercellular hTau transmission, which is dependent on the balance of release and uptake mechanisms69. EV-hTau could also be first taken up by microglia, as a mechanism to ultimately degrade toxic tau18 or eliminated into the CSF via the glymphatic system during sleep96, which may be dysregulated in AD97. We do not find any overt differences in microglia numbers or activation in 4-month-old rTgArc KO mice, but this does not preclude a role of microglia in EV-hTau transmission, especially later in disease progression. We observed considerable deficits in intercellular hTau transmission in Arc KO neurons in vitro and in Arc KO mice in vivo. In addition, EVs derived from rTgArc KO exhibit decreased hTau seeding potential, which suggests that Arc packaging and release of EV-hTau plays a critical role in intercellular hTau transmission and possibly the spread of tau pathology, which will require further investigation. The mechanisms that are most important for the uptake of monomeric tau, such as through LRP receptors, are likely not as efficient for phosphorylated tau98 and EV-tau uptake may be more efficient than free tau12. Thus, EV-tau uptake in neurons may play an important role in the transmission of seed competent tau. Our findings suggest that anti-tau antibody therapy may not prevent all forms of tau transmission as EVs may preclude antibody binding and could explain the lack of efficacy of tau antibodies17, although this remains to be determined clinically. Thus, understanding the mechanisms of EV-tau transmission will inform current therapeutic applications and may lead to new targets for preventing the spread of tau pathology in AD and other tauopathies.

It is possible that the phenotypes observed in Arc KO mice and neurons could be due to indirect mechanisms that result from loss of Arc protein. For example, hTau release could be affected by neuronal activity levels. Previous studies showed that Arc KO primary neurons have increased surface AMPARs and larger mini-EPSC amplitudes99, although in vivo basal synaptic transmission is normal in the hippocampus of Arc KO mice100. We find that primary cultured Arc KO neurons have similar basal spontaneous neuronal activity and intrinsic excitability as WT neurons, while Arc KO neurons in slices have elevated excitability (Figure S11). Previous studies have shown that high neuronal activity leads to an increase in tau release8,9, thus it is unlikely that increased neuronal activity in Arc KO mice would result in less hTau release. Regardless, our data support a significant role of Arc and EV-hTau in intercellular hTau transmission.

Our causal mechanistic studies are limited to transgenic mice that express mutant hTau at high levels, although we and others have clearly observed hTau in human brain derived EVs13 and Arc levels are highly correlated with phosphorylated hTau in human brain EVs. Future studies will determine the causal role of Arc-dependent EV-tau release in tau pathology and spread.

Using the sequence of the human Tau (2N4R, Uniprot: P10636-8) four repeat domains (R1-R4, residues 244-369), we generated models for its complex with mouse (Uniprot: Q9WV31) and human Arc (Uniprot: Q7LC44) proteins using the AlphaFold program110. We predicted the structures for both the wild-type Tau sequence, as well as the disease-associated P301L Tau mutant and used three representative wild-type and five mutant Tau conformations as starting models for the simulation. The models were placed into rhombic dodecahedron simulation cell (20 nm edges), were solvated with TIP3Pε waters and ions were added to model 150 mM NaCl. The simulation system contained approximately ~1 million particles. Using CHARMM36m force field 107 the system was optimized and heated to 310K while gradually removing positional restraints. The system was equilibrated using molecular dynamics simulations with GROMACS106, while monitoring the hydrodynamic radius (Rg). When Rg converged, due to the collapse of the disordered Arc tails, the system was re-solvated. We performed an NPT molecular dynamics simulations using the re-solvated systems with ~500 000 atoms, with velocity-rescaling thermostat and a Parrinello-Rahman barostat with 2 fs integration steps. For each model a 200 ns simulation was carried out, and the first 100 ns trajectory was discarded. The trajectories of the different models were combined for analysis, which was performed by the ConAn program 111. The secondary structure populations, in particular the β-sheet propensities, computed as the fraction of the combined 500 ns equilibrated trajectory using the STRIDE algorithm112. The contacts were defined with 5.5 Å threshold between the closest heavy atoms.

Access resistance was maintained at ≤ 30 MΩ. Recordings were obtained with a Multiclamp 700B and a Digidata 1440A (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA), sampled at 25 kHz, and low-pass filtered at 10kHz.
