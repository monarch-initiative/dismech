# Characteristics associated with clinical response to pomalidomide in hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia.
**Authors:** Al-Samkari H, Thomas SM, Marsh D, Kasthuri RS, Iyer VN, Pishko AM, Decker JE, Weiss CR, Whitehead KJ, Conrad MB, Zumberg MS, Zhou JY, Parambil J, Carper B, Clancy M, McCrae KR
**Journal:** Blood Adv (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1182/bloodadvances.2025019484](https://doi.org/10.1182/bloodadvances.2025019484)

## Content

Hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT) results in recurrent severe epistaxis, chronic gastrointestinal bleeding, and other morbid manifestations. Pomalidomide was recently demonstrated to be efficacious for bleeding in HHT in the landmark PATH-HHT trial. Given that the vascular remodeling that results in bleeding improvement may require several months, characteristics associated with treatment response would be invaluable to plan treatment. Using data from PATH-HHT (NCT03910244), we identified characteristics associated with change from baseline in Epistaxis Severity Score (ESS; a well-validated 10-point HHT bleeding scale and the trial's primary end point) using multivariable-adjusted repeated-measures models. Characteristics associated with a better response to pomalidomide at weeks 16 to 24 of the trial included a higher (more severe) baseline ESS (change from baseline, -0.76 [95% confidence interval (CI), -0.99 to -0.54] points per 1-point higher baseline score, P< .001) and concomitant antifibrinolytic therapy (-0.73 [95% CI, -1.48 to 0.01] vs no therapy, P = .05). Characteristics associated with a less robust response were older age (0.29 [95% CI, 0.05-0.53] per 10 years older, P = .02) and underlying ACVRL1 germ line mutation (0.62 [95% CI, -0.04 to 1.27] vs ENG, SMAD4, or mutation not known, P = .06]. Characteristics notably associated with placebo response (placebo effect) were patient-reported outcome measures, such that those experiencing more severe disease at baseline had less placebo effect. In conclusion, several baseline patient and disease characteristics were associated with pomalidomide response, findings that may help guide patient selection for the use of pomalidomide in HHT. This trial was registered at www.Clinicaltrials.gov as NCT03910244.

Hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT; Osler-Weber-Rendu disease) is an autosomal dominant vascular bleeding disorder afflicting 1 in 4000 to 5000 people, making it the second most common inherited bleeding disorder1,2 and the most morbid inherited bleeding disorder of women.3 Recurrent, often severe epistaxis affects nearly all patients with HHT and is the manifestation resulting in the greatest negative impact on health-related quality of life (HRQoL), including major impacts on psychosocial health.4,5 Most patients with HHT also experience morbidity from organ arteriovenous malformations (AVMs), including chronic gastrointestinal bleeding from gastrointestinal AVMs and solid organ AVMs in the lung, liver, and brain that may result in morbid bleeding and nonbleeding complications.6,7 These severe and multisystem impacts result in reduced overall survival relative to the general population.8, 9, 10

Longitudinal management of HHT-associated bleeding has gradually shifted from repeat surgical procedures11 to efficacious medical therapies with hemostatic and antiangiogenic properties,12, 13, 14, 15 and now multiple such agents are used; however, there remain no approved therapeutics for HHT. It remains impossible to predict how successfully a patient will respond to a given medical therapy before its initiation. This is of particular concern in HHT given that the disease-modifying antiangiogenic drugs with ample or high-level evidence supporting their use, systemic bevacizumab and the immunomodulatory imide drugs thalidomide and pomalidomide, are expensive, currently only approved for patients with malignancy, and have well-described toxicity risks.16 Additionally, due to the underlying mechanism of these agents (vascular remodeling), it may take several months to observe a clinical response in some patients. Therefore, baseline patient or disease characteristics associated with response or nonresponse to a given agent would be of significant clinical utility.

Because HHT has been neglected among the more common inherited bleeding disorders,17 large prospective clinical trials to date have been few. The largest such trial to date, the PATH-HHT trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03910244), was a randomized, controlled trial comparing pomalidomide with placebo in patients with HHT presenting with moderate-to-severe bleeding.14,18 The trial was successful in demonstrating the efficacy of pomalidomide in HHT; however, a wide spectrum of responses to pomalidomide were observed across the study population. The size and prospective nature of PATH-HHT allow for a critical examination of potential baseline characteristics of the participants and their disease that may be associated with degree of response to pomalidomide. This is the aim of the present study.

A comprehensive set of baseline characteristics was identified to evaluate for potential association with change in epistaxis severity. These included demographics (age, sex, race, ethnicity, and living in colder climate at enrollment), assessments of baseline epistaxis severity (Epistaxis Severity Score [ESS]; individual ESS items for epistaxis frequency, intensity, and presence of anemia; hemoglobin and ferritin values; and intensity-weighted daily epistaxis duration self-reported by daily epistaxis diary in the preceding 4 weeks), concomitant use of oral antifibrinolytic agents (allowed by protocol if at a stable dose), underlying HHT germ line mutation, HHT complications other than epistaxis (brain AVMs, pulmonary AVMs, liver AVMs, gastrointestinal AVMs/bleeding), relevant medical history (red blood cell transfusion/emergency department visit in the preceding 6 months, amount of elemental iron infused in the preceding 6 months, previous endoscopic interventions for epistaxis, previous receipt of systemic bevacizumab [concurrent use not allowed by protocol], diagnoses of heart failure/pulmonary hypertension, systemic hypertension, allergic rhinitis, history of infections, depression/anxiety), and baseline HRQoL assessments (HHT-specific Quality of Life Scale [HHT-QoL], Neuro-QoL Satisfaction with Social Roles and Activities, PROMIS Emotional Distress-Depression, PROMIS Fatigue). We also evaluated the presence of common pomalidomide side effects for association with a treatment response (development of rash, constipation, or neutropenia, and change in neutrophil count from baseline after 4 and 24 weeks of treatment). Association among characteristics was evaluated via Spearman rank correlation.

For each characteristic, we assessed the association with change in ESS from baseline to 24 weeks, and the average of weeks 16, 20, and 24 within both the pomalidomide and placebo groups using a repeated-measures linear model with adjustment for baseline ESS value, clinical site (with lower enrolling sites pooled), age, treatment group, and study visit. Association was assessed for each treatment group separately via a 3-way interaction between treatment, characteristic, and visit. Assessments were made at 24 weeks because that was the study’s primary time point; for this exploration, we also evaluated the average across weeks 16 to 24 to obtain a more stable estimate as the study sample size was modest for association analysis and primary results indicated a consistent and strong pomalidomide response during weeks 16 to 24.14 All evaluations included adjustment for age because a strong association between age and response was identified early in the analysis process. Analyses were conducted for all randomized and treated participants with postbaseline data (modified intent-to-treat population).

Although there was no formal adjustment for multiple testing, we selected characteristics to further explore if they met the following a priori criteria: binary characteristics: adjusted mean difference for ESS change from baseline averaged across weeks 16 through 24 of ≥0.5 between levels and sample size in the smaller level of at least n = 13 (corresponding to 15% in the pomalidomide arm and 25% in the placebo arm); ordinal: mean difference between lowest and highest categories approaching 0.5 and a clear ordinal trend; continuous: P value ≥ .05. We selected 0.5 as a cutoff for the ESS score because although the minimal clinically important difference for ESS is 0.71,19 we considered a value of 0.5 to be appropriate for exploration.

For each treatment group, the smallest subset of characteristics with multivariable-adjusted association with change in ESS across the study was identified by backward selection using a repeated-measures linear model, limited to participants only in that treatment group. The model began with all characteristics identified for further evaluation in the first step, plus clinical site and study visit. Characteristics were removed 1 at a time based on the largest P value until all remaining characteristics had a P value < .05. Treatment groups were analyzed separately to identify separate characteristics associated with response to active treatment and placebo. Strength of association with ESS was assessed across all time points to identify the most rigorous associations.

A final multivariable-adjusted repeated-measures model for both groups combined was fit, with factors for the strongest covariates identified by the treatment-specific multivariable-adjusted models, plus clinical site, treatment group, and visit. Each covariate was evaluated for interaction with treatment group, and backward selection identified a final single model of the characteristics found to have the strongest association (P < .05) with ESS change from baseline across all visits.

This study was approved by the Cleveland Clinic institutional review board and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The intensity-weighted daily epistaxis duration and HHT-QoL were both correlated with the ESS (r = 0.56 and 0.38, respectively) and with each other (r = 0.36), and all 4 HRQoL assessments were correlated with each other (r > 0.3). In the placebo group, many of the characteristics meeting the threshold for further evaluation were representative of overall disease severity, such that those with more severe disease at baseline had less improvement in response to placebo (placebo effect), supporting the conclusion that the placebo response was primarily seen in patients with less severe disease overall. Specifically, baseline characteristics associated (P < .05) with lesser ESS improvement from baseline in the placebo group either to week 24 or to the average across weeks 16 to 24 were HHT-QoL, Neuro-QoL Satisfaction with Social Roles and Activities T-score, PROMIS Fatigue T-score, and previous receipt of systemic bevacizumab. In contrast, higher (more severe) baseline ESS scores were associated with more improvement in ESS, as seen in the pomalidomide group (supplemental Table 2).

In the final multivariable-adjusted repeated-measures model for treatment that combined both pomalidomide and placebo groups, characteristics associated (P < .05) with more change in ESS across study visits were higher baseline ESS (mean difference in ESS averaged across weeks 16-24 for 1-point higher [worse]: −0.92 [95% CI, −1.11 to −0.74]; P < .001), younger age (mean difference in ESS averaged across weeks 16-24 for 10 years older: 0.37 [95% CI, 0.17-0.57]; P < .001), and better baseline HHT-QoL score (mean difference in ESS averaged across weeks 16-24 for 1-point higher [worse]: 0.14 [95% CI, 0.06-0.23]; P = .002). Use of concomitant antifibrinolytic therapy had a separate relationship for pomalidomide and placebo groups (interaction P = .03). Participants in the pomalidomide group on antifibrinolytic therapy improved more than those not on antifibrinolytics (mean difference in ESS averaged across weeks 16-24: −0.48 [95% CI, −1.18 to 0.21]; P = .17), whereas participants in the placebo group on antifibrinolytic therapy improved less than those not on antifibrinolytics (mean difference in ESS averaged across weeks 16-24: 0.89 [95% CI, −0.01 to 1.80]; P = .05; Table 4). Because the vascular remodeling of telangiectasias and AVMs that provides for durable hemostasis in HHT is a gradual process, typically taking weeks to months,20,21 use of antiangiogenic drugs to treat HHT requires patience on the part of both patient and clinician, as well as considerable up-front investment in time, money, and opportunity cost.22 Pomalidomide in particular remains expensive in many countries (it was demonstrated to be a leading pharmacy cost driver in a recent US health economic study of HHT),23 and regulatory agencies require monthly clinical and laboratory monitoring owing to its teratogenic potential. Additionally, premature discontinuation of pomalidomide or other antiangiogenics before the delayed development of efficacy may inappropriately deprive a patient of an effective disease-modifying therapy that could have provided years of durable disease control. For all of these reasons, a better understanding of patient and disease characteristics associated with a greater likelihood and depth of response is critical, given that current therapeutic selection in HHT is largely empiric or driven by considerations of toxicities and route of administration rather than likelihood of response.11,24

In this study rigorously analyzing the in-depth patient- and disease-specific characteristics of patients receiving pomalidomide in the randomized, placebo-controlled PATH-HHT trial, we observed several easily assessed baseline characteristics associated with greater or lesser response to pomalidomide. In particular, higher ESS at baseline and younger patient age emerged as consistent factors associated with better response to pomalidomide. Patients with more severe ESS scores at baseline are more likely to experience a greater depth of response owing to their greater potential for improvement. Those with a baseline ESS in the mild range did not show an appreciable improvement in ESS, suggesting that pomalidomide might not be an appropriate treatment option for certain patients with mild HHT-associated epistaxis, given its cost, off-label nature, and association with potentially serious side effects. Older patients appeared likely to respond as well as younger patients. This may be due to underlying biological aspects of more advanced HHT or potentially adherence issues with use of the medication, as adverse events associated with pomalidomide, in particular hematologic toxicity, were more common and more significant in older adults in the PATH-HHT After Trial Longitudinal Assessment Study.18

The presence of a germ line ACVRL1 mutation was associated with lesser response to pomalidomide relative to a germ line ENG mutation or no known mutation, although responses were observed in many patients with this genotype. ACVRL1 participants had similar baseline ESS and age as other participants but less lung involvement and less anemia. Given the ongoing lack of deep understanding of the exact mechanisms by which pomalidomide exerts its therapeutic effect in HHT, it is difficult to speculate why improvements in epistaxis appear to be less overall in patients with ACVRL1 mutations, and this is an important topic of further study.

Concomitant use of antifibrinolytics was associated with greater response in patients in the pomalidomide group but not in the placebo group. This suggests the potential for additive or synergistic effects between pomalidomide and antifibrinolytic drugs, which is logical given the differing therapeutic mechanisms of these agents both leading to improved hemostasis. Given that both agents have a theoretical thromboembolic risk, there may be some hesitation among clinicians to combine them. Only very limited data pertaining to combination antiangiogenic-antifibrinolytic treatment in HHT have been published and are limited at this time to small case series.25 Although the overall number of combination therapy patients in the present study (n = 20 for pomalidomide plus antifibrinolytic agent) is small, to our knowledge it is the largest cohort of such combination therapy to date in the published literature and, taken together, does not provide a signal for increased thromboembolism. Ultimately, these findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and provide an avenue for further dedicated study of combination therapy in HHT. Differences in response to other agents, such as antifibrinolytics and systemic bevacizumab, were not seen in other studies,13 but none of these studies undertook a rigorous analysis for associations as was done in the present study. Further investigation into the additive or synergistic efficacy that may be seen with the combination of systemic therapies with different, complementary mechanisms of action and nonoverlapping toxicity seems particularly prudent given the numerous novel agents currently in development to treat HHT.

Finally, we found that the strongest associations with response to placebo (the placebo effect) were patient-reported outcomes, both disease- and nondisease-specific instruments representative of the patient’s experience of their overall disease severity and HRQoL (such that those experiencing more severe disease at baseline had less of a placebo effect). This is an important consideration for the conduct of future trials in HHT. Because epistaxis measurement in HHT clinical trials relies on epistaxis diaries and validated epistaxis recall instruments, a modest placebo effect is well documented.20 These findings support the hypothesis that patients experiencing relatively less severe disease may be more prone to experiencing an improvement on placebo due to a shift in attitude and/or outlook, whereas patients experiencing relatively more severe disease are less likely to have a placebo effect given the harsher realities of the disease manifestations that they face on a regular basis. Nevertheless, these observations argue for inclusion of placebo groups in future trials of emerging agents in HHT.

Our study has several notable strengths, including the rigorous and comprehensive prospective collection of data as occurs in a randomized clinical trial and the fact that the trial was double-blind, both enhancing the analysis of the present study. It also has important limitations. Although PATH-HHT remains the largest randomized trial in HHT to date, the number of patients with certain characteristics was small, limiting the sample size of certain analyses. It is also possible that our selection process may have overfit the models. Given that there was no formal adjustment for multiple testing, backward selection of predictors may increase the potential for type I error. The associations found in this study must be validated in future patient cohorts in order to be confirmed as predictors of response. Nevertheless, in the meantime, in this rare and morbid disease in which clinicians are in great need of guidance, the associations discovered in this study may be helpful in making clinical determinations in daily practice.

In conclusion, higher ESS measurement at baseline, younger age, and concomitant use of antifibrinolytic therapy were associated with more robust response to pomalidomide, whereas the presence of an ACVRL1 germ line mutation and worse HHT-QoL measurement at baseline were associated with less robust response to pomalidomide. Further assessment of these findings in future studies of pomalidomide or other emerging agents in HHT is needed.
