A 45-year-old woman presents for a hematology consultation prior to planned repeat breast biopsy. A breast biopsy completed 1 month prior was complicated by excessive bruising and a large site hematoma. On interview, she describes a history of lifelong easy bleeding. In childhood, she had frequent nosebleeds and bleeding following tonsillectomy that required a repeat operative procedure. Prior to having a progesterone IUD placed, she received regular intravenous iron infusions for heavy menstrual bleeding. She received a blood transfusion after hemorrhage with the birth of her first child. An International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH)–Bleeding Assessment Tool score is calculated to be 12 (<6 is considered normal for adult women). Medical history is significant for postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome and migraine headaches. von Willebrand testing 2 years prior was normal, as were recent blood counts, liver, and metabolic panel. Laboratory tests completed at today's visit (including repeat von Willebrand factor antigen and GP1BM activity; prothrombin time/activated partial thromboplastin time; factors VIII, IX, and XI; Clauss fibrinogen; and light transmission aggregometry) were all normal.

Bleeding disorder of unknown cause (BDUC) is a diagnosis of exclusion encompassing patients who have clinical symptoms indicative of a mild congenital bleeding disorder yet have negative hemostatic evaluations. Clinically, these patients present with mucocutaneous-predominant bleeding symptoms indistinguishable from nonsevere type 1 von Willebrand disease or a mild qualitative platelet defect. The etiology of bleeding is postulated to be due to defects in platelet function, fibrinolysis, collagen integrity, and/or other coagulation defects not well measured in standard laboratory assays.1

In previously described cohorts of patients with BDUC, a history of postoperative bleeding is common, reported in 44% to 75% of patients.2-5While considered a “mild” bleeding disorder, periprocedural bleeding can at times be quite significant, resulting in a need for repeat intervention, blood product transfusion, and emotional distress for the patient. While coagulation factor levels do not correlate well with bleeding phenotype in many inherited bleeding disorders (including von Willebrand disease and rare factor deficiencies), in BDUC, there is no laboratory parameter to “correct.” A history of abnormal bleeding, in the absence of laboratory diagnosis, may lead to hesitancy on the part of both patient and proceduralist to proceed with future operative procedures necessary for health.

The goal of periprocedural management in BDUC is to mitigate the risk of excess bleeding while limiting the adverse effects of hemostatic therapy (Figure 1). This task is undertaken with certain challenges. First, while clinical experience in caring for patients with BDUC is growing, most of our data are gleaned from limited retrospective observational studies. Second, patients with BDUC are heterogeneous and surgical planning is nuanced, necessitating an individualized approach. Finally, the diagnosis of BDUC is likely unfamiliar to patients and proceduralists and, without proper care, could lead to added confusion and distress. Throughout the hemostatic planning process, education and counseling must be provided to both the patient and the proceduralist to ensure goal-aligned care.

Goals and challenges of BDUC procedure management.

An individualized approach to procedural management is necessary in BDUC, starting with careful assessment of both patient and procedural factors (Figure 2).1Once a plan is determined and agreed upon with patient and surgeon, both bleeding outcome and tolerance of the hemostatic therapy should be carefully assessed. These outcomes, in turn, will help inform management planning for future surgeries or procedures the patient may undergo.

Patient assessment begins with a careful examination of bleeding history. Usage of a bleeding assessment tool, like the ISTH– Bleeding Assessment Tool, ensures a thorough, standardized approach. Independent of diagnosis, a history of surgical bleeding is associated with the risk of future bleeding,6and thus special care should be taken to understand prior surgical bleeding events and any relevant mitigating factors. For example, some patients with BDUC have a history of excess bleeding in surgeries involving mucosal surfaces (eg, gynecologic or ear, nose, and throat procedures), but relatively less bleeding during prior abdominal or orthopedic procedures. If the patient has received hemostatic products previously, efficacy and tolerance should be assessed.

Beyond bleeding history, other patient factors may impact bleeding risk and selection of hemostatic therapies. Some hemostatic therapies may carry a greater risk of adverse events in patients with advanced age or cardiovascular comorbidity. Medications should be reviewed in detail, particularly any prescribed anticoagulants or antiplatelet agents.

Given the multifactorial contributors to perioperative bleeding, our understanding of patient-level risk factors in patients specifically designated as “BDUC” is still growing but has been evaluated in the Vienna Bleeding Biobank. A total of 392 patients with mild bleeding disorders, including 246 with BDUC, in the Vienna Bleeding Biobank were asked to self-report recurrent bleeding events a median of 4.3 years after initial diagnosis. Patient-level risk factors for surgical bleeding after diagnosis included prior surgical bleeding (odds ratio, 2.40 [95% CI 1.06-5.46]) and blood type O (odds ratio, 3.17 [95% CI 1.57-6.40]).6

In tandem with evaluation of patient factors, it is important to carefully assess the proposed procedure itself. Surgical factors, independent of any intrinsic hemostatic defect in the patient, are significant contributors to procedural bleeding risk. While definitions vary, major procedures typically include cardiovascular, orthopedic, head and neck, urologic, gynecologic, and general surgeries lasting greater than 45 minutes and carry a bleeding risk of 2% to 4%. Minor procedures may include simple dental, dermatologic, or endoscopic procedures with a 0% to 2% risk of a major bleeding event.7Every effort should be undertaken to understand factors that may increase the anticipated complexity or bleeding risk of the procedure itself. Thrombotic risks, including whether routine or extended pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis may be indicated, should be examined. As 1 potential hemostatic agent, desmopressin (DDAVP), requires a fluid restriction after administration, the anticipated need for perioperative fluids or volume resuscitation should be examined.

Patients should be otherwise medically optimized for surgery, which includes assessment and correction of iron deficiency through intravenous or oral iron administration (Figure 3). Antiplatelet and anticoagulant medications should be held appropriately prior to the procedure.

Components of a procedural hemostatic plan.

Local resource availability and the setting of the procedure itself should be incorporated into preplanning. This includes the ability to administer additional blood products if bleeding is encountered or to monitor a patient postprocedure. When bleeding risks are high, a decision may be made to perform a procedure in a hospital operative theater rather than an ambulatory center, facilitating prompt response should bleeding be encountered.

Once patient and surgical factors have been considered, including the setting of the procedure itself, recommendations can be made regarding the administration of specific hemostatic agents. Antifibrinolytic agents (particularly tranexamic acid), DDAVP, and platelet transfusion have been studied in BDUC (Table 1). The overall rationale for including these agents in surgical planning is based on the hypothesized mechanisms of bleeding in BDUC (unmeasured defects in platelets or hyperfibrinolysis) and the efficacy of these agents in bleeding disorders with similar phenotypic presentations (von Willebrand disease, fibrinolytic disorders, and qualitative platelet defects).

Hemostatic therapies in BDUC

Limited availability of intranasal formulation at present due to manufacturing recalls.

Tranexamic acid, a specific inhibitor of fibrinolysis that works to stabilize clot formation, is the cornerstone of hemostatic management in BDUC. Tranexamic acid is widely available in both oral and intravenous formulations, is inexpensive, has a favorable safety profile, and has been extensively studied in a variety of inherited bleeding disorders.8Side effects are minimal. Tranexamic acid is the most widely used treatment for BDUC cited in international surveys9and the most studied in prior retrospective cohorts.1While antifibrinolytics should be avoided in patients with active thrombosis, there was no evidence of additional thrombotic risk with tranexamic acid in large-scale randomized controlled trials in trauma or pregnancy.8Tranexamic acid should be used with caution in patients undergoing procedures involving the renal pole/upper urinary tract, given the risk for obstructive urinary clot, although this association is not based on firm data.10

DDAVP, which works to increase endogenous secretion of factor VIII, von Willebrand factor, and other clotting factors,11has also been considered a hemostatic treatment in BDUC. While DDAVP has historically been available in intravenous, subcutaneous, and intranasal formulations, access to intranasal DDAVP at concentrations suitable for hemostatic therapy (1.5   mg/mL, 10 mcg/0.1  mL) is now limited due to manufacturing recalls.12Particularly for younger patients who have had a good prior tolerance of DDAVP, DDAVP may be a helpful adjunct. Lower-dose DDAVP (0.15    µg/kg as compared to 0.3  µg/kg) has also been described.13However, DDAVP carries a risk of hyponatremia, which can be clinically severe, necessitating a 1.5- to 2-L volume restriction (both oral and intravenous) for 24 hours postadministration. Risks of therapy may be greater in patients with advanced age or cardiac comorbidity.

Finally, given concern that some cases of BDUC may be due to unidentified defects in platelet function, platelet transfusion can be considered, similar to treatment of severe bleeding or high-risk surgeries in inherited platelet disorders. Platelets are, of course, a limited resource, and repeated platelet transfusions could place a patient at risk of alloimmunization. In practice surveys, providers have reported rarely using alternative hemostatic agents, including recombinant factor VIIa, for refractory bleeding.9

A simplified approach to hemostatic management, adapted from the ISTH Scientific and Standardization Committee communication on the standardization of definition and management in BDUC,1is provided inFigure 4. Patients undergoing low-risk procedures may be closely observed, but we should have a low threshold to prescribe tranexamic acid, especially if the patient has additional risk factors for bleeding (eg, history of postoperative bleeding). For higher bleeding risk procedures, prophylactic hemostatic treatment is more clearly indicated, the mainstay of which is tranexamic acid. Considering both patient and procedural factors, DDAVP and/or platelet transfusion can be administered prophylactically or to have on hand in the event of excess bleeding.

Bleeding risk for patients with BDUC continues into the postoperative period. Treatment with tranexamic acid is often extended for several days postprocedure to promote hemostasis. Repeat doses of DDAVP are given sparingly, both due to tachyphylaxis and the need for free water restriction.

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs may increase bleeding risk, particularly in patients with an underlying bleeding diathesis. Celecoxib, a selective COX-2 inhibitor, may carry a lower bleeding risk. Unlike other nonselective nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (eg, ibuprofen, naproxen), celecoxib does not utilize the COX-1 pathway and impair platelet function. Patients should be informed when they may resume prescribed antiplatelet and anticoagulant medications.

The plan for monitoring the patient for bleeding complications, either via serial examination in the inpatient setting or via outpatient symptom reporting, should be established with the patient and surgeon. If significant bleeding was encountered, a complete blood count should be completed. In patients receiving DDAVP, adherence to fluid restriction should be monitored. A repeat sodium should be performed after major surgery (when fluid shifts are expected) or if a patient is unable to adhere to fluid restriction.

Results of diagnostic testing and diagnosis of BDUC are reviewed with the patient and the referring proceduralist. As breast biopsy is a low bleeding risk procedure, tranexamic acid is prescribed at a dosage of 1300   mg 3 times a day, which the patient begins the night prior to the procedure and continues for 5 days. No excess bleeding occurs, and at follow-up, the patient reports only mild bruising.

Close patient follow-up and communication with the surgical team are necessary to assess hemostatic outcomes and tolerance of therapies. These outcomes can then be used to inform future surgical planning. While many centers have strong institutional experience using these hemostatic agents in surgical management,9published studies are limited. To date, no therapeutic interventional trials have been performed specifically in BDUC.

The risk of clinically relevant bleeding in patients with BDUC who undergo minor procedures appears to be low. In an analysis of 70 minor procedures completed following a diagnosis of BDUC, none of the 54 patients who received hemostatic prophylaxis had major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding, as compared to 1 of 16 patients for whom hemostatic prophylaxis was omitted.2

Estimates for bleeding risk in major procedures are more variable across observational studies, and data are more limited. Aggregating across 3 published retrospective cohorts,2,4,57.8% of major surgeries (n = 71) were complicated by major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding in patients who received hemostatic prophylaxis (Table 2). How many of these procedures would have been complicated by bleeding had hemostatic treatment been omitted is challenging to determine. Patients with BDUC may report a history of bleeding only with certain prior procedures, and observational data on untreated patients are limited.

No prophylactic platelet transfusions are given, but platelets remain on-hand in the event bleeding is encountered. The patient has mild oozing during the procedure that is controlled with local cautery and no postoperative bleeding complications.

For patients with clinically relevant bleeding symptoms yet no apparent laboratory diagnosis, a diagnostic label of BDUC may foster access to multidisciplinary hemostatic planning around procedural interventions. An individualized approach, incorporating both patient and procedural bleeding risks, is indicated. Many questions remain insufficiently interrogated (Table 3), but for now, a pragmatic approach extrapolated from experience managing phenotypically similar bleeding disorders is advised, in accordance with recommendations from the ISTH. The safety of antifibrinolytic therapy is well established, and limited retrospective data signal efficacy. DDAVP and platelet transfusions are used in clinical practice, but risks of these therapies are potentially more significant, and it remains unclear which patients benefit. Future multisite observational and therapeutic clinical trials in BDUC may help us better understand multifactorial bleeding risk and further define the role of hemostatic therapies in this population.
