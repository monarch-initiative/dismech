# Wnt/β-catenin signaling in hepatocellular carcinoma: a key player in resistance to targeted and immunotherapies.
**Authors:** Park C, Wong TL, Ma S
**Journal:** Carcinogenesis (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1093/carcin/bgag040](https://doi.org/10.1093/carcin/bgag040)

## Content

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), accounting for nearly 90% of primary liver cancers, remains a major global health challenge due to late diagnosis and therapy resistance. Up to 50% of cases exhibit aberrant activation of the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway, driven by CTNNB1 mutations or inactivating alterations in AXIN1, adenomatous polyposis coli, or ZNRF3, which are mutually exclusive. This hyperactivation has complex implications: it has been associated with drug resistance, larger tumors, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, vascular invasion, cancer stemness, and immune evasion, yet in some contexts, it correlates with a less aggressive, well-differentiated phenotype and improved survival. This heterogeneity highlights the need for further research to clarify its prognostic significance. This review explores recent mechanisms by which aberrant nuclear β-catenin accumulation fosters resistance to frontline tyrosine kinase inhibitors like sorafenib and lenvatinib through ferroptosis evasion, cancer stemness, and β-catenin stabilization. It also highlights how hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin promotes an immunosuppressive "cold" tumor microenvironment, impairing immunotherapy efficacy. These include cytokine profile alterations, CD8+ T-cell exclusion, dendritic cell repression, and recruitment of immunosuppressive cells such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells, tumor-associated neutrophils, macrophages, and regulatory T cells. Recent preclinical studies show that combining Wnt inhibitors with immunotherapy or tyrosine kinase inhibitors can reprogram the tumor microenvironment, restore ferroptosis sensitivity, eradicate cancer stem cells, enhance CD8+ T-cell infiltration, boost antitumor immunity, and overcome resistance with minimal side effects. Targeting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway offers a promising strategy to transform HCC treatment and improve outcomes by exploiting this pathway as a key therapeutic vulnerability.

Liver cancer remains a major global health burden, ranking as the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with a recent report of 866 136 new cases diagnosed in a single year [1]. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for nearly 90% of primary liver tumors, with 70–80% of patients presenting with unresectable advanced disease at diagnosis [2, 3]. HCC exhibits heterogeneity with various subtypes driven by chronic liver disease, such as cirrhosis from hepatitis B or C infections, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, or alcohol abuse, as well as molecular diversity involving mutations in oncogenes and tumor-suppressor genes, whose complexity hinders effective treatment strategies [2]. First-line treatments for advanced HCC include tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [4]. However, a study reported that more than three-fourths of patients discontinued TKI treatment due to tumor progression [5], while another study showed that only about one-third of patients receiving immunotherapy had an objective response (ORR) [6]. These findings underscore the limitations of current therapeutic approaches and highlight the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies to improve HCC management.

HCC is characterized by a heterogeneous genomic landscape, with many driver mutations. Among these, mutations in CTNNB1 (∼32.8% of cases), along with the inactivation of negative regulators such as AXIN1 (∼15.2%), adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) (∼1.6%), and zinc and ring finger 3 (ZNRF3) (3%), significantly contribute to aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway (∼50%) [7, 8]. Another study reports that β-catenin mutations are detected in 44.1% of cases, while aberrant nuclear accumulation of the protein is observed in 61.8% of cases in HCC cases among Chinese populations [9]. The high prevalence of Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation is drawing increasing attention, with researchers actively investigating its characteristics, potential survival advantages for tumor cells, and possible role as a therapeutic target for improved treatments. Although direct clinical causality between Wnt/β-catenin pathway hyperactivation and therapeutic resistance in HCC has not yet been definitively established, this review explores how such activation can promote drug resistance and immune evasion in HCC based on compelling preclinical studies, while proposing strategies for more effective therapies.

Initially, β-catenin was identified as a key molecule connecting E-cadherin to the actin cytoskeleton, thereby stabilizing cell–cell adhesions and maintaining epithelial integrity at the plasma membrane [10, 11]. Later, in the 1990s, researchers discovered its additional role as a transcriptional coactivator in partnership with T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer factor (TCF/LEF) family transcription factors, completing the canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [12, 13]. In the presence of Wnt ligands, such as Wnt3a, Wnt1, and Wnt5a, these ligands bind to the transmembrane Frizzled (FZD) receptors and their co-receptor lipoprotein receptor-related protein (LRP5/6), recruiting Disheveled and inactivating a regulatory complex comprising glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β), casein kinase I (CKI), AXIN1, and APC [14] to allow nonphosphorylated β-catenin to accumulate in the cytoplasm [15, 16]. The accumulated stable β-catenin then translocates to the nucleus and acts as a cofactor for the TCF/LEF to drive downstream target genes [11, 16]. However, in the absence of the Wnt ligands, cytosolic β-catenin is phosphorylated by the regulatory complex [14]. Specifically, AXIN1 facilitates complex formation, enabling CKI to phosphorylate β-catenin at serine 45, followed by GSK3β phosphorylation at serine 33, serine 37, and threonine 41 [17]. APC then recruits phosphorylated β-catenin to the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway for degradation, mediated by the E3 ubiquitin ligase β-transducin repeat-containing protein [11, 18–21]. On top of these negative regulators, ZNRF3 also inhibits the pathway by ubiquitinating and degrading the FZD receptors and LRP5/6 with the help of Disheveled [22]. The Wnt/β-catenin pathway relies on a tightly orchestrated network of proteins, each with a specific role in signal regulation. Disruption in any component—whether through mutation, loss of function, or dysregulation—can destabilize this balance, leading to aberrant pathway activation and loss of cellular control. Mutations in exon 3 of the CTNNB1 gene, such as missense mutations or deletions at or surrounding the serine and threonine residues as mentioned above, prevent phosphorylation, leading to uncontrolled β-catenin nuclear accumulation and constitutive activation [23, 24]. Likewise, mutations in AXIN1, APC, and ZNRF3 also fail to balance the system, leading to constitutive activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [14, 25]. Therefore, nuclear accumulation of β-catenin is observed more frequently than CTNNB1 mutations alone [26], and this multilayered dysregulation complicates therapeutic targeting of hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin signaling in HCC.

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is highly conserved across species and plays a central role in key biological processes such as regulation of cell pluripotency, apoptosis, resistance, inflammation, proliferation, embryonic development, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), tissue homeostasis and regeneration, cell cycle, and reparative responses [27–30]. In HCC, although the hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin pathway displays unique clinical, pathological, and genetic characteristics, no clear consensus has been reached regarding its prognostic implications. HCC harboring CTNNB1 mutations is associated with a less aggressive phenotype, characterized by a more differentiated state, a nonproliferative subtype, and favorable survival outcomes. It is associated with an improved 5-year survival rate, early-stage tumors [31], well-differentiated tumors [32], and consistent classification within the nonproliferative subgroup [33–35], collectively supporting a more indolent clinical behavior. On the other hand, studies have reported a more aggressive phenotype for this entity, characterized by larger tumor size [36]. According to a meta-analysis of 2334 cases, cytoplasmic and nuclear aberrant β-catenin accumulation significantly correlated with reduced overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival at 1, 3, and 5 years, as well as metastatic progression and vascular invasion [37]. The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is reported to transcriptionally activate the genes encoding vascular endothelial growth factor A and C (VEGF-A and VEGF-C), which are the key proangiogenic factors that can stimulate endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and new vessel formation [38, 39]. This enhanced angiogenesis supports tumor development, nutrient delivery, invasion, and metastasis, highlighting the critical role of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in HCC development. Recently, it has also been well established for its role in immune evasion in HCC, resulting in resistance to immunotherapy [40, 41]. Thus, by promoting tumor progression [36], angiogenesis [39], EMT and invasion [42], drug resistance [43], and immunotherapy evasion [44], β-catenin enhances tumor survival in hostile microenvironments, contributing to its association with poor prognosis in HCC.

Moreover, although different mutations such as CTNNB1, APC, AXIN1, and ZNRF3 all contribute to the aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and mutations of CTNNB1, APC, and AXIN1 are mutually exclusive in HCC, indicating that they converge on a common downstream effector [38], they are not equivalent regarding their downstream effects and prognostic outcomes. For example, AXIN1 mutations are associated with high proliferation and poor differentiation [45], and are also reported to activate Notch and YAP rather than the Wnt/β-catenin pathway alone, different from CTNNB1 mutations [46]. Although HCC from Znrf3-deleted mouse models shows a more similar overall human gene expression profile to that of CTNNB1 mutation-driven HCC than to AXIN1 mutation-driven HCC, it expresses lower levels of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and its classical downstream effectors (e.g. glutamine synthetase) [47]. Various studies have also emphasized that different mutations in the same β-catenin gene contribute to tumor heterogeneity in HCC. In particular, amino acid substitution mutations in the D32–S37 region are associated with the highest β-catenin transcriptional activity, mutations at T41 with moderate activity, and those at S45 with the weakest activity [48]. Additionally, despite greater than 90% concordance between mouse K-Ras models of serine 45 mutations and serine 33 mutations, the serine 45 mutation is linked to slightly faster tumor growth, and they show subtle yet nonnegligible differences in gene expression profiles [49]. This could be due to their being phosphorylated and controlled by different regulators, such as serine 45 by CKI and serine 33 by GSK3β, although further research is needed to confirm the drivers of these differences. Thus, there remains insufficient evidence to draw definitive conclusions regarding the overall prognosis of the hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin pathway due to its distinctly heterogeneous biological features, which necessitates further studies.

Drug therapy for HCC primarily consists of three therapeutic approaches: chemotherapy, molecular targeted agents, and immunotherapy. The current therapeutic landscape for HCC is dominated by TKIs and ICIs as both first-line and second-line treatments [50].

Before 2007, treatment options for advanced HCC were limited to conventional chemotherapy, which caused significant toxicity due to nonselective cytotoxicity toward noncancerous cells [51]. The recognition that multiple kinases are critical drivers of hypervascular HCC growth and metastasis, coupled with their frequent dysregulation in HCC, led to the approval of sorafenib—the first HCC TKI—by the FDA in 2007, marking the beginning of an era of effective and better-tolerated systemic therapy for advanced HCC [52, 53]. To date, various kinases, including rapidly accelerated fibrosarcoma (Raf), mitogen-activated protein kinase, extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), vascular endothelial growth factor receptors (VEGFRs), platelet-derived growth factor receptors (PDGFRs), KIT, Tie2, and c-MET, have been reported to be overexpressed and overactivated in HCC [54–60]. This diverse molecular landscape allowed for multiple FDA-approved HCC TKIs beyond sorafenib, each targeting distinct combinations and ranges of these kinases with clinical benefits (Table 1), such as regorafenib in 2017 [61], lenvatinib in 2018 [62], and cabozantinib in 2018 [63]. These have been used as effective first- or second-line options for HCC patients since their approval [51].

Although TKIs showed promising results upon their introduction, the long-term therapeutic benefit of TKI treatment in HCC remains limited due to the acquired drug resistance, disease progression, and limited response, representing a fundamental challenge in HCC management. For example, past clinical trials reported discontinuation rates of 50–62% for sorafenib and 64% for regorafenib, while real-world studies showed 80% for lenvatinib and 47.8% for cabozantinib, due to disease progression [5, 62, 64–66]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin pathway could contribute to TKI resistance in HCC patients. In a cohort of patients treated with regorafenib, CTNNB1 mutations were observed only in 10 nonresponders and none of the 7 responders, suggesting potential resistance, though larger studies could bring a more definite answer to these preliminary findings [67]. Additionally, in 41 HCC patients treated with sorafenib, nuclear β-catenin and activation were observed more frequently in nonresponders and predicted poor response [68]. Various preclinical studies in mouse models further support the role of the hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin pathway in TKI resistance, showing that inhibiting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in combination with TKIs results in enhanced antitumor activity, prolonged survival, and restoration of drug sensitivity [69–71]. Conversely, other studies suggest potential benefit or enhanced sensitivity to TKIs in CTNNB1-mutated HCC. Sorafenib has been shown to reduce nuclear β-catenin levels and suppress the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in vitro and in vivo, with better survival in a subcutaneous xenograft mouse model [72]. Also, retrieval-augmented large language models integrating clinical data and in vitro drug screenings identified an association between CTNNB1 mutations and greater sorafenib sensitivity [73]. In the IMbrave150 trial, CTNNB1-mutated patients showed improved OS in the sorafenib arm versus atezolizumab + bevacizumab, while wild-type patients showed worse prognosis in the sorafenib arm [41]. This unresolved controversy regarding whether the hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin pathway drives TKI resistance in HCC underscores the need for further detailed research. Future studies are required to understand reasons for these discrepant prognostic values, including whether they arise from differences in specific TKI mechanisms of action, the heterogeneity of CTNNB1 mutations, or other additional factors such as tumor microenvironment interactions or possible co-occurring genetic alterations. Also, it is important to note that direct clinical causality between Wnt/β-catenin pathway hyperactivation and therapeutic resistance to TKIs in HCC has not yet been firmly established.

One mechanism by which TKIs, such as sorafenib, lenvatinib, and regorafenib, exert their antitumor effects is by inducing ferroptosis, a distinct iron-dependent mode of regulated cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation [74–77]. Although the involvement of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in ferroptosis regulation remains relatively understudied in HCC, it has been established as a key modulator in other malignancies, including ovarian and gastric cancers, where it directly upregulates transcription of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4)—a glutathione-dependent reductase that alleviates lipid peroxidation and thereby suppresses ferroptosis [78, 79]. Similarly, in melanoma and prostate cancer, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway confers ferroptosis resistance through solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), a downstream target of c-Myc, which serves as a canonical effector of Wnt/β-catenin signaling [16, 80, 81].

In HCC, emerging evidence has begun to elucidate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway’s role in ferroptosis resistance. One investigation into the deubiquitinating enzyme USP8, which modulates Wnt/β-catenin signaling, demonstrated that USP8 depletion leads to reduced GPX4 expression via β-catenin degradation [82]. This finding indicates that, akin to other cancers, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in HCC directly governs GPX4 levels to counteract ferroptosis. Additionally, CTNNB1-mutated HCC has been recently reported to promote ferroptosis resistance by regulating the glutathione (GSH)–GPX4 axis through solute carrier family 13 member 3 (SLC13A3) [83], which is part of the mutant β-catenin-specific gene signature [84]. Mechanistically, TCF7L2 serves as the direct upstream regulator that binds to the SLC13A3 promoter sites and controls its expression. Knockdown of SLC13A3 sensitized HCC cells toward ferroptosis through reducing uptake and intracellular accumulation of GSH, a reactive oxygen species scavenger, along with its synthetic precursors. Sorafenib treatment in vitro induced greater ferroptosis in SLC13A3 knockdown cells than in the control, and this heightened sensitivity was reversed by GSH replenishment [83]. The synergistic effects were observed with the combination therapy of sorafenib and the SLC13A3 inhibitor, N-(p-amylcinnamoyl) anthranilic acid (ACA) in subcutaneous HepG2 xenografts in mice. Notably, in the hydrodynamic tail vein injection (HTVI) mouse model of c-Met/β-catenin and AKT/β-catenin, single treatment of ACA rendered the mice tumor-free after 15 weeks post-HTVI [83, 85]. In a study on melanoma, SLC13A3 has also been shown to import itaconate derived from tumor-associated macrophages and activate the NRF-SLC7A11 axis, thereby protecting tumor cells from ferroptosis [86]. Although further research is required to confirm this same axis in HCC, β-catenin-mutated HCC could potentially resist ferroptosis through upregulation of SLC7A11, which serves as the target of lenvatinib [87] and sorafenib [88] for inducing ferroptosis. These findings illustrate that ferroptosis resistance is a potential feature enabling β-catenin-mutated HCC to survive and thrive under TKI treatment.

An alternative mechanism involves glutathione S-transferase alpha 1 (GSTA1), an enzyme that protects cells from drug-induced ferroptosis in various cancers by exhibiting glutathione peroxidase activity, facilitating the conversion of reduced GSH to its oxidized form and thereby diminishing lipid peroxides [89, 90]. Several glutathione S-transferases, such as alpha 3, omega1, and mu1, but not alpha 1 (GSTA1), have been identified as targets of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, with functions such as in cholestasis [91, 92]. In HCC, GSTA1 has recently supported the link between β-catenin mutation and ferroptosis resistance under TKIs [93]. GSTA1 is directly transactivated by TCF7L2 and physically interacts with β-catenin, promoting its nuclear translocation and creating a positive feedback loop that sustains Wnt/β-catenin signaling [93]. GSTA1 is upregulated in sorafenib-resistant HCC cell lines, patient samples, organoids, and mouse models, indicating that HCC with the hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin pathway could utilize GSTA1 to evade sorafenib-induced ferroptosis. Mice bearing sorafenib-resistant patient-derived organoids treated with combination therapy of the GSTA1 inhibitor, curzerene, and the β-catenin inhibitor, β-catenin-IN-2 [94] demonstrated synergistic reductions in tumor burden and proliferation. Although the study did not utilize sorafenib with the combined treatment to show efficacy in restoring sorafenib sensitivity, this provides valuable insights into how hyperactive Wnt/β-catenin signaling could drive GSTA1 expression to potentially suppress TKI-induced ferroptosis and resist sorafenib treatment.

An additional mechanism entails Wnt/β-catenin signaling’s transcriptional induction of the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)-inducing kinase (NIK), which activates noncanonical NF-κB signaling and subsequently elevates nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) levels—a transcription factor that orchestrates genes involved in glutathione synthesis and reactive oxygen species detoxification, thereby conferring resistance to TKI-induced ferroptosis [95–97].

Although it remains unknown whether different mutations driving hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation affect ferroptosis resistance differently or whether certain mutational subtypes are more sensitive to ferroptosis, these discoveries underscore the multifaceted contributions of the hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin pathway to ferroptosis evasion (Fig. 1), with ongoing research unveiling novel mechanisms that may inform therapeutic advancements in HCC.

GSTA1, SLC13A3, GPX4, and NIK, which are transcriptionally regulated by the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, promote resistance to TKI via modulating TKI-induced ferroptosis. SERPINA12 and EPHB2, also controlled by the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, enhance cancer stemness and TKI resistance. HKDC1, CDK6, and LAPTM4B sustain Wnt/β-catenin signaling, thereby enabling tumor cells to withstand TKI treatment.

Another way the Wnt/β-catenin pathway contributes to drug resistance is through maintaining the stemness of cancer stem cells (CSCs). These cells are well-known to exhibit stem-like properties, including self-renewal capacity and multilineage differentiation potential, conferring advantages in plasticity, therapy resistance, and immune escape in HCC [98–102]. The Wnt/β-catenin pathway has been observed in various cancer stem cell (CSC) subpopulations, including epithelial cell adhesion molecule-positive cells (EpCAM+) [103], granulin–epithelin precursor-positive cells [104], and CD133-positive cells (CD133+) [105, 106] which are well-known key CSC markers in HCC. CSCs are difficult to eradicate, leading to recurrence and therapy resistance [98]. Recently, studies have established how CSCs utilize and tightly control the Wnt/β-catenin pathway with a positive feedback loop for maintenance of self-renewal, survival, stemness, and therapeutic resistance properties, highlighting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway as a key driver of cancer stemness (Fig. 1).

One such study has revealed that SERPINA12, which is overexpressed in CD133+ HCC cells but not in CD133+ normal hepatic stem cells, confers resistance to TKIs by forming a positive feedback loop with the Wnt/β-catenin pathway to enhance cancer stemness [107]. Mechanistically, activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway promoted the binding of TCF7L2 to the promoter region of SERPINA12. Reciprocally, SERPINA12 binds to GRP78 and activates AKT/GSK3β/β-catenin signaling cascade, completing the positive feedback loop with Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Suppression via AAV8-shSERPINA12 in a mouse model sensitized HCC cells to sorafenib, prolonged survival, and reduced tumor-initiating cell frequency and CD133+ populations in vivo. High SERPINA12 expression is linked to microvascular invasion, worse survival, and high stemness signatures, found mostly in CTNNB1-mutated HCC patients.

Similarly, EPH receptor B2 (EPHB2) was reported to be a critical mediator of sorafenib resistance and cancer stemness in CSCs through activating and being regulated by the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [69]. Transcriptomic profiling of sorafenib-resistant patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models revealed EPHB2 as one of the most prominently upregulated kinases, with its expression correlating with established liver CSC markers, including CD133, CD90, and CD24. Using a spontaneous mouse HCC model, genetic suppression of EPHB2 resensitized HCC to sorafenib, as shown by markedly reduced both the number and size of tumor nodules. High expression of EPHB2 activated β-catenin via SRC/AKT/GSK3β phosphorylation. In turn, activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway promoted TCF binding to the EPHB2 promoter, forming a positive feedback loop. Collectively, these findings demonstrate how targeting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, which CSCs rely on through various regulators and feedback loops, could reduce cancer stemness and restore sensitivity to TKIs.

Additional studies have discovered multiple strategies for how HCC reinforces and secures the Wnt/β-catenin pathway through inhibition of CTNNB1 degradation to maintain cancer stemness, TKI resistance, and EMT (Fig. 1).

One mechanism by which Wnt/β-catenin signaling is stabilized is through lysosome-associated protein transmembrane 4β (LAPTM4B). LAPTM4B has been shown to interact with ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolases 1 and 14 (USP1 and USP14), thereby inhibiting β-catenin ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation, accompanied by alterations in both mRNA and protein levels of GSK3β and APC [108]. LAPTM4B triggers EMT in CD133+ CSCs, and increases lenvatinib resistance, highlighting its protumor role in regulating β-catenin degradation. Mice with subcutaneous injection of CD133+ Huh7-shLAPTM4B cells had significantly reduced tumor size, and overexpression of β-catenin rescued the phenotype. Also, the β-catenin degradation promoter, XAV-939 [109], suppressed stemness-related genes in LAPTM4B-overexpressing cells, showing LAPTM4B’s role in securing the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway to trigger EMT, upregulate lenvatinib resistance, and promote cancer stemness.

Another pathway involves hexokinase domain containing 1 (HKDC1), which contributes to sustaining Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity [110]. Specifically, HKDC1 has been identified to bind to GSK3β, as supported by immunoprecipitation using a FLAG-tagged HKDC1 plasmid. Furthermore, HKDC1 knockout led to higher levels of the phosphorylated β-catenin (Ser33/37/Thr41), and treatment with the GSK3β inhibitor, laduviglusib [111], could restore the stemness features in HKDC1 knockout cells [110]. Collectively, these findings indicate that HKDC1 further stabilizes the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by inhibiting its negative regulator. An additional candidate for stabilizing the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by interacting with and regulating GSK3β is the endogenous kinase, CDK6 [112]. CDK6 is upregulated in lenvatinib-resistant HCC cell lines and patients, and it exhibits stemness features and influences the CSC markers of CD47, CD90, CD133, and EpCAM [112]. By treating lenvatinib-resistant HCC cell-derived xenografts and resistant mouse models with a CDK6 inhibitor, lenvatinib sensitivity could be restored with reduced total β-catenin levels. This further supports that blocking the CDK6 to regulate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway represents a promising strategy to overcome TKI resistance in HCC.

However, it should be noted that strategies aimed at inhibiting LAPTM4B, HKDC1, and CDK6 to enhance β-catenin degradation through regulatory complex activities such as GSK3β, AXIN1, and APC are likely to be ineffective in CTNNB1-mutated HCC, as the mutation inhibits regulation by phosphorylation (e.g. serine 33, serine 37, and threonine 41 mutations prevent GSK3β-induced phosphorylation [17]) and subsequent proteasomal degradation. By contrast, such approaches may retain therapeutic potential in alternative mechanisms of Wnt/β-catenin hyperactivation, such as ZNRF3 mutation, where the β-catenin is responsive to GSK3β activity. Whether the benefits of inhibiting LAPTM4B, HKDC1, and CDK6 could still emerge in the CTNNB1-mutated subset needs further investigation.

ICIs have emerged as one of the most transformative advances in cancer therapy over the past decade, demonstrating significantly improved clinical outcomes and long-term survival rates for patients across a spectrum of cancer types by harnessing the immune system to target malignancies [113]. Cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4 and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) are the primary targets in HCC, for which there are many clinically approved monoclonal antibody inhibitors [113, 114]. These immune checkpoints play a crucial role in suppressing T-cell immune activity, orchestrating a delicate balance to prevent excessive immune responses, and regulating homeostasis.

PD-1, with its ligand PD-L1, prevents T-cell receptor signaling and inhibits T-cell growth and cytokine production, resulting in T-cell exhaustion [115]. Their approved ICIs include nivolumab, pembrolizumab, atezolizumab, sintilimab, tislelizumab, and durvalumab [116]. CTLA4, located on the activated T-cell membrane, primarily regulates T-cell activation in the early stages within lymph nodes by competing with CD28 for binding to B7 ligands on antigen-presenting cells, thereby inhibiting T-cell proliferation and cytokine production [117]. Some of its approved ICIs include ipilimumab and tremelimumab [116]. Single-agent immunotherapy in HCC patients has shown limitations, with 20% showing ORRs in nivolumab [118], 16% in pembrolizumab [119], and 17.6% in tremelimumab [120]. Combination therapies, such as atezolizumab + bevacizumab in IMbrave150 [121], tremelimumab + durvalumab in HIMALAYA [122], and pembrolizumab + lenvatinib in LEAP-002 [123], have shown promising results in improving OS and quality of care, transforming the treatment approach for patients with advanced liver cancer. Although the combination therapy has led to a significant advancement in HCC management, the highest response rate was only about one-third, observed in the IMbrave150 with atezolizumab and bevacizumab combination treatment [6], emphasizing the complex microenvironment and immune evasion in HCC. Therefore, there remains a critical need for a deeper understanding of the HCC immune landscape for better ICIs efficacy.

The low infiltration of T cells in the immune-excluded, noninflamed, or “cold” tumors results in reduced response rates and efficacy of ICIs compared to inflamed or “hot” tumors, which are characterized by abundant immune cell activity [124]. Within the tumor microenvironment (TME), immune cells play pivotal roles, with CD8+ T cells serving as key drivers of immune surveillance, effectively targeting tumor cells [125]. Protumor immune cells such as macrophage type 2 (M2) cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, as well as other contributors in TME, such as cancer-associated fibroblasts, also contribute to “cold” tumors through regulating CD8+ T cells, further complicating immune responses [125–127]. These intricate interactions within the TME highlight the challenges in treating “cold” tumors. Although some patients (∼33%) with hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin pathway are classified in the inflamed class [128], the majority are associated with immune evasion and the “cold” tumor phenotype, as demonstrated by enrichment in the immune-excluded class or non-T-cell inflamed tumors, limiting the effectiveness of ICIs [40, 41, 44, 124]. To overcome these barriers and enhance ICI therapy outcomes, strategic approaches are needed to target this pathway to transform “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors for robust immune infiltration and better ICI treatments.

Although definitive clinical causality remains to be established, numerous preclinical studies have underscored the role of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in fostering an immunosuppressive “cold” TME in HCC. Prior research has linked its activation to inducing T-cell exhaustion and apoptosis [129], accumulation of polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs) [130], recruitment of Tregs [131], depletion of dendritic cells (DCs) [44, 132], a shift from antitumor M1-like to protumor M2-like tumor-associated macrophages [133, 134], and reduced chemokine production essential for recruiting many antitumor immune cells [135]. Many studies have previously reported that hyperactivation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is associated with a reduction in the expression of cytokines that can attract immune cells [44, 135–137], and more recent investigations have further elucidated novel mechanisms underlying this regulatory effect, providing deeper insights into the interplay between Wnt/β-catenin signaling and immune responses (Fig. 2).

Schematic illustration of the association of Wnt/β-catenin pathway hyperactivation in HCC with resistance to immunotherapy by fostering an immune-cold TME. The Wnt/β-catenin pathway suppresses CCL5, SASP cytokines, and other proinflammatory cytokines, thereby impairing recruitment and activity of CD8+ T-cells and DCs. Conversely, upregulation of PF4, MMP9, CXCL1, VEGFα, and PD-L1 attract protumor immune cell types such as the Tregs, TANs, M2-like macrophages, and PMN-MDSCs, reinforcing an immunosuppressive TME.

A study reports how the hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway could recruit TANs in TME, which are well-known for their protumor function in impairing T-cell proliferation, function, and infiltration, leading to immunotherapy evasion [138]. One recent finding is through the CT10 regulator of kinase-like (CRKL), which competes with and inhibits AXIN1 in binding to β-catenin, preventing phosphorylation of β-catenin and thereby increasing the half-life and nuclear localization of β-catenin [127]. Overlapping upregulated downstream proteins in CRKL overexpressing cells were CXCL1 and VEGFα, which increased the infiltration of PD-L1+ Ly6G + protumor neutrophils in the mouse model [127]. In patients with Progressive Disease (PD) compared to those with partial response in anti-PD-1 treatment, CRKL, β-catenin, VEGFα, and CXCL1 protein levels are significantly elevated as well, with increased CD66b + TANs infiltration and diminished CD8+ T-cell infiltration. Combination therapy of XAV939 [109] and anti-PD-1 in a mouse model with CRKL-overexpressing cells orthotopically implanted restored the sensitivity of the anti-PD-1 treatment, enhanced tumor-suppressive effects, decreased TANs, and increased CD8+ T-cell activity. Similarly, combination treatment with the Wnt/β-catenin inhibitor XAV939, VEGFα inhibitor bevacizumab, and lenvatinib demonstrated significant efficacy in restoring sensitivity to anti-PD-1 treatment in patient-derived organotypic tumor spheroids co-cultured with CD8+ T cells and CD66b + TANs [127]. These findings indicate that inhibition of the hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin pathway and VEGFα shows promising effects in treating immunosuppressive HCC tumors, although further research into CXCL1 inhibition is still needed. One of the interesting roles of TANs is their capacity to release chromatin-based structures known as neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), web-like structures to fight microbial invasions, which are also reported in HCC for their contribution to immunosuppressive TME, metastasis, and growth [139–141]. Future investigations could explore whether TANs, upregulated in HCC with hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin pathway, result in more NET formation, or whether activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway itself could directly stimulate NET release, to create an immunosuppressive environment.

DCs are another critical antitumor immune cell type that stimulates CD8+ T cells and helps with the infiltration of CD8+ T cells [142]. A study highlighted the Wnt/β-catenin pathway’s role in reducing CCL5 and impairing DC recruitment in the TME of a MYC + CTNNB1 HCC mouse model and human CTNNB1-mutant HCC samples [44]. An additional recent study supported this suppression of CCL5 through the transcription factor IKZF1, which is upregulated with activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, thereby disrupting the CCL5-CCR5 interaction of DCs [132]. The combination therapy of anti-PD-1 therapy and ICG-001, a Wnt/β-catenin signaling inhibitor, increased CCL5 expression, resulting in enhanced DC and CD8+ T-cell infiltration in the TME of a mouse HCC model [132]. This showed how the hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin pathway can contribute to a cold tumor environment by decreasing DC and CD8+ T-cell activity, along with TANs recruitment.

Further research highlighted how aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway recruits PMN-MDSCs, which inhibit CD8+ T cell activity and promote tumor progression [130]. A study found abundant proportions of PMN-MDSC in the mass cytometry by time of flight (CyTOF) in a stable β-catenin-overexpressed mouse tumor compared to the control [143]. Platelet factor 4 (PF4), or CXCL4, upregulated by TCF1 and LEF1 transcription factors, can attract MDSCs to the tumor. Although intratumoral injection of recombinant PF4 cytokines in mice did not show significant liver/body weight changes, it reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration and increased MDSC accumulation in the tumor, as indicated by the MDSC markers of S100A8 and S100A9 levels. The study reported that PF4 promotes MDSC recruitment via CXCR3, as anti-CXCR3 and AMG-487 inhibitors [144] blocked PF4-mediated MDSC migration in vitro and in vivo [143]. These findings established that CTNNB1-driven MDSC accumulation is CXCR3-dependent through PF4 regulation [143]. It is noteworthy that CXCR3 is also expressed on natural killer (NK) cells [145], which could potentially be recruited by PF4, secreted as a result of activated Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Nevertheless, CyTOF analysis from the PF4 study clearly demonstrated a reduction in NK cells in the tumor compared with the nontumor tissue [143]. Additional studies could be conducted to verify the role of PF4 in NK cell recruitment and, if confirmed, to identify other factors that may exert a stronger suppressive effect on NK cells than PF4, resulting in significantly decreased NK-cell infiltration in TME.

Multiple studies have shown that the Wnt/β-catenin pathway manipulates numerous cytokines, thereby promoting an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). For instance, Wong et al. demonstrated that Wnt/β-catenin signaling inhibits several senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) cytokines, including IL6, CXCL8 (IL8), CXCL9, and CXCL5, via TNFRSF19, a downstream target of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [146]. In patient-derived cell cultures of NAFLD-HCC and CTNNB1-mutated cell lines, TNFRSF19 was identified as a key effector that regulates p38 MAPK levels [146], which in turn can modulate SASP secretion [147]. Notably, TNFRSF19 knockdown in vivo increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration, thereby countering an immunosuppressive TME. Accumulating evidence supports the idea that Wnt/β-catenin activation manipulates cytokine release, helping to evade immunotherapy.

Aberrant Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation has also been reported to promote an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by utilizing other TME modulators, such as the enzyme matrix metallopeptidase 9 (MMP9), which is transcriptionally regulated by TCF7 through the β-catenin pathway [148]. In an MMP9 knockdown orthotopic hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) model, researchers observed not only reduced tumor formation and improved survival but also a shift from protumor M2-like macrophages to antitumor M1-like macrophages, downregulation of PD-1 in CD8+ T cells, and enhanced immune memory responses. Moreover, CD8+ T cells stimulated with MMP9 showed reduced secretion of granzyme B, interferon gamma, and tumor necrosis factor α. MMP9 proteolyzes SSH1, preventing its interaction with CXCR3 and inhibiting ERK and AKT phosphorylation in CD8+ T cells, thereby impairing their chemotaxis, activation, and migration.

Another mechanism is through upregulating immune checkpoints. Lehrich et al. [149] identified several transcription factors, such as IRF2 and POU2F1, that are upregulated in β-catenin knockout livers of mice and mice responding to the treatment with CTNNB1 siRNA encapsulated in a lipid nanoparticle (LNP-CTNNB1). The TCGA-LIHC cohort showed that patients with an activated Wnt/β-catenin pathway had low expression of IRF2 and POU2F1 target genes, which was further supported by observations of reduced tumor burden and fewer Tregs, as well as increased CD4+ T cells and B cells, when these genes were upregulated in β-catenin-mutated mouse liver. IRF2 is known for repressing PD-L1 to increase CD8+ T-cell killing [150], as it can compete with IRF1 in the IRF response elements in the CD274 (PD-L1) promoter sites, and block IRF1-mediated PD-L1 transcription [151]. β-Catenin can repress the IRF2 transcription factor to elevate IRF1-mediated PD-L1 transcription, leading to T-cell exhaustion and an immunosuppressive TME [149]. Although POU2F1 has been implicated in tumor proliferation and metastasis [152], its specific role in conferring immunosuppressive characteristics remains poorly understood and requires further studies to elucidate the mechanism and explore potential therapeutic strategies. An additional method involves β-catenin regulation of the NF-κB pathway. β-Catenin forms a physical complex with p65 to suppress its translocation and activation, thereby inhibiting NF-κB pathway activation in HCC [153]. Consequently, the uncontrolled accumulation of β-catenin in the HCC with β-catenin mutation suppresses the NF-κB pathway, a master regulator of innate immunity and inflammation in the TME [154].

However, given the intricate networks of cytokines with feedback loops, synergies, and antagonisms, and the complexity of diverse types of immune-cell recruitment, inhibition of certain released cytokines or even the whole Wnt/β-catenin pathway may be minimally effective, risk disrupting beneficial immune activity, and induce unintended toxicities. Moreover, the majority of supporting data comes from preclinical in vitro experiments and mouse models, whose relevance to human tumors in the TME may not fully recapitulate; for example, mice lack the homologous gene for CXCL8, a key neutrophil-attractant [155], and IL26, an activator of macrophages [156]. Also, there could be not-yet-known cytokines regulated by the Wnt/β-catenin pathway that could retain antitumor activity. For example, the leukocyte cell-derived chemotaxin 2 (LECT2) is directly upregulated by the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in HCC [157], and is reported to inhibit homing of and control cytokine polarization in invariant natural killer T cells, thereby producing a less aggressive TME, although its precise mechanism remains unknown [158]. Therefore, given the complex heterogeneity and lack of consensus regarding the prognostic impact of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in HCC, more studies are required to determine its precise roles in shaping the TME and to establish whether its net effect is ultimately favorable or unfavorable for patient outcomes. Even if immunosuppressive features predominate, carefully designed clinical studies will be essential to determine the safety and efficacy of targeting protumor immune-modulatory effects observed in preclinical models.

Immunotherapy, alone or in combination with TKIs, has emerged as a promising treatment for HCC patients. However, the hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway drives resistance to both TKIs and so-called revolutionary immunotherapy [159] as mentioned above, limiting their efficacy. Although no Wnt/β-catenin–targeted single or combination therapy has been fully evaluated in clinical trials, many recent preclinical studies have shown how knocking down CTNNB1 with LNP-CTNNB1 or inhibiting its pathway with small molecule inhibitors such as WNTinib [160], ICG-001 [161], or tankyrase inhibitors XAV939 and IWR-1 [162] enhances the sensitivity of HCC to TKIs or immunotherapy by increasing the drug efficacy or reprogramming the TME to favor antitumor immune response, showing promising results in preclinical models [132, 134, 149, 163–165].

In preclinical studies, WNTinib, a multikinase inhibitor that can increase nuclear EZH2 to suppress Wnt signaling, exhibited greater tumor regression compared to standard treatments, including sorafenib, regorafenib, lenvatinib, and cabozantinib, in organoid allografts derived from the MYC + Δ90 β-catenin mouse model [160]. This enhanced efficacy was also observed in advanced-stage tumor settings, where MYC-CTNNB1 xenografts reached volumes of 300–400 mm3. Furthermore, WNTinib even outperformed sorafenib in an autochthonous CTNNB1-driven HCC model, which recapitulates immunotherapy-resistant HCC. Notably, treatment with WNTinib resulted in complete tumor eradication in 60% of treated animals, extending survival beyond 300 days post-treatment.

Additionally, PRI-724 [166], a potent and selective CREB-binding protein (CBP)/β-catenin antagonist that disrupts the interaction between β-catenin and the transcriptional co-activator CBP, has demonstrated marked synergy with sorafenib in preclinical HCC models. In in vitro studies and in vivo xenograft tumor models, the combination significantly enhanced apoptosis, suppressed clonogenic survival, reversed sorafenib resistance, and achieved greater tumor growth inhibition than sorafenib monotherapy, supporting its potential as another promising Wnt/β-catenin-targeted agent for overcoming TKI resistance in HCC [68].

Another study using mice injected subcutaneously with Hepa1-6 vector or Hepa1-6 CTNNB1 (S45P) and treated with or without Wnt/β-catenin inhibitor ICG-001 showed significantly increased levels of CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 chemokines, CD8+ T cells, and M1-like macrophages in ICG-001-treated CTNNB1 mice compared to the untreated control group [163]. An additional study exploring Hepa1-6 and H22-induced mouse tumors found that combining ICG-001 with anti-PD-1 therapy markedly boosted CD8+ T-cell and DC infiltration while reducing Tregs, enhancing antitumor immune responses [132].

Further extending the therapeutic potential of Wnt/β-catenin inhibition, treatment with XAV939 and IWR-1 in vitro reduced PD-L1 expression, and treatment with XAV939 plus anti-PD-L1 in vivo showed significantly smaller tumors with enriched T-cell function and innate immunity pathways, and M1-like macrophage functions [134]. This was consistent with previous findings that Wnt/β-catenin signaling reduces M1-like macrophage surface markers and promotes M2-like macrophage polarization, and knocking down β-catenin or blocking Wnt signaling reprograms the TME by converting protumor to antitumor macrophages, increasing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and decreasing Tregs [165].

Consistent with the findings, a separate study found the antitumor effect from treating a β-catenin-Nrf2 HTVI mouse model with LNP-CTNNB1 [149]. The treated mice showed a 3-fold increase in M1-like proinflammatory macrophages, increased T-cell and B-cell infiltration, and enhanced T-cell proliferation. However, the CTNNB1 siRNA-treated group had some mice with minimal response, accompanied by higher T-cell exhaustion and increased PD-L1 and PD-1 expression. When combined with anti-PD-1 treatment, the poorly responding mice showed improved outcomes, with reduced tumor growth and improved survival, demonstrating the effectiveness of the combined treatment.

Lastly, another study utilized a nanoparticle that can inhibit hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway (sBBI) and block PD-1/PD-L1 in the HCC patient-derived xenograft model [164]. The combination-treated group had significantly reduced tumor nodule size and weight and increased infiltration of CD3+/CD8+ T cells compared to the single treatment of sBBI or anti-PD-L1 group.

However, despite these advantages, the clinical development of many Wnt/β-catenin inhibitors faces significant hurdles, primarily due to toxicity concerns. The pathway is essential for the normal functioning of healthy tissues, especially in stem cell maintenance and tissue regeneration. Systemic inhibition can lead to gastrointestinal toxicity [167], bone homeostasis impairment [168], and disruptions in normal liver functions or tissue regeneration [169]. These safety concerns have driven the field toward advanced therapeutic approaches capable of robust pathway suppression with an improved safety profile. Recently, preclinical studies have shown promising drugs that could inhibit Wnt/β-catenin pathways without side effects. For instance, targeted delivery of LNP-CTNNB1, as mentioned above, achieved potent and sustained β-catenin knockdown in tumor tissue with no observable side effects. In treated mice, at doses yielding therapeutically relevant pathway inhibition, there was no mortality, no changes in body weight or liver function, and no histopathological changes in major organs such as lung, spleen, intestine, and heart. Moreover, animals exhibited no clinical signs of neurological, gastrointestinal, genitourinary, cardiovascular, or respiratory dysfunction [149]. A Phase 1 clinical trial is currently underway to evaluate LNP-CTNNB1 as monotherapy or in combination with pembrolizumab, which may offer new personalized therapeutic options for tumors driven by the hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin pathway while minimizing off-target effects (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT06600321) [170].

In another recent study, the multikinase inhibitor, WNTinib, demonstrated a favorable therapeutic index in a CTNNB1-driven HCC mouse model as previously mentioned. At doses that effectively abrogated tumor growth, WNTinib elicited no detectable toxicity across multiple organs, including the liver, intestine, and kidney [160]. These latest studies demonstrate that potent, tumor-specific Wnt/β-catenin inhibition can achieve robust antitumor efficacy without inducing toxicities, strongly supporting the need for the clinical trials and the development of safer precision therapies for patients with Wnt/β-catenin driven cancers.

Thanks to numerous studies, many patients now have various options to overcome their disease. However, as cancers rapidly adapt and develop resistance to novel therapies, the focus has shifted toward personalized treatments that optimize efficacy for individual patients. One such group includes HCC patients with the hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Although a consensus on its prognostic significance has not yet been reached, this review highlights recent preclinical evidence supporting that hyperactivation of the pathway could perpetuate poor prognosis despite advances in ICIs and TKIs by fostering cancer stemness, evading ferroptosis, sustaining tumor-cell survival, and contributing to an immunosuppressive “cold” tumor microenvironment that excludes effector T cells while recruiting protumor immune cells. Through these mechanisms, hyperactivation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway could thwart frontline treatments, contributing to drug resistance and underscoring the urgent need for alternative therapeutic strategies. Preclinical models demonstrate that integrating Wnt/β-catenin inhibitors can reprogram the TME and restore drug sensitivity, yielding reduced tumor burden, survival benefits, and improved responses. This approach could represent a promising targeted therapy for HCC patients who do not respond to the first-line treatment due to the hyperactivated Wnt/β-catenin pathway.

However, important questions remain because of the inconsistent prognostic data reported across studies. Therapeutic inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in HCC tumors with hyperactivation may not produce favorable outcomes, contrasting with the promising results observed in preclinical studies. This lack of consensus highlights significant research gaps arising from the marked heterogeneity of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in HCC. A currently understood heterogeneity arises from mutations in different genes. Although various gene mutations activate the same Wnt/β-catenin pathway, the resulting tumor characteristics, TME composition, and downstream effects differ substantially. This complexity is further increased by different point mutations within the same gene. For example, mutations at residues T41, S45, and the D32–S37 region of β-catenin have been shown to produce distinct levels of pathway activity. Therefore, further studies are required to clarify additional contributing factors, such as co-occurring genetic alterations, specific mutation sites, underlying etiology, and crosstalk with other intratumoral signaling pathways or the surrounding TME.

Additionally, different Wnt/β-catenin pathway inhibitors also have distinct targets, response profiles, and efficacies. Whether the use of single or multiple mutation-specific inhibitors in genes responsible for aberrant Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation, or targeting its specific downstream, is more effective requires further studies. Although no Wnt/β-catenin-targeted single or combination therapy has yet been fully tested in clinical trials and demonstrated its clinical benefit for HCC patients, the reported data justify clinical exploration. Therefore, ongoing research and clinical trials are critical to transform the treatment landscape for HCC patients with Wnt/β-catenin pathway hyperactivation, offering the potential for improved outcomes for those who do not respond to current first-line treatments.
