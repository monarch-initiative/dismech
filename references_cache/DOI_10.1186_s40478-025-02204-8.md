# Biallelic null RAB3GAP1 variants impair cortical development and autophagy in Warburg Micro syndrome: evidence from fetal brain tissue and patient fibroblasts

Warburg Micro syndrome (WARBM) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by ocular, neurodevelopmental, and neuroendocrine abnormalities, most commonly caused byRAB3GAP1variants.RAB3GAP1encodes the catalytic subunit of the RAB3 GTPase-activating protein (RAB3GAP), which regulates vesicular release and membrane trafficking through Rab GTPase modulation. Although in vitro studies suggest that RAB3GAP1 dysfunction impairs autophagy, the neuropathology of WARBM in the developing Human brain remains undocumented. Here, we present the first detailed neuropathological and molecular analysis of Human WARBM, based on two related cases: a 3-month-old infant and a 23-week gestation fetus with biallelicRAB3GAP1pathogenic variants. Histological examination of the fetal cerebral cortex and cerebellum revealed selective vulnerability, with cortical plate thinning, while Purkinje cells appeared preserved. Consistently, immunohistochemistry demonstrated widespread RAB3GAP1 expression in the frontal cortex of controls, but no detectable staining in WARBM, coinciding with disrupted neurogenic niches, including reduced SOX2-positive progenitor cells, disorganized radial glia, and increased caspase-3 expression. These changes were associated with fewer DCX- and CTIP2-positive neurons, which may reflect a combination of altered neurogenesis, impaired neuronal migration and increased cell death. However, no gross brain malformations were observed in the fetus, whereas cortical malformations were evident in the 3-month-old infant, pointing to a progressive neurodevelopmental disruption potentially driven by fetal corticogenesis defects. These changes were accompanied by marked dysregulation of autophagy markers, linking impaired autophagy to disrupted cortical development. In contrast, autophagy disruption in the fetal lens was already associated with bilateral cataracts. Studies in patient-derived skin fibroblasts using immunocytochemistry, western blotting, and electron microscopy confirmed autophagy defects and tissue-specific consequences to RAB3GAP1 disruption in WARBM.

Collectively, our findings provide the first in-depth neuropathological characterization of WARBM, highlighting the essential role of RAB3GAP1 in early Human and support autophagy as a key pathogenic feature in this severe disorder.

Warburg Micro syndrome (WARBM) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by ocular, neurodevelopmental, and neuroendocrine abnormalities [1]. Diagnosis is based on clinical presentation and characteristic brain MRI findings, including postnatal microcephaly, polymicrogyria, hypoplasia of the corpus callosum, cerebellar atrophy, and myelination defects, in association with biallelic variants in one of four genes:RAB3GAP1,RAB3GAP2,RAB18, orTBC1D20[2,3,4]. Most cases are caused by disease-causing variants inRAB3GAP1,which encodes the catalytic subunit of the RAB3 GTPase-activating protein (RAB3GAP) complex. Together with its regulatory subunit, RAB3GAP2, it modulates the activity cycles of RAB family small GTPases, including RAB3 and RAB18 [5,6,7], which are essential for intracellular membrane trafficking and vesicular release [8,9,10,11]. Despite being described over three decades ago [1], the neuropathological features of WARBM remain poorly understood. Current knowledge is largely based on neuroimaging, genetic, and clinical studies in affected children [4,12,13,14]. Only a single prenatal case has been reported to date, a 19-week fetus diagnosed via ultrasound and genetic testing due to bilateral cataracts, but this case provided no information on fetal brain development or pathology [15]. As a result, the pathophysiology of WARBM remains speculative, limiting efforts to identify therapeutic targets that might improve clinical phenotype. This lack of understanding stems not only from the extreme rarity of the disorder but also from the fact thatRab3gap1knockout mice fail to faithfully recapitulate the human phenotype. For instance,Rab3gap1knockout mice show synaptic transmission and plasticity defects linked to RAB3 dysregulation, but they do not develop the brain, ocular, or genital abnormalities observed in Humans [10]. In model organisms such asC. elegans, combined loss of function ofRab3gap1(rbg-1) withRab3gap2(rbg-2) orRab18leads to impaired autophagy [16,17], a fundamental pathway that maintains cellular homeostasis through the degradation and recycling of intracellular components [18,19]. Consistently, depletion of RAB3GAP1 with RAB3GAP2, or RAB18 in Human fibroblasts similarly impairs autophagy [20,21]. More recently, Ghate et al., showed that downregulation ofRAB3GAP1in Human iPSC-derived cortical neurons leads to reduced neurite outgrowth [22], suggesting a direct role in neuronal maturation and cortical circuit formation. However, the potential disruption of autophagy in these cells was not investigated, leaving this aspect unaddressed. In fact, autophagy defects have not yet been examined in Human WARBM brain tissue, and the effects of biallelicRAB3GAP1pathogenic variants on neurodevelopment and corticogenesis remain unexplored.

Here, we present the first in-depth neuropathological and molecular analysis of WARBM, based on two related cases harboring compound heterozygousRAB3GAP1variants: a proband studied at 3 months of age and a fetus at 23 gestational weeks (GW). This unique pairing provides a rare opportunity to compare early developmental abnormalities with their postnatal consequences. Neuropathological analysis of the fetus revealed that RAB3GAP1 loss-of-function caused pronounced cellular alterations in the cerebral cortex without macroscopic brain defects, alongside major impairments in the autophagy pathway. Altogether, this study expands our understanding of the early pathophysiological events underlying cortical malformations in WARBM and lays the groundwork for future investigations into critical developmental windows and cellular mechanisms in this devastating neurodevelopmental disorder.

The proband is a boy born to healthy parents following an uneventful full-term pregnancy and spontaneous vaginal delivery. WARBM diagnosis was suspected at one month old because of the association of bilateral microphthalmia, cataract, axial hypotonia, 3rd percentile head circumference and micropenis with unilateral cryptorchidism (Additional File 1: TableS1). During a subsequent pregnancy, bilateral cataracts were detected prenatally, raising suspicion of WARBM; termination of pregnancy (TOP) was performed at 23 gestational weeks (GW), hereafter referred to as FWARBM. The parents later had a third child, a female, who is currently healthy. For neuropathological and western blot analyses, brains from 23 GW control fetuses (FControl) were used (Additional File 2: TableS2; ABM approval: PFS25-11599).

The first child of a healthy, unrelated couple was evaluated at birth for a constellation of anomalies, including bilateral microphthalmia, congenital cataract, axial hypotonia, 3rd percentile head circumference, micropenis and unilateral cryptorchidism. The proband was profoundly blind despite undergoing bilateral cataract extraction at 2 months of age. Targeted gene panel analysis identified a heterozygous nonsense variant inRAB3GAP1(NM_012233.3:c.68G > A), inherited from the father, predicted to introduce a premature stop codon at position 23 (NP 036365.1:p(Trp23Ter)) (Fig.1a). Subsequent exome sequencing identified a heterozygous deletion of exons 12–17 inRAB3GAP1inherited from the mother (Fig.1a). The 5′ and 3′ ends of the deletion were located in introns 11 and 17, respectively. Because the precise limits of the deletion were not established, the variant was designated NC_000002.12 (NM_012233.3):c.(79321 + 1_79322-1)_(103105 + 1_103106-1)del, according to HGVS nomenclature, and is hereafter referred to as g.ex12_ex17del (Fig.1a). The canonical RAB3GAP1 transcript comprises 24 exons and encodes a 921–amino acid protein. The deletion is predicted to generate a truncated protein of 340 amino acids resulting from a frameshift-induced premature stop codon (NP_036365.1:p.(Asp327Thrfs*14). The truncation at amino acid 340 removes the C-terminal GTPase activating domain [6], and some residues involved in the interaction with RAB3GAP2 [5]. Together, these alterations are consistent with a loss-of-function mechanism, in agreement with previous reports linking dysfunctional RAB3GAP1 catalytic domain to WARBM [12]. Brain MRI performed at 3 months of age demonstrated bilateral frontoparietal polymicrogyria and a short, thin and dysmorphic corpus callosum consistent with callosal dysgenesis (Fig.1b). During a subsequent pregnancy, the 18-week gestational ultrasound did not reveal any cerebral anomalies but detected bilateral increased echogenicity of the lenses, raising suspicion for cataracts and recurrence of the syndrome (Fig.1c). Post-termination (TOP) genetic testing confirmed the fetus harbored the same biallelicRAB3GAP1pathogenic variants as the proband (Fig.1a). Western blot analysis of protein extracts from the cortex and cerebellum of the WARBM fetus (FWARBM) showed absence of both the expected 130 kDa RAB3GAP1 band and the smaller 85 kDa isoform observed in control fetal brain tissue (FControl) (Fig.1d). Post-TOP examination at 23 weeks of gestation revealed no external malformations except a telecanthus, short palpebral fissures, micrognathism and slightly short hallux (Additional File 5: TableS4). Fetal growth parameters were within normal ranges (weight: 530 g, 50th percentile; crown-heel length: 30 cm, 50th percentile; crown-rump length: 21 cm, 50th percentile; head circumference: 20 cm, 25th–50th percentile; biparietal diameter: 53 mm, 25th percentile). The fetal brain appeared small (occipito-frontal diameter: 66 mm, 50th centile; weight:65 g, 5th–10th centiles) but structurally normal, with cortical gyration appropriate for gestational age (Additional File 6: Fig.S2). There were no anomalies observed after brain sectioning, the parenchyma was conserved, and the structures of the median line were visualized. Histological analysis of the eyes revealed rosettes of retinal tissue at the optic nerve insertion, consistent with bilateral focal retinal dysplasia, and small lenses composed of highly irregular, swollen, and extensively vacuolated fibers, confirming the presence of bilateral cataracts (Fig.1e). The couple subsequently had a third healthy daughter, heterozygous for the paternalRAB3GAP1nonsense variant (NM_012233.3:c.68G > A) (Fig.1a).

Normal corticogenesis depends on the production of an adequate number of neural stem/progenitor cells and the elaboration of a large variety of intermediate progenitors that will give rise to cortical neurons (~ 7 GW to 27 GW) and subsequently glial cells (beginning at ~ 16 GW for astrocytes) [26,27,28]. These events take place in the germinative zones formed by the ventricular (VZ) and subventricular (SVZ) zones. After neurogenesis, newly born neurons migrate through the intermediate zone and subplate (SP) in an inside-out manner to form the layered CP where post-migration neuronal development and organization takes place [28,29]. To explore further the consequences of RAB3GAP1 loss-of-function in corticogenesis, we examined RAB3GAP1 expression in the frontal lobe. We found that RAB3GAP1 was ubiquitously expressed in the VZ, SVZ, SP, and CP of control fetuses, whereas no staining was detected in FWARBM(Fig.3). To assess early neurodevelopmental processes, we analyzed SOX2, a transcription factor expressed by neural stem cells and intermediate progenitors within the germinative zones of the developing neocortex [30,31]. In FWARBM, the germinative zone area was reduced by approximatively 50% and remained significantly decreased (by approximatively 20%) after normalization to the total section surface area (8.9 ± 0.2% for FControlvs 7.1 ± 0.2% for FWARBM;p< 0.05) (Additional File 7: Fig.S3a and b).

As expected, SOX2-positive (SOX2⁺) cells were localized in the VZ and SVZ in both FWARBMand FControl(Fig.4a). However, the density of SOX2⁺ cells within the VZ of FWARBMwas reduced compared to controls (Fig.4b), despite no significant change in the overall cell density (Fig.4c). In contrast, the SOX2⁺ cells density in the SVZ remained comparable between FWARBMand FControl(Fig.4b), suggesting a preferential disruption of the proliferative VZ. At 23 GW, the prefrontal VZ is predominantly composed of radial glial (RG) progenitor population [32]. To further investigate their organization, we performed immunostaining for glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), an astroglial marker prominently expressed in apical RG (aRG) and basal RG (bRG) neural progenitors at this stage of Human cortical development [26,33]. In FControl, GFAP immunolabeling revealed a well-organized network of apical RG cells, with cell bodies in the VZ and long apical processes terminating in endfeet at the ventricular surface (Fig.4d).In contrast, FWARBMexhibited fewer discernible GFAP⁺ cells in the VZ, and their apical processes appeared disorganized and poorly structured, lacking clearly identifiable endfeet at the ventricular wall (Fig.4d). Morphological abnormalities of GFAP⁺ cells were also found in the SVZ, where basal processes, typically emitted by basal RG cells, were observed in FControl, but not in FWARBM(Fig.4d). Disruption of basal RG cells morphology was further confirmed by co-immunostaining GFAP with HOPX, a marker of those cells [26] (Fig.4e). Given the critical role of RG cells scaffolds in supporting neuronal migration, we next assessed the organization of migrating neurons by examining doublecortin (DCX), a marker of immature migrating neurons [3]. In FWARBM, the leading processes of DCX⁺ neurons migrating within the subplate were either markedly shorter compared to those in FControlor absent (Fig.5a). In the CP, FWARBMDCX⁺ neurons displayed a rounded morphology, contrasting with the elongated shape observed in controls and suggesting an alteration of neuronal polarity (Fig.5b). Quantification revealed a significant reduction in the number of DCX⁺ neurons in FWARBMcompared to FControlin both the CP and SP (Fig.5c). While in the SVZ of FControl, DCX immunolabeling highlighted organized columns of migrating neurons exiting the SVZ, this pattern was absent in FWARBM, where only rare, scattered DCX⁺ cells were detectable (Additional File 7: Fig.S3c and d).

We next examined the generation and positioning of deep-layer excitatory neurons by assessing CTIP2, a marker of layer V cortical neurons [34]. CTIP2⁺ cells displayed a similar distribution within the CP of both FWARBMand FControl, with mean distances to the marginal zone of 588.6 ± 48.5 µm in FControland 513.6 ± 23.8 µm in FWARBM, indicating substantial early cortical lamination is preserved (Fig.5d, Additional File 7: Fig.S3e and f). These distances were not affected after normalization to CP thickness (Additional File 7: Fig.S3e and f). However, the percentage of CTIP2+cells within the CP was significantly reduced in FWARBMeven after normalization to CP thickness (Fig.5e). The associated reduction in immature DCX⁺ migrating neurons and CTIP2⁺ cortical neurons together with defects in RG cell density and morphology, suggest that multiple neurodevelopmental processes, including cell proliferation, neurogenesis and migratory scaffolding, may contribute to the corticogenesis defect.

Given that cortical plate thinning may involve both proliferative zone disruption and increased apoptosis, we assessed cleaved caspase-3, a reliable marker of cell death, in paraffin-embedded sections [35]. In FControland FWARBM, cleaved caspase-3 positive cells were rarely detected among postmitotic neurons within the cortical plate and subplate (Additional File 8: Fig.S4a and b). In contrast, apoptotic cells were highly observed within the proliferative zones of FWARBM, particularly in the subventricular zone, with a 6.7 fold increase in caspase-3⁺ cells compared to the control (Additional File 8: Fig.S4c-e). This revealed an increased cell death, that may preferentially affect progenitor populations or immature neurons.

Alongside the marked cortical abnormalities, we also investigated the cerebellum, a region frequently impacted in this syndrome [36]. At 23 GW, no gross malformations were observed. However, Purkinje cells appeared less well delineated and discernible in FWARBMcompared to controls, although calbindin expression remained similar between groups (Additional File 9: Fig.S5), indicating that these cells were not markedly affected at 23 GW. Altogether these observations reveal that the early development of the cerebral cortex is particularly vulnerable at 23 GW to the disruption of functional RAB3GAP1.

Autophagy is a dynamic and tightly regulated cellular process, comprising four main stages following its induction: membrane nucleation (phagophore formation), membrane elongation with cargo recognition, autophagosome closure, and subsequent fusion with the lysosome to form the autolysosome, where degradation occurs [37]. The autophagosome is a double-membrane vesicle that sequesters cytoplasmic material, including proteins and organelles, targeted for degradation [37]. To investigate potential autophagy dysregulation in FWARBM, we analyzed key molecular markers involved in autophagosome biogenesis. Membrane expansion involves a tightly regulated sequence of events driven by core autophagy-related (ATG) proteins. In particular, two ubiquitin-like conjugation systems are central to this process: ATG3, an E2-like conjugating enzyme, and the ATG5-ATG12-ATG16L1 complex, which acts as an E3-like ligase. These systems mediate the lipidation of LC3-I with phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), generating LC3-II, its membrane-associated form essential for autophagosome maturation [38]. We also assessed p62/SQSTM1, a selective autophagy receptor that binds ubiquitinated cargo and interacts with LC3-II and that is widely used as an indicator of impaired autophagic flux [18,39,40]. Strikingly, we observed large p62- and LC3-positive aggregates in the lenses of FWARBM, while these proteins were almost undetectable in FControl(Fig.6a and b). In the frontal cortex and cerebellum of FWARBM, p62 was undetectable, ATG3 was markedly reduced, and in the cerebral cortex both ATG16L1 and LC3-I accumulated abnormally (Fig.6c).

Although WARBM was first described in 1993, the mechanisms by which RAB3GAP1 loss-of-function disrupts Human brain development remain poorly defined. Here, we provide the first in-depth neuropathological characterization of a Human fetal case ofRAB3GAP1-related WARBM at 23 GW, offering critical insights into the prenatal onset of cortical pathology. Our analysis reveals early alterations in cortical development, preceding macroscopic abnormalities, and highlights the disruption of key cellular processes, including radial glial density and organization, cell polarity, and autophagy along with increased cell death near the ventricle. All of which may individually or in combination contribute to defective cortical development, such as cortical plate thinning and reduced neuronal populations. The availability of both fetal and postnatal material from siblings carrying the same disease-causing variant enabled a unique comparison across developmental timepoints, providing a rare opportunity to trace the temporal emergence of pathological features. Our results suggest that the brain MRI cortical anomalies observed in WARBM children may result from cumulative neurodevelopmental defects, with the third trimester of gestation and early postnatal period further exacerbating cortical abnormalities. Altogether, our findings identifyRAB3GAP1as a critical regulator of Human corticogenesis and cellular homeostasis, advancing our understanding of the early pathophysiological events that contribute to brain malformations and dysfunctions in WARBM and related neurodevelopmental disorders.

In the lens of the fetus with WARBM, the loss of functional RAB3GAP1 has major early consequences, leading to in utero bilateral cataracts observable at 19 GW [15]. Induction of the Human lens occurs at around 28 days of gestation by the formation of the lens vesicle from the ectoderm that will be filled by fiber cells derived from epithelial cells in the lens germinative zone [43]. During their migration and differentiation, fiber cells lose their organelles and are compacted to create the organelle-free zone of the lens [44]. These processes continue throughout life, and their disruption during development or later in life leads to cataract formation [44]. Similarly to mice lackingTbc1d20,encoding another WARBM-associated RAB GTPase-activating protein [45], we observed significant alterations in fetal lens fiber cells, which was associated with high accumulation of p62 and LC3, suggesting that RABGAP-associated cataracts result from an autophagy process alteration. This is supported by the role of autophagy in lens organelle removal [44] and its link to cataract observed in autophagy-related gene disorders [46].

WARBM patients present severe neurodevelopmental abnormalities affecting both brain morphology and function [3]. However, the precise timing and cellular origins of these defects remain poorly understood. In this study, we show that polymicrogyria, a cortical malformation characteristic of WARBM, is already evident by 3 months of age. In contrast, the 23 GW fetal brain appears structurally intact, its size is reduced but still within the expected range for this developmental stage. The most notable change is a marked reduction in CP thickness. At this developmental stage, CP thickness predominantly reflects the number of newly generated excitatory neurons that have successfully migrated to their appropriate laminar positions, as neuronal maturation, neurite extension, and synaptogenesis are only beginning around 22–24 GW [27]. Thus, the early reduction in CP thickness likely reflects fewer neurons reaching the cortical plate, as no increase in cell death was observed within the CP itself. Our findings reveal a marked reduction in SOX2⁺ progenitor cells within the VZ of the FWARBMbrain, consistent with impaired generation and/or maintenance of the apical RG progenitor pool. Additionally, GFAP immunostaining further showed that the radial glia scaffold in the germinative zone was severely disorganized and morphologically altered, reflecting disrupted progenitor architecture and polarity. Elevated cell death within the germinative zone, along with these defects, likely reduces neuronal numbers and disrupts corticogenesis, ultimately leading to cortical plate thinning and the later emergence of cortical malformations [47]. In line with this, we observed a significant decrease in the number of immature DCX⁺ migrating neurons in the SP and CP, many of which displayed abnormal rounded morphologies and loss of leading processes, consistent with impaired neuronal polarity and migration. These alterations were accompanied by a substantial reduction in CTIP2⁺ deep-layer cortical neurons, which may result from a combination of reduced neuronal production, migration failure, and/or increased vulnerability to apoptosis in the FWARBMcortex. Whether these apoptotic cells correspond to newly generated neurons that fail to reach their proper cortical destinations, defective neuronal progenitors, or both remains to be determined. Given the critical role of CTIP2 in corticospinal motor neuron development [48], its reduced expression may underlie, at least in part, the motor deficits observed in WARBM patients [2,3]. Interestingly, these profound cortical abnormalities contrast with the cerebellum, where Purkinje cells appear morphologically intact at this developmental stage. This suggests a region- and/or time-specific vulnerability to RAB3GAP1 dysfunction during brain development. Since most of the WARBM patients suffer from cerebellar hypoplasia [36], it is plausible that cerebellar pathology emerges later in development or may primarily affect granule cells population. This is supported by the protracted timeline of cerebellar neurogenesis, with the majority of granule cells generated after birth [28]. Whether this temporal specificity reflects differential expression dynamics of RAB3GAP1, distinct molecular interactors in cerebellar versus cortical cells, or both, remains an important area for future investigation.

Taken together, our findings indicate that RAB3GAP1 loss-of-function disrupt corticogenesis in the Human fetal brain, with progenitor depletion, RG disorganization, reduced number of neurons, and increased cell death. While based on a single developmental time point (23 GW), these early defects likely contribute to postnatal cortical atrophy and malformations in WARBM.

Despite the presence of defective autophagy in FWARBM, no macroscopic abnormalities are observed at 23 GW, aligning with prior evidence that pathogenic variants in autophagy-related genes exert limited effects on early brain morphology [49]. However, autophagy plays a central and pleiotropic role during neurodevelopment, controlling multiple steps of central nervous system development, including stem and progenitor cell proliferation, neuronal migration, differentiation and maturation [38]. This explains why variants in autophagy-related genes are associated with neurodevelopmental disorders [38,50]. Defective autophagy may compromise cellular quality control and the renewal of cell content, triggering apoptosis and likely contributing to the increased cell death observed in the germinative zones of FWARBM, a mechanism also described in aging-related brain diseases [51,52]. Such early disruptions in cellular homeostasis can be particularly critical around birth, when autophagy becomes increasingly important to cope with environmental and nutritional changes [3]. Consequently, constitutive defects in autophagy may exacerbate neurodevelopmental abnormalities postnatally and contribute to the dramatic differences in cortical development observed between 23 GW and 3 months. In this context, RAB3GAP1 deficiency may further impair post-migratory cortical organization, such as neuronal maturation, synaptogenesis, synaptic and neuronal activity [53]. This is further supported by recent findings showing that RAB3GAP1 downregulation disrupts neuronal maturation and neurite outgrowth in Human iPSC-derived cortical neurons [22]. Given its established role in synaptic homeostasis [10] and autophagy,RAB3GAP1pathogenic variants could significantly impact the formation and refinement of neural circuits during early postnatal life.

We observed that RAB3GAP1 deficiency also led to morphological abnormalities in polarized cells of the fetal frontal cortex. Priorin vitrostudies have shown that RAB3GAP1 regulates intracellular trafficking [22,54], a process essential for oriented transport in polarized cells, which underlies proper brain development [55,56] and supports efficient autophagic flux [57,58]. Structural analyses suggest that RAB3GAP1 shares similarities with Zwilch, a component of the outer kinetochore that recruits dynein, a motor protein important for retrograde transport and which contributes to neuronal migration and cell division [5,59]. These findings in the human fetal brain support previous evidence, confirming the tight interconnection between intracellular trafficking and autophagy and their joint importance for neurodevelopment and brain function [38,50,60]. Clarifying the respective contributions of these processes to the clinical manifestations of WARBM will be essential for a better understanding of the disease’s pathophysiology. Importantly, WARBM appears to represent a valuable model for investigating the interplay between intracellular trafficking and autophagy, and their roles in developmental brain abnormalities.

In the WARBM fetal brain, our results suggest that LC3-I lipidation is altered and support a role for RAB3GAP1 in autophagosome biogenesis [21]. In contrast, in the fetal lens and proband fibroblasts, no evidence supported a defect in autophagy initiation, but a defect in autophagosome degradation. The reduced activation of autophagy by starvation suggests a complex alteration in autophagic flux during stress-induced autophagy, as reported inRAB3GAP1KO HeLa cells [61]. Interestingly, p62 accumulated in the fetal lens and proband fibroblasts but was almost undetectable in the brain, supporting previous thoughts that autophagy defects are tissue-dependent [3,16]. Subcellular ultrastructural analysis inRAB3GAP1-mutated Human neurons will be necessary to assess the exact consequences ofRAB3GAP1pathogenic variants on autophagic flux in post-mitotic cells, as well as the possible tissue specificity, which may depend on tissue-specific molecular partners.

RAB3GAP1 was initially described as a modulator of synaptic release through RAB3 activation [10,62], which in turn can act on autophagy [63]. In Human fibroblasts, the functions of RAB3GAP1 in autophagy are RAB3-independent [21] but appear to rely on the upstream regulation of RAB18 [20]. By contrast, in HeLa cells, RAB3GAP1 contributes to autophagosome formation through RAB3A modulation [64]. These observations underscore the context-dependent nature of autophagy regulation, which can differ both by cell type and by the method used to modulate autophagic flux. Interestingly, RAB3GAP1 and RAB18 are involved in lipid droplet catabolism adjusting the availability of lipid-derived metabolites [61], a suggested lipid source for autophagosome formation [65], more important under nutrient-deprived conditions [61]. In this context, the accumulation of lipid droplets observed in patient-derived fibroblasts suggests an impairment in lipid mobilization, potentially explaining their inability to upregulate autophagy in response to starvation, when cellular lipid demand is heightened. In theC. elegansmodel of Vici syndrome (EPG5variants), loss-of-function ofrgb-1(RAB3GAP1 in Humans) activates lysosomal biogenesis and function through RAB7 modulation, which restores autophagy [17]. Such a mechanism seems unlikely in Human brain where RAB3GAP1 dysfunction leads to a defect in autophagosome biogenesis. In Human cells, proteomic analysis suggested RAB3GAP1 as a potential interactor of the ATG8 family [66]. Identifying the specific molecular partners of RAB3GAP1 in autophagy will be a central point of investigation for understanding the molecular pathophysiology of WARBM, and may provide insights for future therapeutic exploration.
