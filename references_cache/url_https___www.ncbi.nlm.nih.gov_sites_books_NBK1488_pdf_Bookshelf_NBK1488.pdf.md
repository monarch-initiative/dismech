Summary
Clinical characteristics
PTEN hamartoma tumor syndrome (PHTS) is characterized by skin and mucosal lesions, macrocephaly,
gastrointestinal polyps, and a high risk for benign and malignant tumors of the breast, thyroid, and
endometrium. There is also an increased risk of renal cell carcinoma, colorectal carcinoma, and melanoma.
Some individuals have developmental delay and/or autism spectrum disorder. PHTS includes the following
clinical phenotypes: Cowden syndrome (CS), Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndrome (BRRS), Proteus-like
syndrome, and apparently isolated adult-onset Lhermitte-Duclos disease (LDD). Regardless of clinical
phenotype, individuals with a germline PTEN pathogenic variant are thought to have the same cancer risks.
Diagnosis/testing
The diagnosis of PHTS is established in a proband with a heterozygous germline PTEN pathogenic variant
identified by molecular genetic testing.
Management
Treatment of manifestations: Management per multidisciplinary care team, ideally with PHTS expertise, includes
treatment for symptomatic skin lesions; endoscopy with polypectomy; standard treatment for colorectal cancer,
renal cell carcinoma, and endometrial cancer; monitoring of thyroid nodules and multinodular goiter; treatment
of breast cancer per breast cancer specialist; prophylactic mastectomy as needed; preventative hysterectomy on
completion of childbearing; total abdominal hysterectomy for multiple fibroids; developmental and educational
support; seizure treatment per neurologist; management of vascular anomalies per multidisciplinary vascular
team; for atypical LDD, referral to neuro-oncologist, neurologist, and neurosurgeon; for those with suspected
cerebrospinal fluid obstruction, referral for surgical evaluation; treatment of musculoskeletal features per
orthopedist and physical therapist; family and social work support.

Surveillance: Annual comprehensive physical and dermatologic exam; education regarding signs and symptoms
of cancer annually starting at age 18 years or five years before the youngest cancer diagnosis in the family;
annual thyroid exam and ultrasound starting at age 12 years; monthly breast self-exam starting at age 18 years;
clinical breast exam every six to 12 months starting at age 25 years; mammogram with consideration of
tomosynthesis and breast MRI with contrast annually starting at age 30 years; consider kidney imaging every
one to two years starting at age 40 years; assess for symptoms of endometrial cancer every one to two years
starting at age 30 years with consideration of endometrial biopsy; transvaginal ultrasound in postmenopausal
women as needed; assess for clinical manifestations of gastrointestinal polyps and colorectal carcinoma annually;
colonoscopy every five years starting at age 35 years and more frequently in those with polyps; monitor
developmental progress and educational needs annually throughout childhood and adolescence; assessment for
autism spectrum disorder as needed; brain imaging every three to 12 months in those with typical LDD; brain
imaging every six to 12 months following LDD resection; monitor meningiomas with imaging; annual clinical
assessment for scoliosis and musculoskeletal asymmetry; in those with clinical phenotype of Proteus syndrome,
follow recommended surveillance; assess family and social work needs annually.
Agents/circumstances to avoid: Cutaneous lesions should be excised only if malignancy is suspected or symptoms
(e.g., pain, deformity, increased scarring) are significant. Surgical removal may lead to keloid formation and
rapid recurrence of lesions. Therefore, a multidisciplinary team should evaluate the risks and benefits of lesion
removal. Exercise caution with diagnostic/therapeutic radiation exposure; balance benefit versus potential
secondary malignancy risk.
Evaluation of relatives at risk: Molecular genetic testing for the familial PTEN pathogenic variant is
recommended for at-risk relatives of an affected individual. Family members who have the PTEN pathogenic
variant (and therefore have PHTS) require an initial evaluation and ongoing surveillance. Molecular genetic
testing for the familial PTEN pathogenic variant in early childhood is often appropriate because timely detection
of early-onset manifestations can guide management.
Genetic counseling
PHTS is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. Some individuals diagnosed with PHTS have an affected
parent. (Note: Because external manifestations of PHTS may be subtle or variably expressed, diagnoses within
families are sometimes only recognized after identification of a more severely affected individual.)
Approximately 10%-44% of individuals diagnosed with PHTS have the disorder as the result of a de novo
pathogenic variant. Each child of an individual with PHTS has a 50% chance of inheriting the PTEN pathogenic
variant. Once the PTEN pathogenic variant has been identified in an affected family member, predictive genetic
testing for at-risk family members and prenatal/preimplantation genetic testing are possible.

Diagnosis
Clinical diagnostic criteria cannot be used to establish the diagnosis of PTEN hamartoma tumor syndrome
(PHTS); molecular testing is required to establish the diagnosis. A risk assessment tool is available to determine
the likelihood of identifying a germline PTEN pathogenic variant in a proband.
Suggestive Findings
PHTS should be suspected in a proband with the following clinical and imaging features and family history.
Clinical features in children
• Macrocephaly
• Autism spectrum disorder
• Developmental delay / intellectual disability
• Skin and mucosal lesions: lipomas, trichilemmomas (see Figure 1), oral mucosal papillomatosis,
papillomatous papules (see Figure 2), glycogenic acanthosis, acral (palmoplantar) keratoses, fibromas,
penile freckling
• Hamartomatous intestinal polyps (Other histologic types observed include ganglioneuromas, juvenile
polyps, hyperplastic polyps, and adenomatous polyps.)
• Thyroid lesions, disease, and/or cancer: multinodular goiter, adenomatous nodules, follicular adenomas,
Hashimoto thyroiditis, thyroid cancer (epithelial [non-medullary], especially follicular)
• Vascular malformations: arteriovenous malformations, hemangiomas
• Germ cell tumors (ovarian or testicular)
Additional clinical and imaging features in adults
• Cancers: breast cancer, thyroid cancer (epithelial [non-medullary], especially follicular), renal cell
carcinoma, endometrial carcinoma, colorectal carcinoma, and melanoma
• Fibrocystic disease of the breast
• Uterine fibroids
• Genitourinary malformations (e.g., bicornuate uterus, duplicated ureters)
• Adult-onset Lhermitte-Duclos disease, defined as the presence of a cerebellar dysplastic gangliocytoma
with a characteristic "tiger-striped" appearance
Family history is consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance (e.g., affected males and females in multiple
generations). Absence of a known family history does not preclude the diagnosis.
Establishing the Diagnosis
The diagnosis of PHTS is established in a proband with suggestive findings and a heterozygous pathogenic (or
likely pathogenic) variant in PTEN identified by molecular genetic testing (see Table 1).
Note: (1) Per American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) / Association for Molecular
Pathology variant interpretation guidelines, the terms "pathogenic variant" and "likely pathogenic variant" are
synonymous in a clinical setting, meaning that both are considered diagnostic and can be used for clinical
decision making [Richards et al 2015]. Reference to "pathogenic variants" in this GeneReview is understood to
include likely pathogenic variants. (2) Identification of a heterozygous PTEN variant of uncertain significance
does not establish or rule out the diagnosis.

Option 1
Single-gene testing. Sequence analysis of PTEN is performed first to detect missense, nonsense, and splice site
variants and small intragenic deletions/insertions. Note: Depending on the sequencing method used, single-
exon, multiexon, or whole-gene deletions/duplications may not be detected. If no variant is detected by the
sequencing method used, the next step is to perform gene-targeted deletion/duplication analysis to detect exon
and whole-gene deletions or duplications. If a pathogenic variant is not identified with deletion/duplication
analysis, perform sequence analysis of the PTEN promoter region for variants that decrease PTEN gene
expression.
A multigene panel that includes PTEN and other genes of interest (AKT1, PIK3CA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD,
SEC23B, and WWP1; see Differential Diagnosis) may also be considered to identify the genetic cause of the
condition while limiting identification of pathogenic variants and variants of uncertain significance in genes that
do not explain the underlying phenotype. Note: (1) The genes included in the panel and the diagnostic
sensitivity of the testing used for each gene vary by laboratory and are likely to change over time. (2) Some
multigene panels may include genes not associated with the condition discussed in this GeneReview. (3) In some
laboratories, panel options may include a custom laboratory-designed panel and/or custom phenotype-focused
exome analysis that includes genes specified by the clinician. (4) Methods used in a panel may include sequence
analysis, deletion/duplication analysis, and/or other non-sequencing-based tests.

Clinical Characteristics
Clinical Description
Individuals with PTEN hamartoma tumor syndrome (PHTS) diagnosed in childhood are more likely to present
with macrocephaly, developmental delay, and neurobehavioral manifestations. Adults most often present with
benign and malignant tumors of the breast, thyroid, and endometrium and/or multiple gastrointestinal (GI)
polyps. Additional common features include skin or mucosal lesions. Some individuals have seizures, vascular
anomalies, soft tissue overgrowth, and/or other cancers (renal cell carcinoma, colorectal carcinoma, and
melanoma). PHTS includes the following historical clinical phenotypes: Cowden syndrome, Bannayan-Riley-
Ruvalcaba syndrome, Proteus-like syndrome, and apparently isolated adult-onset Lhermitte-Duclos disease
[Y ehia et al 2020] (see Nomenclature).

Childhood Presentation of PHTS
Children often present with macrocephaly, developmental delay, and neurobehavioral manifestations including
autism spectrum disorder (ASD), though these features are not universally present [Butler et al 2005, Hansen-
Kiss et al 2017, Y ehia et al 2020, Dhawan et al 2024, MacFarland et al 2024].
Macrocephaly (due to megalencephaly) is nearly universal and often severe. Head circumference is often more
than five standard deviations above the mean. Macrocephaly in those with PTEN-related ASD is more
pronounced compared to macrocephaly associated with idiopathic ASD.
Developmental delay (DD) and neurobehavioral/psychiatric disorders. Approximately half of children
diagnosed with PHTS have a neurodevelopmental disorder that includes ASD and/or DD. Children with PHTS-
related ASD have a distinctive neurocognitive and behavioral phenotype including delayed language
development, poor working memory and processing speed, and adaptive and sensory abnormalities [Frazier et
al 2015, Busch et al 2019]. Individuals with PHTS exhibit a wide range of neurocognitive outcomes, from high

functioning to significant impairment. In a large study, neurodevelopmental disorders, including ASD, DD,
and/or intellectual disability, were identified in 35% of children and 23% of adults with PHTS [Dhawan et al
2025b]. The neurocognitive phenotype is variable, with overall severity ranging from mild to severe; however,
among individuals with ASD, clinical manifestations are most often in the mild-to-moderate range, although
broader interindividual variability is well recognized. Psychiatric disorders, including anxiety, depression,
bipolar disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and psychosis, have
been documented in PHTS, affecting approximately 11%-44% of individuals [Hansen-Kiss et al 2017, Balci et al
2018, Dhawan et al 2025b].
Seizures. Individuals with PHTS are at increased risk for developing epilepsy. Seizures may arise in association
with structural brain abnormalities, including focal cortical dysplasia, polymicrogyria, and other intracranial
lesions such as cavernomas. Recent studies have estimated the prevalence of epilepsy in PHTS to range from 6%
to 17%, substantially higher than the approximately 1% prevalence observed in the general population [Liu et al
2024, Dhawan et al 2025b, Jonker & Schieving 2026]. Individuals with PHTS and ASD have a higher risk of
having epilepsy. Epilepsy in individuals with PHTS may be either generalized or focal [Dhawan et al 2024].
Dermatologic features. Pigmentary features including café au lait macules and penile freckling (in males) can
be seen in childhood. Children can also have one or more lipomas at presentation. Linear nevi may be present in
those with overgrowth.
Early-onset thyroid disease and thyroid cancer. Thyroid nodules are common. Hashimoto thyroiditis can
occur. Early-onset thyroid cancer as young as age four years has been reported. Thyroid cancer is more often
follicular than papillary.
GI manifestations. A pediatric series reported the prevalence of GI polyps is approximately 25%-30% of
individuals with PHTS [Liu et al 2024]. The age at identification in childhood is variable and often driven by
symptoms (e.g., bleeding, anemia, abdominal pain), with identification of polyps typically occurring in mid-
childhood through adolescence. However, the overall range of onset spans from infancy to adolescence.
Although uncommon, very early onset in infancy has been reported. These individuals usually have juvenile
polyposis of infancy (JPI), associated with 10q23 microdeletions that encompass PTEN and BMPR1A. ASD-
associated GI symptoms (e.g., abdominal pain, constipation, diarrhea) may be exacerbated by the presence of
polyps. GI hamartomatous polyps may occasionally be associated with intussusception, but rectal bleeding and
oozing of "serum" is more common.
Vascular anomalies and soft tissue overgrowth [Tan et al 2007, Kurek et al 2012, Pilarski 2019] include
hemangiomas and arteriovenous malformations (AVM), which may be extracranial (e.g., intramuscular) or
intracranial (e.g., developmental venous anomalies). AVM can be chronic and significantly impact quality of life.
Cavernous hemangiomas are reported in some individuals.
PTEN hamartoma of the soft tissue (PHOST) is a rare benign overgrowth involving vessels, fat, connective
tissue, and nerves, leading to localized deformities that resemble Proteus syndrome.
Other malignancies. Germ cell tumors of the testes (e.g., seminoma, testicular mixed germ-cell tumor) and
ovaries (e.g., dysgerminoma, immature teratoma) have been reported rarely in children with PHTS [Y ehia et al
2025]. Case studies and case series have also identified hematologic malignancies (e.g., lymphoma),
angiosarcoma, and neuroblastoma rarely in children with PHTS [Y ehia et al 2025].
Adult Presentations of PHTS
Adults with PHTS often come to medical attention after being diagnosed with cancer or multiple polyps on
endoscopy. Other clinical characteristics of PHTS that are often recognized in adults include dermatologic
manifestations, vascular anomalies, soft tissue overgrowth, autoimmunity, lymphoid hyperplasia, metabolic

features, neurocognitive manifestations, and neuroimaging findings (including Lhermitte-Duclos disease
[LDD]). In some individuals, LDD is the only apparent clinical manifestation.
PHTS is associated with increased lifetime risk of breast, thyroid, endometrial, kidney, colon, and prostate
cancer, soft tissue sarcomas, and melanoma [Tan et al 2012, Bubien et al 2013, Nieuwenhuis et al 2014,
Hendricks et al 2023, Y ehia et al 2023, Y ehia et al 2025]. PHTS is associated with increased risk of bilateral and
multifocal cancers and second primary malignant neoplasms [Ngeow et al 2014].
Fibrocystic breast disease / breast cancer. Women with PHTS have up to 67% risk of benign breast disease. A
prospective study of probands and family members with a germline PTEN pathogenic variant revealed an
85%-91% lifetime risk for female breast cancer, with 50% penetrance by age 50 years [Tan et al 2012, Y ehia et al
2023]. The risk for multifocal/bilateral cancer is increased. Although breast cancer has been described in males
with a PTEN pathogenic variant [Fackenthal et al 2001], it was not observed in a study of more than 3,000
probands [Tan et al 2011].
GI polyps and colorectal cancer. GI polyps are highly prevalent in individuals with PHTS, identified in more
than 90% of those who have undergone upper and/or lower endoscopic evaluation [McGarrity et al 2003, Heald
et al 2010, Stanich et al 2014, Khare et al 2022]. GI polyps are histologically diverse and may include
hamartomatous, ganglioneuromatous, juvenile, hyperplastic, and adenomatous types. Upper GI polyps are also
observed in some individuals. PHTS-related hamartomatous polyps are different in histomorphology from the
polyps seen in Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, with the latter characterized by a distinctive arborizing network of
smooth muscle bundles extending from the muscularis mucosae and partitioning mucosal epithelium into
lobules.
Approximately 20% demonstrate glycogenic acanthosis, characterized by plaques within the esophageal mucosa
[Bender et al 1973, Nishizawa et al 2009].
The lifetime risk of colorectal cancer in individuals with PHTS is estimated to be as high as 17%, with risk
increasing beginning in the late 30s [Y ehia et al 2023]. Colorectal cancer in this population often arises in the
setting of preexisting or concurrent colonic polyps.
Thyroid nodules / thyroid cancer. Benign multinodular goiter of the thyroid as well as adenomatous nodules
and follicular adenomas are common in adults [Harach et al 1999]. The lifetime risk for epithelial thyroid cancer
is 33%-35% [Tan et al 2012, Y ehia et al 2023]. Median age of onset is 37 years; age seven years is the youngest age
at diagnosis reported to date [Ngeow et al 2011]. Follicular histology is overrepresented in adults compared to
the general population, in which papillary histology is overrepresented. No medullary thyroid carcinoma was
observed in a cohort of individuals with PHTS.
Endometrial disease. Benign uterine fibroids are common. Lifetime risk for endometrial cancer is 28%-48%,
with the starting age at risk in the late 30s to early 40s [Tan et al 2012, Y ehia et al 2023].
Renal cell carcinoma (RCC). Lifetime risk for RCC is 30%-35%, with the starting age at risk in the 20s [Tan et al
2012, Kim et al 2020, Y ehia et al 2023]. The predominant histology is papillary renal cell carcinoma [Mester et al
2012], although other subtypes such as chromophobe and clear cell have been reported.
Prostate cancer. Compared with standard population risks, a recent study observed elevated risk of prostate
adenocarcinoma (standardized incidence ratio [SIR]: 4.1; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.9-7.8). The median age
at diagnosis is 59 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 56-61) for prostate cancer versus 67 years in Surveillance,
Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program of the National Cancer Institute (United States). The youngest
age at diagnosis is 45 years [Y ehia et al 2025].
Dermatologic and mucosal manifestations. Common benign mucocutaneous lesions in adults with PHTS
include primarily facial trichilemmomas, oral mucosal papillomatosis, papillomatous papules, acral/

palmoplantar keratoses, and lipomas. Glycogenic acanthosis (multiple small, white, esophageal papules due to
hyperplastic squamous epithelium with increased glycogen) is a characteristic mucosal feature of Cowden
syndrome.
Testicular lipomatosis may occur in males [Lindsay et al 2003, Woodhouse et al 2005, Woodhouse & Ferguson
2006].
Lifetime risk for cutaneous melanoma is estimated at up to 6%. The earliest age at onset is age three years [Tan et
al 2012].
Vascular anomalies and soft tissue overgrowth. Vascular anomalies (hemangiomas, AVM) and PHOST can
significantly impact quality of life due to chronic pain, progressive and infiltrative overgrowth, and functional
limitations, particularly when lesions involve major muscle groups or the extremities. The clinical course is often
marked by lesion growth, recurrence after intervention, and the need for repeated procedures or long‑term
medical therapy. Together, these factors contribute to substantial physical morbidity, treatment burden, and
psychosocial impact [Tan et al 2007, Kurek et al 2012, Pilarski 2019]. Cavernous hemangiomas are reported in
some individuals.
Soft tissue sarcomas. Compared with standard population risks, a recent study observed elevated risk of soft
tissue sarcomas (SIR: 10.7; 95% CI: 3.9-23.7) individuals with PHTS. The median age at diagnosis is 46 years
(IQR: 28-47) compared to 62 years in the SEER Program. Diagnoses include malignant giant cell tumor of
tendon sheath, chondrosarcoma, cutaneous leiomyosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, and liposarcoma [Y ehia et al
2025].
Autoimmunity and lymphoid hyperplasia are observed in up to approximately 40% of individuals [Ngeow et al
2011, Heindl et al 2012, Henderson et al 2014, Mauro et al 2017, Liu et al 2024]. Reported autoimmune disorders
include Hashimoto thyroiditis, eosinophilic esophagitis, colitis, and vasculitis.
Metabolic features of PHTS in adults include increased insulin sensitivity despite higher rates of obesity; lower
fasting insulin levels and higher glucose utilization; and increased body mass index (BMI) driven by greater
adiposity without major changes in fat distribution. Adults have a paradoxical increased cancer and obesity risk
but reduced risk of diabetes mellitus type 2 [Pal et al 2012].
Neurocognitive manifestations. Adults with PHTS have a wide range of cognitive abilities, with common
deficits in motor function, executive function, and memory even without any history of developmental delay in
childhood [Balci et al 2018, Dhamija et al 2018, Ciaccio et al 2019].
Neuroimaging findings. Brain imaging may reveal white matter abnormalities, meningiomas, vascular
malformations, prominent perivascular spaces, cortical dysplasia, and gray matter heterotopias. Lhermitte-
Duclos disease (LDD), characterized by cerebellar hamartomatous overgrowth, is a rare but pathognomonic
finding of PHTS. Imaging findings of LDD often shows unilateral cerebellar enlargement with a "tiger-striped"
pattern.
Genitourinary malformations are an infrequently reported and incompletely characterized feature of PHTS.
Reported findings include bicornuate uterus (females), testicular lipomatosis (males), and duplicated ureters.
The true incidence of structural genitourinary malformations in PHTS is unclear, as these features have been
reported only sporadically and are not consistently observed in cohort studies.
Genotype-Phenotype Correlations
Large studies of individuals with PHTS have found that germline PTEN frameshift variants are overrepresented,
but not absolute, in those with thyroid cancer, nonsense pathogenic variants in those with colorectal cancer,
promoter variants those with breast cancer, and missense variants in individuals with autism spectrum disorder

(ASD) [Ngeow et al 2011, Tan et al 2012, Frazier et al 2015]. Multiple studies have demonstrated individuals with
PTEN-related ASD had less severe pathogenic variants than those identified in individuals with PHTS without a
diagnosis of ASD [Spinelli et al 2015, Leslie & Longy 2016, Mighell et al 2018].
Penetrance
Penetrance for PTEN pathogenic variants is age related; no non-penetrant individuals are known to be reported.
Nomenclature
The historical clinically defined phenotypes of Cowden syndrome, Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndrome
(BRRS), Proteus-like syndrome, and apparently isolated adult-onset Lhermitte-Duclos disease (LDD) were
initially thought to be disparate disorders. Cowden syndrome was originally described as an adult-onset
genodermatosis that imparted increased risk for certain cancers. BRRS was considered a pediatric-onset
disorder characterized by macrocephaly in combination with intestinal hamartomatous polyposis, vascular
malformations, lipomas, hemangiomas, and genital freckling. Following the identification of germline PTEN
pathogenic variants in individuals with these diagnoses, it became apparent that the four phenotypes are part of
the same disease spectrum. To facilitate diagnosis and subsequent medical management, the term PTEN
hamartoma tumor syndrome (PHTS) was coined to include all individuals with a germline PTEN pathogenic
variant regardless of clinical manifestations. All phenotypes associated with a PTEN pathogenic variant should
be termed PHTS, with all of its implications for management [Y ehia et al 2020].
Cowden syndrome, Cowden disease, and multiple hamartoma syndrome have been used interchangeably.
Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndrome, Bannayan-Ruvalcaba-Riley syndrome, Bannayan-Zonana syndrome, and
Myhre-Riley-Smith syndrome refer to a similar constellation of signs that comprise what has been referred to as
BRRS.
A form of PTEN-related Proteus-like syndrome with a clinical presentation similar to that first described by
Zhou et al [2000] was termed SOLAMEN (segmental overgrowth, lipomatosis, arteriovenous malformation, and
epidermal nevus) syndrome [Caux et al 2007].
Prevalence
A recent study evaluated the prevalence of germline pathogenic or likely pathogenic PTEN variants in two large
research cohorts [White et al 2025]. In the US-based All of Us Research Program, which included 245,000
studied participants, the estimated prevalence of PHTS was approximately one in 9,000. Similarly, the UK
Biobank cohort, comprising 470,000 studied individuals, yielded a prevalence estimate of one in 13,000.
Genetically Related (Allelic) Disorders
No phenotypes other than those discussed in this GeneReview are known to be associated with germline
pathogenic variants in PTEN.
Contiguous gene deletions involving PTEN and BMPR1A have been reported in individuals with juvenile
polyposis of infancy (JPI) (OMIM 612242). JPI is characterized by polyps that develop within the first few years
of life, hypoproteinemia, protein-losing enteropathy, diarrhea, anemia, anasarca, and poor growth. The
phenotype may mimic Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndrome.
Sporadic tumors (including endometrial cancer, prostate cancer, and glioblastoma) occurring as single tumors
in the absence of any other findings of PHTS frequently contain a somatic variant in PTEN that is not present in
the germline. PTEN is one of the most frequently somatically mutated or deleted genes in cancer. In these
circumstances predisposition to these tumors is not heritable.

Developmental Delay / Intellectual Disability Management Issues
The following information represents typical management recommendations for individuals with developmental
delay / intellectual disability in the United States; standard recommendations may vary from country to country.
Ages 0-3 years. Referral to an early intervention program is recommended for access to occupational, physical,
speech, and feeding therapy as well as infant mental health services, special educators, and sensory impairment
specialists. In the US, early intervention is a federally funded program available in all states that provides in-
home services to target individual therapy needs.
Ages 3-5 years. In the US, developmental preschool through the local public school district is recommended.
Before placement, an evaluation is made to determine needed services and therapies and an individualized
education plan (IEP) is developed for those who qualify based on established motor, language, social, or
cognitive delay. The early intervention program typically assists with this transition. Developmental preschool is
center based; for children too medically unstable to attend, home-based services are provided.
All ages. Consultation with a developmental pediatrician is recommended to ensure the involvement of
appropriate community, state, and educational agencies (US) and to support parents in maximizing quality of
life. Some issues to consider:
• IEP services:
⚬ An IEP provides specially designed instruction and related services to children who qualify.
⚬ IEP services will be reviewed annually to determine whether any changes are needed.
⚬ Special education law requires that children participating in an IEP be in the least restrictive
environment feasible at school and included in general education as much as possible, when and
where appropriate.
⚬ PT, OT, and speech services will be provided in the IEP to the extent that the need affects the child's
access to academic material. Beyond that, private supportive therapies based on the affected
individual's needs may be considered. Specific recommendations regarding type of therapy can be
made by a developmental pediatrician.
⚬ As a child enters the teen years, a transition plan should be discussed and incorporated in the IEP .
For those receiving IEP services, the public school district is required to provide services until age
21.
• A 504 plan (Section 504: a US federal statute that prohibits discrimination based on disability) can be
considered for those who require accommodations or modifications such as front-of-class seating,

assistive technology devices, classroom scribes, extra time between classes, modified assignments, and
enlarged text.
• Developmental Disabilities Administration (DDA) enrollment is recommended. DDA is a US public
agency that provides services and support to qualified individuals. Eligibility differs by state but is typically
determined by diagnosis and/or associated cognitive/adaptive disabilities.
• Families with limited income and resources may also qualify for supplemental security income (SSI) for

Motor Dysfunction
Gross motor dysfunction
• Physical therapy is recommended to maximize mobility and to reduce the risk for later-onset orthopedic
complications (e.g., contractures, scoliosis, hip dislocation).
• Consider use of durable medical equipment and positioning devices as needed (e.g., wheelchairs, walkers,
bath chairs, orthotics, adaptive strollers).
• For muscle tone abnormalities including hypertonia or dystonia, consider involving appropriate specialists
to aid in management of baclofen, tizanidine, botulinum toxin, anti-parkinsonian medications, or
orthopedic procedures.
Fine motor dysfunction. Occupational therapy is recommended for difficulty with fine motor skills that affect
adaptive function such as feeding, grooming, dressing, and writing.
Speech, language, and communication issues. Speech-language evaluation should be considered early in
development for children who have delayed communication milestones or who are not yet talking. Evaluation
for alternative means of communication (e.g., augmentative and alternative communication [AAC]) is
appropriate for individuals who have speech or receptive and expressive language difficulties. An AAC
evaluation should be completed by a speech-language pathologist who has expertise in the area. This evaluation
typically takes into account cognitive abilities, sensory impairments, and motor skills to determine the most
appropriate form of communication. AAC devices can range from low-tech, such as picture exchange
communication, to high-tech, such as voice-generating devices. Contrary to popular belief, AAC devices do not
hinder verbal development of speech, but rather support optimal speech and language development. Many
children will continue to require AAC into later childhood and adulthood, while some may use their AAC for a
shorter time to help aid speech and language development.
Neurobehavioral/Psychiatric Concerns
Children may qualify for and benefit from interventions used in treatment of autism spectrum disorder,
including applied behavior analysis (ABA). ABA therapy is targeted to the individual child's behavioral, social,
and adaptive strengths and weaknesses and typically performed one on one with a board-certified behavior
analyst.
Consultation with a developmental pediatrician may be helpful in guiding parents through appropriate behavior
management strategies or providing prescription medications, such as medication used to treat attention-deficit/
hyperactivity disorder, when necessary.
Concerns about serious aggressive or destructive behavior can be addressed by a pediatric psychiatrist.

Evaluation of Relatives at Risk
Molecular genetic testing for the PTEN pathogenic variant identified in the proband is recommended for at-risk
relatives of an affected individual. Family members who have the familial PTEN pathogenic variant (and
therefore have PHTS) require an initial evaluation and ongoing surveillance.
Although there is no consensus on the optimal timing of genetic testing of at-risk family members, molecular
genetic testing for the familial PTEN pathogenic variant in early childhood is often appropriate because timely
detection of early-onset manifestations such as thyroid disease and cancer can guide management. (The earliest
age of documented PTEN-related breast cancer and thyroid cancer are age 17 years and age four years,
respectively.) Neurodevelopmental features such as developmental delay and autism spectrum disorder may also
benefit from early intervention.
Family members who have not inherited the familial PTEN pathogenic variant and their subsequent offspring
have cancer risks similar to the general population.
See Genetic Counseling for issues related to testing of at-risk relatives for genetic counseling purposes.
Therapies Under Investigation
Although mTOR inhibitors show promise for treatment of malignancies in individuals who have a germline
PTEN pathogenic variant, use should be limited to clinical trials. A Phase II open-label clinical trial
(NCT00971789) utilized the mTOR inhibitor sirolimus in adults with PHTS and showed evidence of
improvement of clinical manifestations throughout the duration of the trial [Komiya et al 2019]. A natural
history study is completing accrual of pediatric, adolescent, and young adults with germline PTEN pathogenic
variants and autism spectrum disorder (NCT02461446). Through this study, a double-blind drug-placebo
crossover clinical trial investigated the mTOR inhibitor everolimus for its safety and potential neurocognitive
benefits in individuals with PHTS (NCT02991807) [Srivastava et al 2022]. The preliminary analysis showed that
everolimus is well tolerated, but no significant improvement occurred on most neurocognitive and behavioral
measures. However, several secondary efficacy endpoints moved in the direction of improvement, including

Genetic Counseling
Genetic counseling is the process of providing individuals and families with information on the nature, mode(s) of
inheritance, and implications of genetic disorders to help them make informed medical and personal decisions. The
following section deals with genetic risk assessment and the use of family history and genetic testing to clarify genetic
status for family members; it is not meant to address all personal, cultural, or ethical issues that may arise or to
substitute for consultation with a genetics professional. —ED.
Mode of Inheritance
PTEN hamartoma tumor syndrome (PHTS) – which encompasses the historical clinical phenotypes of Cowden
syndrome, Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndrome, Proteus-like syndrome, and apparently isolated adult-onset
Lhermitte-Duclos disease – is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner.
Risk to Family Members
Parents of a proband
• Some individuals diagnosed with PHTS have an affected parent. Based on practical experience, about 50%
of individuals diagnosed with PHTS have an affected relative [C Eng, unpublished data].
• Approximately 10%-44% of individuals diagnosed with PHTS have the disorder as the result of a de novo
pathogenic variant [Y ehia et al 2020]. Virtually all individuals with PTEN-related Proteus-like syndrome
have a de novo pathogenic variant.
• If the proband appears to be the only affected family member (i.e., a simplex case), molecular genetic
testing is recommended for the parents of the proband to evaluate their genetic status, inform recurrence
risk assessment, and determine their need for evaluation and surveillance.
⚬ Note: A proband may appear to be the only affected family member because of failure to recognize
the disorder in family members, early death of the parent before the onset of symptoms, or late
onset of the disorder in a parent. Because external manifestations of PHTS may be subtle [Y ehia et
al 2020] or variably expressed (even among relatives), diagnoses within families are sometimes only
recognized after identification of a more severely affected individual. Therefore, de novo occurrence
of a PTEN pathogenic variant cannot be confirmed unless molecular genetic testing has
demonstrated that neither parent is heterozygous for the PTEN pathogenic variant identified in the
proband.
• If the pathogenic variant identified in the proband is not identified in either parent and parental identity
testing has confirmed biological maternity and paternity, the following possibilities should be considered:
⚬ The proband has a de novo pathogenic variant.
⚬ The proband inherited a pathogenic variant from a parent with gonadal (or somatic and gonadal)
mosaicism.* Note: Testing of parental leukocyte DNA may not detect all instances of somatic
mosaicism and will not detect a pathogenic variant that is present in the germ (gonadal) cells only.

* A parent with somatic and gonadal mosaicism for a PTEN pathogenic variant may be mildly/

Sibs of a proband. The risk to the sibs of the proband depends on the genetic status of the proband's parents:
• If a parent of the proband has the PTEN pathogenic variant identified in the proband, the risk to the sibs
of inheriting the pathogenic variant is 50%. The penetrance of PHTS is close to 99% by adulthood in
individuals with a PTEN pathogenic variant.
• If the PTEN pathogenic variant identified in the proband cannot be detected in the leukocyte DNA of
either parent, the recurrence risk to sibs is estimated to be 1% because of the possibility of parental
gonadal mosaicism [Zemet et al 2022].
• If the parents have not been tested for the PTEN pathogenic variant but are clinically unaffected and are in
their 30s, the risk to the sibs of a proband appears to be low. However, sibs of a proband with clinically
unaffected parents are still presumed to be at increased risk for PHTS because of the possibility of age-
related penetrance in a heterozygous parent or parental gonadal mosaicism.
Offspring of a proband. Each child of an individual with PHTS has a 50% chance of inheriting the PTEN
pathogenic variant.
Other family members. The risk to other family members depends on the status of the proband's parents: if a
parent has the PTEN pathogenic variant, the parent's family members may be at risk.

Molecular Pathogenesis
PTEN encodes phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate 3-phosphatase and dual-specificity protein phosphatase
PTEN, which belongs to a subclass of phosphatases called dual-specificity phosphatases that remove phosphate
groups from tyrosine as well as serine and threonine. PTEN localizes to specific nuclear and cytoplasmic
components. The wild type protein is a major lipid phosphatase that downregulates the PI3K/AKT pathway to
cause G1 cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. In addition, phosphatase appears to play an important role in inhibition
of cell migration and spreading, as well as downregulating several cell cyclins. It appears that nuclear PTEN
mediates cell cycle arrest, while cytoplasmic PTEN is required for apoptosis [Y ehia & Eng 2018, Y ehia et al 2019].
Germline pathogenic variants have been found throughout PTEN and include missense, nonsense, and splice
site variants, small deletions, insertions, and large deletions. Nearly 40% of pathogenic variants are found in
exon 5, which encodes the phosphate core motif [Y ehia et al 2019]. Most pathogenic variants are unique,
although several recurrent pathogenic variants have been reported (see Table 6).
The majority (76%) of germline pathogenic variants in PTEN predict either truncated PTEN protein, lack of
protein (haploinsufficiency), or dysfunctional protein. Many missense variants are functionally null and several
act as dominant negatives [Papa et al 2014]. When PTEN is absent, decreased, or dysfunctional, phosphorylation
of AKT1 is uninhibited, leading to the inability to activate cell cycle arrest and/or to undergo apoptosis. In
addition, through lack of protein phosphatase activity, the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway is
dysregulated, leading to abnormal cell survival [Y ehia et al 2019].

PTEN-specific laboratory technical considerations. PTEN has a highly homologous pseudogene (PTENP1) on
chromosome 9 [Dahia et al 1998]. Special consideration should be taken when designing PTEN-specific primers
to ensure specificity and to avoid cross-amplification of this pseudogene [Ngeow & Eng 2016].
