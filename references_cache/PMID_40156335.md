# Novel In-Frame FGF14 Deletion Causes Spinocerebellar Ataxia Type 27A: Clinical Response to Deep Brain Stimulation and 4-Aminopyridine.
**Authors:** Keller Sarmiento IJ, Bovenzi R, Kinsinger M, Kinsley L, Bustos BI, Krainc D, Mencacci NE
**Journal:** Mov Disord (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mds.30183](https://doi.org/10.1002/mds.30183)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Spinocerebellar ataxia 27A (SCA27A) is a rare neurodegenerative disorder characterized by childhood-onset tremor and progressive cerebellar dysfunction. SCA27A is usually caused by loss-of-function FGF14 variants.

OBJECTIVES: We report the identification of a novel FGF14 variant in a five-generation family with autosomal dominant ataxia and describe the clinical phenotype and response to subthalamic nucleus deep brain stimulation (STN-DBS) and 4-aminopyridine (4-AP).

METHODS: Whole genome sequencing was performed on the proband, two affected sisters (Patients 2 and 3), and one unaffected sister (III5). Sanger sequencing was performed to confirm the variant and sequence additional family members.

RESULTS: A novel heterozygous in-frame deletion (p.Val119del) in FGF14 was identified in this family affected by childhood-onset tremor followed by late-onset progressive ataxia. Two patients showed significant tremor reduction following STN-DBS and balance improvement with 4-AP.

CONCLUSIONS: We identified a novel likely pathogenic FGF14 variant segregating in a family with SCA27A. Additionally, we suggest STN-DBS and 4-AP as promising treatment options for this condition. © 2025 The Author(s). Spinocerebellar ataxias (SCAs) are a heterogeneous group of rare neurodegenerative genetic disorders characterized by progressive cerebellar dysfunction.
1
 While most SCAs arise from pathological repeat expansions, others are linked to conventional coding variants.

The FGF14 gene (OMIM #601515) encodes fibroblast growth factor 14, a protein highly expressed in the cerebellum where it regulates the activity of voltage‐gated sodium channels and maintains proper firing patterns in Purkinje cells.
2



Conventional coding heterozygous variants in FGF14, mostly loss‐of‐function but also missense variants, cause an extremely rare long‐recognized form of autosomal dominant SCA, formerly known as SCA27 (OMIM #193003).
3
 More recently, a GAA intronic expansion variant in FGF14 was recognized as one of the most common causes of adult‐onset autosomal dominant cerebellar ataxia worldwide. This led to the assignment of two different names to the allelic disorders: SCA27A for ataxia caused by the rare coding variants and SCA27B (OMIM #620174) for ataxia caused by the more common intronic repeat expansion.
4
,
5



Importantly, it has been shown that patients with SCA27B may respond remarkably to the administration of 4‐aminopyridine (4‐AP, dalfampridine), suggesting that it could be a form of treatable ataxia.
6
 However, the effect of this medication in patients with SCA27A has not been studied.

Herein, we present a family affected by childhood‐onset tremor and adult‐onset, slowly progressive cerebellar ataxia spanning at least five generations, which harbored a segregating novel heterozygous in‐frame deletion in FGF14, consistent with a diagnosis of SCA27A. Furthermore, we describe a positive response to subthalamic nucleus deep brain stimulation (STN‐DBS) surgery and a promising response to the medication 4‐AP, highlighting potential therapeutic options for this rare condition.

This study involved an American family of European ancestry evaluated at the Movement Disorders Genetics Clinic of Northwestern Medicine, Chicago, IL, USA. Figure 1A shows the family pedigree.

STN‐DBS had been placed in two subjects from the family to treat tremor, prior to their evaluation in our clinic. The family had previously undergone extensive repeat expansion analysis including SCA 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 12 14, 17, ATN1.

A clinical ataxia next generation sequencing (NGS)‐based comprehensive panel including sequencing and copy number variation (CNV) analysis was performed by Centogene (https://www.centogene.com/diagnostics/ngs-panels) in the proband (Patient 1).

Subsequently, research whole genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on the three affected sisters (Patients 1, 2, and 3) and one unaffected sister (subject III5 in the pedigree, Fig. 1A). Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions and deletions were filtered and prioritized as reported elsewhere.
7
 In addition, we looked for repeat expansions in all known SCA‐associated genes using the bioinformatic tool ExpansionHunter.
8
,
9



Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)‐certified genetic testing through Sanger sequencing was conducted in Patients 1, 2, 3, and Patient 3's affected daughter (IV2).

Written informed consents were obtained from all the subjects involved in this study.

Research WGS data are available upon request.

The proband (Patient 1, subject III2, Fig. 1A) is a 65‐year‐old right‐handed female presenting with upper limb action tremor at age 12 years followed by progressive imbalance since her mid‐40s (Table 1). At age 50 years, she underwent bilateral STN‐DBS placement, leading to a marked improvement in tremor severity. After undergoing routine implanted pulse generator (IPG) replacement, she developed an infection of her IPG pocket, extensions, and leads that were therefore removed and never replaced. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed minimal small vessel ischemic changes, the sequelae of DBS surgery, and mild vermian atrophy (Fig. 1B).

The patient's neurological examination showed left‐predominant moderate tremor of the upper limbs and head (Video 1). She reported blurry vision. Saccadic pursuit with slow saccades was observed, but no nystagmus. Moderate dysmetria was seen on finger‐to‐nose and heel‐knee‐shin maneuvers, more marked on the left side. Her gait was wide‐based and ataxic. She was recently started on 4‐AP 10 mg two tablets daily with marked improvement of her gait (Video 1) and vision. Her Scale for the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia (SARA) score decreased from 18 to 13.5 since starting the treatment (Table 1).

Patient 1's examination before and after 4‐aminopyridine (4‐AP). Note the head tremor, coarse kinetic tremor with dysmetria, and gait ataxia. Note the improvement after treatment with 4‐AP, especially as regards the gait.

Patient 2 (III3) is a 58‐year‐old right‐handed female, younger sister of Patient 1. At age 3 years a hand tremor was noticed. Her gait became unstable at around the age of 33 years. At age 42 years she underwent bilateral STN‐DBS, resulting in a dramatic improvement in her tremor. Over time, she developed right‐hand dystonia, which was treated with botulinum toxin injections.

Her neurological examination revealed mild bilateral action tremor of the upper extremities, and dystonic posturing of the right upper limb with wrist hyperextension and finger flexion (Video 2). She had saccadic pursuit and gazed‐evoked downbeat nystagmus on horizontal and vertical gaze. Symmetric dysdiadochokinesia and dysmetria were observed in the upper extremities. Her gait was severely ataxic, requiring bilateral support.

Patient 2's examination. Observe the left hand rest tremor, bilateral hand dystonic posturing, upper limb action tremor with dysmetria, and gait ataxia. Deep brain stimulation (DBS) was on.

Patient 3 (III1) is a 67‐year‐old right‐handed female, older sister of Patients 1 and 2. She developed a hand tremor at age 12 years. In her 30s she reported impaired balance and slurred speech. Later in life, her clinical picture became more complex, including fatigue, episodic dizziness, blurry vision, head tremor, lightheadedness, attention and memory issues, and dysphagia. She reported a significant improvement in her gait, vision, and fatigue after starting 4‐AP 10 mg twice daily.

The patient's neurological examination was notable for horizontal nystagmus on lateral gaze, and upbeat nystagmus on vertical gaze (Video 3). Bilateral kinetic tremor, dysdiadochokinesia, and dystonic posturing were noted in the upper extremities. The heel‐to‐shin test demonstrated marked bilateral deviations, and her gait was wide‐based and ataxic.

The patients' father (subject II1) was affected by ataxia and tremor, and their great uncle had ataxia (I1, Fig. 1A). In addition, several of Patients 1, 2, and 3's children and grandchildren reported ataxia, tremor, and a combination of neurodevelopmental disorders including motor delay, intellectual disability, autism, and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD).

Patient 3's examination. Note the lateral and vertical gaze‐evoked nystagmus, upper extremities action tremor, and severe gait ataxia.

Patient 1 underwent a comprehensive ataxia genetic panel, which identified a heterozygous in‐frame deletion of three base pairs (ENST00000376131: c.356_358del; p.Val119del) in the FGF14 gene. This variant is not reported in the gnomAD database and has a Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) score of 22.5.
10
 According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria, this variant is classified as likely pathogenic (PP1, PM2, PM4, PP3). The Vall119 amino acid is completely conserved in vertebrates (https://www.mutationtaster.org/, Fig. 1C). Its AlphaMissense score indicates pathogenicity (0.923).
11
 This is a metric used to predict the likelihood that a missense variant is pathogenic across all possible amino acid substitutions at that position (Fig. 1D).

Subsequent research WGS identified the p.Val119del variant in all three affected sisters (Patients 1, 2, 3) and not in the unaffected sister (subject III5). No other relevant conventional or repeat expansion variants were observed in any other ataxia‐related genes. The FGF14 p.Val119del variant was additionally detected in Patient 3's daughter (IV2), who was not seen at our clinic, but reported a hand tremor starting at age 7 years, and gait ataxia later in her 20s.

In this study, we present a five‐generation SCA27A family comprising 14 individuals affected by early‐onset tremor and ataxia, who carried a novel segregating in‐frame deletion variant in FGF14.

Since 2003, when a missense variant (F145S) in FGF14 was first identified in a large Dutch family with autosomal dominant ataxia,
3
 a small number of conventional coding FGF14 variants, mostly frameshift and nonsense variants, have been identified. These variants typically cause haploinsufficiency due to reduced FGF14 protein levels. Common clinical features in these cases include a combination of early‐onset tremor, late‐onset cerebellar ataxia, nystagmus, intellectual disability, orofacial dyskinesias, dysarthria, dysphagia, and pyramidal signs.
6



Unlike the more commonly reported loss‐of‐function variants, the p.Val119del variant identified in our pedigree is an in‐frame deletion in exon 3, affecting the FGF domain of the protein. The previously reported pathogenic missense variant p.F145S, located in this domain, compromises the stability of the FGF14 protein, leading to its degradation, and causing disease through haploinsufficiency.
12
 Likewise, we speculate that the p.Val119del variant, which deletes a highly phylogenetically conserved amino acid in the FGF domain, likely affects the protein function with a similar mechanism.

Clinically, all affected individuals in our family presented with childhood‐onset tremor and adulthood onset of slowly progressive gait and limb ataxia, a pattern of symptoms that appears to be a clinical hallmark of the SCA27A condition.
13
 We observed significant phenotypic variability, including the variable presence of motor symptoms such as dystonia and nystagmus, along with neurodevelopmental issues, consistent with previous reports of SCA27A families.
14



Dystonia has been rarely described in SCA27A patients in the context of paroxysmal episodes.
15
,
16
,
17
 In our family, both Patients 2 and 3 showed clear upper limb dystonic posturing, which benefitted from botulinum toxin injections.

Another novel finding from this study is the potential positive clinical response to two different therapeutic options.

Although SCA27A was described over 20 years ago, only one report of a SCA27A patient treated with DBS is currently available.
18
 In this case, targeting the ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM) nucleus of the thalamus markedly improved the patient's tremor. In two of our patients (Patients 1 and 2), targeting the bilateral STN also persistently reduced tremor severity. Limited data exist on the use of STN‐DBS in SCAs.
19
 A major role of fibers from the cerebellar nuclei to the thalamus has been hypothesized to drive tremor. Animal models suggested that STN stimulation can modulate these fibers,
20
 possibly explaining the tremor improvement in our patients.

Finally, this study is the first to report a potential benefit of 4‐AP in SCA27A patients. While, 4‐AP has been shown to reduce ataxic symptoms in SCA27B patients,
21
 its effect has never been tested in individuals with SCA27A. As a potassium channel blocker, 4‐AP is believed to improve symptoms in SCA27B by restoring the rhythmic firing properties of Purkinje cells, with an additional possible neuroprotective effect.
22
 Here, we reported significant improvements in both balance and vision in Patient 1 (Videos 1) and 3, suggesting that 4‐AP may also benefit those individuals with SCA27A.

In conclusion, we have identified a novel SCA27A family carrying the previously unreported likely pathogenic variant p.Val119del in FGF14. Affected family members presented with the typical SCA27A phenotype, including tremor developing in early childhood and gait ataxia only appearing decades later. Additional families carrying the p.Val119del variant in FGF14 are needed to confirm its pathogenicity and provide a more detailed description of its associated clinical phenotype. A previously unreported clinical finding was the presence of arm dystonia in two cases. Additionally, our findings suggest that DBS targeting the STN is a promising treatment for SCA27A, describing the first two cases in the literature. Finally, we highlight for the first time the positive impact of 4‐AP. Of note, the possibility of a placebo effect influencing the response to 4‐AP cannot be excluded. Larger studies are warranted to confirm these preliminary observations.
