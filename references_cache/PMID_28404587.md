# CIC-DUX4 Induces Small Round Cell Sarcomas Distinct from Ewing Sarcoma.
**Authors:** Yoshimoto T, Tanaka M, Homme M, Yamazaki Y, Takazawa Y, Antonescu CR, Nakamura T
**Journal:** Cancer Res (2017)
**DOI:** [10.1158/0008-5472.CAN-16-3351](https://doi.org/10.1158/0008-5472.CAN-16-3351)

## Content

CIC-DUX4 sarcoma (CDS) or CIC-rearranged sarcoma is a subcategory of small round cell sarcoma resembling the morphological phenotypes of Ewing sarcoma (ES). However, recent clinicopathologic and molecular genetic analyses indicate that CDS is an independent disease entity from ES. Few ancillary markers have been used in the differential diagnosis of CDS, and additional CDS-specific biomarkers are needed for more definitive classification. Here, we report the generation of an ex vivo mouse model for CDS by transducing embryonic mesenchymal cells (eMC) with human CIC-DUX4 cDNA. Recipient mice transplanted with eMC-expressing CIC-DUX4 rapidly developed an aggressive, undifferentiated sarcoma composed of small round to short spindle cells. Gene-expression profiles of CDS and eMC revealed upregulation of CIC-DUX4 downstream genes such as PEA3 family genes, Ccnd2, Crh, and Zic1 IHC analyses for both mouse and human tumors showed that CCND2 and MUC5AC are reliable biomarkers to distinguish CDS from ES. Gene silencing of CIC-DUX4 as well as Ccnd2, Ret, and Bcl2 effectively inhibited CDS tumor growth in vitro The CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib and the soft tissue sarcoma drug trabectedin also blocked the growth of mouse CDS. In summary, our mouse model provides important biological information about CDS and provides a useful platform to explore biomarkers and therapeutic agents for CDS. Cancer Res; 77(11); 2927-37. ©2017 AACR.

CIC-DUX4 sarcomas (CDS) belong to a highly aggressive subgroup of small round cell sarcoma, affecting predominantly children and young adults (1). Although EWS-ETS-negative small round cell sarcomas were previously classified as Ewing-like sarcoma or Ewing sarcoma-like round cell sarcoma, increasing evidence suggests the distinct biology of CDS with CIC-DUX4 gene fusions, secondary to either a t(4;19)(q35;q13) or t(10;19)(q26.3;q13) translocation (1, 2). CDS morphologically show small- to medium-sized, round to ovoid cells, packed in solid sheets, lacking any line of differentiation. Thus the differential diagnosis from Ewing sarcoma (ES) is often difficult without detecting the CIC related fusions (1, 3). CDS show a poor outcome and overall survival of CDS patients was worse than that of ES patients (3). It is therefore important to clarify the biological characteristics responsible for the different behaviors between CDS and ES. The differences may be caused by distinct signaling pathways regulated by CIC-DUX4 and EWS-ETS as well as the potential different cell-of-origin of each tumor type.

Immunohistolochemical detection of ETV4, a transcriptional target of CIC-DUX4, is a useful marker for the histologic diagnosis of CDS (2-6). This suggests that further molecular characterization of CDS will expand our understanding on their biological behavior, as well as provide novel biomarkers and molecular targets for therapy. To this end we developed an animal model that recapitulates the phenotypes of human CDS. CIC-DUX4 gene fusion is the initiating and causative event in CDS, encoding a chimeric transcription factor consisting of the large part of CIC including its DNA-binding HMG box, and the transcriptional activation domain derived from the DUX4 C-terminus (2). Deregulation of CIC target genes such as ETV4 is one of the key events in the development and progression of CDS (2), and chimeric transcription factors act as an oncogene only in proper cellular context (7-9). We have previously successfully generated an Ewing sarcoma mouse model utilizing an ex vivo-based technology (7). In this model EWS-FLI1 appropriately upregulates its target genes in the sarcoma cells as well as in the embryonic osteochondrogenic progenitors, considered the likely cell-of-origin of ES, exhibiting morphologic and molecular features of human ES. In addition, an alveolar soft part sarcoma model mouse that recapitulates histologic features and high metastatic potency of human counterpart has been generated (10).

Taking advantage of this ex vivo gene transduction system, we have developed a similar mouse model for human CDS. Mouse embryonic mesenchymal cells transduced with the CIC-DUX4 develop small blue round cell tumors, as seen in human CDS, which showed a highly aggressive growth. Gene microarray analyses of CDS revealed distinct gene expression profiles of CDS from ES, with significant upregulation of the cell cycle progression pathway, such as Ccnd2 gene in CDS. Furthermore, Cyclin D2 and MUC5AC were found as novel biomarkers for diagnosis of CDS, and a growth inhibitory effect of trabectedin was observed. Modeling fusion gene-associated sarcomas thus provides important tools for further our understanding in the pathogenesis of these rare types of neoplasms.

N-terminal HA-tagged CIC-DUX4 was introduced into the pMYs-IRES-GFP vector. Limbs of Balb/c mouse embryo (Clea Japan, Tokyo, Japan) were removed aseptically on 18.5 dpc, and embryonic mesenchymal cells (eMC) were obtained by dissection using two mg/mL collagenase (Wako Pure Chemical, Tokyo, Japan) at 37˚C for 2 h. eMC were cultured in growth medium composed of Iscove's Modified Dulbecco's Medium (Invitrogen) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum and subjected immediately to retroviral infection without further purification. The analysis of cell surface marker expression suggested that eMC might share overlapping characteristics with mesenchymal stem cells (Supplemental Fig. S1). Retroviral stock was added into the medium containing eMC with six μg/mL of Polybrene (Sigma) and then spun at 700 g for 1 h. The spin infection was repeated after 24 hours. Transduced eMC were mixed with growth factor-reduced Matrigel (Becton Dickinson) and 1 × 106 cells were transplanted into the subcutaneous regions of Balb/c nude mice was performed as described previously (7). Transduction efficiency of the CIC-DUX4 retrovirus was confirmed by flow cytometry using FacsCalibur (Beckton Dickinson) (Supplemental Fig. S2). All experiments described in this study were performed in strict accordance with standard ethical guidelines and approved by the animal care committee at the Japanese Foundation for Cancer Research under licenses 10-05-9 and 0604-3-13.

Paraffin blocks from ten cases of both CDS and ES specimens were obtained from the Memorial Sloan-Kettering Cancer Center. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Memorial Sloan-Kettering Cancer Center (Protocol 02-060). Synovial sarcoma (hSS), rhabdomyosarcoma (hRS) and extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcoma (hEMCS) samples were obtained from the Japanese Foundation for Cancer Research.

For light microscopic analysis, formaldehyde-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor tissues were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using standard techniques. Immunohistochemical staining was performed using the Simple Stain MAX-PO kit (Nichirei Bioscience), the Histofine SAB-PO (R) kit (Nichirei Bioscience) or the BenchMark GX automated slide preparation system (Ventana). The following primary antibodies were used: anti-MUC5AC (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-ETV4 (Abcam), anti-CCND2 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology).

The CIC-DUX4 protein was immunoprecipitated using total cell lysates of mouse CDS cells using the anti-DUX4 antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). The samples were then subjected to SDS-PAGE and CIC-DUX4 was detected using the polyclonal anti-CIC antibody (2).

The mice transplanted CDS tumor cells introduced with the luciferase cDNA were administrated with luciferin. Illumination from the labeled tumor cells were monitored by the IVIS Lumina LT imaging system (Perkin Elmer).

Total RNA extraction, reverse transcription and RNA quantification were performed according to methods described previously (2). Conventional RT-PCR and real-time quantitative RT-PCR were performed using a Gene Amp 9700 thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems) and a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems), respectively. The sequences of the oligonucleotide primers used are shown in Supplemental Table S1.

GeneChip analysis was conducted to determine gene expression profiles. The murine HT MG-430 PM array (Affymetrix) was hybridized with aRNA probes generated from eMC 48 h after transduction with pMYs-CIC-DUX4 or empty vector, CDS and ES tumor tissues, or a mixture of mouse normal tissues according to methods described previously (11). The expression data were analyzed using GeneSpring ver 12.6 (Agilent Technologies) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA-P 2.0 software (12). The microarray data sets are accessible through the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo), with an accession number GSE90978.

The microarray data from six mCDS and five hCDS samples (1) were compared with human sarcoma microarray data sets. Data from the ONCOMINE data base (https://www.oncomine.org/) were accessed in June 2011. CEL files from E-MEXP-1142, E-MEXP-353, GSE21122, GSE7529, mCDS and hCDS samples were summarized by MAS5 algorithm with Affymetrix Power Tool software version 1.12.0 (Affymetrix). Each probe set that had same Gene Symbols was collapsed into a single probe set that showed the highest median value in the raw MAS5 dataset. Each mouse gene of collapsed datasets was joined into the same gene symbol of human collapsed datasets that resulted in one dataset with 10,166 probe sets. Signal intensities of combined dataset were normalized by quantile algorithm with ‘preprocessCore’ library package on Bioconductor software (13, 14). To obtain the genes that are highly (or lowly) expressed in hCDS than other human samples, linear models for microarray analysis (limma) package of Bioconductor software were applied (15). Hierarchical clustering was performed using 1,450 probe sets as hCDS-specific genes by MeV software and Pearson's centered measurements (16). The criteria of the specific genes were limma p-value < 0.05 and absolute log-fold-change (|logFC|) > 1.

siRNAs against mouse Ccnd2, mouse Ret, human CIC (QIAGEN) and mouse Bcl2 (Life Technologies) were transfected into mouse CDS cell lines using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). The list of siRNA is shown in Supplemental Table S2. Knock-down efficiencies of each gene were confirmed by RT-PCR.

Mouse CDS cells were treated with palbociclib, vandetanib, ABT199, lenalidomide, pazopanib, bortezomib (Selleckchem), trabectedin and eribulin (Taiho Pharmaceutical) in vitro. The cell proliferation and viability were determined by measuring cell numbers with Trypan blue exclusion. For in vivo experiments, 1 × 106 cells were transplanted subcutaneously into Balb/c nude mice, and the mice were treated with palbociclib and/or trabectedin. Palbociclib was orally administrated 150 mg/kg daily for two weeks, and trabectedin was intravenously 100 mg/kg/day three times for every four days, total 300 mg/kg in a course of experiment.

All data are given as the means ± SEM. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier life table method and survival between groups was compared with the log-rank test. Continuous distributions were compared with a 2-tailed Student's t test.

First we aimed to establish an ex vivo mouse model expressing CIC-DUX4 using an established ex vivo technology (7). We introduced the human CIC-DUX4 cDNA into murine eMC derived from embryo limbs on dpc 18.5. The eMC were then transplanted subcutaneously into nude mice. All the recipient mice developed solid tumor masses at 100% penetrance with a mean latency of 24 days (Fig. 1A, B). The tumor latency of mouse CDS (mCDS) was significantly shortened than that of mouse ES (mES) reported previously (Fig. 1A; ref 6). Although transduced cell types were different between CDS and ES models, CIC-DUX4 expression in osteochondrogenic progenitors also developed CDS in a similar manner with limb eMC (data not shown), suggesting that CIC-DUX4 might be acceptable by more diverse cell lineages than EWS-FLI1. Characterization of eMC by the cell surface marker analysis suggest that they are composed of multi-lineage stem/progenitors (Supplemental Fig. S1).

Histological analysis showed that the majority of mCDS tumors consisted of small round to short spindle cells arranged in solid sheet pattern with rather abundant stroma (Fig. 1C, 1D). Tumors were frequently associated with a prominent extracellular matrix (ECM) (Fig. 1E). This histologic heterogeneity of CDS was also described in human tumors (1, 3, 4). The tumors (1 × 106 cells) were serially transplantable at 100% penetrance to both nude mice (16/16) and immune competitive Balb/c mice (9/9), and spontaneous metastasis to the lung was observed in 28% (total 7/25, 2/16 in nude mice and 5/9 in Balb/c mice) of secondary transplanted recipients with mean latency of 5.9 weeks (5.8 weeks in nude mice and 6.6 weeks in Balb/c mice) (Fig. 1F). When tumor cells were injected via tail vein, systemic metastases in the lung, liver, ovary and thoracic cavities were observed (Fig. 1G). The CIC-DUX4 protein was detected by immunoprecipitation followed by western blotting in CDS cells (Fig. 1H).

To examine the characteristic molecular signatures in CDS, comprehensive gene expression profiling was carried out. Comparing the gene expression profiles of the eMC transduced with the CIC-DUX4 retrovirus versus the empty vector, and between murine CDS (mCDS) and eMC with an empty vector. Gene expression signatures in mCDS were also compared to the mouse ES (mES). The microarray analysis showed that 4,659 and 353 genes were upregulated in CIC-DUX4-expressing eMC vs eMC with an empty vector; fold change > 2.0, p < 0.05, and in mCDS tumors vs eMC with an empty vector; fold change > 2.0, p < 0.05, respectively. Furthermore, 69 genes were shown to be upregulated in both categories (Fig. 2A). In addition, 758 and 89 genes were downregulated in CIC-DUX4-expressing eMC and mCDS tumors in the same criteria, respectively. The lists of genes significantly up- or down-regulated in each category are shown in Supplemental Tables S3 and S4. Notably a subset of PEA3 family genes of ETS transcription factors such as Etv1 and Etv4 were significantly upregulated in mCDS tumors and Etv1 and Etv5 in CIC-DUX4-expressing eMC (Table 1 and Supplemental Table S3), consistent with the previous findings that PEA3 family genes are transcriptional targets of CIC-DUX4 (2). Moreover, the upregulated genes of CIC-DUX4-expressing eMC, mCDS tumors or both include Crh, Vgf, Dlk1, Zic1 and Ccnd2 (Table 1 and Supplemental Table S3) that were found upregulated in human CDS (hCDS) (1), indicating that mouse and hCDS share common gene expression signatures.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed strong correlation between gene expression in CIC-DUX4-expressing eMC and ECM organization, and in mCDS tumors and the Cyclin D1 signature (Fig. 2B). The data suggest that induction of ECM production might be initiated by CIC-DUX4 expression, and that Cyclin D-associated cell cycle progression might be one of the important genetic hallmarks of CDS.

The expression profile of mCDS was then compared with that of mES. A principal component analysis showed distinct gene expression signatures in two tumor types (Fig. 2C), and hierarchical clustering showed 1,661 genes differentially expressed in mCDS or mES (Fig. 2D and Supplemental Table S5). In these 952 and 709 genes were upregulated in mCDS or mES, respectively, and Bcl2, Ccnd1, Ccnd2, Crh, Etv1, Etv4, Etv5, Fli1, Muc5ac, Ret and Zic1 were found as top 40 upregulated genes in mCDS (Table2). Expression of these genes were confirmed by quantitative RT-PCR (Fig. 2E, Supplemental Fig. S3). In addition, expression of these genes was also upregulated when CIC-DUX4 was introduced into NIH3T3 cells (Supplemental Fig. S4). Our previous study also showed upregulation of ETV1 and ETV5 by CIC-DUX4 in the human osteosarcoma U2OS cell, and CIC-DUX4 expression induced anchorage-independent growth of NIH3T3 cells (2). In contrast, upregulation of Bcl2, Ccnd1, Ccnd2, Muc5ac and Ret, and growth promotion were not observed when CIC-DUX4 was introduced into bone marrow-derived MSC (BM-MSC) (Supplemental Fig. S4), suggesting CIC-DUX4 function is cell context-dependent at least in part.

GSEA showed significant enrichment of gene sets such as the VEGF signaling pathway and Cyclin D1 (Fig. 2F), the latter indicating that the cell cycle progression is even more aggressive in mCDS than in mES. Many ECM genes such as collagen, fibromodulin, proteoglycan genes were also manifested in mCDS compared with mES (Table 2). Furthermore, expression profiles of mCDS were compared with eight types of human sarcomas, including hCDS, hES, synovial sarcoma, osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, myxoid liposarcoma, malignant fibrous histiocytoma and neuroblastoma. Hierarchical clustering using common gene sets between mice and humans (1,450 probes selected from 10,166 probe sets) showed that the mCDS was most closely related to hCDS (Fig. 2G), indicated similar gene expression profiles between mCDS and hCDS.

The distinct gene expression profiles between mCDS and mES provide useful information to identify CDS-specific biomarkers for potential use for immunohistochemistry. CCND2 (Cyclin D2) and MUC5AC were selected for further validation by immunohistochemistry, using ETV4 as a positive control. Consistent nuclear overexpression for CCND2 and ETV4 was noted in mCDS tumor cells, but CCND2 was negative in mES (Fig. 3A). ETV4 was occasionally detected in mES. MUC5AC, a mucin component, was positive in mCDS and negative in mES (Fig. 3A). The frequencies of positive cells for CCND2, MUC5AC or ETV4 were quantitated. The results for CCND2 and MUC5AC were consistent in all mCDS (n = 6) and mES tumors (n = 9) tested.

CCND2 and MUC5AC expression was further examined in human CIC-rearranged sarcoma (hCIC) and human ES (hES). Nuclear and cytoplasmic expression with variable proportion of CCND2 in > 70% of tumor cells was evident in all hCIC tested (10/10 cases), whereas negative in seven cases or only limited expression (< 20% tumor cells) in two cases of 10 ES samples (Fig. 3B). Eight out of 10 hCIC samples showed scattered positive staining of MUC5AC in cytoplasm and/or extracellular matrix. In contrast, no MUC5AC staining was identified in 10 hES samples (Fig. 3B). Moreover, CCND2 and MUC5AC was rarely positive in hSS, hRS and hEMCS which often show small round cell morphologies. These results indicate that CCND2 and MUC5AC are promising biomarkers useful for diagnosis of CIC-rearranged sarcomas.

Gene expression data provides further support of the critical role of CIC-DUX4 and its downstream signals in mCDS growth, which can be utilized as effective targets for therapy. Ccnd2, Ret and Bcl2 were selected from the upregulated genes in the mCDS based on expression rates and biological importance in cancer. RNA interference-mediated gene knockdown experiments showed significant growth inhibition by suppression of these genes as well as CIC (Fig. 4A, B).

We have previously shown that the mouse model for ES could be used as a platform for evaluation of inhibitory drugs targeting sarcoma (7). Therefore, the current CDS model was expected to provide important information for novel therapeutics. To this end specific inhibitors for cyclin dependent kinases 4 and 6 (palbociclib), Ret (vandetanib) and Bcl2 (ABT199) were tested for their tumor suppressive activities against mCDS cells (17-19), as knockdown of Ccnd2, Ret and Bcl2 genes was effective for growth inhibition. These inhibitors showed substantial growth inhibitory effect with palbociclib as the highest efficacy (Fig. 5A). However, mCDS-specific inhibitory effect of palbociclib was not observed as the effects were similar with the human clear cell sarcoma cell line, KAS, and the osteosarcoma cell line, U2OS. Anti-cancer drugs and inhibitors, bortezomib, eribulin, lenalidomide, pazopanib and trabectedin that are used for soft tissue neoplasms were also tested for mCDS cells (20-24). Among these a proteasome inhibitor bortezomib and an alkylating agent trabectedin showed significant effects both for mCDS and mES cells (Fig. 5B and data not shown).

Anti-tumor effect of the trabectedin, palbociclib and bortezomib were then tested in vivo. Since preliminary experiments showed little effect of bortezomib (data not shown), trabectedin and palbociclib were administrated alone or in combination to the mCDS bearing mice. Trabectedin showed marked growth suppression, whereas palbociclib did not show significant effect nor enhancement of trabectedin's inhibition (Fig. 5C). There was no significant difference in the degree of necrotic areas and BrdU labeling index (Supplemental Fig. S3). Trabectedin efficacy for ES was reported (25). Our results suggest similar treatment might be selected for CDS, however, it is ideal to develop treatment targeting CIC-DUX4 and related signaling pathways for precision medicine-based treatment. Nonetheless, the current model provides an effective tool to explore and evaluate novel therapeutic drugs both in vitro and in vivo.

Human CDS and CIC-rearranged sarcoma is a recently established disease entity, formerly considered as Ewing-related sarcoma. The morphologic overlap might be related in part to the activation of PEA3 family genes, as some of them are also involved in ES gene fusions (2, 26). However, more recent clinical, genetic and histopathologic investigation elucidated the distinct nature of CDS from ES (1, 3, 4, 27). Here, we have established the first mouse model for CDS expressing the human CIC-DUX4 fusion gene. The model faithfully recapitulates the highly aggressive nature of mCDS, the distinct morphologic and biologic characteristics similar to hCDS.

The distinct gene expression profiles of these mouse models suggest a molecular basis for the phenotypic differences between CDS and ES. In CDS, the abundant ECM noted correlated with an upregulation of various collagen genes, their modifying enzymes and matrix proteases, consistent with enhanced ECM induction (28). Similarly, abundant ECM in CDS was also reported in human CDS either as dense fibrous septa or myxoid stroma (29). Interestingly, spontaneous hematogenous metastases, which were never found in the ES mouse model, were observed in the CDS model, which might be related to enhanced vasculogenesis in CDS. The ECM in CDS contains mucin components, and MUC5AC is one such component secreted by tracheobronchial and gastrointestinal epithelium (30), which was expressed in a small subset of the tumor cells and ECM of CDS, though its functional significance in sarcoma cells remain to be clarified.

The upregulation of cell cycle progression signature was prominent in mCDS, even more than in mES in which activation of CCND1 was reported (31). In mCDS, the top upregulated Cyclin D gene was Ccnd2. A previous report showed that transcription of Ccnd2 is directly regulated by ETV4 (32), suggesting that Ccnd2 upregulation is achieved by multiple PEA3 family proteins induced by CIC-DUX4 in CDS. Therefore, the CCND2 can be used as an informative diagnostic tool, though CCND1 expression is also enhanced. Like Cyclin D1 Cyclin D2 is a regulatory subunit of the holoenzymes functioning with catalytic partners CDK4/6 that phosphorylates and inactivates RB. Although Cyclin D2 is less frequently involved in human cancer, it has an ability to replace Cyclin D1 function (33-35). Inhibition of CDK4/6 is therefore expected to suppress cell proliferation of mCDS. The CDK inhibitor palbociclib indeed inhibited mCDS growth in vitro, however, it showed only limited effect for tumor growth in vivo. The cause of weak response of mCDS to palbociclib in vivo remains unclear. The use of novel CDK4/6 inhibitors may improve the effect of CCND2 targeting. In contrast, trabectedin was effective for mCDS tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo. Trabectedin is a Caribbean tunicate-extracted alkylating agent and is used for ovarian cancer and soft part sarcoma including leiomyosarcoma and liposarcoma (24, 25, 36, 37). Previous study reported that trabectedin exhibited growth inhibition of ES as a single agent, and improved effect in combination with an IGFR inhibitor (25). Therefore, trabectedin is a promising therapeutic agent for CDS despite lack of the synergistic effect with palbociclib.

CIC is an HMG-box transcription factor homologous to drosophila capicua (38). As a downstream of receptor tyrosine kinase signaling, it acts as a transcriptional repressor associating with TLE co-repressors (39). By fusion to DUX4 the transrepressional activity of CIC is converted to strong transactivation by replacement with the DUX4 C-terminal transactivation domain whose activity was indicated in the wild-type DUX4 function (2, 40). In our model, upregulation of the CIC-DUX4 targets such as Etv4, Crh and Zic1 is consistently manifested, indicating the important role of CIC-DUX4 transactivation in tumorigenesis. In this context, juxtaposition of DUX4 to the IgG enhancer in human B cell lymphoblastic leukemia where the transactivation domain of DUX4 is deleted is intriguing (41). CIC is also involved in the majority (70-80%) of human oligodendroglioma and infantile primitive neuroectodermal neoplasms of brain (42-44). In oligodendroglioma, mutations result in loss-of-function of CIC. Collectively these findings indicate aberrations of CIC function is one of key molecular events in multiple human cancer.

In summary, we generate a CDS mouse model by CIC-DUX4 expression in murine eMC. The eMC exhibit sufficient plasticity to tolerate cancer-associated fusion genes, possibly by bypassing oncogene-induced senescence (9). mCDS of the current model can be generated rapidly and be maintained in the immune competent mouse, which enable to evaluate host responses against the tumor more precisely than in the xenograft model. Introduction of fusion genes into eMC will therefore provide accurate and reliable modeling of human sarcoma, useful for biomarker and novel therapy discovery.
