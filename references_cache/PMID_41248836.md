# Zebrafish col4a1 loss-of-function models mirror key neurovascular and ocular features of COL4A1/A2 syndrome and enable human variants assessment in vivo.
**Authors:** Paradisi G, Bonavolontà V, Venditti M, Fasano G, Pedalino C, Del Bene F, Tartaglia M, Lauri A
**Journal:** Matrix Biol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.matbio.2025.11.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.matbio.2025.11.004)

## Content

Pathogenic variants in COL4A1 and COL4A2, encoding type IV collagen α1 and α2 chains-core components of all basement membranes-cause a multisystem disorder with variable expressivity. Affected individuals commonly present with cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD), unmanageable intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH), drug-resistant epilepsy, microphthalmia, and congenital cataract. Severe phenotypes are often linked to glycine substitutions that disrupt α1/α2 heterotrimer assembly, though insertions, deletions, and haploinsufficiency seem to also be pathogenic. Limited insight into collagen IV α1 and α2 biology and how specific variants affect their functions-coupled with a lack of rapid in vivo assays for functional variants classification-hampers patient stratification and therapy development. Here, we established and characterized two complementary col4a1 knockdown (KD) models in zebrafish. Taking advantages of their transparency and rapid development we set-up in vivo assays for neurovascular and ocular phenotyping. Both models reproduced key features of human disease, including ventriculomegaly, vascular fragility with spontaneous and trauma-induced ICH, microphthalmia, and cataracts. Notably, expression of human wild-type COL4A1 partially rescued most of the observed defects, while pathogenic glycine-substitution variants failed to do so. These findings validate col4a1 KD in zebrafish as a robust in vivo model of some aspects of COL4A1/A2 syndrome, highlighting a conserved role of collagen IV α1 in neurovascular and ocular development. Our results also support haploinsufficiency as a contributing pathogenic mechanism, alongside dominant-negative effects. This work lays the foundation for the use of zebrafish to support rapid COL4A1 and COL4A2 variants pathogenicity assessment and mechanistic studies, with the potential to accelerate development of targeted therapies.

Collagen type IV α1 and α2 chains, encoded by the COL4A1 and COL4A2 genes, are among the most ancestral and evolutionary conserved collagens. They assemble into heterotrimers, typically as α1α1α2(IV), and serve as essential structural components of all basement membranes (BMs) [[1], [2], [3]]. As specialized extracellular matrices (ECMs), BMs provide critical mechanical support and regulate signalling pathways involved in tissue development and homeostasis [4,5].

Pathogenic COL4A1 and COL4A2 variants—either inherited or de novo—are increasingly recognized as the cause of a spectrum of multisystem disorders now collectively termed “Gould syndrome” or “COL4A1/A2 syndrome”, characterized by variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance across multiple organs [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Clinically, affected individuals exhibit a wide range of phenotypes that often involve a serious pathology of the cerebrovascular system, white matter and ocular structures [[9], [10], [11]] (OMIM:
175,780,
614,483,
618,564), as well as kidneys and muscles [10,12] (i.e. HANAC syndrome, OMIM:
611,773) [[13], [14], [15]].

The neurological features—particularly cSVD—are among the most frequent and severe manifestations. cSVD can lead to oligosymptomatic microbleeds or more often severe strokes underlying ICH, occurring at various stages of life and leading to particularly severe consequences when occurring prenatally [10,[16], [17], [18], [19]]. Stroke itself is a devastating outcome, often arising in the absence of prior cardiovascular symptoms, and resulting in long-term disability, dementia, or death [10,16].

In children with COL4A1/A2 pathogenic variants, severe cSVD and ICH frequently co-occur with porencephaly—which is characterized by brain cysts or large cavities—alongside other brain malformations, white matter abnormalities, and drug-resistant epilepsy. Collectively, these neurological symptoms are estimated to occur in over 70 % of cases [10,12,20].

Ophthalmological features are similarly common and include congenital cataracts, retinal vascular anomalies, and anterior segment dysgenesis [4,9,10,12,21]. Notably, ocular manifestations can be present even in mildly affected carriers as isolated traits, highlighting their potential diagnostic/prognostic value [10].

The tropism of these clinical signs aligns with the known location, abundance and existing functional evidence of collagen IV-rich BMs. As demonstrated in knockout (KO) studies in animal models [4,[22], [23], [24]] regulated interactions between the BM and endothelial cells in the brain are fundamental to maintain neurovascular integrity. Similarly, collagen IV, including α1 and α2 chains, are key components of the ocular BM supporting proper eye morphogenesis and vasculogenesis [[25], [26], [27], [28]].

At the molecular level, most pathogenic variants follow an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern [8] and involve substitutions of glycine residues in the conserved Gly-Xaa-Yaa motif of the triple helical domain [7,8] —crucial for collagen heterotrimer formation [2].

Mouse models have so far been instrumental in establishing causality between COL4A1 mutations and the disease. They have also begun to elucidate possible pathomechanisms including impaired trimerization, intracellular retention, and decreased collagen IV α1 secretion [29], which collectively contribute to cerebrovascular fragility and other organs’ pathologies [6,16,30]. Compelling data from Col4a1 mutant mice begin to reveal candidate genotype-phenotype relationships and tissue-specific mechanistic insights relevant to therapy development. These include impaired TGF-β signalling [31,32] and ER stress and UPR activation [6,16,30] as well as defective integrin signalling [29].

Moreover, variants of unknown significance (VUS) (HGMD database [10,12,33]) as well as reports of likely pathogenic variants affecting non-glycine residues [34,35], splicing variants, and 3′ UTR insertions leading to COL4A1 overexpression [12,36,37] are increasingly being reported. Additionally, cases of haploinsufficiency have been identified in severely affected patients with porencephaly, ICH, and ocular disease [12,38]. This expanding mutational spectrum further suggests that COL4A1/A2 syndrome may involve complex, dosage-sensitive mechanisms whose developmental impact is still being elucidated, with in vivo investigations of this hypothesis now emerging [39].

COL4A1/A2 syndrome remains without cure and effective management. Only recently, efforts by European consortia, patients' associations initiatives and international expert panels have been directed to establish diagnostic and management guidelines [20,40]. However, marked phenotypic variability—even among individuals carrying the same variant and within a single family—points to the influence of genetic background, unknown modifier loci, and/or environmental factors, hindering genotype-phenotype correlations, clinical risk prediction, and the development of generalizable care recommendations. Multidisciplinary approaches, particularly including neurological and ophthalmological evaluation in carriers, are increasingly advocated together with guidance from updated insights into tissue-specific mechanisms arising from in vivo models [10,20].

The speed by which novel missense and non-canonical variants awaiting validation and investigation are discovered, and the unmet need for in vivo mechanisms and drug assessment currently outpaces the functional testing capacity of labour-intensive rodent models.

Although valuable insights have also emerged from simpler in vitro and in vivo models, i.e. fruit flies [41,42] and patient-derived iPSCs [43], there is a growing need for complementary vertebrate in vivo models enabling rapid, tissue-specific visualization and mapping of developmental and functional phenotypes. Ideally, such models should be cost-effective, scalable, and vertebrate-based, with compatibility for medium- to large-scale drug screening.

We reasoned that the zebrafish (Danio rerio) presents such an opportunity. As a vertebrate with a single conserved ortholog for each of COL4A1 and COL4A2 [44] and near-transparent and externally-developing embryos of small size, zebrafish enables real-time imaging of brain, vasculature, and ocular development without invasive procedures. The model is also highly amenable to both transient and stable gene manipulations, including standard and advanced CRISPR/Cas9 editing [[45], [46], [47]] and supports rapid imaging with high resolution [48]. Its proven utility in modelling and understanding monogenic brain malformations - and basement membrane physiology [49,50] as well as in studies of cerebrovascular genetics [[22], [23], [24],51,52] makes it ideally suited for this context.

In this study, we established and characterized two complementary col4a1 loss-of-function (LoF) zebrafish models. Through imaging in live fish and ad hoc functional assays, we reveal high penetrance of neurovascular and ocular phenotypes that recapitulate key aspects of COL4A1/A2 syndrome. Furthermore, we demonstrate the utility of this model to functionally assess the pathogenicity of human COL4A1 variants in vivo.

To evaluate the potential of zebrafish as a model to study COL4A1/A2 related disorders and validate emerging COL4A1 variants, we first generated col4a1 knockdown (KD) embryos. To this end, we injected 1-cell stage embryos with an antisense morpholino oligonucleotide (MO) targeting the ATG start site of the endogenous col4a1 transcript. An initial gross phenotyping using standard MO doses to downregulate col4a1 showed a dose-dependent effect on embryo growth and overall brain/eye size and morphology (Fig. S1).

Effective col4a1 downregulation in morphant fish injected with the intermediate/low-dose MO (0.6 mM) was confirmed by detecting collagen IV α1 protein levels at the BM of the myotendinous junctions (MTJs) - an easily recognizable structure forming at the vertical myosepta of developing myotomes [53]. Quantitative analysis here revealed an approximate 50 % decrease in BM collagen IV α1 signal compared to control embryos (Fig. S2A,B). A dose-dependent reduction in BM collagen IV α1 was also observed across a range of MO concentrations from 0.4 mM to 0.8 mM (Fig. S3A,A’), consistent with a progressive specific BM disruption phenotype that became more severe at higher doses (Fig. S3B,B’).

To minimize potential toxic effects that could be coupled to high MO doses in subsequent detailed phenotypic analyses, we selected the penetrant intermediate/low MO dose (0.6 mM) for in-depth brain and eye phenotyping.

Quantitative assessment in col4a1 KD embryos at 48 hpf post fertilization (hpf) revealed a significant increase of the fluid-filled ventricular space along the anterior to posterior brain axis, consistent with occurrence of ventriculomegaly (Fig. 1A-C). Assessment of brain area in the Tg(NBT:DsRed) background, marking differentiated neurons, showed that col4a1 KD ventriculomegaly is associated with an overall reduction of the anterior brain mass (Fig. 1C’, Fig.S4A), a feature commonly observed in affected individuals. In fish injected with high-dose col4a1 MO we also observed large brain cavities, resembling hydrocephalus-like conditions reported for severe cases showing early pre-natal ICH [11,12,54] (Fig. S4B). In addition, by careful inspection under a stereomicroscope, we noted that circa 10.5 % on average of the col4a1 KD fish developed spontaneous deep intracerebral brain haemorrhage (ICH) by 72hpf (Fig. 1B,D'). Haemorrhagic events increased to nearly 30 % on average considering other sites besides the central nervous system (Fig. 1D), e.g. at the level of the caudal vessels in the posterior trunk and the retina Fig. S5A,B). Confirming the specificity of the observed phenotypes, co-injection of col4a1 ATG MO with human COL4A1WT mRNA partially rescued both ventriculomegaly and anterior brain size, with a significant reduction of the number of fish exhibiting spontaneous ICH (Fig. 1A-C). Having established (1) the presence of relevant brain phenotypes in col4a1 KD models and (2) the feasibility of documenting phenotype modulation through genetic perturbation, we next assessed the capacity of our in vivo assay to capture the pathogenicity of known human COL4A1 variants. To this aim, we selected two recurrent substitutions —p.(Gly755Arg) (G755R) and p.(Gly773Arg)(G773R)— residing within a glycine-rich region frequently implicated in disease and modelled in mice [7,8]. The pathogenic nature of these variants, particularly in neurovascular and ocular systems, is supported by their identification in at least five unrelated families [9,[55], [56], [57], [58], [59], [60], [61], [62]] (Table S1). Therefore, these variants provide a positive control to test the efficacy of our assay. Different from what was observed for the COL4A1WT mRNA, co-injection of col4a1 ATG-MO with either COL4A1G773R or COL4A1G755R mRNA failed to rescue the brain phenotypes, ranging from mild to severe ventriculomegaly, brain malformations and ICH (Fig. 1B-F, Fig. S5B).

These findings demonstrate that zebrafish col4a1 deficiency can model the neurological pathology seen in COL4A1/A2 syndrome and showcase the potential utility of this system for functionally validating the pathogenicity of human COL4A1 variants.

To further investigate the impact of col4a1 KD and expand our understanding of collagen IV α1/α2 function in neurovascular physiology, we evaluated susceptibility to trauma-induced intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH). In addition to spontaneous pre/perinatal ICH, stress-induced brain haemorrhage represents a clinically severe and potentially life-threatening complication of COL4A1/A2-related disease [16], with variable age of onset [16,18,63,64]. For this assay, zebrafish larvae were raised until 4 days post fertilization (dpf), a stage at which the brain and neuromuscular systems are functionally mature, and a cartilaginous scaffold begins to provide partial structural support to the brain [65,66]. At this stage, larvae exhibit active swimming and may encounter mechanical stimuli capable of unmasking latent vascular fragility.

As outlined in Fig. 2A, we evaluated two mechanical stimulation protocols of differing intensities, reasoning that an optimal stressor would selectively trigger a significant ICH increase only in vulnerable groups. Specifically, we tested: (1) short, repeated centrifugation at 4000 rpm (“major” stress), and (2) slow undulatory motion at 240 rpm (“minor” stress). To detect and quantify brain haemorrhage, larvae were immediately fixed and stained with o-dianisidine, which forms a brown precipitate upon reaction with haemoglobin, enabling visualization of haemorrhagic sites under light microscopy—even within the complex brain architecture of older larvae. While the “major stress” protocol induced ICH in both controls and col4a1 morphants, the “minor stress” protocol was able to reveal an increased ICH incidence selectively upon col4a1 downregulation at both 48 and 96 hpf, supporting an increased susceptibility to ICH upon col4a1 deficiency (Fig. 2B, B’). In rescue experiments, co-injection of col4a1 morpholino with human COL4A1WT partially restored resistance to trauma-induced ICH (Fig. 2C), supporting the specificity of the observed phenotype.

We next compared trauma-induced ICH severity among col4a1 morphants co-injected with the pathogenic COL4A1G773R or COL4A1G755R variants, alongside controls and col4a1 morphants co-injected with COL4A1WT. Since haemorrhages in col4a1 KD seemed widespread—affecting multiple regions from forebrain to hindbrain, including periorbital areas (Fig. 2B’)— we further refined the analysis by scoring the incidence of limited ("score 1″, single-region bleeds) versus extended ("score 2", hematomas spanning at least two major brain subdivisions) ICH (Fig. 2D). To better assess overall ICH severity across groups, we established a categorical scoring system, which further included “spontaneous” ICH (detectable in live larvae between 48 and 96 hpf without prior stimulation) as the most severe ICH category. While co-injection with COL4A1WT reduced the occurrence of severe ICH types, small bleeds (score 1) remained the predominant phenotype (Fig. 2E).

Last, mutant COL4A1, which failed to rescue the col4a1 knockdown phenotype, exhibited differential effects on the incidence of spontaneous vs trauma-induced intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH). Notably, the COL4A1G773R variant was associated with a higher incidence of early-onset spontaneous ICH, a pattern consistent with clinical reports of pre/perinatal haemorrhages in patients carrying the G773R substitution (Table S1), although the overall rarity of these events precludes definitive genotype–phenotype correlation. In severely affected larvae, we occasionally observed complete lack of o-dianisidine staining (Fig. 2E). Its relation to possible anaemia, reported in affected individuals [67,68], or to staining issues should be investigated.

These data demonstrate that both spontaneous and trauma-induced intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) can be effectively measured in a col4a1 LoF zebrafish model, and further validate functionally the deleterious impact of the human COL4A1G773R and COL4A1G755R variants associated with COL4A1/A2 syndrome on neurovascular stability.

To better understand the neurovascular impairments underlying the increased susceptibility to ICH in col4a1 KD fish, we next inspected the overall morphology of the cerebral vasculature in 48 hpf embryos. We employed Tg(kdrl:EGFP) larvae as genetic background, labelling endothelial cells and therefore allowing in vivo visualization of brain vasculature [69]. Confocal live imaging and volumetric z-stack reconstructions revealed varying degrees of neurovascular abnormalities in col4a1 morphants compared to controls (Fig. 3A-B). We confirmed occurrence of ventriculomegaly upon col4a1 downregulation, evident as an expanded ventricular space between the mesencephalic veins (MsV) (red contour and arrow in the reconstruction shown in Fig. 3B), along with irregularly shaped vessels and a marked reduction in the overall complexity of cranial vascular architecture, affecting the midbrain, hindbrain, and eyes (Fig. 3A–C). In severe cases, there was a near-complete absence of the mesencephalic and cerebellar central arteries (MMCtA and CCtA), which normally supply regions that we observed as most prone to haemorrhage in col4a1 morphants (Fig. 3A,B). Additionally, abnormally large-calibre vessels were also observed (asterisks in Fig. 3A). Quantitative measurements confirmed a significant reduction in the extension of the MMCtA and CCtA in col4a1 KD fish (Fig. 3C–C’). Volumetric reconstruction data showed that in 48 hpf control fish the hyaloid vasculature supplying the developing eye formed a branched and well-organized network, consistent with previous reports for this developmental stage [[70], [71], [72]]. In contrast, col4a1 KD fish exhibited an underdeveloped eye vasculature, characterized by a less cavitated/structured mesh vessels, with the presence of abnormally large-calibre or tortuous vessels at the level of the ventral and dorsal radial vessels (VRV and DRV, respectively) (Fig. S6A). By 72 hpf, control fish displayed a more refined and mature hyaloid artery vascular network in the eye, as reported at this stage [70], characterized by an overall decreasing of number of branches, whereas the hyaloid basket vasculature in col4a1 KD fish remained immature and underdeveloped (Fig. S6B).

All ocular basement membranes (BMs) are enriched in collagen IV α1/α2 [73,74] and are essential for proper eye and lens development [75]. Given the eye vascular abnormalities observed in our col4a1 KD zebrafish model and with the high incidence of ocular defects reported in affected individuals [10] (Table S1) and corresponding mouse models [30,73], we next investigated whether col4a1-deficient zebrafish exhibited ocular abnormalities.

As shown in Fig. 4A, immunolabeling against the collagen IV α1 chain confirmed a marked reduction of the signal in the BM surrounding the developing eye of col4a1 KD embryos compared to controls (indicated by green and black arrows Fig. 4A for control and col4a1 KD, respectively). Laminin-1 labelling, serving as a reference for the eye BM structures in controls (green arrow, Fig. 4A, right panel), did not show significant changes between controls and col4a1 KD fish (Fig. S7). At 48 hpf, col4a1 KD embryos injected with high-dose morpholino exhibited pronounced microphthalmia (Fig. 4A,B). Consistent with the known role of the lens BM in maintaining lens positioning and shape [76,77], these embryos displayed loose, misshapen lenses, frequently displaced within the eye field. Collagen IV α1 immunoreactivity was also reduced in the BM surrounding the retina (red arrow in controls in Fig. 4A, as reference), including the optic fissure region. Remodelling of this specialized collagen IV α1/α2-enriched BM is crucial for optic fissure fusion and therefore for retinal morphogenesis, and proper eye growth [[78], [79], [80]]. Consistently, we found that col4a1 morpholino injection led to delayed optic fissure closure, a defect partially rescued by co-injection of human COL4A1WT mRNA (Fig. S8). Microphthalmia and its partial rescue by COL4A1WT were confirmed through quantitative measurements from an independent set of experiments, in which embryos were mounted in a 3D hydrogel (1.5 % low-melting-point agarose) to minimize mechanical compression artifacts (Fig. 4B, C). Notably, neither COL4A1G773R nor COL4A1G755R mutant mRNAs restored normal eye development (Fig. 4C), consistent with clinical data from affected individuals carrying these pathogenic variants (Table S1). These findings demonstrate the pathogenicity of COL4A1 deficiency and of the tested COL4A1 variants in the context of ocular development.

In individuals affected with COL4A1/A2 syndrome, congenital cataracts frequently co-occur with microphthalmia and anterior segment dysgenesis, contributing to severe visual impairment [7] (Table S1). Recent studies also link impaired eye vascular development and persistent foetal vasculature (PFV) to both microphthalmia and congenital cataract in affected subjects [81].

Given the vascular and morphological ocular defects observed in col4a1 KD zebrafish, we next assessed lens opacity—a proxy for cataract— readily observable by simple differential interference contrast (DIC) microscopy in living embryos treated with the depigmentation agent 1-phenyl 2-thiourea (PTU). As reported [76,82] cataract was determined by the presence and size of central aggregates within the lens that disrupt its transparency and smooth appearance (Fig. 5). While control embryos maintained clear lenses till 72 hpf, at this stage a high percentage of col4a1 morphant fish developed such central lens opacities (Fig. 5A). We finally compared cataract severity in all genetic conditions and observed that compared to col4a1 KD fish groups injected with either COL4A1WT or with the pathogenic COL4A1G773R and COL4A1G775R mutants showed a milder cataract phenotype (Fig. 5C).

These findings support a dosage-sensitive role for collagen IV α1 in lens development and transparency, and suggest that in our experimental settings col4a1 deficiency is more severe than the variants tested.

To quantify these structural abnormalities, we implemented a semi-automated imaging platform and deconvolution using 96-well fluorescent imaging plates allowing consistent positioning of stained larvae in low melting agarose volume on the lateral side, suitable for lens imaging (Fig. 6C). We profiled phalloidin intensity along the lens radial axis from the peripheral reference point P (blue line in Fig. 6A,D,D’).

Fluorescence intensity profiles along the radial axis were analysed and fitted using linear regression to evaluate the spatial distribution of actin fibers from the periphery to the center of the lens. In non-injected controls, a steep and regular decline in phalloidin signal was observed from the periphery toward the center, that fitted an expected exponential decay (R² = 0.8), reflecting proper peripheral localization of actin-rich fibers and clear central zones (Fig. 6D–F).

In contrast, an irregularly shaped F-actin fibers network was observed with low-resolution imaging in col4a1 KD lenses (Fig. 6D’). The fluorescent decay from the peripheral to the center of the lens showed a significantly flatter trajectory (R² = 0.5), indicating a slower loss of actin signal towards the center of the lens compared to controls, consistent with the observed maintenance of central fibers and lens opacity (Fig. 6D-F). Notably, co-injection of col4a1 KD embryos with WT human COL4A1 mRNA partially rescued the actin distribution phenotype, as evidenced by a steeper decline in the intensity profile (R² = 0.6), though full restoration to control levels was not achieved (Fig. 6D’–F). Irregular deposition of F-actin fibers towards the center of the lens was observed in larvae expressing either the COL4A1G755R and COL4A1G773R mutant mRNAs (Fig. 6D’). Among these, injection of COL4A1G773R mRNA supported partial recovery (R² = 0.65), while COL4A1G755R exacerbated the defect observed in col4a1 KD fish, exhibiting the most severe disorganization and the flattest decay profile (R² = 0.4) (Fig. 6D’–F, Fig.S9).

These findings demonstrate that col4a1 KD can recapitulate the cataract phenotype observed in the patients and that proper COL4A1 function in the eye BM is critical for guaranteeing correct lens development and differentiation.

As confirmed by direct sequencing performed from a pool of crispants heads, approximately 90 % of the assessed col4a1 alleles carried indels and/or large deletions (Fig. S10G, left). Out-of-frame indels resulting in premature termination were detected at all three targeted exons, albeit with variable efficiency (Fig. S10B–D, G). In addition, the expected large deletions >2 kb (spanning exons 11 to 30) were frequently observed (Fig. S10E–F), with near-full penetrance of these events predicted to introduce stop codons within the central region encoding the collagenous domain (Fig. S10G). qRT-PCR further confirmed reduced col4a1 transcript levels (Fig. S11), consistent with the effective genomic editing.

Last, phenotypic assessment revealed hallmark features of COL4A1/A2-related pathology as those observed in col4a1 morphant fish. Col4a1 crispants exhibited ventriculomegaly at 48 hpf (Fig. 7B) and spontaneous ICH, clearly visible by 72 hpf (Fig. 7B′). Upon minor mechanical stress at 96 hpf, a subset of crispants developed extensive haemorrhages throughout the anterior brain, spanning forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain, that were not observed in controls (Fig. 7B″), confirming increased vascular fragility. In addition to neurovascular defects, eye development was impaired: microphthalmia was evident at 72 hpf with significantly reduced eye area (microphthalmia) in crispants compared to controls (Fig. 7C).

In contrast to col4a1 morphant fish, only mild lens opacities, consistent with early cataract, were observed in a minority of embryos at 72 hpf (Fig. 7C′). Analysis of cranial and ocular vasculature in col4a1 crispants in Tg(kdrl:EGFP) background at 48 hpf confirmed a marked reduction in the extension of key midbrain vessels, including the middle mesencephalic central artery (MMCtA) and the cerebellar central artery (CCtA) compared to controls (Fig. S12). Abnormally thickened vessels were observed in the brain and eye regions of crispants, suggestive of defective vessel patterning and remodelling (Fig. S12).

Collectively, these findings in col4a1 crispants fish corroborate the phenotypes observed in fish injected with antisense oligonucleotide against col4a1 ATG. Altogether the data support the hypothesis that collagen IV α1 deficiency in the BM might underlie at least some of the neurovascular and ocular aspects of the COL4A1/A2 syndrome [4].

The catalogue of COL4A1 and COL4A2 mutations is rapidly expanding from classic glycine substitutions to out-of-frame indel, splice site, and regulatory variants, mirroring an equally broad, multiorgan phenotype spectrum, complicated by variable expressivity and penetrance, even within families. Yet variant discovery has outpaced functional insights and interpretation. However, many variants remain of uncertain clinical significance, hampering diagnosis, patient stratification and counselling.

In addition, knowledge on tissue-specific pathomechanisms is limited, with dominant‑negative effects caused by collagen IV α1/α2 mis‑assembly and/or haploinsufficiency in BM hypothesized and explored mainly in costly, low‑throughput mouse models [4]. Despite useful, emerging patient-derived iPSCs models lack the complexity of tissue contexts and developmental dynamics [88,89]. International recommendations from clinical experts call for models that reproduce organ‑specific pathology and support variant interrogation and testing of mechanism-inspired therapies [10,20].

To meet this need, we tested zebrafish col4a1 LoF models, leveraging the fish genetic tractability and its live 3D imaging capability to model core disease features and accelerate in‑vivo pathogenicity assessment.

We focused on neurovascular alterations, i.e. acute and trauma-induced ICH and ocular abnormalities, which altogether affect over 70 % of patients [10]. Besides epilepsy, haemorrhagic strokes associated with cSVD represent a significant disease burden, contributing to both disability and death—particularly when they occur prenatally. In such cases, they can lead to severe brain malformations ranging from porencephaly to hydranencephaly [11,54,55,90,91] or go unnoticed, even in adulthood, until triggered by trauma [92]. On the other hand, subtle ocular features may be among the earliest or sole manifestations in some carriers, making them clinically valuable indicators for evaluating unclassified variants [10].

We obtained two independent col4a1 LoF models in fish, using both morpholino (MO)-based knockdown and CRISPR/Cas9. Both models reliably reproduced key features of COL4A1/A2-related disease, i.e. near-full penetrance of neurovascular defects: ventriculomegaly of variable severity, reduced brain mass and spontaneous or stress-induced ICH in conjunction with ocular pathology. Increasing MO doses exacerbated neurodevelopmental phenotypes, mimicking severe human presentations for early-onset disease, such as hydranencephaly [54]. 3D live imaging in transgenic brains, not easily possible in mouse models, allowed us to visualize and quantify a fundamentally disrupted cerebrovascular architecture with missing midbrain and hindbrain arteries at ICH sites, suggesting developmental vascular defects underlie the disease.

In absence of birth trauma, a proposed contributor to prenatal ICH in patients and mouse [16], we subjected col4a1-deficient larvae to mild mechanical stress. These larvae developed significant ICH, providing an important in vivo support for clinical guidelines advising caution around environmental physical trauma in affected individuals [10].

Thanks to fish near-transparency, we occasionally detected extracerebral haemorrhages by simple bright field microscopy inspection of the embryos. To the best of our knowledge extracerebral events are rare in patients, but can occur [13], and similar events have can be observed also in mouse models [73,93]. However, analysing the frequency of extracerebral events in deep brain regions of mammalian models remains challenging due to limited optical accessibility. In contrast, zebrafish allow immediate detection of such events throughout the intact tissue in vivo, without the need for sectioning or deep-tissue imaging. This represents a clear advantage of the zebrafish disease model presented here, which can be integrated with evidence from other systems to refine variant-specific risk assessment.

The ocular phenotyping we observed further highlighted functional conservation of the role of collagen IV α1. Col4a1-deficient embryos exhibited delayed early optic fissure closure, microphthalmia, and lens opacities. The occasional trauma-induced lens displacement we observed in our models suggest lens laxity might occur in some individuals—an aspect that should be considered during ocular interventions. Thanks to the accessibility of the whole-developing eye in zebrafish we also quantitatively uncovered lens differentiations defects and disorganized actin lens fibers organization underlying ocular pahtology. However, further investigation is needed to prove the causal correlation between lens fibers differentiation defects and cataract development.

Live imaging showed that the developing hyaloid vasculature, normally enriched in collagen IV α1 and essential for retinal morphogenesis [94], remained immature in cola1 KD fish. Persistency of foetal vasculature is a common cause of microphthalmia and cataract, and was recently reported also for a patient harbouring COL4A1 pathogenic variants [81]. Eye vascular defects are also present in mouse models [21]. This evidence might suggest that at least part of the ocular phenotype arises from primary vascular defects and it is in line with mice data excluding a strict cell-autonomous mechanism for ocular disease [95].

The functional rescue of the phenotypes that we observed with human WT COL4A1, even if incomplete, reflects high conservation between human and zebrafish COL4A1 orthologs. Among collagen IV chains, zebrafish collagen IV α1 shows indeed the highest degree of conservation with the human counterpart. Conservation reaches even >80 % identity if only the functional NC1 domain is considered, a domain crucial for trimerization and collagen network formation [44].The partial rescue observed may reflect species-specific sequence divergence. Nevertheless, the use of the human WT COL4A1 ortholog in our experiments establishes a robust calibration standard for evaluating potential pathogenic effects of human variants. Future studies should aim to quantitatively assess potential differences in rescue efficiency between zebrafish and human COL4A1 orthologs.

Although lens opacity was not visibly reversed, actin fibers’ organization in the lens was significantly restored by human WT COL4A1, indicating partial rescue of lens differentiation. Nevertheless, further investigation is required to assess the actual ability of human collagen IV α1 to correctly assemble into a functional trimers with the zebrafish α2 chain.

It should be considered that cataract can occur also in a/symptomatic individuals [10,96] and by transgenic induction of various collagen IV chains expression (such as α1, α3, α4) in mouse models [75]. Hence, we can hypothesize lens tissue development to be particularly sensitive to correct COL4A1 dosage that our transient model cannot easily address.

Recurrent pathogenic glycine-substitution variants (G755R, G773R), which have been associated with severe cerebrovascular and ocular phenotypes in humans [9,[55], [56], [57], [58], [59], [60], [61], [62]], failed to rescue severe brain manifestations in col4a1-deficient zebrafish. In some cases, they worsened the phenotype, consistent with the hypothesized dominant-negative effect. Their incomplete impact on ocular phenotypes, however, further suggests tissue-specific thresholds or modifier influences [97]. Factors such as the use of depigmentation agents and specific transgenic backgrounds in our experimental settings might further modulate penetrance and should be accounted in future studies.

The independent LoF models shown here, the col4a1 crispants, reproduced the core neurovascular and ocular phenotypes seen in col4a1 morphant fish. Despite potential mosaicism in the F0 population, we could demonstrate high penetrance of stop-codon-producing indels, generated in the targeted collagenous domain, often involved in disease [8]. COL4A1 transcript levels were strongly reduced in crispants, supporting effective gene disruption.

While the neurovascular phenotypes mirrored what observed in col4a1 morphants, the crispants’ phenotypic variability with respect to cataract severity may reflect differential effects across tissues due to mosaicism or differential maternal mRNA contribution. This aspect should be further examined in stable CRISPR-Cas9 progeny.

Altogether, these findings show robustness of the model and the essential role of collagen IV α1 in maintaining basement membrane (BM) integrity during early brain and eye development.

Mechanistically, the dose sensitivity observed in our MO-mediated KD models, that often showed collagen IVα1 levels < 50 % —together with genetic evidence in humans—supports that reduced gene dosage alone can also be pathogenic. This is validated by the CRISPR–Cas9 model and overall consistent with COL4A1 extreme LoF intolerance (pLI = 1, gnomAD) and high haploinsufficiency score [98]. Haploinsufficiency may also contribute to disease pathogenesis in cases of glycine substitutions by limiting the availability of correctly folded, functional trimers in BM, as hypothesized [4]. Supporting this, pharmacological enhancement of protein secretion has improved ICH in mouse models [99]. Additional studies in vivo, along the line of the current efforts in Drosophila [42], are needed to further dissect general tissue-specific mechanisms and clarify whether negative dominance and haploinsufficiency might act synergistically.

In contrast to the embryonic lethality of homozygous Col4a1 and Col4a2 KO (-/-) mice [100,101], zebrafish crispants remained viable. This might be due to maternal contribution of Col4a1 mRNA and protein, lasting beyond zygotic transcription activation in fish and that can support early phases of development. In addition, despite the high mutagenic efficiency, the F0 population is still to be considered inherently mosaic, with cells harbouring different indel combinations that can result in variable transcript levels and residual protein. Hence, the col4a1 crispant fish we generated might be more comparable to haploinsufficiency models, as confirmed by the similar phenotypes in col4a1 morphants. While this evidence complicates direct comparison with mouse models, it provides a unique opportunity to examine early phenotypes caused by collagen IV α1 deficiency that would otherwise be inaccessible. Col4a1+/− mice exhibit low-penetrance mild cerebrovascular and ocular abnormalities, despite showing a range of residual protein expression similar to our models [39] and only a age dependent adult onset of vascular defects is observed in heterozygous Col4a2
+/− mice [89]. This evidence prompts further examination into species-specific compensatory mechanisms that may modulate phenotype severity.

In conclusion, col4a1-deficient zebrafish effectively model core and prevalent neurological and ocular features of COL4A1/A2 syndrome, validating it as a scalable, developmentally tractable system for studying the disease. We showcase the potential of the model for rapid in vivo assessment of human alleles under homeostatic and stress conditions. Our findings confirm evolutionary compatibility between human and zebrafish collagen IV α1, supporting functional rescue and variants’ testing. While observed phenotypes were informative, the limited number of variants tested and their low prevalence inhibit genotype-phenotype correlation, underscoring the need for a broader set of functionally validated alleles in vivo.

Importantly, this work also lays a foundation for preclinical evaluation of candidate therapeutics and genetic modifiers using rapidly assessed quantitative ICH and eye phenotypes in whole (even live)-tissues. Future efforts should also expand phenotyping to other affected organs, including kidney and muscle, and assess epileptic activity—another common and detrimental feature of COL4A1/2 disease.
