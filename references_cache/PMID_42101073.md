# Complicated Spastic Paraparesis: Study of a Patient With a De Novo Pathogenic Variant in ELOVL1.
**Authors:** Vaia Y, Mura E, Bruschi F, Zambrano S, Brena M, Tadini G, Arrigoni F, Antonello CE, Uggetti C, Parazzini C, Veggiotti P, Tonduti D
**Journal:** Int J Dev Neurosci (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/jdn.70137](https://doi.org/10.1002/jdn.70137)

## Content

Very long-chain fatty acid elongase-1 (ELOVL1) is essential for fatty acid elongation and is widely expressed in numerous tissues, including the central nervous system, being involved in the elongation of very long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs), which are essential for biological processes such as myelin formation. Monoallelic pathogenic variants in ELOVL1 have been described in association to a condition characterized by ichthyosis, spasticity, nystagmus and cerebral hypomyelination, although a similar but more severe presentation has been reported in patients with biallelic variants. We report the case of a new patient affected by ELOVL1-related leukodystrophy. Clinical data regarding pregnancy, delivery, age and symptoms at onset, psychomotor development and molecular diagnosis were systematically collected, as well as data on disease complications, neurological progression, the possible occurrence of epileptic seizures and instrumental examinations performed. The clinical picture was characterized by congenital ichthyosis, progressive spastic paraparesis and nystagmus. Brain MRI showed slightly progressive supratentorial and infratentorial white matter abnormalities, associated with thinning of the corpus callosum. Genetic analysis identified the de novo pathogenic variant p.Ser165Phe in ELOVL1. Although the spectrum of VLCFA catabolism defects is well documented, little is still known about disorders related to their biosynthesis. This study highlights the need to consider ELOVL1 mutations in the differential diagnosis of a child presenting with white matter abnormalities, progressive spastic paraplegia and congenital ichthyosis and emphasizes the importance of thorough neuroradiological and genetic investigations in the diagnosis and understanding of this rare neurometabolic disorder.

Elongation of very long chain fatty acids‐1 (ELOVL1) is one of the seven elongases identified in humans (Leonard et al. 2004). ELOVL1 plays a key role in the initial step of the fatty acid elongation cycle within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), being responsible for the elongation of very long‐chain fatty acids (VLCFAs), particularly saturated and monounsaturated C22–C26 (Yeh 1988), which are essential for processes such as myelin formation (Yeh 1988; Sargent et al. 1994). In fact, ELOVL1 is particularly abundant in highly myelinated regions of the central nervous system (CNS) (Kutkowska‐Kaźmierczak et al. 2018). Two unrelated children sharing the same dominant de novo pathogenic variant in ELOVL1 were reported by two independent research groups who studied the same two families (Kutkowska‐Kaźmierczak et al. 2018; Mueller et al. 2019), and recently, a third patient carrying the same heterozygous pathogenic variant was reported (Marcoux et al. 2025).

We report the longest clinical and neuroradiological follow‐up to date of a patient with ELOVL1‐related leukoencephalopathy carrying the same heterozygous de novo pathogenic variant, providing new insights into the interpretation of the characteristic white matter abnormalities associated with this autosomal dominant condition.

The patient first came to our attention at the age of 10 years, when she was addressed to our Service of Paediatric Neurology for the diagnostic work‐up regarding an unspecified gait disorder. She was of Italian origin and had no significant family or perinatal history. Psychomotor development was normal. Main concern in her past medical history was congenital pruritic ichthyosis. In particular, she presented at birth as a collodion baby, with subsequent evolution into an ichthyotic erythroderma characterized by diffuse hyperkeratosis and occasional bullous lesions. Keratinopathic ichthyosis was initially suspected but subsequently excluded by genetic testing. She underwent a first neuropsychiatric examination at around 18 months of age for nystagmus, which showed absence of any focal neurological deficits. At the age of 2 years, a brain MRI was performed showing mild nonspecific white matter abnormalities (Figure 1A). At the age of 6 years, she presented with progressive gait abnormalities, and an orthopaedic examination led to the diagnosis of bilateral pronator syndrome. At the age of 10, due to an abnormal posture with lumbar hyperlordosis and anteversion of the pelvis, as well as the presence of pyramidal signs in lower limbs, the patient was addressed to our Center. Neurological examination showed a clinical picture characterized by spastic paraparesis, nystagmus and intentional tremor. Cognitive functioning was within the normal range, as evaluated by the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children—Fourth Edition (WISC‐IV), with a Full‐Scale IQ of 109. Index scores show average verbal comprehension (VCI = 104), high‐average perceptual reasoning (PRI = 115), average working memory (WMI = 109) and average processing speed (PSI = 97). She was a level I Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) and Communication Function Classification System (CFCS). Total score at Gross Motor Function Measurement‐88 (GMFM‐88) was 93.2% (A: 100%, B: 100%, C: 83.3%, D: 92.3%, E: 90.3%). EEG showed a well‐organized and spatially structured trace, with a posterior background rhythm at 8 Hz, with only sporadic short sequences of right occipital sharp slow waves. Central conduction delay was observed at visual, somatosensory and auditory evoked potentials (VEP, SEP and BAEP). Electroretinogram (ERG) was normal. The audiometric examination revealed a bilateral high‐frequency sensorineural deafness. MRI examination confirmed the presence of mild periventricular and deep white matter signal abnormalities, which appeared more diffuse compared to the MRI performed at 2 years of age (Figure 1B,D,E). Mild cerebral atrophy with thinning of corpus callosum were also noted (Figure 1C,F). Given the clinical association of ichthyosis, brain white matter abnormalities and spastic paraparesis, Sjögren–Larsson syndrome was initially suspected; however, MR spectroscopy performed at that time was normal and did not support this diagnosis (Figure 1G). A follow‐up scan performed 3 years later at the age of 13 years confirmed the findings, highlighting only a slight increase in the supratentorial and infratentorial cortical spaces.

T2 brain MR images obtained at 2 (top row) and at 10 years of age (bottom row) illustrate the progression of white matter (WM) abnormalities. (A) At 2 years, mild T2‐hyperintensity is observed in the centra semiovalia WM.(D) At 10 years, the same regions show a more evident and extensive T2‐hyperintensity, with discrete myelination of the U‐fibres (red dotted line). A slight increase of subarachnoid spaces is detected(B, E) Magnified views of the basal ganglia reveal mild T2 hyperintensity of the internal capsule (white arrows) and of the optic radiations (blue arrows) more evident at 10 years.(C, F) The corpus callosum appears normal at 2 years while it is thinned at 10 years (dashed arrows). (G) Proton MR spectroscopy demonstrates the absence of the characteristic lipid peak typically observed in Sjogren–Larsson syndrome and appears normal. (H) Appearance of the skin abnormalities at 10 years of age and three‐years follow up (I), with the appearance of isolated small areas of clinically normal skin that gradually expanded over time, evolving into large, symmetric patches predominantly involving the chest and upper trunk.

At this time, the skin abnormalities had progressively evolved, with the appearance of isolated small areas of clinically normal skin that gradually expanded over time, evolving into large, symmetric patches predominantly involving the chest and upper trunk (Figure 1H,I). Next‐generation sequencing (NGS) analysis focusing on a panel of genes responsible for progressive symmetrical erythrokeratoderma, including GJB3, GJB6, KRT83, LOR, KDSR, TRPM4, ABCA12 and ELOVL1 genes, was performed and revealed a p.Ser165Phe (c.494C>T) heterozygous pathogenic variant in ELOVL1 gene. Segregation analysis showed that the variant was not detected in either parent, suggesting that it most likely arose de novo in the proband.

At last evaluation at 15 years of age, the patient attends the first year of high school, remains stable from a communicative perspective (Level I at CFCS), but experienced a slight worsening in gross motor functions, showing a Level II GMFCS and a total of 85.4% at GMFM‐88, with a worsened performance at Standing and Walking and Running and Jumping scales (A: 100%, B: 100%, C: 100%, D: 76.9%, E: 50%). The longitudinal comparison of cognitive functions, assessed through WISC‐IV, highlighted a moderate worsening in efficiency‐based cognitive functions, particularly processing speed (PSI = 76) and, to a lesser extent, working memory (WMI = 91), against a background of generally preserved abilities (Full‐Scale IQ = 95, VCI = 100, PRI = 111).

Heterozygous pathogenic variants in ELOVL1 (p.Ser165Phe) were described in literature for the first time in 2018 (Kutkowska‐Kaźmierczak et al. 2018; Mueller et al. 2019), in association with a neurological disorder named ichthyotic keratoderma, spasticity, hypomyelination, and dysmorphic features defined as IKSHD (OMIM #618527). The two reported patients (the same individuals described in both publications) exhibited a clinical and neuroradiological phenotype characterized by congenital pruritic ichthyosis and progressive spastic paraparesis beginning in infancy, followed by high‐frequency sensorineural hearing loss, nystagmus and mild dysarthria. Brain MRI showed mild T2 hyperintensity involving the periventricular, deep and central white matter, as well as the middle cerebellar peduncles and cerebellar white matter (Kutkowska‐Kaźmierczak et al. 2018; Mueller et al. 2019). A third patient has been recently described with a more severe phenotype, never being able to walk independently and experiencing significant motor regression from age 13 years. Brain MRI in this case (at 3 and 10 years of age) showed what is described as hypomyelinating leukodystrophy with small optic tracts, with no further specifications (Marcoux et al. 2025).

Our patient carries the same de novo heterozygous pathogenic variant in ELOVL1 found in the other cases. Of the total of four patients described, our patient shows the milder phenotype and maintained longer autonomous ambulation, whereas Patient 1 described by Kutkowska‐Kaźmierczak et al. lost independent ambulation before 12 years of age and Patient 2 had not yet acquired it at 4 years of age (Kutkowska‐Kaźmierczak et al. 2018). As in the other patients described, MRI scans of the brain performed in our patient showed a picture of mild supratentorial WM abnormalities, hyperintense on T2WI and isointense in T1WI, consistent with abnormal myelination, but quite different to what is observed in ‘classic’ hypomyelinating leukodystrophies. In fact, the WM abnormalities were not diffuse, with relative sparing of the subcortical U‐fibres, and were therefore not consistent with any stage of myelination. Moreover, our patient has the longest neuroradiological follow‐up that demonstrated a slow progression of the white matter abnormalities on MRI, with increasing extent over time. This pattern is not compatible with a hypomyelination and is instead more suggestive of a progressive loss of myelin.

In 2022, an autosomal recessive form of the disease has been described in two brothers from a consanguineous couple carrying the c.376‐2A>G homozygous pathogenic variant in ELOVL1 of parental inheritance (Takahashi et al. 2022). And, more recently, seven patients carrying autosomal recessive pathogenic variants in ELOVL1 have been reported to share a consistent neurocutaneous phenotype. The clinical presentation in these cases appears to be more severe compared with the dominant form, being characterized by congenital ichthyosis, severe developmental delay with no acquisition of autonomous ambulation and progressive spasticity, nystagmus, tremor often associated with myoclonus and dysarthria, with brain MRI reporting a clear pattern of classic hypomyelination with thinning of corpus callosum (Wong et al. 2025). It should be noted that a similar spectrum of presentations, ranging from hypomyelination to milder patterns of secondary WM abnormalities is commonly observed in leukoaxonopathies, including TUBB4A‐related leukodystrophy and POLR3‐related leukodystrophy (Blumkin et al. 2014; Curiel et al. 2017).

Although the pathophysiology of the disease remains unclear, a central role was initially attributed to the impaired synthesis of VLCFAs, which are essential substrates for myelin lipid production (Wong et al. 2025); however, a possible primary neuronal impairment has been suggested as a potentially complementary explanation, considering that VLCFAs synthesized by ELOVL1 are critical components of axonal sphingolipids, which are necessary for maintaining axonal integrity and for mediating axon‐derived signals that promote oligodendrocyte differentiation (Yang et al. 2025).

In conclusion, ELOVL1 dominant pathogenic variants need to be considered in the differential diagnosis of children presenting with a complicated spastic paraparesis associated with mild cerebellar signs, congenital ichthyosis and WM abnormalities, particularly when associated with nystagmus and high‐frequency sensorineural deafness. Our observations support the hypothesis that the primary abnormality may lie in neurons, and particularly in a disruption of axon–oligodendrocyte interactions (i.e., a leukoaxonopathy) leading to secondary myelination defects, rather than representing a primary oligodendrocyte pathology.
