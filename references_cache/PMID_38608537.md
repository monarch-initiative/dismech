# Structure-function of type I and III interferons.
**Authors:** de Weerd NA, Kurowska AK, Mendoza JL, Schreiber G
**Journal:** Curr Opin Immunol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.coi.2024.102413](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.coi.2024.102413)

## Content

Type I and type III interferons (IFNs) are major components in activating the innate immune response. Common to both are two distinct receptor chains (IFNAR1/IFNAR2 and IFNLR1/IL10R2), which form ternary complexes upon binding their respective ligands. This results in close proximity of the intracellularly associated kinases JAK1 and TYK2, which cross phosphorylate each other, the associated receptor chains, and signal transducer and activator of transcriptions, with the latter activating IFN-stimulated genes. While there are clear similarities in the biological responses toward type I and type III IFNs, differences have been found in their tropism, tuning of activity, and induction of the immune response. Here, we focus on how these differences are embedded in the structure/function relations of these two systems in light of the recent progress that provides in-depth information on the structural assembly of these receptors and their functional implications and how these differ between the mouse and human systems.

The interferons (IFNs) are a family of innate immune cytokines that are central in providing a rapid response to pathogens, modulating the immune system, providing cancer protection, and more [1]. They are classified into three distinct families based on their amino acid sequence and receptor usage [2]. Type I and type III IFNs are both comprised of multiple subtypes – in humans, type I IFNs include 12 IFNαs, IFNβ, IFNε, IFNκ and IFNω and type III IFNs include IFNλ1, IFNλ2, IFNλ3, and IFNλ4. A lone type II IFN has been identified, IFNγ [2]. In this review, we limit the discussion to type I and type III IFNs, which signal through the same components of the JAK/STAT pathway (JAK1 and TYK2 kinases activating STAT1 and STAT2). However, while type I IFNs activate all nucleated cells, due to the restricted expression of their receptor, the activity of type III IFNs is mostly restricted to epithelial cells lining mucosal sites [3] and some immune cells including neutrophils [4], B cells, and dendritic cells [5].

Type I and type III IFNs operate via distinct heterodimeric receptor complexes. While type I IFNs bind and signal through the IFNAR1 and IFNAR2 receptor chains, type III IFNs signal through IFNLR1 and IL10R2 receptor chains, where IL10R2 is also a shared receptor of IL-10, IL-22, and IL-26. In both cases, one chain is a high-affinity receptor chain (IFNAR2 and IFNLR1) and one is the low IFN affinity receptor chain (IFNAR1 and IL10R2). However, for type I IFNs the high and low-affinity receptors exhibit nM and μM affinity, while for type III IFNs the affinities are μM and tens of μM, respectively [6,7]. Functional consequences of the much weaker affinities of type III IFNs were discussed elsewhere [2]. Soluble forms of both IFNAR2 and IFNLR1 have been discovered. Soluble IFNAR2 is elevated in the disease [8] and can display intrinsic protective IFN-like activities in human cells [9]. A soluble form of IFNLR1 has also been identified [10–12] which antagonizes IFNλ3 signaling in human cells [13].

Since the type I and type III IFN receptors both dock TYK2 and JAK1 kinases, ligand engagement of the receptor complexes activate similar canonical JAK/STAT signaling pathways [14], although, as demonstrated using a selective inhibitor, the role of TYK2 is more pronounced for type I IFN activities than for those of the type III IFNs [15]. Non-canonical signaling pathways have also been identified for type I and type III IFNs including AKT, MAPK/ERK, p38, CrkL-RAP1, and JNK pathways [16,17]. There is also a recent report of the involvement of JAK2 in IFNλ1 signaling in hepatocytes [18].

The overall architecture and structural organization of the ternary IFN-receptor complexes of all three IFN families were recently reviewed [2]. We will build on that review delving into the molecular determinants of the ligand-receptor interfaces on the receptor extracellular domains (ECDs), the role of the transmembrane domains (TMD) in signaling and the organization of the intracellular domains (ICDs) of both the type I and type III IFN systems. We will also discuss the differences between type I and type III IFN systems across species focusing on the murine and human systems and the characteristics of IFN cross-reactivity across these species.

Type I and III IFNs differ in their chromosomal location and gene structures. All type I IFN genes are located on chromosome 9 and lack introns in the coding region, with only the IFNκ gene possessing an intron within the 3’-untranslated region. Genes encoding type III IFNs contain multiple exons whose number varies between subtypes, with IFNλ1 and IFNλ4 having five exons and IFNλ2 and IFNλ3 having six exons, the gene structure shared with IL-10 family cytokines. All type III IFN genes are clustered on chromosome 19 [1]. Both type I and type III IFNs are relatively small proteins, composed of a single polypeptide chain of approximately 187–208 and 179–200 amino acids, respectively, including a leader sequence of 19–24 amino acids cleaved upon secretion. The level of conservation is subtype-specific, with the sequence identity for the human proteins ranging from 35% to 95% for type I IFNs and 28% to 96% for type III IFNs [2].

Despite some divergence at the sequence level, the three-dimensional structures are highly conserved between sub-types with clear similarities between the families (Fig.1). Due to type III IFNs being evolutionarily linked to the IL-10 superfamily, there is a stronger structural conservation between IFNλ proteins and IL-22 and IL-10 rather than to the type I IFNs. Two main structural differences are found between type I and type III IFN signaling complexes. The first is the orientation by which the low-affinity subunits engage the ligands, with the receptors facing each other in the type I IFN complex, while they are angled in the type III IFN receptor complex. The second difference is that IFNAR1 has four fibronectin type 3 (FN3) domains, three of which engage the type I IFN ligands (Fig. 1) [7,19]. Beyond structure, the two families differ in the mechanism by which the complexes are formed. While the assembly and stability of the type I IFN receptor complex is solely controlled by IFN-IFNAR2 and IFN-IFNAR1 affinities, type III IFN receptor complex formation is cooperative, requiring IFN-IFNLR1 to be pre-formed before IL10R2 can engage. Since there is no measurable binding of IL10R2 directly to its ligands (type III IFNs, IL-10, or IL-22), cooperative binding is a conserved mechanism by which the IL-10 superfamily has evolved to enable ternary complex formation and pathway activation [7,20–22]. However, mathematical models demonstrating the effect of receptor concentrations on function have not been explored for type III IFN signaling, which may help to better understand this mechanism.

Even though both type I and type III IFNs utilize the same JAK-STAT pathway for signaling and induce a similar set of genes, type III IFNs display distinctly slower kinetics than type I IFNs in vitro, a feature not solely determined by lower receptor affinity. For both type I and type III IFNs, a transient, short-lived binding signal is sufficient to induce an antiviral state (termed robust activity), while the induction of antiproliferative and immunomodulatory activities (‘tunable’ activities) is a feature of type I IFNs, which require a more pronounced signal inducible using either higher IFN concentrations and/or a tighter binding variant (reviewed in [23]). An engineered high-affinity type III IFN ligand improves antiviral activity, but not to the level observed by type I IFNs suggesting there are other contributing factors [7]. Moreover, in vitro treatment of cell lines (specifically HepG2, Huh7.5, and WISH cells) with type III IFNs failed to induce an antiproliferative response, except when IFNLR1 was artificially over-expressed, suggesting that the level of endogenous IFNLR1 restricts signaling potency for type III IFNs in vitro [7]. In contrast, recent in vivo studies demonstrate a role for type III IFNs in the inhibition of epithelial proliferation during influenza infection [24] and inhibition of tissue repair in the lung [25] perhaps reflecting cell type specific potency for type III IFNs.

Observed differences in signal strength between type I and type III IFNs are likely attributable to the expression levels of their high-affinity receptors. There is a growing body of evidence that levels of the type I and type III IFN receptors vary substantially between cell types. In intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) where expression levels of type I and type III IFN receptors have been directly compared, expression of IFNAR1 and IFNAR2 mRNA were lower than those observed in lamina propria lymphocytes, and therefore IECs demonstrate lower reactivity to type I IFNs than lymphocytes [26]. That IECs were also shown to express high levels of IFNLR1 and IL10R2 mRNA explains the critical contribution of IFNλ to antiviral protection in the gut in vivo [26]. Indeed, the number of mapped reads for IFNLR1 mRNA has been shown to be greater than those observed for IFNAR2 mRNA in IECs, reinforcing the importance of type III IFN responses in the gut [27]. It is certain that the levels of expression of the type I and type III IFN receptors vary between cell types and tissues thus influencing the responsiveness of these cells and tissues to the IFNs.

Besides IFN-receptor affinities and receptor densities and distribution, other factors, such as intracellular receptor geometry [28], precise modifications to residues surrounding energetically critical residues [2,29], phosphorylation, and STAT recruitment and release, are equally crucial for efficient signaling.

Two recent studies have shed new light on understanding the roles of the receptor ICDs and associated signaling adaptors to the distinct kinetics displayed by the type I and type III IFNs. In one, the ICDs of type I and type III IFN receptors were fused to a heterodimeric EPO ligand-ECD system thereby mimicking the geometry and ligand binding for IFNAR1/IFNAR2 and IFNLR1/IL10R2. Regardless, signaling via IFNAR1/IFNAR2 ICDs was stronger and more transient than that displayed by IFNLR1/IL10R2 ICDs, with the latter showing weaker but more prolonged signaling outcomes [30]. This observation was shown to be due to differences in the ‘box motifs’ that bind JAK1 on IFNAR2-ICD versus IFNLR1-ICD, sufficient to enable differential signaling outcomes to be mediated by the type I versus type III IFNs [30]. In a second study, Wilder et al showed that type I and type III IFNs induce a common set of antiviral genes in epithelial cells [31]. In addition, higher doses of IFNβ activated an inflammatory gene expression program in contrast to IFNλ3 which elicited only the common antiviral gene program [31]. These observations suggest that IFNβ which induces stronger STAT2 and IRF9 expression, provides a positive feedback loop, resulting in a second phase of ISGF3 activation, a consequence of the strong initial IFNβ response. Contrary, IFNλ3 induces gene expression to a constant level over prolonged times, which is sufficient for an antiviral response, but not for the expression of inflammatory genes (robust versus tunable genes) [31,32]. Thus, the different kinetics of activation by type I and type III IFNs seem to play a major role in their different mode of gene activation resulting in a much stronger immunomodulatory activity for type I IFNs. This may also provide an explanation for the elaborate negative feedback loops identified for type I IFN signaling (see Fig. 2B) to which type III IFN signaling is unaffected, thereby enabling prolonged antiviral activity by the latter [33,34].

Like all cytokine receptors, type I and type III IFNs include a structural extracellular domain (ECD), a transmembrane domain (TMD), and an intracellular domain (ICD). The ECDs are composed of FN3 domains, the TMD is a helix spanning the membrane while the ICD is an intrinsically unstructured domain (IUD) (Fig. 2). While for some cytokine receptors, such as growth hormone receptor and EPOR, a pre-dimerization model of the two receptors was suggested, where the ligand binding induces a conformational shift that activates the receptor [35,36], this is not the case for type I and type III IFNs, where signaling is initiated through the formation of a ternary complex of IFN with its two trans-membrane receptor subunits. This model has been verified for both type I and type III IFNs through the use of non-native extracellular domains [37,38], engineering of the transmembrane domain [39], and following receptor assembly in the plasma membrane of living cells [40,41]. Ligand binding brings the two receptors near, driving cross-phosphorylation of their associated JAK kinases, TYK2 bound to IFNAR1 and IL10R2 and JAK1 bound to IFNAR2 and IFNLR1. Interestingly, interdomain dynamics in IFNAR1-ECD have been linked to signaling through the ICD, but this is through the regulation of IFN binding affinity that dictates biological activity [42].

Following ternary complex formation, the activated kinases bound to the ICDs of the receptors phosphorylate specific tyrosine residues on the receptors, which were reported to serve as docking sites for signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) proteins that are also phosphorylated by the JAKs (Fig. 2 and reviewed in [17]). Both families activate multiple STAT family members, but STAT1 and STAT2 are the primary STATs driving IFN responses. Upon phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT2, they form a heterotrimer together with IRF9 and are imported to the nucleus to drive gene transcription. The ICDs of the low-affinity receptors are ~82 and 100 amino acids long in human IL10R2 and IFNAR1 respectively, binding the TYK2 kinase in an area containing the Box1 and Box2 motifs [43] (Fig. 2A). IFNAR1-ICD contains four tyrosine residues that are possible targets for phosphorylation. Two non-conserved tyrosine residues in the TYK2 binding region (466 and 481) and two conserved (527 and 538) (reviewed in [44]). Their role in STAT recruitment and activation is under debate (reviewed in [17,45]), with two recent studies showing no change in type I IFN function upon their mutations to Phe [38,46]. Deletion of 50 amino acids from the C-terminus of IFNAR1, right up to the TYK2 binding site [43], did not affect the level of STAT phosphorylation and gene expression [46]. However, the C-terminus of IFNAR1 is important in regulating its ubiquitin-dependent endocytosis [47], which protects tissue from inflammatory injury. Further deletion into the TYK2 binding site of IFNAR1 abrogated activity completely [46], however, it has been shown for both type I and type III IFNs that JAK/STAT signaling can also proceed in the absence of TYK2, with the activity of type I IFNs being more affected by the absence of TYK2 than type III IFN [15,38,48].

Most of the signaling activity of type I and type III IFNs is activated through the high-affinity receptor chains IFNAR2 and IFNLR1, respectively. The ICDs of human IFNAR2 and IFNLR1 are 250 and 270 amino acids in length, respectively, and contain multiple tyrosine residues that are phosphorylated by the JAKs and play roles in effector recruitment. The binding of JAK1 is essential for IFNAR2 activity, to a degree where a dimer of IFNAR2 was shown to activate STATs, drive gene transcription (albeit to a much lower degree than JAK1/TYK2), and inhibit cell proliferation [38]. The first high-resolution cryo-EM structure of a dimer of the ICD Box1 and Box2 regions of two IFNLR1 receptors bound to JAK1 captured as an activated homodimer was recently published [49] (Fig. 2A). The dimerization of JAK1 is mediated by the pseudo-kinase (PK) domains from the monomeric subunits. The C-terminal tyrosine kinase (TK) domains that phosphorylate the receptor-bound STAT proteins are projecting from the FERM–SH2 domains. The structure, combined with functional information [50,51] supports an allosteric activation mechanism. The structure suggests an autoinhibitory role for the PK domain, with the PK-TK domains forming head-to-head dimers through interactions between kinase N-terminal lobes, which sterically occlude substrate binding and catalytic activity. The TYK2 inhibitor BMS-986165 that blocks the receptor-mediated activation of the kinase targeting PK domain rather than the active site of the catalytic domain provides further conformation for the allosteric mechanism [52]. Similar contributions of Box1 and Box2 were reported for JAK1 association and activation on IFNAR2 [53]. Despite the similarities of the Box domains on IFNAR2 and IFNLR1, a recent paper has shown that the Box domain on IFNAR2 results in more robust activation of JAK1, leading to stronger STAT activation and higher gene induction than that observed by type III IFNs signaling via IFNLR1 [30].

A matter of debate is the role of the tyrosine residues on IFNAR2-ICD on STAT binding and activation. HuIFNAR2-ICD contains seven tyrosine residues (269, 306, 316, 318, 337, 411, 512), five of which are conserved in muIFNAR2-ICD [53,54]. Single mutations of each of the seven tyrosine residues on HuIFNAR2 did not result in any change in activity [46,54]. Conversely, in muIFNAR2, a single mutation in Y510 (the equivalent to human Y512) reduced STAT phosphorylation and biological activity [53]. Surprisingly, mutating all seven tyrosine residues on IFNAR2 simultaneously did not completely abrogate type I IFN-induced phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT2, but did so for STAT3 [46], which is in contrast to work on human U5A cells [54]. STAT2 was previously reported to bind IFNAR2 constitutively, independent of type I IFN activation [55]. Indeed, mutating tyrosine residues on IFNAR2 did not affect the level of STAT1 or STAT2 binding to IFNAR2, while levels of phosphorylation of pSTAT1 and pSTAT2 were significantly decreased [46]. It was suggested that the constitutive binding site of STAT2 is needed for regulation and not for signaling [56]. This is supported by USP18 binding to STAT2, which abrogates JAK1 binding to IFNAR2 [57]. As both STAT2 and USP18 are IFN-induced genes (Fig. 2B), increased levels of both would decrease levels of JAK-mediated STAT phosphorylation over time, as indeed observed [57]. Interestingly, perhaps by virtue of its effect on JAK1 binding to IFNAR2, USP18 preferentially inhibits IFNα-mediated signaling with limited effects on signaling by the IFNλs (see Fig. 2B) [33]. The preference of STAT2 for type I and type III IFN-mediated pathways [58] makes it a candidate for a specific regulation only of these cytokines. If the phospho-tyrosine residues on IFNAR2 are not recruiting STATs, what is their role? Recent simulations suggested that phosphorylation of the tyrosine residues on IFNAR2-ICD contributes to the rapid pSTAT dissociation required to obtain high pSTAT accumulation [46]. This suggests that tyrosine phosphorylation of IFNAR2-ICD weakens its interaction with STATs, rather than strengthening it.

Unlike IFNAR2-ICD, HuIFNLR1-ICD contains only three tyrosine residues (343, 406 and 517). While the roles of these residues on IFNLR1 function have not been systematically investigated, Y343 and Y517 are important in IFNλ1-mediated STAT2 activation contributing to its antiviral and antiproliferative activity in BW5147 T lymphoma cells [10]. While single tyrosine to phenylalanine mutations had only minor effects on IFNλ1 activity, the double phenylalanine showed a dramatic reduction in IFN activity [10]. Interestingly, IFNλ1-mediated activation of STAT4 (and STAT3) was not dependent on these tyrosine residues [10].

The STAT binding regions on IFNAR2 are not yet fully resolved. As opposed to the 'all or none' phosphorylation pattern observed for IFNAR1, deleting increasing segments from IFNAR2 resulted in a gradual decrease in STAT binding and phosphorylation [46]. The decreased STAT phosphorylation is independent of JAK1 phosphorylation, which was not affected by the deletions [46]. These findings support a model of multiple regions of IFNAR2-ICD supporting STAT binding, which suggests that the ICD is making multiple, not sequential contacts to STAT proteins [46] (Fig. 2B).

Although the overall architecture, receptor organization, and functionality of the type I and type III IFN systems on the cell surface are conserved between humans and mice, there are clear fundamental differences between the two systems, the most noticeable of which is the number of IFN subtypes (Table 1). While both humans and mice have multiple IFNαs, and lone IFNβ, IFNε, and IFNκ, mice lack IFNω but express a distinct IFNζ that is absent in humans. For type III IFNs, there are four IFNλ proteins (IFNλ1–4) in humans but only two in mice (IFNλ2–3) [11,59]. IFNλ2 and IFNλ3 share 96% sequence identity in both species, human (Hu)IFNλ1 displays ~75% sequence identity, a distinct pattern of disulfide bond formation [60], and differs functionally from HuIFNλ2/3 [61,62]. HuIFNλ4 is the most divergent with only ~40% sequence identity to HuIFNλ2/3 [63]. Interestingly, GWAS studies suggest that the fully functional IFNλ4 protein is only expressed in people of a certain haplotype with expression linked to poorer spontaneous and IFN-treatment induced clearance of hepatitis C virus infection [64]. Since mice do not express IFNλ1 or IFNλ4 there is difficulty in delineating IFNλ1/IFNλ4 activities from IFNλ2/IFNλ3 activities using murine models of disease that are especially needed to understand the role and biological questions surrounding IFNλ1 and IFNλ4.

While human and murine IFNβ share 47% sequence similarity and their folds are nearly identical, they differ in the preferential affinities they display for IFNAR1 in their respective systems (Table 1). While HuIFNβ conforms to the paradigm, using IFNAR2 as the high-affinity receptor (displaying affinities of ~0.1 nM and 50 nM to IFNAR2 and IFNAR1, respectively) [65], murine (mu)IFNβ displays a higher affinity for IFNAR1 than IFNAR2 (12.7 nM vs ~1600 nM, respectively) [66] and indeed has been shown to signal independently of IFNAR2 in mice, albeit activating a different set of genes [67]. The extracellular regions of human and murine IFNAR1 and IFNAR2 share 49–51% sequence identity, with the divergence reaching the cytokine binding regions. Indeed, the structures of human and murine IFNAR1-ECD subdomains D1-D3 are almost identical making the differential receptor bias displayed by IFNβ for IFNAR1 between species intriguing. In line with the human proteins, murine IFNαs bind IFNAR2 as the high-affinity receptor and IFNAR1 as the low-affinity receptor [65].

The most intensely studied human IFN subtypes, IFNα2, IFNβ, and IFNω, are not cross-reactive on murine cells. Conversely, murine IFNε is highly active in human cells [66]. While the type I IFNs themselves, considering the exception above, lack cross-reactivity, all known signaling components and accessory proteins are conserved between species. It is on this basis and with the desire to cross the species barrier, transgenic mice expressing HuIFNAR1-ECD and HuIFNAR2-ECD fused to the transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains of the murine receptors were developed [68]. These mice highlighted significant differences in the duration of HuIFNβ-mediated signaling outcomes between species, revealing intrinsic downstream differences in the human and murine IFN systems that are independent of ligand engagement of the cell surface receptors [68]. In comparison to the type I IFNs, specific type III IFNs have been demonstrated to be cross-reactive – murine IFNλ2 is active on a human hepatocyte cell line [18] while HuIFNλ1, HuIFNλ2 and to a lesser extent HuIFNλ3, are active on murine epithelial cells [69] (Table 1). The breadth of cross-reactivity for all type I and type III IFNs on a range of cell types has not yet been thoroughly explored and contributes to challenges in studying human IFNs in mouse models.

Type I IFNs were amongst the first biological drugs used against a variety of diseases, including viral diseases (hepatitis C), cancers (melanoma, non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma), and autoimmune diseases (multiple sclerosis). In recent years type I IFNs have been replaced with more specific drugs against various maladies (for review see [70]). Type III IFNs were explored during the last 15 years for their potential as a drug, particularly as side effects associated with type I IFNs should be milder [1]. For example, a clinical study of early treatment with PEGylated type III IFN for COVID-19 showed a significant reduction in hospitalization or emergency department visits after receiving a single dose compared to those who received a placebo [71]. Randomized studies of PEGylated type I and III IFNs in patients with chronic hepatitis C showed variable results. A phase IIb study where PEG-IFNλ1a or PEG-IFNα2 were administered once weekly with ribavirin showed improved efficacy after 4 and 12 weeks of PEG-IFNλ1a treatment, but by 24 weeks the IFNs demonstrated similar efficacy. While serious adverse effects were similar, PEG-IFNλ1a treatment was associated with fewer extrahepatic adverse events [72]. Contrary, a phase III study of PEG-IFNλ1a or PEG-IFNα2 administered together with ribavirin and telaprevir showed increased side effects with no treatment advantage for PEG-IFNλ [73]. In addition, the potential benefits of type III IFNs in several cancers are being explored in pre-clinical situations [74]. While type III IFNs have no FDA-approved uses, further exploration of their potential is granted.

Insights into the mechanism of action of type I and type III IFNs have been gained by determination of the structures of the ECDs of the ternary signaling complexes, alongside studies focused on understanding the contributions that the TMDs and ICDs make to receptor functionality, JAK/STAT activation, and IFN signaling outcomes. The affinity the ligands have for their receptor ECDs is not the sole determinant in dictating the potency of IFN-mediated signaling events. Advancements in understanding IFN receptor distribution and expression levels, how the receptor TMDs and ICDs orchestrate receptor-associated JAKs, docking STAT molecules, and receptor regulatory processes provide insight into how each step and structure are determinants of IFN signaling. While the architecture and receptor organization of the IFN systems across species are well conserved, it is important to appreciate the differences to understand the applicability of murine models of disease for human afflictions. Maybe the most intriguing question here is whether type III IFNs could be used as a drug, with lower side effects in comparison to type I IFNs. The structure/function aspects described here provide the background, on which this question can systematically be investigated.
