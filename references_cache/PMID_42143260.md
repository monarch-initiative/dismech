Fetal choroid plexus cysts (CPCs) are common incidental findings during mid-trimester ultrasound. Although typically benign, their detection often causes parental concern because of potential associations with chromosomal abnormalities, particularly trisomy 18. This systematic review summarizes current evidence on the natural history, associated anomalies, and prognostic implications of CPCs to guide prenatal counseling.

We systematically searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library for original studies published between 2010 and 2025. Eligible studies included fetuses with CPCs diagnosed by ultrasound and reported data on anomalies, genetic testing, or pregnancy outcomes. Case reports and reviews were excluded. Two reviewers independently screened and extracted data. Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale and Joanna Briggs Institute checklists. Findings were synthesized qualitatively due to heterogeneity.

Ten studies met inclusion criteria, comprising over 10,600 screened fetuses, with several hundred CPC cases. Prevalence ranged from 0.5% to 2.5%, most detected between 16 and 22 weeks. Isolated CPCs, particularly in pregnancies with normal biochemical screening or negative non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), were not significantly associated with chromosomal abnormalities. By contrast, CPCs with additional sonographic findings showed a markedly increased risk of trisomy 18 and clinically significant copy number variations. Most isolated CPCs resolved spontaneously and were followed by favorable neonatal outcomes.

Isolated CPCs in the context of low-risk screening should be regarded as benign variants with excellent prognosis, and invasive testing is unnecessary. Conversely, CPCs with additional risk factors require further evaluation, including chromosomal microarray, supporting a risk-adapted approach.

Choroid plexus cysts (CPCs) are fluid-filled structures within the choroid plexus of the lateral ventricles, most frequently identified during routine second-trimester ultrasound examinations. They are reported in approximately 1–2% of second-trimester fetuses and are generally regarded as benign and transient findings, particularly when not accompanied by other abnormalities [1,2]. Nevertheless, CPCs have attracted considerable clinical attention because of their historical association with chromosomal abnormalities, especially trisomy 18, which can generate uncertainty in prenatal counseling [3,4].

The clinical implications of CPCs depend largely on their context. Isolated CPCs, detected in pregnancies with normal biochemical screening or negative non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), are increasingly recognized as normal developmental variants with favorable outcomes [1,5,6]. By contrast, CPCs occurring alongside additional soft markers or structural malformations may indicate a substantially increased risk of aneuploidy, particularly Edwards syndrome [5,7,8]. These cases usually warrant further evaluation, including detailed anomaly scans, amniocentesis, or chromosomal microarray analysis [9].

Over the past decade, advances in high-resolution imaging and genetic testing have shifted management toward a more conservative, risk-adapted approach [7,10,11]. Several large cohort studies have demonstrated spontaneous resolution of isolated CPCs without adverse neurodevelopmental sequelae [1,6,12]. However, practice variation persists, and consensus is lacking regarding the necessity of invasive testing for isolated CPCs [7,9,10,11]. Moreover, smaller case series continue to report associations with pathogenic copy number variations or central nervous system anomalies, raising questions about residual risk [13,14,15].

Despite reassuring findings, long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes have not been systematically evaluated, and uncertainty remains regarding the prognostic significance of CPCs in complex or borderline cases [3,12]. This review synthesizes contemporary evidence to clarify when CPCs can be considered benign variants (typically when isolated and screening is low-risk) and when additional evaluation is warranted (non-isolated CPCs or abnormal screening), with explicit attention to cyst definition and size. In this review, a choroid plexus cyst (CPC) was defined as a well-circumscribed anechoic lesion within the echogenic choroid plexus of the lateral ventricles, typically round/oval, ≥ 2 mm in diameter, without internal vascular flow on color Doppler, and clearly separable from the ependymal wall. Lesions ≥ 5 mm were labeled “large” for descriptive analyses. This harmonizes with definitions used in large series [1,2] and contemporary practice.

This systematic review was designed and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement [16]. The study protocol was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO.

We included original studies reporting fetuses with CPCs diagnosed by prenatal ultrasound as per the operational definition above. We prespecified two analytic strata: (i) isolated CPC (no additional soft markers or structural anomalies), and (ii) non-isolated CPC (≥ 1 soft marker and/or structural anomaly). Studies had to report at least one of: associated findings, genetic testing (karyotype/CMA), or pregnancy/neonatal outcomes. We extracted cyst diameter; where available we recorded the proportion with ≥ 5 mm (“large”) and analyzed whether size independently modified risk. Both retrospective and prospective designs (cohort, case–control, and cross-sectional studies, as well as case series with ≥ 10 fetuses) were considered.

We excluded case reports with fewer than 10 fetuses, narrative or systematic reviews, editorials, conference abstracts without full data, and studies focusing on central nervous system anomalies unrelated to CPCs.

A comprehensive search of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library was conducted from January 2010 to June 2025. Search terms combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text words related to CPCs, prenatal diagnosis, ultrasound, prognosis, and outcomes. The PubMed strategy was as follows:

("choroid plexus cyst" OR CPC) AND

("prenatal diagnosis" OR "fetal ultrasound" OR "antenatal ultrasound") AND

("pregnancy" OR fetus) AND

("outcome" OR prognosis OR "natural history")

Equivalent search syntax was adapted for other databases. No restrictions were placed on language or publication status.

All identified records were imported into EndNote (Clarivate Analytics) and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers (HM, SS) independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review of potentially relevant studies. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, with arbitration by a third reviewer (SK) if required. The selection process was summarized in a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig.1).

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized form. Extracted variables included study design, sample size, population characteristics, gestational age at diagnosis, CPC features (size, laterality, number), associated anomalies, genetic testing results, and pregnancy/neonatal outcomes. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus. Information on ultrasound protocol and imaging parameters was also collected where available.

Methodological quality was appraised independently by two reviewers. Cohort and case–control studies were evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [17]. Case series were assessed with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist [18]. Studies rated as low quality were excluded from the final synthesis.

Due to substantial heterogeneity, definitions, and reported outcomes, a quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible. Findings were therefore synthesized narratively and stratified according to: (i) isolated versus non-isolated CPCs, (ii) presence of additional anomalies or soft markers, and (iii) type of genetic testing performed.

When reported, we extracted gestational age windows (target 18–22 weeks), probe type/frequency, planes (axial atrial; coronal transventricular), and whether color Doppler or fetal neurosonography was used to confirm intra-choroidal location. We documented whether authors distinguished CPCs from ventricular synechiae/adhesions and whether follow-up scans were performed.

As this study was based exclusively on published literature, no ethical approval was required.

The database search identified 60 records. After removal of 28 duplicates, 32 titles and abstracts were screened. Ten records were excluded, leaving 22 full-text reports sought for retrieval. Eleven reports could not be retrieved, and the remaining 11 were assessed for eligibility. Of these, one study was excluded because it was not published in English. Finally, 10 studies fulfilled the eligibility criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis (Fig.1).

Across the 10 included studies, a total of ≈ 10,600 fetuses underwent mid-trimester anomaly scans, among whom ≥ 230 CPC cases were identified (exact counts vary due to incomplete reporting in some cohorts). Study-level details are provided in Table1.

Sample sizes varied widely, ranging from small single-center series (n= 29) to a large cross-sectional study (n= 6,240). Study designs included prospective and retrospective cohorts, cross-sectional studies, and one meta-analysis. The investigations were conducted across Asia, Europe, North America, and Australia. Isolated CPC. In cohorts reporting stratification, isolated CPCs showed spontaneous resolution in the vast majority and no clinically meaningful increase in aneuploidy when first-trimester screening/NIPT was low-risk [2,6].

CPC with soft markers. When CPC co-occurred with soft markers (e.g., increased nuchal fold, short long bones), risk of aneuploidy—particularly trisomy 18—increased, but remained context-dependent and driven by the overall marker pattern [1,7,11].

CPC with structural anomalies. Where cardiac/CNS/limb anomalies or FGR were present, the pretest probability of aneuploidy and yield of CMA were substantially higher [10,13].

Effect of cyst size. Where size was reported [8,10,13], “large” CPCs (≥ 5 mm) were not independently associated with aneuploidy when isolated and screening was low-risk. Larger cysts were more likely to be noted and re-scanned, but size alone did not justify invasive testing in the absence of additional risk factors [1,5,7,11,19].

The reported prevalence of CPCs in unselected populations ranged from 0.5% to 2.5%, with the highest detection rates between 16 and 22 weeks of gestation [1,2,6,9]. Bilateral cysts were more frequent than unilateral ones, accounting for more than 60% of cases [2,5,6]. Most cysts were small (< 10 mm), anechoic, and resolved spontaneously by the third trimester [6,7,9,11].

Isolated CPCs were not significantly associated with aneuploidy in pregnancies with low-risk first-trimester screening or negative non-invasive prenatal testing [6,9,10,19]. In a cohort of 591 fetuses with soft markers, Lu et al. reported a prevalence of clinically significant chromosomal abnormalities below 1.5% in isolated CPCs [1]. Conversely, CPCs accompanied by additional sonographic markers such as increased nuchal translucency, echogenic intracardiac focus, or short femur length were strongly associated with trisomy 18 [7,8,11,13,20]. In confirmed trisomy 18 cohorts, CPCs were present in 40–50% of cases but always in conjunction with multiple structural anomalies such as clenched fists, congenital heart defects, or intrauterine growth restriction [7,13,19].

Across all studies, over 90% of fetuses with isolated CPCs demonstrated spontaneous resolution and favorable neonatal outcomes, with no neurodevelopmental impairment reported on follow-up [3,6,9,19]. Several authors emphasized that isolated CPCs should be considered benign sonographic variants and not an indication for invasive testing when other screening results are reassuring [7,10,11,12].

Chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) detected pathogenic copy number variations in up to 4.3% of fetuses with CPCs and additional anomalies, particularly those with short long bones or abnormal nuchal translucency [8,20]. No pathogenic CNVs were reported in isolated CPCs. Medical exome sequencing (MES) was performed selectively in complex malformations, but CPCs were never the sole abnormality in such cases [13].

Two small series [5,7] (showed benign natural history of AHE when isolated, with ~ 90–100% resolution and normal neonatal outcomes. AHE did not modify risk in the absence of other findings.

Imaging and pathological correlation.

Fetal MRI studies confirmed the incidental nature of CPCs in high-risk pregnancies, including those affected by CMV infection, without evidence of adverse neurological outcomes [12]. Postmortem autopsy data showed > 95% concordance between prenatal ultrasound findings and histopathology, supporting the benign nature of isolated CPCs [9,19].

This systematic review synthesizes contemporary evidence on fetal choroid plexus cysts (CPCs) and provides three overarching messages. First, CPCs are common, transient sonographic findings in the mid-trimester, with a population prevalence of approximately 0.5–2.5% and a strong tendency toward spontaneous resolution [1,2,6,9]. Second, when CPCs occur in isolation—particularly in pregnancies with reassuring first-trimester screening or negative non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT)—they are not meaningfully associated with aneuploidy and are followed by excellent neonatal outcomes [3,6,9,10,19]. Third, the risk profile changes materially when CPCs coexist with additional soft markers or structural malformations; in that context, the probability of trisomy 18 rises and targeted diagnostic evaluation becomes appropriate [4,7,21].

Invasive testing (amniocentesis with CMA) is reasonable when CPCs are non-isolated (additional soft markers or structural anomalies) or when prior screening is high-risk. For isolated CPCs with low-risk serum screening/NIPT, evidence and guidelines [10,11] support reassurance without invasive testing, irrespective of cyst size.

Historically, CPCs were viewed with caution because early reports—largely predating contemporary screening paradigms—linked them to Edwards syndrome [4,21]. Subsequent meta-analytic and cohort evidence refined this perception, showing that the apparent association was largely confounded by coexisting anomalies and by ascertainment in high-risk populations [4,7,9]. In the current screening era, the incremental risk conferred by an isolated CPC after low-risk biochemical screening or a negative NIPT is very small, often indistinguishable from background risk [3,6,9,10,20]. This shift has important counseling implications: for most patients with isolated CPCs and otherwise reassuring assessments, additional invasive testing is unnecessary and reassurance is an evidence-based course.

The benign clinical course of isolated CPCs is supported by their natural history and by follow-up studies that report normal neurodevelopment in infancy and childhood [3,6,9,19]. Resolution typically occurs before the third trimester, and postnatal outcomes are uniformly favorable in the absence of additional anomalies [6,9,19]. Concerns about subtle neurocognitive sequelae have not been substantiated by prospective developmental assessments [3]. Likewise, the “anterior horn extension” pattern—once considered atypical—has demonstrated no adverse prognostic significance when isolated [5,7].

By contrast, non-isolated CPCs convey a different risk signal. When CPCs are accompanied by increased nuchal translucency, echogenic intracardiac focus, short long bones, cardiac malformations, growth restriction, or other structural anomalies, the likelihood of trisomy 18 and clinically significant genetic abnormalities increases substantially [4,7,11,21]. In cohorts with confirmed trisomy 18, CPCs are frequently observed but almost always in conjunction with multiple anomalies rather than as solitary findings [4,7,13,19]. In such settings, a structured diagnostic pathway—including detailed anatomic survey, genetic counseling, and consideration of amniocentesis with chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA)—is warranted [8,13,20,22].

Advanced genetic testing should be individualized. CMA provides higher diagnostic yield than karyotyping in fetuses with structural abnormalities and can detect pathogenic copy-number variants that are invisible to conventional cytogenetics [8]. In series that applied CMA to fetuses with CPCs and additional sonographic abnormalities, pathogenic variants were identified in a minority (on the order of a few percent), particularly when long-bone shortening or increased nuchal translucency were present [8,20]. However, the incremental yield of CMA appears limited in isolated CPCs with otherwise low-risk screening, and routine use in that subgroup is not supported by the available evidence [6,9,10,20,22]. Medical exome sequencing has been used selectively in complex malformation patterns; importantly, CPCs have not been the sole abnormality in such cohorts [13].

Imaging and pathological correlations further support the benign interpretation of isolated CPCs. Fetal MRI—often employed in high-risk scenarios such as suspected congenital cytomegalovirus infection—has generally confirmed CPCs as incidental, non-specific findings without evidence of neural injury [12]. Autopsy series demonstrate high concordance between prenatal ultrasound identification of CPCs and histopathological confirmation, underscoring that CPCs reflect transient anatomic variants rather than destructive processes [9,19].

From a clinical operations perspective, practice variability persists, and parental anxiety remains high despite reassuring data. A clear, risk-adapted counseling script can reduce unnecessary invasive procedures while preserving diagnostic vigilance when co-markers are present. Based on the synthesized evidence and contemporary guideline principles, a pragmatic approach is: (i) Isolated CPC + low-risk screening → reassurance and no further testing; (ii) CPC + abnormal screening or multiple soft markers → offer diagnostic testing with CMA; (iii) CPC + structural anomalies → comprehensive neurosonography, consideration of fetal MRI, genetic counseling, and CMA; and (iv) persistent CPC into the third trimester without co-findings → usually benign; follow-up ultrasound can be considered for reassurance [5,6,7,8,9,10,12,13,20,22].

Strength of evidence and limitations. The overall direction of evidence across different geographies and study designs was consistent; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. Most included studies were retrospective and single-center, with heterogeneous and sometimes inconsistent definitions of “isolated” CPCs, introducing the possibility of selection and classification bias. CPC size thresholds and criteria for isolation were variably applied, and imaging protocols were not standardized across studies. Reporting of long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes was limited, restricting conclusions about later prognosis. Substantial heterogeneity in imaging techniques, diagnostic thresholds, and genetic testing strategies precluded quantitative meta-analysis and may have obscured subtle subgroup effects. In addition, part of the background literature informing clinical practice was derived from the pre-NIPT era, which may overestimate the historical association between CPCs and aneuploidy compared with contemporary screening contexts. These factors may bias effect estimates and limit the generalizability of findings.

Implications and future directions. For most pregnancies, isolated CPCs should be framed as a benign sonographic variant with an excellent prognosis, avoiding invasive testing when screening is otherwise low-risk [3,6,9,10,19,20,22]. Future work should prioritize prospective, multicenter cohorts with standardized ultrasonography, prespecified definitions of “isolated” versus “non-isolated,” uniform genetic testing algorithms, and longitudinal neurodevelopmental follow-up. Development and external validation of multivariable risk tools that integrate maternal age, screening results, CPC characteristics (size, bilaterality, anterior extension), and co-markers would help harmonize counseling. Finally, research on patient-centered outcomes (decisional conflict, anxiety, and satisfaction) and on the cost-effectiveness of divergent testing strategies would inform guideline updates [23].

In sum, when CPCs are identified in isolation and in the context of low-risk screening, the evidence supports conservative management and reassurance. The presence of additional sonographic or biochemical risk factors should prompt targeted diagnostic evaluation using contemporary genomic technologies, with counseling anchored in the overall phenotype rather than the cyst itself [3,6,8,9,10,13,22]. This systematic review demonstrates that fetal choroid plexus cysts (CPCs) are typically benign and transient sonographic findings. When detected in isolation and in the context of low-risk biochemical or genomic screening, CPCs are not associated with a clinically meaningful increase in aneuploidy and are followed by favorable neonatal outcomes. In contrast, CPCs accompanied by additional soft markers or structural anomalies carry a substantially higher risk of trisomy 18 and other chromosomal abnormalities, warranting detailed imaging and targeted genetic evaluation.

From a clinical perspective, risk-adapted counseling is essential. Isolated CPCs should generally be managed conservatively with reassurance, whereas non-isolated CPCs require further diagnostic assessment, including chromosomal microarray analysis when appropriate. Future multicenter prospective studies with standardized imaging and genetic protocols are needed to refine risk stratification and optimize counseling strategies.
