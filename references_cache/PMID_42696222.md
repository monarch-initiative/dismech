# Cold agglutinin disease: pathophysiology, diagnosis, and the evolving therapeutic landscape.
**Authors:** Choi DH
**Journal:** Blood Res (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s44313-026-00169-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44313-026-00169-8)

## Content

Cold agglutinin disease (CAD) is a rare form of autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) that accounts for approximately 15%-25% of AIHA cases and is characterized by two interdependent features: a clonal bone marrow B-cell lymphoproliferative disorder producing monoclonal immunoglobulin M (IgM)-κ autoantibodies, and complement-mediated hemolysis driven by the classical pathway. Cold agglutinins bind to erythrocytes at low temperatures and trigger C1-initiated complement activation, predominantly leading to C3b-mediated extravascular hemolysis in the liver, with intravascular hemolysis occurring chiefly during acute exacerbations. CAD must be distinguished from cold agglutinin syndrome (CAS), which occurs secondary to infection, malignant lymphoma, or other disorders, and is managed by treating the underlying cause. The 2022 World Health Organization classification recognized CAD-associated lymphoproliferative diseases as distinct entities. Beyond anemia, patients experience disabling cold-induced circulatory symptoms, profound fatigue, and elevated thromboembolic risk. Diagnosis rests on evidence of hemolysis, a monospecific direct antiglobulin test result positive for C3d but negative for immunoglobulin G (IgG), a cold agglutinin titer ≥ 1:64, and bone marrow evaluation; thermal amplitude can predict clinical severity better than titer alone. Corticosteroids and splenectomy are generally ineffective and not recommended. Contemporary management combines B-cell-directed therapy with complement inhibition, the latter being transformed by the anti-C1s antibody, sutimlimab. This review summarizes the pathophysiology, diagnostic approaches, and rapidly evolving treatment landscapes for CAD, including emerging complement- and clone-directed agents.

Autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) consists of a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by autoantibody-mediated red blood cell destruction. Among these, cold agglutinin disease (CAD) occupies a distinctive position, both mechanistically and clinically. Unlike warm AIHA, which is driven by immunoglobulin G (IgG)-mediated, Fc receptor-dependent splenic clearance, CAD is defined by the convergence of two processes: a low-grade clonal B-cell lymphoproliferative disorder (B-LPD) of the bone marrow that secretes a monoclonal immunoglobulin M (IgM) cold agglutinin, and antibody-triggered activation of the classical complement pathway that mediates hemolysis [1].

This dual nature has important consequences. First, it explains why therapies effective in warm AIHA, such as corticosteroids and splenectomy, are largely ineffective in CAD, where hemolysis is complement-driven and predominantly hepatic rather than Fc-mediated and splenic. Second, it frames the two complementary therapeutic strategies that define modern management: targeting pathogenic B-cell clones and inhibiting complement activation.

A critical distinction is that between CAD, a primary clonal disease requiring directed therapy, and cold agglutinin syndrome (CAS), in which cold agglutinins arise secondary to infection (e.g.,Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Epstein–Barr virus [EBV]), B-cell lymphoma, or other conditions; in infection-associated CAS, the cold agglutinins are typically polyclonal and transient, resolving with the underlying condition, whereas lymphoma-associated CAS is frequently characterized by monoclonal cold agglutinins produced by an underlying clonal B-cell disorder [2]. The 2022 World Health Organization classification formally recognized CAD-associated lymphoproliferative disease as a distinct entity from lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL) and Waldenström macroglobulinemia (WM); the molecular and histopathological basis for this distinction is detailed below [3].

Although CAD is rare, its clinical burden is substantial and historically underestimated, encompassing transfusion-dependent anemia, debilitating cold-induced circulatory symptoms, marked fatigue [4], and a heightened risk of thrombosis [5]. The therapeutic landscape has been transformed by the introduction of complement inhibition—most notably the anti-C1s monoclonal antibody sutimlimab—alongside established B-cell–directed regimens, although access to these agents remains uneven across regions. This review outlines the epidemiology, pathophysiology, diagnostic approaches, and current and emerging treatments for CAD, with an emphasis on practical clinical management.

CAD is a rare disorder, with an estimated prevalence of 5–20 per million population and an annual incidence of approximately 0.5–1.9 per million [5,6]. It accounts for roughly 15%–25% of all cases of AIHA [7]. The disease predominantly affects older adults, with a median age at diagnosis of approximately 67–72 years, and shows a female predominance. Despite its chronicity, overall survival is relatively preserved; a large multinational study estimated a median survival of approximately 16 years from diagnosis—close to the ~18.4-year life expectancy of the age-matched general population; thus, the disease only slightly increased mortality, so morbidity rather than mortality dominates the clinical picture [5,8].

Nevertheless, the morbidity rate is considerable and has long been underestimated. Cold-induced circulatory symptoms—acrocyanosis of the fingers, toes, ears, and nose, Raynaud-like phenomena, and livedo reticularis—are reported by the great majority of patients (approximately 90%) and can substantially impair daily activities [6]. Importantly, these symptoms are not complement-mediated and are therefore not addressed by complement inhibitors, a distinction with direct therapeutic relevance. Anemia is frequently transfusion-requiring; more than half of the patients receive red blood cell transfusions over the course of their disease, and seasonal worsening during colder months and febrile exacerbations of hemolysis are common [9,10].

CAD is associated with a clinically meaningful risk of thrombosis. Patients show an increased incidence of venous thromboembolism, with a reported hazard ratio of approximately 3.3 relative to the general population, and arterial thrombotic events are also more frequent in patients with CAD [11]. Complement-driven endothelial activation is thought to contribute to this prothrombotic state. Therefore, thromboprophylaxis is warranted in high-risk situations such as acute hemolytic exacerbations and during the perioperative period.

The pathophysiology of CAD is best understood as a two-hit process. The first hit is a clonal B-cell expansion within the bone marrow—a low-grade lymphoproliferative disorder that produces a monoclonal IgM-κ autoantibody [12,13]. This clone characteristically exhibitsIGHV4-34gene rearrangement and lacks theMYD88L265P mutation, distinguishing it from LPL and WM [14]. The presence of this component forms the rationale for B-cell–directed therapy, including treatment with rituximab, bendamustine, and, in selected settings, Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors [15].

The second hit is complement activation. At temperatures below the physiologic core temperature, the monoclonal IgM binds the I antigen on the red blood cell surface in the cooler peripheral circulation and activates the classical complement pathway in the sequence C1 → C4 → C2 → C3. The C3b opsonization of erythrocytes leads to their clearance by Kupffer cells in the liver, which predominantly produces extravascular hemolysis. Complete progression to the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) causes intravascular hemolysis, which occurs mainly during acute exacerbations. The presence of this component forms the rationale for complement inhibition, including treatment with the anti-C1s antibody sutimlimab and investigational agents such as riliprubart and iptacopan [1].

A landmark histopathologic study characterized the bone marrow clone in CAD as a distinct B-cell lymphoma, separable from LPL/WM [3]. CAD-associated LPD shows nodular CD20-positive B-cell infiltration with minimal plasmacytic differentiation and the absence of the perinodular mast cells typical of LPL/WM. The combination ofIGHV4-34restriction and the absence ofMYD88L265P provides a key molecular distinction, and these features are instrumental in the 2022 World Health Organization recognition of CAD-associated LPD as its own entity [3,16]. Mapping the classical pathway onto available and investigational drugs can clarify contemporary treatment strategies. Sutimlimab targets C1s and selectively blocks the classical pathway while sparing the lectin and alternative pathways, thus preserving the important arms of innate immune surveillance. Riliprubart is a next-generation anti-C1s antibody directed at active C1s, with early data suggesting markedly extended dosing intervals. Iptacopan is an oral factor B inhibitor acting more proximally on the alternative pathway amplification loop, and pegcetacoplan, a subcutaneous C3 inhibitor, demonstrated efficacy in a phase 2 study—raising hemoglobin levels and reducing hemolysis in CAD—although it has not advanced to pivotal development specifically for CAD [17]. Notably, terminal C5 inhibition with eculizumab is poorly suited to CAD because hemolysis is largely C3b-mediated and extravascular, lying upstream of C5 [1,18,19]. More broadly, complement activation has been increasingly implicated across autoimmune cytopenias—including immune thrombocytopenia, in which lower serum complement levels have been associated with disease activity—reinforcing the rationale for complement-directed therapy in antibody-mediated cytopenias [20]. The diagnostic approach proceeds in logical steps [1]. The first step is to confirm hemolysis through elevated levels of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and indirect bilirubin, reticulocytosis, and reduced levels of haptoglobin, together with a review of the peripheral blood smear, which may show erythrocyte agglutination. The second step is a monospecific direct antiglobulin test (DAT) to differentiate the pattern of positivity. CAD characteristically shows strong C3d positivity with a negative or, uncommonly, only weakly positive result for IgG. An isolated strong C3d pattern is typical, but occasional mixed patterns with concomitant weak IgG positivity may occur and do not, by themselves, exclude the diagnosis. The third step is subtyping and confirmation: warm AIHA is predominantly IgG-positive, whereas cold AIHA (CAD or CAS) is predominantly C3d-positive with a cold agglutinin titer ≥ 1:64. The final step distinguishes primary CAD from secondary CAS by evaluating the underlying infection, autoimmunity, or lymphoma, with bone marrow examination to confirm the clonal process.

Because these two entities differ fundamentally in their mechanisms and treatments, reliable separation is essential. The principal distinguishing features, antibody class and clonality, thermal behavior, mechanism and site of hemolysis, DAT pattern, and response to corticosteroids and splenectomy, are summarized in Table2. The central practical message is that corticosteroids and splenectomy, the mainstays of warm AIHA management, are generally ineffective in CAD because hemolysis in this condition is complement-mediated rather than Fc-mediated; therefore, these treatment approaches are generally not recommended for CAD.

A working diagnosis of CAD rests on the following findings: (1) chronic hemolysis supported by clinical and laboratory evidence; (2) a monospecific DAT result strongly positive for C3d and negative for IgG; (3) a cold agglutinin titer of at least 1:64 at 4 °C; (4) the absence of overt malignant lymphoma on clinical and imaging evaluation; and, where performed, (5) bone marrow findings showing clonal B-cell LPD, typically with absentMYD88L265P. Criteria (1)–(4) are required to establish the diagnosis, whereas bone marrow examination and molecular studies, although valuable for disease characterization and confirmation of the clonal nature of the process, are not mandatory for every patient and should be applied according to the clinical context [21,22].

Several practical points can improve the diagnostic accuracy. First, sample handling matters: blood should be collected and transported at 37 °C, because cooling causes in vitro agglutination that can spuriously elevate the mean corpuscular volume and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration and lower the measured red blood cell count; these artifacts resolve on warming the sample to 37 °C. Second, a monospecific DAT result is essential—C3d is strongly positive with IgG negativity—because polyspecific DAT alone is insufficient to distinguish CAD from warm AIHA. Third, thermal amplitude is a better predictor of clinical severity than titer: a thermal amplitude of approximately 28 °C–30 °C or higher marks a pathogenic threshold, and a high titer combined with a low thermal amplitude may be clinically silent. Fourth, secondary CAS must be actively excluded by evaluating for infection (Mycoplasma, EBV, cytomegalovirus [CMV]) and lymphoma (diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [DLBCL], marginal zone lymphoma, LPL/WM); when present, management is directed at the underlying cause [1,22].

Not all patients with CAD require pharmacological therapy. Treatment is indicated for cases showing clinically significant or symptomatic anemia (e.g., hemoglobin level < 10 g/dL or a significant decline), transfusion dependence, disabling circulatory symptoms, and anticipated cold exposure or surgery. General measures apply to all patients and include thermal protection and avoidance of cold triggers, folic acid supplementation, consideration of thromboprophylaxis in high-risk settings, and mandatory use of a blood warmer during transfusion. Corticosteroids and splenectomy have a limited role and are generally not recommended; however, terminal C5 inhibition with eculizumab is generally not appropriate, given the predominantly C3b-mediated extravascular mechanism of hemolysis, although uncommon exceptions may exist (for example, CAD with mixed warm and cold features or atypical DAT patterns) [21,23].

Because pathogenic IgM is largely intravascular, therapeutic plasma exchange can rapidly reduce the cold agglutinin burden—a single session may remove a substantial fraction of circulating IgM—and serves as an effective emergency management for an acute severe hemolytic crisis and as a bridge before hypothermic procedures such as cardiac surgery. This effect is transient. A critical procedural caveat is that the extracorporeal circuit can cool blood below the thermal amplitude and precipitate agglutination within the tubing; therefore, maintenance of strict normothermia with a fluid warmer at 37 °C throughout the procedure is mandatory [24].

B-cell-directed therapy targets the underlying clone and offers the prospect of durable, treatment-free remission. Rituximab monotherapy (375 mg/m [2] weekly for four weeks) produces overall response rates of approximately 50%–60% with low complete response rates and a median response duration of about 11 months; it is best suited to frail or elderly patients and can be repeated at relapse [25]. The combination of bendamustine and rituximab (BR) is a preferred first-line option for many patients without other illnesses, with overall response rates of 71%–78%, complete response rates of 40%–53%, and, notably, durable responses—five-year sustained remission in approximately 77% in the Nordic prospective experience—at the cost of myelosuppression, including grade 3–4 neutropenia in roughly one-third of patients [5,26]. Real-world experience reinforces the durability of this approach: in a recent single-center series of strictly defined primary CAD—confirmed to beMYD88L265P–negative by digital droplet polymerase chain reaction—fixed-duration rituximab-based chemoimmunotherapy achieved responses in all treated patients (overall response rate, 100%) with a median response duration of 90 months [27]. Sutimlimab, a humanized monoclonal antibody against C1s, was the first complement inhibitor extensively studied in CAD and the first approved therapeutic agent for the disease. In a phase 3 open-label CARDINAL trial of 24 transfusion-dependent patients, sutimlimab met its composite primary endpoint in 54% of the patients, raised mean hemoglobin levels from 8.6 to 11.2 g/dL, rendered 71% of the patients transfusion-free, and improved fatigue (FACIT-Fatigue +10.9 points), with responses evident within the first week; it received US approval in February 2022 [28]. The phase 3 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled CADENZA trial in 42 non-transfusion-dependent patients confirmed these findings, with the primary endpoint achieved in 72.7% versus 15% (odds ratio, 15.9; p < 0.001) and hemoglobin level of at least 12 g/dL in 50% versus 7% [29].

The two practical considerations are as follows: First, sutimlimab controls complement-mediated hemolysis but does not target the underlying clone; disease activity recurs upon discontinuation, so therapy is continuous, with efficacy maintained over long-term follow-up [30,31,32,33]. This makes a combination strategy with B-cell–directed treatment attractive when a fixed-duration approach is desired. Second, because the agent modulates the complement system, vaccination against encapsulated bacteria should be completed or updated at least two weeks before the first dose. The US prescribing information for sutimlimab specifically identifiesNeisseria meningitidis(serogroups A, C, W, Y, and B),Streptococcus pneumoniae, andHaemophilus influenzaetype b as organisms of concern and recommends vaccination against encapsulated bacteria in the most current Advisory Committee on Immunization Practice (ACIP) guidelines, together with patient counseling and provision of a patient safety card; reassuringly, no meningococcal infections were reported in the pivotal trials, consistent with preservation of the lectin and alternative pathways.

Several clone- and complement-directed options have been investigated for the treatment of relapsed or refractory CAD. Daratumumab, an anti-CD38 antibody targeting long-lived plasma cells, produced hemoglobin responses in a small retrospective series with an improvement in circulatory symptoms. BTK inhibitors such as ibrutinib offer dual inhibition of the B-cell and mast-cell pathways, with reports describing rapid improvement in hemolytic anemia and resolution of acrocyanosis. Bortezomib, through plasma-cell depletion, achieved an overall response rate of approximately 32%, with a median response duration of approximately 16 months in a GIMEMA phase 2 study limited by neuropathy [34,35,36].

Two complement-directed agents are particularly notable. Riliprubart, a next-generation antibody against active C1s, produced rapid and sustained hemoglobin improvement after a single dose in a phase 1b study, with responses maintained for around 15 weeks, raising the prospect of dosing as infrequently as every 12 weeks in comparison with the two-week schedule of sutimlimab [37]. Iptacopan, an oral factor B inhibitor, achieved the primary endpoint in 50% of a small phase 2 CAD cohort, with a mean hemoglobin increase of 2.2 g/dL at week 12, normalization of LDH levels in all patients, and improvement in fatigue [38]. An oral option would represent a meaningful reduction in treatment burden. Likewise, the subcutaneous C3 inhibitor pegcetacoplan showed activity in a phase 2 study, increasing hemoglobin levels (median, + 2.4 g/dL at week 48 in the CAD cohort) and reducing hemolysis, although it has not been advanced to pivotal development in CAD [17].

Response criteria derived from the Nordic prospective studies define complete response as normalization of hemoglobin levels, transfusion independence, resolution of hemolytic markers, and disappearance of the clonal disease on bone marrow biopsy, whereas partial response denotes a significant increase in hemoglobin levels (mean, approximately +3.9 g/dL in the Nordic data) and reduced transfusion needs with possible persistence of the clone. These definitions emphasize that the most durable outcomes require clone eradication, not merely hemolysis control.

These observations motivate a rational sequential strategy that exploits the complementary mechanisms of the two drug classes: a complement inhibitor first to achieve rapid control of hemolysis (a bridge); then B-cell-directed therapy to target the clone (induction), followed by careful tapering of complement inhibition with monitoring for relapse once clonal control is established [39]. This approach, which combines the speed of complement inhibition with the durability of clone-directed therapy, is a promising framework for fixed-duration treatment, consistent with recently proposed management algorithms [40], although it remains to be validated prospectively.

Access to these therapies varies considerably among regions, and the gap between the evidence base and routine availability is a practical reality in CAD management. In some health systems, including Korea, sutimlimab has not yet been approved for CAD, and bendamustine-based therapy is not formally reimbursed under a CAD indication. In practice, clone-directed treatment may be accessible only through off-label use under individual pre-authorization. Similarly, investigational agents are constrained by limited trial availability. These constraints mean that for many patients outside regions where complement inhibitors are approved, evidence-based options remain limited, and supportive measures, together with B-cell–directed therapy, continue to carry much of the therapeutic burden. This economic dimension is increasingly pertinent; formal cost-effectiveness analyses indicate that continuous sutimlimab therapy markedly exceeds conventional willingness-to-pay thresholds, whereas fixed-duration rituximab-based chemoimmunotherapy delivers durable responses at a substantially more favorable cost. This argument is especially salient in settings where complement inhibitors are inaccessible [27]. This gap is clinically relevant since it reveals a clear unmet need.

Several investigational strategies may further reshape CAD management (Table5). Clone-directed therapy is being extended to next-generation agents, including the BTK inhibitor zanubrutinib (CaZa; NCT06067048), A proliferation-inducing ligand (APRIL)/B-cell activating factor (BAFF) dual-antagonist povetacicept (RUBY-4; NCT05757570) [41], and the B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)-targeting agent OM336 (NCT07083960). In the complement axis, classical-pathway inhibition with GL-0719 (NCT05318534) and oral factor B inhibition with iptacopan (NCT06847607) are under evaluation, potentially improving convenience and cost relative to current options. In addition, CD19-directed chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy (inaticabtagene autoleucel; NCT07091370) is entering early-phase studies for refractory AIHA, building on the efficacy of CD19 CAR T cells in other autoimmune diseases [42] and raising the prospect of deeper and potentially durable clonal control. Key priorities remain the prospective validation of sequential and combination regimens, development of therapies for non-complement-mediated circulatory symptoms, and broader, more affordable access.

CAD is defined by the interplay of a clonal B-LPD and classical-pathway complement-mediated hemolysis and should always be distinguished from secondary CAS. Diagnosis depends on a monospecific DAT that is C3d-positive and IgG-negative, a cold agglutinin titer of at least 1:64, and bone marrow evaluation, including assessment ofMYD88status, with thermal amplitude serving as a better predictor of severity than the titer. Corticosteroids and splenectomy have limited roles and are generally not recommended. For patients without other diseases, bendamustine plus rituximab is a commonly preferred first-line option, whereas rituximab monotherapy is generally favored for frail or elderly patients. Treatment selection remains individualized, whereas sutimlimab provides rapid, reliable control of complement-mediated hemolysis but requires continuous administration, favoring its combination with clone-directed therapy as a fixed-duration strategy, and this approach requires prospective validation. For patients with relapsed or refractory disease, ibrutinib-, daratumumab-, and bortezomib-based approaches are options, and the next-generation agents riliprubart and iptacopan, although still in the early phase of development, point toward less burdensome and potentially oral complement inhibition. Continued progress will depend on prospective evaluation of sequential and combination strategies and on broadening access to effective therapies worldwide.
