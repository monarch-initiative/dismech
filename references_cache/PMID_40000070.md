# Ichthyosis Prematurity Syndrome: A Systematic Review of the Literature.
**Authors:** Li GX, Chen K, Sebaratnam DF, Pham JP
**Journal:** Pediatr Dermatol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1111/pde.15909](https://doi.org/10.1111/pde.15909)

## Content

BACKGROUND/OBJECTIVES: Ichthyosis prematurity syndrome (IPS) is a rare autosomal recessive congenital disorder characterized by premature birth, neonatal respiratory distress, eosinophilia, and a thick, clay-like vernix at birth. This review aims to summarize the available reported cases of IPS, including genetic etiology, clinical features, management, and prognosis.

METHODS: A search was conducted using MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and CINAHL, from inception to September 3, 2024 using the search terms "IPS" or "ichthyosis congenita type IV". This systematic review was registered on the PROSPERO database (CRD42024533988). Any publication reporting a case of IPS was suitable for inclusion. Quality appraisal was performed according to CARE guidelines. Descriptive statistical analysis was performed using R v4.3.1 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS: We identified 26 studies reporting 59 cases of IPS (PROSPERO database: CRD42024533988). While IPS was previously thought to predominantly affect Norwegian and Swedish patients, we identified cases from a broad range of ethnicities. The median duration of the cutaneous scale was 10 weeks, though patients usually have persisting atopy. Long-term prognosis is generally good, although six (10.2%) fulminant cases were identified with an estimated 6-month survival rate of 87.6% (95% confidence interval: 78.8-97.4). Mortality in these cases was attributed to respiratory and/or cardiac sequelae or sepsis. Limitations include heterogeneity in the reporting of cases and low quality of data in several aspects of data collection.

CONCLUSIONS: The cutaneous manifestations of IPS resolve over a period of weeks, and patients generally have a favorable long-term prognosis. However, rare cases of death and other complications have been reported.

Ichthyosis prematurity syndrome (IPS), also known as ichthyosis congenita type IV, is characterized by premature birth, neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, eosinophilia, and a thick, clay‐like vernix at birth [1, 2]. IPS is attributed to variants of the SLC27A4 gene that result in defective fatty acid transporter protein 4 (FATP4) [3, 4, 5]. Long‐term prognosis is good, and ichthyosis frequently resolves, leaving only an atopic diathesis as a persisting clinical feature [2]. Prevalence worldwide is estimated to be 1 in 200,000 cases, though recent cases from diverse ethnic backgrounds suggest that frequency may be underestimated [6, 7]. This review aims to summarize the available reported cases of IPS, including genetic etiology, clinical features, management, and prognosis.

A search was conducted using MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and CINAHL, from inception to September 3, 2024. Search terms employed were “IPS” or “ichthyosis congenita type IV” (Table S1). This review was registered on the PROSPERO database (CRD42024533988) and performed according to PRISMA guidelines.

Any publication reporting a case of IPS was suitable for inclusion. Publications were not excluded based on language, publication date, or type. For cases described in both a conference abstract and a later publication, the latter was used as the primary data point. Two investigators (G.X.L. and K.C.) reviewed the validity of all cases. Quality appraisal was performed according to CARE guidelines. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion with senior author D.F.S.

Data was collected using a protocol‐driven spreadsheet. The following information was extracted: patient sex, gestation, ethnicity, birthweight, APGAR scores, parental consanguinity, presence of polyhydramnios, presence of respiratory distress following birth, chest X‐ray imaging, management, distribution of scale, presence of erythroderma and dermographism, histopathologic and genetic findings, duration of cutaneous scaling, skin quality following resolution of scale, and age of last follow‐up. Missing data was recorded and analyzed as “NA” (not available).

Descriptive statistical analysis was performed using R v4.3.1 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). Survival outcomes were pooled from cases with sufficient reporting using the survival package v3.7.0.

Twenty‐six publications reporting on 59 patients were included, including our case report, which was unpublished at the time of the search but has since been published (Figure S1 and Table S1). Population characteristics are outlined in Table 1. The clinical presentation, management, and outcomes of IPS are summarized in Table 2.

The pathogenesis of IPS involves sequence variants in the SLC27A4 gene encoding FATP4 [3, 21, 22]. The 28 identified variants [3, 23] of SLC27A4, inherited in both homozygous (n = 14) and heterozygous (n = 15) forms, are summarized in Table S2. The latter were presumed to be compound heterozygotes, though this was not always specified. Furthermore, due to limited research on SLC27A4 in control populations, penetrance remains undetermined. Consequently, it is impossible to ascertain whether all individuals with SLC27A4 variants, including those of unknown significance, will develop IPS.

FATP4 plays an important role in transporting exogenous fatty acids into cells and acts as an acyl‐CoA synthetase to activate fatty acids for subsequent metabolism [21]. Although FATP4 is expressed in multiple tissues, murine models suggest variants in SLC274A common to IPS are predominantly associated with cutaneous involvement [22]. Recent evidence suggests that the skin barrier dysfunction observed in IPS results from impaired acylceramide synthesis, leading to hyperkeratotic scale [21, 22, 23, 24, 25].

Given the autosomal recessive inheritance of IPS, families should receive genetic counseling once an affected child is born, with recommendations to monitor future pregnancies and prepare for possible preterm labor and respiratory interventions in those that may be affected.

As IPS was first reported in individuals of Norwegian and Swedish descent, it has been thought to be of the highest incidence in this group [26, 27]. However, we found that the incidence of IPS in other ethnicities (European, African, Middle Eastern, Asian, North American and South American) is higher than expected (Table 1), supporting the need for more comprehensive reporting across diverse ethnicities to better elucidate the epidemiology, clinical findings, and best management.

Polyhydramnios remains the most frequently reported antenatal finding in IPS and was identified in 27 cases (45.8%). Keratin debris deposition likely contributes to polyhydramnios by obstructing the passage of amniotic fluid through the fetal gastrointestinal tract [6, 8, 28]. Other prenatal abnormalities were identified in eight cases, including hyperechoic amniotic fluid, chorioamnionitis, separation of chorionic and amniotic sacs, and increased sediment. The presence of chorioamniotic membrane separation in IPS and other skin disorders may be explained by the shared embryological origin of the amnion and surface ectoderm [29]. Heightened awareness of these antenatal features, which are typically seen from 28 weeks gestation onwards, may improve prenatal diagnosis and facilitate appropriate perinatal planning, particularly in preparing for respiratory support at birth.

All identified cases had classical cutaneous characteristics of IPS: congenital thick, clay‐like scale (Figure 1). Distribution was largely consistent across cases, with the most commonly affected site being the scalp (93.2%) and the least affected being the face (62.7%). The clay‐like scale of IPS is pathognomonic, but depending on the phenotype, differential diagnoses may include the thick scale of harlequin ichthyosis or the feathery scale of Conradi‐Hünermann‐Happle syndrome (Figure 2).

Erythroderma was also a feature in most cases (n = 41, 69.5%). A mastocytosis‐like presentation associated with dermographism and a positive Darier's sign was reported as a potential presentation of IPS in one case [30]. Bilateral ectropion was reported in one case [28]. Our case reported scales occluding the external auditory canals, resulting in an abnormal newborn hearing screen test result, which was normalized at 4 months [9].

Out of the 49 cases (83.1%) that reported on respiratory issues, the most common postnatal complication was respiratory distress syndrome (RDS). Pathogenesis is likely due to keratin debris in the amniotic fluid that accumulates in the airways and is aspirated at birth [1, 6]. Other respiratory complications may include aspiration pneumonia [31], pneumothorax [32], and biventricular systolic dysfunction with pulmonary hypertension [32]. The absence of a precise definition for RDS complicates data interpretation [33]. RDS incidence rates typically decrease with gestational age, ranging from 98% incidence at 24 weeks to 5% at 34 weeks [33, 34]. The cases in our study had an average gestational age of 32.30 weeks, with 79.7% reporting respiratory distress, significantly higher than the 5% approximate age‐matched incidence for the general population.

Six cases resulted in mortality attributed to respiratory and/or cardiac sequelae, or sepsis, occurring between birth and 6 months of age (10.2%). Follow‐up time was specified in 51 out of 59 cases (86.4%), with a median reported value of 3 years. To mitigate potential lead time bias resulting from variable follow‐up durations, we conducted a survival analysis (Kaplan–Meier) using the cases with reported follow‐up. This identified a 6‐month survival rate of 87.6% (95% confidence interval; 78.8–97.4) (Figure 3).

Kaplan–Meier curve for survival up to 60 months, constructed from cases with follow‐up duration reported (n = 51).

Compartment syndrome in the distal phalanges of the hands and feet due to constrictive ichthyotic scale was identified as a complication in one case [10], and has been noted in other congenital ichthyoses [35, 36]. Early detection enabled management with iloprost infusion, serial escharotomy—fasciotomy, sympathetic brachial plexus blockade, nitroglycerin skin patches, and motor rehabilitation, avoiding the need for digit amputation. Though uncommonly reported in the literature, we found one patient born with the dysmorphic features of polydactyly, ear abnormalities, and retrognathia [37].

Chest X‐ray findings included bilateral infiltrates [1, 38], diffuse opacities or densities [11, 32], confluent consolidation [8], atelectasis [6, 12, 13, 28], and features of aspiration pneumonia [14]. Eosinophilia was identified in 37 out of 38 cases reporting blood eosinophil count, with values ranging from 1050 to 14,175 cells/μL and 9%–38% of white cell count. Raised IgE was identified in 17 out of 19 cases reporting IgE count. Histological analysis was performed in 15 cases, with hyperkeratosis identified as the most common finding (n = 11). Some cases reported psoriasiform hyperplasia [39, 40], acanthosis [7, 15, 16], inflammatory [39] or lymphohistiocytic [30] infiltrate, and follicular plugging [7, 39]. Aggregates of curved multilamellar structures in the upper epidermis on electron microscopy were identified in 9 cases, and are pathognomonic for IPS, though the pathophysiological significance remains unclear.

Treatment primarily consisted of respiratory support to manage respiratory distress. Respiratory support was required in 27 cases (45.8%), including non‐invasive ventilation (n = 16, 27.1%) and invasive ventilation involving intubation (n = 14, 23.7%). Seven patients received both forms. Non‐invasive ventilation encompassed the use of continuous positive airway pressure, nasal cannula oxygen, and/or high‐frequency oscillatory ventilation. The mean duration of non‐invasive and invasive ventilation was 25.73 days (standard deviation, 45.41 days; range, 1–150 days) and 10.6 days (standard deviation, 12.86 days; range, 0.42–42 days), respectively. Fifteen cases used an incubator or humidifier (25.4%). Although surfactant administration was reported, the clinical benefit remains contentious as the underlying cause of poor respiratory function is likely keratin debris rather than insufficient surfactant [11].

Eleven cases reported using emollients (18.6%), including petroleum jelly [10, 17, 18, 28, 37, 38], paraffin [37], lanolin [28], and dexpanthenol [8]. There is insufficient evidence that emollients confer any therapeutic advantage, as the scale‐like vernix sheds spontaneously; however, considering potential complications of a defective skin barrier [22, 28], emollients are a simple and safe intervention that is well established in other forms of ichthyosis [41, 42, 43]. Furthermore, given the propensity for developing atopic disease in IPS, there may be merit in implementing a newborn skincare regimen with emollients, similar to those used for patients with atopic dermatitis. Use of bleach compresses (n = 1) [11], triclosan (n = 1) [13], and prophylactic broad‐spectrum antibiotics have been reported (n = 5) [8, 14, 28, 37], but our review does not support an increased risk of infection in IPS. Although salicylic acid use was reported in one case [10], usage should be weighed against the risk of salicylism [41]. Nutritional supplementation is likely needed due to increased metabolic demand associated with prematurity and epidermal hyperproliferation [28].

IPS has a generally favorable long‐term prognosis characterized by an almost complete resolution of cutaneous signs. This may be explained by the critical role of FATP4 in early skin development and functional compensation by other FATPs later in life [3]. Due to the disfiguring initial presentation, clinicians should provide reassurance regarding the favorable disease course. For the cases that did not result in mortality, where reported, the vernix lasted for a median duration of 10 weeks (standard deviation, 10.27 weeks; range, 0.86–32 weeks). Skin quality following resolution was characterized by persistent mild ichthyosis and/or follicular keratosis. Patients typically have a predisposition to atopic disease, possibly due to a defective skin barrier, epicutaneous sensitization, or as‐of‐yet effects of germline SLC27A4 variants on immune cell polarisation [40, 44]. Non‐specific pruritus of variable severity was reported in four studies [6, 12, 14, 26]. Other long‐term implications include chronic spontaneous urticaria [45], recurrent skin infections [6], alopecia [19, 26], hypohidrosis [19], and ophthalmological complications, including photophobia, epiphora, and conjunctivitis [1].

Lifelong management was poorly reported, and further prospective studies are required to delineate the optimal long‐term management of IPS to improve the quality of life for IPS patients.

Limitations in this study include heterogeneity in reporting and low quality of data for several aspects of data collection. In particular, our analysis of ethnicity was limited by insufficient clarity in the reporting of ethnicity in the case studies, with 40 cases not explicitly reporting ethnicity (67.8%). A large majority of these cases were cases from a Norwegian institution [26], though the assumption that these cases were ethnically Norwegian was not made. Data extraction for skin distribution was also subject to reporter and assessor bias, as in several instances, assessment was based on poor‐quality images. Reporting of clinical signs in individual case reports was likely subject to publication bias. Genotype–phenotype correlation is difficult to conclude due to the potential for confirmation bias, whereby case reports frequently report abnormal gene findings in the context of abnormal clinical presentation. Furthermore, analysis of prognosis was limited by insufficient follow‐up and a lack of reporting of follow‐up time. Analysis of the studies that reported follow‐up time found that the median follow‐up was 3 years. Thus, the short follow‐up time is also a likely source of significant bias, and larger cohort studies are required to adequately discuss long‐term sequelae.

The incidence of IPS may be higher than previously thought. IPS also affects a broad range of ethnicities. Ichthyosis lasts for a median duration of 10 weeks, though patients typically have persisting atopy. While the long‐term prognosis is generally favorable, rare cases of death, and other complications have been reported.
