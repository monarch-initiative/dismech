Fluoride in drinking water plays a dual role in dental health, where both 
excessive and insufficient intake present significant public health challenges. 
This cross-sectional study analyzed data from 3,980 children aged 8 to 12 years 
in Jinan, Shandong Province, China, collected in 2019 and 2024. The aim was to 
determine the optimal fluoride concentration in drinking water that balances the 
prevention of dental caries with the control of dental fluorosis. The 
associations between dental health outcomes and water fluoride concentrations 
were examined using combined data from the two independent cross-sectional 
surveys conducted in 2019 and 2024. The results indicated that each 1 mg/L 
increase in fluoride concentration was associated with a 1,246% (OR: 13.46, 
95%CI: 8.30, 21.84) increase in the risk of dental fluorosis. Conversely, the 
risk of dental caries exhibited a U-shaped association, with fluoride levels 
ranging from 0.5 to 1.0 mg/L linked to a lower risk of dental caries (OR: 0.85, 
95%CI: 0.74, 0.97) while also being associated with a controlled prevalence of 
dental fluorosis (OR: 2.51, 95%CI: 1.88, 3.35). These findings suggest that 
establishing a fluoride concentration range of 0.5 to 1.0 mg/L is optimal for 
balancing the prevention of dental caries and the control of dental fluorosis.

Fluoride is abundant in nature and readily accumulates in groundwater1. Drinking water is the primary source of daily fluoride exposure2. Both excessive and insufficient fluoride intake can have detrimental effects on human health3, particularly for children, who are a sensitive group in which fluoride accumulates most rapidly during tooth formation and growth. Therefore, ensuring appropriate fluoride intake for children has long been a focal point of research4–6.

Long-term excessive fluoride intake during tooth development can lead to dental fluorosis7. Mild cases may present as scattered white spots that are barely visible, while severe cases can result in damage to the entire enamel8. Dental fluorosis is primarily a concern for children aged 8 years or younger, as permanent teeth are fully formed after this age9. In contrast, appropriate fluoride intake plays a crucial role in preventing dental caries10,11. Due to regional differences in fluoride distribution and population sensitivity, countries worldwide have established context-specific standards. For instance, the United States recommends a fluoride concentration of 0.7 mg/L in drinking water to balance the prevention of caries with the risks of fluorosis12; Ireland has adjusted its recommended range from 0.8 to 1.0 mg/L to 0.6–0.8 mg/L as of 20075. The World Health Organization (WHO) further specifies an optimal range of 0.5 to 1.0 mg/L for community water supplies13. Notably, these international standards provide a clear reference for balancing dental health outcomes. However, China—one of the countries most affected by endemic fluorosis associated with drinking water—currently sets only a maximum fluoride limit of 1.0 mg/L in drinking water (GB 5749)14, with no specified minimum or recommended concentration, thereby creating a critical regulatory gap.

China is one of the countries facing a significant epidemic of endemic fluorosis attributed to drinking water15. In response, the government has implemented prevention and control measures aimed at enhancing water quality to mitigate fluoride-related health risks16. Notably, the detection rate of dental fluorosis in children has exhibited a downward trend due to the continuous execution of defluoridation projects, decreasing from 34.87% in 2009 to 10.19% in 202215. Conversely, the incidence of dental caries among children in China is on the rise. According to the Fourth National Oral Disease Epidemiological Survey conducted in 2015, the caries rate of primary teeth in 5-year-old children increased from 66% in 2005 to 71.9%, while the caries rate of permanent teeth in 12-year-old children rose from 28.9% to 38.5%17,18. In this context, it is crucial to determine the optimal range of fluoride concentration that can effectively prevent dental caries while controlling the prevalence of dental fluorosis in China. Currently, the fluoride limit for drinking water in China is established at 1.0 mg/L, with no minimum concentration limit or recommended concentration specified14. Previous studies19,20 on the effects of drinking water fluoride exposure on dental fluorosis and dental caries in China have predominantly focused on single-disease analyses. Although some research21–23 has examined both conditions, these studies primarily conducted correlation analyses between fluoride concentration and prevalence, lacking substantial evidence regarding the appropriate fluoride concentration in drinking water. Given the varying sensitivity to fluoride among different populations, it is vital to investigate the suitable fluoride concentration in drinking water for the Chinese population.

This study aims to analyze the effects of fluoride in drinking water on dental fluorosis and dental caries, while exploring the appropriate concentration of fluoride for China. Utilizing a cross-sectional design, we examined data from two rounds of surveillance conducted in 2019 and 2024 in Jinan, Shandong Province. The findings will serve as a foundation for adjusting control strategies in the country to prevent dental caries resulting from insufficient fluoride intake, while simultaneously avoiding dental fluorosis caused by excessive fluoride intake among the Chinese population.

This study utilized data from two independent cross-sectional surveys of endemic fluorosis in drinking water conducted in Jinan, Shandong Province, China, during the years 2019 and 2024. The surveillance was organized by the National Health Commission of China, with the aim of evaluating fluoride concentrations in drinking water and the trends of dental fluorosis in endemic areas, using villages as units of analysis. Participants included children aged 8 to 12 years who were born and residing in these areas, with a survey participation rate of no less than 90%. In addition to the national program’s required surveillance content, dental caries examinations were conducted for the same group of children. The participants from the two periods of data collection were distinct, indicating that this study was cross-sectional. All data involved in this study were strictly anonymized to eliminate any personally identifiable information, including names and contact details. The anonymized data were utilized solely for statistical analyses pertinent to this study and not for any other purposes. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of Center for Endemic Disease Control, Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention owing to the design of the study and the use of fully anonymized data.

In Jinan, all endemic fluorosis areas associated with drinking water have implemented water improvement projects that have been operational for over five years. In accordance with the requirements of the surveillance program, one tap water sample was collected from each surveillance village to determine the fluoride concentration. Each sample was measured in duplicate, and the average value was calculated. In cases where multiple water improvement projects existed within a village, the highest fluoride concentration among these projects was designated as the representative value for that village. Water samples were collected in 5.0 L polyethylene bottles and stored at 4 ℃ prior to analysis. The fluoride concentration in the water was determined using ion chromatography, following the standard examination methods for drinking water (GB/T 5750)24,25 established by the Chinese national authorities.

Dental examinations were conducted by dentists who had completed standardized training. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Diagnosis of dental fluorosis (WS/T 208)26 and Oral health surveys: basic methods27. Dental fluorosis was assessed under natural light, ensuring that any contaminants on the lip and cheek surfaces of the teeth were removed using cotton. Enamel damage was evaluated under dry conditions with a cleaning mouth mirror. The diagnostic criteria for dental fluorosis were based on the Diagnosis of dental fluorosis (WS/T 208)26, categorizing the fluorosis status of each tooth into six classifications: normal, questionable, very mild, mild, moderate, and severe fluorosis. After assessing the enamel damage on the buccal surface of each tooth, two of the most severely affected teeth were selected for diagnosis based on the extent of enamel damage. In instances where the selected teeth exhibited varying degrees of damage, the tooth with lesser damage was diagnosed, representing the dental fluorosis diagnosis for the subject. When both deciduous and permanent teeth were present, only the permanent teeth were evaluated for dental fluorosis. In this study, normal and questionable tooth results were classified as non-dental fluorosis, while very mild, mild, moderate, and severe fluorosis were categorized as dental fluorosis. Dental caries examinations were conducted under natural light using a probe, following the diagnostic criteria27: caries were identified if observable on pit and fissure points or smooth surfaces, or if there was enamel destruction or detectable softening of the cavity’s bottom or walls.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Center for Endemic Disease Control, Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention. It adhered to the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimation Reporting for Population Health Research. Given that all participant data were anonymous, informed consent was not required.

Means and standard deviations (SD) were utilized to summarize continuous variables, while frequencies and percentages (%) were employed to describe categorical variables. Differential analyses were conducted to compare variables across key groups. Specifically, independent samples t-tests were used to compare the means of continuous variables between the two surveillance years (2019 vs. 2024). Additionally, independent samples t-tests were applied to compare continuous variables between outcome groups. For all comparisons involving categorical variables, Chi-square tests were utilized. The study outcomes, dental fluorosis and dental caries, were defined as binary variables (presence or absence) for each participant. The associations between dental health outcomes and water fluoride concentrations were examined using the combined data from the two independent cross-sectional surveys (2019 and 2024). These associations were analyzed utilizing binary logistic regression, adjusting for age, gender, and the surveillance period (included as a categorical variable to accoumodel 2, adjusted for age group, gender, and surveillancent for population-level differences between the two survey years). Restricted cubic splines (RCS) with four knots were employed to establish exposure-response (E-R) relationships between water fluoride concentration and the risk of dental fluorosis and dental caries. Finally, based on the results of the E-R relationships and the national limit for fluoride in drinking water (1.0 mg/L)14, water fluoride concentrations were categorized into three groups: <0.5 mg/L, 0.5–1.0 mg/L, and > 1.0 mg/L. This categorization was used to analyze the associations between dental health outcomes (dental fluorosis and dental caries) and water fluoride levels using binary logistic regression. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (OR and 95%CI) were reported. Statistical analyses were performed using R (Version 4.4.1), with statistical significance set at a two-sided P < 0.05.

The characteristics of the study participants are summarized in Table 1. A total of 3,980 children participated in the study, with 2,138 participants in 2019 and 1,842 in 2024. The age and gender distribution among participants remained comparable across the two surveillance years. Notably, a significant decrease in the mean water fluoride concentration was observed from 2019 to 2024, with values of 0.59 mg/L and 0.49 mg/L, respectively (P < 0.001). Concurrently, the prevalence of dental caries increased significantly from 27.9% to 35.4% (P < 0.001), while the prevalence of dental fluorosis exhibited a non-significant downward trend, decreasing from 7.6% to 6.6% (P = 0.222). The majority of dental fluorosis cases were classified as very mild or mild, accounting for 89.8% of the total cases.

Table 2 presents the descriptive statistics for dental fluorosis and dental caries. For dental fluorosis, no significant differences were observed in age [mean (SD), 10.19 (1.29) years in cases vs. 10.08 (1.31) years in non-cases, P = 0.154], age group distribution (P = 0.086), or gender (P = 0.211). However, notable disparities were found in water fluoride concentration [0.67 (0.31) mg/L in cases vs. 0.53 (0.18) mg/L in non-cases, P < 0.001] and in the distribution of water fluoride levels (P < 0.001). Specifically, 62.8% of dental fluorosis cases were exposed to water fluoride levels of 0.5–1.0 mg/L, while 13.0% were exposed to levels exceeding 1.0 mg/L, both proportions surpassing those observed in non-cases (P < 0.001).

Regarding dental caries, a significant difference was noted in gender (P < 0.001), with a higher prevalence in girls (52.1% in cases vs. 44.6% in non-cases). Additionally, water fluoride levels showed a significant association (P = 0.020). No significant associations were identified concerning age (P = 0.073) or age group (P = 0.103). Furthermore, water fluoride concentration did not differ significantly between caries cases and non-cases (P = 0.582).

High concentrations of fluoride in drinking water are associated with health risks for approximately 200 million individuals across 25 countries15. In China, the epidemic of drinking water fluorosis significantly impacts 28 provincial-level administrative divisions (PLADs) and over 70 million individuals. In the 1980 s, approximately 15.06 million individuals in China were diagnosed with dental fluorosis28. This condition manifests as visible changes in the enamel, resulting in a mottled and defective appearance, typically occurring before the age of eight9. It adversely affects dental health and contributes to negative emotions and psychological issues in children29. The results of this study indicate that each 1 mg/L increase in water fluoride concentration is associated with a 1,246% increase in the risk of dental fluorosis. Furthermore, the association between drinking water fluoride concentration and the risk of dental fluorosis is non-linear, with the rate of risk increase accelerating as fluoride concentration rises. Improving water quality and reducing fluoride levels are the most effective methods for preventing and controlling dental fluorosis. The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) indicates that the decline in dental fluorosis prevalence from 87.3% in 2013–2014 to 68.2% in 2015–2016 may be attributed to the 2015 recommendation by the US Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS) to lower water fluoride concentrations from 1.2 to 0.7 mg/L to minimize the risk of dental fluorosis9. Surveillance results from endemic fluorosis areas in China show that the median fluoride concentration in drinking water decreased from 0.84 mg/L to 0.59 mg/L between 2014 and 201828, while the prevalence of dental fluorosis declined from 27.74% to 22.07%15. In 2019, the prevalence of dental fluorosis among children aged 8 to 12 in Jinan was lower than the national average of 15.5%15, standing at 7.6%. However, surveillance conducted in Jinan in 2019 and 2024 revealed a trend consistent with national observations: the prevalence of dental fluorosis decreases as fluoride concentration is reduced. These findings are consistent with the idea that dental fluorosis can be managed by regulating fluoride concentration in drinking water, suggesting that strategies aimed at reducing the prevalence of dental fluorosis are beneficial.

When considering policies aimed at reducing dental fluorosis, it is essential to account for the potential loss of cavity protection. Dental caries is one of the most prevalent chronic diseases, affecting nearly 60% to 90% of children and presenting a significant burden on both the economy and quality of life10. Research and practice have confirmed that during tooth development, fluoride replaces calcium and phosphate ions, facilitating the formation of fluorapatite, a compound that is weakly acidic and less soluble. This process enhances the strength of teeth and aids in the prevention of dental caries. Furthermore, in the initial or pre-caries stage, fluoride exhibits remineralizing and antimicrobial effects, enabling the repair or remineralization of early dental caries7. The findings of this study indicate that as the concentration of fluoride in drinking water decreased, the prevalence of dental caries among children in Jinan increased from 27.9% to 35.4%. This trend aligns with studies conducted by Fan21 and Jin30 on children in Shaanxi and Tianjin, China, respectively. However, when fluoride was analyzed as a continuous variable, no statistically significant association was identified between fluoride concentrations and the risk of dental caries. This absence of association may be explained by the U-shaped association observed between fluoride concentration and dental caries, a phenomenon that has also been documented in other studies21,22,31. Specifically, as fluoride concentration increases, the risk of dental caries initially decreases before subsequently rising. This phenomenon may occur because high fluoride concentration leading to more severe dental fluorosis might also increase susceptibility to dental caries21. The research findings suggest that fluoride, at appropriate concentrations, may have a protective effect against dental caries.

The findings indicate that a grouped analysis of fluoride concentration is associated with a lower risk of dental caries when fluoride levels range from 0.5 to 1.0 mg/L compared to groups with concentrations below 0.5 mg/L. Although there is a risk of dental fluorosis, this risk is lower than that associated with fluoride concentrations exceeding 1.0 mg/L. Furthermore, the majority of children exhibiting dental fluorosis displayed very mild or mild forms (89.8%). Do and Spencer32 found no negative correlation between mild dental fluorosis and dental appearance, self-rated dental health, or the perceptions of dental health held by either the children or their parents. These findings suggest that for Chinese children, a fluoride concentration in drinking water of 0.5 to 1.0 mg/L represents an ideal safety threshold, as it effectively reduces the incidence of dental caries while controlling the prevalence of dental fluorosis. While the optimal fluoride range aligns with the recommendations of WHO13, this study provides novel insights through methodological advancements and evidence specific to the population, thereby addressing critical gaps in public health practices in China. However, the optimal fluoride concentration range differs from those observed in Ireland5 and the United States12. This discrepancy may be attributed to socioeconomic factors, dietary habits, and individual oral hygiene practices. Furthermore, populations across different countries exhibit varying sensitivities to fluoride.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, it is cross-sectional rather than longitudinal; while it demonstrates an association between fluoride exposure and both dental fluorosis and caries, this does not necessarily imply causation. The cross-sectional nature of our data limits the ability to infer causal relationships between water fluoride levels and dental health outcomes. Additionally, the focus on the population of Jinan restricts the generalizability of the results. Furthermore, the study exclusively concentrated on fluoride from drinking water and measured fluoride levels at a single time point, which may not accurately represent exposure levels during the critical years of permanent tooth development. Due to the inability to obtain the number of affected teeth, our analytical options have been limited to the use of binary outcomes instead of counts of affected teeth, rendering the calculation of prevalence ratios infeasible. Future studies should consider collecting count data to facilitate more in-depth analyses. Moreover, the investigation was confined to domestic drinking water, excluding other sources such as purified or bottled water, which could provide a more comprehensive view of fluoride exposure. Lastly, the study did not analyze related factors such as lifestyle habits and economic conditions that impact dental health. Therefore, further research is necessary to establish a more robust foundation for implementing water improvement measures and for preventing and controlling dental fluorosis and dental caries.

This cross-sectional study reveals significant associations between fluoride concentrations in drinking water and the prevalence of dental fluorosis and dental caries among Chinese children. The findings suggest that a fluoride concentration range of 0.5 to 1.0 mg/L is associated with a lower risk of dental caries while simultaneously controlling the prevalence of dental fluorosis. Therefore, it is recommended to establish a lower limit, in addition to the existing maximum limit, for fluoride levels in drinking water. Furthermore, alongside enhancing water quality surveillance, it is essential to conduct research aimed at mitigating the dual dental health burdens, thereby ensuring safer fluoride exposure for children.
