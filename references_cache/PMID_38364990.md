# An update on clinical presentation and responses to therapy of patients with hereditary hypophosphatemic rickets with hypercalciuria (HHRH).
**Authors:** Zhu Z, Bo-Ran Ho B, Chen A, Amrhein J, Apetrei A, Carpenter TO, Lazaretti-Castro M, Colazo JM, McCrystal Dahir K, Geßner M, Gurevich E, Heier CA, Simmons JH, Hunley TE, Hoppe B, Jacobsen C, Kouri A, Ma N, Majumdar S, Molin A, Nokoff N, Ott SM, Peña HG, Santos F, Tebben P, Topor LS, Deng Y, Bergwitz C
**Journal:** Kidney Int (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.kint.2024.01.031](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.kint.2024.01.031)

## Content

Pathogenic variants in solute carrier family 34, member 3 (SLC34A3), the gene encoding the sodium-dependent phosphate cotransporter 2c (NPT2c), cause hereditary hypophosphatemic rickets with hypercalciuria (HHRH). Here, we report a pooled analysis of clinical and laboratory records of 304 individuals from 145 kindreds, including 20 previously unreported HHRH kindreds, in which two novel SLC34A3 pathogenic variants were identified. Compound heterozygous/homozygous carriers show above 90% penetrance for kidney and bone phenotypes. The biochemical phenotype for heterozygous carriers is intermediate with decreased serum phosphate, tubular reabsorption of phosphate (TRP (%)), fibroblast growth factor 23, and intact parathyroid hormone, but increased serum 1,25-dihydroxy vitamin D, and urine calcium excretion causing idiopathic hypercalciuria in 38%, with bone phenotypes still observed in 23% of patients. Oral phosphate supplementation is the current standard of care, which typically normalizes serum phosphate. However, although in more than half of individuals this therapy achieves correction of hypophosphatemia it fails to resolve the other outcomes. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics score correlated with functional analysis of frequent SLC34A3 pathogenic variants in vitro and baseline disease severity. The number of mutant alleles and baseline TRP (%) were identified as predictors for kidney and bone phenotypes, baseline TRP (%) furthermore predicted response to therapy. Certain SLC34A3/NPT2c pathogenic variants can be identified with partial responses to therapy, whereas with some overlap, others present only with kidney phenotypes and a third group present only with bone phenotypes. Thus, our report highlights important novel clinical aspects of HHRH and heterozygous carriers, raises awareness to this rare group of disorders and can be a foundation for future studies urgently needed to guide therapy of HHRH.

Hereditary hypophosphatemic rickets with hypercalciuria (HHRH; OMIM: 241530)1–4 is an autosomal recessive renal phosphate-wasting disorder originally described by Tieder et al.5–7. HHRH is caused by compound heterozygous or homozygous (comp/hom) inactivating pathogenic variants on both parental alleles of the solute carrier family 34, member 3 (SLC34A3), the gene encoding the sodium (Na+)-dependent phosphate cotransporter 2c (NPT2c). Individuals with HHRH show increased urinary phosphate excretion leading to hypophosphatemic rickets, bowing, and short stature. Characteristically and appropriately suppressed fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) causes elevated 1,25-dihydroxy vitamin D (1,25(OH)2D), which results in hypercalciuria because of enhanced intestinal calcium absorption and reduced parathyroid hormone (PTH) - dependent calcium reabsorption in the distal renal tubules.

While the genetic cause of HHRH is now well established, many aspects of the resulting illness remain poorly understood, particularly regarding the renal and skeletal phenotypes. This problem is due to data sparsity in this regard, as recently reviewed by us8, 9 and others10–14. For instance, it is unknown why 53.5% of patients have no clinical evidence of renal calcifications and 23.2% of patients have no clinical evidence of bone disease8. In contrast, in others, these outcomes fail to respond to therapy with oral phosphate supplementation8. While some biomarkers, such as bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (ALP) improve, 1,25(OH)2D often remains elevated despite intensive oral phosphate therapy, and in some cases, hypercalciuria, nephrocalcinosis, and kidney stone disease persist. Even if bone pain resolves, many patients fail to reach peak bone mass. It also remains unknown whether HHRH predisposes to accelerated bone loss (or osteoporosis) during adulthood. The few available bone biopsies obtained from pediatric cases show increased osteoid volume and osteoblast surface, with reduced mineral apposition rate and osteoclast number consistent with osteomalacia15, 16. However, increased cortical porosity and reduced trabecular number consistent with osteoporosis are observed in adults17. Data before and after therapy in one pediatric and one adult case show incomplete or no improvement of the osteoid volume to phosphate supplementation, respectively, and bone mineral density (BMD) remains below expected for age in these individuals16, 18.

In the current report, we performed a pooled analysis of clinical and laboratory records of 304 individuals from 145 kindreds, including 20 previously unreported HHRH kindreds, in which two novel SLC34A3 pathogenic variants were identified. Their data were used for a detailed evaluation of genetic and non-genetic predictors of disease severity and for variable response to therapy with oral phosphate supplements.

This study was approved by the Yale Research Ethics Committee (IRB Protocol ID: 1501015216).

All laboratory studies, genetic analyses and imaging studies were performed at laboratories used by the different investigators (normal ranges are provided in parentheses after each value). 25-Hydroxy vitamin D (25(OH)D) levels were measured by liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectroscopy or chemiluminescence immunoassay, and 1,25(OH)2D levels were measured by radioimmunoassay or enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Serum intact PTH (iPTH) levels were determined by ELISA, and FGF23 levels were measured by an ELISA that detects the intact hormone and C-terminal fragments thereof (Immutopics) as well as intact FGF23 (Immutopics or Kainos). The renal tubular reabsorption of phosphate (TRP; %) was calculated using the following equation: 100×(1-(urine phosphorus×serum creatinine)/(serum phosphorus×urine creatinine). Tubular maximum phosphate reabsorption per glomerular filtration rate (TmP/GFR) was estimated using the Walton and Bijvoet nomogram19. Urine phosphate excretion (TmP/GFR or TRP (%)) and urinary calcium/creatinine ratio (U-Ca/Crea) were determined in fasting spot urines.

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) examination was used to assess BMD. As described in our previous study8, bone phenotype was defined as the presence of bone pain, rickets/osteomalacia, pathological fracture, bowing, corrective surgery and/or osteopenia/osteoporosis. Renal ultrasound or abdominal CT was used to determine renal phenotype including nephrocalcinosis, renal stones and/or renal cysts.

In the current study, the term ‘genetic variant’ is defined as a different version of a particular gene that occurs naturally within a population, the term ‘pathogenic variant’ is defined as the genetic variant which causes a phenotype, and the term ‘carrier’ is more broadly used as individual carrying heterozygous (het) or comp/hom pathogenic variants, as both are phenotypically affected. Sequence variants of SLC34A3 were determined by whole exome sequencing (WES), as described8. In kindreds 073 and 095, novel pathogenic variants were identified and confirmed using PCR-based assays using the primer sequences previously reported by us1. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) convened a working group in 2013 comprised of representatives from the ACMG, the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP), and the College of American Pathologists (CAP) to revise the standards and guidelines for the interpretation of sequence variants20. We used this revised ACMG score 20, 21 to classify SLC34A3 sequence variants into benign (1), likely benign (2), a variant of unknown significance (3), likely pathogenic (4), and pathogenic (5). The sum of individual ACMG scores was calculated when more than one variant was identified. Pathogenicity will not increase by another cis variant in the same allele, if there is one loss-off-unction variant with ACMG score 5. Cis variants with ACMG scores less than 5 will be added to not exceed a score of 5. The maximum sum of ACMG scores is therefore 5 for het individuals and 10 for comp/hom individuals, as has been suggested by ACMG (https://www.acmg.net).

To obtain follow-up data, we contacted authors of the original publication and reviewed the medical records of unpublished individuals in a retrospective observational approach. Data on pre-and post-treatment with oral phosphate supplementation with 20 mg phosphorus/Kg/day divided over several doses were included for analysis.

We introduced a series of HHRH pathogenic variants using Quikchange site-directed mutagenesis into an expression plasmids (pEGFP-NaPi-IIc) containing enhanced green fluorescence protein (EGFP) concatenated to the cDNA encoding wild type human SLC34A3/NPT2c, as previously described[1], followed by the generation of capped and polyadenylated mRNA for microinjected into X. oocytes. On day three after injection oocytes were incubated in 40Na (40 mM NaCl, 60 mM choline chloride, 2 mM KCl, 1.8 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 10 mM HEPES-Tris (pH 7.4)) containing 0.1 mCi 33P/ml ([33P]orthophosphoric acid, PerkinElmer NEX080) for one hour Absolute uptake, correction of uptake over surface expression, and simple linear regression analysis with the ACMG scores of the mutant NPT2c transporters studied were performed.

For our summary statistics, we used clinical and laboratory findings (LFs) before therapy was initiated. For statistical evaluation, we determined the number of individuals affected by renal and/or bone phenotypes, as described in our previous study8. Asymptomatic individuals were negative for the above renal and bone phenotypes. To increase the sample size, we treated all renal phenotypes or bone phenotypes collectively. Age-related reference values (RVs) were obtained from www.mayomedicallaboratories.com, and respective clinical laboratories; age-related RVs for TmP/GFR are in refs.22–25, and age-related RVs for U-Ca/Crea are in ref.26. Assuming normal distribution, LFs can be transformed into z-scores using the formula: z-score=(LFs - RV mean)/SD27. Z-scores were used for all statistical analyses with the following exceptions: absolute values are presented for 25(OH)D, 1,25(OH)2D, and TRP (%). The data for iPTH, age-specific U-Ca/Crea, and FGF23 (iFGF23 and cFGF23) are presented as percentage of the upper limit (%UL) of RVs. These exceptions are important, because for these assays, upper and lower RVs have not been established in sufficiently large number of normal age-specific controls28. For brevity, we will omit ‘z-score’ or ‘%UL’ when mentioning LFs in the text.

To account for the correlation among subjects within same kindred, the generalized estimating equation (GEE) model29 was used to compare baseline data among groups stratified by genotypes, phenotypes or quartiles, using exchangeable correlation structure30 and logit link for binary variable and identity link for continuous variable (GENMOD, SAS). Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons31. The linear mixed effects model was used to compare paired data between pre-treatment and post-treatment groups, considering the cluster effect of kindreds (MIXED, SAS)32. To explore the genetic and non-genetic predictors for renal and bone phenotypes, univariable and multivariable GEE models with logit link were built (GENMOD, SAS). Univariable GEE models with identity link were employed to evaluate the association between continuous variables. Additionally, due to missing values on these non-genetic predictors, sensitivity analyses were performed on imputed data and results were pooled to evaluate the robustness of the identified predictors. To assess the uncertainty of imputed data, we employed Expectation Maximization (EM)-algorithm33 using the ‘Amelia’ R package and the Multiple Imputation (MI) procedure34 in SAS to generate imputed datasets, and both pooled results are provided.

For independently normally distributed variables, t test or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s corrections were performed for comparisons between groups as appropriate. For independently non-normally distribution variables, non-parametric Mann-Whitney test or Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s corrections were used for comparison as appropriate. Pearson correlation coefficient was used to analyze the correlation between continuous variables.

All statistical analyses were performed using Prism (version 9.0; GraphPad Software Inc.), R software (version 4.1.2; https://www.R-project.org) and SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Inc.).

The genetic and clinical evaluation of 20 new kindreds led to the discovery of two kindreds with novel pathogenic variants: The index case 073/II-2 was found to be compound het for a novel missense variant (SLC34A3.het.c.497G>A.p.G166D) and a previously reported deletion (SLC34A3.het.c.925+20_926–48del)14. c.497G>A.p.G166D is absent from the 1000 Genome35, dbSNP36, and the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute Gene Ontology Exome Sequencing Project (http://evs.gs.washington.edu/EVS/) databases. The variant is predicted by Polyphen to reduce protein function 37, 38. The index case 095/II-1 was found to be heterozygous for a missense variant SLC34A3.het.c.1009G>A.p.G337S previously not associated with HHRH. c.1009G>A.p.G337S has a global minor allele frequency (MAF) of 0.037 by dbSNP36. However, c.1009G>A.p.G337S affects an amino acid that is conserved in 21 out of 29 available species (PhyloP 46-way conservation score)39 and c.1009G>A.p.G337S segregates with the phenotype when additional carriers in the kindred 095 were examined and, therefore, is likely disease-causing. Detailed clinical information on the kindreds (073 and 095) is given in Supplementary Material and Supplementary Figure S1.

We reviewed available public databases with the search terms HHRH or SLC34A3, resulting in 163 entries, further manually curated for reports of 125 kindreds, comprising 277 individuals1–3, 7, 8, 10, 11, 13, 14, 16–18, 21, 40–68. After including 20 unpublished kindreds, 145 kindreds, comprising 304 individuals, were evaluated (Figure 1; Table 1). HHRH kindreds were reported on all continents and in multiple races (Supplementary Figure S2a-b), with a high frequency in North America, the Middle East, and Europe.

The frequency of renal phenotypes is significantly increased in comp/hom carriers (Figure 1a) and het carriers (Figure 1b) of SLC34A3 pathogenic variants, as compared to the frequency in the wild type relatives (3.45%; Figure 1c) which is comparable to the frequency of nephrolithiasis (5.6%) in the general population69. Likewise, the frequency of bone phenotypes was found to increase in het and in comp/hom carriers (Figure 1a-c). The percentage of renal and bone phenotypes in het carriers is intermediate between comp/hom carriers and wild type relatives (Figure 1a-c). The detailed frequency of renal and bone phenotypes is summarized in Table 1. A separate analysis of children (age <18 y.) and adults showed an increase in renal phenotypes with age (Figure 1d-i). Additionally, children carrying comp/hom pathogenic variants (Figure 1d) were more likely to present with isolated bone phenotypes when compared to adults (Figure 1g), whereas the frequencies of isolated renal phenotypes were similar in both age groups (Figure 1d; 1g). There was no difference in bone and renal phenotypes between children and adults carrying het pathogenic variants (Figure 1e; Figure 1h).

Pooled phenotypic analysis showed serum phosphate and TmP/GFR (Figure 2a-b) significantly decreased in het and comp/hom carriers of SLC34A3 pathogenic variants when compared to wild type, and TRP (%; Figure 2c) was decreased only in comp/hom carriers. Likewise, serum 1,25(OH)2D and urine calcium excretion (Figure 2d-e) were significantly increased in het and comp/hom carriers when compared to wild type. Consistent with the frequency of bone phenotype, the total hip BMD was significantly lower, iPTH was decreased and serum ALP was increased in comp/hom carriers compared to het carriers (Figure 2f-h). FGF23 was significantly decreased in comp/hom and het as compared to wild type (Figure 2i), where available. Serum 25(OH)D and serum calcium (Supplementary Figure S2e-f) showed no significant differences among the three groups. The differences to het carriers were intermediate. Renal function was normal among groups as illustrated by serum creatinine (Supplementary Figure S2g) with the exception of three outliers (for details see Supplementary Results).

Oral phosphate supplementation without active vitamin D analogs to not worsen the hypercalciuria has been proposed to treat HHRH, but the response to this therapy is understudied. We therefore contacted our kindreds and asked colleagues to collect information on their kindreds to evaluate the response of the phenotypic parameters described in Table 1 and Figures 1–2 to oral phosphate supplementation. 39 individuals with available data on pre- and post-treatment were included for analysis. As expected, all comp/hom and het carriers improved their serum phosphate after oral phosphate supplementation (Figure 3a). However, iPTH, urine calcium excretion, L1-L4 BMD, serum ALP, 25(OH)D, 1,25(OH)2D, TmP/GFR, calcium, serum creatinine, and TRP (%) were quite variable, and a significant difference in paired analysis of pre-treatment and post-treatment data was only seen for iPTH and urine calcium excretion in comp/hom individuals (Figure 3b-i; Supplementary Figure S3a-b). When classified as incomplete-responder based one or more of the following: persistent hypercalciuria, elevation of serum calcium and 1,25(OH)2D and suppression of iPTH, overall, response to therapy with phosphate supplementation was incomplete in 22/39 cases (56.4%; Supplementary Figure S4).

An additional explanation for variable presentation at baseline and response to oral phosphate supplementation could be the severity of the SLC34A3 pathogenic variant when evaluated by the ACMG score 20, 21 and by Na-dependent X. oocytes phosphate uptake (Figure 4a). The absolute 33P-uptake mediated by mutant as fraction of wild type SLC34A3/NPT2c was negatively associated with ACMG score (Figure 4b), which is consistent with the results generated from published studies (Supplementary Figure S5c)10, 11, 66, 70. For effects of individual pathogenic variants on surface expression, see Supplementary Figure S5a and for 33P-uptake corrected over surface expression, see Supplementary Figure S5b.

The sum of ACMG scores of both SLC34A3 alleles was significantly higher in individuals with renal phenotype when compared to those without renal phenotype (Figure 5a). Similarly, individuals with bone phenotype had a higher ACMG score when compared to those without bone phenotype (Figure 5b). Moreover, carriers with either renal or bone phenotype had an intermediate ACMG score to those with neither or both phenotypes (Figure 5c).

To perform a similar analysis for the continuous phenotypic variables, individuals were grouped by quartile, with quartile 1 assigned to the lowest value. Significant associations with a higher ACMG score were seen in the group with a lower level of serum phosphate, TmP/GFR, or iPTH (Figure 5d-f) and in the group with a higher level of 1,25(OH)2D, urine calcium excretion or ALP (Figure 5g-i). ACMG score was not significantly associated with a difference in serum 25(OH)D, calcium, TRP (%), or serum creatinine (Supplementary Figure S6a-d).

A sub-analysis for het and comp/hom individuals separately showed no significant associations of ACMG score with renal or bone phenotype, and with the continuous phenotypic variables evaluated in Figure 4 (Supplementary Figure S7-S9), suggesting that the number of mutant alleles may be the key predictor for disease severity at presentation.

It is possible that high variability at presentation for private pathogenic variants obscure a genotype-phenotype association. We, therefore next evaluated baseline characteristics for a subgroup of SLC34A3 pathogenic variants present in three or more individuals. Indeed, the average baseline serum phosphate was variable among different het pathogenic variants, although these differences did not reach significance (Supplementary Figure S10a). However, average baseline serum phosphate did not correlate to the ACMG score or 33P-uptake in vitro (Supplementary Figure S10b).

We next explored whether the ACMG score predicts the response to phosphate supplementation. The ACMG score was positively correlated to the delta 25(OH)D (delta value = post-treatment value – pre-treatment value; Supplementary Figure S11a). No significant correlation of the response to phosphate supplementation to ACMG score was found for the other parameters (Supplementary Figure S11b-j). We also tested whether the number of mutant alleles predicts the response to phosphate supplementation. However, there was no significant difference of the response to phosphate supplementation between mono- and biallelic pathogenic variants (Supplementary Figure S3c-k).

The univariable GEE model suggested that renal/bone phenotype was more likely to occur in biallelic vs. monoallelic carriers of SLC34A3 pathogenic variants and in individuals with a higher ACMG score (Table 2, unadjusted section), which remained significant after adjusting for age and sex (Table 2, adjusted section). However, the association of ACMG score and renal/bone phenotype was not significant when the analysis was limited to comp/hom individuals (Table 2, comp/hom only section).

For an exploratory analysis to identify predictors of renal and bone phenotype independent of genotype and variant severity, the same model was used. The univariable GEE model suggested that renal phenotype was more likely to occur in individuals with bone phenotype. Individuals were more likely to have renal phenotype if they had decreased serum phosphate, TmP/GFR, TRP (%) and iPTH but increased serum 1,25(OH)2D and urine calcium excretion at baseline (Table 3, unadjusted section). Additionally, TRP (%) and iPTH were predictors for renal phenotype independent of the number of SLC34A3 mutant alleles or ACMG score (Table 3, adjusted section). TRP (%) and iPTH were not significant when the analysis was limited to comp/hom individuals (Table 3, comp/hom only section). Similarly, the univariable GEE model showed that a bone phenotype was more likely to occur in individuals with renal phenotype (Table 4, unadjusted section). Additionally, serum phosphate, 1,25(OH)2D, TmP/GFR, and TRP (%) were found to be predictors for bone phenotype independent of the number of SLC34A3 mutant alleles or ACMG score (Table 4, adjusted section). However, renal phenotype was not independently associated with bone phenotype (Table 4, adjusted section). Serum phosphate, 1,25(OH)2D and TmP/GFR remained significant predictors of bone phenotype when the analysis was limited to comp/hom carriers (Table 4, comp/hom only section).

Although exploratory due to missing values, sensitivity analyses on imputed data using the EM-algorithm and MI procedure verified the robustness of these genotype-dependent and genotype-independent predictors (Supplementary Table S2-4).

Given that TRP (%) is a genotype-independent predictor for renal and bone phenotypes in SLC34A3 pathogenic variant carriers, we next applied a GEE model to analyze the correlation of baseline TRP (%) to baseline parameters. These analyses showed that TRP (%) was positively correlated to serum phosphate and TmP/GFR, and negatively correlated to urine calcium excretion and serum ALP (Supplementary Figure S12a-d). No significant correlations were noted to other baseline parameters (Supplementary Figure S12e-i). Moreover, this evaluation confirmed data from Tables 3 and 4 as patients with decreased TRP (%) had significantly increased incidence of both bone phenotype and renal phenotype (Supplementary Figure S13a-b).

We further determined whether the response to therapy correlates with baseline TRP (%). The GEE model showed that baseline TRP (%) was negatively correlated to delta serum phosphate, delta iPTH, and delta 25(OH)D (Figure 6a-c). Meanwhile, there was a trend that baseline TRP (%) positively correlated to delta 1,25(OH)2D (Figure 6d). No significant correlations to delta serum calcium, urine calcium excretion, serum creatinine, TmP/GFR, TRP (%) or ALP (Supplementary Figure S14a-f) were observed.

A schematic representation (Figure 7a) shows all known SLC34A3 variants identified in individuals with HHRH or idiopathic hypercalciuria (IH). Pathogenic variants that only present with renal phenotypes, and only present with bone phenotypes are highlighted. A third group of pathogenic variants resistant to therapy with oral phosphate supplementation includes the common pathogenic variants S192L and G196, localize to intracellular and extracellular domains in locations that possibly interact with regulatory pathways (Figure 7a-b) as reviewed in9, 71, 72.

Since our last review of HHRH in 20148, the number of known kindreds has tripled. This report identified 83 different pathogenic variants in SLC34A3, of which 49.4% (41/83) are missense pathogenic variants, 28.9% (24/83) are frameshift/stop pathogenic variants and 8.4% (7/83) are intronic deletions. HHRH has been reported on all continents and in multiple races with a high frequency in North America (39.7%) and the Middle East (22.4%). Several pathogenic variants are more common than others, suggesting that there is a founder effect as has been suggested for S192L11 or a variant “hot-spot” caused by unequal cross-over during meiosis due to sequence repeats, i.e. del101 bp50, a deletion observed in multiple continents consistent with independent recombination events.

Overall penetrance is 92% for comp/hom individuals and 50% for het carriers. Because het nucleotide changes that alter the amino acid sequence or introduce deletions/insertions have an estimated prevalence of 1:50071 in SLC34A3, these findings may have important implications for the general population.

Renal calcifications and bone disease in het carriers of this autosomal recessive disorder were not reported in the initial descriptions of HHRH5, 6. However, 22.7% and 38.3% of het carriers present with bone and renal symptoms, respectively. The observation that renal calcifications can be the only presenting symptom in 24.4% of comp/hom and in 27.0% of het individuals establishes SLC34A3 as an important risk gene for nephrocalcinosis and nephrolithiasis as has been proposed by us8 and others73, 74. Different from nephrolithiasis, nephrocalcinosis can be asymptomatic, and its incidental detection should prompt an evaluation of renal phosphate handling once more common causes such as primary hyperparathyroidism are excluded.

Renal cysts were observed in HHRH by us8, 53 and others12, 13, 57, 73 and their number quite low, 11% among those 79 comp/hom individuals and 2% out of those 89 het individuals, who had renal ultrasounds. This is not including the nine recently reported cases12, for which we don’t have the genotype. The pathogenesis of this renal phenotype is unclear but appears to overlap with renal calcifications in most cases and could therefore be caused by micro-obstructions which may in turn activate polycystic kidney disease–associated signaling pathways75. Because also liver cysts appear to be more prevalent73, systemic factors such as 1,25(OH)2D excess may be additional pathogenic factors.

Interestingly, a higher percentage of isolated bone phenotypes was seen in comp/hom children compared to comp/hom adults, which might indicate that this age group is at higher risk for bone disease. Furthermore, isolated bone phenotypes can be seen in 11.4% of het carriers who previously were thought to be only at risk for renal calcifications. Low BMD can improve with phosphate supplementation. Thus, monoallelic SLC34A3 pathogenic variants should be considered in the differential diagnosis of patients with (stress) fractures and low bone density, and bone density of comp/hom is further reduced compared to het individuals.

Finally, it is unknown whether secondary hyperparathyroidism can develop in HHRH as observed in FGF23-dependent hypophosphatemic disorders76. We here show that phosphate supplementation increases iPTH in 22 individuals, and frank secondary hyperparathyroidism developed in three comp/hom and one het individual. Therefore, iPTH should be monitored during phosphate supplementation.

It is unclear which genetic and biochemical criteria best predict the risk of developing renal and bone phenotypes in our HHRH kindreds. Using the larger number of individuals, we confirmed our previous observations8, that SLC34A3 pathogenic variant carrier status increases the risk for renal phenotypes 5.5-fold and for bone phenotypes 7.7-fold. Furthermore, TRP (%) at baseline is an important predictor of presence of renal and bone phenotypes independent of genotype or ACMG score.

Following oral phosphate supplementation, serum phosphate was improved in all 39 evaluated cases, suggesting good compliance and sufficient follow-up time. However, this could not be independently verified or controlled for, and some individuals failed to correct into the normal range for serum phosphate. With these caveats in mind, urine calcium excretion was significantly decreased after phosphate supplementation. However, not all 39 individuals responded with improvement of hypercalciuria and there was no significant difference in serum calcium, 1,25(OH)2D, ALP, creatinine, 25(OH)D, and TRP (%), renal imaging, and BMD due to variable response of these parameters to therapy. Small numbers limit the interpretation of these results, and controlled longitudinal studies are required to clarify whether phosphate supplementation decreases the risk of renal calcifications by attenuating urine calcium excretion or the risk for fractures and bone pain by improving metabolic bone disease and its marker ALP. This is particularly important when metabolic bone disease is absent as phosphate supplementation is often held for concern that it may increase the risk of renal calcification by raising urine phosphate in these individuals.

As mentioned already, renal impairment was only observed at baseline in three individuals (2.8%) and may be explained by other causes. However, an increase in creatinine was observed after phosphate supplementation in 11 (47.8%; Supplementary Figure S3a) carriers of mono- or biallelic SLC34A3 pathogenic variants, for whom pre- and post-treatment information was available. Furthermore, a recent chart review of adult carriers of S192L in an unselected cohort reported an odds ratio of 1.45 for eGFR<60 ml/min when compared to control73. These findings resemble observations in the Npt2a−/− mouse model of HHRH77, which most closely resembles the renal phenotype of HHRH, whereas ablation of Npt2c in mice has little effect due to differences between mice and men78, 79. Taken together, it suggests that renal function on phosphate supplementation needs to be monitored carefully and presence of nephrocalcinosis or renal cysts may affect renal function and prognosis in HHRH.

We noticed that oral phosphate supplementation improved urine calcium excretion in some individuals but not their metabolic bone disease. Others may improve ALP and BMD but continue being hypercalciuric and form renal calcifications, which reflects the clinical experience when treating HHRH. Causes for variable response to therapy may include variable penetrance and expressivity80, 81, and genetic background82, 83. We here tested two additional hypotheses: 1) variant severity based on ACMG score and radioactive phosphate uptake could explain the variable response to therapy, and 2) baseline biochemical parameters that we find predict renal and bone phenotypes independent of genotype may also predict response to therapy.

Different from dominant negative pathogenic variants that are typically located in functionally important hot spots, HHRH pathogenic variants are quite evenly distributed over the length of the NPT2c transporter protein, consistent with loss-of-function84. However, loss-of-function can be caused by different mechanisms: we for example found that several missense pathogenic variants affect membrane expression, others affect transport activity and some impair both when expressing a mutant transporter in X. oocytes, consistent with previous reports for these and other pathogenic variants10, 11, 66, 70. To determine whether the type of SLC34A3 variant is responsible for the variable response to phosphate supplementation, we first showed that the ACMG score correlates well with 33P-uptake. ACMG score furthermore predicts disease severity at presentation. However, no correlation between variant severity and response to therapy was observed, which may indicate that non-genetic modifiers exist that determine response to therapy in HHRH.

Indeed, TRP (%) at baseline correlates with the risk of developing bone and renal phenotypes after controlling for genotype. Furthermore, individuals with low TRP (%) had a better response to phosphate supplementation. It is possible that individuals with low TRP (%) predominantly suffer from a defect in phosphate reabsorption. Conversely, individuals with higher TRP (%) may be resistant to phosphate-sensing in the proximal tubules. Studies in the Npt2a−/− mouse model of HHRH showed that high phosphate diet is unable stimulate expression of Cyp24a185. New research suggests that loss of phosphate transport in the proximal tubules modifies the regulation of FGF23 via glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P)86. It is conceivable that oral phosphate supplementation can correct serum phosphate in individuals with a sensing defect, and, thus, normalize metabolic bone disease. However, phosphate supplementation may fail to stimulate G3P86 and expression of CYP24A185 inside the proximal tubular cells. As a result, these individuals continue to have higher 1,25(OH)2D, and suppressed iPTH levels that sustain their hypercalciuria. In these cases, when FGF23 remains suppressed despite correction of serum phosphate and metabolic bone disease, supplementation of G3P or lysophosphatidic acid (LPA)87, blocking CYP27B1 with fluconazole54, or stimulation of CYP3A4 (an alternate disposal pathway for calcitriol) with rifampin88, 89 may have a role in the therapy of HHRH. Candidate incomplete-responder pathogenic variants are listed in Supplementary Figure S4, which includes carriers of the common pathogenic variants S192L and G196R. Interestingly, these pathogenic variants might localize to the intra- and extracellular presumed regulatory domains of the NPT2c transporter protein as reviewed in9, 71, 72.

Compared to our last update on HHRH8, the present study significantly increased the sample size. Regardless, small numbers and incomplete datasets remain a limitation, especially for evaluating response to therapy, although the imputation analysis verified the robustness of identified predictors for renal and bone phenotype. Additionally, this retrospective analysis does not have detailed information regarding dosage, compliance and treatment duration for those individuals receiving oral phosphate supplementation. However, the finding that all individuals corrected their serum phosphate levels suggests sufficient dosage, compliance and duration. Furthermore, our finding that heterozygous carriers have bone and renal phenotypes may be due to failure to detect variants on the second allele or due to the presence of SLC34A1 digenic pathogenic variants49. Meanwhile, family members of index cases, who are heterozygous for a SLC34A3 variant or who carry no variant have been less intensively studied, raising concerns of selection bias. Finally, the specific application of ACMG scores to comp/hom pathogenic variants may require careful consideration and interpretation, as the combination of two or more pathogenic variants can have complex effects on a gene’s function and may require confirmation by functional analysis.

In summary, we here present 20 previously unreported HHRH kindreds in whom SLC34A3 nucleotide sequence analysis identified two novel pathogenic variants. Review of the clinical presentation of the currently known 145 HHRH kindreds suggests that heterozygous carriers can develop not only renal but also bone phenotypes. The ACMG score correlates with functional analysis of frequent SLC34A3 pathogenic variants in vitro and baseline disease severity but not with response to therapy. The number of mutant alleles and baseline TRP (%) were identified as predictors for renal and bone phenotypes, baseline TRP (%) furthermore predicts response to therapy. Certain SLC34A3/NPT2c pathogenic variants can be identified with partial responses to therapy, whereas with some overlap others present only with renal phenotypes, and a third group presents only with bone phenotypes. Our report highlights important novel clinical aspects of HHRH and heterozygous carriers. It raises awareness to this rare group of disorders and will be a foundation for future studies urgently needed to guide therapy of HHRH.
