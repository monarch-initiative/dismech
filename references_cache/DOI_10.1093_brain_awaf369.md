# Atrophy progression in frontotemporal lobar degeneration-TDP-C with primary progressive aphasia
**Authors:** Elena Barbieri, Allegra S Kawles, Michelle Los, Jordan Behn, Changiz Geula, Tamar Gefen, Sandra Weintraub, M Marsel Mesulam
**Journal:** Brain (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1093/brain/awaf369](https://doi.org/10.1093/brain/awaf369)

## Content

Abstract
Clinicopathologic correlations in neurodegenerative diseases have led to novel insights on the neurobiology of selective vulnerability and the anatomy of cognitive networks. The neuropathologic entity of frontotemporal lobar degeneration with abnormal precipitates of the transactive response DNA binding protein TDP-43 (FTLD-TDP) of type C (TDP-C) is one of the most distinctive examples. TDP-C invariably starts with neurodegeneration (atrophy) confined to the temporopolar regions (TPR). The process is usually asymmetric, causing behavioral abnormalities and associative agnosia when predominantly right-sided, semantic Primary Progressive Aphasia (PPA) when left-sided, and semantic dementia when bilateral.
In this study, we investigated the relationship between atrophy progression and the progressive dissolution of word comprehension in TDP-C patients with asymmetric left TPR degeneration. For the sake of homogeneity, and in order to use a common yardstick of functional progression, we focused on patients with leftward asymmetry and initially isolated verbal impairment. Using data from 24 visits with structural MRI scans and specialized tests of naming, word definition, and word-to-picture matching, we stratified the anatomy of peak degeneration sites according to three increasingly more advanced stages of impaired noun representations. According to this pattern of progression, an initial stage of relatively isolated anomia is followed by additional intra-category blurring of word meaning and, at still more advanced stages, inter-category blurring. Voxel Based Morphometry (VBM) maps of gray matter volume were binarized to identify regions most frequently atrophied at each of these stages.
Results illustrate that the progressive dissolution of word meaning is associated with caudal progression of atrophy from the left temporopolar cortex at initial stages to more posterior fusiform, lateral temporal and temporooccipital regions in later stages. The stratification of progressive atrophy by precisely characterized stages of word comprehension impairment, rather than by estimated time of symptom onset, offers a functional rather than just chronological anatomy of progression for TDP-C and a clearer view of the relationship between TPR components and the cognitive mapping of word representations.

Keywords:primary progressive aphasia, frontotemporal lobar degeneration, atrophy, temporal lobe, conceptual knowledge

The two major families of frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD) are known as tauopathies (FTLD-tau) and transactive response DNA binding proteinopathies (FTLD-TDP). Among all species of FTLD-TDP, the “Type C” variant (TDP-C) stands out through its invariable predilection for the temporopolar region (TPR) as the initial site of neurodegeneration. Recent findings have underscored the uniqueness of TDP-C by showing that the abnormal pathological inclusions observed at post-mortem contain not only TDP-43 but also annexin 11 (ANXA11).1,2No other FTLD-TDP subtype displays an obligatory co-aggregation with ANXA11 and no other FTLD-TDP subtype consistently presents with TPR atrophy. These features make TDP-C particularly well suited for investigating the neurobiology of selective vulnerability as well as the behavioral anatomy of TPR.

The initial TPR degeneration in TDP-C is most frequently asymmetric, giving rise to at least three distinctive syndromes. Atrophy that predominantly encompasses the language-dominant hemisphere (usually left) is associated with semantic variant primary progressive aphasia (svPPA), a syndrome where object naming and word comprehension are severely impaired on a background of relatively preserved non-verbal face and object recognition at initial stages.3,4In contrast, predominantly right-sided atrophy can give rise to progressive associative agnosias for faces and objects or behavioral abnormalities. Bilateral atrophy is frequently associated with the syndrome of semantic dementia (SD), a condition where word and object recognition are both impaired and where behavioral disturbance may also be prominent5–9. Nearly 90% of svPPA and SD cases are caused by TDP-C, so these clinical diagnoses have been used as surrogates for the presumptive diagnosis of underlying TDP-C neuropathology.

The most prominent feature of svPPA is a decline of word comprehension in all modalities of input.10–12The underlying weakness of verbal conceptual knowledge is initially manifested as a relatively isolated but severe naming difficulty (anomia), presumably reflecting perturbations in the multimodal representation of nouns denoting the desired object.13This contrasts with the retrieval anomia seen in other forms of PPA, especially logopenic, where the problem lies at the stage of accessing phonological word representations that appear semantically intact. The initial anomic stages of svPPA are followed by more profound word comprehension deficits during disease progression.3,14These subsequent stages are characterized first by progressive blurring of conceptual boundaries that distinguish the meaning of nouns denoting members of the same category (intra-category blurring) and, later, by blurring of boundaries that distinguish the meaning of nouns denoting members of different categories (inter-category blurring).15–18

There have been several reports on the progression of neurodegeneration in svPPA/SD. In one,19using a region-of-interest approach, the authors found atrophy encompassing the entire left temporal lobe, the left amygdala and the right medial temporal lobe in all individuals at their earliest neuroimaging assessment but at variable stages of severity. In a case-based study where clinical severity was more clearly defined,3a participant with autopsy-confirmed TDP-C was found to have atrophy limited to the left TPR. In the same study, two participants with the full clinical picture of SD showed a bilateral atrophy pattern that also included the fusiform gyrus, a finding that is in line with the evidence that the bilateral neurodegeneration of SD also leads to visual associative agnosia and prosopagnosia (i.e., difficulty in recognizing objects and faces).20One study21that employed a more fine-grained parcellation of the anterior temporal lobe (defined as in Papinutto et al.22) showed that atrophy first appeared in inferior-medial and superior-medial regions of TPR, followed by inferior-lateral and superior-lateral regions.

Most published reports4,19,21,23offer a duration-based staging of neurodegeneration in verified or presumptive cases of TDP-C. However, individuals who report similar durations of disease may show widely different patterns of atrophy due to variability in the speed of progression and the susceptibility (or cognitive reserve) of the patient. Furthermore, the timing of symptom onset is fraught with considerable uncertainty, subject to the perceptiveness of the patient and family. In the present study on verified TDP-C cases, we identified progression stages not by estimated time of onset but by aligning them with performance on neurolinguistic measures of word comprehension along the stepwise progression described above (see also24for a similar approach to the neuropsychological investigation of surface dyslexia). The resultant map integrates the anatomy of neurodegeneration with the progression of neurolinguistic impairments.

Eighteen participants with a clinical diagnosis of semantic (n=17) or mixed PPA (n=1) and autopsy-confirmed TDP-C as the primary pathologic diagnosis were identified from the Northwestern PPA Program. PPA clinical diagnosis was determined by a behavioral neurologist and required a clinical history of progressive language impairment without consequential decline in other cognitive domains within the initial stages of the disease.11,12Further classification into PPA subtypes was based on neuropsychological and neurological tests, guided by the criteria of Gorno-Tempini et al. (2011) and Mesulam et al. (2009).10,25Written informed consent was obtained from all participants who committed to brain donation, and biologic specimens were stored within the NIH-funded Northwestern University Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center Brain Bank. Post-autopsy preparation followed previously published protocols.26,27The study was approved by the Northwestern University ethical committee and was conducted in full compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The pathologic diagnosis of FTLD and specification of its variants was rendered by neuropathologists using published consensus criteria of the Consortium for FTLD.28All but one participant was right-handed.

Participation in the PPA program included neuropsychological testing and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at biannual intervals; testing and imaging data were longitudinally collected until a participant became too cognitively impaired to successfully complete either. Of the eighteen unique participants, data from one to four study visits were available. For each participant, data were included only from visits for which both a high-resolution 3T structural MRI and performance on the Northwestern Multidimensional Naming Assessment (NOMINA) battery were available (see below). In addition, because the purpose of the study was to trace the progression of atrophy in TDP-C as it relates to disruptions in conceptual knowledge, only participants who exhibited predominantly leftward hemisphere atrophy, indicating lateralization of the language network to the left hemisphere, were included in the study. Two participants (both right-handed) did not meet these criteria and were therefore excluded for the purposes of this study. A total of 16 unique participants (15 with svPPA, 1 with mixed PPA;Table 1) and 24 total visits fulfilled these criteria. Of the 24 visits included in the study, 15 were first visits, and 9 were 2-year or 4-year follow-up visits.

Participants were administered several standardized language measures as part of the Northwestern PPA program, as described in other papers.25For inclusion in the present study, we utilized measures derived from the Northwestern Multidimensional Naming Assessment (NOMINA,Figure 1A)15, a test battery that assesses several aspects of naming and object knowledge for 20 objects belonging to one of four semantic categories (animals, tools, food, clothing) using three tasks with varying level of difficulty: object naming (most difficult), word definition (intermediate difficulty), and word-to-picture matching (least difficult). Participants were classified based on performance for the “animal” category on the NOMINA, as this category appeared to pose the greatest difficulty for patients across disease stages (seeSupplementary Table 1). Prior studies also show that the semantic distinction between living items tend to be affected early in the course of the disease.29–31Based on performance for “animals”, participants were classified as belonging to one of the following stages at a given visit.

Impairment in object naming, but intact word comprehension; naming impairments were indicated by poor performance on the Boston Naming Test (BNT; a test of confrontation naming)32and the naming portion of the NOMINA. Impaired performance on the BNT was defined as < 90% correct, based on previously established norms.25The same cut-off was applied to all the three subtests of the NOMINA.

Both naming and word comprehension are impaired; word definitions and word-to-picture matching indicate difficulties distinguishing between members of the same semantic category. Predominance of within-category semantic substitutions on the NOMINA word-to-picture matching subtest (e.g.,camelforlion), and responses describing within-category non-target items on the NOMINA word definitions subtest were considered as indicative of intra-category blurring.

Both naming and word comprehension are impaired; word definitions and word-to-picture matching indicate difficulties distinguishing between members of different semantic categories. Predominance of outside-category semantic substitutions (e.g.,camelforpumpkin), vague responses, or no responses on the NOMINA word-to-picture matching subtest, and responses that either described outside-category non-target items or were too vague to identify the item were considered as indicative of intra-category blurring.

In addition to the NOMINA, participants’ language and cognitive abilities were assessed using a variety of tests. SeeSupplementary Materialsfor a complete list of tests and for a description of the guidelines used for interpretation.

As part of the Northwestern PPA Program, structural MRI scans were obtained within 72 h of the cognitive testing for every participant. Details regarding image acquisition and pre-processing are provided in theSupplementary Materials.

For each participant at each visit, individual atrophy maps were generated using Voxel-Based Morphometry (VBM)33by comparing gray matter volume maps obtained from each participant to those obtained from a group of healthy age-matched individuals scanned on the same scanner (n=43 for Trio, n=40 for Prisma). Data were first modeled using a two-sample t-test to generate simple contrasts (e.g., healthy > patient); non-parametric statistical comparisons were then run using the threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE,https://github.com/ChristianGaser/tfce), which does not require setting an arbitrary cluster-defining threshold and is based on the idea of combining both focal effects with high voxel values and broader effects with lower voxel values. Maps generated using TFCE (n = 5000 permutations) were then corrected for multiple comparisons using a family-wise threshold (FWE) at p < .1.

Maps obtained from individuals at each time point were then binarized and grouped together based on the defined stage of taxonomic blurring. For this purpose, each map (even when obtained from the same individual at different time points) was considered as an independent data point. There were no instances where two maps obtained from the same participant at different time points (e.g., baseline visit and 2-year follow-up) were included in the same stage. Separately for each stage group (anomic, intra-category blurring, inter-category blurring), binarized atrophy maps were overlaid onto each other to identify regions where all individuals at that stage showed significant atrophy. Within Stage I, regions where all participants exhibited significant atrophy were considered the earliest target of TDP-C pathology. Within Stage II and Stage III, regions that were atrophied in all participants within that stage and that were not atrophied in the preceding stage illustrated the progression of TDP-C. Stage-based overlays were visualized in CAT12 (seeSupplementary Materialsfor details).

Associations between atrophy and verbal and non-verbal conceptual impairments were also assessed using a VBM regression approach. For this purpose, analyses were run on a larger dataset (N = 127) of individuals included in the Northwestern PPA program database who had a clinical diagnosis of PPA and for whom an MRI scan and performance on the NOMINA battery were available at their baseline assessment. Details regarding the VBM analysis procedures are provided in theSupplementary Materials.

In all cases, TDP-C was the primary pathologic diagnosis at autopsy, consistent with the tight clinicopathologic correlation between svPPA and TDP-C.4,34,35At early disease stages, peak areas of atrophy are circumscribed to the left temporopolar regions, which includes the left temporopolar cap and anterior fusiform gyrus (Figure 2A). At this stage, the posterior left temporal lobe and right temporal lobe are relatively unaffected. Autopsy occurred 10+ years after these initial conditions, so there is often no consistent overlap between these sites of maximal neuronal loss (atrophy) and sites of maximal TDP inclusions.26There are case-based exceptions as well. For example, in one case with left fusiform but not superior temporal atrophy at very early stages of svPPA, autopsy 10 years later found the left fusiform gyrus to show a much higher density of TDP neurites than the left superior temporal gyrus (Figure 2).

All the nine individuals in Stage I exhibited atrophy in the left temporopolar region (TPR,Figure 4AandFigure 5) encompassing the pole (area TG), the most anterior portion of the superior temporal gyrus and sulcus (aSTG/STS), as well as the anterior third of the middle and inferior temporal gyri (aMTG/ITG). In addition, atrophy was present in all participants in the para-insular and anterior insular areas, as well as in the piriform cortex (Pir), planum polare, anterior fusiform gyrus, and the posterior ventral portion of the pars orbitalis of the inferior frontal gyrus (IFGorb). On the medial surface, areas of atrophy overlap in all participants were observed in the perirhinal cortex (PRC). The hippocampus and amygdala were not among the areas of consistent peak atrophy at Stage I.

Compared to participants in Stage I, all six individuals in Stage II exhibited additional areas of atrophy (Figure 4BandFigure 5) in the left lateral and medial temporal lobe, as well as in limbic areas. Areas that were atrophied in Stage II (and not in Stage I) included the anterior and posterior portion of the dorsal STG. Additional areas of atrophy were found on the lateral temporal lobe, namely in the mid-posterior portion of the MTG and ITG (midMTG/ITG) and within the mid-portion of the fusiform gyrus (midFG). On the medial surface, atrophy was found across all individuals in Stage II in the hippocampus, parahippocampal area (PHA), pre-subiculum (PreS), entorhinal cortex (EC), and lateral and medial nuclei of the amygdala (Figure 4B).

Compared to participants in Stage II, all the four individuals in Stage III exhibited additional areas of atrophy (Figure 4C) in the insular-opercular region, the auditory association cortex and the planum temporale. In addition, atrophy was present in all participants in the posterior portion of the STG/STS. Furthermore, additional areas of atrophy were found within the posterior portion of the ITG (pITG) and posterior lateral fusiform gyrus (overlapping with most anatomical definitions of visual word form area, VWFA43–45). Some individuals (3 out of 4) exhibited atrophy at the right TPR, representing the first region affected in the right hemisphere. Maps for individuals falling in between Stage II and Stage III are provided in theSupplementary Materials(Supplementary Figure 1).

To identify regions of atrophy associated with verbal conceptual knowledge, VBM regressions were performed on the NOMINA naming total score; analyses were conducted with and without accounting for performance on the PPT picture version (Figure 6Aand6B, respectively). As shown inFigure 6A, areas associated with NOMINA naming accuracy after accounting for performance on the PPT pictures included the lateral and medial surfaces of the left anterior temporal pole, with peak statistics falling within the left aSTS, PRC/EC and aFG. Areas of association also encompassed the left TPR, aMTG/ITG, the insula and the IFGorb on the lateral surface, and the parahippocampal area on the medial surface of the left hemisphere. Subcortical associations with the left lateral amygdala and head of the left hippocampus were also found. When performance on the PPT pictures was not accounted for (Figure 6B), additional areas of association were also evident. In the left hemisphere, areas of significant association included the pITG/VWFA, and the pSTG/STS. Most notably, significant areas of association were also found in the right hemisphere, with peak association falling within the TPR, aSTG/STS and piriform cortex. The work reported here is based on a relatively homogeneous population of 18 participants with the left hemisphere predominant form of TDP-C based on postmortem brain autopsy. The results address the functional anatomy of the left anterior temporal lobe and the location of the components with the earliest susceptibility to TDP-C.

The common denominator at the mildest stages of svPPA was the presence of moderate-to-severe difficulties in naming objects, even high-frequency items. As noted above, the confrontation naming test entails a heavier load of information processing than the other two components of the NOMINA, perhaps due to the mapping between verbal conceptual knowledge and phonological representations being arbitrary.13It is therefore not surprising that individuals in Stage I did not necessarily make errors in the word definition or word to object matching tasks. However, their word definitions were incomplete or somewhat vague (e.g., P4, defining the word “lion”: “…that’s another animal. See, I don’t know which one those are. That’s another animal… he’s one of those in Europe, China, or in things like that”). These findings show that the initial profound impairment of object naming in left-predominant TDP-C is at least partially caused by a lack of precision (i.e., blurring) of verbal conceptual representations, as previously suggested.13,46,47In contrast, naming deficits in other variants of PPA tend to reflect failures of retrieval even when conceptual representations are otherwise relatively intact16.

At Stage II, the anomia became accompanied by intra-category blurring of word meaning, where the representations of words denoting objects of the same class were less sharply delineated. In contrast, representations of words denoting exemplars of different categories maintained their boundaries. At this stage, mispointing errors in the word object matching task were almost always directed to objects of the same semantic class, as also reported in previous studies on semantic dementia.48,49In addition, non-verbal knowledge for both faces and objects (faces > objects) was impaired across all participants in Stage II. We note that the performance on the NOMINA word-to-picture matching, which includes only high-frequency, “easy” objects, was more severely impaired than performance on the PPT pictures task, which includes a larger number of low-frequency items.

In Stage III, inter-category blurring of word meaning became conspicuous and PPT performance further declined, indicating that both verbal and non-verbal knowledge became equally compromised. These patterns suggest that the dissolution of verbal conceptual knowledge begins in Stage I and becomes fully evident in Stage II with the emergence of intra-category blurring. In contrast, the dissolution of non-verbal conceptual knowledge is consistently observed across participants only at Stage II, where it remains less impaired than verbal conceptual knowledge. Only in Stage III are both non-verbal and verbal conceptual knowledge become equally impaired. Together, these findings indicate that while some individuals may exhibit non-verbal conceptual impairment in the early stages of the disease, impairment of non-verbal conceptual knowledge follows that of verbal conceptual knowledge.

Surface dyslexia emerged at Stage II and indicated a disruption of the ventrally located route of “whole-word” reading, which relies on access to verbal conceptual knowledge.50In our study, surface dyslexia was not consistently found in the early stages of TDP-C with svPPA, as reported in other studies.24,51,52While our pool of selected items included a mix of high-frequency and low-frequency items, as well as both one- and two-syllable words in line with previous recommendations24,51, we cannot exclude the possibility that our limited number of selected items may have underestimated the presence of surface dyslexia in Stage I. By Stage III, reading impairment extended to all types of written words. Repetition impairment was consistently found across participants only in Stage III, indicating preserved phonological processing skills up to Stage II. Throughout all stages, comprehension of sentences based on syntactic cues (as indexed by performance on the NAVS) remained largely spared, reflecting preserved syntactic processing even when conceptual knowledge was severely impaired.

Another main domain of impairment was behavior. Changes in behavior emerged in all participants starting in Stage II, when prominent atrophy was also seen in the amygdala. The most frequently endorsed behavioral symptoms were apathy and changes in dietary habits. These results are in line with recent observations that highlight the pervasiveness of changes in behavior in svPPA and SD, which are quantitatively (but not qualitatively) different from those defining the clinical presentation of behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD)53,54. Non-verbal memory was largely preserved, and dementia ratings remained within the range of questionable to mild throughout Stage III.

The areas consistently atrophied at Stage I are presumably also those affected most severely at the onset of TDP-C. These included the temporal pole (area TG), the planum polare, the most anterior portions of the superior, middle, and inferior temporal gyri. In addition, atrophy was present in all participants in the anterior fusiform gyrus, perirhinal cortex, mid-to-anterior insular areas, and the posterior portion of the pars orbitalis of the inferior frontal gyrus (IFGorb). Our approach cannot clarify whether only one or all of these areas are essential to sustain verbal conceptual knowledge. Further, this set of areas encompasses interconnected limbic, paralimbic and association cortices. Based on this heterogeneity, it is not yet possible to determine whether TDP-C has a predilection for a specific cytoarchitecture or cell type. Progression does occur along the trajectories of the uncinate and inferior longitudinal fasciculi, but the mode of spread (contiguously or network selectively) remains to be determined. The one remarkable common denominator is the radial emanation of peak atrophy from a circumpolar focus at a distance of no greater than 2–3 centimeters from the pole. Accordingly, there were no TDP-C cases where the first visible atrophy seemed to skip the polar end of the temporal lobe. Future work with site specific proteomics and transcriptomics could potentially clarify distinctive features that confine the initial TDP-C predilection to this circumpolar orbit.

In Stage II, atrophy extended dorsally and caudally into the superior, middle, inferior temporal and fusiform gyri. Individuals in Stage II also exhibited entorhinal, hippocampal and amygdaloid atrophy. In Stage III, atrophy was found also in the left posterior STG and planum temporale. In addition, atrophy was found across all participants in the posterior fusiform gyrus (including the visual word form area, VWFA) and the most anterior ventral portion of the occipital lobe. By Stage III, atrophy had become bilateral, with the progression pattern on the right hemisphere lagging behind that of the left but mirroring it in anatomical detail.

Compared to other studies in the literature3,4,19,21, the current paper reveals a more focal and more strictly left-lateralized pattern of initial TDP-C neurodegeneration in svPPA. Some of the differences noted between this and other studies may also stem from differences in inclusion criteria (individuals with the non-verbal recognition impairments of SD were included in some studies19,21but excluded from the present study) or in methodology (a region-of-interest approach in some19,21versus a whole-brain analysis approach in the present study).

It is safe to conclude that the set of areas consistently atrophied at Stage I is critical for the representation of nouns at a level of precision that enables object naming. This conclusion is consistent with the connectivity of the anterior temporal regions, which collectively constitute the downstream confluence site of multi-synaptic pathways that link auditory and visual word forms to their distributed non-verbal and experiential associations. The anatomical substrates of the further impairments in Stages II and III can be attributed to the additional areas of atrophy. However, informatics also needs to be considered. Thus, if a certain load of synaptic activity is necessary to recognize a noun at the generic level (e.g., as referring to an ‘animal’), greater synaptic processing will be necessary to recognize the same word at the additional, specific level of meaning (e.g., as referring to a ‘lion’). Hence, the intensification of the comprehension deficit from Stage I to Stage II may also reflect additional loss of synaptic volume at sites of atrophy seen in Stage I.

The results of this study align with the extensive evidence linking impaired15,55,56- and more generally impaired verbal conceptual knowledge16,55,57–60- to left TPR atrophy. As noted above, atrophy of the left TPR is sufficient to yield severe naming deficits and, in most cases albeit not all, incomplete and/or vague word definitions. In later stages of the TDP-C, atrophy progression to the mid-posterior MTG/ITG and anterior fusiform gyrus (in Stage II) co-occurred with a blurring of the boundaries that differentiated the meaning of nouns denoting exemplars of the same category (e.g., naming a ‘hippopotamus’ a ‘camel’, or defining the word ‘strawberry’ as something big and red that you pick from a tree (describing an apple)). As discrimination between (and definition of) members of animals and many edibles heavily relies on visual features,61,62disruption of the visual ventral object recognition pathway (on which both the MTG/ITG and the fusiform lie) may be at the root of intra-category blurring and may also explain why some semantic categories are affected earlier than others. However, it is important to keep in mind that the deepening impairment of noun-object associations in svPPA is likely to reflect a further and critical disruption of the visual representations from verbal representations through the left TPR. Non-verbal representations are supported bilaterally, which is why non-verbal object recognition is spared during the initial years of svPPA.59,63As atrophy became more bilateral and progressed to the ventral occipital lobe in Stage III, non-verbal conceptual knowledge also became impaired. These conclusions are supported by our VBM regression analyses, in which we demonstrated that naming difficulties on the NOMINA were associated with atrophy in the left anterior temporal lobe when accounting for loss of non-verbal (object) conceptual knowledge, specifically in a set of regions that showed large overlap with regions of atrophy that defined Stage II. Conversely, when the effects of non-verbal (object) conceptual knowledge loss on naming performance were not accounted for, associations between naming impairment and atrophy extended to posterior temporal and anterior inferior occipital regions in the left hemisphere, and also affected the TPR, perirhinal cortex and anterior fusiform gyrus in the right hemisphere. These same right-hemisphere regions, together with the insula, were also the brain areas in which the association between atrophy and loss of non-verbal conceptual knowledge was the strongest.

With respect to non-language areas, early involvement of the left anterior insula and piriform cortex suggests a possible link between atrophy of these regions and the changes in dietary preferences and olfaction that are frequently reported in the literature on svPPA.64–74Furthermore, our results showed extensive atrophy, starting in Stage II, in the left parahippocampal region and entorhinal cortex. Episodic memory is spared in svPPA despite the entorhino-hippocampal atrophy probably because this function is bilaterally represented. In fact, verbal memory is severely impaired in svPPA with TDP-C. Stage II also witnessed atrophy progression to the left amygdala, a structure that plays a pivotal role in emotional regulation and in modulating stress responses.75–78As such, the prominent atrophy of the amygdala in Stage II and beyond may underlie the emergence of changes in mood and behavior as shown in our study and previous literature.66–68

The present study bears some limitations. First, the small number of participants poses limitations to the generalization of the reported findings. Future research will seek to replicate the present findings in a large cohort of autopsy-confirmed participants. The lack of functional and structural connectivity is also a major limitation of the current study. Future reports should plan to include longitudinal data on structural and functional connectivity to improve our understanding of network-based disease spread and its link to clinical and neuropsychological progression.

The study provides arguably the first symptom-based description of the neuropsychological and anatomical trajectory of autopsy-proven TDP-C in individuals with PPA. The results underscore the preference of TDP-C for the temporopolar region in the early stages of the disease and reveal a pattern of atrophy progression that follows an anterior-to-posterior gradient within the temporal cortex and extends to the left amygdala and hippocampal head, before moving to the contralateral temporopolar region. The anatomical trajectory of the disease closely follows the progressive dissolution of verbal semantics, which supports the hypothesis that the left anterior temporal lobe provides a neural route to verbal conceptual knowledge.
