itemprop="text"><div id="dup15q.Summary" itemprop="description"><h2 id="_dup15q_Summary_">Summary</h2><div><h4 class="inline">Clinical characteristics.</h4><p>Maternal 15q <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a> syndrome (maternal dup15q) is characterized by hypotonia and motor delays, intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and epilepsy including infantile spasms. Rarely, maternal dup15q may also be associated with psychosis or sudden unexplained death. The extra copy or copies most commonly arise by one of two mechanisms:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>A maternal isodicentric 15q11.2-q13.1 supernumerary <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/chromosome/">chromosome</a> &#x02013; idic(15) &#x02013; typically comprising two extra copies of 15q11.2-q13.1 and resulting in tetrasomy for 15q11.2-q13.1 (~60-80%);</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>A maternal interstitial 15q11.2-q13.1 <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a> that typically includes one extra copy of 15q11.2-q13.1 within <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/chromosome/">chromosome</a> 15, resulting in trisomy for 15q11.2-q13.1 (~20-40%).</div></li></ul></div><div><h4 class="inline">Management.</h4><p><i>Treatment of manifestations:</i> Multidisciplinary team evaluation of motor and speech development and to assist in referrals for appropriate educational programs. Supportive care may include: feeding therapy, occupational and physical therapy, alternative and augmentative communication, behavioral therapy (e.g., applied behavioral analysis therapy), psychotropic medications for behavioral manifestations, and standard management for seizures.</p><p><i>Surveillance:</i> Growth and nutritional assessment at each visit. Periodic: neurodevelopmental and/or developmental/behavioral assessments, and monitoring for evidence of seizures and/or change in seizure type.</p><p><i>Agents/circumstances to avoid:</i> Seizure triggers (e.g., sleep deprivation, stress).</p><p><i>Evaluation of relatives at risk:</i> Consider genetic testing of sibs of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> (known to be at increased risk for an inherited maternal interstitial 15q11.2-q13.1 <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a>) in order to refer those with the interstitial duplication promptly for multidisciplinary team evaluation and developmental support.</p></div><div><h4 class="inline">Genetic counseling.</h4><p>Maternal dup15q caused by:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Maternal idic(15).</b>
<i>De novo</i> in all affected individuals reported to date; thus, risk to sibs is low, but presumed to be marginally greater than in the general population because of the possibility of maternal <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline-mosaicism/">germline mosaicism</a>;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Maternal interstitial 15q11.2-q13.1 <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a>.</b>
<i>De novo</i> in approximately 85% of probands and inherited from the mother in approximately 15%. If the mother has the 15q interstitial duplication, the risk to each child of inheriting the duplication is 50%.</div></li></ul><p>Prenatal testing or <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a> using <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/chromosomal-microarray/">chromosomal microarray</a> (CMA) will detect the 15q interstitial <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a>; however, prenatal test results cannot reliably predict the severity of the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> even in a pregnancy known to be at increased risk for maternal dup15q.</p></div></div><div id="dup15q.GeneReview_Scope"><h2 id="_dup15q_GeneReview_Scope_"><i>GeneReview</i> Scope</h2><div id="dup15q.Tc" class="table"><p class="large-table-link" style="display:none"><span class="right"><a href="/books/NBK367946/table/dup15q.Tc/?report=objectonly" target="object">View in own window</a></span></p><div class="large_tbl" id="__dup15q.Tc_lrgtbl__"><table class="no_bottom_margin"><thead><tr><th id="hd_h_dup15q.Tc_1_1_1_1" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">Maternal 15q Duplication Syndrome: Included Genetic Mechanisms</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td headers="hd_h_dup15q.Tc_1_1_1_1" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;"><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Maternal isodicentric 15q11.2-q13.1 supernumerary <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/chromosome/">chromosome</a> [idic(15)] resulting in tetrasomy or hexasomy for 15q11.2-q13.1</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Maternal interstitial 15q11.2-q13.1 <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a> or triplication</div></li></ul>

suspected</b> in individuals with the following clinical features:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Moderate-to-severe hypotonia in infancy and early delays in reaching motor class="half_rhythm"><div>Behavioral difficulties including hyperactivity, anxiety, or emotional lability [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.al_ageeli.2014.5">Al Ageeli et al 2014</a>]</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>A characteristic EEG biomarker involving excessive beta oscillations (12&#x02013;30&#x02009;Hz) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.frohlich.2016.e0167179">Frohlich et al Diagnosis</h3><p>The diagnosis of maternal dup15q <b>is established</b> in a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> by detection of at least one extra <b>maternally</b> derived copy of the Prader-Willi/Angelman <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/critical-region/">critical region</a> (PWACR), a region approximately 5 Mb long within target="object" rid-ob="figobdup15qTgenomictestingusedinmaterna">Table 1</a>).</p><p>The proximal 15q region includes five regions of segmental duplications or low copy repeats (designated by breakpoints [BPs]), which result in increased susceptibility to <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genomic/">genomic</a> rearrangements [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.hogart.2010.181">Hogart et al 2010</a>]. Although CMA results may indicate whether the duplication is interstitial, this is not common.</p></div></dd><dt>7. </dt><dd><div id="dup15q.TF.1.7"><p class="no_margin">Targeted <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a> analysis methods can include <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/fish/">FISH</a>, <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/quantitative-pcr/">quantitative PCR</a> (qPCR), and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) as well as other targeted quantitative methods.</p></div></dd><dt>8. </dt><dd><div id="dup15q.TF.1.8"><p class="no_margin">Targeted <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a> analysis is not appropriate for diagnosis of an individual in whom the 15q11.2-q13.1 duplication was not detected by CMA designed to target this region.</p></div></dd></dl></div></div></div></div></div><div id="dup15q.Clinical_Characteristics"><h2 id="_dup15q_Clinical_Characteristics_">Clinical Characteristics</h2><div id="dup15q.Clinical_Description"><h3>Clinical Description</h3><p>Maternal 15q <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a> syndrome (maternal dup15q) is characterized by hypotonia and motor delays, intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and epilepsy including infantile spasms. These clinical findings differ significantly between people with a maternal interstitial duplication and those with a maternal isodicentric supernumerary <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/chromosome/">chromosome</a>, or idic(15) (<a href="/books/NBK367946/table/dup15q.T.maternal_15q_interstitial_dupli/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobdup15qTmaternal15qinterstitialdupli">Table 2</a>). Those with a maternal idic(15) are typically more severely affected than those with an interstitial duplication. However, severity varies even among individuals who have increased dosage by the same genetic mechanism. <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.conant.2014.396">Conant et al [2014]</a>
</p></div></dd></dl></div></div></div><p><b>Hypotonia and motor skills.</b> Hypotonia in newborns and infants with maternal dup15q is associated with feeding difficulties and gross motor delays [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.depienne.2009.349">Depienne et al 2009</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.hogart.2010.181">Hogart et al 2010</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.urraca.2013.268">Urraca et al 2013</a>]. Hypotonia also contributes to gastrointestinal issues in maternal dup15q, such as constipation.</p><p>Although childhood hypotonia impairs motor development, most children achieve independent walking after age two to three years (younger in children with an interstitial <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a>) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.hogart.2010.181">Hogart et al 2010</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.piard.2010.1933">Piard et al 2010</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.al_ageeli.2014.5">Al Ageeli et al 2014</a>].</p><p>A wide-based or ataxic gait is common [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.bundey.1994.736">Bundey et al 1994</a>]. Delays and persistent impairment in both fine and gross motor skills affect adaptive living skills and distinguish children with maternal dup15q from children with nonsyndromic autism spectrum disorder [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.distefano.2016.19">DiStefano et al 2016</a>].</p><p><b>Developmental delay and intellectual disability.</b> Developmental delay in early childhood is nearly universal. This can be more specifically diagnosed as intellectual disability after age five years.</p><p>In addition to motor delays, speech and language development is particularly affected, with universal delays ranging from moderate to severe [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.hogart.2010.181">Hogart et al 2010</a>]. Some individuals exhibit echolalia, pronoun reversal, and stereotyped utterances, while others may lack functional speech [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.battaglia.1997.1081">Battaglia et al 1997</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.battaglia.2008.30">Battaglia 2008</a>].</p><p>Most children and adults with maternal dup15q function in the moderate-to-severe range of intellectual disability; however, there is some variability, with a higher range of cognitive abilities seen in those with an interstitial <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a> [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.distefano.2020.71">DiStefano et al 2020</a>].</p><p>Individuals with maternal dup15q who have a diagnosis of epilepsy have lower verbal, daily living, socialization, fine motor, and gross motor skills compared to individuals with maternal dup15q who do not have epilepsy [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.distefano.2016.19">DiStefano et al 2016</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.distefano.2020.71">DiStefano et al 2020</a>].</p><p><b>Autism spectrum disorder (ASD).</b> Most children and adults with maternal dup15q meet criteria for ASD. In one study, 25/27 individuals with a maternal idic(15) met criteria for autism on the ADOS, while the remaining 2/27 met criteria for ASD [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.distefano.2020.71">DiStefano et al 2020</a>]. Among individuals with a maternal interstitial dup15q <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a>, 10/12 met criteria for autism, one individual met criteria for ASD, and one individual did not meet criteria for either. Compared to other CNVs known to cause ASD, maternal dup15q confers the greatest risk (odds ratio &#x02265;2.6) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.malhotra.2012.1223">Malhotra &#x00026; Sebat 2012</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.morenodeluca.2013.1090">Moreno-De-Luca et al 2013</a>]. Manifestations of ASD, particularly difficulties with social interaction, may increase from early to late childhood [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.simon.2010.463">Simon et al 2010</a>].</p><p>Compared to children with nonsyndromic ASD, children with maternal dup15q-ASD demonstrate a distinctive behavioral profile, including preserved responsive social smile and directed facial expressions towards others &#x02013; features that may inform behavioral interventions [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.distefano.2016.19">DiStefano et al 2016</a>].</p><p><b>Epilepsy.</b> More than half of individuals with maternal dup15q have epilepsy, usually involving multiple seizure types including infantile spasms and myoclonic, tonic-clonic, absence, and/or focal seizures [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.conant.2014.396">Conant et al 2014</a>]. Seizures most often begin between ages six months and nine years [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.battaglia.2008.30">Battaglia 2008</a>]. Incidence of epilepsy is higher in individuals with maternal dup15q resulting from an idic(15) compared to those with maternal dup15q resulting from interstitial <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a> (57% and 6% respectively) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.distefano.2020.71">DiStefano et al 2020</a>].</p><p>Maternal dup15q is one of the most common known causes of infantile spasms [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.conant.2014.396">Conant et al 2014</a>]. Infantile spasms in individuals with maternal dup15q often progress to Lennox Gastaut syndrome and other complex seizure patterns that may be difficult to control. As many as 40% of individuals with seizures present initially with infantile spasms; of this group, approximately 90% subsequently develop other seizure types.</p><p>Intractable epilepsy in individuals with maternal dup15q may result in disabling secondary effects, including falls or developmental regression. This occurs in more than half of individuals with frequent, uncontrolled seizures or nonconvulsive status epilepticus [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.battaglia.1997.1081">Battaglia et al 1997</a>].</p><p>Children with epilepsy have been found to have lower cognitive and adaptive function than those without epilepsy [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.distefano.2020.71">DiStefano et al 2020</a>].</p><p><b>Dysmorphic features.</b> Minor <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/dysmorphic/">dysmorphic</a> features often reported in individuals with maternal dup15q include flat occiput, downslanting palpebral fissures, depressed nasal bridge, short nose with an upturned nasal tip, low-set ears, long philtrum, high-arched palate, thick vermilion of the upper and lower lips, and micrognathia [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.battaglia.1997.1081">Battaglia et al 1997</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.borgatti.2001.111">Borgatti et al 2001</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.hogart.2010.181">Hogart et al 2010</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.urraca.2013.268">Urraca et al 2013</a>]. These features are typically subtle and may be missed in infancy.</p><p><b>Psychosis.</b> Although maternal idic(15) has been reported in schizophrenia cohorts [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.rees.2014.108">Rees et al 2014</a>], psychosis is not a commonly ascertained comorbidity in maternal dup15q &#x02013; a finding that may reflect the difficulty of recognizing and diagnosing psychosis in individuals with low cognitive functioning and limited verbal skills. For instance, psychosis is a common comorbidity in Prader-Willi syndrome caused by <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/uniparental-disomy/">uniparental disomy</a>, which similarly involves a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a> of the maternally contributed 15q11.2-13.1 [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.bassett.2011.350">Bassett 2011</a>]. These individuals tend to have higher cognitive and verbal abilities than individuals with maternal dup15q. Conversely, with a high rate of ASD in individuals with maternal dup15q, psychosis related to mood disorder may be misdiagnosed as schizophrenia.</p><p><b>Sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP)</b> occurs in a small but significant minority of individuals with maternal dup15q [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.friedman.2016.1">Friedman et al 2016</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.devinsky.2011.1801">Devinsky 2011</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.wegiel.2012.382">Wegiel et al 2012</a>]. These deaths almost always occur during sleep and most (though not all) have occurred in teenagers and young adults with epilepsy. Nonambulatory status and poor seizure control appear to be risk factors for SUDEP in individuals with maternal dup15q [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.friedman.2016.1">Friedman et al 2016</a>].</p><p>The mechanism underlying SUDEP is not well understood; however, available evidence suggests that in most instances a tonic-clonic seizure is followed by a shutdown of brain function and cardio-respiratory arrest. SUDEP occurs in 9% of individuals with epilepsy; the incidence of SUDEP in individuals with maternal dup15q is unknown.</p></div><div id="dup15q.Penetrance"><h3>Penetrance</h3><p>In maternal idic(15) <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/penetrance/">penetrance</a> is 100%.</p><p>In maternal interstitial 15q11.2-q13.1 <a class="def" may have mild features and appear unaffected.</p><p>Penetrance is the same for males and females.</p></div><div id="dup15q.Nomenclature"><h3>Nomenclature</h3><p>Terms used to refer to maternal 15q <a Many of these are preventable. For example, status epilepticus may be prevented with the use of rescue medications such as rectal diazepam or nasal midazolam. In the US, early intervention is a federally funded program available in all states that provides in-home services to target individual therapy needs.</p><p><b>Ages 3-5 years.</b> In the US, developmental preschool through the local public school district is recommended. Before placement, an evaluation is made to determine needed services and therapies and an individualized education plan (IEP) is developed for those who qualify based on established motor, language, social, or cognitive delay. The early intervention program typically assists with this transition. Developmental preschool is center based; for children too medically unstable to attend, home-based services are provided.</p><p><b>All ages.</b> Consultation with a developmental pediatrician is recommended to ensure the involvement of appropriate community, state, and educational agencies (US) and to support parents in maximizing quality of life. Some issues to consider:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>IEP services:</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>An IEP provides specially designed instruction and related services to children who qualify.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>IEP services will be reviewed annually to determine whether any changes are needed.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Special education law requires that children participating in an IEP be in the least restrictive environment feasible at school and included in general education as much as possible, when and where appropriate.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Vision and hearing consultants should be a part of the child's IEP team to support access to academic material.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>PT, OT, and speech services will be provided in the IEP to the extent that the need affects the child's access to academic material. Beyond that, private supportive therapies based on the affected individual's needs may be considered. Specific recommendations regarding type of therapy can be made by a developmental pediatrician.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>As a child enters the teen years, a transition plan should be discussed and incorporated in the IEP. For those receiving IEP services, the public school district is required to provide services until age 21.</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>A 504 plan (Section 504: a US federal statute that prohibits discrimination based on disability) can be considered for those who require accommodations or modifications such as front-of-class seating, assistive technology devices, classroom scribes, extra time between classes, modified assignments, and enlarged text.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Developmental Disabilities Administration (DDA) enrollment is recommended. DDA is a US public agency that provides services and support to qualified individuals. Eligibility differs by state but is typically determined by diagnosis and/or associated cognitive/adaptive disabilities.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Families with limited income and resources may also qualify for supplemental security income (SSI) for their child with a disability.</div></li></ul></div><div id="dup15q.Motor_Dysfunction"><h4>Motor Dysfunction</h4><p><b>Gross motor dysfunction.</b> Physical therapy is recommended to maximize mobility.</p><p><b>Fine motor dysfunction.</b> Occupational therapy is recommended for difficulty with fine motor skills that affect adaptive function such as feeding, grooming, dressing, and writing.</p><p><b>Oral motor dysfunction</b> should be assessed at each visit and clinical feeding evaluations and/or radiographic swallowing studies should be obtained for choking/gagging during feeds, poor weight gain, frequent respiratory illnesses, or feeding refusal that is not otherwise explained. Assuming that the child is safe to eat by mouth, feeding therapy (typically from an occupational or speech therapist) is recommended to help improve coordination or sensory-related feeding issues. Feeds can be thickened or chilled for safety. When feeding dysfunction is severe, an NG-tube or G-tube may be necessary.</p><p><b>Communication issues.</b> Consider evaluation for alternative means of communication (e.g., <a href="https://www.asha.org/NJC/AAC/" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">augmentative and alternative communication</a> [AAC]) for individuals who have expressive language difficulties. An AAC evaluation can be completed by a speech &#x00026; language pathologist who has expertise in the area. The evaluation will consider cognitive abilities and sensory impairments to determine the most appropriate form of communication. AAC devices can range from low-tech, such as picture exchange communication, to high-tech, such as voice-generating devices. Contrary to popular belief, AAC devices do not hinder verbal development of speech, but rather support optimal speech and language development.</p></div><div id="dup15q.SocialBehavioral_Concerns"><h4>Social/Behavioral Concerns</h4><p>Children may qualify for and benefit from interventions used in treatment of autism spectrum disorder, including applied behavior analysis (ABA). ABA therapy is targeted to the individual child's behavioral, social, and adaptive strengths and weaknesses and typically performed one on one with a board-certified behavior analyst.</p><p>Consultation with a developmental pediatrician may be helpful in guiding parents through appropriate behavior management strategies or providing prescription medications, such as medication used to treat attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), when necessary.</p><p>Concerns about serious aggressive or destructive behavior can be addressed by a pediatric psychiatrist.</p></div></div><div id="dup15q.Surveillance"><h3>Surveillance</h3><div id="dup15q.T.recommended_surveillance_for_in" class="table"><h3><span class="label">Table 6. </span></h3><div class="caption"><p>Recommended Surveillance for Individuals with Maternal 15q Duplication Syndrome</p></div><p class="large-table-link" style="display:none"><span class="right"><a href="/books/NBK367946/table/dup15q.T.recommended_surveillance_for_in/?report=objectonly" target="object">View in own window</a></span></p><div class="large_tbl" id="__dup15q.T.recommended_surveillance_for_in_lrgtbl__"><table class="no_bottom_margin"><thead><tr><th id="hd_h_dup15q.T.recommended_surveillance_for_in_1_1_1_1" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">System/Concern</th><th id="hd_h_dup15q.T.recommended_surveillance_for_in_1_1_1_2" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">Evaluation</th><th id="hd_h_dup15q.T.recommended_surveillance_for_in_1_1_1_3" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">Frequency</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td headers="hd_h_dup15q.T.recommended_surveillance_for_in_1_1_1_1" scope="row" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">

<i>Genetic counseling is the process of providing individuals and families with
information on the nature, mode(s) of inheritance, and implications of genetic disorders to help them
make informed medical and personal decisions. The following section deals with genetic
risk assessment and the use of family history and genetic testing to clarify genetic
status for family members; it is not meant to address all personal, cultural, or
ethical issues that may arise or to substitute for consultation with a genetics
professional</i>. &#x02014;ED.</p><div id="dup15q.Mode_of_Inheritance"><h3>Mode of Inheritance</h3><p>Maternal 15q <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a> syndrome (maternal dup15q) is an <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-dominant/">autosomal dominant</a> disorder typically caused by a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> genetic alteration.</p></div><div id="dup15q.Risk_to_Family_Members"><h3>Risk to Family Members</h3><div id="dup15q.Maternal_Isodicentric_15q11_2q13"><h4>Maternal Isodicentric 15q11.2-q13.1 Supernumerary Chromosome &#x02013; Idic(15)</h4><p>
<b>Parents of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a></b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>All probands reported to date with maternal dup15q caused by idic(15) whose parents have undergone <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genomic/">genomic</a> testing have had the disorder as the result of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> genetic alteration.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Evaluation of the mother by <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genomic/">genomic</a> testing to determine if she has the genetic alteration present in the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> is recommended to confirm her genetic status and to allow reliable <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/recurrence-risk/">recurrence risk</a> counseling. Note: Maternal transmission of supernumerary partial trisomy of the region has been reported in one instance [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.michelson.2011.230">Michelson et al 2011</a>].</div></li></ul><p><b>Sibs of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>.</b> The risk to the sibs of the proband depends on the genetic status of the proband's mother:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The risk to sibs is presumed to be low as the idic(15) has occurred as a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> alteration in all affected individuals reported to date.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If the idic(15) identified in the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> is not identified in a parent, the risk to sibs is presumed to be marginally greater than in the general population because of the theoretic possibility of maternal <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/germline-mosaicism/">germline mosaicism</a> for the idic(15).</div></li></ul><p><b>Offspring of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>.</b> Individuals with idic(15) are not known to reproduce.</p><p><b>Other family members.</b> Given that all probands with maternal dup15q caused by idic(15) reported to date have the disorder as a result of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> genetic alteration, the risk to other family members is presumed to be low.</p></div><div id="dup15q.Maternal_15q_Interstitial_Duplica"><h4>Maternal 15q Interstitial Duplication</h4><p>
<b>Parents of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a></b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">Approximately 85% of individuals with maternal dup15q caused by a maternal interstitial 15q11.2-q13.1 <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a> have a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> genetic alteration.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">Approximately 15% of individuals with maternal dup15q caused by a maternal interstitial 15q11.2-q13.1 <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a> inherited the genetic alteration from the mother.</div><div class="half_rhythm">If the mother of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> inherited a 15q interstitial <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a> from her father (i.e., the maternal grandfather of the proband), the mother will not have maternal dup15q syndrome. Instead, she may appear to be unaffected or have features associated with paternal duplications, which &#x02013; although distinct from those of the proband &#x02013; may share some similarities (see <a href="#dup15q.Genetically_Related_Disorders">Genetically Related Disorders</a>).</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm">Evaluation of the mother by <a class="def" DNA, the most likely explanation is a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> 15q interstitial duplication in the proband.</div></li></ul><p><b>Sibs of a <a <b>Family planning</b>

These five regions are termed BP1 through BP5. The Prader-Willi/Angelman <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/critical-region/">critical region</a> (PWACR) lies between BP2 and BP3 (see <a class="figpopup" href="/books/NBK367946/figure/dup15q.F1/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-figpopup="figdup15qF1" rid-ob="figobdup15qF1">Figure 1</a>) and is always included in the interstitial duplications or the idic(15) that cause maternal dup15q. Duplications that extend from BP1 to BP3 are referred to as Class I duplications; those that span BP2 and BP3 only are Class II duplications. The PWACR is <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/imprinted/">imprinted</a>: maternally derived increases in copy number cause maternal dup15q while paternally derived increases are typically associated with more variable and sometimes different neurodevelopmental phenotypes [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.cook.1997.928">Cook et al 1997</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.urraca.2013.268">Urraca et al 2013</a>].</p><p>The maternal isodicentric 15q11.2-q13.1 &#x02013; or idic(15) &#x02013; is typically a bisatellited <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/chromosome/">chromosome</a> thought to arise from U-type exchange during meiosis. Idic(15), which typically includes two mirrored copies of 15pter-q13.1 (p arm of chromosome 15, centromere, and 15q11.2-q13.1) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.roberts.2003.1061">Roberts et al 2003</a>], is sometimes referred to as inv dup(15). The distal breakpoint is typically BP3 (approximate position [hg38] chr15:28300000) on both sides of truly isodicentric chromosomes and BP4 and BP5 (approximate positions [hg19] chr15:30600000 and [hg38] chr15:32200000) for asymmetric supernumerary chromosomes (see <a class="figpopup" href="/books/NBK367946/figure/dup15q.F2/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-figpopup="figdup15qF2" rid-ob="figobdup15qF2">Figure 2</a>) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.hogart.2010.181">Hogart et al 2010</a>]. Idic(15) usually results in tetrasomy for 15q11.2-q13.1.</p><div class="iconblock whole_rhythm clearfix ten_col fig" id="figdup15qF2" co-legend-rid="figlgnddup15qF2"><a href="/books/NBK367946/figure/dup15q.F2/?report=objectonly" target="object" title="Figure 2. " /></a><div class="icnblk_cntnt" id="figlgnddup15qF2"><h4 id="dup15q.F2"><a href="/books/NBK367946/figure/dup15q.F2/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobdup15qF2">Figure 2. </a></h4><p class="float-caption no_bottom_margin">Asymmetry in dup15q, as seen in: A. Interstitial triplication of 15q11.2-13.1; and</p></div></div><p>Interstitial duplications leading to maternal dup15q arise by <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/nonallelic-homologous-recombination/">nonallelic homologous recombination</a> (NAHR) between two different breakpoint regions (e.g., BP1 and BP3). The distal breakpoint for maternal interstitial duplications is typically BP3 (approximate position [hg38] chr15:28300000), and the proximal breakpoint is typically either BP1 or BP2 (approximate positions [hg38] chr15:22300000 or [hg38] chr15:23300000, respectively). Interstitial duplications usually result in trisomy for 15q11.2-q13.1.</p><p>Variations of these primary mechanisms include the following:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Interstitial 15q11.2-q13.1 triplication, which results in tetrasomy of 15q11.2-q13.1. This <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> tends to be more severe than that of maternal interstitial 15q11.2-q13.1 <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a> and more like that of maternal isodicentric 15q11.2-q13.1 supernumerary <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/chromosome/">chromosome</a> [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.ungaro.2001.26">Ungaro et al 2001</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.hogart.2010.181">Hogart et al 2010</a>] (see <a class="figpopup" href="/books/NBK367946/figure/dup15q.F3/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-figpopup="figdup15qF3" rid-ob="figobdup15qF3">Figure 3</a>).</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Isodicentric 15q hexasomy. The <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> is severe, including profound intellectual disability, intractable epilepsy, and more prominent <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/dysmorphic/">dysmorphic</a> features (myopathic facies and low-set ears) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.mann.2004.104">Mann et al 2004</a>] (<a class="figpopup" href="/books/NBK367946/figure/dup15q.F3/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-figpopup="figdup15qF3" rid-ob="figobdup15qF3">Figure 3</a>).</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Asymmetric isodicentric or interstitial maternal triplications, which typically result in tetrasomy for 15q11.2-q13.2 and trisomy for 15q13.2-13.3. These asymmetric copy number variations are observed in approximately 10%-15% of isodicentric and interstitial chromosomes (Dup15q Alliance International Registry, 3-14-14) (see <a class="figpopup" href="/books/NBK367946/figure/dup15q.F2/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-figpopup="figdup15qF2" rid-ob="figobdup15qF2">Figure 2</a>).</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Ring <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/chromosome/">chromosome</a> 15. Rarely, supernumerary ring chromosomes that include the PWACR have been seen. These are typically mosaic, indicating the unstable nature of ring chromosomes [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.wang.2008.2">Wang et al 2008</a>].</div></li></ul><div class="iconblock whole_rhythm clearfix ten_col fig" id="figdup15qF3" co-legend-rid="figlgnddup15qF3"><a href="/books/NBK367946/figure/dup15q.F3/?report=objectonly" target="object" title="Figure 3. <b>Genes of interest in this region</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm"><b><i>UBE3A</i>,</b> the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> implicated in Angelman syndrome, is also thought to be the primary gene responsible for the intellectual impairment, anxiety, and reduced seizure threshold of maternal dup15q [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.copping.2017.3995">Copping et al 2017</a>], as well as contributing to the autism features seen in the disorder [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.glessner.2009.569">Glessner et al 2009</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.greer.2010.704">Greer et al 2010</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.lasalle.2015.1213">LaSalle et al 2015</a>]. Transgenic mice with overexpression of <i>UBE3A</i> exhibit learning deficits, anxiety-like behaviors, and reduced seizure thresholds, with normal social interactions and no increased motor stereotypies [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.copping.2017.3995">Copping et al 2017</a>]. <i>UBE3A</i> is <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/imprinted/">imprinted</a> with maternal-specific expression in postnatal neurons, and thus expressed at a higher dosage in brain from individuals with a maternally derived <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/duplication/">duplication</a> [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.hogart.2010.181">Hogart et al 2010</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.urraca.2013.268">Urraca et al 2013</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm"><b><i>GABRB3</i>, <i>GABRA5</i>, and</b>
<b><i>GABRG3</i>,</b> genes that encode GABA<sub>A</sub> receptor subunits, are implicated in the seizures observed in maternal dup15q [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.menold.2001.245">Menold et al 2001</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.samaco.2005.483">Samaco et al 2005</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.hogart.2007.691">Hogart et al 2007</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.frohlich.2019.29">Frohlich et al 2019</a>]. Knockout mouse models of these genes develop neurologic problems including seizures [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.delorey.1998.8505">DeLorey et al 1998</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.delorey.2008.207">DeLorey et al 2008</a>]. In addition, individuals with maternal dup15q have a characteristic EEG pattern that is similar to the EEG pattern seen in typically developing children after being administered midazolam, a GABA<sub>A</sub> modulator [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.frohlich.2019.29">Frohlich et al 2019</a>].</div><div class="half_rhythm"><i>GABRB3</i> may also contribute to the autism <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> in maternal dup15q [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.conant.2014.396">Conant et al 2014</a>], as single-nucleotide polymorphisms in this <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> are associated with autism [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.menold.2001.245">Menold et al 2001</a>] and expression is reduced in brain tissue samples of individuals with ASD [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.samaco.2005.483">Samaco et al 2005</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.hogart.2007.691">Hogart et al 2007</a>]. <i>GABRB3</i> has been implicated in ASD by genome-wide <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/de-novo/"><i>de novo</i></a> <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/single-nucleotide-variant/">single-nucleotide variant</a> studies [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.sanders.2015.1215">Sanders et al 2015</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm"><b><i>HERC2</i></b> is an E3 ubiquitin ligase. Individuals with <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> <i>HERC2</i> pathogenic variants can have intellectual disability [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.puffenberger.2012.1639">Puffenberger et al 2012</a>] or a severe Angelman syndrome-like neurodevelopmental disorder [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.harlalka.2013.65">Harlalka et al 2013</a>]. This <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> has increased expression in the neurons of individuals with maternal dup15q [<a class="bk_pop" href="#dup15q.REF.urraca.2018.6">Urraca et al 2018</a>].</div></li></ul></div></div><div id="dup15q.Chapter_Notes"><h2 id="_dup15q_Chapter_Notes_">Chapter Notes</h2><div id="dup15q.Acknowledgments"><h3>Acknowledgments</h3><p>The authors are indebted to the Dup15q Alliance for its efforts to advance research into maternal dup15q.
