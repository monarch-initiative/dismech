# Dupilumab Dampens Mucosal Type 2 Response During Acetylsalicylic Acid Challenge in N-ERD Patients.
**Authors:** Eckl-Dorna J, Morgenstern C, Poglitsch K, Arnoldner T, Gangl K, Bartosik TJ, Campion NJ, Tu A, Stanek V, Schneider S, Bangert C
**Journal:** Clin Exp Allergy (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/cea.70184](https://doi.org/10.1111/cea.70184)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug-exacerbated respiratory disease (N-ERD) is characterised by the clinical triad of hypersensitivity to NSAIDs, nasal polyposis, and asthma. The cells and mediators causing acute symptoms when driving the hypersensitivity reaction to acetylsalicylic acid (ASA) ingestion, remain poorly defined.

OBJECTIVE: To investigate the dynamics of nasal mediators during ASA provocation in N-ERD patients before and 24 weeks after therapy with the IL-4 receptor alpha-blocking antibody dupilumab (EudraCT (2019-004889-18) and ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04442256)).

METHODS: Nasal mucosal lining fluids of patients with N-ERD, chronic rhinosinusitis patients with nasal polyp (CRSwNP) and healthy disease controls were collected at selected time points up to 2 h after ASA provocation. Analysis of thirty-three different inflammatory mediators as well as transcriptomic profiling was performed. In N-ERD patients, provocation was repeated after 24 weeks of dupilumab therapy.

RESULTS: Sixty minutes after provocation with ASA, N-ERD patients showed a significant increase in type 2 associated cytokines (i.e., TSLP, IL-5 and eotaxin-3) as compared to the other patient groups. This effect was diminished after 24 weeks of dupilumab therapy and was independent of the development of ASA tolerance. Transcriptomics revealed dampened upregulation of type 2 associated pathway genes (i.e., AREG) as well as enhanced downregulation of lipid (i.e., ALOX15) and peroxisome metabolisms (i.e., NOS2) at ASA provocation after dupilumab therapy.

CONCLUSION AND CLINICAL RELEVANCE: Treatment with dupilumab leads to reduced nasal type 2 cytokine secretion and distinct changes in transcriptomic profile during ASA provocation, but changes in type 2 mediators show no association with tolerance development.

TRIAL REGISTRATION: EudraCT (2019-004889-18) and ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04442256).

Non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drug (NSAID)‐exacerbated respiratory disease (N‐ERD) comprises the triad of asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) and intolerance of cyclooxygenase‐1 (COX‐1) inhibitors like acetylsalicylic acid (ASA) [1]. It is typically an adult‐onset disease affecting about 7% of asthmatic and 10% of CRSwNP patients [2, 3]. Two phases of the disease can be distinguished: the chronic phase, marked by a persistent burden of asthma and nasal polyposis due to baseline chronic inflammation of the airway, and the acute respiratory hypersensitivity reaction occurring only upon ingestion of COX‐1 inhibitors [4]. The latter is characterised by nasal congestion, coughing, wheezing and sudden impairment in lung function within 20–120 min after intake of NSAIDs.

It is well established that one important driver of N‐ERD is the imbalance in the arachidonic acid pathway leading to overproduction of selected cysteinyl leukotrienes (CysLTs) and Prostaglandin D2 (PGD2) on the one hand and decreased Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) synthesis on the other hand [5, 6, 7]. In this context, mast cells but also platelets, eosinophils, T helper 2 cells (Th2), B cells and innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) are considered key players in driving the accentuated type 2 response underlying N‐ERD [8, 9, 10, 11]. However, to what extent immune cell‐derived cytokines and mediators play a role in driving the acute hypersensitivity reactions is less well understood [12, 13].

Other biologics interfering with type 2 mediated pathways such as mepolizumab and dupilumab, targeting IL‐5 and the IL‐4 Receptor α (IL‐4Rα), respectively, also decrease polyp size and—in the case of dupilumab—are also known to possibly induce ASA tolerance in a subset of patients [14, 15]. Recent research indicates that dupilumab can restore the disrupted arachidonic acid metabolism by boosting the production of PGE2 and decreasing the release of proinflammatory CysLTs [16].

Although a reduction of type 2 mediators and associated cell subsets was observed in the chronic phase of the disease in patients undergoing therapy with the respective biologics [17, 18], these studies have not addressed the impact on cytokine dynamics during acute hypersensitivity reactions. In this regard, data on immune kinetics following ASA challenges are scarce and indicate an increase in systemic proinflammatory IL‐6 [19] and nasal eosinophils as well as ILC2 influx already 1 h after provocation, but no increase in serum chemotactic proteins such as eotaxin‐1 [20]. Thus, despite the involvement of lipid mediators being firmly established, the contribution from other inflammatory mediators, especially from the type 2 pathway to acute hypersensitivity reactions, remains enigmatic.

Here we investigate immune mediators and other factors driving the acute reaction to ASA in N‐ERD patients using two complementary strategies: Firstly, we compared the kinetics of nasal immune mediator profiles during the first 2 h following oral ASA provocation in N‐ERD patients to those of CRSwNP and disease controls (DC). Secondly, we investigated potential changes in the immune mediators and transcriptomic profile of N‐ERD patients undergoing ASA challenge before and after dupilumab therapy to dissect the impact of IL‐4Rα chain blockade on ASA‐induced reactions.

The eleven N‐ERD patients included in this study were randomly selected from a cohort of thirty‐one N‐ERD patients under dupilumab therapy as reported previously [15]. All subjects signed informed consent and were recruited at the Department of Otorhinolaryngology and of Dermatology at the Medical University of Vienna, Austria. The study was approved by the local ethics committee (EK 1044/2020) and registered at EudraCT (2019‐004889‐18) and ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04442256). All N‐ERD and CRSwNP patients were advised to use nasal corticosteroids throughout the study period.

Oral ASA challenges were conducted before and 24 weeks after the start of dupilumab treatment as previously described [15] (Figure 1A). Positive reactions to ASA were defined by the onset of clinical symptoms and a drop (≥ 20%) in forced expiratory volume in 1 min (FEV1). Briefly, increasing ASA doses (125, 250 and 500 mg) were orally administered to N‐ERD patients as done before [13]. To avoid desensitisation to ASA by using low doses we exposed the patient to a starting dose of 125 mg followed by an observational period of 2 h before increasing to the next highest dose. Clinical indicators of upper and lower respiratory tract reactions including FEV1 and nasal mucosal lining fluids (nMLFs) were obtained before challenge (baseline) and at various time points (10, 20, 40, 60 and 120 min) up to 2 h following provocation. Once symptoms started, further nMLF samples and clinical parameters were taken at 10, 30, 60 and 120 min after reaction onset. Symptom onset was then defined as timepoint zero. Nasal mucosal cells for transcriptomic analysis were collected before and 2 h after reaction onset in N‐ERD patients. There was no post‐reaction reading for NERD11 at provocation two and only 10 min post‐reaction reading for NERD02.

Disease controls and NSAID‐tolerant CRSwNP patients (referred to as simply ‘CRSwNP’ throughout) received the highest dose (500 mg) of oral ASA once and had nasal samples taken before challenge and at 10, 20, 40, 60 and 120 min.

Nasal mucosal lining fluid was collected using nasosorptions (Nasosorption FX‐I, Hunt Developments (UK) Limited, Midhurst, West Sussex, United Kingdom). A nasal sampling device was inserted in each nostril for 1 min, transported on ice from the clinic to the laboratory and immediately processed. Briefly, pooled nasosorptions were eluted in 600 μL of PBS buffer containing 0.05% Tween 20 and 1% BSA while vortexing. Subsequently, samples were centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C and stored at −80°C for further usage.

Standardised nasal epithelial samples were collected by gently scraping the nasal mucosal surface of the inferior and middle turbinate (not from the polyp tissue) 2–3 times using the rhinoprobe curette (Arlington Scientific, Springville, Utah, USA) in the absence of local anaesthesia. Two rhinoprobes were collected per nostril and immediately transferred into RLT buffer for transport to the lab. Subsequently, samples were processed with the Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

The thirty‐three cytokines and chemokines measured in nMLFs using the electrochemiluminescence technology MSD multiplex U‐Plex platform were IL‐1α, IL‐1β, IL‐2, IL‐3, IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐6, IL‐7, IL‐8, IL‐9, IL‐10, IL‐12p40, IL‐12p70, IL‐13, IL‐15, IL‐16, IL‐17A, IL‐17E/IL‐25, IL‐21, IL‐22, IL‐27, IL‐33, IL‐1RA, IFN‐γ, TSLP, TNF‐α, TNF‐β, GM‐CSF, G‐CSF, VEGF, CCL11/eotaxin‐1, CCL17/TARC and CCL26/eotaxin‐3.

Nasal secretions from the left and right nostrils were pooled before being applied to the MSD plates. All measurements were performed following the manufacturer's instructions (https://www.mesoscale.com/en). Briefly, linkers were complexed with 200 μL of the target antibody, diluted to 1×, and 50 μL of this solution was applied to the plates on a shaker for 1 h at room temperature. The combinations of linkers with antibodies were recorded. The plates were washed three times with PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 (PBS‐T), dried and stored at 4°C for up to 7 days before use. Standards were freshly reconstituted and serially diluted in four‐fold steps using diluent 43 provided with the kit. A 50 μL sample or standard was incubated on the plate with agitation overnight at 4°C under a seal. The plates were washed three times, and detection antibodies, diluted with diluent 3, were applied (50 μL per plate) for 1 h at room temperature with agitation under a seal. The plates were washed three times with PBS‐T and immediately measured using 150 μL of MSD GOLD read buffer on a MESO SECTOR S 600 plate reader. The DISCOVERY WORKBENCH software (MSD, Rockville, Maryland, USA) was used to input batch‐specific standard concentrations, and sample analyte concentrations were extrapolated by the software.

The lower detection limit was 0.0 pg/mL for all mediators except IL‐1RA (8000 pg/mL), VEGF (550 pg/mL), CCL11/eotaxin‐1 (20 pg/mL), IL‐13 (3 pg/mL), CCL26/eotaxin‐3, IL‐21, CCL17/TARC (1 pg/mL). The upper detection limit for VEGF was 25,000 pg/mL and did not apply to any other mediator.

Additionally, tryptase and leukotriene E4 were measured by ELISA. LTE4 levels in nMLF and urine were measured using the Leukotriene E4 ELISA Kit (Cayman Chemicals, Michigan, USA) and the Tryptase beta‐2 (Invitrogen, California, USA), respectively, following the manufacturer's protocol. Urine was collected at baseline and 120 min post reaction from N‐ERD and CRSwNP patients and immediately stored at −80°C. All measurements were done in triplicates; background was subtracted. For detailed protocols, please refer to Schneider et al. [15].

The total poly score (TPS) was assessed endoscopically and ranges from 0 (no polyp) to 4 (large polyps) for each nostril. Changes in the mean TPS score between baseline and dedicated study time points in N‐ERD patients were determined by a paired t‐test.

The forty‐item University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) was used to measure the individual's ability to detect odours at baseline and the end of the study. The change in mean UPSIT score was assessed by individual t‐test.

Twenty‐two items of the Sino‐nasal Outcome Test (SNOT‐22) test were analysed as total sum as well as split into the four domains (nasal symptoms: NS01–NS09, otologic symptoms: OS1–OS3, sleep symptoms: SS01–SS05, emotional symptoms: ES01–ES05) [21]. Missing data were imputed as reported previously [15]. Briefly, data from questionnaires were included in the analysis if they were > 80% complete and contained at least two patient visits. Missing values were imputed using the mean of the two closest visits. Item‐total correlations were assessed by calculating Cronbach's Alpha and Spearman–Brown Coefficient using the Python library pingouin.

To assess the cumulative event rate of an ASA reaction in N‐ERD patients, Kaplan–Meier plots were used to visualise the survival curves. A log‐rank test was applied to compare the curves between the two provocations. R packages survival (v3.5–8) and survminer (v0.4.9) were employed to compute and visualise survival analysis.

Thirty‐three nasal cytokines and chemokines were log2 transformed and subjected to unsupervised clustering using the Euclidean distance metric and ward.D2 linkage method. Cytokines were scaled by row using Z‐scores. The correlations were visualised in a heatmap using the R package pheatmap (v1.0.12).

Principal component analysis of thirty‐three scaled raw cytokine and chemokine values was performed at baseline and 60 min after reaction onset at provocation one and two in N‐ERD patients using the prcomp function. Results were visualised with the factoextra (v1.0.7) package.

Cytokine‐co‐clustering was evaluated by the CytoMod software as described [22]. Briefly, cytokines were log10 transformed and clustering was performed using the Pearson‐signed metric and complete linkage method. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the Tibshirani gap statistic. Clustering results were visualised by heatmap (pheatmap, v1.0.12) with colours denoting the reliability score (yellow: zero reliability, red: high reliability) that was computed across 1000 random samples and represented the fraction of times each pair of cytokines clustered together. Clustering was performed on adjusted cytokine values.

Module scores for each subject were calculated as the mean value of the standardised (mean zero and unit variance) cytokine values within each module.

RNA was sequenced using the Lexogen QuantSeq chemistry on an Illumina NextSeq550 system to generate single end reads with 75 bp lengths at the Next Generation Sequencing facility of the Vienna Biocenter (VBCF, Vienna, Austria). To quantify transcripts, the pseudo‐mapper Salmon was used on a 17 mer index of the human transcriptome (Gencode.v.41) [23].

Quality and adaptor trimming of fastq‐files was done with TrimGalore! (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore/tree/0.6.10) and employing the ‐‐three_prime_clip_R1 argument to clip twelve base pairs from the 3′ end of the reads to remove unwanted bias. Mapping was done with the following parameters ‐l A, ‐p 6, ‐‐validateMappings, ‐‐softclip, ‐‐minScoreFraction 0.55.

After examining the sequences and samples, one sample was excluded because of large sequence duplication rate and one due to potential misclassification. Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 (v1.42.0) [24]. The ashr algorithm was used to shrink the log2‐fold changes [25].

Functional enrichment analyses of significant gene lists were performed using the g:GOSt tool of g:Profiler employing the g:SCS algorithm for calculating the significance threshold [26]. A separate analysis was performed for significantly up‐ and downregulated genes at provocation one and two, respectively.

Full STRING networks of DEGs from provocation one and two were constructed using Cytoscape (v3.10.1) [27] with a confidence cut off score of 0.4. Subsequently, networks from provocation one and two were merged and clustered by employing the MCL algorithm with a granularity parameter of 4. Log2 fold changes of both provocations were visualised using Omics Visualizer (v2.0.3).

To assess if the data were normally distributed, histogram plotting and the Shapiro–Wilk test were applied. The Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test was used to assess differences between multiple groups of non‐parametric data. For post hoc pairwise tests, Wilcoxon signed‐rank and Mann–Whitney U‐tests were applied for non‐parametric dependent and independent comparisons, respectively. Differences in parametric variables were assessed by t‐test. For comparisons of multiple binary outcome variables, Fisher's exact test was used.

The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied in multiple testing to retrieve adjusted p‐values. Significance was obtained at p‐values smaller than 0.05.

All data processing and statistical analyses were performed with Python (v3.9.6, Van Rossum, G. and Drake Jr., F. L., The Netherlands), R (v4.3.1., R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and RStudio version 2023.09.0 + 463. Figure panels were created with Affinity Publisher 2 (v2.5.6).

In this exploratory study, we enrolled ten NSAID‐tolerant CRSwNP patients without asthma (referred to as simply ‘CRSwNP’ throughout), eleven with N‐ERD, and ten disease controls (DC). DCs had no acute or chronic nasal disease and no regular medication intake at the time of provocation. Patient groups were well‐balanced, except for DCs being younger than patients with polyposis (Table S1).

When determining baseline levels of thirty‐three different inflammatory mediators in nMLFs, we observed distinct patterns between the three patient groups (Figure 1B–M, Figure S1 and Table S2). CRSwNP and N‐ERD patients showed increased mediator levels in two clusters containing alarmins (e.g., IL‐33 and IL‐17E/IL‐25; Figure 1B) and type 2 mediators (e.g., eotaxin‐3, IL‐9 and CCL17; Figure 1B). Interestingly, while most of the inflammatory mediators assessed were comparable between CRSwNP and N‐ERD patients, we detected significant differences between these groups as compared to DC in the expression of the type 2 response‐associated cytokine eotaxin‐3 (Figure 1C), differential levels of Th1‐associated cytokines (IL‐12p70, TNF‐β; Figure 1D,E) as well as an increase in cytokines involved in cell proliferation and survival (G‐CSF, IL‐3, IL‐7 and IL‐21; Figure 1F–I), immune regulation (IL‐27; Figure 1J) and tissue repair (IL‐22; Figure 1K). Furthermore, these two groups demonstrated significantly reduced levels of the soluble IL‐1 receptor antagonist (IL‐1RA; Figure 1L) and increased levels of leukotriene E4 (LTE4; Figure 1M).

Cytokine, tryptase and LTE4 levels were analysed at predefined timepoints after ASA provocation in patients with N‐ERD, CRSwNP and DC (Figure 1A). The median percentage change in cytokine concentration from baseline (= timepoint of ASA administration) for DC and CRSwNP and from reaction onset for N‐ERD across 120 min was determined (Figure 2A–H and Figure S2A–AA). We observed distinct changes in mediator levels between the groups and timepoints, peaking at 60 min after baseline for most mediators (Figure 2A–H and Figure S2AA). Interestingly, both N‐ERD and CRSwNP patients showed elevated LTE4 concentrations 120 min after provocation, significantly differing from DC (Figure 3A). The epithelial alarmin TSLP was markedly elevated in N‐ERD patients as compared to CRSwNP and DC already after 60 min of symptom onset (Figure 3B). This was accompanied by a peak in type 2‐associated mediator levels such as IL‐4 (Figure S3A), IL‐5 (Figure 3C), CCL17 (Figure 3D) and IL‐10 (Figure 3E) in N‐ERD patients only. Cytokines associated with type 1 responses such as IL‐12p40 (Figure 3F), and TNF‐β (Figure 3G) were also specifically elevated in ASA‐sensitive subjects after 60 min. Eotaxin‐3 (Figure 3H), GM‐CSF (Figure S3B), IFN‐γ (Figure S3C) and VEGF (Figure S3D) concentrations were increased in both CRSwNP and N‐ERD groups as compared to DCs after 60 min. The acute phase pro‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐6 started to rise 10 min after ASA provocation in N‐ERD patients, peaked at 60 min, and showed a sharp decline thereafter (Figures S2E and S3E). In addition, we observed an increase in tryptase levels in 5 out of 10 N‐ERD patients from 10 to 30 min after reaction onset (Figure S3AA,AB). LTE4 urine levels, as determined by ELISA, showed an increase in N‐ERD as compared to CRSwNP but were not statistically significant (data not shown).

Next, we assessed changes in ASA‐induced nasal cytokine secretion in N‐ERD patients before and after treatment with dupilumab. While nasal baseline leukotriene levels showed a trend towards reduction after dupilumab treatment (Table S3), as previously shown [15], we were surprised that N‐ERD patients still expressed substantial amounts of LTE4 after the second ASA provocation (Figure 4A and Figure S5A). In contrast, secretion of TSLP in response to the ASA challenge was nearly abrogated after 24 weeks of dupilumab treatment (Figure 4B and Figure S5B) and the levels of other type 2 mediators such as IL‐5 (Figure 4C and Figure S5C), CCL17 (Figure 4D and Figure S5D), eotaxin‐3 (Figure 4E and Figure S5F) and IL‐10 (Figure 4F and Figure S5E) were also significantly reduced. A similar pattern was observed for IL‐12p40, TNF‐β and VEGF in all patients (Figure 4G,H, Figures S4A and S5G,H,AH).

Strikingly, the median time to symptom occurrence upon ASA provocation increased from provocation one to provocation two from approximately 50 min to 180 min (Figure 5E). One patient was completely tolerant to ASA at the second provocation and four patients tolerated higher dosages (Table S4; summarised as ASA‐tolerant patients including both fully and partially tolerant patients), whilst six patients remained intolerant to ASA. Importantly, in contrast to the first provocation, all patients suffered only from upper tract respiratory symptoms 60 min after ASA intake (Figure 5F, Table S5). In accordance with this observation, patients had significantly higher FEV1 levels throughout the second provocation (Figure 5G). Patients developing ASA tolerance (n = 5) as compared to those remaining ASA intolerant (n = 6) did not show any difference by principal component analysis in baseline cytokine levels (Figure S6A,B) or in any of the modules identified at provocation one or two using the CytoMod software [22] (Figure S6C–F). Also, by comparing changes in cytokine levels in ASA‐tolerant versus ASA‐intolerant subjects between baseline and 60 min after the first and second provocation, no significant differences for most mediators (Figure S7A–AH) were observed. Interestingly, we detected a difference in LTE4: subjects developing ASA tolerance had a significant increase in LTE4 concentrations during the first and a slight decrease during the second provocation, while those remaining intolerant showed an increase during both provocations (Figure S7AI). In this line, ASA‐tolerant subjects also showed a trend towards higher TPS reduction, which did not reach significance (Figure S8).

We assessed transcriptomic changes in nasal cells of N‐ERD patients only by comparing mRNA expression before and after ASA provocation, at treatment start and after 24 weeks of dupilumab therapy. Changes at the transcriptomic level were assessed by comparing mRNA expression in nasal cells before and after ASA provocation, both at treatment start (ASA Provocation 1) and after 24 weeks of dupilumab therapy (ASA Provocation 2). Significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined based on the following thresholds of log2 fold change (FC) and Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p‐value: |FC| > 1.0, p < 0.05. Among the top DEGs with the greatest log2 fold changes after the first ASA provocation as to baseline were CYP1B1 (−2.89 FC) encoding a member of the cytochrome P450 family involved in arachidonic acid metabolism [28] and IL5 (4.1 FC) (Figure 6A). At the second provocation, downregulated genes after provocation were the cystatins CST1 (−6.71 FC), CST4 (−5.93 FC) and CST2 (−5.62 FC) as well as MUC5B (−2.58 FC) among which CST1 has been shown to be highly expressed in nasal polyps [29, 30] (Figure 6B). Compared to the first provocation, SPINK 7, a serine peptidase inhibitor involved in barrier function and inflammatory responses [31], as well as the fibroblast growth factor encoding protein FGF5 were more upregulated at the second provocation, while proinflammatory mediators CCL16, IL11 and the antimicrobial peptide psoriasin S100A7 showed less pronounced upregulation (Figure 6C). APOE, which we previously observed to be downregulated in mast cells of N‐ERD patients [11] increased in expression during the second provocation (Figure 6D). The genes significantly downregulated before or during the second provocation included the calcium‐activated chloride channel regulator (CLCA1, Figure 6E), the 15‐lipoxygenase (ALOX15, Figure 6F) and the oxidative stress‐associated inducible nitric oxide synthetase (NOS2, Figure 6G). Additional peroxisome‐associated genes (KEGG:04146) were also downregulated in individual patients at the second provocation independently of the development of ASA tolerance (Figure 6H, Figure S9A–D).

We found that amphiregulin (AREG), which is associated with type 2 immunity [32], was upregulated at both provocations albeit at lower levels after dupilumab therapy (Figure 6I). The phospholipase A2 Group IVB encoding gene PLA2G4B was more upregulated at provocation two, whilst its isoenzyme PLA2G4D showed stronger expression at provocation one (Figure 6J). Interestingly, enzymes of the 12‐lipoxygenase pathway (ALOX12B, SDR9C7, Figure 6J) also showed reduced upregulation at provocation two.

Here, we investigated—for the first time—cytokine dynamics in N‐ERD patients during 2 h following ASA provocation, not only as compared to ASA‐challenged CRSwNP and DC, but importantly also before and after therapy with dupilumab. Sixty to 120 min after oral provocation with ASA, N‐ERD patients not only showed a significant increase in type 2 associated cytokines (TSLP, IL‐5, CCL17, eotaxin‐3 and IL‐10), but also in type 1 associated molecules such as IL‐12p40 and TNF‐β as compared to DC. Interestingly, ASA provocation resulted in increased eotaxin‐3 and VEGF in both CRSwNP and N‐ERD subjects after 60 min as compared to DC. Secretion of these cytokines upon ASA challenge was significantly reduced after 24 weeks of dupilumab therapy in all N‐ERD patients. At the transcriptomic level, changes in the arachidonic acid pathway, type 2‐associated pathways and the peroxisome metabolism were observed. Interestingly, the development of tolerance was not associated with specific cytokine patterns.

In accordance with previous data [10, 33, 34], we observed an accentuated nasal type 2 cytokine pattern in CRSwNP and N‐ERD patients at baseline, with the latter showing the highest levels of type 2 cytokines. Additionally, cytokines such as VEGF or eotaxin‐3 and interestingly also LTE4 were upregulated in both groups 60 min after ASA provocation. The fact that changes in LTE4 levels are lower than expected [35] may be due to the differential sensitivity of the ELISA as compared to mass spectrometry. However, the observation that LTE4 was upregulated in both CRSwNP and N‐ERD corroborates the notion that the pathomechanisms underlying N‐ERD and eosinophilic nasal polyps have many similarities in terms of cytokine composition and that the quantity of these may be an important factor in driving disease severity [6]. Interestingly, CRSwNP and N‐ERD patients also show higher baseline LTE4 levels as compared to DC. This is in accordance with the literature, where heightened LTE4 levels are observed in nasal secretions and urine of polyp patients, regardless of aspirin hypersensitivity, and is also associated with polyp recurrence [36, 37, 38]. In light of these findings and the fact that previous studies highlighting LTE4 as a hallmark of N‐ERD used non‐aspirin‐sensitive asthmatic control groups largely without polyps [39, 40], it can be hypothesised that higher local levels of LTE4 production may be a key feature of nasal polyps, independent of the status of aspirin hypersensitivity.

The pathophysiology underlying N‐ERD involves an imbalance in arachidonic acid metabolism with increased levels of CysLTs and PGD2, reduced production of anti‐inflammatory PGE2 and lowered expression of the EP2 receptor for PGE2 [7]. The acute reaction after NSAID intake is thought to be partly induced by blocking COX‐1‐mediated constitutive PGE2 secretion, which under stable conditions inhibits eosinophil and mast cell activation by ASA ingestion. Recent work suggests that innate lymphoid cells type 2 (ILC2) are implicated in acute reactions as they can be activated by both PGD2 and PGE2, are found in increased numbers in nasal scrapings after ASA administration [41] and could lead to tissue eosinophilia by producing large amounts of IL‐5 shortly after ASA provocation [20]. Interestingly, a dysregulation of the COX‐1 pathway similar to the one observed in N‐ERD patients can be achieved by treating in vitro cultures of fibroblasts with IL‐4 and IFN‐y in addition to IL‐β thereby emphasising the role of a mixed T1/T2 environment in mediating the phenotype of N‐ERD [42, 43, 44]. Our findings of increased TSLP, IL‐4, IL‐5 and eotaxin‐3 levels after ASA ingestion support the hypothesis of type 2‐associated cell recruitment, including ILC2 and eosinophils in acute reactions and their role in modulating the COX‐1 pathway [45].

As dupilumab inhibits both IL‐4 and IL‐13 mediated signalling, it can impact a variety of mechanisms involved in N‐ERD ranging from Th2 cell differentiation to airway smooth muscle cell proliferation [46]. We and others have previously demonstrated that dupilumab leads to a marked downregulation of type 2 mediators in serum and nasal secretions of N‐ERD patients over time [15, 18, 47]. Interestingly, this reduction was not associated with the development of tolerance to ASA as patients becoming tolerant to ASA during dupilumab treatment showed a similar cytokine pattern—excluding LTE4—during the second provocation, compared to those remaining intolerant. Nevertheless, these findings may indicate that although activation of type 2 immunity plays a central role in driving the chronic phase of N‐ERD and is also involved during ASA ingestion, the symptoms during the acute reaction in a majority of patients may be driven by additional, type 2 mediator‐independent mast cell and eosinophil activation such as IgE as well as COX‐pathway dysregulation as detailed below. Our observation of attenuated LTE4 secretion during the second provocation in ASA‐tolerant, but not intolerant subjects, corroborates this hypothesis. Interestingly, the IgE targeting monoclonal antibody omalizumab [12, 13] leading to a faster and more pronounced drop in total IgE levels, induces complete tolerance to ASA in around 60% of patients after 24 weeks of therapy, as compared to only 20%–40% in patients treated with dupilumab [15, 48]. This indicates that chronic IgE‐induced activation of mast cells may be one of the potential factors modulating acute reactions in N‐ERD.

In N‐ERD patients, both the COX‐ and LOX‐pathways are dysregulated, leading to an increased production of CysLTs. Previously, ALOX15 levels in epithelial cells of N‐ERD patients were found to correlate with disease severity [11, 49]. Interestingly, we observed reduced expression of genes encoding enzymes of both the 15‐lipoxygenase (ALOX15) and the 12‐lipoxygenase (ALOX12B, SDR9C7) pathways at the second provocation as compared to the first. This is in agreement with previous studies which found ALOX15 to be downregulated after dupilumab treatment [50]. At the same time, CYP1B1 encoding cytochrome P450, which is responsible for metabolising arachidonic acid to 20‐hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid and has been shown to lead to relaxation of airways [28, 51], was strongly downregulated during provocation one but not provocation two. This could indicate that long‐term treatment with the anti‐IL‐4Rα inhibitor dupilumab may ameliorate the imbalance in the arachidonic acid pathway. In this line, it has been shown that patients with N‐ERD show an abnormal regulation of the autocrine metabolic loop regulating the COX‐2 pathway which is most likely driven by high levels of IL‐4 and IFN‐γ [43, 52]. Thus, it has been hypothesised that dupilumab—by blocking IL‐4 induced mechanisms—may normalise the perturbed autocrine metabolic loop in N‐ERD and consequently PGE2 synthesis and thus restore aspirin tolerance [7]. However, at a transcriptional level, we found no coherent differences in PGE2 pathway or EP2 regulation between aspirin‐tolerant and intolerant subjects after dupilumab therapy (data not shown).

Additionally, various genes being implicated in CRSwNP and especially N‐ERD pathology show reduced expression levels after ASA provocation upon 24 weeks of dupilumab treatment: (i) CST1 and other members of the cystatin family that play a role in amplifying type 2 inflammation [29] and correlate with disease status and polyp burden [30, 53]; (ii) MUC5B [54] and CLCA1 [55], which are overexpressed in CRSwNP and in the regulation of mucus production and electrolyte transport; (iii) NOS2, the oxidative stress‐associated nitric oxide synthetase, an important biomarker of type 2‐high asthma and highly expressed in CRSwNP [56]. Together with NOS2, we found twenty‐seven more downregulated DEGs that are associated with the peroxisome in functional enrichment analysis (Figure 6H, Table S6). On the other hand, transcription of the APOE gene encoding apolipoprotein, which ameliorates airway hyperreactivity in asthma [57] and is known to be downregulated in N‐ERD patients [11], is increased during the second provocation. Thus, dupilumab therapy induces a variety of changes in the transcriptome leading to alleviation of airway hyperresponsiveness and oxidative stress improving acute reaction symptoms to ASA challenge.

Limitations of this study include the small sample size as well as the lack of CRSwNP and DC groups at provocation two due to the nature of this pilot trial. Especially the findings regarding differences in baseline levels between ASA tolerant and intolerant are underpowered and warrant investigation in a larger cohort. CRSwNP and N‐ERD patients, but not disease controls, received intranasal corticosteroids throughout the study, which may have influenced cytokine levels. However, as the patients had a high symptom burden, it was not possible to stop their local nasal therapy for the duration of the study. Furthermore, although our panel of cytokines was carefully selected, it is still only a fraction of available mediators. The fact that we did not assess COX‐pathway mediators apart from LTE4 in this study is a clear limitation. However, we intended to keep the sampling burden low due to the intense time course and were thus limited in sample amount and focused on immune mediators only.

In summary, our work provides first insights into the type 2 immunity‐associated processes governing the response to ASA provocation with and without dupilumab therapy. Our results warrant investigations in a larger cohort of patients undergoing long‐term therapy with dupilumab using large‐scale proteomics as well as single‐cell analysis to further delineate the factors involved in acute reactions to ASA.
