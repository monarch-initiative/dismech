# Impact of Inner Retinal Layer Thinning on Visual Function in OPA1 Autosomal Dominant Optic Atrophy and Associations With Age and Genetic Variant Class.
**Authors:** Schrittwieser J, Reitner A, Kircher K, Bertich M, Stapf C, Lilja SV, Paquay A, Reiter GS, Bittner RE, Schmidt WM, Pemp B
**Journal:** Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1167/iovs.67.4.13](https://doi.org/10.1167/iovs.67.4.13)

## Content

PURPOSE: Inner retinal layer thinning in autosomal dominant optic atrophy (ADOA) can affect visual acuity (VA), but impact on perimetric parameters and disease-related changes with increasing age are undefined.

METHODS: One hundred eight patients with ADOA harboring a disease-causing variant in OPA1 were analyzed retrospectively, including best-corrected VA, mean deviation (MD) from 30-2 threshold perimetry, MD in the papillomacular bundle (PMB) subfield, and retinal layer thickness in spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (OCT).

RESULTS: Twenty-three of 57 detected variants in OPA1 are newly reported. In multivariable mixed-effect models, peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (pRNFL) and macular ganglion cell layer (mGCL) thicknesses impacted visual function, with an average deterioration of 0.1 logMAR per 3.2 µm mGCL reduction (P < 0.001), PMB-MD loss of 0.75 dB/µm mGCL (P = 0.002), and MD loss of 0.11 dB/µm pRNFL (P = 0.048). Age impacted mGCL thickness (-0.06 µm/year; P = 0.023). In available long-term follow-ups mGCL lost 0.26 ± 0.10 µm/year. Missense variants caused worse VA (0.83 vs. 0.49 logMAR, P = 0.016), MD (-11.48 vs. -3.04 decibel [dB], P = 0.005), and PMB-MD (-16.25 vs. -4.17 dB, P = 0.001) than haploinsufficiency variants, and lower mGCL (20.12 vs. 21.97 µm, P = 0.044) and pRNFL thickness (52.41 vs. 66.41 µm, P < 0.001).

CONCLUSIONS: VA and central scotoma severity in OPA1-related ADOA are significantly associated with inner retinal layer thickness, which is impacted by patient age. The mGCL thickness and sensitivity in the PMB subfield were the most indicative clinical parameters for disease-related changes, with worse outcomes in heterozygotes with missense variants in OPA1.

Autosomal dominant optic atrophy (ADOA) is the most common hereditary optic neuropathy (HON).1–3 The term “ADOA” is often used synonymously for HONs caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in the OPA1 gene (optic atrophy 1, OMIM #165500), because it was discovered as the first causative gene and is responsible for approximately 40% to 80% of all cases.4–6
OPA1 encodes a dynamin-related GTPase controlling efficiency of mitochondrial fusion and mitochondrial network adaption to cellular energy demand.7,8 However, over the last years, a growing number of other genes important for mitochondrial function have been identified as being responsible for rarer cases of isolated ADOA, including OPA3 (OPA3), DNM1L (OPA5), ACO2 (OPA9), AFG3L2 (OPA12), SSBP1 (OPA13), MIEF1 (OPA14), WFS1, MFN2, SPG7, SDHA, PMPCA, UCHL1, and most recently PPIB.6,9–11

The pathologic hallmark in ADOA is a bilateral apoptotic degeneration of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) predominantly in the papillomacular bundle (PMB), which features thinner axons with fewer mitochondria per cell and less myelination in the optic nerve.8,12 This may increase vulnerability to compromised mitochondrial function and reactive oxygen species-induced apoptotic degeneration.13,14 Accordingly, a typical binocular pattern of thinning of the inner retinal layers of the PMB and its adjacent sectors can be visualized in patients with ADOA using optical coherence tomography (OCT), particularly in the temporal peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (pRNFL), the macular ganglion cell layer (mGCL), and the macular inner plexiform layer (mIPL).15–17


OPA1-related ADOA shows a high but not complete penetrance, leading to a varying degree of optic atrophy and insidious vision loss with symptom onset typically occurring in the first decade of life.1,3,18 Vision loss can progress slowly with age, but the speed and extent of impairment is highly variable even within the same family.1,19 In clinical examination, patients typically show loss of sensitivity in the central visual field.20,21 However, associations between RGC loss in OPA1 ADOA and perimetric defects have not been detailed previously.

In contrast to OPA1 variants, like nonsense or truncating “loss-of-function” variants that lead to haploinsufficiency induced by diminished functional protein (e.g. through nonsense-messenger RNA decay),22 select missense variants have been shown to exert a dominant-negative effect most likely due to the formation of competing dysfunctional enzyme.23 This may lead to more pronounced visual impairment and optic atrophy, which in turn results in a poorer prognosis.17,24,25

In this study of a large patient cohort with OPA1-related ADOA, we aim to evaluate the effect of inner retinal layer thinning on visual function, with particular attention to results from visual field testing. Furthermore, we assess differences between haploinsufficiency and missense variants, and explore previously unknown impacts of aging and parent-of-origin sex on disease-related changes.

This retrospective analysis was conducted in accordance with the tenets set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee approved the study protocol and waived the requirement for written informed consent.

Individuals were identified from an in-house patient registry. Clinical data from patients who met the specified inclusion and exclusion criteria were extracted from electronic health records and pseudonymized. Visual function was assessed using the best-corrected visual acuity (VA) notated in logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR), and the mean deviation (MD) in static automated perimetry (30-2 Threshold Test, Humphrey Field Analyzer; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany). Visual field tests with error rates above 30% were excluded. In addition, MD in the PMB was calculated as the mean value of the Garway-Heath central cluster, which comprises the central six values in the total deviation map on the visual field report.26 In addition, data of refractive error were collected and the spherical equivalent (SE) in diopters (dpt) was calculated. The inner retinal structure was evaluated using spectral-domain OCT (Spectralis OCT; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). The thicknesses of the mGCL and the mIPL were measured from macular volume scans covering the central 20-degree area of the retina. The pRNFL thickness was measured using a 12-degree ring scan centered on the optic disc. Semiautomated layer segmentation was conducted using the built-in software (Heidelberg Eye Explorer, software version 6.9a) with review of all scans and manual correction in cases of false annotations. OCT scans were only included if they fulfilled the OSCAR-IB quality control criteria.27 Thicknesses of pRNFL, mGCL, and mIPL were extracted as total average, and as sectoral thickness from the 4 quadrants of the Spectralis pRNFL analysis, or from the 9 subfields in the fovea-centered circular 6-mm Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) grid, respectively. In addition, the average values of the four macular quadrants (superior, nasal, inferior, and temporal) were calculated. Subject to availability, the most recent complete record or the examination with the greatest amount of data was utilized.

The present analysis included patients with a disease-causing variant in the OPA1 gene and signs of optic atrophy on fundoscopy and/or OCT. Variant carriers with normal results in functional testing were only included if their OCT showed clear pathologic changes compared to normative reference data. Asymptomatic individuals without signs of optic atrophy were excluded, as incomplete penetrance in ADOA was described.18 Eyes with concurrent pathologies of the retina or the optic pathway were also excluded.

Genetic analysis is detailed in Supplementary Methods S1. All pathogenic variants are reported with regard to the Matched Annotation from NCBI and EMBL-EBI (MANE)28 NM_130837.3 reference sequence (MANE Select). The genetic variants were formally classified into haploinsufficiency-causing (nonsense variants, frame-shift causing deletions, insertions and intragenic deletions) or missense variants. In-frame intragenic deletions and splice-site variants were treated as likely haploinsufficiency causing.

Descriptive statistics were based on the mean and standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data, and the median and interquartile range (IQR) for data not demonstrating normal distribution (MD and PMB-MD). Distribution of the residuals was checked for all models using histograms, Q–Q plots, and residual-versus-fitted plots. A Pearson correlation coefficient was used to check for correlations among age, visual function, and OCT parameters. Exploratory univariable linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were created for analyses assessing (1) the influence of age on parameters of visual function and retinal layers, and (2) the impact of the individual retinal layer thicknesses on visual function parameters. For final analysis, multivariable LMMs were fitted to investigate the effect of all three retinal layer thicknesses and genetic variant class on visual function parameters in one model. In addition, pairwise comparisons of missense and haploinsufficiency variants were conducted for each structural parameter using multivariable LMM.

In all models, we included a nested random intercept with the random intercept of the patient nested within the genotype, to correct for intra-genotype clustering. In addition, age, sex, and SE were included as covariates in all analyses. For all parameters, in particular the parameters that were not normally distributed (MD and PMB-MD), normality of the model residuals was evaluated. As no substantial deviations from normality or homoscedasticity were observed, LMMs were considered appropriate and retained. For the multivariable models, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was checked to rule out multicollinearity. A VIF <5 was deemed acceptable. The marginal r2 and the conditional r2 were calculated for the multivariable models to assess the model fit. The models with the smallest Akaike information criterion (AIC) were selected.

In families with available patients from two generations, we also explored if the clinical severity varied differently in families with paternal compared to maternal transmission, using Mann-Whitney U tests.

Only P values < 0.05 were deemed significant. Correction for multiple testing using the Bonferroni method was performed for all final, multivariable analyses. All calculations were conducted using R-statistical software package version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

One hundred thirteen individuals genetically confirmed with 1 of 57 different disease-causing heterozygous variants in OPA1 were identified (see Supplementary Table S2 for a complete list of variants including specific type and classification). Twenty-three of the detected variants have not been reported previously. Two novel complex variants are detailed in Supplementary Figures S3 and S4. Three individuals were excluded from the study due to other binocular diseases accompanying ADOA, and two due to the lack of ADOA signs. Therefore, a total of 108 individuals from 65 different families harboring 54 different variants in OPA1 could be included.

The variants of 92 patients were classified as causing or likely causing haploinsufficiency. Sixteen patients harbored missense variants. Seven individuals, carrying either of the missense variants c.1499G>A p.(Arg500His) (n = 3), c.1628G>C p.(Gly543Ala) (n = 3), or c.1800C>G p.(Ser600Arg) (n = 1) presented with ADOA plus sensorineural hearing loss. One of these patients also exhibited signs of myopathy. Two female patients, one with the nonsense variant c.1852C>T p.(Gln618*) and one with the splice-site variant c.1754+5G>T r.(spl?), had a neurologically confirmed diagnosis of multiple sclerosis (MS). However, both showed no signs of optic neuritis at the time of their examination.

Two individual eyes were excluded from the analysis due to concurrent monocular pathology, leaving 214 eyes for morphometric analysis. Two individual macular OCT scans and four perimetry examinations were excluded due to insufficient quality. In univariable LMM, age significantly impacted the MD in the PMB (−0.108 dB/year, P = 0.003) and mGCL thickness (−0.044 µm/year; P = 0.037), but not VA (P = 0.22), MD (P = 0.13), mIPL thickness (P = 0.20), and pRNFL thickness (P = 0.19). Final multivariable examinations also showed a significant association of age with mGCL thinning (−0.057 µm/year, P = 0.023). Long-term OCT follow-ups (median = 6 years and range = 4–15 years) of macular volume scans were available in 46 eyes of 23 patients (initial age = 28 ± 15 years and range = 4–58 years), and showed an average loss of 0.26 ± 0.10 µm (i.e. 1.20 ± 0.48%) in mGCL thickness and 0.22 ± 0.11 µm (i.e. 0.96 ± 0.47%) in mIPL thickness per year (mGCL + mIPL = 0.48 ± 0.17 µm/year, i.e. 1.08 ± 0.38%).

In univariable LMM-based examination of the effect of neuroretinal structure on visual function, VA was significantly affected by thickness of total mGCL (−0.053 logMAR/µm, P < 0.001), mIPL (−0.067 logMAR/µm, P < 0.001), temporal pRNFL (−0.011 logMAR/µm, P < 0.001), and total pRNFL thickness (−0.013 logMAR/µm, P < 0.001). In addition, mGCL (0.41 dB/µm, P = 0.001) and total pRNFL thickness (0.16 dB/µm, P < 0.001) both had highly significant effects on visual field MD. Even more influence was shown on the MD in the PMB subfield by mGCL (0.74 dB/µm, P < 0.001), mIPL (0.55 dB/µm, P = 0.039), and total pRNFL thickness (0.24 dB/µm, P < 0.001), but not by temporal pRNFL thickness (P = 0.22). The univariable analysis also revealed significant associations between all quadrant sectors of the inner retinal layers and visual function (see Supplementary Tables S6, S7, S8). Among the mGCL sectors, the superior sector demonstrated the highest impact on VA (−0.045 logMAR/µm, P < 0.001) and MD (0.39 dB/µm, P < 0.001), and the inferior sector on PMB-MD (0.68 dB/µm, P < 0.001).

Results from the multivariable analyses are presented in Tables 2, 3, and Figure 2. They revealed a significant influence of mGCL (−0.031 logMAR/µm, P < 0.001) and mIPL (−0.043 logMAR/µm, P < 0.001) but not the temporal pRNFL thickness (P = 0.47) on VA. When the total pRNFL thickness was included in the model it also showed no significance on VA (P = 0.52), whereas mIPL thickness showed the highest impact (−0.043 logMAR/µm, P < 0.001), followed by mGCL thickness (−0.029 logMAR/µm, P = 0.002). MD, on the other hand, was significantly influenced by pRNFL (0.11 dB/µm, P = 0.048) but not by mGCL (P = 0.82) or mIPL thickness (P = 1.0), whereas the PMD-MD demonstrated an association only with mGCL thickness (0.75 dB/µm, P = 0.002).

Comparison of missense and haploinsufficiency categories revealed a significantly worse outcome for patients with a missense variant (see Fig. 3; Tables 12–3). In multivariable LMM, this adverse effect was evident in the functional parameters VA (0.49 vs. 0.83 logMAR, P = 0.016), MD (−3.04 vs. −11.48 dB, P = 0.005), and most clearly in the PMD-MD (−4.17 vs. −16.25 dB; P = 0.001). Absolute differences between an affected father or mother and the affected offspring in available data are summarized in Supplementary Table S9. In families with paternal transmission, the difference in the PMB-MD was significantly greater compared with families with maternal transmission, with median differences of 5.58 (3.21–10.08) dB and 0.71 (0.40–1.69) dB, respectively (P < 0.001; see also Supplementary Figure S10). Generational differences in the other variables were not significantly influenced by the sex of the parent-of-origin.

We provide an extensive data set on clinical information of 108 patients with ADOA, including visual field data for most of them. Whereas associations of mGCL and pRNFL thickness with VA have been described previously in ADOA, this is the first analysis demonstrating the aggravating effect of inner retinal layer thinning on threshold perimetry, and specifically on the reduced sensitivity in the PMB subfield. The rather diffuse RGC loss throughout the macula in OPA1-related ADOA represents a good structural correlate for the visual impairment in and around the fovea. Similar associations between inner retinal degeneration and visual function have been demonstrated in the chronic stage of Leber hereditary optic neuropathy, however, at lower functional levels.29,30

Our results clearly show that inner retinal layer thinning has a significant negative impact on VA. Both thinning of 3.2 µm in the mGCL and 2.3 µm in the mIPL predicted a reduction in VA of 0.1 logMAR (i.e. one logMAR line). This is in accordance with previous findings, where the degeneration of the GC-IPL was associated with VA reduction.17,31,32 Interestingly, in our models, global pRNFL thickness – and also the temporal quadrant – did not significantly affect VA. This suggests that the integrity of the macular inner retinal layers may represent a better morphological correlate of VA in ADOA than the pRNFL, which is likely subject to a floor effect especially in the temporal sector. However, associations between the pRNFL thickness and VA have also been described.15,33

Previous studies also examined individual macular sectors in smaller cohorts of patients with ADOA, and found the nasal sectors most affected by thinning.17,34,35 In our cohort, nasal (and inferior) thinning was predominant only in the haploinsufficiency subgroup, whereas in the missense group, the temporal sector was the thinnest. Corajevic et al. proposed that a more prominent reduction in the nasal mGCL and temporal pRNFL, both representing the PMB, have the greatest prognostic value for VA, with the GCL being the most critical layer.34 However, when investigating the effects of individual sector changes on VA, the strongest association was found with GC-IPL thickness in the superior/superotemporal sectors,31,35 which seem less affected especially in mild phenotype or early stage ADOA. The present results in our larger cohort confirm that among the individual sectors, mGCL thickness in the superior quadrant has the highest estimates for preserved visual function in OPA1-related ADOA, including both VA and MD. However, the total mGCL showed even higher estimates in LMMs, and may therefore be the more reliable and practical parameter.

Here, we report not only the standard assessment of MD, but also sensitivity reduction in the PMB cluster of the 30-2 grid. To our knowledge, visual field parameters in ADOA were not directly linked to retinal integrity objectified by OCT in previous reports. Two smaller studies have looked for associations between macular sensitivity in microperimetry and inner retinal layer thickness, but showed conflicting results.36,37 We demonstrate that pRNFL thinning significantly reduces the MD measured in the whole 30-degree radius, whereas mGCL thinning negatively impacts the sensitivity in the PMB cluster. This is especially important because the perimetric defect in ADOA is usually most pronounced in the central area.8,18,21

Our results also provide evidence that age significantly impacts disease-related changes in OPA1-related ADOA, especially in the macular area represented by light sensitivity in the PMB cluster and mGCL thickness, whereas VA, global MD, mIPL thickness, and global pRNFL thickness were statistically not associated with age. Similar to these findings, the existing literature is inconclusive regarding the impact of age on retinal structure and function in ADOA.3,16,19,21,24,31,33,38 It has been demonstrated that different OPA1 genotypes may show heterogenous disease progression.32 Based on cross-sectional results in smaller cohorts, it was also hypothesized that age-dependent loss of pRNFL and mGCL thickness occurs at a similar rate to that observed in healthy individuals, which is why abnormal disease-related retinal thinning was doubted.16,31,33,39 Because patients with ADOA usually experience a loss of RGCs early in life, often exceeding a critical threshold for visual impairment, purely age-related loss would also be responsible for the worsening of symptoms.16,40 To account for this possible effect, we corrected for age in all our models. Nevertheless, the cross-sectional analysis detected a residual effect of age on sensitivity in the PMB subfield, and also on mGCL thickness. Althought the latter effect was lost in the multivariable model, the overall results point to a relevant disease-related loss of structure and function with increasing age in eyes of patients with OPA1-related ADOA, especially in the macular area. Cross-sectional analysis may likely underestimate individual changes in this heterogeneous disease, which is evident when looking at changes in available long-term OCT follow-ups. There, the average loss of 0.48 µm (1.08%) mGCL + mIPL thickness per year was considerably higher, and also more than the reported longitudinal changes in healthy cohorts with comparable age showing mean losses of 0.09 to 0.26 µm (0.12% to 0.38%) ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer (GC-IPL) per year.41–43 Overall, this suggests that neuroretinal degeneration over time in ADOA cannot be attributed solely to normal age-related changes, and it stresses the need for longitudinal studies in ADOA to better characterize disease progression.

Missense variants in OPA1 have been reported to have a negative impact compared with variants causing haploinsufficiency, as the protein defect caused by a single amino acid change might exert a dominant negative effect.17,24,25 In this study, patients harboring missense variants showed significantly worse outcomes in multiple functional and structural parameters, giving our study a more complete view of disease-associated changes in OPA1-ADOA than previous reports.17,24,25,44 Remarkably, patients with haploinsufficiency variants were older, but nevertheless showed less reduction in visual function and retinal structure despite the likely disease progression with age.

Missense variants are also more strongly associated with the optic atrophy plus syndrome (OMIM #125250), which is reported in approximately 20% of all cases.24 Interestingly, seven individuals in our cohort are affected by sensorineural hearing loss, all of them harboring missense variants that were frequently associated with poor prognosis and additional symptoms including hypacusis.24,45–49 In addition, two individuals (1.9%) had a confirmed diagnosis of MS, a co-occurrence that has previously been estimated to be higher in patients with OPA1.24

When exploring phenotypic differences in maternal versus paternal inheritance of the pathogenic allele, we found a significantly greater variation in the PMB-MD between the affected father and child compared with families with maternal transmission. This could indicate that functional impairment in OPA1-related ADOA is to some part modified by the mitochondrial DNA background, which changes in case of paternal transmission and could improve or worsen cellular adaptation to OPA1 deficits. Parent-of-origin differences in disease expression have previously been shown for other genes (e.g. POLG, SLC25A4, HTT, or TTR),50–53 pointing to an important role of mito-nuclear interaction in hereditary mitochondrial diseases.

To this date, therapeutic options in ADOA are very limited. Studies investigating treatment effects of the ubiquinone analog idebenone in OPA1-related ADOA were either retrospective or had limited case number and duration,54,55 which reduces their evidential value. Preclinical in vivo studies investigating antisense oligonucleotides, whole gene, or trans-splicing gene therapy effectively increased wild-type OPA1 protein in the retina, and give hope for future etiotropic treatment options.56–58 Future clinical studies investigating treatment effects should include the most indicative parameters for disease-related changes in this study, namely mGCL thickness and light sensitivity in the central visual field.

This study has several notable strengths. Primarily, the inclusion of clinical data of 214 eyes of 108 individuals represents a relatively large sample, which enabled a more detailed statistical analysis in this rare disease. Albeit retrospectively analyzed, the extracted data set from our single center had a highly coherent methodology and underwent strict quality control. However, it is also important to consider the limitations of this study. Due to its retrospective nature, a complete data set was not available for each of the patients. Second, because this is a cross-sectional analysis and longitudinal data were too sparse for an in-depth analysis, the interpretation of individual changes over time was limited. Furthermore, data of axial length was not available for this cohort and therefore no correction could be performed in the analyses, as axial length might influence the measurements of retinal layer thickness. Inclusion of SE as a covariate aimed to reduce possible bias but this has to be kept in mind when interpreting the results, particularly because SE significantly affected pRNFL thickness but not mGCL or mIPL thickness. Furthermore, due to the high genetic heterogeneity in OPA1-related ADOA, an in-depth interpretation of differences for different genotypes was not possible, and could therefore only be corrected for by adding a random intercept. Only 16 patients with missense variants were included, possibly limiting statistical power.

Currently, only the class of missense variants can be assigned to a more severe phenotype, whereas other genetic variant subgroups (nonsense, frame-shift, splice-site, and deletions) did not differ in disease severity (data not shown). In the future, quantitative analyses at the RNA expression level could possibly provide more information on the individual pathogenicity of different OPA1 variants.59 Another limitation is that, in some splice-site or deep intronic variants, molecular biological verification of haploinsufficiency was not available. However, in most of these cases aberrant splicing was a very likely consequence, consistent with OPA1 haploinsufficiency and the clinical phenotype of ADOA.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that VA and the severity of the central scotoma in OPA1-related ADOA are closely associated with inner retinal layer thickness. It indicates a slow but relevant progression of the disease over time, and confirms that heterozygous missense variants in OPA1 more likely lead to a more severe phenotype. The integrity of the inner retinal layers in patients with OPA1-related ADOA can be used as a good estimate for visual function. The inclusion of perimetry as a clinical biomarker may allow for a more comprehensive monitoring of ADOA and is recommended given the importance of patient-oriented outcomes.
