# Targeted gene sequencing and hearing follow-up in 7501 newborns reveals an improved strategy for newborn hearing screening.
**Authors:** Hao C, Hu X, Guo R, Qi Z, Jin F, Zhang X, Xie L, Liu H, Liu Y, Ni X, Li W
**Journal:** Eur J Hum Genet (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41431-024-01711-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41431-024-01711-x)

## Content

Hearing loss is a common congenital condition. Concurrent newborn hearing and limited genetic screening has been implemented in China for the last decade. However, the role of gene sequencing screening has not been evaluated. In this study, we enrolled 7501 newborns (52.7% male, 47.3% female) in our Newborn Screening with Targeted Sequencing (NESTS) program, and 90 common deafness genes were sequenced for them. Hearing status assessments were conducted via telephone from February 2021 to August 2022, for children aged 3 to 48 months. Of the universal newborn hearing screening, 126 (1.7%) newborns did not pass. Targeted sequencing identified 150 genetically positive newborns (2.0%), with 25 exhibiting dual-positive results in both screening. Following diagnostic audiometry revealed 18 hearing loss newborns and half of them had abnormal results in both screening. The positive predictive value for universal newborn hearing screening alone was merely 14.3% (18/126). However, when combined with targeted sequencing, this rate increased to 36.0% (9/25). Furthermore, limited genetic screening identified 316 carriers of hot-spot variants, but none exhibited biallelic variants. All 15 hot-spot carriers who failed physical screening demonstrated normal hearing during follow-up. In this cohort study of 7501 Newborns, Combining targeted sequencing with universal newborn hearing screening demonstrated technical feasibility and clinical utility of identifying individuals with hearing loss, especially when coupled with genetic counseling and closed-loop management. It is suggested to use this integrated method as an improved strategy instead of the current limited genetic screening program in some regions of China.

Abstract

Hearing loss is a common congenital condition. Concurrent newborn hearing and limited genetic screening has been implemented in China for the last decade. However, the role of gene sequencing screening has not been evaluated. In this study, we enrolled 7501 newborns (52.7% male, 47.3% female) in our Newborn Screening with Targeted Sequencing (NESTS) program, and 90 common deafness genes were sequenced for them. Hearing status assessments were conducted via telephone from February 2021 to August 2022, for children aged 3 to 48 months. Of the universal newborn hearing screening, 126 (1.7%) newborns did not pass. Targeted sequencing identified 150 genetically positive newborns (2.0%), with 25 exhibiting dual-positive results in both screening. Following diagnostic audiometry revealed 18 hearing loss newborns and half of them had abnormal results in both screening. The positive predictive value for universal newborn hearing screening alone was merely 14.3% (18/126). However, when combined with targeted sequencing, this rate increased to 36.0% (9/25). Furthermore, limited genetic screening identified 316 carriers of hot-spot variants, but none exhibited biallelic variants. All 15 hot-spot carriers who failed physical screening demonstrated normal hearing during follow-up. In this cohort study of 7501 Newborns, Combining targeted sequencing with universal newborn hearing screening demonstrated technical feasibility and clinical utility of identifying individuals with hearing loss, especially when coupled with genetic counseling and closed-loop management. It is suggested to use this integrated method as an improved strategy instead of the current limited genetic screening program in some regions of China.

Subject terms: Genetics research, Genetic testing

Introduction

Hearing loss (HL) is a common condition that can result in language delay, poor social skill development, and academic performance when early onset. The incidence is approximately 1.5 per 1000 newborns and over half of cases are caused by genetic alterations [1, 2]. This number increases to 2.7 and 3.5 per 1000 children at the age of 5 years and during adolescence, respectively [1]. By age 80, about 50% of the population experiences hearing loss [3]. In China, around 60,000 newborns are expected to have hearing loss per year [4].

While newborn hearing screening programs have improved early detection since the 1990s, they target mild to severe prelingual hearing loss and lack the ability to identify genetic causes. Recent efforts in China have introduced concurrent hearing and limited genetic screening (LGS) [5–9]. However, focusing solely on homozygous or compound heterozygous hot-spot variants has been considered insufficient due to the genetic complexity of hearing loss, involving more than 100 genes [10, 11]. Nevertheless, studies regarding the technical feasibility and clinical effectiveness of expanded genetic newborn screening for hearing loss have been rarely reported.

The advent of next-generation sequencing (NGS) has opened new possibilities for newborn screening (NBS) [12–17]. More recently, we provided evidence that the Newborn Screening with Targeted Sequencing (NESTS) program is feasible for NBS in clinical practice [18], and 90 well-curated common deafness genes were included (Table S1). This study evaluates the outcomes of 7501 newborns that underwent NESTS-based genetic hearing screening and universal newborn hearing screening (UNHS). Our results suggest that targeted sequencing of newborn hearing screening (TSNHS) integrated with UNHS holds considerable potential for enhancing newborn hearing screening and offers a promising strategy to replace the LGS program.

Materials and methods

Newborns recruitment

Newborns were recruited from Shunyi Maternal and Children’s Hospital of Beijing Children’s Hospital from October 2018 to May 2021. After receiving pre-test counseling, voluntary pregnant women participated in this study before delivery. Dried blood spot specimens were collected three days after birth for subsequent TSNHS. This study received approval from the institutional review board of Beijing Children’s Hospital (2017-K-39). Written informed consents to participate were obtained from the parents of all newborns.

Hearing screening

Initial screening of UNHS was conducted by otoacoustic emission (OAE) test within 3 days after birth in the Obstetrical Department. For newborns who failed to pass the initial hearing screening, both OAE and automated auditory brainstem response (AABR) were retested at 42 days of age. Those with persistent failure during re-examination were recommended for diagnostic audiometry at three months of age. Further screening at the age of both 6–11 months and 1–2 years was also recommended in community hospitals.

Limited genetic screening

Current newborn genetic hearing screening employed a multiplex allele-specific PCR-based universal array to identify limited hot-spot variants in Beijing [5]. These hot-spot variants include GJB2 (MIM: 121011) variants c.235delC, c.299_300delAT, c.176del16, and c.35delG, as well as variants c.919-2 A > G, c.2168 A > G, c.1174 A > T, c.1226 G > A, c.1229 C > T, c.1975G > C, c.2027 T > A and c.1707+5 G > A in SLC26A4 (MIM: 605646), c.538 C > T  in GJB3 (MIM: 603324) and mtDNA 12S rRNA (MIM: 561000) variants m.1555 A > G and m.1494 C > T. These results were collected from guardians of infants through medical records or genetic counseling follow-up for a comparison with TSNHS results.

Targeted sequencing and variants interpretation

Hearing loss genes were collected from public databases such as Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man, Human Gene Mutation Database, Genetics Home Reference, Hereditary Hearing Loss Homepage, and Deafness Variation Database. Gene curation was performed for the collected genes. Specifically, gene-disease relationship curation results were collected from Gene Curation Coalition (GenCC) Database (thegencc.org/) and ClinGen Gene-Disease Validity Database (clinicalgenome.org). For genes not collected in GenCC or ClinGen, the relationship was curated manually according to ClinGen Gene-Disease Validity Standard Operating Procedure, version 7 (clinicalgenome.org/curation-activities). Detailed information on library structure and sequencing, quality control criteria, variants calling, and interpretation was described in the previously published study [18]. Locally optimized ACMG/AMP interpretation guidelines were used to classify the variants [18, 19]. Newborns with the following three types of genotypes were considered as genetically positive: 1) heterozygous pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were detected in dominant genes; 2) homozygous/hemizygous pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants were detected in recessive genes; and 3) two or more variants in the same recessive gene were detected, and at least one was pathogenic or likely pathogenic. Reports were issued before Day 42 after birth referred for routine physical re-examination.

Phone follow-up

The first round of phone follow-up was conducted for the parents of all tested newborns from February 2021 to August 2021, when the ages of the newborns ranged from 3 months to 34 months. Information on up-to-date hearing status, language development, self-reported diagnostic audiology evaluation results if applicable, and hearing loss family history was collected. For newborns with genetically positive results, a second round of phone follow-up was performed from July to August 2022, when the ages of the newborns ranged between 19 months and 48 months.

Genetic counseling

Genetic counseling was offered after the parents received the genetic reports. For newborns with genetically positive results, we would take the initiative to contact them and strongly recommend genetic counseling, which covers variant pathogenicity, inheritance mode, age of onset, clinical symptoms, and recurrence risk. Parental validation and specialized clinical examinations were recommended as needed.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 20.0 and Prism 8 software. The chi-square test was used to evaluate differences in the false-negative rate of LGS across two variant types, as well as disparities in genetic counseling rates and family histories among newborns with different TSNHS results; the corrected chi-square test was used to assess the TSNHS positive rate among newborns with normal and abnormal UNHS results, with significance threshold set at P < 0.05.

Results

Study population

TSNHS was conducted on 9426 newborns from Shunyi Maternal and Children’s Hospital of Beijing Children’s Hospital from October 2018 to May 2021. 401 newborns without allele-specific array results and 220 newborns with incomplete or missing UNHS results were excluded. Phone interviews were finished in 85.2% (7501/8805) newborns (Fig. 1). Therefore, 7501 newborns (52.7% male, 47.3% female) completed the comprehensive screening and were included in the study.

A total of 90 genes were curated as hearing loss causative genes

The screening panel encompassed 135 conditions resulting from 90 genes (Table S1). Most conditions (64.7%) manifested during newborn or infancy, with only 3.0% occurring in adolescence (Table S1). Conditions onset after adulthood were not screened due to ethical reasons. Notably, 93.2% of gene-disease relationships were categorized as Definitive or Strong (Table S1).

UNHS and limited genetic screening results

For UNHS, 92.7% newborns passed the initial Day 3 OAE hearing screening (6950/7501), while those who did not pass (551/7501) underwent a Day 42 OAE/AABR re-examination (50 were not available). Of these, 375 (74.9%) had normal results of both OAE and AABR, and 126 (25.1%) had abnormal result(s) for at least one test. The positive rate was 1.68% (126/7501) after the two-step screening (Fig. 2).

Furthermore, for the 37 newborns with abnormal results of Day 3 OAE, Day 42 OAE, and Day 42 AABR tests, the GPR was as high as 24.3%. UNHS was complemented by LGS for hot-spot variants in GJB2, SLC26A4, GJB3 and MT-RNR1 [5, 20, 21], which identified 316 carriers and 15 failed to pass the two-step UNHS. However, none of them were diagnosed with hearing loss during follow-up (Fig. 2). Integrating LGS with UNHS did not improve the detection efficiency of true positive cases in this cohort.

Comparison between TSNHS and LGS

After target sequencing, we compared the hot-spot variants results of GJB2 and SLC26A4 identified by TSNHS with LGS. We found that LGS missed 20 carriers that were identified by TSNHS and further confirmed by Sanger sequencing, resulting in a 5.9% false-negative rate (Table 1, Fig. 3). Notably, indels were more frequently overlooked than SNVs (P < 0.001, χ2 = 0.86) according to our results.

Additionally, LGS results showed that none of these newborns carried biallelic hot-spot variants. In contrast, TSNHS identified 11 newborns with a single hot-spot variant and another non-hot-spot variant. During follow-up, 55% (6/11) failed to pass both initial and second hearing screening. Thus, TSNHS is more technically reliable and feasible in identifying the genetic basis of hearing loss compared to LGS.

The genetically positive rate and gene distribution

After interpretation following the locally optimized ACMG/AMP guidelines [19], 150 cases (2.0%) were classified as genetically positive, encompassing 163 pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in 39 genes. The mutation types of 163 P/LP variants are shown in Fig. 4a. A substantial portion of these variants (77/163, 47.2%) were previously unreported in ClinVar or HGMD, emphasizing the population-specific genetic spectrum. GJB2 was the most affected gene, followed by USH2A, MYO7A, and SLC26A4 (Fig. 4b). Among genetically positive cases, autosomal dominant inheritance and autosomal recessive inheritance accounted for 52.7% and 47.3%, respectively.

TSNHS helped to increase the positive predictive value and effectively targeted the hearing loss individuals

For the 150 genetically positive cases, 131 finished the second round of phone follow-up between 19 and 48 months of age (Figs. 1 and 2). Among them, 25 failed to pass both the initial OAE test and Day 42 re-examination included in UNHS. (Their genetic findings are shown in Table S2). In this dual-positive group, 14 underwent diagnostic audiology evaluations and nine (Nos. 4, 7, 9, 10, 11, 15, 17, 18, and 23, 64.3%) had already been diagnosed with hearing loss. Among them, two (Nos. 4 and 17) already received cochlear implantation by the time of follow-up. In the genetically negative cases (7351/7501), 48 infants underwent diagnostic audiology evaluations. Nine of genetically negative cases (18.8%) failed the test and most of them had conductive hearing loss. Other 64 newborns who failed UNHS did not undergo the diagnostic audiology evaluation due to apparently normal hearing (Fig. 2). Altogether, the results showed that the clinical diagnosis rate for early-onset hearing loss was 2.4‰ (18/7501).

TSNHS increased the positive predictive value, from 14.3% (18/126) for UNHS alone to 36.0% (9/25) for combined screening. Genetically positive rates in newborns who passed or did not pass UNHS were 1.7% (125/7325) and 19.8% (25/126), respectively (P < 0.001, χ2 = 197.42, Figs. 2, S1 and Table S3). Therefore, these results indicated TSNHS can narrow down the number of newborns with suspected hearing loss and more accurately target suspected positive individuals.

Targeted sequencing information was correlated with the newborn’s family history and provided awareness and precaution of hearing loss risk

Out of 7501 newborns, 5375 (71.6%) parents opted to visit the genetic counseling clinic upon receiving their child’s reports. Because hearing loss was not the only disorder screened in the program [18], the choice to visit the clinic did not significantly differ between genetically positive (73.5%) and negative (71.6%) newborns(P = 0.64, χ2 = 0.21). After genetic counseling and phone follow-up, 102 newborns (1.5%) had a family history of hearing problems, 10 of them were genetically positive and 92 were negative. Family history was significantly more reported in the genetically positive group 6.7% (10/150), than the 1.3% (92/7351) in the negative group (P < 0.001, χ2 = 30.78). In the ten genetically positive newborns, five also failed to pass UNHS (Nos. 11, 12, 14, 16, and 21, Table S2), the other five passed UNHS but all carried likely pathogenic variants in autosomal dominant genes which might cause delayed-onset hearing loss (Table S4). Among the 92 newborns with hearing loss family history and negative genetic results, 59 (64.1%) had variants of uncertain significance in dominant inherited genes which need long-term follow-up for pathogenicity assessment (e.g., MYH14, WFS1, MYO7A, etc.), highlighting the value of targeted sequencing in predicting delayed-onset and progressive hearing loss.

Targeted sequencing is informative for clinical management

Of the six diagnosed newborns with hearing loss, two received cochlear implantation by the time of follow-up (No. 5 and 18, Table S2). The patient (No. 5) with the SOX10 c.470 C > T (p. Ala157Val) variant causing Waardenburg syndrome, was identified by TSNHS [18]. He was found to have Hirschsprung disease and nystagmus after birth. TSNHS promptly identified the pathogenicity of the c.470 C > T variant, expediting diagnosis and intervention. Patient No. 18, with homozygous GJB2 c.109 G > A (p.Val37Ile) variant, underwent cochlear implantation. This variant led to deafness with age-dependent low penetrance (17%) [22]. Many people (44%) were asymptomatic or had delayed-onset hearing impairment [23]. Genetic counseling for this variant is extremely complex and should be more rigorous. We identified 13 newborns with homozygous GJB2 c.109 G > A variant and 11 with a heterozygous c.109 G > A and another GJB2 pathogenic variant. For the 13 homozygotes, 92.3% failed to pass the Day 3 OAE screening, and 61.5% also did not pass the Day 42 OAE/AABR tests. For 11 compound heterozygotes, 63.6% (7/11) failed to pass both Day 3 OAE screening and Day 42 OAE/AABR tests. Annual examination by physicians familiar with hereditary hearing impairment was recommended for them. Six of them (25.0%, 6/24) were diagnosed with hearing loss by three months (Table S2). Cochlear implantation was considered for GJB2-related hearing impairment, with a focus on noise exposure avoidance.

Discussion

UNHS, established in the 1990s, successfully identifies infants with hearing loss [24]. However, it has certain limitations: 1) it primarily detects moderate, severe and profound hearing loss, missing milder cases; 2) It doesn’t catch progressive or delayed-onset hearing loss; 3) it is prone to false positives due to environmental factors, e.g., debris in the ear canal and fluid in the middle ear; and 4) it does not provide information on genetic etiology, which may be meaningful for intervention. Over the last decade, LGS has emerged as a valuable addition for UNHS. Studies across different regions of China have confirmed its effectiveness [5–9]. However, it has a significant limitation: primarily targets hot-spot variants, neglecting many other potential causes of hereditary hearing loss.

In light of advancements in sequencing technology, some expert panels proposed considering the use of NGS-based genetic screening for newborns since 2012 [25–27]. The current study was designed to evaluate whether the addition of NGS to UNHS could improve the performance of UNHS, and the combination of UNHS with LGS. We have performed panel sequencing for newborn screening since 2016, providing evidence that targeted sequencing is a practical and cost-effective approach in clinical settings [18]. Expanding on our previous work, this study focuses on implementing NGS for screening hereditary hearing loss in newborns, demonstrating the technical feasibility and clinical utility of TSNHS when integrated with genetic counseling and a closed-loop management.

Hearing loss is an extremely heterogeneous disorder. Different institutions use gene panels of various sizes (less than 100 genes to over 200 genes) for diagnosis [28]. In our study, we selected 90 common early-onset hearing loss genes in our screening program. After sequencing and analysis of 7501 newborns, 150 were classified as genetically positive, involving 39 genes. However, none of genetically positive newborns could be detected by LGS. Meanwhile, the method of allele-specific array also had a nonnegligible false-negative rate according to our results (Table 1). Further improvement is urgently needed for better screening performance.

The GJB2 c.109 G > A (p.Val37Ile) variant was omitted from previous LGS due to its relatively high carrier rate in various Chinese cities (2.9% to 14.3%) [29–31]. In our study, it had an allele frequency of 2.5% (37/15002) in 7501 newborns, comparable to previous reports [30, 31]. A total of 24 homozygotes or compound heterozygotes were identified and six of them (25.0%, 6/24) were diagnosed with hearing loss by three months (Table S2), showing p.Val37Ile leads to deafness, potentially prelingual [23]. The remaining 18 infants needed regular audiology evaluations as they might develop hearing loss in childhood or adulthood, underlining TSNHS’s enhanced screening efficiency and early diagnosis benefits.

TSNHS had benefits in shortening the diagnosis duration and uncovering positive cases that initially passed UNHS. In our study, TSNHS combined with UNHS revealed 25 dual-positive individuals, including nine with sensorineural hearing loss. The clinical confirmative diagnosis rate of genetic hearing loss was 1.2‰ (9/7501), but this proportion is underestimated for the following reasons: 1) some subtypes of hearing loss onset in childhood, and accordingly, clinical confirmation is not available in the early ages; 2) According to our telephone interview, a large fraction of neonatal guardians with abnormal UNHS results were reluctant to undergo a diagnostic hearing assessment, and the guardians believe that the infant has a hearing response. Based on this, we believe that genetically high-risk neonates who fail to pass postnatal hearing screening should be recommended to directly undergo clinical audiology diagnosis, which not only shortens the diagnosis duration but also reduces loss to follow-up due to multiple hospital visits.

Genetic screening also had the advantage in uncovering HL cases who passed UNHS, which was emphasized in previous literature [5, 6, 9, 32]. In our study, among 122 infants with genetically positive results who passed OAE and AABR screening, and five newborns failed UNHS but passed diagnostic audiometry (Table S5). These newborns require additional follow-up based on different genetic results: 1) for newborns with one P/LP variant and one VUS variant in recessive hearing loss genes (e.g., USH2A in 12 cases): perform parental verification to detect the status of in-trans or in-cis first. For in-trans cases, the pathogenicity of the VUS variant needs further determination based on follow-up hearing results. If the child remains normal beyond the disease onset age, the VUS variant may be downgraded to likely benign. 2) for newborns with heterozygous P/LP variant in genes causing both recessive and dominant hearing loss (e.g., MYO7A in 8 cases): recessive hearing loss usually presents earlier (e.g., prelingual), whereas dominant hearing loss manifests later (e.g., childhood-onset). If the child has no hearing issues beyond the dominant hearing loss onset age, the heterozygous variant may be related to recessive hearing loss. 3) for newborns with P/LP variant in genes with dominant late-onset or Slowly Progressive Hearing Loss (e.g., SLC17A8 in 6 cases): A careful review of the family history is necessary. Sanger sequencing for the parents should be carried out if required. Based on the age of onset, close follow-up of the hearing condition should be conducted. 4) for newborns with mutation of incomplete penetrance or variable expressivity: For instance, the GJB2 c.109 G > A variant (in 11 cases) can cause hearing loss at any age or even not affect hearing. Also, diseases caused by some gene mutations may have significant clinical variability, with hearing loss as one possible symptom. On the other hand, five newborns (4.1%) had a familial hearing loss history and carried likely pathogenic variants in autosomal dominant genes (Table S4). According to our phone follow-up, none of them received diagnostic audiometry test at three months, and all parents reported seemingly normal hearing. Their potential for delayed-onset or progressive hearing loss warrants continued family verification and long-term follow-up.

It’s important to note that congenital hearing loss can be also caused by non-genetic factors. Maternal infections during pregnancy, such as rubella, cytomegalovirus, or toxoplasmosis, may lead to inner ear malformations and subsequent hearing impairment in the child [33, 34]. Furthermore, exposure to ototoxic drugs like certain antibiotics, anti-epileptic and anti-cancer medications during pregnancy can also contribute to hearing loss [35, 36]. Other non-genetic factors include birth complications such as prematurity, low birth weight, severe jaundice, and delivery-related trauma that can damage the auditory system [37, 38]. In our study, 18 newborns failed diagnostic audiometry at three months of age. Nine of them were diagnosed with genetic mutations, while the other nine tested negative for genetic factors. This aligned with previous research findings, suggesting that approximately half of congenital hearing loss cases are genetic in nature [1, 2]. Among these nine genetic negative cases, five exhibited indications of conductive hearing loss during the diagnostic audiometry. This suggested that possibilities such as middle ear infections or foreign bodies in the ear canal cannot be ruled out. Further examination may be warranted to investigate these potential causes.

Providing additional newborn screening information may raise parental anxiety [17, 39, 40]. Therefore, effective genetic counseling is critical. For a successful newborn genetic and hearing screening program, genetic counseling and post-screening management are vital. Otherwise, early identification of HL will have no benefits but increase the psychological burden of guardians. Our study achieved a 71.6% genetic counseling rate (the rest usually have no variants revealed in the reports). A previous survey showed that 87.4% of respondents believed that genetic counseling was helpful. It relieved anxiety and stress in 95.2% of participants [18]. Positive screening results led to parental validation, clinical examinations, and specialized treatment, greatly supporting early screening, diagnosis, and intervention.

This study also has limitations: 1) the information collected from telephone interviews, to some extent, limits the rigor of the conclusion, and long-term follow-up is needed to assess whether they develop delayed-onset hearing loss. Under this constraint, the accurate positive predictive value is not available. 2) This research is a single-center study with relatively homogenous newborn genetics, and the diagnosed newborns were limited; multicenter studies on diverse populations and more diagnosed HL newborns may yield more reasonable conclusions. 3) While sequencing costs reduced significantly, information regarding cost-effectiveness analysis is not conducted in this study. Admittedly, the current cost of TSNHS is significantly higher than that of UNHS. However, advancements in technology and increased screening capacity are expected to further reduce sequencing costs, and the development of variant databases will decrease labor costs associated with interpretation. 4) The best choice of the gene list for screening depends on the genetic spectrum of different populations, and novel HL-related genes are consistently being identified. This panel should be open for revision based on clinical practice. An optimized library capture strategy by mixing customized and commercial probes will include the mtDNA variants and pathogen DNA (e.g., cytomegalovirus) in future research. In addition, implementing NGS for newborn screening requires robust data quality control and report interpretation capabilities.

This study demonstrates the technical feasibility and clinical utility of applying targeted sequencing for newborn hearing screening. Combining UNHS and targeted sequencing could provide valuable clinical benefits. As sequencing technology advances and in-house databases grow, there is potential to transform hearing loss genetic screening from limited allele-specific arrays to comprehensive targeted sequencing. Further research is needed to determine cost-effectiveness and long-term benefits, as well as the generalizability of the findings in other large populations.
