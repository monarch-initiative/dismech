1. INTRODUCTION
Pediatric heart failure (HF) secondary to cardiomyopathies, acquired heart disease, and congenital heart disease
(CHD) remains associated with significant morbidity and mortality. These guidelines represent an update from the
International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation guidelines for the management of pediatric HF that were
published in 2014.
1
The purpose of this update is to incorporate advancements in pharmacologic therapies and
new approaches in the evaluation and management of children with HF since the prior guideline publication.
These guidelines are not intended for use in the management of HF related to left to right shunts and are primarily
focused on the population of children with HF due to dysfunction of the myocardium. Because the indications for
heart transplantation in children, selection and management of ventricular assist devices in children and patients
with CHD, management of cardiovascular implantable electronic devices in children, the diagnosis and treatment
of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), and the diagnosis and management of cardiac complications of
muscular dystrophies are covered by existing consensus statements or guidelines, they are largely not included
in this document.
2-6
The strength and level of evidence were determined based on the standard approach for International Society
for Heart and Lung Transplantation guidelines as shown in the table below. However, due to limited evidence and
the paucity of clinical trials in children with HF, most of the guidelines are based on consensus and therefore
classified as Level C. Additionally, in some areas of HF management, there is a complete lack of information so no
recommendations were made. These challenges highlight opportunities for future research to better inform the
care of children with HF.

2. DEFINITION AND CLASSIFICATION OF PEDIATRIC HEART FAILURE
HF in children is a complex clinical syndrome resulting from impaired ventricular function (ejection or relaxation),
volume or pressure overload, either alone or in combination.
1,7
There are numerous etiologies that require a
synthesis of clinical, hemodynamic, and neurohumoral data. There is no universal approach to diagnosing HF as
each case may weigh different elements of medical history, cardiac imaging, cardiac catheterization, or
increasingly biomarkers. Signs and symptoms can also be diverse based on pathophysiology and age.
Traditional symptoms include poor growth, feeding difficulties, respiratory distress, fatigue, and exercise
intolerance.
2.1. Staging and Classification
The American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA) Staging of HF has been previously
adopted within pediatric HF guidelines.
1,7
This staging system has the advantage of identifying patients at risk
(Stage A) and those with early cardiac disease (Stage B) prior to the onset of HF symptoms (Stage C) or
advanced HF (Stage D). This staging strategy also provides a longitudinal assessment that discriminates between
current and past HF symptoms.
Progression and advanced stages of HF have been associated with reduced survival in adult HF
8
and have
guided therapeutic interventions. The most recent iteration of the ACC/AHA staging from adult HF guidelines
addresses the evolving role of biomarkers and structural changes for recognition of patients at risk of developing
HF and can be adopted in pediatrics with minor revisions
9
( Table 1 ). This iterative change remains important as
new therapeutic interventions target early disease as highlighted in a recent AHA Statement focused on treatment
strategies for pediatric cardiomyopathy.
10

2.1.2. Ross Classification
In lieu of NYHA classification, the Ross Classification was developed for grading HF in infants and young
children
11
( Table 2 ). It preserves the simplicity of the NYHA classification while also integrating common
symptoms of HF in younger patients.
2.1.3. Other Scoring Systems
There have been other attempts to classify or score HF. One proposed system is the New York University Pediatric
Heart Failure Index.
12
In this tool, points are assigned based on signs and symptoms, the patient’s medical
regimen, and the ventricular anatomy to derive a total score between 0 and 30 and showed initial promise of
grading pediatric HF; however, the index is influenced by practice variation, has not been validated in larger trials,
or achieved wider adoption. The recently concluded PANORMA-HF trial comparing sacubitril/valsartan to
enalapril within pediatric left ventricular systolic dysfunction utilized a novel global rank end-point derived by
ranking patients based on clinical events, NYHA/Ross classification and patient–reported HF symptoms
13
;
however, the trial results have not been published and the applicability of the global rank outside of a trial remain
unclear.
RECOMMENDATION
Documentation of HF severity and staging is recommended to facilitate monitoring of disease progression
and patient management (Class I, Level of Evidence C).
2.2. Classification by Ejection Fraction
The 2022 Adult Heart Failure guidelines proposed a new mechanism for classifying HF by left ventricular ejection
fraction (EF). This was sought because of differing prognosis and response to treatments and because most adult
clinical trials select patients based on EF. It is also recognized that EF in isolation does not differentiate the
complex etiologies and trajectories of adult HF patients. The guideline harmonized recent adult randomized
controlled trials (RCTs) to identify 4 distinct categories
9
: HF with reduced EF (HFrEF), HF with improved EF
(HFimpEF), HF with mildly reduced EF (HFmrEF), and HF with preserved EF (HFpEF).
While various algorithms have been investigated in adults for the diagnosis of HFpEF, it is not clear how these
may translate to the pediatric or congenital population.
14-16
Diagnosis of HFpEF in children should include a
thorough history (e.g., orthopnea, exertional dyspnea, fatigue, exercise intolerance, feeding intolerance), physical
exam (e.g., hepatomegaly, jugular venous distention, peripheral edema, S3 gallop, pulmonary crackles or rales),
electrocardiogram, and an echocardiogram.
17-23
Assessment of natriuretic peptides, exercise stress testing,
cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and right heart catheterization can be helpful adjunctive diagnostic
tools. The most common etiologies of HFpEF in children include CHD and cardiomyopathies.

3. DIAGNOSIS
The basic diagnostic approach in children with suspected HF involves clinical evaluation (history and physical
examination), 12-lead electrocardiogram, chest X-ray, and echocardiogram. Genetic testing and laboratory
evaluations, such as serum B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) or N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-
proBNP), have utility in the diagnosis and prognosis of disease.
3.1. Genetic Testing
Genetic testing in pediatric patients with cardiomyopathy is a rapidly evolving field and the recommendations
have been updated to reflect the latest data.
25-27
Testing can include a targeted panel, a comprehensive panel,
exome sequencing, and/or genome sequencing. Genetic testing can establish a molecular diagnosis which may
aid in management and prognostication. Moreover, when a pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant is found,
cascade genetic testing can be offered to first–degree family members to identify those who are gene positive
and at risk to develop disease. There are some special circumstances where exome or genome sequencing may
be the most appropriate first step. Mitochondrial sequencing is a separate test that should be considered in cases
where there is a high suspicion for a mitochondrial disorder.
RECOMMENDATION
1. Comprehensive or targeted panel genetic testing is recommended for pediatric patients affected with any
subtype of cardiomyopathy (Class I, Level of Evidence C).
2. Variant–specific genetic testing is recommended for first–degree family members after the identification of a
causative pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant in the index case (Class I, Level of Evidence C).
3. Exome or genome sequencing can be useful in patients with severe and/or complex cardiomyopathy after
inconclusive panel gene testing (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).

3.2. Laboratory Testing
As there is general consensus that BNP/NT-proBNP testing should be performed as part of the initial evaluation
and is recommended for monitoring and to assess response to therapy this recommendation was updated to
Class I.
28
Testing for underlying metabolic or mitochondrial causes of cardiomyopathy may be indicated during
the initial presentation and if there is suspicion of a multisystem disease.
29,30
RECOMMENDATION
1. BNP/NT-proBNP is recommended in the integrated evaluation and monitoring of patients with known HF to
further define disease severity, disease progression, and response to therapy (Class I, Level of Evidence B).
2. BNP/NT-proBNP is recommended as an adjunctive marker, not a stand-alone test, to aid in the diagnosis of
new HF in symptomatic patients (Class I, Level of Evidence B).
3. Laboratory evaluation for metabolic disorders, including lactate, pyruvate, ammonia, plasma amino acids,
urine organic acids, free/total carnitine, and acylcarnitine profile, is reasonable in patients with cardiomyo -
pathy (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
3.3. Cardiac Imaging—Echocardiography
Echocardiogram is the initial imaging tool in establishing HF phenotype. There is growing experience with the use
of three-dimensional echocardiography, strain, and tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) to assess cardiac function in
pediatric patient.
18,31,32
Use of these modalities is reasonable in the assessment of children with HF, but evidence
for their utility remains limited.
RECOMMENDATION
1. Standard two-dimensional (2D) imaging to evaluate cavity dimensions, wall thickness, left ventricle (LV) mass,
and LV function is recommended in all children with cardiomyopathy or HF to aid in diagnosis, assess re -
sponse to therapy, track disease progression, or assess for LV remodeling (Class I, Level of Evidence B).
2. Diastolic function, including mitral inflow patterns, mitral annular TDI, and pulmonary venous Doppler flow
patterns by echocardiography, can be useful in children with cardiomyopathy or HF (Class IIa, Level of
Evidence C).
3. Three-dimensional imaging, measurement of strain, and TDI can be useful for the evaluation of children with
cardiomyopathy or HF (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
4. Doppler-derived estimations of right heart pressures (right ventricular systolic pressure and pulmonary artery
pressures) can be useful to monitor response to treatment and progression of disease (Class IIa, Level of
Evidence C).
5. Fractional area change from the apical 4-chamber view and tricuspid annular planar systolic excursion are
reasonable for the quantitative assessment of right ventricle (RV) function by two-dimensional
echocardiography (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
6. TDI assessment of tricuspid annular motion can be useful for the assessment of RV longitudinal
function (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
7. Doppler echocardiography may be useful for the assessment of RV diastolic function (Class IIb, Level of
Evidence C).
8. Quantitative techniques, such as fractional area change, EF, annular excursion, and TDI, may be considered
for serial follow-up of children with single ventricle heart disease (Class IIb, Level of Evidence C).

Cardiac MRI can be used in patients with HF for assessment of fibrosis, edema, or inflammation, to define the
phenotype of cardiomyopathy (arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, left ventricular noncompaction, sarcoidosis, or

1. MRI is recommended in the diagnostic evaluation of arrhythmogenic ventricular cardiomyopathy in older

2. Cardiac MRI is reasonable to assess LV function, LV remodeling, and for the phenotyping of cardiomyopathy
in children if echo imaging windows are limited (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
3. In children with RV failure, MRI is reasonable to assess RV size and EF (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
4. MRI is reasonable for the assessment of fibrosis, edema, or inflammation in unexplained cardiomyopathy or

5. Cardiac MRI can be useful for volumetric and functional analysis of a single ventricle (Class IIa, Level of
Evidence B).
6. Cardiac computerized tomography angiogram is reasonable to define coronary artery origins and anatomy if
the echocardiogram is equivocal (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
3.5. Exercise and Physical Activity
3.5.1. Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing
Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) is an important component of the diagnostic and prognostic evaluation
of pediatric HF. Giardini
40
evaluated the association of peak oxygen consumption with clinical outcomes in 82
children with DCM and found, in an adjusted analysis, that a peak VO
2
of < 62% predicted was associated with a
significantly higher likelihood of death or heart transplant within 2 years (50.6% vs 4.4%, p = 0.003, HR = 10.78).
Similar findings were noted by Lytrivi and colleagues,
41
who evaluated 50 children followed at a single center
undergoing heart transplant evaluation, and found that those with biventricular circulation and a peak VO
2
< 50%
predicted (p < 0.001, HR 4.7) and/or a VE/VCO
2
slope ≥34 (p < 0.001 HR 2.7) were more likely to reach the
composite outcome (death prior to heart transplant, initiation of mechanical circulatory support [MCS], or heart
transplant at highest urgency status). Furthermore, the AHA scientific statement published in 2007 proposes that
heart transplant is indicated for children with Stage C HF and severe limitation of activity, defined as a peak
oxygen consumption < 50% predicted if measurable.
2
3.5.2. Six-Minute Walk Test
The 6-minute walk test (6MWT) is a standardized measure of physical endurance that can be performed in most
children ≥6 years old and does not require the same level of equipment or specialized staff as a CPET, while still
offering valuable information. den Boer and colleagues prospectively studied the association of 6MWT distance in
49 children with DCM ≥6 years old and found that subjects with a 6MWT distance < 63% predicted had a 2-year
transplant-free survival of 73%, in contrast to 92% in patients with distance ≥63% predicted (p = 0.003).
42
RECOMMENDATION
1. CPET with measurement of peak VO
2
consumption is reasonable for assessment and prognosis of children
with HF (Class IIa, Level of Evidence B).
2. A 6MWT as part of the assessment and risk stratification of patients with HF may be considered if CPET is not
feasible (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
3.6. Cardiac Catheterization
Cardiac catheterization and the use of endomyocardial biopsy to make a diagnosis and guide management in
new onset HF should only be considered in a minority of children due to the risk of morbidity and mortality from the
procedure. Biopsies may be most useful and associated with the lowest risk of adverse events when performed in
children with suspected myocarditis, with a relatively low yield and higher risk of complications in children with
presumed cardiomyopathy.
36,43,44

3.7. Sleep Evaluation
Sleep disordered breathing (SDB), including central sleep apnea (CSA) and Cheyne-Stokes respiration,
is prevalent in adults with HF, and their occurrence is associated with HF severity and the risk of mortality, likely
due to neurohumoral and hemodynamic responses that are caustic to the failing heart. Furthermore, treatment of
these conditions with noninvasive ventilation is associated with increased LVEF and improved survival. Less is
known about the prevalence and consequence of CSA in pediatric HF. den Boer and colleagues completed a
prospective observational study of 37 children with DCM; CSA was found in 19% of subjects, and there was no
demonstrated association with severity of HF.
48
Similarly, Wheeler and colleagues
49
found no correlation between
LVEF and CSA in children. That said, the lack of associations may be secondary to sample size, or that children in
these cohorts had not experienced CSA for a duration long enough to have a phenotypic impact. Given the known
long-term impact of SDB on cardiovascular health, it is reasonable to evaluate SDB in children with HF and a
history consistent with sleep apnea.
RECOMMENDATION
Children with HF and a history consistent with sleep apnea should undergo evaluation for SDB (Class I, Level
of Evidence C).
3.8. Remote Monitoring and Wearable Devices
Pulmonary congestion secondary to elevated LV filling pressures is a frequent cause for HF-related symptoms and
hospital admissions. The COMPASS-HF
50
and CHAMPION studies showed that implantable hemodynamic monitors
could detect volume overload and guide pharmacologic therapy in adults with HF. Using an invasively implanted
pressure sensor in children comes with challenges, including smaller vessel size and a higher threshold for performing
invasive procedures. Small studies
51,52
have demonstrated the feasibility of using the CardioMEMS device in children,
and the Advanced Cardiac Therapies Improving Outcomes Network (ACTION) CardioMEMS database is developing a
cohort of pediatric patients with implantable pulmonary artery pressure monitors to determine safety and efficacy in
children. However, further evaluation is needed before any formal recommendation can be made.
Meta-analyses comparing home–based cardiac rehabilitation programs using wearable technology to in-hospital
rehabilitation showed similar effects in adults with HF.
53
Additionally, a randomized trial using a wearable sensor showed
that remote cardiac rehabilitation was associated with less sedentary time and was more cost-effective, suggesting
wearable devices may offer more affordable cardiac rehabilitation options than facility–based supervised programs.
54
Given the importance of social determinants of health in children with heart disease, measures that increase equitable
access to physical activity or cardiac rehabilitation programs for pediatric HF patients are critically important. While
wearable devices and remote monitoring are being rapidly incorporated into the care of adult HF, more evidence is
needed to determine the role of wearables, such as smart watches, rings, patches, etc., in assessing heart rate
variability, activity levels, sleep, and fluid status in children with cardiomyopathy and HF.
RECOMMENDATION
Including wearable technology in cardiac rehabilitation and physical activity programs may be considered to
increase access and effectiveness of these therapies in children with HF (Class IIb, Level of Evidence C).

4. PHARMACOLOGIC TREATMENT OF HEART FAILURE WITH REDUCED
EF WITH A BIVENTRICULAR CIRCULATION AND A SYSTEMIC LV
Treatment for the child with HFrEF is largely extrapolated from guideline–directed medical therapy (GDMT)
demonstrated to be efficacious in the adult population. There is limited evidence to support GDMT in children with
HF so as was the case in the 2014 guidelines, the majority of recommendations remain Level of Evidence C.
However, new medical therapies and studies have emerged and were used to inform these updated
recommendations.
As outlined in the adult HF guidelines, while there remains significant practice variation, it is reasonable to
develop a systematic approach regarding which medications to start first, the initial dose, a plan for uptitration, a
defined target dose, and a strategy for adding additional therapies.
9
The same GDMT premises recommended in
adults with HF could be extrapolated to children and include: (1) prioritize the GDMT believed to be the most
beneficial for the individual patient, while considering etiology of HF and social determinants of health that could
impact access, (2) uptitration should prioritize tolerance over achieving target dosing, (3) consider patient–related
outcome measures in the decision-making process, and (4) when possible engage multidisciplinary team–based
care. The pediatric HF learning network, ACTION, has developed provider education materials to work

4.1. Diuretics
Diuretics inhibit sodium reabsorption in the kidneys, thereby promoting urinary salt and free water excretion
57
and
are widely used to treat the symptoms of congestion in HF.
Adult data from the OPTIMIZE-HF registry showed that in elderly patients hospitalized for HF, those who were
discharged home on loop diuretic therapy had significantly lower 30 day mortality and lower risk of HF
readmission.
58
Diuretic therapy has class 1b recommendation for adults with fluid retention in American and
European professional consensus HF guidelines.
9,59
In children, diuretic therapy is used for symptomatic management of fluid retention despite lack of trials
showing survival advantage or reduced hospitalizations.
60,61
RECOMMENDATION
In children with fluid retention and ventricular dysfunction (HF stage C), diuretic therapy is recommended to
achieve a euvolemic state (Class I, Level of Evidence C).
4.2. Renin-Angiotensin Aldosterone System
Medications that inhibit the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system form part of the core therapies recommended
for all adult HF patients with HFrEF. These recommendations are based on multiple RCTs that have demonstrated
the use of these medications being associated with significantly improved morbidity and mortality.
9
While there
have been no RCTs of angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), or
aldosterone antagonists in pediatric HF patients, the recommendation for their use is based on extrapolation for
adult studies given the limited pediatric data available.
62-64
RECOMMENDATION
1. In children with left ventricular systolic dysfunction, ACE inhibitors should be routinely used unless there is a
specific contraindication (Class I, Level of Evidence B).
2. In children with systemic ventricular dysfunction, ARB are a reasonable alternative for those who are intolerant
of ACE inhibitors (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
3. In children with symptomatic systemic left ventricular dysfunction, treatment with aldosterone antagonists is
reasonable (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).

the degradation of natriuretic peptides and inhibiting it can lead to improved natriuresis, reduced sympathetic

4.5. Cardiac Glycosides
While digitalis has been used for hundreds of years to treat cardiac disorders, there is a paucity of pediatric data
to support its use in HFrEF. Among adult patients with symptomatic HFrEF, a large RCT found that digoxin use was
not associated with improved survival, but was associated with fewer hospitalizations. Importantly, more adverse
events were seen in patients with higher serum concentrations of digoxin.

Based on extrapolation from studies

In children with symptomatic HFrEF, digoxin (at lower serum digoxin levels, e.g., 0.5-0.9 ng/ml) may be
considered to relieve symptoms (Class IIb, Level of Evidence C).

4.7. Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter 2 Inhibitors
Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors work on the renal tubules and result in glucosuria and reduced
sodium absorption. This last mechanism has been suggested to be both cardio- and renoprotective, though the
precise mechanism of these medications in HF patients has yet to be elucidated. Nonetheless, multiple RCTs in
adult patients with HF have demonstrated a consistent improvement in mortality and HF in patients with and
without diabetes.
93,94
As a result, SGLT2 inhibitors are now recommended in adults with symptomatic HFrEF to
decrease hospitalizations and cardiovascular mortality.
9

95,96

kidney injury and 6 with a symptomatic urinary tract infection.
RECOMMENDATION
In children with symptomatic HFrEF, SGLT2 inhibitors may be considered, although their safety and
effectiveness in children have not been systematically studied (Class IIb, Level of Evidence C).
4.8. Soluble Guanylate Cyclase
The nitric oxide (NO)—soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC)—cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) pathway
plays an important role in regulating the cardiovascular system. Under normal physiological conditions, NO is
produced by endothelial cells, diffuses to neighboring cells, and binds to sGC. sGC, an intracellular protein,
cleaves guanosine triphosphate to cGMP. As a secondary messenger, cGMP can regulate vascular tone, cardiac
remodeling and myocardial contractility directly, or by acting on other downstream targets, such as protein kinase
G or phosphodiesterases.
sGC stimulators enhance endogenous NO binding to sGC and likely produce a more physiological effect on
blood pressure. Riociguat, the first oral sGC stimulator, has not progressed for HF therapy due to its short half-
life and fluctuating levels of drug and tissue cGMP.
98,99
Modifications to the riociguat molecule to increase half-life
and reduce clearance led to the discovery of vericiguat which has shown important benefits in adult systolic HF
studies.

4.9. Positive Inotropic Agents
Milrinone is commonly used for weeks to months as a bridge to transplant for children with HF. Improvements in
left ventricular systolic function, cardiac output, and mitigation of underperfusion of end-organs have been
demonstrated in small studies.
101,102
Additionally, milrinone is used for children with end-stage HF as a palliative
measure or in the setting of hospice care to improve symptoms.
103

discussion and recommendations specific to the use of inotropic agents in the management of acute HF.
RECOMMENDATION
1. Chronic inotropic therapy with milrinone can be used as a bridge to transplant (Class IIa, Level of
Evidence C).
2. Inotropic therapy may be considered for symptomatic relief in the palliative setting (Class IIa, Level of
Evidence C).
4.10. Anticoagulation
Studies support the use of anticoagulation for adult patients with HF who are in atrial fibrillation, but the efficacy for
adult patients with HF who are in sinus rhythm has never been proven.
104
While there is no data specific to the
pediatric population, there have been attempts to develop consensus approaches for indications for
anticoagulation for children with heart disease.
105,106
As newer agents emerge, treatment options have expanded
and a phase 2 open-label clinical study of apixaban demonstrated safety and efficacy in children with heart
disease, suggesting direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are a reasonable treatment option for children.
107
Despite the lack of data, prophylactic anticoagulation is sometimes used for children in HF with indwelling
catheters in the intensive care setting. Based on the absence of supportive data in adults, there is no proven utility
of anticoagulation in children with HF outside the intensive care unit who do not have a history of a prior thrombus
and are in sinus rhythm.
RECOMMENDATION
1. Patients with intracardiac thrombus should receive anticoagulation (Class I, Level of Evidence B).
2. In children with low EF and persistent or uncontrolled paroxysmal atrial fibrillation or flutter, systemic antic -

3. For patients with a history of thrombus or a thromboembolic event who have an EF < 25% (fractional short -

4. In children with a history of thrombus or thromboembolic event who have an EF < 25%, DOACs may be
considered (Class IIb, Level of Evidence C).
5. For patients with severe cardiac dysfunction in the intensive care setting or who have indwelling intravascular
catheters, prophylactic anticoagulation may be considered (Class IIb, Level of Evidence C).

5. PHARMACOLOGIC TREATMENT OF HEART FAILURE WITH PRESERVED
EF WITH BIVENTRICULAR CIRCULATION AND A SYSTEMIC LV
Similar to HFrEF, pharmacologic treatment of HFpEF in children is without a significant evidence basis and
recommendations are largely derived from expert consensus and extrapolation of findings from adult clinical
trials. Most RCTs in adults for the treatment of HFpEF have been neutral. As a result, renin-angiotensin
antagonists, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and ARNIs, phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors, calcium channel
blockers, digoxin, intravenous beta-agonists, prostaglandins, and endothelin receptor antagonists are not
recommended for the routine treatment of HFpEF.
108-111
5.1. Diuretics
Diuretics are a Class I recommendation for the treatment of congestive symptoms in adults with HFpEF and are
recommended to achieve euvolemia in children as well.
112
However, careful titration is recommended due to
preload dependence and risk of relative volume depletion which could result in decreased cardiac output.
RECOMMENDATION
In children with HFpEF, diuretics to establish a clinically euvolemic state are recommended, although
uptitration of doses should be done cautiously to avoid volume depletion (Class I, Level of Evidence C).
5.2. Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter 2 Inhibitors
The most recent addition to the adult HF guidelines for treatment of HFpEF is the Class IIa recommendation for the
use of SGLT2 inhibitors.
113
While this class of medications was initially developed for the treatment of diabetes, as
described in the previous section, they were found to be effective in improving outcomes of adults with HFrEF who
did not have diabetes.
93,114
The EMPEROR trial of empagliflozin in adults with HFpEF and an EF > 40%
demonstrated a reduced risk of cardiovascular death or HF hospitalizations, independent of an underlying
diagnosis of diabetes. There are a couple of small uncontrolled studies of the use of SGLT2 inhibitors in children
with HF, but no systematic study has been performed in the pediatric population.
95,96
Acknowledging the lack of
evidence and absence of pharmacokinetic data in children, given the high mortality of HFpEF in the pediatric
population, it is reasonable to consider use of SGLT2 inhibitors for children with symptomatic HFpEF. However, a
systematic study of this class of medications is needed in the pediatric and CHD HF populations to better inform
our approach and assess safety and adverse effects of these medications, including acute kidney injury and
genitourinary tract infections.
RECOMMENDATION
In children with symptomatic HFpEF, SGLT2 inhibitors may be considered (Class IIb, Level of Evidence C).
6. PHARMACOLOGIC TREATMENT OF SPECIAL POPULATIONS
In general, it is reasonable to apply the GDMT for HFrEF (Section 4) and HFpEF (Section 5) as described in this
document for children with a biventricular circulation and a systemic LV regardless of the underlying etiology of
their HF. In this section, we describe additional therapies that exist for some specific populations impacted by
cardiomyopathy and HF.
6.1. Congenital Heart Disease Associated Heart Failure
Patients with single ventricle physiology or biventricular physiology with a systemic RV may respond differently to
GDMT, suggesting caution in the extrapolation of adult clinical trial data to this patient population. For example,
the pediatric carvedilol trial demonstrated that the cohort of patients with single ventricle physiology or a systemic
RV tended to do worse with carvedilol compared to placebo.

Patients with genetic, metabolic, or mitochondrial disorders resulting in HFrEF or HFpEF should be treated based
on the guidelines previously described. However, there are some specific targeted therapies that can influence

hypertrophy and systolic function (global longitudinal strain, EF). Similarly, enzyme replacement therapy

and septal hypertrophy, although it does not alter valvar pathology. Based on the putative downstream effects of
the specific gene mutation in pediatric patients with RASopathy-associated HCM, MEK1 inhibitors (trametinib)

6.4. Dystrophinopathies
There is a wide range of neuromuscular disorders associated with cardiomyopathies and HF, the most prevalent

7. LIFESTYLE MODIFICATIONS
Behavior that promotes a healthy diet, exercise, and sleep in addition to management of comorbidities that can be a
consequence of obesity and a sedentary lifestyle, such as hypertension, is important in the comprehensive care of
children with HF. Kamsheh and colleagues performed a single–center retrospective cohort study and identified 34
children with systolic dysfunction attributed to systemic hypertension. Most patients (81%), including 92% of infants, had
resolution of their systolic dysfunction with treatment, demonstrating the importance of identification and treatment of
hypertension–associated pediatric HF.
125
Treatment of systemic hypertension should be in accordance with the
American Academy of Pediatrics Clinical Practice Guidelines.

monitoring of hemodynamics and renal function is indicated due to the enhanced risk of hypotension and renal toxicity.
7.1. Exercise Training and Activity
A hallmark of symptomatic HF is exercise intolerance, namely fatigue and dyspnea with exertion. To maximize functional
status in pediatric patients with HF, physician and parental physical activity restrictions should not be imposed unless
there is a substantiated concern for an adverse event or progression of disease with exercise. Exercise testing may be
helpful in identifying cardiac limitations to exercise in HF patients and an exercise training or cardiac rehabilitation
program may be beneficial. It is reasonable to include aerobic and resistance training in an individualized exercise
prescription, which can specify frequency, intensity, time, type, volume, and progression. Once an exercise regimen is
established, home exercise training programs with individualized goals and reinforcement may be beneficial in
improving functional capacity and quality of life.

2015 AHA/ACC Scientific Statement which addresses participation in competitive sports.

should be performed prior to initiating an exercise training program and to inform appropriate guidance
around exercise intensity and content (Class I, Level of Evidence C).

and appropriate recommendations for a defibrillator as primary or secondary intervention should be per -

3. Exercise training in a supervised setting should be prescribed, if deemed safe, as an adjunctive approach to

8. ELECTROPHYSIOLOGY ASSESSMENT AND INTERVENTIONAL
THERAPIES
Electrophysiological pathologies are both a complication and a potentially correctable cause of pediatric HF. Note
that the HF-specific recommendations for electrophysiological interventional therapies are intended to be
interpreted in addition to, rather than superseding, the 2 recent cardiac implantable electronic device guidelines.
These are the 2021 PACES expert consensus on pacemakers and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs)
and the 2023 HRS/APHRS/LAHRS Guideline on Cardiac Physiologic Pacing.
132,133
8.1. Ambulatory ECG Monitoring and Electrophysiology Testing and Treatment
Tachyarrhythmias can be a causative etiology of HF in children and electromechanical dyssynchrony contributes
to deterioration in cardiac function and may be secondary to iatrogenic or congenital factors. The clinician should
be cognizant of markers of electrical dyssynchrony (most obviously quantified on the surface ECG via the QRS
duration) and mechanical dyssynchrony (which is observed qualitatively on echocardiogram and other
multiphase imaging). Causes of electromechanical dyssynchrony include not only bundle branch block, but also
ventricular pacing, pre-excitation via an accessory pathway, and frequent beats of ventricular origin: all of these
may be targets for physiologic pacing or ablation.
134-136
RECOMMENDATION
1. In a pediatric patient with HF who presents with palpitations or suspected cardiac syncope, ambulatory
monitoring is recommended (Class I, Level of Evidence C).
2. Evaluation for tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy is recommended in any patient presenting with
DCM (Class I, Level of Evidence B).
3. In the pediatric patient with tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy, ablation therapy is recommended when
medical therapy fails or is associated with intolerable side effects (Class I, Level of Evidence B).
4. Ablation is recommended for frequent ventricular ectopy or tachycardia, predominantly from a single focus
thought to be causing ventricular dysfunction, when medical therapy is either not effective or is associated
with intolerable adverse effects (Class I, Level of Evidence B).
5. In pediatric patients with HF and a high risk of developing atrial or ventricular arrhythmias, or heart block,
regular ambulatory monitoring is reasonable (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
6. In pediatric patients with presyncope or syncope with at least moderately impaired LV function, electro -
physiology testing or long-term monitoring can be useful (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
7. Ablation therapy is reasonable as first-line therapy in larger pediatric patients (generally > 15-30 kg) with
tachycardia-induced myopathies or frequent ventricular ectopy or tachycardia, predominantly from a single
focus thought to be causing ventricular dysfunction (Class IIa, Level of Evidence B).
8. Ablation of the accessory pathway is reasonable in those with Woff-Parkinson-White pattern and ventricular
dysfunction presumed secondary to dyssynchrony(Class IIa, Level of Evidence B).

In addition, the pediatric HF population is at a poorly quantified risk of malignant arrhythmias, which may result in
sudden cardiac arrest (SCA). The role of an ICD for secondary prevention is well-established, but more detailed
guidelines for primary prevention ICD implantation in the pediatric population subgroups (including
arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, HCM, CHD, and DCM) have been published in the “2021 PACES expert

9.3. Airway and Parenchymal Respiratory Disorders
Respiratory distress and sleep disordered breathing (SDB) are common presenting HF symptoms among

9.5. Frailty
End-stage HF carries an increased risk of frailty and sarcopenia, impaired growth, reduced physical
performance, and altered metabolism can occur as a result of HF.
171
In the adult population, frailty has been

171

items defines prefrailty. Treatment of frailty includes nutritional support and physical rehabilitation.
171
The

172

9.7. Mental Health
Children and teens with HF are at risk for impaired mental health, which has the potential to impact HF
presentation and course, treatment adherence, candidacy for advanced cardiac therapies, and overall emotional
and physical wellbeing. While large-scale, multisite prospective and randomized mental health-focused studies
are lacking, expert consensus and literature to date underscore the significance of screening for and intervening
upon the mental health care needs of this patient population. Psychiatric diagnoses are observed in 30% of 11- to
17-year-olds and in 25% of 6- to 10-year-olds with HF.

10. MANAGEMENT OF ACUTE DECOMPENSATED HEART FAILURE
The initial presentation of HF in children is often acute and of sudden onset and prompt assessment and early
management can influence outcomes.
10.1. Diagnosis and Monitoring
The diagnosis of acute HF should be based on signs and symptoms combined with supportive evidence from
chest X-rays (e.g., cardiomegaly, pulmonary plethora, pleural effusions), ECGs (looking for the presence of sinus
rhythm or arrhythmias, abnormal Q waves, ST or T wave abnormalities suggesting a strain pattern or abnormal
repolarization and low voltages) with echocardiography often confirming the diagnosis. Laboratory investigations,
including natriuretic peptide levels, liver and renal function (to assess for end-organ dysfunction), blood gas
analysis, inflammatory markers and viral serology, complete blood count, and other tests looking for possible
underlying causes such as metabolic diseases and thyroid function, should be performed early. Symptoms of HF
in children and adolescents are often nonspecific and typical symptoms such as dyspnea and cough or
respiratory distress and exercise intolerance may develop late in the course of the disease which may already be
advanced at presentation. Respiratory symptoms are common in all age groups whereas adolescents more
commonly present with symptoms such as abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, anorexia, and weight loss.
190
Chest
pain, cardiac arrest, and cardiogenic shock are relatively rare presentations.
An assessment should be made of the severity of HF, the patient’s volume status as evidenced by the degree
of fluid retention/congestion (the “wet vs dry” patient) and the adequacy of perfusion (the “cold vs warm” patient)
as initial treatment may be slightly different depending on the hemodynamic presentation. These descriptions or
profiles also correlate with patient outcomes with “cold and wet” patients having the highest mortality in adult
studies.
191
Reversible causes need to be looked for so that appropriate treatment can be started early.
The initial evaluation should aim to identify patients at risk of rapid deterioration, those requiring enhanced
levels of monitoring, and those with evidence of poor perfusion or hemodynamic instability who should be
managed in an intensive care unit. Continuous ECG monitoring should be used routinely to assess for
arrhythmias, and early transfer to a center with pediatric HF specialists and the facilities for mechanical support or
transplant evaluation should be considered. Judicious use of central venous catheters to allow for measurement
of central venous pressure and mixed venous saturations and provide secure access for administration of
medications and fluids is reasonable. Placement of an arterial catheter for continuous blood pressure monitoring
is often employed in those requiring inotropic support. Pulmonary artery catheters (“Swan-Ganz catheters”) allow
for direct measurement of right atrial, right ventricular, pulmonary arterial, and pulmonary wedge pressures and
are used commonly in adult practice but are not recommended for routine use in pediatrics as there are no data
demonstrating improved outcomes and the risk of complications, including bleeding, thromboembolism, cardiac
perforation, and infection in children, is significant, particularly in small children or in those with an intracardiac
shunt.
192
Near-infrared tissue spectroscopy may be helpful in providing trends in cerebral and somatic oxygen
saturation and can be used as a proxy of oxygen extraction.
Cardiac catheterization is not routinely used for diagnosis of HF, but can be helpful to identify coronary
abnormalities when noninvasive imaging has not documented normal coronary flow, in patients with CHD to clarify
potential structural causes of deterioration, and to provide hemodynamic measurements to aid in management.
RECOMMENDATION
1. The diagnosis of acute HF should be based on signs and symptoms of HF combined with supportive evi -
dence from chest X-ray imaging, ECG, echocardiography, and laboratory evaluations, including natriuretic
peptide levels (Class I, Level of Evidence B).
2. The severity of HF should be assessed based on the degree of congestion and adequacy of perfusion (Class
I, Level of Evidence C).
3. In patients with severe acute HF, it is reasonable to initiate transfer to a center with pediatric HF specialists
who have the expertise and ability to optimize medical therapy, evaluate for heart transplantation, and provide
mechanical support (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
4. For patients with acute HF, serial testing to monitor for electrolyte abnormalities, hemoglobin levels, end-organ
perfusion, and response to therapy is indicated (Class I, Level of Evidence C).
5. In children hospitalized with acute HF, evaluation and monitoring for arrhythmias with continuous ECG
monitoring/telemetry is warranted (Class I, Level of Evidence C).
PEDIATRIC HF GUIDELINES
© The Journal of Heart and Lung Transplantation | 21 | 141 West Jackson Suite 1340, Chicago, IL 60604 USA | ishlt.org

6. In children with decompensated HF, central venous catheters are reasonable for administration of medica -
tions and to measure central venous pressure and mixed venous saturation (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
7. In children with acute decompensated HF, it is reasonable to place an arterial catheter for continuous blood
pressure measurement (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
8. In decompensated patients with acute HF, trends in cerebral or somatic regional oxygen saturation and
venous oxygen saturations levels may be helpful (Class IIb, Level of Evidence C).
9. In patients with palliated or repaired CHD who present with acute HF, cardiac catheterization is indicated if a
noninvasive evaluation fails to establish a definitive diagnosis or if invasive hemodynamic measurements are
needed for management decisions (Class I, Level of Evidence C).
10.2. Fluid Management and Diuretics
The mainstays of management in the initial stabilization of the child with acute HF are diuresis, inotropic agents,
vasodilators, and respiratory support with the initial treatment choice dependent on the clinical presentation and
evaluation of fluid status.
Patients who are well perfused but with signs of congestion/volume overload (e.g., tachypnea, hepatomegaly)
may benefit from diuresis. Loop diuretics are commonly used as first-line therapy either with intermittent dosing or
as a continuous infusion.
193
Edema of the gastroenterology tract can present as feeding intolerance in infants or
abdominal pain in older children, making consideration of intravenous diuretics necessary. Continuous infusions
of loop diuretics may be of increased benefit in neonates and children with HF who are critically ill by increasing
the likelihood of achieving a negative fluid balance and mitigating rapid fluid shifts.
194
While a low sodium diet and fluid restriction are often considered, especially in older children with HF, there
are no data (even in adults) to support this practice.
195-197
RECOMMENDATION
1. An initial and ongoing assessment of fluid status should be performed in all patients admitted to the hospital
with acute HF (Class I, Level of Evidence C).
2. Diuretics are the first-line therapy for patients with acute HF with evidence of fluid overload (Class I, Level of
Evidence C).
3. Careful monitoring for side effects of anticongestive therapies, including renal function, electrolytes, and
hypotension, should be performed in all patients with acute HF (Class I, Level of Evidence C).
10.3. Inotropic Agents
In patients with evidence of low cardiac output and compromised systemic and end-organ perfusion, such as
those with hypotension and cardiogenic shock, there is a Class I indication for the use of intravenous inotropic
support. This is generally considered a short-term strategy, as failure to improve with inotropic support should
result in consideration for mechanical support. The choice of inotropic agent will depend on the clinical
presentation with milrinone and/or dobutamine generally used as first-line agents in children. If there is
hypotension and ongoing poor end-organ perfusion, then epinephrine is often added.
Dobutamine is a catecholaminergic agent acting primarily on the myocardial beta-1 receptors which has a
quick onset of action, increases heart rate, stroke volume, and cardiac output, and also causes venous and
arterial vasodilatation resulting in reduced pulmonary capillary wedge pressure which is of benefit in patients with
pulmonary edema.
198
It does, however, cause an overall increase in myocardial oxygen consumption which limits
its use to short–term rescue therapy. Dobutamine should be used with caution in patients who are on beta-
blockers as an unbalanced alpha-1 effect in these patients may result in peripheral vasoconstriction.
Milrinone is a phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor which increases intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate
and has both inotropic and vasodilator properties resulting in a decrease in both systemic and pulmonary arterial
resistances. It delivers more effective afterload reduction and improved coronary perfusion pressure compared to
dobutamine with less effect on heart rate and myocardial oxygen demand
199
and plays a central role in acute HF
management in pediatrics. Milrinone can also be used for longer term management than dobutamine but it does
have a long half-life and due to its clearance by the kidneys dosing needs to be adjusted in patients with renal
impairment.

Epinephrine is a catecholamine that activates myocardial beta-1 adrenergic receptors causing inotropy and at lower
doses it activates beta-2 receptors resulting in reduced systemic vascular resistance whereas at higher doses it results in
increased systemic vascular resistance through its effect on the peripheral alpha-adrenergic receptors. It has rapid
effects in patients with hypotension and poor perfusion but can increase the risk of arrhythmias and causes increased
myocardial oxygen consumption and reduced distal perfusion with longer term use.
RECOMMENDATION
1. Intravenous inotropic support should be used in patients with acute HF presenting as cardiogenic shock or
low cardiac output with poor systemic and end-organ perfusion (Class I, Level of Evidence C).
2. Milrinone or dobutamine is reasonable as a first-line rescue therapy, with epinephrine playing a role in the face
of refractory hypotension and poor end-organ perfusion in children with acute HF (Class IIa, Level of
Evidence C).
10.4. Vasodilators
In patients with acute HF who do not have evidence of hypotension, especially in those with pulmonary edema or
significant volume load, such as valvar regurgitation, vasodilators such as nitroprusside, may be used, often in
combination with diuretics. Vasodilators may improve stroke volume and cardiac output and reduce pulmonary
capillary wedge pressure but may impair ventricular preload and therefore concurrent use of an inotropic agent is
usually needed.
200
Vasodilators should be used with caution in patients with HCM or left-sided obstructive lesions
as they can lead to reduced coronary perfusion and may exacerbate left ventricular outflow tract obstruction.
RECOMMENDATION
In pediatric patients with acute HF, vasodilators may be used in the absence of hypotension and in
combination with diuretics for symptomatic relief (Class IIb, Level of Evidence C).
10.5. Calcium Sensitizing Agent
Levosimendan is a calcium sensitizing agent which binds to cardiac troponin C causing a change in the configuration of
tropomyosin resulting in increased inotropy without increased myocardial oxygen consumption. Afterload reduction
occurs through peripheral vasodilatation. Although side effects in pediatric patients are few, the impact of levosimendan
on major clinical outcomes remains undetermined and the quality of evidence for its use is low.
201
Where available, it may
be considered in children with acute HF who are unresponsive to traditional inotropic therapy.
RECOMMENDATION
Levosimendan may be considered in children with acute HF unresponsive to traditional inotropic
therapy (Class IIb, Level of Evidence C).
10.6. Adrenal Insufficiency
Adrenal insufficiency may contribute to the hemodynamic instability seen in critically ill patients with a low cardiac
output and therefore treatment with stress dose steroid regimens could be considered if indicated.
RECOMMENDATION
In patients with hemodynamic compromise secondary to acute HF, evaluation and treatment of adrenal
insufficiency may be considered (Class IIb, Level of Evidence C).

Although prophylactic antiarrhythmic treatment is not recommended for children with acute HF, potential
precipitating factors for arrhythmias (e.g., electrolyte disturbance) should be evaluated and corrected as
appropriate.
RECOMMENDATION
In patients hospitalized with acute HF, potential precipitating factors for arrhythmias (e.g., electrolyte
abnormalities) should be corrected to prevent arrhythmias (Class I, Level of Evidence C).

10.8. Thyroid Disorders
Thyroid disease, both hypo- and hyperthyroidism, can contribute to or be the precipitating cause of HF.
202
Screening of thyroid function is recommended in all children with HF. Treatment of thyroid disorders should be
based on general endocrine guidelines.
RECOMMENDATION.

10.9. Respiratory Support
Children with acute HF often require respiratory support to improve oxygenation and reduce respiratory distress.
Non–invasive positive pressure ventilation, such as continuous positive airway pressure, can have positive
benefits of improved oxygenation and reduced work of breathing by increasing the alveolar distending pressure
thereby redistributing pulmonary edema and reducing lung and airway resistance. Noninvasive ventilation may be
considered as an adjunct to other medical therapies in patients with increased respiratory effort and acute HF.
The subsequent reduced work of breathing then has favorable impacts on the balance of oxygen demand and
delivery.
203
Positive pressure ventilation also reduces left ventricular afterload and improves cardiac output in
patients but if respiratory failure progresses in the setting of low cardiac output or pulmonary edema, intubation
and mechanical ventilation may be necessary.
RECOMMENDATION
1. Invasive ventilation is recommended for children with acute HF and respiratory failure (Class I, Level of
Evidence C).
2. Noninvasive ventilation is reasonable for the treatment of children with acute HF, pulmonary edema, and
significantly increased work of breathing (in the presence or absence of pulmonary edema) as an adjunct to
other medical therapies (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
10.10. Acute Right Heart Failure
Acute right HF can present most commonly following surgical repair of CHD but can also be seen in the setting of
sepsis, acute myocarditis, and in pulmonary hypertension. It is also rarely seen following blunt chest trauma. Low
cardiac output in these patients is associated with high right-sided filling pressures, subsequent reduced left
ventricular filling, and venous and lymphatic congestion. It is reasonable to use milrinone as an inotrope and
pulmonary vasodilator in this setting and dobutamine, dopamine, or low dose epinephrine may be required. Loop
diuretics are also often used in this context.
RECOMMENDATION
1. Low dose epinephrine, dobutamine, or dopamine is reasonable in the setting of acute right HF (Class IIa,
Level of Evidence C).
2. Use of milrinone as an inotrope and pulmonary vasodilator is reasonable in the setting of acute right HF (Class
IIa, Level of Evidence C).
10.11. Perioperative Acute Heart Failure
In the postoperative CHD patient with a low cardiac output state, early evaluation for hemodynamically significant
residual lesions should be performed. Milrinone reduces the risk of a low cardiac output state following congenital
cardiac surgery

and other inotropic agents may be needed. In children unable to be weaned from
cardiopulmonary bypass, ECMO support is often instituted early with transition to more durable forms of
mechanical support required in patients who do not recover quickly.
RECOMMENDATION
1. Prompt evaluation and treatment of residual hemodynamically significant lesions should be performed for
those with early postoperative HF (Class I, Level of Evidence C).
2. Milrinone therapy after cardiac surgery in infants and children to reduce the risk of low cardiac output syn -
drome is reasonable (Class IIa, Level of Evidence B).

11. ANESTHETIC CONSIDERATIONS
Children with HF frequently present for diagnostic, surgical, or catheter-based interventions requiring anesthesia
care. Although anesthesia for children is safe, it is well documented and accepted that children with heart disease
undergoing noncardiac procedures have an increased risk of cardiac arrest, significant complications, and
mortality.
205-207
Among children with heart disease, patients with ventricular dysfunction requiring medication are
classified as having severe heart disease as defined by the American College of Surgeons National Surgical
Quality Improvement Program based on residual lesion burden and functional status, and incur the highest
mortality (operating room 7.32).
207
Hypotension requiring inotropic support under anesthesia is a common
complication.
208
Consequently, in children with impaired heart function, the need for general anesthesia, even for
short, minimally invasive procedures, remains a considerable risk.
Comprehensive perioperative planning with multidisciplinary input is essential in managing patients with HF
and preventing significant morbidity and mortality. Preoperatively, this should include risk assessment, evaluation
of medical and surgical history, optimization of cardiac functional status, medication, and fasting management,
review of pertinent cardiac imaging studies and laboratory results, identification of specific surgical/procedural
concerns, location and timing of the procedure, and team composition.
209,210
Intraoperative anesthetic
management should be individualized based on the preoperative assessment to maintain hemodynamic stability,
end-organ perfusion, and prevent cardiovascular collapse. Before proceeding, a clear understanding of the likely
impact of the anesthetic agents, ventilation strategies, and operative technique on the failing cardiovascular
system is essential and should be communicated to the operative team. Intraoperative considerations include
appropriate monitoring with potential for invasive lines; adequate venous access; use of induction and
maintenance agents that maintain hemodynamic stability with minimal effect on myocardial contractility, rate, and
rhythm, and support the balance between systemic and pulmonary resistances; anticipate the effects of positive
pressure ventilation; fluid management, including the possibility of blood transfusion; need for vasoactive agents;
arrhythmia management; and rescue strategies in the event of cardiovascular collapse or cardiac arrest.
211-216
Given the risk of cardiac arrest, thorough perioperative discussion must include appropriateness and availability
for MCS. This discussion should include the timing of MCS (backup vs elective vs emergent), cannulation and
device strategies, location logistics, and procedure being performed.
217
Postoperative care is an extension of the
above considerations, including the location and availability of expertise to manage these patients successfully.
RECOMMENDATION
1. In children with HF requiring anesthesia, a multidisciplinary team approach with meticulous perioperative
planning is recommended to minimize the morbidity and mortality (Class I, Level of Evidence C).
2. Perioperative care of children with HF should be provided by, or in consultation with, an anesthesiologist with
expertise in caring for children with heart disease (Class I, Level of Evidence C).
3. In the anesthetic management of children with HF, careful consideration should be given to location, timing of
procedure, team composition, and availability of MCS (Class I, Level of Evidence C).
12. HEALTH CARE DELIVERY
For patients who are able to achieve medical stability, discharge planning is essential to prepare families for
management at home and educate them regarding recognition of progression of HF. This effort aims to reduce
hospitalizations or the severity of illness when patients return for care. Discharge criteria, such as outlined in

Ongoing follow-up is crucial for recognizing early signs of congestion and decompensation, monitoring end-
organ function, and continually optimizing medical management. While traditionally, most monitoring in pediatrics
has occurred in person, the COVID-19 pandemic allowed for the establishment of virtual options for monitoring
and treatment. Virtual care has shown potential in pediatric HF to be a promising option for ongoing follow-up,

218
Equity aims to ensure that all patients, regardless of sex, race, or socio-economic or demographic

14. GOALS OF CARE
Despite notable advancements in medical management and advanced cardiac therapies, morbidity and mortality
remain high in pediatric HF. Advanced heart disease has been defined as complex or severe CHD or acquired
heart disease with a high possibility of serious adverse events or mortality in the next 2 years.
228,229
14.1. Palliative Care
Palliative care, provided by both primary cardiology teams as well as subspecialty palliative care teams, focuses
on reducing distress and improving quality of life in patients with serious illnesses through symptom management
interventions, goals of care and decision-making support, and psychosocial interventions for patients and
families.
230
There is growing literature in adult HF for the utility of early palliative care interventions in improving outcomes
in patients with advanced heart disease.
231
Several guideline documents are helpful
9,232,233
in summarizing the
current support. These guidelines agree on the importance of early integration of palliative care across all stages
of HF. These guidelines outline the details of symptom management, decision-making, and communication of
prognosis and life-sustaining therapies, as well as advance care planning.
PEDIATRIC HF GUIDELINES
© The Journal of Heart and Lung Transplantation | 27 | 141 West Jackson Suite 1340, Chicago, IL 60604 USA | ishlt.org

The recent AHA Statement on palliative care in pediatric heart disease
232
provides a more detailed approach
to this topic. Parents of children who die of advanced heart disease, including HF, report high symptom burden at
their child’s end of life. Breathing difficulties, feeding intolerance, poor sleep, and fatigue were some of the most
distressing symptoms as perceived by parents. Two-thirds describe their child’s quality of life in the month before
death to be fair to poor.
234
Among children who die of advanced heart disease, most deaths occur in intensive
care settings with invasive interventions common at end of life.
235,236
Multicenter research specific to pediatric
VAD has also shown high symptom burden, specifically in the domains of procedural distress, limitations in
activity, postoperative pain, sadness, and fatigue.
179
Similar to the more heterogeneous advanced heart disease
cohort, the majority of pediatric patients on VAD die in the hospital setting with interventions, such as mechanical
intervention common at end of life.
237
Ethical frameworks
238
and standardized pediatric VAD deactivation
checklists have been developed to facilitate compassionate care at end of life.
239,240
Taken together, these single-
center and registry-based studies highlight the important need for multidisciplinary palliative care for pediatric
patients with end-stage HF and those receiving ECMO, VAD support, or listed for transplantation.
Beyond symptom management and goals of care discussions, pediatric HF research has also underscored
the need for assessing communication preferences specific to prognosis, outcome, clinical trajectory, and goals
of care at least yearly. Research has demonstrated discrepancies in parent/physician agreement of child
functional class, symptom burden, life expectancy, and impact on activities of daily living in pediatric advanced
heart disease.
241,242
Moreover, research has demonstrated that the majority of adolescents and young adults
living with HF desire communication and decision-making involvement regarding their prognosis, treatment
options, and advance care planning.
243,244
Lastly, efforts to enhance palliative care training for trainees and HF
cardiologists and care teams are critical.
RECOMMENDATION
1. Pediatric palliative care should be incorporated into the care of all patients with advanced heart disease,
including patients with end-stage HF and those receiving ECMO, VAD support, and patients listed for
transplantation (Class I, Level of Evidence B).
2. Standard communication by pediatric cardiology or palliative care teams with parents or guardians, ado -
lescents, and young adults with HF about prognosis, outcome, clinical trajectory, and goals of care is rea -
sonable at least yearly, and with each hospitalization or procedure (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
14.2. Hospice Care
Hospice care emphasizes comfort-based, quality of life focused care in the final months of life. In the United
States, children do not need to stop all life-sustaining treatment to be eligible for hospice care. Pediatric patients
with HF can receive concurrent care, which can include home-based inotropes or VAD support, in addition to
hospice care. It is important to understand patient and family goals of care so that timely and appropriate referrals
to hospice can be made when indicated.
RECOMMENDATION
Development of standardized and ethical approaches to withdrawal of life-sustaining therapies, including
ventilators, ICDs, and MCS, is reasonable to guide care at end of life in pediatric patients with HF (Class IIa, Level
of Evidence C).
15. QUALITY METRICS AND REPORTING
HF in children is classified as a rare disease, as per the definition suggested by the Genetic and Rare Diseases
Information Center in the NIH of a prevalence of less than 60 cases/100,000 population, equivalent to 200,000
cases in the United States (https://rarediseases.info.nih.gov/about). Rare diseases pose unique challenges for
patients and clinicians alike, some of which can be mitigated by implementing specific features of care.
According to the National Academy of Medicine, use of routinely collected data for continuous learning in a
learning health system should be considered as a means to improve outcomes in all diseases.
245
This is even
more pressing for rare diseases, in which other forms of medical information such as clinical trials data are more
likely to be deficient. While a true learning health system extends far beyond a clinical registry, there is little doubt
that systematic aggregation of multicenter data is a key driver of the effectiveness of learning health systems. A
PEDIATRIC HF GUIDELINES
© The Journal of Heart and Lung Transplantation | 28 | 141 West Jackson Suite 1340, Chicago, IL 60604 USA | ishlt.org

critical aspect of learning health systems in driving improved outcomes is that data must not only be collected but
also shared.
246
Within pediatric HF, the ACTION has promoted practices of both multi–center data collection and
data sharing in support of generating real-world evidence. Other networks in pediatric cardiology such as the
Pediatric Cardiac Critical Care Consortium and the Pediatric Acute Care Cardiology Collaborative have also
promoted similar goals, with a focus more on congenital heart surgery and structural heart disease rather than on
HF.
247-249
Linkage of these data sources, also in accordance with recommendations from the National Academy
of Medicine, offers further potential to improve outcomes for children with HF, some of whom also have structural
heart disease.
In 2001, the Institute of Medicine issued a seminal report on the US health system, evaluating opportunities to
improve quality across the system.
250
Among the key recommendations in this report was the need for patient-
centered care, which is characterized as “care that is respectful of and responsive to individual patient
preferences, needs, and values.” To achieve this goal, one important step is the collection of patient-reported
outcomes to inform clinicians of key preferences. Although this is not yet a routine component of clinical care
delivery, it is gaining wider acceptance, and was the topic of detailed FDA guidance in 2009 on integration of
patient-reported outcomes data into clinical trial end-points (https://www.fda.gov/media/77832/download). While
there is as yet, no clearly preferred system of patient-reported outcomes for pediatric HF, there are circumstances
in which these data have been systematically collected and it is timely to acknowledge the value of such data in
helping to promote high–quality clinical care in pediatric HF, to match the progress that has been made in adult
HF care through systems, such as Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire and Minnesota Living with Heart
Failure.
179,251,252
RECOMMENDATION
1. Participation in multi–center data collection to facilitate clinical care improvement is reasonable for centers
that treat children with HF (Class IIa, Level of Evidence C).
2. Incorporating one or more patient–entered data elements/outcomes (e.g., assessments of quality of life,
anxiety, or depression) into the comprehensive care of children with HF may be considered (Class IIb, Level
of Evidence C).

SECTION 10: MANAGEMENT OF ACUTE DECOMPENSATED HEART FAILURE
10.1 Diagnosis and Monitoring
Class I The diagnosis of acute HF should be based on signs and symptoms of HF combined with supportive evidence from chest X-ray

Level of Evidence B
The severity of HF should be assessed based on the degree of congestion and adequacy of perfusion.
Level of Evidence C
In patients with palliated or repaired CHD who present with acute HF, cardiac catheterization is indicated if a noninvasive

decisions.
Level of Evidence C
For patients with acute HF, serial testing to monitor for electrolyte abnormalities, hemoglobin levels, end-organ perfusion, and
response to therapy is indicated.
Level of Evidence C
In children hospitalized with acute HF, evaluation and monitoring for arrhythmias with continuous ECG monitoring/telemetry is
warranted.
