# Efficacy of Aspirin Therapy After Desensitization on Chronic Rhinosinusitis With Nasal Polyps Patients With Asthma and Non-steroidal Anti-inflammatory Drug Exacerbated Respiratory Disease -A Randomized Clinical Trial.
**Authors:** Helevä A, Lyly A, Salmi V, Mäkelä M, Kauppi P, Laulajainen-Hongisto A, Hafrén L, Virkkula P, Nuutinen M, Toppila-Salmi S
**Journal:** Allergy (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1111/all.16679](https://doi.org/10.1111/all.16679)

## Content

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) exacerbated respiratory disease (N-ERD) is a chronic inflammatory condition typically involving hypersensitivity to NSAIDs, asthma, and/or chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) with nasal polyps (CRSwNP). N-ERD increases the risk for severe hypersensitivity reactions, recurring sinonasal surgeries, or the need for systemic corticosteroid treatment. Aspirin (ASA) treatment after desensitization (ATAD) is a treatment option for N-ERD. Desensitization to ASA is reached with increasing ASA doses over a few days, followed by a long-term treatment. This study evaluated the efficacy of ATAD with an ASA dose of 250 mg in adult CRSwNP patients with asthma and N-ERD. Forty-one CRSwNP patients with comorbid asthma and N-ERD were recruited for this randomized double-blind clinical trial. Twenty-six patients had successful ASA desensitization, were randomized into placebo and ASA arms, and continued to 11 months of daily ASA, with a target dose of 250 mg. The outcome measures, including Sino-Nasal Outcome Test 22 (SNOT-22), nasal polyp score (NPS), Asthma Control Test (ACT), and EPOS 2012 CRS clinical control assessment, were evaluated at control visits at 1, 5, 11, and 12 months after the start of the treatment. In the linear mixed effects model, p-values for the combined effect of group and time showed no difference between placebo and ATAD for SNOT-22, ACT, and NPS, with p-values 0.17, 0.45, and 0.18, respectively. Neither group showed significant improvement in the EPOS 2012 CRS control assessment. In conclusion, no significant clinical difference was observed between ATAD and placebo in the treatment of CRSwNP patients with asthma and N-ERD.

Non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drug (NSAID) exacerbated respiratory disease (N‐ERD) is an inflammatory condition of the respiratory system, typically presented with NSAID intolerance, asthma, and/or chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) [1].

N‐ERD represents a severe, difficult‐to‐treat phenotype in both asthma and CRSwNP [1, 2]. Most N‐ERD patients have moderate to severe asthma, and compared to non‐N‐ERD asthma patients, they have an increased risk for a severe asthma disease (RR 1.58), uncontrolled asthma (RR 1.96), as well as asthma exacerbations leading to emergency room visits (RR 1.79) or hospitalizations (RR 1.37) [1, 3]. Compared to CRSwNP patients without N‐ERD, patients suffering from CRSwNP with N‐ERD are more prone to have partial or total loss of smell, worse upper airway symptoms, and recurrent nasal polyps [1, 2]. N‐ERD patients may need frequent endoscopic sinus surgeries to control their polyposis, burdening both the patients and the healthcare system.

NSAIDs trigger a reaction in N‐ERD patients within 30–180 min of ingestion, comprising many upper and/or lower symptoms, such as nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, laryngospasm, wheezing, coughing, and shortness of breath [1, 2]. In addition to the respiratory symptoms, gastrointestinal (GI) and cutaneous symptoms may be involved in the reaction [1, 2]. The disease is chronic by nature, and sole avoidance of NSAIDs does not fully prevent the symptoms of CRSwNP, asthma, and recurrent nasal polyposis [1].

The prevalence of N‐ERD among adult asthma patients varies between 5.5% and 12.4% depending on diagnostic methods, and it is higher in patients with severe asthma (14.9%) [4]. The prevalence of N‐ERD is 9.7% in patients with nasal polyps and 8.7% in patients with chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) [4]. In Finland specifically, the prevalence of N‐ERD in the general adult population is approximately 1.4% [5], and 17% among the adult CRSwNP patients [6].

Aspirin (ASA) treatment after desensitization (ATAD) is recommended mainly for patients with severe N‐ERD lacking response to standard treatments of asthma and/or CRSwNP, and for patients with the need of ASA for cardiovascular health or chronic anti‐inflammatory treatment [1]. In ATAD, the patient is first desensitized with increasing doses of ASA, during which the respiratory reactions should gradually alleviate and disappear, and thereafter the patient continues a daily dose of ASA to maintain the desensitization [2].

Previous randomized double‐blind clinical trials (RDBCTs) have evaluated the efficacy of ATAD [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Their results showed that ATAD might improve health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) and airway symptoms. The problems of this treatment are the side effects of ASA, especially the GI problems such as gastritis or gastrointestinal bleeding, and the daily intake of the medication, as the desensitization effect subsides quickly if ASA is not taken every day [11].

This randomized double‐blind clinical trial of ATAD was initiated because of the small number of RDBCTs investigating the clinical impact of the treatment. Biologic treatments have emerged and seem safe and effective [1, 14], yet they are the distinctly more expensive treatment alternative in severe cases of N‐ERD. Therefore, it is important to investigate the possible benefits and risks of ATAD, to help healthcare systems make, first and foremost, safe but also cost‐effective treatment decisions. The objective of this study was to evaluate the benefits of ATAD regarding HRQoL, asthma, and CRS control, and the occurrence of adverse effects. We hypothesized that ATAD improves HRQoL, sense of smell, and Nasal Polyp Score (NPS) more than placebo.

This superiority RDBCT study was conducted at the Skin and Allergy Hospital, the Department of Allergology, Inflammation Center, Helsinki University Hospital. The study was conducted in accordance with Finnish legislation and the Declaration of Helsinki. The ethical committee for the Hospital Districts of Helsinki and Uusimaa (nro. 1801‐2017) approved the study. Written informed consent was obtained from the study subjects. The study has been registered in Clinical.Trials.gov [15] (ID: NCT03825757). The trial monitoring was conducted by the monitoring unit of the Hospital District of Helsinki and Uusimaa, and an independent monitoring committee from the Clinical Research Unit at the Academic Medical Center Helsinki [16]. The study protocol by Toppila‐Salmi et al. [16] and Appendix S1 describe the study design in greater detail.

A total of 41 adult N‐ERD patients with uncontrolled CRSwNP were recruited in Helsinki and the Uusimaa Hospital district (Figure 1). Even though asthma was not in the inclusion criteria, all the recruited patients had appropriately diagnosed comorbid asthma. All the participants were diagnosed according to the N‐ERD and CRSwNP criteria and had undergone prior partial or total ethmoidal surgery without gaining disease control [16]. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are specified in the Appendix S1.

Four patients (three from the ASA arm and one from the placebo arm) underwent polypectomy about 1 month before the start of the trial, as initially designed. However, the polypectomies could not be continued because of a lack of resources and/or prohibited elective surgeries at the hospital as part of COVID‐19 precautions. The four patients who had polypectomy after recruitment continued the trial protocol as usual, and their data are included in the analysis unless told otherwise.

About 2.5 months after recruitment, 31 of the 41 recruited participants underwent a 4‐day ASA challenge and desensitization in a hospital setting, aiming to confirm N‐ERD diagnosis by a respiratory reaction to ASA, and desensitize the patients to ASA [16]. The ASA (Primaspan tablet, Orion Pharma, Espoo, Finland) doses were administered in the morning, 2 h apart, according to the following scheme: Day 1: 25 mg and 25 mg; Day 2: 50 mg and 25 mg; Day 3: 75 mg and 25 mg; Day 4: 100 mg and 25 mg [16].

Randomization of participants to the ASA and placebo arms (in a 2:1 ratio, respectively) was done by Galena Pharma (Kuopio, Finland), which provided the IMP. All researchers were blinded during the trial. The IMP was either ASA (Primaspan 250 mg tablet, Orion Pharma, Espoo, Finland) or a validated similar placebo in identical packages.

After AD, the participants continued directly to IMP treatment at home, with a starting dose of 125 mg ASA. During the trial, there were four control visits with a physician and a nurse, at 1, 5, 11, and 12 months after the start of the medication (Figure 1). The visits included clinical status and interview of potential adverse events/exacerbations, answering a selection of questionnaires, and taking clinical measurements. On the first visit (1 month), the dose of IMP was doubled from ½ 250 mg tablet of IMP to one full 250 mg tablet. On the third visit (11 months), the IMP was discontinued. The final visit (12 months) took place 1 month after medication cessation.

The treatment was discontinued if severe adverse effects occurred or if the patients forgot to take IMP for over 24 h, as this could risk losing the desensitization effect. The participants were able to withdraw from the study at any time of their own wish and were encouraged to contact the investigators in case of any adverse effects or other issues. All adverse events were appropriately recorded, and severe adverse events were reported to the Finnish Medicines Agency (Fimea).

The primary outcome was the change in the Sino‐Nasal Outcome Test 22 (SNOT‐22) score [16]. The secondary outcomes were Nasal Polyp Score (NPS), CRS clinical control assessment according to the European Position Paper on Rhinosinusitis and Nasal Polyps 2012 [17] (further shortened as EPOS 2012 CRS control) (Table S4), Asthma Control Test (ACT), olfaction performance in Sniffin' Sticks identification test, observed forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) of the individual predicted FEV1 [18] (FEV1%), and peak expiratory flow (PEF), as % of individual reference value (to obtain the PEF, we calculated the mean of the three best PEF values from 1 week PEF measurement series, and divided it by the individual reference PEF value [19, 20], see Appendix S1) [16].

The randomization key was opened only after officially closing the trial, and the analyses were unblinded. The data were studied 2 months after the trial had ended for all patients. All statistical analyses and modeling were performed using the R software [21] (version 4.3.2) with the stats [21] (base R), lme4 [22] (version 1.1.36), exact2x2 [23] (version 1.6.9), survival [24] (version 3.5.7), and simputation [25] (version 0.2.9) packages. The statistical methods are detailed in Appendix S1.

Baseline was defined as the recruitment visit. The IMP treatment was discontinued at 11 months after the start of medication. The last measurements were done at 1 month without treatment (at 12 months) (Figure 1).

Mann–Whitney‐U‐test (MWU), Fisher's exact test, Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, McNemar's exact test [23], linear mixed effects models [22] (LMM), and log‐rank test [24] were used in the analyses. A significance level of p < 0.05 was applied to all analyses.

For selected variables (see Appendix S1), missing data were imputed with linear regression imputation [25]. Selected missing recruitment visit values were supplemented from other time points before IMP start.

To the knowledge of the authors, there are no official criteria for response to ATAD. However, there are criteria for response to biologic treatments [26]. We modified these criteria to suit the ATAD treatment (See Appendix S1), and to be classified as a responder, a subject had to complete the trial and fulfill three out of the four following criteria after the cessation of IMP: NPS reduction of ≥ 1 point; SNOT‐22 reduction of ≥ 12 points or total SNOT‐22 points < 40; Sniffin' sticks points improvement of ≥ 3 points; less OCS courses during IMP than in the year before. With these criteria, four patients in the ASA arm were regarded as responders (excluding drop‐outs and patients who had a preceding polypectomy from the analysis). Changing the SNOT‐22 reduction criteria from ≥ 12 points (minimal clinically important difference [MCID] for medical management [27]) to ≥ 9 points (MCID for surgical interventions [28]) did not alter the patient selection in the responder group.

We studied the differences between responders and nonresponders. In the analyses, drop‐outs and patients who had polypectomy before AD were excluded. There were no statistically significant differences at baseline, but a nonsignificant trend showed that the responders had lower ACT scores at baseline (responders: median 18 [IQR 16–21]; nonresponders: median 23 [IQR 22–24], p = 0.086) (Table S1).

When we performed an additional baseline responder analysis where we included the drop‐outs and classified them as nonresponders (Table S2), the difference in ACT scores was significant (p = 0.043), and nonsignificant differences were seen in the distributions in ACT classification (p = 0.077) and number of OCS courses in the year before recruitment (0.069). The nonresponder group had more patients with well‐controlled asthma (89% vs. 50%) and more patients with no OCS courses in the year before AD (56% vs. 0%).

When comparing the responders to the nonresponders in the LMM analysis (Figure S1) in NPS, SNOT‐22, and ACT, no statistically significant group differences were seen over the course of the trial. However, within‐group changes from before to after treatment (Figure S2) visually, only the responder group (N = 4) seemed to improve in NPS and ACT, but this was not statistically significant. Nevertheless, this is expected as the response classification is based on the change in these scores.

There was no significant difference in the rate of OCS courses needed or experienced adverse events between the responder and nonresponder groups (Figure S3, Table S5).

26 participants started the trial and 21 (80.8%) finished it. Four (15.4%) participants, three from the ASA arm and one from the placebo arm, dropped out of the study because of adverse events during IMP. Additionally, there was one dropout not related to adverse events: the patient had an upcoming otolaryngological surgery and a poor response from IMP, which was placebo. The total adverse‐event related dropout rate in the ASA arm was 18.8%, and in the placebo arm, it was 10.0%. The dropout rates are in line with the estimated dropout rate of 15% [16].

We compared the ASA arm patients finishing the trial (N = 13) with the ones who dropped out (N = 3) at baseline. The groups significantly differed in ACT total points (finishers: median 22 [IQR 21–23]; drop‐outs: median 25 [IQR 24–25]; p = 0.048), the drop‐out group having higher ACT total points reflecting better asthma control at baseline. There were nonsignificant trends indicating a possible difference in nighttime awakenings (finishers: median 1 [IQR 1–1]; drop‐outs: median 4 [IQR 3–4]; p = 0.061) and sex distribution (finishers: 7.7% males; drop‐outs: 67% males; p = 0.071).

Additionally, we performed an analysis comparing the ASA and placebo groups at baseline, excluding all patients who dropped out during the trial (Table S3). In this analysis, there were statistically significant differences between the groups in sex distribution, BMI, and number of OCS courses during 2 years before the trial; the placebo group having more males (75% vs. 7.7%), higher median BMI (29 kg/m2 vs. 24 kg/m2) and more patients with 4–5 OCS courses 2 years prior to the trial (38% vs. 0%).

We performed three post hoc sensitivity analyses: one excluding imputed values, one excluding the 4 patients that underwent polypectomy before ASA challenges, and one excluding drop‐outs. Overall, the results remained similar to the main analysis and are described in more detail in the Appendix S1.

In this randomized double‐blinded clinical trial, we studied whether ATAD at an ASA dose of 250 mg was better than placebo at improving symptoms, disease control, and quality of life in adult CRSwNP patients with asthma and N‐ERD. This trial was initiated because of the lack of RDBCT studies investigating the clinical impact of ATAD. Evidence‐based information on ATAD helps healthcare systems make safe but also cost‐effective treatment decisions, as new promising [14] but expensive biological treatments emerge.

In our study, we did not find ATAD significantly better than placebo at improving HRQoL or lung function, reducing nasal polyps, or improving the clinical control of CRS or asthma in adult CRSwNP patients with asthma and N‐ERD. However, statistical trends suggested a minor improvement effect of ATAD on NPS and HRQoL. Yet, side effects were common and often led to treatment cessation; thus, the overall benefit of ATAD is arguable.

The results of this study are not in contradiction with the results of previous RDBCT studies of ATAD, but the benefits of ATAD are minor in comparison to the other studies. This could in part be due to the smaller ASA dose used in this study compared to most of the other RDBCTs, which may limit the interpretation of the results. The guidelines generally recommend dosing between 300 mg to 1300 mg [1]. According to our retrospective cohort data, N‐ERD patients tolerate relatively small ASA doses in our population [29], and to ensure a safely performed trial, we decided knowingly to use a dosage smaller than in the guidelines. Moreover, a RDBCT study by Fruth et al. observed beneficial effects on nasal polyposis and HRQoL with a maintenance dose of ASA as low as 100 mg [9]. In addition, a 12‐year follow‐up study studied a long‐term ASA dose of 300 mg after endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS) and AD. They showed that ATAD could be beneficial in decreasing the need for surgery and improving symptom scores [30]. Another 3‐year follow‐up study with 300 mg ASA with preceding ESS showed that ATAD could decrease the need for surgery, OCS courses, and sinusitis episodes, as well as alleviate upper airway symptoms [31]. These studies show that low doses of ATAD may result in beneficial effects.

We started with 125 mg of ASA for the first month and proceeded to 250 mg of ASA for the remaining 10 months. In previous literature, a different approach has been used as well: to start with large doses and decrease the dose in follow‐up [32]. Three of the previous RDBCTs [8, 9, 10] have used a stable dose of ASA for the whole treatment period, and two have applied a double dose for the first month, after which the dose was decreased to the maintenance dose [12, 13].

The small number of participants reduces the statistical power and limits the interpretation of the results. The trial was performed through the COVID‐19 pandemic, with simultaneous generalization of the use of biologics for N‐ERD, posing additional challenges in recruiting patients. Small sample size has been a problem in other RDBCTs as well [7].

This study did not assess individual tolerance to ASA or other NSAIDs. This would have required a patient‐specific dose‐finding approach, which was not feasible within the constraints of the RDBCT design. Evaluating individual tolerance to ASA and other NSAIDs remains an important area for future research.

The existing RDBCT studies show that ATAD may improve HRQoL and alleviate nasal polyposis and upper and lower airway symptoms, but simultaneously, it may increase adverse event occurrence [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. A meta‐analysis by Chu et al. (2019) acknowledges that although asthma desensitization has benefits on HRQoL and airway symptoms, it may increase adverse events [11]. With low certainty, they found that asthma exacerbations and gastritis occurrence may increase. A Cochrane review by Lourijsen et al. (2025) found that ATAD may improve HRQoL; however, there is uncertainty on the observed improvements in asthma control and airway symptoms [7]. They observed with low certainty that ATAD may not significantly increase GI adverse effects, and it may not affect nasal polyp size. Contrary to the meta‐analysis by Chu et al. [11], the Cochrane review found that ATAD may decrease exacerbations of asthma [7].

Our primary endpoint was to study the effect of ATAD on HRQoL, measured by SNOT‐22. In our within‐group analyses, SNOT‐22 total points decreased significantly in the ASA group but not in the placebo group, possibly reflecting better efficacy of ASA. However, there was no significant between‐group difference over the timespan of the trial. In the nasal subdomain of SNOT‐22, both groups showed a significant decrease in points from before to after treatment. No significant difference between groups was detected over the course of the study. Previous RDBCTs on ATAD treatment have consistently reported beneficial effects on HRQoL compared to placebo [7, 8, 9, 11, 12, 13].

In our study, ATAD showed no significant improvements in CRSwNP. The groups did not significantly differ in NPS change over time. Nevertheless, as the ASA group showed a nonsignificant trend indicating a reduction in nasal polyps and the placebo group did not, ATAD could have a minor effect on nasal polyps. Similarly, one previous RDBCT by Fruth et al. [9] found a nonsignificant trend showing that ATAD might improve NPS. This is the first RDBCT to report the EPOS 2012 CRS control distribution, which was similar in both groups during the study, and neither of the groups significantly improved. Interestingly, half of the subjects receiving a placebo had partly controlled CRS at IMP cessation. This could be due to better overall treatment compliance in the regular treatments for nasal polyps during the trial. Placebo‐arm improvements are often seen in RDBCTs [33]. The changes in Sniffin' sticks classification were similar for both the ASA and placebo groups, and no significant improvement was observed in either group. This result is contrary to the results of previous RDBCTs, which indicate that ATAD may improve smell compared to placebo [8, 9]. This study considered OCS instead of inhaled or intranasal corticosteroids and did not find a difference between ATAD and placebo during the study.

ATAD did not affect asthma control. Across the study, no significant difference between groups was observed in ACT total points, and neither group showed significant within‐group change. The groups did not show significant improvements in FEV1%, PEF, or eNO. However, all this could be due to the relatively good asthma control at baseline, as this study succeeded in preventing patients with very poor asthma control from entering the trial part. According to previous literature, ATAD may improve the results of asthma control assessments compared to placebo, and it may also improve FEV1, although not clinically significantly [8, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Only one other study has taken PEF into account, with similar results to ours; no change in placebo or ATAD groups during follow‐up [8].

We identified four patients receiving ASA (patients with polypectomy before AD excluded) who were classified as responders, as they benefitted from ATAD in multiple clinical parameters and could continue the treatment for the whole follow‐up. Interestingly, in our responder analyses, some indications were seen that the responders had worse asthma control at baseline than nonresponders, which was unexpected. Future studies should address whether there is a way to identify the patients who could respond to the treatment.

In our study, adverse effects and drop‐outs were more common in the ASA group. In previous RDBCTs, the reported adverse effects have been GI symptoms and skin rash [8, 9, 10, 12, 13]. The meta‐analyses concluded that ATAD may increase the occurrence of adverse events; however, its effect on significant GI adverse events may be minimal [7, 11]. Surprisingly, in our study, GI symptoms were more common in the placebo than the ASA group, perhaps reflecting their overall high prevalence in the population [34]. The most significant difference between groups was in bleeding tendency and respiratory symptoms. The occurrence of asthma/CRS exacerbations was equal in both groups.

In the ASA arm, the total response rate was 25.0%, and the drop‐out rate was 18.8%. Previous studies show that the usage of other conservative treatments for CRSwNP is low (18% for intranasal corticosteroids and 1% for nasal douching), and this could result from poor compliance, a good response from surgical treatments, or the treatment guidelines not reaching the physicians [35, 36]. In our study, the high compliance to the treatment could result from participating in a trial with several follow‐up visits, but longer follow‐up times could reveal more compliance problems.

Our results highlight that ATAD requires balancing between tolerable dosage and side effects, and still less than half of the patients tolerating the treatment may be seen as responders. The poor response could be in part related to our small ASA dose, or the fact that in this study ATAD was used without preceding polypectomy. However, previous studies have shown beneficial results with similar ASA doses to us, with or without preceding ESS [9, 10, 12, 13].

Direct conclusions on the future of the use of ATAD cannot readily be made on the basis of this study. RDBCT studies comparing biologics, ATAD, and placebo, and meta‐analyses are needed to decide between the treatments. However, since the inclusion criteria align with those of other clinical trials, for example, trials on biologics for N‐ERD [37], the results may be suitable for future meta‐analyses. Direct RDBCT studies comparing ATAD and biologics are lacking, and the sample sizes are small, especially in ATAD RDBCTs [14]. A review and network meta‐analysis comparing placebo, biologic treatments, and ATAD ranked Dupilumab among the best in the compared treatments for all seven studied outcomes, and ATAD for only one outcome [14]. Additionally, ATAD increased adverse events, whereas the biologics were comparable to placebo. As biologics are on their way to becoming the gold standard treatment for severe CRSwNP with N‐ERD, reasoning new ATAD trials may become challenging.

As a conclusion, ATAD may not be significantly better than placebo for achieving clinical improvements for the majority of adult CRSwNP patients with asthma and N‐ERD. In addition, adverse events are common and oftentimes lead to discontinuing the treatment.
