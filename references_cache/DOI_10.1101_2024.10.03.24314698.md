# From Past to Present: Pompe Disease, Pseudodeficiency Alleles, and Diagnostic Challenges
**Authors:** Florencia Giliberto, Paula Inés Buonfiglio, Gabriel Capellino, Carmen Llames Massini, Viviana Dalamón, Leonela Luce, Micaela Carcione
**DOI:** [10.1101/2024.10.03.24314698](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.10.03.24314698)

## Content

Abstract

                  Pompe disease is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by
                  GAA
                  variants leading to acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency. Diagnosis is challenging due to the variable phenotypic presentation and overlap with other conditions. Traditionally, diagnosis relies on measuring enzyme activity, but next-generation sequencing (NGS) advancements have improved accuracy. However, interpreting variants is complex, especially because pseudodeficiency alleles mimic disease-causing variants. We present two patients harboring the pseudodeficiency allele NM_000152.5(
                  GAA
                  ):c.271G>A, p.Asp91Asn, which is confusing due to inaccurate reports and results related to enzymatic activity. The first case was a recently published controversial case of a 700-year-old mummy in which the authors classified the variant as pathogenic. The second patient had symptoms compatible with late-onset Pompe disease and was homozygous for the variant. We aimed to determine the correct variant classification using
                  GAA
                  :c.271G>A as a model and to achieve a genetic diagnosis of the second patient. This variant was analyzed following international guidelines (ACMG-AMP) and reviewed with the Lysosomal Diseases Variant Curation Expert Panel. The second patient underwent NGS. We demonstrated that
                  GAA
                  :c.271G>A meets the criterion of being classified as benign for Pompe. Additionally, the second patient carried a heterozygous pathogenic
                  PABPN1
                  variant associated with oculopharyngeal muscular dystrophy, which better explained the clinical features. This underscores the importance of expanding the genetic analysis in the presence of pseudodeficiency alleles that can mask the true cause of the disease and highlights the fact that an accurate diagnosis should adhere to guidelines on variant curation to reduce the risk of misdiagnosis, which could result in inadequate care and risky medical decisions.

Introduction 56 
Pompe disease, also named glycogen storage disease type II or acid maltase deficiency, is a 57 
rare genetic disorder with an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern. Globally, the 58 
prevalence of this disease is estimated to be between 5,000 and 10,000 people, with an 59 
incidence of 1 in 57,000 people for the late -onset form [1]. Pompe disease is caused by 60 
alterations in the GAA gene, leading to a deficiency of acid alpha -glucosidase, a crucial 61 
lysosomal enzyme for degrading glycogen [2–4]. Its deficiency causes glycogen accumulation 62 
in lysosomes, leading to progressive cellular dysfunction and cell death. It primarily affects 63 
neurons and skeletal, smooth and cardiac muscles, resulting in muscle function impairment 64 
and progressive and irreversible deterioration [5]. 65 
Pompe disease exhibits significant clinical heterogeneity, variation is based on the age of 66 
onset and symptoms progression. Residual acid alpha -glucosidase activity determines the 67 
onset of the disease. Infantile -onset Pompe disease (IOPD) is the most severe form of the 68 
disease and occurs when the enzyme activity is less than 1%, conversely, late -onset Pompe 69 
disease (LOPD) occurs when the residual enzyme activity ranges between 2% and 30% [4]. 70 
Symptoms of IOPD include progressive muscle weakness, hypotonia since birth, severe 71 
respiratory problems, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and hepatomegaly. Patients also often 72 
experience delays in motor development, such as difficulties in sitting, crawling, or walking 73 
[2,5]. However , symptoms in LOPD patients can have a juvenile or adult onset, and patients 74 
tend to develop symptoms more slowly than IOPD patients. Patients may present progressive 75 
muscle weakness, respiratory problems that worsen in advanced stages, muscle pain, fatigue, 76 
and difficulty in walking. Muscle atrophy and proximal weakness are also common features 77 
of this disease [1].  78

The type of molecular alteration and its location in the GAA gene often predict the clinical 79 
phenotype of Pompe disease [6]. To date, more than 300 alterations in GAA have been 80 
described [2–4]. Most (96%) of them are single nucleotide variants (SNVs), while only 4% are 81 
copy number variants (CNVs). Therefore, sequencing is the ideal strategy for executing the 82 
GAA genetic diagnostic algorithm [7,8].  83 
The measurement of acid alpha-glucosidase activity in dried blood spots was traditionally the 84 
primary method for diagnosing Pompe disease. In recent years, the advances and 85 
popularization of NGS technologies have led to a paradigm shift toward genetic/molecular 86 
diagnosis. To reduce arbitrariness in the classification of identified sequence variants, the 87 
American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for 88 
Molecular Pathology (AMP) have developed international consensus guidelines to estimate a 89 
score based on collected evidence that allows variant classification into one of five possible 90 
categories: pathogenic (≥10), likely pathogenic (6 to 9), uncertain significance (0 to 5), likely 91 
benign (-1 to -6), or benign (≤-7) [9]. Current recommendations indicate that the diagnosis of 92 
Pompe disease should be established when both a deficit of acid alpha -glucosidase enzyme 93 
and two pathogenic trans variants in the GAA gene are detected [10]. Accurate and early 94 
diagnosis of Pompe disease is even more relevant now that an enzyme replacement therapy 95 
for treating  these patients has been approved [11,12]. 96 
It should be noted that diagnosing Pompe disease can be challenging. Clinically speaking, the 97 
variable presentation of the disease and the symptoms overlap with other conditions and 98 
often delay diagnosis. Additionally, the interpretation of GAA variants can be complex, 99 
especially in the presence of pseudodeficiency alleles, which can mimic disease -causing 100 
variants. The main difficulty lies in the fact that pseudodeficiency alleles exhibit low acid 101

Here, we present two cases in which the pseudodeficiency allele 110 
NM_000152.5(GAA):c.271G>A, p.Asp91Asn, also known as GAA2, was identified. The first is 111 
a controversial case of a 700-year-old mummy published in 2021, where the authors classified 112 
the change as pathogenic and as an LOPD case, confusing the interpretation of the variant 113 
within the scientific community [15]. The second patient referred to our laboratory was 114 
clinically suspected of presenting  LOPD. We demonstrated the importance of adhering to 115 
international guidelines for accurate classification of variants that follow Mendelian 116 
inheritance using this pseudodeficiency allele as a model. Finally, our goal was to achieve a 117 
definitive and accurate diagnosis of the second patient, underscoring the critical role of 118 
precise genetic diagnosis in guiding appropriate clinical management. 119

Results 152 
Case 1: Clinical characteristics 153 
The proband is the Cangrande della Scala (1291–1329 CE), a prominent figure from medieval 154 
Italy known for his military and political accomplishments as the Lord of Verona. Due to the 155 
destruction of the Scaliger family archives, the little we know about his health relies on 156 
limited, ambiguous and, often, biased historical accounts. Contemporary sources indicate 157 
that Cangrande suffered from several bouts of illness throughout his life. At the age of 23, he 158 
presented a significant ailment that temporarily hindered his mobility. Later, at the age of 34, 159 
he suffered from a severe and prolonged illness that was nearly fatal. Last, at the age of 38 160 
years, he died from an illness vaguely described as fluxum, often interpreted as vomiting or 161 
diarrhea. The accounts report that his last three days were marked by fever and 162 
gastrointestinal symptoms. In 2004, an autopsy and a toxicology analysis performed on 163 
Cangrande's mummified remains detected toxic doses of digitalis in his preserved organs, 164 
which could be interpreted as poisoning or as a remedy for a cardiac condition. Overall, the 165 
above mentioned accounts are nonspecific and could be due to various conditions, ranging 166 
from battle injuries to infectious or chronic diseases.  167

Classification of the NM_000152.5(GAA):c.271G>A, p.Asp91Asn variant 181 
The GAA2 variant was found in a heterozygous state together with the pathogenic 182 
NM_000152.5(GAA):c.1465G>A in the mummy from case 1 and in a homozygous state in case 183 
2. Therefore, to reclassify the variant and provide genetic diagnosis, evidence was gathered 184 
from various literature reviews and databases and rigorously analyzed. A summary of the 185 
applied criteria is provided in Table 1. Notably, there is evidence indicating that the GAA2 186 
variant affects splicing and in vitro enzymatic activity; however, this effect was proven to be 187 
compensated by an increase in substrate concentration [13,19,20]. Therefore, neither the PS3 188 
nor the BS3 criteria were applied. Additionally, BA1 was implemented given the variant’s high 189 
allele frequency in general population databases, a criterion that could not be excluded owing 190 
to the lack of strong and solid evidence supporting its pathogenicity. Overall, the GAA2 variant 191 
meets the criterion of being classified as benign for Pompe disease. Moreover, the correlation 192 
between the presence of GAA2 and decreased alpha -glucosidase activity indicated that the 193 
variant was a pseudodeficiency allele. 194

Classification of the NM_004643.4 (PABPN1):c.18_23dup variant  195 
To provide a genetic diagnosis for patient 2 and due to the reclassification of the GAA2 variant 196 
as benign, the WES analysis was expanded to other genes with clinical features overlapping 197 
with LOPD. This screening revealed a heterozygous 6 -nucleotide duplication in PABPN1 198 
[NM_004643.4 ( PABPN1):c.18_23dup]. This gene is associated with autosomal dominant 199 
oculopharyngeal muscular dystrophy (OPMD; OMIM #602279), a disease caused by the 200 
abnormal expansion of GCN trinucleotide repeats. The detected duplication resulted in an 201 
expansion of the GCG triplet, leading to the addition of two alanine residues in the polyalanine 202 
tract region (Figure 1). Evidence was gathered to classify the variant, and a score of 10 points 203 
was achieved, reaching the pathogenic classification. A summary of the applied criteria is 204 
provided in Table 2. Therefore, the proband was diagnosed with OPMD instead of the 205 
suspected late-onset Pompe disease. 206 
Discussion 207 
For several years, the interpretation of the NM_000152.5(GAA):c.271G>A pseudodeficiency 208 
allele has led to controversy among the  scientific community due to its inaccurate in vitro 209 
enzymatic activity [13,19–21]. Iadarola et al. (2021) further contributed to this confusion by 210 
classifying the variant as pathogenic in a renowned journal in 2021 [15]. Furthermore, the 211 
authors concluded that Cangrande della Scala suffered from LOPD; thus, he was the first 212 
known case of this disease. These authors based their conclusions on the interpretation of 213 
insufficient, imprecise and biased clinical data obtained from limited reliable sources over 7 214 
centuries of age, together with the finding of two variants in the GAA gene (the 215 
pseudodeficiency allele c.271G>A and a pathogenic variant c.1465G>A) in the mummified 216 
sample. According to the current diagnostic guidelines for Pompe disease, which requires 217

mRNA transport from the nucleus to the cytoplasm and degradation [24]. OPMD is a rare, 237 
late-onset autosomal dominant neuromuscular disorder characterized by proximal muscle 238 
weakness, ptosis, and difficulty in swallowing [25]. The molecular mechanism of this 239

Despite being distinct disorders, OPMD and Pompe disease present significant clinical overlap 245 
[1]. These patients share clinical features such as progressive muscle weakness, dysphagia, 246 
and, to a lesser extent, ptosis and proximal muscle involvement. However, Pompe disease is 247 
distinguished by a more significant respiratory engagement and a more generalized muscle 248 
weakness [1,27]. Overall, Case 2 illustrates the importance of considering a differential 249 
diagnosis when faced with ambiguous enzymatic and genetic results. 250 
On the one hand, this case allowed us to confirm that the patient’s decreased in vitro acid 251 
alpha-glucosidase enzymatic activity was due to the pseudodeficiency variant in 252 
homozygosity and that it was not associated with the patient's final diagnosis which was 253 
OPMD [10]. Hence, caution should be taken when considering these cases, as 254 
pseudodeficiency alleles can mask the underlying cause of the disease if they are not carefully 255 
evaluated using 4 -MUG as a substrate [10]. Moreover, if the patient in case 2 had been 256 
diagnosed with LOPD, she would have undergone unnecessary enzyme replacement therapy, 257 
while the OPMD would have remained undetected. On the other hand, patient 2 serves as an 258 
example exome analysis scope, as it permits in silico panel customization to find alterations 259 
in genes that are not the primary clinical suspicion. 260

NM_000152.5(GAA):c.271G>A, p.Asp91Asn variant as benign considering all the available 262
