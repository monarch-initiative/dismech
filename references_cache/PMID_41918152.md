# Monitoring and Managing of Ergot Alkaloids in Cereal Grains: A Comprehensive Analysis of Analytical Challenges, Sampling Uncertainty, and Processing for Food Safety.
**Authors:** Kodikara C, Bandara N, Tittlemier SA
**Journal:** Compr Rev Food Sci Food Saf (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/1541-4337.70456](https://doi.org/10.1111/1541-4337.70456)

## Content

Ergot alkaloids (EAs), toxic secondary metabolites produced by Claviceps purpurea, pose food and feed safety concerns for cereal grains, particularly rye and wheat. While EAs are most frequently associated with rye, their occurrence in other cereals has been increasingly reported across diverse regions. This review critically synthesizes current knowledge on the global occurrence, analytical detection, regulatory control, and processing behavior of EAs, with a particular focus on factors that limit data reliability and risk management. A defining challenge in EA monitoring is the highly heterogeneous distribution of ergot sclerotia and alkaloids within bulk grain, which introduces substantial sampling uncertainty that often exceeds analytical variability. Recent advances in comminution, spatial sampling models, and composite sampling strategies have improved representativeness, yet remain underemphasized relative to instrumental developments. Analytical methods have progressed from nonspecific HPLC approaches to high-throughput LC-MS/MS and LC-HRMS platforms, enabling sensitive, multi-analyte detection and epimer-specific quantification; however, persistent limitations, including matrix effects, limited availability of reference materials, and insufficient characterization of conjugated or matrix-bound EAs, continue to constrain comparability across studies. Regulatory approaches vary globally, with the European Union adopting chemical limits for total EAs, while other regions rely primarily on sclerotia-based grading. Climate change and evolving agricultural practices further complicate exposure assessment and mitigation. By integrating sampling theory, analytical chemistry, processing effects, and regulatory frameworks, this review provides a unified perspective on EA risk management and identifies priorities for future research, including harmonized sampling strategies and high-throughput analytical platforms capable of capturing both free and conjugated EA forms.

In recent years, ergot contamination has re‐emerged as a significant threat to cereal production systems worldwide, with profound implications for food safety, livestock health, and economic stability (King 2025; Senatra et al. 2025; Walkowiak et al. 2022). Triggered by changing climatic conditions and agronomic practices, the incidence and severity of ergot outbreaks are increasing, particularly in wheat, rye, and related grains. In this context, understanding the nature of ergot alkaloids (EAs) and developing effective monitoring and mitigation strategies has become more critical.

EAs are a diverse group of biologically active indole‐derived mycotoxins primarily produced by the fungus Claviceps purpurea. This fungus infects flowering cereal crops, replacing the developing grain with hardened fungal structures called sclerotia (King 2025; Walkowiak et al. 2022). These sclerotia are rich in EAs, a class of indole‐derived secondary metabolites characterized by a tetracyclic ergoline ring structure and diverse peptide or amide side chains that contribute to their biological activity (Florea et al. 2017). These alkaloids exist as pairs of epimers, consisting of a C8‐(R)‐configured form (commonly referred to by the suffix “‐ine”) that has generally been associated with biological activity, and a corresponding C8‐(S)‐epimer (denoted by the suffix “‐inine”) historically considered less or non‐bioactive (Dänicke 2016). However, emerging evidence suggests that the S‐epimers may retain some degree of activity under certain conditions, indicating that their biological relevance cannot be fully excluded (Cherewyk, Grusie‐Ogilvie, Parker, et al. 2022). Reports indicate greater ergot pressure outside rye in some regions, with humidity during anthesis being a consistent risk factor; several datasets suggest rising incidence in regions where climate variability has increased (Bacque 2025; King 2025; Malysheva et al. 2013; Peloso et al. 2024; Tittlemier et al. 2015). The toxicological relevance of EAs lies in their ability to mimic neurotransmitters, such as serotonin and dopamine, and to constrict blood vessels, leading to a condition known as ergotism (Liegl and McGrath 2016). Symptoms can range from hallucinations and convulsions to limb ischemia and gangrene. Historic outbreaks, such as medieval cases of “St. Anthony's Fire,” highlight their potential public health impact. Although average levels are generally low in modern supply chains, EA residues can persist after cleaning/milling and may be present even when sclerotia are not visually detected; consequently, the European Union (EU) set maximum levels for the sum of 12 EAs in cereals and cereal products in 2021. In modern food systems, contamination levels are generally lower but still pose food and feed safety risks, particularly when sclerotia are fragmented and dispersed during mechanical processing, making visual detection more difficult (European Food Safety Authority [EFSA] 2012; Peloso et al. 2024).

When considering cereals and cereal‐based products, wheat is a particularly challenging commodity for EA management, due to interactions among Claviceps infection biology, kernel morphology, and modern harvesting and processing practices (King 2025; Senatra et al. 2025). Infection occurs during flowering, and the resulting sclerotia often resemble wheat kernels in size and density, complicating their physical separation during cleaning. Moreover, sclerotia may fragment during harvesting and milling, leading to the dispersal of alkaloid‐rich particles that are not reliably detected by visual inspection alone (EFSA 2012; Franzmann et al. 2011). Combined with the highly heterogeneous distribution of EAs within and between wheat lots, these factors contribute to substantial sampling uncertainty and sporadic exceedances of regulatory limits, even when mean concentrations appear low. Consequently, effective control of EAs in wheat depends not only on postharvest interventions but also on agronomic and environmental conditions that influence infection and alkaloid production in the field.

Numerous agronomic and environmental factors influence the global occurrence of EAs in cereal grains. For instance, zero tillage, grassy field margins, and excessive rainfall during flowering increase the likelihood of Claviceps infection and subsequent EA production (Peloso et al. 2024; Tittlemier et al. 2015). Studies in Canada, Europe, and North Africa have reported contamination in both food and feed wheat, with total EA concentrations occasionally exceeding 1 mg/kg in harvested grain (Walkowiak et al. 2022). However, final processed products generally exhibit lower levels due to cleaning and milling processes (Malysheva et al. 2014). Given the widespread occurrence of EA contamination across different regions and cereal supply chains, efforts to mitigate these toxins during food and feed production have become increasingly important. Various decontamination strategies commonly used for mycotoxins, including mechanical separation through milling (Franzmann et al. 2011), thermal processing, such as baking and extrusion (Fajardo et al. 1995), chemical treatments such as chlorine gas application (Halm et al. 2023), and emerging approaches such as cold atmospheric pressure plasma (CAPP) (Gavahian and Cullen 2020; Murtaza et al. 2024; Ranjan et al. 2023), have shown varying levels of success. However, the effectiveness of these methods, specifically for EAs, remains limited, with most conventional techniques yielding only partial reductions.

From a regulatory perspective, the EU has implemented some of the most comprehensive regulations, recently lowering maximum levels for total EAs in both unprocessed and processed cereals under Regulations EU 2023/915 and 2024/1808, which reflect growing concern over EA exposure in vulnerable populations, including infants (Publications Office of the European Union 2024). Canada, the United States, and Australia primarily regulate ergot based on sclerotia content by weight (e.g., percentage m/m) rather than total EA concentration. Because sclerotia counts do not always correlate with alkaloid levels, significant EA residues may remain undetected even when visual grading limits are met. This creates gaps in food safety, particularly when grain is traded internationally with jurisdictions that set maximum levels for total alkaloids (e.g., the EU) (Canadian Grain Commission [CGC] 2024; FDA 2024). These discrepancies not only affect domestic monitoring by creating inconsistencies in testing targets but also lead to variability in detection limits and regulatory compliance assessments. Such inconsistencies also pose barriers to international trade, as noncompliance with import‐country limits can lead to costly shipment rejections.

One of the primary challenges in EA risk monitoring is the uneven distribution of sclerotia and alkaloids within grain consignments. Research indicates that EA contamination is highly heterogeneous, with significant variation both within and between grain lots, making representative sampling difficult (Tittlemier, Blagden, et al. 2025; Tittlemier, Cramer, et al. 2025). This variability complicates both sampling and analysis, making it difficult to obtain representative measurements. Traditional reliance on visual inspection for sclerotia, as used in North America and parts of Asia, does not reliably ensure grain safety, since small fragments of sclerotia or ergot “dust” can adhere to otherwise sound kernels and remain undetected, leaving EAs present even after the visible bodies are removed (Mainka et al. 2005). This does not automatically render grain unsafe, but it highlights that visual grading cannot confirm alkaloid concentrations, which require chemical testing. While one non‐peer‐reviewed report (Gordon et al. 2025) suggested possible translocation of alkaloids into surrounding grains, peer‐reviewed studies to date provide no evidence supporting such diffusion (Franzmann et al. 2011). Moreover, visual inspection does not provide any indication of the alkaloid concentration within the sclerotia themselves, which can vary significantly among individual bodies (Appelt and Ellner 2009; Young 1981).

Advancements in analytical techniques have significantly improved the identification and quantification of EAs in cereal grains. Among these, liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) emerged as the most widely used method due to its high sensitivity, specificity, and ability to simultaneously quantify multiple EAs and their epimers in complex cereal matrices (Silva et al. 2023). However, the accuracy and sensitivity of LC–MS/MS are often challenged by matrix effects, ion suppression, and the chemical instability of alkaloids, particularly during extraction and storage. High‐resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) platforms, such as Orbitrap and quadrupole time‐of‐flight (QTOF) mass spectrometers, are increasingly employed for EA analysis. The high mass accuracy and resolving power of HRMS improve the differentiation of analytes from background signals, thereby mitigating some consequences of matrix effects. Although ionization suppression primarily occurs during electrospray ionization (ESI) rather than during MS detection (Furey et al. 2013). HRMS can still enhance analyte identification by separating target compounds from co‐eluting interferences. Moreover, HRMS can assist in detecting and differentiating unstable or epimerized alkaloid forms, even when degradation complicates quantification, by providing high mass accuracy, structural elucidation, and retrospective analysis in complex matrices coupled with proper sample preparation (Nam et al. 2024). These methods also enable retrospective study, which helps identify emerging EA variants. Nevertheless, HRMS remains costly and technically demanding, limiting its accessibility in routine monitoring. The epimerization of alkaloids during storage, with the conversion of active “‐ine” forms into their less toxic “‐inine” counterparts, adds further complexity, as both forms may need to be quantified to accurately assess exposure. Despite significant progress, challenges remain due to the limited number of certified reference materials, internal standards, and rapid tests for the accurate quantification of EAs.

In light of these challenges, this review aims to provide an comprehensive overview of current approaches for the identification and quantitation of EA contamination in cereal grains with a particular focus on the role of sample preparation and sampling strategy in reducing variability, recent advances in analytical detection techniques, the effect of processing on EA, global occurrence patterns and contamination hotspots, and the evolving regulatory frameworks governing EA limits. The review also identifies critical gaps in harmonized monitoring. It highlights the need for international collaboration in research, regulation, and capacity‐building to better manage this persistent threat to cereal grains and food safety.

EAs are fungal‐derived secondary metabolites that pose significant challenges in agriculture and food safety. These compounds exhibit a variety of biological effects due to their interactions with monoamine neurotransmitter receptors, such as dopamine, serotonin, noradrenaline, and adrenaline (Florea et al. 2017). The structural diversity of EA influences their receptor‐binding properties, determining whether they act as agonists or antagonists. Furthermore, variations in receptor subtypes contribute to the complexity of their biological activities. These interactions can produce a variety of effects, including therapeutic benefits and adverse reactions such as hallucinations, vasomotor changes, muscle contractions, and disturbances in sleep patterns (Panaccione et al. 2012). Unintended side effects have been noted in some cases, highlighting the unpredictability of these compounds' interactions with different receptor types.

The fungi that synthesize EAs are found across diverse taxonomic groups and ecological niches. Notably, species from the families Clavicipitaceae (order Hypocreales) and Trichocomaceae (order Eurotiales) are known producers, though these represent a minority within their respective families (Florea et al. 2017; Panaccione et al. 2012). In Clavicipitaceae, genera such as Claviceps, Neotyphodium, and Periglandula are well‐recognized for producing EAs. Claviceps species, commonly referred to as ergot fungi, target the reproductive tissues of specific grasses, where they infect flowers and replace seeds with sclerotia. These sclerotia serve as storage structures, rich in EAs and other metabolites, enabling the fungi to persist through unfavorable conditions (Figure 1). This adaptive mechanism suggests possible ecological roles for EAs, especially defense against herbivores and microbial antagonists, consistent with hypotheses in the literature (Beaulieu et al. 2021; Schardl et al. 2006).

EAs have a unique tetracyclic core structure known as the ergoline ring (Figure 2). This structure features a methylated nitrogen atom at the N‐6 position, specific substitutions at C‐8, and double bonds located either between C‐8 and C‐9 or C‐9 and C‐10 (Flieger et al. 1997). The chemical and biological properties of EAs are influenced by these structural variations, which allow for their classification into different groups. These structural differences have profound implications for their biological activity and detection. EAs are typically divided into three main categories. The first group, clavines, represents simpler forms of EAs that lack extensive oxidation at C‐17. Clavines can function as intermediates in the biosynthesis of more complex compounds or as end‐products in non‐lysergic‐producing fungi. The second group consists of lysergic‐derived alkaloids, which include simple amides, such as ergometrine, and complex peptide‐containing molecules known as ergopeptines, including ergotamine and ergocornine (Flieger et al. 1997). The third group, dihydro EAs, is characterized by a saturated D‐ring, which distinguishes them structurally and alters their fluorescence properties compared to lysergic‐derived compounds. The C‐8 position in EAs is important in the biological and chemical classification of EAs. Isomers with an R‐configuration at C‐8, known as ergopeptines, are biologically active and include compounds such as ergotamine (Flieger et al. 1997). Their S‐configuration counterparts, termed ergopeptinines, were historically regarded as biologically inactive, but emerging evidence indicates that some retain measurable biological activity under certain conditions (Flieger et al. 1997; Schardl et al. 2006; Panaccione et al. 2012; Florea et al. 2017; King 2025). The inactive ergopeptinines can revert to the active ergopeptines when exposed to specific solvents such as methanol or under acidic conditions. This epimerization process is an important consideration during the extraction, clean‐up, and quantification of EAs, as both forms may be present and contribute to contamination levels in cereals (Bacque 2025; Panaccione et al. 2012). The stability of EAs is affected by light sensitivity, leading to isomerization and degradation (Krska and Crews 2008; Bacque 2025).

Chemical structures of the 12 major ergot alkaloids, including both the ‐ine and ‐inine epimers. Epimerization at C‐8 leads to two stereoisomers of ergot alkaloids: the ‐ine form (biologically active) and the ‐inine form (less active, more stable); these alkaloids are the primary bioactive compounds responsible for the toxicological effects associated with ergot contamination in food and feed.

The presence of double bonds in the D‐ring also gives EAs distinctive fluorescence properties, which are valuable for their detection and identification (Flieger et al. 1997). These compounds are generally soluble in organic solvents but show limited solubility in water, a characteristic that affects their extraction and purification methods in analytical processes. Historically, the first EA to be isolated, ergotamine, was identified in 1920 and used therapeutically to manage postpartum hemorrhage. Since then, over 50 EAs have been characterized, demonstrating their relevance in agriculture, medicine, and biochemical research (Maruo et al. 2018). The intricate chemistry of EAs, including their structural diversity, fluorescence properties, epimerization, and stability, highlights their importance and complexity in both scientific and practical applications.

Globally, the prevalence and severity of EA contamination in wheat are strongly influenced by agricultural practices, environmental conditions, and variations in fungal strains (Agriopoulou 2021; Berraies et al. 2024). Occurrence studies included in this review were identified through structured literature searches of Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed using combinations of keywords such as “ergot alkaloids,” “cereals,” “occurrence,” and “monitoring.” Studies were included if they reported quantitative measurements of EAs in cereal grains or cereal‐based products and represented different geographic regions and cereal types.

Regions with wet, humid climates during the flowering period of cereals are particularly susceptible to outbreaks, as demonstrated in multiyear surveys from Europe and North America (Babič et al. 2020; Müller et al. 2006). For example, comprehensive monitoring in western Canada has highlighted the persistent presence of ergot sclerotia and EA, particularly during years of widespread infection. In 2011, approximately 20% of wheat in western Canada showed some level of ergot infection, and surveys of Canadian Western Red Spring (CWRS) and Canadian Western Amber Durum (CWAD) wheat revealed measurable ergot incidence, with durum wheat being especially vulnerable (Coufal‐Majewski et al. 2016; Tittlemier et al. 2015). These authors found that ergot sclerotia percentages in harvest samples correlated directly with EA concentrations, highlighting the link between visible infection and chemical risk. However, this relationship may not hold true in all cases due to the heterogeneous distribution of sclerotia fragments and alkaloids within grain lots. Correspondingly, EA concentrations ranged from below the detection limit to 7.81 mg/kg. Durum wheat exhibited greater susceptibility to ergot infection and subsequent alkaloid accumulation, as indicated by steeper concentration curves and gradients across sampling increments within consignments compared with common wheat, possibly due to environmental or varietal factors. The study found that while harvest samples showed higher alkaloid concentrations, postharvest cleaning and grading processes significantly reduced contamination levels in export shipments. For instance, the median total alkaloid concentration in wheat shipments was 0.195 mg/kg, much lower than in harvest samples. These findings underscore the effectiveness of mechanical sorting and sieving in mitigating contamination. Another key observation was the epimerization of “ine” forms to “inine” forms during prolonged storage, highlighting the importance of controlled storage conditions. Freshly harvested samples contained higher levels of biologically active “ine” alkaloids, whereas older samples, including shipments, exhibited increased proportions of inactive “inine” epimers. In terms of alkaloid patterns, ergocristine and ergocristinine were the predominant compounds across Canadian wheat samples (Walkowiak et al. 2022). This pattern is consistent with barley and rye in the region but contrasts with European studies, in which ergocryptine or ergotamine is often dominant (Malysheva et al. 2014). These differences likely reflect regional variations in C. purpurea strains and environmental influences on alkaloid biosynthesis. Agricultural practices such as zero tillage and grass field margins have been implicated in the rising prevalence of EAs. Zero tillage leaves sclerotia on the soil surface, enabling the release of infective spores during subsequent growing seasons. Combined with environmental factors such as precipitation and temperature, these practices have contributed to an increase in ergot contamination in western Canada over the last decade (Tittlemier et al. 2015).

EAs are also a recurring contaminant in wheat across Europe, with surveys reporting contamination in 10%–20% of samples and concentrations occasionally exceeding regulatory limits established for food (Babič et al. 2020; Müller et al. 2006). A large‐scale study assessing 1065 cereal and cereal product samples from various European countries found that 67% of wheat‐based food products were contaminated with EAs. In contrast, contamination in wheat feed samples was comparatively lower at 27%. EA concentrations in wheat food ranged to a maximum of 591 µg/kg, with a median of 7 µg/kg (Malysheva et al. 2014). In wheat feed, alkaloid levels reached up to 701 µg/kg, though the median remained below the limit of quantification (LOQ) of 1 µg/kg. These findings highlight that while wheat is less susceptible than rye to Claviceps infection, contamination remains a significant concern due to its extensive cultivation and widespread use in multigrain products. The predominant alkaloids detected in European wheat were ergosine, ergocryptine, and ergocristine, often found alongside their inactive epimers, such as ergosinine and ergocryptinine. Regional variability in contamination levels was also noted, with higher median EA concentrations in wheat products from Germany and Poland compared to those from Sweden and Finland (Malysheva et al. 2014). Such differences can be attributed to climatic conditions, as regions with high humidity and moderate temperatures during the flowering stage of wheat are more conducive to Claviceps infection (Liegl and McGrath 2016; Walkowiak et al. 2022).

Mulder et al. (2015) reported the occurrence of EAs in grain‐based products intended for infants and young children in the Netherlands across multiple survey years (2011, 2012, and 2014). Among the 47, 46, and 20 samples analyzed, EAs were detected in 25, 16, and 13, respectively. Mean total EA concentrations (sum of ‐ine and ‐inine epimers) were 10.6, 6.2, and 8.6 µg/kg, while maximum concentrations reached 79.9, 115.4, and 83.1 µg/kg across the respective survey years. These findings indicate that EAs can occur in cereal‐based foods for infants, although generally at relatively low concentration levels. Official monitoring programs in the Netherlands similarly illustrate the variability and sampling challenges associated with EA contamination. A national survey of cereals and compound feeds conducted under the Dutch National Plan for Feed Safety reported detectable EAs even in samples where visible sclerotia were absent. Among these visually negative samples, the total EA concentrations averaged approximately 75 µg/kg, with a maximum of 297 µg/kg. Rye samples consistently exhibited higher contamination levels than wheat, reflecting its known susceptibility. These findings reinforce the limitation of relying solely on visual inspection and highlight the importance of representative sampling and chemical analysis (Mulder et al. 2012).

Similarly, a Belgian market survey of cereal‐based baby foods evaluated 49 commercial products for EA contamination. EAs were not detected in 26 samples, and only one sample exceeded proposed regulatory limits, with a total EA concentration of 42 µg/kg. Most samples contained either non‐detectable or low contamination levels, highlighting that, while occurrence in infant foods is generally limited, continued monitoring remains important given ventricular safety thresholds for vulnerable populations (Huybrechts et al. 2021).

Large‐scale monitoring in Slovenia further demonstrates the widespread occurrence of EAs in cereal supply chains. Babič et al. (2020) analyzed 517 cereal grain samples intended for animal feed and detected EAs in approximately 17% of samples. Total EA concentrations ranged from below detection limits to 3400 µg/kg, with rye samples exhibiting higher contamination frequencies and concentrations than wheat. The dominant alkaloids included ergosine, ergocristine, and ergocryptine, along with their corresponding epimers. These findings highlight the strong influence of cereal type on contamination risk and further illustrate the highly heterogeneous distribution patterns characteristic of EA contamination.

Recent occurrence data from southeastern Europe further confirm the continued presence of EAs in cereal production systems. Pleadin et al. (2022) analyzed 64 grain samples collected during the 2021 harvest in Croatia, including 53 wheat and 11 rye samples. EAs were detected in approximately 18% of rye samples and in a single wheat sample, with total alkaloid concentrations ranging from below detection limits to 167.4 µg/kg in rye and 68.5 µg/kg in wheat. Rye samples exhibited both higher incidence and higher concentrations than wheat, consistent with rye's greater susceptibility to Claviceps infection. The study also reported that visible sclerotia were present in only two rye samples, illustrating the potential disconnect between visual grading and chemical contamination and reinforcing the importance of representative sampling strategies.

In Italy, EAs present a significant contamination risk in wheat, exacerbated by climatic conditions. A recent study in Lombardy during the summer of 2023 illustrated the impact of extreme weather on fungal development and alkaloid contamination (Peloso et al. 2024). Heavy rains during the flowering and grain‐filling stages created a microclimate of high humidity and low ventilation, ideal for fungal infection. This led to the formation of visible sclerotia on wheat ears at harvest, with alkaloid levels reaching up to 4951 mg/kg in sclerotia, 33 mg/kg in wheat ears, and 1 mg/kg in kernels. The variability in total EA content of sclerotia, reported between 100 and 5000 mg/kg, reflects the influence of fungal strain and host plant interactions (Peloso et al. 2024). Among the 12 monitored EAs, ergosine predominated in 33% of sclerotia samples, while ergometrine was most frequently detected in wheat spikes. In kernels, ergocristine was the dominant alkaloid. This diverse alkaloid profile highlights the need for comprehensive monitoring to assess the contamination risks. The study also simulated potential contamination scenarios. It is estimated that even minimal incorporation of sclerotia during milling could lead to EA concentrations exceeding the legal limits established by EU Regulation 2023/915. For instance, the worst‐case scenario of 0.02% sclerotia (i.e., 0.2 g/kg) sclerotia (m/m) predicted a contamination level of 231 µg/kg in durum wheat flour, surpassing the maximum permissible limit of 100–150 µg/kg for human consumption and posing a particular threat to infant foods with stricter limits of 20 µg/kg. While the predicted 231 µg/kg exceeds regulatory limits in worst‐case scenarios, this does not imply all consumer products exceed safe levels; the modeling illustrates potential exceedance under extreme conditions rather than general non‐compliance. The findings align with broader European observations, as Peloso et al. (2024) documented EA contamination in Italian wheat following torrential rains in 2023 and discussed how such weather extremes may influence outbreak risk, though they did not explicitly attribute the event to climate change (Peloso et al. 2024). Multiyear field monitoring studies in northern Italy further illustrate the strong influence of environmental conditions and cereal species on EA occurrence. Carbonell‐Rozas et al. (2023) evaluated 162 cereal samples collected over three consecutive growing seasons and detected EAs in approximately 60% of samples. Total alkaloid concentrations ranged from detection limits to 15,389 µg/kg, with rye consistently exhibiting the highest contamination levels, reaching up to 4302 µg/kg. The study demonstrated substantial year‐to‐year variability associated with climatic conditions during flowering and confirmed that cereal genotype and environmental factors play critical roles in determining contamination risk. Lattanzio et al. (2021) reported occurrence data from regulatory monitoring of cereals and cereal‐based products in Italy between 2017 and 2020. Among the analyzed samples, more than 86% had EA concentrations below the quantification limit, while positive samples showed total alkaloid levels up to 94.2 µg/kg. These findings indicate that although most commercially marketed products contain low levels of EAs, sporadic contamination events still occur, necessitating continued surveillance.

A study by Carbonell‐Rozas, Gámiz‐Gracia, et al. (2021) aimed to assess the prevalence of EAs in 60 cereal samples sold in Algeria and estimate the daily intake of EAs for the Algerian population. Out of the 60 samples analyzed, 12 were found to be contaminated with EAs, with wheat showing a higher contamination rate than barley. Specifically, 26.7% of wheat samples tested positive, with EA concentrations ranging from 3.66 to 76.0 µg/kg and an average of 33.1 µg/kg. Ergocryptine, ergosine, and ergocristine were the most frequently detected alkaloids in wheat. In barley, 13.3% of the samples were contaminated, with EA concentrations ranging from 17.8 to 53.9 µg/kg and an average of 35.4 µg/kg. These findings align with other studies on EA contamination in cereals, highlighting the relatively low but notable occurrence of EAs in wheat and barley. The study highlighted the challenge of removing sclerotia in dry‐climate regions, where sclerotia are similarly sized to grains, complicating separation (Carbonell‐Rozas, Mahdjoubi, et al. 2021).

The study on the occurrence of EAs in wheat from Albania, based on samples collected during the 2014 and 2015 harvests, reported measurable EA occurrence in Albanian wheat, providing the first dataset for this region (Topi et al. 2017). Out of the 71 wheat samples tested, 33.8% were contaminated with EAs, with a higher contamination rate observed in 2014 (48.6%) compared to 2015 (19.4%). In 2014, a total of 17 out of 35 wheat samples were contaminated with EAs, with concentrations ranging from 17.3 to 975.4 µg/kg. The mean EA concentration in these positive samples was 337.2 µg/kg, with a median value of 226.7 µg/kg. The most frequent EAs detected were ergotamine, ergosine, and ergocristine, along with their epimers, which were found in 62.5% of the contaminated samples. In the highest‐level samples, up to nine different EAs were detected, highlighting the chemical diversity of EA occurrence. In 2015, contamination was lower, with only 19.4% of the 36 samples showing positive results for EAs. The concentrations in these contaminated samples ranged from 10 to 500 µg/kg. This research, being the first of its kind in Albania, provides valuable data on the presence of EAs in wheat and highlights the need for further investigations to better understand the contamination patterns and trends in the country (Topi et al. 2017). As summarized in Table 1, occurrence studies consistently demonstrate that EA contamination is highly variable across regions, cereal types, and supply chain stages. Rye generally exhibits both higher incidence and greater alkaloid concentrations than wheat, reflecting its greater susceptibility to Claviceps infection, although wheat, particularly durum wheat, remains vulnerable under favorable environmental conditions. Most large‐scale monitoring surveys report relatively low median concentrations, often below 10 µg/kg in cereal‐based foods. In contrast, raw grains and feed materials can exhibit substantially higher levels, occasionally exceeding several thousand µg/kg due to the highly heterogeneous distribution of sclerotia within bulk consignments. Contamination events are frequently associated with humid growing seasons and increasingly influenced by climate variability, which affects fungal infection dynamics during flowering. While alkaloid profiles vary across regions, multiyear surveys typically report prevalence rates between 10% and 30%, with sporadic exceedances of regulatory limits. Studies comparing visual inspection with chemical analysis further demonstrate that sclerotia presence does not reliably predict alkaloid content, underscoring the dominant role of sampling uncertainty in determining measurement accuracy. Despite extensive monitoring in Europe and North America, long‐term occurrence data remain limited in Africa, Asia, and South America, highlighting the need for more harmonized surveillance and exposure assessments to better characterize global risk.

The resilience of EAs during food and feed processing presents a challenge for ensuring safety in cereal‐based products. While several conventional and emerging processing strategies have been investigated, current approaches vary greatly in efficacy, often leading to incomplete detoxification and raising concerns about residual toxicity and nutritional integrity (Figure 3) (Gavahian and Cullen 2020; Halm et al. 2023; Tittlemier et al. 2019).

Available processing strategies for mycotoxin decontamination in food and feed; overview of currently applied physical, chemical, and biological processing methods developed to reduce levels of major mycotoxins (aflatoxins, fumonisins, zearalenone, deoxynivalenol, nivalenol, patulin, and others) in food and feed products; despite extensive research on processing interventions for these mycotoxins, no validated or systematic studies have been conducted for ergot alkaloids.

Milling remains the first‐line processing strategy for reducing EA concentrations. Physical cleaning, such as removing sclerotia using gravity tables, color sorters, and optical or visual sorting, is a critical complementary intervention. Studies show this can substantially reduce alkaloid levels: for example, industrial cleaning reduced total mean EAs from 72 to 2 µg/kg in rye, and dramatically lowered concentrations in barley and oats (Coufal‐Majewski et al. 2016; Fajardo et al. 1995; Food Standards Agency 2014). While standardized in some regions, such advanced cleaning equipment is not universally adopted (Food Standards Agency 2014; Garcia 2025). Therefore, expanding access to and adoption of effective physical cleaning technologies in milling operations worldwide represents a key area for further development and investment.

EAs originate in sclerotia rather than kernels. During milling, intact or fragmented sclerotia concentrate in outer fractions such as bran, wheat shorts (a flour‐milling by‐product consisting of fine bran, germ, and residual flour particles) (Huang et al. 2012), and mill feed, while semolina and refined flours typically contain lower levels. The presence of EAs can also occur through abrasion during grain handling, where small sclerotia particles adhere to healthy kernels (Franzmann et al. 2011). For instance, Tittlemier et al. (2019) reported that 84% of the total EA mass was found in the bran and shorts fractions, while semolina retained less than 10%. This partitioning is beneficial for reducing exposure in human food products; however, it also concentrates toxins in by‐products often used in animal feed. Importantly, even intensive milling (e.g., production of refined flours with small particle size) does not fully eliminate EAs, and ergot sclerotia dust generated during cleaning may remain in processed grain fractions, contributing to background contamination (Centre for Food Safety 2021).

Thermal processing methods such as baking, cooking, and extrusion have shown only limited effectiveness in reducing EA concentrations. Baking reduced alkaloid levels in bread crust by 25%–55%, while reductions in the crumb were negligible (Fajardo et al. 1995). Reported EA losses during boiling of pasta and noodles ranged from 47% to 75%. Still, these results are difficult to interpret because the study only quantified R‐epimers, did not normalize for moisture gain, and did not include mass‐balance recovery of alkaloids in the cooking water, which reflects methodological differences, particularly whether only R‐epimers were quantified, how moisture gain during cooking was normalized, and whether cooking water was analyzed. When both R‐ and S‐epimers were included, and a mass‐balance approach was applied, cooked spaghetti retained approximately 90%–93% of the original EA content from semolina, with negligible transfer to cooking water (Tittlemier et al. 2019). Feed processing techniques such as pelleting and extrusion show little impact on overall EA levels but can alter the R:S epimer ratio. Halm et al. (2023) found that steam explosion processing reduced total EAs by 40%–50%, while pelleting and extrusion (at 90°C) had negligible effects on EA content, despite increasing the proportion of S‐epimers. In swine feeding trials, animals fed with steam‐exploded ergot diets showed improved weight gain and feed intake compared to those fed unprocessed contaminated feed, demonstrating the practical value of partial detoxification (Halm et al. 2023). These findings suggest that the apparent losses observed in some studies are primarily due to epimerization of R‐epimers into S‐counterparts rather than true degradation or leaching. Additional evidence from controlled processing studies further demonstrates that thermal treatment primarily induces structural transformation rather than true degradation of EAs. For example, a controlled rye bread baking study showed that total EA concentrations remained largely stable during baking, with apparent reductions in the crust mainly attributable to moisture redistribution and epimerization rather than chemical breakdown (Bryła et al. 2019). Similarly, earlier work investigating baking and simulated gastrointestinal digestion reported significant conversion of biologically active R‐epimers into their corresponding S‐epimers under thermal and acidic conditions, while the overall mass balance of total alkaloids remained largely unchanged (Merkel et al. 2012). These findings confirm that processing can substantially alter epimeric composition without necessarily reducing total EA burden, reinforcing the importance of quantifying both epimeric forms in exposure assessments and regulatory monitoring.

Older chemical and physical detoxification approaches, such as chlorine gas, dry heat (≥ 150°C), and ultraviolet (UV) exposure, have shown reductions in EA levels under laboratory conditions (Young et al. 1981). For instance, chlorine gas at 0.032% and high‐heat treatments (150°C–200°C) achieved reductions of up to 90% within 4 h, whereas UV exposure was largely ineffective, even after 54 h. While these approaches are unlikely to be adopted in food production due to safety, practicality, or quality concerns, they provide valuable insights into the stability of EAs and their resistance to degradation within sclerotia.

Among emerging technologies, CAPP presents high potential for mycotoxin reduction and decontamination (Doddabematti Prakash et al. 2023; Harikrishna et al. 2023; Hojnik et al. 2017; Murtaza et al. 2024; Siciliano et al. 2016). Although not yet tested directly on EAs, CAPP has achieved up to 99% reduction of aflatoxins, ochratoxins, and deoxynivalenol in cereal‐based matrices within 1–2 min of treatment (Hojnik et al. 2017; ten Bosch et al. 2017). Plasma jets and dielectric barrier discharge systems operating at room temperature have degraded 70%–90% of mycotoxins in dried nuts and grains (Ranjan et al. 2023; Siciliano et al. 2016). These mechanisms, driven by reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS/RNS) and UV photons, could oxidize or cleave toxic peptide moieties in EAs, which structurally resemble aflatoxins and cyclic peptides. Despite its promise, the application of cold plasma for EAs remains an unexplored research frontier. Parameters such as gas composition, humidity, power input, and matrix properties significantly impact degradation outcomes. Furthermore, plasma‐induced degradation products must be identified and assessed for safety. Existing studies provide a mechanistic foundation for other mycotoxins, but systematic evaluations for EAs, including degradation kinetics, toxicological safety, and scalability, are still lacking and should be prioritized in future research (Doddabematti Prakash et al. 2023; Gavahian and Cullen 2020; Hojnik et al. 2017; Murtaza et al. 2024). Importantly, none of the referenced studies systematically evaluated whether breakdown or transformation products of EAs are more or less hazardous, nor did they assess the nutritional impact of these interventions on cereals or feed. This represents a major gap compared with research on other mycotoxins, such as DON or aflatoxins, where nutritional and toxicological endpoints are more commonly studied.

Despite comprehensive surveys of the free epimeric forms of EAs, a limitation in current exposure assessments is the lack of data on potential conjugated or non‐extractable forms. For example, in a methodological study on cleavage of ergopeptines, the hydrazinolysis method using dihydroergocristine achieved a quantitative yield (≈ 100%) within 40–60 min. In contrast, standard extraction without cleavage left significant unaccounted material (Kuner et al. 2021). Furthermore, Crews (2015) reported recovery rates for several ergopeptines in cereals of only ∼60‐70% under standard QuEChERS extraction, indicating that ∼30%–40% may remain unrecovered under routine workflows. EFSA (2012) indicated that existing monitoring likely underestimates total EA burden because methods do not include matrix‐bound or modified species. To date, no structurally characterized glucosylated, sulfated, or glucuronidated EA derivatives have been reported in cereal matrices, and the review literature emphasizes that the biosynthetic and analytical exploration of such conjugates is lacking (Chen et al. 2017; Guerre 2015). Moreover, although in vitro cleavage studies reveal additional “hidden” ergopeptine signal, there are no published human or animal digestion studies demonstrating that conjugated EA forms are hydrolyzed in vivo to release free alkaloids and increase bioavailability. As a result, the magnitude and dietary relevance of such hidden EA pools remain unknown, representing a research gap that must be addressed before assessing the full effectiveness of downstream processing interventions and other risk mitigation strategies.

EAs are potent mycotoxins with vasoconstrictive and neuroactive properties that underlie their adverse health effects in humans and animals (Table 2). Historically, acute poisoning led to “ergotism,” manifesting as convulsive symptoms (hallucinations, spasms, seizures) and gangrenous outcomes (ischemia, necrosis, amputation). Although large‐scale outbreaks are now rare, this history illustrates the strong pharmacological activity of EAs through dopaminergic, serotonergic, and adrenergic receptor pathways. Contemporary risk assessments have shifted from case descriptions to the definition of health‐based guidance values (HBGVs). EFSA (2012) conducted a comprehensive review of available animal and human data and established a group acute reference dose (ARfD) of 1 µg/kg body weight (bw) and a tolerable daily intake (TDI) of 0.6 µg/kg bw/day for the sum of the main EAs, including R‐ and S‐epimers, assuming equal potency due to limited comparative toxicology data (EFSA 2012). Recent studies indicate that epimeric composition may also change after ingestion. Simulated gastrointestinal digestion experiments have shown partial reconversion of S‐epimers to their biologically active R‐forms under acidic conditions, suggesting that processing‐induced shifts toward less active epimers may not fully persist during digestion (Bryła et al. 2019; Merkel et al. 2012). This dynamic behavior underscores the importance of accounting for epimer interconversion when assessing actual exposure and health risks associated with EAs. Building on this, the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA 2021) reassessed the toxicological evidence with a broader dataset and with a more conservative approach. Using vascular and neurotoxic endpoints judged most relevant, JECFA established lower group values, for instance ARfD = 0.4 µg/kg bw and TDI = 0.4 µg/kg bw/day (WHO/JECFA 2021). These values represent the current global benchmark for acute and chronic human exposure. For livestock, EFSA's most recent re‐evaluation highlights pigs and cattle as particularly sensitive, with effects including reduced feed intake, reproductive dysfunction, impaired thermoregulation, and necrosis of extremities (Liegl and McGrath 2016; Senatra et al. 2025). Even subclinical exposures can affect productivity, underscoring the importance of managing feed contamination (EFSA 2012). From a food and feed safety perspective, consensus among expert bodies remains that rigorous monitoring and adherence to JECFA and EFSA HBGVs are essential to protect consumers and livestock.

The regulation of EAs in grains varies widely across regions, reflecting differences in risk perception, technical capacity, and food safety priorities (Table 3). Among all jurisdictions, the EU maintains the most comprehensive framework, with Regulation (EU) 2023/915 and its amendment 2024/1808 setting maximum limits for both ergot sclerotia and total EAs (the sum of 12 EAs including the major ‐ines; ergometrine, ergotamine, ergosine, ergocryptine, ergocristine, ergotamine and their epimers) in raw and processed cereals (EU 2024). Specifically, the EU has established a maximum level of 0.05% by mass (0.5 g/kg) for ergot sclerotia in unprocessed rye, with a planned reduction to 0.02% (0.2 g/kg) originally scheduled for July 2024 but subsequently postponed to July 1, 2025, under Commission Regulation (EU) 2024/1808 (EU 2024). For processed wheat, rye, barley, oats, and spelt milling products, the maximum level of total EAs is currently 100 µg/kg, which will be further reduced to 50 µg/kg by 2028, reflecting a precautionary approach to consumer protection (EU 2024). In addition to setting maximum levels, the European Union has also established minimum analytical performance criteria for EA determination. Under Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/2782, LOQs for individual EAs should not exceed 4 µg/kg in cereals and cereal‐based products and 2 µg/kg in infant foods. These performance requirements aim to ensure sufficient analytical sensitivity for regulatory enforcement (EU 2023). In the United States, the FDA has established action levels for ergot sclerotia in raw grains, including 0.05% by mass for wheat and 0.3% for rye, but has not set limits for total EA content (Berraies et al. 2024; FDA 2024). In Canada, the CGC sets grading standards based on ergot sclerotia content, with a limit of 0.04% (m/m) in wheat to qualify for the highest grade. However, the CGC does not establish toxicological maximum levels for EAs; such regulatory limits would fall under the mandate of Health Canada, which has not yet set EA‐specific thresholds (CGC 2024). Australia and New Zealand have established a 0.05% sclerotia limit, but, as in Canada, lack EA regulations for flours or cereals (FSANZ 2024). Globally, there is no Codex standard for EAs, although discussions are ongoing to harmonize international guidance (Codex Alimentarius 2024). However, whether regulatory efforts should move beyond sclerotia grading is not straightforward. Although physical cleaning and sorting remove a substantial proportion of ergot bodies before processing, not all regions have the analytical infrastructure (i.e., LC–MS) required to quantify total EAs, and visual inspection routinely remains the only feasible option in many grain‐handling environments. Moreover, the interconversion of ‐ine and ‐inine epimers due to the external factors (i.e., temperature, light) complicates the translation of raw grain measurements to processed food safety. Therefore, establishing maximum limits for EA will require balancing scientific accuracy with practical feasibility, economic and food security considerations, and the varied analytical capacities across global grain supply chains.

Sampling strategies for EA analysis in agricultural products, particularly in grain consignments, have faced challenges due to the highly heterogeneous distribution of EAs (Grusie et al. 2017) (Table 4 and Figure 4). Unlike other mycotoxins, EAs are primarily concentrated in ergot sclerotia, which are irregularly distributed throughout grain lots (Tittlemier, Cramer, et al. 2025). This variability in distribution, which can vary dramatically from one part of a consignment to another, complicates the ability to obtain representative samples. While sampling strategies for other mycotoxins, such as DON, have evolved, those for EA analysis require further refinement. Current approaches have improved representativeness but remain limited by low sampling frequency, inconsistent comminution practices, and the highly localized distribution of sclerotia, which together reduce reliability in exposure estimates. Targeted sampling remains underdeveloped, even though ergot contamination is highly localized in “hotspots” within fields or storage lots (Jacobson et al. 2024). Sampling frequency is often too low to capture temporal and spatial variability, particularly in regions with changing climate patterns that alter infection pressure. Homogenization practices are inconsistent, which is problematic given that EAs are concentrated in sclerotia fragments; insufficient grinding or mixing leads to large variance and biased subsamples (Jacobson et al. 2024; Tittlemier, Cramer, et al. 2025). Addressing these gaps is essential to reducing sampling error, ensuring accurate exposure estimates, and improving the reliability of downstream analytical results (Jacobson et al. 2024; Tittlemier, Cramer, et al. 2025). Composite sampling, wherein small increments are collected from different parts of a consignment and pooled into a single sample, remains the most commonly used method in regulatory and industry settings. While this approach increases coverage, it is challenged by the highly heterogeneous distribution of ergot sclerotia and alkaloids, which can lead to substantial sampling error if contamination is uneven. For example, Tittlemier, Blagden, et al. (2025) reported EA concentrations ranging from 0.009 to 0.486 mg/kg across different wheat consignments, demonstrating that while composite sampling improves coverage in heterogeneous lots, it may still fail to capture localized clusters of sclerotia, leading to substantial underestimation or overestimation of EA concentrations. In one instance, a small, localized cluster of ergot sclerotia was found to elevate the alkaloid concentration in that location by more than an order of magnitude compared to the rest of the sample, yet such a cluster might easily be missed by random increment sampling.

(1) Ergot alkaloids occur in discrete Claviceps sclerotia, resulting in constitutional heterogeneity and spatially clustered (distributional) contamination within cereal lots; (2) improper or biased sampling creates unequal probability of selecting sclerotia, leading to nonrepresentative estimates of contamination; (3) increasing the number of randomly distributed increments improves spatial coverage and averages heterogeneity within the sampling target; (4) adequate particle size reduction before subsampling reduces distributional heterogeneity, whereas poor comminution can negate gains from primary sampling; (5) representative laboratory samples require formation of an aggregate sample and appropriate subsampling tools (e.g., rotary riffle dividers), while coning and quartering is not recommended; and (6) sampling and sample preparation contribute more to total measurement uncertainty than analytical determination, emphasizing their critical role in ergot alkaloid assessment. This concern is further validated by Bourgeois and Lyman (2012), who applied Monte Carlo simulations to model uncertainty in mycotoxin sampling. Their findings, based on ochratoxin A (OTA) distribution in grain, demonstrated that even at low contamination levels, the probability of false negatives could remain high unless sampling frequency was increased and the increment mass was optimized. Although OTA and EAs differ in origin and toxicology, the underlying issue of spatial variability in contamination is similar. In their simulations, they showed that increasing the number of increments while reducing their mass reduced the overall sampling variance. This approach, though logistically challenging in large‐scale grain handling, may also apply to EA sampling where variability is extreme.

Studies such as those by Raamsdonk and van der Voet (2022) further emphasize that measurement uncertainty is strongly influenced by both the number of increments collected and the aggregate weight of the pooled sample prepared from them. Their work demonstrated that the relative standard deviation (RSD) in visual detection of ergot sclerotia can exceed 100% up to 173% under reproducibility conditions, particularly when contamination levels are very low, and only a few sclerotia are present in the inspected sample. The implications of such high uncertainty are significant in regulatory contexts, where a consignment might falsely pass or fail compliance checks based solely on how well the contaminated areas were represented in the sample. Their results suggest that increasing the number of subsamples, even doubling it, can considerably reduce the false‐negative rate, particularly at contamination levels near regulatory limits.

Improving the representativeness of samples also requires effective comminution and processing techniques. Comminution and representative subsampling of the bulk sample are strongly recommended for EA analysis, as their clustered distribution, driven by discrete sclerotia, can lead to extreme measurement variability when subsampling is applied to insufficiently processed material (e.g., > 100% RSD reported under low contamination scenarios) (Krska and Crews 2007). Bosman et al. (2024) demonstrated the impact of grinding methods on the detection of DON in wheat. Using a 3D‐printed portable device, they showed that coarse grinding could reduce extraction efficiency, leading to underestimated contamination levels. For EAs, which are concentrated in discrete sclerotia, insufficient grinding mainly increases variance, as fragments are unevenly distributed among test portions; however, large intact sclerotia may also introduce systematic bias if they are excluded from subsamples (Whitaker 2006). The findings from Champeil et al. (2004) supported this argument, showing that flour‐flour protocols (where replicate analyses are performed on subsamples taken after the bulk grain has been ground into flour) produced more consistent mycotoxin measurements than grain‐grain protocols (where replicate subsamples are taken directly from whole grain before grinding), with the former yielding lower standard deviations across repeated analyses.

Laboratory‐scale homogenization strategies have also been explored to reduce subsampling variability in heterogeneous mycotoxin systems. For example, slurry‐based homogenization approaches that add water before subsampling have been investigated in highly heterogeneous contamination scenarios. A recent study evaluating aflatoxin distribution in maize demonstrated that slurry mixing significantly reduced analytical variability compared with conventional dry mixing by improving apparent sample uniformity and reducing subsampling error associated with localized toxin particles (Kumphanda et al. 2019). Although EAs differ in physicochemical properties from aflatoxins, both contamination systems share a key characteristic: toxins are concentrated within discrete particulate sources that are unevenly distributed within bulk grain. Consequently, improved homogenization approaches that enhance the uniform dispersion of contaminated particles during laboratory subsampling may help reduce analytical variability in EA measurements. However, the applicability of slurry‐based homogenization for EA analysis has not yet been systematically evaluated, representing an important area for future methodological research. However, such approaches primarily enhance analytical homogeneity rather than addressing the underlying spatial heterogeneity of contaminants within grain consignments and therefore cannot substitute for representative increment sampling and appropriate comminution practices.

In addition to random sampling, which is designed to generate representative mean concentrations across a lot, targeted sampling approaches are used when the objective is to identify localized “hotspots” of contamination. Sampling approaches must be tailored to the study objective. For regulatory compliance and exposure assessment, strategies aim to minimize variance and generate representative mean concentrations. However, when the goal is to understand field epidemiology or to identify localized “hotspots,” different approaches are required. Yi et al. (2021) found that in field conditions, mycotoxin contamination, specifically aflatoxin B1 and deoxynivalenol, was significantly higher in peripheral and upwind regions of fields. These areas were more exposed to windborne fungal spores and higher humidity, conditions conducive to fungal growth. Translating this to EAs, field‐level sampling that accounts for factors such as wind direction, topography, and moisture‐prone areas could improve hotspot detection. These results suggest that sampling intensity should be adjusted to environmental risk zones rather than distributed uniformly across a field or storage lot (Yi et al. 2021).

Geospatial modeling can further enhance hotspot‐focused sampling. Castano‐Duque et al. (2023) utilized machine learning algorithms to predict aflatoxin and fumonisin risks in corn based on spatial variables such as temperature, precipitation, and soil properties. Their models achieved high predictive accuracy, and similar tools could be adapted to predict EA risks in cereals. Integrating such predictive systems with sampling protocols would allow efforts to be concentrated where risk is highest, reducing the chance of missing localized contamination, particularly during harvest or grain loading when rapid sampling decisions are required. Real‐time monitoring during storage and transport offers another avenue to refine sampling. Sensors measuring moisture, temperature, and airflow can be integrated with AI‐driven analytics to dynamically flag risk zones. For instance, elevated humidity detected in a silo section could trigger intensified sampling in that zone. While EAs are produced in the field rather than during storage, high moisture can promote secondary fungal growth. It may influence the stability or redistribution of alkaloids from fragmented sclerotia. Incorporating humidity‐driven adaptive sampling during storage could therefore help identify sections at greater risk of elevated alkaloid residues, ensuring that analytical resources are focused where potential exposure is most likely (Cherewyk et al. 2023; Schummer et al. 2020). Such adaptive strategies would not only improve detection accuracy but also focus analytical effort where it is most needed.

Alternative methods, such as dust sampling, have been explored to capture mycotoxin contamination more efficiently. Ciasca et al. (2022) showed that dust samples collected during grain unloading contained significantly higher concentrations of DON compared to the corresponding grain samples. The DON concentration in dust was over 100 times higher than in the bulk grain, suggesting that outer layers of kernels or broken grain particles are more prone to mycotoxin accumulation. While this method has not yet been fully validated for EAs, its success with Fusarium toxins suggests potential application. Given that ergot sclerotia are brittle and may fragment during handling, fine particles can transfer alkaloids onto surrounding kernels and into the dust fraction. This suggests that dust collected during grain handling could contain concentrated alkaloids and may represent a useful matrix for rapid screening (Franzmann et al. 2011; Tittlemier et al. 2015), although dedicated studies are needed to confirm this potential.

Therefore, the current landscape of sampling for EA measurement is marked by progress but still faces substantial challenges. Composite sampling remains foundational, but it can be strengthened by incorporating more targeted, frequent, and informed approaches to account for spatial variability. Incorporating grinding and homogenization protocols that ensure sample consistency, leveraging geospatial and AI‐based predictive models, and adopting real‐time monitoring and alternative sampling matrices, such as dust, all represent promising avenues for improvement. Future research should continue to refine these strategies, develop standardized protocols for field‐to‐laboratory transitions, and validate novel methods across various grain types and processing environments to support more accurate food safety assessments and regulatory enforcement. Recent advancements in MS/MS have significantly enhanced the analytical capabilities for detecting EAs in food and feed matrices (Figure 5). These improvements have focused on both high detection capability (sensitivity) and specificity, incorporating ultra‐ and high‐performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC/HPLC) coupled with triple quadrupole or HRMS (QqQ or HRMS) systems. Most studies now use multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) to selectively target parent‐to‐product ion transitions, allowing for accurate quantification of structurally similar EAs, including their biologically relevant epimers. A major methodological advancement was achieved by Krska et al. (2008), who first developed an LC–MS/MS method capable of simultaneously quantifying the R‐ and S‐epimers of EAs in cereals. This is critical, as S‐epimers have traditionally been underreported despite growing evidence of their bioactivity. Another notable advancement was the use of deuterated lysergic acid diethylamide as an internal standard, which was particularly novel, correcting for losses during sample prep and enabling precise quantification at low levels, down to 0.00893 µg/kg for ergocristinine (Cherewyk, Grusie‐Ogilvie, Blakley, et al. 2022). This detection capability allowed the study to show that S‐epimers made up 35% of total alkaloid content in naturally contaminated wheat, emphasizing the necessity of including both epimers in routine monitoring.

Analytical platforms applied to ergot alkaloid determination range from rapid field screening to high‐resolution confirmatory analysis. Screening assays support on‐site triage but cannot resolve epimers or quantify individual alkaloids. Fluorescence or ultraviolet detectors provide estimates of total ergot alkaloid content. Triple‐quadrupole liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry enables routine quantification across food and feed matrices, while high‐resolution and ion mobility‐mass spectrometry provide the structural resolution required for epimer differentiation and surveillance of emerging ergot alkaloid analogues.

Other studies, such as Carbonell‐Rozas, Gámiz‐Gracia, et al. (2021), focused on optimizing extraction and clean‐up methods. Their work on oat‐based functional foods employed a reduced‐volume QuEChERS protocol combined with C18/Z‐Sep+ dispersive solid‐phase extraction (SPE), achieving recoveries between 89.7% and 109% and minimal matrix effects (< 20%). Their method proved robust for both major alkaloids and epimers, demonstrating its applicability to complex processed matrices such as food supplements. Carbonell‐Rozas et al. (2022) incorporated travelling wave ion mobility spectrometry (TWIMS) into LC–MS/MS to improve the selectivity of EA analysis. By measuring collision cross‐section (CCS) values, this method separated epimers such as ergosine and ergosinine (CCS difference of 3.4%) and reduced matrix interference by removing co‐eluting species from extracted ion chromatograms. This innovation represents a promising direction for overcoming the epimeric overlap that challenges traditional MS methods. Similarly, Tölgyesi et al. (2025) extended method versatility by developing a multi‐analyte LC–MS/MS method that simultaneously detects carboxyatractyloside, tropane alkaloids, and EAs in soybean feed. Their instrument‐dependent findings emphasized the importance of optimizing ionization sources and highlighted how matrix‐matched calibration is essential for quantitative accuracy, especially under strong suppression scenarios such as the 30% reduction observed in some samples (Tölgyesi et al. 2025).

Kim et al. (2022) further contributed by validating an HPLC–MS/MS method for 23 mycotoxins, including EAs, in Korean grains. Their comprehensive survey revealed matrix‐specific challenges, with ergometrine suppression reaching 67% in barley, reinforcing the need for matrix‐matched calibrations in grains. Despite such effects, the method showed excellent linearity and precision across barley, wheat, and oats, detecting contamination in nearly one‐third of the samples.

In addition to research‐driven method developments, standardized protocols have been established to support regulatory enforcement. For example, the European standard EN 17425:2021 describes an LC–MS/MS method for the determination of six major ergopeptines (ergocornine, ergometrine, ergocristine, ergotamine, ergosine, and ergocryptine) and their corresponding ‐inine epimers in cereals following dispersive SPE clean‐up. The method employs external solvent calibration and has demonstrated satisfactory performance within its validated range (approximately 13–168 µg/kg). However, validation data indicate reduced precision at lower contamination levels, and the absence of isotope‐dilution calibration means that matrix‐dependent suppression effects may not be fully compensated in complex cereal and feed matrices. Thus, while EN 17425 provides an important harmonized framework for official control, advanced quantitative applications may benefit from the inclusion of stable isotope‐labelled internal standards and matrix‐matched calibrations (CEN 2021).

Despite the progress, several limitations remain. Matrix suppression remains a critical barrier to reliable EA analysis, particularly in complex commodities such as baby food, animal feed, and high‐fibre matrices. Reported signal losses range from 44.5% in infant cereal products (Tölgyesi et al. 2023) to as high as 67% in barley (Kim et al. 2022), highlighting the extent of the problem. The impact is currently mitigated through matrix‐matched calibration, improved clean‐up strategies such as SPE, and the development of stable isotope‐labelled internal standards. Stable isotope‐labelled internal standards would be valuable for addressing compound‐ and matrix‐specific suppression effects, but currently no commercial standards exist for EAs. Expanding their availability should be a priority in future method development to compensate for compound‐ and matrix‐specific suppression effects. While certified reference materials remain limited, proficiency testing (PT) schemes provide important support for analytical quality assurance in EA monitoring. International PT programs such as FAPAS (UK) and BIPEA (France) routinely offer test materials containing naturally contaminated cereals or feeds, enabling laboratories to assess method performance, comparability, and measurement accuracy across institutions (BIPEA 2026; FAPAS 2026). However, PT samples primarily serve as interlaboratory performance evaluation tools and do not provide the traceable calibration and long‐term stability required of certified reference materials. Consequently, the continued development of well‐characterized certified reference materials remains essential for method validation and regulatory enforcement. Nevertheless, alkaloids with intrinsically poor ionization efficiency, including ergometrine and ergosine, remain challenging to quantify near regulatory thresholds (Nam et al. 2024). These findings underscore the need for further refinement of sample preparation and ionization techniques and for continued development of high‐sensitivity LC–MS/MS and HRMS platforms to ensure robust quantification across diverse food and feed matrices.

Another unresolved issue is the co‐elution of isomeric forms such as α‐ and β‐ergocryptine and their corresponding ergocryptinine epimers. Lattanzio et al. (2021) and Veršilovskis et al. (2020) both encountered this problem, noting that current chromatographic methods do not fully resolve these isomers, leading to summed reporting of concentrations that may obscure differences between isomers, which could have distinct toxicological relevance. Similar chromatographic limitations have been documented in the European Union Reference Laboratory PT reports, which highlighted incomplete separation of α‐ and β‐ergocryptine and ergocryptinine on conventional reverse‐phase columns (Oliveira et al. 2017).

To address the unavailability of internal standards for specific EAs, Herter et al. (2024) synthesized ergotamine‐13CD3 and its epimer, ergotaminine‐13CD3, through electrochemical N‐demethylation and methylation using iodomethane‐13CD3. The main advantage of stable isotope‐labelled standards is that they enable precise quantification in LC–MS/MS workflows through isotope dilution analysis. By correcting for matrix effects, they reduce variability and improve analytical accuracy. This is especially critical in regulatory settings where trace‐level quantification is required.

Recent work has significantly advanced the availability of isotope‐labelled standards by reporting the semi‐synthesis of a complete suite of stable isotope‐labelled priority EAs, including all six major ergopeptines (ergotamine, ergosine, ergocristine, ergocryptine, ergocornine, and ergometrine) and their corresponding epimers (Herter et al. 2025). The authors employed a selective N
6‐demethylation followed by isotopic remethylation strategy, producing labelled analogues that co‐eluted, exhibited identical fragmentation patterns, and showed comparable ionization efficiencies relative to native compounds in LC–MS/MS workflows. This development represents a major analytical milestone, as the availability of a full isotopic standard set enables robust isotope‐dilution quantification, improves correction for matrix‐dependent suppression, and enhances interlaboratory comparability. Importantly, it addresses a longstanding limitation in EA analysis by supporting traceable calibration and more reliable enforcement of regulatory limits.

HRMS has advanced EA analysis by enabling simultaneous multi‐mycotoxin detection, improving resolution of R/S epimers, and allowing retrospective nontargeted screening (Figure 5). These capabilities represent a significant step beyond conventional triple quadrupole MS, which was limited to targeted quantification (Liao et al. 2015; Tsagkaris et al. 2021), resolution, and the ability to perform both targeted and nontargeted screening in complex matrices. Several recent studies have demonstrated the strengths of HRMS methods, particularly those coupled with UHPLC, for the simultaneous detection and quantification of EAs and other co‐occurring mycotoxins in diverse food and feed matrices.

Liao et al. (2015) developed a UHPLC‐Q‐Orbitrap HRMS method for multi‐mycotoxin analysis in grain and nut products. The method achieved mass accuracies of < ±2.0 ppm and reported LOQs ranging from 0.02 to 11.6 µg/kg depending on matrix and analyte, confirming the platform's analytical detection capability (sensitivity). While the matrix‐matched calibration and extraction procedure were critical for achieving the excellent recoveries (91%–99%) and low RSDs (RSD < 15%) across six matrices, the strength of the HRMS approach lay in its ability to combine high mass resolution with full‐scan data acquisition. Similarly, Uhlig et al. (2021) applied LC–HRMS/MS to explore the EA and indole diterpenoid metabolomes of C. purpurea, and the method was able to separate major and minor EAs, including R/S epimers such as ergocristine and ergocristinine, demonstrating the potential of HRMS for comprehensive alkaloid profiling rather than routine quantification.

Tsagkaris et al. (2021) presented a UHPLC‐HRMS method validated for 19 mycotoxins, including EAs, in wheat, maize, and rye flour. This method was notable for its low limits of quantification (LOQs as low as 0.5 µg/kg for EAs), rapid analysis time (under 7 min), and high recovery (68%–104%). The use of parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) in both ionization modes further enhanced its throughput. Despite not using isotopically labelled internal standards due to cost considerations, matrix‐matched calibration curves helped mitigate matrix effects. Bessaire et al. (2021) extended the HRMS application to a multi‐residue workflow analyzing 156 contaminants, including EAs, in plant‐based foods. The method relied on a QuEChERS‐based extraction and Q‐Exactive HF‐X HRMS with mass accuracy < 5 ppm. Their approach resolved isomeric EAs using optimized mobile phase pH conditions and reported LOQs as low as 10 µg/kg. Although matrix effects were generally below 20%, co‐elution of certain isomers, such as ergocristine and ergocristinine, persisted.

Nam et al. (2024) focused on developing an HRMS‐Orbitrap method that quantified total EAs via transformation into lysergic acid amide (LSA) for simplified analysis. Their approach used data‐dependent acquisition (DDA) to capture both targeted and untargeted ions. It validated their method for wheat flour with high recovery (> 90%) and LOQs as low as 1.3 µg/kg. SPE minimized matrix effects. At the same time, the key challenge lay not in epimer separation, which is circumvented by the transformation step, but in ensuring complete and reproducible conversion of diverse EAs into LSA without degradation or side‐product formation, but lacked the EA‐specific data.

León et al. (2016) introduced an Orbitrap‐based HRMS method for analyzing 77 target and 425 untargeted compounds, including EAs, in animal feed. This method achieved high recovery (80%–120%) and matrix compensation via matrix‐matched calibration. While the method facilitated retrospective analysis and identified multiple contaminants, the epimer separation challenge remained unresolved. Sivagnanam et al. (2016) and Dopstadt et al. (2017) applied matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization time‐of‐flight (MALDI‐TOF MS) and matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry Imaging (MALDI‐MSI), respectively, for EA profiling and spatial mapping. While MALDI‐TOF is a form of HRMS, these methods are not field‐deployable or high‐throughput; their main value lies in research applications where direct profiling and tissue localization are needed.

Despite these advancements, several analytical challenges persist across the literature. A major limitation is the persistent issue of matrix effects, particularly in complex food and feed samples. While matrix‐matched calibration and SPE have improved accuracy, complete mitigation of matrix interference remains difficult (León et al. 2016; Nam et al. 2024). Signal suppression or enhancement due to co‐extracted compounds continues to impact quantification, particularly for EAs with low ionization efficiency, such as ergometrine or ergosine. Epimeric separation remains a significant challenge in EA analysis. Because R‐ and S‐epimers (e.g., ergocristine/ergocristinine) are isobaric and share identical fragmentation patterns, HRMS cannot resolve them on mass accuracy alone. Their separation depends on chromatographic conditions (e.g., stationary phase, mobile phase pH, and temperature), and even when coupled to HRMS, complete resolution is often difficult (Liao et al. 2015; Uhlig et al. 2021). Even with pH‐modified mobile phases, co‐elution remains a concern. In addition, the quantification of minor alkaloids and conjugated mycotoxins is underexplored. Many methods focus on major EAs, often neglecting minor or conjugated forms, which could contribute significantly to total toxicity. The use of isotopically labelled standards and improved extraction techniques, such as supercritical fluid extraction (SFE), may help improve accuracy and reduce underreporting (Bessaire et al. 2021; Tsagkaris et al. 2021).

Finally, most current methods are tailored for targeted analysis. While full‐scan HRMS allows for retrospective screening, proactive identification of emerging or unregulated mycotoxins requires expanded spectral libraries and improved nontargeted workflows (León et al. 2016). Enhancing database coverage and developing more robust data‐mining tools will be crucial to future‐proofing these methods for regulatory and food safety applications. Although LC–MS/MS and LC‐HRMS are widely regarded as the gold standards for EA analysis due to their detection capability (sensitivity), specificity, and ability to handle complex matrices, they come with substantial barriers to routine adoption. These include specialized laboratories with controlled ventilation and temperature, highly trained operators, significant capital investment in instrumentation, and ongoing costs for consumables such as solvents and high‐purity gases. In this context, the continued development of simpler, lower‐cost, and rapid screening tools remains essential to complement high‐end mass spectrometry, particularly for routine monitoring, field applications, or use in resource‐limited settings.

Although LC–MS/MS is widely considered the reference technique for EA determination, earlier HPLC‐based methods remain relevant in certain analytical settings. Fluorescence‐based HPLC approaches have demonstrated reliable quantification of major EAs in cereals and feed, particularly when combined with selective extraction and solid‐phase clean‐up (Kowalczyk et al. 2016; Schummer et al. 2018). For example, Müller et al. (2006) developed an HPLC‐FLD method capable of simultaneously detecting twelve EAs, including both ‐ine and ‐inine forms, in rye and rye flour, achieving detection limits below 1 µg/kg and recoveries near 90%–101%. Similarly, Ruhland and Tischler (2008) reported a robust HPLC‐FLD method for five major alkaloids in feed matrices, demonstrating recoveries of 82%–120% and detection limits of approximately 5–10 µg/kg, with contamination detected in over 90% of surveyed feed samples. Kowalczyk and Kwiatek (2023) validated an HPLC with fluorescence detection (HPLC‐FLD) method for compound feeds, achieving LODs of 0.8–2.6 µg/kg with recoveries of 85.2%–117.8%. Although fluorescence detection lacks the selectivity of MS, the method's simplicity, robustness, and regulatory alignment make it viable for routine feed screening. These methods offer lower operational costs and simpler instrumentation requirements, making them suitable for routine monitoring in resource‐limited laboratories (Pleadin et al. 2022; Krska and Crews 2008). These studies highlight that HPLC methods can provide reliable screening capability with simpler instrumentation requirements. However, their analytical selectivity is limited compared with LC–MS/MS, particularly for resolving co‐eluting epimers and distinguishing structurally similar alkaloids. Furthermore, HPLC methods generally exhibit higher limits of detection and greater susceptibility to matrix interferences, restricting their applicability for trace‐level regulatory enforcement. Nevertheless, they continue to provide a practical screening tool in regions where access to advanced instrumentation and specialized analytical expertise remains limited.

Several studies focused on portable or rapid screening alternatives to laboratory‐based methods. Jakob et al. (2024) developed the 2LabsToGo system, combining high‐performance thin‐layer chromatography with fluorescence detection for on‐site EA analysis in rye, achieving LOQs of 180 µg/kg and repeatability of 4.1%. While cost‐effective and user‐friendly, its limitation is its focus on ergocristine as a representative compound, which limits its applicability to full‐spectrum EA monitoring.

Shi et al. (2019) investigated the use of near‐infrared (NIR, 680–2500 nm) and Fourier‐transform mid‐infrared (FT‐MIR, 4000–700 cm−1) spectroscopy for detecting EAs in naturally contaminated barley. Their study showed that these techniques are nondestructive and suitable for rapid bulk grain screening, but they do not directly measure EAs. Instead, classification models rely on detecting spectral changes caused by the presence of ergot sclerotia and associated alterations in grain composition, rather than the alkaloids themselves. As a result, calibration models were weak (R
2 < 0.6), with especially poor performance in samples containing low EA levels, making them unsuitable for regulatory monitoring (Shi et al. 2019). A broader review by Freitag et al. (2022) confirmed that infrared spectroscopy applied to mycotoxins, including EAs, largely reflects fungal or sclerotia‐induced matrix changes and cannot resolve individual alkaloids or epimers. Thus, while promising for rapid, high‐throughput pre‐screening of ergot contamination, NIR and FT‐MIR cannot replace LC–MS/MS or HRMS for definitive EA quantification (Freitag et al. 2022).

Immunoassay‐based techniques have been explored for EA monitoring as cost‐effective, high‐throughput alternatives to chromatographic and mass spectrometric methods. A range of formats has been reported, from early radioimmunoassays to modern enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs), but only one ELISA kit has already been marketed for screening cereals, feed, and plant materials (Randox 2025). Despite this utility, limitations persist in terms of specificity, matrix interferences, quantification accuracy, and incomplete coverage of structurally diverse or trace‐level EAs (Boško et al. 2024; Kodisch et al. 2020).

The earliest radioimmunoassay for EAs was developed by Kleimola (1978), employing antisera raised against a lysergic acid–human serum albumin conjugate. The method achieved detection limits of 0.5 ng/mL for methylergometrine and 2.2 ng/mL for dihydroergotoxine, with recoveries from biological matrices ranging between 59% and 79%. Although the method exhibited acceptable detection capability (sensitivity), it also showed broad cross‐reactivity among EAs. This feature can be advantageous for rapid screening of overall occurrence, as demonstrated in subsequent immunoassay developments, but the inability to accurately quantify individual alkaloids or epimers remains a major drawback for toxicological evaluation, regulatory compliance, and monitoring shifts in EA profiles (Kodisch et al. 2020; Versilovskis et al. 2019).

The shift toward ELISAs allowed for non‐radioactive, safer, and more practical immunoanalytical approaches. For instance, Gross et al. (2018) developed a competitive ELISA for total EAs in cereal flours and bread, using a polyclonal antibody raised against ergometrine conjugated to keyhole limpet hemocyanin. The assay achieved extract‐based LODs of 20 ng/g in flour and 14 ng/g in bread, with recovery rates of 85%–110%. The method exhibited broad cross‐reactivity among major EAs and their epimers, making it suitable for screening total EA contamination but not for quantifying individual alkaloids. While the assay provided broad‐spectrum detection and correlated well with HPLC‐FLD (r
2 = 0.981), its inability to differentiate among alkaloids when sample compositions deviated from calibration standards introduced uncertainty, particularly in regulatory contexts where individual alkaloid identification is required.

Further specificity was achieved by Molloy et al. (2003), who developed monoclonal and polyclonal ELISAs for dihydroergosine (DHES), the dominant alkaloid in Claviceps africana. The monoclonal assay showed high specificity, with negligible cross‐reactivity to related alkaloids at concentrations below approximately 100 mg/kg in grain equivalents. The limit of detection was 0.01 mg/kg, and recovery rates for spiked grain and feed samples ranged from 77% to 103%. Despite the high accuracy of DHES, the assay's narrow selectivity meant it could miss other alkaloids present in contaminated samples, underscoring a common trade‐off in immunoassay design: enhanced specificity at the expense of broader coverage.

Schnitzius et al. (2001) developed an ELISA targeting the lysergic acid moiety shared by many EAs. The monoclonal antibody showed 50% binding efficiency at extract concentrations equivalent to approximately 2600 µg/kg (ergonovine) and 214 µg/kg (lysergic acid) in plant tissue, but binding was poor for more structurally complex ergopeptides such as ergocristine, ergocornine, and ergotamine. Intra‐run coefficient of variation (CV) averaged 10.2% in straw and 13.3% in seed samples, while inter‐run CVs were substantially higher (up to 24.5%). The ELISA produced significantly higher total EA concentrations than HPLC measurements for ergovaline, with ELISA/HPLC ratios ranging from 0.62 to 8.66 in straw, reflecting overestimation due to nonspecific binding. These inconsistencies highlight the limitations of relying solely on ELISAs for quantitative analysis in complex feed and forage matrices (Schnitzius et al. 2001).

Bauer et al. (2018) demonstrated the use of polyclonal antibody‐based EIAs for rapid screening of ergometrine equivalents in grass seeds and plants. In perennial ryegrass seeds, EAs were detected at concentrations up to 1800 µg/kg. While the assay's detection limit of 3.4 µg/kg in seeds and fresh grass made it highly sensitive, the values had to be expressed as ergometrine equivalents due to cross‐reactivity and lack of specificity for individual alkaloids. Recovery rates ranged from 60% to 68% at low spike levels (25 µg/kg), suitable for rapid screening but limited in specific and accurate quantification. More recently, Höfs et al. (2023) developed a magnetic bead‐based immunoassay with amperometric detection that enabled rapid ergometrine screening (LOD ∼200 µg/kg matrix) within 45 s, offering a portable and more sustainable approach. However, its 37%–51% recovery and single‐analyte focus suggest limited reliability for broader EA screening unless further refined. Kodisch et al. (2020) demonstrated the shortcomings of ELISA for EA quantification, reporting a 43‐fold overestimation compared to HPLC due to cross‐reactivity and matrix interference.

Despite considerable advances, key research gaps remain in immunoassays for EAs. One of the most persistent challenges is achieving a balance between broad cross‐reactivity, which is useful for total EA screening, and the specificity needed to reliably quantify individual alkaloids or epimers (Boško et al. 2024). Most polyclonal‐based assays detect a wide range of alkaloids but cannot differentiate between epimers or closely related analogs. Conversely, monoclonal assays provide high specificity but fail to recognize the full spectrum of EAs present in contaminated samples. Furthermore, immunoassays continue to be influenced by matrix effects that can suppress or enhance signals, complicating quantification. These effects are especially pronounced in high‐fat, high‐protein, or high‐fiber matrices such as whole grains, bread, or animal feed. In practice, organic solvents such as methanol or acetonitrile generally extract EAs more efficiently but must be diluted or neutralized to avoid antibody denaturation, whereas buffer‐based extractions are more immunoassay‐compatible but often recover lower amounts of alkaloids.

Commercially, only a subset of EAs and their epimers, ergometrine, ergotamine, ergosine, ergocristine, ergocornine, and α‐ergocryptine, are available in pure form (Biocrick 2025). Critically, β‐ergocryptine and β‐ergocryptinine remain unavailable from suppliers, forcing their estimation via α‐epimers in official methods (EURL 2020). Certified matrix reference materials for EAs are very limited. The sole published example is a rye‐based matrix material formerly produced by Biopure Standards GmbH (BRM 003020) (Crews 2015), though its current availability and certification status are not clearly documented. More recent work has instead relied on naturally contaminated rye materials for validation (Kuner et al. 2021, 2023, 2024).

This restricted availability complicates method validation, interlaboratory comparisons, and regulatory adoption, particularly for rarer or emerging EA analogs. This hinders accurate calibration and comparability across labs and makes the development of more refined immunoassays difficult (Boško et al. 2024; Kodisch et al. 2020). To improve the future applicability of immunoassays for EA monitoring, advancements in antibody engineering (e.g., recombinant antibodies or nanobodies) are essential. These innovations could offer tailored binding specificity, improved stability, and scalability. In addition, integrating immunoassays with confirmatory techniques, such as immunoaffinity chromatography followed by LC–MS/MS, may offer a hybrid solution that combines the speed of screening with the precision of targeted analysis. Finally, developing multiplex immunoassays that can simultaneously quantify several alkaloids with minimal cross‐reactivity would represent a significant step forward, as they could provide faster, lower‐cost, and more comparable screening data for regulatory surveillance. Such tools would enable high‐throughput monitoring of cereals and feeds, improve alignment with regulatory requirements for compound or group‐level limits, and reserve LC–MS/MS confirmation for only the most critical cases.

Aptamer‐based sensing technologies, conceptualized initially as synthetic analogues to antibodies, have demonstrated utility in the detection of various mycotoxins (Raza et al. 2025). However, their use in EA analysis remains at a developmental stage. Compared to the advancements observed for mycotoxins such as aflatoxins, ochratoxins, T‐2 mycotoxin, and fumonisins, the aptasensor field for EAs has been underdeveloped, with only two publications (Rouah‐Martin et al. 2012, Rouah et al. 2014) addressing this application to date. These early aptamer studies targeting lysergamine and metergoline demonstrated the feasibility of developing DNA aptamers against the ergoline scaffold (Flieger et al. 1997; Krska and Crews 2008). While scientifically valuable, their direct relevance to the current regulatory landscape is limited, as the EU maximum levels (Regulation 2023/915) apply to the sum of 12 specific ergopeptines and their epimers in cereals and cereal‐based foods. Since lysergamine and metergoline differ structurally from these dominant compounds, aptamers selected against them are unlikely to provide the specificity required for regulatory compliance testing. Future aptamer development should focus on ergopeptine‐specific targets to align with enforcement needs.

These early studies highlighted key limitations, including restricted binding to smaller alkaloids, incomplete coverage of major ergopeptines, and dependence on chromatographic confirmation for quantification. Consequently, current aptamer tools lack the specificity, analytical range, and validation required for routine monitoring applications. In contrast, aptasensor technologies for other mycotoxins have advanced significantly, achieving low detection limits and enabling real‐time and multiplex detection. The limited progress in EA aptasensing reflects both the structural complexity of EAs and the widespread adoption of LC–MS/MS as the regulatory reference method, which has reduced the incentive for rapid‐test development.

The most pressing gap in current research is the absence of aptamers with high affinity and broad specificity for the full spectrum of EAs, especially the six major ergopeptines and their corresponding epimers, which are the targets of regulatory limits in the EU (set at 100–500 µg/kg depending on the commodity). The development of such aptamers would likely require a shift in (Systematic Evolution of Ligands by EXponential Enrichment) SELEX methodology to use whole EA mixtures or real matrix‐bound toxins (e.g., cereal extracts) as selection targets rather than individual synthetic analogues. In addition, there is a complete absence of validated quantitative aptasensor platforms for EAs, unlike the well‐characterized LC–MS/MS and HRMS methods currently in use. No aptamer‐based sensors have yet been commercialized, nor have any achieved interlaboratory validation or regulatory acceptance for routine screening purposes. While initial studies have demonstrated the conceptual feasibility of aptamer‐based detection and extraction of select EAs, the current tools fall short in terms of range, quantitative accuracy, and real‐world applicability (Pohanka 2020; Rouah et al. 2014; Rouah‐Martin et al. 2012; Scott 2007). Compared to the advanced aptasensor technologies available for other major mycotoxins, the field of EA aptasensing remains in an early proof‐of‐concept stage. Bridging this gap will require the development of broad‐spectrum aptamers, improved SELEX strategies, and integration with scalable transduction technologies such as electrochemical or optical sensors. Only then can aptamer‐based systems begin to complement or compete with existing chromatographic methods in ensuring food and feed safety.

Despite considerable advances in analytical methods for detecting EAs, persistent challenges persist and impact effective risk assessment and regulatory enforcement. Based on current evidence, three areas emerge as critical priorities: improving sampling, developing robust reference standards, and expanding monitoring beyond free alkaloids.

Sampling has been identified as a major contributor to measurement variability in EA testing, with studies showing that sampling variance can account for a substantial proportion of total measurement variance (Tittlemier, Blagden, et al. 2025). Unlike more uniformly distributed mycotoxins such as DON, EAs are concentrated in highly localized clusters associated with sclerotia contamination in grain. When composite sampling protocols do not capture these spatial hotspots, the resulting test portion does not represent the true lot mean, leading to systematic underestimation of EA concentration if contaminated kernels are missed, or overestimation if a small number of contaminated kernels are disproportionately included. This sampling‐driven deviation from the true lot average is the mechanism that produces variation in reported results. The problem is compounded during sample preparation, where insufficient grinding and mixing fail to distribute sclerotia fragments evenly across subsamples. Addressing these weaknesses requires refining sampling protocols to mitigate heterogeneity, adopting geospatial or sensor‐based models to guide hotspot targeting when relevant, and reducing variation in test results. Improving the representativeness of sampling would enhance the reproducibility of test results, reduce international trade disputes linked to inconsistent testing outcomes, and most importantly, ensure that consumers are not exposed to underestimated levels of EAs because contamination was overlooked during sampling, and reduce the variability of the test results.

Another critical barrier to accurate and precise EA analysis lies in the limited availability of certified reference materials and stable isotope‐labelled standards. Although a subset of major EAs, such as ergometrine, ergotamine, ergosine, ergocristine, and α‐ergocryptine, can be purchased, several regulated analogues and epimers remain unavailable in high‐purity form (α,β‐ergocryptine and α,β‐ergocryptinine (Uhlig et al. 2021). Without standardized materials, differences in extraction efficiency, ion suppression, and epimer handling could translate into inconsistent quantification across methods, even when state‐of‐the‐art LC–MS/MS or HRMS platforms are used. While PT programs such as FAPAS and BIPEA provide valuable tools for evaluating interlaboratory performance and method comparability, they do not substitute for certified reference materials. PT samples are primarily designed for benchmarking analytical performance and typically lack the long‐term stability, traceable certification, and defined uncertainty required for calibration and validation purposes. Therefore, despite the availability of PT schemes, the development of well‐characterized certified reference materials remains a critical priority for improving method harmonization, regulatory reliability, and comparability of EA measurements across laboratories. Expanding the pool of commercially available and validated standards would allow laboratories to calibrate on the same foundation and improve method validation. Establishing these resources would not only strengthen scientific confidence in reported concentrations but also reduce the likelihood that consignments would be unfairly rejected in international trade. For consumers, reliable quantification supported by common standards ensures that regulatory thresholds reflect true exposure risks, regardless of where food is tested or produced.

Finally, current monitoring approaches almost exclusively target free alkaloids, neglecting potential conjugated forms such as glycosides and sulphates that have been documented in cereals and grasses (Flieger et al. 1997; Uhlig et al. 2021). These compounds are far from irrelevant: evidence from other mycotoxins shows that such “masked” forms can undergo hydrolysis during mammalian digestion, releasing the biologically active parent compounds (Berthiller et al. 2013). This means that focusing only on free alkaloids risks may systematically underestimating actual dietary exposure. Analyzing potential conjugated EAs enhances toxicological understanding because many of these forms could be converted back to active alkaloids during digestion, contributing to internal exposure. Including them in surveillance, therefore, provides a more accurate estimate of the true bioavailable toxin burden, preventing underestimation of risk. For consumers, this expansion ensures that monitoring reflects not only what is detectable in the laboratory but also what is biologically relevant upon ingestion.

Therefore, many challenges remain in the detection and quantification of EAs, and those that directly influence the reliability of exposure assessments: reducing sampling uncertainties, creating robust reference standards, and broadening surveillance to include potential EA conjugated forms.

EAs remain a persistent mycotoxicological concern in global cereal production, with wheat representing a particularly complex and high‐impact commodity followed by rye, due to its extensive cultivation, heterogeneous contamination patterns, and central role in food and feed supply chains. Despite substantial advances in chromatographic and mass spectrometric techniques, the reliability of EA surveillance is still constrained by fundamental challenges related to sampling uncertainty, epimeric instability, and matrix effects. Sampling remains the dominant source of uncertainty in EA measurement. The heterogeneous distribution of sclerotia and their fragmentation during handling and processing require close attention to sampling plans to mitigate the uncertainty due to sampling. Consequently, improvements in analytical sensitivity alone are insufficient. Future progress will depend on integrating robust sampling designs, incorporating increased increment numbers, effective comminution, targeted hotspot sampling, and emerging geospatial or risk‐based approaches with validated, high‐throughput analytical platforms. Addressing sampling as a core component of risk management is therefore essential for producing data that are fit for regulatory, trade, and exposure assessment purposes. From an analytical perspective, LC–MS/MS and HRMS have substantially improved the detection and quantification of EAs and their epimers; however, key gaps remain. These include limited availability of certified reference materials and isotopically labelled internal standards, persistent matrix suppression in complex cereal and feed matrices, and insufficient understanding of potential conjugated or matrix‐bound alkaloids. Food processing interventions provide only partial mitigation of EAs. While cleaning and milling effectively reduce contamination in refined food fractions, alkaloids are frequently concentrated in bran and by‐products destined for animal feed, shifting rather than eliminating risk. Thermal and chemical treatments generally alter epimer ratios rather than achieving true detoxification, and emerging technologies such as CAPP remain largely unexplored for EAs. Systematic evaluation of degradation mechanisms, transformation products, toxicological relevance, and scalability is needed before such approaches can be adopted with confidence. Regulatory disparities may further complicate EA management. The divergence between jurisdictions that regulate based on sclerotia content and those that impose chemical limits for total alkaloids could potentially create inconsistencies in monitoring objectives and trade compliance. Regulatory approaches to managing the presence of EAs differ across jurisdictions, with some systems relying on sclerotia‐based thresholds and others imposing chemical limits for total EAs. While each approach is defensible within its own regulatory context, these strategies target different attributes of contamination and do not exhibit a consistent quantitative relationship. As a result, lots that comply with sclerotia‐based criteria may not necessarily meet chemical limits, and vice versa, complicating monitoring strategies and creating uncertainty in cross‐jurisdictional trade and compliance decisions. Ultimately, effective global management of EA risks requires harmonization not only of regulatory limits but also of analytical performance capability and representative sampling practices, as variability in any of these components can undermine the reliability of exposure assessment and regulatory enforcement. Looking forward, effective management of EAs will require a multidisciplinary and coordinated approach that links agronomy, sampling science, analytical chemistry, processing technology, toxicology, and regulation. Climate‐driven shifts in ergot prevalence further amplify the need to develop adaptive monitoring systems capable of responding to changing risk patterns. Only through such a multidisciplinary and coordinated approach can we ensure reliable EA surveillance, effective food safety management, and the long‐term security of cereal‐based food and feed supplies worldwide.
