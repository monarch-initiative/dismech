INTRODUCTION AND IMPORTANCE: Beta-ketothiolase deficiency (BKTD) is a rare
inborn error of metabolism that impairs both isoleucine catabolism and ketone
body utilization. The disorder may present with acute metabolic crises, often
precipitated by infections or fasting, and can lead to life-threatening
complications if not promptly diagnosed and managed.
CASE PRESENTATION: We report the case of a previously healthy 2.8-year-old man
who developed vomiting, diarrhea, and fever, followed by progressive loss of
consciousness. Neurological examination revealed generalized muscle rigidity,
limb spasticity, and asymmetrical pupils. Laboratory investigations showed
severe metabolic acidosis, hyperammonemia, and acute kidney injury. Neuroimaging
findings included bilateral basal ganglia involvement and cerebellar
abnormalities. Metabolic workup demonstrated elevated urinary organic acids,
confirming BKTD, which was subsequently validated through genetic analysis
identifying an ACAT1 gene mutation.
CLINICAL DISCUSSION: This case underscores the importance of considering
metabolic disorders in critically ill pediatric patients presenting with acute
neurological symptoms and metabolic deterioration. Early recognition of BKT
deficiency is crucial, as targeted metabolic management - including dietary
intervention and comprehensive supportive care - can mitigate acute crises and
prevent long-term neurological sequelae.
CONCLUSION: Early metabolic evaluation in children with unexplained neurological
decline remains essential. Prompt diagnosis and timely initiation of appropriate
management strategies significantly improve outcomes in patients with BKTD.

Beta-ketothiolase deficiency (BKTD), or mitochondrial acetoacetyl-CoA thiolase (MAT) or T2 deficiency, is a rare autosomal recessive disorder that affects the metabolism of isoleucine and ketone bodies. The primary enzymes involved in ketolysis include succinyl-CoA: 3-oxoacyl-CoA transferase and MAT[1,2]. Daum et al first identified this metabolic disorder in 1971[3], and since then, over 100 cases have been documented[2,3]. The genetic basis of the disease involves mutations in the ACAT1 gene, which is located on chromosome 11q22.3-23.1[4]. Newborn screening programs do not reliably detect MAT deficiency, leading to underdiagnosis in some cases[5,6]. The estimated incidence of this disorder is approximately 1 in 232 000, based on data from newborn screening programs in Minnesota, USA[6]. Clinically, MAT deficiency manifests as recurrent episodes of ketoacidosis, with neonatal-onsetpresentations being uncommon. Affected individuals generally remain asymptomatic between metabolic crises. This report describes the case of a young child diagnosed with BKTD, presenting with severe metabolic decompensation, acute renal impairment, and neurological dysfunction. The case highlights the challenges in diagnosing and managing this rare metabolic disorder. This case is reported due to the unusual combination of acute neurological deterioration, cerebellar magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) changes, and genetically confirmed BKTD in a previously healthy child.

A 2.8-year-old man, previously healthy except for a mild language delay, was admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) on 8 October 2024, following a rapid deterioration in his condition. His symptoms began with vomiting, diarrhea, and fever, which progressed to altered consciousness. Initially treated at an external hospital in Lebanon with intravenous fluids, he continued to decline and was transferred to a specialized children’s hospital. Due to the unavailability of an intensive care unit (ICU) bed at that facility, he was ultimately transferred to our institution, where he required endotracheal intubation. His medical history was unremarkable, and his growth and development had been appropriate. Born via cesarean section without perinatal complications, he had been on a varied diet with good weight gain and had received all vaccinations according to the national immunization schedule. However, a significant family history raised concern, as two paternal cousins had died at the ages of 9 months and 2 years, with one diagnosed with BKTD, an organic aciduria. No psychosocial barriers to care were noted, and the family was cooperative with medical recommendations. Upon admission, the clinical examination revealed a critically ill child requiring mechanical ventilation in a pressure-controlled mode. Neurological assessment showed poor responsiveness, generalized muscle rigidity, hyperreflexia, and a positive Babinski sign in both feet. His pupils were asymmetrical, with the right pupil constricted and the left pupil dilated, likely due to prior mydriatic use.

Cardiovascular findings included a heart rate of 110 beats per minute with moderate perfusion; at the same time, signs of severe dehydration were evident, such as dry mucous membranes, sunken eyes, and a strong ketotic odor. The abdominal examination was notable for hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 4 cm below the right costal margin, 3 cm in the epigastrium, and 6 cm from the flank. The respiratory assessment revealed coarse rales bilaterally.

The progression of his condition followed a timeline of critical events. His initial symptoms in early October 2024 led to the admission at a local hospital, where intravenous fluid therapy failed to prevent further deterioration. By 8 October 2024, he had developed severe neurological impairment, necessitating PICU admission, mechanical ventilation, and intensive metabolic assessment. Between 10 and 15 October 2024, laboratory and metabolic investigations confirmed BKTD, prompting targeted management strategies, including metabolic stabilization, electrolyte correction, and infection control. Over the next months, spanning from November 2024 to January 2025, efforts focused on metabolic balance and neurological rehabilitation. While he remained ataxic and exhibited persistent tremors, he showed gradual recovery. By 27 January 2025, he was successfully weaned off ventilatory support, achieved partial neurological improvement, and was discharged with a specialized metabolic diet and a structured rehabilitation program.

A comprehensive diagnostic assessment was undertaken to clarify the underlying pathology. Laboratory findings on admission included a white blood cell count of 10 700/µL, hemoglobin at 8.2 g/dL, and a low mean corpuscular volume of 69 fl. Platelet levels were reduced at 80 000/µL, while electrolyte imbalances were significant, with hypokalemia at 2.1 mmol/L and hypochloremia at 91 mmol/L. Markedly, elevated renal function markers indicated acute kidney injury, with urea at 131 mg/dL and creatinine at 3.9 mg/dL. Inflammatory markers were also high, with a C-reactive protein level of 3.3 mg/dL and procalcitonin at 0.8 ng/mL. Liver function tests showed mildly elevated GPT at 37 IU/L and hypoalbuminemia at 2.8 g/dL.

Coagulation studies revealed prolonged prothrombin time (at 65%) and elevated d-dimer levels at 162 ng/mL. The cerebrospinal fluid analysis was notable for a clear yellow appearance with a white blood cell count of 8, red blood cells at 50, glucose at 151, protein at 28, and a significantly elevated lactate level of 14.9, while cultures remained sterile. Urinalysis demonstrated pronounced ketonuria (+3), granular casts, and trace hemoglobin. Metabolic investigations confirmed the presence of elevated 3-hydroxy butyric acid, consistent with BKTD (Table 1). Neuroimaging findings were significant during the patient’s evaluation. An initial axial CT scan at admission (Fig. 1) revealed multiple hypodense foci in the cerebellum, while another axial CT scan (Fig. 2) demonstrated bilateral symmetric hypointensity in the basal ganglia. A follow-up MRI prior to discharge showed notable changes. An axial FLAIR MRI (Fig. 3) revealed a small focal hyperintensity in the left cerebellum following management.

An axial T2-weighted MRI obtained after treatment (Fig. 5) showed symmetrical normal intensity in the basal ganglia, suggesting improvement. The diagnostic process was delayed due to the need for specialized metabolic testing, but no financial or cultural barriers affected access to these investigations. The diagnosis was ultimately confirmed as BKTD, a rare organic aciduria that had led to metabolic decompensation in the context of septicemia and acute renal failure. Other differential diagnoses, including mitochondrial disorders, urea cycle defects, and other organic acidurias, were considered, but confirmatory metabolic testing established the final diagnosis.

Management involved a multidisciplinary therapeutic approach. Initial stabilization focused on correcting metabolic acidosis, electrolyte imbalances, and acute renal failure. Fluid resuscitation, potassium replacement, and albumin infusions were administered to restore the volume status and organ perfusion. Seizure control was achieved with levetiracetam at 20 mg/kg, and clonazepam at 0.04 mg/kg/day, though the latter was discontinued due to excessive secretions. Empirical antimicrobial therapy included teicoplanin and meropenem, both given for 16 days, in addition to fluconazole for 7 days to cover potential fungal infections. A high-carbohydrate, protein-restricted diet was introduced to reduce metabolic stress, supplemented with l-carnitine and arginine to facilitate metabolic stabilization. As treatment progressed, dietary modifications were refined, particularly as intermittent myoclonic jerks and ataxia persisted. Discontinuing clonazepam led to improved motor function.

Follow-up and outcome assessments indicated gradual improvement. By discharge on 27 January 2025, the patient’s vital signs were stable, seizure activity had ceased, and his neurological function had partially recovered, though moderate cerebellar ataxia persisted. Residual deficits included partial neck instability and ongoing tremors. A structured discharge plan included levofloxacin, levetiracetam, carnitine, arginine, and essential vitamin supplementation, including Vitamin D3, B12, and zinc.

The family was instructed on strict dietary adherence and metabolic monitoring. Scheduled follow-up appointments included a neurology clinic review in 3 months, routine metabolic testing to monitor ammonia and ketone levels, and an EEG to evaluate any residual seizure activity. Physiotherapy was recommended to aid motor recovery. Intervention adherence was closely monitored, and the family demonstrated a strong commitment to maintaining the prescribed regimen. No unexpected hospital-acquired infections or severe complications occurred during treatment. This case underscores the critical importance of early metabolic screening in pediatric patients presenting with unexplained neurological deterioration. While the patient demonstrated significant improvement with appropriate metabolic and neurological management, he remains at risk for long-term neurological sequelae. Continued metabolic surveillance, dietary regulation, and rehabilitative support will be essential in optimizing his developmental trajectory and quality of life.

BKTD presents a valuable model for understanding the complex interplay between metabolic pathways and neurological outcomes in pediatric populations. The present case underscores the importance of early recognition and comprehensive management in preventing long-term sequelae. A key strength of this report lies in the integration of clinical findings, metabolic screening, and confirmatory genetic testing, which collectively enabled an accurate and timely diagnosis. Furthermore, longitudinal follow-up demonstrated notable neurological recovery and developmental gains, emphasizing the efficacy of prompt intervention. Nonetheless, the case is not without limitations. Given the rarity of BKTD, generalizability remains restricted. The absence of long-term follow-up beyond 4 months limits our ability to comment on sustained outcomes or the potential emergence of late-onset complications. Additionally, while biochemical markers strongly supported the diagnosis, the availability of these specialized tests may be limited in resource-constrained settings, potentially delaying diagnosis elsewhere. The lack of functional studies confirming the impact of the specific ACAT1 mutation represents another limitation. The clinical presentation in this patient – comprising developmental delay, recurrent gastrointestinal symptoms, and metabolic acidosis – is consistent with existing reports in the literature[7,8]. Previous studies have described similar episodes of ketoacidosis triggered by catabolic stress, particularly during fasting or intercurrent illness[9]. As BKTD impairs ketolysis and isoleucine catabolism, the accumulation of 2-methyl-3-thiobutyric acid and other toxic intermediates contributes to encephalopathy and mitochondrial dysfunction[10].

Management principles, drawn from established guidelines[11], stress the importance of preventing catabolic states through dietary modifications, avoidance of fasting, and aggressive treatment during metabolic crises. Therapeutic decisions were grounded in this pathophysiological understanding. Maintaining carbohydrate intake, correcting acidosis, and ensuring adequate hydration. The observed improvements in neuromotor function, speech articulation, and seizure control following discharge highlight the reversibility of some symptoms with appropriate care. Although gene-targeted therapies remain experimental, emerging research on enzyme enhancement and molecular correction holds future promise.

This case illustrates the critical importance of early metabolic screening in children with unexplained neurological symptoms and acidosis. With timely intervention, even severe presentations of BKTD can result in significant recovery. Multidisciplinary management and parental adherence to prescribed regimens are pivotal in optimizing outcomes. Ongoing research into novel therapeutic options may further improve the prognosis for individuals with this condition.

As shared by the patient’s parents:

When our child first started showing signs of delayed development and repeated vomiting, we were deeply concerned but unsure of what was wrong. Each hospital visit brought more questions than answers, and seeing him have seizures was terrifying. Once the diagnosis was made, everything changed. The doctors explained the condition to us with great care and developed a plan that we could follow. It was overwhelming at first, especially the dietary restrictions and constant monitoring, but we were just relieved to have a path forward.

Since starting treatment, we’ve seen such a change in our son. He is more alert, can walk and sit by himself, and even started saying more words. The seizures have stopped, and we feel more confident managing his condition with the support of our medical team. The physiotherapy sessions have also helped him build strength. We are hopeful for his future and grateful for the care he has received.
