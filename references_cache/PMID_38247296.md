# Clinical phenotypic spectrum of CTNNB1 neurodevelopmental disorder.
**Authors:** Sudnawa KK, Garber A, Cohen R, Calamia S, Kanner CH, Montes J, Bain JM, Fee RJ, Chung WK
**Journal:** Clin Genet (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1111/cge.14487](https://doi.org/10.1111/cge.14487)

## Content

Pathogenic heterozygous loss of function variants in CTNNB1 are associated with CTNNB1 neurodevelopmental disorder. We report the clinical phenotype of individuals with CTNNB1 neurodevelopmental disorder using both caregiver-reported data (medical history, adaptive function, quality of life, and behavior issues) and in-person clinical assessments (neurological, motor, and cognitive function) in 32 individuals with likely pathogenic or pathogenic CTNNB1 variants. Most individuals had truncal hypotonia, muscle weakness, hypertonia, dystonia, microcephaly, and many had a history of tethered cord. Visual problems included strabismus, hyperopia, and familial exudative vitreoretinopathy. Half of individuals walked without an assistive device. The mean Gross Motor Functional Measure-66 score was 56.6 (SD = 14.8). Average time to complete Nine-Hole Peg Test was slower than norms. Mean general conceptual ability composite scores from Differential Ability Scales Second Edition were very low (M = 58.3, SD = 11.3). Fifty-five percent of individuals had low adaptive functioning based on the Vineland Adaptive Behavioral Scales. Based upon the Child Behavior Checklist total problems score, the majority (65%) of individuals had behavioral challenges. The mean overall Quality of Life Inventory-Disability score was 81.7 (SD = 11.9). These data provide a detailed characterization of clinical features in individuals with CTNNB1 neurodevelopmental disorder.

Pathogenic heterozygous loss of function variants in the Catenin Beta-1 (CTNNB1) gene (OMIM#116806) are associated with CTNNB1 syndrome or CTNNB1 Neurodevelopmental Disorder (CTNNB1-NDD) or Neurodevelopmental Disorder with Spastic Diplegia and Visual Defects (NEDSDV) (OMIM#615075). This condition is a rare autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by global developmental delay, intellectual disability, truncal hypotonia, peripheral spasticity, microcephaly, strabismus, exudative vitreoretinopathy, and behavioral challenges including autism, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and aggression1. CTNNB1 neurodevelopmental disorder is a common genetic cause of cerebral palsy (CP)2. Genetic variants causing CTNNB1 neurodevelopmental disorder are largely de novo, and include missense, nonsense, frameshift, splice, and whole gene deletions1. The proposed mechanism of disease is loss of function leading to haploinsufficiency of beta catenin, that is encoded by CTNNB1.

Beta-catenin belongs to the armadillo family of proteins and is a part of the canonical Wingless-related integration site (Wnt)/Beta-catenin signaling pathway, involved in regulating cell differentiation and proliferation throughout neural development3. Moreover, beta catenin is an integral structural component of the cadherin-based adherents junction protein complex, which has a major role in cell adhesion and migration during cortical development4.

We report on the clinical phenotype of individuals with pathogenic/likely pathogenic CTNNB1 variants using both caregiver-reported data and in-person clinical assessments using standardized measures.

This study was approved by the Columbia University Institutional Review Board, and electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. Participants were recruited through CTNNB1 Connect and Cure. All individuals had pathogenic or likely pathogenic CTNNB1 variants according to the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) classification guidelines5 and were able to participate in-person assessments. Individuals with variants of uncertain significance and dual genetic diagnoses affecting the nervous system were excluded. All individuals were also enrolled in the Simons Searchlight study (https://www.simonssearchlight.org/).

Online clinical data were obtained from the Simons Searchlight study. The medical history was collected by phone interview with genetic counselors. Individuals’ adaptive function was evaluated using the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales6 administered by phone interview. Individuals’ quality of life and behavior issues were assessed by online questionnaires [Quality of Life Inventory-Disability (QI-Disability), Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL), Social Communication Questionnaire-current (SDQ), and Children’s Sleep Habits Questionnaire (CSHQ)] completed by primary caregivers (Supplemental Table 1).

In person clinical evaluations were conducted during CTNNB1 Connect and Cure conference and included measurement of weight, height, and head circumference, neurological and functional assessment by a board-certified child neurologist, gross and fine motor assessments by a physical therapist, and cognitive assessment by a neuropsychologist (Supplemental Table 1).

Individual’s function was assessed in person and categorized using the Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS)7, Manual Ability Classification System (MACS)8, Communication Function Classification System (CFCS)9, and Eating and Drinking Ability Classification System (EDACS)10. The Pediatric Feeding Oral Intake Scale (pFIOS) was also used to assess feeding habits and concern for dysphagia11.

In-person motor function assessment: the Gross Motor Functional Measure-88 (GMFM-88)12 was performed to quantify the level of gross motor skills achieved. GMFM-88 scores were converted to GMFM-66 scores. The GMFM-66 score has a maximum of 100% with higher scores indicating better function. For those individuals who could follow instructions, upper limb function was measured using the Revised Upper Limb Module (RULM)13, as well as the Box and Blocks Test (BBT)14 and Nine-Hole Peg Test (9HPT) on both sides 15. Ambulatory function was assessed in individuals who walked without assistance using the 10-meter Walk Run Test (10MWR)16, Six-Minute Walk Test (6MWT)17, and Timed Up and Go Test (TUG)18.

In person cognitive assessment: the Differential Ability Scales Second Edition (DAS-II) was performed in individuals ranging in age from 2.5-17.9 years. DAS-II has reported internal reliability ranging from 0.74 to 0.919. Individuals were excluded from the assessment if their age was out of DAS-II range, they could not perform the assessment, or English was not their primary language. The General Conceptual Ability (GCA), verbal, nonverbal reasoning, and spatial cluster scores were used as primary outcome measures in the analyses. Each score has a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15. Composite and cluster scores were categorized to average (score=90-109), below average (score=80-89), low (score=70-79), and very low (score≤69).

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS (version 28). Categorical variables are reported as frequency and percentages. Continuous variables with normal distribution are reported as mean (M) with standard deviation (SD); non-normal distribution variables are reported as median with interquartile range (IQR). Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post hoc analysis were used respectively to compare means of two and three or more groups of continuous normally distributed variables. A Pearson's correlation coefficient was computed to assess the relationship between continuous variables. A value of p≤0.05 was considered significant.

Our cohort included 32 individuals. There were slightly more male (56.3%) than female individuals. The mean age of individuals was 8.5 years (SD=5.6) and the median age was 6.7 years (range 1.8-22.7). All but three individuals were minors (<18 years).

All individuals had likely pathogenic or pathogenic CTNNB1 variants (13 frameshift, 11 nonsense, 4 missense, 2 splice, 1 inversion, and 1 deletion variant). The inversion [Inv(3)(p24.3p22.1)] involved exon 1-9 of the CTNNB1 gene with an approximate breakpoint at chr3:16710965-41275270. The deletion [3p22.1(41265559_41267353)x1] included exons 2-6 of CTNNB1. Variants were demonstrated to be de novo in 24 cases (75%), a pair of male-female siblings with presumed parental mosaicism, and of unknown inheritance due to lack of parental testing in six cases (Figure 1).

The most common pregnancy complications were small for gestational age (26.1%) and oligohydramnios (13%). The mean gestational age of individuals was 38.8 weeks (SD=1.8). Preterm labor was noted in 14%, with the earliest gestational age of 34 weeks. Birth weight (median birth weight z-score −1.1, IQR=1.7) and birth length (median length z-score −0.4, IQR=2.9) were normal. The most common perinatal problem was respiratory distress (30.4%) with two individuals requiring intubation after birth. The most common neonatal problems were feeding difficulty (47.8%), irritability (30.4%), hypotonia (26.1%), and hyperbilirubinemia (26.1%) with three individuals requiring phototherapy.

Most individuals were of normal weight and height. For individuals between 2-20 years old, the mean BMI z-score was 0.1 (SD=0.9, range –1.5 to 2.1). The mean BMI in individuals >20 years old was 25.4 kg/m2 (SD=5.1). Four individuals (14%) were overweight, and one was obese. The mean height z-score was −0.8 (SD=1.0, range −2.5 to 1.4). Three individuals were short-stature (z-score <−2).

Visual problems were common and included strabismus in 62.5%, hyperopia in 31.3%, familial exudative vitreoretinopathy (FEVR) in 25%, myopia in 9.4%, and nystagmus in 9.4%. Thirteen individuals (40.6%) had strabismus surgery. Forty-one percent reported a history of otitis media with 15.7% having more than five occurrences and 41% treated with tubes. Three individuals had hearing impairment, including two with sensorineural hearing impairment and one with conductive hearing impairment. Gastrointestinal problems were common and included constipation in 50% and gastroesophageal reflux disease in 18.8%. Relatively minor congenital heart malformations were reported in four individuals (one with ventricular septal defect (VSD) and atrial septal defect, one with a VSD, one with a patent ductus arteriosus, and one with tricuspid regurgitation). Cryptorchidism was noted in 3 males (17%) and two males required orchiopexy (11%). Medications commonly used were baclofen (31.3%), methylphenidate (18.8%), levodopa (15.6%), selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (15.6%), and melatonin (15.6%).

By caregiver report, neurological issues were ubiquitous (Table 1), and all reported hypotonia with a median age of recognition at 6 months old. Additional neurological issues included hypertonia, excessively clumsy or uncoordinated, and tethered cord. Eight individuals (25%) had surgical tethered cord release. Half of individuals had been diagnosed with CP. Seizures were reported in three individuals with one individual with electrical status epilepticus during slow-wave sleep. Two individuals were on levetiracetam and one on clobazam.

From the in-person neurological examination, microcephaly (head circumference z-score <−2) was present in 60% with mean head circumference z-score −2.3 (SD=1.3). Head circumference z-score was positively correlated with age (r=0.48; p<0.05). Muscle weakness was present in 90.6% with proximal muscle, trunk, and lower extremity weakness in most. Tone abnormalities were present in 96.9% with the majority with truncal hypotonia and lower extremity hypertonia. Many ambulatory individuals had diplegic gait, ataxic gait, and/or toe walking. Abnormal movements were noted in the majority and included dystonia, stereotypic movements, and rarely tremor. Musculoskeletal abnormalities included pes planus in 71.9% and rarely scoliosis and joint contractures.

Fifty-six percent of individuals walked without an assistive device (GMFCS I/II) and eight (25%) were non-ambulatory. Most individuals could handle objects without assistance (MACS I/II). Fifty-five percent of individuals could communicate independently in most environments (CFCS I/II). Most individuals were able to eat and drink safely (EDACS I/II) (Figure 2). Several individuals reported concerns for dysphagia when younger, but most outgrew it. One individual had a feeding tube, and all others were orally fed with most eating a regular diet.

The mean GMFM-66 score was 56.6 (SD=14.8). Age was positively associated with GMFM-66 score (r=0.41; p=0.02). Of the 18 ambulatory individuals, most completed the 10MWR (88.9%), with fewer individuals completing the 6MWT (72.2%) and TUG (66.7%) due to difficulty following instructions and distractibility. The time to completed 10MWR (M=6.5, SD=1.9) was slower than normal with median z-score 3.0 (range −1.4 to 6.3) and 56.3% had completion times more than 2SD greater than normal16. The mean distance walked on the 6MWT was 321.1 meters (SD=112.8), which represents 53.2% (SD=13.5) of predicted for age, sex, and height. The mean TUG time (M=9.6, SD=2.6) was slower than normal with median z-score 2.8 (range −6.5 to 8.4) and 53.8% had times greater than 2SD above normal20.

Upper limb function was assessed in 23 individuals (71.9%). The mean age of individuals who did not complete upper limb assessments was 4.6 years (SD= 3.3). Of those assessed, all participants were able to lift the arms against gravity (at least 3/5 strength testing) to or above shoulder height and had an average RULM score of 27.6 (SD=8.5) out of a maximum of 37. Nineteen individuals (59.4%) completed BBT, and twelve individuals (37.5%) completed 9HPT. Mean BBT scores using the dominant hand were similar to published norms21 (M=19.0, SD=9.7). Average time to complete 9HPT was slower than published norms22 (M=51.8, SD=19.0).

According to caregiver report, most individuals had a history of global developmental delay (90.6%). Individuals took their first independent steps at a mean age of 2.3 years (SD=0.9) and said their first word at a mean age of 2.3 years (SD=1.2). Common behavioral diagnoses included autism, ADHD, and anxiety (Table 1).

Cognitive assessments were performed in 15 individuals. Individuals who did not perform cognitive assessments were very low functioning (nonverbal) (21.9%), out of age range for DAS-II (9.4%), English not their primary language (6.3%), or declined by the caregiver (12.5%). The seven individuals who were nonverbal had a median age of 4.2 years old (range 3.1-12.1). The mean GCA scores (M=58.3, SD=11.3) was classified as very low. The very low mean spatial score (M=47.5, SD=13.3) was significantly lower compared to low mean verbal (M=73.6, SD=22.1), and nonverbal reasoning scores (M=70.2, SD=10.1); (p<0.001) (Figure 3A). There was variability within individual DAS-II scores such that 26.7% were in the average range in the verbal domain and 6.7% were in the average range for the nonverbal reasoning domain, whereas the majority of spatial cluster scores were very low (93.3%) (Figure 3B).

The mean Vineland Adaptive Behavior Composite (ABC) standard score was 66.5 (SD=13.4). All Vineland subdomains were approximately equally affected: communication (M=68.2, SD=18.6), daily living skills (M=64.0, SD=17.3), socialization (M=66.9, SD=17.1), and motor skills (M=66.1, SD=9.8), with no significant differences across subdomains (p>0.05) (Figure 4A). Fifty-five percent (11/20) of individuals was classified as low adaptive functioning (ABC standard score ≤70) (Figure 4B).

Based upon the CBCL total problems score, the majority (65%) of individuals had behavioral challenges (n=17; M=63.4, SD=9.8). When grouping behavioral symptoms, internalizing and externalizing problems were reported in 47.1% and 35.3%, respectively. All subdomains were equally affected including internalizing problems (M=61.5, SD=9.7), externalizing problems (M=57.4, SD=10.2), and attention problems (M=62.8, SD=9.2). Twelve individuals (66.7%) had sleep problems based upon the CSHQ with a mean total score of 44.0 (n=18; SD=6.4). The most common sleep problems were short sleep duration (5/18) and parasomnia (5/18). The total CSHQ score was positively correlated with the CBCL total problems score (r(16)=0.64, p<0.05). According to the SCQ, one-third of individuals had a risk of social communication impairment (total score >15) with a group mean score of 15 (n=15; SD=6.9).

The mean overall quality of life score was 81.7 (SD=11.9) on the QI-Disability. The negative emotions (M=74.5, SD=13.3) was significantly lower than the positive emotions subdomain (M=90.3, SD=11.9). There was no significant difference between the physical health (M=86.1, SD=12.0), social interaction (M=81.8, SD=17.3), leisure and the outdoors (M=81.6, SD=15.8), or independence (M=75.6, SD=21.5) subdomains (Figure 5).

We report detailed clinical characteristics from caregiver reports and in-person assessments in 32 individuals with likely pathogenic or pathogenic CTNNB1 variants. The most common clinical symptoms were global developmental delay/intellectual disability, autism, ADHD, tone abnormalities, a cerebral palsy clinical diagnosis, dystonia, microcephaly, and ophthalmologic issues including strabismus and FEVR. Less frequent but important additional features include tethered cord and seizures.

Neurological characteristics are similar to previous reports (Supplemental Table 2). However, Kayumi et al.1 reported a higher frequency of microcephaly (79.8%) and lower frequency of dystonia (7.5%) and tethered cord (9.3%) compared to our study. The Yan et al series23 reported a similar frequency of microcephaly (69.6%) and higher frequency of dystonia (87.5%) compared to our series.

In mice, homozygous deletion of beta-catenin causes a marked reduction in the overall size of the spinal cord and brain, due to a reduced proliferation rate of neural progenitors, with a shift in the balance between self-renewal and differentiation24. Kayumi et al1 reported microcephaly in 79.8% of their case series (65.6% congenital and 34.4% postnatal onset). Previous studies25,26 reported progressive microcephaly with smaller head circumference after birth. We reported microcephaly in 60% at the time of assessment, by direct in-person head circumference measurement. Our study found a positive correlation between head circumference z-score and age. Collecting birth head circumference and a longitudinal study to document the clinical course of head size is needed.

Dystonia was frequent in our study and was reported in the medical interviews and confirmed by in-person neurological examinations. A previous study using exome sequencing in children with movement disorders27 and dystonia24 identified CTNNB1 as one of the most frequent genetic diagnoses. A case study of a CTNNB1 individual with dystonia reported a significant response to levodopa treatment27, maybe due to the role of beta-catenin in controlling dopamine neurogenesis and deficiency of these dopaminergic neurons28. Several individuals in this study had used levodopa in the past or were currently on levodopa.

Tethered cord was reported in 25% of participants, which is higher than other studies1. This may be due to ascertainment bias of who attended our meeting and participated. The cause of the tethered cord may be abnormal growth of the neural tube during the fetal period. Beta-catenin is an essential component of canonical Wnt signaling, in the absence Wnt-1/Wnt3a mutant mice resulted in a reduction of the dorsal neural tube29 and may explain the mechanism of the tethered cord. Examining for midline cutaneous lumbosacral dimples, progressive neurological deficits such as weakness or numbness, or progressive scoliosis or foot deformities can assist in detection of a tethered cord.

There was a range of motor difficulties found among all 32 individuals, which seem to affect gross motor (44% GMFCS III/IV) more than fine motor skills (18% MACS III/IV). GMFM-66 scores were positively correlated with age, suggesting continued gain of gross motor skills with age, however a prospective longitudinal study is necessary to confirm this hypothesis. The locomotor assessment showed that about 50% of ambulatory individuals still had gross motor difficulties. The mean distance walked on the 6MWR is shorter, but 10MWR is faster when compared to children with spastic CP30 (Supplemental Table 3), suggesting endurance limitations. The mean TUG time is similar to the previous study in children with CP31 and Down syndrome32 but longer than children with autism33. While individuals were able to perform the BBT test similar to normative values, finger dexterity was impaired as shown on the 9HPT. The 9HPT requires precise fine motor skills, coordination, attention, and good vision to perform the task, so individuals with impairments in fine motor skills, coordination, or vision may be slower to complete this task.

The most common ophthalmic manifestations were strabismus, hyperopia, and FEVR, similar to previous studies1. The lower frequency of FEVR may be due to underdiagnosis, and we did not perform a direct eye exam. FEVR is a vitreoretinopathy characterized by abnormal retinal vessel development. In the retina, the Wnt signaling cascade orchestrates normal retinal vascular formation. Mild forms of the disease often are asymptomatic and show only peripheral vascular abnormalities. However, the disease can progress to retinal detachment and profound vision loss34. Eye examination alone can miss the early-stages of FEVR, and wide-field fluorescein angiography is necessary for accurate diagnosis35. Management of FEVR is focused on preventing further progression, and laser is the mainstay of treatment. Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor therapy may have a role as an adjunctive treatment before surgery34. Regular eye examination with a retinal specialist is recommended.

Developmental delay and cognitive deficits have been found to be universal in individuals with CTNNB1 neurodevelopmental disorder. CTNNB1 has been identified as one of the replicated neurodevelopmental genes in sequencing studies of autism, intellectual disability, and CP36. Our study used standard cognitive and adaptive function assessments to quantify these challenges. It is important to note that cognitive assessment in individuals with CTNNB1 neurodevelopmental disorder who have significant visual and fine motor impairments may not be an accurate reflection of cognitive capacity, and this should always be considered in the interpretation of results. This is likely why individual spatial cluster scores were significantly lower than other domains. Verbal and nonverbal reasoning cluster scores likely provide a more accurate assessment of cognitive function than the GCA composite score given these potential confounds. From our study, cognitive function as assessed by DAS-II using GCA indicated very low overall, which is similar to a previous study37 showing intellectual disability in 84.9% of the sample. When examining individual verbal and nonverbal reasoning performance in our sample, 53.3% had verbal abilities in the low/very low range, and 86.6% had nonverbal reasoning in low/very low performance which indicating that a small percentage of individuals are in the average/below average range. Additionally, verbal ability was correlated with overall Vineland adaptive function which 55% of individuals had low adaptive function. These data suggest that cognitive functioning within this population is more variable than previously appreciated, and it is important to understand individual strengths and weaknesses to maximize cognitive and learning interventions.

One large autism genetic study38 identified CTNNB1 as a risk gene for autism. In our study, caregivers reported an autism diagnosis in 34%, which is higher than the previous studies1,23. The report of autism is supported by the results from the SCQ, which found that 33% of individuals had a risk of social communication impairment. The higher frequency of autism in our study may be due to the method of data collection (specifically asking parents about autism) and the older age of our cohort.

Using the CBCL, we documented frequent externalizing behaviors (delinquent and aggressive behaviors), which is similar to the previous report of aggressive behavior in 21-48%37,39. Our study found that 47% of individuals indicated internalizing problems (withdrawn, somatic complaints, and anxiety/depressed problems), which is higher than the percentage of an anxiety diagnosis (15.6%) in this cohort and has rarely been reported in other studies. We reported internalizing problems using the caregiver reported CBCL, a screening questionnaire for behavioral and emotional problems in children, which may have limitations in parental perceptions in children with intellectual disabilities40. Diagnosis of mood disorders in individuals with intellectual disability is difficult and may be misdiagnosed or underdiagnosed. An individual with intellectual disability often has less ability to communicate and describe mood symptoms effectively41. Further study of behavioral problems is necessary to identify common challenges in the group and develop effective interventions.

Short sleep duration and parasomnia were the most common sleep problems, similar to other neurodevelopmental disorders42. We found that the total score on the CSHQ was positively correlated with the CBCL total problems score. A previous study43 found that children with insomnia had more overall behavioral problems than controls, and there was a significant interaction between sleep duration and externalizing behaviors, internalizing behaviors, and mood variability. The causes of sleep problems are multifactorial and include intrinsic pathophysiological factors, physical comorbidities, psychiatric comorbidities, and parenting practices44. Finding the cause of sleep problems and starting first-line treatment with parent-based education and behavioral interventions may ameliorate some of the secondary behavioral and cognitive issues.

The mean overall quality of life score in individuals with CTNNB1 neurodevelopmental disorder (M=81.7, SD=11.9) was higher than some other neurodevelopmental disorders (Down syndrome, Rett syndrome, Cyclin-dependent kinase-like 5 (CDKL5) deficiency disorder, CP and autism) (M=67.8, SD=13.4)45. In our study, the lowest subdomain scores for quality of life were negative emotion and independence. To improve the quality of life of individuals with CTNNB1 neurodevelopmental disorder, we need to build good emotional support and behavioral management, along with allowing for greater independent daily living skills.

A strength of our study is the comprehensive data collection from caregiver reports and in-person clinical assessments to independently confirm caregiver reports. The study collected a wide range of clinical characteristics using a detailed neurological examination and functional assessments. Moreover, motor skill, cognitive function, and quality of life data were collected using standardized measures. These data provide a more complete and deeper understanding of clinical features in individuals with CTNNB1 neurodevelopmental disorder to inform clinical management, maximize affected individuals’ potential, and prepare for future clinical trials. Additional studies with larger numbers of individuals and longitudinal data are needed to assess trajectories.

The most common clinical features in individuals with CTNNB1 neurodevelopmental disorder were developmental delay/intellectual disability, motor impairment, truncal hypotonia with peripheral spasticity, microcephaly, dystonia, tethered cord, strabismus and FEVR. Assessments for tethered cord and FEVR are important and specific to this condition. Internalizing and externalizing behaviors, and sleep problems may be underdiagnosed and undertreated. Overall, the quality of life in individuals with CTNNB1 neurodevelopmental disorder is good and likely reflects the general lack of medical issues and seizures in most individuals. We hope to further improve quality of life by maintaining greater functionality and independence with recognition and treatment of associated features.
