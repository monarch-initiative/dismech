Lupus nephritis (LN) is a severe manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus
with approximately 50% of patients having renal involvement. Patients who fail
to respond to appropriate induction immunosuppression are considered to have
refractory disease. Refractory LN is associated with increased risk of
progression to end-stage chronic kidney disease and mortality. Despite the
development of modern therapeutics, refractory LN remains challenging in
clinical practice and continues to be associated with treatment failure.
Complement has a well-described role in the pathogenesis of LN; however,
complement inhibition is not routinely used for refractory disease. We present
the case of a woman in her 30s with refractory LN who presented with atypical
haemolytic uraemic syndrome, macrophage activation syndrome and low-severity
COVID-19 infection. She was treated with eculizumab with rapid clinical and
biochemical improvement. The patient achieved complete remission of her
refractory LN after eculizumab and remains in remission 2 years post eculizumab.

BACKGROUND
Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a multi-
system autoimmune disease characterised by loss 
of self-
 tolerance,
 production of autoantibodies and 
immune complex activation of complement.1 Lupus 
nephritis (LN) is characterised by renal deposition of 
immune complexes, resulting in renal injury.
2 Acti-
vation of the complement pathway by these immune 
complexes can result in atypical haemolytic uraemic 
syndrome, defined by thrombocytopaenia, micro-
angiopathic haemolytic anaemia and microvascular 
thrombi, causing systemic organ damage, including 
acute kidney injury.
1 Patients with LN who develop 
atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome have poor 
outcomes with conventional immunosuppression 
and thus the use of complement blockade with eculi-
zumab has been described.
3 There remain limited 
studies investigating the response to eculizumab in 
LN that is refractory to conventional immunosup-
pressive agents. We report a case whereby eculi -
zumab achieved sustained remission for refractory 
LN when it was used to treat concomitant atypical 
haemolytic uraemic syndrome in the setting of 
macrophage activation syndrome and COVID-
 19 
infection. 
The refractory LN of the patient remains 
in remission 2 years later with low-
 dose prednis
-
olone and hydroxychloroquine without any addi-
tional immunosuppressive agents.

Meanwhile, the presence of immune complexes in LN leads to 
pathological activation of the complement pathway, overacti-
vation of the terminal membrane attack complex (C5b-

While significant literature has demonstrated the integral 
role of complement in the pathogenesis of LN, complement 
inhibition is not routinely used for refractory disease. Instead, 
an extensive range of other immunosuppressive agents is used, 
including corticosteroids, mycophenolate mofetil, azathio-
prine, rituximab, calcineurin inhibitors and cyclophosphamide.
1 
However, inducing clinical remission in LN remains challenging, 
with between 20% and 70% of cases refractory to conventional 
treatment.
6 In the case presented, the patient had been treated 
with the aforementioned immunosuppressive agents plus abata -
cept, a soluble CTLA-
 4 analogue, 
without sustained treatment 
response. Her clinical manifestations reached the severity of 
patients who undergo anti-
 19 chimeric 
antigen receptor T-
 cell 
therapy for SLE and she was being considered for this treatment.7
Eculizumab is a humanised monoclonal antibody which binds 
to complement protein C5, inhibiting cleavage to C5a and C5b 
and subsequently blocking formation of the terminal membrane 
attack complex.
8 Eculizumab has been well reported to be an 
efficacious treatment for thrombotic microangiopathy secondary 
to LN.
3 Multiple studies have shown early intervention with 
eculizumab leads to improved clinical outcomes and reversal of 
organ damage in thrombotic microangiopathy.
3
Limited literature exists to determine the role for eculizumab 
as adjunctive therapy in refractory LN. A systematic review by 
Wright et al found 93% of patients administered eculizumab for 
thrombotic microangiopathy secondary to LN had resolution 
of lupus symptoms and recovery of renal function. Eculizumab 
treatment was successfully stopped in 46% of patients with no 
relapse of disease in a median follow-
 up period of 7 months. 3 
Furthermore, a study to assess the membrane attack complex as 
a potential biomarker for disease severity in LN found increased 
C9 deposition in glomerular tissue correlated to more intense 
disease and treatment-
 refractory LN.9
While previous studies, including Wright et al have demon -
strated that eculizumab successfully treats thrombotic micro-
angiopathy secondary to LN, there are minimal cases where 
eculizumab also induced remission of previously refractory 
LN. The effect of eculizumab in this case is further supported 
by remission sustained at 2 years post eculizumab treatment 
without the recommencement of mycophenolic acid or other 
steroid-
 
sparing treatments.
This rare case describes refractory LN with concomitant 
atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome, macrophage activation 
syndrome and COVID-
 19 infection. It suggests that complement 
blockade 
with eculizumab may have a role in the treatment of 
patients with refractory LN. Additional studies are required to 
elucidate the role of eculizumab in the management of severe 
LN.
Learning points
 ► Refractory lupus nephritis (LN) increases the risk of 
progression to end-
 stage chronic kidney disease and 
mortality.
 ► Refractory LN is a challenging clinical entity which is 
commonly associated with treatment failure.
 ► Production of autoantibodies in LN results in immune 
complex activation of complement and renal injury.
 ► Complement blockade with eculizumab is well described in 
patients with LN who develop atypical haemolytic syndrome; 
however, more research is required to understand the role of 
eculizumab in refractory LN.
