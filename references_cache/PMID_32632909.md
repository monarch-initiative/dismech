# A different clinical manifestation in a Japanese family with autosomal dominant distal renal tubular acidosis caused by SLC4A1 mutation.
**Authors:** Sakuraya K, Nozu K, Oka I, Fujinaga S, Nagano C, Ohtomo Y, Iijima K
**Journal:** CEN Case Rep (2020)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s13730-020-00500-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13730-020-00500-x)

## Content

Mutations in SLC4A1, encoding the chloride-bicarbonate exchanger known as anion exchanger 1, have been reported as the sole genetic cause of autosomal dominant distal renal tubular acidosis (dRTA). This disorder is extremely rare and most patients show no clinical symptoms during childhood. Here, we report a case of an infant with early-onset autosomal dominant dRTA caused by SLC4A1 mutation p.Gly609Arg that is detected as a hot spot world widely. Despite the fact that the patient's mother and sister had the same SLC4A1 mutation, all family members presented different clinical courses. A 9-month-old boy was referred to our hospital because of insufficient body weight gain. At the initial visit, his height and weight were 68.2 cm (-1.0 SD) and 6.4 kg (-2.2SD) respectively. Metabolic acidosis with a normal serum anion gap and inappropriate alkaline urine were detected. Abdominal ultrasound indicated bilateral renal medullary high-echoic lesions which suspected nephrocalcinosis. The genetic test revealed a heterozygous mutation c.1825G > A (p.Gly609Arg) in SLC4A1 that directed his diagnosis of autosomal dominant dRTA. The genetic test was performed on the patient's family members, indicating that the same SLC4A1 mutation was detected in his mother and sister. His mother had nephrocalcinosis and metabolic acidosis at the age of 35 years. However, his sister had no clinical symptoms at the age of 6 years without any laboratory abnormalities. This familial case demonstrated that the significant heterogeneity in clinical manifestations may develop even among familial members sharing the same variant.

Keywords: Autosomal dominant distal renal tubular acidosis, SLC4A1 mutation, Heterogeneity in clinical manifestations

Distal renal tubular acidosis (dRTA) is a disease of defective urinary acidification characterized by impaired H+ secretion into the urine leading to metabolic acidosis [1, 2]. This disorder is usually accompanied by inappropriately high urinary pH, normal plasma anion gap, hypokalemia, hypercalciuria, nephrocalcinosis, and/or nephrolithiasis [1–3]. dRTA is mainly caused by autoimmune and/or systemic diseases, or by drugs. Inversely, hereditary dRTA is rare. Mutations in SLC4A1, encoding the chloride–bicarbonate exchanger known as anion exchanger 1 (AE1), have been reported as the sole genetic cause of autosomal dominant dRTA [1, 2, 4] and also rarely cause autosomal recessive dRTA [5]. A renal form of AE1 (kAE1) is expressed in the basolateral membrane of the α-intercalated cells of the kidney collecting ducts and is involved in acid secretion [6].

Autosomal dominant dRTA was predominantly reported in Caucasians [7, 8], whereas only a few cases were seen in Asian countries [9–11]. The spectrum of clinical severity is very wide, and most patients with autosomal dominant dRTA present with nephrocalcinosis during adulthood and show no clinical symptoms in their childhood. Thereby few pediatric cases have been reported [12, 13]. Here, we report on a Japanese infant with early-onset autosomal dominant dRTA caused by the SLC4A1 mutation c.1825G > A (p.Gly609Arg). Although the patient’s mother and sister had the same SLC4A1 mutation, they had different clinical courses.

A 9-month-old boy was referred to our hospital because of insufficient body weight gain. He is the second child of non-consanguineous Japanese parents. He was born at full term with a birth weight of 2982 g. At the initial visit, his height and weight were 68.2 cm (−1.0 SD) and 6.4 kg (−2.2SD) respectively. Physical examination revealed normal results. Laboratory examination result is shown in Table 1. Metabolic acidosis with a normal serum anion gap and inappropriate alkaline urine was detected. His red cell morphology on a blood smear was normal without ovalocytosis or spherocytosis. Abdominal ultrasound showed bilateral renal medullary high-echoic lesions which suspected nephrocalcinosis. Based on these clinical symptoms and laboratory data, we diagnosed him with dRTA. After the initiation of bicarbonate and potassium supplementation, metabolic acidosis was successfully corrected and he showed improvement in his growth. Unfortunately, we did not investigate his urinary β2microglobulin during follow up.

Laboratory examination

His mother showed typical clinical features of dRTA, though she had a different clinical course from the patient. She had no clinical symptoms during childhood and also had no laboratory abnormalities on her first pregnancy at the age of 30 years old. Though she had no health checkup after first pregnancy, nephrocalcinosis was detected by chance at the age of 35 years old. Then metabolic acidosis and moderate chronic renal insufficiency were also identified. The clinical data of the patient and the affected family are shown in Table 2.

Based on the clinical symptoms and laboratory data of the patient and his mother, both were diagnosed as having autosomal dominant dRTA. A genetic test was performed including the patient’s family members. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. As a result, a heterozygous mutation c.1825G > A (p.Gly609Arg) in exon 15 of the SLC4A1 was detected in both the patient and his mother (Fig. 1). Because this mutation has been described as a mutational hot spot in SLC4A1 in patients with dRTA beyond races [8, 9], the patient and his mother were diagnosed as having familial dRTA with an autosomal dominant trait caused by an SLC4A1 mutation. The same mutation was also detected in his 6-year-old sister, though she had no clinical symptoms, no nephrocalcinosis, and no laboratory result abnormalities including metabolic acidosis.

Patient, his mother, and his sister were shown to carry the heterozygous mutation Gly609Arg in exon 15 of the SLC4A1

Although the present case showed relatively severe clinical symptoms of bilateral renal medullary high-echoic lesions which suspected nephrocalcinosis and failure to thrive, we suspected the possibility of having autosomal dominant dRTA because it has been identified that his mother exhibited a typical clinical feature of dRTA during her adulthood. His mother had no clinical symptoms or laboratory abnormalities on her first pregnancy. She was identified dRTA 5 years after and her disease might be progressed caused by environment factors such as delivery, smoking, and obesity. Although the phenotype of the patient and his mother was different, they had the same SLC4A1 mutation. Interestingly the patient’s older sister possessing the same mutation had no clinical symptoms or laboratory data abnormalities.

The clinical manifestations of autosomal dominant dRTA are generally milder than that of autosomal recessive dRTA. However, the spectrum of phenotypic severity of this disorder is considerably broad, and some studies presented cases with a severe phenotype [8–10]. However, the previous studies have shown that the affected individuals in the same family tend to exhibit the similar clinical manifestations [10, 11]. Shao et al. reported six patients from a Chinese family with severe phenotypes [10]. A proband and his affected three children had growth impairment, nephrocalcinosis, and renal insufficiency, severe in two cases and moderate in two. Two affected young cousins, aged 4 and 7 years old, also showed clinical features of dRTA, metabolic acidosis in the presence of inappropriately high urinary pH with absence of nephrocalcinosis or kidney dysfunction. Wang et al. reported a proband and his father having the same SLC4A1 mutation p.Arg388Cys [11]. They had a late clinical onset of the disease and presented with no signs of acidosis except intermittent hypokalemia, alkaline urine and hypercalciuria in adulthood. In this present case, a different clinical course was observed among the patient, his mother, and his sister despite all having the same SLC4A1 mutation.

Most cases of autosomal dominant dRTA caused by SLC4A1 mutations have been reported in Caucasians and only one report has described Japanese familial cases [8, 9]. This p.Gly609Arg mutation was first identified in a large Caucasian pedigree of patients with autosomal dominant dRTA [8]. In this report, the proband case developed dRTA at 6 years of age and affected family members of this particular kindred exhibited a striking degree of nephrocalcinosis and premature renal failure. The mechanism of autosomal dominant dRTA is due to the mutated kAE1 in the heterodimer, which induces a trafficking defect of wild-type kAE1, resulting in the so-called dominant negative effect. It was suggested that Gly609 in the seventh transmembrane domain played an important role in trafficking kAE1 to the correct cell surface compartment, and that the p.Gly609Arg mutation had a dominant negative effect that impaired trafficking and expression of AE1 on the basolateral membrane [14].

The first Japanese familial cases with autosomal dominant dRTA caused by the same SLC4A1 mutation p.Gly609Arg was reported in a patient and her mother who had the same clinical history of polydipsia, polyuria, and periodic paralysis caused by hypokalemia at 2 years of age [9]. Although the number of reported cases is too small to permit definite genotype–phenotype correlations, this mutation might be associated with relatively early onset in their childhood. In our case, although the proband case developed dRTA in early childhood, his affected mother developed dRTA in adulthood. His elder sister who had the same SLC4A1 mutation had no symptoms. She showed potential for developing dRTA in the future. After obtained the result of genetic testing, we proceeded to her follow-up by investigating physical, laboratory, and ultrasonography findings. Unfortunately, urinary β2microglobulin data was available only at the first visit. It is well known that urinary β2microglobulin is a good marker for efficacy of alkali therapy [15]. We will follow-up urinary β2microglobulin of patient and his sister.

Because autosomal dominant dRTA is a rare disorder, it is unclear how this phenotype variety in our familial case was caused. To know genotype–phenotype correlation in these rare inherited kidney diseases, it is generally recommended to conduct systematic review analysis using the data from previously reported cases. Actually, we have previously conducted systematic review analysis for autosomal recessive Alport syndrome, a very rare inherited kidney disease and proved this disease show very strong genotype–phenotype correlation [16]. It is also a good idea to conduct systematic review for dRTA to show positive or negative genotype–phenotype correlation. It is generally considered that autosomal dominant inherited diseases frequently show intra-familial phenotype variety. It is also observed in major inherited kidney diseases such as autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease or autosomal dominant Alport syndrome [17, 18]. The reason for this serious variety in autosomal dominant diseases is unclear. Autosomal dominant diseases tend to show milder phenotypes, so we speculate milder diseases are likely to be influenced by secondary factors such as environmental factors or existence of modifier genes.

In conclusion, we revealed the clinical variety in a family with autosomal dominant dRTA. We suggest that clinicians should take into account that even familial cases with autosomal dominant dRTA may show significant heterogeneity in clinical manifestations.
