Abstract  1
CANVAS caused by RFC1 biallelic expansions is a major cause of inherited sensory 2
neuronopathy. Detection of RFC1 expansion is challenging and CANVAS can be associated 3
with atypical features. 4
We clinically and genetically characterized 50 patients, selected based on the presence of sensory 5
neuronopathy confirmed by EMG. We screened RFC1 expansion by PCR, repeat -primed PCR, 6
and Southern blotting of long- range PCR products, a newly developed method. 7
Neuropathological characterization was performed on the brain and spinal cord of one patient. 8
Most patients (88%) carried a biallelic (AAGGG) n expansion in RFC1. In addition to the core 9
CANVAS phenotype (sensory neuronopathy, cerebellar syndrome, and vestibular impairment), 10
we observed chronic cough (97%), o culomotor signs (85%), motor neuron involvement (55%), 11
dysautonomia (50%), and parkinsonism (10%). Motor neuron involvement was found for 24 of 12
38 patients (63.1%). First motor neuron signs, such as brisk reflexes, extensor plantar responses, 13
and/or spasti city, were present in 29% of patients, second motor neuron signs, such as 14
fasciculations, wasting, weakness, or a neurogenic pattern on EMG in 18%, and both in 16%. 15
Mixed motor and sensory neuronopathy was observed in 19% of patients.  Among six non-RFC1 16
patients, one carried a heterozygous AAGGG expansion and a pathogenic variant in GRM1. 17
Neuropathological examination of one RFC1 patient with an enriched phenotype, including 18
parkinsonism, dysautonomia, and cognitive decline, showed posterior column and lumbar 19
posterior root atrophy. Degeneration of the vestibulospinal and spinocerebellar tracts was mild. 20
We observed marked astrocytic gliosis  and axonal swelling of the synapse between first and 21
second motor neurons in the anterior horn at the lumbar level. The cerebellum showed mild 22
depletion of Purkinje cells, with empty baskets, torpedoes, and astrogliosis characterized by a 23
disorganization of the Bergmann's radial glia. We found neuronal loss in the vagal nucleus. The 24
pars compacta of the substantia nigra was depleted, with widespread Lewy bodies in the locus 25
coeruleus, substantia nigra, hippocampus , entorhinal cortex, and amygdala.  26
We propose new guidelines for the screening of RFC1 expansion, considering different 27
expansion motifs. Here, we developed a ne w method  to more easily detect pathogenic RFC1 28
expansions. We report frequent motor neuron involvement and different neuronopathy subtypes. 29

Introduction  1
Sensory neuronopathies (or ganglionopathies) are a subgroup of sensory neuropathies caused by 2
the degeneration of neurons in the dorsal root ganglia, followed by secondary degeneration of 3
ascending ﬁbers of the posterior column in the spinal cord. The diagnosis of gangliono pathy is 4
based on nerve conduction studies that show severe impairment of sensory conduction in the 5
upper and lower limbs or its absence. The most frequently inherited forms are Friedreich ataxia 6
(OMIM #229300), caused by intronic GAA biallelic expansions in FXN; sensory ataxic 7
neuropathy, ptosis, and ophthalmoparesis (OMIM #607459), caused by POLG biallelic 8
pathogenic variants; and CANVAS syndrome, caused by an intronic AAGGG biallelic expansion 9
in RFC1.1 CANVAS is an adult -onset, slowly progressive disorder that shows autosomal 10
recessive transmission.2 Series of CANVAS patients of different ethnicity have been reported 3–6, 11
describing the phenotype. The evidence of novel repeat motifs, (ACAGG)exp
7, (AAGAG)exp
7, and 12
(AGAGG)exp
6, suggests a dynamic nature of the pentanucleotide expansion in the RFC1 gene.  13
The core phenotype associated with CANVAS includes sensory neuronopathy (constant), 14
cerebellar ataxia, and a reduced visually enhanced vestibulo -ocular reflex. Additional clinical 15
features include chronic cough, usually arising years before the onset of other symptoms, 16
abnormal eye movements, and cerebellar, cerebral, and spinal cord atrophy by MRI.4  17
Detection of the biallelic expansion in CANVAS is technically challenging because of (i) the 18
relatively high frequency of non -pathogenic expansions of (AAAAG) exp and (AAAGG) exp, and 19
probably other motifs, with a cumulative allelic frequency of approximately 24% in the general 20
population,1 and (ii) the l arge size of the pathogenic expansions, requiring Southern blotting of 21
genomic DNA to detect the two pathogenic alleles. The Southern -blot method is time -22
consuming, requires a significant amount of input DNA (≥ 5 µg), and is highly dependent on 23
DNA quality.  24
We aimed to clinically and genetically characterize 50 patients that we selected based on the 25
presence of sensory neuronopathy, confirmed by nerve conduction studies, and for whom 26
Friedreich ataxia and POLG-related disorders were excluded. We optimized the detection of 27
pathogenic expansions in the RFC1 gene and performed exome sequencing to identify other 28
genetic causes of ganglionopathy.  29

Materials and methods  1
Patients 2
We recruited 50 patients from 37 families with sensory neuronopathy from a historical case 3
database of ataxic patients without molecular diagnosis through the two National Reference 4
Centers for Rare Diseases in Paris, neurogenetic and neuromuscular ( n = 42), and in Lille, 5
neuromuscular (n =  8). Index cases were selected on the presence of  a sensory neuronopathy 6
based on an electrophysiological examination and a Camdessanché score >  6.5.8 All patients had 7
abolished or severely decreased sensory nerve action potential (SNAPs) in four limbs. Then, we 8
included apparently affected siblings too. All patients were Caucasian, except for two from 9
North Africa.  10
Patients were examined by a neurologist or geneticist with expertise in neurogenetics and/or 11
movement disorders (G.C, R.D, F.M, T.S, D.G, T.M, B.G, C.T, JB.D, ML.M, C.E, K.V, P.C, 12
I.LB, A.B, and A.D) and went through a diagnostic work up, including cerebral and spine MRI  13
(to exclude common differential diagnoses, i.e. cerebral small vessel disease, radiculopathy, 14
compressive myelopathy) and the exclusion of known causes of neuronopathy, such as vitamin 15
B12 or E deficiency, toxic medications, and immune conditions. Dysimmu ne neuropathy was 16
excluded when the clinical history was indolent or when other necessary workup was negative 17
(CSF analysis and plexus MRI).  18
These patients without causal diagnosis were highly suspected to suffer from a sensory 19
neuronopathy caused by a ge netic disease. GAA expansions in the FXN gene were ruled out for 20
all patients using short -florescent PCR and triplet -primed PCR. Screening of POLG was 21
performed for those with cerebellar syndrome using Sanger or next-generation sequencing (panel 22
for 359 at axia genes, Supplementary Methods). We have performed an extensive molecular 23
screening according to the clinical features and/or familial history including repeat expansion in 24
SCA genes, mitochondrial DNA analyses and different targeted sequencing using ne xt 25
generation sequencing and/or exome sequencing.  26

Oculomotor recording 15
Participants were seated in a dark, silent room, with their head held in place by chin-level and 16
forehead supports. Horizontal and vertical movements of both eyes were reco rded with a video -17
based eye tracker (Eyebrain eye tracker) at a sampling rate of 300 Hz, allowing the accurate 18
analysis of saccades. Paradigms consisted of a visually guided saccade task (reflexive saccades 19
triggered toward a suddenly presented visual targ et), an anti-saccade task (saccade performed in 20
the opposite direction of a visual target), and a horizontal smooth pursuit task, in which the 21
subject was asked to accurately follow a target moving with a sine wave motion at 12 and 25°/s 22
maximum velocities. We recorded saccade latency, amplitude, and velocity, the accuracy of both 23
centrifugal and centripetal saccades, the percentage of errors ( i.e., saccades initially triggered 24
towards the target) in the anti -saccade task, pursuit abnormalities, the presenc e and type of 25
nystagmus, and the presence of square wave jerks. A visually enhanced vestibulo -ocular reflex 26
(VVOR) was sought only for the group of patients for whom vestibular impairment was 27
suspected.  28

RFC1 analysis 1
The identification of biallelic AAGGG expansions in RFC1 was performed as presented in the 2
flowchart (Fig. 1).  3
We performed PCR with fluorescent primers as described previously 1, with the following 4
exceptions: the forward primer was fluorescently -labelled with 6 -carboxyfluorescein (6 -FAM) 5
and the PCR premix included 10% DMSO. After PCR amplification, the length of the amplified 6
PCR products was analyzed by capillary electrophoresis on an ABI3730 DNA analyzer (Applied 7
Biosystems®, Saint-Aubin, France). We considered an allele to be normal if the PCR product 8
consisted of a single peak without PCR slippage, a fluorescence intensity > 500 arbitrary units, 9
and a length of 348 bp ( i.e. reference allele (AAAAG) 11) ± 10 bp, which corresponds to normal 10
alleles with 9 to 13 pentanucleotide (AAAAG) repeat units.  11
In the absence of amplification, suggesting the presence of two expanded alleles, we performed 12
repeat-primed PCR  (RP-PCR), as previously described 1, to detect the presence of at least one 13
pathogenic (AAGGG) n motif. Then, we confirmed  the presence of a homozygous expanded 14
pathogenic allele (AAGGG) n by Southern blotting of long -range PCR (LR -PCR) products, 15
similarly to other methods commonly used for the molecular diagnosis of other repeat -expansion 16
diseases.11 We adapted the LR -PCR reported by Cortese et al. 1 adding agarose gel 17
electrophoresis of LR -PCR products, capillary transfer to a nylon membrane, and Southern 18
blotting of long-range PCR-products (Fig. 2). 19

We report a cohort of patients with sensory neuronopathy without causal diagnosis and found 1

extensive molecular screening. The high rate of patients with biallelic RFC1 expansion does not 4
represent the proportion of CANVAS in sensory neuropathy or ataxic patients. It is not 5
surprising to find RFC1 as the most frequent diagnosis of sensory neuronopathy in our cohort, 6
given the high frequency of heterozygotes for the expan ded (AAGGG) exp allele in the healthy 7
population (0.7%)1, similar to the frequency of the intronic GAA expanded allele in FXN. The 8
rate of the heterozygous expanded (AAGGG) exp allele in our cohort was 2% (1/50 patients). 9
Akçimen et al, report a frequency of ≈4% in 163 healthy controls, whereas 15.8% patients from 10
three ataxia cohorts were heterozygous for the pathogenic expansion (AAGGG)exp.6  11
In addition to the usual CANVAS phenotype, we also identified a frequent feature, consisting of 12
motor neuron involvement, present in 63% of our RFC1 patients, which was not previously 13
identified4,5,14,17,despite the fact that motor neuropathy was recently reported for 18/45 (40%) 14

deep tendon re flexes were normal (~70% at upper limbs and ~ 45% at knee) or even brisk 20

fasciculations as the first signs. In CANVAS, motor conduction is frequently normal, but a 22
moderate decrease in motor action potentials can be observed with preserved muscle strength. 3,19 23
