Prune belly syndrome (PBS) is characterized by the triad of abdominal flaccidity, a variable degree of urinary tract involvement and cryptorchidism. Most cases of PBS are sporadic and have a normal karyotype, with 95% patients being male. In the last decade, mutations in known genes that regulate embryonic genitourinary myogenesis have been identified and with increasing knowledge of these critical genes involved in bladder maldevelopment, advances can be made in genetic counseling. A multidisciplinary approach is necessary and individualization of care is recommended according to phenotypic severity. Some patients require abdominal and urinary tract reconstruction while others require as little as bilateral orchiopexies. Major treatment objectives are: preservation of renal function and upper urinary tract; polyuria management; adequate bladder emptying; improvement of corporal image and quality of life; preservation of fertility and adequate sexual function. Long-term surveillance of the urinary tract is essential up to adulthood, because functional dynamics can change over time.

Keywords:prune belly, anatomy, genetics, management, urinary tract reconstruction

Prune belly syndrome (PBS) is characterized by the triad of 1) lax “prune-like” abdominal wall secondary to deficient abdominal wall skeletal musculature, 2) urinary tract distension from dysfunctional smooth muscle, and 3) intra-abdominal testes1,2. Associated anomalies include the orthopedic, pulmonary and gastrointestinal systems1,2, affecting over 60% of cases3,4. There is a wide spectrum of severity of the syndrome, particularly regarding the urinary tract, with some patients not surviving the newborn period and others being minimally affected1,2. Fortunately, most of these children have normal cognition, but overall, their life prognosis depends on the severity of lung and renal dysfunction. The evaluation, management and survival of PBS patients have significantly improved due to better knowledge of the genetics and physiopathology of the syndrome and improved protocols of care1,2.

Incidence of PBS varies from 1 in 29,000 to 40,000 live births, with 95% occurring in males1,2. In the US, it has been estimated that there are about 1,500 living PBS cases under the age of 18 years5. The rare females with PBS have abdominal wall deficiency and urinary tract abnormalities without any gonadal anomaly1,2. In developed countries, the incidence appears to be declining, mostly due to prenatal diagnosis and pregnancy termination2. There are no known environmental causes for PBS.

Historically, there have been two major theories for the embryonic maldevelopment of PBS. The first theory proposes an earlyin uteroposterior urethral obstruction resulting in severe dilation of urinary tract and possible fetal ascites and oligohydramnios6. However, this theory does not explain all extra-genitourinary PBS manifestations and bladder outlet obstruction is not universally documented in infant autopsies cases of PBS1,2.

In a second theory, a primary embryonic mesodermal arrest occurs due to altered gene regulation of myogenesis. Maldevelopment of the somatic and splanchnic mesoderm results in dysplastic or absent muscle of the abdominal wall, urinary tract, and gubernaculum7. Dilation of the urinary tract occurs without anatomical obstruction, but massive hydronephrosis or ureteral kinking may further impair urinary drainage. The problem with this hypothesis is that the key regulatory drivers of myogenesis and the details of the genetic dysregulation were previously unknown. However, now there is new information.

Most cases of PBS are sporadic and have a normal karyotype8–11. Nevertheless, several facts support a genetic basis to PBS. Approximately 95% of PBS cases are male. Even though most published twin cases have been discordant for PBS, there have been rare concordant twins with PBS2. The strongest evidence is found in numerous publications that have reported multiplex families with two or more affected individuals with PBS12. Despite the decreased survival and diminished reproductive fitness associated with PBS, by studying these multiplex families with new array copy number variation and next generation sequencing methodologies, there have recently been DNA mutations (aka genetic variants) identified that cause PBS or megabladder.

In the last 5 to 10 years, there have been 14 genes identified as crucial to normal bladder embryonic development and that cause megabladder when mutated (Table 1andSupplemental Table 1). These genes encode smooth muscle cell transcription factors, contractile filament proteins or enzymes, cytoskeletal regulatory proteins, and neural enzymes or receptors. Two key examples of novel PBS genes are myocardin (MYOCD) and filamin A (FLNA).

In 2019, Houwelinget al.11published a series of 4 unrelated multiplex families (7 affected males from 3 families) with megabladder harboring heterozygous truncation or missense mutations or microdeletion in the MYOCD gene. Carrier females were unaffected. One compound heterozygous female had megabladder and several congenital heart defects. In addition, the authors showed two genetically engineered compound heterozygous mutant mouse models with altered MYOCD production that had the megabladder phenotype. MYOCD is a critical smooth- and cardiac-specific master regulatory cofactor of serum response factor that binds promoters and activates transcription of many smooth muscle genes, leading to vascular and visceral smooth muscle cell differentiation in during embryonic development.

Baker and colleagues identified an interesting multiplex family wherein the mother had two spontaneous miscarriages and two living children with PBS fathered by two different fathers. This strongly suggested X chromosome linkage. In addition to PBS, both young males had facial anomalies, micrognathia, bilateral cleft palate, clinodactyly with hypoplastic distal phalanges, and foot and toe anomalies called ‘tree frog feet’, consistent with otopalatodigital spectrum disorder. Their mother was more mildly affected with a facial dysmorphism. Expanding the pedigree deeper showed additional individuals with these facial features, two additional infant deaths with possible PBS, and several spontaneous miscarriages always on the maternal side of the family. Whole exome sequencing of the mother and her two sons identified a novel missense point mutation in FLNA, which is located on human chromosome Xq28. After identifying these 2 males with ‘Syndromic’ PBS caused by FLNA mutation, we searched and found 2 additional individuals with ‘Isolated’ PBS and different novel FLNA mutations9. FLNA is a critical regulatory and structural protein in the cell membrane-bound focal adhesion complex of a smooth muscle cell. It is highly expressed in differentiating detrusor smooth muscle cells during embryogenesis. While one end of the FLNA protein binds the contracting actin and myosin filaments, the other end binds or releases beta-integrins in the focal adhesion complex. The identified PBS FLNA mutations cause FLNA protein to be stuck bound to beta-integrin, which at the molecular level, may make a smooth muscle cell feel chronically stretched and unable to contract9.

However, the vast majority of PBS cases remain genetically unexplained. This research is still in the early phases and additional study subjects are still needed. Nevertheless, now we can definitively conclude that some PBS cases are caused by human mutations in known genes that regulate embryonic genitourinary myogenesis (Table 1). By identifying these critical genes in bladder maldevelopment, advances can be made in genetic counseling, permitting fetal screening of megabladder cases for DNA variants in these genes and additional megabladder/PBS genes discovered in the future. It will require longitudinal studies to obtain statistically sound data for genotype-phenotype correlations, segregation analyses in kindreds, and pregnancy recommendations. Since other organs contain smooth and cardiac muscle and can be adversely affected by these DNA mutations, future research will be needed to create far-reaching recommendations for medical and radiological screening for early detection of major genetic complications outside of the GU tract. While targeted drug therapies and even gene therapies are feasible to modulate or cure this disorder, presently these strategies are still in the future. This field will need to mature more with knowledge so that we can target specific mutated genes for therapeutics.

The single most important determinant of long-term survival is the severity of the urinary tract involvement, particularly renal dysplasia. The spectrum of renal abnormalities extends from normal renal parenchyma to hydronephrosis and renal dysplasia, which is present in 50% of cases, varying in degree and laterality13. The renal collecting system is characteristically dilated, often to a severe degree, that may not correlate with degree of renal dysplasia or obstruction. The ureters are typically dilated, tortuous and redundant, with the distal segments usually more affected than the proximal segments, although kinking and functional obstruction may occur at all levels, including the ureteropelvic and ureterovesical junctions. Histologic ureteral evaluation demonstrates decrease and dysplasia of smooth muscle cells and increase in fibrous connective tissue, both contributing to the poor peristalsis13. Vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) is present in 75% of children with PBS2,13.

The megabladder is usually enlarged by 2-8 times normal capacity without being obstructed, usually presenting a urachal pseudodiverticulum. The urachus is patent at birth in 25% to 30% of cases13. The bladder wall is thick but smooth, with increased ratio of collagen to muscle fibers. While the normal bladder detrusor consists of fascicles of smooth muscles with intervening bands of connective tissue, the PBS detrusor has lost all organization of fascicles and on electron microscopy the smooth muscle cells are separated from each other by a thick sea of collagen. The bladder neck opens widely into a dilated prostatic urethra1,2. Urodynamic assessment generally shows large capacity and normal to increased compliance, with a delayed first sensation to void14. The ability to empty the bladder is variable, about 50% of patients voiding spontaneously with normal pressures and flow rates, presenting low post-void residuals, while others have significant residuals15.

Posterior urethral dilation is due to prostatic hypoplasia, related to abnormal mesenchymal-epithelial development13. The anterior urethra is usually normal, but atresia, hypoplasia and megalourethra have been reported16.

Bilateral intra-abdominal testes with wispy or absent gubernaculum are usually found lying over the iliac vessels and adjacent to the dilated ureters17. Microscopically, they do not differ from non-syndromic intra-abdominal testes17,18. No PBS patient has been reported so far to father a child spontaneously. Nonpaternity is the rule, caused by impaired spermatogenesis and retrograde ejaculation19. The association of anatomical malformations such as hypoplastic prostate and seminal vesicle abnormalities also precludes normal sperm production19. However, usingin vitrofertility techniques, 4 children were born after sperm retrieval from PBS patients19.

The abdomen has characteristic but variable degrees of flaccidity (Figure 1), with bulging in the flanks, being covered with wrinkled, redundant skin. Some have extreme abdominal wall laxity which permits the displacement of intra-abdominal organs into what is essentially a massive ventral abdominal wall hernia disrupting abdominal function of Valsalva and causing cosmetic disfigurement. With growth, the abdomen becomes less wrinkled and takes on a pot-bellied appearance in the standing position1,2. The poor support of the lower chest wall is often associated with flaring of the costal margin1.

Besides the obvious abdominal wall defect, 60-75% of the PBS patients present other abnormalities1,2,4. Furthermore, nearly 50% of these children are born premature, which significantly contributes to comorbidities1,2,5. Orthopedic abnormalities, ranging in incidence from 30% to 45%, are second in frequency to those of the genitourinary tract and abdominal wall1,2. Many accompany the compressive effects of oligohydramnios, such as the frequent dimpling of the lateral aspect of the knees, talipes equinovarus (26%), hip dysplasia (5%), and congenital scoliosis (4%)1. Pulmonary hypoplasia results from oligohydramnios secondary to renal dysplasia or to functional bladder obstruction, eventually causing newborn demise. Additionally, pneumothorax and pneumomediastinum can present with or without hypoplasia. In nearly half of PBS newborns, intubation and ventilatory support are required. As the abdominal muscles are accessory respiratory muscles of expiration, the diminished ability to generate significant intra-abdominal pressure, decreasing cough effectiveness, may contribute to pneumonia and lobar atelectasis, with frequent pulmonary difficulties in PBS survivors1,2. With limited intra-abdominal pressure and megacolon, constipation also becomes a lifelong problem1,2.

PBS presents prenatally by fetal ultrasound with findings common to bladder outlet obstruction, as in posterior urethral valves or megacystis-megaureter syndrome1,2. Antenatal intervention is justified in the rare situations of urethral atresia with progressive oligohydramnios or when decompression of the urinary tract is necessary to prevent dystocia1,2.

A classification system which scored PBS children into one of three categories of phenotypic severity in the neonatal period was proposed by Woodard and has been used for management of these children1,20.Category Irepresents neonates who experienced marked oligohydramnios secondary to severe renal dysplasia or functional bladder obstruction, with resultant pulmonary hypoplasia and Potter’s features. Those in this category who are not stillborn commonly succumb within a few days of life to pulmonary hypoplasia or somewhat later to renal failure. Approximately 20% of newborns with PBS die in the perinatal period1.Category IIpatients present the full triad spectrum with variable unilateral or bilateral dysplasia and hydroureteronephrosis, with eventual impairment of renal function. Pulmonary hypoplasia may occur but is not a prominent feature. The clinical course can be the stabilization of renal function at or somewhat below normal, but the frequent urinary tract abnormalities, associated with impaired ureteral and vesical drainage, polyuria, VUR and recurrent urinary tract infection (UTI), impose further deterioration of renal function.Category IIIpatients present mild or incomplete forms of PBS, with lesser degrees or even absence of significant renal dysplasia, hydroureteronephrosis and VUR, as well as normal pulmonary function. They usually have adequate bladder emptying, that prevents recurrent UTI, and remain with stable renal function without polyuria1,20.

In order to more specifically grade the severity of living patients in Woodard categories II and III, Wonget alpublished in 2019 a new PBS phenotypic severity scoring system3.Table 2displays the rubric for the RUBACE scoring system. The term RUBACE is an acronym derived from the six sub-scores of the categories. “RUBAC” stands for renal, ureter, bladder, abdominal wall, and cryptorchidism. Each of these categories have their own maximum score that can be achieved. “E” stands for extra-genitourinary, which can be scored based on the organ system affected or by existing congenital syndrome or associations. The score from each of the six subcategories is summed to yield the RUBACE score which can range from 5-31. Most of the 50 test cases were in the mild to moderate PBS severity category3. Using the RUBACE scoring system on 50 PBS cases, we segregated PBS into 3 categories, including 1) ‘Isolated’ PBS (76%), 2) ‘Syndromic’ PBS that included known syndromic or genetic conditions (i.e. Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy, Pierre Robin Sequence, VACTERL, or Megacystis, microcolon, hypoperistalsis syndrome [MMIHS]) (12%), and 3) ‘PBS Plus’ that included other unusual conditions (i.e. hepatoblastoma, hearing loss, infantile stroke with optic nerve atrophy, early gout with type 2 diabetes mellitus and pancreatic atrophy, or mydriasis) (10%). The overarching goal of the new scoring system is to group similar cases to genetically identify the cause of PBS, to perform genotype-phenotype correlative studies, to potentially monitor for patient deterioration with aging, and to permit subgrouping of PBS phenotypes to match severity groups for surgical and medical treatment outcome analyses.

Caring for patients with PBS requires a large multi-disciplinary team approach to help these children thrive, gain weight, and be prepared for urological surgery if needed. Pediatric urologists often must lead the team and make referrals. Physical therapists can fit PBS children for trunk compressive garments that can improve Valsalva activities and support trunk core musculature so the child can sit up and develop fine motor skills. Pediatric pulmonologists can help prevent recurrent debilitating pneumonia by medications and mechanical airway clearance devices (high-frequency chest wall oscillation). Pediatric nephrological evaluation and care is important to help stabilize and preserve the renal function. Pediatric gastroenterologists and dieticians are critical to maximize intestinal motility and minimize constipation to facilitate proper nutrition and growth. Older children may require orthopedic and psychiatric evaluation and treatment.

There is little disagreement that in category I PBS patients nothing beyond bladder drainage and supportive care is justifiable, because their fate cannot be altered1. At the other end of the spectrum, category III patients rarely need urinary tract intervention, requiring only regular monitoring of the urinary tract. Occasional intervention is reserved to the few patients who present urinary tract deterioration, related to repeated UTI, stasis or VUR1.

The optimal management of Woodard category II / high RUBACE scoring patients is controversial, as they require individualized management due to variable degrees of severity of each facet of PBS1–3. Aggressive surgical intervention was derived from early reports of poor prognosis for category II infants as a group, with more than 80% mortality with or without surgical intervention, due to UTI and progressive renal insufficiency1. As a result, surgical reconstruction of the urinary tract in cases of severe hydroureteronephrosis or VUR with recurrent UTI and impaired renal function was advised around 6 to 18 months of age to improve the fate of these children1,21.

Urinary tract reconstruction includes resection of the redundant or refluxive distal ureter, preserving the usually less dilated proximal segment, followed by vesicoureteral reimplantation. Occasionally, tailoring or infolding of this segment still is necessary for adequate reimplantation into the abnormal bladder with anti-reflux techniques1,21. Although rarely, unilateral dysplastic or hydronephrotic kidneys with severely reduced function may be removed. Reduction cystoplasty is justified to eliminate a large urachal diverticulum22. In some patients, particularly those with polyuria, elevated bladder residuals are treacherous and a trial of tamsulosin may facilitate bladder emptying. Triple timed voiding in those volitionally voiding can be extremely effective. However, if these maneuvers fail, intermittent catheterization will ensure complete bladder emptying, until they are able to achieve better voiding pressures with age, therefore an appendico-vesicostomy may be recommended2. In patients who underwent earlier ureteral or vesical diversion, the reconstruction may be equally performed, if anatomical and functional stabilization of the upper tract has been achieved2. This approach has been successful in achieving anatomic and functional improvement, evidenced by stable creatinine values and radiographic studies, as well as reduced incidence of UTI1. Urinary tract reconstruction can be done in conjunction with orchiopexy, abdominoplasty and circumcision, without increasing the morbidity of the procedure. In an institutional experience with a median follow-up of 143 months, 35 patients who underwent such reconstruction maintained normal creatinine levels, while only five demonstrated moderate to severe renal insufficiency, with three of these requiring renal transplantation23.

An alternative approach with limited surgical intervention has also been employed. Proponents advocate close surveillance with medical management of bacteriuria and recommend surgical intervention only in patients with proven obstruction or intractable UTI. VUR is also managed conservatively, except in cases of high-grade reflux, as spontaneous improvement in ureteral appearance, function and grade of reflux can occur with somatic growth and elongation of the ureters. Success with this approach has been reported24,25. Woodhouseet al. reviewed a series of PBS patients managed conservatively from infancy up to 24 years, most remaining well with normal voiding patterns and stable renal function, with few UTIs24. Certainly, patients in category III and some of category II are candidates for conservative management. Furthermore, some patients with gross anatomical abnormalities of the urinary collecting system survived decades without medical attention2.

The paucity of long-term data for category II patients, the variation in assignment of disease severity in treatment groups, and the variable natural history of the disease make comparison of these retrospective studies difficult. Yet progressive uronephropathy is well known in PBS patients and many ultimately require renal transplantation26.

In patients of categories II and III, cryptorchidism requires correction during the first year of life. Transabdominal bilateral orchiopexy around 6 to 12 months of age is currently considered the approach of choice, as dictated by the current understanding of early treatment of intra-abdominal testes2. Nevertheless, the procedure can be anticipated or somewhat delayed, if it is to be performed concomitantly to other necessary surgical procedures2,27. Without the need for other abdominal surgeries, one or two-step orchiopexy can be also accomplished laparoscopically28.

Children with mild degrees of abdominal muscular deficiency may show improvement in the abdominal wall laxity as they mature, but many patients of group II and some of group III will benefit from abdominoplasty, if significant flaccidity persists or worsens during childhood, leaving them with a psychologically crippling defect29. Smith and colleagues demonstrated improved bladder emptying following abdominal wall reconstruction30. Further positive effects include more effective cough and defecation1. Abdominal wall reconstruction can be associated to other surgical interventions, namely urinary tract reconstruction and orchiopexy1,2. If upper tract remodeling is not anticipated, the abdominal wall can be addressed at any time, having been performed as early as 6 months of age1. There are several static fascial plication techniques for open abdominoplasty in PBS patients31–33. Open or laparoscopic abdominal wall plication has also been described in patients without need for laparotomy34,35. A dynamic abdominoplasty incorporating simultaneous fascial plication and bilateral pedicled rectus femoris muscle transfers has been reported to give functional improvement36.

The nadir creatinine value less than 0.7 mg/dL is a useful predictor of stable long-term renal function during childhood in PBS patients, unless there is further renal compromise by pyelonephritis, obstruction or stasis26,37. Polyuria also plays a significant role in the destabilization of the urinary tract, by progressively increasing ureteral dilatation and post-void bladder residuals37. The importance of urinary tract monitoring by imaging and creatinine values, with prompt treatment of progressive urinary tract dilatation cannot be overemphasized. Unfortunately, the risk of UTI is constant in the setting of urinary tract dilation and stasis. Up to 40% of patients, particularly those with impaired renal function at initial evaluation, develop chronic renal failure during childhood or adolescence38. Renal transplantation may be necessary for these patients, to ensure normal growth and development. Success with transplantation in PBS patients is equal to other age-matched groups2.

Post-pubertal PBS patients undergoing orchiopexy in childhood have normal sex hormone levels19,39. Sexual function with normal erections and orgasm is the rule, but retrograde ejaculation is common39. Regarding fertility, in a study with adult patients who underwent early orchiopexy and urinary tract reconstruction, 62.5% had spermatozoa retrieved in the ejaculate or post-masturbation urine sample, with 50% presenting motile spermatozoa19. With the advances of assisted-reproduction technologies, these findings might translate in an enhanced paternity chance for these patients, making deeper understanding of the genetics of this disorder vitally important.

Significant advances have been made in understanding the etiology, genetics and pathophysiology of PBS. The overall outlook for survival and quality of life for the PBS patient improved considerably due to advances in their management2. Individualization of care is recommended, because some patients require abdominal and urinary tract reconstruction while others require as little as bilateral orchiopexies2. Long-term surveillance of the urinary tract is essential up to adulthood, because functional dynamics can change over time. Therefore, PBS children and caregivers suffer a significant impact in their lifestyle due to the requirements and limitations imposed by the syndrome40.
