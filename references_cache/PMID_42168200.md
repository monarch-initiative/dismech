# Macrophage ALDH2 drives immunotherapy resistance by silencing CXCL9 through metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk.
**Authors:** Fang H, Sun J, Xiao Q, Ren H, Chen Y, Liu J, Song D, Chen T, Wu L, Wang P, Yue J, Wang X, Yang Q, Wei Y, Du H, Peng W, Qiu Z, Zhang L, Wan J, Liu T, Ren G, Li H
**Journal:** Nat Commun (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41467-026-73234-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-73234-6)

## Content

The metabolic basis of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)-driven immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) resistance remains poorly understood. Here, in patients with immunotherapy-resistant cancer, we identify significant enrichment of TAMs marked by elevated aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) expression. Myeloid-restricted ALDH2 ablation converts TAMs from a pro-tumorigenic phenotype to immunostimulatory regulators, concomitantly amplifying CD8+ T cell infiltration and cytotoxicity to improve ICB responsiveness. Mechanistically, ALDH2 deficiency induces the intracellular accumulation of reactive aldehydes, specifically 4-hydroxynonenal, which activates the PI3K-AKT signaling axis. This pathway phosphorylates and suppresses EZH2 methyltransferase activity, leading to the erosion of H3K27me3-mediated epigenetic silencing at CXCL9 promoter region. Subsequent CXCL9 derepression in TAMs facilitates persistent CD8+ T cell infiltration and enhances their cytotoxic effector functions. Clinical validation confirms that pronounced ALDH2 elevation in TAMs correlates with accelerated immunotherapy failure. Therapeutically, as a clinically approved ALDH2 inhibitor, disulfiram exerts its anti-tumor effect by selective reprogramming TAMs metabolism. Overall, our findings delineate a druggable ALDH2-metabolism-epigenetics axis in antitumor immunity, nominating ALDH2 inhibition for combination immunotherapy.

Cancer immunotherapy has redefined oncological therapeutics through its capacity to mobilize endogenous antitumor immunity, with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) targeting cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) and programmed death 1 (PD-1)/PD ligand 1 (PD-L1) pathways standing as transformative clinical advancements1,2. Yet primary and adaptive resistance critically limit therapeutic efficacy in most patients3. This resilience stems from multidimensional crosstalk between neoplastic cells and tumor microenvironment (TME) constituents, necessitating mechanistic dissection of immune-evasive pathways to develop next-generation combinatorial strategies4,5.

As principal immune regulators within the TME, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) exhibit remarkable functional plasticity shaped by local cytokine gradients, metabolic intermediates, and epigenetic reprogramming6. TAM populations dynamically shift between immunostimulatory and immunosuppressive states, with their polarization status dictating clinical responses to immunotherapy7. Immunosuppressive TAMs promote immune evasion through multiple mechanisms, including secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β to establish an immunosuppressive microenvironment, expression of coinhibitory ligands that drive T cell exhaustion, and chemokine-mediated exclusion of cytotoxic lymphocytes8,9. Our prior work revealed that histamine-HRH1 signaling in TAMs reinforces immune resistance via VISTA upregulation10, further underscoring the immunosuppressive potential of TAMs. Despite these advances, the molecular logic governing TAM-mediated immune evasion remains incompletely resolved.

Aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) is a mitochondrial enzyme that oxidizes aldehydes produced during ethanol metabolism and lipid peroxidation, thereby limiting the accumulation of reactive aldehydic species11. While mounting evidence implicates ALDH2 in tumor progression, current understanding centers predominantly on cell-autonomous tumor cell effects, leaving its roles within the TME and antitumor immunity undefined12–14.

In this work, we show that ALDH2 is predominantly expressed in TAMs, particularly in immunotherapy-nonresponsive tumors. Functionally, the ALDH2-mediated aldehyde metabolism pathway epigenetically suppresses the expression of the pivotal chemokine C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9), thereby promoting macrophage polarization toward a tumor-promoting phenotype and establishing an immunosuppressive milieu conducive to tumor growth and ICB resistance. These findings underscore the substantial role of ALDH2 in the TME and position it as a potential target for cancer therapy.

To elucidate the differential characteristics of the immune microenvironment between ICB-resistant and ICB-sensitive malignancies, we conducted single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis on pretreatment tumor biopsies from 10 triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) patients undergoing anti-PD1 therapy. Patients were stratified into nonresponders (n = 6) and responders (n = 4) based on RECIST 1.1 criteria. This differential distribution was found to be particularly pronounced within macrophage subpopulations (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). To validate these findings across tumor types, we analyzed scRNA-seq datasets from skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) patients treated with anti-PD1 or anti-CTLA4 agents. To delineate molecular drivers of TAM-mediated immunotherapy resistance, we analyzed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in TAMs from nonresponders versus responders to ICB in our in-house TNBC cohort. Notably, ALDH2 emerged as significantly upregulated in nonresponder TAMs (Fig. 1e). To deconvolute macrophage heterogeneity, we performed unsupervised clustering across myeloid populations, resolving 9 transcriptionally distinct monocyte/macrophage subclusters (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The ALDH2+ macrophage subset co-expressed immunosuppressive markers (ACP5, F13A1, and NUPR115–17; Supplementary Fig. 3b) and was substantially enriched in nonresponsive tumors (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). To further validate this finding, we analyzed a public single-cell dataset of pre-treatment TNBC samples from patients who received anti-PD-L1 therapy. Re-annotation and sub-clustering of the macrophage compartment identified 11 distinct subpopulations (Fig. 1g). Consistent with our internal TNBC cohort, the proportion of ALDH2⁺ macrophages was significantly higher in nonresponders than in responders (Fig. 1h). Furthermore, analysis of a public melanoma cohort revealed that the proportion of ALDH2+ macrophage subclusters was markedly enriched in nonresponders (Fig. 1i), reinforcing the association between ALDH2+ TAMs and immunotherapy resistance. Consistent with these findings, in additional melanoma cohorts treated with anti-PD1 or anti-CTLA4/PD1 therapies18,19, patients with high ALDH2+ macrophage infiltration demonstrated elevated rates of progressive disease (PD) and reduced frequencies of complete response (CR)/partial response (PR) relative to patients with diminished ALDH2+ macrophage infiltration (Supplementary Fig. 3e). Furthermore, low ALDH2+ macrophage levels in these cohorts correlated with significantly prolonged overall survival relative to high-infiltrated counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 3f).

To further validate the findings from human immunotherapy samples in animal models and to verify their generalizability across multiple cancer types, we performed parallel assessments in two immunocompetent murine models. Analysis of the anti-PD1-treated EO771 mammary carcinoma and anti-CTLA4-exposed MC38 colorectal adenocarcinoma models demonstrated augmented TAM accumulation in therapy-resistant tumors compared to responsive counterparts (Fig. 1j, k). Flow cytometric quantification in the two models confirmed enhanced ALDH2 expression in TAMs from ICB-resistant tumors (Fig. 1l, m). Cross-validation in the MC38 immunotherapy model through RNA sequencing of sorted TAMs revealed conserved upregulation of Aldh2 among nonresponders (Supplementary Fig. 3g). This multi-platform investigation consistently demonstrates TAM accumulation as a hallmark of ICB resistance.

To systematically validate the macrophage-specific expression pattern of ALDH2, we conducted multi-platform analyses across computational and experimental systems. Utilizing the tumor immune estimation resource database, we observed significant negative correlations between ALDH2 expression and tumor purity, coupled with positive associations with macrophage marker CD68 in both breast cancer (BRCA) and SKCM (Fig. 2a). Parallel analysis of TNBC, SKCM and COAD scRNA-seq datasets confirmed ALDH2’s cell-type specificity, with dominant expression in monocyte/macrophage versus other leukocyte subsets (Fig. 2b–d). Complementing these findings, flow cytometry of EO771 and MC38 tumors demonstrated significantly elevated ALDH2 expression in macrophages relative to tumor cells and other immune populations (Fig. 2e, f). Multiplex immunofluorescence (IF) analysis of clinical TNBC specimens demonstrated marked enrichment of ALDH2 expression in CD68+ TAMs versus CD68⁻ stromal compartments (Fig. 2g). Consistent with these phenotypic observations, comparative western blot assays revealed significantly elevated ALDH2 protein levels in macrophage populations compared to tumor cell lines (Fig. 2h). It is noteworthy that compared to TAMs, resident macrophages in the spleen, lung, liver, and brain exhibit relatively lower expression levels of ALDH2 (Supplementary Fig. 3h), indicating that elevated ALDH2 is associated with the TME. To investigate the functional role of ALDH2 in macrophage polarization and tumor immunity, we established three syngeneic tumor models (EO771 mammary carcinoma, MC38 colorectal adenocarcinoma, and B16-F10 melanoma) in wild-type (WT) and Aldh2 knockout (Aldh2-/-) mice. Across all models, Aldh2-/- TAMs exhibited elevated MHCII (M1 marker)/CD206 (M2 marker) ratios compared to WT counterparts (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 4a, b), indicating that Aldh2 deficiency promotes macrophage repolarization toward antitumor M1-like phenotypes. This immunomodulatory shift was accompanied by enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration in Aldh2-/- tumors versus WT controls (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 4c, d), without concomitant significant changes in the abundance of stromal cells, specifically cancer-associated fibroblasts, or other immune cell populations (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). This was coupled with a concomitant upregulation of cytotoxic effector molecules (IFN-γ, TNF-α) in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 4g, h). To dissect cell-autonomous effects of ALDH2 on CD8+ T cell function, we performed comparative functional profiling of WT versus Aldh2-/- CD8+ T cells. No genotype-dependent differences were observed in migratory capacity, proliferative kinetics, or direct cytotoxic activity (Supplementary Fig. 4k), excluding intrinsic T cell mechanisms as primary mediators of enhanced antitumor responses. Importantly, CD8+ T cell depletion via neutralizing antibodies abrogated the tumor growth inhibition phenotype in Aldh2-/- mice (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 4l), mechanistically anchoring ALDH2-mediated immunomodulation to CD8+ T cell effector dependency.

To establish the macrophage-specific immunoregulatory function of ALDH2, we generated myeloid-targeted Aldh2 knockout mice (Aldh2flox/floxLyz2cre, Aldh2 cKO) through crossbreeding Aldh2flox/flox (Aldh2f/f) mice with Lyz2cre strains. Flow cytometric and western blot analyses confirmed the efficient and specific ablation of ALDH2, which was predominantly restricted to macrophages with minimal impact on neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. 4m, n). Mirroring observations in global Aldh2-/- mice, Aldh2 cKO tumors displayed elevated MHCII:CD206 ratios in macrophages, increased CD8+ T cell infiltration, and enhanced IFN-γ/TNF-α production by tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3f–h and Supplementary Fig. 5a–f). Comparable tumor growth suppression was achieved in both myeloid-specific and global knockout models (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 5g, h). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) revealed a significantly higher density of CD8+ cells in tumors from Aldh2 cKO mice compared to Aldh2f/f mice (Supplementary Fig. 5i). These parallel findings between systemic and macrophage-restricted Aldh2 deletion models collectively indicate that ALDH2 regulates antitumor immunity primarily through its function in macrophages rather than other cell lineages. To further determine the critical function of ALDH2 in macrophages, we performed intratumoral adoptive transfer of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) from either WT or Aldh2 cKO donors. Injection of Aldh2-/- BMDMs into tumors of WT mice recapitulated the key tumor-suppressive phenotype of Aldh2 cKO mice, including inhibited tumor growth alongside enhanced infiltration and effector function of CD8+ T cells. Conversely, transfer of WT BMDMs into Aldh2 cKO hosts, leading to accelerated tumor growth and diminished CD8+ T cell activity (Fig. 3j, k, and Supplementary Fig. 5j). Together, these data demonstrated that ALDH2 activation on macrophages suppresses CD8+ T cell activity and promotes tumor growth.

To assess the clinical relevance of these findings, we analyzed our in-house TNBC cohort. Expanded immunohistochemical analysis incorporating ALDH2/CD68 co-localization identified an inverse correlation between stromal ALDH2+ CD68+ cell density and CD8+/GZMB+ T cell infiltration (Fig. 3l). Furthermore, stromal ALDH2 levels were positively correlated with advanced clinico-pathological characteristics, including tumor stage and lymph node metastasis (Supplementary Fig. 5k).

To further clarify ALDH2-mediated macrophage-T cell interactions, we conducted functional validation using BMDMs from WT and Aldh2-/- mice, with WT BMDMs additionally treated with the ALDH2 inhibitor disulfiram (DSF). Following tumor cell-conditioned medium (TCM) exposure, both Aldh2-deficient and DSF-treated macrophages exhibited elevated MHCII:CD206 ratios compared to untreated controls (Supplementary Fig. 5l), indicative of M1-like polarization. These macrophages demonstrated enhanced chemotactic capacity for T cell recruitment (Supplementary Fig. 5m) and augmented T cell cytotoxic function, evidenced by improved elimination of OVA-expressing EO771 tumor cells by OT-I T cells and increased IFN-γ/TNF-α production (Supplementary Fig. 5n–p). Notably, DSF administration failed to augment MHCII:CD206 ratios, T cell migration, or cytotoxic responses in Aldh2-/- macrophages compared to vehicle-treated counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 5l–p), confirming that DSF’s immunomodulatory effects on macrophages are strictly ALDH2-dependent.

To define the systemic impact of TAM-specific Aldh2 depletion on immune remodeling in the TME, we performed scRNA-seq on CD45+ immune cells isolated from EO771 tumors in WT and Aldh2 cKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Unsupervised clustering through uniform manifold approximation and projection resolved 12 major immune cell populations (Fig. 4a, b, and Supplementary Fig. 6b), with further subclustering revealing four transcriptionally distinct macrophage subsets: The APC-like macrophage subset exhibited high expression of Cd74, H2-Aa, and H2Eb1, consistent with an antigen-presentation phenotype. The C1q macrophage subset exhibited elevated C1qa/b/c expression, consistent with a heterogeneous TAM state with immunoregulatory and tissue-remodeling features; in our model, the concurrent enrichment of Spp1 within this population is compatible with an immunosuppressive skew. The FABP4 macrophage subset was characterized by lipid-associated genes, including Fabp4 and Acot1, and was capable of suppressing antitumor immunity. The interferon-stimulated (IFN) macrophage cluster was characterized by prominent expression of interferon-stimulated genes, including Ifit1/2/3 and Isg15/Isg20, defining an IFN-responsive transcriptional state rather than a strictly homogeneous functional subset, and consistent with an antitumor-associated program within the TME20 (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). Strikingly, Aldh2 cKO mice exhibited TME polarization toward antitumor immunity, characterized by the expansion of cytotoxic CD8+ effector T cells (Teff), IFN macrophages, and a marked reduction in immunosuppressive C1q macrophages and exhausted T cells (Tex) (Fig. 4b, c, and Supplementary Fig. 6e). To elucidate the mechanism by which macrophage Aldh2 governs antitumor immunity, we interrogated intercellular crosstalk dynamics using CellChat analysis, leveraging the scRNA-seq dataset generated above. Systemic comparison of cell-cell communication networks revealed that Aldh2 cKO tumors exhibited enhanced interaction strength between IFN macrophages and CD8+ Teff crosstalk compared to WT tumors (Fig. 4d). Analysis of CD8+ T cell functionality showed that Aldh2 depletion in macrophages augments the cytotoxic activity of CD8+ Teff, as indicated by upregulated expression of interferon-gamma (Ifng) and granzyme A (Gzma) (Fig. 4e). Deconstruction of ligand-receptor pairs identified divergent signaling patterns: IFN macrophages preferentially engaged CD8+ Teff by driving T cell activation (Tnfsf9) and recruiting chemokines (Cxcl9, Ccl4, Ccl3) (Fig. 4f). Notably, Aldh2 ablation in macrophages substantially amplified CXCL9-CXCR3 axis signaling between IFN-Mac and CD8⁺ Teff, as evidenced by ligand-receptor interaction analysis (Fig. 4f). To further validate the systemic impact of macrophage Aldh2 ablation on the tumor immune microenvironment, we next performed cytometry time of flight (CyTOF) analysis of CD45+ immune cells isolated from MC38 tumors in WT and Aldh2 cKO mice, which resolved 15 phenotypically discrete immune subsets (Fig. 4g). To substantiate the ALDH2-CXCL9 regulatory axis, we reanalyzed scRNA-seq data from our in-house TNBC immunotherapy cohort (related to Fig. 1). CXCL9 expression was significantly higher in responders than in nonresponders. Furthermore, subclustering of macrophages based on ALDH2 expression revealed that ALDH2low macrophages exhibited markedly higher CXCL9 levels compared to ALDH2high macrophages (Fig. 5d). Expanded IF analysis incorporating CXCL9/CD68 co-localization in these samples showed a significantly higher density of CXCL9⁺CD68⁺ cells in responders relative to  nonresponders (Fig. 5e). In an independent breast cancer cohort, immunohistochemical analysis incorporating ALDH2/CD68 co-localization identified an inverse correlation between stromal ALDH2+CD68+ cell density and CXCL9 expression (Fig. 5f). Genetic ablation of Aldh2 increased Cxcl9 transcriptional output in TAMs (Fig. 5g), accompanied by systemic elevation of CXCL9 protein in both circulatory (serum) and tumoral compartments (Fig. 5h, i, and Supplementary Fig. 7e, f). Flow cytometric profiling of TAMs revealed cell-autonomous increases in CXCL9 secretion across syngeneic EO771 and MC38 models (Fig. 5j and Supplementary Fig. 7g). Simultaneously, we uncovered a systemic dimension: plasma CXCL9 levels were significantly elevated in tumor-bearing mice compared to non-tumor-bearing controls (Supplementary Fig. 7h), which is likely attributable to the tumor-associated inflammatory state and widespread immune alterations. Complementing these findings, genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition (DSF) of ALDH2 in BMDMs triggered combinatorial upregulation of CXCL9 mRNA and protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 7i–k).

Clinical relevance of CXCL9-mediated immune regulation was substantiated through pan-cancer the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) analyses demonstrating significant positive associations between CXCL9 expression and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration signatures (Supplementary Fig. 7l). Functional interrogation in vivo revealed antibody-mediated CXCL9 neutralization reversed the antitumor effects of Aldh2 deficiency. Treatment with anti-CXCL9 antibodies attenuated CD8⁺ T cell recruitment and restored tumor progression in EO771 and MC38 models (Fig. 5k, l, and Supplementary Fig. 7m, n). in vitro, CXCL9 neutralization abrogated enhanced CD8+ T cell migration induced by Aldh2-deficient macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 7o). Together, these findings position ALDH2 as a transcriptional suppressor of CXCL9 in TAMs, mechanistically linking its immunosuppressive function to impaired CD8⁺ T cell recruitment.

As a mitochondrial aldehyde-detoxifying enzyme, ALDH2 metabolizes multiple aldehydic substrates generated under oxidative stress, including ethanol-derived acetaldehyde and lipid peroxidation-derived aldehydes, such as 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) and malondialdehyde (MDA)21. Among these substrates, 4-HNE is widely recognized as a highly bioactive lipid peroxidation-derived aldehyde due to its strong electrophilicity and capacity to modulate cellular signaling22. Given its prominent role, we focused on 4-HNE and, for comparison, evaluated MDA as another key lipid peroxidation product to assess their functional effects in our system. In EO771 tumor models, Aldh2-/- and Aldh2 cKO mice exhibited significantly increased levels of 4-HNE in serum and tumor tissues compared to WT controls. A comparable trend was observed in the MC38 model (Fig. 6a, b, and Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). Similar increases in 4-HNE were observed in Aldh2-deficient or DSF-treated BMDMs versus controls (Supplementary Fig. 8c). To assess the functional impact of 4-HNE on CXCL9 production, TAMs and BMDMs were exposed to 4-HNE, resulting in upregulated CXCL9 mRNA and protein levels compared to vehicle-treated macrophages (Fig. 6c, d, and Supplementary Fig. 8d–f). Furthermore, 4-HNE induced an M1-like polarization shift in BMDMs, characterized by elevated MHCII:CD206 ratios (Supplementary Fig. 8g). This pro-inflammatory macrophage state potentiated CD8+ T cell effector functions, with co-cultured lymphocytes exhibiting amplified IFN-γ and TNF-α production (Fig. 6e). To assess the direct effect of 4-HNE on macrophage viability and exclude secondary effects arising from cellular injury or death, we measured the survival rate of BMDMs. No statistically significant difference in cell viability was observed between the 4-HNE-treated and vehicle control groups at 24, 48, or 72 h (Supplementary Fig. 8h). These findings indicate that ALDH2-mediated aldehyde clearance regulates macrophage immunomodulatory function. In parallel, we assessed the impact of MDA. Although MDA levels were modestly increased in Aldh2-deficient macrophages and tumor tissues (Supplementary Fig. 9a–c), exogenous MDA treatment did not significantly upregulate CXCL9 at either the transcript or protein level in macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 9d, e). To determine whether epigenetic reprogramming drives the 4-HNE-mediated CXCL9 upregulation in Aldh2-deficient TAMs, we profiled genome-wide chromatin accessibility using assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing (ATAC-seq). Notably, a significant opening was confirmed at the promoter region of Cxcl9. Genome-wide identification of differentially accessible regions revealed that Aldh2 deficiency led to a broader increase in chromatin accessibility within TAMs (Fig. 6f, g). To pinpoint the histone modification responsible for the increased chromatin accessibility, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays for other major marks. Compared to WT BMDMs, Aldh2-/- BMDMs showed no significant increase at the Cxcl9 promoter in either the active marks—histone H3 lysine 4 trimethylation (H3K4me3) and histone H3 lysine 36 trimethylation (H3K36me3)—or the repressive mark histone H3 lysine 9 trimethylation (H3K9me3) (Fig. 6h)23. Instead, a marked reduction in histone H3 lysine 27 trimethylation (H3K27me3) was specifically observed, a result that was consistently recapitulated upon treatment with the ALDH2 inhibitor DSF (Fig. 6h and Supplementary Fig. 8i). H3K27me3 is a well-established repressive histone mark. Its diminution constitutes a critical epigenetic event for the activation of target genes23,24. Immunoblotting analysis demonstrated a marked reduction in H3K27me3 levels upon inhibition of ALDH2 or direct exposure to 4-HNE in macrophages, relative to controls (Fig. 6i). This phenotype was consistent across TAMs isolated from Aldh2 cKO mice bearing EO771 or MC38 tumors (Fig. 6j and Supplementary Fig. 8j). To assess the promoter-level selectivity of H3K27me3 regulation, we analyzed H3K27me3 levels at promoters of additional T-cell-regulatory genes that were differentially expressed between WT and Aldh2-/- TAMs, including Ifng, Il12b, Ccl5, Ccl20, Il21, and Il2. No significant reduction was detected at these loci (Supplementary Fig. 8k), indicating that H3K27me3 reduction is not uniformly distributed across immune-related genes but is selectively enriched at the Cxcl9 promoter among the genes examined. To determine if scavenging 4-HNE could restore H3K27me3 levels, we treated BMDMs with the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC). Both western blot and flow cytometric analyses confirmed that NAC treatment effectively restored H3K27me3 levels in Aldh2-/- BMDMs (Supplementary Fig. 8l, m).

Mechanistically, phosphorylation of enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) at Ser21 reduces H3K27me3 deposition by inactivating its methyltransferase function25. Remarkably, Aldh2-deficient macrophages displayed elevated p-EZH2 (Ser21) levels, mirrored by DSF or 4-HNE-treated WT macrophages (Fig. 6k). To directly assess the cell-intrinsic effect of EZH2 on macrophages, we used the EZH2 inhibitor EPZ-6438 in vivo and in vitro. To specifically evaluate the myeloid-intrinsic role of EZH2 in the TME, we adoptively transferred EPZ-6438-treated BMDMs into tumor-bearing mice. This transfer alone recapitulated the key phenotype: it inhibited tumor growth and remodeled the immune microenvironment, enhancing CD8+ T cell activity and CXCL9 expression while reducing H3K27me3 (Fig. 6l–n and Supplementary Fig. 8n). Correspondingly, EPZ-6438 treatment reduced global H3K27me3 levels, elevated CXCL9 secretion, and increased the MHC-II/CD206 ratio in BMDMs in vitro (Supplementary Fig. 8o–q). Additionally, Aldh2 deficiency or 4-HNE treatment did not significantly alter the levels of the histone demethylases KDM6A and KDM6B (Supplementary Fig. 8r).

AKT inhibition with MK2206 effectively neutralized Aldh2 deficiency-associated effects, abrogating EZH2 phosphorylation at Ser21, reinstating H3K27me3 chromatin marks, and reducing CXCL9 expression levels (Fig. 7f, g). To determine whether the signaling and epigenetic changes described above were specific to 4-HNE accumulation, we next examined whether MDA elicited comparable effects. In addition to its limited effect on CXCL9 expression, MDA treatment failed to activate the AKT–EZH2 signaling cascade, exerting minimal impact on p-AKT, p-EZH2, or H3K27me3 levels (Supplementary Fig. 9f–h). We then performed a genetic rescue experiment by overexpressing Aldh2 in Aldh2-/- macrophages to validate the causal role of ALDH2 loss in this pathway. Restoration of Aldh2 (Aldh2 OE) in Aldh2-/- BMDMs effectively lowered 4-HNE accumulation, reduced CXCL9 expression, attenuated AKT and EZH2 phosphorylation, and reinstated H3K27me3 deposition (Supplementary Fig. 9i–m). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ALDH2 deficiency promotes CXCL9 upregulation via 4-HNE–dependent activation of the AKT-EZH2 axis and consequent chromatin remodeling.

Crucially, DSF administration in Aldh2 cKO mice induced no additive effects on TAM polarization, molecular markers, and tumor growth compared to vehicle-treated counterparts (Fig. 9n–r and Supplementary Fig. 10m–q). Macrophage depletion via diphtheria toxin in Lyz2cre/iDTR mice bearing EO771 and MC38 tumors fully phenocopied DSF’s therapeutic effects, with combined macrophage depletion and DSF treatment yielding no synergistic effects (Fig. 9s and Supplementary Fig. 10r). These orthogonal approaches unequivocally pinpoint macrophage ALDH2 inhibition as the nonredundant mechanism governing DSF’s antitumor efficacy.

To evaluate the safety profile of DSF in vivo, we monitored hematological and biochemical parameters and performed histopathological analysis on major organs in mice treated with varying doses (25, 50, and 75 mg/kg). No significant abnormalities in blood cell counts or biochemical parameters were observed at any dose compared to vehicle controls (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Furthermore, hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining indicated no significant toxic effects of DSF on the heart, liver, lung, kidney, or spleen (Supplementary Fig. 11a). To exclude off-target effects of DSF on the major ALDH isoform Aldh1, we performed Aldh1 knockdown in BMDMs. Knockdown of Aldh1 did not recapitulate the phenotypic cascade observed upon ALDH2 ablation. Notably, Aldh1 knockdown did not significantly elevate 4-HNE levels, nor did it induce detectable changes in the levels of p-PTEN, p-AKT, H3K27me3, or CXCL9 (Supplementary Fig. 11b–h).

The immunosuppressive polarization of TAMs represents a critical barrier to effective cancer immunotherapy29. Our study identifies ALDH2 as a macrophage-enriched metabolic checkpoint driving ICB resistance through coordinated chemokine silencing and immune evasion. We demonstrate that TAMs exhibiting high ALDH2 expression accumulate in nonresponsive tumors of multiple cancer types, correlating with diminished CD8+ T cell infiltration, impaired cytotoxic function, and accelerated disease progression. These findings position ALDH2 as a functional biomarker of adaptive immune resistance, mechanistically linking TAM metabolism to the hallmark features of immunotherapy-refractory tumors. Unlike previous reports emphasizing tumor-intrinsic ALDH2 roles in PD-L1 stabilization or VISTA induction13,14, our work defines its immunosuppressive activity as macrophage-specific, highlighting compartmentalized metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk within the TME. Recent research has revealed that TAMs generate and secrete acetate via the lipid peroxidation-ALDH2 metabolic axis, which is subsequently taken up by hepatocellular carcinoma cells to fuel their metastatic progression30. In parallel, our work identifies an additional, distinct mechanism through which ALDH2 modulates the metabolism of reactive aldehydes in macrophages, thereby regulating the production of the chemokine CXCL9 and contributing to the suppression of antitumor immunity. These findings underscore the multifaceted and critical role of ALDH2 in orchestrating macrophage metabolic reprogramming within the TME.

Our findings reveal a mechanism whereby ALDH2 ablation epigenetically derepresses CXCL9 via H3K27me3 erosion, establishing a functional bridge between lipid peroxidation metabolites and chemokine network regulation. While CXCL9 is essential for T cell recruitment31,32, we demonstrate that its suppression in TAMs is not a passive state but actively enforced through ALDH2-mediated aldehyde detoxification. Central to this process, 4-HNE, a lipid peroxidation byproduct traditionally linked to DNA damage through adduct formation33, emerges as a redox-sensitive activator of the PI3K-AKT pathway. ALDH2 deficiency repurposes this pleiotropic metabolite into an immunostimulatory signal, paradoxically enhancing AKT activity (typically associated with immune resistance34) to phosphorylate and suppress EZH2 methyltransferase. This context-dependent rewiring dismantles H3K27me3-mediated epigenetic silencing at the CXCL9 locus, converting accumulated oxidative stress into a chemotactic signal that redirects TAMs toward immunogenic reprogramming. These observations redefine PI3K-AKT signaling plasticity, demonstrating how microenvironmental metabolic states dictate its functional duality, shifting from pro-tumorigenic to immune-sensitizing outcomes. The PI3K-AKT signaling pathway is a recognized therapeutic target in oncology, with several inhibitors approved or under investigation for cancers with relevant alterations. Current therapeutic paradigms primarily aim to inhibit this pathway within tumor cells to curb their growth35. Our data show that AKT activation within TAMs can contribute to an immunostimulatory microenvironment, highlighting the context-dependent role of this pathway. This observation underscores the complexity of targeting widely expressed signaling nodes and highlights the importance of cellular context for therapeutic outcome.

As a canonical ALDH2 inhibitor, DSF has been clinically used for alcohol dependence management for several decades36. Emerging evidence now substantiates its anticancer potential, with studies implicating its modulation of the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, redox homeostasis, and key oncogenic signaling cascades37–39. However, the mechanistic basis for DSF’s immunomodulatory effects within the TME remains poorly defined. Building upon our prior work demonstrating DSF-mediated TME remodeling and immunotherapy sensitization13, this study reveals a macrophage-centric mechanism: ALDH2 displays predominant expression in immune cells, particularly TAMs, rather than tumor cells. DSF reprograms macrophages toward an immunostimulatory phenotype, which in turn augments CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity. Crucially, the absence of therapeutic enhancement in macrophage-depleted or ALDH2-ablated models confirms that DSF’s antitumor efficacy primarily stems from macrophage ALDH2 targeting rather than direct tumor cell effects. These findings establish a mechanistic foundation for repurposing DSF as a stromal-targeted adjuvant in cancer immunotherapy regimes.

While the ALDH2–H3K27me3–CXCL9 axis established here provides a mechanistic basis for enhanced anti-tumor immunity, its clinical relevance will require validation in expanded patient cohorts. Translational efforts should assess whether circulating 4-HNE or CXCL9 levels predict DSF-ICB responsiveness, enabling biomarker-guided patient stratification. Although our data support a functional role for 4-HNE downstream of ALDH2 in macrophage epigenetic regulation, ALDH2 likely influences broader metabolic networks. Unbiased metabolomic profiling in future studies will help comprehensively define additional ALDH2-dependent metabolic alterations and further delineate the metabolic landscape shaping macrophage function in the TME. Additionally, alterations in other immune subsets, such as Tregs, were observed, which may represent concomitant adaptations within the remodeled microenvironment, and their functional significance warrants further investigation.

In conclusion, this study redefines ALDH2 as a macrophage-centric metabolic gatekeeper enforcing immunotherapy resistance through epigenetic silencing of T cell chemotactic signals. By harnessing clinically approved ALDH2 inhibitors, this work provides an actionable strategy to recalibrate the TME and expand the therapeutic reach of cancer immunotherapy.

Quantitative PCR was conducted using GoTaq® qPCR Master Mix, 2X (A600A, Promega) on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the threshold cycle (2−ΔΔCT) method and normalized to GAPDH. The specific primers used for Real-time PCR are listed in the Supplementary Table 5.

The levels of 4-HNE and CXCL9 in mouse serum, tumor tissues, and BMDMs culture supernatants were measured using 4-HNE (D751041, Songon) and CXCL9 (EK2143, Multi Sciences) ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

The levels of MDA in BMDMs and mouse tumor tissues were measured using a micro-method MDA assay kit (D799762, Sangon) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The primary antibodies used are listed below: H3K27me3 (ab6002, Abcam, 1:1000), EZH2 (5246, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), p-EZH2 (AF3822, Affinity, 1:1000), AKT (4691, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), p-AKT (4060, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:2000), p-PTEN (29246-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:2000), PTEN (60300-3-Ig, Proteintech, 1:2000), KDM6A (HA500524, Huabio, 1:1000), KDM6B (DF13101, Affinity, 1:1000), H3 (17168-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:16000), GAPDH (ZB15004-HRP-100, Servicebio, 1:2000), and β-Actin (66009-1-Ig, Proteintech, 1:20000). Uncropped Western blot scans are provided in the Source data file.

ChIP assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol provided with the SimpleChIP® Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Magnetic Beads). Anti-H3K27me3 antibody (ab6002, Abcam, 10 µg for 25 µg chromatin), Anti-H3K4me3 antibody (A22146, ABclonal, 5 µg for 10 µg chromatin), Anti-H3K9me3 antibody (ab8898, Abcam, 4 µg for 25 µg chromatin), and Anti-H3K36me3 antibody (4909, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:50) were used for immunoprecipitation, with normal rabbit IgG (2729, Cell Signaling Technology, 1 µg for 10 µg chromatin) serving as the negative control. ChIP-enriched chromatin was analyzed by Real-Time PCR with SYBR Green Master Mix and normalized to the input. The ChIP-PCR primers were listed in the Supplementary Table 6.

AKT kinase activity in BMDMs lysates was determined using the commercial AKT Kinase Activity Kit (ADI-EKS-400A, Enzo) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, an equal amount of protein from each sample was incubated in substrate-coated wells with ATP to initiate the phosphorylation reaction. The phospho-substrate was then detected colorimetrically via an ELISA-based readout at 450 nm, with activity quantified relative to a standard curve.

For Aldh1 knockdown, a plasmid encoding Aldh1-specific shRNAs was obtained from Tsingke (shAldh1−1:

5′-GATCGGCTCATGTTCATTTGGAAGATCTCGAGATCTTCCAAATGAACATGAGCTTTTTT-3′, shAldh1−2:

5′-GATCGCGCAATGAAGATATCTCAGAACTCGAGTTCTGAGATATCTTCATTGCGTTTTTT-3′). For Aldh2 overexpression in Aldh2-knockout BMDMs, an expression plasmid encoding the mouse Aldh2 cDNA was obtained from Tsingke. The respective plasmid was transfected into target BMDM cells using Lipofectamine™ 3000 reagent (L3000150, Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The culture medium was replaced 6 h post-transfection. Cells were harvested for analysis 48–72 h after transfection to assess knockdown or overexpression efficiency.

For IHC staining, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor tissues were sectioned at 4 μm, deparaffinized with xylene, and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was carried out in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using pressure heating, followed by quenching of endogenous peroxidase activity with 3% H₂O₂ and blocking with 5% normal goat serum. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ALDH2 (ab108306, Abcam, 1:2400), CD8a (98941, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:500), GZMB (ab4059, Abcam, 1:100), and CXCL9 (ab202961, Abcam, 1:200). After incubation with the appropriate biotinylated secondary antibodies, signals were developed using DAB (ab64238, abcam), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. For IF staining, paraffin-embedded tumor sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 and blocked with 5% donkey serum. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD68 (ab955, Abcam, 1:3000) and ALDH2 (ab108306, Abcam, 1:2400). After washing, the sections were incubated with donkey anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 488 (A21202, Invitrogen, 1:1000) and donkey anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 594 (A21207, Invitrogen, 1:1000), followed by DAPI nuclear staining. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope and analyzed with Leica software (v4.7.0).

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2) and R (version 4.3.2). Continuous data between two groups were compared using an unpaired, two-sided Student’s t test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s or Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test were applied. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Results are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and exact P-values are annotated in all figures.
