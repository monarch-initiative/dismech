# Identification and functional analysis of a novel TBC1D23 pathogenic variant in a Chinese family with pontocerebellar hypoplasia.
**Authors:** Liu K, Chen Y, Meng Y, Wang X, Tang X, Li H, Chen J, Zhong Z
**Journal:** Hum Genomics (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s40246-025-00782-1](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40246-025-00782-1)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Pontocerebellar hypoplasia type 11 (PCH11) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by variants in TBC1D23. The molecular basis for its clinical heterogeneity remains poorly understood. Here, we identified a novel TBC1D23 variant in a Chinese family, investigated its underlying pathogenic mechanisms, and systematically reviewed the clinical phenotypes of all reported cases of PCH11.

RESULTS: We identified a novel homozygous frameshift variant, c.511_512delTT (p.F171Qfs*8), in TBC1D23. The patient exhibited a severe phenotype, including marked pontocerebellar hypoplasia, a thinned corpus callosum, global developmental delay, and severe language and motor impairments. Mechanistic studies in a zebrafish model revealed that the mutant transcript partially escaped nonsense-mediated decay (NMD), with expression levels at approximately 50% of the wild-type. In vitro, the resultant truncated protein showed enhanced stability and aberrant cytoplasmic distribution instead of its normal Golgi localization. Furthermore, its expression significantly inhibited cell proliferation.

CONCLUSION: Our study identifies c.511_512delTT as a novel pathogenic variant in TBC1D23. We propose the severe phenotype stems from a primary loss-of-function (LoF), which is likely exacerbated by the cytotoxic effect of the truncated protein produced via partial NMD escape. Our findings suggest this mutant protein exhibits increased stability. This model provides a novel explanation for the phenotypic heterogeneity in PCH11 and expands the mutational spectrum of this disorder.

Pontocerebellar hypoplasia (PCH) is a hereditary neurodegenerative disorder characterized by atrophy and developmental abnormalities of the pons and cerebellum. Pathological changes typically occur prenatally, with degeneration or arrested development of the pons and cerebellum coinciding with critical stages of neurodevelopment. This results in poor prognosis, with most patients experiencing death during infancy or early childhood [1–3]. PCH11 is a distinct subtype of PCH, currently considered non-degenerative. Clinically, PCH11 patients typically exhibit pontocerebellar hypoplasia, microcephaly, global developmental delay, and moderate-to-severe intellectual disability [4–6]. Common features include ataxia, motor disorders, developmental delays, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) [4, 5, 7]. Some patients may also have dysphagia and dysarthria. Seizures are relatively rare, but focal seizures have been reported in some cases [6]. Next-generation sequencing technologies have accelerated the discovery of new genetic variants associated with PCH, enhancing our understanding of its pathogenesis and providing a richer foundation for molecular diagnostics.

In 2017, the TBC1D23 gene was identified as responsible for Pontocerebellar Hypoplasia type 11 (PCH11) [4, 5]. TBC1D23, located on chromosome 3q12.1, encodes a bridging protein localized to the trans-Golgi network (TGN), essential for recognizing and capturing vesicles from endosomes, thereby facilitating cargo delivery to the Golgi [4, 5, 8]. TBC1D23 belongs to the Tre2/Bub2/Cdc16 (TBC) family, typically functioning as RAB-GTPase-activating proteins (GAP). Although TBC1D23 lacks conventional catalytic activity, its high conservation across numerous eukaryotes and tissues highlights its functional significance [8, 9]. Structurally, TBC1D23 connects endosomal vesicles to the TGN and facilitates cargo transport via its three-domain architecture: an N-terminal TBC domain, a central Rhodanese-like domain, and a C-terminal pleckstrin homology (PH) domain [10, 11].

Notably, PCH11 exhibits considerable phenotypic heterogeneity. Reported cases show substantial variability in motor milestones (e.g., independent walking), language abilities, corpus callosum integrity, and intellectual levels. Some patients achieve certain motor and language capabilities, whereas others present with more severe global developmental delays and structural brain abnormalities [4, 5]. Furthermore, although most PCH11 cases are classified as non-progressive, Laugwitz et al. reported a patient exhibiting distinct cerebellar atrophy characterized by lobular sparing [6]. These phenotypic variations suggest diverse molecular mechanisms through which different TBC1D23 variants influence normal neurological development.

Nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD) maintains cellular homeostasis by degrading transcripts containing premature termination codons (PTCs) [12, 13]. In recessive genetic disorders, NMD-mediated allele inactivation is a common molecular mechanism [14]. Although NMD efficiently removes defective mRNA, natural variability in NMD efficacy exists among transcripts [15, 16]. Some transcripts harboring PTCs escape degradation, producing truncated proteins linked to disease phenotypes [17]. Since PCH11-causing variants generate PTCs in TBC1D23 transcripts, and no prior research has explored NMD effects of TBC1D23 pathogenic variants, investigating these effects may elucidate the complexity of PCH11. Zebrafish serve as valuable in vivo models for studying NMD genetics, pathogenesis, and drug development due to their highly conserved NMD mechanisms and key proteins shared with humans [18–20].

In this study, we report a novel Chinese PCH11 family carrying a previously unidentified frameshift pathogenic variant in TBC1D23, c.511_512delTT (p.F171Qfs*8), exhibiting autosomal recessive inheritance segregating with phenotype. To clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying this disease, we investigated whether mutant mRNA undergoes NMD using a zebrafish model. Additionally, we characterized the biochemical properties of the truncated mutant protein and examined its cellular impacts in vitro, providing new insights into the pathogenic mechanisms and phenotypic heterogeneity of PCH11.

In this study, we recruited a Chinese family affected by pontocerebellar hypoplasia (PCH) and obtained informed consent from all participating family members. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tongji University School of Medicine (Shanghai, China) and was conducted in strict accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All family members underwent neurology and clinical evaluations, with one patient also receiving an MRI examination. Approximately 5 ml of blood was voluntarily provided by each participant, from which genomic DNA was extracted using the Tiangen DNA extraction kit (DP304).

Genomic DNA from the family was sent to Genesky Biotechnologies Inc. for whole-exome capture and sequencing. Whole-exome capture was performed using the Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon Kit. The captured genomic DNA was then loaded onto the HiSeq 2000 platform, with read lengths of 150 bp. The sequencing data were aligned to the UCSC Genome Browser hg19 by the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner algorithm.Variants were identified using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (version 2.4-9 of GATK) and subsequently annotated with Annovar software. Next, variants were then filtered through multiple databases, including the 1000 Genomes Project, esp6500si_all, ExAC, and gnomAD. Mutations with a frequency of zero in the database were considered candidate variants. The ClustalW algorithm [21] was used to perform multiple sequence alignment of TBC1D23 sequences from various species available in the NCBI database to analyze sequence conservation.

To determine if the disease phenotype co-segregates with the candidate gene in this family, we amplified DNA samples from family members by PCR. The amplification protocol followed the method described in the previous article [22]. The PCR products were compared with sequence from the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information database)(http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).

Given the high sequence and functional homology of key NMD proteins between humans and zebrafish, together with the efficiency of CRISPR/Cas9 in generating gene-specific mutants in zebrafish [23, 24], this organism is well suited for modelling human variants. Sequence alignments confirmed substantial conservation between the human TBC1D23 transcript and its exon 5 carrying the c.511_512delTT variant and their zebrafish counterparts (72% identity, 1396/1949 nucleotides, and 71% identity, 79/111 nucleotides, respectively; data from Nucleotide BLAST, https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). Because patient-derived cells were unavailable, we used the zebrafish model to evaluate the NMD effect of this mutant allele. Using the online tool CHOPCHOP (https://chopchop.cbu.uib.no/), sgRNAs were designed near the zebrafish homologous site of the TBC1D23 c.511_512delTT (p.F171Qfs*8) pathogenic variant identified in this family. The Px330 vector was used as a template for PCR amplification with an upstream primer containing a T7 promoter and a corresponding downstream primer (sequences provided in Supplementary Table 1), generating a DNA fragment containing the T7 promoter and sgRNA. The sgRNA was then synthesized in vitro using T7 RNA polymerase (Roche, 1000 U) and extracted with Trizol reagent (TIANGEN, DP424). Cas9 protein (IDT, Cas9-GFP V3) was assembled with sgRNA at room temperature and microinjected into zebrafish one-cell stage fertilized embryos the following day. F0 zebrafish carrying frameshift variants at the targeted sites were crossed with AB strain zebrafish. After maturation, heterozygous F1 individuals carrying the same frameshift mutation were screened and subsequently inbred to obtain homozygous F2 mutant zebrafish.

The wild-type (WT) TBC1D23 was cloned from the coding sequence (CDS) region of HeLa cells, and then integrated into the vector pEGFP-C using the pEASY®-Uni Seamless Cloning and Assembly Kit (TransGen Biotech, China). Using the recombinant plasmid pEGFP-C-WT-TBC1D23 as a template, we designed primers containing specific site mutations for overlap PCR (primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1). The mutated fragment was then ligated with the linearized pEGFP-C vector using homologous recombination to generate the TBC1D23 mutant plasmid pEGFP-C-MT-TBC1D23. Finally, the wild-type and mutant plasmids were verified by Sanger sequencing. The primers used for plasmid construction in this article are listed in Supplementary Table 1. HeLa cells (CCL-2) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA).

For the in vivo assays, thirty embryos each of wild-type AB zebrafish and of heterozygous and homozygous tbc1d23 frameshift mutants were collected at 3 days post-fertilisation (dpf). For the in vitro assays, wild-type and mutant plasmids were transfected into HeLa cells for 36 h. Total RNA was extracted with the Tiangen TRNzol Universal Reagent (DP424), reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Tiangen KR118 kit, and subjected to quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) on a Roche platform with the Tiangen FP205 kit.

Thirty-six hours after transfecting HeLa cells with wild-type and mutant TBC1D23 plasmids using Vigofect (Vigorous Biotechnology, Inc.), proteins were extracted using the same method as shown previously [22]. Proteins were fully denatured using SDS-PAGE sample buffer (P0015L; Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Haimen, China). Proteins were then separated on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore, Catalog No. ISEQ00010) for western blotting. The PVDF membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk at 25℃ for 1 h. After blocking, the membrane was washed three times with TBST. Anti-EGFP mouse monoclonal antibody (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) was diluted 1:1000 to detect the EGFP-TBC1D23 recombinant protein. The secondary antibody (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) was used at a dilution of 1:5000. The membrane was imaged using an enhanced chemiluminescence protein blotting substrate (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.) and the Tanon imaging system (Tanon-5200; Tanon Science & Technology Co. Ltd.).

HeLa cells were plated on coverslips overnight, and then transfected with TBC1D23 wild-type and mutant plasmids, respectively. Cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed with pure methanol for 10 min. After washing three more times, the cells were treated with DAPI (0.5 µg/mL) for 1 min. Finally, images were captured using a Zeiss LSM 900 inverted confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Germany).

HeLa cells were seeded in 96-well plates and transfected with empty vector plasmid (control), wild-type TBC1D23 plasmid, and mutant TBC1D23 plasmid. After transfection, 10 µl of CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, C0038) was added to each well. Absorbance measurements at 450 nm were taken multiple times over a 60-hour period. Statistical analysis was performed on the absorbance data of the three groups at the 60-hour time point.

We used PEST-Find (https://emboss.bioinformatics.nl/cgi-bin/emboss/epestfind) and GPS-Uber (https://gpsuber.biocuckoo.cn/index.php) [25] to predict the degradation signals and ubiquitination modifications of TBC1D23.

Densitometric quantification of Western-blot bands was performed with ImageJ v1.5.0. Statistical analyses of the Western-blot, qPCR, and CCK-8 datasets were carried out in GraphPad Prism v10.1.2. Data from at least three biological replicates are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Two-group comparisons were evaluated with a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, whereas multiple-group comparisons were analysed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test.

This study recruited a Chinese family affected by Pontocerebellar Hypoplasia (PCH). The family is consanguineous and has three children: a phenotypically normal 12-year-old daughter (II:1) and two affected children, an 8-year-old boy (II:2, proband) and a 5-year-old girl (II:3) (Fig. 1B). Both affected children exhibited significant clinical features, including global psychomotor developmental delays, severe language impairment, short stature, hypotonia, inability to walk, and severe underweight. The male patient (II:2) additionally exhibited aggressive and self-injurious behavior. Although formal evaluations for ataxia and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) were not performed, both patients displayed clinical characteristics associated with these conditions, such as impaired coordination and attention deficits. Additionally, mild facial dysmorphisms were observed. Brain MRI of the proband (II:2) revealed pronounced hypoplasia of the pons and cerebellum, thinning of the corpus callosum, and microcephaly (Fig. 1A). These imaging findings are consistent with diagnostic criteria for PCH. Compared with other reported PCH11 cases, the degree of corpus callosum thinning and overall brain developmental abnormalities in this patient appeared more severe. To identify the pathogenic gene variant in this family, whole-exome sequencing (WES) was conducted on family members I:2 (mother), II:2 (affected boy), and II:3 (affected girl). Bioinformatics analysis and data filtering identified a homozygous two-base deletion variant, c.511_512delTT, in exon 5 of the TBC1D23 gene (NM_001199198.3). This variant causes a frameshift in the coding sequence, resulting in the substitution of phenylalanine (F171) by glutamine (Q), followed by the introduction of a PTC at the 8th amino acid downstream (p.F171Qfs*8). Sanger sequencing validation in other family members confirmed that both affected children (II:2 and II:3) were homozygous carriers, while the mother (I:2) and phenotypically normal sister (II:1) were heterozygous carriers, consistent with an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern (Fig. 1B and C).This c.511_512delTT variant was not reported in public population databases, including ExAC, gnomAD, the 1000 Genomes Project, and esp6500si_all. MutationTaster software predicted this variant as “disease-causing.” Cross-species protein sequence alignment revealed high conservation of the mutated phenylalanine residue (F171) among different species (Fig. 1D), suggesting its critical importance in the structural or functional integrity of the TBC1D23 protein.

The novel variant identified in this study, c.511_512delTT, lies in exon 5 and is predicted to shift the reading frame at phenylalanine 171, introducing a PTC at the eighth subsequent triplet (p.F171Qfs*8) (Fig. 2A). Transcripts containing PTCs typically activate the intracellular NMD pathway. To investigate whether the human TBC1D23 c.511_512delTT variant activates NMD and influences transcript abundance in vivo, we generated a corresponding zebrafish (Danio rerio) gene variant model using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. As illustrated in Fig. 2B, we designed an sgRNA targeting a region within the zebrafish tbc1d23 gene homologous to the human c.511_512delTT variant, aiming to introduce a similar PTC into zebrafish tbc1d23 mRNA transcripts. Sanger sequencing confirmed the successful generation of F2 homozygous mutant zebrafish (MT) carrying the intended in-frame deletion. Figure 2C shows comparative chromatograms from wild-type (WT) and mutant (MT) zebrafish at the targeted locus, clearly demonstrating CRISPR/Cas9-induced nucleotide deletions and sequence alterations in the mutant line, consistent with a frameshift and subsequent PTC. The sgRNA target sequence and corresponding homologous site of the human TBC1D23 c.511_512delTT variant are also indicated in the figure.We subsequently extracted total RNA from wild-type (WT), heterozygous mutant (HE), and homozygous mutant (HO) zebrafish embryos at 3 days post-fertilization (dpf) and assessed the relative expression of tbc1d23 mRNA using real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR). Compared with WT zebrafi sh, HO mutant embryos carrying the PTC showed a signifi cant reduction in tbc1d23 mRNA expression, approximately 50% of WT levels, while heterozygotes had about 75% of WT levels (Figure 2D, p < 0.001). These findings indicate that, in the zebrafish model, transcripts with the PTC resulting from the homologous c.511_512delTT variant are recognized and partially degraded by cellular NMD mechanisms, suggesting activation of the NMD pathway in vivo by this variant.

To further investigate the cellular biological characteristics and functional impacts of the truncated TBC1D23 protein generated by the c.511_512delTT variant (p.F171Qfs8), we performed in vitro overexpression experiments using HeLa cells. We first analyzed the potential structural domain changes caused by this variant. As shown in Fig. 3A, the wild-type TBC1D23 protein contains an N-terminal TBC (Tre2-Bub2-Cdc16) domain, a central Rhodanese-like (Rhod) domain, and a C-terminal Pleckstrin homology (PH) domain. The p.F171Qfs8 variant leads to a severely truncated protein retaining only a partial TBC domain.

We constructed plasmids expressing EGFP-tagged wild-type (WT) and truncated mutant (MT) TBC1D23 proteins. Western blot analysis indicated that cells overexpressing the truncated mutant TBC1D23 protein exhibited significantly higher expression levels compared to those expressing the wild-type protein (Fig. 3B and C, p < 0.01). Additionally, as expected, the molecular weight of the mutant protein was markedly reduced, consistent with its truncated nature. These results suggest that, in the current in vitro overexpression system, the truncated mutant protein might possess greater stability or accumulate more efficiently.

Next, we evaluated the subcellular localization of the wild-type and mutant TBC1D23 proteins in HeLa cells using immunofluorescence. As illustrated in Fig. 3D, the wild-type TBC1D23-EGFP fusion protein localized primarily to the Golgi apparatus, showing characteristic granular or vesicular distribution, consistent with its known functional role. However, the truncated mutant TBC1D23-EGFP protein completely lost its Golgi localization capability, displaying diffuse distribution throughout the cytoplasm. These findings indicate that the p.F171Qfs*8 mutation significantly disrupts the proper localization of TBC1D23 protein, directly evidencing its loss of function. We further utilized the CCK-8 assay to evaluate the effects of wild-type and mutant TBC1D23 overexpression on HeLa cell proliferation. Cells were transfected with empty vector (CON), wild-type TBC1D23 plasmid (WT), or mutant TBC1D23 plasmid (MT), and cell viability was assessed at various time points. As shown in Fig. 3E and F, cells expressing truncated mutant TBC1D23 exhibited significantly reduced proliferation activity over a 60-hour culture period compared to both empty-vector controls and wild-type TBC1D23-expressing cells (Fig. 3F, p < 0.05). In contrast, wild-type TBC1D23 overexpression had no significant effect on cell proliferation (WT vs. CON, not significant). These results suggest that the mislocalized and potentially elevated expression levels of the truncated TBC1D23 protein negatively impact cellular proliferation processes.

To theoretically explore potential mechanisms underlying increased expression levels of the truncated TBC1D23 (p.F171Qfs8) protein observed in vitro, we used bioinformatics tools to predict degradation-related signal sequences. Firstly, the online tool PEST-find was utilized to identify potential PEST sequences within the full-length wild-type TBC1D23 protein sequence. PEST sequences, enriched in proline (P), glutamic acid (E), serine (S), and threonine (T), typically mediate rapid degradation in many short-lived proteins. Analysis revealed two potential PEST sequences in the C-terminal region (approximately residues 400–600) of the wild-type TBC1D23 protein, scoring + 6.74 and + 15.07, respectively—both exceeding the threshold of + 5.00—suggesting these regions might facilitate rapid protein degradation (Fig. 4A). However, due to premature termination at amino acid 178 caused by the p.F171Qfs8 variant, these two potential PEST sequences in the C-terminus are entirely lost in the truncated mutant protein.

Furthermore, we employed the GPS-Uber online service to predict potential ubiquitination sites within TBC1D23 protein. Ubiquitination is a critical mechanism for regulating protein degradation. The prediction identified numerous potential lysine (K) ubiquitination sites in wild-type TBC1D23, many of which (e.g., at residues 190, 377, 384, 484, 489, 495, 504, 549, 627, 684, 687, and 692) lie downstream of the truncation site introduced by the p.F171Qfs*8 mutation (Fig. 4B). Thus, these C-terminal ubiquitination sites are also absent in the truncated mutant protein. In summary, bioinformatics predictions suggest that the p.F171Qfs*8 truncation mutation results in the loss of several potential rapid-degradation signals (PEST sequences) and ubiquitination sites at the C-terminal region of the TBC1D23 protein. The absence of these degradation signals likely contributes to increased stability of the truncated mutant protein, aligning with our observations of elevated protein expression levels in vitro.

To date, five reports have documented pathogenic variants in TBC1D23. The detailed clinical features of all reported PCH cases with TBC1D23 mutations are presented in Supplementary Table 2. A total of 21 patients with homozygous TBC1D23 variants from 10 PCH families have been reported, involving 10 distinct TBC1D23 variants. Supplementary Table 3 summarizes these reported mutations. The patients originate from Turkey, Lebanon, Iran, Pakistan, and Egypt, with a gender ratio of 12 males to 9 females. Regional and ethnic backgrounds may influence the subtypes of PCH, with PCH11 predominantly found among Middle Eastern populations. This phenomenon is also observed in other subtypes. PCH10 has been exclusively observed in individuals of Turkish origin [26–28], while PCH2E has been identified in Moroccan Jews [29]. There is no evidence of sex differences affecting the phenotypic manifestations of PCH11. Symptoms in all patients typically manifest during infancy or early childhood, primarily as developmental delay, indicating the disease’s early-onset nature. According to available clinical information on PCH11 patients, the majority display low weight (14/15), short stature (12/14), microcephaly (16/16), facial dysmorphisms (9/14), large ears (8/8), hypotonia (8/9), psychomotor developmental delays (16/16), intellectual disabilities (16/16), speech delays (9/10), cerebellar syndromes (9/11), walking difficulties (8/9), poor motor coordination (6/8), spastic hypertonia (7/8), autism (7/7), pontocerebellar hypoplasia (15/15), corpus callosum dysgenesis (9/12), and cortical dysplasia (5/9); a few also have ocular disorders (4/7) and epilepsy (2/9)(Supplementary Table 2).

This study identifies and reports a novel homozygous truncating pathogenic variant in the TBC1D23 gene, c.511_512delTT (p.F171Qfs*8), associated with Pontocerebellar Hypoplasia Type 11 (PCH11). Although PCH11 is known to result from loss-of-function (LoF) variants in TBC1D23, indicated by homozygous truncating variants [4–7, 30], clinical phenotypic heterogeneity among patients suggests a potentially more complex molecular regulatory mechanism. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the patient in this study demonstrated marked thinning of the corpus callosum, a reduced and flattened pons, and significant atrophy of cerebellar hemispheres and vermis (Fig. 1A), with corpus callosum thinning more severe than previously reported [4]. Additionally, the patient exhibited severe PCH11 phenotypes, including communication impairments characterized by the use of simple and non-fluent language, aggressive behavior, hyperactivity, repetitive movements, attention deficits, and inability to walk. Through an in-depth analysis of the novel variant, the study revealed partial NMD escape, aberrant biochemical properties of the resulting truncated protein, and associated effects on cellular function, thereby providing new insights into the phenotypic variability observed in PCH11.

The homozygous pathogenic variant in TBC1D23, c.511_512delTT (p.F171Qfs8), is consistent with the known pathogenic mechanism of PCH11. This variant results in an extremely early truncation of the TBC1D23 protein at the N-terminal TBC domain, causing the loss of critical functional regions, including the Rhodanese-like and PH domains, which are essential for Golgi localization and its bridging role in vesicle trafficking [11, 31]. Consistent with this, our immunofluorescence experiments clearly demonstrated that the truncated p.F171Qfs8 protein completely lost Golgi localization capability (Fig. 3D), directly substantiating severe LoF, which forms the molecular basis of PCH11 pathogenesis.

We further explored the NMD response of transcripts carrying PTCs in vivo. Using a zebrafish model, our study showed that the frameshift variant at the homologous position generates a PTC capable of activating the NMD pathway, leading to approximately 50% degradation of mutant mRNA in homozygotes (Fig. 2D). The NMD pathway, including its core molecular machinery (e.g., UPF1-3, SMG complex) and key surveillance mechanisms, is highly conserved between zebrafish and humans [13, 32]. Zebrafish embryos express maternal mRNAs of these factors early in development, maintained throughout embryogenesis [23] Studies indicate species-specific recognition sequences for NMD; structural features triggering NMD (e.g., upstream ORFs, start site-driven leaky scanning, introns in 3′UTRs) often are not conserved across homologous transcripts [33, 34]. This underscores caution in cross-species functional studies, as NMD regulation of a gene in one species may not replicate in another. NCBI BLAST analysis showed substantial homology between human TBC1D23 transcripts and exon 5 (carrying c.511_512delTT) with zebrafish (72% and 71% identity, respectively), supporting the suitability of the zebrafish model in this study. Mutant mRNAs containing PTCs are efficiently degraded in zebrafish, as demonstrated by ~ 5-fold reduction in slc24a5 and ~ 10-fold in GFP-β-tubulin reporter gene mRNAs containing PTC variants [35, 36]. Based on our zebrafish model findings, TBC1D23 mutant transcripts likely undergo incomplete NMD degradation. Partial escape from NMD may result in truncated proteins causing more severe or distinct phenotypes than complete LoF [17], analogous to truncated β-globin chains causing anemia and phenotypic variability in Becker muscular dystrophy [37, 38]. Partial NMD escape provides a biological basis for truncated protein production, emphasizing the need to examine the characteristics and cellular effects of these truncated proteins.

Next, we explored the biochemical characteristics of the truncated TBC1D23 protein p.F171Qfs8 through in vitro overexpression in HeLa cells. Remarkably, the truncated p.F171Qfs8 protein accumulated significantly higher intracellularly compared to wild-type TBC1D23, despite similar plasmids and transfection conditions (Fig. 3B and C). This phenomenon suggests abnormal stability of the truncated protein. Among the TBC protein family, TBC1D3 degradation depends on interactions with the CUL7 E3 ligase complex [39]. Moreover, the E3 ubiquitin ligase β-TrCP2 ubiquitinates and destabilizes TBC1D7 [40]. Degradation signal predictions revealed that the p.F171Qfs8 truncation caused the loss of several potential PEST degradation sequences and ubiquitination sites in the C-terminal region of TBC1D23 (Fig. 4A and B). Loss of these degradation signals likely makes the truncated protein resistant to proteasomal recognition and degradation, leading to enhanced stability and abnormal intracellular accumulation. In contrast, Ivanova and Marin-Valencia et al. detected no corresponding protein in patients carrying longer truncations such as c.1475_1476delTG (p.Val492Glyfs8), c.1687 + 2T > A (p.His534Trpfs36), and c.1687 + 1G > A (p.His534Trpfs36) [4, 5], possibly due to these longer truncated proteins retaining degradation signals and exposing degradation-prone regions, or due to enhanced degradation from new signals arising from extended frameshift regions [41–44]. TBC1D23 is highly expressed in fetal and adult brains, particularly in corpus callosum, pons, cerebellum, spinal cord, and skeletal muscle [4]. Studies indicate NMD efficiency in nervous (especially cerebellum and cortex) and reproductive tissues is significantly lower than in other tissues [16], and the brain’s ability to clear truncated proteins is relatively limited compared to metabolically active tissues, making accumulation of truncated proteins more likely [45]. Thus, partially NMD-escaping and stably accumulating truncated proteins identified in this study could constitute a key pathogenic mechanism contributing to variability in PCH11 phenotypes.

Neurodevelopmental disorders associated with pontocerebellar hypoplasia (PCH) are often linked to abnormalities in proliferation and differentiation of neural precursor cells. For example, CHMP1A, the gene associated with PCH8, limits the proliferation of neural progenitor cells by regulating the BMI1-INK4A axis, while knockout of the PCH7-associated gene Toe1 significantly suppresses neural stem cell (NSC) proliferation and reduces neuronal and glial differentiation [46–48]. We evaluated the impact of the truncated protein on cell proliferation activity. The CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that overexpression of the truncated p.F171Qfs*8 protein significantly inhibited HeLa cell proliferation, whereas wild-type TBC1D23 protein showed no such effect (Fig. 3E and F). These findings suggest that this truncated protein possesses potential cytotoxicity or the capability to disrupt normal cellular processes. Considering the recessive inheritance pattern and phenotypic heterogeneity in this family, we speculate that the truncated TBC1D23 protein p.F171Qfs*8, produced by partial escape from NMD, exacerbates neurodevelopmental defects caused by loss of TBC1D23 function through inhibition of cellular proliferation, thereby worsening the clinical phenotype observed in our patient. Conversely, heterozygotes retain sufficient functionality from a single normal allele (haplo-sufficiency [49]), and the truncated protein does not reach a threshold for adverse effects, resulting in normal phenotypes in carriers.

This study elucidates a composite pathogenic mechanism for a novel TBC1D23 variant, where LoF is likely exacerbated by a toxic truncated protein produced via partial NMD escape. These findings not only expand the mutational and phenotypic spectrum of PCH11 but also suggest new therapeutic avenues targeting NMD or the mutant protein itself [50, 51]. This study is not without limitations. The proposed composite pathogenic mechanism, derived from model systems, requires validation in a patient-specific context. Future work should therefore focus on patient-derived cells, such as iPSC-differentiated neurons, to confirm the partial NMD escape and assess the endogenous truncated protein’s stability and downstream cytotoxic effects. Furthermore, analyzing a larger PCH11 cohort is crucial to establish a broader correlation between the nature of different truncating variants and the observed clinical heterogeneity.

This study identifies a novel homozygous frameshift variant, c.511_512delTT, in TBC1D23, suggesting that the severe phenotype may arise from a composite pathogenic mechanism. We propose that a primary LoF from protein truncation is exacerbated by a secondary toxic effect from the truncated protein, which exhibits increased stability and is produced via partial escape from NMD. This model provides a new framework for understanding the phenotypic heterogeneity in PCH11 and expands the mutational spectrum of the disorder.
