# Clinical Meaningfulness of Donanemab in Early Symptomatic Alzheimer Disease: Data From the Randomized Phase 3 TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 Trial.
**Authors:** Atri A, Apostolova LG, Iwata A, Wessels AM, Atkins A, Lu M, Ye W, Ryan S, Doty EG
**Journal:** Neurol Clin Pract (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1212/CPJ.0000000000200621](https://doi.org/10.1212/CPJ.0000000000200621)

## Content

BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES: Understanding the meaningfulness of clinical trial outcomes is essential for people living with Alzheimer disease (AD) and their clinicians to make evidence-based shared treatment decisions in real-world clinical care. Donanemab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the insoluble form of β-amyloid found in plaques, significantly slows cognitive and functional decline of AD in participants with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or mild AD-related dementia. This analysis reviews the efficacy of donanemab across various clinical outcome assessments, using both published data and new complementary analyses to provide context on its potential benefits for patients and caregivers.

METHODS: We present findings from prespecified and post hoc analyses from the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trial. Clinical outcomes assessed were Integrated AD Rating Scale (iADRS), comprising the 13-item AD Assessment Scale-Cognitive Subscale (ADAS-Cog13) and AD Cooperative Study-Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (ADCS-iADL); Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR)-Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB) for clinical severity and individual cognitive and functional domains; CDR-Global for clinical severity stage progression; meaningful within-patient change (MWPC); and ADCS-Activities of Daily Living dependence score.

RESULTS: Donanemab reduced the risk of progression from MCI to mild AD by 33% (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.67; 95% CI 0.52-0.87; p = 0.003) and from mild to moderate AD by 50% (HR = 0.50; 95% CI 0.33-0.78; p = 0.002). In addition, donanemab reduced MWPC risk over 76 weeks by 38% for CDR-SB (HR = 0.62; 95% CI 0.52-0.75; p < 0.001) and 30% for iADRS (HR = 0.70; 95% CI 0.58-0.84; p < 0.001). Donanemab-treated participants exhibited significant slowing of clinical progression across multiple ADAS-Cog13 and ADCS-iADL items and all CDR-SB cognitive and functional domains. Donanemab also slowed progression of dependence least-squares mean change difference, -0.14 [95% CI -0.24 to -0.04; p = 0.007]), representing 23% slowing of progression (95% CI 6.17%-40.32%), and reduced risk of progression to requiring in-home support by 27% (HR = 0.74; 95% CI 0.59-0.91; p = 0.005).

DISCUSSION: These results add to the evidence and further support clinically meaningful donanemab-mediated effects on cognition and function for patients and their caregivers and may aid communication of realistic treatment expectations and informed decision-making.

Alzheimer disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder associated with progressive loss of cognitive and functional abilities that ultimately leads to severe disability and dependence on others for care.1 It is the leading cause of dementia globally, contributing to 60%–70% of the 57 million people living with dementia.2 In the United States alone, approximately 6.9 million Americans 65 years or older are living with Alzheimer dementia.1 The complex neuropathology of AD begins clinically silently decades before symptoms manifest. Brain accumulation of β-amyloid plaques occurs early in the disease process, up to 15–25 years before symptom onset, and is followed by formation of neurofibrillary tangles of intraneuronal hyperphosphorylated tau protein.3 This and other downstream pathologic changes, including neuroinflammatory dysregulation and vasculopathy, lead to progressive synaptic, glial, and neuronal dysfunction and degeneration and ultimately to progressive symptoms and impactful decline in cognition, function, and behavior.4 Arguably, the greatest impact of AD for patients and their caregivers is on quality of life.5 The burden of disease takes a devastating emotional, health, and financial toll on individuals, families, communities, and healthcare systems.1,6

Until recently, pharmacotherapy for AD has been limited to symptomatic therapies (e.g., acetylcholinesterase inhibitors and memantine) that address cognitive and behavioral manifestations of the disease but do not address the underlying cause or change the course of the disease.1 At a group level, the benefit of symptomatic therapies are variable across the continuum of symptomatic disease, the slope of clinical decline is not altered, and symptoms worsen when treatment is stopped.7,8 Decades of persistent research, building on incremental learnings from negative trials and advancements in measurement of neuropathologic biomarkers of disease, have resulted in the emergence of disease-modifying therapies.9 Monoclonal antibodies with greater effects on removing amyloid plaques from the brain have shown significant impacts on substantially reducing or clearing amyloid plaques and demonstrated efficacy for slowing clinical progression, including reducing the slope of clinical decline.9,10 Donanemab is an immunoglobulin G1 monoclonal antibody that targets the N-terminal-truncated, modified, insoluble form of β-amyloid specific to brain amyloid plaques.10,11 In the phase 3 TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 study, donanemab treatment significantly lowered brain amyloid levels (amyloid PET) and slowed the trajectory of cognitive and functional decline in participants with early symptomatic AD, compared with placebo, over the 18-month trial period.10 In addition, donanemab-treated participants had a lower risk of disease progression to the next clinical stage than participants who received placebo, by the Clinical Dementia Rating scale (CDR)–Global (CDR-G) score, and exhibited higher clinical stability (defined as no change in the CDR–Sum of Boxes [CDR-SB] score at 1 year).11

While clinical trials of amyloid-targeting therapies have demonstrated statistically significant between-treatment group benefits, whether these benefits are meaningful for patients and care partners has been the subject of debate. Clinical “meaningfulness” is a conceptual, and latent, construct that is highly multidimensional and challenging to measure with a single clinical outcome assessment or analytic method across the clinical spectrum of disease.12-14 In addition, the definition and impact of a clinically meaningful treatment benefit depends on the stakeholder's perspective, whether that be the patient, caregiver, clinician, or payer.13 Thus, it is important to consider treatment effects across clinical outcome assessments and complementary analyses to help contextualize disease-modifying treatment outcomes for people living with AD, their care partners, their clinicians, and other stakeholders.12,15

Communicating the clinical meaningfulness of study outcomes to patients living with AD and their caregivers is paramount to promoting informed, evidence-based decisions in clinical care.16-20 The results from several surveys support that patients, caregivers, and clinicians highly value treatments that slow clinical progression such that patients remain at earlier clinical stages of the disease for longer.17,18 A slowing of clinical progression, including the ability to maintain their current level of independence, spend more time with loved ones, and participate in activities that matter most to them for longer, translates to meaningful outcomes for individuals with AD and their caregivers.10,21

This report reviews the efficacy of donanemab across several clinical outcome assessments and analytic approaches. In addition to previously published data,10,11 we report complementary analyses to contextualize the meaningful benefit of donanemab treatment in ways that may be more readily interpretable and that may better resonate with people living with AD, caregivers, and practicing clinicians.

This report presents a series of prespecified and post hoc analyses from the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trial to contribute to the understanding of clinical meaningfulness of donanemab treatment.

The full study design has been previously published.10 Participant flow diagram is found in supplemental information (eFigure 1) and has been published in full previously.10 Trial participants were 60–85 years. A ≥ 6-month history of gradual and progressive change in memory function reported by either participant or informant was required. Eligible participants had a baseline Mini-Mental State Examination score of 20–28 and presence of amyloid and tau pathology, confirmed by florbetapir F18 and flortaucipir F18 PET scans, respectively. Participants were stratified by tau pathology into low/medium and high tau10 groups by visual and quantitative reads of the PET scans. As tau PET quantification is not routine clinical practice, the analyses of clinical outcomes in this report focus on the overall population.

Eligible participants were assigned at random to receive donanemab (700 mg for the first 3 doses, followed by 1,400 mg for subsequent doses) or placebo. The study drug was administered by IV injection every 4 weeks for up to 72 weeks. Amyloid plaque level was assessed by PET scan at 24 and 52 weeks. Participants with amyloid plaque levels of <11 Centiloids on any single PET scan or <25 but ≥11 Centiloids on 2 consecutive scans were switched from donanemab to placebo in a blinded procedure.

Meaningful within-patient change (MWPC) is defined as the smallest magnitude of change on a clinical outcome assessment that can be indicated by the instrument and of sufficient value to be of importance to an individual patient, their loved one, or clinician, essentially an impactful degree of change (change threshold) in expected clinical progression, such as worsening of a symptom(s) or diminishment or loss of an ability (or abilities), that would be of “meaning” to a person. This threshold evaluates individual patient change and is not fit for interpreting between-group differences such as mean change from baseline-to-end point differences between drug and placebo groups. The MWPC threshold is dependent on the outcome/instrument, time, and clinical stage—with differences across clinical outcome measures, length of time under consideration, and levels of clinical disease severity. In TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2, MWPC for the CDR-SB was defined as a change of ≥1 point for mild cognitive dementia (MCI) due to AD and ≥2 points for mild dementia due to AD at 2 consecutive visits from baseline. MWPC for the Integrated Alzheimer's Disease Rating Scale (iADRS) was defined as a change of ≥5 points for MCI due to AD and ≥9 points for mild dementia due to AD, respectively, at 2 consecutive visits. These MWPC thresholds were established based on a triangulation of approaches (anchor-based, distribution-based, and cumulative distribution plots), which is a recommended approach by the US Food and Drug Administration.22

Patient dependence, including the nature and extent of assistance needed for daily tasks, was evaluated using a revised 7-point AD Cooperative Study (ADCS)-Activities of Daily Living (ADCS-ADL) dependence score (DS) (range: 0–6) derived from responses provided on the partner-reported ADCS-ADL using a validated algorithm. Participant scores were assigned by the algorithm as follows: 0 if fully independent (no care needs), 3 if mildly dependent (requires frequent in-home support), and 6 if fully dependent (requires 24-h care/supervision).23

The electronic statistical analysis plan (eSAP2) for the study can be found in the supplementary information. All analyses in this report are for exploratory purposes and were not controlled for multiple comparisons. A Cox proportional hazards model was applied to assess the risk of progressing to the MWPC by treatment arm, as well as the similar analyses of progression to the next disease stage (from MCI to mild AD or from MCI or mild AD to moderate AD) and the need for frequent in-home support (DS ≥3). The models were adjusted for baseline score, age, and AD medication use, with investigational sites and baseline tau categories (designated as low/medium or high tau by quantitative and visual reads)24-28 as stratifying factors to allow for unique baseline hazard function within each individual level of these factors. Ties in event times were handled using the discrete method, as the trial measurements were collected at scheduled visits and the exact event times were unknown. The number needed to treat (NNT) was calculated based on the proportion of participants progressed to the event by treatment arm as the inverse of absolute risk reduction. In addition, the number needed to harm (NNH) was calculated as the inverse of the absolute risk increase, based on the proportion of participants who experienced the event in each treatment arm. A mixed model for repeated measures was used to assess the treatment difference for individual domain box scores of the CDR and item scores of the 13-item cognitive subscale of the AD Assessment Scale (ADAS-Cog13) and the ADCS-Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (ADCS-iADL). The models included fixed factors of treatment, visit (as a categorical variable), and treatment-by-visit interaction and were adjusted for baseline score, age, AD medication use, investigational sites, and baseline tau category. The natural cubic spline (NCS) with 2° (NCS2) model was used to analyze DS. The NCS basis functions with knot locations at 0 weeks, the median observation time, and 76 weeks, were created using the protocol-specified week value for each participant. The model restricted baseline estimates were the same for treatment and placebo groups. Dependent variables in the model were the baseline score and each scheduled postbaseline score. The model's independent variables included NCS basis expansion terms (2 terms), NCS basis expansion term × treatment interaction (2 terms), baseline age, concomitant acetylcholinesterase inhibitor and/or memantine use at baseline (yes/no), and randomization stratifying factors (pooled site and baseline tau category [baseline tau category in combined population only]). The degrees of freedom for the mixed model for repeated measures and NCS2 model were handled with the Kenward-Roger approximation, and within-subject correlation was adjusted using an unstructured variance-covariance matrix. A shift analysis was performed to show the number of participants in each treatment group that progressed from one clinical stage to the next. The shift of the CDR-G score to the next level was summarized by treatment arm, and trends between arms were compared using the Cochran-Armitage trend test.

TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 was a 76-week, phase 3, multicenter, placebo-controlled, randomized, double-blind, parallel trial that enrolled participants across 277 sites in 8 countries. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04437511) on June 17, 2020. Patient enrollment ran to November 5, 2021, and database lock of the double-blind phase occurred on April 28, 2023. The trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Conference on Harmonization guidelines for Good Clinical Practice, and local regulatory requirements. The study protocol, available as electronic statistical analysis plan (eSAP1) was approved at each site by independent ethics committees/institutional review boards. All trial participants and their study partners provided written informed consent. Trial oversight was provided by an independent data and safety monitoring board.

In the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 study, participants with MCI and mild dementia due to AD who were treated with donanemab experienced significant slowing of cognitive and functional decline, relative to participants who received placebo, as measured by the iADRS and CDR-SB.10,11 We report additional complementary post hoc analyses that further support the meaningful benefits of donanemab. These cumulative findings help contextualize the clinical relevance of slowing the rate of disease progression and support interpretation and communication of the meaningful treatment benefits of donanemab for patients living with AD, caregivers, and other stakeholders.

The meaningful benefits of donanemab treatment over 76 weeks are demonstrated in multiple ways in both the overall and low-medium tau populations including slowing clinical progression of disease, reducing the risk of MWPC on measures of clinical decline, slowing progression across several individual iADRS items, both cognitive and functional, and across all 6 domains of the CDR, and slowing progression of dependence on others for completion of daily tasks (Table 3, eTable 1, eTable 2). The findings are consistent across multiple clinical outcome measures and complementary analyses. Treatment with donanemab resulted in statistically significant slowing of disease progression, including progression from MCI to mild AD dementia, and progression from mild AD dementia to moderate AD dementia. Slowing clinical progression along the disease continuum matters to patients, care partners, and families because it enables the person living with AD to stay at earlier stages of disease for longer, when they may have more independence and autonomy; and such delays appear particularly impactful and valued in early symptomatic AD.11 For example, a transition from MCI to mild dementia is a meaningful change for patients and their caregivers, marked by a significant loss of independence. With this transition, the person with AD may become dependent on others for tasks such as shopping, managing financial affairs, and preparing cooked meals (eFigure 2). This can also include withdrawal from hobbies and social activities. A transition from mild dementia to moderate dementia further erodes independence with the person living with AD potentially becoming fully reliant on others for instrumental activities of daily living and needing assistance with personal care. The changes that occur with clinical progression of disease have a significant impact on how a person living with AD functions in their daily life and on those who provide care.33,34 With each step of progression through the clinical stages of disease, the person with AD recedes further from engaging in activities they enjoy and the burden of caregiving increases.35

Treatment with donanemab significantly reduced the risk of MWPC across the CDR-SB and iADRS at 76 weeks36 The reduced risk of MWPC means that participants treated with donanemab had a lower risk of experiencing a meaningful decline in cognition and function and progression of dementia severity during the study. This impactful reduction in progression of disability at an individual patient level further supports the clinical meaningfulness of treatment with donanemab for patients and their loved ones and provides another way to communicate and to help inform patients and their care partners, so they can better make personalized decisions regarding risk/burden and benefit.

Donanemab treatment had a significant effect on all cognitive and functional domains of the CDR-SB, items in the episodic memory and executive functioning domains for the ADAS-Cog13, and items in the communication, household, and outdoor activities clusters for the ADCS-iADL. The consistent results observed across multiple items and domains of the clinical outcome assessments (Figure 3) support that treatment benefits with donanemab are not driven by impact on just 1 component of disease, such as memory. These robust findings impacting salient abilities are considered meaningful by patients, care partners, and clinicians.16,17

This was also reflected at the group level where, on average, donanemab-treated participants experienced significantly less decline in independent functioning compared with participants who received placebo. Although dependence increased in both groups, the donanemab-treated participants experienced a reduced loss of independence throughout the treatment period.​ The preservation of independence and functional abilities means that patients may continue engaging in activities they and their families enjoy and want to do, with less need for care from others.17

Understanding trial outcomes in terms of potential time saved with treatment and clinical stability (no progression) are also tangible concepts for patients and their caregivers, as well as for clinicians. In the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 overall population, time saved with donanemab treatment was reported as 2.5 months, as indicated by the iADRS, and 5.4 months, as indicated by the CDR-SB, over the 18-month clinical trial in the overall population.10 In addition, Klein et al.11 previously reported that 36% of those treated with donanemab in the overall population vs 23% of placebo recipients demonstrated clinical stability, defined as no change in the CDR-SB score at 1 year (p < 0.001). Having further time in less impaired, more functional stages of disease, is meaningful and notable for patients and care providers as it is essential for maintaining independence and autonomy.14

Conversations between patients and their healthcare providers may include discussion of the potential benefits of treatment alongside the observed risks. Some of the most common safety findings reported for the class of monoclonal antibodies directed against aggregated forms of β-amyloid include amyloid-related imaging abnormalities of edema/effusion (ARIA-E) and infusion-related reactions (IRRs).37 The NNT and NNH are considered to be intuitively meaningful and potentially useful tools for clinicians to translate the potential benefits and risks of a treatment from clinical research into clinical practice.38,39 In the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 overall population, the NNT with donanemab ranged from 8.5 to slow clinical progression from MCI to mild AD dementia, to 21.3 to slow progression from mild dementia to moderate AD dementia over 76 weeks. The NNH for the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 dosing regimen was 16.7 for symptomatic ARIA-E and 12.2 for IRRs. In the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 6 study, a modified dosing regimen resulted in a significantly lower risk of ARIA-E.40 Thus, the NNH for the modified dosing regimen should be considered in risk-benefit conversations in markets where it has been approved for use.

While the calculation of NNT and NNH may help to contextualize benefit and risk, it has several limitations.39 The NNT does not capture the potential treatment benefits at the individual patient level and can be limited by the time interval of the study. Assessing slowing of clinical progression in AD with treatment over a longer time interval, such as from long-term real-world evidence, may yield a more clinically relevant NNT. Conversely, the NNH can overstate the associated treatment risks for common side effects such as ARIA-E and IRRs, particularly when these events tend to be relatively brief, self-limited, and may not disrupt treatment. Notably, most clinical trial participants who experienced symptomatic ARIA-E and IRRs saw their symptoms resolve. Appropriately contextualizing the benefits and risks of this class of treatment will be imperative to assure realistic treatment expectations.41

The arrival of disease-modifying therapies has demonstrated that slowing of clinical progression is possible for those living with MCI and mild dementia due to AD. Translating clinical trial results into concepts that are more easily interpretable for clinicians, patients, and their loved ones may help facilitate treatment planning conversations. By reducing progression across clinical stages with donanemab, patients may be able to maintain greater independence with the help of lists or reminders,17 manage personal finances without or with minimal assistance,17 continue to participate in some social activities, maintain hobbies,17 and perform self-care activities such as bathing, cooking, dressing, and eating without requiring assistance.17 Maintaining earlier stages of the disease for longer reduces the burden of caregiving and need for advanced care.17 Slowing the clinical progression of disease supports patients living with AD maintaining, for longer, their current level of independence and abilities, as well as the ability to participate in activities most important to them.17-20

In summary, the totality of evidence supports the clinically meaningful impact of donanemab treatment on cognition and function and provides benefits for patients and their caregivers.
