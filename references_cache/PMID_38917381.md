# Antibodies Against ZSCAN1 in Pediatric and Adult Patients With Non-Paraneoplastic ROHHAD Syndrome.
**Authors:** Serafim AB, Olivé-Cirera G, Ortega-González Á, Kruer MC, Weese-Mayer D, Rand CM, Fons C, Fernández-Ramos JA, Clemente M, Simabukuro MM, Embiruçu EK, Ibáñez-Micó S, Dalmau JO, Graus F, Armangué T, Sabater L
**Journal:** Neurol Neuroimmunol Neuroinflamm (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1212/NXI.0000000000200276](https://doi.org/10.1212/NXI.0000000000200276)

## Content

OBJECTIVES: To report the association of zinc finger and SCAN domain containing 1 antibodies (ZSCAN1-abs) with rapid-onset obesity, hypothalamic dysfunction, hypoventilation, and autonomic dysregulation (ROHHAD) syndrome in patients without tumor.

METHODS: Patients with symptoms compatible with ROHHAD syndrome but without an associated tumor were selected from our database. Serum and CSF samples were examined for the presence of ZSCAN1-abs by an in-house cell-based assay. In addition, samples from 149 patients with several inflammatory and noninflammatory disorders and 50 healthy participants served as controls.

RESULTS: Thirteen patients with ROHHAD syndrome were identified. Of these, we had paired serum/CSF samples from 6 patients and only serum from the other 7. Five of 6 patients (83.3%) with paired serum/CSF (4 children, 1 adult) had ZSCAN-abs only in CSF and 1 had antibodies in serum and CSF. ZSCAN1-abs were not detected in the remaining 7 patients with ROHHAD with only serum available or in any of the 199 control samples.

DISCUSSION: Patients with ROHHAD syndrome should be investigated for the presence of ZSCAN1-abs in CSF. The antibodies do not necessarily predict the presence of a tumor. The detection of ZSCAN1-abs in an adult patient suggests that this condition also occurs beyond the pediatric age.

Rapid-onset obesity with hypothalamic dysfunction, hypoventilation, and autonomic dysregulation (ROHHAD) syndrome is a rare disease characterized by hypothalamic dysfunction, including hyperphagia, sleep disorder with obstructive sleep apnea, behavioral problems, hypernatremia, and endocrinopathies. 1 , 2 ROHHAD syndrome has a high morbidity and mortality due to cardiorespiratory arrest secondary to central hypoventilation. The diagnosis is based on clinical criteria defined in 2007 including (1) onset of rapid and extreme weight gain after age 1.5 years (typically 2–7 years) in a previously nonobese and seemingly normal child, (2) evidence of hypothalamic dysfunction, (3) alveolar hypoventilation, and (4) features of autonomic dysregulation. 1 The diagnosis can be challenging because of the clinical similarities with other pediatric disorders caused by genetic alterations such as congenital central hypoventilation syndrome caused by mutations in the PHOX2B gene, and Prader-Willi syndrome produced by a loss of function of genes on chromosome 15. 3 - 6 The pathogenesis of ROHHAD syndrome has been suggested to be immune-mediated because of the frequent coexistence with neuroblastic tumors such as neuroblastoma, ganglioneuroblastoma, or ganglioneuroma, which can potentially trigger the neurologic syndrome through paraneoplastic mechanisms. 7 Antibodies targeting the zinc finger and SCAN domain-containing protein 1 (ZSCAN1) were recently identified in 7 of 9 pediatric patients with both ROHHAD syndrome and an underlying neuroblastic tumor, suggesting these antibodies as distinct biomarkers of paraneoplastic ROHHAD syndrome in children. 8 We aimed to examine whether ZSCAN1 antibodies (ZSCAN1-abs) might also occur in children without an underlying tumor or in adults suspected to have this disorder.

Thirteen patients fulfilling diagnostic criteria of ROHHAD syndrome 1 without tumor association were retrospectively identified in our database. From 6 of them, we had paired serum and CSF samples, and from the other 7 patients, only serum was available. As controls, we included sera and/or CSF of 90 patients with inflammatory diseases: 43 (CSF: 39) with opsoclonus-myoclonus (10 with neuroblastoma and 6 with teratoma; median age: 2 years; age range 2 months to 22 years); 32 (CSF: 32) with NMDAR encephalitis (10 with teratoma; median age: 21 years; range 3 to 43 years); and 15 (CSF: 11) with seronegative autoimmune encephalitis (10 with tumor; median age: 10 years; range 3 to 15 years). Additional controls included 59 (CSF: 55) patients without inflammatory diseases (9 with psychiatric disorders [median age: 12.7 years; range 6 to 16 years] and 50 with Alzheimer disease) and 50 sera from adult healthy blood donors.

Anonymized data not published within this article will be made available by request from any qualified investigator.

HEK293 cells were cultured and transfected as previously described. 9 In brief, HEK293 cells cultured on poly-d-lysine coated coverslips with human ZSCAN1 MYC-DDK-tagged plasmid (RC221074, Origene, Rockville, MD) using lipofectamine-2000 (Thermo-Fisher, Waltham, MA). Twelve hours later, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 5 minutes. Transfected cells were then incubated overnight at 4°C with serum (1:50) or CSF (1:5) of patients or controls and subsequently with mouse anti-MYC-tag antibody (2276S; diluted 1:2,500, from Cell Signalling Technology) or ZSCAN1 polyclonal antibody made from rabbit (PA552488, Thermo-Fisher) for 1 hour at room temperature. Then, fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies (goat anti-human Alexa-fluor 488 plus goat anti-mouse Alexa-fluor 594 or goat-anti-rabbit Alexa-fluor 594, diluted 1:1,000, from Thermo-Fisher) were applied for 1 hour at room temperature. Results were visualized on an Axioscope microscope (ZEISS, Jena).

The study was approved by the internal review board of Hospital Clinic (#HCB/2018/0192 protocol). Consent was obtained from all patients or proxies for the use of samples for research purposes.

ZSCAN1-abs were identified in 5 of 6 patients with ROHHAD syndrome (83.3%, 2 female, 3 male patients) with paired serum and CSF available (Figure and Table). Four were positive only in CSF and one in serum and CSF. Four of the ZSCAN1-abs–positive patients (1 female, 3 male patients) were children (median age 3.6 years, range: 22 months to 7 years). The clinical features of 2 patients were previously published, one before the discovery of ZSCAN1-abs (patient 1) 10 and the other (patient 2) without determination of these antibodies. 11 This was a 58-year-old woman with history of autoimmune hepatitis, exhibiting rapid-onset obesity, hyperphagia, sleepiness, bradycardia, central hypoventilation, and dysautonomia (Table). CSF studies showed mild pleocytosis in 1 of 4 patients, without oligoclonal bands in 3 of 3 patients. Brain MRI was normal in 4 of 5 patients; one patient showed atrophy of the left hippocampus. Three patients with ZSCAN1-abs received first-line immunotherapy, and one of them who also received cyclophosphamide showed mild improvement. Seven additional patients with ROHHAD syndrome but with only serum available (median age 11 years (range 9–22); 4 male patients) were all ZSCAN1-abs negative. The clinical features for patients with and without ZSCAN1-abs were similar (eTable 1). Samples from all 199 controls tested were negative for ZSCAN1-abs.

The discovery of ZSCAN1-abs as a biomarker of ROHHAD syndrome in children with an underlying tumor has strengthened the hypothesis of the immune-mediated pathogenesis of the syndrome. Our study confirms the previously reported association between ROHHAD syndrome and ZSCAN1-abs in children 8 and expands the clinical profile to patients without tumor who might also include adults with the same syndrome.

ZSCAN1-abs were initially identified in 7 of 9 children with ROHHAD syndrome, all of them with a ganglioneuroma or neuroblastoma. 8 By contrast, in our series of 5 patients with ROHHAD syndrome, no tumor was detected after a long follow-up or before they died, indicating that patients with idiopathic ROHHAD syndrome should also be tested for these antibodies. Our findings are in line with a recent study that detected ZSCAN1-abs in serum of 12 of 14 pediatric patients without tumor with an in-house ELISA. 12 However, this study only used 20 control samples (15 from healthy individuals and 5 from patients with autoimmune encephalitis), and additional studies with a larger number of control samples are needed to confirm the utility of the ELISA test. In our study, ZSCAN1-abs were preferentially detected in CSF when examined with cell-based assay (CBA), and only one patient was positive in the paired serum. By contrast, none of the 7 patients with ROHHAD syndrome and only serum available was found positive for ZSCAN1-abs. Thus, our findings are similar to those of the initial report on ZSCAN1-abs where the 2 patients with only serum available were found to be antibody negative. Taken together, these data suggest that CSF is the sample of choice when ZSCAN1-abs are tested by CBA in patients with suspected ROHHAD syndrome.

Before the description of ZSCAN1-abs, antibodies against rat subfornical organ had been described in serum of 11 patients with ROHHAD syndrome using indirect immunofluorescence with rat brain perfused and postfixed with 20% formalin. 13 Although ZSCAN1 does not have a rodent ortholog, ZSCAN1 immunoreactivity with the rat subfornical organ was also detected with a commercial ZSCAN1 rabbit polyclonal antibody. Clarification of the antigen recognized by these immunohistochemistry studies will be important to establish a relationship, if any, between ZSCAN1 and rat subfornical organ antibodies. Our findings have several important clinical implications. First, ZSCAN1-abs should be tested preferentially in the CSF of patients with suspected ROHHAD syndrome irrespective of the presence or absence of a tumor. Second, findings of the current and previous studies suggesting an immune-mediated pathogenesis of ROHHAD syndrome together with the poor prognosis of the disease (and the absence of alternative treatments) support a trial of immunotherapy in patients who have severe, progressive, or refractory symptoms, as suggested by the mild improvement observed in one patient who received cyclophosphamide. Finally, the identification of an adult patient with ROHHAD and ZSCAN1-abs suggests that the syndrome might be under-recognized in adults with obesity associated with endocrinological alterations, apneas, and hypoventilation.
