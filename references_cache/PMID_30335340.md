# Premenstrual Disorders.
**Authors:** Miller C, Carlson K

## Content

Premenstrual symptoms encompass a constellation of mood, behavioral, and physical manifestations that follow a predictable cyclic pattern. These symptoms develop during the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle, typically beginning 1 to 2 weeks before menstruation and resolving shortly after menstrual bleeding begins. Most females experience only mild discomfort, with minimal or no disruption of personal, social, or professional activities. In contrast, 5% to 8% of women develop moderate-to-severe symptoms that result in significant distress and functional impairment. Women affected by moderate-to-severe premenstrual syndrome (PMS) or premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) experience greater reductions in quality of life, more substantial losses in work productivity, and higher healthcare utilization and associated costs than women with absent or only mild premenstrual symptoms. Although many females experience common premenstrual symptoms, PMS refers specifically to the subset of women whose symptoms reach sufficient severity to impair daily functioning and routine activities. PMDD, recognized as an official psychiatric diagnosis in 2013, represents the most severe manifestation of the premenstrual disorder spectrum and produces the greatest degree of functional impairment and diminished perceived quality of life. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), classifies PMDD as a distinct diagnosis within the category of Depressive Disorders. Diagnosis of PMDD requires fulfillment of specific criteria during the majority of menstrual cycles over the preceding year. The following components comprise the criteria that must be met to diagnose PMDD: Criterion A (timing): Symptoms must occur during the final week before the onset of menstruation, begin to improve within a few days after menstrual bleeding starts, and resolve or become minimal during the week following menses. Criteria B and C (symptoms): At least 5 of the following 11 symptoms must occur, including at least 1 of the first 4 core symptoms. Core symptoms include: Markedly depressed mood, feelings of hopelessness, or self-deprecating thoughts. Marked anxiety, tension, or feelings of being "keyed up" or "on edge". Marked affective lability. Persistent and marked anger or irritability, or increased interpersonal conflict. Additional symptoms include: Decreased interest in usual activities, including work, school, friendships, and hobbies. Subjective difficulty with concentration. Lethargy, easy fatigability, or marked lack of energy. Marked changes in appetite, overeating, or specific food cravings. Hypersomnia or insomnia. A subjective sense of feeling overwhelmed or out of control. Physical symptoms (eg, breast tenderness or swelling, headaches, joint or muscle pain, bloating, or weight gain). Criterion D (severity): Symptoms must cause clinically significant impairment in social, occupational, sexual, relational, or academic functioning. Criterion E (exclusion of exacerbation): Symptom occurrence must demonstrate a clear relationship to the menstrual cycle rather than representing an exacerbation of another psychiatric disorder, eg, major depressive disorder, panic disorder, dysthymic disorder, or a personality disorder, although premenstrual symptoms may occur concurrently with these conditions. Criterion F (prospective confirmation): Prospective daily symptom ratings collected across at least 2 consecutive symptomatic menstrual cycles must confirm fulfillment of Criteria A through D. Clinicians may establish a provisional diagnosis before prospective confirmation becomes available. Criterion G (exclusion of alternative causes): Symptoms must not result from substance use, medication adverse effects, or another medical condition, including disorders (eg, hyperthyroidism).
