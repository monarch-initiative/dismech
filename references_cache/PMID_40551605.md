# Occupational Lead Exposure Among Civilian Indoor Shooting Range Workers in Korea: A Report of Blood Lead Levels and Airborne Lead.
**Authors:** Park S, Park J, Lee B, Lee YR, Kim J, Cho Y, Choi H, Kim K
**Journal:** J Korean Med Sci (2025)
**DOI:** [10.3346/jkms.2025.40.e116](https://doi.org/10.3346/jkms.2025.40.e116)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Indoor shooting ranges have emerged as a major source of lead exposure. However, occupational lead exposure in civilian indoor shooting ranges has been understudied in South Korea. This study aimed to assess blood lead levels (BLLs) and airborne lead concentrations among workers at these establishments.

METHODS: This descriptive observational study included 21 workers from three indoor shooting ranges in South Korea. BLL was measured using atomic absorption spectrophotometry, and airborne lead concentrations were assessed using personal air sampling devices. Data regarding occupational history, job type, and work area were collected through interviews.

RESULTS: The mean BLL among workers was 39.82 µg/dL, with shooting coaches exhibiting the highest levels, substantially exceeding those of the general population. Airborne lead concentrations near firing zones were approximately seven times higher than the occupational exposure limit. Both air lead levels and BLL differed significantly based on job role and work area within the shooting ranges, showing a strong correlation.

CONCLUSION: This study highlights considerable lead exposure in civilian indoor shooting ranges, indicating the urgent need for improved safety regulations and preventive measures to mitigate lead exposure risks. Enhanced ventilation systems, regular health screenings, and better education on safety practices are recommended to protect workers' health.

Occupational lead exposure in industries such as battery manufacturing and smelting has been extensively documented and is regulated by the law. 1 2 3 Consequently, lead exposure in developed countries, 4 including South Korea, has significantly decreased over the past few decades, except for a few industries such as smelting. 5 6 7 8 Lead levels among workers in lead-related industries have steadily declined over the past 30 years. 9

However, indoor shooting ranges have emerged as a new source of lead exposure. Globally, shooting sports are the second-largest source of lead emissions, following the battery industry. 10 In the United States and Europe, where firearms are more widespread, research has highlighted lead exposure among shooting range workers and users due to lead-containing ammunitions. 11

Increasing interest in shooting sports has led to an increase in indoor shooting ranges and potentially exposed populations. It is estimated that there are 16,000–18,000 indoor ranges in the United States alone, employing thousands of workers and serving over a million law enforcement officers. 12 In South Korea, approximately 15–20 indoor shooting ranges are operational, reflecting growing public interest in shooting sports.

Lead and antimony particles are the primary metals emitted from lead-based ammunition. Previous studies have established a positive correlation between shooting activities and elevated blood lead levels (BLL). 11 13 14 15 A recent review of 36 studies across 15 countries found that BLLs exceeded 10 µg/dL and > 40 µg/dL in 31 and 15 studies, respectively, significantly higher than the general population’s typical levels of 1–2 µg/dL. 11 Chronic lead poisoning cases among shooting range workers, characterized by BLLs > 40 µg/dL and symptoms such as paresthesia, hypertension, abdominal cramps, and fatigue, are increasingly reported. 8 16 17

In February 2024, a case of acute lead poisoning was reported in a worker at an indoor shooting range in Seoul, South Korea. The worker, employed for just 1 month, experienced severe abdominal pain and constipation. The airborne lead concentration in the shooting range was more than nine times the permissible exposure limit (0.05 mg/m3). This incident highlighted the potential health risks faced by workers in civilian indoor shooting ranges, prompting an investigation by the Korea Occupational Safety and Health Agency (KOSHA). The agency conducted a thorough investigation, assessing the workplace environments of several indoor shooting ranges and conducting special health examinations on workers.

While most lead exposure studies in South Korea have focused on military personnel owing to restrictions on civilian firearm use, the growing interest in shooting sports among civilians highlights the urgent need for research on occupational lead exposure in civilian settings. A 2014 study reported BLLs of 11.33 ± 8.7 µg/dL (range: 2.0–64.0 µg/dL) among 120 military indoor shooting range workers 8 ; however, little is known about exposure levels in civilian ranges. This study aimed to fill this gap by reporting BLLs and airborne lead concentrations among workers in civilian indoor shooting ranges and highlighting the need for improved safety measures and regulations to protect their health.

This study was conducted at three indoor shooting ranges: two located in Seoul (each with 6 employees) and one in another region (nine employees), to be referred to as “Range 3” for convenience and clarity. A total of 21 workers participated in the study, conducted by the Korean Medical Institute (KMI), a health screening center and medical institution in South Korea. The study period for the Seoul ranges and Range 3 were from April 22–25 and April 24 to May 25, 2024, respectively.

All participants underwent a special health examination focused on BLLs. Occupational medicine specialists conducted the examinations through individual interviews, gathering detailed work histories, current job roles, access to shooting areas, smoking, and drinking habits, and clinical symptoms potentially related to lead exposure. Job roles were generally categorized into shooting coaches, safety managers, and sales or customer service personnel; however, workers often had overlapping duties due to the small size of these shooting ranges. For example, safety managers responsible for supervising range safety occasionally perform coaching tasks when necessary. Additionally, tasks such as collecting bullet casings, tidying up or cleaning the range, and maintaining firearms were often performed by workers in roles other than coaching. Clinical symptoms were assessed using structured self-report questionnaires, which included inquiries about general (headaches, fatigue), gastrointestinal (abdominal pain), musculoskeletal (muscle pain), neurological (numbness in hands), cardiovascular (chest pain, palpitations), and respiratory symptoms (dyspnea, cough). Following the interview, participants underwent blood pressure measurements and urine tests (including pH, hematuria, proteinuria, and leukocyturia), along with blood sampling to measure BLLs and complete blood counts.

The researchers obtained internal approval for data use and passed a data usage suitability assessment using KMI health examination data. The provided data were anonymized by removing personally identifiable information such as names and social security numbers. Consequently, this study was waived by the institutional review board. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

To determine the levels of lead exposure, BLLs were measured using standardized methods from the KOSHA (KOSHA GUIDE H-21-2021). 18 Whole blood samples were diluted with 1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and analyzed using graphite furnace atomic absorption spectrophotometry (ICE 3400Z; Thermo Fisher, Cheshire, UK), calibrated using deuterium (D2) or Zeeman background correction methods.

Work environment measurements were conducted in March 2024 under typical working conditions, without any experimental manipulation to alter the environment. Air lead concentrations were measured by collecting both area and employee personal samples at each shooting range. A total of 21 samples were collected from personal breathing zones 1.5 meters above the ground.

Seoul 1: Two personal samples (from shooting positions) and 6 area samples (from shooting positions, bullet impact areas, and reception and training areas).

Seoul 2: Five personal samples (from shooting positions, reception, and reservation areas) and 5 area samples (from shooting positions, target areas, reception, and reservation areas).

Range 3: Two personal samples (from shooting positions) and 1 area sample (from the cartridge table behind the shooting positions).

Sample collection and measurement were conducted by occupational environment measurement agencies based on KOSHA standardized guidelines for lead measurement and analysis (KOSHA GUIDE A-2-2019). 18 Airborne lead was collected on mixed cellulose ester filters for at least 6 hours, with pump flow rates corrected before and after sampling using a flow calibrator to account for any discrepancies.

An on-site investigation was conducted on April 15, just before the temporary special health examination. This investigation focused on Seoul 2 as a representative sample. The objectives were to assess detailed job tasks and spatial arrangements, lead dust distribution, personal protective equipment use, and adherence to personal hygiene practices.

Descriptive statistics included means and standard deviations for age, work tenure, BLL, and air lead concentration. BLL and air lead concentration statistics were computed for the overall sample and stratified by shooting range, job type, and work area. To compare the average BLL and air lead levels by work area, the sampling locations were classified into two groups based on their proximity to the shooting range: near the shooting area (shooting positions, bullet impact areas, target areas, and the cartridge table behind the shooting positions) and customer service area (waiting rooms used for reception, reservation, and training). Workers were categorized into two groups: near shooting areas (coaches, safety managers, and other employees who frequently accessed the range) and customer service areas (sales and reservation staff who reported minimal range access). These classifications were based on the primary location of each worker’s duties and their reported range access.

Due to the relatively small sample size, the normality of the distributions of BLL and air lead concentration was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk Test. The results showed that BLL followed a normal distribution, so parametric methods were used. Student’s t-test and analysis of variance were used to compare the mean BLLs of all workers based on job type and working area. As air lead did not follow a normal distribution, the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the median air lead concentrations by working area, with the median and interquartile range (IQR) values reported. Comparisons between different ranges were deemed unnecessary and were not performed. Analyses above were performed using Microsoft Excel (version 2016; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA).

Additional data processing and visualization were performed using Python (version 3.8.16; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) to examine the correlation between average air lead concentration and BLL across different ranges and sites. To visualize the relationship, a scatter plot with a linear regression line was generated using the Seaborn library (version 0.11.2) and Matplotlib (version 3.4.3). Pearson correlation analysis between air lead and BLL was performed using the SciPy library (version 1.7.1). The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) quantifies the strength and direction of a linear relationship between 2 variables, ranging from −1 (perfect negative correlation) to +1 (perfect positive correlation). The larger the coefficient (closer to −1 or +1), the stronger the correlation. Values of 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 indicate weak, moderate, and strong correlations, respectively. 19 In all statistical analyses, P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

The researchers obtained internal approval for data use and passed a data usage suitability assessment for using Korea Medical Institute health examination data. The provided data were anonymized by removing personally identifiable information such as names and social security numbers. Consequently, the study was waived by the Institutional Review Board (approval No. 2024-1217-001). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Table 1 presents a comprehensive description of the workers, both overall and by shooting range. The study included 21 workers (18 males and three females) with a mean age of 36.5 ± 8.6 years. Most of the males were shooting coaches (13/18), while the remaining had other jobs, including safety managers (n = 2), reception (n = 2), and sales (n = 1). All 3 females worked in customer service and reception. The average work tenure at the shooting ranges was 4.4 ± 4.0 years, with all participants working 5 days a week for 8 hours daily. The participants’ smoking history showed that 11 were non-smokers, four were former smokers, and five were current smokers. Alcohol consumption was reported to be less than once a week for 10 workers and more than once a week for the other 10. Clinical symptoms assessed through structured self-reports, which allowed for multiple responses, revealed cases of headaches (n = 4), abdominal pain (n = 3), muscle pain (n = 3), and fatigue (n = 3).

Table 2 summarizes the distribution and mean of BLL by workplace. The mean BLL for all workers was 39.82 ± 20.53 µg/dL. By location, Range 3 had the highest mean BLL (58.81 µg/dL), followed by Seoul 2 (29.07 µg/dL) and Seoul 1 (22.09 µg/dL). A statistically significant difference in BLL was observed based on job type (P < 0.001), with shooting coaches having the highest mean BLL (52.89 µg/dL), followed by safety managers (24.9 µg/dL) and customer service or sales staff (16.48 µg/dL). By working area, workers near shooting areas had a significantly higher mean BLL (47.53 µg/dL) compared to those in customer service areas (15.17 µg/dL) (P = 0.002).

Table 3 shows the airborne lead concentrations for overall, by ranges, and by specific work areas. Median ± IQR of the time-weighted average (TWA) air lead concentration for all shooting ranges was 0.229 ± 0.320 mg/m. By work area, the median of TWA near shooting areas (0.278 mg/m3) was significantly higher than that in customer service areas (0.004 mg/m3) (P < 0.001).

On-site investigations and work environment measurements revealed that none of the three shooting ranges conducted regular environmental measurements or specialized health examinations. Additionally, the ranges lacked adequate local exhaust ventilation systems at the firing points and benches, and effective respiratory protection was not consistently worn. Fig. 3 illustrates the interior of one of the firing ranges where the participants were employed. The image clearly shows that the range is enclosed with insufficient ventilation. Furthermore, it appears that workers do not effectively utilize protective equipment.

In Range 3, where the average BLL was the highest, most workers reported not distinguishing between work and regular clothing, often wearing work clothes for commuting. Furthermore, facilities for washing, bathing, and laundering work clothes after work shifts were inadequate.

Since all workers had BLLs exceeding 10 µg/dL, a threshold traditionally considered the “level of concern,” 20 occupational medicine specialists who conducted the special health examinations implemented proactive measures, including temporary work restrictions at each site. Some workers with BLLs exceeding 20 µg/dL visited local medical facilities and underwent concurrent treatment with d-penicillamine based on the clinics' recommendation. Eight shooting coaches from Range 3 were referred to a higher-level medical facility for hospitalization and further treatment, as their BLLs exceeded 40 µg/dL, the criterion for diagnosing lead poisoning in Korea. 21 22

According to the Korean special health examination guidelines, the biological exposure limit for BLL is 30 µg/dL. 23 The average BLL of shooting coaches working in the location with the highest airborne lead concentration was 52.89 µg/dL, exceeding this standard. Even workers in roles with less frequent access to the shooting ranges, such as customer service and sales (16.48 µg/dL) and safety management (24.9 µg/dL), had BLLs lower than this limit but still above 10 µg/dL. These levels are markedly higher than the average BLL (1.82–2.26 µg/dL) observed in the general population in Korea. 20 24 Due to the small size of the shooting ranges, job roles were not strictly separated. On-site investigations revealed that safety duties are usually conducted in the firing range, and customer service and sales staff may have entered the shooting range for tasks such as safety guidance and cleaning. Additionally, reports on work environment measurements consistently noted ineffective ventilation and space separation, which likely allowed lead particles to spread beyond the shooting area to the customer service areas.

The BLLs reported herein were higher than those reported recently for Korean military indoor shooting range workers. A 2016 study found that the average BLL of 120 military indoor shooting range workers was 11.3 ± 9.4 µg/dL, and 14.0 ± 8.3 µg/dL for professional shooters in the military. 25 Furthermore, the BLLs herein were higher than those of manufacturing workers. For example, workers in the electrical equipment manufacturing industry in Korea had the highest BLL at 7.80 µg/dL. 22 This result is consistent with industry-specific blood lead test results reported in the United States, where BLLs among shooting range workers (23.8–32.8 μg/dL) were higher than those observed among workers in battery and painting industries (21.5–30.5 μg/dL). 26

The airborne lead concentrations in the overall shooting ranges averaged 0.330 mg/m3 at firing points and target areas, approximately seven times higher than the permissible exposure limit for lead in air in South Korea of 0.047 mg/m3. In contrast, the average concentration in customer service areas was 0.005 mg/m3, around 1/10th of the exposure standard. Recent monitoring of workplace exposure in Korea has shown a gradual annual decrease of 6% over the past 30 years. 9 However, this study revealed contrasting results, highlighting serious concerns regarding the detrimental health impacts of lead exposure in indoor shooting ranges.

Indoor shooting range workers exhibited BLLs consistent with expected exposure levels based on their work area and specific job duties, as well as the airborne lead concentrations measured in their work areas. Although customer service and sales staff entered the firing line due to the small size of the ranges, coaches and safety managers were exposed to higher levels of lead owing to their frequent duties in the firing line, such as shooting, cleaning, and casing retrieval, compared to customer service workers. The work areas where these tasks were performed had statistically significantly higher airborne lead concentrations than customer waiting areas. Workers at Range 3, which had the highest airborne lead concentrations, were all shooting coaches and had higher BLLs than workers in other ranges, which had a mix of customer-facing workers. This finding aligns with previous studies on police officers in Germany and shooting instructors in Italy, indicating that elevated BLLs are proportional to shooting exposure. 15 27 Furthermore, workers in Range 3 primarily served as shooting coaches and reported wearing work attire for commuting, which may explain the higher airborne lead concentrations and BLLs at this facility. Insufficient ventilation systems and cramped spaces are also suspected factors contributing to these conditions, compared to other shooting ranges.

Lead is recognized by the World Health Organization as one of the ten chemicals of major public health concern. 28 Occupational exposure to lead is widely known to harm the hematopoietic system, cardiovascular system, peripheral nervous system, and kidneys. 29 It also affects males’ reproductive ability. BLLs exceeding 40 μg/dL in males can lead to changes in sperm count, volume, motility, and morphology, reducing their partner’s reproductive ability and increasing the risk of miscarriage and stillbirth. 29 30 In this study, the 13 shooting coaches with high lead exposure and BLLs averaging 52.89 μg/dL were relatively young males with an average age of 33.8 years. Their BLLs substantially exceeded 40 μg/dL, indicating a notable risk of reproductive toxicity. Therefore, the potential reproductive toxicity in these individuals warrants careful consideration in future studies.

Indoor shooting ranges receive lead contamination primarily from lead-containing ammunition. After shooting, lead and dust particles are absorbed into the respiratory system as airborne particles. This study identified tasks such as range cleaning, casing retrieval, and firearm maintenance as major sources of exposure in addition to shooting activities. Another exposure route involves ingesting lead-containing particles that can be transferred to the body or clothing.

Despite notable indicators of lead exposure, such as air lead concentrations and BLLs, no clinical symptoms clearly indicative of chronic exposure were observed. This suggests that essential tests should be mandatory in clinical practice for workers with suspected of lead exposure. The study revealed a highly significant positive correlation (r = 0.95) between air lead concentrations and BLLs, highlighting shooting as the primary source of lead exposure, leading to elevated BLLs. Thus, enhancing air quality management and environmental conditions in shooting ranges is crucial for promoting the health of workers engaged in shooting activities.

Unfortunately, the ventilation systems in the investigated indoor shooting ranges were inadequate. Furthermore, the consistent use of respiratory protective equipment in these ranges is insufficient. Additionally, adherence to personal hygiene practices was found inadequate, with risky and careless habits, such as eating immediately after shooting or wearing work clothes for commuting, remaining prevalent. In addition to structural ventilation deficiencies, poor personal hygiene in the workplace markedly increases the risk of lead ingestion and elevated BLLs. In particular, clothing and shoes brought home after work can serve as sources of lead exposure for family members at home. 31 To prevent the spread of health impacts, emphasizing and educating on the importance of strict personal hygiene practices is crucial while also implementing policy measures to improve ventilation systems.

This study had some limitations. First, the study was conducted at only three indoor shooting ranges, limiting the generalizability of the study findings to a broader range of establishments across South Korea. Future studies should include a larger sample of shooting ranges to enhance representativeness. Second, the sample size of 21 workers is relatively small, potentially reducing the statistical power of the analysis. However, the key statistical results, including mean comparisons and correlations, were all significant. Third, lead exposure measurements were limited to BLLs, without considering other biomarkers for lead exposure. Future research should include markers such as delta-aminolevulinic acid and zinc protoporphyrin to provide a more comprehensive understanding of occupational health risks in shooting-range settings.

In conclusion, the BLLs of civilian shooting range workers in this study were exceptionally high. Their compliance with protective gear, hygiene, and safety protocols is presumed to be poorer than the more stringent practices expected in military shooting ranges reported by Park et al. 25 in 2016–2017 due to high BLLs from lead exposure. Despite concerns raised by previous studies advocating for preventive measures in civilian shooting ranges, this study’s findings indicate that legal and regulatory oversight remains lacking. Therefore, urgent and comprehensive efforts are needed to prevent and reduce lead exposure among civilian shooting range workers and potential exposure groups, such as civilian users. This includes regular monitoring of worker BLLs and workplace air lead concentrations, improvements in ventilation systems, and education aimed at enhancing awareness of personal hygiene practices such as wearing respiratory protective equipment and changing work clothes.
