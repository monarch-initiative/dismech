Abstract. Liddle syndrome is a rare monogenic form of hypertension that can be misdiagnosed as essential hypertension
or, in the context of obesity, be mistaken for metabolic syndrome. This case report describes a 19-year-old Chinese male
with obesity, hypertension, insulin resistance, impaired glucose tolerance, dyslipidemia, and hyperuricemia—a classic
presentation of metabolic syndrome. However, the early onset of severe hypertension and a strong family history
prompted further investigation. Laboratory evaluation revealed mild hypokalemia, suppressed plasma renin activity and
normal aldosterone levels, raising suspicion for Liddle syndrome. Whole exome sequencing was performed to explore
monogenic causes of hypertension, which identified a novel heterozygous frameshift variant in the SCNN1B gene
(NM_000336.3:c.1711_1713delinsAA). This variant leads to the loss of the PY motif—a functionally critical and
indispensable structural element of the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) β-subunit—and was classified as likely pathogenic
in accordance with the ACMG/AMP guidelines. The patient was initiated on targeted therapy with amiloride, which rapidly
normalized his blood pressure and hypokalemia, thereby confirming the diagnosis. This case highlights the diagnostic
challenge of distinguishing monogenic hypertension from metabolic syndrome in young, obese individuals. It emphasizes the
necessity of systematically investigating for secondary causes in this population. Key steps include obtaining a family history,
screening for hypokalemia, evaluating renin and aldosterone levels, and assessing the response to standard therapy. Identifying
a rare disorder like Liddle syndrome allows for a paradigm shift in management from empiric to targeted treatment, which is
essential for preventing the debilitating long-term sequelae of chronic hypertension.

Introduction
Hypertension is the leading modifiable risk factor for
global cardiovascular morbidity and premature mortality,
with secondary causes accounting for 5%–15% of all
cases of hypertension [1, 2]. While genetic causes of sec‐
ondary hypertension are rare, identifying them is crucial
as they often guide targeted therapeutic interventions [ 1,

dominant disorder caused by gain-of-function pathogenic
variants in genes encoding epithelial sodium channel
(ENaC) subunits, particularly the β subunit encoded by
the SCNN1B gene [ 4, 5]. Pathogenic variants in these
genes typically disrupt the conserved PY motif in ENaC
subunits, impairing channel degradation and leading to
excessive sodium reabsorption. This syndrome is
characterized by early-onset hypertension, hypokalemia,
metabolic alkalosis, and suppressed levels of aldos‐
terone and renin, and it typically responds well to ENaC
blockers [6]. To date, over 100 familial or sporadic cases
of genetically confirmed Liddle syndrome have been
reported in the literature, predominantly involving
SCNN1B variants, with novel variants continuing to
expand the genetic spectrum of this disorder [7].

metabolic syndrome, a common condition characterized
by obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and early
blood pressure elevation. This phenotypic overlap may
lead to misdiagnosis or delayed recognition of the under‐

Given the variable phenotypic presentation of Liddle
syndrome, high-throughput sequencing has emerged as a
powerful diagnostic tool. Here, we present the case of a
Chinese male patient manifesting early-onset hyperten‐
sion and hypokalemia in the context of obesity and other
metabolic features, in whom exome sequencing revealed
a novel frameshift variant in the SCNN1B gene, expand‐
ing the mutational landscape of this condition.
Case presentation
Patient information and clinical features
A 19-year-old male patient was referred to the
Department of Endocrinology and Metabolism at the
First Affiliated Hospital of China Medical University
with an eight-year history of hypertension. Despite
receiving a combined antihypertensive regimen of
nifedipine (30 mg daily) and losartan (80 mg daily), his
blood pressure remained inadequately controlled, consis‐
tently reaching 150/100 mmHg. His father was diag‐
nosed with hypertension at an unknown age and was on
antihypertensive treatment; however, detailed biochemi‐
cal profiles (serum potassium, renin, and aldosterone
levels) were unavailable.
Clinical findings
On physical examination, the patient was 180.0 cm
tall, weighed 102.0 kg, and had a body mass index
(BMI) of 31.48 kg/m 2. Persistent mild hypokalemia
(3.15–3.35 mmol/L) and mild alkalemia (HCO 3
–: 26.5–
30.8 mmol/L; reference range: 22.0–29.0 mmol/L) were
identified, prompting further diagnostic evaluation. Lab‐
oratory tests revealed a normal serum aldosterone level
(0.15 ng/mL) but a notably low plasma renin activity
(0.31 ng/mL/h; normal range: 0.93–6.56 ng/mL/h).
Arterial gas analysis revealed a pH of 7.39 (normal
range, 7.35–7.45), which was within the normal range.
Additional evaluations, including thyroid hormones,
adrenocorticotropic hormone, cortisol, metanephrine,
and normetanephrine, were all within normal limits. The
oral glucose tolerance test indicated a fasting blood glu‐
cose level of 4.59 mmol/L, a fasting insulin level of
14.14 mIU/L, a HOMA-IR of 2.88, and a 2-hour plasma
glucose level of 9.9 mmol/L, suggesting impaired glu‐
cose tolerance and insulin resistance. The patient also
presented with hyperuricemia (546 μmol/L; normal
range: 208–428 umol/L) and reduced HDL cholesterol
levels (0.75 mmol/L; normal range: 0.91–1.92 mmol/L).
Enhanced 3D computed tomography showed no abnor‐
malities in the adrenal glands. Echocardiography and
color Doppler ultrasound of the carotid and renal arteries
revealed no significant structural or functional changes.
Genetic testing
After obtaining written informed consent from the
patient and his family members, peripheral blood
samples were collected. Genomic DNA was extracted
using a commercial blood DNA extraction kit (product
of Tianjing Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing,
China). Genetic testing was conducted via whole-
exome sequencing (WES) with high-throughput technol‐
ogy on the Illumina platform. For data analysis, the
VeritaTrekker® Variant Site Detection System and
Enliven® Variant Site Annotation & Interpretation
System (both developed by Berry Genetics) were
employed; these systems were based on the latest ver‐
sions of the following databases: dbSNP, 1,000 Genomes
Project, gnomAD, CADD, ClinVar, and HGMD.
Sequencing results identified a novel heterozygous variant
in the SCNN1B gene (reference transcript: NM_000336.3;
variant notation: c.1711_1713delinsAA, which corre‐
sponds to the protein change p.Tyr571Lysfs*105). Sub‐
sequent pedigree analysis confirmed that this variant was
inherited from the patient`s father (Fig. 1). This variant
results in the loss of the PY motif (amino acids 616–620:
PPPNY) at the C-terminus of the β-subunit of epithelial
sodium channels (ENaCs) (Fig. 2). According to the
2015 ACMG/AMP guidelines, this variant was classified
as likely pathogenic based on the following criteria: PM1
(variant localizes to the functionally critical PY motif of
ENaC β-subunit), PM2 (variant absent from the latest
version of genomic databases including ClinVar, dbSNP,
HGMD, LitVar, and gnomAD v4.1.0), PM4 (frameshift
variant leading to PY motif loss and truncated protein),
and PP1 (variant cosegregates with hypertension in the
proband and his father).
Diagnosis and treatment
Primary aldosteronism was excluded based on normal
serum aldosterone levels, unremarkable adrenal imaging,
and absence of pathogenic variants in associated genes.
Based on the patient’s clinical manifestations and genetic
test results, a diagnosis of Liddle syndrome was con‐
firmed. The patient was advised to implement lifestyle
modifications, including adopting a low-salt diet, engag‐
ing in regular exercise, and reducing body weight. His
antihypertensive regimen was adjusted to include
amiloride/hydrochlorothiazide (5 mg/50 mg daily) in
combination with nifedipine (30 mg daily).

Follow-up
At 4 weeks of follow-up after the initiation of these
therapeutic and lifestyle interventions, his blood pressure

Discussion
Genetic forms of secondary hypertension typically
manifest with distinct clinical characteristics, including
early onset in children, adolescents, or young adults, and
a strong familial predisposition. The majority of mono‐
genic hypertensive disorders share a common pathogenic
mechanism, which involves enhanced sodium reabsorp‐
tion in the renal tubules [ 2, 8]. Consequently, the detec‐
tion of suppressed plasma renin concentration (PRC) or
plasma renin activity (PRA) should prompt clinical sus‐
picion of secondary hypertension mediated by sodium
retention [2]. Among the spectrum of rare genetic hyper‐
tensive disorders, Liddle syndrome represents one of the
more prevalent forms, particularly within the Chinese
population [ 9, 10]. We reviewed familial and sporadic
cases of Liddle syndrome genetically confirmed with
SCNN1B variants that have been reported in the litera‐
ture, with Chinese patients accounting for more than half
of the reported cases (33 of 64 families, Supplementary
Table 1), highlighting the importance of considering this
diagnosis in patients of Chinese ancestry presenting with
early-onset hypertension.
The main clinical features of Liddle syndrome are
often distinct and obvious; however, some atypical cases
exhibit significant phenotypic variability, highlighting
the heterogeneity of the condition [11]. We reviewed 143
patients with Liddle syndrome carrying SCNN1B gene
variants from 64 families, among whom 30.7% had nor‐
mal aldosterone levels, 21.4% had normal blood potas‐
sium levels, and even 3.6% had normal blood pressure
(Supplementary Table 1). These findings underscore the
limitations of phenotype-based diagnosis and emphasize
the critical role of next-generation sequencing in identi‐
fying monogenic forms of hypertension. The precise
molecular diagnosis enables implementation of targeted
therapeutic strategies: ENaC inhibitors (amiloride or
triamterene) for Liddle syndrome, thiazide diuretics
for Gordon syndrome (pseudohypoaldosteronism type
II), and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists for
glucocorticoid-remediable aldosteronism [ 2]. This preci‐
sion medicine approach not only optimizes blood pressure
control but also minimizes adverse effects, ultimately
improving long-term cardiovascular outcomes.

is the hyperactivation of the ENaCs in renal tubules.

Mechanistic studies have demonstrated that among the
three ENaC subunits, the β and γ subunits play a crucial
role in channel activity modulation [6]. Structurally, each
ENaC subunit contains a highly conserved PY motif
(PPPXY) in its cytoplasmic carboxyl-terminal domain.
This motif serves as a critical binding site for the WW
domain of the ubiquitin protein ligase NEDD4-2, thereby
regulating channel degradation through ubiquitination
[12]. Genetic analyses have revealed that most cases of
Liddle syndrome result from nonsense or frameshift
variants in the C-terminal cytoplasmic regions of the
SCNN1B and SCNN1G genes [ 4]. Currently, 30 distinct
pathogenic variants have been identified in the SCNN1B
gene, with variants either disrupting the upstream region
leading to PY motif loss or directly altering the PY motif
itself, particularly affecting residues 616–620 (Fig. 2,
Supplementary Table 1).
Essential hypertension frequently coexists with sec‐
ondary hypertension, significantly complicating both
diagnosis and clinical management. In the present case,
the patient exhibited metabolic syndrome, including obe‐
sity, hypertriglyceridemia, and hyperuricemia, alongside
Liddle syndrome—highlighting the coexistence of
monogenic and acquired metabolic drivers in his hyper‐
tension, and the resulting diagnostic challenge that
necessitates a multifaceted treatment approach.
Notably, there is no direct evidence that ENaC overac‐
tivity induces insulin resistance or obesity. However,
ENaC overactivation can lead to hypokalemia; if mono‐
genic hypertension is accompanied by long-term
hypokalemia, this electrolyte disturbance may impair
insulin secretion and reduce insulin sensitivity [13]. Con‐
versely, individuals with obesity and insulin resistance
often experience ENaC overactivation due to underlying
metabolic abnormalities [ 14]—creating bidirectional
associations between Liddle syndrome, metabolic syn‐
drome, obesity, and insulin resistance. These connections
underscore the need for future research to explore
glucose metabolism disorder data and related mecha‐
nisms in a larger cohort of patients with monogenic
hypertension, to further clarify the interplay between
these conditions.
This case illustrates that its features can be mistaken
for, or coexist with, metabolic syndrome, requiring a
high index of suspicion. As such, lifestyle modifications
are a critical component in the comprehensive manage‐
ment of secondary hypertension. Key interventions,
including sodium restriction (targeting <2 g/day), moder‐
ation of alcohol intake, adoption to a balanced diet,
engagement in regular physical activity ( ≥150 minutes/

In summary, we present a Chinese pedigree with
genetically confirmed Liddle syndrome caused by a
novel heterozygous variant in the SCNN1B gene, leading
to the complete deletion of the functionally critical PY
motif (Graphical Abstract). Although Liddle syndrome is
classically characterized by early-onset hypertension,
hypokalemia, and suppressed renin activity, its clinical
presentation can exhibit significant heterogeneity. This
case illustrates that Liddle syndrome can be misdiag‐
nosed as, or coexist with, metabolic syndrome in young
obese patients, emphasizing the need for a high index of
suspicion in this population. The implementation of next-
generation sequencing technologies has revolutionized
the diagnostic approach to monogenic hypertension,
enabling accurate molecular diagnosis in young hyper‐
tensive patients. This precision medicine strategy not
only facilitates the initiation of targeted therapy with
ENaC inhibitors but also supports comprehensive man‐
agement through dietary sodium restriction, potassium
supplementation, and long-term surveillance to prevent
cardiovascular and renal complications.
