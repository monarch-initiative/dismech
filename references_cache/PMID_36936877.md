Refeeding syndrome is a major clinical problem that leads to fatal complications 
in patients suffering from malnutrition. Hypophosphatemia inevitably is observed 
at the onset of refeeding syndrome and therefore is monitored during refeeding; 
however, the causes of metabolic changes in phosphate concentration during 
refeeding remain poorly understood. In a previous study, we established a 
refeeding syndrome model employing total parenteral nutrition with 
insulin-induced hypophosphatemia, but the symptoms were severe and the metabolic 
mechanisms in this model may not have been representative of clinical 
conditions. Therefore, we established a new animal model of mild refeeding 
syndrome by using a shorter fasting period followed by a single refeeding. These 
mild refeeding syndrome-model rats exhibited hypophosphatemia without increases 
in urinary phosphate excretion. Interestingly, administration of the combination 
of phosphate and insulin during refeeding promoted insulin secretion during 
refeeding. This model implies that Pi may directly promote insulin secretion in 
pancreatic cells. These results clarify the interaction between phosphate and 
glucose metabolism pancreatic cells during refeeding syndrome in a mild 
refeeding syndrome model.

Refeeding syndrome (RFS) is a major problem related to nutritional management in clinical practice. RFS causes fatal complications such as heart failure, arrhythmia, respiratory failure, and cardiac arrest; therefore, prevention and effective treatment of RFS are needed.(1,2) RFS occurs when malnourished patients with long-term high energy intake, especially those with cancer and infectious diseases, gastrointestinal disorders, gastrointestinal surgery, or anorexia nervosa are re-fed by high-calorie infusion or enteral nutrition.(3–5) Japan is a super-aging society, and the number of patients experiencing extremely low nutrition (BMI <16) for extended periods of time (e.g., due to sarcopenia, frailty, or aspiration pneumonia) is increasing; thus, many elderly people in Japan are at high risk of developing RFS. In addition, about 50% of patients have disease-related malnutrition, and it has been reported that malnutrition increases mortality.(6,7) The National Institute for Health and Care Technology (NICE) practice guidelines provide selection criteria for high-risk patients with RFS,(8) but no clear diagnostic criteria. Moreover, there currently exists no effective treatment for RFS, and the only preventive treatment is to increase the feeding dose slowly and gradually to the target energy intake when resuming nutrition in malnourished patients. Interestingly, since hypophosphatemia occurs at the onset of RFS, monitoring of the blood inorganic phosphate (Pi) concentration is recommended during re-feeding, along with Pi supplementation as needed.(3) However, there exist to our knowledge few well-supported clinical papers on RFS, and effective treatment and prevention methods remain unclear.

The mechanism of RFS onset is hypothesized as follows: During starvation or malnutrition, gluconeogenesis and protein catabolism are enhanced, resulting in weight loss and water, vitamin, and mineral deficiencies. In that state, glucose-based refeeding increases insulin secretion from the pancreas, resulting in increased glucose uptake and protein synthesis in the liver and muscle. Furthermore, intracellular translocation of Pi, magnesium, and potassium causes hypophosphatemia, hypomagnesemia, and hypokalemia (respectively), resulting in turn in the development of RFS.(9,10) Hypophosphatemia is inevitable in RFS, although other deficiency symptoms do not necessarily occur; therefore, hypophosphatemia is considered key to the development of RFS.(11) In addition, hypophosphatemia has been reported to increase mortality in critically ill patients.(12,13) Thus, elucidation of the mechanism of RFS and hypophosphatemia is important for the development of effective treatment and prevention methods for RFS.

Glucose is a critical source of energy. Dietary glucose is absorbed by the intestine, where this sugar is taken up into the blood. Blood glucose transport into cells for use as energy is mediated via insulin secretion by the pancreas. The intestinal hormones glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) are known to promote insulin secretion.(14) Glucose transporter 2 (GLUT2) mediates glucose uptake in the intestine and liver, and glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) mediates glucose uptake into cells through insulin regulation in muscle.(15,16) In glucose metabolism, Pi plays a role as a component in the phosphorylation of glucose and in ATP synthesis. Furthermore, in insulin signaling, the activity of enzymes such as Akt, which is downstream of the insulin receptor, is regulated by phosphorylation.(17,18) On the other hand, it has been reported that patients with type 2 diabetes who have abnormal glucose metabolism also have abnormal Pi metabolism, such that the Pi regulatory mechanism does not function properly.(19) Together, these results suggest the existence of an interaction between Pi metabolism and glucose metabolism. Similarly, RFS is known to be associated with abnormalities in both glucose and Pi metabolism, suggesting that the interaction between these pathways needs to be clarified.

The lack of RFS model animals is among the reasons why the mechanism of RFS onset remains poorly understood. To address this issue, we previously employed total parenteral nutrition (TPN) to construct an RFS animal model that exhibited hypophosphatemia.(20) In this RFS model, rats are fasted for 5 days and then continuously administered glucose via TPN, at which point hypophosphatemia is induced by administration of insulin. Notably, insulin proved to be a trigger for the development of RFS. We used this model in an attempt to elucidate the mechanism of RFS onset. However, these animals exhibited severe symptoms, suggesting that the metabolic mechanisms of RFS might be disrupted, impeding characterization of these processes. Consequently, we created a milder animal model of RFS that shortens the fasting time and provides refeeding by administration of a single oral dose of glucose. We then investigated the interaction between Pi and glucose metabolism during refeeding in this model.

Seven-week-old male Sprague Dawley rats were obtained from Japan SLC (Shizuoka, Japan). Rats were maintained on a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle (9:00–21:00) with free access to water and standard MF diet (Oriental Yeast, Tokyo, Japan) except as noted below. The experimental procedure was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Hyogo, School of Human Science and Environment.

The experimental scheme is shown schematically in Fig. 1. After fasting for 24 h, the rats were re-fed by oral gavage (via gastric tube) administration of glucose [at 10 g/kg body weight (BW)] alone and in combination with administration of insulin and/or Pi. Specifically, animals were re-fed with glucose alone (G group; n = 6), with glucose and injected with 0.75 U/kg BW of fast-acting insulin (Humalog®; Eli Lily, Hyogo, Japan) (GI group: n = 8), with glucose and 90 mg/kg BW of KH2PO4 (GP group; n = 9), and with glucose and 90 mg/kg BW of KH2PO4 and injected with 0.75 U/kg BW of fast-acting insulin (GPI group; n = 8). Blood was collected prior to refeeding and again at 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h after refeeding. Urine samples were collected for 24 h starting with refeeding.

Levels of Pi, glucose, and creatinine (in plasma and urine) were determined using the Phosphour C-test kit, the glucose CII-test Wako kit, and the Labo assayTM creatinine kit (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan), respectively. Plasma insulin and GLP-1 concentrations were measured using a rat insulin enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Morinaga Institute of Biological Science, Tokyo, Japan) and a GLP-1 ELISA Kit Wako, High Sensitivity (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan), respectively.

A subclonal rat INS-1E cell line stably expressing Insulin-GLase (iGL cells) were obtained from Cosmo Bio Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan) and the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (JCRB) cell bank (Tokyo, Japan), respectively. iGL cells were cultured in iGL Culture Medium and grown in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C.

iGL cells were cultured in low-glucose (2 mM), normal-Pi (1.2 mM) or low-Pi (0.1 mM) medium for 60 min and then stimulated with high-glucose (20 mM), normal-Pi (1.2 mM), or low-Pi (0.1 mM) medium for 60 or 120 min. A luciferase assay (JNC, Tokyo, Japan) was used to measure the amount of luciferase produced by the INS-1E reporter construct carried by the iGL cells. In this cell line, an insulin-Gaussia luciferase fusion protein is expressed under the control of a modified INS promoter and secreted by the same pathway used to secrete native insulin; therefore, luciferase serves as a reporter for insulin production. To investigate the effect of Pi inhibitors on insulin secretion (as assessed by the reporter), the cells were cultured with low glucose and low Pi for 2 or 4 h. After that, cells were incubated for 120 min in high-glucose medium containing normal levels of Pi in the presence (+) or absence (−) of 5 mM phosphonoformic acid (PFA), an inhibitor of Pi transporters. The spent medium then was assayed for levels of secreted luciferase. In order to correct for the effect of PFA, the luciferase levels were normalized to the total protein content of the spent medium, which was determined by the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and corrected.

The significance of differences between groups was assessed using a two-tailed One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc non-paired Student’s t tests and Tukey–Kramer multiple comparison tests. Data are presented as the mean ± SE. Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05.

As a first step, rats were fasted for 24 h. As a result of fasting, the body weights of rats were decreased by approximately 10% compared to baseline (Table 1). The rats then were divided into 4 groups and subjected to a single refeeding using a gastric tube to deliver a dose of glucose (Fig. 1). We monitored biochemical parameters to confirm the onset of RFS. The plasma Pi levels were significantly lower in the GI group compared to the G group at 24 h after refeeding (Fig. 2A and B). In contrast, urinary Pi excretion over the 24 h following refeeding was significantly increased in the GP and GPI groups compared to the G group but was not affected by insulin administration (Fig. 2C). Since the GI group became hypophosphatemic after 24 h, this group was used as an animal model for mild RFS.

In all groups, the single refeeding event following a 24-h fast appeared to result in increases in plasma glucose levels at 2 to 4 h following glucose administration; plasma glucose levels subsequently fell through the 24 h following the refeeding (Fig. 2D). Specifically, the peak plasma glucose levels were achieved at 2 h after refeeding in the G, GI, and GP groups, but at 4 h in the GPI group. Interestingly, the blood glucose levels at 2 h post-refeed were significantly lower compared to the G group only in the GPI group (Fig. 2E).

Since the changes in plasma glucose levels were significantly different (compared to the G group) only in the GPI group, the blood concentrations of insulinotropic hormones were measured in these animals. We found that blood insulin levels did not differ significantly among the groups following refeeding (Fig. 3A). To examine the amount of insulin secretion, we calculated the area under the curve (AUC) of plasma insulin levels during the 24 h following refeeding (Fig. 3B). Notably, the AUC of plasma insulin levels were significantly higher in the GPI group than in the G group.

Although the GI and GPI groups received the same amount of insulin per body weight at the time of refeeding, the AUC of plasma insulin levels was significantly higher only in the GPI group compared to the G group. To elucidate the mechanism of this difference, we assessed the levels of GLP-1, a gastrointestinal hormone that promotes insulin secretion (Fig. 3C). GLP-1 was significantly elevated in the G, GI, and GP groups (but not in the GPI group) at 2 h post-refeeding (compared to the baseline level prior to refeeding). This result indicated that GLP-1 does not mediate the increase in insulin secretion observed in the GPI group.

The increased AUC of insulin levels in the GPI group of mild RFS model animals suggested that Pi may directly stimulate pancreatic insulin secretion. Therefore, the effect of Pi on insulin secretion was investigated using iGL cells, a line that has been engineered to facilitate the rapid detection via a reporter gene of glucose-responsive insulin secretion. Cells exposed to RFS-like conditions of low Pi with glucose stimulation exhibited significantly decreased the level of secreted reporter protein compared to that in cells grown under normal Pi conditions (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, exposure of cells to PFA, an inhibitor of Pi uptake, under normal Pi conditions significantly decreased the level of secreted reporter protein compared to that seen in the absence of PFA (Fig. 4B). These results suggested that intracellular Pi may stimulate pancreatic cells to secrete insulin following culturing under malnutrition conditions.

We established a mild RFS animal model with hypophosphatemia by subjecting rats to short-term fasting following by refeeding with a single oral dose of glucose. We then employed this model to evaluate the interaction between Pi and glucose metabolism during refeeding.

The mild RFS model showed that fasting for 24 h resulted in hypophosphatemia. In our previous study, an RFS model with prolonged (5-day) fasting followed by refeeding with central venous glucose and insulin showed 23% weight loss, hypophosphatemia at 10 h post-refeeding, insulin deficiency, convulsions during refeeding, respiratory failure, and other severe symptoms.(20) Although the RFS model in this paper is mild, the model still exhibits symptoms characteristic of RFS, notably including the ability of refeeding to induce insulin secretion, without the major metabolic disturbances seen in our previous model. Therefore, the mild RFS model is suitable for elucidating metabolic changes after refeeding.

In the mild RFS model, the administration of insulin and Pi at refeeding increased insulin secretion, suggesting that Pi may affect insulin metabolism. Multiple studies have reported that GLP-1 levels increase upon refeeding, thereby promoting insulin secretion.(14,21,22) Therefore, we hypothesized that the blood level of GLP-1, one of the incretins that promote insulin secretion, would be increased by administration of Pi during refeeding. Interestingly, while plasma GLP-1 levels were significantly higher in the G, GI, and GP groups at 2 h compared to baseline, plasma GLP-1 levels were not similarly significantly elevated compared to baseline in the GPI group, a result that was not consistent with our hypothesis. However, it has been reported that GLP-1 is degraded by Dipeptidyl Peptidase-4 (DPP-4) in vivo and has a short half-life.(21) The present study measured GLP-1 levels 2 h after refeeding; clarification of the relationship between blood GLP-1 and insulin concentrations may require assessment in the blood immediately following refeeding. In addition, examination of another incretin, GIP, may clarify the mechanism by which Pi regulates insulin secretion via GIP.

To examine whether Pi directly affects insulin secretion in the pancreas, we used iGL cells, which are highly glucose-responsive and express a reporter for insulin secretion.(23) When cultured in low-nutrient, low-Pi medium and then shifted to low-Pi, high-glucose medium. iGL cells exhibited decreased reporter gene expression, corresponding to decreased insulin secretion. This result suggests that pancreatic cells grown under low-nutrition, low-Pi conditions do not secrete insulin in response to exposure to high glucose levels, indicating that Pi is required for insulin secretion. It has been reported that insulin is secreted by pancreatic beta cells in response to glucose uptake and metabolism, as well as ATP production.(24,25) Pi is required for ATP synthesis, and it has been reported that Pi depletion impairs insulin secretion by pancreatic islets in rats.(26) Furthermore, genes related to glucose metabolism are activated by phosphorylation. Therefore, the depletion of Pi in pancreatic beta cells may interfere with the normal insulin secretion mechanism. Clearly, further studies will be needed to elucidate the effects of Pi on insulin secretion.

In our mild RFS animal model, administration of insulin and Pi at the time of refeeding suppressed hyperglycemia,(20) which may indicate that glucose metabolism is altered in the liver and muscle under these conditions. It also has been hypothesized that Pi in the blood is translocated into cells specially after refeeding. Therefore, we examined the mRNA expression levels of genes encoding GLUT2 and GLUT4 as glucose transporters, Pi transporters, and glucose metabolism proteins in the liver and skeletal muscle in animal of our mild RFS model; however no clear data were obtained that Pi and glucose transporter and changes those plasma concentration (data not shown). The failure to detect effects at the level of transcript accumulation may reflect the dynamic metabolic changes known to occur in the body following refeeding. Also, we need to understand interaction Pi and glucose metabolism. For instance, it has been reported that rats fed a low-Pi diet do not develop hypophosphatemia in a rat model of type 1 diabetes.(27) Additionally, in a study of nondiabetic patients, a low-Pi diet did not cause hypophosphatemia. A separate study of non-diabetic patients reported that hypophosphatemia correlated with postprandial hyperglycemia and decreased insulin sensitivity.(28) In the future, we hope that it will be possible to elucidate the metabolic changes that occur after refeeding by capturing such effects comprehensively.

One of the risks for developing RFS is insulin injection,(8) and the results of the present study are consistent with that observation. Other high-risk factors include post-surgery, low nutrition due to eating dysfunction, and alcoholism. However, Pi and insulin also may pose a risk for developing RFS. Conditions with abnormalities in insulin metabolism include diabetes mellitus,(25) and conditions with abnormal Pi metabolism include chronic kidney disease and hereditary hypophosphatemic rickets. Also recently, it is reported that eating habit especially carbohydrate associated with serum levels of phosphorus on Japanese healthy people.(29) By clarifying the relationship between these conditions and the development of RFS, it should be possible to improve screening for the development of RFS in undernourished patients, which may lead to prevention and treatment.

In conclusion, we have created an animal model of mild RFS with hypophosphatemia. Using this model, we showed that administration of insulin and Pi during refeeding may prevent hypophosphatemia and promote insulin secretion in animals experiencing malnutrition, an observation that is expected to have important clinical implications.
