# Familial Hypomagnesemia With Hypercalciuria and Nephrocalcinosis in a 7-Year-Old Girl: A Case Report.
**Authors:** Tonny RT, Mumu FA, Sharmim S, Huque SS
**Journal:** Clin Case Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ccr3.71612](https://doi.org/10.1002/ccr3.71612)

## Content

Familial hypomagnesemia with hypercalciuria and nephrocalcinosis (FHHNC) is a rare autosomal recessive renal tubular disorder, caused by mutations in the Claudin-16 or Claudin-19 genes. It is characterized by renal wasting of calcium and magnesium, bilateral nephrocalcinosis, and progression to kidney failure eventually. We had a pediatric patient with hypomagnesemia, hypercalciuria, and nephrocalcinosis with the presence of a novel missense variant of c.175C>T (p.Arg59Cys) in the CLDN16 gene on whole exome sequencing (WES). This variant falls in the category of variants of uncertain significance as per the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guideline. MutationTaster, Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant, and Combined Annotation-Dependent Depletion support its deleterious effect, putting it into the pathogenic category. The variant found in our case is compatible with the patient phenotype. WES is a powerful tool to confirm clinical, biochemical, and radiological diagnoses in cases of rare genetic disorders, like FHHNC.

Familial hypomagnesemia with hypercalciuria and nephrocalcinosis (FHHNC) is a rare autosomal recessive (AR) renal tubular disease with an estimated prevalence of < 1/1,000,000 individuals [ 1 , 2 , 3 ]. It is characterized by severe urinary calcium (Ca2+) and magnesium (Mg2+) wasting, which leads to hypercalciuria, hypomagnesemia, bilateral nephrocalcinosis, and progression to renal failure by the second or third decade of life. This rare monogenic disorder occurs as a result of mutations in the Claudin‐16 (CLDN16) and Claudin‐19 (CLDN19) genes that encode proteins CLDN16 and CLDN19, respectively. Both proteins are co‐expressed in the tight junction of the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle and form an essential complex for paracellular reabsorption of Ca2+ and Mg2+ [ 4 , 5 ]. The patients with FHHNC commonly present with polyuria, polydipsia, enuresis, failure to thrive, recurrent urinary tract infections, and seizures [ 6 ]. No specific therapy for FHHNC exists to date. Currently, the only therapeutic option is supportive and consists of high fluid intake, dietary salt restriction, oral supplementation of magnesium, and thiazide diuretics. Renal transplantation is the only curative approach in end‐stage kidney failure patients and is usually required during the second to third decades of life. Early diagnosis of this rare disease is of paramount importance to prevent morbidity and mortality [ 1 , 2 , 4 ]. Here we have reported a case of FHHNC who had polyuria, polydipsia, recurrent urinary tract infection, constipation, nocturnal enuresis without any significant family history, and the diagnosis was confirmed by the detection of a CLDN16 gene mutation.

Our reported case, a 7‐year‐old girl, third issue of consanguineous parents (Pedigree shown in Figure 1), presented with polyuria and polydipsia since 2 years of age, recurrent high‐grade fever for the last 6 months with occasional dysuria and a history of recurrent abdominal pain, constipation, and nocturnal enuresis. She was treated several times with oral and injectable antibiotics for recurrent urinary tract infection (UTI). There was no history of hematuria, salt craving, visual or hearing abnormality, or any positive family history. She was delivered at 32+ weeks with low birth weight (LBW). There was a maternal history of one abortion. Our reported case was vitally stable and anthropometrically underweight (weight 14.5 kg, < 3rd centile, Standard deviation score (SDS) value −3.3), stunted (height 105 cm, < 3rd centile, SDS value −3.4), and wasted (WHZ −2.5) with normal findings on systemic examination. Eye evaluation revealed myopia (6/18 in both eyes), and the hearing assessment was normal.

The plan of investigations was made to diagnose the disease (biochemical abnormality such as low Mg and urinary profile analysis for hypercalciuria), to exclude differential diagnosis (Renal tubular acidosis and other tubulopathies), to exclude UTI, and determine chronic kidney disease (CKD) staging for management purposes.

Patient had hypomagnesemia (1.1 mg/dL), raised parathyroid hormone level (110.6 pg/mL), and insufficient vitamin D level (28 mg/mL). Routine microscopic examination of urine (Urine R/M/E) revealed pus cells 8–12/HPF and RBC 6–8/HPF. Spot calcium creatinine ratio (0.46) and serum creatinine (0.74 mg/dL) were raised with CKD stage 3 (eGFR 58.6 mL/min/1.73 m2). Renal tubular acidosis was excluded as serum electrolyte was normal and no acidosis on ABG. Other salt losing tubulopathy such as Bartter syndrome and Gitelman syndrome were excluded as there was no dyselectrolytemia (hypokalemia, hypochloremia) and metabolic alkalosis. Intravenous urography (IVU) showed bilateral multiple small renal calculi in the upper, middle, and lower poles of both kidneys (Figure 2).

IVU film showing calcific deposition scattered throughout parenchyma of both kidneys indicated by blue arrows, suggestive of bilateral nephrocalcinosis.

WES analysis revealed a homozygous missense variant c.175C>T in exon 2 of the CLDN16 gene that results in the amino acid substitution of p.Arg59Cys. The observed variant has a minor allele frequency of 0.00040 in gnomAD exomes and is novel in genomes, respectively. The severity of the impact of this variant on the protein is medium, based on the effect of the protein and REVEL score, putting it into the pathogenic category. This variant was interpreted as a variant of uncertain significance according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guideline (ACMG) criteria (Table 2). Her mother and other siblings were healthy with normal urine and USG findings.

The patient was treated with adequate hydration, Mg‐containing diet including supplementation with Mg, zinc, and vitamin D, antibiotic therapy due to UTI along with prophylaxis, hydrochlorothiazide as well as other supportive measures.

The index case is followed up on an outpatient basis on several occasions. She is normotensive, anthropometrically moderately underweight (weight 17 kg, < 5th centile, SDS value −2.4) and moderately stunted (Height 114 cm, < 5th centile, SDS value −2.2). The biochemical characteristics and the other relevant lab parameters are provided in Table 1. The patient had no hypercalciuria after receiving hydrochlorothiazide and her serum magnesium levels normalized with magnesium supplementation. Her creatinine and parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels worsened. According to the current status, her eGFR drops down to 53 mL/min/1.73 m2. Familial hypomagnesemia with hypercalciuria and nephrocalcinosis (FHHNC) was first described by Michelis in 1272. It is a rare genetic renal tubular disease, transmitted by an autosomal recessive mode of inheritance [ 7 ]. Parental consanguinity has an increased risk for FHHNC [ 6 ]. The grandparents of our case were first cousins (Pedigree shown in Figure 1).

Although the age of onset is highly variable, reported cases suggest earlier manifestation of symptoms within the first 5 years of life [ 2 ]. Our index case presented with polyuria, polydipsia, recurrent UTI, enuresis, and failure to thrive like other reported cases [ 2 , 8 ]. Additional presenting features were occasional abdominal pain and vomiting, but no history of gross hematuria, rickets, and tetany, which may also be consistent with this condition.

The most common laboratory and radiological findings include hypomagnesemia, hypermagnesuria, hypercalciuria, hyperuricemia, elevated serum creatinine, sterile leukocyturia, hematuria, nephrolithiasis, and medullary nephrocalcinosis [ 9 ]. All findings were present in our reported case except hyperuricemia. Despite hypomagnesemia being one of the most common features, serum magnesium may persist within the normal range at diagnosis in some patients. This is due to the presence of underlying moderate to advanced CKD and a parallel reduction of urinary magnesium loss [ 10 ].

Another hallmark of FHHNC is the presence of hyperparathyroidism during the earlier course of the disease, which is independent of CKD stage [ 10 , 11 ]. It has been reported in a recent study that pediatric patients with FHHNC due to CLDN16 mutation had significantly higher PTH levels (184 vs. 29.8 pg/mL, p < 0.001) compared to the control group [ 3 ]. Similarly, the PTH was elevated in our reported case.

Early progression to CKD in FHHNC is attributed to different risk factors, such as nephrocalcinosis, recurrent UTI, chronic dehydration due to polyuria [ 12 ], and our case possesses all these risk factors. The risk of progression of CKD was reported to be higher in patients with the CLDN19 than the CLDN16 mutation [ 6 ]. A similar reflection was noted here.

Previous case reports suggest extra‐renal findings such as ocular abnormalities and neurological manifestations in patients with CLDN19 mutations. Ocular defects are seen in CLDN19 mutations, which include mainly myopia magna, horizontal nystagmus, and macular colobomata leading to severe visual impairment in some cases. Only a small subset of patients with CLDN16 defects displays refractive error [ 13 ], as observed in our case.

Based on phenotypic presentations, genetic diagnosis should be done with single CLDN16 or CLDN19 analyses by traditional Sanger sequencing or whole exome sequencing (WES) [ 1 , 2 , 3 ]. In our case, the WES analysis revealed a homozygous missense variant of c.175C>T (p.Arg59Cys) (NM_006580.4) in the CLDN16 gene. It was preliminarily categorized as of uncertain clinical significance according to the ACMG guideline. In silico pathogenicity assessment of variants, including Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant (SIFT), MutationTaster, Polyphen‐2, and Combined Annotation‐Dependent Depletion (CADD), predicts its deleterious effect and puts it into pathogenic variety. It should be noted that FHHNC is an ultra‐rare renal tubulopathy and its population prevalence is unknown [ 1 ]. A characteristic geographic distribution of genetic variants also exists, reported in existing literature [ 2 ]. It is less studied in our population. Our findings widen the spectrum of genetic diagnosis of this rare disease, as it is underdiagnosed among this population. There is no specific therapy for FHHNC to date. Renal transplantation is the only curative treatment [ 6 ]. Our case was managed with adequate hydration, magnesium salt, and hydrochlorothiazide, like other reported cases [ 1 , 2 , 4 ]. To halt the progression of CKD, renin‐angiotensin aldosterone system inhibitors may be used as well [ 9 ]. Active vitamin D is advised for the treatment of secondary hyperparathyroidism rather than inactive forms to avoid worsening of hypercalciuria, which may aggravate nephrocalcinosis. It has been evident that normal vitamin D levels are associated with attenuation of chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression [ 15 ].

We report a case of a rare disorder, named FHHNC with typical clinical and biochemical features, radiologic evidence of nephrocalcinosis and identification of a novel missense mutation in the CLDN16 gene. A patient with polyuria, polydipsia, and enuresis may be the initial clinical presentation. FHHNC must be taken into consideration in such cases. Early diagnosis and supportive management of the disease may prevent its progression to CKD.
