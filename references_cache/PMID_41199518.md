# Beyond the Usual Suspects: Unexplained Childhood Hemolytic Anemia with Myopathy Unveiled as Glycogen Storage Disease Type XII.
**Authors:** Nayak AR, Panda T, P M, Hota R, Kulkarni MV, Mishra SK
**Journal:** Turk J Haematol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.4274/tjh.galenos.2025.2025.0354](https://doi.org/10.4274/tjh.galenos.2025.2025.0354)

## Content

To the Editor,

A 10-year-old girl presented with a history of transfusion-dependent anemia since infancy. She had delayed motor milestones and, by the age of 3 years, she had proximal muscular discomfort and high creatinine kinase (CK). With a provisional diagnosis of inflammatory myositis, she was given a trial treatment of steroids and methotrexate, but the symptoms persisted. On examination, she had pallor, icterus, hepatosplenomegaly, and skeletal abnormalities (symphalangism, clinodactyly, and short fifth fingers). X-rays revealed bilateral symphalangism of the second digits and clinodactyly of the fifth digits, indicating brachymesophalangy type A3 (Figure 1). Upon investigation, her blood sample showed gross hemolysis (Figure 2A), hemoglobin of 6 g/dL, total leukocyte count of 13.1x109/L, and platelet count of 350x109/µL. She also had indirect hyperbilirubinemia (total bilirubin of 2.2 mg/dL, indirect bilirubin of 1.7 mg/dL), high serum lactate dehydrogenase (1100 U/L), and high reticulocyte count (8%). A peripheral smear showed normocytic normochromic anemia with anisopoikilocytosis, oval macrocytes, nucleated red blood cells, target cells, polychromasia, basophilic stippling, and sporadic binucleated cells (Figure 2B). There was erythroid hyperplasia in the bone marrow (Figure 2C). Hemoglobin electrophoresis, glucose-6-phosphate levels, osmotic fragility, and Coombs test results were non-diagnostic. CK-MB (5911 U/L) was significantly higher. Clinical exome sequencing revealed compound heterozygous pathogenic mutations in the ALDOA gene: c.242G>A (p.Gly81Asp, exon 3) and c.383T>C (p.Ile128Thr, exon 4). Both deleterious missense variants likely impaired structural stability and enzymatic function, confirming aldolase A deficiency (glycogen storage disease type XII). The patient remains on intermittent transfusion support, folate supplementation, and physiotherapy. Currently, she requires two to three transfusions a year. Serum ferritin is being monitored and the latest value was 235 ng/mL.

Biallelic pathogenic ALDOA mutations cause glycogen storage disease type XII, an autosomal recessive disorder affecting the glycolytic enzyme aldolase A, which splits fructose-1,6-bisphosphate into glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate and dihydroxyacetone phosphate. Aldolase A is mainly expressed in skeletal muscle and red blood cells. Its deficiency leads to oxidative stress, membrane instability, and chronic non-spherocytic hemolytic anemia due to impaired ATP production in erythrocytes [1,2]. In muscle, especially under anaerobic stress, impaired energy metabolism results in rhabdomyolysis, muscle pain, and elevated CK [1]. Patients may also show metabolic abnormalities, short stature, or mild intellectual disability. Our patient had intellectual delay along with the skeletal anomalies of brachymesophalangy and symphalangism, previously unreported in this condition.

Reported cases show phenotypic heterogeneity. In 1973, Beutler et al. [4] described a child with hemolytic anemia, developmental delay, and dysmorphism but no rhabdomyolysis. In 1981, Miwa et al. [5] reported two Japanese siblings with isolated hemolytic anemia. In 1996, Kreuder et al. [2] described hemolysis with myopathy, while in 2004, Yao et al. [1] reported rhabdomyolysis with elliptocytosis. More recently, in 2021, Kara et al. [3] described a Turkish boy with chronic anemia, rhabdomyolysis requiring dialysis, and dermatological features initially mistaken for biotinidase deficiency; whole exome sequencing confirmed a homozygous ALDOA mutation.

In patients with myopathy and unexplained non-spherocytic hemolytic anemia, deficits of enzymes such as phosphofructokinase, phosphoglycerate kinase, triosephosphate isomerase, and aldolase A should be taken into consideration. Early performance of next-generation sequencing can confirm the diagnosis. Glycogen storage disease type XII has no cure; its management includes rest, hydration, prompt fever control, transfusions, and awareness of hemolysis and rhabdomyolysis [3].
