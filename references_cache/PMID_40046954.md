Scimitar syndrome is a rare vascular malformation characterized by partial or 
total abnormal flow of the right pulmonary vein into the inferior vena cava, 
located above or below the diaphragm. Scimitar syndrome is a rare disease has an 
incidence of approximately 1-3 per 100,000 live births. And it can be divided 
into infantile type and child/adult type. Infantile patients have more 
deformities and poor prognosis, so it is urgent to combine multidisciplinary 
early precision diagnosis and treatment. Most children with childhood/adult 
forms are asymptomatic or have mild symptoms and can live normally without 
medical intervention. Here we report a case of a 3 year, 8-month-old girl who 
was diagnosed with Scimitar syndrome in our hospital. By presenting multiplanar 
CT images of malformed drainage veins in this patient's lungs and analyzing a 
series of cardiopulmonary abnormalities resulting from them, we have 
demonstrated in detail the diagnostic process of this rare disease, Scimitar 
syndrome. For patients with Scimitar syndrome, whether to take surgical 
treatment should be determined according to the specific cardiopulmonary 
function of patients, mild patients can take regular follow-up strategy. For 
patients with obvious clinical symptoms, surgery can significantly improve the 
long-term prognosis.

Scimitar syndrome or hypogenetic lung syndrome was first described as an autopsy finding by the French doctor Raoul Chassinat in 1836 [1], It is a rare variant of a group of congential cardiovascular anomalies, known as partial anomalous pulmonary venous return (PAPVR) [2], in which pulmonary vein flows into the inferior vena cava (IVC) rather than the left atrium (LA). In two thirds of the patients, the scimitar vein provides drainage for the entire right lung, but in one third, this vein drains only the lower portion of the right lung [3].

The term “scimitar” was first coined by Halasz in 1956 to describe the X-ray finding of a curvilinear [4], scimitar or Turkey sword-shaped density parallel to right cardiac border. this phenomenon is now found to be a disorder vein that connects the right pulmonary to the IVC, instead of LA in normal cases, creating a left to right shunt.

According to clinical presentation, Scimitar syndrome can be divided into 2 main forms. Symptoms can start during infancy (infantile form) or beyond (childhood/adult form). The infantile form generally presents within the first 2 months of life with tachypnea, recurrent pneumonia, failure to thrive, and signs of heart failure. Currently the infantile form is considered to be severe form of this disease with a worse prognosis, with major associated cardiac lesions and pulmonary hypertension. Meanwhile, the adult form was usually diagnosed incidentally, as part of them can be completely asymptomatic [5,6].

The diagnosis of Scimitar Syndrome is made by the demonstration of partial anomalous pulmonary venous return from the right lung to the IVC, the inferior cava-atrial junction, and the right atrium. this can be done by cardiac catheterization, and computed tomography (CT)and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Though conventional catheter-based angiography considered to be “gold standard” for the evaluation of aberrant Scimitar vein [7], it is an invasive examination. with the regard to the Scimitar syndrome as a complex form of anomalous venous return, it is now possible to make the diagnosis of the Scimitar syndrome using multidetector CT and MRI. Noninvasive assessment of these abnormal structures helps to make the diagnosis and aids in the surgical treatment. Here we report a case of a 3-year-old girl with Scimitar syndrome who underwent CT examination, yet without a chest x-ray examination, highlighting the typical findings, clinical presentations, and implications for management of this rare disorder.

A 3 years and 8 months old girl was admitted to our hospital in January 2021 as she caught a cough for about 20 days and got a fever for over 4 days. Her parent said there was no obvious cause. her cough was paroxysmal, with phlegm but not easy to cough up. The patient had no chills, vomiting, diarrhea, convulsions, wheezing, shortness of breath, or cyanosis. She was once taken to a local hospital, where she recovered soon and was released. Then 4 days ago, the patient developed fever with a thermal peak of 39.5°C. After taking antipyretics medicine, the body temperature could drop to normal level yet easily rise again. on admission her parents stated that the child had a history of repeated respiratory infections, denied a history of hepatitis, tuberculosis and other infectious diseases, also denied a history of drug allergy or food allergy. After admission, the patient underwent a series of laboratory tests. Except for hypersensitive C-reactive protein (CRP) elevated to 19.96 mg/L, which was above normal level, white blood cells, red blood cells, neutrophils, neutrophils percentage, lymphocyte ratio, hemoglobin, and platelets were all in the normal range. Liver function, renal function, myocardial enzyme, immunoglobulin and other tests showed no problems. Three days after admission, the sputum of the patient was identified by bacterial culture and drug susceptibility test, and haemophilus influenzae was cultured.

In terms of radiology, initially noncontrast computerized tomography of the chest was performed using a 64-detector row scanner (GE medical systems), the scan was acquired in helical mode and using pediatric protocol, the thorax from the thoracic inlet to the upper abdomen was included. Plain CT showed that the volume of the right lung was smaller than that of the contralateral lung, and the curved tubular shadow was visible in the middle and lower zone of right lung, which gradually thickened from top to bottom and eventually drainage into the inferior vena cava (Fig. 1). The radiologist then suggested that an enhanced CT scan would provide more detail. Two days later, the kid underwent a post contrast CT scan (pulmonary computerized tomography angiography), This time the aberrant vein connecting the pulmonary artery branch to the inferior vena cava (Fig. 2), shaped like a scimitar, parallel to the right cardiac border, showed more prominently. At the same time, it can be observed that the diameter of the main pulmonary artery is larger than that of the ascending aorta, the lumen of the right pulmonary artery is smaller than that of the opposite side, the volume of the left atrial is significantly smaller than that of the right atrial, and it can be seen that a small branch emanating from the abdominal trunk deviates upward to supply the lower lobe of the right lung (Fig. 2), These signs confirm the diagnosis of scimitar syndrome.Fig. 1(Nonenhanced CT) coronal view of soft tissue window showed the aberrant vein descending towards the caudal direction, resembling a scimitar (A), and the volume of right lung smaller than that of left lung can be observed visually (B). In admission, the patient also underwent a cardiac ultrasound exam, and it found that the heart was located in the median position, which was described as “mesocardia” in medical terms, meanwhile, the left ventricular systolic function and diastolic function were in the normal range, and no congenital malformation such as atrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect, and arterial ductus was found.

After the diagnosis was confirmed, the medical team provided the patient symptomatic support treatment such as piperacillin tazobactam for anti-infection, ambroxol for phlegm reduction, fluid rehydration and antipyretic remedy. ten days after treatment, the child's condition improved and was discharged. The pediatrician suggested that the patient should go to superior medical institution for further examination, or come back to our hospital for regular outpatient follow-up. And recently, we conducted a telephone follow-up of the patient, and the child has not undergone surgery until now, 3 years have passed, her other conditions are normal except that her athletic ability is slightly worse than that of her peers. We were told the child's parents was planning to bring her to a superior hospital for further examination as soon as possible.

Scimitar syndrome is a rare variant of a group of congential cardiovascular anomalies, known as partial anomalous pulmonary venous return (PAPVR), the hallmark of Scimitar syndrome is an anomalous right pulmonary vein that drains part or all of the right lung into the IVC, associated anomalies are variable and include hypoplasia of the right lung, dextroposition of the heart, hypoplasia of the right pulmonary artery (BPA), and anomalous systemic arterial supply from the aorta to the right lung [5]. Beside the heart was located in the median part (mesocardiac) in our case, other anomalies were detected as mentioned above, yet the artery supplied the lower lobe of right lung is origin from celiac trunk rather than aorta. In two thirds of the patients, the scimitar vein provides drainage for the entire right lung, but in one third, this vein drains only the lower portion of the right lung [3]. From this perspective our case should be categorize into the former type as the Scimitar vein drains the whole right lung into IVC.

Scimitar syndrome is a rare disease has an incidence of approximately 1 to 3 per 100,000 live births. the true incidence maybe higher as many older children or adult patients are asymptomatic, some of them were detected by routine chest radiograph. This child/adult form of scimitar syndrome typically has mild symptoms and a benign prognosis. meanwhile, infantile form patients which become symptomatic soon after birth often complicated by hypertension and heart failure, often has a poor prognosis. Due to the small number of cases, it is impossible to conduct a large sample study. our researchers searched the hospital's case system over the past decade and collected only one case. Reading the literature of the past 3 decades, in the global perspective, only French scholars have studied more than 100 cases of Scimitar syndrome (sample size of 122 cases) [8], and the 2 subsequent studies on Scimitar syndrome conducted by the University of Padua in Italy have 68 cases and 44 cases respectively [9,10]. Therefore, every case of scimitar syndrome deserves careful study. in 2020, Liliana Sá et al. report a case of a 36-week-old new born admitted to the neonatal intensive unit with worsening respiratory distress, chest radiography showed hypoplasia of the right lung and a tubular structure paralleling the right heart border, which diagnosed as Scimitar syndrome and confirmed by computed tomography later. Though her condition once improved during hospitalization, finally she died due to the progressive necrotizing enterocolitis which confirmed by anatomopathological examination. This case teaches us that when treating children with scimitar syndrome with other congenital heart defects, we should be alert to other malignant disease that is occurring in other systems [4]. Kabbani et al. described a unique bilateral Scimitar syndrome case seen in an infant with involvement of both lungs, totally anomalous pulmonary venous connection, horseshoe lung and right pulmonary cyst [11]. up to date there are only about 10 such cases reported wordwide. In 2019, Aklilu et al. from Ethiopia reported a case that a 3 days old male neonate with imperforate anus presented with abdominal distention. his colostomy was done to relieve abdominal distension. The chest x-ray revealed a curved shadow on the right mid lung zone extending to the diaphragm resembling scimitar sign. Abdominal ultrasound, transthoracic echocardiography and CTA confirmed presence of Scimitar vein and other cardiopulmonary malformation. At that time, only 4 cases of co-existence of imperforate anus and scimitar syndrome have been reported in the global literature. The authors of this paper, like other authors, are confused about the relationship between imperforate anus and Scimitar syndrome, whether Scimitar syndrome and imperforate anus share a common pathogenetic pathway or not remains unanswered. Finally, the authors suggested that genetic testing may offer deeper insight [12].

Asymptomatic adults or older children with Scimitar syndrome resulting in small shunts can lead normal lives without surgical intervention, they just need consecutive follow up. Yet surgery is the definitive treatment for symptomatic scimitar syndrome, such patients often are infantile type. The approach to surgical management depends on the anatomy of the PAPVR. One technique separates the scimitar vein from the IVC and implants it directly into the LA using a polytetrafluoroethylene interposition tube graft. Other surgical options include baffling of the anomalous venous return into the LA. This is accomplished using the anterior wall of the RA to form a tunnel through which the scimitar vein flow is diverted toward the LA, and septostomy through which the baffled blood passes into the LA [1,7].

Surgical treatment should be considered in patients who have hemodynamically significant left-to-right shunting (Qp:Qs greater than 2:1), evidence of right heart failure, or recurrent pulmonary infections, and in those undergoing surgical repair of other major cardiac lesions. The patient in our case had recurrent pneumonia and evidence of pulmonary hypertension which met the indications for surgery intervention, but her parents were cautious about the risky of surgery, so a conservative follow-up strategy was adopted. So far, the child is generally in good condition.

Hence, scimitar syndrome is a rare cardiopulmonary malformation disease. Its diagnosis requires a multi-disciplinary approach, and involves various investigational modalities. Surgical treatment mainly aims to correct the pulmonary venous return, and may require lobectomy. for those older children and adult patients who present asymptomatic, a regular follow up strategy should be adopted. Prognosis depends on the severity of the vascular lesions and the associated anomalies. And the prognosis needs to be alert for those patients who present in infancy.
