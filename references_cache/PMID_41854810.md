# Simplified dosimetry using two-time-point kinetic modeling of (124)I-MIBG PET for (131)I-MIBG therapy in neuroblastoma.
**Authors:** Wang Y, Huh Y, Matthay KK, Vo KT, Pampaloni MH, DuBois SG, Seo Y
**Journal:** Med Phys (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mp.70388](https://doi.org/10.1002/mp.70388)

## Content

BACKGROUND: 131I-metaiodobenzylguanidine (131I-MIBG) therapy is an established and effective treatment for metastatic neuroblastoma. Due to the substantial variability in absorbed dose across different tumor sites and organs, 131I-MIBG dosimetry, such as achieved via SPECT imaging, is critical for enabling personalized therapy planning. However, conventional imaging-based dosimetry typically requires three or more imaging sessions to reliably estimate time-integrated activity (TIA) of tumors and organs, which imposes workflow burdens and increases patient inconvenience. Therefore, there is a clear need for dosimetry methods that can maintain accuracy while requiring fewer imaging sessions.

PURPOSE: This study aims to develop and validate a simplified dosimetry method for 131I-MIBG therapy that enables robust estimation of TIA using only two imaging time points. The method leverages kinetic modeling to estimate tumor and organ time-activity curves (TACs) and TIAs from limited imaging data and was validated using 124I-MIBG PET imaging data.

METHODS: Five subjects with neuroblastoma underwent 124I-MIBG PET/CT imaging at three or four time points post-administration. Two imaging time points (∼28 and ∼113 h post-administration) were selected for TIA estimation using a kinetic modeling framework. To obtain the blood input function, left ventricular activity at the two time points was extracted and fitted to a mono-exponential function. With this input function, a one-tissue compartmental model was then applied to estimate tumor and organ TACs from the two-time-point data, and the corresponding TIAs were calculated by integrating the modeled TACs. The proposed method was compared with (1) a conventional mono-exponential fitting method using the same two-time-point data, and (2) a reference standard based on bi-exponential fitting of all available three- or four-time-point data. To evaluate the performance of the proposed method, relative errors in TIA estimation for tumors and organs were calculated using the bi-exponential fitting results as the reference.

RESULTS: The proposed method achieved substantially improved accuracy over mono-exponential fitting. Taking the bi-exponential method as the reference, the proposed method yielded an average TIA estimation bias of 0.3%, a standard deviation of 13.8%, and a root mean square error (RMSE) of 14.2%. In contrast, mono-exponential fitting resulted in a higher bias of 14.9%, a standard deviation of 36.3%, and an RMSE of 39.5%. Specifically, the proposed method outperformed mono-exponential fitting in tumors, adrenal glands, brain, and thyroid.

CONCLUSIONS: We developed a novel dosimetry method based on two-time-point imaging and kinetic modeling that enables simplified TIA estimation in 131I-MIBG therapy. Validated using 124I-MIBG PET data, this approach demonstrated improved TIA estimation performance compared with conventional mono-exponential fitting. It may offer a physiologically motivated and more clinically applicable solution that supports personalized dosimetry and facilitates individualized treatment planning in radiopharmaceutical therapy.

Radiopharmaceutical therapy with 131I-metaiodobenzylguanidine (131I-MIBG) is an established and effective treatment for patients with metastatic neuroblastoma and has been widely used to deliver targeted radiation to neuroblastoma cells via the norepinephrine transporter pathway. 1– 4 However, the absorbed dose delivered to organs and tumors during 131I-MIBG therapy can vary substantially among patients and across different tumor lesions 5 , 6 . This variability contributes to differences in therapeutic response and clinical outcomes of 131I-MIBG therapy 7 . Given this variability, dosimetry is critical for quantifying patient- and lesion-specific absorbed dose. The dosimetry analysis can be used to guide the safe administration of therapeutic activity 8 and to quantify absorbed dose at specific tumor sites for evaluating treatment efficacy 7 , 9 . Furthermore, it supports individualized treatment planning, informs decisions on subsequent treatment cycles or combination regimens 10 , 11 , and hence plays an important role in advancing precision medicine approaches in 131I-MIBG therapy for neuroblastoma.

Dose estimation for 131I-MIBG therapy at the organ and tumor levels can be performed using imaging-based dosimetry, such as through posttherapy single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) imaging. The regional absorbed dose calculation generally follows the Medical Internal Radiation Dose (MIRD) schema 12 , which requires the estimation of two key components: the time-integrated activity (TIA), which reflects the total number of disintegrations in a volume of interest (VOI), and the S value, representing the absorbed dose per unit TIA 13 . Therefore, accurate TIA estimation is essential, as it directly influences the calculated absorbed dose delivered to tumors and organs. TIA estimation typically involves acquiring a series of three or more SPECT or planar scans at different time points following 131I-MIBG administration 5 , 14 , 15 to measure VOI activity retention over time. The acquired activity data are then used to generate regional time-activity curves (TACs), from which TIA is derived. However, this multi-time-point imaging approach places heavy logistical and operational demands on imaging centers and may increase the imaging burden for patients, limiting its routine clinical implementation.

To address this problem, simplified dosimetry methods that reduce the number of imaging sessions have been explored for the 131I-MIBG therapy and other radiopharmaceuticals. For example, Seo et al. 16 proposed an empirical approach to estimate TIA using only two imaging time points, while Madsen et al. 17 developed a population-based statistical model to estimate TIA from single-time-point data. These strategies aim to streamline clinical workflows and improve patient compliance by reducing imaging burden. However, limited temporal sampling presents challenges in accurately capturing the pharmacokinetic behavior of radiopharmaceuticals in tumors and normal organs. As a result, TIA estimation from sparse data requires careful modeling and validation to improve the reliability of absorbed dose calculations. 18

In this study, we propose a novel simplified dosimetry method for 131I-MIBG therapy using only two posttherapy SPECT imaging sessions and validate the method using 124I-MIBG PET data. Inspired by dynamic PET studies 19– 21 , a kinetic modeling framework is built for the TIA estimation of tumors and organs. We estimate the patient-specific blood input function TAC from imaging-derived blood activity and apply a one-tissue compartment model 22 to reconstruct tumor and organ TACs, from which TIAs are computed. In addition to pursuing simplified dosimetry and enhancing clinical feasibility, the proposed method may also extend the application of kinetic modeling beyond its traditional role in kinetic parameter estimation 20 , 23 . In this study, we leverage it for the reconstruction of TACs and the subsequent estimation of TIAs. We hypothesize that this physiologically motivated kinetic modeling framework can improve the accuracy and robustness of TIA estimation from limited data, thus offering a practical and theoretically grounded solution for personalized dosimetry in 131I-MIBG therapy. To evaluate the performance of the proposed method, its TIA estimation using two-time-point imaging data is compared to a reference method based on bi-exponential fitting of all available three- or four-time-point data. For comparison purposes, the conventional mono-exponential fitting method using the same two-time-point data as the proposed method was also studied, and its TIA estimation was compared to the reference method.

This study included five subjects (age range: 8–23 years; 2 males, 3 females) with metastatic neuroblastoma 24 . The 124I-MIBG PET imaging and dosimetry protocol has been authorized by patient written consent and approved by the Institutional Review Board.

Each subject received PET/CT imaging sessions at three or four time points following the 124I-MIBG administration (1.06 MBq/kg). Scans were performed on a GE Discovery VCT PET/CT system (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI) for four subjects, and on a Philips Gemini TF PET/CT system (Philips Healthcare, Highland Heights, OH) for the other subject. The PET/CT imaging acquisition and reconstruction settings are summarized in the Supplementary Materials. The field of view of both the CT and PET imaging covers the whole body of each subject. The four post-administration scan time points were as follows: t1=2.1±0.9h, t2=27.6±6.8h, t3=52.9±9.7h, and t4=113.0±17.1h. Three subjects had scans at all four time points, while two had scans at t1, t2, and t4 only.

A total of 46 tumors and lesions were identified from the three or four whole-body PET/CT image sets acquired for each subject, with volumes of interest (VOIs) placed accordingly. The tumor and lesion VOIs were delineated manually on the PET/CT images, informed by all three or four PET scans to improve localization. The VOIs were also placed on multiple organs, including the lungs, liver, brain, spleen, adrenal glands, heart, kidneys, and thyroid. The organ VOIs were generated using manual segmentations primarily based on the CT component of each whole-body PET/CT scan. In addition, an ellipsoidal VOI (volume range: 5–10 cm3) was placed in the left ventricle (LV) to acquire the blood activity. The VOI placement was performed for all three or four image sessions of each subject. The 124I-MIBG activity of each VOI at each imaging session was extracted.

We aim to reduce the number of required imaging sessions for TIA estimation of MIBG and propose using only two-time-point imaging data. Specifically, image data acquired at t2=27.6±6.8h and t4=113.0±17.1h post-administration were selected based on considerations of clinical feasibility. In many medical centers, a pretherapy 123I-MIBG scan for diagnostic purposes is typically acquired ~24 hours post-administration, while posttherapy 131I-MIBG imaging is commonly performed ~100 hours post-administration. By leveraging the ability to convert the diagnostic 123I-MIBG image into an equivalent 131I-MIBG image at ~24 hours, this two-time-point selection may facilitate more practical and clinically implementable dosimetry protocols. Additional analyses of time-point selection are provided in the Supplementary Materials. With this time-point selection, the proposed method for TIA estimation employs compartmental modeling and involves two steps: (1) estimating the blood input function and (2) deriving the organ/tissue TAC and calculating the organ/tissue TIA.

To assess the blood input function, we assume the blood concentration of 124I-MIBG approximates the mono-exponential decay with the combined effect of biological and physical decay. The proposed simplified dosimetry method involves only two-time-point MIBG imaging; therefore, blood activity measurements are available only at two time points t2 and t4. Suppose the measured LV blood activity at these time points are cLVt2 and cLV(t4), respectively. The corresponding decay-corrected blood activities CLV at the two time points are: (1) CLVt2=cLVt2expλt2 (2) CLVt4=cLVt4expλt4

Here, λ is the decay constant of 124I. λ=ln2/T1/2, and T1/2=4.18days is the physical half-life of 124I. The decay-corrected blood input function Cb(t) is then modeled by mono-exponential decay: (3) Cbt=A0exp−k0t

A0 and k0 are the magnitude and biological decay constant of the blood input function, respectively, and are determined via fitting the two-time-point data CLV(t2) and CLV(t4).

With the blood input function obtained, we model the decay-corrected tissue TAC CT(t) with a one-tissue compartment model, as shown in Fig. 1. The tissue activity CT,1T(t) is modeled as a temporal convolution between the impulse response function of the one-tissue compartmental model H(t) and the blood input function Cb(t): (4) CT,1Tt=Ht⊗Cbt (5) Ht=K1exp−k2t.

The parameters K1 and k2 indicate the blood-to-tissue and tissue-to-blood delivery rates of 124I-MIBG, respectively. ⨂ indicates the temporal convolution. K1 and k2 are determined by fitting the decay-corrected two-time-point tissue activities CT(t2) and CT(t4), in which (6) CTt2=cTt2expλt2, (7) CTt4=cTt4expλt4. cTt2 and cTt4 are the organ/tumor tissue activities measured by PET at the corresponding time points, without decay correction.

With all parameters A0,k0,K1,k2 determined, the tissue TAC with decay correction, CT,1T(t), is determined and can be expressed in a closed-form expression: (8) CT,1Tt=A0K1k0−k2exp−k2t−exp(−k0t).

Accordingly, the tissue TAC without decay correction cT,1T(t) is: (9) cT,1Tt=A0K1k0−k2exp−(k2+λ)t−exp−k0+λt.

As a result, the estimated time-integrated activity (TIA) of the proposed method is the time integration of cT,1Tt: (10) TIA1T=∫0∞cT,1Ttdt=A0K1(k2+λ)(k0+λ).

To evaluate the performance of the proposed method, we compared it with two alternative approaches: a baseline method using mono-exponential fitting and a reference standard method using bi-exponential fitting.

The baseline approach fits the same two-time-point tissue activity data used in the proposed method, cT(t2) and cT(t4), using a conventional mono-exponential function. The tissue TAC without decay correction is modeled as: (11) cT,m(t)=Amexp−kmt

Here, Am and km are the fitted amplitude and effective decay constant, respectively. The corresponding TIA is computed as: (12) TIAm=∫0∞cT,mtdt=Amkm

To establish a reference method for performance evaluation, we also fitted all available three- or four-time-point data using a bi-exponential function. Assuming zero activity prior to the MIBG administration, the tissue activity without decay correction is modeled as: (13) cT,bt=Abexp−kb1t−exp−kb2t.

Here, Ab is the amplitude, and kb1 and kb2 are the two effective decay constants. The corresponding TIA is: (14) TIAb=∫0∞cT,btdt=Abkb2−kb1kb1kb2.

It is worth noting that cT,1Tt from the proposed method (Eq. 9) and cT,bt from the bi-exponential method (Eq. 13) have similar expressions, and both start from a value of zero at t=0.

The proposed method assumes that the blood input function Cb(t) follows a mono-exponential decay, which can be approximated using the two-time-point activity data CLVt2 and CLV(t4). To evaluate this assumption, we fitted all available three or four time points of CLV(t) using a mono-exponential function. The fitting result was then examined and compared with the fit obtained using only the two-time-point data, as employed in the proposed method.

To evaluate the performance of the proposed TIA estimation method, we computed the relative error of TIA for each tissue VOI using both the proposed method and the conventional mono-exponential fitting. The bi-exponential fitting using all available time-point data served as the reference standard. The relative error for the ith VOI is defined as: (15) REki=TIAki−TIAbiTIAbi×100%.

Here, k=1T for the proposed method based on the one-tissue compartment model, and k=m for the conventional mono-exponential fitting. The bias, standard deviation, and root mean square error (RMSE) of the relative error across all VOIs of each subject were then calculated.

Here, M is the total number of VOIs for that subject. The bias, standard deviation, and RMSE of the relative error were also averaged across the five subjects.

To further assess method performance for both tumor and non-tumor tissues, similar calculations were performed for all tumor VOIs and all organ VOIs across all subjects.

The proposed method, based on two-time-point data and one-tissue compartment modeling, achieved an average bias of 0.3%, a standard deviation of 13.8%, and an RMSE of 14.2%. In comparison, the conventional mono-exponential fitting method yielded a larger bias of 14.9%, a standard deviation of 36.3%, and an RMSE of 39.5%. The results indicate that the proposed method using the two-time-point data can approximate the TIA estimated from the bi-exponential fit using all available data, while substantially improving performance over the conventional mono-exponential approach. In addition, the proposed method is computationally efficient, with TIAs of all tumors and organs of the five subjects estimated within minutes using a personal computer.

Compared with the mono-exponential fit, the proposed method can better recover both the increase in activity from zero in the early phase and the subsequent decrease in the late phase. This study presents a novel method for TIA estimation to enable a simplified dosimetry method for 131I-MIBG therapy, and the proposed method was validated with 124I-MIBG PET/CT data. In contrast to conventional approaches that typically rely on three or more posttherapy imaging sessions, the proposed method requires only two imaging sessions. The time points of the two imaging sessions, i.e., ~24 hours and ~100 hours post-administration, resemble the pretherapy 123I-MIBG diagnostic imaging time and the posttherapy 131I-MIBG imaging time, respectively. Because the pretherapy 123I-MIBG image can be converted to an equivalent 131I-MIBG image, these time points can be readily incorporated into the dosimetry workflow, making the approach more clinically feasible. By reducing the imaging burden while maintaining accurate TIA estimation, it may offer practical advantages for both patients and imaging centers by enhancing operational efficiency and improving patient convenience.

A key challenge in the TIA estimation with two-time-point data is the inherent limitation of fitting complex pharmacokinetics using sparse data. The commonly used bi-exponential fitting model 16 , 25– 27 is not feasible in this setting, as it requires at least three data points to reliably estimate the amplitude and the two decay components of the curve. While mono-exponential fitting can be applied to two-time-point data, it may oversimplify the TAC estimation, particularly by omitting the early uptake phase, which has been observed in organs and tumors. 5 , 28

The proposed method addresses these limitations through a two-step modeling strategy. First, the blood input function is estimated by fitting the two-time-point left ventricle activity to a mono-exponential function. This provides a patient-specific and physiologically informed input function that reflects the change in blood MIBG concentration over time. Second, a one-tissue compartmental model is applied using the estimated input function to fit the two-time-point VOI activity data of organs or tumors. The model contains only two parameters K1 and k2, which can be quantified by fitting the two-time-point VOI activity data. Compared with the conventional mono-exponential fitting, the VOI TACs estimated by the proposed method follow a bi-exponential format and contain both the rising and the decreasing phases, enabling better description of the temporal dynamics of MIBG, which can lead to improved TIA estimation, as shown in Figs. 4–5 and Table I.

The proposed method is inspired by the compartmental modeling widely used in dynamic PET imaging analysis. 19– 21 However, we emphasize that this study introduces a key innovation by enabling kinetic modeling using only two-time-point data, far fewer than the 20–100 dynamic frames typically acquired in common dynamic PET protocols 29– 31 . First, for blood input function modeling, while exponential fitting (typically with multiple terms) has been previously explored, 31 , 32 our results demonstrate that mono-exponential fitting remains feasible even under sparse sampling conditions, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Second, the application of the one-tissue compartmental model can reconstruct tissue TACs using two-time-point data and yield robust TIA estimation. As a result, the proposed method achieves substantially lower bias and higher stability in TIA estimation compared to the conventional mono-exponential fit, particularly in regions such as tumors, adrenal glands, thyroid, and brain (Table I). Furthermore, compared with empirical or population-based methods for simplified dosimetry 16 , 18 , our method offers a clearer theoretical foundation grounded in kinetic modeling, which may enhance generalizability across patient populations.

In addition to the technical innovation, another important aspect of this work lies in the objective of the compartmental modeling framework. Traditional compartmental modeling in dynamic PET often focuses on the quantification of kinetic parameters, such as the blood-to-tissue delivery rate constant K1, the net influx rate Ki, or the distribution volume VT, for physiological or pathological characterization. 20 , 23 In contrast, our study emphasizes the estimation of TACs to calculate the TIA for radiopharmaceutical dosimetry purposes, thereby expanding the scope of kinetic modeling from diagnostic evaluation toward therapeutic assessment. Notably, this modeling framework may also be applicable to the simplified dosimetry of other radiopharmaceuticals, which could broaden its utility beyond 131I-MIBG therapy.

This study has several limitations. First, the input function was derived from LV blood activity without accounting for metabolites of MIBG 33 , which may affect accuracy in TAC estimation for certain organs. Future work could incorporate metabolite correction and validation with blood sampling to refine the input model. Second, the proposed method universally applies a one-tissue compartmental model to tumors and all types of organs. While both the proposed method and the bi-exponential method (used as the reference) satisfy a zero-activity boundary condition at t=0, which may improve stability in TIA estimation, the potential risk of bias introduced by this constraint should be further evaluated. It is also worth exploring the potential of other kinetic models (such as the Patlak model 34 , 35 , which has two parameters Ki and VT and permits non-zero activity at t=0) and associated kinetic model selection 36 , 37 . However, it is worth noting that when using two-time-point data, modeling of the blood input function and tissue time-activity curves should use simplified kinetic assumptions that are compatible with the limited number of measurements, since models with more than two kinetic parameters cannot be reliably fitted with only two data points. Third, the current analysis included only five subjects. Although improvements compared with the baseline mono-exponential model were observed across subjects, validation in a larger patient cohort is essential to confirm the robustness, generalizability, and clinical utility of the proposed method. Fourth, the proposed method was validated using 124I-MIBG PET data in this study. Future work should validate the approach using 131I-MIBG SPECT data to fully assess its applicability in clinical settings.

In this study, we developed and evaluated a simplified dosimetry method for 131I-MIBG therapy of neuroblastoma, using a kinetic modeling framework that relies solely on two-time-point imaging (~28 hours and ~113 hours post-administration). The method was validated using 124I-MIBG PET/CT data. It demonstrated improved performance in estimating time-integrated activity compared with conventional mono-exponential fitting for both tumors and normal organs. This framework represents an extension of kinetic modeling approaches and provides a practical strategy for simplified dosimetry of radiopharmaceuticals.
