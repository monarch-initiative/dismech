# Natural history of NGLY1 deficiency: motor function & clinical features.
**Authors:** Morrison G, Dwight S, Landy H, Mueller WF, Ventola P, Deck R, Schweighardt B, Wilsey M, Lee KJ, Suter B
**Journal:** Hum Mol Genet (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1093/hmg/ddag013](https://doi.org/10.1093/hmg/ddag013)

## Content

N-glycanase 1 (NGLY1) Deficiency is an ultra-rare neurodevelopmental disorder caused by biallelic loss-of-function mutations in the NGLY1 gene, leading to severe impairments in neurocognitive and motor function abilities in the affected patient population. Its core clinical features include global developmental delay, hyperkinetic movement disorders, elevation of liver transaminases, (hypo)alacrima, and progressive sensorimotor neuropathy. Due to the range of phenotypes and severity within the confirmed patient population, ongoing characterization of the disease is critical. A prospective natural history study (NHS) was conducted to further elucidate disease phenotypes and examine any changes in neurocognitive or motor function over a one-year period. Fifteen participants, representing roughly 10% of the total identified patient population, were enrolled to complete three onsite visits. Participants in this study exhibited phenotypes previously reported, including core disease phenotypes, hypotonia, scoliosis, musculoskeletal abnormalities, abnormal electroencephalogram (EEG) results, and visual and genitourinary abnormalities. Results from validated neurocognitive and motor function assessments revealed profound global developmental delays in all participants, including communication, gross and fine motor function, and cognitive domains. Age-equivalent scores identified a widening developmental gap between participants and neurotypical peers over time, indicating delayed milestone achievement and/or regression. Although fluctuations were observed, measurements for individual participants were mostly stable over the course of the NHS. Liver transaminase and biomarker N-acetylglucosamine-asparagine (GlcNAc-Asn; GNA) levels were elevated in all participants. Results build on previous studies and inform the selection of potential endpoints for emerging interventional clinical trials, including laboratory values, neurocognitive and motor function assessments, and caregiver reported outcomes.

N-glycanase 1 (NGLY1) Deficiency (OMIM #615273), also referred to as NGLY1 congenital disorder of deglycosylation (NGLY1-CDDG), is an ultra-rare, autosomal recessive disorder caused by loss-of-function variants in the NGLY1 gene with no approved treatment [1, 2]. Patients with NGLY1 Deficiency exhibit five core clinical features: global developmental delay and/or intellectual disability, hyperkinetic movement disorders, transient elevation of liver transaminases, decreased tear production, and peripheral neuropathy [3–6]. The patient population is heterogeneous, with significant variability in affected systems and the severity of symptoms among patients, even between siblings [7]. In addition to the five core clinical features, patients often exhibit additional clinical features, including but not limited to ataxia, hypotonia, absent speech, progressive cerebral atrophy, microcephaly, neurodegeneration, epilepsy, visual abnormalities, feeding difficulty with oromotor dysfunction, constipation, sleep disorders, hepatomegaly and other hepatic abnormalities, adrenal insufficiency, hypocholesterolemia, reduced ability to sweat, hearing loss, scoliosis, and joint contractures that limit mobility [4, 5, 8, 9].

Since the first reported case of NGLY1 Deficiency in 2012 [2], approximately 160 patients have been identified worldwide (Grace Science Foundation, unpublished data). The disease prognosis and lifespan are unknown; however, there have been 28 known patient deaths, with a median age of death of 14.4 years, supporting an urgent need for research and development of potential therapies (Grace Science Foundation, unpublished data). As there is currently no approved therapy for NGLY1 Deficiency, daily symptom management is the existing standard of care, as most patients are unable to walk or speak and need assistance with daily bathing, toileting, and feeding. This burden of care may increase over time as many caregivers reported regression of motor and/or cognitive function during puberty [10].

NGLY1 Deficiency is caused by biallelic mutations in the NGLY1 gene that lead to loss of function of the NGLY1 (N-glycanase 1) protein, a cytosolic deglycosylation enzyme. NGLY1 is responsible for cleaving N-glycans from proteins as part of the endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation (ERAD) pathway, a pathway critical in processing misfolded glycoproteins. During this process, NGLY1 hydrolyzes the proximal N-acetylglucosamine-asparagine (GlcNAc-Asn; GNA) bond to assist in the removal of N-linked glycans from proteins [11]. GNA accumulation has been shown to be a biomarker for NGLY1 Deficiency [12, 13] and loss of NGLY1 function has been implicated in proteostasis and ferroptosis dysfunction [14, 15].

Given the recent discovery of NGLY1 Deficiency and its small patient population, there have been only a few cohort investigations of the disease [1, 4, 5, 10, 16]. To advance the discovery of future therapeutics for this disorder, additional observational studies are necessary to gain a comprehensive understanding of the disease and its progression and to identify clinically relevant endpoints.

In a previous prospective NHS (NCT03834987), results indicated significant global developmental delays and declines in both gross and fine motor function [5]. Deterioration in motor function was observed with increased difficulties in sitting and standing over the duration of the study. This regression was also described in a caregiver survey study that reported all patients 14 years or older exhibited regression, especially diminished motor function [10]. Impaired neurocognitive ability and motor function were reported to be the most troublesome symptoms by caregivers, making it important to capture a longitudinal understanding of these clinical features and develop effective therapeutics that improve these domains.

The data reported here are based on a prospective NHS of participants with NGLY1 Deficiency conducted at Baylor College of Medicine (NCT06122766) and expand on the results of prior studies through the collection of longitudinal data using validated instruments to assess developmental delay and motor function. Specifically, this NHS investigates different standardized instruments than those utilized in prior natural history studies, in an effort to provide important information for the development of clinical trial endpoints for therapeutic interventional studies. The instruments were selected to allow for characterization of the disease and its clinical features, including disease progression in a timeframe relevant to therapeutic trials.

There is currently an active interventional clinical trial investigating the safety and efficacy of an NGLY1 gene replacement therapy for patients with NGLY1 Deficiency (NCT06199531). The elucidation of clinical manifestations, disease course over time, and identification of potential clinical endpoints provided through this NHS and previous patient studies provide important insights to guide the interpretation of interventional study outcomes.

The NHS enrolled a total of 15 participants, including 2 pairs of siblings, all of whom had a confirmed diagnosis of NGLY1 Deficiency. Participants completed all study procedures on-site at the Baylor College of Medicine (BCM) Texas Children’s Hospital, which involved clinical evaluations and neurocognitive and motor function assessments at 3 timepoints: baseline (visit 1), month 6 (visit 2), and month 12 (visit 3) (Fig. 1). Two participants withdrew from the study after month 6 due to enrollment in a concurrent interventional trial (NCT06199531) and one participant withdrew after month 6 due to a leg injury unrelated to the NHS that impacted the participant’s ability to travel, resulting in 12/15 (80.0%) participants completing the full observation period.

Of the 15 participants enrolled, 9 (60.0%) participants were female and 6 (40.0%) were male (Table 1). Participants’ ages at baseline ranged between 4–13 years old, and the mean (SD) age at baseline was 8.08 (3.23) years. At baseline, the mean (SD) weight was 21.29 (5.78) kg, and the mean (SD) height was 113.35 (13.02) cm. Participants’ mean (SD) head circumference at baseline was 49.87 (2.60) cm. All participants had head circumference measurements at or below the 49th percentile, with a mean (SD) percentile of 16.07 (16.45). Four participants had head circumference measurements in the 1st percentile, indicating microcephaly. Participants received a molecular diagnosis of NGLY1 Deficiency using whole exome sequencing/exome array (10/15), gene panels (2/15), single NGLY1 gene sequencing (2/15), or GNA biomarker testing (1/15). Review of genetic testing results revealed biallelic variants in NGLY1 including nonsense, missense, frameshift, and splice mutations (Supplementary Table 1). The most common variant reported was c.1201A > T, identified in 9/15 (60.0%) participants. Genotype–phenotype evaluations revealed no significant correlation between variants and disease manifestations (data not shown).

Participant demographics, diagnosis, and family history.

The mean (SD) age at NGLY1 Deficiency diagnosis for the 15 participants was 2.6 (1.99) years, with the first symptom reported at a mean (SD) age of 3.6 (2.2) months, suggesting a ~ 2-year delay between the identification of disease manifestation and receiving an accurate diagnosis (Table 1). First symptoms experienced, ordered by most common, included floppiness (86.7%), irritability or colic (80.0%), disrupted sleep patterns (80.0%), reflux disorder (66.7%), poor feeding and/or swallowing (53.3%), lethargy (40.0%), and ‘too calm for a baby’ (26.7%). The mean (SD) age at regression, defined as a degeneration of developmental skills or behavior, was reported at 3.58 (3.76) years with a range of 0.33–9 years, illustrating variability in the reported onset of developmental delays within the NGLY1 Deficiency patient population.

The collected family history for participants enrolled in the study is summarized in Table 1. The most common conditions in the participants’ family histories were anxiety for 7/15 (46.7%) participants, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) or attention deficit disorder (ADD) for 6/15 (40.0%) participants, and NGLY1 Deficiency for 6/15 (40.0%) participants. The high percentage of NGLY1 Deficiency cases observed in family histories, 6/15 (40.0%), was due to study participants with affected siblings.

A medical history questionnaire was completed by caregivers and reviewed by the study team at the baseline visit to capture neurological and musculoskeletal abnormalities in participants (Table 2). The most common neurological abnormalities reported were movement disorders in 13/15 (86.7%) participants, sleep disorders in 11/15 (73.3%) participants, and neuropathy in 8/15 (53.3%) participants. Of the 13 participants with a reported movement disorder, 11 participants further categorized their movement disorder as hyperkinetic (7/11 [63.6%]) or hypokinetic (2/11 [18.2%]). Seven participants with a hyperkinetic movement disorder reported specific movement types including tremor in 6/7 (85.7%) participants, myoclonus in 6/7 (85.7%) participants, and dystonia in 4/7 (57.1%) participants. Seven participants had 2 or more movement disorder types reported.

Neurological and musculoskeletal symptoms.

Thirteen of fifteen participants reported a musculoskeletal abnormality. The most common musculoskeletal abnormality type was low muscle tone, reported in 12/13 (92.3%) participants; however, there were a few reports of other abnormality types in participants, including joint laxity/dislocation (1/13 [7.7%]), fracture (2/13 [15.4%]), and brittle bones (2/13 [15.4%]). Scoliosis was reported in 5 (33.3%) participants.

Eight of fifteen (53.3%) participants reported seizures during their lifetime, and 3/15 (20.0%) reported clinical seizures during the period of the study (Table 2). Of the 8 participants who had reported a current or historical presence of seizures, the median seizure onset was 8.0 months (range 4–113 months), and the most common seizure types were myoclonic (4/8 [50.0%]) and atonic (4/8 [50.0%]), followed by absence (2/8 [25.0%]). Five participants reported successful seizure remission, with 2 participants requiring continued use of anticonvulsant medications. The most commonly used anticonvulsant medications were lamotrigine and levetiracetam, followed by valproic acid. Three participants used a combination of two or more anticonvulsant medications.

Caregivers reported abnormalities in systems other than musculoskeletal and neurological using a medical history questionnaire at the baseline visit (Table 3). Types of system abnormalities that were reported in participants included: visual abnormalities in 7/15 (46.7%), respiratory disorders in 4/15 (26.7%), genitourinary system anomalies in 4/15 (26.7%), and liver problems in 2/13 (15.4%). There were no reports of hearing loss, or cardiovascular, dermatologic, endocrine, or hematologic disorders.

Abnormalities in other systems.

For the 7/15 (46.7%) participants with a reported visual abnormality, 5 participants were assessed for visual abnormality types. The most common abnormality types included hypermetropia in 2/5 (40.0%), strabismus in 2/5 (40.0%), and amblyopia in 2/5 (40.0%) participants.

Genitourinary abnormalities (Table 3) had a higher association with male participants. Genitourinary anomaly types reported included small penis (100.0%) and anomalies of the kidney (25.0%).

Participants provided a list of current medications during each visit to assess common medication usage within the cohort population and to track changes in medication use over time. Overall, the most common concomitant treatments included laxatives in 10/15 (66.7%) participants, drops for dry eye in 7/15 (46.7%) participants, antibiotics in 7/15 (46.7%) participants, seizure medications in 6/15 (40.0%) participants, and medications for reflux/peptic ulcers in 5/15 (33.3%) participants.

The Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, 4th Edition (BSID-4) is a comprehensive tool used to measure development in early childhood including cognition, language, motor skills, social, emotional, and adaptive behavior skills. Due to profound impairment in patients with NGLY1 Deficiency, this tool was appropriate to assess all participants, regardless of age, due to their developmental level. Fifteen participants were assessed at baseline and month 6, and 12 of these participants were assessed at month 12. All participants had significant global developmental delay (Fig. 2b), most with a developmental quotient (DQ) below 25, reflecting profound impairment that remained consistent over the course of the study (Table 4). Younger participants, although still impaired, had higher DQs than older participants, suggesting that the developmental gap between individuals with NGLY1 Deficiency and neurotypical peers widens with age (Fig. 2a). Conversion to DQ scores allows comparison to age-normative scores but attenuates the scale, resulting in a floor effect. This effect was not observed using Growth Scale Value (GSV) scores since the GSV scoring system does not normalize by age. When analyzing specific domains of the BSID-4 using GSV scores, participants had the lowest scores in expressive communication and the highest scores in motor function (fine and gross motor) (Fig. 2c; Table 4). Participants’ DQ and GSV domain scores from the BSID-4 remained relatively unchanged from baseline to the month 12 visits, suggesting a lack of improvement in developmental skills across all domains within this timeframe.

Developmental outcome scores and mean changes from baseline.

The Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scale, 3rd Edition (Vineland-3) was administered to assess adaptive behaviors and evaluate developmental disability in participants with NGLY1 Deficiency through assessments in communication, daily living, socialization, and motor function. Twelve participants were assessed with the Vineland-3 at all three timepoints. All participants exhibited significant delays in adaptive skills compared with the neurotypical pediatric population, as demonstrated by Adaptive Behavior Composite (ABC) standard scores (Fig. 2d; Table 4). Participants scored significantly below neurotypical mean scores (mean 100, SD 15). As with the BSID-4, younger participants’ ABC standard scores were higher than those of older individuals, again suggesting the developmental gap widens in individuals with NGLY1 Deficiency over time. The individual Vineland-3 subdomains showed the highest GSV scores in fine/gross motor function and expressive and receptive language (Fig. 2e; Table 4). All subdomains were generally stable over time with the exception of coping skills, which showed a moderate increase over time. In general, the Vineland-3 composite score (ABC score) and subdomain scores (GSV) remained stable from baseline to the end of the study.

The Communication Function Classification System (CFCS) is an assessment tool used to classify everyday communication abilities in individuals with cerebral palsy. The CFCS scoring system is a 5-point scale, where a score of 1 signifies effective and independent communication and a score of 5 indicates a participant is ineffective as a sender or receiver of communication, even with familiar partners. Twelve of 15 (80%) participants had CFCS assessed at all 3 timepoints. Participants exhibited significant impairments in everyday communication, as demonstrated by a baseline mean (SD) score of 2.7 (1.28) (Fig. 2g). Four participants had worsening scores over time (higher scores), while one participant demonstrated improvement (lower score) (Fig. 2f). Mean scores remained generally unchanged across the study duration (Table 4), despite changes observed within individuals.

The Gross Motor Function Measure (GMFM-88) measures gross motor skills across 5 dimensions: lying/rolling, sitting, crawling/kneeling, standing, and walking/running/jumping. Scores are represented as a percentage of motor tasks completed, where a neurotypical individual scores 100% through completing tasks successfully. Eleven participants had GMFM-88 results at all three timepoints (partial participation is captured in Table 4). Participants were significantly delayed in all dimensions of the GMFM-88, as demonstrated by a mean (SD) total score of 37.7 (23.51) at baseline (Fig. 3b). Lying/rolling and sitting dimensions had the highest scores, and the lowest scores were observed in the standing and walking/running/jumping dimensions, demonstrating that subjects were less proficient at higher-level skills. Participants demonstrated fluctuations in scores over time; however, these changes were generally inconsistent and were independent of age (Fig. 3a). Cohort mean scores demonstrated substantial and sustained motor function impairment over time.

The Gross Motor Function Classification System, Expanded & Revised (GMFCS, E&R) is a single-score assessment that categorizes participant gross motor function on a 5-point scale in which higher scores indicate more severe limitation in gross motor function and a need for supportive aid. Twelve of 15 (80%) participants were evaluated at all 3 timepoints using the GMFCS, E&R assessment (Fig. 3c). The mean (SD) score for participants was 3.4 (1.12) at baseline and was stable over 12 months, demonstrating consistent impairment of gross motor function over time (Table 4; Fig. 3d). Many participants required supportive aids to assist in activities, such as walking. Two out of 12 participants demonstrated a decrease in score by month 12, and 1 participant showed a single-point fluctuation that returned to baseline by the end of the study (Fig. 3c). Gross motor function was generally consistent and independent of age. GMFCS, E&R cohort means at all timepoints consistently demonstrated impaired gross motor function that remained stable over time.

During the Investigator’s standard physical examination, participants were assessed for their sitting and standing abilities. Most participants were able to sit unaided (Fig. 3e). For the 12 participants that completed all 3 visits, 9/12 (75%) participants could sit unaided at baseline, which increased to 10/12 (83.3%) by month 12 of the study. For standing ability, 6/12 (50%) participants were able to stand unaided at baseline, which increased to 7/12 (58.3%) by month 12 (Fig. 3f). Overall, the sitting and standing assessments in the neurological exam demonstrated variability within the participant cohort, with most participants exhibiting continued deficits in ability throughout the course of the study. Sitting and standing abilities within participants did not correlate with age. Participants’ abilities trended towards positive gain, although this was variable and significantly delayed compared to neurotypical peers, where sitting and standing milestones are usually achieved by 1 year of age.

The clinician and caregivers assessed the severity of participants’ disease using the Clinician Global Impression of Severity (CGI-S) and Caregiver Global Impression of Severity (CaGI-S) assessments, respectively. These scales rate a participant’s disease severity on a 7-point scale, with a score of 1 demonstrating ‘no illness at all,’ and a score of 7 signifying a participant is ‘amongst the most extremely ill.’ Twelve participants were assessed at all 3 study visits (partial participation indicated in Table 4). Caregivers generally rated participants more severely than clinician scores (Fig. 4a and b). The overall mean (SD) CGI-S score was 4.1 (1.2) at baseline and remained generally stable over time with a minor increase over subsequent timepoints, demonstrating the clinician’s average impression of participants’ severity was ‘moderately ill.’ The CaGI-S overall mean (SD) score was 5.1 (1.21) at baseline, with a minor decrease in severity over time (Table 4), rating participants on average as ‘markedly ill.’ Individual participant scores revealed some disagreement between clinician and caregiver assessments (Fig. 4c; Fig. 4d). For example, the clinician noted an increase in overall disease severity from ‘mildly ill’ to ‘markedly ill’ for participant 48 580 over the course of the study, while the caregiver noted a decrease in severity, from ‘severely ill’ to ‘mildly ill.’

Clinicians and caregivers exhibited similar trends in scoring the individual domains. In both the CGI-S and the CaGI-S, the domains that were scored as most severe included gross motor and fine motor, while the domains scored as least severe were sleep and behavior. Although there were changes noted in individual participants over the course of the study, neither the mean overall score nor the mean domain scores revealed meaningful change over the study timeframe.

In addition to the CGI-S and CaGI-S assessments, participants were scored using the Clinician Global Impression of Change (CGI-C) and Caregiver Global Impression of Change (CaGI-C). These assessments investigated the clinician’s and caregivers’ impressions of change for each participant (Fig. 4e) at month 6 (n = 15) and month 12 (CGI-C, n = 11; CaGI-C, n = 12) compared to baseline. The mean (SD) CGI-C score was 3.7 (1.0) at month 6 and 3.1 (0.6) at month 12, indicating the clinician viewed participants in general as ‘minimally improved’ (CGI-C score = 3) to ‘no change’ (CGI-C score = 4) over the duration of the study. Likewise, the mean (SD) CaGI-C score suggested participants were perceived to be ‘minimally improved’ at month 6 (3.5 [0.7]) and showed no further change at month 12 (3.5 [0.7)]).

The Schirmer’s Lacrimation Test is a diagnostic procedure used to measure tear production by placing a sterile filter paper under a participant’s lower eyelid and recording the distance tears travel. Participants’ mean (SD) distance traveled was 19.8 mm (11.3 mm) suggesting most participants had normal levels of tear production (>10 mm). However, baseline results showed that 4/15 (33.3%) participants had hypolacrima, defined as tear wetting of 5–10 mm, and 1/15 (8.3%) participants had alacrima, defined as tear wetting of less than 5 mm (Fig. 5a). Across all timepoints recorded, 8/15 (53.3%) participants had at least one visit measuring close to a 10 mm level, indicating reduced tear production. Results were similar for both eyes in all participants. There was noticeable variation observed across timepoints within individuals, suggesting tear production was variable over time.

Plasma GNA levels were elevated above the upper limit of normal (ULN) (45 ng/ml) in all participants at each study timepoint; however, levels varied significantly between participants and also within individual participants across the course of the study (Fig. 5b). Fifteen participants had GNA values measured at baseline and month 6, and 12 participants had GNA values measured at month 12. The mean (min, max) plasma GNA level across participants was 150 (43.7, 214) ng/ml with a mean Coefficient of Variation (CV%) of 19.2%, signifying large variability among individuals. Most participants had minor changes in GNA concentration over the 12-month period, but 6 individuals had changes greater than 50 ng/ml between timepoints. Plasma GNA concentrations did not appear to correlate with age, as represented by the black regression line (R2 = 0.043).

Nearly all participants were above the ULN for alanine transaminase (ALT) (28 U/l) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (50 U/l) levels at all timepoints (Fig. 5c; Fig. 4d). There were 15 participants at baseline and month 6, and 12 participants at month 12 that had ALT/AST values measured. The mean (SD) ALT value was 75.2 (67.04) U/l at baseline and 77.8 (39.30) U/l at month 12, demonstrating that most participants’ ALT laboratory results were, on average, ~ 2.7-fold the ULN threshold (indicated by the gray shaded region) and that there was no significant change across the course of the study. Likewise, the mean (SD) AST (U/L) level was 72.1 (39.26) at baseline and 72.8 (23.14) at month 12, demonstrating consistent yet elevated AST levels within participants that were, on average, ~ 1.4-fold above the ULN. Additional liver function assessments included alkaline phosphatase (ALP), albumin, total protein, and bilirubin levels, which showed concentration values within the normal range for all participants (Supplementary Fig. 1).

A study protocol amendment added the analysis of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) levels at month 12. As 4 of the 12 participants assessed at month 12 had already completed this visit prior to the amendment, no data were available to report for those subjects. However, the remaining 8/12 (66.7%) participants had ACTH values recorded at month 12 (Fig. 5e). Two of the 8 participants (25.0%) with ACTH values reported at month 12 had high ACTH levels (94.0 and 359.0 pg/ml), while all other participants with recorded values had ACTH levels within the normal range (7.2–63 pg/ml). This impacted the overall mean (SD; min, max) of 86.9 (125.5; 8.0, 359) pg/mL compared to the mean without the outliers, 23.7 (12.1; 8.0, 40.0) pg/ml.

Electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings were performed at the month 6 and month 12 visits. Abnormal EEG findings, most often including epileptiform discharges, were present in all participants 15/15 (100%) for at least one timepoint (data available upon request). Generalized interictal discharges and combined generalized + focal interictal discharges occurred in 6/15 (40.0%) participants each, while focal + multifocal discharges occurred in 3/15 (20.0%) participants. Only 9 participants had sufficient follow-up information as some families elected to not repeat the procedure, or due to participant withdrawal from the study. For the 9 participants who completed two study timepoints, 2 (22.2%) participants demonstrated mild worsening of the interictal epileptiform discharges, 3 (33.3%) had mild improvement, and 4 (44.4%) remained stable over a six-month period.

The sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) and compound muscle action potential (CMAP) testing in this population demonstrated severe pre-existing axonal neuropathy at the time of the baseline exams in all participants, such that further (gradual) worsening was not discernible within the timeframe of the study (data available upon request).

This publication presents the results of a prospective NHS of 15 participants with NGLY1 Deficiency. Disease manifestations reported here confirm and extend previous observations (Table 5; 4–5, 10). In particular, results demonstrate that participants with NGLY1 Deficiency between the ages of 4–13 years old show profound motor and developmental delay relative to age-matched neurotypicals and plateau in their development, with cohort means remaining stable over a 1-year period.

NHS results in concordance with NGLY1 patient literature.

Consistent with previous reports, participants in this cohort display core disease phenotypes, including profound global developmental delay [5], elevated liver transaminases [4], elevated plasma GNA [13], movement disorders [22], hypolacrima [3], and abnormalities in neurological and musculoskeletal systems [8, 23]. Across all assessments of neurocognitive skills and motor function (BSID-4, Vineland-3, CFCS, GMFM-88, and GMFCS, E&R), the NGLY1 Deficiency study participants consistently exhibited abnormally low proficiency and marked deficits in their abilities to perform assigned tasks, with no meaningful improvement detected in cohort means over the time period the participants were monitored, similar to previous reports [4, 5]. In addition, the assessments of developmental progression show participants did not reach new developmental milestones typical for their age, as indicated by an increasing developmental gap compared to age-normative neurotypical peers and by relatively lower DQ scores in older participants. These results are consistent with what was observed in a previous NGLY1 NHS [5]. The delayed development and lack of overall improvement were also captured in clinician and caregiver global impression scales of severity and change (CGI-S/CaGI-S and CGI-C/CaGI-C). Furthermore, these results complement findings from previous patient studies [5, 10] that reported caregivers identifying the most bothersome symptoms of NGLY1 Deficiency as issues with language and communication, followed by gross and fine motor deficits.

Results here identified a few key differences from previous studies (Table 5). Of participants with reported seizures, the age of seizure onset was roughly 3 years earlier than in previous reports [23]. Although the mean age of first symptom was consistent with literature (3.6 months), the mean age of diagnosis was significantly younger in this cohort (2.6 years) compared to the previously published NGLY1 patient registry [6.6 years (10)] and NHS [7.1 years (5)] studies. This may be due to an increased awareness of NGLY1 Deficiency, increased accessibility to genetic testing, and the inclusion of 2 sibling pairs in this cohort that allowed for early diagnosis in the younger siblings. In addition, this study reported instances of sporadic, slight improvement in a participant’s ability to sit and/or stand, which were not captured in the same assessment in a previous NHS [5]. However, these observations did not correlate with age or with improved performance in other assessments. Results from the Schirmer’s Lacrimation Test identified reduced tear production (hypolacrima and alacrima) in participants at lower rates than previously reported [5]. Finally, the rate of scoliosis in this cohort was lower compared to rates identified in previous NGLY1 patient studies [8, 10].

All study participants had detectable levels of the biomarker GNA in plasma above the normative range reported for control groups, consistent with previous reports [13]. However, the GNA values within participants varied widely across timepoints and, unlike biomarkers in other single enzyme deficiencies, elevated GNA levels did not correlate with any clinical measure. In addition, there was no association observed between the level of GNA and age or disease severity of the NGLY1 Deficiency participants.

The assessment scales used to investigate neurocognitive skills and motor function (BSID-4, Vineland-3, CFCS, GMFM-88, GMFCS, E&R) were suitable for this population, demonstrated by their sensitivity and lack of floor effect. The assessments were determined to be appropriate for measuring the specific developmental domains relevant to NGLY1 Deficiency based on their ability to identify subtle fluctuations in participant abilities and differences in domain performance.

This prospective NHS provides critical information to support the development of clinical endpoints for current and future interventional trials for NGLY1 Deficiency, including neurocognitive and motor function assessments, caregiver reported questionnaires, and laboratory tests. Any improvements observed in the developmental and motor scale scores used in this study, such as BSID-4 or GMFM-88, could be used as effective endpoints to assess treatment benefit in interventional studies. Results from these assessments indicated that participants were relatively stable over the 1-year observational period, which was further confirmed by clinician and caregiver assessments. The lack of improvement over time suggests any consistent positive change observed over a similar period of time in an interventional trial would likely be attributable to the intervention.

There were limitations to the study design, including small sample size, length of observation, limited range of participants’ ages, and lack of focus on disease-related movement disorders. The limited age range included in the study, 4–13 years old, is not expected to capture early developmental milestone attainment or early regression in participants younger than 4 years old, or potential disease-related changes that may occur following puberty and into adulthood. Additionally, this study had no evaluation of the movement disorders associated with NGLY1 Deficiency that are a major contributor to disease-related disability [26, 27]. Future studies will hopefully expand upon the findings reported here by addressing these limitations.

The data and results presented in this publication primarily confirm and extend observations from previous studies and report on additional standardized measurements of the clinical symptoms of NGLY1 Deficiency. As the number of participants in this NHS represents roughly 10% of the global NGLY1 Deficiency patient population and many of the findings are supported by previous studies, this cohort is considered representative of the clinical spectrum of NGLY1 Deficiency in children between the ages of 4 and 13. The data provide the basis for outcome assessments in current and future interventional clinical trials and may further serve as a historical comparator for studies in which conventional controls are not feasible.

The reported NGLY1 NHS study was a single-center, longitudinal observational study of the motor and neurocognitive function and clinical status in children with NGLY1 Deficiency conducted by Baylor College of Medicine (http://clinicaltrials.gov, NCT06122766). This research study was sponsored by the Grace Science Foundation (GSF) (www.gracescience.org), a non-profit scientific research and patient advocacy organization dedicated to NGLY1 Deficiency and the NGLY1 family community. Approximately 20 participants were expected to enroll in this study and were recruited through GSF. On-site assessments for participants occurred at three timepoints: visit 1 (baseline), visit 2 (6 months ±4 weeks), and visit 3 (12 months ±4 weeks). During each visit, study procedures included detailed standardized motor function measures, blood samples for laboratory analysis, and clinical measures including growth and neurodevelopmental assessments.

Participants included males and females (4–13 years old) with a confirmed molecular diagnosis of NGLY1 Deficiency via genetic testing or supported by GNA biomarker testing. Subjects and/or parents or legal guardians were required to provide written informed consent and assent (if applicable) and be willing to travel to the study site and follow study procedures, including participating in clinical assessments and providing biological samples.

Any participants with prior or current participation in an interventional study for NGLY1 Deficiency were not eligible to participate in the NHS; however, the plan for future participation in an interventional study did not preclude participants from entering this study. If a participant later enrolled in an interventional study, the participant was withdrawn from the remainder of the study. Participants could withdraw from the study at any time at their own request or their caregiver’s request for any reason.

All participants were scheduled for three on-site visits, with approximately 6 months (± 4 weeks) between each visit. Most assessments began at the baseline visit, except for the CGI-C and CaGI-C questionnaires, which occurred only during the month 6 and month 12 visits, and the ACTH assessment, which was conducted at visit 3 in 8 of the 12 participants. As a result, these assessments have fewer data points.

Participants’ medical histories were provided by a caregiver questionnaire at baseline and reviewed with them by the study team. This included family history, medical history, surgical history, prenatal and birth history, the caregiver’s chief complaint, developmental skill history, visual/eye abnormalities, tear production, hearing loss, feeding tube placement, dysautonomia, respiratory disorders, cardiovascular disorders, liver disorder, gastroenterological disorders, genitourinary/kidney anomalies, musculoskeletal/scoliosis disorders, movement disorders, dermatologic disorders, endocrine disorders, hematologic disorders, psychiatric disorders, infection history, sleep disorders/habits, neuropathy, and seizure history. In this study, the terms ‘disorder,’ ‘abnormality,’ and/or ‘anomaly’ refer to any diagnosis or medically relevant finding that was reported by caregivers in the medical history questionnaire at baseline.

A physical examination was performed at baseline and at every visit by the Investigator. Height, weight, and head circumference were measured as part of this assessment.

Ophthalmological examinations (including the Schirmer’s Lacrimation Test) were performed by the same ophthalmologist at all timepoints. If necessary, participants could receive topical anesthesia for the Schirmer’s Lacrimation Test; if used, it was used consistently for an individual subject for the duration of the study, when possible.

The objectives of this study included (1) understanding the clinical spectrum and progression of NGLY1 Deficiency using standardized fine and gross motor scales and clinical and neurodevelopmental assessments, and (2) identifying potential clinical and laboratory endpoints for use in the interventional clinical trials. No hierarchy of study endpoints (i.e. primary, secondary, or exploratory) was followed for this study.

The following established scales were used to capture the clinical spectrum of NGLY1 Deficiency and to develop potential endpoints for future therapeutic studies: BSID-4, Vineland-3, GMFM-88, GMFCS, E&R, and CFCS. All assessments were administered by a trained developmental pediatrician and were administered by the same rater for an individual subject for the duration of the study, when possible.

The BSID-4 [28] is designed for infants and toddlers from 16 days to 42 months of age but due to the level of impairment in individuals with NGLY1 Deficiency, the scale was utilized independent of the age of the subject; therefore, age-independent normative scores were analyzed. This scale was not used for subjects who were too developmentally advanced for it to be applicable, at the discretion of the rater. The scale is divided into five domains (cognitive, language, motor, social–emotional, and adaptation behavior). Only the cognitive, language (expressive and receptive) and motor (fine and gross) domains were administered in this study. DQ was calculated using the participant’s AE divided by chronological age (CA) multiplied by 100 (DQ = [AE/CA × 100).

The Vineland-3 measures age-appropriate adaptive functioning, specifically communication, daily living skills, socialization, and motor skills and can be administered for individuals from birth to age 90 [29]. The optional maladaptive behavior index was not used in this study. The survey form was administered to the parents or caregivers using a semi-structured interview format.

The GMFM-88 is an assessment tool designed to measure changes in gross motor function over time in children with cerebral palsy; however, this tool was also appropriate for assessing gross motor function changes in NGLY1 Deficiency participants [30]. Assessment items span the spectrum of gross motor activities in five dimensions (lying/rolling; sitting; crawling/kneeling; standing; walking/running/jumping). The items are appropriate for those with motor skills at or below those of a 5-year-old child without any motor disability. The administration could be broken up into sessions of 15 minutes if deemed necessary by the rater, and modules considered not appropriate (e.g. walking/running/jumping) could be omitted. Omitted modules were recorded in the electronic case report form (eCRF). Total scores were still calculated if a participant had a module omitted due to incompletion. The completion time was dependent on the motor level of the subject and was expected to be 45–60 minutes.

The GMFCS, E&R [31] is a 5-level classification system that describes the gross motor function of children and youth based on their self-initiated movement, with particular emphasis on sitting, walking, and wheeled mobility. It was developed for children with cerebral palsy and has been used with other neuromuscular pediatric diseases. Distinctions between levels are based on functional abilities, the need for assistive technology, including hand-held mobility devices (walkers, crutches, or canes) or wheeled mobility, and to a much lesser extent, quality of movement. The GMFCS, E&R is not expected to be performed on subjects aged 5 years or younger.

The CFCS [32] was developed to classify the everyday communication performance of an individual with cerebral palsy into one of five levels, which was repurposed to assess participants with NGLY1 Deficiency. The overall effectiveness of the communication performance is based on how individuals usually take part in everyday situations requiring communication, rather than their best capacity. This classification system has been used in cerebral palsy clinical trials and in other pediatric disorders. The CFCS was not expected to be performed on subjects aged 5 years or younger.

In addition to developmental and motor function assessments, participants’ clinical manifestations and disease severity were further understood using clinician and caregiver reported measures from baseline to the end of the study. The following questionnaires were included in this study to further capture disease severity and potential change over time: CGI-S, CaGI-S, CGI-C, and CaGI-C.

The CGI-S and CaGI-S, adapted for NGLY1 Deficiency, assess severity of the subject’s symptoms of NGLY1 Deficiency as rated by a clinician or caregiver, respectively [33]. The CGI-S and CaGI-S are questionnaires rated on a 7-point [1–7] scale of symptom severity from normal/no impairment (score of 1) to pervasive impairment (score of 7). The CGI-S was administered by the Investigator, and the CaGI-S was completed by the same caregiver when possible.

The CGI-C and CaGI-C, adapted for NGLY1 Deficiency, focus on clinicians’ or caregivers’ observations of change in the subject’s symptoms of NGLY1 Deficiency from initiation of treatment, or in this case, at baseline [33]. Following a baseline assessment that is not scored, the CGI-C is rated at each additional visit as: marked improvement, moderate improvement, minimal improvement, no change, minimal worsening, moderate worsening, or marked worsening. The CGI-C was administered by the Investigator, and the CaGI-C was completed by the same caregiver when possible.

Whenever possible, participants submitted laboratory samples in the morning to ensure a standardized time of collection. In addition to the GNA biomarker, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, and total protein were tested in participants’ samples. ACTH was collected at visit 3, as it was a later addition to the study protocol.

Human plasma samples were analyzed at BioAgilytix Labs (San Diego CA, 92121) using high-performance liquid chromatography (LC–MS) with mass spectrometric (MS/MS) detection to quantify GNA concentration. This method is applicable to detect concentrations ranging from 5.00 ng/ml to 2500 ng/ml.

Human K2EDTA plasma samples containing GNA and GNA-d3 as the internal standard (IS) (Omicron Biochem Inc. Cat. # AAG-003 and AAG-004) were subjected to dilution in acetonitrile solution and precipitated using a Waters Ostro plate (Thermo, Part #278752) per manufacturer’s specifications. The extracts were analyzed by LCMS. The mobile phase was nebulized using heated nitrogen set to electrospray positive ionization mode and the compounds were detected using MS/MS.

Peak areas of GNA and the IS were acquired using MassLynx software. Calibration curves were obtained by fitting the peak area ratios of GNA/IS and the standard concentrations to a linear weighted (1/×2) equation using MassLynx. The equations of the calibration curves were then used to interpolate the concentration of GNA in the samples using their peak area ratios.

EEG recordings were performed in the conscious state without general anesthesia. If sedation was required, the medical monitor was informed prior to the procedure.

Nerve conduction studies (NCS) were allowed to be performed with or without sedation, and general anesthesia was not utilized. Studies were performed longitudinally using the same nerve and limb. Motor nerves recorded during the NCS included median, peroneal, tibial, and ulnar. Sensory nerves recorded during the NCS included sural, radial, median, and ulnar. These were conducted in the participants’ right and left upper extremities, as well as the right and left lower extremities.

Standard descriptive statistics were used to evaluate all demographic and other clinical subject information. Fine and gross motor function data from the domains of the BSID-4 and Vineland-3, GMFM-88 and GMFCS, E&R were compared against normative data. Analyses were performed at the baseline evaluation, as well as the second and third timepoints.

For descriptive statistical summaries, continuous variables are summarized by the number (n) of observations, mean, and standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables are summarized by the number and percentage of participants in each category. Missing data were not to be inputted. No formal statistical hypothesis tests were conducted.

All graphical figures were generated using GraphPad Prism software (Version 10.4.2, Jun 25, 2025). Data presented as graphs represent mean values with error bars indicating SD. Regression analysis was performed on GNA biomarker data to characterize plasma GNA concentration over time.
