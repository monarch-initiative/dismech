ABSTRACT 4 
Background and Purpose:  Sotos syndrome is a rare autosomal dominant condition, caused by 5 
pathogenic mutations in the NSD1 gene, that presents with craniofacial dysmorphism, overgrowth, 6 
seizures, and neurodevelopmental delay. Macrocephaly, ventriculomegaly, and corpus callosal 7 
dysmorphism are typical neuroimaging features that have been described in the medical literature. The 8 
purpose of this study is to expand upon the neuroimaging phenotype by detailed analysis of a large 9 
cohort of genetically proven Sotos syndrome patients.  10 
Materials and Methods: This multicenter, multinational retrospective observational cohort study 11 
systematically analyzed the clinical characteristics and neuroimaging features of 77 individuals with 12 
genetically diagnosed Sotos syndrome, via central consensus review with three pediatric 13 
neuroradiologists. 14 
Results: In addition to previously described features, malformations of cortical development were 15 
identified in most patients (95.0%), typically dysgyria (92.2%) and polymicrogyria (22.1%), varying in 16 
location and distribution . Incomplete rotation of the hippocampus was observed in 50.6% of patients 17 
and was associated with other imaging findings, in particular with dysgyria (100% vs. 84.2%, p=0.012). 18 
Conclusion: Our findings show a link between the genetic-biochemical basis and the neuroimaging 19 
features and aid in better understanding the underlying clinical manifestations and possible treatment 20 
options. These findings have yet to be described to this extent and correspond with recent studies that 21 
show NSD1 takes part in brain development and has interactions with other known relevant genetic 22 
pathways. 23

INTRODUCTION 45 
Sotos syndrome is a rare autosomal dominant condition with an estimated prevalence of 1/14,000 46 
live births 1, first described in 1964 2. The genetic basis of Sotos syndrome is caused by 47 
haploinsufficiency of the NSD1 (Nuclear Receptor Binding SET Domain Protein 1) gene, either through 48 
deletions or intragenic pathogenic variants3,4. The NSD1 protein is a methyltransferase that is known to 49 
play an important role in embryonic development5. 50 

 
 
3  
The main clinical features include craniofacial dysmorphism, overgrowth (macrocephaly, 51 
advanced bone age, and tall stature), and developmental delay / intellectual disability6. Other features 52 
may also occur with varying frequency, including but not limited to, scoliosis, conductive hearing loss, 53 
and cardiac abnormalities 1. Seizures have been reported in up to 25% of individuals with Sotos 54 
syndrome7. Seizure semiology is diverse with febrile seizures, infantile spasms, absence, tonic –clonic, 55 
and myoclonic seizures8. Few cases were reported to have drug-resistant intractable epilepsy9. 56 
Neuroimaging findings in individuals with Sotos syndrome typically include macrocephaly, 57 
ventriculomegaly, dysgenesis of the corpus callosum 9, enlarged CSF spaces, prominent perivascular 58 
spaces, and abnormalities of the septum pellucidum 10. Despite the high prevalence of seizures, 59 
malformations of cortical development have been scarcely described but include heterotopias 11, 60 
polymicrogyria12, and cortical dysplasias13. Here, we aim to expand upon the neuroimaging phenotype 61 
by detailed analysis of a large cohort of genetically confirmed Sotos syndrome patients. 62 
MATERIALS AND METHODS 63 
Study design: 64 
This multicenter multinational retrospective observational study aimed to perform deep 65 
phenotyping of neuroimaging findings in a large cohort of children with genetically confirmed Sotos 66 
syndrome. Site-specific institutional review board approval was obtained from all collaborating 67 
institutions prior to commencement, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Data are reported 68 
in line with the STROBE statement (Supplementary Materials). 69 
Inclusion criteria: 70 
• Genetically confirmed diagnosis of Sotos syndrome (i.e., likely pathogenic or pathogenic 71 
variants in NSD1). 72 
• Sufficient clinical data available for interrogation. 73 
• Good quality, multisequence, multiplanar magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain, 74 
obtained by 1.5T or 3T scanners, with at least three sequences: 75 

 
 
4 
o Sagittal T1WI 76 
o Axial T2WI or T1WI 77 
o Coronal T1WI or T2WI 78 
Exclusion criteria: 79 
Individuals with pathogenic co-variants in any other gene. 80 
Clinical data 81 
The electronic health record was reviewed retrospectively by the referring clinician from each 82 
center and interrogated for demographic, clinical, and genetic information. Growth was assessed 83 
according to modified World Health Organization growth charts; overgrowth was defined as 2 standard 84 
deviations above the mean. Data regarding developmental delay (defined in children aged <5 years) and 85 
intellectual disability (in children aged ≥ 5 years) were also collected. The seizure type was classified in 86 
accordance with the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) 2017 criteria. 87 
Imaging Review 88 
Brain MRI studies of each patient were reviewed by a central panel consisting of a pediatric 89 
neuroradiologist from the patient’s center and three other authors (K.M., S.S. , and A.B.), all expert 90 
pediatric neuroradiologists (15, 20, and 10 years of experience, respectively). In case of disagreement 91 
between the radiologists, a joint review was sought to achieve a consensus. In patients with additional 92 
sequences or several imaging studies - all were reviewed, including follow-up MRI scans. The reviewing 93 
panel inspected the entire scan with particular attention to the cortex, brainstem, cerebellum, ventricular 94 
system morphology, and external dysmorphic features.  95 
Malformations of cortical development, if present, were defined according to the latest published 96 
consensus classification 14,15, and the malformation subtype, distribution , and location were noted for 97 
each case. Dysgyria was noted when the cortex appeared to have variable thickness with a smooth gray-98 
white transition and exhibited an abnormal sulcation pattern including sulcal depth irregularities, 99 
abnormal sulcal orientation, or excessive gyration (polygyria) not fulfilling the criteria of 100 

 
 
5  
polymicrogyria. Hippocampus structure was noted as abnormal (incomplete rotation) if it presented with 101 
a round or pyramidal shape or with a deep collateral sulcus16. 102 
Statistical Analysis 103 
Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Age distribution was 104 
evaluated using a histogram. Since age distribution was skewed, it was reported as median and 105 
interquartile range. The association between categorical variables was evaluated using the Chi-square 106 
test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All statistical tests were two-sided and p<0.05 was considered 107 
statistically significant. SPSS software was used for statistical analyses (IBM SPSS statistics, version 108 
29.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA, 2023). 109 
RESULTS 110 
Seventy-seven patients met the inclusion criteria, of which 48.1% were female, with a total of 111 
114 brain MRIs. Age at the time of the first imaging study varied from 6 days to 16 years, with a median 112 
of 1.75 years (interquartile range 4.9 - 0.8 years). One patient had a prenatal MRI scan which was 113 
reviewed as well as the postnatal scan. 114 
All patients had a genetic diagnosis of Sotos syndrome, with a likely pathogenic or pathogenic variant 115 
in NSD1. Fifty-two patients had a full genetic report available, detailing 26.9% whole gene deletions, 116 
25% missense, 26.9% frameshift (indel), 11.5% nonsense, and 7.7% splice site variants.  117 
Clinical presentation 118 
Overgrowth was a core clinical feature in most patients, consisting of tall stature and weight gain 119 
in 59.7% and macrocephaly in 67.5% (Table 1). Developmenta l delay was noted in 88.3% of patients, 120 
and 57.1% of the patients were later diagnosed with intellectual disability of varying severity. A 121 
relatively high prevalence of autism spectrum disorder and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder was 122 
found in our cohort, with nine (11.7%) and six (7.8%) patients respectively.  123 
Seizures were reported in 24 patients (31.2%), nine of which (11.7%) had an abnormal 124 
electroencephalogram (EEG), with a variance in seizure presentation and response to medication. 125 
Thirteen patients had available information about the seizure subtype, of whom eight (61.5%) had 126 

 
 
6 
generalized seizures: six tonic-clonic, one myoclonic, and one combined myoclonic-tonic-clonic. Three 127 
patients (23.1%) had focal seizures and two (15.4%) had febrile spasms.  128 
Neurological findings including hypotonia, nystagmus, and increased intracranial pressure were 129 
present in 71.4%. Ophthalmological findings were present in 37.6%. Twenty-seven patients (35.1%) 130 
presented with idiopathic scoliosis (thoracic, lumbar, or combined) and were either observed only or 131 
treated with a back brace. Six patients (7.8%) presented with craniosynostosis. One patient was deceased 132 
at the time of writing secondary to complications of a pulmonary disease at the age of two. 133 
Neoplasia 134 
Four patients (5.2%) presented with neoplasms, either malignant or benign. One patient 135 
presented with a tectal glioma and secondary obstructive hydrocephalus treated with an endoscopic third 136 
ventriculostomy. A second patient presented with an in-utero adrenal mass, later diagnosed as a 137 
neuroblastoma in the perinatal period which resolved spontaneously over the observation period. A third 138 
patient presented with a sacrococcygeal teratoma. The fourth patient presented with retinal 139 
melanocytoma, diagnosed at one year of age, showing spontaneous involution.  140 
Imaging findings 141 
Malformations of cortical development were noted in 73 patients (94.8%) (Table 2). Most 142 
patients (71; 92.2%), exhibited dysgyria, which was typically bilateral and had a predominant frontal 143 
involvement in 59/71 (83.1%) and perisylvian involvement in 33/71 (45.2%). The main features of the 144 
dysgyria were shallow sulci (42.3%) and polygyria (50.7%). Seventeen patients (22.1%) showed 145 
polymicrogyria, mostly bilateral, of which 12/17 (70.6%) were perisylvian. (Figure 1). Most patients 146 
with polymicrogyria were noted to have dysgyria as well, with no significant statistical correlation 147 
(94.1% vs 91.7%, p>0.999). 148 
Incomplete rotation of the hippocampus was observed in 39 cases (50.6%) and was associated 149 
with other imaging findings (Table 3). Dysgyria was more common in patients with incomplete rotation 150 
of the hippocampus (100% vs. 84.2%, p=0.012) as well as a thin corpus callosum (79.5% vs 50%, 151 
p=0.007) and white matter signal change (25.6% vs. 2.6%, p=0.004) (Table 3). The interthalamic 152 

 
 
7  
adhesion (massa-intermedia) was missing in 23.4% and the anterior commissure appeared thin in 49.4%. 153 
(Figure 2) 154 
Structural anomalies were noted in most of the patients including a macrocephalic skull (79.2%), 155 
ventriculomegaly (87.0%), corpus callosum abnormality (68.8%), ventricular dysmorphism (63.6%) 156 
and hypertelorism (58.4%). Less common were prominent perivascular spaces (39.0 %), abnormalities 157 
of the septum pellucidum (33.8%), and multifocal patchy white matter signal changes (14.3%). In 158 
23.4%, the brainstem was slightly dysmorphic, typically exhibiting a shallow pontomedullary sulcus, 159 
accompanied by cerebellar tonsillar ectopia in 18.2%.  160 
No statistically significant correlations were found between imaging findings and clinical 161 
presentation. In particular, no association was found between seizure incidence and malformations of 162 
cortical development. Cortical malformations were seen in 91.7% of patients with seizures and in 96.2% 163 
of patients without seizures (p=0.59), similarly for dysgyria (87.5% vs. 94.3%, p=0.37). 164 
A single patient had a prior fetal MRI, at 32 weeks of gestation, due to macrocephaly on prenatal 165 
screening ultrasound and slightly elevated -human Chorionic Gonadotropin (-hCG). The scan showed 166 
biometrics consistent with macrocephaly as well as similar features of dilated and mildly dysmorphic 167 
ventricles, enlarged CSF spaces, hypertelorism, and incomplete rotation of the hippocampi. The cortical 168 
folding was appropriate for gestational age with the exception of some asymmetry. This patient had a 169 
follow-up MRI at the age of one year old and exhibited mild ventriculomegaly, thin corpus callosum, 170 
and mild dysgyria. (Figure 3). 171 
DISCUSSION 172 
Imaging Findings 173 
Arguably the most intriguing finding in this study is the 95% prevalence of cortical development 174 
malformations in patients with Sotos syndrome. This high prevalence has not been described in previous 175 
studies, most published before the term dysgyria was introduced. This may link the role of the NSD1 176 
gene in brain development with the appearance on MRI. 177 

 
 
8 
Several previous studies have investigated brain development in Sotos syndrome, with only a 178 
few cases describing gray matter heterotopia 11 and a single case report of polymicrogyria 12. There has 179 
been one additional report of an individual with polymicrogyria and an unbalanced translocation 180 
including a deletion encompassing the NSD1 gene10. The contribution of one or more of the numerous 181 
other deleted or duplicated genes to the imaging phenotype of this individual cannot be excluded, 182 
although our current findings are in support of the NSD1 deletion as a possible cause for polymicrogyria 183 
in that patient.  184 
Incomplete hippocampal rotation can be a normal variant, especially when it occurs unilaterally 185 
on the left, or alternatively, can be a developmental abnormality 17. In our cohort, incomplete 186 
hippocampal rotation tended to be bilateral and more common than in normal children (50.6% of cases), 187 
suggesting that it may be secondary to regional temporal dysgyria from abnormal cortical folding. 188 
Normal hippocampal folding is theorized to be secondary to adjacent cortical development, thus 189 
potentially explaining concurrent cortical malformations 18. A statistically significant association was 190 
found between incomplete hippocampal rotation and dysgyria, suggesting that it may represent imaging 191 
stigmata of Sotos syndrome. 192 
Whitehead et al. 19 showed abnormalities involving the massa intermedia and anterior 193 
commissure to be more common in patients with midline abnormalities than in those without. We found, 194 
in concordance, massa intermedia absence in 23.4% and a thin anterior commissure in 49.4% of Sotos 195 
syndrome patients. These commonly affected midline structures should be evaluated as part of routine 196 
MRI reporting in Sotos syndrome patients. 197 
Slight dysmorphism of the brainstem, mainly a shallow pontomedullary sulcus, was observed in 198 
23.4% of patients. It is most likely a feature secondary to the described cranial abnormalities; hence no 199 
true malformation was observed, but possibly represent another aspect of brain maldevelopment. 200 
Other imaging findings corroborate previous reports, with macrocephaly, ventriculomegaly, and midline 201 
abnormalities observed in most of the MRI scans inspected and in all age groups. 202 
Fetal Imaging 203 

 
 
9  
Published data regarding the prenatal presentation and imaging features of Sotos syndrome are 204 
sparse. Zhang et al. 20 described a case series of seven patients, with subtle nonspecific sonographic 205 
findings such as ventriculomegaly. Lu et al. 21 published a case with a fetal MRI showing 206 
ventriculomegaly and polyhydramnios. 207 
The findings on one fetal MRI included in our study are similar to those found postnatally for 208 
this child (Figures 2 and 3). However, the follow-up postnatal MRI revealed maturation of dysgyria, 209 
which was merely hinted at in the fetal MRI as asymmetry only. Together, cortical gyral abnormalities 210 
or asymmetries, abnormal hippocampal rotation, ventriculomegaly, and enlargement of the cavum 211 
septum pellucidum should raise the possibility of Sotos syndrome and the possible need for further 212 
genetic testing. 213 
Neoplasia 214 
Sotos syndrome, like other overgrowth syndromes, is known to entail an increased risk of 215 
malignancies22. The prevalence of 5.2% in our cohort supports the evidence of elevated neoplasm risk 216 
these patients have with a variance in tumor type and location. 217 
Clinical Findings 218 
Clinical findings in our cohort are consistent with the previous literature, with core features of 219 
overgrowth, psychocognitive impairment, seizures, scoliosis, and other neurological or ophthalmologic 220 
abnormalities. The high prevalence of developmental delay / intellectual disability, autism spectrum 221 
disorder, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder have been described in previous studies 23,24. The 222 
somewhat lower prevalence of autism spectrum disorder and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder may 223 
be explained by the young age in our cohort, patients not yet evaluated for such conditions. This 224 
understanding could enable early assessment and proper educational adjustments. 225 
Genetic and Biochemical Literature Review 226 
Several findings support the important role of the NSD1 gene in brain development and provide 227 
possible correlates for the observed malformations of cortical development in our cohort. Human 228 
expression data (Human Protein Atlas, GTEx) demonstrate that NSD1 is expressed in the brain and in 229 

 
 
10 
the cerebral cortex. It binds upstream of the promoter regions thereby regulating transcription through 230 
interactions with methylated lysine 36 of histone H3 (H3K36) and RNA polymerase II 25. Oishi et al 26 231 
Have shown that there is a predominantly neuronal expression of NSD1 in the cerebral cortex of mice. 232 
Complete knock-out of NSD1 impaired migration and laminar positioning of cortical neurons 27 and 233 
caused high apoptosis and inability to complete gastrulation, ultimately resulting in lethality5. 234 
A key downstream effector of NSD1 is the APC2 gene. The APC2 gene has a crucial role in 235 
neuronal migration 28, and bi-allelic pathogenic variants in APC2 are known to lead to a 236 
neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by brain malformations, including cortical dysplasia and 237 
lissencephaly among others29. Interestingly, Almuriekhi et al.27 and Mastrangelo et al.30 have described 238 
three individuals from two families with bi-allelic pathogenic variants in the APC2 gene presenting with 239 
features resembling Sotos syndrome, including macrocephaly. Additionally, APC2 is a regulator of the 240 
Wnt signaling pathway, and there is substantial evidence of a connection between NSD1 and the Wnt 241 
signaling pathway31, which could possibly contribute to the increased incidence of neoplasms in Sotos 242 
syndrome. 243 
The findings of Quintero-Rivera et al. 32 suggest that NSD1 overexpression influences the 244 
PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. As the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway has a well-established role in 245 
cell growth, they propose that the undergrowth seen in NSD1 overexpression is related to the effect it 246 
has on this pathway. It would therefore be feasible that NSD1 loss of function may likewise cause 247 
overgrowth through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Pathogenic variants in genes of the 248 
PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway are a significant cause of megalencephaly33, once again linking NSD1 with 249 
malformations of cortical development. 250 
Implications 251 
The unexpected high prevalence of cortical malformations in our study carries important 252 
clinical-practical implications. When classical Sotos syndrome-related neurological signs and symptoms 253 
are present, brain MR imaging should be performed and scrutinized for typical midline abnormalities 254 
and cortical malformations which may be subtle. The cortical malformations in Sotos syndrome often 255 

 
 
11  
manifest as dysgyria or nonspecific cortical asymmetries on fetal MRI, and therefore may be easily 256 
overlooked, especially without optimal MR technique or when there is motion artifact. 257 
Limitations 258 
Variability in imaging was an unavoidable consequence in this multicenter, retrospective study 259 
that included examinations acquired with various MRI hardware, magnet field strengths, and imaging 260 
protocols. Our inclusion criteria aimed to eliminate or at least diminish the effect of these differences. 261 
The clinical data were available in the majority of patients and did not require patient re-examination. 262 
The multi-center structure does pose a strength in the reduction of selection bias. Furthermore, the wide 263 
consensus with an expert from each center reduces observer bias. 264 
Brain malformation classification and terminology have had a great deal of revisions over the 265 
years, which may explain the previous underdiagnosis and underreporting. Dysgyria, a rather recent 266 
term, was introduced in association with the Tubulin gene family pathogenic variants and has since been 267 
identified in other conditions. A qualitative assessment of dysgyria rather than a measurement-based 268 
grading, may present a limitation of our study, and further studies should focus on such measurements 269 
of the cortical structure as well as better classifying dysgyria.  270 
The assessment of the anterior commissure was only possible in patients with a high-resolution 271 
sagittal T1WI. Due to the differences in imaging protocols, we did not perform a measurement and used 272 
a binary qualitative criterion based on visual inspection. Further research regarding dysgyria subtypes, 273 
quantitative brain measurements, as well as genetic, histologic, and clinical correlations will be 274 
necessary for a better, repeatable, and precise classification. 275 
CONCLUSIONS 276 
Malformations of cortical development, anterior commissure hypoplasia, thalamic massa 277 
intermedia absence, and incomplete hippocampal rotation are common and underrecognized imaging 278 
features in Sotos syndrome, in addition to the classically described macrocephaly, ventriculomegaly, 279 
and callosal dysmorphology. These findings support the presumed role of NSD1 in brain development. 280
