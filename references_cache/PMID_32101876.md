High-Phosphate Diet Improved the Skeletal Development of Fam20c-Deficient Mice.

Zhang H(1), Li L(2), Kesterke MJ(2), Lu Y(2), Qin C(2).

Author information:
(1)Department of Biomedical Sciences, Texas A&M University College of Dentistry,

(2)Department of Biomedical Sciences, Texas A&M University College of Dentistry,

FAM20C (family with sequence similarity 20 - member C) is a protein kinase that 
phosphorylates secretory proteins, including the proteins that are essential to 
the formation and mineralization of calcified tissues. Previously, we reported 
that inactivation of Fam20c in mice led to hypophosphatemic rickets/osteomalacia 
along with increased circulating fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) levels and 
dental defects. In this study, we examined whether a high-phosphate (hPi) diet 
could rescue the skeletal defects in Fam20c-deficient mice. Fam20c conditional 
knockout (cKO) mice were generated by crossing female Fam20c-floxed mice 
(Fam20cfl/fl) with male Sox2-Cre;Fam20cfl/+ mice. The pregnant female 
Fam20cfi/fl mice were fed either a normal or hPi diet until the litters were 
weaned. The cKO and control offspring were continuously given a normal or hPi 
diet for 4 weeks after weaning. Plain X-ray radiography, micro-CT, histology, 
immunohistochemistry (FGF23, DMP1, OPN, and SOX9), and in situ hybridization 
(type II and type X collagen) analyses were performed to evaluate the effects of 
an hPi diet on the mouse skeleton. Plain X-ray radiography and micro-CT 
radiography analyses showed that the hPi diet improved the shape and mineral 
density of the Fam20c-deficient femurs/tibiae, and rescued the growth plate 
defects in the long bone. Histology analyses further demonstrated that an hPi 
diet nearly completely rescued the growth plate-widening defects in the long 
bone and restored the expanded hypertrophic zone to nearly normal width. These 
results suggested that the hPi diet significantly improved the skeletal 
development of the Fam20c-deficient mice, implying that hypophosphatemia 
partially contributed to the skeletal defects in Fam20c-deficient subjects.

Family with sequence similarity 20 – member C (FAM20C) is a protein kinase that catalyzes the attachment of phosphates to serine residues within the Ser-x-Glu/pSer motifs of proteins that are transported through the Golgi apparatus [Ishikawa et al., 2012; Tagliabracci et al., 2012; Cui et al., 2015; Tagliabracci et al., 2015], Fam20c is widely expressed, with high levels in the mineralized tissue-forming cells, including osteoblasts in bone and ameloblasts and odontoblasts in tooth [Nalbant et al., 2005; Hao et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2010]. FAM20C phosphorylates a very broad spectrum of secretory proteins, including mammalian milk casein and a family of secretory calcium-binding phosphoproteins (SCPPs) that regulate biomineralization [Ishikawa et al., 2012; Tagliabracci et al., 2012; Cui et al., 2015]. The SCPP family include members of the small integrin-binding ligand, N-linked glycoprotein (SIBLING) family that play key roles in the development of the mineralized tissues including bone and dentin [Fisher et al., 2001; Qin et al., 2004; Tagliabracci et al., 2012; Tagliabracci et al., 2015]. It also include the enamel matrix proteins such as amelogenin, enamelin, ameloblastin and amelotin (AMTN), that are essential for enamel formation [Kawasaki and Weiss, 2003; Kawasaki, 2011].

Mutations in FAM20C in humans may cause lethal or non-lethal form of Raine syndrome (OMIM #259775), which are inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. The lethal form is manifested as osteosclerotic bone dysplasia, and the affected patients usually die shortly after birth [Raine et al., 1989; al-Mane et al., 1998; Simpson et al., 2007; Faundes et al., 2014; Whyte et al., 2017]. In contrast, the patients with non-lethal Raine syndrome may survive into childhood/adulthood [Simpson et al., 2009; Fradin et al., 2011; Rafaelsen et al., 2013; Takeyari et al., 2014; Acevedo et al., 2015; Elalaoui et al., 2016; Rolvien et al., 2018; Sheth et al., 2018]; these non-lethal cases may exhibit skeletal and dental abnormalities as well as hypophosphatemia and increased serum fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) levels [Simpson et al., 2009; Rafaelsen et al., 2013; Takeyari et al., 2014; Acevedo et al., 2015; Elalaoui et al., 2016; Rolvien et al., 2018]. Global deletion of Fam20c in mice results in hypophosphatemic rickets/osteomalacia and dental defects, accompanied by hypophosphatemia and elevation of FGF23 level in the circulation [Vogel et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2015], reminiscent of the non-lethal form of Raine syndrome. Hypophosphatemia in human non-lethal Raine syndrome patients and in Fam20c-deficient mice apparently results from the elevation of FGF23 level in the circulation, as FGF23 is a potent phosphaturic hormone that promotes phosphorus excretion in the kidney [Martin et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2012; Rafaelsen et al., 2013; Tagliabracci et al., 2014; Takeyari et al., 2014; Bon et al., 2018; Rolvien et al., 2018]. However, it has not been determined whether hypophosphatemia directly contributes to the skeletal defects of the Fam20c-deficient subjects. Previous human and mouse genetic studies have substantiated the pathogenic role of hypophosphatemia in the hypophosphatemic rickets associated with mutations in several other genes. The inactivating mutations in the phosphate-regulating gene with homologies to endopeptidases on the X chromosome (PHEX) gene lead to an elevation of serum FGF23 levels and cause X-linked hypophosphatemia (XLH), which accounts for most human cases of hypophosphatemic rickets [The HYP Consortium, 1995; Bitzan and Goodyer, 2019]. Dietary phosphorus supplement has been the main treatment for the XLH patients [Bacchetta et al., 2019; Bitzan and Goodyer, 2019]. In April 2018, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved CRYSVITA® (burosumab-twza), a neutralizing anti-FGF23 monoclonal antibody for the treatment of XLH in adult and pediatric patients of 1 year of age and older. The murine models for human XLH are the Hyp mice, in which hypophosphatemic rickets is caused by spontaneous mutations in the mouse Phex gene [Eicher et al., 1976; The HYP Consortium, 1995]. Studies have shown that high phosphate (3%) diet could improve the skeletal development of Hyp mice [Eicher et al., 1976; Hayashibara et al., 2007].

The activating missense mutations causing the substitutions of arginine residues at position 176 or 179 in human FGF23 lead to autosomal dominant hypophosphatemia (ADH); such mutations prevent this protein from being cleaved by subtilisin-like proprotein convertase, which leads to a higher level of full-length FGF23 and a lower level of phosphorus in the blood [ADHR Consortium, 2000; White et al., 2001; Shimada et al., 2002; Bai et al., 2003; Gonciulea and Jan De Beur, 2017]. Treatment of human ADH includes phosphorus supplements and iron therapy if iron deficiency is present [Christov and Juppner, 2018; Kommalapati et al., 2018; Bitzan and Goodyer, 2019]. While mouse models mimicking human ADH [Farrow et al., 2011] or transgenic mice overexpressing FGF23 [Larsson et al., 2004; Shimada et al., 2004] have been created to study the effects of direct elevation of FGF23, there has been no report regarding the application of high phosphate diet to these Fgf23-mutant animals.

Inactivating mutations in dentin matrix protein 1 (DMP1) and ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase/phosphodiesterase 1 (ENPP1) cause autosomal recessive hypophosphatemic rickets (ARHR) [Feng et al., 2006; Lorenz-Depiereux et al., 2006; Levy-Litan et al., 2010; Lorenz-Depiereux et al., 2010; Christov and Juppner, 2018]. The mouse models associated with the null mutations of these two genes closely mimic the manifestations of human ARHR [Feng et al., 2006; Mackenzie et al., 2012]. The dietary supplement consisting of 2% phosphorus has been used to treat the Dmp1-null mice, which manifest hypophosphatemic rickets; this high phosphate diet partially rescued the rachitic defects in the Dmp-null mice [Feng et al., 2006]. Therapeutic effects of anti-FGF23 antibodies has also been reported in these Dmp1-null mice [Zhang et al., 2011], but the application of burosumab to the ARHR patients is yet to be explored.

In this study, we created Fam20c conditional knockout (cKO) mice by crossing female Fam20c-floxed mice (Fam20cf/f) with male Sox2-Cre;Fam20cfl/+ mice, so that Fam20c was inactivated in nearly all the tissues and cells in the cKO mice. We treated the cKO mice with high phosphate diet to examine to what extent the skeletal defects of the Fam20c-deficient mice may be attributed to hypophosphatemia. Our data showed that the high phosphate diet partially rescued the skeletal defects of the cKO mice.

Sox2-Cre;Fam20cfl/fl conditional knockout (cKO) mice were generated and genotyped as we described previously [Wang et al., 2012]. Briefly, we first crossbred the Fam20cfl/fl mice [Wang et al., 2012] with Sox2-Cre transgenic mice (B6.Cg-Edil3Tg(Sox2-cre)1Amc/J, Jackson Lab) to create Sox2-Cre;Fam20cfl/+ mice. The Sox2-Cre;Fam20cfl/+ male mice were bred with female Fam20cfl/fl mice to generate Sox2-Cre;Fam20cfl/fl mice, which were referred to as “conditional knockout (cKO) mice” in this report. The Sox2-Cre transgene is active in epiblasts at embryonic day 6.5 [Hayashi et al., 2002]; therefore, Fam20c is inactivated in nearly all the tissues and cells in the cKO mice. The age and sex-matched Fam20cfl/+ or Fam20cfl/fl mice were used as normal controls; our previous studies have confirmed that these mice have normal skeleton and teeth [Wang et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2013].

To examine the effects of high phosphate (hPi) diet on the skeletal development of cKO mice in comparison with the normal controls, we treated the mice with hPi diet, starting at embryonic day 14.5 (i.e., given to the pregnant mothers). The breeding pairs of Sox2-Cre;Fam20cfl/+ male and Fam20cfl/fl female mice were randomly divided into Group 1 and Group 2; the mice in Group 1 fed a standard rodent chow (0.7% phosphorus, Harlan Teklad) while the Fam20cfl/fl female mice in Group 2 fed a high phosphate rodent chow (1.5% phosphorus, TD. 03625, Harlan Teklad, Madison WI). All mice had free access to the diets and water. Fam20cfl/+ or Fam20cfl/fl mice from Group 1 (receiving standard diet) or Group 2 (receiving hPi diet) were used as the normal controls. The control and cKO offspring from each group were weaned at postnatal 3 weeks. After weaning, the mice in Group 1 were continuously given standard diet while those in Group 2 continued to receive hPi diet. Mice were sacrificed for serum biochemical analyses or microcomputed tomography and histology analyses.

The long bones dissected from the mice of all groups were first imaged with a Faxitron MX-20 Specimen Radiography System with a digital camera attached (Faxitron X-ray Corp., Buffalo Grove, IL). The femurs from 7-week-old mice fed with standard diet (Group 1) or with HPi diet (Group 2) were further scanned using a Scanco μCT35 imaging system (μCT35, Scanco Medical, Bassersdorfm Switzerland) with a slice increment of 7 μm at medium resolution to assess the overall shape and structure. The three-dimensional (3-D) structure was constructed at the threshold of 150. Following the assessment of the overall shape and structure, a high-resolution scan in 3.5 μm slice increments was performed to examine the cortical bone of the midshaft (central region of the diaphysis). The analyses of morphometric parameters were performed using the built-in software of the μCT system. The cortical bone volume fraction (bone volume/total volume, expressed as percentage) and material density were obtained from 231 slices at the mid-shaft of the femurs at a threshold of 283. The data acquired from the high-resolution scans of four samples per group (n = 4) were used for the quantitative analyses.

After the μCT analyses were performed, the undecalcified femurs were dehydrated through a series of graded ethanol (70% to 100%), embedded in methylmethacrylate (MMA), sectioned at 5-μm, and stained with Goldner’s trichrome. For the cortical bone, unmineralized osteoid is stained red, and mineralized bone is stained green/blue. The osteoid area and mineralized area of the cortical bones in the femurs were quantified, respectively using Image J software (National Institutes of Health). The ratio of osteoid area to total bone area was calculated for each sample. Four independent mice were analyzed for each group of mice.

For histologic analysis, the bone samples were fixed in freshly prepared 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4) at 4°C overnight, then decalcified in 15% ethylenediaminetetraacetate (EDTA) solution (pH 7.4) at 4°C for 5~14 days depending on the ages of the animals. The samples were embedded in paraffin using standard histological procedures. The tissue blocks were cut into 5-μm thick serial sections and were then used for Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, Safranin O staining, toluidine blue staining or immunohistochemistry (IHC). IHC was carried out to detect the relative amount and/or distribution of proteins of interest, as previously described [Zhang et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2017]. The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit monoclonal anti SOX9 antibody (Abcam, ab 185230) at a dilution of 1:400; rabbit polyclonal anti-DMP1 antibody (857-3) [Sun et al., 2010] at a dilution of 1:800; rabbit polyclonal anti-biglycan antibody (LF-159, a gift from Dr. Larry Fisher at the Craniofacial and Skeletal Disease Branch, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) at a dilution of 1:1000; and rabbit polyclonal anti-OPN antibody [McFarland et al., 1995] at a dilution of 1: 800. The same concentrations of normal rabbit IgG were used to replace the rabbit primary antibodies serving as negative controls. A monoclonal antibody against FGF23 (anti-FGF23-79) [Jani et al., 2016] was used to detect this protein in the mouse long bone. The same concentration of mouse IgG was used to replace the anti-FGF 23 antibody, acting as a negative control for this mouse monoclonal antibody. The immunostaining signals (in brown) were visualized using the DAB (3,3’-diaminobenzidine) kit (Vector Laboratories), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sections were counterstained with methyl green (Sigma, Saint Louis, MO).

In situ hybridization was carried out to assess the expression of type II collagen (Col II) and type X collagen (Col X) in 7-week-old long bone samples as described previously [Zhang et al., 2016]. Brifly, the RNA probes were labeled with digoxigenin (DIG) using a RNA Labeling Kit (Roche, Indianapolis, IN) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. DIG-labeled RNA probes were detected by an enzyme-linked immunoassay with a specific anti-DIG-alkaline phosphatase antibody conjugate (Roche, Indianapolis, IN) and a blue alkaline phosphatase chromogen substrate BCIP/NBT (5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl phosphate/nitroblue tetrazolium) (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA), which produced a blue color for positive signals. The sections were counterstained with nuclear fast red.

Serum phosphorus was measured using a phosphorus Lipui-UV test kit (Stanbio Laboratory). Serum calcium was measured using a colorimetric calcium kit (Stanbio Laboratory).

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25 software and were expressed as mean ± SD (standard deviation). Multiple-group comparison was performed with a one-way ANOVA. If significant differences were found with the one-way ANOVA, LSD post-hoc test was used to determine the differences between two individual groups. P < 0.05 was considered as the statistical significant difference.

We first performed X-ray radiography analyses to assess the long bone structure and morphology. There were no significant differences in the skeletons between the normal control (Fam20cfl/+ or Fam20cfl/fl, denoted as “Norm” in the figures) mice receiving standard diet and those receiving high phosphate diet, which is consistent with previous reports that high phosphate diet does not affect the development of normal mice at a significant level [Maginot et al., 2015]. In this report, the data obtained from the Norm (normal control) mice receiving high phosphate diet (Norm+Pi) were included for the comparative analyses versus cKO mice that fed standard diet or high phosphate diet.

Plain X-ray radiography analyses (Fig. 1) revealed that at postnatal 7 weeks, the cortexes in the femurs and tibiae of the cKO mice receiving high phosphate diet (cKO+P1) had a lower radiopacity than the normal control group fed with the same high phosphate diet (Norm+Pi) but displayed higher radiopacity than those of the cKO mice receiving standard rodent chow. The femurs and tibiae of cKO mice receiving hPi diet also appeared longer than the cKO mice with standard diet. The improvement in long bone development is more remarkable at postnatal 7 weeks (Fig. 1B) than at postnatal 4 weeks (Fig. 1A). Therefore, for the μ-CT and histological analyses, we only reported the rescue effects of hPi diet on the skeletal defects of 7-week-old cKO mice.

The low resolution μ-CT analyses further confirmed that the high phosphate diet improved the mineralization in the long bones of cKO mice (Figs. 2 and 3). Compared to the Norm+Pi mice, the femur of cKO mice had bulged (enlarged) metaphysis (Fig. 2); the metaphysis of cKO mice receiving high phosphate diet appeared less bulging. The cortex of cKO mice receiving high phosphate diet was thicker and less porous than the cKO mice receiving standard diet (Fig. 2, the sagittal section images). The most significant improvement in the skeleton for the high phosphate group occurred in the growth plate. Compared to the Norm+Pi mice, the growth plate of the cKO mice receiving standard diet was remarkably enlarged and the thickness of growth plate in the cKO mice receiving high phosphate diet was very close to that of the normal control mice (Fig. 2, arrows). The quantitative analyses showed that the high phosphate diet increased the femur length of the cKO mice by 33.7% (Fig. 2, right panel).

High-resolution μ-CT scans were performed to examine the cortical bone in the midshaft region (central region of the diaphysis) of the femurs for better imaging and quantitative analysis (Fig. 3). The 3D reconstruction images of the femoral midshaft (Fig. 3, upper panel) showed that the cortical bones in cKO mice appeared more porous, which was likely due to hypomineralization or the presence of excessive osteoid. Although the cortical bones of cKO+P1 mice still had a noticeable disorganization of structure compared to the normal controls, the bones of cKO+P1 were less porous than cKO mice receiving normal phosphate diet. Quantification analyses showed that high phosphate diet increased the bone volume fraction (expressed as the ratio of bone volume to the total volume, BV/TV) of the cKO mice by 75.0% and restored the bone apparent density by 56.7% and material density by 34.1%. Nevertheless, the apparent density and material density of cKO+P1 mice were still significantly lower than the normal controls. Consistently, Goldner’s trichrome staining and bone histomorphometry analyses demonstrated that the cortical bones of the femurs of the cKO mice exhibited significant increase in osteoid area, compared to those of the Norm+Pi mice; the phenotypic change of osteoid increase was nearly completely reversed by high phosphate diet in the cKO+P1 mice (Fig. 4A and 4B). These findings indicated that high phosphate diet significantly improved the formation and mineralization of the cortical bones in cKO mice although this dietary treatment could not fully rescue the bone defects of the cKO mice.

H&E staining (Fig. 4C) revealed that the cortical bones in the tibiae of cKO mice was malformed and had abnormal accumulation of osteoids (arrow) compared with the Norm+Pi mice. High phosphate diet greatly improved the morphological defects of the cortical bone in cKO mice. The cortical bones of the cKO+Pi mice had fewer osteoid-like structure and showed more parallel cortical lamellae. Phosphorous-regulating hormone FGF23 and SIBLING members, DMP1 and OPN are the substrates of FAM20C, and their alterations may contribute to the skeletal defects [Ishikawa et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2012]. In agreement with our previous findings [Wang et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2017], immunohistochemistry staining (Figs. 4D, 4E and 4F) showed that the cortical bones in the tibiae of cKO mice had more FGF23 and OPN and less DMP1 than those of the Norm+Pi mice. High phosphate diet did not show obvious correcting effects on the alterations of these extracellular matrix proteins.

One of the striking phenotypic changes in the X-ray images of the long bones in cKO mice is the widening (enlargement) of the growth plates (Figs. 1 and 2). H&E and Safranin O stainings (Fig. 5) revealed that the growth plates in the cKO mice were not only widened but also irregular with the hypertrophic zones being mostly enlarged. The growth plates of cKO+Pi mice receiving high phosphate diet were not only thinner but also better organized than the cKO mice receiving standard diet; morphologically the growth plates of the high phosphate-treated cKO mice were very similar to those of the normal control mice (Fig. 5). These findings indicate that the high phosphate diet nearly completely corrected the widening defects in the growth plates of long bones. We assessed several chondrocyte differentiation markers. SOX9 is a transcription factor that plays an essential role in chondrogenesis and chondrocyte differentiation [Lefebvre et al., 1997; Leung et al., 2011]. As shown in Fig. 6A, in the normal control mice SOX9 is highly expressed in immature/proliferating chondrocytes with little found in hypertrophic chondrocytes; in cKO mice strong signals of SOX9 were observed in both proliferative and hypertrophic chondrocytes. The localization profiles of SOX 9 in cKO mice receiving high phosphate diet were similar to those observed in the normal control mice, suggesting that the high phosphate diet corrected the abnormal expression and distribution patterns of SOX9 in the cKO mice (Fig. 6A). We performed in situ hybridization (ISH) analyses to assess the mRNA levels of Type II collagen (Col II, a marker for the proliferating chondrocytes) and Type X collagen (Col X, a marker for the hypertrophic chondrocytes). As shown in Figs. 6B and 6C, cKO mice had more hypertrophic chondrocytes that expressed both Col II and Col X than the Norm+Pi mice. The expression and distribution patterns of Col II and Col X mRNA in cKO mice receiving high phosphate diet were similar to those observed in the normal control mice, indicating that the altered Col II and Col X mRNA were restored to nearly normal by high phosphate diet.

Collectively, these results demonstrated that high phosphate diet given from prenatal stage to postnatal 7 weeks nearly completely rescued the defects of growth plate enlargement in the long bones and restored the expanded zones of the hypertrophic chondrocytes to nearly normal thickness.

To determine if the high phosphate diet restored the serum phosphorus level of cKO mice, we collected serum samples from 4-week-old mice and measured the serum calcium and phosphorus levels (Fig. 7). High phosphate diet did not have significant effects on serum calcium and phosphorus levels in the normal mice. High phosphate diet fully recovered the serum calcium levels of cKO mice. The serum phosphorus levels in cKO mice receiving either standard or high phosphate diet were significantly lower than those of the normal mice. The high phosphate diet significantly increased the serum phosphorus level of cKO mice by 38.7%.

In this study, we fed the Fam20c cKO mice with high phosphate diet, and found that the high phosphate diet partially rescued the skeletal defects of the cKO mice. Our findings highlighted the pathogenic role of hypophosphatemia in the skeletal development of Fam20c-deficient subjects, implying the clinical significance of correcting the reduction of serum phosphorus level in patients with non-lethal Raine syndrome.

The data in this study are in agreement with our previous findings that the Fam20c conditional knockout (cKO) mice displayed rickets/osteomalacia, decreased mineralization, and altered levels of some FAM20C substrates, including the reduction of DMP1 and the increase of FGF23 and OPN [Wang et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2017]. We further demonstrated that a high phosphate diet containing 1.5% phosphorus significantly increased the lengths of the cKO mouse legs and restored the mineralization level of the cortical bone of the femurs by approximately 50%. The extent of improvements in the long bone length and mineralization were comparable to those reported in the studies in which phosphate supplementation was administrated to other hypophosphatemic rickets mouse models including the Dmp1-null mice [Feng et al., 2006] and Hyp mice [Eicher et al., 1976; Hayashibara et al., 2007]. In addition, we found that application of the high phosphate diet beginning at postnatal 3 weeks also improved the skeletal defects of the cKO mice (Suppl. Fig.1). However, the high phosphate diet did not show significant correcting effects on the alterations of FAM20C substrates, including DMP1, FGF23 and OPN. These findings indicate that the mineralization defects in the cKO mice may be partially attributed to hypophosphatemia.

Nevertheless, the high phosphate diet dramatically improved the growth plate of the cKO mice. The thickness and morphology of growth plate in the cKO mice were almost restored to normal by the high phosphate diet, and the altered levels of chondrocyte markers in the cKO mice were nearly totally corrected with the dietary phosphorus supplementation. These observations indicate that the growth plate defects in the cKO mice were predominantly due to the lower level of serum phosphorus and were not attributed to the direct effects of FAM20C inactivation in the cartilage cells; such postulates are consistent with previous reports showing that chondrogenesis and endochondral ossification are highly sensitive to serum phosphorus level [Zhang et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2016].

Several factors may contribute to the partial rescue of the skeletal defects of the Fam20c cKO mice by high phosphate diet in this study. First, FAM20C substrates include secretory proteins that are essential for skeletal development [Fisher et al., 2001; Qin et al., 2004; Tagliabracci et al., 2012; Tagliabracci et al., 2015]. Thereby, the loss of phosphorylation of these proteins may cause skeletal defects, which cannot be rescued by high phosphate diet. Secondly, the Fam20c cKO mice have elevated circulating FGF23 levels [Wang et al., 2012], which may exert a direct adverse effect on the skeletal development [Sitara et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2008; Shalhoub et al., 2011]. Lastly, the high phosphate diet used in this study contains 1.5% phosphorus, which is lower than those used in other mouse models (2% or 3%) [Feng et al., 2006; Hayashibara et al., 2007; Maginot et al., 2015]. Indeed, the high phosphate diet used in this study failed to fully restore serum phosphorus levels of the Fam20c cKO mice, which may also explain the partial rescue effect of the high phosphate diet.

Dietary phosphorus supplement has been used clinically to treat other patients with hypophosphatemic Rickets/osteomalacia associated with PHEX mutations [Bacchetta et al., 2019; Bitzan and Goodyer, 2019]. The patients with non-lethal Raine syndrome manifest rickets/osteomalacia and hypophosphatemia [Simpson et al., 2009; Rafaelsen et al., 2013; Takeyari et al., 2014; Acevedo et al., 2015; Elalaoui et al., 2016; Rolvien et al., 2018]. Our current findings suggest that dietary phosphorus supplement may also be considered as an alternative therapy to improve the skeletal development of patients with non-lethal Raine syndrome.

In summary, this is the first report regarding the effects of dietary phosphorus supplement on the skeletal development of mice with Fam20c being inactivated. We show that high phosphate diet can significantly improve the skeletal development of the hypophosphatemic Fam20c-deficient mice. Our findings suggest that in the future, the clinical administration of dietary phosphorus supplement may need to be taken into consideration when physicians treat the non-lethal cases of Raine syndrome.
