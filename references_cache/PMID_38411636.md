# Adult-Onset Cancer Predisposition Syndromes in Children and Adolescents-To Test or not to Test?
**Authors:** Kratz CP, Lupo PJ, Zelley K, Schienda J, Nichols KE, Stewart DR, Malkin D, Brodeur GM, Maxwell K, Plon SE, Walsh MF
**Journal:** Clin Cancer Res (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-23-3683](https://doi.org/10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-23-3683)

## Content

With the increasing use of comprehensive germline genetic testing of children and adolescents with cancer, it has become evident that pathogenic variants (PV) in adult-onset cancer predisposition genes (aoCPG) underlying adult-onset cancer predisposition syndromes, such as Lynch syndrome or hereditary breast and ovarian cancer, are enriched and reported in 1% to 2% of children and adolescents with cancer. However, the causal relationship between PVs in aoCPGs and childhood cancer is still under investigation. The best-studied examples include heterozygous PVs in mismatch repair genes associated with Lynch syndrome in children with mismatch repair deficient high-grade glioma, heterozygous PVs in BARD1 in childhood neuroblastoma, and heterozygous PVs in BRCA2 in children with rhabdomyosarcoma. The low penetrance for pediatric cancers is considered to result from a combination of the low baseline risk of cancer in childhood and the report of only a modest relative risk of disease in childhood. Therefore, we do not advise that healthy children empirically be tested for PVs in an aoCPG before adulthood outside a research study. However, germline panel testing is increasingly being performed in children and adolescents with cancer, and exome and genome sequencing may be offered more commonly in this population in the future. The precise pediatric cancer risks and spectra associated with PVs in aoCPGs, underlying cellular mechanisms and somatic mutational signatures, as well as treatment response, second neoplasm risks, and psycho-oncological aspects require further research.

Cancer predisposition syndromes (CPS) are (epi)genetic conditions associated with elevated cancer risks compared to those without these syndromes (1). In recent years, it has become evident that CPS underpin cancer risk across all age groups. Here, a group of cancer predisposition syndrome experts participating in an American Association of Cancer Research (AACR) pediatric cancer predisposition workshop discuss the potential role of so-called adult-onset CPS (aoCPS) in children with cancer.

Cancer risk in the general population is low in children and adolescents, but dramatically increases with age. Although only ~1/320 newborns will develop cancer during the pediatric age range (2), the lifetime cancer risk through adulthood approaches 50% (3). Due to the low population cancer risk in children and adolescents, only those CPS that are associated with 10- to 100-fold or greater increased relative risk of cancer will lead to a cumulative cancer risk or “penetrance” that is high enough to be clinically apparent. Selected examples of highly penetrant CPS that manifest in the pediatric age range include dominantly inherited conditions such as retinoblastoma predisposition (RB1), Wilms tumor predisposition (WT1) or Li-Fraumeni syndrome (TP53) (Figure 1, panel A) (1). A number of recessive conditions with high childhood cancer risk also result from biallelic pathogenic variants (PVs) in genes, which when in the heterozygous state, are associated with dominant aoCPS, such as constitutional mismatch repair deficiency (CMMRD) (MSH2, MSH6, MLH1, or PMS2), Fanconi anemia (FA) complementation group FA-D1 (BRCA2) or FA-N (PALB2), and ataxia telangiectasia (ATM) (4,5).

In contrast, due to the higher general population cancer risk in adults, those CPS that are associated with more moderate relative cancer risks in children and adolescents are still highly penetrant in adults (Figure 1, panel B). Prominent examples of aoCPS include hereditary breast and ovarian cancer caused by PVs in BRCA1, BRCA2 or PALB2 as well as Lynch syndrome caused by PVs in MSH2, MSH6, MLH1, or PMS2 (6–8).

Exome and genome studies of childhood cancer cohorts (9–31) (Table 1) have demonstrated that heterozygous PVs in these aoCPGs occur and are statistically significantly enriched in children with certain cancers. Examples include heterozygous PVs in BRCA1, RAD51C or BARD1 in childhood neuroblastoma (29,30), heterozygous PVs in BRCA2 in childhood rhabdomyosarcoma (19,26), heterozygous PVs in mismatch repair genes in childhood high-grade glioma (32), heterozygous PVs in BRCA2 and PALB2 in medulloblastoma (15), and heterozygous PVs in BRCA2 in childhood non-Hodgkin lymphoma (33). Selected studies providing evidence for a causal relationship between PVs in aoCPGs and childhood cancer or subsequent neoplasms in survivors are summarized in Table 2. For example, data from case-control studies, meta-analyses, cohort and somatic signature studies are available for the association between childhood cancer - especially mismatch repair deficient high-grade glioma - and Lynch syndrome (21,32,34–38). Notably, PVs in aoCPGs are found in children with a range of brain and non-brain solid tumors as well as hematological neoplasms (32), and in individual cases, these associations may be non-causal.

Case-control studies demonstrating an enrichment of PVs in aoCPGs in children with cancer versus controls are, at least in part, characterized by several limitations including differences in underlying ancestry, comparison groups, age, and pipelines used to call variants and assess pathogenicity, weakening the validity of these studies (32,39–42). Also, early somatic mutational signature studies show that only some tumors harbor characteristic mutational signatures and/or loss of the second aoCPG allele (17,21,31,43,44). For these reasons, the role of PVs in genes such as BRCA1 and BRCA2 in the pathogenesis of childhood cancer is still the subject of debate (39–43). Thus, there is a need for well-powered case-control studies and burden testing with appropriate controls to address this issue. These studies should include identical variant calling and pathogenicity assessment pipelines, as well as mutational signature and functional studies to address the possible role of germline PVs in aoCPGs in pediatric patients with cancer.

Children of families with heterozygous germline PVs in aoCPGs rarely develop childhood cancer (45–47). As shown in Figure 1, panel A, PVs in aoCPGs are thought to have a low penetrance in children and adolescents (often less than 1%) whereas the effect of the same PVs are associated with substantially higher penetrance in adults (Figure 1, panel B). The low penetrance in children may be due to a combination of their low baseline risk of cancer and the only modest relative risk of disease in childhood reported in these cohorts (19,30,32). Alternatively, given the prevalence of PVs in aoCPGs in the general population, they may be fortuitous and not causative coincidences.

For a healthy child or adolescent, a PV in an aoCPG is unlikely to be clinically relevant regarding cancer risk and there are no surveillance recommendations until adulthood (Figure 2). We recommend following the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guideline that does not recommend testing in childhood unless there are medical actions to be taken during childhood (48), however, predictive genetic testing may be appropriate in limited circumstances (49). In deciding whether a child should undergo predictive genetic testing for late-onset conditions, the focus must be on the child’s medical best interest. Parents and guardians may also consider the potential psychosocial benefits and harms to the child and the extended family (50). We suggest that families requesting testing of children who don’t have cancer for aoCPG be referred for genetic counseling to consider the benefits, risks, and limitations of testing on a case-by-case basis (CCR Genetic Counseling Paper of this series). Also, testing and surveillance may need to be individualized based on an early diagnosis such as breast cancer in the family or if there is concern for the pediatric-onset recessive conditions described previously.

Nevertheless, in a child with cancer the finding of a PV in an aoCPG may be of value because it may influence treatment decisions (e.g. use of immune checkpoint inhibitors in patients with Lynch syndrome) and increase awareness and surveillance for second neoplasms (51,52). However, in practice, this information is currently rarely used to change treatment protocols (31). Also, the finding of a PV in an aoCPG in a child with cancer informs recurrence risk for future offspring and should trigger cascade counseling and testing in first degree adult family members. Finally, a childhood cancer patient with a PV In an aoCPG should be offered genetic counseling after reaching the appropriate age during young adulthood.

PVs in selected aoCPGs are likely to play a role in the pathogenesis of certain childhood cancers. They may contribute to childhood cancer risk in a direct fashion as seen in some hypermutated high-grade gliomas or a polygenic fashion (53). Well-powered case-control and burden testing studies using suitable controls and identical pipelines for variant calling and pathogenicity assessment are necessary to more precisely quantify the cancer risks and characterize the resulting cancer spectra. Further somatic mutational landscape as well as functional studies to uncover the mechanisms through which such variants may contribute to cancer in children are required. Further, investigation into outcomes, toxicities, risk of second neoplasms as well as psycho-oncologic aspects are necessary. The absolute cancer risks in childhood are small and generally do not justify testing of unaffected individuals before age 18 years, however, germline panel/exome/genome sequencing should be strongly considered in children and adolescents with cancer. During adulthood, the same PVs become increasingly penetrant with well-developed risk management strategies often beginning in the 20’s.
