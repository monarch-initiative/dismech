# Functional studies of human variants in C. elegans link iron metabolism to DPD deficiency and 5-FU sensitivity.
**Authors:** Li X, Menendez Perdomo IM, van Kuilenburg ABP, Tarailo-Graovac M
**Journal:** Genetics (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1093/genetics/iyaf228](https://doi.org/10.1093/genetics/iyaf228)

## Content

Dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD), encoded by DPYD, is a key enzyme in pyrimidine catabolism, and its deficiency leads to severe toxicity in patients treated with 5-fluorouracil (5-FU). While pathogenic DPYD variants account for many cases of DPD deficiency, they do not fully explain all instances of 5-FU sensitivity, suggesting additional genetic factors are involved. Recent studies have implicated variants in CIAO1, a gene encoding a subunit of the cytosolic iron-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster assembly targeting complex, in reducing DPD stability and function. In this study, we established a C. elegans model to assess DPD deficiency and 5-FU sensitivity. Using a dpyd-1 knockout and CRISPR-generated ciao-1 variants that mirror patient-derived variants (p.Trp184Cys, p.His193Tyr, and p.Arg65Trp), we provide the first in vivo evidence that pathogenic variants in CIA complex components can lead to DPD deficiency and, consequently, heightened 5-FU toxicity. Our findings highlight the critical role of CIAO1 in DPD function and 5-FU tolerance, expanding the genetic landscape of DPD deficiency and offering a robust platform for functional evaluation of pathogenic variants.

Dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase (DPD), encoded by DPYD, is the initial and rate-limiting enzyme in the catabolism of thymine and uracil ( Van Kuilenburg et al. 1999 ). The critical role of DPD in human health is underscored by the fact that individuals with homozygous or compound heterozygous deleterious variants in the DPYD gene exhibit reduced DPD activity, leading to DPD deficiency (OMIM # 274270). This deficiency manifests with a broad spectrum of clinical presentations, ranging from asymptomatic cases to severe neurological symptoms, including epilepsy, intellectual disability, microcephaly, delayed motor skills development, and autistic behaviors ( Van Kuilenburg et al. 1999 ). The clinical significance of DPD extends beyond rare genetic disorders. DPD is also essential for the breakdown of fluoropyrimidines, a class of chemotherapeutic agents, including 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) and its prodrugs, capecitabine and tegafur, widely used in the treatment of various solid tumors ( van Kuilenburg 2004 ). Although DPYD variants are known to be the primary cause of severe toxicity in fluoropyrimidine treatment, the absence of these variants in many cases of toxicity suggests that additional genetic factors may also contribute to reduced DPD activity ( Knikman et al. 2024 ).

At the molecular level, DPD functions as a homodimeric iron-sulfur protein, relying on redox cofactors like FAD, FMN, and four iron-sulfur (Fe-S) clusters per subunit for efficient electron transfer during substrate reduction ( Dobritzsch et al. 2001 ; Schnackerz et al. 2004 ). The Fe-S clusters, acting as a conduit for electron transfer between FAD and FMN, are indispensable for pyrimidine reduction ( Schnackerz et al. 2004 ). The critical role of Fe-S clusters in DPD function is further emphasized by the observation that multiple DPYD variants affecting Fe-S coordinating domains result in DPD deficiency ( Dobritzsch et al. 2001 ; van Kuilenburg 2004 ). The biogenesis of cytosolic Fe-S proteins, such as DPD, involves the cytosolic iron-sulfur cluster assembly (CIA) targeting complex. This complex, composed of CIAO1, MMS19, and CIAO2B, plays a pivotal role in the final step of Fe-S cluster incorporation into apoproteins, including DPD ( Gari et al. 2012 ; Stehling et al. 2012 ; Paul and Lill 2015 ). Studies have shown that depletion of CIAO1, MMS19, or CIAO2B leads to reduced DPD activity and protein levels, likely due to impaired Fe-S cluster insertion (Stehling et al. 2012 , 2013 ).

Although more than a dozen human diseases are associated with mitochondrial Fe-S cluster biogenesis and Fe-S cluster containing proteins have been identified ( Wachnowsky et al. 2018 ), the specific role of the CIA targeting complex in development and disease remains poorly understood. This knowledge gap persists despite the complex's critical function in delivering Fe-S clusters to numerous essential proteins ( Paul and Lill 2015 ; Wachnowsky et al. 2018 ). We and others have identified rare biallelic variants in CIAO1 in patients presenting with rare neurodegenerative or neuromuscular conditions ( Maio et al. 2024 ; van Karnebeek et al. 2024 ). However, the direct impact of these variants on 5-FU sensitivity has not been functionally evaluated.

In this study, we leverage the model organism Caenorhabditis elegans to establish a 5-FU sensitivity assay, confirming the link between DPD loss and 5-FU sensitivity. Importantly, we also investigate the impact of defective CIA targeting complex on DPD function, particularly focusing on CIAO1 variants identified in patients. By generating C. elegans knockout and knock-in mutants mirroring human variants, we provide the first in vivo evidence that pathogenic variants in CIA complex components can lead to DPD deficiency and heightened 5-FU toxicity. This work expands the understanding of the genetic landscape associated with DPD deficiency and 5-FU toxicity, offering a valuable C. elegans model for assessing the pathogenicity of variants in genes relevant to these conditions.

We first characterized the dpyd-1 knockout strain, dpyd-1(ko). Under standard culture conditions at 20°C, these knockout worms appeared phenotypically normal, exhibiting brood sizes and hatch rates comparable to wild-type worms (Supplementary Fig. 1a and b). At elevated temperatures, dpyd-1(ko) worms displayed a reduction in brood size but not in hatch rate (Supplementary Fig. 1c and d).

Given the sensitivity of DPD-deficient patients to fluoropyrimidine drugs, we investigated whether the loss of DPD in C. elegans would similarly confer sensitivity to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) at 20°C. However, previous studies have revealed that the E. coli OP50 commonly used for worm culture metabolizes 5-FU into toxic compounds (Fig. 1a), particularly fluorouridine monophosphate (FUMP) and fluorouridine triphosphate (FUTP), which drastically potentiates the effect of 5-FU ( Garcia-Gonzalez et al. 2017 ; Scott et al. 2017 ). Indeed, in a pilot 5-FU assay using the dpyd-1(ko) strain, both wild-type (N2) and knockout worms were strongly affected by 5-FU treatment, even in the nanomolar range (Supplementary Fig. 2). These findings suggest that bacterial metabolism may have masked the impact of DPD deficiency. To evaluate the true contribution of DPD in 5-FU tolerance, we adopted a mutant E. coli strain BW25113 (Δudp Δupp Δudk::kan) as a food source for worms ( Scott et al. 2017 ). This strain is incapable of converting 5-FU into FUMP and FUTP via the alternative pathways (Fig. 1b). When fed BW25113 (Δudp Δupp Δudk::kan), wild-type worms exhibited tolerance to significantly high 5-FU concentrations, reaching the micromolar range (Fig. 1c). Importantly, dpyd-1 knockout worms displayed marked sensitivity to 5-FU, with no viable embryos at concentrations over 32 µM. These findings allowed us to establish a robust 5-FU assay for evaluating DPD function in C. elegans.

CIAO1 and DPD proteins are highly conserved from yeast to human (Fig. 2a) ( Li et al. 2024 ). The three CIAO1 variants identified in the two patients from our previous study ( van Karnebeek et al. 2024 ) are invariant between C. elegans and humans (Fig. 2a). To investigate the functional impact of these variants, we employed CRISPR/Cas9 to introduce the patient-derived variants into the corresponding ciao-1 gene and to compare the resulting phenotype to a ciao-1 knockout (ciao-1(ko)) ( Li et al. 2024 ). Three CRISPR lines were generated: ciao-1(mod213 [p.His200Tyr]), ciao-1(mod214 [p.Trp190Cys]), and ciao-1(mod212 [p.Arg71Trp]), mimicking the human variants CIAO1 (p.His193Tyr), CIAO1 (p.Trp184Cys), and CIAO1 (p.Arg65Trp), respectively (Table 1 and Fig. 2a). Homozygous mutants for all three variants were viable with normal brood sizes and hatch rates at 20°C and 25°C (Supplementary Fig. 3). In contrast, homozygous worms for ciao-1(ko) were sterile ( Li et al. 2024 ). However, ciao-1(Arg71Trp) worms exhibited a temperature-dependent weak mortal germline (Mrt) phenotype, with 70% of the replicates becoming sterile by generation 21 when incubated at 25 °C (Supplementary Table 1).

To assess the impact of these variants on 5-FU sensitivity, we performed assays exposing worms to 32 μM of 5-FU, at which the dpyd-1 knockout strain showed complete loss of embryonic viability (Fig. 1b). Exposure to 5-FU drastically reduced the hatch rate of ciao-1(W190C) and ciao-1(H200Y), showing only 40% of embryonic viability, whereas ciao-1(R71W) resembled the drastic sensitivity observed in dpyd-1(ko) mutants (Fig. 2b). These results suggest that ciao-1(His200Tyr), ciao-1(Trp190Cys), and ciao-1(Arg71Trp) are hypomorphic alleles that still significantly compromise DPD enzyme function.

For over six decades, the primary focus in understanding both complete and partial DPD deficiency has been on variants within the DPYD gene itself. However, DPYD genotype alone fails to explain a significant proportion of 5-FU toxicity cases, suggesting the involvement of additional genetic factors in DPD deficiency. Recent human genetic studies have identified patients with biallelic variants in CIAO1, associated with reduced DPD levels and activity ( Maio et al. 2024 ; van Karnebeek et al. 2024 ).

In this study, we characterized a C. elegans dpyd-1 knockout model and established an effective 5-FU sensitivity assay to assess DPD function in vivo. Given the conservation of DPD and the ease of introducing patient-derived variants in C. elegans, we anticipate that this model will be valuable for determining the pathogenicity of human DPYD alleles.

Considering the essential role of iron-sulfur (Fe-S) clusters in DPD function and our previous findings that compound heterozygous variants in human CIAO1 reduce DPD activity, we explored the effects of individual ciao-1 alleles in our C. elegans model by assessing organismal fitness and 5-FU sensitivity. Our results demonstrate that the ultra-rare CIAO1 variants p.Trp184Cys and p.His193Tyr, as well as the more common p.Arg65Trp variant, exhibit a hypomorphic effect, all significantly contributing to 5-FU toxicity. These findings align with clinical observations, explaining the survival of two patients harboring biallelic CIAO1 variants—p.Trp184Cys or p.His193Tyr and p.Arg65Trp—allowing sufficient residual CIAO1 function for viability. During the preparation of this manuscript, Maio et al. (2024) reported seven additional CIAO1 alleles in four patients, including the p.Arg65Trp allele in two patients (compound heterozygous with another CIAO1 allele).

The relatively high prevalence of the CIAO1 (Arg65Trp) variant in the general population (0.16%, gnomad_r4) representing large number of carriers (2647, gnomad_r4) with highest frequency in non-Finish Europeans but no homozygotes further supports the detrimental effect of this variant. Understanding the role of ciao-1 in DPD deficiency is particularly important, since the three variants assessed here are classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS) in ClinVar. This classification limits their diagnostic value and typically precludes proper medical treatment and patient care. In addition to 5-FU sensitivity, ciao-1(R71W) worms exhibited a weak temperature-dependent Mrt phenotype, producing fewer progeny and becoming progressively sterile over 21 generations when incubated at elevated temperatures, further confirming the pathogenicity of this variant. We speculate that fertility loss leading to the weak Mrt phenotype in ciao-1(R71W) worms could reflect a spectrum of effects associated with reduced CIAO-1 protein stability at higher temperatures, DPD loss of function, and impaired activity of other cytoplasmic/nuclear Fe-S proteins such as DOG-1 ( Li et al. 2024 ), RTEL-1, and POLD-1 ( Maio et al. 2024 ). Finally, it is also important to note that 5-FU assays are conducted at a lower temperature (20°C), at which none of the strains tested (dpyd-1(ko), ciao-1(H200Y), ciao-1(W190C), and ciao-1(R71W)) exhibits reduced hatch rate or brood size and therefore, the reduced hatch rate detected is due to the inability of mutant worms to break down 5-FU due to loss of DPD function, rather than any inherent hatching rate variability.

Overall, these findings establish C. elegans as a powerful platform for assessing 5-FU activity in vivo and for efficiently modeling the broader impact of CIAO1 variants on Fe-S cluster proteins. This work expands our understanding of the genetic landscape underlying DPD deficiency and 5-FU toxicity, with potential implications for improving diagnostics and developing personalized treatment strategies for patients at risk of severe 5-FU-related adverse events. Due to its genetic tractability and symbiotic microbial interactions, this C. elegans platform may also serve as an effective starting point for exploring additional genetic and microbial contributions to host responses to anticancer drugs, utilizing a combination of bacterial genetics, dietary modulation, and chemical compounds.

C. elegans (var. Bristol) were maintained on E. coli OP50-seeded NGM plates at 15°C as described previously ( Brenner 1974 ) unless otherwise specified. dpyd-1(ko), VC3927 strain, was generated using a CRISPR/Cas9-assisted method developed by Dr. Don Moerman's lab at the University of British Columbia ( Au et al. 2019 ). The remaining strains were generated in our lab using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing adapted from a method by Dr. Craig Mello's lab ( Dokshin et al. 2018 ). The sequences for crRNAs and repair templates are provided in Supplementary Table 2. All strains and their genotypes used here are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Brood size was determined by plating individual L4 larvae to separate plates and transferring the corresponding adults daily for four days at the indicated temperatures. Eggs laid were scored for each plate for overall brood size and further assessment on embryonic (unhatched) arrest.

5-FU assays were conducted on NGM plates (kanamycin 50 mg/ml) seeded with BW25113 (Δudp Δupp Δudk::kan) ( Scott et al. 2017 ). Strains previously maintained on OP50 were axenized, transferred to BW25113 plates, and allowed to propagate for at least one week before assays. NGM agar plates containing 5-FU were prepared by mixing 5-FU stocks (1000X in DMSO) into melted agar before pouring.

L4-stage worms were placed on 5-FU plates and allowed to develop into adults at 20°C. The following day, adults were transferred to fresh 5-FU plates for 24 h to lay eggs, after which the adults were removed. Embryos were allowed to hatch for an additional 24 h, and the number of dead embryos and hatched larvae were counted. 5-FU sensitivity was calculated as the hatch rate (Hatch rate = the number of hatched larvae/(number of dead embryos + number of hatched larvae)) x 100. For each 5-FU concentration, hatch rates were determined from embryos laid by at least five worms, three replicates per strain.

Strains were assessed for Mrt phenotype according to a previously reported assay ( Saber et al. 2022 ). The N2 strain was used as a control. Cryopreserved strains were thawed, and genotyped worms were selected to start a homogeneous population. Worms were incubated at 20 °C for 1 week to lay progeny. Then, three worms in the L4 stage were plated (10 replicates per strain) and incubated at either 20 °C or 25 °C (G0). Subsequently, three L4s were transferred every 3–4 d until no progeny was detected on the plates or until generation 21 (G21).
