Enterovirus D68 (EV-D68) is a respiratory virus that causes neurological
complications such as acute flaccid myelitis (AFM) and death in children. No
vaccine or antiviral is available for EV-D68. We report the structure-based
design of the EV-D68 VP1 capsid inhibitors with in vivo antiviral efficacy in a
neonatal mouse model of EV-D68-associated paralytic myelitis. Cryo-EM structures
show that Jun11787 and Jun11695 bind the hydrophobic canyon region in VP1 and
display nanomolar potency against multiple EV-D68 strains and single-digit
micromolar potency against EV-A71 and CVB3 in vitro. Jun11787 and Jun11695 also
significantly reduce the spinal cord viral titer, prevent the progression of
paralysis, and improve weight gain in EV-D68-infected male and female mice when
treatment is initiated immediately, 24 h, and even 4-6 days post-infection.
Overall, Jun11787 and Jun11695 represent promising leads for treating EV-D68
infection.

Enterovirus D68 (EV-D68) is a respiratory virus that typically causes asymptomatic or mild respiratory disease in children. However, EV-D68 infection can also lead to neurological complications such as acute flaccid myelitis (AFM), with arm or leg weakness, and rarely death1–3. EV-D68 is a non-enveloped, positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus mainly transmitted through the respiratory route, making it a public health threat4. EV-D68 is considered a pathogen with pandemic potential. During its first outbreak in 2014, there were over a thousand confirmed infections and millions of potential undetected cases in the United States and many countries in Europe and Asia5–7. Compared to historical EV-D68 strains, contemporary strains isolated from the 2014 outbreak in the United States show more severe pathogenicity6,8–10. Contemporary strains, such as EV-D68 (US/KY/14) and EV-D68 (US/MO/47), were able to infect and cause cytopathic effects in neuronal cell lines, which was not observed with earlier isolated EV-D68 strains, including the prototypical Fermon strain10,11.

Several compounds have been identified as EV-D68 antivirals from phenotypical screening that show varying potency, selectivity index, and antiviral spectrum in cell culture2,12. Representative examples include the viral VP1 capsid inhibitors pleconaril and pirodavir, the 3C protease inhibitor rupintrivir, the 3D polymerase inhibitors ribavirin and gemcitabine, the 3A inhibitor enviroxime, and the 2C inhibitors dibucaine and fluoxetine13–18. Given their potent in vitro antiviral activity, enviroxime, pirodavir, pleconaril, ribavirin, and rupintrivir have been tested in EV-D68 respiratory or neurological disease mouse model19. However, none of these antivirals showed in vivo antiviral efficacy19, possibly due to the low potency, pharmacokinetic (PK) liabilities, or off-target side effects. The 2C inhibitor fluoxetine also had no antiviral activity in a mouse model of neurological EV-D68 infection20. Our recent study showed that the 2A protease inhibitor telaprevir and the 2C inhibitor Jun6504 had moderate to potent in vivo antiviral efficacy in preventing paralysis in an EV-D68 infection neurological mouse model21,22. A pleconaril analog 11526092 displayed in vivo antiviral efficacy in a respiratory mouse model with EV-D68 infection but was not active in the neurological mouse model23. Designing small-molecule antivirals with potent in vivo antiviral efficacy in a mouse model of neurological EV-D68 infection, especially when dosed post-viral infection, remains an unmet medical need.

As a non-enveloped virus, EV-D68 is surrounded by an icosahedral viral capsid shell formed by viral capsid proteins24. The capsid protein of a single virion comprises 60 protomers, and each protomer consists of three capsid proteins VP1, VP2, and VP3, and an internal protein VP412,24. There is a concave pocket in VP1, referred to as a canyon. The canyon floor is formed primarily by the G-H loop of VP1, which connects the β-strands G and H. The canyon pocket is relatively hydrophobic and is typically occupied by a lipid24. This pocket is vital for regulating the stability of viral particles and binding to the host cell receptor2. VP1 protein is a validated antiviral drug target. Pleconaril, a capsid inhibitor of rhinovirus that failed in clinical trials due to the lack of efficacy and side effects, showed antiviral activities against multiple EV-D68 strains in cell culture25. Additional EV-D68 capsid inhibitors include pirodavir and R85693226–30. However, pleconaril and pirodavir showed no efficacy in reducing viral titer or improving survival in a respiratory or neurological model of EV-D68 infection in AG129 mice19. Therefore, VP1 remains to be validated as a viable antiviral drug target.

Here, we report on the rational design of the EV-D68 capsid inhibitors with in vivo antiviral efficacy in the EV-D68 infection neurological mouse model. First, we performed a virtual screening of inhibitors targeting the hydrophobic canyon pocket in the capsid protein VP1 and identified several hits with potent antiviral activity against EV-D68 in cell culture. Next, we applied a structure-based design to optimize the antiviral potency and pharmacokinetic (PK) properties. We validated the mechanism of action using cryo-EM, biochemical assays, and resistance selection. Finally, we showed that two lead compounds, Jun11695 and Jun11787, had potent in vivo antiviral efficacy in the EV-D68 infection neurological mouse model when treatment is initiated immediately, 24 h, and even 4–6 days post-infection. Together, these data reveal promising EV-D68 capsid inhibitors with translational potential to be further developed as antiviral drugs and validate VP1 as an antiviral drug target.

Compound Z281155752 showed a comparable docking score to pleconaril (−9.428 vs −9.441) (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1). We resynthesized this compound, which inhibited EV-D68 US/MO/14-18947 replication in human rhabdomyosarcoma (RD) cells with an EC50 value of 1.58 µM in a cytopathic effect (CPE) assay (Fig. 1). We subsequently performed initial hit optimization on Z281155752 (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 2). Several compounds showed improved antiviral activity with submicromolar EC50 values, including Jun11301 (EC50 = 49.8 nM), Jun11352 (EC50 = 100.1 nM), Jun11353 (EC50 = 174.9 nM), and Jun11459 (EC50 = 123.1 nM). However, the most potent compound, Jun11301, had low stability in the mouse liver microsomal stability assay (T1/2 = 1.7 min).

Next, we initiated a multi-parameter hit-to-lead optimization to improve antiviral potency (EC50), cellular cytotoxicity (CC50), and mouse liver microsomal stability (T1/2). Superimposing the binding poses of Z281155752 and pleconaril in the VP1 binding site revealed additional binding cavities at both ends of the molecule (Fig. 1b). For the convenience of synthesis and availability of starting materials, we chose 8-aminoquinoline as the core structure instead of 1-naphthylamine in Z281155752. The superimposed structures of pleconaril and Z281155752 suggest that the 4-position of the quinoline core in Z281155752 can be conjugated with a heterocycle substitution, which will occupy the same subsite as the trifluoro-oxadiazole in pleconaril (Fig. 1b,c). Similarly, the para-position of the benzoic acid in Z281155752 can be substituted with an alkoxyl or a heterocycle substitution to fill in the subsite near the entrance of the hydrophobic pocket (Fig. 1b, c). The docking pose of Z281155752 indicates that the central amide linker does not form hydrogen bonds with VP1. Therefore, we hypothesized that the amide linker could be substituted with an amine or ether linker (Fig. 1c). To validate this design hypothesis, we designed and synthesized three series of compounds (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4).

For the amide linker series of compounds, we first examined the 4-position R1 substitution on the 8-aminoquinoline (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Consistent with the design hypothesis, introducing a heterocycle substitution improved the antiviral activity significantly (Jun11722 vs Jun11227). Compounds with the thienyl (Jun11297), furanyl (Jun113910), pyrazolyl (Jun11787), pyrimidinyl (Jun11453), and pyridalyl (Jun11614) substitutions were among the most potent and selective compounds, showing nanomolar antiviral potency with a selectivity index of over 1000. These compounds also had improved microsomal stability. Next, a second sub-library was designed by fixing the 4-substitution as the trifluoroethyl pyrazolyl and varying the benzoic acid substitution (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The para-position of the benzoic acid tolerates both alkoxy and heterocycle substitutions. Collectively, compound Jun11787 was identified as the most promising lead compound with potent antiviral activity (EC50 = 27.8 nM), a high selectivity index (SI = 4,496), and favorable microsomal stability (T1/2 = 114.5 min).

The amine and ether linker series of compounds showed similar antiviral potency as the amide series of compounds (Supplementary Fig. 4), corroborating the docking pose of Z181155752 and its superimposed structure with pleconaril (Fig. 1b). The most promising candidate Jun11695 had potent antiviral activity (EC50 = 16.8 nM) and high microsomal stability (T1/2 = 83.3 min), while being non-cytotoxic (CC50 > 60 µM).

Collectively, structure-based hit-to-lead optimization led to the discovery of two lead compounds, Jun11787 and Jun11695, as promising EV-D68 antivirals.

To profile the broad-spectrum antiviral activity of Jun11695 and Jun11787, we tested them against five strains of EV-D68 isolated from the 2014 epidemic, including clade B1 viruses US/MO/14-18947 and US/MO/14-18949, clade B2 viruses US/IL/14-18952 and US/IL/14-18956, and clade D virus US/KY/14-18953. Jun11695 and Jun11787 showed consistent antiviral activity against all strains with EC50 values ranging from 7.0 to 39.4 nM and were more potent than pleconaril (Fig. 2a). To investigate the cross-resistance of Jun11695 and Jun11787 with pleconaril, we generated recombinant EV-D68 viruses that are resistant to pleconaril, rMO-V81A, rMO-V81I, and rMO-F159V, using the US/MO/14-18947 reverse genetic system (Supplementary Fig. 6a)31,32. The amino acid substitution VP1-V81A was previously identified as a pleconaril-resistant mutant from the serial viral passage experiment27. To further confirm the drug resistance at this residue, we also generated the VP1-V81I recombinant virus. rMO-V81A and rMO-V81I showed a 7.6- and 2.2-fold increase in EC50 values compared to pleconaril (Supplementary Fig. 6b), suggesting V81 is a drug-resistant hot spot for capsid inhibitors that bind to the hydrophobic canyon. In addition, we performed an independent viral passage experiment with an escalated drug selection pressure of pleconaril. The EC50 of passage 7 virus was over 5 µM, compared to 0.2 µM at passage 0, suggesting the emergence of drug resistance. Sequencing the viral genome revealed the VP1-F159V mutant. Drug resistance was confirmed with the recombinant rMO-F159V virus, showing an EC50 over 1.5 µM against pleconaril (Supplementary Fig. 6b). V81 and F159 are located at the pleconaril binding pocket and form hydrophobic interactions with the propyl linker and the trifluoro-oxadiazole in pleconaril. Mutations at V81 or F159 reduce hydrophobic interactions or cause steric clashes with the capsid inhibitors (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Jun11695 showed similar cross-resistance as pleconaril, with the EC50 values for rMO-V81A and rMO-V81I recombinant viruses increased by 5.8- and 19.2-fold, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6c). In contrast, Jun11787 showed consistent inhibition against the pleconaril-resistant strains with EC50 values increasing only by 1-, 3.0-, and 3.4-fold for rMO-V81A, rMO-V81I, and rMO-F159V, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6d).

The broad-spectrum antiviral activities of Jun11695 and Jun11787 were further evaluated against EV-D68 US/MO/14-18947 (clade B1), and other enteroviruses including EV-A71 Tainan/4643/1998 and Coxsackievirus B3 Nancy, using plaque assays, with pleconaril serving as a control. All three compounds showed nano-molar level potency, ranging from 5.7 nM to 26.9 nM, against EV-D68, consistent with CPE results (Fig. 2b). Both compounds demonstrated moderate potency against EV-A71, with EC50 values of 2.8 µM for Jun11695 and 3.6 µM for Jun11787, while pleconaril was inactive (EC50 > 30 µM) (Fig. 2c). Jun11695 exhibited higher potency (EC50 = 2.1 µM) compared to Jun11787 (EC50 = 2.7 µM) in inhibiting CVB3, while pleconaril was inactive (EC50 > 30 µM) (Fig. 2d). Although the antiviral activity of these compounds was approximately 1000-fold lower than EV-A71 and CVB3 compared to EV-D68, this reduction may be attributed to sequence variations in capsid proteins across these strains (Supplementary Fig. 5). Nonetheless, the sub-micromolar potency observed underscores the potential of this scaffold as a promising starting point for developing broad-spectrum capsid inhibitors. Jun11695 and Jun11787 inhibited the replication of EV-D68 US/MO/14-18947 virus at multiplicity of infections (MOIs) from 0.0003 to 30 in the viral titer reduction assay (Fig. 2e). In the combination therapy experiment between Jun11695 and telaprevir, the highest synergy score (ZIP-score) is 48.54, corresponding to 11.1 nM of Jun11695 and 333.3 nM of telaprevir (Fig. 2f). Telaprevir is an FDA-approved HCV drug and was found to inhibit EV-D68 by targeting the 2 A protease (2Apro)32,33.Jun11787 similarly showed synergistic antiviral effect with telaprevir with the highest synergy score (ZIP-score) is 27.53, corresponding to 11.1 nM of Jun11787 and 333.3 nM of telaprevir (Fig. 2f)34,35. The observed synergistic effect was consistent with the experiment result using control compounds, pleconaril and telaprevir, where the highest synergy score (ZIP-score) of 70.14, corresponding to 33.3 nM of pleconaril and 333.3 nM of telaprevir (Supplementary Fig. 6e). A synergy score larger than 10 means synergistic.

The binding of all inhibitors had almost no impact on the overall structure of the virus capsid or the chemical environment in the pocket, indicating that there is no destabilizing effect, and the inhibition mechanism is likely based on stabilization of the capsid, as has been demonstrated before for this class of inhibitors.

To pharmacologically demonstrate the inhibitors playing antiviral activities as capsid inhibitors, a drug time-of-addition experiment was performed with Jun11695 and Jun11787 to quantify the intracellular viral VP1 protein level using immunofluorescence imaging. In this experiment, RD cells were infected with EV-D68 US/MO/14-18947 (clade B1) at an MOI of 1, and Jun11695,
Jun11787, and pleconaril were added at different time points either before, during, or after viral infection (Fig. 3b). Jun11695,
Jun11787, and pleconaril showed nearly complete inhibition of VP1 protein synthesis when presented before viral entry at -1-8h, 0-8 h, and 0-1 h periods (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 8a). In contrast, all three compounds dramatically lost inhibitory activities when added post-viral entry at 2–8 h. The results suggest that Jun11695 and Jun11787 inhibit the early stage of EV-D68 viral replication, similar to pleconaril.

To unbiasedly validate the mechanism of action, we next performed serial passage experiments with EV-D68 US/MO/14-18947 (clade B1) in the presence of increasing drug selection pressure of Jun11695 (Fig. 3d). Genome sequencing revealed that two mutations occurred during the serial passage of Jun11695, VP1-V207I and VP3-V192A (Fig. 3d). Jun11695 drug resistance emerged at passage 4 (P4) with the EC50 value increased by 115-fold (Fig. 3e).

Structural analysis showed that the Jun11695 drug-resistant mutant VP1-V207I is located at the pleconaril binding pocket, while the VP3-V192A at the adjunct protomer is located distal (about 33.2 Å) to the pleconaril binding pocket (Supplementary Fig. 8b,8c). We subsequently employed reverse genetic systems to generate recombinant EV-D68 viruses encoding VP1-V207I (rMO VP1-V207I), VP3-V192A (rMO VP3-V192A), and both mutations (rMO VP1-V207I & VP3-V192A) to validate their roles in conferring resistance to Jun1169532. Plaque assays showed that the rMO VP1-V207I & VP3-V192A (76-fold to rMO-WT) is more resistant than the rMO VP1-V207I (20-fold to rMO-WT) and rMO VP3-A192A (4-fold to rMO-WT) against Jun11695 (Fig. 3f).

To compare the fitness of replication of the recombinant viruses rMO VP1-V207I & VP3-V192A to rMO-WT, we generated growth curves and conducted a competition growth assay. The Jun11695-resistant virus rMO VP1-V207I & VP3-V192A had a similar growth rate as the rMO-WT (Supplementary Fig. 8d). In the competition growth assay, rMO-WT overtook rMO VP1-V207I & VP3-V192A at passage 5 (Supplementary Fig. 8e), suggesting the resistant viruses had reduced replication fitness compared to rMO-WT.

Following the success of this model, we next tested both capsid inhibitors at 50 mg/kg in a 24-hour delayed treatment model in which treatment is withheld until 24 h after infection. In this model, there was again a significant difference in paralysis scores between control and Jun11787-treated animals as well as between control and Jun11695-treated animals (p < 0.0001, repeated measures ANOVA for both groups), with the control group having a paralysis score that was, on average, 7.2 points higher than the Jun11787-treated group (with an average paralysis score of 0.4), and 6.2 points higher than the Jun11695-treated group (with an average paralysis score of 1.4) (Fig. 5c). This numerical difference in paralysis scores is equivalent to an animal with complete paralysis in 2 limbs and weakness in a third, compared to an animal with minimal/moderate weakness in only a single limb. Jun11787-treated animals also had significantly increased weight at the end of the experiment compared to controls (Fig. 5d) (p = 0.0093 repeated measures ANOVA), suggesting that animals in the capsid inhibitor-treated group did not get as sick as control animals. Jun11695-treated animals also had increased weight compared to controls, but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.0645 repeated measures ANOVA).

To examine how Jun11787 affects viral titers in the muscle and spinal cord, we collected quadriceps muscles and spinal cords from EV-D68-infected pups in our 24-hour delayed treatment model at 4-, 6-, and 8-day post-infection. There was a significantly less infectious virus in the spinal cords (Fig. 5e) and muscles (Fig. 5f) of Jun11787-treated mice at 4-, 6-, and 8-day post-infection. The viral titer in Jun11787-treated animals never exceeded the limit of detection at any time point. We also measured the paralysis scores of mice immediately prior to collecting tissue and found that there was a significantly reduced paralysis in all animals from the Jun11787-treated group (p < 0.0001 ANOVA) (Fig. 5g), suggesting that Jun11787 is reducing the paralysis score by inhibiting viral replication in the spinal cord and muscle tissue. Blood was also collected, but no viral titer was found in any animal from any group.

To examine whether the improved paralysis scores in the Jun11787-treated animals were due to improved survival of motor neurons in this group, we performed immunohistochemistry staining of the lumbar enlargement in spinal cord sections from treated and untreated animals in a 24 h delayed treatment model. We found that in the control (corn oil) group, animals with a paralysis score of 6 (complete paralysis in both hind limbs) had no choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) positive motor neurons evident in the lumbar spinal cord region (Figs. 5h, 5i bottom panel, and 5k). However, when the same region was examined in Jun11787-treated animals, with a paralysis score of 0, there were many ChAT-positive motor neurons present (Figs. 5h, 5i top panel, and 5j). To quantify this difference, we counted motor neurons in serial sections through the ventral horn of the lumbar enlargement (spaced 200 microns apart) and found that the average number of motor neurons in Jun11787-treated animals (approximately 30 motor neurons per 20 µm section) is equivalent to the low-end counts for uninfected control animals, compared to complete loss of ChAT+ motor neurons in EV-D68 infected control (corn oil) treated animals (Fig. 5h). The difference in motor neuron counts between the right and left ventral horn suggests that some motor neurons can be lost from the spinal cord before paralysis becomes evident, and that Jun11787 treatment at 24 h post-infection can spare enough motor neurons that no signs of paralysis are evident.

In our 24-hour delayed treatment model, paralysis typically begins somewhere between 3 and 6 days post-infection. To examine whether Jun11787 effectively prevent paralysis when treatment is discontinued prior to the expected onset of paralysis, we began treatment of infected mice at 24 h post-infection, as before, but discontinued daily dosing after 3- or 5-consecutive daily doses. We found Jun11787 prevents paralysis even when mice receive only 3 daily doses of drug beginning 24 h post-infection (p < 0.0001 repeated measures ANOVA) (Fig. 5l). Unsurprisingly, Jun11787 was also effective in preventing paralysis when dosed in the same model for 5 days (p < 0.0001 repeated measures ANOVA). Mice treated for 3- or 5-days with Jun11787 also had, on average, significantly increased weight at the end of the experiment (3-day treatment p = 0.0183; and 5-day treatment p = 0.0494, both repeated measures ANOVA) (Fig. 5m).

Since most patients will not seek medical help until EV-D68 infection has progressed, from the prodromal respiratory phase to the AFM phase, we developed a model, called the clinical treatment model, in which treatment is withheld until after the onset of paralysis. In this model, we waited until a mouse had an unmistakable weakness in at least one limb (a score of 1.5 or higher) before beginning treatment. In this clinical treatment model, the average day of Jun11787 treatment was 4.7 ± 1.3 days, and the average paralysis score at the time of treatment was 2.1 ± 0.6. When Jun11787 was tested in our clinical treatment model, the paralysis scores from Jun11787-treated animals were significantly lower at the end of the experiment compared to control-treated animals (Fig. 5n). While the difference was statistically significant (p = 0.0174 repeated measures ANOVA), the actual difference in paralysis score was only 1.8 (which is the difference between complete paralysis and partial weakness of a single limb). We also tested Jun11695 in our clinical treatment model, with the average day of Jun11695 treatment at 4.6 ± 1.2 days and the average paralysis at the time of treatment being 2.3 ± 0.6, and again found that treated animals had lower paralysis scores at the end of the experiment compared to control animals (Fig. 5n), but the results were not quite statistically significant (p = 0.0628 repeated measures ANOVA). Moreover, the actual difference in paralysis scores between these groups was only 1.3 (which is the difference between complete paralysis and partial paralysis of a single limb). The weights were not significantly different between groups in the clinical treatment model (Fig. 5o). To examine how the results from Jun11695 and Jun11787 compare with the current standard of care (treatment with pooled human intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)), we tested the efficacy of human IVIG in our clinical treatment model. For this experiment, the average day of treatment for hIVIG was 4.5 ± 0.2 days and the average paralysis was 2.3 ± 0.1. We found that when mice were treated with human IVIG in our clinical model, the paralysis score was improved by an average of 1.5 on our seizure scale (Supplementary Table 2). Therefore, while the improvement afforded by Jun11787 is modest, it is roughly equivalent to the current standard of care, IVIG, in the clinical treatment model.

This study reports the rational design of two EV-D68 capsid inhibitors, Jun11787 and Jun11695, with in vivo antiviral efficacy in a neurological paralysis mouse model. They represent the first druglike VP1 capsid inhibitors showing in vivo antiviral efficacy in the EV-D68 infection-induced neurological mouse model. EV-D68-induced paralysis and death is a rare but severe disease with no vaccine or antiviral available. Despite the potent antiviral activity of repurposed drugs, such as fluoxetine, rupintrivir, and pleconaril, against EV-D68 in cell culture, none have so far shown antiviral efficacy in mouse models of EV-D68 infection. To fill the knowledge gap, we initiated a structure-based design to identify inhibitors targeting the viral VP1 hydrophobic canyon binding site. Lead optimization and PK profiling identified Jun11787 and Jun11695 as the in vivo lead candidates.

Jun11787 and Jun11695 inhibited multiple strains of EV-D68 with nanomolar potency and a high selectivity index. Cryo-EM structures showed that Jun11787 and Jun11695 fit in the VP1 hydrophobic canyon with binding poses similar to pleconaril. Both inhibitors also blocked viral entry, corroborating with their mechanism of action by targeting the VP1 capsid protein. Significantly, Jun11787 was active against pleconaril-resistant EV-D68 strains. The differences in chemical structures and inhibitor/protein interactions account for the different genetic barrier to drug resistance, Jun11695 form more extensive hydrophobic interactions with VP1 than pleconaril, which explains their higher potency. Jun11787 and Jun11695 displayed high stability in mouse microsomes and a long in vivo half-life in mice when dosed by i.p. injection. Jun11787 and Jun11695 were well tolerated in mice and significantly improved paralysis score and weight gain when given immediately or 24 h after EV-D68 infection. Viral titers in the spinal cord showed that Jun11787 treatment significantly reduced viral titer in the spinal cord by 4 days post-infection and kept viral titer below the limit of detection thereafter. The same holds true for viral titer in the infected quadriceps muscle. The improvement in paralysis scores may be primarily driven by the drug’s efficacy in the muscle, spinal cord, or both. Examination of the lumbar enlargement (a thickened region in the lumbar spinal cord important for hindlimb movement) showed complete loss of ChAT+ motor neurons in untreated animals with complete paralysis of the hindlimbs, whereas the ChAT+ motor neurons were present in unparalyzed animals that had been treated with Jun11787. This data indicates that EV-D68 destroys motor neurons and that treatment with Jun11787 prevents the development of paralysis by sparing motor neurons. These results are consistent with previously published data showing that EV-D68 specifically targets motor neurons in the spinal cord11; even when EV-D68 is injected directly into the cerebrum, viral titer is only present in the brain for a short time following infection, subsequently moving to the spinal cord before the onset of paralytic symptoms. Significantly, Jun11787 even improved the final paralysis score (compared to control-treated animals) when treatment was initiated after the onset of paralysis (4-6 DPI). The antiviral efficacy of Jun11787 in the clinical treatment model is comparable to human IVIG, which is the current standard of care. Overall, Jun11787 and Jun11695 represent promising antiviral drug candidates for the prevention and treatment of EV-D68 infection.

All drug treatments were administered via daily intraperitoneal injection in the various mouse models (immediate treatment, 24-hour delayed treatment, and clinical treatment models). While Supplementary Fig. 9 shows that Jun11787 and Jun11695 have promising oral PK profiles in adult mice, daily oral gavage was not possible in neonatal mice. Therefore, the mouse paralysis models used for this study do not perfectly mimic the PK profiles that may be seen if patients are given oral therapy for mild EV-D68 infection. While most patients infected with EV-D68 will experience mild respiratory symptoms, which will resolve without the need for medical intervention, a small subset of patients will develop a progressive and irreversible paralytic disease called AFM in which EV-D68 infects and kills motor neurons in the spinal cord. Patients who present at the clinic with AFM often require prolonged hospitalization. Intraperitoneal injection of antiviral drug straight into systemic circulation in our AFM mouse model (negating the first pass effect) is very similar to the therapeutic approach to an AFM patient who is admitted to the hospital and receives antiviral therapy via IV drip. Therefore, the paralytic mouse models used in this study more closely mimic the PK profile of a hospitalized AFM patient than a patient sent home with oral antivirals for a respiratory infection.

Likewise, mice in our AFM mouse model were infected with EV-D68 virus via intramuscular injection into the quadriceps muscle. Intramuscular infection represents a different route of infection than viral infection of the respiratory tract by airborne droplets in humans. Therefore, the kinetics of viral spread and/or the immune response may differ between our AFM mouse model and humans. However, by the time human AFM patients present at the hospital, they already have an active viral infection in the CNS (where virus is actively killing motor neurons), and since EV-D68 induced AFM is a far greater concern than respiratory illness, we suggest that our AFM mouse model closely mimics the human AFM patient who presents at the hospital with an active infection in the CNS. Intramuscular infection of mice reliably leads to infection and loss of motor neurons in the CNS and therefore this model is clinically relevant to AFM and is better suited to testing the efficacy of antiviral drugs against active viral infection in the CNS than to testing antiviral efficacy against the prodromal respiratory illness.

Capsid inhibitors are generally regarded as a class of antivirals prone to rapid resistance development. Antiviral drug resistance is an unavoidable challenge, and no antiviral is exempt from this issue. In our study, we demonstrate that the therapeutic potential of capsid inhibitors can be enhanced through a combination therapy, supported by synergistic antiviral effects with telaprevir in cell-based assays. We are actively pursuing further drug combination experiments with our capsid inhibitors in a separate study. Importantly, the threat of resistance should not hinder antiviral development, especially for EV-D68, where therapeutic options are scarce. Few drug-like molecules have shown in vivo efficacy, and no candidates are currently in clinical trials. Given the association of EV-D68 with serious illnesses such as AFM, there remains a critical need for effective antivirals. In our EV-D68 neurological infection model, neonatal mice were used, and daily oral gavage in 2-day-old pups is technically challenging and carries a high risk of mortality. As such, oral administration is impractical for dosing compounds in this model. As a result, the VP1 capsid inhibitors Jun11695 and Jun11787 were administered via i.p. injection. Since adult mice do not develop paralysis upon EV-D68 infection, there is currently no suitable disease model that permits evaluation of antiviral efficacy through oral gavage. Nonetheless, we demonstrated that both Jun11695 and Jun11787 achieved favorable in vivo drug exposure following oral administration in BALB/c mice (Supplementary Fig. 9), indicating their potential for development as orally available therapeutics when needed.

Briefly, the serial passages were performed in the presence of DMSO or increasing concentrations of testing compounds, as shown in Fig. 3. RD cells were infected with EV-D68 US/MO/14-18947 at an MOI of 0.1. Generally, the virus was harvested after 3 days, as a significant cytopathic effect was observed. For the passage without observing the cytopathic effect for more than 5 days, the experiment was repeated, treating with a lower concentration of the testing compound. The drug sensitivity of the passage P4 virus from the Jun11695 serial passage and the passage P5 virus from the Jun11787 serial passage were determined following the procedure of the CPE assay and plaque assay.

The genome sequences were then determined following the procedure as previously described30. Briefly, the viral RNA was first purified using a QIAamp viral RNA minikit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Reverse transcriptions were then performed according to the manufacturer protocol using SuperScript III first strand reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen) with an oligo(dT) primer, followed by PCR amplification. All four capsid proteins, VP1, VP2, VP3, and VP4, were then sequenced by GENEWIZ (AZENTA Life Science) with the reported primers30.

The primers of mutant residues were obtained using QuikChange Primer Design (Agilent) and ordered from Integrated DNA Technologies. By using a modified plasmid-based reverse genetic system for EV-D68 US/MO/14-18947 generated in a pHH21 vector ordered from GeneScript, the mutations on the capsid proteins were introduced via site-directed mutagenesis with an Agilent Technologies Quik-Change II XL kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol and confirmed by sequencing following the procedure of viral genome sequencing.

RD cells were infected with R-WT, rMO VP1-V207I & VP3-V192AF separately at a MOI of 0.01 in a 6-well plate. After aspiration and washing away the inoculant virus, 4 mL fresh medium was added. Amplified viruses in the supernatant were harvested at 24, 48, and 72 hpi, and the viral titers were determined following the procedure of plaque reduction assay.

The viral competition growth assay was carried out as previously described30,40,41. The same volume of 560 PFU R-WT and 56,000 PFU rMO VP1-V207I & VP3-V192A was mixed to get a virus mixture with a ratio of 1:100 as passage P0. Progeny virus in the supernatant was harvested at 48 hpi and quantified following the procedure of plaque assay. 28,000 PFU progeny viruses were used for the next passage. After 4 passages, the sequence coding VP1 and VP3 capsid proteins of each passage was determined following the procedure of viral genome sequencing. The proportional percentage of R-WT and the recombinant virus was estimated by measuring the height of the nucleotide sequence electropherogram peak of each mutant residue.

The EV-D68 US/MO/14-18947 isolate was used for structural studies. RD cells were grown in 150 mm dishes to 80% confluency and infected with EV-D68 at an MOI of 0.01. Three days after infection, RD cells were collected along with supernatants and centrifuged at 9,500 rpm for 1 hour at 4 °C in a Beckman JA-10 rotor. Cells were lysed by multiple freeze/thaw cycles and spun at 9,500 rpm for 50 min at 4 °C in a Beckman JLA-16.25 rotor. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.2 µm filter and pelleted at 48,000 rpm for 2 h at 4 °C in a Ti50.2 rotor. Pellets were resuspended overnight in 250 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 250 mM NaCl buffer, and treated with final concentrations of 5 mM MgCl2, DNase (0.01 mg/mL; Sigma Aldrich), trypsin (0.8 mg/mL), 15 mM EDTA, and 1% n-lauryl-sarcosine. The virus was pelleted at 48,000 rpm for 2 hrs at 4 °C in a Ti50.2 rotor and incubated overnight in 250 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 250 mM NaCl buffer. The resuspended virus pellet was purified using a 10–40% potassium tartrate gradient spun at 36,000 rpm for 2 h at 4 °C. The pure virus was visualized as a blue band in the tube. The virus was extracted, and the buffer was exchanged with 1X NTE buffer (20 mM tris, 120 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA).

For structural studies, 11 µl of purified virus was mixed with 1 µl of compound at a concentration of 10 µM in 10% DMSO and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Lacey Carbon grids (Ted Pella) were glow discharged for 60 s at 25 mA using an easiGlow glow discharger (Ted Pella) and 3 µl of sample was applied. After a 15 s incubation at 22 °C and 95% humidity, grids were blotted from the front side for 4 s and plunged into liquid ethane at -185 °C using a Leica GP2 (Leica) and subsequently stored in liquid nitrogen. Data was collected using a Titan Krios G1 equipped with a BioQuantum K3 camera using either Leginon or EPU. Movies were collected at a nominal magnification of 64,000x in super resolution mode, resulting in a pixel size of 0.664 Å with an exposure time of 3.12 s fractioned over 40 frames with a flux of 20e/ppx/s, corresponding to a cumulative dose of 35.4 e/Å. The energy filter slit width was at 20 eV. Additional details for each dataset are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

All datasets were processed using the cryoSPARC Live workflow for motion correction, CTF estimation, and particle picking using a blob-based picker. After multiple rounds of 2D classification, the classes containing full particles were selected and an initial model with no symmetry was generated using the ab-inito model workflow in cryoSPARC. Subsequently, all particles were refined against this model using a homogenous refinement workflow, including refinement of per particle defocus and group CTF parameters, resulting in 2.3 Å structures for all four inhibitors as judged by the FSC 0.143 criteria.

Model building was initiated by placing the model from 7T9P (native virus) in the density map and optimizing the fit in ChimeraX. Inhibitor models and restrains were generated using phenix.elbow and the inhibitor molecule was fitted in the corresponding density in ChimeraX. After merging of the models, model building was done using the ISOLDE plugin in ChimeraX and models were refined using phenix.real_space_refine, followed by a validation step using phenix.validation_cryoem and the PDB validation server. This procedure was repeated until convergence.

Female BALB/c mice were purchased from Taconic Biosciences, Inc (Germantown, NY). All mice were housed under conditions of controlled temperature (22 °C) with on–off light cycle, with food and water provided ad libitum. 14 female BALB/c mice with body weight of around 20 g were randomized into two groups. The mice were given Jun11787 or Jun11695 through i.p injection at the dose of 50 mg/kg (10% DMSO, 60% PEG400, 30% of 20% hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin). Blood samples (10 µl) were collected through tail vein using heparinized capillary tubes at the time points of 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12 h. Plasma was prepared through centrifugation at 10000 g for 5 min and stored at -30°C until analysis. All animal studies were approved by the IACUC at the University of Arizona (Protocol number 17–355).

The plasma samples (5 µl each) were mixed with 45 µl methanol and 10 µl of internal standard (IS; UAWJ102 100 ng/ml in methanol). The mixture was vortexed for 30 s, centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 5 min, then the supernatant was diluted with 900 µl Milli Q and loaded onto an ISOLUTE® C18 SPE Columns (1 ml/100 mg, Biotage, Salem, NH) for Jun11695 or an OASIS® HLB cartridge (C18, 1 ml/30 mg, Waters Corporation, Milford, MA) for Jun11787, pre-conditioned with 1 ml methanol followed by 1 ml water, and the cartridge was then washed with 1 ml water. The analytes were finally eluted from the cartridges with 1 ml methanol, 2 µl of each was used for HPLC-MS/MS analysis.

The plasma concentration of Jun11695 or Jun11787 at each time point was determined by LC-MS/MS using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). For quantification of each compound, a calibration curve was constructed using the pure Jun11695 or Jun11787 via linear regression. The correlation coefficient values of both compounds in plasma were >0.99 for a range of 10-10000 ng/ml. The limit of Quantification (LOQ) was 10 ng/ml with the signal to noise ratio >10:1. The LC-MS system consisted of an Agilent 1290 UPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) and a Sciex Qtrap6500+ Mass Spectrometer (AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA). Analytes were separated on an EclipsePlus C18 column (2.1×50 mm, 1.8 µm, Agilent) at temperature of 35 °C, with mobile phase A containing 0.1% formic acid (v/v) in water and mobile phase B containing 0.1% formic acid (v/v) in acetonitrile. Elution was at a flow rate of 0.3 ml/min as follows: 10% B (0-0.5 min), 10% B → 95% B (0.5-3.5 min), 95% B (3.5-6.5 min), 95% B → 10% B (6.5-6.6 min), 10% B (6.6-8 min). The MS was operated in the positive ion mode, using electrospray ionization. The ion spray voltage and temperature were set at 5000 V and 500 °C, respectively. Curtain gas, ion source gas1 and ion source gas 2 were set at 40, 40, 10 psi, respectively. Jun11695 and the IS were detected using Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM), with a dwell time of 200 msec per transition, at m/z 395.0/107.0 and 289.1/139.1, respectively. Retention times for Jun11695 and the IS were 5.48 and 1.66 min, respectively. Jun11787 and the IS were also detected using MRM, with a dwell time of 200 msec per transition, at m/z 455.0/121.2, and 289.1/139.1, respectively. Retention times for Jun11787 and the IS were 4.18 and 1.66 min, respectively. For quantitative analysis of Jun11695 and Jun11787, pure compounds (10 to 10000 ng/mL in 5 µl methanol), along with 10 µl IS (at 100 ng/ml in methanol), were added to 5 µl of blank mouse plasma to construct the calibration curve.

The pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated using PK solver (Microsoft, Redmond, WA) by assuming a noncompartmental model.

Litter size was kept constant between 9 and 11 animals per dam by mixing litters of the same age. A mixture of male and female mouse pups were used for all experiments. When pup weight was between 1.5-2.0 grams (Postnatal day one or two) Swiss Webster mouse pups were intramuscularly (I.M.) injected with 10 µL of EV-D68 at 10,000xTCID50/pup into the left quadriceps muscle. Animals were infected with EV-D68 isolate US/IL/14-18952, which was purchased from BEI resources NR-49131 LOT#63205985. Drug was dissolved in 10% DMSO and mixed with 90% corn oil immediately prior to intraperitoneal injection at a concentration of 50 mg/kg (25 µL total injection volume per pup). “No drug” treated controls were dosed intraperitoneally with 25 µL of 90% corn oil + 10% DMSO. Pups were checked once daily to record paralysis scores and weight for each animal. For the immediate treatment model treatment was initiated immediately following i.m. viral infection and continued daily for 14 days. For the 24-hour treatment model, treatment was initiated at 24 h post-infection and continued once daily for the next 13 days. For the clinical treatment model, treatment was initiated when an individual pup was observed to have a paralysis score greater than or equal to 1.5 and then continued daily until the end of the experiment. On average pups began treatment at 4–6 days post-infection. Animal weights, paralysis scores, and any deaths were recorded daily for 14 days. All mice were maintained in an Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC)-accredited animal facility at the University of Colorado. All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Colorado under protocol number B-34716 (03) 1E. Animals were housed at 23 °C, 43% relative humidity, and kept on a 10- to 14-hr dark/light cycle. The virus challenge experiments were conducted at ABSL2 facilities.

Paralysis scores were assessed as previously published by Hixon et al20, with the exception that in rare instances, a score of 1.5 or 2.5 was used to better capture limb paralysis that was borderline between bins. Briefly, each limb was scored from 0–3, where 0 represents normal limb movement and 3 represents complete loss of function in the limb. The score from each limb was added together to give a total paralysis score up to 12 for a completely paralyzed, quadriplegic animal. An animal was sacked if its paralysis score was ≥11 or if its weight dropped below 60% of the litter average.

Rhabdomyosarcoma cells (ATCC CCL-136) were plated at 3000 cells/well into 2 sets of 5 columns in a 96-well flat-bottomed cell culture plate. After 24 h, serial 10-fold dilutions of EV-D68 were added to the appropriate wells and incubated at 33 °C for 7 days. All wells were examined for signs of cytopathic effect and TCID50 (50% tissue culture infectious dose) values were calculated using the Reed-Muench method. All TCID50 values in this paper represent TCID50/mL even if it is not explicitly stated.

Spinal cord tissue was collected from Swiss Webster pups at 8 days post-infection, in the 24 h delayed treatment model. Spinal cords were placed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, briefly rinsed in PBS, and then soaked in 30% sucrose for 48 h. Tissue was then frozen in OCT and the lumbar enlargement was sectioned at 20 um using a cryotome. Standard IHC protocol for fresh frozen tissue was followed. Antigen retrieval was performed for 30 min using 10 mM citrate buffer at pH 6.0. Sections were blocked in (0.1% triton, 5% NGS, 1% BSA, 0.05% tween20, in PBS) for 1 hour, then primary antibodies were added to blocking buffer as follows: anti-NeuN mAb from Abcam (ab279297) used at 1:100; anti-ChAT mAb from Abcam ([EPR16590] ab178850) used at 1:50. Samples were incubated in primary antibody for 24 h at room temperature. Nuclear stain was Hoechst 33342, trihydrochloride used at 1 µg/ml.

JMP®, Version 15.0.0. SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, 1989–2007, was used for all statistical calculations pertaining to the paralysis mouse model. We did not observe sex differences in survival or paralysis for any experiment and all experiments included a mix of male and female mice. We did observe litter effects, so pups were mixed between litters (whenever possible) and all litters included a 50/50 mix of treated and control animals. A total of 30 animals were tested in the 24-hour delayed treatment model, 33 in the clinical treatment model, and 45 animals were used for tissue collection. Any animal that died before the end of the experiment had the last observed paralysis score carried forward until the end of the experiment. The weight for animals that died prior to the end of the experiment was estimated using a trendline based on the last 5 days of recorded weight data. Estimated animal weights were used for statistical analysis but not for generating figures.
