# Differential Impact of Cholecystokinin and Ghrelin on 2-Year Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder Symptoms.
**Authors:** Trolio V, Scharner S, Tabri N, Lee H, Burton-Murray H, Becker KR, Kambanis PE, Breithaupt L, Gydus J, Holsen L, Misra M, Eddy KT, Micali N, Lawson EA, Thomas JJ
**Journal:** J Clin Psychiatry (2026)
**DOI:** [10.4088/JCP.25m16046](https://doi.org/10.4088/JCP.25m16046)

## Content

Objective: Individuals with avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) commonly endorse lacking interest in eating/food, difficulties recognizing hunger, and early satiation. Cholecystokinin (CCK) and ghrelin may be important biologic mechanisms in ARFID given their role in appetite signaling. We investigated whether youth with ARFID-lack of interest had greater CCK and lower ghrelin levels compared to youth without this presentation across timepoints and whether greater reductions in CCK and increases in ghrelin were associated with ARFID symptom improvement over a two-year period. Methods: One hundred youth (49% female, mean [SD] age=15.87 [3.89] years, mean [SD] body mass index percentile=37.71 [35.16]) recruited from a single-center clinic and surrounding community, initially meeting DSM-5 criteria for full/subthreshold ARFID completed the Pica, ARFID, and Rumination Disorder Interview at baseline and follow-up over two years (July 2016-September 2022) to determine ARFID diagnosis, presentation type, and severity scores. We obtained fasting plasma levels of CCK and ghrelin at each timepoint. Results: Youth with ARFID-lack of interest had higher CCK than those without this presentation, though there were no differences in fasting ghrelin. Greater decreases in CCK from Baseline to Years 1 and 2 were associated with decreases in lack of interest severity at Years 1 and 2, whereas greater increases in ghrelin from Baseline to Years 1 and 2 were associated with decreases in ARFID severity at Years 1 and 2. Conclusion: In the first longitudinal study of neuroendocrine abnormalities in individuals with ARFID, we found that decreases in CCK and increases in ghrelin were associated with improvements in ARFID symptomology.

Avoidant/restrictive food intake (ARFID) is a psychiatric condition characterized by severe dietary restriction in terms of amount and/or variety of food intake leading to serious physical and/or psychosocial consequences.1 Eating disturbances observed in ARFID are not effortful attempts to influence body shape/weight, and instead are typically attributable to extreme sensory sensitivity, fears of aversive consequences of eating, and/or lack of interest in eating/food. Estimates of the population prevalence of ARFID in children and adolescents vary from 0.3%−15.5%.2 Though individuals with ARFID experience significant medical, psychological, and social impairments, research on mechanisms underlying eating behaviors in ARFID remains limited.

ARFID is a heterogeneous disorder comprised of distinct but overlapping presentations that may have unique neurobiological underpinnings.3 Of the three presentations described in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (5th ed., text rev),1 the lack of interest presentation (ARFID-lack of interest) is one of the most common amongst treatment-seeking samples.4,5 Individuals with ARFID-lack of interest endorse lacking a drive for eating/food, finding eating to be a chore, forgetting to eat, and feeling satiated after eating small portions of food. This manifestation may contribute to long-term faltering growth,5 with greater lack of interest symptom severity at baseline predicting greater persistence of an ARFID diagnosis at two-year follow-up.6 Consequently, there is a significant need to understand processes underlying this eating disturbance to pursue novel treatment development.

Neuroendocrine abnormalities have been proposed to contribute to the onset and maintenance of these disturbances.3 Our team has observed atypical levels of appetite-regulating hormones in individuals with ARFID compared to healthy controls and other eating disorders.7–9 Amongst these, cholecystokinin (CCK) is an anorexigenic (satiety-promoting) peptide hormone secreted from the small intestine that rises during and immediately following food consumption in healthy individuals.10 When exogenously administered before a meal, CCK reduces meal size in both humans and non-human animals and leads to earlier reporting of satiety in humans, whereas administration of CCK antagonists results in increased food intake in animals.11,12 Our team has previously found that youth with full/subthreshold ARFID have significantly higher fasting levels of CCK compared to healthy controls,13 suggesting that atypically elevated levels of CCK may contribute to early satiation and lack of interest in eating in individuals with ARFID.

In contrast, ghrelin is an orexigenic (hunger-stimulating) peptide hormone that is secreted from the stomach. In healthy individuals, ghrelin rises shortly before and quickly decreases following food consumption and has been shown to increase energy intake when administered exogenously before a meal,10,14 suggesting a direct involvement in stimulating food consumption. In individuals with anorexia nervosa, fasting levels of ghrelin are elevated compared to healthy controls, suggesting an adaptive response to the chronic malnutrition and low-weight status characteristic of this population.15 Despite endorsing restrictive eating as severe as individuals with anorexia nervosa,16 individuals with ARFID have unexpectedly lower fasting levels of ghrelin compared to individuals with anorexia nervosa of similarly low weight, and comparable levels to those observed in healthy controls.9 Thus, unexpectedly low levels of ghrelin may contribute to the absence of hunger cues and drive to eat in individuals with ARFID.

Growing evidence suggests atypical appetite signalling in individuals with ARFID, though studies have yet to investigate how neuroendocrine abnormalities relate to specific ARFID symptoms or presentations. As individuals with ARFID-lack of interest report lacking hunger, feeling full quickly, and forgetting to eat, dysregulation in hormones that signal satiation or hunger (like CCK and ghrelin) may be particularly relevant in the pathophysiology of this presentation. Existent studies have been limited in their use of cross-sectional designs, though exploring longitudinal relationships between ARFID symptoms and appetite-regulating hormones can clarify which features of this disorder are impacted by atypical appetite signalling, and whether neuroendocrine disturbances may be relevant treatment targets.

Thus, in a sample of male and female youth with full/subthreshold ARFID over a two-year period, we investigated (1) whether youth with the lack of interest differed in their fasting CCK and ghrelin compared to youth without, and (2) whether changes in fasting CCK and ghrelin were associated with lack of interest symptom severity and (3) ARFID severity over time. We hypothesized that (1) youth with the lack of interest profile would have significantly higher fasting CCK and lower fasting ghrelin than youth without across timepoints, and (2) decreases in fasting CCK and increases in fasting ghrelin over time would be associated with decreases in lack of interest symptom severity and (3) ARFID severity at follow-ups.

This single-centre longitudinal cohort study recruited youth with full or subthreshold ARFID from Boston, Massachusetts between 2016 and 2021 to participate in a National Institute of Mental Health-funded study (R01MH108595) approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board. Data collection occurred from July 2016 through September 2022. Eligibility was determined at a screening visit based on meeting full DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for ARFID on the Eating Disorder Assessment for DSM-5 (EDA-5)17 or endorsing ARFID symptoms on the Kiddie-Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia-Present and Lifetime Version18 at Baseline. Full ARFID diagnoses were assigned via the Pica, ARFID, and Rumination Disorder Interview (PARDI)19 when all diagnostic criteria were met; subthreshold ARFID was defined by restricted food intake by volume and/or variety without reaching diagnostic criteria at the required severity level on the PARDI (e.g., insufficient caloric intake to meet daily energy needs due to lack of interest in eating/food, though the restriction did not result in the degree of faltering growth or nutritional deficiency required on the PARDI). Presence of an eating or feeding disorder besides ARFID and/or clinically significant disordered eating symptoms were determined based on meeting criteria for a non-ARFID eating disorder on the EDA-5 during the screening visit, or exceeding the clinical cut-off score on the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (i.e., >4.0 Global Score).20

Additional exclusion criteria included a medical history of intellectual disability, psychosis, gastrointestinal tract surgery, contraindications for fMRI (as part of the larger study), current suicidal ideation, substance/alcohol use disorder, pregnancy, breastfeeding within the past eight weeks, and use of systemic hormones. Participants were permitted to have previously sought treatment for eating/feeding difficulties, and a portion of the current sample at Baseline (33%) reported receiving some form of treatment in their lifetime (e.g., psychotherapy, tube feeding).

After procedures were fully explained, participants aged 18 years and older provided written consent, and participants less than 18 years provided both verbal assent and parental written consent to participate.

Participants completed three study visits over a two-year period occurring ~12-months apart (M[SD] = 12.0 [3.0]). Baseline sessions occurred within eight weeks of the screening visit, and follow-up visits were conducted in-person at Massachusetts General Hospital, by phone, or via Zoom. Before study visits, participants fasted overnight (water permitted). Trained nursing staff collected blood samples and anthropomorphic measures. The PARDI was administered by a post-bachelor, PARDI-trained research assistant or doctorate-level psychologist to determine ARFID diagnostic status, presentation type, and severity scores at each timepoint. Weekly diagnostic case conferences, chaired by a licensed clinical psychologist, reviewed clinical interview scoring and verified diagnoses (prior work with a subset of the current sample demonstrated inter-rater reliability of κ = 0.75).19

The PARDI is a semi-structured, multi-informant interview that evaluates current (i.e., past month) DSM-5 criteria for pica, ARFID, and rumination disorder and provides continuous severity scores for all three disorders and ARFID presentations. The ARFID severity (PARDI-severity) and lack of interest severity (PARDI-lack of interest) scores were used in the current study. The PARDI-severity scale evaluates severity of food avoidance/restriction and associated medical/psychosocial consequences (e.g., “Over the past month, has there been concern (e.g., from doctors, family) that you are having difficulty meeting your calorie needs [by eating]?”) while the PARDI-lack of interest scale evaluates the qualitative experience of lack of appetite/interest in eating (e.g., “Over the past month, have you had a good appetite (i.e. when food is in front of you, you feel like you want to eat it)?”). Clinical cut-off scores were used to determine participant ARFID presentation type.21

Blood samples were placed on ice following fasting blood draws and spun in a refrigerated centrifuge. Plasma samples were stored at −80°C until retrieved for analysis and were sent in the same shipment to avoid potential batch effects. Enzyme linked immunosorbent assays were used to determine plasma total CCK (RayBio: Peachtree Corners, GA; intra-assay CV <10% and inter-assay CV <15%; lower limit of detection 0.2 pg/mL) and ghrelin (EMD Millipore: Burlington, MA; intra-assay coefficient of variation [CV] 1.32% and inter-assay CV 6.62%; lower limit detection 50.0 pg/mL) levels.

Analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics software (Version 27.0). Regression assumptions were assessed prior to conducting analyses. All assumptions were satisfied except the assumption of homoscedasticity in regression models with PARDI-lack of interest as the dependent variable. A log-transformation was applied to PARDI-lack of interest scores and the assumption was satisfied. Univariate outliers were identified and removed using Tukey’s method for analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs), and influential multivariate outliers were identified and removed if above the critical value (i.e., 4/n) of Cook’s distance for regression analyses. Univariate outliers were removed instead of recoded due to the small number of affected values and the distributional extremity or within-person implausibility of the values.

To test whether youth with ARFID-lack of interest differed in fasting CCK and ghrelin compared to youth without this presentation, we conducted ANCOVAs at each timepoint, controlling for age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) percentile given their potential influence on endocrine functioning.22 Only participants meeting full criteria for ARFID at Baseline were included in ANCOVA analyses given research suggesting differences between full- and subthreshold ARFID on lack of interest and ARFID severity scores.19 To test whether changes in fasting CCK and ghrelin from Baseline to Years 1 and 2 were associated with PARDI-severity and PARDI-lack of interest at Years 1 and 2, we conducted linear regressions with hormone change scores as independent variables and PARDI scores as dependent variables. Covariates included age, sex, BMI percentile, and Baseline PARDI scores to account for baseline illness severity and reduce residual variance. Participants with both full- and subthreshold ARFID at Baseline were included given the continuous nature of these analyses.

Missing data were addressed via listwise deletion. We conducted binary logistic regressions to determine whether key Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics predicted hormone data missingness (0 = missing, 1 = present) at follow-up (see Table 1 for results and data availability). Logistic regression models were non-significant (χ2s [7] = 8.15–13.90, ps = .05–.32, Nagelkerke R2s = .12–.20) and did not identify significant predictors of missingness (except for ARFID status on Year 1 ghrelin missingness, p = .04), suggesting that Baseline characteristics do not predict missing data at follow-up (i.e., available data are likely representative of the larger sample).

One hundred youth aged 9–23 years (Mage 15.9 [SD = 3.88]; 49% female) with full (n = 88) or subthreshold (n = 12) ARFID were recruited at Baseline. At Year 1, 78 participants completed a study visit, and 74 at Year 2. Hormone data availability was less than total participant sessions due to restrictions on in-person sessions during the COVID-19 pandemic, inability to sample enough blood, or participant refusal at time of blood draw. Thus, 56 participants were included in analyses at Year 1 and 43 at Year 2. Table 2 presents demographic information.

Table 3 presents results of ANCOVAs. Consistent with hypotheses, participants that met the cut-off for ARFID-lack of interest had significantly higher fasting CCK at Year 1 compared to those that did not meet the cut-off, with a large effect (ηp2 = .180). However, there was no significant difference between the groups in fasting CCK at Baseline or Year 2. Contrary to hypotheses, there were no differences in fasting ghrelin between participants that met versus did not meet cut-off for ARFID-lack of interest across timepoints.

Table 4 presents results of longitudinal linear regressions. Consistent with hypotheses, changes in fasting CCK from Baseline to Years 1 and 2 were associated with changes in lack of interest severity scores across timepoints (Adj. R2 = .41, F[1, 41] = 4.36, p = .043, and Adj. R2 = .62, F[1, 30] = 7.00, p = .013, respectively). Specifically, greater decreases in fasting CCK from Baseline to Years 1 and 2 were associated with greater decreases in lack of interest severity scores at Years 1 (f2 = 0.11) and 2 (f2 = .23), respectively. Contrary to hypotheses, changes in fasting ghrelin from Baseline to Years 1 and 2 were not associated with changes in lack of interest severity scores at Years 1 (Adj. R2 = .38, F[1, 42] = 3.91, p = .06) nor 2 (Adj. R2 = .50, F[1, 29] = 1.56, p = .22).

Against hypotheses, changes in fasting CCK from Baseline to Years 1 and 2 were not associated with changes in ARFID severity scores at Years 1 (Adj. R2 = .27, F[1, 40] = 1.86, p = .18) nor 2 (Adj. R2 = .36, F[1, 30] = 0.39, p = .54). In line with hypotheses, changes in fasting ghrelin from Baseline to Years 1 and 2 were inversely associated with changes in ARFID severity scores from Baseline to Years 1 and 2 (Adj. R2 = .35, F[1, 41] = 6.00, p = .02, and Adj. R2 = .50, F[1, 28] = 6.38, p = .02, respectively). Specifically, greater increases in fasting ghrelin from Baseline to Years 1 and 2 were associated with greater decreases in ARFID severity scores at Years 1 (f2 = .15) and 2 (f2 = .23), respectively.

This is the first study to explore appetite-regulating hormones in relation to ARFID presentations and as predictors of longitudinal symptom trajectories. We assessed fasting CCK and ghrelin in youth with and without the ARFID-lack of interest presentation at three timepoints over two-years. Consistent with hypotheses, youth with ARFID-lack of interest had higher fasting CCK levels than youth without, though only at one timepoint. Against hypotheses, we found no differences in fasting ghrelin between groups at any timepoint. We also investigated whether changes in fasting CCK and ghrelin over two years predicted changes in lack of interest and ARFID symptom severity at follow-ups. As expected, decreases in CCK over time were associated with improvements in lack of interest symptom severity, though not ARFID symptom severity. Conversely, increases in ghrelin over time were associated with improvements in ARFID symptom severity, though not lack of interest severity. Importantly, these results were observed when accounting for factors that may influence appetite hormones (i.e., age, sex, BMI percentile), suggesting that the results cannot be accounted for by these potential confounders. Results suggest differential roles of CCK and ghrelin in ARFID pathophysiology, such that CCK may contribute to lack of interest symptoms, while ghrelin may relate to ARFID severity.

Given the role of CCK in early satiety, inhibited gastric emptying, and reduced meal intake,10,23 we posit that CCK is particularly relevant to the ARFID-lack of interest presentation. We found that youth with this presentation had higher fasting CCK than youth without at certain stages of illness, and that greater reductions in CCK over time were associated with improvements in lack of interest symptoms. CCK’s role in promoting early satiation and inappropriate feelings of fullness may be central to its relationship with lack of interest symptoms. CCK does not appear to delay meal initiation but instead reduces meal sizes without necessarily reducing daily energy intake.10 This effect may explain its persistent association to lack of interest severity, which captures subjective experiences of early satiety, fullness, and boredom during a mealtime, even when controlling for differences in participant BMI percentile, and may also explain CCK’s lack of association to ARFID severity, which captures concerns meeting energy/nutritional needs and medical/psychosocial consequences of eating difficulties. Though CCK typically peaks within the first hour following food consumption,10 individuals with ARFID – particularly those with ARFID-lack of interest – have elevated levels of CCK at fasting,13 likely contributing to the chronic low appetite in this population. As fasting CCK decreases over time, feelings of satiation may reduce while appetite (i.e., interest in eating/food) improves, reflecting symptom improvements at follow-up. Given this association, future studies should investigate factors that may reduce CCK in individuals with ARFID.

Unlike CCK, we did not find a relationship between ghrelin and lack of interest symptoms in group comparisons or longitudinal regressions. Instead, longitudinal increases in ghrelin were associated with decreases in ARFID severity. Ghrelin is considered a hunger-stimulating hormone given its pre-prandial rise and post-prandial fall, and evidence demonstrating decreased energy intake following ghrelin antagonist administration and increased intake following agonist and ghrelin administration, including in humans with malnutrition.14,24,25 Interestingly, ghrelin appears to relate more to meal initiation than size,10 which may explain its relation to ARFID severity rather than lack of interest. Specifically, avoidance of meals may contribute to concrete decreases in daily energy/nutritional intake, leading to significant medical and/or psychosocial consequences (e.g., faltering growth, concerns from family/friends), which are specific to ARFID severity as measured on the PARDI. Regression analyses demonstrated that ARFID severity improved with greater increases in hunger-stimulating ghrelin over time, suggesting that individuals with ARFID may consume more energy/nutrition via more frequent meal initiation in response to increased ghrelin. Future studies should explore mechanisms underlying ghrelin changes and whether ghrelin mediates increased energy/nutritional intake and improved ARFID severity.

This study has several strengths. Foremost, this is the first longitudinal study of neuroendocrine abnormalities in ARFID, enabling the analysis of biological changes alongside ARFID symptom trajectories over time. Further, our sample reflected the relatively equal sex distribution seen in ARFID,26 overcoming a common limitation of eating disorder samples,27 and was diverse in age and weight status. Finally, we measured participant symptoms via rigorous structured interviews, reducing measurement error associated with self-report. Concerning limitations, longitudinal participant attrition – heightened by the COVID-19 pandemic – reduced endocrine data availability at follow-up and potentially limited power in longitudinal analyses. However, baseline characteristics did not predict later data missingness, suggesting that included participants are representative of the larger sample. Moreover, we did not control for presence of other ARFID presentations in our analyses due limited statistical power given our sample size. While co-occurring ARFID presentations are common in treatment settings,5 future work should replicate our findings in larger samples that allow for modelling of presentation overlap. Relatedly, our ANCOVA analyses detected a significant difference in CCK between participants with ARFID-lack of interest and ARFID-other at one timepoint. Though the former group had numerically higher levels of CCK than the latter group at each timepoint (including a difference with a medium effect at Year 2), we were likely underpowered to detect statistically significant differences. Further, given the observational nature of the study, we were unable to test causality. Future research should include experimental studies using hormone receptor agonists or antagonists that can demonstrate whether hormone changes directly lead to symptom changes. Finally, our sample was predominantly White, limiting generalizability to other racial or ethnic groups where cultural factors may affect ARFID symptom presentation. Future studies should prioritize larger, racially diverse cohorts.

Findings support our hypothesis that hormonal abnormalities play a role in the pathophysiology of ARFID, suggesting a biological component to commonly endorsed symptoms. We found that CCK and ghrelin are associated with ARFID symptomology, such that CCK may be implicated in lack of interest symptoms and ghrelin in ARFID severity. These associations highlight potentially targetable biological mechanisms in treating ARFID, for which no standardized pharmacological treatments currently exist.28 CCK antagonists could be explored to reduce inappropriate feelings of fullness and ghrelin agonists evaluated to stimulate hunger and meal onset. Combination therapy targeting these endocrine pathways may be most effective given possible different mechanisms of action. Addition of pharmacological agents to ARFID treatment could bolster effects of existing psychological therapies (e.g., CBT-AR)29 by facilitating volume eating or food exposures. Future research should investigate whether pharmacologic treatments are effective in improving course and outcomes of individuals with ARFID and should examine whether levels of CCK and ghrelin change in response to psychological treatments.
