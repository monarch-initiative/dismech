# Clinical manifestations, diagnosis and treatment of hereditary fibrinogen Aα-chain renal amyloidosis: one case report and systematic review.
**Authors:** He L, Zhou J, Wang M, Chen J, Liu C, Shi J, Rui Y, Wu H
**Journal:** Int Urol Nephrol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s11255-024-04236-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11255-024-04236-w)

## Content

PURPOSE: We reported a confirmed case of Fibrinogen Aa-chain (AFib) amyloidosis and conducted systematic review of the genetic and protein mutation types, clinical manifestations, diagnostic methods and treatment for patients with this disease worldwide.

METHODS: We reported a case of AFib amyloidosis. Meanwhile, a systematic search was performed using defined terms and updated up to November 2023 in the Wanfang, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, VIP, PubMed, and Web of Science databases to identify reported cases of AFib renal amyloidosis worldwide, according to PRISMA guidelines.

RESULTS: A 46-year-old male patient was admitted for more than half a month because of oedematous lower limbs. Renal tissue mass spectrometry suggested an AFib type. Gene detection demonstrated that the patient carried the c.1673del (p.Lys558Argfs*10) locus heterozygous mutation of Fibrinogen Aα-chain gene (FGA). The patient was treated with haemodialysis because of uncontrollable hypertension. This systematic review comprised 46 cases. We found the onset age to be lower in women than in men (P < 0.05). All patients showed incipient symptoms including proteinuria; 10 (21.7%) patients progressed to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) or received renal replacement therapy (including dialysis and kidney transplantation) within 1 year; 18 (39.1%) patients progressed to ESRD or received renal replacement therapy within 1-5 years, and 4 (8.7%) patients did not progress to ESRD or received renal replacement therapy within 5 years.

CONCLUSION: AFib amyloidosis progresses rapidly. The diagnosis of this disease is primarily based on renal biopsy, mass spectrometry, and molecular gene detection. Reducing proteinuria is the main method of treating this disease.

Amyloidosis is a group of diseases induced by the extracellular deposition of insoluble fibers formed by misfolded proteins, leading to structural and functional tissue changes [1]. To date, 36 proteins have been associated with the formation of human amyloid proteins [2]. AFib amyloidosis is an autosomal dominant disease [3, 4] caused by mutations in genes encoding FGA. Its clinical manifestations include proteinuria, hypertension, and renal failure. Said et al. [5] analyzed 474 patients with renal amyloidosis evaluated at the Mayo Clinic Kidney Pathology Laboratory between 2007 and 2011 and discovered only 6 (1.3%) cases of AFib amyloidosis. Therefore, AFib amyloidosis is rare.

At present, domestic and foreign reports on AFib amyloidosis are mostly case reports, while large-sample clinical research is lacking. Medical history characteristics in patients with AFib amyloidosis remain unsummarised with inconsistent evaluation of disease diagnosis and treatment. Consequently, we report a case of AFib amyloidosis and systemically reviewed domestic and foreign case reports and small-sample observational studies to further investigate the clinical manifestations, diagnostic process, and treatment of hereditary AFib amyloidosis.

A 46-year-old male patient was admitted for more than half a month because of oedematous lower limbs. Half a month prior, the patient developed edema with no obvious cause in both lower limbs, which was mild pitting edema showing bilateral symmetry, mild in the morning and severe in the evening, alleviated after rest, aggravated after overwork, and accompanied by foamuria. Routine examination at the local hospital revealed proteinuria 3 + ; liver function: albumin (ALB) 30 g/L; and kidney function: creatinine (Scr) 99 μmol/L. The patient was admitted and treated at our hospital from 6 August 2022 to 16 August 2022. During the duration of hospital stay, proteinuria 3 + , microalbuminuria 1022.60 mg/L, Scr 72.58 μmol/L, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, based on the CKD-EPI equation) 114.08 mL/ min/1.73 m^2, 24-h urinary protein quantity 1869.6 mg/24 h, hemoglobin (Hb) 160 g/L, blood pressure (BP) 193/115 mmHg, total cholesterol (TC) 9.12 mmol/L, triglyceride (TG) 2.26 mmol/L, and uric acid (UA) 496.6 μmol/L were reported.

The patient presented with the clinical manifestations of nephritic syndrome. A renal needle biopsy was performed to determine the disease, and renal biopsy pathology revealed glomerulopathy with special substance deposition; the possibility of renal amyloidosis could not be excluded (Fig. 1). Therefore, mass spectrometry (Fig. 2) and FGA analyses (Table 1) were performed. The results revealed that the patient had AFib amyloidosis and carried a c.1673del (p.Lys558Argfs*10) locus heterogeneous mutation in FGA. Gene-detection reports of patient

Based on the clinical symptoms, laboratory tests, mass spectrometry analysis, and gene-detection results, the patient was finally diagnosed with AFib amyloidosis. Hereditary nature of the disease prompted tracing of the patient’s family history. Consequently, his mother had died of uraemia, and his father was in good health. His mother had three sisters, of which one had been confirmed to have uraemia and was receiving haemodialysis for the time being. She received FGA detection in Shanghai Ruijin Hospital, but a detailed report was unavailable, and mass spectrometry analysis was not performed. One patient died of uraemia, and the other was in good health. The son of the proband underwent genetic testing in November 2022, which revealed the presence of the c.1673del (p.Lys558Argfs*10) locus heterogeneous mutation in FGA. However, routine urine tests did not show any abnormalities, and the patient did not show any clinical symptoms at present. Based on the clinical manifestations, laboratory test results, family history, renal biopsy pathology, mass spectrometry, and gene-detection results, the patient was finally diagnosed with AFib amyloidosis. The patient was treated with nifedipine sustained-release tablets 30 mg twice daily, allisartan isoproxil 240 mg once daily to reduce blood pressure and proteinuria, atorvastatin 20 mg once daily to reduce blood lipid levels, and febuxostat tablets 20 mg once daily to reduce uric acid levels. After the treatment mentioned above, the blood pressure of the patient was controlled; the 24-h urinary protein quantity and microalbuminuria decreased compared with those at admission; however, the creatinine level increased relative to that at admission, and the glomerular filtration rate decreased relative to that before. Thereafter, the microalbuminuria and Scr levels progressively increased, the edema gradually progressed, and the hypertension could hardly be controlled; the BP was still high even after multiple adjustments of antihypertensive drugs, with the systolic pressure fluctuating at 180–200 mmHg, the systolic pressure fluctuating at 100–120 mmHg. Therefore, the patient received haemodialysis therapy starting in October 2023 to improve blood volume and control BP. The BP and edema in both lower limbs improved, and the patient’s condition was stablilised.

This study was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA). We searched for articles with searching terms ‘Fibrinogen A α-Chain Amyloidosis’ OR ‘Fibrinogen Aα-chain variant and Amyloidosis’ OR ‘AFib amyloidosis’ OR ‘hereditary fibrinogen amyloidosis’ in the PubMed,Web of Science, Wanfang, China National Knowledge Infrastructure and VIP databases from inception to November 2023 (Supplemental appendix A).

Inclusion criteria: ① diverse studies related to Afib amyloidosis; ② for duplicate literature from the same medical centre, the recently published article was retained; ③ renal biopsy confirmed renal amyloidosis, identified as the amyloid–fibrinogen α chain mutant.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: ① repeatedly reported cases from different databases; ② cases lacking clinical data; ③ reviews.

Two authors independently reviewed the titles, abstracts, and full texts of the retrieved papers and assessed whether these studies satisfied the inclusion criteria. A third reviewer mediated any disagreement between the reviewers. Of the two studies that described the same patient, the study with more details was selected. The latest literature was retained for duplicate studies. Excel was used to analyze the final patient data from enrolled studies were classified and analyzed using Excel. The following data were collected: age, sex, race, incipient symptoms (such as proteinuria, edema, and nephrotic syndrome), renal biopsy pathology, gene mutation, protein mutation, presence or absence of a family history of renal disease, presence or absence of concurrent hypertension, presence or absence of renal insufficiency at the first onset of disease, extrarenal-involved sites, presence or absence of misdiagnosis, and age of progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) or receiving renal replacement therapy. For patients lacking information on one or more items, vacancies were provided in the d-ing position. We aimed to provide an overview of the clinical manifestations, diagnostic methods, and treatments of AFib amyloidosis; therefore, the risk of bias in the enrolled studies was not considered. Consequently, the methodological quality of the included studies was not formally evaluated.

SPSS software (version 26.0) was used for statistical analysis. Measurement data conforming to normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the Student’s t test between different groups. Measurement data conforming to a non-normal distribution were expressed as median and quartile intervals and compared between groups using the rank sum test. Enumeration data are described as rates. At N ≥ 40 and T ≥ 5, Chi-square test was used for comparison between different groups; at N ≥ 40 and at least one 1 ≤ T < 5, corrected Chi-square test was used, and at N < 40 or T < 1, the Fisher’s exact probability method was adopted. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

A total of 285 related studies were retrieved, of which 209 duplicates were excluded. After screening the titles and abstracts, 21 irrelevant studies were excluded. The full texts of the remaining articles were carefully read, and 25 studies were excluded based on the exclusion criteria (Fig. 4). Thirty studies met the inclusion criteria [3, 6–34], including 28 case reports and two case series involving 45 cases. We statistically analyzed the clinical manifestations of 46 patients (Table 4) and discovered that these 46 patients had the age of onset of 46 ± 14.0 years, and all of them had proteinuria. In addition, 13 (28.3%) patients presented with nephrotic syndrome, 9 (19.6%) had edema in both lower limbs, and 4 (8.7%) had palpebral edema. In addition, 20 patients (43.5%) had renal insufficiency at the time of the first diagnosis. Moreover, 23 patients (50.0%) developed hypertension at the initial onset of the disease or thereafter. Among the patients, 27 (58.7%) had a family history of renal disease, including 9 (19.6%) with a confirmed family history of AFib amyloidosis. After disease onset, the extrarenal organs mainly included the liver (13.3%) and heart (13.3%). Amyloidosis is characterized by the formation of insoluble fibers due to protein misfolding and deposition in the extracellular matrix [1]. Local deposition of these proteins can lead to local amyloidosis, resulting in systemic amyloidosis leading to multiple organ dysfunction, including hereditary amyloidosis. AFib amyloidosis belongs to a kind of hereditary amyloidosis. The disease involves fibrinogen, a protein synthesized by liver cells that primarily helps maintain the elasticity and tension of arteries and veins and has a procoagulant function. Mutations in the gene encoding fibrinogen can cause various diseases, most of which are associated with coagulation [35–38]. Individuals with an abnormal fibrinogen levels or functions typically suffer from an acquired or hereditary fibrinogen deficiencies [39]. Fibrinogen deficiency can be divided into two types, afibrinogenemia and hypofibrinogenaemia, characterized by the absence or reduction of immunoreactive fibrinogen, which can lead to very mild (or even nonexistent) to extremely severe bleeding manifestations [40]. Afibrinogenemia is predominantly an autosomal recessive genetic disorder, whereas hypofibrinogenaemia is inherited in an autosomal dominant or recessive manner. The second is dysfibrinogenemia, which is a functionally abnormal fibrinogen disorder. It is an autosomal dominant inherited disease [40, 41]. Fibrinogen is composed of dysfunctional proteins produced by mutated genes and fibrinogen produced by normal genes. This disease has low penetrance, with some individuals exhibiting abnormal bleeding or thrombosis [42]. The first case of AFib amyloidosis was reported by Benson et al. [3] in 1993, in a Mexican patient of Peruvian descent. The patient exhibited nephrotic syndrome at the age of 36 years, underwent kidney transplantation at the age of 40 years, and enjoyed a healthy life for 8 years after surgery. However, the disease relapsed thereafter, and the patient underwent a second kidney transplantation when he was 50 years old, but finally died of septicaemia regardless of normal transplant function.

As suggested by death certificates from England, the prevalence of amyloidosis is approximately 1 per 100,000 people, with a mortality rate of 0.58 per 1000 people [43]. Wechalekar AD et al. [44] evaluated 5100 patients with British national amyloidosis between 1987 and 2012 and discovered that only 87 (1.7%) patients had AFib amyloidosis. Thus, it can be figured that AFib amyloidosis is a rare disease.

Patients with AFib amyloidosis do not generally exhibit symptoms until adulthood. The initial symptoms may be proteinuria or clinical manifestations of nephrotic syndrome. Some patients may develop hypertension and renal insufficiency or, in some rare cases, extrarenal organ involvement [31]. In our study, all patients displayed proteinuria in the early stage of disease onset, and half of the patients had hypertension. However, this finding differs from that of other common forms of amyloidosis. Patients with Amyloid A (AA) and AL amyloidosis often tend to have low blood pressure, which may be due to the involvement of microvessels in the AA and AL-amyloid deposits. In contrast, Afib amyloidosis primarily affects the glomeruli, leading to water and sodium retention, which can influence blood pressure, thereby resulting in this difference. However, these clinical features are non-specific.

We reviewed the relevant literature to accurately diagnose Afib amyloidosis. We found that the method of diagnosing Afib amyloidosis using immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) staining targeting fibrinogen was not reliable because of its variable degrees of positivity [31, 45]. IHC is commonly used to diagnose AA amyloidosis, whereas IF is used to diagnose AL amyloidosis [46]. In addition,spikes and silver staining are usually negative for Afib [5]. However, if detection is carried out through genotyping and amyloid protein typing for this genetic disease, the accuracy of the results will be greatly improved. Therefore, we believe that the diagnosis of this disease is mainly through renal biopsy puncture, mass spectrometry analysis and molecular genetic testing.Amyloid deposits are stained with Congo red, which will display unique apple green birefringence when observed under the polarized light [47]. Through mass spectroproteome analysis, the protein subunits and accompanying amyloid proteins in the deposits can be identified to determine the subtype [48], whereas molecular gene detection can directly determine the mutation site. The combined application of these three methods can determine disease diagnosis and reduce missed diagnoses and misdiagnosis rates. In addition, a clear family history of hereditary diseases would further confirm this diagnosis; however, in our study, we found that 50% of the patients had a family history of kidney disease and that Afib amyloidosis was autosomal dominant. This result may be related to different patient genotypes. Some studies found that penetrance is more likely to be affected when the genotype is heterozygous [49]. Gorram F etal al [50] found that males have a higher penetrance of transthyretin amyloidosis, which is also an autosomal dominant disorder; however, our study found no difference in disease onset between men and women with this condition. To date, there have been few reports in this area and we look forward to more research to answer this question. In our study, we discovered an interesting finding: Afib amyloidosis is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder. The clinical manifestations and prognosis of the disease are similar between men and women. However, our research found that women tend to develop the disease earlier than men. We reviewed the relevant literature and found that studies have shown that women have higher fibrinogen levels, which may be related to their higher estrogen levels [51]. Therefore, when a mutation occurs in the fibrinogen A alpha chain, women with higher fibrinogen levels may develop the disease earlier. We also analyzed whether there were gender-distribution differences in Afib amyloidosis among different races, and the results suggested that the difference was not statistically significant.In our research, we discovered seven misdiagnosed cases among the 46 cases, all of whom were misdiagnosed with AL. We analysed these seven cases and discovered that the disease was misdiagnosed because mass spectrometry and gene detection were not conducted. Instead, only renal biopsy detection was performed to detect Congo red amyloid protein deposition, bone marrow puncture biopsy suggested λ light chain dominance and manifested as plasma cytopathia, therefore, the disease was speculated to be AL. The patient underwent chemotherapy and stem-cell transplantation. Due to further deterioration of the patient’s renal function, mass spectrometry and gene-detection analyses were performed to determine AFib amyloidosis. According to previous reports, the misdiagnosis of AL amyloidosis is not unique to Afib amyloidosis. In patients with renal dysfunction caused by amyloidosis, an increase in free light chains may lead to brighter non-specific background staining in the renal tissue, which could potentially result in misdiagnosis of AL amyloidosis [52]. Consequently, the disease is mainly diagnosed through renal biopsy, mass spectrometry, and molecular gene detection. While the cause of plasma cytopathia may be a chance factor, which can not be used to diagnose AL [52].

Currently, there is no specific treatment for AFib renal amyloidosis, which is generally managed by mitigating organ injury symptoms, maximally delaying amyloid protein production, restricting further renal injury, and maintaining or improving renal function, such as the reduction of proteinuria, kidney transplantation, and dialysis. Moreover, fibrinogen is mainly synthesized in the liver; therefore, liver transplantation is important in patients with amyloidosis induced by corresponding gene mutations. However, transplantation of the affected organs alone cannot cure this disease. Concurrent liver and kidney transplantation has been proven to eradicate amyloidosis and prevent further deposition of amyloid protein [53]. However, the surgery-related mortality rate is relatively high and its long-term effects have not been verified [54]. In our study, six cases of post-transplant recurrence involved patients who underwent kidney transplantation alone, without combined liver and kidney transplantation. All patients experienced recurrence of Afib amyloidosis within 1–8 years after kidney transplantation, consistent with previous research findings. In addition, more than half of the patients in our study underwent renal replacement therapy within 5 years. However, owing to the lack of follow-up data, the long-term effects cannot be compared.

This study collected case data of patients with AFib amyloidosis reported in different areas as much as possible; however, not all the literature sufficiently described the clinical data of patients, as discovered during data collection. Therefore, one or more data points were lacking, which might have resulted in bias in the research results. Moreover, the follow-up times were different, thereby causing bias in the judgment of disease prognosis. This was a preliminary exploration of global AFib-amyloidosis cases, and the results should be confirmed in well-designed prospective studies.

In summary, for AFib amyloidosis, due to non-specific clinical manifestations, it is important to learn how to timely determine the diagnosis and typing to avoid missed diagnosis or misdiagnosis in patients so that the patients can receive timely and precise diagnosis and treatment, thus postponing disease progression.
