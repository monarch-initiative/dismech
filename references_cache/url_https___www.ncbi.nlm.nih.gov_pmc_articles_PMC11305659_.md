</div></section></section><section><section><section><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section><h3>Background</h3>
<p>Hereditary hearing loss is a genetically heterogeneous neurosensory disorder that affects many people. Deafness and infertility can coexist in some cases, creating the hearing impairment infertile male syndrome. There are several known molecular mechanisms that can cause deafness either on its own or in conjunction with infertility.</p></section><section><h3>Methods and Results</h3>
<p>Here, we represent two consanguineous families (A, B), both families had clinical evidence of deafness, and family B also had infertility, so we referred to them as having nonsyndromic hearing loss (NSHL) and hearing impairment infertile male syndrome (HIIMS), respectively. These families’ genetic makeup was examined using an Affymetrix GeneChip 250K Nsp array followed by Sanger sequencing. In family A, we identified a novel homozygous stop gain variant [<a>NM_003672.4</a>; c.1000C&gt;T; p.(Gln334*)] and a homozygous missense variant [<a>NM_003672.4</a>; c.684C&gt;A; p.(Asn228Lys)] in family B in <em>CDC14A</em> gene (MIM#603504). In animal models, the <em>CDC14A</em> gene causes both hearing loss and infertility; in addition, it also causes NSHL and HIIMS in humans.</p></section><section><h3>Conclusions</h3>
<p>Our study on the <em>CDC14A</em> gene has identified two novel variants, crucial for delineating disease boundaries. Variants in exon 10 and upstream cause HIIMS, and those in exon 11 and downstream are linked exclusively to hearing impairment. This precision enhances diagnostics and offers potential for targeted interventions, marking a significant advancement in understanding the genetic basis of these conditions.</p></section><section><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> Profound deafness,
<em>CDC14A</em> gene, SNP array, Hearing impairment infertile male syndrome, Novel variant</p></section></section><section><h2>Introduction</h2>
<p>Hereditary hearing loss in humans is a common genetically heterogeneous neurosensory disorder. Approximately 1/500 infants have congenital hearing loss caused by genetic variants in different genes in at least half (50%) of the cases [<a>1</a>]. Genetic hearing loss is mainly classified according to the onset of the disorder, inheritance type, vestibular phenotype, audiological phenotypes, and responsible gene/locus. It is classified as a monogenic disorder and can be associated with other disorders referred to as syndromic hearing loss (20–30% of cases), and 70% of the cases account for nonsyndromic hearing loss (NSHL) [<a>2</a>]. In addition, 10–20% of cases underlie autosomal dominant inheritance, while 77–93% of cases are reported in an autosomal recessive form. The literature has also reported mitochondrial inheritance and X-linked [<a>3</a>, <a>4</a>].</p>
<p>
<em>CDC14A</em> resides on chromosome 1p21.2 and encodes an evolutionarily conserved protein, tyrosine phosphatase, which belongs to a family of proteins that function in various pathways, including DNA damage response, centrosome duplication cycle and cytokinesis, cell adhesion, Cdk1 activity control and migration, primary cilia dynamics, and transcription regulation [<a>5</a>–<a>7</a>]. CDC14A is a member of the dual-specificity phosphatases family. Dual-specificity phosphatases are a large group of phosphatases that are indispensable regulators of many functions in many tissues, including the testis and inner ear. They are categorized broadly by their capability to dephosphorylate threonine and serine/tyrosine residues and subclassified based on their targets [<a>8</a>]. Disease-causing variants in <em>CDC14A</em> have been associated with HIIMS and NSHL (HGMD). In the present investigation, clinical and molecular analysis of two consanguineous families identified a novel homozygous nonsense variant [c.1000C&gt;T; p.(Gln334*)] in family A and a homozygous missense variant [c.684C&gt;A; p.(Asn228Lys)] in family B in the <em>CDC14A</em> gene.</p></section><section><h2>Material and Methods</h2>
<section><h3>Ethical Approval</h3>
<p>Two families were recruited from the remote areas of Pakistan. In compliance with the Helsinki Declaration, written informed consent was obtained from the parents/guardians for the publication of data and any accompanying images. IRB of Civil Hospital Quetta (IRB-CH-453) and COMSATS University Islamabad, Pakistan approved the research study.</p></section><section><h3>Clinical Investigation</h3>
<p>For each patient from both families (A and B), a pure-tone audiometry test for sensorineural HI was conducted. According to WHO 5th edition recommendations, patients in both families underwent a semen assay because it is the first indicator test for infertility. In order to more assess infertility, a hormonal profile was performed.</p></section><section><h3>Genomic DNA</h3>
<p>DNA was extracted from available affected and normal members of the families using GenElute™ Blood Genomic DNA Kit and quantified using Nanodrop-1000 spectrophotometer using standard methods.</p></section><section><h3>Genotyping</h3>
<p>In families A and B, all the available family members were subjected to SNP analysis through Affymetrix GeneChip 250K <em>Nsp</em> array. Analysis of SNP data was conducted using the online tool AutoSNPa.</p></section><section><h3>
<em>CDC14A</em> Gene Sanger Sequencing</h3>
<p>Primer for Sanger sequencing was designed using primer-3 software. All exons of the <em>CDC14A</em> gene (<a>NM_003672.4</a>) were Sanger sequenced in all available members of each family using standard procedures as described earlier. Sequence variants were analyzed using BIOEDIT sequence alignment editor version 6.0.7.</p></section><section><h3>In silico Analysis</h3>
<p>The pathogenicity of the identified variants was checked using different online pathogenicity detector tools such as Mutation Taster, Polymorphism Phenotyping V2, Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant, and VarSome. The general population frequency was determined using gnomAD, TOPMed Bravo, and ALL of Us. ACMG classification of both variants was accessed through Genetic Variant Interpretation Tool of University of Maryland, Baltimore, USA [<a>9</a>].</p></section></section><section><h2>Results</h2>
<section><h3>Clinical Features</h3>
<p>In the family history of our study, it was documented that at the time of recruitment, all affected members were hearing loss patients. According to the information provided by the elders of the families, the severity of their hearing impairment was reported to be as profound then as it is now. Despite the congenital nature of their condition, none of the affected individuals wore hearing aids or underwent cochlear implantation.</p></section><section><h3>Family A</h3>
<p>A consanguineous pedigree with two affected (IV-1, IV-2) and four unaffected (III-3, III-4, IV-3, and IV-4) members participated in the study (<a>Fig. 1</a>a). Both affected individuals, IV-1 and IV-2, were born via normal vaginal delivery. At the time of the study, affected individual IV-1 was 25, and IV-2 was 23 years old. Both affected individuals revealed bilateral profound sensorineural HI through a pure-tone audiometry test (<a>Fig. 1</a>c). Both patients’ semen analyses revealed normal patterns for sperm count, motility, and morphology and the hormone analysis, including FSH, LH, Prolactin, and Testosterone, for both affected members, was within normal intervals (<a>Table 1</a>).</p>

<div><a>Open in a new tab</a></div></section></section><section><h3>Family B</h3>
<p>In family B, two affected (IV-1, IV-2) and two normal individuals (III-1, IV-3) participated in the study (<a>Fig. 1</a>b). Both affected individuals were born to a consanguineous family normal vaginal delivery and recruited to study at 19 and 22 years, respectively. Through a pure-tone audiometry test, both members were diagnosed with bilateral profound sensorineural HI (<a>Fig. 1</a>d). Semen analysis showed that both patients (IV-1, IV-2) had severe oligozoospermic infertility, which is characterized by an abnormally low sperm count compared to the normal range (<a>Table 1</a>). Hormonal profiles are normal among both affected individuals (<a>Table 1</a>). Both of patients have normal height and developed secondary characteristics. Patient IV-2 engaged in unprotected sexual activity after getting married to a healthy wife 3 years prior. No ciliary dyskinesia phenotypes were observed in affected individuals. Upon clinical and physical examination, no other abnormalities were recorded in all affected individuals who participated in the study.</p></section><section><h3>Genotyping</h3>
<p>Both families’ analysis of SNP array data showed mapping to ∼9.6 Mb and ∼7.1 Mb regions on chromosome 1p21.2, respectively (<a>Fig. 1</a>e, f). No other convincing homozygous regions were found except on chromosome 1 (online suppl. Fig. 1; for all online suppl. material, see <a>https://doi.org/10.1159/000536016</a>). This region contains the <em>CDC14A</em> gene already reported in underlying HIIMS and NSHL.</p></section><section><h3>Sanger Sequencing</h3>
<p>Screening <em>CDC14A</em> gene identified a nonsense variant [c.1000C&gt;T; p.(Gln334*)] in exon 11 in family A and a missense variant [c.684C&gt;A; p.(Asn228Lys)] in exon 9 in family B (<a>Fig. 2</a>a–c). Both variants were absent in gnomAD, TOPMed Bravo, ALL of Us, and 300 matched control from the respective area. The variant (c.1000C&gt;T) in family A has a CADD Phred score of 38 and GERP++ of 6.54, and the variant (c.684C&gt;A) in family B has a CADD Phred score of 24.3 and GERP++ of 5.78 (<a>Fig. 2</a>e). Both variants were predicted to be pathogenic by Mutation Taster, Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant, and Polymorphism Phenotyping V2 and both variants were classified as variants of uncertain significance by ACMG classification [(Gln334*: PP1, PM2, PP3); (Asn228Lys:PP1, PM2: PP3)].</p>

<p>In the current study, two distinct consanguineous families from a remote region of Pakistan were clinically and molecularly characterized. According to clinical findings, family A contained NSHL, whereas family B comprised HIIMS. At the genetic level, the <em>CDC14A</em> gene contains two novel homozygous variants including p.(Gln334*) and p.(Asn228Lys) that indicate the presence of a recessive inheritance pattern. According to our knowledge to date, 14 variants have been reported in <em>CDC14A</em> underlying HIIMS and NSHL. Cell Division-Cycle 14 A gene (CDC14A) encodes an evolutionarily conserved dual-specificity phosphoprotein phosphatase that dephosphorylates proteins at tyrosine or serine/threonine residues. It is mostly involved in cell mitosis exit and DNA replication initiation [<a>10</a>]. CDC14A largest isoform has seventeen exons and contains different domains, such as a dual-specificity phosphatase domain (DSPn), a core dual-specificity phosphatase domain (DSPc), and a Nuclear Export Signal domain [<a>11</a>].</p>
<p>In family A, featuring NSHL identified a homozygous nonsense variant [c.1000C&gt;T; p.(Gln334*)] in <em>CDC14A</em>. The variant lies in the 11th exon of <em>CDC14A</em>. In exon 11, five variants have previously been identified from literature searches in NSHL. The degree of hearing loss manifested by each affected person carrying the variant in exon 11 varied, with profound hearing loss being the least common such as variant (c.1041dup), has been associated with Sensorineural Hearing Loss, whereas the variant (c.1126C&gt;T; c.1015C&gt;T) underlies Severe to Profound Deafness. Additionally, variants including c.1033C&gt;T and c.1126C&gt;T have been linked to moderate-to-profound deafness [<a>12</a>–<a>14</a>]. The affected participants in the current study had severe hearing loss, and semen analysis found no abnormalities (<a>Table 1</a>). Transcripts with truncating variants in exon 11 potentially evade nonsense-mediated decay due to the existence of a shorter isoform comprising only 11 exons, in contrast to the longest isoform containing 17 exons [<a>12</a>]. Six different transcripts resulting from alternative splicing have been reported (NCBI RefSeq: <a>NM_003672.4</a>, <a>NM_033312.2</a>, <a>NM_033313.2</a>, <a>NM_001319210.1</a>, <a>NM_001319211.1</a>, and <a>NM_001319212.1</a>) [<a>13</a>].</p>
<p>Along with deafness, a severe form of infertility known as severe oligozoospermia was observed in family B (<a>Table 1</a>). In the current investigation, we have found a missense variant [c.684C&gt;A; p.(Asn228Lys)] in <em>CDC14A</em> that underlies HIIMS in family B. The variant lies in the DSPc of CDC14A. All four members of CDC14 s contain the conserved DSPc domain. DSPc contains the catalytic site where phosphoserine, phosphotyrosine, and phosphothreonine residues are dephosphorylated [<a>15</a>]. Considering what we have found and what we know, six variants have so far been linked to HIIMS.</p>
<p>Mutant alleles of mouse <em>Cdc14a</em>, specifically engineered to impact phosphatase activity, were developed. Then the engineered mice were mated with normal and proven fertile female mice. Despite repeated mating attempts, these males failed to produce offspring. <em>Cdc14a</em> homozygous mutant deaf females, whose phosphatase activity was affected, successfully gave birth to multiple healthy litters of pups. Variants in <em>Cdc14a</em> C-terminal region solely impact hearing, while variants affecting the phosphatase domains (DSPc/PTPc and dual-specificity phosphatase domain) lead to HIIMS. This underscores the essential role of Cdc14a’s phosphatase activity in both male fertility and hearing [<a>12</a>]. In humans, most variants causing HIIMS are clustered in exon 9, 10, or upstream of exon 9, affecting the evolutionary conserved DSPc domain. Conversely, nearly all the variants causing deafness feature cluster in exon 11 or downstream (<a>Fig. 2</a>d). However, here we report a missense and a stop gain variant with the feature of HIIMS and NSHL, consistent with previous findings, and provide more evidence about the genotype/phenotype correlations.</p>
<p>The encoded protein Cdc14a in mice is crucial for the maintenance of hair cells, while its human ortholog, CDC14A, is essential for both male fertility and the maintenance of normal hearing [<a>12</a>]. In the murine auditory system, Cdc14a accumulates in microtubule networks of the hair cells (basal bodies and the kinocilia). However, it is also localized at the tips of the stereocilia [<a>16</a>]. Male mice compound heterozygous or homozygous for two defective alleles of <em>Cdc14a</em> displayed infertility. In contrast to the auditory system, Cdc14a plays a significant role in maintaining and developing the morphological integrity and structure of the seminiferous tubules. Consequently, these mice also had abnormal sperm count and were not fertile. Female mice with aberrant Cdc14a activity displayed only hearing loss [<a>17</a>]. These phenotypes mimic those seen in male and female patients who lost CDC14A in previous and current studies [<a>12</a>].</p>
<p>Finally, we present the clinical and genetic findings in two families with HIIMS and NSHL in order to better define the genotypic and phenotypic spectrum associated with <em>CDC14A</em> variants. Our findings suggest that individuals presenting with HIIMS and NSHL in consanguineous populations should undergo genetic testing that includes <em>CDC14A</em>.</p></section><section><h2>Acknowledgments</h2>
