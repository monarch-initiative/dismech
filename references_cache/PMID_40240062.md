Pouchitis, defined as inflammation of the ileal pouch, is the most common 
complication following restorative proctocolectomy for refractory ulcerative 
colitis. Antibiotics remain the first line of therapy for pouchitis, but the 
majority of patients develop subsequent episodes and some are refractory to 
antibiotic therapy. This highlights the need for more effective treatment 
options and points to a more complex pathophysiology beyond the role of th pouch 
microbiome, similar to what is seen in inflammatory bowel disease. In this 
review, we outline the putative mechanisms of pouchitis, including genetic 
predisposition, microbiome alterations, dysfunction of the intestinal barrier 
and the immune system and review the available animal models of pouchitis. In 
addition, we introduce the concept of pouchitis as a possible transmural disease 
and discuss the potential role of non-immune cells, including stromal cells, in 
perpetuating inflammation and intestinal barrier dysfunction. We discuss future 
directions, implications for novel therapies and propose novel multicellular 
disease models that can better capture the complexity of pouchitis pathogenesis.

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a form of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) characterized by mucosal inflammation limited to the large bowel in a continuous pattern with proximal extension from the anus. Several medical therapies are available to treat UC including aminosalicylates, immunomodulators, steroids, biologics (tumor necrosis factor inhibitors [TNFi], anti-integrin, antiIL-12/23) and more recently small molecules (S1P modulators and Janus kinase [JAK]-inhibitors) [1]. Despite this broad therapeutic armamentarium, approximately 10–20% of patients still need to undergo proctocolectomy for refractory disease [2, 3]. Risk factors for surgery include male gender, younger age at diagnosis, prior exposure to TNFi and an extensive colitis phenotype [4]. Restorative proctocolectomy with ileal pouch-anal anastomosis (IPAA) is the preferred surgical treatment option as it maintains continence without a permanent ostomy. However, IPAA does not constitute disease cure. Complications following IPAA include various inflammatory phenotypes (pouchitis, cuffitis, Crohn’s-like disease of the pouch), post-operative/structural changes (leaks, abscesses, fistulas, strictures), infections (C. difficile, Cytomegalovirus [CMV], etc.) and malignancy [5]. Pouchitis is the most common complication, occurring in up to 80% of cases after IPAA creation. These complications can lead to pouch failure, defined as the need for a permanent ileostomy with or without pouch excision, which can occur in up to 10% of IPAA procedures [6]. For the purposes of this review, we will focus on inflammatory pouch phenotypes, the most common forms of complication.

The pouch can be surgically configured as a J (2 limbs), S (3 limbs) or W (4 limbs), the J configuration being the most common. The IPAA procedure can be performed in 3-, 2- or 1-stages, although typically occurs in 3- or 2-stages. In the 2-stage procedure, total proctocolectomy with IPAA (pouch creation) and diverting loop ileostomy is performed as the initial step. The second stage occurs 3–6 months later with ileostomy closure. In a 3-stage approach, subtotal colectomy is performed first with end ileostomy as initial step. Next, completion proctectomy and IPAA after the patient has recovered from the acute/chronic illness and been optimized. The final step involves ileostomy closure [5]. Anatomically, the J-pouch can be divided into the afferent limb, the pouch inlet, the pouch body, the efferent limb and the rectal cuff.

Pouchitis, defined as inflammation of the ileal pouch, is the most frequent complication of IPAA, occurring in up to 80% of patients having IPPA because of underlying UC [7]. Interestingly, in patients with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP), an inherited autosomal dominant syndrome involving a mutation in the adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) gene, that undergo colectomy and IPAA for prevention or treatment of colonic malignancy, pouchitis only occurs in 8% of cases, highlighting a different pathophysiology and predisposition to pouchitis in UC patients [8]. Known non-surgical risk factors associated with increased rate of pouchitis include extensive ulcerative colitis, prior use of anti-TNF or NSAIDs and history of primary sclerosing cholangitis[9, 10, 11]. Putative surgical risk factors for pouchitis include number of stages (2-stage procedure greater increases risk compared to 3-stage), type of anastomosis (hand sewn greater than stapled), short distance between the anastomosis and the dentate line and short J pouch length[12, 13, 14].

Symptoms of pouchitis include abdominal pain, increased number of bowel movements, change in stool consistency, urgency and rectal bleeding. The diagnosis of pouchitis requires endoscopic or histologic evaluation to confirm presence of mucosal inflammation and to exclude other inflammatory and non-inflammatory conditions of the pouch. Several scoring systems have been developed to standardize the diagnosis and disease activity in pouchitis. The Pouchitis Disease Activity Index (PDAI) score (Supplementary Figure), a commonly utilized scoring system in clinical research, combines endoscopic, histologic and clinical assessments into an 18-point scale, with a cutoff score of ≥ 7 being consistent with pouchitis [15, 16]. In clinical practice, the modified PDAI scoring (12-point scale), which does not include histologic scoring, is more commonly used, and a score of ≥ 5 indicates the presence of pouchitis [5].

Pouchitis can be classified based on its etiology (primary or secondary), symptom duration (acute or chronic), response to antibiotics (antibiotic-responsive, antibiotic-dependent or antibiotic refractory) and anatomic distribution. In most cases, an underlying cause is not identified and pouchitis is classified as primary or idiopathic. In secondary pouchitis, an underlying etiology, such as infection, ischemia, structural or inflammatory causes (IgG4, PSC) can be identified, and usually a distinct anatomical distribution of disease is seen. Interestingly, PSC-associated pouchitis has been shown to have a distinct clinical phenotype with increased risk of chronic pouchitis and lower response to antimicrobial therapy, although underlying mechanisms are not known[17]. The presence of cuffitis, defined as inflammation of the rectal cuff (the remaining colonic mucosa) should be excluded by endoscopic evaluation. Chronic pouchitis is defined when symptoms have a greater than 4 weeks duration despite antibiotic therapy or ≥3 episodes of pouchitis a year and can be further classified as chronic antibiotic-dependent (CADP) or chronic antibiotic-resistant pouchitis (CARP). More recently, a simplified definition of CADP has been proposed, based on recurrent episodes of pouchitis that respond to antibiotic therapy but that relapses shortly after its discontinuation. A previous requirement of a certain number of pouchitis episodes in a 12-month period was removed [18, 19]. The macroscopic changes observed in pouchitis during endoscopic evaluation including edema, granularity, exudates, bleeding, friability and ulceration, all findings used for diagnostic purposes. Pouchitis can coexist with Crohn’s-like disease of the pouch (CLDP), defined as presence of a perianal or other fistula that developed at least 12 months after the final stage of IPAA surgery, stricture of the pouch body or pre-pouch ileum, and the presence of pre-pouch ileitis. Microscopic changes of acute pouchitis include infiltration of polymorphonuclear cell types, cryptitis/crypt abscess, and ulceration. Presence or absence of granulomas, pyloric gland metaplasia, architectural distortion, and dysplasia are also assessed in histologic evaluation [20].

Antibiotics remain the first line therapy for active pouchitis with a typical treatment duration of 2–4 weeks. Ciprofloxacin (500 mg twice daily) and/or Metronidazole (500 mg 2–3 times daily) are the most studied and commonly used antibiotics for the management of pouchitis in clinical practice [21]. Vancomycin and rifaximin are alternative options in patients that are unable to tolerate ciprofloxacin or metronidazole. A recent metanalysis shows a pooled response of 65% (95% CI 52–75) to antibiotics for patients with acute or chronic pouchitis[21] supporting the role of the gut microbiome in pathophysiology of pouchitis. Nevertheless, after an initial response, up to 80% of patients will have at least one subsequent flareup [7]. Chronic pouchitis occurs in up to 30% of patients. Treatment options for chronic pouchitis include antibiotics, topical steroid therapies, immunomodulators, biologics and small molecules [22]. When antibiotics are used to manage chronic pouchitis, to lower the risk of antimicrobial resistance and side effects, the lowest effective dose should be used and antibiotics rotated [18]. Recently, the EARNEST trial demonstrated the efficacy and safety of vedolizumab, an anti-α4β7-integrin,in chronic pouchitis [23, 24]. Limited available data on other advanced therapies including TNF antagonists, JAK inhibitors and interleukin-12 and −23 antagonist have been shown to be effective[18]. Alicaforsen, an antisense oligonucleotide inhibiting ICAM-1 (cell surface glycoprotein and adhesion receptor) given in the form of enema has been shown to be effective to treat chronic pouchitis in both a case series and a phase 3 randomized trial[25, 26].

The role of probiotics in the management of pouchitis has been investigated. Based on available evidence and recent guidelines, probiotics have not been recommended for the treatment of pouchitis. However, based on data from three randomized control trials that demonstrated a pooled 87% lower relapse rate of pouchitis over 12 months in patients that received probiotics compared to placebo for recurrent episodes of pouchitis, probiotics are recommended for secondary prevention of pouchitis[27]. Although the microbiome is thought to play a critical role in the pathogenesis of pouchitis, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is not recommended for treatment of pouchitis due to sparce data (only two randomized controlled trials) and mixed results regarding efficacy and safety[28]. The results of FMT studies support the notion that the pathogenesis of pouchitis goes beyond the microbiota as proposed in this review.

As outlined above, although the microbiome appears to play an important role in the development of pouchitis, its recurrent nature points to a more complex pathophysiology, particularly in patients with history of UC. In the subsequent sections, we will systematically outline macroscopic, microscopic and molecular changes that occur following IPAA that may set the stage for the development of pouchitis and outline current concepts of pathogenesis and future directions.

During IPAA a reservoir is created using a segment of small bowel (terminal ileum), which is composed of absorptive enterocytes with exuberant microvilli, goblet cells, Paneth cells, enterochromaffin-like cells and stem cells. Fecal stasis and a significant bacterial load occurs in the normal colon but, after creation of the J pouch, these features are passed to the pouch ileal segment, causing the small bowel mucosa become exposed to a ‘colon-like' luminal milieu rich in microbes, their products and their metabolites [29]. The small intestine mileu has a higher concentration of oxygen and antimicrobials than the colon and therefore its microbial composition includes facultative anaerobes (such as Lactobacillaceae and Streptococcaceae), while in the colon there is lower proportion of facultative anaerobes with predominance of obligate anaerobes (such Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae) as summarized elsewhere and mostly derived from 16S rRNAseq data[30]. Macroscopic and microscopic changes of the pouch mucosa have been reported as early as 3 months following IPAA [20]. Macroscopically there is a characteristic loss of small bowel villi with the emergence of a colon-like mucosa (Figure 1). Microscopically, there is a remodeling of the small intestinal mucosa that has been referred to as colonic metaplasia, with villous blunting and atrophy accompanied by crypt hyperplasia, an increase in the number of mucin-producing goblet cells and in the density of lamina propria lymphocytes and plasma cells [20]. Intraepithelial lymphocytosis may also be seen [31]. Similarly, in a rat model of pouchitis, villous atrophy and increased number of goblet cells was seen following IPAA. Interestingly, increased thickness of the muscularis was observed, a postulated compensatory mechanism in the setting of fecal stasis [32].

This shift to a “colon-like” mucosa has been recently corroborated by a human pouch mucosa transcriptomic analysis of a longitudinal cohort of patients post IPAA demonstrating an expression profile showing upregulation of genes characteristic of the colon, including mucin 1 (MUC1) and mucin 4 (MUC4), and downregulation of ileum-associated genes, including apolipoprotein A4 (APOA4) and intestinal alkaline phosphatase I (ALPI) [33]. Interestingly, such changes are not observed in patients with FAP undergoing IPAA for indication of neoplasia, suggesting a unique adaptive mechanism in patients with underlying UC. The mechanisms driving those changes are not yet clear, but it is reasonable to speculate that those changes are required for the later development of pouch inflammation.

Animal models are a critical tool to provide mechanistic insights into multiple human pathologic condition, and this is also true for pouchitis.

The two commonly used rat models of pouchitis use the same procedure performed in humans (Figure 2A) and involves colectomy, proximal proctectomy followed by construction of a J pouch using the terminal ileum and anastomosis of the pouch to the distal rectum [34]. One model involves the oral intake of 4–5% dextran sodium sulphate (DSS) for 4 days starting 28–30 days after the surgical procedure [34]. In this model, mild inflammation was observed after J pouch formation without DSS including diarrhea and increased myeloperoxidase (MPO) levels. Bacterial overgrowth was seen in the J-pouch, resembling that of the distal colon in non-operated rats. Following administration of 5% DSS orally rats developed diarrhea, rectal bleeding, neutrophil infiltration with crypt abscess, ulceration and villous blunting. This model has been the most widely adopted to date and includes increased levels of mucosal pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α and IFN-γ as well as decreased tight junction expression (ZO-1 and occludin) (Table 1). More recently, a 2,4,6-trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid (TNBS) rat model of pouchitis has been described [35]. In this model, following colectomy and J-pouch formation and anastomosis to the distal rectum, rats are treated with TNBS rectally once on post-operative day 31. Compared to the existing DSS model, the TNBS group demonstrated more severe histologic inflammation of the pouch, including severe ulceration and infiltration of inflammatory cells, although tissue levels of IL-6 and TNF showed a comparable fold increase to the DSS model. There was significant reduction in fecal bacterial diversity in the TNBS group and DSS groups compared to the sham group (switch abdominal surgery), similar to what has been described in humans [36]. A major limitation of rat models is the absence of robust gene knockdown strains compared to mice.

The only reported mouse model of pouchitis is a self-filling intestinal loop (SFL) model [37], as illustrated in Figure 2B. In this model, SFLs were surgically created in C57Bl/6 and IL-10 knockout mice, the loops supposedly representing blind ends that fill with intestinal content. In contrast to humans that undergo J-pouch creation, the colon is not removed due to increased mortality. Post-operatively, 2% DSS was administered orally for 3 days to the wild type C57Bl/6 mice and animals were euthanized after 5 weeks. The creation of self-blind loop and associated fecal stasis led to the development of a colon-like microbiota in the absence of DSS, comparable to what is seen in human pouches, and the intestinal loop mucosal gene expression profile measured by microarray was consistent with colonic metaplasia. However, inflammation only occurred in wild type mice when DSS was administered or in IL-10 knockout mice (which are prone to inflammation), suggesting that a “second hit’ is required to trigger inflammation in addition to the fecal stasis caused by SFL creation. Using a toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) knockout mouse the TLR4 pathway was shown to be involved in the development of metaplasia and mucosal inflammation, while no metaplasia was found in SFL of TRL4KO mice. In double knockout mice (IL10−/−/TLR4−/−) no changes in IFN-γ or TNF expression were found compared to self-emptying intestinal loops (SEL) mice [37].

Each of the above rat and mouse models have advantages and disadvantages related to overall resemblance with human inflammatory pouch disease, technical skills required, ease of genetic manipulation (Table 1). A major advantage of the available rat models is high anatomical resemblance of the post-surgical rat ileoanal pouch to the human ileal pouch-anal anastomosis (IPAA). This has not been performed in mice, which highlights the larger animal size as an advantage when performing complex surgical procedures. However, a major disadvantage is the lower ability of genetic manipulation compared to mice. In fact, the only available model of pouchitis that has included genetic manipulation is the self-filling loop model with IL-10 knockout. Disadvantages of the SFL model include high technical expertise necessary to perform the procedure and the lack of colectomy with IPAA and J pouch creation. The development of a mouse-based pouchitis model that incorporates creation of an IPPA should be attempted to more closely resemble human pouch anatomy and inflammation.

The pathogenesis of pouchitis is complex, dynamic and multifactorial (Figure 3). It is currently thought to involve a genetic predisposition, epigenetic changes, microbial dysbiosis, metabolic changes, as well as a dysfunction of the barrier and the immune system. The microbiome appears to play a particularly important role in pouchitis given its rapid and profound response to antibiotic therapy in most patients, an effect not seen in luminal UC or CD. In this section, we will focus on the pathogenesis of primary acute and chronic pouchitis. Secondary causes of pouchitis, including ischemic, structural and CLDP, will not be discussed. Mechanistic insights derived from available animal models of pouchitis will also be highlighted.

Genetic polymorphisms associated with an increased risk for IBD are well described and over 240 risk loci have been identified through genome-wide association studies (GWAS) [38], while limited information is available for pouchitis.

Gene variants linked to pouch disorders have been evaluated in a few studies. Supporting the role the microbiome in pouchitis pathophysiology, polymorphisms of genes associated with microbial sensing, such as NOD2 and TLR9 [39, 40], have been described in patients with and chronic pouchitis, but these polymorphisms (NOD2, TLR9) have also been associated with IBD. In pouchitis, there is activation of both the innate and adaptive immune systems (refer to immune dysfunction section in this review), and polymorphisms of immune-related genes coding for TNFSF15, IL-1β (rs1143627) and IL-1 receptor antagonist 1 (IL-1RA) (rs419598) have been linked to pouchitis [41, 42].

The role of epigenetics, which studies regulation of gene expression not associated with changes in the DNA sequence, has been well-described in immune and non-immune IBD cell populations that play a role in bacterial sensing and cellular memory [43, 44, 45]. To the best of our knowledge, only one study has investigated epigenetic changes in pouchitis. Changes in chromatin accessibility and gene expression in epithelial cells of the ileal pouch and pre-pouch of patients with UC that underwent IPAA were evaluated by single cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing (scATACseq) and scRNAseq, respectively [46]. In this study, the authors demonstrated significant changes in cellular composition in the pouch compared to the pre-pouch (ileal segment above the pouch) and identify colon-like epithelial cell population along with ileal-like epithelial cells, again supporting the notion of colonic metaplasia within the pouch. Through scATAC seq, the authors demonstrated many differentially accessible regions between colon-like and ileal-like cell populations suggesting epigenetic regulation and plasticity of the pouch epithelial cell populations. Furthermore, through motif analysis, this study identified transcriptional regulators in the pouch associated with colon-fate decision such as CDX1, CDX2, EHF and NFIA [47]. It remains to be explored whether epigenetic differences in patients with chronic pouchitis and intermittent acute pouchitis exist, and whether UC patients have epigenetic changes in their small bowel that predispose them to the development of pouchitis, possibly explaining the differences between FAP and UC IPAA.

Increased permeability of the intestinal epithelial barrier is a common manifestation of mucosal inflammation in IBD patients [48, 49], suggesting that a leaky epithelial barrier could also play a role in pouchitis. However, minimal data exist on epithelial barrier disruption in pouchitis and the underlying mechanisms remain poorly explored in IPAA.

Formation of the epithelial barrier is a key function of the intestinal epithelium in the healthy gut, whereas its disruption is a hallmark of mucosal inflammation [50]. The intestinal barrier is regulated by multiprotein complexes at the plasma membrane level known as tight junctions (TJs), and adherens junctions (AJs). Both junctional complexes are built by transmembrane adhesive proteins such as claudins and occludin at TJs and E-cadherin and nectins at AJs [51]. Interestingly, most claudins are seal-forming, decreasing the epithelial paracellular permeability, whereas claudin-2 forms paracellular pores resulting in the increased permeability [52]. Epithelial junctions are also composed of multiple cytoplasmic scaffolding proteins, including those of the ‘zonula occludens’ (ZO) family and different catenins. Both TJs and AJs are physically linked with a prominent peri-junctional cytoskeleton [53], critical for assembly, stabilization and disruption of TJs and AJs [54].

In vivo barrier permeability can be measured in the urine by orally ingesting probes of small sizes (5–8 Å), such as mannitol, and large sizes (9.5–11 Å), such as 51Cr-EDTA and lactulose. Ex vivo, the intestinal barrier can be assessed by mounting intestinal mucosal samples into Ussing chambers, a system that allows measuring transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) [55]. In vitro, using either cell lines or primary human cells, intestinal barrier can be assessed through TEER or fluorescently labeled probes of different sizes.

Merrett et al., assessing pouch epithelial permeability using the 51Cr-EDTA assay, found permeability to decrease after ileostomy closure (compared to pouch prior to ileostomy closure) and parallel to colonic metaplasia and villous atrophy. These findings are consistent with the known tighter colonic epithelial barrier compared to that of small intestinal epithelium [56], and suggest that tightening of the epithelial barrier may be an important functional consequence of the transformation of the ileal into the colonic epithelium. Importantly, the authors demonstrated increased permeability during pouchitis [57], which suggests either a delay or reversal of the development of the colon-like intestinal barrier leading to barrier dysfunction in inflammation. In contrast, a subsequent study by a different group examining barrier permeability by measuring TEER of epithelial tissue using Ussing chambers and investigating transepithelial permeability to mannitol did not find significant changes in barrier function in pouchitis, although impaired secretory and absorptive ion transport function was observed [58]. Subsequently, they investigated the relevance of severity of inflammation in pouch barrier dysfunction. While their initial observations did not demonstrate increased intestinal permeability, marked reduction of TEER was found in a cohort of patients with severe pouchitis as assessed in Ussing chambers [59]. In summary, while there is still controversy regarding the presence of barrier dysfunction in mild pouchitis, increased intestinal permeability has been consistently demonstrated in severely inflamed pouches.

The precise mechanisms of barrier dysfunction in pouchitis remain poorly understood, although most studies point towards disruption of the molecular composition of TJs in both the intact pouch as well as during pouchitis (Table S1), but deregulation of AJs or junction-associated actomyosin cytoskeleton has not been evaluated. Downregulation of ZO-1, claudin-1, and upregulation of pore-forming claudin-2 has been consistently demonstrated in both humans and animal models of pouchitis [59, 60]. In a prospective cohort of patients with UC and IPAA, expression of tight junction proteins during severe pouchitis found downregulation of claudin-1 and upregulation of pore-forming claudin-2, while the expression of claudin 3, 4, 5 and 7 remained unchanged compared to ileum from non-IBD controls [59]. This suggests a switch in TJ molecular composition from ‘tight’ claudin isoforms to a ‘leaky’ claudin-2 that could contribute to increased barrier permeability. On a gene microarray analysis, downregulation of ZO-1, ZO-2, claudin 4, 8, 15, 23 and occludin was demonstrated in the inflamed compared to the non-inflamed pouch, while claudin 1 and 2 were found to be upregulated [61]. Interestingly, even in the absence of pouch inflammation in IPAA, comparable expression patterns of intestinal barrier genes were found in the pouch when compared to the healthy pre-pouch ileum of patients with history of UC [33]. These findings, suggest that changes in the expression of tight junction genes occur in the pouch mucosa of patients with UC in both inflamed and non-inflamed pouches.

The composition and density if microbes in the small and large bowel are drastically different. In the small bowel aerobic bacteria predominate, while in the colon the microbiota is mostly anaerobic, and the bacterial load in the colon is markedly higher with increased numbers of Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes [62]. Following IPAA, the ileum used to create the J-pouch is exposed to an entirely different bacterial density and composition, triggering a change in the microbial composition and their metabolites and ultimately leading to a colon-like phenotype [37];[35]. Fecal stasis could also contribute to inflammation [63].

After colectomy with IPAA in patients with UC, the microbiota of the pouch transitions to a colon-predominant bacteria such as Bacteroides fragilis and Clostridium coccoides [64, 65], and most bacterial species become similar to those of the colon prior to colectomy [66]. A study found that bacterial diversity of the normal ileoanal pouch was similar to that of UC patients in remission, although different from that of healthy controls [67].

A major difference in bacterial composition between the UC pouch compared to FAP pouch is a higher proportion of sulfate-reducing bacteria and lower proportion of secondary bile acid-producing bacterial species, such as Ruminococcacea [68, 69, 70]. Sulfate reducing bacteria increase the production of hydrogen sulfide, which in turn inhibits butyrate oxidation, decreasing concentration of butyrate, an important nutrient for enterocytes and also an epigenetic regulator of histone deacetylase inhibition [71]. In addition, FAP pouches have a higher proportion of butyrogenic bacteria such as Bacteroidetes and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and higher proportion of Firmicutes [70].

Most studies of acute pouchitis demonstrate significantly altered abundancy of bacteria that play roles in production of short chain fatty acid (SCFA), bile acid processing and mucin-degradation [29]. A prospective study of patients with UC pouches demonstrated that patients who developed pouchitis within 1 year of the study had decreased microbial diversity and depletion of Ruminococcus [72] compared to the group that did not develop pouchitis. These bacteria are important in the production of SCFAs, including butyrate, which is a critical energy source for the gastrointestinal epithelium [73] and also an epigenetic regulator [71, 74]. A separate study found that, compared to non-inflamed UC pouches, Ruminococcus gnavus strains, Escherichia and Shigella were significantly enriched in pouchitis, while Bacteriodetes decreased [75, 76]. Compared to FAP pouch, UC pouchitis patients have higher proportion of Firmicutes and lower proportion of Bacteriodetes and Proteobacteria [77]. However, no significant differences in microbial diversity in UC pouchitis compared to FAP pouches were also reported (62).

In addition to depletion of butyrate in the context of pouchitis, the microbiota can also modulate inflammation through bile acid metabolism deregulation. Specifically, secondary bile acid levels (lithocholic acid and deoxycholic acid) are decreased in the UC pouch compared to FAP pouch [70, 78]. The anti-inflammatory effect of secondary bile acid supplementation has been demonstrated in murine models of colitis and shown to be at least partially mediated through a TGR5 bile acid receptor dependent mechanism [70].

While the role of bacterial microbiota in IBD pathogenesis has been extensively investigated, less is known regarding the role of fungal dysbiosis [79]. In pouchitis, data on fungal microbiota composition is very limited. Compared to patients without pouch inflammation, patients with pouchitis were found to have lower alpha (α) diversity in mycobiota composition and increased abundance of Saccharomyces (41). Using a rat IPAA + DSS model of pouchitis, fungal dysbiosis induced by fluconazole treatment aggravated pouch inflammation (41).

Most data on immune dysregulation in pouchitis is extrapolated from UC and few available studies directly investigated the pouch mucosa [47, 61, 80, 81]. The immunopathogenesis of UC involves genetic susceptibility and both the innate and adaptive arms of the immune system [82]. Immune cell players and mediators include macrophages and T cells (Th1, Treg, Th9) and secreted pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL1β, IL-6, IL-17 and IL-23 [82]. More recently non-immune cells such as inflammation-associated fibroblasts (IL13RA2+, IL11+) have also been shown to play a role in intestinal inflammation in both CD and UC [83, 84].

Activation of the innate as well as the adaptive arms of the immune system have also been noted in pouchitis (Table S2). Several studies have demonstrated an increase in proinflammatory cytokines both on a gene and protein expression level compared to the non-inflamed pouch (Table S2). Specifically, on a microarray-based gene expression level, a study assessing the transcriptional profile of the inflamed pouch mucosa found upregulation of IL-1β and IL-6 [61], while a separate qPCR study found increase in IL-1β and IL-8 [85, 86]. On a protein level, in addition to the aforementioned cytokines, elevated TNF [87, 88] and IFN-γ [89] were also found, while a separate study found elevation of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8 but not TNF [90]. Interestingly, even in the absence of pouchitis, the pouch mucosa of patients with UC compared to FAP had significantly higher expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines by immunoblotting including TNF, IL1β and IL-6, again suggesting a predisposition to pouchitis in patients with history of UC [91]. In agreement, two separate studies found significant changes on a gene expression level of the non-inflamed pouch mucosa of patients with history of UC when compared to either the pre-pouch ileum or pouch mucosa of FAP patients [33, 61] with increase expression of immune/inflammatory gene networks. Similarly, in animal models of pouchitis, increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines in the pouch intestinal mucosa treated with either DSS or TNBS compared to controls was observed (Table S1). In the oral DSS rat model of pouchitis, increased gene expression of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF, and IL-17 in a pouch mucosa was noted [92, 93, 94, 95]. In a TNBS rat model of pouchitis (TNBS given by enema), elevation of IL-6 and TNF was found [35]. On a protein level, only one study using the mouse SFL model found increase in TNF [37].

Recently the first scRNAseq data from human pouch endoscopic mucosal biopsies was reported [81]. This study focused on mucosa-associated CD45+ hematopoietic cells. The authors found greater proportion of monocytes/macrophages in pouchitis compared to uninflamed pouch, while there was no significant difference between UC inflamed colon and pouchitis, suggesting a major role of myeloid cells in pouch-associated inflammation. Two subpopulations of myeloid cells, SOX4+/MAFA+ and IL1B+/LYZ+ monocyte/macrophages expressing cells, were found to be increased in UC and pouchitis. Within the SOX4+/MAFA+ population, there was increased expression of IL1RN and LTB in inflamed samples, while within the in the IL1B+/LYZ+ expressing cells, increased expression of IL8 and CXCL2 was found. Within the twelve T cell clusters identified, FOXP3+ T regulatory cell cluster and specifically increased proportion of FOXP3+/BATF+ cells, were found to be significantly increased in both inflamed pouch and UC compared to uninflamed pouch. This highlights the potential of future T regulatory-modulating therapies in the treatment of pouchitis as recently demonstrated in Crohn’s disease, where FOXP3+ proinflammatory T cells suppressive phenotype was rescued in vitro following treatment with a histone deacetylase inhibitor (vorinostat) [96]. The authors concluded that the pouchitis and UC may have similar inflammatory mechanisms [81].

The presence of an immune dysfunction in IBD is corroborated by the efficacy of biologics and small molecules targeting immune mediators/pathways [97]. In the intestinal mucosa immune cells interact with non-immune cell types such as epithelial cells and stromal cells, both of which can produce pro-inflammatory cytokines [98, 99]. In the context of IBD, recent scRNAseq studies from CD and UC have identified a subpopulation of inflammatory fibroblasts expressing IL13RA2+/IL11+ associated with resistance to anti-TNF therapy [84]. The potential interaction between fibroblasts and pouch epithelial cells has not been investigated in the context of pouchitis.

When in 1969 Nils Kock originally reported the procedure bearing his name (the Kock continent ileostomy) describing the surgical reshaping of the terminal into a rectum-like reservoir (pouch) in patients who had a total proctocolectomy (IPAA) because of UC, he was aspiring at giving his patients a better quality of life [100]. He could have never imagined the clinical, translational, experimental and pathophysiological consequences of his transformative procedure. At his time IBD was a vaguely recognized and barely understood entity, the distinction of UC from colonic CD (then called transmural colitis) was still questioned, studies of mucosal immunology were non existing, and the gut microbiome was a practically ignored component of intestinal pathophysiology. Progressively, the complexity of the IBD pathophysiology became increasingly appreciated as well as all its immune and non-immune cell interactions [101].

The first reports of inflammation of the pouch (“pouch ileitis”) were simply interpreted as recurrence of IBD in the ileal reservoir [102], and a long time went by before pouchitis was recognized as a new entity in its own right, with its own conditioning and mediator factors and with a highly complex pathogenesis as clearly demonstrated in this review. However, much remains to be done to fully understand why and how pouchitis develops and evolves into a distinctive form of acute and chronic intestinal inflammation bearing a multitude of challenges that resemble those of UC, CD or IBD overall.

The current understanding of the pathogenesis of pouchitis is limited by the lack of better in vitro models and more true-to-life animal models which would allow for a more in-depth interrogation of relevant mechanisms through genetic, cellular and molecular manipulations. In addition, most of the data on human pouchitis derive from mucosal pinch biopsies rather than whole tissue resection, limiting the understanding of other potentially relevant players such as stromal cells. In fact, pouchitis could very well be a transmural disease involving a variety of interacting cell types [101]. For instance, inflammatory myofibroblasts could negatively impact the intestinal epithelial barrier either directly (unknown mediators) or indirectly (further activation of the immune system). While the role of myofibroblasts has been studied in IBD [103], it is yet to be thoroughly investigated in the context of pouchitis (Figure 3). This introduces an even more complex cell-cell pathogenic model of pouchitis that incorporates immune, epithelial and stromal cells interacting with the microbiome. In addition, novel experimental systems should be explored, such as in vitro models that capture the complexity of pouchitis and allow to dissect cell-cell interactions mechanisms by co-culturing human pouch organoids with myofibroblasts and immune cells in the presence and absence of the microbiota. Only by accepting new concepts and new models can solutions to the challenges of pouchitis be achieved.
