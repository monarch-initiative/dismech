# Discovery of a new hereditary RECQ helicase disorder RECON syndrome positions the replication stress response and genome homeostasis as centrally important processes in aging and age-related disease.
**Authors:** Datta A, Sommers JA, Jhujh SS, Harel T, Stewart GS, Brosh RM Jr
**Journal:** Ageing Res Rev (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.arr.2023.101887](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.arr.2023.101887)

## Content

Characterizing the molecular deficiencies underlying human aging has been a formidable challenge as it is clear that a complex myriad of factors including genetic mutations, environmental influences, and lifestyle choices influence the deterioration responsible for human pathologies. In addition, the common denominators of human aging, exemplified by the newly updated hallmarks of aging (López-Otín et al., 2023), suggest multiple avenues and layers of crosstalk between pathways important for genome and cellular homeostasis, both of which are major determinants of both good health and lifespan. In this regard, we postulate that hereditary disorders characterized by chromosomal instability offer a unique window of insight into aging and age-related disease processes. Recently, we discovered a new RECQ helicase disorder, designated RECON syndrome attributed to bi-allelic mutations in the RECQL1 gene (Abu-Libdeh et al., 2022). Cells deficient in RECQL1 exhibit genomic instability and a compromised response to replication stress, providing further evidence for the significance of genome homeostasis to suppress disease phenotypes. Here we provide a perspective on the pathology of RECON syndrome to inform the reader as to how molecular defects in the RECQL1 gene contribute to underlying deficiencies in nucleic acid metabolism often seen in certain aging or age-related diseases.

Hereditary disorders characterized by clinical features of aging have provided tremendous insight into the molecular pathways that preserve the genome and ensure cellular homeostasis, defined by the twelve hallmarks of aging (López-Otín et al., 2023). Among these are classic features like genomic instability and cellular senescence, but more recently new hallmarks such as reduced autophagy and mitophagy have been associated with certain accelerated aging disorders (Fang et al., 2019; Fang et al., 2014), raising new interest in the interdependence of aging characteristics. Although typically rare in the population, such premature aging disorders offer a unique context to examine the process of cellular aging. The knowledge gained from studying these disorders may not only help with identifying potential therapeutic strategies to treat these diseases, but may also aid with the management of clinical pathologies associated with age-related diseases found more commonly within the general population, such as cancer, osteoporosis, arthritis, heart disease, stroke, Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and dementia (Zhang et al., 2020). In this section, we will provide a perspective on some of the more notable genetic premature aging disorders and how these relate to a newly identified hereditary disease designated RECON syndrome that is characterized by a compromised response to replication stress and defective DNA repair (Abu-Libdeh et al., 2022). For a broader and more extensive discussion of hereditary premature aging disorders, see a recent review from Schumacher and colleagues (Rieckher et al., 2021).

Early developments in the study of premature aging disorders initially focused on Xeroderma pigmentosum (XP), an ultraviolet light (UV) hypersensitivity syndrome associated with multiple genetic complementation groups and a spectrum of clinical symptoms that overlap with two other related disorders, Cockayne Syndrome (CS) and Trichothiodystrophy (TTD) (for review, see (DiGiovanna and Kraemer, 2012)). Phenotypic analysis of the cellular response to UV-induced DNA lesions using patient-derived cell lines followed by the biochemical characterization of the XP gene products and reconstitution of nucleotide excision repair machinery in vitro (Sancar, 1994) firmly established that XP is a bona fide DNA repair deficiency disorder and that an inability to repair damaged DNA contributes not only to cancer development but also other age-related pathologies, such as neurodegeneration. A recent and insightful review presents a compelling case that XP serves as a model disease for human premature aging (Rizza et al., 2021).

Fanconi Anemia (FA) also represents another genetically complex DNA repair disorder, with over 20 causative genes implicated thus far. FA (Datta and Brosh, 2019), is thought to resemble a cellular senescence and accelerated aging condition (Brosh et al., 2017; Helbling-Leclerc et al., 2021). The most prominent feature of FA is progressive bone marrow failure leading to anemia, which is attributed to premature senescence of the stem cell compartment. However, individuals with FA also display chronic inflammation and have an increased predisposition to the development of certain cancer types, namely acute myeloid leukemia and squamous cell carcinoma of the head/neck. The accelerated aging phenotype characteristic of FA is accompanied by compromised endocrine function, sarcopenia and osteopenia. The classic hallmark of FA is hypersensitivity of patient-derived cells to DNA interstrand cross-links (ICL) causing agents. These lesions are typically repaired by the homologous recombination (HR) repair pathway. Several studies from the Patel lab and colleagues provide strong evidence that aldehydes arising either as a byproduct of normal biochemical processes e.g. histone demethylation, or the consumption of alcohol represent physiological genotoxins capable of inducing DNA and DNA-protein crosslinks, which are substrates for the FA pathway (Garaycoechea et al., 2012; Garaycoechea et al., 2018; Hodskinson et al., 2020; Langevin et al., 2011). However, recent evidence suggests that endogenous formaldehyde may also disrupt redox homeostasis in cells that are defective in the FA pathway (Umansky et al., 2022). As such, a defect in the response to oxidative stress (Kumari et al., 2014; Pagano et al., 2004), and poor repair of DNA damage induced by reactive oxygen species may also contribute to the aging phenotype exhibited by FA patients (D’Andrea, 2001).

An important distinction in premature aging disorders is the classification of disease symptoms that manifest early in life versus those which become overtly apparent during or after adolescence (e.g., Werner syndrome (WS) (discussed below)). The clinical features of accelerated aging and a failure to thrive in Hutchinson-Gilford progeria syndrome (HGPS) typically appear during the first year (Gordon et al., 1993). HGPS arises as a consequence of a dominant mutation in the gene encoding lamin A/C, an intermediate filament protein that makes up the principal component of the nuclear matrix. Pathological mutations in the LMNA/C gene result in the production of a truncated protein termed progerin, which upon incorporation into the nuclear matrix surrounding the nuclear envelope disrupts its integrity. Consequently, this affects a multitude of different cellular processes which are influenced by the integrity of the nuclear envelope, including chromatin compaction, transcription, DNA repair and DNA replication. In addition to growth deficiency, perturbation of lipid, bone and dermis are apparent in HGPS. The vascular lesions greatly predispose individuals to heart attacks or strokes. Recent advances in small molecule therapies for the treatment of HGPS have raised excitement in the field and serve as a potential model for the treatment of other hereditary accelerated aging disorders (for perspective see (Macicior et al., 2021)).

The discoveries of the XPD and XPB helicase genes and the association of mutations in these genes to not only XP, but also CS and TTD, initiated a plethora of studies characterizing the roles of these two helicase subunits of the general transcription factor TFIIH in DNA repair and transcription. These studies provided the first connection between defective repair of DNA damage in transcriptionally active genes and age-related decline in neurological fitness (Egly and Coin, 2011). The association of mutations in the XPB and XPD helicase genes to diseases that exhibit features of premature aging ushered in an era in which multiple clinically distinct genetic diseases were discovered to be a consequence of bi-allelic mutations in genes encoding helicase enzymes.

In 1995, the first gene, BLM encoding the protein RECQL3/BLM, a member of a family of human proteins bearing a conserved RECQ helicase core domain (Figure 1), named after the prototype RecQ bacterial helicase, was discovered (Ellis et al., 1995). Beyond the conserved motor ATPase/helicase core in the five human RECQ helicases can be found other conserved regions, as well as unique domains of considerable interest (Estep and Brosh, 2018) (Figure 1). Bi-allelic mutations in BLM are linked to Bloom syndrome (BS), a chromosomal instability disorder in which affected individuals are strongly predisposed to a wide variety of cancers, immunodeficiency, short stature and features of premature aging manifest throughout development and adulthood. Experimental evidence from cell-based systems and model genetic organisms have implicated the BLM helicase as a key regulator of recombination that helps to mediate the cellular response to replication stress or DNA damage in the form of highly toxic double-strand breaks (DSBs) (de Renty and Ellis, 2017). However, there is some evidence to suggest that it has overlapping roles with the other human RECQ helicases (Lu and Davis, 2021), which has made it quite difficult to establish definitive causal genotype-phenotype relationships among the RECQ helicase disorders.

Just a year after the BLM gene was identified, mutations in the related gene, WRN encoding the protein RECQL2/WRN, were linked to the premature aging disorder Werner Syndrome (WS) (Yu et al., 1996). WS is distinct from BS on multiple levels. Notably, the clinical features of WS are prominently displayed after adolescence, usually in an individual’s mid to late 20’s. WS is considered by many as the model premature aging disorder because a vast number of clinical features affecting many tissue types and organ systems resembling those observed in normal aging, e.g. cataracts, diabetes, osteoporosis, muscle atrophy and cancer predisposition. Interestingly, in addition to the conserved RECQ helicase core domain, the WRN coding sequence also comprises a conserved 3’ to 5’ proofreading exonuclease domain. However, it is still unclear if the two catalytic activities of WRN operate in a coordinated or separate capacity to maintain genomic stability and suppress the cellular deficiencies characteristic of cells from WS individuals. Like BLM, WRN is thought to be important for DSB repair and the replication stress response; moreover, WRN and BLM, like the Fe-S cluster helicase RTEL1 (discussed below), may facilitate DNA transactions at specialized structures such as telomeres, which are implicated in cellular senescence and aging (Estep and Brosh, 2018).

Following the discovery of WRN and BLM, a third RECQ helicase gene designated RECQL4 was implicated in three distinct genetic disorders known as Rothmund-Thomson syndrome (RTS) (Kitao et al., 1999), Baller-Gerold syndrome (BGS) (Van Maldergem et al., 2006) and RAPADILINO syndrome (Siitonen et al., 2003). All three genetic diseases are characterized by stunted growth and skeletal abnormalities with some overlapping features of premature aging. It remains a mystery why there is such genetic heterogeneity for RECQL4 mutations. Two other members of the mammalian RECQ helicase family exist: RECQL1 and RECQL5. While heterozygous mutations in both the RECQL1 or RECQL5 genes have been suggested to predispose individuals to certain types of cancer, it was only this year (2022) that a genetic disorder was linked to bi-allelic mutations in RECQL1 (Abu-Libdeh et al., 2022), highlighted below. To date, a genetic disease linked to RECQL5 mutation still remains to be identified. Experimental evidence from cell-based studies and biochemistry has implicated a role for RECQL5 in resolving transcription-replication conflicts that might lead to genomic instability (Urban et al., 2016).

A second conserved family of DNA helicases implicated in diseases of premature aging are represented by those molecular motors which possess an Fe-S cluster nested within the ATPase/helicase core domain. One of these is the FANCJ helicase, implicated in DSB repair, in which mutations are linked to FA (mentioned above) or associated with breast or ovarian cancer (Cantor and Guillemette, 2011). Figuring prominently in the Fe-S helicase family is the RTEL1 helicase which is shown to have roles in telomere stability in both mouse (Vannier et al., 2012) and human cells (Ballew et al., 2013a; Ballew et al., 2013b; Deng et al., 2013; Le Guen et al., 2013; Walne et al., 2013). Individuals with bi-allelic mutations in RTEL1 have the genetic disease Dyskeratosis congenita (DC) or the more severe form, Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome (HHS) (Ballew et al., 2013b; Le Guen et al., 2013; Walne et al., 2013). A particular challenge in the field is to understand how disruption of RTEL1’s role in maintaining genome stability gives rise to the clinical features of DC/HHS, which are different from that of other helicases localized to telomeres. For example, both RTEL1 (Vannier et al., 2013) and FANCJ (Brosh and Wu, 2021) have been implicated in the resolution of G-quadruplex (G4) DNA structures that are formed within guanine-rich regions of the genome particularly prevalent at the telomeres. Despite this, mutations in these two Fe-S cluster helicases have been associated with clinically very distinct genetic disorders. As we shall discuss below, dissecting the molecular functions of these related DNA helicases and understanding how their loss gives rise to the specific cellular and clinical phenotypes associated with each syndrome is a formidable challenge. As such, this makes it very difficult to extrapolate how bi-allelic mutations in RECQL1 give rise to the clinical and cellular phenotypes associated with the recently discovered RECON syndrome.

With the recent advances in high-throughput sequencing strategies the identification of pathogenic mutations in individuals with rare, uncharacterized genetic disorders has become increasingly easier. Using whole-exome sequencing, a homozygous, potentially pathogenic, missense variant in the RECQL gene (NM_032941.2; c.1375G>T; p.Ala459Ser) was identified in two affected siblings from a single family of Middle Eastern descent with similar clinical features, exhibiting similar developmental clinical characteristics (Abu-Libdeh et al., 2022) (Figure 2). Interestingly, this missense variant is located within the conserved Zn2+ binding domain of RECQL1 immediately adjacent to a pathogenic heterozygous missense mutation (M458K), identified in an individual with hereditary breast cancer, which was shown to compromise its helicase activity. Sequencing of DNA from a proband in a second family, also of Middle Eastern descent, with clinical features similar to the first family, identified the same homozygous missense variant in RECQL. All three probands of ages 9 yr 8 mo, 4 yr, 0 mo, and 14 yr, 9 mo at time of evaluation displayed photosensitivity, xeroderma, characteristic progeroid facial features, and slender elongated thumbs accompanied by other skeletal abnormalities. This new RECQ-associated genetic disorder was named RECON (RECql ONe) syndrome (Abu-Libdeh et al., 2022).

Western blot analysis of whole cell extracts from lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs) derived from an affected individual from each of the two families demonstrated a similar level of RECQL1 protein expression compared to those present in LCLs derived from an unaffected individual, suggesting that the A459S mutation did not exert a gross effect on protein stability (Abu-Libdeh et al., 2022). To assess the functional impact of the p.A459S missense variant, the mutant protein was purified and biochemically characterized in vitro. Notably, the RECQL1-A459S mutant protein had significantly compromised ATPase, helicase and fork restoration activity but not strand annealing activity. Furthermore, its oligomerization properties were largely unaffected. At a cellular level, the A459S RECQL1 missense mutation not only affected the repair of DNA lesions induced by inhibiting topoisomerase 1 and topoisomerase 2 but also reduced the efficiency of DNA replication in the presence of these genotoxins. Interestingly, cells from individuals with RECON syndrome additionally failed to efficiently restart replication forks stalled by nucleotide depletion mediated by hydroxyurea (HU) exposure or DNA damaged induced by the alkylating agent methylmethanesulfonate (MMS), suggesting that the role of RECQL1 in dealing with replication-associated DNA damage is not limited to abortive topoisomerase cleavage complexes but may play a more wide-ranging role in promoting replication fork restart.

Whilst it appears that the clinical features of RECON syndrome are less severe than those associated with loss of other RECQ helicases (Abu-Libdeh et al., 2022), it should be noted that only 3 affected individuals have been identified, all with the same mutation. Furthermore, all the patients are relatively young, such that many age-related deficits may not have had sufficient time to appear. Despite this, it seems that there is overlap in a subset of the clinical features of RECON syndrome with other diseases associated with RECQ helicase deficiency, such as skin photosensitivity and skeletal abnormalities. The postnatal growth retardation observed with RECON syndrome is also exhibited by patients with BS, RTS, RAPADILINO and BGS (Xu et al., 2021), whereas in contrast, the lack of a growth spurt, that usually occurs during the teenage years, is only observed in patients with WS (Oshima et al., 1993). However, the facial abnormalities and a progeroid appearance in RECON syndrome are shared with WS (Oshima et al., 2017). Recently, an assessment of individuals with WS from 2011 to 2020 demonstrated that non-epithelial cancers are the most dominant source of death (Kato et al., 2022), whereas it is yet unclear if cancer is prevalent in RECON syndrome. A full appreciation for the entire range of progeroid characteristics is at present hampered by the young age of the affected individuals. Nonetheless, the cellular sensitivity to agents that impose replication stress suggests that RECQL1 may have at least some overlapping functions with the other human RECQ helicases. Three hallmarks of aging (López-Otín et al., 2023), namely genomic instability (Sharma and Brosh, 2007; Sharma et al., 2007), epigenetic alterations manifested by changes in transcription (Li et al., 2014; Lu et al., 2021), and telomere attrition (Popuri et al., 2014) have been reported to persist in mammalian cells deficient of RECQL1. To ascertain what functions of RECQL1 may be unique and distinct from the other human RECQ helicases and understand how loss of these contribute to the clinical features of RECON syndrome, it is useful to consider what is known about the molecular functions of RECQL1 in genome homeostasis and how its pathway roles align with the clinical and cellular phenotypes of RECQL1 deficiency.

Like the other human RECQ helicases, RECQL1 displays a fairly broad substrate specificity capable of unwinding a variety of partial duplex DNA substrates (Popuri et al., 2008; Sharma et al., 2005), and more complex three-stranded and four-stranded mobile branch-migrating DNA structures that represent key intermediates of homologous recombination (HR) repair (Bugreev et al., 2008; Mazina et al., 2012). The branch-migration activity of RECQL1 may be important for HR-dependent repair of DSBs, consistent with the finding that Recql knockout mice embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) are hypersensitive to ionizing radiation (Sharma et al., 2007), as are human cells depleted of RECQL1 by siRNA (Sharma and Brosh, 2007). A role of RECQL1 in the regulation of HR is further suggested by the elevated sister chromatid exchanges (SCE’s) observed in both Recql knockout MEFs (Sharma et al., 2007) and RECQL1-depleted human cells (Sharma and Brosh, 2007).

Despite RECQL1 being classified as a 3’ to 5’ helicase with respect to the strand it translocates to unwind the duplex (Cui et al., 2003), it is capable of efficiently unwinding a three-stranded 5’ flap substrate that lacks a pre-existing 3’ single-stranded DNA tail (Sharma et al., 2005). The ability of RECQL1 to load in a structure-specific manner on the 5’ flap substrate suggests a potential role in the metabolism of such structures that arise during lagging strand maturation or DNA repair caused by DNA synthesis in which strand displacement can occur. In contrast to its ATP-dependent unwinding or resolving activity, RECQL1 robustly performs annealing of complementary single-stranded DNA molecules (Sharma et al., 2005). The annealing activity of RECQL1 may be important for a specialized pathway of HR-mediated DSB repair such as synthesis-dependent strand annealing. This process requires repeated cycles of duplex unwinding and strand annealing to enable the processed single-stranded ends to reach across a break site and anneal to a complementary sequence. This promotes rejoining of the DNA ends via DNA synthesis across the gap and ligation of the DNA ends to restore genomic integrity. Indeed, RECQL1 efficiently resolves displacement (D) loop and Holliday Junction substrates (Sharma et al., 2005), and is capable of branch-migrating mobile three-stranded and four-stranded DNA structures resembling intermediates of recombinational repair (Bugreev et al., 2008; Mazina et al., 2012) (Figure 3).

Consistent with a proposed role in DSB repair, RECQL1 was reported to physically and functionally interact with several proteins that have important roles to regulate HR, including EXO-1 (Doherty et al., 2005a), MSH2/6 (Doherty et al., 2005a), and RPA (Cui et al., 2004; Cui et al., 2003). The latter, RPA, serves to stimulate ATP-driven RECQL1 helicase to catalytically disrupt protein-DNA complexes, which may play a role in clearing protein obstacles from DNA, such as nucleic acid processing events during DNA repair (Sommers et al., 2014). RECQL1 was also found to interact with Ku70/80, suggesting a role in DNA end-joining of DSBs (Parvathaneni et al., 2013).

An unusual (but apparently not unique (Datta et al., 2021a)) catalytic function of RECQL1 is to restore regressed replication forks capable of resuming DNA synthesis (Berti et al., 2013) (Figure 4). Regressed forks can arise during conditions of replication stress, and experimental evidence invokes a model in which RECQL1 facilitates replication restart by restoring intact replication forks in a manner that is regulated by poly(ADP)ribose polymerase (PARP) (Berti et al., 2013). In support of the proposed role of RECQL1 in restarting reversed forks, reversed forks were observed to accumulate, as visualized by electron microscopy, in RECQL1 depleted cells exposed to the topoisomerase inhibitor camptothecin (CPT); a genotoxin known to induce replication fork reversal. Additional studies provided experimental evidence that PARP1 negatively regulates the RECQL1-dependent branch-migration activity to facilitate fork restart and that this was independent of the form of replication stress, i.e. CPT-induced topological stress, mitomycin-induced DNA cross-links or stalled forks generated by nucleotide depletion following exposure to HU (Zellweger et al., 2015).

More recently, it has been suggested that RECQL1 plays an integral role in speeding up replication forks through its fork reversal activity in a manner that is negatively regulated by CARM1, which in turn interacts with PARP1 and stimulates its DNA binding and catalytic function to suppress RECQL1-mediated fork restoration (Genois et al., 2021). This suggests that the control of fork speed not only influences replication fidelity but also the tolerance to replication stress in a delicately balanced manner. Despite the evidence for RECQL1’s role to restore replication forks and enable cells to cope with replication stress, recent findings from our lab suggest that WRN may function in a biochemically similar manner in the cellular context of a BRCA2 deficiency (Datta et al., 2021a). However, the ability of PARP1 to regulate WRN-dependent fork stabilization and/or restart was not examined.

Structure-function analysis of RECQL1 using site-directed mutations revealed that the conserved aromatic loop within the helicase core domain is essential for the helicase activity of RECQL1. Moreover, it was determined from genetic complementation experiments that RECQL1 plays a key role in governing the availability of the nuclear single-stranded DNA binding protein RPA to respond to replication stress and suppress DNA damage at forks (Banerjee et al., 2015). These studies, along with work from the Vindigni lab (Berti et al., 2013), point toward a key role of RECQL1 to ensure smooth replicative DNA synthesis even under conditions that are sub-optimal for normal fork progression.

Whether or not RECQL1 has a unique role during replication stress that is distinct from the other human RECQ helicases is an important question that may help to explain the unique molecular pathology underlying RECON syndrome. RECQL1 (Berti et al., 2013), like WRN (Datta et al., 2021a), can reverse branch-migrate a regressed replication fork structure, suggesting both helicases act to reset the fork during periods of replication stress. Purified recombinant WRN (Machwe et al., 2006), like BLM (Machwe et al., 2006; Ralf et al., 2006), also promotes fork regression of a model replication fork. The mechanisms whereby the RECQ helicases operate during replication stress may not only be dependent on the type of DNA structure that arises during fork remodeling but also i) whether the RECQ helicase collaborates with other factors at stalled replication forks via protein-nucleic acid and/or protein-protein interactions, ii) what the nature of the replication stress is (e.g., DNA damage, alternative DNA structure, nucleotide pool abundance), and iii) the genetic background of the cells experiencing replication stress.

Work from the Vindigni lab showed that the structure-specific nuclease DNA2 operates with WRN ATPase/helicase to catalytically degrade reversed forks and promote replication restart in human cells exposed to the nucleotide-depleting HU, whereas RECQL1 limits DNA2 nucleolytic activity to prevent extensive nascent strand degradation (Thangavel et al., 2015). Thus, the two RECQ helicases maintain fork stability and prevent genomic instability by DNA2-related mechanisms that are distinct from one another.

Interaction of a protein complex containing BLM, topoisomerase IIIα, RMI1 and RMI2 (BTR) with RPA was found to be required for the stable recruitment of BLM to sites of DNA replication stress and promote fork restart (Shorrocks et al., 2021). Whether RECQL1 requires an association with RPA to facilitate fork restart has not been carefully examined, although RECQL1 (Cui et al., 2004), like BLM (Brosh et al., 2000) and WRN (Doherty et al., 2005b), has been shown to physically and functionally interact with RPA. Recently, it was demonstrated that BLM’s interaction with the structure-specific DNA nuclease EXO5 is required for ATR-regulated replication restart (Hambarde et al., 2021), consistent with a role of BLM to regulate processing of stalled forks to allow smooth resumption of DNA synthesis.

Although the use of drugs to induce replication stress has been valuable to interrogate the cellular mechanisms required to protect replication forks and allow DNA synthesis to be restarted, it is difficult to translate these findings to endogenous sources of replication stress. DNA structures that deviate from the conventional B-form structure is one potential source of endogenous replication stress. For example, microsatellite repeat sequences can form hairpin or cruciform structures that can stall replication forks. Here, WRN helicase appears to play a unique role to prevent DSBs at TA-dinucleotide repeats (van Wietmarschen et al., 2020). WRN has also been proposed to play an important role in the resolution of G-quadruplexes (G4) that may impede normal DNA synthesis at the G-rich telomeric repeats (Crabbe et al., 2004). Unlike WRN and BLM’s preference to resolve G4 DNA substrates (reviewed in (Estep and Brosh, 2018)), purified recombinant human RECQL1 acts poorly to resolve intermolecular G-quadruplexes in vitro (Popuri et al., 2008; Wu et al., 2008); however, a recent study showed that endogenous RECQL isolated from bovine (Bos taurus) can resolve an intramolecular G4 DNA substrate, as demonstrated by single-molecule fluorescence resonance energy transfer assays (Liu et al., 2021). Currently, it remains unclear if RECQL1 plays a role in the replication stress response centered on G4 DNA synthesis bypass.

We had mentioned that the genetic background of a cell may dictate the relative importance and mechanism of action of a RECQ helicase in response to replication stress. This is evident from the recent study of the role of WRN during replication in human cancer cells deficient in BRCA2-dependent replication fork protection (Datta et al., 2021a; Datta and Brosh, 2022). To our knowledge, the importance of RECQL1 in fork protection in defined genetic mutant backgrounds has not been fully examined and deserves study.

Although beyond the scope of this review, the other two RECQ helicases RECQL4 and RECQL5 seem to be most closely aligned with DNA replication initiation (Kliszczak et al., 2015; Thangavel et al., 2010) and resolution of conflicts at the interface of replication and transcription (Chappidi et al., 2019), respectively. While RECQL1 may play a role in nascent DNA synthesis, it seems that RECQL4 is the more prominent player (Thangavel et al., 2010). RECQL4’s interaction with the MCM replicative helicase complex may be a key component of its function in human cells (Xu et al., 2009). Thus, a greater focus and understanding of the roles RECQ helicases play in DNA replication could be important for our understanding of cancer biology. Especially in the context of developing synthetic lethal approaches for cancer therapy exploiting these distinct genetic interactions (Datta et al., 2021b). Most tantalizing is the possible application of this knowledge to developing small molecule modulators of RECQ/DNA helicases (Datta and Brosh, 2018). Both these areas have become increasingly important and active areas of research and development over the past decade.

Multiple studies have provided evidence supporting the concept that transcriptional changes are observed in aging and may be the root of key pathways in which de-regulation underlie the hallmarks of aging (for review, see (López-Otín et al., 2023)). Changes in transcription caused by genomic instability, DNA repair defects, and epigenetic alterations lead to stem cell exhaustion, mitochondrial dysfunction, and dysfunctional proteostasis. Consistent with this model, human cells mutated or deficient in genes linked to hereditary premature aging disorders like WS (Balajee et al., 1999), or CS (Balajee et al., 1997; Dianov et al., 1997) display abnormal transcription. Further studies demonstrated that the respective helicases WRN (Johnson et al., 2010; Tang et al., 2016) and BLM (Nguyen et al., 2014), or helicase-like protein Cockayne syndrome B (CSB) (Scheibye-Knudsen et al., 2016), target G4 DNA structures to regulate gene expression, suggesting that the loss of G4 interaction by these helicases contributes to disease pathogenesis. In an insightful review from the Di Antonio laboratory (Robinson et al., 2021), the authors discuss novel modes of transcriptional regulation mediated by G4 DNA structures beyond the simple roadblock to transcription concept. These principles may very well apply to RECQL1 (Figure 5 and discussed below) and other G4 binding proteins. Indeed, new evidence suggests that G4-folded promoters and their interacting transcription factors epigenetically regulate transcription to influence cell fate determination (Lago et al., 2021). It is highly plausible that specialized molecular motor helicase proteins play a unique or tissue-specific role in chromatin accessibility or protein recruitment that modulate gene expression. Characterization of gene regulatory defects in hereditary premature aging disorders may provide insight to abnormalities associated with what is considered normal aging.

Recent studies by the Sharma lab have demonstrated an important regulatory role of RECQL1 in gene expression that may provide a clue to understanding not only its unique role in genome metabolism and cancer but also possibly its importance in aging (Li et al., 2014). RECQL1 has been implicated in transcriptional regulation using combined experimental approaches: 1) RNA-seq assessment of global changes in gene expression upon acute depletion of RECQL1; 2) ChIP-seq mapping of RECQL1 binding sites in the human genome (Debnath et al., 2022). This dual approach led to a working model in which RECQL1 preferentially binds to guanine (G)-rich sequences in target promotor elements that it directly regulates (Li et al., 2014). Recently, it was reported that RECQL1 acts as a regulator of the estrogen response pathway in breast cancer cells (Lu et al., 2021), suggesting a role in transformation and by inference, senescence programming. Characterizing the putative role of RECQL1 as a senescence driver via its modulation of gene expression, perhaps in a tissue or cell type specific manner, may hold the key to its apparently unique importance in the clinical features of RECON syndrome, and perhaps more broadly in other diseases.

Taking into account, the conservation of human RECQ helicases by their ATPase/helicase core domain, the Sharma lab investigated the potential functional overlap of RECQL1, WRN and BLM to regulate gene expression in two cancer cell types: 1) a highly aggressive, invasive and poorly differentiated triple-negative cancer cell line with epithelial-like properties known as MDA-MB-231; 2) a breast tissue epithelial cell line obtained from a woman with metastatic adenocarcinoma (MCF7) (Lu et al., 2016). In this study, the focus was candidate genes which the group had already reported to be downregulated in RECQL1-depleted cells; moreover, 8 of the 10 genes were enriched for predicted G4-forming sequences in their promoters. Notably, WRN depletion in the MDA-MB-231 cells often followed the effect of RECQL1 depletion on the genes that were tracked, whereas BLM depletion generally did not. However, in MCF7 cells the results were more variable with respect to the effect of RNAi depletion on RECQL1, WRN or BLM. Although further study is required, it is of interest to determine if the estrogen receptor (ER) status plays a role in the impact of RECQ helicase deficiency on targeted gene expression because MDA-MB-231 cells lack ERs, whereas MCF7 cells possess ERs. Overall, it is still difficult to make a strong conclusion about the shared versus unique targets of human RECQ helicases in cancer, and how the genetic background is an influential factor, but this may hold a key to the unique types of cancers observed in WS versus BS (Fu et al., 2017). It is still not possible to make an assessment if cancer is prevalent in RECON syndrome.

Although apparently distinct from the adolescent growth spurt deficiency in WS (Oshima et al., 1993), RECON syndrome is also characterized by short stature (Abu-Libdeh et al., 2022), suggesting a defect in chondrogenesis. A recent advance using a zebrafish mutant WRN model provided experimental evidence that WRN promotes bone development and growth through its ability to resolve G4 structures found at the G-rich promoter of short-stature homeobox (SHOX) gene, which plays an important role in the determination of human height (Tian et al., 2022). It is plausible that RECQL1’s preferential interaction with G-rich promoter elements may play a role in gene regulation as proposed for WRN. Further studies are warranted, as gene expression alterations in RECON syndrome and WS may differ from each other, as is the case in a comparison of WS and BS (Tang et al., 2016).

Over 25 years ago, the RECQL (RECQL1) gene was cloned (Puranam and Blackshear, 1994; Seki et al., 1994). Ironically, it was the first human RECQ helicase gene to be identified yet until only very recently, has it been genetically linked to a hereditary disease. Now RECQL1’s association with a novel chromosomal instability disorder brings the investigation of RECQ helicase deficinciess and their proposed roles in human disease full circle. The lesson suggested by the hereditary linkage of RECQL1 mutation to disease is that a robust and intertwined response to replication, efficient DNA repair and controlled gene expression is paramount to the suppression of pathologically relevant clinical features. The causal relationship of replication stress to collapsed replication forks and their associated DSBs ushers in a new sense of urgency to determine the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the pathology for each genetic condition, including the involvement of abnormal gene regulation. This research will surely be significant in the early diagnosis and treatment of individuals with the genetic disease RECON syndrome. Moreover, the RECQ helicase disease states will continue to be an important model for the study of diseases characterized by chromosomal instability and abnormal genome metabolism.
