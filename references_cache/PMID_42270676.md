# O-linked glycan-dependent gating of TPC2 controls lysosomal excitability and organelle remodeling.
**Authors:** Lin A, Lin NY, Keller M, Schütz R, Wu YZ, Burton W, Kinanti NP, Yao CY, Urban N, Liu YW, Bracher F, Galione A, Hsu HJ, Kuo CY, Chen CC
**Journal:** Nat Commun (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41467-026-74268-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-74268-6)

## Content

Two-pore channel 2 (TPC2) is a lysosomal cation channel involved in calcium and sodium signaling and membrane remodeling. Its dysfunction has been linked to diseases including viral infections, neurodegenerative disorders, and cancer. Here, we identify mucin-type O-linked glycosylation at two conserved luminal residues of TPC2, Ser612 and Ser613, as a structural gating brake. Combining structure-guided mutagenesis, lysosomal patch-clamp recordings, calcium nanodomain imaging, molecular dynamics simulations, and small-molecule modulation, we show that genetic, enzymatic, or pharmacological glycan removal enhances basal and ligand-evoked TPC2 activity. This hyperactivation induces lysosomal tubulation, increases vesicle mobility, and promotes TPC2 clustering. In cancer-related models, glycan-deficient TPC2 also increases cell migration, which can be reversed by inhibitors such as tetrandrine and SC-3. These findings establish luminal glycosylation as a key post-translational regulator of TPC2 gating and reveal a mechanism by which ion channel activity controls lysosomal architecture and disease-relevant cell behavior.

Two-pore channels (TPCs) comprise three mammalian isoforms—TPC1, TPC2, and TPC3—and are cation-permeable channels localized to endolysosomal membranes1,2. TPCs can be activated by endogenous ligands such as NAADP and PI(3,5)P₂3,4 leading to the release of calcium and sodium from acidic organelles. In particular, NAADP-induced TPC2 activation generates localized lysosomal Ca²⁺ nanodomains that contribute to secondary calcium mobilization from the endoplasmic reticulum5,6. These nanodomains regulate diverse cellular processes, including FcγR-mediated phagocytosis7, lysosomal fusion8, osteoclast differentiation9, autophagy and vesicle trafficking10,11. In addition to calcium signaling, TPC2 mediates sodium efflux, which has been implicated in lysosomal membrane remodeling events such as lysosomal tubulation12,13. Loss or alteration of TPC2-mediated Ca²⁺ conductance has been linked to Ebola virus infection14, tauopathies15, metabolic disorders10, embryonic defects16, angiogenesis17, hypopigmentation18, cancer progression19,20, and Parkinson’s disease21. Together, these studies underscore the central role of TPC2 in maintaining lysosomal cation homeostasis in health and disease.

Glycosylation is a widespread and functionally significant post-translational modification that shapes protein folding, trafficking, and activity. N-linked glycosylation, occurring on asparagine residues, is primarily critical for protein folding, stability, and quality control during trafficking through the secretory pathway22. In contrast, mucin-type O-linked glycosylation involves the attachment of N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) to the hydroxyl group of serine or threonine residues23. In ion channels, both N- and O-linked glycans have been shown to modulate gating behavior, voltage dependence, and surface expression. For example, O-linked sialylation of voltage-gated potassium channels Kv2.1, Kv4.2, and Kv4.3 shifts their activation thresholds by 8–16 mV24, while N-linked glycosylation alters temperature sensitivity and trafficking in TRP channels such as TRPM8 and TRPM525,26. In cardiomyocytes, glycosylation of Na_v1.4 and Na_v1.5 differentially regulates gating, and extracellular glycosidases like Klotho can dynamically reshape channel function27. This modification is also essential for the trafficking of Piezo1 and hERG channels, highlighting its broad relevance in channelopathies28. Moreover, asparagine-linked glycosylation enhances the permeability and pore opening of Cav3.2 T-type calcium channels29. While these findings emphasize the critical role of glycosylation in regulating ion channels at the plasma membrane, much less is known about its impact on intracellular channels, particularly those in the endolysosomal system. TPC2, for instance, is known to undergo N-linked glycosylation at conserved luminal residues N611 and N61830,31; however, these studies merely identified putative glycosylation sites without addressing their functional significance. To date, the role of O-linked glycosylation in modulating TPC2 channel activity has not been explored.

Mucin-type O-glycosylation is initiated by the addition of N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) to serine or threonine residues, forming the Tn antigen. This core structure can be further branched and elongated into eight possible core glycans, with cores 1 and 2 being most common32,33. The enzyme C1GALT1 is responsible for synthesizing the core 1 structure, and its genetic disruption leads to the accumulation of unelaborated Tn antigens—truncated O-glycans that are associated with cancer, autoimmune disorders, and neurodegeneration34. By leveraging C1GALT1 knockdown, it is possible to selectively perturb mucin-type O-linked glycosylation while preserving other post-translational modifications. Although mucin-type O-glycosylation has been characterized in a wide range of secretory and membrane proteins, its functional roles have been most extensively examined in mucins and immune receptors. The role of mucin-type O-glycans in ion channel regulation—especially within the endolysosomal system—remains unknown.

Here, we show that mucin-type O-linked glycans on TPC2 function as endogenous modulators of channel gating. By combining live-cell intracellular organelle calcium imaging, whole-lysosome patch-clamp recordings, and structure-guided mutagenesis, we identify conserved O-glycosylated residues within the pore-forming domain of TPC2 that constrain its Ca2+/Na+ conductance. These modifications regulate lysosomal morphology as well as the migratory behavior of leukemia-derived cells. Our findings reveal a distinct regulatory axis for lysosomal ion channel function through glycan-mediated modulation of pore gating.

To investigate whether TPC2 is modified by mucin-type O-glycans synthesized by the glycotransferase C1GALT1, we transiently expressed TPC2 in HeLa cells with or without C1GALT1 knockdown (KD). Efficient knockdown was confirmed by Western blot analysis showing reduced C1GALT1 protein levels (Fig. S1a, left). To assess the O-glycosylation status of TPC2, we immunoprecipitated TPC2-YFP using anti-GFP antibodies, followed by lectin blotting with Vicia villosa agglutinin (VVA), which specifically binds to GalNAc residues characteristic of Tn antigens. The analysis with VVA lectin revealed that C1GALT1 knockdown led to a robust accumulation of unextended O-glycans on TPC2, resulting in significantly enhanced VVA reactivity. This confirms that TPC2 carries mucin-type O-glycans that are normally elongated by C1GALT1 (Fig. S1a, right).

To selectively examine compartment-specific Ca²⁺ signaling, we employed well-characterized small-molecule agonizts: TPC2-A1-N and TPC2-A1-P for TPC2, and ML2-SA1 for TRPML2. TPC2-A1-N and TPC2-A1-P activate TPC2-mediated Ca²⁺ release from LEL, while ML2-SA1 selectively targets TRPML2 without cross-activating TPC237,38. TRPML2 served as a spatial control for calcium release from RE/EE, allowing us to assess the compartmental specificity of O-linked glycan effects. Stimulation with TPC2-A1-N and TPC2-A1-P elicited robust Ca²⁺ transients, and this response was further enhanced by C1GALT1 knockdown in cells expressing either TPC2-GCaMP6s or TPC2-G-GECO1.2 (Fig. 1a–c, f; Fig. S1f, g). In contrast, TRPML2-mediated signals from RE/EE compartments showed no significant difference between control and C1GALT1 KD conditions (Fig. 1d, e, g; Fig. S1h, i), supporting the specificity of O-glycosylation–mediated modulation on TPC2 at the LEL (Fig. 1g, h).

Thus, mucin-type O-glycosylation does not measurably affect TRPML2-mediated Ca²⁺ release from recycling and early endosomes under our experimental conditions, but instead selectively modulates TPC2 function within late endosomes/lysosomes.

To further validate the compartmental and channel specificity of this effect, we examined Ca²⁺ signaling mediated by TRPML1, another major Ca²⁺-permeable channel localized to late endosomes/lysosomes35,39. Using a TRPML1–G-GECO1.2 Ca²⁺ nanodomain imaging strategy (Fig. S1j), we found that ML-SA1–evoked TRPML1 Ca²⁺ responses were not significantly altered by C1GALT1 knockdown (Fig. S1k), indicating that truncation of mucin-type O-glycans does not globally potentiate lysosomal Ca²⁺ release.

We next quantified the activation kinetics of TPC2-mediated Ca²⁺ signals by measuring the initial slope of fluorescence increase following TPC2-A1-N stimulation. This analysis revealed that TPC2-mediated Ca²⁺ signals exhibited significantly faster activation rates in C1GALT1-deficient cells (Fig. S1l), supporting the notion that mucin-type O-glycosylation imposes a channel-specific regulatory constraint on TPC2 gating.

Consistent with this interpretation, pharmacological inhibition of O-linked glycan elongation using benzyl-α-GalNAc similarly enhanced TPC2-mediated Ca²⁺ responses (Fig. S1m), further supporting a functional role of mucin-type O-glycosylation in restricting TPC2 activity.

To establish the physiological relevance of mucin-type O-linked glycosylation in regulating TPC2 gating, we extended our analysis to primary bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs), which endogenously express high levels of TPC2. Using whole-lysosomal patch-clamp recordings, a technically demanding method that enables direct electrophysiological measurement of native lysosomal ion channel activity, we found that C1GALT1 knockout markedly enhanced TPC2 channel activity in these native cells (Fig. 1h–n).

Together, these findings establish that mucin-type O-glycans constrain TPC2-mediated Ca²⁺ release in a compartment- and channel-specific manner, as evidenced by enhanced responses following genetic or pharmacological truncation in both heterologous and endogenous contexts.

We next examined currents evoked by the Na⁺-permeable TPC2 agonist TPC2-A1-P. Consistent with the results obtained using TPC2-A1-N, KD macrophages displayed significantly larger basal currents and enhanced A1-P–evoked inward currents compared with WT cells (Fig. 2d–f).

Additional representative recordings from TPC2-overexpressing HeLa cells further illustrated pronounced differences in both basal and agonist-evoked currents between control and O-glycan–deficient conditions following stimulation with either TPC2-A1-N or TPC2-A1-P (Fig. 2g–l). Notably, humans and mice lack a functional TPC3 gene40,41, and A1-N/A1-P do not activate TPC137, ensuring that the lysosomal responses observed in HeLa cells arise specifically from TPC2.

To assess channel specificity, we performed control experiments in systems lacking functional TPC2. Under these conditions, TPC2-A1-N–evoked inward currents were undetectable in non-transfected HeLa cells and in TPC2 knockout macrophages, regardless of KD status (Fig. S2a–d), supporting the conclusion that the enhanced basal and agonist-evoked currents observed under O-glycan–deficient conditions depend on the presence of TPC2.

To further evaluate whether O-glycan truncation affects endosomal ion conductance more broadly, we performed whole-endosome patch-clamp recordings and examined PI(3,5)P₂–evoked cation currents. The amplitudes of PI(3,5)P₂–evoked currents were comparable between WT and KD macrophages (Fig. S2e–h), suggesting that O-glycan truncation does not measurably alter PI(3,5)P₂-responsive endosomal conductance under these recording conditions. Together with the TRPML2-G-GECO1.2 imaging results, these findings argue against a generalized effect of O-glycan truncation on Ca²⁺ or cation signaling at recycling and early endosomes.

Collectively, these data support a model in which truncation of mucin-type O-linked glycans selectively relieves a suppressive gating constraint on TPC2, resulting in enhanced basal and agonist-evoked Ca²⁺ and Na⁺ currents in the lysosomal membrane.

To determine whether the enhanced Ca²⁺ responses were attributable to upstream changes rather than intrinsic channel modulation, we first measured intra-lysosomal Ca²⁺ content using Oregon Green Dextran 488 BAPTA, a ratiometric calcium indicator that reports lysosomal Ca²⁺ stores42. No significant difference was observed between control and O-glycan–truncated cells, suggesting that the augmented Ca²⁺ release is not due to elevated luminal Ca²⁺ levels (Fig. 3a, b). We next assessed whether O-glycan truncation alters TPC2 expression. RT-qPCR of TPC2 transcripts showed no significant differences between control and C1GALT1-deficient cells (Fig. 3c). Since Rab7a is known to facilitate TPC2 activation43, we asked whether the enhanced Ca²⁺ signals observed upon O-glycan truncation might result from altered Rab7a expression or localization. We first confirmed that C1GALT1 knockdown did not affect Rab7a protein levels (Fig. 3d), and bioinformatic predictions indicated that Rab7a is unlikely to undergo mucin-type O-glycosylation. We then assessed the subcellular distribution of TPC2 and Rab7a and found no significant differences in colocalization between control and glycan-deficient cells (Fig. 3e, f), suggesting that the observed enhancement in TPC2 activity is not attributable to changes in Rab7a expression or localization. Together, these results indicate that the enhanced TPC2-mediated Ca²⁺ signals observed upon O-glycan truncation are not accompanied by detectable changes in luminal Ca²⁺ stores, TPC2 abundance, or subcellular localization. Instead, these findings are most consistent with a functional enhancement of TPC2 channel activity, likely involving glycan-dependent modulation of channel gating. Notably, although C1GALT1-deficient cells exhibit elevated basal TPC2 activity, intra-lysosomal Ca²⁺ content remained unchanged under resting conditions. This observation is consistent with the fact that our assay reports steady-state luminal Ca²⁺ levels rather than rapid Ca²⁺ flux. To directly evaluate channel function, we recorded Ca²⁺ currents following stimulation with the selective agonist TPC2-A1-N. Cells with truncated O-glycosylation exhibited significantly larger inward Ca²⁺ currents compared to controls, even in the absence of TPC2-A1-N stimulation, indicating enhanced basal Ca²⁺ efflux from lysosomes to the cytosol (Fig. 3g–j). We next asked whether O-glycan truncation affects channel sensitivity to ligand activation. Dose–response analysis revealed that TPC2-A1-N elicited concentration-dependent currents in both wild-type and glycan-truncated cells. Notably, glycan truncation led to a pronounced leftward shift in the concentration–response curve and a marked increase in the maximal current amplitude (Fig. S3), indicating enhanced potency and efficacy. To determine whether O-glycan truncation also alters the intrinsic biophysical properties of TPC2, we analyzed current–voltage (I–V) relationships under standard recording conditions. As a lysosomal Ca2+/Na+ channel, TPC2 mediates both Ca²⁺ release and Na⁺ efflux depending on ligand context and electrochemical gradients37,45. Given that our results suggested a glycosylation-dependent gating effect, we further examined whether O-glycan status influences relative Ca²⁺/Na⁺ permeation. To test this, we performed whole-lysosomal patch-clamp recordings under bi-ionic conditions to simultaneously measure inward Ca²⁺ and outward Na⁺ currents (Fig. 3k). While TPC2-A1-N evoked characteristic bidirectional currents in both control and glycan-truncated cells, the latter exhibited significantly greater fold increases in both Ca²⁺ and Na⁺ conductance (Fig. 3l–m). Although the apparent reversal potentials differed between WT and KD cells under bi-ionic conditions, both Ca²⁺- and Na⁺-permeable currents were enhanced. Thus, O-glycan truncation increases overall TPC2 conductance, while altering the relative contribution of Ca²⁺ and Na⁺ under bi-ionic recording conditions. These findings indicate that mucin-type O-linked glycans act as a gating brake on TPC2, limiting not only overall channel activity but also its dual Ca²⁺/Na⁺ permeability.

Having established that TPC2 undergoes mucin-type O-linked glycosylation, we next sought to identify the specific serine (Ser) or threonine (Thr) residues responsible. Using NetOGlyc 4.0, we identified 17 predicted O-glycosylation sites among the 81 Ser/Thr residues in human TPC2. Of these, only three—S107, S612, and S613—are predicted to face the lysosomal lumen (Supplementary Table 1), with S612 and S613 located near the S5–S6 pore-forming region (residues 255–279 and 636–659).

Notably, Ser613 aligns with a highly conserved motif across mammalian TPC2 orthologs and corresponds to Ser612 in TPC1 paralogs (Fig. 4a, b). Both S612 and S613 are adjacent to proline residues—hallmarks of mucin-type glycosylation sites46—and are positioned in the luminal loop between transmembrane domains 5 and 6 (Fig. 4a), which contributes to the ion-conducting pore47. To test the functional importance of these sites, we generated alanine substitution mutants targeting Ser613 alone (S613A) and both Ser612/613 (S612/613A). Western blot analysis confirmed reduced glycosylation in the mutants, consistent with disruption of glycan modification (Fig. 4c). This conclusion was further supported by VVA lectin blotting, which showed a marked loss of GalNAc-specific signal in the S612/613A mutant (Fig. S1a), confirming that these residues account for the major mucin-type O-glycosylation on TPC2.

To exclude the possibility that the enhanced channel activity arises from altered subcellular localization, we examined the intracellular distribution of WT and S612/613A TPC2 by confocal microscopy. Both WT and mutant TPC2 exhibited comparable colocalization with the lysosomal marker LAMP1, with no detectable difference in lysosomal targeting between the two constructs (Fig. 4d). Consistently, colocalization analysis with endosomal Rab markers revealed that the S612/613 A mutation did not alter the distribution of TPC2 along the endosomal pathway, with both WT and mutant TPC2 showing minimal overlap with Rab4-positive RE or Rab5-positive endosomes and predominant localization to Rab7-positive LEL (Fig. S4a, b). In contrast, colocalization with the ER marker R-CEPIA was low for both WT and S612/613 A TPC2, indicating no increased ER association upon mutation. Quantitative analysis using Pearson’s correlation coefficient confirmed that S612/613A does not alter the subcellular distribution of TPC2. These results indicate that the enhanced channel activity observed in the S612/613 A mutant cannot be attributed to changes in intracellular localization.

Whole-lysosomal patch-clamp recordings showed that TPC2S612/613A exhibited significantly enhanced currents upon stimulation with either TPC2-A1-N or TPC2-A1-P, compared to wild-type (Fig. 4e–h). A similar enhancement was observed upon application of PI(3,5)P₂ (Fig. 4i–k), paralleling the phenotype seen in C1GALT1 knockdown cells and supporting the conclusion that loss of glycosylation at these sites facilitates channel activation.

To further validate that truncation of mucin-type O-glycosylation enhances TPC2 activity at the level of individual channels, we performed single-channel recordings from isolated lysosomal membranes using an outside-out patch-clamp configuration under calcium-rich luminal conditions (105 mM Ca²⁺, pH 4.6) (Fig. S4c). Compared with wild-type TPC2, both C1GALT1-deficient lysosomes and the TPC2S612/613A mutant exhibited increased single-channel activity, as reflected by larger unitary current amplitudes and right-shifted all-points amplitude histograms. These results indicate that loss of O-glycosylation enhances TPC2 channel activity at the single-channel level.

As an orthogonal validation to exclude the possibility that the enhanced activity of TPC2S612/613A arises from alanine-specific structural effects, we generated an independent mutant in which Ser612 and Ser613 were replaced with cysteines (TPC2S612/613C). Whole-lysosome recordings showed that TPC2S612/613C phenocopied the alanine mutant, exhibiting increased basal currents and enhanced agonist-evoked currents in response to both TPC2-A1-N and TPC2-A1-P (Fig. S4d, e).

We further tested whether additional global truncation of mucin-type O-glycans could further potentiate TPC2 currents once the Ser612/613 sites were removed. Notably, neither C1GALT1 knockdown nor pharmacological inhibition of O-glycan elongation using benzyl-α-GalNAc produced an additional increase in TPC2S612/613A currents (Fig. S4f–i). This epistasis is consistent with Ser612/613 being the dominant functional O-glycosylation sites that impose a suppressive constraint on TPC2 gating.

Together, these results identify Ser612 and Ser613 within the Domain II pore loop as key regulatory residues for TPC2 gating, with their glycosylation state directly modulating both basal activity and ligand responsiveness.

Calcium and sodium flux through lysosomal ion channels orchestrate distinct aspects of intracellular organization. Lysosomal calcium release has been shown to regulate LEL trafficking, motility, and TFEB-mediated transcriptional responses6,48–50, whereas sodium efflux through TPC2 promotes lysosomal membrane tubulation and remodeling, particularly during macropinocytosis12. These findings highlight that both Ca²⁺ and Na⁺ signaling are critical components of lysosomal dynamics.

To assess whether pharmacological inhibition could suppress non-glycosylated TPC2 hyperactivity, we first evaluated SC-3, a synthesized tetrandrine-derived small-molecule analog designed to target TPC2 (Fig. S5a; Supplementary Information). SC-3 was selected for initial characterization because its chemical scaffold is closely related to tetrandrine, yet it exhibits improved suitability for electrophysiological assays, rather than to establish a comparative potency hierarchy among TPC2 inhibitors. Tetrandrine, a plant-derived alkaloid, was previously identified as a TPC2 inhibitor in a screen for compounds blocking viral entry14. Whole-lysosomal patch-clamp recordings in glycosylation-deficient THP-1 cells demonstrated that SC-3 robustly inhibited both basal and TPC2-A1-N–stimulated inward currents (Fig. S5b, c), confirming its efficacy in directly suppressing TPC2 activity enhanced by O-glycan truncation. Consistent with these observations, whole-lysosome patch-clamp recordings from HeLa cells expressing TPC2 further demonstrated that SC-3 inhibits TPC2-A1-P–evoked inward currents in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. S5d). Quantification of current amplitudes at −100 mV revealed progressive suppression of TPC2-mediated currents with increasing SC-3 concentrations (0.1–100 μM) (Fig. S5e), indicating that SC-3 directly attenuates TPC2 conductance independently of the permeant ion species.

To independently compare the inhibitory efficacy of SC-3 with its parent compound tetrandrine, we next employed a plasma membrane–targeted TPC2LL/AA variant and measured Ca²⁺ signals using Fura-2 imaging37. Under these conditions, both SC-3 and tetrandrine suppressed TPC2-dependent Ca²⁺ responses in a concentration-dependent manner, with SC-3 exhibiting slightly greater inhibitory potency (Fig. S5f). Given that TPC2 hyperactivation leads to pronounced changes in lysosomal dynamics and ion flux, we next asked whether these alterations might influence cell-level behaviors such as migration and adhesion. This question is particularly relevant in disease contexts where glycosylation is dysregulated, such as in tumors and chronic inflammation52. Truncated O-glycans are frequently observed in both solid cancers and hematological malignancies, and altered glycan structures have been shown to impact membrane protein trafficking, cytoskeletal organization, and cell motility53–56. Because TPC2 activity has been implicated in regulating integrin trafficking and cell motility in several cell types, including leukocytes and invasive cancer cells20,57,58, we examined whether glycan-dependent TPC2 activation could modulate the migratory properties of THP-1–derived macrophages—a model widely used to study monocytic cell function and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) biology59,60. To evaluate the downstream impact of TPC2 hyperactivation, we examined THP-1 macrophage behavior. Cells with elevated TPC2 activity exhibited significantly increased migration and adhesion compared to wild-type controls (Fig. S5i, k). Notably, treatment with SC-3, tetrandrine, or Ned-19 effectively suppressed the hyper-adhesive phenotype, restoring cell behavior to near-control levels.

To determine whether hyperactive TPC2 regulation similarly influences epithelial cell behavior, we next performed wound-healing assays in HeLa cells expressing either WT TPC2 or TPC2S612/613A mutant (Fig. S5j, l). Cells expressing TPC2S612/613 exhibited significantly accelerated wound closure compared with WT TPC2–expressing cells (Fig. S5l), indicating enhanced migratory capacity. These findings demonstrate that TPC2 hyperactivation promotes cell migration not only in immune cells but also in epithelial contexts.

Together, these findings identify Ser612 and Ser613 within the Domain II pore loop as critical regulatory nodes for TPC2 function. Their glycosylation state constrains both agonist-induced activation and intrinsic channel gating, preserving lysosomal ion homeostasis. These findings reveal that glycan-dependent regulation of TPC2 not only shapes lysosomal ion dynamics but also modulates cell-level behaviors relevant to immune function and leukemia biology.

Further conformational analysis elucidated the structural basis of this enhanced flexibility. In the glycosylated model, the bulky glycan restricts loop mobility and prevents it from approaching the pore domain. By contrast, in the non-glycosylated model, the luminal loop shows a pronounced tendency to tilt downward, moving closer toward the central pore region (Fig. 6c, d). These findings collectively demonstrate that glycosylation at S613 plays a critical role in constraining the dynamic behavior of the Domain II luminal loop. We next analyzed the spatial distribution of Na+ ions near the luminal entrance of the pore. In the glycosylated system, 3D ion density maps showed that Na+ ions accumulate not directly above the selectivity filter (SF) or entrance but rather around the glycosylated S613 site (Fig. 6e). This observation suggests that while the hydrophilicity of the glycan may attract cations, its bulky structure also serves as a physical barrier that hinders further ion penetration into the true conduction pathway.

Overall, glycosylation at S613 creates a “molecular gate” at the luminal entry that traps but obstructs cations. Removal of this glycan eliminates the spatial hindrance, enabling more efficient ion entry into the channel and providing a direct molecular-level explanation for the experimentally observed increase in macroscopic currents following de-glycosylation.

To further determine whether removal of the S613 glycan permits not only ion access but also conductive rearrangements of the SF, which has been shown to adopt multiple ion-selective conformations in TPC channels, we performed additional molecular dynamics simulations under an applied transmembrane potential of +225 mV61,62. Under voltage-driven conditions, the non-glycosylated hTPC2 system exhibited a spontaneous dilation of the SF, reflected by increased intersubunit distances between key SF-forming residues (V652, N653, and N654) after approximately 300 ns (Fig. S6a, b). Concomitant with this structural rearrangement, a Na⁺ ion was observed to traverse the SF and enter the central cavity, providing direct evidence of voltage-driven permeation (Fig. S6c, d).

In contrast, neither SF dilation nor ion permeation events were detected in the glycosylated systems or in simulations performed without an applied potential. These observations indicate that glycosylation at S613 suppresses not only luminal ion access but also the SF conformational plasticity required for conductive transitions. Together, these results provide a structural framework linking luminal glycosylation to suppressed channel conductance by limiting both ion access and the conformational adaptability of the selectivity filter.

For decades, glycosylation has been recognized as a critical post-translational modification (PTM) that governs the function of ion channels at the plasma membrane. However, its impact on channels within the endolysosomal system has remained largely unexplored. Here, we identify a regulatory mechanism, establishing that mucin-type O-linked glycosylation acts as a structural brake restraining the activity of the key lysosomal cation channel, TPC2. This conclusion is built on convergent evidence: truncating O-glycans, either by knocking down C1GALT1 or via the pharmacological inhibitor benzyl-α-GalNAc, consistently and robustly enhanced TPC2 activity in systems ranging from overexpressing HeLa cells to endogenous-expressing THP-1 macrophages and mouse primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs). Notably, whole-lysosome patch-clamp recordings provided direct proof, showing that de-glycosylated channels exhibited profoundly enhanced Ca2+ and Na+ currents upon stimulation with the agonizts or the endogenous ligand PI(3,5)P₂.

Recent studies have raised concerns regarding the specificity of TPC2-A1-N in whole-cell cytosolic Ca²⁺ imaging assays, where Ca²⁺ signals can originate from multiple intracellular sources63. To avoid potential misinterpretation, it is therefore important to distinguish between experimental contexts. In the present study, our conclusions are based exclusively on lysosomal patch-clamp recordings and lysosomal Ca²⁺ nanodomain imaging, which directly assess ion fluxes across the lysosomal membrane and are not confounded by cytosolic or ER Ca²⁺ signaling. Moreover, key findings were independently validated using TPC2-A1-P, further supporting the robustness of our conclusions.

To understand the molecular basis of this enhanced activity, we turned to structural modeling and mutagenesis. Our structural modeling and functional data delineate a clear molecular mechanism. Bioinformatic predictions, confirmed by mutagenesis, pinpoint this regulation to the highly conserved Ser612 and Ser613 residues in the Domain II pore loop. The S612/613A double mutant perfectly phenocopied the C1GALT1 knockdown, displaying significantly higher basal and agonist-evoked currents than the wild-type, confirming that removing glycosylation at these sites is sufficient to unleash channel activity. Molecular dynamics simulations further revealed the structural basis for this effect: in the glycosylated wild-type channel, the glycan on S613 forms a hydrogen bond with R664 in the pore region, which serves to stabilize and lock the pore loop’s conformation. Upon removal of this glycan, the loop’s conformational flexibility increases dramatically, allowing it to “tilt” inwards and thus relieve steric hindrance to the ion entry pathway—a compelling explanation for the enhanced currents. Indeed, earlier observations have hinted that luminal domains may act as channel regulators: a previous study found that a truncated TPC2 pore, created by removing the large luminal loop of Domain I, remained functional and was even energetically more favorable for ion permeation64. This finding suggested that large luminal loop structures might impose a constraint on channel function—a general principle our study now directly confirms, by precisely defining the key role of O-linked glycans in the Domain II loop in executing this constraint.

These findings not only define the structural brake imposed by glycosylation, but also raise questions about how this regulatory mechanism integrates into broader models of TPC2 activation. It has been proposed that ions access the TPC2 pore through lateral fenestrations rather than along the central vertical axis61, and that the selectivity filter (SF) possesses intrinsic flexibility to transition from a non-conductive “pre-open” to a conductive “open” state62. Our simulations reveal that the glycan linked to S613 is positioned directly above the central pore axis and generates a strong negative electrostatic potential. This spatial and electrostatic configuration repels incoming cations away from the central conduit, potentially diverting ion entry toward peripheral or lateral access routes. In addition, the S613 glycan forms a stabilizing hydrogen bond with R664 in the pore domain, reinforcing a rigid loop conformation that may further restrict axial ion permeation. Upon glycan removal, the Domain II luminal loop undergoes a pronounced downward tilt. This conformational shift partially occludes the central vertical entry path but may also relieve constraints that previously limited access through lateral fenestrations. Thus, glycosylation at S613 not only alters the local electrostatic environment but also shapes the pore’s three-dimensional architecture in a manner that modulates ion entry preference (Fig. 6f). While our study does not directly resolve the precise permeation route, the observed biophysical consequences of luminal glycosylation provide a structural rationale for the lateral ion access model proposed in prior simulations61. By identifying the S613 glycan as a central structural determinant that regulates the balance between vertical and lateral ion entry, our findings introduce an additional gating layer. Building on the current framework of TPC2 activation, which involves (I) allosteric priming by ligand binding and (II) spontaneous rearrangement of the SF into a conductive state62, our findings introduce a third layer of regulation: (III) fine-tuning of conductance amplitude through the glycosylation-dependent modulation of ion access geometry.

The loss of this glycan brake, leading to TPC2 hyperactivity, drives a cascade of striking cellular consequences. Using super-resolution imaging, we observed that the S612/613A mutant caused pronounced lysosomal membrane tubulation and aberrant channel clustering. These phenomena align with previous reports linking TPC2-mediated Na+ flux to membrane remodeling12. Crucially, these phenotypes, driven by aberrant channel gating, are reversed by TPC2 antagonists, including the inhibitor SC-3, tetrandrine, and Ned-19, firmly establishing a causal link from molecular gating to cellular function. This O-glycan–dependent gating mechanism of TPC2 extends beyond ion conductance to influence whole-cell behavior. In THP-1–derived macrophages, glycan-truncation–induced TPC2 hyperactivity leads to enhanced migration and adhesion—phenotypes consistent with TPC2’s established role in integrin signaling58,65. Truncated glycans, in particular, often arise under conditions such as hypoxia, inflammation, or Golgi stress, and are known to modulate membrane protein behavior65. As a monocytic leukemia-derived line frequently used to study immune cell dynamics, THP-1 provides a relevant model to examine how post-translational regulation of TPC2 may affect disease-associated cellular behaviors.

In this context, it is noteworthy that O-linked glycosylation in immune cells is dynamically remodeled during inflammatory activation, including LPS/TLR4 signaling, frequently resulting in altered glycan elongation and sialylation66–68. Such glycan remodeling has been widely implicated in tuning membrane protein function during immune responses. Our findings raise the possibility that inflammation-associated changes in O-glycan structure may represent a physiological mechanism for modulating TPC2 activity in immune cells. At the same time, inflammatory stimulation is likely to engage additional regulatory layers beyond glycosylation alone, a complexity that warrants further investigation.

Intriguingly, previous studies have reported elevated TPC2 expression in AML cells compared to normal bone marrow69,70, suggesting a potential role for this lysosomal ion channel in leukemogenesis or disease progression. In line with these observations, exploratory analysis of the Beat AML cohort revealed that TPCN2 overexpression in KMT2A-rearranged patients is associated with a trend toward shorter overall survival, and transcriptional programs characteristic of aggressive disease, including enrichment of E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, inflammatory response, and gene sets linked to cancer invasiveness (Fig. S7). Although these associations are correlative and do not establish causality, they support the notion that TPC2-dependent pathways may be particularly relevant in high-risk AML subtypes. Meanwhile, aberrant glycosylation, especially truncation of mucin-type O-glycans, is a well-documented feature of leukemia and has been implicated in impaired differentiation, altered signaling, and therapy resistance55,71,72. Our mechanistic findings in THP-1 cells suggest that glycosylation-dependent modulation of TPC2 activity can directly influence cellular behaviors associated with malignancy, including migration and adhesion. Taken together, these observations raise the possibility that dysregulated glycosylation in AML may indirectly shape disease phenotypes by altering the functional state of lysosomal ion channels such as TPC2. Further investigation into the interplay between TPC2 and specific glycosylation pathways, including the role of enzymes such as C1GALT1, may therefore provide an insight into glycan-based regulatory mechanisms in AML.

Together, these observations support a broader conceptual model in which glycosylation serves as a dynamic regulatory layer that tunes lysosomal ion channel activity and shapes downstream organelle and cellular behavior. Our study identifies O-linked glycosylation as a critical suppressive mechanism of lysosomal ion channel activity, establishing a direct functional role for mucin-type glycans in modulating TPC2 gating. By integrating structural modeling, electrophysiology, imaging, and cell-based assays, we uncover a glycan-mediated constraint at the Domain II pore loop that controls ionic flux, organelle morphology, and downstream cellular behavior. These findings extend the well-established regulatory principle of glycosylation from plasma membrane channels to the endolysosomal system. In doing so, we reveal a unifying mechanism by which post-translational modifications, such as glycan trimming under pathological stress, may reshape lysosomal excitability and cellular responses. This work not only provides insights into the molecular control of TPC2 but also highlights glycan gating as a potentially generalizable paradigm linking metabolic or inflammatory cues to ion channel behavior in health and disease.

The protein was embedded in a 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) bilayer and solvated with NaCl to neutralize the system and induce the physiological ion concentration of 0.15 M. All simulations were conducted using the AMBER99 SB-ILDN force field with the GROMACS-2020 software package. For glycosylated systems, sugar moieties were added to the same site on both Domain I and Domain II of the TPC2 homodimer, resulting in two independent systems designated as TPC2_S612 and TPC2_S613. GLYCAM06 force field parameters were then converted from AMBER to GROMACS format using Acpype software, which enables effective topology generation for glycan–protein complexes. The attached glycan was modeled as the Core 1 disaccharide (Galβ1-3GalNAc), and corresponding parameters were derived using the GLYCAM06 force field. During system preparation, particular attention was given to glycosidic linkages and the structural conformation of sugar–serine bonds to ensure accurate modeling of glycoprotein interactions. All simulations were performed in the NPT ensemble, maintaining a temperature of 310 K and a pressure of 1 bar using a velocity-rescaling thermostat. Semi-isotropic pressure coupling was implemented with a coupling time of 0.1 ps and a compressibility of 4.5 × 10⁻⁵ bar⁻¹ in both the xy-plane and z-direction. Long-range electrostatic interactions were computed using the Particle-Mesh-Ewald (PME) method with a Fourier spacing of 0.12 nm and fourth-order interpolation. The equilibration protocol comprised two stages. Initially, the system temperature was gradually increased from 0 K to 100 K, then 200 K, and finally 310 K, with each step run for 500 ps. During this phase, positional restraints with a force constant of 1000 kJ mol⁻¹ nm⁻² were applied to all complex atoms. In the subsequent phase at 310 K, restraints were progressively reduced from 1000 kJ mol⁻¹ nm⁻² to 500 kJ mol⁻¹ nm⁻², then to 300 kJ mol⁻¹ nm⁻², and finally to 100 kJ mol⁻¹ nm⁻². After completion of equilibration, all positional restraints were removed for the production runs. Production molecular dynamics simulations were performed for different durations depending on the system. For the glycosylated TPC2 systems, two independent simulations were carried out corresponding to glycosylation at residues S612 and S613, with each simulation run for 150 ns.
