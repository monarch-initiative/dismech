Eukaryotic transcription factors (TFs) form local, high-concentration hubs at
specific genomic loci through dynamic, multivalent protein-protein interactions
mediated by their low-complexity domains. The hub formation behavior plays an
essential role in TFs' transcriptional activation activities. Characterizing the
dimensions, dynamics, and regulation of TF hubs requires high-resolution imaging
of TFs in their native cellular environment, but much of such biophysical
characterization remains missing. Here, we combined CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome
editing and advanced quantitative cell imaging, including single-molecule
microscopy, to investigate the dynamic behaviors of the endogenous oncogenic
fusion TF EWS::FLI1 in Ewing sarcoma cells. We found that endogenous EWS::FLI1
forms dynamic, sub-diffraction-limit hubs with mechanisms of dissolution that
prevent the hubs from achieving macroscopic liquid-liquid phase separation. Hub
formation is a neomorphic behavior of EWS::FLI1 that is not directly conferred
by its parental proteins, EWSR1 and FLI1. We found that during mitosis,
EWS::FLI1 hubs dissolve, but EWS::FLI1 molecules continue to dynamically bind
and unbind mitotic chromosomes, revealing a role of EWS::FLI1 in mitotic
bookmarking. Nascent RNA destabilizes EWS::FLI1 hubs on chromatin, but it does
not affect the dimensions of the hubs. Finally, we visualized endogenous
EWS::FLI1 hubs upon treatment with various compounds that were previously
indicated to affect EWS::FLI1 function. We found that LY2835219 and trabectedin
significantly alter the nuclear distribution of endogenous EWS::FLI1, disrupting
and mislocalizing EWS::FLI1 hubs, respectively. This finding highlights the
therapeutic potential of both compounds for Ewing sarcoma. Together, our results
reveal new insights into the assembly and regulation of endogenous EWS::FLI1
hubs at an unprecedented resolution. The methodology developed here will be
useful for characterizing the functional hubs of many regular and pathological
TFs in the future.

Introduction  29 
DNA-binding transcription factors (TFs) are crucial regulators of eukaryotic transcription, comprising DNA-30 
binding domains and transactivation domains that interact with other TFs and components of the 31 
transcriptional machinery to activate transcription. The transactivation domains usually contain 32 
intrinsically disordered low -complexity domains (LCDs) that do not fold into stable three -dimensional 33 
structures, thus are not amenable to investigation through the conventional structure -function 34 
framework. Recent work based on live -cell single-molecule imaging has revealed that TF LCDs undergo 35 
dynamic, multivalent, and selective protein-protein interactions, which drive the formation of high local 36 
concentration TF hubs at target genes. Under certain conditions, such as when LCDs are overexpressed, 37

to a variety of hub-forming transcriptional regulators10–12. TF LCDs are also frequently mutated in diseases, 43 
including many types of cancers, and the pathological implications of aberrant TF hubs have been 44 
documented in an increasing number of reports 13–17. We note that here we use the term “hub” to refer 45 
to local high -concentration TF clusters mediated by LCD -LCD interactions. Historically, similar clusters 46 
have been described using various other terms, most often "condensate", which was initially linked to 47 
liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) and now more broadly defined as membrane-less compartments that 48 
selectively concentrate biomolecules, regardless of whether LLPS is involved 18. We adopt this broader 49 
definition of condensates, preserve the original terminology used in the cited studies, and use “assembly” 50 
as an inclusive, mechanism-agnostic term to describe LCD-mediated biomolecular clusters. 51 
Despite the critical role of TF hubs in transcriptional control and disease mechanisms, many questions 52 
about them remain unanswered due to limitations of widely used imaging and sample preparation 53 
methods. Specifically, most existing works have investigated TF hubs using conventional, diffraction -54 
limited microscopy techniques, which are unable to precisely characterize the dynamics, spatial 55 
distribution, and dimensions of sub -diffraction objects, thereby limiting the ability to link the physical 56 
properties of small TF hubs to their functional contributions 19. In addition, many studies imaged 57 
fluorescently labeled proteins overexpressed in cells. While valuable insights have been generated in such 58 
settings, a significant caveat is that multivalent interactions and hub formation behaviors are sensitively 59 
dependent on protein concentrations20,21. Studies that perform extensive characterization of endogenous 60 
TF hubs using high -resolution imaging techniques are highly desirable but remain very limited in 61 
number2,22.  62 
To fill in this gap, here we use single-molecule imaging methods, including photoactivated localization 63 
microscopy (PALM)23 and single-particle tracking (SPT)24,25, to characterize the dimensions, dynamics, and 64 
regulation of endogenous TF hubs in the cell. We use EWS::FLI1, an oncogenic fusion TF that is the 65 
hallmark of Ewing sarcoma, as the model TF in this study. It is encoded by a fusion gene produced by the 66 
t(11;22)(q24;q12) chromosomal translocation that fuses the intrinsically disordered LCD of EWSR1, an 67 
RNA-binding protein, with the well -structured DNA-binding domain of FLI1, a TF that plays roles in 68 
development and homeostasis 26. We and others have previously reported that endogenous EWS::FLI1 69 
forms local, high-concentration hubs at GGAA microsatellites via a combination of EWS::FLI1-DNA binding 70 
and homotypic, multivalent interactions of EWS LCD 1,27,28. Yet, the dimensions of endogenous EWS::FLI1 71 
hubs across the cell nucleus remain unquantified. It is also unknown whether the hub formation behavior 72 
is a property of either parental protein of EWS::FLI1 (EWSR1 or FLI1) or a neomorphic property of the 73 
oncogenic fusion. Another unexplored question is how EWS::FLI1 hubs are regulated during mitosis, when 74 
some TFs are evicted from chromatin as it compacts, and others remain bound to specific genomic loci29. 75 
The behaviors of TF hubs during mitosis impact how the gene expression program is restored after cell 76 
division30. In addition, although RNA is known to play a role in nuclear organization31 and modulate phase 77 
separation of specific transcription regulators, including MED1 and ENL32,33, it remains unknown whether 78

Endogenous EWS::FLI1 forms sub-diffraction-limit hubs that undergo dynamic turnover 86 
To quantify the dimensions of endogenous EWS::FLI1 hubs, we performed PALM on our previously 87 
established knock -in Ewing sarcoma cell line A673, where we fused a HaloTag to the C -terminus of 88 
endogenous EWS::FLI1 via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing1. With this labeling, we visualized the 89 
dynamic behaviors of EWS::FLI1 in its native cellular context with single-molecule resolution. PALM, based 90 
on the single-molecule localization technique, offers a spatial resolution of ~20 nm with our imaging setup, 91 
significantly higher than diffraction-limited microscopy. A well -documented issue with traditional PALM 92 
is spurious clustering caused by repeated appearances of the same molecule across multiple frames, due 93 
to factors including frame discretization of a single emission event, variable photobleaching times, and 94 
photoblinking35–41. Among different correction methods, pair-correlation PALM (PC -PALM) is known to 95 
faithfully report true clustering, making it an excellent tool for probing the presence of clusters and their 96 
length scale 40,42,43. We applied PC -PALM to the raw single -molecule localizations of EWS::FLI1 44–46 to 97 
determine whether the apparent clustering of localizations represents true EWS::FLI1 clusters or artifacts. 98

EWS::FLI1 hubs dissolve during mitosis, and EWS::FLI1 dynamically interacts with mitotic chromatin 208 
Since IDR-mediated biomolecular assemblies are involved in many cellular processes,  their evolution 209 
during mitosis can impact many cellular functions. Despite this, the mitotic behaviors of only a handful of 210 
assembly-forming proteins have been investigated thus far; for example, mitotic hyperphosphorylation of 211 
the nucleolar proteins Ki-67 and NPM1 is reported to alter the charge blockiness of their IDRs, i.e. whether 212 
charged amino acids are arranged in large blocks or randomly dispersed , thereby modulating their 213 
condensation levels69. However, mitotic regulation of TF hubs remains unexplored. To fill this gap , we 214 
tracked the fate of endogenous EWS::FLI1 hubs throughout the cell cycle and compared the interaction 215 
and diffusion dynamics of EWS::FLI1 during mitosis and interphase. Since the association of many TFs with 216 
mitotic chromatin is known to be affected by cell fixation70, here we imaged EWS::FLI1 hubs in live knock-217 
in A673 cells that express endogenous EWS::FLI1-Halo. We treated the cells with the anti-mitotic agent 218 
nocodazole to synchronize them in the cell cycle. Meanwhile, we stained the cells with a DNA dye (Hoechst 219

Discussion 406 
An important caveat in the characterization of IDR-mediated biomolecular assemblies arises from their 407 
strong dependence on protein concentration. Numerous studies have shown that overexpress ed IDR-408 
containing proteins can form large, liquid-droplet-like puncta in the cell, which may or may not form under 409 
endogenous concentrations21,91. The formation of excessive condensates in such overexpression systems 410 
can distort or obscure the true properties, functions, and regulat ory mechanisms of the IDR of interest. 411 
Despite this, few assembly-forming transcription regulators  have been studied at  strictly endogenous 412 
concentrations. Here, we fill this gap by systematically investigating the dimensions, energetics, dynamics, 413 
and regulation of endogenous EWS::FLI1 hubs in Ewing sarcoma cells using quantitative live-cell imaging 414 
methods with single-molecule resolution (Figure 6).  415

Using PALM, we quantified the dimensions of sub-diffraction-limit EWS::FLI1 hubs and found that 417 
their size distribution is consistent with that of a supersaturated system predicted by the classical theory 418 
of homogeneous nucleation. While preceding studies have predicted and observed supersaturated (and 419 
subsaturated) protein clusters in vitro92–97 or in the cell98,99, this is the first time that the energetics and 420 
kinetics of endogenous TF hub  formation have been characterized in the framework of the classical 421 
nucleation theory. Our finding raises the question of why the EWS::FLI1 hub size distribution appears to 422 
be held at a  stationary nonequilibrium steady state  without progressing to larger, phase -separated 423 
droplets, implying the existence of a hub dissolution mechanism. Although the identity of the dissolution 424 
pathway remains unknown, our estimated hub nucleation rate  ( 34.15  hubs per second ) sets the 425 
approximate timescale of the dissolution process. It will be of future interest to investigate the possible 426 
TF hub dissolution pathways that can occur at rates in this scale, such as  chaperone-mediated 427 
dissolution54,55,100, phosphorylation -mediated dissolution 57,58,101, and competition between multivalent 428 
interactions and DNA-protein binding102. In addition, our results suggest that endogenous EWS::FLI1 hubs 429 
are supersaturated clusters8 rather than bona fide LLPS droplets and are likely different from transcription 430 
condensates formed at super-enhancers reported in previous work8,103,104. 431 
EWS::FLI1 exhibits multiple neomorphic behaviors that its parental proteins (EWSR1 and FLI1) lack , 432 
including remodeling GGAA microsatellites into de novo  enhancers105,106, inactivating canonical ETS 433 
enhancers by displacing wild -type ETS TFs106, and inducing aberrant alternative splicing 107. Here, using 434 
PALM to quantify protein distributions in the cell with sub -diffraction resolution, we showed that hub 435 
formation is another neomorphic behavior of EWS::FLI1. Specifically, we found that while both parental 436 
proteins formed puncta, neither exhibited the same degree of clustering as EWS::FLI1. This result supports 437 
our previous conclusion that hub formation behavior plays an important role in the oncogenic functions 438 
of EWS::FLI1, underscoring the potential of targeting this behavior in therapeutic development. Many 439 
oncogenic fusion proteins resulting from chromosomal translocation s have been reported to undergo 440 
LLPS when overexpressed in the cell108. In the future, it will be interesting to investigate whether LLPS or 441 
related assembly formation is  a neomorphic behavior of fusion proteins other than EWS::FLI1 and how 442 
these neomorphic assemblies may contribute to the oncogenesis of different cancer s. The imaging and 443 
analysis approach we develop here will provide helpful tools for this purpose. 444 
We then investigated whether EWS::FLI1 hubs are regulated during mitosis or by RNA  using various 445 
live-cell imaging approaches. We found that during mitosis, EWS::FLI1 hubs dissolve, but EWS::FLI1 446 
remains capable of binding to and dissociating from chromatin, albeit more dynamically and with a smaller 447 
bound fraction than during interphase. This suggest that similar to several reported TFs75, EWS::FLI1 can 448 
also serve as a mitotic bookmark  that helps to restart transcriptional programs in daughter cells after 449 
mitosis. It will be of future interest to investigate whether and how mitotic bookmarking by EWS::FLI1 450 
may contribute to  the tumorigenesis of Ewing sarcoma. In addition , since a burst in protein 451 
phosphorylation occurs as cells go into and progress through mitosis 109,110, our observed dissolution of 452 
EWS::FLI1 hubs during mitosis indicates a potential role of phosphorylation in regulating the hubs  and 453 
warrants future investigation of this regulation mechanism.  454

Contrary to mitosis, when we remove nascent RNA from the cells, EWS::FLI1 binds to chromatin more 455 
stably with the hub dimensions  unchanged. This finding highlights similarities and differences between 456 
EWS::FLI1 hubs and other transcription-related condensates, such as Mediator condensates, where RNA 457 
plays an essential role in condensate formation. Specifically, v arying RNA concentrations  strongly 458 
influences both the dimensions and dynamics of Mediator condensates , with transcriptional inhibition 459 
increasing both condensate size and lifetime. In contrast, EWS::FLI1 hubs are predominantly maintained 460 
by protein -DNA and protein -protein interactions , with RNA contribut ing only to regulating EWS::FLI1 461 
binding kinetics. The differential roles of RNA in the regulation of Mediator condensates and EWS::FLI1 462 
hubs could be due to  differences in the chemistry underlying the molecular interactions that drive the 463 
formation of the respective assemblies. The IDR of the Mediator subunit MED1 contains alternating blocks 464 
of charged amino acids that drive condensate formation 3,111. RNA, a highly -charged polyanion, also 465 
interacts with the MED1 IDR primarily via electrostatic interactions, thus strongly coupling the formation 466 
and dissolution of M ED1 condensates to local RNA concentration 33. On the other hand , EWS::FLI1 hub 467 
formation is driven mainly by interactions between aromatic residues , particularly tyrosines in the EWS 468 
LCD1. These interactions are less sensitive to changes in bulk electrostatics 33,112, thus resulting in a lower 469 
impact of local RNA concentration on EWS::FLI1 hub  assembly. Our observations are also related to 470 
previous work showing that an increase in the RNA-protein ratio can reduce the ability of EWSR1 and FUS, 471 
members of the FET family of RNA -binding proteins 113, to form condensates . Notably, this effect was 472 
previously observed when the proteins were artificially driven to phase separation via overexpression. In 473 
contrast, our measurements of EWS::FLI1 under endogenous expression levels suggest that RNA regulates 474 
only the binding dynamics of EWS::FLI1, without altering its hub size. 475 
Finally, we showed that in live  Ewing sarcoma cells , LY2835219 partially dissolves endogenous 476 
EWS::FLI1 hubs and trabectedin redistributes EWS::FLI1 to the periphery of the nucleolus, away from its 477 
target genes. This is a useful finding given that recent work indicates that assemblies of disease-causing 478 
TFs are potential alternative therapeutic targets, owing to the notoriously “undruggable” nature of most 479 
TFs, which is due to their largely disordered nature and lack of well-defined binding pockets. For EWS::FLI1 480 
specifically, our earlier work shows that its hub formation mediated by multivalent LCD-LCD interactions 481 
plays an essential role in oncogenic transcription in Ewing sarcoma 1,9, suggesting disruption or 482 
mislocalization EWS::FLI1 hubs as a potential therapeutic strategy. Here, our finding that LY2835219 and 483 
trabectedin can alter the nuclear distribution of endogenous EWS::FLI1 further supports the previously 484 
reported therapeutic potential of the compounds. 485 
Overall, our study combined CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing and  quantitative live-cell and 486 
single-molecule imaging techniques to characterize the formation and regulation of endogenous TF hubs 487 
at an unprecedented resolution . We quantified the dimensions of EWS::FLI1 hubs along with the 488 
energetics and kinetics of hub nucleation . We uncovered the neomorphic nature of EWS::FLI1 hubs and 489

to diverse TFs, including regular and pathological ones. Future studies applying similar approaches to a 494 
broad range of TFs will inform the generalizability of our findings. 495
