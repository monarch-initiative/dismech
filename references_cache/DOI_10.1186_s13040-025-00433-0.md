Abstract
Background Acne is a chronic inflammatory condition affecting the hair follicles and 
sebaceous glands. Recent research has revealed significant advances in the study of 
the acne skin microbiome. Systematic analysis of research trends and hotspots in the 
acne skin microbiome is lacking. This study utilized bibliometric methods to conduct 
in-depth research on the recognition structure of the acne skin microbiome, identifying 
hot trends and emerging topics.
Methods We performed a topic search to retrieve articles about skin microbiome in 
acne from the Web of Science Core Collection. Bibliometric research was conducted 
using CiteSpace, VOSviewer, and R language.
Results This study analyzed 757 articles from 1362 institutions in 68 countries, the 
United States leading the research efforts. Notably, Brigitte Dréno from the University 
of Nantes emerged as the most prolific author in this field, with 19 papers and 334 
co-citations. The research output on the skin microbiome of acne continues to 
increase, with Experimental Dermatology being the journal with the highest number 
of published articles. The primary focus is investigating the skin microbiome’s 
mechanisms in acne development and exploring treatment strategies. These findings 
have important implications for developing microbiome-targeted therapies, which 
could provide new, personalized treatment options for patients with acne. Emerging 
research hotspots include skincare, gut microbiome, and treatment.
Conclusion The study’s findings indicate a thriving research interest in the skin 
microbiome and its relationship to acne, focusing on acne treatment through the 
regulation of the skin microbiome balance. Currently, the development of skincare 
products targeting the regulation of the skin microbiome represents a research 
hotspot, reflecting the transition from basic scientific research to clinical practice.

psychological and social burdens experienced by adolescents. While acne vulgaris has 
traditionally been attributed to androgen-driven sebaceous gland hyperactivity, follicular 
hyperkeratosis, and inflammation caused by Cutibacterium acnes (C. acnes ) [2], recent 
studies suggest a broader ecological role of the skin microbiome in acne pathogenesis. 
This shift in focus underscores the importance of ecological imbalances rather than the 
simple proliferation of a single pathogen [3].
The skin microbiome is a highly complex ecosystem consisting of bacteria, fungi, 
viruses, and other microorganisms that collectively maintain skin homeostasis [ 4]. The 
study of the skin microbiome has emerged as a pivotal area in dermatology, offering 
profound insights into the pathogenesis and treatment of skin diseases. The human skin 
harbors diverse microbial communities that are essential for pathogen defense, immune 
regulation, and skin homeostasis. Dysbiosis of these communities has been implicated in 
the pathogenesis of several dermatological conditions, including atopic dermatitis, pso -
riasis [ 5], and acne vulgaris, among others [ 3]. Emerging evidence suggests that varia -
tions among C. acnes  strains are central to the ecological imbalances underlying acne 
pathogenesis. These findings suggest that therapeutic strategies targeting pathogenic 
strains of C. acnes while preserving overall microbiome balance may provide significant 
clinical benefits [3].
Bibliometrics is a valuable methodology for quantitatively and qualitatively assessing 
research output, providing critical insights into global research trends, collaboration 
networks, and emerging research hotspots. In the context of acne microbiome studies, 
bibliometric analyses enable researchers to identify key contributors, such as prolific 
authors and influential institutions, while mapping the global distribution of research 
efforts. Despite the growing significance of skin microbiome in acne research, system -
atic bibliometric analyses remain scarce, limiting our understanding of global research 
priorities and collaborative opportunities. To address this gap, the present study applies 
bibliometric methods to comprehensively analyze acne microbiome research conducted 
over the past two decades. This study aims to (1) map global research trends, (2) iden -
tify key contributors, including authors, institutions, and collaborative networks, and (3) 
highlight emerging research hotspots. By providing a comprehensive roadmap for future 
research, this study seeks to advance international collaboration and support the devel -
opment of microbiome-based therapeutic strategies for acne management.
Materials and methods
Data sources and search methods
A comprehensive literature search was conducted on October 20, 2023, using the Web of 
Science Core Collection (WoSCC). The search criteria, detailed in Table S1(Additional 
file 1), were defined as follows: TS= (microbiome* or microbiota* or microflora* or 
microbiology* or microecology* or microorganism* or micro-ecosystem* or “microbial 
ecological” or “microbial ecology” or “microbial ecosystem” or “microbe ecology” or 
“microbial communit*”) AND TS=(acne*) AND TS= (skin or dermatology) AND DOP= 
(2000-01-01/2023-10-20). Articles and reviews written in English were included, while 
62 articles were excluded from the analysis. These exclusions comprised 18 proceed -
ings papers, 12 early access articles, 11 editorial materials, 11 meeting abstracts, 8 book 
chapters, and 2 letters. Ultimately, 757 relevant articles were identified, including 531 
research articles and 226 review articles.

We employed CiteSpace, VOSviewer, and R language to perform a comprehensive 
bibliometric analysis, leveraging the unique capabilities of each tool to address specific 
analytical objectives. CiteSpace was chosen for its ability to emerging trends, visualize 
research dynamics, and generate dual-map overlays, providing critical insights into the -
matic and temporal patterns within the field. Its citation-based network analysis allows 
for detailed tracking of how concepts evolve in scientific literature, offering valuable 
insights into the trajectory of specific topics. VOSviewer was selected for its strengths 
in constructing and visualizing bibliometric networks, including co-authorship, insti -
tutional collaborations, and keyword co-occurrence. Its ability to produce high-quality 
graphical representations made it particularly advantageous for network analysis. VOS -
viewer also provides advanced clustering techniques, aiding in the identification of pat -
terns and groupings within large datasets. R language was adopted for its versatility in 
statistical computing and data visualization, enabling detailed examinations of publica -
tion trends, country-level metrics, and the temporal evolution of keywords. R’s flexibility 
allows for the creation of customized scripts and models, facilitating detailed and repro -
ducible analysis. By integrating these complementary tools, we established a robust and 
methodologically transparent framework for bibliometric analysis, ensuring comprehen-
sive and reliable insights into the research landscape.

(2011–2017), and Phase III (2018–2023). Figure  2 visualizes the progression of research 
output across these phases.

microbiome research. Phase II saw steady growth, averaging 25.3 publications per year,

position in publication output. Notably, China ranks sixth in cited articles, which may 
reflect its relatively late entry into this field and suggest further attention is warranted.

Emerging Research Areas: insights from keywords analysis
Keywords co-occurrence analysis provides a precise method for capturing the evolution 
and development trends of research focuses within a specific field. Table  6 presents the 
top 10 high-frequency keywords in this domain, reflecting current research hotspots 
and trends while also suggesting potential future research directions. Among these, 
“Propionibacterium acnes ” appears over 300 times, underscoring its prominence as a 
central focus of acne skin microbiome research. Keywords that appeared five or more 
times were selected for further analysis, and clustering analysis was conducted using 
VOSviewer (Fig. 8A).
The trend analysis of keywords over time (Fig.  8B) reveals a shift in research focus. 
Between 2008 and 2016, studies primarily emphasized colonization by C. acnes  and S. 
epidermidis, with key terms such as Staphylococcus, microbiology, P. acnes, and bacteria 
being predominant. However, since 2017, researchers have increasingly investigated the 
pathogenesis and therapeutic approaches for acne vulgaris. The main keywords during 
this period include Propionibacterium, the human microbiome, bacteria, Malassezia, 
antibiotics, and antibiotic resistance. Moreover, in the past two years (2022–2023), terms 
such as skincare, gut microbiome, and treatment have frequently appeared, reflecting 
emerging research hotspots in acne prevention and treatment.

Discussion
The study results demonstrate a consistent rise in literature focusing on the application 
of the skin microbiome in acne research. This trend reflects rapid advancements in skin 
microbiome studies, which hold transformative potential for disease prediction, diagno -
sis, and classification. Projections based on curve fitting indicate an impending surge in 
global participation and researcher engagement, highlighting the growing importance 
of this field. The evolution of acne-related skin microbiome research can be delineated

Propionibacterium acnes/Cutibacterium acnes
In acne cases, the resident microbiome predominantly includes C. acnes  and S. epider-
midis, while the transient microbiome often features S. aureus [3, 17]. The association 
between C. acnes and acne has been thoroughly investigated. First identified in 1893, P. 
acnes was originally named Bacillus acnes by Gilchrist in 1903. It was later reclassified as 
Corynebacterium acnes  by Bergey et al. in 1923 based on morphological characteristics 
and subsequently assigned to the genus Propionibacterium by Douglas et al. in 1946, a 
classification confirmed through biochemical studies. Recently, dermatology researchers 
have adopted the nomenclature C. acnes, which comprises six phylotypes: IA1, IA2, IB, 
IC, II, and III [27, 28].
Recent findings suggest that bacterial overgrowth is not the primary driver of acne 
vulgaris; an ecological imbalance between the skin microbiome and specific C. acnes  
phylotypes plays a pivotal role. Studies have shown significant differences in C. acnes  
phylotype distribution between healthy individuals and acne patients. For instance, 
healthy individuals predominantly harbor phylotypes IA1 (39.1%) and II (43.4%), while

acne patients exhibit a markedly higher prevalence of phylotype IA1 (84.4%), especially

erties, including pronounced lipase activity, which facilitates the breakdown of triglyc -

its ability to produce biofilms enhances adhesion to keratinocytes and accelerates the 
formation of microcomedones [ 31]. Furthermore, phylotype I strains produce higher 
levels of porphyrins, which generate reactive oxygen species that induce keratinocyte 
inflammation [32]. These findings suggest that restoring microbial balance and enhanc -
ing C. acnes phylotype diversity represent promising avenues for future acne treatment 
strategies.
From an immunological perspective, C. acnes activates TLR2 receptors, driving sebo -
cytes to express pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-2, IL-8, and TNF-α [ 33, 34]. 
Moreover, C. acnes induces the NLRP3 inflammasome and caspase-1 activation, releas -
ing the key inflammatory mediator IL-1 [ 35]. In addition, C. acnes stimulates sebocytes 
and keratinocytes to secrete β-defensin-2, an antimicrobial peptide that facilitates neu -
trophil chemotaxis, thereby amplifying the inflammatory response within acne lesions 
[36]. Apart from neutrophil infiltration, acne lesions exhibit significant increases in 
Th1 and Th17 cells [ 37]. Importantly, C. acnes has been shown to differentially regu -
late CD4 + T cell responses, driving the generation of Th17 cells [ 38]. These findings 
underscore the complex immunological pathways involved in acne pathogenesis and 
highlight the central role of C. acnes  in mediating inflammatory responses and disease 
progression.
Future research on acne, focusing on C. acnes  subtype distribution, may enable per -
sonalized treatment strategies by identifying distinct microbial profiles and guiding tar -
geted interventions. Additionally, elucidating the role of C. acnes in immune modulation 
could lead to the development of novel biologic therapies, significantly advancing acne 
management.
Staphylococcus epidermidis
S. epidermidis  plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of acne through its interac -
tion with C. acnes , acting as both a regulator and a target. On one hand, S. epidermi-
dis exhibits anti-inflammatory properties that suppress the pro-inflammatory activity 
of C. acnes . Specifically, C. acnes  activates Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2), triggering the 
release of pro-inflammatory cytokines in monocytes and keratinocytes. However, S. epi-
dermidis secretes lipoteichoic acid (LTA), which induces miR-143 expression in kera -
tinocytes. This microRNA destabilizes TLR2 mRNA, reduces TLR2 protein levels, and 
ultimately suppresses the inflammatory response induced by C. acnes [39]. Additionally, 
S. epidermidis inhibits the proliferation of C. acnes  and reduces cytokine release from 
keratinocytes. On the other hand, C. acnes counteracts S. epidermidis by hydrolyzing tri-
glycerides to release propionic acid, creating an inhibitory effect. These interactions sug-
gest that, under normal physiological conditions, S. epidermidis  and C. acnes  regulate 
each other to maintain a dynamic equilibrium. Disruption of this balance may contrib -
ute to acne development, highlighting the importance of targeting microbiome interac -
tions for potential therapeutic strategies [40].
Based on these findings, future research should prioritize therapeutic strategies tar -
geting the interaction between S. epidermidis  and C. acnes , especially via microbiome

modulation. Potential clinical applications include microbiome-based therapies, such as 
probiotics or topical agents, designed to restore microbial balance and mitigate inflam -
mation in acne.
Gut microbiome
The skin and intestines, both rich in blood vessels and neural innervation, play pivotal 
roles in neuroendocrine and immune regulation. Increasing evidence supports a strong 
connection between these organs, particularly through the gut-brain-skin axis, which 
links gut health to skin homeostasis [ 41]. First proposed in 1930 by Stokes and Pills -
bury, the “gut-brain-skin theory” posits that emotional states and gut microbiome are 
closely linked to skin conditions. The gut microbiome, recognized as the largest micro -
bial community in the human body and often referred to as an overlooked vital “organ, ” 
is strongly associated with acne pathogenesis through this axis [ 42]. Recent studies have 
shown that the mTOR signaling pathway, a critical regulator of cellular metabolism, is 
significantly upregulated in acne patients. Specifically, mTORC1 expression is elevated 
in the skin of acne patients, and serum IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor-1) levels are 
positively correlated with high glycemic load diets [ 43, 44]. These findings highlight the 
influence of dietary regulation on acne pathogenesis, offering a novel perspective for 
therapeutic strategies [ 45]. Animal models further demonstrate that changes in mTOR 
activity not only affect intestinal health but also alter gut microbiome composition, 
leading to intestinal inflammation [ 46]. Notably, the mTOR pathway affects gut micro -
biome balance by regulating intestinal barrier function [ 47]. These findings underscore 
the complex relationship between the gut microbiome and mTOR signaling, suggesting 
that disruptions in this interaction may contribute to the exacerbation of acne. Future 
research targeting both the gut microbiome and the mTOR pathway, through dietary, 
probiotic, and therapeutic interventions, holds promise for developing innovative strate-
gies for acne management and opening new avenues for clinical applications.
Skincare
Antibiotics are commonly used in clinical practice to treat moderate to severe acne. 
However, the increasing prevalence of antibiotic resistance and the adverse effects of 
prolonged antibiotic use on the skin and gut microbiome highlight the urgent need for 
alternative therapeutic strategies [40, 48, 49]. In this context, microbiome-targeted ther-
apies, including prebiotics, probiotics, and postbiotics, are gaining recognition as inno -
vative and effective approaches. These therapies leverage diverse mechanisms of action, 
such as selectively inhibiting pathogenic microorganisms (e.g., C. acnes  and S. aureus) 
[50], modulating inflammatory pathways by downregulating cytokines like IL-8 and 
IL-33 [ 51, 52], providing antioxidative effects to mitigate oxidative stress, and restor -
ing microbial balance to enhance skin barrier integrity. Recent studies underscore their 
clinical promise, demonstrating significant improvements in acne symptoms through 
enhanced microbial diversity, reduced inflammatory markers, and better maintenance of 
skin homeostasis.
Prebiotics are substances that selectively promote the growth of beneficial microor -
ganisms. Common sources include fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS), galacto-oligosaccha -
rides (GOS), and plant-derived extracts [ 53]. For example, clinical studies have shown 
that FOS and GOS supplement significantly improves metabolic parameters, including

blood glucose, insulin, and lipid levels, which are particularly relevant for hormone-
related acne [ 54]. Blackcurrant extract, rich in polyphenols and obtained via optimized 
enzyme-assisted extraction, has demonstrated the ability to selectively modulate the 
skin microbiome by promoting the growth of beneficial bacteria, such as S. epidermidis, 
while inhibiting C. acnes . This dual action helps restore microbiome balance and alle -
viate acne symptoms [ 55]. Although research on prebiotics for acne is still in its early 
stages, their potential to modulate microbial communities and improve skin health war -
rants further exploration.
Probiotics, defined as live microorganisms that provide health benefits to the host, 
play a pivotal role in acne management by regulating the microbiome and immune 
responses. Topical applications of probiotics, such as Lactobacillus paracasei , have 
demonstrated efficacy comparable to 2.5% benzoyl peroxide in clinical trials, with fewer 
side effects, such as dryness and irritation [ 56]. Additionally, Lactobacillus plantarum 
MH-301 has shown synergistic effects when combined with isotretinoin, significantly 
reducing inflammation, improving skin barrier integrity, and restoring microbial diver -
sity [ 50]. Oral supplementation with Lactobacillus plantarum CCFM8661  in animal 
models has exhibited anti-inflammatory effects, regulation of lipid metabolism, and 
improvements in gut microbiome composition, highlighting its potential as a systemic 
treatment for acne [57].
Postbiotics, comprising metabolites or inactive derivatives of probiotics, offer unique 
advantages due to their stability and effectiveness without requiring live microorgan -
isms. For instance, the TAC/Collagen formulation has demonstrated potent antimicro -
bial and anti-inflammatory properties by reducing pathogenic bacteria and modulating 
inflammatory markers such as TSLP and IL-33 [ 51]. Clinical trials further highlight its 
ability to improve skin hydration and accelerate wound healing. Similarly, LactoSporin®, 
derived from Bacillus coagulans, has demonstrated comparable efficacy to benzoyl per -
oxide in reducing acne lesions while outperforming it in reducing sebum production and 
skin oiliness. Its mechanisms include pH modulation, biofilm inhibition, and disruption 
of ionic balance [ 58]. Additionally, postbiotics, such as sugarcane straw extracts fer -
mented by Saccharomyces cerevisiae, exhibit antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, and anti -
microbial properties, further supporting their potential in acne treatment [59].
Although microbiome-targeted therapies show significant potential for the treatment 
of acne, further research is necessary to confirm their long-term safety and efficacy. 
Additionally, personalized treatment strategies tailored to diverse populations remain a 
critical area of development. Future studies could focus on optimizing the formulations 
of microbiome-targeted therapies to enhance their stability and bioavailability, particu -
larly in combination therapies (e.g., the synergistic effects of prebiotics and probiotics). 
Addressing these challenges could establish microbiome interventions as a core strategy 
in acne treatment, providing patients with safer, more effective, and personalized thera -
peutic options.
Limitations
Bibliometrics is a powerful tool widely used to analyze scientific research structure, 
dynamics, and trends. In the acne microbiome, bibliometric analysis and visualization 
provide insights into the research status and development trajectory. However, this study 
has inherent limitations that require careful consideration. First, the visualization tools

Zhang et al. BioData Mining           (2025) 18:19 
used, such as VOSviewer and CiteSpace, have limitations in data processing. These tools 
rely on citation analysis to construct a knowledge graph but cannot analyze the full con -
tent of publications. Consequently, they may overlook important research details and 
viewpoints, leading to biases in the analysis results. To overcome this limitation, future 
research can combine advanced technologies like text mining and machine learning to 
analyze the entire content more accurately, reflecting the research status and develop -
ment trends. Additionally, focusing mainly on English publications may underesti -
mate the influence of non-English papers. For example, two relevant studies published 
in French and Turkish were excluded from this analysis, but their findings align with 
key concepts in the field. The French study [ 60] highlighted the involvement of C. acnes 
in acne, with focusing on strain differences, a topic that has been previously discussed 
in English-language research [ 9]. Similarly, the Turkish study [ 61]on the antimicrobial 
susceptibility of C. acnes identified phylotypes in patients with acne vulgaris, a concept 
also discussed in English literature, such as Dagnelie et al. ’s study on C. acnes  biotypes 
and phylotypes [ 29]. While these studies provide valuable insights, they were excluded 
due to language constraints, potentially narrowing the scope of our analysis. To gain a 
comprehensive understanding of global research trends, future studies should include 
publications in multiple languages to better reflect international research. Finally, due to 
the short publication time of some high-quality literature, their citation frequency may 
not have peaked, potentially leading to differences between this study’s results and the 
actual situation. Thus, future research needs to continuously update bibliometric data to 
accurately reflect scientific trends and hotspots. Although bibliometrics has been crucial 
in acne microbiology, its inherent limitations cannot be ignored. Future research should 
consider these limitations and adopt various strategies to overcome them, aiming for a 
more comprehensive understanding of the research status and development trends in 
this field.
Conclusions
The skin microbiome and its role in acne pathogenesis have become central to dermato -
logical research, with increasing efforts directed toward regulating microbiome balance 
for effective therapeutic outcomes. While the United States remains at the forefront with 
substantial research contributions, fostering international collaboration through stan -
dardized data-sharing platforms and global research consortia is essential to drive sus -
tained progress in this field.
Despite significant advancements, several critical knowledge gaps remain. A deeper 
understanding of the interactions between C. acnes  and the host immune system is 
imperative for unraveling the mechanisms of acne pathogenesis. Longitudinal studies 
are needed to examine the dynamic evolution of the skin microbiome over time and its 
responses to therapeutic interventions. Moreover, robust clinical trials are required to 
evaluate the long-term efficacy and safety of microbiome-targeted therapies, includ -
ing prebiotics, probiotics, and postbiotics. The recent emergence of skincare products 
focusing on microbiome regulation demonstrates the successful translation of founda -
tional research into clinical and commercial applications, underscoring a transforma -
tive trend within the skincare industry. To address these gaps, future research should 
integrate insights from microbiome science, genetics, and environmental factors while 
fostering interdisciplinary collaboration among academic researchers, clinicians, and

industry stakeholders. Such efforts will expedite the development of evidence-based, 
personalized therapies.
In conclusion, the increasing focus on the skin microbiome highlights its pivotal role 
in advancing our understanding of acne pathogenesis and developing innovative treat -
ment strategies. Addressing the existing research gaps will not only improve acne man -
agement but also establish a model for enhancing broader dermatological care and 
personalized skin health strategies.
