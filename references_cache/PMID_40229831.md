Enterovirus 71 (EV71) is a major etiologic pathogen for hand-foot-and-mouth 
disease (HFMD) in young children. Severe cases of EV71 infection could lead to 
neurological complications and even death, while the mechanism inducing 
neurological complications remains poorly understood. In this study, we firstly 
proved that microvesicles (MVs) could carry EV71 virions and mediate a higher 
efficiency in infection. Utilizing an in vitro blood-brain barrier (BBB) model, 
we observed that MVs containing virions (MVsEV71) could cross the BBB with 
greater efficiency compared to EV71 alone. Through in vivo imaging, we confirmed 
the ability of MVs to cross the BBB. qPCR assays showed a higher copy number of 
EV71 in both blood and brain samples in the mice treated with MVsEV71 compared 
to those treated with free EV71. Also, our investigation unveiled that MVsEV71 
infection of animals induced cerebral hemorrhage and more severe inflammatory 
infiltration in the brain compared to animals infected by EV71 in vivo. 
Furthermore, we found a reduction in the expression of junction proteins such as 
zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and occludin. Moreover, the uptake of MVs by brain 
cells was examined using chemical inhibitor to block the endocytic pathway. Our 
experiments elucidated that the internalization of MVs occurred via a 
non-clathrin-dependent mechanism and a portion of the internalized MVs proceeded 
to enter lysosomes. In addition, we identified damaged mitochondria as the 
"cargo" of MVs, which facilitated MVsEV71 crossing the BBB and inducing cellular 
apoptosis. Meanwhile, MVsEV71 crossing the BBB further induced mitochondrial 
damaged and activated NOX4-derived ROS pathway in U251 cells. Taken together, 
these findings suggested that MVs transported EV71 virions across the BBB, while 
damaged mitochondria facilitated this process and aggravated the brain injury. 
Overall, these observations provide new insights into EV71-induced neurogenic 
complications and present a novel therapeutic target for the treatment of viral 
encephalitis.

EV71 is a non-enveloped, single-stranded RNA virus belonging to the family Picornaviridae, and it is the primary virus responsible for causing hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD) [1]. Severe cases can cause neurological complications, such as encephalitis and neurogenic pulmonary edema, and even death [2, 3]. Data from clinical and animal studies supported the direct infection of neurons by EV71, as evidenced by the detection of EV71 RNA and protein expression within the neurons of patients that died of EV71 infection [4]. The blood-brain barrier (BBB) serves a pivotal barrier in preventing infectious pathogens and immune cells from entering the CNS, which is composed of the endothelial cells, tight junction proteins, basement membranes, and pericytes [5, 6]. However, the mechanism by which EV71 crosses the blood-brain barrier to cause severe central nervous system complications remains elusive.

Microvesicles (MVs) exhibit a heterogeneous size ranging from 100 nm to 1 μm in diameter, derived from the shedding of the plasma membrane [7, 8]. MVs are released from various cell types and can carry multiple “cargoes”, such as phospholipids, proteins and other molecules [9, 10]. It is widely accepted that MVs play a crucial role in cell-to-cell communication, participating in various physiological and pathological processes, including inflammation, tumor progression, immune response, and viral spread [11–13]. For example, research reported that MVs containing HSV-1 virions were able to infect CHO cells and avoided immune surveillance [14]. Martins et al. reported that EVs secreted by infected cells in a DENV model could modulate mdDCs and carry viral component to promote infection [15]. Additionally, viruses such as SARS-CoV, HBV, and Zika could also utilize EVs for transmission [16, 17]. In recent years, studies found that MVs were capable of crossing the BBB, spreading to body fluids and accessing distant tissues. Microvascular endothelial cells-derived MVs were implicated in ischemic stroke through the modulation of astrocyte function, blood-brain barrier integrity, and cerebral blood flow [18].

Interestingly, studies have revealed that mitochondria can be packaged into extracellular vesicles and transported to the brain cells. For instance, MVs, the larger EV fraction, but not exosomes, containing mitochondria, led to enhanced mitochondrial function in hypoxic endothelial cultures [19]. However, studies also suggested that mitochondrial stress triggered the release of molecules such as mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which exhibited potent proinflammatory properties [20, 21]. Evidence demonstrated that mitochondrial dysfunction was related to aging and neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [22, 23]. Meanwhile, studies showed that extracellular mitochondria, encapsulated within MVs released from injured brains, could activate microglia and contribute to neuroinflammation in traumatic brain injury [24]. Study also indicated that these extracellular mitochondria mediated cerebral edema in a ROS-dependent manner [24], and ROS significantly contributed to the neurodegeneration observed following traumatic brain injury (TBI) [25]. These evidences proved that mitochondria were crucial for the pathogenesis of neurological disorders. It is plausible to postulate that mitochondria may also play a role in EV71-induced neurological complications.

In this study, we investigated if MVs play a role in mediating EV71 crossing the blood-brain barrier and explored the mechanism of EV71 crossing the BBB. We found that MVs could carry EV71 virions to cross the BBB in vitro and vivo, disrupting tight junctions and inducing apoptosis in hCMEC/D3 cells. Furthermore, our investigation revealed that MVs crossed BBB via non-clathrin-dependent manner. Moreover, the study demonstrated that damaged mitochondria encapsulated within MVsEV71, and the damaged mitochondria enhanced the ability of MVs to cross the BBB. Once across, these MVs further impaired mitochondrial function and elevated ROS levels in astrocyte, ultimately contributing to brain injury. Overall, our findings showed that MVs played a critical role in EV71-induced neurological manifestations.

HeLa cells (human epithelial carcinoma cell lines) and U251 cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection and maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Human cerebral microvascular endothelial cells (hCMEC/D3) were purchased from iCell Bioscience Inc. and cultured in Endothelial Cell Medium supplemented with 5% FBS, 1% ECGS and 1% P/S. EV71 BrCr strain was generously provided by Professor Bin Wu, Jiangsu Provincial Centers of Disease Control.

HeLa cells were cultured in 15 cm dishes overnight and then cultured with serum-free DMEM. Subsequently, the cells were infected or uninfected with EV71. After 36 h, the supernatant was harvested, centrifugated at 400 g for 10 min, 2500 g for 20 min, and finally, at 10,000 g for 35 min. The pellets were washed twice with PBS and prepared for the subsequent experiments.

The cells and microvesicular pellets were lysed with RIPA buffer and the lysates were cleared through centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 min. The protein concentrations of cells or MVs lysates were measured by BCA protein assay kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). After separation on a 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel, the proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). Subsequently, the membranes were blocked with 4% nonfat milk for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The antibodies against Alix, GML30, CD98, CD63, CD9, GAPDH, ZO-1, occluding, VDAC1 and NOX4 were purchased from Proteintech and diluted at 1:1000. The antibodies against COX IV were purchased from Proteintech and diluted at 1:4000. The antibodies against VP1 were purchased from abcam and diluted at 1:1000. The antibodies against 2 C were purchased from GeneTex and diluted at 1:1000. Secondary antibodies were either IRDye Fluor 680-labeled IgG or IRDye Fluor 800-labeled IgG (1:10000 dilution, Li-Cor Bioscience). The images were scanned and acquired using an Odyssey Imaging System (LI-COR, Bio-sciences).

For assessing the cellular uptake capability of MVs, the cells were seeded in 12-well culture plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Subsequently, the cells were treated with MVs labeled with DiI. After 12 h, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100, and after a 10-minute incubation, they were washed three times before being blocked with 4% nonfat dry milk for 1 h. The cells were then stained with anti-VP1 antibody (1:1000 dilution, Abcam, UK), followed by secondary staining with goat anti-rabbit Alexa 488 (1:1000 dilution). The nuclei were stained with DAPI. The images were acquired using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope. The anti-ZO-1 antibody was purchased from ABclonal (1:100 dilution). All the data show mean ± SE from three experimental replicates.

Total RNA was isolated from HeLa or hCMECD/3 cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, NY, USA). Then the complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized with a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) under the following conditions: 37 °C for 15 min, followed by 85 °C for 5 s. The results were obtained on an ABI Viia 7 Real-Time PCR system (ABI, NY, USA) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The relative mRNA expression levels were determined using the 2^-ΔΔCt method. The relative primer sequences were shown in Table 1.

Furthermore, we investigated whether MVs isolated from HeLa cells infected with EV71 contained viral proteins and/or virions, and showed that MVs isolated from EV71-infected HeLa cells contained EV71 RNA as well as EV71 proteins, including VP1 and 2 C (Fig. 1F and G). Given that EV71 is a non-enveloped virus, an additional confirmation of the presence of virus particles within the MVs was conducted using RNase A, an enzyme that specifically degrades RNA but not viral particles. qPCR assay showed that the viral RNA was reduced by 1.8log in the presence of Triton-X 100, in comparison to the untreated MVsEV71 (Fig. 1H) with a significant fraction of viral RNA (3.5 × 107 copies/µL) remained detectable. This finding suggests that the MVs may contain not only viral RNA but also viral particles which protects viral genomic RNA from the treatment of Triton-X 100 and RNase A. When MVs were immunopurified with an antibody specific for protein CD98, the co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay detected both the viral proteins VP1 and 2 C in MVs, which further confirmed that MVs contained EV71 virions (Fig. 1I). We also used the MVs immunopurified with an antibody specific for protein CD98 to infect the HeLa cells and the results showed a significant cytopathic effect (CPE) in the MVsEV71 infection group (Fig. 1J).

Based on the findings above, we firstly investigated if MVs could enter the cells. Immunofluorescence assay indicated that both MVsMock and MVsEV71 could enter the HeLa cells and hCMEC/D3 cells (Supplementary Fig. 1A and B), as detected by the expression of VP1 in the MVsEV71-treated HeLa cells and hCMEC/D3 cells (Fig. 2A and B). Moreover, we compared the relative infectivity of MVsEV71 and free EV71 virus in HeLa cells and showed that the viral RNA became detectable 2 h post MVsEV71 infection, whereas it was detectable 4 h post free EV71 infection and the virus replicated to a significantly higher level in MVsEV71 infection than in free EV71 infection (Fig. 2C). Western blot analysis also confirmed the earlier detection of the viral protein VP1 in the MVsEV71 treatment group (Fig. 2D). We compared the infection efficiency of MVsEV71 and free EV71 in hCMEC/D3 cells, which were utilized as an in vitro model to investigate the physiological and pathological mechanisms of the blood-brain barrier. The results showed that MVs-mediated EV71 entry had higher efficiency compared to free EV71 in hCMEC/D3 cells (Fig. 2E and F). Additionally, the L929 cells, lacking expression of SCARB2, the primary receptor for EV71, were susceptible to MVsEV71 infection as both viral RNA and protein being detectable at 48hpi, in contrast to free EV71 treatment, with virtually no viral RNA and protein being detected (Fig. 2G and H), suggesting that MVs delivered the virus into the cells independent of the receptor and more efficiently than free virions. Taken together, these observations demonstrated that MVs from EV71-infected cells could effectively infect L929 cells in a receptor-independent manner, and exhibited higher infection efficiency than free EV71.

Severe EV71 infection induced neurological syndromes, such as encephalitis and brainstem encephalitis [1, 26]. We therefore postulated that MVs might mediate EV71 entry across BBB and play a role in EV71-induced neurological manifestation. To explore the ability of MVs to cross BBB, we established a BBB model using hCMEC/D3 cells, as shown in Fig. 3A. Transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) assay and fluorescein sodium leakage analysis showed high resistance and low permeability, suggesting that the cells formed a tight monolayer and the BBB model was successful (Fig. 3B-D). Firstly, we observed that MVsEV71, but not MVsMock, increased the permeability of the blood-brain barrier, as demonstrated by the fluorescein sodium leakage assay (Fig. 3E and F), suggesting that MVsEV71 disrupted the integrity of BBB, leading to a permeability increase from 41.25 to 51.62%. Subsequently, we observed that MVsEV71 crossing the BBB monolayer entered the HeLa cells at the lower chamber, as shown by immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 3G). To further confirmed the viral replication in brain cells, we compared the efficiency of MVsEV71 and free EV71 virions in crossing the BBB monolayer and infecting U251 cells in the lower chamber by measuring the VP1 mRNA level using qPCR assay and showed that the MVsEV71 treatment resulted in a significantly higher viral replication compared to the free EV71 virion treatment in vitro (Fig. 3H), with increased BBB permeability compared to the EV71 group (Fig. 3I). This was also confirmed by the VP1 protein level in the U251 cells cultured in the lower chamber (Fig. 3J). Taken together, these results demonstrated that MVsEV71 could cross the BBB and had higher infection efficiency than free EV71 in vitro. This enhanced efficiency may be due to the natural membrane structure of MVs, which is identical to the cellular membranes and has low immunogenicity. Additionally, the receptor-independent internalization of MVs likely contributes to their improved ability to penetrate the BBB.

To assess the ability of MVsEV71 to cross the BBB in vivo, two-week-old AG6 mice were subjected to intraperitoneal injection of DiR-labeled MVs and the distribution of MVsEV71 was analyzed by in vivo imaging over a period of 0–48 h. The results showed that the fluorescence was detected in brain at 24 hpi and remained detectable at 48 hpi, suggesting effective crossing of BBB by MVsEV71 in vivo (Fig. 4A). We observed a significantly higher enrichment of EV71 RNA in both blood and the brain tissues of the mice injected with MVsEV71 than those injected with free EV71 (Fig. 4B and C). These results indicated that MVsEV71 could cross the BBB in vivo and exhibited higher infection efficiency than free EV71, demonstrating that MVs may play a critical role in EV71-induced neurological manifestation.

Understanding the endocytic process of MVs could offer vital insights into how endothelial cells regulate the transport of various cellular components across the blood-brain barrier. Endocytosis is recognized as the major route by which cells internalize exosomes and microvesicles [29, 30]. To investigate the internalization mechanism of MVs, we investigated the impacts of three endocytosis inhibitors of distinct working mechanisms. Firstly, chlorpromazine, an inhibitor of clathrin, was used to inhibit the clathrin-mediated endocytosis. The results showed that chlorpromazine pretreatment did not affect the level of VP1 in both RNA and protein level in hCMEC/D3 cells, suggesting that MVsEV71 internalization was not dependent on clathrin-mediated endocytosis (Fig. 6A - C). Secondly, genistein, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor involved in caveolae-mediated endocytosis, could significantly reduce both the RNA and protein of VP1 in hCMEC/D3 cells, indicating that MVsEV71 uptake was dependent on clathrin-independent endocytosis (Fig. 6D - F). Thirdly, MVsEV71 entry was also greatly diminished by the treatment of hCMEC/D3 with dynasore, an inhibitor of dynamin-mediated endocytosis pathway (Fig. 6G - I). TCID50 assay confirmed this phenomenon (Fig. 6J).

Furthermore, we identified the intracellular vesicles involved in the processing of MVsEV71 following internalization in hCMEC/D3 cells. The cellular distribution of MVsEV71 labeled with DiI was observed by confocal microscopy, with lysosomes stained with Lyso-Tracker green. The quantification of the fluorescence intensity indicated that 17% of MVsEV71 were found in lysosomes, suggesting that a portion of MVs undergo lysosomal degradation (Fig. 6K and L). Additionally, other MVsEV71 may localize to various organelles, such as the endoplasmic reticulum and early endosomes. Taken together, these data clearly indicate that MVs crossed the BBB via endocytosis pathway.

Earlier studies demonstrated that MVs packaged not only proteins, lipids and RNAs, but also subcellular organelles such as mitochondria [31–33]. Here, we observed that MVs isolated from the infected HeLa cells were positive of COX IV, a mitochondrial related protein, and showed that EV71 infection increased the expression of COX IV (Fig. 7A). In addition, equal masses of MVsMock and MVsEV71 were immunopurified using an CD98-specific antibody. The co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay revealed the presence of mitochondrial proteins COX IV and VDAC1 associated with the MVs (Fig. 7B), suggesting that EV71 infection upregulated mitochondria in MVs. To further confirmed this phenomenon, the MVs were stained with Mito Tracker and analyzed using flow cytometry. The data confirmed that MVs contained mitochondria (Fig. 7C). In addition, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) indicated that MVsEV71 contained damaged mitochondria-like structures with a double membrane, approximately 500 nm in diameter, and lacking mitochondrial cristae (Fig. 7D and Supplementary Fig. 2). Flow cytometric analysis showed that MVs isolated from cells infected with EV71 exhibited elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), indicating that MVs contained damaged mitochondria (Fig. 7E). Furthermore, confocal microscopy assay showed that MVsEV71 contained a significant number of mitochondria transferred into the HeLa cells (Fig. 7F). Together, these results demonstrated that EV71 infection enhanced the encapsulation of damaged mitochondria within MVs and the MVs were capable of transporting mitochondria into recipient cells.

We further investigated whether damaged mitochondria played a role in MVs crossing BBB. Firstly, the mitochondria isolated from the cells infected with or without EV71 were stained with Mito Tracker and analyzed by flow cytometry. The data showed a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) upon EV71 infection (Fig. 8A), suggesting that we isolated functional mitochondria. DCFH-DA staining indicated that the ROS levels in the purified mitochondria from EV71-infected HeLa cells were increased. These results suggested that the purified mitochondria from EV71-infected cells were damaged (Fig. 8B). Moreover, immunofluorescence assay revealed that the isolated mitochondria were internalized by hCMEC/D3 cells (Fig. 8C). Furthermore, we explored whether damaged mitochondria played a role in MVsEV71 crossing BBB. Transwell culture assay with hCMEC/D3 cells-based BBB model and U251 cells as recipient cells in the lower chamber showed that mitochondria isolated from EV71-infected cells increased the permeability of the blood-brain barrier under MVsEV71 infection, as demonstrated by the fluorescein sodium leakage assay (Fig. 8D). Additionally, we detected the expression of VP1 in U251 cells cultured in the lower chamber when the upper chamber hCMEC/D3 BBB was co-treated with MVsEV71 + Mito-EV71, while in contrast only trace VP1 was detected in wells infected with MVsEV71 alone (Fig. 8E), suggesting that the presence of Mito-EV71 increased virions crossing the blood-brain barrier (Fig. 8E). Moreover, we showed that Mito-EV71 promoted the replication of MVsEV71 and EV71 as shown by both Western blot analysis of VP1 (Fig. 8F and G) and qPCR assay of the VP1 mRNA level (Fig. 8H). We further investigated the effects of Mito-EV71 on MVsEV71-induced cell apoptosis and found that Mito-EV71 significantly elevated the expression of cleaved-Caspase 3, indicating increased apoptosis in hCMEC/D3 cells (Fig. 8I). This suggests that Mito-EV71 encapsulated in MVs may have been released into hCMEC/D3 cells, triggering apoptosis, which might be a mechanism by which Mito-EV71 facilitates MVs-EV71 in breaching the blood-brain barrier. Taken together, these results suggest that damaged mitochondria played a facilitating role in MVs crossing BBB and inducing brain damage.

As being a critical subcellular structure for normal cellular and organismal function [34, 35], mitochondria dysfunction can cause a number of human diseases, such as Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease [36]. Reports showed that mitochondrial dysfunction is central to the pathogenesis of neurological disorders [37]. Therefore, we aimed to explore whether MVsEV71 could cross BBB to induce mitochondria damage in U251 cells and observed that MVsEV71 infection resulted in an ROS elevation in U251 cells, increasing from 0.75 to 3.45%, as determined by flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 9A). Western blot assay also showed that MVsEV71 infection reduced the level of mitochondrial proteins, such as VDAC1 and COXIV (Fig. 9B). We then analyzed mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) loss in MVsMock- and MVsEV71-infected U251 cells by JC-1 staining and showed a significant decrease in membrane potential in MVsEV71-infected cells (Fig. 9C). These findings suggested that MVsEV71 crossed the BBB to damage mitochondria in glial cells. In addition, qPCR assay showed that MVsEV71 infection elevated the mRNA level of NOX4 in both U251 cells and HeLa cells compared to MVsMock group (Fig. 9D and E), which was supported by Western blot analysis of the protein expression (Fig. 9F and G). NOX4 is a member of the NOX family of ROS-generating NADPH oxidases [38]. Our further analysis proved that MVsEV71 infection induced significant elevation of NOX4 in mitochondria (Fig. 9H and I). Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that overexpression NOX4 in HeLa cells increased the ROS production (supplementary Fig. 3). Additionally, the GLX351322, a specific inhibitor of NOX4, was used to inhibit the expression of NOX4. The results showed that GLX351322 pretreatment inhibited the CPE caused by MVsEV71 and reduced VP1 expression in U251 cells (Fig. 9J - I). Collectively, these findings suggest that MVs could transport EV71 virions, thereby instigating brain injury through mitochondrial impairment and activation of the NOX4-mediated ROS pathway.

EV71 has been shown to be neuroinvasive in patients, as it was detected in the brains of four children who died from culture-proven EV71 infection during a hand, foot, and mouth disease outbreak in 1997 [4]. However, the mechanism by which EV71 penetrates the blood-brain barrier to induce severe central nervous system complications remains unclear. Recent studies have indicated that EV71 infection triggers neurodegeneration by activating TLR7 signaling and promoting IL-6 production [39]. However, how EV71 overcomes the BBB and establishes infection of neuronal targets is not fully understood. In the previous studies, Gu et al. reported that EV71 utilized exosomes to cross the BBB for infecting the central nervous system [40]. Here we revealed a novel mechanism by which MVs carrying EV71 virions crossed the BBB to induce brain injury and damaged mitochondria contained in MVs promoted this process. MVs, unlike exosomes, originate from the shedding of the plasma membrane and have been shown to participate in the intercellular communication process, such as cell adhesion, tumor progression and viral infections [7, 11]. Firstly, we observed that EV71 infection could promote the release of MVs, and these MVs contained EV71 virions (Fig. 1) and demonstrated that the EV71 virion-containing MVs (MVsEV71) could be internalized by and initiate infection of both HeLa and hCMEC/D3 cells. Both in vitro and in vivo evidence demonstrated that MVsEV71 resulted in more efficient infection of the cells than free virions (Figs. 2 and 4), suggesting a potential mechanism for MVs to penetrate the blood-brain barrier and enter the central nervous system. It is plausible that the EV71 virions encapsulated in MVs may benefit from the protection of MVs and avoid host immunity in addition to a higher efficiency of transmission due to the lipophilic nature of the MVs. However, unlike our study, previous research has shown that PS-rich EVs in body fluids contribute to innate defense against infection through viral apoptotic mimicry, as observed in viruses such as Zika and Ebola virus [41]. This suggests that EVs may have a dual role in viral infections—not only facilitating viral spread but also exhibiting potential antiviral properties. This warrants further in-depth investigation.

The BBB is a highly complex network that restricts the passage of circulating molecules, cells, and pathogens into the CNS parenchyma [42]. It is considered to be one of the primary routes for viral entry into the CNS. The in vitro experiments showed that MVsEV71 compromised the integrity of the BBB, allowing for higher penetration efficiency than the free EV71 (Fig. 3). We also found that MVsEV71 showed a higher infection efficiency in brain than EV71 free virions in vivo (Fig. 4). In addition, inflammation was observed in mice injected with MVsEV71 (Fig. 4), consistent with previous reports that EV71 infection promoted IL-6 production [39]. In our results, EV71 did not induce severe hemorrhagic reactions, possibly due to an insufficient viral load in the brain or a relatively short infection duration. These results proved that EV71 may utilize MVs for crossing the BBB.

There are two proposed mechanisms for MVsEV71 to cross the BBB: disrupting the integrity of the BBB, and transcytosis [29, 43]. Our evidence showed that MVsEV71 caused the reduced expression of tight junction proteins, such as ZO-1 and occludin, and induced caspase-3-mediated apoptosis of hCMEC/D3 cells (Fig. 5), suggesting that MVsEV71 disrupted the integrity of BBB. On the other hand, our data revealed that MVsEV71 could be internalized through non-clathrin-dependent pathways (Fig. 6). The results also indicated that a portion of MVs underwent lysosomal degradation (Fig. 6), while other MVsEV71 may be localized in other organelles, such as the endoplasmic reticulum and early endosomes [44], which requires further investigation. It is currently unclear what causes the disruption of tight junctions of BBB but it is likely the cargo molecules encapsulated in MVs, which require further investigation.

In recent study, it was found that mitochondrial dysfunction was central to the pathogenesis of neurological disorders, which could trigger inflammatory response to activate microglia [19, 45]. Interestingly, researchers reported that mitochondria could be encapsulated in MVs, and they subsequently performed a variety of functions (22, 46–47). Our data showed that EV71 infection promoted the encapsulation of mitochondria in MVs and that these mitochondria were damaged since a higher level of ROS in MVsEV71 compared to MVsMock (Fig. 7). Besides, in vitro BBB model demonstrated that damaged mitochondria facilitated MVsEV71 crossing the BBB and inducing cellular apoptosis (Fig. 8). This might be due to MVs releasing damaged mitochondria into the cells, inducing apoptosis in HCMEC cells and thereby promoting MVs to cross the BBB. However, what components of the damaged mitochondria contribute to the disruption of the BBB still requires further investigation in the future. We also determined the effect of MVsEV71 on glial cells, and the results showed that MVsEV71 infection caused mitochondrial damage and triggered NOX4-mediated ROS pathway. Oxidative stress (OS) was a significant pathobiological feature of secondary brain damage [48]. Chen et al. also reported that inhibiting the ROS/NLRP3 inflammasome pathway alleviated the brain injury [49]. Together, the present study demonstrated that MVsEV71-induced mitochondrial damage and elevated ROS levels could result in brain injury, suggesting that mitochondria played an important role in EV71-induced neurological complications.

In conclusion, our data indicated that MVs could carrying EV71 virions to infect brain vascular endothelial cells and cross the BBB via disrupting tight junctions (TJs) and non-clathrin-dependent endocytosis pathways. Furthermore, we proved damaged mitochondria could be encapsulated in MVs and promoted the MVs to cross the BBB. Additionally, MVs carrying EV71 virions could damage the mitochondria in U251 cells and induce the NOX4-mediated ROS pathway, leading to further brain injury (Fig. 10). These results further enhance our understanding of the mechanisms underlying CNS invasion by EV71.
