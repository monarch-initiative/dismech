# Lorlatinib monotherapy or combination therapy in anaplastic lymphoma kinase-driven high-risk neuroblastoma.
**Authors:** Xie W, Zhang Y, Lu S, Sun F, Zhu J, Que Y, Huang J, Song M, Zhen Z, Wang J, Zhang Y
**Journal:** NPJ Precis Oncol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41698-026-01487-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41698-026-01487-x)

## Content

This study evaluated the clinical efficacy and safety of lorlatinib in 25 patients with anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) -driven neuroblastoma. Among 17 evaluable patients, the objective response rate (ORR) was 64.7% (11/17). All six newly diagnosed high-risk patients who received lorlatinib in combination with induction chemotherapy achieved an objective response. Marked response heterogeneity was observed according to molecular profile: patients with ALK hotspot mutations alone achieved an ORR of 100%, whereas those with concurrent MYCN amplification, rare ALK mutations, or ALK amplification demonstrated significantly reduced efficacy (ORR 25.0%, p = 0.006). Acquired resistance was associated with potential bypass pathway alterations, including BRAF fusions, MET amplification, and NF1 mutations; however, functional validation of these mechanisms is still required. Pulmonary toxicity was observed in patients receiving lorlatinib combined with anti-GD2 immunotherapy or chemotherapy. These preliminary findings suggest potential efficacy of frontline lorlatinib in ALK-driven neuroblastoma, highlight molecular determinants of sensitivity, and reveal challenges of resistance and treatment-related toxicity.

Anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) -driven neuroblastoma, characterized by oncogenic ALK mutations or amplifications, occurs in approximately 10–21.5% of newly diagnosed patients and in about 20% of those with relapsed disease1–4. ALK mutations encompass both common hotspot mutations and rare mutations5. The third-generation ALK inhibitor lorlatinib has demonstrated safety and efficacy as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy for relapsed/refractory disease; however, most clinical experience is limited to patients harboring common ALK hotspot mutations (e.g., F1174, R1275)6,7. The differential sensitivity of various ALK genetic variants to lorlatinib and strategies for managing lorlatinib-resistant disease remain poorly understood7.

In this study, we enrolled 25 patients with ALK-driven neuroblastoma who had not previously received an ALK inhibitor between August 2023 and April 2025 (Table 1). The cohort included 9 newly diagnosed high-risk patients and 16 with refractory/progressive disease, with a median age of 6.0 years (range: 1.1–46.6). A total of 25 ALK alterations were identified, comprising 22 mutations and 3 amplifications. Among the mutations, 17 were hotspot variants (R1275Q, F1174L, F1245V, F1174C), including one compound mutant (F1174L/F1174C/R1275Q) and one germline R1275Q mutation. The remaining five mutations were minor sites (L365V, E128D, P1139S, L1240V, and R1192P). The median mutant allele frequency was 16.0% (range, 0.08–71.8%). Lorlatinib was administered during different treatment phases. Among the nine newly diagnosed patients, six received lorlatinib during both induction and maintenance, while three received it only during maintenance. Most patients with refractory/progressive disease (13/16) received lorlatinib in combination with salvage chemotherapy, with three patients subsequently transitioned to lorlatinib maintenance after achieving a response. Eight patients received lorlatinib concurrently with anti-GD2 monoclonal antibody (dinutuximab beta or naxitamab) and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF). After a median follow-up of 16.7 (8.3–25.3) months and a median treatment duration of 15.8 (6.8–23.4) months, 15 patients remained on lorlatinib at the last follow-up in December 2025.

Among the 25 patients, 17 had evaluable lesions while 8 patients lacked evaluable disease as they had achieved complete remission (CR) following comprehensive prior therapy. Following treatment with lorlatinib, either as monotherapy or in combination, the best overall responses included six CR, five partial responses (PR), two cases of stable disease (SD), and four with progressive disease (PD), yielding an objective response rate (ORR) of 64.7% (11/17) (Table 2). Notably, all six newly diagnosed high-risk patients who received lorlatinib with induction chemotherapy achieved responses (three CR, three PR). While these results are promising, the small sample size and prior treatment heterogeneity limit definitive conclusions. The Children’s Oncology Group (COG) Phase III trial (NCT03126916) investigating lorlatinib plus standard therapy in newly diagnosed ALK-altered neuroblastoma is ongoing, with results pending. Among the 11 evaluable patients with refractory/progressive disease, the ORR was 45.5% (three CR, two PR). The response was notably influenced by the molecular profile. Patients with ALK hotspot mutations without MYCN amplification (n = 3) achieved an ORR of 100%, whereas those with ALK hotspot mutations plus MYCN amplification (n = 5), minor ALK mutations (n = 2), or ALK amplification (n = 1) had a significantly lower ORR of 25.0% (P = 0.006). A prior COG study of 1596 neuroblastoma tissue samples used ALK exons 21–28 sequencing, supplemented by biochemical assays and computational analysis, to identify oncogenic ALK mutants and predict their sensitivity to ALK inhibitors. This study confirms that the three hotspot residues (R1275, F1174, F1245) represent activating mutations, whereas rare variants (e.g., I1183, A1200) are non-activating and are likely associated with insensitivity to ALK inhibitors5. Consistent with this classification, all three patients in our cohort harboring rare mutations within the ALK tyrosine kinase domain (TKD)—P1139S, L1240V, and R1192P—failed to respond to lorlatinib. Notably, the P1139S mutation has not been previously reported and is predicted to be non-activating. While current COG and SIOPEN studies primarily focus on TKD mutations, data regarding mutations within the extracellular domain (ECD) of ALK remain limited. In our study, two patients harboring ECD mutations (L365V, E128D) experienced disease progression during lorlatinib treatment, suggesting that this mutation class may also derive limited therapeutic benefit from ALK inhibition.

Previous studies have shown that patients with ALK-mutated neuroblastoma and concurrent MYCN amplification exhibit low response rates to lorlatinib monotherapy6,7. In this study, the ORR was 16.7% among six patients with refractory/progressive neuroblastoma harboring MYCN gene amplification, consistent with prior reports. These findings suggest that ALK-driven neuroblastoma with MYCN amplification is relatively insensitive to lorlatinib, highlighting the need to explore novel therapeutic approaches.

Established mechanisms of lorlatinib resistance in neuroblastoma include off-target resistance mediated by activation of the ras/mitogen-activated protein kinase (RAS-MAPK) pathway, acquisition of secondary ALK mutations, and clonal expansion of alterations targeting cell differentiation7,8. However, the clinical application of combination targeted therapies for patients with lorlatinib-resistant, ALK-driven relapsed/refractory neuroblastoma has not been previously reported. In this study, three patients with refractory/progressive disease experienced relapse or progression despite salvage therapy with lorlatinib in combination with chemotherapy. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis at the time of progression revealed, in addition to the pre-existing ALK mutation, the emergence of new actionable genetic alterations in all three cases, including a BRAF-KIAA1549 fusion, an NF1 mutation (c.503 C > G, p.Ser168*), suggesting loss of NF1 function, and MET amplification (copy number: 4.5). Dynamic changes in plasma ctDNA during treatment are illustrated in Supplementary Figure 1. Although these alterations are biologically plausible as potential bypass mechanisms, these observations should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating due to the limited number of cases and absence of functional validation. These patients subsequently received lorlatinib in combination with tunlametinib (a MEK inhibitor), selumetinib, or bozitinib (a c-MET inhibitor), respectively, to target the newly identified pathways. Despite these interventions, all three patients experienced further disease progression, underscoring the complexity of lorlatinib resistance and the urgent need to develop more effective therapeutic strategies.

The safety profile of lorlatinib was manageable and is summarized in Table 3. The most common grade I–II adverse events were weight gain (62.4%), hypertriglyceridemia (58.3%), and hypercholesterolemia (58.3%). Grade III events were less frequent and included weight gain and hypertriglyceridemia (12.5% each). Notably, two cases of pulmonary toxicity were observed. Previous reports from the COG ANBL1531 study and a retrospective analysis have identified an increased risk of pulmonary toxicity when lorlatinib is combined with dinutuximab or naxitamab, particularly in conjunction with GM-CSF9. Temporarily withholding of lorlatinib during anti-GD2 immunotherapy has therefore been proposed as a strategy to mitigate this risk. Although data on pulmonary toxicity associated with lorlatinib combined with dinutuximab beta are limited, one patient in our cohort developed respiratory failure after five cycles of lorlatinib plus dinutuximab beta, which resolved following discontinuation of lorlatinib. Notably, pulmonary toxicity was not reported in a clinical trial evaluating lorlatinib combined with chemotherapy for relapsed/refractory neuroblastoma6. However, one patient in our study receiving lorlatinib with chemotherapy developed non-infectious pulmonary inflammation after 1 month of treatment, which resolved after lorlatinib cessation. In summary, incorporation of lorlatinib into induction chemotherapy for treatment-naive, high-risk ALK-driven neuroblastoma demonstrated promising preliminary efficacy, though larger controlled studies are needed. Concurrent MYCN amplification and rare or non-kinase domain ALK alterations were associated with reduced sensitivity to lorlatinib. Efforts to overcome acquired resistance through combination targeted therapy proved challenging in this small cohort, highlighting the biological complexity of resistance mechanisms and the need for novel therapeutic strategies. Finally, careful monitoring for pulmonary toxicity is essential when lorlatinib is used in a combination regimen, regardless of the accompanying therapy.

We conducted a long-term prospective clinical study (NCT05076071) evaluating the application of next-generation sequencing (NGS)-guided precision therapy in pediatric solid tumors in China. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (Approval No. B2021-213). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. Patients with pediatric solid tumors received matched targeted therapies when actionable alterations and corresponding agents were available. Treatment decisions were made by the physician based on molecular findings, clinical status, and overall therapeutic considerations, and were discussed within our institutional multidisciplinary team to ensure appropriate patient selection. We report on the cohort of patients with ALK-driven neuroblastoma treated with the third-generation ALK inhibitor lorlatinib, evaluating efficacy, survival, safety, and molecular sequencing data. Eligible patients included those with newly diagnosed high-risk or refractory/progressive neuroblastoma.

Lorlatinib was administered orally at weight-based doses (<18 years: 115 mg/m²; ≥18 years: 150 mg) once daily, either as monotherapy or in combination with conventional therapies according to clinical indication. For patients with newly diagnosed high-risk disease, lorlatinib was given as maintenance therapy for one year after completion of frontline treatment. For patients with relapsed or refractory disease, treatment was continued until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, or patient decision to discontinue therapy. All patients underwent NGS testing, and nine of them received longitudinal circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) monitoring. Tumor response was assessed radiologically according to the International Neuroblastoma Response Criteria (INRC). Patients who had achieved complete remission after prior comprehensive therapy before initiation of lorlatinib and had no measurable or evaluable lesions according to INRC at baseline were classified as “not evaluable” for objective response assessment. These patients were monitored for disease relapse during treatment. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to enrollment.

The NGS testing protocol began with the acquisition of fresh tumor biopsy specimens or formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections, together with paired venous blood samples. For patients undergoing dynamic ctDNA monitoring, peripheral blood samples are collected periodically. DNA extraction was performed using specimen-specific protocols, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Extracted DNA was quantified and quality-controlled using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer.

Libraries were constructed using a minimum input of 20 ng of DNA and included fragment purification, end repair, adapter ligation, size selection, polymerase chain reaction amplification, and post-amplification cleanup, followed by library quality control. Target enrichment was performed using a 535-gene panel (Genetron Health) under optimized probe hybridization conditions.

Libraries were subsequently circularized to generate single-stranded DNA, followed by DNA nanoball preparation via rolling circle amplification. Sequencing was performed on the MGI-T7 platform using cyclic array-based sequencing with iterative single-base synthesis and fluorescence detection, with targeted mean coverage depths of at least 300× for matched white blood cell control samples, 2000× for tumor tissue samples, and 5000× for plasma ctDNA samples. All NGS testing procedures were conducted by Nanjing Genetron Health Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Sequence reads were processed using standardized bioinformatics pipelines with alignment to the human reference genome (hg19/GRCh37). Single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertion/deletion mutations (indels) were identified using validated variant calling algorithms. Variants were retained at a variant allele frequency (VAF) threshold of >1% for tumor tissue and >0.1% for ctDNA, with at least three unique mutant reads; the validated limit of detection for ctDNA was approximately 0.1% VAF. Copy number variants were analyzed using CNVkit with ploidy-adjusted thresholds of ≥1.6 for gains and ≤0.6 for losses. Gene fusions were identified using DELLY with a minimum split-read threshold of three reads for tissue and one read for ctDNA. Matched white blood cell DNA was used as the normal control to distinguish somatic alterations from germline variants and to exclude potential clonal hematopoiesis. All clinically relevant variants were manually reviewed using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV).

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables, including treatment response, were compared among groups using Fisher’s exact test. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
