# De novo POLR2A p.(L1127F) variant presenting with neurodevelopmental regression and dystonia, with favorable response to low-dose dopamine therapy: a case report and literature review.
**Authors:** Cao Z, Shen Q, Tangzhu C, Guo X, Huang Q, Ge X, Zhao Q, Shi X
**Journal:** Front Neurosci (2026)
**DOI:** [10.3389/fnins.2026.1997676](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2026.1997676)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia and variable intellectual and behavioral abnormalities (NEDHIB) is a rare autosomal-dominant condition caused by pathogenic POLR2A variants. It typically presents in infancy with hypotonia, developmental delay, and multisystem manifestations. Fewer than 40 patients have been reported worldwide, suggesting frequent misdiagnosis and underdiagnosis. To our knowledge, neurodevelopmental regression has not been documented in confirmed NEDHIB cases, and no standardized therapeutic regimens are established for clinical management.

CASE DESCRIPTION: The patient presented with neonatal hypotonia and developmental delay. At 2 years and 4 months, she developed sleep-associated dystonia-like nodding and limb tremors that were initially suspected to represent epileptic seizures. By 3 years of age, she exhibited marked neurodevelopmental regression: loss of the ability to walk and sit independently, together with deterioration of language function. Electroencephalography (EEG) demonstrated background slowing accompanied by epileptiform discharges. Whole-exome sequencing identified a de novo heterozygous POLR2A variant c.3379C > T (p. L1127F), which confirmed the diagnosis of NEDHIB.

RESULTS: Long-term rehabilitation training yielded only modest clinical benefits. After initiation of the dopamine agent levodopa-benserazide hydrochloride, marked improvements in motor and cognitive performance were observed, accompanied by the complete resolution of nodding episodes. With dose titration during follow-up, her clinical status remained stable without symptom relapse.

CONCLUSION: This case expands the phenotypic spectrum associated with POLR2A-related NEDHIB and suggests that neurodevelopmental regression may occur within the phenotypic spectrum of this disorder. Our findings also highlight the potential benefit of low-dose dopamine therapy, offering novel insights into the clinical diagnosis, differential diagnosis, and therapeutic management of NEDHIB.

RNA polymerase II (Pol II) transcribes protein-coding genes in eukaryotes, containing 12 conserved subunits. The largest catalytic subunit RPB1 is encoded by POLR2A on chromosome 17 (17p13.1) with 30 exons. Its catalytic core and the conserved CTD domain are crucial for transcription ( Li et al., 2024 ; Hou et al., 2021 ).

Accumulating evidence indicates that POLR2A abnormalities are associated with various diseases, including cancers and osteoporosis ( Liu et al., 2015 ; Zhou et al., 2012 ; Yoo et al., 2017 ). However, the neurodevelopmental syndrome caused by pathogenic POLR2A variants, termed neurodevelopmental disorder with hypotonia and variable intellectual and behavioral abnormalities (NEDHIB), is extremely rare. NEDHIB is an autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder. Its main clinical features encompass severe hypotonia and developmental delay in infancy, neurological abnormalities, and multisystem involvement. Among these clinical manifestations, developmental delay and hypotonia are the predominant clinical features.

The novel variant reported in this article is a missense mutation (c.3379C > T, p. L1127F) located in a moderately conserved residue, which has not been previously reported in HGMDpro or ClinVar. The clinical phenotype associated with this newly identified variant is characterized by marked motor and language regression at 3 years of age and significant clinical improvement after low-dose dopamine therapy. This study expands NEDHIB’s phenotypic spectrum, evaluates dopamine therapy, and aids in diagnosing dystonia vs. epilepsy in POLR2A variant patients.

The patient was a 3-year-old female child admitted to the Pediatric Neurology Department of a tertiary children’s hospital between January 3 and 9, 2025, presenting with a 3-year history of motor and speech developmental delay and an 8-month history of abnormal nodding behavior. The child presented with abnormal nodding, initially suspected to be nodding epilepsy. This episode was later confirmed as a dystonia-related movement disorder.

The patient was born at term via vaginal delivery with a birth weight of 3.0 kg. Mild hypotonia was noted at birth, with no history of asphyxia or resuscitation. At 6 months of age, the patient was unable to roll over or sit independently and could maintain head control for less than 30 s, indicating instability. She also exhibited hypotonia in all four limbs and diminished responsiveness. By 7 months, she could hold her head steady and roll over. At 8 months, she attempted to sit independently but was unable to maintain the position. Stable independent sitting was achieved at 12 months, and independent walking was achieved at 2 years of age. At 2 years and 4 months, recurrent abnormal nodding emerged, accompanied by limb tremors during sleep. Electroencephalography (EEG) at an external institution revealed background slowing accompanied by persistent sharp-and-slow-wave discharges in the frontal, central, and parietal regions, consistent with epileptiform discharges, prompting a suspicion of “epilepsy.” She was treated with oral sodium valproate (Depakine) for six months, yet her symptoms showed no improvement. At 3 years, she could say simple words like “Daddy” and “Mommy,” but then experienced developmental regression, losing walking ability and independent sitting, with limited communication. The patient was alert and cooperative, with 5/5 muscle strength in all limbs, generalized hypotonia, no pathological reflexes, and craniofacial findings of macrocephaly, frontal bossing, hypertelorism, and left esotropia.

Developmental Assessment: At 6 months of age, assessment using the Gesell Developmental Schedules yielded a total developmental quotient (DQ) of 84.8, with subscale scores as follows: Gross Motor 70, Fine Motor 93.3, Adaptive Skills 77.8, Language 93.3, and Personal-Social 89.4. At 7 months of age, the Gesell assessment revealed a total DQ of 76. The Alberta Infant Motor Scale (AIMS) percentile score was 1, indicating significant gross motor developmental delay and an attention deficit. Following an extended period of rehabilitation training, the AIMS percentile score improved to 16. However, reassessment at 14 months of age showed that the score had declined to below the 1st percentile (<1), indicating severe gross motor developmental delay (corresponding to a developmental level of 10–11 months). Clinical manifestations included poor lower limb postural control, characterized by ankle valgus (more pronounced on the left) and bilateral hip abduction while maintaining the four-point kneeling position.

Auxiliary examinations: The electroencephalogram (EEG) at age 3 showed increased slow-wave activity with sharp-slow waves, sharp waves, and spike waves in bilateral central, parietal, occipital, and temporal regions (Figure 1). Brain MRI was normal. Metabolic and other screenings were unremarkable.

Whole-exome sequencing performed in March 2022 for developmental delay and hypotonia identified a de novo POLR2A c.3379C > T (p. Leu1127Phe) variant, yielding a molecular diagnosis of NEDHIB. Because neurodevelopmental regression and dystonia were absent at that time, the full clinical significance of this variant was not recognized initially. Upon admission for developmental regression and dystonic nodding episodes, re-evaluation of the genetic results led to reclassification of the paroxysmal events as dystonia rather than epilepsy; this reassessment also informed the subsequent therapeutic trial of low-dose levodopa-benserazide hydrochloride. Genetic Testing: Peripheral blood samples from the proband (II-1) and her parents (I-1 and I-2) were collected with informed consent and sent for whole-exome sequencing in March 2022. Genetic analysis identified a de novo heterozygous variant c.3379C > T (p. L1127F) in the POLR2A gene, absent in both parents and unreported in ClinVar or HGMDpro databases.

Further genetic analysis revealed that the p. L1127F variant in the POLR2A gene corresponds to a substitution of leucine with phenylalanine at position 1,127 (Figure 3). Jaview analysis gave this site a conservation score of ‘+’, indicating moderate conservation. While variants at such sites usually have lower pathogenic potential, they can still affect protein function if they significantly alter amino acid properties.

Structural schematic of the RPB1 protein structure encoded by the POLR2A gene.

Furthermore, functional impairment predictions for this site were as follows: a REVEL score of 0.92 (≥0.5 classified as ‘likely pathogenic’), a PolyPhen-2 score of 0.877 (≥0.85 classified as ‘likely pathogenic’), a SIFT score of −1.904 (≤0.05 classified as harmful), and a CADD score of 28.4. According to ACMG guidelines, this variant is classified as Likely Pathogenic (evidence combination: PS2 + PM2_Supporting + PP3; PS2 denotes a confirmed de novo variant; PM2_Supporting indicates the absence of the variant in general population databases; PP3 indicates that multiple in silico tools consistently predict the variant to be deleterious).

Clinical Management: Upon admission, initial diagnosis was “epileptic encephalopathy.” Treatment included methylprednisolone pulse therapy and oral valproate sodium, but nodding symptoms persisted. Considering dystonia, corticosteroids and valproate were stopped. Low-dose levodopa-benserazide (31.3 mg twice daily) was started to improve muscle tone. The patient was discharged on this regimen.

During the follow-up period, the medication dosage was adjusted according to fluctuations in the child’s condition. With pharmacological management, the child’s clinical symptoms improved significantly, and their motor and language skills showed improvement. A temporal association was observed between treatment onset and this marked functional improvement, and the dose adjustment successfully maintained the therapeutic effect and prevented symptom recurrence.

We performed a systematic literature review of published cases with pathogenic POLR2A germline variants associated with NEDHIB. We searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD-Pro) up to March 2025, using search terms: (“POLR2A”) AND (“neurodevelopmental disorder” OR “NEDHIB” OR “hypotonia” OR “dystonia” OR “developmental regression”). In addition, we manually screened the reference lists of all retrieved articles to identify any further studies that met eligibility criteria.

Inclusion criteria: (1) patients carried de novo or germline heterozygous pathogenic/likely-pathogenic POLR2A variants; (2) the articles contained sufficient individual-level clinical and genetic details; (3) the studies were original human case reports or case series. Exclusion criteria included: review articles without individual patient data, somatic tumour-only variants, and animal or in-vitro experimental studies.

Data extraction was independently performed by two authors, capturing information regarding variant type, cDNA and protein alterations, sex, age, neurological presentations, multi-system involvement, and severity scores. Duplicate patients identified across overlapping publications were excluded based on variant, sex, and clinical characteristics. Ultimately, 30 patients were included in the criteria, whose clinical-genetic profiles are systematically summarized in Table 2.

All reported POLR2A-related developmental disorders involve heterozygous variants, including missense (n = 22), in-frame deletions (n = 4), nonsense (n = 2), and frameshift variants (n = 2). Among these variants, missense variants in the catalytic core often cause more severe phenotypes; for instance, p. Ile457Thr is linked to severe epileptic encephalopathy, while p. Glu1289Lys is associated with ependymoma. All patients show moderate to severe global developmental delay, and most with pathogenic POLR2A variants have generalized hypotonia in infancy. Epileptic symptoms are highly heterogeneous, seen only in a small subset of NEDHIB patients, and are not a core phenotype ( Evans et al., 2022 ; Haijes et al., 2019 ; Hansen et al., 2021 ; Giacomini et al., 2022 ; Paparella et al., 2022 ). Furthermore, POLR2A variants may be linked to autism spectrum disorder (ASD), with some children exhibiting complex ASD features, suggesting these variants as genetic risk factors for ASD ( Evans et al., 2022 ). Overall, POLR2A gene variants are highly heterogeneous, with different variant sites potentially leading to distinct clinical phenotypes through varied transcriptional regulatory mechanisms. Even identical missense variants can show variable expression among patients. For example, the p. Asn1251Ser variant was linked to developmental delay, hypotonia, and multisystem disorders in three patients (IDs 4, 5, and 25), but only one (ID 4) had epilepsy and cardiac malformations ( Haijes et al., 2019 ; Hansen et al., 2021 ). Additionally, a 12-year-old female with p. Ile457Thr had early-onset encephalopathy, refractory epilepsy, and progressive cerebellar atrophy, while a 9-year-old male with the same variant mainly had severe hypotonia without ASD-related features ( Haijes et al., 2019 ; Giacomini et al., 2022 ). This phenotypic heterogeneity may result from factors like modifier genes and environmental influences.

The POLR2A gene encodes RPB1, the catalytic subunit of RNA polymerase II. Its CTD dynamically regulates transcription through phosphorylation, playing a critical role in the transcriptional process. Variants in POLR2A cause NEDHIB through mechanisms including transcriptional dysregulation and CTD dysfunction.

The novel variant p. L1127F is located within the C-terminal core catalytic domain of Pol II, in close proximity to two known pathogenic variants, p. L1124P (case 23) and p. L1125del (case 24)(Table 2). Both previously reported patients exhibited mild infant-onset hypotonia and developmental delay accompanied by strabismus and other multisystem manifestations. Neither individual demonstrated neurodevelopmental regression, dystonia, or epilepsy; furthermore, dopaminergic therapy was not documented for either. While our p. L1127F patient shared these early-life core features, she exhibited distinct clinical features: prominent neurodevelopmental regression at age 3, sleep-related dystonic nodding and limb tremors mimicking epileptic seizures, and substantial clinical improvement following low-dose levodopa-benserazide treatment. None of these distinctive features have been described in patients harboring these two adjacent variants. Such phenotypic divergence among tightly clustered alterations underscores considerable intra-region variability within the POLR2A catalytic core domain, even for residues located in close primary-sequence proximity.

Despite these unique clinical events, the patient remained within the mild phenotypic spectrum. According to the severity scoring system established by Haijes et al. (2019) , the child’s score remained below 12 points prior to the age of three, meeting the criteria for a ‘mild phenotype’. Previous studies suggest that relatively mild clinical phenotypes are typically associated with loss-of-function variants and haploinsufficiency ( Hansen et al., 2021 ; Giacomini et al., 2022 ). Furthermore, in contrast to the refractory epilepsy described in previously reported cases, the nodding episodes accompanied by limb tremors during sleep in this patient were initially presumed to be epileptic. However, antiepileptic drugs (such as sodium valproate) proved completely ineffective; follow-up electroencephalograms revealed no improvement in electrographic activity, and the symptoms persisted or recurred with sleep/wake state changes, without disturbance of consciousness during the episode. After discontinuing sodium valproate and initiating levodopa-benserazide hydrochloride therapy, the nodding episodes and sleep-related limb tremors resolved. Based on the therapeutic response and etiological analysis, we concluded that these nodding manifestations were not true epileptic seizures, but rather a dystonia-related movement disorder induced by the POLR2A p.(L1127F) variant. Furthermore, the follow-up EEG performed still showed persistent background slowing and epileptiform discharges despite resolution of the clinical episodes. This further supports the notion that these events represent dystonic rather than epilepsy. Currently, with levodopa-benserazide hydrochloride therapy, the patient exhibits improved muscle tone relative to her baseline, and her motor function has improved significantly, allowing her to walk, run, and jump independently. Given the clinical overlap between dystonic movements, patients presenting with dystonia-induced nodding episodes accompanied by abnormal EEG discharges are frequently misdiagnosed as having epilepsy in clinical practice. The diagnostic and therapeutic experience from this case provides a valuable reference for the clinical evaluation and differential diagnosis of similar dystonia-related episodic disorders.

A further distinguishing feature of this case is the distinct disease trajectory: unlike the typical progressive developmental delay seen in most POLR2A variant patients, this child showed marked developmental regression at age 3, including loss of sitting, motor decline, and language regression. Existing literature has not clearly linked POLR2A variants to developmental regression. This rare manifestation suggests that POLR2A variants may not only cause neurodevelopmental delay but also precipitate neurodevelopmental regression in certain affected individuals.

We hypothesize that the POLR2A gene mutation compromises the transcription function of RNA polymerase II, thereby inducing transcriptional regulation disorders in central dopaminergic neurons. This disruption affects dopamine synthesis, release, and signal transduction, ultimately manifesting as muscle tone disorders and motor dysfunction. Nevertheless, this mechanistic model remains highly speculative due to the lack of functional experimental evidence for this patient. Therefore, in response to the patient’s condition, the clinical team selected levodopa-benserazide hydrochloride tablets (a dopamine agent, containing levodopa/benserazide) to improve motor impairment. Levodopa-benserazide tablets contain two active ingredients that exert synergistic therapeutic effects: levodopa directly replenishes brain dopamine levels, rapidly alleviating motor impairment symptoms, while benserazide reduces the peripheral metabolism of levodopa, thereby enhancing central efficacy and reducing side effects ( Beaulieu and Gainetdinov, 2011 ). The mechanism may involve dopamine replacement therapy partially compensating for neurotransmitter imbalances induced by transcriptional abnormalities, thereby improving neural function ( Beaulieu and Gainetdinov, 2011 ). However, levodopa-benserazide is primarily a symptomatic therapy and cannot correct the underlying transcriptional imbalance caused by POLR2A gene mutations. Although low-dose dopamine therapy was temporally associated with marked improvement in this single patient, its therapeutic value requires validation in larger studies.

Research on POLR2A gene variant-associated disorders remains in the exploratory stage, and diagnosis depends on the integration of clinical manifestations and genetic testing results. The core features of neurodevelopmental disorders caused by POLR2A gene variants are infantile hypotonia and developmental delay, and our case suggests that neurodevelopmental regression may occur in POLR2A-related NEDHIB, although further cases are needed to confirm this observation. No specific treatment is currently available, and clinical management primarily relies on symptomatic management. Although dopamine replacement therapy showed marked efficacy in this patient, its therapeutic value requires further validation through additional research. Moreover, this approach does not address the root cause of the underlying transcriptional abnormalities. Consequently, future research should prioritize the development of gene therapies and small-molecule drugs that target transcriptional regulation.
