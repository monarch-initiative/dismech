# Spontaneous aging-associated inflammation and genome instability in the immune system of turquoise killifish.
**Authors:** Morabito G, Dönertas HM, Sperti L, Seidel J, Poursadegh Zonouzi A, Poeschla M, Valenzano DR
**Journal:** Nat Aging (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s43587-026-01086-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43587-026-01086-2)

## Content

Turquoise killifish (Nothobranchius furzeri) are naturally short-lived vertebrates that recapitulate key aspects of human aging. However, the molecular and cellular causes of systemic aging in killifish are poorly understood. Here we ask whether killifish undergo age-dependent changes in the main hematopoietic organ (kidney marrow), which may contribute to systemic aging. To characterize immune aging in killifish, we used single-cell RNA sequencing, cytometry and functional in vitro assays on kidney marrow cells from young-adult and old killifish, together with proteomic profiling of both kidney marrow-derived cells and plasma. We show that old killifish display increased markers of inflammation; while immune progenitor-like cell clusters from adult killifish display markers of active proliferation and replication-independent DNA repair, immune cell progenitors from old killifish display increased markers of DNA damage. Within less than 10 weeks, killifish exhibit age-related transformations within the immune system, underscoring the value of killifish for developing immune-system-targeted antiaging interventions.

Aging of the immune system has dramatic consequences on host physiology, reducing tissue regeneration and turnover1, lowering the establishment and maintenance of lasting immune memory2, and increasing the risk for chronic infections3 and autoimmune diseases4. The causes of aging of the immune system are complex and still poorly understood. Both cell-intrinsic and cell-extrinsic factors have been proposed as contributors to aging of the immune compartment5. In mammals, aging of the immune compartment has been associated with increased DNA damage, to a shift in immune cell pools toward more myeloid over lymphoid cells (myeloid shift) and to an increased secretion of inflammatory mediators6. At the systemic level, aging of the immune system has been associated with a low-grade, chronic inflammatory state called inflammaging7. Chronic inflammation, in turn, exacerbates aging of the immune niche8.

To date, the extent to which markers of immune aging displayed in mammals are shared in other vertebrates is poorly understood9, with some evidence of immune senescence occurring in birds10 and some teleosts11,12.

By studying the cellular and molecular basis of immune aging in nonmammalian vertebrates, such as teleosts, we aim to uncover conserved mechanisms of immune aging and identify new targets for interventions to enhance immune health during aging.

While studying immune aging in long-lived species could in principle help us discover the basis for extended homeostasis and immune health, naturally short-lived species offer the advantage of helping reveal the mechanistic basis for age-dependent homeostatic dysfunction.

African killifish of the genus Nothobranchius are characterized by a strikingly short lifespan for a vertebrate, with species living as short as 4 months both in captivity and in nature, after having reached sexual maturity in only 3–4 weeks13,14. Despite being short-lived, these fish display a wide range of age-dependent transformations after the onset of sexual maturation, including telomere shortening15,16, tumorigenesis12, neurodegeneration17,18, decreased capacity of tissue regeneration19, increased markers of cellular senescence in heart, brain and skin17,20,21, loss of microbial richness in the intestine22, decline in sperm quality23 and decline in the diversity of the B cell antibody repertoire24. Given their wide range of conserved aging phenotypes, several killifish species—especially turquoise killifish (Nothobranchius furzeri)—have been rapidly adopted in the past years as a powerful in vivo vertebrate experimental aging model system25,26. Whether organ-wide aging is caused by shared causes or whether different organs age independently from one another remains an open question, which is as relevant in killifish biology as it is in other organisms, including humans27.

In this study, we explored the changes in aging-dependent expression of canonical molecular markers of immune system aging in turquoise killifish, with a specific focus on the main hematopoietic organ. Teleost fish harbor their main hematopoietic niche in the kidney marrow (also called ‘head kidney’), which is regarded as homologous in structure and function to the mammalian bone marrow28–30. To gain insights into molecular changes specific to different cell types and to functionally test immune responses in young versus old killifish, as well as male and female killifish, we used cytometry, transcriptomics, proteomics and a functional in vitro assay in immune cells stimulated with endotoxin (lipopolysaccharide (LPS)). Furthermore, to characterize the age-dependent changes within the main hematopoietic organ and in the plasma of turquoise killifish, we generated ‘KIAMO’, an R Shiny app that combines different ‘omics’ data. Together, we generated a comprehensive reference dataset for immune aging in turquoise killifish and shed light on the mechanistic bases of spontaneous aging-dependent functional decline in a nonmammalian vertebrate.

Turquoise killifish evolved a short lifespan, accompanied by organ-wide accumulation of aging markers31. Recent work focused on the B cell compartment has shown that turquoise killifish display age-dependent decline in the antibody repertoire diversity, a bona fide marker of aging of immune effector cells24. We asked whether turquoise killifish display systemic markers of immune aging by performing tandem mass tag–mass spectrometry (TMT–MS) in plasma from five adult (7 weeks old) and five aged-adult (16 weeks old) male turquoise killifish (Fig. 1 and Methods). For this analysis, we chose male over female killifish for technical reasons, because of their larger absolute size, which makes blood drawing more convenient (Fig. 1a). We found that plasma from adult killifish has a distinct proteome from old killifish. We then asked whether aged killifish plasma shows enrichment for terms associated with immune system activation and inflammatory processes. None of the protein peptides identified using MS are bona fide pro-inflammatory cytokines. However, we detected higher expression levels for several acute-phase proteins among the core enrichment genes of aged fish plasma, including complement proteins and coagulation factors, which have been used as inflammatory markers in other teleost models32,33 and in mice34 (Fig. 1c). In addition to the previous set of standard inflammatory markers, aged fish plasma also showed other core enrichment genes reported to be involved in aging and inflammatory processes: carboxy-peptidase N1 (ref. 35), annexin A4 (ref. 36) and apolipoprotein D37 (Fig. 1c). Noteworthy, plasma from aged fish showed higher levels of insulin38 and IGF1 (ref. 39) compared to young adults, supporting an age-dependent systemic metabolic imbalance (Fig. 1c). A potent anti-inflammatory protein, annexin A1 (lipocortin-1)40 was decreased in plasma from aged fish compared to young-adult fish (Fig. 1c).

To further explore whether inflammation also affected cells residing in the killifish main hematopoietic organ (that is, the kidney marrow), we investigated the presence of histological markers of inflammation. We assessed the presence of tissue fibrosis, a marker of tissue inflammation41, performing Sirius Red/Fast Green staining (Methods) in three 7-week-old (young-adult) and three 16-week-old (aged) killifish. We detected collagen deposition exclusively in the aged killifish tissues (Extended Data Fig. 1), supporting that aged killifish also undergo organ-specific inflammation.

Together, plasma proteomics and histology revealed that killifish from the older age group displayed increased markers of inflammation both systemically and in the main hematopoietic cell niche, in line with published results obtained in killifish livers, brains and intestine22,42.

To characterize the cellular composition of the killifish main hematopoietic organ (the kidney marrow), we performed a single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis using 10X technology. We sequenced cells from three nonaged adult and three aged male killifish. Five of six samples showed high-quality reads and sufficient read numbers (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 2a). We performed three sequencing runs; the first one detected around 4,000 cells per fish sample, while the second and third ones detected around 6,000 cells per fish sample (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 2a). We clustered the cells using shared nearest neighbor graph construction and the first ten principal components, yielding 16 clusters (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 2b). We annotated each cell cluster using differentially expressed and cluster-specific genes across the dataset (Fig. 2a and Methods). We observed a large cell cluster containing neutrophils (for example, expressing ncf1, ncf2 and ncf4) and monocytes/macrophages (for example, expressing mrc1b and marco). We also observed several clusters containing red blood cells (for example, expressing blvrb, gata1a and hbm) and red blood cell progenitors (for example, expressing pcna in addition to blvrb, gata1a and hbm). Next, we identified two yet undefined progenitor-like cell clusters (expressing pcna and cdk1), which did not show any obvious cell-type marker, a B cell cluster (for example, expressing swap70a, cd74b and cd79a), a natural killer (NK) cell cluster and two smaller hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) clusters. To characterize whether cells from either cluster underwent changes in cell number between young and aged fish, we extracted cells from the kidney marrow of five young-adult (8-week-old) and four aged (17-week-old) male killifish and analyzed them with the Amnis ImageStreamX MkII Imaging Flow Cytometer. Cells from cluster B showed a significant increase in relative abundance in aged fish compared to adult fish (two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test P = 0.016) (Fig. 3b). However, the other two cell clusters did not show any significant age-dependent change (two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test P = 0.90 and 0.29, respectively). Together, our cytometry analysis broadly indicates that a cell cluster that putatively contains teleost progenitor cells within the kidney marrow undergoes age-dependent changes between adult and aged-adult killifish.

To assess which biological processes in the immune cells of the kidney marrow are affected the most during aging, we ran TMT–MS on proteins extracted from kidney marrow immune cells isolated from the same young-adult and old individuals used for plasma proteomics. Many more proteins were detected in head kidney proteomics than in plasma proteomics; several of them were differentially expressed between the two age groups (Extended Data Fig. 4). We asked which biological processes differ most with age and found that aged individuals had lower expression of proteins involved in cellular proliferation, ‘DNA repair’, ‘cellular detoxification from toxic substances’ and ‘negative regulation of chemotaxis’ (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 4). Aged individuals had enrichment for proteins involved in immune cell activation, such as ‘interleukin-1b production’, a prominent pro-inflammatory cytokine46, ‘response to molecule of bacterial origin’ and ‘response to external biotic stimulus’ (Fig. 4a–c and Extended Data Figs. 4 and 5). Furthermore, several terms upregulated in old indicate active metabolic detoxification processes (for example, ‘alcohol metabolic process’ and ‘cellular aldehyde metabolic process’), as well as oxidative stress defense (for example, ‘glutathione metabolic process’) and remodeling of the extracellular matrix (for example, ‘uronic acid metabolic process’, ‘extracellular matrix organization’ and ‘biological adhesion’). Proteomics data showed enrichment for cell cycle and DNA repair terms in immune cells from young-adult individuals. We asked whether the higher expression of proteins associated with DNA repair in young-adult individuals was entirely explained by proliferation-dependent DNA damage. We set up an independent experiment using immune cells isolated from five 8-week-old (young-adult) and four 17-week-old (aged-adult) turquoise killifish. Because several of the DNA repair proteins overexpressed in young individuals participate in homologous recombination (Extended Data Fig. 5), a pathway specifically used by cells to repair DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), we assessed the presence of DSBs and genomic instability using an antibody against γH2AX, a well-established marker of DSBs47 (Methods). First, we tested whether commercial antibodies for γH2AX work in killifish to recognize DNA damage. To this end, we induced DNA damage with ultraviolet (UV) irradiation and tested the signal for γH2AX in immune cells extracted from the kidney marrow (Methods). Indeed, UV irradiation induced higher signal for either used antibody (cat. no. 9718S, Cell Signaling Technology, and cat. no. GTX127342, GeneTex) compared to nonirradiated control cells (Extended Data Fig. 6). Then, we used the Amnis ImageStreamX MkII Imaging Flow Cytometer as a platform for a high-throughput imaging of the γH2AX signal48 (Extended Data Fig. 7a). In each sample, to assess the DSB signal in 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI)+positive cells, we used two different measures: the fraction of cells positive for DSBs over the total number of cells, calculated as the number of cells positive for γH2AX over the total number of DAPI+ cells, and the median intensity of the fluorescent signal for γH2AX+ cells. Immune cells isolated from the kidney marrow of aged fish contained more γH2AX+ cells and higher signal intensities compared to those derived from young fish, indicating a higher signal of DNA damage in cells from aged fish (Extended Data Fig. 7b) and confirming that in killifish, as in other organisms, somatic DNA damage increases as a function of age49–51 (Extended Data Fig. 1b,c).

Then, we asked whether cytometry confirmed the proteomics signal of higher cell proliferation in immune cells from younger fish. To this end, we used the DAPI signal of immune cells extracted from the kidney marrows of our aging cohorts52, which provided us with a measure of the phase of the cell cycle. Indeed, in kidney marrows from young-adult fish, the fraction of cells in the S/G2/M phase was higher compared to aged fish, supporting the higher proliferation signal in immune cells isolated from young-adult fish (Extended Data Fig. 5).

If the signal for DSBs is uniquely associated with the cell cycle phase, we would expect cells under proliferation to carry a higher signal for DSB. Therefore, we asked whether the higher signal of DNA damage that we observed in immune cells from old killifish was associated with a higher fraction of proliferating cells (cells in the S/G2/M phase). We observed that cells from aged killifish, while having a smaller fraction of dividing cells compared to cells from young killifish, had a stronger signal for DNA damage (γH2AX) (Extended Data Fig. 7). We then reasoned that an increased DNA damage signal in cells from aged head kidneys could result as the consequence of (1) fewer dividing cells presenting a higher signal for DNA damage or (2) non-dividing cells displaying markers of DNA damage. As the DNA damage signal in replicating cells does not significantly change during aging (Extended Data Fig. 7c), we considered unlikely that the increased age-dependent signal of DNA damage might be attributable to the pool of dividing cells. Our findings suggest that cell proliferation may not be the primary cause of DNA damage signals in cells from aged killifish (Fig. 6). As we observed an age-dependent increase of DNA DSBs, we asked which cell populations were most affected by this signal. Using a cytometry-based approach, we found that cells from cluster B (probably attributable to progenitors) showed most of the age-specific changes in γH2AX signal (Extended Data Fig. 7). To better explore the cell identity of the cells carrying most of the γH2AX signal, we adopted a machine-learning-based approach (Methods), consisting in classifying different cell types based on brightfield images acquired using ImageStream (Fig. 6a). First, to automatically classify each cell type, we manually annotated all the main immune cell types based on their appearance as brightfield images. Then, we ran the model on a total of approximately 90,000 cells. To measure the concordance between manual classification and model prediction, we manually scored 19% of all cells from each cluster identified by the model (Methods), which gave us an accuracy value ranging from 75% for erythrocytes to 96% for polynucleated giant cells (Extended Data Fig. 8). Combining γH2AX antibody staining with our machine-learning-based approach, we could support our finding that among all cell types, the cell classified as progenitors had the strongest signal for DSB within the killifish kidney marrow during aging (Fig. 6d).

We further asked whether in the cells that, based on cytometry, we scored as putative progenitors, that is, cluster B, the signal for DSBs correlated with the proportion of dividing cells. Despite an increased signal for DSBs in both progenitor cell clusters in old versus young killifish, we did not observe an increased age-dependent proportion of dividing progenitors (Fig. 6c–e and Extended Data Fig. 9), supporting our previous finding. Therefore, the accumulation of signal for DNA damage in aged killifish progenitors is not associated with cell proliferation.

In our machine learning model, the progenitor population identified as progenitor-2 showed in general less age-related differences in DSB signal and dividing cell ratio compared to the progenitor-1 population. However, we acknowledge that this could be explained by the lower specificity of progenitor-2 classification in our model, which classifies around 50% of erythrocytes as progenitor-2 (false positives), hence buffering the signal in this population.

To independently validate at the molecular level our machine learning classifier, we designed probes using the scRNA-seq data, which provides information about genes with high cell-type-specific expression (for example, Fig. 2b). We used pcna as a marker for progenitors, and mrc1, ncf1 and swap70 as additional control markers, which—based on our scRNA-seq data—showed higher expression in macrophages, neutrophils and B cells, respectively. To label each cell type, we designed fluorescent probes against each marker and performed fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) on whole immune cells isolated from kidney marrows in ten nonaged adult (five male, five female) and ten aged (five male, five female) killifish (Methods). To acquire information about cell morphology, FSC and SSC, we ran the samples on the ImageStream cytometer (Extended Data Fig. 9b). Indeed, cells that our machine learning classifier scored as progenitors, showed the highest signal for pcna. To note, cells from male and female fish showed higher levels of pcna in two distinct progenitor clusters (progenitor-1 for male and progenitor-2 for female killifish). Moreover, cells classified as progenitors did not show high signal for mrc1, ncf1 and swap70a. Conversely, cells that our machine-learning-based classifier scored as myeloid or lymphoid, showed lower pcna signal compared to cells scored as progenitors. Unsurprisingly, mrc1, ncf1 and swap70a did not fully agree with our machine-learning-based classifier for myelomonocytes and lymphocytes, possibly because more markers should be integrated to capture cell type diversity within the main killifish immune organ.

Next, we asked whether the aging-related increase of γH2AX signal is localized to telomeres, a typical source of signal for genomic DSB53. We performed an immunofluorescence assay on immune cells isolated from young and aged individuals using an antibody for γH2AX and a peptide nucleic acid (PNA) probe for FISH against telomeric repeats (Methods). Noteworthy, we did not observe any clear localization of γH2AX in the telomeres across age groups, suggesting that the signal of DNA DSB in old killifish kidney marrows might not be specifically linked to telomeric regions (Extended Data Fig. 10a).

Upon LPS-induced activation, different immune cell types, including peripheral blood mononuclear cells, form cellular aggregates55,56. LPS induces potent immune activation in several cell types, including T cells, NK cells57, dendritic cells58 and macrophages59. We asked whether LPS induced observable response in freshly extracted immune cells from young and aged fish. We found that LPS induced immune cell aggregate formation in a dose-dependent and age-dependent manner. As our previous results showed that cells isolated from aged fish have higher markers of DNA damage, we asked whether cells from aged fish also presented typical markers of aging-dependent senescence. We performed a β-galactosidase (β-gal) assay and measured the signal with the ImageStream cytometer (Fig. 7d–f). Compared to nonaged adults, we observed a higher senescence signal in immune cells from aged killifish, similar to mammals60 (Fig. 7d). Strikingly, immune cells from aged killifish also showed a higher proportion of large-size senescence cells, which were nearly absent in nonaged adult fish (Fig. 7e,f). The presence of large senescent cells in the kidney marrow of aged killifish recapitulates a conserved senescence-specific feature previously identified in budding yeast and mammalian cells61,62.

Following our machine-learning-based classification, we identified that the age-dependent increase in senescence signal was the highest in the two progenitor clusters (Fig. 7g).

We further asked whether sex affects senescence across cell types; indeed, we found that sex differentially affects baseline senescence levels in erythrocytes and in both progenitor cell types (Fig. 7h). Furthermore, we found that immune cells classified as progenitor-1 and progenitor-2 had different levels of pcna signal—a marker of proliferation—depending on sex. Specifically, cells classified as progenitor-1 had a higher pcna signal in male killifish, while cells classified as progenitor-2 had a higher proliferation signal in female killifish (Extended Data Fig. 9a). In either sex, the pcna signal was lower in aged compared to nonaged adult fish, which is consistent with the age-dependent reduction in proliferation markers observed in the proteomics data.

Next, we asked whether the senescence signal in cells from aged killifish contributed to the outcome of the LPS-induced cell cluster. To this end, we pretreated for 24 h immune cells isolated from young and aged fish with 15 μM of the senolytic fisetin63. After fisetin treatment, we seeded the cells with 50 μg ml−1 LPS. Immune cells derived from aged individuals treated with fisetin showed a number of cell aggregates upon induction with LPS comparable to young cells; immune cells from young-adult individuals treated with fisetin showed no obvious differences compared to control, untreated cells (Extended Data Fig. 10a). In nonaged controls, carrying a limited number of senescent cells, fisetin slightly affected the number of senescence-associated β-gal+ cells in a dose-dependent manner, with a high dose of fisetin leading to genome instability (Extended Data Fig. 10). While the role of cellular senescence in the outcome of LPS-induced immune activation still remains to be fully characterized, these findings show that in a cell-based assay of immune induction, after pretreatment with a senolytic, immune cells extracted from aged donors could respond to immune activation in a young-like fashion.

To allow multi-omics comparison of plasma proteomics, head kidney proteomics and scRNA-seq in the kidney marrow, we developed KIAMO (Killifish Immune Aging MultiOmics; https://github.com/mdonertas/KIAMO/wiki).

KIAMO is a web-based tool designed to explore gene expression in aging killifish (GRZ-AD strain) at the proteomics and scRNA-seq levels. To facilitate direct visualization and comparison across these datasets within immune organs, we designed KIAMO using a user-friendly Shiny interface (https://genome.leibniz-fli.de/shiny/kiamo/). KIAMO provides comparisons across multi-omics data, including plasma proteomics, kidney marrow proteomics and scRNA-seq from kidney marrow, enabling researchers to conduct comparative analyses of immune aging in killifish. Furthermore, KIAMO provides intuitive visualization tools, such as scRNA-seq t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) highlighted per gene, heatmaps showing cell type specificity of gene expression and box plots for proteomics data, including statistical tests, which help identify differences in protein levels between young and aged samples. KIAMO serves as a resource for the wider research community, offering initial exploration of gene and protein expression patterns, either focusing on certain age-related changes or specific genes across immune organs and cell types. To identify biomarkers of immune aging, KIAMO allows to query proteins, for example, with increased abundance in older plasma and head kidney tissues, and to correlate these findings with scRNA-seq data from the same tissues.

The immune system provides systemic surveillance, protecting organisms from a range of insults that come from the environment, for example, tissue lesions, parasites and pathogens, and from internal processes, for example, neoplastic transformations. Together, an effective immune system preserves homeostasis, while immune system malfunction is associated with a plethora of diseases, from chronic infections to cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, metabolic diseases and systemic inflammation. During the aging process, the immune system becomes compromised because it is no longer able to effectively fight off pathogens and promote successful repair in damaged tissues. However, immune system aging is not solely associated with ‘lowered’ immune effector function. Immune system aging is often characterized by hyperreactive immune responses64 and autoimmune inflammation, where the immune system is no longer able to properly discriminate self from non-self, resulting in increased autoreactivity65.

Immune system aging might be one of the main drivers of systemic aging-related functional decline at the organismal level across organisms4,66,67. Cell-intrinsic mechanisms underlie aging-dependent failure in the HSC niche68, including reduced regenerative capacity and an age-dependent increase in genes involved in the myeloid versus lymphoid lineage fate. Immune effector cells can also be affected during aging, including lymphocytes69,70, as well as cells from the myeloid lineage71,72. However, to date, the main molecular and cellular drivers of systemic aging of the immune system are still largely unclear.

Our work aimed to characterize the molecular and cellular signatures of vertebrate immune system aging. To this end, we adopted as a model system the turquoise killifish (N. furzeri), a naturally short-lived vertebrate with a lifespan ranging from 4 to 8 months13,26,31, and a plethora of spontaneous age-dependent transformations, including neoplasias73, neurodegeneration74 and intestinal dysbiosis22. Recent studies showed that aged killifish display markers of intestinal inflammation22 and undergo expansion of large B cell clones systemically, and a decrease in naive B cell diversity in mucosal organs24.

In this study, we explored immune aging in turquoise killifish both systemically and in the main immune organ, that is, the kidney marrow. Furthermore, to allow easy access and visualization of single-cell transcriptomic data, and proteomic data in plasma, in the head kidney of young and aged killifish, we developed an R Shiny app that we named KIAMO.

Killifish plasma shows age-dependent, spontaneous increased abundance of insulin and insulin-like growth factor 1, two major markers of aging-related metabolic and hormonal imbalance75. Plasma from aged killifish is enriched with peptides associated with inflammatory processes, including acute-phase proteins, and has declined levels of anti-inflammatory markers, such as annexin A1. We further asked whether the kidney marrow in killifish undergoes significant age-dependent transformations. Charting cell diversity using scRNA-seq, we found that killifish are characterized by the presence of all the main immune cell types found across vertebrates, including stem cells, clusters of progenitors and differentiated cells from the erythroid, myeloid and lymphoid lineages. Our cytometry analysis suggests an increased presence of progenitor cells during aging, coupled with decreased overall proliferation, supported by both cytometry and proteomics. Together, our results suggest the presence of an aging-dependent increased pool of progenitor cells, which could be compatible with impaired differentiation into effector cells.

Our results further indicate a spontaneous increase in interleukin-1 beta production in cells from the kidney marrows of aged individuals. As interleukin-1 beta is the key mediator of inflammasome responses, our findings in killifish directly link inflammasome activation in the primary immune organ with aging. Proteomics in the kidney marrow between young-adult and aged killifish showed age-dependent enhanced oxidative stress buffering, including increased glutathione metabolism and upregulation of peptides involved in detoxification processes, which may help clear accumulated metabolic byproducts, such as alcohols, hydroxy compounds and aldehydes. Peptides associated with tissue remodeling and cytoskeletal reorganization in aged killifish suggest loss of niche integrity; aged-killifish-specific increased secretory activity suggests a senescence-associated secretory phenotype or inflammatory signaling. Cells from the killifish kidney marrow present higher levels of markers of DNA DSBs and replication stress in aged versus nonaged adults, while proteomics markers of active cell cycle and DNA repair are lower in aged adult killifish compared to young adults. Markers of DNA damage can occur in association with active cell replication. However, our results support the notion that, overall, the higher signal for DNA damage in immune cells from aged individuals is independent from cell replication. A possible explanation for a higher DNA DSB signal in progenitors of aged fish, despite a lower cell cycle, is that the cell cycle, while being a direct source of DNA damage, is also strictly connected with active DNA repair76. It is conceivable that the epigenetic shift occurring during aging might explain why immune cells from aged killifish kidney marrows might have a suppressed DNA repair machinery. Exploring further the chromosomal and cellular localization of DNA DSBs in cells from aged kidney marrows, we found no specific localization to telomeric regions, as well as enrichment in progenitor-like cells.

Together, progenitors undergo DNA damage and possibly replication stress upon aging, while they might be protected by active DNA repair mechanisms in nonaged, younger adults.

Hence, contrary to the expectation that hematopoietic progenitors would be geno-protected throughout life, we found that progenitor cells from aged killifish carry higher levels of DNA damage compared to all other cell types in the hematopoietic organ, as recently shown in aged mammalian HSCs77,78, and to some extent, in aged-adult humans79. To note, adopting a machine-learning-based classification approach, we found that different classes of progenitor cells express markers of active proliferation in male versus female killifish hematopoietic organs across age groups, which might in principle lead to functional shifts in effector cell abundance and function in either sex. Alongside decreased age-dependent proliferation and increased DNA damage in immune cells from the kidney marrow, we found an age-dependent spontaneous increase in signal for cellular senescence in the same compartment. To gain further insights into cell function, we tested cellular responses to cell activation using LPS stimulation. Cells from old and young killifish showed different classes of responses (cell aggregate size and numbers). To test whether increased senescence in immune cells from the kidney marrow contributed to the cellular activation in young versus aged killifish, we treated cells from young and aged killifish with a senolytic (fisetin) before immune cell stimulation using LPS. In cells from older donors, this treatment partly restored cellular responses to a younger level. While our in vitro results show a putative role for senescence in immune cell functional changes during killifish aging, whether the molecular and cellular changes occurring in the killifish kidney marrow during aging lead to systemic functional consequences remains to be assessed in more detail.

Remarkably, in about 2 months, that is, from nonaged adults to aged-adult killifish, we observed the rapid onset of extensive markers of immune aging in the main killifish hematopoietic organ. The deep evolutionary conservation of molecular and cellular attributes that change during aging between killifish and mammals makes killifish a highly suitable experimental system to test concrete manipulations that can directly affect aging in the immune compartment and that might have practical applications also in mammals, including humans.

To prepare the libraries for 10X scRNA-seq, we used the 10X Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3’ Library (PN-120234) & Gel Bead Kit v2 (PN-120235) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. We isolated immune cells from the kidney marrows of three young-adult (8-week-old) and three geriatric (21-week-old) fish. Then, for each sample, single-cell suspensions were encapsulated into gel beads in emulsion (GEMs) using the Chromium Controller. Barcoded reverse transcription primers were used to capture the polyadenylated mRNA; complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed within the GEMs. After GEM incubation, GEMs were broken and cDNA was purified. The cDNA underwent amplification and library construction through end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation and sample index PCR. cDNA yield and size distribution was measured using an Agilent Bioanalyzer. Sequencing of the single-cell libraries was performed by the Cologne Centre on Genomics, Cologne, Germany.

Proteins from immune cells isolated from frozen kidney marrows were extracted with a guanidine chloride protocol; 20 μl of lysis buffer (6 M guanidinium chloride, 2.5 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine, 10 mM chloroacetamide, 100 mM Tris-HCl) were added to the pellet. The samples were heated for 10 min at 95 °C and then lysed with a Bioruptor for ten cycles of 30-s sonication and 30-s break on high performance. The samples were then centrifuged at 20,000g for 20 min. The supernatant was transferred into a new tube and the protein concentration was measured using NanoDrop. To prepare peptides from both serum and immune cell protein samples, 300 μg of protein per sample was digested with 1:200 trypsin (w/w) at 37 °C overnight. The digest was then acidified with formic acid (FA) to a final concentration of 1% to stop tryptic digest. The samples were then centrifuged at 20,000g for 10 min to pellet any remaining debris. The peptides were then cleaned with a StageTip protocol (C18-SD tips), including a series of wetting, equilibrating, washing and eluting steps. The C18-SD tips were first washed with 200 μl methanol using centrifugation for 1 min, followed by a wash with 200 μl 40% acetonitrile/0.1% FA using centrifugation for 1 min. The tips were then equilibrated with 200 μl 0.1% FA using centrifugation for 1 min. The digests were then loaded onto the tips and centrifuged for 2 min to ensure proper loading. The tips were washed twice with 200 μl 0.1% FA, followed by elution of the peptides with 100 μl 40% acetonitrile/0.1% FA by centrifuging for 4 min at 1,500g. The eluates were dried in a Speed-Vac at 45 °C for 45 min, then resuspended in 0.1% FA and quantified with NanoDrop; 4 μg of the eluted peptides were dried and reconstituted in 9 μl of 0.1 M tetraethylammonium bromide. Labeling with TMTs (TMTproTM 16plex, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Proteomics data were analyzed using MaxQuant v.1.6.10.43 (ref. 80). Peptide fragmentation spectra were searched against the canonical sequences of the N. furzeri proteome (downloaded September 2019 from UniProt). Protein names and primary gene names, corresponding to the UniProt IDs were downloaded from UniProt and used to annotate the data. Methionine oxidation and protein N-terminal acetylation were set as variable modifications; cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as a fixed modification. The digestion parameters were set to ‘specific’ and ‘Trypsin/P’. Quantification was set to ‘Reporter ion MS3’. The isotope purity correction factors, provided by the manufacturer, were imported and included in the analysis. The minimum number of peptides and razor peptides for protein identification was one; the minimum number of unique peptides was zero. Protein identification was performed at peptide spectrum matches and a protein false discovery rate of 0.01. The ‘second peptide’ option was on. TMT reporter intensities were normalized using vsn81 and log2-transformed in R v.3.4.3 (ref. 82). We calculated the differences between log2 median protein abundance levels of adult and aged-adult samples to determine differential expression. Statistical significance was assigned based on Wilcoxon signed-rank test using the ‘wilcox.test’ function in R.

GSEA was performed using the ‘gseGO’ function in the ‘clusterProfiler’ package (v.4.2.2)83, using human gene symbols and the ‘org.Hs.eg.db’ annotation package in R. The Ensembl database and R ‘biomaRt’ package (v.2.50.2) were used to map ortholog genes between human and N. furzeri genes (date of access: 17 February 2022). We analyzed the GO Biological Processes categories with a minimum of ten and maximum of 500 annotated genes. We used Benjamini–Yekutieli correction for multiple testing and considered a Benjamini–Yekutieli-corrected P < 0.05 as significant. Details of human-*N. furzeri* orthology mapping are explained under ‘Helper functions/data -> Gene ID & Orthology mapping’. Two outputs from the ‘gseGO’ function were used: core enrichment genes (that is, genes that contribute most to the enrichment result) and normalized enrichment score. As the GO enrichment results gave many significant GO categories but most of them showed overlaps, we used an in-house method to choose representative GO categories for visualization and summarization purposes. To choose representative GO categories, we calculated the Jaccard similarity between GO categories based on core enrichment genes. We then performed hierarchical clustering of the similarity matrix, cutting the tree at different levels (20–70 clusters). We calculated the median Jaccard index within each cluster. We took the minimum number of clusters, k, where at least half of the clusters had a median Jaccard index of 0.5 or higher. This resulted in 20 clusters (that is, representatives) for plasma proteomics and 50 clusters (that is, representatives) for kidney marrow proteomics.

N. furzeri primary genome assembly and genome annotations were downloaded from Ensembl (v.105). Genome annotations were filtered to include only the protein-coding immunoglobin or T cell receptor genes using the ‘cellranger mkgtf’ command. A custom reference for N. furzeri was built using the ‘cellranger mkref’ command. A count matrix was generated for each sample in each run separately using the ‘cellranger count’ function. Only cells with a minimum of 200 and a maximum of 2,500 features were kept. First, all samples in each run were log-normalized independently using the ‘NormalizeData’ function in Seurat. Next, 2,000 variable features were selected using the ‘vst’ method implemented in the ‘FindVariableFeatures’ function in Seurat. For clustering, we used shared nearest neighbor graph construction with a resolution of 0.3, which provided the last most stable clustering based on the clustering tree. Then, differentially expressed markers for each cluster were identified. All genes expressed in at least 25% of cells in a cluster were tested. Based on the resulting marker list, cell types were hand-annotated using CellMarker 2.0 (http://bio-bigdata.hrbmu.edu.cn/CellMarker/) as the reference database. As four clusters suggested contamination from gonads, we excluded those cells from the analysis. The same steps were repeated except we used the first 20 principal components for clustering, uniform manifold approximation and projection, and t-SNE plots. The previous steps for cell clustering and cell type annotation were repeated, yielding 16 clusters. To further refine these annotations, we reanalyzed our dataset using a recent scRNA-seq dataset30. First, to assess the accuracy of reference-based annotation, we subsampled 20% of kidney cells from male fish (436 cells) from the Teefy et al.30 dataset and reannotated these cells using Seurat (v.3.2.2) in R, with the remaining cells from their dataset as the reference. This approach achieved approximately 80% accuracy across clusters, although progenitor cells showed lower annotation performance, with accuracy around 50–60%. Subsequently, we applied the Teefy et al.30 dataset as a reference to reannotate our own data. Most clusters aligned well with our manual annotations, with over 60% of cells in each cluster annotated consistently. Exceptions included clusters 4 and 13, which corresponded closely to multiple cell types without a clear, distinct annotation. We decided to maintain the labeling of clusters 4 and 13 as B cells and NK cells, respectively; a different labeling was suggested using the Teefy et al.30 annotation. In particular, cluster 4 was suggested to contain both B cells and erythrocytes using the Teefy et al.30 annotation; however, it shows cluster-specific expression of B cell markers like CD74b and CD79a, not usually expressed by erythrocytes84,85. Cluster 13 was suggested to contain macrophages and erythrocytes by the Teefy et al.30 annotation. However, we decided to maintain NK cell labeling for this cluster because it is very close to other lymphocyte clusters and expresses both macrophages and lymphoid markers.

Four GO representative categories—Cellular detoxification, DNA repair, DNA replication and Immune cell activation—were chosen for further investigation in the scRNA-seq data based on the results of the proteomics dataset from the kidney marrow. These GO categories represent 35, 14, 55 and 15 GO categories, respectively. All human genes associated with these categories were obtained by propagating gene membership in the GO hierarchical tree using the ‘GO.db’ (v.3.14), ‘AnnotationDbi’ (v.1.56.2) and ‘org.Hs.eg.db’ (v.3.14.0) packages in R (date of access: 7 April 2022). We then mapped this information to the orthologs in N. furzeri, intersecting with the scRNA-seq dataset, getting the genes with an absolute value of log2(fold change) of 1 in the kidney marrow proteomics data and only using the genes present in only one of the four categories. For each cell in the dataset, we calculated the percentage of the expressed genes in each representative category and divided this number by the percentage of expressed genes among all genes detected using the scRNA-seq. Thus, we aimed to normalize the overall transcriptional profile of cells and obtained an OR showing the enrichment of genes specific to the GO categories of interest. We colored cells on t-SNE with this OR.

We analyzed all the raw data (.rif files) using Amnis IDEAS v.6. First, to train the machine learning algorithm (linear discriminant analysis machine learning algorithm), we created a merged.rif file containing the raw data of all the adult and aged-adult samples; then, we manually labeled all cell types based on specific cytometry features86. The training was performed by using 316 cells manually annotated as ‘erythrocytes’, 291 cells manually annotated as ‘progenitor-1’, 137 cells manually annotated as ‘myelomonocytes’, 235 cells manually annotated as ‘lymphocytes’, 261 cells manually annotated as ‘polynucleated cells’ and 116 cells manually annotated as ‘progenitor-2’. To differentiate between the two classes of progenitors, based on the available fish literature, we used the following approach: progenitor-1 cells have an irregular cell shape, no black dots in the cytoplasm and low cell brightness in the brightfield. Instead, progenitor-2 cells have a round shape, no black dots in the cytoplasm and a darker color under brightfield. In addition, both cell types are smaller than myeloid cells and are larger than lymphoid cells. To test the accuracy of the model, we manually labeled randomly for each training class additional cells as ‘TRUE’ representatives of the class (corresponding to 19% of the training class cell number). These additional ‘TRUE’ classes do not overlap with the cells used in classes for the training algorithm. Then, for each ‘TRUE’ class, we checked the ratio of predicted classes. A classification index of zero was used as a threshold to determine the predicted classes. The percentages of the true cell populations in predicted classes are provided as a measure of accuracy. Then, we used the initial training set to classify different clusters predicted in each single.rif data file for adult and aged-adult samples and in the two merged.rif files we generated for both adult and aged-adult conditions.

To assess the changes in β-gal staining between aged and nonaged adult killifish, we used a hierarchical Bayesian model, using the rstan package in the R programming environment. The model was designed to assess the effects of age and cell type on the expression of β-gal (bgal_cl) across different immune cell types.

The dataset included measurements of β-gal levels (bgal_cl) in five distinct immune cell types—erythrocytes (E), lymphocytes (L), monocytes (M), progenitor-1 (P1) and progenitors-2 (P2)—collected from killifish at two different ages (8 weeks and 17 weeks). The variable week was transformed into a binary variable (age_binary), where 0 indicated young killifish (8 weeks) and 1 indicated aged killifish (17 weeks). The cell_type variable was converted into a numeric factor to facilitate its use in the hierarchical model.

Given the nature of the β-gal measurements, which are constrained between 0 and 1, a Beta distribution was chosen as the likelihood function for this hierarchical Bayesian model. The Beta distribution is particularly suited for modeling proportions and rates, making it a better fit than the Gaussian distribution for these data.

Non-informative normal priors were placed on the intercept (alpha), the main effect of age (beta_age), the cell-type-specific intercepts (alpha_cell) and the interaction effects (beta_age_cell), with a mean of 0 and an s.d. of 10.

A weakly informative Gamma prior was used for the precision parameter (phi), ensuring that the model can accommodate a wide range of variances in β-gal expression levels.

The likelihood function assumed that the observed β-gal levels (bgal_cl) followed a Beta distribution, with a mean parameterized as a linear combination of the overall intercept, the main effect of age, the cell-type-specific intercept and the interaction between age and cell type, as follows:

bgal_cl[i] ~ Beta(mu[i] * phi, (1 - mu[i]) * phi)

where mu[i] = logit^(-1)(alpha + beta_age * age_binary[i] + alpha_cell[cell_type[i]] + beta_age_cell[cell_type[i]] * age_binary[i]).

The model was fitted using the stan() function, with 2,000 iterations across four Markov Chain Monte Carlo chains. Convergence was assessed using trace plots and the Gelman–Rubin diagnostic, with no indication of non-convergence. We further compared the performance of the likelihood function based on a Beta distribution to one using a normal distribution. We performed model cross-validation using leave-one-out in rstan. The analysis is available on our GitHub page at https://github.com/valenzano-lab/public/tree/main/GM.

All experiments used turquoise killifish (N. furzeri, GRZ strain) raised under standardized husbandry conditions in recirculating aquaculture systems at MPI-AGE and FLI. Age groups and sample sizes were chosen based on prior experience with this model, feasibility of tissue and cell yields for each assay, and established practice for comparable omics and cytometry experiments in killifish. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Biological replicates (individual fish) are stated in the figure legends, and include (non-exhaustive): plasma TMT proteomics (n = 5 adult versus n = 5 aged-adult male fish); kidney marrow TMT proteomics (n = 5 adult versus n = 5 aged-adult male fish, same individuals as for the plasma proteomics); ImageStream cytometry for cellularity, proliferation and γH2AX (n = 5 adult versus n = 4 aged-adult male fish); histology (Sirius Red/Fast Green, n = 3 adult versus n = 3 aged-adult); and LPS aggregate formation assays (typically n = 3 adult versus n = 3 aged-adult). Technical replication was used where appropriate (for example, repeated antibody validation for γH2AX after UV irradiation; repeated FISH validation experiments; multiple images and fields quantified per individual for histological readouts as described).

For scRNA-seq, one of six sequenced samples did not meet the quality and read-depth criteria and was excluded before downstream analysis; the remaining five samples were retained. In the scRNA-seq analysis, cells mapping to clusters indicative of gonadal contamination were excluded before final clustering and annotation; downstream analyses were repeated on the filtered dataset. Apart from these predefined quality control and contamination-filtering steps, no data were excluded from the analyses.

Allocation of animals to age groups was determined according to chronological age; therefore, it was not randomized. The experiments were not randomized. Investigators were generally aware of age group allocation during the experiments and outcome assessment; however, for the colony-forming unit/aggregate quantification, cluster counts were performed blinded in Fiji. Overall, the investigators were not blinded to allocation during the experiments and outcome assessment.

Statistical analyses were performed in R v.3.6.1 unless otherwise indicated. For two-group comparisons of continuous outcomes with small sample sizes and non-normal distributions, two-sided nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used (for example, cytometry-derived proportions and intensities; proteomics differential abundance testing via wilcox.test). For the proteomics GSEAs, multiple-testing correction was performed using Benjamini–Yekutieli adjustment, with a Benjamini–Yekutieli-adjusted P < 0.05 considered significant; reporter ion intensities were variance-stabilized (vsn) and log2-transformed before testing. Where Bayesian modeling was used (β-gal), a hierarchical Beta regression model was implemented in R using rstan, with four Markov Chain Monte Carlo chains and 2,000 iterations per chain; model convergence was assessed using trace plots and Gelman–Rubin diagnostics; model comparison and cross-validation was performed using leave-one-out procedures as described. Exact statistical tests, sidedness, multiple-testing procedures, thresholds for significance and the definition of box plot elements (median, IQR, whiskers) are reported in the figure legends. The code used for the omics analyses and the KIAMO Shiny app is publicly available (the GitHub links have been provided in the paper), and the primary datasets are deposited in public repositories as stated under ‘Data availability’.
