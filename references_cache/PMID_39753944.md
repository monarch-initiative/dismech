# Novel variants of FSIP2 and SPEF2 cause varying degrees of spermatozoa damage in MMAF patients and favorable ART outcomes.
**Authors:** Dai CL, Yin XY, Peng ZY, Lin H, Zhang P, Liu G, Li WN
**Journal:** J Assist Reprod Genet (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10815-024-03378-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10815-024-03378-1)

## Content

PURPOSE: This study identified novel variants of the FSIP2 and SPEF2 genes in multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella (MMAF) patients and to investigate the potential effect of variations on male infertility and assisted reproductive outcomes.

METHODS: Whole-exome sequencing was performed in 106 Chinese MMAF patients. The discovered variants were evaluated in silico and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. A mini-gene assay and immunofluorescence staining were used to determine the effects on mRNA and protein. Assisted reproductive technology (ART) based on intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) and in vitro fertilization (IVF) was used for MMAF patients carrying novel variants.

RESULTS: Biallelic variants in FSIP2 or SPEF2 involving nineteen novel variations were found in eleven MMAF patients. These variations included fourteen missense variants, two nonsense variants, two frameshift variants, and a splicing variant. The FSIP2 protein was markedly reduced or mislocalized to the spermatozoa head. Two novel missense variants of SPEF2 reduced cell diameter. Eleven MMAF couples had 12 ICSI cycles and 2 IVF cycles. The 2PN fertilization rate, good-quality embryos rate, and clinical pregnancy rate were 80.1% (133/166), 74.4% (99/133), and 45.7% (16/35). Four of them have seven babies born.

CONCLUSION: Our work revealed that missense variations of FSIP2 or SPEF2 might cause a milder spermatozoa damage. The infertility caused by FSIP2 and SPEF2 variants can be mitigated through ICSI or even IVF. The results of this study presented signs of the correlation of phenotype/genotype for the FSIP2 and SPEF2, which might provide a reference for clinical genetic and fertility consultation.

Keywords:FSIP2,SPEF2, Novel variation, Multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella (MMAF), Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), In vitro fertilization (IVF)

Teratozoospermia is a leading cause of male infertility and represents a heterogeneous group of men with a wide range of abnormal sperm phenotypes affecting, solely or simultaneously, the head, neck/midpiece, and tail of the sperm and, possibly, the presence of abnormal cytoplasmic residues [1,2]. Multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella(MMAF) is a severe form of teratozoospermia caused by structural abnormalities of the flagellum characterized by absent, short, coiled, bent and/or irregular-caliber flagella [3]. Regarding the ultrastructure, MMAF is caused mainly by defects in the flagellar axoneme, including in the nine peripheral microtubule doublets (MDs), one central pair (CP), outer dense fibers (ODFs), the inner dynein arm (IDA), the outer dynein arm (ODA), the mitochondrial sheath (MS), and the fibrous sheath (FS) [4]. Theoretically, genetic abnormalities associated with the composition and function of these structures can lead to this condition. Along this line, since mutations inDNAH1, a member of the dynein axonemal heavy chain (DNAH) family was first identified as the disease-causing gene of MMAF [3], more than 23 genes have been confirmed [5].

Because flagella and motile cilia share conserved 9 + 2 axonemal structures, MMAF can also be described as a manifestation of primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) [6]. However, a marked decrease in or even complete loss of sperm motility is the dominant or isolated phenotype of MMAF-associated teratozoospermia in the majority of patients, and the resulting male primary infertility is the focus of clinical treatment for MMAF [3]. At present, assisted reproductive technology (ART) via intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) is the first option for patients with MMAF who wish to achieve a conjugal pregnancy [7]. Advances in high-throughput sequencing have enabled scientists to identify novel genes more efficiently, and the discovery of novel genes has been a hot topic in MMAF research for the past few years. However, little has been reported about the effects on male fertility and reproductive outcome of the different variants that occur in a given gene. The ICSI outcomes of more than 10 patients can be found for two genes,DNAH1(n= 6 [8] + 12 [9] + 3 [10] + 11 [11] + 7 [12]) andDNAH17(n= 11 [13]). The other genes were mostly reported as isolated cases. Furthermore, for the vast majority of MMAF patients, tail deformity does not involve all spermatozoa, and the onset of infertility does not always occur during young adulthood. Therefore, we aimed to better understand the impacts of different variants in a gene on MMAF and obtain more useful information from medical practice to enable the development of more effective and precise treatments for male infertility.

In this study, we performed whole-exome sequencing (WES) for 106 patients from a cohort of unrelated Han Chinese infertile men with MMAF and identified 19 homozygous or compound heterozygous variants ofFSIP2andSPEF2. All of those variants were novel and have not been reported in the previous literature. On this basis, we evaluated the effect of each variant on sperm morphology and pregnancy outcomes and attempted to obtain more information on the genotype/phenotype relationships for MMAF patients.

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Reproductive & Genetic Hospital of CITIC-Xiangya (LL-SC-2019–018). We enrolled 106 Chinese individuals of Han origin who were diagnosed with teratozoospermia according to the guidelines of the World Health Organization (WHO) laboratory manual for the examination and processing of human semen (fifth edition). Before enrollment, all patients had completed a routine diagnostic workup and had normal Y-chromosome microdeletion test results. A detailed history of infertility progression and written consent has been obtained for each participant.

Routine semen analysis was conducted according to the WHO5thguidelines. Ejaculated semen was obtained from patients after 3–7 days of sexual abstinence. Semen volume, semen concentration, and sperm motility were calculated. The morphology of at least 200 sperm cells was examined by papanicolaou staining to assess the percentage of morphologically abnormal spermatozoa.

Genomic DNA (gDNA) from peripheral blood samples was extracted using a QIAamp DNA Blood Mini kit (Qiagen, 51,104, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Next-generation sequencing was conducted on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform. The human whole-exome data analysis was sequentially performed using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA), Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK), and ANNOVAR. The candidate variants were identified using an in-house bioinformatics pipeline: variants with a minor allele frequency > 1% according to gnomAD (http://gnomad-sg.org/) were discarded. Variants causing frameshifts, in-frame insertions/deletions, stop gains, or stop losses and those impacting splice donor/acceptor or missense variants were retained. Variants that met all three of the following conditions were removed: CADD score < 10, classified as “tolerated” by SIFT, and classified as “benign” by Polyphen-2. In the last step, variants clinically associated with MMAF were qualified. Verification of the candidate variants was conducted using a Green Mix kit (Promega, M7123, USA) and target site primers (TableS1).

The effect of the splice site variant c.2142-1G > T ofSPEF2 was tested using a mini-gene assay. Exons 15 to 17 of the wild-type and mutantSPEF2gene fragments were amplified, cloned, and inserted into the mammalian expression vector pCDNA3.1-Myc-HisC (-) (MIAOLING BIOLOGY, P1017, China). Approximately 2 × 105HEK293T cells were grown to 90% confluency and transfected with 1 µg of plasmid DNA using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000001, USA). After 72 h of culture, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Ambion, 15,596,018, USA). cDNA was generated and amplified with the following primers: GTGCTCAGCTTGGTGCAAAA (forward) and TCTGGTCTCTTAAATTTTGGTCTCC (reverse). Agarose gel electrophoresis and Sanger sequencing were used to detect aberrant transcripts produced by theSPEF2splicing variant.

To confirm the pathogenicity for the novel missense variation ofSPEF2, fully sequenced humanSPEF2cDNA clones were purchased from Bochu Biotechnology Company (Changsha, China). Wild-typeSPEF2was fused to CMV-MCS-FLAG-SV40-Neomycin. Two mutants c.3368G > A(p.R1123Q) and c.578 T > C(p.I193T) were induced into wild-typeSPEF2plasmid using Mut Express II Fast Mutagenesis kit V2 (Vazyme, C214) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

HEK293T cell was cultivated at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2in 24-well plate with cell slides in Dulbecco-modified eagle medium (DMEM) media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10,091–148). When cell density reached 60%, wild and mutantSPEF2plasmids were transiently transfected into HEK293T using X-tremeGENE™ HP DNA Transfection Reagent (Roche, XTGHP-RO). Cells were harvested 48 h later.

The spermatozoa and plasmids-transfected cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and to be fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min; thereafter, the samples were permeabilized using 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocked with 10% donkey serum for 1 h at 25 °C before being incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: FSIP2 polyclonal antibody (Invitrogen, PA5-112,962, 1:100); Flag tag Ab (Abways, AB0008); monoclonal anti-β-Tubulin antibody (Sigma, T4026, 1:500); and lectin PNA (Invitrogen,L32458, 1:200). Then, the slides were subjected to a 1-h incubation at 37 °C with highly cross-adsorbed secondary antibodies, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Life Technologies, A21121, 1:500) and Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Life Technologies, A31572, 1:500). Finally, 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was used to label the nuclei for 5 min at room temperature. Fluorescence microscopy images were obtained with the Olympus IX51 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and analyzed with Video TesT-FISH 2.0 software. The maximum diameter across the nucleus was qualified using ImageJ.

Transvaginal egg retrieval mediated by B-ultrasound was conducted for the female partner. The obtained oocytes were rinsed with 80 IU/mL hyaluronidase to remove cumulus cells after 3–5 h of culture in vitro. Then, the mature oocytes were transferred to G-MOPS media (Vitrolife, 10,130, Sweden) and maintained in incubators at 37 °C with 6% CO2. Depending on the semen parameters and previous assisted reproductive treatment outcomes, the oocytes were inseminated by sperm with progressive motility through IVF procedure, or, first, spermatozoa from patients with normal motility and morphology were selected and then injected into the oocyte using a microinjection needle in the ICSI Procedure. The subsequent culture of the injected oocytes was conducted in G-IVF medium in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 6% CO2, 5% O2, and 89% N2. Cleavage-stage embryos were sorted by integrating the morphology indices of cell number, fragmentation rate, blastomere uniformity, and multinucleation according to the ESHRE guidelines [14] and Chinese expert consensus [15]. Pregnancy was confirmed by human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) measurement 14 days after embryo transfer, and clinical pregnancy was confirmed by the presence of a fetal heartbeat on ultrasound at the seventh week of gestation.

We identified 11 patients (11/106, 10.4%) with compound heterozygous or homozygous variants in theFSIP2(9/106, 8.5%) andSPEF2(2/106, 1.9%) gene. All nineteen variants were novel, and none of them has been reported in the previous literature. The details of the variants are provided in Table1. The frameshift or stop-gain variants c.9234dupA(p.Leu3079ThrfsTer6), c.14107delA(p.Ser4703AlafsTer18), c.232C > T(p.Arg78Ter), and c.2137C > T(p.Arg713Ter) ofFSIP2were predicted to insert a premature stop codon and abrogate the production of a functional protein. The intron variation c.2142-1G > T ofSPEF2was located in the classical splicing acceptor region and was shown to cause abnormal RNA splicing of exon 16 via mini-gene analysis (Fig.1C left), which was speculated to generate a 714-residue-truncated protein. The other fourteen variations were missense, and the bioinformatic signatures were insufficient to confirm their pathogenicity. However, the frequency of these variants was extremely low, and no deleterious variants of other MMAF-related genes were detected. The potential role of these missense variants in MMAF patients cannot be ruled out. All the variants were scattered in theFSIP2andSPEF2genes (Fig.1). No obvious mutation hotspot region was found.

The ages of the individuals who carried variants ranged from 22 to 39 years. The male external genitalia were normal. Patients 3 and 10 each had a left varicocele. Infertility history records revealed that patients 3, 8, and 9 had secondary infertility (Table2).

Clinical phenotypes of patients carrying biallelic variants ofFSIP2orSPEF2

As shown in Table3, the semen parameters of the 11 individuals with variants were abnormal. Patients 2–4 and patient 9 had asthenospermia, patient 11 had oligospermia, and patients 1, 5–8, and 10 had oligoasthenospermia. The spermatozoa in patients 5, 7, 8, and 10 were completely motionless.

The mean normal morphology of 5.0% ± 1.7% was very poor in the sperm population of 9 patients withFSIP2variants. Among the five morphological abnormality types, short flagella were the most common (32.2% ± 21.2%). The average percentages of absent flagella and coiled flagella were 21.7% ± 12.0% and 23.8% ± 19.3%, respectively, and the percentage of irregular-caliber flagella was 19.8% ± 16.5%. Sperm with bent flagella was very low, ranging from 0 to 6%, with an average of 2.6% ± 2.7%. For two men withSPEF2variants, only 4% and 2% of the sperm were normomorphic. The proportion distribution of abnormal flagella morphology was similar to that in theFSIP2-mutant group, but the more frequent abnormality types were the short, absent, and coiled malformations, with the average percentages being 35%, 30%, and 29%, respectively. Bent flagella and irregular caliber accounted for only 6% and 5%, respectively (Table3).

Subsequently, we performed immunofluorescence analysis to determine the impact of variants on FSIP2 protein expression and localization in patients 1, 4, 6, and 9 (Fig.2A). The FSIP2 staining results of normal control individual spermatozoa were consistent with those reported in the literature [16], with expression distributed throughout the flagella and head. However, in the patients, FSIP2 signal displayed as: (1) a significant reducement or absence through the whole sperm, (2) an even signal in the sperm head but no signal in the tail; (3) an enhanced signal in the sperm collar and no signal in the sperm tail. These conditions may be related to the differences of variation type. Loss-of-function variants such as frameshift and nonsense variation product truncated proteins, affecting the protein expression level. While missense variation may cause protein conformational changes and affect protein interaction resulting in protein error-localization. This protein alteration provides evidence beyond the genome level that biallelicFSIP2variants might contribute to the MMAF phenotype in these patients. The other 7 patients had insufficient sperm samples to undergo protein testing.

For the four missense variants of the FSIP2 gene c.3351G > A(p.Met1117Ile), c.4345G > A(p.Gly1449Arg), c.488C > G(p.Ala163Gly), and c.10726G > C(p.Ala3576Pro) identified in patients 7 and 8, the structures of the wild-type and variant proteins were predicted using AlphaFold (https://deepmind.google/technologies/alphafold/). Notable changes in the interaction forces between amino acids and local conformational alterations were observed for these variants (Fig.S1A), potentially affecting the protein’s stability, hydrophobicity, and flexibility, which could have functional implications.

Owing to sperm unavailability for SPEF2-mutant patients, the effects of missense variants c.578 T > C(p.I193T) and c.3368G > A(p.R1123Q) were confirmed by transient transfection on HEK293T. The expression and localization of mutant protein were not different from that of wild-type SPEF2 (Fig.S1B, 2B). Considering in situ protein in spermatozoa mainly contribution to tail elongation, we further measured the diameter of cells expressing exogenous SPEF2, to determine if cell morphological extension during growth was affected. Compared to wild-type cells, a shorter diameter was observed from mutant cells. The mean diameter of wild-type vs I193T and R1123Q was 320.15, 220.29, and 210.71 respectively (Fig.2B,C).

As shown in Table4, 11 patients underwent a total of 12 ICSI cycles and 2 IVF cycles, patient 3 received only IVF, and patient 4 underwent an IVF cycle and an ICSI cycle. The rates of two-pronuclear (2PN) and good-quality embryos among patients who had aFSIP2mutation were 81.1% (107/132) and 74.8% (80/107), respectively, and these rates were 76.5% (26/34) and 73.0% (19/26), respectively, among patients withSPEF2mutations. Regarding pregnancy outcomes, the clinical pregnancy rate per embryo transfer was 42.9% (12/28) in theFSIP2mutation group and 57.1% (4/7) in theSPEF2mutation group. To date, 5 babies have been sired by 3 patients with MMAF carryingFSIP2variants, and one patient carrying aSPEF2variant has sired 2 babies via two ICSI cycles.

In the present study, nineteen novel variants ofFSIP2andSPEF2were identified in eleven patients with MMAF. Previous studies have reported that the frequency of biallelic FSIP2 mutations to be 2.9% (3/105) in patients with oligoasthenozoospermia [17], 5% (2/40) in Chinese men with MMAF [18], and 5.1% (4/78) in North African patients exhibiting the typical MMAF phenotype [19]. Our findings revealed a higher prevalence ofFSIP2variants (8.5%), with loss-of-function (LOF) variants accounting for 3.8% (4/106) and missense variants representing 4.7% (5/106) of cases. RegardingSPEF2mutations, the diagnostic positive rate in our study was 1.9%, which is lower than previously reported in Han Chinese MMAF patients (4% [2/50] [20], 4.8% [2/42] [21], and 12.8% [6/47] [22]). These discrepancies may be attributed to regional variations among patient populations, as well as the relatively small sample size, which can cause fluctuations in detection frequencies. Furthermore, while prior reports have focused exclusively on LOF mutations, our study included both LOF and highly suspected pathogenic missense variants, given the incomplete understanding of the phenotypic consequences ofFSIP2andSPEF2variants.

Functional analysis of two novel missense variants of SPEF2 demonstrated a mild impact on morphological extension in a transfected cell model. Under normal conditions, SPEF2 protein is predominantly localized in the tails of elongating spermatids and mature sperm. It plays a crucial role in sperm differentiation by transporting tail proteins from the Golgi complex to the elongating sperm tail, and in the structuring of the sperm tail via intramanchette and intraflagellar transport mechanisms [23]. There was no flagella formation in HEK293T cells, but cell growth involves substances delivering and the extension of cytoskeleton structure in the cytoplasm. These cells exhibited alterations in cell diameter, likely reflecting impaired cargo protein transport due to SPEF2 mutations, thereby affecting cell growth and extension.

FSIP2 is thought to be involved in mitochondrial sheath assembly and sperm flagellar formation, particularly during sperm metamorphosis. In our study, patients displayed severe abnormalities in sperm tail structure. However, in contrast to previous reports [17] where sperm concentration was typically normal, seven out of nine patients withFSIP2variants had sperm concentrations below the reference value of (15.0 × 106/mL). This suggests thatFSIP2variants may not only affect sperm tail structure but could also disrupt spermatogenesis, in addition to their role in sperm morphology.

Reproductive outcomes following ICSI involving at least 15 genes associated with MMAF have been reported in several studies (Table5), although the number of cases per gene remains limited, with fewer than five cases for most genes, except forDNAH1andDNAH17. Despite the small sample sizes, average fertilization rates were relatively high at 66.8% (460/689), though pregnancy rates varied widely, with an average of 39.0% (57/146), ranging from 0 to 100%. These findings suggest that while MMAF-associated genes do not appear to significantly affect fertilization, their roles in embryo development are more variable, influencing pregnancy outcomes. For example, deficiencies in centrosomal proteins, such asCEP135, may impair microtubule and spindle assembly in fertilized ovum, leading to poor ICSI outcomes. Therefore, determining the genetic etiology of MMAF is crucial for predicting the prognosis of assisted reproductive technologies (ART).

ForFSIP2andSPEF2, only five cases have been reported in the literature (Table5). In the present study, we documented a total of 14 ART cycles involving 11 patients. Both fertilization and pregnancy rates exceeded 70%, indicating favorable embryogenesis. These results suggest that FSIP2 and SPEF2 primarily influence spermatogenesis and sperm metamorphosis, affecting sperm concentration and motility, with minimal impact on early embryonic development. Notably, ten of the eleven patients in our cohort achieved pregnancy through ICSI or in vitro fertilization (IVF), with the exception of the wife of patient 1, who failed to conceive, likely due to her underlying health conditions, including obesity, a uterine leiomyoma, and endometritis. Overall, these findings indicate that male infertility resulting from FSIP2 and SPEF2 mutations can be effectively managed through ART.

Among the MMAF patients in our study, patients 3 and 4 achieved successful births via IVF, with less severe reproductive symptoms, potentially due to their genotypes. Patient 3 carried a complex heterozygous genotype with two missense variants, and patient 4 had a combination of a frameshift mutation and a missense variant. We hypothesized that these variations may not fully impair protein function, allowing for the presence of sufficient normal sperm for successful IVF. In addition, patients 3, 8, and 9 experienced secondary infertility, and we suggest that the progression of MMAF symptoms, exacerbated by age, may lead to a gradual decline in sperm motility and/or accompanying a decrease in spermatogenesis.

This study acknowledges several limitations that should be considered. First, due to constraints in sample availability, we were only able to obtain sufficient semen samples from four out of the eleven individuals with mutations, which limited our ability to observe protein alterations in vivo. Additionally, due to the unique characteristics of the FSIP2 gene, conducting further functional experiments in vitro proved to be highly challenging. As a result, for the missense variants identified in these patients, the available functional evidence regarding their pathogenicity remains insufficient. Second, the small sample size of variant-positive patients restricts the ability to draw robust conclusions about genotype–phenotype correlations. Consequently, the findings may serve more as a reference for assisted reproductive treatment rather than providing definitive insights into the relationship between genotype and phenotype.

In this study, we identified novel variants in the FSIP2 and SPEF2 genes and tracked the reproductive outcomes of MMAF patients carrying these variations. The missense variants identified may be associated with milder forms of sperm impairment. Our findings suggest that intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) is an effective treatment for male infertility caused by mutations in FSIP2 and SPEF2. In cases where sperm concentration is normal and sperm motility is only mildly impaired, in vitro fertilization (IVF) could be considered an initial approach. This work expands the spectrum of known variants and clinical ART outcomes for MMAF patients with FSIP2 and SPEF2 mutations, offering clues on genotype/phenotype correlations in MMAF patients. The information presented in this study may contribute to more accurate estimations of the prevalence ofFSIP2andSPEF2variants, as well as help in predicting ART outcomes, thereby benefiting genetic counseling and reproductive counseling for MMAF patients.
