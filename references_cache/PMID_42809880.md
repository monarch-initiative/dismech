# [Hereditary folate malabsorption due to an SLC46A1 mutation with cerebral folate deficiency and refractory epilepsy: first case reported in Argentina].
**Authors:** Grosso CL, Peralta LV, Paviolo M, Pautasso J, Guelbert GA, Bovina Martijena MDP, Laróvere LE
**Journal:** Rev Fac Cien Med Univ Nac Cordoba (2026)
**DOI:** [10.31053/1853.0605.v83.n3.50324](https://doi.org/10.31053/1853.0605.v83.n3.50324)

## Content

Hereditary folate malabsorption (HFM) is an autosomal recessive metabolic disorder caused by pathogenic variants in SLC46A1, the gene encoding the proton-coupled folate transporter. This defect impairs intestinal folate absorption and its transport into the central nervous system (CNS), leading to cerebral folate deficiency even with normal serum levels. Clinically, it presents with megaloblastic anemia, gastrointestinal symptoms, immunological alterations, and progressive neurological involvement. Measurement of 5-methyltetrahydrofolate (5-MTHF) in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is the specific diagnostic method, and treatment is based on folinic acid or levofolinic acid administration. Case: A male patient with early-onset microangiopathic anemia, developmental delay, refractory epilepsy, and hyperhomocysteinemia. Whole-exome sequencing identified a homozygous pathogenic variant in the SLC46A1 gene, c.981_982del (p.Tyr327Ter). Upon diagnosis, folic acid was discontinued and treatment with folinic acid was initiated, later replaced by intramuscular levofolinic acid due to clinical deterioration and decreased CSF 5-MTHF levels. The multimodal therapeutic approach achieved normalization of biomarkers and control of refractory epilepsy. This is the first report of an Argentine patient with HFM due to an SLC46A1 mutation associated with cerebral folate deficiency. Early-onset refractory epilepsy and hyperhomocysteinemia were key diagnostic clues. Monitoring of CSF 5-MTHF proved essential for assessing therapeutic response. Levofolinic acid showed superior clinical and biochemical efficacy compared to leucovorin. This case broadens the genotypic and phenotypic spectrum of this rare disease and underscores the importance of early diagnosis and individualized management to optimize neurological outcomes.

arterial. También hay trabajos que hallaron que una baja ingesta de calcio durante el embarazo
influye en la presión arterial de la descendencia.

La hipertensión tiene una alta prevalencia a nivel mundial. Una adecuada ingesta de calcio se
encuentra asociada a menores valores de presión arterial. La OMS recomienda la suplementación con
calcio durante el embarazo para reducir los trastornos hipertensivos del embarazo. Se ha observado
que la descendencia de madres suplementadas con calcio durante el embarazo presenta menores valores
de presión arterial. Aún permanece desconocido el mecanismo por el cual se produce este efecto. El
trabajo presenta los modelos en los cuales se ha estudiado el efecto del calcio dietario en la
presión arterial, buscando cuál es el modelo más adecuado para estudiar estos mecanismos subyacentes
a la hipertensión.


Globally, arterial hypertension represents the greatest risk factor in terms of years of life lost due to
disability and death, leading to significant costs in medical care. Preventing arterial hypertension is
one of the most important strategies to reduce cardiovascular diseases incidence and burden, the leading
global cause of death.
(1)

However, the precise mechanisms underlying the development of hypertension have yet to be fully
elucidated. Therefore, clarifying these underlying mechanisms is clinically essential to the development
of new therapeutic strategies. (2,
3) It is known that the different aetiologies of hypertension are the product of dynamic
interactions between genetics and environmental elements, leading to the alteration of biological
pathways and ultimately to hypertension. (4,
5) A large genetic association study involving one million patients identified 535 new
genetic loci related to blood pressure, demonstrating that hypertension has a multigenic nature.
(6)

Currently, there is substantial epidemiological evidence that intrauterine stress can "program" the
later development of the disease. Several animal studies have linked maternal nutritional status from
conception to weaning with persistent changes in cardiovascular and offspring metabolic function. (7, 8)


Several studies have found an inverse association between calcium (Ca) intake and blood pressure in rats,
young individuals
(9)

and pregnant women.
(10)

Moreover, Ca supplements administration during pregnancy has been shown to be associated with a
significant reduction in the incidence of preeclampsia, especially in communities with a calcium
deficient diet. (10-12)


The mechanism by which low Ca intake produces an increase in blood pressure appears to be mediated by the
interaction between calciotropic hormones and blood pressure regulators. Low Ca intake stimulates
parathormone (PTH) secretion and increases the conversion of vitamin D into its active form
(calcitriol). Both PTH and calcitriol raise intracellular calcium concentration, leading to the
contraction of vascular smooth muscle cells and resulting in vasoconstriction. On the other hand, renin
release is stimulated by both low Ca intake and PTH. Additionally, PTH increases the synthesis of
angiotensin II and aldosterone, which also leads to vasoconstriction and increases renal water
reabsorption, thereby elevating blood pressure.
(13)

However, the mechanism by Ca intake reduces the preeclampsia development is unknown.


Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that low Ca intake during pregnancy results in offspring’s blood
pressure increase in both animal models and humans. (14-16) A
randomized clinical trial evaluated the effects of Ca supplements during pregnancy
(10)

on blood pressure of children of 7-year-old. This study showed that offspring of mothers who received Ca
supplementation had a lower incidence of high blood pressure than the offspring of mothers who received
placebo.
(17)

This same effect was observed in other clinical trials and in laboratory animals.
(16,18–21)

Rats whose mothers had Ca deficiency during pregnancy developed higher blood pressure during adulthood.
(16)

This observation is supported by epidemiological studies linking adverse foetal environment to an
increased risk of hypertension in adults (Barker Hypothesis), (22, 23) and recent animal studies showing that hypertension can
also be determined during foetal life by maternal protein restriction.
(24)

Animal models fed with a protein-deficient diet during pregnancy have been widely used to study the
mechanisms involved in this association, finding changes in gene expression.
(25)

However, the effect of maternal Ca intake on the development of high blood pressure in the progeny has
been poorly studied.


Therefore, the aim of this review is to elucidate the most suitable normotensive rat strain to carry out
studies to perform research on the mechanisms involved in the effect of low maternal Ca intake during
pregnancy on the offspring blood pressure in adulthood.


The literature was searched on March and repeated on December 2023 and September 2024, using the
databases of MEDLINE (National Library of Medicine) and Scopus. Blood pressure, calcium intake, calcium
diet, rats, and offspring were used as keys terms.


The first search was done for “blood pressure” AND “calcium intake” OR “calcium diet” AND rats found 396
articles. A second search was performed in MEDLINE and Scopus with the following criteria: "blood
pressure" AND ("Ca intake" OR “calcium diet”) AND rats AND offspring, eight studies were found.


A total of 404 papers were found and screened. After removing duplicated ones, 338 articles were selected
from which 166 did not evaluate calcium effects on blood pressure, 21 were reviews, 11 were carried out
in humans and 82 in hypertensive rats. The studies carried out with strains of normotensive rats were
selected for full text screening (n=57). 6 studies were discarded because details of the experimental
design were not available (three are written in Japanese, one is written in Chinese and two did not
report the strain of rat used). 13 studies were discarded because blood pressure was not reported (n=10)
or calcium was not administered in diet (n=3). Finally, Thirty Eight studies were selected (see
Figura 1)

38 studies evaluating the effect of Ca on blood pressure of normotensive rats were selected: 13 of them
were carried out in Wistar rats, 14 in Sprague Dawley rats and 10 in Wistar Kyoto rats. All of them are
models of healthy rats. Only one study uses other strains (Fischer 344, Wistar Furth or ACI rats) all of
them are strains for same specific pathologies; they are not healthy rats.


The inverse association of Calcium intake and blood pressure was widely reported using different calcium
content (from 0 to 4% of Ca) and time of administration (13±10, from 4 to 42 weeks). In Wistar rats, a
significant reduction of blood pressure in the groups with higher calcium intake was shown in 9 out of
the 13 studies, 10 out of the 14 in Sprague Dawley and 4 out of the 5 in Wistar Kyoto. The study done by
Huie et al
(26)

showed that a calcium restricted diet increases blood pressure in Fischer 344 rats but not in Wistar
Furth or ACI rats. However, there are some studies that show a contradictory calcium effect on blood
pressure.
(27–29)

Bukoski et al did not find an inverse relationship between calcium intake and blood pressure in the
Wistar Kyoto rats.
(27)

The other studies by Takaya et al. reported different effects of calcium on blood pressure in the
offspring of mothers who received diets with varying calcium content during pregnancy and lactation, in
a sex-dependent manner.

The effect of a low calcium intake during pregnancy on blood pressure of the offspring has been reported
several times. In humans, observational studies (19-21) and
clinical trials (17,
18, 32) have been reported. Two meta-analyses conclude
that a low calcium intake during pregnancy predisposes offspring to higher blood pressure values. (33, 34) A recent study
showed that calcium supplementation during pregnancy may lower systolic blood pressure in childhood and
adolescence.
(32)

However, no studies have elucidated the underlying mechanism.


Recent evidence suggests that exposure to certain environmental conditions during pregnancy may have
significant effects on the development of susceptibility to chronic diseases in adulthood.
(8)

Epigenetic changes triggered in the foetal genome during pregnancy development appear to be one of the
possible mechanisms for such effects beyond genomic changes. Among environmental factors, it is
highlighted that maternal nutrition can induce persistent changes in the genome/epigenome of the
offspring that may predispose them to potential diseases and risks in adulthood. (
7, 35)


Maternal Ca deficiency during pregnancy can cause epigenetic changes that affect the regulation of gene
expression and lead to different metabolic phenotypes in the offspring, such as insulin resistance and
changes in lipid metabolism. (30, 36) In
addition, Ca intake in pregnant women may be inversely related to high BP in their progeny.
(37)

A low Ca prenatal diet is associated with higher BP in offspring. It is possible that the action of
calcium-regulating hormones exerts a foetal programming of blood pressure.
(15)



Interaction between hormones regulating phosphocalcic metabolism and controlling blood pressure are
proposed as the mechanism by which Ca intake regulates blood pressure in an adult individual. Such
changes involve an increase in intracellular calcium in arteriolar muscles resulting in vasoconstriction
and increased blood pressure. There is also evidence of a bidirectional relationship between calcium
regulatory hormones and renin angiotensin aldosterone system
(38)

and epigenetic changes associated with hypertension have been shown too.
(39)

Therefore, there is sufficient information to suggest that a low calcium intake during pregnancy could
produce epigenetic alterations that modify the expression of genes linked to phosphocalcic metabolism
and/or blood pressure control leading to higher blood pressure in adult life of the offsprings. In this
regard, as shown in this review, there are only five studies carried out with experimental animals that
research the effects of maternal calcium intake during pregnancy on the blood pressure of offspring.
However, none of them explore the mechanisms involved.
(16,28–31)

Consequently, similar studies on the effect of maternal calcium intake and offspring blood pressure are
needed to validate previous findings
(16)

and to explore the mechanisms involved.


In this review, we explore the rat strains useful for studying the mechanisms involved in the prenatal
programming of offspring blood pressure induced by maternal calcium intake. We found that only Wistar
strain or its variant, Wistar Kyoto, have been used to study the effects of maternal Ca intake during
pregnancy; and no study of this type using Sprague Dawley rats was found. The Wistar or Sprague Dawley
strains are widely used in basic research. Both strains are considered multipurpose models of healthy
rats. (40, 41) The Wistar
Kyoto rat is a strain of Wistar rats from Kyoto School of Medicine, belonging to the same group in which
the spontaneously hypertensive rat (SHR) strain was developed.
(42)

Only one study uses other strains: Wistar Furth (a rat strain used to study macrothrombocytopaenia,
oncology, carcinogenesis, leukaemia and grand mal seizure),
(43)

Fischer 344 (widely used in gerontology and in carcinogenicity testing programs),
(44)

and the ACI (an animal model for congenital genitourinary anomalies).
(26)



The results of the analysed studies indicate that Wistar, Sprague Dawley and Wistar Kyoto strains are
suitable models for studying the effects of Ca intake on blood pressure in normotensive rats. Most
studies show an inverse relationship between Ca intake and blood pressure. However, some studies report
contradictory effects of Ca intake on blood pressure. (27-29)


For instance, Bukoski et al. did not find an inverse relationship between Ca intake and blood pressure in
Wistar Kyoto rats.
(27)

The authors suggest that this discrepancy compared to other reports could be due to basic nutrient
composition of the diet used in their study. Specifically, the diet did not adhere to AIN guidelines for
sodium and potassium content, both of which affect blood pressure. (
45, 46) Other studies by Takaya et al reported the
effect of maternal Ca intake on offspring blood pressure, similar to the findings of Bergel and Belizan,
but with different results in similar models. Bergel and Belizan
(16)

used Wistar Kyoto rats and found higher blood pressure values in offspring of mothers with lower Ca
intake (0%) compared to those with normal Ca intake (0.5%). Conversely, the studies of Takaya et al
(28-31) using Wistar rats did not find an effect of maternal Ca
intake on blood pressure in the first generation. However, in the second generation, offspring of
mothers with low Ca intake showed a reduction in blood pressure values. Although the results of Bergel
and Takaya seem contradictory, it is important to note that several differences in the experimental
designs between the studies. These include the Ca concentration and the treatment duration, as well as
the age at which blood pressure was measured in the offspring. Therefore, we could conclude that the
Wistar Kyoto strain seems to be more suitable for studying the mechanism involved in the relationship
between maternal Ca intake and offspring blood pressure.
