BACKGROUND: Although video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) thymectomy has been 
widely adopted for thymoma resection, its comparative effectiveness versus open 
approaches (thoracotomy and sternotomy) remains controversial. This study aimed 
to comprehensively evaluate perioperative and long-term outcomes among these 
surgical modalities.
METHODS: In this retrospective cohort study, 317 consecutive patients with 
pathologically confirmed thymoma underwent surgical resection at Beijing Chest 
Hospital (2005-2024). Patients were stratified by approach: VATS (n = 178), 
unilateral thoracotomy (n = 73), and median sternotomy (n = 66). Propensity 
score weighting (PSW) was applied to balance baseline covariates. Primary 
endpoints included intraoperative metrics (blood loss, operative time), 
postoperative recovery (chest tube duration, hospital stay), and oncologic 
outcomes (recurrence-free survival [RFS], overall survival [OS]).
RESULTS: Intraoperative Outcomes: Sternotomy demonstrated significantly longer 
operative time (median 138.0 [IQR 120-180] vs. 100.0 [75.4-150.7] vs. 120.0 
[90-150] minutes; P = 0.008) and greater blood loss (median 291.5 [200-403] vs. 
100.0 [50-200] vs. 50.0 [50-100] mL; P < 0.001) compared to thoracotomy and 
VATS, respectively. Postoperative Outcomes: Sternotomy required higher 
postoperative pleural drainage volume (median 848.4 [620-1524.7] vs. 632.9 
[450-1020] vs. 540.0 [340-742.2] ml; P = 0.002) and extended hospitalization 
(median 14.0 [11-19.5] vs. 9.16 [8-14] vs. 9.0 [7-13] days; P < 0.001). Survival 
Analysis: Multivariable Cox models revealed no statistically significant 
differences were observed in OS (thoracotomy vs. VATS: HR = 2.44, 95% CI 
0.70-8.42, P = 0.158; sternotomy vs. VATS: HR = 1.89, 95% CI 0.45-7.48, 
P = 0.361) or RFS (thoracotomy HR = 0.56, 95% CI 0.16-1.94; sternotomy 
HR = 0.74, 95% CI 0.16-3.37) across approaches (all P > 0.05). Advanced 
Masaoka-Koga stage (III/IV) independently predicted worse OS (HR = 2.88, 95% CI 
1.13-7.33, P = 0.027).
CONCLUSION: Preliminary evidence showed that VATS provided short-term superior 
perioperative outcomes with significantly less blood loss and shorter hospital 
stays compared to sternotomy, while maintaining comparable long-term survival 
across surgical approaches. Given the retrospective nature and methodological 
limitations of this study, these observations require rigorous confirmation 
through prospective multicenter studies to establish evidence-based guidelines 
for surgical approach selection.

Thymoma is a rare mediastinal tumor with a low incidence, accounting for less than 1% of adult malignancies [1,2,3]. Surgical resection remains the primary treatment, effectively controlling disease progression and improving patient survival [4,5]. In clinical practice, thymoma resection can be performed via median sternotomy, thoracotomy, or video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). Median sternotomy is preferred for complex or large tumors but is associated with prolonged recovery and higher complication rates [6,7]. Lateral thoracotomy, typically indicated for unilateral tumors, offers intermediate invasiveness between sternotomy and VATS [8]. In recent years, VATS has gained increasing attention due to its advantages in faster recovery, fewer complications, and superior cosmetic outcomes [9,10,11].

The optimal surgical approach for thymoma remains controversial [11,12,13]. Although median sternotomy is considered the “gold standard” for its wide surgical exposure, its higher invasiveness and longer recovery period limit its universal applicability [13]. VATS, while minimally invasive, is often reserved for smaller tumors and early-stage disease due to concerns about oncologic adequacy [12]. Importantly, existing comparative studies frequently fail to account for baseline confounders, potentially leading to biased conclusions.

To address these limitations, this study is the first to employ propensity score weighting (PSW) to balance intergroup disparities, enabling a robust comparison of perioperative and survival outcomes. Our key findings include: Minimally invasive advantage: VATS significantly reduced intraoperative blood loss and hospital stay duration without increasing complication rates.Oncologic equivalence: Multivariable Cox regression models confirmed no significant association between surgical approach and overall survival (OS) or recurrence-free survival (RFS). Instead, advanced tumor stage (III/IV) independently predicted worse OS. As the first propensity-weighted analysis of 317 thymectomies (2005–2024), this study uniquely evaluates: PSW-adjusted survival outcomes (RFS/OS) across different surgical approaches; The dominant prognostic role of tumor stage over surgical technique.

This study collected data from the electronic medical records of patients who underwent thymoma resection at Beijing Chest Hospital from December 2005 to August 2024. All patients were pathologically confirmed to have thymoma and were grouped by surgical approach: median sternotomy, thoracotomy, and VATS.

Inclusion criteria: Age ≥ 18 to < 75 years; pathologically confirmed thymoma; stable control of comorbidities, with surgical tolerance assessed by physicians; complete tumor resection (R0); complete medical records; and complete follow-up data.

Exclusion criteria: Pregnant or lactating women; uncontrolled comorbidities with significant impairment of heart, liver, lung, or kidney function; pathologically confirmed thymic carcinoma; poor compliance or mental disorders preventing follow-up.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chest Hospital (Ethics Approval Number: 2019, Clinical No. 85).

Propensity Score Weighting (PSW) is a statistical method used in observational studies to reduce confounding bias. The propensity score is defined as the probability of an individual receiving a specific treatment or exposure given the covariates. PSW assigns weights to each individual to balance the distribution of covariates between the treatment and control groups, thereby simulating the effect of a randomized controlled trial.

Overall Survival (OS): Time from surgery to death from any cause. Living patients were censored at the last verified contact (documented clinic visit or telephone follow-up before March 31, 2024).

Recurrence-Free Survival (RFS): Time from surgery to the first event among: (a) histologically/cytologically confirmed recurrence, or (b) radiological progression (RECIST v1.1). Patients without events were censored at the last disease assessment.

R0 Resection: No residual cancer cells are found microscopically at the surgical margins, and no macroscopic or microscopic cancer residue is observed, indicating complete tumor removal. R1 Resection (Microscopic Residue): All gross lesions are removed, but microscopic cancer cells are present at the surgical margins. R2 Resection (Macroscopic Residue): Grossly visible residual tumor is present at the surgical margins.

Baseline Characteristics: Patient baseline characteristics were collected, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), history of smoking (past or present), maximum tumor diameter, comorbidities, Osserman MG staging for myasthenia gravis (MG) [14], Masaoka staging (I, II, III, and partial IV) [15], and whether patients with locally advanced disease received neoadjuvant therapy (including preoperative radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy). Preoperative auxiliary examinations included imaging studies, pulmonary function tests, and laboratory tests.

Surgical Data: We collected surgical data, including the approach, intraoperative blood loss, operative time, whether the phrenic nerve was resected, whether vascular replacement was performed, whether adipose tissue was cleared, and whether the pericardium and lung tissue were resected.

Postoperative Data: Postoperative-related data included the duration of postoperative drainage, length of hospital stay, and postoperative complications.

Follow-Up: Follow-up was conducted from December 2005 to March 2024 by trained healthcare professionals through face-to-face or telephone interviews with thymoma surgery patients. Three senior attending physicians independently verified primary outcomes, including OS, RFS, and MG recurrence requiring rehospitalization.

The overall goal of this study was to adopt standardized data entry, verification, follow-up protocols, training programs, and follow-up methods. The follow-up plan and content were reviewed and validated by senior thoracic surgeons and supervised by general practitioners who underwent rigorous training.

Patients with histopathologically diagnosed thymoma were divided into three groups based on the surgical approach. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. whereas non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were summarized as numbers (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test. Perioperative outcomes, including operative time, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative complications, duration of chest drainage, and length of hospital stay, were compared among the three groups. Survival curves were generated by Kaplan-Meier method and compared via log-rank test.

To reduce baseline imbalance among the three groups, propensity score weighting was performed. Propensity scores were estimated using a multinomial logistic regression model that included baseline covariates potentially related to treatment selection and prognosis. Four weighting approaches were evaluated, including inverse probability weighting, entropy weighting, overlap weighting, and treatment weights. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values below 0.1 considered acceptable. Among the candidate approaches, overlap weighting achieved the best overall covariate balance and was therefore used in the primary analysis. The comparison of balance across weighting methods is shown in Supplementary Tables 1 and Figure S1. Overlap weighting without truncation or stabilization was selected for two reasons: (1) it achieved the best overall covariate balance, and (2) it reduced the influence of extreme propensity scores. The corresponding estimand is the average treatment effect in the overlap population (ATO). Weighted survival analyses were then performed using the selected weights. In the overlap-weighted analysis, weights were normalized so that the total weight within each treatment group equaled one; therefore, the reported weighted sample sizes represent normalized pseudo-sample sizes rather than actual patient counts. Kaplan–Meier curves were estimated in the weighted sample, and long-term outcomes were compared using weighted Cox proportional hazards models. Because several variables remained slightly imbalanced after weighting (Table2), an additional weighted Cox model further adjusted for covariates with residual imbalance (Post-weighting SMDs: gender [0.102], smoking history [0.118], tumor size [0.134], Masaoka stage [0.114], and DLCO% predicted [0.108]). We further applied doubly robust estimation by including these variables in the weighted Cox model (Lunceford & Davidian 2004), including sex, smoking history, tumor size, Masaoka stage, and DLCO% predicted. Thus, the primary inference was based not only on the weighted model but also on this residual-adjusted model. As a sensitivity analysis, conventional multivariable Cox regression without weighting was also performed. Due to the limited number of outcome events, the final multivariable model included only covariates clinically relevant to thymoma prognosis. All analyses were conducted using R software version 4.4.1, mainly with the PSweight, cobalt, survey, and survival packages. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 317 thymoma patients were included, including 178 treated with VATS, 73 with latero-thoracotomy, and 66 with median sternotomy. Before weighting, baseline characteristics differed across surgical approaches. Compared with the VATS and latero-thoracotomy groups, patients in the median sternotomy group were younger, more often male, had fewer concomitant diseases, larger tumors, a higher prevalence of MG, more frequent preoperative radiotherapy, more advanced Masaoka stage, more frequent organ invasion, more vascular replacement, and different WHO histologic distributions and surgical periods. Preoperative pulmonary function also differed, whereas BMI, hemoglobin, albumin, and CRP were comparable among groups. Operative time, blood loss, phrenic nerve sacrifice, and vascular replacement also differed significantly before weighting, (Table1). VATS, Lateral thoracotomy DLCO% predicted VATS Lateral thoracotomy.

After propensity score weighting, baseline covariates were well balanced across the three groups overall, with most SMDs below 0.1 and no material differences in weighted distributions of demographic, tumor-related, pulmonary function, WHO histology, organ invasion, or surgical period variables, (Table2and Fig.1). However, several variables, including sex, smoking history, tumor size, Masaoka stage, and DLCO% predicted, remained slightly above or close to 0.1, and were therefore additionally adjusted for in the residual-adjusted weighted Cox models. The high consistency between the primary and residual-adjusted models confirms that these minor imbalances did not materially affect the validity of our survival conclusions.

In the weighted analysis, median sternotomy showed significantly greater blood loss (median 291.5 vs. 100.0 vs. 50.0 mL,P< 0.001), longer operative time (P= 0.008), longer thoracic drainage duration(P= 0.002), and longer postoperative hospital stay(P< 0.001) than the other two approaches. In contrast, 30-day mortality and postoperative complication rates were not significantly different among the three groups(P= 0.193), (Table3).

For long-term outcomes, no significant differences in overall survival were observed among the three approaches in any of the three Cox models. In the PS-weighted model, compared with VATS, the HR was 2.44 (95% CI 0.70–8.42) for latero-thoracotomy and 1.89 (95% CI 0.45–7.48) for median sternotomy. Results were similar after residual adjustment and in the conventional multivariable model, (Table4and Fig.2). Likewise, recurrence-free survival did not differ significantly by surgical approach. In the PS-weighted model, the HR was 0.56 (95% CI 0.16–1.94) for latero-thoracotomy and 0.74 (95% CI 0.16–3.37) for median sternotomy versus VATS, and the findings remained consistent in the residual-adjusted and unweighted multivariable models (Table5and Fig.2).

Thymoma resection can effectively control disease progression and improve patient survival rates, but it may also lead to significant postoperative complications in thymoma patients [16,17,18]. The choice of surgical approach should be based on tumor size, location, the patient’s overall health status, and the surgeon’s experience. A precise and individualized surgical plan can enhance surgical safety and improve long-term survival in tumor patients [19,20,21]. This study analyzed 317 thymoma patients to explore the differences in clinical characteristics and surgical outcomes among three surgical approaches (VATS, lateral thoracotomy, and median sternotomy). Our results showed that there were differences in clinical characteristics among patients undergoing the three common surgical approaches for thymoma resection. After balancing baseline clinical characteristics using Propensity Score Weighting (PSW). No significant differences in long-term survival or recurrence were observed among the three groups (allP> 0.05 by log-rank test). However, the median sternotomy group had higher intraoperative blood loss, longer operative time, a higher proportion of vascular replacement, and increased duration of chest drainage and postoperative hospital stay.

Differences were observed among the surgical approaches in terms of gender ratio, age, proportion of comorbidities, maximum tumor diameter, MG proportion, preoperative radiotherapy, and modified Masaoka staging. Compared to the VATS and Lateral thoracotomy groups, the median sternotomy group had younger patients with fewer comorbidities, and a higher proportion of males, larger maximum tumor diameter, MG, and preoperative radiotherapy. This reflects real-world clinical practice, where surgeons tailor surgical approaches to patient-specific characteristics. Wang GW et al. reported similar findings [6]. Due to the significant differences and specific indications among patients undergoing different surgical approaches, it is challenging to conduct randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in clinical practice. This study used PSW analysis based on case registry data to simulate an RCT and compare the short- and long-term efficacy of the three surgical approaches.

Our results showed that after PSW adjustment of baseline characteristics, the sternotomy group had significant differences in operative time, intraoperative blood loss, intraoperative phrenic nerve sacrifice, and vascular replacement compared to the VATS and Lateral thoracotomy groups. This may be related to the complexity and invasiveness of the sternotomy procedure [22]. sternotomy requires splitting the sternum, providing a larger surgical field but also increasing surgical trauma and bleeding risk. Additionally, the complexity of lesions in sternotomy patients may explain the higher proportion of vascular replacement and phrenic nerve sacrifice. Similar findings were reported in our previous studies [8,23].

The sternotomy group had significantly longer chest drainage duration (median: 848.4 vs. 632.9 vs. 540.0 min;P= 0.002) and postoperative hospital stay (median 14.0 vs. 9.16 vs. 9.0 days;P< 0.001), likely due to greater surgical trauma despite its wider operative exposure and may increase the risk of complications and healthcare resource utilization. In contrast, minimally invasive surgeries are associated with less trauma, faster recovery, and better short-term outcomes [24].

Notably, the comparable long-term outcomes across approaches suggest that achieving R0 resection—rather than the surgical modality itself—is the critical prognostic factor for thymoma. This aligns with prior studies emphasizing the importance of complete tumor excision [25,26,27,28]. However, the higher perioperative morbidity associated with median sternotomy (e.g., blood loss, hospital stay) warrants careful risk-benefit evaluation, particularly in patients with borderline resectability.

This study found no differences in mortality or recurrence risk among the three surgical approaches. We believe this may be related to three factors. First, the biological characteristics of thymoma may be the key determinant of long-term prognosis. Thymomas typically grow slowly, and most are low-grade malignancies, with the completeness of surgical resection being crucial for prognosis [25,26,27,28]. As long as R0 resection is achieved, long-term survival and recurrence risk may be similar regardless of the surgical approach. Second, factors such as pathological staging, histological type, and the presence of MG may have a greater impact on long-term prognosis than the surgical approach itself [29,30,31,32]. Additionally, this suggests that the choice of surgical approach for thymoma should be based on individualized patient needs and intraoperative safety rather than solely aiming to improve long-term survival [6]. However, further research, particularly multicenter, large-sample prospective studies, is needed to validate these findings and comprehensively evaluate the impact of different surgical approaches on long-term outcomes. We acknowledge that overlap weighting did not completely eliminate imbalance for all baseline covariates, leaving minor variations in factors such as tumor size, smoking history, gender, Masaoka stage, and DLCO% predicted. However, additional residual-adjusted weighted Cox models and conventional multivariable Cox analyses yielded results highly consistent with the primary weighted analysis, supporting the robustness of the conclusion that surgical approach was not independently associated with OS or RFS.

This study has certain limitations. First, it is a single-center case registry study. However, the study includes nearly 20 years of thymoma patient cases from our center, making it a relatively large sample size study for this rare tumor type in the context of surgical treatment. Second, although PSW adjusted for some confounding factors, there may still be unmeasured confounding variables that were not corrected, potentially affecting the study results. Additionally, the completeness and accuracy of long-term follow-up data may also impact the reliability of the conclusions. Therefore, future larger-scale and longer-term prospective studies are needed to further validate these findings and explore the mechanisms underlying the impact of different surgical approaches on patient outcomes.

Our study contributes preliminary but mechanistically plausible evidence that VATS facilitates accelerated recovery pathways, demonstrating superior perioperative metrics without compromising 5-year survival. These findings align with evolving ERAS paradigms but require careful interpretation due to Residual confounding from non-randomized design; Institutional variability in postoperative care standardization༛Multicenter RCTs utilizing the STAND-VATS framework are urgently needed to: Establish procedure-specific enhanced recovery protocols. Validate cost-effectiveness thresholds. Define patient selection algorithms using machine learning.
