# Sporadic cone-rod dystrophy caused by a heterozygous RAB28 p.Ser23Phe pathogenic variant and a de novo 4p16.1p15.33 deletion.
**Authors:** Park J, Jang W, Ahn Y, Lee HJ
**Journal:** BMC Med Genomics (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12920-026-02402-w](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12920-026-02402-w)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Cone-rod dystrophy (CRD) is an inherited retinal dystrophy characterized by decreased visual acuity, color vision defects, and progressive loss of peripheral vision. RAB28-associated CRD is genetically homogeneous and results from pathogenic variants in the RAB28 gene, which is essential for photoreceptor function.

CASE PRESENTATION: Fundus examinations, optical coherence tomography, color vision tests, and full-field electroretinography (ERG) were performed in the proband. Genetic analysis, including clinical exome sequencing (CES), Sanger sequencing and chromosomal microarray (CMA), was conducted to determine the genetic cause in the proband. The patient exhibited significantly reduced visual acuity, severe color vision impairment, macular atrophy, and thinning of the photoreceptor layers. Full-field ERG revealed severely diminished cone responses, while rod responses were mildly reduced. Genetic analysis identified that the heterozygous RAB28 c.68 C > T/p.(Ser23Phe) pathogenic variant was inherited exclusively from the father. Additionally, a 3.4 Mb deletion on chromosome 4p16.1p15.33 was detected only in the proband. Four genes associated with Mendelian diseases, including CLNK (OMIM *611434), HS3ST1 (*603244), NKX3-2 (*602183), and RAB28 (*612994), are located within this deletion region.

CONCLUSIONS: This case illustrates a rare combination of a heterozygous RAB28 c.68 C > T/p.(Ser23Phe) pathogenic variant and a de novo 4p16.1p15.33 deletion, which is associated with AR CRD. Although extremely rare, bi-allelic RAB28 pathogenic variants can result from one inherited allele and a de novo deletion in the other.

Inherited retinal dystrophies (IRDs) are a diverse group of retinal degenerative conditions characterized by significant genetic variability. More than 200 genes have been linked to the different syndromic and nonsyndromic types of IRD [ 1 ]. Due to overlapping clinical symptoms among various IRD forms, accurately diagnosing them based solely on clinical features is challenging. Cone-rod dystrophy (CRD; ORPHA:1872) is a subtype of IRD characterized by early loss of cone photoreceptors and following loss of rod photoreceptors. Initial symptoms, typically presenting in early childhood, include decreased visual acuity, impaired color vision, and reduced central visual field sensitivity, which reflects the primary loss of cones. These are later followed by night blindness and peripheral visiual field loss due to rod impairment which in some cases may occur simultaneously with cone dysfunction [ 2 ]. In patients with CRD, fundus examination and optical coherence tomography often reveal characteristic changes, including foveal alterations, perifoveal outer retinal atrophy, and a “bull’s eye” pattern in the retina [ 3 ].

CRDs exhibit genetic heterogeneity and can be inherited in autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive (AR), or X-linked recessive patterns, with the AR form being the most common [ 4 ]. Among the limited number of genes identified, the RAB28 gene located on chromosome 4p15.33, was recently identified as being linked to AR cone-rod dystrophies (CRDs). As a member of the Ras-associated Rab small GTPase family, the protein encoded by this gene acts as a potential regulator of intracellular transport pathways. Rab GTPases act as molecular switches that regulate vesicle transport, vesicle budding, and membrane fission, playing a crucial role in vesicle trafficking between organelles. In Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans), the rab-28 protein is associated with an intraflagellar transport cargo via the Bardet-Biedl syndrome (BBS) cargo-adaptor protein complex, known as the BBSome [ 5 ]. Several cases of inherited AR CRDs caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous variants in RAB28 have been reported [ 6– 10 ]. First described in 2013 by Roosing et al., [ 6 ] only a few cases have been noted thereafter, with the majority of them involving European families. In 2017, Lee et al. reported a novel homozygous missense variant p.Ser23Phe in the RAB28 gene, the first identified case in an Asian individual [ 8 ].

In this report, we present a case of sporadic AR CRD caused by a heterozygous RAB28 pathogenic variant, p.Ser23Phe and 4p16.1p15.33 deletion. Singleton clinical exome sequencing (CES), followed by chromosomal microarray (CMA), enabled the identification of a heterozygous RAB28 pathogenic variant inherited from the father, along with an incidental 4p16.1p15.33 deletion in the proband, leading to the establishment of a genetic diagnosis.

A 7-year-old female patient was referred to the Department of Ophthalmology, Jeonbuk National University Hospital (Jeonju, Republic of Korea) due to decreased vision and intermittent exotropia. Her parents reported that the school had noticed her possible color vision deficiency. None of her family members including her parents and her younger brother had experienced any difficulties with vision or color recognition. Upon examination, her best-corrected visual acuity was 20/40 in both eyes. Color vision tests using the Hardy–Rand–Rittler (HRR) pseudoisochromatic plates demonstrated severe red–green color vision defect with mild-to-moderate blue–yellow defect. She also exhibited exotropia of 25 prism diopters. Fundus examination revealed macular atrophy and temporal pallor of the optic discs in both eyes. Fundus autofluorescence showed bull’s eye maculopathy with hyperfluorescent fovea. Spectral-domain optical coherence tomography displayed loss of photoreceptors, while sparing the peripheral retina (Fig. 1). Central macular thickness in optical coherence tomography was reduced to 108 μm in both eyes. Full-field electroretinography showed markedly reduced light-adapted (photopic) responses, whereas dark-adapted (scotopic) responses were relatively preserved with mildly reduced amplitudes (Fig. 2). The patient did not show dysmorphic features or systemic abnormalities. Growth and developmental milestones were appropriate for age, and no developmental delay was noted. Both of her parents demonstrated visual acuity, color vision, and fundus appearance within normal limits. The patient underwent bilateral lateral rectus recession and epiblepharon repair surgery due to intermittent exotropia and epiblepharon. At the last follow-up visit, when the patient was 9 years old, visual acuity was 20/60 in the right eye and 20/40 in the left eye. Refractive error was − 3.0 diopters spherical equivalent in the right eye and − 1.5 diopters spherical equivalent in the left eye.

To determine the genetic cause of decreased vision and intermittent exotropia in the proband, CES was performed using the MiSeq Dx sequencer with the TruSight One Sequencing Panel (Illumina; San Diego, CA, USA), targeting the exonic regions of more than 4,800 clinically relevant genes. Base calling, alignment, variant calling, annotation, and quality control reporting were performed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) best practice pipeline for germline short variant discovery (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us). The interpretation of pathogenic variants followed the standards and guidelines established by the 2015 American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) [ 11 ]. Variants were evaluated based on the following criteria: [ 1 ] location within exons of protein-coding genes linked to Online Catalog of Human Genes and Genetic Disorders (https://omim.org/); [ 2 ] allele frequencies < 0.01, sourced from gnomAD v4.1.0 (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/); [ 3 ] predicted functional impact such as nonsynonymous, nonsense, splice-site, or frameshift variants; [ 4 ] inheritance patterns compatible with autosomal or X-linked recessive disease, including homozygous or compound heterozygous variants in the proband with unaffected parent; [ 5 ] genes related to ophthalmologic features, including significantly decreased visual acuity, bull’s eye maculopathy, foveal hyperpigmentation, peripapillary atrophy, dyschromatopsia, loss of photopic responses, and diminished scotopic responses on electroretinography, aligning with a CRD phenotype. As a result, CES identified a homozygous RAB28 pathogenic variant, c.68 C > T/p.(Ser23Phe), as the best candidate for causing the CRD phenotypes in the proband (Reference transcript ID: NM_001017979.3). However, Sanger sequencing did not confirm the segregation of this RAB28 pathogenic variant, because the patient’s RAB28 variant appeared to be homozygous; however, parental testing revealed that the same variant was heterozygous only in the father. Consequently, this could not explain the autosomal recessive nature of the disease.

To clarify the inheritance pattern discrepancy where the RAB28 pathogenic variant is homozygous in the patient, heterozygous in the father, and wild-type in the mother, a trio CMA was conducted using the Affymetrix CytoScan Dx Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Santa Clara, CA, USA) to assess the copy number variation (CNV) status of the alternate allele. As a result, a deletion of approximately 3.4 Mb, annotated as arr[GRCh37] 4p16.1p15.33(10,379,065_13,755,445)×1, was detected only in the proband on chromosome 4p16.1p15.33 (Fig. 3B). According to the DECIPHER genome browser [ 12 ], four genes associated with Mendelian diseases—CLNK (OMIM *611434), HS3ST1 (*603244), NKX3-2 (*602183), and RAB28 (*612994)—are located within this deleted region (https://www.deciphergenomics.org/browser#q/4:10,379,065-13,755,445/location/4:10254065-13880445). The RAB28 gene was entirely encompassed within the heterozygous deletion at 4p16.1p15.33, as identified by CMA. Consequently, breakpoint sequencing specifically for the RAB28 gene was not performed. Thus, the proband was genetically diagnosed with sporadic AR CRD caused by a heterozygous RAB28 p.Ser23Phe pathogenic variant inherited from the father and a de novo 4p16.1 deletion encompassing RAB28.

Inherited cone function disorders are traditionally categorized into two main subtypes: stationary and progressive. Stationary cone disorders, also known as cone dysfunction syndromes, manifest at birth or in early infancy and result in isolated cone dysfunction [ 13 ]. In contrast, progressive cone dystrophies typically have a later onset and often include involvement of rod photoreceptors [ 14 ]. However, some overlap exists, as certain types of cone dysfunction syndromes, such as achromatopsia, may show limited progression over time in a small number of individuals [ 15 ]. Significant advancements have been achieved in understanding the molecular genetics and the genotype–phenotype relationships related to CRDs [ 4 ]. Several studies have been carried out to investigate the mechanisms by which mutations in RAB28 cause cone-rod dystrophy [ 5 , 6 ]. Rab-28, a protein located in the cone cilium, its rootlet, and the retinal pigment epithelium, is essential for the processes of cone outer segment shedding and phagocytosis [ 16 ]. Specifically, RAB28 pathogenic variants impair the normal function of cones and rods by interfering with the GTPase activity of the Rab-28 protein [ 9 ]. Pathogenic variants in RAB28 are understood to hinder these processes, resulting in accumulation of membrane material, which explains the hyperfluorescent central macula on FAF imaging [ 9 ]. In addition, Lee et al. suggested that the p.Ser23Phe variant, situated in the GTP binding region of the Rab-28 protein, may disrupt its normal function by interfering with ligand binding [ 8 ]. Further in-depth studies are needed to better understand the mechanisms linking each variant to RAB28 function. Although the severity of RAB28-associated CRD can vary between individuals, its clinical presentation is largely similar to that of other AR CRDs. What is drawing more attention than these clinical features is the relationship between refractive errors and RAB28 pathogenic variants. In a recent study, Iarossi et al. [ 9 ], in their 5-patient cohort study, described that all patients exhibited myopia, further supporting the existing evidence linking RAB28 variants to myopia. Our patient exhibited mild myopia. As the patient is still in the growing stage of childhood, where myopia progression is common, careful monitoring over time will be necessary to assess potential changes in refractive status.

While RAB28-associated CRD shares phenotypic traits with other AR CRDs, our case emphasizes the significance of de novo deletions in the causes of inherited retinal diseases. Our RAB28 pathogenic variant is classified as PM2 according to the ACMG guidelines. Although functional studies were not conducted, a previous case report in the Korean population identified the same homozygous variant [ 8 ], and more recently, this same variant was reported in a Haitian female [ 17 ]. To our knowledge, our case is the first report of sporadic AR CRD caused by a heterozygous RAB28 p.Ser23Phe pathogenic variant and a de novo 4p16.1p15.33 deletion. In autosomal recessive inheritance, parents who are carriers of a pathogenic variant are typically asymptomatic. Consistent with this, both parents of our patient were clinically unaffected, demonstrating normal visual acuity, color vision, and fundus appearance. While disease-causing alleles in such cases are often inherited from carrier parents as part of an ancestral ‘founder variant’, our case is unique as it involves the combination of one inherited allele and a de novo deletion [ 18 ]. Bi-allelic variations leading to autosomal recessive conditions can involve various types of genomic alterations, including single nucleotide variants (SNVs), small insertions/deletions (indels), and copy number variations (CNVs). Like SNVs and indels, CNVs can contribute to recessive diseases when they occur in a homozygous state or in a compound heterozygous state with another variant type [ 19 ]. CNVs affecting a single disease-associated locus can serve as the only variant type responsible for an AR disorder by impacting both copies of a gene in homozygous or compound heterozygous configurations. Furthermore, occurrences involving a single gene, such as an AR CNV paired with an SNV or indel in trans, have been documented to lead to recessive disorders [ 20 ]. Additionally, a recessive genic variant may become apparent, resulting in a recessive disorder through compound heterozygosity [ 21 ]. Simultaneous analysis of both SNVs/indels and CNVs is essential for a more thorough assessment of the genetic alterations present in the personal genome of a clinically affected individual. Several cases of molecularly diagnosed CRD involving CNVs have been reported, emphasizing the important contribution of CNVs to the etiology of recessive disease [ 22– 24 ]. Therefore, the clinical value of combining CNV and SNV/indel analyses for a more thorough molecular diagnostic assessment.

In conclusion, this case illustrates a rare combination of a heterozygous RAB28 p.Ser23Phe pathogenic variant and a de novo 4p16.1p15.33 deletion, which is associated with AR CRD. Although extremely rare, a bi-allelic RAB28 pathogenic variant can result from one inherited allele and a de novo deletion in the other. Genetic analysis using CES and CMA is invaluable tools for diagnosing rare inherited retinal diseases. Further studies are needed to fully understand the pathogenic mechanisms of AR CRD caused by RAB28 pathogenic variant.
