# A case of vitamin D-dependent rickets type 2A presenting with hypophosphatemia without hypocalcemia.
**Authors:** Tantivit N, Jacobsen CM
**Journal:** JCEM Case Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1210/jcemcr/luag189](https://doi.org/10.1210/jcemcr/luag189)

## Content

Vitamin D is essential for calcium and phosphate homeostasis and skeletal development. Variants in the vitamin D receptor gene (VDR) cause vitamin D-dependent rickets type 2A (VDDR2A), characterized by end-organ resistance to 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (1,25(OH)2D). We describe a child of nonconsanguineous Middle Eastern parents who presented at age 9 months with poor linear growth and inadequate weight gain. Initial evaluation showed normocalcemia, hypophosphatemia, hyperparathyroidism, markedly elevated alkaline phosphatase, and radiographic rickets, leading to an initial diagnosis of hypophosphatemic rickets. Despite treatment with low-dose calcitriol, cholecalciferol, calcium, phosphate, and bicarbonate, biochemical abnormalities persisted. Further evaluation revealed markedly elevated 1,25(OH)2D, and whole-exome sequencing identified a homozygous pathogenic VDR c.1027C>T (p.R343C) variant, confirming VDDR2A. High-dose calcitriol therapy resulted in progressive biochemical improvement and catch-up growth. This case highlights that VDDR2A may initially present with hypophosphatemia without hypocalcemia, leading to misclassification as hypophosphatemic rickets. Early recognition of elevated 1,25(OH)2D and timely genetic testing are essential for accurate diagnosis and management.

Vitamin D is essential for maintaining calcium and phosphate homeostasis, which is critical for skeletal health. It promotes intestinal absorption of calcium and phosphate, reduces renal calcium excretion, and mobilizes calcium from bone to maintain serum calcium levels, thereby supporting bone mineralization and remodeling [1-3]. Beyond its classic role in bone metabolism, vitamin D also regulates cellular proliferation, differentiation, and immune function and may have roles in cardiovascular, neurologic, reproductive, and other systemic functions, although these associations remain under investigation [4, 5].

Vitamin D activation is a 2-step process. First, vitamin D is transported to the liver, where it is hydroxylated by 25-hydroxylase enzymes to form 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D), the main circulating form used clinically to assess vitamin D status. Second, 25(OH)D is transported to the kidney and undergoes 1α-hydroxylation in the proximal tubule to form 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (1,25(OH)2D, calcitriol), the active form of vitamin D [6, 7]. 1,25(OH)2D binds to the vitamin D receptor (VDR), a nuclear hormone receptor, and mediates its biological effects [1, 5].

Vitamin D–dependent rickets type 2A (VDDR2A), also known as hereditary vitamin D–resistant rickets (HVDRR), is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by variants in the vitamin D receptor gene (VDR), leading to end-organ resistance to 1,25(OH)2D. Typical biochemical features of VDDR2A include hypocalcemia, hypophosphatemia, elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP), elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH), normal or low 25(OH)D, and markedly elevated 1,25(OH)2D. VDDR2A typically presents in early infancy with clinical and radiographic features of rickets. Approximately half demonstrate growth failure, and alopecia is observed in the majority of cases [8, 9]. Hypophosphatemia is not consistently observed, occurring in around 58% of reported patients [9].

While these presentations are frequently observed, the clinical and biochemical overlap with other forms of rickets can complicate timely diagnosis. Here, we describe a case initially misclassified as hypophosphatemic rickets due to hypophosphatemia in the absence of hypocalcemia, highlighting the phenotypic variability of VDDR2A.

A term, appropriate-for-gestational-age male infant was evaluated abroad at age 9 months for poor linear growth with length below the third percentile, inadequate weight gain resulting in declining weight-for-age percentile (Fig. 1), and delayed motor milestones. Despite adequate nutritional intake, there was no improvement in growth velocity. At the time of initial presentation, clinical signs of rickets were not clearly documented.

He was born to nonconsanguineous parents of Middle Eastern origin following an uncomplicated pregnancy and delivery. The mid-parental height was 5 feet 9 inches (176 cm). The patient’s maternal and paternal grandparents had a history of kidney stones, but there was no family history of end-stage renal disease, vitamin D–related disorders, rickets, frequent fractures, or early-onset osteoporosis.

At age 11 months, biochemical testing revealed normal serum calcium 8.7 mg/dL (2.18 mmol/L) (reference range, 8.5-10.0 mg/dL [2.1-2.5 mmol/L]), low phosphorus 2.6 mg/dL (0.84 mmol/L) (reference range, 4.0-6.5 mg/dL [1.29-2.10 mmol/L]), normal 25(OH)D 44 ng/mL (110 nmol/L) (reference range ≥20 ng/mL [≥50 nmol/L]), elevated ALP 1410 U/L (23.5 µkat/L) (reference range, 122-469 U/L [2.0-7.8 µkat/L]), and elevated PTH 517 pg/mL (54.8 pmol/L) (reference range, 10-65 pg/mL [1.06-6.89 pmol/L]). Calcium supplementation and calcitriol 0.25 mcg daily were given.

Over the next several months, despite the addition of calciferol and progressive increase of calcitriol up to 1.25 mcg daily and calcium carbonate supplementation (up to 750 mg elemental calcium per day), the patient continued to demonstrate persistent hypophosphatemia (2.4 mg/dL [0.78 mmol/L]), elevated ALP (865 U/L [14.4 µkat/L]), and elevated PTH (800.6 pg/mL [84.9 pmol/L]). He exhibited frontal bossing, wrist widening, and bowing of the lower limbs. Although prior radiographs were not available for direct review given that the patient underwent initial evaluation in another country, outside records documented metaphyseal fraying and cupping of the wrists and knees, consistent with rickets.

On subsequent evaluation, the noted mild metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate 18 mEq/L [18 mmol/L]) (reference range, 22-30 mEq/L [22-30 mmol/L]) prompted consideration of hypophosphatemic rickets from renal tubular dysfunction. Bicarbonate and phosphate supplementation were initiated, and calcitriol was reduced to 0.25 mcg daily.

By age 20 months, repeat evaluation demonstrated normal calcium (8.4 mg/dL [2.10 mmol/L]), normal phosphorus 4.4 mg/dL [1.42 mmol/L], elevated ALP 1045 U/L (17.5 µkat/L), and elevated PTH (604 pg/mL [64.1 pmol/L]). Radiographs demonstrated mild expansion of the anterior ribs at the costochondral junctions, flaring and fraying of metaphyses, and mild bowing of the femora, without fracture.

Despite combined therapy with calcitriol, calciferol, calcium, phosphate, and bicarbonate, biochemical parameters remained outside the range, leading to assessment of alternative underlying causes on presentation to our clinic at age 24 months.

All medications were withheld for 24 hours, and repeat laboratory testing revealed normocalcemia (8.9 mg/dL [2.23 mmol/L]), hypophosphatemia (3.2 mg/dL [1.03 mmol/L]), elevated ALP (832 U/L [13.9 µkat/L]), elevated PTH (343 pg/mL [36.4 pmol/L]), vitamin D insufficiency (25(OH)D 21.1 ng/mL [52.8 nmol/L]), and markedly elevated 1,25(OH)2D (>600 pg/mL [>1440 pmol/L]; reference range, 45-152 pg/mL [113-363 pmol/L]) prompting evaluation for VDDR2A. Notably, the patient did not exhibit alopecia.

Genetic testing using whole-exome sequencing identified a homozygous pathogenic variant in the VDR (c.1027C>T, p.R343C), confirming the diagnosis of VDDR2A. Both parents were found to be heterozygous carriers.

Therapy included high-dose calcitriol (up to 2 mcg/day, administered in 2 divided doses) and calcium carbonate (2 g elemental calcium per day), with close biochemical monitoring.

After initiation of appropriate treatment, serum calcium gradually increased (9-10 mg/dL [2.25-2.50 mmol/L]), and serum phosphate increased to the normal range (4-5 mg/dL [1.29-1.62 mmol/L]), and ALP and PTH levels progressively declined, reaching normalization within 4 months.

By age 3 years, the patient demonstrated catch-up growth with a height velocity of 10 cm/year (Fig. 2). Radiographs showed improvement of rickets with mild bilateral genu varum, mild lateral bowing deformity of the mid to distal femoral shafts, symmetric medial cortical thickening of both tibiae, and mild diffuse demineralization. There was irregularity along the distal femoral epiphyses, but the growth plates were not widened, frayed, or cupped (Fig. 3). Calcitriol was continued at 1 mcg twice daily and calcium carbonate was gradually reduced.

Follow-up evaluations from age 4 to 7 years demonstrated biochemical parameters within target range. The patient’s calcium remained in the high-normal to mildly elevated range without significant hypercalcemia (9.7-10.7 mg/dL [2.43-2.68 mmol/L]), phosphorus levels were normal (4.2-4.8 mg/dL [1.36-1.55 mmol/L]), ALP was stable (226-266 U/L [3.8-4.4 µkat/L]), and PTH remained within reference limits (15.8-31.1 pg/mL [1.68-3.30 pmol/L]). Urine calcium-to-creatinine ratios were 0.2-0.4 mg/mg (0.56-1.12 mmol/mmol), and serial renal ultrasounds showed no evidence of nephrocalcinosis.

At age 7 years 11 months, he exhibited normal dentition, appropriate linear growth with height at the third percentile and weight at the 61st percentile on the growth chart. He actively participates in physical activities without bone pain or deformity. The longitudinal course showed progressive biochemical and radiographic improvement under high-dose calcitriol and calcium supplementation, consistent with improved mineral homeostasis in genetically confirmed VDDR2A.

The VDR contains 2 key functional domains: C-terminal ligand-binding domain (LBD) that binds 1,25(OH)2D and N-terminal DNA-binding domain (DBD) that recognizes vitamin D response elements (VDREs) in the promoter regions of the target genes. After entering the nucleus, 1,25(OH)2D binds to the LBD, inducing conformational change and heterodimerization with the retinoid X receptor (RXR). The VDR-RXR complex binds to VDREs via the DBD and recruits coactivators or corepressors to regulate transcription, enabling both gene activation and repression [10, 11].

The VDR c.1027C>T (p.R343C) variant results in a missense substitution of arginine, a polar amino acid, to cysteine, a neutral and slight polar amino acid, at position 343, which is located within the LBD of the VDR [12]. This variant is classified as likely pathogenic and is extremely rare in the general population, with a reported allele frequency of 3.10 × 10−6 in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) [13].

The homozygous VDR p.R343C has been previously reported in a child from Egypt with HVDRR [14]. The affected individual was born to consanguineous parents. Biochemical evaluation revealed hypocalcemia, hypophosphatemia, markedly elevated ALP, elevated PTH, and elevated 1,25(OH)2D. He exhibited alopecia with sparse eyebrows and eyelashes, short stature, and radiographic findings consistent with rickets. Parental testing confirmed heterozygosity in both parents, and the variant was absent in 200 ethnically matched control chromosomes, supporting its pathogenicity [14].

In contrast to the previously reported case, our patient presented with hypophosphatemia in the absence of hypocalcemia, which contributed to the diagnostic challenge at the time of initial evaluation. Although hypocalcemia is commonly noted in VDDR2A, near-normal calcium levels have also been described in previous reports, including the systematic review by Dodamani et al, which reported a median calcium level of 7.4 mg/dL (interquartile range, 6.4-8.2 mg/dL) [15]. The preserved serum calcium level may reflect compensatory secondary hyperparathyroidism, in which markedly elevated PTH maintains normocalcemia through increased renal calcium reabsorption and bone resorption. In contrast, elevated PTH promotes renal phosphate wasting, contributing to hypophosphatemia. This is further supported by the markedly elevated PTH and ALP observed in our patient.

The diagnosis became clear after laboratory studies demonstrated markedly elevated 1,25(OH)2D. Even though calcitriol had been withheld for only 24 hours prior to the testing, the markedly elevated 1,25(OH)2D (>600 pg/mL [>1440 pmol/L]) was unlikely to be explained solely by administered calcitriol, given its relative half-life of 5 to 8 hours. Genetic testing subsequently confirmed the VDR p.R343C variant. Prior characterization of the R343H variant affecting the same region of VDR by Tseng et al [16] showed impaired vitamin D–dependent transactivation due to defective cofactor recruitment, while preserving VDR expression, VDR-RXR heterodimerization, and nuclear localization, suggesting retained partial receptor activity. The affected patients also responded to supraphysiologic doses of active vitamin D, supporting the favorable response to high-dose calcitriol in variants affecting this region. Following initiation of high-dose calcitriol therapy, the patient’s biochemical abnormalities progressively normalized, accompanied by improvements in growth velocity, weight gain, and gross motor development, underscoring the importance of early recognition and targeted treatment in VDDR2A.

Although alopecia was present in the majority (81.5%) of genetically confirmed VDDR2A cases, a systematic review showed that alopecia occurred in only 51.4% of patients with nontruncating LBD variants, as seen in our patient, whereas it was universal in truncating LBD and all DBD variants [15]. The absence of alopecia in nontruncating LBD variants may be explained by preservation of some VDR functions involved in hair follicle cycling, which depend on VDR-RXR heterodimerization and DNA binding. In these variants, the receptor may still retain its ability to interact with RXR and bind DNA in keratinocyte stem cells despite impaired vitamin D signaling [17].

This case highlights that vitamin D–resistant rickets may initially present with hypophosphatemia without hypocalcemia, which can lead to misdiagnosis as hypophosphatemic rickets. In patients with rickets and markedly elevated PTH levels, vitamin D–related rickets, including VDDR2A, should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Early recognition of elevated 1,25(OH)2D and timely genetic confirmation are essential to avoid diagnostic delay and to initiate appropriate therapy.
