BACKGROUND: Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS) is a rare autoimmune 
neuromuscular disorder characterized by proximal muscle weakness, autonomic 
dysfunction and hyporeflexia. Numerous studies have reported the association of 
LEMS with anti-SOX1 antibodies and small cell carcinoma, but LEMS with double 
antibody positivity and cerebellar ataxia accompanied by small cell carcinoma is 
relatively rare.
CASE PRESENTATION: A 56-year-old female patient developed progressive limb 
weakness in November 2023, with symptoms gradually worsening over six months to 
include gait instability, impaired grip, chest tightness, dyspnea, dysphagia, 
and aspiration. Electrodiagnostic testing confirmed Lambert-Eaton myasthenic 
syndrome (LEMS) with positive VGCC antibodies. Paraneoplastic antibody testing 
showed positive anti-CV2/CRMP5 antibodies (24 AU) and borderline anti-SOX1 
antibodies (9 AU), while other antibodies were negative. Initial imaging 
(contrast-enhanced chest CT and PET-CT) revealed no abnormalities, but follow-up 
one year later detected mediastinal small cell lung cancer. The patient 
underwent three plasma exchange sessions with corticosteroid therapy with 
limited response, then showed significant improvement after switching to 
subcutaneous Telitacicept (80 mg) combined with Fampyra Comprimidos and 
Mycophenolate Mofetil capsules, achieving independent ambulation and normal grip 
strength with only mild residual limb weakness. The patient has since been 
transferred to a local hospital for chemotherapy.
CONCLUSION: We find that LEMS with concurrent anti-SOX1 and anti-CV2/CRMP5 
positivity manifests as rapid progression, prominent proximal weakness, and 
potential cerebellar and autonomic involvement. Early and sustained oncological 
surveillance—particularly of mediastinal lymph nodes—and combined 
immunotherapeutic strategies can significantly improve neurological outcomes.

Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS) is a rare autoimmune disorder of the presynaptic neuromuscular junction, clinically characterized by proximal muscle weakness, autonomic dysfunction, and reduced tendon reflexes [1]. In most cases, autoantibodies against the P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channel (VGCC) impair acetylcholine release at the motor endplate [2], producing the hallmark electrophysiological pattern of CMAP amplitude decrement at low-frequency stimulation (2–5 Hz) and increment at high-frequency stimulation [3]. Approximately 50–60% of LEMS cases are paraneoplastic, most frequently associated with small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC). Recent advances in onconeural antibody testing—including anti-Hu, anti-CV2/CRMP5, and anti-SOX1—have enhanced diagnostic sensitivity for paraneoplastic neurological syndromes (PNS); notably, anti-SOX1 is detected in up to 65% of SCLC-associated LEMS cases [4, 5], whereas anti-CV2/CRMP5–related PNS is less common and often presents with multifocal neurological signs such as chorea, cranial neuropathies, peripheral neuropathy, and cerebellar ataxia [6]. Therefore, this study aims to detail the clinical manifestations, diagnostic, and therapeutic approaches for a rare case of double antibody-positive LEMS associated with occult mediastinal small cell carcinoma. This analysis is intended to serve as a valuable reference for future diagnosis and management strategies for LEMS.

A 56-year-old woman presented to our hospital on October 6, 2024, with a one-year history of progressive limb weakness and exertional dyspnea. In November 2023, she developed insidious limb weakness without any apparent trigger, yet her ability to grasp objects and ambulate remained preserved. One month later, she developed fever (maximum 38.0 °C) and dyspnea on exertion, which worsened with activity, accompanied by gradually progressive limb weakness and an unsteady gait; she was treated for a presumed respiratory infection at a local general hospital, after which her fever resolved and dyspnea partially improved, but weakness and gait instability persisted. In January 2024, she was admitted to Rizhao Port Hospital for suspected myasthenia gravis, but experienced no clinical improvement. In February 2024, worsening dyspnea with weak productive cough and further deterioration of limb strength prompted referral to Linyi People’s Hospital, where serology revealed anti-SOX1 antibody positivity (1:30) and electromyography suggested a myasthenic disorder; following a nine-day course of Mecobalamin, Vitamin B1, Mouse Nerve Growth Factor, Oseltamivir, and Pyridostigmine Bromide, she was discharged without appreciable improvement in either dyspnea or muscle strength. Over the subsequent four months, her symptoms rapidly progressed to severe proximal weakness—rendering her unable to hold objects securely—and an unsteady gait requiring assistance to walk up to approximately 10 m, accompanied by exertional dyspnea, dysphagia, clear consciousness but poor mood and appetite; The patient underwent three courses of plasma exchange combined with corticosteroid therapy. Each session utilized an exchange volume of 2300 mL, accompanied by methylprednisolone pulse therapy at 500 mg daily for 5 days. The dosage was then reduced by half every 3 days until reaching 80 mg, which was maintained for 5 days. However, the therapeutic response was suboptimal. During the preceding three months she also developed blurred vision, choking on liquids, difficulty urinating and constipation, and had lost 2.5 kg since symptom onset; The patient is a non-smoker but has been exposed to secondhand smoke through her spouse, who has smoked 20 cigarettes per day for over 40 years.

Neurological examination revealed mild bulbar dysarthria without other cranial nerve deficits; The patient had no ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, or nystagmus, and no fatigable muscle weakness; proximal lower‐limb strength was Medical Research Council grade 3 + and distal grade 4, with similarly reduced strength in the upper limbs; deep tendon reflexes were diminished in all four extremities and Gordon’s sign was positive on the right; light touch and pinprick sensation were intact bilaterally, and bilateral heel–knee–shin testing was positive; gait was broad‐based, with inability to tandem walk or stand on one foot; Romberg’s sign was positive and accentuated on eye closure; the edrophonium (Tensilon) test was negative.

Laboratory tests showed elevated lipids—total cholesterol 6.79 mmol/L and LDL‐cholesterol 4.70 mmol/L—with otherwise unremarkable complete blood count, metabolic panel, coagulation profile, cardiac enzymes, Creatine Kinase, BNP, thyroid function tests, folate, vitamin B₁₂, intrinsic factor antibodies, antinuclear antibody panel, immunoglobulin levels (including IgG4), vasculitis markers, rheumatoid factor panel, and myositis antibody profile; paraneoplastic serology by radioimmunoassay demonstrated markedly elevated anti‐VGCC antibodies (772.97 pmol/L, reference ≤ 30 pmol/L), negative neuromuscular junction antibody panel(such as AchR and MuSK), and semiquantitative paraneoplastic antibody testing revealed anti‐CV2/CRMP5 positivity (24 AU, reference < 5 AU) with anti‐SOX1 in the gray zone (9 AU, gray zone 5–10 AU) and all other onconeural antibodies negative.

Brain MRI (February 2024) revealed right frontal lobe encephalomalacia with perilesional gliosis, ischemic changes in the bilateral frontal lobes, corona radiata, and centrum semiovale, accompanied by paranasal sinusitis, empty sella, and cerebral arteriosclerotic changes. In May 2024, electromyography with repetitive nerve stimulation was positive: high‐frequency stimulation of the right median and ulnar nerves produced incremental CMAP amplitudes, while low‐frequency stimulation of the right accessory nerve induced a decremental response, alongside myopathic features(At 30 Hz, the median and ulnar nerves exhibited amplitude increases of 166.7% and 240.6%, and area increases of 100.9% and 223.2%). Contrast‐enhanced chest CT demonstrated chronic bronchitic changes with regional hypoventilation, a tiny right pulmonary nodule, and localized emphysema. PET/CT showed bilateral pulmonary inflammation, right breast calcifications, focal high FDG uptake in the right lower abdomen (suggestive of inflammation), and non‐specific FDG activity in the left parapharyngeal space, left upper‐limb soft tissues, and left scapular region—likely motion artifacts—with no evidence of malignancy. A follow‐up contrast‐enhanced chest CT in November 2024 (Fig. 1) revealed a mediastinal mass (suspicious for lymphoma; pathological confirmation advised), chronic bronchitic changes, small fibrotic foci in the right lung, right breast calcifications, fatty liver, and bilateral renal cysts. The patient exhibited generalized weakness, more pronounced proximally, with diminished deep tendon reflexes. Repetitive nerve stimulation was positive—high‐frequency stimulation of the right median and ulnar nerves produced a significant increment in compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitude, whereas low‐frequency stimulation of the right accessory nerve induced a marked decrement—consistent with presynaptic neuromuscular transmission failure and concurrent myopathic changes. Serology demonstrated markedly elevated anti-VGCC antibody titers, positivity for anti-SOX1 and anti-CV2/CRMP5 antibodies, and a negative panel for other neuromuscular‐junction autoantibodies; the edrophonium test was also negative, effectively excluding myasthenia gravis. Taken together, these findings established a diagnosis of Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome secondary to mediastinal small cell carcinoma.

On October 16, 2024, the patient received her first subcutaneous injection of Telitacicept 80 mg, with a second dose administered one week later. Concurrent therapy included Fampyra Comprimidos (10 mg BID) and Mycophenolate Mofetil (0.75 g BID). Within days, she reported significant relief of chest tightness and improvement in lower-limb strength; weekly tarlatamab injections were continued, resulting in marked resolution of dysphagia and dyspnea and restoration of independent ambulation. Upon discharge, muscle strength in all four limbs was grade 4, with normal heel-knee-shin test and Romberg's test. She was transitioned to her local oncology center for standard chemotherapeutic management of her small cell carcinoma.

In patients with LEMS, proximal leg muscle weakness is typically the initial symptom noticed by 80% of them. Arm weakness may already be present or develop rapidly. The weakness usually spreads from the proximal to the distal parts, affecting the feet and hands, and from the caudal to the cranial regions, ultimately reaching the oculo- bulbar area [8]. This case also began with lower limb involvement, but the disease advanced rapidly, and even the respiratory muscles exhibited signs of involvement.

Dual positivity for anti-SOX1 and anti-CV2/CRMP5 is rare and has predominantly been reported in sensory‐autonomic neuropathies [9] or paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration [10]. In our patient, PNS serological testing shows positivity for anti-CV2/CRMP5 antibodies with a borderline positive result for anti-SOX1 antibodies. The borderline positivity of anti-SOX1 antibodies is significant and an active search for underlying malignancy, particularly bronchogenic carcinoma is always warranted. The patient's cerebellar ataxia—positive Romberg's sign and heel-knee-shin tests—may be associated with an immune response targeting Purkinje cells in the cerebellum, potentially triggered by dual antibodies. To date, cases of LEMS with co-positivity for these antibodies have not been described. Given the aggressive course and heavier symptom burden seen in double-positive patients, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for LEMS when both antibodies are detected, with regular follow-up electrophysiological studies and serial oncological screening.

Despite initially negative imaging, the patient was ultimately diagnosed with mediastinal small cell carcinoma after one year of follow-up. In antibody-positive LEMS, early tumor detection can be challenging—primary lesions may be small and occult, particularly in mediastinal lymph nodes—necessitating prolonged surveillance with contrast‐enhanced chest CT at three- to six-month intervals and, when indicated, adjunctive PET-CT. Therapeutically, the combination of Telitacicept, Fampyra Comprimidos, and Mycophenolate Mofetil achieved substantial clinical improvement in this case, whereas prior plasmapheresis and corticosteroid regimens had yielded only modest benefit. The majority of SCLC cases are associated with smoking. Although the patient is a non-smoker, long-term exposure to secondhand smoke suggests a potential etiological link to the development of this malignancy.

In summary, Patients with dual anti-SOX1 and anti-CV2/CRMP5 positivity in LEMS frequently present with severe and rapidly progressive symptoms, such as bulbar dysfunction and respiratory involvement. In the early stage, imaging may fail to detect occult small cell lung cancer (SCLC). Therefore, it is necessary to perform prolonged chest computed tomography (CT) surveillance (≥ 6 months) and positron emission tomography-CT (PET-CT) when appropriate. The combination of Telitacicept, Fampyra Comprimidos, and Mycophenolate Mofetil can significantly relieve symptoms.
