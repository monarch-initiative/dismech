Treatment strategies to improve outcomes in patients with metastatic
castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) are evolving. Of particular
interest are therapies that target DNA damage responses in tumor cells by
inhibiting poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) activity. Several PARP inhibitors
have recently received regulatory approval for the treatment of patients with
mCRPC, of which olaparib was the first for prostate cancer. Olaparib received
approval as a monotherapy following the PROfound study (NCT02987543) and in
combination with abiraterone following the PROpel study (NCT03732820) for mCRPC.
Both PROfound (homologous recombination repair mutation biomarker-selected) and
PROpel (biomarker unselected) patients demonstrated statistically significant
longer radiographic progression-free survival (rPFS) with olaparib versus their
respective control arms in the intention-to-treat population. In both studies,
the greatest clinical benefit with olaparib was seen in patients with BRCA1
and/or BRCA2 mutations (BRCAm): PROfound rPFS hazard ratio (HR) 0.22 (95%
confidence interval [CI] 0.15-0.32); PROpel rPFS HR 0.23 (95% CI 0.12-0.43).
Clinical benefit was also observed in terms of overall survival: PROfound HR
0.63 (95% CI 0.42-0.95); PROpel HR 0.29 (95% CI 0.14-0.56). We provide a
comprehensive overview of the utility of olaparib for patients with mCRPC
harboring a BRCAm. Key clinical and safety data in BRCAm subgroup populations
are discussed, predominantly based on findings from PROfound and PROpel, as well
as investigator-initiated studies, to help inform treatment decision-making in
this patient population. We also discuss the importance of genetic testing to
identify patients who may optimally benefit from treatment with olaparib, either
as a monotherapy or in combination with abiraterone.

Plain Language Summary: In the USA, prostate cancer is the most commonly
diagnosed cancer in men. It affects approximately one in eight men during their
lifetime. Metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) occurs when
the cancer spreads beyond the prostate gland and the disease progresses despite
treatment with standard hormonal therapy. Patients who have cancers with
mutations in BRCA1 and/or BRCA2 genes have poor outcomes, and additional
life-prolonging treatments are needed. Olaparib is a drug approved to treat
certain patients with mCRPC, both alone and in combination with abiraterone.
Approval was based on two landmark clinical trials called PROfound and PROpel.
PROfound compared olaparib directly with the hormonal therapies abiraterone or
enzalutamide. PROpel evaluated whether combining olaparib with abiraterone would
delay the progression of cancer compared with just abiraterone. After these two
studies were completed, results were analyzed specifically in patients who had a
BRCA mutation in their cancer. We have compiled the results in patients with
mCRPC with BRCA mutations and show that both olaparib on its own or in
combination with abiraterone resulted in substantial clinical benefits in
delaying disease progression and improving survival over standard treatments for
mCRPC. The side effects that patients with a BRCA mutation experienced were
similar to those in the overall patient population originally analyzed. We also
discuss the importance of testing men with prostate cancer for these genetic
mutations before starting treatment to help identify patients who may benefit
the most from olaparib on its own or in combination with abiraterone.

Olaparib received approval for the treatment of patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) as a monotherapy following the PROfound study and in combination with abiraterone following the PROpel study.This study compiles the results from these trials in patients with mCRPC with BRCA mutations and shows that olaparib, both on its own and in combination with abiraterone, resulted in substantial clinical benefits in delaying disease progression and improving survival over standard mCRPC treatments.We also discuss the importance of testing for genetic mutations before starting treatment to help identify patients who may benefit the most from olaparib therapy.

Prostate cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in men in the USA. An estimated 1.5 million diagnoses were made and 400,000 related deaths occurred worldwide in 2022, according to the International Agency for Research on Cancer [1]. Many patients with prostate cancer develop metastases, with disease progression to metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), for which the prognosis is poor [2]. Real-world evidence from the USA indicates that the median survival of patients with mCRPC is < 2 years [3], with only ~ 30–50% of patients receiving a second line of therapy [3, 4]. Studies have shown that approximately 20–30% of patients with mCRPC harbor deleterious mutations in homologous recombination repair (HRR) genes involved in repairing DNA damage [5–7]. The most common of these HRR mutations (HRRm) is BRCA2, accounting for 29–44% of identified HRRm in patients with mCRPC (3–9% of patients with mCRPC overall), with both BRCA1 and/or BRCA2 mutations together (BRCAm) encompassing 33–51% of patients with an HRRm (4–11% of patients with mCRPC overall) [5–9]; both prospective and observational studies in patients with mCRPC and BRCAm have indicated poor prognoses and worse outcomes on current standard-of-care treatments compared with patients without BRCAm or HRRm [10, 11]. As such, there remains an urgent need for improved life-prolonging treatments, particularly in the first-line mCRPC setting.

Treatment selection in mCRPC involves the sequential addition of androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs), chemotherapy, radiopharmaceuticals, and/or use of experimental agents or interventions being tested within clinical trials [12–14]. The selection of optimal treatment(s) depends on several factors, including disease stage and previous treatments received, concomitant medications with the potential for drug–drug interactions, other comorbidities, and patient preferences. Genetic tumor profiling for patients with advanced disease is also an important factor, as endorsed in treatment guidelines [12–14].

Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibition is a recommended treatment option for patients with mCRPC [12–14]. As a monotherapy, PARP inhibitors have been shown to be efficacious in cells harboring an HRRm [15]. The mechanism of action of clinically approved PARP inhibitors involves inhibition of PARP enzymatic activity and trapping of PARP on DNA single-strand breaks, thereby preventing DNA repair. This leads to the accumulation of double-stranded DNA breaks that cannot be accurately repaired in tumor cells with defects in HRR genes, resulting in tumor cell death [16, 17]. PARP targeting in cancer cells with defects in HRR genes is the first clinical example of therapeutic applicability of the genetic concept of synthetic lethality, whereby toxicity of the treatment requires defects in a cellular process that is impaired specifically in malignant cells, thus having the potential to spare normal tissue [18].

Several preclinical reports have shown improved efficacy of PARP inhibitors when used in combination with an ARPI, such as abiraterone, in models with no HRR deficiencies through several possible mechanisms, including the ability of ARPIs to downregulate HRR genes, further impairing DNA repair [19–21]. A number of PARP inhibitors have now been approved for the treatment of mCRPC (Table 1). Olaparib (Lynparza), a potent inhibitor of PARP1 and PARP2 activity, was the first, both as monotherapy [5] and in combination with the ARPI abiraterone [9]. Olaparib is also approved for the treatment of patients harboring tumors with BRCAm or HRR deficiencies in other advanced solid tumors based on the results from several key clinical trials [22–30]. Subsequently, rucaparib as a monotherapy and two other combinations of PARP inhibitors with ARPIs (niraparib plus abiraterone and talazoparib plus enzalutamide) have been approved in mCRPC following The aim of this review was to provide a comprehensive overview of the utility of olaparib for patients with mCRPC identified as harboring a BRCAm, predominantly based on findings from the two pivotal olaparib phase III studies, PROfound and PROpel, and also on findings from phase II investigator-initiated studies.

PROfound was a randomized, open-label, multicenter trial of olaparib versus treatment of physician’s choice using either enzalutamide or abiraterone in patients with HRR-gene-mutated mCRPC and disease progression on at least one ARPI. Patients were required to have a deleterious or suspected deleterious mutation in one or more of 15 genes with a direct or indirect role in HRR (Table 2). PROpel was a randomized, multicenter, double-blind study of olaparib plus abiraterone versus placebo plus abiraterone for the first-line treatment of patients with mCRPC [9]. Clinical guidelines recommended molecular testing as a standard of care in mCRPC. If not previously performed, both germline and tumor tissue testing are recommended to check for mutations in HRR genes, microsatellite instability status, and tumor mutational burden, as well as to inform the familial risk of inherited cancers [13, 14, 35]. Tumor tissue testing has long been considered the reference standard when sufficient tumor tissue can be obtained as it has shown high clinical sensitivity and validation in clinical trials. However, obtaining high-quality, fresh tumor samples can be challenging, particularly from bone metastases; furthermore, old or insufficient tumor samples are associated with a high rate of test failure [36–38].

Several molecular testing platforms are approved for molecular testing, which can identify a wide range of genes relevant for patients with mCRPC. FoundationOne® CDx (Foundation Medicine, Inc.; Cambridge, MA, USA) is a US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved companion diagnostic platform for tumor tissue testing, which detects both somatic and germline mutations. In addition, the FoundationOne® Liquid CDx platform is an FDA-approved circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)-based test. As a minimally invasive procedure, ctDNA testing can be particularly useful when no tumor tissue is available or tumor testing results are inconclusive, although there is a small risk of generating false-positive and false-negative results [37–40]. Peripheral blood or saliva testing are also options as minimally invasive and repeatable procedures, though they provide germline data only [37, 41]. Notably, approximately half of patients with BRCAm with mCRPC have somatic mutations [42]; therefore, mutation status could be missed in these patients if a test providing germline-only data was used. In a retrospective analysis in PROfound, 54.5% of evaluable patients had germline BRCAm and 45.5% had somatic BRCAm [43]. In the multicenter, international PREVALENCE study (NCT03871816) prospectively designed to evaluate HRRm frequency, the occurrence of a BRCAm-positive test in patients with mCRPC was highest by tumor tissue (8.7%) and ctDNA testing (5.4%) [44]. Use of tumor or ctDNA testing is therefore important to identify as many patients as possible with an HRRm.

Tumor testing (from archival or recent biopsy tissue) was conducted in PROfound using an investigational clinical trial assay based on the FoundationOne® CDx next-generation sequencing test, developed in partnership with Foundation Medicine, Inc. The clinical trial assay was used to prospectively identify patients harboring HRR gene mutations for inclusion in the trial [5]. The PROfound study is the largest study to date to screen patients prospectively for genes with a direct or indirect role in HRR by tumor tissue testing (4858 tissue samples from 4047 patients). Results were obtained for 58% of samples (69% of patients), which included a mix of archival and newly obtained tissue samples taken both from the primary prostate tumor and from non-prostate metastatic sites [45]. Clinical findings in the BRCAm subgroup are based on tumor tissue test results only. Retrospective exploratory plasma-derived ctDNA analysis was conducted to investigate whether ctDNA testing can complement tissue testing in identifying patients in PROfound with BRCAm, and high concordance between tumor tissue and ctDNA was shown in patient-matched samples [46]; discordance may occur because of clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential interference and limitations in detecting BRCAm structural variants (such as homozygous deletions) in ctDNA [46, 47].

In the PROpel study, tumor tissue (FoundationOne® CDx; mostly archival) and ctDNA (FoundationOne® Liquid CDx) were analyzed after randomization but before primary analysis and subsequently used to generate aggregate test results [9, 48]. With the aggregated results, it was possible to maximize the number of patients with known BRCAm status while minimizing potential false negatives, facilitating a robust analysis within this patient subgroup. In general, there was good agreement (overall percent agreement 85%) between matched tumor tissue and ctDNA-based test results [48].

Biomarker testing is important in supporting informed risk/benefit assessment of treatment options by assessing prognosis and familial risk, as well as identifying patients with BRCAm who may benefit from olaparib monotherapy or olaparib plus abiraterone over an ARPI alone. In addition, it is important to test patients early enough, in a test-to-treat approach, to ensure the acquisition of sufficient information when considering first-line treatment choices. Findings from PROfound and PREVALENCE indicate that BRCA mutations likely arise early [43–45], reinforcing the preference for testing tissue collected at diagnosis as early as possible to acquire biomarker information. Clinical trial outcomes from PROfound and PROpel provide clear level one evidence to perform routine genetic testing for patients with mCRPC [49]. Rates of genetic testing have increased. However, disparities between academic/cancer centers and the community setting [50] and across racial and ethnic groups are recognized [51]. Unfortunately, HRRm status remains unknown in many patients, who therefore do not have the option of receiving a targeted therapy that requires genetic testing results. In a real-world analysis from the USA, including a cohort of 1022 patients with mCRPC diagnosed or treated between January 2020 and December 2023, 64% received HRRm testing, of whom 9% had testing failure [52]. Only 19% of patients were tested at the time of diagnosis. Overall, 38% of tested patients were positive for HRRm; of these, only 58% received treatment with a PARP inhibitor, most often (65%) after more than three lines of therapy [52].

PROfound demonstrated the efficacy and safety of olaparib monotherapy in patients with mCRPC whose disease progressed while receiving an ARPI (e.g., enzalutamide or abiraterone) for mCRPC. In patients with mCRPC who had a mutation in BRCA1, BRCA2, or ATM (cohort A), olaparib led to significantly longer radiographic progression-free survival (rPFS) than the control arm of treatment with physician’s choice of enzalutamide or abiraterone (median 7.4 vs. 3.6 months; hazard ratio [HR] 0.34; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.25–0.47; p < 0.001) [5]. An rPFS benefit was also observed in the overall trial population including patients with any of the 15 prespecified HRRm genes. Overall survival (OS) was also significantly longer in patients randomized to olaparib versus physician’s choice (cohort A: median 19.1 vs. 14.7 months; HR 0.69; 95% CI 0.50–0.97; p = 0.02) [53], despite substantial crossover from control therapy to olaparib (66% of patients). In 2020, the FDA approved olaparib for the treatment of adult patients with deleterious or suspected deleterious germline or somatic HRR-gene-mutated mCRPC (which includes BRCA1/BRCA2) with disease progression on prior treatment with enzalutamide or abiraterone; determination of HRRm must be based on an FDA-approved companion diagnostic (Table 1) [54].

Findings in the BRCAm subgroup (according to tumor test results) are based on post hoc exploratory analyses.

PROpel demonstrated the efficacy and safety of olaparib plus abiraterone as first-line treatment for patients with mCRPC, who were enrolled irrespective of HRRm status. The study met its primary endpoint (Table 1) and demonstrated a statistically significant and clinically meaningful rPFS benefit with olaparib plus abiraterone versus placebo plus abiraterone, according to investigator assessment in the intention-to-treat (ITT) population (median 24.8 vs. 16.6 months; HR 0.66; 95% CI 0.54–0.81; p < 0.001), consistent with blinded independent central review (median 27.6 vs. 16.4 months; HR 0.61; 95% CI 0.49–0.74) [9]. OS was a key secondary endpoint with alpha control and, although not reaching statistical significance in the final prespecified analysis (which reached only 47.6% maturity at data cut-off 3 [October 12, 2022]), olaparib plus abiraterone demonstrated a median OS of 42.1 months, the longest reported to date in mCRPC, with a > 7-month improvement compared with abiraterone monotherapy, a life-prolonging standard-of-care therapy [58]. In general, patients with a BRCAm enrolled in PROpel randomized to receive olaparib plus abiraterone showed greater rPFS and OS benefits than those without such mutations [9, 58]. On the basis of the PROpel study, in May 2023, the FDA approved olaparib in combination with abiraterone and prednisone or prednisolone for the treatment of adult patients with deleterious or suspected deleterious BRCAm mCRPC, provided that BRCAm status has been assessed by an FDA-approved companion diagnostic test (Table 1) [54]. In contrast, the European Medicines Agency granted approval of olaparib plus abiraterone and prednisone/prednisolone for the treatment of adult patients with mCRPC in whom chemotherapy is not clinically indicated (Table 1) [59].

The TOPARP program included a series of phase II clinical trials aimed at identifying predictive biomarkers for response to PARP inhibitors in patients with mCRPC [63]. The phase II (off-label) BRCAAway trial (NCT03012321) enrolled biomarker-preselected patients with mCRPC with BRCA1/BRCA2 and/or ATM mutations randomized 1:1:1 to receive abiraterone 1000 mg daily plus prednisone 5 mg twice daily (n = 19), olaparib 300 mg twice daily (n = 21), or olaparib plus abiraterone/prednisone (n = 21) [64]. Although the findings are not exclusively in patients with BRCAm, they support olaparib plus abiraterone over either agent alone or sequentially. Median radiographic or clinical PFS was 8.6 months (95% CI 2.9–17) for abiraterone plus prednisone, 14 months (95% CI 8.4–20) for olaparib, and 39 months (95% CI 22–not reached) for olaparib plus abiraterone/prednisone. For patients crossing over from abiraterone to olaparib (n = 8/19) or from olaparib to abiraterone (n = 8/21), median PFS from randomization was 16 months (95% CI 7.8–25) and 16 months (95% CI 11–NR), respectively.

Patients with mCRPC and BRCAm have poor prognosis and worse outcomes on standard-of-care treatments than patients with non-BRCAm or non-HRRm [10, 11]. There is a need to optimize effective treatments to improve outcomes for patients with mCRPC. Olaparib was the first PARP inhibitor approved for the treatment of mCRPC, both as monotherapy and in combination with the ARPI abiraterone. Both treatment regimens demonstrated substantial clinical benefits, both in phase II trials [63, 64] and in phase III trials versus the respective control arms in patients with BRCAm with mCRPC (Table 8) [43, 61]. A more informative approach to genetic testing that encompasses both germline and somatic tissue testing is important for patients with mCRPC. As well as educating healthcare providers on the expected risk–benefits of olaparib monotherapy or olaparib plus abiraterone in both patients with BRCAm and those with non-BRCAm, testing results can inform on familial risk and tumor biology and increase our understanding of the genetic landscape of mCRPC for future research. Although widely recommended, with rates of HRR gene mutation testing increasing, the use of and access to genetic testing remains suboptimal [49, 50]. Patients with HRRm do not exhibit significantly different clinical phenotypes from the overall mCRPC population, so genetic testing is recommended in all patients with mCRPC. Despite the prognostic value of HRRm (notably BRCA, as highlighted by PROfound and PROpel [5, 43, 58, 61], as well as other pivotal studies of PARP inhibitors in patients with mCRPC [32, 33, 65]), even in the era following the integration of PARP inhibitor therapies into treatment guidelines, many patients still do not receive HRRm testing for a multitude of reasons, including lack of availability in some healthcare systems and cost, with socioeconomic factors associated with deprivation correlating with a lower likelihood of receiving testing [66].

The findings from PROfound show that olaparib monotherapy is an important treatment option for patients with mCRPC with a BRCAm whose disease has progressed after receiving enzalutamide or abiraterone [12–14]. The combination of olaparib with abiraterone, as investigated in PROpel, expands the potential to use olaparib in a broader patient population who have not received treatment with ARPIs. As such, patients in the castration-resistant setting could potentially receive the combination of olaparib plus abiraterone earlier in their therapeutic sequencing [12–14]. Real-world evidence from the USA indicates that many patients with mCRPC with HRRm receive several lines of therapy in the mCRPC setting before receiving olaparib monotherapy [4]. However, the clinical benefits observed in patients with BRCAm who had not received previous taxane-based chemotherapy in the PROfound study support early treatment with olaparib in these patients [43], a finding supported by results from the phase III TRITON-3 study, in which rPFS was significantly prolonged with the PARP inhibitor rucaparib versus physician’s choice of docetaxel or ARPI in patients with mutations in BRCA or ATM who had not received previous chemotherapy for mCRPC [65]. Furthermore, findings from the phase II (off-label) BRCAAway trial support use of the upfront combination of olaparib plus abiraterone over either agent alone or sequentially in the first-line treatment of patients with mCRPC harboring BRCA1, BRCA2, or ATM mutations [64]. Crossing over to olaparib plus abiraterone from placebo plus abiraterone was not permitted in PROpel. The findings from the BRCAAway study provide further supportive evidence for first-line treatment of patients with mCRPC with combined olaparib plus abiraterone, although these data require further consolidation in larger-scale studies. Although the use of ARPI in the mHSPC setting is increasing, approaches to subsequent treatment following progression to mCRPC remain diverse, particularly as the best course of treatment is often influenced by patient preferences, pace of disease progression, symptoms, patterns of metastatic spread, and tumor genotype, alongside recommendations of physicians. Further study is required to inform treatment sequencing with PARP inhibitors in combination with ARPIs in patients previously treated with ARPIs. In addition, with the approval of 177Lu-PSMA-617 for patients with PSMA-positive mCRPC who have previously received ARPIs [67], it will be important to understand the efficacy in patients with overlapping biomarkers to optimize treatment outcomes. That said, the findings from all these studies emphasize the potential to improve outcomes for patients who receive genetic testing when considering first-line treatment options.

Although findings are limited by a smaller subgroup of patients with BRCAm in the PROfound and PROpel studies, the safety profile of olaparib or olaparib plus abiraterone in patients with BRCAm was generally consistent with that observed in the ITT populations [5, 9, 43, 58, 61]. The preliminary findings from BRCAAway also indicated that the combination of olaparib plus abiraterone as a first-line treatment in biomarker-selected patients with mCRPC was well tolerated [64]. Further analyses from PROfound and PROpel in the ITT populations have also indicated that the most commonly occurring AEs (including anemia, nausea, and fatigue) were typically Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events grade 1 and 2, peaked within the first 2 months of treatment, and were manageable with appropriate supportive care [57, 68]. Additional investigation of early, upfront treatment with olaparib or olaparib plus abiraterone on the occurrence and management of AEs in patients with BRCAm would be of interest to help further current understanding.

In summary, results from phase II and the pivotal phase III studies, PROfound and PROpel, support the use of olaparib as a monotherapy or in combination with abiraterone in patients with mCRPC with a BRCAm. To translate these results into practice, there is a need to test all patients, regardless of phenotype, before or immediately at the time of mCRPC diagnosis to inform prognosis and familial risk, and to identify those with a BRCAm who may benefit the most from receiving olaparib early in their mCRPC treatment.
