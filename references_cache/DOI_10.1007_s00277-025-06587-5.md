Case presentation
A 70-year-old white male with a past medical history of 
JAK2-PV progressing to myelofibrosis was referred for 
evaluation of abnormal bleeding, prior to placement of a 
central venous access device for an allo-HSCT. The patient 
had no prior personal or family history of abnormal bleed -
ing in childhood or as an adult and had undergone right total 
hip arthroplasty, inguinal hernia repair, open splenectomy 
and right total knee arthroplasty with no excessive bleeding 
Introduction
Inherited plasmatic bleeding disorders, in general, are con -
sidered rare bleeding disorders [ 1] and inherited platelet 
disorders (IPD) are considered ultra-rare bleeding disor -
ders [2]. IPDs can be classified based on platelet number, 
function, size, and abnormalities in platelet granule content 
[2]. Gray platelet syndrome (GPS), an IPD, is characterized 
by a variable bleeding phenotype, large platelets, and an 
absence or severe deficiency of platelet al.pha granules [ 3]. 
Inheritance is typically autosomal recessive, but cohorts of 
autosomal dominant transmission have been described [ 3]. 
Clinical management of bleeding includes platelet transfu -
sion or intravenous desmopressin (DDA VP) [3]. 
Acquired platelet function defects are likely more com -
mon than IPD and have a wide variety of underlying eti -
ologies [4]. Acquired GPS is extremely rare with only two

Abstract
We report the case of a 70-year-old male with a history of JAK2 mutation-positive post-polycythemia vera myelofi -
brosis and an acquired bleeding disorder. Investigations revealed platelet dysfunction, and platelet electron microscopy 
demonstrated an acquired gray platelet syndrome. His platelet function improved with desmopressin administration, and 
he successfully underwent a central venous catheter placement and hematopoietic stem cell transplant with no bleeding 
complications. Follow up testing demonstrated normalization of platelet function testing and resolution of his acquired 
gray platelet syndrome.

Discussion
Acquired platelet function disorders are common, but 
acquired GPS is rare with paucity of data in current litera -
ture. Acquired etiologies in addition to MPNs [ 6] included 
medication side effects (aspirin, clopidogrel, non-steroidal 
anti-inflammatory drugs, selective serotonin reuptake inhib-
itors [ 4, 7]), and idiopathic causes. In this case, we were 
convinced of an acquired GPS given the patient’s absence 
of abnormal bleeding in childhood, longstanding history 
of JAK2-PV , and confirmatory findings of GPS on PTEM. 
While the patient was on aspirin for some period of time, 
his second bleeding episode occurred spontaneously while 
off aspirin. Of note, the patient was taking ruxolitinib a few 
months before the time of the bleeding episodes, which 
could have also contributed to the platelet dysfunction.
Inherited GPS is typically inherited in an autosomal 
recessive fashion with germline homozygous mutations in 
the neurobeachinlike 2 ( NBEAL2) gene [ 8] that is respon -
sible for the genesis and retention of alpha granules in plate-
lets [9–11]. Patients typically experience bleeding episodes 
that are mild to moderate secondary to platelet dysfunc -
tion, including easy bruising, epistaxis, and heavier men -
strual bleeding [ 12]. However, severe bleeding has been 
noted in some patients [ 13]. Bone marrow fibrosis has also 
been described to be present in 88% of GPS patients [ 13]. 
Another characteristic feature noted in patients with GPS 
includes an elevated vitamin B12 level [13]. Acquired GPS 
is extremely rare, with 1 reported case by Ido et al. 2020 in

There are likely multiple underlying mechanisms that 
lead to the onset of acquired GPS in patients with MPNs, the 
main one being increasing inflammation caused by clonal 
megakaryocytes leading to bone marrow fibrosis. There is 
subsequent defective megakaryocyte production and plate -
let maturation, inducing platelet dysfunction with a loss of 
alpha granules [11].
Platelet transfusions have been used for management 
and described in the case of Ido et al. 2020 [ 6]. Our patient 
was managed with DDA VP prior to allo-HSCT, given the 
robust, positive response in his platelet aggregation tracings 
and its known beneficial mechanism of improving platelet 
adhesion and aggregation via increased production of von 
Willebrand factor and factor VIII.
This case highlights the importance of suspecting 
acquired GPS and the need for LTA and PTEM in making 
a diagnosis of acquired GPS especially in those with MPNs 
and an acquired bleeding disorder. To our knowledge, this 
is the first case to demonstrate normalization of the LTA in 
response to DDA VP, and normalization of PTEM post allo-
HSCT. Based on results of the donor chimerism testing, the 
circulating platelets are likely normal donor platelets post 
allo-HSCT.
