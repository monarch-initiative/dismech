</div></section></section><section><section><section><h2>Significance</h2>
<p>We hear sounds varying in intensity over six orders of magnitude using spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs), each of which changes its firing rates over only a fraction of this range. Somehow, the SGNs with different dynamic ranges collectively encode the full range of sound levels represented in the receptor potential of the inner hair cell (IHC) in the mammalian cochlea. Our study, combining subcellular imaging, mouse genetics, and auditory systems physiology, offers a unifying synaptic hypothesis for wide dynamic range sound encoding in the spiral ganglion. We propose that IHCs, from one receptor potential but via presynaptic active zones that vary in the voltage dependence of Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx, generate complementary codes on sound pressure level in functionally distinct SGNs.</p>
<section><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> auditory system, spiral ganglion neuron, dynamic range, synaptic strength, presynaptic heterogeneity</p></section></section><section><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>For sounds of a given frequency, spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs) with different thresholds and dynamic ranges collectively encode the wide range of audible sound pressures. Heterogeneity of synapses between inner hair cells (IHCs) and SGNs is an attractive candidate mechanism for generating complementary neural codes covering the entire dynamic range. Here, we quantified active zone (AZ) properties as a function of AZ position within mouse IHCs by combining patch clamp and imaging of presynaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx and by immunohistochemistry. We report substantial AZ heterogeneity whereby the voltage of half-maximal activation of Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx ranged over ∼20 mV. Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx at AZs facing away from the ganglion activated at weaker depolarizations. Estimates of AZ size and Ca<sup>2+</sup> channel number were correlated and larger when AZs faced the ganglion. Disruption of the deafness gene <em>GIPC3</em> in mice shifted the activation of presynaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx to more hyperpolarized potentials and increased the spontaneous SGN discharge. Moreover, <em>Gipc3</em> disruption enhanced Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx and exocytosis in IHCs, reversed the spatial gradient of maximal Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx in IHCs, and increased the maximal firing rate of SGNs at sound onset. We propose that IHCs diversify Ca<sup>2+</sup> channel properties among AZs and thereby contribute to decomposing auditory information into complementary representations in SGNs.</p></section><hr/>
<p>The auditory system enables us to perceive sound pressures that vary over six orders of magnitude. This is achieved by active amplification of cochlear vibrations at low sound pressures and compression at high sound pressures. The receptor potential of inner hair cells (IHCs) represents the full range (<a>1</a>), whereas each postsynaptic type I spiral ganglion neuron (hereafter termed SGN) encodes only a fraction (<a>2</a>–<a>6</a>). SGNs with comparable frequency tuning but different spontaneous spike rates and sound responses are thought to emanate from neighboring, if not the same, IHC at a given tonotopic position of the organ of Corti (<a>2</a>, <a>5</a>, <a>7</a>, <a>8</a>). Even in silence, IHC active zones (AZs) release glutamate at varying rates, evoking “spontaneous” spiking in SGNs. SGNs with greater spontaneous spike rates respond to softer sounds (high-spontaneous rate, low-threshold SGNs), than those with lower spontaneous spike rates (low-spontaneous rate, high-threshold SGNs) (<a>2</a>, <a>9</a>). This diversity likely underlies the representation of sounds across all audible sound pressure levels in the auditory nerve, to which neural adaptation also contributes (<a>10</a>).</p>
<p>How SGN diversity arises is poorly understood. Candidate mechanisms include the heterogeneity of ribbon synapses that differ in pre- and/or postsynaptic properties even within individual IHCs (<a>7</a>, <a>11</a>–<a>14</a>). IHC AZs vary in the number (<a>11</a>, <a>15</a>) and voltage dependence of gating (<a>11</a>) of their Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels regardless of tonotopic position (<a>16</a>). Lateral olivocochlear efferent projections to the SGNs regulate postsynaptic excitability (<a>17</a>) and contribute to the establishment of a gradient of presynaptic ribbon size along the “modiolar–pillar” axis (<a>18</a>), where the modiolar side faces the ganglion and the pillar side is away from the ganglion, toward the pillar cells.</p>
<p>In postnatal development of the mouse, high-spontaneous-rate SGNs coemerge with AZs that exhibit stronger maximal Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx. Moreover, IHCs deficient for the AZ protein bassoon lack the population of AZs with strongest Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx and, concurrently lack SGNs with high spontaneous rates (<a>19</a>). Based on these correlations and given the eminent role of presynaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx in controlling synaptic strength (<a>20</a>–<a>22</a>), we proposed that the varying Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx at a given IHC AZ largely determines the difference in spontaneous and evoked spiking among SGNs (<a>19</a>). Larger and more complex AZs (<a>7</a>, <a>13</a>, <a>14</a>) with stronger Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx (<a>16</a>) tend to reside on the modiolar IHC side. However, according to classical findings from the cat cochlea, modiolar synapses seem weaker as they drive low-spontaneous-rate, high-threshold SGNs (<a>2</a>, <a>7</a>). This discrepancy suggests that factors other than AZ size and amplitude of Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx contribute to differences in SGN properties.</p>
<p>Here, we live-imaged most, if not all, AZs of individual IHCs for analyzing the amplitude and voltage dependence of Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx as well as AZ size and position within the IHC. Combined with immunohistochemical estimation of ribbon size and Ca<sup>2+</sup>-channel abundance of AZs, our data indicate opposing gradients of maximal amplitude and voltage dependence of Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx along the modiolar–pillar axis: modiolar AZs, thought to drive low-spontaneous-rate, high-threshold SGNs, are, on average, larger and have more Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels but operate in a more depolarized range. We propose that the more hyperpolarized activation range of Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx at pillar AZs poises them to enable high spontaneous rates and low sound thresholds of SGN firing. We studied candidate regulators of Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx at IHC AZs and found that disruption of the deafness gene <em>GIPC3</em> in mice causes a hyperpolarizing shift of Ca<sup>2+</sup>-channel activation and increases spontaneous SGN firing.</p>
<section><h2>Results</h2>
<section><h3>Heterogeneity of Presynaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> Influx Among the AZs of IHCs.</h3>
<p>The strength of presynaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx and the large (∼2 µm) distance between hair cell AZs (<a>16</a>, <a>23</a>) enable spatiotemporally resolved optical analysis of presynaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> signaling (<a>11</a>, <a>24</a>, <a>25</a>). However, given the limited duration of stable Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx in whole-cell patch-clamp recordings, the low speed of laser-scanning confocal microscopy had prohibited a comprehensive comparison of the Ca<sup>2+</sup>-influx properties among the AZs within a given IHC. To overcome this technical limitation we combined spinning disk microscopy with fast piezoelectric focusing to sequentially and rapidly (frame rate 100 Hz) image confocal IHC sections. This enabled the analysis of voltage dependence and maximal amplitude of the Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx, visualized as local fluorescence increase of the low-affinity Ca<sup>2+</sup> indicator Fluo-8FF (<em>K</em><sub>d</sub> = 10 µM) at most if not all synapses of a given IHC (<a>Fig. 1<em>A</em></a>) while the IHC Ca<sup>2+</sup> current remained stable (rundown &lt;25%; a few IHCs were excluded from the analysis because of rundown &gt;25%).</p>

<p>Do larger AZs contain more Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels and display stronger maximal Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx? <p>Maximal AZ Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx showed a positive correlation with the RIBEYE-peptide fluorescence in live-imaging experiments, suggesting that, indeed, larger AZs exhibit stronger maximal Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx (<a>Fig. 1<em>G</em></a>, green). <p>Next, we examined the voltage dependence of synaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx for most if not all AZs of a given IHC. The mean V<sub>0.5</sub> of synaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx at 210 AZs of these IHCs (−27.4 mV) was very similar to that of the mean whole-cell Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx. <p>How does the IHC, a compact presynaptic cell, establish heterogeneity of Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx among its AZs? Here, we considered two classes of candidate mechanisms: (<em>i</em>) differences in the subunit composition of the Ca<sup>2+</sup>-channel complex and (<em>ii</em>) different interaction partners. The pore-forming Ca<sub>V</sub>1.3α and the auxiliary Ca<sub>V</sub>β<sub>2</sub> are the dominating subunits of IHC Ca<sup>2+</sup>-channel complexes, but other subunits contribute (<a>27</a>, <a>28</a>, <a>32</a>, <a>33</a>). Differences in the biophysical properties of Ca<sub>V</sub>1.3 Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels among IHC AZs might arise from the preferential abundance of specific auxiliary subunits (<a>32</a>, <a>33</a>) or splice variants of the pore-forming α subunit (<a>34</a>, <a>35</a>).</p>
<p>Indeed, analysis of splice variants with short C terminus or long C terminus, both expressed by IHCs (<a>36</a>), in HEK293 cells revealed more hyperpolarized activation and higher open probability for short splice variants of the channel (<a>34</a>, <a>35</a>). Here, we studied synaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx in IHCs of knock-in mice expressing the pore-forming Ca<sub>V</sub>1.3α with an HA-tag in exon 49 (<a>36</a>). The HA-tag disrupts the C-terminal automodulatory domain in the long Ca<sub>V</sub>1.3α splice variant. This preserves potential C-terminal protein interaction domains but when expressed in HEK293 cells functionally converts the gating of “long” splice variants to that of “short” ones. Therefore, we expected to find more hyperpolarized activation of synaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx. However, the mean V<sub>0.5</sub> (<em>t</em> test) and its variance (Levene’s test) were unchanged (<a>Fig. 5 <em>A</em>–<em>C</em></a>), whereas the maximal AZ Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx (<a>Fig. 5 <em>D</em> and <em>E</em></a>, <em>P</em> &lt; 0.005, Wilcoxon rank sum test) and whole-cell Ca<sup>2+</sup> current (<a>Fig. 5<em>F</em></a>) were larger. Next, we studied sound encoding at the single SGN level in <em>Ca</em><sub><em>V</em></sub><em>1.3</em><sup><em>HA/HA</em></sup> mice and found a normal distribution of spontaneous firing rates (<a>Fig. 5<em>G</em></a>), normal sound-evoked firing rates (<a>Fig. 5<em>H</em></a>), and normal sound-pressure dependence of firing (<a>Fig. 5<em>I</em></a>). The observed lack of effect of the C-terminal Ca<sub>V</sub>1.3α manipulation on the voltage dependence of Ca<sup>2+</sup> current activation may be due to strong regulation of Ca<sub>V</sub>1.3 in IHCs by interactions with Ca<sup>2+</sup>-binding proteins (<a>37</a>–<a>39</a>). The increased maximal Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx, likely resulting from an increased open probability (<a>34</a>), apparently did not change SGN firing behavior.</p>

<em>Gipc3</em> Disruption Causes a Hyperpolarized Shift in Ca<sup>2+</sup> Channel Activation and Enhances Spontaneous SGN Firing.</h3>
<p>Another PDZ protein and candidate regulator of Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels and AZ heterogeneity is Gipc3 (GAIP interacting protein, C terminus 3). Defects of the human <em>GIPC3</em> gene cause human deafness (<a>48</a>, <a>49</a>) and <em>Gipc3</em> disruption in mice lead to audiogenic seizures and progressive hearing loss (<a>48</a>). GIPC proteins share a central PDZ domain flanked by GIPC homologous domains. Gipc1 has broad roles in intracellular protein trafficking and in hair cells is required for development of stereociliary bundles and planar cell polarity (<a>50</a>–<a>52</a>). We studied Black Swiss (BLSW, hereafter <em>Gipc3</em> mutant) mice that carry a missense mutation in the <em>Gipc3</em> gene (c.343G &gt; A), which replaces a highly conserved glycine with an arginine at position 115 (Gly115Arg) located within the PDZ domain. Early-onset hearing impairment in Gipc3 mutant mice has been attributed to dysfunction of hair cell stereocilia, although its localization within hair cells resembles a cytoplasmic vesicular pattern similar to myosin VI (<a>48</a>). In the brain, glutamate release was shown to depend on interaction between myosin VI and its binding partner Gipc1 (<a>50</a>). In hair cells, a synaptic function for Gipc proteins is yet unknown.</p>
<p>In <em>Gipc3</em> mutant IHCs we found an increased whole-cell Ca<sup>2+</sup> current (<a>Fig. 6 <em>A</em> and <em>D</em></a>, <em>P</em> &lt; 0.05, Wilcoxon rank sum test) and a more hyperpolarized V<sub>0.5</sub> of activation [<a>Fig. 6<em>B</em></a>, <em>P</em> = 0.004, Wilcoxon rank sum test; <em>Gipc3</em> mutant IHCs: V<sub>0.5</sub> = −31.2 ± 1.4 mV, <em>n</em> = 19 compared with C57BL/6J control IHCs (WT): V<sub>0.5</sub> = −24.1 ± 1.8 mV, <em>n</em> = 20]. Moreover, we found Ca<sup>2+</sup> current inactivation to be reduced in <em>Gipc3</em> mutants when comparing Ca<sup>2+</sup> currents elicited by 500-ms-long depolarizations (I<sub>res</sub>/I<sub>peak</sub> 0.59 ± 0.04 for 10 <em>Gipc3</em> mutant IHCs vs. 0.41 ± 0.03 for 8 WT IHCs, <em>P</em> = 0.007, Wilcoxon rank sum test). We also observed enhanced exocytosis reported as membrane capacitance increments (<a>Fig. 6 <em>C</em> and <em>D</em></a>, significant for 50- and 100-ms-long depolarizations), likely as a consequence of the increased Ca<sup>2+</sup> current. At the AZ level, the distributions of maximal Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx (<a>Fig. 7<em>A</em></a>) and RIBEYE-peptide fluorescence (<a>Fig. 7<em>B</em></a>) and were comparable to WT, but the V<sub>0.5</sub> distribution was broader in <em>Gipc3</em> mutant IHCs and AZs activated at more hyperpolarized potentials on average (<a>Fig. 6<em>C</em></a>, <em>P</em> &lt; 0.01 for variance, Brown–Forsythe test and <em>P</em> &lt; 0.01 for mean, Wilcoxon rank sum test). Opposite that in WT, maximal AZ Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx (<a>Fig. 7<em>D</em></a>) exhibited a pillar–modiolar gradient in <em>Gipc3</em> mutants. As a result, the pillar AZs, operating at even more hyperpolarized potentials than in WT, also had stronger maximal Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx on average (<a>Fig. 7 <em>D</em>–<em>F</em></a>).</p>

<section><h3>Animals.</h3>
<p>C57BL/6 mice of either sex were used for hair cell physiology and morphology at p14–20 (<a>Figs. 1</a>–<a>4</a>). <em>Ca</em><sub><em>V</em></sub><em>1.3</em><sup><em>HA/HA</em></sup> mice (<a>36</a>) and littermate controls were provided by Jörg Striessnig, University of Innsbruck, Innsbruck, Austria, and used at p14–18 (patch clamp) and 6–8 wk (in vivo). <em>Dfcr</em> mice (<a>46</a>), Jax (Balb/C background), and littermates were used at p14–17. <em>Gipc3</em> mutants (<a>48</a>) were compared with age-matched C57BL/6 at p14–27 (<a>19</a>).</p></section><section><h3>Patch Clamp and Confocal Ca<sup>2+</sup> Imaging.</h3>
<p>Apical cochleae (first 2/3 turns) were freshly dissected. Inner border cells and phalangeal cells were gently removed by a suction pipette, exposing an IHC basolateral membrane. The patch pipette solution contained (in millimolar) 123 Cs-glutamate, 1 MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 1 CaCl<sub>2</sub>, 10 EGTA, 13 tetraethylammonium (TEA)-Cl, 20 Hepes, 2 MgATP, and 0.3 NaGTP (pH 7.3). For live imaging, it contained the Ca<sup>2+</sup> indicator Fluo-8FF (0.8 mM; AAT Bioquest) and the TAMRA-conjugated CtBP2/RIBEYE-binding dimer peptide (20 µM; Biosynthan). For <a>Fig. S5</a>, we used 111 mM <span>l</span>-glutamate, 4 mM MgATP, 1 mM glutathione, 0.8 mM Fluo-4FF penta-K<sup>+</sup> salt (Life Technologies), and 10 µM TAMRA-conjugated CtBP2/RIBEYE-binding dimer peptide. IHCs were continuously superfused with an extracellular solution containing (in millimolar) 102.2 NaCl, 2.8 KCl, 1 MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 5 CaCl<sub>2</sub>, 35 TEA-Cl, 10 Hepes, and 2 g/L glucose (pH 7.2). An EPC-10 amplifier and Patchmaster software (HEKA Elektronik) were used and Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx was evoked by step depolarizations from −87 mV to −7 mV or ramp depolarizations from −87 mV to 63 mV. All voltages were corrected for liquid junction potential (17 mV) and voltage drops across series resistance (R<sub>s</sub>). IHCs were held at −87 mV. Recordings were discarded when holding current exceeded 50 pA, R<sub>s</sub> exceeded 10 MΩ within 4 min after break-in, or rundown of Ca<sup>2+</sup> current exceeded 25%. Ca<sup>2+</sup> imaging was performed with a spinning disk confocal scanner (CSU22; Yokogawa) mounted on an upright microscope (Axio Examiner; Zeiss) with 63×, 1.0 N.A. objective (W Plan-Apochromat; Zeiss). Images were acquired by a back-illuminated CCD camera with 80 × 80 pixels within a 19.2- × 19.2-mm CCD chip (NeuroCCD; Redshirt Imaging) or a scientific complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor camera (Neo; Andor). Ca<sup>2+</sup> indicators and TAMRA-conjugated peptide were excited by diode-pumped solid-state lasers with 491-nm and 561-nm wavelength, respectively (Cobolt AB). The spinning disk was set to 2,000 rpm to synchronize with the 10-ms acquisition time of the camera. Using a piezo positioner for the objective (Piezosystem), focal planes were acquired in an order randomized in each cell to avoid systematic effects of fluorophore bleaching. C<sub>m</sub> measurements were performed as previously described (<a>69</a>) and exocytic C<sub>m</sub> changes calculate from the difference of C<sub>m</sub> before and after the depolarization (400 ms, skipping the initial 40 ms).</p></section><section><h3>Immunohistochemistry and Confocal Imaging.</h3>
<p>The organ of Corti apical 2/3 turn was prepared for “whole-mount” imaging as described in ref. <a>70</a>, but the protocol was modified after the secondary antibody rinse. To minimize differences in attenuation at different focal depths, tissue was embedded in 2,2′-thiodiethanol (<a>71</a>). The fit was used to calculate the fractional activation curve, dividing it by the extrapolated maximal ΔF/ΔV estimated by linear fitting of the decay of fluorescence with voltage. Then, this trace was fitted by a Boltzman function to estimate the V<sub>h</sub> and slope factor of the Ca<sup>2+</sup>-indicator fluorescence at a given AZ. Synaptic ribbon fluorescence was estimated as the ratio of TAMRA fluorescence to that of the nearby IHC cytoplasm typically eight or nine pixels away (F<sub>ribbon</sub>/F<sub>nearby</sub>), measuring the pixel with strongest intensity. IHC cytoplasmic volumes were displayed with the plugin “3D viewer” in Fiji software.</p>
<p>To analyze the intensity and position of synapses in confocal images of fixed organ of Corti whole mounts, a customized algorithm was developed as a program in MATLAB software. The locations of synapses were defined as the centers of mass of fluorescent spots after thresholding by a subjective intensity criterion for each of the three channels. The average voxel intensity in the volume, excluding voxels that exceeded the threshold value, was subtracted as background. Gaussian functions were fitted in three dimensions to determine the center of mass of each cluster. Immunofluorescence intensities were measured on each channel as the sum of the voxel values within a defined region of interest (± 500, 500, and 835 nm or ± 10, 10, and 2 pixels in X, Y, and Z) with origin at the center of mass of each GluA2-positive cluster. After marking the center of each IHC nucleus, observing the relative orientation of the presynaptic ribbon and the postsynaptic receptor array allowed us to assign each synapse to an IHC. The Cartesian coordinates of synapses were transformed to cell-centric cylindrical coordinates to define the cellular axes and to adjust differences in cellular orientation relative to the XYZ axes of the microscope. Keeping one end of the line centered on the nucleus, the angle of the axis was adjusted once in the XY plane and once in the YZ plane to compensate for the pitch and yaw of each hair cell (<a>Fig. S3</a>). After adjustment, points along the central axis were defined as zero on the tonotopic and modiolar–pillar axes. Intensities of synaptic puncta as a function of position could be analyzed for multiple cells by overlaying their central axes with alignment to the center of each nucleus.</p>

<p>The ear encodes changes in sound pressure over six orders of magnitude using SGNs that change their firing rates over different fractions of the audible range, but the mechanisms underlying such dynamic range fractionation are unknown. Therefore, we used fluorescence imaging to characterize heterogeneity among AZs in cochlear IHCs as a candidate mechanism. Our study indicates that IHCs decompose information on sound intensity into different outputs by varying the Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx among their AZs. Two key determinants of presynaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx, the voltage dependence and the number of Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels, differed greatly among the AZs within IHCs and exhibited shallow opposing spatial gradients along the pillar–modiolar IHC axis. AZs of the pillar face, on average, were smaller but activated at more hyperpolarized potentials and, hence, are likely presynaptic to high-spontaneous-rate, low-threshold SGNs. The number of Ca<sub>V</sub>1.3 Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels and maximal synaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx, on average, were greater for modiolar AZs, which also were larger in size. We propose that these AZs, given their more depolarized operation range, are recruited by stronger sounds and likely drive low-spontaneous-rate, high-threshold SGNs. Disruption of <em>Gipc3</em> reversed the normal modiolar–pillar gradient of maximal AZ Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx, shifted Ca<sup>2+</sup> channel activation to more hyperpolarized potentials, and increased the fraction of SGNs with high-spontaneous firing rate.</p>
<section><h3>Dynamic Range Fractionation Through Heterogeneity of Synaptic Voltage Dependence.</h3>
<p>Each presynaptic AZ in a given IHC is controlled by a common potential and provides the sole excitatory input to “its” postsynaptic SGN. How the operating range of Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx at an AZ matches the IHC resting and receptor potential critically determines its transmitter release and hence the spontaneous and sound-evoked firing of the postsynaptic SGN. Because technically challenging in vivo tight-seal patch-clamp recordings from IHCs have not yet been achieved there is uncertainty about the resting (in quiescence) and the receptor (during sound stimulation) potential. Pioneering recordings with sharp electrodes from guinea pig IHCs indicated a resting potential of −40 mV and maximal receptor potentials between ∼10 and 30 mV (<a>1</a>), and a patch-clamp study trying to emulate physiological ionic conditions in the explanted gerbil organ of Corti estimated a resting potential of −55 mV (<a>55</a>). Regardless of the precise resting potential it is evident that Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx is partially active at least at a subset of IHC AZs (<a>Fig. 3</a>) and the relatively depolarized IHC resting potential is expected to facilitate Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx and transmitter release (<a>56</a>, <a>57</a>). Moreover, the range covered by Ca<sup>2+</sup> activation at the various AZs (V<sub>0.5</sub> spanning from −38 mV to −18 mV) matches the reported range of IHC receptor potentials very well (<a>Fig. 3</a>). The SGNs with identical frequency tuning but different sound-response properties presumably receive input from the same IHC and collectively convey acoustic information across the entire audible range of sound pressures to the brain. Candidate mechanisms underlying the diversity of SGN response properties include presynaptic (<a>7</a>, <a>11</a>, <a>12</a>), postsynaptic (<a>13</a>), and efferent (<a>17</a>, <a>18</a>) mechanisms. Here, we asked how IHCs might fractionate the auditory signal for different parallel outputs through heterogeneous Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx among AZs. Fast 3D live imaging enabled us to analyze functional and morphological properties of most, if not all, AZs as a function of position within an individual IHC. In parallel, we performed semiquantitative confocal microscopy of immunolabeled IHC synapses. AZs differed considerably in size, number, and voltage dependence of Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels whereby differences among AZs within individual IHCs explained most of the variance of the entire AZ population. The estimates for Ca<sup>2+</sup>-channel number were positively correlated with our proxy of AZ size in both live-imaging and immunohistochemistry experiments, even at the level of the single IHC.</p>
<p>Modiolar AZs were larger, had more Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels, and tended to show stronger maximal Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx. The hypothesis that these AZs drive SGNs with lower spontaneous firing rate and higher sound threshold, at first sight, seems counterintuitive because a greater number of Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels is expected to elicit more spontaneous and evoked release. However, we found that modiolar AZs, on average, required more depolarization for their activation (<a>Fig. 4</a>), consistent with the idea that modiolar synapses drive high-threshold SGNs (<a>2</a>, <a>7</a>, <a>8</a>). Modiolar AZs that hold more Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels may support higher maximal rates of transmitter release, which could explain the higher susceptibility of low-spontaneous-rate, high-threshold SGNs to excitotoxic insult during acoustic overexposure (<a>58</a>). The operation of pillar AZ at relatively hyperpolarized potentials may contribute to high-spontaneous firing rates and responses to soft sounds found for SGNs innervating the pillar side (<a>2</a>, <a>7</a>). Based on the spatial distribution of the voltage dependence of presynaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx found here for mice, and the spatial distribution of postsynaptic spontaneous rates in cats, we hypothesize that the voltage dependence of presynaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx is a major determinant of spontaneous and sound-driven SGN firing. This hypothesis is supported by the more hyperpolarized V<sub>0.5</sub> of AZ Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx in IHCs (<a>Fig. 7</a>) and the increase in SGN spontaneous firing rates in <em>Gipc3</em> mutant mice (<a>Fig. 8</a>), but not in in <em>Ca</em><sub><em>V</em></sub><em>1.3</em><sup><em>HA/HA</em></sup> mice, for which, too, we found more AZ Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx but no change in voltage dependence. However, we note that different from classic experiments in mature cats (<a>2</a>, <a>7</a>) the present data were obtained from mice and we cannot rule out some immaturity of the afferent synaptic connectivity because we recorded soon after the onset of hearing. Moreover, the variability of voltage dependence of presynaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx was high in both the modiolar and pillar halves, whereby the differences between V<sub>0.5</sub> among modiolar or pillar synapses could exceed that between the average pillar and modiolar synapse. In addition, the spatial segregation of functionally distinct classes of type SGNs at the level of IHC innervation in mice might deviate from that described for cats. Future experiments simultaneously addressing presynaptic properties and postsynaptic firing will be required to further test our hypothesis.</p></section><section><h3>Regulation of the Number and Voltage Dependence of Ca<sup>2+</sup> Channels at IHC AZs.</h3>
<p>Which mechanisms determine the number and voltage dependence of Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels at an AZ? The number of Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels at the presynaptic AZ is governed by the expression of the specific Ca<sup>2+</sup>-channel subunits and splice variants (<a>33</a>, <a>36</a>, <a>59</a>) as well as by that of scaffold proteins that tether Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels to the AZ (<a>15</a>, <a>42</a>, <a>43</a>, <a>60</a>, <a>61</a>) and/or regulate their turnover (<a>44</a>) by direct or indirect interaction with the channels. Moreover, subunit composition as well as splice variants and interacting proteins also modulate functional properties of the specific Ca<sup>2+</sup> channel (<a>62</a>). The Ca<sup>2+</sup>-channel complex at IHC AZs is likely composed of splice variants of the pore-forming subunit Ca<sub>V</sub>1.3α containing exons 43S or 43L (<a>36</a>), of the Ca<sub>V</sub>β2 (<a>33</a>), and less likely of other Ca<sub>V</sub>β subunits (<a>32</a>), and of a yet-to-be-identified Ca<sub>V</sub>α2δ subunit. Ca<sub>V</sub>β2, the synaptic ribbon, and the presynaptic scaffolds bassoon and RIM2α and β were previously shown to promote the abundance of Ca<sub>V</sub>1.3 at IHC AZs (<a>15</a>, <a>33</a>, <a>43</a>), whereas harmonin seems to reduce the number of synaptic Ca<sub>V</sub>1.3 (<a>44</a>). Work in zebrafish hair cells has revealed an intriguing molecular interplay between RIBEYE and Ca<sub>V</sub>1.3 channels, whereby RIBEYE overexpression promotes the formation of Ca<sub>V</sub>1.3-positive AZ-like specializations and synaptic Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx negatively regulates RIBEYE abundance at the AZ (<a>63</a>, <a>64</a>). The present study corroborates the notion that the Ca<sup>2+</sup>-channel number is proportional to ribbon size in cochlear hair cells (<a>11</a>, <a>30</a>). We speculate that release rate for a given open probability scales linearly with the number of Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels, assuming nanodomain coupling (<a>29</a>, <a>65</a>, <a>66</a>), regardless of the AZ size. Future simultaneous measurements of AZ Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx and exocytosis or SGN firing will be required to analyze the consequences of presynaptic heterogeneity for transmitter release at IHC AZs.</p>
<p>How do IHCs form opposing gradients of AZ size, Ca<sub>V</sub>1.3 abundance, and voltage dependence of activation? The modiolar–pillar gradient of AZ size was lost upon lesion of the lateral olivocochlear efferents (<a>18</a>), and it is tempting to speculate that they provide an instructive influence on the postsynaptic SGN terminal and/or the IHC AZ. Here, we found that the intracellular gradient of maximal Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx was altered in <em>Gipc3</em> mutant mice. In addition, <em>Gipc3</em> disruption increased Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx and caused a hyperpolarizing shift in its activation. These presynaptic changes likely underlie the enhanced spontaneous and sound-onset firing in <em>Gipc3</em> mutant mice, although alternative explanations exist. How the Gipc3 protein might be involved directly or indirectly for establishing a modiolar–pillar gradient remains to be elucidated. Considering analogy to Gipc1 (<a>50</a>, <a>52</a>), it is tempting to speculate that Gipc3 serves to adapt components of the Ca<sup>2+</sup>-channel complex to motor proteins and thereby assists their trafficking to AZs with a preference for the modiolar face. Signals instructing polarized trafficking might originate from lateral olivocochlear fibers, SGNs themselves, and/or the planar cell polarity that sets the orientation of the apical hair bundle (<a>67</a>, <a>68</a>) and involves Gipc1 signaling (<a>50</a>). Future work should address the mechanism by which Gipc3 operates to traffic and/or regulate Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels.</p></section></section><section><h2>Methods</h2>
