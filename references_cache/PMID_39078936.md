# Conditions Leading to Ketene Formation in Vaping Devices and Implications for Public Health.
**Authors:** Wang P, Jacob P 3rd, Wang ZM, Fowles J, O'Shea DF, Wagner J, Kumagai K
**Journal:** Chem Res Toxicol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1021/acs.chemrestox.4c00190](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.chemrestox.4c00190)

## Content

The outbreak of e-cigarette or vaping use-associated lung injury (EVALI) in the United States in 2019 led to a total of 2807 hospitalizations with 68 deaths. While the exact causes of this vaping-related lung illness are still being debated, laboratory analyses of products from victims of EVALI have shown that vitamin E acetate (VEA), an additive in some tetrahydrocannabinol (THC)-containing products, is strongly linked to the EVALI outbreak. Because of its similar appearance and viscosity to pure THC oil, VEA was used as a diluent agent in cannabis oils in illicit markets. A potential mechanism for EVALI may involve VEA's thermal decomposition product, ketene, a highly poisonous gas, being generated under vaping conditions. In this study, a novel approach was developed to evaluate ketene production from VEA vaping under measurable temperature conditions in real-world devices. Ketene in generated aerosols was captured by two different chemical agents and analyzed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) and liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). The LC-MS/MS method takes advantage of the high sensitivity and specificity of tandem mass spectrometry and appears to be more suitable than GC-MS for the analysis of large batches of samples. Our results confirmed the formation of ketene when VEA was vaped. The production of ketene increased with repeat puffs and showed a correlation to temperatures (200 to 500 °C) measured within vaping devices. Device battery power strength, which affects the heating temperature, plays an important role in ketene formation. In addition to ketene, the organic oxidant duroquinone was also obtained as another thermal degradation product of VEA. Ketene was not detected when vitamin E was vaped under the same conditions, confirming the importance of the acetate group for its generation.

The e-cigarette or vaping use-associated lung injury (EVALI) epidemic of 2019 resulted in 2807 hospitalized patients with 68 deaths. Laboratory analyses of vaping products from victims showed that vitamin E acetate (VEA), an additive in some tetrahydrocannabinol (THC)-containing vaping products, was strongly linked to the affected cases.1 One of the patients that died in the epidemic was a 17-year-old boy from Detroit, who had severe pulmonary damage following vaping of a VEA-containing product.2

The investigation of the EVALI epidemic also brought to light the putative role that highly toxic gaseous ketene could play as a causal factor of acute respiratory disease.3-5 Theoretical and empirical studies of VEA thermolysis first demonstrated that ketene is formed from VEA under real-world vaping conditions3 and were subsequently confirmed by others.6 The commonality of thermal degradation chemistry for acetates and some esters has led to an increase in the detection of ketene formation due to vaping action on several other chemicals used in vaping products.7,8

Ethenone (compound 1 in Figure 1) is the simplest of the ketenes and a highly reactive chemical known to be formed from the high-temperature transformation of acetic acid, acetates, and other starting materials. These reactions were first investigated in 1905 by Staudinger,9 and the first industrial production was in the 1920s where heating to temperatures in the range of 735–755 °C was used to affect conversions of acetic acid into ketene.10 Subsequent investigations discovered that when these reactions were performed in the presence of a metal or other catalytic surfaces, the activating temperature required for ketene formation could be dramatically lowered.11,12 Metal oxides of iron, titanium, copper, and silica have been shown to give high conversions of acetic acid at temperatures as low as 355 °C. The catalytic pathway is the adsorption of acetic acid onto the metal surface with subsequent deprotonation to acetate from which the on-surface transformation to ketene and water occurs, following which desorption releases ketene and water.13 Ketene formation from phenyl acetate has been found to occur at temperatures as low as 250 °C when catalyzed by metal halide clusters.14 Until recently, ketenes have not factored prominently into public health considerations for exposures and risk assessments due to their transient nature and reactivity with water, mitigating their potential risks. They are mainly synthesized in closed reactors as reactive organic intermediates in industry. However, occupational exposures still occur, and the toxicological effects of ketenes have been recognized and characterized by the U.S. EPA AEGL committee.15 Case studies have demonstrated the type and severity of effects that occur.16 More recently, high concentrations of atmospheric ketene could be detected in the emission plumes from a petrochemical facility in South Korea using airborne time-of-flight mass spectrometry.17 This previously undocumented source of ketene highlights the urgent need for research into the detection of unknown sources of this elusive toxicant.

Vaping devices employ heating filaments that have metallic components that include nickel, chromium, iron, and aluminum.18 These metals, including copper, titanium, silver, and other metal oxides, are known to serve as catalysts in the production of ketene under industrial application conditions.19-21 Indications are emerging that the metal components within vaping devices play a role in ketene formation, with reports showing that the thermal decomposition of VEA occurs at 356 °C in vaping, considerably lower than in a laboratory tube furnace (800–1200 °C), which does not contain metal components.22 Ketene is difficult to measure using conventional chromatography from collected air samples due to its highly reactive and transient nature. During public health investigations on VEA in the context of vaping product use, it became clear that methods for measuring and quantifying ketenes under conditions of actual use were needed to better understand the variable factors affecting the quantities of ketene being produced under conditions of use. Ideally, methods can be developed and employed to assess the ability of ketenes to form from any vaping device and ingredients to ensure that this important aspect of product safety is assessed and controlled.

Table 1 illustrates the extreme potency of ketene in causing severe respiratory effects and lethality over a short acute exposure period. In the table, AEGLs represent threshold exposure limits (ppm or μg/m3) for exposure periods ranging from 10 min (min) to 8 h (h). AEGL-1 represents notable discomfort, irritation, or certain asymptomatic, nonsensory effects; AEGL-2 represents irreversible or other serious, long-lasting adverse health effects or an impaired ability to escape; and AEGL-3 represents life-threatening health effects or death.

Ketene’s mode of action is similar to that of the chemical warfare agent phosgene, namely, indiscriminate acylation of proteins and other macromolecules, leading to disruption of the blood–air barrier, edema, and inflammation. The alteration of proteins in this manner can trigger an immunological reaction, which results in cytokine release and scarring of tissue.5

As an acylating agent, it can be surmised that low-level ketene exposure would acylate and thus alter proteins, with the consequent formation of haptens and allergens. An associated immune response, damage to cellular membranes, and increases in localized inflammation would be expected to follow.24 It is notable that the EVALI patients experienced delayed onset of acute respiratory distress,25 a characteristic also seen with ketene and phosgene exposures. One case report described a chemical industry worker who developed hypoxic respiratory failure and diffuse ground glass opacities on chest imaging, consistent with acute respiratory distress syndrome, hours after a 5-min exposure to a highly concentrated mixture of ketene and crotonaldehyde.4

As reported by Rebulli et al.,25 although VEA has been suspected as a primary chemical responsible for EVALI outbreaks, it is unclear whether EVALI is associated with any one toxicant or biological pathway. In this study, we aimed to investigate the potential contribution of the poisonous gas ketene (produced due to thermal degradation of VEA immediately prior to lung inhalation) to EVALI disease. Two different chemical agents, benzylamine and N-methylaniline, were employed to capture generated ketene gas when vaping VEA using retailed vaping devices with vitamin E (VE) used as a control. The derivative bench stable ketene adducts, N-benzylacetamide and N-methylacetanilide, were analyzed using liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) with chemical ionization. Figure 2 shows the derivatization schemes of ketene with benzylamine (Figure 2a) and N-methylaniline (Figure 2b). The elemental composition of the heating filament and working temperature were also characterized. To prevent another EVALI from happening and to avoid repeated low-level acylation of lung proteins resulting in chronic disease, it is essential that analytical methods for ketene gas be developed to capture this reactive and elusive chemical to inform product safety. In summary, the focus of this study is to investigate the conditions leading to ketene formation during the vaping process and provide a practical approach for the evaluation of ketene exposure.

Figure 3 shows a diagram of the vaping experiment and aerosol generation setup (photograph shown in Figure S1). A Gram’s Universal Vaping Machine (Gram Health Inc., Oakland, CA) was used to generate aerosols. All flow-path surfaces and the syringe that contacted vapors were replaceable and were connected by Tygon tubing (SKC-West Inc., Fullerton, CA). The vaping process was controlled by software embedded in the vaping machine, following the CORESTA (Cooperation Centre for Scientific Research Relative to Tobacco) Research Method 81 vaping regimes (puff volume 55 mL, puff duration 3 s, and puff interval at 30 s) except that the exhalation duration was set for 10 s to allow aerosols to have sufficient time for reaction with the trapping agent. Vaping experiments with 10, 20, 30, and 40 puffs were conducted. Each puffing event was conducted on different days, and the order of the experiments was randomized with respect to number of puffs. After the syringe pump withdrew an inhalation, the 3-way valve switched (Figure 3) and expelled the aerosols to pass through the two sequential glass impingers (SKC-West Inc.). Before each vaping test, each impinger was filled with 2 mL of a trapping solution. Two different trapping agent solutions were tested to capture generated ketene. Trapping solution 1 contained 150 μL of N-benzylamine (≥99.0%) in 2 mL of dichloromethane (≥99.8%) (Sigma-Aldrich Inc., St. Louis, MO). Trapping solution 2 contained 20 μL of N-methylaniline (≥99%, Sigma-Aldrich) in 2 mL of chloroform (≥99%, Sigma-Aldrich). The reason for using the lower concentration of N-methylaniline in the second trapping solution was to minimize any potential direct reaction of the solution with VEA. Overall, 10–15 glass beads with a diameter of 5.45 ± 0.37 mm were added to each impinger to prevent trapping solutions from escaping from the impingers during vaping. When each vaping episode was complete, the trapping solution was transferred to a 2 mL glass volumetric flask. An additional trapping solution was added to fill up the flask if needed to compensate the evaporated solvent during the vaping process. This trapping solution was immediately transferred to an amber glass vial and stored at 4 °C in a refrigerator until GC-MS or LC-MS/MS analysis.

Pure VEA (≥99%) and VE (≥96%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Inc. without further purification before use. They were used as vaping liquids to generate aerosols. Vaping cartridges tested in this study were collected from students in California public high schools during 2019.26 These cartridges were 510 thread type and were prefilled with cannabis oils. A picture of these devices is shown in Figure 4. After analysis of these oils, the cartridges were emptied, thoroughly cleaned with isopropanol (IPA), and dried in a 100 °C oven. Then, VEA or VE was added to these cartridges. The consumed VEA or VE for each vaping episode was recorded by weighing the device before and after vaping. Three types of heating batteries were tested: battery-1 is an original disposable type for cannabis oil vaping, which was collected from students;26 while battery-2 and battery-3 were rechargeable with output powers of 2.6 and 3.7 V, respectively. They were purchased online in January of 2022. Battery-2 was advertised to be fit for 510 cartridges and was ideal for vaporizing THC and CBD oils. Battery-3 was also advertised to be fit for 510 cartridges but did not mention if it is specifically for cannabis or nicotine vaping. All 3 batteries had a 510 thread, so they were compatible with the tested vaping cartridges from California public high schools. Before each vaping episode, the batteries were fully charged, and the cartridges were fully filled with VEA or VE solutions to avoid “dry puff” conditions.

In the Supporting Information, see ST1 and ST2 for details of the analytical procedures. After vaping tests, the trapping solutions were analyzed by GC-MS or LC-MS/MS. Briefly, 200 μL solutions were added to glass culture tubes containing 800 μL of methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) and 0.5 mL of aqueous 2N H2SO4. The tubes were vortexed and centrifuged, aqueous layers were frozen in a dry ice/acetone bath, and the organic layers containing the ketene adducts were poured into clean vials. This resulted in a 1:5 dilution of the original samples. Aliquots of these extracts, 50 μL, were diluted with 950 μL of methanol at a 1:20 dilution. Overall, the dilution of the original samples was 1:100. Aliquots, 10 μL, were injected into the LC-MS/MS system and analyzed using a Supelco HSF5 column, 15 cm × 4 mm, gradient of 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in water to 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid in methanol. Atmospheric pressure chemical ionization (APCI) and selected reaction monitoring (SRM) were used. Peak areas for N-methylacetanilide, a derivative compound formed from the N-methylaniline-trapped ketene, were recorded for 3 SRM transitions. These were 150 → 93, 150 → 106, and 150 → 108. Peak areas for N-benzylacetamide, a derivative compound formed from the benzylamine-trapped ketene, were recorded for the SRM transition of 150 → 91. Concentrations were estimated from peak areas of analytes compared with those of known amounts of N-benzylacetamide and N-methylacetanilide standards.

For GC-MS analysis (Agilent 5973), the sample pretreatment is the same as LC-MS/MS, with the exception that the MTBE extract is analyzed without dilution with methanol. It was carried out on an Agilent HP-5 capillary column using isobutene for chemical ionization (CI). The m/z 150 MH+ and m/z 165 MH+ were used for the ketene–benzylaniline adduct, N-benzylacetamide, and duroquinone (another VEA thermal decomposition product) identification, respectively.

Scanning electron microscopy/energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDS) analyses were conducted using an FEI XL30 Environmental SEM instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Madison) equipped with a Bruker Quantax EDS System and SEMView 8000 electronics (SEMTech Solutions, North Billerica, WI). To minimize the amount of sample charging under the electron beam, 0.6 mbar water vapor was injected into the SEM chamber for wet-mode imaging. Vaping cartridges were dissembled. The heating components including filament and associated ceramic or glass fibers were imaged and analyzed at a working distance of 10 mm, 20 kV, and 50×–5000× using the SEM’s backscattered electron (BSE) detector.

Figure 5 shows the schematic setup for measuring the temperature profile for the tested e-devices during the puff cycle under the CORESTA Research Method 81 vaping regimes, as described above. The temperature was recorded by an EXTECH Thermocouple Thermometer (Industrial Electronics, Inc., Knoxville, TN, Model EA11A, K type), which was inserted into the device through the mouthpiece and directly onto the filament surface. The thermocouple tip was moved around during the measurement to different locations on the heating surface while continuously puffing until the highest temperature position was achieved. The advantage of this setup is that it does not need to break the device or interfere with the device’s operation, so the measurements can be obtained under real working conditions.

Initially, we used GC-MS to detect the ketene adduct with benzylamine, N-benzylacetamide. However, LC-MS/MS proved to be more satisfactory for quantitative analysis of multiple samples. Because the samples contained large amounts of high-boiling-point VEA, but small amounts of N-benzylacetamide, GC chromatography was not ideal, especially using narrow bore capillary columns. The VEA appeared to overload the column, distorting analyte peak symmetry and requiring a high column temperature and long run times to elute. These initial experiments did not include an acid wash following sample collection, and benzylamine appeared to react to a small extent with VEA in the heated injection to give N-benzylacetamide. Formation in the injection port could be prevented by washing the extracts with aqueous acid as described in the Experimental Section.

Therefore, we decided to develop LC-based methods. Because of the much higher capacity of LC columns compared to narrow bore capillary columns, we anticipated that the relatively large amounts of VEA would not distort analyte peak symmetry, which was found to be the case. We developed an LC-MS/MS SRM method for N-benzylacetamide. See the Experimental section and Supporting Information, ST1.

Another possible complication, if trapped solutions are stored for prolonged times before analysis, in using amine trapping agents is that esters can react directly with primary and secondary amines to give the corresponding amides. We found that benzylamine reacts slowly over days with VEA and that the reaction is temperature-dependent. Acid washing of the trapping solution to remove excess amine eliminates the direct reaction.3,27 This was confirmed by preparing a control with similar amounts of VEA as would be expected to be transferred to the trapping solution during vaping experiments, plus the same amount of benzylamine. One aliquot was washed with acid, and the other was not. Washing with acid greatly reduced the amount of N-benzylacetamide formation from the direct reaction with VEA. Thus, when using amines as the trapping agent, an acid wash should be carried as part of the protocol as previously described,3 which minimizes the direct reaction.

The LC-MS/MS chromatograms of m/z 150 → 91 for the N-benzylacetamide standard and benzylamine-trapped vaping aerosols are shown in Figure 6a,b, respectively. The area of N-benzylacetamide peak for transition 150 → 91 at different puffs and VEA control are plotted in Figure 7. The areas of N-benzylacetamide increased for 30 puffs compared with that for 2 and 15 puffs. It is worth noting that the GC-MS results in the SIM mode of m/z 150 MH+ and m/z 165 MH+ confirmed the formation of both N-benzylacetamide and duroquinone, respectively (Figure S2).

It occurred to us that a less reactive amine might not react appreciably with VEA but would still react readily with ketene due to its high reactivity. Wang and Schueler28 used aniline to trap and analyze ketene gas. N-methylaniline was chosen in this study to test this possibility because it is a secondary amine, and the nitrogen atom is more sterically hindered than benzylamine. We found that N-methylaniline did not react appreciably with VEA, though small amounts of N-methylacetanilide were detected as a solvent impurity but did not increase over time.

An N-methylaniline-trapping solution was used to capture ketenes in the generated aerosol for the two rechargeable batteries, which are denoted as battery-2 (2.6 V) and battery-3 (3.7 V). LC-MS/MS chromatograms of m/z 150 → 108, m/z 150 → 106, and m/z 150 → 93 for the 1 μg/mL N-methylacetanilide standard and a vaped VEA aerosol are shown in Figure 8. All of these 3 transition ions were used for the quantitation of ketene. Results were averaged.

After vaping pure VEA using the 2.6 V battery-2, the detected ketene amounts were slightly higher than that for the nonvaped VEA control for all trapping solutions regardless of the vaping puffs (as shown in Figure 9). For ketene in the first impinger solutions, ketene amounts were at 0.2, 0.17, and 0.32 μg/mL for 20, 30, and 40 puffs (Figure S3), respectively, while for the second impinger solutions, the ketene amount was around 0.2 μg/mL for all cases.

Upon switching to the higher 3.7 voltage battery-3 with the same cartridge tested for battery-2, as shown in Figure 10, the total generated ketene amount, including in impinger1 and impinger2, was steadily increased from 9.81 ± 0.12 to 35.2 ± 0.38 μg/mL for 30 and 40 puffs, respectively. However, the ketene amount was similar for 10 and 20 puffs at about 5.8 μg/mL. This nonlinear increase in ketene is not directly proportional to puff numbers and highlights the substantial variance from puff to puff. The amounts of ketene detected in the second impingers for the higher power battery were much lower than those in the first impingers (shown in Figure S4). The trapping efficiency in the first impinger was at around 77.8 ± 3.5% after the background correction, which demonstrated the necessity of two series of impingers for ketene collection.

It was noteworthy that when comparing the 40 puff trapping results of the 2.6 and 3.7 V batteries, the latter indicated about 76-fold (35.2/0.46 = 76.5) greater production of ketene.

To illustrate the effect of battery power on working temperatures with the same cartridge, batteries-2 and 3 were fully charged before temperature measurement experiments with the cartridge filled with a VEA solution (battery-1 was not included in the temperature measurement as it was nonrechargeable). The experimental setup (Figure 5) allowed measurement of the working temperature while the VEA was vaped simultaneously. Batteries-2 and 3 had output voltages of 2.6 and 3.7 V, which correspond to the power strength of 660 and 1000 mAh, respectively. The filament working temperature for battery-2 was in the range of 180–200 °C while continuously vaping VEA (Figure 11, Top) using the same puffing regimes as the ketene-trapping tests; whereas, when the same cartridge was connected to battery-3, the working temperature was significantly increased ranging from 460 to 510 °C (Figure 11, Bottom). To measure the temperature under simulated “dry puff” conditions, during which the heating element may not be entirely covered by VEA, the cartridge was emptied and reconnected to battery-3. Under these vaping conditions, the temperature ranged between 600 and 650 °C (Figure S5). Narimani et al. computationally predicted that for ketene concentrations to reach acutely toxic levels in the lungs (as high as 30 ppm), dry puff conditions would be necessary.29 Our results clearly demonstrate that temperature and, by association, battery power are key drivers of ketene formation.

This paper describes methodological approaches to measure ketene under normal vaping scenarios using benzylamine and N-methylaniline as trapping reagents.

We conclude from these studies that ketene can be formed under realistic vaping conditions from VEA under conditions of sufficient heat and catalysis. Given that the EVALI outbreak involved acute exposure to ketene, what the conditions and implications are for low-level ketene formation and exposures over a chronic period remain unknown. No experimental data exist to inform the ideal construction of such exposure conditions. Our results showed that the generated ketene amount is closely associated with vaping temperature, consumed VEA amount, and the repeated puffing regime. Metallic materials, including nickel, chromium, and silica, in the heating parts may act as catalysts14,30,31 to facilitate the VEA decomposition forming toxic ketene gas during vaping. However, this catalysis may not be consistent between devices or even within a single device as the exact conditions for catalysis remain unclear.

While the EVALI epidemic was due to acute respiratory damage from ketene derived from VEA, the general principle of acetates, including acetic acid itself, serving as starting materials for ketene formation holds and has been extensively documented in the chemistry literature.13,17,20 In the gas phase, the reaction of ketene with water to form acetic acid has been shown to be relatively slow due to a high activation energy barrier, which is consistent with its potential to inflict lung tissue damage if inhaled.32 Computational and experimental studies of chemical reaction energies indicate that a wide range of other acetates can also form ketene.8,13,17,20 Thermal degradation of various esters into acids, aldehydes, olefins, and ketene has been previously well documented.33,34 Among them, the thermal decomposition of phenyl acetate brought much attention recently because it shares a similar aryl acetate group with VEA, a possible causative chemical compound that is responsible for the EVALI outbreak. The key products in phenyl acetate gas-phase thermal degradation included phenol and ketene.35 Using benzylamine as a trapping agent, O’Shea and Wu3 reported the ketene formation when VEA was vaped, and Strongin et al.7 demonstrated that acetates of THC, cannabinol (CBN), and cannabidiol (CBD) could also emit ketene gas under real-world vaping conditions. N-methylaniline is a relatively weaker nucleophile compared with benzylamine due to nitrogen electron pair delocalization, but it can also successfully capture ketene, forming bench stable N-methylacetamide and avoiding the potential direct reaction of VEA with N-methylaniline, which may limit sensitivity of detection. Our results not only confirmed the ketene formation from VEA vaping but also detected another VEA thermal decomposition product, duroquinone (Figure S2), from VEA decomposition, as previously reported.3 A recent study of the potential for oxidative lung damage from vaping-induced duroquinone formation indicated that multiple factors, including other degradation compounds and metals from the device, may be at play.36

Although trapping experiments showed that ketene emissions increased with increasing puff numbers, this correlation was not linear, indicating either that puff-to-puff variation is possible even within a single device or that this increase is associated with the continuation of puffing. This point highlights the unpredictability of ketene formation in real-world vaping situations and that the 68 deaths in the EVALI outbreak of 2019 may have resulted from a combination of circumstances, causing a higher-than-normal ketene concentration to be formed. Also of significant concern is the sustained nonlethal ketene exposure affecting larger numbers of people as the longer-term health impacts of this remain unknown. Employing deuterated isotopically labeled CBN-OAc-D3, Strongin et al.37 unambiguously reconfirmed that the acetate moiety is the ketene source. Since VEA had the acetate moiety, while VE did not, when the vaping solution was switched from VEA to VE, our results (Figure 10) showed that no ketene was detected within experimental error, confirming that the acetate moiety of VEA was the ketene source.

Vaping temperature was considered to be one of the most important factors that determine VEA thermal decomposition.7,29 In this work, we measured the working temperature of two commercial batteries with output voltages of 2.6 and 3.7 V, respectively. The higher voltage battery gave temperature profiles that reached as high as 510 °C even with cartridges that were fully filled with the VEA solution. Under “dry puff“ conditions, the filament temperature was even higher at ~650 °C. When a lower voltage battery was employed, the measured working temperature was much lower <200 °C. Lynch et al.6 observed that mean temperature maxima for all examined devices during normal vaping at the selected power exceeded 430 °C, with a range of 375–569 °C in ceramic oil-style devices. Given the wide variety of commercial vaping device types and battery power strengths on the market, real vaping temperatures can vary very widely among devices and for a single device, depending on how it is being used. According to Morgan et al.,38 300 °C is the high end of cannabis user preferences and ~200 °C is median for liquid/oil cannabis e-liquids vaping. However, these temperatures are based on cannabis users’ self-reported value. Our unique temperature profiling setup enabled us to measure the filament temperature while simultaneously vaping VEA under the same regimes as the aerosol collection. It is worth noting that the filament temperature may be much higher than that of the surrounding liquids. Importantly as many vaping flavor chemicals contain ester functional groups, their thermal decomposition temperatures are dependent upon individual ester chemical structures and their individual thermal degradation chemistries.6 Although the uncatalyzed gas-phase thermal degradation of phenyl acetate forming phenol and ketene occurs between 650–850 °C6 under real-world vaping conditions, ketene was detectable upon vaping CBN-acetates even as low as 278 °C using a dab platform, where ambient O2 significantly promoted the ketene generation.37 Canchola et al.31 reported that the presence of O2 significantly enhances the degradation of VEA at both high (356 °C) and low (176 °C) temperatures, resulting in the formation of numerous degradation products previously identified in VEA vaping emissions. Jaegers et al.39 also found that the degradation temperature of (propylene glycol) PG/(vegetable glycerol) VG e-liquid is lowered between 133 and 175 °C upon oxygen availability, both in the presence and absence of a material surface. Our temperature results with battery-3 (3.7 V) clearly demonstrated that a real vaping temperature of ~510 °C is consistently reachable, which is more than sufficient for VEA thermal decomposition.

The heating element for the cannabis vaping device used in this study (Figures 12 and 13) was a metal coil wrapped around a ceramic rod. Another type of heating element often seen in vaping devices is a fiber-glass-based rod wrapped with a metal coil (Figure S6), which has also been observed in school ground devices.26 In the devices tested in this study, which were in common use at the time of the EVALI outbreak, the metal coils were not directly submerged in the e-liquid solutions. Upon inhaling, a small amount of e-liquid is drawn into the inner part of the stainless-steel cylinder through the two holes and comes into direct contact with the heated metal coil, as shown in Figure 12. VEA and cannabis oils have much higher viscosities compared with the PG/VG carriers commonly used in nicotine-containing e-liquids. This can easily increase the vaping temperature.6,40 Therefore, the working temperature is related not only to the device design and heating materials but also to the e-liquid viscosity.

The exact mechanism of the catalytic effect on ketene formation during vaping is not well understood. Various metals, including nickel, chromium, copper, iron, and aluminum, as well as silicon-rich rubbers, fluorinated polymers, and glass fiber, were found in the devices used by EVALI patients.18 Tissot and colleagues20 established that ketene is formed under conditions of some heat (approximately 350 °C) and that the energy required for this reaction is greatly reduced in the presence of a copper metal catalyst. The common factor in these reactions is the presence of heat and contact with metallic surfaces. As we know, the products of a high-temperature reaction are closely dependent on the reaction conditions, such as temperature and source materials.41 Traditionally, No. 24 chromel A wire, which is an alloy containing approximately 80% nickel and 20% chromium (by weight), with low-level quantities of Si (1%) and Fe (0.5%), was employed as catalysts for the ketene generator.28 Although the exact metal or metals that could catalyze the ketene formation in a vaping scenario warrants further investigation, a high abundance of nickel, iron, silica, and chromium in the heating materials of vape devices provides potential catalysts for the ketene formation.

Vaping various substances, including nicotine with flavor chemicals and cannabis, has gained popularity, especially among a younger demographic.45 Although the quantitative and varied health risks associated with vaping are not clearly established, as evidence of the outbreak of EVALI, it is important for public health agencies to characterize and minimize any such health impacts from using these vaping devices and products. Information for the e-liquid ingredients, design of the vaping device, heating materials, and safety measures for temperature control are needed to protect public health from vaping. On the other hand, vaping behaviors also play critical roles in the formation of toxicants upon vaping. Since EVALI has primarily occurred among young adults and is largely driven by using THC-containing products from informal sources,46 continued efforts are warranted, including at the school level, to educate, prevent, and reduce youth vaping and limit access to such products, especially from illicit markets.

This research reports the current status of our results. More detailed work is needed in the future for statistical analysis regarding the variabilities across a myriad of variables. Although one hypothesized causative link to EVALI was that of acute exposures to ketene, to date, there is no sufficient evidence to rule out other chemicals that have played a role in the occurrence of EVALI.46 We investigated only the potential contribution of ketene exposure of vaping VEA to EVALI in this study. Heating VEA could also generate other known toxic compounds, such as carbonyls, quinones, and other volatile products, which are not analyzed in this study. These chemicals could act by different pathophysiologic mechanisms. On the other hand, as reported by Rebulli et al.,25 VEA itself could significantly change pulmonary surfactant surface tension, contributing to lung atelectasis. Furthermore, fine and ultrafine droplets of heated VEA via an e-cigarette may enhance pulmonary deposition, resulting in substantially increased bioavailability in the lungs. Other ingredients, especially an acetate moiety containing flavors and THCs, warrant further investigation.47 Further, we used pure VEA as the vaping liquid to test the three real-world vaping devices. However, given the complexity of e-liquid and the wide variety of e-cigarette vaping devices on the US market, more studies are needed to better understand the effects of real e-liquid mixtures, device characterization, and vaping regimes on the ketene emission from vaping complex chemical mixtures. In addition, our ketene measurement approach is semiquantitative. Using isotopically labeled N-methylacetanilide as an internal standard may provide a more accurate quantitation of ketene in the future. Optimization of the ketene trapping conditions, including trapping agent concentrations and trapping efficiency tests, needs to be further investigated.

In this study, a novel approach was developed to evaluate ketene exposure from VEA and VE vaping. Ketene in generated aerosols was captured by two different chemicals: benzylamine and N-methylaniline. The bench-stable trapped vaping aerosols were analyzed by GC-MS and LC-MS/MS. Our results confirmed the ketene formation when VEA was vaped. The amount of ketene produced increased nonlinearly as more puffs were conducted and showed a correlation to temperatures measured within vaping devices. Device battery power strength, which affects heating temperature, played an important role in ketene formation. The working temperature can reach 510 °C for the battery with a 3.7 V output power under normal vaping conditions. In addition to ketene, duroquinone, a member of the p-benzoquinone family of oxidants, was also observed when VEA was vaped. Ketene was not detected when VE was vaped under the same conditions. The potential catalytic roles of device heating components, including nickel, iron, silica, chromium, and aluminum, for the ketene formation were discussed. The current ketene measurement methods can be readily used for the ketene exposure evaluations in the future. Since highly polar substances are amenable to LC-MS analysis, these methods could be modified for analysis of ketene adducts with biologically relevant molecules such as proteins, nucleic acids, or their component monomer amino acids and nucleosides.
