# Novel Cranial Imaging Findings and a Splice-Site Variant in a Patient with Tyrosinemia Type III, and a Summary of Published Cases.
**Authors:** Kahraman AB, Akar HT, Güleray Lafcı N, Yıldız Y, Tokatlı A
**Journal:** Mol Syndromol (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1159/000519256](https://doi.org/10.1159/000519256)

## Content

Tyrosinemia type III is an extremely rare autosomal recessive disease, with only 19 patients yet reported. It is caused by a deficiency of the 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase enzyme, resulting from biallelic mutations in the HPD gene. Although the clinical spectrum of the disease is not fully known, most patients present with neurodevelopmental symptoms. We report on a 20-month-old patient who was investigated due to developmental delay and dysmorphic features. The girl had a novel splice-site mutation in the HPD gene and ventriculomegaly in cranial imaging, which was not previously associated with tyrosinemia type III. Our patient had mild subjective improvement in social skills and language development after dietary therapy was started and her tyrosine levels decreased. We also summarize clinical, biochemical, and genetic findings of previously published patients with biallelic HPD mutations.

Tyrosine is a nonessential amino acid derived from degradation of proteins or through hydroxylation of phenylalanine. It is involved in the structure of integral proteins and is a precursor for dopa, thyroxine, and melanin [Chakrapani et al., 2016]. It is a glucogenic and ketogenic amino acid, catabolized to fumarate and acetoacetate in 5 enzymatic steps [Russo et al., 2001]. Inherited defects in each of these steps are associated with different disorders of tyrosine metabolism, some of which are characterized by elevated tyrosine levels (tyrosinemia types I, II, and III).

Tyrosinemia type III is an extremely rare autosomal recessive disease, with only 19 patients yet reported [Ellaway et al., 2001; Heylen et al., 2012; Szymanska et al., 2015; Coskun et al., 2016; Najafi et al., 2018; Barroso et al., 2020]. It is caused by the deficiency of 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase enzyme (4-HPPD), resulting from biallelic mutations in theHPDgene. The enzyme is synthesized in the liver, kidney, neutrophils, and neurons, and catalyzes the conversion of 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvic acid into homogentisic acid. As a result of the metabolic block, there is an increase in the concentration of tyrosine in plasma and of 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate, 4-hydroxyphenyllactate, and 4-hydroxyphenylacetate in urine. Although the clinical spectrum of the disease is not fully known, most patients present with neurodevelopmental symptoms [Chakrapani et al., 2016]. The mechanism of neurological dysfunction in tyrosinemia type III is not fully understood, and there are various hypotheses on this issue. Neuropathological findings of tyrosinemic patients include delay in myelination [de Andrade et al., 2011]. High tyrosine levels provoke oxidative stress and cause mitochondrial dysfunction and DNA damage. Oxidative stress parameters were shown to increase in the cerebellum, hippocampus, and striatum, similar to the pattern sometimes observed in mitochondrial diseases [Macêdo et al., 2013]. Hypertyrosinemia also impairs cerebral protein and neurotransmitter synthesis by preventing the uptake of other amino acids into the brain [Thimm et al., 2011]. Genotype-phenotype relationship is not established. In some patients with enzymatic deficiency, the mutations could not be identified [Rüetschi et al., 2000; Ellaway et al., 2001].

We present a 20-month-old female patient who was investigated due to developmental delay and dysmorphic features. She had a novelHPDgene mutation and cranial imaging findings previously not associated with tyrosinemia type III.

The girl was referred to our center at the age of 20 months due to developmental delay. Past history was significant for preterm birth via cesarean section due to fetal distress at 35 weeks and 3 days of gestation, with a birth weight of 2,600 g as the first child of healthy, consanguineous parents. The perinatal course was otherwise uneventful. Global developmental delay was evident since the age of 6 months (Table1). Due to enlarged head circumference, cranial computerized tomography had been performed, demonstrating that the ventricular system was wide; widths of the right and left lateral ventricles at the level of atrium and of the third ventricle were 12, 10, and 6.5 mm respectively, consistent with hydrocephalus. Extra-axial laminations were increased bilaterally in the anterior parts of the temporal lobes. Bilateral bulbus oculi, retroorbital fat plane, extraocular muscle groups and optic nerves, cerebellar hemispheres, the brainstem, bilateral coronae radiatae, centra semiovale, subcortical white matter, and cerebral cortical sulci were normal on computerized tomography of the brain. Cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed ventriculomegaly and thin corpus callosum (Fig.1a). Brain stem, cerebellum, and fourth ventricle widths were normal. Bilateral basal ganglia, thalami, external and internal capsules, centra semiovale, and diffusion-weighted imaging of the brain were normal. Clinical, radiological, and fundoscopic signs of increased intracranial pressure were absent. Surgical treatment of hydrocephalus was deemed unnecessary.

In her physical examination at our clinic, her weight, height, and head circumference were 15.5 kg (>97th percentile, standard deviation score [SDS]: 2.69), 87 cm (75th–90th percentile; SDS: 1.45), and 49 cm (90th–95th percentile; SDS: 1.59), respectively. She had wooly hair, and it looked kinky in the posterior regions. (Hair examination under light microscope was normal.) She had dysmorphic facial features (Fig.1b) and tapering fingers. Neurological examination was significant for axial hypotonia. Muscle mass was normal. Deep tendon reflexes were increased in the lower extremities, but there were no pathological reflexes, spasticity or joint contractures. She had stereotypical movements like clapping hands when she got excited. She could walk with support. Physical examination was otherwise normal. In Denver II Developmental Screening Test performed at the age of 24 months, personal-social, language, fine motor, and gross motor development were at the levels of 9–10 months, 14 months, 9–10 months, and 12 months, respectively.

Her complete blood count, liver and kidney functions were within normal limits (online suppl. material AppendixA; seewww.karger.com/doi/10.1159/000519256). Plasma amino acid analysis was significant for markedly elevated tyrosine (1,200 μmol/L; reference range: 19–119). Urinary organic acid analysis showed marked elevations of 4-hydroxyphenylacetic acid, 4-hydroxyphenyllactic acid, and 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvic acid and mildly increased N-acetyltyrosine. Succinylacetone (<1 nmol/L), free carnitine and acylcarintine levels on dried blood spots were normal. Ophthalmologic examination was unremarkable without keratitis or conjunctivitis.

Our primary diagnosis was tyrosinemia type III because of markedly elevated tyrosine levels without hepatorenal findings or elevated succinylacetone (ruling out tyrosinemia type I and secondary hypertyrosinemia due to hepatic disease) or oculocutaneous findings (ruling out tyrosinemia type II). Genetic analysis was performed to unravel the underlying etiology of hypertyrosinemia in the absence of the clinical and biochemical findings observed in tyrosinemia types I and II. Targeted next-generation sequencing using SOPHIA Hereditary Disorder SolutionTM(Sophia Genetics) on Illumina NextSeq 500® platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) revealed a novel homozygous c.402–2A>G variant in theHPDgene (RefSeqNM_001171993), suggesting tyrosinemia type III (Fig.2a). The parents were heterozygous for the same variant (Fig.2b). Additionally, high-resolution chromosomal microarray studies were performed using Illumina Human CytoSNP-12 v2.1 BeadChip Array platform (Illumina) to investigate copy number variants contributing to the index patient's phenotype. Microarray data were analyzed using BlueFuseTMMulti Analysis Software for Molecular Cytogenetics (Illumina) based on human genome assembly GRCh37 (hg19). No copy number variants were detected. Causality of the c.402–2A>G splice acceptor variant to the phenotype was further strengthened with loss-of-heterozygosity data obtained from microarray studies, as theHPDgene lies within a 11-Mb-long contiguous stretch of homozygosity region on chromosome 12 between 12q24.22 and 12q24.32.

This newly identified splice acceptor variant in the 9th intron ofHPDhas not been previously reported in the literature or in the Genome Aggregation Database [Karczewski et al., 2020], which includes genome data from 60,146 healthy individuals. This novel variant is anticipated to be a pathogenic change by multiple assessment tools, including MutationTaster [Schwarz et al., 2014]. In addition, this change is also considered to be pathogenic according to ACMG 2015 guidelines (PVS1, PM2, PP3) [Richards et al., 2015] and is predicted to result in mis-splicing and/or nonsense-mediated decay of the HPD mRNA.

With the help of a phenylalanine- and tyrosine-free amino acid formula, a phenylalanine- and tyrosine-restricted diet was started with 400 mg phenylalanine (25 mg/kg/d), 400 mg tyrosine (25 mg/kg/d), and 30 g protein (2 g/kg/d) daily, enabling plasma tyrosine levels to drop dramatically from 1,821 to 298 μmol/L. After she started the diet, her mother noticed that she wanted to interact with her more, and that her movements had become a little faster. Her spoken vocabulary increased by a few words. At the time of writing, at the age of 30 months, she can walk independently, and her mother states that she is more aware of her surroundings, understands simple commands better, has a vocabulary of 5 words, and has more social skills, such as associating objects and places with their appropriate functions (e.g., smart phone and videos, kitchen and meals). While our patient was investigated at the age of 20 months for the etiology of her developmental delay, she was diagnosed with hereditary tyrosinemia type III due to a novel mutation. It is important to consider inherited metabolic causes and obtain plasma amino acid analysis in patients with developmental delay [Moeschler et al., 2014]. Ventriculomegaly and dysmorphic features are not specific to tyrosinemia type III or other disorders, and the diagnosis would have been missed if this analysis had not been performed as part of routine screening in developmental delay.

Tyrosinemia type III was first described by Giardini et al. [1983] in a 17-month-old girl with acute intermittent ataxia, hypotonia, and motor incoordination without eye, skin or liver lesions, who went on to have normal development afterwards. It has proven to be an extremely rare cause of developmental delay and of elevated tyrosine levels. In their study involving 13 patients, Ellaway et al. [2001] showed that 75% of patients with tyrosinemia type III had neurological manifestations, which included intellectual disability, ataxia, increased deep tendon reflexes, tremors, microcephaly, dyslexia, attention deficit, ataxia, hypotonia, and seizures. Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and autism spectrum disorder in these patients have been previously reported in the literature [Tahiroğlu et al., 2008; Barroso et al., 2020]. We planned to follow-up our patient for autism spectrum disorder because she had stereotypical movements and made less eye contact compared to peers. The MRI findings of this disease can vary; subcortical and brainstem white matter changes have been reported [Rüetschi et al., 2000]. Our patient instead had thin corpus callosum and ventriculomegaly, which had not been previously associated with tyrosinemia type III.

Differential diagnosis can be made with tyrosinemia type I and type II with the aid of succinylacetone (diagnostic for type I) and clinical features. Neurological findings in type II are quite variable and may be completely normal or microcephaly, seizures, self-mutilation, and behavioral disorders may be seen, while oculocutaneous features are more characteristic [Fois et al., 1986]. Type III also presents with neurological findings, but oculocutaneous findings are not expected. Eye, skin, and liver damage were absent, and succinylacetone was undetectable in our patient, which made us suspect tyrosinemia type III. If succinylacetone-negative persistent hypertyrosinemia is detected and treated soon after birth, preventing the development of symptoms, distinction between tyrosinemia type II and III can only be made via enzymatic or genetic testing.

Although the pathogenesis of the neurological findings is not known, it has been predicted that the neurological outcomes of the patients who are diagnosed during the newborn period and started on phenylalanine- and tyrosine-restricted diet fare better, which is supported by the report of asymptomatic cases diagnosed via newborn screening and treated thereafter [Ellaway et al., 2001]. It is difficult to determine a clear tyrosine target level above which neurological complications develop. In patients with tyrosine levels greater than 1,000 μmol/L, neurological findings are generally present, which was consistent with our observation regarding the present patient (Table2). It has been suggested to maintain tyrosine levels between 200 and 500 μmol/L [Heylen et al., 2012]. While the effectiveness of treatment is known for type I and type II patients, it is not clear for type III, mainly because the natural history and the treated phenotype are not well-established due to the low number of published patients. We observed that our patient began to interact with her mother more and increased the number of words after dietary therapy was started, decreasing her tyrosine levels.

A summary of all previously reported patients with biallelicHPDmutations is presented in Table2[Cerone et al., 1997; Standing et al., 1998; Rüetschi et al., 2000; Tomoeda et al., 2000; Heylen et al., 2012; Szymanska et al., 2015; Coskun et al., 2016; Barroso et al., 2020]. Of these patients, 4 were diagnosed by neonatal screening, and diet was initiated in all of them. Among these 4 early-treated patients, case 7 had normal development, whereas the other 3 had delayed language or cognitive development. Case 11 was diagnosed late and had ADHD at the age of 15, and her ADHD remained stable after the initiation of the diet. In patients diagnosed in infancy (cases 5 and 6), intellectual disability and abnormal behavior were observed after the diet was started. Therefore, a diet with restricted phenylalanine and tyrosine does not appear to be a curative or completely preventive treatment option. The clinical neurological findings in our patient (developmental delay) are nonspecific and consistent with the published literature, justifying the initiation of a phenylalanine and tyrosine-restricted diet for possible benefit. However, with so few patients reported in the literature, it is difficult to attribute the dysmorphic features and hydrocephalus to tyrosinemia type III in our patient, but it is possible that these may be novel findings in this rare disease since the diagnosis of tyrosinemia type III is established and no other likely causes could be uncovered.

Targeted sequencing for suspected metabolic diseases and microarray studies were performed to discover the molecular etiology of the index patient. Therefore, it is not possible to rule out any additional variant/s in another gene/s, that are not included in the targeted panel, contributing to the extended phenotype [Li et al., 2016].

Here, we have reported a new patient with tyrosinemia type III, who has novel brain imaging findings and harbors a novel splice-site variant in theHPDgene. Tyrosinemia type III is an inherited disorder of amino acid metabolism with a diverse neurological phenotype. A consistent natural history, adequate evidence of benefit of treatment, and genotype-phenotype correlations are not established. Larger cohorts and multicenter studies are probably needed to better characterize this extremely rare disease.
