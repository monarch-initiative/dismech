Small cell neuroendocrine cancers share biologic similarities across tissue 
types, including transient response to platinum-based chemotherapy with rapid 
progression of disease. We report a phase 1b study of pembrolizumab in 
combination with platinum-based chemotherapy in 15 patients with stage III-IV 
small cell bladder (cohort 1) or small cell/neuroendocrine prostate cancers 
(cohort 2). Overall response rate (ORR) is 43% with two-year overall survival 
(OS) rate of 86% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.63, 1.00) for cohort 1 and 57% 
(95% CI: 0.30, 1.00) for cohort 2. Treatment is tolerated well with grade 3 or 
higher adverse events occurring in 40% of patients with no deaths or treatment 
cessation secondary to toxicity. Single-cell and T cell receptor sequencing of 
serial peripheral blood samples reveals clonal expansion of diverse T cell 
repertoire correlating with progression-free survival. Our results demonstrate 
promising efficacy and safety of this treatment combination and support future 
investigation of this biomarker. This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov 
(NCT03582475).

Small cell carcinomas (SCCs) are aggressive malignancies found in multiple tissue types including the lung, bladder, prostate, ovaries, and breast.1 They share common histologic features such as small, round-to-oval cells with high nuclear-to-cytoplasm ratios, inconspicuous nucleoli, and incomplete penetrance of histologic markers including neuron-specific enolase, chromogranin A, and synaptophysin, as well as CD56 and CD57.2 Patients with SCCs typically exhibit relapse after initial response to platinum-based chemotherapy, a rapid progression to metastatic dissemination, and poor overall survival (OS) rates.3,4,5 These similarities infer a common molecular basis to the disease across tissue types with high rates of TP53 and RB1 inactivation.6 Indeed, an in vivo genetic model showed that a conserved set of oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes can generate SCCs from their respective benign human lung, prostate, and bladder epithelium.7,8

Small cell bladder cancer (SCBC) is a rare variant comprising less than 1% of all bladder cancers and typically arises de novo with up to 70% mixed with urothelial carcinoma, suggesting origins from a multi-potent cancer cell.4,9,10 SCBCs are frequently treated with etoposide and platinum chemotherapy mimicking the treatment of small cell lung cancers (SCLCs) and have an initial 40% to 60% response rate with median OS of 7–13 months in patients with extensive disease, with no known differences in response based on extent of small cell component.4,11,12,13 Primary small cell cancers of the prostate may arise de novo and are extremely rare occurring in less than 1% of prostate cancers. By contrast, a small cell/neuroendocrine prostate cancer (NEPC) can develop as a treatment-emergent disease in 10%–20% of metastatic castrate-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) patients treated with androgen deprivation therapy (ADT).5,14 Treatment includes etoposide and cisplatin or docetaxel and carboplatin, as taxanes have shown activity against both the neuroendocrine and CRPC components.15 Prognosis of metastatic NEPC is poor, with median OS ranging from 7 to 9 months from the time of diagnoses.16,17 As the majority of the 34,000 deaths from prostate cancer in the US and 370,000 worldwide arise from CRPC, the development of NEPC in up to 20% of patients will be an increasingly significant unmet clinical need.18

We established a clinical trial to explore the impact of immunotherapy in combination with standard-of-care chemotherapy in the treatment of patients with small cell neuroendocrine bladder and prostate cancer. At the time of inception, there was an encouraging overall response rate (ORR) using pembrolizumab, a humanized IgG4 monoclonal antibody inhibitor of programmed cell death 1 (PD-1), in the heavily pre-treated SCLC cohort as well as precedence of combining chemotherapy with checkpoint inhibitors (CPIs).19,20 Due to the biologic similarities among SCCs of the bladder, lung, and prostate, we hypothesized that pembrolizumab will have a favorable response rate as first-line therapy in combination with chemotherapy in SCCs of the bladder and prostate. After initiation of our enrollment, results from two phase 3 clinical trials of CPIs with chemotherapy for SCLC showed improvement in progression-free survival (PFS) in both trials and OS in one trial.21,22 Recent case series of CPI in SCBC have been encouraging,23 but with limited efficacy using CPI monotherapy in NEPC.24,25 Our investigator-initiated phase 1b clinical trial titled “Pembrolizumab with Platinum-based Chemotherapy in Small Cell/Neuroendocrine Cancers of Urothelium and Prostate,” was conducted with 15 subjects in 2 cohorts, cohort 1 for patients with treatment-naive small cell cancer of the bladder and cohort 2 for patients with primary small cell or NEPC.

Between 1/23/2019 and 1/21/2022, 15 patients consented to enroll in the study at a single institution in two cohorts, metastatic or locally advanced naive small cell bladder cancer (cohort 1, n = 7) and primary small cell cancer or NEPC (cohort 2, n = 8). One patient (2-09) withdrew from the trial after cycle 1 on his own volition and was excluded in the efficacy analysis with limited exposure to the study drug. Of the 14 patients that completed the protocol, the median duration of pembrolizumab was 31 cycles (range 2–35; 35 cycles in cohort 1 and 13 cycles in cohort 2). All patients completed 35 cycles of maintenance pembrolizumab (n = 7) or progressed at the time data were frozen. All patients in cohort 1 received etoposide and cisplatin, while 38.5% (n = 3) in cohort 2 received etoposide and cisplatin and 62.5% (n = 5) received docetaxel and carboplatin based on physician preference and perceived contribution of adenocarcinoma to disease impact. In cohort 1, 57.1% (n = 4) of patients (T3aN0M0 (3), T3aN1M0 (1)) underwent radical cystectomy after the initial chemotherapy-pembrolizumab therapy as a neoadjuvant regimen, while one patient underwent radical cystectomy during the surveillance period at 42 months after trial start with carcinoma in situ only in the bladder and was not considered to have progressive disease (PD). One patient in cohort 1 underwent external beam radiation for brain metastases.

Of the remaining 2 patients with RECIST v.1.1 evaluable lesions, one patient exhibited PD and one patient showed stable disease (SD) after initial assessment. Endpoints evaluating PFS and OS are reported with a median follow-up of 34.2 months (range 2.0–59.5 months) (Figure 3D; Table S1). With the heterogeneity within cohort 1 and between the two cohorts, these results are not intended to compare cohorts, but to estimate frequency of responses and guide the development of future studies. At 12 months, PFS was 64% (95% CI: 0.44, 0.95) across cohorts, 86% (95% CI: 0.63, 1.00) within cohort 1, and 43% (95% CI: 0.18, 1.00) within cohort 2. At 24 months, PFS was 50% (95% CI: 0.30, 0.84) across cohorts, 86% (95% CI: 0.63, 1.00) within cohort 1, and 14% (95% CI: 0.2, 0.88) within cohort 2. 5 patients in the study died from disease-related causes. OS rate at 12 months was 79% (95% CI: 0.60, 1.00) across cohorts, 86% (95% CI: 0.63, 1.00) within cohort 1, and 71% (95% CI: 0.45, 1.00) within cohort 2. At 24 months, OS rate was 71% (95% CI: 0.51, 1.00) across cohorts, 86% (95% CI: 0.63, 1.00) within cohort 1, and 57% (95% CI: 0.30, 1.00) within cohort 2. At the time of analysis, median PFS and OS were not reached in cohort 1. In cohort 2, median PFS was 7 months (1, N/A) and OS 27 months (6, N/A), with upper limit of 95% CI not available due to small sample size and scarcity of events. Our results compared to historical data for chemotherapy alone in these patient populations show a positive signal that we believe warrants additional investigation.4,11,12,16,17 Gender differences were not examined due to the small sample sizes of cohorts.

In patients that proceeded with extirpative surgery, we had the ability to assess pathologic responses. Of the 3 patients with T3aN0M0 disease that underwent radical cystectomy after the initial chemotherapy-pembrolizumab therapy, one had a complete pathologic response (pT0) while two remained with muscle-invasive disease and negative lymph nodes (pT2aN0). One patient with T3aN1M0 disease exhibited a PR by RECIST v.1.1 criteria and pathologic stage T2N1. The patient with initial T2N0M1b that underwent radical cystectomy after completing adjuvant therapy was staged pathologic TisN0.

The secondary endpoint was to assess the safety and tolerability of pembrolizumab in combination with chemotherapy in patients that received at least one dose of the study drug. Treatment-related Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) grade 3 or higher adverse events were reported in 40% (n = 6) of patients overall and 57% (n = 4) and 25% (n = 2) in cohorts 1 and 2, respectively (Table S2). There were 5 patients with serious adverse events for fatigue, pyelonephritis, seizure, aspiration pneumonia, and dysphagia, including one treatment-related grade 4 hypocalcemia that resolved with medical therapy, with no grade 5 events. No patients required high-dose steroids or delay in study drug dosing, and no patients discontinued therapy secondary to treatment toxicity. The most common treatment-related adverse events were weakness and fatigue, skin-related diseases including rash or pruritus, diarrhea, peripheral neuropathy, and hypothyroidism. Grade 3 or high treatment-unrelated events were recorded in 73% (n = 11) of patients and 71% (n = 5) and 75% (n = 6) in cohorts 1 and 2, respectively. Details of treatment-unrelated events are further given (Table S3).

There are no predictive biomarkers for the use of CPI immunotherapy in small cell bladder cancer or NEPC as the clinical experience is limited. Current biomarkers for anti-PD-1/PD-L1 CPIs on a broader scale include extent of PD-L1 expression, tumor mutation burden (TMB), and microsatellite instability (MSI).27 In urothelial bladder cancer, PD-L1 expression correlates with response to CPIs as first-line therapy in cisplatin-ineligible patients but has limited practical use with many instances of response in patients that do not meet positive criteria.28,29 We did not observe a statistically significant correlation between response and CPS score, which could be due to our limited sample size. Our bladder cohort 1 had a significantly higher average TMB at 7.54 mt/Mb (standard deviation 6.2) compared to cohort 2 at 1.76 mt/Mb (standard deviation 1.2) p < 0.05, but only 2 patients exceeded TMB >10 mt/Mb as a cutoff to be predictive of response to CPI.30 MSI is typically low in all genitourinary malignancies, and we did not observe any patients with high microsatellite instability (MSI-H) (Figure 2).

To examine potential biomarkers and explore predictors of response, we initially divided patients into responders (CR + PR + SD) that did not progress on therapy, to initial responders that progressed on therapy, and non-responders (PD) (Figure S1A). Using this categorization, PFS and OS of these 3 groups are shown with significant differences (Figure S1B). However, this approach naturally segregated patients into our two cohorts, which could bias the results based on inherent biological differences between the cohorts. To more rigorously assess for potential biomarkers, we elected to perform the subsequent analyses based on PFS as a continuous variable, with limit set at 45 months based on the shortest follow-up time of ongoing responders that exceeded the last patient to progress to minimize lead-time bias.

Pre-treatment formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tumor tissues were subject to bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to examine the tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and estimate the proportion of individual immune cell populations in 10 patients where tissue was available (cohort 1, n = 6; cohort 2, n = 4) (Figure 4A). No significant trends were noted in immune populations between cohorts or in relationship to PFS. Notably only patient 2-03 with primary small cell cancer of the prostate exhibited significant pre-treatment CD8+ T cell infiltration. Furthermore, the remainder of the NEPC tumors had similar immune infiltration to that from small cell urothelial cancers. The T cell repertoire and clonal expansion are shaped by interaction between the host and cancer, encompassing the anti-tumor immune response.32 Recent evidence suggests that clonotypic diversity of tumor-infiltrating T cells is represented in peripheral blood and that the periphery may be a source of non-exhausted cells for cancer immunity.33 To explore the immunological basis and a potential predictive biomarker for our clinical responses, we followed the longitudinal development of immune phenotypes and T cell repertoire of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) by single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) for 5′ gene expression as well as single-cell T cell receptor sequencing (scTCR-seq). We tested up to 3 time points per patient, starting prior to treatment (cycle 1), at 21 weeks (cycle 7) to provide a washout period of 3–6 weeks after cessation of chemotherapy, and at 51 weeks (cycle 17) or time of progression, whichever occurred earlier. Two patients were excluded from this analysis, as subject 2-03 was missing cycle 1 and subject 2-09 withdrew after cycle 1. From the remaining 13 patients, 36 total time points were sampled with a total of 253,630 cells. Combining scRNA-seq with scTCR-seq identified 45,375 T cells with T cell receptor (TCR) sequencing yielding 29,531 unique clonotypes, 29,479 private and 52 common.

Gene expression of all PMBCs across time points was profiled into a single uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), segregated into clusters of similar cells and labeled against reference gene signatures from published datasets, separating into 23 clusters (Figure S2). We plotted and examined the proportions of all clusters longitudinally against PFS comparing fold change at cycle 1 to cycle 7 or progression and cycle 1 to cycle 17 or progression (Figure S3). We noted a trend of increasing effector cells including CD8+ natural killer T (NKT) and CD8+ T effector memory (TEM) cells from cycle 1 to cycle 7/progression as well as from cycle 1 to cycle 17/progression (Figure 4C).

To explore the T effector population, we examined clonal diversity of study participants and generated a T cell-specific UMAP. Although heterogeneous among different cancer types, TCR diversity is frequently associated with improved disease progression including in those found in peripheral blood.34,35 Complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) α and β chain identity was used to define and track clonal expansion across time in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. We categorized our private clonotypes as “lost” if the clonotype was only observed in cycle 1, “persistent” if the clonotype was observed in cycle 1 and either cycle 7 or 17/progression, or “novel,” if the clonotype was not observed in cycle 1. When plotted on a T cell-limited UMAP, the lost clones were predominantly CD4+ naive, memory T cells, and T regulatory cells, while novel clones included both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. By contrast, persistent clones predominantly clustered into the CD8+ TEM cluster, as well as CD8+ NKT, mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT), and CD4+ cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) cells, with a small proportion in CD4+ memory T cells and CD16+ natural killer (NK) cells (Figure 4D). The vast majority of clonotypes were singlets at various time points suggesting a diverse repertoire of T cells. When we examined effector T cells populations, including all CD8+ T cells and including CD4+ CTLs, out of 6,257 unique clones, 25.2% were lost, 11.4% were persistent, and 63.4% were novel clones (Figure 4E). However, out of 23,340 unique CD4+ T cell clones (excluding CD4+ CTLs), 33.8% were lost, 1.6% persistent clones, and 64.6% novel clones; thus, the majority of CD4+ T cells following treatment are unique clonotypes from pre-treatment (Figure 4E). CD4+ T cells play a critical role in sustaining anti-tumor adaptive immune responses.36 Perhaps these refreshed CD4+ T cells that fall into the CD4+ memory and CD4+ naive cell clusters represent neoantigen-specific CD4+ T cells that may provide direct effector function as well as support CD8+ T cells.37,38

TCR clonal expansion has also been correlated with reduced disease progression.39,40 To further explore clonal expansion in our study, we set a cutoff of >0.1% of total PBMCs to select for highly expanded clonotypes. This criterion identified 219 private clonotypes as a reflection of T cell diversity and clonal expansion, which predominantly corresponded to the effector T cell populations (Figure 5A). The majority of expanded clones consisted of CD8+ TEM cells and also included CD8+ NKT, CD4+ CTL, and MAIT cells. Of the 219 private expanded clonotypes, only 1.4% were lost clones, 15.5% novel, and vast majority at 83.1% persistent clones (Figure 5B). When all expanded clonotypes were pooled, we noted 3 additional clonotypes that were shared by more than 1 patient, with the majority of common clonotypes corresponding to MAIT cells. We then calculated the Jaccard index for highly expanded compared to lost, persistent, and novel clonotypes. By far, the highly expanded clonotypes contributed to the largest percentage of persistent clonotypes at 15.76% compared to lost and novel clonotypes (Figure 5C). To examine the correlation of T cell expansion with treatment effect, we combined T cell clonotype diversity and expansion for all individual patients across time points as a proportion of all PBMCs compared to PFS (Figure 5D). An increasing trend for peripheral expansion of expanded clonotypes between cycle 1 and cycle 7/progression compared to PFS was observed, and a statistically significant trend was observed between cycle 1 and cycle 17/progression compared to PFS (Figure 5E). Thus, the expansion of clonotypes over the course of treatment correlates with the treatment response of combined anti-PD-1 CPI and chemotherapy in our small cell/neuroendocrine population. A distinct gene expression profile was identified that persisted in cycle 1 and throughout all cycles, suggesting that these clonotypes can be identified even prior to treatment (Figures 6A and S4). SCCs uniformly progress along an aggressive clinical course, and SCC of the bladder and small cell cancer/NEPC are no exception. Molecular similarities among small cell cancers suggest commonalities in susceptibilities and treatment. Here, we performed a clinical trial combining anti-PD-1 CPI, pembrolizumab, and platinum-based chemotherapy in urologic malignancies of SCC of the bladder and small cell cancer/ NEPC for patients who would have received systemic chemotherapy as standard management. We acknowledged the inherent heterogeneity of our patient population. However, we concluded that this combination was safe and tolerable, while no patient delayed or discontinued therapy secondary to treatment toxicity. We observed exceptional responders in both cohorts that support the clinical utility of this treatment strategy. Only 1 of 8 SCBC patient progressed while OS of our small cell cancer/NEPC cohort was 27 months compared to 7–9 months historically for NEPC patients. With the exception of one primary small cell prostate cancer patient, the remaining patients of cohort 2 were homogeneous and reflective of the NEPC population.

Although our SCBC cohort 1 had more favorable results then our prostate cohort 2, there may be many reasons for this disparity. In cohort 1, 71% were AJCC stage III and 29% stage IV compared to all stage IV in cohort 2. Of the 4 patients in cohort 1 staged T3N0M0 at the time of enrollment, 3 have ongoing responses, consistent with similar favorable results observed in a cohort of cT1-4N0M0 SCBC patients with 30% T3-4N0M0 following neoadjuvant chemotherapy and radical cystectomy with a median OS of 159.5 months.13 However, 3 of our patients with visceral and/or bone metastatic (1-01 and 1-04) or regional lymph node (1-07) disease have also experienced continued ongoing responses with a minimal 34 months of follow-up, which exemplifies the durability of our treatment in patients with SCBC. In addition, urothelial bladder cancer in general is considered responsive to systemic CPI immunotherapy, in part due to expression of PD-L1, infiltration of inflammatory cells, and high TMB.47 By contrast, use of CPIs in patients with CRPC has only benefited a small subset with MSI-H, TMB >10 mut/Mb, and mutations in mismatch repair genes, with limited and poor results of CPI monotherapy in NEPC patients.24,48 This may be due to a host of proposed mechanisms, including sparse infiltrating of immune cells, infiltration of negative immune regulators, and low tumor mutational burden.5 While the majority of NEPC tumors are immune depleted, the minority of inflamed NEPC tumors have been shown to enrich for PD-1 expression.49 Interestingly, our deconvolution of NEPC tumor RNA-seq shows similar immune infiltration as small cell bladder cancers but did not define more granular immune subsets. Nonetheless, patients in cohort 2 that contributed to our favorable OS included the patients with extensive visceral and bone metastases (2-04), bone and lymph node disease (2-02), metastatic lymph node only disease (2-06), local pelvic lymph node involvement (2-08), and bone and bone marrow involvement (2-05).

Our findings in NEPC are currently being validated in the ongoing phase 2 trial KEYNOTE-365 (NCT02861573), which also includes pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib or pembrolizumab plus vibostolimab for platinum pre-treated NEPC. Additional strategies for treatment of NEPC are being investigated including combination of nivolumab and ipilimumab with carboplatin and cabazitaxel (NCT04709276) and pembrolizumab and lenvatinib for patients with NEPC and RB1 deletions or mutations (NCT04848337).50 Furthermore, the high prevalence of somatic and germline DNA damage repair gene mutations in our NEPC cohort suggests the inclusion of poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibition as a treatment strategy.

The rarity of small cell bladder cancer with incidence of 1–9/1,000,000, will make definitive phase 2 and 3 trials difficult to accrue and perform.11 Combined with the performance of phase 3 studies of combination CKI and chemotherapy in SCLC, we suggest adopting this combination as de facto standard of care for SCBC.21,22 Interestingly, molecular analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas bladder cohort of 412 tumor samples as well as a retrospective tissue cohort of 307 transurethral resected untreated muscle-invasive urothelial carcinoma has described a neuronal/neuroendocrine subtype associated with a poor prognosis that lacks typical histopathologic features of SCBC.51,52 This implies that bladder cancers with a neuronal/small cell phenotype may be more pervasive than previously described. Although, the formal susceptibility of neuronal bladder subtypes to etoposide and cisplatin has largely been untested, implementation of molecular subtyping of bladder cancer to select treatment allocation will identify more patients that could benefit to this treatment strategy.

This clinical trial allowed us the opportunity to study host-cancer evolution in the periphery through single-cell RNA and TCR sequencing of peripheral lymphocytes prior to treatment and during therapy. We were interested to determine if combination chemotherapy and anti-PD-1 CPI in this patient population will expand the TCR repertoire or induce novel clonotypes in the periphery, and if these clonotypes would be in an exhausted population or already in an activated state. Our results extend the results of earlier studies, and several observations can be made. First, the majority of clonal diversity existed prior to treatment with an activated phenotype in our persistent clones. Second, the treatment combination led to clonal expansion of a subset of clonotypes that exhibited a gene expression signature enriched in cytotoxic genes and depleted in exhausted genes that remained in an activated state in the periphery rather than reversing an exhausted phenotype. Third, the extent of clonotype expansion over the course of treatment may predict PFS in this patient population. Taken together, a liquid biopsy to examine the gene expression of peripheral TCRs prior to therapy could characterize diversity of the TCR repertoire and predict clonotypes that may expand with therapy. The analysis of highly expanded clones may also have clinical utility by predicting response to therapy and as a strategy to identify novel neoantigen-specific TCRs for adoptive T cell therapy.53 Finally, we observed refreshed novel CD4+ T cell clonotypes following treatment, the implications of which will be subject of future studies.

Lastly, this is a phase 1 study with a small sample size not powered to assess statistical significance. We had few patients with tumor tissue following treatment or at the time of progression, which precluded the correlation between peripheral T cell clonal expansion and intratumor clonal diversity as well as whether the activated peripherally expanded T cells remain activated or exhausted in tumors.54 In the future, study of TILs would also allow us to further examine differences between immune-infiltrated small cell/neuroendocrine bladder cancers compared to immune-depleted environments such as in NEPC through the study of spatial distribution and spatial transcriptomics.55,56 Finally, we identified highly expanded clonotypes in patients with exceptional responses but have not further investigated the identity of putative tumor antigens. Tackling these limitations will be the direction of future translational and clinical studies.

In designing the trial, we were promoting the concept of how cancers can be treated based on molecular similarities irrespective of the primary site. However, combining these two cohorts created heterogeneity in the study population. Furthermore, the inclusion of all patients who would normally receive chemotherapy allowed for patients with both locally advanced and metastatic disease (AJCC stage 3 and stage 4) and added to the patient heterogeneity. This limited the assessment of response by RECIST v.1.1 criteria particularly in cohort 1 as 4 patients were not evaluable. Despite these differences, we showed evidence correlating T cell expansion with PFS and present a clinical rationale for this treatment strategy in both bladder and prostate cancer patients. To strengthen our findings, validating our results with larger sample sizes would be beneficial.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board #18-000435, Internal Scientific Peer Review Committee, and Food and Drug Administration Investigational New Drug #137011.

Age and gender of participants are described in Table 1. Out of 15 patients, one patient is Hispanic, one African American, and 13 Caucasian. This is a single center, investigator initiated, single arm, phase Ib study. Pre-treatment tumor tissues were obtained, and histology including immunohistochemical staining for synaptophysin, chromogranin A, CD57, and/or neuron-specific enolase were performed at the discretion of and confirmed by GU pathologists. In both cohorts, subjects received pembrolizumab 200 mg on day 1 for each 3-week cycle with maximum of 35 doses. Chemotherapy consisted of etoposide 100 mg/m2 on day 1–3 and cisplatin 75 mg/m2 or carboplatin 5 AUC on day 1 for both Cohorts 1 and 2. Carboplatin could be substituted for cisplatin-ineligible subjects. Cohort 2 had an option for docetaxel 60 mg/m2 on day 1 and carboplatin 5 AUC on day 1. Chemotherapy regimen and duration (4–6 cycles) were determined by treating physician. Dose delays were permitted. Clinical and laboratory assessments were assessed at baseline and at every treatment. Blood for study purposes were collected prior to infusion on cycle 1 and collected prior to infusion every 6 weeks at odd cycles. Imaging assessments occurred at baseline, initially at 8 weeks, and subsequently every 12 weeks until progression or two years from treatment allocation. Additionally, in Cohort 2, bone scans and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels were evaluated in conjunction with cross sectional imaging. Subjects who experienced disease progression were biopsied if feasible and blood sample obtained. Central safety and efficacy were monitored by the institutional data safety and monitoring board (DSMB). Adverse events were monitored throughout the trial and graded in severity according to the guidelines outlined in the NCI CTCAE v4.0. All imaging obtained on study was assessed using RECIST v.1.1 when applicable for determination of ORR, and PFS, OS and for radiographic PFS (rPFS) by Prostate Cancer Working Group 3 (PCWG3) in Cohort 2. Treatment on trial continued until disease progression, unacceptable AE(s), subject consent withdrawn, or the subject received 35 trial treatments of pembrolizumab.66 After the end of trial treatment, each subject was followed for 30 days for AE monitoring. All drug-related SAEs were reported regardless of time frame. All subjects were followed for overall survival every 12 weeks until death, withdrawal of consent, or the end of the trial. The primary endpoint was to examine preliminary efficacy of pembrolizumab in combination with standard of care cisplatin-based chemotherapy by assessing the ORR, PFS by RECIST v.1.1 when applicable, and OS in Cohorts 1 and 2, and rPFS by PCWG3 and PSA response in Cohort 2. The secondary endpoint was to determine the safety and tolerability of pembrolizumab in combination with etoposide and cisplatin/carboplatin or docetaxel and carboplatin assessed by parameters of adverse events (AEs). Exploratory analyses evaluated the RNA-seq of total tumor tissue and coupled gene expression and T cell receptor sequencing of PBMCs at multiple time points prior to and during therapy to assess the ability of peripheral blood parameters of T cell repertoire expansion to predict treatment response in this patient population.

The study was intended to broadly enroll bladder and prostate cancer patients with histologically confirmed small cell/neuroendocrine cancers that would otherwise receive chemotherapy as standard of care in two cohorts. Cohort 1 included patients with mixed urothelial small cell bladder cancer that were either locally advanced (muscle-invasive with or without pelvic lymphadenopathy) or metastatic. Patients could not have received prior chemotherapy for their bladder cancer. Patients could undergo radical cystectomy and pelvic lymph node dissection as standard of care therapy after chemotherapy with holding pembrolizumab during the recovery period prior to continuation on study. Cohort 2 included patients with primary small cell prostate cancer and patients with NEPC. Patients with NEPC remained on their ADT regimen. Prior chemotherapy was not allowed except prior docetaxel was permitted for treatment of prostate adenocarcinoma with at least 6 months elapsed from last dose. External beam radiation was allowed for brain metastases. Enrollment was not restricted to PD-L1 status.

Targeted, tumor-normal-matched DNA panel detecting single-nucleotide variants, insertions and/or deletions, and copy number variants in 648 genes, as well as chromosomal rearrangements in 22 genes were determined by next-generation sequencing using the Tempus xT assay (Tempus Labs, Chicago, IL), as previously described.67 TMB was calculated by dividing the number of non-synonymous mutations by the megabase size of the panel including non-silent somatic coding mutations, including missense, indel, and stop-loss variants with coverage greater than 100× and an allelic fraction greater than 5%.

PBMCs were stored at liquid nitrogen. Recovered cells had >80% viability and approximately 10,000 PMBCs per sample were loaded on the 10x Genomics platform used for library preparation. Libraries were quantitated and separated by high sensitivity DNA electrophoresis for quality control (Agilent) and sequenced on a Novaseq S4 (Illumina) with 100 bp paired ends targeting 10,000 cells with at least 250 x 106 reads per sample at 200 x 106 reads for 5′ gene expression and 50 x 106 reads for VDJ sequencing. The quality of sequencing runs was assessed using FastQC (Babraham Bioinformatics).

Total tumor RNA-seq of formalin-fixed paraffin embedded pre-treatment tumor tissue were performed by next-generation sequencing using the Tempus xR assay (Tempus Labs, Chicago, IL). Utilizing STAR-2.7.11a, reads were aligned to the reference genome hg38.57,58 The "mappedReadsPerGene.out.tab" files were utilized for quantifying normalized expression values in Counts per Million (CPM). Deconvolution based on CPM was performed using CIBERSORTx, employing "S-mode" for batch correction, "True" to disable quantile normalization, "relative" as the run mode, 100 permutations, and the Single Cell RNA-Seq Melanoma reference.

Bar plots were generated with ggplot2 to illustrate the deconvolution of cell types across tumor RNA-seq samples, with Z-scores calculated based on CPM.59 For a specified set of genes, the Z-scores of these genes were summed within each sample to generate a gene score, which was then used to calculate the Pearson correlation with PFS.

Cellranger multi 7.1.0 was used for read alignment to reference genome hg38 and gene quantification. Filtered gene expression matrix was used for single cell analysis, and filtered contig annotations were used for single cell immune profiling analysis.

The R package Seurat v4.2.3 was used for single cell analysis. Cells with less than 100 transcripts, larger than 20000 transcripts, or more than 15% of mitochondrial expression were filtered out as low-quality cells.60 The function NormalizeData was used to normalize expression level with scale.factor as 10000. The FindVariableFeatures function was used to detect variable features with default parameters. The ScaleData function was used to scale the dataset. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the variable genes. RunHarmony function was used to remove batch effects among samples.61 Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) dimensional reduction was performed using RunUMAP function with “n.neighbors = 80” and “min.dist = 0.01”.62 The clusters were obtained using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions with the resolution set to 0.5. The cluster marker genes were found using the FindAllMarkers function with “min.pct = 0.3, logfc.threshold = 0.1, only.pos = TRUE”. The cell types were annotated based on cluster markers.

The Python package Scirpy v0.13.0 was used for single cell immune profiling analysis.63 T cells clones that both passed quality control in above single cells analyses, and with one single pair of T cell receptors, were used for immune profiling analyses. The function define_clonotype_clusters with the parameters “sequence = aa, metric = alignment, receptor_arms = all” was used to define clonotypes based on CDR3 amino acid sequences from both alpha and beta chain. Clonotypes with the count of cells larger than 0.1% of total PBMCs were defined as high expanded clonotypes. We categorized all private clonotypes as lost if the clonotype was only observed in cycle 1, persistent if the clonotype was observed in cycle 1 and either cycle 7 or 17/progression, or novel, if the clonotype was not observed in cycle 1. The common clonotypes were not defined as lost, persistent, or novel clones. Dot plot of top 10 genes in expanded versus non-expanded CD8+ TEM cells, CD8+ NKT, CD4+ CTL, and MAIT cells were generated for each time point and means generated. Patient specific genes were filtered by removing genes expressed in less than 30% cells.

To visualize scRNA-seq and single-cell immune profiling, ggplot2 was employed to create boxplots, pie charts, and Sankey plots. Venny plots were generated using ggVennDiagram.64 Utilizing Seurat, UMAPs and dot plots were produced. In Figure 6B, enrichment bar graph was conducted using Metascape, applying cluster markers with an adjusted p value greater than or equal to 0.05.65

ORR (CR + PR) was summarized with 2-sided 95% confidence intervals using the Clopper-Pearson method. PFS was defined as time from treatment initiation to progression by RECIST v 1.1 criteria or death from any cause. OS was defined as treatment initiation to death. PFS and OS were summarized with Kaplan-Meier estimates. Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.2.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Student’s t test was used for two group comparisons.
