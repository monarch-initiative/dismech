OBJECTIVES: To evaluate outcomes of Da Silva cone repair (CR) using autologous 
pericardial patch for septal leaflet augmentation in Ebstein anomaly (EA) and 
identify tricuspid valve (TV) morphology associated with patch use.
METHODS: This single-site retrospective study included patients undergoing CR 
for EA between January 2016 and November 2024. Patients with a prior Starnes 
procedure or TV surgery were excluded. Patients were categorized into patch and 
nonpatch groups. The primary outcome was TV function (stenosis or regurgitation) 
at the latest follow-up, with a secondary composite outcome including 
reoperation or >moderate residual tricuspid regurgitation (TR). Adjusted 
regression analyses identified factors associated with outcomes and patch 
augmentation.
RESULTS: Of 134 patients identified, 108 were included in our analysis (50 
patch, 58 nonpatch). Patch patients were older (13.1 vs 6.9 years; P = .04). TR 
improved from ≥moderate in 78.7% pre-CR to ≤mild in 79.0% at follow-up. TV 
function and follow-up TV-related reoperation rates were comparable in the patch 
and nonpatch groups (6.0% vs 1.7%; P > .05), with a median follow-up of 1.1 
years (interquartile range, 0.6-3.2 years). Intraoperative factors associated 
with patch use were multiple TV orifices (odds ratio [OR], 4.24; 95% confidence 
interval [CI], 1.59-11.27; P < .01), absence of severe inferior leaflet 
displacement (OR, 3.24; 95% CI, 1.11-9.47; P = .03), and absence of extreme TV 
rotation (OR, 3.19; 95% CI, 1.21-8.41; P = .02).
CONCLUSIONS: Autologous pericardial patch augmentation of the septal leaflet 
provides comparable early TV function and freedom from reoperation, making it an 
effective complement during CR.

Ebstein anomaly (EA) is a congenital cardiac lesion characterized by failure of
delamination of the tricuspid valve (TV) leaflets, causing rotational displacement of
the functional TV coaptation point with underlying right ventricular (RV) myocardial
dysfunction.1 Da Silva cone
repair (CR) achieves near-anatomic repair via circumferential delamination of all
available leaflet tissue with reattachment at the true tricuspid annulus.2,3
Early and mid-term reoperation rates at major centers range from 5% to 10% with very low
early mortality (1%-3%),2,4–6
considerably lower than previously reported rates for EA repairs.7

Significant anatomic variability in EA often requires additional intraoperative
maneuvers to provide an effective and durable repair.8 Pericardial patch augmentation of the septal or anterior
leaflets has been reported to complete the CR procedure with minimal suture line tension
and without valve distortion.8,9 In our center, the pericardial patch is attached
directly to the TV annulus, and the native septal leaflet with preserved distal
attachment is sutured to the distal aspect of the patch.8 This approach is thought to optimize
leaflet-to-leaflet coaptation and promote repair durability in patients with
insufficient valvar tissue. Reports of pericardial patch durability in the literature
led us to evaluate this practice change.10 No previous studies have directly compared CR TV function between
patients who received leaflet patch augmentation and those who did not.

Here we compared the postoperative outcomes of autologous pericardial patch use
for septal leaflet augmentation to classic CR without patch use. We identified the
morphologic features of the EA TV associated with septal leaflet augmentation. We
hypothesized that septal leaflet patch augmentation during CR would allow good-quality
repair in patients with insufficient leaflet tissue, with comparable short-term
reoperation rates and TV function as seen in patients who did not require patch
augmentation.

We performed this single-center retrospective cohort study from January
2016 to November 2024, including all consecutive patients with an EA diagnosis
who underwent CR at the Children’s Hospital of Pittsburgh. Patients with
previous TV repair or replacement at an outside institution or previous Starnes
procedure were excluded. Some patients who underwent CR had only mild
preoperative TV regurgitation. These patients had severe rotation of the TV with
a small functional right ventricle, resulting in cyanosis due to atrial
shunting. In the absence of an atrial shunt, low exercise tolerance with
impaired diastolic function was considered an indication for CR.3

The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology
(STROBE) guidelines were following in the preparation of this report.11 Data were extracted from
inpatient hospitalization records, outpatient visits, imaging, and procedures
and stored using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap).12,13 Qualitative TV function was recorded as reported by the
reading cardiologist. Preoperative and intraoperative anatomic factors and patch
utilization were determined from operative reports. This study was approved by
the University of Pittsburgh’s Institutional Review Board (STUDY20080084;
approved June 1, 2022), and the need for informed consent was waived owing to
the study’s retrospective nature.

The primary outcome was TV function, defined as qualitative residual TV
insufficiency (>moderate) or stenosis (>moderate gradient).
Secondary outcomes included a composite of TV-related reoperation with TV
function and morphologic features of the TV associated with patch use. We
evaluated the following morphologic characteristics: septal leaflet hypoplasia,
severe inferior or anterior leaflet tethering, distal anterior leaflet linear
attachment, severe inferior leaflet displacement, leaflet muscularization,
leaflet gap, extreme TV rotation, and multiple TV orifices.

Data are presented as frequency and percentage for categorical
variables, as mean and standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed
continuous variables, and as median and interquartile range (IQR) for
non-normally distributed continuous variables. Baseline demographic data,
anatomic characteristics, and outcomes were compared between the patch and
nonpatch groups using the 2-sample t test,
Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test, and χ2 or Fisher exact tests, as
appropriate.

Competing risk curves were used to compare the cumulative incidence of
reoperation and composite outcomes between the patch and nonpatch groups,
treating mortality as a competing risk via the Fine-Gray method. Multivariable
Fine-Gray regression was used to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) and 95%
confidence interval (CI) for patch status and TV function, defined by follow-up
TV-related reoperation and a composite with >moderate TV regurgitation.
Patients were followed from discharge to first event or last follow-up. Models
were adjusted for age and preoperative anatomy. Logistic regression was used to
identify factors associated with leaflet patch use, with separate models for
intraoperative-only and combined preoperative/intraoperative factors. Odds ratio
(OR) and 95% CI were calculated, and factors shown to be marginally associated
with leaflet patch in univariate analysis were considered for inclusion in each
multivariable model. Only factors significant at P < .05
were retained. All tests were 2-sided, and an α value of 0.05 was used to
determine statistical significance. Multicollinearity was assessed using
variance inflation factors, and proportional hazards assumptions were upheld.
Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

A total of 134 consecutive patients with a diagnosis of EA were
evaluated for eligibility (Figure 1), of
which 108 patients (49.1% female; P = .55) who underwent CR at
a median age of 8.4 years (range, 0.14-61.8 years) were included in our analysis
(Table 1). Patch patients were
significantly older than nonpatch patients (median, 13.1 [IQR, 3.5-33.7] years
vs 6.9 [IQR, 2.4-14.0] years; P = .04). All other preoperative
characteristics were similar between the 2 groups (Table 1).

We previously described our approach, and a surgical video is
available.8 In brief,
we performed extensive circumferential delamination of all available anterior,
inferior, and septal leaflet tissue. Chordal attachments at the leading edge of
each leaflet were preserved to maintain subvalvular support. Vertical plication
of the atrialized right ventricle was performed when indicated, and the true
annulus was reduced to an adequate size through serial interrupted or running
plication sutures. After assessment of leaflet tissue mobility and quantity, the
intraoperative decision to perform septal leaflet augmentation was made based on
whether native tissue was sufficient to achieve tension-free central coaptation
without risking suture line dehiscence, functional regurgitation or stenosis
(Figures 2 and 3).

When an autologous pericardial patch was used to augment the septal
leaflet, the patch size was individualized to the anatomic deficit but was
<20% to 30% of the annular circumference in all cases. The patch was made
redundant, as we anticipated some shrinkage over time. The patch was sutured in
the supra-annular position to avoid heart block, with a distal edge anastomosis
to the free edge of the native septal leaflet (Figure 3). This allowed native septal leaflet coaptation with the
anterior and inferior leaflets, preserving a full leaflet-to-leaflet interface
rather than a leaflet-to-patch coaptation. Valve competency was confirmed with a
saline injection test before atriotomy closure (Figure 2). Antiplatelet therapy with aspirin 81 mg daily was used in
most patients, especially those with RV dilation.3

Patch patients were significantly more likely than nonpatch patients to
have multiple orifices (84.0% vs 58.6%; P < .01). There
were no other morphologic differences between patch and nonpatch patients (Figures E1 and E2). In multivariable
analyses of intraoperative factors, the presence of multiple TV orifices (OR,
4.24; 95% CI, 1.59-11.27; P < .01), the absence of
severe inferior leaflet displacement (OR, 3.24, 95% CI, 1.11-9.47;
P = .03), and the absence of extreme TV rotation (OR, 3.19;
95% CI, 1.21-8.41; P = .02) were associated with higher odds of
leaflet patch augmentation. In models considering preoperative and
intraoperative factors, older age at operation (OR, 1.03 per 1-year increase;
95% CI, 1.01-1.06; P = .02) and the presence of multiple TV
orifices (OR, 3.64; 95% CI, 1.42-9.38: P = .01) were associated
with higher odds of patch use (Table
2).

Tricuspid regurgitation (TR) improved from ≥moderate in 78.7%
preoperatively to ≤mild in 79.0% at follow-up (71.4% for patch vs 85.7%
for nonpatch; P = .28), without significant stenosis (Table 3). There was no difference between
the 2 groups in TV insufficiency or TV stenosis at discharge or last documented
follow-up. Video 1 shows the
echocardiographic results in two patients. The rate of early reoperation during
the index hospitalization was 3.7% (n = 4). Nonpatch patients had a slightly
higher rate of early re-repair (6.9% vs 0%), but the difference did not reach
statistical significance (P = .12) (Table 3).

The median duration of follow-up for the whole cohort was 1.1 years
(IQR, 0.6-3.2 years) and was similar in the patch and nonpatch groups (1.7 [IQR,
0.6-3.8] years vs 0.9 [IQR, 0.5-2.2] years; P = .35) (Table 3). Approximately 93% of the patients
in both groups were in New York Heart Association Class I status at latest
follow-up (P = 1.00). The median intensive care unit length of
stay was 3.0 days (IQR, 2.0-5.0 days) for the total cohort, with similar
duration in the 2 groups (P = .08). Among the 8 patients who
had undergone pacemaker implantation, 6 had a preexisting rhythm abnormality,
and placement was anticipated preoperatively (6.0% for patch vs 8.6% for
nonpatch; P = .72). No operative deaths occurred during the
study period, but a 48-year-old patient died after 3.0 years due to cancer, and
a 4-year-old patient died after 3.8 years during a transhepatic ablation attempt
for atrial flutter at an outside institution. Both patients had good TV function
at follow-up.

Follow-up redo CR in 4 patients and TV replacement in 2 patients
occurred at a median of 2.2 years (IQR, 1.1-2.8 years). There were no
differences between the patch and nonpatch groups in the cumulative incidence of
reoperation (P = .52) or in the composite outcome
(P = .32) (Figure 4).
The most common indication for reoperation was significant TR, often associated
with leaflet dehiscence, annular dilation, or dysfunction of the repaired
anterior or septal leaflet (Table E1). An unusual case that required reoperation for TV stenosis
was in a 1.6-month-old patient who developed subvalvar fusion after needing
postoperative extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support for an intractable
arrhythmia that ultimately resolved with medical treatment. Two adult cases that
required reintervention included a 29-year-old who underwent re-repair and
ultimately replacement 1 year later and a 33-year-old who underwent TV
replacement at an outside hospital 2 years after the index repair.

This study demonstrated excellent short-term CR functional outcomes, with
low rates of reoperation, regardless of the need for septal leaflet augmentation
with an autologous pericardial patch. Our group performed CR as the first operation
in 100% of EA patients, even in more complex anatomic presentations. Selective use
of septal leaflet patch augmentation optimized CR outcomes and facilitated the use
of CR in patients with unfavorable anatomy by improving leaflet depth and
coaptation.

In this series, extreme TV rotation and severe inferior leaflet displacement
were associated with decreased patch use. We believe that this may be due to a
larger tethered leaflet area that can be delaminated and used during repair. In
contrast, multiple TV orifices were associated with increased patch use, because
primary closure of accessory orifices reduced the leaflet tissue available for
reconstruction. Additionally, CR performed during late adolescence or adulthood
carried an increased odds of patch augmentation. In our experience, the inferior
leaflet was usually rotated to complete the cone when there was minimal septal
leaflet tissue (Figure 2), but this was often
insufficient to compensate in this older age group with a longer duration of TR and
more dilated annuli. Surgeons also were more liberal with incorporating a patch,
given that future somatic growth was not expected.

A key finding is that a hypoplastic septal leaflet alone did not increase
the need for septal leaflet augmentation, and a more complex interaction of valvular
morphology dictated this addition. Although other centers have reported that
patients with a muscularized anterior leaflet and an absent septal leaflet have been
associated with early CR failure,14 we propose that larger anterior leaflet and inferior leaflet
tissues actually compensate for leaflet muscularization or septal leaflet
hypoplasia, particularly in younger patients.

Our analysis did not show a statistically significant difference in the rate
of reoperation in those with inadequate leaflet tissue, but all 4 patients who
required index re-repair were in the nonpatch group. We believe this modification
allowed us to perform CR in patients with otherwise unfavorable anatomy. The
inclusion of a patch in patients with insufficient native leaflet tissue allowed for
a good valve repair in those who would have been at greater risk for residual
stenosis, regurgitation, or higher rates of reoperation had a patch not been used.
Patch use provided similar results as those seen in patients who did not need patch
augmentation, which allowed our group to achieve excellent results with almost no
need for valve replacement in all patients accepted for EATV surgery. The evolution
of surgical management with more liberal patch utilization may have contributed to
lack of significance by mitigating the risk of excessive tension on the repairs,
which otherwise would have compromised durability. These factors underscore the
importance of a tailored approach based on anatomic and morphologic
characteristics.

A cornerstone of the CR is extensive delamination of all available leaflet
tissue from the underlying RV myocardium, which maximizes the construction of a
leaflet cone anchored to the true TV annulus. Our use of patch augmentation was
applied to address cases in which native leaflet tissue, particularly the septal
leaflet, remained insufficient to achieve leaflet-to-leaflet coaptation despite
extensive delamination. Our center exclusively applies patch augmentation at the
proximal septal leaflet annulus to create a central coaptation point that more
closely mimics native TV anatomy. We routinely used autologous pericardium with
surgeon preference for fresh over glutaraldehyde-treated pericardial patch owing to
the risk of calcification and thickening.15 More recent studies showed minimal degeneration and
shrinkage of fresh autologous pericardial patch in the TV long-term, and a redundant
patch was used to further reduce the risk of late CR failure.15,16
Patch augmentation did not exceed 20% to 30% of the annular circumference, which we
believe reduces the risk of stenosis in younger patients who require future valve
growth. Other groups also reported using patch augmentation during CR, with variable
frequency, location, and technical emphasis. Sha and colleagues17 reported that at a mean follow-up of 39
months, no patients required reoperation for the recurrence of hemodynamically
significant TR when autologous pericardial patch augmentation was used in the septal
and inferior leaflets. Dearani and colleagues9 performed anterior leaflet augmentation in 31% of their
patients undergoing CR, typically using autologous pericardium or CorMatrix to
increase leaflet height and avoid tricuspid stenosis when the native leaflets were
insufficient with few reoperations. Wu and colleagues18 described the first use of autologous
pericardium for EA TV surgery in 2001 with construction of a freestanding prosthetic
septal leaflet. Our positioning of the pericardial patch differs in that
supplemental tissue is placed within the body of the leaflet (Figure 5) and is not incorporated into the leading edge, a
critical component for preservation of coaptation mechanics.8

In summary, general indications for CR in this study align with the recent
AATS Consensus Statement,3 and the
septal leaflet augmentation outcome data and factors associated with its use were
reassuring. Most patients had successful and durable operations, but in the minority
who had underwent reoperation, we found no intraoperative evidence of patch
degeneration or calcification, confirming noninvasive cardiac imaging findings. In
this cohort, there were 3 reoperations performed in patients submitted to CR at age
<4 months, when valvar tissue is fragile. Central regurgitation or suture
line dehiscence at the annulus were the usual findings. Shallow sutures applied in
the septal region to avoid heart block and the quality of leaflet tissue in that
region may have contributed to dehiscence.

Some limitations of this study were inherent to its retrospective design.
Operative reports were used to classify anatomic characteristics, but not all were
explicitly described. Missing data in the operative reports on were assumed to be
the absence of a characteristic. A key limitation of the anatomic factor analyses
was subjective surgeon decision making, which may have evolved over time. A
prospective study of baseline EA TV anatomy correlated with operative maneuvers and
outcomes could provide additional insight into their interrelationship. A predictive
risk score identifying patients who would receive the greatest benefit from patch
augmentation also may be useful in centers with less robust CR experience.
Interoperator variability in echocardiographic assessment could impact the
interpretation of TV regurgitation severity, especially those performed at outside
institutions. Higher-powered studies with longer follow-up are recommended to
confirm outcomes and patch durability, particularly considering the progressive
regurgitation or stenosis noted in some studies with long-term follow-up.

This study provides a valuable comparison of short-term outcomes among
patients submitted to CR with those requiring additional septal leaflet
augmentation. Older age, the presence of multiple TV orifices, and the absence of
extreme TV rotation or severe inferior leaflet displacement were factors associated
with increased odds for patch use in this series. Excellent TV function was achieved
regardless of requirement for septal leaflet augmentation, facilitating CR in those
with less favorable anatomy.
