INTRODUCTION: Malignant meningiomas lack effective immunotherapeutic options. 
NY-ESO-1 is a potential immunotherapeutic target, because it is the most 
frequently expressed cancer-testis-antigen in meningiomas. We investigated the 
efficacy of T-Cell-Receptor-Transduced T-Cells (TCR-T) targeting NY-ESO-1 in 
vitro and in vivo in meningiomas.
METHODS: Immunohistochemistry was performed on Grade I-III meningioma specimens. 
Primary meningioma culture LB3750(Grade I) and immortalized cultures 
SF1335(Grade I) and CH157-HLA-A2.1(Grade III) were established and maintained in 
vitro. NY-ESO-1 TCR-T (HLA-A2.1 restricted) cells were co-cultured with primary 
and immortalized meningioma cells and assessed for real-time tumor killing in 
vitro. Immunodeficient NSG mice were intracranially implanted with 
CH157-HLA-A2.1 and SF1335 cells, treated with systemic adoptive cell transfer 
(ACT) of TCR-T, and assessed for overall survival in vivo.
RESULTS: NY-ESO-1 expression correlated with tumor grade (n = 35; p < 0.01). 
High NY-ESO-1 nuclear expression predicted a worse progression-free-survival 
(p = 0.0167). CH157-HLA-A2.1 cells, with native high NY-ESO-1 expression, 
experienced > 60% and then nearly 100% cytolysis after co-culture with TCR-T for 
10 and 24 h, respectively, compared with control T-cells (p < 0.0001). SF1335 
and LB3750 cells, with low NY-ESO-1 expression, experienced 20% cytolysis after 
24 h of co-culture with TCR-T compared to the control (p < 0.0001). Systemic ACT 
of TCR-T significantly increased the median overall survival in NSG mice bearing 
intracranial xenografts of CH157-HLA-A2.1 by 49% (p < 0.001).
CONCLUSIONS: NY-ESO-1 TCR-T induces cytolysis in meningiomas in vitro, and its 
efficacy correlates with NY-ESO-1 expression. Systemic ACT results in 
significantly increased survival in vivo in high-grade meningioma. Therefore, 
targeting NY-ESO-1 may be a clinically feasible immunotherapeutic strategy for 
treating high-grade meningiomas.

Publisher: No effective immunotherapy exists for malignant meningioma. NY-ESO-1 
is widely expressed in meningiomas but not in normal cells, making it an ideal 
immunotherapeutic target. Engineered T cells have been used to target NY-ESO-1 
in melanomas and sarcomas but not in brain tumors. We found that T cells 
targeting NY-ESO-1 effectively killed meningiomas in vitro, with the efficacy 
correlating with tumor NY-ESO-1 expression. Adoptive cell transfer of these T 
cells results in significantly increased survival in vivo in high-grade 
meningiomas. Our study demonstrated that targeting NY-ESO-1 with T cells may be 
a clinically feasible adjuvant immunotherapeutic strategy for treating 
high-grade meningiomas.

Meningiomas are the most common primary central nervous system tumors in adults over 35, with over 170,000 cases diagnosed annually in the U.S.[1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies them as grade I, II, or III, increasing in malignancy. While most are benign (grade I), up to 20% are recurrent and/or biologically aggressive (grade II and III) [2, 3]. Prognosis remains poor: for grade II meningiomas, 5- and 10-year survival rates are 78.4% and 53.3%, respectively; for grade III, 44.0% and 14.2%[2]. Current treatments are limited to surgery and radiation, with no FDA-approved adjuvant therapy, including immunotherapy. Although immunotherapy research has grown for primary malignant brain tumors[3-6], these efforts have shown minimal objective therapeutic efficacy for malignant meningiomas. This is partly due to the lack of well-defined, consistently expressed tumor antigens that are absent from normal somatic tissue.[4] Thus, current clinical trials for recurrent/aggressive meningiomas are mostly limited to the evaluation of various checkpoint inhibitors either alone or in combination with radiation therapy[7].

Cancer testis antigens (CTA) are a class of proteins normally expressed in germ cells, silenced in somatic cells, but overexpressed in cancer cells[8, 9]. Their unique expression in tumors makes them ideal immunotherapy targets, particularly for minimizing on-target-off-tumor effects. New York-Esophageal Squamous Cell Carcinoma-1 (NY-ESO-1) was found to be the most frequently expressed CTA in meningiomas in an unbiased screen, and its expression positively correlated with meningioma tumor grade and recurrence status[10]. Therefore, the higher grade meningiomas, which are more likely to recur, may particularly benefit from NY-ESO-1–targeted immunotherapy. NY-ESO-1–directed therapies, particularly adoptive T-cell transfer, have shown safety and efficacy in early trials for metastatic melanomas and sarcomas[11-14]. Because NY-ESO-1 is expressed intracellularly, NY-ESO-1 targeted T-cell based therapy typically requires endogenous or engineered TCRs that specifically recognize NY-ESO-1 peptides presented on MHC complexes.

We previously demonstrated the efficacy of targeting NY-ESO-1 through systemic adoptive cell transfer of engineered TCR-transduced T-cells (TCR-T) in glioblastomas [15, 16]. However, this approach required the administration of the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor Decitabine to induce the expression of NY-ESO-1, which is rarely expressed endogenously in gliomas (~2%). In contrast, NY-ESO-1 appears commonly expressed in meningiomas, especially in high-grade and recurrent tumors[10]. Thus, for meningiomas, engineered T-cell therapy alone may be effective without decitabine.

In this study, we further characterized NY-ESO-1 expression in meningiomas and investigated the efficacy and feasibility of NY-ESO-1–targeted TCR-T therapy in vitro and in vivo, supporting its potential as an immunotherapy strategy for these tumors.

Human meningioma cell lines CH157 and SF1335 were obtained from Marco Giovannini. CH157 was derived from a WHO grade III meningioma with chromosome 22 loss (1977)[17][18] , and SF1335 from a WHO grade I convexity meningioma (1993)[19]. LB3750 is a primary culture from a WHO grade I tumor, used at low passages (≤ passage 8). Primary meningioma cultures were established from freshly resected tumors (IRB# 10-000655), minced, enzymatically dissociated (collagenase type I and dispase, 2 hours), then mechanically dissociated via pipetting and plated in laminin-coated flasks with DMEM + 15% FBS + 1% Pen/Strep + 10 μg/mL insulin. CH157 cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 with 10% FBS; SF1335 and LB3750 in 15% FBS with insulin. All cell lines and cultures were HLA-A0201–typed at the UCLA immunogenetics core.

CH157-HLA-A0201 was generated by lentiviral transduction with pRRL-MSCV-HLA-A2 (courtesy of Cristina Puig Saus and Antonio Ribas, UCLA), and expression was confirmed by flow cytometry (BD Pharmingen™ APC Mouse Anti-Human HLA-A2, RRID:AB_10646036). PBMCs were obtained from healthy volunteers via leukapheresis at UCLA Medical Center; lymphocytes were isolated by density gradient centrifugation as previously described[20].

Paraffin-embedded meningioma samples were obtained from the UCLA pathology core (2010–2018). Diagnoses were re-verified in 2019. Tumors were randomly selected from a pathology list of meningiomas, with inclusion based on a minimum 1×1×1 cm sample size to allow repeat analysis under updated WHO diagnosis criteria for meningiomas and for immunohistochemistry. Written informed consent and institutional review board approval were obtained for all studies involving human blood and tissues.

The PG13-based stable retroviral packaging cell line encoding an HLA-A0201-restricted NY-ESO-1157-165 specific TCR (specifically recognizing the peptide SLLMWITQC corresponding to residues 157 – 165 of NY-ESO-1 with an HLA-A*0201 restriction element) was obtained from Dr. Paul Robbins (Surgery Branch, NCI/NIH) and generated as previously described.[21] Briefly, the MSGV-1 retroviral expression vector was generated by transfection into Phoenix Eco cells and the vector supernatants were subsequently used to transduce PG13 packaging cells. Anti-CD3 antibody (Biolegend Cat # 317302, clone OKT-3; 50 ng/mL, RRID: AB_571927) was used to stimulate lymphocytes for 48 hours before they were double transfected with supernatants from the PG13 retroviral generating cell line[12, 15]. The lymphocytes were then expanded for 3-5 days in the presence of 300IU of IL-2, and then rested for 1 day in the presence of 30IU of IL-2, before co-culture experiments.

Tumor cell killing was assessed using the xCelligence Real-Time Cell Analyzer System (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). Meningioma target cells were plated in a specialized 96-well plate on day 0 at a confluence target of 80% by 24 hours, in 150 μL medium per well. After overnight adherence, NY-ESO-1 TCR-T effector cells were added at effector:target (E:T) ratios of 10:1, 5:1, 2:1, and 1:1. 1% Triton X-100 was used as a positive control for maximal cell lysis. Cell index values (reflecting adherent cell impedance) were collected over 30 hours and normalized to the maximal index immediately after effector cell addition. Percentage cytolysis was calculated based on the normalized cell index at each time point relative to the index at initial effector cell plating.[22][23].

Female NSG mice (Jackson Laboratory, Strain #:005557, RRID:IMSR_JAX:005557) were implanted with CH157 HLA-A2.1 meningioma cells (1×103 in 2 μL DPBS) via stereotactic injection into the right frontal lobe (2 mm lateral and 0.5 mm anterior to bregma, 3 mm deep), as previously described[15]. Mice were randomized into two groups: systemic adoptive cell transfer (ACT) with either NY-ESO-1 TCR-T cells or control T cells (≥10 mice/group). T cells were injected intravenously (1×107 in 50 μL) via tail vein. IL-2 (1×105 IU in 100 μL) was administered intraperitoneally immediately after ACT, and again at 24 and 48 hours for T cell support. Mice were monitored for disease progression and humanely euthanized per institutional guidelines upon signs of tumor burden.

A power analysis (Shoenfeld formula) with α = 0.05, hazard ratio = 0.50–0.60, and event rate = 100% indicated 8–12 mice per group. After consulting the institutional animal research committee, a sample size of 10 mice per group was selected to balance rigor and ethical considerations. Additional NSG mice were implanted with SF1335 cells (1×104 in 2 μL DPBS) using the same protocol. All procedures were approved by the UCLA Animal Research Committee.

RRID:AB_2242334) and then visualized with a secondary anti-rabbit horseradish peroxidase (HRP) antibody (Cell Signaling Technology Cat #: 7074, RRID: AB_2099233).

Primary monoclonal antibodies (Anti CD3 and Anti CD8 from BioCare Medical; Anti-CTAG1B from Abcam) were added to the deparaffinized sections and incubated for 60 min in the dark. Then secondary antibodies (Opal-TSA fluorophore 540; PerkinElmer) and DAPI were applied, following the manufacturer’s instructions (PerkinElmer NEL81101KT). Human testicular tissue obtained from the UCLA pathology tissue core service served as a positive NY-ESO-1 control, and human tonsillar tissue served as a negative NY-ESO-1 control. Photomicrographs were captured with an Olympus microscope, and the slides were digitally scanned at 200x using the Aperio image software system. The image analysis and supervised unbiased quantification in each sample were performed using the Halo Image Analysis Software (Proscia, Philadelphia, PA, USA).

After cells were suspended in single cell suspension, 200,000 cells were washed twice in 5% FBS in PBS, and then incubated at 4C in the dark for 30 minutes in 200ul 5%FBS in PBS with 5ul of APC-conjugated mouse anti HLA-A2 antibody (BD Biosciences Cat# 561341, RRID:AB_10646036)

All statistical tests and plots were created using the GraphPad Prism (RRID:SCR_002798) software. Mann-Whitney U tests were performed to compare the means between two non-parametric groups (continuous variables), assuming random selection from the population each group represents. Simple linear regression was also used to evaluate the trends between multiple groups (continuous variables). Kaplan-Meier Survival analyses were performed using the log-rank test for proportional hazard analysis, with censorship allowed and assuming independence of survival times or event times of individuals in each group. For each statistical test, statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

To assess NY-ESO-1 expression in meningiomas, we performed immunohistochemistry on 35 specimens evenly distributed across WHO grades I–III. Representative stains from each grade are shown in Figure 1A. We conducted supervised quantification of NY-ESO-1–positive tumor cells using Halo Image Analysis Software (Proscia, Philadelphia, PA) (Supplemental Table 1). After stratifying by WHO grade, we found a greater proportion of grade II and III meningiomas exhibited moderate to high NY-ESO-1 expression, defined as >25% of tumor cells staining positive (Figure 1B). A statistically significant correlation between the percentage of tumor cells expressing NY-ESO-1 and the tumor grade (p = 0.04) (Figure 1C).

Clinically, patient survival was stratified by WHO grade and showed the expected inverse correlation (Supplemental Figures 1 and 2). Based on prior findings that nuclear NY-ESO-1 expression in addition to cytoplasmic expression predicts worse survival [24], we stratified tumors by nuclear expression, dividing them into high (above median) and low (below median) nuclear NY-ESO-1 groups. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that patients with high nuclear NY-ESO-1 expression had significantly worse progression-free survival (p = 0.0167) (Figure 1D). In contrast, cytoplasmic NY-ESO-1 expression alone was not significantly associated with progression-free survival (p = 0.064; data not shown).

Grade III meningiomas are the most aggressive subtype, associated with poor prognosis and elevated NY-ESO-1 expression. We tested the in vitro cytotoxic efficacy of NY-ESO-1–specific TCR-engineered T cells (TCR-T) against CH157, a grade III meningioma cell line [12,21]. CH157, with NF2 loss [17,18], also exhibits high NY-ESO-1 expression (Figure 2A). The TCR-T is HLA-A*0201 restricted. And since CH157 lacks the HLA-A*0201 allele, we transduced this allele into the CH157 cell line with a lentivirus, achieving 98% efficiency (Figure 2B), to permit appropriate MHC presentation of NY-ESO-1 epitopes recognizable by the TCR-T.

To assess TCR-T efficacy, CH157 HLA-A*0201 cells were co-cultured with NY-ESO-1 TCR-T cells or with control T cells lacking the engineered TCR. Real-time impedance-based cytotoxicity assays were performed at a 1:1 effector-to-target ratio. After 10 hours, TCR-T cells induced >60% cytolysis, compared to <5% by control T cells (Figure 2C&D). The kill time to 50% cytolysis (KT50) was 8.12 hours. Increasing the effector-to-target ratio significantly enhanced cytolysis at 10 hours (p < 0.001) (Figure 2E). Cytolysis also increased over time, reaching nearly 100% by 24 hours (Figure 2F). As expected, co-culture with native CH157 cells lacking HLA-A*0201 showed no significant cytolysis (Figure 2G).

Although low-grade meningiomas can express NY-ESO-1, expression levels are typically lower. We tested NY-ESO-1 TCR-T efficacy against two low-grade, HLA-A*0201–positive meningioma models. SF1335, a WHO grade I cell line with low NY-ESO-1 expression (Figure 2A), exhibited 20% cytolysis after 10 hours of co-culture at a 1:1 effector-to-target (E:T) ratio (p < 0.001) (Figure 3A). Similarly, LB3750, a primary grade I meningioma culture with low NY-ESO-1 expression, showed 20% cytolysis at 10 hours under the same conditions (p < 0.001) (Figure 3B). While TCR-T treatment resulted in statistically significant cytolysis compared to control T cells, the effect size for the low-grade meningiomas was lower than that observed with grade III CH157 HLA-A*0201 cells.

To evaluate the feasibility and efficacy of systemic TCR-T cell therapy in vivo, we implanted CH157 HLA-A*0201 and SF1335 cells intracranially in NSG mice (Figure 4A). Four days post-implantation, mice received systemic TCR-T via tail vein injection with concurrent intraperitoneal IL-2 infusion (Figure 4B). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed a statistically significant improvement in overall survival in the TCR-T–treated group compared to controls, particularly in the CH157 HLA-A*0201 xenograft model (p = 0.0007) (Figure 4C). A similar trend was seen with the SF1335 intracranial xenograft model as well, though with a smaller effect size (Figure 4D).

We present a novel immunotherapeutic approach for malignant meningiomas using systemic adoptive cell transfer targeting the tumor-specific antigen NY-ESO-1. To our knowledge, this is the first study demonstrating both in vitro and in vivo efficacy of TCR-T–based therapy in malignant meningiomas. Currently, there are no FDA-approved adjuvant therapies for this tumor type. Most immunotherapy trials have focused on checkpoint inhibitors, either alone or with radiation therapy[4, 7], but have shown limited survival benefit. A major barrier to developing effective immunotherapy in meningiomas has been the lack of tumor-specific antigens not expressed in normal tissue.

NY-ESO-1 is the most frequently expressed cancer testis antigen in meningiomas. In a study by Baia et al., NY-ESO-1 was expressed in 108 of 110 meningioma samples, with 63% showing moderate to strong immunohistochemical staining [10]. They also found higher expression in high-grade and recurrent tumors, suggesting that biologically aggressive meningiomas may be more responsive to NY-ESO-1–targeted therapies. Our data on NY-ESO-1 expression levels (Figure 1) align with these findings and further support targeting NY-ESO-1 in malignant and recurrent meningiomas. A meta-analysis of 23 studies linked NY-ESO-1 expression with worse survival in solid tumors [24], and nuclear localization of NY-ESO-1 has been associated with poor prognosis in head and neck carcinomas.[25] Consistent with this, our results indicate that high nuclear NY-ESO-1 expression in meningiomas may also serve as a biomarker for accelerated tumor recurrence.

Malignant meningiomas sometimes mimic carcinomas, melanomas, or sarcomas histologically.[26-28] Interestingly, meningiomas, melanomas, and certain sarcomas (e.g., Ewing and synovial sarcomas) share neural crest origins.[29] Nevertheless, Sarcoma-based chemotherapy has shown limited success in treating malignant meningiomas,[30] and checkpoint inhibitors that have been successful in melanomas and carcinomas have shown only modest benefit in meningiomas.

Adoptive cell transfer (ACT) of antigen-specific T cells has shown variable results in solid tumors, often limited by widespread metastatic burden.[31] Meningiomas, however, mostly recur locally and rarely metastasize, meaning even partial cytoreduction can yield clinical benefit. Because meningiomas typically become symptomatic only after reaching a critical size, modest reductions in tumor burden can improve neurological function and quality of life. Thus, ACT therapies that limit growth or reduce burden have clear translational relevance in neuro-oncology. Recently, the first FDA-approved TCR-T therapy—targeting MAGE-A4, another cancer testis antigen related to NY-ESO-1—was approved for metastatic synovial sarcoma.[32]

In our study, we used a retroviral system to engineer NY-ESO-1–specific TCR-T cells recognizing NY-ESO-1 in HLA-matched malignant meningioma cells. These cells demonstrated robust cytotoxicity against high–NY-ESO-1–expressing malignant meningioma lines in vitro, with >60% cytolysis at 10 hours and near-complete killing by 24 hours. In lower-grade primary meningioma cultures with reduced NY-ESO-1 expression, TCR-T still induced ~20% cytolysis at 10 hours. It is unclear whether the other 80% of the remaining meningioma cells in those cultures had either insufficient NY-ESO-1 native expression or insufficient antigen presentation of NY-ESO-1, and further work is needed to elucidate the heterogeneity in response at the individual tumor cell level. Conversely, the near-complete cytotoxicity in high-grade meningioma culture likely reflects sufficient antigen expression and MHC presentation for effective targeting.

In vivo, systemic ACT of TCR-T improved survival in mice with intracranial malignant meningioma xenografts. Our model used intravenous delivery with IL-2 support, rather than intratumoral injection, because meningiomas exist outside the blood-brain barrier, and because meningeal lymphatics may enable better trafficking of immune cells to meningiomas.[33-35] Additionally, we have previously demonstrated sufficient efficacy of systemic delivery of TCR-T, even in intracranial glioma models[15]. The success of systemic administration expands the translational potential of TCR-T therapy, avoiding the need for invasive intracranial delivery. Importantly, no on-target/off-tumor toxicity has been observed in clinical trials of NY-ESO-1–targeted ACT for melanoma and synovial sarcoma following systemic delivery.[14, 36, 37] This supports the safety of NY-ESO-1 as a target and encourages its exploration in CNS tumors, where minimizing off-target effects is critical.

One of the limitations of the study is the specific TCR-T used, which is restricted to the HLA A2.1 allele, meaning that only tumors from patients with HLA A2.1 allele in the clinical setting can benefit from our specific TCR-T. Nevertheless, the HLA A2.1 allele is generally the most frequently expressed HLA class 1 allele across all ethnicities. Through direct DNA sequencing of a large group of randomly selected participants in the U.S., Ellis et al. reported that the HLA A2.1 is present in 47% of Caucasians, 20% of African-Americans, 19% of Asian/Pacific Islanders, 34% of Hispanic, and 46% of Native Americans[38] . As such, HLA A2 is an inclusion criterion in over 80% of HLA based clinical trials for T cell therapy, and the most identified HLA subtype was the HLA-A*02:01 allele in 115 trials (43.7% of all participants)[39]. The annual meningioma incidence rates out of 100,000 people based on ethnicity was 9.62 for white, 11.43 for black, 9.33 for Hispanic, 6.18 for American Indian/Alaska Native, and 7.01 for Asian/Pacific Islander[40] . This translates to eligible HLA A2.1 meningiomas at an annual incidence out of 100,000 people at 4.58 for Caucasians, 2.33 for African-Americans, 1.34 for Asian/Pacific Islander, 3.19 for Hispanic, and 2.89 for Native American populations. Despite this disparity between different ethnic groups, we believe a significant overall population in North America and Europe can still benefit from this treatment modality. Fortunately, alternative HLA restricted NY-ESO-1 therapies have been developed by various groups, and the creation of alternative HLA subtype-based T cell therapies is an area of active cancer immunotherapy research.

Another limitation of this study is we don’t have data on whether NY-ESO-1 expression remains present in mice treated with TCR-T cells and succumbing to disease progression. Clinically, there was a report of a sarcoma patient who developed tumor recurrence after initial clinical remission with NY-ESO-1 TCR-T treatment, and was found to have loss of NY-ESO- 1 expression in the post-treatment sample in the setting of extensive methylation of the NY-ESO- 1 promoter region[41] . Conversely, we have previously seen in gliomas that when NY-ESO-1 expression is restricted by promoter methylation, treatment with a hypomethylating agent such as decitabine, an FDA approved adjuvant therapy for other cancers, promotes NY-ESO-1 expression and immunogenicity[42]. It's not clear yet how meningiomas resist TCR-T efficacy, and this will need to be closely examined in future studies.

Overall, our findings suggest that systemic TCR-T cell therapy is a promising, noninvasive strategy for targeting NY-ESO-1–expressing malignant meningiomas. While NY-ESO-1 was the focus of this study, this approach could be extended to other cancer testis antigens, broadening the therapeutic landscape for aggressive CNS tumors.

NY-ESO-1 specific TCR-T can induce cytolysis in meningiomas in vitro, with the effective size correlating with NY-ESO-1 expression. Systemic adoptive cell transfer of NY-ESO-1 TCR-T significantly increases survival in vivo for mice with intracranial high-grade meningioma. Therefore, targeting NY-ESO-1 may be a clinically feasible immunotherapeutic strategy for treating high-grade meningiomas.
