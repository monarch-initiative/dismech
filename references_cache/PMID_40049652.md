Early-life undernutrition is known to be a potentially important risk factor for
the development of diabetes mellitus in adult life. Additionally, some
literature suggests that undernutrition during later life or adulthood may lead
to metabolic consequences, including diabetes, despite an individual's normal
nutritional status earlier in life. Notably, individuals with diabetes and
undernutrition show some unique features that do not fit the usual phenotype of
type 2 diabetes mellitus, such as a low body mass index of <18.5 kg/m2,
resistance to ketosis, and low serum C-peptide levels. Many global descriptions
of this unique phenotype have led to a controversy that
"undernutrition-associated diabetes mellitus" is a distinct form of diabetes,
deserving a separate diabetes category in the WHO classification of diabetes.
However, a few investigators argue that undernutrition-associated diabetes
mellitus is one of the variants of the classical forms of diabetes. The second
controversy is whether adult undernutrition is independently associated with
metabolic abnormalities. Its pathophysiology has been difficult to determine,
given confounding factors such as infections that can complicate the direct
effects of malnutrition. Studies have shown that insulin deficiency due to
pancreatic β-cell impairment is likely to contribute to the development of
undernutrition-associated diabetes. To examine these controversies, further
research is warranted to understand the role of undernutrition in the
pathogenesis of undernutrition-associated diabetes. This review aims to shed
more light on these controversies, focusing on whether diabetes in malnourished
individuals represents a distinct diabetes category and the association between
malnutrition and diabetes in adults.

In 2021, the prevalence of DM around the world was 537 million adults (about 1 out of 10), out of which about 433 million (80%) were living in low-middle-income countries (LMICs) (1). A pooled analysis of individual-level data from population-based surveys from 29 LMICs showed that among the participants with diabetes, 5.5% were underweight with a body mass index (BMI) of <18.5 kg/m2 (2). However, there are limited studies about the pathophysiology of diabetes in this cohort. The term “Malnutrition-Related Diabetes Mellitus” (MRDM) was introduced by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 1985 (3) which was later removed based on the opinion that “the evidence that diabetes can be caused by malnutrition or protein deficiency per se is not convincing” (4). Several studies were released after that, summarized by Bavuma and collaborators in a systematic literature review of 22 articles, outlining this atypical form of diabetes in LMICs (5). This atypical form of DM comprises these unique phenotypical characteristics: young age of onset, male preponderance, features of chronic malnutrition, very low BMI (<19 kg/m2), ketosis resistance, and poor glycemic control (fasting plasma glucose >200 mg/dl) with relatively high insulin requirements (3,5,6).

While initial reports emerging from Jamaica and India pointed to geographic or ethnic specificity, subsequent findings suggest a disorder of global magnitude that mainly affects the LMICs in geographical regions with a backdrop of insufficient food supply. Over the last 70 years, the global footprint of the disease has been reported in India, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nigeria, Rwanda, Ethiopia, Korea, Thailand, Tanzania, Brazil, and Uganda, amongst others - highlighting the need to better understand this unique condition (5–13) (Figure 1). Despite the WHO’s call for further research into this area, the removal of the classification seems to have hindered further attention to this condition, thereby limiting our understanding of how to manage very lean individuals with a type of DM that appears to elude classic presentations of both Type 1 (T1D) and Type 2 diabetes (T2D). More research is therefore warranted to resolve the controversy of whether malnutrition-related diabetes is a unique form of diabetes. Furthermore, it is equally crucial to understand the extent to which the pathophysiology of undernutrition-associated diabetes is attributable to undernutrition per se and/or whether there are other contributing factors, such as infections and other medical conditions, that can also contribute to the development of undernutrition-associated diabetes. Once these controversies are resolved, collective evidence will better equip healthcare providers and policymakers regarding diagnostic and management approaches. This review article discusses the current evidence to address these controversies. For the context of this article, undernutrition is presumed to be the result of chronic deficiency of protein, calories, and micronutrients and is phenotypically associated with BMI <18.5 kg/m2.

While several reports have described an atypical form of diabetes in malnourished individuals globally over the last several decades, its status as a distinct form of diabetes remains controversial. One perspective is that this represents a distinct form of diabetes, owing to unique metabolic and phenotypic characteristics that distinguish it from T1D and T2D. However, another viewpoint suggests that this form of diabetes represents merely a subset of T2D, characterized by low BMI, low C-peptide levels, and insulin sensitivity. Interestingly, yet another perspective proposes that it is a modified presentation of T1D with antibody negativity, moderate insulin requirements, and delayed onset due to the modulating effects of malnutrition on the autoimmune process. To add to this, there are other clinical conditions, such as fibrocalcific pancreatic diabetes (14), which have low BMI and appear malnourished with associated diminished pancreatic β cell function, that are often under-recognized in tropical countries (15). This disorder is characterized additionally by features of fat malabsorption, recurrent hypoglycemia, and hyperglucagonemia in a subset of patients (16). Given these divergent opinions, it is generally accepted that more research is required to determine whether undernutrition-associated diabetes deserves a separate category in the classification of diabetes and, if so, whether there are specific diagnostic biomarkers distinguishing it from T1D and T2D.

The justification for reestablishing undernutrition-associated diabetes as an official WHO diabetes classification can be explored by examining its metabolic characteristics and clinical presentations. Diabetes in individuals with malnutrition has a unique metabolic profile, distinct from that of T1D and T2D. Lontchi-Yimagou et al. conducted a study in Indian males using state-of-the-art stepped hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic pancreatic clamp studies to understand the metabolic characteristics of this atypical form of diabetes (17). The investigators compared lean individuals (BMI <19 kg/m2) with diabetes and those classified as having T1D, T2D, and a BMI-matched control group without diabetes. Insulin secretion, hepatic and peripheral insulin sensitivity, and hepatic and myocellular lipid contents were analyzed using highly sophisticated techniques in the study participants (17). The study results demonstrated that total insulin secretion was lower in individuals with low BMI diabetes (a term used to denote undernutrition-associated diabetes) in those with T2D but higher than in individuals with T1D (17). Lean individuals with diabetes had a higher insulin sensitivity than those with T2D. Additionally, truncal and total fat mass were significantly lower in lean individuals with diabetes than in those with T2D (17). This unique metabolic profile among lean individuals with diabetes suggests that after the correction of glucose toxicity, lean individuals with diabetes had a fundamentally distinct phenotype from T1D and T2D (17).

Similar phenotypic characteristics have been included in a systematic literature review of atypical forms of diabetes in LMICs (5). The authors noted that the specific phenotype of undernutrition-associated diabetes has been classified under several different nomenclatures, including “Ketosis Resistant Diabetes,” “Malnutrition-Related Diabetes Mellitus (MRDM),” and “Protein Deficient Pancreatic Diabetes” (PDPD) (5). In that systematic review, the authors carefully compared the phenotypic characteristics of undernutrition-associated diabetes with those of T1D and T2D, summarized in Table 1 below. The age of onset of diabetes symptoms in MRDM is reported in the third decade or at age <30 years, whereas the most common age of onset for T1D is <18 years, and that for T2D diabetes is ≥40 years in the majority of individuals (5). Unlike the extensive literature on moderate family history in T1D and strong family history in T2D, the aspect of family history in association with undernutrition-associated diabetes has not been appropriately determined. In most studies, individuals with undernutrition-associated diabetes had a history of childhood malnutrition followed by either catch-up growth or persistent malnutrition. Additionally, by definition, individuals with undernutrition-associated diabetes have a BMI <18.5 kg/m2, in contrast to individuals with T1D, which is not defined by a specific BMI, given the wide range of BMIs associated with this condition. Those assigned to the nomenclature of T2D are often overweight or obese, with a BMI of ≥ 25 kg/m2. Individuals with undernutrition-associated diabetes show no evidence of ketosis, in contrast to T1D or ketosis-prone diabetes. Lean individuals with diabetes have higher insulin sensitivity than those with T2D (17). Pancreatic β cell function is impaired in undernutrition-associated diabetes, although to a lesser degree than the absolute deficiency ultimately observed in T1D, but apparently to a more severe degree than in T2D (5).

In addition to T1D and T2D, there are other distinct but rare diabetes phenotypes: Ketosis Prone Diabetes and Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY). KPD has a comparatively later age of onset than undernutrition-associated diabetes (5). The individuals with KPD have a higher BMI and have ketosis at onset. Family history is positive more frequently in this type of diabetes (5). MODY is another phenotype of diabetes characterized by a primary defect in pancreatic β cell function, onset in the third decade, and autosomal dominant inheritance caused by genetic mutation (18). It can be misdiagnosed as T1D or T2D; however, unlike undernutrition-associated diabetes, it has a strong family history of diabetes (19). Undernutrition-associated diabetes can be distinguished from these other phenotypes based on its unique metabolic profile as consistently reported by observations in several studies worldwide (6–13). These distinct features strongly support the theory that undernutrition-associated diabetes deserves a separate category in the diabetes classification.

A contrasting theory states that undernutrition-associated diabetes is a subcategory of T2D, which is seen mainly in Asians with low BMI. Previous studies from Sweden and India have described novel subgroups of T2D, with one of the subgroups or clusters being SIDD (Severe-Insulin Deficient Diabetes)(20,21). The mean BMI of individuals with T2D from the SIDD cluster in one of these studies was reported to be slightly lower (BMI of 24.9 kg/m2) than that of their Western counterparts (21). The other phenotypic characteristics in the SIDD cluster were low waist circumference, low C-peptide, low HOMA-β (Homeostasis Model Assessment of β-Cell Function), lower HOMA-IR (Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance), and highest HbA1c among all the clusters (20,21). Proponents of this theory postulate that the association of BMI with diabetes is likely dependent on ethnicity and that leanness may reflect the physical differences in populations studied rather than a specific clinical phenotype (22).

However, individuals classified in the SIDD cluster may represent an entirely different cohort from individuals with undernutrition-associated diabetes. While the SIDD cluster showed a lower BMI relative to the rest of the clusters in these studies, their mean BMI (28.9 kg/m2 in the Swedish SIDD cluster and 24.9 kg/m2 in the Indian cluster) (20,21) was still higher than the BMIs observed in individuals with diabetes and malnutrition (<18.5 kg/m2). While BMI is not an established criterion for diagnosis of undernutrition, and it is still unclear whether it is an appropriate demarcation for undernutrition-associated diabetes, a consensus statement by The European Society of Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) proposed that malnutrition can be diagnosed based on a BMI <18.5 kg/m2 (23). Additionally, it is not evident whether the difference in the metabolic profile in the SIDD cluster from other clusters is due to chance or whether it is statistically significant. Research has shown that the insulin secretion impairment seen in undernutrition-associated diabetes is worse than the insulin secretion defects seen in individuals with T2D (24). Additionally, these studies did not mention whether individuals from the SIDD cluster had lower fat mass than those from other clusters. Furthermore, there is no evidence that individuals from the SIDD cluster belong to a lower socioeconomic status and are undernourished. Thus, although ethnicity might play a role in slightly lower BMIs in Asians and Africans with T2D, other characteristics such as insulin secretion impairment, lack of visceral adiposity, insulin sensitivity, and strong association with malnutrition clearly differentiate undernutrition-associated diabetes from T2D.

Results from a few studies from India support the theory that undernutrition-associated diabetes may be a subcategory of T2D seen in individuals with low BMI. Rajamanickam et al. conducted a study in Tamil Nadu, a southern state of India, in participants with diabetes and low BMI (BMI<18.5 kg/m2) and normal BMI (between 18.5 and 24.9 kg/m2) to examine metabolic and immunological characteristics in lean individuals with diabetes (25). They found that individuals with low BMI showed significantly higher levels of HbA1c, random blood glucose, insulin, and glucagon than individuals with normal BMI (25). Although individuals with a low BMI showed different metabolic characteristics, the authors classified all study participants as having T2D. The article did not discuss their intriguing observation of higher insulin levels in the study participants with low BMI. These newly diagnosed patients may have compensatory hyperinsulinemia in response to hyperglycemia in the initial stages of diabetes when the study was conducted. The insulin levels might have reduced in later stages due to the atrophy of pancreatic β cells (26,27). Despite the above arguments, it is critical to prove that the distinctive features of undernutrition-associated diabetes are generalizable and replicable in the presence of undernutrition across different geographical regions. Further research is required to explore this contrasting theory to investigate pathophysiological mechanisms that may distinguish it from T2D.

Another contrasting theory states that undernutrition-associated diabetes is a nutritionally modified form of T1D that may coexist or precede T1D in undernourished individuals (6). The theory suggests that when it coexists with malnutrition, T1D clinically presents with a delayed onset and islet cell autoantibody negativity. Studies of individuals classified as having T1D in Ethiopia and other Sub-Saharan countries showed a peak age of incidence between 16–18 years, and it remained high up to the age of 30 years, as opposed to the age of incidence of <15 years in Western countries (28). Additionally, only 30–45% of individuals with T1D in sub-Saharan countries show evidence of antibodies (28). Undernourished individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds from this study who demonstrated a later age of onset of diabetes with a substantially lower antibody positivity point towards a possibility that many or most of these individuals did not have T1D. While it is not always feasible to measure circulating antibodies due to limited resources available in LMICs, and they are not helpful after one year of diagnosis, undernutrition-associated diabetes seems to be completely distinct from T1D because of a lack of history of ketoacidosis (29). It is interesting to note that, despite delayed onset and antibody negativity, individuals with apparent T1D and low BMI have not been reported to present with ketoacidosis. It has been suggested that individuals with undernutrition-associated diabetes have low insulin levels but are not low enough to develop ketoacidosis. This is supported by a study comparing the metabolic characteristics of diabetes in lean individuals with those of T1D and T2D (24). Among lean individuals with diabetes, C-peptide levels (C-peptide is a part of pro-insulin and is released in equimolar amounts to endogenous insulin; therefore, it is used widely to measure pancreatic β cell function or insulin secretion. Lower C-peptide levels indicate low insulin secretion) (30) tend to be lower but not as low as T1D, showing some response to meals (as opposed to the C-peptide levels in T1D, which showed no response) (24). Thus, resistance to ketosis and moderately low insulin levels distinguish undernutrition-associated diabetes from the typical presentation of T1D. For future studies, it would be helpful to conduct follow-up studies in these individuals after nutritional rehabilitation and measure the levels of C-peptide and reappearance of antibodies and ketosis to distinguish undernutrition-associated diabetes from T1D.

In summary, undernutrition-associated diabetes shows distinct clinical and metabolic features that do not fit into either T1D or T2D. Younger age of onset, lower C-peptide levels, insulin sensitivity, and lower BMI differentiate this form of diabetes from T2D. On the other hand, ketosis resistance, antibody negativity, somewhat higher age of onset, and comparatively higher C-peptide levels differentiate it from T1D. Additionally, as will be further elaborated upon below in the second controversy, various aspects of diabetes and its management (including loss of calories through glucosuria as well as the weight-reducing impact of dietary and pharmacological management) can themselves result in a lower BMI. However, these individuals have been excluded from many studies describing this entity. Longitudinal cohort studies with long-term follow-up of adult lean individuals (with BMI<18.5 kg/m2) vs. individuals with normal and high BMI for the development of diabetes with measurement of C-peptide levels (fasting and post-stimulation), antibody levels, insulin sensitivity, evidence of ketosis, HbA1c, and glucose levels, and development of diabetes-related complications would be helpful in determining whether undernutrition-associated diabetes is a distinct category of diabetes. More importantly, once the pathophysiology of undernutrition-associated diabetes is clearly understood, evidence of a specific diagnostic biomarker uniquely associated with this type of diabetes would be helpful to designate undernutrition-associated diabetes as a distinct entity in the diabetes classification (22). However, identifying such a biomarker may not be possible since many genes that hinder pancreatic β cell functioning and development in undernutrition can also accelerate pancreatic β cell exhaustion in obesity. This would make it challenging to identify a biomarker or putative genes that could definitively distinguish this condition.

Fetal undernutrition is generally recognized to program the fetus for persistent undernutrition in postnatal life. It is postulated to cause permanent alterations in pancreatic β cell function, leading to a higher risk of impaired insulin secretion during postnatal life. In children, severe malnutrition may increase the vulnerability of pancreatic β cells to oxidative stress (31), possibly leading to increased destruction, reduced regeneration, and impaired functioning of pancreatic β cells. These events may cause affected children to pass through a phase of hyperinsulinemia (due to hypertrophy of islet cells) in subclinical malnutrition before deteriorating to the hypoinsulinemia associated with frank malnutrition (due to atrophy of islet cells and acinar tissue) (26,27). These adverse effects of early-life undernutrition on the risk of diabetes in later life have been studied extensively by various researchers worldwide. In contrast, the association between adult undernutrition and diabetes remains controversial, and it is uncertain whether undernutrition increases the risk of developing diabetes or whether it is merely an unrelated medical condition observed in individuals with diabetes who are already lean.

Cross-sectional studies have reported a striking epidemiological association of undernutrition-associated diabetes with poverty and malnutrition. A study in Ethiopia showed a strong association between insulin-dependent diabetes and low socioeconomic conditions, such as low education, income, and employment (32). These socioeconomic factors are likely to be associated with limited access to nutritious food and undernutrition. Participants with diabetes also had a history of childhood malnutrition. They had significantly lower weight, height, BMI, and fat mass than those without diabetes (32). Tripathy and Samal described the clinical features of diabetes in malnourished individuals over three decades in India. They reported that all patients experienced poverty and presented with emaciation, asthenia, extreme weakness, body muscle cramps, evidence of skin infections, and dehydration (6). Over 90% of these individuals were poor, residing in rural areas, and had a history of childhood malnutrition. Additionally, individuals with diabetes had a mean BMI of 14 kg/m2 and were lean even before the onset of symptoms of diabetes (6). They had lower insulin levels and higher levels of counter-regulatory hormones such as growth hormone and glucagon. The authors postulated that the associated micronutrient deficiency in the study participants might be responsible for free radical damage to pancreatic β cells, leading to the onset of diabetes (6).

One study in Tanzanian adults investigated the association between adult undernutrition and its long-term effects on insulin production (12). They followed 630 Tanzanian adults recruited for other studies and reported their BMI 7–14 years prior in conjunction with current BMI, HIV status, and fasting postprandial plasma insulin levels (12). C-peptide levels were not included in the study. The prior BMI of the study participants was categorized as <17 kg/m2 (severely malnourished), 17 – 18.5 kg/m2 (moderately malnourished), and >18.5 kg/m2 (not malnourished), whereas the current BMI of the participants was categorized as <18.5 kg/m2 (malnourished), 18.5 – 25 kg/m2 (normal) and >25 kg/m2 (overweight). The investigators observed that the participants’ current BMI showed a strong positive association with plasma insulin levels at baseline, 30 minutes, and 120 minutes after an oral glucose tolerance test, indicating that the lower the BMI of the study participants, the lower the plasma insulin levels (12). Furthermore, the authors reported that men with severe malnutrition are at a high risk of developing persistently lower insulin production, even after gaining weight within normal ranges (12).

Another study from Eastern India measured the relationship between undernutrition and diabetes (33). Study participants in the underweight category (BMI<18.5 kg/m2) showed a trend of higher blood glucose levels (>270 mm/dl) than participants in the normal and overweight categories. Additionally, the researchers found a negative correlation between blood glucose levels and waist circumference as well as hip circumference, meaning individuals with lower waist and hip circumference showed higher blood glucose levels (33). The authors suggested that malnutrition superimposed by infections and pollution may exacerbate the damage to pancreatic β cells and the onset of diabetes in rural/semi-urban settings. Although lower BMI was associated with hyperglycemia, this study did not measure insulin levels. Hyperglycemia in these individuals might be due to inadequate medical care or lower treatment adherence. Also, low BMI in these individuals might be due to weight loss secondary to hyperglycemia and/or dietary or pharmacological management of diabetes. More research is required to elucidate any such potential factors that might be responsible for a low BMI in individuals with diabetes.

In the studies mentioned above, adults with low BMI had a significantly higher prevalence of diabetes, supporting the view that adult undernutrition is independently associated with metabolic abnormalities. In addition to BMI, other signs of malnutrition, such as abnormal body composition, which may reflect persistent malnutrition, are also significantly associated with diabetes (24,32). One controversial finding regarding the association between malnutrition and diabetes is that while undernutrition is highly prevalent in LMICs such as Ethiopia, most individuals with undernutrition do not develop diabetes (34). Another conflicting point of view may suggest reverse causation, i.e., diabetes is responsible for causing poverty and malnutrition in LMICs (32). However, the research in Ethiopia indicates no association between markers of poverty and the duration of diabetes but found an association between factors preceding the onset of diabetes (such as poor education), countering the idea of reverse causation (32). Although the cross-sectional design of most of the studies did not provide the highest level of evidence to establish a causal association between malnutrition and diabetes, it points toward the theory that malnutrition is a necessary (although not sufficient) factor in the development of diabetes in individuals with low BMI. Hence, further research is needed to understand the natural history of diabetes in undernourished individuals.

Understanding the pathophysiological mechanisms of undernutrition-associated diabetes would contribute to evaluating the association between adult malnutrition and diabetes (Figure 2). The first possible mechanism of the direct effects of undernutrition states that undernutrition may compromise the availability of essential nutrients required for the pancreatic β cell health and function, such as amino acids, vitamins, and minerals. This may lead to impairment of insulin secretion in response to glucose stimuli, contributing to glucose dysregulation and diabetes (35). It has also been hypothesized that ketosis resistance in this type of diabetes could be nutritionally mediated, given that lysine, the precursor of carnitine, is commonly deficient in the diets of undernourished individuals. Carnitine, in turn, is essential for ketogenesis in the liver, helping to translocate free fatty acids across the mitochondrial membrane for β-oxidation to ketones (35). However, more studies on such amino acids are required to understand whether their levels in individuals with undernutrition and diabetes are sensitive and specific enough to be determined as one of the pathophysiological mechanisms of undernutrition-associated diabetes. This can potentially be studied using omics in understanding the mitochondrial function and, thereby, the oxygen transport chain compared to age-matched normal individuals and patients with T2D and T1D. These studies should be ideally done in the peripheral blood as well as at the tissue level, especially through a muscle biopsy at the muscular level. Antenatal undernutrition can concordantly be associated with defects in endocrine pancreatic diabetes and the subsequent evolution of diabetes mellitus (36). The association of low birth weight with subsequent adult-onset diabetes has been well established in its descriptions with the Barker hypothesis (37). Compromise in pancreatic β cell function in the offspring can be associated with maternal infections and infestations in tropical countries, such as Malaria acquired during pregnancy (38,39). Prolactin is a major stimulator of β-cell proliferation and survival along with insulin, IGF-1, and glucose. Low prolactin in the setting of low adipose tissue in conjunction with low IGF levels could further exacerbate the development of undernutrition-associated diabetes (40).

Cassava, or tapioca, is widely cultivated and consumed as a staple food in many tropical countries (41). It is a cheap source of high carbohydrates but contains low amounts of proteins (41). It is also known to contain cyanogenic glycosides, linamarin, and lotaustralin, which are postulated to increase the risk of diabetes (41). High consumption of cassava over a longer duration is correlated with a higher prevalence of undernutrition-associated diabetes (41). This finding is supported by studies in rats where a high consumption of tapioca led to a 3.2 times increased risk of diabetes (41). However, these findings are inconsistent with other studies that found no significant association between cassava consumption and the risk of diabetes (41). Thus, although cassava seems to be one of the risk factors for the development of undernutrition-associated diabetes, more research is needed to confirm the strength of this association.

Cytochrome P450 activates a number of protoxins, and the increased expression of Cytochrome P450 2E1mRNA in peripheral blood mononuclear cells was found in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus, associated with liver dysfunction (42). Furthermore, cytochrome p450 1A enzymes are induced in human islets following pollutant exposure and possible interactions with proinflammatory cytokines (43). Epidemiological studies found that contamination of the environment with exogenous chemicals (xenobiotics in industrial waste chemicals, plastics, pesticides, and pharmaceuticals can contribute to a higher risk of diabetes (44,45).

Another possible mechanism for developing undernutrition-associated diabetes is that antioxidant deficiency in malnutrition may expose the pancreatic β cells to higher oxidative stress (a high prooxidant/antioxidant ratio), evident from increased lipid peroxidation and reduced antioxidant reserve (46). In a study aimed to assess the levels of oxidative stress in patients with undernutrition-associated diabetes, plasma antioxidant vitamin A levels were significantly lower in undernutrition-associated diabetes compared to undernourished controls (46). The activities of catalase and glutathione peroxidase were significantly lower in patients with diabetes and malnutrition (46). Ceruloplasmin levels were significantly higher in patients with undernutrition-associated diabetes (46). Additionally, DNA damage expressed as a percentage of single-stranded DNA was higher in undernutrition-associated diabetes compared to their non-insulin dependent counterparts (46). Furthermore, research shows that chronic oxidative stress leads to defective insulin gene expression by causing dysfunction of two transcription factors: PDX-1 and MafA (47). This results in the inactivation of insulin promoter, impacting pancreatic β cell function and reducing insulin secretion (47). Also, excessive levels of reactive oxygen species may lead to increased apoptosis and reduced cell mass of pancreatic β cells (47). Studies show that these harmful effects of oxidative stress on pancreatic β cells can be partially reversed by a combination of antioxidant agents, including vitamins A, C, and E (47). Based on these data, it can be hypothesized that a combination of increased oxidative stress and malnutrition may be one of the significant contributory factors in the pathogenesis of pancreatic β cell dysfunction and undernutrition-associated diabetes (46).

Persistent undernutrition seems to impact insulin sensitivity and the risk of diabetes in adults. However, studies report inconsistent results of insulin requirements depending on whether glucose toxicity was reversed in the study participants. A few earlier studies have reported lower insulin sensitivity (possibly without correcting hyperglycemia) in individuals with a history of malnutrition. A case-control study compared insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance in Kwashiorkor and Marasmus survivors and community controls among Jamaican adults (48). Marasmus survivors had lower insulin sensitivity than kwashiorkor survivors and community controls (48). Nyirenda et al. compared underweight and normal-weight Ugandan adults, both with new-onset non-autoimmune diabetes, to find that under-weight individuals had lower visceral adiposity and lower waist circumference. Despite this, both groups had similar waist-to-hip ratios (0.91 vs. 0.9). This suggests that insulin resistance is less likely, and instead, beta cell secretory dysfunction may play a more prominent role in pathogenesis (49,50). Additional South Asian studies have found higher diabetes incidence in low BMI subgroups with low adiposity compared with the US population, also suggesting that factors other than insulin resistance play an important role in the natural history of diabetes in low BMI diabetes (51–55). In one of the studies mentioned previously, the investigators used stepped hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic pancreatic clamp studies to measure and analyze hepatic and peripheral insulin sensitivity in lean individuals with diabetes and those with T1D and T2D (24). In contrast to previous reports that these individuals had high insulin requirements, this study kept the study participants’ HbA1c levels lower than 8% for at least three months prior to the studies and performed all studies at normoglycemic state. Under these controlled conditions, these individuals demonstrated normal insulin sensitivity under insulin clamp conditions. So, more such studies would be beneficial to understand the effect of undernutrition on insulin sensitivity.

Kanungo et al. conducted genetic and immunological studies in individuals with ‘malnutrition-modulated diabetes’ (as named in the article) and healthy controls from Cuttack, India. Individuals classified as having malnutrition-modulated diabetes reported weakness, prostration, weight loss, increased appetite, and osmotic symptoms without ketonuria. Their mean age was 26 years, and only 2.8% of individuals in this group had a family history of diabetes. Antibodies to either glutamate decarboxylase 65 (GAD65-Abs) or tyrosine pyrophosphatase (IA2-Abs) were positive in 30% of individuals, and antibodies to ICA512 were positive in 14% of individuals with malnutrition-modulated diabetes. Of these, 53% had at least one autoantibody, and the rest of the study participants were negative for known antibodies (56). Autoantibody-negative patients were analyzed for HLA and MICA markers, and they had higher DR7-DQ9 and MICA allele 9 markers, indicating a possible autoimmune reaction to an unidentified dietary antigen (56). These findings suggest that a small proportion of the adults in this study may have a form of autoimmune diabetes, which may be expected to progress more slowly in adults and may also be impacted by nutritional status. Therefore, those studies that aimed to carefully explore the physiology of this condition have meticulously excluded individuals with positive antibodies(17,49,50).

Fetal and early postnatal undernutrition is postulated to be one of the risk factors for the development of diabetes in later life (57). Studies suggest that undernutrition in early life leads to epigenetic modifications, including DNA methylation and changes in patterns of expression of genes (57). The genes associated with glucose metabolism, such as pancreatic β cell function, glucose tolerance, and insulin sensitivity, have also been found to be affected by epigenetic modifications (57). Dutch famine studies in individuals exposed to the famine in early life found lower IGF-2 (Insulin-like Growth factor, critical in human growth and development) methylation levels compared to unexposed control subjects six decades after the famine (57).

Histone post-translational modification is influenced by genetic and environmental factors, and histone methylation and acetylation have been associated with various pathological processes of diabetes, such as in the ‘metabolic memory’ phenomenon and inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction. Compared with non-diabetic patients, increased acetylation of histone H3 binding to TNF-α promoters have been found in diabetic blood monocytes, which promote inflammatory gene transcription in the setting of hyperglycemia (58). Leptin deficiency and leptin resistance can exacerbate the onset of both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, and leptin levels have also been found to be reduced in lean patients with type 2 diabetes (59,60)(59,60). Pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) neurons expressing leptin receptors coordinate metabolic responses to fasting by suppression of leptin levels (61). In the setting of chronic fasting and undernutrition, reduced leptin levels could potentially be involved in the pathophysiology of lean diabetes. If individuals with a history of early life undernutrition are exposed to persistent undernutrition in adulthood, the effects of these epigenetic modifications may further increase the risk of undernutrition-associated diabetes.

Previous studies compared the fat distribution in lean individuals with diabetes with the fat distribution in lean individuals without diabetes, those with T1D and T2D (17). A study by Lontchi-Yimagou et al. reported that total fat mass and truncal fat mass in lean individuals with diabetes were significantly lower compared to study participants with T2D (17). Additionally, the study found that the percentage of hepatocellular lipids in individuals with undernutrition-associated diabetes was significantly lower than in those with T2D, suggesting that individuals with undernutrition-associated diabetes do not have hepatic steatosis (17). Even though the fat mass was reduced, we cannot exclude the possibility of abnormalities in the adipose tissue, such as inflammation of adipose tissue.

A study mentioned previously, which compared underweight and normal-weight Ugandan adults, both with new-onset non-autoimmune diabetes, reported that under-weight individuals had lower total body fat (13.5% vs. 23.3%), visceral fat (level 2 vs.6), and waist circumference (74.5 vs. 84 cm) (49,50). Lipoprotein Lipase (LPL) activity is low in malnutrition and starvation states, which impairs lipolysis of Very Low-Density Lipoproteins (VLDL) and slows High-Density Lipoproteins (HDL) particle formation (62). Furthermore, LPL activity has an opposing effect on adipose tissue and red muscle fibers in regard to triglyceride hydrolysis and fuel production. Impaired HDL levels and function can be associated with inflammation. As such, it would be warranted to further study the adipose tissue of low BMI individuals with diabetes to characterize if their adipose tissue has evidence of increased inflammatory markers. Furthermore, during states of prolonged decreased caloric intake and starvation, prolactin levels decrease. Prolactin, in addition to growth hormone and glucose, stimulates beta cell growth. As such, chronic malnutrition, in conjunction with low prolactin levels, could slow the continued renewal of beta cell tissue, leading to functional impairment. Low prolactin levels are also associated with decreased plasma total and Low-Density Lipoprotein-Cholesterol levels, which could theoretically further stimulate low HDL-mediated inflammatory adipose tissue responses (63,64).

Undernutrition-associated diabetes is distinctly different from that of lipodystrophy. In partial and complete lipodystrophy, there are critical features that distinguish it from undernutrition-associated diabetes, as outlined in one of the recent publications by Rajan et al. Elevated C-peptide levels are present in lipodystrophy as opposed to deficient C-peptide levels in individuals with undernutrition-associated diabetes (65). Associated biochemical anomalies in terms of elevated hepatic enzymes, which are seen in most patients with Metabolic-dysfunction Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD), are present in lipodystrophy. MASLD is virtually absent in patients with undernutrition-associated diabetes mellitus, and hepatic enzymes are normal (65). Patients with lipodystrophy require significantly higher dosages of insulin than those with T2D, and those with partial lipodystrophy may respond in part to pioglitazone (65). Fifty percent of patients with undernutrition-associated diabetes mellitus may respond to oral antidiabetic agents, whereas the remaining 50% need insulin in low to moderate doses. The common feature that may be comparable between lipodystrophy and undernutrition-associated diabetes is that they generally have a lower percentage of total body fat (<20%) on Dual X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scans.

It would be interesting to consider the potential effects of persistent insulin deficiency on fat and muscle mass in malnutrition-related lean diabetes. Since insulin is an anabolic hormone that promotes lipogenesis and protein synthesis, its deficiency could lead to increased lipolysis and muscle wasting, possibly contributing to lower BMI (66). This can be explained by an understanding of the molecular mechanisms of insulin-mediated protein synthesis. In the case of normal insulin levels, the insulin and IGF-1 receptors play a role in the activation of AKT through the PI3K/AKT pathway. Activated AKT phosphorylates FoxO transcription factors and inhibits their transcription. This inhibits the upregulation of atrophy-related genes (MAFbx and MuRF1) in skeletal muscle, which represses skeletal muscle atrophy. However, in the case of insulin deficiency, loss of insulin-IGF-action in skeletal muscle leads to increased transcriptional activity of FoxO factors, which promotes the expression of muscle atrophy-related genes and increases muscle atrophy. Thus, in insulin-deficient states, the usual repression of muscle atrophy fails, resulting in muscle wasting (67). Thus, these biochemical actions of insulin support the argument that insulin deficiency can exacerbate muscle wasting in undernutrition-associated diabetes.

Insulin also plays a significant role in lipid metabolism. Insulin inhibits FoxO transcription factor activity. In the absence of insulin, FoxO proteins become more active. This leads to the upregulation of genes involved in lipolysis (68). Additionally, FoxO activation can increase the expression of genes involved in fatty acid oxidation, thereby increasing free fatty acid levels in the blood (68). FoxO proteins also regulate the genes involved in lipid synthesis and storage. Insulin deficiency leading to increased FoxO activity may enhance the expression of the enzymes responsible for de novo lipogenesis (68). Additionally, enhanced activity of FoxO proteins in the liver increases the production of VLDL, leading to elevated levels of triglycerides in the blood (68). Thus, undernutrition is responsible for insulin deficiency, thereby leading to lipolysis and loss of muscle mass, resulting in a lower BMI and a vicious cycle.

The effect of low insulin levels on fat mass can be evaluated using diazoxide studies because this drug inhibits pancreatic β cell release of insulin and replicates the possible mechanism of malnutrition in diabetes. Studies have shown that diazoxide causes loss of body fat in patients with obesity. One recent study of obese males who received diazoxide daily for one year along with lifestyle changes demonstrated greater weight loss and fat mass decline compared to controls undergoing just lifestyle changes (69). Since severe insulin deficiency leads to muscle wasting and lipolysis, it is crucial to conduct more research into this hypothesis to determine whether the low insulin levels in patients with this form of diabetes are low enough to impact protein and lipid metabolism.

The association between malnutrition and diabetes has also been studied using animal models. Animal studies have suggested that rats fed a low-protein diet showed decreased protein kinase cAMP-dependent catalytic subunit (PKA) in pancreatic islets and lower insulin secretion than those fed a normal diet (70). A study on rats fed a low-protein diet reported significantly lower basal and glucose-induced insulin secretion than those fed a normal diet (71). Another study in dogs found that dogs fed on a suboptimal protein diet had impaired glucose tolerance and reduced insulin sensitivity compared to dogs fed a high-protein diet (72). Despite the low protein diet meeting adult maintenance needs, this study showed that it resulted in persistent glucose metabolism impairments. These abnormalities have also been induced in young rhesus monkeys by feeding an isocaloric low-protein diet (73). In rhesus monkeys on a low-protein diet, insulin secretion decreased both at baseline and during glucose stimulation, reflecting reduced insulin storage and synthesis. This diminished insulin output contributed to carbohydrate intolerance, potentially exacerbated by hepatic dysfunction and elevated cortisol and growth hormone levels (73). Further demonstration of persistent impairment of insulin secretory response after a limited period of low-protein diet in early life (74,75) supports these studies on the impact of a low-protein diet on insulin secretion. These studies also reported that feeding a normal diet later in life did not restore the insulin secretion to optimal levels in adulthood.

The pathophysiological mechanisms described above support the hypothesis that malnutrition plays a role in the development of diabetes in adults. However, a causal link between malnutrition and diabetes has not been established. Further research is needed to understand whether this association is consistent in different populations and whether malnutrition precedes diabetes in undernutrition-associated.

Given the above literature, two controversies exist regarding undernutrition-associated diabetes in adults. The first is whether it deserves a distinct category in the official classification of diabetes. Undernutrition-associated diabetes has been consistently reported in LMICs, presenting a unique metabolic and clinical phenotype that distinguishes it from the classical types of diabetes. Identifying specific diagnostic criteria, such as specific clinical signs, genetic markers, biochemical findings, or pathological mechanisms differentiating it from T1D and T2D, would help solve the first controversy. However, the identification of such biomarkers may be challenging. The second controversy is whether malnutrition is independently associated with metabolic abnormalities in adults. It has not yet been established whether malnutrition is a sufficient factor for the causation of diabetes, whether this risk is reversible after nutritional rehabilitation, and what the impact of malnutrition is at various stages of the life cycle. Longitudinal studies with meticulous follow-up of individuals with malnutrition for the development of diabetes would help to understand the relationship between malnutrition and diabetes.
