# Prospective 2-Year Course and Predictors of Outcome in Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder.
**Authors:** Kambanis PE, Tabri N, McPherson I, Gydus JE, Kuhnle M, Stern CM, Asanza E, Becker KR, Breithaupt L, Freizinger M, Shrier LA, Bern EM, Eddy KT, Misra M, Micali N, Lawson EA, Thomas JJ
**Journal:** J Am Acad Child Adolesc Psychiatry (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jaac.2024.04.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaac.2024.04.010)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: To evaluate the 2-year course and outcomes of full and subthreshold avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) in youth aged 9 to 23 years at baseline using a prospective longitudinal design to characterize the remission and persistence of ARFID, evaluate diagnostic crossover, and identify predictors of outcome. Greater severity in each ARFID profile-sensory sensitivity, fear of aversive consequences, and lack of interest-was hypothesized to predict greater likelihood of illness persistence, controlling for age, sex, body mass index percentile, ARFID treatment status, and baseline diagnosis.

METHOD: Participants (N = 100; age range, 9-23 years; 49% female; 91% White) were followed over 2 years. The Pica, ARFID, and Rumination Disorder Interview was used across 3 time points (baseline, year 1, year 2) to measure the severity of each ARFID profile and evaluate illness persistence or remission, and the Eating Disorder Assessment for DSM-5 was used to evaluate diagnostic crossover.

RESULTS: Across the 2-year follow-up period, half the participants persisted with their original diagnosis, and 3% of participants experienced a diagnostic shift to anorexia nervosa. Greater severity in the sensory sensitivity and lack of interest profiles was associated with higher likelihood of ARFID persistence at year 1 only; greater severity in the fear of aversive consequences profile was associated with higher likelihood of ARFID remission at year 2 only.

CONCLUSION: Findings underscore the distinctiveness of ARFID from other eating disorders and emphasize its persistence over 2 years. Results also highlight the predictive validity and prognostic value of the ARFID profiles (ie, sensory sensitivity, fear of aversive consequences, lack of interest).

PLAIN LANGUAGE SUMMARY: In this longitudinal study, the authors examined the course and outcomes of avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) in a sample of 100 youth aged 9 to 23 years over the course of 2 years. Across the 2-year follow-up period, half the sample persisted with their original diagnosis, and 3% of participants developed anorexia nervosa. Results indicate that profiles of greater sensory sensitivity to food and lack of interest in food/eating were associated with higher likelihood of ARFID persistence at year 1 only, whereas greater severity in the fear of aversive consequences of eating was associated with higher likelihood of ARFID remission at year 2 only. These findings highlight the persistence of ARFID and suggest that outcomes may vary depending on an individual's ARFID presentation.

Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) is a serious and impairing feeding/eating disorder (ED) characterized by avoidant/restrictive eating by volume/variety resulting in any of the following consequences: (a) weight loss/failure to gain weight; (b) nutritional deficiency; (c) supplemental feeding dependence; and (d) psychosocial impairment1. Prevalence of ARFID in children and adolescents ranges from 0.3%2 to 16%3 in community-based samples. Estimates suggest that ARFID occurs in approximately 0.3%4 to 4% of adults5. As ARFID is a newly defined diagnosis, little is known about its longitudinal course and outcomes. In contrast to other EDs, which are motivated by disturbance of shape/weight, ARFID is driven by sensory sensitivities to food, fear of aversive consequences, and/or lack of interest in food/eating. Growing evidence suggests that these three ARFID profiles frequently overlap6–8. Indeed, our team’s three-dimensional neurobiological model of ARFID describes the three core presentations occurring along a mutually inclusive continuum of severity, wherein an individual may present with symptoms across one, two, or all three domains and a range of severity9. To date, cross-sectional evidence supports the presence of these phenotypes and their co-occurrence7,8,10. However, evidence for the predictive validity of ARFID profiles is lacking.

No prior studies have evaluated the naturalistic, prospective, longitudinal course of ARFID. A handful of studies, however, have retrospectively examined outcomes following specific treatment interventions. Overall, most individuals were in remission immediately post-treatment (62% remitted11) and at 15-month (77% remitted12) and 7-year (79% remitted13) follow-up. Extant evidence indicates substantial diagnostic shifts occurring between other EDs (e.g., anorexia nervosa [AN] and bulimia nervosa [BN]14–18), with individuals frequently migrating between diagnoses. Four studies have investigated diagnostic crossover from ARFID to other EDs13,19–22. The majority13,20–22 were retrospective studies based out of single treatment centers with modest sample sizes. Only one retrospective study21,22 documented diagnostic shift to AN occurring in 12% of patients over the course of treatment. Neither of the other two retrospective studies observed shift from ARFID to other EDs at 7-year13 or 16-year20 follow-up. A prospective study similarly found no diagnostic shift over an 18-month period in adolescents and youth adults with restrictive EDs19. Furthermore, no prior study has identified prospective predictors of ARFID course. This is an important research gap as the clinical presentation of the disorder is phenotypically heterogenous7–10 and it is possible that this heterogeneity could be leveraged clinically for prognostic purposes. Prospectively designed studies with larger sample sizes and structured clinical interviews that assess multiple ARFID profiles in a naturalistic manner are warranted to further clarify the course and outcomes of ARFID.

To this end, we aimed to evaluate the 2-year course and outcomes of full and subthreshold ARFID using a prospective longitudinal design and rigorous structured interview protocol. First, to characterize the longitudinal course of ARFID, we ascertained levels of remission and persistence at 1- and 2-year follow-up. We had no specific hypotheses about the frequency of persistence versus remission given that this is the first naturalistic prospective longitudinal study to focus on ARFID exclusively; however, we hypothesized that diagnostic crossover to other EDs would not occur. Second, we evaluated predictors of outcome. Specifically, we predicted that greater severity in the sensory sensitivity, fear of aversive consequences, and lack of interest in food/eating profiles would predict greater likelihood of illness persistence at 1- and 2-year follow-up, controlling for potential confounders (i.e., age, sex, body mass index [BMI] percentile, ARFID treatment status, and baseline diagnosis).

Participants were 100 males and females (ages 9–23 years) with full and subthreshold ARFID recruited through a funded study of the neurobiology of ARFID (R01MH108595) from Massachusetts General Hospital, community advertisements, and other area hospitals in the Greater New England Area. A small subset of the current sample (n = 7) partook in R01MH103402; 18-month outcomes are reported in Breithaupt et al19. The assessment period for these seven participants overlapped by an average of 12.43 ± 7.14 months, range = 0–19 months, depending on study entry. Demographics are presented in Table 1. Our sample was 49% female and 33% had received prior treatment for ARFID. Twenty-one percent of the sample reported a current comorbid neurodevelopmental, disruptive, or conduct disorder; 42% reported a current comorbid anxiety, obsessive-compulsive, or trauma-related disorder; and 11% reported a current depressive or bipolar-related disorder. Exclusion criteria were (a) active suicidal ideation by the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia – Present and Lifetime Version (KSADS-PL23); (b) past-month active substance/alcohol use disorder by the KSADS-PL; and (c) any feeding/ED other than ARFID by the Eating Disorder Assessment for DSM-5 (EDA-524); (d) current, clinically significant disordered eating as evidenced by an Eating Disorder Examination – Questionnaire25 Global Score > 4.0, or self-reported binge eating, self-induced vomiting, laxative/diuretic use, fasting, or compensatory exercise over the prior 28 days; (e) intellectual disability (IQ < 70) by history; and (f) contraindications to fMRI (for other study procedures).

The KSADS-PL23 is a structured clinical interview used to diagnose current/lifetime psychopathology. We used the KSADS-PL at the screening visit to evaluate exclusion criteria, identify individuals with potential ARFID symptoms for study inclusion, and assess psychiatric comorbidities. Percent agreement on individual diagnoses ranged from 94%–100%26.

The EDA-524 is a brief, semi-structured clinical interview that derives DSM-5 feeding/ED diagnoses. We used the EDA-5 at the screening visit to rule out EDs other than ARFID and to identify individuals with potential ARFID symptoms for study inclusion. We also used the EDA-5 at the 1- and 2-year follow-up visits to assess presence of EDs other than ARFID.

The PARDI6 is a semi-structured clinical interview that we used at the baseline visit to confirm full or subthreshold ARFID diagnoses. Following the PARDI diagnostic algorithm, participants who met ARFID DSM-5 Criterion A (i.e., weight loss, nutritional deficiencies, supplement dependence, psychosocial impairment), B (not explained by lack of available food/culturally sanctioned practice), C (ARFID does not exclusively occur during the course of AN/BN; no disturbance of shape/weight), and D (not explained by another medical/psychiatric disorder) were diagnosed with ARFID (n = 88). We classified the remaining 12 participants, all of whom restricted their dietary intake by volume and/or variety but did not meet Criterion A to the severity required by the PARDI, as subthreshold ARFID. These participants endorsed Item 29 on the PARDI (“Do you/others think you have an eating problem that involves avoidance or restriction of food?”) and scored a 1–3 on items pertaining to components of Criterion A, indicative of mild severity27. Participants with subthreshold ARFID still met Criteria B, C, and D for ARFID.

We also used the PARDI to evaluate the severity of each of the three ARFID profiles. Each profile subscale contains 10 items evaluating sensory sensitivity (e.g., “Have you been sensitive to variation in taste?”), fear of aversive consequences (e.g., “Have you felt afraid of eating?”), and lack of interest in food/eating (e.g., “Have you forgotten to eat or found it difficult to make time to eat?”). The PARDI provides dimensional scores for each profile and overall symptom severity ranging from 0–6; higher scores indicate greater severity. In the initial validation sample of the PARDI, which included a subset of the current sample6, inter-rater reliability of ARFID diagnosis was .75, and intraclass correlation coefficients ranged from .98-.99 for the three profiles. Participants also completed the PARDI at 1- and 2-year follow-up to characterize the persistence or remission of full and subthreshold ARFID.

The present study was approved by the Mass General Brigham Human Research Committee. At the screening visit, following informed consent (for ages ≥ 18) or parental consent plus participant assent (for ages 9–17), study staff measured participants’ anthropometrics to calculate 50th percentile BMI for sex/age (based on the 2000 Centers for Disease Control and Prevention growth charts28) for adolescents < 20 years and BMI for adults ≥ 20 years. Participants then completed the KSADS-PL and EDA-5 to confirm eligibility and ascertain psychiatric comorbidities. At the baseline visit, participants completed the PARDI and study procedures (e.g., blood draws, fMRI) described elsewhere29,30. We repeated the EDA-5 and PARDI at each follow-up visit. Assessors for clinical interviews included bachelor-level research assistants and doctoral-level psychologists who received extensive training/supervision and attended weekly meetings chaired by a licensed clinical psychologist (JJT) to address scoring questions and ensure inter-rater reliability.

At baseline, we asked participants whether they had received any treatment for their ARFID. We categorized participants who reported treatment specifically for feeding/eating difficulties (e.g., cognitive-behavioral therapy, family therapy, nutrition counseling, ED partial hospitalization program, occupational therapy, speech therapy) as receiving treatment for ARFID. We defined study outcomes as follows: (1) persistence of baseline diagnosis (i.e., presence of full or subthreshold ARFID on the PARDI and absence of another feeding/ED on the EDA-5); (2) remission from baseline diagnosis (i.e., absence of full or subthreshold ARFID on the PARDI and absence of another feeding/ED on the EDA-5); (3) relapse to baseline diagnosis at Year 2 following remission from baseline diagnosis at Year 1; and (4) diagnostic crossover to another ED (i.e., presence of another feeding/ED on the EDA-5 that either replaced or trumped any residual ARFID symptoms).

For participants who did not complete a follow-up visit, we conducted a thorough review of their medical records to ascertain outcomes. Authors PEK and JJT reviewed each participant record in its entirety beginning on the date of the participant’s baseline visit, examining charting completed closest to participants’ anticipated follow-up date to determine whether they met ARFID criteria. In some cases, an ARFID diagnosis was explicitly documented in participants’ medical records. In others, we utilized provider notes describing concurrent symptom presentation (e.g., dietary variety, weight status, nutrition deficiencies, supplement dependence, and psychosocial impairment) to determine diagnoses.

We obtained Year 1 follow-up data on 92% of participants, including 78% who completed a PARDI, and 14% whose diagnostic information we obtained through medical records. The remaining 8% were lost-to-follow-up and did not have information available to ascertain outcome; they were characterized as missing data. We obtained Year 2 data on 85% of the sample, including 74% who completed a PARDI, and 11% whose diagnostic information we obtained through medical records. The remaining 15% were lost-to-follow-up. Retention by baseline diagnosis is included in Table 2.

To characterize the longitudinal course of full and subthreshold ARFID over the 2-year study period, we computed the frequency of each outcome for each follow-up time point. Given the broad age range of our sample and variability in development during stages of adolescence, we report outcomes for the overall sample as well as by age range: early adolescence (ages 10–13 years), middle adolescence (ages 14–17 years) and late adolescence (ages 18–23 years) to permit a nuanced understanding of ARFID outcomes. Given that individuals with subthreshold ARFID score significantly higher than healthy controls on ARFID psychopathology6, our team has proposed subthreshold ARFID for inclusion in the “other specified feeding or ED” category in DSM-527. We therefore implemented our proposed definition of subthreshold ARFID at all time points; however, we also present results for the full threshold ARFID sample only (excluding subthreshold cases).

We undertook data analyses using Mplus31. We assessed data for missing values, outliers, and assumptions of normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity. The distribution for the PARDI fear of aversive consequences subscale was positively skewed and leptokurtic, suggesting the possibility of outliers. We smoothed two univariate outliers to one unit above the next highest score, reducing the impact of these outliers while retaining them in the dataset32. This procedure resulted in a normal distribution; we used the smoothed variable in all analyses. We included participants with full (n = 88) and subthreshold (n = 12) ARFID in analyses; however, we excluded the three participants who experienced diagnostic shift. Out of the 89 participants who did not cross over into another ED and had data available for Year 1, and the 82 participants who did not cross over into another ED who had data available for Year 2, one each were missing data on the PARDI profiles at baseline. Missing data on the independent and dependent variables were assumed to be missing at random, so we used full information maximum likelihood estimation to include all participants with missing data in analyses.

We used two binary logistic regression analyses, one for each follow-up point, to test our hypothesis that greater severity in the sensory sensitivity, fear of aversive consequences, and lack of interest in food/eating profiles would predict greater likelihood of illness persistence at 1- and 2-year follow-up, controlling for potential confounders. We controlled for age, sex, BMI percentile, and ARFID treatment status given known associations between these variables and ED outcomes33,34. We also controlled for baseline diagnosis (i.e., full or subthreshold ARFID) to ensure that observed effects were not confounded by baseline differences in diagnostic status and to account for the heterogeneity of ARFID presentation within our sample. We regressed our dependent variable on the predictor variables (i.e., each PARDI subscale) and covariates. The binary dependent variable indexed whether participants were in remission = 0 (which included ARFID to remission, ARFID to subthreshold ARFID, and subthreshold ARFID to remission) or persisted = 1 (which included full and subthreshold ARFID persistence and subthreshold ARFID to full ARFID) with symptoms. Pearson zero-order correlations fell below the .90 threshold indicative of problematic multicollinearity32. Thus, we moved forward with analyses.

We replicated Aim 2 analyses, examining whether binary presence/absence of each ARFID profile predicted ARFID persistence, aiming to reflect the decision-making process about ARFID profiles in clinical settings, where presentations are often dichotomized for simplicity. Additionally, to ensure it was the ARFID profiles themselves that were contributing to any potential observed differences rather than ancillary characteristics associated with the profiles – such as psychiatric comorbidity26 or treatment-seeking – we conducted two additional exploratory analyses. First, we included current KSADS-PL psychiatric comorbidities in the binary logistic regression model described in Aim 2. Second, we conducted a separate binary logistic regression, regressing our dependent variable, treatment-seeking status (0 = no treatment; 1 = treatment), onto our predictor variables – each ARFID profile subscale.

Table 2 summarizes the longitudinal course of full and subthreshold ARFID at 1- and 2-year follow-up. Figure 1 depicts the longitudinal course of full and subthreshold ARFID. In the full sample (N = 100), 44% (n = 44) persisted in their original diagnosis across both follow-ups, while 12% (n = 12) achieved remission at Year 1 that was sustained through Year 2. Six percent (6%; n = 6) retained their original diagnosis at Year 1 but remitted by Year 2. Eleven percent (11%; n = 12) remitted from their original diagnosis at Year 1 but had relapsed by Year 2. Five percent (5%; n = 5) of those with subthreshold ARFID at Year 1 developed full ARFID by Year 2 and 2% (n = 2) of those with full ARFID at Year 1 transitioned to subthreshold ARFID by Year 2. Finally, of the 12 participants with subthreshold ARFID at baseline, 3% (n = 3) and 4% (n = 4) transitioned to full ARFID at Year 1 and Year 2, respectively.

Contrary to our hypothesis that diagnostic crossover would not occur, three participants (3%) experienced diagnostic shift to another the restricting type of AN (ANr) at Year 1 and retained ANr at Year 2. We individually examined the baseline characteristics and new ED symptoms of these participants (Table 3), focusing on age, sex, BMI/BMI percentile, Tanner stage, and scores on each ARFID profile. The participants who experienced diagnostic shift were two female adolescents (pre- or mid-pubertal) and one male adult, all of whom, at baseline, had low weight and high scores on PARDI lack of interest in food/eating, compared to the other two profiles. At baseline, they did not endorse symptoms consistent with shape/weight-related EDs on three clinical interviews (i.e., KSADS-PL, EDA-5, PARDI) and had self-reported scores that fell well below the clinical cutoff on the Eating Disorders Examination – Questionnaire (EDE-Q25). All participants continued to meet ARFID Criterion A and endorse ARFID profile symptoms at follow-up, despite having also developed clinically significant body image disturbance that ultimately qualified them for a trump diagnosis of ANr.

Table 4 summarizes results from logistic regression analyses in the full sample of 100 participants with full and subthreshold ARFID. Consistent with our hypothesis (and controlling for age, sex, BMI percentile, ARFID treatment, and baseline diagnosis) at 1-year follow-up, greater baseline severity in the sensory sensitivity and lack of interest in food/eating profiles each uniquely predicted greater ARFID persistence. Specifically, for each 1-unit increase on the PARDI sensory sensitivity subscale at baseline, there was a 59% increase in the odds of persisting with ARFID one year later. For each 1-unit increase on the PARDI lack of interest subscale, there was a 42% increase in the odds of persisting with ARFID one year later. Contrary to our hypotheses, the PARDI fear of aversive consequences subscale was not uniquely predictive of ARFID persistence at Year 1.

At Year 2, also contrary to our hypothesis (and controlling for age, sex, BMI percentile, ARFID treatment, and baseline diagnosis), greater severity in the PARDI fear of aversive consequences profile was uniquely associated with ARFID remission at 2-year follow-up, but in the opposite direction of that anticipated. Specifically, for each one-unit increase on the PARDI fear of aversive consequences subscale at baseline, there was a 58% reduction in the odds of ARFID persistence two years later. In contrast to findings for Year 1, neither PARDI sensory sensitivity nor lack of interest predicted ARFID persistence at Year 2. Of note, although age (as a continuous variable) was not a significant predictor of ARFID outcomes, remission rates were numerically lower for older participants.

Results from analyses using binary presence or absence of each ARFID profile to predict ARFID persistence are reported in Supplementary Table 1; these secondary categorical analyses mostly replicated the results of our primary continuous findings. However, at 1-year follow-up, when sensory sensitivity was conceptualized categorically, it no longer predicted illness persistence (p = .724). Conversely, at 2-year follow-up, categorical lack of interest uniquely predicted illness persistence (p = .049). Results from analyses including current KSADS-PL psychiatric comorbidities in our Aim 2 models are reported in Supplementary Table 2; briefly, the presence of comorbid anxiety, obsessive-compulsive, or trauma-related disorder at baseline was predictive of remission at Year 1 (p = .006). Lastly, in an exploratory binary logistic regression model regressing treatment-seeking status (0 = no treatment; 1 = treatment) onto the three ARFID profiles, only severity in the ARFID lack of interest profile uniquely predicted treatment-seeking (p = .002).

We prospectively evaluated 2-year ARFID trajectory in 100 children and adolescents with full and subthreshold ARFID to characterize longitudinal course and, for the first time, identify predictors of outcome. The frequency of illness persistence was similar across both years, with almost half retaining their original diagnosis at each follow-up point (45% persistence at Year 1 and 46% persistence at Year 2), and 44% persisting across both years. Twenty-six percent and 24% experienced remission at 1- and 2-year follow-ups, respectively, and 12% experienced sustained illness remission across both years. Contrary to our hypothesis that diagnostic crossover would not occur, transition from ARFID to ANr occurred in 3% of participants. Regarding predictors of outcome, as hypothesized, greater severity in the sensory sensitivity and lack of interest in food/eating profiles predicted a greater likelihood of ARFID persistence at 1-year (though not 2-year) follow-up. However, contrary to our hypothesis, greater severity in the PARDI fear of aversive consequences profile predicted greater likelihood of ARFID remission at 2-year (though not at 1-year) follow-up. Our study is the first naturalistic prospective longitudinal study to focus on ARFID exclusively and highlights the clinical significance of the three ARFID profiles in predicting outcomes.

When applying our definition of subthreshold ARFID, outcomes were slightly less favorable. With our proposed definition of subthreshold ARFID, almost half of those in remission would be classified as having subthreshold ARFID at both years. Further, of those with subthreshold ARFID at baseline, 25% and 33% developed full ARFID at 1- and 2-year follow-ups, respectively. These findings have important implications for the diagnostic utility of subthreshold ARFID and support its potential inclusion as an “other specified feeding or ED” category in DSM-527. Specifically, subthreshold ARFID may capture individuals who might otherwise be considered remitted from ARFID and has predictive validity for the later onset of full threshold ARFID. Alternatively, perhaps subthreshold ARFID may also be conceptualized as partial remission from ARFID, which is not included as a specifier in DSM-5-TR1. However, partial remission would not capture the 25–33% of participants with subthreshold ARFID who subsequently transitioned to full ARFID. Future research is needed to further evaluate the inclusion of subthreshold ARFID in DSM-5.

The persistence of ARFID, even in subthreshold cases, is noteworthy. Unlike picky eating, a developmentally normative early childhood behavior35,36 that generally resolves with minimal or no intervention by healthcare providers37, ARFID persists in almost 50% of cases over a 2-year period. This finding highlights the crucial need for early recognition and tailored intervention strategies to address this enduring feeding/ED. Diagnostic crossover from ARFID to ANr occurred in 3% of cases. All participants who shifted to ANr were underweight at study entry and retained their low-weight status. They endorsed low interest/appetite at baseline, perhaps increasing their vulnerability for crossover. Of note, their ARFID symptoms persisted despite crossover; that is, the participants who had PARDIs available at follow-up still endorsed Criterion A for ARFID and experienced minimal change in their PARDI profile scores, yet received AN diagnoses at follow-up due to the current DSM-5 trumping scheme. The primary symptom that emerged and contributed to their ultimate transition to another ED was body image disturbance. An individual with ARFID who presents with food avoidance/restriction motivated by a lack of interest in food/eating resulting in low body weight and subsequently develops ANr due to fear of gaining weight/becoming fat need not also develop a de novo ED behavior. Rather, the function of the existing behavior (i.e., food avoidance/restriction) changes, which may further facilitate crossover. Another possibility is that these three participants might have had AN without any weight and shape concern rather than ARFID at baseline38. Perhaps behaviors reported by these participants – especially the two pre/mid-pubertal adolescent females – were consistent with pre-pubertal AN39. The differential diagnosis between ARFID and pre-pubertal AN might be complex in individuals at low weight who endorse lack of appetite, especially in those with developmental low-weight. Our results are also consistent with population-based studies showing persistent picky eating and undereating predict adolescent AN40. Future research is warranted to further disentangle the longitudinal relationship between ARFID and AN.

The observed frequency of diagnostic shift is noteworthy considering higher rates of crossover between the other EDs in both adolescents17,19 and adults15. Our findings suggest that ARFID may represent a more stable and enduring diagnosis than other eating disorders, perhaps less susceptible to instability of core symptoms than other EDs, which themselves are theorized to be united by the core psychopathology of overvaluation of body shape/weight41 that is lacking in ARFID. ARFID shares the hallmark feature of restrictive eating with ANr, perhaps explaining why crossover occurred to this ED, but not others. Shared symptoms may increase the risk of transitioning to a subsequent ED. The low rate of diagnostic crossover from ARFID to ANr supports the inclusion of ARFID as a distinct ED diagnosis in DSM-5, characterized by its own unique features, diagnostic stability, and predictive validity.

Importantly, the three ARFID profiles differentially predicted outcomes. Specifically, consistent with hypotheses, greater severity in the sensory sensitivity and lack of interest profiles predicted higher likelihood of ARFID persistence at Year 1, whereas, contrary to predictions, greater severity in the fear of aversive consequences profile predicted higher likelihood of ARFID remission at Year 2. The sensory sensitivity and lack of interest profiles may contribute to greater illness persistence, since these symptoms often develop in early childhood, rather than in response to an acute traumatic event42. These profiles may represent maintaining mechanisms with a more enduring impact on patterns of food avoidance/restriction that are challenging to modify. Indeed, greater severity in the sensory sensitivity profile is associated with higher likelihood of lifetime psychiatric comorbidities26. Further, severity in the lack of interest profile uniquely predicted treatment-seeking but also predicted illness persistence; in other words, severity in lack of interest was a strong predictor of poor outcome, even within the context of receiving treatment. Of note, the loss of predictive utility of the sensory sensitivity profile when conceptualized categorically may have been due to threshold effects or loss of statistical power. By contrast, individuals presenting with the fear profile typically experience a sudden and acute onset of food avoidance/restriction following a traumatic event (e.g., choking/vomiting). In this respect, ARFID fear of aversive consequences might be more akin to a specific phobia, with an acute but remitting course. Prospective, longitudinal research demonstrates that, although youth anxiety disorders are typically characterized by persistence over time, specific phobias demonstrate a less stable course43–46. Given the similarities between the fear of aversive consequences profile and specific phobia (e.g., some individuals now diagnosed with ARFID have fears that may have previously been diagnosed as a specific phobia—like emetophobia—assuming that overall nutrition is impacted), the fear profile may follow a similar course. The inclusion of comorbid anxiety, obsessive-compulsive, or trauma-related disorders in our binary logistic regression model alongside fear of aversive consequences might have detected unique contributions of both constructs to illness remission due to shared variance or potential mediation/interaction effects. In sum, our longitudinal findings complement cross-sectional data7,8,10 and support the clinical significance, utility, and predictive validity of the ARFID profiles, supporting the argument that these clinical presentations are meaningful phenotypes of ARFID in DSM-5, rather than reflecting heterogeneity within the disorder without predictive validity.

Our study results should be interpreted considering limitations. First, though larger than the sample sizes of several previous studies examining ARFID outcomes, our sample size was modest. Our retention rate, though high, was less than 100%. Diagnosis in 14% of the sample at Year 1 and 11% at Year 2 relied on medical record review, which may have affected outcome classification. The lack of Year 1 predictors continuing to predict Year 2 outcomes may be due to insufficient power, given greater missing data at Year 2 than Year 1. Our sample comprised youth representing a broad age range (10–23 years) and we combined adolescents and young adults for analyses. Our 2-year follow-up period was longer than prior studies, but still relatively short compared to other longitudinal studies of EDs. Because the sample included both treatment- and nontreatment-seeking individuals participating in a specific study, findings may not be representative of all individuals with ARFID. Little is currently known about the epidemiology of ARFID and its prevalence across demographic groups. However, the underrepresentation of marginalized individuals with ARFID in our study aligns with the historical lack of representation of these groups in research47. Future research ensuring adequate representation to address gaps in knowledge is warranted.

Limitations notwithstanding, our study adopted a prospective, naturalistic design and used structured clinical interviews with strong psychometric properties to confer ARFID and other ED diagnoses. Our findings provide valuable insight into the longitudinal course of ARFID and predictors of its outcome. Outcomes for participants with subthreshold ARFID at baseline were comparable to those of participants with full ARFID, especially at Year 2. In fact, most participants with subthreshold ARFID at baseline either persisted with their subthreshold illness at Year 2 or developed full ARFID. The persistence of ARFID – including subthreshold ARFID – parallels the chronicity of AN. Thus, clinicians should intervene on ARFID with the same urgency and dedication that they demonstrate when treating other EDs. Remission rates were numerically lower for older participants, highlighting the importance of early intervention. The low frequency (3%) of diagnostic crossover from ARFID to AN underscores the distinctiveness of ARFID from other EDs, firmly establishing its place in DSM-5 as a unique diagnosis. Finally, our findings highlight the importance of thorough assessment of the ARFID profiles given support for their predictive validity and prognostic value.
