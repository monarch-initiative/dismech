BACKGROUND: Visceral metastases are known to occur in advanced prostate cancer,
usually when the tumour is resistant to androgen deprivation and, have worse
outcomes regardless of therapies.
OBJECTIVE: To analyse genomic alterations in tumour samples according to their
lymphatic, bone, and visceral metastatic stages and overall survival.
DESIGN, SETTING, AND PARTICIPANTS: We selected 200 patients with metastatic
prostate cancer. Genomic profiling of 111 genes and molecular signatures
(homologous recombination deficiency [HRD], microsatellite instability, and
tumour burden mutation) was performed with the MyChoice test (Myriad Genetics,
Inc, Salt Lake City, UT, USA).
OUTCOME MEASUREMENTS AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS: The association between genomic
profiles and visceral metastatic evolution was evaluated using logistic
regression. Kaplan-Meier and Cox proportional hazard analyses were used for
analyses of early death.
RESULTS AND LIMITATIONS: A total of 173 (87%) genomic profiles were obtained.
Eighty-four (49%) patients died during the follow-up period (median
duration = 76 mo). TP53 was the most frequently mutated gene, followed by FANC
genes, including BRCA2, and those of the Wnt-pathway (APC/CTNNB1). TP53 gene
mutations were more frequent in patients of European (42%) than in those of
African (16%) ancestry. An HRD score of >25 was predictive of FANC gene
mutations. The mutational status of TP53 (p < 0.001) and APC (p = 0.002) genes
were significantly associated with the risk of visceral metastases. The
mutational status of CTNNB1 (p = 0.001), TP53 (p = 0.015), BRCA2 (p = 0.027),
and FANC (p = 0.005) genes were significantly associated with an earlier age at
death. The limitations are the retrospective study design based on a selection
of genes and the low frequency of certain molecular events.
CONCLUSIONS: Mutations in the TP53 gene and genes (APC/CTNNB1) related to the
Wnt pathway are associated with metastatic visceral dissemination and early
death. These genomic alterations could be considered as markers to identify
prostate cancer patients at a high risk of life-threatening disease who might
benefit from more intensified treatment or new targeted therapies.
PATIENT SUMMARY: In this report, we evaluated the relationships between genomic
profiles (gene mutations and molecular signatures) of tumour samples from
patients with metastatic prostate cancer and early death. We found that
mutations of specific genes, notably TP53 and APC/CTNNB1 related to the Wnt
pathway, are associated with visceral metastatic progression and an earlier age
at death.

Introduction 65 
Prostate cancer (PCa) is the fifth leading cause of cancer death in men worldwide, with an 66 
estimated 1.4 million cases diagnosed and an estimated 375,000 PCa deaths in 2020 [1]. 67 
However, important disparities in its incidence and mortality are seen among different 68 
populations around the world [1]. Due to the lack of recommendation for PCa screening, and 69 
the increased sensitivity of imaging that identifies metastases in a higher number of men 70 
affected with PCa, leading to stage migration phenomenon, the incidence of PCa diagnosed 71 
at a metastatic stage has increased for the 20 last years [2]. If the most common site of PCa 72 
metastasis is the bone (>80%), the visceral metastases, which used to be infrequently 73 
observed, have become more common. Their most frequent locations are in the lung and 74 
liver, each representing approximately 40% of visceral metastases. Their incidence in men 75 
who died from metastatic PCa significantly increased from 26% in 2009 to 40% in 2016 [3]. 76 
Thanks to multiple systemic therapies approved since 2010 with proven survival benefits, 77 
men with metastatic PCa are living longer than ever [4], but thus may later develop visceral 78 
metastases. The increase in the number of lines of therapy may also be behind the increase 79 
in visceral disease. After progression under androgen deprivation therapy, visceral 80 
metastases confer worse median survival duration [5].  81 
The lethality of PCa is related to the evolution of metastatic castrate-resistant clones [6]. The 82 
landscape of genomic alterations reported in metastatic PCa was established from analyses 83 
of germline [7] and tumour [6, 8-10] samples. The most frequent germline mutations carried by 84 
patients with metastatic PCa are in the BRCA2 and ATM genes, with a prevalence estimated across 85 
different reports to be approximately 5% and 2%, respectively [11]. In tumours from patients with 86 
metastatic castration-resistant PCa (mCRPC), the most common genomic alterations are 87 
observed in the AR and TP53 genes, with frequencies above 40% [12]. The characterization of 88 

6 
 
some of these genomic alterations or their molecular signatures is used as new companion 89 
diagnostic tests for targeted therapies such as PARP-1 inhibitors [13] and immune check-90 
point inhibitors [14], or for suggested new targeted therapies such as PI3K/AKT [15] and Wnt 91 
[16] pathway inhibitors. Here we report the prevalence of specific mutational profiles and 92 
functional signatures (Homologous Recombination Deficiency: HRD, Tumor Mutational 93 
Burden: TMB, Microsatellite Instability: MSI) in PCa evolving with visceral metastasis, and 94 
their impact on early death. 95

Patients and Methods 97 
Patients 98 
The 200 patients included in this study were selected based on metastatic status among the 99 
patients from the PROGENE study. They all provided written informed consent to participate 100 
in this study that complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the CCP Ile 101 
de France IV (IRB: 00003835). Ancestry was based on self-report and on skin phenotype 102 
selected by the clinician in the medical questionnaire. Patients of Asian ancestry, from North 103 
African origin, born in India, or too complex to be classified were annotated as “other” and 104 
were excluded from comparison between patients from African and European ancestries. 105 
Genomic analyses 106 
Archival tumour tissues were provided from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded blocks of 107 
primary samples: 158 prostate biopsies, 28 transurethral resections of the prostate, 8 radical 108 
prostatectomies, and of metastatic tissues: 2 lymphadenectomies and 4 metastatic biopsies. 109 
After histopathological review, tumour tissues were submitted to Myriad Genetics, Inc. 110 
Tumour DNA was extracted from areas containing >30% of cancerous cells as described in 111 
[17]. Germline DNA was extracted from saliva or blood using standard protocol. From 200 ng 112 

7 
 
of tumor DNA (or all available DNA if total yield was <200 ng) or 100 ng of germline DNA, 113 
gene mutation detection and SNP whole genome analysis were performed using a custom 114 
hybridization capture method described in [17], with a panel targeting approximately 27,000 115 
genome-wide single nucleotide polymorphisms and the coding regions of 111 genes. Next 116 
Generation Sequencing (NGS) was performed on an Illumina HiSeq2500 using a 200 cycle 117 
HiSeq Rapid SBS Kit v2 and a HiSeq Rapid PE Cluster Kit v2. The method used to calculate the 118 
HRD score was previously described [17]. MSI status and TMB score (number of 119 
mutations/Megabase) were also determined [18]. 120 
Statistical analyses 121 
Khi2 Fisher test was used to compare qualitative variables. Correlations between 122 
quantitative variables were assessed with Pearson correlation. Comparisons of quantitative 123 
data in different subpopulations were performed using the Mann-Whitney and Kruskal-124 
Wallis tests. Logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios. Receiver Operating 125 
Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to define the better threshold to predict genes 126 
alterations. Kaplan-Meier and Cox proportional hazards analyses were used for analyses. Of 127 
early death All tests were two-sided, and p<0.05 was considered to indicate statistical 128 
significance. These statistical analyses were carried out with the softwares R++ (Toulouse, 129 
France) and XLSTAT v2022.3.2 (Addinsoft, Paris, France).  130

Results 132 
Of the samples collected from the 200 patients, twenty-seven (14%) did not provide 133 
genomic results, all were biopsy samples. Absence of genomic results was significantly 134 
associated with an older sample age (p<0.001) (Supplementary Figure 1). The median age of 135 
the samples was 44 months (IQR=73.25), with 38 months (IQR=52.0) for the samples with 136 

8 
 
sequencing results, compared to 145 months (IQR=15.75) for samples without result. The 137 
characteristics of the 173 patients with genomic results are presented in Table 1.  138 
The median age at metastatic stage was 71.0 years old (interquartile range [IQR]=14.0), with 139 
no difference between patients of European and African ancestry (68.5 vs 71.0, p=0.5). The 140 
majority of patients (80%) were metastatic at the time of diagnosis. Typology of metastases 141 
was ranged using the 8th edition Tumor-Node-Metastasis classification system [19], as (M1a) 142 
only in extra pelvic lymph nodes (21%), (M1b) bone metastasis (56%) and (M1c) visceral 143 
metastasis (23%). 90 (52%) of patients were castration-resistant at the end of their follow-144 
up, but only 16 (9%) of the samples used for the genomic analysis were collected from tissue 145 
exposed to androgen deprivation therapy. Fifteen (9%) patients had a positive family history 146 
of PCa, and 22 (13%) had a personal history of cancer, of which 17 (10%) ones had another 147 
cancer and five (2.9%) two other cancers (Table 1, Supplementary Table S1). The risk of 148 
visceral metastasis or early death (LogRank) was not significantly different between patients 149 
with other cancers and those with only PCa. The median duration of patient follow-up was 150 
76 months (IQR= 14.0). Eighty-four (49%) patients died during this follow-up period, their 151 
median age at death was 76.0 years (IQR=14.0). Age at death was correlated with age at 152 
diagnosis (Spearman correlation; rho=0.94; p<0.001).  153 
Germline mutations were observed in 19 of 111 analysed genes (Supplementary table S2). 154 
The most frequently mutated genes at the germline level were BRCA2, POLN, and ERAP2, 155 
with five (2.9%), three (1.7%) and two (1.2%) patients carrying a mutation, respectively. Each 156 
mutation was observed in only one patient. Somatic mutations were observed in 64 of the 157 
111 sequenced genes (Supplementary Table S3). No association was found between the 158 
mutational status and the origin of the sample (primary prostate tissue or metastasis). 159 
Eighteen genes had a mutation frequency higher than 2% (Figure 1). TP53 was the most 160 

9 
 
frequently mutated one, with 56 patients (32%) carrying a somatic mutation in this gene. 161 
The mutation frequency of the TP53 gene was significantly different according to ancestry 162 
(43% and 16% in patients of European and African ancestry, respectively; p=0.002). The 163 
alterations in the Wnt-pathway genes (APC and CTNNB1) and PTEN/AKT1 counted for 9% 164 
and 7% of mutations, respectively, none of these mutations was found at the germline level. 165 
Mutations in the genes belonging to the Fanconi Anemia complementation group (FANCA, 166 
BRCA2, FANCE, FANCI, BRIP1, FANCM, PALB2, BRCA1, and UBE2T) were found in 18 patients 167 
(10%), including 10 (56%) which were present in germline. The mutations in the APC and 168 
CTNNB1 genes, on one hand, and, those in the BRCA2, ATM and CDK12 genes, on the other 169 
hand, were mutually exclusive.  170 
Using univariate analysis, castrate resistant status (Odds ratio [OR]: 2.935; 95% Confidence 171 
Interval [95% CI] 1.289-6.685; p=0.010), TP53 (OR: 4.734; 95% CI 2.243-9.995; p<0.001) and 172 
APC mutational status (OR: 9.192; 95% CI 2.254-3748; p=0.002) were significantly associated 173 
with the risk of visceral metastasis. In univariate analysis, using the Kaplan-Meier (Log-rank), 174 
visceral metastasis status (p<0.001), CTNNB1 (p=0.001), TP53 (p=0.015), BRCA2 (p=0.027), 175

at death (Figures 2 & 3). In Cox proportional hazards, visceral metastasis (HR: 3.777; 95% CI 177 
1.709-8.345; p=0.001) and, for molecular events, mutations in genes CTNNB1 (HR: 10.95; 178 
95% CI 3.133-38.25; p=0.003), and TP53 (HR: 1.833; 95% CI 1.011-3.322; p=0.047) were 179 
significantly related to early death. 180 
Regarding the molecular signatures, median HRD score increased significantly with the 181 
metastatic clinical status (p=0.010): from M1a (15.0; IQR=10.3), M1b (21.0; IQR=14.0) to M1c 182 
(24.0; IQR=13.5) (Figure 4). The median value of the HRD score was higher in the group of 183 
tumours sharing FANC genes mutations than other ones (29.0; IQR=23.0 vs 20.0; IQR=14.0; 184

p=0.011) (Figure 5). Using HRD score, the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) to predict BRCA2 185

Discussion 193 
Prostate cancer is a heterogeneous disease with different molecular drivers involved in 194 
worse outcomes [5]. New companion diagnostic tests based on these genomic alterations or 195 
their molecular signatures have been suggested to be used for targeted therapies [13-14]. 196 
The PROFOUND study, one of the first studies to analyse the response to PARP inhibitors 197 
according to these alterations, showed some limitations in the molecular screening of 198 
tumours in clinical practice [20]. In our study, the failure rate of NGS from archival samples 199 
was 14%, lower than that observed in the PROFOUND one [20]. A sample age above 10 years 200 
was the main cause of failure. 201 
In our study, TP53 was the most frequently mutated gene (32%), with a higher mutation 202 
frequency in metastatic PCa patients of European ancestry compared to those of African 203 
origin (42% versus 16%). This disparity is consistent with the results reported by Mahal et al. 204 
[9]. Together, mutations in BRCA2 or ATM genes accounted for 10% of cases, as did those in 205 
the FANC group of genes. Alterations in Wnt-pathway genes (APC or CTNNB1) were also 206 
frequently observed (9%). No difference in mutation frequency was found between patients 207 
of different ancestry for genes other than TP53. 208 

11 
 
It is now widely recognized that inactivation of TP53 is associated with genetic instability 209 
with inverted rearrangements and a complex catastrophic disorganization of the genome 210 
named chromotrypsis [8]. Defective TP53 was initially described as a late event during the 211 
natural history of PCa [21]. Germline TP53 mutations were notably reported to be very rare 212 
in PCa cohorts [22], which is consistent with the absence of germline TP53 mutations that 213 
we observed in our study. Recent extensive sequencing showed that tumors from patients 214 
with metastatic PCa had the highest rates of TP53 mutations, but a relatively high frequency 215 
of TP53 mutations was also found in aggressive primary prostate cancers [23]. Concordantly, 216 
we previously reported a higher frequency of TP53 alterations in tumor samples from 25 217 
cases of aggressive localized PCa, compared to those from 132 patients with localized PCa 218 
(52% vs. 12%) [24-25]. Moreover, 33% of tumours with biallelic alterations and 32% with a 219 
single copy loss or pathogenic mutation of this gene were observed in samples from 410 220 
mCRPC patients [26]. We found that TP53 mutations in PCa tissues were associated with the 221 
risk of visceral metastases and early age at death, confirming that these alterations are 222 
predictive of worse metastatic and lethal outcome [26, 27].  223 
On its side, BRCA2 is known to be the gene with the highest rate of germline mutations in 224 
PCa, with a frequency reaching 2-6% in metastatic PCa patients [11]. Somatic mutations in 225 
this gene only occur in approximately 5-10% of metastatic PCa, of which 80% correspond to 226 
biallelic inactivation, and more than 50% are linked to germline mutations [28]. 227 
Concordantly, we found 5% of BRCA2 mutations in our study, of whom 56% were germline 228 
ones. Our results therefore supported that BRCA2 is the most frequently mutated gene at 229 
the germline level in metastatic PCa and, that mutations in BRCA2, ATM and CDK12 genes 230 
are mutually exclusive [8]. Germline mutations in the BRCA2 gene were reported to be 231 
associated with distant metastasis at diagnosis and poor survival [28]. In our study including 232 

12 
 
only metastatic PCa patients, we did not find significant association between BRCA2 or FANC 233 
genes mutations and a specific site of metastasis. However, in univariate analyses, they were 234 
both associated with an earlier age at death.  235 
Molecular signatures such as HRD score [17], TMB score [14] and MSI status [18] allow 236 
functional assessment of the DNA repair pathway. They already are companion theragnostic 237 
markers of PARP or immune checkpoint inhibitors. From the experience of ovarian cancer, 238 
HRD score >42 and TMB score ≥10 or MSI status are supposed to be relevant for therapeutic 239 
choices [18]. However, recent reports suggested that the appropriate threshold for prostate 240 
tumours will be around 20 [29]. We indeed showed that HRD score >25 has the best 241 
sensitivity (Se) and specificity (Sp) values to predict BRCA2 (Se=71%, Sp=69%) and FANC 242 
(Se=71%, Sp=71%) genes mutations. In our study, high HRD and TMB scores were not 243 
completely mutually exclusive, with one patient carrying a germline mutation in the MSH2 244 
gene whose tumor was MSI positive, had a TMB and HRD score of 19.33 and 28, 245 
respectively. Furthermore, we observed an increase in the HRD score with the typology of 246 
metastases and, that an HRD score >25 was associated with the risk of bone metastases, but 247 
not with early death.  248 
Although mutations in Wnt-pathway genes (APC and CTNNB1) are generally rare in primary 249 
PCa (<10%), they are relatively more common in metastatic PCa [30]. Additionally, APC 250 
hypermethylation, observed in at least 30% of PCa cases, was associated with worse 251 
outcomes in CRPC patients [31]. Dysregulation of the Wnt-pathway was shown to promote 252 
metastatic dissemination and treatment resistance, and notably, somatic Wnt-activating 253 
mutations were associated with first-line resistance to abiraterone/enzatulamide [31]. Our 254 
study confirmed, in one hand, that APC mutations were associated with the risk of visceral 255 
metastases, and on the other hand, that CTNNB1 mutations are associated, after 256 

13 
 
multivariable adjustments for concurrent alterations in other mutated genes, with an earlier 257 
death. 258 
Our study has certain limitations, it is a retrospective study with real data, based on a 259 
selection of 111 genes which did not include some genes important for PCa, such as AR [9, 260 
12, 21], RB1 [9] or ZNRF3 of the Wnt-pathway [32], and due to the number of tumors 261 
analysed, certain molecular events are rare. However, the results are consistent with 262 
previous reports and reinforce the role of genes involved in the Wnt-pathway in determining 263 
worse outcomes. 264 
 265 
Conclusions 266 
Recent developments of targeted therapies for advanced PCa have focused attention on 267 
genes involved in homologous recombination deficiency and microsatellite instability. 268 
However, our results confirmed that mutations in the TP53 gene and genes (APC/CTNNB1) 269 
related to the Wnt-pathway are associated with unfavourable outcome with metastatic 270 
visceral dissemination and early death. These genomic alterations could be considered as 271 
markers to identify PCa patients at high risk of life-threatening disease who might benefit 272 
from more intensified treatment or new targeted therapies.  273
