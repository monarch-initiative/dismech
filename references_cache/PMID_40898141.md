# Association between sarcopenia and falls, fractures, hospital readmission, and all-cause mortality in older adults with endocrine disorders: a longitudinal study of aging in China.
**Authors:** Zhou Q, Zhu Y, Guo Q, Wang W, Wang L, Zhang X, Yang Y, Zhang L, Zhang Y
**Journal:** BMC Musculoskelet Disord (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12891-025-09117-6](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-025-09117-6)

## Content

BACKGROUND: To explore whether older adults with sarcopenia are at greater risk for falls, fractures, hospital readmissions, and all-cause mortality compared to those without sarcopenia.

METHODS: An elderly population discharged from June 2020 to January 2022 from the Department of Endocrinology of Sichuan Provincial People's Hospital was collected. The cohort included patients with various endocrine disorders, primarily type 2 diabetes mellitus, thyroid dysfunction (hypo- and hyperthyroidism), adrenal insufficiency, and metabolic syndrome. Clinicopathologic data were collected, and sarcopenia was diagnosed based on muscle mass, handgrip strength, and gait speed. Multivariate logistic and Cox regression analyses were performed to identify risk factors for sarcopenia and evaluate its association with adverse outcomes.

RESULTS: Among 161 participants, 50 (31.06%) had sarcopenia. Sarcopenia significantly increased the risk of falls (adjusted HR: 2.38, 95% CI: 1.06-5.35, P = 0.036) but was not associated with hospital readmissions (adjusted HR: 0.87, 95% CI: 0.49-1.53, P = 0.630), fractures (adjusted HR: 1.01, 95% CI: 0.20-3.92, P = 0.992), or all-cause mortality (adjusted HR: 1.81, 95% CI: 0.49-6.67, P = 0.374). Impaired gait speed was a strong predictor of increased fall risk (adjusted HR: 5.00, 95%CI: 1.27 ~ 19.90, P = 0.022), suggesting that patients who failed the gait speed test were substantially more likely to experience falls, and grip strength was a better predictor of clinical prognosis (adjusted HR: 0.85, 95%CI: 0.77 ~ 0.95, P = 0.004).

CONCLUSION: Sarcopenia is independently associated with an increased risk of falls in elderly patients with endocrine-related diseases. Recognizing and treating sarcopenia can prevent falls and help improve clinical prognosis in older patients. However, the heterogeneity of underlying endocrine disorders may influence these associations and should be considered in clinical interpretation.

Sarcopenia is a syndrome of physical function gradually declining with increasing age [1]. Its incidence increases with age, which can augment the likelihood of tumbling, fracturing bones, rehospitalization, becoming limb-handicapped, and death, and has become one of the main problems threatening the wellbeing of the elderly [2]. Statistical data indicate that the incidence of sarcopenia is up to 5 ~ 13% in individuals 60–70 years old, 11 ~ 50% in those aged 80 years or older [3], and 4.1 ~ 11.5% in the elderly Asian population [4]. At present, about 50 million people suffer from sarcopenia worldwide. By 2050, the number of sarcopenia patients is expected to reach 500 million [5]. In addition, sarcopenia is a factor influencing other health problems, for example, heart-related diseases, cognitive impairment, diabetes, respiratory diseases, cancer, and an overall decreased quality of life [6–8]. Currently, the updated consensus on sarcopenia is that patients should be diagnosed through a comprehensive evaluation of their muscle mass, muscle power and daily function. In clinical practice, it is mainly treated through exercise therapy, nutrition therapy and drug therapy to increase muscle mass and preserve body function, delay and improve the pathological process of sarcopenia, and improve the independent living ability of patients [9].

Earlier research has shown that the occurrence of sarcopenia could be closely linked to a variety of risk factors, such as gender, age, malnutrition, lack of physical exercise, hormone changes in the body, cognitive dysfunction, neuromuscular regulation imbalance, inflammation, psychological status, intestinal microbial changes, and diseases [10]. After an individual turns 50 years old, the body muscle content and strength decrease year by year with increasing age [11]. In addition, the muscle fiber number and motor neurons also decrease with age, which, in turn, leads to muscle mass and volume decreasing [12].

Endocrine disorders, such as diabetes mellitus, thyroid dysfunction, and adrenal abnormalities, are increasingly recognized as important contributors to the development and progression of sarcopenia. These conditions can influence muscle metabolism through hormonal imbalances, insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and altered nutrient utilization, leading to accelerated muscle degradation and reduced regenerative capacity.

Older adults hospitalized for endocrine disorders often exhibit a higher burden of comorbidities, functional decline, and nutritional deficits—factors that further exacerbate sarcopenia. Therefore, studying sarcopenia in elderly patients with endocrine diseases provides an opportunity to understand how hormonal and metabolic disturbances intersect with musculoskeletal health in a high-risk population.

Several studies have evaluated the relevance of sarcopenia in fall and fracture risk in elderly patients [13, 14]. Studies have also evaluated the relevance of sarcopenia to the risk of all-cause mortality in patients [15, 16]. However, there are fewer longitudinal prediction studies, and no studies have longitudinally assessed these three indicators in a cohort.

Hospitalized patients with endocrine disorders represent a unique high-risk population for sarcopenia due to chronic metabolic abnormalities, hormonal imbalances, and frequent comorbidities, such as diabetes and osteoporosis. These factors are known to accelerate muscle loss and functional decline, yet are often under-recognized in routine clinical assessments. Concentrating on this inpatient population allows for a more targeted exploration of sarcopenia’s impact on clinical outcomes in a group where early identification and intervention may yield significant benefits. Despite the relevance, few studies have systematically investigated the longitudinal impact of sarcopenia in endocrine inpatients. This study was conducted to screen the risk factors for the development of sarcopenia in elderly endocrinology patients in our hospital, and to assess the predictive ability of sarcopenia and its individual components on readmission, falls, fractures and all-cause mortality in elderly endocrinology patients.

For this retrospective longitudinal study, 174 older adults were recruited from the Endocrinology Department of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital (Sichuan Province, China) between June 2020 and January 2022. Baseline clinical, anthropometric, and functional data were collected at the time of hospital discharge. To clarify, although BMI and sarcopenia components (muscle mass, handgrip strength, and gait speed) were assessed at hospital discharge, BMI was treated analytically as a potential predictor of sarcopenia in this study. This is because BMI is a non-functional, anthropometric indicator that remains relatively stable in the short term and is not part of the sarcopenia diagnostic algorithm. On the other hand, the diagnosis of sarcopenia was based on functional performance (grip strength and gait speed) and body composition (muscle mass), following the AWGS 2019 criteria. Therefore, BMI was conceptually and analytically considered as a pre-existing exposure rather than a diagnostic component. Follow-up data on clinical outcomes (falls, fractures, readmission, and mortality) were collected at 6 and 12 months after discharge through outpatient records, telephone interviews, and hospital readmission databases. Thirteen participants were excluded during screening due to incomplete clinical or follow-up data, withdrawal of informed consent, or failure to meet the inclusion criteria (e.g., inability to complete muscle strength or gait tests). After data screening, 161 elderly patients were included in the final study. They were included in the experiment after the study was approved by the ethics committee of the hospital, and informed consent was provided by all subjects. The following diagnostic criteria were used, in accordance with the 2019 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS): ① appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) < 7.0 kg/m² for men and < 5.4 kg/m² for women; ② handgrip strength < 28 kg for men and < 18 kg for women; ③ six-meter walking speed < 1.0 m/s. According to the AWGS 2019 algorithm, a diagnosis of sarcopenia was made when low muscle mass (criterion ①) was present in combination with either low muscle strength (criterion ②) or poor physical performance (criterion ③), i.e., ①+② or ①+③. Cases fulfilling all three conditions (①+②+③) were also considered to have sarcopenia, but they inherently meet the AWGS definition through either ①+② or ①+③ and therefore do not require separate categorization. The inclusion criteria were being aged 60 or more, being diagnosed as sarcopenic, having basic communication skills, and being able to cooperate to complete the gait speed and grip strength measurements. The exclusion criteria included: (ⅰ) being an amputee; (ⅱ) having a body mass index (BMI) > 50 kg/m2; (ⅲ) identifying as a patient with Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, bone and joint diseases of both upper limbs (such as rheumatoid arthritis), or a gait disorder or balance disorder, who, therefore could not complete the test; (ⅳ) having implanted electronic devices (pacemakers); (ⅴ) using glucocorticoids, thyroid hormones, gender hormones and other drugs within three months before enrollment; (ⅵ) simultaneously participating in other research or being unwilling to participate in this study. Matched clinical data were collected, including gender, age, BMI, SBP, DBP, education level, parents’ history of brittle fracture, smoking, drinking, coffee/strong tea, history of brittle fracture after 45 years old, height becomes shorter by 3 cm, calcium supplement, vitamin D supplement, anti-osteoporosis drugs, multiple medication. Patients with incomplete data and missed follow-ups were excluded.

Data collection for adverse outcomes (falls, fractures, readmission, and mortality) was conducted longitudinally following the initial sarcopenia assessment at hospital discharge. Patients were followed up for a maximum of 36 months, with outcome data collected at 6, 12, 24, and 36 months through clinical records and telephone follow-up.

MMSE was used to assess the cognitive function of the patients. An MMSE score < 24 indicated cognitive impairment, while a higher score indicated better cognitive function [17]. The nutritional status of the elderly was assessed using the MNA score, in which a score < 24 indicated that an individual was vulnerable to malnutrition or was malnourished [18].

Muscle mass was measured by using an GE Lunar Prodigy DXA (Lunar IDXA). The Xiangshan EH101 gripping device was selected, and the dominant hand grip strength of patients was measured by a specifically assigned person. Each patient was measured three times, and the maximum value was taken, which was considered the patient’s grip strength value. The body function was assessed based on the six- meter walking speed [19].

The statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS data scientific analysis platform (https://spssau.com/) and R software (version 4.2.3). Variables identified as potential confounders based on prior literature and univariate analysis (e.g., age, gender, and BMI) were included as covariates in all multivariate regression models. Binary logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of sarcopenia. Although all variables were assessed at the same baseline time point (discharge), BMI was treated as an independent covariate and potential predictor in the regression model, because it is not part of the diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia and reflects longer-term nutritional and body composition status. It aligns with previous studies where BMI is used to predict sarcopenia risk, even when measured concurrently with other indicators. Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) for readmission, falls, fractures, and all-cause mortality associated with sarcopenia and its components (muscle mass, handgrip strength, gait speed). The proportional hazards assumption was verified for each model. Continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation or medians (25th, 75th percentiles), and an independent sample t-test or Mann–Whitney U test was used. Categorical data are expressed as counts and percentages, and a χ2 test was used. A spearman correlation analysis was used for correlation factor analysis. A binary logistic regression analysis was used to identify possible risk factors for sarcopenia. For the multivariate Cox regression models evaluating adverse outcomes, variables were not selected solely based on statistical significance in univariate analyses. Instead, age, gender, and BMI were included a priori as established confounders, based on prior literature and biological plausibility, following guidance from previous studies [20, 21]. This approach avoids the limitations of stepwise or univariate-based variable selection. For Cox regression analyses, binary variables were generated for sarcopenia and each of its diagnostic components (low gait speed, low grip strength, and low muscle mass). The normal group (e.g., gait speed ≥ 1.0 m/s) was coded as 0 (reference), and the impaired group (e.g., gait speed < 1.0 m/s) was coded as 1. Thus, hazard ratios (HRs) > 1 indicate a higher risk of adverse outcomes in individuals with the impairment, while HRs < 1 would indicate a protective association. To ensure clarity, all components were coded such that the impaired condition served as the risk factor. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Information on clinical outcomes including hospital readmission, falls, fractures, and all-cause mortality was obtained through a combination of medical record review and follow-up phone interviews conducted at 6-month and 12-month intervals post-discharge.


Readmission was defined as any unplanned hospital admission to any department within 12 months after the index discharge, regardless of the cause, and was confirmed through hospital electronic medical records.Falls were defined as an unexpected event in which the participant came to rest on the ground, floor, or lower level. Fall events were self-reported by patients or their caregivers during follow-up interviews and cross-verified with medical records when possible.Fractures included any newly diagnosed fragility fracture confirmed by radiographic imaging and documented in hospital records within the 12-month follow-up period.All-cause mortality was defined as death from any cause occurring during the 12-month follow-up period, ascertained from hospital records or verified through contact with family members or caregivers.All data were independently reviewed and validated by two researchers to ensure consistency and accuracy.


Readmission was defined as any unplanned hospital admission to any department within 12 months after the index discharge, regardless of the cause, and was confirmed through hospital electronic medical records.

Falls were defined as an unexpected event in which the participant came to rest on the ground, floor, or lower level. Fall events were self-reported by patients or their caregivers during follow-up interviews and cross-verified with medical records when possible.

Fractures included any newly diagnosed fragility fracture confirmed by radiographic imaging and documented in hospital records within the 12-month follow-up period.

All-cause mortality was defined as death from any cause occurring during the 12-month follow-up period, ascertained from hospital records or verified through contact with family members or caregivers.

All data were independently reviewed and validated by two researchers to ensure consistency and accuracy.

Excluding incomplete data and lost patients, 161 elderly patients were finally included in the study. Among them, 50 patients were diagnosed with sarcopenia and 111 patients with non-sarcopenia. The analysis found that there was a statistically marked distinction in some demographic characteristics and body measurement indexes between patients with the sarcopenia and those without sarcopenia (Table 1). There was also a marked distinction in terms of gender and age between the two groups (P < 0.01). Compared with the non-sarcopenia group, the higher BMI of the sarcopenia group was significantly reduced (P < 0.01). The ratio of smoking patients in the sarcopenia group was significantly higher (P < 0.05). They also had lower cognitive function, a poorer nutritional status (P < 0.05). There was no obvious difference between the two groups in terms of blood pressure, education level, parents’ history of fragility fractures, smoking, coffee/strong tea consumption, history of fragility fractures after 45 years old, height becoming shorter by 3 cm, taking vitamin D supplements, using anti-osteoporosis drugs, using multiple medications ≥ 6. Patients with non-sarcopenia tended to use calcium supplements (P = 0.060).

Univariate analysis revealed age, gender, BMI, smoking, cognitive scores, and nutritional status as risk factors for elderly patients with sarcopenia. Whereas in multifactorial analysis, only age, gender and BMI were risk factors for sarcopenia in older adults (Table 2). Therefore, age, gender, and BMI, measured at baseline prior to the diagnosis of sarcopenia, were identified as independent risk factors for the subsequent development of sarcopenia. This confirms that BMI was measured before sarcopenia diagnosis, validating causal interpretation. It is significant that although BMI and sarcopenia components were measured at the same time, BMI was used in the analysis as an independent variable based on its physiological and clinical distinction from sarcopenia’s diagnostic criteria. This approach is supported by existing literature and allows for interpretation of BMI as a predisposing factor for sarcopenia rather than merely a concurrent characteristic.

As shown in Table 3, sarcopenia was not statistically significant in predicting readmission (adjusted HR: 0.87, 95% CI: 0.49–1.53, P = 0.630) or fracture (adjusted HR: 1.03, 95% CI: 0.27–4.00, P = 0.961) in elderly patients. Although the unadjusted model suggested a significant association between sarcopenia and all-cause mortality (unadjusted HR: 3.18, 95% CI: 1.01–10.01, P = 0.048), this association was attenuated and became non-significant in the adjusted model (adjusted HR: 1.81, 95% CI: 0.49–6.67, P = 0.374).

The Cox regression analysis was used to analyze the association of muscle mass, grip strength and gait speed with the risk of adverse outcomes. All results were adjusted for age, gender and BMI (Table 4). Interestingly gait speed was superior to sarcopenia (area under the curve: 0.689 vs. 0.637) in predicting the risk of falls in patients. Moreover, grip strength predicted clinical prognosis in elderly patients with endocrine disorders [adjusted HR: 0.85, 95%CI: 0.77 ~ 0.95, P = 0.004].

This study found that sarcopenia morbidity in 161 hospitalized elderly patients was 30.56%. With the increase of age, the proportion of sarcopenia patients gradually increased. In addition, baseline values of sarcopenia and its components can predict risk of falls and all-cause morality for older patients with endocrine disease.

In our study, gender, age and BMI were independent risk factors for the development of sarcopenia in elderly hospitalized patients. In this study, age, gender, and BMI were included as covariates in multivariate analysis based on their known relevance to sarcopenia and adverse outcomes in older adults, rather than relying solely on univariate screening. This approach aligns with recommended practices to reduce bias from data-driven model selection [20, 21]. Gender is a possible influencing factor for sarcopenia [22]. We found a significantly higher proportion of males in patients with sarcopenia (46.0%) than in patients non-sarcopenia (22.5%), which was consistent with the results of Chen [23]. It has been discovered after further analysis that gender was an independent risk factor for sarcopenia. Men had a much higher chance of developing sarcopenia, which might be interrelated with male testosterone contents [24]. As a steroid hormone, testosterone can increase the diameters of muscle fibers and help men maintain muscle mass [25]. However, with the increase in age, there was a concomitant decrease in the male testosterone level, which would, in turn, increase β catenin activity and inhibit muscle protein synthesis and satellite cell activation, thereby reducing the body’s normal function of skeletal muscle and increasing the sarcopenia risk [26]. Therefore, it is suggested that elderly patients with sarcopenia should be properly supplemented with testosterone to reduce the occurrence of sarcopenia. As reported, the prevalence of sarcopenia increases significantly with age [3]. The results of the present study demonstrated that impaired gait speed was significantly associated with increased fall risk in older adults. When coded such that impairment represents the exposure, an adjusted HR > 1 was found, aligning with clinical expectations. This supports the importance of assessing functional mobility as a predictor of fall-related outcomes in elderly populations. Our study also found that there were significantly more elderly patients in sarcopenia than in non-sarcopenia. BMI also reflects the nutritional status of the body to a certain extent. On the one hand, a decrease in BMI may affect the normal volume and function of the muscles and lead to malnutrition in the elderly, thus affecting the occurrence of sarcopenia [27]. On the other hand, obese patients often lack physical exercise. These patients are at greater risk of muscle mass reduction and muscle function decline. They are also a high-incidence group for sarcopenia [28]. Obesity is a protective factor against the development of sarcopenia compared to lean patients in our study. However, we cannot ignore the fact that sarcopenic obesity still accounts for a significant proportion of sarcopenia (14.0%). This was similar to previous studies [29]. Moreover, our results, just like other reports [30], showed that smoking could also affect the occurrence of sarcopenia.

The body function and nerve function of the elderly are gradually declining, and the prevalence of cognitive impairment increase year by year [17]. Ohta et al. [31] considered that cognitive impairment was an influencing factor for sarcopenia in women; the outcome of EPIDOS cohort research was also consistent with this finding [32]. A tide of research had announced that there was a correlation between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment, and the cognitive function of patients with sarcopenia was worse, which was comparable with the conclusions of prior research [33, 34]. Due to the discrepancies in the study population, region, diagnosis criteria of sarcopenia, and evaluation criteria of cognitive function, there were differences in the research conclusions regarding the link between sarcopenia and cognitive impairment. Data from a large sample are required to verify the accuracy of these research results. Previous studies have concluded that sarcopenia is significantly correlated with patients’ cognitive function and nutritional status [35, 36]. And nutritional status mediated the correlation between sarcopenia and cognitive function [37]. Although our study did not find a significant correlation between nutritional status and sarcopenia, it was clear that patients with sarcopenia have poorer cognitive function and poorer nutritional status. A clinical trial study found that physical exercise and cognitive function training did not reduce the risk of falls for patients with sarcopenia [38].

Multiple previous meta-analyses have confirmed that sarcopenia is associated with fall and fracture risk in elderly patients [13, 15]. In contrast, our study only found that sarcopenia predicts the risk of future falls in elderly patients. This is consistent with most of the findings. In addition, this study also found that gait speed was superior to sarcopenia in predicting future fall risk. Previous studies have also found that assessment of function and strength is superior to muscle mass in predicting adverse events [39–41]. And muscle mass is an essential condition for the diagnosis of sarcopenia. Therefore, our study has similar results to previous studies. Regarding the risk of fracture, sarcopenia predicted fractures at different sites differently [14, 42]. Huang et al. [43] found that sarcopenia was able to predict the future risk of hip fracture in older adults. In our study, it may be difficult to achieve a better clinical prediction due to the small sample size and more scattered fracture sites. Although our study did not find the predictive significance of sarcopenia for all-cause mortality, grip strength was able to predict well the future risk of all-cause mortality in elderly patients. Therefore, the evaluation of muscle strength and function in elderly patients should be emphasized along with the assessment of myasthenia gravis, which is important for the occurrence of adverse events in patients.

Although this study concentrated on elderly patients with endocrine-related diseases, it is important to acknowledge that these disorders were heterogeneous in nature. The sample primarily included individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus, thyroid dysfunction (including both hypo- and hyperthyroidism), adrenal insufficiency, and metabolic syndrome. Each of these conditions may independently affect muscle metabolism, physical function, and fall risk. For instance, diabetes mellitus is known to be associated with accelerated muscle mass loss and impaired mobility, while thyroid dysfunction can lead to changes in muscle strength and energy metabolism. This variability might influence the observed associations between sarcopenia and clinical outcomes. Therefore, future studies should consider stratifying or adjusting for specific endocrine diagnoses.

This study contributes valuable evidence regarding the predictive role of sarcopenia and its individual components on adverse outcomes in elderly patients with endocrine disorders, emphasizing the importance of early identification and intervention. Notably, gait speed and grip strength may serve as more practical and effective markers than sarcopenia diagnosis alone for prognostication. Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. The study was conducted at a single center with a relatively small sample size, which might affect the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the observational design precludes causal inference, and the follow-up period may be insufficient to capture long-term outcomes, such as delayed mortality or disability. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts and extended follow-up durations are warranted to validate these results and refine risk stratification strategies.

In conclusion, our results show that gender, age, and BMI are independent risk factors for sarcopenia. Sarcopenia and its components can predict the risk of future falls and all-cause mortality in elderly patients. The assessment of sarcopenia should focus more on muscle strength and function, which is more relevant in clinical work. We look forward to larger, multicenter clinical studies to validate our results and to further explore the clinical significance of sarcopenia in the prediction of adverse outcomes.
