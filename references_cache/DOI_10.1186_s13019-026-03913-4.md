# Experience with patients presenting with the clinical features of Holt-Oram syndrome: a single center retrospective study
**Authors:** Xuechen Liu, Lei Yang, Jian Cui, Mengqian Liao, Zonghui Hou, Mengqi Zhao, Lianyi Wang

Holt-Oram syndrome, also known as atrio-digital syndrome, is a rare autosomal dominant genetic disorder primarily characterized by upper limb malformations and congenital heart defects, associated with mutations in the TBX5 gene. Diagnosis is typically based on clinical presentation, and the prognosis for affected individuals is closely related to the severity of cardiac involvement. Our study aims to highlight some key features of Holt-Oram syndrome to improve the level of clinical diagnosis.

We collected 11 patients with clinical features strongly suggestive of Holt-Oram Syndrome at the First Affiliated Hospital of Tsinghua University from January 2010 to January 2025. These patients exhibited both limb malformations and cardiac abnormalities. We then analyzed the characteristics of their diseases.

The skeletal abnormalities and cardiac defects presented in diverse forms among the 11 patients. Among the congenital heart diseases, atrial septal defect (ASD) was the most common, accounting for 80% of cases. However, some patients presented with severe conditions such as tetralogy of Fallot or Ebstein’s anomaly. Regarding upper limb malformations, the most frequent finding was triphalangeal thumb (8/10 patients), but thumb hypoplasia or aplasia and radial bone abnormalities were also observed.

The predominant cardiac structural abnormality in this group of patients was atrial septal defect. Upper limb malformations were predominantly characterized by polydactyly or syndactyly. However, arrhythmias appeared to be mainly supraventricular tachycardia, and upper limb involvement did not seem to show a clear left-sided predominance. The prognosis following cardiac corrective surgery was favorable. Nevertheless, for this condition, emphasis should be placed on prevention.

Holt-Oram syndrome is a rare autosomal dominant genetic disorder with an incidence rate of approximately 1 in 100,000 [1,2]. It is primarily characterized by upper limb malformations, congenital heart disease, and/or cardiac conduction abnormalities. Current research suggests that the syndrome arises due to loss-of-function mutations in the TBX5 gene located on chromosome 12. This gene plays a critical role in the development of the upper limbs and heart [3,4], but is not involved in lower limb development. Some studies suggest that when one parent has Holt-Oram syndrome, their offspring may exhibit more severe symptoms [5]. Furthermore, related research has found that different frameshift mutations in various segments of the TBX5 gene can lead to different clinical presentations. For instance, one pattern might involve severe cardiac defects with relatively mild skeletal abnormalities, while another might present with severe upper limb malformations but minimal cardiac involvement [6]. Unfortunately, due to the limited financial resources of many of our patients, who often come from remote or impoverished mountainous areas, comprehensive TBX5 gene testing has not yet been fully implemented in our hospital. Based solely on clinical presentation, this group of patients was highly suspected of having Holt-Oram syndrome. Further confirmation still requires genetic testing.

The limb abnormalities in Holt-Oram syndrome typically manifest as varying degrees of unilateral or bilateral upper limb involvement, most commonly affecting the hands [6,7,8]. These can present as a variety of malformations, including absent thumbs, floating thumbs, syndactyly, polydactyly, and may or may not be accompanied by skeletal variations or aplasia of the forearm or wrist, such as shortening due to oligodactyly or amelia [9]. Since the TBX5 gene is not expressed in the lower limbs, this condition typically does not involve limb abnormalities in the lower extremities [10]. Patients often present to orthopedic clinics due to upper limb abnormalities. Many clinicians might focus solely on bone-related corrective procedures, overlooking the connection between skeletal and cardiac issues. This can lead to missed cardiac disease screening, resulting in delayed diagnosis and treatment, which negatively impacts patient prognosis. This highlights the importance for clinicians that any child presenting with unexplained skeletal abnormalities must undergo an evaluation for potential cardiac defects and conduction abnormalities [11].

On the other hand, heart is also the primary organ affected in Holt-Oram syndrome, which can impact cardiac structure and/or the conduction system. Structural cardiac abnormalities are mainly manifested as septal defects. Some studies suggest [12] that the most common manifestation of cardiac involvement is an ASD [9]. This can be corrected through surgical intervention. However, more severe lesions can also occur, such as pulmonary atresia or tetralogy of Fallot, which typically severely impacts the child’s quality of life [2]. Regarding cardiac conduction abnormalities, conditions such as atrioventricular block, tachycardia, or atrial fibrillation may occur. These can be treated clinically through relevant electrophysiological procedures [13]. In clinical practice, a small proportion of patients may exhibit only arrhythmias without structural heart defects [14]. If clinicians fail to recognize the presence of Holt-Oram syndrome, leading to missed diagnosis, it may subsequently increase the risk of serious complications such as cardiomyopathy or thromboembolism.

The preliminary diagnosis of Holt-Oram syndrome is largely established through clinical presentation. However, definitive confirmation necessitates genetic proof. Relevant research suggests that a diagnosis of Holt-Oram syndrome requires the presence of at least upper limb abnormalities confirmed by X-ray examination, along with congenital heart disease and/or cardiac conduction abnormalities [8,15,16]. However, the clinical presentation of the disease is highly variable. In some cases, it may manifest only as mild cardiac conduction abnormalities that are insufficient to cause symptoms such as hypoxia or cyanosis. Alternatively, the only abnormality might be a delay in skeletal development noted on X-ray. These subtle findings can potentially lead to delayed diagnosis. Furthermore, some research suggests that the clinical manifestations of Holt-Oram syndrome in the Chinese population may differ slightly from those observed in other ethnic groups. A new classification system for upper limb malformations [17] was designed to better reflect the characteristics of the disease in the Chinese population, thereby facilitating more accurate diagnosis in clinical practice.

Holt-Oram syndrome is a rare disease. While clinical manifestations can serve as a preliminary diagnosis, the gold standard remains genetic testing. Currently, there is limited research on this condition. Our study aims to highlight some key features of Holt-Oram syndrome to improve the level of clinical diagnosis.

We retrospectively reviewed 11 patients with clinical features strongly suggestive of Holt-Oram Syndrome at our hospital from January 2010 to January 2025. We performed statistical descriptions regarding the classification of their cardiac disease, the manifestations of upper limb malformations, laboratory test results, and surgical interventions. Additionally, we conducted telephone follow-ups to assess patient prognosis and family history. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Tsinghua University (2025-042-01).

We conducted a detailed review of the patients, including aspects such as limb malformation manifestations, X-ray findings, cardiac structural abnormalities, electrocardiograms, and relevant laboratory tests. Among the 11 patients, there were 7 males and 4 females.

Regarding cardiac abnormalities, we found that the cardiac manifestations were highly variable. Isolated septal defects were rare, and patients often had other associated congenital heart diseases (Table1). Among the 11 patients, 10 had congenital heart disease, while only 1 exhibited arrhythmia alone. Among these 10 patients with congenital heart disease, ASD was the most common, occurring in 8 cases, accounting for as high as 80%. The most frequent type of ASD was the secundum type, comprising 62.5% of all ASDs. Ventricular septal defect (VSD) was present in 2 cases, and patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) in 4 cases. Furthermore, some patients exhibited severe congenital heart diseases. For instance, patient No. 2 had both an ASD and pulmonary atresia, patient No. 3 presented with severe tetralogy of Fallot and bilateral superior vena caves, patient No. 4 had a single atrium, single ventricle, and bilateral superior vena caves, and patient No. 5 exhibited Ebstein’s anomaly. Patients with severe cardiac structural involvement had cardiothoracic ratios exceeding normal values, whereas patients No. 8 and No. 9 had cardiothoracic ratios within the normal range.(Figure1.)

Out of the 11 patients, 10 exhibited abnormalities on their electrocardiograms. Among these, 4 patients showed supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), 3 patients presented with sinus tachycardia, patient No. 2 demonstrated both SVT and first-degree atrioventricular block, patient No. 9 had bundle branch block, and patient No. 11 presented with atrial fibrillation, which carries a higher risk of thromboembolism. Notably, interventional electrophysiological procedures can effectively address the patients’ arrhythmias to a certain extent.

Regarding limb abnormalities, all 11 patients exhibited phenotypes and varying degrees of severity in their malformations (Table2). Among them, 10 patients had malformations confined to the hands. One patient had involvement in both hands, with four affected on the left side and five on the right side. One patient also had limb malformations affecting the lower limb. However, patient No. 1 had a broader area of involvement, affecting both the right thumb and the right radius. Only two patients underwent surgical excision for polydactyly. (Fig.2.)

At the point when Holt-Oram syndrome is highly suspected in patients, we examined their first-degree relatives for upper limb and cardiac abnormalities, but found no relevant issues. Initially, a genetic mutation was suspected in patients. The median follow-up time for the patients was 7.2years, during which patient No. 2 passed away at the age of 15 due to worsening of the condition, while the other patients recovered well.

In clinical practice, there are many conditions that can present with congenital heart disease and upper limb malformations, necessitating careful differential diagnosis. For example, Okihiro syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder that can manifest with anomalies of the anus, kidney, heart, and feet, and is generally considered to be caused by mutations in the SALL4 gene [18,19]. Fanconi anemia, in addition to hematological abnormalities, also presents with multiple developmental anomalies, such as those affecting the skin and skeleton [20]. TAR syndrome, on the other hand, is characterized clinically by radial ray defects and neonatal thrombocytopenia [21]. All of these conditions can lead to heart disease or upper limb malformations, causing confusion in clinical diagnosis. However, based on the combination of the patients’ upper limb and cardiac malformations, we still have a high degree of suspicion for Holt-Oram syndrome. The next step should be to establish a complete genetic evidence chain and conduct a family history investigation.

Holt-Oram syndrome, also known as heart-hand syndrome, was first described by Holt and Oram in 1960, and is primarily characterized by congenital heart defects and skeletal malformations of the upper limbs [1].

The most common congenital heart defect in Holt-Oram syndrome is atrial septal defect, with secundum atrial septal defect being the most prevalent [22,23]. Sometimes, it can manifest as complex conditions such as Tetralogy of Fallot, coarctation of the aorta, or double outlet right ventricle, which is consistent with our research findings. However, some related studies have suggested that during prenatal screening, if a fetus exhibits significant and isolated right atrial enlargement not caused by tricuspid regurgitation or other cardiac malformations, Holt-Oram syndrome should be considered, and further examination for upper limb malformations should be conducted by clinicians [24]. Due to the limited number of cases and the fact that all our patients were diagnosed with Holt-Oram syndrome after birth, we are unable to assess the accuracy of this clue. It is certain that the autosomal dominant inheritance rate is 50%, with a tendency for the condition to worsen in successive generations. Fetal ultrasound is crucial for guiding clinical pregnancy decisions and reducing the birth of malformed infants, as it is an important non-invasive diagnostic examination during pregnancy [25,26]. Furthermore, due to the presence of cardiac structural abnormalities, including an increased cardiothoracic ratio compared to normal children, we should be vigilant for the subsequent development of heart failure and arrhythmias. Cardiac involvement is related to prognosis and overall survival, and therefore deserves significant attention from clinicians.

At the molecular genetic level, the etiology of Holt-Oram syndrome has been precisely attributed to mutations in the TBX5 gene. Located on the long arm of chromosome 12, the TBX5 gene directs the normal development of organs such as the heart and upper limbs by precisely regulating the expression of downstream target genes [27,28]. Mutations in this gene disrupt embryonic developmental programs through various mechanisms, such as loss of function, dominant-negative effects, or gain of function. During cardiac development, TBX5 serves as a critical factor in determining atrial and ventricular identity, regulating cardiac septation, and forming the conduction system [29,30]. Its essential role in maintaining normal cardiac electrical activity and structural integrity may account for the occurrence of atrial septal defects (ASD) or arrhythmias in these patients. Conversely, in upper limb development, the precise expression of TBX5 is the decisive factor initiating the growth of the forelimb bud and guiding the development of the hand skeleton (particularly the thumb) [31]. This molecular mechanism underlies the characteristic clinical manifestations of thumb hypoplasia or agenesis observed in patients.

However, it is noteworthy that studies indicate a high rate of sporadic de novo mutations in Holt-Oram syndrome. Approximately 85% of cases are attributed to de novo mutations, meaning the disease manifests for the first time in the family and the parents do not carry the pathogenic gene [32]. This also explains why the cases in our study lacked a clear family history.

Studies have identified a correlation between the type and location of TBX5 mutations and the severity of clinical manifestations in Holt-Oram syndrome. Mutations resulting in complete loss of TBX5 protein function, such as nonsense or frameshift mutations, are associated with more severe cardiac and skeletal malformations, as well as a higher risk of arrhythmias [33,34]. Conversely, certain missense mutations or splice site mutations with a milder impact may be associated with less severe clinical phenotypes, where patients may present with only isolated skeletal abnormalities or mild cardiac defects [34]. This explains the diversity of cardiac and upper limb malformations observed in our study.

Cardiac conduction is the abnormality coexist with congenital heart defect in Horner’s syndrome. Previous studies have shown that in children with Horner’s syndrome, the condition commonly manifests as bradycardia or atrioventricular block, with supraventricular tachycardia being less frequently observed [11,22]. However, in this study, the prevalence of supraventricular tachycardia reached up to 40%, indicating that the clinical manifestations of arrhythmias in Horner’s syndrome may need to be reexamined.

Upper limb malformations are also a necessary part of the diagnosis of Holt-Oram syndrome, in addition to cardiac involvement. The upper limb malformations in affected children frequently manifest as absent thumbs, polydactyly, or hypoplasia, which can be unilateral or bilateral, symmetrical or asymmetrical. Left-sided involvement is commonly observed, and the lower limbs are generally not affected [6,8,15,35]. According to this research, patients predominantly exhibit polydactyly, aligning with the morphological manifestations of the malformation, but the involvement of both sides is almost identical, showing no marked preference for left-sided involvement.

In our center’s clinical experience, none of the aforementioned cases were diagnosed with Holt-Oram syndrome at their initial visits in local hospital. Missed diagnoses are likely due to non-specialist physicians lacking acknowledges with the diagnostic criteria. However, the treatment of these 11 patients was not delayed when they come for cardiac management. Multidisciplinary treatment (MDT) is crucial in the management of this disease, often requiring collaborative decision-making involving the Heart center, orthopedics, and genetics departments. After receiving supplementary diagnoses and undergoing surgery at our center, the patients had good prognoses, with only 1 patient passing away 13 years postoperatively due to disease progression. It is noteworthy that family members of patients may be inclined to conceal medical histories due to the stigma associated with genetic diseases. This necessitates professional communication from clinicians to screen high-risk individuals. When encountering family members who may be at risk of a genetic disorder, it is important to explain the significance of prenatal ultrasound screening. Maintaining a balance between reproductive autonomy and disease burden, in collaboration with the patients’ families, to make more informed decisions highlights the value of preventive healthcare.

Overall, our study highlights some key features of Holt-Oram syndrome, such as congenital heart defects including septal defects, and upper limb malformations affecting the hands or radius, or even the shoulders. However, the diagnosis of this disease is quite challenging, as its variability in expression and overlap with other conditions complicate the diagnostic process. As a rare genetic disorder, Holt-Oram syndrome should be suspected when upper limb abnormalities associated with cardiac defects are observed clinically, and genetic confirmation should be pursued whenever possible. It is important to emphasize that this disease follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, and a family history investigation is necessary upon diagnosis.

Certainly, this study also has limitations. Firstly, the data were derived from a single center, which may not fully represent the disease characteristics of the Chinese population. Secondly, this article is based on a retrospective study, and there is missing data; the study was unable to obtain more comprehensive family information and genetic testing, resulting in an incomplete chain of evidence for the disease.

Holt-Oram syndrome is a rare disease with significant challenges in diagnosis. This condition not only involves the cardiovascular and skeletal systems but also presents with a wide variety of clinical symptoms. This work reports 11 cases who were highly suspected of Holt-Oram syndrome, with the cardiac structural manifestations primarily being atrial septal defects, and the upper limb deformities mainly presenting as polydactyly or syndactyly, which is consistent with previous studies. Differently, the arrhythmias reported in our cases were predominantly supraventricular tachycardias, and the upper limb deformities did not seem to show an obvious predilection for the left side like previous studies showed. Our clinical experience advocates for a MDT involving cardiology centers, orthopedics, and genetics. The prognosis for cardiac corrective surgery in this disease is favorable, however, the emphasis should be placed on prevention for Holt-Oram syndrome.
