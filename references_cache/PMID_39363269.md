BACKGROUND: X-linked intellectual disability-hypotonic facies syndrome-1 
(MRXHF1) and Alpha-thalassemia X-linked intellectual disability (ATR-X) syndrome 
are caused by pathogenic variant in the ATRX gene, a member of the 
switch/sucrose non-fermentable (SWI-SNF) protein family that exhibits chromatin 
remodeling activity. These syndromes show a wide spectrum of clinical 
manifestations, such as distinctive dysmorphic features, mild-to-profound 
intellectual disability, motor development delay, seizures, urogenital 
abnormalities, and gastrointestinal disorders.
CASE PRESENTATION AND LITERATURE REVIEW: A 3-year-old boy from a Chinese 
non-consanguineous family was diagnosed with MRXHF1 by whole-exome sequencing. 
Comprehensive family history information was obtained. The Medline database was 
searched until 1st Aug 2023 for articles related to ATRX pathogenic variant. 
Data on gene/protein mutations and clinical symptoms were extracted. The proband 
showed intellectual disability, motor development delay, typical facial 
abnormalities, urogenital defect, behavior problems, and optical nerve 
dysplasia. A novel frameshift mutation c.399_400dup, (p.Leu134Cysfs*2) in the 
ATRX gene was the primary cause, which occurs right before the ATRXDNMT3-DNMT3L 
(ADD) domain of ATRX protein. Missense mutation is the most common variation 
type. The ADD and helicase-like domains are the most frequently affected 
domains. Epilepsy, congenital heart disease, urogenital defect, acoustic defect, 
and optical defect are more prevalent in patients with frameshift mutations 
compared to those with missense mutations. There are more urogenital defects 
with C-terminal frameshift mutations than with N-terminal frameshift mutations.
CONCLUSION: We described a novel frameshift mutation in the ATRX gene in a 
patient with MRXHF1 syndrome and summarized the genotype-phenotype relationship 
of ATRX pathogenic variant by variation type and affected protein domain. The 
regulatory mechanism underlying ATRX variant requires comprehensive analysis in 
future studies.

Background
ATRX pathogenic variant was first identified in patients 
with alpha-thalassemia X-linked intellectual disabil -
ity (ATR-X) syndrome (OMIM # 301040), after which 
the ATRX gene was named. ATR-X syndrome is a rare 
genetic condition with an estimated prevalence of 
1/30,000–1/40,000 in male newborns [ 1–5]. The gene 
encoding ATRX maps to Xq21.1, and it encodes at 
least two alternatively spliced transcripts that give rise 
to slightly different proteins of 265  kDa and 280 kDa 
[6]. The protein product is a member of the SNF2 fam -
ily of chromatin remodeling factors and has two main 
domains. At the N-terminus is the ADD domain, a plant 
homeodomain (PHD)-like zinc finger with an additional 
C2-C2 motif. In the C-terminal half of the protein lies 
the helicase/adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) domain 
comprising several highly conserved colinear helicase 
motifs [ 7, 8]. ATRX germline mutations mainly cause 
ATR-X syndrome, while somatic ATRX mutations can 
cause alpha-thalassemia myelodysplastic syndrome 
(ATDMs), a neoplastic disorder of the myeloid lineage of 
hematopoiesis, and various tumors [4].
Multiple clinical syndromes other than ATR-X syn -
drome are attributed to ATRX germline mutations, for 
example, X-linked intellectual disability-hypotonic facies 
syndrome-1 (MRXHF1, OMIM #309580) and syndromes 
previously reported as sporadic cases that were later 
demonstrated to result from ATRX mutations, such as 
Carpenter-Waziri, Chudley-Lowry-Hoar, Juberg-Mar -
sidi, Renier-Gabreels-Jasper, Smith-Fineman-Myers, and 
Holmes-Gang syndromes [9].
Syndromes caused by ATRX germline mutation share 
numerous symptoms, such as global development delay 
(GDD), intellectual disability (ID), typical facial features, 
epilepsy, congenital heart abnormality, and urogenital 
defects; more rarely, autism spectrum disorder, acoustic 
defects, and optical defects occur. However, few stud -
ies have been conducted on the intrinsic connection 
between genotypes and clinical phenotypes [8, 10–13].
In this article, we present a case of a 3-year-old boy 
diagnosed with MRXHF1 resulting from a novel frame -
shift mutation in ATRX (c.399_400dup, p. Leu134Cysfs*2, 
NM_000489.6) that has not been reported previously. We 
also screened ATRX case reports. We sorted the cases by 
pathogenic variant type and associated the clinical symp -
toms with variation types and affected protein domains. 
We found that frameshift and nonsense mutations had 
a higher prevalence of specific clinical symptoms, such 
as epilepsy and urogenital, acoustic, and optical defects, 
than missense mutations. The ADD (mostly GATA-like 
domain and PHD-like domain) and helicase-like domains 
are the most affected domains in view of clinical symp -
toms. Other domains, such as the EZH2 binding domain, 
RNA binding domain, and MECP2 interacting domain, 
also participate in ATR-X syndrome.
Subjects and methods
Subject
A 3-year-old boy from a Chinese non-consanguineous 
family diagnosed with MRXHF1 was analyzed. His two 
brothers were normally developed, and his mother was 
confirmed to be heterozygous. Comprehensive family 
history information was obtained during genetic coun -
seling. Informed written consent was obtained from the 
child’s parents as guardians according to the ethics com -
mittees and review boards of Anhui Provincial Children’s 
Hospital.
Whole-exome sequencing
We used xGen Exome Research Panel version 2.0 (IDT, 
IA, USA) to extract DNA from peripheral blood leu -
kocytes. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150  bp) was per -
formed by DNBSEQ-T7 (MGI, China) with no less 
than 99% regions covered at least 10 ×. Raw sequence 
readouts were processed by fastp. Paired-end sequence 
reads were aligned to the GRCh37/hg19 genome with 
Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA). GATK was used for 
initial mapping and variant site identification. Annota -
tions were retrieved from the online system by Chigene 
(Beijing, China), including allele frequency in the Single 
Nucleotide Polymorphism (dbSNP), 1000 Genomes Proj-
ect, Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC), Genome 
Aggregation Database (gnomAD), ESP , and Chigene 
in-house MAF databases; positions and associated 
clinical phenotypes were determined using the UCSC, 
RefGene, GENCODE, ENSEMBL, LOVD, SWISS, Clin -
vitae, HGMD, OMIM, and ClinVar databases. Amino-
acid changes were predicted using software packages 
(Provean, SIFT, PolyPhen2, MutationTaster, M-CAP , 
REVEL, and CADD; MaxEntScan, dbscSNV, and GTAG; 
GERP , phyloP , and phastCons). Variant filtering was con-
ducted with minor allele frequency (MAF) < 1. Patho -
genic or likely pathogenic variants were evaluated in 
accordance with the American College of Medical Genet-
ics (ACMG) practice guidelines.
Literature search and data extraction
We searched the Medline database for articles until 1st 
Aug 2023. The search criteria included “ ATRX, ” “XNP , ” 
“ ATMDS, ” “MRXHF1, ” “SFMS, ” “Chudley-lowry syn -
drome, ” “Holmes-Gang syndrome, ” “Carpenter-Waziri 
syndrome, ” “Juberg Marsidi syndrome, ” and all their 
synonyms. The search strategy details are presented in 
Supplementary material 1. The inclusion criteria were 
as follows: (1) case reports, series, or any type of articles 
related to diseases caused by ATRX gene pathogenic vari-
ant; (2) only patients with confirmed ATRX pathogenic

Result
Case presentation
The proband was born by normal spontaneous vaginal 
delivery at 41 weeks of gestation and weighed 3150  g. 
He was the third child of his mother, and the develop -
ment process of the two elder brothers (13 and 10 years 
old at the time of writing) was normal. No family history 
of neuromuscular disease was reported. The proband 
was admitted to the hospital for feeding difficulties and 
failure to thrive in the neonatal period. Echocardiog -
raphy showed an atrial septal defect (ASD) of 0.16  cm, 
which was diminished at age 3. Whole-exome sequenc -
ing showed a frameshift mutation in the ATRX gene 
due to c.399_c.400dup(exon6) causing p.Leu134Cysfs*2 
(NM_000489.6); the pathogenic variant was consid -
ered likely pathogenic according to the ACMG guide -
line PVS1 + PM2-Supporting and was related to 
MRXHF1(OMIM:309580), no other likely pathogenic/
pathogenic variant was identified that could potentially be 
contributing to the patient’s phenotype. Sanger sequenc -
ing further revealed that the mother was heterozygote, 
and the father was not affected (Fig.  1). Verification of 
pedigree was refused. The diagnosis was made MRXHF1 
for him based on the patient’s clinical symptoms and 
WES result later on. The patient experienced recurrent 
severe respiratory infections before 3 years of age. His 
global development was profoundly delayed, he could not 
walk until 2 years of age, he spoke no meaningful words, 
and could barely comprehend language. His behavioral 
abnormalities included unprovoked screaming, hyper -
activity, and somnipathy. He was admitted to our hospi -
tal for severe intellectual disability at 3 years of age. He 
was 90 cm ( − 2 SD) in height and weighed 16 kg ( + 1 SD) 
on admission. His occipitalfrontal circumference (OCF) 
was 48  cm ( − 2 SD). The clinical examination showed

Discussion and conclusions
In this case report and literature review, we described 
the case of a patient with a novel ATRX frameshift muta-
tion (c.399_400dup, p.Leu134Cysfs*2, NM_000489.6) 
causing severe ID, facial abnormalities, and multi-organ 
defects. We then reviewed the literature on ATRX patho-
genic variant, summarized genotype–phenotype correla -
tions, and sorted clinical symptoms by variation type and 
affected protein domain.
ATRX has emerged as a key player in gene expression, 
DNA repair, replication, and recombination. It acts on 
tandem repeat (TR) sequences and influences the depo -
sition of a histone variant, prevention of replication fork 
stalling, and the suppression of a homologous recombi -
nation-based pathway of telomere maintenance [55].
In accordance with Baden et al. ’s findings, ADD domain 
(mostly influence the PHD-like domain) and the heli -
case domain are hotspots of ATRX pathogenic variants 
and accounts for most of the clinical symptoms of ATRX 
pathogenic variant [ 10], however, with growing knowl -
edge of ATRX protein domain and by incorporating 
more patients with ATRX mutation, we proposed that, 
GATA-like domain is also a high frequency pathogenic 
variant spot.

Unusually, a broad phenotypic spectrum is associated 
not only with ATRX mutation type but also in those with 
the same mutation. Multiple mechanisms underlying 
ATRX mutations could explain this phenomenon.
Alpha-thalassemia in ATR-X syndrome is attributed 
to reduced expression of the α-globin genes located on 
chromosome 16 [55]. Downregulation of α-globin expres-
sion leads to an excess of β-globin chains, which tend to 
form beta tetramers (HbH) [ 8]. Although cells with HbH 
inclusions are not seen until α-globin expression is down-
regulated by 50%, the proportion of cells with HbH inclu-
sions can be used as a rough indicator of alpha globin 
gene expression. Law et al. demonstrated that the most 
prominent feature of the ATRX targets in both human 
and mouse is the presence of variable number tandem 
repeats (VNTRs), which in many cases are G and C rich 
and contain a high proportion of CpG dinucleotides. In 
ATR-X syndrome, when ATRX function is compromised, 
the degree of perturbation in gene expression is related 
to the size of the tandem repeat sequence, which has the 
potential to form G-quadruplex (G4) DNA structures. 
Law et al. showed that ATRX binds to G4 DNA in vitro, 
and a significant correlation was seen between the level 
of HbH inclusions and the size of the TR. These find -
ings explain the variable percentage of HbH inclusion in 
patients with identical ATRX pathogenic variants [12].
ID is an important clinical symptom of ATR-X syn -
drome, and its degree ranges from mild/moderate to 
severe. Gibbons et al. [8] and Badens et al. [56] confirmed 
that mutations in the ADD domain are associated with 
more severe psychomotor retardation than those in the 
helicase region. Indeed, the three missense mutations 
and one frameshift mutation associated with mild to 
moderate ID discussed here were all located in the C-ter -
minal helicase-like domain (in total, 32 cases provided 
with the degree of ID which was marked in supplemen -
tary material 2). Shoida et al. reported that Atrx mutation 
induces aberrant upregulation of Xlr3b expression in the 
mouse brain. ATRX normally binds to G-quadruplexes in 
CpG islands of the imprinted Xlr3b gene, regulating its 
expression by recruiting DNA methyltransferases. Over -
expression of Xlr3b inhibits the dendritic transport of 
the mRNA encoding CaMKII-α, promoting synaptic dys -
function. 5-ALA is a G-quadruplex ligand that inhibits 
Xlr3b expression. Administering 5-ALA represses Xlr3b 
transcription and rescues decreased synaptic plasticity 
and cognitive deficits in ATR-X model mice [ 57]. Thus, 
ATRX perturbs cognition-related genes through binding 
to G-quadruplexes in CpG islands.
One nonsense mutation c.324  C > T (Arg37*) was 
found to be related with mild ID [ 15]. Although non -
sense mutations have been identified in the ATRX gene, 
in ATR-X cases, some full-length proteins derived from 
the mutant allele have been identified in protein extracts

Urogenital abnormality is not rare in ATR-X syndrome. 
It has been established that the C-terminus of the ATRX 
protein may play a specific role in urogenital develop -
ment, and mutation in the C-terminus is frequently 
associated with severe genital abnormalities, includ -
ing micropenis and genital ambiguities. Gibbons et al. 
reported that 5 out of 7 mutations associated with severe 
urogenital abnormalities are truncating mutations that 
result in the loss of the C-terminal domain [ 13]. Indeed, 
the urogenital defects we described and reviewed here 
are more frequent in those with frameshift and nonsense 
mutations, which have the tendency to cause a truncated 
protein product; moreover, 5 out of 7 frameshift muta -
tions discussed here caused the loss of the C-terminus of 
the protein, specifically the MECP2 interacting region. 
The other two frameshift mutations occurred in the 
N-terminus and resulted in a more severely truncated 
protein product.
Acoustic and optical defects are rare, but by no means 
absent. These two conditions are associated with mis -
sense and frameshift mutations. The case we reported 
here showed optic nerve dysplasia and an elevated 
response threshold on both sides of the ears by BAEP . 
Acoustic defects reported in the literature included hear -
ing loss [ 17] and hearing defect for high pitched sounds 
[58], while optical defects included strabismus, hyperme -
tropia [17], and optic atrophy [ 51]. Acoustic and optical 
abnormalities are less frequently seen in those with mis -
sense mutations than in those with frameshift mutations. 
Almost all cases have missense mutations located in the 
C-terminus. No case of acoustic and optical abnormali -
ties was seen in those with nonsense mutations, and the 
underlying mechanism for this is unclear.
However, there are some limitations of this article. 
Reporting bias could not be neglected, due to authors 
could only report partial clinical sign and other crucial 
symptoms could be missed, besides, clinical phenotype 
such as epilepsy, congenital heart disease and autism 
could be age dependent, which would influence the accu-
racy of this report.
In this article, we reported a novel frameshift mutation 
in ATRX in a patient with typical MRXHF1 syndrome 
and described the ATRX pathogenic variant genotype–
phenotype relationship. It is hoped that the genotype–
phenotype relationship will be more accurately illustrated 
in the future with larger sample-size studies and that the 
regulatory mechanism underlying ATRX mutation will be 
comprehensively understood.
