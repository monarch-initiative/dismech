Using the well-studied two-stage model of skin carcinogenesis, the first transgenic mouse with targeted expression of a polyamine metabolic enzyme was generated 30 years ago. Ornithine decarboxylase (ODC), a key regulating enzyme of polyamine biosynthesis, was constitutively expressed in the outer root sheath cells of hair follicles near the bulge stem cell niche using a keratin 6 promoter in K6/ODC mice. Early studies using K6/ODC mice demonstrated that polyamines play an essential role in the early promotional phase of skin tumorigenesis. Treatment with inhibitors of ODC activity blocked the formation of skin tumors and caused the rapid regression of existing tumors. We review how use of the K6/ODC mouse has shown that elevated polyamines in epithelial cells stimulate proliferation and invasiveness, recruit stem cells, alter chromatin remodeling and cell signaling leading to metabolic reprogramming, increase vascularization, activate underlying fibroblasts, and have powerful effects on immune cell function, all contributing to the development and progression of tumors.

Nonmelanoma skin cancer (NMSC) is a major worldwide health concern due to its high incidence, associated medical costs, morbidity, and cosmetic defects resulting from current treatments. There are two major types of NMSC including basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) that account for 70% and 25% of all diagnosed skin cancers, respectively. Skin cancers are the most common of all types of cancer in the United States with 5.4 million NMSC cases diagnosed each year (Ciuciulete et al.2022).

The mouse skin model of two-stage skin carcinogenesis has been a widely used model for the study of the sequential and stepwise development of skin tumors and has provided an important instrumental framework for defining many current concepts regarding human neoplasia (Yuspa1986, Abel et al.2009). Mice are initiated with a low subthreshold topical dose of a carcinogen such as 7,12-dimethylbenz(a)anthracene (DMBA) which results inH-rasgene mutations but no tumor formation. However, subsequent, repetitive treatments with tumor promoting agents such as 12-O-tetradecanoylphorbol-13-acetate (TPA) or wounding lead to the development of skin tumors. Tumor promoting agents such as TPA are not mutagenic but cause chronic hyperplasia, edema, and a robust inflammatory response in the skin, resulting in a neoplastic lesion that contains one or more genetic lesions. Because tumors can be elicited in carcinogen-initiated skin even with long intervals between initiation and promotion, it is believed that carcinogen target cells in the skin are long lived, slowly cycling stem cells found in the bulge region within the outer sheath of hair follicles (Gerdes and Yuspa2005, Singh et al.2012, Morris et al.2000, Owens and Watt2003).

Elevated levels of polyamines (putrescine, spermidine, and spermine) have long been associated with tumorigenesis. Tightly regulated metabolism of polyamines is critical for cell survival and normal skin homeostasis, and these controls are dysregulated during tumor formation resulting in constitutively elevated levels of polyamines in tumors compared to normal skin. More than 50 years ago, use of the DMBA-TPA skin carcinogenesis mouse model demonstrated that a key enzyme in polyamine biosynthesis, ornithine decarboxylase (ODC) is transiently induced following topical treatment with tumor promoting agents (O’Brien1976). In order to determine whether the induction of ODC by tumor promoting agents is essential for skin tumorigenesis in mouse skin, a keratin 6-drivenODCtransgenic mouse model in which ODC is constitutively expressed in the outer root sheath of hair follicles was generated (Megosh et al.1995). In order to mimic the constitutively high levels of ODC activity in skin tumors, a premature stop codon at position 427 in the mouse ODC cDNA was placed downstream of the K6 promoter resulting in a carboxyl-truncated ODC protein with a much longer in vivo half-life than the full-length ODC protein (Ghoda et al.1989, Clifford et al.1995). These sustained high levels of polyamine biosynthesis in K6/ODC mouse skin lead to hair loss beginning at 3 weeks of age when the second cycle of hair growth begins followed by the subsequent development of follicular dermal cysts (Megosh et al.1995, Soler et al.1996). Treatment with the ODC inhibitor α-difluoromethylornithine (DFMO) prevented this hair loss in K6/ODC transgenic mice and reactivated hair growth in mice with complete hair loss, suggesting that ODC is an important regulator of hair follicle function (Soler et al.1996).

This review describes how the use of the K6/ODC mouse model not only has demonstrated the essential role of polyamines in the early tumor promotion stages of tumorigenesis but also has revealed multiple ways in which polyamines regulate normal skin homeostasis and tissue remodeling including wound healing and tumor development. The K6/ODC mouse model has contributed to a better understanding of mechanisms by which polyamines affect multiple biological pathways leading to increased cellular proliferation, invasiveness, stem cell recruitment, angiogenesis, fibroblast activation, and changes in immune cell function. In addition to its impact in the cancer field, we describe how the K6/ODC mouse served as an important animal model for the recently identified human gain-of-function ODC genetic disorder (the Bachmann-Bupp syndrome) that facilitated a relatively quick treatment for these patients.

The multistage tumorigenesis models in wildtype mouse skin have been useful to define and evaluate modifying factors on tumor initiation, promotion, and progression. However, fundamental questions remained concerning (1) constitutive expression of ODC as a sufficient condition for tumor promotion, and (2) the identity of target cells for chemical carcinogens in the skin. Whereas treatment with a tumor promoter such as TPA after initiation with DMBA is required in wildtype mice for tumor development, studies with the K6/ODC mouse model showed that treatment of newborn or adult K6/ODC mice with a single topical application of DMBA resulted in papilloma development 6–8 weeks later without the use of tumor promoter agents (O’Brien et al.1997). Although the C57Bl/6 mouse strain is resistant to skin tumorigenesis, ODC overexpression in K6/ODC transgenic mice dramatically increased the susceptibility of these mice when on a C57Bl/6 background to DMBA-induced carcinogenesis. Skin tumors were similarly induced in K6/ODC mice following a single treatment with other carcinogens from different chemical classes (Chen et al.2000) as well as following UVB irradiation (Ahmad et al.2001). Furthermore, the formation of skin tumors in K6/ODC transgenic mice was shown to be dependent on transgene expression and polyamine biosynthesis, especially putrescine, since treatment with the ODC inhibitor α-difluoromethylornithine (DFMO) completely blocked the formation of skin tumors following carcinogen or UVB irradiation and caused the rapid regression of existing tumors (Ahmad et al.2001, Peralta Soler et al.1998). These early studies with K6/ODC transgenic mice demonstrated that ODC overexpression can greatly increase the susceptibility of the skin to initiation by a carcinogen or UVB irradiation and that polyamines play an essential role in the early promotional phase of skin tumorigenesis.

Activating mutations in thec-Ha-rasgene are early genetic changes in a high proportion of mouse skin tumors resulting from an initiating dose of DMBA (Balmain et al.1988) as well as in human tumors (Bos1989). ODC/Ras double transgenic mice, generated by breeding K6/ODC mice with TG.AC v-Ha-rastransgenic mice, developed spontaneous keratoacanthomas at 5–8 weeks of age and exhibited a high frequency of conversion to malignant carcinomas (Smith et al.1998). Treatment with DFMO caused these tumors to quickly regress with no change in v-Ha-rasexpression, demonstrating that polyamine synthesis is required for tumor maintenance. In contrast, transgenic mice that possessed only the K6/ODC or the v-Ha-rastransgene did not develop tumors unless treated with either a carcinogen or tumor promoter, respectively. Similar results were obtained using ODC-ER transgenic mice in which cutaneous ODC activity can be induced following topical treatment with the inducing agent 4-hydroxytamoxifen (Lan et al.2005). Additional studies evaluated the cooperativity of different Ras effector mutants with overexpressed ODC in an in vivo tracheal xenotransplantation assay for epithelial cell invasiveness (Gilmour et al.2001, Hayes et al.2006). Using primary cultures of keratinocytes from newborn K6/ODC mouse skin or normal littermate skin, increased ODC activity alone or activation of downstream effector Ras pathways (Raf/MEK/ERK or PI 3-kinase) were not sufficient to convert normal keratinocytes to an invasive phenotype (Hayes et al.2006). However, conversion of normal keratinocytes to invasive, malignant cells minimally required both activation of the Raf/MEK/ERK signaling pathway and a threshold level of increased ODC activity (Hayes et al.2006, Smith et al.1997).

An initiating oncogenic event in K6/ODC mice is not unique to a mutatedrasgene, as basal cell carcinomas developed in mice that are both heterozygous null for thepatchedtumor suppressor gene and overexpressing ODC in follicular cells with the K6/ODC transgene (Tang et al.2004). Treatment with DFMO or even modest reductions in ODC expression reduced skin tumor susceptibility as demonstrated by decreased tumor development inOdc+/−haploinsufficient mice subjected to a two-state initiation-promotion protocol (Guo et al.2005). Thus, K6/ODC mice are genetically “promoted” with tumor development dependent upon ODC expression.

Many tumors arising in K6/ODC transgenic mice are keratoacanthomas that originated from the follicular cysts in the dermis. Targeting the overexpression of ODC to only the outer root sheath (ORS) keratinocytes of the hair follicle in K6/ODC mice or to both ORS and basal keratinocytes using a keratin 5 promoter resulted in no difference in tumor yield in DMBA-initiated K6/ODC transgenic mice compared to K5/ODC transgenic mice (O’Brien et al.1997). Indeed, ODC induction following multiple topical TPA treatments has a heterogeneous staining pattern with ODC staining specifically in perifollicular cells (Gilmour et al.1986). These findings support the hypothesis that DMBA-initiated tumors arise from the bulge stem cells residing in the ORS of hair follicles and are activated by ODC overexpression to clonally expand to form tumors (Morris et al.2000, Morris et al.1986, Cotsarelis et al.1990, Cotsarelis2006).

Importantly, α6 integrin+CD34+bulge stem cells remain dormant during normal epidermal cell cycling and are recruited from the bulge region only in response to wounding or tumor promoters such as TPA (Ito et al.2005, Trempus et al.2007). To determine whether induction of ODC activity is a sufficient stimulus to activate hair follicle stem cells in a similar manner to that seen with TPA (Ito et al.2005, Morris et al.2004), triple transgenic reporter mice were generated using K15CrePR1;R26R reporter mice in which treatment with RU486 induces permanent expression of lacZ in the epidermal stem cells in the bulge and all subsequent progeny of the labeled bulge cells in order to allow tracking of keratin 15-expressing bulge stem cells (Morris et al.2004, Hayes et al.2014). These K15CrePR1;R26R reporter mice were bred with ODC-ER mice in which cutaneous ODC activity can be inducedde novofollowing topical treatment with the inducing agent 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4OHT) (Lan et al.2005, Hayes et al.2014). Unlike K6/ODC mice in which ODC activity is constitutively expressed leading to hair loss and the development of follicular cysts in the dermis, ODC-ER mice maintain a normal skin phenotype followingde novoinduction of epidermal ODC. As expected, blue stained cells expressing beta-galactosidase activity remained in the bulge region of telogen hair follicles in K15CrePR1; R26R mice (Hayes et al.2014). However, blue cells were observed not only in the bulge region but also tracked to the upper hair follicle and in the interfollicular epidermis in K15CrePR1;R26R; ODC-ER mice following induction of ODC activity (Hayes et al.2014). These data identified polyamines as endogenous stimulators of stem cell recruitment in a manner similar to wounding or treatment with the tumor promoter TPA (Ito et al.2005, Trempus et al.2007, Li et al.2012). This was the first definitive evidence that elevated epidermal polyamine levels alone can stimulate the recruitment of bulge stem cells which are then activated to give rise to a hierarchy of transit amplifying cells. That elevated polyamine levels can stimulate the recruitment of bulge stem cells in quiescent skin is significant with regard to the stem cell origin of skin cancer since long lived, slow-cycling stem cells found in the bulge region can remain dormant while harboring genetic lesions and never expand to tumors without a stimulus to recruit them from their bulge stem cell niche (Gerdes and Yuspa2005, Singh et al.2012, Morris et al.2000, Owens and Watt2003).

Wounding can also promote the development of tumors (Argyris1980), and chronic wounds are a well-known risk factor for skin cancer (Dvorak1986, Fleming et al.1990, Schafer and Werner2008). Interestingly, wound repair in abraded K6/ODC and ODC-ER transgenic skin exhibited exaggerated epidermal hyperproliferation and persistent inflammation leading to the development of tumor-like growths (Hayes et al.2011). This wound-induced tumor formation took place in the absence of carcinogen treatment and was not observed in normal littermate mice. Two weeks after abrasion, epidermal hyperplasia and the epidermal proliferative index was greatly reduced or absent in normal littermates, whereas K6/ODC and 4OHT-treated ODC-ER mice maintained a significantly elevated epidermal proliferative index and a progressive epidermal hyperplasia with the development of papillomatous growths at the wound site. Treatment with the anti-inflammatory agent dexamethasone prevented the wound-induced tumor growths in abraded ODC transgenic skin (Hayes et al.2011). These results indicated a role for polyamines in exacerbating wound-associated inflammation and stimulating recruitment of bulge stem cells, both of which are sufficient to sustain epidermal hyperplasia and benign tumor growth even in the absence of genetic damage.

Since K6/ODC transgenic mice are genetically promoted to develop skin tumors, they are an excellent model to study molecular mechanisms by which polyamines promote tumorigenesis. Elevated levels of polyamines have long been known to be associated with rapid proliferation in both normal and neoplastic cells and tissue (Casero and Marton2007, Pegg2009). Not surprisingly, the epithelial cells lining the follicular dermal cysts of K6/ODC transgenic mouse skin express high levels of ODC protein and also demonstrate a high proliferative index (Gilmour et al.1999). Epithelial basal cells in skin tumors that spontaneously formed in ODC/Ras transgenic mice also possessed increased proliferation markers. However, although the ODC/Ras tumors rapidly regressed with DFMO inhibition of ODC activity, the proliferation index remained unchanged in these DFMO-regressed tumors (Lan et al.2000). Moreover, before the ODC/Ras tumors regressed with DFMO treatment, the tumors became significantly less vascularized followed by increased tumor apoptosis. These observations along with the increased dermal vascularization of K6/ODC skin compared with that of normal littermate skin suggested that one mechanism by which elevated ODC enzyme activity contributes to the survival of epidermal tumors is by up-regulating essential angiogenic factors (Lan et al.2000).

Reversible protein phosphorylation and aberrant signaling pathways are involved in tumor promotion and is another mechanism by which elevated polyamines contribute to tumor development. Polyamines directly interact with the regulatory beta-subunit of the CK2 enzyme and modulate its activity and structure. Moreover, elevated levels of polyamines in ODC overexpressing keratinocytes led to translocation of CK2 from the cytoplasm to the nucleus with increased phosphorylation of the nucleolar phosphoprotein B23 (Shore et al.1997, Lawson et al.2005). Besides B23, many CK2 substrates are nuclear proteins involved in cell cycle regulation, DNA replication, and transcription. As with polyamines, abnormally high CK2 expression/activity is found in a wide range of cancers, maintaining an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, and is considered an adverse prognostic marker associated with tumor aggressiveness and poor patient survival (Karmakar et al.2025, Chua et al.2017). Interestingly, both ODC and CK2 protein were found to colocalize in the cells lining the follicular cysts found in K6/ODC transgenic dermis (Shore et al.1997). Since transgenic mice overexpressing CK2 also exhibit increased susceptibility to tumor development, it is likely that elevated polyamines may contribute to tumor promotion by altering normal signal transduction pathways such as CK2.

The essential role of polyamines for normal cell growth and differentiation as well as in tumor promotion can be attributed in part to their effects on gene expression, a process regulated by the acetylation state of nucleosomal histones. Polyamines can regulate gene expression by altering DNA structure and by modulating the binding of transcription factors to response elements in target genes (Peng and Jackson2000, Thomas and Thomas2003). K6/ODC and ODC/Ras transgenic mice were used to provide the first in vivo evidence that polyamines influence chromatin via multiple mechanisms (Hobbs et al.2002, Hobbs et al.2003, Hobbs et al.2006). Intrinsic histone acetyltransferase (HAT) was found to be elevated in ODC transgenic skin compared with skin from normal littermate mice (Hobbs et al.2002). Skin tumors forming spontaneously in ODC/Ras double transgenic mice exhibited dramatically high HAT activity having a distinct preference for Lys-12 in the tail domain of histone H4 (Hobbs et al.2002). Indeed, both p300/CBP-associated HAT activity and the Tip60 HAT enzyme were elevated following ODC overexpression in K6/ODC transgenic skin (Hobbs et al.2006). Intrinsic deacetylase activity was also elevated in ODC transgenic skin but was comparable or lower in tumors relevant to that in normal or nontumorigenic skin. However, studies comparing K6/ODC mouse skin with that of normal littermate skin revealed that elevated polyamine levels do not have major impact on the overall acetylation of nucleosomal histones in bulk chromatin. This is consistent with the notion that polyamines promote only very localized changes in the acetylation state of histones in chromatin, perhaps potentiating altered transcription of a small fraction of genes. Taken together, polyamines influence histone acetylation via multiple mechanisms which implies the potential for polyamines to mediate decreased acetylation of nucleosomal histones at one gene promoter while stimulating increased acetylation at another promoter, resulting in different transcriptional outcomes for those genes (Hobbs et al.2002, Hobbs et al.2003, Hobbs et al.2006). Thus, polyamine-stimulated chromatin remodeling can alter the recruitment of a subset of transcription factors to the regulatory regions of genes to influence gene expression and promote the neoplastic process.

Subsequent studies have found spermidine-induced acetylation changes in autophagy-associated proteins and gene promoters including Atg7 that may trigger beneficial effects on life span and aging and be involved in many diseases (Eisenberg et al.2009, Morselli et al.2011, Minois et al.2011). In addition, ODC and putrescine alter the activity of chromatin-modifying enzymes that control not only histone acetylation but also methylation resulting in attenuation of the M1 proinflammatory response in macrophages (Hobbs and Gilmour2000, Hardbower et al.2017). Conversely, ODC-depleted macrophages demonstrate changes in histone methylation marks including increased histone 3, lysine 4 (H3K4) mono-methylation and H3K9 acetylation but decreased H3K9 di/trimethylation that have been associated with open chromatin leading to increased transcription (Hardbower et al.2017). Thus, polyamine-modulated epigenetic enzymes play a role in activation of immune cell types and the immunosuppressive activity of myeloid cells by altering gene expression leading to metabolic reprogramming.

Although ODC enzyme activity is directed to the epidermal layer of K6/ODC mouse skin, multiple cell types in the underlying dermis are activated as well. Elevated polyamine biosynthesis in K6/ODC transgenic mouse skin leads to activation of dermal fibroblasts with increased expression of fibroblast activation markers including fibroblast activation protein (FAP), α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), TGF-β, latent transforming growth factor β binding protein-1 (LTBP1) (Cai et al.2020), fibroblast-growth factor-10 (FGF10), podoplanin (PDPN), IL-8, CXCL2, and CXCL5. Deposition of extracellular matrix proteins such as tenascin-C and collagen are also increased in the dermal layer of ODC-ER transgenic mice following induction of ODC activity and is accompanied by a diminution of the fat layer (Lan et al.2005, Alexander et al.2025). Dermal tenascin-C expression is strongly induced in dermal tissue adjacent to hyperproliferative epidermis during wound healing (Latijnhouwers et al.1996) and is implicated in angiogenesis (Tanaka et al.2004) as well as cell adhesion and cell motility (Swindle et al.2001, Midwood et al.2004). Moreover, elevated epidermal ODC enzyme activity significantly stimulates the vascularization of both K6/ODC and ODC-ER transgenic skin with a decrease in dermal PEDF, an anti-angiogenic factor. These results suggest that induction of ODC activity in adult mouse skin activates keratinocytes and dermal components and stimulates vascularization in a manner similar to skin undergoing wound healing and tumor development. Mechanistically, this may be due in part to activation of GCN2 (general control non-derepressible-2) triggered by decreased levels of polyamine precursors, arginine and methionine, resulting from the elevated polyamine biosynthesis in K6/ODC skin (Alexander et al.2025). GCN2 is one of the main sensors of amino acid starvation stress, and its activation rewires cellular metabolism to survive the significant nutritional stress during tumorigenesis and activates fibroblasts to drive tumor progression (Ye et al.2010, Verginadis et al.2022, Gold and Masson2022). Indeed, DMBA-initiated skin tumor development was significantly reduced in K6/ODC mice deficient in GCN2, indicating that polyamine-promoted skin tumor development is dependent upon GCN2 activation (Alexander et al.2025). Polyamine-activated fibroblasts secrete multiple pro-tumorigenic cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors that increase invasiveness of epidermal cells and stimulate the generation of a pro-tumorigenic immune cells. Tumor-prone K6/ODC mice were shown to have more immunosuppressive MDSCs (Alexander et al.2025). GCN2 activation plays an essential role in the polarization of M2-like macrophages and MDSCs that suppress immune clearance of stem cells harboring mutations (Halaby et al.2019).

Tumor promoting activity of compounds such as TPA depend upon both inflammatory processes and epithelial hyperplasia (DiGiovanni1992). Moreover, early studies showed that the chronic inflammation induced by repeated TPA treatments can lead to suppression of adaptive T-cell mediated immune responses, thus allowing the immune escape of developing cutaneous tumors (Halliday et al.1988, Kodari et al.1991). Use of K6/ODC mice enabled a first-time examination of the effect of elevated epidermal polyamine levels on underlying immune cell responses. Interestingly, increased ODC activity in K6/ODC mice suppressed a hapten-induced contact hypersensitivity (CHS) response which is a classic adaptive T-cell mediated immune response (Keough et al.2011). CHS induction is dependent upon activation of both dendritic cells and T-cells (Xu et al.1996). The CHS suppression caused by increased epidermal ODC in K6/ODC mice was mediated by reduced cytokine levels that suppressed migration and antigen-presenting function of dendritic cells that is essential for T-cell activation and subsequently resulted in reduced immune cell infiltration to the site of hapten application (Keough et al.2011). Additional studies showed that activation of antigen-specific T cells is suppressed (as measured by ELISpot assays for IFN-γ-producing splenocytes) in K6/ODC and ODC-ER transgenic mice following intradermal injection with tumor cells expressing known antigens such as OVA or the LacZ peptide (Hayes et al.2014). This suppressed antigen-specific T-cell response was accompanied by greater tumor growth in the ODC-ER transgenic mice compared to that in normal littermate mice (Hayes et al.2014). These findings support the notion that elevated epidermal polyamine levels contribute to the T-cell immunosuppression inherent in most tumors, thus permitting a more permissive microenvironment for tumor growth and survival. Indeed, a strength of polyamine blocking therapy strategies to treat cancers is that they can act strongly to prevent immune escape by the tumor via decreasing immunosuppressive myeloid cells and increasing tumor-specific cytotoxic T-cells (Hayes et al.2014, Hayes et al.2014, Alexander et al.2017, Alexander et al.2020, Alexander et al.2021, Alexander et al.2023).

Whereas increased ODC activity provides a strong proliferation stimulus, the expression of p53, p21Waf1, p27Kip1, and Bax are paradoxically increased in the epidermis and dermis of ODC transgenic mice compared to their normal littermates (Gilmour et al.1999). Additional studies found that elevated ODC activity increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation via subsequent induction of polyamine catabolic oxidases, leading to DNA damage and triggering cell cycle arrest and subsequent activation of the p53-ATM-DNA damage response pathway (Wei et al.2008). Although this often leads to apoptosis, senescence can also be induced by cell stress or DNA damage leading to secretion of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, proteases, and growth-stimulatory molecules known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). Senescent fibroblasts and the associated SASP factors can promote the development of cancer (Ghosh and Capell2016, Ruhland et al.2016, Silva et al.2019). Bothp16mRNA expression and SASP factors such asTgfβ,Mmp9,CXCL2,CXCL5are elevated in dermal fibroblasts from K6/ODC mouse skin and further increased in dermal tissue from non-tumor-bearing, DMBA-initiated K6/ODC mouse skin compared to their normal littermates (Alexander et al.2025). Using a senescence-reporter/deleter (p16-3MR) mouse model in which senescent fibroblasts can be eliminated with ganciclovir treatment, Alimirah et al. (Alimirah et al.2020) demonstrated that eliminating TPA-induced senescent cells with ganciclovir in DMBA-initiated p16-3MR mice reduces tumor growth. Similarly, using K6/ODC transgenic mice bred with p16-3MR mice, carcinogen-initiated tumor development was greatly inhibited when senescent fibroblasts were eliminated in K6/ODC; p16-3MR mice (Alexander et al.2025). These results suggest that chronic elevated polyamine metabolism can activate underlying dermal fibroblasts as well as the accumulation of senescent fibroblasts that secrete SASP factors to create a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment.

Nearly 25 years after the first K6/ODC mouse was generated, the first patient with a rare autosomal dominant gain-of-function variant in the ODC gene was described in 2018 (Bupp et al.2018). Remarkably, the heterozygous de novo mutation in the ODC gene of this patient and others subsequently identified as having the Bachmann-Bupp syndrome (BABS) introduces a stop codon in the ODC gene, leading to premature abrogation and deletion of the c-terminal 14 amino acids of the ODC protein, as was originally designed in the K6/ODC mouse to stabilize the ODC protein and lead to elevated ODC enzyme activity (Megosh et al.1995). Similar to the phenotypic abnormalities observed in K6/ODC transgenic mice, human patients with this ODC mutation also displayed alopecia, accelerated nail growth and skin cysts (Bupp et al.2018). However, unlike the K6/ODC mouse where this mutation was directed to the skin, the ODC mutation is expressed in all tissues of BABS patients resulting in macrosomia, macrocephaly, neurodevelopmental delay, spasticity, hypotonia, delayed visual maturation, and sensorineural hearing loss (Bupp et al.2018, Bachmann et al.2024). Clearly, reducing these high levels of ODC activity during the early development of children with BABS is essential. Having the well described K6/ODC animal model for this syndrome was important in quickly identifying DFMO as a treatment strategy for patients with this disabling ODC mutation. In 2019 using doses of DFMO previously used to treat children with neuroblastoma as a guide, the first patient diagnosed with BABS was treated with DFMO to reduce the high levels of ODC activity. Within a short time of DFMO treatment, the child was growing hair with resolution of recurrent follicular cysts and showed improvement in muscle tone, hypotonia, and other neurological symptoms (Rajasekaran et al.2021, Afrin et al.2023). Currently there are 6 patients with BABS being successfully treated with DFMO, and it is likely that these numbers will grow with more comprehensive and early genetic testing, leading to prompt interventional treatment.

The K6/ODC mouse model has contributed to a better understanding of how elevated epidermal ODC activity and polyamine biosynthesis affect many biological pathways leading to increased cellular proliferation, invasiveness, and stem cell recruitment. Although ODC expression is targeted to epidermal cells in the K6/ODC mouse skin, stromal cells in the dermis are activated by increased polyamines as well resulting in increased vascularization, fibroblast activation, and changes in immune cell function leading to increased M2-like myeloid cells and T cell dysfunction. Increased polyamine levels trigger changes in chromatin remodeling (including both histone acetylation and methylation) and cell signaling (such as activation of GCN2 and the protein kinase CK2) that lead to altered gene expression, protein function, and metabolic processes. Overall, polyamine-activation of keratinocytes and underlying stromal cells create a more permissive microenvironment for tumor development

Since its creation more than 30 years ago, the K6/ODC mouse has taken on the role of a “Mighty Mouse” that has enabled us to better understand the many mysterious ways in which polyamines are not only essential for normal tissue homeostasis but also promote and sustain cancer development. Although the K6/ODC mouse was originally created to address whether elevated ODC and polyamines play an obligatory role in skin tumorigenesis, this mouse model also gave us first-time in vivo insights into mechanisms by which elevated polyamine levels impact both normal and neoplastic tissues. We discovered that elevated ODC and polyamines in K6/ODC skin stimulate epidermal proliferation, alter keratinocyte differentiation status, increase neovascularization, and recruit bulge stem cells in a manner similar to that seen in wound healing (Fig.1). It became apparent that elevated polyamine levels activate not only epidermal cells but also underlying stromal cells to create a more permissive microenvironment for tumor development. Dermal fibroblasts are activated resulting in increased synthesis of extracellular matrix proteins and accumulation of senescent, pro-tumorigenic fibroblasts. Immunosurveillance designed to eliminate rogue cells harboring mutations is suppressed with increased polarization of M2-like macrophages and MDSCs and suppression of antigen-specific T cells. Mechanistically on a cellular level, elevated ODC activity and polyamine biosynthesis in K6/ODC mouse skin alters chromatin remodeling and cell signaling changes that lead to altered gene expression, protein function, and metabolic reprogramming. Polyamine-promoted changes in metabolism and gene expression have far-reaching effects on normal skin homeostasis as seen in the K6/ODC mouse and also on overall human development as demonstrated by BABS patients when ODC is overexpressed in all tissues. However, perhaps the K6/ODC mouse model has made its biggest impact by providing us insights into the many ways in which polyamines promote and sustain tumorigenesis. While inhibition of polyamine biosynthesis has been shown to be an effective chemoprevention strategy for non-melanoma skin cancer, new therapeutic strategies to block the tumor-promoting effects of polyamines hold great promise to better treat multiple advanced types of cancer.
