BACKGROUND: Our previous epidemiological study revealed a significantly higher
incidence of liver angiosarcoma in Asian patients than that in their European
counterparts. Elucidating the etiology and hallmarks of this aggressive
malignancy may offer insights into the development of novel therapeutic
strategies.
METHODS: We analyzed samples from liver and non-liver angiosarcoma patients
treated at the National Taiwan University Hospital, as well as data from the
angiosarcoma cohort of the Count Me In Project. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)
samples were used as controls for liver tumors. Whole-exome sequencing and de
novo extraction of single-base substitution mutation signatures were performed.
Transcriptomic profiling was used to assess oncogenic pathways and the tumor
microenvironment. Telomere lengths were estimated using TelSeq and telomeric
repeat-containing RNA levels were quantified using TERRA-QUANT.
RESULTS: De novo extraction identified four mutation signatures (A-D). Signature
A exhibited high similarity to SBS22 (cosine similarity = 0.93), which was
associated with aristolochic acid exposure. This signature showed a
significantly greater contribution to liver angiosarcoma and HCCs but not to
non-liver angiosarcomas. According to the mSigAct analysis, 80% (8/10) of liver
angiosarcoma cases displayed a significant SBS22 signature. Moreover, liver
angiosarcomas demonstrated a significantly higher tumor mutation burden,
increased frequencies of TP53 and ATRX mutations, and greater T-cell
infiltration than non-liver angiosarcomas. Telomere length was significantly
longer in liver angiosarcomas than in matched normal tissues, a pattern that was
not observed in Taiwanese non-liver angiosarcomas or HCCs. Notably, liver
angiosarcomas exhibited markedly lower telomerase activity than their non-liver
counterparts and HCCs, suggesting a reliance on the alternative lengthening of
telomere (ALT) pathway.
CONCLUSION: Our study provides compelling evidence that aristolochic acid
exposure is associated with liver angiosarcoma, and elaboration of ALT
activation in liver angiosarcomas offers new insights into their underlying
biology and may guide the development of targeted therapeutic approaches.

Liver angiosarcoma (LAS) is a rare and highly aggressive liver cancer with poor prognosis [1]. Our prior epidemiological study demonstrated that the incidence of angiosarcoma is significantly higher in Asian populations than in Europeans, with Asian patients exhibiting markedly increased odds for LAS (odds ratio 10.72, p < 0.001) [2]. This striking disparity raises critical questions regarding the distinct etiological factors and pathogenic mechanisms underlying LAS.

Angiosarcomas are broadly classified into two etiological categories: primary (idiopathic) and secondary (associated with a specific precipitation factor). The pathogenesis of primary angiosarcomas remains poorly understood, whereas secondary angiosarcomas are often associated with identifiable causes. Radiation-associated angiosarcoma, which is frequently characterized by MYC amplification and overexpression, is the most common subtype of secondary angiosarcoma [3]. Additionally, chronic lymphedema can induce secondary angiosarcoma, as seen in Stewart-Treves syndrome. Exposure to toxic agents such as vinyl chloride has historically been implicated in LAS, although environmental safeguards have considerably reduced the number of vinyl chloride-related cases [4, 5]. Furthermore, chronic ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure, particularly among the elderly, has been associated with scalp and facial angiosarcomas, which typically exhibit high mutational burden [6, 7]. Despite these well-established risk factors, a subset of angiosarcoma cases lacks clear etiological drivers, suggesting the presence of unidentified pathogenic mechanisms. This etiological heterogeneity appears to be particularly pronounced when angiosarcoma subtypes are compared based on their anatomical locations.

In this study, we performed comprehensive genomic and transcriptomic analyses of angiosarcoma samples from both the Asian and Western cohorts. By comparing the molecular profiles of tumors from different organs, we aimed to elucidate the pathogenic mechanisms contributing to the observed geographic and anatomical disparities. Our findings indicate that LAS possesses distinct etiological and molecular features, highlighting the need for further research on the novel risk factors and therapeutic targets for this formidable malignancy.

Angiosarcoma samples with adequate amount of tissue for analysis diagnosed at National Taiwan University Hospital (NTUH) from 2015 to 2021 were obtained. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) samples were also obtained from NTUH within the years 2019–2021. The access to the genomic and clinical data of Count Me In Angiosarcoma Project [6] was approved by the US National Institutes of Health (Project ID 29033, May 2021).

Hematoxylin and eosin staining and immunohistochemistry slides from the National Taiwan University Hospital were reviewed by sarcoma pathologists (J.-C.L. and K.P.C.) to confirm the diagnosis of angiosarcoma.

Genomic DNA was isolated from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor and adjacent normal tissue sections using a QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA capture and library generation were conducted using Nextera Flex for Enrichment and Exome Panel (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 600 platform, achieving an average depth of ×400 across all the samples.

Total RNA was extracted using the QIAGEN RNeasy FFPE Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the Takara SMARTseq stranded kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 600 platform using the paired-end method, targeting 50 million reads per sample.

Raw sequencing reads were assessed for quality and adaptor/primer contamination using FastQC (v0.11.9). Adaptor sequences were trimmed using Cutadapt (v3.5), and reads shorter than 50 bases were discarded.

Trimmed reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17). Duplicate reads were marked, and base quality score recalibration was performed following GATK Best Practices (v4.2.3). Somatic variants were identified using MuTect2 (GATK) and annotated using ANNOVAR (release 20210819). To reduce false positives, variants meeting all of the following criteria were retained: total read depth ≥30, variant-supporting reads ≥5, and variant allele frequency ≥0.05. The mutation burden of each sample was calculated as the number of nonsynonymous mutations per megabase (Mb) in the exome-enriched regions.

Copy number alterations (CNAs) and estimates of tumor purity and ploidy were obtained using Sequenza software. Gene-level amplification was defined as mean gene copy number ≥2 × ploidy + 1, whereas deletions were defined as mean gene copy number <0.5. The copy number burden of each sample was calculated as the total length of genomic regions with CNAs.

To extract mutational patterns across samples de novo, the frequencies of 96 possible single-base substitution (SBS) types were generated using the mut.to.sigs.input function in the deconstructSigs R package. Non-negative matrix factorization was applied to identify the minimal number of components that could explain the maximum variance among samples. To explore the potential etiology of these components, they were compared to 96 cancer signatures from the COSMIC database (v3.2) and 54 carcinogen signatures reported by Kucab et al. [8] using cosine similarity. After decomposing the matrix of the 96 SBS types into 4 signatures, the contribution of each signature in the individual samples was estimated using the ExtractSignatures function from the MutationalPatterns R package. Alternatively, we also fitted 96 COSMIC (v3.2) cancer signatures to the matrix of 96 SBS types using the fit_to_signatures_strict function from MutationalPatterns. The presence of specific mutational signatures was tested using the SignaturePresenceTest function of the mSigAct R package.

Four-digit HLA class I alleles were inferred using T1K [9]. For each tumor sample, somatic mutation data and HLA alleles were input into NeoPredPipe. NeoPredPipe generates mutated peptide sequences ranging from 8 to 11 amino acids and uses NetHMCpan (v4.1) to predict binding affinities to MHC class I molecules. Predicted peptides with binding ranks ≤0.5% were classified as strong binders, whereas those with binding ranks between 0.5% and 2% were considered as weak binders.

Trimmed reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR aligner (v2.7.8a) with a two-pass mapping strategy. Gene-level raw counts were generated using STAR based on GENCODE annotation (v35). Only protein-coding genes with an average transcript per million across all samples >1 were retained. Data were normalized using the trimmed mean of M-values method, and batch effects were corrected using the removeBatchEffect function in the limma R package. The transcript per million values were used for downstream analysis.

Pre-rank gene-set enrichment analysis was conducted using the function implemented in the clusterProfiler R package. Genes were ranked based on signal-to-noise ratio. The gene sets were obtained using MSigDB (v7.4).

Immune cell profiles were estimated for each sample by calculating the average log-transformed expression of cell type-specific marker genes as defined by Danaher et al. [10]. TcellExTRECT [11] was used to estimate the T-cell fraction from the whole-exome sequencing (WES) data.

Telomere content was estimated from WES data using TelSeq [12] with default parameters. To account for age-related effects, telomere content was expressed as log2(T/N), where T represents the telomere content from the tumor tissue and N represents that from the adjacent normal tissue. Telomerase enzymatic activity was inferred from RNA-seq data using EXTEND [13]. Genes associated with telomere maintenance mechanisms (TMMs) were obtained from [14] and the activity of each TMM was estimated by calculating the average log-transformed expression of these genes. Telomeric repeat-containing RNA (TERRA) expression was estimated using a method developed by TERRA-QUANT [15].

Angiosarcoma samples were obtained, comprising 8 LAS and 17 non-liver angiosarcomas (NLAS) (9 head and neck, 3 primary breast, 2 cardiac, 2 extremity and trunk) for IHC analysis. The antibody clones are CD3 (2GV6), CD8 (SP239), PD1 (NAT105), p53 (DO-7), and ATRX (HPA001906). Specifically, we evaluated immune cell markers (T cells [CD3], cytotoxic T cells [CD8], and regulatory T cells [PD-1]) and proteins relevant to telomere biology and tumor suppressor pathways (TP53 and ATRX).

For the interpretation of immune infiltrates, areas of 1 mm2 with the highest density of immune cells within the tumor were selected by our sarcoma pathologist (J.-C.L.). For the interpretation of p53 status, we defined aberrant expression as either ≥60% of tumor cells with strong nuclear staining or <5% of tumor cells with nuclear staining (null). For ATRX, we consider ATRX loss if more than 50% of cancer cells have lost nucleus ATRX expression.

FFPE tissue blocks were sectioned onto Xenium Slides and processed according to the “Xenium In Situ for FFPE-Tissue Preparation Guide” (CG000578 Rev D, 10X Genomics) protocol. Samples were hybridized with the Xenium Prime 5K Human Pan Tissue and Pathways Panel (PN-1000671, 10X Genomics) at 50°C for 18 h, followed by post-hybridization washing, ligation, additional washes, and DNA amplification. Cell segmentation staining was then performed, and slides were treated with an autofluorescence quencher and counterstained with DAPI before being loaded into the Xenium Analyzer, in accordance with the “Xenium Analyzer User Guide” (CG000584 Rev F, 10x Genomics).

Processed Xenium data were imported into the R environment using the LoadXenium function in the Seurat package. Cells with fewer than 50 detected genes or exceeding the 98th percentile of gene counts were excluded. Gene expression matrices were normalized to the median of all cells, and the top 2,000 highly variable genes were identified. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed, and the top 30 components were retained for downstream analysis. A nearest-neighbor graph was constructed from the PCA space, and cell clusters were delineated using the Leiden algorithm at a resolution of 0.5. Major cell types were manually annotated based on canonical marker gene expression reported in the literature.

All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using the R language (v4.4.2). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to test the differences between variables with non-normal distribution.

From the National Taiwan University Hospital, we retrieved tumor samples diagnosed within the year 2015–2021 along with their normal tissue counterpart from ten LAS and nine NLAS, comprising five scalp angiosarcomas, two primary breast angiosarcomas, one truncal angiosarcoma, and one cardiac sarcoma. The mean ages (range) for liver, scalp, and other angiosarcomas were 72 (55–87), 76 (71–89), and 41 (32–54), respectively. Forty-six angiosarcoma patients from the Count Me In (CMI) Project [6] were included in the study. Ten patients with HCC from NTUH were also included in the analysis for organ and region control comparisons.

We first analyzed WES data to investigate whether angiosarcomas from different organs exhibit distinct mutation patterns, which may suggest differences in carcinogenic etiology. SBS profiles were extracted for each sample (online suppl. Fig. 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000549466), revealing that most angiosarcomas exhibited a high fraction (>50%) of C>T transitions (Fig. 1a). However, LAS had a significantly higher proportion of T>A transversions (Fig. 1a). PCA of the SBS profiles further demonstrated that LAS clustered distinctly from NLAS and were positioned closer to HCC (Fig. 1b), suggesting that tumors from the liver share a similar etiology.

To unveil the potential etiology of the mutation process, a de novo extraction approach was applied to the SBS profiles, including all angiosarcomas from various organs along with HCCs (see Methods). Four distinct mutational signatures were identified and designated as signatures A, B, C, and D (Fig. 1c). To determine the potential etiology of each mutational signature, we assessed their similarity to mutational spectra from the Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC) (version 3.2) and carcinogen signatures derived from human-induced pluripotent stem cells exposed to various environmental carcinogens [8] (Fig. 1d). Surprisingly, signature A showed high similarity to SBS22 (cosine similarity = 0.93), a mutational signature associated with aristolochic acid (AA) exposure (Fig. 1d), which was also highly similar to the experimental signature from cells exposed to aristolochic acid I (AAI) (cosine similarity = 0.97) (Fig. 1d). Signature B exhibited high similarity to COSMIC SBS7a/b (cosine similarity = 0.93 and 0.92), which is linked to UV light exposure, and to the experimental signature from cells exposed to simulated solar radiation (cosine similarity = 0.97). Additionally, signature C was highly similar to SBS1 (cosine similarity = 0.88), which was associated with spontaneous deamination and enzymatic processes. However, while signature D shares similarities with SBS40 and SBS5 (cosine similarity = 0.73 and 0.72, respectively), the etiology of these COSMIC signatures remains undetermined (Fig. 1d).

We examined the contributions of these signatures across all samples (Fig. 1e) and found that the contribution of signature A in LAS was significantly higher than that in NLAS (Fig. 1f). Although the contribution of signature A was comparable between LAS and HCC, the contributions of signatures C and D were significantly different between LAS and HCC (Fig. 1f), suggesting that although AA plays a significant role in these two types of liver cancer, other etiologies may be related to its pathogenic pathway.

To elucidate the impact of the AA signature on LAS, we applied an alternative approach to quantify the contribution of mutational signatures in each sample. By fitting mutational spectra to COSMIC SBS signatures, we found a significantly higher proportion of AA-associated signature SBS22 in LAS but not in NLAS (Fig. 1g). We used the mSigAct signature presence test [16] to evaluate the presence of the SBS22 signature in the mutational spectra of all samples. Eighty percent (8/10) of LAS samples but only three out of nine NLAS samples showed a significant presence of SBS22 (Fig. 1h).

We also examined the presence of vinyl chloride-associated signatures in the LAS cohort. Vinyl chloride is a carcinogenic chemical that causes multiple types of liver cancer, but its impact has faded out after stricter environmental regulations [4, 5]. Studies in mice found that vinylidene chloride exposure-induced liver cancer was associated with SBS19 and SBS42 [17], both of which had lower contributions to our LAS mutational profile (online suppl. Fig. 2). Our mutational signature analysis confirmed that AA exposure is a critical etiological factor in the pathogenesis of LAS but not in other NLAS subtypes.

Because AA forms DNA adducts and is difficult to repair owing to its bulky size, cancers associated with AA exposure generally have a higher tumor mutation burden (TMB) [18]. Indeed, LAS had a significantly higher TMB than NLAS in Taiwan (Fig. 2a), but there were no significant differences in the neoantigen burden (Fig. 2b). The difference in the proportion of patients with high TMB between the LAS and CMI groups was not significant. This may be explained by the fact that some facial and scalp angiosarcoma patients with CMI have chronic UV exposure and are associated with higher TMB (online suppl. Fig. 3). Other genomic alterations, such as CNA burden and tumor heterogeneity estimated by the mutant-allele tumor heterogeneity score, were not significantly different between the LAS, NLAS, CMI, and HCC groups (Fig. 2c, d).

The genomic alteration profile also differs between liver and non-liver angiosarcoma. KDR was the only genomic alteration found among the top 10 genomic alterations in both liver (30%, 3/10) and non-liver angiosarcoma (33%, 3/9). The proportion of patients with TP53 and ATRX mutations and genomic alterations commonly found in other soft tissue sarcomas but less prevalent in angiosarcomas was also higher in LAS (TP53 mutation: liver vs. non-liver 60% vs. 22%, p = 0.17; ATRX mutations: liver vs. non-liver 50% vs. 0%, p = 0.03). Notably, six of the eight (75%) TP53 mutations in LAS (one LAS patient had two different TP53 mutation types) showed a typical T>A transversion caused explicitly by AA-induced DNA damage [18, 19].

We further examined the protein status of the mutated genes that was found to be significantly different between liver (n = 8) and non-liver (n = 17) angiosarcoma. Striking differences were observed in ATRX expression: 7 of 8 (87.5%) LAS demonstrated loss of ATRX protein expression, whereas none of the 16 NLAS showed loss (Fisher’s exact test, p < 0.001) (online suppl. Fig. 4A, B; online suppl. Table 1).

For TP53, we defined aberrant expression as either ≥60% of tumor cells with strong nuclear staining or < 5% of tumor cells with nuclear staining (null). Seven of eight (87%) LAS demonstrated aberrant p53 expression, compared with 7 of 17 (41%) NLAS (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.04). Among the 7 LA patients with p53 aberrancy, 6 had strong nuclear staining and only one showed null nuclear staining (online suppl. Fig. 4C, D; online suppl. Table 1).

One patient with LAS treated successfully with local therapy was diagnosed with urothelial bladder cancer 5 years prior. Two years after the diagnosis of LAS, he developed supraclavicular lymph node metastasis, which was confirmed to be metastatic urothelial carcinoma. Genomic analysis of his LAS and the two different urothelial carcinoma metastatic lesions from two different time points (one before the diagnosis of LAS and the other after) revealed that all three samples exhibited a predominant T>A transversion pattern, with signature A contributing to more than 60% of the single-nucleotide mutation profile (Fig. 3a, b). Interestingly, although the majority of these mutations could be attributed to AA exposure, the genomic mutation profiles were distinct. Among the mutated genes in bladder cancer and LAS, most mutations were not shared (Fig. 3c), suggesting that, even when tumors arise from exposure to the same carcinogen, different cancer types may follow distinct molecular pathways.

GSEA reveals that immune-associated pathways have significantly higher enrichment scores in LAS compared to NLAS (Fig. 4a). Consistently, immune cell composition analysis using Danaher signatures showed a significantly higher number of T cells in LAS (Fig. 4b). This finding aligns with the Gene Ontology (GO) term analysis related to T-cell activation and proliferation, which also demonstrated higher enrichment scores in LAS (Fig. 4c). Using WES data to quantify the T-cell fraction, we also observed a higher level of T-cell infiltration in LAS than in NLAS, although the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 4d). Furthermore, cell surface markers and cytokines associated with T-cell activation were more highly expressed in LAS than in NLAS (Fig. 4e). Notably, signatures predictive of the response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [20–22] were significantly enriched in LA (Fig. 4f), suggesting that ICI therapy warrants further investigation for AA-associated angiosarcoma.

For immune infiltrates, areas of 1 mm2 with the highest density of immune cells within the tumor were selected by our sarcoma pathologist (JCL). Median CD3+ T-cell density was significantly higher in liver versus non-liver angiosarcoma (206.5 vs. 110.0 cells/mm2, Mann-Whitney U test, p = 0.005), as was CD8+ T-cell density (111.5 vs. 61.0 cells/mm2, p = 0.010). In contrast, PD-1+ cell density, used as a proxy for Tregs, did not differ significantly (39.5 vs. 13.0 cells/mm2, p = 0.080) (online suppl. Table 1; online suppl. Fig. 4E, F).

To identify the localization of T cells, we performed high-resolution spatial transcriptomics analysis on one LAS and one NLAS (cardiac) using Xenium platform. Through images, we confirmed that the number of CD8+ T cells was indeed higher in the liver compared with the non-liver tumor (CD8+ T cells/tumor cells [%], liver vs. cardiac 65,778/23,068 [28.5%] vs. 373/17,621 [2.12%]). Interestingly, in LAS, CD8+ T cells were predominantly located at the tumor periphery rather than within the tumor core (online suppl. Fig. 5). These data show that AA-associated LAS is strongly correlated with a more inflamed tumor microenvironment with higher T-cell activity, paving the way to consider ICIs for these patients as a treatment option.

Telomere maintenance is essential for cancer cell survival. While approximately 90% of cancers rely on telomerase to preserve telomere length, many sarcomas employ the alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALTs) mechanism. We investigated ALT levels in angiosarcoma by evaluating telomere length using WES and TelSeq [12], quantifying TERRA expression through RNA sequencing and TERRA-QUANT [15], and performing pathway enrichment analyses related to telomere maintenance.

Telomere length estimation using TelSeq revealed that LAS tumors had significantly longer telomeres than matched normal tissues (Fig. 5a). In contrast, no significant differences were observed in Taiwanese patients with NLAS (Fig. 5a). As elevated TERRA expression is a hallmark of ALT-positive cells [23], we quantified TERRA levels in tumor samples (adjusted for tumor fraction). Although TERRA expression varied widely among samples, it was significantly correlated with telomere content (Fig. 5b), and no differences were detected between angiosarcomas arising from different anatomical locations (Fig. 5c).

Pathway enrichment analysis demonstrated that telomere maintenance pathways were significantly more activated (FDR <0.05) in LAS than in NLAS (Fig. 5d). ALT-positive tumors are typically characterized by low telomerase activity. In agreement with this, LAS exhibited significantly lower expression of TERT and TERC, key components of telomerase, than NLAS (Fig. 5e). These findings suggest that LAS preferentially employs the ALT pathway, which distinguishes it from other cancer types that predominantly rely on telomerases.

At the protein level, as shown in the previous section, we confirmed that there is loss of ATRX nucleus staining in 7 out of 8 (88%) LAS patients while 0 out of 17 (0%) NLAS had lost ATRX protein expression. POT1 is a part of the shelterin complex responsible for telomere maintenance and has been reported to be related to angiosarcoma [24, 25]. In our cohort, one LAS patient had POT1 deletion, and three NLAS patients had POT1 alteration (one amplification, one deletion, and one point mutation), while in the Count Me In cohort 21% of patients were reported with POT1 alterations. Comparing the mRNA expression of POT1 between liver and non-liver angiosarcoma, we did not find significant differences among these two groups (p = 0.39) (online suppl. Fig. 6). These findings suggest that LAS preferentially employs the ALT pathway for telomere maintenance through loss of ATRX function, distinguishing it from NLAS and HCC, which predominantly rely on the telomerase activity.

In a related analysis, we observed that both LAS and HCC had a higher proportion of patients exposed to AA, based on DNA mutation signature analysis (Fig. 1f). When comparing TMMs, the difference in telomere length between tumor and normal tissues was significantly greater in LAS than in HCC (Fig. 6a), indicating that ALT is present in LAS but not in HCC. In contrast, telomerase activity was higher in HCC tissues (Fig. 6b). Despite the common exposure to the DNA-mutating effects of AA, these data suggest that LAS and HCC employ distinct mechanisms for telomere maintenance, which may extend to other oncogenic pathways.

Our study demonstrated that a significant proportion of LAS was associated with AA exposure, as evidenced by the dominant AA mutation signatures detected via WES. Although AA is well established in the pathogenesis of urothelial carcinoma and HCC [16, 26, 27], its involvement in LAS has only recently been reported by Huang et al. [28]. In contrast to the previous report, we analyzed angiosarcoma patients in Taiwan along with a broader cohort from North America (the Count Me In Project) [6], strengthening the specificity of the AA signature in LAS. Importantly, we are the first to show that compared to both NLAS and HCC, LAS preferentially employs the ALT pathway over telomerase activation to maintain the telomere length of cancer cells.

Our and Huang et al.’s [28] findings on AA exposure in LAS may help explain our previous epidemiological observation that LAS is significantly more common in Taiwan than in France [2]. Prior to the regulatory ban on AA-containing Chinese herbal products (AA-CHPs), it was estimated that up to one-third of the Taiwanese population was exposed to AA-CHPs [29]. In addition to its known urothelial carcinogenicity, AA can induce renal dysfunction [30], as supported by Huang et al. who previously demonstrated that end-stage renal disease in Taiwanese patients was associated with an increased risk of LAS [28]. Following the ban on AA-CHPs in 2003 in Taiwan, the incidence of urothelial and renal pelvic carcinomas declined markedly [31]. However, a persistently high incidence of LAS remains [2]. Although precise exposure histories for AA were not available in our cohort, the significantly older age of LAS patients compared to those with NLAS and HCC suggests that a longer latency period may be required for AA-associated LAS to manifest. Continued surveillance is warranted to determine whether the incidence of LAS will eventually follow the declining trends observed in other AA-associated malignancies such as urothelial carcinoma and HCC.

Understanding the specific etiological drivers of angiosarcoma is crucial for developing molecular-guided treatment strategies. For instance, scalp and facial angiosarcomas linked to UV exposure exhibit high TMB and a favorable response to ICIs [32]. Similarly, radiation-associated angiosarcomas characterized by MYC amplification may be susceptible to emerging MYC-targeted therapies [33]. Reflecting the DNA-damaging effects of AA [18], LAS in our cohort displayed both a higher TMB and a more inflamed tumor microenvironment than NLAS. These observations mirror findings in other AA-associated cancers, such as urothelial carcinoma and HCC [16, 26, 27], and suggest that AA-driven LAS may be particularly amenable to immunotherapy.

Our data further revealed that angiosarcomas originating from different anatomical sites harbor distinct genomic alterations, reflecting their varied etiologies. Although TP53 mutations are detected in approximately 30% of angiosarcomas, their prevalence increases to nearly 60% in LAS, suggesting a unique pathogenic mechanism or environmental exposure that affects hepatic endothelial cells.

In addition to identifying ALT as the predominant TMM in LAS, we found that alterations in ATRX, a gene frequently linked to the ALT phenotype in soft tissue sarcoma [34–36], were significantly more prevalent in the liver than in NLAS. In contrast, mutations in POT1, a gene involved in telomere protection that was previously associated with ALT-positive angiosarcomas [24, 37], were absent in our LAS cohort and was found in only 1 patient with head and neck angiosarcoma in the NTUH cohort. These findings suggest that LAS is more likely to rely on the ATRX loss-of-function-mediated ALT pathway rather than POT1 dysfunction or telomerase activation for telomere maintenance. Further investigation into the molecular mechanisms of ALT in angiosarcoma should consider the mechanisms leading to telomere maintenance to understand its pathogenesis and therapeutic implications. The downstream protein alterations associated with TP53 and ATRX genetic alterations were confirmed by IHC, supporting that these genomic differences between liver and non-liver angiosarcomas warrant further characterization to elucidate their roles in pathogenesis.

Moreover, our findings highlight that cancers initiated by the same carcinogen can diverge during their subsequent molecular evolution. For example, although both LAS and HCC in Taiwan are linked to AA exposure, they differ markedly in their TMMs; LAS predominantly employ the ALT pathway, whereas HCC activate telomerase. In a unique case involving a patient diagnosed with both urothelial carcinoma and LAS, although all the tumors exhibited AA signatures, their overall genomic mutation profiles were largely distinct. This reinforces the concept that exposure to a common initiator can lead to divergent genomic landscapes, with important implications for treatment selection.

Last, but not least, is the critical question why is there such a striking difference in terms of gene expression between the liver and non-liver angiosarcoma, given they have similar endothelial cell markers. There are several potential explanations to this observation. First, divergent etiologies likely give rise to distinct genomic landscapes and consequent variation in the tumor microenvironment. The DNA-damaging effects of AA and the markedly higher TMB observed in LAS may drive enhanced immune cell infiltration and elevated interferon-gamma signaling. Second, intrinsic genomic and proteomic differences may underlie the transcriptional divergence. For instance, ATRX alterations were found in 87% of LAS but was absent in NLAS. Although ATRX is best known for its role in telomere maintenance, prior work by Chu et al. [38] has shown that ATRX also exerts broad regulatory effects on gene expression across the genome. Lastly, although direct evidence remains limited, liver and non-liver angiosarcomas may arise from distinct endothelial cell populations. Liver sinusoidal endothelial cells are specialized, fenestrated cells lacking a basement membrane and possessing high endocytic capacity, whereas endothelial cells in tissues such as the skin exhibit a continuous morphology with tight junctions and well-defined basement membranes [39]. Although speculative, such developmental distinctions may contribute to the profound molecular and phenotypic divergence observed between these subtypes.

This study had several limitations. First, all LAS samples were derived from a Taiwanese cohort, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to a broader and ethnically diverse population. However, considering the unrecognized use of AA-containing herbal medicines in various regions across Asia, Eastern Europe, and beyond, the etiological role of AA in LAS may have broader relevance and warrants further investigation in global cohorts [18, 40, 41]. Second, although AA-associated LAS demonstrate a high TMB and an immune-inflamed tumor microenvironment, features that are typically associated with responsiveness to ICIs, clinical evidence for the efficacy of ICIs in this context of LAS remains scarce. Prospective clinical trials specifically targeting visceral angiosarcomas are urgently needed to assess the therapeutic potential of immunotherapy in this rare but aggressive malignancy.

In conclusion, our analysis revealed that, similar to the findings of Huang et al. [28], AA exposure represents a novel etiology for LAS. We further demonstrated that LAS is characterized by a distinct molecular profile, including a propensity for the ALT phenotype, which sets them apart from NLAS. A deeper understanding of these pathogenic mechanisms may ultimately pave the way for more personalized and effective therapeutic strategies for patients with aggressive malignancies.
