# Functional network dynamics in photosensitive epilepsy depend on stimulation frequency and photosensitivity type
**Authors:** Lili Timar, Sina Deplazes, Julia Bothmann, Roland Renzel, Debora Ledergerber, Tena Dubcek, Lukas Imbach
**DOI:** [10.1101/2024.12.21.24319242](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.12.21.24319242)

## Content

Abstract

Objective
To describe the underlying dynamical mechanisms of photosensitive epilepsy (PSE) and different photosensitivity types.


Methods
This retrospective study included healthy controls (HC), non-PSE patients with epilepsy (PWE), and PSE patients. All participants were stimulated with flickering white light (1-60Hz) under 10-20 EEG monitoring.


Results
We observed significantly higher occipital Photoparoxysmal response (PPR) power in PSE patients compared to HC for stimulation frequencies 10Hz-20Hz. This activity was highest in Type 4, which shows a type-specific variation of the PPR. However, the excitability of the visual cortex, investigated by the occipital transient (P100) and steady-state visual evoked potentials (ssVEP), remained unchanged in most PSE types compared to HC and to PWE. The ssVEP power only increased significantly in Type 4 compared to HC. Instead, PSE patients showed apparent differences in functional connectivity in the PPR band (3-4Hz) with hypoconnected centro-parietal regions and hyperconnected anterior and anterio-posterior areas compared to HC and PWE for stimulation frequencies 10Hz-20Hz.


Conclusion
PSE is a network effect modulated by hyperconnected anterior and anterio-posterior regions, accompanied by a hyperexcitable visual cortex.


Significance
We provide novel evidence that altered network dynamics in PSE patients are likely a prerequisite for the propagation of the PPR and type-specific responses.

1. Introduction 39 
Photosensitive epilepsy (PSE) affects 5% of epilepsy patients worldwide, with a high prevalence in females, 40 
children, and adolescen ts (Fisher et al., 2022; Martins da Silva & Leal, 2017) . It is diagnosed via the 41 
Intermittent Photic Stimulation procedure (IPS) under concurrent Electroencephalography (EEG) 42 
monitoring (Kasteleijn-Nolst Trenité et al., 2012). The IPS delivers white flashes of light that evoke transient 43 
visually evoked potentials (tVEP) for lower and steady -state visual evoked potentials (ssVEP) for higher 44 
stimulation frequencies. Both the tVEP and the ssVEP  are normal physiological reactions, time -locked to 45 
the stimulus and linked to the triggering frequency (Herrmann, 2001). However, stimulation frequencies 46 
specific to PSE patients, mainly between 15 and 20 Hz, can induce abnormal EEG activity, characterized by 47 
3-4 Hz spikes or spike-slow wave complexes , called the photoparoxysmal response (PPR) (Fisher et al., 48 
2005; Harding & Harding, 2010; Kasteleijn -Nolst Trenité et al., 2012; Taoufiqi et al., 2016) . Based on the 49 
propagation of the PPR, PSE can be further categorized into four different types ranging from least to most 50 
severe (Type 1 -4) (Waltz et al., 1992) . Photosensitivity occurs mainly in genetic (idiopathic) generalized 51 
epilepsies (GGE) but also in focal idiopathic photosensitive occipital epilepsy (IPOE)  and has a high 52 
prevalence in certain generalized epilepsy syndromes such as juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME) or juvenile 53 
absence epilepsy (JAE) (Guerrini & Genton, 2004; Martins da Silva & Leal, 2017; Taylo et al., 2013) . Both 54 
focal (occipital) and generalized (whole brain) network effects have been hypothesized and investigated 55 
as the driving mechanism of PSE (Brinciotti et al., 2020a; Moeller et al., 2013; Strigaro et al., 2012; Varotto 56 
et al., 2012).  57 
Previous studies in PSE have revealed increased amplitude of the  occipital tVEP, the so-called P100, in 58 
response to single- and paired flashes compared to healthy controls (HC) (Brinciotti et al., 2020; Strigaro 59 
et al., 2012) . It was suggested that augmentation of the P100 amplitude indicates a focal 60 
hypersynchronization of the occipital cortex, potentially leading to hyperexcitability and seizures (Wilkins 61 
et al., 2004). Further indication of a hyperexcited visual cortex associated with PPR was shown in a study 62 
using transcranial magnetic stimulation  that found lower phosphene thresholds  in individuals with PPR  63 
compared to those without (Siniatchkin et al., 2007a). Recent papers have also identified abnormal activity 64 
of specific EEG frequency bands in the visual cortex of PSE patients  in the form of increased alpha and 65 
gamma oscillations,  highlighting its altered excitatory and inhibitory processes (Hermes et al., 2017; 66 
Vaudano et al., 2017).  67 
While the focal hyperexcitability of the visual cortex has been associated with PSE, the extent to which it 68 
is causally involved in generating and propagating the PPR is still debatable. Moeller et al. (2009a) found 69 
that during a PPR, blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) responses increased in the occipital cortex of PSE 70 
patients, however, similar increases were observed in the parietal and frontal regions . In a later study , 71 
Moeller et al. (2013) proposed that PPR is a cortical phenomenon and modeled the general network of its 72 
propagation to involve occipital, parietal , and frontal areas.  Similarly, a connectivity stud y in the EEG -73 
gamma band during photic stimulation pointed towards hyperconnectivity as the facilitator of the PPR. 74 
They showed higher anterior functional connectivity , suggesting that the PPR might depend on 75 
hyperconnected frontal regions (Varotto et al., 2012). These findings indicate that the hyperexcitability of 76 
the visual cortex is a concomitant rather than the primary driver of the PPR and that network-based effects 77 
are responsible for PSE.  78

Here, we aimed to solidify the role of the network in PSE and to provide a more detailed understanding of 79 
its underlying dynamics. We retrospectively analyzed scalp EEG recordings under IPS stimulation  of PSE 80 
patients (Type 2, Type 3, and Type 4) , non-PSE epilepsy patients  (PWE), and healthy controls (HC) . We 81 
assessed the activation of the visual cortex  by analyzing the physiological ( P100 and ssVEP) and 82 
pathological (PPR) response patterns in reaction to IPS. Additionally, the propagation characteristics of the 83 
PPR were investigated by evaluating the functional connectivity (FC) in the low-frequency band (3-4 Hz), 84 
where pathological activity occurs but so far remained unexplored.  85

2. Methods & Materials 87 
Our aims for the present study were to  (i) characterize type - and stimulation frequency -specific PPR 88 
activity in PSE groups, (ii) directly assess and compare the activity of the visual cortex in response to IPS in 89 
PSE, non -PSE, and HC groups, (iii) map  dynamic FC patterns of PSE patients in response to high -risk 90 
stimulation frequencies in the PPR band. 91 
2.1 Study populations 92 
We retrospectively reviewed a large  cohort of epilepsy patients and healthy participants  (HC) who 93 
underwent a routine EEG recording at the Swiss Epilepsy Center. All participants were stimulated with IPS 94 
under EEG monitoring along standardized protocols according to the International Federation of Clinical 95 
Neurophysiology (IFCN) guidelines (Peltola et al., 2022) . Recordings were carried out as routine EEG 96 
patient follow-ups and as occupational fitness assessments for the HC. Two blinded independent medical 97 
professionals evaluated the encrypted EEG recordings and determined the PSE Type (2, 3, or 4) or lack 98 
thereof (PWE) . A third medical professional provided the final classification in case of differing 99 
classification. The patients' epilepsy syndromes were categorized based on the International League of 100 
Epilepsy (ILAE) criteria (Berg et al., 2010; Hirsch et al., 2022) , into genetic generalized (GGE), focal ( FE: 101 
structural, non -structural or unknown), combined ( CM: generalized and focal) or epilepsy of unknown 102 
origin (UC).  103 
We selected 45 PSE patients who fulfilled the criteria of Type 2 (n = 11; mean age 29.5 ± 16.5, 10 females), 104 
Type 3 (n = 14; mean age 22.8  ± 12.9, 12 females ), or Type 4 (n = 20; mean age 21.1 ± 8. 6, 19 females). 105 
Photosensitivity was determined by the observation of the PPR under IPS stimulation. Occipital spikes and 106 
slow waves with parietal propagation were categorized as Type 2, parieto-occipital spikes and slow waves 107 
with spread to the frontal regions as Type 3, and generalized spike and wave complexes as Type 4  (Waltz 108 
et al., 1992). Type 1 patients were excluded due to frequent ambiguity with PSE Type 2.  109 
We also recruited 19 non-PSE patients with epilepsy (PWE, mean age 22.2 ± 8.7, 15 females) and 20 healthy 110 
individuals (HC, 21 ± 2.7, 3 females) as control groups. The PWE group consisted of epilepsy patients who, 111 
under IPS stimulation, exhibited no PPR nor sensitivity upon eye closure, indicating no photosensitivity. 112 
Finally, individuals of the HC group exhibited no PPR under IPS stimulation nor sensitivity upon eye closure 113 
and had no current or history of epilepsy. Additionally, the participants in this group took no CNS active 114 
medication nor had cognitive deficits . For the categorization of the patient s' (PSE, PWE) epilepsy 115 
syndromes and ASM intake, refer to Table 1.  116 
All subjects’ consent was obtained according to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local 117 
ethical committee (Kantonale Ethikkommission, KEK Zurich, KEK number 2024-00401). 118

2.2 EEG recording 119 
EEG was recorded with 23 sintered Ag/AgCl scalp EEG electrodes under the 10-20 system with a low-pass 120 
filter of 70 Hz . An additional pericentral electrode was  used as reference located close to Cz. Electrod e 121 
impedance was kept between 2-5 KΩ. For most participants (n=73), the recordings were captured with the 122 
NicOne System (NicoletOne™ 44 channel amplifier, Natus, United States ) or with the Micromed 123 
Ambulatory System (Micromed® Evolution Plus, 40 channels ). The sampling frequency was 256  Hz. EEG 124 
recordings were conducted in a suitably equipped room at the Swiss Epilepsy Center, Zürich, Switzerland. 125 
All participants were lying in a darkened room and completed an approximately 20-minute-long recording 126 
with three conditions: resting state (with eyes closed and eyes open), hyperventilation, and IPS (with eyes 127 
closed and eyes open) . An EEG specialist verbally instructed eye closure and opening, as well as the rate 128 
of hyperventilation. During classification, the whole recording was evaluated. However, the analysis in this 129 
present study only considered the IPS condition. The IPS stimuli were delivered in four different sections 130 
according to IFCN guidelines (Peltola et al., 2022). The participants' physical state and EEG recording were 131 
constantly monitored by the EEG specialists present. IPS was stopped after observing a PPR response or at 132 
the participant's request.  133 
2.3 EEG signal preprocessing steps 134 
EEGs recorded with the Nicone System were reviewed with Study Room (NicoletOne LTM version 5.95.0.2) 135 
and exported in .edf format, while EEGs recorded with Micromed were reviewed with Brain Quick (System 136 
Plus Software version 1.02.0002). For further analysis, 19 channel EEG recordings were loaded with Python 137 
(version 3.12) (Python Software Foundation)  from .edf or .trc formats, respectively . We applied a high-138 
pass filter of 0.01  Hz to remove baseline wander or drift caused by movement, respiration , or some 139 
impedance changes of the electrode. This chosen cutoff frequency  also allowed us to observe activity 140 
following a stimulation frequency of 1 Hz. Notch filters were used to filter out line noise at 50 Hz.  The 141 
average of all electrodes served as a reference for all further analyses. Triggers linked to the IPS stimulation 142 
carried annotations on the exact stimulation  frequencies given throughout the EEG recording.  The 143 
following stimulation frequencies were consistently recorded for all included individuals: 1Hz, 5Hz, 10 Hz, 144 
15 Hz, 20 Hz, and 25 Hz. Hence, our analyses considered responses to these five conditions. 145

2.4 Visual Evoked Potentials (P100)  147 
 148 
We calculated the transient visual evoked potentials  (tVEP) for the 1Hz condition by creating 1s epochs, 149 
500 ms before and after every single stimulus, and averaging those for each individual. Automatic rejection 150 
of epochs with amplitude greater than +/- 100 μV was applied to reduce artifacts and interferences related 151 
to the PPR (Brinciotti et al., 2020). Based on this, two recordings (one Type 4 and one PWE) were excluded 152 
from this analysis. The tVEP has two main parts: the main complex, the positive peak (P100), and the after-153 
discharge (Strigaro et al., 2012) . In previous studies,  the amplitude of the  P100 showed significant 154 
differences between PSE and non-PSE participants (Brinciotti et al., 2020; Siniatchkin et al., 2007b; Strigaro 155 
et al., 2012) . Hence, we calculated the peak -to-peak amplitude of the P100 wave and its latency as an 156 
additional measure. For group comparisons, we estimated grand averages of the P100; however, for 157 
statistical analysis, we considered the individual amplitude and latency values . In nine patients (five Type 158

2.5 Power Spectral Density  162 
Power spectral densities (PSD) were calculated on recording segments  in response to the remaining 163 
stimulation frequencies (5Hz, 10Hz, 15Hz, 20Hz, and 25Hz). We used the fast Fourier transformation (FFT) 164 
with a 2s window size, which provided PSD values in a frequency window of 0-60 Hz in bins of 0.5 Hz. We 165 
computed PSD at occipital (O1 and O2 averaged), parietal (P3, P4, Pz averaged; in supplementary material), 166 
and at frontal electrodes (F3, F4, Fz averaged; in supplementary material) for every individual since these 167 
were previously shown to be affected by PPR (Moeller, et al., 2009a).  168 
For each stimulation frequency , spectral power  at the fundamental ssVEP band  (i.e., the narrow band 169 
corresponding to the stimulation frequency ) was calculated by the sum of EEG power at three bins 170 
respective to the stimulation frequency (4.5-5.5 Hz, 9.5-10.5 Hz, 14.5-15.5 Hz, 19.5-20.5 Hz, 24.5-25.5 Hz) 171 
and multiplying that by 0.5 (bin size), resulting in a total power value . The same calculation was done for 172 
each stimulation frequency to estimate PPR power, except here, we summed the EEG power in the three 173 
bins of the PPR frequency band of 3-4 Hz (3 Hz, 3.5 Hz, and 4 Hz). 174 
There were three patients without a 25  Hz recording ( two Type 4, one Type 2) and one Type 2 patient 175 
without a 5 Hz recording. Hence, these patients were excluded from this part of the analysis.  176 
2.6 Scalp-scalp Functional Connectivity 177 
FC responses to the stimulation frequencies of interest (5Hz-25Hz) were measured by calculating the Phase 178 
Locking Value (PLV)  with the same segmentation parameters as for the PSD analysis.  PLV is suitable for 179 
investigating task-induced changes measuring connectivity by estimating the phase-locking consistency of 180 
two signals across multiple trials, i.e., whether two signals systematically rise and fall together  (Cui et al., 181 
2023). The frequency band of interest for investigating FC changes  was the PPR band  (3-4 Hz). PLV was 182 
calculated between all channels, and to gain more regional dependent measures, we calculated it region-183 
wise by averaging the corresponding electrodes  together. The regions included: Fronto-polar (Fp): Fp1, 184 
Fp2; Fronto-central (Fc): F3, F4, Fz; Fronto -temporal (Ft): F7, F8; Central (C): C3, C4, Cz; Temporal (T): T3, 185 
T4, T5, T6; Parietal (P): P3, P4, Pz; Occipital (O): O1, O2. The same 4 patients excluded from the PSD analysis 186 
(under section 2.5) due to missing recordings were also excluded from this analysis. 187 
2.7 Statistical analysis 188 
All analyses and statistical tests were carried out in Python 3.12 (Python Software Foundation). Our data 189 
showed unequal variances and was not normally distributed. Hence, for all comparisons between groups, 190 
we used robust permutation tests. Permutation tests can be applied to non-normally distributed data and 191 
control for family-wise error rates for comparisons in multidimensional data, making them well-suited for 192 
FC calculations  channel- and region-wise (Smith & Nicols 2008; Rempala & Yang 2013).  We used the 193 
difference between sample means as test statistics, with 1000 permutations generated with  random 194 
resampling. Bonferroni correction was applied to correct for multiple comparisons between groups. All 195 
statistical tests were independent and two-sided. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05. 95 % CI was 196 
calculated for significant differences with Bootstrapping along the exact specifications as the permutation 197 
test. 198

3. Results  199 
3.1 Study cohorts 200 
A total of 84 participants  were included in this analysis. Our cohort includes children and adults and is 201 
predominantly female (Table 1) since PSE is more prevalent in females, children, and adolescents (Fisher 202 
et al., 2022). Importantly, we found no significant age difference between any of our groups (all p > 1.0). 203 
3.2. Visual evoked potentials (P100) 204 
First, to characterize the general reactivity of the primary visual cortex to photic stimulation, we measured 205 
the P100. The latency of the P100 showed no significant differences between PSE patients and HC (all p > 206 
0.119), in line with previous findings (Brinciotti et al., 2020; Siniatchkin et al., 2007b). The PWE group did, 207 
however, exhibit a significantly larger latency compared to HC (p = 0.008, CI95 = [-29.632, -12.067]) (Figure 208 
1A&B). Contrary to the previous literature, the  P100 ( peak-to-peak) amplitude showed no  significant 209 
differences between HC and either of the patient groups (PWE or PSE) (all p > 0.152) (Figure 1A&B). Overall, 210 
we observed no significant P100 amplitude or latency alterations in PSE patients  compared to control 211 
subjects. 212

3.3 Steady-state visual evoked potentials and Photoparoxysmal responses 214 
Next, we analyzed the occipital ssVEP in response to higher temporal frequencies  to further investigate 215 
the reactivity of the primary visual cortex (Porciatti et al., 2000) . This was assessed at the fundamental 216 
frequency band, corresponding to the narrow band around the stimulation frequency. The fundamental 217 
ssVEP power increased significantly in the PSE Type 4 group, compared to HC for stimulation frequencies 218 
5 Hz, 15  Hz, and 20 Hz . However , this remained unchanged in Type s 3 and 2 PSE patients (Figure 1C; 219 
Supplementary Table 1). We also observed a significantly increased ssVEP power in PWE compared to HC 220 
for stimulation frequency 5 Hz (Figure 1C ; Supplementary Table 1 ). No further difference s were found 221 
when comparing PWE to HC (Figure 1C) or to any of the PSE groups (Supplementary Section 3.1). Additional 222 
comparisons between PSE patients revealed a significant ssVEP power increase for stimulation frequency 223 
of 10 Hz in Type 4 compared to the other PSE groups (Supplementary Section 3.2.).  224 
To characterize the epileptic activity of PSE patients, we analyzed the occipital PPR power in response to 225 
the same temporal frequencies as for ssVEP. As expected, the PPR power increased significantly in all PSE 226 
patients compared to HC (Figure 2A ; Supplementary Table 1 ), with the highest values observed for 227 
stimulation frequencies between 10 -20 Hz (Figure 2B). The peak group power within the established 10 -228 
20 Hz risk range was noted for different stimulation frequencies (15 Hz for Type 2 and 3; and 10 Hz for 229 
Type 4 patients). PPR power increased with type severity, showing the highest values in Type 4 followed 230 
by Type 3 (Figure 2A&B, Table 2 ). The effect of type severity was further evidenced by Type 4 reaching 231 
significantly larger occipital PPR power compared to the other PSE groups for stimulation frequencies 10-232 
25 Hz (Supplementary Section 4.1.). For the same stimulation frequencies, Type 4 also showed significantly 233 
higher occipital PPR power compared to PWE (Supplementary Section 4.2.). Surprisingly, PWE exhibited 234 
significantly higher PPR power compared to HC for stimulation frequencies 20 Hz and 25 Hz (Figure 2A; 235 
Supplementary Table 1). However, we do not consider this an actual PPR activity since PWE showed a rise 236 
in power unspecific to a frequency band (between 1-15Hz), concurrent with the HC group showing a slight 237 
decline in power in response to these stimulation frequencies. Together, these most likely facilitated the 238

3.4 Scalp-scalp Connectivity  247 
Finally, functional network effects  were explored by  analyzing FC patterns in the 3-4Hz frequency band 248 
(PPR band)  in response to stimulation frequencies 5 -25 Hz.  We identified only a few decreased  FC 249 
(hypoconnected) patterns in PWE compared to HC. These hypoconnected FC patterns were found 250 
between central and frontotemporal  regions for stimulation frequencies 5 and 20 Hz  and between 251 
temporal and occipital regions  for stimulation frequencies 10 Hz  (Figure 3) . In contrast,  different FC 252 
patterns in PSE patients compared to HC were apparent for risk range frequencies (10 -20 Hz) established 253 
in our PSD analysis . We observed  significant hyper - and hypoconnectivity patterns in  PSE patients 254 
compared to HC, governed by type severity and stimulation frequency . The most prominent patterns of 255 
decreased FC in all PSE patients were shown for stimulation frequencies 10 -25 Hz between central and 256 
parietal regions and between central and f rontopolar regions (Figure 3; Supplementary Table 2). This 257 
centro-parietal and centro -frontopolar hypoconnectivity was less pronounced when comparing PSE 258 
patients to PWE (Supplementary Section 7 & Figure 3). Increased FC (h yperconnectivity) patterns were 259 
only observed in patients with PPR propagation, i.e., in Types 3 and 4. These were subtle in Type 3, with a 260 
slight FC increase between frontal electrodes and between occipital and  frontal channels  (Figure 3) . 261 
Hyperconnectivity became quite prominent in Type 4, specifically between frontocentral and frontopolar 262 
and between occipital and frontocentral regions for stimulation frequencies 10 -20 Hz  (Figure 3; 263 
Supplementary Table 3). Significantly increased within -anterior and between -posterior-anterior FC  264 
seemed to be even more evident in Types 3 and 4 compared to PWE (Supplementary Section 7 & Figure 265 
3).  266 
In brief, we showed FC differences in PSE patients' responses  to IPS compared to control subjects. These  267 
involved hypoconnected centro-parietal and centro-frontopolar areas in all PSE types and hyperconnected 268 
frontal occipito-frontal connectivity in PSE patients with PPR propagation.  269

4. Discussion 271 
 272 
We examined the underlying mechanisms of type -specific PSE responses using IPS and  scalp EEG, aiming 273 
to provide a comprehensive, systematic analysis of PSE and reveal new insights into the underlying 274 
mechanisms of the condition. To date, multiple studies have investigated PSE and the hyperexcitability of 275 
the visual cortex  (Brinciotti et al., 2020; Siniatchkin et al., 2007 a,b; Strigaro et al., 2012; Wilkins et al., 276 
2004). However, possible network differences driving the propagation of the PPR have yet to be 277 
investigated in detail.  In this present study, we observed that PPR activity was affected by both type 278 
severity and stimulation frequency. However, hyperexcitability of the visual cortex was only apparent in 279

Our results show evident  pathological activity (PPR) at occipital electrodes in PSE patients, with 284 
significantly enhanced PPR EEG power compared to HC. The occipital PPR power was affected by 285 
stimulation frequency , with the highest values shown for the range of 10 -20 Hz , in line with previous 286 
studies, and by type severity, with Type 4 showing the highest PPR power  (Harding & Harding, 2010; 287 
Kasteleijn-Nolst Trenité et al., 2012; Porciatti et al., 2000; Taoufiqi et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2023). As part 288 
of a supplementary analysis, we identified similar patterns at parietal and frontal channels, which further 289 
solidifies the involvement of these two areas in PPR propagation, as previously suggested by Moeller et al. 290 
(2013; 2009a).  291 
Unlike prior studies, we did not observe an increased P100 amplitude in PSE patients, which was previously 292 
proposed as an indication of the hyperexcitability of the visual cortex  and key to the PSE reaction.  293 
(Brinciotti et al., 2020; Genç et al., 2005; Siniatchkin et al., 2007b). Albeit not significant, we showed trends 294 
for the highest P100 amplitudes in Type 4 and  lowest in the HC group, similar to previous findings using 295 
single flashes (Strigaro et al., 2012). Interestingly, a trend for increased P100 amplitude also arose in PWE 296 
compared to HC . Although PWE individuals are not photosensitive , their responses could deviate from 297 
those of the HC due to underlying neurological differences . The latency of the P100  only showed a 298 
significant increase in PWE compared to HC, which is not unprecedented (Genç et al., 2005). Several factors 299 
can account for prolonged latency in these patients, such as ASMs (Geller et al., 2005; Verrotti et al., 2000), 300 
abnormal synaptic transmission (Gokcay et al., 2003; Mervaala et al., 1987), or minor changes in underlying 301 
neurophysiologic structures (microdysgenesia) (Meencke & Janz, 1985), many of which could also apply to 302 
the PWE group.  303 
The occipital ssVEP power, however, did increase significantly at the fundamental frequency band in Type 304 
4 compared to HC  in line with Poricatti et al. (2000). This provides  evidence that PSE patients have 305 
heightened occipital activity in response to ongoing visual stimulation, which we only found a trend for in 306 
the P100 analysis. The fact that ssVEP power only increased in Type 4 compared to HC agrees with the 307 
study of Siniatchkin et al. (2007b) , which observed higher VEP amplitudes in patients with propagating 308 
PPRs compared to patients with more local PPRs. The ssVEP power also increased in PWE compared to HC, 309 
although for only stimulation frequency 5 Hz. Such a ssVEP power increase argues for increased neuronal 310 
excitability in the visual cortex of not only the PSE but also non-PSE epilepsy patients compared to HC. The 311 
absence of a significant ssVEP power difference between PWE and the PSE groups further supports this 312 
claim. Together, these findings suggest that PSE patients do  show hyperexcitability in the visual cortex to 313 
external visual stimulation, also shown by previous papers (Brinciotti et al., 2020b; Moeller et al., 2009 a; 314 
Siniatchkin et al., 2007b; Strigaro et al., 2012) . However, in our study, this local effect was small and not 315 
significantly different from those in PWE. Hence, a  hyperexcitable visual cortex  might be a requirement 316 
for PPR , however, it  most likely must be coupled with other factors to generate  and propagate  the 317 
pathological response of PSE patients . This aligns with a study by Moeller et al. (2009a), which showed 318 
that in PSE patients, PPR was associated with activity in frontal and parietal regions , suggesting that the 319 
PPR depends on network differences rather than overactivity of the occipital cortex alone. Another study 320 
found higher visual cortex activation in HC compared to PSE patients during IPS, which could support the 321

Our FC results corroborate this hypothesis, showing significantly different connectivity patterns observed 324

We argue that these pathological hypo-and hyperconnectivity alterations  drive the PPR and are 326 
responsible for its propagation.  327 
All PSE patien ts demonstrated hypoconnectivity between central and parietal, central and frontopolar, 328 
and central and frontotemporal regions when compared to HC. We infer that t hese subnetworks likely 329 
correspond to the default mode network  (DMN), a collection of regions active when the brain is at rest 330 
and not engaged in an activity. It includes the frontal central and parietal areas with the ventral and dorsal 331 
medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex , and lateral parietal cortex (Raichle, 2015). The DMN 332 
was previously shown to decrease its activity in PSE patients and to further decrease during PPR  (Bartolini 333 
et al., 2014; Moeller, et al., 2009 b). Resting-state DMN hypoconnectivity was also shown to accompany 334 
multiple epilepsy syndromes (Gonen et al., 2020; Luo et al., 2011; McGill et al., 2012; Parsons et al., 2020). 335 
Hence, it is not surprising that PWE shows some minimal hypoconnectivity between DMN -related areas 336 
compared to HC. The fact that PWE also exhibited some hypoconnectivity in the DMN could account for 337 
less pronounced hypoconnectivity differences when compared to PSE  patients. Furthermore, decreased 338 
connectivity between DMN regions was suggested to facilitate hyperconnectivity between other  areas 339 
(Vollmar et al., 2012) , which could fit our observations of  several hyperconnected electrodes with 340 
concurrent hypoconnectivity in the inferred DMN regions (Figure 3). 341 
As the severity of the PSE type increased, hyperconnectivity became more evident, demonstrated by 342 
increasingly widespread patterns  compared to HC . While hypoconnectivity was observed in all PSE 343 
patients, hyperconnectivity only characterized th ose with PPR propagation in response to  the high-risk 344 
stimulation frequencies. Type 3 patients showed some abnormal connectivity of the posterior regions and 345 
a slight increase in FC  between occipital and frontal electrodes and between frontal electrodes. Type 4 346 
patients with generalized PPR had quite a clear posterior-anterior connectivity, the directionality of which 347 
was modeled in a study by  Moeller et al. (2013), and within anterior hyperconnectivity . The increased 348 
anterior connectivity agrees with findings from Varotto et al. (2012) who found hyperconnectivity within 349 
anterior regions of PSE patients compared to HC. Increased synchronization of frontoparietal networks 350 
was also associated with PPR in a study on baboons (Szabo et al., 2016) . In a supplementary analysis, 351 
hyperconnectivity patterns in Type 3 and 4 seemed to be enhanced compared to PWE, as opposed to when 352 
these groups were contrasted with HC, particularly in Type 3, where hyperconnectivity was much more 353 
evident. Our ssVEP analysis indicated  that, in general,  epilepsy patients might  have higher local 354 
susceptibility to visual stimuli independent from PSE. It could be that, as a protective mechanism, in PWE, 355 
these areas (anterior regions  and posterior-anterior regions) are less  connected to prevent a PPR from 356

4.1.1 Limitations and outlook 364 
 365 
There is a possibility that the lack of a significant P100 amplitude increase in all patient groups compared 366 
to HC may be due to ASM intake. However, this remains debatable, as some studies have reported reduced 367 
VEP amplitude in patients taking ASM (Geller et al., 2005; Kanazawa & Nagafuji, 1997), while others claim 368 
that it only affects the latency rather than the amplitude of the P100  (Verrotti et al., 2000; Yüksel et al., 369 
1995). Another limitation we must address is the sex imbalance between the HC group (mostly male) and 370 
the patient groups (mostly female). It has been shown that generally, females have higher ssVEP 371 
amplitudes compared to males (Krishnan et al., 2005; Skosnik et al., 2006). In this present study, we cannot 372 
entirely exclude whether the significant ssVEP power increase in patient groups was only due to sex 373 
differences or due to a combination of sex and photosensitivity effects. However, we consider the former 374 
unlikely since we observed variability in the ssVEP power governed by stimulation frequency and type 375 
severity, as evidenced by only Type 4 patients showing significant differences compared to HC. Regarding 376 
FC analyses, we noted minimal distinctions between PWE and HC. Since PWE and PSE groups are similar 377 
in sex distribution, this indicates that the variations in FC between PSE patients and HC were 378 
predominantly influenced by PSE type, the presence of PPR, and the stimulation frequency rather than sex 379 
differences. Finally, in the present study, the analyzed FC patterns were restricted only to responses to 380 
periodically flashing lights. In the future, functional network alterations could also be studied in reaction 381 
to a variety of visual triggers that better represent real -life environments, e.g., stimuli of periodically 382 
varying chromaticity or stimuli of differing field size.  383 
 384 
5. Conclusions  385 
 386 
Overall, the results of our retrospective study suggest t hat the  visual cortex  of PSE patients is 387 
hyperexcitable and remains significantly more active during higher stimulation frequencies of  the IPS 388 
compared to HC. Furthermore, we showed that PSE-type severity increases the magnitude of the PPR at 389 
occipital, parietal , and fr ontal electrodes. However, the hyperexcitability of the cortex is not the only 390 
determinant of PPR and PSE in general , as we did not observe increased activity in the occipital region of 391 
non-PSE patients compared to PSE patients. Instead, it appears that PPR relies on a hyperexcitable visual 392 
cortex, providing the basis for a local frequency -specific cortical overactivation and an altered brain 393 
network connectivity that propagates this overactivation in a type-specific manner. 394
