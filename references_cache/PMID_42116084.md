# Spondylo-ocular syndrome: xylosyltransferase 2 gene mutation and clinical observations-a case report.
**Authors:** Amini MJ, Hajishah H, Noorian S, Kazemi D
**Journal:** J Med Case Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13256-026-06106-8](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13256-026-06106-8)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Spondylo-ocular syndrome (SOS) is a rare genetic disorder marked by skeletal and ocular abnormalities, typically inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. Mutations in the XYLT2 gene, which encodes an enzyme critical for extracellular matrix formation, lead to systemic disease with multi-organ involvement. Clinical features include osteoporosis, multiple fractures, and visual impairments.

CASE PRESENTATION: We present a case of a 9-year-old Iranian girl from a consanguineous family diagnosed with SOS. Genetic testing revealed a homozygous missense mutation in the XYLT2 gene (c.1967A>G, p.Glu656Gly) through whole exome sequencing (WES). The patient had recurrent urinary tract infections in infancy and later developed hyperopia and esotropia strabismus. At age 5, she presented with multiple vertebral compression fractures after a minor injury, prompting referral to a pediatric endocrinologist who diagnosed osteoporosis. Although treated with bisphosphonates, she later sustained a left femoral shaft fracture requiring surgical stabilization with a titanium elastic nail. Despite worsening hyperopia and esotropia, ophthalmologic examination did not reveal cataracts or retinal detachment.

CONCLUSION: This case highlights the clinical spectrum of spondylo-ocular syndrome and the importance of recognizing its manifestations for effective management. Increased awareness among healthcare providers can enhance diagnostic accuracy and improve patient outcomes. Further research into the genotype-phenotype correlations in SOS may provide deeper insights into its pathophysiology and management strategies.

In 2001, a new genetic-related disorder, known as spondylo-ocular syndrome, was proposed, adding to the list of recognized genetic diseases [1]. A combination of ocular and spondylar symptoms reported in 6 children of a consanguineous family convinced the physicians to introduce a possible new syndrome [1]. Affected individuals commonly present with a diverse array of spinal and ocular symptoms, among which osteoporosis, bone fractures, spondylar deformities, cataracts, and retinal detachment stand out as the most prevalent manifestations [2]. Also, patients may suffer from other organ involvement causing symptoms like short stature, facial dysmorphism, genito-urinary manifestations, and hearing defects. This condition is exceptionally rare, with published reports documenting fewer than 40 affected individuals thus far [3]. In 2015, Munns et al. [4] identified mutations in the xylosyltransferase 2 (XT-II) gene as the primary etiological factor underlying this syndrome. XT-II, a protein belonging to the glycosyltransferase family, serves as the isoform responsible for initiating the biosynthesis of glycosaminoglycan chains in proteoglycans [5, 6]. In comparison, xylosyltransferase 1, responsible for the Baratela–Scott syndrome [7], catalyzes the first step in the biosynthesis of proteoglycans which is transferring xylose from uridine diphosphate (UDP)-xylose to a serine residue in a core protein [8]. Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), essential components of the extracellular matrix, are key to the growth and maintenance of bone, cartilage, skin, and other tissues [9, 10]. Proteoglycans are found throughout the body and serve various functions, contributing significantly to signaling pathways and cellular activities [11]. Dysregulation at any stage of proteoglycan synthesis can lead to a disorder affecting multiple systems. Thus far, a total of 24 reported cases have been documented with the syndrome, each presenting a diverse array of symptoms that can vary widely from one individual to another. The last known case has been a 23-year-old male from India reported in 2024 [12]. In this report, we present the case of a 9-year-old girl from Iran who has recently been diagnosed with spondylo-ocular syndrome. Our aim is to investigate potential novel genetic mutations and phenotypic variations associated with this condition, thereby enriching our understanding of the syndrome.

The patient is a 9-year-old Iranian girl from a consanguineous family, second-degree cousins (Fig. 1), evaluated at Imam Ali Hospital in Karaj, Iran. The anthropometry along with sexual maturity ratings are as follows: height: 128 cm, weight: 26 kg, body mass index (BMI): 15.87 kg/m2, head circumference: 52 cm, breast development: Tanner Stage II, pubic hair development: Tanner Stage I. She was born pre-term (35 weeks of gestation) with 2450 g weight, and 47 cm height. She is the second-born in the family and has an older brother who has not been identified with any notable genetic conditions. Although the father displayed no discernible genetic disorders, the mother was a case of beta thalassemia minor. Consequently, the daughter was also diagnosed with this hereditary condition. She did not exhibit any other abnormality at the age of birth. During the initial months after birth, the patient was diagnosed with recurrent urinary tract infections (UTI). To investigate the reason of recurrent UTI, further examination by a urologist consultant was performed, and it was determined that the genito-urinary distance was below the expected normal range. Despite this finding, the medical team did not recommend any invasive interventions and instead advised the parents to await the child's growth. Also, at the age of 4, she was diagnosed with a visual impairment (hyperopia), and esotropia strabismus.

At the age of 5, the patient first visited our medical center after experiencing back pain following a minor injury in the playground and sought medical attention in the emergency department of Imam Ali Hospital, Karaj, Iran. The pain had persisted for 24 hours and was localized to the thoracic and upper lumbar region. The patient denied any leg pain, numbness, or weakness. The patient's vital signs were within normal limits. She was alert, with no signs of fever or weight loss. Motor strength was normal in both lower extremities, and reflexes were intact with no pathological findings. Localized tenderness over the thoracic vertebrae was observed. Mild paraspinal muscle spasm noted on both sides of the lumbar region. The patient refused to walk due to back pain, and avoided any position that strains the back. Severe limitation of lumbar flexion, extension, and lateral flexion due to pain was observed.

Following a thorough examination of the MRI by a neurosurgeon, the patient was found to have multiple compression fractures in the vertebrae at T2, T4, T6, T8, and T12 without retropulsion of posterior elements (Fig. 2). A complete medical procedure for the compression fracture was done, and after additional assessment to explore potential child abuse, the patient was referred to a pediatric endocrine specialist for further examination.Fig. 2Magnetic resonance imaging showing vertebral collapse of spines

A dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA—GE lunar) bone density test was conducted, revealing osteoporosis in both spinal and femoral bones. As a result, a comprehensive evaluation was conducted to determine the underlying cause of osteoporosis in the child. The assessment included investigations for potential etiologies such as vitamin D deficiency, celiac disease, renal osteodystrophy, hyperparathyroidism, and leukemia. laboratory tests performed included 25-hydroxyvitamin d, parathyroid hormone (PTH), calcium, phosphorus, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, anti-tissue transglutaminase (Anti-tTG), creatinine, and a complete blood count (CBC). After ruling out these conditions based on the results of these investigations, the patient was provisionally diagnosed with an osteogenesis imperfecta-like disease. Treatment involved the administration of a bisphosphonate infusion (Amp Pamidronate 25 mg every 4 months). However, after few months, owing to the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic and the precautionary of parents to attend in hospital for infusion, the medication was changed to oral (Tab Alendronate 10 mg once daily). In light of atypical manifestations accompanied by osteoporosis at this particular age, a genetic examination was undertaken to explore potential involvement of intricate or uncommon syndromes. Whole exome sequencing (WES) was performed by Agiomix company through Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) using illumina® TruSeq library preparation. The libraries were sequenced to mean > 80-100X coverage on NovaSeq 6000 using 150 by 150 base pairs (bp) paired end read. The sequences obtained are aligned to human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19). The Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) Best Practices framework was followed for variant calling in the patient sample. Gene annotating of the variants was performed using ANNOVAR software.

A blood sample was taken in order to extract the deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA). Then, after isolating and sequencing the exons, the results were compared with the reference genome to identify genetic variants (whole exome sequencing test). As a result, a homozygous mutation in the XYLT2 gene (c.1967A > G, p.Glu656Gly [14], OMIM: 605822) was discovered (Fig. 3). This mutation is characterized as a missense alteration, involving a singular substitution of amino acid. Specifically, adenine was substituted with guanine at position 1967 of the gene, leading to an amino acid transformation from glutamic acid to glycine. Based on the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics [15] (ACMG), this mutation is classified as “likely pathogenic”. The variant qualifies for Pathogenic Very Strong 1 (PVS1), as it is a variant in a gene where loss of function is a recognized disease-causing mechanism. Additionally, it meets Pathogenic Moderate 2 (PM2) criteria due to its absence in control databases. Due to the abnormal result of WES indicating a potential syndrome known as Spondylo-ocular, the patient was referred to an ophthalmologist for further examination. Despite being diagnosed with a worsened previously diagnosed hyperopia (with a degree of: Oculus Dexter + 4.00–1.25*180, Oculus Sinister + 3.25–1.00* 180) and esotropia, no indications of cataract or retinal detachment were observed.Fig. 3Novel homozygous missense mutation in XYLT2: NC_000017.10 (NM_022167.4):c.1967A > G (p.Glu656Gly)

Herein, we discussed a 9-year-old Iranian girl possibly diagnosed with spondylo-ocular syndrome. The patient presented osteoporosis, vertebral compression fracture, long bone fracture (oblique fracture of femur), ocular pathology (hyperopia and esotropia), and a reduced genito-urinary distance at the infancy period. Also, a homozygous missense mutation of XYLT2 gene (c.1967A > G, p.Glu656Gly) was found. As stated before, XYLT2 is a gene located on long arm of chromosome 17, and encodes an enzyme called xylosyltransferase 2.

To date, only 11 papers including 36 patients have reported this syndrome [1–4, 16–21]. Initially, Schimdt et al. [1] introduced a possible new syndrome called spondylo-ocular in an Iraqi family affecting 6 children (out of 7). In a study conducted by Munns et al. [4] in 2015, it was demonstrated that a mutation in the XYLT2 gene is responsible for this syndrome. Subsequently, seven other papers reported this mutation along with manifestations of the syndrome. The most recent one is a study conducted by Misgar et al. [12] which presented a 23-year-old Indian male with a novel pathogenic frameshift variant of XYLT2 gene mutation. The symptoms of the mentioned case were as follows: repeated long bone fractures, congenital heart defects, bilateral corneal opacities, atrophic bulbi, short stature, and two novel findings: genu valgum and right-sided hydrocele.. Various types of mutations in the gene have been observed including nonsense (n = 3), six missense (n = 6), two frameshift duplication (n = 2), and four frameshift deletions (n = 4); however, similar to our study, the most prevalent type is a missense mutation [11, 16, 18–21]. A missense mutation is recognized as a single alteration in the DNA sequence, involving the replacement of one nucleotide with another, causing an amino acid transformation. In the case under examination in our research, there was a substitution of adenine with guanine at position 1967 of the gene, resulting in a change in the amino acid from glutamic acid to glycine. In contrast to our patient, all the other cases reported in previous studies were diagnosed with cataracts. However, our case presented other ocular symptoms, including hyperopia and esotropia. Other common symptoms seen in other cases were similar to our case, including bone fractures, vertebral compression fractures, and osteoporosis. Moreover, a relatively rare genito-urinary finding was observed in our study, similar to just four cases in previous studies. No hearing defects, intellectual disability, or cardiac manifestations were observed in this case, in contrast to some previous studies.

As shown in Table 2, SOS presents with a highly variable phenotype. While cataracts, retinal detachment, or cardiac defects are frequent in many published SOS cases, their absence in this patient may result from several contributing factors. First, the type of mutation can influence residual XYLT2 enzyme activity. Missense variants may permit partial function, thereby producing milder or delayed ophthalmologic or cardiologic phenotypes. Second, age at assessment is important. Certain ocular findings, such as cataracts, and cardiac manifestations may develop later in childhood or adolescence, and in a 9-year-old these features might not yet have emerged. Third, individual genetic background and modifier genes likely modulate phenotypic expressivity, altering when and how severely various organ systems are affected. Fourth, environmental or treatment factors, such as early detection, good nutritional status, or bisphosphonate therapy for bone disease, may mitigate systemic stress and delay or prevent damage in sensitive tissues like the lens or myocardium. To discuss the potential differential diagnoses of SOS, the three conditions that most closely share its clinical manifestations are osteogenesis imperfecta (OI), hypophosphatasia (HPP), and achondroplasia. The bone manifestations of SOS and OI are somewhat similar, particularly in terms of bone fragility and recurrent fractures. However, OI can be distinguished by its characteristic extraskeletal features, including blue sclerae, dentinogenesis imperfecta, and hearing loss, as well as the presence of pathogenic variants in COL1A1 or COL1A2 [22]. HPP may share similar skeletal features with SOS; however, it is characterized by low serum alkaline phosphatase activity, premature loss of teeth, and Alkaline Phosphatase, Liver/Bone/Kidney (ALPL) gene mutations, which are not observed in SOS [23]. On the other hand, achondroplasia is characterized by rhizomelic limb shortening, frontal bossing, and FGFR3 mutations leading to impaired endochondral ossification—features that are not seen in SOS [24].

To date, all the treatments administered to patients with this syndrome are symptomatic, focusing on bone health, fracture stabilization, and ophthalmologic support. Bisphosphonates, both intravenous (IV) and oral, have been used to improve bone mineral density, while orthopedic and ophthalmologic interventions address skeletal and ocular complications. Although no SOS-specific data on alendronate is available, oral alendronate has been reported as a practical alternative in other bone fragility disorders when intravenous bisphosphonate therapy is not feasible. In the present case, the COVID-19 pandemic and parental reluctance to pursue hospital-based intravenous treatment made oral alendronate a more convenient and accessible option. Moreover, the strong response to bisphosphonates made it unnecessary to prescribe potential alternative therapies (for example, teriparatide, denosumab). Depending on each case’s specific manifestations, physicians have suggested different therapies. For example, in this reported case, bisphosphonates (oral and IV) were administered for osteoporosis, orthopedic treatment for spinal fractures, and visual aids for ocular manifestations. Also, genetic counseling was recommended for the entire family to prevent the disease in future pregnancies. However, our study encountered some limitations that are important to mention. As we know, this mutation is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. It would be beneficial to fully examine the genetic sequence of the entire family. Unfortunately, we were unable to do so due to financial and insurance issues. Secondly, given that the diagnosis of this case coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, the medical team faced challenges in thoroughly monitoring the progression of disease manifestations. On the other hand, to elucidate the strengths of our study, it is pertinent to point out that, due to the early diagnosis of the disease, we are able to follow up with the patient, monitor all upcoming symptoms, and try different therapeutic regimens. Furthermore, we believe that the specific clinical manifestations observed in our patient—notably esotropia and low anogenital distance—offer valuable insights that expand the known features of spondylo-ocular syndrome. Characterization of this novel mutation and its corresponding phenotype may not only enhance diagnostic capabilities but also inform future treatment considerations by refining the understanding of disease progression in patients with specific genotypic variations.
