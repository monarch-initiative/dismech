# Resolving Hexose-Phosphates by LC-MS Leads to New Insights in PGM1-CDG Pathophysiology.
**Authors:** Driesen K, De Craemer S, Morava E, Cassiman D, Witters P, Ghesquière B
**Journal:** ACS Omega (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1021/acsomega.5c07074](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsomega.5c07074)

## Content

Hexose-phosphates play a role in many metabolic pathways, such as glycolysis and glycosylation. To understand the molecular basis of diseases such as congenital disorders of glycosylation (CDG), information about the source and abundance of hexose-phosphates is imperative. Mass spectrometry (MS)-based tracer metabolomics can provide this information, but hexose-phosphates are structural isomers with similar physicochemical properties, which makes them difficult to differentiate using MS. Here, we present and compare two optimized liquid-chromatography-based MS methods for the identification of relevant hexose-phosphates, compatible with tracer metabolomics. A combination of these two methods led to the analysis of eight hexose-monophosphates and two hexose-bisphosphates that can occur in humans. Both methods displayed linearity in 3 to 4 orders of magnitude, with limits of quantification between 0.5 and 50 nM, which is well within the cellular concentration range. The applicability of these methods to biological models was then proven in a study of the effect of galactose treatment in phosphoglucomutase 1 (PGM1)-CDG fibroblasts. Here, we show, for the first time, the hexose-phosphate profiles in CDG and how these change upon treatment.

Glycosylation is one of the most essential
post-translational modifications,
involving the attachment of sugar chains (glycans) to proteins or
lipids. Dysfunction in any of the enzymes responsible for these steps
can lead to severe clinical conditions classified as congenital disorders
of glycosylation (CDG). To date, over
200 types of CDG have been identified, including 18 subtypes categorized
as disorders of monosaccharide synthesis and interconversion, where
hexose-phosphates play a crucial role. A representative example of
this group is phosphoglucomutase 1 (PGM1)-CDG (OMIM #614921), a disorder
caused by defects in the enzyme that catalyzes the interconversion
of glucose-6-phosphate (Glc-6-P) and glucose-1-phosphate (Glc-1-P).


Historically, CDG research primarily focused
on the final step
of glycosylation, examining incomplete or missing sugar chains on
major glycoproteins such as intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1)
and transferrin.
,
 However, investigating sugar–phosphate
metabolism is equally critical to understanding the molecular basis
of these disorders. Hexose-phosphates not only serve as central intermediates
in metabolic pathways such as glycolysis and gluconeogenesis but also
provide the precursors (sugar nucleotides) necessary for glycan biosynthesis.
The key hexose-phosphates present in central carbon metabolism and
glycan biosynthesis are Glc-6-P, Glc-1-P, fructose-6-phosphate (Frc-6-P),
fructose-1-phosphate (Frc-1-P), galactose-1-phosphate (Gal-1-P), galactose-6-phosphate
(Gal-6-P), mannose-1-phosphate (Man-1-P), mannose-6-phosphate (Man-6-P),
fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (Frc-1,6-BiP), and glucose-1,6-bisphosphate
(Glc-1,6-BiP). Mapping the regulation of these sugar–phosphates
in specific biological contexts is therefore essential for uncovering
underlying biochemical disturbances and identifying potential therapeutic
interventions. A key example of such an intervention is the oral administration
of galactose in PGM1-CDG patients.
−




In PGM1-CDG, the
mechanism of action behind galactose supplementation
was previously explored using (tracer) metabolomics by Radenkovic
et al. However, the study missed detailed
insights into the contribution of galactose to hexose-phosphate metabolism,
primarily due to the absence of a compatible analytical method. Indeed,
hexose-phosphates exist as structural isomers with highly similar
chemical properties, making their differentiation via mass spectrometry
(MS) particularly challenging. Even tandem MS fragmentation patterns
do not fully allow to distinguish common hexose-monophosphates, as
their fragmentation patterns are often quite similar; thus, hyphenation with a selective chromatographic method
is required to cover a wide range of sugar phosphates. Previous CDG
studies have relied on two primary approaches:
,−


 (i) targeted analysis of specific hexose-monophosphates, which provides
limited insight beyond expected metabolic disruptions; and (ii) gas
chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS), which poses technical
difficulties for the application of tracer metabolomics. Indeed, electron
ionization (EI)the standard ionization technique used in GC-MScauses
extensive fragmentation, leading to the loss of the entire carbon
backbone of hexose-phosphates. Consequently, this prevents accurate
tracking of stable isotope-labeled metabolites, which is key to understanding
the molecular path of nutrients. While
softer ionization techniques for GC-MS exist, common silylation strategies
to allow GC-MS analysis of nonvolatile metabolites result in very
low yields for hexose-1-phosphates such as Man-1-P.
,



LC-MS methods are an interesting alternative as they usually
rely
on the soft electrospray ionization technique and do not require silylation.
Existing strategies for LC-MS analysis of hexose-phosphates include
enantioselective columns, reversed-phase
chromatography after derivatization,
,
 hydrophilic
interaction liquid chromatography, ion
exchange chromatography, ion pairing, or a combination.
,
 However, most
of these studies only considered a limited set of hexose-phosphates
of interest, and none of the described methods could effectively detect
Man-1-P, a hexose-phosphate that plays a major role in glycosylation.
Interestingly, Lane et al. described an alternative approach in which
they used differential mobility spectrometry to analyze five hexose-phosphates,
including Man-1-P, but were unable to separate these five compounds
in a mixture.


We improved two of
these LC-MS methods
,
 to enable the separation of eight
biologically relevant hexose-phosphates,
including Man-1-P, and two hexose-bisphosphates. By implementing these
highly selective LC-MS techniques, we provide a robust and sensitive
framework for studying hexose-phosphate metabolism. This method, combined
with 13C-labeled glucose and galactose tracing, allows
us to investigate the mode of action of galactose supplementation
in PGM1-CDG, ultimately paving the way for improved diagnostic and
therapeutic strategies in CDG (and beyond) research.

Five control and six PGM1-CDG
fibroblasts (P1 and P3–P7) were maintained at a maximum of
23 passages and passaged at around 80% confluency. The cells were
cultured at 37 °C at 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator.
The medium used was DMEM (Gibco) containing 5.5 mM glucose, 1 mM sodium
pyruvate, and 2 mM glutamine and supplemented with 10% fetal bovine
serum (FBS, Gibco).

Fibroblasts from controls and patients were plated at 50,000 cells
per well in a 6-well plate. After 24 h, the cells were rinsed with
PBS, and the medium was replaced with tracer medium, which consists
of depleted DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 2 mM glutamine, 10% dialyzed
FBS (Gibco), and (i) 5.5 mM 12C6 glucose, (ii)
5.5 mM 13C6 glucose, (iii) 5.5 mM 13C6 glucose, and 2 mM 12C6 galactose,
or (iv) 5.5 mM 12C6 glucose and 2 mM 13C6 galactose. After 48 h of treatment, at approximately
80% confluency, the metabolites were extracted. For this, the medium
was removed, the cells were rinsed with cold 0.9% NaCl before the
plates were transferred to ice, and 250 μL of ice-cold extraction
buffer (80% methanol (VWR) with five internal standards: 1 μM
D5
L-tryptophan, 3 μM 13C5-D5-15N glutamic acid, 3 μM D7-15N4 arginine, 5 μM D7 glucose, and 1 μM D27 myristic acid) was added.
Then, the cells were scraped, and the resulting cell extract with
precipitate was transferred to 1.5 mL tubes and stored overnight at
−80 °C. After centrifugation (17,000 g, 15 min, 4 °C), the supernatant was either used as such (IP-RP-LC-MS)
or further derivatized (RP-LC-MS), and the protein pellet was used
for quantification for normalization through the bicinchoninic acid
assay. Statistical analysis (student’s t test)
was performed in R and GraphPad Prism. Patients and controls were
compared with unpaired t-tests, and the effect of
galactose treatment was assessed by ratio paired t-tests.

The
samples were derivatized using a modified version of the methoximation
and propionylation method described by Rende et al. First, 100 μL of extracts from fibroblasts was dried,
resuspended in 25 μL of 2% methoxamine in pyridine, and incubated
for 30 min at 60 °C. This reaction was allowed to continue overnight
at room temperature. The next day, 6 μL of 1-methylimidazole
and 12 μL of propionic anhydride were added and incubated for
30 min at 37 °C. The resulting mixture was dried and redissolved
in 100 μL of 0.1% formic acid (VWR).

Mass spectrometry
measurements were performed using a Vanquish LC System (Thermo Scientific)
coupled via heated electrospray ionization to a Q Exactive Orbitrap
Focus mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) operated in negative mode.
Ten μL of sample was injected onto a C18 BEH column (ACQUITY
UPLC BEH C18 Column, 130Å, 1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 150 mm,
1/pk). A linear gradient was carried out, starting with 95% solvent
A (2% formic acid) and 5% solvent B (2% formic acid in acetonitrile).
From 2 to 7 min, the gradient changed to 25% B. The gradient was kept
on 25% B for 7 min and followed by a change to 30% B. This gradient
was again kept for 11.5 min before increasing to 90% B from 26 to
33 min. After maintaining this gradient for 1 min, the gradient dropped
again to 5% B over a course of 2 min. Chromatography was stopped at
41 min. The flow was kept constant at a flow rate of 0.25 mL/min.
The column’s temperature was kept constant at 40 °C. The
mass spectrometer operated in full scan (range [250.0000–1050.0000])
and negative mode using a spray voltage of 3.2 kV, capillary temperature
of 320 °C, sheath gas at 45 °C, and auxiliary gas at 15.
The AGC target was set at 3.0 × 10006 using a resolution
of 35,000 and 3 microscans. Data collection was performed using Xcalibur
software (Thermo Scientific).

This method was adapted
from Lu et al. First, 10 μL or
5 μL of each fibroblast extract was loaded into a Dionex UltiMate
3000 LC System (Thermo Scientific Bremen, Germany) equipped with a
C18 column (Acquity UPLC -HSS T3 1.8 μm; 2.1 × 150 mm,
Waters) coupled to a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo
Scientific) operating in negative ion mode. A step gradient was carried
out using solvent A (95% 10 mM tributylamine (TBA) and 15 mM acetic
acid in water and 5% methanol) and solvent B (100% methanol). The
gradient started with 100% solvent A and remained at 0% B until 13
min postinjection. A linear gradient to 33.3% B was carried out until
14 min and increased to 36.4% until 25 min. Between 25 and 26 min,
the gradient increased to 90.9% of B and remained at 90.9% B for 4
min. At 30 min, the gradient returned to 0% B. Chromatography was
stopped at 40 min. The flow was kept constant at 0.25 mL/min, and
the column was placed at 40 °C throughout the analysis. The MS
operated in full scan mode (m/z range:
[200.0000–800.0000]) using a spray voltage of 4.80 kV, a capillary
temperature of 300 °C, sheath gas at 40.0,°C, and auxiliary
gas at 10.0 °C. The AGC target was set at 3.0 × 10006 using a resolution of 35,000 and 3 microscans, with a maximum IT
fill time of 128 ms. Data collection was performed using Xcalibur
software (Thermo Scientific).

We modified an existing method
for separating glycolytic intermediates, including three hexose-phosphates,
after methoximation and propionylation. This derivatization facilitates separation since methoxamine only
reacts with compounds carrying a free carbonyl group, such as the
open-chain forms hexose-6-phosphates and Frc-1-P, and Frc-1,6-biP,
leading to derivatives that differ in mass for other mono- or bisphosphates,
respectively.


The most important
changes that were made to the original method (chromatographic separation
of the hexose-phosphates using the original method is shown in Figure S1) were (i) changing the 5 cm HSS to
15 cm BEH columns, the longer length enhances selectivity for hexose-phosphate
separation, and the BEH particle type enhances column lifetime when
employing 2% formic acid (FA) and (ii) switching mobile phase B from
methanol (MeOH) to acetonitrile (ACN). ACN is a stronger and more
commonly used eluent in reversed-phase (RP) chromatography and resulted
in a substantial increase in the amount of (partially) separated peaks,
even with lower concentrations of FA (0.1% instead of 2% in MeOH).
Raising the concentration of FA in ACN to 2% further improved peak
separation for hexose-6-phosphates and is required to separate Frc-6-P
from the tailing of preceding peaks (Figure S2).

The final optimized method allowed for the selective analysis
of
Frc-6-P, Gal-1-P, Gal-6-P, Glc-6-P, Man-1-P, and Man-6-P (Figure 
). The hexose-6-phosphates
appear as two peaks because they exist as both α- and β-anomers.
Most of these analytes were chromatographically separated from other
hexose-phosphates and have a different mass than various inositol-phosphates
following the propionylation step. Albeit beyond the scope of this
study, this could enable the study of different inositol-phosphate
species in the presence of (more abundant) hexose-phosphates as well.

The only unresolved hexose-phosphates are Glc-1-P
and Frc-1-P that
coelute (Figure S3). However, the method
showed 10 times higher sensitivity for Glc-1-P based on the standards.
This implies that Frc-1-P interference would only be significant in

the original method is capable of separating
these from each other and other hexose-phosphates (Figure S1). Additionally, hexose-bisphosphates displayed poor
and inconsistent recovery during derivatization, which is further
discussed in the next section, rendering them incompatible with this
method.

In conclusion, this first optimized method combines
a two-step
derivatization with RP-LC-MS and allows for the analysis of six out
of the eight hexose-monophosphates of interest, including Man-1-P.
When Frc-1-P concentrations are similar to or lower than Glc-1-P,
it is also able to quantify the latter with little or no interference,
bringing the total to seven out of eight. To the authors’ knowledge,
this is the first method that manages to separate Man-1-P in real
samples using a chromatography-MS-based method. Downsides of this
method are that Frc-1-P could not be detected interference-free and that the hexose-bisphosphates are not compatible
with the derivatization. Therefore, we set out to optimize a second
method that does not require derivatization to accurately detect the
remaining compounds of interest.

The second methodthat does
not require derivatizationwas based on a routine method covering
several metabolite classes, including glycolytic and TCA intermediates,
and some amino acids. A comprehensive
list of metabolites that can be measured with the unadapted method can be found as the Supporting Information to this paper. Additional metabolites of interest
can be checked through references to the original paper. The gradient was optimized for separation of
isobaric hexose-phosphates and inositol-phosphates, followed by hexose-bisphosphates.
Since early-eluting compounds displayed reduced chromatographic resolution,
caused by peak fronting for the earlier-eluting hexose-phosphates,
due to the apolarity of the sample extraction solvent (80% MeOH) relative
to the chromatographic starting conditions (5% MeOH), we tested different
injection volumes and the resuspension of standard mixtures in 5%
MeOH. This showed that a 5 μL injection offers superior chromatographic
separation of Gal-6-P, Glc-6-P, and Glc-1-P compared to a 10 μL
injection (Figure S4), although it is inferior
to the separation of the reversed-phase method. Meanwhile, a 10 μL
injection improves the sensitivity for hexose-bisphosphates. SpeedVac
drying at 30 °C and resuspension in 5% MeOH and subsequent 10
μL injection did not improve sensitivity for hexose-phosphates
but revealed a noticeable reduction in detected hexose-bisphosphates
(Figure S5). We did not investigate this
further, but the loss of bisphosphates might depend on temperature
settings and the time the samples are left in the SpeedVac.

In conclusion, the IP-RP-LC-MS method provides an interference-free
separation of Frc-1-P and Glc-1-P, as well as for Frc-1,6-biP and
Glc-1,6-biP, which makes it complementary to the RP-LC-MS method (Figure

A series of standards (0.5 or 1 nM
to 2, 5, or 10 μM) was used to determine linearity. Table 
 shows the linear
range for each hexose-phosphate for their compatible method(s). Blank
signals on Orbitrap MS instruments are 0 for most of these compounds.
For these, the limit of quantification (LOQ), noted in Table 
 as the lower limit of linearity,
was the lowest standard concentration that was on the linear curve
generated by higher concentration standards. This lowest linear standard
was usually the first standard that produced a peak with signals consistently
above the Orbitrap noise cutoff within a reasonable retention time
range, allowing it to be recorded in the data file generated by the
measurement. As the hexose-bisphosphates did have a blank signal above
the Orbitrap noise cutoff, their LOQ was calculated as the average
plus 10 times the standard deviation of the blank. The highest standard
is taken as the upper limit of linearity, as none of the calibration
slopes started decreasing at these concentrations. This means that
for RP-LC-MS, we show linearity over at least 3 orders of magnitude,
with correlation coefficients above 0.99 and LOQs between 0.5 and
2 nM (Table 
, Figure S6). For IP-RP-LC-MS, linearity is observed
over 2 orders of magnitude for the hexose-monophosphates and 4 for
the hexose-bisphosphates, with LOQs between 6.7 and 50 nM and with
correlation coefficients over 0.99 (Table 
, Figure S7).

Overall, RP-LC-MS is more sensitive for hexose-phosphates
that
can be detected with both methods, except for Frc-1-P. For IP-RP-LC-MS,
the preferred approach for bisphosphates and Frc-1-P is a 10 μL
injection without resuspension. A variation using a 5 μL injection
is less sensitive for these compounds but allows chromatographic separation
of Glc-1-P and partial separation of Glc-6-P and Gal-6-P. This method
is easier to implement as it does not require derivatization, although
it requires a dedicated LC-MS setup as the ion pairing agent TBA suppresses
positive ionization mode and is hard or impossible to fully remove.

The variation was determined within triplicate extracts of the
same cell line, seeded and processed separately, and then quantified
using an external calibration curve. The minimum, median, and maximum
concentrations in the samples used for this are shown in Table 
. These results reinforce
the relevance of the linear ranges of the different compounds in biological
samples.

Of note, both the RP-LC-MS and IP-RP-LC-MS methods
resolved, at
least partially, closely eluting interferences, respectively near
Frc-6-P and near Glc-1,6-biP. These interfering compounds incorporate
up to six nutrient carbons from glucose and galactose, suggesting
they are likely additional hexose-monophosphates and bisphosphates,
respectively (Figures S8 and S9). The interfering
bisphosphate in question could be Frc-2,6-biP, another bisphosphate
that is present in human metabolism. This
could not be verified since no suitable standard was available. In
methods optimized for and tested on a less diverse panel of (bis)­phosphates,
these compounds are more likely to coelute with targeted hexose (bis)­phosphates.

the presented methods, we decided to build upon previous work from
our group, in which the pathomechanism of PGM1-CDG and the mode of
action of galactose treatment on N-glycosylation were elucidated using
(tracer) metabolomics. We used our new
methods to sharpen our research focus on the disturbances in hexose-phosphate
levels in PGM1-CDG fibroblasts (Table S1) and the effect and contribution of galactose supplementation. The
results are summarized in Figure

Interestingly, we found that Glc-1-P was depleted
1.5-fold (p = 0.01) and that Glc-6-P was accumulating
(2-fold, p = 0.01) in PGM1-CDG fibroblasts, both
of which returned
to levels similar to the controls upon galactose treatment (p < 0.05 for both). Of note, this is the expected effect
of a PGM1 disorder, in which the conversion from Glc-6-P to Glc-1-P
is disturbed. Notably, approximately 80% of Glc-1-P originated from
galactose, via the Gal-1-P → UDP-galactose → UDP-glucose
pathway. This indicates that galactose not only supplements UDP-glucose
to support glycosylation restoration but also directly replenishes
metabolites downstream of PGM1. We detected no Frc-1-P with the IP-RP-LC-MS
method, so we can assume the Glc-1-P detected with the RP-LC-MS method
is interference-free. Similarly to what was described before, we found lower Gal-1-P levels (0.6-fold of controls
on glucose, p = 0.003, for the comparison of Gal-1-P
without galactose, see Figure S10) in PGM1-CDG
skin-derived fibroblasts that increased dramatically upon galactose
treatment (50-fold increase relative to controls on glucose, p < 0.0001). While this was anticipated, we are the first
to confirm, using 13C-galactose tracing, that Gal-1-P is
derived exclusively from galactose. Additionally, we observed the
formation of Gal-6-P upon galactose treatmenta noteworthy
finding that would not have been possible with a less sensitive method.
Though Gal-6-P spontaneously forms in certain bacteria upon lactose
ingestion, humans lack the enzymes for this alternate lactose metabolism
pathway. Reports of Gal-6-P in eukaryotes are rare, though one study did detect it in erythrocytes of galactosemic
patients following galactose ingestion. There is evidence that PGM1 can catalyze the conversion of Gal-1-P
to Gal-6-P, although this happens at a slow rate. This hypothesis is supported by our results, in which we
find lower levels of Gal-6-P in PGM1-CDG fibroblasts than the controls
(p = 0.001), and two out of six patients showed levels
below the detection limit. Frc-6-P shows similar relative abundances
for patients and controls (p = 0.21). Galactose does
not contribute to Frc-6-P biosynthesis and even slightly decreases
the levels of Frc-6-P in controls and patients (p < 0.05 for both). Similarly, Frc-6-P’s downstream metabolites
Man-1-P and Man-6-P displayed higher relative abundances in the patients
than in the controls (1.6- and 2-fold, p = 0.02 and p = 0.02, respectively), which decreased upon galactose
treatment (p < 0.05 for all). The accumulation
in patients is likely caused by the glycosylation deficiency, and
the subsequent decrease could point toward improved glycosylation.
The most striking effect was a four-fold decrease in Man-1-P levels
in control cells following galactose supplementation (p = 0.02), which could help explain why galactose treatment is ineffective
in PMM2-CDG. PMM2-CDG is characterized
by disturbances in mannose metabolism, and the disease is possibly
aggravated by a further loss of Man-1-P, which plays an important
role in glycosylation.

In addition to the hexose-monophosphates,
the hexose-bisphosphates
were also examined. Frc-1,6-biP behaved differently among the 6 PGM1-CDG
patients. Three of them displayed very high levels of Frc-1,6-biP,
whereas the other three grouped together with the controls (p = 0.1). These levels were slightly lower upon galactose
treatment (p = 0.02). Glc-1,6-biP, the intermediate
product in the interconversion between Glc-6-P and Glc-1-P that is
catalyzed by PGM1, was not detected in these samples.

Deciphering the pathomechanisms and treatment
mechanisms of congenital
disorders of glycosylation (CDG)and inborn errors of metabolism
more broadlycan pave the way for novel therapeutic strategies
for these often-untreatable diseases. Tracer metabolomics is a powerful
approach for this purpose as it not only reveals changes in metabolite
abundances but also identifies the nutrient sources fueling specific
metabolic pathways.

In this study, we introduced two optimized
LC-MS methods for detecting
hexose-phosphates that are fully compatible with tracer metabolomics.
Both methods effectively eliminate interferences that could compromise
data accuracyinterferences that less selective chromatographic
techniques might fail to detect. Avoiding these confounding factors
is critical for drawing reliable conclusions about metabolic flux.

These refined methodologies enabled us to comprehensively investigate
the hexose-phosphates that are crucial for glycan biosynthesis, now
with an added layer of information detailing the nutrient origins
of these sugars. Our findings reinforce previous observations and
provide new insights into the mode of action of galactose treatment
in PGM1-CDG. In conclusion, PGM1-CDG causes major disturbances in
hexose-phosphate metabolism, and we could, for the first time, show
that galactose could correct most of these disturbances. The methods
described are not only interesting in the context of CDG but could
also be used in a broader sense, for example, in cancer research,
where glycolysis and glycosylation are known to be altered.
