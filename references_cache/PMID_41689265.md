# Expanding Phenotype of GINS1 Deficiency: A Case Report and Review of the Literature.
**Authors:** Mackley MP, Brager R, Geddie H, Breakey V, Hough R, Stavropoulos DJ, McNiven V
**Journal:** Clin Genet (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/cge.70147](https://doi.org/10.1111/cge.70147)

## Content

Pathogenic variants in GINS1 are believed to cause a primary combined immunodeficiency and growth retardation syndrome with natural killer cell deficiency and chronic neutropenia. To date, however, very few cases have been reported. Thus, the role of GINS1 in disease, as well as the spectrum of variants and their associated phenotype, remains unclear. We present a 2-year-old female with growth retardation, chronic neutropenia, distinctive facial features, and glaucoma. Exome sequencing revealed two likely pathogenic variants in GINS1, c.-48C>G p.? and c.247C>T p.Arg83Cys, conferring a diagnosis of GINS1 deficiency. She has overlapping features with the previously reported individuals, cementing growth retardation, neutropenia, and natural killer cell deficiency as core features. We additionally present a review of all nine individuals reported to date. We highlight that our proband, unlike the others, has no history of infections, and that glaucoma has now been observed in multiple unrelated individuals, pointing toward possible phenotypic expansion. Efforts to identify affected individuals, including those with different variants and phenotypes, are needed to understand ways in which GINS1 may be implicated in disease and the phenotypic spectrum of this ultrarare inborn error of immunity.

GINS1 deficiency is an ultrarare combined primary immunodeficiency and growth retardation syndrome. It is characterized by natural killer (NK) cell deficiency, chronic neutropenia, as well as pre‐ and post‐natal growth retardation [1]. The syndrome appears to be caused by biallelic loss‐of‐function variants in the GINS1 gene. This gene encodes a component of the GINS complex, which acts as a replicative helicase, playing an essential role in DNA replication and cell cycle progression [1, 2, 3]. Homozygous knockouts of genes in the GINS complex are embryonically lethal in mice, highlighting their essential functions, which are being increasingly linked to human disease [3]. Reported disease‐causing variants have all been splicing or missense variants with some residual activity, likely contributing to the survival of affected individuals [1].

To date, only eight individuals from six families have been reported with GINS1 deficiency: five individuals were described by Cottineau et al. [1], two of whom were previously described [4, 5], an additional individual was reported in a cohort with short stature by Homma et al. [6], and two siblings were reported in a cohort with congenital neutropenia by Marti et al. [7] In addition to the growth and immune phenotypes, other features that were highlighted include mild facial dysmorphisms, eczema, and recurrent infections [1]. However, given the small number of published cases, the phenotypic spectrum of GINS1 deficiency remains unclear. This challenges our ability to identify affected individuals, counsel families, and guide management.

In recent years, countless inborn errors of immunity (IEI) have been described, facilitating tailored counseling, management plans, and paving the way for therapies [8, 9, 10]. Even among IEI, cases of GINS1 deficiency remain extremely rare. Furthermore, interpretations of implicated variants remain conflicting, demonstrating a lack of consensus around the role of GINS1 in disease. We describe a Canadian female child with growth failure, neutropenia, and reduced NK cells, diagnosed with GINS1 deficiency. This additional case adds strength to the role of GINS1 variants in human disease and provides a possible expansion of the related phenotype, which can guide counseling and management.

Written, informed consent for publication was obtained from the individual's parent. As only one individual is presented, approval from the institutional Research Ethics Board was not obtained. Phenotyping was performed through direct assessment by authors (V.M., R.B., H.G., V.B.) and review of medical records. PHELIX reporting guideline [11] for descriptions of ultrarare genetic conditions was followed (Supporting Information S1). Duo exome sequencing (ES) was performed at the Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto, Ontario, Canada (See Supporting Information S2 for details).

The proband was born at 36 weeks and 3 days of gestation via emergency cesarean section due to a non‐reassuring fetal heart tracing to a 31‐year‐old primigravida mother. The pregnancy was complicated by symmetric fetal growth restriction and oligohydramnios.

Birth growth parameters (with gestational age‐corrected Z‐scores) were as follows: weight, 1780 g (Z‐score −2.3); length, 43 cm (Z‐score −1.6); and head circumference, 30.5 cm (Z‐score −1.4). She was found to be persistently neutropenic, with a negative septic workup: her absolute neutrophil count at birth was 2.3 × 109/L (normal = 1.5–8.0), dropping to 0.3 at 2 weeks of age and fluctuating between 0.3 and 1.3 over the following 2 years. She had multiple small ventricular septal defects, which resolved spontaneously, and unilateral congenital glaucoma, causing secondary high myopia. She had bilateral equinovarus deformities, determined to be positional, without casting required. Plain radiographs of her hips at 2 years of age showed bilateral hip dislocations with marked bilateral hip dysplasia, hypoplastic and underdeveloped acetabula, and no ossification of the proximal femoral epiphyses. Apart from motor impairment seemingly related to her hip dislocations, she had normal development.

She was referred for genetics evaluation at 2 years of age, where physical examination revealed a distinctive appearance, including a triangular face with delicate and fine facial features, low‐set small and appropriately formed ears, a high and prominent forehead, infra‐orbital creases with long eyelashes, an unremarkable nose with wide nasal bridge, full cheeks, thick vermillion border, and mild micrognathia. She had postnatal growth retardation despite efforts to augment weight gain: weight, 6.4 kg (Z‐score −6); height, 68.7 cm (Z‐score −6.17); and head circumference, 47.2 cm (33rd centile). Family history was noncontributory. Further clinical information can be found in Supporting Information S3.

ES revealed two heterozygous variants in GINS1. The first, c.247C>T p.Arg83Cys (NM_021067.5), was maternally inherited and reported as likely pathogenic using the ACMG criteria (PP1, PP3, and PS3) [12]. It has been previously reported in a compound heterozygous state in unrelated individuals with growth deficiency and deficiency of NK cells and neutrophils [1, 6]. This variant was reported to be in trans with the other detected variant, c.‐48C>G (p.?), in four affected individuals in three unrelated families and has been shown to co‐segregate with disease (PP1) [1, 7]. This variant is observed at an allele frequency of 0.066% in population controls in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD), with a maximum allele frequency of 0.4% in the European (Finnish) population. In silico prediction programs predict this variant to impact protein function (PP3). Importantly, functional studies on patient‐derived fibroblasts and transformed cells suggest this variant has a deleterious effect on protein function (PS3) [1].

The second variant, c.‐48C>G p.? (NM_021067.5), is of unknown inheritance (not maternal), and reported as likely pathogenic (PM2, PP1, PS3). This is a noncoding variant in the 5′‐untranslated region of GINS1. In silico prediction programs predict that this variant may create a new cryptic donor splice site. Functional studies suggest this variant causes aberrant splicing, which results in a deletion in the Exon 1 coding sequence (PS3) [1]. As above, this variant was reported to be in trans with the p.Arg83Cys variant in multiple individuals and to co‐segregate with disease (PP1) [1, 7]. This variant is observed at a low frequency (0.00039%) in gnomAD (PM2). This variant was not detected in her mother (in blood) and is therefore suspected to be in trans with the other variant. However, as the father was unavailable for testing, we are unable to confirm whether this variant was inherited or de novo. Although the phasing is not certain, given the overlap with previous cases, these two variants were interpreted as being most likely in trans and therefore causative with respect to the individual's growth failure and hematological phenotype. This conferred a diagnosis of autosomal recessive GINS1 deficiency (also called immunodeficiency‐55; OMIM: 617827).

Subsequent immune phenotyping identified low NK cells. Her absolute CD3− CD16+ CD56+ count was found to be 30 × 106/L (reference range: 130–740). Her CD3+ count was 222 × 106/L and her CD4+ count was 980 × 106/L. Immunoglobulins were as follows: IgG 10.58 g/L (normal), IgM 0.29 g/L (normal), IgA 2.19 g/L (high). These results are in keeping with the expected immune profile of a deficiency of GINS1 [13]. Notably, the proband had a robust vaccine response and, at the time of reporting, had not yet had any significant infections.

This report describes a novel case of GINS1 deficiency in a 2‐year‐old female who is unrelated to the scarce individuals previously described and represents the first detailed report of the disorder since Cottineau et al. [1] demonstrated the disease‐causing role of inherited GINS1 deficiency. She shares several features with previously reported individuals [1, 7], including prenatal and postnatal growth failure, chronic neutropenia, and reduced NK cells (Table 1), cementing them as key characteristics of the syndrome. While all reported cases have growth failure, her predominantly postnatal growth failure is profound, with only one previously reported individual having comparable parameters [1]. Consideration was given to initiating growth hormone therapy; however, it was felt that potential risks may outweigh the unknown potential benefit. Finally, the facial features seen in our proband are similar to those of the first siblings photographed by Bernard et al. [4]: they all have delicate triangular facies with small chins, full lower lips, and high foreheads.

Importantly, our proband has features that warrant consideration as part of the expanding GINS1 phenotype. First, our proband was subsequently diagnosed with glaucoma, causing severe myopia. Notably, one individual described by Cottineau et al. [1] and Marti et al. [7] also had glaucoma, yet not highlighted as a component of the syndrome. Glaucoma has been documented in primary immunodeficiencies, although not at a high rate (4%) [14]. With glaucoma reported in three of nine cases of GINS1 deficiency (33%), ophthalmological assessment and monitoring for individuals who receive this diagnosis will be essential. Additionally, our proband had no recurrent or severe infections, contrasting with most published cases. Importantly, Cottineau et al. [1] demonstrated that residual GINS1/CMG complex activity did not segregate with immune phenotype—all patients developed significant infections. We therefore do not attribute our proband's lack of infections to differing GINS1 activity. Age‐related penetrance also seems an unlikely complete explanation, as a single other individual had no infections and is the oldest reported case (Table 1) [7]. Instead, it seems more likely to represent the well‐described phenomenon in IEI whereby individuals with identical, fully penetrant cellular phenotypes can show divergent infectious courses, influenced by stochastic pathogen exposure, genetic modifiers, and environmental factors. This highlights that, for individuals with GINS1 deficiency, recurrent and severe infections may not be universally present.

Our proband has features that remain unique to her, including congenital cardiac and musculoskeletal anomalies (Table 1). While these findings may be multifactorial or have an alternative etiology, the possibility that they are related to her syndrome remains. Although they did not share the same osseous deformities, the siblings described by Bernard et al. [4] had diffuse osteopenia, metaphyseal splaying of the long bones, and round epiphyses with delayed bone age. Careful musculoskeletal examination and imaging, as well as echocardiography, in future cases of GINS1 deficiency will be important.

Notably, across the nine individuals with suspected GINS1 deficiency, only five distinct variants have been reported (Table 1). Seven individuals have the c.‐48C>G variant and eight have the p.Arg83Cys variant. It remains unclear, however, as to why these variants are recurrent. Although there may be bias toward their identification given their previous publication, they remain seemingly rare in the general population. Where ethnicity/ancestry is reported, it is in common (European; Table 1); however, Cottineau et al. [1] reportedly performed haplotype analysis, suggesting that the shared alleles were not due to founder effects or a common ancestor. Further consideration should be given to the recurrent nature of these variants, particularly in case some populations may be at increased risk. Furthermore, all reported individuals have one splicing variant and one missense variant, apart from the individual described by Homma et al. [6]. This individual had two missense variants, including a unique c.139G>A variant, and appears to have a discrepant phenotype (Table 1). This may represent further expansion of the phenotype or point to an additional or alternative molecular diagnosis. Thus, further identification and phenotyping of individuals with suspected GINS1 deficiency, including those with other variants, together with variant‐specific functional studies, will be essential.

Taken together, this case helps solidify the role of GINS1 and these recurrent variants in an ultrarare autosomal recessive combined immunodeficiency and growth retardation syndrome. Our proband, together with the published cases summarized in Table 1, raises the possibility of glaucoma as a feature of GINS1 deficiency, highlighting the need for ophthalmologic evaluation. The lack of infections in our proband highlights that individuals with penetrant cellular phenotypes can show divergent infectious courses. Given the presence of cardiac and musculoskeletal anomalies in our proband, consideration of relevant imaging should also be given. Identification of further cases is needed to clarify the phenotype and contextual incidence of each feature. Reports of disease‐causing variants different from those described, ideally with associated functional studies, are needed to understand alternative mechanisms of disease. Finally, IEI, such as this, should be considered among the genetic causes of growth retardation, further highlighting the role of broad sequencing in their diagnostic workup.
