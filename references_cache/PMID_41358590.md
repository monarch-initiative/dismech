# Prime Editing for p47(phox)-Deficient Chronic Granulomatous Disease.
**Authors:** Gori JL, Haddad E, Frangoul H, Kohn DB, Morris EC, Martin BN, Deary BA, Nickerson M, Scholz RL, Fernandez I, Leveille K, De Ravin SS, Kang EM, Pierzynski M, Estwick T, Littel P, Kuhns DB, Long Priel DA, Teira P, Turvey S, Arnold J, Evans MD, McManus M, Carpenter B, Waterman DP, Anzalone AV, Petrusich A, Osuna CE, O'Malley TT, Stewart-Ornstein J, Heath JM, Nehilla BJ, Asmal M, Malech HL
**Journal:** N Engl J Med (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1056/NEJMoa2509807](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2509807)

## Content

Chronic granulomatous disease (CGD) is a severe monogenic immunodeficiency caused by damaging variants in genes required for microbicidal NADPH oxidase activity. Autosomal recessive p47phox-deficient CGD (p47-CGD) is predominantly caused by a two-nucleotide deletion in exon 2 (delGT) of NCF1. We developed PM359, an autologous CD34+ hematopoietic stem-cell therapy in which prime editing is used to correct delGT. Two participants received PM359 after myeloid conditioning with busulfan: neutrophils and platelets engrafted promptly in both patients. Adverse events were consistent with myeloid conditioning with busulfan. NADPH oxidase activity was observed in neutrophils within 1 month and was maintained for 6 months and 4 months as of the last follow-up visit in Participants 1 and 2, respectively. These results support further investigation of prime editing of CD34+ cells to treat p47-CGD. (Funded by Prime Medicine; ClinicalTrials.gov number, NCT06559176.).

Chronic granulomatous disease (CGD) is an inherited immunodeficiency affecting ~1 in 200,000 births and caused by mutations in genes encoding subunits of the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase complex (gp91phox, p22phox, p40phox, p47phox, p67phox, and EROS), 1 , 2 which generates reactive oxygen species to kill pathogens via direct lysis, granule protease activation, and neutrophil extracellular traps. 3 Individuals with CGD have reduced or absent NADPH oxidase and are susceptible to life-threatening infections and inflammatory complications, with bacterial and fungal infections being leading causes of mortality, 4 and non-infectious inflammatory conditions (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease), being major sources of morbidity. Measures of NADPH oxidase function, such as the dihydrorhodamine (DHR) assay, correlate with clinical disease manifestations, and partial residual NADPH oxidase activity (20% DHR positive neutrophils) is sufficient to protect from severe infection 5 .

The p47phox-deficient AR form of CGD (p47-CGD) is caused by damaging variants in NCF1 and accounts for ~25% of CGD cases in North America and Europe. 2 The most common mutation, a two-nucleotide (nt) deletion in exon 2 (delGT), is found in ~80% of individuals with p47-CGD. 6– 8 The NCF1 gene is flanked by the pseudogenes NCF1B and NCF1C (NCF1B/C), which share >99% sequence identity and include the same inactivating delGT that is found in NCF1 of most individuals with p47-CGD (Figure 1A), therefore most individuals with p47-CGD have 6 potentially correctable delGT alleles.

In theory, prime editing, which uses a Cas9 nickase fused to a reverse transcriptase (RT), was developed for precise correction of most mutations associated with monogenic illness. Unlike other gene-editing modalities evaluated clinically (e.g., CRISPR-Cas9 nuclease,8,9), prime editing is designed to mediate a precise repair without requiring double-strand breaks. 9 Previous studies have shown that delGT correction in NCF1 or NCF1B/C results in NADPH oxidase activity. 10 PM359 is an autologous p47-CGD CD34+ cell therapy in which prime editing reverts delGT to ‘GTGT’ in NCF1 gene and/or pseudogenes (Figure 1A).

The study sponsor (Prime Medicine, Inc.) designed the study protocols, with oversight provided by the steering committee and an independent data monitoring committee. Representatives of the sponsor gathered and analyzed the data and wrote the first draft of the manuscript (Supplementary Appendix). All authors had access to the data and approved the decision to submit the manuscript for publication. All authors vouch for the accuracy and completeness of the data generated from their respective sites, and the representatives of Prime Medicine, Inc. vouch for the fidelity of the study to the protocol, which is available with the full text of this article at NEJM.org.

The study was approved by the institutional review board or ethics committee at each participating study site and all participants gave written informed consent. Two study participants were enrolled, underwent CD34+ cell collection, PM359 manufacture and treatment with PM359. No additional participants were enrolled or underwent PM359 manufacture. The study was stopped after treatment of two study participants at the sponsor’s discretion, for reasons unrelated to safety or lack of efficacy.

In the PM359 clinical study, participants with p47-CGD received a single intravenous infusion of PM359 (Table 1). Per the study protocol, eligibility for the first study cohort was limited to adults (aged ≥ 18) with a diagnosis of CGD due to the delGT variant in NCF1 causing p47phox dysfunction. Eligible participants were required to have been treated and followed for at least the preceding 2 years in a specialized center and must have had at least 1 prior severe CGD-related infection or an ongoing severe CGD-related infection requiring therapy or that was refractory to standard therapy, or an autoimmune or inflammatory condition related to CGD that was active or requiring therapy to maintain remission. Additional eligibility criteria are described in the Supplementary Appendix, available at NEJM.org.

Study participants underwent mobilization with G-CSF and Plerixafor, with subsequent enrichment of CD34+ cells from the apheresis product. PM359 was manufactured from enriched, mobilized CD34+ cells by electroporation of PM359 Prime Editor components which included an mRNA encoding the prime editor protein (SpCas9 nickase domain and RT domain), a prime editor guide RNA (pegRNA) and a nicking guide RNA (ngRNA). DNA sequencing was used to evaluate the percentage of allelic editing at the on-target site in the CD34+ cell product and in clones (plated in colony forming cell [CFC] assays). Participants received single-agent, pharmacokinetically adjusted busulfan myeloid conditioning before the infusion of PM359. Additional information on PM359 is provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

Participants were monitored for hematopoietic engraftment, adverse events and NADPH oxidase activity (via DHR assay by flow cytometry) in neutrophils. Participants were also evaluated for changes in severity of pre-existing CGD-associated infectious or inflammatory complications that were present at the time of study enrollment, as well as development of incident infectious and inflammatory complications of disease, and for use of antimicrobial and immunosuppressive medication for treatment and prophylaxis of these complications. DNA sequencing was used to measure the fraction of total DNA that was edited at the on-target site in nucleated peripheral blood cells and in colony forming cells (CFCs) generated from bone marrow mononuclear cells. (Details regarding the measures are provided in the Supplementary Appendix).

We assessed the effect of prime editing on CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPC) from four individuals with p47-CGD (Supplementary Appendix). On average, 82% of CFCs carried ≥1 corrected allele (Figure S1A); the remainder carried no corrected alleles. Less than 2% of CFCs had unintended edits (e.g., indels, typically adjacent to the edit site, see Supplemental Table S1) at the targeted site of NCF1/B/C and these occurred in cells with ≥1 corrected allele. Following transplantation of PM359 Prime Edited (PE) p47-CGD CD34+ cells into immunodeficient mice, the frequency of PE CFCs and number of corrected alleles were slightly greater in mouse engrafted bone marrow CFCs and PM359 (Figure S1D, S1E) suggesting similar, slightly greater levels of long-term HSPCs than in the bulk drug product. The editing frequency was similar in mouse bone marrow–generated myeloid, lymphoid, and erythroid human cells (Figure S1F). Cell potency, engraftment, and blood reconstitution were maintained (Figure S1G–I).

NADPH oxidase activity was observed in myeloid progeny of mouse bone marrow engrafted CD34+ cells as measured by by DHR and nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) assays (Figure S1B). Transcriptome analysis showed elevated levels of interferon-signaling in unedited bone marrow–engrafted p47-CGD CD34+ cells, a known feature of CGD 11 ; this interferon signal was reduced in PE p47-CGD CD34+ cells (Figure S2).

We tested PE p47-CGD cells from three donors for the presence of off-target edits and chromosomal alterations. The arrangement and sequence similarity of NCF1 and NCF1B/C alleles raises the risks of DNA loss during gene editing 12 13 ; indeed, frequent loss of the intervening sequences between NCF1/B/C was observed in clones edited with SpCas9 alone. PE p47-CGD cells showed no evidence of genome sequence loss (Figure S1C) or chromosomal abnormalities (Table S1). Deep targeted sequence analysis of edited cells identified on-target editing, but not off-target editing within the limits of detection of the assay; the potential off-target sites that we tested were identified using in silico prediction and two in vitro methods (Figure 1B, Figure S3). 14 , 15

The first study participant was an 18-year-old male with history of p47phox-deficient AR CGD due to the nonsense variant c.75_76del in NCF1. Diagnosed at age 6, active disease manifestations at enrollment included CGD-associated colitis (CAC) well-controlled with maintenance mesalamine. Prior CGD-defining complications included suppurative skin and soft tissue infections and invasive Burkholderia cepacia pulmonary infection. Participant 1 had been maintained on long-term antibiotic and antifungal prophylaxis.

Following peripheral stem cell mobilization with G-CSF and plerixafor, leukapheresis over two consecutive days yielded 40.7 × 106 cells/kg. His PM359 contained 9.1 × 106 cells/kg, with on-target editing of ≥1 allele in approximately 68% of CFCs and an editing frequency (%GTGT DNA sequencing reads) of 13% in the bulk cell population. He underwent myeloid conditioning with intravenous busulfan for 4 consecutive days, with daily pharmacokinetic monitoring of therapeutic exposure (AUC0–24 range of 4155 – 5491 μM·min, with per-protocol target range of 4500–5500 μM·min). PM359 was administered via central venous catheter without complication.

Overall safety was generally consistent with myeloid conditioning with busulfan (Supplemental Table S4); the participant experienced two episodes of hypertension (Grade 2) and dizziness (Grade 1) which both started and resolved on Day 1 following infusion. No serious adverse events (SAEs) occurred after PM359 administration. Standard post-treatment supportive care was provided, including engraftment G-CSF and one donor platelet transfusion. (Treatment recommendations about post-treatment supportive care were at the discretion of the investigator.)

Neutrophil engraftment occurred on day +16 (Figure 2A) and platelet engraftment on day +19 (Figure 2B). Participant 1 was discharged on day +24. Monthly analysis of peripheral blood for the intended edit (Figure 2C) and DHR activity (Figure 2D) confirmed that both were detected and maintained over time. Flow cytometry on day +30 (1 month relative to PM359 infusion) demonstrated 69% DHR+ neutrophils with a median fluorescence intensity comparable to a healthy donor control (Figure 2G). p47 protein was also detected in neutrophils (Figure 2H). Neutrophil NADPH oxidase activity has remained durable through 6 months. The proportion of prime-edited CFCs and number of corrected alleles in a bone marrow specimen collected at 6 months were similar to those of the PM359 drug product (Figure S4).

Maintenance mesalamine for CAC was stopped prior to transplant and chronic CGD antimicrobial prophylaxis was discontinued on day +181. Participant 1 remains free of intercurrent infection, new CGD-related complications and clinically apparent CAC disease activity.

A 57-year-old male with autosomal recessive p47-CGD due to the NCF1 nonsense variant c.75_76del also underwent treatment with PM359. Diagnosed in early childhood, his only active disease manifestation at enrollment was CGD-associated colitis (CAC), with moderately active symptoms despite maintenance therapy with azathioprine. History of prior CGD manifestations included invasive pulmonary fungal infection definitively treated with partial lobectomy, hepatic abscesses, and recurrent skin and soft tissue infections. At enrollment, Participant 2 was receiving antimicrobial prophylaxis with levofloxacin and posaconazole.

Following peripheral stem cell mobilization with G-CSF and plerixafor, leukapheresis over three consecutive days yielded 18.1 × 106 CD34+ cells/kg. His PM359 contained a total viable CD34+ cell dose of 4.3 × 106 cells/kg, with on-target editing of ≥1 allele in approximately 91% of colony-forming cells and a prime-editing frequency (%GTGT DNA sequencing reads) of 23% in the bulk cell population. He underwent myeloablative conditioning with intravenous busulfan administered for 4 consecutive days, with estimated 24-hour exposure for doses 1 and 3 of 4826 uM*min and 6141 uM*min, respectively.

Overall safety was consistent with myeloid conditioning with busulfan (Supplemental Table S4), with no PM359-related AEs reported, and no SAEs after PM359 administration. Neutrophil engraftment occurred on day +14 (Figure 2A) and platelet engraftment by day +12 (Figure 2B), without requirement for blood products or growth factor support. Participant 2 was discharged on day +19.

Monthly analysis of peripheral blood for prime editing (Figure 2C) and DHR activity (Figure 2E) confirmed that both were detected and maintained over time. Flow cytometry on day +30 demonstrated 83% DHR+ neutrophils, with mean fluorescence intensity comparable to that of healthy controls (Figure 2G). p47 protein was detected in peripheral blood cells (Figure 2H). Neutrophil NADPH oxidase activity was sustained and the participant did not experience CGD-related complications or significant intercurrent illnesses through 4 months. Levels of fecal calprotectin, a marker of CAC activity, have decreased since transplant (Figure 2F), and chronic CAC-related diarrhea has abated.

We have shown that prime editing of autologous CD34+ cells is feasible at clinical scale and is precise. Our results are consistent with therapeutic benefit to two participants and demonstrate high on-target editing activity. Engraftment of neutrophils and platelets occurred rapidly after busulfan myeloid conditioning; quicker than engraftment times for other ex vivo hematopoietic stem cell therapies 17– 19 . The relatively high recovery rate of viable corrected cells after prime editing, which did not require multiple cycles of mobilization to achieve the minimum cell dose for administration, as well as the rapid reconstitution of the hematopoietic system after infusion, support the hypothesis that the mechanism of prime editing may be better tolerated by HSPCs than viral gene insertion 16 or CRISPR-mediated gene editing. 12

Functional correction of neutrophils was confirmed by DHR testing and observed during engraftment. DHR activity exceeded the 20% threshold which in natural history studies has been correlated with protection from severe CGD-associated infectious complications. 5 DHR activity remained stable over time in both participants, suggesting that gene correction occurred in long-term repopulating HSPCs. The mean fluorescence intensity of DHR-positive neutrophils was equivalent to those of healthy donors, underscoring functional equivalence of corrected cells. These data support the value of in situ correction of genetic mutation: with precise correction of the defective gene in its native locus, normal physiologic regulation is preserved, as the genetic correction retains the context of usual and full transcriptional elements surrounding the gene. Precise gene correction at the endogenous genetic locus is a key differentiator of prime editing technology that contrasts to ectopic gene replacement therapy with either lentivirus or adeno-associated virus and nuclease-mediated gene editing which can lead to variable genetic outcomes and double strand breaks.

Both participants remain free of new CGD-related complications, and Participant 2 showed improvement in the biomarker of inflammatory bowel disease, fecal calprotectin. Observed toxicities were consistent with busulfan-based conditioning., predominantly cytopenia, mucositis/stomatitis and metabolic abnormalities.

The treated participants represent distinct aspects of the CGD population: Participant 1 is an adolescent who had relatively mild manifestations prior to PM359 infusion and Participant 2 had advanced multi-organ involvement that would have put him at elevated risk of complications should he have undergone an allogeneic HSCT. In both participants, PM359 restored NADPH oxidase activity, supporting the applicability of this approach across age ranges and disease severity.

The primary limitation of this report is the short duration of follow-up and limited number of study participants. Longer observation will be essential to confirm sustained hematopoietic correction and to demonstrate that restoration of near-normal DHR activity will result in complete resolution of existing and prevention of future complications of CGD. Because the current clinical study has been terminated by the sponsor, treatment of additional individuals with p47-CGD will be contingent upon the sponsor identifying alternative avenues to make the treatment accessible to patients. In summary, prime editing of autologous CD34+ cells corrected the molecular defect in p47-CGD and restored neutrophil function to near-healthy levels in the two study participants, establishing that prime editing can result in high levels of precise correction. These and the clinical data and provide support for the development of this approach as a therapeutic platform for CGD and other genetic diseases.
