At the beginning of the last century, quinidine had been shown to be highly
effective in the management of atrial fibrillation and soon after, of
ventricular arrhythmias. At the end of the same century, quinidine was quickly
abandoned, and its manufacturing ceased, resulting in limited accessibility
across numerous countries. Paradoxically, this decline in use occurred alongside
accumulating evidence supporting quinidine's therapeutic benefit in managing
rare, life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias occurring in patients with no
organic heart disease (Idiopathic ventricular fibrillation, Brugada syndrome,
Early repolarization syndrome, Short QT syndrome, Multifocal ectopic
Purkinje-related premature contractions), as well as in those with organic heart
disease involving the Purkinje network (acute myocardial infarction and
hypertrophic cardiomyopathy). The present review will extensively deal with all
these topics.

The history of quinidine dates back several centuries, originating from the cinchona tree, long valued for its medicinal properties. This tree grows on the mountain slopes of the Andes, whose bark has fever-reducing properties, that were known to the Peruvian Incas for generations. The therapeutic potential of cinchona bark was first documented in 1633 by a Jesuit missionary in Peru, marking the beginning of its global use in managing febrile illnesses, most notably malaria, from the 17th century onward. In 1820, two French chemists, Pierre Pelletier and Joseph Caventou, extracted and chemically identified a bitter powder from the bark of the cinchona tree, an active alkaloid, which they named quinine. Subsequently, several pure alkaloids were derived from cinchona, such as quinidine. A noteworthy mention of quinidine appears in an 1853 communication by Louis Pasteur to the French Academy of Sciences, reflecting early scientific interest in its pharmacological properties. The recognition of quinidine's antiarrhythmic potential likely stemmed from a clinical observation in which a patient receiving quinine for malaria exhibited arrhythmic episodes that resolved without recurrence during therapy.

The first formal investigation into the electrophysiological effects of quinidine alkaloids was conducted in 1914 by Dutch internist Karel Frederik Wenckebach, marking a pivotal moment in the study of antiarrhythmic therapy. In 1918, Walter Frey demonstrated the high efficacy of quinidine in converting atrial fibrillation (AF) to sinus rhythm that rapidly led to it becoming the main drug used worldwide for this purpose. Soon after, quinidine was used in the treatment of ventricular arrhythmias, including in a patient suffering from apparently idiopathic ventricular fibrillation (IVF) one century ago [1]. In the 1990's, the introduction of new class 1C antiarrhythmics and radiofrequency ablation for treating AF, as well as a meta-analysis showing that quinidine is associated with increased mortality in the setting of AF therapy [2] resulted in a dramatic decline in the use of the medication. Similar conclusions followed the publication of The Cardiac Arrhythmia Suppression Trial (CAST) [3] showing increased mortality after class I antiarrhythmic drugs were given for the prevention of sudden death in patients with asymptomatic ventricular arrhythmias and impaired left ventricular function. Despite serving for decades as the first-line treatment for a broad spectrum of cardiac arrhythmias, quinidine was quickly abandoned, and its manufacturing ceased, resulting in limited accessibility across numerous countries [4,5]. Paradoxically, this decline in use occurred alongside accumulating evidence supporting quinidine's therapeutic benefit in managing rare, life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias occurring in patients with no organic heart disease [IVF, Brugada syndrome (BrS), Early repolarization syndrome (ERS), Short QT syndrome (SQTS), Multifocal ectopic Purkinje-related premature contractions (MEPPC)] as well as in those with organic heart disease involving the Purkinje network.

IVF is diagnosed by exclusion of any known cardiac, respiratory, metabolic and toxicological etiologies that may lead to cardiac arrest with documented VF [6]. Data from the CASPER registry [7] suggest that careful clinical assessment of patients surviving a cardiac arrest in the absence of structural heart disease [(normal cardiac function on echocardiogram, no evidence of coronary artery disease, and a normal electrocardiogram (ECG)], can diagnose IVF in more than half of these cases. A staged cascade screening approach was associated with an incremental diagnostic yield in this cohort: a) ECG, signal-averaged ECG, telemetry; b) magnetic resonance imaging with and without contrast; c) provocative tests (exercise stress test, epinephrine infusion, sodium channel blocker test); d) electrophysiologic (EP) study and voltage map; e) ventricular biopsy; and f) targeted genetic testing which has a limited role in the diagnosis of IVF [8] with variants identified in 4.9–6.7% of patients mainly in genes associated with cardiomyopathy rather than primary electrical disease; the DPP6 risk haplotype is presently the only genetic marker associated with familial IVF and sudden death particularly in the Dutch population.

Normality of this workup should rule out “inherited arrhythmia syndromes” such as BrS, catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT), long and short QTS as well as ERS.

IVF affects patients of either sex, mainly those in their early 40s at mean ages ranging from 38 years to 45 years [[9], [10], [11]].

Quinidine therapy has demonstrated high efficacy in patients with short-coupled IVF, significantly reducing arrhythmic events. The most outstanding case of short-coupled IVF storm treated with quinidine, was reported by Moe from Norway in 1948 [12]. Their 38-year-old male patient was treated with low-dose quinidine (200 mg daily) for 42 years and remained free of recurrent syncope or arrhythmias until his death at age 83 from lung cancer [13]. We encountered our first short-coupled IVF patient in 1979 [14,15] and the circumstances of discovery of this case have been described in detail elsewhere [16]. In our review of the literature on short-coupled IVF [11], quinidine given to 12 patients represented the most effective oral medication (83.3 % efficacy rate), by far better than oral amiodarone and flecainide (60 % each, both given in 5 patients) and oral verapamil (50 %) given to 24 patients. The efficacy rate obtained with quinidine (83.3 %) using a mean dose of 844 ± 335 mg during a follow-up period of 16.5 ± 17.1 years was identical to that reported by Steinberg et al. in 12 patients treated with this medication using lower dose of quinidine (667 ± 400 mg) [10].

In THESIS (THerapy Efficacy in Short or long-coupled idiopathic ventricular fibrillation: an International Survey) our group compared the results of radiofrequency ablation (n = 112 patients) versus quinidine therapy (n = 69 patients). Patients were followed during a mean follow-up of 84.8 ± 64.5 months. Therapy success was achieved in the ablation or drug treated group in 69 (62.2 %) and 49 (71 %) patients, respectively (p = 0.29). The right ventricular Purkinje was the main targeted ablation site in 46 (47.4 %) patients. A higher success rate with ablation compared with quinidine was observed when the site of origin of the arrhythmia was the left ventricle as compared with the right ventricle (82.1 % vs 60.3 %, p = 0.019) (Tovia-Brodie, Belhassen, and coworkers, personal communication). Extensive works from our laboratory in series of patients with apparently IVF who underwent diagnostic EP studies showed a 90 % inducibility rate of sustained ventricular polymorphic tachyarrhythmias [[17], [18], [19]]. This high inducibility rate enabled us to test the effects of class 1A drugs (mainly quinidine) and to demonstrate their great clinical and EP efficacy [[17], [18], [19]] as well the good reproducibility of these results [19] (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5). BrS is an inherited arrhythmic disorder displaying a distinct ECG pattern (specifically, right bundle branch block with coved ST elevation in leads V1-V3) associated with syncope and malignant ventricular tachyarrhythmias [20]. Initially, the syndrome was exclusively associated with polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (PVT)/VF [20]. However, later studies revealed that monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (MVT) could also be part of the arrhythmic spectrum [21,22].

About 90 % of BrS patients exhibiting arrhythmic events are male [23]. The vast majority (94.2 %) of BrS patients were 16–70 years old at the time of their arrhythmic event (peak rate between 38 and 48 years) whereas pediatric (<16 years) and elderly patients (>70 years) comprised 4.3 % and 1.5 %, respectively [24].

Genetic studies indicated that familial BrS was primarily due to loss-of-function variants in the SCN5A gene, which encodes for the α-subunit of the NaV1.5 sodium channel [25]. Only SCN5A gene variants are definite disease causing BrS. Currently, an identifiable SCN5A variant is found only in ∼20 % of patients with BrS [25]. However, proband pediatric (age <16 years) and female patients with arrhythmic events were found to have the highest proportion of pathogenic/likely pathogenic SCN5A variants [26].

Growing evidence from cardiac imaging, epicardial ablation, and pathology studies suggests the presence of an epicardial arrhythmic substrate within the right ventricular outflow tract [25].

Although guidelines have consistently recommended ICD implantation in patients with BrS and a history of resuscitated cardiac arrest as well as in those considered to be at high risk for developing an arrhythmic event [6], our group has raised the question of whether an ICD is truly the only therapeutic option available [27,28]. Hermida et al. [31] obtained similar results using a different slow-release quinidine preparation (quinidine hydrochloride 600–900 mg daily). We also administered quinidine hydrochloride in some of our patients [30] following AstraZeneca's decision to discontinue the production of quinidine bisulfate [4].

Electrical storms of VF represent the most malignant form of ventricular arrhythmia in patients with BrS, occurring in up to 10 % of untreated individuals [32] and accounting for as many as 38 % of arrhythmic events in patients with ICDs [33]. These storms may be the first manifestation of the disease as well, and are frequently triggered by fever, specifically in pediatric patients [34]. Multiple DC shocks can lead to psychologic disorders, proarrhythmia, deleterious effect on cardiac function, electromechanical dissociation, and death. Heart transplantation has even been used as the last resort in a patient with intractable VF [35].

Quinidine has shown efficacy in preventing arrhythmic storms and is presently the drug of choice for this scenario [32]. Given the high incidence—reported at up to 38 %—of adverse effects leading to drug discontinuation with 1.5 g doses of quinidine bisulfate [29,30], lower doses of quinidine (200–600 mg/day) have been tested for the treatment of arrhythmic storms. Márquez et al. [36] described a series of 6 patients with arrhythmic syncope or appropriate ICD discharges (including 4 with arrhythmic storm) complicating BrS, who were treated with low doses of quinidine (≤600 mg daily). They compiled their results with those reported on 14 patients collected from case reports. Twelve patients from this cohort (60 %) suffered arrhythmic storms. Márquez et al. observed low doses of quinidine (≤600 mg) to be well tolerated and associated with acute and long-term arrhythmia control in 85 % of patients. Moreover, 4 patients who stopped taking the medication experienced recurrent arrhythmias that were successfully controlled after treatment was reinitiated.

Regardless of its precise mechanism of action, as thoroughly reviewed by Márquez et al. [36], we consider quinidine to embody many of the characteristics of an ‘ideal’ antiarrhythmic agent for the management of patients with BrS [37]. At “high doses” it prevents inducibility of VF and recurrent arrhythmic events [29,30]. At “lower doses”, as shown by Márquez et al. [36], it ends arrhythmic storms in 85 % of patients. To the best of our knowledge, this is the only drug in our therapeutic armamentarium that exhibits such outstanding antiarrhythmic action for managing a life-threatening ventricular arrhythmia both acutely and chronically.

ERS is diagnosed in the presence of an early repolarization pattern (ERP) in the inferolateral leads in an individual resuscitated from cardiac arrest or sudden cardiac death, in the absence of another identifiable cause [38]. ERP is defined by the presence of J-point elevation (end-QRS notch on the downslope of a prominent R-wave) ≥1 mm involving ≥2 leads (excluding V1 to V3), where the QRS duration is < 120 ms [38]. Interestingly, the presence of ERP in patients with an established primary arrhythmic syndrome such as LQT syndrome, BrS, CPVT, or SQTS has been reported to be associated with worse outcomes [38].

ERS has also been termed “le syndrome d’Haissaguerre” [39] particularly in light of the pioneering and innovative work of Michel Haïssaguerre in this field.

The effect of quinidine in ERS was assessed in several case reports [[40], [41], [42], [43], [44], [45]] and 1 case series [46]. In all patients except one [43], quinidine abolished the arrhythmic events. Interestingly Voskoboinik et al. showed that quinidine effectively suppressed the arrhythmias in 5 of 5 patients but required dose escalation to >1 g/d in 3 of them [46], suggesting that the dose of hydroquinidine (800 mg daily) which failed in the patient reported by Chatzidou et al. [43] might have been too low. It is also noteworthy that quinidine decreased the ERP in most patients.

However, the most extensive clinical experience to date has been reported by Haissaguerre and coworkers [47]. From their multicenter cohort of 122 patients with IVF and ERP in the inferolateral leads, 33 patients having at least 3 episodes of VF over a mean follow-up of 69 months were selected (including 16 patients with electrical storm) for treatment with antiarrhythmic drugs. Among all the pharmacological agents tested, only quinidine (in 3 patients) and hydroquinidine (in 6 patients) proved effective, achieving complete suppression of recurrent ventricular fibrillation in 9 out of 9 patients (100 %). The arrhythmic burden was reduced from a mean of 33 ± 35 episodes (median 25; IQR 8–62) to zero during a mean follow-up of 25 ± 18 months on therapy. The daily dose of hydroquinidine was 600 mg in 6 patients resulting in blood levels ranging from 1.7 to 4.2 μg/ml (therapeutic range 2–5 μg/ml). In addition, quinidine reduced the magnitude of ERP or restored a normal ECG in all patients.

SQTS is a rare cardiac channelopathy characterized by abnormally abbreviated QT intervals on the ECG and an increased risk of both atrial and ventricular arrhythmias [38]. Clinical manifestations of SQTS include cardiac arrest or sudden cardiac death (SCD), arrhythmic syncope, and atrial arrhythmias [38]. The pathophysiologic basis of SQTS is thought to be related to a decrease of the cardiac action potential duration (APD) with a gene variant identified in about 25 % of reported cases [48]. Initial genetic studies showed that the abbreviation of the cardiac APD mainly resulted from gain-of-function variants in potassium channels (KCNH2, KCNJ2, and KCNQ1), and to a lesser extent from loss-of-function variants in calcium channels (CACNA1C and CACNB2b) [49]. More recently, however, nonsynonymous variants in the anion exchanger (AE3)-encoding SLC4A3 gene [50] were found to represent the most common cause of SQTS [48]. In a recent report, a novel variant in the SLC4A3 gene associated with a gain-of-function effect causing SQTS has been reported [51].

A QTc ≤320 ms on baseline ECG confers a diagnosis of SQTS [6,52] although a cutoff of ≤315 ms has been suggested in children [53]. Currently, the Bazett correction is proposed [52] although consensus is lacking [54]. Using a cutoff of QTc ≤330 ms, the prevalence of SQTS within the general population-screening ECG studies ranges between 0 % and 0.2 % [38] with generally favorable outcomes in these cases in the absence of other cardiac abnormalities [38]. In survivors of unexplained cardiac arrest, or those who carry a pathogenic gene variant, or have a suspicious family history, a QTc ≤360 ms may confer a diagnosis of SQTS [6].

In 2011, Giustetto and coworkers [55] from the European Short QT Registry provided data on 33 SQTS patients (75 % males; median age: 26 years) who were followed up for 64 ± 27 months. Twenty-eight patients underwent an EP study. The ventricular effective refractory periods (ERPs) at the right ventricular apex at a cycle length of 600 to 500 ms were very short, varying between 140 and 200 ms (mean: 166 ± 21 ms). No differences were found between patients with a history of cardiac arrest or syncope and those without. VF was induced in 16 patients (57 %), while 7 (44 %) of the 16 had mechanical induction of VF during catheter positioning. The atrial ERPs were also shortened and ranged between 120 and 200 ms (mean: 163 ± 22 ms). AF was induced in 8 (36 %) patients.

In 22 patients, hydroquinidine was tested to assess its efficacy in prolonging the QT interval. Twelve patients (8 adults and 4 children) received hydroquinidine for a mean period of 76 ± 30 months (range: 27–105 months); the mean dose in adults was 870 ± 186 mg per day (range: 600 to 1000 mg per day). ECG before and after drug therapy were available for analysis from 18 patients. In 8 patients, the EPS was repeated after the drug had reached the steady-state concentration, in 6 cases via the ventricular lead of the ICD. Ventricular ERPs were prolonged significantly (from 154 ± 12 ms to 205 ± 23 ms; p = 0.02). Seven patients had VF induced at baseline (87 %), whereas none had VF induced after hydroquinidine (p = 0.016).

In 2017, Mazzanti and associates [56] demonstrated for the first time that treatment with hydroquinidine was associated with a lower incidence of lethal arrhythmic events (LAE) in SQTS patients. They compared the occurrence of LAE during 6 ± 1 years before and after hydroquinidine in 17 patients, and found a reduction in both the rate of LAE from 40 % to 0 % (p = 0.03) and the number of LAE per patient from 0.73 ± 0.3 to 0 (p = 0.026). Furthermore, the annual rate of LAE in the 16 patients with a previous cardiac arrest dropped from 12 % before hydroquinidine to 0 on therapy (p = 0.028). Importantly, no pro-arrhythmic events were observed in the treatment group, thus confirming the safety of the medication for SQTS patients.

In 2012, Laurent and coworkers [57] were the first to report three unrelated families comprising 21 individuals affected by MEPPC characterized by numerous PVCs with right and/or left bundle branch block patterns competing with narrow junctional and occasional sinus activity. A fully penetrant SCN5A gain-of-function mutation was found to be responsible for this new entity. Dilated cardiomyopathy was identified in 6 patients, nonsustained atrial tachyarrhythmias or paroxysmal AF were detected in 9 patients, and sudden death was reported in 5 individuals. Invasive EP study demonstrated that the PVCs originated from the Purkinje network. Dilated cardiomyopathy patients with a MEPPC phenotype responded relatively poorly to standard heart failure medical therapy and catheter ablation as the PVCs originate from all parts of the fascicular Purkinje fiber network. In contrast, hydroquinidine (but also flecainide), were shown to have a remarkable beneficial effect on the ectopic burden and the associated cardiomyopathy. Notably, twelve studies encompassing a total of 23 patients reported successful treatment outcomes in cases of PVC-predominant cardiomyopathy using sodium channel-blocking drug therapy (flecainide or quinidine) [58]. R222Q SCN5A mutation has been shown to be associated with reversible ventricular ectopy and dilated cardiomyopathy after antiarrhythmic therapy [59].

Viskin and coworkers elegantly summarized the topic of PVT/VF occurring in the setting of coronary heart disease [60]. They identified two main categories of patients with PVT/VF: a) PVT/VF occurring at the acute myocardial ischemia stage, in whom the beneficial effect of quinidine was reported in a single patient only [61]; and b) PVT/VF occurring within days of an otherwise uncomplicated myocardial infarction or coronary revascularization procedure [62] which responded excellently to quinidine. In a study involving 43 patients [62], the PVT events were invariably triggered by extrasystoles with short (364 ± 36 ms) coupling intervals. Arrhythmic storms (4–16 events of PVT deteriorating to VF) occurred in 23 (53 %) patients. These arrhythmic storms were always refractory to conventional antiarrhythmic therapy, including intravenous amiodarone, but invariably responded to quinidine therapy. In-hospital mortality was 17 % for patients with arrhythmic storm. Patients treated with quinidine consistently survived to hospital discharge. During long-term follow-up (of 5.6 ± 6 years; range, 1 month to 18 years), 3 (16 %) out of the patients discharged without quinidine developed recurrent PVT. There were no recurrent arrhythmias during quinidine therapy.

Komatsu and coworkers [63] nicely demonstrated that the ectopic beats that trigger the PVT/VF episodes at the various acute, subacute and remote phases after myocardial infarction were found to originate from Purkinje fibers located usually within the border zones of the infarct scar, or less commonly, deep within the infarct-related scar. The Purkinje origin of most of PVT/VF can explain the beneficial response of quinidine observed in the group of patients described by Viskin et al. above [62]. Quinidine is a strong blocker of the transient outward potassium current [64] which is highly expressed in Purkinje fibers, with recovery kinetics that are different from those of the surrounding myocardial cells [65].

Viskin and coworkers also described another group of coronary artery disease patients presenting with out-of-hospital PVT without a recent coronary event or an obvious precipitating factor [66]. These patients whose arrhythmias also responded uniquely to quinidine therapy, share many of the characteristics of the group of patients described earlier [62].

Hocini and coworkers [67] were the first to report on 5 patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy who underwent ablation after resuscitation from recurrent episodes of VF. PVCs, seen to initiate VF in certain cases, were recorded noninvasively before the ablation procedure. PVC triggers were localized invasively to the distal left Purkinje conduction system. All targeted VF triggers were successfully eliminated by radiofrequency ablation in the left ventricle. In a later study, the Purkinje system was implicated as a trigger or driver in 6 of 8 (75 %) of patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [68].

Kamakura and coworkers [69] reported the case of a woman with hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy and recurrent VF episodes after ablation of the Purkinje-myocardial junction and abnormal potentials in the apical left ventricular aneurysm. After adding a low dose of quinidine (200 mg) to the on-going ineffective amiodarone treatment, no further VF recurrence was reported for 1 year. This is the first case showing the efficacy of quinidine on Purkinje ventricular arrhythmias in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

In certain countries, such in India, quinidine is manufactured but was not available for local use until recently. Therefore, quinine, the quinidine's levorotatory diastereomer which shares the cardiac electrophysiologic effects of quinidine, and had the advantage of being readily available, was used instead of quinidine. A similar high efficacy of quinine was shown in 3 patients from India who suffered from malignant ventricular arrhythmias in the setting of BrS [70,71] or short-coupled IVF [72]. Noteworthy is the fact that quinine does not affect significantly the QTc interval [73] and thus is associated with a lower incidence of torsades de pointe due to its stereoselective effect, resulting in 14 times less potency in blocking the hERG channel, the potassium channel essential for myocardial repolarization [74].

These side effects mainly include abdominal pains, diarrhea, fever, photosensitivity, and cinchonism. Thrombocytopenia, hemolytic anemia, lupus-like syndrome and hepatotoxicity are rarely observed [75]. Importantly, these side effects almost always resolve upon drug discontinuation without additional sequelae [30].

The second challenge is a proarrhythmic effect such as torsades de pointes, which is the most feared complication by many physicians when using the medication. This arrhythmia is unlikely to occur in patients with normal cardiac function and normal baseline QT interval. The prolongation of QT interval that follows quinidine administration usually is modest. In our experience, this has never been the reason for drug discontinuation in the types of patients which comprise the present paper. Major QT prolongation during quinidine therapy should lead the physician to suspect the rare coexistence of long QT syndrome, and should be easily diagnosed during ECG monitoring at the initiation of treatment.

The third challenge with quinidine therapy is the possible deleterious effect of concurrent therapy with noncardiac drugs that prolong the QT interval or result in bradycardia, as well as electrolyte disorders such as hypokalemia or hypomagnesemia that may contribute to proarrhythmic effects.

Certain populations (such as Japanese) have been found to be more susceptible to the side effects of quinidine. In these patients, daily low doses of quinidine (<300 mg) are usually prescribed and found to be very effective [76]. Similar low doses have also been effective in Chinese patients [77].

The doses of quinidine markedly differ whether the goal is to prevent the inducibility of PVT/VF during EP testing (and eventually avoid the implantation of an ICD) or to prevent the spontaneous occurrence of ventricular tachyarrhythmias in a patient implanted with an ICD. In the first setting, doses ranging from 1000 to 2000 mg may be used. In the second setting daily “low doses” ranging from 100 to 600 mg may be used. In BrS patients treated with “very low” daily dose (100–225 mg), it has been recommended that the medication is taken before sleep, considering that most arrhythmic episodes occur during night or early in the morning [76]. For patients with IVF, BrS and SQTS, the ESC 2022 guidelines recommend a dose of 600–1600 mg orally/day with the possibility of a loading dose of 200 mg every 3 h until effect (with a maximum of 3000 mg over 24 h [78].
