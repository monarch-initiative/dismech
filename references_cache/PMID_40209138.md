PURPOSE: Small cell bladder cancer (SCBC) is a rare histologic variant of 
bladder cancer with an aggressive disease course and poor outcomes. Given its 
uncommon nature, there is a paucity of high-quality data characterizing genomic 
drivers of this disease, and most patients are treated with approaches mirroring 
small cell lung cancer (SCLC). Leveraging the Tempus Lens deidentified 
clinically annotated genomic data set, we sought to evaluate the mutational 
landscape of SCBC relative to urothelial carcinoma (UC) and SCLC.
METHODS: Somatic pathogenic genomic alterations in patients with SCBC, UC, and 
SCLC of any stage who underwent blood- or tissue-based genomic profiling through 
the Tempus assay were cataloged. Baseline clinical and demographic features were 
compared across histologic groups. Alterations were collated and summarized 
using descriptive statistics. Pairwise comparisons were performed to assess 
differences in mutation frequency across pathologic cohorts.
RESULTS: In total, 149 SCBC, 4,350 UC, and 1,697 SCLC patients were included in 
the study. The most common genomic alterations in SCBC were in TP53 (87%), TERT 
(75%), and RB1 (70%). Among SCBC patients with TP53 mutations, RB1 comutations 
were observed in 77% of patients. Compared with UC, SCBC patients were 
significantly enriched for TP53, RB1, KMT2D, and KDM6A mutations. Compared with 
SCLC, SCBC patients were enriched for TERT, ARID1A, and CREBBP mutations, among 
others (P < .05). Multiple clinically targetable mutations were observed in 
SCBC, including PIK3CA (19%), ERBB2/3 (13%), and ALK (10%). Limitations of this 
study include its retrospective nature.
CONCLUSION: This study represents one of the most extensive efforts to 
characterize SCBC to date, providing a novel understanding of the genomic 
alterations underlying the disease and revealing actionable mutations that could 
serve as potential targets for improved clinical outcomes.

Small cell bladder cancer (SCBC) is a rare histologic variant that accounts for less than one percent of all bladder cancer cases diagnosed annually (1). Histologically, SCBC is a subset of neuroendocrine carcinoma and is often characterized by the expression of neuron-specific enolase, chromogranin, and synaptophysin (2). The clonal origin of SCBC remains unclear, with previous work demonstrating concordant allele loss between urothelial carcinoma (UC) and SCBC samples in patients with both tumor types, suggestive of a common cell of origin (2–4). Next-generation sequencing and tumor amplification of SCBC samples has yielded loss of RRM1, MGMT, and TS, as well as high expression of ERCC1 and MRP1 as molecular signatures (5,6). Additional studies have suggested that Kulchistky-type neuroendocrine cells present in bladder tissue could be the cellular predecessors to SCBC (7).

Even when diagnosed at a clinically localized stage, patients with SCBC tend to have a significantly worse prognosis compared to that of UC, with a reported 5-year survival as low as 14% versus 71%, respectively (1,8). Genetic alterations in TP53, cMET, RB1, FBXW7, and PTEN could partly explain its aggressive course (9). The treatment paradigm for UC has changed dramatically in recent years, with regulatory approvals of antibody-drug conjugates, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI), and targeted agents (10–16). In contrast, the treatment of SCBC is limited to cytotoxic chemotherapy regimens with limited efficacy (17). For patients with localized SCBC, concurrent chemoradiotherapy and/or perioperative chemotherapy remains the standard of care (17). Despite curative intent therapy, recurrences are common, and systemic cytotoxic chemotherapy extrapolating from small cell lung cancer (SCLC) studies, given the clinical and histologic commonalities of these neuroendocrine tumor types, continues to be the cornerstone of therapy (18).

Genomic profiling of SCLC tumoral samples has yielded a widely heterogeneous landscape, with some overlaps in the molecular aberrations described for SCBC. Mutations in TP53, RB1, ATM, cMET, PTEN, BRAF, SMAD4, and KRAS have been described (19). Although the treatment of SCBC has traditionally mirrored that of SCLC, an evaluation of the comparative biological similarities between both entities remains largely unexplored. Further, given the rarity of SCBC, it has historically been excluded from concerted large-scale studies aimed at genomic characterization of bladder carcinoma (20,21). Leveraging an extensive repository of molecular data from SCBC specimens, this retrospective study herein catalogues clinical and molecular data from patients with SCBC, UC, and SCLC to provide comparative genomic characterization and evaluate the prevalence of potentially actionable targets for therapeutic intervention in this rare tumor subtype.

The de-identified Tempus Lens dataset was queried (September 2023) using filters for patients diagnosed with UC, small cell carcinoma of the renal pelvis, bladder or ureter, or SCLC of any stage (T1–4, N0–2, M0–1) with clinical metadata and somatic mutation analysis with or without germline sequencing data. Somatic genomic alterations identified by tissue (xTT, xO, and xE) from primary or metastatic site or blood-based (xF) next-generation sequencing assay, (22) along with baseline demographic characteristics such as gender, age, race, ethnicity, and smoking status were catalogued from the Tempus Lens dataset. Patients were grouped into three groups based on underlying pathology: UC, SCBC, and SCLC. Clinical variables were summarized using descriptive statistics and compared between the groups (UC versus SCBC and SCBC versus SCLC) using a Fisher’s exact test with an alpha level of 0.05 used for significance. Genomic alterations in each group were summarized using descriptive statistics. Most frequently altered genes in SCBC were selected and compared pairwise with UC and SCLC using a two proportions Z-test with a significance level of 0.05 after correction for multiple hypothesis testing to control for false discovery rate. The most frequently altered genes in SCBC were analyzed for co-mutations with other frequently altered genes using descriptive statistics. All statistical analyses were performed in R Statistical Software, version 4.2.1.

Institutional review board (IRB) approval was in place for this study (IRB No. 17–001313), and patient informed consent has been waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

A total of 5,479,928 patients were included in the Tempus Lens database at the time of query (August 2023), including 149 patients with SCBC, 4,350 with UC, and 1,697 with SCLC who met inclusion criteria. Baseline demographic variables of the analyzed cohort are provided in Table 1. No significant differences were observed with regards to gender between UC and SCBC, with 72.7% (n=3,164) and 75.2% (n=112) of patients being male, respectively (P=0.57). In contrast, SCBC had a significantly lower proportion of females (24.8%) compared to SCLC (50.7%; P<0.00001). The median age of patients in the SCBC, UC, and SCLC cohorts were 71, 71, and 67 years, respectively (p>0.05 for all comparisons). Additionally, no significant differences were detected regarding race, ethnicity, or smoking status between SCBC, UC, and SCLC.

The most common genomic alterations observed in SCBC group were TP53 (87.3%), TERT (75.2%), RB1 (69.8%), ARID1A (34.2%), KMT2D (24.2%), KMT2C (22.8%), CREBBP (20.1%), SYNE1 (20.1%), KDM6A (20.1%), and PIK3CA (18.8%; Table 2). Patients with these mutations were analyzed for the presence of the other most frequently occurring mutations (Figure 1). The most frequently co-occurring somatic mutations with TP53 include TERT (83.0%), RB1 (76.9%), and ARID1A (38.5%). Other than TP53, the most frequently co-occurring somatic mutations with TERT included RB1 (79.4%) and ARID1A (40.2%). Notable co-occurring mutations with RB1 included CREBBP (83.3%) and KDM6A (93.3%). The prevalence of clinically significant gene alterations was comparable between tissue- and blood-based sequencing with TP53 (95.9%), TERT (81.0%), RB1 (74.3%), ARID1A (21.6%), and CDKN1A (17.5%) being the most frequently identified. No notable discrepancies in mutation prevalence were observed between tissue- and blood-derived samples.

Clinically actionable mutations were noted with varying frequency in SCBC cases. FGFR3 alterations were noted in only 6% of cases, but 14% of patients had an alteration in FGFR1–4. DNA damage repair alterations were seen in a significant proportion of patients, which included BRCA1/2 (15.4%), ATM (12.0%), BRIP1 (8.7%), CDK12 (4.7%), PALB2 (4.0%), and RAD51 (5%). Mismatch repair gene alterations (MSH2/6 and PMS2) were registered in 9.4% of patients. Additional targetable alterations identified amongst the SCBC population included PI3KCA (18.9%), ERBB-2 (12.6%), ERBB-3 (13.4%), and PTEN (11.4%). With several novel therapeutic agents targeting these genomic alterations currently in development (Table 3), these targetable alterations in SCBC provide novel therapeutic opportunities.

A comparison of somatic variants between SCBC and UC demonstrated significant differences between the two groups for multiple genes of interest (Figure 2). Compared to UC, the prevalence of alterations in TP53 (87.3% vs. 60.3%, P<0.00001), RB1 (69.8% vs. 22.7%, P<0.00001), CDKN1A (14.8% vs. 8.9%, P=0.02), MYCL (11.4% vs. 4.9%, P=0.0009), PTEN (11.4% vs. 5.9%, P=0.009), and GRIN2A (10.0% vs. 5.6%, P=0.03) was significantly higher in the SCBC group. In contrast, alterations in KDM6A (20.1% vs. 29.0%, P=0.02), KMT2D (24.2% vs. 32.7%, P=0.03), and CDKN2A (9.4% vs. 32.0%, P<0.00001) were less frequent in the SCBC group compared to UC. Interestingly, both groups also demonstrated shared genomic characteristics, and several genomic alterations known to be frequently present in UC were also prevalent in SCBC, including TERT (75.1% vs. 73.6%), ARID1A (34.2 vs. 28.0%), and ERCC2 (14.8% vs. 9.8%).

When comparing genomic alterations between SCBC and SCLC, the prevalence of TP53 (87.3% vs. 88.3%; P=0.79), RB1 (69.8% vs. 67.18%; P=0.57), and PTEN (11.4% vs 12.1%; P=0.89) alterations was similar between the two groups. In contrast, mutations in TERT were almost exclusive to the SCBC group (75.2% vs. 6.2%, P<0.0001). Similarly, ARID1A (34.3% vs. 12.3%, P<0.00001), CREBBP (20.1% vs. 10.8%, P=0.001), KDM6A (20.1% vs. 4.7%, P<0.00001), PIK3CA (18.8% vs. 6.6%, P<0.00001) and ERCC2 (14.8% vs. 2.5%, P<0.00001) were significantly higher in the SCBC group compared to SCLC. NOTCH1 mutations in SCLC were detected at a significantly higher rate of 15.7% vs. 6.0% in SCBC (P=0.002), without significant differences in the prevalence of NOTCH2–4 mutations. All detected genomic variants in SCBC, UC, and SCLC are shown in Figure 2.

This study represents one of the most extensive efforts to characterize SCBC to date, providing a genomic comparison with UC, given their shared organ of origin, and SCLC, given their similar treatment strategies and presumed biological similarity. Our analysis revealed both significant genomic overlap alongside potentially clinically meaningful as well as notable genomic differences, which could contribute to their divergent clinical behaviors. A significant proportion of SCBC tumors also had potentially actionable mutations that could inform future therapeutic strategies.

It is notable that the distribution of age, race, ethnicity, and smoking status was not significantly different between SCBC, UC, and SCLC groups. Most patients in all three groups were older, of male gender, and had either a prior or current smoking history, which likely reflects relatively similar occupational and environmental exposures linked to these diseases. However, gender was statistically significant between groups, with female gender depicting a higher prevalence of SCLC than UC and SCBC. Both SCBC and SCLC had a high frequency of co-occurring mutations in TP53 and RB1, which are a signature of poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas, including those arising from the bladder (23). This prevalence of co-occurring mutations has been described in SCLC cohorts, as well as prior smaller studies in SCBC, and likely reflects the underlying loss of G1-to-S phase cell cycle regulation, which has been described in small cell carcinoma irrespective of origin (24,25). Interestingly, co-occurring mutations in TP53 and RB1 were also enriched in the “neuronal” or “neuroendocrine-like” molecular subtypes within UC and correlated with aggressive disease course and worse prognosis, but also simultaneously higher responsiveness to ICI (26). In SCLC, ICI in combination with chemotherapy has already demonstrated improvement in overall survival in the front-line setting (27,28). Given these biological similarities seen between SCBC and SCLC in ours and other studies, clinical trials such as the South-west Oncology Group (SWOG) S2012 (NCT05058651) investigating combination chemo-immunotherapy in extrapulmonary neuroendocrine carcinoma, including SCBC, will play a crucial role in testing the hypothesis that these ICI-based combination strategies can improve treatment outcomes for patients diagnosed with this rare disease.

In addition to TP53 and RB1, several additional genomic alterations (e.g. TERT, KMT2D, ARID1A, CREBBP, PIK3CA, EP300) were noted in SCBC, which are also commonly found in UC (21). Both SCBC and UC had a similar high prevalence of TERT mutations (>70% in both), which was not observed in the SCLC cohort (6.1%). The prevalence of TERT alterations seen in our study is in line with that reported previously in UC, SCLC, and SCBC (23,29,30). Considering TERT alterations are early genomic events observed in both low- and high- grade bladder tumors, their concordant prevalence in UC and SCBC suggests similar clonal cells of origin for both histologic subtypes, as has previously been reported (31). The prevalence of clinically significant gene alterations was comparable between tissue- and blood-derived samples, suggesting that sample type does not introduce a confounding variable in detecting genomic alterations within our cohort.

Our analysis also revealed several additional genomic alterations of potential therapeutic significance. In contrast to prior studies, we found a significant proportion of SCBC tumors harboring actionable genomic alterations. Although FGFR-3 alterations were uncommon, any FGFR mutation was seen in 14% of patients. Considering the established efficacy of pan-FGFR inhibitors such as erdafitinib in UC and other tumor types, these findings warrant evaluation of FGFR inhibition in SCBC (32). HER(ERBB)-2 alterations were noted in 12.6% of SCBC tumors. Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) targeting HER-2 have demonstrated encouraging efficacy in HER-2 over-expressing tumors (33). Given the frequency of HER-2 alterations noted in our dataset, further evaluation of HER-2 expression and the role of HER-2 targeted ADCs in SCBC is warranted.

Additionally, a significant proportion of tumors had alterations in NOTCH 1–4. NOTCH signaling pathway is involved in tumor cell neuroendocrine differentiation, proliferation, and even chemoresistance (34). In a prior study of 110 SCLC samples, NOTCH family mutations were shown to have tumor-suppressive properties in SCLC (35). Lurbinectedin is approved after progression on first-line therapy for advanced SCLC, with a recent study showing that drug efficacy is in part driven by modulation of NOTCH activity (18,36). One case report of lurbinectedin in metastatic SCBC demonstrated a metabolic complete response after first-line platinum-based chemotherapy (37). Delta-like ligand 3 (DLL3), a ligand of the NOTCH signaling pathway, is characteristically overexpressed on the surface of tumor cells, making it an attractive therapeutic target (38). Several DLL3-targeting therapies, including antibody-drug conjugates, T-cell engagers, and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells, are currently under evaluation (38). These findings provide preliminary evidence of the NOTCH pathway as a therapeutic vulnerability for patients with SCBC harboring NOTCH1 mutations. Further investigation of these findings could yield clinical benefits in patients with SCBC and potentially other undifferentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas.

Limitations of this study include its retrospective nature. Furthermore, given that data is extracted from a large database of patients who have undergone genomic testing, there may be a bias towards those with disease presenting to centers where such testing is available and done routinely. Extensive clinical information such as ECOG performance status, clinical stage, and survival were limited to a small number of patients, preventing survival analysis adjusted for these clinical parameters. It is possible that amongst patients classified as having either UC or SCBC, an element of the other histology was present, given the high rates of co-occurrence of these diseases in patients, which could have influenced genomic findings (23,39). Lastly, the impact of these genomic alterations on oncologic outcomes with contemporary chemotherapy and/or immunotherapy could not be evaluated, which could add additional prognostic value, due to lack of data on treatment in the database.

This study yields the largest genomic characterization of SCBC to date, revealing a relatively unique genomic profile with significant overlap and differences between SCBC and UC. Importantly, these results indicate a significant proportion of SCBC tumors harbor clinically actionable alterations. Inclusion of SCBC in biomarker-selected tumor agnostic trials is imperative to further outcomes for this rare histologic variant of bladder cancer.
