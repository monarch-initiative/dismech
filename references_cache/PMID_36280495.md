# Mechanistic perspectives on anti-aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase syndrome.

Antisynthetase syndrome (ASSD) is an autoimmune disease characterized by 
circulating autoantibodies against one of eight aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases 
(aaRSs). Although these autoantibodies are believed to play critical roles in 
ASSD pathogenesis, the nature of their roles remains unclear. Here we describe 
ASSD pathogenesis and discuss ASSD-linked aaRSs - from the WHEP domain that may 
impart immunogenicity to the role of tRNA in eliciting the innate immune 
response and the secretion of aaRSs from cells. Through these explorations, we 
propose that ASSD pathogenesis involves the tissue-specific secretion of aaRSs 
and that extracellular tRNAs or tRNA fragments and their ability to engage 
Toll-like receptor signaling may be important disease factors.

Antisynthetase syndrome (ASSD; see Glossary) is an autoimmune disease named after the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (aaRSs). The clinical symptoms include inflammatory myopathies, polymyositis, dermatomyositis, interstitial lung diseases (ILD), polyarthritis, and Raynaud’s phenomenon [1-4]. The high incidence of ILD in ASSD makes it a potentially life-threatening condition. The hallmark of the disease is the serum titers of the autoantibodies against one of the aaRSs. To date, eight aaRSs have been described as the target of ASSD autoantibodies (Table 1). Among them, histidyl-tRNA synthetase (HisRS or HARS1), eliciting anti-Jo-1 antibodies (Abs), is the most common, followed by anti-PL-7 and anti-PL-12 targeting threonyl- and alanyl-tRNA synthetases (ThrRS or TARS1 and AlaRS or AARS1), respectively [5]. The titers of the serum anti-Jo-1 antibodies correlate with disease severity, suggesting a role of these autoantibodies in the pathogenesis and/or progression of ASSD [6, 7].

The aaRSs are a family of ancient enzymes involved in the establishment of the universal genetic code. Through a two-step reaction, the enzymes select and attach amino acids onto their cognate tRNAs, which can then be used to decode the genetic information stored in the mRNAs for translation (Figure 1A). Each of the proteinogenic amino acids has its own tRNA synthetase, making a total of 20 family members supporting protein synthesis in the cytoplasm [8, 9]. Interestingly, translocation of aaRSs from the cytosol to other cellular compartments, including the extracellular space and the nucleus, has been frequently observed in different cell types to allow aaRSs to perform functions beyond tRNA aminoacylation [10-14]. The translocations and the non-enzymatic functions of aaRSs are often enabled through the acquisition of new structural domains beyond their catalytic core during the evolution of eukaryotes [15, 16]. One of these domains (i.e., the WHEP domain) contains the major epitope for anti-HisRS (anti-Jo-1) antibody recognition and reportedly has various immunoregulatory activities [17-20].

The clinical and pathogenic features of ASSD have been extensively studied (and recently reviewed [5]), however, many aspects of the disease mechanism remain elusive. For example, is the involvement of aaRSs in autoimmunity unique from other RNA-binding proteins and why are some, but not all, aaRSs targeted by the autoantibodies? Based on recent findings on the secretion and extracellular functions of aaRS and tRNA [21], and the comparison with related diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), we attempt to derive some new perspectives on the disease mechanism of ASSD. We first introduce ASSD pathogenesis and describe aaRSs linked to ASSD, focusing in particular on the WHEP domain of HisRS. We then describe the relationship of ASSD and the innate immune response, including discussions of secreted aaRSs.

Multiple factors including genetic, immunological, and environmental factors contribute to the development of ASSD. Development of an autoimmune disease requires B and T lymphocytes to bypass self-tolerance. Adequate extracellular exposure of the autoantigen at the site of disease initiation is also necessary. ASSD is likely to initiate in muscle or lung tissues (Figure 1B). A triggering event for ASSD could be a viral or bacterial infection that elicits local innate immune reactions with inflammation, or exposure to certain drugs (such as statins [22]) or environmental factors (such as pollution or smoking [23]) that causes damage to muscle or lung tissues. The tissues then undergo a continuous process of repair and regeneration, during which the expression and secretion of autoantigens may be stimulated [24]. Tissue damage also activates the expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules on myofibers [25]. The overexpressed MHCs can aberrantly present an overexpressed self-antigen, such as a HisRS peptide, and consequently, stimulate autoreactive CD8+ T cells and their cytotoxicity.

The damaged or regenerating muscle cells and the activated immune cells may release certain aaRSs and expose them to the extracellular space [24]. The tissue damage and inflammation would also activate professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs), such as dendritic cells, to incorporate and present aaRS peptides with class II MHCs to activate a subset of aaRS-specific CD4+ T cells. This subset of CD4+ T cells can then help specific B-cells to proliferate and produce aaRS autoantibodies. The subsequent formation of antigen-antibody immune complexes further activates the immune system to perpetuate the process mounting to chronic inflammatory conditions (see below). A constant presence of extracellular aaRS-derived antigens may be provided through active secretion in specific tissues (see below). The circulating autoantibodies can also spread the inflammation to other tissues (Figure 1B).

Genetically, increases of HLA-B*08:01 (class I MHC) and HLA–DRB1*03 (class II MHC) and decrease of HLA–DRB1*07 (class II MHC) allele frequencies are strongly associated with ASSD [26]. Variations in cytokine genes have also been linked to ASSD susceptibility [27].

As protein synthesis is required for all cells to survive, aaRSs are ubiquitously expressed, which poses a striking contrast to the tissue-specific phenotypes of ASSD. Interestingly, the tissue specificity is strongly linked to the specific autoantibodies. For example, myositis is more common in anti-Jo-1 patients, whereas ILD is more frequent and severe in non-anti-Jo-1 ASSD patients [2, 28, 29]. This indicates that the target tissues have unique features that can shape the autoimmune response and/or the autoantigen has a special role in the target tissues. Nevertheless, ASSD patients share a spectrum of inflammatory symptoms in multiple tissues, indicating that ASSD-linked aaRSs may have unique features that are not shared with non-ASSD-linked aaRSs. Therefore, understanding the commonality of the ASSD-linked aaRSs and their collective uniqueness from the rest of the family may provide some insight into the selectivity.

According to their active site architecture, the 20 aaRSs are evenly divided into 2 classes (Table 1) [30]. Generally, class I aaRSs function as a monomer in charging tRNAs, whereas homodimer formation is required for enzymatic activity of class II aaRSs. The capacity to function as a homodimer allows class II aaRSs to have a shorter polypeptide chain compared with class I aaRSs. The exceptions in class I are TyrRS and TrpRS, which are dimers and the smallest aaRSs among both classes of aaRSs. AlaRS is the exception in class II, which can function as a monomer and has the longest polypeptide chain among class II aaRSs. PheRS is another exception and the only human aaRS with two different polypeptide chains and functions as an (αβ)2 tetrameter in charging tRNA.

From flies to humans, a conserved feature of cytoplasmic aaRSs is the formation of a multisynthetase complex (MSC) with 9 aaRSs and 3 nonenzymatic factors [31]. Interestingly, except for IleRS, all ASSD-linked aaRSs are not found in the MSC, suggesting mobility is an important factor for an aaRS to be an autoantigen. There is also a clear preference for class II, especially class IIa aaRSs as an autoantigen (Table 1). Further, it should be noted that class IIa (and class Ic) synthetases are also least associated with the MSC (Table 1). Collectively, ~85% of ASSD patients have autoantibodies against a class IIa aaRS (i.e., HisRS, ThrRS, AlaRS, and GlyRS) [5]. The subgroup classifications are based on sequence and structural similarities of the catalytic core, suggesting that the presence of certain shared structural and sequence motifs within the catalytic core may be another factor for an aaRS to be involved in ASSD. As one of the most ancient protein families, the catalytic core of an aaRS is evolutionarily conserved from bacteria to humans. This observation might support the molecular mimicry hypothesis, which suggested that the resemblance between autoantigens and bacterial or viral protein can evoke autoimmunity [32]. It is possible these shared motifs serve as the epitopes for T-cells and/or B-cells, at least during the initial phase of the immune response. Further activation of the immune system may lead to the expansion of epitopes to go beyond the conserved motifs. Martin et al. indicated that anti-Jo-1 sera react with at least three distinct regions in human HisRS, including the major epitope residing in the WHEP domain outside (see below) and two separate epitopes within the catalytic core [20].

A ThrRS/TARS1 homolog, TARS3, was recently discovered in higher eukaryotes. In contrast to ThrRS, TARS3 is associated with the MSC through an N-terminal extension that is absent in ThrRS [33]. Examining the remarkable early works of Mathews and his colleagues that characterized the anti-PL-7 autoantibodies [34], we conclude that the size of the protein pulled down by the autoantibodies corresponds to TARS1 (83 kD for ThrRS/TARS1 versus 93 kD for TARS3). Currently, we lack sufficient knowledge on the epitopes for anti-PL-7 to evaluate whether the autoantibodies can cross-react with TARS3. However, because TARS3 is much less abundant than TARS1 in all tissues (e.g., The TARS1/TARS3 ratio in mRNA level is about 55 and 3-fold in mouse lung and muscle tissues, respectively [33]), the free-standing TARS1 is likely the main target of the ASSD autoantibodies.

As the most predominant autoantigen in ASSD, HisRS has been the focus of disease mechanism studies. As stated above, the major epitope for HisRS autoantibodies was identified to be the WHEP domain [20], which is also found in GlyRS but no other ASSD-linked aaRSs [35]. The epitope for GlyRS autoantibodies however has not yet been identified. The WHEP domain is a small (~50 aa) structured motif with a helix-turn-helix conformation outside the catalytic core. It was first acquired as an N-terminal extension by HisRS and GlyRS at an early stage of eukaryotic evolution and eventually also appended to three other non-ASSD human aaRSs, including TrpRS (Box 1), MetRS, and GluProRS [16, 35].

Most B-cell epitopes are conformation-dependent and the helix-turn-helix (similar to a coiled-coil) conformation of the WHEP domain is an enriched motif among conformational B-cell epitopes [36]. The strong immunogenicity of the WHEP domain might also be related to its high solvent exposure. As an appended domain outside the catalytic core of the synthetase (composed of a catalytic domain and an anticodon-binding domain) (Figure 2A), the WHEP domain is not required for enzymatic activity. It is highly exposed and flexible, often disordered in the crystal structure [37]. Some conformational states of the WHEP domain may not have been tolerized during lymphocyte development, leading to the proliferation of autoreactive B cells in certain tissues or under certain conditions where alternative conformations of the WHEP domain exist. Indeed, Levine et al. found HisRS exists with a unique WHEP domain conformation in the lung [18]. Interestingly, this unique conformation of HisRS is also more susceptible to granzyme B cleavage that releases the WHEP domain (aa 1-48) from the synthetase [18] (Figure 2A). A splice variant of HisRS encoding the WHEP domain plus some extra sequences at the C-terminus (aa 1-60) (Figure 2A) was identified in human skeletal muscle and found to be highly enriched in human lung compared with other tissues [17]. These processes that generate different forms of the WHEP domain as stand-alone proteins may create potential non-tolerized cryptic epitopes that can lead to autoimmunity.

The WHEP domain of HisRS may also contain epitopes for T cell activation. A 13-mer peptide from the WHEP domain (HisRS11-23) was predicted to contain two HLA–DRB1*03:01 (class II MHC) binding motifs. Indeed, CD4+ T cells specific for HisRS11-23 were identified in the blood and lung of myositis patients [19].

The fact that the major epitope (WHEP domain) in HisRS is not shared by most other ASSD-linked aaRSs supports the unique prominence of anti-Jo-1 antibodies (66%) in ASSD patients. Although the WHEP domain is a key antigenic determinant for HisRS, it is neither sufficient nor necessary for an aaRS to be involved in ASSD [38]. As noted above, two other top ASSD autoantigens, AlaRS and ThrRS, do not have a WHEP domain and two other WHEP-domain containing aaRSs, GluProRS and MetRS (both are components of MSC), are not involved in ASSD or other autoimmune diseases [38]. While GlyRS also has a WHEP domain [35], the prevalence of anti-EJ antibodies is relatively low (3%), suggesting the unique sequence of HisRS WHEP domain and/or its immunoregulatory functions (see below) that may not be shared by other aaRSs are key factors for autoantibody production.

Compared with other well-known and related autoimmune diseases, such as SLE, ASSD is more restricted in both clinical features and circulating autoantibodies (Table 2). In contrast to ASSD, autoantibodies targeting more than 180 autoantigens were reported in SLE. Among them, antinuclear antibodies (e.g., anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm/RNP, and anti-Ro/La) are most common, and antibodies against RNA-associated proteins (e.g., U1 snRNP) are clinically most relevant [39].

Both B cell and T cell activation against self-antigens require two distinct signals. The first activation signal (signal 1) occurs upon antigen binding to B cell receptors (BCRs) or antigen presented by MHCs binding to T cell receptors (TCRs). The second activation signal (signal 2) for breaking self-tolerance may be provided by RNA signaling through a toll-like receptor (TLR) (Figure 3A). Recently, TLR7 was shown to be a key upstream driver of SLE [40]. Because TLR7 is activated by pathogen- or host-derived single-stranded RNAs, the study highlights the importance of RNA in SLE pathogenesis.

Consistent with the findings demonstrating the critical involvement of TLR7 in SLE [40], many studies highlighted the importance of innate immunity in HisRS-immunization-induced myositis [41]. For example, immunization with HisRS (in the absence of adjuvant) induced myositis in RAG2−/− mice with deficient antigen-specific B-cell and T-cell responses [42]. However, the myositis phenotype was markedly reduced in TLR2−/− and TLR4−/− double knockout mice [43]. The recombinant HisRS proteins used in these studies were purified from E. coli overexpression and contained endotoxin, which was considered to contribute to the initiation of the myositis and the activation of MyD88-dependent signaling downstream of the TLRs [43]. The endotoxin possibly acted as signal 2 to provide the co-stimulation required for breaking self-tolerance. It is feasible to speculate that in patients with ASSD, the required co-stimulation signal may be provided by endogenous agents, such as aaRS-associated tRNA or tRNA fragments, also acting through TLR(s) (see below) (Figure 3B).

It is important to note that autoantibody generation per se may not cause ASSD. Anti-Jo-1 antibodies are found in healthy people and non-ASSD patients [44, 45]. Consistently, mice injected with murine HisRS, or its isolated WHEP domain, in the presence of an adjuvant, were able to break tolerance and develop high-titer anti-HisRS antibodies. However, no obvious myositis and lung phenotypes were observed until the mice were challenged with toxins administered into the muscle or the lung [46]. In another mouse model, HisRS autoantibodies were generated with intramuscular injection of a fusion protein of MBP and the first 151 aa of murine HisRS (including the WHEP domain). However, the autoantibody production was not sustainable over time, and the local inflammation at the injected site did not develop into systemic inflammation unless an endosomal TLR7/8 agonist (R-848) was co-injected with the HisRS antigen [47]. Sciorati et al. showed that type I interferon (IFN) (downstream of TLR7/8 activation) was responsible for most actions of R-848, including prolonged autoantibody generation, spreading of muscle inflammatory reaction, and muscle fiber MHC class I overexpression [47]. These results suggested a role for TLR7/8 in mediating HisRS autoantibody-induced toxicity and some mechanistic similarities between ASSD and SLE.

Endogenous activation of TLR7/8 involves single-stranded RNA. Independently, using sera or purified IgG from ASSD patients who were positive for anti-Jo-1 to combine with necrotic cell material, Eloranta et al. showed that the immune complexes formed with anti–Jo-1 antibodies can induce type I IFN production in an RNA-dependent manner [48]. Earlier work by Mathews and colleagues demonstrated that ASSD autoantibodies (including anti-Jo-1) can co-immunoprecipitate their cognate tRNA and proposed that the autoimmune response is elicited by RNA-bound antigens [1, 34]. Some anti-AlaRS (anti-PL-12) autoantibodies can even directly recognize tRNAAla [49, 50].

Because TLR7/8 are predominantly located on endosomes, we speculate that the immune complex of anti-Jo-1/HisRS in association with tRNA (or tRNA fragment) can bind to Fcγ receptors (FcγR) of immune cells and be internalized to endosomes, where the tRNA can activate TLR7/8 (Figure 3C). Interestingly, Pawar et al. recently identified a 5’-half molecule of tRNAHisGUG that is generated under mycobacterial infection and selectively released from cultured human monocyte-derived macrophages via extracellular vesicles (EVs) [21]. The EV-associated tRNA fragment can be delivered into endosomes in recipient cells and activate endosomal TLR7 [21]. Selective tRNA fragments are also enriched in EVs released during T cell activation [51]. The most abundant tRNA fragments identified from EVs of C2C12 mouse myoblasts also include tRNAHisGUG [52]. A similar 5’-fragment of tRNAHisGUG was found to be significantly upregulated in SLE patient sera without lupus nephritis (LN) and in the serum-derived EVs of SLE patients with LN, compared with healthy controls [53]. The fact that this tRNAHis fragment is cognate to the most prominent autoantigen in ASSD is surely interesting and warrants further investigations to understand whether this or other tRNA fragments are enriched in ASSD patient sera and involved in ASSD pathogenesis.

An important aspect in eliciting immune responses against an autoantigen is its extracellular accessibility. Secretion of aaRS was first reported for TyrRS from human histiocytic lymphoma U-937 cells [10]. Later, many other ASSD-associated aaRSs, including HisRS, ThrRS, GlyRS, and AlaRS [10, 11, 13, 17, 46, 54, 55], were found to be secreted out of various cells. These observations prompt the question of whether there is a connection between the secretion of aaRSs from certain cells and tissues and their selective involvement with ASSD. Interestingly, when human skeletal muscle cells (HSkMCs) were treated with IGF-1, a stimulator of muscle growth and regeneration, secretion of HisRS, but not MetRS (a MSC component and not known to be associated with ASSD), was detected [46]. As the expression of HisRS was shown to increase in regenerating muscle cells in the lung of myositis patients [24], HisRS secretion is likely in turn stimulated. Activated immune cells (such as macrophages) were also found to secrete certain ASSD-associated aaRSs (e.g., GlyRS [13] and TyrRS [56]).

Because aaRSs do not have signal peptides that can direct them to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-Golgi secretory pathway, they are released from cells through non-classical mechanisms, and multiple lines of evidence suggested aaRSs are secreted from cells via EVs [11, 57]. For example, we showed that the secretion GlyRS from C2C12 myocytes is not affected by brefeldin A, an inhibitor of classical secretion but by GW4869 [58], a sphingomyelinase inhibitor, which blocks exosome biogenesis and release [11]. Remarkably, Kim and colleagues provided clear evidence that GlyRS secreted from macrophages was anchored to the surface of EVs [57], allowing for extracellular interactions and regulation of immune cells [59].

The above-mentioned studies usually only focused on one or few aaRSs. To gain an unbiased assessment of the selectivity of aaRS secretion in ASSD-relevant cells, we also searched for mass spectrometry-based secretome analysis of human muscle cells and macrophages [60-62]. In one of the studies, proteins in the exosomes were also analyzed [60]. Interestingly, most aaRSs detected from the secretome/exosome of human differentiating myoblasts and virus-infected primary macrophages are associated with ASSD or other autoimmune diseases (Table 1). We speculate that tissue-specific secretion of certain aaRSs (and tRNAs) may contribute to their selective involvement in ASSD.

In addition to physiological secretion, aaRS release might also be enhanced by tissue damage and subsequent inflammation triggered by environmental factors. The respiratory system is especially sensitive to harmful environments, such as air pollution and tobacco smoking, which can trigger cell death and lead to the leakage of intracellular molecules that are recognized as alien by the immune system [63]. Therefore, both active secretion and tissue damage-induced cellular leakage can supply extracellular aaRSs, perhaps in different forms (i.e., full length versus fragments, with or without tRNA and/or EV associations) (Figure 2C). So far, most functional studies of extracellular aaRSs were performed with purified proteins (or fragments) and not in association with tRNA or EVs.

In principle, an active role of aaRS in immune regulation is not necessary to explain its ASSD association. Sufficient extracellular exposure with a possible nucleic acid association in a particular configuration could be all that is needed for an aaRS to participate in an aberrant immune response in susceptible populations. However, growing evidence suggests that extracellular aaRSs are involved in regulating the immune system and such involvement may be related to their ASSD association. For example, Howard et al. found HisRS (as well as another ASSD antigen, AsnRS) has proinflammatory activities and can induce migration of CD4+ and CD8+ lymphocytes, activated monocytes, and immature dendritic cells (iDCs) through interaction with the extracellular domain of CC chemokine receptor CCR5 [64]. These proinflammatory activities of HisRS may contribute to breaking the self-tolerance of B and T cells. Plenty of independent evidence also suggested that other aaRSs, including HisRS, TyrRS, and TrpRS, can directly or indirectly activate TLR2/4 [14, 43, 56, 65, 66] and trigger proinflammatory responses. As mentioned above, it is possible that aaRS-bound endotoxin may contribute to the activation of TLR2/4 in some studies. Further, recent efforts to overexpress aaRSs with ClearColi, a genetically modified E. coli strain with altered lipopolysaccharide (LPS) that does not cause an endotoxic response in human cells [56], might help to distinguish aaRS-specific activity and the potential contribution from endotoxin, as well as to investigate the possible role of tRNA in mediating the proinflammatory activities of aaRSs.

Unexpectedly, in a recent report, Adams et al. demonstrated immune modulation rather than proinflammatory activities for HisRS [46]. HisRS inhibits CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell activation also in a WHEP-domain-dependent manner (Figure 2B). In mouse and rodent models of acute inflammatory diseases, HisRS administration downregulates immune cell infiltration and proinflammatory cytokine expression. Because the WHEP domain is the major epitope for anti-Jo-1 antibodies [20, 67], Adams et al. suggested that the sequestration of the WHEP domain contributes to the morbidity of the anti-Jo-1-positive ASSD (Figure 3D). Subsequently, Neuropilin-2 (NRP2) was identified as the receptor for the WHEP domain of HisRS (https://atyrpharma.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/2020ATS_ZX-Poster_Final.pdf), mediating its immune suppressive activity (Figure 2B). NRP2 and its close homolog NRP1 are multifunctional receptors expressed in various immune cells and play important roles in immune regulation [68]. Interestingly, the binding of the WHEP domain is specific for NRP2 but not observed with NRP1. The extracellular region of NRPs contains two N-terminal CUB domains (C1r/C1s, uEGF, bone morphogenetic protein; also known as a1 and a2), two coagulation factor V/VIII homology domains (F5/8; also known as b1 and b2), and a single MAM domain (meprin, A5, and μ-phosphatase; also known as c). Domain swapping studies in which individual domains of cell-surface-expressed NRP2 were replaced with the corresponding, structurally similar domain from NRP1 demonstrated that b1 is the key domain for binding, while the a2 and b2 domains likely also provide contacts with the WHEP domain [68].

In Howard et al., the WHEP domain was also shown to recapitulate the pro-inflammatory activities of HisRS [64]. The WHEP domain construct used in this study is comprised of aa 1-48, corresponding to the cleavage product by granzyme B (Figure 2B). In contrast, the HisRS WHEP domain showing immunomodulatory activity was based on the splice variant [17], which has a C-end-rule peptide (K57TPK60) at the C-terminus (Figure 2B). A C-end-rule peptide (K/RXXK/R) is well known for mediating NRP interaction [69]. Therefore, it is possible that the pro- versus anti-inflammatory activity of the HisRS WHEP domain is dictated by how the WHEP domain is generated and its C-terminal sequence (Figure 2A, 2B). While the released HisRS (with or without bound tRNA or tRNA fragment) and its granzyme B cleavage product may promote the activation of the immune system through CCR5 and other receptors, different forms of HisRS, such as the HisRS1-60 splice variant, may also be produced to counteract the immune activation through NRP-mediated immune suppressive activities (Figure 2B). However, the existence of the anti-Jo-1 antibodies may tilt the balance toward overactivation of the immune system through immune complex formation on one hand (Figure 3C) and, on the other hand, neutralizing the immune suppressive activity of certain aaRS forms (Figure 3D). Therefore, autoantibody-mediated dysregulation of extracellular aaRS activities may be a critical component of ASSD pathogenesis.

A fusion protein comprised of human IgG1 Fc and the HisRS WHEP domain (aa 1-60), named ATYR1923 or Efzofitimod (Figure 2B), was shown to reduce inflammation and fibrosis in animal models of ILD (https://atyrpharma.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/2019-ATS-Pharmacology-Campaign-Summary-FINAL.pdf). Efzofitimod was also evaluated in a placebo-controlled, double-blinded Phase 1b/2a study in anti-Jo-1-negative patients with pulmonary sarcoidosis, a major form of ILD. Dose-dependent benefits of Efzofitimod were indicated on several endpoints, including steroid reduction, lung function, clinical symptoms, and inflammatory biomarkers (https://atyrpharma.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/ATS-2022_Sporn-et-al_Efzofitimod-ATYR1923-C-002-Clinical-Data_Final.pdf). These results support the immunosuppressive activity of a HisRS-derived biologic and provide the first clinical proof-of-concept for an aaRS-based therapy.

There is enough evidence to suggest that the presence of anti-aaRS autoantibodies in ASSD is not an epiphenomenon, but closely linked to the disease mechanism. However, our understanding of the role of the ASSD autoantibodies is limited and we do not know why a selective set of aaRS family members is involved (see Outstanding Questions). By analyzing the molecular features of ASSD-linked aaRSs, we conclude that they are usually not associated with the MSC and are most likely to be a free-standing class IIa aaRS. By mining mass spectrometry-based secretome analysis, we found an apparent correlation between autoimmune disease-involvement of an aaRS and its secretion from human muscle cells and macrophages. A comparative review of current knowledge on ASSD and SLE, together with recent findings of tRNA as a potential endogenous TLR agonist, led us to propose that extracellular tRNAs/tRNA fragments and their ability to engage TLR-signaling may be important disease factors. By scrutinizing studies showing either pro- or anti-inflammatory activities of the HisRS WHEP domain, we suspect that the opposing immune-regulatory activities are dictated by the different C-termini of the constructs used in the studies. Each form can be generated through a natural mechanism and represents the diverse immune-regulatory activities embedded in an aaRS. The recent development of Efzofitimod highlights the potential in exploring these activities for treating inflammatory diseases. We hope this perspective piece has provided some insights that would guide future studies to elucidate the ASSD mechanism and in turn reveal new therapeutic strategies for the disease.
