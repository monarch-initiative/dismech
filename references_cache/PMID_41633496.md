# Loss of Zmiz1 in Mice Leads to Impaired Cortical Development and Autistic-Like Behaviors.
**Authors:** K C R, Patel NR, Thurmon A, Kantor BL, Lorino MG, Tiemroth AS, Morrison V, Akumuo M, Shenoy A, Blanco-Fernandez X, Baccini V, Siddika MA, Gu C, Meadows SM, Galazo MJ
**Journal:** Biol Psychiatry (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.biopsych.2026.01.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2026.01.014)

## Content

BACKGROUND: De novo mutations in transcriptional regulators are emerging as key risk factors contributing to the etiology of neurodevelopmental disorders. Human genetic studies have recently identified ZMIZ1 and its de novo mutations as a cause of a neurodevelopmental syndrome strongly associated with intellectual disability, autism, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, microcephaly, and other developmental anomalies. However, the role of ZMIZ1 in brain development or how ZMIZ1 mutations cause neurological phenotypes is unknown.

METHODS: We generated forebrain-specific Zmiz1 mutant mice (Zmiz1-knockout) to assess ZMIZ1 function in cortical development. Neural progenitors, excitatory neurons, and glia were assessed using immunolabeling. Neuron-specific reconstruction was applied to callosal projection neurons to analyze dendritic arborization and projection through the corpus callosum. Behavioral tests assessed motor activity, anxiety, communication, and social interactions. RNA sequencing at multiple developmental stages and chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) revealed molecular pathways and targets regulated by ZMIZ1.

RESULTS: Loss of ZMIZ1 led to cortical microcephaly, corpus callosum dysgenesis, and abnormal differentiation of upper-layer cortical neurons. Zmiz1-knockout mice showed alterations in motor activity, anxiety, communication, and social interactions with strong sex differences, resembling phenotypes associated with autism. Zmiz1 mutation led to transcriptomic changes disrupting neurogenesis, neuron differentiation programs, and synaptic signaling. We identified Zmiz1-mediated downstream regulation of key neurodevelopmental genes, including Lhx2, Auts2, and EfnB2. Importantly, reactivation of the ephrin-B2 pathway rescued the dendritic outgrowth deficits in Zmiz1 mutant cortical neurons.

CONCLUSIONS: Our in vivo findings provide insight into Zmiz1 function in cortical development and reveal mechanistic underpinnings of ZMIZ1 syndrome, thereby providing valuable information for future studies on this neurodevelopmental disorder.

Neurodevelopment is a tightly controlled multistep process, including neuron generation, differentiation, wiring, and synaptic formation. Dysregulation at any stage can lead to neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs). Transcription factors, transcription coregulators, and chromatin remodelers have emerged as critical orchestrators of neurodevelopment and major risk factors for NDDs (1). However, the contribution of mutations, particularly de novo mutations, in these regulatory factors to NDDs remains poorly understood (2–6).

Recent human genetic studies have linked de novo loss-of-function mutations in ZMIZ1 to a neurodevelopmental syndrome strongly associated with intellectual disability (ID), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), microcephaly, and other developmental anomalies, including motor, sensory, and communication deficits; seizures; anxiety; emotional dysregulation; and facial and skeletal dysmorphisms (7–13). Single nucleotide variations in a ZMIZ1 enhancer have been identified as ASD risk factors (6), underscoring the link between ZMIZ1, neurodevelopment, and ASD. Zmiz1 functions as a transcriptional co-activator for important developmental pathways, including Notch1 (14), androgen receptor (15), p53 (16,17), and Smad3/4 (18,19), and interacts with the SWI/SNF-like BAF chromatin remodeling complex (20). Despite its strong association with NDDs, the role of ZMIZ1 in neurodevelopment and how ZMIZ1 mutations contribute to ASD risk and NDDs remain unknown.

Abnormal cortical formation and connectivity are hallmarks of complex NDDs and neurological phenotypes (2,20). In humans and mice, Zmiz1 expression is strong in the embryonic cortex and decreases postnatally (21,22). Zmiz1 cortical expression, its roles in transcriptional control, and its link to NDDs suggest that it plays an important role in cortical development; however, its function and molecular targets in the cortex have not been studied.

Here, we generated a forebrain-specific Zmiz1 mutant mouse model to investigate its role in cortical development, examining cortical morphology, behavioral outcomes, and dysregulated molecular pathways. Loss of Zmiz1 in the cortex leads to cortical microcephaly by decreasing progenitor proliferation and neurogenesis. Also, loss of Zmiz1 causes corpus callosum (CC) dysgenesis, a phenotype frequently observed in NDDs. Focusing on layer 2/3 (L2/3) callosal projection neurons, we found that Zmiz1 regulates their differentiation, affecting axonal and dendritic morphology. Behaviorally, Zmiz1-deficient mice exhibit alterations resembling aspects of human ZMIZ1-associated neurodevelopmental phenotypes. These phenotypes emerge at early postnatal stages and persist into adulthood; manifest differently by sex; and include deficits in communication, motor activity, anxiety, sensory gating, and social interactions.

By integrating transcriptomic and Zmiz1–chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) analyses across multiple developmental stages, we identified transcriptional downstream targets regulated by Zmiz1, including Auts2, Lhx2, EphA5, Unc5D, and EfnB2 (ephrin-B2). Importantly, reactivation of the Eph-ephrin pathway rescues the dendritic growth deficits in Zmiz1 mutant cortical neurons.

Collectively, our findings reveal essential roles for Zmiz1 in cortical development and illuminate molecular mechanisms underlying the ZMIZ1 neurodevelopmental syndrome, thereby deepening our understanding of the complex interplay between molecular processes and NDDs.

Adult mice express Zmiz1 throughout the brain, with the highest expression in the cortex and cerebellum. Zmiz1 is expressed across cell types, including neurons, oligodendrocytes, microglia, astrocytes, and endothelial cells (21,26). Although previous studies identified Zmiz1 as an early cortical developmental gene (27), its function in the cortex is unknown. To understand the role of Zmiz1 in cortical development, we investigated its expression using single-cell transcriptomic atlases of the developing cortex (22,26,28) and immunolabeling (Figure S1). Zmiz1 messenger RNA is highly expressed during embryonic cortical development in radial glia, apical and basal progenitors, and postmitotic neurons (Figure S1A–D). Postnatally, expression persists in neurons and astrocytes (Figure S1E, F). Cortical expression decreases from embryonic to postnatal stages (21,22,27) (Figure S1G, H).

To investigate the role of Zmiz1 in cortical development, we generated a conditional Zmiz1 mutant (Emx1-Cre;Zmiz1fl/fl—hereafter Zmiz1-knockout [Zmiz1-KO]). Efficient deletion was confirmed via ZMIZ1 immunostaining (Figure S2A, B). To assess morphological consequences of Zmiz1 deletion, we measured dorsal cortical surface area and midline length on P1, P3, P7, P14, and P21 in Zmiz1-KOs and controls (Zmiz1fl/fl) (Figure 1A–C). Zmiz1-KOs displayed reduced cortical surface area beginning on P7. Cerebellar surface was not affected, as expected in a nonmutated area (Figure S2C, D). Midline length was significantly decreased from P1 (Figure 1C). To evaluate whether cortical thickness and laminar organization were affected, we performed immunostaining for TBR1, CTIP2, and CUX1, key development markers of neurons in L6, L5, and upper layers (L2/3 and L4), respectively. Although the overall laminar pattern was preserved, both total cortical thickness and thickness of individual layers were reduced in Zmiz1-KOs (Figure 1E). When normalized to total cortical thickness, only L6 thickness was significantly reduced (Figure 1G); therefore, the Zmiz1-KO cortex was thinner overall compared with controls, but L6 was significantly more reduced. Neuron numbers (TBR1+, CTIP2+, and CUX1+) were significantly decreased per cortical column (Figure 1F), consistent with the observed cortical thinning, but neuronal densities within each layer remained unchanged (Figure 1H). Overall, Zmiz1 mutation resulted in smaller cortices with reduced surface area, thickness, and number of neurons in all layers.

Given the association between cortical malformations such as microcephaly and impaired neurogenesis (29), we examined whether Zmiz1 regulates progenitor proliferation and neurogenesis. We determined whether the number of progenitors is affected in Zmiz1-KO cortices. On E13.5, we labeled radial glia progenitors using phospho-vimentin (pVim), and at E15.5, we labeled apical progenitors (APs), SOX2+, and intermediate progenitors (IPs), TBR2+ (Figure 2A, G). We found significantly fewer pVim+, SOX2+, and TBR2+ progenitors in Zmiz1-KOs (Figure 2C, H, I). Importantly, no differences in cell death were observed (Figure 2A, B, G, J), indicating that progenitor loss was not due to increased cell death.

To assess progenitor proliferative activity, we performed BrdU birthdating at E12.5, targeting APs during deep-layer neurogenesis, and on E14.5, targeting IPs during upper-layer neuron production. We analyzed the number of BrdU+ cells and the fraction of BrdU+ progenitors no longer undergoing mitosis (quit fraction; BrdU+Ki67−) 24 hours after each BrdU pulse. Zmiz1-KOs had significantly fewer BrdU+ cells at both stages (E13.5, E15.5), indicating reduced cell proliferation (Figure 2D–F, K–M). Quit fraction at E13.5 was elevated, which reduced the progenitor pool throughout corticogenesis. We then tracked the fate of BrdU-labeled cells in the postnatal cortex. On P7, Zmiz1-KOs showed a significant reduction in BrdU+ neurons born at E12.5 and E15.5 in deep and upper layers, respectively (Figure 2N–S). Despite reduced numbers, BrdU+ neurons were correctly positioned according to their birthdates, indicating that migration was not disrupted. However, the reduction in progenitors and their proliferative capacity from early stages of corticogenesis resulted in less neuron production and cortical microcephaly in Zmiz1-KO brains.

The reduced midline length in Zmiz1-KO brains (Figure 1) suggests abnormal development of midline structures. The CC is a midline structure frequently affected in NDDs, particularly in ID and/or ASD (30–34). Abnormalities in the CC and midline structures have been reported in patients with ZMIZ1 mutations (9,12,13). Therefore, we investigated whether the CC and surrounding midline structures were affected in Zmiz1-KOs.

First, we assessed the CC structure, length, area, and callosal fiber orientation. The CC area and length were significantly reduced in Zmiz1-KOs compared with controls (Figure 3A–E). Also, callosal axons were abnormally oriented. Axons coiled in the cortical white matter, forming Probst bundles, and extended ectopically into the septum, and many failed to cross the midline (Figure 3H–M and Figure S3; Supplemental Videos S1–S4).

Callosal dysgenesis may result from abnormal development of either callosal neurons, midline structures guiding callosal axons, or both (35,36). We evaluated whether midline structures, namely, the glial wedge, midline glial zipper (MGZ), glial sling (GS), and indusium griseum (IG), are compromised. Immunolabeling with GFAP on P7 revealed a significant reduction in astroglial populations in Zmiz1-KOs, particularly in the MGZ and GS, at the boundary between the CC and septum (Figure 3F, G). GFAP+ astroglia were asymmetrically distributed across the midline, indicating disorganized glial architecture (Figure 3F, G). The IG, at the dorsal border of the CC, was also disrupted (Figure S4). IG neurons, revealed by TBR1 and CTIP2 markers, failed to form a proper boundary separating CC axons and the interhemispheric fissure (Figure S4A–F). These data confirm that multiple midline structures were malformed in Zmiz1-KO mice.

To assess the contribution of midline astroglia to the CC dysgenesis, we performed astrocyte-specific deletion of Zmiz1 using GFAP-Cre. Astrocyte-specific deletion resulted in disorganization of astrocytes at midline but not in stark structural alterations of the CC (Figure 3N–R). This strongly suggests that the callosal dysgenesis arises primarily from Zmiz1 loss in neurons rather than glia.

Because L2/3 neurons contribute the majority of callosal axons (37), we focused on this population to investigate Zmiz1 functions in neuron differentiation. In differentiating N2a cells, Zmiz1 small interfering RNA (siRNA)–mediated knockdown significantly reduced neurite outgrowth (Figure S5), and in Zmiz1-KO mice, callosal axons developed abnormally (Figure 3H–K and Figure S3). Next, we examined the effect of Zmiz1 loss of function in dendritic development. We sparsely labeled L2/3 neurons via IUE of a GFP (green fluorescent protein) construct in Zmiz1-KO and control embryos on E14.5, followed by dendritic reconstruction and Sholl analysis on P14. Zmiz1-KO L2/3 neurons displayed markedly impaired apical dendritic development, with reduced length, branching, and complexity, especially in distal segments, indicating a reduced apical tuft (Figure 3S–X). In contrast, basal dendrites were largely unaffected (Figure S4G–J). This phenotype was recapitulated in Cre-electroporated neurons from Zmiz1fl/fl embryos, confirming a role for Zmiz1 in apical dendritic growth in L2/3 neurons (Figure S4K–P). Collectively, these data indicate that loss of Zmiz1 leads to callosal dysgenesis, primarily through abnormal differentiation of L2/3 neurons, resulting in aberrant formation of axonal projections and dendritic arbors.

To determine whether cortical deletion of Zmiz1 in mice leads to behavioral alterations relevant to the developmental delays, ID, ASD, and ADHD present in humans with ZMIZ1 mutations, we performed behavioral assessment of Zmiz1-KO mice. We examined behaviors associated with these disorders, including locomotor activity, anxiety-like behaviors, sensory gating, social interactions, and communication (Figure 4 and Figure S6). Given known sex differences in these behaviors in mouse models of NDDs (38–41), we assessed males and females independently.

Locomotor activity and anxiety-related behaviors were evaluated using the open field test and elevated plus maze (42). Zmiz1-KO males and females traveled more distance in the open field compared with controls (Figure 4A, B, E, F). However, only Zmiz1-KO males exhibited increased movement in the outer zone (Figure 4B–F), spending more time moving there and less time in the inner zone (Figure 4C, G). Males also showed more frequent episodes of stereotypies in the outer zone, a pattern not observed in females (Figure 4D, H). Anxiety assessment via the elevated plus maze revealed that Zmiz1-KO males spent less time exploring open arms and displayed fewer exploratory behaviors, such as head dips (Figure 4I, J, N, O), indicating heightened anxiety. These findings indicate increased locomotor activity across sexes and elevated anxiety, specifically in males.

We assessed sensory gating using the acoustic startle/prepulse inhibition (PPI) test. Zmiz1-KO males showed significantly less PPI in response to lower prepulse levels, while females exhibited deficits at both lower and intermediate levels (Figure 4K, P), indicating mild sensory gating impairments in both sexes. Using the T-maze, we found no differences in working memory in Zmiz1-KOs of either sex compared with controls (Figure S6A).

Social behavior, often disrupted in NDDs and patients with ZMIZ1 mutations (7–9,13), was tested using the 3-chamber assay. We evaluated social preference and novelty. Male and female Zmiz1-KOs and controls interacted more with a conspecific than with an object, demonstrating normal social preference (Figure 4L, Q). Interestingly, Zmiz1-KO males showed increased interaction with conspecifics compared with controls (Figure 4L). In tests of social novelty preference, Zmiz1-KO males favored novel mice over familiar ones and spent more time with novel conspecifics than control males (Figure 4L). Conversely, Zmiz1-KO females lacked the typical preference for novel conspecifics observed in controls (Figure 4Q).

Delayed speech onset and deficits in communication are universal in patients with ZMIZ1 variants (7–11,13). We examined early communication deficits by analyzing ultrasonic vocalization (USV) during the early postnatal period (P4–P10). Zmiz1-KO males emitted fewer USVs than controls on P4, P5, P7, and P9, while females showed a significant decrease only on P5 (Figure 4M, R and Figure S6B). Also, Zmiz1-KO males produced a less diverse repertoire of syllables compared with controls and females (Figure S6C–F). These vocalization deficits were not due to respiratory or physical impairments, as pups showed normal weight and unaffected USV parameters, and the mutation was forebrain specific (Figure S6G–J).

Collectively, sex-dependent behavioral alterations in Zmiz1-KOs emerge during early postnatal stages and persist into adulthood. Importantly, the observed deficits in communication, motor activity, anxiety, sensory gating, and social interactions are consistent with the neurodevelopmental phenotypic spectrum of patients with ZMIZ1 mutations.

We examined gene expression changes induced by the Zmiz1 mutation in cortical E15.5 progenitors, immature cortical neurons (P1), and mature cortical neurons (P14) via RNA-seq (Figure 5A, E, I). Analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed numerous upregulated and downregulated genes in E15.5 Zmiz1-KO progenitors (E15.5 DEGs), P1 Zmiz1-KO neurons (P1 DEGs), and P14 Zmiz1-KO neurons (P14 DEGs) (Figure S7). DEGs from these 3 datasets substantially overlapped with DEGs in P7 whole cortex from a previous study (21) (Figure S8). Gene ontology (GO) analysis in upregulated and downregulated DEGs revealed neuron development as a broadly affected process in all 3 populations (Figure S9). Axon guidance, axonogenesis, neuron projection guidance/development, neurogenesis, and neuron differentiation were processes also affected in E15.5 progenitors and P1 neurons (Figure 5B, F, J). Interestingly, P14 DEGs were heavily associated with synaptic transmission and signaling (Figure 5J). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analysis showed alteration in processes associated with synaptic transmission at different stages (Figure S10). The reactome pathway shows EPH-ephrin signaling and other protein-protein interactions at synapses as affected processes. The Jensen Compartment subcellular location database shows dendrite/dendritic tree, axon/axon collateral, and synapse/presynapse/postsynapse as the compartments preferentially associated with P14 DEGs (Figure S10).

We found comparable changes in expression on E15.5 and P1 in axon development/neuron differentiation DEGs. Notably, Lhx2, EfnB2, Sema3c, Epha5, Tbr1, Robo2, Cntn2, Unc5d, and Tiam2 were axon development/neuron differentiation DEGs downregulated in E15.5 progenitors and P1 neurons, suggesting that Zmiz1 may regulate these genes throughout early developmental stages (Figure 5C, D). Neuron development DEGs such as Epha5, Tbr1, Cdh4, Unc5a, Sema3a, Plxna4, Neurod2, and Negr1 were downregulated in P1 and P14 neurons (Figure 5G, H). Interestingly, P14 DEGs were highly enriched in synaptic transmission/signaling genes, including Drd1/2, Gabbr1/2, Gabbra2, Grin1/2a/2d/3a, Gria1/4, Grid1, Grm1/2/3/5/6, Homer1, Mef2c, Htr2c, Nrxn1/3, Reln, Shank2, Nlgn1/2, Snca, Syp, and Syt1/2/4/5/7 (Figure 5K). Overall, this transcriptomic analysis suggests that Zmiz1 regulates expression of genes controlling neuron differentiation early in development and synaptic signaling genes later in development.

We performed correlation analysis between DEGs and ASD (SFARI) and ID (43) risk gene databases (Figure S11). A significant number of Zmiz1-KO DEGs in E15.5 progenitors, P1 neurons, and P14 neurons were disease risk factors for ASD and ID (Figure S11B–D). Five genes, AUTS2, PLCB1, SATB2, SYP, and TBR1, mapped to all 3 datasets (SFARI, ID risk, and Zmiz1-KO DEGs) on E15.5 (Figure S11B). Four genes, AUTS2, FOXP1, LAMA1, and TBR1, mapped to the 3 datasets on P1 (Figure S11C), and 42 genes, including CNTNAP2, GRIA3, GRIN1, GRIN21, NRXN1, RELN, SATB2, SHANK2, SYNGAP1, and TBR1, mapped to the 3 datasets on P14 (Figure S11D).

GO analyses revealed strong involvement of Zmiz1-KO DEGs in neuron development, differentiation, and synaptic transmission. Alterations in neuron differentiation and synaptic communication are thought to produce excitatory/inhibitory imbalance, leading to circuit dysfunction in ASD and other NDDs (44–47). To investigate whether Zmiz1 loss preferentially affects excitatory versus inhibitory neurons, we mapped DEGs to markers of excitatory or inhibitory cell types (48). Zmiz1-KO DEGs were genes mainly expressed in excitatory neurons (Figure S11E). Additionally, we mapped the DEGs to the excitatory versus inhibitory synaptic cleft proteome (49). We found 10 E15.5 DEGs, 23 P1 DEGs, and 94 P14 DEGs corresponding to proteins found in excitatory synapses (including Gria1/2/4, Grin1/2b, Nrxn1/2, Shisa7). In contrast, 3 E15.5 DEGs, 3 P1 DEGs, and 25 P14 DEGs corresponded to inhibitory synaptic proteins (including Gabra1/2/3, Cdh10, Gabrb1/2/3, Nrxn3, Slc6a1, Gabbr1, Reln) (Figure S11F). Two E15.5 DEGs, 1 P1 DEG, and 9 P14 DEGs corresponded to proteins found in both excitatory and inhibitory synapses (including Cntnap1, Nrxn2, EphB6, Tenm2, Nlgn1) (Figure S11F).

Given the enrichment of GO pathways in synaptic function, we compared Zmiz1-KO DEGs with the synaptic GO database SynGO (50). Forty-two E15.5 DEGs, 62 P1 DEGs, and 437 P14 DEGs were identified as synaptic genes (Figure S11G–I). GO analysis of DEG-synaptic genes showed enrichment of biological processes involved in the regulation of synaptic organization and transmission at E15.5, P1, and P14 (Figure S11J, K, L).

To investigate the molecular mechanism and downstream targets regulated by Zmiz1, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq). Hemagglutinin-tagged ZMIZ1 (Zmiz1-HA) was overexpressed in N2a cells, followed by ChIP-seq analysis (Figure 6A and Figure S12). ZMIZ1 ChIP-seq revealed thousands of peaks and GTGTATGTGTGT as the top Zmiz1 motif (Figures S13 and S14). We integrated ZMIZ1 ChIP-seq and RNA-seq data to reveal Zmiz1-regulated targets. When we compared the ChIP peaks with the E15.5 and P1 DEGs involved in axon guidance/neuron differentiation, we found 6 genes overlapping in these 3 datasets: Auts2, Lhx2, EfnB2, Epha5, Unc5d, and Slit2 (Figure 6B). These were downregulated in the E15.5 progenitors and P1 neuron RNA-seq datasets from Zmiz1-KO cortex, except for Slit2, which was upregulated on E15.5 and downregulated on P1 (Figure S14B).

To confirm that ZMIZ1 regulates transcription of these potential downstream targets, we cloned ZMIZ1 ChIP peaks found in Auts2, Lhx2, EfnB2, Epha5, and Unc5d into a luciferase activity–reporter vector and performed luciferase assay. Luciferase expression from Auts2, Lhx2, EfnB2, Epha5, and Unc5d ChIP peaks was significantly decreased upon Zmiz1 knockdown in N2a compared with siRNA-control treated cells (Figure 6C–G). Scrambled sequences of ChIP peaks and sequences of the Actb promotor/enhancer were tested; however, luciferase expression was not altered by expression of Zmiz1 siRNA in any of these controls (Figure S13F). In these genes, peaks pulled down by ZMIZ1 were found in intronic or intergenic loci (Figure 6H; Figures S13 and S14). Overall, these data indicate that ZMIZ1 binding contributes to regulating the expression of Auts2, EfnB2, Lhx2, Epha5, and Unc5d in the cortex.

The EphB-ephrinB receptor-ligand system functions in a bidirectional, contact-mediated fashion between opposing cells and plays essential roles in neurite outgrowth and neuronal connectivity (51–54). The ephrin-B2 ligand (encoded by EfnB2 gene) is expressed in the developing cortex (Figure S14C, D) and is critical for CC development (54,55). EfnB2 is a target of Zmiz1 and is downregulated in the Zmiz1-KO cortex (Figures 5 and 6; Figure S14). Given the abnormal callosal and dendritic development in Zmiz1-KOs, we investigated whether dysregulation of the ephrin-B2 pathway contributes to these phenotypes.

EPHRIN-B2 protein levels were reduced in the Zmiz1-KO cortex (Figure S14). We investigated EPHRIN-B2 function in neurite outgrowth in Zmiz1-KO cortical neurons. First, we cultured neurons 5 days in vitro (DIV), immunolabeled for MAP2, and performed Sholl analysis on reconstructed neurons. Neurite branching was decreased in Zmiz1-KO neurons compared with wild-type neurons (Figure 6I, J). To assess the effect of EPHRIN-B2 treatment on neurite growth, we applied EPHRIN-B2-fc recombinant protein in control and Zmiz1-KO cortical neurons at 2 DIV and measured neurite branching at 5 DIV. We compared neurons from control, control+EPHRIN-B2-fc, Zmiz1-KO, and Zmiz1-KO+EPHRIN-B2-fc groups. Neurite branching was not different in EPHRIN-B2-fc–treated control neurons compared with nontreated controls; however, the branching defect in Zmiz1-KO neurons was statistically rescued to control numbers upon EPHRIN-B2-fc treatment (Figure 6I, J). To test this rescue effect in vivo, we expressed CAG-ephrin-B2-GFP via IUE in E14.5 Zmiz1-KO embryos and assessed dendritic branching in L2/3 neurons on P14. Given that EPHRIN-B2 activates forward and reverse Ephrin signaling components upon binding transsynaptically to its receptors, we assessed putative effects in ephrin-B2-GFP+ neurons and in their neighboring neurons, which have a high likelihood of being connected (56,57). We co-electroporated CAG-ephrin-B2-GFP and CAG-tdTomato constructs at different ratios, analyzed apical dendritic branching in ephrin-B2-GFP+ and neighboring ephrin-B2-GFP−/tdTomato+ neurons, and compared branching data with controls (Zmiz1fl/fl) or Zmiz1-KO embryos electroporated with control plasmids (GFP or tdTomato) (Figure 6K–P). We found that the apical branching defect in Zmiz1-KO neurons was rescued in ephrin-B2-GFP+ to the same numbers as controls (Figure 6O). We observed only a partial rescue effect in neighboring tdTomato+ neurons, likely due to high variability within this group, which may reflect effects in tdTomato+ neurons connected to ephrin-B2-GFP+ neurons and no effect in non-connected neurons. Together, our data demonstrate that ephrin-B2 is a downstream target of Zmiz1, that dysregulation of the ephrin-B2 pathway contributes to Zmiz1-KO phenotype, and that reactivation of the ephrin-B2 pathway rescues the neurite branching defect in Zmiz1-KO cortical neurons.

Mutations in ZMIZ1 cause a neurodevelopmental syndrome characterized by ID, ASD, microcephaly, and craniofacial abnormalities as core clinical features (7–12). ZMIZ1 is a strong candidate ASD risk gene by SFARI, but its roles in brain development and ASD are unknown. Understanding ZMIZ1 functions requires mechanistic studies not feasible in humans. We generated a Zmiz1 mutant model that recapitulates key phenotypes observed in human ZMIZ1 syndrome, including cortical microcephaly, callosal dysgenesis, and ASD-related behaviors (13). Our findings demonstrate that Zmiz1 is essential for cortical neurogenesis, neuron differentiation, and synaptic transmission.

We showed that Zmiz1 regulates cortical progenitors and neuron differentiation. Zmiz1 mutation significantly reduced the progenitor pool, resulting in thinner cortices and microcephaly, consistent with observations in patients with ZMIZ1 mutations (8,9), which highlights the validity of this mouse model. Transcriptomic analyses revealed that Zmiz1 regulates gene networks involved in neurogenesis, neuron differentiation, axon guidance, and synaptogenesis, underscoring its broad impact on cortical development.

Zmiz1 was identified as a gene enriched in callosal and corticothalamic neurons (27). However, its functions in any neuron type were previously investigated. Callosal neuron development abnormalities are implicated in ASD and cognitive deficits (37,58–60). Single-cell RNA-seq from cortices of patients with ASD revealed developmental disruptions in upper-layer callosal neurons (48). We revealed that Zmiz1 regulates L2/3 callosal neuron differentiation, including axonal extension through the CC, dendritic growth, and expression of synaptic proteins. These findings are consistent with clinical evidence that callosal abnormalities are highly prevalent in ASD and ID (61–63), reducing interhemispheric connectivity, which is linked to excitatory/inhibitory imbalance (64) and cognitive deficits (46,65).

Zmiz1-KO mice show altered dendrite morphology in L2/3 neurons, a feature commonly observed in ASD (66,67). L2/3 neuron apical dendrites play important roles in signal integration (68,69). Their disruption contributes to altering the excitatory/inhibitory balance, which is characteristic of ASD and other neurological disorders (44), and likely contributes to the behavioral deficits observed in Zmiz1-KO mice. Notably, Zmiz1 is a co-activator of p53 and Notch1, both regulators of dendritic development and neurogenesis (70–74), suggesting potential mechanistic links to these abnormalities.

Molecularly, Zmiz1 regulates high-confidence NDD risk genes, including Lhx2 and Auts2, which govern neurogenesis, neurite, and spine growth (75–80). We also identified the Eph-ephrin pathway, critical for neurogenesis, synaptic development, and CC formation (81–84), as a Zmiz1 target. Ephrin-B2 signaling is critical for NMDA receptor synaptic function (85,86). Although we have not evaluated synaptic functions in Zmiz1-KO, a significant number of DEGs were associated with synaptic transmission. Importantly, ephrin-B2 regulates neurite outgrowth in cortical neurons (54), and we showed that EPHRIN-B2 treatment rescues dendritic growth deficits in Zmiz1-KO neurons, highlighting a potential therapeutic avenue. Future studies will determine whether Zmiz1 plays a role in synaptic transmission, whether Eph-ephrin pathways mediate Zmiz1 synaptic functions, and whether Eph-ephrin pathway can be targeted to ameliorate phenotypes produced by Zmiz1 dysfunction.

Zmiz1 likely impacts multiple neuron subtypes relevant to NDDs. RNA-seq data suggest regulation of the ASD-associated gene TBR1, important for corticothalamic neuron development (87–90). Corticothalamic neurons are important for sensory processing and social behavior (91) and, as suggested by the significant thinning of L6, are likely disrupted in Zmiz1-KOs. Future studies will explore the functions of Zmiz1 in other neuron types.

Our study establishes Zmiz1 as a key regulator of cortical development and neuron differentiation, the loss of which causes anatomical and behavioral deficits resembling phenotypes observed in the human ZMIZ1 syndrome. By uncovering Zmiz1’s transcriptional targets and pathways, we provide a foundation for understanding the mechanisms underlying ZMIZ1 syndrome and related NDDs.
