BACKGROUND: Primary angiitis of the central nervous system (PACNS) is an 
uncommon inflammatory condition that affects small- to medium-sized central 
nervous system vessels. Diagnosis remains challenging owing to the nonspecific 
clinical and radiological characteristics that frequently reflect glioblastoma 
or other neurological disorders. This study presents a case of a solitary 
tumefactive PACNS lesion initially misdiagnosed as glioblastoma. We also 
conducted a systematic analysis to assess the diagnostic variability, 
clinical-radiological characteristics, and treatment outcome.
METHODS: A 42-year-old woman with a 20-year history of migraines presented with 
unusual headaches for 6 weeks. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan findings 
indicate a right parietal lesion diagnosed initially as glioblastoma. Following 
minicraniotomy and resection, histopathological examination revealed PACNS. A 
Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis-guided 
systematic review of published cases from 2000 to 2023, identified 34 tumor-like 
PACNS cases that exhibited characteristics similar to glioblastoma. Demographic 
data, predictive diagnostic imaging factors, histology, and management were 
collected and examined.
RESULTS: The review revealed a mean age of 35.92 years (range: 5-68 years), with 
a nearly equal sex distribution. Common symptoms included focal neurological 
impairments (71%), seizures (26%), and headaches (19%). MRI revealed T2 
hyperintense lesions (87% with edema), which were frequently misinterpreted as 
glioblastoma owing to mass effect (68%). Magnetic resonance angiography was 
insensitive (48% abnormal). Histopathology identified lymphocytic (81%) or 
granulomatous (16%) vasculitis. Steroids (100%) were used in combination with 
cyclophosphamide (61%) or mycophenolate (23%). Relapses occurred in 26% of the 
cases, whereas 74% responded to therapy. Delayed diagnosis was associated with 
persistent impairment (29%).
CONCLUSION: Tumefactive PACNS lesions frequently exhibit radiographic 
characteristics similar to glioblastoma, necessitating histological confirmation 
for accurate diagnosis. Immunosuppression is effective; however, standardized 
protocols are lacking. Despite the improvement in MRI predictive factors, future 
studies should investigate blood and cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, advanced 
imaging, and conduct multicenter trials to enhance the diagnosis and treatment 
of this rare condition.

Primary angiitis of the central nervous system (PACNS) involves the inflammation of small- and medium-sized vessels of the brain, spinal cord, and leptomeninges without other systemic localization, with an incidence of 2.4/million/year.[8,34] Diagnosis of PACNS is challenging because it initially mimics multiple diseases and lacks specific serological tests.[13] Strokes or recurrent ischemic attacks account for 30–50% of the PACNS presentations, resulting in neurological deficits.[14,33] The presentation of a tumefactive, tumor-like mass, PACNS is rare, and is estimated to be 5% of all PACNS cases.[23] The postulated pathophysiology involves damage to small vessels, leading to breakage of the blood-brain barrier and invasion of inflammatory cells into the perivascular and parenchymal regions.[47] This case-based systematic literature review demonstrates the challenges in tumefactive PACNS diagnosis, showing radiological and clinical findings suggestive of brain tumors.

The primary objectives of this study were to present a case of tumefactive PACNS and conduct a comprehensive systematic review of the existing literature on similar cases that exhibit characteristics resembling glioblastoma. The study aims to evaluate the patients’ demographic and clinical profiles, as well as pre-operative radiological predictors that contribute to the diagnosis and subsequent management of these cases.

A 42-year-old female with a documented 20-year history of migraine headaches presented with an atypical alteration in headache characteristics on the right side for 6 weeks. This alteration was accompanied by an electrical sensation in the left arm, intermittent blurred vision, and vertigo. There were no systemic manifestations such as fever, weight loss, or decreased appetite, and she had no prior history of stroke.

The general physical examination was unremarkable, with no notable findings, including skin rash or painless oral ulcers. A neurological examination revealed mild altered cognitive status in the form of decreased concentration, characterized by a unilateral hemianopsia. However, no other focal cranial nerve or extremity neurological deficits were observed.

Upon admission, laboratory tests, including a complete blood count (white blood cell), hematology, and renal profiles, indicated normal results. Serological tests for herpes simplex virus, human immunodeficiency virus, measles, and varicella–zoster antibodies were all unremarkable, ruling out infectious etiologies. Adenovirus Immunoglobulin G antibodies were positive, indicating a prior exposure but not directly linking it to the observed pathology.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed an infiltrating lesion with dimensions of 2.4 cm × 4.5 cm × 3.4 cm located in the right occipitoparietal region, encompassing the cortex, subcortical white matter, and periventricular deep white matter. The lesion appeared isointense and barely discernible on T1-weighted sequences [Figure 1a]. Heterogeneous low signal intensity was observed on T2-weighted and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences, accompanied by multiple punctate blooming artifacts on susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI), indicative of microhemorrhages [Figures 1b-d]. The lesion is circumscribed by vasogenic edema that encompasses the right parietal and occipital lobes, resulting in a slight mass effect on the adjacent sulci. Post-contrast axial, sagittal, and coronal T1-weighted images demonstrated intense nodular enhancement devoid of central necrosis or hemorrhage. The lesion exhibited a triangular configuration characterized by a broad cortical base and apex directed towards the deep white matter, resulting in a trans-mantle enhancement pattern that closely resembled a high-grade glioma (HGG) [Figures 1e and f].

Based on the provided presentation, the initial differential diagnosis included HGG (glioblastoma), with subsequent additions of lymphoma, metastasis, cerebral amyloidoma, and neurosarcoidosis.

In contrast, further MRI analysis revealed a centrally coursing curvilinear hypointense vessel and multiple punctate susceptibility foci consistent with microhemorrhages in SWI sequences [Figure 2a], which was corroborated in early post-contrast imaging by the presence of a linear enhancing vessel within the lesion, suggesting a vascular-centered inflammatory process [Figure 2b].

Time-of-flight magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) 3D reformatted frontal and lateral views revealed no significant stenosis, occlusion, or beading of large vessels. Incidental vascular variants included a persistent trigeminal artery on the left side, in addition to anatomical variations such as the absence of the right A1 segment of the anterior cerebral artery (ACA) and the azygous ACA. The absence of large-vessel involvement suggests a pattern of small-vessel vasculitis, which aligns with the later established pathological diagnosis of PACNS [Figure 3a and b]. No other lesions are discernible in any other regions of the brain.

In light of the radiological suspicion of a HGG, surgical intervention was deemed appropriate. The patient consented to biopsy/minicraniotomy. During surgery, a right navigation-guided multiple biopsies were conducted. However, intraoperative frozen section examination revealed an infiltrating necrotic tissue, rendering the definitive diagnosis uncertain. We proceeded with a parietal minicraniotomy through a linear incision and performed an ultrasonic aspirator-assisted microscopic gross total resection of a relatively vascular lesion with areas of necrosis.

Pathological examination of resected central nervous system (CNS) tissue is remarkable for transmural angiocentric lymphoplasmacytic inflammatory infiltrate involving mediumsized and small vessels. The inflammation is transmural, affecting all vascular layers, resulting in thrombosis, necrosis, and macrophagic infiltration [Figure 4a-c]. Some vessels are associated with fibrinoid necrosis [Figure 4b]. The intervening CNS parenchyma also shows marked reactive gliosis. The inflammatory infiltrate is a mixture of CD3+ T-cells, CD20+ B-cells, and plasma cells. No cytological atypia or light chain restriction was identified, and Epstein–Barr virus was negative by in situ hybridization. No granulomas or microorganisms were detected in any specimen.

The patient experienced a seamless post-operative recovery. Subsequent vasculitis workup yielded no significant findings, except for a positive antinuclear antibody result. An initial post-operative MRI examination demonstrated an uncomplicated gross total resection. The patient underwent consultations with neurology and rheumatology services and commenced immunosuppressive therapy (oral azathioprine, 100 mg daily) for 1 year.

After a year, her outpatient clinical assessment revealed complete remission. Axial T2-weighted (a) and post-contrast T1-weighted (b) sequences MRI demonstrated no recurrence, abnormal enhancement, or surrounding parenchymal edema, thereby confirming gross total resection of the inflammatory lesion and indicating disease-free status [Figure 5].

A systematic review was performed based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis guidelines.[27]

We used the electronic databases of three (PubMed, Google Scholar, and Web of Science) from January 2000 to December 2023. We performed the following search parameters: ALL = (Primary CNS vasculitis glioma) OR ALL = (Primary CNS vasculitis Brain tumor) OR ALL = (Primary CNS angiitis glioma) OR ALL = (Primary CNS angiitis brain tumor).

Cases of tumefactive (tumor-like) PACNS that exhibited radiological and histological characteristics resembling brain glioblastoma or HGGs were included. Vasculitis is characterized by systemic vessel inflammation; cases where the clinical course and management received were unclear, and non-English language publications were excluded from the study. The quality and eligibility of the studies were evaluated and reviewed by two distinct reviewers, G.M.F and M.M.F. These reviewers utilized the same databases and conducted independent screenings. Initially, the titles were evaluated, followed by the abstracts of the articles. Subsequently, we conducted a comprehensive screening of each article, adhering to our predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. All disagreements and ineligibilities were resolved among the reviewers. Any discrepancies were resolved through confrontation or by contacting the principal investigator (SSB).

Data extracted from full-text reviews were conducted for the studies that met the inclusion criteria from eligible articles. The data included the number of cases of PACNS mimicking brain tumors, demographic data of the patients, authors, year of publication, investigations, and management.

The quality of the included case reports was appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports, and bias was assessed by two independent reviewers. Based on the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports, most of the case reports in our review provided clear descriptions of the demographics, clinical presentations, diagnostic methods, and outcomes, as most answered “Yes.”

The search strategy and selection process identified a total of 1216 reports across databases [Figure 6]. Following the removal of duplicate records, 1179 reports underwent title and abstract screening, resulting in the exclusion of 1073 reports. Subsequently, 53 full-text articles published cases of PACNS that met the inclusion criteria were independently assessed. Thirty-two studies, comprising 34 cases of PACNS, are deemed eligible for inclusion and summarized in Table 1.[1,2,7,10-12, 16-19,21,22,24-26,28-32,35,37,38,41-45,47,48]

Synopsis of the reported tumefactive PACNS cases in the literature.

The study included 34 patients with PACNS with insignificant male preponderance (18 men and 13 women) and a mean age of 36.7 years (range: 5–68 years). Clinically, most patients had focal neurological impairments (71%), and seizures (26%) and headaches (19%) were also prevalent. This demographic and clinical profile underscores the variability in the presentation of PACNS, which frequently coexists with other neurological conditions, such as glioblastoma.

Majority of the lesions were located in the frontal (45%) and parietal lobes (29%), with a smaller percentage affecting the deep gray matter (16%) and the brainstem (10%). Typically, the lesions on MRI were heterogeneous and hyperintense on T1/T2-weighted scans, with perilesional edema present in 87% of the cases. Several contrast enhancement patterns were observed: ring-like (42%), nodular (32%), and diffuse (26%). Notably, 68% of the patients exhibited mass effects and diffusion limitations, which are typically associated with glioblastoma. Advanced imaging techniques, including magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRSpect), have demonstrated elevated choline (Cho) and reduced N-acetylaspartate (NAA) ratios. These findings are typically indicative of malignancy, thereby complicating the diagnostic process.

The radiological similarities between PACNS and glioblastoma or any HGG led to early misdiagnosis in 90% of the patients, with most lesions being mistaken for malignant gliomas. Conventional angiography indicated vascular abnormalities in only 48% of the patients, demonstrating its low sensitivity for small-vessel vasculitis and stressing the requirement for tissue diagnosis. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, regardless of how often conducted, yielded nonspecific findings, with mild pleocytosis or increased protein levels observed in 65% of the cases. These results underscore the problems faced during PACNS diagnosis and the significance of combining clinical, imaging, and histopathological data for proper diagnosis.

A definitive diagnosis necessitates a biopsy in all cases, with histological evaluation confirming lymphocytic vasculitis (81%). This condition is characterized by intramural vascular infiltration, typically involving CD3+ T-cells, often accompanied by CD20+ B-cells. Granulomatous inflammation was observed in 16% of the patients, while necrosis and fibrinoid alterations were present in 23%. These findings emphasize the paramount importance of biopsy in distinguishing PACNS from glioblastoma, particularly when imaging and clinical presentations are ambiguous.

The majority of patients (58%) underwent surgical resection, either partially or completely, before the confirmation of a precise PACNS diagnosis. On identification, all patients received high-dose corticosteroids as the initial treatment regimen. In cases of refractory or severe disease, cyclophosphamide (61%) or mycophenolate mofetil (23%) was administered as an additional treatment option. Despite undergoing therapy, 26% of the patients experienced relapse, necessitating the administration of additional medications such as rituximab or infliximab. In summary, 74% of the patients responded effectively to immunosuppressive medication. However, delayed diagnosis and treatment were associated with persistent neurological impairment in 29% of the cases. These findings emphasize the necessity of early and accurate diagnosis to mitigate unnecessary surgical interventions and optimize therapeutic outcomes.

Tumefactive PACNS is a rare and challenging-to-diagnose condition characterized by inflammation of the small and medium-sized vessels within CNS.[20] The clinical and radiological features of this condition frequently resemble those of other neurological disorders, particularly glioblastoma, leading to frequent misdiagnosis and delayed treatment.[18,46-49] In the ensuing discussion, we provide a concise summary of the findings from our case-based systematic review. The review specifically addresses diagnostic challenges, clinical and radiological variability, predictive pre-operative MRI diagnostic factors, histological confirmation, therapeutic approaches, and broader implications for the management of tumefactive PACNS. The diagnosis of tumefactive PACNS presents a significant clinical challenge due to its nonspecific symptoms and radiological characteristics that overlap with those of other central nervous system pathologies, including gliomas, infections, and demyelinating diseases.[3,4,46-49] In our systematic review, majority of the patients were initially misdiagnosed with malignant gliomas, underscoring the difficulty in distinguishing PACNS from neoplastic lesions using imaging modalities.[6] The absence of precise serological markers further complicates the diagnostic process, necessitating histological confirmation.

Our case study presented herein elucidates these challenges. A 42-year-old female patient with a 20-year history of migraines presented with a recent alteration in headache characteristics. Radiological findings suggested a probability of right parietal glioblastoma. The initial MRI revealed an ill-defined infiltrating lesion with enhancement, which is characteristic of glioblastoma. However, histological examination following craniotomy revealed angiocentric lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, thereby confirming the diagnosis of PACNS. This case exemplifies the paramount significance of biopsy in differentiating PACNS from other mass-forming diseases, particularly when imaging and clinical presentations are ambiguous.[5,46-49]

Our systematic analysis of 34 cases revealed a diverse demographic and clinical spectrum of PACNS [Table 1]. The average age of the patients was 35.92 years, with no discernible age preference despite the fact that the disease is less prevalent in children. The sex distribution was approximately equal, with some studies suggesting a slight male predominance. Clinically, patients presented a diverse array of symptoms, encompassing headaches (19%), focal neurological impairments (71%), and seizures (26%). These findings align with those of prior studies, which have demonstrated that headaches are the most prevalent symptom, frequently accompanied by cognitive abnormalities or focal deficits contingent upon the affected brain region.[3,9] The heterogeneous clinical presentation of tumefactive PACNS underscores the significance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for this condition, particularly when symptoms are progressing or atypical for more common conditions such as headaches or malignancies. Our patient’s longstanding history of headaches, which ultimately underwent a significant change in nature, exemplified the unpredictable nature of PACNS.

MRI stands as the most sensitive imaging modality for the detection of PACNS, identifying abnormalities in approximately 90–100% of cases. However, its specificity remains limited.

In our case, an MRI scan revealed a right parietal cortical-subcortical lesion with infiltrative morphology extending into the periventricular white matter. The lesion exhibited mild hyperintensity on T2-weighted and FLAIR sequences, accompanied by associated vasogenic edema and mild mass effect, characteristics commonly associated with neoplastic processes. The SWI sequences identified multiple punctate blooming artifacts indicative of microhemorrhages, supporting a vascular etiology. Post-contrast MRI images revealed a curvilinear, centrally enhancing vessel and avid heterogeneous nodular enhancement, extending transependymally into the trigone and occipital horn of the right lateral ventricle. The diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) sequences highlighted scattered mild areas of restricted diffusion, resembling inflammatory microinfarcts.

These findings align with radiological patterns elucidated in our systematic review of 34 PACNS cases that mimicked brain tumors. Notably, majority of the lesions (45%) were localized in the frontal lobes, followed by the parietal lobes (29%), deep gray matter (16%), and brainstem (10%). MRI scans consistently revealed heterogeneous T1/T2 hyperintense lesions accompanied by perilesional edema in 87% of cases. Contrast enhancement patterns exhibited variability, encompassing ring-like (42%), nodular (32%), and diffuse (26%) configurations. Particularly, 68% of cases demonstrated mild diffusion restriction and localized mass effect, characteristics that frequently resemble glioblastoma, albeit without the typical necrotic or infiltrative margins.

Recently, Zedde et al. further characterized tumefactive PACNS lesions as ill-defined, solitary, and often wedge- or triangular-shaped, with a base near the cortex and apex pointing toward the ventricles.[46] This morphology was observed in our case. While conventional vascular imaging, such as MRA and CTA, may be unremarkable due to small-vessel involvement, as seen in our patient, advanced imaging modalities provide additional diagnostic insights. A comprehensive review of advanced MRI imaging characteristics in tumefactive PACNS was conducted to facilitate its differentiation from glioblastoma. Tumefactive PACNS typically exhibit low or normal relative cerebral blood volume (rCBV) due to the absence of neovascularization. This is in contrast to glioblastoma, which demonstrates elevated rCBV as a result of angiogenesis. Furthermore, MRSpect differs from glioblastoma, which exhibits an elevated Cho-to-NAA ratio, in that PACNS frequently presents with nonspecific or mildly altered spectroscopic profiles, thereby diminishing diagnostic specificity. Diffusion imaging (DWI/apparent diffusion coefficient characteristics of tumefactive PACNS exhibit scattered punctate restricted diffusion, indicative of microinfarction patterns. Conversely, gliomas typically demonstrate dense, confluent areas of diffusion restriction. SWI sequences revealed blooming artifacts, suggesting a higher prevalence of microbleeds in PACNS compared to gliomas. This observation implies vessel wall damage resulting from vasculitis. When available, high-resolution vessel wall imaging may demonstrate concentric vessel wall thickening and enhancement, which was demonstrated in our case [Figure 2] a relatively specific but underutilized finding for small-vessel vasculitis. The role of fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) in differentiation has not been conclusive. Glioblastomas frequently exhibit elevated metabolic activity, while PACNS may demonstrate reduced uptake. However, this distinction is limited by overlap and variability, rendering standalone FDG-PET analysis less reliable.

In our case, when considered comprehensively, a constellation of features, including a solitary T2/FLAIR hyperintense lesion with variable enhancement, scattered diffusion restriction, microbleeds, minimal mass effect, and absence of major vessel abnormalities, should raise suspicion for tumefactive PACNS. However, due to significant overlap with demyelinating and neoplastic processes, particularly glioblastoma, histopathological confirmation remains essential for diagnosis and initiation of immunosuppressive therapy. Histopathological examination serves as the definitive diagnostic criterion for vasculitis, encompassing PACNS.[3,36,39] In our review, lymphocytic vasculitis and granulomatous inflammation were frequently observed. Common findings included necrosis and fibrinoid alterations. The significance of biopsy or resection should be underscored, as it not only establishes the diagnosis but also serves as a guiding principle for treatment. In our case, the resected tissue demonstrated lymphoplasmacytic angiitis with secondary thrombosis. Infections and neoplastic causes were ruled out. Consequently, the patient was administered the appropriate immunosuppressive medication.

Given the unknown incidence of tumefactive PACNS, its uncommon occurrence, and the absence of controlled trials, there are currently no established treatment regimens for this condition. Despite the availability of alternative treatments, high-dose corticosteroids are universally employed as the initial line of therapy, occasionally in conjunction with immunosuppressive agents such as cyclophosphamide (61% of cases) or mycophenolate mofetil (23% of cases) for refractory or severe cases. Notably, in our study, 58% of patients underwent surgical resection before the confirmation of PACNS, suggesting an initial misdiagnosis as a tumor. Although surgical intervention can alleviate the mass effect, it does not constitute a definitive cure. Consequently, immunosuppression continues to serve as the primary therapeutic modality.[40,46]

In the studies included in our systematic review, the outcomes exhibited variability. Notably, 74% of the patients responded favorably to immunosuppression, while 26% experienced relapse, necessitating the initiation of additional therapy, such as rituximab or infliximab. Delayed diagnosis was associated with persistent neurological impairment in 29% of the cases, emphasizing the necessity of early detection and treatment. Our patient was referred for urgent neurological assessment to commence immunosuppressive medication in accordance with the literature’s recommendations for a multidisciplinary approach.

This study has certain limitations. The limited sample size of the reported tumefactive PACNS in the literature reduced the generalizability of the findings. The reported cases exhibited heterogeneity, characterized by variations in diagnostic criteria and treatment regimens. In addition, the rarity of tumefactive PACNS restricted the scope of large-scale prospective investigations, thereby leaving several unresolved concerns. Furthermore, the refinement of advanced imaging techniques, such as high-resolution vascular wall MRI, is imperative to enhance the diagnostic accuracy of tumefactive PACNS and differentiate it from diseases that exhibit similar characteristics to glioblastoma. Future research should prioritize the identification of reliable serological CSF biomarkers to mitigate the reliance on invasive biopsies for diagnosis. Furthermore, there is a critical need to establish evidence-based treatment protocols, particularly for immunosuppressive therapy, which encompasses the role of novel biological medications such as rituximab in refractory patients. Multicentre collaboration facilitated by global registries will be pivotal in aggregating data, enhancing our comprehension of the prevalence of tumefactive PACNS, and evaluating the long-term efficacy of treatment modalities. These initiatives will address existing diagnostic and therapeutic disparities, ultimately leading to enhanced patient care for this uncommon and intricate condition.

Tumefactive PACNS is a rare and challenging-to-diagnose condition that frequently mimics glioblastoma and presents significant treatment difficulties. Our case-based systematic review underscores the paramount importance of histological confirmation, the variability in clinical and radiological presentations, and the necessity for individualized immunosuppressive approaches. Although corticosteroids and cyclophosphamide continue to serve as the primary therapeutic modalities, further research is imperative to enhance diagnostic criteria and refine therapeutic strategies. This study contributes to the expanding body of data on PACNS by advocating for heightened clinical suspicion and interdisciplinary collaboration to enhance patient outcomes. PACNS, a rare vascular disease, necessitates an integrated approach involving neurology, rheumatology, and neurosurgery for its diagnosis and treatment. Continued research and public awareness are crucial in addressing the unmet needs of patients with this condition.
