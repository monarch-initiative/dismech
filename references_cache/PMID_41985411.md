# Glutaredoxin 1 promotes intestinal epithelial cell copper toxicity in inflammatory bowel disease.
**Authors:** Zhang T, Zhang X, Cai H, Yu M, Liu Q, Song X, Lin B, Xu L, Li L, Shen Z, Yu C, Zhang J
**Journal:** Redox Biol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.redox.2026.104164](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.redox.2026.104164)

## Content

Protein S-glutathionylation (PSSG), a redox-sensitive post-translational modification, regulates protein conformation and activity, potentially resulting in cell death. PSSG is dynamically reversed by glutaredoxin 1 (GRX1), a critical oxidoreductase. Here, we observed elevated PSSG levels in colonic tissues of ulcerative colitis (UC) patients and experimental mouse models. Quantitative redox proteomics revealed glutathione disulfide oxidoreductase activity as the most significantly altered pathway in UC patients compared to controls. Interestingly, GRX1 undergoes site-specific S-glutathionylation at its catalytic Cys8 residue in UC patients, resulting in enzymatic inactivation. Grx1 deficiency exacerbated colonic PSSG accumulation and increased susceptibility to dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)- and 2,4,6-trinitrobenzene sulfonic acid (TNBS)-induced colitis, whereas Grx1 overexpression attenuated PSSG and ameliorated disease severity. Transcriptomic profiling identified GRX1 as a regulator of metal ion transmembrane transport, with Grx1 loss inducing copper overload and cuproptosis in vitro. Copper chelation therapy rescued colitis progression in Grx1-/- mice. Mechanistically, GRX1 associates with ATPase copper-transporting beta (ATP7B), a key regulator of cellular copper export and homeostasis, thereby stabilizing the protein by suppressing ubiquitin-mediated degradation. Therapeutic upregulation of GRX1 via pirfenidone administration mitigated DSS-induced colitis. Our findings establish GRX1 as a guardian against copper toxicity and cell death in colitis and propose GRX1 activation as a novel therapeutic strategy for UC. Directly visualizing cuproptosis in vivo would represent a critical direction for future research.

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD), is mainly characterized by anemia, abdominal pain, diarrhea and weight loss. With its rapidly increasing incidence, IBD has become a global health concern, imposing a significant economic burden on society [1]. Redox imbalance has been implicated in IBD pathogenesis [2,3]. Glutathione (GSH), the most abundant intracellular thiol, plays a critical role in maintaining cellular redox homeostasis in IBD [4]. This tripeptide exerts multifaceted protective effects by neutralizing oxidative stress, enhancing phase II metabolic detoxification processes, and modulating the oxidative states of cellular proteins through post-translational modifications [5]. While the latter function is essential for cellular homeostasis, its role in IBD pathophysiology remains less well understood. The cysteine residue of GSH can form a disulfide bond with the thiol group (-SH) of a target cysteine within a reactive protein, a process known as protein S-glutathionylation (PSSG) [6]. PSSG is reversible and facilitates the dynamic regulation of protein structure [7]. However, sustained elevation of PSSG levels can induce irreversible structural alterations, leading to protein dysfunction, subsequent cellular apoptosis, and ultimately, tissue damage [8]. To date, it remains unclear whether PSSG regulates the oxidation state of proteins during the development of IBD.

Glutaredoxin 1 (GRX1) is a key glutathione-dependent mercaptan oxidoreductase, and ubiquitously expressed across various human tissues [5,9]. GRX1 catalyzes the reversible deglutathionylation of proteins, modulating their activity while releasing GSH [10]. Genetic ablation of Grx1 in mice leads to pathological accumulation of PSSG, exacerbating multiple organ pathologies, including pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic injury, acute lung injury, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa pneumonia [11,12]. Notably, GRX1 serves as a pharmacological target for the anti-fibrotic agent pirfenidone, which demonstrates therapeutic efficacy against both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis through modulation of PSSG homeostasis [13,14]. However, the cysteine residues of GRX1 (C8 and C83) are prone to oxidative modification and susceptible to S-glutathionylation [10]. Therefore, it is of great interest to determine the role of GRX1 in redox homeostasis and IBD.

Copper is a redox-active, essential trace element regulated by two principal transporters: ATPase copper-transporting alpha (ATP7A) and beta (ATP7B), which govern cellular copper efflux and homeostasis [15]. Elevated serum copper concentrations have been observed in children with IBD [16]. Copper exposure triggers an intestinal inflammatory response and damages intestinal villi. Particularly, aberrant intracellular copper accumulation initiates cuproptosis—a recently identified metal-dependent cell death pathway [17]. Intracellular copper binds to lipoylated components in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, causing aggregation of lipoylated proteins, the reduction of Fe–S (iron–sulfur) cluster proteins and induction of HSP70, resulting in proteotoxic stress and ultimately cell death [17]. While the role of cuproptosis in IBD has not been reported, bioinformatic analyses indicated a downregulation of cuproptosis hub genes in UC patients [18], suggesting a potential role of cuproptosis in IBD.

In this study, we observed elevated levels of PSSG and increased S-glutathionylation of GRX1 in both human IBD samples and mouse models. Furthermore, GRX1 deficiency worsened colitis through enhanced mucosal copper toxicity and cell death, resulting in epithelial barrier disruption. These findings suggest that targeting GRX1 expression could serve as a potential therapeutic strategy for colitis.

To investigate the physiological role of S-glutathionylation, we first measured total PSSG levels in IBD. Elevated colon PSSG levels were observed in both UC patients (Fig. 1A) and in a mouse model of dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis (Fig. 1B). To assess the role of PSSG in IBD, we performed a screening of colon samples from UC patients undergoing endoscopy, using iodoacetyl tandem mass tag (iodo-TMT) labeling liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) (Fig. 1C). This analysis identified 40 differentially S-glutathionylated proteins in the colons of UC patients compared with controls (Fig. S1A). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that glutathione disulfide oxidoreductase activity was the most significantly regulated molecular function (Fig. 1D and E). Interestingly, we observed a profound increase in S-glutathionylation at the C8 position of GRX1 in UC (Fig. 1F and Fig. S1B). PSSG immunoprecipitation using an anti-GSH antibody further confirmed increased GRX1 glutathionylation in DSS-treated colon tissues (Fig. 1G) and TNF-α-treated HT29 cells (Fig. 1H). The C8 cysteine is highly reactive with glutathione disulfide (GSSG) and plays a key role in the GSSG-mediated inactivation of GRX1 activity [10]. Enzymatic assays further confirmed a substantial reduction in GRX1 activity in both DSS-induced colitis and TNF-α-treated cellular models (Fig. 1I and Fig. S1C). To elucidate GRX1's role in colitis, we generated whole-body Grx1 knockout (Grx1−/−) mice. The Grx1 knockout mice exhibited normal developmental parameters and baseline phenotypes compared to wild-type (WT) littermates (Fig. S2A–D). In the acute DSS colitis model, Grx1−/− mice showed more significant weight loss (Fig. 2A), higher disease activity index (DAI) score (Fig. 2B), and more severe colon shortening (Fig. 2C and D) compared to WT mice. Histological examination of colon sections corroborated the clinical observations (Fig. 2E). Mucus secretion, evaluated through Alcian Blue-Periodic Acid Schiff (AB-PAS) staining, revealed that the extent of intestinal epithelium damage was markedly more severe in DSS-treated Grx1−/− mice compared to that in WT mice (Fig. 2E). Moreover, immunohistochemical results of F4/80 and myeloperoxidase (MPO) showed more macrophage and neutrophil infiltration in the DSS-treated Grx1−/− group (Fig. 2E). The proinflammatory cytokine (Tnfa, interleukin (Il)-6 and Il-1b), intercellular cell adhesion molecule 1(Icam1) and chemokine (Ccl2 and Cxcl1) transcription in the colon were substantially exacerbated in DSS-treated Grx1−/− mice (Fig. 2F). Consistently, transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) increased relatively slowly in GRX1 knockdown Caco-2 cells in vitro (Fig. 2G). And lower expression of tight junction protein (CLAUDIN1) was also found in the DSS-treated Grx1-silenced group in vivo (Fig. 2H). Consistent with its role as the principal glutathione-dependent thiol oxidoreductase, GRX1 deficiency led to marked accumulation of PSSG (Fig. 2I). The exacerbation of colitis observed in Grx1 knockout models led us to investigate the potential protective effects of Grx1 overexpression. To this end, we successfully overexpressed Grx1 in WT mice through tail vein injection of AAV9-Grx1 (Fig. S4A–B). We found that overexpression of Grx1 significantly ameliorated colitis, as evidenced by reduced weight loss (Fig. 3A), a lower DAI score (Fig. 3B), increased colon length (Fig. 3C and D), diminished intestinal mucosal destruction (Fig. 3E), and decreased macrophage and neutrophil infiltration (Fig. 3E) in comparison to the control AAV-NC group. Furthermore, levels of proinflammatory factors were also reduced in the DSS-treated AAV-Grx1 group (Fig. 3F). The impaired epithelial barrier in Grx1 overexpression group has been correspondingly improved both in vitro (Fig. 3G) and in vivo (Fig. 3H). Additionally, overexpression of GRX1 significantly reduced the level of PSSG in the TNF-α induced cell model (Fig. 3I). To determine the relevant cell populations that contributed to GRX1-mediated protection against colitis, bone marrow chimera experiments were performed. Recipient WT and Grx1−/− mice underwent sublethal irradiation (8.0 Gy) followed by intravenous reconstitution with donor marrow from either WT or Grx1−/− mice. Four weeks after bone marrow reconstitution, the genetic information in bone marrow was identified (Fig. S5A) and the mice were subjected to DSS treatment. In the mice with a knockout of Grx1 specifically in hematopoietic cells (Grx1−/−→WT), no differences were observed in body weight (Fig. S5B), colon length (Fig. S5C–D), histological examination outcomes (Fig. S5E–F), or proinflammatory factor levels (Fig. S5G) when compared with WT→WT group. Subsequently, a reverse transplant was also performed. In WT→Grx1−/− mice, where Grx1 was intact only in hematopoietic cells (Fig. S5H), there were no improvements in colitis when compared to Grx1−/−→Grx1−/− mice (Fig. S5I–N). Collectively, these data indicated that the GRX1 in the epithelium, but not in immune cells, was critical for improving colitis.

Next, we harvested colonic epithelial stem cells from WT and Grx1−/− mice. Organoids derived from Grx1−/− mice exhibited increased cell death when exposed to TNF-α compared to their counterparts from WT mice (Fig. S6A). We then treated HT29 cells with TNF-α to simulate intestinal inflammation in vitro. GRX1 expression was knocked down by siRNA, resulting in a significant elevation of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines (Fig. S6B).

To determine the molecular mechanism underlying GRX1-mediated protection against colitis, we conducted RNA sequencing on distal colon segments from Grx1−/− and WT mice. This analysis identified 8107 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; FDR<0.05), with 8027 exhibiting colitis-specific dysregulation in DSS-treated Grx1−/− mice (Fig. 4A and B). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed pronounced disruption of metal ion homeostasis, particularly in transmembrane transporter activity (Fig. 4C). Therefore, we reasoned that ion homeostasis might be disrupted in DSS-treated Grx1−/− mice. Notably, elevated copper levels, rather than other metal ions (Na, K, Ca, Fe, Zn and Mg), were observed in serum and colon tissue samples from Grx1−/− mice compared with control mice (Fig. 4D–E and Fig. S7A–G). Higher intracellular copper levels were also found in GRX1-deficient HT29 cells compared with control cells (Fig. 4F–G and Fig.S7H). We also found that copper imbalance occurred in IBD. Higher copper levels were found in the inflamed intestinal regions of CD patients (Fig. 4H) and in serum of IBD patients (Fig. 4I). Copper imbalance also disrupted mitochondrial function. Copper overload activates cell death [19]. A significant increase in cell death, as measured by CCK-8 assay, was observed in GRX1-deficient HT29 cells stimulated with TNF-α at 24 h, 48 h and 72 h (Fig. 4J). This increase in cell death was further confirmed by flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 4K). To further validate the cell death induced by GRX1 knockdown, we additionally treated Caco-2 cells with a mixture of inflammatory mediators (Mix) and found that GRX1 deficiency also led to increased cell death in Caco-2 cells (Fig. S7J). Furthermore, overexpression of GRX1 WT, but not its C8S mutant, suppressed cell death in both TNF-α-treated HT29 cells (Fig. S7K) and in Mix-treated Caco-2 cells (Fig. S7L–M). To clarify which type of cell death was induced by GRX1 deficiency, inhibitors of cell death, including cuproptosis (TTM), apoptosis (Boc-D-FMK) and necroptosis (Nec-1) were used to abrogate the GRX1 deficiency-induced cell death. First, we conducted a concentration gradient experiment, and found that 20 μM TTM, 50 μM Boc-D-FMK or 10 μM Nec-1 did not affect cell activity under the basal condition (Fig. S7N–O). Notably, among the inhibitors tested, only TTM significantly attenuated cell death in both TNF-α-treated, GRX1-deficient HT29 cells (Fig. 4L and Fig. S7P–Q) and Mix-treated, GRX1-deficient Caco-2 cells (Fig. S7R). These results indicated that GRX1 deficiency exacerbated copper toxicity and cell death in vitro. Additionally, KEGG-enriched cell death pathways, including apoptosis, autophagy, ferroptosis and necroptosis remain unchanged between DSS-treated Grx1−/− mice and WT mice (Fig. S7S). Strikingly, transcriptional profiling revealed a coordinated downregulation of genes involved in the protein lipoylation pathway, the pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex and Fe–S cluster biosynthesis in colonic tissues from DSS-treated Grx1−/− mice (Fig. 4M). Furthermore, the protein levels of lipoylated DLAT and DLST, as well as FDX1, NDUFS8 and LIAS, were reduced in Grx1−/− mice only under DSS-treated conditions and in GRX1-silenced HT29 cells treated with TNF-α as well as GRX1-silenced Caco-2 cells treated with Mix (Fig. 4N–O and Fig. S7T). DLAT aggregation, a specific marker of cuproptosis, was observed in the colonic tissues of the Grx1−/− mice treated with DSS and in GRX1-silenced cells (Fig. 4P and Q). Moreover, JC-1 staining confirmed that GRX1 silencing significantly impaired mitochondrial membrane potential compared to siNC controls, which was rescued by TTM (Fig. 4R). These results indicated that GRX1 deficiency aggravates copper toxicity in intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) and suggested that it might trigger cuproptosis.

The downstream targets of GRX1 in regulating copper toxicity and cell death in IBD remain unclear. Based on GRX1's established role in protein deglutathionylation, we postulated that its deficiency might alter S-glutathionylation patterns of copper transport-related proteins. In the acute DSS colitis model, Grx1 deficiency resulted in significant accumulation of protein S-glutathionylation modifications, particularly in high molecular weight proteins (>130 kD) (Fig. 5A). Subsequent interrogation of the STRING interaction database identified three candidate interactors: ATP7A (163 kD), ATP7B (165 kD), and mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase 5 (MAP3K5) (155 kD) (Fig. 5B). While ATP7A/ATP7B are established mediators of cellular copper efflux [20]. MAP3K5 represents a stress-responsive kinase with no known copper-related functions [21]. Other copper transporters/chaperones such as copper transport 1 (CTR1) (26–34 kD), antioxidant 1 copper chaperone (ATOX1) (7 kD), and copper chaperone for superoxide dismutase (CCS) (34 kD) exhibit lower molecular weights. Recent studies have reported that glutathionylation can affect protein degradation [22,23]. Indeed, a notably shorter half-life of ATP7B was observed in the GRX1-silenced HT29 cell compared to control group after cycloheximide (CHX) treatment (Fig. 5I). Pharmacological inhibition studies using chloroquine (CQ, lysosomal inhibitor) and MG132 (proteasomal inhibitor) demonstrated that ATP7B destabilization in GRX1-deficient cells specifically depended on the ubiquitin-proteasome activity (Fig. 5J and K). Overexpression of wild-type GRX1, but not the C8S mutant, significantly stabilized ATP7B and extended its protein half-life after CHX treatment in HT29 cells (Fig. 5L). Furthermore, ATP7B re-expression significantly attenuated GRX1 deficiency-induced cuproptosis, as evidenced by restoration of cuproptosis-related proteins LIAS and NDUFS8 (Fig. 5M). Under physiological conditions, ATP7B undergoes dynamic trafficking from the trans-Golgi network to lysosomal compartments, ultimately fusing with the apical membrane to mediate copper excretion [24]. Intriguingly, GRX1 deficiency significantly impaired ATP7B's membrane fusion capacity (Fig. 5N), suggesting compromised copper efflux efficiency. These findings collectively indicated that GRX1 deficiency promotes ATP7B degradation through proteasomal targeting. Furthermore, we generated Atp7b−/− mice and found Atp7b−/− mice exhibited aggravated TNBS-induced colitis, manifested as shorter colon length and higher histological scores (Fig. S8C–F).

In this study, we identified GRX1 as a potential therapeutic target for IBD. Our findings reveal that GRX1 undergoes enzymatic inactivation through S-glutathionylation at the Cys8 residue in UC. GRX1 deficiency aggravated colitis by driving the ubiquitin-proteasome degradation of ATP7B, thereby inducing pathological copper toxicity that might lead to IECs death. Our results were clinically relevant. Foremost, we established GRX1 as a promising therapeutic target for IBD. Additionally, PFD, a clinically approved anti-fibrotic drug, showed potential therapeutic effects in UC by targeting GRX1, demonstrating repurposing potential (graphical abstract).

Redox imbalance was found in IBD, and the reduced protein deglutathionylation would result in a decline in major antioxidant GSH levels, leading to redox imbalance and even cell death [25]. Our study showed accumulation of PSSG in both IBD patients and DSS-induced mice model. Using a quantitative redox proteomics approach, we found that glutathione disulfide oxidoreductase activity is the most regulated pathway in UC patients by GO enrichment. GRXs family members are major oxidoreductase enzymes [26,27]. Interestingly, GRX1 itself is particularly susceptible to S-glutathionylation at the Cys8 residue in UC, resulting in a loss of enzymatic activity. To date, it was unclear whether the decrease activity of GRX1 was a result or a cause in IBD pathogenesis. Therefore, we constructed Grx1 knockout mice and Grx1 overexpression mice to clarify its function. In addition, we were the first to demonstrate that GRX1 might be a vitally important factor in the protection against intestinal epithelium injury since completely knocking out Grx1 increased susceptibility to DSS-induced and TNBS-induced acute colitis. These findings suggested that the alterations of GRX1 activity might contribute to the development of IBD.

Cuproptosis, a new and emerging mode of programmed cell death, can be induced by excessive copper accumulation [17]. Machine learning algorithms have identified cuproptosis-related genes as promising immunoregulatory biomarkers in IBD [28]. Early studies indicated that GRX1 may be associated with copper metabolism [29]. GRX1 also protects neuronal cells from copper-induced toxicity through the GSH system [30]. Compared to WT mice, Grx1−/− mice exhibited induced copper toxicity, suggesting that this might trigger cell death. Indeed, measurements by CCK-8 assay, flow cytometry, and LDH release consistently revealed that siGRX1 induced a modest but statistically significant increase in cell death in TNFα-treated HT29 cells. To further validate the cell death induced by GRX1 knockdown, we additionally treated Caco-2 cells with Mix and found that GRX1 deficiency also led to increased cell death in Caco-2 cells. Then, we used a series of GRX1 plasmids and found that while wild-type overexpression alleviated cell death and suppressed copper toxicity across models, the Cys8 mutation completely abrogated these benefits (Fig. S7K-M, Fig. 5H and L). The functional role of Cys8 has thus far been established only in vitro, and whether self-glutathionylation at this residue fully accounts for GRX1 function in vivo remains to be determined. A limitation of our study is the lack of a Cys8-mutant mouse model and the absence of direct in vivo intervention targeting this residue. Furthermore, given that GRX1 contains multiple glutathionylation sites (e.g., Cys83 and Cys26) [10], it is plausible that self-glutathionylation at Cys8 alone does not fully explain the observed phenotypes. Cys8 may contribute to GRX1 function in IBD through both glutathionylation-dependent and -independent mechanisms. For instance, as reported by Elizabeth et al., Cys8 plays a critical role in dimer formation and aggregation [10], representing a glutathionylation-independent function. Future studies employing site-specific glutathionylation mimics or mass spectrometry-based approaches will be essential to delineate the relative contributions of these distinct mechanisms.

The precise mechanism by which GRX1 regulates systemic copper homeostasis remains incompletely understood. In mammalian cells, copper balance is tightly controlled at the levels of absorption, distribution, and excretion. Intestinal epithelial cells serve as the primary site of dietary copper uptake, importing copper mainly through the high-affinity transporter CTR1. Once inside, copper is distributed by three major chaperones: CCS, which delivers copper to superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1); ATOX1, which transfers copper to the transporters ATP7A and ATP7B; and COX17, which supplies copper to cytochrome c oxidase for mitochondrial respiration [31]. ATP7B, localized at the trans-Golgi network, incorporates copper into cuproenzymes and, in response to elevated intracellular copper, translocates to the basolateral membrane to export excess copper into the portal circulation. Previous studies indicated that deficiencies in copper transporters/chaperones impair colonic function. First, we observed that knockdown of COMMD1 or CLUSTERIN did not reduce ATP7B levels to the same extent as GRX1 knockdown (Fig. S8B). Subsequently, overexpression of either wild-type or C8S mutant GRX1 did not alter the expression of COMMD1 or CLUSTERIN (Fig. S12B). Furthermore, deficiency in Grx1 did not affect the expression of CTR1, CCS or ATOX1 (Fig. S12C). These results led us to focus on the GRX1-ATP7B regulatory axis in subsequent investigations. For instance, ATP7A deficiency in the intestine leads to reduced copper efflux into the blood, accumulation of copper in enterocytes. ATP7A silencing in IECs impairs cell proliferation [32]. Although ATP7B is less abundant in the colon than ATP7A, we confirmed its expression by WB (Fig. 5). Fontes et al. reported that Atp7b−/− rodents develop spontaneous colitis characterized by crypt distortion, barrier disruption, and mitochondrial dysfunction, which likely stems from intracellular copper mislocalization rather than colon copper absorption [33]. Consistently, in our TNBS-induced colitis model, Atp7b−/− mice exhibited exacerbated disease, as shown by reduced colon length and elevated histopathological scores (Fig. S8C–F).

Our work demonstrates that epithelial GRX1 is critical for maintaining copper homeostasis by stabilizing ATP7B. Loss of GRX1 leads to ATP7B degradation, thereby impairing copper efflux. The resulting intracellular copper accumulation in enterocytes ultimately triggers cell death. Notably, in DSS-induced Grx1−/− mice, we observed a simultaneous increase in copper levels in both colon tissue and serum. We postulated that the elevated serum copper reflects liberation of copper into the systemic circulation following copper-dependent cell death. This pathomechanism mirrors observations in ATP7B-mutant Wilson disease patients: early-stage hepatic copper accumulation with hypocupremia ultimately progresses to toxic copper release upon exceeding hepatic storage capacity, inducing ectopic deposition and extrahepatic injury.

Dysfunction of the GRX1–ATP7B axis compromises epithelial barrier integrity through multiple, interconnected mechanisms. Impairment of this axis induces intestinal epithelial cell copper toxicity, which diminishes the population of viable epithelial cells and thereby directly weakens the physical barrier. Furthermore, GRX1 deficiency downregulates the key tight-junction protein CLAUDIN1 in vivo and reduces TEER in vitro, whereas GRX1 overexpression elevates CLAUDIN1 levels and enhances TEER, effects abolished by the catalytically inactive C8S mutant. Given that GRX1 regulates CLAUDIN1, a protein that not only forms the structural scaffold of tight junctions but also anchors to the cytoskeleton and controls paracellular charge selectivity, disruption of the GRX1–ATP7B axis likely undermines tight-junction assembly and integrity. Together, these alterations delineate a coordinated pathway through which GRX1–ATP7B dysfunction perturbs epithelial homeostasis and compromises intestinal barrier function.

Previous studies revealed that GRX1 regulated the S-glutathionylation of multiple proteins to inhibit or activate their functions. First, we found that total tissue PSSG levels were increase at a molecular weight higher than 130 kD in DSS-treated Grx1−/− mice. Then, we utilized the STRING database to identify proteins that interact with GRX1. Interestingly, we identified only ATP7B (165 kD) as the bona fide substrate and key effector for the anti-copper toxicity of GRX1. Further study confirmed that GRX1 associated with ATP7B. Protein S-glutathionylation can change the structure and function of target proteins, thereby may affect their degradation [34]. Previous data indicated that ATP7B could be degraded via the lysosomal degradation pathway or the proteasomal pathway [35]. In our studies, we found the deficiency of GRX1 induced the degradation of ATP7B in the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway in colitis. Further studies are needed to determine the specific E3 ligases and deubiquitinases responsible for regulating ATP7B degradation. Additionally, definitive proof of direct binding between GRX1 and ATP7B requires future validation by techniques such as proximity ligation assay (PLA). Notably, our analysis reveals conserved PSSG elevation across four analogous molecular weight ranges (40-55, 100-130, 130-180, and >180 kD) in both human ulcerative colitis and DSS-induced mice colitis, indicating shared redox stress mechanisms in inflammatory bowel disease pathogenesis (Fig. 1A and B). However, there were significant differences in the main types of PSSG among human and mice. Human samples exhibit peak elevation at 40-55 kD, whereas mice samples demonstrate predominant high-molecular-weight smears (>150 kD). This discrepancy likely reflects distinct pathophysiological contexts: chronic human inflammation versus acute mice injury and/or species-specific protein targeting. Critically, the absence of proteomic data for mice PSSG modifications currently limits mechanistic interpretation of these high-MW complexes, necessitating future mass spectrometry characterization to resolve cross-species functional implications. It is important to acknowledge that the clinical cohort for the glutathionylation analysis was relatively modest; consequently, larger, well-powered studies are required to validate the generalizability of these findings to a broader IBD population.

In clinical applications, PFD, a clinical drug used for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [36], has been shown to induce the expression of GRX1 and ameliorate DSS-induced colitis in our study. These results demonstrate that PFD is a promising therapeutic agent for IBD. In idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, PFD is generally believed to act on fibroblasts by inhibiting the TGF-β pathway [37]. However, emerging evidence indicates that PFD can also modulate cellular inflammatory responses. Regarding its potential link to GRX1 expression, we propose two possible mechanisms based on recent findings. First, PFD may regulate GRX1 at the transcriptional level. Transcriptomic analysis in a liver fibrosis model revealed that Grx1 was among the most significantly altered genes following PFD treatment, potentially through modulation of STAT5b expression and its subsequent binding to the Grx1 promoter region [13]. Second, given that PFD can scavenge ROS and inhibit lipid peroxidation [14], it may indirectly influence the glutathionylation status of GRX1 by altering the cellular redox environment. Further studies are warranted to identify the direct drug targets of PFD in the context of IBD and to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which PFD regulates GRX1 expression, ultimately exploring its therapeutic potential in human IBD and establishing GRX1 as a viable target for intervention.

The Grx1 knockout (Grx1−/−) and Atp7b knockout (Atp7b−/−) mouse model on a C57BL/6 genetic background was generated via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome engineering by Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou, China). All experimental animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at the Medical Science Institution of Zhejiang Province (Hangzhou, China). 6- to 8-weeks-old male mice were used for all experiments. All animal experiments were performed according to guidelines approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, College of Medicine, Zhejiang University (approval No.2024-1189).

To construct IBD mice model, mice were administered with 3%-3.5% DSS (MP Biomedicals, 9011-18-1) in the drinking water for 5-7 days or rectally injected with 100 μL 2.5% TNBS (Sigma-Aldrich, P2297), as described previously [38,39].

For Grx1 overexpression, adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) constructs carrying either murine glutaredoxin-1 (AAV9-Grx1) or null control (AAV9-NC) were acquired from GENECHEM (Shanghai, China). Systemic transduction was achieved via tail vein injection of 3 × 1011 viral genomes (vg) per mouse suspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

Daily copper toxicity inhibition was implemented through intragastric administration of 20 mg/kg TTM (Sigma-Aldrich, 323446). Concurrently, intervention of PFD (Selleckchem, S2907) involved once-daily oral gavage at 240 mg/kg dose.

Serum and colon sections from IBD patients or healthy control subjects were obtained from the Department of Gastroenterology, First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University. All the experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, College of Medicine, Zhejiang University, and the studies abided by the Declaration of Helsinki principles (approval No. IIT20240282B-R1).

GRX1 activity was assayed as described previously [12]. Briefly, colon tissues and cells were lysed in 137 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 130 mM NaCl, and 1% NP-40. Lysates were then cleared by centrifugation and incubated with reaction buffer consisting of 137 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0), 0.5 mM GSH (Sigma-Aldrich, G4251), 1.2 units GSSG reductase (Roche, 10105678001), 2.5 mM 2-hydroxyethyl disulfide (Sigma-Aldric, h380474), 0.35 mM NADPH (Roche, 10107824001), 1.5 mM EDTA (pH 8.0). The reaction was prepared in a 96-well plate and proceeded at 30 °C. The consumption of NAPDH was followed spectrophotometrically at 340 nm. Data were expressed in units (U)/g protein in which 1 U equals the oxidation of 1 μmol NADPH/min.

S-glutathionylated proteins in colon tissue were assayed by iodo-TMT labeling method provide by PTM BIO company (Hangzhou, China). The resulting MS/MS data were processed using MaxQuant search engine (v.1.6.15.0). Tandem mass spectra were searched against the Homo_sapiens_9606_SP_20231220.fasta (20429 entries) concatenated with reverse decoy and contaminants database. Trypsin/P was specified as cleavage enzyme allowing up to 2 missing cleavages. Min. Peptide length was set as 7 and max. number of modification per peptide was set as 5. The mass tolerance for precursor ions was set as 20 ppm in first search and 20 ppm in main search, and the mass tolerance for fragment ions was set as 20 ppm. Carbamidomethyl on Cys was specified as fixed modification. Acetylation on protein N-terminal, oxidation on Met and S-glutathionlyation were specified as variable modifications. False discovery rate (FDR) of protein, peptide and PSM was adjusted to < 1%.

Vials containing snap-frozen colon samples were sent on dry ice to Novogene (China), where RNA was extracted from samples using standard extraction protocols (TRIzol). RNA was quality-checked with electropherograms, gel images and RNA integrity number (RIN); RNA with an RIN of greater than six was deemed of sufficient quality for gene expression profiling experiments. Library preparation, clustering, and sequencing were done by Novogene Co., Ltd. The raw sequencing data have been deposited in NCBI BioProject (PRJNA1197761).

All results were presented as the mean ± SD. Two-tailed Student's t-test (for two group comparison) or ANOVA analysis (for multi-group comparison) were performed using GraphPad Prism 8. A difference was considered statistically significant when P < 0.05. Statistical significance: ∗, P < 0.05; ∗∗, P < 0.01; ∗∗∗, P < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗, P < 0.0001; ns, not significant.
