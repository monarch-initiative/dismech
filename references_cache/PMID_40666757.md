# Gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina with vitreous hemorrhage complication in a Vietnamese child.
**Authors:** Minh-Phu N, Giang NH, Ngan-Ha N, Thu NHH, Ha NH
**Journal:** Oman J Ophthalmol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.4103/ojo.ojo_247_24](https://doi.org/10.4103/ojo.ojo_247_24)

## Content

Gyrate atrophy (GA) of the choroid and retina is a rare autosomal recessive inherited disease characterized by elevation of plasma ornithine levels due to deficiency of the mitochondrial enzyme ornithine aminotransferase resulting in progressive chorioretinal atrophy, nyctalopia, and possibly blindness. This report presents the case of a 7-year-old Vietnamese boy with no history of trauma and a complaint of sudden visual loss over the past 6 weeks. Fundus examination showed vitreous hemorrhage in the right eye and chorioretinal atrophy with hyperpigmented margins in the periphery retina of the left eye. Based on the high serum ornithine level and compound heterozygous mutations detected in the ornithine aminotransferase (OAT) gene of the patient, he was diagnosed with GA. The patient underwent a right eye vitrectomy and was supplemented with Vitamin B6 and an arginine-restricted diet. Four months after surgery, the patient's visual acuity was significantly improved, and there was no progression of chorioretinal atrophy or recurrent vitreous hemorrhage. GA is an extremely rare disease and difficult to diagnose, especially when accompanied by vitreous hemorrhage. Combining genetic tests with clinical examinations helped diagnose accurately, and prompt treatment could restore the patient's visual function.

Gyrate atrophy (GA), first described by Fuchs in 1896, is a rare autosomal recessive disease caused by mutations in the ornithine aminotransferase (OAT) gene, leading to the deficiency in ornithine-δ-aminotransferase (OAT). The OAT is a mitochondrial matrix enzyme that catalyzes the transfer of the delta-amino group from L-ornithine. The low level of OAT accounts for the tenfold to twentyfold elevation in plasma ornithine levels, which results in progressive chorioretinal degeneration.[1]

Patients with GA initially complain of decreasing visual acuity and visual field constriction. Ocular manifestations include the appearance of sharply demarcated, circular areas of chorioretinal atrophy with hyperpigmented margins in the midperipheral fundus. These symptoms usually appear in the first three decades of life and often lead to blindness in the fourth to seventh decade. In addition, myopia, posterior subcapsular cataracts, and vitreous opacities may also be present.[2] To date, a broad spectrum of mutations in the OAT gene locus has been identified in GA patients. Therefore, OAT gene analysis helps diagnose a person with clinically suspected GA.

We report a rare GA case of a Vietnamese child hospitalized with clinical complications of sudden visual acuity decrease and vitreous hemorrhage. Further examinations, treatment indications, and outcomes will be reported in detail.

A 7-year-old boy, with no history of trauma, presented with a complaint of sudden, painless vision loss over the past 6 weeks. His best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was light perception in the right eye and 20/80 in the left eye. His refractive error measured +0.50/−3.50 × 40 for the right eye and plano/−3.25 × 170 for the left eye. Anterior segment examination and intraocular pressure were normal. In the right eye, fundus examination showed vitreous hemorrhage [Figure 1a], and brightness (B)-scan ultrasound showed multiple mobile dot echoes in the vitreous cavity [Figure 1b]. In the left eye, fundus examination showed patchy, sharply demarcated circular areas of chorioretinal atrophy with hyperpigmented margins in the peripheral retina [Figure 1c], whereas the fovea was unaffected [Figure 1d]. Cystoid macular edema was detected in the left eye on optical coherence tomography [Figure 1e]. A full-field electroretinogram showed markedly impaired photopic and scotopic responses in both eyes. Amino acid analysis revealed a high serum ornithine level (742.34 μmol/L), compared to the normal range of 10–163 μmol/L. The levels of arginine, creatinine, lysine, glutamine, and other amino acids in the serum were within the normal range. The clinical diagnosis was consistent with OAT deficiency and GA of the choroid and retina.

Whole exome sequencing followed by Sanger validation analysis of the patient’s DNA identified that he was compound heterozygous for variants in the OAT gene (Reference transcript ID NM_000274.3), which were classed as pathogenic according to the Clinical Variant database. One mutation was a C-to-T transition at nucleotide 722 (c.C722T), resulting in a substitution of proline at position 241 to leucine (p.P241 L). The other was a C-to-T transition at nucleotide 677, resulting in a substitution of alanine at position 226 to valine (p.A226V). The extended genetics test for unaffected family members has shown that his father was heterozygous for c.C722T (p.P241 L), and his mother was heterozygous for c.C677T (p.A266V). His elder sister and younger brother did not carry mutant alleles of the OAT gene [Figure 2].

The patient underwent vitrectomy and retinal endolaser photocoagulation for the right eye. During surgery, the cause of the vitreous hemorrhage was identified as neovascularization at the border of atrophic retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) in the peripheral retina. At the end of the surgery, the patient received an antivascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) injection (1.25 mg/0.05 mL). Post-surgery, the patient was supplemented with Vitamin B6 and placed on an arginine-restricted diet.

After 4 months of treatment, the patient’s BCVA was 20/60 in both eyes. Fundus examination showed no progression of chorioretinal atrophy or recurrent vitreous hemorrhage. Hyporeflective spaces, separated by linear bridging elements suggestive of cystoid macular edema, were observed in the right eye. Ultrawide field fundus images of both eyes showed multiple sharply demarcated lesions sparing the macula, typical of GA of the choroid and retina [Figure 3a and b]. In addition, neovascularization elsewhere was observed as the cause of vitreous hemorrhage [Figure 3a]. Fluorescein angiogram in the late phase shows leakage at the macula [Figure 3c]. Moreover, there was a mild leakage of fluorescein at the margin of the healthy-appearing retina, where it abuts the gyrate lesions on fluorescein angiogram [Figure 3d-f].

This is the first report of GA of the choroid and retina in a Vietnamese child complicated by vitreous hemorrhage caused by pathogenic variants in the OAT gene.

Clinically, patients with GA initially complain of decreased visual acuity and night vision loss. The visual acuity may suddenly reduce due to vitreous hemorrhage, an infrequent complication of GA. The mechanism underlying this complication is likely the rupture of fragile retinal vessels, typically resulting from retinal neovascularization caused by ischemia in areas of chorioretinal atrophy. Only two cases of vitreous hemorrhage complication in GA patients have been reported to date.[34] The most challenging aspect of surgery for GA with vitreous hemorrhage is stopping the bleeding from neovascularized vessels. Diathermy is not an ideal solution because it may cause iatrogenic retinal tears. Although laser energy is not absorbed in areas of choroidal atrophy due to the lack of pigment in the RPE, the heat effect of the laser may cause vasoconstriction and vascular occlusion. Therefore, laser photocoagulation directed at fragile, flowing vessels may be an effective solution.

For ophthalmic evaluation of the condition, optical coherence tomography, ultrawide field imaging, electroretinography (ERG), and fluorescein angiography should be performed in GA patients. Optical coherence tomography is useful in the evaluation of the macular area. It can reveal various features, including intraretinal cystic spaces, cystoid macular edema, foveoschisis, macular holes, choroidal neovascularization, epiretinal membranes, foveal thinning, outer retinal tubulations, and choroidal thinning. Ultra-wide field imaging can provide superior visualization of the retinal periphery along with the posterior pole. Fluorescein angiography shows prominent RPE transmission window defects and choriocapillaris atrophy with a hyperfluorescent lining along the borders of these defects. Large choroidal vessels can be seen through these defects. Moreover, fluorescein angiography can also demonstrate leakage in cases of GA due to choroidal neovascularization. ERG typically reveals diminished a- and b-wave responses of rods and cones, which may become undetectable in advanced disease; however, nearly normal ERG findings in GA have also been reported.

The main differential diagnoses of GA include choroideremia, retinitis pigmentosa, congenital stationary night blindness, X-linked retinoschisis, pigmented paravenous retinochoroidal atrophy, and bifocal chorioretinal atrophy. Advanced choroideremia, characterized by generalized atrophy of the RPE and choriocapillaris, may be mistaken for GA. Choroideremia is an X-linked disorder in which the macula may be affected earlier than in GA.

Treatment of GA includes dietary modifications to lower the elevated systemic ornithine levels and treatment for complications such as vitreous hemorrhage or posterior cataracts. Restricting arginine in the diet (avoiding nuts, seeds, cereals, dairy products, seafood, meat, chicken, watermelon, and chocolate), the precursor amino acid for ornithine has been shown to effectively lower plasma ornithine levels and slow the progression of chorioretinal degeneration in both humans and GA mouse models.[5] However, many patients continue to experience progressive chorioretinal degeneration despite reduced plasma ornithine levels following arginine restriction, possibly due to genetic heterogeneity. Some patients may also benefit from Vitamin B6 supplementation, which lowers plasma ornithine levels by increasing the activity of the pyridoxine-dependent OAT enzyme. The response to treatment depends on whether patients are pyridoxine responders or nonresponders, possibly due to different mutations affecting the OAT gene. However, fewer than 10% of patients with GA reported in the literature are “B6 responders.”[6]

Recently, the combination of clinical and genetic tests has been shown to be helpful in improving the diagnosis of pediatrics with retinal diseases in Vietnam.[7] Genetic examinations have revealed the etiology of the disease in patients and identified the risk of its development in each family member. This study confirmed that the patient’s siblings did not inherit pathogenic variants and are not at risk of developing GA. Their parents are carriers who have lived for generations in an area where marriage within the same commune is common. The surveillance and management of recessive pathogenic variants in that area should be prioritized.

We combined clinical and genetic examinations to diagnose our first GA patient with complications of vitreous hemorrhage. The patient underwent a vitrectomy, an anti-VEGF injection, Vitamin B6 supplementation, and an arginine-restricted diet. The evaluation of treatment processes is positive in protecting the patient’s vision. In addition, we have provided new information on pathogenic OAT variants in a specific geographical region of Vietnam.
