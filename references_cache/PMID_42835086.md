# PACS1 syndrome variant alters the proteomic landscape of developing cortical organoids.
**Authors:** Gomez-Maqueo X, Rylaarsdam LE, Woo A, Schroder AL, Rakotomamonjy J, Bossert CL, Smith TA, Ruiz S, Gilardi J, Klei L, Devlin B, MacDonald ML, Guemez-Gamboa A
**Journal:** iScience (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.isci.2026.117566](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2026.117566)

## Content

Patient-derived brain organoids have been used to model NDDs and identify pathogenic mechanisms. While most studies have evaluated the mechanisms by which NDD variants affect the transcriptome, studies examining the proteome remain limited. Here, we examined the effect of the PACS1 p.R203W variant, which causes PACS1 syndrome, on the proteomic landscape of brain organoids and compared the results with a transcriptomic dataset. Although we observed limited overlap between transcripts and proteins, most dysregulated processes converged. The proteomic data not only reinforced the importance of these developmental processes in p.R203W pathogenesis but also revealed dysregulated pathways not identified in the transcriptomic analysis. Key dysregulated transcripts and proteins in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids were classified as NDD-causative and ASD-risk genes. Our results highlight the potential of proteomics to complement transcriptomic studies by providing deeper insight into the mechanisms underlying NDDs while revealing additional therapeutic targets that may not be identifiable through transcriptomics alone.

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are heterogenous and usually present with complex etiology. Several large-scale genetic studies investigating the cause of NDDs such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD), intellectual disability (ID), and epilepsy have been conducted, and have led to the identification of hundreds of risk genes. However, our understanding of the mechanisms by which the observed genetic variants act has lagged and consequently has limited translation of genetic findings into clinical treatments.1,2

In recent years, patient-derived brain organoids have been increasingly used to identify pathogenic mechanisms and possible therapeutic targets.3 While most of these studies investigate the mechanisms by which risk genes and pathogenic variants affect the transcriptome,4,5,6,7,8,9,10 studies considering the proteome are limited.11,12,13 However, mRNA levels do not always predict protein levels, as protein expression and degradation are regulated by mechanisms beyond transcription.14 Here, we uncover that investigating the proteomic landscape of brain organoids during maturation provides further resolution of NDD pathogenesis, adding to the mechanisms observed with transcriptomic analysis. To do so, we examine the effect of the pathogenic variant PACS1 p.R203W, causative of PACS1 syndrome, on the proteomic landscape of brain organoids using tandem mass tag (TMT) mass spectrometry.

PACS1 syndrome (MIM615009) is characterized by ASD, ID, craniofacial abnormalities, and epilepsy15,16,17 and results from a recurrent de novo c.607C>T variant in phosphofurin acidic cluster sorting 1 (PACS1) that leads to an arginine to tryptophan (p.R203W) substitution. This missense variant is present in all reported patients except for one known arginine to glutamine p.R203Q substitution.17
PACS1 encodes a multifunctional sorting protein required for furin localization to the trans-Golgi network (tGN).18,19 In addition to tGN retrieval, PACS1 mediates the recycling of endocytosed furin from early endosomes to the cell surface and the delivery of proteins to the primary cilium.20,21,22 PACS1 has been shown to interact with over 50 other proteins20,23,24,25,26 via acidic cluster sorting motifs.24,27,28,29 PACS1 is also present in the nucleus30,31 where it mediates DNA replication repair,32,33 contributes to the nuclear export of viral transcripts.31

Recent reports explore the pathogenesis of the PACS1 p.R203W variant. Mice overexpressing PACS1 p.R203W exclusively in cortical neurons present with increased dendrite arborization and decreased postsynaptic currents, possibly due to a pathogenic interaction between PACS1 p.R203W and HDAC6. Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) directed toward both PACS1 and HDAC6 ameliorate these defects.34 Transcriptomic analysis in patient-derived brain organoids revealed a PACS1 p.R203W dependent dysregulation in cell projection organization in immature neurons, while mature neurons exhibited an upregulation in synaptic signaling processes. Subsequent characterization of spontaneous electrical activity revealed that induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived neurons harboring the p.R203W variant display asynchrony and an increased network burst duration.9 Finally, the PACS1 syndrome-associated variant altered PACS1 localization but had no effect on neuron morphology and synaptic patterns in C. elegans.35 Although these studies have started to investigate the impact of the PACS1 p.R203W variant, the mechanisms by which the variant affects neurodevelopment are still poorly understood. This in turn results in limited therapeutic options for patients, which at present only encompass non-invasive behavioral approaches and sensory integration therapies to manage symptoms.36,37 Here we assess the developmental proteomic landscape in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids to further understand PACS1 syndrome pathogenesis. We then compared the dysregulated process uncovered both by proteomic and transcriptomic approaches. Our findings underscore the importance of integrating data across multiple regulatory levels to capture the complex pathogenic mechanisms underlying NDDs.

Using TMT mass spectrometry, we identified 3,766 unique proteins across organoids at different developmental stages. Approximately 60% of these proteins overlapped with those detected in the fetal brain, whereas overlap decreased when compared with the postnatal or adult brain (Figure S1A and Table S1). Higher correlation on protein expression was primarily observed within organoids and brain samples at earlier developmental stages (post-conception week 16 through 26), with protein abundance correlation increasing with organoid maturation (Figure S1B). First, we aimed to understand the proteomic landscape of maturing control brain organoids. We identified groups of proteins with common temporal trajectories by performing time series and clustering analysis. We classified proteins into three temporal patterns: upregulation, downregulation, and stable abundance over time. The up and downregulated patterns were further clustered into two main profiles: (1) continuous protein change, with steady increase/decrease in abundance (clusters “steady-up” and “steady-down”); and (2) delayed trend, where protein abundance remained stable during early differentiation (from day 21 to day 40), and subsequently increased/decreased at day 90 (clusters “delayed-up” and “delayed-down”) (Figure 1B and Table S2).

Next, we used gene ontology (GO) analysis to determine the biological processes involved within these distinct patterns (Table S3). As expected, many developmental processes changed with time, including neuron differentiation, axon extension, dendrite morphogenesis, synaptic organization/function upregulation (Figure 1C), and cell cycle and stem cell population maintenance downregulation (Figure 1D). This is consistent with the differentiation of neural precursors to neurons during organoid maturation. Within the upregulated clusters, the steady-up contained mostly neurodevelopmental processes, while the delayed-up was mostly associated with signaling. Likewise, clusters steady-down and delayed-down shared a downregulation of cell cycle and DNA replication or repair (Figure 1D). Stable proteins were associated with basic functions like primary metabolism, regulation of gene expression, and ribosome/translation-related processes (Figure S1C).

Finally, we compared the observed organoid processes with the developing fetal brain dynamics obtained from a reference dataset38 (Figure S1D and Table S4). We found significant overlap between clusters in organoids and the fetal brain (Table S5). Most enriched processes during fetal brain development were captured in the corresponding organoid clusters. These results confirm that our brain organoids recapitulate early stages of brain development, as previously reported,38 supporting their use to model protein dysregulation in PACS1 syndrome.

We then profiled the proteome of PACS1 syndrome patient-derived brain organoids to evaluate the effects of the PACS1 p.R203W pathogenic variant on protein regulation during development (Figure 1A). Organoids carrying the p.R203W variant developed similarly to control organoids by day 21, with only five proteins (HMGB3, HTATSF1, HM13, TCEAL3 and TCEAL5) showing upregulation (Figure S2A). However, upon maturation, 329 proteins in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids showed dysregulation when compared to controls (Table S6). Oxidative phosphorylation (ATP6V0A1, CYC1, NDUFA7) and intracellular calcium ion homeostasis (ATP1B1, CALB2, LETM1, NPTN) were among the observed enriched processes resulting from 167 upregulated proteins in organoids carrying the p.R203W variant (Figure S2B and Table S7). By day 40, most dysregulated proteins were related to established PACS1 functions relating to endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-to-Golgi transport (ARF4, SEC23A, SNAPIN). Upregulated proteins at day 40 were involved in neuron projection development, such as the ligand of Eph-related receptor tyrosine kinases EFNB1 and the transcription factor FOXG1. This was confirmed to be upregulated by the western blot (Figure S2C). In contrast, neuron projection development and synapse signaling related proteins like EFNB3, NF1, VDAC3, and SV2A were enriched by day 90. Other upregulated processes include the metabolism of amino acids (ASS1, GLO1, CTH, HSD17B10, PLOD2, SMS), and glycerolipids (PITPNM1, AGPAT4, TMEM68). Interestingly, the PACS1 interactors PTBP1,39 which is involved in mRNA processing, and WDR37,40 were also upregulated (Figures S2A and S2B). On the other hand, 173 down-regulated proteins were enriched in processes including cell cycle division, DNA replication, and mRNA splicing. This further included the RNA-binding protein PCBP4, which is involved in mRNA stability41 and RNA-binding/splicing regulator PTBP2, which is also involved in brain development. The intermediate filament protein NES, which plays a role in proliferation and differentiation,42 was also found to be among the downregulated proteins. Decreased abundance of NES was confirmed by the western blot (Figure S2C).

After the data normalization and exploration phase (Figure S3), principal component analysis (PCA) indicated maturation time to be the strongest driver of variance when comparing PACS1(+/+) and PACS1(+/R203W) organoids at days 21, 40, and 90 (Figures S3C–S3E). Consequently, we investigated whether the presence of the PACS1 variant impacts groups of proteins that share common temporal trajectories. We used the short time series (STEM) Java analytical pipeline to compare maturation trajectories.43 PACS1(+/R203W) organoids also presented with the three major patterns (upregulation, downregulation, and stable) and profiles (steady vs. delayed) observed in controls (Figure 2A and Table S8). However, several proteins switched abundance patterns (Figure 2B). The profile rearrangement resulted in four additional clusters, for a total of eight clusters found in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids. The stable-up cluster from PACS1(+/R203W) organoids had a strong enrichment in oxidative phosphorylation (23 out of 33 proteins), brain development, and neuron differentiation. Proteins that gain upregulation include glucose transporter GLUT1, transcription factor FOXG1, and mitochondrial genes UQCRQ, VDAC1, and ATP5PF (Figure 2C and Table S10). The stable-down cluster was enriched in regulation of mRNA processing and translation. Proteins that gain downregulation include ribosomal subunit protein RPL38, alpha subunit of the elongation factor-1 complex EEF1A1, and developmental proteins Nestin,42 BRD2,44 and Wnt family member 7B WNT7B.45

The down-stable cluster was enriched in the metabolism of the nucleotides process. Proteins related to metabolism that lost their down regulation and remained stable over time have also been associated with brain development and function. These proteins include, ribokinase (RBKS), implicated in glucose metabolic disorders and recently associated to psychiatric disorders,46 phosphoribosylpyrophosphate synthetase 1 (PRPS1), implicated in multiple syndromic and non-syndromic diseases causing hearing loss, hypotonia, and ataxia,47 as well as catalase (CAT), implicated in oxidative stress-related diseases and ASD.48

Finally, the up-stable cluster was comprised eight proteins which lost their temporal upregulation and remained stable in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids, with no significant enrichment. In this group, however, several proteins have relevant functions in the brain, such as dendrite morphogenesis (SS18L1/CREST),49 autophagy, and neuron projection regeneration after injury (VIM),50 and regulation of intracellular pH homeostasis (CA2).51

These results suggest altered developmental trajectories in the PACS1(+/R203W) organoids with premature or ectopic upregulation of selected neuronal/metabolic programs and processes related to known PACS1 functions (i.e., Golgi to ER vesicle transport). Although the observed protein clustering and enrichment of neuronal and metabolic processes could be driven by altered cell-type proportions, scRNA sequencing, and immunostaining confirmed that the PACS1 p.R203W variant does not alter the cytoarchitectural composition or organization of organoids.9 To better understand the possibility of altered developmental trajectories in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids, we performed pseudotime analysis from our previously generated scRNA-seq data.9 Despite similar organoid composition, we observed a significant shift in the developmental trajectories of PACS1(+/R203W) organoids when compared to controls (KS test, all lineages: D = 0.014613, p = 0.01803; Figure S4). The main developmental trajectory bifurcated into two branches, representing immature neurons and mature neurons (Figures S4A and S4B). Pseudotime ordering showed PACS1(+/R203W) organoids contained a significantly greater density of cells at later pseudotime values than controls within both the immature (Figures S4C and S4D, middle) and mature (Figures S4C and S4D, right) neuronal lineages. A one-sided Kolmogorov-Smirnov test confirms the cumulative distribution of pseudoage values in the PACS1(+/R203W) organoids in contrast to controls in all tested subgroups (Figure S4). Further investigation is required to determine whether these transcriptomic and proteomic shifts reflect accelerated maturation due to the PACS1 p.R203W variant. However, although PACS1(+/R203W) organoids exhibited signatures of early neuronal maturation, they also showed dysregulation of numerous mRNA-related processes, including transport and processing, which may contribute to defective or incomplete differentiation (Table S10). Finally, our results further uncovered the dysregulation of known PACS1 interactors at the protein level that were not resolved on the transcriptome level.9

Post-translational phosphorylation is a main regulator of protein function. Thus, we analyzed whether the PACS1(+/R203W) organoids also presented with alterations in phosphorylation during maturation. Quantification of phospho-peptides in organoids resolved a total of 4,238 high-confidence phosphorylation sites mapped to 1,754 unique proteins (Tables S6 and S11). Of these mapped phosphorylated peptides, 1,112 (63.4%) had a corresponding protein that allowed to correct phosphorylation occupancy by protein abundance (Figure S5). The time series and clustering analysis considering phosphorylation abundance uncovered the same eight clusters and stable patterns that resulted from the protein abundance analysis (Figure 3A and Tables S12 and S13). A total of 530 phosphorylation sites on 325 unique proteins were dysregulated in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids (Table S14). Examples include two PACS1-known interactors, PACS2 (PACS1 paralog) and HDAC1, both significant at abundance and occupancy levels. PACS1(+/R203W) organoids presented with increased PACS2-S437 phosphorylation, which is required for PACS2-mediated trafficking and repression of apoptosis.52 HDAC1 had decreased phosphorylation at residues S421 and S423, which is essential for its repressive function.53 Interestingly, both serine residues are also phosphorylated by CK2 (found to have lost its temporal regulation and remained stable in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids). Due to lack of specific antibodies for the aforementioned phosphosites, future validation experiments are required to confirm these phosphorylation events and their potential involvement in PACS1 syndrome pathogenesis.

Given that phosphorylation may result in either an induction or inhibition of protein function, and the activation status of a protein itself could have diverse effects on pathway activity, we used the phosphosite database to understand the functional impact of the observed dysregulated phosphorylation sites.54 Phosphosite contained annotations on 51 out of 530 phosphorylation sites across 41 unique proteins, with few proteins having multiple phosphorylated sites (Figure S6 and Table S15). To perform the functional enrichment analysis, we grouped phosphorylated proteins that changed in abundance (clusters: stable -up and -down) or lost (clusters up- and down-stable) phosphorylation regulation in contrast to the controls. Although the early-up cluster also gained upregulation, due to its uniqueness to this dataset, we performed the functional enrichment analysis separately (Figure 3C and Table S16).

The phosphorylated proteins that gained up/downregulation in the PACS1(+/R203W) organoids over time while remaining stable in controls are involved in axonogenesis, as well as synaptic function and organization processes (Figure 3C, orange pie plot). Examples of these proteins include synapsin1 (SYN1, significant at abundance and occupancy), implicated in both synaptogenesis and neurotransmitter release, whose phosphorylation at residues S568/S570 causes conformational changes that prevents its binding to actin or synaptic vesicles55; synaptojanin1 (SYNJ1, significant at abundance and occupancy), which has a prominent role in synaptic vesicle recycling56; the beta subunit of the AP-3 protein complex (AP3B2, significant at abundance level), a known PACS1 interactor that helps form clathrin-coated synaptic vesicles56; and DNM1-S774 (significant at abundance level), which regulates clathrin-mediated endocytosis through its activation by dephosphorylation.57 Further validation experiments are required to confirm the phosphorylation status of these proteins in PACS1(+/R203W) brain organoids. These experiments will be prioritized upon the availability of the required phospho-specific antibodies.

Similarly, as observed in the other gain of regulation clusters, the early-up cluster had enrichment for axon-related function and organization, but showed novel terms related to signaling through semaphorin 3 (SEMA3E) (Figure 3C, green pie plot). This result is in consensus with single-cell RNA sequencing analysis, which identified SEMA3E to be upregulated in PACS1(+/R203W) brain organoids.9 Our proteomic dataset shows that while there are no changes in SEMA3E, several proteins involved in its signaling pathway are phosphorylated, including CRMP1, DPYSL2, and DPYSL3 (all significant at abundance level). In particular, DPYSL2-S522 phosphorylation by CDK5 is involved in axonal guidance and spine development.58 Future studies aimed at investigating SEMA3E signaling in PACS1(+/R203W) models will confirm these observations.

Meanwhile, intracellular protein transport was the most enriched process in the clusters that lost regulation (Figure 3C, brown pie plot). Proteins that remained stable in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids include GLI3 (significant at abundance and occupancy), which has a dual function as a transcriptional activator and a repressor of the sonic hedgehog (Shh) pathway dependent on phosphorylation59 and LIN28A (significant at abundance level), an RNA-binding protein that is localized to the peri-ER and has been recently described as a global suppressor of genes in the secretory pathway.60

We next analyzed the overlap of protein identities with dysregulated abundance and phosphorylation status in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids, and with previously reported transcriptomic data.9 We re-analyzed the transcriptomic profiles of p.R203W organoids using a pseudobulk approach to estimate gene expression. This yielded a total of 454 differentially expressed genes between day 40 and day 90 (Figure S8 and Table S17). Surprisingly, we observed a low overlap of the total and dysregulated proteins, phosphopeptides, and the transcriptome, with just JAM2, a cell-adhesion protein from the immunoglobulin subfamily and key regulator of axon myelination61 (Figure 4A). Nevertheless, several affected processes converged (Figure S8). For example, neurogenesis, synapse organization, and axon-related processes are enriched due to dysregulated RNA expression, protein abundance, and phosphorylation status over time in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids when compared to controls (Figure S8). Converging processes were observed also only at the protein and phosphorylation level including mRNA transport, actin and microtubule organization, and cell cycle dynamics. Finally, some processes were exclusive, including oxidative phosphorylation and energy production that resulted only from the dysregulation of protein abundance (purple); and RNA splicing showing a gain of upregulation in phosphorylation status over time (yellow) (Figure S7). Next, we investigated whether there are convergent dysregulated genes in PACS1 syndrome compared to other NDDs. We performed gene set enrichment analysis on the dysregulated proteins and transcripts using the DISGENET, the Simons Foundation ASD (SFARI), and the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) gene databases. A total of 328 genes (20.7%) dysregulated in the PACS1(+/R203W) organoids compared to controls are additionally reported in these databases. In DISGENET, we found 249 genes that showed a significant enrichment in multiple nervous system disorders (Figure 4B and Table S18). These disorders overlapped with phenotypes observed in PACS1 syndrome patients like ASD, ID, seizures, and abnormal morphogenesis of the nervous system or facial features,16,62 as well as phenotypes yet to be reported, such as neurodegeneration. Interestingly, the core neurodevelopmental features of PACS1 syndrome like ID, delayed speech, and epilepsy15 were enriched in all levels of regulation, while the facial and muscular abnormalities were enriched in the proteome and phosphoproteome (i.e., wide nasal bridge, orbital separation, limb hypertonia, and unsteady gait).

We also identified multiple genes dysregulated in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids that are reported as causing NDD in OMIM or as ASD-risk genes in the SFARI database (Figures 4C and 4D and Table S19). Fifty-five NDD and 57 ASD genes showed dysregulation in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids over maturation (Figure 4C), mostly at the phosphoproteomic level. We also identified additional genes that were dysregulated at specific developmental timepoints across regulation levels (194 NDD and 98 ASD genes) (Figure 4D). These results highlight the complementarity of gene regulation mechanisms during development, supporting the benefits of including proteomic analysis to expand our understanding of NDD pathogenesis.

NDDs are highly heterogenous and can arise from variants across hundreds of genes.63,64 Several limitations have hampered our understanding of the mechanisms by which these variants act, limiting translation of genetic findings into the clinic.65 Here, we leveraged the potential of proteomics in brain organoids to gain insights into the pathogenic mechanisms of PACS1 syndrome. Proteomic approaches are usually bulk and therefore lack single-cell resolution. Thus, we reanalyzed our transcriptomic data,9 using a whole-organoid pseudobulk approach to better compare proteomic and transcriptomic profiles. The recaptured gene regulation signals showed high concordance of affected biological processes between both approaches. Despite the low overlap of dysregulated genes identified at the mRNA, protein, and phosphorylation levels in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids, most dysregulated processes converged. However, PACS1(+/R203W) organoids also presented with dysregulated abundance and phosphorylation dynamics of temporally regulated proteins that were not identified by transcriptomics, highlighting the importance of complementing transcriptomic studies with proteomic analysis. Experimental approaches that target both proteomics and transcriptomics from the sample samples could help understand discrepancies in identified dysregulated gene identities.

At the transcriptomic level, we identified many neurogenesis and synaptic function-associated processes affected primarily in maturing neurons.9 The proteomic data not only reinforced the importance of these processes for p.R203W pathogenesis, but also detected dysregulated processes linked to known PACS1 functions. For example, PACS1’s role in protein sorting explains the overrepresentation of vesicle-related processes. Moreover, several established PACS1 interactors were uncovered as dysregulated proteins, such as its paralog, PACS2. PACS1 and PACS2 evolutionarily emerged as a duplication in higher metazoans from an ancient PACS gene.19 PACS1 function primarily involves sorting from endosomes to the tGN, which showed temporal dysregulation of over 20 proteins in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids. Meanwhile, PACS2 evolved cargo-sorting functions from the Golgi to ER and vesicular coat preference for COPII; both processes upregulated in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids. The upregulation in these processes is consistent with the potential gain of PACS2 regulation through increased phosphorylation at S437, which favors PACS2 trafficking function.52 The increase in phosphorylated PACS2 could explain the vesicular transport and mitochondrial protein import defects suggested by the dysregulated proteomic signature in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids. Conversely, it may also explain the downregulation of apoptotic signaling pathway genes. Experiments designed to functionally measure these processes are required to confirm these observations. Of interest, a recurrent de novo missense mutation in the regulatory middle region of PACS2 is associated with PACS2 Syndrome, another NDD.66 Future research aimed at understanding the interconnection of PACS1 and PACS2 function, as well as their dysregulation and compensatory effects in disease, will help shed light on the pathogenesis of both PACS1 and PACS2 syndromes.

The processes that lost downregulation over time were related to recently described PACS1 functions, including proteins related to the maintenance of genome stability and DNA repair via interaction with HDAC2, HDAC3, and HDAC6 (20, 22). Although we did not observe protein abundance and phosphorylation status changes in HDAC2, 3, or 6, we found downregulation in HDAC5 at day 40, and HDAC1 had decreased phosphorylation over maturation. This suggests that PACS1 could also modulate DNA stability through these proteins. HDAC1 interacts with RB1 and SMARCA4 (both dysregulated at the phosphorylation level) in a repressive complex in resting neurons. This complex is abolished in a calcium-dependent manner to enable transcription,67 suggesting that the PACS1 p.R203W variant affects transcription regulation by destabilizing the repressive complex formed by HDAC1, RB1, and SMARCA4. Transcription dysregulation has been reported previously by our group and others.9,32,34 However, it remains unclear whether the HDAC1, RB1, and SMARCA4 complex plays a role in these processes.

Processes unrelated to known PACS1 functions also emerged, such as the enrichment associated with regulating nucleotide metabolism and protein translation in the down-stable cluster. There is growing evidence of the impact of protein translation on the pathogenesis of NDDs, including ASD and fragile X syndrome.68 Functional studies aimed at confirming these defects in protein translation would be a logical next step. This is the first time that either process has been implicated in the PACS1 syndrome pathogenesis, as these processes were not identified by transcriptomic analysis.9 Consequently, this result highlights the importance of collecting data from multiple regulatory levels to get a more complete picture of the pathogenic mechanisms when modeling NDDs.

Processes that seem unrelated to PACS1 function, but which have displayed upregulation in the PACS1(+/R203W) organoids, should be further explored. Considering current evidence suggests that the PACS1 p.R203W pathogenic variant may result in a gain of function,9,34,69 there is increased interest in generating targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at suppressing its toxic effects.34,36 Thus, processes that seem unrelated to PACS1 function but with observed upregulation in the PACS1(+/R203W) organoids should be a point of interest. Understanding whether the specific proteomic dysregulation results from the toxicity associated with the variant or as a compensatory mechanism to maintain cellular homeostasis would be especially relevant. Of further interest, most genes that we found dysregulated in PACS1(+/R203W) organoids have also been linked to NDDs and ASD.59,60,62 However, the overrepresentation of NDD- and ASD-related genes could also reflect the interconnectedness of neurodevelopmental pathways. Additional mechanistic analyses are required to understand if these effects are direct or indirect.

Finally, the presence of the PACS1 p.R203W variant seems to affect every regulatory level as we see convergence in processes related to the clinical features that patients manifest. This includes ASD, ID, and seizures,15,16 as well as phenotypes yet to be reported, such as neurodegeneration, all reflected in the dysregulation of mRNA and protein abundance as well as phosphorylation status. Interestingly, the facial and muscular abnormalities including wide nasal bridge, orbital separation, limb hypertonia, and unsteady gait, resulted mainly from enrichments in the proteome and phosphoproteome. This study provides a foundation for hypothesis generation and for designing experimental approaches aimed at validating the proposed pathogenic mechanisms. Our results highlight the potential of proteomic analyses to complement transcriptomic studies to enhance our understanding of NDD mechanisms and uncover additional therapeutic targets that may facilitate therapy development.

While we captured a broad range of proteins and phosphorylated peptides that recapitulate aspects of early brain development in brain organoids, this dataset does not fully represent the complexity or temporal dynamics of neurodevelopment. Proteomic data were generated from bulk tissue and therefore lack the single-cell resolution of transcriptomic approaches, limiting direct comparisons between these technologies. Likewise, the phosphopeptide findings require further validation to determine their mechanistic roles in PACS1 p.R203W pathogenesis. Pathway and biological function enrichment analyses are also limited by incomplete gene and pathway annotations. Although useful for interpreting large datasets, these analyses are descriptive and correlative and do not establish causal relationships. Similarly, pseudotime analysis provides a computational ordering of cellular states based on molecular profiles rather than a direct measure of developmental time and, therefore, should be interpreted with caution. Lastly, although brain organoids recapitulate key features of human brain development, they lack cell types that emerge later in development or originate from other germ layers, including astrocytes and mesoderm-derived microglia. These cells may also contribute to PACS1 syndrome pathogenesis. Therefore, the mechanisms identified in this study should be viewed as a foundation for future investigation rather than a comprehensive account of disease pathogenesis.

Stem cells were passaged as needed using ReLeSR medium (Stem Cell Technologies, 05872). AggreWell800 24-well plate (Stem Cell Technologies, 34811) were prepared and 3 million cells from each line were seeded per well. STEMdiff Forebrain Organoid Formation Medium was exchanged for 5 days and on day 6, embryoid bodies from each Aggrewell were filtered through a 37 μM strainer (Stem Cell Technologies, 27250) and transferred to two wells of a 6-Well Ultra-Low Adherent Plate (Corning, 3471) containing STEMdiff Forebrain Organoid Expansion Medium. STEMdiff Forebrain Organoid Expansion Medium was changed every other day until day 26 when it was replaced with STEMdiff Forebrain Organoid Differentiation Medium. Organoids were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight and transferred to 30% sucrose for at least 16h at 4 °C. Organoids were then embedded in OCT, snap frozen in dry ice and ethanol, and stored at −80 °C. Frozen organoids were cryosectioned 15–20 μm thick, dried for at least 1 h and washed with PBS for OCT removal. Antigen retrieval was performed by steaming slices at 99 °C for 10 min in a 10 mM citrate solution at pH 6.0. Slides were washed and blocked in a 10% NDS, 0.3% Triton X-100 PBS solution and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Primary antibodies used in this study include: CTIP2 (Abcam, ab18465, Dilution 1:200); SOX2 (EMD Millipore, Dilution 1:200). The overnight incubation was followed by PBS washes and secondary antibody incubation at 1:200 in a 5% NDS, 0.15% Triton X-100 solution for 90 min at room temperature. Secondary antibodies used in this study include donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen) and donkey anti-rat Alexa Fluor 594 (Invitrogen, A21209, Lot 2078918). DAPI (2 μg/mL) was incubated for 20 min. Slides were mounted with Fluoromount-G (Thermo Scientific, 00–4958–02) and imaged on a Nikon W1 spinning disk confocal microscope or BZX710 Keyence epifluorescent microscope.

Total protein was extracted from the organoids by sonication in lysis buffer (1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 125 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB), 75 mM NaCl, and HaltTM protease and phosphatase inhibitors). Lysate samples from two additional control cell lines were included to improve protein detection. See Table S20 for a detailed description of mass spectrometry samples.

Protein extraction and trypsin digestion. Total protein was extracted from the organoids by sonication in lysis buffer (1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 125 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB), 75 mM NaCl, and HaltTM protease and phosphatase inhibitors). Lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. Lysate samples from two additional control cell lines were included to improve protein detection. A different randomized block was used for each stage of sample processing, TMT labeling, and mass spectrometry analysis, balanced for genotype and timepoint. Total protein was estimated using microBCA assay according to the manufacturer instructions (Cat. No. 23235, Thermo Fisher). 20 μg total protein from each sample was reduced with dithiothreitol (DTT), alkylated with iodoacetamide (IAA), and digested with trypsin on S-Traps (Protifi) per manufacture protocol.

TMT labeling. Samples were first assigned to one of five sets of TMTPro16 blocks (plex’s), such that each plex was balanced for genotype and timepoint, with a random order in each plex (Tables S21 and S22). Organoid samples from two additional, age-matched control cell lines with no isogenic pair (WT4 and WT5) were included within each plex to improve peptide diversity capture. However, these samples were later removed from downstream analyses since they lacked an isogenic p.R203W. Pooled reference control for internal reference scaling (IRS) normalization was included within each plex. These reference controls were prepared by pooling equal amounts of lysate from all samples, which were digested separately, and then split in aliquots for TMT labeling. Peptide digests were reconstituted in 20 μL 100 mM TEAB and sonicated for 15 min at RT for TMT labeling. TMTPro reagents were brought to room temperature and dissolved in 20 μL Optima LC/MS-grade acetonitrile (ACN). 6 μL of ∼59 mM TMTPro reagents was added to 20 μL reconstituted peptide accordingly. All TMT plex’s were labeled at the same time. For the reference control, peptides were reconstituted in 80 μL. A total of 40 μL was labeled with 12 μL TMTPro 134 N. Labeling was performed at RT for 90 min with shaking at 400 rpm. The reaction was quenched by adding 3 μL 5% hydroxylamine/100 mM TEAB at RT for and shaken 15 min (6 μL for reference channel). All samples with in a plex were mixed along with 20 μg aliquots of each labeled pooled control, giving 320 μg total TMT labeled peptides per plex, and dried in a vacuum concentrator. Of this, 80 μg were used for peptide/protein quantification, and 240 μg was subject to phosphopeptide enrichment. Prior to peptide/protein quantification, the 80 μg aliquot was separated into 8 fractions using the Pierce High pH Reversed Phase Fractionation Kit per manufacturer protocol. The resulting fractions were dried down in a speed vac, and resuspended in 3% ACN, 0.1% formic acid.

Phosphopeptides enrichment. For phosphopeptide enrichment, the 240 μg TMT-labeled peptide aliquot were desalted with C18 spin column. Samples were resuspended and sonicated for 10 min in 300 μL buffer A (LC MS water 0.1% TFA) with 2% ACN. Resin was packed by centrifugation at 5,000 g for 2 min, activated twice with 300 μL ACN, and conditioned twice with 300 μL buffer A. Peptides were loaded once by centrifugation at 3,000 g for 1 min. Column was washed three times with 300 μL buffer A, and twice with 300 μL 5% methanol buffer A. Peptides were eluted twice with 300 μL 50% ACN 0.1% TFA and dried in a vacuum concentrator. Phosphopeptides were enriched using the AssayMAP Agilent Bravo instrument with Fe(III)-NTA IMAC resin according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Agilent, Cat. No. G5496-60085). Peptides were resuspended in 210 μL 80% ACN 0.1% TFA, bath sonicated for 10 min, vortexed for 10 min, and quickly centrifuged. The resin was primed with 50% ACN 0.1% TFA and equilibrated with equilibration/wash buffer 80% ACN 0.1% TFA. Samples were loaded into the syringes then dispensed into the Flow Through Collection plate. Syringes were washed with the Cartridge Wash Buffer discarded into waste, then repeated with Syringe Wash Buffer. The elution buffer was aspirated into the syringes and dispensed into the Eluate Collection plate. Phosphopeptides were eluted in 18 μL 1% ammonium hydroxide solution and acidified with 1.8 μL 20% FA, and subject to high pH reverse phase fractionation as described above but condensed to 5 fractions.

For peptide/protein quantification, TMT labeled peptides (∼1 μg) were loaded onto a heated PepMap RSLC C18 2 μm, 100 Å, 75 μm × 50 cm column (Thermo Scientific) and eluted over 180 min gradients optimized for each high pH reverse-phase fraction.82 Sample eluates were electrosprayed (2000 V) into a Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer for analysis. MS1 spectra were acquired at a resolving power of 120,000. MS2 spectra were acquired in the Ion Trap with CID (35%) in centroid mode. Real-time search (max search time = 34 s; max missed cleavages = 1; Xcorr = 1; dCn = 0.1; ppm = 5) were used to select ions for synchronous precursor selection for MS3. MS3 spectra were acquired in the Orbitrap with HCD (60%) with an isolation window = 0.7 m/z and a resolving power of 60,000, and a max injection time of 400 ms. For phosphopeptide quantification, 4 μL (out of 20) of the TMT labeled phosphopeptide enrichments were loaded onto a heated PepMap RSLC C18 2 μm, 100 Å, 75 μm × 50 cm column (Thermo Scientific) and eluted over a 180 min gradient: 1 min 2% B, 5 min 5% B, 160 min 25% B, 180 min 35% B. Sample eluate was electrosprayed (2000 V) into a Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer for analysis. MS1 spectra were acquired at a resolving power of 120,000. MS2 spectra were acquired in the Orbitrap with HCD (38%) in centroid mode with an isolation window = 0.4 m/z, a resolving power of 60,000, and a max injection time of 350 ms.

The raw MS files were processed using Proteome Discoverer version 2.4 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA). MS spectra were searched against the Homo sapiens Uniprot/SwissProt database. SEQUEST search engine was used (enzyme = trypsin, max. missed cleavage = 4, min. peptide length = 6, precursor tolerance = 10 ppm). Static modifications include carbamidomethyl (C, +57.021 Da) and TMT labeling (N-term and K, +304.207 Da for TMTpro16). Dynamic modifications include oxidation (M, +15.995 Da), Phosphorylation (S, T, Y, +79.966 Da, only for phosphopeptide dataset), acetylation (N-term, +42.011 Da), Met-loss (N-term, −131.040 Da), and Met-loss + Acetyl (N-term, −89.030 Da). PSMs were filtered by the Percolator node (max Delta Cn = 0.05, target FDR (strict) = 0.01, and target FDR (relaxed) = 0.05). Proteins were identified with a minimum of 1 unique peptide and protein-level combined q values <0.05. Reporter ion quantification was based on corrected S/N values with the following settings: integration tolerance = 20 ppm, method = most confident centroid, co-isolation threshold = 70, and SPS mass matches = 65.

Protein was extracted from organoids using a mixture of a 1× RIPA buffer (EMD Millipore Corp, #20–188) and protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo scientific, #1861284) in a 1:100 ratio. Organoids were lysed in this mixture using an insulin syringe and then incubated on ice for 5 min. Afterward, samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 10,000 × g at 4°C, and the resulting protein lysate supernatant was isolated. To quantify the protein lysate, a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo scientific, #23225) was used. The samples were then mixed with prepared 4× Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad, #1610747) and boiled at 95°C for 10 min. Protein lysate was loaded and ran on SDS page gels and then transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (thermo scientific, #88518). Membranes were blocked for an hour using Protein Free Blocking Buffer (LICORbio, #927–60001) and then immunoblotted in a primary antibody solution composed of primary antibody at the indicated dilution, 10 μL Tween 20 and protein free blocking buffer for a total of 5 mLs. Membranes were incubated for 1–3 nights at 4°C with gentle shaking. Primary antibodies used included Nestin Polyclonal antibody (1:1000), Anti-FOXG1 antibody [EPR18987] (1:1000), and Anti-alpha Tublin antibody [DM1A] (1:10,000). After primary antibody incubation, membranes were washed three times with TBST and then incubated at room temperature for an hour with a secondary antibody solution composed of 10 μL Tween 20, 5 μL 10% SDS buffer, and protein free blocking buffer for a total of 5 mLs. Secondary antibodies were used at 1:1000 and included IRDye® 800CW Goat anti-Rabbit IgG Secondary Antibody, IRDye® 680RD Goat anti-Mouse IgG Secondary Antibody, and IRDye® 800CW Goat anti-Mouse IgG Secondary Antibody. After incubation, membranes were washed three times with TBST and then imaged for protein signal using the Odyssey® Fc Imaging System (Li-Cor) and quantified with Image Studio (Li-Cor). Two membranes with two replicates per genotype each were used. Signal values were normalized by calculating a lane normalization factor to alpha-Tubulin within each membrane and then dividing each lane signal to its corresponding normalization factor. Normalized values in the WT were then averaged per membrane to then calculate the fold change in expression in p.R203W samples using the same reference. Fold change values were then log2 transformed to match the scale of the proteomic data.

Protein intensity data values were first normalized to adjust each TMT experiment to an equal signal per channel. A scaling value for each TMT plex and the internal reference scaling (IRS) factor were then calculated using the pooled reference samples. After which, a median normalization was performed followed by log2-transformation (Figures S4A and S4B). Relative occupancy of phosphorylated sites was also calculated for those peptides where the corresponding protein was also quantified by subtracting the peptide abundance from the log2 normalized values.83 Both occupancy and phosphopeptide abundance were used for downstream analyses. Further analysis through PCA indicated a batch effect associated with multiplex labeling in the second component of the graph (Figures S4C and S4D), which we ameliorated in the R package limma (v3.58.0) by duplicate correlation for each observation to model the plex as a batch, and included covariates to fit the limma model as ∼ genotype + day + cell line, while using the function removeBatchEffect for visualization of the model fit.71 Only the proteins and phosphopeptides present in all plexes and with high detection/localization confidence were used for downstream analysis. This stringent filter was implemented to better control batch effects, it filtered sex-dependent proteins and addressed the impossibility of differentiating the missing values from non-expressed proteins. Despite this stringent filter, we captured a diverse pool of proteins and phosphopeptides similar to other publicly available datasets.3

We used the Short Time-series Expression Miner software (STEM, v1.3.13),43 which compares and assigns each expression profile from the dataset to a set of distinct and representative models of temporal expression profiles based on the total timepoints. These model profiles are independent of the data and correspond to possible profiles of a protein change in expression over time. For its implementation, the batch-corrected normalized protein abundances in the organoids were averaged by isogenic line and timepoint. Samples that corresponded to the same genotype, isogenic line, timepoint, and differentiation were averaged to generate a representative replicate of that group. Then, a single input file containing all timepoints for each genotype, isogenic, and differentiation group were generated for STEM (Figure S9 and Tables S23 and S24). We also used a fetal brain proteome previously reported by Djuric et al.38 and selected the prenatal samples from all reported brain regions (ventricular zone, intermediate zone, subplate zone, and cortex). Fetal brain samples were processed using each region as a replicate for clustering. The STEM algorithm then transforms all trends to start at 0, generating a vector (0, v1—v0,
v2—v0, …, vn—v0) for each protein. Proteins are then assigned to the model profile to which their time series most closely matches based on correlation coefficient. The number of proteins assigned to each model profile is then tested for statistical significance against the number of proteins expected to be assigned to a profile. This number is estimated by randomly permuting the original time points, scaling the protein abundances, and then assigning them to the most closely matching model profiles. The average number of proteins assigned to a model profile over all permutations was used as the estimate of the expected number of genes assigned to the profile. This process was performed using a significance level of 0.05 of the Bonferroni-corrected p-value. The values used for each parameter are presented in Table S25. Several preliminary runs were performed to select the values that both offered clustering of most proteins and diversity of profiles, while also controlling the number of highly similar profiles. Protein overlap among analyzed datasets were performed using the packages GeneOverlap (v.1.40.0)73 and UpSetR (v.1.4.0).74

Previously published PACS1 syndrome single cell data9 was reanalyzed to quantify pseudo-bulk gene expression profiles. Data was first normalized using standard Seurat commands v5.372 and the count matrix was aggregated by organoid sample using DESeq2 v1.46 (number of organoids per group: WT D40 = 2, p.R203W D40 = 3, WT D90 = 2, p.R203W D90 = 2).75 Differential gene expression was assessed using a likelihood ratio test with an interaction design model on organoid age and genotype as the full model to test effects over maturation time, and a reduced model without the interaction term. For comparison of cell type lineages across pseudotime, all genotypes were down sampled to 10,000 randomly selected cells per organoid and used monocle3 to calculate transcriptomic landscape transitions from progenitor cells toward neuronal lineages.80 A one-sided Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to test whether the p.R203W trajectories were accelerated in contrast to controls by comparing the distribution of pseudotime values (Figure S4). Cell type proportions between genotypes were previously assessed, with no significant differences.9

Testing for enrichment was performed with the online tool Metascape (v3.5.20240901)76 using a hypergeometric test followed by the Benjamini-Hochberg p-value correction, selecting statistically significant terms at a cutoff value below −1.3 Log(q-value),76 and then simplifying redundancy in the resulting lists using rrvgo in R (v1.16).84 Enrichment tests were performed using the Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes85,86 and the gene-disease association (DisGeNET)87 databases. Visualizations were performed using webr (v.0.1.5)78 and ggplot2 functions (v3.5.1).79 The GO-term network was performed in Metascape using default settings and the human genome as a reference background. In brief, the 20 top-score clusters (lowest p-values) were selected from each dysregulated expression trend and then used the Kappa similarity statistic above 0.3 to connect similar terms.76 The resulting network was then edited in Cytoscape (v3.10.2).77 Additionally, we consulted the SFARI Gene database (2024 Q3 release)88 and OMIM89 to detect dysregulated proteins that have been annotated as ASD-risk genes and/or pathogenic variants on such genes cause NDD.

The objective of this investigation is to provide further understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms of PACS1 syndrome by investigating proteomic landscape regulation during brain organoid maturation. We used tandem mass tag (TMT) mass spectrometry in brain organoids generated from control and iPSC lines carrying the unique PACS1 p.R203W variant. Organoids were collected on days 21, 40, and 90 to better understand the maturation process. Protein and phosphorylated peptides were processed through mass spectrometry. Outputs were normalized using IRS scaling90 in R. Processed outputs were then fitted into a linear model and tested for differential expression over maturation time using the package limma, with genotype as fixed effect, accounting for day and isogenic line as covariates, and calculating duplicate correlation to model plex (batch).71 Normalized data were also aggregated by isogenic pairs (Table S20) and imported into the short time series (STEM)43 pipeline for expression trend clustering (Figure S9).
