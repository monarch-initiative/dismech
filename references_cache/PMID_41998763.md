# Clinical case of a pregnant woman with arteriovenous malformation, hemorrhagic venous infarcts and epileptic seizures: a case report.
**Authors:** Vasilev P, Viteva E
**Journal:** J Med Case Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13256-026-06041-8](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13256-026-06041-8)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Acute cerebrovascular incidents and epileptic seizures during pregnancy remain significant challenges for neurologists. The required neuroimaging techniques in both conditions and treatment options may have serious negative effects on the fetus.

CASE PRESENTATION: A 30-year-old pregnant white European woman at the 6th gestational week was hospitalized due to acute focal neurological symptoms from the left cerebral hemisphere. The patient conceived through an in vitro procedure and was on hormonal therapy with progesterone and estradiol. The brain CT showed suspicion of a venous infarct in the left cerebral hemisphere. Three days later, new acute neurological symptoms occurred, this time from the right cerebral hemisphere. The brain MRI revealed an arteriovenous malformation (AVM) in the left parietal lobe, superior sagittal sinus thrombosis, and hemorrhagic venous infarcts in both cerebral hemispheres. Ten days after the first symptoms, the patient developed an epileptic status characterized by focal clonic jerks on the right side of the face and right arm. Due to worsening health condition, a decision was made to perform an abortion. In addition, the patient was diagnosed with thrombophilia. Endovascular embolization of the arteriovenous malformation was not performed due to the high risk of complications. The patient was left on conservative treatment with levetiracetam and apixaban, but after two more hospitalizations because of epileptic status, the patient was referred for stereotactic radiosurgery.

CONCLUSION: This clinical case presents interest due to the combination of medical conditions, which resulted in cerebrovascular incidents with different localizations and characteristics during pregnancy, complicated with epileptic seizures.

Pregnancy is a state of hypercoagulation due to increased estrogen levels, external restriction of blood flow caused by the gravid uterus and increased venous stasis [1]. In pregnant women, the coagulation profile is characterized by a physiological reduction in protein S and tissue plasminogen activator, accompanied by elevated levels of D-dimer, prothrombin fragment 1 + 2 (F1 + 2), and coagulation factors VII, VIII, X, and von Willebrand factor [2, 3]. The risk of venous thrombosis is 4–6 times greater during pregnancy than in the nonpregnant state and is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality during pregnancy [1, 4, 5].

Cerebral venous thrombosis could be one of the possible clinical manifestations and is characterized by headache, nausea, vomiting, visual impairment, focal neurological deficit, altered consciousness, and epileptic seizures [6]. Epileptic seizures during pregnancy are related to miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm delivery, developmental delay, congenital malformation, and injuries [7]. In addition, treatment options for epilepsy in pregnant women are restricted because of the teratogenic effects of some antiseizure medications, such as valproic acid and topiramate. Other medications used during epileptic seizures, like benzodiazepines, phenobarbital and phenytoin, are also associated with negative effects on the fetus [8–10].

A 30 year-old pregnant white European woman at the 6th gestational week presented to the Emergency Department with headache and acute neurological symptoms from the left cerebral hemisphere–deviation of the eyes and head to the left, pathologically increased tendon reflexes, right greater than left, right-sided severe hemiparesis, and Babinski positive on the right. The patient conceived through an in vitro procedure and was on hormonal therapy with vaginal progesterone 3 × 1 globules and oral estradiol 3 × 2 mg. The brain CT indicated suspicion of a venous infarct in the left cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 1). After stabilization and partial regression of the neurological symptoms, the patient was discharged on the seventh day. Three days later, the patient was admitted again to the hospital, with an epileptic status represented by focal clonic jerks on the right side of the face and right arm with preserved consciousness. Treatment with levetiracetam 2 × 1000 mg per day was enough to stop the seizures, along with antiedema therapy including mannitol 10% and dexamethasone. EEG showed focal left temporal epileptiform activity and right parieto-occipital slow-wave changes (Fig. 4). Due to worsening of the health condition, limited diagnostic and treatment options, taking into consideration the pregnancy, a decision was made to perform an abortion. Genetic tests revealed that the patient has thrombophilia–she is a heterozygote carrier of the PAI-1 4G/5G genotype, ACE I/D genotype and Factor XIII Val/Leu genotype, which is associated with a cumulative risk for reduced fibrinolytic activity. Furthermore, the patient is a homozygote carrier of the GPIIIa PIA2/PIA2 genotype and a heterozygote carrier of the TAFI (c. + 505A > G) genotype, which are related respectively to an increased risk for platelet aggregation and dysregulation of the fibrinolytic system. After the medical abortion, digital subtraction angiography showed that the arteriovenous malformation was feeding from the left cerebral medial artery and draining through dilated veins to the left transverse sinus. In the next 6 months, the patient did not experience any seizures, and the EEG findings and neurological deficit improved during follow-up. After that period the patient was hospitalized twice in one month, both times in epileptic status with focal clonic jerks on the right side of the face, right arm, and impaired consciousness. The daily dose of levetiracetam was increased to 3000 mg, carbamazepine 600 mg was added to the therapy, and the patient was referred for stereotactic radiosurgery of the arteriovenous malformation, which was performed 8 months after the initial symptoms. After the procedure, an urticarial rash occurred all over the body except the face. The rash was very well affected by corticosteroids and antihistamines. After the EEG results improved, carbamazepine was slowly withdrawn from the therapy.

Two years after symptoms onset, the patient remains on therapy with levetiracetam 3 × 1000 mg, pregabalin 2 × 300 mg and apixaban 2 × 5 mg per day. She has 3–4 mild focal seizures per year, presenting with dizziness, stare, and impaired consciousness for several seconds. The EEG shows left temporal focal irritative activity (Fig. 5). From the neurological status persist increased tendon reflexes and hypesthesia on the right side. The patient has been receiving psychotherapy for more than a year. Currently, after good seizure control and promising results from EEG and MRI, the patient is determined to have another pregnancy through in vitro fertilization. She was consulted by three obstetric-gynecologists (OGs), neuroradiologist, and the neurologists who follow her. One of the OGs is strongly against another pregnancy, whereas the other two OGs and the neuroradiologist are in support of another pregnancy, of course highlighting the potential serious complications. We, as neurologists, consider that the current seizure control is satisfactory, concerning another pregnancy.

We suggest that the combination of pregnancy, thrombophilia, and estradiol intake led to multiple thromboses in the cerebral venous system. The disturbed venous drainage caused increased venous pressure in the affected brain areas, including the AVM, resulting in hemorrhagic infarction, reduced arterial blood supply, and perifocal edema in different brain regions, which caused various neurological symptoms. The known hypercoagulable state during pregnancy was exacerbated by the underlying genetic predisposition. In addition, estrogen is known to have a procoagulant effect by increasing the levels of factors VII, X, XII, and XIII of the coagulation cascade and reducing the concentration of protein S [11].

We believe that the AVM was responsible for the epileptic seizures. After terminating pregnancy and consistently receiving anticoagulation therapy, the patient gradually improved, the neurological symptoms regressed, and the subsequent MRI scans were optimistic. Unfortunately, the epileptic seizures persisted, and the patient needed to be put on 2 antiseizure medications. The epileptiform activity on EEG correlated with the location of the AVM; furthermore, after the size of the AVM was reduced through stereotactic surgery, both seizure control and EEG improved.

Cerebral arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) are dysplastic vascular niduses in the brain composed of abnormal tangles of arteries and veins that communicate directly without capillaries. After brain hemorrhage, epileptic seizures are the second most common clinical presentation of cerebral AVM, affecting approximately 20–40% of patients [12, 13]. Hemorrhage, edema, hemosiderosis, and gliosis are thought to be responsible for epileptic seizures, but epileptogenesis, especially that of unruptured cerebral AVMs, is not yet fully clear. Studies have shown certain anatomical characteristics related to an increased risk of epileptic seizures: size of the AVM, cortical location (mainly frontal and temporal), superficial blood supply, middle cerebral artery feeding, long pial course of the draining vein, and venous outflow stenosis [14, 15]. The treatment of epilepsy due to cerebral AVM includes a combination of antiseizure medications and techniques aimed at obliterating or removing the AVM, such as endovascular embolization, microsurgical resection, or stereotactic radiosurgery. All of the mentioned techniques have their place in treatment protocols, but there is still debate regarding which technique is superior in terms of seizure control and safety [12, 15].

Advising in favor of or against pregnancy in women with complicated AVM requires individual approach. A team, consisting of a neurologist, neurosurgeon, neuroradiologist, obstetric-gynecologist and psychologist, must fully inform the patient about the potential risks. If the patient decides to conceive, she should be strictly followed through frequent neurological and obstetric examinations, EEG, and serum levels of the antiseizure medications.

This case is of clinical interest because of the combination of medical conditions, which resulted in cerebrovascular incidents with different localizations and characteristics during pregnancy, complicated with epileptic seizures.

Patients who undergo in vitro fertilization and receive hormonal therapy afterward should be carefully considered for prophylaxis therapy with anticoagulants.

In this particular case the patient has a strong desire for another pregnancy and it needs to be taken into account the risk of thrombosis due to pregnancy and thrombophilia, and the risk of bleeding, which is the most common complication of cerebral AVM. Seizure aggravation is also possible during another pregnancy. This is an example of a multidisciplinary and personalized approach in a patient with multiple risk factors and the possibility of completely opposite clinical exacerbations.
