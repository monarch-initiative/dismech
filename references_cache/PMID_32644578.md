# Staphylococcal Pneumonia.
**Authors:** Farmer A, Tobin EH, Hicks MA

## Content

Staphylococcus aureus (S aureus) colonizes 15% to 33% of the human population, with a predilection for certain sites, including the intestine, nares, and skin. S aureus is known to cause a range of clinical entities, including skin and soft tissue infections, bacteremia, osteoarticular infections, and pleuropulmonary infections eg, pneumonia. S aureus remains at the forefront of infectious disease due to its production of protease, lipase, and hyaluronidase, as well as its ability to produce penicillin-binding protein 2A (PBP-2A) through the mecA gene, leading to the staphylococcal strain known as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), which is resistant to beta-lactam antibiotics. Please see StatPearls' companion resources, "Staphylococcus aureus Infections" and "Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus" for further information Staphylococcal pneumonia accounts for approximately 2% to 3% of community-acquired pneumonia cases but is associated with significant morbidity due to its potential for rapid clinical deterioration. S aureus is a common cause of hospital-acquired and ventilator-associated pneumonia. Early recognition and prompt diagnosis are critical, as delayed treatment can lead to severe complications, including necrotizing pneumonia, bacteremia, and sepsis, which may occur with or without septic shock. This infection often develops following a recent viral illness, particularly influenza. Staphylococcal pneumonia should be considered in patients with specific risk factors, eg, recent hospitalization, prior antibiotic exposure, hemodialysis, or intravenous drug use. The management of staphylococcal pneumonia relies on interprofessional collaboration as coordinated, team-based care enhances clinical decision-making, supports timely interventions, and ultimately improves patient outcomes in this potentially life-threatening condition.
