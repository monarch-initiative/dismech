Ubiquitin (Ub) C-terminal hydrolase L1 (UCHL1) is a multifunctional protein that 
is expressed in neurons throughout brain at high levels. UCHL1 deletion is 
associated with axonal degeneration, progressive sensory motor ataxia, and 
premature death in mice. UCHL1 has been hypothesized to play a role in the 
pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases and recovery after neuronal injury. 
UCHL1 hydrolyzes Ub from polyubiquitinated (poly-Ub) proteins, but also may 
ligate Ub to select neuronal proteins, and interact with cytoskeletal proteins. 
These and other mechanisms have been hypothesized to underlie UCHL1's role in 
neurodegeneration and response to brain injury. A UCHL1 knockin mouse containing 
a C90A mutation (C90A) devoid of hydrolase activity was constructed. The C90A 
mouse did not develop the sensory and motor deficits, degeneration of the 
gracile nucleus and tract, or premature death as seen in UCHL1 deficient mice. 
C90A and wild type (WT) mice were subjected to the controlled cortical impact 
(CCI) model of traumatic brain injury (TBI), and cell death, axonal injury and 
behavioral outcome were assessed. C90A mice exhibited decreased spared tissue 
volume, greater loss of CA1 hippocampal neurons and greater axonal injury as 
detected using anti-amyloid precursor protein (APP) antibody and anti- 
non-phosphorylated neurofilament H (SMI-32) antibody immunohistochemistry after 
CCI compared to WT controls. Poly-Ub proteins and Beclin-1 were elevated after 
CCI in C90A mice compared to WT controls. Vestibular motor deficits assessed 
using the beam balance test resolved by day 5 after CCI in WT mice but not in 
C90A mice. These results suggest that the hydrolase activity of UCHL1 does not 
account for the progressive neurodegeneration and premature death seen in mice 
that do not express full length UCHL1. The hydrolase activity of UCHL1 
contributes to the function of the ubiquitin proteasome pathway (UPP), 
ameliorates activation of autophagy, and improves motor recovery after CCI. 
Thus, UCHL1 hydrolase activity plays an important role in acute injury response 
after TBI.

Ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1 (UCHL1) is a multifunctional protein that is expressed at high concentrations in neurons and axons throughout brain and the peripheral nervous system (Larsen et al., 1996). UCHL1 may play an important role in repair of axons and neurons after injury by removal of abnormal proteins through the UPP and autophagy (Bheda et al., 2010; Bilguvar et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2010; Kabuta and Wada, 2008; Liu et al., 2019). UCHL1 closely interacts with proteins of the neuronal cytoskeleton and may be involved in axonal transport and maintaining axonal integrity (Liu et al., 2002; Sakurai et al., 2008). UCHL1 also regulates synaptic function and long term potentiation and has been implicated in memory function (Chen et al., 2010; Gong et al., 2006; Smith et al., 2009; Zhang et al., 2014). Deletion of UCHL1 produces progressive neurodegeneration, sensory and motor ataxia, and premature death in mice (Chen et al., 2010; Coulombe et al., 2014; Reinicke et al., 2019; Saigoh et al., 1999; Walters et al., 2008; Yamazaki et al., 1988). UCHL1’s hydrolase activity cleaves ubiquitin from proteins, allowing Ub to be recycled for other functions (Bett et al., 2015; Wilkinson et al., 1989). But UCHL1 has additional activities and mechanisms by which it exerts its physiological and pathological actions including a hypothesized ubiquitin ligase activity, antioxidant and structural roles, and interactions with proteins in other signal transduction pathways including autophagy (Coulombe et al., 2014; Kabuta and Wada, 2008; Liu et al., 2002). Thus, whether the neurodegeneration, motor deficits and premature death seen in UCHL1 deficient mice are associated with its hydrolase activity or other mechanisms is unclear.

Acute trauma produces axonal damage that is manifested by focal disruption of axonal transport resulting in accumulation of proteins and other structures at the site of axonal injury (Povlishock and Christman, 1995). Preservation of axonal integrity and synaptic function play a pivotal role in restoration of motor and cognitive function after TBI (Cao et al., 2002; Goldberg and Ransom, 2003). Since UCHL1 deletion is associated with significant axonal pathology and motor impairment, we hypothesized that UCHL1 activity may be an important determinant of axonal injury and recovery after TBI. This thesis is supported by the observation that transduction of neurons with trans-activating transduction (TAT) -UCHL1 proteins decreased the extent of axonal injury and neuronal cell death after TBI (Liu et al., 2017).

To determine whether UCHL1’s hydrolase activity plays a role in neurodegeneration and TBI, a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) transgenic mouse bearing a C90A mutation within the hydrolase active site in UCHL1 that is devoid of UCHL1 hydrolase activity was constructed. Motor behavior was observed and brain pathology evaluated at 18 month(mo)s of age in UCHL1 C90A and WT mice. TBI was induced in 10–12 week old C90A and WT mice using the CCI model and the effects the C90A mutation on protein ubiquitination, neuronal cell death, white matter injury, and motor function after CCI were assessed.

Detailed information on primary and secondary antibodies used in the study are summarized in Table 1. Luxol fast blue staining reagents were purchased from Electron Microscopy Sciences (Hatfield, PA); hematoxylin and eosin reagents were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Pittsburgh, PA).

The mouse UCHL1 gene was cloned into the pET-22b(+) expression vector (Millipore Sigma, Burlington MA) using NdeI and XhoI restriction sites at the 5’ and 3’ ends respectively (forward primer 5’ -GGGAATTCCATATGCAGCTGAAACCGATGGAGATTAACC-3’ and reverse primer 5’ -AAATTTCTCGAGTTAGGCTGCTTTGCAGAGAGCCAC-3’ were used), resulting in a protein with eight additional residues (LEHHHHHH) at its C terminus as described (Koharudin et al., 2010). The Cys-to-Ala mutation of C90A UCHL1 was generated by Quickchange site-directed mutagenesis (Millipore Sigma). For protein expression, Escherichia coli Rosetta (DE3) cells (Novagen) were transformed with UCHL1 wild type (WT) or UCHL1 C90A vectors. Cells were initially grown at 37°C, shaking at 300 rpm, for approximately 24 h, induced with 1 mM Isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 35°C when the OD 260 was 0.8–1.0, then grown for an additional 5 h. Proteins were purified using a Qiagen Ni-NTA Fast Start Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Qiagen, Germantown, MD). Protein purity was assessed by SDS–PAGE and Coomassie blue staining.

The UCHL1 hydrolase activity assay was performed as previously described with slight modifications (Liu et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2002). Briefly, 100 nM C90A UCHL1 recombinant protein was mixed with 500 nM ubiquitin-7-amido-4-methylcoumarin (AMC, Boston Biochem, Cambridge, MA) diluted with UCH hydrolase buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.6, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.1 mg/ml ovalbumin, 5 mM DTT). Free AMC fluorophore was read using a fluorescence plate reader (ex 360 nm, em 460 nm) over a period of 14 mins with 2 min intervals between each reading. WT UCHL1 protein (100 nM) was included as a positive control.

The in vitro hydrolase activity assay confirmed that mutation of the cysteine 90 to alanine in the UCHL1 construct completely abolished the hydrolase activity of the C90A recombinant protein (Fig. 1A). A UCHL1 C90A mouse was then constructed following a procedure described previously to convert the cysteine 90 located on exon 4 to alanine (TGT>GCT)(Liu et al., 2015). As shown in Fig. 1B, a C57BL/6J mouse genomic clone from the bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) library was modified to carry the TGT<GCT mutation as well as a G418 resistance cassette (PGK-neo) flanked by flippase (FLP) recognition target (FRT) sites. The FLP recombinase enzyme recognizes FRT sites and is used to remove the cassette. The targeting vector was constructed by gap repair from the BAC and was confirmed by sequencing to ensure the correct position of the TGT<GCT mutation, insertion of the PGK-neo cassette as well as the presence of the diagnostic Nsi I restriction enzyme site for the identification of gene-targeted embryonic stem (ES) cells. The vector was then selected in mouse C57BL/6J ES cells as described previously (Liu et al., 2015). ES cells with homologous recombination identified by southern blots were shipped to Taconic Artemis (Koeln, Germany) and injected into blastocysts to generate heterozygous UCHL1 C90A mice on a C57BL6 background. UCHL1 C90A heterozygous males were then back bred to C57BL6 female mice (Jackson Laboratories, Bar Harbor, ME), and the resulting heterozygous offspring were crossbred to produce homozygous mutant UCHL1 C90A and WT UCHL1 lines. Mouse genotypes were verified by genomic DNA extraction and PCR amplification with primers 5’TGTTTATCTCTTTAGAACTGGCTGG-3’ and 5’-CTAGAGAATAGGAACTTCGCGG-3’.

This study was carried out in accordance with the recommendations in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health. The protocol was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Animals were housed in a temperature and humidity controlled environment with 12 h light cycles and free access to food and water. Surgery was performed under isoflurane anesthesia, and all efforts were made to minimize suffering. Animals were randomized across groups into multiple cohorts based on availability of the appropriate genotype and age. The surgeon was blinded to the genotype of the mouse. The CCI model of traumatic brain injury used in this study was performed as described (Dixon et al., 1991; Liu et al., 2017). Male mice, age 10 – 12 weeks, were anesthetized using 5% isoflurane in 60% nitrous oxide, balance oxygen, and then placed in a stereotaxic frame (David Kopf Instruments, Tujunga, CA) fitted with a nosecone. A midline incision was made, the skin retracted, and a 5 mm craniectomy was performed over the right parietotemporal cortex (bregma – 2.5 mm, lateral right + 3.0 mm). The impactor tip (3 mm) was zeroed to the surface of the brain prior to injury (impact speed: 6 m/sec, injury depth: 1.6 mm, dwell time: 50 msec, angle of impact 20°); the wound was closed without repair of the craniectomy. Sham surgery was identical to that described above without trauma. Mice were monitored daily post injury. Animals exhibiting signs of infection (redness, swelling or discharge), pain (hunched posture, freezing, or vocalization) or weight loss greater than 20% of controls were removed from the study and euthanized using the carbon dioxide inhalation method.

Vestibulomotor function was measured on D0 (baseline, pre-injury) and D1 – 5 post injury as described previously (Dixon et al., 1987; Feeney et al., 1981). UCHL1 C90A and WT CCI and sham-operated mice were placed on a 10 mm round beam above a padded surface and latency of up to 60 sec was measured in 3 trials daily. N = 8–10 per group.

WT and C90A hippocampi were dissected from mouse brains 24 h and 7 d post CCI, rapidly frozen on dry ice and stored at −80°C until use. For UCHL1 detection, naïve WT and C90A cortices and hippocampi were used. Total protein extraction, quantification and sample preparation were performed as described previously (Liu et al., 2019). Samples were resolved on 12% (for mono-Ub) or 4–20% (for all other markers) Mini-protein TGX precast protein gels (BioRad, Hercules, CA). Equal number of samples from each group were loaded onto each gel. Two replicates of pooled mouse forebrain lysates were included on each gel serving as internal controls. Gels were transferred onto Odyssey® Nitrocellulose Membranes (LICOR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE), which were then probed with indicated markers following LICOR’s protocol. The immunoreactive bands were visualized using an Odyssey CLx Imaging System (LICOR). Densitometric analysis was performed using Image Studio Lite Ver. 5.2 (LICOR) and data were normalized to the average of the two internal controls on each gel to reduce gel to gel variation (N=8–10 per group). To examine the effect of the UCHL1 C90A mutation on protein ubiquitination in aged mice, cortical and hippocampal tissues were dissected from naïve 18 mo old WT and C90A mice and processed following the procedures described above (N=5 per group). GAPDH or β- actin was used as loading control as indicated in figure legends. Data were normalized to the loading controls.

Cell survival measurement was performed as described previously (Liu et al., 2017). After euthanasia, mice were transcardially perfused with 20 ml of heparinized saline (4 units/ml) followed by 10 ml of 10% buffered formalin 21 days post CCI. Whole brains were embedded in paraffin and 7 μm thick serial sections were collected every 0.5 mm. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and hippocampi were examined using 20X brightfield magnification. Neurons exhibiting normal morphology with visible nucleoli were counted in the ipsilateral medial 1mm of CA1 region of hippocampus and normalized to the respective contralateral region (bregma −1.9 mm). Data are expressed as percent contralateral. N = 10 per group.

Spared tissue volume was calculated by measuring surviving brain tissue in ipsilateral and contralateral hemispheric areas every 0.5 mm (bregma −4.3 to +3.0 mm) using ImageJ 1.50i software (NIH, Bethesda, MD). Spared volume was determined by multiplying slice area by slice interval thickness then adding together slices and is expressed as percent contralateral hemisphere (Swanson et al., 1990). N = 9–10 per group.

Assessment of gracile nucleus and gracile fasciculus demyelination was performed by examining coronal sections of medulla (bregma −7.5 mm) and C1 spinal cord in 18 mo old WT and C90A mice (N=4 per group) after staining with Luxol fast blue following the protocol described previously (Kluver and Barrera, 1953).

APP immunostaining was performed on WT and C90A mouse brain sections (bregma 2.1 mm) 24 h post CCI as well as in cross sections of medulla from 18 mo old naïve WT and C90A mice as described previously (Liu et al., 2017; Marmarou and Povlishock, 2006). In injured mice, APP-positive spots in thalamus (mediolateral 1.25 – 2.25, dorsoventral 2.5 – 3.0) and lateral corpus callosum (mediolateral 3.0, dorsoventral 2.0 – 2.5), with diameters between 5 and 10 μm, were counted as previously described (Xia et al., 2018) and divided by area to yield APP density. APP density in 18 mo old WT and C90A mice was assessed in gracile nucleus (bregma −8.0 mm) and normalized to area. SMI-32 and myelin basic protein (MBP) immunofluorescent staining was performed on WT and C90A mouse brain sections 7 d post CCI. Sections were immunostained with anti-SMI-32 and MBP antibodies, then incubated with CY3 and AlexaFluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse and goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies, respectively. SMI-32 and MBP expression were assessed by measuring fluorescence intensity using ImageJ software after normalization of backgrounds. Brain sections incubated without application of primary antibody served as negative controls.

Data are expressed as means +/− SEM. SMI32/MBP ratio, APP expression, cell counting, spared tissue volumes, and densitometric comparison of immunoblots in post-injury mice were analyzed using two-way ANOVA; Beam latency was analyzed using two-way repeated measures ANOVA with LSD post hoc testing. Comparisons of APP quantification and immunoblot densitometric analysis for 18 mo old WT and C90A mice were performed using Student’s T test. All statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS statistical software (IBM Corp, Armonk, NH). Results were considered to be significant when p ≤ 0.05.

To investigate whether C90A mice develop the motor deficits, neurodegeneration and premature death exhibited in gracile axonal dystrophy (GAD) mice and UCHL1 null mice, we maintained UCHL1 C90A mice and colony WT controls to 18 mos of age. No significant phenotypic differences or gross behavior changes were observed in UCHL1 C90A mice, as compared to 18 mo old WT controls except for body weight. UCHL1 C90A mice had significantly higher body weights at 18 mos of age than their male age-matched WT counterparts (51.00 ± 1.71 g vs. 40.80 ± 1.72 g, respectively). Expression levels of UCHL1 in cortex and hippocampus were similar between WT and C90A mice (Fig. 1C and D). C90A mice lived to 18 mos of age without developing ataxia, tremor or difficulty moving the hind legs (Supplementary videos 1 and 2) compared to that described in UCHL1 null mice at 80 days of age (Yamazaki et al., 1988). Histological assessment with Luxol fast blue and APP immunostaining did not reveal any differences in either the gracile nucleus or the gracile fasciculus in UCHL1 C90A mice compared to age-matched WT controls as described in mice that do not express full length UCHL1 (Yamazaki et al., 1988) (Fig. 2 A–B). Western blot analysis indicated that the levels of total poly-Ub proteins, K48-linked poly-Ub proteins, and USP14 are comparable between WT and C90A mice. However, the level of mono-Ub is significantly decreased in the C90A mouse cortex as shown in Fig. 2 C.

To determine the effects of the UCHL1 C90A mutation on CCI-induced UPP function and autophagy, poly-Ub proteins and the autophagy marker Beclin-1 were detected in ipsilateral hippocampus of 10–12 wk old mice 24 h and 7 d after CCI by immunoblotting. There was increased accumulation of total poly-Ub proteins in C90A but not in WT mouse 24 h post CCI. The level of Bcl2-interacting protein Beclin-1 in C90A mouse was significantly higher than that of the WT mouse 24 h post CCI (Fig. 3A). There were no significant differences detected in K48-linked UPP-dependent, K63-linked UPP-independent poly-Ub proteins or mono-Ub 24 h post CCI (Fig. 3A).

By 7 d after CCI, differences between C90A injured mice and C90A sham mice in poly-Ub and Beclin-1 were resolved, and the difference in Beclin-1 between C90A injured mice and WT injured mice was no longer detectable. Additionally, we did not observe any significant differences between sham WT and C90A mice in all five markers at either 24 h or 7 d post surgery (Fig. 3A and B).

White matter injury was determined by detection of abnormal accumulation of APP in axons, and SMI32, an indicator of nonphosphorylated pathological neurofilaments in neurites after CCI. MBP was used as a marker of myelin integrity (Fig. 4). APP accumulation in axons was significantly increased in ipsilateral thalamus and lateral corpus callosum at 24 h after CCI; SMI-32 expression in ipsilateral internal capsule and contralateral medial corpus callosum was symptomatic of injury to neurites 7 days after CCI. The ratio of SMI-32 to MBP was significantly greater in C90A mice compared to WT controls 7 d after CCI. These data demonstrate that the UCHL1 C90A mutation exacerbates injury-induced white matter injury.

To examine whether mutation of the C90A residue of UCHL1 affects cell viability and cortical contusion-induced tissue loss after CCI, WT and C90A mice underwent CCI or sham surgery and were sacrificed 21 days post CCI for histological analysis. As shown in Fig 5A, the spared hemispheric volume in C90A and WT mice was significantly less after CCI compared to respective sham-operated mice, and this decrease in spared hemispheric volume was significantly larger in C90A compared to WT mice after CCI. Furthermore, while cell survival in CA1 hippocampus was significantly diminished in both WT and C90A groups after CCI, this decrease was significantly greater in C90A as compared to WT mice (Fig. 5B). Taken together, the above data demonstrate that the UCHL1 C90A mutation exacerbates CCI-induced neuronal injury on hippocampal cell loss and lesion volume after TBI.

Vestibulomotor function was determined by the beam balance test (Fig. 6). Analysis by two-way repeated measures ANOVA of all time points revealed significant differences in latencies between sham WT and sham C90A and between WT CCI and C90A CCI. There was no significant interaction between the injury and mutation groups. At day 5, analysis by one-way ANOVA demonstrated significant differences between the CCI C90A mice and all other groups. These results indicate that the C90A mutation delays the recovery of vestibulomotor function after CCI.

The major findings of the study are 1) The UCHL1 C90A mutation results in increased accumulation of ubiquitinated proteins and activation of autophagy in hippocampus after CCI. 2) UCHL1 C90A mice have more extensive white and gray matter injury after CCI than WT controls. 3) The UCHL1 C90A mutation inhibits motor recovery after CCI. 4) UCHL1 C90A mice do not develop the motor deficits and premature death exhibited in UCHL1 null mice.

Ubiquitination of misfolded or otherwise damaged proteins and their transport to the proteasome via the UPP is an important cellular repair mechanism in many disease states (Ciechanover and Kwon, 2015; Gubellini et al., 1997; Huang and Figueiredo-Pereira, 2010; Vannucci et al., 1998; Yao et al., 2008). UCHL1’s hydrolase activity has been hypothesized to play an important role in recycling ubiquitin for reuse in the UPP and other cellular signal transduction pathways (Bett et al., 2015; Wilkinson et al., 1989). Previous studies using the lateral fluid percussion brain injury model have shown that there is an increase in ubiquitinated proteins and a decrease in free ubiquitin after TBI in rat hippocampus and that these alterations in ubiquitination may contribute to cell death and dysfunction (Sakai et al., 2014; Yao et al., 2008). Although we did not detect a significant increase in poly-Ub proteins in WT mice post injury in this model of TBI, mice bearing the UCHL1 C90A mutation had significant increases after CCI. This suggests that UCHL’s hydrolase activity is important in the function of the UPP and other ubiquitin signal transduction systems after CCI. A modest accumulation in K48 and K63 poly-Ub proteins was also detected in C90A mice after CCI, but the increase did not reach statistical significance. These data suggest that these types of Ub linkages contribute to the elevated total poly-Ub accumulation observed in C90A mice seen after CCI.

Autophagy is induced after TBI in the CCI model and may contribute to cell death (Lai et al., 2008). In the current study, we found that the Bcl2-interacting protein Beclin-1 is significantly increased in hippocampus of UCHL1 C90A mice compared to that of WT mice, consistent with activation of autophagy in UCHL1 C90A mice. Impaired clearance of damaged proteins caused by compromised UPP function after CCI may result in compensatory activation of autophagy (Pan et al., 2008). Thus, increased activation of autophagy in the UCHL1 C90A mouse could be mediated by multiple mechanisms.

Recovery after TBI requires restoration of both gray and white matter function. The current results demonstrate that UCHL1 C90A mice have larger tissue injury volumes and increased hippocampal CA1 cell death than WT controls after CCI consistent with more severe gray matter injury. Proteasome function has been shown to be an important mechanism ameliorating neuronal cell death and subsequent gray matter injury after TBI (Ding et al., 2017). The C90A mice also had more extensive APP accumulation and SMI32-positive axons after CCI compared to WT controls, consistent with more severe white matter injury. Protein ubiquitination plays an important role in recovery after brain injury by removing abnormal proteins via the UPP and maintaining normal axonal and synaptic function (Cai et al., 2010; Dong et al., 2014; Feldmann et al., 2019; Sakai et al., 2014; Yao et al., 2008). These results are consistent with a role for UCHL1 hydrolase activity in preserving axonal integrity. Thus, UCHL1 hydrolase activity contributes to preserving both gray and white matter, permitting recovery of motor function after CCI.

Several naturally occurring mutations and splice variants in UCHL1 and UCHL1 null mice do not express detectable UCHL1 protein. These mice all develop progressive neurodegeneration and severe sensory and motor dysfunction, dying by 5–8 mo. of age (Coulombe et al., 2014; Reinicke et al., 2019; Saigoh et al., 1999; Walters et al., 2008; Yamazaki et al., 1988). In contrast, the UCHL1 C90A mice, which have a selective mutation in the hydrolase site and express full length UCHL1, survive to at least 18 mo. of age without observed gross motor deficits. C90A mice did not develop the neuropathological changes in gracile nucleus and fasciculus in cervical spinal cord that are seen in GAD mice, nor were there significant differences in total and K48-linked species of polyubiquitinated proteins between UCHL1 C90A and WT control brain. USP14 protein was increased in brains of UCHL1 null mice nm3419 that developed progressive neurodegeneration and premature death (Walters et al., 2008); but this protein was unchanged in 18 mo old C90A mice compared to WT controls in the current study. The only phenotypic difference noted was that the body weights of C90A mice were significantly greater than WT controls by 18 mos of age. This difference could be related to effects of UCHL1 on pancreatic beta cell survival (Costes et al., 2014).

The lack of significant change in poly-Ub proteins in 18 mo old C90A mice compared to WT controls suggests that UCHL1 hydrolase activity is not required for maintaining normal protein ubiquitination in the aging mouse brain and that there are redundant mechanisms of protein ubiquitination that may be induced to compensate for the loss of UCHL1 hydrolase activity. However, the decreased levels of mono-Ub observed in C90A mouse brain suggest that UCHL1 hydrolase activity is important in maintaining mono-Ub reserves that are available to ubiquitinate proteins after cellular stress. Progressive motor dysfunction and premature death do not occur in the C90A mice. This suggests that UCHL1 hydrolase activity is not responsible for the progressive motor deficits and premature death observed in mice that do not express full length UCHL1. The neurodegeneration seen in UCHL1 null mice and mutant mice may be due to other activities or other domains within the UCHL1 protein besides the hydrolase site, including ubiquitin ligase activity (Bheda et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2002), the C220 site associated with a toxic gain of function in Parkinson’s disease (Liu et al., 2009) and interactions of UCHL1 with cytoskeletal elements such as tubulin (Kabuta and Wada, 2008; Wilkinson et al., 1989). Thus, UCHL1 is a complex molecule that may contribute to disease states through a variety of sites within the molecule and mechanisms in addition to its hydrolase activity.

Mice lacking UCHL1 hydrolase activity exhibit dysfunction of the UPP, increased activation of autophagy, more extensive gray and white matter injury, and impaired motor recovery after CCI. Thus, UCHL1 hydrolase activity plays an important role in the acute injury response after TBI. Restoring or preserving UCHL1 activity could be a new therapeutic strategy to reduce injury and improve motor function after TBI. One approach to restore UCHL1 activity is to replace endogenous UCHL1 with exogenous UCHL1 protein via systemic administration. UCHL1 protein may be modified to contain the prothrombin domain of the HIV capsid protein TAT. The resultant TAT-UCHL1 fusion protein crosses blood brain barrier and has central nervous system effects when administered systemically (Gong et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2017). Systemic administration of TAT-UCHL1 has been shown to decrease axonal injury and decrease neuronal cell death after CCI (Liu et al., 2017). Another therapeutic strategy may be to develop inhibitors that compete for binding of reactive lipids at the UCHL1 C152 site which unfolds and inactivates the enzyme thus preserving UCHL1 function after TBI (Kunz et al., 2002; Liu et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2015). Additional studies are needed to further address these strategies for restoring and preserving UCHL1 activity after TBI.
