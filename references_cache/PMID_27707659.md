# Precision Molecular Diagnosis Defines Specific Therapy in Combined Immunodeficiency with Megaloblastic Anemia Secondary to MTHFD1 Deficiency.
**Authors:** Ramakrishnan KA, Pengelly RJ, Gao Y, Morgan M, Patel SV, Davies EG, Ennis S, Faust SN, Williams AP
**Journal:** J Allergy Clin Immunol Pract (2016)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jaip.2016.07.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaip.2016.07.014)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase (MTHFD1) deficiency has recently been reported to cause a folate-responsive syndrome displaying a phenotype that includes megaloblastic anemia and severe combined immunodeficiency.

OBJECTIVE: To describe our investigative approach to the molecular diagnosis and evaluation of immune dysfunction in a family with MTHFD1 deficiency.

METHODS: The methods used were exome sequencing and analysis of variants in genes involved in the folate metabolic pathway in a family with 2 affected siblings. Routine laboratory and research data were analyzed to gain an in-depth understanding of innate, humoral, and cell-mediated immune function before and after folinic acid supplementation.

RESULTS: Interrogation of exome data for concordant variants between the siblings in the genes involved in folate metabolic pathway identified a heterozygous mutation in exon 3 of the MTHFD1 gene that was shared with their mother. In view of highly suggestive phenotype, we extended our bioinformatics interrogation for structural variants in the MTHFD1 gene by manual evaluation of the exome data for sequence depth coverage of all the exons. A deletion involving exon 13 that was shared with their father was identified. Routine laboratory data showed lymphopenia involving all subsets and poor response to vaccines. In vitro analysis of dendritic cell and lymphocyte function was comparable to that in healthy volunteers. Treatment with folinic acid led to immune reconstitution, enabling discontinuation of all prophylactic therapies.

CONCLUSIONS: Exome sequencing demonstrated MTHFD1 deficiency as a novel cause of a combined immunodeficiency. Folinic acid was established as precision therapy to reverse the clinical and laboratory phenotype of this primary immunodeficiency.

Introduction 106 
Derivatives of folic acid play a critical role in cellular one carbon metabolism, acting as donors and 107 
recipients of one-carbon units involved in synthesis and breakdown of amino acids as well as in the 108 
synthesis of thymidine and purines (1). C-1-tetrahydrofolate synthase (C1-THF synthase)    is a 109 
cytoplasmic tri-functional enzyme essential for processing single carbon folate derivatives, encoded by 110 
the gene methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase (MTHFD1). Mutations in MTHFD1 have 111 
previously been reported to cause a syndrome displaying megaloblastic anemia, severe combined 112 
immunodeficiency (SCID), mild mental retardation and treatment-refractory epilepsy that was 113 
responsive to metabolic supplementation (2, 3).  Four further case reports (4), two whom died of 114 
overwhelming sepsis have extended the phenotype to include atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, 115 
microangiopathy and retinopathy. We present the long term follow up data of a family with two 116 
siblings who carry novel compound heterozygous mutations in the MTHFD1 gene. Specifically, we 117 
have characterized the immune function in more detail and documented the response in immune 118 
function to folinic acid supplementation. 119

Methods 122 
Subjects: Two brothers born to a mother of Southeast Asian and father of Caucasian origin managed in 123 
our specialist immunology service for more than a decade. Consent for research investigations was 124 
obtained (NHS Research Ethics 09/H502/4). 125

exons 3 and 13 are summarized in table S1 in the online repository.  131 
RNA amplification: RNA was extracted from lithium heparin anti-coagulated whole blood using 132

amplified using MTHFD1 gene specific primers (cDNAF and cDNAR) as per manufacturer 134 
instructions using SuperScript™ III One-Step RT-PCR System (Life technologies).  135

dendritic cell assays. Data were analyzed using FACS Diva software and gating strategy is illustrated 140

Results 142 
Clinical manifestations 143 
The elder sibling (patient A) presented at 4 months of age with clinical and radiological features 144 
consistent with Pneumocystic jirovecii pneumonia (PCP). SCID was diagnosed on the basis of 145 
susceptibility to PCP and severe lymphopenia involving all lymphoid subsets. Subsequent 146 
investigations found no mutations in the genes known to cause SCID at that time. Patient A remained 147 
free of significant infections on immunoglobulin replacement, prophylactic cotrimoxazole  and 148 
fluconazole. Whilst on follow up, he presented aged 4 with severe macrocytic anemia (hemoglobin 149 
level 34 grams/L) requiring transfusion that was confirmed to be megaloblastic on bone marrow 150 
examination. Although stem cell transplant was considered in infancy, the lack of molecular diagnosis 151 
and clinical stability on prophylactic treatment persuaded the clinicians to delay this procedure 152 
indefinitely. 153 
 The younger sibling (patient B) was investigated at birth and found to have severe 154 
lymphopenia similar to his brother. He was given prophylactic cotrimoxazole and fluconazole . 155 
Immunoglobulin replacement was delayed in order to establish a better assessment of humoral immune 156 
function by vaccination. B was given the UK immunisation schedule primary course of inactivated 157 
vaccines between 2 and 5 months of age. Assessment of vaccine specific immunoglobulin response 158 
showed that he responded to protein (tetanus toxoid) but not conjugated polysaccharide  (Haemophilus 159 
influenzae type B) vaccines (See table S3b in the online repository). Regular antibacterial 160 
(azithromycin) prophylaxis was added to his treatment regimen in view of poor humoral response to 161 
vaccines. Despite this, he was hospitalised with pneumococcal septic arthritis at 9 months of age  after 162 
which immunoglobulin replacement was commenced. He remained susceptible to pyogenic bacterial 163 
infections, with further hospitalisations at 19 months (pneumonia), 22 months (peri-orbital cellulitis) 164 
and 24 months (pneumococcal bacteraemia). He also developed severe episodes of megaloblastic 165 
anemia during the course of his hospitalisation with septic arthritis at 9 months and pneumonia at 19 166 
months of age. 167 
 Retrospective review of both patients’ RBC indices revealed a persistent macrocytosis from 168 
young infancy (Fig 1). A detailed chronology of clinical manifestations is presented in the online 169 
repository (See case description in the online repository). 170

Response to folinic acid treatment 189 
A presumed diagnosis of a defect in the folate metabolic pathway was made and both siblings were 190 
empirically treated with regular folinic acid supplement. As shown in figure 1, the mean corpuscular 191 
volume (MCV) normalised within weeks of supplementation. There have been no further episodes of 192 
anemia.  In the first months of therapy there was a partial reconstitution of the lymphocyte 193 
compartment with absolute lymphocyte and all subsets returning to lower limits of normal for age (See 194 
tables S2a & S2b in the online repository). The CD4+ TREC count improved 10 fold in both patients 195 
(Fig 1). IgM (Fig 1) and IgA levels (See tables S3a & S3b in online repository) also improved to 196 
normal levels over months suggesting a reconstitution of the humoral compartment. Immunoglobulin 197 
supplements were discontinued and both patients have maintained normal levels of all isotypes during 198 
the subsequent four years of follow up (See table S3a & S3b in the online repository). They have both 199 
been re-immunised and have responded well to both protein and conjugate vaccines (See table S3a & 200

Exome Analysis 203 
Suspecting a defect in folate metabolism without a single candidate gene, we undertook whole exome 204 
sequencing (WES) to analyze multiple gene variants (6). Interrogation of variants within genes 205 
involved in folic acid metabolism (GO:0046655(7)) for which the brothers were concordant identified 206 
a maternally inherited novel point mutation in exon 3, heterozygous T- to -C transition at c.152 207 
position (c.152T>C; NM_005956.3), that would lead to a substitution of a conserved amino acid 208 
(p.Leu51Pro). This amino acid is situated within the methylenetetrahydrofolate 209 
dehydrogenase/cyclohydrogenase domain of C1-THF synthase and the amino acid substitution is 210 
predicted to adversely impact protein function. The base within the Leu-51 residue is well conserved 211 
evolutionarily, with a GERP++ score of 3.2(8). Leu-51 is located within a α-helix, with the adjacent 212 
Tyr-52 and the proximal Lys-56 involved in substrate binding within the 213 
dehydrogenase/cyclohydrolase active site (9). Proline residues are well documented to distort α-helices. 214 
The lack of an amide proton, coupled with the conformational rigidity of the cyclic residue canonically 215 
results in an approximately 300 bend in the helix (10) and the identified L51P mutation would be 216 
expected to distort this α-helix, reducing the affinity of the active site for the substrates.  217 
In the previous report of C1-THF synthase deficiency, the heterozygous parents were reported 218 
to be asymptomatic (2). Structural variations (SVs) that are also important for Mendelian disease are 219 
not easily detected using an exome approach (11). Most exome studies conducted so far on specific 220 
diseases focus on single nucleotide variants, rather than SVs such as insertion/deletion variants (12). 221 
This is due in part to the fact that insertion/deletion bioinformatics analysis methodologies are still 222 
being refined. Read depth and split reads provide a clue to structural variants within exome data (13). 223 
Therefore, we extended our bioinformatic interrogation of the MTHFD1 gene by manually examining 224 
sequencing depth coverage of the raw alignments in affected brothers compared to an unrelated 225 
individual concurrently sequenced. For exons with apparent reduced coverage, read depth was 226 
enumerated, normalized according to total aligned reads for the sample and assessed statistically (one 227 
tailed t-test) alongside 13 unrelated samples from the same sequencing batch, processed identically 228 
(Fig 2b). This process identified a paternally inherited deletion encompassing exon 13, in both brothers 229 
(Fig 2c).  230

Confirmation of mutation by Sanger sequencing 231 
PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing using primers (Primers EX3F & EX3R; See table S1 in the 232 
online repository), confirmed that the novel point mutation in exon 3 was present in both patients and 233 
the mother but not the father (Fig 2d). 234 
 Identification by traditional sequencing of the breakpoints of exon deletions suspected on the 235 
basis of read depth coverage in the exome data can be arduous as the breakpoints are likely to be 236 
located in the intronic regions upstream and downstream of the identified exon which may span many 237 
thousands of base pairs (bps). However, we noted that in the read pile up of our patients, there were 238 
anomalous read pairs spanning over 2000 bps (Fig 2c). Primers were designed (Ex13LF & EX13LR; 239 
See table S1 in the online repository) to encompass this region. The predicted PCR product size in the 240 
reference template is 2350 bps (See Fig S4a in the online repository). PCR amplification of this region 241 
revealed an expected 2350 bps product in the mother but only a product of 600 base pairs in the 242 
patients, confirming a deletion involving exon 13 (Fig 2e). We were subsequently able to delineate the 243 
breakpoints accurately by Sanger sequencing of the above PCR products from the mother and patients 244 
(See Figs S3a & S3b in the online repository). We were also able to confirm the heterozygous state for 245 
deletion of exon 13 in the patients by allele specific PCR (See Fig S4a & S4b in the online repository). 246 
Further analysis revealed that the deletion boundaries were within homologous positions of flanking, 247 
parallel, AluS repeats (See Fig S5a & S5b in the online repository), previously observed as a 248 
pathogenic mutational mechanism in other genes, such as in BRCA1 (14). For interested readers, 249 
further discussion is  provided in the online repository. 250 
Allelic expression at mRNA level 251 
The deletion of exon 13 is predicted to introduce a premature stop codon resulting in a 252 
truncated protein of 422 amino acids. The nonsense mediated decay (NMD) pathway exists in 253 
eukaryotic cells to ensure fidelity of transcription and prevent translation of truncated proteins with 254 
potentially deleterious gain-of-function or dominant-negative activity (17).  We hypothesised that the 255 
deletion of EX13 would target the mRNA from paternal allele (with a premature stop codon), for 256 
NMD. Allelic expression of the MTHFD1 gene was undertaken using the point mutation in the exon 3 257 
to analyse the relative expression of the maternal and paternal allele (18). MTHFD1 cDNA was 258 
amplified using gene specific primers covering the point mutation in the EX3 (inherited from their 259

Discussion 263 
Patients A and B carry compound heterozygous mutations consistent with autosomal recessive 264 
inheritance (Fig 2a), L51P inherited from their mother and deletion of exon 13 inherited from their 265 
father. In silico analysis of mutant L51P predicts an adverse impact on the function of the protein. A 266 
mutation in the vicinity (S49F) has recently been shown to be significantly less functional in 267 
biochemical studies (4). Allelic expression data is consistent with the deletion of exon 13 producing 268 
haploinsufficiency. The partial response to folinic acid therapy is consistent with a residual functioning 269 
enzyme as previously described (4).  270 
MTHFD1 mutations cause highly variable clinical phenotypes with megaloblastic anemia 271 
being the only consistent feature (3, 4). This is likely the result of variable degree of residual enzyme 272 
function, but the limited number of patients currently reported makes it impossible to draw conclusions 273 
regarding genotype to phenotype correlation. Clinical manifestations described in smaller proportion of 274 
patients include severe immunodeficiency, haemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS), mild mental 275 
retardation, epilepsy and retinopathy. Severe immunodeficiency has been reported in 3 out 5 patients 276 
and was the most likely cause of mortality in two of the patients. Macrocytosis, lymphopenia involving 277 
all subsets and low TREC (Fig 1) were present from infancy in the patients reported here and may be a 278 
clue to the need to investigate folate metabolic pathways. MTHFD1 deficiency should be considered  in 279 
the aetiology of megaloblastic anemia where there are normal levels of vitamin B12 and folate; and in  280 
CID where there is macrocytosis.. 281 
We have characterized the immune function in our kindred that delineate the immune function 282 
in MTHFD1 deficiency. The antigen presenting cells in the peripheral blood were present in normal 283 
numbers and responded to Toll Like Receptor (TLR) ligands in a comparable fashion to controls  (data 284 
not shown). Both cell mediated and humoral immunity were defective in our patients. The clinical 285 
improvement in susceptibility to infections, partial reconstitution of cell numbers and improvement in 286 
both overall immunoglobulin levels and vaccine responses suggest that the immunodeficiency cause by 287 
this condition can be reversed to a large extent with folinic acid treatment.  288 
The major limitation of exome sequencing is the inability to comprehensively represent 289 
genomic structural variations (SVs) such as deletions, inversions or duplications (11). Many groups 290 
have designed algorithms that use a read depth or read pair-based approach for predicting structural 291 
variation. However, these approaches are not very efficient at identifying SVs with exome data  due to 292 

  Ramakrishnan 
 
15 
the discontinuous nature of the data, with only ~ 1% of the genome having significant read coverage 293 
(19). Our approach was successful in identifying the exon 13 deletion due to strong clinical suspicion. 294 
While it may not be possible to detect all forms of structural variations, we have demonstrated the 295 
possibility of analysing specific genes using read depth, split reads and read pairs to provide clues to 296 
structural variants. Where improved detection of structural variants are required, whole-genome 297 
sequencing (WGS) should be used. 298 
The importance of establishing a definitive molecular diagnosis in children with primary 299 
immunodeficiency cannot be overemphasized. Both patients were considered to have SCID and were 300 
on the bone marrow transplant list for the first few years of life. Knowledge of the specific molecular 301 
diagnosis has led to the identification of a precision treatment in the form of life-long folinic acid 302 
supplementation. Other prophylactic therapies have been able to be discontinued. The responsiveness 303 
of this disorder to folinic acid supplementation emphasizes the importance of precision diagnostics in 304 
the diagnosis of primary immunodeficiency prior to the consideration of stem cell transplantation, and 305 
to stratify appropriate treatment modalities to reduce risk and improve outcomes. Clinical exome 306 
sequencing, even in single families, is a powerful adjunct to the investigation of metabolic pathways 307 
that may lead to distinctive immunodeficiency disorders. 308

Abstract 78 
 79 
Background: Methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase (MTHFD1) deficiency has recently been 80 
reported to cause a folate responsive syndrome displaying a phenotype that includes megaloblastic 81 
anemia and severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID). 82 
Objective: To describe our investigative approach to the molecular diagnosis and evaluation of 83 
immune dysfunction in a family with MTHFD1 deficiency. 84 
Methods: Exome sequencing and analysis of variants in genes involved in the folate metabolic 85 
pathway in a family with two affected siblings. Routine laboratory and research data were analyzed to 86 
gain an in depth understanding of innate, humoral and cell mediated immune function before and after 87 
folinic acid supplementation. 88 
Results: Interrogation of exome data for concordant variants between the siblings in the genes 89 
involved in folate metabolic pathway identified a heterozygous mutation in exon 3 of the MTHFD1 90 
gene that was shared with their mother.  91 
In view of highly suggestive phenotype, we extended our bioinformatics interrogation for 92 
structural variants in the MTHFD1 gene by manual evaluation of the exome data for sequence depth 93 
coverage of all the exons. A deletion involving exon 13 that was shared with their father was identified.  94 
Routine laboratory data showed lymphopenia involving all subsets and poor response to 95 
vaccines. In-vitro analysis of dendritic cell and lymphocyte function were comparable to healthy 96 
volunteers. Treatment with folinic acid led to immune reconstitution enabling discontinuation of all 97 
prophylactic therapies. 98 
Conclusion: Exome sequencing demonstrated MTHFD1 deficiency as a novel cause of a combined 99 
immunodeficiency. Folinic acid was established as precision therapy to reverse the clinical and 100 
laboratory phenotype of this primary immunodeficiency. 101

Introduction 106 
Derivatives of folic acid play a critical role in cellular one carbon metabolism, acting as donors and 107 
recipients of one-carbon units involved in synthesis and breakdown of amino acids as well as in the 108 
synthesis of thymidine and purines (1). C-1-tetrahydrofolate synthase (C1-THF synthase)    is a 109 
cytoplasmic tri-functional enzyme essential for processing single carbon folate derivatives, encoded by 110 
the gene methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase (MTHFD1). Mutations in MTHFD1 have 111 
previously been reported to cause a syndrome displaying megaloblastic anemia, severe combined 112 
immunodeficiency (SCID), mild mental retardation and treatment-refractory epilepsy that was 113 
responsive to metabolic supplementation (2, 3).  Four further case reports (4), two whom died of 114 
overwhelming sepsis have extended the phenotype to include atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, 115 
microangiopathy and retinopathy. We present the long term follow up data of a family with two 116 
siblings who carry novel compound heterozygous mutations in the MTHFD1 gene. Specifically, we 117 
have characterized the immune function in more detail and documented the response in immune 118 
function to folinic acid supplementation. 119

Results 142 
Clinical manifestations 143 
The elder sibling (patient A) presented at 4 months of age with clinical and radiological features 144 
consistent with Pneumocystic jirovecii pneumonia (PCP). SCID was diagnosed on the basis of 145 
susceptibility to PCP and severe lymphopenia involving all lymphoid subsets. Subsequent 146 
investigations found no mutations in the genes known to cause SCID at that time. Patient A remained 147 
free of significant infections on immunoglobulin replacement, prophylactic cotrimoxazole and 148 
fluconazole. Whilst on follow up, he presented aged 4 with severe macrocytic anemia (hemoglobin 149 
level 34 grams/L) requiring transfusion that was confirmed to be megaloblastic on bone marrow 150 
examination. Although stem cell transplant was considered in infancy, the lack of molecular diagnosis 151 
and clinical stability on prophylactic treatment persuaded the clinicians to delay this procedure 152 
indefinitely. 153 
 The younger sibling (patient B) was investigated at birth and found to have severe 154 
lymphopenia similar to his brother. He was given prophylactic cotrimoxazole and fluconazole . 155 
Immunoglobulin replacement was delayed in order to establish a better assessment of humoral immune 156 
function by vaccination. B was given the UK immunisation schedule primary course of inactivated 157 
vaccines between 2 and 5 months of age. Assessment of vaccine specific immunoglobulin response 158 
showed that he responded to protein (tetanus toxoid) but not conjugated polysaccharide  (Haemophilus 159 
influenzae type B) vaccines (See table S3b in the online repository). Regular antibacterial 160 
(azithromycin) prophylaxis was added to his treatment regimen in view of poor humoral response to 161 
vaccines. Despite this, he was hospitalised with pneumococcal septic arthritis at 9 months of age  after 162 
which immunoglobulin replacement was commenced. He remained susceptible to pyogenic bacterial 163 
infections, with further hospitalisations at 19 months (pneumonia), 22 months (peri-orbital cellulitis) 164 
and 24 months (pneumococcal bacteraemia). He also developed severe episodes of megaloblastic 165 
anemia during the course of his hospitalisation with septic arthritis at 9 months and pneumonia at 19 166 
months of age. 167 
 Retrospective review of both patients’ RBC indices revealed a persistent macrocytosis from 168 
young infancy (Fig 1). A detailed chronology of clinical manifestations is presented in the online 169 
repository (See case description in the online repository). 170

Exome Analysis 203 
Suspecting a defect in folate metabolism without a single candidate gene, we undertook whole exome 204 
sequencing (WES) to analyze multiple gene variants (6). Interrogation of variants within genes 205 
involved in folic acid metabolism (GO:0046655(7)) for which the brothers were concordant identified 206 
a maternally inherited novel point mutation in exon 3, heterozygous T- to -C transition at c.152 207 
position (c.152T>C; NM_005956.3), that would lead to a substitution of a conserved amino acid 208 
(p.Leu51Pro). This amino acid is situated within the methylenetetrahydrofolate 209 
dehydrogenase/cyclohydrogenase domain of C1-THF synthase and the amino acid substitution is 210 
predicted to adversely impact protein function. The base within the Leu-51 residue is well conserved 211 
evolutionarily, with a GERP++ score of 3.2(8). Leu-51 is located within a α-helix, with the adjacent 212 
Tyr-52 and the proximal Lys-56 involved in substrate binding within the 213 
dehydrogenase/cyclohydrolase active site (9). Proline residues are well documented to distort α-helices. 214 
The lack of an amide proton, coupled with the conformational rigidity of the cyclic residue canonically 215 
results in an approximately 300 bend in the helix (10) and the identified L51P mutation would be 216 
expected to distort this α-helix, reducing the affinity of the active site for the substrates.  217 
In the previous report of C1-THF synthase deficiency, the heterozygous parents were reported 218 
to be asymptomatic (2). Structural variations (SVs) that are also important for Mendelian disease are 219 
not easily detected using an exome approach (11). Most exome studies conducted so far on specific 220 
diseases focus on single nucleotide variants, rather than SVs such as insertion/deletion variants (12). 221 
This is due in part to the fact that insertion/deletion bioinformatics analysis methodologies are still 222 
being refined. Read depth and split reads provide a clue to structural variants within exome data (13). 223 
Therefore, we extended our bioinformatic interrogation of the MTHFD1 gene by manually examining 224 
sequencing depth coverage of the raw alignments in affected brothers compared to an unrelated 225 
individual concurrently sequenced. For exons with apparent reduced coverage, read depth was 226 
enumerated, normalized according to total aligned reads for the sample and assessed statistically (one 227 
tailed t-test) alongside 13 unrelated samples from the same sequencing batch, processed identically 228 
(Fig 2b). This process identified a paternally inherited deletion encompassing exon 13, in both brothers 229 
(Fig 2c).  230

Confirmation of mutation by Sanger sequencing 231 
PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing using primers (Primers EX3F & EX3R; See table S1 in the 232 
online repository), confirmed that the novel point mutation in exon 3 was present in both patients and 233 
the mother but not the father (Fig 2d). 234 
 Identification by traditional sequencing of the breakpoints of exon deletions suspected on the 235 
basis of read depth coverage in the exome data can be arduous as the breakpoints are likely to be 236 
located in the intronic regions upstream and downstream of the identified exon which may span many 237 
thousands of base pairs (bps). However, we noted that in the read pile up of our patients, there were 238 
anomalous read pairs spanning over 2000 bps (Fig 2c). Primers were designed (Ex13LF & EX13LR; 239 
See table S1 in the online repository) to encompass this region. The predicted PCR product size in the 240 
reference template is 2350 bps (See Fig S4a in the online repository). PCR amplification of this region 241 
revealed an expected 2350 bps product in the mother but only a product of 600 base pairs in the 242 
patients, confirming a deletion involving exon 13 (Fig 2e). We were subsequently able to delineate the 243 
breakpoints accurately by Sanger sequencing of the above PCR products from the mother and patients 244 
(See Figs S3a & S3b in the online repository). We were also able to confirm the heterozygous state for 245 
deletion of exon 13 in the patients by allele specific PCR (See Fig S4a & S4b in the online repository). 246 
Further analysis revealed that the deletion boundaries were within homologous positions of flanking, 247 
parallel, AluS repeats (See Fig S5a & S5b in the online repository), previously observed as a 248 
pathogenic mutational mechanism in other genes, such as in BRCA1 (14). For interested readers, 249 
further discussion is  provided in the online repository. 250 
Allelic expression at mRNA level 251 
The deletion of exon 13 is predicted to introduce a premature stop codon resulting in a 252 
truncated protein of 422 amino acids. The nonsense mediated decay (NMD) pathway exists in 253 
eukaryotic cells to ensure fidelity of transcription and prevent translation of truncated proteins with 254 
potentially deleterious gain-of-function or dominant-negative activity (17).  We hypothesised that the 255 
deletion of EX13 would target the mRNA from paternal allele (with a premature stop codon), for 256 
NMD. Allelic expression of the MTHFD1 gene was undertaken using the point mutation in the exon 3 257 
to analyse the relative expression of the maternal and paternal allele (18). MTHFD1 cDNA was 258 
amplified using gene specific primers covering the point mutation in the EX3 (inherited from their 259

Discussion 263 
Patients A and B carry compound heterozygous mutations consistent with autosomal recessive 264 
inheritance (Fig 2a), L51P inherited from their mother and deletion of exon 13 inherited from their 265 
father. In silico analysis of mutant L51P predicts an adverse impact on the function of the protein. A 266 
mutation in the vicinity (S49F) has recently been shown to be significantly less functional in 267 
biochemical studies (4). Allelic expression data is consistent with the deletion of exon 13 producing 268 
haploinsufficiency. The partial response to folinic acid therapy is consistent with a residual functioning 269 
enzyme as previously described (4).  270 
MTHFD1 mutations cause highly variable clinical phenotypes with megaloblastic anemia 271 
being the only consistent feature (3, 4). This is likely the result of variable degree of residual enzyme 272 
function, but the limited number of patients currently reported makes it impossible to draw conclusions 273 
regarding genotype to phenotype correlation. Clinical manifestations described in smaller proportion of 274 
patients include severe immunodeficiency, haemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS), mild mental 275 
retardation, epilepsy and retinopathy. Severe immunodeficiency has been reported in 3 out 5 patients 276 
and was the most likely cause of mortality in two of the patients. Macrocytosis, lymphopenia involving 277 
all subsets and low TREC (Fig 1) were present from infancy in the patients reported here and may be a 278 
clue to the need to investigate folate metabolic pathways. MTHFD1 deficiency should be considered  in 279 
the aetiology of megaloblastic anemia where there are normal levels of vitamin B12 and folate; and in  280 
CID where there is macrocytosis.. 281 
We have characterized the immune function in our kindred that delineate the immune function 282 
in MTHFD1 deficiency. The antigen presenting cells in the peripheral blood were present in normal 283 
numbers and responded to Toll Like Receptor (TLR) ligands in a comparable fashion to controls  (data 284 
not shown). Both cell mediated and humoral immunity were defective in our patients. The clinical 285 
improvement in susceptibility to infections, partial reconstitution of cell numbers and improvement in 286 
both overall immunoglobulin levels and vaccine responses suggest that the immunodeficiency cause by 287 
this condition can be reversed to a large extent with folinic acid treatment.  288 
The major limitation of exome sequencing is the inability to comprehensively represent 289 
genomic structural variations (SVs) such as deletions, inversions or duplications (11). Many groups 290 
have designed algorithms that use a read depth or read pair-based approach for predicting structural 291 
variation. However, these approaches are not very efficient at identifying SVs with exome data  due to 292 

  Ramakrishnan 
 
15 
the discontinuous nature of the data, with only ~ 1% of the genome having significant read coverage 293 
(19). Our approach was successful in identifying the exon 13 deletion due to strong clinical suspicion. 294 
While it may not be possible to detect all forms of structural variations, we have demonstrated the 295 
possibility of analysing specific genes using read depth, split reads and read pairs to provide clues to 296 
structural variants. Where improved detection of structural variants are required, whole-genome 297 
sequencing (WGS) should be used. 298 
The importance of establishing a definitive molecular diagnosis in children with primary 299 
immunodeficiency cannot be overemphasized. Both patients were considered to have SCID and were 300 
on the bone marrow transplant list for the first few years of life. Knowledge of the specific molecular 301 
diagnosis has led to the identification of a precision treatment in the form of life-long folinic acid 302 
supplementation. Other prophylactic therapies have been able to be discontinued. The responsiveness 303 
of this disorder to folinic acid supplementation emphasizes the importance of precision diagnostics in 304 
the diagnosis of primary immunodeficiency prior to the consideration of stem cell transplantation, and 305 
to stratify appropriate treatment modalities to reduce risk and improve outcomes. Clinical exome 306 
sequencing, even in single families, is a powerful adjunct to the investigation of metabolic pathways 307 
that may lead to distinctive immunodeficiency disorders. 308

Methods: allele specific PCR  
In a heterozygous state with one allele carrying a deletion, PCR products of two sizes are anticipated. We were 
unable to demonstrate the undeleted allele in the patients with primers EX13LF & EX13LR despite varying 
PCR conditions. To demonstrate the heterozygous state in the patients, we used allele specific primer (EX13SF) 
that is predicted to anneal to the deleted region. We set the PCR conditions to exclude the longer product (2350 
bps) by limiting the extension time. As shown in the supplementary figure 4a the PCR product from the primers 
EX13SF and EX13LR is possible only from the undeleted allele and is predicted to be around 1500 bps. With 
extension time limited to exclude the 2350 bps product, PCR product (from EX13LF & EX13LR) is only 
possible from the allele carrying exon 13 deletion and is expected to be of 600 bps size. As shown in the 
supplemental figure s4b, analysis by agarose gel electrophoresis of PCR products from patient A and the mother 
shows a heterozygous state for EX13 deletion in the patient.

AluS repeats as the mechanism for exon 13 deletion: 
Following breakpoint identification, further analysis revealed that the deletion boundaries around exon 13 were 
within homologous positions of flanking, parallel, AluS repeats (supplementary figure 5a & 5b). Alu elements 
are short interspersed elements, of approximately 300 bp in length and derive their name from a single 
recognition site for the restriction enzyme AluI located near the middle of the Alu element. Alu elements have 
amplified in primate genomes through a RNA dependent mechanism termed retropo sition and human 
chromosomes contain about 1,000,000 Alu copies, which equal 10% of the total genome (3, 4). Alu elements 
continue to amplify at a rate of 1 in 200 births and insertion of Alu elements contribute to approximately 0.1% 
of human genetic disorders. In addition to disease caused by insertion of Alu elements, their dispersion 
throughout the genome provides ample opportunity for unequal homologous recombination that can lead to 
deletion or insertion mutations and most often occur intrachromosomally (3). As illustrated (Fig S5a & S5b), 
these nearby low-complexity sequence regions likely permitted replication slippage to occur, excising the 1745 
bp region; this has been previously observed as a pathogenic mutational mechanism in other genes, such as in 
BRCA1 (6).
