# A Novel Complete F8 Tandem Duplication Causing Elevated Factor VIII Activity and Associated with Venous Thromboembolism.
**Authors:** Mao Y, Lu Y, Wu W, Ding Q, Wang X, Dai J, Wu X
**Journal:** TH Open (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1055/a-2938-6510](https://doi.org/10.1055/a-2938-6510)

## Content

Background Coagulation factor VIII (FVIII) is a critical component of the intrinsic coagulation pathway. While elevated FVIII levels are an established risk factor for venous thromboembolism (VTE), genetic variants in the F8 gene directly causing such elevations remain scarce. Here, we report a novel complete F8 tandem duplication identified in a female patient with splanchnic venous thrombosis (SVT). Methods We performed genetic testing using a thrombophilia panel targeting 35 genes involved in thrombosis and haemostasis to detect both point variants and copy number variations (CNVs). Family co-segregation analysis and phenotypic assays for FVIII and von Willebrand factor (VWF) were conducted. The structural basis of the identified F8 copy number gain was elucidated using optical genome mapping (OGM). Full-length F8 mRNA amplification, quantitative PCR, plasma FVIII Western blotting, and X-chromosome inactivation analysis were performed to assess the functional consequences of the duplication. Thrombin generation test (TGT) was employed to assess the hypercoagulable state. Results Genetic testing identified three copies of all 26 exons of the F8 gene in the proband, which was also detected in her mother (CNVs = 3) and son (CNVs = 2). One-stage clotting and chromogenic assays confirmed persistently elevated FVIII activity in the proband and her mother, accompanied by increased FVIII antigen levels. The OGM analysis confirmed a 229 kb tandem duplication including the F8 gene on one of the proband's X chromosomes. The junction regions exhibited high sequence homology and were rich in repetitive sequences, which precluded precise breakpoint mapping. Full-length F8 mRNA amplification revealed no aberrant transcripts, whereas quantitative PCR showed increased F8 mRNA expression in all carriers. Plasma FVIII Western blotting indicated FVIII heavy and light chains of expected molecular weights with increased band intensity in carriers. X-chromosome inactivation analysis in female carriers showed no significant skewing. TGT in two available carriers showed increased thrombin generation compared with a normal control at both low (1 pM) and high (5 pM) tissue factor concentrations. Conclusion We identified a novel complete F8 tandem duplication associated with increased FVIII expression and a hypercoagulable phenotype in a female patient with SVT. These findings support F8 gene dosage gain as a rare gain-of-function mechanism contributing to elevated FVIII levels and thrombophilia, while variation in VWF levels and acquired risk factors may modify thrombotic penetrance.

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), manifesting as deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and/or pulmonary embolism (PE), is one of the major contributors to the global burden of disease. 1 However, given the multifactorial etiology and inherent complexity of the disease, its accurate prediction, effective prevention, and optimal treatment remain clinically challenging. 2 Various genetic variants responsible for inherited hypercoagulable states have been identified as contributing to VTE risk. 3 Currently recognized genetic risk factors include loss-of-function variants in anticoagulant proteins (such as antithrombin [AT], 4 protein C [PC], 5 6 7 and protein S [PS] 8 ) and gain-of-function variants in coagulation factors (such as factor V Leiden 9 and prothrombin G20210A 10 ). In addition to these two relatively common single nucleotide polymorphisms, which confer a modest thrombotic risk, rare gain-of-function variants in coagulation factors have also been identified, including FIX Padua 11 and prothrombin Yukuhashi, 12 that are associated with a higher thrombotic risk in affected families. Advances in high-throughput genomic technologies have further enabled the identification of such gain-of-function variants in coagulation factor genes.

Coagulation factor VIII (FVIII), which forms a complex with activated coagulation factor IX to activate factor X, plays a key role in the intrinsic coagulation pathway. 13 Quantitative or qualitative deficiencies in FVIII cause hemophilia A, a bleeding disorder for which 6211 unique F8 variants have been reported to date. 14 Conversely, elevated FVIII activity levels have been reported to be associated with VTE recurrence. 15 Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have also revealed genes that influence FVIII levels. 16 Nevertheless, gain-of-function variants in F8 that increase FVIII activity and lead to thrombosis remain rare. To date, only one large duplication involving exon 1 and intron 1 of the F8 gene has been reported in association with markedly elevated FVIII activity and severe thrombophilia. 17 More recently, a naturally occurring gain-of-function missense variant (NM_000132.4:c.1768C > A, p.Arg590Ser) in the F8 gene has been identified, 18 further supporting the existence of rare F8 variants that enhance coagulation activity and promote thrombosis.

Here, we identified a heterozygous complete duplication of F8 in a young female patient who presented with acute splanchnic vein thrombosis. Molecular and functional analyses indicated that the complete F8 duplication led to elevated factor VIII activity, contributing to a hypercoagulable state and the development of VTE.

The patient was a 34-year-old female who presented with sudden onset of acute abdominal pain and haematochezia. An abdominal contrast-enhanced computed tomography scan revealed extensive thrombosis in the splenic vein, main portal vein, and superior mesenteric vein. The event occurred while she was receiving a short course of oral contraceptives for menstrual irregularities. After admission, she underwent portal venography, thrombectomy, and catheter-directed thrombolysis. Following clinical stabilization, a partial jejunectomy was performed. Post-discharge anticoagulation therapy with rivaroxaban was initiated and has been maintained. Laboratory screening for common thrombophilia risk factors including activities of AT, PC, PS, lupus anticoagulants, and antiphospholipid antibodies revealed no abnormality. Family investigations showed that there was no history of thrombotic events. Informed consent was obtained from all participating individuals, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital.

A comprehensive thrombophilia panel targeting the coding regions of 35 coagulation-related genes ( F2 , F5 , F7 , F8 , F9 , F10 , F11 , F12 , FGA, FGB, FGG, F13A1, F13B, VWF, SERPINC1 , PROC , PROS1 , PROCR , THBD , SERPINA10, SERPIND1 , TFPI , PLG , PLAT, PLAU, SERPINE1, SERPINF2, CPB2, HABP2, CALR, GP6, JAK2, MPL, HRG , and ADAMTS13 ) was employed to screen for inherited risk factors as previously described. 19 The analysis encompassed the detection of both single nucleotide variants using massively parallel sequencing based on NGS and copy number variations (CNVs) using the CNVplex technique across the coding sequences of these genes. The CNVplex technique is a high-throughput DNA copy number detection technique that employs a ligase-mediated specific ligation reaction for target region hybridization and ligation. In our thrombophilia panel, 516 probes covering all the targeted regions of selected genes and 120 probes for reference target sequences were designed. After hybridization and ligation, DNA products were amplified and separated by capillary electrophoresis. The copy number of each target region was calculated by sample area analysis.

Peripheral blood was collected from the proband and her related family members using vacuum blood collection tubes containing 0.129 mol/L sodium citrate to obtain platelet-poor plasma (PPP). The FVIII activity (FVIII:C) was assessed using both a one-stage clotting assay and a chromogenic substrate assay on a Sysmex CS-5100 automatic coagulometer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan). FVIII antigen levels (FVIII:Ag) were assessed using the VisuLize FVIII Antigen Kit (Affinity Biologicals, Ancaster, ON, Canada) and the normal range was obtained from 10 healthy individuals of both sexes aged 20 to 50 years old. VWF antigen (VWF:Ag) and VWF activity (VWF:Act) levels were measured by a latex-enhanced automated immunoturbidimetric assay based on antibodies against VWF epitopes that are uninvolved or involved in VWF-GPIb binding, respectively. All methods mentioned above were conducted on an ACL-TOP automatic coagulometer (Instrumentation Laboratory, Bedford, MA, USA) as described before. 20 The VWF propeptide (VWFpp) antigen level was assessed using an ELISA method with the anti-human VWFpp MW1939 antibody pair (Sanquin, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) as previously described. 21

To further confirm the CNVs detected by CNVplex technique, optical genome mapping was performed as previously described. 19 Briefly, high molecular weight (HMW) DNA was extracted from the proband’s peripheral blood using the Bionano Prep SP Blood and Cell Culture DNA Isolation Kit (Bionano Genomics, CA, USA) and quantified by the Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). The integrity and size of DNA were validated by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis. The DNA was then labeled with the DL-Green fluorophore using the Bionano Prep DLS Kit (Bionano Genomics). This kit employs the DLE-1 enzyme, which specifically recognizes the CTTAAG sequence on genomic DNA. Subsequently, the Bionano Saphyr instrument imaged genomic DNA molecules on a chip by detecting DL-Green fluorescent signals within the blue DNA background. High-resolution charge-coupled device cameras generated real-time imaging data, which were automatically converted into molecular data and integrated into a genome-wide optical map. De novo assembly analysis was performed using the Bionano Access V1.7 software.

Breakpoint analysis was performed using a sequencing alignment-based approach with the hg38 reference genome. Sequencing data of the breakpoint region and potential low-copy repeats near the breakpoint were analyzed using the Human BLAT Search Tool ( http://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi-bin/hgBlat ). Repeat Masker ( www.repeatmasker.org ) was used to identify repetitive elements associated with rearrangement mechanisms, such as long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs), short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs), long terminal repeats, and DNA transposons.

Reverse transcription-PCR (RT-PCR) was performed to assess the effect of the rearrangement on F8 mRNA splicing. Total RNA was isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells of the proband by a phenol-chloroform-based RNA extraction method. cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Full-length F8 mRNA was detected and sequenced by overlapping PCR as previously described. 22 The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S1 (online only). An internal control of β-actin ( ACTB ) was included to monitor reverse transcription efficiency. The same samples were amplified using Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, Dalian, Japan) with primers located in exons 25–26 of F8 ( Supplementary Table S1 (online only)). Relative F8 expression levels were normalized to two housekeeping genes ( GAPDH and ACTB ) using the 2 −ΔCp method.

Western blotting was performed to characterize the expressed FVIII protein in the carriers’ plasma. Plasma samples from the three carriers (I-1, II-1, and III-1) and a normal control (FVIII:C 98.6%) were diluted 1:4 with PBS buffer. B-domain-deleted recombinant human FVIII (Omfiloctocog alfa, Anjiayin, China) was used as a positive control for human FVIII. The samples were separated on a 6% SDS-PAGE gel (Epizyme, Shanghai, China) and then electroblotted onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. A mouse anti-human factor VIII monoclonal antibody (#10104, QEB, UK) and an HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (ab205719, Abcam, UK) were used to detect FVIII in the samples.

To determine whether skewed X-chromosome inactivation (XCI) was present in the proband and her mother (I-1), the human androgen receptor (HUMARA) assay was performed as previously described. 23 Briefly, genomic DNA samples were incubated with 10 U Hpa II (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) at 37 °C overnight, followed by heat inactivation of the enzyme at 80 °C for 20 min. The highly polymorphic androgen receptor promoter region was amplified from both the Hpa II-treated samples and untreated control samples, and the products were analyzed on an ABI 3100 DNA Analyzer using GeneMapper software version 3.7. The XCI ratio was calculated based on the peak areas of the smaller and larger alleles using the following formula: XCI ratio = D1/U1 ÷ (D1/U1 + D2/U2) × 100%. D1 and D2 represented the peak areas of the two alleles in the digested sample, and U1 and U2 represented the peak areas of the corresponding alleles in the undigested sample. An XCI ratio ≥80% was defined as skewed X inactivation, whereas an XCI ratio <80% was defined as random inactivation.

The comprehensive coagulation state of the identified variant carriers (I-1 and III-1) with available plasma samples and not receiving anticoagulant therapy were evaluated by TGT, which was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions (Diagnostica Stago, Asnières-sur-Seine, France) using PPP. The reaction was initiated by the PPP reagent low with 1 pM tissue factor (TF) or the PPP reagent with 5 pM TF. The hydrolysis of FluCa substrate was measured by the Fluoroskan Ascent FL luminometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) for 60 minutes. Thrombinoscope Software was used to plot thrombin generation curves and calculate relevant parameters including lag time (LT, min), peak height (Peak, nM), endogenous thrombin potential (ETP, nM•min), and time to peak (ttPeak, min).

Genetic screening for inherited thrombophilia revealed three copies of all 26 exon fragments of the F8 gene in the proband ( Fig. 1A ). No pathogenic variant was detected in the other 34 genes included in the thrombophilia panel. Subsequent CNV analysis of available family members showed that the mother (I-1, CNV = 3) and son (III-1, CNV = 2) also carried the same F8 copy number variation ( Fig. 1B ).

The proband (II-1), her mother (I-1), and son (III-1) all exhibited elevated FVIII activity (272.2%, 197.5%, and 148.5%, respectively), compared with a non-carrier family member (I-2, 103.2%), and elevated FVIII activity was accompanied by mildly shortened activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), although all values remained within the normal reference range ( Table 1 ). Repeat measurements obtained approximately 23 months later confirmed persistent FVIII:C elevation in the proband and her mother (283.9% and 218.9%, respectively), whereas FVIII:C in the son had decreased to 111.2%. The FVIII antigen levels among the family members were found to be closely correlated with their respective FVIII activity. In addition, the one-stage and chromogenic FVIII:C results were concordant in all three carriers (II-1, 283.9% vs. 241.5%; I-1, 218.9% vs. 210.9%; and III-1, 111.2% vs. 108.7%). The baseline one-stage FVIII:C/FVIII:Ag ratios were 0.93, 0.97, 0.83, and 1.13 for II-1, I-1, I-2, and III-1, respectively. At follow-up, the corresponding one-stage ratios were 1.05, 1.02, and 1.09 for II-1, I-1, and III-1, while the chromogenic ratios were 0.90, 0.99, and 1.07. These values did not indicate a consistent increase in FVIII activity per unit of FVIII antigen.

To explore potential modifiers of the variable FVIII phenotype, we further evaluated ABO blood group and VWF-related parameters in all participating family members ( Table 1 ). All four family members were blood group O. The proband, who had the highest baseline FVIII:C, also had markedly elevated VWF:Act and VWF:Ag (195.8 and 183.6%, respectively). In contrast, the mother and son had VWF values within the reference interval, with the son showing the lowest VWF levels (VWF:Act 82.5%; VWF:Ag 84.1%). The baseline FVIII:Ag/VWF:Ag ratios were 1.60, 1.63, 0.92, and 1.56 for II-1, I-1, I-2, and III-1, respectively. The proband also demonstrated the highest VWFpp level (161.4%) among the tested individuals. Her VWFpp/VWF:Ag ratio was similar to that of I-1 (0.88 vs. 0.85), which is compatible with increased VWF synthesis or secretion rather than reduced clearance as the major explanation for her elevated VWF level. By contrast, III-1 had the highest VWFpp/VWF:Ag ratio (1.39), a pattern compatible with relatively faster VWF clearance and potentially contributing to his lower and non-persistent FVIII:C.

Due to the limitations of targeted CNV detection in resolving the precise structural details of the duplicated F8 region, optical genome mapping was performed and identified a duplicated fragment of approximately 229 kb on one ChX (g.154789168_155018882; Fig. 2A ). This duplicated segment essentially contained the complete F8 gene, except for the 5′ UTR and exon 1 region, which could not be detected due to the absence of probes. The segment was connected in tandem with the normal F8 gene ( Fig. 2B ). Combined with the identified three copies of the 5′ UTR and exon 1 region by CNVplex technique, these data suggested the presence of a tandem duplication involving two complete F8 genes. Additionally, the duplicated region included partial MPP1 (g.154789168_154805485), complete SMIM9 (g.154823348_154834662), and partial FUNDC2 (g.155026844_155032804).

A segment of approximately 23.5 kb could not be precisely resolved owing to the absence of OGM probe signals ( Fig. 2B ), and this region likely harboured the breakpoint responsible for the duplication. Integrating the results from CNVplex and OGM, the putative breakpoint was mapped to the interval between the terminal point of the CNVplex-defined duplication (‘a’ in Fig. 2B ) and the initiation point identified by OGM (‘b’ in Fig. 2B ). To amplify the breakpoint region, a genome walking strategy was employed based on the known DNA sequences flanking the breakpoint. Gene-specific primers were designed in both directions from the two known loci (a and b). However, extensive homologous sequences prevented amplification of the breakpoint region. Sequence analysis of the potential breakpoint region revealed an abundance of highly homologous and low-complexity repeats, primarily LINE elements ( Fig. 2C ), which likely precluded successful PCR amplification.

The full-length F8 mRNA was amplified as nine overlapping fragments from the proband. The sizes of the fragments and sequencing results revealed no aberrant transcripts, suggesting normal transcription of the F8 gene ( Fig. 3A ). In addition, quantitative RT-PCR showed that the relative F8 expression level in the proband was approximately 2-fold higher than that in normal controls. A similarly significant elevation was also observed in the proband’s mother and son, both of whom carried the same duplication ( Fig. 3B ). The molecular weights of the FVIII heavy and light chains in plasma from carriers were comparable to those of the normal control, and the normalized band intensity (relative to the pure protein) was significantly higher in carriers than in the normal control ( Fig. 3C,D ). These findings suggested that the tandem duplication enhanced F8 transcription and FVIII protein expression.

HUMARA analysis was performed in the proband and her mother to investigate whether -XCI affected the expression of the duplicated F8 allele in female carriers. The XCI ratios were 75:25 in the proband and 78:22 in her mother ( Table 2 ). Neither of the ratios met the predefined criterion for skewed XCI (≥80%). Therefore, both female carriers were considered to have non-skewed XCI.

The coagulation profile associated with the tandem F8 duplication was assessed via TGT using plasma samples from the two identified variant carriers (I-1 and III-1), whose plasma samples were available and who were not receiving anticoagulant therapy ( Fig. 4 ). When activated with a low TF concentration (1 pM), the carriers exhibited markedly elevated peak thrombin levels (312.88 and 289.28 nM vs. 177.96 nM). The time to peak was also modestly shortened. Consequently, plasma from the carriers showed increased ETP compared with the normal control (1570.41 and 1322.05 nM·min vs. 1195.46 nM·min). Under a high TF concentration (5 pM), the peak level remained elevated in the carriers relative to the normal control (461.27 and 483.45 nM vs. 347.99 nM). Slightly elevated ETP values (1766.44 and 1685.03 nM·min vs. 1583.57 nM·min) and modestly shortened time to peak compared with the normal control were also observed. Consistent with their FVIII activity levels, the ETP value of III-1 was slightly lower than that of I-1 at both low and high TF concentrations. Collectively, these results indicated that the tandem duplication of the F8 gene led to elevated FVIII activity and conferred a hypercoagulable phenotype.

Deficiency in FVIII, a key component of the intrinsic coagulation pathway, causes haemophilia A, whereas elevated FVIII activity is a recognized prothrombotic risk factor. However, thrombosis directly linked to gain-of-function variants in F8 remains exceedingly rare. In this study, we identified a novel heterozygous tandem duplication involving an additional intact copy of F8 in a female patient who presented with SVT. The duplication co-segregated with elevated FVIII activity in the available family members and was associated with increased F8 mRNA and FVIII protein expression. No aberrant transcripts or grossly abnormal FVIII heavy or light chains were identified. TGTs in the available carriers further supported a hypercoagulable phenotype, characterized by increased peak thrombin and endogenous thrombin potential. Together, these findings suggested that the F8 tandem duplication increased FVIII expression, most likely through a gene-dosage effect, and contributed to a prothrombotic state.

Gain-of-function mechanisms in coagulation factor genes are uncommon but clinically important. The best-known examples included FIX Padua 11 and prothrombin Yukuhashi, 12 in which single amino-acid substitutions markedly increase coagulant activity or confer resistance to natural anticoagulants. Gain-of-function variants of the F8 gene are rarer. A previously reported partial F8 duplication, factor VIII Padua, was associated with markedly elevated FVIII levels and familial thrombophilia through altered regulation of F8 expression. 17 More recently, FVIII p.Arg590Ser was reported in recurrent thrombosis and was shown to increase FVIII functional activity and confer resistance to activated protein C. 18 In contrast, the variant identified in this study was a complete tandem duplication involving the F8 gene and was associated with increased F8 mRNA and plasma FVIII protein without obvious transcript or protein abnormalities. Therefore, our findings expand the spectrum of F8 gain-of-function mechanisms from regulatory rearrangements and qualitative functional changes to complete gene-dosage gain. Previous work has reported an association between F8 copy-number variation and VTE risk. 24 Taken together, the co-segregation data and the increase in full-length F8 transcript, FVIII antigen, and FVIII activity support complete F8 duplication as a plausible thrombophilia-associated genetic risk factor. However, as the evidence is derived from a single pedigree with incomplete clinical penetrance and no population-level frequency data, this duplication should currently be regarded as a candidate risk variant rather than a definitively established pathogenic thrombophilia variant. Accordingly, F8 CNV analysis may nevertheless be considered in select patients with otherwise unexplained, preferably persistent FVIII elevation after common acquired causes and ABO/VWF-related effects have been evaluated, particularly in patients with recurrent thrombosis, thrombosis at a young age or an unusual site, or a suggestive family history. Larger cohort and population studies are required before formal clinical screening recommendations could be made.

The clinical phenotype was not uniform among carriers, indicating incomplete penetrance. Although all three carriers had elevated FVIII activity, only the proband developed a thrombotic event, suggesting that the F8 duplication may act as a predisposing genetic factor rather than a sole determinant of thrombosis. This is consistent with a multifactorial model of venous thrombosis, in which increased F8 gene dosage raises baseline thrombotic susceptibility, while additional acquired or environmental triggers determine clinical manifestation. 25 The proband’s higher FVIII activity coincided with elevated VWF antigen, VWF activity, and VWF propeptide levels, which may further stabilize FVIII or reflect endothelial activation. 26 Oral contraceptive exposure may also act as an acquired prothrombotic trigger of the proband’s thrombotic event. 27 However, other potential contributors, including local vascular injury and unrecognized genetic or environmental modifiers, 28 29 were not systematically assessed in this study. Thus, the absence of thrombosis in other carriers does not argue against the prothrombotic relevance of the duplication, but instead highlights its incomplete penetrance and the multifactorial nature of thrombotic disease.

The variability of FVIII activity among carriers may reflect additional modifiers beyond F8 gene dosage. Because the F8 gene is located on the X chromosome, XCI may influence FVIII expression in female carriers. 30 HUMARA analysis showed no significant skewing of XCI in the proband and her mother, suggesting that preferential expression of the duplicated X chromosome was unlikely to be the major determinant of different FVIII levels in these two female carriers. Nevertheless, XCI patterns may differ among tissues, 31 and we did not assess XCI directly in FVIII-relevant tissues. A tissue-specific effect cannot be completely excluded. In addition, VWF, the protective plasma carrier that stabilizes FVIII and protects it from premature clearance, may also contribute to variability in FVIII activity. ABO blood group is a well-established determinant of circulating VWF and FVIII levels, with group O individuals generally having lower VWF levels than non-O individuals, largely because ABO-related glycosylation affects VWF clearance. 32 Because all participating family members were blood group O, ABO status cannot explain the intrafamilial differences in VWF or FVIII. Consistent with reports associating elevated VWF with increased FVIII in VTE, 26 the proband had the highest VWF and FVIII levels. The similar VWFpp/VWF:Ag ratios in the proband and her mother, together with the higher absolute VWFpp level in the proband, are compatible with increased synthesis or secretion rather than reduced clearance. 33 In contrast, the higher VWFpp/VWF:Ag ratio in the son is compatible with relatively faster VWF clearance and may partly explain his lower and non-persistent FVIII:C.

Due to the high homology and extensive repetitive elements in the breakpoint region, we were unable to design primers to confirm the precise breakpoint sequence. Repeat Masker analysis confirmed that the breakpoint region was densely populated with LINEs, which were known to be a significant source of genomic instability. 34 During DNA replication or repair, misalignment between LINEs on sister chromatids or homologous chromosomes can provide substrates for illegitimate recombination. Recombination between such non-allelic LINEs may lead to an erroneous exchange of genetic material, resulting in deletions or duplications. 35 This mechanism may explain the tandem duplication observed in the proband, although other repeat-mediated mechanisms cannot be excluded. In addition, without nucleotide-level breakpoint mapping, we cannot fully exclude the possibility that local regulatory elements or chromatin architecture were disrupted or repositioned by the rearrangement and may also influence F8 expression. Nevertheless, the presence of an intact duplicated F8 copy, together with increased full-length F8 transcript and elevated plasma FVIII protein, supports increased gene dosage as the most plausible explanation for the FVIII elevation.

This study has several limitations. First, it was based on a single pedigree with only three identified carriers. TGT was performed in only two carriers (I-1 and III-1), as the proband was receiving anticoagulant therapy, and the results were compared descriptively with those of a single normal control. Given the small sample size and the absence of thrombosis in the two tested carriers, the TGT findings should be interpreted as supportive evidence of a hypercoagulable phenotype rather than proof of a direct relationship among the F8 duplication, thrombin generation, and clinical thrombosis. Second, although full-length F8 mRNA amplification and plasma FVIII Western blotting did not reveal obvious abnormal transcripts or altered FVIII proteins, more detailed biochemical and functional studies of FVIII were not performed. Third, the prevalence of this complete F8 tandem duplication in larger thrombosis cohorts and in the general population was not investigated. However, this structural duplication has not been identified in thrombophilia patients at our centre to date, suggesting that it is at least uncommon in our clinical practice. Future screening for this structural variant in VTE patients, particularly those with persistently elevated FVIII levels, as well as in general populations, is needed to determine its frequency, penetrance, and clinical significance.

In summary, we identified a novel complete tandem duplication of F8 associated with elevated FVIII expression and a hypercoagulable phenotype in a female patient with SVT. The available evidence supported increased F8 gene dosage as the major mechanism, while variation in VWF levels and acquired risk factors, such as oral contraceptive exposure, may modify FVIII levels and thrombotic penetrance. These findings highlight the importance of considering structural variants in patients with unexplained thrombosis and elevated FVIII levels when conventional sequencing failed to identify pathogenic variants.
