<section><div><h2>Abstract</h2><div><p><i>PTEN</i> hamartoma tumour syndrome (PHTS) is a diverse multi-system disorder predisposing to a high hereditary risk of breast, thyroid, endometrial, and a moderate risk of renal, and colorectal cancer and skin melanoma. Besides the risk of cancer, PHTS is also associated with benign tumours such as skin and connective tissue tumours, vascular malformations and neurodevelopmental disorders, including autism spectrum disorders. New evidence on cancer risks and the effectiveness of surveillance has been published since the last iteration of the European Reference Network on Genetic Tumour Risk Syndromes (ERN GENTURIS) guidelines from 2020, necessitating the update presented here A comprehensive literature review was undertaken, and guidelines were revised by clinicians with PHTS expertise from relevant medical disciplines, together with PHTS patients and their representatives. Revised recommendations were put forward for surveillance for breast, thyroid, endometrial, renal, and colorectal cancer and skin melanoma. The proposed cancer surveillance recommendations for PHTS require significant patient commitment as well as a coordinated multidisciplinary medical approach. There is a need for prospective evaluation of the effectiveness of these recommendations in the PHTS population.</p></div></div></section>

<section><div><h2>Introduction</h2><div><p><i>PTEN</i> hamartoma tumour syndrome (PHTS), OMIM:158350, ORPHA:306498, is caused by germline pathogenic variants (gPVs) in the phosphatase and tensin homologue (<i>PTEN</i>) gene. <i>PTEN</i> is a tumour suppressor gene, the loss of which results in increased cell proliferation and survival leading to tumorigenesis [<a>1</a>,<a>2</a>,<a>3</a>,<a>4</a>]. The prevalence of PHTS was previously estimated to be 1 in 200,000–250,000 [<a>1</a>, <a>3</a>], but is now thought to be higher at around 1 in 9000 to 1 in 15,000 [<a>5</a>, <a>6</a>]. Older terms for PHTS, such as Cowden syndrome (CS), Bannayan-Riley-Ruvalcaba syndrome (BRRS), Lhermitte-Duclos disease, Segmental outgrowth-lipomatosis-arteriovenous malformation-epidermal nevus syndrome (SOLAMEN) and Proteus-like syndrome can be confusing and should no longer be used. PHTS is a diverse multi-system disorder. Individuals with PHTS have an increased risk of breast, thyroid, endometrial, and possibly of colorectal, renal cancer and skin melanoma [<a>7</a>]. PHTS also predisposes to the development of multiple hamartomatous growths, including benign polyps in the gastrointestinal tract and skin lesions and for neurodevelopmental disorders, including autism spectrum disorders [<a>8</a>].</p><p>Earlier studies reported average lifetime risks of cancer in individuals with PHTS ranging from 85–89% for any cancer, 67–85% for female breast cancer, 6–38% for thyroid cancer, 19–28% for endometrial cancer, 2–34% for renal cancer, 9–20% for colorectal cancer (CRC) and 0–6% for melanoma [<a>2</a>, <a>9</a>,<a>10</a>,<a>11</a>,<a>12</a>]. These estimates are likely to be at the upper end of the true range because of ascertainment bias in published studies. The latest study [<a>13</a>] has excluded index cases when calculating cumulative risk, which reduces ascertainment bias and likely provides more accurate risk estimates, which are lower than were previously published (Table <a>1</a>). Ultimately, larger prospective studies, including those individuals diagnosed in childhood because of developmental problems, and asymptomatic relatives with <i>PTEN</i> gPV, will be needed to further refine the risks.</p><div><figure><figcaption><b>Table 1 Cumulative risks of tumours in individuals with PHTS.</b></figcaption><div><a><span>Full size table</span><svg><use></use></svg></a></div></figure></div><p>PHTS is rare and its diagnosis relies on the identification of a gPV in <i>PTEN</i>. Early identification of individuals and appropriate surveillance are key to the timely detection of premalignant or early cancer lesions and can precede the development of advanced cancer by several years [<a>14</a>]. Individuals with PHTS have an increased risk of various cancers which are amenable to early detection. In 2020, the first European guidelines on cancer surveillance in PHTS were published [<a>15</a>]. Since then, new data became available evaluating the effectiveness of the proposed cancer surveillance guidelines as well as on cancer risks.</p><p>Key clinical questions addressed in this guideline are:</p><ul>
<li>
<p>What is currently known about the efficacy, frequency and potential surveillance methods for breast, thyroid, endometrial, renal, or colorectal cancers, or skin melanoma in PHTS?</p>
</li>
</ul><p>For the different tumour types (breast, thyroid, endometrial, renal, colorectal, skin):</p><ul>
<li>
<p>What methods (image modality) should be used in surveillance of [tumour type]?</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>At what age should surveillance of [tumour type] start?</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>What is the frequency for surveillance of [tumour type]?</p>
</li>
</ul><p>This guideline seeks to balance the risk of harm from the overdiagnosis of cancer with the potential benefits of early identification of cancers.</p><p>This guideline has been prepared to support the decision-making of health professionals discussing surveillance with individuals who have a diagnosis of PHTS.</p></div></div></section><section><div><h2>Methods</h2><div><p>The ERN GENTURIS (European Reference Network for patients with a rare and/or complex genetic tumour risk syndrome) guideline group for cancer surveillance for individuals with PHTS (ERN GENTURIS PHTS guideline group) was established by 17 experts in PHTS from nine countries as well as three patient representatives acting as community representatives, one of whom is also a medical expert in PHTS. The ERN GENTURIS PHTS guideline group consisted of a core working group comprising 8 clinical experts of which 1 was also a patient representative. The core working group met online and drafted the guideline scope, clinical questions, recommendations and guideline document and obtained feedback from the extended ERN GENTURIS PHTS guideline group. The recommendations were finalised in a modified Delphi approach in which the PHTS guideline group (including the core working group) and 48 additional experts participated. The guideline was based on an initial review of 106 published articles extracted from PubMed and additional hand search and expert recommendations. The following terms were used: Search: (screening[title/abstract] OR surveillance[title/abstract]) AND (PTEN[title] OR Cowden[Title]) AND ‘humans’ OR ‘humans’[MeSH Terms] Filters: from 2019 to 2024 ((‘screening’[Title/Abstract] OR ‘surveillance’[Title/Abstract]) AND (‘PTEN’[Title] OR ‘Cowden’[Title]) AND ‘humans’ OR ‘humans’[MeSH Terms]) AND (2019:2024[pdat]) Search: (screening[title/abstract] OR surveillance[title/abstract]) AND (PHTS[title]) AND ‘humans’ OR ‘humans’[MeSH Terms]. Date of searches: 16th Sept 2024.</p><p>Of these 106 articles screened, 30 were selected for full paper review as they could potentially answer the health questions addressed in the guideline. In the end, 16 papers [<a>13</a>, <a>16</a>,<a>17</a>,<a>18</a>,<a>19</a>,<a>20</a>,<a>21</a>,<a>22</a>,<a>23</a>,<a>24</a>,<a>25</a>,<a>26</a>,<a>27</a>,<a>28</a>,<a>29</a>,<a>30</a>] contained evidence directly related to the recommendations and were included in this guideline.</p><p>The full details of the guideline, including literature search, reference list and Delphi process can be found at: <a>https://www.genturis.eu/l=eng/Guidelines-and-pathways/Clinical-practice-guidelines.html</a>.</p><p>Recommendations were written in one of four stylistic formats: Should &amp; Should Not, (most well-informed people (those who have considered the evidence) would take this action, Should Probably &amp; Should Probably Not the majority of informed people would take this action, but a substantial minority would not). Using the modified Delphi process and multiple rounds of review by the core working group, 17 recommendations based on the original guideline and adapted by the core working group were made relating to breast, thyroid, endometrial, and renal cancer, CRC and skin melanoma. Participants assessed recommendations by a four-point Likert scale (totally disagree, disagree, agree, totally agree) and consensus was defined when &gt;60% of participants responded ‘agree’ or ‘totally agree’, in accordance to the threshold of consensus used in other ERN GENTURIS guidelines [<a>31</a>,<a>32</a>,<a>33</a>,<a>34</a>,<a>35</a>]. However, even if consensus was met, recommendations were still modified if a higher consensus was thought to be achievable from the written responses that accompanied the ratings.</p><p>To balance the weight of both published evidence and quantify the wealth of expert experience and knowledge, we graded the recommendations according to a three-point scale: Strong=expert consensus AND consistent evidence; Moderate=expert consensus WITH inconsistent evidence AND/OR new evidence likely to support the recommendation; Weak=Expert majority decision WITHOUT consistent evidence.</p><p>The process for developing this guideline has been based on the principles of Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) to ensure the quality of the guidelines, but is tailored for rare disease guidelines, using an “enhanced GRADE” approach to mitigate the challenges associated with rare disease research. This ensures that guidelines are both evidence-based and clinically relevant to the complex needs of rare disease populations [<a>36</a>]. Within this process the core working group completed an internal assessment of the quality of the guideline against AGREE II criteria.</p></div></div></section><section><div><h2>Results</h2><div><p>There is strong evidence of a high or moderately increased hereditary risk of multiple cancers in individuals with a gPV in <i>PTEN</i> e.g. for women breast- and endometrial-cancer and for all thyroid-, renal-, colorectal-cancer and melanoma (Table <a>1</a>). We provide the cumulative cancer risks up to age 70 years, but it is worth noting there are very few data on PHTS individuals over 60-70 years leading to wider confidence intervals of risk estimates in this age range [<a>13</a>]. Recently the cumulative risk for non-sex related PHTS associated cancers has been shown to be higher in females than males, for example thyroid cancers. At present, we are not making sex-specific surveillance recommendations for these cancers, but this may change in the future [<a>37</a>].</p><p>All cancer surveillance recommendations are summarised in Table <a>2</a>. The 17 recommendations included in this manuscript (see Table <a>3</a>) reached agreement between 72 and 98% and are specified below. For less than a decade PHTS surveillance guidelines have been implemented. Both because of this and the rarity of PHTS there only is reasonable published evidence for the positive effects of surveillance on earlier stage of detection in breast cancer [<a>13</a>, <a>20</a>, <a>29</a>, <a>30</a>].</p><div><figure><figcaption><b>Table 2 Summary of the surveillance protocol.</b></figcaption><div><a><span>Full size table</span><svg><use></use></svg></a></div></figure></div><div><figure><figcaption><b>Table 3 Guideline recommendations.</b></figcaption><div><a><span>Full size table</span><svg><use></use></svg></a></div></figure></div><h3>Breast cancer recommendations (Table <a>3</a>, rec 1–5 [<a>13</a>, <a>20</a>, <a>29</a>, <a>30</a>])</h3><p>Breast cancer risk for females with a gPV in <i>PTEN</i> is greatly increased compared to the general population. Various studies have consistently shown an increase in breast cancer risk [<a>2</a>, <a>11</a>,<a>12</a>,<a>13</a>, <a>38</a>]. One recent study estimated the risk at age 70 years as being 60% (95% CI, 47–72%) excluding cancer-index patients and 80% (95% CI = 65–92%) including cancer-index patients [<a>13</a>]. Evidence suggests surveillance can detect breast cancer early and lead to lower tumour stage at diagnosis and improved survival in these individuals [<a>13</a>, <a>20</a>, <a>29</a>, <a>30</a>]. Breast cancers detected outside of a surveillance programme were shown to be of a higher stage [<a>20</a>]. Females with PHTS tend to develop breast cancer early, with an age range of 16–85 years and a median age below 50 years at first diagnosis. The highest increase in breast cancer incidence was observed between the age of 30 and 40 years, with 15% of the individuals developing breast cancer before the age of 29 years [<a>13</a>, <a>20</a>, <a>30</a>].</p><p>Breast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed annually has been shown to have the greatest sensitivity for detecting breast cancers in these individuals with mammography being of no additional benefit [<a>20</a>].</p><p>With evidence increasingly suggesting that breast cancer risk in PHTS is similar to that in women with gPVs in <i>BRCA1/BRCA2</i>, many recommendations can be derived from the much larger evidence base, which exists for hereditary breast and ovarian cancer predisposition syndrome. Performing yearly breast MRI is therefore recommended from the age of 25 years. In addition, mammography every 1–2 years can be considered, preferably from the age of 40 years or in line with national guidelines for surveillance of <i>BRCA1</i> patients. Recently, Hendricks et al. reported evidence for high second primary cancer risks in individuals with PHTS, driven primarily by breast cancer diagnoses in females, with a 10-year risk for second primary breast cancer after first primary breast cancer of 46% (95% CI, 33–60%) [<a>39</a>]. This reinforces our recommendation to offer risk reducing surgery in individual risk management discussion.</p><h3>Thyroid cancer recommendations (Table <a>3</a>, rec 6–9 [<a>13</a>, <a>16</a>,<a>17</a>,<a>18</a>, <a>21</a>, <a>25</a>,<a>26</a>,<a>27</a>,<a>28</a>,<a>29</a>,<a>30</a>])</h3><p>The risk of thyroid cancer is increased in individuals with PHTS. Recent studies of two large separate PHTS cohorts have found comparable risk estimates with life time risk of 33% (95% CI, 24–40%, cumulative incidence rates by age 80 years) [<a>30</a>] and 29% (95% CI, 15–53% cumulative risk at age 70 years) [<a>13</a>], with a lower risk (17% (95% CI, 8–32%, cumulative risk at age 70 years)) in non-probands [<a>13</a>]. The median age to develop thyroid cancer is 42 years [<a>13</a>]. However, several studies have reported cases of thyroid cancer in children or adolescents with few cases diagnosed before the age of 10 years [<a>16</a>, <a>17</a>, <a>21</a>, <a>25</a>, <a>26</a>, <a>28</a>, <a>29</a>].</p><p>Ultrasound should be used for surveillance for early detection of cancer of benign thyroid disease, which is also common in PHTS. Thyroid cancer in children with PHTS has in general a favourable prognosis, and the evidence that early detection of thyroid cancer is associated with improved survival is weak. Furthermore, surveillance is associated with a risk of overdiagnosis of small, clinically indolent, follicular or papillary thyroid cancers [<a>21</a>]. Therefore, the appropriate age to initiate surveillance in children with PHTS remains uncertain. Surveillance in asymptomatic children under the age of 10 years is not recommended due to a low risk of both cancer and benign lesions in this age group [<a>16</a>, <a>21</a>]. We therefore advise to start before the age of 18 years, but not before the age of 10 years. Two studies suggest that follow-up frequency can be reduced if there are no suspicious findings in the exams [<a>18</a>, <a>27</a>].</p><h3>Endometrial cancer recommendations (Table <a>3</a>, rec 10–13 [<a>13</a>, <a>23</a>, <a>29</a>, <a>30</a>])</h3><p>The risk of endometrial cancer in female carriers of germline pathogenic <i>PTEN</i> variants is increased compared to the general population. Two larger studies that include index patients have shown a cumulative risk of 28% and 48% [<a>11</a>, <a>30</a>]. The latter study had an age range of diagnosis of 21–78 years (median 49 years) and the former showed an increase in risk from 35 years. In the one study that excluded index cases to reduce ascertainment bias, the cumulative lifetime risk of endometrial cancer by age 70 years was 33% (based on 195 non-index females). The median age of diagnosis was 49 years also in this study, with a 2% cumulative risk at 40 years and a 9% risk at 50 years [<a>13</a>]. While the age-specific risk estimates remain uncertain due to small sample sizes, the increase in risk seems to start from 35 years so we suggest that surveillance starts at 35–40 years, which is five years later than the American [<a>40</a>], international [<a>41</a>], Japanese [<a>42</a>] and Danish [<a>43</a>] recommendations.</p><p>Surveillance of endometrial cancer in Lynch syndrome, a more common hereditary tumour risk syndrome where this question has been studied in more detail, has been controversial. Even without surveillance, most cases of endometrial cancer are diagnosed at an early stage due to abnormal uterine bleeding, survival is already high, and surveillance has not demonstrated a survival advantage [<a>44</a>]. Multiple small studies on <i>PTEN</i> and Lynch syndrome carriers have shown that endometrial surveillance with transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS) in combination with endometrial biopsy (EB) can detect hyperplasia/early cancer prior to bleeding symptoms [<a>23</a>, <a>45</a>, <a>46</a>]. However, EB is painful and may lead to discontinuation of surveillance. A recent retrospective study from the Netherlands with 1046 female Lynch syndrome carriers has shown that endometrial surveillance with TVUS and EB can detect more cancers, but there was no difference in stage or survival between the surveillance and non-surveillance group [<a>47</a>]. It remains unclear if a risk-reducing hysterectomy is warranted given the early detection of endometrial cancer and the excellent prognosis in most cases. Therefore, international guidelines do not recommend routine hysterectomy in PHTS [<a>40</a>,<a>41</a>,<a>42</a>,<a>43</a>]. Thus, we recognise that hysterectomy is an option that can be discussed as part of the individualised follow-up and may be warranted in individual cases including those with multiple uterine polyps and/or notable family history of uterine cancer.</p><p>In conclusion, it is important that all women with germline pathogenic <i>PTEN</i> variants are educated on early signs of endometrial cancer. Surveillance options and the potential possibility of risk-reducing hysterectomy should be discussed on an individual basis and should be offered from 35-40 years of age. There are no studies demonstrating that routine TVUS surveillance improves outcomes, routine TVUS surveillance may be offered by some expert centres and this should ideally be done as a clinical trial.</p><h3>Renal cancer recommendation (Table <a>3</a>, rec 14 [<a>13</a>, <a>29</a>, <a>30</a>])</h3><p>Renal cancers types are varied with a preponderance of papillary subtypes (albeit based on small numbers) [<a>48</a>]. There is a moderate increase in renal cell carcinoma (RCC) in individuals with PHTS. Based on series of 455 individuals, 372 of whom had prospective follow-up for a median of 6 years, the cumulative risk of renal cancer ranged from 3% (95% CI, 1–11%), excluding cancer-index patients) to 9% (95% CI, 3–22%) including cancer-index patients by 70 years [<a>13</a>]. The median age to develop RCC was 51 years, while the youngest reported case in Hendricks et al. was 33 years [<a>13</a>]. A second prospective longitudinal cohort study of 701 individuals estimated 18–42% risk by age 80 years [<a>30</a>], however this study included individuals with variants of uncertain significance and, importantly, did not correct for ascertainment, which likely explains the large difference in estimates between the two studies.</p><p>In general, early identification of RCCs can lead to better outcomes [<a>49</a>] and allows for less invasive therapeutic options, e.g. partial nephrectomy and ablative therapies [<a>50</a>]. The use of MRI or ultrasound are appropriate modalities for the surveillance of RCCs [<a>51</a>, <a>52</a>]. This considered, there is an ongoing stage shift in renal cancer with an increasingly large proportion of patients being diagnosed with low-stage disease, i.e. with small tumours, regardless of genetic predisposition. Currently, it is unknown whether early detection through surveillance offers specific benefits for renal cancer in individuals with PHTS, but there is no evidence indicating that RCCs in PHTS behave differently to sporadic RCCs. Our recommendation is (in the absence of other risk factors) to offer a baseline scan at 35–40 years rather than regular surveillance for renal cancer. We would recommend the use of MRI as modality for the baseline imaging as there is some evidence that MRI is more sensitive for papillary subtypes [<a>51</a>].</p><h3>Colorectal cancer recommendations (Table <a>3</a>, rec 15–16 [<a>13</a>, <a>19</a>, <a>22</a>, <a>24</a>, <a>29</a>, <a>30</a>])</h3><p>Recent evidence confirms that individuals with PHTS have a moderately increased CRC risk, with risk level depending on colonic polyp burden. In a large cohort study, Hendricks et al. estimated the cumulative CRC risk at 5–15% by age 70 years, while the combined risk of CRC and adenomatous polyps was about tenfold higher than in the general population [<a>13</a>]. The median age to develop CRC was 59 years [<a>13</a>]. Similar findings were reported by Yehia et al. in a cohort of 701 individuals, with CRC observed from age 21 years onwards [<a>30</a>].</p><p>Smaller studies (e.g. [<a>19</a>, <a>22</a>, <a>24</a>]) underline early and frequent polyp formation in PHTS. Data from Nieuwenhuis et al. and Bubien et al. support risk stratification based on histological and quantitative polyp characteristics [<a>2</a>, <a>12</a>].</p><p>Based on this, a baseline colonoscopy between the ages of 35 and 40 years is recommended, preferably to be performed in a centre with recognised expertise in PHTS or hereditary colorectal syndromes (e.g., healthcare providers participating in ERN GENTURIS where available). Surveillance intervals should be risk-adapted based on polyp number, size, histology, and degree of dysplasia. Clinically relevant polyps with malignant potential include adenomas, serrated lesions, juvenile-type polyps with dysplasia, and mixed/hybrid lesions; these should be completely removed. Isolated benign hamartomatous lesions without dysplasia (e.g., few small ganglioneuromatous polyps) do not mandate intensified surveillance beyond risk-stratified intervals. Faecal immunochemical testing (FIT) is not recommended as standalone surveillance in PHTS but may complement national population screening programmes in carefully selected low-risk cases (no polyps with malignant potential). In individuals with a family history of early-onset CRC, earlier initiation may be appropriate. In cases with only hamartomatous or no polyps, general population CRC surveillance intervals may be applied, with the option to repeat colonoscopy after 5–10 years.</p><h3>Skin melanoma recommendation (Table <a>3</a>, rec 17 [<a>13</a>, <a>30</a>])</h3><p>Although dermatological manifestations occur in almost all individuals with PHTS, the vast majority are benign lesions (hamartomas or other types such as trichilemmomas or papillomatous lesions). There is some evidence that PHTS is also associated with an increased risk of melanoma [<a>2</a>, <a>11</a>, <a>13</a>, <a>30</a>], but risk figures are conflicting. One observational study on 546 individuals reported a risk excess, as compared with the general population (SIR), of 39 in males and 28 in females [<a>2</a>], while other studies yielded lower estimates, with SIR ranging from 6.6 to 10.6 [<a>11</a>, <a>13</a>, <a>30</a>], leading to cumulative lifetime risks ranging from 6% up to age 83 years [<a>11</a>] to 12% up to age 70 years [<a>13</a>]. Hendricks et al. reported a median age of 30 years to develop melanoma [<a>13</a>]. The level of the risk, together with the absence of evidence supporting a benefit of surveillance for every individual with PHTS, led us to recommend a baseline dermatological evaluation at the age of 30 years, with subsequent management based on individual risk assessment (including recognised factors such as phototype, presence of congenital or dysplastic naevi and/or other lesions, family history of melanoma, etc.), similar to the general population. In general, when counselling individuals (particularly those deemed to be at increased melanoma risk), awareness should be raised of the risk associated with UV exposure, referring to the WHO recommendations to avoid excessive exposure and to use sunscreen and protective clothing.</p></div></div></section><section><div><h2>Discussion</h2><div><p>To improve the overall survival of patients at high hereditary cancer risk surveillance for early cancer detection and cancer prevention by removing defined precancerous lesions are crucial. Therefore, this ERN GENTURIS guideline on cancer surveillance for individuals with PHTS was developed from the best available evidence and the consensus of experts with input from patients and a patient advocate group. The ERN GENTURIS guidelines is an update of the previous guideline published in 2020 [<a>15</a>] as considerable new evidence on cancers risks and effectiveness of our 2020 surveillance recommendations has been published (Supplementary Table <a>1</a> provides a comparison of the 2020 and current recommendations). The current guideline has many similarities with the 2025 international PHTS guideline [<a>41</a>]. There are differences between the two guidelines as a result of a longer period of literature search, with new evidence included in the current recommendations, and the need to adapt guidelines to the European healthcare systems in which cost-effectiveness and access to affordable health care for every citizen is prioritised (Supplementary Table <a>2</a>).</p><p>The goal of cancer surveillance is to detect cancer at an earlier stage than symptomatic presentation, when interventions have a better chance of being curative. It is also important to remember that individuals with PHTS are at risk of multiple cancers over their lifetime and surveillance for second cancers should not be overlooked. The here proposed cancer surveillance recommendations for PHTS require significant patient commitment as well as a coordinated multidisciplinary medical approach.</p><p>The recommendations are specifically made for those individuals with a gPV in <i>PTEN</i>. For individuals that meet the clinical diagnostic criteria for PHTS, but where no gPV in <i>PTEN</i> has been identified, surveillance should be tailored on a case-by-case basis, taking into account the personal and family history of cancer, as their cancer risk is less certain and to avoid unnecessary surveillance.</p><p>The evidence base for cancer surveillance in this guideline are growing as is the quality of the evidence regarding cancer risks. However, this evidence is from non-randomised studies and still based on relatively small numbers. A better understanding of the age-related penetrance, specifically in the age group over 60 years, is critical to improve risk counselling and risk-based recommendations for cancer prevention. We therefore recommend that national, ERN GENTURIS [<a>53</a>] and international registries are used to collect prospective data on PHTS individuals undergoing surveillance. Such collaboration is also essential to gain more data and knowledge on for instance risk reducing surgery, cancer treatment results, psychosocial functioning, new surveillance modalities and new cancer biomarkers in PHTS patients.</p><p>Patient education is a critical component of effective cancer surveillance. This relates to both prevention (healthy living and avoidance of cancer-causing behaviours) and early detection (awareness of red flag symptoms for the key cancers, e.g. postmenopausal bleeding in endometrial cancer). Patient information groups can assist greatly with these aspects and are a powerful resource for individuals with PHTS. When discussing a surveillance plan with a patient it is important that psychological aspects are taken into account given the increased prevalence of neurocognitive issues in PHTS.</p><p>Despite an expanding published evidence base since the 2020 ERN GENTURIS guideline, data supporting cancer surveillance in PHTS remain limited, particularly for renal, colorectal, skin, and endometrial cancers. Prospective national and international registries, such as the GENTURIS registry, are essential to improve understanding of age-related cancer penetrance, refine personalised risk estimates, and evaluate the effectiveness of surveillance strategies. Further research is needed to define genotype–phenotype correlations, assess cancer treatment outcomes, evaluate risk-reducing breast surgery, and develop tailored management approaches for individuals with PHTS. International collaboration will be critical to advance research into novel surveillance technologies (including liquid biopsies), drug prevention trials and to generate the evidence needed for future guideline development.</p><p>It is essential to prospectively evaluate the effectiveness of our recommendations of cancer surveillance in the PHTS population and therefore to foster global collaborations with data sharing to enhance clinical care and research opportunities for this group of high-risk individuals. We do hope that these recommendations will be adapted and implemented in every nation of Europe and beyond.</p></div></div></section>
