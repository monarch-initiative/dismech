# Comprehensive needle and syringe program and opioid agonist therapy reduce HIV and hepatitis c virus acquisition among people who inject drugs in different settings: A pooled analysis of emulated trials.
**Authors:** van Santen DK, Lodi S, Dietze P, van den Boom W, Hayashi K, Dong H, Cui Z, Maher L, Hickman M, Boyd A, Prins M
**Journal:** Addiction (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1111/add.16147](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16147)

## Content

BACKGROUND AND AIMS: Although the Netherlands, Canada and Australia were early adopters of harm reduction for people who inject drugs (PWID), their respective HIV and hepatitis C (HCV) epidemics differ. We measured the pooled effect of needle and syringe program (NSP) and opioid agonist therapy (OAT) participation on HIV and HCV incidence in these settings.

DESIGN: For each cohort, we emulated the design and statistical analysis of a target trial using observational data.

SETTING AND PARTICIPANTS: We included PWID at risk of HIV or HCV infection from the Amsterdam Cohort Studies (1985-2013), Vancouver Injection Drug Users Study (1997-2009) and Melbourne Injecting Drug User Cohort Study (SuperMIX) (2010-2021).

MEASUREMENTS: Separately for each infection and cohort (only HCV in SuperMIX), marginal structural models were used to compare the effect of comprehensive (on OAT and 100% NSP coverage or on OAT only if no recent injection drug use) versus no/partial NSP/OAT (no OAT and/or <100% NSP coverage) participation. Pooled hazard ratios (HR) and 95% CI were calculated using random-effects meta-analysis.

FINDINGS: We observed 94 HIV seroconversions and 81 HCV seroconversions among 2023 and 430 participants, respectively. Comprehensive NSP/OAT led to a 41% lower risk of HIV acquisition (pooled HR = 0.59, 95% CI = 0.36-0.96) and a 76% lower risk of HCV acquisition (pooled HR = 0.24, 95% CI = 0.11-0.51), compared with no/partial NSP/OAT, with little heterogeneity between studies for both infections (I2  = 0%).

CONCLUSIONS: In the Netherlands, Canada and Australia, comprehensive needle and syringe program and opioid agonist therapy participation appears to substantially reduce HIV and hepatitis C acquisition compared with no or partial needle and syringe program/opioid agonist therapy participation. These findings from an emulated trial design reinforce the critical role of comprehensive access to harm reduction in optimizing infection prevention for people who inject drugs.

In 2019, most of the burden of disease because of drug use was attributed to opioid use [1]. The use of opioids is known to be associated with injecting drug use or transitioning to injection drug use, which increases the risk of HIV and hepatitis C virus (HCV) acquisition [2]. Of the estimated 11.2 million people who inject drugs (PWID) globally in 2019, 1.4 and 5.6 million have acquired HIV and HCV, respectively [1].

Harm reduction programs (HRP) are the cornerstone for prevention and care among PWID [3]. Core components of HRP include needle and syringe programs (NSP) and opioid agonist therapy (OAT). To date, there have only been observational studies in which each of these components, particularly their combination, has been associated with reductions in HIV and/or HCV infection risk [4–6]. The traditional criteria for grading quality of evidence in systematic reviews automatically assess evidence from observational studies as low, and therefore, evidence for the effect of comprehensive NSP and OAT participation has been deemed to be of low quality [4, 7]. Despite this ‘low quality’ evidence, the World Health Organization (WHO) strongly recommends a comprehensive package of HRP to reduce transmission of HIV, HCV and hepatitis B virus (HBV) among PWID [8]. Global coverage of these programs remains nonetheless suboptimal [3].

A randomized controlled trial (RCT) could potentially strengthen the evidence on the effect of comprehensive NSP/OAT, but given that the benefits of HRP across a range of outcomes are historically and widely recognized, it would be unethical to include an arm without HRP [9, 10]. In the absence of an RCT, data from observational studies can be used to emulate a target trial. We previously estimated the effect of these interventions on the risk of HIV, HCV and HBV infection using an emulated target trial design aligning our observational data from the Amsterdam Cohort Studies (ACS) to an ideal RCT [11]. We found that comprehensive NSP/OAT participation in Amsterdam was associated with an 84% reduction in HCV acquisition and a 45% reduction in HIV acquisition compared to no or partial participation. It is unknown whether NSP and OAT are as effective in reducing HIV and HCV acquisition in settings with different HRP coverage and epidemiological contexts.

The Netherlands, Canada and Australia were early adopters of OAT and NSP [12–14]. However, other components of HRP, such as drug consumption rooms (DCRs), were available in the Netherlands much earlier than in Canada and Australia [12, 15, 16]. In 2017, coverage of both OAT and NSP was high among PWID in Australia and the Netherlands, whereas moderate NSP and OAT coverage was observed in Canada [3]. Despite some similarities in the history of access to HRP, the demographic characteristics of PWID and local HIV and HCV epidemics differ significantly between these settings. In the Netherlands, injection drug use and overdose mortality have decreased substantially over the past three decades, and HIV and HCV transmission among PWID has been largely stopped [11, 17, 18]. In contrast, the estimated population prevalence of PWID in Canada increased by 27% between 2011 and 2016 [19]. Since 2012, deaths from drug toxicity have significantly increased in Canada primarily because of the unregulated drug supply of potent illicitly manufactured synthetic opioids (e.g. fentanyl), leading the Canadian province of British Columbia to declare a public health emergency in 2016 [20]. Although HIV incidence has decreased among PWID in British Columbia during the last two decades, reported HCV cases are disproportionately higher than in most other provinces with no evidence of a decline [15, 21]. In Australia, HIV incidence among PWID is low and stable [22] since the start of the HIV epidemic, and although there is some evidence of declining HCV incidence among PWID, transmission is ongoing [14, 23]. However, overdose mortality in Australia has increased in the past decade [24, 25].

It is important to determine whether the effect of NSP/OAT is consistent and generalizable across settings. We hypothesized that if PWID had access to these programs, a significant reduction of HIV and HCV risk would be observed in any setting. Using a target trial approach, we aimed to determine the effect of comprehensive NSP/OAT participation on HIV and HCV infection risk using observational cohort data from Vancouver, Amsterdam and Melbourne.

To assess the pooled effect of NSP/OAT on HIV and HCV acquisition, we included data from three long-standing cohorts in different geographical settings (selection of these cohorts is described in the Supporting Information, Section 1.1). We analysed data from the ACS (1985–2013), the Vancouver Injection Drug Users Study (VIDUS) (1997–2009) and the Melbourne Injecting Drug User Cohort Study (MIX/SuperMIX) (2010–2021) (Table 1). Each cohort has been previously described [11, 13, 26]. Briefly, in all cohorts, participants were followed at regularly scheduled intervals (Table 1), and self-reported data on NSP/OAT participation and injecting behaviours were collected. Additional details can be found in the Supporting Information, Section 1.1.

Participants were recruited at methadone outposts, at a sexually transmitted infection outpatient clinic for sex workers who used drugs, and by word of mouth. Inclusion criteria included injecting or use of hard drugs defined as regular use of heroin, cocaine, amphetamines and/or methadone. The cohort remained open for enrolment until 2013 and closed in 2016. HIV testing was performed prospectively at each cohort visit, and HCV testing retrospectively until 2005 and prospectively thereafter [27].

Participants were recruited through self-referral and street outreach. Eligibility criteria include being ≥18 years old, having injected unregulated drugs at least once in the previous month, and residing in the Greater Vancouver region [13]. Since December 2005, VIDUS only recruits HIV-negative participants. HIV and HCV testing was performed prospectively at each visit [28].

Participants were recruited through a combination of respondent-driven and snowball sampling and street-based outreach [26] having injected heroin and/or amphetamines at least once a month in the 6 months before baseline. From 2009, blood samples were collected annually and tested prospectively for HIV and HCV [26].

We emulated the study design and statistical analysis of the same target trial for each cohort. Additional details of the methodology can be found in the Supportin information, Section 1 and elsewhere [11]. We summarize each component below. We did not pre-register the study before analysis.

Individuals were eligible for inclusion if they reported an injection drug use episode in the 2-year period before baseline, had used opioids, were ≥18 years old and had a negative antibody result for HIV or HCV. We define baseline as the first study when all inclusion criteria were met and complete data was available. Individuals had to have at least two HIV or HCV test results obtained during at least two cohort visits. Given the stable low HIV incidence among PWID in Australia [22], we did not perform an analysis for HIV risk using SuperMIX data.

Outcomes were HIV and HCV seroconversion. The date of seroconversion was estimated as the midpoint date between the last antibody negative test date and the first positive test date (positive RNA and/or antibodies for HCV).

We defined the intervention strategies based on self-reported level of NSP coverage, a recent injection drug use episode (i.e. current IDU) and daily prescribed use of OAT (i.e. current OAT, >0 mg) (Figure S1). In SuperMIX, NSP coverage was based on a method to calculate individual-level syringe coverage including syringes acquired, syringe distribution and frequency of injecting [29]. In VIDUS and ACS, NSP coverage was based on self-reported level of needle/syringe acquisition from an NSP (Table S2). When individuals reported current OAT, we assumed that they received OAT daily and were compliant between visits.

No/partial participation: <100% NSP coverage and/or no current OAT among participants reporting current IDU.

Comprehensive participation: 100% coverage of NSP and current OAT among participants reporting current IDU or current OAT among participants reporting no current IDU.

Individuals reporting both comprehensive NSP/OAT and borrowing used syringes/needles (i.e. receptive needle/syringe sharing) were reclassified as exposed to the no/partial strategy assuming that NSP coverage was below 100%.

We designed the study to emulate a sequence of trials that could start within a calendar year and have a maximum duration of 2.5 years for each ‘trial.’ During each trial, individuals were followed from the first visit at which they met all the eligibility criteria (i.e. baseline). PWID were classified as participating in either intervention strategy at the baseline visit. Follow-up was censored from the visit at which the participant deviated from their initial baseline intervention strategy (i.e. nonadherence), incident infection, loss to follow-up, administrative censoring date (Table 1) or 2.5 years after baseline, whichever occurred first. Stopping both injecting and OAT use was considered as remaining adherent, regardless of intervention. Lost to follow-up was defined as having no study visit for more than 2.5 years. Participants could be reincluded after a censoring event if they met inclusion criteria again and had not experienced the outcome.

Using a directed-cyclic graph approach (Figure S2), baseline confounders were time since first injecting, age at first injecting and sex. Time-varying confounders were frequency of injecting and having a steady PWID partner (i.e. having a stable relationship with an individual who currently or has ever injected drugs) [30–35]. For VIDUS, we also included living in Downtown Eastside Vancouver as a time-varying confounder (additional details in the Supporting Information, Section 1.4).

We used marginal structural models to estimate the effect of comprehensive versus no/partial NSP/OAT on HIV and HCV separately for each cohort, pooling all ‘trials’ into a single analysis. Under the assumptions of exchangeability, consistency and positivity, observational data can be conceptualized as a conditionally randomized experiment and effect estimates can be interpreted as causal (model and assumption details in the Supporting Information, Section 1.6). To account for the potential inclusion of individuals in multiple trials of the same cohort, we calculated 95% CI using the 2.5 percentile and 97.5 percentile from bootstrapped variance estimations of 1000 samples with replacement within cohorts. Pooled hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were calculated using random-effects meta-analysis in which the study log (HR)s were modelled with the inverse variance method and the DerSimonian–Laird estimator [36]. These estimates were obtained using the meta package in R [37]. Between-study heterogeneity was evaluated using the I2 statistic [38].

As we accounted for changes in intervention status throughout follow-up and censored individuals, this approach corresponds to a per-protocol analysis of the target trial [39].

Unstable housing has been shown to be associated with higher HIV and HCV risk acquisition as well as access to care, including HRP [40]. We performed an analysis including homelessness (yes/no) as a confounder using the data from VIDUS, because information on this variable was limited in the ACS (available since 1989 onward) and a relatively small overall sample size in SuperMIX. Moreover, we computed E-values for the point estimates and its 95% CI for each cohort to assess the impact of unmeasured confounding [41]. Second, we performed an analysis including the same set of confounders that were available in all cohorts (i.e. sex, duration since first injection and frequency of injection). Last, we performed an analysis where any recent NSP participation, rather than coverage of NSP, was used to define the intervention strategies.

Analyses were conducted using Stata (v15.1), SAS (v9.4, SAS Institute) and R (v4.1.1).

Of 1661 ACS, 2534 VIDUS and 1328 SuperMIX participants ever included in each cohort, 983 (56.2%), 2173 (86.3%) and 673 (50.6%) had at least two cohort visits and had ever injected drugs, respectively (Table 1). Median follow-up in the cohort was 12.3 years (interquartile range [IQR] = 5.6–20.2) among 983 PWID in ACS, 7.7 (IQR = 3.1–11.9) in 2173 PWID in VIDUS and 9.0 (IQR = 3.2–10.6) in 673 PWID in SuperMIX. After applying the eligibility criteria, we observed a total of 94 HIV seroconversions among 2023 participants and 81 HCV seroconversions among 430 participants (Table 2).

When comparing socio-demographic and behavioural characteristics among participants included in the HIV analysis at the first eligible visit (Table 3), median duration of time since first injection was higher in VIDUS (14.7 years old, IQR = 6.7–25.3) than in ACS (6.9 years, IQR = 2.7–13.0). Among those reporting recent injection, a higher proportion of VIDUS participants reported daily injection (64.1%) than ACS (34.8%) participants. Most ACS participants reported OAT use (84.1%) and participating in an NSP program (56.9%), whereas participants in VIDUS more often reported NSP participation (92.0%) than OAT (28.1%).

Among participants included in the HCV analysis at first eligible visit (Table 3), SuperMIX participants had a lower median age at injection initiation (18 years) and a longer duration since first injection (10.0 years) compared to VIDUS (20 years old; 6.1 years since first injection) and ACS (25 years old; 1.0 year since first injection) participants. In SuperMIX and VIDUS, all participants had a history of recent injection at the first eligible visit compared to 81.7% in the ACS. Among those reporting recent injection, daily injection was more frequently reported in VIDUS (61.6%), compared to participants from the ACS (15.8%) and SuperMIX (27.7%). VIDUS participants also reported the highest NSP participation (91.2%), but the lowest OAT (21.3%), compared to ACS (36.7% NSP and 74.2% OAT) and SuperMIX (89.8% NSP and 59.6% OAT).

Compared to no/partial NSP/OAT, comprehensive NSP/OAT participation led to a 47% decrease in risk of HIV acquisition among ACS (HR = 0.53, 95% CI = 0.30–0.92, P = 0.023) and a 10% decrease in risk in VIDUS participants (HR = 0.90, 95% CI = 0.29–2.72, P = 0.846) (Figure S3: inverse probability weights). When combined across cohorts, comprehensive NSP/OAT led to a 41% lower risk of HIV acquisition (pooled HR = 0.59, 95% CI = 0.36–0.96) compared with no/partial NSP/OAT (Figure 1), with little heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 0%).

Compared to no/partial NSP/OAT, comprehensive NSP/OAT participation led to a 78% decrease in risk of HCV acquisition among ACS participants (HR = 0.22, 95% CI = 0.08–0.67, P = 0.008), 92% decreased risk in VIDUS (HR = 0.08, 95% CI = 0.01–0.60, P = 0.014) and 59% decreased risk in SuperMIX (HR = 0.41, 95% CI = 0.11–1.55, P = 0.187) (Figure S4: inverse probability weights). When combined across cohorts, comprehensive NSP/OAT led to a 76% lower risk of HCV acquisition (pooled HR = 0.24, 95% CI = 0.11–0.51), compared with no/partial NSP/OAT (Figure 1), with little heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 0%).

Sensitivity analyses did not alter conclusions from the main analyses (Supporting Information, Section 3). E-value analysis showed that observed most HRs were unlikely to be influenced by potential uncontrolled confounders, with the lowest E-value at 1.5 for the HIV analysis in VIDUS and 4.3 for the HCV analysis in SuperMIX (Supporting Information, Section 3.4).

Our emulated target trials using observational data from cohorts in high-income countries demonstrated that comprehensive participation in NSP and OAT led to a 41% lower risk of HIV acquisition and a 76% lower risk of HCV acquisition, compared with no/partial NSP/OAT participation.

A protective effect of NSP/OAT on HIV risk was observed across both cohorts, although CIs were wider in VIDUS than in ACS. This difference in variation is likely because of fewer HIV seroconversions observed relative to the sample size and follow-up in VIDUS compared to ACS. The study period in the ACS included data from the era before combination antiretroviral therapy (cART), with most events occurring during that period (i.e. before 1996), whereas in VIDUS, all data were collected and analysed during the cART era (since 1996). Uncertainty in this effect would arise from increased cART uptake in this population, which has led to declining community HIV viral load [42]. Similar to HIV, a protective effect of NSP/OAT on HCV risk was observed in all three cohorts. Across studies, the 95% CIs were widest in VIDUS and narrowest in the ACS. This may also be partly explained by differences between studies in the number of events, sample sizes and followup periods. For example, SuperMIX included data in the period both before and after the widespread availability of HCV direct-acting antivirals, which have been shown to reduce HCV viremic prevalence and, potentially, incidence [43]. The stronger effect of NSP/OAT observed for HCV compared to HIV may derive from the fact that HCV is primarily transmitted via injecting practices in this population, whereas condomless sex is another likely transmission route for HIV.

In our initial emulated trial using only ACS data, we included OAT dosing as part of the definition for the intervention strategy in which a dose of ≥60 mg methadone per day was considered adequate [11]. Although in the current analyses we considered any OAT dosing adequate to align data across cohorts, results from the current analysis were similar to the initial ACS target trail emulation. The lack of differences in results would suggest that the majority of PWID were likely receiving adequate doses. Nevertheless, adequate dosing remains an important element of OAT to prevent infection, consistent with a previous study reporting a dose–response relationship between OAT dose and HCV risk [44].

It is encouraging that all effect estimates point toward a strong protective effect of comprehensive NSP/OAT participation in reducing infection risk across all three settings and that between-study heterogeneity for the pooled estimates was negligible. It is noteworthy that HCV and HIV transmission is ongoing in Vancouver and Melbourne, albeit at lower levels compared to 1980s to 1990s, whereas barely any new HIV or HCV infections have been observed among PWID in Amsterdam [11]. Timely availability of a range of lowthreshold HRP in Amsterdam, including NSP, OAT and DRCs (not measured in the current study) at the height of the heroin crisis, may have led to a historically more rapid and higher HRP coverage than in other countries, contributing to earlier interruption of transmission chains among PWID [12, 16, 45–47]. For example, it was not until after 2000 that infrastructure for harm reduction services were considerably expanded and NSP were decentralized in Vancouver [15]. Moreover, as early as the 1970s, a drug tolerance approach and a strong collaboration between public health officials and the local police were established in the Netherlands. Individuals using and/or possessing small amounts of drugs are unlikely to have been prosecuted in the Netherlands, whereas targeting of people who use drugs for small scale drug offenses was still occurring in Australia and Vancouver at the time of the study [45]. These differences may have led to higher rates of incarceration of PWID with limited HRP in prisons globally [48], and prison has also been associated with a 81% and 62% increased risk of HIV and HCV acquisition, respectively [49]. Selective HIV-related mortality, particularly in the pre-cART era, among those at highest risk of HIV and HCV infection in Amsterdam compared to Melbourne (where HIV was never prominent in this population) may have decreased the number of PWIDs able to transmit these viruses to others. Moreover, changes in population demographics (e.g. aging PWID population), drug markets, drug availability and demand leading to a decreasing PIWD population have likely contributed to halting transmission in Amsterdam [17]. Importantly, polysubstance/stimulant use/injection and particularly the rise of methamphetamine use among PWID in Vancouver and Melbourne may be contributing to ongoing transmission of HIV and HCV [50, 51], whereas this is not the case in the Netherlands [52]. Preventing these infections among people injecting stimulants remains a public health challenge given that there are limited evidence-based treatment options for stimulant dependency and no agonist therapy as for opioids.

Our study has several limitations. First, we cannot exclude residual confounding, and some prespecified confounders were missing or operationalized differently across cohorts. It is reassuring that including a critical set of the same confounders across cohorts obtained similar pooled effect estimates. When we included homelessness as a confounder in the VIDUS HIV analysis, the effect of comprehensive compared to no/partial NSP/OAT participation became slightly stronger and 95% CI became narrower, although overlapping with the main analysis. Moreover, based on the E-values, the association between homelessness or any other potential confounder, and both NSP/OAT participation and infection risk would need to be strong to explain away the observed effects, particularly for HCV. NSP coverage, one of the two main components of the intervention, was collected differently in SuperMIX than in VIDUS and ACS; using an alternative definition with NSP participation rather than NSP coverage resulted in similar pooled effect estimates as the main analyses, but differed significantly for the HCV analysis in VIDUS. Hence, any effect of misclassifying the HRP strategy would appear minimal for the pooled estimates. We lack data on history or duration of NSP/OAT participation before baseline and assume that this does not impact infection risk. We defined any opioid use as an eligibility criterion, whereas OAT is only indicated for individuals with an opioid use disorder (i.e. abuse or harmful use of opioids or dependence) [2]. Therefore, we may have misclassified PWID who do not inject opioids regularly as eligible. We were unable to assess different levels of exposure to NSP/OAT, including a no NSP/OAT strategy separate from partial participation, because of small sample sizes and high coverage of NSP and OAT in our three sites. Last, we only analysed data from high-income countries with universal access to health care, and, therefore, it is unclear whether our results are generalizable to other countries. This highlights the need to strengthen evidence using emulated trials in low- and middle-income countries or settings with lower levels of HRP coverage to understand whether comprehensive NSP/OAT is equally effective in reducing infection risk and to disentangle the effect of partial versus no exposure to these programs. Our study nonetheless exemplifies how observational data provide a critical resource to strengthen evidence for policy recommendations in settings with contrasting epidemiological contexts.

When aligning the interventions, target populations and outcome definitions across cohorts and therefore, applying a target trial approach to infer causality from observational data [39], we demonstrated that comprehensive NSP/OAT led to a substantial reduction of HIV and HCV acquisition compared to no/partial participation in different settings of high-income countries. Coupled with the fact that OAT has been previously associated with reductions in injection drug use, mortality, nonfatal overdoses requiring hospitalization, engagement in care and reductions in criminal activity [2, 53], our findings reinforce the crucial role of access to HRP. Given that most countries lack access to comprehensive NSP/OAT, and global HCV and HIV elimination is unlikely to be reached by 2030 [54, 55], increased coverage of these key interventions will be vital to preventing new infections. Moreover, our findings suggest that WHO targets should emphasize access to comprehensive NSP and OAT rather than each component separately.
