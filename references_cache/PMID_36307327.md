# Cellular Genome-wide Association Study Identifies Common Genetic Variation Influencing Lithium-Induced Neural Progenitor Proliferation.
**Authors:** Wolter JM, Le BD, Matoba N, Lafferty MJ, Aygün N, Liang D, Courtney K, Song J, Piven J, Zylka MJ, Stein JL
**Journal:** Biol Psychiatry (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.biopsych.2022.08.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2022.08.014)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Bipolar disorder is a highly heritable neuropsychiatric condition affecting more than 1% of the human population. Lithium salts are commonly prescribed as a mood stabilizer for individuals with bipolar disorder. Lithium is clinically effective in approximately half of treated individuals, and their genetic backgrounds are known to influence treatment outcomes. While the mechanism of lithium's therapeutic action is unclear, it stimulates adult neural progenitor cell proliferation, similar to some antidepressant drugs.

METHODS: To identify common genetic variants that modulate lithium-induced proliferation, we conducted an EdU incorporation assay in a library of 80 genotyped human neural progenitor cells treated with lithium. These data were used to perform a genome-wide association study to identify common genetic variants that influence lithium-induced neural progenitor cell proliferation. We manipulated the expression of a putatively causal gene using CRISPRi/a (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats interference/activation) constructs to experimentally verify lithium-induced proliferation effects.

RESULTS: We identified a locus on chr3p21.1 associated with lithium-induced proliferation. This locus is also associated with bipolar disorder risk, schizophrenia risk, and interindividual differences in intelligence. We identified a single gene, GNL3, whose expression temporally increased in an allele-specific fashion following lithium treatment. Experimentally increasing the expression of GNL3 led to increased proliferation under baseline conditions, while experimentally decreasing GNL3 expression suppressed lithium-induced proliferation.

CONCLUSIONS: Our experiments reveal that common genetic variation modulates lithium-induced neural progenitor proliferation and that GNL3 expression is necessary for the full proliferation-stimulating effects of lithium. These results suggest that performing genome-wide associations in genetically diverse human cell lines is a useful approach to discover context-specific pharmacogenomic effects.

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a highly heritable neuropsychiatric illness commonly treated with lithium salts (1–4). Lithium treatment is effective at preventing relapse of BD episodes in 40% to 60% of individuals, indicating a high degree of variability in clinical response (5–7). Genetic background influences the risk of BD (8) as well as lithium response in individuals with BD (9–11). These genetic effects are underscored by studies demonstrating lithium responsiveness in induced pluripotent stem cell lines generated from individuals with BD (12–15). Despite recent advances in identifying the genetic loci that underlie BD risk (8), much less is known about the genetic underpinnings of lithium responsiveness (16). This is at least in part because of the difficulty in performing sufficiently powered pharmacogenomic studies in human populations and the diverse context- and cell type–dependent effects of lithium (17). The mechanism underlying lithium’s therapeutic effects is unclear, but an attractive hypothesis is that lithium increases adult neurogenesis by stimulating neural progenitor cell (NPC) proliferation, as has been observed for some antidepressant drugs (18–23). While the presence and timing of adult neurogenesis in the human brain has been questioned (24,25), recent studies using single-cell RNA sequencing in the macaque and human hippocampus have detected adult neural progenitors or immature neurons (26–28). Furthermore, lithium accumulates in neurogenic brain regions such as the hippocampus (29,30), increases the volume of the hippocampus in individuals with BD (31–34), and stimulates adult hippocampal NPC proliferation in rodents (23). Based on this evidence, we designed an experiment to identify common genetic variants that influence lithium-induced NPC proliferation. To do this, we used a library of genetically diverse primary human NPCs previously characterized by genotyping, RNA sequencing, and assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) (35–37).

Human NPC lines in this study originated from prenatal tissue collected following voluntary termination of pregnancy at the University of California Los Angeles Gene and Cell Therapy facility following institutional review board regulations.

Primary human NPCs were obtained from fetal brain tissue assumed to be derived from the dorsal telencephalon based on visual inspection at approximately 14 to 21 gestation weeks, as previously described (35–37). After single-cell dissociation, cells were initially cultured as neurospheres before plating on fibronectin-coated (F1141; Sigma-Aldrich) and poly-L-ornithine–coated (P3655; Sigma-Aldrich) plates, passaged 2 or 3 times, cryopreserved, and transferred to the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. NPC media (Neurobasal A, 10888-022; Life Technologies) was supplemented with 100 μg/mL primocin (ant-pm-2; InvivoGen), 10% BIT 9500 (09500; StemCell Technologies), 1% glutamax (100x, 35050061; Life Technologies), 1 μg/mL heparin (H3393-10KU; Sigma-Aldrich), 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor/fibroblast growth factor (PHG0313/PHG0023; Life Technologies), 2 ng/mL leukemia inhibitory factor (PHC9481; Life Technologies), and 10 ng/mL platelet-derived growth factor (PHG1034; Life Technologies). We followed previously established protocols to maintain NPCs as proliferating neural progenitors and inhibit differentiation into neurons (36). No mycoplasma contamination was detected during regular preassay screens of cell culture media (30-1012K; ATCC).

Here, we describe the general approach for the lithium exposure performed in all experiments. Lithium chloride (LiCl) (203637; Sigma-Aldrich) was diluted in water to 3M and stored in single-use aliquots at −80 °C. NPCs were plated at densities dependent on the culture plate size as described below for each experiment. The following day, LiCl was diluted in NPC media without growth factors to 10× concentration in 10% of the total volume media in each well. Control wells (vehicle) contained a volume of water equal to the amount of 3M LiCl required to obtain 10× concentration.

We thawed cryopreserved NPCs in batches of approximately 8 to 10 NPC donor lines, which were pseudorandomized for biological and technical variables (sex, gestation week, and passage). We included at least 1 technical replicate (distinct cryovials of the same NPC donor line thawed and assayed multiple times) in each batch to assess technical reproducibility (29 unique NPC donor lines replicated in duplicate) (Figure S3H in Supplement 1). Each batch was cultured with weekly passaging for 2 weeks to allow for recovery from thawing. On week 3, cells were lifted using 0.05% trypsin (25300062; Gibco), and 12,500 cells per well were plated in each well of 96-well plates (3610; Corning). Twenty-four hours later, either LiCl (0.75, 1.5, or 3 mM) or vehicle (water) was added as described previously. After 46 hours, cells were treated with 10 μM EdU with a 10% media addition and incubated for 2 hours. Cells were then lifted off the plate using 0.05% trypsin (25300062; Gibco) and transferred to a U-bottom 96-well plate. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline for 10 minutes. EdU labeling was performed using the Click-iT EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (C10337; Thermo Fisher Scientific) per manufacturer’s protocol. Total DNA content was labeled with the FxCycle Far Red dye (F10347; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell suspensions were quantified using the Attune NxT 96-well Flow Cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For each LiCl concentration, 4 wells per NPC donor line were quantified. For vehicle treatment (water), 8 wells per NPC donor line were acquired. Replicates were spatially distributed across each plate to avoid positional bias. All liquid handling steps, including the additions of LiCl, EdU, trypsin, fixation, and the Click-iT reaction, were performed using the Evo liquid handling robot (Tecan) to reduce handling variability.

Flow Cytometry Standard (FCS) output files from the Attune NxT were initially analyzed using FlowJo, with SSC used to retain only singlets (Figure S2A, B in Supplement 1). To perform automated gating of stages of the cell cycle, we combined technical replicates into a single FCS file, and bounds were drawn for G0/G1, S-phase, and G2/M using the automated gating software FlowDensity (38) with 3 non-elliptical gates (Figure S2C in Supplement 1). These gates were then applied to each well independently. Technical replicates were averaged to obtain the percentage of cells in the S-phase for each NPC donor line in each condition. Wells that failed for technical reasons (such as a bubble in the flow cytometer or no EdU labeling due to a failed Click-iT reaction) were identified by calculating the coefficient of variation followed by manual inspection of FCS files for all conditions where the coefficient of variation was >0.1. This filtering removed approximately 0.5% of wells. For donor lines that were assayed in multiple rounds, we averaged all replicates together for genetic associations.

The percentage of cells in the S-phase for each experimental condition for each NPC donor line was calculated using the average across all wells. ΔS-phase was calculated by subtracting the percentage of cells in S-phase in vehicle condition from the percentage of cells in the S-phase for each concentration of LiCl. We used a linear mixed-effects model to conduct genetic association tests implemented with the EMMAX software (39). In this model, we included an m × m Balding Nichols kinship matrix inferred from the NPC genotypes (K) (40) as a random effect variable to control for effects of cryptic relatedness and 10 multidimensional scaling genotype components as covariates to mitigate confounding effects of the population structure. While we did not find that sex or gestation week correlated with proliferation phenotypes (Figure S3J–L in Supplement 1), we included sex and gestation week as standard technical covariates in the model.

Guide RNAs (gRNA) were selected using the GPP sgRNA Designer (Broad Institute) and cloned into CRISPRi (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats interference) (pLV hU6-sgRNA hUbC-dCas9-KRAB-T2a-GFP [71237; Addgene]) or CRISPRa (CRISPR activation) (pLV hU6-gRNA [antisense] hUbC-VP64-dCas9-VP64-T2A-GFP [66707; Addgene]) plasmids, which were kind gifts from the laboratory of Charles Gersbach. See Supplement 1 for lentivirus production. An NPC donor line heterozygous for rs11709284 was plated at 200,000 cells per well in 12-well plates. Seventy-five microliters of each viral prep was delivered to each well. Cells were incubated for 96 hours. RNA extraction, complementary DNA synthesis, and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) were performed as described in Supplement 1. The consistency of the lentivirus infection and dCas9 expression was verified using qPCR for dCas9. The negative control gRNA is a random sequence that does not align with the human genome.

Materials and methods for RNA extraction and qPCR, NPC line selection for genome-wide association study (GWAS) and quality control, quantifying proliferation via EdU incorporation and high content imaging, lentivirus production, Wnt signaling luciferase assays, GWAS-wide significance threshold, GWAS colocalization, cross-trait enrichment analysis, and allele-specific expression are described in Supplement 1.

To identify genetic variants that modulate lithium-induced proliferation, we used a library of fetal-derived, genetically diverse primary human NPC lines. Genetic effects on adult NPC proliferation may be observable in fetal-derived NPCs because embryonic and adult neural stem cells exhibit similar gene expression profiles, signaling pathways, morphology, and cellular differentiation behaviors (41–44). We measured proliferation in these cells in the presence or absence of lithium using an EdU incorporation assay, which fluorescently labels cells in the S-phase of the cell cycle. Following a 2-hour EdU pulse, cells were fixed and dual labeled with a fluorescent DNA content dye and EdU, and the number of cells within each phase of the cell cycle was quantified using flow cytometry (Figure 1A). We found that lithium increased NPC proliferation at relatively low concentrations (≤2.5 mM) and decreased proliferation at higher concentrations (≥5 mM) (Figure S1A, B in Supplement 1). Lithium has been shown to induce proliferation by activating Wnt signaling through competitive inhibition of GSK3B (45). However, we did not observe activated Wnt signaling at proliferation-inducing concentrations using either a beta catenin–activated luciferase reporter assay or a qPCR measuring endogenous Wnt target gene expression (Figure S1C–E In Supplement 1). GSK3B also directly phosphorylates many transcription factors, which in turn activate signaling pathways such as CREB (cAMP response element–binding protein) and NFAT (nuclear factor of activated T cells) (46). We found that lithium activated the expression of target genes of the NFAT signaling pathway at proliferation-inducing concentrations in NPCs (47) but did not detectably affect the gene expression of Wnt- or CREB pathway–associated genes (Figure S1G–J).

Because the target serum concentration for long-term lithium treatment is 0.6 to 1.2 mM, we focused on concentrations that approximate therapeutically relevant levels (0.75 and 1.5 mM), as well as a higher concentration (3 mM) that we hypothesized might be associated with negative side effects (48). We measured lithium-induced proliferation using the previously described EdU incorporation and flow cytometry assay in a population of 80 genetically diverse NPC donors (Figure 1A; Figures S2, S3A–C and Supplemental Materials and Methods in Supplement 1). NPCs from all donor lines increased proliferation in a concentration-dependent manner at 0.75 and 1.5 mM LiCl, while some NPC donor lines decreased proliferation at 3 mM (Figure 1B). Phenotypic values were normally distributed, and technical replicates (the same donor thawed at different times) were reasonably correlated (Figure S3D–H in Supplement 1). Proliferation rates in vehicle and lithium conditions were strongly correlated (Figure S3I in Supplement 1), which could mask the effect of genetic variation on lithium-induced proliferation. Therefore, our primary phenotype, termed ΔS-phase, entailed subtracting the vehicle proliferation rate from the lithium exposure proliferation rate.

To identify genetic variants associated with lithium-induced NPC proliferation, we performed GWA tests using a linear mixed-effects model (39). While proliferation rates were not correlated with any measured biological or technical variables (Figure S3J–N in Supplement 1), we included gestation week, sex, and multidimensional scaling components as covariates in the association model to control for ancestry. We identified 80 nominally significant (p < 1 × 10−5) loci across all conditions (vehicle, ΔS-phase 0.75, 1.5, and 3 mM LiCl) and 1 study-wide significant association [p < 1.67 × 10−8, corrected for the number of independent tests (49)] (Figure 1C; Figure S4 in Supplement 1; Table S1 in Supplement 2). The study-wide significant locus was associated with lithium-induced proliferation (ΔS-phase) at 1.5 mM LiCl, but the association was attenuated in response to other lithium concentrations and was not detectably associated with vehicle proliferation rate (Figure 1D–G). In all, these results demonstrate that a GWAS performed in cultured cells can identify novel context–dependent pharmacogenomic effects (50).

The study-wide significant locus at chr3p21.1 spans a >500-kb gene-dense region (Figure 2A). This locus contains the 2 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with the lowest study-wide p values (rs352140, p = 5.71 × 10−9; rs11709284, p = 8.00 × 10−9), which are weakly correlated by linkage disequilibrium (r2 = 0.22). To evaluate whether rs352140 and rs11709284 tag independent loci, we conditioned the associations on either SNP, both of which reduced the significance of the association of the other, indicating that the association signal identified a single but complex locus (Figure S5A–C in Supplement 1).

To explore the association of this locus with other complex brain traits, we tested for colocalization with existing brain-related GWAS summary statistics using conditional analysis (51). We identified colocalizations with risk for BD (8), schizophrenia (52), and interindividual differences in intelligence (53) (Figure 2B–D). Conditioning on each index SNP from these brain-related GWASs markedly reduced proliferation associations, especially for rs11709284 (Figure S5D–F in Supplement 1), suggesting that causal variants affecting lithium-induced proliferation and these complex brain traits are shared. We expect that the colocalization between lithium-responsive proliferation, interindividual differences in intelligence, BD risk, and schizophrenia risk are driven by the strong genetic correlation and polygenic overlap among these traits (8,54,55), which suggests that the GNL3 locus is broadly relevant to brain function. Moving forward, we focus on rs11709284 as marking the lithium-induced proliferation-associated locus given its stronger association with these other traits. The lithium-induced proliferation-increasing allele is a risk allele for BD and schizophrenia and an intelligence-decreasing allele. The lithium-induced proliferation locus was not associated with lithium response in individuals with BD (10) (Figure S5G in Supplement 1). The lone genome-wide significant locus associated with lithium response in individuals with BD was also not associated with lithium-induced proliferation or risk of BD (Figure S6 in Supplement 1). To explore patterns of polygenic overlap between lithium-sensitive proliferation and other traits, we selected variants associated with 1.5 mM lithium-induced proliferation at increasing significance thresholds and plotted other trait GWAS p values at these selected variants using quantile-quantile plots (Figure S7 in Supplement 1; Table S2 in Supplement 2). SNPs most significantly associated with lithium-induced NPC proliferation phenotype showed the highest enrichment in neuropsychiatric disorder GWAS (bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, major depressive disorder, autism spectrum disorder), relatively moderate enrichment in nondisorder brain traits (intelligence, global cortical thickness), and little enrichment in nonbrain traits (high-density lipoprotein being the only exception) (Figure S7A–D in Supplement 1). This pattern of enrichment suggests that the genetic variants associated with lithium-sensitive NPC proliferation are also associated with the risk of neuropsychiatric disorders.

To identify putative causal genes underlying the association with lithium-induced proliferation, we considered an approximately 1.2-Mb window containing 29 protein-coding genes surrounding rs11709284 (Figure S8A in Supplement 1). Previous expression measurements via RNA sequencing in these NPCs showed that 21 of these genes had detectable expression at baseline (35,36) (Figure S8B in Supplement 1). We quantified the expression of each of these genes over time in response to 1.5 mM LiCl in 1 NPC donor line using qPCR, and GNL3 was the only gene with significantly different expression at multiple time points (Figure S8C in Supplement 1). Repeating this experiment in NPCs from 3 additional donors confirmed that 1.5 mM LiCl transiently induced GNL3 expression, peaking 8 hours after exposure (Figure 3A). Increasing lithium concentration also resulted in relatively higher GNL3 expression at 8 hours, without extending the duration of expression (Figure S8D in Supplement 1).

GNL3, also known as nucleostemin, regulates cell cycle progression and genome stability and is essential for maintenance of stem cell fate (56,57). GNL3 was also identified as a putative BD risk gene in the most recent BD GWAS (8) and in an integrated expression quantitative trait loci analysis of previous BD GWASs (58). rs11709284 is an expression quantitative trait loci in the GTEx (Genotype-Tissue Expression) project for multiple nearby genes (PBRM1, NT5DC2, GLYCTK, NEK4), but most significantly for GNL3 in brain tissues, including the frontal cortex and hippocampus (59) (Figure 3B, C), and these associations colocalize (Figure S9A–G in Supplement 1). The allele associated with increased GNL3 is also associated with increased lithium-induced proliferation (Figure 1F). Next, we assessed allele-specific GNL3 expression in the population of NPCs using SNPs in linkage disequilibrium with rs11709284 in the GNL3 open reading frame and found that the alleles associated with increased GNL3 expression at baseline were also associated with increased lithium-induced proliferation (35) (Figure 3D; Figure S9H, I in Supplement 1). To assess how lithium affects the expression of each GNL3 allele, we used allele-specific qPCR probes targeting a SNP in the 5′ untranslated region of GNL3 (r2 = 0.68) (Figure S10A in Supplement 1). Again, we observed that the allele associated with increased proliferation rates had increased GNL3 expression in a temporally controlled manner (Figure 3E, F). Immunolabeling for GNL3 suggested that cells in the S-phase had increased nuclear GNL3 puncta compared with cells not in the S-phase (Figure 3G, H). While lithium increased GNL3 RNA in bulk qPCR measurements (Figure 3A), we did not observe further increase in GNL3 protein levels in individual cells due to lithium (Figure 3H). This suggests that lithium caused more cells to enter the cell cycle but did not directly affect GNL3 expression on an individual cell basis. In all, these data suggest that GNL3 is a lithium- and proliferation-responsive gene and that common genetic variants associated with increased GNL3 expression are also associated with increased lithium-induced NPC proliferation.

Next, we sought to determine whether manipulating GNL3 expression would be sufficient to affect lithium-responsive NPC proliferation. We targeted the promoter of GNL3 with dCas9 fused to either a chromatin-repressive domain (KRAB) or a chromatin-opening domain (VP64) (60,61) and identified gRNAs capable of decreasing or increasing GNL3 expression (Figure 4A and Figure S10A in Supplement 1). We found that these constructs did not detectably affect expression of genes within a 250-kb window surrounding the gRNA target sites except for PBRM1, which shares a bidirectional promoter with GNL3 (Figure S10A–C in Supplement 1). Because PBRM1 was not found to have lithium-responsive gene expression, we focused on the effects of modulating GNL3 at this locus (Figure S8C in Supplement 1). Changing baseline GNL3 levels did not affect the relative magnitude of the lithium activation of GNL3 expression (Figure S10B in Supplement 1). Increasing GNL3 expression was sufficient to increase proliferation in the absence of lithium, while decreasing GNL3 expression had no detectable effect on baseline proliferation (Figure 4B). In contrast, NPCs with decreased GNL3 expression had less lithium-induced proliferation (Figure 4C). Increasing GNL3 expression did not further affect the lithium induction of proliferation (Figure 4C), which we speculate could be a ceiling effect because the dCas9 VP64-treated NPCs already had approximately 4% increased proliferation rate at baseline (Figure 4B). In all, these experiments indicate that GNL3 is necessary for the full proliferative effects of lithium in NPCs and is sufficient to induce NPC proliferation.

We performed a GWAS in cultured human NPCs and identified common genetic variants that affect impact lithium-induced proliferation. The chr3p21.1 locus was only detectable following lithium treatment (Figure 1D, F), supporting the hypothesis that certain genetic variants influence traits only in specific contexts (62). We also detected a colocalization between the genetic effects on lithium-induced proliferation and BD risk, schizophrenia risk, and interindividual differences in intelligence (Figure 2; Figure S5 in Supplement 1), demonstrating that genetic variation at this locus is also relevant for differences in brain-related traits and risk of neuropsychiatric disorders. We identified a single gene in this locus, GNL3, whose expression changed in response to lithium, has previously been implicated in BD risk (8,58), and has known roles in neural stem cell self-renewal by mitigating DNA damage induced by DNA replication (56,57) (Figure 3; Figure S8 in Supplement 1). We found that common genetic variation influences GNL3 expression, and using CRISPR interference/activation, we demonstrated that modulating GNL3 expression can influence proliferation in both the presence and absence of lithium (Figure 4). In all, using a cell-culture GWAS system in a pharmacogenomics design and CRISPR-based experimental manipulations, we discovered and validated a gene that contributes to differences in neural progenitor proliferation in response to lithium.

Colocalization analysis suggests that genetic variation at the chr3p21.1 locus modulates molecular, cellular, cognitive, and neuropsychiatric risk traits. Genetic predisposition to a disorder has previously been shown to be related to treatment response in that disorder (63), which could explain the colocalization between risk of BD and cellular lithium response. Combining schizophrenia and BD polygenic risk scores improves genetic prediction of lithium response in individuals with BD (64), suggesting that a shared genetic variation modulating risk for either disorder may also influence treatment responses. Previous studies describing strong genetic correlation and polygenic overlap between risk of BD, risk of schizophrenia, and interindividual differences in intelligence also lead us to expect detection of some shared genetic effects for these traits (8,54,55). GNL3 may also function through additional cellular contexts other than lithium exposure in NPCs to alter the risk for these disorders and interindividual differences in cognitive ability.

Our results can be interpreted in environmental, developmental, and clinical contexts. Lithium’s effects on NPC proliferation or other cellular processes via environmental or dietary sources could explain the correlation between lithium exposure and population-level measures of psychiatric health (65–67). Lithium is also classified as a class D teratogen due to increased risk of fetal cardiac malformations (68,69), but effects on human brain tissues resulting from fetal exposure to lithium are unclear (70,71). While fetal lithium exposure may lead to subtle neuro-developmental alterations in animal models (72–75), it is unknown if lithium exposure leads to similar effects in humans. Given the limited evidence for the teratogenic effects of lithium on the developing human brain, we favor interpreting our results in the context of the clinical lithium response. If fetal-derived NPCs can model the proliferative activity of adult hippocampal progenitor cells and NPC proliferation provides therapeutic benefit as has been shown for antidepressants (18–22), then our results suggest that genetic effects on GNL3 expression and lithium-induced NPC proliferation may contribute to variance in the clinical outcomes of lithium treatment.

Our study must also be viewed in light of some limitations. The study-wide significant locus discovered here was not identified in 2 previous GWASs of lithium response in individuals with BD (10,11). The disparity between these cellular and clinical phenotypes may be a result of limited sample sizes or differing population structures. Clinical lithium response GWASs were conducted using approximately 12,000 individuals in the study by Song et al. (11) and approximately 1500 individuals in the study by Hou et al. (10), both with substantially smaller sample sizes than those of well-powered neuropsychiatric disorder GWASs (8,52). Population structure differences may also obscure shared genetic effects (76). The Hou et al. (10) and Song et al. (11) lithium response GWASs were restricted to individuals of European ancestry, while only 10% of the cell lines used in our study were of European ancestry. Future lithium response GWASs with increased sample size and genetic diversity will improve our understanding of the genetic architecture of lithium response in BD and provide greater statistical power for assessing colocalization. Another potential limitation of our study is that lithium-induced proliferation in cultured NPCs may not accurately model the clinical lithium response. While lithium stimulates hippocampal NPC proliferation in adult rodents and the fetal NPCs used here share many properties with adult hippocampal NPCs (23,42–44), further experiments are necessary to evaluate a connection between NPC proliferation and clinical lithium response in humans. Experiments testing whether lithium-induced NPC proliferation differs in induced pluripotent stem cells derived from lithium responders or nonresponders may provide further insight into the clinical relevance of our findings (12–15). Finally, while our study focused on the acute (48 hours) effects of lithium on proliferation following administration of a single therapeutically relevant concentration, the beneficial effects of lithium typically occur through sustained maintenance treatment (77). Further experiments will be necessary to evaluate how genetic variation alters proliferation or differentiation outcomes after prolonged exposure to lithium in a cell culture–based model.

Despite these limitations, our data lead us to hypothesize that manipulating GNL3 expression during lithium treatment may enhance clinical outcomes. Our findings also suggest that the therapeutic effects of lithium will be reduced in individuals with genetic backgrounds producing lower GNL3 expression. These hypotheses can be tested by determining the effects on NPC proliferation, neurogenesis, and relevant behavioral paradigms after experimental manipulation of GNL3 expression in the brain during lithium exposure in rodents (78). In conclusion, our study sheds light on how common genetic variation can influence the cellular effects of a widely used mood stabilizing treatment. Our approach also suggests that the genetic diversity inherent in libraries of human cell lines can be leveraged to identify novel therapeutically relevant genes and pathways.
