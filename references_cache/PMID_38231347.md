# Management of Atopy with Dupilumab and Omalizumab in CADINS Disease.
**Authors:** Diaz-Cabrera NM, Bauman BM, Iro MA, Dabbah-Krancher G, Molho-Pessach V, Zlotogorski A, Shamriz O, Dinur-Schejter Y, Sharon TD, Stepensky P, Tal Y, Eisenstein EM, Pietzsch L, Schuetz C, Abreu D, Coughlin CC, Cooper MA, Milner JD, Williams A, Armoni-Weiss G, Snow AL, Leiding JW
**Journal:** J Clin Immunol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10875-023-01636-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-023-01636-y)

## Content

The caspase activation and recruitment domain 11 (CARD11) gene encodes a scaffold protein required for lymphocyte antigen receptor signaling. Dominant-negative, loss-of-function (LOF) pathogenic variants in CARD11 result in CARD11-associated atopy with dominant interference of NF-κB signaling (CADINS) disease. Patients with CADINS suffer with severe atopic manifestations including atopic dermatitis, food allergy, and chronic spontaneous urticaria in addition to recurrent infections and autoimmunity. We assessed the response of dupilumab in five patients and omalizumab in one patient with CADINS for the treatment of severe atopic symptoms. CARD11 mutations were validated for pathogenicity using a T cell transfection assay to assess the impact on activation-induced signaling to NF-κB. Three children and three adults with dominant-negative CARD11 LOF mutations were included. All developed atopic disease in infancy or early childhood. In five patients, atopic dermatitis was severe and recalcitrant to standard topical and systemic medications; one adult suffered from chronic spontaneous urticaria. Subcutaneous dupilumab was initiated to treat atopic dermatitis and omalizumab to treat chronic spontaneous urticaria. All six patients had rapid and sustained improvement in atopic symptoms with no complications during the follow-up period. Previous medications used to treat atopy were able to be decreased or discontinued. In conclusion, treatment with dupilumab and omalizumab for severe, refractory atopic disease in patients with CADINS appears to be effective and well tolerated in patients with CADINS with severe atopy.

JOCI-D-23-00155: Response to Reviewers 
 
We thank the Reviewers for their thoughtful critiques of our paper. Please find below our point-by-point 
response, which prompted several revisions which have clarified and strengthened the manuscript. We 
hope the revised paper is now suitable for publication in Journal of Clinical Immunology. 
 
Reviewer #1: In this manuscript Diaz-Cabrera document the treatment of 5 patients with CADINS disease 
due to DN LOF mutations in CARD11 with the biologics Dupilumab (anti-IL-4Ra) and Omalizumab 
(anti-IgE). Specifically, 4 patients were treated with Dupilumab for atopic dermatitis and 1 with 
Omalizumab for chronic spontaneous urticaria. On follow-up all patients display rapid improvement with 
no complications. Overall, this is a nice report detailing the utility of biologics such as Dupilumab and 
Omalizumab in the treatment of atopic disease in CADINS patients. However, I do have the following 
queries/suggested changes: 
 
    -       From a clinical treatment perspective can the authors add comments on their recommendations as 
to when Dupilumab vs Omalizumab should be prescribed in CADINS disease. It was not clear why 
Dupilumab could not be offered to patient #5 esp since Omalizumab treatment has been stopped - why are 
you only considering anti-IL-13? Are there instances where these biologics should not be used in 
CADINS patients? 
 
Answer: We thank the Reviewer for their overall positive assessment, and the excellent question 
regarding choice of dupilumab vs. omalizumab for treatment of CADINS disease. We have now included 
additional clinical history on the 1 patient who received omalizumab, now labeled as Patient 1 in the 
revised manuscript. To summarize, an attempt was made to switch her from omalizumab to dupilumab in 
2020. After each of 2 doses (300 mg sc) of dupilumab, the patient experienced injection site reactions, 
angioedema, headaches and severe fatigue/exhaustion. Given the severity of these side effects, she was 
switched back to Omalizumab. However, her skin improved remarkably with dupilumab treatment, with 
itching resolved and a significant reduction in the need for topical treatments. Tralokinumab was also 
attempted but was not effective. The patient is currently receiving omalizumab and a recently added JAK 
inhibitor. See table 1 and lines 229-235.  
 
    -       Could Table 1 be expanded or an addition of another table to document changes post treatment 
including - lymphocyte counts esp eosinophil and serum Ig levels esp IgE. 
 
Answer: We thank the Reviewer for this excellent suggestion. We have now expanded Table 1 to include 
all available laboratory test data post-treatment. Moreover, we have also included new data on an 
additional CADINS patient [Patient 5 (cousin of Patient 4, R75W variant)] successfully treated with 
dupilumab (see Figure 1 and Table 1). 
 
 
    -       Did other atopic issues improve or change post treatment ie asthma, food, drug and environmental 
allergies, allergic rhinitis etc 
 
Answer: Patient 1 had no other atopic conditions. Patients 3-6 had improvement in asthma while on 
biologic therapy. We have provided additional details in Table 1 where available.

    Reviewer #2: Diaz-Cabrera et al report the response to monoclonal antibody therapy in 5 patients with 
refractory AD in the context of LoF CARD11 mutations. The information provided in the manuscript is 
useful but I have a few requests for clarification: 
 
    1. This is a retrospective series but there is no description of how the cohort was established. The 
authors should provide information about case ascertainment. For example, were they identified with 
CADINS and then considered to be candidates for therapy, or were they identified because they had 
severe AD, received monoclonal antibody therapy, and then underwent genotyping? Were there any 
CADINS patients that were either not treated, or treated without response? 
 
Answer: We thank the Reviewer for this important question. All cases were initially diagnosed with 
severe AD, and then subsequently referred to Dr. Milner or Dr. Snow on the suspicion of a pathogenic 
CARD11 LOF/DN variant that might be responsible for CADINS disease phenotypes, detected through a 
targeted gene panel or whole exome/genome sequencing. Our laboratories receive frequent referrals for 
CARD11 variants of unknown significance (VUS), which can be adjudicated efficiently in the Jurkat cell 
transfection system described herein. However, some patients were treated prior to validation of a 
pathogenic CARD11 DN variant. Based on Dr. Leiding’s observation of a promising clinical response to 
dupilumab in Patient 1, we surveyed our recent referrals for any experience using biologicals, and 
assembled a cohort of all known CADINS patients with confirmed CARD11 DN mutations that had 
recently received dupilumab or omalizumab (n=6), all of whom showed a positive clinical response in 
significantly ameliorating atopic disease. Although we are unaware of any CADINS patients that were 
treated with either drug and did not show a positive response, we have added text to the discussion to 
acknowledge that possibility and describe in more detail how the cohort was established. Of note, an 
additional case report described similar positive results for a single CADINS patients (see Charvet et al, 
ref #38). See lines 160-167, 386-387. 
 
    2. The introduction to the manuscript discusses how rare monogenic versions of atopic disease might 
provide insights into disease mechanisms. I agree with this statement. It is not clear, however, why 
selection of dupilumab (or omalizumab) is guided by the presence of CARD11 LoF mutations. The 
argument could quite reasonably be made that genotyping is uninformative for treatment decisions 
because CADINS patients can be managed in the same way as other patients with severe AD. The authors 
should comment and also discuss whether dupilumab rather than say a JAKi is likely to be the treatment 
of choice in CADINS. 
 
Answer: The Reviewer makes an excellent point. Although dupilumab and omalizumab can be effective 
for many atopic patients regardless of underlying genotypes, our knowledge of the pathogenic molecular 

mechanisms driving atopic disease in CADINS logically informs to potential utility of both drugs. We 
have previously shown that CADINS patient T cells skew toward a Th2 phenotype, making less IFN-g 
and more IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13. This is likely tied to impaired TCR-induced, CARD11-dependent 
signaling to NF-kB, mTORC1, and potentially JNK after – prior evidence from the literature suggests 
defects in all three pathways may contribute to enhanced Th2 differentiation. Moreover, ~80% of 
CADINS patients display high serum IgE, which manifests through the Th2 diathesis. Thus we argue that 
blockade of IL-4 signaling (dupilumab) or IgE (omalizumab) represents a “semi-targeted” therapy for 
CADINS disease based on the pathophysiology, whereas a JAK inhibitor would disrupt signaling through 
numerous cytokines that operate outside the Th2 axis, potentially leading to unnecessary 
immunosuppression and a higher incidence of infection or malignancy in CADINS patients. Nevertheless, 
we have added more text to the Discussion to consider these points and speculate on the utility of other 
modern treatment modalities for severe atopic disease. See lines 389-395.  
 
    3. The authors do not provide any biological readouts of response to treatment, only the clinical scores. 
It would be useful to know if there were any other changes in response to therapy (e.g. eosinophilia, total 
IgE). 
 
Answer: We thank the Reviewer for this helpful critique, congruent with the suggestion from Reviewer 1. 
We now include additional data from available laboratory values post-treatment (Table 1) and lines 344-
348.  
 
    4. The evidence for DN action of the mutations identified is not overwhelming. Furthermore, the 
summary figure presents data GFP MFI, while the histograms show percentage of transfectants that 
upregulate GFP expression. Both readouts should be displayed for representative histograms and 
summary plots. 
 
Answer: Per the Reviewer’s suggestion, we have included data on % GFP+ cells from our JPM50.6 
transfection assay in Figure 2. All variants tested in our assay showed statistically significant dominant 
interference of WT CARD11-dependent NF-kB signaling in T cells based on GFP MFI, which we 
consider to be the gold standard for validating pathogenicity. While TCR-induced NF-kB activation is 
considered digital, GFP+ MFI describes an analog component to the strength of NF-kB activation that we 
have observed to be more sensitive in adjudicating mild DN effects for a variant like M65L, which 
showed no significant difference in %GFP+ (i.e. proportion of cells that can induce NF-kB activation). 
These results are similar to previously published reports by our group and others describing variable 
potency of numerous CADINS-related CARD11 variants, depending on the location of the mutation 
and/or the actual amino acid change. We have now included additional language in the Discussion to 
clarify these points. See lines 389-395.  
 
    Minor comment 
   The table should mention the treatment given. 
 
Answer: Per the Reviewer’s suggestion, we have included the specific treatment given for each patient in

Introduction 124 
 125 
Recalcitrant atopic dermatitis (AD) can be refractory to standard therapies, including wet wraps, 126 
topical agents, phototherapy and/or conventional immunosuppressive drugs. Difficulty in 127 
management of atopic conditions in these patients has prompted consideration of newer biologic 128 
therapies that specifically target relevant signaling pathways and effector molecules like cytokines 129 
(1). Indeed, agents targeting effector  molecules including IgE (omalizumab) and IL -4/IL-13 130 
receptor (dupilumab) have shown remarkable efficacy in treating certain forms of severe AD and 131 
asthma (2). However, these therapies are not always successful or appropriate for all patients, given 132 
the inherent genotypic and phenotypic heterogeneity in atopic disease. Furthermore, it is not clear 133 
whether patients with rare causes of allergic disease (e.g. genetic, autoimmune, etc) should be 134 
treated in a similar fashion to those with “common” allergic disease. Monogenic “primary atopic 135 
disorders” (PADs) can provide insi ght into fundamental molecular and cellular pathways that 136 
cause allergy, and simultaneously present targets for precision therapy. For example, patients with 137 
loss of function mutations in STAT3 can develop significant atopic disease  (i.e. hyper IgE 138 
syndrome, HIES), and aberrantly upregulate expression of the IL-4 receptor. These HIES patients’ 139 
clinical response to dupilumab has been reported to be excellent (3-8), suggest ing a viable 140 
approach to ameliorating their atopic diathesis.   141

Caspase activation and recruitment domain 11 ( CARD11) encodes a scaffold protein linking 143 
lymphocyte antigen receptor activation with downstream NF-B, JNK, and mTOR signaling (9). 144 
Germline CARD11 mutations that alter  the function of these signaling pathways have been 145 
associated with a wide array of clinical phenotypes. While homozygous loss -of-function (LOF) 146 
mutations result in severe combined immune deficiency (SCID)  (10-12), heterozygous gain-of-147 
function (GOF) mutations lead to a B-cell lymphoproliferative disease known as B cell expansion 148 
with NF-B and T cell Anergy (BENTA) disease (13), and heterozygous dominant negative (DN) 149 
LOF mutations result in CARD11-associated atopy with dominant interference of NF-B signaling 150 
(CADINS) disease. In CADINS, T cells skew toward a Th2 phenotype, making less interferon 151 
gamma and more IL -4, IL-5, and IL-13 (14-16). CADINS is most often characterized by severe 152

Results  312 
The six individuals described above were found to harbor novel germline heterozygous CARD11 313 
variants, two of which were previously demonstrated (T43M, R75W) to be LOF/DN mutations 314 
(17, 19). Patients 4 and 5 are cousins  harboring the same R75W variant . None of the variants 315 
appear in large genomic databases (ExAC, gnomAD, 1000 Genomes). Multiple i n silico 316 
algorithms predicted that four of five were clearly impactful; for example, three of four missense 317 
variants exhibited Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) scores exceeding the 318 
Mutation Significance Cutoff for CARD11 (>22), strongly suggesting they are deleterious (21, 22). 319 
MutationTaster indicated all variants were likely “disease causing”  (23). To empirically test if 320 
these variants altered T cell receptor  (TCR)-induced, CARD11-dependent NF-B activation, we 321 
transfected plasmids encoding wild-type (WT) or mutant CARD11 into JPM50.6 cells, a CARD11-322 
deficient Jurkat line with an integrated NF-B-responsive GFP reporter. Relative to WT CARD11, 323 
all variants exhibited attenuated NF -B activity (measured as GFP mean fluorescence intensity 324 
(MFI) and % GFP+)  in response to anti -CD3/anti-CD28 and/or PMA/ionomycin stimulation  325 
(Figure 1A). Moreover, all variants were capable of disrupting WT CARD11 signaling when co-326 
expressed at a 50:50 ratio, indicating dominant interference (Figure 1B). LOF/DN activity was 327 
noticeably weaker for one variant (M65L), consistent with a lower CADD score (9.9). All variant 328 
proteins were comparably expressed relative to WT CARD11 and a -actin loading control. 329 
 330 
Consistent with a diagnosis of CADINS disease, all individuals had recurrent infections and severe 331 
atopic disease manifestations including AD, asthma, FA, and /or AR. All six were treated with 332 
dupilumab for refractory AD and one with omalizumab for refractory chronic spontaneous 333 
urticaria. Marked improvement in skin pathology was observed in all patients, including in images 334 
taken before and after treatment in Patients 3 and 5 (19) (Figure 2). The degree of atopic severity 335 
was measured via different scoring systems depending on each patient’s country of origin . For 336 
those receiving dupilumab, SCORAD for Patient 6 decreased from 84 to 40, and from 67 to 16.9 337 
in Patient 4, and from 14 to 0 in Patient 5. In Patient 3, the EASI decreased from 21.75 to 4.0, and 338 
in Patient 1, IGA score improved from 3 -4 to 0, all within a few months of therapy. Urticaria in 339 
Patient 2 also improved substantially upon administration of omalizumab, based on serial 340 
improvements in UAS , and although this patient did not tolerate dupilumab, her AD improved 341 
while receiving it. Moreover, her EASI score decreased from 39 to 9 after two months of treatment. 342

Discussion 350 
In recent years, several “primary atopic disorders” linked to single gene defects have been 351 
discovered and characterized. Patients with a family history of severe AD in an autosomal 352 
dominant pattern of inheritance should undergo genetic testing to determine monogenic causes  353 
(24, 25). The ability of heterozygous LOF mutations to dominantly interfere with WT CARD11 354 
signaling has been recently described in CADINS disease (14-16). Attenuation of TCR signaling 355 
can lead to Th2 phenotypes (26), and ~90% of patients harbori ng DN/LOF CARD11 mutations 356 
suffer from severe allergic manifestations  (9, 15). Indeed, most CADINS patients have elevated 357 
serum IgE  (~75%) and eosinophilia ( ~50%). Moreover, patients’ CD4 + T cells demonstrate 358 
enhanced production of IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 after in vitro stimulation, with reduced secretion of 359 
IFN- (15, 16). Although diminished signaling to NF-B, mTOR and JNK may all contribute to 360 
Th2 skewing in these patients (27-30), it remains unclear if and how targeting any or all of these 361 
downstream signaling pathways might constitute a viable therapeutic strategy for CADINS in the 362 
future. Moreover, genotype-phenotype correlations that might help to predict disease penetrance, 363 
severity or expressivity remain nebulous, including the quantitative or qualitative degree of antigen 364 
receptor signaling defects (15). Indeed, Patient 3 presented with marked atopic disease despite 365 
harboring a relatively mild LOF/DN missense mutation that would have otherwise been considered 366 
benign based on CADD score a lone, further underscoring the continued importance of empirical 367 
testing for novel CARD11 variants.  368

alternative and effective approach for treating recalcitrant atopic disease. As an IL-4R antagonist, 371 
dupilumab blocks signaling downstream of both IL -4 and IL -13, and has shown remarkable 372 
efficacy to date in treating AD patients regardless of genetic etiology (31). Indeed, dupilumab has 373

utility of biologics like dupilumab and omalizumab in the beneficial treatment of CADINS.  401

Abstract 93 
The caspase activation and recruitment domain 11 (CARD11) gene encodes a scaffold protein 94 
required for lymphocyte antigen receptor signaling. Dominant negative, loss-of-function (LOF) 95 
pathogenic variants in CARD11 result in CARD11-associated atopy with dominant interference 96 
of NF-B signaling (CADINS) disease. Patients with CADINS suffer with severe atopic 97 
manifestations including atopic dermatitis, food allergy, and chronic spontaneous urticaria in 98 
addition to recurrent infections and autoimmunity. We assessed the response of dupilumab in 5 99 
patients and omalizumab in 1 patient with CADINS for treatment of severe atopic symptoms. 100 
CARD11 mutations were validated for pathogenicity using a T cell transfection assay to assess 101 
impact on activation-induced signaling to NF-B. Three children and 3 adults with dominant 102 
negative CARD11 LOF mutations were included. All developed atopic disease in infancy or 103 
early childhood. In five patients, atopic dermatitis was severe and recalcitrant to standard topical 104 
and systemic medications; one adult suffered from chronic spontaneous urticaria. Subcutaneous 105 
dupilumab was initiated to treat atopic dermatitis and omalizumab to treat chronic spontaneous 106 
urticaria. All six patients had rapid and sustained improvement in atopic symptoms with no 107 
complications during the follow-up period. Previous medications used to treat atopy were able to 108 
be decreased or discontinued. In conclusion, treatment with dupilumab and omalizumab for 109 
severe, refractory atopic disease in patients with CADINS appears to be effective and well 110 
tolerated in patients with CADINS with severe atopy.  111

Introduction 124 
 125 
Recalcitrant atopic dermatitis (AD) can be refractory to standard therapies, including wet wraps, 126 
topical agents, phototherapy and/or conventional immunosuppressive drugs. Difficulty in 127 
management of atopic conditions in these patients has prompted consideration of newer biologic 128 
therapies that specifically target relevant signaling pathways and effector molecules like cytokines 129 
(1). Indeed, agents targeting effector molecules including IgE (omalizumab) and IL -4/IL-13 130 
receptor (dupilumab) have shown remarkable efficacy in treating certain forms of severe AD and 131 
asthma (2). However, these therapies are not always successful or appropriate for all patients, given 132 
the inherent genotypic and phenotypic heterogeneity in atopic disease. Furthermore, it is not clear 133 
whether patients with rare causes of allergic disease (e.g. genetic, autoimmune, etc) should be 134 
treated in a similar fashion to those with “common” allergic disease. Monogenic “primary atopic 135 
disorders” (PADs) can provide insight into fundamental molecul ar and cellular pathways that 136 
cause allergy, and simultaneously present targets for precision therapy. For example, patients with 137 
loss of function mutations in STAT3 can develop significant atopic disease  (i.e. hyper IgE 138 
syndrome, HIES), and aberrantly upregulate expression of the IL-4 receptor. These HIES patients’ 139 
clinical response to dupilumab has been reported to be excellent (3-8), suggesting a viable 140 
approach to ameliorating their atopic diathesis.   141 
 142 
Caspase activation and recruitment domain 11 ( CARD11) encodes a scaffold protein linking 143 
lymphocyte antigen receptor activation with downstream NF-B, JNK, and mTOR signaling (9). 144 
Germline CARD11 mutations that alter  the function of these signaling pathways have been 145 
associated with a wide array of clinical phenotypes. While homozygous loss -of-function (LOF) 146 
mutations result in severe combined immune deficiency (SCID)  (10-12), heterozygous gain-of-147 
function (GOF) mutations lead to a B-cell lymphoproliferative disease known as B cell expansion 148 
with NF-B and T cell Anergy (BENTA) disease (13), and heterozygous dominant negative (DN) 149 
LOF mutations result in CARD11-associated atopy with dominant interference of NF-B signaling 150 
(CADINS) disease. In CADINS, T cells skew toward a Th2 phenotype, making less interferon 151 
gamma and more IL -4, IL-5, and IL-13 (14-16). CADINS is most often characterized by severe 152 
atopic manifestations , often accompanied by recurrent infections , autoimmunity and/or other 153 
sequelae. Indeed, data remain limited regarding the safety and efficacy of biologic therap ies in 154 

patients with CARD11 LOF mutations. Here we describe the clinical course of six CADINS 155 
patients treated with specific biologic modifiers (dupilumab or omalizumab).  156

Methods 159 
Patients 160 
A cohort of potential CADINS patients were collected by the authors. Patient 6 had been treated 161 
with dupilumab prior to functional validation of her CARD11 variant. Based on her favorable 162 
response, a cohort of patients was identified by the authors harboring suspected CARD11 163 
LOF/DN heterozygous variants, initially detected through targeted next generation gene panels 164 
or whole exome sequencing. After exhibiting poor responses to conventional treatments, this 165 
cohort was solicited for response to biologic therapy for treatment of atopic disease, with 166 
pathogenic CARD11 variants validated as LOF/DN as described below.   167

Plasmid preparation 169 
A wild-type pUNO-CARD11 (Invivogen) engineered with a C-terminal FLAG tag was used as a 170 
template for site-directed mutagenesis as previously described (16), using primers designed to 171 
introduce missense or indel variants corresponding to each patient (see Supplemental Table 1). 172 
Plasmids were amplified using Phusion High Fidelity Polymerase (ThermoFisher), digested with 173 
DpnI (ThermoFisher) to eliminate methylated DNA, and transformed into NEB 5-alpha 174 
competent E. coli (New England BioLabs) under blasticidin selection (Invivogen). Mini-prep 175 
DNA isolated from individual colonies were then PCR amplified and Sanger sequenced to 176 
confirm the presence of each variant (Psomagen), followed by purification using the GenElute 177 
HP Plasmid Maxiprep Kit (Sigma). A pUNO-CARD11 construct containing a C-terminal V5 tag 178 
was produced by removing the FLAG tag using BamHI/NheI digestion, followed by ligation of 179 
annealed primers (Supplemental Table 1) encoding the V5 tag using NEBuilder HiFi DNA 180 
Assembly Master Mix (New England BioLabs). 181

(JPM50.6) were electroporated with 3 g pUNO plasmids containing WT or mutant CARD11 -187 
FLAG, or an empty vector (EV) using an ECM 630 (BTX) at 260V, 950 F. For DN testing, cells 188

V5. At 24 hrs post -transfection, 0.5 x106 transfectants were stimulated overnight with 1 g/mL 190

Results  312 
The six individuals described above were found to harbor novel germline heterozygous CARD11 313 
variants, two of which were previously demonstrated (T43M, R75W) to be LOF/DN mutations 314 
(17, 19). Patients 4 and 5 are cousins  harboring the same R75W variant . None of the variants 315 
appear in large genomic databases (ExAC, gnomAD, 1000 Genomes). Multiple i n silico 316 
algorithms predicted that four of five were clearly impactful; for example, three of four missense 317 
variants exhibited Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) scores exceeding the 318 
Mutation Significance Cutoff for CARD11 (>22), strongly suggesting they are deleterious (21, 22). 319 
MutationTaster indicated all variants were likely “disease causing”  (23). To empirically test if 320 
these variants altered T cell receptor  (TCR)-induced, CARD11-dependent NF-B activation, we 321 
transfected plasmids encoding wild-type (WT) or mutant CARD11 into JPM50.6 cells, a CARD11-322 
deficient Jurkat line with an integrated NF-B-responsive GFP reporter. Relative to WT CARD11, 323 
all variants exhibited attenuated NF -B activity (measured as GFP mean fluorescence  intensity 324 
(MFI) and % GFP+)  in response to anti -CD3/anti-CD28 and/or PMA/ionomycin stimulation  325 
(Figure 1A). Moreover, all variants were capable of disrupting WT CARD11 signaling when co-326 
expressed at a 50:50 ratio, indicating dominant interference (Figure 1B). LOF/DN activity was 327 
noticeably weaker for one variant (M65L), consistent with a lower CADD score (9.9). All variant 328 
proteins were comparably expressed relative to WT CARD11 and a -actin loading control. 329 
 330 
Consistent with a diagnosis of CADINS disease, all individuals had recurrent infections and severe 331 
atopic disease manifestations including AD, asthma, FA, and /or AR. All six  were treated with 332 
dupilumab for refractory AD and one with omalizumab for refractory chronic spontaneous 333 
urticaria. Marked improvement in skin pathology was observed in all patients, including in images 334 
taken before and after treatment in Patients 3 and 5 (19) (Figure 2). The degree of atopic severity 335 
was measured via different scoring systems depending on each patient’s country of origin . For 336 
those receiving dupilumab, SCORAD for Patient 6 decreased from 84 to 40, and from 67 to 16.9 337 
in Patient 4, and from 14 to 0 in Patient 5. In Patient 3, the EASI decreased from 21.75 to 4.0, and 338 
in Patient 1, IGA score improved from 3 -4 to 0, all within a few months of therapy. Urticaria in 339 
Patient 2 also improved substantially upon administration of omalizumab, based on serial 340 
improvements in UAS , and although this patient did not tolerate dupilumab, her AD improved 341 
while receiving it. Moreover, her EASI score decreased from 39 to 9 after two months of treatment. 342 

There were no adverse effects  reported, indicating all b iologic medications were well tolerated . 343 
Subjectively, other atopic conditions such as asthma, CSU, FA, and AR improved in 5 of the 5 344 
who had these symptoms. Clinical improvement was the best marker of biologic response, but in 345 
those with available informa tion, AEC and IgE reduced. Two of five patients had reduction in 346 
AEC and three of three with available data had reduction in serum IgE. There were no 347 
perturbations in other immunphenotyping attributable to dupilumab therapy (Table 1).  348

Discussion 350 
In recen t years, several “primary atopic disorders” linked to single gene defects have been 351 
discovered and characterized. Patients with a family history of severe AD in an autosomal 352 
dominant pattern of inheritance should undergo genetic testing to determine monoge nic causes 353 
(24, 25). The ability of heterozygous LOF mutations to dominantly interfere with WT CARD11 354 
signaling has been recently described in CADINS disease (14-16). Attenuation of TCR signaling 355 
can lead to Th2 phenotypes (26), and ~90% of patients harboring DN/LOF CARD11 mutations 356 
suffer from severe allergic manifestations  (9, 15). Indeed, most CADINS patients have elevated 357 
serum IgE  (~75%) and eosinophilia ( ~50%). Moreover, patients’ CD4 + T cells demonstrate 358 
enhanced production of IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 after in vitro stimulation, with reduced secretion of 359 
IFN- (15, 16). Although diminished signaling to NF-B, mTOR and JNK may all contribute to 360 
Th2 skewing in these patients (27-30), it remains unclear if and how targeting any or all of t hese 361 
downstream signaling pathways might constitute a viable therapeutic strategy for CADINS in the 362 
future. Moreover, genotype-phenotype correlations that might help to predict disease penetrance, 363 
severity or expressivity remain nebulous, including the quantitative or qualitative degree of antigen 364 
receptor signaling defects (15). Indeed, Patient 3 presented with marked atopic disease despite 365 
harboring a relatively mild LOF/DN missense mutation that would have otherwise been considered 366 
benign based on CADD score alone, further underscoring the continued importance of empirical 367 
testing for novel CARD11 variants.  368 
 369 
Fortunately, newer biologics that block key ef fector molecules of Th2-driven pathology offer an 370 
alternative and effective approach for treating recalcitrant atopic disease. As an IL-4R antagonist, 371 
dupilumab blocks signaling downstream of both IL -4 and IL -13, and has shown remarkable 372 
efficacy to date in treating AD patients regardless of genetic etiology (31). Indeed, dupilumab has 373 

proven useful in treating patients with rare monogenic “hyper IgE syndromes”, whether autosomal 374 
dominant (STAT3 DN) (3, 7) or recessive (ZNF341, DOCK8) (32, 33) in nature. Omalizumab has 375 
also been a useful tool for treating severe and refr actory AD cases by depleting serum IgE and 376 
effectively disabling FcR1 bearing mast cells and basophils over time, attenuating IgE-dependent 377 
allergen sensitivity. Omalizumab is currently indicated for the treatment of severe allergic asthma, 378 
CSU and nasal polyps. Its efficacy for AD has been quite variable, although it can be used as a 379 
third- or fourth-line treatment for refractory cases of AD  (34-37). Indeed while the influence of 380 
IgE and mast cell activity on AD remains unclear, certain AD endotypes (and perhaps genotypes) 381 
may be caused by a tighter link between elevated IgE and AD. Treatment of AD in CADINS 382 
disease remains challenging, but our results suggest dupilumab or omalizumab  can be highly 383 
effective alternative treatments to refractory disease , resulting in impressive dermatological 384 
improvement. Our results mirror a recent single case report that showed significant improvement 385 
of AD in a CADINS patient treated with dupilumab  (38), and we are not aware of any CADINS 386 
patients that did not respond to biologic treatment. Nevertheless, longer follow-up and an expanded 387 
cohort of CADINS patients treated with biologics are necessary to understand the long-term 388 
efficacy and safety for use of biologics in these patients. Although Th2 skewing and high IgE 389 
levels typically associated with CADINS pathogenesis justify the potential utility of either biologic 390 
as a “semi -targeted therapy”  with no clear contraindications, clinical benefit must be weighed 391 
against the severity of any observed si de effects (see Patient 2). Indeed, o ther modern treatment 392 
modalities such as JAK inhibitors may also be beneficial  and may be worth investigation . Such 393 
broader immunosuppressive treatments must be  considered carefully in light of potential  394 
exacerbation of infectious complications and/or malignancy observed in some CADINS patients. 395 
 396 
In conclusion , we demonstrated that d upilumab and omalizumab treatment s were safe and 397 
efficacious in treating 5 unrelated pediatric and adult  patients with CADINS disease  that were 398 
otherwise refractory to standard therapies. Further follow-up is necessary to determine continued 399 
efficacy and safety , but we hope our findings will prompt larger clinical studies to confirm the 400 
utility of biologics like dupilumab and omalizumab in the beneficial treatment of CADINS.  401
