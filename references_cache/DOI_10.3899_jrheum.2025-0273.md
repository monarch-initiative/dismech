# Exploring the Genetic Landscape of Psoriatic Arthritis: A Narrative Review of Recent Genomic Studies
**Authors:** Hugues Allard-Chamard, Proton Rahman
**Journal:** The Journal of Rheumatology (2026)
**DOI:** [10.3899/jrheum.2025-0273](https://doi.org/10.3899/jrheum.2025-0273)

## Content

The recent availability of large-scale genomic datasets in psoriatic disease, combined with advances in molecular tools, next-generation genomic technologies, and informatics, has led to a better understanding of the genomic basis of psoriatic arthritis (PsA). Although no current genetic tests exist for the management of PsA, the potential for early diagnosis and treatment orientation through genomic studies remains a source of continued optimism. Ongoing studies aim to advance the stratification, prognosis, and pharmacogenomics of PsA. This review highlights recent advances in the genomics of PsA, focusing on genomic variants that may become clinically actionable. We will discuss the importance of elucidating family history, highlight potential clinically significant psoriatic genes, emphasize genetic variants that may identify PsA among patients with psoriasis, and explore the emerging roles of transcript profiling, single-cell sequencing, and spatial omics in PsA.

Abstract
The recent availability of large-scale genomic datasets in psoriatic disease, combined with 
advances in molecular tools, next-generation genomic technologies, and informatics, has led to a 
better understanding of the genomic basis of psoriatic arthritis (PsA). While no current genetic 
tests exist for the management of PsA, the potential for early diagnosis and treatment orientation 
through genomic studies remains a source of continued optimism. Ongoing studies aim to advance 
the stratification, prognosis, and pharmacogenomics of PsA. This review highlights recent 
advances in the genomics of PsA, focusing on genomic variants that may become clinically 
actionable. We will discuss the importance of elucidating family history, highlight potential 
clinically significant psoriatic genes, emphasize genetic variants that may identify PsA among 
patients with psoriasis, and explore the emerging roles of transcript profiling, single-cell 
sequencing, and spatial omics in PsA.
Introduction 
Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a complex, multifactorial disease that affects approximately 20% 
to 30% of patients with psoriasis, influenced by a combination of genetic, mechanical, metabolic, 
microbial, and environmental factors1. From a genetic perspective, PsA can be considered a 
disease within a disease, as there is significant overlap between the genetic factors associated 
with psoriasis and those linked to PsA. This overlap complicates efforts to isolate the genetic 
contributions specific to inflammatory arthritis. Despite these challenges, extensive research has 
underscored the important role of genetic predisposition in the pathogenesis of PsA1. Genetic

epidemiological studies on patients of North European ancestry estimate the heritability of 
psoriasis to be between 50% and 90%2, while estimates for PsA range from 80% to 100%3. 
Major genetic risk factors for PsA are primarily located within the major histocompatibility 
complex (MHC) class I region, particularly the HLA-B alleles, which are also associated with 
specific clinical subtypes of PsA. In contrast, HLA-C*06 is recognized as the most significant 
risk allele for psoriasis. Genome-wide association studies have identified numerous susceptibility 
loci, including variants involved in antigen processing, as well as loci within the IL-23/IL-17 
axis, NF-κB signaling, and innate immune pathways4. Although the cellular mechanisms 
underlying most genetic associations remain poorly understood, polygenic risk scores (PRS) and 
Mendelian randomization (MR) have provided valuable insights and may offer some clinical 
utility.
To further enhance our understanding of the disease mechanisms underlying PsA, it is 
imperative to investigate pathogenic cell types and integrate (epi)genomic, transcriptomic, 
proteomic, and metabolomic data with functional genomics through computational techniques. In 
this review, we will highlight some of the major advances in psoriatic disease over the past five 
years that may offer clinical utility and discuss emerging studies in single-cell sequencing, 
spatial transcriptomics and transcript profiling. The authors selected articles that they considered 
relevant for the reader, though this does not constitute a systematic review.
Overlooking the family history of psoriatic disease is a missed opportunity. 
According to the classification criteria for PsA, CASPAR, a family history of psoriasis is 
a core element in classifying PsA 5. Epidemiological studies have reported that the heritability of 
PsA is three to ten times greater in PsA than psoriasis 3,6. However, these heritability estimates

from clinic-based studies are influenced by the population prevalence of PsA, meaning they vary 
depending on the underlying disease prevalence.  With the advent of large-scale genome-wide 
association studies (GWAS), it is now possible to estimate the heritability of PsA using a method 
known as SNP heritability (h²SNP). This method offers unbiased variance estimates, independent 
of population prevalence7. Two recent studies have indicated that h²SNP heritability for cutaneous 
psoriasis (PsC) and PsA is similar 8,9. These SNP-based heritability studies suggest that having a 
family history of either psoriasis or PsA confers similar susceptibility risks for the proband.  
A recent study from an international longitudinal PsA cohort in Turkey indicates that 
clinical manifestations of PsA patients can differ based on whether the family history is of PsC or 
PsA10. Specifically, a family history of PsC is associated with a higher likelihood of enthesitis in 
the proband with PsA. In contrast, a family history of PsA is linked to an earlier onset of articular 
disease, more substantial joint deformities, and a reduced risk of plaque psoriasis. Further 
validation of these findings with an independent, well-phenotyped cohort is nonetheless necessary. 
Additionally, previous clinical studies have reported an excess of paternal transmission, 
suggesting that fathers are more likely to pass on PsA and psoriasis than mothers 11,12. However, 
these studies are vulnerable to recruitment bias, incomplete family member ascertainment, and 
variable ages of onset for PsA. Lakhal-Chaieb recently developed score-type statistics that account 
for sampling bias, providing additional statistical support for the observed increased risk of 
paternal transmission in PsA13. 
Given this evidence, it is important to take note of a family history of psoriatic disease 
among first-degree relatives. Clinicians should consider documenting whether the family history 
pertains to cutaneous psoriasis or PsA, as well as the mode of parental transmission.

Assessing causality of potential risk factors and associated diseases in PsA: results from 
Mendelian randomization studies 
Epidemiological studies in PsA have consistently identified several modifiable risk factors 
associated with the disease, particularly smoking, obesity, and hyperlipidemia14. PsA is also linked 
to various extra-musculoskeletal, such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and anterior uveitis14. 
However, establishing causation and the direction of these associations has proven challenging. 
Mendelian randomization (MR) studies aim to shed light on these relationships. By tagging known 
single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with modifiable risk factors or related 
diseases, researchers can assess the causal effects of exposures on PsA using MR, even in the 
presence of unobserved confounding factors15.
Numerous validated methods exist for conducting MR studies, and these approaches have 
subtle differences in instrument selection, modeling, and statistical inference procedures 16. The 
Inverse Variance Weighted (IVW) method is the most commonly used MR approach, often 
complemented by sensitivity analyses, pleiotropy assessments, and heterogeneity tests to ensure 
findings’ robustness16. All MR studies operate under the assumption that the genetic variant is 
associated with the exposure; there is no common link (confounder) between genetic variant and 
outcome; and the genetic variant influences the outcome solely through that exposure17. 
Based on epidemiological studies, a convincing association exists between obesity and 
psoriatic disease18. Mendelian randomization studies in psoriatic disease indicate that an increased 
body mass index (BMI) may lead to psoriasis and PsA.  Ashley Budu-Aggrey et al. found that a 
higher BMI causally increases the odds of psoriasis by 9% for every one-unit increase in BMI, 
while no reverse association was noted between genetic predisposition for psoriatic disease and 
BMI19. A study involving European and Japanese cohorts also highlighted a significant causal

relationship between genetically increased BMI and the risk of psoriasis 20. Additionally, Zhao et 
al. suggested that genetic predisposition to childhood body size is associated with an increased risk 
of developing PsA, and this was not observed for rheumatoid arthritis (RA) or ankylosing 
spondylitis (AS)21.
Population-based studies have indicated an association between hyperlipidemia and 
psoriatic disease that is independent of obesity 22. Based on these findings, Zhou et al. conducted 
an MR study of cholesterol and psoriasis. They demonstrated that high levels of total cholesterol 
and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol are genetic risk factors for psoriasis 23. This was 
further validated by Xiao et al., who found that genetic predisposition to high-density lipoprotein 
(HDL) and high triglyceride levels was associated with incident psoriasis, particularly in women24. 
The association between inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), particularly Crohn's disease 
(CD), and PsA is well established 14. A MR study by Li et al. suggests that genetic predisposition 
to IBD is associated with an increased risk of psoriasis, specifically for Crohn's disease rather than 
ulcerative colitis (UC). Regarding bi-directionality, genetic predisposition to psoriasis was not 
associated with IBD, and for PsA only a marginal association with IBD was noted25. Cai et al. also 
confirmed that genetic predisposition to IBD, particularly CD, is associated with an increased risk 
of both psoriasis and PsA26. 
MR studies investigating the causal relationships between two autoimmune diseases must be 
interpreted with caution due to the potential for shared genetic underpinnings. Violating the key 
assumption that there is no common confounder linking the genetic variant to the outcome can 
lead to biased results. This concern is particularly relevant in the context of autoimmune diseases, 
which are often polygenic and exhibit pleiotropy15. In these cases, genetic variants may influence 
the disease through multiple pathways, complicating the interpretation of causal relationships.

Moreover, MR studies can yield conflicting results due to various factors, including selection 
bias, insufficient statistical power, a limited number of instrumental variables, the pleiotropic 
effects of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with other exposures, and issues 
related to population stratification and linkage disequilibrium. The presence of multiple gene-
environment interactions and time-varying exposures also present significant challenges for MR 
studies, as the genetic instruments used may not adequately capture these dynamic relationships15. 
Additionally, it is crucial to assess the biological plausibility of the proposed causal pathways, as 
MR can only test hypotheses that are genetically mediated.
The large number of MR studies recently published, including those in rheumatic diseases, 
highlights the importance of understanding these nuances to advance research in immune-mediated 
complex rheumatic conditions. To strengthen causal inferences, it is essential to triangulate MR 
findings with other lines of evidence and conduct thorough sensitivity analyses 15. By addressing 
these limitations, researchers can enhance the robustness of MR as a tool for elucidating the 
complex relationships between exposures and autoimmune diseases, while minimizing false 
claims.
Update on genome wide significant variants in psoriatic disease 
Given the inter-relatedness of psoriasis vulgaris and PsA, recent advances in the genomics 
of psoriasis are potentially relevant to PsA and will be briefly highlighted. An international 
psoriasis consortium recently completed a meta-analysis of 18 GWAS, representing psoriasis's 
largest and most comprehensive genetic association study 27. The meta-analysis involved 36,466 
psoriasis cases and 458,078 controls and interrogated 11,808,957 autosomal variants. One hundred 
nine (109) distinct psoriasis susceptibility loci were identified, forty-five of which were not

among psoriasis cohorts with documented PsA or from exclusive PsA cohorts. Numerous other 
genetic variants have been proposed to be associated with PsA, particularly in the pre-GWAS era.

Can currently identified genetic loci facilitate identification of PsA among patients with 
psoriasis?
Theoretically, a gene specific to PsA could help identify inflammatory arthritis in patients 
with psoriasis, significantly assisting clinicians. However, the identification of genetic variants 
unique to PsA has been challenging. Recent efforts have compared patients with cutaneous 
psoriasis, defined as psoriasis without arthritis, to those with PsA, and have generated interesting 
results, even though they are not yet clinically actionable. 
Presently, PsA specific variants are limited and primarily reported in selected HLA alleles 
and a handful of non-MHC genes. The relative risk associated with these individual susceptibility 
alleles is modest. The HLA B alleles -B*27:05, -B*39:01, -B*38:01, and -B*08:01 are consistently 
associated with an increased risk of developing PsA, whereas -B*44:02 is associated with a

alleles in the HLA-B locus and persisted even after accounting for other associated HLA alleles

Regarding, PsA disease expression, HLA-B*08:01 is associated with a higher risk of 
peripheral arthritis, joint damage, ankylosis, and asymmetrical sacroiliitis. HLA-B*27:05 
demonstrated the strongest association with PsA spondylitis, symmetrical sacroiliitis, enthesitis, 
and dactylitis30-32. Additionally, HLA-C*06:02 occurred less frequently in PsA than in psoriasis 
vulgaris and was linked to an earlier onset of psoriasis and longer intervals between the onset of 
skin manifestations and inflammatory arthritis29.
A SNP-based analysis by Stuart et al. (2015) identified genetic loci encompassing IL-23R 
and TNFAIP3 that likely exhibit a specific effect on PsA. Importantly, these loci were not in 
linkage disequilibrium (LD) with any known psoriasis signal. Bowes et al. also noted a distinct 
PsA-specific variant at the IL23R locus (rs12044149), remaining highly significant even after 
controlling for the psoriasis SNP (rs9988642), thereby providing strong evidence for a unique PsA 
risk variant33. This finding has been confirmed in an independent cohort 34. However, the effect 
sizes of all these variants are quite small, with odds ratios (OR) between 1.1 and 1.4.
Polygenic risk scores have been successfully developed for ankylosing spondylitis, 
demonstrating promising discriminative ability, with superiority over MRI, HLA-B27 and CRP 
testing25. Patrick et al. used a machine learning algorithm with 200 genetic variants to differentiate 
between PsA and PsC, achieving an ROC curve score of 0.8235. This study involved a comparison 
of 5,065 patients with PsA and 21,286 healthy controls, alongside 4,340 patients with PsA and

Pharmacogenomics
The therapeutic response to biologic and targeted synthetic agents varies significantly 
among individuals suggesting that pharmacogenetic profiling may help tailor therapy. Prior 
pharmacogenetic studies have indicated that specific genetic variants within selected genes such 
as TNFα, TNFAIP3, TNFR1, TNFR1A, TNFRSF1A, TRAIL-R1, TNFRSF10A, FCGR2A, and 
FCGR3A are associated with anti-TNF response in PsA36.  Variants in some genes, such as MYD88 
(rs7744) are associated with good response to anti-TNF agents,  while   others, such                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          
as TNF-308 (rs1800629) and TNF-238 (rs361525), were associated with poor response to anti-
TNF agents in PsA. Most of these studies are small in scale, with marginal effect sizes and yielding 
non-replicated results. Currently, no specific variant has been identified that robustly predicts drug 
response to PsA. It is a similar scenario for psoriasis apart from ustekinumab response among 
HLA-C*06:02 carriers. A meta-analysis by Van Vugt et al. with over one thousand patients noted 
that the median PASI75 response among HLA-C*06:02 carriers was 92% compared to 67% among 
those that do not carry this allele, resulting in a significant difference in favor of HLA-C*06:02 
positive patients37.

patients were identified either as Th1 cell-predominant type, Th17 cell-predominant type,

The Emerging Power of Transcript Profiling, Single-Cell Sequencing, and Spatial Omics in 
PsA
Although classic genetic analysis has been slow in offering tools to assist the clinician in 
their everyday management of PsA patients, transcriptomic is now paving the way for a more in 
depth understanding of the immune dysregulation of PsA as well as demasking interindividual 
variation and putative therapeutics targets, slowly cracking the door of personalized medicine for 
PsA patients. Peripheral blood transcriptomic profiling is leading the field with promising 
approaches to enhance comprehension of the evolution of Psoriasis to PsA and PsA itself. Due to

readily accessible tissue, most of the available transcriptomic data available is from the skin with

the joins, is still being characterized and analyzed in the context of skin psoriasis studies. PsA joint

part, the observed disconnect in treatment responses between skin and joints, where cutaneous 
psoriasis tends to respond more consistently to IL-17 and IL-23 blockade compared to joint

degree of histological inflammation, such as high-grade synovitis, than with clinical disease 
activity scores42. In keeping with previous study joint synovium was enriched in genes related to

the possibility of differential mechanisms underlying skin and joint inflammation in PsA.

This model is interesting, but the single-cell RNAseq potential is even broader as it could 
be used to unveil cell-specific immune dysregulation with pathways being used differentially 
between a subset of immune cells. Using peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients 
fulfilling the PsA CASPAR criteria, single-cell RNAseq and further using the NanoString

TNFAIP3 were overexpressed in PsA vs. healthy control and patients with psoriatic disease limited 
to the skin. Nonetheless, higher levels were only detected in CD4+ T cells while CD8+ T cells 
expressed lower levels of both proteins. This unexpected result suggests that mechanisms 
inhibiting NFKBIA and TNFAIP3 are at play in CD8+ T cells and might explain their unusually 
strong reactivity 47. Harnessing the power of artificial intelligence, the identification, at the cell

published genetic risk factors for PsA was able to distinguish between psoriasis and PsA with the 
best models achieving AUROC ≥ 0.87. Such machine learning-based methodology are rapidly 
gaining attention with the recent democratization of artificial intelligence48.

cell RNAseq, tissue organization is retained while capturing individual cell transcriptomes in 
spatial single-cell RNAseq. It allows for the inference of cell-cell interactions and understanding

The synovial organization of the PsA is yet to be defined in greater depth, but it appears to 
be different from RA. Synovial biopsies of RA and PsA are shedding light on how cells organized 
in the synovium 50. The first observation is that in both diseases, due to differential λ and κ 
immunoglobulin light chain usage, synovial plasma cells are probably recruited from peripheral 
blood and not derived from the local memory B cell pool infiltrating the synovial region. 
Additionally, fibroblast and endothelial cells are characterized by differential transcription factor 
usage suggesting that the stromal cell of PsA joints is unique. By direct comparison, several 
characteristics of the RA synovium do not dominate in the PsA synovium. For instance, using 
receptor–ligand interactions, Floudas et al. identified that RA-specific synovial T cell-derived 
transforming growth factor (TGF)-β in combination with macrophage-derived interleukin-1β 
triggers the invasiveness of RAFAPα+THY1+ synovial fibroblasts, while these cells are scarce in 
PsA50. It suggests that bone erosion in PsA uses alternative mechanisms that are yet to be defined 
but that are different than those observed in RA patients.
Taken together, transcriptomic analyses are progressively helping to better characterize the 
complexity of PsA manifestation and are now a pivotal set of tools to characterize PsA immune

Conclusion 
Genomics remains central to precision medicine. Multinational collaborations, extensive 
genome-wide association studies, and advancements in informatics and statistical methods have 
identified over one hundred genomic loci associated with psoriatic disease. The findings from 
these studies have laid the groundwork for polygenic risk scores, which may eventually have 
clinical applications and provide insights into the causation of modifiable risk factors in PsA. 
Preliminary research utilizing single-cell sequencing, along with spatial transcriptomics, promises 
to enhance our understanding of the pathogenesis of PsA, leading to the discovery of new 
biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Furthermore, advancements in genomic editing and 
sequencing technologies are expected to clarify the genetic basis further, paving the way for 
continued progress in PsA precision medicine.
