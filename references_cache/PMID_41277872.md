# Prospective, Multicenter Evaluation of Transcarotid Artery Revascularization in Standard-Risk Patients: 30-Day Outcomes of the ROADSTER 3 Study.
**Authors:** Jim J, Dermody M, Peterson B, Berman SS, Mouawad NJ, Ricotta J, Saqib N, Faries P, Martin A, Adams JD, Tripathy U, Calderon D, O'Connor D, Shah R, Macdonald S, Schermerhorn ML
**Journal:** Ann Neurol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ana.78104](https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.78104)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: To evaluate the real-world safety and efficacy for standard surgical risk patients with extracranial internal carotid artery disease undergoing transcarotid artery revascularization.

METHODS: ROADSTER 3 is a prospective, multicenter, single-arm post-approval study. Patients <80 years old without anatomic or physiologic high-risk factors were included. Thirty-day incidence of major adverse events (stroke, death, or myocardial infarction) and cranial nerve injury were assessed. An independent clinical events committee adjudicated events, and independent certified health care personnel performed neurological assessments.

RESULTS: Between 2022 and 2024, 344 patients (intent to treat population) were enrolled at 48 sites in the United States. Because of 24 major protocol deviations, 320 patients remained in the Food and Drug Administration's analysis population (per protocol). In the per protocol population, there were 136 (42.5%) females and 49 (15.3%) symptomatic patients. Among symptomatic patients, 12 (24.5%) were treated within 2 weeks of the prior event. Technical success was 100%. For the entire study population, there were strokes in 3 patients with no instances of death or myocardial infarction. The 2 instances of cranial nerve injury (0.6%; 95% CI, 0.1-2.2% in the per protocol population) both resolved within 6 months. The composite 30-day stroke/death/myocardial infarction rate was 0.6% (95% CI, 0.1-2.2%) for the per protocol population and 0.9% (95% CI, 0.2-2.5%) for the intent to treat population.

INTERPRETATION: The 30-day results of the ROADSTER 3 study confirm that transcarotid artery revascularization is safe and effective in the standard-risk population. Longer-term follow-up is pending to confirm durable stroke prevention for these patients. ANN NEUROL 2026;99:705-712.

Transcarotid artery revascularization (TCAR) combines transcarotid arterial access and robust reversal of antegrade internal carotid artery flow to provide embolic protection during carotid angioplasty and stenting (CAS) for stroke prevention. The clinical efficacy of this technique was first demonstrated in patients at high risk for complications from carotid endarterectomy (CEA) in the pivotal ROADSTER Investigational Device Exemption trial,
1
 which garnered approval from the United States (US) Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the ENROUTE neuroprotection system (ENROUTE NPS). The ROADSTER 2 trial was then completed between 2015 and 2019 as a condition of the FDA's approval for commercial use of the ENROUTE stent (ENROUTE TSS) in TCAR procedures in high surgical risk patients.
2



The real‐world effectiveness of TCAR is demonstrated in multiple peer‐reviewed studies using prospectively collected, retrospectively reviewed data from the TransCarotid Revascularization Surveillance Project (TSP) of the Society for Vascular Surgery's Vascular Quality Initiative (VQI) carotid stenting module (including peripheral access CAS and TCAR).
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
 This registry is overseen by the Society for Vascular Surgery Patient Safety Organization. The TSP is an FDA and Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) approved registry that captures more than 95% of all TCAR procedures performed in the United States (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02850588).
7
 The remaining 5% reflect TCAR procedures performed in the Veterans Affairs hospital system with federal data collection. The initial commercial use of TCAR was mostly limited to patients considered at high surgical risk. The available data consistently show that TCAR is associated with significantly lower risks of perioperative and 1‐year stroke or death compared to transfemoral CAS and equivalent outcomes compared to CEA in the high‐risk population.
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
 In purely high‐risk patients, TCAR may have the lowest periprocedural stroke hazard of all 3 carotid revascularization techniques when propensity matching and/or multivariate logistic regression is performed.
8
,
9



Although positive outcomes for TCAR in high‐surgical‐risk patients have been well documented, published data on outcomes in standard‐risk populations is limited to VQI‐derived analyses.
8
,
10
 In multivariable adjusted analysis of standard‐risk patients undergoing carotid revascularization procedures between 2015 and 2020, odds of perioperative stroke were similar for TCAR versus CEA,
8
 and in 3:1 propensity‐matched analysis of 8,886 patients undergoing CEA and 2,962 patients undergoing TCAR, Liang et al
10
 demonstrated similar outcomes between TCAR and CEA. Results from prospective trials with independent event adjudication are lacking.

CMS expanded coverage for TCAR in standard‐risk patients in exchange for ongoing participation in the TSP, and FDA granted standard‐risk approval for the ENROUTE TSS. The ROADSTER 3 trial, a prospective evaluation of the safety and efficacy of TCAR in standard‐risk patients, was performed as a condition of this approval. This article reports on the 30‐day outcomes of the ROADSTER 3 trial.

The ROADSTER 3 study is a prospective, multicenter, single‐arm post‐approval study designed to assess the real‐world treatment of standard surgical risk patients with carotid artery disease undergoing TCAR using the ENROUTE TSS in conjunction with the ENROUTE NPS. The trial included 48 enrolling sites within the United States between September 2022 and June 2024. The participating locations and site investigators are listed in the Supporting Information (Table S1). The trial was sponsored by Silk Road Medical (now a wholly owned subsidiary of Boston Scientific Corporation) and registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier NCT05365490). This study complies with the Transparency and Openness Promotion Guidelines, and anonymized data and materials have been made publicly available and can be accessed at ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier NCT05365490). Each institution's investigational review board reviewed and approved the protocol before trial enrollment.

All patients who signed an informed consent and underwent the procedure were enrolled in the study (intention to treat [ITT] population, n = 344). The per‐protocol (PP, n = 320) population was defined as patients who underwent the study procedure without major protocol deviations, including not meeting inclusion and exclusion criteria.

The primary end point is a hierarchical composite of major adverse events defined as stroke, death, or myocardial infarction (S/D/MI) within 30 days of the index procedure plus the rate of ipsilateral stroke at 1 year. An important secondary end point is the incidence of cranial nerve injury (CNI) within 30 days. Thirty‐day outcomes are reported here, and long‐term follow‐up is ongoing for the 1‐year ipsilateral stroke component of the primary end point. The following neurological assessments were obtained before and within 24 hours from the end of the procedure, as well as at 30 days: National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), modified Rankin Scale (mRS), and Cranial Nerve Palsy Assessment. These tests were performed by an independent neurologist or an NIHSS‐certified and mRS‐certified provider independent of the operating surgeon and will be repeated at the 1‐year follow‐up visit. To ensure adequate data quality assurance, monitoring visits to the sites were made periodically to assess compliance with the protocol and Good Clinical Practices guidelines and to verify the data recorded on the electronic case report forms. An independent Clinical Events Committee (Supporting Information Table S2) reviewed and adjudicated all major adverse events (definitions in Supporting Information). Clinical follow‐up was performed at 30 days (+7 days) and will continue at 1 year (365 ± 45 days). A 6‐month (180 ± 30 days) visit was required for patients who had suspected CNI at 30 days.

Patients with atherosclerotic extracranial internal carotid artery stenosis as determined by duplex ultrasound, computed tomographic angiography (CTA), magnetic resonance angiography, or catheter angiography were screened and enrolled. CTA or magnetic resonance angiography had to be performed within 180 days of the procedure. Both symptomatic and asymptomatic patients were eligible for enrollment. Symptomatic patients had to have an ipsilateral ≥50% stenosis with prior stroke (minor or non‐disabling), transient ischemic attack, or amaurosis fugax within 180 days of the procedure. Asymptomatic patients could be enrolled with a stenosis ≥70% without any neurological symptoms within the prior 180 days.

All patients had to be <80 years old and free of any clinical or anatomic criteria considered high‐risk for CEA and were screened for additional exclusion criteria (Supporting Information). All patients were at least 18 years of age and provided signed informed consent. Preoperative coronavirus disease 2019 testing was to be completed within 7 days of the procedure.

The technical details of a TCAR have previously been described.
10
,
11
 The anesthetic technique was at the discretion of the operator. Briefly, a surgical incision is made at the base of the neck ipsilateral to the carotid artery stenosis. The common carotid artery (CCA) just superior to the clavicle is identified and systemic anticoagulation is administered. Using initial micro‐puncture access, an 8 French arterial sheath is subsequently placed under direct vision into the proximal CCA. An 8 French venous sheath is typically placed in the contralateral common femoral vein under ultrasonic guidance. A “flow controller” with an external filter connects the arterial and venous sheaths to create an arteriovenous shunt. Standard angiography is performed through the arterial sheath to delineate the carotid anatomy. The blood pressure is elevated to a mean arterial blood pressure target of 100mmHg (140–160 mmHg systolic pressure). The proximal common carotid artery is occluded by surgical means, which includes the use of silastic loops or a clamp, and reversal of flow is confirmed. Carotid angioplasty and stent placement are then performed. This typically involves crossing the lesion using a 0.014″ wire, pre‐dilatation with a balloon angioplasty catheter, and stenting with the self‐expanding ENROUTE TSS. Post‐dilatation is performed, if necessary, for significant residual stenosis. After completion of angiography, all catheters and wires are removed, and antegrade flow is restored through the carotid arteries. The arteriotomy is closed with a pre‐placed polypropylene suture, and protamine is administered. The neck incision is then reapproximated.

The calculated sample size for this study was derived separately for the primary end point and one of the key secondary end points (incidence of CNI within 30 days of the index procedure) using 80% power and a type 1 error rate of 2.5% and performing an exact test for binomial proportions with continuity adjustment. For the primary end point, an assumed incidence of 4% was used,
1
,
2
 and an a priori threshold was set at 8%. Using a nominal alpha of 2.5% and a power of 80%, the calculated sample size is 315. For the key secondary end point (incidence of CNI within 30 days of the index procedure), an assumed incidence of 0.5% was used, and an a priori threshold was set at 2.7%. The threshold of 2.7% was reported in the VQI in standard‐risk patients undergoing CEA, and the testing strategy was to reject 2.7% with 97.5% confidence. Using a nominal alpha of 2.5% and a power of 80%, the calculated sample size is 300. To account for the larger of the 2 calculated sample sizes and facilitate a more complete interrogation of the clinical data, a maximum of 400 patients treated per protocol was planned for enrollment.

Outcomes through 30 days were analyzed. Per FDA requirements, the PP population comprised the primary analysis group. The pre‐procedure observations served as the baseline values for calculating post‐procedure changes from baseline. Tabulations of summary statistics and statistical analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.2. The statistical analyses were based on data pooled across sites in aggregate. Demographic and clinical parameters were summarized for the ITT and PP population using descriptive statistics (N, mean, median, standard deviation, minimum and maximum value, and 95% 2‐sided confidence limits).

The clinical characteristics of the ITT and PP cohorts enrolled in this study can be found in Table 1. Notably, 7 patients were enrolled who did not meet inclusion/exclusion criteria on review. The protocol mandated that all patients were treated with aspirin, clopidogrel (or equivalent P2Y12 inhibitor for anti‐platelet effect), and a statin before and for at least 30 days following the TCAR procedure. However, 16 patients underwent TCAR and were discovered post‐procedurally to have not started or to have prematurely discontinued their medications. Therefore, these patients were not on dual antiplatelet and statin therapy while undergoing carotid intervention or during the periprocedural follow‐up period and, therefore, were not treated per protocol. These patients are included in the ITT analysis. One patient had placement of 3 stents (1 to treat an access‐site dissection in the CCA), a treatment impacting the ability to evaluate either safety or effectiveness and was considered a protocol deviation. The overall protocol deviation rate was 7.0% (24/344).

The majority of the PP population were male (57.5%) and younger than 75 years of age (75.6%). Forty‐nine (15.3%) PP patients were symptomatic, including 12 (24.5%) treated within 2 weeks of the index event. Of PP patients, 9.0% (29/320) had a prior ipsilateral hemispheric stroke, 9.4% prior ipsilateral hemispheric transient ischemic attack (TIA), and 4.7% prior ipsilateral amaurosis fugax. The anatomic characteristics can be found in Table 2. Approximately 1 in 6 patients (16.2%) had significant calcification of the carotid bifurcation lesion, characterized as 51 to 99% circumferential, 100% circumferential, or protruding into the lumen (Table 2). Almost half (47.8%) had type 2 or 3 aortic arch configuration, and over one‐quarter (28.1%) had atheroma in the arch on imaging. At baseline, the mean NIHSS was 0.2 ± 0.5 and mRS was 0.1 ± 0.3 (Supporting Information Table S3 and Table S4). The procedural data can be found in Table 3. The vast majority of cases (85.2% for ITT) were done under general anesthesia, and the mean procedure time was less than 1 hour (56.6 minutes). The mean “reverse flow” time (duration of proximal CCA occlusion) was 9 minutes. The procedure was completed in 100% of cases, and there were no instances of intolerance to flow reversal in awake patients.

All patients were admitted to the hospital and observed overnight. A total of 342 (99.4%) ITT patients (99.7%; 319/320 PP) completed 30‐day clinic visits. As shown in Table 4, there were 2 strokes (0.6%; 95% confidence interval 0.1%–2.2%) in the PP population of 320 patients. As adjudicated by the Clinical Events Committee, 1 patient developed a major stroke with stent thrombosis on the first postoperative day, treated with open and endovascular thrombectomy. The patient's mRS and NIHSS were both 0 at baseline. At the time of the event NIHSS was 11. At 30‐day follow‐up NIHSS was 6 and mRS was 3. A second patient, with mRS and NIHSS both 0 at baseline, developed a minor stroke with the presentation of contralateral facial muscle spasm on postoperative day 3 that responded to anti‐epileptic medication. It was felt to be because of a reperfusion phenomenon, but was adjudicated by the Clinical Events Committee to be a minor ischemic stroke. The patient's NIHSS was 1 the day after event onset, and both NIHSS and mRS were 0 at 30‐day follow‐up. In the ITT population of 344 patients, there were 3 strokes (0.9%; 95% confidence interval 0.2%–2.5%). In addition to the 2 patients above, a third patient discharged on postoperative day 1 developed a hemorrhagic event with major intracranial hemorrhage on postoperative day 9. The patient's NIHSS and mRS were both 0 at baseline. At the time of the event NIHSS and mRS were 17 and 3, respectively, and they were 2 and 3 at 30‐day follow‐up. Treatment of this patient required withholding of the antiplatelet medications, constituting a major protocol deviation, therefore, disqualifying the patient from the PP cohort. This stroke event is included in the ITT population analysis. There were no deaths or MIs in the entire study population within 30 days of the index procedure (Table 4). The composite S/D/MI rate was 0.6% for the PP cohort and 0.9% for the ITT cohort. Post‐procedure outcomes were stratified by symptom status. All 3 stroke events occurred in asymptomatic patients. The composite S/D/MI rates in the symptomatic and asymptomatic patients were 0% and 0.7% in the PP cohort and 0% and 1.0% in the ITT cohort.

There were 2 incidences of CNI (Table 4), with both patients developing voice hoarseness (consistent with vagus or recurrent laryngeal nerve injuries) within the first 30 days. Both recovered within 6 months with noninvasive treatment (pharmacotherapy and speech therapy). These 2 events yield an incidence rate of 0.6% (PP) and 1‐sided 97.5% confidence of 2.2%, which is less than the a priori threshold of 2.7% (p = 0.0077) (Table 4).

There was a total of 7 (2.0%) access site complications in the ITT cohort, with 1 hematoma that was observed and 6 arterial dissections. Of the dissections, 3 were repaired during the index procedure with additional ENROUTE TSS placement or prolonged low‐pressure angioplasty balloon inflation. The remaining 3 were monitored and did not require any additional intervention. None of the patients with arterial dissection suffered a S/D/MI or CNI. No patient required blood transfusion because of bleeding.

In May 2022, the FDA approved the expansion of TCAR indications to include patients at standard surgical risk for CEA. As a condition of the approval, the ROADSTER 3 trial was established to evaluate prospectively the safety and efficacy of TCAR among the standard‐risk population. In this first‐ever independently adjudicated study evaluating TCAR in standard surgical risk patients, the procedure is shown to be safe with the lowest demonstrated rates of stroke for the treatment of significant carotid artery stenosis in a prospective, independently adjudicated clinical trial.

Carotid revascularization remains an important treatment option for stroke prevention in patients with significant carotid artery disease. Since its introduction in the 1950s, CEA has long been considered the preferred treatment modality for carotid revascularization. However, there are “high‐risk” factors that are associated with increased rates of stroke and death after CEA.
12
 Transfemoral CAS was introduced in the 1990s as a less invasive alternative to CEA, especially for the high‐surgical‐risk population. However, transfemoral CAS has been consistently associated with a significantly higher stroke risk compared to CEA.
13
,
14
,
15
 CMS reimbursement (and, therefore, utilization) of CAS in the United States was limited to symptomatic patients with a ≥70% stenosis with either anatomic or clinical high‐risk factors.

TCAR was introduced in the 2010s, and its clinical effectiveness was clearly demonstrated in the ROADSTER and ROADSTER 2 trials.
1
,
2
 In ROADSTER,
1
 141 patients at high risk for complications from CEA were enrolled at 18 sites between 2012 and 2014. The technical success rate was 99.3% and there were 2 minor strokes contributing to a stroke rate of 1.4%. There were 2 deaths and 1 additional myocardial infarction. In the ROADSTER 2 trial
2
 there were 43 enrolling sites within the United States and European Union with >70% investigators naïve to TCAR per FDA‐directive. Among 632 high‐risk patients enrolled following the protocol (ie, the FDA‐analysis population), technical success was 99.7%. There were strokes in 4 patients (0.6%), 1 patient (0.2%) died of a ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm that had been previously treated by endo‐grafting, and myocardial infarction occurred in 6 patients (0.9%) leading to a composite 30‐day S/D/MI rate of 1.7%. TCAR was shown to be superior to transfemoral CAS with lower stroke and death rates in the real‐world setting.
3
 Compared to CEA, stroke and death rates were similar, but with lower rates of myocardial infarction and CNI.
4
,
5
 As the majority of patients treated with TCAR were those in the high‐risk population, the initial approval and reimbursement for TCAR remained tied to the same “high‐risk” criteria applied to transfemoral CAS.

With the ROADSTER 3 study results, it is reassuring that the excellent clinical trial results of TCAR in high‐surgical‐risk patients can be translated to and improved on for the standard‐risk population. The outcomes confirmed the consistently low rates of stroke with TCAR in the 3 ROADSTER TCAR trials. Furthermore, in addition to the low stroke rate, there were no instances of perioperative myocardial infarction or death in this study. The latter finding likely can be attributed to the potentially lower cardiovascular disease burden seen in a standard surgical risk population.

In this prospective study, where only standard‐surgical risk patients were enrolled, those 80 years old or above were excluded from the trial. This age limit is consistent with most carotid revascularization trials, including the ongoing CREST‐2 clinical trial.
16
 However, it should be noted that TCAR in octogenarians had been tested in the clinical trial setting (28% of ROADSTER patients and 21% of the ROADSTER 2 patients were ≥80 years old) with excellent results. Furthermore, TCAR has also been shown to be effective in the real‐world setting regardless of advanced age.
17
,
18
 Relatively few enrolled patients were symptomatic, perhaps reflecting that these patients tend to have more severe and/or advanced atherosclerotic disease that tends to place them into a category of high clinically defined surgical risk. From an anatomic standpoint, over 10% had type III aortic arch anatomy, which is a standard exclusion criterion for transfemoral CAS trials. Furthermore, almost 30% of patients had the presence of atheroma in the aortic arch, a known potential source of cerebral emboli associated with arch manipulation during peripheral access CAS (transfemoral or transradial/transbrachial). There were 2 patients treated with 50 to 59% stenosis. Both were symptomatic, with 1 having a prior ipsilateral TIA and the other having a prior ipsilateral stroke.

During the completion of patient enrollment for this clinical trial, CMS reconsidered the National Coverage Decision for CAS. In October 2023, CMS expanded reimbursement for all forms of CAS (including TCAR) to include patients at standard risk. This study confirms that TCAR can be safely performed in patients at standard risk for CEA. Without the need for “high‐risk” criteria to qualify for reimbursement, TCAR may be offered to more patients who may simply prefer it as a less invasive option to CEA. Regarding anatomic suitability, and although not the focus of this trial, TCAR is anatomically appropriate for >85% of patients undergoing carotid revascularization.
19
,
20
 Furthermore, the availability of TCAR at a hospital has been associated with a decrease in perioperative adverse cardiovascular (S/D/MI) events across all carotid revascularizations in centers that adopt TCAR.
21



As with other clinical trials, this study does have some limitations that should be addressed. This report only presents the early 30‐day outcomes of the study, and 1‐year outcomes will be subsequently reported to satisfy the FDA‐directed primary end point. It should be emphasized that TCAR is a unique technique for carotid stent delivery that aims to reduce periprocedural complications. The differences between peripheral access‐CAS, TCAR, and CEA are in the 30‐day hazards. There is already longer‐term data supporting the durability of carotid stents for stroke prevention beyond 30 days for up to 10 years from randomized trials of transfemoral CAS versus CEA.
22
,
23
,
24
 Longer‐term extended follow‐up for this study cohort is ongoing. ROADSTER 3 is a single‐arm study without a comparative group, and potential bias can exist without randomization. Although comparisons with other carotid revascularizations are indirect, the accuracy of the absolute adverse event rates in this trial is bolstered by independent neurologic review and independent adjudication of events. It has been estimated that a randomized trial would need to enroll over 100,000 patients to be sufficiently powered to prove superiority between TCAR and CEA using classical clinical end points—a number that far exceeds the scope of this study—but this does not mean there are no clinically meaningful differences between TCAR and CEA. Risk of CNI is an example. Whether revascularization is warranted for subsets of or any asymptomatic patients with significant carotid artery disease is a lingering but critically important question, however, one that ROADSTER 3 was not designed to answer. SPACE 2,
25
 a German national trial intending to definitively answer the question of whether optimized medical therapy alone is sufficient for the management of all or most patients with asymptomatic carotid artery stenosis, unfortunately stopped short of its recruitment target by 3,735 patients because of futility and lack of funding, and is, therefore, significantly underpowered.
26
 CREST2 will investigate the role of intensive medical management alone versus intensive medical management in conjunction with CAS or CEA,
16
 and this trial may provide answers to current unknowns.

ROADSTER 3 included experienced TCAR operators at busy clinical sites, but also included operators with fewer than 10 prior TCAR procedures. TCAR has been clinically available for over 10 years, and there is no reason to include predominantly only TCAR‐naïve operators (as mandated by the FDA for the ROADSTER 2 trial). Furthermore, the learning curve for TCAR has been demonstrated to be quite short, and the procedure can be performed with excellent stroke and mortality rates even in the early stages.
27



Despite the above limitations, the ROADSTER 3 study demonstrates excellent outcomes for TCAR in patients at standard surgical risk. TCAR is a safe and less invasive alternative to CEA that should be considered in the shared decision‐making process with patients needing carotid revascularization.
