# Integrative genetic and functional analysis of autosomal dominant hearing loss in 108 multigenerational families.
**Authors:** Oziębło D, Leja ML, Gan N, Bałdyga N, Toliat MR, Budde BS, Skarżyński H, Ołdak M
**Journal:** J Mol Med (Berl) (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00109-026-02692-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00109-026-02692-8)

## Content

Autosomal dominant hearing loss (ADHL) is a highly heterogeneous Mendelian disorder with numerous causative genes, yet large well-characterized European cohorts remain limited. We investigated 108 families of Polish origin with confirmed dominant inheritance using a tiered strategy integrating targeted sequencing of 237 hearing loss genes, genome-wide linkage analysis, genome sequencing, segregation studies in 437 individuals, and functional validation by minigene splicing assays. This approach established a molecular diagnosis in 52% of families, with 67% of identified pathogenic variants being previously unreported. The genetic architecture proved highly heterogeneous, with recurrent contributions from MYO6, TBC1D24, WFS1, GSDME, and POU4F3 alongside numerous single-family findings. While missense variants predominated, splice-altering and truncating changes were also frequent. Importantly, genome-based analyses uncovered pathogenic mechanisms beyond canonical coding regions, including the first deep intronic EYA4 variant associated with ADHL and a large 3'UTR deletion in ATP11A affecting alternative transcripts. Functional assays confirmed aberrant splicing induced by synonymous, non-canonical splice-site, and deep intronic variants. These findings refine the gene-specific contribution to dominant hearing loss, expand the noncoding mutational landscape, and underscore the importance of integrating genomic and functional analyses for accurate variant interpretation and improved molecular diagnostics of hereditary hearing loss in clinical practice.

Autosomal dominant hearing loss (ADHL) is the second most common form of inherited hearing loss (HL), accounting for ~ 20% of prelingual nonsyndromic cases and constituting the predominant inheritance pattern in postlingual HL forms. ADHL typically manifests after the first decade of life, often as progressive, high-frequency sensorineural HL. To date, 68 genes have been implicated in ADHL, reflecting its high genetic heterogeneity. This form of HL does not have a single major gene or a founder mutation responsible for a large proportion of pathogenic variants, in contrast to the recessive DFNB1-related HL. ADHL encompasses more than 90 subtypes, collectively designated “DFNA” (DeaFNess Autosomal Dominant, ORPHA:90635) with some loci still awaiting gene discovery, including 16 DFNA regions with no identified causative gene [1–3].

Given this complexity, comprehensive genetic testing in ADHL is required. Targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) is standard, often followed by exome (ES) and genome sequencing (GS). In autosomal dominant disorders, a single causative variant is sufficient to explain the phenotype. Many of the detected variants remain difficult to classify, resulting in a high proportion of variants of uncertain significance (VUS), which complicates interpretation, counseling, and clinical decisions [4]. Determining whether a patient carries an ADHL-causative variant requires careful integration of clinical features, familial segregation analysis, and data from variant databases. Functional studies can supply additional evidence and tests such as minigene assays are a valuable tool for assessing the effects of putative splice-altering variants [5].

The overall detection rate of causative variants in ADHL is still limited and varies depending on the study design, including factors such as cohort size, patient selection criteria, and the sequencing methods employed. Most ADHL data come from small cohorts, whereas larger studies usually combine inheritance patterns, which can obscure the genetic architecture of dominant HL [6]. To overcome these limitations, we recruited 108 families with HL across at least three generations, each with at least one confirmed paternal-to-offspring transmission, thereby enriching for cases with a strong likelihood of dominant inheritance. After initial panel-based NGS testing, a second-tier approach combining linkage analysis with GS was applied. This strategy enabled the identification of causative variants in a substantial proportion of families and provides new insights into the genetic architecture of ADHL, including the contribution of noncanonical variants relevant for molecular diagnostics.

The probands (n = 108) and their family members (n = 437) were recruited from the outpatient genetic clinic of the Institute of Physiology and Pathology of Hearing, Warsaw, Poland. All studied families were of Polish origin. Only families with a vertical inheritance pattern of HL in at least three generations and confirmed paternal transmission were selected. Medical and family histories were recorded, including age of HL onset, ototoxic medication exposure, noise exposure, tinnitus, vertigo, and other chronic disorders. Probands with environmental HL causes or DFNB1 mutations were excluded.

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples (or occasionally saliva or buccal swabs). Targeted sequencing of HL genes was performed with a custom 237-gene panel (Supplemental Table S1) (SeqCap EZ Choice, Roche, Switzerland), as previously described [7]. GS libraries were prepared with the TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and sequenced (2 × 150 bp, ~ 30x coverage) on NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina). Single nucleotide variants (SNVs), indels, copy number variants (CNVs) and mitochondrial variants were evaluated. Variant pathogenicity was assessed based on amino acid properties, evolutionary conservation, allele frequency, database annotations and literature. In silico prediction tools, including PolyPhen-2, SIFT, MutationTaster, LRT, CADD, GERP++, and REVEL, were used as described in our previous report [7]. Splicing effects were analyzed using SpliceAI and combined tools from the Alamut Visual Plus Software (Sophia Genetics, Rolle, Switzerland). Presence of detected variants and their segregation with HL in the families were verified by Sanger sequencing. Novel variants were classified according to ACMG/AMP guidelines with further specifications for HL [8–10].

Selected variants in MYO6, GSDME, EYA4 and MYO7A were functionally tested using minigene constructs as previously described [11]. Briefly, patient genomic DNA fragments containing exons of interest and flanking introns were cloned into the pDEST pCI-NEO RHO (provided by Prof. F.P.M. Cremers, Radboud University, the Netherlands). To introduce the c.1127A>G variant in GSDME, site-directed mutagenesis (QuikChange II Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was performed on the pDEST pCI-NEO RHO plasmid containing the corresponding wild-type GSDME fragment. HEK293T cells were cultured in 6-well plates (200,000 cells/well) and transfected with 5 µg of wild-type or mutated constructs per well. After 48 hours, RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis was performed with the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific), according to the provided protocol, using the RHO_e5_cDNA primer (5’-CACCTGGCTCG-3’) and 500 ng RNA per reaction. The generated cDNA was used in PCR, and the product was analyzed by gel electrophoresis and Sanger sequencing. Empty vector controls and controls without reverse transcription were routinely included in all minigene experiments (data not shown).

Nine largest unsolved ADHL families underwent genome-wide SNP genotyping using a high-density (~ 800,000 markers) array (Axiom PMRA, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Genotypes were called with Axiom Analysis Suite v4.0, and linkage analyses were performed as described previously [12]. Multipoint LOD scores were calculated to assess the likelihood of genetic linkage across multiple loci, and haplotypes were reconstructed to refine candidate regions. Genome sequencing (GS) was performed only in the proband if linkage was detected at a known HL locus, or in three distantly related affected family members if the locus was previously unreported.

After targeted panel sequencing, the genetic cause of ADHL was identified in 52% (56/108) of the studied families (Fig. 1A, Supplemental Fig. S1). Among the detected variants, approximately 67% (34/51) were novel (Fig. 1C). The most frequent mutation types were missense 63% (32/51), followed by splice site (19%, 10/51), stop gained (8%, 4/51), frameshift (8%, 4/51), and copy number variants (2%, 1/51) (Fig. 1D). The genetic profile of ADHL was highly diverse, with only a handful of genes being repeatedly implicated in HL development across several families. Causative variants were most common in MYO6 (11 families), TBC1D24 and WFS1 (6 families each), GSDME and POU4F3 (5 families each), and KCNQ4 (4 families). In contrast, there were also several genes in which a causative variant was identified only once in the entire cohort, each in a single family (Fig. 1B; Table 1).

The MYO6 gene, encoding myosin 6, was the most frequently mutated gene in the study, with unique variants identified in 11 different families. Only the complex allele, c.[1295T>C;1309A>C], p.[(Phe432Ser;Asn437His)], was found in two ADHL families. The detected variants included missense, splice site and stop gained mutations. Most were absent from population databases (gnomAD, ExAC, EVS, UK10K) or had not been previously linked to HL. Only two MYO6 stop gained variants, c.1666C>T, p.(Arg556Ter) [13] and c.3496C>T, p.(Arg1166Ter) [14], had been reported before. Most variants had high CADD scores (many > 20), indicating potential deleterious effects (Table 1; Fig. 1B). Minigene assays were performed for novel splice variants regardless of SpliceAI scores (Fig. 4A-C).

Consistent with our earlier reports [15, 16], the substantial contribution of TBC1D24 variants in Polish ADHL patients has been further supported by two additional novel variants, c.905T>G, p.(Leu302Arg) and c.1444T>C, p.(Phe482Leu), in the TBC and TLDc domains. This finding has reinforced the significance of TBC1D24 in ADHL, and, together with WFS1, these genes represent the second most frequent causes of ADHL in our cohort. WFS1 variants were found in six families. All were missense changes in exon 8, with only one previously reported, and none were recurrent (Table 1; Fig. 1B).

GSDME variants were identified in five ADHL families, including the known c.1183+1G>A mutation in three families and two novel variants absent from population databases (gnomAD, ExAC, EVS, UK10K), c.991del and c.1127A>G, in two other families. Their potential pathogenicity was confirmed using minigene assays (Table 1; Fig. 1B). POU4F3 variants were also identified in five families, all of them were novel. One variant was shared by two unrelated families, while the remaining variants were unique. Among the four distinct variants, one was missense and three were deletions of varying length, each introducing a premature stop codon (Table 1; Fig. 1B).

From the unsolved ADHL cases, nine families with the largest number of available HL affected and unaffected individuals were selected for genome-wide SNP genotyping and linkage analysis. This approach revealed linkage to known HL loci in three families, including two containing mapped HL genes. Probands from these two families (F2868 and F6257) were selected for GS.

In F2868, a LOD score of 1.8 indicated linkage to regions on chromosome 6 encompassing DFNA66 and DFNA10, where CD164 and EYA4 genes are located. GS identified only a deep intronic EYA4 variant NM_004100.5:c.805-492A>G in intron 10 that was absent from population databases, segregated fully with HL, and its impact on splicing was confirmed in a minigene assay p.[(=,Asp269GlufsTer19)] (Figs. 2 and 4F; Table 1).

To investigate the impact of selected noncoding and splice-region variants in MYO6, GSDME, EYA4, and MYO7A, minigene assays were performed. For the MYO6 gene, three novel variants were functionally tested. The c.816+1G>A (F13233) variant abolished the natural donor splice site, resulting in complete skipping of exon 9, leading to an in-frame deletion of 55 amino acids p.(Ser218_Arg272del) (Fig. 4A). The second variant, c.1984-1G>A (F3083), caused weakening of the natural acceptor splice site and the concurrent creation of a new acceptor site one nucleotide downstream, leading to a frameshift and premature termination of translation p.(Gly662GlufsTer12) (Fig. 4B). The third variant, c.3281-13A>G (F5550), weakened the natural splice site and activated a cryptic acceptor splice site, generating an aberrant transcript that differed in size from the wild-type product. It leads to the inclusion of a 12-nucleotide fragment from intron 31 into the mature transcript, resulting in the insertion of four additional amino acids p.(Cys1093_Asp1094insGlyLysCysLeu) (Fig. 4C).

In the GSDME gene, two novel variants located in exon 8, c.991del (F11410) and c.1127A>G (F13138) were analyzed. The c.991del variant led to aberrant transcript formation, with skipping of exon 8, resulting in a frameshift and a premature stop codon. Although the c.991del variant alone would suggest a protein change of p.(Cys331AlafsTer18), minigene analysis revealed a splicing-driven outcome, requiring reannotation as p.(Cys331LysfsTer42) (Fig. 4D). Similarly, the c.1127A>G variant, originally predicted to cause a missense substitution p.(Asp376Gly), also promoted exon 8 skipping and led to the same frameshift effect. In contrast, wild-type transcripts consistently retained exon 8, with exon 9 being absent in some of the isoforms (Fig. 4E).

In EYA4, two novel variants, c.805-492 A>G (F2868) and c.1282-12T>A (F13463) were investigated by minigene assays. The deep intronic c.805-492A>G variant caused inclusion of a 179-nt cryptic exon derived from intron 10, resulting in a frameshift and a premature stop codon p.(Asp269GlufsTer19). This aberrant splicing event was observed in one transcript type, while the other transcript was correctly spliced, identical to the wild type (Fig. 4F). The variant completely segregated with HL in the studied family (Fig. 2A). The second EYA4 variant, c.1282-12T>A, generated two aberrant transcripts with frameshifts p.(Glu428PhefsTer5) and p.(Glu428ArgfsTer2). One mutant transcript included a 10-nucleotide insertion from intron 14 and a partial loss of exon 16, while the other mutant transcript exhibited complete skipping of exon 15 and the same partial deletion of exon 16. An identical partial shortening of exon 16 was also observed for some of the wild type transcripts, however, a considerable fraction of the wild type transcripts contained full-length exon 16 (Fig. 4G). These findings indicate that the c.1282-12T>A variant weakens the natural acceptor splice site and creates a new acceptor splice site.

The MYO7A c.2829G>A (F13897) variant, initially classified as a synonymous change p.(Val943=), created a new donor splice site, shortening exon 23 by 78 nucleotides and causing a frameshift with a premature stop codon p.(Asp944ThrfsTer93) (Fig. 4H). The variant fully segregated with HL in the family (Supplemental Fig. S1). These results demonstrate that variants initially interpreted as synonymous or missense can in fact act through splicing disruption, emphasizing the importance of functional assays for accurate variant interpretation.

Our study represents one of the largest single-center European cohorts specifically focused on ADHL, comprising 108 well-characterized multigenerational families and providing new insights into its genetic architecture. Previous European studies have mainly analyzed mixed HL cohorts or focused on individual genes rather than exclusively multigenerational ADHL families of similar or larger size across multiple genes. The largest European studies on the genetics of HL, including those from Germany (n = 305 probands) [17], France (n = 207) [18], the Netherlands (n = 200) [19], Spain (n = 118) [13] and Italy (n = 125) [20] mainly examined mixed cohorts, including DFNB1 cases, without focusing on ADHL. Notably, the Austrian study of 27 ADHL families represents, to our knowledge, the only published European cohort specifically dedicated to ADHL [6].

By contrast, larger ADHL cohorts have been reported in the USA and Japan [21–23]. A multiethnic cohort published by Sloan-Heggen et al. included 141 ADHL cases [21], while the nationwide study by Iwasa et al. analyzed 75 patients with ADHL [22] and Moteki et al. reported on 51 ADHL patients [23]. Additional Japanese studies focused on single genes such as KCNQ4, POU4F3, TECTA, MYO6, and EYA4 [14, 24–27]. Similarly, collaborative studies conducted between research groups in the USA and Spain analyzed large Spanish ADHL cohorts, including 372 families screened for TECTA mutations and 531 unrelated probands tested for EYA4 variants [28, 29]. As in the Japanese studies, these efforts were limited to single-gene analyses rather than cohort-wide assessment of ADHL etiology. In contrast, our investigation provides comprehensive data on ADHL in a large Polish cohort. It represents the largest single-center European dataset exclusively focused on ADHL and ranks among the most extensive well-characterized cohorts. This makes it a valuable reference for comparing detection rates, variant spectra, and gene-specific contributions across populations.

Previous reports on multigene panel testing for HL have shown highly variable detection rates, ranging roughly from 13% to over 60% [30]. In studies where patient cohorts were stratified and ADHL cases could be analyzed separately, diagnostic yields varied with values of 19%, (15/79, Germany) [17], 27.3% (18/66, The Netherlands) [19], 35% (18/51, Japan) [23], 50% (141 ADHL patients, USA) [21], 59.3% (16/27, Austria) [6] to 61% (46/75, Japan) [22]. Our 52% diagnostic yield (56/108 families) using a 237-gene panel falls within upper range reported globally, comparable to US and Japanese studies [21, 22]. Over 60% of the identified variants were novel, while de novo variants could not be detected as only familial cases were included in the study. Consequently, our study does not provide insight into which genes related to HL are most frequently affected by de novo mutations [31].

While most large-scale studies consistently highlight MYO6, TECTA, WFS1, ACTG1, POU4F3, MYO7A, KCNQ4, MYH14, and COL11A2 as the major contributors to ADHL [4, 6, 9, 21–23, 32], our findings largely support these observations. In our cohort, recurrent involvement of MYO6, WFS1, POU4F3, KCNQ4 and TECTA was confirmed, underscoring their established role in ADHL development. At the same time, we observed a substantial role for GSDME, although it is rather considered a gene of residual impact particularly in patients of European origin [4]. We also confirmed the substantial contribution of TBC1D24 variants to ADHL in Polish patients, consistent with our previous reports [15, 33] and since finishing the study, we have identified additional 3 ADHL families with TBC1D24-related HL (data not shown). These findings indicate that, alongside the core ADHL genes consistently described across populations, additional contributors such as TBC1D24 and GSDME may reflect population-specific differences. Unlike other groups, we did not find pathogenic variants in ACTG1, MYH14 or COL11A2 [4]. Several genes that are typically rare in other studies were also identified only in single families in our cohort, confirming that they are less prevalent contributors to ADHL. Among these, CD164, GRHL2, LMX1A, NLRP3 and PTPRQ have been reported in our previous publications [12, 34–36]. Here, particular attention deserves ATP11A and EYA4, as the mutations in these two genes were detected through GS and are located outside the coding regions of the main transcripts [37].

To date, only three pathogenic ATP11A variants have been reported in association with HL, all affecting the C-terminal region of the protein. Functionally, all reported ATP11A variants appear to converge on a loss of normal ATP11A flippase activity, resulting in defective phospholipid transport and disruption of cell membrane homeostasis. Using the MANE select transcript (ATP11A-202, NM_015205, NP_056020) as a reference, a German family carried a 5.5-kb deletion encompassing the last coding exon and part of the 3’UTR [38]. A Newfoundland family carried a G>A substitution in the 3’UTR, while two Israeli families had a short duplication affecting coding exon 28 [39]. Here, we report a fourth ATP11A variant, a deletion of more than 5 kb, also located in the 3’UTR, which was not expected to affect the coding sequence of the MANE select transcript, similar to the Newfoundland variant. In silico analysis also did not predict its impact on splicing. However, when considering other ATP11A isoforms, discussed in the above studies, our variant removes the last exon of the ATP11A-201 (NM_032189, NP_115565), which is partially coding and in case of the short ATP11-203 isoform, it deletes the penultimate exon, the last coding exon of the transcript. These findings suggest that, although the MANE select transcripts remain the primary reference for interpreting gene variants, focusing exclusively on them may overlook potentially relevant effects on alternative isoforms. Variants located outside the coding regions of MANE select transcripts may disrupt coding exons in alternative isoforms, which could be relevant for inner ear function.

Mutations in the EYA4 gene are a well-established cause of ADHL (DFNA10). Despite a broad mutational spectrum, no deep intronic variants have previously been implicated in EYA4-related HL [27, 29]. Here, we report the first such variant (c.805-492A>G), which we functionally confirmed induces aberrant splicing, leading to a premature stop codon. Despite the mutation a fraction of correctly spliced transcripts is still generated, demonstrating leaky splicing. The resulting limited amount of normal protein can be sufficient to preserve its partial function and modulate the variability in HL phenotype. Similar rescue effects have been observed in other genes, including the hearing-related OTOGL as well as non-hearing-related genes such as GAA or ABCA4 [40–42].

Although minigene assays may not always fully replicate endogenous splicing due to differences in genomic context and regulatory elements, they frequently reflect in vivo patterns and are especially valuable when patient-derived RNA and disease-relevant tissues are unavailable. Analysis of c.1282-12T>A, novel EYA4 non-canonical splice site variant demonstrates that this region generates two transcript forms in the wild type background (a full-length and a shortened exon 16 isoform), as observed in earlier studies in human lymphoblastoid and lens epithelial cell lines and independent minigene experiments [29, 43]. The c.1282-12T>A variant shifts the splicing pattern toward transcripts that include the shortened exon 16, indicating preferential selection of the alternative splice site. This finding illustrates the intrinsic flexibility of EYA4 splicing but also supports the usefulness of minigene assays for investigating splicing patterns.

Our targeted multigene panel was designed to cover the full coding sequence of ADHL genes, with ± 50 bp of intron-exon boundaries and complete 3’UTR and 5’UTR with additional 5’ flank of 250 bp, allowing detection of both coding variants and regulatory variants located nearby the coding sequence. In this study, missense substitutions were the predominant pathogenic variants, while splice-altering variants and truncating changes (including both stop gained and frameshift mutations) occurred at comparable frequencies. This distribution of causative variants is consistent with previous reports on ADHL, which show that missense mutations are predominant in WFS1, KCNQ4, TECTA, and ACTG1 [21, 24, 26, 44, 45], whereas in other major ADHL genes, including those encoding myosins and POU4F3, the mutation spectrum is more diverse [14, 25]. In the context of these frequently observed splice-disrupting variants, it is noteworthy that we also identified two novel pathogenic variants in GSDME. Both variants lead to the same aberrant splicing outcome, skipping of exon 8, irrespective of whether the underlying change was a single nucleotide deletion or a transition. In addition, a previously described splicing mutation in this gene, the canonical donor site variant c.1183+1G>A, was detected in three other families from our cohort, and is also known to cause skipping of exon 8. Together, these findings reveal a convergent mechanism in which diverse GSDME variants disrupt proper exon 8 inclusion, consistent with the observations of Booth et al. who demonstrated that distinct exonic and intronic alterations in GSDME disrupt splicing enhancers or silencers within exon 8 and ultimately trigger its omission, producing the constitutively active truncated protein responsible for HL [46].

In summary, this study delineates the genetic architecture of ADHL in a large European multigenerational cohort. Through integrated genomic and functional analyses, we expand the mutational spectrum of ADHL, confirm the major contribution of recurrent genes, and highlight the extensive locus and allelic heterogeneity underlying the disorder. The identification of splice-disrupting variants, including synonymous and deep intronic changes, underscores the importance of interrogating noncanonical regions in molecular diagnostics. Together, these findings refine variant interpretation and advance the understanding of hereditary HL.
