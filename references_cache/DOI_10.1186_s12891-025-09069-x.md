(CACP) syndrome is a rare genetic disease that impacts

with synovial hyperplasia, coxa vara deformity, and non -

Abstract
Camptodactyly-arthropathy-coxa vara-pericarditis (CACP) syndrome is an inherited autosomal recessive disorder 
resulting from a mutation in the PRG4 gene located on chromosome one. This mutation leads to either a 
deficiency or dysfunction of a glycoprotein known as lubricin, which plays a crucial role in inhibiting synovial 
inflammation and lubricating synovial joints and visceral cavities. CACP syndrome is characterized by a tetrad of 
manifestations, including congenital or early-onset camptodactyly, childhood-onset non-inflammatory arthropathy 
accompanied by synovial hyperplasia, coxa vara deformity, and non-inflammatory pericardial effusion. 
This case report details the medical history of a 12-year-old boy diagnosed with CACP syndrome, following the 
identification of a novel homozygous frameshift variant in the PRG4 gene. The patient first exhibited symptoms at 
10 days of age with respiratory distress due to pleural effusion and later underwent pericardiectomy for significant 
pericardial effusion. At age 2, progressive joint effusion led to an initial diagnosis of juvenile idiopathic arthritis. 
Despite receiving treatment modalities such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), methotrexate, and 
corticosteroids, the patient experienced minimal improvement. The persistence of progressive joint manifestations, 
coupled with a lack of favorable response to treatment, prompted further evaluations, which ultimately confirmed 
the diagnosis of CACP syndrome. This case illustrates diagnostic challenges and rare early cardiac involvement. It 
discusses the differential diagnosis from juvenile idiopathic arthritis and skeletal dysplasia, as well as the role of 
genetic testing in unresolved cases.

Case presentation
The 12-year-old boy, the second child of a consanguine -
ous couple, presented at the rheumatology clinic with 
complaints of joint deformities and difficulties in per -
forming daily activities.  There was no family history of 
musculoskeletal and cardiac disorders. His medical jour -
ney began when he was just 10 days old, characterized by 
respiratory distress due to mild pleural effusion. By the 
time he reached 3 months, he exhibited flexion contrac -
tures at the elbows, which his mother first noticed while 
dressing him. She remarked that he was quite restless 
during clothing changes. At the age of 2 years, the patient 
presented with persistent respiratory distress, prompting 
an echocardiogram that revealed moderate pericardial 
effusion. Subsequently, the patient underwent pericar -
diectomy to attain symptomatic relief. Histopathological 
analysis indicated the presence of a non-inflammatory 
effusion. Afterward, between the ages of 3 and 8, pro -
gressive joint effusion and stiffness in knees, ankles, and 
wrists, accompanied by contractures—especially camp -
todactyly of 4th-5th fingers and clinodactyly of 3rd gradu -
ally developed (Fig. 1). He was unable to play with other 
children and to perform his daily activities; the patient's 
motor skills, including writing abilities, were impacted, 
and deformities made him isolated. Laboratory tests 
showed normal values for blood count, erythrocyte sedi -
mentation rate (ESR), and C-reactive protein, and were 
negative for rheumatoid markers, including antinuclear 
antibody (ANA) and rheumatoid factor (RF) (Table  1). 
Radiographic features demonstrate skeletal abnormali -
ties (Fig. 2). Joint effusion, contracture, and finger defor -
mities led to the diagnosis of juvenile idiopathic arthritis 
(JIA). Treatment with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory 
drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen (40 mg/kg/day) and 
disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) like 
methotrexate (15 mg/m 2/w) was initiated. Despite fre -
quent follow-ups, the arthritis did not resolve; the patient 
showed no significant improvement. An attempted 
course of corticosteroids (prednisolone 7.5 mg/day) 
failed to alleviate the joint limitations and contractures.

Discussion
The CACP syndrome, a rare inherited autosomal reces -
sive condition, was first described by Bulutlar et al. in 
1986 [8, 9]. This syndrome is caused by a mutation in the 
PRG4 gene located on chromosome 1 and is often linked 
to consanguinity in approximately 65.7% of cases [4, 10].
The PRG4 gene encodes Lubricin, a mucin-rich glyco -
protein crucial for maintaining the homeostasis of artic -
ular cartilage, meniscus, and tendons. It is produced by 
synovial fibroblasts and chondrocytes [11, 12].
Characterized by a tetrad of camptodactyly, noninflam-
matory arthropathy, coxa vara deformity, and pericardial 
effusion, CACP syndrome presents unique challenges in 
diagnosis and management [ 13]. Camptodactyly, affect -
ing the majority of patients, is often the initial indica -
tor of the disease and can be managed nonoperatively 
through methods such as splinting. Although typically 
bilateral and congenital, camptodactyly may also mani -
fest in early childhood [6].
In a case series from Saudi Arabia involving 7 CACP 
patients, camptodactyly was commonly the first sign [14]. 
However, in our case, respiratory distress due to mild pleu-
ral effusion was the presenting symptom at just ten days of 
age, and then pericardial effusion appeared at the age of two.
Arthropathy involvement in CACP syndrome typically 
emerges early in childhood, affecting large joints such as 
knees, wrists, elbows, ankles, and hips and sometimes 
being misinterpreted as juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA), 
resulting in misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatments 
[13]. Our case was initially misdiagnosed as JIA due to 
arthritis, joint contractures, and camptodactyly, and treated 
with various medications that failed to yield improvements.

Coxa vara, observed in up to 90% of CACP patients, 
is characterized by femoral changes (short femoral neck 
and flat, irregular femoral head) in more than half of the 
patients. Bahabri et al. suggested that the coxa vara defor-
mity becomes clinically apparent with increasing age. 
Fluid-filled intra-osseous bone cysts, resulting from joint 
capsule herniation, are characteristic of this syndrome 
[1]. However, as we discussed earlier, coxa vara did not 
become apparent in our patient.
Pericarditis, a less common manifestation of CACP 
syndrome, occurs in approximately 30% of patients and 
is usually mild and self-limited but may necessitate emer-
gency interventions in severe cases [ 16, 17]. In our expe -
rience, a pericardiectomy was performed on the patient 
at the age of 2 due to pericardial involvement.
MRI plays a crucial role in differentiating CACP from 
JIA, particularly in cases presenting with joint effusion. 
In CACP , MRI typically reveals large joint effusions 
and synovial thickening without post-contrast synovial 
enhancement, bone marrow edema, or erosive changes, 
features commonly seen in JIA. A characteristic rim-
pattern enhancement of the synovial capsule may be 
observed in CACP , distinguishing it from the diffuse, 
solid enhancement pattern of JIA [18].
Unfortunately, the medical treatment of this syndrome 
is not effective. Regular physical therapy and occupa -
tional therapy are the mainstay interventions for reduc -
ing the adverse effects of the disease and improving the 
quality of life.
CACP syndrome is frequently misdiagnosed as JIA and 
inappropriately treated with immunosuppressive agents 
despite its noninflammatory nature. Patients with CACP 
typically exhibit joint swelling without signs of inflam -
mation, with normal laboratory markers such as ESR and 
CRP . Synovial histology often reveals minimal mononu -
clear infiltration, further supporting the noninflamma -
tory nature of the syndrome [4].
Conclusion
In conclusion, this case report highlights the complex -
ity and severity of CACP syndrome, which can present 
with various clinical symptoms, including joint effusion, 
contractures, pericarditis, and pleuritis. Recognizing 
early cardiac manifestations that may necessitate surgi -
cal intervention although uncommon, holds considerable 
clinical significance. The diagnosis of CACP syndrome 
should be considered in patients who exhibit symptoms 
of joint involvement, pericarditis, and pleural effusion, 
particularly in cases involving consanguinity or when it is 
misidentified as juvenile rheumatoid arthritis with atypi -
cal presentations. This misdiagnosis often arises in the 
absence of clinical or laboratory evidence of inflamma -
tion and unresponsiveness to immunosuppressive ther -
apy. The presence of clinodactyly that is rarely reported 
in the literatures, could support the phenotypic variabil -
ity of this syndrome. This report emphasizes the impor -
tance of accurate diagnosis and management of CACP 
syndrome to improve the quality of life for those affected.
