# Differential Response and Resistance to KRAS-Targeted Therapy.
**Authors:** Liu Z, Lenz HJ, Yu J, Zhang L
**Journal:** Mol Carcinog (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mc.23908](https://doi.org/10.1002/mc.23908)

## Content

KRAS is the most frequently mutated oncogene. In epithelial malignancies such as lung, colorectal, and pancreatic tumors, KRAS is mutated in 25 to above 90% cases. KRAS was considered undruggable for over three decades until the recent development of covalent inhibitors targeting the KRAS G12C mutant. The recent approval of the KRAS G12C inhibitors sotorasib and adagrasib has ushered in a new era of KRAS-targeted therapy. Despite this success, a major challenge in KRAS-targeted therapy is intrinsic and acquired resistance to KRAS inhibitors. Clinical studies have shown that many patients with KRAS G12C cancers did not respond to sotorasib and adagrasib. Colorectal cancer, in particular, has a markedly lower response rate to KRAS G12C inhibitors compared to non-small cell lung cancer. Furthermore, the therapeutic response to KRAS G12C inhibition was short-lived, with quick emergence of acquired resistance. In this review, we summarize several major themes that have emerged from recent clinical and preclinical studies on the mechanisms of intrinsic and acquired resistance to KRAS-targeted therapy in colorectal, lung, and pancreatic cancers. We also discuss various combination strategies for targeting these mechanisms to overcome resistance to KRAS inhibitors.

Discovered in the 1980s as one of the first identified proto-oncogenes [1, 2], KRAS encodes a member of RAS family small GTPases, which also includes NRAS, HRAS, and MRAS [3, 4]. RAS protein family functions as a binary switch for signaling downstream of multiple receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), such as epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) (Figure 1). KRAS is the most studied family member, and the activity of KRAS is controlled by the conversion between the active GTP-bound and inactive GDP-bound states. This conversion is regulated by guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs) such as Son of Sevenless Homolog 1 (SOS1), which promote the exchange of bound GDP with GTP, and by GTPase activating proteins (GAPs) such as neurofibromin 1 (NF1), which stimulate the GTPase activity of RAS to convert bound GTP to GDP [4]. SHP2 is a tyrosine phosphatase that functions as a key mediator between SOS1 and RTKs to promote the activation of KRAS [4]. KRAS regulates many downstream signaling pathways, most notably RAF/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/extracellular signal-related kinase (ERK) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) cascades [3, 5]. KRAS also has numerous downstream effectors such as Ral GTPase and horizontally interacts with various signaling pathways, playing a pivotal role in cell signaling and proliferation (Figure 1) [6, 7].

KRAS is the most frequently mutated oncogene, accounting for approximately 85% of all RAS mutations in 20–25% of all cancers [8–10]. KRAS mutations are most commonly found in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC; 30~35%), pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC; ~90%) and colorectal cancer (CRC; 45~50%) [10–12], and are associated with a worse prognosis [13–15]. Among these mutations, about 95% are found in codon G12 and G13 [12]. While G12D and G12V are most dominant in CRC and PDAC, G12C and G12V are most prevalent in NSCLC [10]. Mutations in G12, G13, or Q61 residue in the active site can lock KRAS in the GTP-bound form by impeding the hydrolysis to GDP catalyzed by GAPs, thus keeping downstream signaling constitutively active [16, 17]. In addition to mutations, gene amplification is another common KRAS alteration in human cancer [10].

KRAS remained undruggable for more than three decades after its discovery for many reasons [8]. A major challenge is that KRAS, unlike many kinases, lacks a deep active pocket amenable to targeting [18, 19]. Its spherical conformation and a shallow binding site for GTP and GDP makes it extremely difficult to design on-site small-molecule inhibitors [20]. A new era for targeting mutant KRAS started in 2013 when Shokat and colleagues reported a mutant-specific and allosteric targeting strategy [21]. They identified a disordered switch-II pocket (SIIP) caused by the mutated cysteine residue in KRAS G12C [21]. This unique structural feature allows the design of small-molecule inhibitors that specifically dock in the disordered SIIP to form a covalent bond with cysteine 12, irreversibly locking KRAS G12C in the GDP-bound inactive state [21]. This strategy eventually led to the development of sotorasib (AMG510) and adagrasib (MRTX849) [22, 23], which showed remarkable preclinical and clinical efficacy [24–27] and subsequently gained accelerated approval in 2021 and 2022 as monotherapy against KRAS G12C NSCLC. Several other KRAS G12C inhibitors were also developed using a similar strategy and exhibited promising results in preclinical studies and early clinical trials, including divarasib (GDC6036), olomorasib (LY3537982), garsorasib (D-1553), opnurasib (JDQ443), and glecirasib (JAB21822) (Table 1) [28–33].

Identification of a similar SIIP as an inhibitory site also provides a solution for targeting other KRAS mutants. For example, MRTX1133 has recently been developed for targeting KRAS G12D, the most common KRAS mutant in human cancer [34–36]. It binds to the SIIP in KRAS G12D through noncovalent bonds [34], instead of the covalent bond of G12C inhibitors. MRTX1133 is currently being evaluated in a Phase I/II clinical trial (NCT05737706). Several other compounds targeting G12D, such as HRS4642, RMC-9805 and the first-in-class KRAS G12D degrader ASP3082, are also currently in clinical evaluations (Table 2) [37–41].

More recently, pan-RAS inhibitors capable of targeting different RAS variants, including KRAS mutants and wild-type KRAS, NRAS, and HRAS, have gained much attention [42]. RMC-6236 and RMC-7977, which are tri-complex inhibitors and derivatives of sanglifehrin A, a polyketide natural product, have been developed by Revolution Medicines [43]. In preclinical studies, these inhibitors demonstrated broad-spectrum activities against multiple KRAS mutants and RAS isoforms across different cancer models [43, 44]. RMC-7977 also showed efficacy in NSCLC preclinical models with acquired resistance to sotorasib/adagrasib [45]. Its analog RMC-6236 has entered early clinical trials (Table 2) [44].

Despite the recent success and promise, a major challenge in KRAS-targeted therapy is the differential response and resistance to KRAS inhibitors. In the CodeBreaK100 and KRYSTAL-1 clinical trials that led to the approval of sotorasib and adagrasib, respectively, over 60–90% of KRAS G12C patients did not respond well to sotorasib or adagrasib (Table 1). These studies revealed a clear cancer-type difference. CRC is much less responsive to KRAS G12C inhibitors compared to NSCLC. For sotorasib, an overall response rate (ORR) of 7.1–9.7% was observed in patients with advanced CRC, compared to 32.2–37.1% in those with advanced NSCLC (Table 1) [26, 46, 47]. For adagrasib, an ORR was 23.0% in CRC, compared to 42.8% in NSCLC (Table 1) [27, 48]. The duration of response (DOR) and progression-free survival (PFS) of CRC patients treated with sotorasib or adagrasib were also lower than those in NSCLC patients (Table 1) [48]. Furthermore, clinical trial results on other KRAS G12C inhibitors consistently showed lower ORR in CRC than NSCLC patients (Table 1). These results suggest intrinsic and tumor-type-specific resistance mechanism to KRAS G12C inhibitors. Even in NSCLC patients, the response to KRAS G12C inhibitors was often short-lived, with a DOR of 10.9–11.1 months and PFS of 6.3–6.8 months for sotorasib, and a DOR of 8.5 months and PFS of 6.5 months for adagrasib (Table 1). These results indicate the rapid development of acquired resistance to KRAS G12C inhibitors [18, 26, 27, 49]. The differential response to KRAS G12C inhibitors were demonstrated by many preclinical studies on KRAS G12C-mutant CRC and NSCLC cell lines and tumor models [24, 25, 32, 50]. In this review, we summarize several major themes that have emerged from recent clinical and preclinical studies on the resistance mechanisms of KRAS-mutant cancers to KRAS-targeted therapy, highlighting the specific resistance mechanisms in CRC.

Genetic alterations, especially secondary RAS mutations, have been identified as one of the most common mechanisms of acquired resistance to KRAS inhibitors in patients [49, 51–55]. Analysis of matched pre- and post-treatment specimens from 43 (36 NSCLC, 3 CRC, and 4 other) sotorasib-treated patients revealed treatment-induced genetic alterations in 27 patients, including 4 with secondary KRAS mutations (G12D, G12V, G12F, V8L) and 3 with copy-number gains of KRAS [51]. Similarly, secondary KRAS mutations (G12D, G12R, G12V, G12W, G13D, Q61H, R68S, H95D, H95Q, H95R, and Y96C) and gene amplifications were detected in 9 out of 38 (27 NSCLC, 10 CRC, and 1 other) patients treated with adagrasib through the analysis of tumor tissues or circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) [49]. Acquired mutations of NRAS, BRAF, RTK, and MYC were also found in patients treated with sotorasib or adagrasib monotherapy [49, 51, 53, 54]. ctDNA analysis showed that 16 (3 NSCLC, 9 CRC, and 4 other) of 29 patients (8 NSCLC, 16 CRC, and 5 other) treated with divarasib acquired at least one genetic alteration that may mediate therapeutic resistance [29]. The genetic alterations identified in these studies are highly heterogeneous and may mediate therapeutic resistance via diverse mechanisms, such as changing inhibitor binding affinity, reactivating KRAS activity, and bypassing KRAS-mediated signaling [54, 56]. Levels of KRAS G12C in ctDNA were found to be associated with the response of NSCLC and CRC patients treated with sotorasib or divarasib, suggesting KRAS gene amplification as a resistance mechanism [57, 58].

The higher frequency of acquired genetic alterations also explains the reduced response of CRC to KRAS G12C inhibition than NSCLC. In the biomarker analysis of CodeBreaK100 trial for sotorasib, 73% (33/45) of CRC patients were found to acquire at least one genetic alteration, compared to 28% (19/67) of NSCLC patients. Among these patients, 16% (7/45) of CRC patients gained secondary RAS alterations, compared to 3% (2/67) in NSCLC [59]. Similarly, a higher frequency of genetic alterations was identified by ctDNA analysis in adagrasib-treated CRC patients (4/5) than NSCLC patients (2/7) [49]. In a recent study integrating ctDNA profiling data from 22 patients with KRAS G12C PDAC treated with sotorasib or adagrasib, along with previously published ctDNA profiling results of patients treated with sotorasib, adagrasib, or divarasib across many tumor types, it was observed that treatment emergent KRAS alterations were more common in CRC (41%) but less so in NSCLC (21%) and PDAC (29%) [55]. Furthermore, emerging genetic alterations have been shown to mediate resistance to KRAS G12C inhibitors combined with EGFR inhibitors in CRC patients. Analysis of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) in 12 CRC patients treated with sotorasib/panitumumab or adagrasib/cetuximab combinations identified secondary RAS mutations, KRAS amplifications, or both in 58.3% of sampled patients [53].

The functional role of the identified genetic alterations was verified in preclinical models. Secondary RAS mutations and other genetic alterations were detected in various preclinical models with acquired resistance to KRAS G12C inhibitors [49, 51, 52, 55]. Expression of the identified KRAS mutants was sufficient to confer resistance to KRAS G12C inhibitors in KRAS G12C cancer cells [49, 51, 60]. Together, these studies demonstrate that the secondary mutations and/or genetic alterations lead to target-specific acquired resistance to KRAS inhibitors. Understanding how specific genetic alterations in the resistance to KRAS inhibitors will be beneficial for improved targeting of KRAS-mutant cancers [61].

KRAS-mutant tumors are often insensitive to KRAS-targeted therapy due to feedback reactivation of upstream RTKs, which are crucial for cancer cell signaling [62, 63]. In clinical settings, KRAS mutations are widely used as biomarkers to determine the use and efficacy of RTK-targeted therapies, including those targeting EGFR and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) [64, 65]. Genetic alterations in components of RTK signaling pathways are frequently found in cancer and can lead to constitutive activation of downstream pathways such as RAS/RAF/MAPK, PI3K/AKT, and JAK/STAT [66, 67]. Adaptive feedback activation of various RTKs is ubiquitously involved in mediating resistance to targeted therapy. For example, EGFR activation mediates resistance of CRC to the BRAF V600E inhibitor vemurafenib through reactivation of downstream signaling [68, 69]. Rebound signaling of fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) was found in KRAS-mutant NSCLC cells treated with the MEK inhibitor trametinib, leading to reactivation of RAS-mediated signaling [70]. It is therefore not surprising that RTKs function as crucial mediators of response to KRAS-targeted therapy [71]. Genetic alterations in RTKs are widespread and heterogenous. Amplifications or gene fusions of MET, EGFR, FGFR2, FGFR3, and ALK have been detected in patients with acquired resistance to sotorasib and adagrasib monotherapy [49, 51].

The role of RTKs in resistance to KRAS-targeted therapy was demonstrated in many preclinical studies. Treating NSCLC and PDAC cells with ARS-1620, a second-generation KRAS G12C inhibitor, can induce elevated expression of various RTKs, such as EGFR, FGFR, IGFR1, MET, VEGFR1, PDGFRA/B, and ERBB2/3, as well as their ligands, including EGF, FGF2, and VEGF A/B [72]. EGFR activation was found to be responsible for the adaptive response to ARS-1620 in NSCLC cells [73]. Amplification of MET was observed in KRAS G12C NSCLC cells with acquired resistance to sotorasib [74]. Feedback activation of EGFR, c-MET, and EphA2 was also found in cell lines resistant to another KRAS inhibitor, AZ1569 [75]. Additionally, RTK activation is responsible for elevated downstream signaling [73, 76]. Rapid reactivation of RAS signaling, mediated by EGFR, FGFR, HER2, and c-MET, was detected in multiple KRAS G12C CRC, PDAC, and NSCLC cell lines treated with ARS-1620 and sotorasib [71]. Increased levels of phospho-EGFR and phospho-IGFR were detected in sotorasib-resistant KRAS G12C NSCLC, PDAC, and CRC cell lines [76].

RTK activation upon KRAS G12C inhibition could be mediated by elevated ligand secretion. For instance, some cells treated with KRAS G12C inhibitors exhibited elevated secretion of heparin-binding epidermal growth factor (HBEGF), which promoted an adaptive response by activating EGFR and shifting oncogenic KRAS more toward the GTP-bound active state [73]. RTKs were also implicated in resistance to KRAS G12C inhibition associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [77, 78]. Mesenchymal-like KRAS G12C NSCLC cells treated with sotorasib showed induction of RTKs, including FGFR1 and ERBB3, and stable PI3K signaling maintained by the insulin-like growth factor (IGFR)/insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS1) axis [77]. Phospho-proteomics analysis revealed that ERBB2/3 signaling mediates an adaptive response to KRAS G12C inhibition in epithelial cells, while feedback activation of FGFR and AXL was found in mesenchymal cells [78].

The activation of RTKs may explain highly prevalent intrinsic resistance of CRC to KRAS G12C inhibitors. Compared with other cancer types, CRC generally has a high basal level of RTK activity, which may play a more dominant role in determining the response to KRAS-targeted therapy [79, 80]. EGFR is the major RTK responsible for the resistance to KRAS inhibitors in CRC [79]. CRCs with KRAS G12C have higher rates of basal EGFR activation compared to other cancers [80]. Additionally, HER2 is commonly amplified or overexpressed in 7% to 47.4% of CRC patients, activating downstream signaling in a similar manner [81–83]. CRC is heterogenous and can be classified by 4 Consensus Molecular Subtypes (CMS). Both CMS1 (14%; defective DNA mismatched repair) and CMS3 (13%; metabolically deregulated) subtypes exhibit hyperactivated RTK and MAPK signaling [84, 85]. Interestingly, CMS3 CRCs also have the highest frequency (68%) of KRAS mutations [84], suggesting that this subtype may have a poorer response to KRAS inhibitors.

The above findings support a critical role of crosstalk of RTKs and mutant KRAS in resistance to targeted therapies in CRC and provide a strong rationale in their co-targeting [86]. Preclinical studies have shown that inhibiting EGFR can overcome the intrinsic resistance of CRC to KRAS-targeted therapy [79]. Encouragingly, in June 2024, the US FDA granted accelerated approval to the combination of adagrasib and cetuximab for KRAS G12C CRC, based on the results of KRYSTAL-1 clinical trial [48]. This is the first approved KRAS G12C inhibitor-based therapy in CRC. In January 2025, the US FDA approved sotorasib with panitumumab for patients with KRAS G12C metastatic CRC, based on the results of CodeBreaK 300 (NCT05198934) [87]. Other combinations of KRAS G12C and EGFR inhibitors, such as divarasib/cetuximab, are being evaluated in several ongoing trials and have shown promising results [88].

Unfortunately, RTK-mediated resistance is highly heterogeneous even within a single type of tumors, limiting the efficacy of targeting any single RTK. Therefore, targeting SHP2, which functions between KRAS and RTKs, has emerged as a promising strategy. SHP2 allosteric inhibition can significantly enhance the inhibition of multiple components in RTK signaling, including EGFR, BRAF, MEK1/2, and oncogenic KRAS [72]. TNO155, a small-molecular SHP2 inhibitor, has demonstrated efficacy when combined with KRAS G12C inhibitors in various preclinical models [72, 89, 90]. SOS1 inhibitors, such as BI3406, have been shown to overcome resistance of KRAS inhibitors in KRAS G12C NSCLC and CRC preclinical models [91]. Multiple clinical trials for co-targeting KRAS and SHP2 or SOS1, such as adagrasib combined with TNO155, are ongoing (NCT04330664) [92].

In addition to shifting oncogenic KRAS to the active GTP-bound state, feedback activation of RTKs can also activate wild-type RAS, leading to the bypass of oncogenic KRAS and resistance to KRAS-targeted therapy (Figure 1) [93, 94]. In KRAS G12C PDAC, NSCLC and CRC cells treated with ARS-1620 or AMG510, RTK-mediated accumulation of active wild-type NRAS and HRAS led to reactivation of MAPK signaling [93]. Targeting the upstream SHP2, which integrates multiple RTK signals leading to RAS activation, blocked the activation of NRAS and HRAS and the downstream effector signaling [93]. While the recently developed MRTX1133 was effective in KRAS G12D preclinical models [36], its therapeutic effect in CRC was rendered by the feedback activation of EGFR via wild-type HRAS and NRAS [94], similar to KRAS G12C inhibitors. KRAS G12C inhibition led to induced expression of MRAS, which binds to SHOC2, a leucine-rich repeat protein that is a positive regulator of the RAS pathway, promoting feedback activation of MAPK signaling [95]. Furthermore, mutant KRAS can also activate wild-type RAS through SOS1 and subsequently RTK downstream signaling, leading to resistance to KRAS-targeted therapies [96].

The differential sensitivity of NSCLC, PDAC, and CRC to KRAS-targeted therapy could be partially explained by their differential dependency on oncogenic KRAS signaling, which may be modulated by wild-type RAS [97]. An integrated model for predicting KRAS dependency has recently been developed and may be useful for precision therapy and treatment planning [98]. Together, these studies indicate that KRAS-mutant tumors could increase their dependency on RTK and activate wild-type RAS and vice versa upon KRAS inhibition, thereby evading its therapeutic effect.

Hippo signaling pathway, a kinase cascade that regulates many physiological processes such as organ size determination, cell proliferation, and tissue regeneration, has been shown to play a key role in resistance to KRAS inhibition [99]. Two central downstream effectors of the Hippo signaling pathway include Yes-associated protein (YAP) and transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ) [100]. Upon activation, YAP/TAZ bind to the TEAD family of transcriptional factors to induce the expression of genes that promote cell proliferation and inhibit apoptosis, leading to resistance to cancer therapy (Figure 1) [100]. Hippo signaling can cross-talk with MAPK signaling under many circumstances to promote carcinogenesis [101].

YAP is known to serve as a parallel survival effector when RAF or MEK is inhibited [102]. Similarly, it has long been known that YAP/TAZ interact with oncogenic KRAS-mediated signaling and modulate the response to KRAS inhibition. An early study of a PDAC mouse model showed that inactivation of KRAS G12D led to spontaneous tumor relapse [103]. YAP1 amplification and YAP1/TEAD2 signaling enabled relapsed tumors to bypass oncogenic KRAS addiction for tumor maintenance [103]. A recent CRISPR/Cas9 screen identified loss of the Hippo pathway components and activation of YAP1/TAZ as a mediator of resistance to KRAS G12C inhibition in cancer cell lines [104]. In this case, YAP/TAZ overcame KRAS dependency through TEAD-dependent transcription programs that are normally regulated by ERK, serving as a bypass of ERK signaling. These studies provide a rationale for targeting YAP/TAZ signaling and TEAD to overcome resistance to KRAS-targeted therapy. GNE7883, a small-molecule allosteric TEAD inhibitor, has been developed and shown to suppress oncogenic YAP/TAZ signaling and overcome KRAS G12C inhibitor resistance in both in vitro and in vivo models [105]. Resistance to KRAS G12C inhibition was also shown to be caused by mis-localization of Scribble, an apical-basal polarity protein, which led to YAP nuclear translocation and YAP-mediated induction of MRAS [95]. Additionally, YAP1 can induce tolerance to adagrasib in NSCLC via the activation of RTK AXL [106]. Together, these studies demonstrate a critical role of the YAP/TAZ-TEAD signaling axis in mediating resistance to KRAS-targeted therapy.

KRAS not only drives the RAF/MAPK/ERK pathway but also directly activates PI3K to promote the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling [107]. The PI3K/AKT pathway is often hyperactivated in cancer due to activating mutations in PIK3CA, which encodes the p110α catalytic subunit of PI3K, or loss or inactivating mutations of PTEN tumor suppressor gene, which encodes a protein phosphatase that negatively regulates PI3K activity [108]. Inactivating mutations in PTEN were detected in KRAS G12C patients with acquired adagrasib resistance [49]. Co-mutational analysis revealed that genetic alterations in the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway may lead to poorer outcomes of advanced NSCLC patients treated with KRAS G12C inhibitors [109].

Several preclinical studies demonstrated a critical role of the PI3K/AKT pathway in mediating resistance to KRAS G12C inhibition. Analysis of a panel of KRAS G12C NSCLC cell lines treated with ARS-1620 revealed that variations of cellular response to KRAS G12C inhibition are associated with the suppression of MEK/ERK and PI3K/AKT signaling [110]. Combing ARS-1620 with PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway inhibitors could overcome resistance to ARS-1620 in both in vitro and in vivo models, including patient-derived xenograft models [110]. Analysis of sotorasib-resistant KRAS G12C cancer cells showed that PI3K integrates signals from multiple RTKs, and the activity of PI3K/AKT was negatively correlated with the sensitivity to KRAS G12C inhibitors [76]. Combining sotorasib with the PI3Kα inhibitor CYH33 was synergistic against KRAS G12C cancer cells in vitro and in vivo [76]. Another study of sotorasib-resistant KRAS G12C cancer cells identified constitutive activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway [111]. Overexpression of the constitutively active PIK3CA mutants or knockdown of PTEN reduced sotorasib sensitivity, and PI3K inhibition by alpelisib significantly increased sotorasib sensitivity [111]. In YAP/TAZ-mediated resistance to KRAS G12C inhibitors, TEAD was found to activate PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway to inhibit apoptosis [104].

The activity of the PI3K/AKT pathway may explain why CRC is intrinsically less sensitive to KRAS-targeted therapy compared to NSCLC. Unlike NSCLC, genetic alterations in the PI3K/AKT pathway, especially mutation of PIK3CA and PTEN, are prevalent in CRC and frequently co-occur with KRAS mutations [10, 112]. Among the four CRC subtypes, the CMS3 subtype has the highest frequency of KRAS mutations, along with PIK3CA mutations and PTEN deletions [84].

PI3K/AKT signaling plays a particularly important role in the intrinsic resistance to targeted therapy in CRC. Previous studies have established that PI3K/AKT signaling is a key mediator of resistance to anti-EGFR and MEK targeted therapy in KRAS-mutant CRC [113–115]. Therefore, there is strong rationale for targeting the interaction between mutant KRAS and PI3K/AKT signaling. A PI3Kα/KRAS interaction breaker, BBO10203, has recently been developed and may provide a new opportunity to overcome the resistance to KRAS-targeted therapy in CRC [116]. Recently developed PI3Kα inhibitors such as STX478 may be useful for overcoming resistance to KRAS inhibitors [117, 118]..

Cell cycle, a fundamental process of cell proliferation and division, is regulated by multiple cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) [119]. Aberrant CDK activation plays a critical role in KRAS-mediated oncogenic signaling. KRAS-driven tumors in mice have been shown to rely on CDK activities for survival [120]. CDK activities are negatively regulated by p16, encoded by the CDKN2A tumor suppressor gene [121]. Over 20% of KRAS-mutant NSCLC and PDAC cases have been found to have inactivating CDKN2A co-mutations [122, 123]. KRAS mutations are also correlated with the epigenetic silencing of CDKN2A by promoter hypermethylation [124]. CDK4 and 6 (CDK4/6) inhibitors, a class of promising anticancer agents, showed therapeutic efficacy against KRAS-mutant solid tumors, including NSCLC and CRC [125]. CDKN2A co-mutations are associated with a poor response in advanced NSCLC to KRAS G12C inhibitors [109]. A recent preclinical study on pan-RAS inhibitor RMC-6236 identified an association between CDKN2A loss and reduced response in KRAS G12-mutant NSCLC [44]. Co-targeting KRAS and CDK4/6 showed synergism in KRAS-mutant PDAC preclinical models [126]. Together, these studies demonstrate a key role of CDK4/6 in the resistance to KRAS mutant-specific and pan-RAS inhibitors and suggest CDKN2A status may serve as a potential predictive biomarker for KRAS-targeted therapy.

Kinases regulating mitosis, such as aurora kinase a (AURKA), have been shown to mediate resistance to KRAS inhibition. AURKA, a member of the AURK family member, is frequently overexpressed in a wide range of cancers and plays a critical role in oncogenesis [127, 128]. AURKA has a close functional relationship with RAS-mediated oncogenic signaling. On one hand, aberrant activation of KRAS and downstream MAPK/ERK signaling promotes AURKA overexpression [129–131]. On the other hand, AURKA, in turn, promotes resistance to therapeutic targeting of EGFR and RAS pathway components by activating RAS downstream effectors such as RalA, ERK1/2, and AKT [132–134]. Inhibition of AURKA could sensitize KRAS-mutant CRC and NSCLC to EGFR-targeted therapy [135, 136]. It is therefore not surprising that AURKA has emerged as a key mediator of resistance to KRAS inhibition and one of the most attractive synthetic lethal targets in KRAS-mutant cancers. In KRAS G12C NSCLC cells developing acquired resistance to ARS-1620, AURKA was found to be upregulated and directly bind to KRAS G12C to activate CRAF and promote adaptive reactivation of KRAS downstream signaling [73]. AURKA activation was identified as a key mediator of the resistance to sotorasib in KRAS G12C NSCLC [137]. Additionally, AURKA was also found to be involved in regulating the Hedgehog/GLI-1 signaling axis, which promotes KRAS re-expression and escape from KRAS G12C inhibition in NSCLC cells [138].

Similar to AURKA, other mitotic kinases, including Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1) and WEE1, are involved in KRAS-mediated oncogenic signaling. PLK1, a direct downstream target of AURKA, is often overexpressed in cancers and regulates multiple therapeutic response pathways [139]. A genome-wide siRNA screen identified PLK1 as one of the most prominent synthetic lethal targets in KRAS-mutant cancers [140]. KRAS-mutant CRC cells were found to be more sensitive to PLK1 inhibition than their wild-type counterparts [141]. PLK1 was found to be overexpressed and function in a positive feedback loop involving c-Myc and the long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) ST8SIA6-AS1 to promote resistance to KRAS G12C inhibition [142]. Similar to AURKA, PLK1 also directly activates CRAF to induce downstream ERK signaling [143]. Inhibition of PLK1 with volasertib could overcome the resistance to KRAS G12C inhibition in both in vitro and cell line xenograft models [142]. A clinical study showed promising efficacy of combining the PLK1 inhibitor onvansertib with FOLIFRI/bevacizumab in in KRAS-mutant CRC [141]. CRISPR and chemical library screening identified WEE1 inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy for KRAS-mutated NSCLC with TP53 mutations [144]. WEE1 inhibition could induce mitotic catastrophe in TP53-mutant KRAS G12C cells [144]. A dual inhibitor targeting both WEE1 and PLK1 has been developed and may be useful for overcoming resistance to KRAS inhibition [145]. Further studying cell cycle-related kinases will provide new mechanistic insights and therapeutic opportunities for tackling resistance to KRAS-targeted therapy.

In addition to RTKs, non-receptor tyrosine kinases such as focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and SRC can promote resistance to KRAS-targeted therapy. FAK is an oncogenic kinase well-known in regulating cancer cell proliferation, migration, and tumor microenvironment (TME) [146]. KRAS G12C inhibition can induce sustained adaptive activation of FAK, mediating therapeutic resistance via both the FAK-YAP signaling axis and FAK-mediated fibrosis [147]. The FAK/SRC axis is responsible for elevated activity of p21 activated kinase (PAK), which activates CRAF and MEK, promoting sotorasib resistance in KRAS G12C cancer cells [111]. Targeting FAK could overcome the resistance to KRAS G12C inhibition in vitro and in vivo [147], suggesting a potential use in combing FAK and KRAS inhibition. In addition to cooperating with FAK, SRC can directly mediate resistance to adagrasib by activating transcription factor JUN [148]. Combining adagrasib with SRC inhibitors (dasatinib, bosutinib) was synergistic in KRAS G12C cancer cells [148].

Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1) degrades nuclear factor erythroid-derived 2-like 2 (Nrf2) by functioning as an adaptor of E3 ubiquitin ligase of Nrf2. Nrf2 is a transcription factor and the master regulator of the cellular antioxidant response and can promote cancer development and progression and therapeutic response [149, 150]. Upregulation of NRF2 was previously shown to promote KRAS-mediated chemotherapy resistance [151]. The KEAP1-NRF2 signaling axis was reported to confer resistance to KRAS G12D inhibitors in PDAC preclinical models [152]. KEAP1 was also found to be frequently mutated and associated with shorter patient survival in advanced NSCLC resistant to KRAS G12C inhibition [109, 153]. Co-occurrence of KEAP1 mutations was found in CRC patients treated with KRAS G12D inhibitors [154].

Oncogenic KRAS is known to shape an immunosuppressive TME, limiting the efficacy of immunotherapeutic approaches, particularly anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy [155]. Sotorasib could induce anti-tumor immunogenic response by increasing the infiltration of tumor-suppressive immune cells [24]. Thus, the induction of anti-tumor immunity is a crucial part of the therapeutic effect of KRAS G12C inhibition. However, co-mutations of other genes, especially STK11/LKB1 and KEAP1, were found to mediate resistance to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy in KRAS-mutant NSCLC [156]. STK11/LKB1 and KEAP1 mutations can reshape a colder TME and suppress the infiltration of T cells [157]. Emerging evidence supports the notion that tumor intrinsic mechanisms such as resistance to oxidative stress, proteotoxic stresses and cell death leads to the adaption and evolution of cold tumors. Cold tumors generally exhibit poor responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies, a challenge that extends to KRAS-mutant tumors as well [158]. This also confers a primary resistance mechanism, diminishing the immunogenic therapeutic effect of KRAS inhibition.

Anticancer therapies often eliminate cancer cells by inducing apoptosis [159]. Failure to induce apoptosis mediates resistance to various anticancer agents, including KRAS inhibitors [159]. In many cases, the activation of oncogenic pathways discussed above can converge on blocking cell death in KRAS-mutant cancer cells. Bcl-XL, an antiapoptotic Bcl-2 family member, was found to mediate resistance of CRC cells to the KRAS G12C inhibitor AZ’1569, which could be overcame by co-targeting Bcl-XL using ABT-263 [75]. Bcl-2 inhibition with venetoclax significantly increased the efficacy of MRTX1133 in KRAS G12D PDAC and resensitized resistant PDAC cells to MRTX1133 [160]. These studies suggest that stimulating apoptosis may be useful for potentiating KRAS-targeted therapy.

Protein homeostasis, or proteostasis, is crucial for the growth of tumor cells [161]. Oncogenic KRAS regulates protein quality control in KRAS-mutant tumor cells [161]. Recent findings revealed that while inhibition of oncogenic KRAS disrupted the proteostasis and triggered proteotoxic stresses, surviving cells were able to rewire the proteostasis network by activating inositol-requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1α) [161]. Targeting proteostasis may serve as a new strategy to overcome resistance to KRAS inhibitors.

Despite recent groundbreaking advances, intrinsic and acquired resistance poses a major challenge for KRAS-targeted therapy. The resistance mechanisms are highly complicated and heterogeneous among different tumor types and even within the same type of tumors. Further complications arise from the varying effects of different KRAS alleles on downstream effector signaling. For example, KRAS G12D more preferentially interacts with the PI3K/AKT pathway in NSCLC compared to other mutants [35]. Different KRAS alleles in CRC have distinct signatures of pathways dependency and protein expression [162]. All these factors contribute to the heterogeneous and cancer-type-specific resistance mechanisms [163]. Fortunately, the involved signaling pathways are interdependent and intertwined into a tangled web of network (Figure 2), creating vulnerable nodal points for effective therapeutic intervention. Interestingly, mutant KRAS seems to engage complex molecular circuitries upon inhibition, often converging on compensatory activation of MAPK/ERK and PI3K/mTOR signaling, which can help surviving cancer cells to resist to growth inhibition, cell killing, and immune TME remodeling (Figure 1). Comprehensive understanding of the macroscopic details of this signaling network and identification of biomarkers will help design improved combinational strategies, which may be the key to improving patient outcomes in the future.
