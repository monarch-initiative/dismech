OBJECTIVES: Craniofacial microsomia (CFM) is a congenital condition that 
typically involves hypoplasia of the ear and jaw. It is often associated with 
adverse effects such as hearing loss and sleep-disordered breathing. There is 
little research on its etiology.
METHODS: We conducted a case-control study from maternal interview data 
collected from mothers of infants with and without CFM. The study included 108 
children with and 84 children without CFM. Logistic regression with adjustment 
for demographic factors was used to evaluate associations between maternal 
exposures of interest and risk for CFM overall, as well as for different 
phenotypic sub-groups of children on the CFM spectrum.
RESULTS: We found a statistically significant association between diabetes 
mellitus (DM) and CFM (OR 4.01, 95% CI 1.6-10.5). The association was slightly 
attenuated after adjustment for BMI. Higher parity was also associated with 
increased risk for CFM (OR 2.0, 95% CI 1.0-4.0). Vitamin A consumption and/or 
liver consumption was associated with a 70% lower risk compared with non-users 
(OR 0.3, 95% 0.1-0.8). Maternal age at the time of pregnancy was not associated 
with CFM.
CONCLUSIONS: These analyses contribute evidence linking maternal DM with an 
elevated risk of having an infant with CFM, which is consistent with previous 
research and adds to the body of knowledge about the strength of this 
association. Further study is warranted to understand the potential mechanisms 
underlying the effect of DM in the developing embryo.

Craniofacial microsomia (CFM), also known as oculo-auriculo-vertebral spectrum (OAVS), is a congenital condition with variable presentation that often includes underdevelopment of one or both sides of the face. Features typically include microtia and/or mandibular hypoplasia in addition to preauricular tags, epibulbar dermoids, lateral oral clefts, and extracranial anomalies. Isolated microtia is the mildest form on the CFM spectrum (Beleza-Meireles et al. 2014; Bennun et al. 1985; Keogh et al. 2007).

Although CFM can have a significant clinical impact on individuals, little is known about its risk factors. As isolated microtia is considered a mild presentation of CFM, studies that evaluate the risk of isolated microtia and anotia are considered here alongside those that evaluate the risk for more heterogeneous and variable presentations of CFM described in the first paragraph. There is likely etiologic heterogeneity, with genetic and non-genetic risk factors (Barisic et al. 2014; Beleza-Meireles et al. 2014; Kaye et al. 1992). Prior investigations have detected an association between prenatal exposure to maternal diabetes mellitus, retinoids, thalidomide, and mycophenolate mofetil and risk of microtia (Anderka et al. 2009; Klieger-Grossmann et al. 2010). Other risk factors identified in the few published case-control studies on microtia and/or CFM include higher maternal parity and maternal acute illness (Castilla and Luquetti 2009; Correa et al. 2008; Luquetti et al. 2013; Ma et al. 2010; Mastroiacovo et al. 1995a; Werler et al. 2004a; Zhang et al. 2009). Low birthweight, advanced maternal age, low maternal education, and Hispanic ethnicity have been reported as risk factors for CFM in cross-sectional, population-based studies (Werler et al. 2004b). Early case-control studies suggested that maternal use of vasoactive medications in the 1st trimester in combination with cigarette smoking was associated with increased risk of CFM (Werler et al. 2004a; Werler et al. 2009). A later study in a larger cohort showed that those exposures or other potential vascular events (such as twinning) did not increase risk (Van Bennekom et al. 2013). Periconceptional intake of folic acid has been associated with reduced risk of microtia among non-obese women (Ma et al. 2010; Van Bennekom et al. 2013). The mechanisms by which these exposures cause microtia have not been fully elucidated, as most publications have focused on the risk for microtia as opposed to the broader CFM spectrum. Furthermore, those that did evaluate the risk for CFM did not have clearly defined case definitions.

We were interested in the influence of maternal diabetes mellitus (DM) on an infant’s risk for CFM since it is one of the more strongly associated risk factors for microtia. Furthermore, obesity is a risk factor for DM and an association between non-isolated microtia and obesity has been reported (Ma et al. 2010; Van Bennekom et al. 2013). We sought to evaluate whether maternal DM, obesity and other pre-pregnancy and prenatal factors are associated with risk of CFM in a well-defined sample of infants with CFM.

The current study uses data collected through a multi-center, longitudinal cohort study titled Craniofacial Microsomia: Longitudinal Outcomes in Children Pre-Kindergarten (CLOCK). The primary goal of the CLOCK study is to evaluate the neurobehavioral outcomes in children with and without CFM (Speltz et al. 2018). Data used in the current analyses were collected between November 2013 and April 2017 when children were between 12 and 24 months of age.

The study included children who were recruited through six US craniofacial centers (Seattle Children’s Hospital (SCH), Children’s Hospital of Los Angeles (CHLA), Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP), Shriners Hospitals for Children, Chicago (SHC) University of Illinois at Chicago (UIC), and University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (UNC)). The recruitment and study protocol have been described in detail elsewhere (Luquetti et al. 2019).. All study procedures were approved by the IRB at each of the six participating sites.

Infants between 12 and 24 months of age were eligible for the study if they had been diagnosed with CFM by a regional craniofacial team and met at least one of the following inclusion criteria: (1) microtia; (2) anotia; (3) facial asymmetry and preauricular tag; (4) facial asymmetry and facial tag; (5) facial asymmetry and epibulbar dermoid; (6) facial asymmetry and lateral oral cleft (e.g. macrostomia); (7) preauricular tag and epibulbar dermoid; (8) preauricular tag and lateral oral cleft; (9) facial tag and epibulbar dermoid; and (10) lateral oral cleft and epibulbar dermoid. Infants were excluded if they had an abnormal karyotype, a syndromic diagnosis, a major medical or neurological condition (e.g., cancer, cerebral palsy) at time of recruitment, increased risk for inability to comply with or complete the study, were born before 34 weeks estimated gestational age, had a sibling already participating in the study or the consenting parent spoke neither English nor Spanish.

Potential participants were identified in the study by querying electronic hospital billing data using ICD-9 codes that matched the inclusion criteria (743.8, 744.0, 744.01, 744.02, 744.09, 744.21, 744.23, 744.1, 744.83, 754.0, 756.0). The clinical database was queried at the SCH site. Study team members also identified potential participants through review of clinic schedules for craniofacial, otolaryngology, audiology, and genetics clinics at the study sites.

Infants without a congenital facial anomaly or injury who were between 12 and 24 months of age were eligible for the study. Individuals were excluded if they met any of the exclusionary criteria for cases. Controls were primarily enrolled through local pediatric practices that were near the study sites from which cases were recruited. Recruitment flyers were posted in practice-based network offices and, for the SCH site, we also contacted additional participants through the University of Washington Participant Pool, which is a database of individuals of all ages from the Seattle metropolitan area that are interested in participating in research studies.

We monitored the balance of case and control on the following characteristics: infant age and sex, family socioeconomic status (SES), and language spoken in the home (English or Spanish), in order to minimize group differences.

We recorded infant age and sex, family socioeconomic status (SES) and language spoken in the home (English or Spanish). SES categorization was determined based on marital/cohabitation status, occupation, and education of the head(s) of the household. Parents of study participants completed an in-person structured interview about their infants’ medical and family history by a trained research personnel. Biologic mothers were asked about their pregnancy history using an instrument developed from questions from the National Birth Defects Prevention Study mother questionnaire (www.nbdps.org). Questions related to DM included type of diabetes (“gestational, that is during pregnancy only; insulin-dependent diabetes, also called type I or juvenile type II; or adult onset”) and time of diagnosis.

Information regarding clinical CFM features was abstracted from patients’ medical charts. We obtained digital photographs of the child’s face (frontal, left and right lateral, and smiling views) following a simplified version of our previously published protocol (Birgfeld et al. 2011b).

We used a modified version of the Phenotypic Assessment Tool for Craniofacial Microsomia (PAT-CFM), which includes a photographic protocol and a modified version of the Pictorial OMENS form to categorize the facial features associated with CFM (Birgfeld et al. 2011a; Birgfeld et al. 2016). We integrated data from the modified pictorial OMENS ratings, health history interview, and medical chart abstraction data to categorize the phenotype of cases and controls. These integrated dataset was compiled in an Access Database and reviewed by a craniofacial pediatrician (CH) to establish the phenotype by feature for each participant.

We developed subcategories based on CFM-related facial phenotypes, with the goal of creating subgroups of adequate size in order to prevent over-stratification.

The distributions of demographic and phenotypic characteristics were calculated, and logistic regression was used to evaluate the associations between exposures of interest and risk for CFM overall as well as within sub-groups of children with CFM.

We included the following covariates in the initial statistical models in order to adjust for their potentially confounding effect: sex, SES, study site and ethnicity. In the statistical models evaluating the association between DM and CFM we also evaluated the potentially confounding effect of parity, mothers’ age at birth, and mothers’ pre-pregnancy BMI, as these are all associated with both DM as well as CFM. As the results from the fully adjusted models did not meaningfully change the risk estimates (i.e., neither changed the threshold into or out of statistical significance, nor changed the magnitude sufficiently to interpret the nature of the relationship differently), the results presented here are from the unadjusted statistical models. All analyses were carried out using Stata 14 (StataCorp. 2015. Stata Statistical Software: Release 14. College Station, TX: StataCorp LP).

Participation rates for cases was 49% and for controls 56%. Cases were more likely than controls to be male (62 versus 44%), Hispanic (55 vs 38%) and white (76 vs 58%). Age at the time of enrollment was comparable (14.5 vs 14.4 months). Mean SES score as measured by the Hollingshead method was slightly lower among case participants (mean score: 35. vs 37.5), and the proportion of cases on Medicaid was slightly higher than among controls (54 vs 48 %). Ninety-five cases had microtia (95%) and, of those, 29% were bilateral. Among those with microtia, the severity based on the more severely affected ear varied as follows: first degree microtia (8%), second degree microtia (24%), third degree microtia (63%), and anotia (5%). Mandibular hypoplasia was present in 59% of cases; most were characterized as mild (71%), 21% were moderate and 8% severe. The distribution of phenotypic characteristics was as follows: 30 cases with microtia only (28%); 59 cases with microtia in the presence of facial asymmetry with or without other characteristics such as tags or pits (55%); or 19 cases characterized as ‘other’(18%) who may have included children who did not fit into the prior two categories, but who met at least one inclusion criterion 7–10 listed above. Full descriptive data for the cohort have been previously published (Luquetti et al. 2019).

Mothers with any form of DM, either prior to or during the cases’ pregnancy, had a four-fold higher risk of having children with CFM (OR 4.0, 95% CI 1.5–10.5; Table 1). This association was slightly higher among women diagnosed with gestational DM than those with Type II DM (OR 4.6, 95% CI 1.5–14.2 vs. OR 2.9, 95% CI 0.6–14.9, respectively), but the difference in odd-ratios between these two subgroups was based on only 2 controls and 6 cases in the group with a history of gestational DM (data not shown). Although slightly attenuated, the increased risk of CFM associated with DM persisted even when we adjusted for mother’s pre-pregnancy BMI (OR 3.65, 95% CI 1.4–9.7; Table 2). Relative to mothers who were primiparous, mothers with one, two, or more prior pregnancies were more likely to have a child with CFM, but the association within each stratum included one (OR 1.2, 95% CI 0.6–2.7 and 2.0, 95% CI 1.0–4.0; Table 1). Mothers with a previous miscarriage did not appear to have a higher risk of having a child with CFM (data not shown). Mothers had similar risk for having a child with CFM irrespective of their age (OR 1, 1, and 0.9 among 25–30, 30–35 and 35–46 year olds relative to women under 25, respectively). While higher BMI was associated with higher risk for CFM, the confidence interval included one(OR 1.7, 95% CI 0.8–3.5 and OR 1.5, 95% CI 0.8–3.0 among over weight and obese women relative to normal weight women, respectively; Table 1).

We evaluated whether use of fertility medications, vitamin A consumption during pregnancy, use of a prenatal vitamin during pregnancy were associated with risk for CFM. Although all were associated with a lower risk for CFM, confidence intervals included one and the estimates were based on small sample sizes (<10). When we looked jointly at vitamin A consumption and/or liver consumption, we found that users had a 70% lower risk of CFM than non-users (OR 0.3, 95% 0.1–0.8; Table 1).

We evaluated whether DM was associated with a specific sub-phenotype of CFM and found that, relative to controls, while the association was strongest among children with isolated microtia (OR 7.1, 95% CI 2.3–22.2), this association was also observed among those with microtia accompanied by facial asymmetry (OR 3.4, 95% 1.2–9.7) and those who met the CFM inclusion criteria but did not fit into either of the first two categories (OR 2.4, 95% CI 0.5–10.6; Table 3).

Our analyses contribute to prior evidence linking maternal diabetes mellitus with an elevated risk of having an infant with CFM (Correa et al. 2008; Garne et al. 2012; Mastroiacovo et al. 1995a; Van Bennekom et al. 2013; Wang, Martinez-Frias, Graham 2002; Werler et al. 2004a). We found that women with DM had a four-fold risk of having a child with CFM in comparison with women without DM. Higher parity was associated with higher risk for CFM and higher BMI was associated with higher CFM risk, but these associations did not reach statistical significance. Fertility treatment, vitamin A and prenatal vitamin consumption were all associated with lower risk for CFM, but the 95% confidence intervals all included one.

A relationship between DM and microtia has been established in the literature (Correa et al. 2008; Ewart-Toland et al. 2000; Garne et al. 2012; Mastroiacovo et al. 1995b; Van Bennekom et al. 2013) with the strength of the association ranging from 1.3 to 18.5. A summary of the results from previous work is presented in Table 4. While some studies evaluated the risk associated with any DM (Mastroiacovo et al. 1995b), others tried to evaluate the risk associated with gestational versus established DM. Only three prior studies have evaluated the risk of the broader CFM spectrum in relation to DM (Barisic et al. 2014; Wang, Martinez-Frias, Graham 2002; Werler et al. 2009). In a case control study with 157 cases, Wang el al. found a 50% higher risk for CFM among mothers with established DM, and a greater than two-fold higher risk --among mothers who were diagnosed with DM during the course of their pregnancy. Similarly, Werler et al, in a case control study involving 230 children with CFM, found that children with CFM were six times more likely to have a mother with DM than were controls. Barisic et al compared the rates of DM among mothers of children with CFM using data from the EUROCAT registry (a European network of population-based registries for the epidemiologic surveillance of congenital anomalies) and found a rate that was 10-fold higher than expected based on published population-based rates; this study did not have a comparison group (Barisic et al. 2014).

In summary, no clear pattern has been established in prior research indicating whether pre-gestational or gestational DM is more strongly associated with microtia or CFM, with some studies finding that established DM is more strongly associated (Van Bennekom et al. 2013) and others finding that gestational DM is more strongly associated (Wang, Martinez-Frias, Graham 2002). In the current analysis, we were unable to evaluate with statistical precision whether there was any difference in risk associated with CFM and type of DM.

Although maternal obesity has been linked to risk of birth defects in general, no study has looked at the impact of obesity on the risk of CFM, and to date, only two other studies have evaluated the specific association with risk of microtia (Ma et al. 2010; Waller et al. 2007). The first (Waller et al. 2007) analysis was carried out on a data set that was nested within the second (Ma et al. 2010), and neither reported an association. Although we did find that overweight women were more likely to have children with CFM, there was not a linearly increasing risk for CFM as women’s BMI increased. Our findings together with those of Waller’s and Ma’ may indicate that obesity itself is not associated with an increased risk of microtia, and the increase in risk of CFM among overweight women may be the result of a proportion of women in the overweight group that have undiagnosed DM. According to the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), the proportion of adults with undiagnosed DM was 4.3% and 3.2% among overweight and obese women, respectively (Gregg et al. 2004). In support of this explanation, in the current analysis, the 2.5-fold higher risk of having a child with CFM among overweight women was attenuated to 2.0 (0.8–5.3) when we adjusted for presence of DM.

Although the mechanisms that underlie the relation between DM and birth defects are not clear, the cellular damage that occurs in adult tissues leading to vascular disease may also occur in the differentiating embryo tissue. This is consistent with the vascular disruption hypothesis that has been posited as a potential explanation for the development of CFM. It also seems likely that the DM CFM relationship may be mediated through hyperglycemia (Eriksson, Cederberg, Wentzel 2003). Among human studies evaluating the effects of maternal glycemic control early in pregnancy, the association between DM and congenital anomalies persisted only among women with hyperglycemia (Suhonen, Hiilesmaa, Teramo 2000), which further implicates glycemic control as a mechanism underlying the relationship between maternal DM and microtia/CFM. In an evaluation of dietary intake of nutrients by mothers participating in the National Birth Defects Prevention Study, no increase in risk for microtia was observed with increases in either glycemic index or glycemic load (Ma et al. 2010). However, these findings were based on women’s responses to a food frequency questionnaire querying their diet at the time of their child’s conception, which was administered when their infants were between 6 weeks and 24 months of age (i.e., as long as 33 months after the time point in question), thus susceptible to recall biases. Furthermore, the interview collected data about a woman’s consumption, but was not able to measure circulating glucose levels directly (which may be impacted by her DM status, or insulin resistance). If obesity is associated with CFM as we suggest here, it is likely related to the fact that DM is more common among individuals with higher BMI. Gregg et al report that the prevalence of DM rises from 4.1 percent to 18.3 percent as BMI rises from <25 to >= 35 percent (Gregg et al. 2004).

Consistent with published reports evaluating the association between parity and microtia (Castilla and Orioli 1986; Harris, Kallen, Robert 1996; Mastroiacovo et al. 1995a), we found that risk for CFM increases with parity. In our cohort, women with two or more prior pregnancies had double the risk of giving birth to an infant with CFM than primiparous women.

Advanced maternal age has been reported as a risk factor for microtia (Forrester and Merz 2005; Harris, Kallen, Robert 1996), but we did not find elevated risk for CFM with increasing age, nor did we find that any maternal age group was at a particularly higher risk for CFM than others.

Periconceptional intake of supplements containing folic acid has been associated with lower risk of microtia among non-obese women, but not among obese women (Ma et al. 2012). The findings from the current analysis suggest that women who took prenatal vitamins had a substantially lower risk for CFM, but these estimates were limited in several respects: only nine women in our study reported having taken no prenatal vitamins and among these, only one was a control; our confidence intervals were wide and included 1.0 (0.02–1.19); we were not able to further stratify into subgroups defined by BMI.

We found use of vitamin A and/or liver consumption (a food rich in vitamin A) to be associated with a 70 percent lower risk for CFM. Retinoic acid is a metabolite of vitamin A and has been reported to be a risk factor for microtia (Carey, Park, Muntz 2006; Lammer et al. 1985; Stern, Rosa, Baum 1984). It may be that these apparently discrepant findings are the result of a dose effect; below a specific threshold of consumption, vitamin A is essential for normal reproduction and development, but beyond some threshold, vitamin A has a teratogenic effect on the developing fetus (Khoury, Moore, Mulinare 1996; Rothman et al. 1995).

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study. We relied on women’s recall of their prenatal DM diagnosis as we did not have access to maternal and/or gestational history from medical records. However, high positive predictive value of self-reported DM is well reported in longitudinal follow-up studies. For example, in a study using the Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS), a self-reported questionnaire, and data abstracted from hospital records as a gold standard; any DM had 91 % sensitivity and 94% specificity (Dietz et al. 2014). In addition, recall bias cannot be ruled out but, previous studies on the field have showed that there is minimal to no recall bias in case-control studies comparing mothers with and without adverse pregnancy outcomes including those with children with birth defects (Feldman et al. 1989; Khoury, James, Erickson 1994; Mackenzie and Lippman 1989; Rockenbauer et al. 2001). Gestational DM is a complex disease, and often is undetected until an individual undergoes a screening evaluation during pregnancy. As a result, women who are diagnosed with gestational DM through screening during their pregnancy may have had DM before conception. It is often difficult to differentiate between the two types of DM and to assess the temporal relationship of the period characterized by poor glucose control. In addition, we did not have data on the severity of women’s associated hyperglycemia.

Our analyses provide additional evidence linking maternal diabetes mellitus as well as higher parity with an elevated risk of having an infant with CFM. Further studies to understand the risk factors for this heterogeneous and complex disorder will require larger sample sizes and the integration of parental exposures with genetic risk factors to clarify the biological mechanism through which risk for the development of CFM is mediated.
