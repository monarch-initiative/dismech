# Incidence and Prevalence of Congenital Myopathies - A Population-Based Study From Western Sweden.
**Authors:** Michael E, Hedberg-Oldfors C, Tulinius M, Nordström S, Oldfors A, Darin N
**Journal:** Ann Neurol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/ana.78078](https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.78078)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: Congenital myopathies are a group of rare genetic muscle disorders. Previous studies have estimated point prevalences which only include surviving individuals. Our aim was to perform an epidemiological study with strict inclusion criteria, using modern diagnostic technology to present both incidences and prevalences, and to describe the genetic and muscle pathological characteristics of these disorders.

METHODS: A retrospective, population-based epidemiological study was conducted in western Sweden between 1985 and 2022. All patients diagnosed with congenital myopathies during this period were included.

RESULTS: In total, 104 patients were identified whereas 25 died during the follow-up. The total birth prevalence (estimated lifetime risk) was 14.9 per 100,000 live births, whereas the total cumulative incidence by age 35 years was 12.3 per 100,000 inhabitants, and the total point prevalence was 3.9 per 100,000 inhabitants. The most common histopathological type was congenital myopathy with nonspecific changes (birth prevalence 4.1 per 100,000 live births), followed by core myopathy and centronuclear myopathy (2.1 per 100,000). The most common causative genes were MYH2 (3.9 per 100,000) and RYR1 (2.0 per 100,000). Despite using next-generation sequencing, 1 in 5 cases lacked a genetic diagnosis. Among those without a genetic cause, 4 of 5 had nonspecific histopathological changes.

INTERPRETATION: By including all patients identified, both alive and deceased, our estimated incidence figures are considerably higher than estimates of point prevalence, which only includes living patients. As the mortality in these diseases is significant, incidence figures better reflect the occurrence in the population and are important for evidence-based health care planning and resource allocation. Congenital myopathies are a group of rare genetic muscle disorders clinically characterized by generalized muscle weakness, hypotonia, hyporeflexia, poor muscle bulk, and sometimes dysmorphic features because of the facial weakness. Whereas most patients with congenital myopathies present at birth or in early infancy, milder forms also exist that manifest later in childhood or adulthood.
1
,
2
 Congenital myopathies have traditionally been subdivided based on the predominant histopathological features identified on muscle biopsy, for example, rods (nemaline myopathy), cores (central core disease and multiminicore disease), central nuclei (centronuclear/myotubular myopathy), and selective hypotrophy of type 1 fiber (congenital fiber type disproportion).
3
 In addition, there is a large proportion of cases with nonspecific pathological changes.
4
,
5
 With the rise of next‐generation sequencing (NGS) methods, genetics have become increasingly important in the diagnostic pathway, and to date more than 30 genes have been identified to cause congenital myopathies.
6
,
7
 Many of these genes encode proteins with alleged roles in calcium homeostasis, sarcomere function, or membrane trafficking.
8
,
9
 The relationship between the histopathological findings and the genetic cause is complicated. Many congenital myopathies can be caused by several genes, and depending on the pathogenic variant, many of the causative genes are associated with more than one histopathological presentation. In addition, the histopathological abnormalities in the muscle can evolve and change over time in an individual and might differ between family members with the same genetic pathogenic variant.

,
11



Epidemiological studies are important for evidence‐based health care planning and for clinical trial readiness, because they give information about the underlying genotype–phenotype correlations and accuracy of diagnostic testing. The precise incidences and prevalences of congenital myopathies are still not known. Previous studies have focused on the point prevalence of congenital myopathies which has been estimated between 3.4 and 5.0 × 10−5 in children and 0.5 and 3.5 × 10−5 in the entire population (Table 1).

The aim of this study was to perform an epidemiological study with strict inclusion criteria, using modern diagnostic technology to present incidence and prevalence of congenital myopathies and to describe the genetic and muscle pathological characteristics of these disorders.

The geographic area studied was Region Västra Götaland in Sweden. Population data were collected from Statistics Sweden, a national statistical website, www.scb.se/en. On the prevalence day (December 31, 2022) the entire population of Region Västra Götaland was 1,758,656 which accounts for 16.7% of the population in Sweden. Eighty percent were of Swedish descent. Of those with foreign background, approximately 2% were from Syria, 1.5% from Iraq, followed by Finland, Somalia, and Polen with approximately 1% each. The study was performed at Sahlgrenska University Hospital, which is the regional hospital and a national, highly specialized care unit for neuromuscular diseases. It is the only hospital in the region performing genetic and pathological diagnostic investigations for these disorders. The hospital has one pediatric and one adult neuromuscular multidisciplinary team that follows patients from the region with neuromuscular disorders according to a standardized follow‐up program.

The study was a population‐based epidemiological, retrospective cohort study. The data were collected from the patients’ medical records using a standardized case report form. We collected information on age at disease onset, age at muscle biopsy, diagnostic delay, family history, clinical features, serum creatine kinase (CK) values, muscle biopsy data, and genetic examinations, as well as neurophysiological tests, such as electromyography (EMG), nerve conduction study, and repetitive nerve stimulation.

All patients diagnosed with congenital myopathies between 1985 and 2022 were included. The muscle biopsy registry from the Department of Pathology, the Department of Clinical Genetics Registry, and the National Causes of Death Register were reviewed to identify all the patients. This information was then compared with our pediatric and adult registries at the neuromuscular centers (Fig 1). The registers from the local habilitation centers in the region were also included to ensure that no patients were missed. First, a list of patients that underwent muscle biopsy and showed histopathological findings of myopathy was generated. This was then compared with a list of all genetically verified patients with congenital myopathies and was also cross‐referenced with the clinical registries to ensure that all cases were included. We then went through the medical records making sure that each patient fitted our criteria for the diagnosis. Muscle biopsies with no identified underlying pathogenic genetic variant were re‐evaluated by the muscle pathologist while the neurologist went through the medical records to ensure that the patient fitted our clinical and pathological criteria for congenital myopathies.

The study received prior approval by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (approval numbers: 056‐97, 317‐05, 893‐17, 2020‐02299, and 2021‐01339). The need for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. All procedures followed were in accordance with the ethical committee responsible for human experimentation (institutional and national) and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2000.

The study period was between 1985, when the muscle pathology unit was established, until the end of 2022. We used 3 different methods to estimate the occurrence of the diseases in the population. Cumulative incidence and birth prevalence were used as estimates of the incidence whereas point prevalence represents the occurrence in the population at a given timepoint.
25
,
26
,
27



The birth prevalence calculation was used to estimate the lifetime risk for congenital myopathies at birth.
28
 It was defined as the number of new cases over a period related to the number of children born alive during the same period, assuming that the rate of postnatal diagnosis is equal to the birth rate for each disorder (complete ascertainment), as proposed by Meikle and coworkers, a method frequently used to describe population figures for inherited metabolic diseases.
26
,
27
,
29
 Confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using generalized estimating equations (GEEs) with a logistic link function, working independence correlation structure, and robust standard errors, thereby accounting for clustering within families diagnosed with congenital myopathy.

The cumulative incidence was based on the number of cases born and diagnosed between 1985 and 2022 in the relation to the total number of live births during the same period. For the period of 1985 to 2022, there were 699,998 live births documented, 360,590 of which were male live births. The age‐specific incidence of any congenital myopathy was estimated using cumulative incidence functions, accounting for differences in follow‐up time and censoring at the end of follow‐up (December 31, 2022). For individuals not diagnosed with the disease, death was treated as a competing event using annual age‐specific mortality data from Statistics Sweden. Estimates were calculated at ages 0 and then every 5 years up to 35 years and represent the risk of developing congenital myopathy by 35 years of age. Subtype‐specific cumulative incidence estimates, stratified by myopathy subtype or genetic pathogenic variant, were obtained similarly using nonparametric cumulative incidence functions for competing risks. In these analyses, both death and diagnoses of other myopathy subtypes were treated as competing events, enabling estimation of the probability of each specific subtype over time while acknowledging that the occurrence of one subtype precludes the occurrence of another.

The point prevalence was defined as the number of affected individuals in the population who lived in the study area and were known to be alive on December 31, 2022, in relation to the total number of individuals who were at risk at that time.
25
 The CIs were estimated using GEE, applying the same methodology as described for the birth prevalence. On our prevalent day, the population of our catchment areas was 1,758,656 inhabitants.

During the timeframe for inclusion in this study (1985–2022), the genetic analysis was performed both with Sanger sequencing based on a candidate gene approach and by whole exome sequencing (WES) as well as whole genome sequencing (WGS). The WES and WGS data were first analyzed using the in silico panel for neuromuscular disorder but when these were negative, the entire data was scrutinized in a search for another explanation for the disease. The variant assessments were based on in silico prediction tools, population frequency, segregation, and muscle histopathology. Any patients that fulfilled criteria for congenital myopathy but had no identified genetic variant, were re‐analyzed with NGS every 3 to 5 years if we had genetic material stored. As late as in September 2022, we re‐analyzed 13 of the 22 unsolved cases with WGS. The remaining had either been lost to follow‐up or had died.

Open muscle biopsy was performed from the quadriceps, vastus lateralis, or the deltoid muscle for histochemical and ultrastructural analyses in accordance with standard protocols.
3
 Based on the predominant histopathological changes, the patients were classified into 1 of 6 different groups (see Table 2 and Fig 2): (1) nemaline myopathy; (2) core myopathy; (3) centronuclear myopathy; (4) congenital fiber type disproportion (CFTD); (5) congenital myopathy with other defined structural changes, such as various protein aggregates or structural changes other than groups 1 to 4, and (6) congenital myopathy with nonspecific myopathic changes.

The statistical analyses for the birth prevalence, point prevalence, and cumulative incidences were performed in R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), using the following packages: geepack (version 1.3.12), survival (version 3.7‐0), cmprsk (version 2.2‐12), and survminer (version 0.5.0). For the remaining statistical analyses, we used SPSS version 29. Descriptive statistics are presented for all measures; mean and standard deviation were used for normally distributed data, and median and interquartile range for skewed data. Categorical data were summarized as frequency and percentage.

The overall point prevalence of congenital myopathies in our population was 3.9 per 100,000 inhabitants (95% CI = 2.4 to 6.4). Table 3 shows the point prevalences of the different histopathological groups of congenital myopathies. The most common type was congenital myopathy with nonspecific changes with a prevalence of 1.2 per 100,000 inhabitants (95% CI = 0.7 to 1.9), followed by core myopathy at 0.7 per 100,000 inhabitants (95% CI = 0.4 to 1.4). The genetic causes are given in Figure 3B. The most common causative genes were MYH2 at 1.1 per 100,000 inhabitants (95% CI = 0.2 to 4.9) and RYR1 at 0.6 per 100,000 inhabitants (95% CI = 0.3 to 1.2).

The underlying genetic cause was identified in 82 of 104 patients (see Fig 3B and Supplementary Table S3). MYH2 was most common, followed by RYR1 and MTM1. Two families had MYH2 pathogenic variants, one with 20 affected members, and the second family with 6. Figure 4B shows the total number of diagnosed patients per gene as well as the number of prevalent patients per gene at the end of the study period.

The inheritance was autosomal dominant in 54 patients, autosomal recessive in 18 and X‐linked in 9. Digenic inheritance, due to variants in TTN and SRPK3, was present in one patient (Fig 3C).

Twenty‐two patients lacked a genetic diagnosis at the end of the study period. Eighteen of them had nonspecific changes on their muscle biopsy, and 2 had core myopathy, whereas fiber type disproportion and centronuclear myopathy was found in one each.

Muscle biopsy had been performed in 77 of 104 of the patients. The genotype–phenotype correlations are given in Figure 3A. All cases with myotubular myopathy had pathogenic variants in MTM1. RYR1 mainly caused core myopathy but also centronuclear myopathy as well as one case of nonspecific myopathy. In one family with 19 members that had MYH2‐related myopathy, 2 young patients (< 10 years old) had core myopathy, whereas 3 older patients (>50 years old) showed myopathy with rimmed vacuoles. In the second MYH2‐family, 3 patients had nonspecific changes, and one had type 1 fiber uniformity. In patients with TPM2‐related disease, the histopathological findings varied between CAP myopathy in 2 patient, nemaline myopathy in 1 patient, and nonspecific myopathy in 1 patient. In one family with the same TMP2 variant, one individual had CAP myopathy, and one had nemaline myopathy.

Nerve conduction studies, repetitive nerve stimulation, and EMG were performed in 42 patients. Myopathic changes with short duration of the motor unit potentials on EMG were found in 29 patients, whereas nerve conduction velocities were normal in all patients. Repetitive nerve stimulation was performed in 33 patients, including 23 children. The most commonly tested muscles were the abductor digiti minimi of the hand and the trapezius. None had decrement on 3 hertz (Hz) repetitive stimulation. Only 2 patients were assessed by single fiber electromyography (SFEMG) during the study's time period and none of them showed signs of neuromuscular transmission abnormalities.

A serum CK level was tested in 69 patients, it was normal in 55 (reference value < 240 IU/L), whereas 14 exhibited elevated CK levels (264–978 IU/L).

At least 89 of 104 patients had clinical onset before 18 years of age. Sixty‐six had symptoms at birth and 6 developed muscle weakness before age 2 years. Of those presenting at birth, 30 had joint contractures and 10 had antenatal symptoms such as polyhydramnios and weak fetal movements. More than 70% had signs of muscle weakness and/or abnormal motor function < 8 years of age.

More than 70% had signs of muscle weakness and/or abnormal motor function < 8 years of age. In our cohort, the median age at symptom onset was at 0 years (from birth until the completion of the first year of life, SD = 9.2 years) and the median age at diagnosis was 14.8 years (SD = 22.9 years).

In this study, we present comprehensive epidemiological data of congenital myopathies in a well‐defined Swedish population and identified a birth prevalence (life‐time risk at birth) of 14.9 per 100.000, and a cumulative incidence by the age of 35 years of 12.3 per 100,000 individuals. To our knowledge, this is the first time such estimates have been used to describe population figures for these disorders. Our estimates for birth prevalence and cumulative incidences, including both living and deceased patients, indicate a higher occurrence of congenital myopathies in the population than has been described previously using point prevalence estimates, which include cases alive at a specific timepoint.

The exact incidence rate of genetic disorders with variable age of onset is challenging to determine, as it requires follow‐up from birth until the age of diagnosis or death in order to identify all affected individuals. Birth prevalence and cumulative incidence are 2 different approaches that have been used for such incidence estimations.
27
,
34
,
35
 Cumulative incidence calculates the risk of developing the disorder over a period of time, which, in our study, was between 1985 and 2022. This method has limitations, as the patients might have relocated out of the catchment area before getting a diagnosis, or that they were not followed long enough for a diagnosis to be made. Birth prevalence is a ratio, and the estimation is affected by the fact that the denominator is not the actual population at risk, but an extrapolation based on the assumption of a stable population over time.
26
,
29
 Consequently, in our study, the birth prevalence estimate was proportionally higher compared to the cumulative incidence until 35 years of age. Our point prevalence estimates were similar to previous studies,
4
,
13
,
14
 but comparisons are hampered by different study designs, different time periods, variable diagnostic work‐up, and the frequent lack of definitions of congenital myopathies in earlier studies.

The main muscle histopathological findings in our cohort were comparable to previous studies on epidemiology of congenital myopathies,

,
12
,
16
 with unspecific myopathic changes being the most common group followed by core myopathy and centronuclear myopathy. The most common causative gene observed in our study population was MYH2. This was at least partly explained by a variant identified in 20 affected individuals from one family.

,
36
 To our knowledge, there is no founder variant in the MYH2 gene specific for the Swedish population. The second family with MYH2 myopathy had another type of variant in that gene. The second most common cause was RYR1, which is consistent with previous reports of RYR1 as the most common genetic etiology of congenital myopathies.

,

,

 As in other studies on the etiology of congenital myopathies,

,

,

 we sometimes found one genotype to be associated with more than one histopathological phenotype. Occasionally, this was found in affected members of the same families with pathogenic variants in either MYH2 or TPM2. One explanation to this variation could be related to differences in age at biopsy, especially in the large family with MYH2‐associated congenital myopathy.

One in 5 patients in our cohort still lacked a genetic cause at the end of the observation period. We did not have material left to perform genetic analysis in some patients but still 16% were unresolved using modern NGS techniques. In previous epidemiological studies on congenital myopathies, the proportion of genetically resolved cases has varied between 60% and 80%.
1
,
4
,
15
 Among the patients without a genetic diagnosis in our study, the muscle biopsy showed nonspecific changes in 82%, indicating the possibility of previously undescribed genetic mechanisms. In addition, a genetic diagnosis can be missed due to technical limitations in testing, such as allele dropout or limitations in detecting copy number variations (CNVs), and challenges in interpreting results, including the influence of genetic modifiers, variants of uncertain significance (VUS), or complex inheritance patterns. It is not completely excluded that some of these patients have another disorder mimicking congenital myopathy. Allen et al has recently described cases where maternal antibodies targeted fetal acetylcholine receptor isoforms causing symptoms that could mimic congenital myopathy.
38
 North et al have previously described difficulties in differentiating congenital myopathies from disorders like congenital myasthenic syndromes and certain congenital muscular dystrophies.
24
 We have tried to limit this possibility by reviewing all cases that lacked a verified genetic cause by repeating the genetic analyses, reviewing medical charts for disease progression, and reanalyze their muscle biopsies and neurophysiological tests.

In our study, the median onset of symptoms consistent with congenital myopathy was before 12 months of age, whereas 70% presented with symptoms before 24 months of age. In many cases, there was a delay of several years before being referred to a tertiary hospital where the diagnosis was eventually established. Because all children in Sweden have regular psychomotor developmental follow‐ups within the national child health care system, there was a possibility that the diagnosis could have been made much earlier with a better awareness of these diseases.

A strength with our study was that it included an entire well‐defined population, using a clear definition of congenital myopathies and estimated incidence figures. We used modern diagnostic methodologies and because we comprise both a national and regional center for diagnostics and clinical follow‐up of neuromuscular disorders, the coverage of patients diagnosed in our study population is probably high. The relatively small population may be considered a limitation because the individual diseases are very rare, and a small population size therefore carries a higher likelihood for observations to be affected by coincidence. Other limitations were the retrospective study design. During the study period diagnostic methods and awareness and standards of care has improved immensely. We therefore believe that our results are an underestimation of the true occurrence of congenital myopathies. In 15 of the unique families identified, there were several relatives that had the same or very similar symptoms as the index patient, but they were not included in the calculation as we did not have access to their medical records, and a diagnosis was not confirmed by either muscle biopsy and/or genetic analysis.

Our results add important epidemiological information necessary for planning of clinical trials and for allocating resources needed to support patients with congenital myopathies during their life. Our epidemiological estimates indicate a higher occurrence of these disorders in the population than what has been described before. We observed that 25% of the patients in our cohort died during the study period, which is a possible explanation to why the point prevalence is much lower than the incidence figures.
