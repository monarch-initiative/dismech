# Frequency of ZFHX3-Mediated Spinocerebellar Ataxia 4 in a US Undiagnosed Ataxia Cohort.
**Authors:** Chen A, Avadhani U, Ngo K, Corona RI, Neto GVC, Figueroa KP, Undiagnosed Diseases Network, Perlman S, Pulst SM, Nelson SF, Wong D, Fogel BL
**Journal:** Mov Disord (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mds.70387](https://doi.org/10.1002/mds.70387)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Spinocerebellar ataxia 4 (SCA4) is a late-onset dominant ataxia with neuropathy caused by exonic GGC repeat expansion in the ZFHX3 gene thought to originate from a Swedish founder event. The GC-rich expansion is highly thermodynamically stable, posing challenges for standard clinical genetic testing methods. Development of a high-throughput relatively inexpensive detection method would benefit both clinical diagnostic testing and large-scale research applications.

OBJECTIVE: Using a cost-effective high-throughput polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay, we assessed the frequency of SCA4 repeat expansion in an undiagnosed US ataxia cohort.

METHOD: Primers flanking the ZFHX3 repeat region were used under optimized PCR conditions to assess for expanded GGC repeats in 687 undiagnosed ataxia patients. Repeat size was determined by fragment analysis and orthogonally confirmed with long-read sequencing (Oxford Nanopore Technologies).

RESULTS: We identified pathogenic SCA4 expansions in three families with cerebellar ataxia and sensory/autonomic neuropathy. The pathogenic alleles were confirmed to be of Swedish ancestry using comprehensive haplotype analysis of 14 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) previously associated with ZFHX3 expansion. Two families carried all 14 SNPs, suggesting this may represent an ancestral haplotype. Utilizing a minimal haplotype present in all reported patients, we bioinformatically assessed 852 additional subjects, for a total of 1539 screened, but did not identify additional SCA4 patients.

CONCLUSION: Using a high-throughput cost-effective PCR assay, we identified three SCA4 families in a large US ataxia cohort. Haplotype analysis supports a common Swedish founder allele, consistent with previously reported SCA4 cases. SCA4 diagnostic testing is recommended for all patients of Swedish ancestry with undiagnosed ataxia. © 2026 The Author(s). To detect pathogenic ZFHX3 expansions, primers flanking the repeat region were labeled with 6‐carboxyfluorescein for fragment analysis to determine the size. Sequences were SCA4‐fwd‐FAM: 5′−/56‐FAM/GTTCCAAAGGTGCAGTACAAGTTGGTCTG‐3′ and SCA4‐rev‐FAM: 5′−/56‐FAM/GTTCTGTGTTTGTGCAAGGCCGACTCGAG‐3′.

The PCR protocol was optimized using a high‐fidelity polymerase designed for high‐GC content (Phusion, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts) and further adapted to disrupt thermodynamically stable GC‐rich DNA secondary structures through the inclusion of betaine
28
 and high denaturation temperatures. Amplification was performed in a 20‐μL reaction volume and consisted of 1X Phusion Flash High Fidelity PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.33 μM forward primer (IDT), 0.33 μM reverse primer (IDT), 50–100 ng/μL DNA template, and 2.2 M of 5 M betaine (Sigma‐Aldrich). PCR was performed using a Biorad C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler with an initial denaturation at 100°C for 3 minute 30 seconds, followed by 35 cycles of 2 minutes at 100°C for denaturation, 30 seconds at 61.9°C for annealing, 40 seconds at 72°C for extension, and a final extension of 1 minute at 72°C. All samples underwent at least two rounds of PCR screening to confirm the presence or absence of an expansion. Fragment analysis was performed on PCR products via capillary gel electrophoresis using Applied Biosystems' 3730xl DNA Analyzers run with GSLiz600 sizing standard, and fragment sizes were determined using Peak Scanner (version 2.0).

Control DNA samples with known sizes of 21, 54, and 71 repeats based on LR sequencing were used as controls for assay calibration, optimization, and verification. DNA template amounts were tested at 20, 50, and 100 ng per 20 μL reaction, and the signal of expanded alleles was detectable with all template amounts using fragment analysis (data not shown). However, larger‐sized expanded allele showed lower amplification with reduced DNA concentrations, and allele dropout was observed with 20 ng of DNA per reaction for the largest control allele (71 repeats). Therefore, to maximize detection, all screening was performed with 100–250 ng of DNA. Control DNAs with repeats of 21, 54, and 71 units were included in duplicate on each 96‐well plate of screened samples to confirm reproducibility and reliability of the PCR assay and to calibrate fragment analysis in each run. Fragment sizes using the GSLiz600 sizing standard were found to be significantly reproducible, and linear correlation was used to calculate repeat units for unknown samples based on the amplicon sizes (bp) from fragment analysis with R
2 = 0.9999. Because expanded alleles can show a series of stuttered peaks, likely due to the natural variability in repeat sequences in tissues, for consistency, the average of the two largest peaks was used to size expanded alleles. PCR and fragment analysis on all subjects were repeated at least twice to confirm that sizing did not vary between runs.

To assess for the presence of pathogenic GGC repeat expansions in ZFHX3 in our undiagnosed ataxia cohort, we utilized a PCR assay (Fig. 1) that was optimized to enable low‐cost, high‐throughput screening with minimal DNA input from either blood or saliva samples. Fluorescent‐labeled primers flanking the repeat region were used for PCR amplification and fragment analysis, with sizing of repeat lengths determined from a correlation with control DNAs of known repeat size. The primer sequences used for optimal binding were identical to the primers reported in Dalski et al,
8
; however, in our method both the forward and reverse primers were fluorescently labeled, and we did not use a repeat‐specific primer. In addition, we utilized a high‐fidelity polymerase optimized for high‐GC content (Phusion, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the reaction was further adapted to disrupt DNA secondary structure caused by GC‐rich sequences through the inclusion of betaine
28
 and by employing high denaturation temperatures. This approach was applied to DNA samples obtained from 687 probands with undiagnosed ataxia from the UCLA Ataxia Center and the UCLA Clinical Neurogenomics Research Center Biorepository. Of these, 348 (50.7%) patients were male and 339 (49.3%) patients were female. The cohort was 69.7% White, 10.5% Asian, 3.5% Black, 0.1% Native American, 0.1% Middle Eastern or North African, and 8.7% Hispanic/Latino. The average age of onset for the cohort was 53 ± 18 years. The clinical presentation of the 687 probands in the cohort was divided between 527 (76.7%) patients with cerebellar ataxia, 36 (5.2%) patients with spastic paraplegia, and 124 (18.0%) patients with complex gait problems that could not be solely classified as ataxia (eg, combined with parkinsonism, dystonia, or other movement disorders). Among the patients with defined cerebellar ataxia, 149 (28.3%) were diagnosed with SCA, 130 (24.7%) with multiple system atrophy, 108 (20.5%) with pure cerebellar ataxia, 43 (8.2%) with ataxia plus neuropathy, 42 (8.0%) with spastic ataxia, and 10 (1.9%) with episodic ataxia.

From our cohort of 687 patients with undiagnosed ataxia, repeat expansions in ZFHX3 were observed in three probands (0.44% of the cohort) and orthogonally confirmed using ONT LR sequencing. This resulted in the identification of three multigenerational families (Fig. 2) with expanded ZFHX3 GGC repeats, the largest of which was 74 repeats (range 51–74 repeats). Unaffected members of these families did not harbor an expanded repeat.

In Family 1, pathogenic expansions were observed in four affected family members. II‐1 (54 repeats) had an age of onset of 40 years and presented with cerebellar ataxia and sensory neuropathy. I‐1 (52 repeats) and II‐2 (59 repeats) both exhibited ataxia, whereas III‐1 (64 repeats) remains asymptomatic in his 20s (Fig. 2).

For Family 2, expanded alleles were identified in five affected family members. VI‐2 (74 repeats) developed cerebellar ataxia, sensory neuropathy, and autonomic neuropathy at 21 years and subsequently died from disease complications. VI‐3 exhibited cerebellar ataxia and peripheral neuropathy, with an age of onset at 31 years. In addition to confirming repeat size via ONT LR sequencing, we used the TRGT to screen PacBio LR sequencing data, with both methods confirming an expanded allele of 71 repeats in this subject (Fig. 2). Of the other affected family members, V‐5 carried 54 repeats, V‐1 carried 58 repeats, and V‐3 carried 56 repeats.

Three affected individuals from Family 3 were identified with a pathogenic expansion. V‐3 had 59 repeats and presented with ataxia and neuropathy, with an age of onset of 39 years. V‐1 was found to have 52 repeats, whereas VI‐1 carried 63 repeats (Fig. 2).

Genetic ancestry inference using GenoTools confirmed that all three families clustered with European ancestry. We performed relatedness analysis on WGS data on patients from each of the three families and discovered that patients from families 2 and 3 share third‐degree relatedness, whereas the proband from Family 1 was not related to the other two families. The probands of families 2 and 3 were not aware of this relationship.

Because WGS has previously been used for bioinformatic repeat expansion detection in ZFHX3 using ExpansionHunter (EH), we validated our results in 29 of the previously screened subjects, including members of Family 1. EH agreed with the results of the PCR assay (Pearson's correlation r = 0.99, P = 5.33E‐08), successfully identifying an expanded allele in the ZFHX3 gene is all three members of Family 1, with repeat sizes of 46 for I‐1 (51 repeats using PCR analysis), 49 for II‐1 (54 repeats using PCR), and 54 for II‐2 (58 repeats using PCR).

We screened 687 patients with undiagnosed ataxia using an optimized low‐cost, high‐throughput PCR assay and identified pathogenic ZFHX3 GGC repeat expansions in three families, with repeat expansions ranging from 51 to 74 units. Two of these families were found to be distantly related to each other genetically. All expansions were confirmed using LR sequencing, and all three families were confirmed to be of Swedish ancestry based on haplotyping. WGS analysis of 29 probands (including the proband of Family 1) agreed with the results of the PCR assay and confirmed detection of an expanded allele in Family 1. Given the strong founder effect in patients of Swedish ancestry, we used haplotype analysis to screen for potential SCA4 candidates based on the presence of the haplotype associated with the expanded allele. An additional 852 subjects were tested for SCA4 bioinformatically, either for 6 coding SNPs using WES (144 subjects) or all 14 associated SNPs using WGS (708 subjects), bringing the total number of patients assessed in this study using all methods to 1539, although no potential SCA4 cases were identified bioinformatically.

Detecting the ZFHX3 repeat is challenging due to its GC‐rich content. The reference allele amplicon has a GC content of 67% based on the primers used here, whereas expanded alleles of 72–74 repeats reach up to ~80% GC. PCR‐free methods for detecting SCA4 repeat expansions may circumvent the difficulty in amplifying the repeats regions but can be more expensive and require larger amounts (micrograms) of DNA, which may not be available in all cases (Table S1). For example, a recent study applied Cas9‐mediated nanopore sequencing for multiple repeat expansions, requiring 5 μg of DNA per sample.
30
 PCR‐based methods, in contrast, allow high‐throughput screening of cohorts with limited amounts of DNA (Table S1). One PCR method for SCA4 has been previously described
8
 but relies on a proprietary commercial assay to disrupt the GC‐rich repeats (Bio‐Techne AmplideX Asuragen PCR/CE FMR1 Reagents), which increases cost. Our modified PCR protocol enables high‐throughput screening, with only 100–250 ng of DNA obtained from blood or saliva at a lower cost per sample. To assist in confirming the repeat size, the amplicons were also sequenced using ONT across the entire repeat region to orthogonally confirm the size of the repeat and the absence of sequence interruptions. The size estimation between fragment analysis and ONT sequencing was found to be highly correlated (Pearson's correlation, r = 0.99, P = 6.97E‐45).

However, given the observed presence of a divergent haplotype, subjects lacking these SNPs cannot be presumed negative, so screening for this broader haplotype, while potentially supportive, cannot be used as a substitute for direct detection of the expanded repeats. Because SNPs 11–14 are located within the repeat expansion region, it is likely that they are highly specific to the expansion, and they have been reported in all SCA4 patients genotyped for all four SNPs (Fig. 3). Supporting this, analysis of 708 undiagnosed ataxia patients did not identify any subjects with all four SNPs 11–14, and only one subject with three of them in a discontinuous pattern, who was subsequently not found to have an expanded ZFHX3 allele. The presence of SNPs 11–14 appears to be the minimally required haplotype for an expanded allele without any interruptions in the repeat region. Of the four SNPs 11–14 located within the repeat region, only SNP 13 was present in the SweGen database, and SNPs 11, 12, and 14 were not present. Comparison of the minimal haplotype (SNPs 11–14) between our patients and the SweGen database yielded a significant P = 0.04; however, this enrichment should be interpreted with caution, as the number of individuals in the SweGen database is small. We suspect that all previously reported SCA4 patients have SNPs 11–14 but not all SNPs were reported. Therefore, the presence of SNPs 11–14 appears to be the most basic haplotype upon which a ZFHX3 pathogenic repeat expansion has been observed and thus serves as a useful adjunct to support bioinformatic repeat expansion detection and, in the absence of bioinformatic detection, should prompt direct screening using either a LR sequencing or PCR‐based method to detect a potentially cryptic pathogenic repeat.

This study has several limitations that require mention. First, we have a small sample size of patients and therefore do not have the power to make a meaningful correlation analysis between repeat size and phenotype or assess for additional genetic or epigenetic modifiers. Second, although the PCR method we outline here was able to detect a range of expansions up to and including the longest repeat length reported so far (74 repeats), it is still possible that longer expansions exist that may be unable to amplify, as amplification of longer sequences of 100% GC content becomes more difficult with increased length. This is a limitation for all current PCR‐based methods of repeat detection and will likely be resolved by advancement in the clinical use of LR sequencing methods, which is the current gold standard for repeat expansion detection and sizing. Lastly, there is the lack of a large cohort of Swedish ancestry or other detailed genomic dataset to comprehensively assess the frequency of the SNPs associated with the expanded alleles within that population. Pairwise comparison of the 14 SNPs previously reported in the literature among the European population within the 1000 Genomes Project supports the majority of the SNPs having tight linkage due to close proximity to each other (D′ = 1).
