# Troriluzole attenuates opioid intake, reinforcing efficacy, seeking behaviours, physical dependence and antinociceptive tolerance in rats.
**Authors:** Galaj E, Inan S, Bi GH, Wiah S, Adamson G, Reitz AB, Xi ZX, Rawls SM
**Journal:** Br J Pharmacol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/bph.70206](https://doi.org/10.1111/bph.70206)

## Content

BACKGROUND AND PURPOSE: Riluzole, approved for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), has a glutamate-modulating profile favourable for mitigating opioid addiction. It reduces neuronal glutamate release and enhances glutamate reuptake, offering advantages over agents that only increase glutamate reuptake. However, riluzole has pharmacokinetic liabilities that limit repurposing. To overcome these limitations, we designed and prepared the prodrug troriluzole (TRLZ), which retains the mechanistic profile of riluzole but with optimized metabolic and pharmacokinetic properties (e.g., higher oral bioavailability and less pharmacokinetic variability). As dysregulation of glutamate systems during opioid exposure contributes to adverse opioid effects, we tested the hypothesis that TRLZ would inhibit several opioid-derived adverse effects in rats.

EXPERIMENTAL APPROACH: In male Long-Evans and Sprague-Dawley rats, effects of TRLZ on oxycodone self-administration were investigated under fixed-ratio and progressive-ratio reinforcement schedules. Effects of TRLZ on food self-administration were also investigated. TRLZ effects on cue-induced reinstatement of oxycodone seeking, and on morphine-induced physical dependence, antinociceptive tolerance and respiratory depression were also investigated. TRLZ was administered intraperitoneally.

KEY RESULTS: TRLZ dose-dependently reduced oxycodone self-administration, reinforcing efficacy and cue-induced reinstatement of oxycodone seeking without affecting inactive lever responding. TRLZ, at high doses, also inhibited food self-administration. TRLZ reduced naloxone-precipitated withdrawal signs in morphine-dependent rats and antinociceptive tolerance during repeated morphine exposure. TRLZ also attenuated morphine-induced respiratory depression.

CONCLUSIONS AND IMPLICATIONS: TRLZ, already in clinical trials for cerebellar ataxia, also reduced opioid taking and seeking as well as opioid-derived adverse effects in rats, supporting further study in treating opioid use disorders and pain management.

Over-prescription of opioids, along with increasing misuse of illicit fentanyl and its analogs, have led to a current opioid epidemic (Jones et al., 2024) While most pharmacological approaches have targeted opioid receptors given their predominant role in opioid effects, current opioid-based therapies such as methadone and buprenorphine present challenges, including tolerance, respiratory depression, and addiction liability (Jordan et al., 2019) Thus, a recent focus in drug discovery research, especially within the NIH Drug Repurposing program, is to characterize non-opioid approaches to develop non-addictive medications (Rasmussen et al., 2019)

Glutamate is one system linked to opioid addiction (Gao et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2018) Opioid exposure and abstinence dysregulates glutamate systems in brain regions underlying opioid-derived behaviors Heroin self-administration (SA) reduces cellular glutamate uptake and downregulates expression of glutamate transporter subtype 1 (GLT-1) (Shen et al., 2014), an astrocytic protein that clears extracellular glutamate Heroin seeking reinstated by cues or heroin prime elicits an increase in extracellular glutamate in the nucleus accumbens (NAC) core that is abolished by inhibiting glutamatergic afferents projecting from the prelimbic cortex to NAC core (LaLumiere and Kalivas, 2008) Ceftriaxone (CTX), a β-lactam antibiotic that enhances GLT-1 levels, reduces cue-induced heroin seeking by restoring glutamate reuptake and preventing synaptic glutamate spillover (Shen et al., 2014) CTX also inhibits development and reinstatement of morphine-induced conditioned place preference (CPP) (Fan et al., 2012; Hearing et al., 2016), physical dependence, analgesic tolerance, hyperalgesia, and hyperthermia in rats (Rawls et al., 2010; Chen et al., 2012; Habibi-Asl et al., 2014; Medrano et al., 2015) Clavulanic acid (CLAV), a CTX analog with better PK and fewer side-effect profiles, inhibits acquisition of morphine-induced CPP in rats and mice and reinstatement of morphine place preference in mice (Schoeder et al., 2014)

Despite strong preclinical evidence that normalizing glutamate reuptake and transmission mitigates opioid-induced behaviors, no glutamate-based agent is approved by the FDA for opioid use disorder One candidate with a favorable pharmacodynamic profile is riluzole, which is approved to treat amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) Riluzole enhances astrocytic glutamate reuptake by increasing GLT-1 expression (Brothers et al., 2013; Carbone et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2011) and inhibits neuronal glutamate release by inhibiting calcium and sodium channels and activating potassium channels (Cheah et al., 2010; Machado-Vieira et al, 2009; Pittenger et al, 2008) In terms of efficacy, the dual mechanism of riluzole offers advantages compared to β-lactam compounds that only enhance glutamate reuptake

Effects of riluzole on opioid use disorders have not been extensively investigated It does reduce development of morphine CPP in rats (Tzschentke and Schmidt, 1997) and mice (Wan et al., 2017) and morphine physical dependence in mice (Sepúlveda et al 1999) Riluzole reduces conditioned place aversion (CPA) in opioid-dependent rats (Jin et al., 2006) and normalizes the increase in extracellular glutamate in the NAC of rats during abstinence from chronic morphine exposure (Sepúlveda et al., 2004) Systemic or ICV administration of riluzole attenuates antinociceptive tolerance to morphine in the rat hot plate assay (Habibi-Asl et al 2009)

PK drawbacks of riluzole (e.g., negative effects on liver function, low water solubility, rapid metabolism and poor oral palatability) have limited repurposing avenues for CNS diseases To overcome these limitations of riluzole, we designed and generated troriluzole (TRLZ), a third generation riluzole prodrug TRLZ retains the glutamate-modulating profile of riluzole but with improved PK and safety profiles (Sevinsky et al., 2024; Bertz et al., 2024; Pelletier et al., 2018) We determined if TRLZ reduces oxycodone SA and reinforcing efficacy, cue-induced reinstatement of oxycodone seeking and opioid tolerance and withdrawal Since respiratory failure is the leading cause of death associated with opioid use, we investigated effects of TRLZ against morphine-induced respiratory depression

Male Long–Evans rats (Charles River, Frederick, MD) (initially weighing 250–300 g) were used in self-administration (SA) experiments During experimentation, rats were housed individually in a climate-controlled animal colony room with 21-23°C temperature and 40-60% humidity, on a reversed 12-h light–dark cycle (lights on at 7am) with access to food and water Experiments were conducted during the active period (dark cycle)

For physical dependence, respiratory depression, and tolerance studies, male Sprague-Dawley rats (Envigo Laboratories, Indianapolis, IN) (initially weighing 250–300 g) were used Housing conditions and animal care were consistent with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council, 2011) Protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees at the National Institute on Drug Abuse Intramural Research Program, Temple University, and Colgate University

Under standard aseptic conditions and xylazine/ketamine (90/10 mg kg−1, ip) anesthesia, rats were prepared for oxycodone SA, as described (Galaj et al., 2022, 2020) Briefly, a microrenathane catheter (Braintree Scientific Inc., Braintree, MA, US) was implanted in the right jugular vein and its free end was fed subcutaneously along the scapula and connected to a 22-gauge stainless connector that was mounted to the rat’s skull using four stainless steel screws and dental acrylic To prevent clogging, catheters were flushed daily with a gentamicin-heparin-saline solution (30 IU/ml heparin; ICN Biochemicals, Cleveland, OH, USA) immediately after surgery and on subsequent days

Intravenous oxycodone SA sessions were conducted in standard operant conditioning chambers (Med-Associates, VT, US), each housed in a sound-attenuating box Each chamber was equipped with two retractable levers, a white light and tone above the active lever and a drug line connected to a syringe pump (PHM-100, 3 33 rpm; Med-Associates, VT, US)

Oxycodone hydrochloride (provided by National Institute on Drug Abuse IRP Pharmacy) was dissolved in 0 9% saline to achieve final doses of 0 05 mg kg−1/infusion Morphine sulfate was purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St Louis, MO, US) and dissolved in saline to achieve a final dose of 5 mg kg−1 Morphine pellets (75 mg) were obtained from the National Institute on Drug Abuse Drug Supply Program (Bethesda, MD, USA) Troriluzole monohydrochloride (TRLZ) was designed and synthesized by Fox Chase Therapeutics Discovery, Inc (Doylestown, PA, US) (Pelletier et al., 2018) TRLZ was dissolved in distilled water and administered (ip) 30 min before testing

We first investigated whether TRLZ reduces oxycodone reward in rats self-administering oxycodone under a fixed ratio 1 (FR1) schedule of reinforcement Animals were trained to self-administer oxycodone (0 05 mg kg−1/infusion) under an FR1 schedule of reinforcement during daily 3-h sessions Responding on the active lever activated the syringe pump for 4 2 s, causing delivery of oxycodone and activation of the light and tone stimuli above the active lever Responses on the inactive lever were counted but had no consequences During the infusion period, additional responses on the active lever were recorded, but did not lead to additional infusions Once animals demonstrated a pattern of stable responding, defined as less than 20 % variability in daily oxycodone intake across three consecutive sessions, and an active/inactive lever pressing ratio exceeding 2:1, they were tested with TRLZ at 0, 8 or 16 mg kg−1 (n=8/group) Animals then received an additional 5–7 days of oxycodone SA until a baseline response rate was reestablished before being tested with another dose of TRLZ

Next, we investigated effects of TRLZ on motivation to seek oxycodone Rats were initially trained under a FR1 schedule of reinforcement as outlined above Rats with stable responding were then allowed to self-administer oxycodone (0 05 mg kg−1/infusion) under a progressive ratio (PR) schedule of reinforcement The number of lever presses required to receive a single infusion of oxycodone increased according to the following schedule: 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 15, 20, 25, 32, 40, 50 and so on Under this PR schedule the animals eventually stopped responding and reached a break point (BP) with final ratio, operationally defined as number of lever presses for the last oxycodone infusion before a 60-min period during which no oxycodone reward was obtained Once responding stabilized with day-to-day variability in BP within 1 or 2 ratio increments for 3 consecutive days, animals were tested with TRLZ Thirty min before a test session, rats were injected with TRLZ [0 (n=8), 8 (n=7), or 16 mg kg−1 (n=7)] and later allowed to lever press for oxycodone under the same conditions In succeeding days behavior was re-stabilized and 5-6 days later rats were tested with a different, randomly chosen, dose of TRLZ

We then determined if TRLZ reduces reinstatement of oxycodone seeking After oxycodone SA under FR1 and PR schedules of reinforcement, the extinction phase began during which responding on either lever produced no consequences; oxycodone was not delivered, and drug cues were not presented This phase continued for 20 days The cue-induced reinstatement test (3-h long) occurred the next day Rats were injected with TRLZ (0, 8, or 16 mg kg−1; n=7 in each group) 30 min before the test session Non-contingent oxycodone cues (4 2 s light/tone and 4 2 s empty syringe pump) were presented twice, 2 min apart, at the beginning of a reinstatement session Active lever presses were reinforced with the oxycodone cue (light/tone/ pump) but oxycodone was not delivered Responding on the inactive lever produced no consequences Presses on active and inactive levers were then analyzed

The effects of TRLZ on food SA were tested in Long Evans rats Rats, weighing on average 350 g at the start of the experiment, were food restricted to bring their weight to the 85% of free feeding weight Rats were introduced to sugar pellets (BioServ, NJ; 45 mg) in their home cages before experimentation Next, rats were placed in the operant chamber with two levers and light/tone stimuli for daily 60-min sessions for 3 weeks Presses on the active lever delivered one food pellet and activated 5-s light/tone stimuli located above the lever, based on an FR1 schedule of reinforcement Presses on the other lever had no programmed consequences After rats showed a stable pattern of responding on 3 consecutive days (defined as no more than 15% of variation in responding), they were tested with vehicle or one of the TRLZ doses (4, 8, or 16 mg kg−1, ip) (n=9 rats in each group, randomly chosen) and their responding for food were measured Each rat was tested with 3 doses of TRLZ after a new baseline was established Tests were conducted 3-5 days apart

Oxygen saturation (SpO2) and respiratory rate (RR) were measured using a MouseOX® Plus – pulse oximeter (mouse and rat pulse oximeter, Starr Life Sciences Corp., Oakmont, PA, USA) in freely moving rats Rats were acclimated individually in observation boxes for 30 min Then, a collar with a sensor was placed to the neck of the rats and without any recording, rats were acclimated for another 30 min To determine if TRLZ affected morphine-induced respiratory depression, rats were pretreated with vehicle (n=7) or TRLZ (8 mg kg−1, n=8) and recording was started to determine whether TRLZ impacted respiration Fifteen min later, all rats received morphine (5 mg kg−1, sc) and recording was continued for another 60 min Respiration and SpO2 were recorded every second and averaged over 1-min periods for a total of 75 min The morphine dose was chosen based on studies showing that 5 mg kg−1 reduces oxygen saturation (Bongiovanni et al., 2022) Separate experiments investigated the effects of riluzole (8 mg kg−1) against SpO2 and respiration following acute morphine (5 mg kg−1)

The effect of TRLZ (8 mg kg−1, ip) on the antinociceptive efficacy of morphine was measured using the hot plate assay of thermal nociception The hot plate (Ugo Basile Srl 7280, Gemonio, Italy) with a pre-set plate temperature of 52 °C was used The assay was performed on day 1 after the first morphine injection, and on day 5 following chronic morphine administration Two doses of morphine, 5 and 7 5 mg kg−1 (sc injection), were tested, and chosen based on previous studies (Paul et al., 2021) On both experimental days, rats were brought to the testing room and acclimated for 1 h Rats were assigned randomly to the following groups: vehicle (ip) + vehicle (sc) (n=7); TRLZ + vehicle (n=7); vehicle + morphine (5 mg kg−1) (n=8); TRLZ + morphine (5 mg kg−1) (n=8); vehicle + morphine (7 5 mg kg−1) (n=7); or TRLZ + morphine (7 5 mg kg−1) (n=7) TRLZ or vehicle (for TRLZ) was administered 15 min before saline or morphine On day 1, following acclimation, baseline response latency was measured two times (1 h apart) and averaged as pre-drug latency The response latency was the time between placing the animal on the heated plate and its reaction (licking, shaking or stepping of hind paws or jumping) Then, rats were injected with TRLZ or vehicle and administered saline or morphine 15 min later Latencies were measured at 30, 60, 90, and 120 min following saline or morphine administration The cut-off time was 60 s to prevent tissue damage Drugs were administered 2 times (8 AM and 6 PM) daily for 4 days On day 5, the hot plate test was repeated following administration of the morning doses Latencies on days 1 and 5 were converted to percent of maximum possible effect (% MPE) according to the formula:

%MPE=((postdruglatency−predruglatency)∕(cut-offlatency−predruglatency))∗100


Rats were euthanized at the end of the testing on day 5

Rats were implanted (sc, flank area) with 75 mg morphine pellets that remained intact for 7 days (Yoburn et al., 1985) Beginning on the first day of pellet implantation and continuing throughout the 7-d implantation period, rats received daily injections of TRLZ (4, 8, or 12 mg kg−1, ip, delivered in a ml kg−1 volume, n=8/group) On test day, rats were transported to the testing room and allowed to acclimate for 60 min before naloxone hydrochloride (10 mg kg−1, ip, delivered in a 1 ml kg−1 volume) was injected to precipitate withdrawal signs Immediately after naloxone administration, rats were placed into individual observation boxes for 60 min and observed for 30 min for withdrawal signs including wet dog shakes, paw tremors, jumping, teeth chattering, and eye blinking Signs were counted and then scored as follows: 0 = none; 1 = 1–5 times; 2 = 6–10 times; and 3 = >10 times A global withdrawal score was calculated as sum of these scores for each group (Inan et al., 2024) Weights were measured before and 2 h after naloxone injection Using the same paradigm described above, we conducted separate experiments to investigate the effects of riluzole (12 mg kg−1) on withdrawal signs and weight in morphine-dependent rats

In behavioral pharmacology experiments, it is a standard practice to use n=7-10 rats per group Based on our previous experience, n=7/group is usually sufficient to achieve significance We calculated a sample size that is needed to obtain power ≥ 0 80 for our studies All studies have n=7-9 rats per group For in vivo studies, the data and statistical analysis are performed according to the recommendations of the British Journal of Pharmacology on experimental design and analysis in pharmacology (Curtis et al., 2018) Studies were also designed to generate groups of approximately equal size No data points were excluded from the analysis in any experiment In cases of variation in group size, this was due to animals being dropped from the experiment for reasons such as removal of a headpiece in SA experiments, obstruction or clogging of IV catheters in SA experiments, or animal health To validate the use of parametric statistics, we ensured that our data were normally distributed, and the assumption of homogeneity was met (aka, data in each group had approximately equal variance) GraphPad Prism (version 10, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used for creation of graphs and statistical analysis Data in graphs are presented as mean + S E M P < 0 05 was considered statistically significant in all cases

For SA studies (Experiments 1-4), for the oxycodone IVSA experiment, the total numbers of infusions were averaged and compared across groups with a one-way ANOVA This was followed by a Dunnett’s test Active and inactive lever presses were averaged and compared across groups using a two-way ANOVA (lever × treatment) Infusions earned under a PR schedule were analyzed with a two-way ANOVA (phase × treatment), followed by a Dunnett’s test For extinction data, active and inactive lever presses were analyzed by separate two-way ANOVAS (session × time) For reinstatement testing, treatments (vehicle or 2 doses of TRLZ) were administered during the reinstatement test Separate one-way ANOVAs were used to assess “lever responses” for active and inactive presses For food SA, we used a two-way ANOVA (phase × TRLZ dose) and one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s test

For non-contingent (experimenter-injected) studies (Experiments 5-7), for respiratory depression data, two-way ANOVA (treatment × time) was used followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test Area under the curve analysis between the 2 groups (morphine by itself or morphine in combination with TRLZ) was performed using an unpaired Student’s t-test For morphine tolerance studies, two-way ANOVA (treatment × time) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test were used For physical dependence studies, one-way ANOVA was used to analyze weight gain and global withdrawal scores followed by Dunnett’s test

TRLZ reduced oxycodone SA under FR1 and PR reinforcement schedules, indicating a capacity to reduce opioid reward and motivation to seek opioids TRLZ reduced cue-induced reinstatement of oxycodone seeking, morphine physical dependence, and morphine tolerance TRLZ also prevented opioid-induced respiratory depression, demonstrating a favorable safety profile Our results are the first evidence that TRLZ, or riluzole, reduces opioid intake, reinforcing efficacy, and relapse-like behaviors in SA assays In the context of SA, riluzole, the parent drug, reduces ethanol SA in mice (Besheer et al., 2009) and cue- or cocaine-primed reinstatement of seeking in rats (Sepulveda-Orengo et al., 2017) Only one study has investigated effects of TRLZ against an addictive drug, where it reduced METH-induced CPP in rats (Wiah et al., 2023) Importantly, in rats, TRLZ is not rewarding or aversive and does not reduce spontaneous locomotor activity at doses that inhibit METH-induced effects (Wiah et al., 2023)

Although cellular mechanisms of TRLZ were not a primary focus of the current study, heroin SA downregulates glutamate uptake and GLT-1 expression in the NAC of rats (Shen et al., 2014) Reinstatement of heroin seeking in rats increases extracellular glutamate in the NAC (Siemsen et al., 2023; LaLumiere and Kalivas, 2008) Both heroin-induced downregulation of GLT-1 and cue-induced heroin seeking behaviors are prevented by CTX (Shen et al., 2014) Since TRLZ, like CTX, enhances glutamate reuptake (Frizzo et al., 2024), it may have reduced reinstatement of oxycodone seeking by normalizing dysregulated glutamate signaling in the NAC TRLZ has the added capacity to inhibit glutamate release from presynaptic terminals by blocking voltage-gated sodium channels (Urbani and Belluzzi, 2000) or voltage-gated calcium channels (Wang et al., 2004) Thus, in the presence of TRLZ, neuronal glutamate release during reinstatement may have also been reduced, leading to diminished oxycodone seeking

Oxycodone SA (FR1 schedule) and reinforcing efficacy (PR schedule) were also reduced by TRLZ Although glutamate systems contribute to opioid reward and reinforcement, mechanisms are less defined than those involved in reinstatement of opioid seeking Acute rewarding effects of opioids are facilitated by increased dopamine release in the NAC However, during chronic opioid exposure, glutamatergic input onto neurons in the NAC is enhanced, leading to a progressive increase in the opioid rewarding effect (Heinsbroek et al., 2021) In our studies, the normal glutamatergic drive onto these NAC neurons during oxycodone SA may have been inhibited by TRLZ, leading to reduced oxycodone SA and reinforcement Our interpretation is supported by evidence that disrupting glutamatergic transmission reduces opioid SA For example, mGluR5 antagonists reduce heroin SA in mice (Brown et al., 2011) and development, but not expression, of morphine CPP in rats (Roohi et al., 2014) mGluR2/3 agonists, which reduce glutamate release, attenuate heroin intake and the break point for reward motivation (Zhu et al., 2017) The possibility that TRLZ acted though non-glutamatergic mechanisms to reduce oxycodone SA cannot be excluded For example, riluzole inhibits protein kinase C (PKC) and prevents PKC depletion (Noh et al., 2000), and PKCγ activation in the limbic forebrain during opioid exposure contributes to opioid reinforcement (Ma et al., 2018) A role for GABA systems cannot be excluded either, since riluzole increases GABA transmission (He et al., 2002) and GABA interneuron activation in the VTA inhibits mesolimbic dopamine release (Shields et al., 2021)

TRLZ reduced food reward but only at the highest dose of 16 mg kg−1 In contrast, OXY-derived SA behaviors were more sensitive to TRLZ, with a dose of 8 mg kg−1 reducing OXY SA under FR1 conditions and reinstatement of OXY seeking These results are consistent with evidence that riluzole reduces reinstatement of cocaine seeking but not reinstatement of sucrose seeking (Sepulveda-Orengo et al., 2018) Since other glutamate transport modulators (e.g., β-lactam compounds) have generally been shown to not reduce natural rewards (e.g., sucrose) (Nall et al., 2022; Knackstedt et al., 2021), the higher dose of TRLZ (16 mg kg−1) may have acted through non-glutamatergic mechanisms to reduce food SA Indeed, riluzole reduces dopamine and acetylcholine release (Jehle et al., 2000), perhaps by inhibiting calcium and sodium channels and activating potassium channels (Cheah et al., 2010; Machado-Vieira et al, 2009; Pittenger et al, 2008) Since blocking dopamine transmission reduces food SA (Soler-Cedeño et al., 2025), the reduction in food SA by the highest dose of TRLZ may have been partly due to inhibition of dopamine release

TRLZ also prevented antinociceptive tolerance to morphine Dysregulation of glutamate transporters, along with NMDA receptor or PKC activation, facilitates opioid tolerance and physical dependence (Zhao et al., 2012) Morphine tolerance is accompanied by reduced GLT-1 activity (Wu et al., 2008) and inhibited by MS-153, a pharmacologically diverse agent that enhances glutamate uptake in COS-7 cells (Nakagawa et al., 2001), and by CTX (Rawls et al., 2010) For our results, a plausible explanation is that TRLZ preserved morphine’s antinociceptive efficacy by normalizing enhanced glutamate transmission at inotropic and metabotropic glutamate receptors that facilitates development of antinociceptive tolerance TRLZ was not antinociceptive in our experiments, but riluzole is antinociceptive in preclinical models of arthritis and neuropathy (Thompson et al., 2015)

TRLZ reduced the severity of morphine physical dependence Acute riluzole administration attenuates naloxone-precipitated morphine withdrawal symptoms, and chronic riluzole treatment prevents development of morphine physical dependence in mice (Sepúlveda et al., 1999) Since activation of glutamate systems in the brain enables development of morphine physical dependence (Trujillo and Akil, 1991), TRLZ may have dampened activation of these systems during chronic morphine exposure, thereby reducing naloxone-precipitated withdrawal behaviors Multiple brain regions (e.g., locus coeruleus, hypothalamus, paraventricular thalamic nucleus (PVT), and NAC shell) contribute to morphine physical dependence, but a role for the PVT is conceivable given that glutamatergic projections from the PVT to NAC shell are activated by chronic morphine exposure and required for the expression of opioid withdrawal signs (Zhu et al., 2018)

We also evaluated the effects of a single dose of riluzole against morphine physical dependence We used a dose of 12 mg kg−1 riluzole to compare outcomes with TRLZ, which was highly effective at this dose in reducing behavioral signs and weight loss associated with naloxone-precipitated morphine withdrawal Riluzole, unlike TRLZ, was ineffective, which may be due to PK differences between the two drugs Our negative results with riluzole were also different than prior results with mice (Sepúlveda et al 1999), where riluzole reduced morphine physical dependence, suggesting that riluzole efficacy may vary across species

TRLZ prevented the reduction in oxygen saturation after acute morphine injection, demonstrating a favorable safety profile Mechanisms underlying TRLZ efficacy are unclear, but glutamate reuptake is disrupted during hypoxia and ischemia In cortical astrocytes, hypoxia for 24 h reduces glutamate uptake as well as GLT-1 levels (Dallas et al., 2007) Thus, in our experiments, TRLZ may have prevented morphine-induced hypoxia by normalizing deficits in glutamate reuptake that contributed to morphine-induced decreases in oxygen saturation Interestingly, glutamate and opioid systems overlap extensively in brainstem regions that regulate breathing Vesicular glutamate transporter 2 (VGLUT2), one of three glutamate transporters that medicate vesicular glutamate uptake, is highly expressed in brainstem cells that are vulnerable to opioid regulation (Furdui et al., 2024) Approximately 50% of these VGLUT2 cells co-express μ-opioid mRNA (Furdui et al., 2024), indicating possible opioid-glutamate crosstalk in the brainstem

A limitation of our study is that TRLZ was not tested in female rats, which often acquire heroin SA faster than males under FR conditions and display an upward shift in the heroin-dose response curve that reflects enhanced vulnerability (George et al., 2021) In addition, male rats can be more susceptible to developing antinociceptive tolerance and more sensitive to naloxone-precipitated withdrawal (Craft et al., 1999) (but see Alonso-Caraballo et al., 2024) Thus, TRLZ may display different efficacy and potency in male and female rats Another limitation of our study is the lack of cellular mechanism studies Thus, further studies are required to address these issues

From a translational perspective, TRLZ retains the diverse glutamate-modulating mechanistic profile of riluzole but with more favorable PK and metabolic properties Riluzole displays extensive hepatic metabolism and exceptionally high levels of patient-to-patient variability related to variable first-pass elimination effects caused by heterogeneous cytochrome P450 isoform Cyp1A2 expression (Groeneveld et al., 2001) This is why many ALS patients do not respond well to riluzole, because they metabolize it so quickly and there is little drug available to have a therapeutic effect Additional problems with riluzole include dose-dependent negative effects on liver function and poor oral palatability TRLZ has a more favorable PK profile including higher oral bioavailability associated with actions on PepT1 (solute carrier that facilitates oligopeptide absorption from the small intestine), longer duration of absorption, and lower PK variability (Sevinsky et al., 2024; Bertz et al., 2024; Pelletier et al., 2018) These PK advantages of TRLZ support the potential of better translational and repurposing avenues compared to riluzole

In conclusion, our studies showed that TRLZ reduced a myriad of opioid-derived adverse effects Since TRLZ enhances both glutamate reuptake and inhibit neuronal glutamate release, it offers potential for greater efficacy than pharmacologically related glutamate drugs that act primarily though one mechanism (CTX, clavulanic acid, N-acetylcysteine) Overall, TRLZ, which is already showing positive effects in a clinical trial for spinocerebellar ataxia, warrants further study for a potential to mitigate opioid-related adverse effects
