# Molecular and Clinical Characterization of the Hb Tübingen [β106(G8) Leu→ Gln, HBB: c.320 T>A] Associated With Congenital Methemoglobinemia in a Chinese Family.
**Authors:** Luo H, Ren Z, Ye Y, Hassan RN, Fu X, Wu T, Chen Y, Luo X
**Journal:** J Clin Lab Anal (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/jcla.70178](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcla.70178)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Congenital methemoglobinemia caused by hemoglobin variants is a rare hematological disorder often misdiagnosed due to overlapping features with enzymatic defects. Hb Tübingen, a β-globin chain variant (βCD106), is characterized by increased autoxidation, heat instability, and cyanosis.

METHODS: A 7-year-old Chinese boy with cyanosis and recurrent neurological symptoms was evaluated using hemoglobin electrophoresis, HPLC, methemoglobin quantification, genetic sequencing, and brain imaging.

RESULTS: Genetic analysis identified the HBB: c.320 T>A mutation, confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Hemoglobin electrophoresis revealed a false elevation of Hb F (52.3%) due to co-migration of Hb Tübingen with Hb F, which was validated by HPLC showing normal γ-globin levels. Methemoglobinemia was elevated to 34.4%, accompanied by hemolytic markers (elevated LDH). Brain imaging confirmed Moyamoya disease, a rare cerebrovascular disorder, suggesting potential hypoxia-driven vascular pathology. Family screening revealed autosomal dominant inheritance, with the mutation traced to the patient's mother and maternal relatives.

CONCLUSIONS: This study underscores the diagnostic challenges of Hb Tübingen, particularly its mimicry of Hb F, necessitating further molecular diagnostic approaches. The association with Moyamoya disease highlights unexplored interactions between chronic hypoxia and cerebrovascular remodeling. Our findings expand the genetic epidemiology of Hb Tübingen and emphasize integrated hematological and neurological evaluations in unexplained cyanosis.

Congenital methemoglobinemia is a rare genetic disorder characterized by elevated levels of methemoglobin in the blood, leading to tissue hypoxia and cyanosis. Hemoglobin normally exists in two forms: oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin [ 1 , 2 ]. However, under certain conditions, hemoglobin can be oxidized to methemoglobin, which cannot effectively release oxygen to tissues. In healthy individuals, a small amount of methemoglobin is continuously formed and converted back to normal hemoglobin by the enzyme NADH‐cytochrome b5 reductase [ 2 ]. However, in individuals with congenital methemoglobinemia, this enzyme is deficient or dysfunctional due to genetic mutations, resulting in the accumulation of methemoglobin in the blood. This accumulation leads to a reduced ability of the blood to deliver oxygen to tissues and organs, thereby causing cyanosis and other related symptoms. The condition can be inherited in an autosomal recessive manner and has been reported in various ethnic populations worldwide [ 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ]. For instance, a case of autosomal recessive congenital methemoglobinemia type I caused by a novel mutation (R192C) in the CYB5R3 gene was identified in eight Indian patients [ 7 ]. In addition to the enzymatic deficiency, certain hemoglobin variants, such as hemoglobin M, can also lead to congenital methemoglobinemia. These variants have an inherent structural abnormality that predisposes them to oxidation, resulting in the formation of methemoglobin [ 2 , 8 ].

Hemoglobin Tübingen (Hb Tübingen) is a variant hemoglobin that has been identified in several studies and is associated with congenital methemoglobinemia. First discovered by Kleihauer and co‐workers in two members of a German family, Hb Tübingen is a β‐chain variant resulting from a point mutation at position βCD106, where leucine is replaced by glutamine (β106Leu → Gln) [ 9 ]. This mutation leads to structural and functional alterations of hemoglobin, making it prone to oxidation and the formation of methemoglobin. The carriers of Hb Tübingen typically present with variable, compensated hemolysis and cyanosis. The hemolytic anemia associated with Hb Tübingen is thought to be due to the instability of the hemoglobin variant, which results in the precipitation of hemoglobin within red blood cells, causing hemolysis. Further studies have shown that Hb Tübingen has a reduced oxygen affinity and increased auto‐oxidation rate, which contributes to the development of methemoglobinemia [ 10 ]. The clinical significance of Hb Tübingen is that it can cause a range of symptoms, from mild cyanosis to more severe hemolytic anemia. The diagnosis of Hb Tübingen is often made through hemoglobin electrophoresis and other laboratory tests. In terms of treatment, the management of Hb Tübingen‐related methemoglobinemia may involve the use of methylene blue or other reducing agents to convert methemoglobin back to normal hemoglobin, although the effectiveness of these treatments can vary depending on the individual's specific genetic makeup and the severity of the condition [ 11 ]. Hb Tübingen is an unstable hemoglobin variant that can lead to hemolysis and methemoglobinemia. The clinical significance of this combination with an unstable variant is that patients with this mutation can present with hemolysis varying in severity, auto‐oxidation and methemoglobinemia, spuriously low pulse oximetry readings, and a shorter lifespan. An astute and resourceful clinical team is required to make a proper and timely diagnosis of a hemoglobin variant. The study of Hb Tübingen and other hemoglobin variants has provided valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying methemoglobinemia and has contributed to the development of more effective diagnostic and treatment strategies for patients with these conditions. Globally, only two families with Hb Tübingen have been reported—the original German pedigree and a Belgian case [ 11 ]. Its rarity and diagnostic challenges (e.g., false elevation of fetal hemoglobin (Hb F) on electrophoresis) underscore the need for advanced techniques, such as high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and genetic sequencing, to avoid misdiagnosis.

Here, we report the first study of Hb Tübingen in the Chinese population, caused by the HBB: c.320 T>A mutation. The patient presented with concurrent Moyamoya disease, a rare cerebrovascular disorder, raising questions about potential hematological contributions to vascular pathology. This study highlights the importance of molecular diagnostics in unraveling complex clinical phenotypes and expands the genetic spectrum of hemoglobinopathies in China.

Written informed consent from all the participants and/or their family members was obtained as outlined by the protocol approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Shenzhen Children's Hospital, Shenzhen, China (Approval number: 202501402). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The proband, a 7‐year‐old male presenting with recurrent aphasia, facial deviation, and right‐sided limb weakness, underwent comprehensive clinical assessment at Shenzhen Children's Hospital. Clinical data, including medical history, physical examinations, neuroimaging (MRI/MRV), and laboratory findings, were systematically reviewed. A detailed three‐generation pedigree was constructed, identifying autosomal dominant inheritance of congenital methemoglobinemia through the maternal lineage: the proband's mother, maternal grandfather, and maternal great‐grandmother were clinically affected (Figure 1A). Peripheral blood samples were collected from the proband and his family members, with EDTA used as an anticoagulant. Genomic DNA was extracted from these samples using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany), following the manufacturer's protocol. The quality and quantity of the extracted DNA were assessed to ensure that the samples met the requirements for Sanger sequencing.

The HBB gene was amplified using primers flanking exon (B‐C‐F1:5′‐GATCCATCTACATATCCCAAAGC‐3′; B‐C‐R1:5′‐TCATTCGTCTGTTTCCCATTC‐3′; B‐C‐F2:5′‐AATCTCTTTCTTTCAGGGCAATA‐3′; B‐C‐R2:5′‐AACCTCCAAATCAAGCCTCTAC‐3′). In this family, DNA samples from four individuals were subjected to Sanger sequencing for co‐segregation analysis of inheritance and variation. The process of amplification, sequencing, and analysis of target fragments has been previously described [ 12 ].

We isolated total RNA from the peripheral blood of family members using Hipure Blood/Liquid RNA Kit (Magen, China). To obtain cDNA, reverse transcription using the PrimeScript RT reagent KIT (Takara Biomedical Technology, Beijing, China) was performed. We used Primer‐BLAST software (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer‐blast/) to design the primers for amplification of the second exon of HBB (5′‐TTGGACCCAGAGGTTCTTTGAG‐3′ and 5′‐ACACCAGCCACCACTTTCTG). BLAST tool (blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) was used to determine the specificity of both pairs of primers.

HPLC analysis was performed using a Aeris 3.6 μm WIDEPORE C4, 200 Å, 150 x 4.6 mm, LC Column (00F‐4486‐E0, Phenomenex) at 220 nm and 100 x 4.6‐mm cation exchange column (104CT0315, Phenomenex) at 418 nm on an HPLC machine (Agilent 1260 Infinity II). Samples were eluted with a gradient mixture of solution A (water/acetonitrile/trifluoroacetic acid, 700:300:0.7) and solution B (water/acetonitrile/trifluoroacetic acid, 450:550:0.5). Analysis and peak integration were performed using Open LAB CDS Chem Station software (Agilent). Lysates of cord blood and peripheral blood RBCs from healthy donors were used as controls.

The patient, a 7‐year‐old male, presented with recurrent aphasia, facial deviation, and right‐sided limb weakness. On clinical evaluation, he exhibited cyanosis with a peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) of 86%. Laboratory investigations revealed an elevated methemoglobin level of 34.4% (normal range: < 1%), indicative of congenital methemoglobinemia. Hematological analysis showed markers of hemolysis, including elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 582 IU/L (Table 1). Hematological analysis revealed the proband's mother was asymptomatic, whereas the father exhibited low mean corpuscular volume (MCV) and low mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH). Similarly, both the proband and his younger brother demonstrated microcytosis (low MCV) and reduced MCH, although all maintained normal hemoglobin concentrations (Table 2). Hemoglobin electrophoresis demonstrated a brownish band migrating similarly to hemoglobin F (Hb F) (Figure 1B). Neuroimaging, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), confirmed the presence of Moyamoya disease (Figure 1C), characterized by bilateral cerebral ischemia, left hemispheric atrophy, and vascular stenosis with extensive collateral vessel formation. Family history revealed that the patient's mother, maternal grandfather, and maternal great‐grandmother were also affected by congenital methemoglobinemia (Figure 1A, Table 2).

The Sanger sequences for the HBB gene of the patient and other family members were analyzed. The previously reported variation associated with β‐thalassemia identified at position c.52A>T in the HBB gene was inherited from the proband's father. The variation associated with methemoglobinemia, HBB: c.320 T>A, was inherited from the proband's mother. A family history of methemoglobinemia was reported on the maternal side, with several affected individuals carrying the same HBB: c.320 T>A variant. This observation supports a consistent genotype–phenotype correlation within the family, reinforcing the pathogenicity of this mutation in relation to methemoglobinemia. The Sanger sequencing chromatograms of the patient and his parents are shown in Figure 1D. To verify transcriptional isoforms of the HBB: c.320 T>A variant, RNA was extracted from the proband and his family members, followed by RT‐PCR and Sanger sequencing. The results confirmed that the HBB: c.320 T>A variant was stably transcribed in the proband, his younger brother, and their mother. The Sanger sequencing chromatograms of the patient and his parents are shown in Figure 2. These findings further support the consistent genotype–phenotype correlation associated with the HBB: c.320 T>A variant. To clarify whether additional γ‐globin variants might account for the apparently elevated Hb F levels in the proband, we performed sequencing of the γ‐globin gene promoters. No mutations associated with hereditary persistence of fetal hemoglobin (HPFH) or other variants were identified (File S1).

Given that routine hemoglobin capillary electrophoresis revealed elevated Hb F (α₂γ₂) levels (ranging from 10% to 52%) in individuals carrying the HBB: c.320 T>A variant, we sought to determine whether this increase in Hb F was genuine. To this end, we performed HPLC to analyze the expression levels of individual globin chains. The results showed that, although the proband's mother carried the HBB: c.320 T>A variant and exhibited an Hb F level of 10% by capillary electrophoresis, no γ‐globin chains were detected by HPLC (Figure 3), indicating that there was insufficient γ‐globin to form Hb F. Similarly, while the proband and his younger brother exhibited low levels of γ‐globin chains (Figure 3), the quantity was not sufficient to account for the markedly elevated Hb F levels (up to 50%) observed by capillary electrophoresis. These findings suggest that in individuals with methemoglobinemia caused by the HBB: c.320 T>A variant, the elevated Hb F levels detected by routine capillary electrophoresis may represent false‐positive results.

In this study, we report the first case of Hb Tübingen caused by the HBB: c.320 T>A (p. Leu106Gln) mutation in the Chinese population. This variant, previously described only in a limited number of families worldwide, including German and Belgian pedigrees [ 10 , 11 ], represents an exceptionally rare cause of congenital methemoglobinemia. The clinical presentation of our proband—persistent cyanosis, evidence of hemolysis, and the unexpected co‐occurrence of Moyamoya disease—highlights the multifaceted systemic impact of this structurally unstable hemoglobin variant and expands its known geographic and phenotypic spectrum.

Hb Tübingen is characterized by a missense mutation at codon 106 in the HBB gene, resulting in the substitution of leucine with glutamine at a highly conserved residue within the G‐helix of the β‐chain [ 10 , 11 ]. Cyanosis, a hallmark of congenital methemoglobinemia, arises from the accumulation of methemoglobin, which impairs oxygen delivery to tissues. The elevated methemoglobin level of 34.4% in this patient is consistent with the pathophysiology of Hb Tübingen. The disruption of the hydrophobic core not only affects the stability of the hemoglobin molecule but also increases its autoxidation rate, as previously reported in studies characterizing Hb Tübingen [ 10 , 11 ]. This results in a hemoglobin molecule that is more susceptible to oxidation, thereby promoting the conversion of normal hemoglobin to methemoglobin. The labile nature of Hb Tübingen also contributes to hemolysis, as the unstable hemoglobin precipitates within red blood cells, damaging the cell membrane and leading to premature erythrocyte destruction. The elevated LDH levels, reticulocytosis, and unconjugated bilirubin observed in this patient are indicative of ongoing hemolysis. Additionally, a negative direct antiglobulin test (DAT) excluded immune‐mediated causes. These findings further corroborate the pathogenic mechanism of Hb Tübingen.

The diagnosis of Hb Tübingen presents unique challenges, particularly due to its misleading electrophoretic pattern. Conventional hemoglobin capillary electrophoresis revealed a brownish band migrating similarly to Hb F, initially suggesting an elevation in Hb F levels. However, this was proven to be a false elevation, as HPLC analysis demonstrated normal γ‐globin chain levels, indicating that the observed band was indeed Hb Tübingen comigrating with Hb F. This finding highlights a critical diagnostic pitfall, as the misinterpretation of Hb Tübingen as Hb F could lead to incorrect diagnoses and subsequent management strategies. The distinction between Hb Tübingen and Hb F is crucial, as their clinical implications differ significantly. Hb F is a normal fetal hemoglobin, while Hb Tübingen is a pathogenic variant associated with methemoglobinemia and hemolysis. The use of HPLC and genetic sequencing becomes indispensable in such scenarios to accurately identify the hemoglobin variant and avoid diagnostic errors. The family screening in this study further emphasized the importance of molecular confirmation, as it revealed an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of the HBB: c.320 T>A mutation through the maternal lineage. This genetic information is vital for providing accurate genetic counseling and anticipatory guidance to the family.

In this study, the proportion of Hb Tübingen + Hb F was 52.3% in the proband, 47.6% in the younger brother, and notably lower at 10.3% in the mother. These values in the children closely align with early descriptions by Kleihauer et al. (~40%) and Köhne et al. (~41.2%) [ 9 , 10 ], reinforcing the reliability of our quantitation. The markedly reduced percentage of Hb Tübingen in the mother may be explained by the absence of the βCD17 (β0‐thalassemia) mutation; unlike the proband and brother, she has fully functional wild‐type β‐globin alleles. This allows for relatively higher production of normal β‐chains, diluting the variant Hb and reducing its proportion. In contrast, the proband and sibling—being compound heterozygotes with βCD17—have reduced normal β‐chain output, leading to relatively increased representation of Hb Tübingen. This genotypic difference provides a plausible biological basis for the observed interindividual variation.

In addition to genetic factors, inter‐individual variation in globin gene expression and methodological differences may also contribute to the observed discrepancy. The original cases described by Kleihauer and Köhne were studied in the 1970s using classical starch‐gel or cellulose acetate electrophoresis, which had lower resolution and limited quantitative accuracy compared with modern capillary electrophoresis. Differences in separation principle, detection wavelength, and calibration standards could result in small but systematic variations in the measured proportions of variant hemoglobins. Together, these biological and methodological factors may explain why the proportion of Hb Tübingen in the mother appears lower than in previously reported cases.

Co‐inheritance of a structural hemoglobin variant with β‐thalassemia may significantly modulate the clinical phenotype, as seen in other contexts such as Hb E/β‐thalassemia, where disease severity can be milder or more severe depending on the specific mutations and modifier genes present [ 13 ]. In our case, the children did not exhibit more severe anemia than expected for a heterozygous β‐thalassemia, which suggests that Hb Tübingen may not exacerbate anemia in this setting. However, detailed longitudinal monitoring and functional studies would be necessary to clarify whether the variant imposes any hematologic burden beyond that of the CD17 mutation alone. Our findings underscore the importance of considering both qualitative and quantitative globin gene defects when interpreting hemoglobinopathy phenotypes.

The co‐occurrence of Hb Tübingen and Moyamoya disease in our patient is both novel and intriguing. Moyamoya disease is a progressive cerebrovascular disorder characterized by bilateral stenosis or occlusion of the internal carotid arteries and the formation of abnormal collateral networks [ 14 , 15 ]. Its etiology remains incompletely understood, though chronic cerebral hypoxia has been implicated as a potential contributing factor to vascular remodeling [ 16 , 17 ]. In our study, the persistent hypoxemia secondary to elevated methemoglobin may have contributed to the development or progression of Moyamoya angiopathy. Alternatively, both conditions may share overlapping genetic or epigenetic susceptibilities that have yet to be elucidated. While a definitive causal relationship cannot be established from a single study, our observation raises important questions regarding the interplay between chronic hematologic hypoxia and cerebrovascular adaptation, warranting further investigation into whether unstable hemoglobin might represent a previously unrecognized risk factor for Moyamoya or other hypoxia‐driven vasculopathies. Notably, no known genetic mutations associated with classic Moyamoya disease (e.g., RNF213) were identified in our proband [ 18 , 19 ], increasing the plausibility of a secondary pathophysiological link between Hb Tübingen and cerebral vasculopathy.

The clinical significance of this study extends beyond the diagnosis of a rare hemoglobinopathy. It underscores the necessity for comprehensive evaluation in cases of unexplained cyanosis, particularly in populations where hemoglobinopathies may be underdiagnosed. The nonspecific presentation of cyanosis, often accompanied by neurological symptoms, can mimic a wide array of conditions, ranging from cardiac and respiratory diseases to neurovascular disorders [ 20 , 21 , 22 ]. In regions with limited resources or expertise in hemoglobinopathies, the diagnosis of Hb Tübingen and other rare hemoglobin variants may be easily overlooked. This study serves as a reminder that a thorough hematological workup, including hemoglobin electrophoresis, HPLC, and genetic testing, should be integral to the diagnostic approach for unexplained cyanosis. Furthermore, the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of Hb Tübingen necessitates genetic counseling for affected families. Family members of individuals with Hb Tübingen should be offered screening to identify carriers and provide them with information regarding the potential risks and reproductive implications. Early identification of affected individuals allows for timely intervention, including monitoring for complications such as hemolytic anemia and methemoglobinemia, as well as screening for associated conditions like Moyamoya disease. The implementation of a multidisciplinary approach, combining hematology, neurology, and genetics, is essential for optimal patient care in such complex cases. This study presents the first Chinese case of Hb Tübingen, expanding the known ethnic and geographic distribution of this rare β‐globin variant. The clinical constellation of cyanosis, hemolysis, and Moyamoya disease in the proband illustrates the broad systemic implications of unstable hemoglobin and highlights the diagnostic pitfalls posed by their electrophoretic mimicry of Hb F. Our findings emphasize the utility of combining HPLC and molecular diagnostics in evaluating atypical hemoglobin profiles and underscore the need for heightened clinical suspicion in cases of unexplained cyanosis. Moreover, the possible link between Hb Tübingen‐induced hypoxia and cerebrovascular remodeling invites further research into the systemic consequences of chronic methemoglobinemia. Ultimately, this case reinforces the importance of comprehensive hematologic and neurologic assessment in patients with congenital cyanosis and calls for greater awareness of rare hemoglobin variants in the differential diagnosis.
