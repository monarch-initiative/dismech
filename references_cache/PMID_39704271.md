# The First Case of Autosomal Recessive Cerebellar Ataxia with Prominent Paroxysmal Non-kinesigenic Dyskinesia Caused by a Truncating FGF14 Variant in a Turkish Patient.
**Authors:** Türkdoğan D, Smolina N, Tekgül Ş, Gül T, Yeşilyurt A, Houlden H, Zuchner S, Brais B, Pellerin D, Başak AN
**Journal:** Mov Disord (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mds.30087](https://doi.org/10.1002/mds.30087)

## Content

BACKGROUND: ATX-FGF/SCA27A has been exclusively associated with heterozygous variants in the FGF14 gene, presenting with postural tremor, slowly progressive cerebellar ataxia, and psychiatric and behavioral disturbances.

OBJECTIVES: This study describes the first case of ATX-FGF/SCA27A linked to a biallelic frameshift variant in the FGF14 gene.

METHODS: Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was conducted using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, and the identified variant was confirmed using Sanger sequencing.

RESULTS: We report the first case of autosomal recessive FGF14-related cerebellar ataxia caused by a c.75del variant resulting in p.Leu26Serfs*51 truncation of the FGF14 protein. This variant was found in a patient born to consanguineous parents and presented with a complex congenital nonprogressive cerebellar disorder accompanied by neurodevelopmental delay, intellectual disability, and prominent drug-responsive paroxysmal non-kinesigenic dyskinesia. Segregation analysis confirmed that the homozygous variant was inherited from heterozygous parents who developed mild gait ataxia and tremor in their 40s.

CONCLUSIONS: Biallelic loss-of-function variants in FGF14 are a rare cause of inherited cerebellar ataxia and expand the current genetic spectrum of ATX-FGF14. © 2024 International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society.

Spinocerebellar ataxias (SCAs) constitute a heterogeneous group of autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorders characterized by progressive cerebellar dysfunction. A rare subtype of SCA, ATX-FGF14/SCA27A (OMIM #193003), formerly known as SCA27, is associated with pathogenic heterozygous point, frameshift, or structural variants in the fibroblast growth factor 14 gene (FGF14, OMIM #601515) located on chromosome 13q331,2.

SCA27A typically presents with postural tremor at a median age of 10 years, followed by slowly progressive cerebellar ataxia (median age at onset: 24 years), psychiatric and behavioral disturbances, intellectual disability/cognitive impairment, and, in some cases, mild axonal polyneuropathy3. SCA27A was initially associated with a monoallelic p.Phe145Ser (chr13:102379135 A>G; ENST00000376143.4:c.434T>C) substitution in the FGF14 protein sequence1,4. However, nearly all genetic variants described afterwards were monoallelic frameshift or nonsense variants, causing protein loss of function (LoF)5–9.

Chromosome translocations involving FGF14 were also observed in patients with ATX-FGF14, causing variable clinical presentations3,10,11. Translocation between chromosomes 5 and 13 resulted in cerebellar ataxia, microcephaly, and severe intellectual disability, whereas translocation between chromosomes 13 and 21 caused paroxysmal non-kinesigenic dyskinesia (PNKD)10,11. Partial duplication or deletion of the FGF14 gene was linked to isolated early-onset nystagmus3.

Here, we report the first case of autosomal recessive ATX-FGF14 caused by a biallelic homozygous FGF14 frameshift variant in a young Turkish patient, presenting with a non-progressive, congenital cerebellar ataxia, neurodevelopmental delay, moderate intellectual disability, and drug-responsive PNKD (Table).

The male patient was born following an uneventful pregnancy as the second son of first-degree consanguineous parents. The postnatal period was unremarkable. At four months of age, he developed paroxysmal bilateral tonic upgaze, and at the age of two years, he began experiencing episodes suggestive of generalized tonic seizures, which were effectively managed with carbamazepine.

At the age of eight, the patient was referred for neurological evaluation due to frequent paroxysmal generalized choreiform and dystonic movements, lasting for 20 to 60 minutes, with occasional episodes of opisthotonus. These episodes were triggered by strong emotions or febrile illnesses. The first neurological examination revealed axial and appendicular hypotonia, dysmetric saccades, gaze-evoked nystagmus in all directions, and gross and fine motor disability. The patient had no verbal communication. Interictal EEG recordings yielded normal results. The paroxysmal attacks of dystonia/dyskinesia markedly diminished in frequency and severity following treatment with low-dose clonazepam. The patient achieved expression of initial verbal communication at the age 10 and independent ambulation along with fine hand functions by the age of 15.

At the time of the last medical visit, at the age of 24, the patient exhibited an independent wide-based ataxic gait. Fine motor functions of the hands were accompanied by an intention tremor. Additional clinical features included gaze-evoked nystagmus, cerebellar dysarthria with limited vocabulary and articulation disorder, mild appendicular dysmetria, increased deep tendon reflexes and upgoing plantar responses. Several facial dysmorphic features were noted, including a long triangular face, elongated chin, broad eyebrows with synophrys, and elongated, slender hands and feet (Suppl. Fig. 1). He also continued to experience infrequent episodes of PNKD, lasting 5–10 minutes (Video). Neuropsychiatric evaluation revealed moderate intellectual disability and significant impairment of executive functions, accompanied by relatively preserved memory functions.

Ophthalmological and hearing examinations revealed normal findings. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain, sleep-deprived EEG, nerve conduction studies and electromyographic evaluation, and visual and somatosensory evoked potentials were unremarkable. Brainstem evoked potentials showed normal amplitudes and latencies of waves I and III, and normal amplitudes but prolonged latencies of wave V (latency on both sides: 7.2 ms, with the normal upper limit of 6.4 ms).

The parents are currently in their late forties to early fifties and have mild intention tremor and mild gait ataxia (more prominent in the father), which do not affect their daily activities. The mother also has a subtle postural tremor that developed long time ago and did not further progress. The parent’s neurological examinations revealed SARA scores of 4/40 for the father and 2/40 for the mother. Both the older and the younger brothers are asymptomatic (SARA scores 0/40).

Exome sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Macrogen, Korea). Bioinformatic evaluation of the raw data was conducted using the SEQ platform (Genomize, Turkey), aligning the reads to the hg19 reference assembly. Noncoding and synonymous variants other than those of splice regions were filtered out. The analysis of all rare variants (MAF <0.01) in genes registered in the OMIM database revealed a homozygous deletion of one nucleotide in the FGF14 gene of the index patient (chr13:103053953_delA; c.75del). This variant is located in exon 1 of FGF14 (FGF14–201; ENST00000376131.4) and induces a frameshift followed by a premature stop codon at position 51 (p.Leu26Serfs*51). The variant was classified as pathogenic according to ACMG criteria (PVS1, PM2, PP1)12, it was not previously reported in GnomAD v2.1.1 database and was absent in the 3,970 samples of the GENESIS database13 and in our cohort (1,793 samples). Using the Genesis database13, we identified that the variant lies within an RoH of 29,402,519 bp length. Sanger sequencing confirmed the homozygous presence of the variant in the patient. It also showed that both parents and the younger sibling are heterozygous carriers; the older brother does not carry it (Fig. 1A, B).

The primary role of FGF14 is to regulate the clustering, axonal targeting, and phosphorylation of neuronal Nav channels in an isoform-specific manner14. The FGF14 gene has two major isoforms, FGF14–1a and FGF14–1b, which differ in their first exons and transcription start sites14,15. Structurally, both isoforms share a conserved core domain with beta-trefoil folds essential for FGF14 signaling modulation14,15. The FGF14 N-terminus is crucial for targeting and functional regulation of Nav channels and FGF14’s neuronal sub-cellular localization16. Both FGF14–1a and FGF14–1b isoforms localize to the axonal initial segment (AIS)16. FGF14 is highly expressed in the brain, particularly in the cerebellum, hippocampus, and nondopaminergic cells of the striatum15.

Knock-out mice lacking Fgf14 display prominent ataxia, paroxysmal dyskinesia, and cognitive defects17. Functional analyses have revealed that FGF14 uniquely modulates Nav channel activity through direct interaction with Nav channel α-subunits at the AIS18,19. These interactions are particularly crucial in cerebellar Purkinje neurons, where FGF14 is required for spontaneous and evoked action potential firing20, and reduces the resurgent Nav current21. In heterologous cells, FGF14–1b reduces the current densities of Nav1.1, Nav1.2, Nav1.5, and Nav1.6, while FGF14–1a has no effect on these currents16,18. In contrast, in cultured hippocampal neurons, overexpression of FGF14–1a or FGF14–1b results in markedly increased Nav current densities18. Recent findings demonstrated that in acute brain slices, Fgf14−/− murine hippocampal pyramidal neurons exhibited higher evoked repetitive firing rates compared to WT controls. Interestingly, localization of Nav channels at the AIS remained unchanged22 in contrast to the earlier studies on dissociated hippocampal neurons, where FGF14 was found to regulate the polarized localization of Nav1.222. Moreover, the results from this study differed from the previously reported reduced spontaneous repetitive firing rates in Fgf14−/− cerebellar Purkinje neurons compared with WT20, thus revealing distinct functional effects of the deletion of Fgf14 in different cell types22.

To date, early-onset SCA27A has been exclusively linked to monoallelic point, frameshift, or structural variants in FGF14 (Supplemental table). Phenotypic diversity characterizes ATX-FGF14, with clinical presentations varying, based on the underlying causal genetic variant. Pathogenesis of SCA27A arises from either FGF14 haploinsufficiency caused by protein truncating variants10 or a dominant negative effect of the FGF14-Phe145Ser variant19.

Here we describe the first case of autosomal recessive ATX-FGF14 caused by a biallelic FGF14 frameshift variant in a Turkish patient with congenital cerebellar ataxia. The identified variant (p.Leu26Serfs*51) lies within the first exon of the FGF14–1b isoform. This contrasts with most of the previously reported variants, which affect the open reading frame (ORF) of both FGF14–1a and FGF14–1b isoforms (Fig. 1C). Most of the previously reported LoF variants are associated with a later age of onset, ranging from 2 to 47 years, compared to the present case (Supplemental table). Similar to our patient, patients with childhood-onset SCA27A often first present with postural tremor and nystagmus and subsequently develop gait ataxia and varying degrees of cognitive impairment (Supplement table). Only one of the previously described SCA27A-causing variants was located outside the shared ORF and was unique to FGF14–1b (c.208 + 1G>A)7. This case presented with mild symptoms of acetazolamide-responsive episodic ataxia (EA) triggered by physical activities in their twenties, dizziness, gaze-evoked nystagmus, delayed motor development, postural tremor, and dysarthria. In comparison, our patient’s parents, both carrying a heterozygous truncating variant in the first exon of FGF14–1b, developed intention tremor, as well as mild gait ataxia in their forties (Table). Their youngest son, also a carrier of the variant, is asymptomatic at the age of 21. These observations suggest that heterozygous LoF variants affecting the FGF14–1b isoform result in a less severe phenotype compared to LoF variants involving both isoforms, suggesting functional redundancy between FGF14–1a and FGF14–1b. Given FGF14’s role in neurogenesis23, biallelic LoF variants affecting both isoforms would likely be incompatible with life. On the other hand, the biallelic LoF variant affecting exclusively FGF14–1b isoform caused early-onset and a more severe form of the disease compared to monoallelic variants in the family described (Fig. 1C).

To date, there is only one documented case of a biallelic variant resulting in a premature stop codon (p.Gln16*) in the first exon of FGF14, observed in lambs with EA24. Notably, an inbred homozygous lamb exhibited more severe clinical manifestations, while some lambs carrying the monoallelic variant remained asymptomatic, likely due to variable penetrance of this variant24. Moreover, studies of mouse cerebellar Purkinje neurons confirmed reduced penetrance due to FGF14 haploinsufficiency compared to the FGF14-Phe145Ser variant19,21. Together, these observations suggest that penetrance may vary depending on the mutation mechanism. Haploinsufficiency may not lead to phenotypic expression in all carriers, while dominant negative effect may result in complete penetrance.

In the case of our patient, the mutation mechanism likely involves nonsense-mediated decay of FGF14–1b transcripts from both alleles, resulting in the absence of transcript and protein, leading to an early onset disease. It is likely that total loss of FGF14–1b isoform function in our patient may be partly compensated by the unaltered function of the FGF14–1a isoform due to potential functional redundancy between both isoforms. This report also adds FGF14 to the short list of genes known to cause both autosomal dominant and autosomal recessive cerebellar ataxia.
