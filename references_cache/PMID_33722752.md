ABSTRACT  
It is now widely accepted that antiphospholipid ant ibodies (aPL) have direct pathogenic effects and 
that B cells, notably through aPL production, play a key role in the development of antiphospholipid 
syndrome (APS). Recent findings strengthened the im plication of B cells with the description of 
specific B cell phenotype abnormalities and inborn errors of immunity involving B cell signaling in 
APS patients. In addition, it has been shown in pre clinical models that cross-reactivity between APS 
autoantigens and mimotopes expressed by human gut c ommensals can lead to B cell tolerance 
breakdown and are sufficient for APS development.  However, B cell targeting therapies are 
surprisingly not as effective as expected in APS co mpared to other autoimmune diseases. Elucidation 
of the B cell tolerance breakdown mechanisms in APS  patients may help to develop and guide the use 
of novel therapeutic agents that target B cells or specific immune pathway. 
 
HIGHLIGHTS 
• Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is characterized by  a hypercoagulable state related to 
pathogenic antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL).  
• B cells, notably through aPL production, have a central role in the pathogenesis of APS. 
• aPL development/persistence may result from mono/po lygenic inborn errors of immunity and 
specific interactions with commensal hosts. 
• Paradoxically, B cell targeting therapies are not a s effective as expected in APS considering 
experiences in other autoimmune diseases.

1. Introduction  
Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune di sorder characterized by the presence of 1 
pathogenic autoantibodies directed against membrane  phospholipids and/or their associated plasma 2 
proteins. Antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) were fi rst described by Hughes et al.  in 1983 [1]. Annual 3 
incidence and prevalence are estimated at approximately 2 and 50 per 100 000 individuals [2]. Clinical 4 
features are related to a hypercoagulable state lea ding to obstetrical complications and/or venous or 5 
arterial thromboembolic events (TEs) affecting almo st every organ system [3]. The criteria derived 6 
from the Sapporo classification revised in 2006 (Si dney) are quite consensual and require the 7 
association of one of these clinical manifestations  and the persistence of  “classical” aPL, namely 8 
lupus anticoagulant (LA), anticardiolipin antibodie s (anti-CL) and anti- β2 glycoprotein I antibodies 9 
(anti-β2GPI) [4]. Patients may encompass non TEs manifesta tions or “non-classical” aPL (including 10 
phosphatidylserine/prothrombin complex, PS/PT, PE, or anti-annexin5A, Anx5A, antibodies) that are 11 
excluded from Sydney criteria due to a role that remain elusive [5].  12 
Recently coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) and th e associated abnormalities in coagulation 13 
measures have put the spotlight back on aPL occurre nce and their clinical relevance. There are strong 14 
evidences that B cells, notably through aPL product ion, have a central role in APS pathogenesis. 15 
Paradoxically, B cell targeting therapies are someh ow not as effective as expected considering 16 
experiences in other autoimmune diseases. Herein we  propose a review that highlights the major role 17 
of B cells and autoantibodies in APS, and how they provide a window into the pathogenesis, the 18 
clinical features and the risk assessment in APS. 19

2. B cells: the leading role? 21 
Pathogenesis of aPL  22 
B cells contribute to APS pathogenesis notably thro ugh antibody production. The pathogenic 23 
mechanisms mediated by aPL have been largely suppli ed by animal models, especially for  β2GPI-24 
dependent aPL. In 1990 Mc Neil et al.  found that the binding of aPL to cardiolipin requi res the 25 
presence of β2GPI as a cofactor [6]. Passive transfer of purifie d human anti– β2GPI IgG, but not anti-26

β2GPI depleted IgG or normal human IgG, potentiates thrombosis formation in animal models  after 27 
vessel injury or pre-treatment with lipopolysacchar ide (LPS) [7,8]. Anti– β2GPI also induce fetal 28 
losses or growth retardation in pregnant mice witho ut the need of additional factors [9], and the 29 
prominent role of the β2GPI/anti-β2GPI complex is highlighted by the absence of throm bosis in 30 
β2GPI deficiencies [10]. The binding of anti- β2GPI antibodies to their antigen is dependent on β2GPI 31 
conformation. β2GPI comprised 5 domains, the fifth domain shieldin g the domain 1 epitope in the 32 
circular conformation form in plasma [11]. Autoanti bodies do not bind this conformation form but 33 
β2GPI binding to negatively charged structures (such as anionic phospholipids) induce conformational 34 
changes [12,13]. As a result, cryptic epitopes in d omain I are exposed and the autoantibodies are able  35 
to recognize β2GPI [14]. The type of interaction is also critical  since human IgG anti-β2GPI domain I 36 
(anti-β2GPI-DmI) with high avidity induced significantly l arger thrombi in vivo  compared to anti-37 
β2GPI-DmI with poor avidity [15].  38 
A part of APS pathophysiology is mediated by the di sruption of AnxA5 shielding over phospholipid 39 
bilayers, making them available for coagulation rea ctions [16]. Interestingly, anti- β2GPI-DmI has 40 
been associated with significantly reduced annexin A5 anticoagulant activity in adults with aPL [17]. 41 
Nevertheless, considering β2GPI-dependent aPL as the only ones with pathologic al relevance would 42 
be too straightforward, since aPL targeting other p hospholipid-binding proteins (such as prothrombin) 43 
[18,19] or cofactor-independent aPL [20] may also e xert thrombogenic effects in mice, but it has 44 
become increasingly evident that the phospholipid b inding protein β2GPI is the major antigenic target 45 
for thrombogenic aPL antibodies. 46

also on the downstream effect of the autoantibodies . aPL or the phospholipid/antibody complex can 48

Despite the central role of aPL, little is known ab out B cell disturbance and characteristics of the a PL 52

APS. A work assessing B cell phenotypic abnormaliti es in obstetric APS women described reduced 54 
memory B cells and increased percentage and absolut e count of CD27 -IgM+ naive B cells in patients 55 
with history of TEs. This higher number of naive B cells was associated with lower serum levels of 56

complications during follow-up [22]. The authors assumed that altered peripheral B cell differentiation 58 
and activation could bypass normal B cell different iation leading to a bias toward plasma cell 59 
maturation. These results were confirmed on another  cohort of 11 patients with thrombotic primary 60

(RA) patients [23]. An increased proportion of tran sitional B cells, a putative source of autoreactive  B 63 
cells, was also noticed [23]. However, a recent wor k heightens the debate, showing a decrease of 64 
naive/immature B cell compartment only in obstetric  PAPS patients [24]. Increase of CD19 +CD5 + B 65

in this population [25]. CD5, firstly described in T cells, is not a distinctive marker but is highly 67 
expressed by B1 cells in humans, a subset producing natural polyreactive IgM with a repertoire biased 68 
towards bacterial and self-antigens [26]. An increa se of CD5 + B cells have been described in several 69

plasmablasts in 26 PAPS and 19 SLE/APS patients. Th is increase was associated with TLR7 SNP 73 
rs38553839 and type I IFN upregulation [28]. Anothe r soluble mediator that could dysregulate B cell 74 
survival and development is B-cell activating factor (BAFF), known as a key cytokine in SLE [29]. In 75 
APS, soluble BAFF level is increased both in patien ts with secondary APS and PAPS and correlates 76 
with prognostic scores of thrombosis [24,30]. 77

3. APS: a “hit(s)-parade” toward B cell maturation  99 
Although aPL are directly implicated in pathophysio logy and correlate with the thrombotic risk, TEs 100 
occur occasionally, suggesting that other factors a re needed to push the thrombotic/haemostatic 101 
balance in favor of thrombosis [37]. The “second hi t” hypothesis is widely accepted and regarded as 102 
an additional procoagulant condition that is needed  to precipitate clotting formation in aPL carriers 103 
[9]. This theory is strongly supported by several e xperiments in APS prone mice models. In this view, 104 
Pierangeli et al. described mouse model of injury-induced thrombosis with surgically induced 105 
thrombus formation and showed in this model the imp ortance of complement activation in aPL’s 106 
pathogenicity [38]. Similarly, the same group showe d that aPL mediated thrombogenic effects were 107 

7 
 
reduced in mice non-responsive to LPS [39]. Hence, a priming factor (e.g. vessel injury or pre-108 
treatment with LPS) is needed for passively transferred human aPL to induce thrombosis [7,8]. 109 
Among various mechanisms, infection agents or oxida tive stress may increase aPL exposure [40], 110 
β2GPI conformation modifications that expose cryptic  epitopes [41], or complement activation. 111 
Whereas β2GPI binding to endothelial cells requires priming with pro-inflammatory factors, uterine 112 
vessels and trophoblast physiologically express hig h level of β2GPI [42]. Thereby, pregnancy 113 
increases specific epitope exposure and could act a s a second hit per se  [9]. Other clinical situations, 114 
such as traumatism, inflammatory state or surgery can trigger clot formation, leading to clinical events 115 
defining APS. However, and as discussed further, su ch additional hits may, in an alternative or 116 
complementary manner, step in earlier in the pathog enic process driving B cells from benign and 117 
transient aPL production toward persistent and pathological autoantibodies generation. 118 
aPL occurence 119 
It is widely accepted that autoimmune diseases, inc luding APS, may arise via an inappropriate 120 
immune response towards infections. Apart from chro nic infectious, such as the one induced by 121 
human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) or hepatitis C v irus, in which link between aPL and APS-122 
related features is controversial [43], most “infec tious” aPL are transient, cofactor independent, bin d 123 
neutral or negative PL with low affinity, and clini cal APS-related complications remain an 124 
epiphenomenon [32,44]. While not frequently reporte d, cytomegalovirus (CMV), Epstein Barr Virus 125 
(EBV), Herpes simplex virus (HSV) and adenovirus in fections could lead to anti-CL production and 126 
transient LA positivity [45]. Development of pathogenic aPL has been reported during Parvovirus B19 127 
infection, with aPL of IgG isotype and cofactor-dep endent binding, similar to autoimmune disease 128 
[45–47]. Recently, high prevalence of LA have been described in patients with SARS-CoV2 [48]. 129 
Among 216 patients, 44 (20%) were found to have a p rolonged activated partial-thromboplastin time 130 
(aPTT), due to LA in 90% of them. Additionally, in a recent series, 15% of 122 infected patients were 131 
positive for anti- β2GPI IgG and about 5% for anti-CL IgG [49]. Similar ly to patients with triple 132 
positive APS or CAPS, IgG isolated from COVID-19 patients increase thrombus extension in a mouse 133 
model of inferior vena cava thrombosis [50]. TEs ar e frequent during acute SARS-CoV2 infection 134

[49]. The role of aPL in this hypercoagulable state  should be elucidated, as well as the question of 138

Regarding bacterial infections, aPL are most common ly associated with Coxiella burnetii, 140 
Mycoplasma pneumonia, streptococci, and  Mycobacterium tuberculosis infections. The latter three 141 
infections may be associated with persistent autoan tibodies and authentic APS [54]. The relatively 142 
high prevalence of anti-CL antibodies found in syph ilis is largely due to a cross-reactivity of syphil is 143 
antibodies against treponemal cardiolipins [43]. Multiple aPL positivity and β2GPI-dependent binding 144 
are found in sera from patients with leprosy [55]. Mice immunized with proteins from Haemophilus 145 
influenzae or Neisseria gonorrhoeae , which share sequence homology with β2GPI-DmIII, led to the 146 
production of anti-CL and anti-β2GPI IgG autoantibodies [56]. Parasite or yeast ind uced-aPL are very 147 
rare and mostly occur after Plasmodium falciparum  or visceral leishmaniasis infection [57].  148 
Finally, several other clinical conditions have bee n associated with the occurrence of aPL. Drug 149 
induced aPL, following antiarrhythmic or antipsycho tic agents, are common, but they are usually 150 
benign and disappear after the drug withdrawal [44] . Reports documented the appearance of aPL after 151 
vaccine administration responses. Indeed, the frequ ency of aPL production after influenza 152 
immunization is higher than after infection [44]. In a prospective study, 8% (7/101) of healthy subjects 153 
developed anti-CL but not anti-β2GPI after seasonal influenzae vaccination. Anti-CL positivity was no 154 
longer observed at week 12 post-vaccination [58]. T etanus toxoid vaccination triggers antibodies 155 
which are cross-reactive with β2GPI as shown in animal models [57]. The role of ad juvants as 156 
stimulators of innate actors in vaccine associated aPL is not elucidated. 157 
From aPL to thrombosis 158 
Several potential mechanisms have been evoked to ex plain the production of aPL during infections, 159 
including epitope spreading, bystander activation, and molecular mimicry. However, little is known 160 
about mechanisms sustaining chronic autoimmunity in  APS. The transient character of aPL after 161

infection might suggest that continuous antigen exp osure is needed for the maintenance of chronic 162 
autoreactive B lymphocyte activation. The microbiome is a potential source of persistent self-antigens  163

with antibiotic regimen leads to lower anti-β2GPI titers and protection from thrombotic events in mice 165

but several checkpoints ensure their counterselecti on or their immunologic ignorance to thwart 178

seropositivity or PAPS, and offer a prototypical co ndition to explore the tolerance breakdown against 189

aPL. We reported recently a family of patients with  APS and an heterozygous variant of IKZF1  190

4. Autoantibodies and clinical implication 207 
aPL and diagnosis 208 
The main role of autoreactive B cells is emphasized  by the central place of aPL in the APS diagnostic 209 
[72]. However, the various association between larg e panels of serological and clinical phenotypes 210 
remains elusive. According to Sydney classification, diagnosis laboratory criteria include LA, anti-CL 211 
and anti- β2GPI, of IgM or IgG isotype at medium-to-high titre s (generally tested by ELISA), that 212 
should be tested repeatedly positive at least 12 we eks apart. Usually, anti-CL and anti-β2GPI from the 213 
same isotype are detected together [73]. The positi vity for LA reflects the ability of aPL to prolong 214 
phospholipid-dependent coagulation assays. The LA a ctivity is often displayed by anti– β2GPI, 215 

11 
 
especially those directed against domain I of β2GPI. Seropositivity of all three-classification cr iteria-216 
aPL is linked with a more aggressive APS phenotype, named "triple-positive". 217 
Alternative aPL, named “non-classical”, have been e xcluded from Sidney criteria due to an initial lack 218 
of clinical implication. However, patients with cli nical manifestations compatible with APS, positive 219 
for such extra criteria aPL, and negative for class ical aPL exist and were named “seronegative APS 220 
patients” (SN-APS). This entity introduced in 2003 by Hughes and Khamashta [74] remains 221 
controversial and three possible explanations have originally been considered to explain the existence  222 
of such SN-APS patients: (i) a wrong diagnosis, (ii ) previously positive aPL tests that became 223 
negative, or more likely (iii) aPL not detected by the current consensual tests. A retrospective study  224 
found no significant differences in clinical manife stations between patients with APS and those with 225 
SN-APS [75].  Zohoury et al. showed that about 35% of SN-APS patients have detec table non 226 
classical aPL [76]. Hence, some consider that SN-AP S is a proof that classic aPL tests are not 227 
sufficient to diagnose APS, arguing for an update t o the current criteria incorporating non-228 
conventional aPL and consensual technical procedure s in diagnosis criteria [77]. Recently, four 229 
profiles of non-criteria APS were proposed : 1) cli nical non-criteria APS, 2) laboratory non-criteria 230 
APS, 3) incomplete laboratory APS: patients fulfill ing clinical criteria, plus positive aPL, but not 231 
fulfilling the classification criteria, and 4) sero negative APS: patients with persistently negative 232 
antiphospholipid antibodies despite fulfilling clinical criteria [78].  233

be of strong clinical relevance. Their presence, to gether with anti-β2GPI-DmI, correlate with vascular 236 
and obstetrical complications, and their combinatio n have 100% predictive value for the diagnosis of 237 
APS [82–84]. In 14 triple positive patients also po sitive for anti-PS/PT (so called “tetra positive AP S 238 
patients”), from whom different type of aPL were af finity purified, anticoagulant activity was largely  239 
attributable to anti-PS/PT antibodies [85]. Anti-PS /PT detection is not influenced by anticoagulant 240 
treatment and could be considered to confirm the presence of LA. Concerning other non-classical aPL, 241 
among a cohort of patients with thrombosis, anti-PE antibodies were found in 15%, being the sole aPL 242 
detected in 67% of the cases [86]. Antibodies targeting phosphatidic acid (PA), or phosphatidylinositol 243

Specific aPL for distinct phenotypes?  248 
Vascular occlusions belong to the classical clinica l pictures of APS, and include deep venous 249 
thrombosis, pulmonary embolism or arterial TEs such  as strokes [91]. In addition to this thrombotic 250 
APS, some patients present with an “obstetric APS” phenotype and suffer from pregnancy morbidity, 251 
including recurrent early miscarriage but also late (after 10 weeks) pregnancy loss and premature birth 252 
(<34 weeks) related to preeclampsia or placental in sufficiency [92]. Patients with APS may also 253 
experience a myriad of additional features, usually  branded as “extra criteria”, including neurologica l 254 
(epilepsy, chorea…) and hematological manifestation s (thrombocytopenia and hemolytic anemia), 255 
livedo reticularis, nephropathy and valvular heart disease [93]. Catastrophic antiphospholipid 256 
syndrome (CAPS) is the most severe form of the disease and characterized by widespread small vessel 257 
thrombosis with multiorgan failure presenting over a short period of time and is associated with a 258 
mortality higher than 30% [91] ,[94]. Despite published association with drugs, mal ignancies and a 259 
wide spectrum of autoimmune diseases [95], secondar y APS is mainly associated with SLE, in about 260 
20-30% of cases [2,5]. aPL positive patients with S LE have higher rates of arthritis, cytopenia, 261 
hypocomplementemia and severe renal disease than those without SLE [96]. 262 
Relying these homogenous clinical phenotypes to ser ological characteristics remains challenging. 263 
Patients with single obstetrical APS tend to have l ower rate of multiple aPL positivity [9,97]. In ser ies 264 
on the Serbian national cohort, patients with secondary APS had more frequent multiple aPL positivity 265 
than those with PAPS, whereas LA alone was more fre quently reported in the latter [98]. However, 266 
these results were not found in the large Euro-Phos pholipid cohort. Additionally, in this cohort of 267 
1000 European patients with APS, no differences wer e found in the clinical presentation of PAPS, at 268 
its onset and during a 10-year follow-up period, according to the presence or absence of anti-CL or LA 269 
[5,91]. In 2016 data from the CAPS Registry, LA was  present in 83% of episodes, anti-CL IgG in 270

81%, IgG anti-β2GPI in 78% whereas anti-CL and anti-β2GPI of IgM isotype were less frequent (50% 271 
and 58% respectively) [99]. Recently, three studies  on large cohort aimed to  aggregate patients into 272 
subgroups sharing common clinical and specific clas sical aPL profiles [97,100,101]. The main results 273 
are depicted in Table 2. Nonetheless, these studies do not consider extra criteria aPL. Using multiplex 274 
line immunoassay, Volkov et al.  evaluated the presence of non-criteria aPL in a co hort of 130 APS 275 
patients. Whereas arterial thrombosis was associated with multiple aPL positivity (>7), venous TE was 276 
not linked to any specific aPL and inversely correl ated with anti-phosphatidic acid IgM detection. The 277 
presence of anti-CL IgG and anti-PS IgG was linked with pregnancy morbidity [102]. These results 278 
suggest that evaluating a broad spectrum of aPL may enable to better define APS phenotype. 279 
Prognostic markers and risk assessment  280 
Identifying the risk of clinical events in patients  with APS is challenging due to the clinical 281 
heterogeneity, the different sets of aPL and their fluctuation in the disease course. Multiple positiv ity 282 
for anti-CL, anti- β2GPI or LA is associated with higher rate of TEs th an single test positivity 283 
[103,104].  Subjects with triple positivity for the se criteria aPL have an annual incidence of TEs of 284 
5,3%, with a cumulative risk of 37% after ten years  [105]. LA positivity is associated with the highes t 285 
risk for both arterial and venous TEs [106] whereas  results are controversial for anti-CL [106–108]. 286 
β2GPI antibodies seems to confer a moderate increase of the risk, with an odd ratio from 1.6 to 2.4 for 287 
venous TEs and of 2.3 for ischemic strokes [109–111 ]. β2GPI-dependent LA activity assay correlated 288 
two times better with TEs than the classic LA assay  [112]. The relevance of anti-CL/ β2GPI IgM 289 
antibodies has been debated. Most studies were unab le to demonstrate a correlation between IgM 290 
positivity and TEs, but Chayoua et al.  recently provided evidence for an independent asso ciation with 291 
pregnancy morbidity [113]. Among non-criteria aPL, anti-β2GPI-DmI positively correlates with the 292 
Global Antiphospholipid Score (GAPSS) [114,115], a validated thrombotic risk score [116]. Hence, 293 
anti-β2GPI-DmI seems to represent a robust biological cri terion, in association with classical aPL, to 294 
better apprehend the thrombotic risk. Other works s howed diverse clinical association with reactivity 295 
to different epitopes on β2GPI and suggested that classification of anti- β2GPI into subgroups 296 
regarding epitope specificity would be of clinical relevance [117–120].  As an example, in a cohort of 297 

14 
 
135 women with persistently positive anti- β2GPI IgG, anti-β2GPI-DmI positivity was associated with 298 
triple positivity, thrombosis and predictive of lat e pregnancy morbidity, whereas anti-domain IV/V 299 
(anti-β2GPI-DmIV/V) antibodies were not associated with cl inical APS manifestations [118]. 300 
Conversely, DmIV/V are preferentially recognized by  anti- β2GPI antibodies from one-year-old 301 
healthy children born to mothers with systemic autoimmune diseases and from subjects with non-APS 302 
conditions [119], and the ratio anti- β2GPI-DmI to anti- β2GPI-DmIV/V IgG has been proposed as a 303 
helpful tool to discriminate patients with systemic  autoimmunity from those carrying less pathogenic 304 
anti-β2GPI antibodies [120]. 305 
Regarding other non-classical aPL, it is widely acc epted that prothrombin is a common antigenic 306 
target for aPL, but assays may detect distinct popu lations of autoantibodies which have different 307 
clinical implications. Positivity for anti-PT, detected by directly coating prothrombin on ELISA plates, 308 
does not seem to be such a strong risk factor for APS [121]. However, the cumulative incidence rate of 309 
thrombotic events is significantly higher when anti -PS/PT, which recognize a conformational epitope, 310 
are detected among aPL carrier patients [122]. Addi tionally, the risk of pregnancy morbidity seems to 311 
be associated with the presence of anti-PE, PA, PS and PI [90]. 312 
Based on the global aPL profile, different models, such as the Antiphospholipid Score, have been 313 
tested to evaluate the risk of having APS and for p redicting thrombosis [123]. The use of antibody 314 
profiles as prognostic markers is however delicate for several reasons. High inter-laboratory variability 315 
and the widespread of retrospective designs may exp lain the large discrepancies between the finding 316 
of clinical studies. Moreover, conventional cardiov ascular risk factors should be considered to assess  317 
the thrombotic risk. The GAPSS proposed by Sciascia  et al. , based on the profile of four aPL and the 318 
presence of dyslipidemia or arterial hypertension, has been proven to represent the probability of 319 
having TEs or pregnancy loss in patients with SLE a nd is an example of risk model that takes in 320 
account such factors [116]. EUREKA is another algor ithm that predicts the risk of pregnancy 321 
morbidity considering aPL titres and profiles [124]. 322

5. Management of APS and B cells: paradox and remaining challenges 324 

15 
 
Despite the widespread of targeted therapies in aut oimmune diseases, anticoagulation remains the 325 
corner stone of management in patients with APS. Br iefly, low dose aspirin (LDA) is recommended 326 
for asymptomatic patients with high risk aPL profil es whereas patients with thrombotic APS should 327 
have long term treatment with vitamin K antagonists. The use of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) is 328 
highly debated [92,125]. Patients with obstetric AP S only should receive prophylactic dose low-329 
molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) and LDA throughout pregnancy. The European League Against 330 
Rheumatism (EULAR) recently developed evidence-base d recommendations for the prevention and 331 
treatment of adults with APS [126]. Considering the crucial role of autoantibodies in the definition and 332 
the pathophysiology of the disease, data on the use  of therapies directed towards B cells are lacking 333 
and somehow disappointing in APS. 334 
B cell targeting therapies  335 
The use of anti-CD20 therapies has been supported b y strong evidences in other autoimmune 336 
diseases[127], but also by clinical and/or serologi cal beneficial effects in case reports concerning 337 
PAPS, secondary APS, or CAPS [128]. In the retrospective literature review by Erre et al ., eight out of 338 
12 patients with classical aPL treated with rituximab, a chimeric monoclonal antibody targeting CD20, 339 
showed normalization or reduction of aPL titers. So me of them were however concurrently treated 340 
with immunosuppressors [129]. Results on TEs recurr ence are debated and limited to small series or 341 
case reports [130]. In a phase II trial, safety and  efficacy of rituximab was evaluated in 19 patients  342 
with APS criteria [131]. Rituximab was efficient fo r some non-criteria manifestations, especially 343 
thrombocytopenia (response rate of 50%) cognitive d ysfunction (80%) and skin ulcers (100%), but 344 
failed to change significantly aPL profiles [131]. Such effect on extra thrombotic manifestations, 345 
including thrombocytopenia, was confirmed in other studies [133,134]. There are concerns about 346 
thrombosis exacerbation since severe acute thrombot ic events were described in two APS/SLE 347 
patients receiving rituximab [132]. In the study fr om Erkan et al. , 12 serious adverse events were 348 
recorded including neurological complications (n = 5), infections (n = 3) and recurrence of TEs (n = 1) 349 
[131]. According to recommendations, rituximab may be considered in CAPS, where aPL production 350 
is associated with systemic inflammatory response s yndrome [94]. In a retrospective review, 13 of 20 351 

16 
 
patients with severe/relapsed CAPS treated with rit uximab recovered. aPL became negative in fifty 352 
percent of those with follow-up (4/8). Comparing 30  patients treated with rituximab versus 353 
contemporaneous patients in the CAPS registry, an ad hoc  analysis provided an odd’s ratio for 354 
survival of 0.41 (95% CI 0.15, 1.11) [133]. However , evidences are not strong enough to use 355 
rituximab as a first line therapy in CAPS and it is  considered as an alternative treatment in severe o r 356 
refractory cases [92]. Novel anti-CD20 therapies su ch as obinutuzumab, a human monoclonal anti-357 
CD20 antibody, induce different cellular response a nd stronger antibody-dependent cell-mediated 358 
cytotoxicity (ADCC) [134]. Their uses in autoimmune  diseases are under evaluation and they could 359 
provide a second life to anti-CD20 therapy in APS. 360 
Because anti-CD20 treatment does not affect plasma cells, the insufficient therapeutic outcome may be 361 
caused by the survival of aPL producing plasma cells. Ex vivo  experiments showed that aPL producing 362 
CD20 negative B cells circulate in peripheral blood  of APS patients [28]. This rational is sustained b y 363 
the successful use of bortezomib on TEs recurrence and aPL titers in case reports [135,136]. As an 364 
alternative, anti-CD38 monoclonal antibodies, such as daratumumab, enter the field of autoimmune 365 
diseases therapy with impressive response in post-t ransplant patient with autoimmune cytopenia 366 
[137,138] and at least in 2 patients with SLE, in association with belimumab [139]. 367 
Cytokines 368 
BAFF blockade has shown therapeutic potential in pr eclinical models. BAFF antagonists reduced 369 
renal and cardiac infarcts and prolonged survival i n NZW×BXSB F1 mice although it did not prevent 370 
anti-CL development [140]. Interestingly, in human,  BAFF level is increased in PAPS and secondary 371 
APS [24,30]. Belimumab, a monoclonal antibody again st BAFF, was found to reduce anti-CL and 372 
anti-β2GPI IgM and IgG titers treatment in 12 SLE/APS pat ients independently of 373 
hydroxychloroquine treatment [141], However, in a r ecent post-hoc analysis on two large randomized 374 
SLE-controlled trials focusing on anti-CL titers, i ntravenous belimumab showed a significant 375 
reduction of anti-CL IgA and IgG only in patients w ith concomitant antimalarial treatment [142]. 376 
Similarly, Bettiol et al ., in another recent post hoc analysis of 249 patie nts with SLE from the BLISS-377 
SC trial, observed a significant reduction of anti- β2GPI IgA and anti-CL IgM titres in the 378 

17 
 
belimumab+antimalarials group as compared with the placebo+antimalarials group, suggesting that 379 
the two treatments may exert beneficial synergistic  effect [143]. The independent role of belimumab 380 
on aPL titre reduction needs to be further evaluated, and, so far, such data are lacking in PAPS.  381 
Alternative therapeutic approaches might consider i nflammatory mediators inhibitors such as type I 382 
IFN antagonists. Anifrolumab, that blocks the type I interferon receptor subunit 1, has proven 383 
efficiency on disease activity in SLE patients [144 ]. The high type I IFN signature observed in about 384 
40% of patients with PAPS [145], together with the positive correlation between plasma cells 385 
expansion and type I IFN upregulation in APS [28], support the evaluation of such antagonists.  386 
aPL targeting 387 
Plasmapheresis and intravenous immunoglobulins (IVI g) could thwart aPL pathogenic effects by 388 
increasing their clearance, inhibiting their action  or through the control of anti-idiotype antibodies . In 389 
CAPS, they are recommended as first line treatment in combination with steroids and heparin [126]. 390 
Agostinis et al.  identified a single-chain fragment variable direct ed against β2GPI-DmI that induced, 391 
in mice, thrombosis and fetal losses in a complemen t dependent matter. Interestingly, a variant of thi s 392 
antibody lacking the CH2 domain, required for C1q binding and complement activation, compete with 393 
human anti-β2GPI IgG and prevent blood clot formation and fetal loss [146]. 394 
The use of peptide therapies recently provided anot her approach to inhibit aPL pathogenicity. The 395 
rational is to block binding to target cells or rec eptors using small synthetic peptides. No clinical trial 396 
has been developed to date but such molecules, incl uding synthetic analogs of β2GPI-DmI or A1 397 
(ApoER2 ligand-binding domain) reduced thrombus for mation in animal models [147,148]. Peptides 398 
therapy, as well as CH2-deleted antibody, could represent an innovative approach potentially useful to 399 
treat refractory APS patients. 400 
Beyond B cells 401 
Other conventional or targeted therapies could over come the apparent limited effectiveness of B 402 
therapies and work synergistically to counterbalanc e pro-coagulation state.  Some of them may exert 403 
indirect action on aPL or autoreactive B cells deve lopment.  In vitro, hydroxychloroquine reverses the 404 

18 
 
disruption of AnxA5 shielding induced by aPL, and t hereby restore the binding of AnxA5 to 405 
phospholipids [16]. Besides decreasing aPL titers in patients with secondary APS, hydroxychloroquine 406 
reduces LA activity [142,149]. Sirolimus may also c ontribute to lower aPL titers in SLE patients 407 
[130]. Statins exert multiple anti-inflammatory effects, including reduction on tissue factor expressi on 408 
by monocytes and cytokine secretion, but little is known about B cell implication. In accordance with 409 
the hypocomplementemia and increased levels of comp lement activation in APS patients [150], anti-410 
C5 antibody prevents the prothrombotic activity of aPL in vitro,  and  precludes aPL-mediated 411 
thrombosis in some murine models [38]. This therapy  showed promising results as adjunction therapy 412 
in CAPS, but results are limited to cases reports a nd do not enable conclusion on aPL titers [130,151]. 413 
Further studies should elucidate the place of these  therapies in the APS therapeutic plan and their 414 
specific effect on B cells and autoantibodies. 415

6. CONCLUSION 417 
The central role of B cells in APS is highlighted b y strong evidence of antibody mediated 418 
pathogenesis, specific B cell phenotype abnormaliti es, and by the development of the disease in 419 
patients with inborn errors of immunity involving B  cell ontogeny. Recent findings questioned the 420 
links between B cell tolerance breakdown, genetic defects, and commensal microorganisms. However, 421 
the origin and characteristics of pathogenic aPL pr oducing clones remain largely unknown. Despite 422 
the discrepancies in clinical trial results, therap ies targeting B cells should remain an important 423 
therapeutic approach in APS. A broad range of immun omodulatory drugs has recently emerged with 424 
serious rational to modulate the inflammatory respo nse and vascular damages in APS. Further studies 425 
should determine if this new therapeutic area could  provide a second wind to the field of B cell 426 
targeting in APS and which homogeneous groups of patients should benefit from it. 427
