BACKGROUND: Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is the most common inherited 
cardiovascular disease characterized by thickening of ventricular walls and 
decreased left ventricular chamber volume. The majority of HCM-associated 
mutations are found in genes encoding sarcomere proteins. Herein, we set out to 
functionally characterize a novel HCM-associated mutation (K206I-TNNI3) and 
elucidate the mechanism of dysfunction at the level of myofilament proteins.
METHODS AND RESULTS: The male index case was diagnosed with HCM after an 
out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, which was followed by comprehensive clinical 
evaluation, transthoracic echocardiography, and clinical genetic testing. To 
determine molecular mechanism(s) of the mutant human cardiac troponin I (K206I), 
we tested the Ca(2+) dependence of thin filament-activated myosin-S1-ATPase 
activity in a reconstituted, regulated, actomyosin system comparing wild-type 
human troponin complex, 50% mix of K206I/wildtype, or 100% K206I. We also 
exchanged native troponin detergent extracted fibers with reconstituted troponin 
containing either wildtype or a 65% mix of K206I/wildtype and measured force 
generation. The Ca(2+) sensitivity of the myofilaments containing the K206I 
variant was significantly increased, and when treated with 20 µmol/L 
(-)-epigallocatechin gallate (green tea) was restored back to wild-type levels 
in ATPase and force measurements. The K206I mutation impairs the ability of the 
troponin I to inhibit ATPase activity in the absence of calcium-bound human 
cardiac troponin C. The ability of calcium-bound human cardiac troponin C to 
neutralize the inhibition of K206I was greater than with wild-type TnI.
CONCLUSIONS: Compromised interactions of K206I with actin and hcTnC may lead to 
impaired relaxation and HCM.

The linkage of mutations in sarcomeric proteins to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) not only provides insights into the control of cardiac remodeling by the state of the sarcomeres, but also provides insights into the molecular mechanisms controlling the contraction and relaxation of the heart.1–3 Experiments reported here highlight these insights in the case of a heterozygous mutation in TNNI3-encoded cardiac troponin I identified as p.Lys206Ile (also p. K206I, c. 617 A>T) in a 13-year-old male, who was diagnosed with HCM after an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) in 2009. These observations stimulated us to investigate the fundamental mechanisms for these phenotypic findings in reconstituted systems with myofilaments regulated by wildtype (WT) human cardiac troponin I (hcTnI), heterozygously expressed mutant human cardiac troponin I (K206I/WT), and mutant human cardiac troponin I (K206I).

The site of the mutation is of particular interest in that, despite extensive investigations of structure-function relations, there are significant gaps in our understanding of the role of the mobile domain (MD) of hcTnI (containing residues 167–210), in the regulation of thin filament-thick filament interactions. There is ample evidence that the relaxed state of the myofilaments involves an inhibition of the reaction of myosin cross-bridges with the thin filament. The inhibition occurs at Ca2+ concentrations below the threshold for binding to a regulatory site on human cardiac troponin C (hcTnC), in which actin binding hcTnI inhibitory peptides flanking a switch peptide act together with the interactions of human cardiac troponin T (hcTnT) with tropomyosin (TM) to block the actin-myosin interaction.1 As the Ca2+ concentration increases, sites on the thin filament are released from inhibition by promotion of binding of a switch peptide of hcTnI to Ca2+- bound- hcTnC (Ca-hcTnC).4, 5 How the MD domain of hcTnI, which contains K206, participates in establishing relaxation and in switching on activation remains unclear. The location of the mutation in hcTnI is the C-terminal region that did not resolve in the crystal structure, and whose function remains unclear.6 A potentially significant role for the MD of hcTnI has been established in studies testing the regulatory role of hcTnI with truncations at the C-terminus.7–10 Our demonstration of HCM linked to a single missense mutation extends our understanding of the role of the hcTnI C-terminus in long term regulation of cardiac remodeling.

The aim of the present study is to provide evidence that the newly discovered K206I mutation in TNNI3 is a HCM-susceptibility mutation, and to determine the effect and mechanism of the mutation on myofilament Ca2+ sensitivity. The TNNI3 gene has thus far been linked to 31 HCM associated mutations2, 11, however, the TNNI3 gene is only represented in <2% of patients with genotype positive HCM, making mutations in TNNI3 relatively rare in this population.12 Interpretation of novel genetic variants in genes such as TNNI3 is difficult due to background noise (~5%), and thus in vitro analysis is essential to infer pathogenicity.13 Our studies revealed that compared to controls, myofilaments regulated by K206I demonstrate modified Ca2+ responsiveness and altered interactions among thin filament proteins. The green tea catechin (−)-epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) has previously been shown to decrease Ca2+ sensitivity in myofilaments,14, 15 thus we tested whether EGCG could be a potential therapeutic for this K206I mutation in TNNI3. EGCG was able to restore myofilament Ca2+ sensitivity to near WT levels.

The subject was identified during evaluation for HCM at Mayo Clinic and was enrolled in a Mayo Clinic IRB approved research protocol # 811-98 and, after discussing with the provider, signed a consent form to participate. Retrospective review of the electronic medical record was performed to identify the clinical phenotype of the patient. All clinical and genetic tests were performed as standard clinical care in the evaluation and treatment of the index’s case disease. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was performed clinically according to standard protocols and appropriate images and tracings from all angles were obtained. (GE, Vivid 7).

Two independent genetic screens were used to identify the genotype. First a comprehensive clinical genetic test was performed on the index case prior to evaluation at Mayo Clinic for 18 HCM genes and catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT) associated genes: RYR2,KCNJ2 and CASQ2 on the index case (GeneDx, Gaithersburg, MD). For a full list of the 18 HCM-associated genes used in the genetic test, please see link: http://www.genedx.com/test-catalog/disorders/hypertrophic-cardiomyopathy-hcm/. Second, targeted in-house mutation specific confirmatory testing for the patient-specific variant was performed for the index case and the index case’s parents. Genotyping of the phenotype positive sibling was performed elsewhere.

The micro-ATPase assay was performed as previously described with some minor modifications.16

pCa-Force measurements were performed with troponin-exchanged skinned papillary fiber bundles from hearts of FVBN mice (3–4 months) as previously described.17

pCa-ATPase and pCa-force relations were fit to a modified four-parameter Hill equation by nonlinear least squares regression with GraphPad Prizm 6.0.18 The equation is defined as 
y=bottom+(top−bottom)/(1+10(pCa50−pCa)⋅HillSlope). Herein, bottom is the Y value at the bottom plateau; top is the Y value at the top plateau; pCa50 is the x value when the response is halfway between bottom and top; and Hill Slope describes the steepness of the curve and is known as the Hill coefficient. The graphs were generated with GraphPad Prizm 6.0. The pCa-ATPase and pCa-force relations were analyzed by 2-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test (GraphPad Prizm 6.0) with a level of significance set at p<0.05 in the experiments with two independent variables (EGCG and mutant). The pCa-ATPase experiments were analyzed by 1-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test (GraphPad Prizm 6.0) with a level of significance set at p<0.05 in experiments with one independent variable (mutant). All values are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Normal Gaussian distributions of the data sets were tested with an D’ Agostino-Pearson omnibus test along with Brown-Forsythe test to check constant variances among groups. Percent maximal ATPase activity was normalized to highest rate in the data set, which was set to100%. Binary comparisons between WT and K206I at each data point in the hcTnI inhibition and hcTnC neutralization experiments were analyzed by a two-tailed Student’s t-test with a level of significance set at p< 0.05 with Microsoft Excel 2007.

The index case was a 13-year-old male, diagnosed with HCM after an OHCA in 2009, when he was jogging during physical education class. He was resuscitated, requiring 2 shocks with an automated external defibrillator (AED), and was implanted with an implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) shortly after the event. Over the 4 following years, he has received at least 12 discrete appropriate, VF-terminating ICD shocks. The first one occurred about 5 months after placement of the ICD and the last one was as recent as February of 2013, despite numerous changes in medications. During the most recent episode, he received multiple shocks in the setting of polymorphic ventricular tachycardia and needed 7 ICD discharges to return to normal rhythm. All events occurred in the setting of some level of aerobic activity.

After this last event, he was referred to Mayo Clinic to evaluate the possible role of left cardiac sympathetic denervation (LCSD) as part of his treatment plan. Upon referral, a follow up transthoracic echocardiogram was performed in June of 2013 showing non-obstructive HCM with severe septal hypertrophy of 25 mm, posterior wall thickness of 18 mm and LV mass of 443 g shown in Figure 1A, 1B and Table1. He also had grade 2/4 diastolic dysfunction (pseudonormalized E-A ratio 1.67) with an enlarged left atrium (LA volume index by A-L cc/m2 of 45) and E/e’ (medial) of 16.7 (Table1). However, the proband did not have very severe repolarization abnormalities on ECG.

The index case has a positive family history of HCM (Fig. 1C). Upon diagnosis of the index case, a screening echocardiogram revealed left ventricular hypertrophy in a younger sibling (III.2). In November of 2013, she had a cardiac arrest while running. She received 3 AED shocks to rescue, and was implanted with an ICD shortly after the incident, which has shocked her several times. Neither parent demonstrates clinical or echocardiographic phenotype of HCM (II.1 and II.2). The mother also had a cardiac MRI performed in January of 2014 which showed no evidence of HCM. There was no family history of HCM or history of sudden cardiac death (SCD) in the extended family.

Due to his OHCA and positive echocardiographic results for HCM, a comprehensive clinical genetic analysis of 18 HCM and CPVT- associated genes was performed prior to evaluation at Mayo Clinic, and a novel, heterozygous mutation was identified in TNNI3-encoded cardiac troponin I. This variant - p.Lys206Ile (also annotated as p.K206I, c. 617 A>T) - was classified as ‘likely disease-causing’ based on the non-conservative nature of the amino-acid substitution, conservation of this residue across species, presence of a previously published, HCM-associated mutation in the same residue (K206Q),19 and absence of this variant in over 6,000 controls. Mutation specific testing of this variant showed that the index case’s phenotype positive sister III.2 as well as his phenotype negative mother were both genotype positive for TNNI3-K206I (Fig. 1C).

To determine functional effects of the hcTnI-K206I mutation, recombinant proteins (troponin subunits) were purified and reconstituted into a functional complex with actin, tropomyosin, and myosin-S1. Actin, tropomyosin, and myosin-S1 were natively purified and are shown individually along with the recombinant troponin proteins separated in 15% SDS-PAGE (Fig. 2). The protein complexes used in functional assays are also shown in the presence and absence of calcium where hcTnC mobility is affected due to conformational changes induced in hcTnC by calcium binding (Fig. 2). The electrophoretic mobility of hcTnC in SDS-PAGE did not differ when present in myofilaments containing either TnI K206I or WT TnI in the presence or absence of Ca2+, suggesting the genetic variant did not cause major conformational changes in hcTnC (Fig. 2).

ATPase activity assays demonstrated that the mutant K206I and 50% mutant K206I/WT caused a significant increase in the Ca2+ sensitivity with cardiac myosin-S1 and skeletal myosin-S1 (Fig. 3, Tables S1 and 2). The max ATPase activity of skeletal myosin-S1 was also significantly increased in the K206I compared to the WT (Table 2 and Fig. 3B). The K206I mutation had a similar functional impact on both cardiac and skeletal myosin S1 (Fig. 3A and 3B). To determine the concentration of EGCG that could decrease the Ca2+ sensitivity, dose response curves were generated as shown in Figure 4. WT (IC50=15.80±1.0) and K206I (IC50=13.40±0.4) mutant dose response curves showed no significant differences at pCa 5.8 (Fig. 4A), however, there was a significant difference in the 50% K206I/WT (IC50=21.40±1.3) compared to the others. Dose response curves were also performed at pCa 4.5 (Fig. 4B), and the IC50s for all three groups were significantly different from each other: WT 19.40±0.01; K206I/WT 22.00±0.01; and K206I 31.70±0.01.

Due to the relatively low rates of ATPase activity in preparations with cardiac myosin-S1, we tested the effect of EGCG on preparations with skeletal-S1. Cardiac and skeletal-S1 demonstrate a similar impact of the K206I and 50% K206I/WT on Ca2+ response (Fig. 3, Table 2 and S1). After treatment with 20 μM EGCG, the K206I and 50% K206I/WT groups showed a similar decrease in Ca2+ sensitivity compared to WT (Fig. 3B and Table 2). Interestingly, the effect of EGCG on the max ATPase activity of WT, and to a lesser extent K206I/WT myofilament was significantly greater than K206I myofilaments in an apparent WT dose dependent manner. This indicates that the K206I mutant impairs the normal interaction of the EGCG at saturating Ca2+ in this basic reconstituted system (Fig. 3B).

Ca2+ sensitivity of mouse papillary skinned cardiac fiber bundles that were exchanged with either WT or 65% K206I/WT troponin (K206I/WT), was increased in the 65% K206I/WT group compared to the WT in a similar trend as the ATPase results (Fig. 5). To determine effects of EGCG dose response curves were generated at both pCa50 (5.6) and high Ca2+ (pCa 4.5) (Figs. 5A and B). 20μM EGCG was chosen based on the dose response curve at pCa 5.6 to restore Ca2+ sensitivity to near WT levels. Treatment with 20μM EGCG reduced the Ca2+ sensitivity to near WT levels as with the ATPase activity (Fig. 5C). Interestingly the max force of the WT was not impacted as greatly by the EGCG as the max ATPase.

In the presence of actin- tropomyosin-myosin-S1, the K206I mutation impairs the ability of the hcTnI to inhibit ATPase activity compared to WT in the absence of Ca2+ and at all concentrations tested (Fig. 6A). When tropomyosin was removed from the assay conditions, the hcTnI (1μM) inhibition was still significantly compromised (Fig. 6B), suggesting that the inhibitory effect can act through both actin and tropomyosin or actin alone.

Percent maximum ATPase activity was measured at high Ca2+ (pCa 4.5) as a function of varying molar ratios of hcTnC:hcTnI to determine Ca2+ dependent interactions. Figure 6C summarizes the ability of hcTnC to neutralize the inhibition of hcTnI, which was significantly enhanced with the K206I compared to the WT (all concentrations tested except 2.0μM). These data indicate an increased interaction between hcTnI (K206I) and hcTnC and/or increased affinity of Ca2+ to hcTnC.

This is the first study to report a discovered novel hcTnI K206I missense mutation associated with HCM that induces increased myofilament Ca2+ sensitivity, which was restored to WT levels with EGCG. The TNNI3 missense mutation was discovered in a 13-year-old male after an OHCA with severely arrhythmogenic HCM that may be evolving towards dilated cardiomyopathy at a relatively young age. After clinical evaluation, both the index case and his phenotype positive sister were diagnosed with HCM and found to harbor the K206I variant, while their mother is genotype (+) but phenotype (−).

The known variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance in HCM creates the challenge in clinical diagnosis and decision making in HCM.20 This is further complicated by the fact that the background rate of rare genetic variants in healthy individuals is about 5% in the nine most common sarcomeric HCM genes.13 The genes represent 90–95% of genetically identifiable HCM, making clinical interpretation difficult, especially in the ‘minor’ genes, including TNNI3.13 Although the current variant K206I-TNNI3 was classified by the genetic test company as ‘likely disease-causing’ based on the non-conservative nature of the amino-acid substitution, conservation of this residue across species, presence of an HCM-associated mutation in the same residue,19 and absence of this variant in over 6,000 controls, in vitro characterization can be extremely useful to determine pathogenicity of a rare, novel variant. A recently reported case demonstrated variable expressivity in HCM in a family that has a HCM mutation in MYBPC3 R597Q.11 The ages of phenotype manifestation were hypervariable (16yrs, 42yrs, and 66yrs old)11 and all were genotype (+) suggesting the possibility that onset of a phenotype in our K206I could be variable and linked to other factors, such as the individuals epigenetics.

Further evidence for K206I-TNNI3 being an HCM pathogenic mutation is supported by biochemical functional analysis and another very closely related mutation, K206Q-TNNI3, described by Kimura et al. in 1997.19 As in our study with K206I, the increase in Ca2+ sensitivity induced by K206Q, determined by in vitro motility assays, was independent of myosin isoform population.21 Myofilament ATPase activity assays also showed an increase in Ca2+ sensitivity with a K206Q mutant22, similar to our measurements with the K206I variant (Figs. 3, 4 and Table 2), supporting the assertion that K206I is a pathogenic mutation. To further support the pathogenicity of the K206I mutation, we also demonstrated that the mutation caused an increase in Ca2+ sensitivity in pCa-force measurements (Fig. 5 and Table 3). Our findings provide further support for the hypothesis that increased myofibrillar Ca2+ sensitivity is one of the final common pathways for sarcomeric HCM.3, 23

Mutations that affect normal molecular mechanisms of myofilament activation and relaxation are likely to involve multiple hcTnI domains. The carboxyl-terminus of hcTnI has three domains: the inhibitory region containing residues 130–150 responsible for inhibiting contraction, the switch region containing residues 151–166 responsible for binding to hcTnC in the presence of Ca2+ to trigger thin filament activation, and the mobile domain (MD) containing residues 167–210 responsible for stabilizing the interaction with actin in the absence of Ca2+.10, 24, 25 Our data on the effect of the K206I mutation provides insights into the role of the hcTnI MD in control of cardiac function. Current understanding of the role of the MD has come largely from truncation studies. For example, myofilaments controlled by hcTnI-1-193 missing 18 C-terminal amino acids demonstrate reduced tension compared to controls.9, 26, 27 These data indicate that the MD is required for homeostatic control of tension. Moreover, hcTnI-1-193 did not affect Ca2+-binding to hcTnC in the ternary Tn complex, but did alter Ca2+-binding in the presence of actin-TM.27 This suggests that long range interactions to regulatory domains (troponin subunits, actin, and TM) of the thin filament are under the control of the MD. Thus, it is plausible that these long range interactions are influenced by the charge state of the MD.

Our data suggest the compromised interactions of K206I with actin and hcTnC may lead to impaired relaxation and HCM. The ability of K206I to inhibit acto-myosin ATPase rate was significantly reduced compared to WT with or without TM present in the reaction. This implies that K206I impaired hcTnI intrinsic inhibitory activity (fig. 6A and B). The MD has been reported to have potential interactions with TM, but we found that the inhibitory effect of K206I was unaffected when TM was not included in the thin filament reconstitution. Thus, effects of the inhibitory and switch peptide appear to dominate the influence of the mutation on response to Ca2+. In skinned cardiac myofibrils regulated by K206Q hcTnI, there was no change in the inhibition of hcTnI from the WT albeit with different experimental conditions.22 The two mutations (K206Q and K206I) differ from each other by the polar hydrophilic nature of glutamine vs the hydrophobic nature of isoleucine of the substituted amino-acids. In terms of inhibitory function, the 206 codon of hcTnI appears functionally dynamic suggesting different mechanisms resulting from a specific mutation. In addition to the K206I’s compromised inhibitory activity, we also found that neutralization of the inhibitory effect of hcTnI by hcTnC is enhanced in the presence of Ca2+ (Fig. 6C). The increased neutralization of the inhibitory hcTnI by hcTnC suggests an enhanced interaction with hcTnC. The possibility also exists that the Ca2+ binding affinity to hcTnC is affected by the K206I mutation. In contrast, experiments with the similar K206Q-mutation noted no significant differences in Ca2+ binding.22

EGCG modulates myofilament force and ATPase rate most likely by interacting with hcTnC. EGCG binds to the carboxyl terminus of hcTnC (ChcTnC) leading to decreased Ca2+ sensitivity.14, 28, 29 EGCG binding is specific for ChcTnC28 and it has been proposed to affect the interaction of hcTnC with hcTnI, thus affecting sensitivity of the thin filament for Ca2+.15, 29–31 In the K206I and K206I/WT variants, Ca2+ sensitivity was restored close to WT levels when myofilaments were treated with 20 μM EGCG. EGCG treatment had a much greater effect on actomyosin (Max 1/sec and Hillslope) preparations regulated by the WT and 50% K206I/WT vs the K206I. The greater inhibitory effect of ECGC on actomyosin ATPase rate regulated by WT versus K206I is expected in view of the promotion of force and ATPase rate in the presence of K206I. That is, with WT, the effect of EGCG was unopposed whereas with K206I, the inhibitory effect was partially offset by the increase in ATPase rate associated with K206I. In contrast to the results with ATPase measurements, in the case of the force measurements, differences in effects of EGCG on tension developed by fibers regulated by WT vs K206I were not as great as in the case of the ATPase measurements (Figs. 3, 5
Table 2 and 3). We speculate that the effects of the charge change in the MD of hcTnI may also be greater in the ATPase activity vs force measurements due to the unloaded vs loaded systems, and/or absence of other regulating proteins particularly the Z-disk proteins.

The EGCG experiments indirectly support the premise of increased interaction between K206I and hcTnC, as EGCG binds to the carboxyl terminus of hcTnC near sites III and IV but not directly affecting Ca2+ binding to site II. This evidence also indicates there are likely little differences in Ca2+ binding due to the mutation. The EGCG concentration used was based on our dose response curves (Figs. 4, 5A and B), which predicted restoration of K206I and K206I/WT (in both ATPase and force measurements) Ca2+ sensitivity at 20 μM. However, previously published reports indicated no effects below 25 μM EGCG in skinned cardiac myofibers.15, 28, 31 In our ATPase experiments, we employed reconstituted thin filaments from individually purified proteins, and added myosin-S1 in vitro to carry out the ATPase assay. So it is likely that in our more simple system, the ChcTnC binding partner for EGCG is more accessible allowing for a lower effective dose. In our pCa-force measurements we saw significant effects of EGCG at 20μM, and Papadaki et al. found 10μM EGCG to be effective in isolated myofibrils,32 suggesting experimental design may contribute to variable efficacy in EGCG.

Restoration of myofilament Ca2+ sensitivity to near WT levels has been suggested as an approach to treatment for HCM. An HCM mouse model expressing a mutation in one of the other ‘minor’ sarcomeric genes – Glu180Gly in TPM1 encoded α-tropomyosin - demonstrated increased myofilament Ca2+ sensitivity, and rescue of the phenotype occured when crossed with a chimeric α-/β-tropomyosin transgenic mouse with a decreased myofilament Ca2+ sensitivity.33 A transgenic mouse model mimicking a human restrictive cardiomyopathy (cTnI Arg193His) with increased Ca2+ sensitivity was also rescued by crossing with a mouse transgene (N-terminus of cTnI truncated) thereby decreasing the Ca2+ sensitivity.34 Recently, Alves et al.35 proposed that reduction in myofilament sensitivity to Ca2+ corrects abnormal relaxation and can delay or prevent HCM. Genetic rescue of a disease phenotype does not elucidate whether intervention during disease progression would be beneficial. It has been suggested that treatment with EGCG may be able to restore Ca2+ sensitivity during disease progression.32

Although green tea may be a therapeutic treatment by affecting Ca2+ sensitivity, it has been linked to adverse events.36 In addition, our study indicate 20μM EGCG as an effective concentration, and has shown cardioprotection by enhancing shortening37, but would likely require dietary supplements. A higher concentration of EGCG (50μM) has also been demonstrated to afford cardioprotection, but increases in incidences of arrhythmia and diastolic dysfunction have been reported.37 The Ohsaki and JPHC studies showed an association with regular green tea consumption and reduced mortality due to cardiovascular disease vs diets with less green tea.38, 39

In summary, this is the first report to describe this newly found hcTnI K206I mutation associated with severely arrhythmogenic HCM. These studies provide evidence in support of K206I-TNNI3 as an HCM pathogenic missense mutation. We have provided direct evidence for the first time that hcTnI K206I induces increased Ca2+ sensitivity in a reconstituted thin filament system with restoration of Ca2+ sensitivity to WT levels with EGCG treatment. The impaired relaxation caused by K206I may be partly explained by induction of a weakened interaction with actin and/or, intrinsic inhibitory activity while increasing the interaction of hcTnI with hcTnC leading to increased Ca2+ sensitivity.
