BACKGROUND: The prevalence of malnutrition is unknown in patients with 
Hirschsprung disease. Undernutrition is associated with poor clinical outcomes. 
This study aims to describe the nutrition status among patients with 
Hirschsprung disease at admission.
METHODS: We retrospectively used data from children with Hirschsprung disease 
admitted to three pediatric surgery centers in China from January 2016 to 
December 2020. The weight-for-age z scores (WAZ), height-for-age z scores (HAZ), 
and body mass index-for-age z scores (BAZ) were calculated as the reference for 
nutrition risk according to the World Health Organization child growth 
standards. The nutrition status of enrolled children was described and nutrition 
risk in each clinical characteristic was compared. The association between 
nutrition status and clinical outcomes was analyzed using univariate and 
multivariate logistic regression.
RESULTS: A total of 624 patients were included in this study. The mean WAZ, HAZ, 
and BAZ of all patients was -0.64 ± 1.40, -0.45 ± 1.78, and -0.43 ± 1.50, 
respectively. Moderate to severe overall undernutrition was 16.3% (102/624). We 
found that WAZ and BAZ were significantly reduced with the length of aganglionic 
segments (P = 0.001). Children who had a definitive surgery at 3 years of age or 
older had significantly lower HAZ (P = 0.001). A multivariate regression model 
assessing postoperative Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis showed that the 
WAZ was one of the independent risk factors (P = 0.001).
CONCLUSION: Undernutrition is prevalent among children with Hirschsprung 
disease. Nutrition assessment to identify individuals at risk of undernutrition 
for further intervention is necessary.

Nutrition screening is essential for hospitalized children to prevent adverse clinical outcomes and allows for further nutrition assessment and intervention for these children to meet energy requirements for catch‐up growth and development. This study provides evidence regarding the unknown prevalence of undernutrition in patients with Hirschsprung disease and shows that simple anthropometric measurements using weight and height could be routinized on admission to assist with the identification of undernourished patients in need of nutrition intervention to achieve adequate growth.

Hirschsprung disease (HSCR) is a congenital disorder characterized by aganglionosis of the colon that results from the abnormal development of the enteric nervous system during the embryonic period. It is one of the most common surgical conditions in pediatrics, with an incidence of ~1/5000 of live births in China.
1
 Although major advances have been made in definitive surgical treatment with low mortality, optimizing overall management is critical for patients with HSCR to achieve good quality of life associated with low rates of postoperative complications.
2
, 
3
, 
4
 Nutrition assessment for further adequate nutrition support is one of the crucial aspects in the overall management of HSCR. Illness‐related malnutrition is prevalent in up to 40% of surgical patients.
5
, 
6
 However, knowledge concerning nutrition status in patients with HSCR is limited. An epidemiological survey showed that nutrition and growth are the second most common concern of parents or guardians of patients with HSCR and that they hope to obtain information from clinicians.
7



In addition, undernutrition was identified as an independent factor predicting poor postoperative outcomes in pediatric patients.
8
, 
9
, 
10
 In patients with HSCR, studies identifying predictors of postoperative Hirschsprung‐associated enterocolitis (HAEC) reported that malnutrition was associated with the presence of postoperative recurrent HAEC and a high risk of mortality.
11
 However, limited studies can be accessed on the nutrition screening and assessment for patients with HSCR pre‐operation or post‐operation. Thus, the present study aims to describe a preliminary result on screening patients with HSCR at nutrition risk prior to surgery using anthropometric measurements on admission for the first time and to explore the association between undernutrition and postoperative HAEC rates. In this article, we hypothesize that undernutrition is underestimated in patients with HSCR and that it is one of the risk factors for postoperative HAEC.

This cross‐sectional multicenter study was conducted at three tertiary care pediatric surgery centers in China consisting of Tongji Hospital of Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology, The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University, and Hunan Children's Hospital. Children were included for this study if they were diagnosed with HSCR on the basis of histological confirmation and underwent a transanal endorectal pull‐through procedure (ERPT) as the primary operation at <5 years of age between January 2016 and December 2020. In total, 718 patients were eligible for reviewing the medical records. Patients with total bowel aganglionosis, with additional nutrition deficiencies, who underwent colostomy prior to pull‐through surgery or had reoperation, or with incomplete medical records were excluded. Thus, a total of 624 patients were eligible for analysis. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or caregivers of each patient before medical records were reviewed. Ethical approval for the present study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Tongji Hospital of Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (approval number TJ‐IRB20191220).

We undertook a review of patients' medical records in the hospital information system at three medical institutions and collected clinical information regarding sociodemographic characteristics (age at surgery and sex), gestational age, complications with additional anomalies or genetic syndromes, length of aganglionosis, and performance of conservative treatment prior to definitive surgery. Clinical outcomes of postoperative complications were also captured from the medical records. Postoperative HAEC was diagnosed if the patients presented any clinical symptoms and signs of fever, vomiting, foul‐smelling watery diarrhea, or abdominal distention.
12



Preoperative diagnosis of HSCR was based on the significant narrow distal bowel on the radiological investigation of barium enema, anorectal manometry, and rectal biopsy. Preoperative biopsies included rectal suction biopsy and full‐thickness biopsy. Acetylcholinesterase histochemical staining is the most common method of suction biopsy staining. HSCR was confirmed by postoperative histopathology. According to the range of aganglionic bowel, HSCR was classified as follows: (1) short‐segment aganglionosis lies in the mid‐distal rectum; (2) classic‐segment aganglionosis does not extend beyond the upper sigmoid; (3) long‐segment aganglionosis extends from the anal to the descending colon and even the transverse colon; (4) total‐colonic aganglionosis involves the ascending colon and even the partial ileum within 30 cm of the ileocecal junction; and (5) total‐intestinal aganglionosis involves the total colon and small intestine beyond 30 cm from the ileocecal junction and even the duodenum.
13



All enrolled cases underwent a primary ERPT. Based on our previous experience and current meta‐analysis results, ERPT was recommended for infants >3 months of age who had been diagnosed with HSCR.
14
, 
15
 For those who were <3 months old at diagnosis or lacked a confirmed diagnosis at a medical visit, temporary conservative therapy was given prior to surgery, including anal dilation, colonic lavage, dietary adjustment for bowel decompression, and administration of oral probiotics until the medical conditions other than HSCR were excluded.

Nutrition risk was evaluated using anthropometric measurements. Weight in kilograms and height in centimeters measured on admission were recorded from electronic medical records. After data collection, weight‐for‐age z scores (WAZ), height‐for‐age z scores (HAZ), body mass index (BMI), and BMI‐for‐age z scores (BAZ) were calculated using the World Health Organization (WHO) Anthro software in relation to growth standards of age and sex based on WHO's 2006 Multicenter Growth Reference study. A z score indicates the number of SDs above or below the mean value in a normal distribution from a study population. WHO has defined pediatric wasting and stunting (chronic malnutrition) status using WAZ and HAZ, respectively.
16
 Patients were then classified into three groups as low (−1 to −1.9 z score), moderate (−2 to −2.9 z score), and severe undernutrition risk (−3 z score or below) groups according to WAZ, HAZ, and BAZ.

Patients were separated into age groups of 0–12 months, 13–36 months, and 37–60 months for statistical analyses. The Shapiro‐Wilk test and Levene's test were applied before further analysis to conform to the distribution normality and homogeneity of variance. WAZ, HAZ, and BAZ conformed to a normal distribution on examination. Data are presented using descriptive statistics, including the mean and corresponding SD for continuous variables or count alongside the frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. We performed t tests and analysis of variance to compare means among groups when the variances were homoscedastic; otherwise, Wilcoxon rank sum tests or Kruskal‐Wallis tests were performed. The chi‐square test was used to compare categorical variables. To assess the association between WAZ and the occurrence of postoperative HAEC, univariate and forward multivariate linear and logistic regression were performed for the occurrence of HAEC, respectively. Factors in the regression model included sex, age at diagnosis, type of disease, type of management, nutrition risk, and so on. Data were analyzed using SPSS 26 (IBM). P < 0.05 with two‐sided indicates a statistically significant difference.

A total of 718 children with HSCR aged <5 years of age were admitted to the hospital from January 2016 to December 2020, with 624 cases analyzed in this study, with a median age of 9 months (0–60 months). The majority of patients were male, accounting for 82.1% (n = 512) of all patients. Preterm infants accounted for 2.1% (n = 13). Patients were categorized into age groups of 0–12 months, 13–36 months, and 37–60 months in the present cohort for data analysis. Patients in the above age groups were not distributed evenly, with a higher percentage of patients diagnosed with HSCR during infancy, with 63.1% (n = 394) in the 0‐ to 12‐month age group, 25.8% (n = 161) in the 13‐ to 36‐month age group, and 11.1% (n = 69) in the 37‐ to 60‐month age group. Among all children, 9.6% had additional congenital anomalies (n = 60). The numbers of short‐segment, classic‐segment, long‐segment, and total‐colon aganglionosis HSCR cases were 32.5% (n = 203), 51.1% (n = 319), 12.3% (n = 77), and 4.0% (n = 25), respectively. At the time of admission, the mean values of WAZ, HAZ, and BAZ of the enrolled children were −0.64 ± 1.40, −0.45 ± 1.77, and −0.43 ± 1.50, respectively. The rate of patients with WAZ of −1 or lower indicating a mild, moderate, or severe undernutrition risk was 18.1%, 10.6%, and 5.8%, respectively. The rates of HAZ ≤ −2 and BAZ ≤ −2 were 15.3% and 17.1%, respectively.

We examined the mean WAZ among groups for each clinical characteristic (Table 2). There were no significant differences in the means of WAZ for sex (P = 0.53), gestational age (P = 0.40), and additional anomalies (P = 0.85). Means of WAZ for the three age groups at diagnosis were calculated and compared. The WAZ of the 0‐ to 1‐year, 1‐ to 3‐year, and 3‐ to 5‐year age groups were −0.67 ± 1.48, −0.65 ± 1.27, and −0.45 ± 1.24, respectively, showing no significant difference (P = 0.49). In each age group, 18.0% (71/394), 14.9% (24/161), and 10.1% (7/69) had moderate to severe undernutrition status (Table 2). The chi‐square test for age groups showed that the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.44). The undernutrition for short‐segment, classic‐segment, long‐segment, and total‐colon aganglionosis HSCR was significantly different, with WAZ values of −0.59 ± 1.44, −0.54 ± 1.27, −0.81 ± 1.56, and −1.91 ± 1.57, respectively (P = 0.001). Consistently, the incidence of moderate to severe undernutrition for each HSCR group was 13.8% (28/203), 13.8% (44/319), 23.4% (18/77), and 48.0% (12/25), with statistical significance (P = 0.001) (Table 3). Based on HAZ data, the highest risk was found in patients who were diagnosed with HSCR at an older age (P = 0.01) (Table 2). The rates of HAZ ≤ −2 were 8.8%, 24.1%, and 25%, respectively, with borderline significance (P = 0.05) (Table 3). Means of BAZ were compared for each clinical characteristic. Consistent with the results of WAZ, patients diagnosed with long‐segment and total colon aganglionosis were found to have lower BAZ (P = 0.001) (Table 2) with a higher incidence of moderate to severe undernutrition status (P = 0.02) (Table 3).

HSCR is one of the most common congenital anomalies requiring surgical interventions in the pediatric population. From an enhanced recovery after surgery perspective, nutrition assessment is an important aspect in the management of pediatric patients. Much of the current literature examining the prevalence of malnutrition of pediatric patients, particularly undernutrition, focuses on cardiovascular diseases, neurological disorders, metabolic diseases, severe illness in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU), and malignancy.
16
, 
17
, 
18
, 
19
, 
20
 The documented prevalence of undernutrition varies depending on the underlying conditions ranging from 9% in patients with dermatological disease
6
 to 50% in patients with congenital heart disease or cancer
17
, 
21
, 
22
 to 84% in the PICU.
18
 However, few studies exist that evaluate undernutrition in patients with HSCR. One study assessed the nutrition status as an end point indicating growth outcome in the HSCR population,
23
 but limited information was available on preoperative nutrition risk screening. To our knowledge, this study is the first to describe the nutrition risk prior to operation using anthropometric measurements in a large sample HSCR population based on three major HSCR treatment centers in China.

The results of our study provide novel data on the prevalence of malnutrition presurgery for patients with HSCR. WAZ, HAZ, and BAZ were used as indicators of nutrition status in pediatric patients. We demonstrated that malnutrition is a silent epidemic; >15% of pediatric patients with HSCR had moderate to severe malnutrition on admission for primary surgery. This state is currently underrecognized by many surgeons and caregivers. Adequate nutrition is required to ensure proper neurodevelopment, a functional immune system, and an adequate response to treatment. Thus, the nutrition status of children with HSCR needs to be assessed on a regular basis to enable early identification and treatment of malnutrition.

In this study, we also determined the contributing factors influencing acute and chronic nutrition risk at admission in patients with HSCR. A significant correlation between the length of aganglionosis and lower BAZ and WAZ was identified, and the same trend applied to age and HAZ. We revealed that the WAZ and BAZ for extended aganglionosis is significantly lower. It can be inferred that the longer the aganglionic segment involves, the greater the likelihood of a failing functioning gastrointestinal tract and the less sufficient nutrients the colon absorbs and uses, consequently leading to an increased nutrition risk for these patients. Age at pull‐through surgery had a significant difference among groups, showing that patients who were at 3 years of age and older were probably at a higher risk of stunting because of a long period of nutrition failure. Although most of the patients with HSCR were diagnosed in early infancy, some patients with HSCR in rural China had delayed diagnosis because of socioeconomic conditions. In low‐ and middle‐income rural subdistricts, the mild constipation of children in the care of nonparental caregivers was more likely to be underrecognized or ignored by guardians until the symptoms were severe, leading to a delayed diagnosis and consequently delayed treatment. In addition, infants were recommended to delay definitive surgery beyond 3 months of age based on our previous experience and a recently published meta‐analysis because the false negative results in barium enema are higher in patients <3 months of age than in older patients
24
 and are associated with an increased rate of postoperative complications.
14
, 
15
 In the setting of delayed diagnosis and treatment, the likelihood of developing nutrition deficiencies is high and increases in these children because of decreased nutrient intake, increased energy expenditure, and altered gastrointestinal function during this period prior to surgery. In addition, patients with preoperative HAEC are in short‐term or chronic inflammatory conditions that may be in a nutrient wasting state, thereby increasing the requirement for energy.
25



Altogether, the etiology of undernutrition in patients with HSCR is a complex interplay between energy intake and loss. Basically, groups of patients who are diagnosed with extended aganglionosis and delayed diagnosis might be at high risk of undernutrition that should be identified early using screening tools on admission. Awareness of nutrition deterioration at hospital admission, or ideally prior to admission, will provide an opportunity to improve nutrition status prior to surgery and decrease the risk of postoperative HAEC.

Finally, our study provides additional evidence concerning moderate to severe undernutrition, as it is significantly associated with poor postoperative outcomes, which is consistent with the results of previous studies identifying risk factors for recurrent HAEC and quality of life in patients with HSCR.
11
, 
26
 Ding et al reported the impact of preoperative nutrition support in patients with HSCR and refractory constipation. They demonstrated that at the 1‐month follow‐up, the incidence of postoperative complications was lower and nutrition status was significantly improved in the group of patients who received enteral nutrition (EN) for 2 weeks prior to surgery compared with those without EN support.
27



In summary, nutrition risk screening for children with HSCR based on the WAZ and HAZ is the first step for the identification of patients at potential risk for undernutrition. Further detailed assessment is required for calculating the total metabolic rate and functional variables.
28
 In terms of nutrition intervention, timely nutrition supportive treatment can prevent development and growth problems related to malnutrition. To prove that timely preoperative nutrition intervention and high‐energy diet supplements could reduce the occurrence of postoperative complications and are beneficial to the long‐term outcome of children with HSCR, a prospective multicenter real‐world study was carried out (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04598841). Nutrition status was assessed as an end point indicating the growth outcome of patients with HSCR to explore the role of perioperative nutrition intervention (formulas for special medical purposes vs standard infant formular) in the sequential treatment of HSCR.

This is a large study from three tertiary care pediatric surgery centers across China with low rates of missing data. This study provided evidence to solidify the scientific basis for evidence‐based nutrition practices in the management of HSCR. One of the limitations of this study is the inherent deficiencies of retrospective studies.

This study provides objective evidence on the prevalence of undernutrition in patients with HSCR. Patients who are diagnosed with a longer aganglionic segment and who have a delayed diagnosis may be at higher nutrition risk and need further nutrition assessment to minimize suboptimal outcomes. Future research is required for optimal nutrition assessment, timing of nutrition intervention, selection and compounding of EN and parenteral nutrition support, and duration of supplementation for patients with HSCR.
