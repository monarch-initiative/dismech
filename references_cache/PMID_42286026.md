# Assessment of gabapentin efficacy in patients with KCNQ2-developmental epileptic encephalopathy.
**Authors:** Xiong J, You X, Li H, Chen C, Duan H, Chen S, Cao X, He F, Fei Y, Xiao Z, Peng J
**Journal:** Sci Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41598-026-55285-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-55285-3)

## Content

KCNQ2‑related developmental epileptic encephalopathy (KCNQ2‑DEE) is characterized by intractable epilepsy, cognitive impairment, and behavioral problems, highlighting the urgent need for precision therapies. This study aimed to promote the clinical application of gabapentin, a known Kv7 channel opener, in KCNQ2‑DEE by evaluating its efficacy in four patients carrying different KCNQ2 variants. Three patients with S6‑segment variants (p.A294V, p.F305I, p.F305del) showed clinical improvement in both seizure control and cognition following add‑on gabapentin. In contrast, the patient with the S4 variant p.L200P did not exhibit electroencephalographic improvement, although some developmental progress was noted. Functional analysis in homomeric expression systems revealed that the three S6 variants produced negligible currents, consistent with severe loss‑of‑function (LoF). The p.L200P variant, however, showed smaller currents than wild‑type (WT) at voltages below + 20 mV, larger currents at higher voltages, slower activation kinetics, and a depolarized shift in half‑activation voltage (V₁/₂), indicating a mixed LoF/gain‑of‑function phenotype. In heteromeric Kv7.2/Kv7.3 channels, p.L200P retained significantly higher current compared to the S6 variants, which may explain the poorer response to gabapentin in this patient. Together, these findings further support gabapentin as a potential precision therapy for a subset of KCNQ2‑DEE patients, addressing an unmet clinical need.

KCNQ2-related developmental epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) typically presents with neonatal or infantile onset of daily multiple seizures, prominent electroencephalogram (EEG) abnormalities, long-term cognitive impairment, and behavioral comorbidities including autistic-like features, aggressive behavior and hyperactivity 1 , 2 . The KCNQ2 gene encodes the Kv7.2 subunit, which together with Kv7.3 assembles to form the voltage‑gated potassium channel that mediates the M‑current, a subthreshold, non‑inactivating potassium current critical for controlling neuronal excitability 3 , 4 . Current KCNQ2-DEE cohorts indicate that some patients achieve seizure remission with empirical regimens like sodium channel blockers (SCBs) and valproic acid (VPA), and that seizure frequency may decrease with age during childhood or adulthood 2 , 5– 8 . However, evidence supporting beneficial effects of these medications on neurodevelopmental outcomes remains insufficient 9 . Therefore, precision therapies are urgently needed.

Most pathogenic KCNQ2 variants lead to suppression of homomeric Kv7.2 currents, ranging from partial to severe loss‑of‑function (LoF). A minority of variants result in gain‑of‑function (GoF) effects 10– 13 . For example, the S6‑domain variant p.A294V has been characterized as a severe LoF mutation 13 , while other variants in the voltage‑sensor or pore regions may exhibit mixed or GoF phenotypes 14 , 15 .Given the predominance of LoF defects, targeted therapeutic efforts have focused on Kv7 channel activators. In recent years, several Kv7.2 openers have been identified or developed, including retigabine (also known as ezogabine), gabapentin, and pynegabine (HN37) 16– 20 . Small‑scale studies in KCNQ2‑DEE have suggested that Kv7 agonists, such as retigabine and gabapentin, may improve not only seizure control but also cognitive outcomes 16 , 19 , 21 , 22 . However, apart from gabapentin, some of these targeted drugs have been withdrawn from the market due to severe adverse effects, while others remain in preclinical or clinical trials. Consequently, gabapentin, a traditional antiseizure medication with multiple antiepileptic mechanisms and a favorable safety profile, may be the most accessible option for these patients worldwide. Nevertheless, previous research has only verified gabapentin’s effects on Kv7 channels through in vitro electrophysiology studies and a single KCNQ2-DEE case 16 , 17 , which limits its widespread clinical application.

In this study, we recruited four KCNQ2-DEE patients carrying de novo heterozygous pathogenic or likely pathogenic KCNQ2 variants. Using patch-clamp recordings, we characterized the electrophysiological properties of these variants in homomeric Kv7.2 and heteromeric Kv7.2/Kv7.3 expression systems. Gabapentin was administered as add-on therapy, and its efficacy was evaluated based on seizure frequency and cognitive changes in the four patients.

Four patients with pathogenic or likely pathogenic KCNQ2 variants diagnosed with KCNQ2-DEE were enrolled in the study. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital of Central South University, China (#201703240) and conducted in accordance with ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants. Clinical characteristics, including age at seizure onset, seizure frequency, treatment regimens, cognitive development, electroencephalography(EEG) findings, and genetic data, were systematically documented.

The identified KCNQ2 variants were introduced into the pcDNA3.1-Kv7.2 plasmid by site-directed mutagenesis using KOD polymerase (TOYOBO), as previously described 10 . For expression of homomeric Kv7.2 channels, 4 µg of KCNQ2 wildtype(WT) or variant KCNQ2 plasmids together with 0.5 µg pEGFP‑N1 were transiently transfected into CHO‑K1 cells (Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China) using Lipofectamine™ 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. To mimic the heterozygous state observed in patients, each KCNQ2 variant was co‑expressed with KCNQ2 WT at a 1:1 ratio; additionally, to further assess variant pathogenicity, each KCNQ2 variant was co‑transfected with KCNQ3 WT at a 1:1 ratio. After 4–6 h of transfection, cells were seeded onto coverslips.

To measure activation kinetics and voltage dependence, cells were held at − 80 mV, and currents were elicited by 2 s depolarizing steps from − 90 mV to + 40 mV in 10 mV increments. Current densities were calculated by dividing the peak K⁺current by the cell capacitance. For conductance–voltage (G–V) relationships, tail currents were measured at − 90 mV, normalized to the maximum conductance, and fitted with a Boltzmann equation to obtain the half‑activation voltage (V₁/₂) and slope factor (k). Activation time constants (τ) were derived by fitting the current rise phase with a single exponential function: I = Ae-t/τ+C.

The enrolled individuals received gabapentin as add‑on therapy. Follow‑up assessments, including monitoring of seizure types, seizure frequency, possible adverse effects of gabapentin, and cognitive development, were conducted via WeChat, telephone consultations, or outpatient visits. Detailed medical information was collected from parents and from the electronic medical record system. EEG recordings were performed before treatment initiation and every 3–6 months during treatment as part of clinical care to evaluate the efficacy of gabapentin.

Data are presented as mean ± SEM, where n represents the number of independent recorded cells. Statistical analyses were performed using pClamp 10.5 (Axon Instruments, Irvine, CA, USA), GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA), and Excel 2021 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Statistical significance was assessed using unpaired t test or one–way/two-way ANOVA. Significance levels are indicated in the figures as follow: ns. P ≥ 0.05, * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001 and **** P < 0.0001.

Four KCNQ2-DEE patients were included (Table 1). Three patients were female and one was male. All experienced their first epileptic seizures within the 48 h after birth. Variable seizure types were observed, including focal tonic, focal tonic-clonic, generalized spasms, generalized tonic and spasms, and unclassified attacks. Since infancy, all patients displayed severe EEG abnormalities, including burst-suppression and hypsarrhythmia (n = 2), continuous epileptic discharges (n = 2), and multifocal/diffuse epileptic activity (n = 1). Genetic testing identified four different de novo heterozygous KCNQ2 variants: p.L200P, p.A294V, p.F305del, and p.F305I. The variant p.L200P in Patient 1 is located in the S4 voltage-sensor segment, where the other three variants lie in the transmembrane S6 domain (Fig. 1a). Residue F305 is fully conserved among KCNQ channels, and residues L200 and A294 also show high conservation (Fig. 1b). Three variants are missense, and one is an in-frame deletion. According to the ACMG standards and guidelines (2015) 23 , p.L200P, p.A294V, and p.F305del were classified as pathogenic, and p.F305I as likely pathogenic.

After genetic diagnosis, first‑line recommended antiseizure medications (ASMs) for this disorder, sodium channel blockers (SCBs), were administered 7 . Three of the four patients responded to oxcarbazepine (OXC), with Patient 3 remaining seizure‑free for nearly three years, whereas Patients 2 and 4 experienced seizure relapse after approximately six months. Patient 1 showed no response to OXC or lacosamide (LCM) and developed severe adverse effects (hyponatremia, hypersomnia) leading to drug withdrawal. Additional ASMs, including valproate, topiramate, levetiracetam, and a ketogenic diet, were tried in Patients 1 and 2 but failed to control seizures before gabapentin treatment (Table 1).

Despite variable responses to prior treatments, all patients exhibited profound intellectual disability with no attainment of developmental milestones. They showed poor visual fixation, lack of emotional responsiveness, and severe motor impairment, including axial hypotonia without head control and quadriplegia.

Whole‑cell patch‑clamp was used to assess the electrophysiological properties of the identified KCNQ2 variants transiently expressed in CHO cells. When KCNQ2 WT was expressed alone, a relatively fast‑activating, non‑inactivating outward current was observed in response to depolarizing voltage steps from − 90 mV to + 60 mV. As shown in Fig. 1c, the S6‑segment variants p.A294V (previously reported as a severe LoF variant 13 , p.F305I, and p.F305del produced no detectable voltage‑activated outward currents, indicating that they are severe loss‑of‑function (LoF) variants. In contrast, cells expressing the S4 variant p.L200P generated smaller Kv7.2 currents than KCNQ2 WT at voltages below + 20 mV but exhibited larger peak current densities at higher voltages, reaching 297.7 ± 48.2 pA/pF at + 60 mV, significantly different from KCNQ2 WT (117.8 ± 22.2 pA/pF, P < 0.01, Fig. 1d). This voltage‑dependent divergence suggests that p.L200P combines LoF and gain‑of‑function (GoF) effects. The steady‑state activation curve of p.L200P showed a significant depolarizing shift of 32.3 ± 2.4 mV (V1/2: −11.7 ± 1.5 mV for WT vs. 20.6 ± 1.8 mV for p.L200P, P < 0.0001, Fig. 1e). In addition, p.L200P exhibited a markedly increased activation time constant, indicating slower activation kinetics compared to KCNQ2 WT (Fig. 1f). Together, these results indicate that p.L200P produces a mixed functional alteration characterized by larger current amplitudes at strongly depolarized potentials but slower activation kinetics, rather than a pure LoF defect.

To simulate the heterozygous state, each variant was co-expressed with KCNQ2 WT (1:1; Supplementary Fig. S1). The three severe LoF S6 variants (p.A294V, p.F305del, p.F305I) all produced detectable currents at + 60 mV, with values of 65.9 ± 14.1, 130.8 ± 26.8, and 126.8 ± 25.7 pA/pF, respectively, indicating partial rescue by the WT allele. In contrast, p.L200P co-expressed with KCNQ2 WT yielded a current density of 219.4 ± 30.0 pA/pF at + 60 mV, which remained numerically higher than that of the WT control (111.1 ± 13.4 pA/pF), although the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05).

Because heteromeric Kv7.2/Kv7.3 channels assemble in vivo, we co‑expressed KCNQ2 WT or each variant with KCNQ3 WT at a 1:1 ratio. In the heteromeric setting (Fig. 2a), p.A294V produced a voltage‑activated current significantly smaller than that of KCNQ2/KCNQ3 WT (50.2 ± 8.1 pA/pF vs. 421.4 ± 50.5 pA/pF at + 60 mV, P < 0.0001), whereas p.F305I and p.F305del gave no detectable currents. These results indicate that the S6 variants exert dominant‑negative effects on the co‑expressed KCNQ3‑WT subunit, similar to previously reported pore‑domain variants¹³. In contrast, p.L200P co‑expressed with KCNQ3 retained much larger currents (344.9 ± 49.0 pA/pF vs. 421.4 ± 50.5 pA/pF at + 60 mV, P > 0.05, Fig. 2b), despite showing a depolarized V1/2 (3.8 ± 1.6 mV vs. −8.2 ± 1.1 mV for WT, P < 0.0001, Fig. 2c) and slower activation kinetics (Fig. 2d). These observations further illustrate the complex functional consequences of KCNQ2 variants located in the S4 segment.

All four patients received add‑on gabapentin. Because seizure identification solely by clinical observation was challenging, video‑EEG recordings were performed before and after gabapentin initiation to evaluate its therapeutic effect (Fig. 3). The duration of gabapentin exposure ranged from 9 months to 2 years, with doses at the last follow‑up varying between 15 mg/kg/d and 35 mg/kg/d; no adverse effects were reported (Table 1).

Fig. 3 EEG recordings of KCNQ2-DEE patients before and after gabapentin treatment. a Atypical hypsarrhythmia patterns during sleep are noticed on the patient 1’s EEG recording performed before (upper left) and after gabapentin (GBP) treatment (bottom left). One notable episode marked by bilateral upper limb elevation with tremor during wakefulness is depicted (upper right). Particularly, this severe EEG manifestation disappeared with the administration of lamotrigine (LTG) (bottom right). b At 1 year and 5 months of age, EEG assessment of patient 4 who carries the p.F305I variant, reveals multifocal sharp waves and sharp-slow wave complexes during sleep stages (upper left), along with several episodes characterized by staring and immobility (upper right). Following three months of treatment with gabapentin, epileptic discharges localize to bilateral frontal lobe, and no seizure attacks are observed during a 4-hour EEG monitoring session (bottom).

Patient 1 continued to experience frequent episodic events (epileptic spasms and tonic seizures occurring during wakefulness and sleep) and showed an atypical hypsarrhythmia pattern while on gabapentin. Subsequently, lamotrigine (LTG) was added; the hypsarrhythmia pattern resolved, and a noticeable reduction in seizure attacks during sleep was observed (Fig. 3a). Increasing gabapentin to 35 mg/kg/d did not achieve seizure remission. However, the parents reported cognitive improvements (increased auditory responsiveness and babbling) after gabapentin initiation.

Patient 2 showed a marked reduction in interictal epileptic discharges after 6 months of gabapentin and became seizure‑free after 1 year and 5 months on a low dose (17 mg/kg/d). She also developed greater environmental awareness, but motor improvement was not observed.

Patient 3 remained seizure‑free on gabapentin (15 mg/kg/d), with improved background activity and reduced interictal epileptiform discharges on EEG. During 2 years of gabapentin treatment, he showed notable advances in sleep quality, emotional regulation, and visual tracking, but motor function did not improve.

Patient 4 became rapidly seizure‑free after gabapentin introduction (15 mg/kg/d). EEG monitoring showed sharp waves and sharp‑slow waves localized to bilateral frontal lobes (Fig. 3b). At the dose of 15 mg/kg/d, improvements in cognition (head control, grasping abilities, and external responsiveness) were observed, but further dose increase to 20 mg/kg/d did not lead to additional significant progress.

In this study, we evaluated the efficacy of add‑on gabapentin in four patients with KCNQ2‑DEE carrying distinct KCNQ2 variants, and characterized the electrophysiological properties of these variants in heterologous expression systems. Our findings suggest that the variable clinical responses to gabapentin may be partially explained by the specific biophysical features of each mutation, although direct mechanistic evidence is lacking.

The S6‑segment variants (p.A294V, p.F305I, p.F305del) all exhibited severe LoF in homomeric Kv7.2 channels, consistent with the most prevalent molecular phenotype among pathogenic KCNQ2 variants in this domain 13 , 24 , 25 . When co‑expressed with KCNQ2 WT (1:1) to mimic the heterozygous state, these variants still showed markedly reduced currents, although a partial rescue was observed (Supplementary Fig. S1). In heteromeric assembly with KCNQ3 WT (1:1), they also produced drastically reduced Kv7.2/Kv7.3 currents compared to wild‑type, as previously reported 13 , 25 . It is plausible that gabapentin, as a Kv7 channel opener, may exert beneficial effects in patients with such severe LoF variants by enhancing residual M‑current.

In contrast, variants located in the S4 voltage‑sensor domain exhibit more heterogeneous functional consequences. These include pure GoF, partial LoF, severe LoF, and mixed phenotypes 14 , 26 , 27 . The p.L200P variant in patient 1 falls into the mixed category: it displayed smaller currents at low voltages, larger currents at strongly depolarized potentials, slower activation kinetics, and a depolarized shift in V₁/₂. When co‑expressed with KCNQ2 WT, p.L200P produced a current density of 219.4 ± 30.0 pA/pF at + 60 mV, which was numerically higher than that of the wild‑type control (111.1 ± 13.4 pA/pF), although the difference did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05; Supplementary Fig. S1). Moreover, in heteromeric Kv7.2/Kv7.3 channels, p.L200P retained significantly higher current than the S6 variants. These unique biophysical properties, including reduced current at near‑resting voltages, slower activation kinetics, a depolarized shift in voltage dependence, and the retention of large currents at strongly depolarized potentials, suggest that p.L200P fundamentally alters channel gating and voltage sensing. It is possible that under physiological conditions, this mixed functional phenotype results in different baseline channel activity compared to severe LoF variants. Consequently, the mutant channel may not require or respond to exogenous activation by gabapentin in the same way as channels with pure LoF defects. This could contribute to the poorer electroencephalographic response observed in patient 1. However, we emphasize that this interpretation remains speculative, as we did not directly test gabapentin’s effect on these mutant channels.

Nevertheless, the cognitive improvements for the four patients with gabapentin in the study were similar based on their parents’ observations. Surprisingly, despite the enhancement of Kv7.2 channel due to variant p.L200P to some extent, patient1 exhibited improved alertness to the environment with gabapentin treatment. Preceding studies have shown that retigabine could worsen seizures, prolong apneas, and lead to severe sedation in a patient with KCNQ2 GoF variant p.R201C 15 . Conversely, retigabine has demonstrated substantial improvements in cognition for a patient with GoF variant p.S195P, but without response after weaning it19. Actually, the underlying pathogenesis of KCNQ2‑DEE remains largely unknown, involving not only conductance properties of Kv7 subunits at different developmental stages but also complex aspects of their neuronal subcellular and brain loci expression 28– 30 . Thus, those Kv7 agonists in vivo may exert more intricate effects than noted in vitro experiments.

Given the complexity of Kv7 channel assembly and the multiple known targets of gabapentin (including the α2δ subunits of voltage‑gated calcium channels) 31 , direct extrapolation from in vitro channel data to clinical response is premature. We therefore acknowledge several important limitations. First, we did not directly test the ability of gabapentin to activate each mutant channel in vitro, as would be required to establish a mechanistic link between genotype and drug response. Second, our sample size is very small (n = 4), and the clinical observations are based on open‑label add‑on treatment without a control group. Third, the cognitive improvements reported by parents were not assessed using standardized neuropsychological instruments. Fourth, the in vivo stoichiometry of Kv7.2 and Kv7.3 subunits may differ from the fixed ratios used in our expression systems, and the random assembly of tetramers adds further uncertainty.

Despite these limitations, our study provides real‑world, hypothesis‑generating data on the use of gabapentin in KCNQ2‑DEE, a disorder with limited treatment options. The observation that patients with severe LoF S6 variants responded more favorably than the patient with a mixed S4 variant suggests that the biophysical nature of the mutation may influence clinical outcome. We hope these findings encourage further, more systematic investigations into the relationship between KCNQ2 mutation type and response to Kv7 openers. For now, gabapentin may be considered as an add‑on therapy in KCNQ2‑DEE patients, particularly those with severe LoF variants in the S6 domain, but should be used with caution, and treatment decisions must be individualized.
