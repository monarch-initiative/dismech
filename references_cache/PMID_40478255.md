# Sirolimus for Colon Polyposis in PTEN Hamartoma Tumor Syndrome.
**Authors:** Stanich PP, Gofar K, Roberts ME, Hussan H
**Journal:** Clin Transl Gastroenterol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.14309/ctg.0000000000000871](https://doi.org/10.14309/ctg.0000000000000871)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: PTEN hamartoma tumor syndrome (PHTS) is a rare condition with a high rate of colon polyposis. Sirolimus may reduce colorectal polyps in PHTS. We designed a trial to assess the safety and impact of sirolimus on colon polyp burden in PHTS.

METHODS: We performed an open-label trial of sirolimus for 1 year in adults with a PTEN pathogenic variant and colon polyposis. The primary outcome was change in polyp burden and staging.

RESULTS: Five participants were enrolled, with 2 completing the planned study course. Baseline colonoscopies showed a mean polyp burden of 107 (range 31-184), with multiple histologic types. For the 2 patients completing the study, polyp burden decreased.

DISCUSSION: Sirolimus has promise for reduction of colon polyp burden in PHTS, but side effects may limit usage. Future treatment trials in rare polyposis syndromes would benefit from the involvement of multiple centers to allow for improved recruitment.

PTEN is a tumor suppressor gene that regulates the cell cycle through the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/Akt/mTOR pathway (1). The PTEN hamartoma tumor syndrome (PHTS), also called Cowden syndrome, is a rare autosomal dominant condition with a reported prevalence of 1/200,000 to 250,000 that is caused by germline pathogenic variants in the PTEN gene (2,3). PHTS is characterized by the growth of hamartomas (disorganized growths of native cells in native tissues) and an increased risk of cancer in multiple organ systems including the colon (4,5).

Polyposis with more than 50 colon polyps is common in PHTS and has been reported in 27%–64% of patients (6,7). Outcomes of the colon manifestations of PHTS are underreported. One series reported that 38% received colectomy despite the relatively low colorectal cancer risk in comparison with other polyposis conditions (such as familial adenomatous polyposis) (8). Although there is a general sense that close endoscopic surveillance is safe, there is a report of a stage II colorectal cancer occurring during active surveillance (9).

Sirolimus, a specific inhibitor of mTOR that is FDA approved for immunosuppression, has been reported to be of clinical benefit in case reports of pediatric patients with severe PHTS manifestations (10,11). More importantly, a small pilot trial of sirolimus therapy for 56 days in patients with PHTS reported 2 of 14 subjects (14.3%) with improvement in gastrointestinal polyposis in the short treatment period, although specific details on the burden of polyps of the subjects, how the polyposis was investigated, or how improvement was determined were not provided (12). Given this, we designed and conducted a pilot trial to specifically assess the safety and impact of sirolimus on colon polyp burden in PHTS.

This study was approved by The Ohio State University Biomedical Sciences Institutional Review Board. It was in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04094675) and was open from November 2018 to June 2024.

The study was an open-label pilot trial of sirolimus 2 mg by mouth once daily for 1 year. The protocol included colonoscopy with colon polyp assessment and standard-of-care polyp resection at trial entrance and at the 1-year mark as well as frequent laboratory and health assessments. Inclusion criteria were adults aged 18 years or older with a confirmed PTEN pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant and with a burden of colon polyps that were too numerous to clear endoscopically and/or at least InSiGHT stage 1 polyposis (13). Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, refusal of subject and/or partners to use effective contraception during the study period, renal or liver disease, and history of colorectal cancer or colorectal adenoma with high-grade dysplasia.

The primary outcome of interest was change in colon polyp burden, as determined by overall count as well as by InSiGHT polyposis staging. Secondary outcomes of interest were changes in measurements of health status through the SF-36 health survey, a validated tool for assessing physical and mental health (14). The study was supported through a grant from the PTEN Research foundation, with the study drug supplied by Pfizer Inc.

Five participants entered the study, with the study terminated before fulfillment because of slow enrollment. Demographic details are in Table 1. Two of the participants completed the planned study course, while 3 subjects were withdrawn early. Withdrawals were due to side effects likely related to sirolimus for 2 subjects (pneumonitis after 6 weeks requiring withdrawal and recurrent vaginal candidiasis leading to withdrawal after 3 months) and for a likely unrelated reason in the third (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug-related gastrointestinal bleeding after 6 months on study).

Sirolimus levels remained within an acceptable range for all subjects, and biochemical parameters only showed mild fluctuations throughout the study. Given this, no adjustments to sirolimus dosing were needed.

Baseline colonoscopies showed a mean of 107 polyps (range 31–184), with InSiGHT stage 1 in 4 participants (80%) and stage 2 in 1 participant (20%). Histologic types of sampled colorectal polyps are included in Table 1. For the patients completing the study, polyp numbers decreased (Figure 1) with InSiGHT stage remaining stable. Histologic types at exit colonoscopies remained similar to initial findings with ganglioneuroma, hamartoma, lipoma, and tubular adenoma found. For the withdrawn subjects, their next clinical colonoscopies were noted to have stable InSiGHT staging.

Scores on the SF-36 across the 8 domains focused on physical and mental health did not have noticeable trends during the time on therapy. However, both subjects who completed the study course requested to remain on therapy afterward because of a general sense of improved daily quality of life and function.

Sirolimus has promise for reduction of colon polyp burden for patients with PHTS who completed 1 year of therapy. Of note, there was not a change in polyposis staging despite the reduction in total colorectal polyp burden. The clinical benefit of polyp reduction without complete response is unclear, and it remains to be proven whether this would lead to a lower colonoscopy burden or decreased utilization of colectomy. The benefit of reduction in colon polyp burden in PHTS may be more relevant than in adenomatous polyposis syndromes, however, as the number of polyps is often the feature leading to endoscopic or surgical interventions rather than advanced neoplasia. In addition, this study has the obvious limitation of a small sample. However, our experience adds to the reports of patients with PHTS showing reduction in polyp burden with sirolimus therapy and supports continued investigation.

Sirolimus has the potential for significant side effects including frequent laboratory abnormalities and risk of infections and interstitial pneumonitis (12). During our study, we had 2 subjects with significant side effects likely related to the therapy. It is clear that sirolimus will have side effects that limit usage for some patients, and this needs to be clearly communicated to future participants in trials of sirolimus and other mTOR inhibitors.

We were not able to meet the study recruitment goals. This is likely for 2 predominant reasons. The COVID-19 pandemic occurred shortly after study opening and hampered recruitment. In addition, the rarity of PHTS limited the potential participant pool and we had lower uptake of study from qualifying participants than expected. This has also been an issue in other studies assessing potential therapies for rare polyposis syndromes (15). Future treatment trials in rare polyposis syndromes would definitely benefit from the involvement of multiple centers with a broad geographic reach. This should allow for a larger study to further assess the impact of sirolimus on polyposis and potentially establish predictors which patients will tolerate and benefit most from the therapy.

In summary, sirolimus has promise for reduction of colorectal polyp burden in PHTS, but side effects may limit tolerance of long-term usage. Future treatment trials in rare polyposis syndromes would benefit from the involvement of multiple centers to allow for improved recruitment.
