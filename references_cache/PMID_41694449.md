# Preventing Cushing Syndrome: Adrenalectomy in PDE11A-Positive Primary Pigmented Nodular Adrenocortical Disease.
**Authors:** Carling T, LaRue M, Lenz AM, Kalik A, Faucz FR, Stratakis CA
**Journal:** JCEM Case Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1210/jcemcr/luaf329](https://doi.org/10.1210/jcemcr/luaf329)

## Content

A 13.5-year-old girl presented with primary amenorrhea, growth plateau, and osteopenia, without overt stigmata of Cushing syndrome. Family history included inherited primary pigmented nodular adrenocortical disease (PPNAD) due to a PDE11A pathogenic variant (c.919C>T, p.R307X), with her mother having undergone bilateral open adrenalectomy at age 19 for severe Cushing syndrome with fragility hip fractures. Biochemical evaluation showed the typical PPNAD paradoxical cortisol rise following an 8-mg dexamethasone suppression test to 15.6 µg/dL (SI: 364 nmol/L), suppressed adrenocorticotropin, mild late night salivary cortisol elevation, and normal urinary free cortisol, indicating mild adrenal hypercortisolism (AHC). Genetic testing confirmed the PDE11A variant. Imaging via adrenal computed tomography was ostensibly normal. To confirm PPNAD and prevent progression to Cushing syndrome, initial unilateral total right mini back scope adrenalectomy (MBSA) was performed, revealing characteristic pigmented nodules (2-5 mm) on histopathology. Completion left total MBSA followed 3 months later and both operations were performed in under 20 minutes. Postoperatively, the patient recovered rapidly, resumed some linear growth and pubertal progression, and is doing well on glucocorticoid replacement. This case highlights the role of early intervention with MBSA to prevent overt Cushing syndrome in germline PDE11A-mutant PPNAD, contrasting the delayed intervention in prior generations.

Primary pigmented nodular adrenocortical disease (PPNAD) is a rare cause of adrenocorticotropin (ACTH)-independent Cushing syndrome, often linked to germline mutations in genes regulating cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) signaling, such as PRKAR1A in Carney complex or PDE11A [1-3]. PDE11A pathogenic variants disrupt phosphodiesterase 11A, impairing cAMP hydrolysis, leading to elevated intracellular cAMP, protein kinase A (PKA) activation, and micronodular adrenocortical disease. The incidence is low, with variable penetrance, but untreated PPNAD can progress to severe adrenal hypercortisolism (AHC; Cushing syndrome), causing growth impairment, diabetes, hypertension, psychiatric disease, significant myopathy, osteoporosis, and fractures with a high mortality rate [4, 5]. Traditional management involves bilateral adrenalectomy for overt disease, sometimes via open approaches, with lifelong glucocorticoid replacement therapy [6]. Early intervention in asymptomatic or mildly affected carriers remains controversial due to perceived risks of surgery, but advances in minimally invasive surgery, like mini back scope adrenalectomy (MBSA), offer very low-risk options [7]. We report early surgical intervention with bilateral MBSA in a pediatric patient with a PDE11A pathogenic variant with mild AHC (aka mild autonomous cortisol secretion due to PPNAD, preventing progression, and contrast it with her mother's open adrenalectomy. This case emphasizes the value of genetic screening, early detection, and individualized surgery in inherited adrenocortical tumor disorders, contributing to evolving paradigms in preemptive endocrine care [8, 9].

The patient, a 13.5-year-old girl, presented to her pediatric endocrinologist and was subsequently referred to the Carling Adrenal Center with concerns of nonprogressive puberty and growth retardation. Bone age was 13 years at chronological age 13 years, 5 months. She reported primary amenorrhea (thelarche at age 10 years), growth plateau (height 147 cm, weight 43.9 kg, body mass index of 20.2), and mild fatigue, but no classic features of Cushing syndrome like moon face, buffalo hump, or striae. Family history was significant: Her mother had PPNAD diagnosed at age 19 with severe Cushing syndrome, including fragility hip fractures, requiring bilateral open posterior adrenalectomy in 1998 with 12th rib resections and 1-month recovery. The pathogenic variant was inherited via the maternal grandmother who similarly developed severe Cushing syndrome and had been managed with bilateral open total adrenalectomy, and their cases were previously reported [1]. The patient was otherwise asymptomatic but dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry revealed osteopenia (Z-scores −1.1 to −1.3 at lumbar spine, femoral neck, and total hip). There were no prior surgeries, medications, or contributory family history beyond PPNAD. Physical examination showed normal blood pressure (110/70 mm Hg), heart rate 78 bpm, and no endocrine stigmata.

Endocrine evaluation revealed a paradoxical cortisol increase after high-dose dexamethasone, a hallmark of PPNAD: cortisol rose from a baseline level of 13.2 µg/dL (SI: 364 nmol/L) to 15.6 µg/dL (SI: 430 nmol/L) on 8-mg overnight dexamethasone testing [1, 2]. ACTH was suppressed (7-8 pg/mL; SI: 1.5-1.8 pmol/L; reference range, 7-63 pg/mL; SI: 1.5-13.8 pmol/L), 24-hour urinary free cortisol normal (43.7 µg/24 hours; SI: 120 nmol/day; reference range, <45 µg/24 hours; SI: <138 nmol/day), and late-night salivary cortisol mildly elevated (0.09-0.12 µg/dL; SI: 2.5-3.3 nmol/L; reference range, <0.09 µg/dL; SI: <2.5 nmol/L), consistent with mild AHC [4]. Luteinizing hormone (LH; <0.02 mIU/mL; SI: <0.02 IU/L) and estradiol (<15 pg/mL; SI: <55 pmol/L) were also suppressed. Routine laboratory evaluation was otherwise normal. Genetic testing (Fulgent Therapeutics LLC) confirmed the heterozygous PDE11A pathogenic variant (c.919C>T, p.R307X), truncating the catalytic domain and matching her mother's. Notably, initial genetic testing was reported as negative because the pathogenic variant calling algorithm classified the PDE11A variant as likely benign, resulting in its omission from the report. The heterozygous PDE11A pathogenic variant was correctly identified only after requesting the vendor to reanalyze the data, incorporating the specific variant from the mother and grandmother. Adrenal computed tomography (CT) (modified protocol) showed grossly normal glands without nodules (Fig. 1A-1D). Given the variable penetrance and subtle clinical presentation, staged bilateral MBSA was planned, right side first for histopathological confirmation and therapeutic intent to prevent progression followed by the left side dependent on response [7, 9].

After detailed multidisciplinary discussion with the patient (who provided assent) and her family, staged bilateral total MBSA was planned under general anesthesia in the prone position using a posterior retroperitoneoscopic approach. This minimally invasive technique, distinct from transabdominal laparoscopic adrenalectomy, avoids peritoneal entry, uses three 5- to 12-mm incisions, direct retroperitoneal access, results in less pain, faster recovery, superior cosmesis, and can frequently be completed in less than 20 minutes by experienced surgeons [7, 10]. Accordingly, the patient initially underwent unilateral total right MBSA, completed in 18 minutes with no complications. Intraoperatively, the adrenal gland appeared normal-sized but displayed dispersed pigmented nodules (2-5 mm), confirmed on gross pathology (Fig. 1E and 1F). Histopathology showed adrenocortical hyperplasia with pigmented micronodules without atypia [5, 11] (Fig. 2A-2C). Three months later, total left MBSA was performed, resecting all adrenal tissue bilaterally. Bilateral partial adrenalectomy (leaving 30% of one gland) was considered for function preservation but rejected due to the genetic predisposition, likely high recurrence risk, and severe Cushing syndrome at an early age both in the mother and grandmother. The decision was also informed by her mother's more than 25-year excellent control on glucocorticoid replacement [6]. The patient was discharged from the hospital within 24 hours after each operation.

Histopathology confirmed PPNAD. In the context of the germline PDE11A variant, these findings are consistent with PDE11A-related micronodular adrenal disease, without evidence of atypia or malignancy. Postoperatively, the patient was managed with glucocorticoid replacement with hydrocortisone 5-5-2.5 mg every 8 hours (8 mg/m²/day) and fludrocortisone 0.1 mg daily, which are well tolerated, with no adrenal crises or evidence of glucocorticoid withdrawal symptoms. Compared to her mother's bilateral 18-cm incisions from an open lateral approach involving rib resections and prolonged healing over a month, the recovery after bilateral MBSA was rapid with excellent cosmetic outcomes (Fig. 2D). The patient resumed linear growth and had progressive puberty development postoperatively with return of pubertal levels of LH and estradiol.

PPNAD, often familial, arises from dysregulated cAMP-PKA signaling, with PDE11A mutations impairing cAMP degradation and promoting nodular tumor proliferation [1, 12]. The penetrance varies, but AHC can cause partly irreversible damage like moon facies, buffalo hump, muscle wasting, severe hypertension, diabetes, fragility fractures, and psychosis, with a significantly elevated mortality rate mainly due to cardiovascular, thromboembolic, and infectious complications [4, 5]. The diagnosis relies on biochemical evaluation demonstrating autonomous AHC and genetics; imaging may be normal, as here, necessitating surgical confirmation [1, 2, 8, 9]. Early intervention and proactive adrenalectomy is debated but justified in patients with known pathogenic variants with mild AHC preventing overt Cushing syndrome [6, 13]. One large family with PPNAD due to a PRKAR1A pathogenic variant followed for 40 years has provided insights into the natural history of PPNAD and various treatment options using unilateral adrenalectomy in 4 and bilateral adrenalectomy in 2 patients, with the earliest age of initial adrenalectomy at age 8 years [6]. Including the present family, a recent systematic review details the known 210 PPNAD cases reported to date and include a total of now 41 patients with a PDE11A pathogenic variant [14].

The decision to proceed with staged adrenalectomy was based on a careful balance of risks and benefits. The history of severe disease at a young age both in the mother and grandmother highlighted the high likelihood of progression to overt Cushing syndrome. In our patient, growth plateau, delayed pubertal development, and reduced bone density already suggested early morbidity despite only mild biochemical changes. In this context, and after detailed discussion with the patient and her family, surgery was selected as a preventive strategy, recognizing the lifelong requirement for adrenal replacement but prioritizing avoidance of irreversible complications of hypercortisolism, such as cardiovascular, skeletal, and metabolic damage [13]. It is noteworthy that the genetic testing was initially reported as negative, highlighting the importance of evaluating germline variants in the context of pathogenic variants identified in other family members. The MBSA minimizes morbidity, pain, recovery time, and is associated with excellent cosmetic outcomes [7, 15]. Function-preserving (partial) adrenalectomy may reduce glucocorticoid replacement needs but risks recurrence in patients with a genetic predisposition and thus was avoided in the present case [7, 10]. The limitations of the study include the small sample size and short follow-up; long-term data are needed to ensure great control with glucocorticoid replacement. This case advances preemptive strategies with early surgical intervention akin to other inherited endocrine tumor susceptibility syndromes, such as prophylactic thyroidectomy in those with RET gene pathogenic variants [16]. To the best of our knowledge, this case represents the first reported use of early intervention with adrenalectomy to prevent Cushing syndrome in germline PDE11A-related PPNAD with mild AHC.


PDE11A pathogenic variants in PPNAD cause variable AHC, and early genetic screening identifies at-risk individuals for improved management.

A paradoxical increase in cortisol after dexamethasone, suppressed ACTH, and genetic testing diagnose PPNAD, even with mild AHC and ostensibly normal imaging.

Early intervention with minimally invasive adrenalectomy prevents progression in patients with PDE11A pathogenic variants, offering reduced morbidity over traditional approaches with delayed treatment until overt Cushing syndrome.
