Since its initial description by Jervell and Lange‐Nielsen (1957), the congenital long QT syndrome (LQTS) has been the most investigated cardiac ion channelopathy. A prolonged QT interval on the surface electrocardiogram is the sine qua non of the LQTS and is a surrogate measure of the ventricular action potential duration (APD). Congenital as well as acquired alterations in certain cardiac ion channels can affect their currents in such a way as to increase the APD and hence the QT interval. The inhomogeneous lengthening of the APD across the ventricular wall results in dispersion of APD, that is, of ventricular repolarization (VR). This, together with the tendency of prolonged APD to be associated with oscillations at the plateau level, termed early afterdepolarizations (EADs), provides the substrate of ventricular tachyarrhythmia (VT) associated with LQTS, usually referred to as torsade de pointes (TdP) VT (El‐Sherif & Turitto,1999). The term was first coined by Dessertenne (1966), who described its ECG pattern of continuously changing morphology of the QRS complexes that seem to twist around an imaginary baseline. The quasi‐musical term and the intriguing ECG pattern have caught the attention of electrophysiologists for years and have been, to some extent, a driving force behind the recent focused interest into the role of genetics and cardiac ion channel pathophysiology in cardiac arrhythmias (El‐Sherif & Turitto,1999).

We will review the genetic, molecular, and phenotype characteristics of congenital LQTS, as well as current management strategies and future directions in the field.

It has long been known that tachycardia‐dependent T‐wave alternans (TWA) occurs in patients with the congenital form of the LQTS and may presage the onset of TdP (Figure1). In analysis of 1,103 LQTS patients with QTc interval >0.44 s from the International LQTS Registry, TWA was recorded in 30 patients (Moss,1995). The frequency of occurrence of TWA was directly proportional to the length of the QTc interval on the enrollment ECG. TWA occurred in one or more occasions during an average 4‐year follow−up in 21% of the patients with QTc >0.60 s, but in <0.2% of the patients with QTc <0.50 s. Patients with advanced forms of TWA (those with bidirectional beat‐to−beat changes in T‐wave polarity;n= 21) were younger, had longer QTc values, had a higher incidence of complex VT, and were more likely to experience a cardiac event (syncope or cardiac arrest) than those with less advanced forms of TWA (those without bidirectional beat‐to‐beat changes in T‐wave polarity;n= 9).

Although overt TWA in the ECG is not common, in recent years, digital signal processing techniques have made it possible to detect subtle degrees of TWA. This suggests that the phenomenon may be more prevalent than previously recognized and may represent an important marker of vulnerability to VT (Verrier et al.,2011). Interest in TWA is attributed to the hypothesis that it reflects a greater degree of underlying DR (Chinushi et al.,1998).

The ventricular myocardial action potential changes are based on the affected ion channels along with the distributive pattern of channels in the layers of the myocardium. Multiple ST‐segment and T‐wave morphologies associated with a particular genotype have been delineated (Zareba,2006). Due to the small available sample sizes, this association has been restricted to the most common genotypes (LQT1, LQT2, and LQT3). Presence of broad‐based T waves in LQT1, low‐amplitude T waves in LQT2, and late peaking T waves in LQT3 was initially demonstrated by Moss et al. (1995). This association was demonstrated in vitro based on models affecting particular ion channels (Antzelevitch,2003). Further delineation of the T‐wave morphology was done by Zhang et al. (2000) using 284 LQTS carriers, demonstrating 10 typical patterns (4 LQT1, 4 LQT2, and 2 LQT3). In this study, LQT1 carriers demonstrated a normal or broad‐based T‐wave pattern consistent with the homogenous action potential prolongation caused by affecting IKr. LQT2 carriers had a bifid T‐wave pattern, thought to be a result of increased transmural dispersion due to greater increase in APD in the M cells of the myocardium compared to the endocardium or the epicardium. Due to the delay in opening of the sodium channels and hence phase 2 of the action potential, LQT3 patients had a late‐peaked T wave (Figure2).

To date, mutations of 16 different genes have been identified in patients clinically diagnosed with LQTS. Since approximately 20% of LQTS eludes genetic classification, this list is expected to grow. Loss of function of one of several potassium (K+) channels, gain of function of the sodium (Na+) and calcium (Ca2+) channels are the three abnormalities that can result in prolongation of APD and LQTS. The three channels' abnormal behavior can be traced to either gene mutations involving the channel protein itself, or mutations of membrane structural proteins that can indirectly affect the function of the channel (El‐Sherif & Boutjdir,2015). LQT1, LQT2, and LQT3 account for an estimated 75%–85% of genotype‐positive LQTS cases, while minor LQT syndromes account for less than 5%. LQT1 is due to loss of function of the KCNQ1 that encodes for IKs(Barhanin et al.,1996; Sanguinetti et al.,1996). LQT2 is due to loss of function of KCNH2 channel that encodes for IKr(Sanguinetti et al.,1995), while LQT3 is due to gain of function of SCN5A α subunit of the Na channel that encodes for INa(Wang et al.,1995).

Homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations of KCNQ1 or KCNE1 occurring in recessive form underlie the relatively rare Jervell and Lange‐Nielsen syndrome (JLNS) (Duggal et al.,1998; Splawski et al.,1997).

Some LQTS types are associated with somatic and neurological changes in addition to prolongation of the QT interval. Loss of function of the KCNJ2 gene which encodes the inward rectifier K channel Kir2.1 (IK1) results in a syndrome termed Andersen‐Tawil syndrome or LQT7. In addition to a long QT interval, periodic paralysis and dysmorphic features are present (Andersen, Krasilnikoff, & Overvad,1971; Tawil et al.,1994). Similarly, gain of function of mutations in the CA CNA1C gene which encodes the L‐Type Ca channel Cav1.2 leads to immunodeficiency and autism in addition to prolonged QT interval and is termed Timothy syndrome or LQT8 (Splawski et al.,2004). Jervell and Lange‐Nielsen syndrome results in severe congenital bilateral sensorineural hearing loss in addition to marked QT interval prolongation with very high risk of cardiac events (Splawski et al.,1997).

Examples of mutations of structural proteins that affect the function of ion channels are LQT4 due to mutations of ankyrin gene encoding the anchoring β subunit ANK2 that leads to intracellular Ca overload (Mohler et al.,2003). LQT9 due to mutations of CAV3 which encodes caveolin‐3 that alters the biophysical properties of the Na channel (Vatta et al.,2006), and LQT 11 due to mutations of an A‐kinase‐anchoring protein (AKAP9‐Yotiao) that binds to KCNQ1, responsible for the most common LQT1 syndrome, and depresses the function of the channel by reducing the c‐AMP‐induced phosphorylation (Chen et al.,2007).

Sympathetic activation caused by conditions such as physical exercise triggers the activation of IKs which results in shorter ventricular repolarization at fast heart rates, which prevents afterdepolarizations caused by β1‐adrenergic stimulation. Patients with LQT1 who have diminished IKs are prone to develop prolonged ventricular repolarization during exercise, whereas patients with LQT2 and LQT3 who have intact IKs are relatively protected against this phenomenon. This failure of the QT interval to adapt at faster heart rates results in a high risk for afterdepolarization which can culminate in TdP. In a study of 670 asymptomatic LQTS patients, Schwartz et al. (2001) demonstrated that the majority of LQT1 patient's lethal and nonlethal cardiac events were triggered by exercise or stress, especially swimming. LQT2 patients are exquisitely sensitive to sudden auditory stimuli which is thought to mimic a fright response resulting in an abrupt release of local catecholamines leading to early afterdepolarizations and resultant TdP (Schwartz et al.,2001; Wilde et al.,1999).

Women with LQT2 are also at higher risk for cardiac events in the postpartum period. This is thought to be multifactorial which likely includes hormonal changes, sleep disruption, and the sudden auditory stimuli caused by the crying neonate (Khositseth et al.,2004). In contrast, LQT3 patients seem to be more prone for cardiac events during sleep; this is hypothesized to be secondary to amplification of the inward flow of Na+ at low heart rate when time spent at a negative potential is increased (Schwartz et al.,2001; Stramba‐Badiale et al.,2000).

In the last several years it became obvious that LQTS shows incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity. In other words, different clinical phenotypes (sudden cardiac death, syncope, asymptomatic) are observed in family members carrying the same familial mutation which may suggest the influence of multiple genetic and environmental factors affecting the pathophysiology of LQTS (Mizusawa, Horie, & Wilde,2014). The discovery of common genetic variants that explain the heritable component of the human ECG, including the QT interval, generated the hypothesis that genetic modifiers may account for phenotypical variability in LQTS. In LQTS, variants in the coding regions of LQTS‐related genes such as KCNE1 D85N (Nishio et al.,2009), KCNH2 K897T (Crotti et al.,2005), and SCNA5A H558R (Makielski et al.,2003) are known to influence the QT interval. Single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in noncoding regions (intron, 3′ UTR) of KCNQ1 are also reported as an important QT interval modifier (Duchatelet et al.,2013). Genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) have revealed SNPs in NOS1AP as modulator of QT interval in LQTS patients (Tomas et al.,2010), as well as in the general population (Arking et al.,2006). However, despite the fact that multiple SNPs have been linked to QT interval duration, the clinical applications of any findings are limited by the small effect sizes conferred by single SNPs and incomplete knowledge of their functional consequences. Nevertheless, there is continuing effort to understand the complexity of genotype–phenotype relationship in LQTS.

Both genotypic (e.g., LQTS genetic, intragenic, and mutation‐specific subtype) and phenotypic (e.g., gender, QTc, and history of cardiac events) characteristics are used to guide the risk stratification, and ultimately the clinical management, of patients with congenital LQTS (Figure4) (Giudicessi & Ackerman,2013). Those patients who harbor LQT1‐causative mutations on >1 KCNQ1 allele (e.g., JLNS and AR‐LQT1) (Goldenberg et al.,2006; Schwartz et al.,2006) have suffered ≥10 cardiac events before the age of 18 (Sauer et al.,2007) or have Timothy syndrome are at highest risk (≥ 80%) of suffering from one or more LQTS‐associated cardiac events before the age of 40, including a high rate of sudden cardiac arrest/death. Those patients require aggressive clinical management, which often involves more invasive approaches such as left cardiac sympathetic denervation (LCSD) and/or use of the implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD).

Similarly, individuals with a QTc ≥550 ms regardless of LQTS genotype (Sauer et al.,2007), a QTc ≥500 ms with an LQT1, LQT2, or males with an LQT3 genotype (Priori et al.,2003), non‐JLNS patients with LQTS‐causative mutations on >1 major LQTS susceptibility allele (e.g., compound heterozygosity or digenic heterozygosity) (Mullally et al.,2013), or individuals who have suffered ≥2 but <10 cardiac events before the age of 18 (Sauer et al.,2007) are at higher risk (≥50%) for suffering an LQTS‐associated cardiac event(s) before the age of 40 and often require a combination of medical, surgical, or device‐related management.

Those individuals with a QTc between 500 and 549 ms regardless of genotype, females with major genotype‐positive LQTS, male LQT3 patients with a QTc <500 ms, and any individual who has experienced <2 cardiac events before the age 18 are at intermediate risk for experiencing an LQTS‐associated cardiac event(s) before the age of 40 and require some form of treatment, typically β‐adrenergic blockers (Priori et al.,2003; Sauer et al.,2007).

Beta‐blocker therapy is the first choice for LQTS. It dramatically decreases event rates from 0.97 to 0.31 events per patient per year (Moss et al.,2000). In addition to beta‐blockers, there are a large number of adjuvant drugs that are tailored for specific genetic abnormality (El‐Sherif & Boutjdir,2015). Patient who fails pharmacotherapy, patients with history of resuscitated cardiac arrest, and patients with J‐LNS or Timothy syndrome are at particularly high risk and require other management modalities that include left cardiac sympathetic denervation (LCSD) or the ICD. Besides medical treatment, life style modification is important, as well as meticulous avoidance of other drugs that can prolong the QT interval.

Beta‐blockers are the main drugs of choice for LQTS irrespective of the genotype (Moss et al.,2000; Sauer et al.,2007). However, clinical data from the International Registry of LQTS found that beta‐blockers are more effective in preventing arrhythmias and SCD in LQT1 patients (81%) compared to LQT2 patients (59%) and LQT3 patients (50%) (Schwartz et al.,2001). This is due to the fact that arrhythmias in LQT1 patients are more frequently triggered by enhanced sympathetic stimulation. Despite beta‐blockers' efficacy in LQTS, a minority of patients remain symptomatic and in those patients additional therapeutic options like LCSD has been advocated (Schwartz et al.,2004). Alternatively, those patients may be candidates for the ICD. Beta‐blockers may be harmful in some patients with LQT3 if the heart rate is significantly slowed since bradycardia‐dependent arrhythmias are more common in LQT3 patients.

Congenital LQTS in adults requires special risk stratification. A report from the International LQTS Registry has shown that the severity of LQTS in adulthood can be risk stratified with information regarding genotype, gender, QT duration, and history of cardiac events. Beta‐blockers effectively reduce but do not eliminate the risk of life‐threatening cardiac events in congenital LQTS in adults (Sauer et al.,2007).

In a study including 382 LQT1 and LQT2 patients, it was shown that not all beta‐blockers are equally effective (Chockalingam et al.,2012). In this regard, propranolol seems to be the most effective drug from this class, due to Na channel blocking action with limited effects on cardiac K channels. However, not only different properties on the various channels may influence the effectiveness of the beta‐blocker therapy, the precise location of a mutation in the underlining gene and the specific type of resultant amino acid exchange has an impact on the effectiveness of the beta‐blocker therapy. For example, LQT1 patients with a missense mutation in a specific cytoplasmic loop region seem to benefit more from beta‐blocker therapy than patients with other mutations (Barsheshet et al.,2012).

Although LQT2 patients experience more life‐threatening events under beta‐blocker therapy when compared with LQT1 patients, it still remains the first choice of therapy. Analyzing 204 cardiac events of 634 genetically confirmed LQT2 patients from the US cohort of the International LQTS Registry, beta‐blocker therapy was associated with a pronounced reduction of exercise‐triggered events, but had a nonsignificant effect on the risk of arousal and nonexercise/nonarousal events (Kim et al.,2010). Beside beta‐blockers, Na channel blockers such as mexiletine may also have a positive effect on the recurrence of cardiac events in LQT2 patients. In a small study of four LQT2 patients, recurrent syncope and polymorphic VT occurred, although they received beta‐blocker therapy at full dose. An additional therapy with mexiletine resulted into a significant reduction in polymorphic VT and no syncope episodes occurred during follow‐up (Ildarova et al.,2012).

Since LQT3 syndrome is caused by gain of function due to delayed Na channel inactivation resulting in increased Na current during the plateau phase of AP, it comes to reason that Na channel blockers may be beneficial in LQT3 patients. Schwartz et al. (1995) demonstrated that a single oral dose of the Na channel blocker, mexiletine, administered to seven LQT3 patients with the AKPQ deletion produced significant shortening of the QTc interval within 4 hr. Similar QTc shortening in LQT3 patients with the AKPQ deletion has been reported with lidocaine and tocainide (Rosero et al.,1997). Benhorin et al. (2000) reported QT shortening with flecainide in LQT3 patients with the D1790G mutation involving the SCN5A gene. Windle et al. (2001) showed normalization of the QTc interval with low doses of oral flecainide administered over 48 hr in five patients with the AKPQ deletion. In vitro studies have shown that flecainide has high affinity for the Na channel protein and provides almost complete correction of the impaired inactivation associated with the AKPQ deletion (Nagatomo et al.,2000). Moss et al. (2005) have shown that chronic low‐dose flecainide significantly shortens the QTc interval in LQT3 subjects with the AKPQ mutation with no major adverse drug effects, but the sample size was not large enough to evaluate the safety of flecainide therapy in patients with this mutation.

Several case reports describe that phenytoin also has antiarrhythmic effects in LQT3 patients (Vukmir & Stein,1991). Phenytoin is thought to restore normal membrane responsiveness, to decrease basic sympathetic activity, and to increase atrioventricular conduction velocity and ventricular automaticity.

The Na channel inhibitor ranolazine was also shown to shorten the QT‐interval effectively in five LQT3 patients (Moss et al.,2008). The underlying mechanism is the inhibition of late component of Na currents (predisposing indirectly to intracellular Ca overload) with only minimal effects on peak Na current during upstroke of the action potential.

In patients with ATS (LQT7), beta‐blockers are often prescribed, although the International ATS Registry showed in most cases that the frequency of ventricular ectopy does not change. A single case report indicated the efficacy of verapamil (by blocking voltage‐dependent Ca channels) suppressing bidirectional ventricular tachycardia in a patient with ATS (Tristani‐Firouzi & Etheridge,2010). There is more convincing evidence of the efficacy of flecainide in ATS patients (Bokenkamp et al.,2007). The underling antiarrhythmic action may result from flecainide's activation of IK1as observed in ventricular myocytes (Caballero et al.,2010). However, in another case report, flecainide was ineffective in a patient with genetically confirmed ATS, but after administration of amiodarone ventricular tachycardia was suppressed (Junker et al.,2002). In some cases, the substitution of K and thereby increasing the serum K level to a high normal value may reduce the occurrence of ventricular arrhythmias and/or neurological features such as the episodic attacks of periodic paralysis (Sansone & Tawil,2007).

Timothy syndrome (LQT8) is associated with a high mortality rate, therefore, if the diagnosis is confirmed, ICD implantation is the most important therapeutic approach. Although there are no specific data concerning antiarrhythmic therapy, beta‐blockers are used on the basis that they are generally effective in LQTS patients. However, mexiletine (Krause et al.,2011), verapamil (Jacobs et al.,2006), and ranolazine (Shah et al.,2012) have been proposed to shorten repolarization by directly or indirectly suppressing the L‐type Ca channels and reduce the risk and burden of ventricular arrhythmias and ICD shocks.

Currently 5 of 15 LQTS are caused by mutations that result in reduction of net K current. Compton et al. (1996) showed that administration of oral K to raise plasma concentration can reduce the QTc interval in patients with LQT2. However, the long‐term clinical efficacy of this approach is not clear. On the other hand, several experimental studies have shown the efficacy of K channel openers like nicorandil (Shimizu et al.,1998) and novel HERG current enhancers like RPR260243 (Kang et al.,2005) to shorten the QT interval in LQT1. The future role of this category of drugs in the clinical management of LQTS remains to be determined.

Transport of ion channel proteins through the secretory pathway involves a selective mechanism in which cargo molecules are concentrated into carrier vesicles. The importance of protein trafficking abnormalities is the recognition that this can be corrected. An early example was the observation that culturing cells expressing the key trafficking‐deficient cystic fibrosis mutation AF 508 at reduced temperature could restore the cellular processing of CFTR protein and Cl transport (Lenhart et al.,2007). This has resulted in an intense search for chemical chaperones that can act as ligands to cause pharmacological correction of trafficking‐deficient mutations. It is estimated that as little as a 10%–20% improvement in protein trafficking would ameliorate most of the clinical syndrome. Compared with a monogenetic defect in cystic fibrosis (1:2000) that occurs at a frequency similar to that in LQTS (1:2500), LQTS is a multigenetic syndrome, and probably because of the complexity of mechanisms and defects, industry interest in LQTS mechanism‐specific drug development has been limited (Lenhart et al.,2007). However, recent studies have indicated that channel trafficking defects are common in LQT2 (Anderson et al.,2006), and therefore, pharmacological agents like thapsigargin (Delisle et al.,2003), or other trafficking rescue approaches are promising therapeutic rationales.

LCSD is a surgical procedure to denervate cardiac sympathetic innervation to the heart. It is indicated as an adjuvant therapy in patients who are refractory to beta‐blocker therapy. LCSD has been shown to significantly reduce cardiac events (Schwartz et al.,2004). In patients with history of syncope, post‐LCSD QTc <500 ms predicts efficacy of the procedure. However, those with persistent QTc prolongation (>500 ms) have a higher chance of SCD and are candidate for the ICD.

The ICD is indicated in patients who have frequent syncopal episodes despite of maximal pharmacotherapy and in those at high risk of recurrent cardiac arrest, for example, symptomatic infants <1 year of age or patients with JLNS.

The susceptibility to acquired QT interval prolongation can be influenced by genetic variation (Niemeijer et al.,2015). This is supported by the fact that the heritability of QT interval duration in the general population (excluding congenital LQTS patients) is estimated to be around 35% (Mahida et al.,2013). Furthermore, first‐degree relatives of patients with congenital LQTS have a higher risk of drug‐induced QT prolongation than nonrelated individuals (Newton‐Cheh et al.,2005). In GWAS, a large number of genes associated with QT interval duration have been identified (Kannankeril et al.,2005). The gene with the strongest signal related to QT interval duration is the nitric oxide synthase 1 adaptor protein gene (NOS1AP), located on chromosome 1 (Kannankeril et al.,2005), which inhibits L‐type calcium channel and influences impulse propagation. Other finding from GWAS included polymorphisms within genes known to be mutated in congenital LQTS, genes associated with intracellular calcium handling, as well as genes previously not known to influence cardiac repolarization (Pfeufer et al.,2009). A recent study that compared 188 patients with drug‐induced LQTS and more than 1,000 patients with congenital LQTS found disease causing mutations in 28% of patient with drug‐induced LQTS. Of interest, under basal conditions, the QTc of drug‐induced patients (453 ± 39 ms) was significantly longer than that of control subjects (406 ± 26 ms) (Niemeijer et al.,2015; Ito et al.,2016).

There is increased recognition that a sizable minority of patients who are susceptible to VT and SCD, and who are usually young and have structurally normal heart have genetically inherited abnormality of the cardiac ion channels. This recognition has heightened interest in the diagnosis and management of this group of patients. Of all malignant channelopathies, the LQTS is the most widely investigated. Although some of the patients may receive the ICD, pharmacotherapy remains the first line of management for the majority of patients. Looking ahead into the future, the role played by modifier genes in determining the phenotype along with studies on induced pluripotent stem cells (Savla et al.,2014) for treatment strategies will continue to enhance our understanding and help optimal management of those patients.
