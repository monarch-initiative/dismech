# Safety, effectiveness, and skin immune response in a controlled human infection model of sand fly transmitted cutaneous leishmaniasis
**Authors:** Vivak Parkash, Helen Ashwin, Shoumit Dey, Jovana Sadlova, Barbora Vojtkova, Katrien Van Bocxlaer, Rebecca Wiggins, David Thompson, Nidhi Sharma Dey, Charles L. Jaffe, Eli Schwartz, Petr Volf, Charles J. N. Lacey, Alison M. Layton, Paul M. Kaye
**DOI:** [10.1101/2024.04.12.24305492](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.04.12.24305492)

Abstract

                  The leishmaniases are globally important parasitic diseases for which no human vaccines are currently available. To facilitate vaccine development, we conducted an open label observational study to establish a controlled human infection model of sand fly-transmitted cutaneous leishmaniasis caused by
                  L. major
                  . Between 24
                  th
                  January and 12
                  th
                  August 2022, we exposed 14 (8F, 6M) participants to infected
                  Phlebotomus duboscqi
                  . The primary objective was to demonstrate effectiveness (take rate) and safety (absence of CL lesion at 12 months), whereas secondary and exploratory objectives included rate of lesion development, parasite load and analysis of local immune responses by immunohistology and spatial transcriptomics. We estimated an overall take rate for CL development of 64% (9/14), or 82% (9/11) if calculated using only participants having confirmed bites following exposure. Lesion development was terminated by therapeutic biopsy in 10 participants with confirmed bites. 2/10 had one and 1/10 had two lesion recurrences 4-8 months after biopsy that were treated successfully with cryotherapy. No severe or serious adverse events were recorded, but scarring was evident as expected. All participants were lesion-free at >12 month follow up. We provide the first comprehensive map of immune cell distribution and cytokine/chemokine expression in human CL lesions, revealing discrete immune niches. This controlled human infection model offers opportunities for rapid vaccine candidate selection and a greater understanding of immune-mediated protection and pathology.

Introduction 60 
The leishmaniases are vector-borne diseases transmitted by phlebotomine sand flies 1 and 61 
have a global impact on health and well-being 2.  Several species of Leishmania infect 62 
humans, causing a diverse spectrum of tegumentary and systemic diseases 1.  Cutaneous 63

autochthonous cases were reported in 2020, though this is widely regarded as a significant 65

transmission, but clinical outcome is typically dependent on parasite species.  Lesions due to 67 
L. major commonly develop and then self-resolve over a period of months and / or respond 68 
well to topical therapy. The resulting scar may, however, have lifelong impact on well-being 69

have metastatic potential (e.g. L. braziliensis, L. guyanensis) leading to mucocutaneous 72

leishmaniasis (e.g. L. aethiopica).  Two species, L. donovani and L. infantum, typically 74 
disseminate from the primary inoculation site to cause the systemic and life-threatening 75 
disease visceral leishmaniasis (VL), endemic in 79 countries. 12,739 cases of VL were 76 
reported to WHO in 2020, a significant decline on past estimates and reflective of the impact 77 
of a regional elimination campaign in South Asia.  VL case fatality rates (2-3%) have, 78 
however, changed little over the past decade and poorer outcomes are reported in some 79 
countries (e.g. Brazil at 7.1%) 3.  Since vector control alone is likely to be insufficient, 80 
controlling the leishmaniases by vaccination is an attractive option. 81

estimates of the global demand for 10 and  affordability of 11 a successful leishmaniasis 87 
vaccine.  However, despite several decades of effort and numerous studies in animal models 88

 97 
CHIM studies are now well-embedded in the vaccine development pathway for numerous 98 
infectious diseases including the parasitic diseases malaria 19,20, schistosomiasis 21 and 99 
hookworm 22.   Deliberate human infection with Leishmania is not new 23 and 100 
“leishmanization”, the inoculation of lesion scapings into cosmetically hidden areas, has been 101 
practiced for centuries in CL endemic countries to induce immunity and minimise visible 102 
scarring and stigma 24.  Leishmanization was proposed for vaccine development in the early 103 
2000’s providing evidence of feasibility 25, but not pursued.  Subsequently, the importance of 104 
vector-associated modulation of immunity has become more fully appreciated 26 and 105 
evidence suggests that vaccines effective against needle challenge may not protect against 106 
natural transmission 27.   107

Study design 117 
We previously reported on prior enabling studies, including development of a cGMP 118 
challenge strain of L. major (MHOM/IL/2019/MRC-02)28, development of a sand fly biting 119 
protocol 29 and focus group assessments of public perceptions of the project 30.  For this first 120 
study involving human challenge (LEISH_Challenge; ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: 121 
NCT04512742), we enrolled 14 healthy Leishmania-naïve volunteers aged 18-50 at the 122 
University of York Translational Research Facility (Fig. 1 and Extended Data 1 and 2).  123 
First and last participants were exposed to infected sand flies between January and August 124 
2022 respectively. There were 8 female and 6 male participants, median 32 years old, all 125 
white ethnicity reflecting our local resident population (98% white ethnicity). Sex was not 126 
explicitly factored into the experimental design. Sex was assigned and gender was not 127 
recorded.  Exclusion and inclusion criteria were as per study protocol (Extended Data 3) and 128 
included absence of Leishmania exposure history, willingness to refrain from travel to 129 
Leishmania major-endemic regions during the study and absence of significant atopy or 130 
active skin disease. All participants had negative HIV, hepatitis B and hepatitis C serology 131 
and gave written informed consent. A pragmatic adaptive design was chosen to expose the 132 
least number of participants to infection and provide flexibility based on initial outcomes.   133 
Five Phlebotomus duboscqi sand flies infected with L. major MHOM/IL/2019/MRC-02 were 134 
allowed access for 30 min to the volar aspect of the proximal forearm, approximately 2-3 135 
centimetres distal to the antecubital fossa, using a bespoke biting chamber with a variable 136 
aperture 29. Participants were considered for therapeutic biopsy when a clinically apparent 137

Recurrence following therapeutic biopsy 191 
Of the 10 participants having therapeutic biopsy, 7 (70%) required no further treatment and 192 
remain lesion free (Fig. 3a-h).  In three cases (LC001, LC004, LC016), a lesion subsequently 193 
developed at the biopsy site 4-8 months after biopsy (Fig. 3i-l).  A second biopsy (punch) 194 
was performed in each participant for parasitological confirmation (30800, 184 and 1658 195 
parasites /mg tissue in biopsies taken at 255 days, 282 days, and 126 days post bite, 196 
respectively).  Cryotherapy was initiated using a standard cryotherapy delivery device 197 
(0.75mm nozzle and repeated 10 second freeze/thaw cycle) as per protocol with all three 198 
volunteers receiving 3 treatments spaced over 6-8 weeks.  All responded well with apparent 199 
healing of their CL lesion. LC004 had a second recurrence 14 months post original biopsy.  200 
This later lesion resolved with an additional cycle of cryotherapy.   201

Scarring in CHIM participants 213 
Scarring was evident in all subjects who had a biopsy. Seven of the participants (LC007, 214 
LC008, LC011, LC016, LC020, LC025, LC026) were left with mild atrophic scarring in 215 
keeping with the excision biopsy plus the effect of CL. Two participants who developed 216 
atypical lesions where punch biopsy did not confirm CL (LC021, LC027) also developed 217 
mild atrophic scarring. One participant (LC003) had moderate scarring. Two  participants 218 
who developed a second CL lesion (LC001, LC004) had more moderate to severe scarring, 219 
potentially as a combined result of CL, the surgical procedure(s) and cryotherapy. LC016 had 220 
mild atrophic scarring, perhaps reflecting earlier biopsy, smaller lesion size and use of punch 221 
vs. elliptical excision biopsy.  Two participants (LC003, LC004) also had wound infection 222 
post biopsy which may have contributed to the resultant scarring.  Measurement of the scar 223 
area at final follow up confirmed reduced scarring in cohort 2 (punch biopsy) compared to 224 
cohort 1 (excision biopsy) (Fig3m).  Scar size was comparable between females and males 225 
across both cohorts (females: median, 200 days, range 56-425; males: median 88 days, range 226 
56-315).  The degree of scarring and potential contributory factors are summarised in227 
Extended Data 2. 228

Discussion 362 
We report clinical, parasitological, and preliminary immunological data from the first CHIM 363 
of sand fly transmitted CL caused by Leishmania major.  We demonstrate that this model has 364 
a take rate comparable to that of other challenge models and is safe and well-tolerated by 365 
participants, suggesting its suitability for evaluating new Leishmania vaccines, pre- or post-366 
exposure therapies, and the immunopathogenesis of CL in humans.  367 
 368 
Previous human infection studies with Leishmania using needle challenge allowed lesion 369 
progression through to self-cure, with most participants developing ulcerated lesions by 60 370 
days post inoculation 25.   In contrast, our study was designed to estimate take rate using a 371 
newly identified and cGMP manufactured L. major strain and a laboratory-reared colony of 372 
P. duboscqi and to utilize therapeutic biopsy 48 to excise the lesion early in development. The 373 
latter approach was guided by a public involvement (PI) exercise 30 and a desire to avoid 374 
extensive lesion development.   Increased understanding of disease mechanisms facilitated by 375 
lesion biopsy was also recognised by our PI group as a desirable outcome 30.   The results 376 
from the first cohort demonstrated a take rate of 83% for all participants (100% for those 377 
receiving a bite). Recruitment was extended to increase confidence in the take rate and to 378 
incorporate changes aimed at minimising lesion size and scarring.  These contributed to 379 
reduced scaring, but the smaller lesions and earlier time points of biopsy reduced overall take 380 
rate.  Nevertheless, this aligns with other CHIMs and the common belief that it is wise to 381 
avoid an overwhelming force of infection in CHIM-based vaccine trials 49.  However, if 382

we observed various stages of epidermal remodelling associated with ulceration or 453 
underlying inflammation. Collectively, these data provide a blueprint for future studies 454
