# Uncovering cell type-specific phenotypes using a novel human in vitro model of transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy

Transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) is characterized by the misfolding of transthyretin (TTR), fibrillogenesis, and progressive amyloid fibril deposition in the myocardium, leading to cardiac dysfunction with dismal prognosis. In ATTR-CM, either destabilizing mutations (variant TTR, ATTRv) or ageing-associated processes (wild-type TTR, ATTRwt) lead to the formation of TTR amyloid fibrils. Due to a lack of representative disease models, ATTR-CM disease mechanisms are largely unknown, thereby limiting disease understanding and therapeutic discovery.

Here, we report a novel in vitro ATTR-CM model which uncovers cell type-specific disease phenotypes by exposing the three major human cardiac cell types to TTR fibrils, thereby providing novel insights into the cellular mechanisms of ATTR-CM disease. Human recombinant TTR proteins (WT, V122I, V30M) and respective fibrils were generated and characterized using Thioflavin T, Amytracker, Congo red and dot blot analyses. Seeding human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived-cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) and endothelial cells (ECs) on TTR fibrils resulted in reduced cell viability. Confocal microscopy revealed extracellular localization of TTR fibrils to hiPSC-CMs, leading to sarcomere disruption, altered calcium handling and disrupted electromechanical coupling, while ECs showed a reduced migration capacity with aberrant cell morphology. hiPSC-fibroblasts (hiPSC-FBs) were largely unaffected by TTR fibrils, presenting normal viability, but showing enhanced localization with TTR fibrils.

Our model shows that WT and variant TTR fibrils lead to cell type-specific phenotypes, providing novel insights into the underlying cellular disease mechanisms of ATTR-CM, thereby facilitating the identification of novel therapeutic targets and biomarkers.

Cardiac amyloidosis is a progressive, life-threatening, and infiltrative disease caused by the deposition of insoluble amyloid fibrils in the myocardium. One of the main types of cardiac amyloidosis is transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM). Transthyretin (TTR) is a homotetrameric protein that is primarily synthesized in the liver and functions as a transporter for thyroxine (T4) and retinol in the blood and cerebrospinal fluid [1]. ATTR-CM can be classified into two subtypes: wild-type TTR (ATTRwt-CM), which is increasingly found in the elderly population, and variant TTR (ATTRv-CM) where a genetic mutation affects TTR protein stability and accelerates fibril formation and deposition. ATTRwt-CM most commonly affects the heart, occurs mainly in elderly men and is considered to have an age-related cause. Although ATTRv-CM is considered a rare hereditary disease, over a hundred different TTR variants have been identified, which progress differently and involve different organs [2]. The V30M variant, in which valine is substituted by methionine at the thirtieth position of the TTR amino acid sequence, primarily manifests with polyneuropathy and cardiomyopathy such as conduction disturbances and heart failure [3]. Another frequent variant, V122I, is predominantly present in the African American population with a prevalence of 3–4% [4]. Clinically, the progressive accumulation of TTR fibrils results in increased ventricular wall thickness, diastolic dysfunction, conduction disorders and finally, a restrictive cardiomyopathy with poor prognosis if left untreated [3,5]. Due to the initial nonspecific symptoms and lack of specific biomarkers, diagnostic delay or misdiagnosis are not uncommon [6], underlining the critical need for finding specific early biomarkers and increasing our mechanistic understanding of early disease progression. This need is amplified by the increasing awareness of ATTR-CM, with more and more patients being identified [7]. Although novel therapies [8] for ATTR–CM, including TTR stabilization [9,10,11], TTR gene silencing [12,13,14] and TTR depletion [15] have been introduced, these interventions do not cure the disease but aim to slow or halt ongoing amyloid formation. Therefore, a better understanding of the pathophysiology and disease progression is becoming increasingly important.

To date, no study has comprehensively demonstrated how the three major cell types (cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts) present within the heart respond when exposed to WT and the two most prevalent TTR variant (V122I and V30M) fibrils. In this study, we developed a new approach to model human ATTR-CM disease in vitro by exposing iPSC-CMs, hiPSC-FBs and ECs to seeded TTR fibrils, followed by evaluating cell viability, and distinct phenotypical and functional cellular responses. Our result uncovered detailed cellular phenotypes of ATTR-CM, providing novel insights into the cellular disease mechanisms, potentially advancing diagnosis and treatment strategies of ATTR-CM.

Given that TTR is not glycosylated physiologically [16], we opted to utilize the bacterial expression system for the generation of recombinant human TTR. Human TTR precursor cDNA (Addgene, #52091) without signal peptide sequence (Supplemental Table1) was subcloned into the pCI8b expression vector (Addgene, #61331). A TAA stop codon was inserted to the end of the hTTR cDNA to halt C-terminal His-tags translation, ensuring that only 6xHis-tags were expressed on the N terminus. Previous studies have stated that the presence of his-tags does not affect TTR functionality [17,18]. Variant V122I and V30M TTR (Supplemental Table1) were generated using the Q5®Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (New England BioLabs Inc., #E0554S) and the QuickChange II kit (Agilent Technologies, #200523) respectively, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The WT, V122I, and V30M hTTR proteins were subsequently expressed in ClearColi BL21competent cells (Lucigen, #60810-1) harboring corresponding plasmids. Protein expression was induced by the addition of 0.5 mM IPTG when the absorbance (A600) of 0.6 was reached. BL21 cells were grown at 30 °C overnight and harvested by centrifugation (5000 g for 10 min at 4 °C). Next, pelleted cells were resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM phosphate buffer, 300 mM NaCl, 0.2 mg/ml lysozyme (Sigma-Aldrich, #L6876), 10 U/mL DNase (Roche, #04536282001), 1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and the EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (Roche, #4693132001), pH 7.4) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Cell lysates were stored at -80 °C for at least 1 h or until use. Lysates were thawed at 37 °C for 30 min and sonicated by applying 10x short cycles of sonication. The supernatant was then collected by centrifugation at 4 °C at 13,000 rpm for 20 min, filtered using 0.45 μm SFCA filters (Corning®, #431220) and stored at 4 °C. For protein purification, the filtered supernatant was loaded into the HiTrap TALON crude column (Cytiva, #28953809). The column was balanced with equilibrated buffer (50 mM phosphate buffer, 300 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) before the protein was eluted (elution buffer: 50 mM phosphate buffer, 300 mM NaCl, 250 mM imidazole, pH 7.4). The TTR corresponding fractions were then pooled and further purified by size exclusion chromatography using a HiLoad Superdex 75pg preparative size exclusion chromatography column (Cytiva, # 28989334) and eluted with Ca2+and Mg2+free PBS (Gibco, #10010015). After purification, the elution samples were pooled together and concentrated by centrifugation at 4 °C at maximum speed using the Amicon®Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Unit (Merck, #UFC9010). The concentration of this sample was then measured using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, #23225) (Supplemental Fig.1A). The levels of endotoxin were analyzed using the Pierce™ Chromogenic Endotoxin Quant Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, #A39553), which is below the stated 1 EU/µg limit of detection compared to BL21(DE3) competent cells (ThermoFisher Scientific, # EC0114) (data not shown), and in line with commercially available low endotoxin recombinant proteins.

4–15% precast polyacrylamide gel (Bio-Rad, #4561084) was used for SDS-PAGE and NATIVE-PAGE. For SDS-PAGE (Supplemental Fig. 1B), the loading mixture containing samples, 4X NuPAGE™ LDS Sample Buffer (Invitrogen™, # NP0007), and 10X NuPAGE™ Sample Reducing Agent (Invitrogen™, # NP0004), were heated at 95 °C for 10 min and ran into the Tris/Glycine/SDS buffer (Bio-Rad, #1610732). For NATIVE -PAGE (Supplemental Fig.1C), samples were mixed with loading buffer (Bio-Rad, #1610738) into 1:1 ratio and ran into the Tris/Glycine buffer (Bio-Rad, #1610734). The purity of proteins was > 95% as judged by SDS-PAGE, stained with InstantBlue®Coomassie Protein Stain (Abcam, # ab119211). Recombinant hTTR size confirmation was checked by NATIVE-PAGE with BSA (66 kDa) (ThermoFisher Scientific, #11020039).

Recombinant WT and variant (V122I and V30M) hTTR fibrils were induced by adapting the protocol as outlined before [19]. Briefly, the acidic buffer (200 mM Sodium acetate, 100 mM NaCl, and 1 mM EDTA at pH 4.3) was mixed 1:1 with purified TTR tetramer (2 mg/mL) to induce TTR dissociation, misfolding, and aggregation. Fibril formation was induced at 37 °C using a heating block for 5 days. The fibrils were dialyzed immediately into PBS (Gibco, #10010015) using a 10k MWCO Slide-A-Lyzer™ dialysis cassette (ThermoFisher Scientific, #66810) at 4 °C. Following dialysis, the freshly prepared fibrils were used for subsequent cell assays within 48 h.

The extent of fibril formation was probed using Thioflavin T (ThT) (Thermo Scientific, # 211760050) and Amytracker540 (Ebba Biotech AB) fluorescence reading. Briefly, 50 µL of ThT solution was mixed with 50 µL of samples to reach 25 µM ThT [20]. ThT fluorescence emission was measured at 482 nm and excitation at 450 nm using the CLARIOstar®Plus plate reader (BMG Labtech). Fifty µL Amytracker540 (1:5000 dilution in PBS) was mixed with 50 µL sample and incubated for 30 min in the dark. Amytracker540 fluorescence emission was measured at 540 nm and excitation at 480 nm, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

TTR fibrils encapsulated in fibrin gel (Sigma-Aldrich, #F8630) were stained with Congo Red (Sigma Life Science, #C6277) to analyze apple-green birefringence signals under polarized light. Further verification of the TTR fibrils was performed by Dot-Blot assay. Briefly, 5 µL of concentrated TTR fibrils solution was added to a nitrocellulose membrane (Bio-Rad, #1620112) and air dried completely. The membrane was blocked using Intercept®(TBS) blocking (LICOR, #927-60001) buffer before incubating overnight at 4 °C with mouse monoclonal antibody against human TTR (Novus Biologicals, #2E10C5) and the amyloid fibril polyclonal antibody (ThermoFisher Scientific, #PA5-77843) with a 1:1000 dilution. After overnight incubation, the membrane was washed three times for 10 min with TBST before incubation for 2 h in the dark at room temperature with secondary antibodies IRDye®680RD Donkey anti-Mouse IgG (LICOR, #926-68072) and IRDye®800CW Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG (LICOR, #926-32213) at 1:20000 dilution. The membrane was then washed three times for 10 min with TBST, once for 15 min with TBS, and rinsed in distilled water before imaging using the Odyssey®M Imaging System (LICOR). The specific immunoreactivity of anti-amyloid fibril antibody was confirmed by insulin fibrils, which were induced by agitating insulin at 1000 rpm at 60 °C for 24 h.

Two healthy human peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC)-derived hiPSC lines were used: UKKi032-C [21] and UKKi037-C. These cell lines have previously been deposited in the European Bank for Induced Pluripotent Stem Cells (EBiSC) and are listed in the online registry for human pluripotent stem cell lines (hPSCreg). All experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the code of proper use of human tissue used in the Netherlands. hiPSCs were grown on a 0.1 mg/mL growth-factor-reduced Matrigel coating (CORNING, #356231) and cultured in the Essential 8™ (Gibco, # A1517001) medium.

hiPSCs were differentiated into cardiomyocytes using a GiWi protocol, as previously described [22,23]. Characterization of the resulting hiPSC-CMs, including flow cytometry and immunofluorescence staining for Troponin T, is shown in Supplemental Fig.10A and10B. hiPSC-CMs were used after 3 weeks of differentiation and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 1x B27 (ThermoFisher Scientific, #17504001).

hiPSCs were differentiated into fibroblasts using an established protocol [24] and cultured in FBM™ Basal Medium (Lonza, #CC-3131) supplemented with FBMTM-3 singleQuot supplements (Lonza, #CC-4525), required for fibroblasts growth. Immunofluorescent characterization of hiPSC-FBs, stained for Vimentin, is shown in Supplemental Fig.10C. hiPSC-FBs were used from passage 1–2 for experiments in this study.

Primary HUVECs were purchased from Lonza and maintained in EGM™-2 Endothelial Cell Growth Medium-2 (Lonza, # CC-3162/6) and cultured according to company instructions. HUVECs were used from passage 6–9 for experiments in this study.

TTR and TTR fibrils were homogenously distributed (Supplemental Fig.5D) in a coating medium containing 0.1 mg/mL Matrigel (Corning, #356231), adapted from previous studies [25,26]. To determine the optimal experimental conditions for subsequent cell assays, we conducted titration tests using concentrations of 50, 125, and 250 mg/L of TTR and TTR fibrils, with treatment durations up to 8 days. Based on the cell viability results (data not shown), 125 mg/L with 2-day and 4-day incubation were selected. Culture plates were then coated for 1 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. At day 0, cells were plated on the coated plates without removal of the TTR or TTR fibrils coating medium and cultured for 24 h when medium was refreshed with standard culture medium (day 1). Following a 2-day incubation period for hiPSC-FBs and ECs (to prevent overconfluence) and a 4-day incubation period for hiPSC-CMs, a series of cell assays were conducted, as detailed below.

Cell viability was evaluated using the LIVE/DEAD Viability/Cytotoxicity kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific-Life Technologies, #L3224) as previously described [27]. Dead cells stained by ethidium homodimer-1 (EthD-1), Live cells stained by Calcein-AM, and the nucleus stained by Hoechst 33,342 were incubated in culture medium for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Imaging was performed using a Thunder microscope (Leica Microsystems) at 10x magnification and analysis was done using FIJI software.

AlamarBlue reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #DAL1025) was used to assess cell viability through metabolic activity in 96-well plates (CELLSTAR, #655180) as described before [28]. Briefly, cells were cultured in culture medium containing 1:10 diluted AlamarBlue for 3 h at 37 °C in the dark. Afterwards, the supernatant was transferred to a new 96-well plate, and AlamarBlue fluorescence emission was measured at 540 nm and excitation at 590 nm using the CLARIOstar®Plus plate reader (BMG Labtech).

The calcium transient analysis of hiPSC-CMs was performed as described [29]. Briefly, cells were stained with 1:1 Cal520 mix (1:1000 Cal520 (Abcam, ab171868), 0.02% Pluronic F127 (Sigma Life Science, #P2443) in FluoroBrite DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A1896701)) in RPMI (ThermoFisher Scientific, #21875), 1% P/S (Gibco™, #15140-122), 1x B27 (ThermoFisher Scientific, #17504001) for 60 min at 37 °C. Live imaging was performed at 37 °C and 10x magnification using a Thunder microscope (Leica Microsystems) for 10 s at a speed of 33 frames per second. The analysis was done using FIJI software and MATLAB software (MathWorks) with a custom MATLAB script [30].

The migration of HUVECs was assessed using a wound-healing assay in 48-well plates (CELLSTAR, #677180) as described previously [31]. 48 h after plating the cells, at near confluency, a linear scratch was made using a 1 mL pipet tip on the monolayer of HUVECs and any floating cells were removed. The wound healing process was monitored using a CytoSMART Omni (Axion Biosystems) for 24 h, taking bright field images every hour. Relative wound closure was calculated relative to the negative control by using FIJI with a standard plugin [32].

Immunofluorescent labeling was performed on fixed cells as previously reported [29]. Cells cultured on coverslips in 24-well plates (Sarstedt, #83.3922.005) were washed with PBS (Gibco™, #14190144) and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Merck, #47608) for 15 min at room temperature and stored in PBS (Gibco™, #14190144) at 4 °C. Cells were permeabilized in 0.3% Triton-X-100 and 5% BSA in PBS (Gibco™, #14190144) for 1 h. The primary antibody was diluted in 0.06% Triton-X-100 and 1% BSA in PBS (Gibco™, #14190144) and incubated at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS (Gibco™, #14190144), the secondary antibody and Hoechst 33,342 were diluted in 0.06% Triton-X-100 and 1% BSA in PBS (Gibco™, #14190144) and incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After washing, the coverslips were mounted onto a glass slide using Fluoromount-G™ Mounting Medium (ThermoFisher Scientific, #00-4958-02). Imaging was performed using a confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems) and processed and analyzed using Fiji software. The number of disrupted sarcomere structures in each hiPSC-CM image was scored blindly by quantifying nuclei number and assessing myofibril sarcomere structure. All used antibodies are listed in Supplemental Table2.

Total RNA was isolated from cells using the RNeasy®Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, #74134), following the manufacturer´s instructions. Roughly 20–150 ng of high-quality total RNA (RIN > 9) were used for transcriptomic interrogation of hiPSC-CMs, ECs, and hiPSC-FBs seeded on fibril-coated culture plates and negative controls (N= 3 for each condition and cell type) using Illumina’s Stranded Total RNA Prep Ligation with Ribo-Zero Plus according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cytoplasmic and mitochondrial rRNAs as well as beta globin transcripts were depleted from the samples. The remaining RNA was fragmented and reverse transcribed. A second strand cDNA synthesis step removed the RNA template while incorporating dUTP in place of dTTP to preserve strand specificity. Next, double-stranded cDNA was A-tailed, then ligated to Illumina anchors bearing T-overhangs. PCR-amplification of the library allowed the barcoding of the samples with 10 bp dual indexes and the completion of Illumina sequences for cluster generation. Libraries were quantified with Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher, #Q32851) and their profile was examined using Agilent’s HS D1000 ScreenTape Assay (Agilent, #5067–5584). Sequencing was carried out in an Illumina NextSeq2000 using paired-end, dual-index sequencing (Rd1: 59 cycles; i7: 10 cycles; i5: 10 cycles Rd2:59 cycles) at a depth of 50 million reads per sample.

Samples were demultiplexed using Illuminabcl2fastqsoftware (v.2.2.0), reads trimmed and quality filtered withTrim Galore(v0.6.10) and aligned to the human genome (GRCh38) withSTAR(V2.7.11b) using default parameters. Gene expression quantification was performed using thefeatureCountsfunction implemented in the R packageRsubread(v2.20.0) counting uniquely mapped reads with reverse strandness. The Ensembl v112 gene annotation was considered as the reference. ThefilterbyExprfunction implemented in theedgeRpackage (v4.4.2) was used to filter out genes with low number of counts for downstream analyses. Data normalization, transformation (considering variance stabilizing transformation), principal component analysis and differential expression analyses with the Wald test and an FDR of 0.05 were performed with theDESeq2package (v1.46.0), including the effect of the differentiation/cell line in the experimental design. Comparisons were performed between each fibril condition and the control. Gene Set Enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed withfgsea(v1.32.4), ranking genes by the Wald statistic and considering the Hallmark, WikiPathways (WP), Reactome and Gene Ontology Biological Processes (GOBP) and Molecular Functions (GOMF) databases. (Supplemental MaterialGSEAExcel file) The gene sets were downloaded from MSigDB (www.gsea-msigdb.org, version 2024.1.Hs). Additionally, overrepresentation analyses of the common differentially expressed genes (adjustedp-value < 0.05) retrieved in the three fibril conditions against the control condition for each cell type were performed withclusterProfiler(v4.14.6) against the same databases as in the GSEA (Supplemental Fig.11and Supplemental MaterialORAExcel file). In both cases, the resultingp-values were corrected with Benjamini-Hochberg, setting a 0.05 cutoff for significance.

All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates, each with four technical replicates. All data are presented as Mean ± SEM and considered significant at ap-value ≤ 0.05, with increasing levels of significance denoted as follows:*P< 0.05,**P< 0.01,***P< 0.001,****P< 0.0001. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad software, San Diego, CA). For datasets that passed normality testing, statistical differences were analyzed using a two-way or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. When the datasets failed to pass normality testing, a one-way ANOVA non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test was used.

To recapitulate the in vivo cardiac pathophysiological effects of TTR fibrils in our in vitro model, we first generated human recombinant WT and variant (V122I and V30M) TTR proteins and induced fibril formation. The recombinant TTR proteins were overexpressed in theE. colistrain BL21, as described in the Methods section. The high purity of produced protein was confirmed by SDS-PAGE (Supplemental Fig.1B). NATIVE-PAGE showed that all types of recombinant TTR proteins have a similar size to BSA, indicating tetramer formation at physiological pH (Supplemental Fig.1C). The formation of TTR fibrils in vitro under mild acidic conditions (Fig.1A) were systematically evaluated. Amytracker 540 staining, a novel fluorescent amyloid marker, showed a significant increase in fluorescent intensity for all TTR types upon mild acidification, with both V122I and V30M variants exhibiting a significantly higher intensity compared to WT TTR (Fig.1B). The fibril formation was further shown by a characteristic shift in the emission spectrum and enhanced ThT fluorescence (Fig.1C). These findings underscore the influence of specific amino acid substitutions on TTR protein stability, leading to enhanced dissociation and aggregation efficiency. Additional validation of TTR fibril formation was shown through Congo red staining and subsequent examination under polarized light, with detection of typical apple-green birefringence confirming amyloid fibril formation (Fig.1D). Furthermore, fibril formation was shown via a Dot-Blot assay, employing antibodies specific to TTR and amyloid fibrils and including insulin-derived fibrils as a positive control (Fig.1E). Notably, V122I and V30M TTR fibrils exhibited a strong immunoreactivity towards the anti-amyloid fibril antibody, in contrast to WT fibrils which exhibited a weaker signal, indicating less efficient amyloid fibril formation.

After successfully producing TTR and generating TTR fibrils in vitro, we aimed to investigate their potential impact on cell viability and morphology by culturing hiPSC-CMs on WT and variant (V30M, V122I) TTR tetramers, and respective fibrils compared to a standard coated surface. AlamarBlue cell viability reagent and Live/Dead staining showed that WT and variant TTR tetramers did not have any adverse effects on cell viability. In contrast, exposure to TTR fibrils resulted in a cytotoxic response, showing a significant reduction in hiPSC-CM cell viability (WT fibril: 90.9% ± 2.4% SEM; V122I fibril: 78.9% ± 2.5% SEM; V30M fibril: 84.6% ± 3.1% SEM). In particular, we observed that V122I fibrils exhibited significantly higher cytotoxic effects than WT fibrils (Fig.2A). The cytotoxicity of different types of TTR fibrils was further confirmed by a reduction in the live cell population (WT fibril: 94.7% ± 1.0% SEM; V122I fibril: 92.1% ± 1.8% SEM; V30M fibril: 90.3% ± 2.0% SEM) with increased cell death staining (Fig.2B and D).

Subsequently, we explored the localization of TTR fibrils and sarcomere morphology of hiPSC-CMs. A significant extracellular localization of TTR fibrils with hiPSC-CMs was found, where TTR fibrils located to the outside of the cells (Fig.2E), as shown by representative orthogonal views (Supplemental Fig. 2A). Immunostaining of cardiac troponin showed an increased presence of irregular sarcomeres in hiPSC-CMs when exposed to TTR fibrils (indicated by arrows in Fig.2E), as compared to cells exposed to the TTR tetramers or the control (WT fibril: 4.0 ± 0.3 SEM; V122I fibril: 4.0 ± 0.3 SEM; V30M fibril: 4.2 ± 0.2 SEM) (Fig.2C). This disruption of sarcomere organization was approximately four-fold higher in the presence of fibrils, indicating a significantly greater extent of structural abnormality. Enlarged figures to exhibit sarcomere disruption was listed in Supplemental Fig.8–10.

Since arrhythmia is generally the most prevalent manifestation in ATTR-CM [33], we evaluated the contraction and relaxation pattern and calcium-handling of hiPSC-CMs when cultured on TTR tetramer and fibrils (Fig.3A and B). Calcium handling parameters, including rise time, decay time, amplitude, and calcium transient duration (CTD90) were evaluated. The calcium transient amplitude of hiPSC-CMs exposed to V122I and V30M fibrils decreased significantly (WT fibril: 91.7% ± 2.7% SEM; V122I fibril: 91.1% ± 2.1% SEM; V30M fibril: 90.1% ± 1.7 SEM) compared to the control (Fig.3C). Although we did not observe statistically significant differences in decay time and CTD90 (Fig.3E and F), a significantly increased rise time in all fibril types (WT fibril: 117.3% ± 3.6% SEM; V122I fibril: 117.7% ± 3.7% SEM; V30M fibril: 116.2% ± 4.6 SEM) was observed when compared to the TTR tetramers and the control (Fig.3D). Besides, hiPSC-CMs replated on WT and variant TTR tetramers or untreated surface displayed normal electrophysiological properties, as shown by synchronized contraction and relaxation of the monolayer with periodic calcium transients (Fig.3G) (Video 1 and 2). Conversely, hiPSC-CMs cultured on TTR fibrils showed disrupted electromechanical coupling, along with chaotic and spontaneous calcium release events in disconnected cell clusters (Fig.3G, Supplemental Fig.3, indicated by circular dashed lines) (Video 3). Next, these data collectively indicate that the TTR fibrils, when localized on hiPSC-CMs, may function as a propagation barrier, thereby disrupting normal electromechanical coupling and reflecting the heart rhythm disturbances observed in ATTR-CM patients. The altered calcium-handling parameters highlight the effect of TTR fibrils on hiPSC-CM contractile function, leading to a situation prone for diastolic dysfunction.

Endothelial cells are one of the most abundant cell types in the heart and amyloid deposits have been associated with endothelial dysfunction in patients [34]. To assess whether deposited TTR tetramer and fibrils are cytotoxic to ECs, we assessed viability using the AlamarBlue and Live/Dead assays. The results showed that TTR tetramers (WT and variants) do not affect EC viability, as observed in hiPSC-CMs. When culturing ECs on TTR fibrils, however, a significant reduction (WT fibril: 69.0% ± 4.6% SEM; V122I fibril: 57.75% ± 4.3% SEM; V30M fibril: 55.75% ± 2.8 SEM) in cell viability was observed after two days (Fig.4A). Cardiac fibroblasts are responsible for producing and remodeling the extracellular matrix and TTR fibrils have been reported to influence the structure and functionality of fibroblasts [26]. Moreover, the accumulation of amyloid fibrils with ECM may contribute to the stiffening of the cardiac tissue. We therefore also assessed cell viability in hiPSC-FBs. Similar to CMs and ECs, we observed no cytotoxic effects from WT and variant TTR tetramers. However, in contrast to the cytotoxic effects observed in hiPSC-CMs and ECs, hiPSC-FBs viability was not significantly affected by any of the TTR fibrils, except for a minimal decrease induced by V122I fibrils (WT fibril: 99.8% ± 0.2% SEM; V122I fibril: 98.8% ± 0.4% SEM; V30M fibril: 98.5% ± 0.8 SEM), suggesting a cell-type specific resilience to TTR fibril exposure (Fig.5A, B and D). Furthermore, the average surface area of hiPSC-FBs showed a minor decrease compared to hiPSC-CMs and ECs, where cell size was markedly affected, indicative of cellular stress (Supplemental Fig.4).

In line with the observation in the hiPSC-CMs and ECs, confocal microscopy images with orthogonal views showed that TTR fibrils predominantly localize with the hiPSC-FBs (Fig.5E and F, Supplemental Fig.2C). By quantifying the a-SMA fluorescent intensity, indicative of fibroblast activation and differentiation to myofibroblasts, no significant increased protein expression was observed in hiPSC-FBs (Fig.5C). Interestingly, in contrast to hiPSC-CMs and ECs, hiPSC-FBs appeared to mediate the distribution of the coated TTR tetramers and TTR fibrils, exhibiting a more pronounced accumulation to the cell surface (Supplemental Fig.5). Taken together, hiPSC-FBs show a high degree of resilience to TTR fibril cytotoxicity and do not transform into the more activated myofibroblast phenotype when exposed to TTR fibrils.

To understand the molecular mechanisms underlying cellular responses to TTR fibrils, we studied the transcriptomic profile of hiPSC-CMs, ECs, and hiPSC-FBs by bulk RNA-seq. Transcriptomic analysis was performed on hiPSC-CMs seeded on fibril-coated culture plates for 4 days, and ECs and hiPSC-FBs seeded for 2 days. In hiPSC-CMs, a gene set enrichment analysis revealed an upregulation for all fibril conditions in processes related to DNA repair, apoptosis, cellular senescence and oxidative stress, as well as those associated with cellular respiration, including oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthesis, which are essential to maintain function in mechanically active cells. On the other hand, cellular adhesion, cell junction assembly and structural organization related processes were significantly downregulated in hiPSC-CMs exposed to fibrils (Fig.6A). These results are also preserved when considering only those genes differentially expressed in all fibril conditions (Fig.6A and Supplemental Fig.11A). Noteworthily, the exposure to fibrils also induced a downregulation of processes related to calcium ion binding and calcium ion transport, in line with our functional experiments (Fig.3D). In ECs, the inclusion of TTR fibrils induced a downregulation of gene sets related to DNA replication, mitochondrial gene expression, gap junction assembly, cell adhesion and wound healing processes, and an upregulation in those related to degradation of ECM, ECM organization and blood vessel endothelial cell differentiation (Fig.6B and Supplemental Fig.11B). Finally, in FBs, the most relevant biological differences were associated with an upregulation in processes related to cell growth, cell adhesion, collagen formation and unfolded protein binding (Fig.6C and Supplemental Fig.11C). Taken together, these RNA-seq results are largely in line with and underscore our observations of the cellular responses to TTR fibrils.

ATTR-CM is an underrecognized cause of heart failure that is becoming more and more prevalent due to the aging population. Through increasing disease awareness and improved non-invasive diagnostic approaches, suspicion and diagnosis are improving [3]. The ATTR-CM disease mechanisms are still largely unknown, especially at the cellular level, thereby limiting disease understanding and therapeutic discovery. Preclinical research models are invaluable platforms to understand cardiac amyloidosis pathology and discover novel therapeutic strategies. Although important progress has been made in the past decades in understanding the complexity of ATTR-CM development using several disease models, the use of human, more predictive ATTR-CM disease models has been extremely limited. Animal models, such as a short-term rat model, where aggregated TTR was injected into the apex of the heart [35], a transgenic V30M mouse model [36], and a S52P TTR overexpression and amyloid seeding model [37] have been shown to recapitulate some aspects of ATTR in vivo. In vitro models, using cultured primary cardiomyocytes from animals or modified AC16 human cardiomyocytes exposed to WT and variant TTR tetramer [38,39,40,41] or fibrils [35,42,43,44] in the culture media, have shown reduced cell viability with increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and aberrant calcium transients. Additionally, RNAseq and ATACseq techniques have been utilized to investigate the earliest response of cardiomyocytes when exposed to WT and variant types of TTR tetramers [45]. Further study using directly differentiated ATTR patient-specific iPSCs into hepatocytes that produce the L55P variant TTR, has shown an impact on oxidative stress and cell death in cardiomyocytes and neurons derived from the same iPSC line [46]. The establishment of several iPSC lines from ATTRv patients has further facilitated in vitro investigation of potential disease mechanisms and therapeutic strategies [47,48,49]. However, to date, no study has demonstrated how the three major cell types in the human heart respond to TTR fibrils. Our model addresses this gap by utilizing acid-induced TTR fibrils to establish a clinically relevant platform that incorporates hiPSC-CMs, hiPSC-FBs, and ECs. We employed seeded TTR fibrils derived from WT and variant TTR to investigate cell-specific responses that reflect clinical manifestations. This approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of the cellular phenotypes of ATTR-CM pathogenesis and offers a robust system for advancing diagnosis and treatment strategies.

In this study, in vitro generated WT and variant types of TTR fibrils were found to reduce cell viability of hiPSC-CMs and ECs but not hiPSC-FBs. Furthermore, extracellularly located TTR fibrils disrupted electro-mechanical coupling and sarcomere organization, affected calcium handling with increased rise time and reduced amplitude in hiPSC-CMs, and impaired EC function as shown by reduced migration capacity. By exposing these major cardiac cell types to fibrils from WT TTR and other prevalent TTR variants (V122I and V30M), we could model the ATTR-CM-relevant fibril-cell interactions and demonstrate cell-specific ATTR-CM phenotypes.

The formation of TTR-derived fibrils is a critical step in establishing a reliable research model for ATTR-CM. However, reconstituting TTR fibrils in vitro that fully reflect the patient-derived structure remains challenging. The most commonly reported and effective method to achieve this is acidic denaturation [19]. By adapting this method, we successfully induced the formation of TTR fibrils that exhibit key characteristics of in vivo amyloid fibrils, as shown by Congo Red, Thioflavin T, Amytracker and amyloid fibril antibody staining (Fig.1). Additionally, both the V122I and V30M variants exhibited significantly higher fluorescent intensity under mild acidic conditions, indicating faster and increased fibril generation compared to their WT counterpart. This increased efficiency for fibrillogenesis observed in variant types of TTR aligns with previous findings, providing further support for their increased susceptibility to fibril formation compared to WT TTR [50]. In addition to the acidic method, recent studies employing modified monomeric TTR and amyloid seeding with TTR fibrils purified from diseased heart tissue have shown well-defined fibril structure under both acidic and neutral conditions, suggesting that seeding with ex vivo amyloid fibrils may represent a more physiological method for generating fibrils in vitro. Nonetheless, the limited availability of primary patient-derived ATTR-CM amyloid fibrils poses an obstacle for implementing this method in in vitro disease models [51,52]. The acid-induced fibril method, with its proven efficacy and ease of use, makes it a reliable and readily available tool.

In this study, the pathophysiological environment was mimicked by coating the culture surface with TTR fibrils. We observed a significant decrease in viability with increased cell death in hiPSC-CMs grown on all types of TTR fibrils (Fig.2A, B and D). Similar results, such as reduced metabolic activity and upregulated ROS production have been shown in neonatal rat cardiomyocytes, HL-1, and H9C2 cardiac murine cell lines upon TTR fibril exposure [35,42,43,44]. While all types of TTR fibrils reduced cell viability and resulted in sarcomere disruption in hiPSC-CMs, the V122I TTR fibrils showed a significantly stronger effect on cell viability compared to the WT fibrils (Fig.2A). These results suggest variant-dependent cytotoxic properties of TTR fibrils, as V122I TTR exhibits a higher propensity for fibril formation due to the destabilizing genetic mutation. This finding matches the clinical observation that specific TTR variants tends to form fibrils more rapidly compared to WT TTR with a more gradual accumulation over time, leading to a more gradual onset of symptoms and predominantly affecting the elderly population.

To the best of our knowledge, no studies have reported the cytotoxic effects of TTR fibrils on all three major cell types present in the heart. Our findings demonstrate a striking reduction in cell viability in ECs when exposed to TTR fibrils, particularly the V30M variant (Fig.4A and B). This suggests a direct toxic effect of the fibrils. Once ECs adhered to the fibril-coated surface, they showed an aberrant cell morphology with reduced cell-cell connection. This phenomenon suggests that TTR fibrils may impede proper ECs adherence, affecting normal cell morphology and function. This raises the possibility that ECs in amyloid-laden areas of ATTR-CM patients’ heart may undergo similar alterations, potentially contributing to disease progression [34,53,54,55]. In contrast to the results seen in the hiPSC-CMs and ECs, hiPSC-FBs showed notable resilience when exposed to TTR fibrils, maintaining their viability (Fig.5A and B, Supplemental Fig.2). We did observe a small decrease in cellular size when exposed to fibrils, indicating that the fibrils induce some cellular stress in FBs. In comparison, Dittloff, et al. showed disorganized actin cytoskeleton, decreased focal adhesion, and flattened nuclear structure in rat primary cardiac FBs subjected to WT TTR fibrils, also indicative of cellular stress [26]. Taken together, these findings suggest that fibroblasts, in contrast to CMs and ECs, are able to adapt to the TTR fibril environment with minor structural changes. Overall, the variant-dependent effects of TTR fibril cytotoxicity and different cell type specific responses highlight the complexity of the pathophysiological effects induced by TTR fibrils.

In vivo, infiltrated TTR amyloid fibrils cause disruption of tissue architecture and cardiac damage. In this study, confocal microscopy analysis showed that TTR fibrils are preferentially located to the cell surface on all cardiac cell types. In hiPSC-CMs, this localization of the TTR fibril with the cell led to sarcomere disruption (Fig.2C and E) and downregulation in cellular adhesion, cell junction assembly and structural organization related processes (Fig.6A), a hallmark of cardiomyopathies with changes in contractility, which reflect the amyloid infiltration phenotypes observed in patients. This observation is consistent with a recent study employing micropatterned WT TTR fibril-coated substrates, which demonstrated significant sarcomere disorganization, loss of sarcomere content, and increased ubiquitin localization to the sarcomere in NRVMs [25]. This group also observed decreased expression of N-cadherin, indicator of the mechanical activity, between NRVMs on TTR fibrils. These findings suggest extracellular TTR fibrils may exert physical stress on cardiomyocytes, disrupting sarcomere integrity and function. TTR fibrils could potentially interact with cell surface receptors or signaling molecules as well, thereby disrupting crucial pathways involved in sarcomere assembly, maintenance and function [56].

Furthermore, acute exacerbations of arrhythmias and diastolic dysfunction are frequently observed in ATTR-CM [33]. In the TTR fibril conditions, we observed strong and asynchronized beating, accompanied by uncoordinated and erratic calcium release between disconnected cell clusters. This indicates that the surface-attached TTR fibrils disrupt cell-cell coupling and negatively affect electrophysiological propagation, which leads to abnormal cardiac rhythm (Fig.3G). Additionally, irregular calcium handling was observed, as indicated by a prolonged rise time and attenuated amplitude (Fig.3B, C and D) and downregulation of processes related to calcium ion binding and calcium ion transport (Fig.6A). This altered calcium handling transients aligns with observations in a recent study where more spontaneous calcium release events and prolonged calcium transients were observed in rat cardiomyocytes exposed to TTR fibrils [25]. Increased rise time and attenuated amplitude, suggesting slower and reduced calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, lead to impaired myofilament contraction and decreased hiPSC-CM contractility, potentially exacerbated by TTR fibril-induced cytotoxicity (Fig.2A and B) and disrupted intercellular communication (Fig.3G). This impaired calcium handling may reflect the potential weakened muscle function in vivo, aligning well with the fibril-induced development of arrythmias and diastolic dysfunction observed in ATTR-CM patients.

Next to these fibril-related myocardial functional effects, amyloid fibrils are also found in perivascular and capillary areas, contributing to narrowed vessel lumen, disrupted capillary and rarefaction [53,54,55]. In our EC model, although WT and variant TTR tetramers did not significantly impact migration, they appeared to exert some influence. This aligns with a previous study demonstrating that V30M TTR can inhibit the expression of pro-angiogenic genes, thereby affecting EC migration [57]. Notably, we found that the extracellular attachment of TTR fibrils resulted in a dramatic decline in migration capacity or wound healing and downregulated wound healing-related gene expression (Figs.4C and F and6B). RNA-seq results from ECs (Fig.6B and Supplemental Fig.11B) cultured on TTR fibrils suggest an impairment of their vascularization capacity and their ability to remodel the environment in response to the cellular stress induced by the TTR fibrils.

Since cardiac fibroblasts are critical in producing and remodeling extracellular matrix, these cells may fulfill an important role in ATTR-CM. Myocardial stiffening in ATTR-CM is possibly attributed to ECM secreted from activated cardiac fibroblasts. However, the myofibroblast activation marker, a-SMA, was expressed in hiPSC-FBs in all conditions and immunofluorescent quantification did not show an upregulation in the presence of TTR fibrils (Fig.5C and F), which may be caused by the experimental conditions, particularly the cell culturing on stiff glass coverslips that can cause a-SMA upregulation. Importantly, we did not find any evidence of TTR fibrils endocytosis in hiPSC-FBs (data not shown), an observation that has been reported in a human fibroblast cell line NIH3T3 and mouse skin fibroblasts [58]. This discrepancy could be explained by variations in the capacity for fibril ingestion among different fibroblast types or differences in the methods used to induce TTR fibrils. Interestingly, we found that hiPSC-FBs specifically relocated the TTR fibrils to areas near the cells, unlike hiPSC-CMs and ECs where TTR fibrils remained equally distributed (Supplemental Fig.3). This intriguing observation, also reflected in our RNA-seq results (Fig.6C and Supplemental Fig.11C), through upregulation of genes associated with matrix remodeling, opens a compelling avenue for further investigation as it could explain the preferential accumulation pattern of TTR fibrils in the heart, potentially contributing to ATTR-CM pathogenesis. Although the RNA-seq results showed only minor differences in hiPSC-FBs compared to the other cell types, it is worth bearing in mind that some of the observed changes still affect essential processes in fibroblast function in the heart, in health and disease.

Our study introduces a novel in vitro ATTR-CM disease model that simulates the pathological conditions of ATTR-CM by exposing three main cardiac cell types (hiPSC-CM, hiPSC-FB and ECs) to both WT and variant TTR-derived tetramers and fibrils. Our findings elucidate how TTR tetramers and fibrils differentially impact cardiac cell viability, structure, and functionality. Notably, while TTR tetramers show no cytotoxic effects on cardiac cells, exposure to coated TTR fibrils significantly reduces viability in hiPSC-CMs and ECs but hiPSC-FBs exhibit significant resistance. Furthermore, fibrils attached to the cell surface hinder cell-to-cell connection, disrupt sarcomere organization, and affect calcium handling in hiPSC-CMs, while also diminishing the migration and proliferation capabilities of ECs. These observations from our ATTR-CM cell model not only provide novel and valuable insights into the clinical manifestations of ATTR-CM patients but also serve as a crucial platform for further understanding the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Therefore, this model may pave the way for the development of targeted therapeutic interventions.
