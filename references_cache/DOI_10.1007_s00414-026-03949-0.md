Abstract
Ricin, a potent protein toxin derived from Ricinus communis , is considered one of the most significant threats in 
cases of bioterrorism, homicide, suicide, and accidental acute poisoning. However, its clinical and pathological fea -
tures in fatal cases have not been systematically summarized. This systematic review aimed to summarize the current 
evidence regarding the clinical manifestations, toxicological biomarkers, autopsy findings, and medico-legal aspects 
of fatal ricin poisoning. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. 
Original studies, case reports, and case series reporting detailed clinical, toxicological, or autopsy data following 
fatal or near-fatal human exposure to ricin were included in the review. The available evidence suggests a relatively 
consistent clinical course, characterized by rapid progression from mild, nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms to 
hypovolemic shock, multi-organ failure, and refractory cardiovascular collapse. Ricinine was consistently detected 
in blood, urine, and postmortem tissues, supporting its role as a reliable biomarker of ricin exposure, although cur -
rent evidence is insufficient to establish definitive correlations with poisoning severity or toxicokinetics. Reported 
autopsy findings commonly included hemolysis, hemorrhagic necrosis of the gastrointestinal tract, hepatic injury, 
renal tubular necrosis, and evidence supporting the presence of a capillary leak syndrome. Several specific medico-
legal challenges emerged from the reviewed literature, including the need for the development of novel analytical 
methods with improved detection limits and quantification capabilities for ricinine, the interpretation of postmortem 
redistribution phenomena, the distinction between lethal and non-lethal exposures, and the differential diagnosis with 
other septic shock-like syndromes. Overall, the available evidence suggests a relatively consistent clinicopathological 
pattern associated with fatal ricin poisoning that can be corroborated through ricinine detection, while emphasizing the 
importance of early toxicological investigations, particularly when no history of exposure is available. These findings 
provide a concise and integrated body of evidence to support clinical management, inform forensic investigations, 
and enhance public health preparedness, while highlighting the need for standardized postmortem sampling protocols 
and further research into the toxicodynamics of ricin in humans.

Introduction
 Ricin is a potent type 2 ribosome-inactivating protein 
(RIP) derived from the seeds of the castor oil plant ( Rici-
nus communis ) and has long been recognized as a highly 
lethal poison [1]. Its high toxicity, low production cost, and 
environmental stability have made it a persistent concern 
for public health authorities, law enforcement agencies, and 
national security organizations worldwide [ 2]. Ricin exerts 
its toxic effects by removing a single adenine residue from 
28 S ribosomal RNA, resulting in irreversible inhibition 
of protein synthesis and the activation of secondary cel -
lular stress pathways that ultimately lead to cell death [ 3]. 
Although the molecular mechanisms underlying ricin toxic-
ity have been extensively investigated in vitro and in ani -
mal models, their translation to the clinical and pathological 
manifestations observed in human fatal poisoning remains 
incomplete and fragmented [4].
Ricin poisoning has long been associated with targeted 
assassinations, most notably the widely reported 1978 death 
of Bulgarian dissident Georgi Markov, which has gener -
ally been attributed to ricin delivered through a modified 
umbrella, although definitive toxicological confirmation 
has never been established. More recently, several incidents 
involving ricin-contaminated letters sent to government 
officials have highlighted the continued threat posed by this 
toxin [5]. These events illustrate the diverse circumstances 
under which ricin may cause fatal poisoning, including 
deliberate homicidal use, accidental laboratory exposures, 
and, less frequently, intentional self-administration [ 6]. 
However, the medical and forensic literature on human ricin 
poisoning remains largely limited to individual case reports 
and small case series, with no comprehensive synthesis of 
the clinical course, diagnostic findings, and autopsy features 
associated with fatal outcomes [7]. The absence of a central-
ized evidence base represents a major challenge for clini -
cians faced with unexplained toxic exposures, for forensic 
pathologists tasked with determining the cause and manner 
of death, and for public health authorities responsible for 
preparedness against potential bioterrorism events.
Several important research gaps continue to limit our 
understanding of fatal ricin poisoning. First, the clinical 
progression from exposure to death has never been system-
atically characterized according to the route of intoxication, 
despite the fact that ingestion, inhalation, and injection each 
involve distinct toxicokinetic and toxicodynamic profiles 
[8]. Second, ricinine, a low-molecular-weight alkaloid natu-
rally associated with castor beans and commonly used as a

biological matrices; however, no comprehensive human

these major gaps in knowledge by systematically evaluat -

clinical manifestations of fatal ricin poisoning, toxicologi -

findings documenting tissue damage caused by ricin toxic -

ricin exposure. By collecting and analyzing all available 
human data in a systematic manner, we aim to integrate cur-
rent knowledge into a coherent model of lethal ricin poi -
soning, spanning the continuum from initial symptom onset 
to the final pathological outcome. This review is intended 
to serve as a valuable resource for clinicians involved in 
the early recognition and management of ricin toxicity, for 
forensic pathologists responsible for establishing the diag -
nosis at autopsy, and for public health and security profes -
sionals engaged in preparedness planning and response to 
potential ricin-related incidents.
Materials and methods
Review protocol
This systematic review was conducted in accordance with 
the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and 
Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [ 11]. A comprehen -
sive literature search was performed up to 1 June 2026 
across three major electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, 
and Web of Science.
For each database, the search strategy was adapted 
according to the specific indexing system and search func -
tionality. Multiple combinations of controlled vocabulary 
(where available) and free-text keywords related to ricin 
exposure, poisoning, clinical manifestations, toxicology, 
pathology, autopsy, and forensic medicine were used to 
maximize the sensitivity and comprehensiveness of the 
search. In PubMed, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were 
incorporated whenever appropriate and combined with free-
text terms. Equivalent search strategies were adapted for 
Scopus (TITLE-ABS-KEY) and Web of Science (TOPIC). 
The representative search strategies are provided in Supple-
mentary Table S1.
In addition, the reference lists of all studies meeting the 
eligibility criteria were manually screened to identify any 
potentially relevant publications that had not been retrieved 
through the electronic database search. No date restrictions 
were applied in order to capture both historical and con -
temporary reports of human ricin poisoning. Only studies 
published in English were considered eligible for inclusion.
The core search string was as follows:
((Ricin OR “Ricinus communis” OR “castor bean”) 
AND (poisoning OR intoxication OR toxicity OR fatal 
OR death) AND (clinical manifestations OR symptoms 
OR toxicokinetics OR biomarkers) AND (autopsy OR 
pathology OR postmortem OR histopathology) AND 
(forensic OR medico-legal OR legal implications OR 
criminal))
Classification of exposure routes
Given the substantial heterogeneity of reported cases, stud -
ies were categorized according to the route of exposure. This 
approach was considered essential because the toxicokinetic 
behavior, clinical evolution, and pathological consequences 
of ricin poisoning vary considerably depending on how the 
toxin enters the body.
Cases involving injection were evaluated separately 
because direct inoculation into tissues or the bloodstream 
results in rapid systemic distribution and is typically associ-
ated with severe toxicity and a high fatality rate. Oral expo-
sure cases, most frequently resulting from the ingestion of 
castor beans or ricin-containing preparations, were analyzed 
as a distinct category because they are characterized by vari-
able gastrointestinal absorption, delayed symptom onset, 
and a different pattern of systemic involvement. Less com -
mon routes of exposure, including inhalational and dermal 
contact, were grouped together owing to the limited num -
ber of documented cases. Despite their rarity, these routes 
remain highly relevant from a public health and bioterror -
ism preparedness perspective.
This classification framework enabled a structured 
comparison of clinical manifestations, toxicological find -
ings, and pathological alterations across different exposure 
modalities while also facilitating the identification of com -
mon features shared among fatal cases.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Strict eligibility criteria were established to ensure the meth-
odological consistency and clinical relevance of the studies 
included in this review. Eligible publications comprised 
original research articles, case reports, case series, obser -
vational studies, experimental toxicology investigations, 
and systematic reviews or meta-analyses. Studies were 
considered suitable for inclusion when they described fatal 
or near-fatal human ricin poisoning and provided detailed 
information regarding clinical manifestations, toxicological 
biomarkers, autopsy findings, histopathological alterations, 
or medico-legal implications. Human subjects of any age, 
sex, or ethnic background were eligible, provided that the 
diagnosis of fatal ricin poisoning had been analytically con-
firmed or was strongly supported by clinical, toxicological, 
and circumstantial evidence.
Included studies were also required to report at least one 
relevant outcome measure, such as the temporal progression 
of clinical symptoms, qualitative or quantitative toxicologi-
cal findings in biological fluids or postmortem tissues, his -
topathological observations, or information relevant to the 
determination of the cause and manner of death. Only peer-
reviewed articles published in scientific journals, available 
in full text, and written in English were considered eligible 
for data extraction.
Studies were excluded when they focused exclusively on 
animal models or in vitro experiments without direct rel -
evance to human fatal poisoning. Reports describing non-
fatal exposures without life-threatening manifestations were 
not considered eligible because the objective of the review 
was to characterize fatal and near-fatal intoxications. Nar -
rative reviews, editorials, commentaries, and letters to the 
editor lacking original case data were excluded. In instances 
of duplicate publication, only the most comprehensive and 
detailed report was retained for analysis. Articles providing 
insufficient clinical, toxicological, pathological, or autopsy 
information to support a diagnosis of ricin poisoning were 
also excluded. Likewise, conference abstracts, scientific 
communications without a corresponding peer-reviewed 
full-text publication, and manuscripts available exclusively 
through preprint repositories were not considered eligible 
for inclusion.
Study selection process
Study selection was conducted according to PRISMA rec -
ommendations to ensure a transparent and reproducible 
review process. Two independent reviewers screened all 
retrieved records at the title and abstract level according to 
the predefined eligibility criteria. Any discrepancies arising 
during the screening process were resolved through discus-
sion and consensus.
All references were imported into reference manage -
ment software (Zotero) to facilitate record organiza -
tion and duplicate removal. The initial database search 
identified 240 potentially relevant records. Following 
the removal of 84 duplicate entries, 156 unique records 
remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 92 
records were excluded because they did not meet the 
eligibility criteria, most commonly due to their focus 
on non-human studies, non-fatal intoxications, or topics 
unrelated to ricin poisoning.
The full texts of the remaining potentially eligible stud -
ies were subsequently assessed. Although 64 articles were 
initially considered for full-text review, only 51 could be 
retrieved despite repeated attempts through institutional 
access, interlibrary loan services, and author contact. The 
remaining 13 articles were therefore excluded because the 
full text was unavailable.
Full-text evaluation led to the exclusion of an additional 
41 studies. The most frequent reasons for exclusion included 
the absence of original case data, insufficient clinical, toxi -
cological, or autopsy information, duplicate publication of 
previously reported cases, conference abstracts lacking a 
corresponding peer-reviewed full-text article, and articles 
published in languages that could not be reliably translated 
for data extraction purposes.
Ultimately, 9 studies fulfilled all predefined eligibility 
criteria and were included in the final qualitative synthesis. 
The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA 
flow diagram presented in Fig. 1.
Quality assessment and limitations
The methodological quality of the included studies was 
assessed using a modified version of the Joanna Briggs 
Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports 
and Case Series. The appraisal focused on the clarity of the 
clinical history, the adequacy of laboratory investigations, 
including toxicological confirmation of ricin exposure, the 
completeness of autopsy and histopathological descriptions, 
and the discussion of relevant medico-legal aspects. Based 
on these criteria, each study was categorized as having a 
low, moderate, or high risk of bias. The results of the meth-
odological quality assessment are summarized in Supple -
mentary Table S2.
Studies were not excluded solely on the basis of method-
ological quality. Instead, the quality assessment was used to 
support the interpretation of findings and to identify poten -
tial sources of bias and uncertainty within the available 
evidence.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The restric-
tion to English-language publications may have introduced 
language bias, potentially excluding relevant cases reported 
in other languages. Second, publication bias is likely to have 
affected the available literature, as unusual or particularly 
severe cases are more likely to be reported than cases with 
negative, inconclusive, or less distinctive findings. Also, the 
limited number of eligible studies precluded quantitative 
meta-analysis and restricted the generalizability of the find-
ings. Finally, considerable variability in reporting quality 
and completeness among studies limited the availability of 
uniform data across all variables of interest.
Results
The temporal distribution of publications demonstrates a 
persistent, although relatively limited, scientific interest in 
fatal ricin poisoning. Most of the available evidence origi -
nated from studies published before 2019, with five of the 
nine included studies appearing during this period. The 
remaining studies were published between 2019 and 2026. 
Although the overall volume of research remains small, the 
continued publication of new studies suggests an ongoing 
interest in ricin poisoning, driven in part by advances in 
analytical toxicology, improvements in biomarker detec -
tion, and persistent concerns regarding bioterrorism and 
public safety.
A notable finding emerging from this review is the absence 
of large-scale epidemiological investigations or multicenter 
studies specifically focused on fatal ricin poisoning. The 
predominance of isolated case reports and small case series 
reflects both the rarity of documented cases and the practi -
cal difficulties associated with studying a relatively uncom-
mon and often clandestine form of poisoning. Nevertheless, 
the continued publication of recent studies indicates that 
research efforts remain active, particularly in areas related 
to novel analytical methodologies, biomarker identification, 
postmortem toxicology, and medico-legal investigation.
Despite this sustained interest, the identification of only 
nine eligible studies over several decades highlights the 
limited nature of the available evidence. This observation 
underscores the need for collaborative multicenter initiatives 
capable of collecting standardized clinical, toxicological,

findings of this review, as it represents an important source

presentation, toxicological confirmation, pathological find -

Injection exposure: Rapid clinical progression and 
ricinine as a key biomarker
Cases involving parenteral administration provide a unique 
opportunity to examine the most rapid and severe manifes -
tations of ricin poisoning. Unlike oral exposure, injection 
bypasses gastrointestinal barriers and delivers the toxin 
directly into tissues or the systemic circulation, result -
ing in greater bioavailability and accelerated toxic effects. 
This route is of particular clinical and forensic importance 
because it has been associated with homicidal attacks, sui -
cidal attempts, and deliberate bioterrorism-related incidents. 
The studies included in this review [12–15, 17, 18, 20] con-
sistently describe a characteristic syndrome characterized 
by rapid clinical deterioration, reproducible toxicological 
findings, and distinctive pathological features.
Overall, injection-related poisoning showed a relatively 
homogeneous clinical presentation across the included 
studies. The most frequently reported manifestations 
included local inflammatory reactions at the injection site, 
early gastrointestinal symptoms, rapidly progressive hypo -
tension, refractory shock, systemic inflammatory response 
syndrome, and multiple organ failure. Despite differences

into highly vascularized tissues or the bloodstream.

Ingestion exposure: Delayed onset, variable 
severity, and the diagnostic role of ricinine
Accidental ricin poisoning most commonly occurs follow -
ing the ingestion of castor beans, particularly among chil -
dren and individuals attempting self-harm. Unlike injection 
exposure, which delivers the toxin directly into tissues or 
the bloodstream, oral exposure subjects ricin to the hostile 
environment of the gastrointestinal tract. For toxicity to 
occur, the protective outer coating of the castor bean must 
be disrupted through chewing or mechanical fragmentation, 
allowing the toxin to be released. Furthermore, ricin is a 
large water-soluble protein that may undergo partial deg -
radation by gastric acid and digestive enzymes, resulting 
in highly variable and often incomplete absorption. Con -
sequently, oral poisoning is generally characterized by a 
delayed onset of symptoms, a more heterogeneous clinical 
presentation, and a lower fatality rate compared with injec -
tion-related exposures.
Exposure circumstances also differed substantially, 
including suicidal, accidental and homicidal poisonings, 
potentially contributing to variability in clinical presentation.
Compared with injection exposure, ingestion-related 
poisoning showed greater variability in clinical severity and 
outcome across the included studies. Gastrointestinal mani-
festations represented the predominant clinical presentation, 
whereas severe cardiovascular complications were reported 
only in isolated cases. The available evidence also suggests 
that disease severity is influenced by toxin absorption, the 
integrity of the castor bean seed coat, and the promptness of 
supportive treatment.
The studies included in this review [ 16, 17, 19] collec-
tively illustrate these features while also emphasizing the 
diagnostic importance of ricinine as a biomarker of expo -
sure. Gastrointestinal manifestations represent the hallmark 
of ricin ingestion and typically develop within several hours 
after exposure. Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and diar-
rhea are the most frequently reported symptoms, although 
the severity of illness varies considerably depending on the 
amount of toxin absorbed.
A representative pediatric case was reported from Nepal 
[16], although only limited clinical details were available. 
More detailed information was provided by the adult case 
described by Steinacher et al. [ 19], involving a 23-year-old 
woman who intentionally ingested approximately 15 cas -
tor beans. Shortly after presentation, she developed a wide-
complex tachycardia with alternating QRS morphology 
consistent with bidirectional ventricular tachycardia. This 
severe arrhythmia occurred in association with gastroin -
testinal symptoms, agitation, and probable aspiration, ulti -
mately necessitating endotracheal intubation. This case is 
particularly noteworthy because it demonstrates that severe 
cardiac toxicity may occasionally dominate the clinical pic-
ture, expanding the recognized spectrum of ricin poisoning 
beyond gastrointestinal injury and systemic inflammatory 
manifestations.
Compared with injection exposure, outcomes following 
ingestion appear substantially more variable. While many 
patients recover with supportive treatment, fatal cases have 
also been documented. Conversely, the patient described 
by Steinacher et al. [ 19] survived despite experiencing 
life-threatening cardiac complications. Following intensive 
supportive management, including airway protection and 
hemodynamic stabilization, the arrhythmia resolved and the 
patient eventually recovered completely after a prolonged 
hospitalization. These findings suggest that aggressive sup-
portive care may substantially improve outcomes even in 
severe ingestion-related poisonings.
The mixed-exposure case described by Røen et al. [ 17], 
involving both oral ingestion and injection, presents addi -
tional interpretative challenges. Although the patient died 
approximately 38 h after exposure, the relative contribu -
tion of the ingested dose cannot be accurately determined 
because systemic toxicity was likely dominated by the 
injected ricin. Consequently, conclusions regarding inges -
tion-specific toxicodynamics should be drawn cautiously 
from this case.
Supportive care remains the cornerstone of treatment 
for ricin poisoning. However, a specific anti-ricin antibody 
therapy (Ricimed ®, Fabentech) has recently received mar -
keting authorization in France for the emergency treatment

outcomes may be achieved even in resource-limited set -

therapeutic information was provided in the case reported 
by Steinacher et al. [ 19]. Activated charcoal was admin -
istered in an attempt to reduce further gastrointestinal 
absorption, while intravenous sodium bicarbonate was 
used to correct metabolic acidosis and assist in arrhythmia 
management. Multiple electrical cardioversions and intra -
venous amiodarone failed to achieve immediate rhythm 
control. Nevertheless, the patient spontaneously converted 
to sinus rhythm after approximately two hours of contin -
ued supportive treatment, suggesting that toxin redistribu -
tion and physiological recovery may have contributed more 
substantially to clinical improvement than antiarrhythmic 
interventions alone. Recurrent episodes of non-sustained 
ventricular tachycardia and atrial fibrillation further sup -
port the hypothesis that ricin may exert direct cardiotoxic 
effects, a phenomenon that has been more frequently dis -
cussed in injection-related poisoning but remains relatively 
underreported following ingestion.
 Confirmation of ricin ingestion relies primarily on the 
detection of ricinine in biological specimens. It should 
be emphasized, however, that ricinine is a biomarker of 
exposure to castor beans rather than to purified ricin itself. 
Consequently, in scenarios involving highly purified ricin 
preparations, such as intentional criminal use or bioterror -
ism, ricinine may be absent or present only in negligible 
amounts. In such circumstances, laboratory confirmation 
requires the direct detection of ricin using specific ana -
lytical techniques, including immunological assays (e.g., 
ELISA), mass spectrometry–based methods, or functional 
assays capable of demonstrating the biological activity of 
the toxin. The mixed-exposure case described by Røen 
et al. [ 17] provided valuable quantitative information 
regarding ricinine kinetics. In that patient, serum ricinine 
concentrations decreased from approximately 33 ng/mL 
to 23 ng/mL between 10 and 29 h after exposure, whereas 
urinary concentrations ranged from 20 to 58 ng/mL. Peak 
urinary concentrations were observed between 12 and 29 h 
post-exposure, further supporting the role of renal elimina -
tion as the principal clearance pathway. However, available 
data on human ricinine pharmacokinetics remain limited 
and are derived from a small number of published case 
reports. Although these data must be interpreted cautiously 
because of the concomitant injection exposure, they provide 
a useful reference framework for the interpretation of rici -
nine concentrations in human poisoning cases.
The studies reviewed consistently highlight the impor -
tance of rapid ricinine detection as a diagnostic tool. This is 
particularly relevant in pediatric patients and in situations 
where a reliable exposure history is unavailable. Early iden-
tification of ricin poisoning may facilitate timely support -
ive management, prevent diagnostic delays, and reduce the 
need for unnecessary investigations aimed at more common 
infectious or toxicological conditions. These considerations 
are especially important in geographical regions where cas-
tor plants are widespread and accidental exposures remain 
possible. Consequently, the development of rapid analytical 
platforms, including point-of-care assays and highly sensi -
tive LC–MS/MS methods for ricinine detection, should be 
regarded as a priority for both clinical toxicology and public 
health preparedness (Table 3 and 4).
Discussion
The evidence synthesized in this systematic review sug -
gests a relatively consistent clinical characterization of fatal 
ricin poisoning. Despite differences in study design, sample 
size, reporting quality, and route of exposure, the available 
literature suggests the presence of a relatively consistent 
clinical pattern characterized by a progression from early 
nonspecific symptoms to shock, multiple organ failure, and 
death. Although the timing and severity of manifestations 
vary according to the route of exposure, injection-related

One of the most important findings emerging from this 
review is the consistent detection of ricinine in all stud -
ies that performed toxicological confirmation. Ricinine 
appeared to be a reliable biomarker of ricin exposure across 
multiple biological matrices, including serum, whole 
blood, urine, vitreous humour, bile, and liver tissue. Vari -
ous analytical platforms, including LC–MS/MS, GC–MS, 
and HPLC, demonstrated excellent sensitivity and speci -
ficity, with reported detection limits as low as 50 pg/mL 
in whole blood [ 12]. The analytical performance of these 
methods, together with the relative stability of ricinine in 
biological specimens, supports its value as a preferred bio -
marker for confirming ricin exposure in both clinical and 
forensic settings.
Although ricinine emerged as the most consistently 
validated biomarker, other laboratory markers may pro -
vide complementary diagnostic information. Detection of 
ricin A-chain offers direct evidence of toxin exposure but 
has been reported only in a limited number of studies. Con-
versely, inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α) and 
markers of organ injury such as troponin or creatine kinase 
primarily reflect the host response and tissue damage rather 
than confirming exposure. Therefore, these biomarkers 
should be regarded as complementary rather than alterna -
tive tools, whereas ricinine currently remains the reference 
biomarker for laboratory confirmation of ricin poisoning.
The quantitative toxicological data reported by several 
studies also provide valuable insight into ricinine toxicoki -
netics. Serial measurements described by Røen et al. [ 17] 
and Verougstraete et al. [20] suggest a biphasic elimination 
pattern characterized by an initial rapid distribution phase 
followed by a slower elimination phase. Urinary ricinine 
concentrations were generally higher than corresponding 
serum concentrations, reflecting the predominant role of 
renal excretion in ricinine clearance. These findings are par-
ticularly relevant for forensic investigations, as they may 
assist in the interpretation of postmortem toxicological 
results and estimation of the interval between exposure and 
death. Although elevated ricinine concentrations appeared 
to be associated with more severe poisoning and rapid clini-
cal deterioration in some cases [ 20], the limited number of 
available observations precludes the establishment of defin-
itive prognostic thresholds.
These observations should nevertheless be interpreted 
cautiously. As highlighted by Lefever et al., current knowl-
edge of ricinine pharmacokinetics is based on a very lim -
ited number of human cases, and no consistent correlation 
has been demonstrated between ricinine concentrations and 
poisoning severity. Therefore, ricinine should primarily be 
regarded as a biomarker of exposure rather than a validated 
prognostic biomarker [21].
Although ricinine remains the most practical biomarker 
in poisoning resulting from castor bean exposure, its diag -
nostic utility may be limited when purified ricin is used. In 
these situations, direct analytical detection of ricin becomes 
essential, particularly in forensic investigations involving 
suspected bioterrorism or deliberate criminal poisoning.
The clinical implications of these findings are substan -
tial. Ricin poisoning should be considered in the differential 
diagnosis of patients presenting with acute gastrointestinal 
symptoms, rapidly progressive shock, and multiple organ 
dysfunction, particularly when conventional diagnostic 
investigations fail to identify an infectious or toxicological 
cause. The studies reviewed demonstrate that ricin poison -
ing can mimic several common medical conditions, most 
notably septic shock. This overlap is further complicated 
by the inflammatory response induced by ricin, which may

whenever ricin exposure is suspected.
From a therapeutic perspective, supportive care remains 
the cornerstone of management. Recently, a specific anti-
ricin antibody therapy (Ricimed ®, Fabentech) received 
marketing authorization in France for the treatment of con -
firmed or strongly suspected acute ricin poisoning. However, 
because all the cases included in this review occurred before 
its availability, supportive care remained the only thera -
peutic option described in the published literature. Current 
treatment is largely supportive and focuses on airway pro -
tection, fluid resuscitation, hemodynamic stabilization, cor-
rection of metabolic disturbances, and management of organ 
dysfunction. Nevertheless, the survival of several severely 
poisoned patients demonstrates that recovery remains pos -
sible when supportive measures are instituted promptly and 
aggressively. Early recognition therefore remains the most 
important determinant of clinical outcome.
The findings of this review also have important impli -
cations for forensic pathology. Although detailed autopsy 
descriptions were available only in a limited number of 
studies, a relatively consistent pattern of pathological find -
ings emerged. Frequently reported observations included 
hemorrhagic gastrointestinal injury, hepatic necrosis, renal 
tubular damage, pulmonary edema, and serosal effusions, 
collectively supporting the hypothesis that endothelial 
injury and capillary leak syndrome play a central role in the 
pathophysiology of fatal ricin poisoning. The detection of 
ricinine in postmortem tissues, including vitreous humour, 
liver tissue, and bile, further strengthens its value as a foren-
sic biomarker, particularly in cases where conventional 
specimens such as blood or urine are unavailable. More -
over, the relatively homogeneous postmortem distribution 
of ricinine described in some studies suggests that multiple 
tissues may be suitable for toxicological confirmation. How-
ever, the limited number of detailed autopsy investigations 
currently available highlights the need for standardized 
postmortem sampling protocols that include blood, urine, 
vitreous humour, bile, liver tissue, and, when appropriate, 
tissue from the suspected injection site.
Ricin poisoning also presents significant medico-legal 
challenges. The nonspecific clinical presentation and its 
resemblance to septic shock or other toxic syndromes may 
complicate both clinical diagnosis and forensic investiga -
tion. In the absence of a known exposure history, cases may 
remain unrecognized unless targeted toxicological testing 
is performed. This issue is particularly important given the 
historical use of ricin in homicidal attacks and its continued 
relevance as a potential bioterrorism agent. The cases iden-
tified in this review demonstrate the diverse circumstances 
under which ricin poisoning may occur, including acci -
dental exposures, suicidal attempts, and deliberate crimi -
nal acts. Consequently, the confirmation of ricin exposure 
should prompt not only appropriate medical management 
but also a thorough forensic and public health investigation 
aimed at identifying the source of exposure and preventing 
additional cases.
Several limitations should be considered when interpret-
ing the findings of this review. First, the available evidence 
remains limited and is derived predominantly from case 
reports and small case series, restricting the generalizabil -
ity of the conclusions and precluding quantitative meta-
analysis. Second, publication bias is likely, as unusual or 
severe cases are more likely to be reported than mild or 
diagnostically uncertain presentations. Third, restricting the 
review to English-language publications may have resulted 
in language bias and the exclusion of relevant reports from 
regions where castor plants are endemic. Fourth, substantial 
heterogeneity in reporting quality, clinical detail, toxico -
logical analysis, and pathological investigation limited the 
availability of comparable data across studies. Finally, the 
absence of documented human inhalational poisoning cases 
represents a major gap in the literature, particularly given 
the longstanding concern regarding the potential aerosoliza-
tion of ricin in bioterrorism scenarios.
An additional source of uncertainty derives from the het-
erogeneity of the available evidence. The included studies 
differed substantially with respect to exposure route, ricin 
preparation, administered dose, timing of clinical presen -
tation, analytical techniques used for toxicological confir -
mation, and the completeness of clinical and pathological 
reporting. These differences limited direct comparisons 
between studies and precluded quantitative synthesis or 
formal meta-analysis. Consequently, the conclusions of this 
review should be interpreted as a qualitative synthesis of the 
currently available.
Also, because this review deliberately focused on fatal 
and near-fatal human poisonings to address specific med -
ico-legal and forensic questions, its findings should not be 
interpreted as representative of the entire clinical spectrum 
of ricin intoxication.
The review also identifies several priorities for future 
research. Large multicenter studies incorporating standard -
ized clinical, toxicological, and pathological data collection 
are urgently needed to strengthen the evidence base. Such

next-generation monoclonal antibodies and other anti-ricin

Conclusions
This systematic review integrates the currently available 
evidence on fatal ricin poisoning and suggests the presence 
of a relatively consistent clinicopathological pattern char -
acterized by rapid progression from early gastrointestinal 
and systemic symptoms to shock, multiple organ failure, 
and death. Although this progression may occur following 
different routes of exposure, injection-related poisonings 
consistently exhibited a more rapid and severe course than 
ingestion-related cases.
Across the studies included in this review, ricinine 
appears to be the most consistently validated biomarker of 
exposure currently available. Its detection in multiple bio -
logical matrices provides robust confirmation of ricin expo-
sure, although further studies are needed to better define 
its relationship with exposure timing and clinical severity. 
These findings suggest an important role of ricinine analysis 
in both clinical toxicology and forensic pathology.
Clinicians should maintain awareness of ricin poison -
ing in patients presenting with unexplained gastrointestinal 
illness, rapidly progressive shock, and multiple organ dys -
function, particularly when infectious causes cannot fully 
explain the clinical picture. Early recognition and prompt 
supportive treatment remain essential. Although a specific 
anti-ricin antibody therapy has recently become available in 
France, none of the cases included in this review received 
this treatment because they predated its clinical introduc -
tion. Forensic pathologists may benefit from the incorpo -
ration of ricinine testing into postmortem investigations of 
unexplained deaths associated with shock, organ failure, or 
suspicious circumstances.
The inclusion of studies published up to June 2026 con -
firms that ricin poisoning continues to attract scientific and 
forensic attention, particularly in relation to advances in 
analytical toxicology, biomarker identification, and post -
mortem investigation. Nevertheless, important knowledge 
gaps remain, including the lack of standardized postmortem 
sampling protocols, limited information regarding lethal 
ricinine concentrations, and the scarcity of human data on 
inhalational exposure. Addressing these gaps through collab-
orative multicenter research will be essential for improving 
diagnosis, forensic investigation, clinical management, and 
public health preparedness in future cases of ricin poisoning.
