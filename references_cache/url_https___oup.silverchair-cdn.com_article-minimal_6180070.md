<h2>Abstract</h2>
<section><p>Sensory neuronopathies are a rare and distinct subgroup of peripheral neuropathies, characterized by degeneration of the dorsal root ganglia neurons. About 50% of sensory neuronopathies are idiopathic and genetic causes remain to be clarified. Through a combination of homozygosity mapping and whole exome sequencing, we linked an autosomal recessive sensory neuronopathy to pathogenic variants in the <em>COX20</em> gene. We identified eight unrelated families from the eastern Chinese population carrying a founder variant c.41A&gt;G (p.Lys14Arg) within <em>COX20</em> in either a homozygous or compound heterozygous state. All patients displayed sensory ataxia with a decrease in non-length-dependent sensory potentials. <em>COX20</em> encodes a key transmembrane protein implicated in the assembly of mitochondrial complex IV. We showed that <em>COX20</em> variants lead to reduction of COX20 protein in patient’s fibroblasts and transfected cell lines, consistent with a loss-of-function mechanism. Knockdown of <em>COX20</em> expression in ND7/23 sensory neuron cells resulted in complex IV deficiency and perturbed assembly of complex IV, which subsequently compromised cell spare respiratory capacity and reduced cell proliferation under metabolic stress. Consistent with mitochondrial dysfunction in knockdown cells, reduced complex IV assembly, enzyme activity and oxygen consumption rate were also found in patients’ fibroblasts. We speculated that the mechanism of <em>COX20</em> was similar to other causative genes (e.g. <em>SURF1</em>, <em>COX6A1</em>, <em>COA3</em> and <em>SCO2</em>) for peripheral neuropathies, all of which are functionally important in the structure and assembly of complex IV. Our study identifies a novel causative gene for the autosomal recessive sensory neuronopathy, whose vital function in complex IV and high expression in the proprioceptive sensory neuron further underlines loss of COX20 contributing to mitochondrial bioenergetic dysfunction as a mechanism in peripheral sensory neuron disease.</p></section> <div></div>
<div><a>sensory neuronopathy</a>, <a>COX20 gene</a>, <a>complex IV deficiency</a>, <a>loss of function</a></div> <h2>Introduction</h2>
<p>Sensory neuronopathies, also called sensory neuron disease or ganglionopathy, are a rare and distinct subgroup of clinically and genetically heterogeneous peripheral neuropathies, whose symptoms vary and depend on the type of primary sensory neurons involved in the dorsal root ganglion.<span></span><a><sup>1</sup></a> Damage to small-dark cells with unmyelinated (C) nerve fibres results in superficial sensory loss or neuropathic pain. While damage to large-light cells with heavily and moderately myelinated A-fibres (Aα and Aβ, respectively) results in abnormal proprioception, depressed or absent reflexes and sensory ataxia.<span></span><a><sup>2</sup></a> Sensory neuronopathies are commonly caused by various acquired pathologies, including paraneoplastic disease, immune-mediated disease, toxic causes, infections.<span></span><a><sup>3</sup></a> Sensory neuronopathies also occur in several genetic and degenerative diseases, including cerebellar ataxia with neuropathy and vestibular areflexia syndrome (CANVAS),<span></span><a><sup>4</sup></a> ataxia with vitamin E deficiency (AVED),<span></span><a><sup>5</sup></a> Friedrich’s ataxia<span></span><a><sup>6</sup></a> and some mitochondrial disorders.<span></span><a><sup>7</sup></a> However, the causes of approximately half of sensory neuronopathies remain unknown, particularly chronic and slowly progressive sensory neuronopathies.<span></span><a><sup>1</sup></a></p><p>In the past decades, multiple genes associated with mitochondrial structure and function have been linked to sensory neuronopathies, including <em>HADHA</em> (MIM: 600890) in mitochondrial fatty acids β-oxidation,<span></span><a><sup>8</sup></a><sup>,</sup><em>MT-TK</em> (MIM: 590060) encoding mitochondrial lysine transfer RNA,<span></span><a><sup>9</sup></a><sup>,</sup><em>FXN</em> (MIM: 606829) in assembly of iron-sulphur clusters,<span></span><a><sup>6</sup></a> and <em>POLG</em> (MIM: 174763) in mtDNA synthesis.<span></span><a><sup>7</sup></a> Particularly, pathogenic variants in the <em>POLG</em> have been reported to cause autosomal recessive sensory neuronopathy, of which sensory ataxia is a key feature caused by degeneration of dorsal root ganglion and posterior columns.<span></span><a><sup>7</sup></a> However, patients with inherited disease discussed above have a wide spectrum of neurological features, such as ophthalmoparesis, cardiomyopathy, myopathy, psychiatric disorders, cognitive impairment, seizures and encephalopathy. Thus, the hereditary sensory neuronopathy is only seen in very rare conditions and is easy to misdiagnose, and further exploration of the probable existence of an underlying genetic cause is needed.</p><p>COX20 (MIM: 614698) is a chaperone protein involved in the assembly of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) complex IV [cytochrome <em>c</em> oxidase (COX)], which stabilizes newly synthesized catalytic core subunit COX2 by facilitating the translocating across mitochondrial inner membrane and maintaining interaction with the ﬁrst transmembrane helix of COX2.<span></span><a><sup>10</sup></a> COX20 also interacts directly with SCO1 and SCO2 metallochaperones in maturation of COX2 redox centre.<span></span><a><sup>11</sup></a> Variants in <em>COX20</em> have been initially associated with complex IV deficiency, an autosomal recessive form of infant-onset mitochondrial disorder that is typically accompanied by growth retardation, hypotonia, and cerebellar ataxia.<span></span><a><sup>12</sup></a> Subsequent studies have broadened the phenotypic spectrum to include milder cases with childhood-onset cerebellar ataxia, dystonia, choreoathetosis and sensory axonal neuropathy.<span></span><a><sup>13</sup></a><sup>,</sup><span></span><a><sup>14</sup></a> More recently, <em>COX20</em> variants were identified in two patients from one family with sensory-dominant axonal neuropathy and static encephalopathy.<span></span><a><sup>15</sup></a></p><p>Here, we identified bi-allelic pathogenic variants within <em>COX20</em> in eight families showing sensory neuronopathy by whole exome sequencing (WES). Five different <em>COX20</em> variants were detected, which were found to be loss-of-function variants that lead to decreased levels of COX20 protein. Histopathological and functional data further indicated that loss of COX20 caused impaired mitochondrial complex IV assembly and defective OXPHOS capacity in sensory neuron cell line. Our findings identify a novel causative gene for autosomal recessive sensory neuronopathy and further support mitochondrial bioenergetic dysfunction as a mechanism in peripheral sensory neuron disease.</p> <h2>Materials and methods</h2>
<h3>Subjects</h3>
<p>The criteria for enrolment were: (i) autosomal recessive or sporadic; (ii) presence of chronic deep sensory loss or sensory ataxia; (iii) absence of other features such as upper motor neuron impairment, mental retardation, and cerebellar ataxia; (iv) electrophysiological results show non-length-dependent sensory abnormalities that fulfil diagnostic criteria for sensory neuronopathy<span></span><a><sup>16</sup></a>; (v) exclusion of acquired causes by routine clinical investigations; and (vi) pathogenic variants in the causative genes known to be associated with sensory neuronopathies or inherited peripheral neuropathies have been detected by multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA), targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS), and WES in our previous reports.<span></span><a><sup>17-21</sup></a> Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral EDTA-treated blood using the Blood Genomic Extraction Kit (Qiagen). This study was approved by the Ethics Committees in Second Affiliated Hospital in Zhejiang University School of Medicine. All participants signed informed consents prior to enrolment.</p> <h3>Genetic analysis</h3>
<p>WES was performed in patients and unaffected family members using Agilent SureSelect<sup>TM</sup> Human All Exon v6 kit for exome enrichment and the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform (XY Biotechnology Co Ltd) for sequencing. Subsequent variant calling, alignment and annotation were performed according to our previously reported protocol.<span></span><a><sup>22</sup></a><sup>,</sup><span></span><a><sup>23</sup></a> In the consanguineous Family 1, homozygosity mapping was performed by HomozygosityMapper<span></span><a><sup>24</sup></a> using WES data from the index patient (Patient II:3), her parents (Subjects I:1 and I:2), and her unaffected siblings (Subjects II:1 and II:2). As the phenotype in Family 1 is inherited in a consanguineous marriage, only those homozygous variants in the index patient that are heterozygous in parents and heterozygous or absent in any of the two unaffected siblings were analysed. Variants were filtered based on a minor allele frequency &lt;1% in 1000 Genomes Project,<span></span><a><sup>25</sup></a> ExAc<span></span><a><sup>26</sup></a> and gnomAD<span></span><a><sup>27</sup></a> databases and functional impact on protein change (non-synonymous and/or affecting mRNA splicing variants). Using an overlap-based strategy, we identified the potential causative genes shared by multiple families. Sanger sequencing was performed to validate the potential variants and co-segregation (all primers are listed in <span></span><span><a>Supplementary Table 1</a></span>). Haplotype analysis was conducted by genotyping seven single nucleotide polymorphism markers ﬂanking <em>COX20</em> c.41A&gt;G and covering a region of 0.1 Mb.</p> <h3>Analysis of gene expression</h3>
<p>Transcriptomic tissue data generated by the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project were used for the expression analysis of <em>COX20</em> in human tissues.<span></span><a><sup>28</sup></a> Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of the adult mouse brain database (<a>http://mousebrain.org/</a>; accessed: 18 January 2021),<span></span><a><sup>29</sup></a> and adult mouse sensory neurons focusing on cell types in dorsal root ganglia (<a>http://linnarssonlab.org/drg/</a>; accessed: 18 January 2021).<span></span><a><sup>30</sup></a> were used to analyse the expression distribution patterns of <em>COX20</em> and other causative genes associated with sensory neuropathy.</p> <h3>Muscle biopsy and histochemical staining</h3>
<p>Skeletal muscle biopsy of the left biceps brachii muscle was performed in Patients 1 and 4. Skeletal muscle biopsy of the right gastrocnemius muscle and quadriceps femoris muscle were performed in Patient 3 and Patient 5, respectively. Muscle samples were precooled using isopentane and cryofixed in liquid nitrogen. Transverse sections (8-μm thick) were obtained for routine and enzyme histochemical staining.</p> <h3>Transmission electron microscopy</h3>
<p>The muscle samples were immediately fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 2 h, washed by PBS, and post-fixed in 1% OsO<sub>4</sub> for 2 h. Next, blocks were dehydrated using graded alcohols, embedded in Epon, and stained with lead citrate and uranyl acetate. Ultrathin sections were cut and viewed through a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi HT7700).</p> <h3>Expression plasmids</h3>
<p>The wild-type (WT) full-length coding region of human <em>COX20</em> cDNA (NM_198076) was cloned into pcDNA3.1-Myc/His vector (Invitrogen). Three missense <em>COX20</em> variants identified in this study (p.Lys14Arg, p.Ser33Leu, p.Trp74Cys) and the p.Thr52Pro variant reported previously were introduced into the wild-type <em>COX20</em> construct using PCR mutagenesis. After confirming the sequences, they were designated as pcDNA3.1-WT-Myc/His (wild-type), pcDNA3.1-Lys14Arg-Myc/His (K14R), pcDNA3.1-Ser33Leu-Myc/His (S33L), pcDNA3.1-Thr52Pro-Myc/His (T52P) and pcDNA3.1-Trp74Cys-Myc/His (W74C), respectively.</p> <h3>Cell culture, plasmid transfection and lentiviral transduction</h3>
<p>Primary fibroblasts were obtained from a 4 mm skin biopsy of affected patients and normal controls, grown in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12, Gibco) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (Gibco) at 37°C in 5% CO<sub>2</sub>. HEK-293T, HeLa and ND7/23 cells were cultured at 37°C under 5% CO<sub>2</sub> in DMEM (HyClone) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (Gibco). To determine whether COX20 protein expression and subcellular location were influenced by the variants, HEK-293T and HeLa cells were transfected with wild-type and mutant plasmids with Lipofectamine<sup>®</sup> 3000 reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacture’s protocol. Forty-eight hours after transfection, transfected cells were collected for further analysis. For COX20 protein stability assay, at 48 h post-transfection, cells were treated with 100 μg/ml cycloheximide (CHX; Sigma-Aldrich) for 0, 3, 6, and 12 h to evaluate the half-life of the COX20 protein. For protein degradation assay, cells were treated with 100 μg/ml CHX and with or without MG132 (20 μM, Selleck) for 6 h. Dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO) was used as a control vehicle. Lentivirus particles with shRNA specific for <em>COX20</em> was purchased from Genomeditech Co. Ltd. ND7/23 cells were transduced with lentivirus and selected with puromycin (2.5 mg/ml) for 4 days. Two cell clones named siCOX20-1 and siCOX20-2 were generated for further experiments.</p> <h3>RNA isolation, quantitation and splicing analysis</h3>
<p>For RNA quantification study, total RNA was extracted from control and patient fibroblasts and synthesis of cDNA was performed using the PrimeScript™ II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara). Quantitative real-time PCR experiments were further performed using SYBR<sup>®</sup> Premix Ex Taq<sup>TM</sup> Kit (Takara) by means of relative quantification (2<sup>−ΔΔCt</sup> method). The primer sequences are available in <span></span><span><a>Supplementary Table 1</a></span>. To examine the effect of the c.157+7A&gt;G variant on mRNA splicing, minigene vectors containing either mutant or wild-type <em>COX20</em> with exon 2 to exon 3 and flanking intron sequence were cloned in pSPL3 vector and transfected into HEK-293T cells<em>.</em> RT-PCR products were visualized in agarose gels and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. To examine the effect of the c.41A&gt;G variant on mRNA splicing, RT-PCR was performed to amplify full length <em>COX20</em> transcript using patient fibroblast-derived cDNA. PCR products were visualized using agarose gels and then cloned into pGM-simple-T TA vector (TIANGEN). The vector was transformed into DH5α cells (TIANGEN), and individual colonies were examined and sequenced by Sanger sequencing.</p> <h3>Mitochondrial extraction and membrane fractionation</h3>
<p>Mitochondria isolation from cultured cells was performed using mitochondria isolation kit (Beyotime). To separate the peripherally bound membrane proteins and integral membrane proteins, 40 µg freshly isolated mitochondria were resuspended in a buffer containing 0.1 M Na<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub>, 250 mM sucrose and 0.2 mM EDTA (pH 10.5), incubated for 1 h on ice with gentle nutation, and then centrifuged at 20 000<em>g</em> for 30 min at 4°C, to separate the supernatant containing the loosely bound membrane proteins and the pellet containing integral membrane proteins. Supernatant and pellet protein samples were analysed by sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and immunoblotting.</p><p>For sub-mitochondrial localization, 40 µg freshly isolated mitochondria were resuspended in isotonic buffer containing 250 mM sucrose, 0.2 mM EDTA and 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), treated with increasing concentrations of digitonin (0%, 0.1%, 0.125%, 0.150%, 0.175%, 0.20% and 0.25%) for 1 h at 4°C and then centrifuged at 20 000<em>g</em> for 30 min at 4°C, to separate the soluble and insoluble fractions. Soluble and insoluble protein samples were analysed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting.</p> <h3>SDS-PAGE and blue native gel electrophoresis and immunoblotting</h3>
<p>Protein samples from HEK-293T cells, fibroblasts and ND7/23 cells were separated by 10% or 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Blue native PAGE (BN-PAGE) was performed as previously described.<span></span><a><sup>31</sup></a> Briefly, mitochondria were isolated from cells using mitochondria isolation kit (Beyotime). Fifty micrograms of mitochondrial protein were separated on 4 to 16% Bis-Tris gradient gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and transferred onto PVDF membranes. For in-gel measurement of complex activity, gels were incubated with substrate solution overnight at room temperature. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-COX20 (Abcam; ab224570), anti-NDUFA9 (Abclonal; A3196), anti-NDUFA13 (Abcam; ab110240), anti-SDHA (Abcam; ab14715), anti-UQCRC2 (Abcam; ab14745), anti-COX1 (Abcam; ab14705), anti-COX2 (Abcam; ab79393), anti-ATP5A (Abcam; ab14748), anti-VDAC1 (Abcam; ab14734), anti-TOM20 (Cell Signaling Technology; 42406S), anti-Myc (Cell Signaling Technology; 2276S), anti-GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology; 8884S), and anti-β-Tubulin (Cell Signaling Technology; 5346S). The grey values of the bands were analysed using Adobe Photoshop software.</p> <h3>Immunofluorescence analysis</h3>
<p>HeLa cells transiently transfected with Myc-tagged COX20 were cultured in glass-bottomed dishes. After 24 h of transfection, cells were ﬁxed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature and blocked in PBS containing 0.1% Triton<sup>TM</sup> X-100 and 5% bovine serum albumin for 60 min. Cells were then incubated with anti-Myc (1:1000, Sigma) antibody and anti-COX2 (1:200, Abcam) in the blocking buffer at 4°C overnight, followed by secondary anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor<sup>®</sup> 488 and anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor<sup>®</sup> 596 antibodies (1:1000, Life Technologies). Cell nuclei were then stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1:5000, Life Technologies). Fluorescence images were captured by Olympus FV3000 confocal system.</p> <h3>High-resolution respirometry</h3>
<p>The Oxygraph-2k (OROBOROS Instruments) was used for measuring mitochondrial respiration.<span></span><a><sup>32</sup></a> For measuring oxygen consumption rate(OCR), 2 million cells were added to each chamber filled with 2 ml of cell culture media. Oligomycin, carbonyl cyanide 4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP), and rotenone + antimycin A were injected sequentially to each chamber. Oligomycin inhibited ATP synthase and provided information on decreased OCR (ATP-linked respiration). FCCP uncoupled oxygen consumption from ATP production and enabled maximal rates of electron transport to occur to raise OCR to a maximal value (maximal respiration capacity). Rotenone and antimycin A blocked complexes I and III and reduced OCR to a minimal value (non-mitochondrial respiration). For measuring mitochondrial complex I, II+III, IV associated oxygen consumption rate, 50 µg freshly isolated mitochondria were added to each chamber. Substrate-uncoupler-inhibitor-titration (SUIT) protocols were assayed as detailed elsewhere.<span></span><a><sup>32</sup></a> In brief, with the presence of pyruvate, malate and glutamate (PMG), substrate-driven OCR was measured for complex I (addition of ADP followed by rotenone, an inhibitor of complex I), complex II+III (addition of succinate followed by antimycin A, an inhibitor of complex III), and complex IV [addition of <em>N</em>, <em>N</em>, <em>N</em>′, <em>N</em>′-tetramethyl-p-phenylenediamine (TMPD)/ascorbate followed by azide, an inhibitor of complex IV].</p> <h3>Assessment of mitochondrial membrane potential</h3>
<p>Cells were stained with 100 nM tetramethyl rhodamine methyl ester (TMRM; Invitrogen) for 0.5 h at 37°C and the ﬂuorescence signal was detected with Olympus fluorescence microscope (excitation/emission 525/590 nm). ImageJ software was used for quantification of the cell number and fluorescence intensity. Quantification was performed in four random visual fields of at least 100 cells from three independent experiments.</p> <h3>Mitochondrial respiratory complex IV activity microplate assay</h3>
<p>Abcam’s Complex IV Human Enzyme Activity Microplate Assay Kit (ab109909) were used to quantitatively measure the activity of mitochondrial respiratory complex IV from fibroblast extracts. The assay followed the protocol suggested by the manufacturer.</p> <h3>Cell proliferation and viability assay</h3>
<p>For cell proliferation assay, the cells were cultured in a 96-well plate at a density of 2000 cells/well and incubated with 25 mM glucose DMEM or no-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10 mM galactose for 4 days. The proliferation of cells in different media of glucose and galactose were assessed every 24 h using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime). Growth rate was determined by the ratio of absorbance with specified medium for 4 days to absorbance seeded initially. For cell viability assay, the cells were cultured in a 96-well plate at a density of 10 000 cells/well and incubated with hydrogen peroxide (200 μM) for 1 h before measuring the absorbance. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using SpectraMax<sup>®</sup> 190 microplate reader (Molecular Devices).</p> <h3>Statistical analysis</h3>
<p>Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). <h3>Identiﬁcation of bi-allelic <em>COX20</em> variants</h3>
<p>WES was performed in all 14 patients and homozygosity mapping was performed in the consanguineous family (Family 1 in <span></span><a>Fig. 1A</a>). A total of 28 shared homozygous regions &gt; 1 Mb were identiﬁed. We specifically looked for potential pathogenic variants located within these regions and present in multiple families. We identified a homozygous variant c.41A&gt;G (p.Lys14Arg) within <em>COX20</em> in three families (Families 1–3 in <span></span><a>Fig. 1A</a>). Compound heterozygous variants including c.41A&gt;G/c.222G&gt;T (p.Trp74Cys), c.41A&gt;G/c.98C&gt;T (p.Ser33Leu), c.41A&gt;G/c.157+7A&gt;G and c.41A&gt;G/c.259C&gt;T (p.Gln87*) were identified in five additional families, respectively (Families 4–8 in <span></span><a>Fig. 1A</a>). These variants were also confirmed by Sanger sequencing (<span></span><a>Fig. 1B</a>) and co-segregated with phenotypes within these families. Allele frequency in population and <em>in silico</em> prediction of five variants were summarized in <span></span><span><a>Supplementary Table 2</a></span>. These variants were extremely rare in global human genome databases and located at evolutionarily highly conserved amino acid residues (<span></span><a>Fig. 1C</a>). Although the c.41A&gt;G (p.Lys14Arg) variant was predicted as benign in three <em>in silico</em> missense predictions, it was predicted to cause abnormal splicing by abolishing the donor splice site of exon 1 (dbscSNV score = 0.998).<span></span><a><sup>33</sup></a></p> <a></a>

<p>The c.41A&gt;G (p.Lys14Arg) was the most common variant in our cohort and present in all eight Chinese Han families from eastern China (seven in Zhejiang province and one in Jiangxi province). The allele frequency of the variant c.41A&gt;G is 0.00132 in the Chinese population based on ChinaMAP database<span></span><a><sup>34</sup></a> (28 of 21 176 alleles examined) and 0.00103 in the eastern Chinese population based on our in-house exomes database (2 of 1934 alleles examined). In comparison, the allele frequency was 0.00003552 based on an allelic count of 5 of 140 748 genomes in the gnomAD v3 database (only one female individual of East Asian ancestry who is heterozygous for the variant), much lower than that in eastern Chinese population. The genomic data supported a hypothesis that the c.41A&gt;G was a founder variant in the eastern Chinese population. We further performed haplotype analysis and a unique haplotype of 106 kb in size containing c.41A&gt;G variant gene was identified in all eight families, suggesting it was a founder variant (<span></span><span><a>Supplementary Table 3</a></span>).</p> <h3>Clinical features</h3>
<p>Altogether, we identified eight index cases with bi-allelic variants in <em>COX20</em>. Of note, seven (87.5%) cases were sporadic, with no evidence of a family history or consanguinity. All patients had similar clinical features and electrophysiological findings characterized by sensory neuronopathy. The mean age of disease onset was 8.2 ± 6.3 (range 1–17) years with the walking difficulty as the most common complaint at onset. Four patients (50%) suffered from delayed motor milestones, and seven (87.5%) reported pes cavus (<span></span><a>Fig. 2A</a>), indicating that neuropathy probably started earlier in their life. All patients exhibited impaired balance with stomping gait. Five (62.5%) patients showed relatively mild limb weakness and 2 (25%) showed muscle atrophy in distal lower limbs (Patients 1 and 4 in <span></span><a>Fig. 2A</a>). Reduced deep tendon reﬂex and vibratory sensation, and positive Romberg sign were observed in all eight patients. MRI of spinal cord showed severe spinal cord atrophy with no intrinsic signal abnormality in Patients 2 and 5 (<span></span><a>Fig. 2B</a>). Brain MRI showed mild cerebral atrophy in Patient 4 (<span></span><span><a>Supplementary Fig. 1A</a></span>), while no remarkable change was observed in other patients. Nerve conduction studies showed global absent or severe decreased sensory nerve action potential in four limbs with normal or mildly abnormal motor nerve conduction studies (<span></span><a>Table 1</a>), indicating that ataxia symptoms were attributable to sensory ataxia. With CMT neuropathy score (CMTNS), clinical severity was mild (score 0–10) in five (62.5%), moderate (score 11–20) in two (25%), and severe (score &gt;20) in one patient. Laboratory studies showed normal lactate and pyruvate levels in blood or CSF and serum creatine kinase levels. The detailed clinical features for these patients are shown in <span></span><a>Table 2</a> and Supplementary material.</p> <a></a>

<p>Sural nerve biopsy in Patient 5 showed marked loss of large and medium myelinated fibres and chronic axonal degeneration (data not shown). Muscle samples were taken from Patients 1, 3, 4 and 5. Haematoxylin and eosin staining revealed no significant endomysial or perimysial fibrosis, no regeneration and necrosis had been found. On modiﬁed Gomori trichrome staining, neither ragged-red ﬁbres nor nemaline bodies were seen. Additionally, there were no abnormal signs in COX staining, succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) staining, or nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide-tetrazolium reductase (NADH-TR) staining (<span></span><a>Fig. 2C</a> and <span></span><span><a>Supplementary Fig. 1B–D</a></span>). Results of electron microscope analyses revealed that numerous mitochondria had abnormal internal organization with vacuoles and concentric lamellae (<span></span><a>Fig. 2D and E</a>), which was suggestive of mitochondrial abnormalities.</p> <h3>Minigene splicing analysis of c.157+7A&gt;G variant</h3>
<p>As the patient-derived RNA of c.157+7A&gt;G variant was not available, we developed a minigene assay to examine the effect of this variant on <em>COX20</em> splicing (<span></span><span><a>Supplementary Fig. 2A</a></span>). Gel electrophoresis of mutant PCR products showed a shorter band with exon 2 skipped splicing pattern confirmed by sequencing (<span></span><span><a>Supplementary Fig. 2B</a></span>). <p>To investigate whether the impairment of OXPHOS complex IV assembly affects cellular bioenergetics, we measured the OCRs in siCOX20 cells. As shown in <span></span><a>Fig. 5K and L</a>, the signals of TMRM in two COX20 silenced cells ranged from 4% to 18%, with a significant decrease against controls, indicating depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane. As shown in <span></span><a>Fig. 5M</a>, growth rate was significantly lower in siCOX20 cells than that in control cells in regular glucose medium, and further decreased in oxidative phosphorylation-promoting galactose media. <p>In this study, we provided a combination of genetic, histopathological, electrophysiological and functional evidence to support that <em>COX20</em> gene was a novel causative gene for autosomal recessive sensory neuronopathy. Genetically, missense, nonsense, and splice site variants in <em>COX20</em> were identified in eight autosomal-recessive families. They were novel or extremely rare variants in public databases and predicted to be pathogenic. These variants led to decreased level of COX20 protein, impaired mitochondrial complex IV assembly, and defective OXPHOS capacity. Patients with <em>COX20</em> variants showed early onset and slowly progressive deep sensory loss and sensory ataxia.</p><p>Genetic data from our cohort as well as from control databases suggested that the predominant pathogenic variant, c.41A&gt;G (p.Lys14Arg), with a carrier frequency of ∼2 per 1000 individuals, was the founder variant in eastern China as the most common genetic cause of in our patients. Therefore, we suggest that <em>COX20</em> should be considered in the molecular diagnostic evaluation of patients with sensory neuronopathy; the following features should help to prioritize <em>COX20</em> for genetic analysis: (i) autosomal recessive or sporadic pedigree; (ii) early-onset progressive sensory ataxia, loss of position and vibration sense, absent tendon reflexes; and (iii) nerve conduction studies show widespread loss or low amplitude of sensory nerve action potentials with non-length-dependent pattern. Motor nerve conduction study findings are normal or mildly decreased.</p><p>The most common variant c.41A&gt;G (p.Lys14Arg) was previously reported in a compound heterozygous state in four patients who presented with hypotonia, dystonia, dysarthria, ataxia, and sensory neuropathy.<span></span><a><sup>14</sup></a> More recently, the c.41A&gt;G (p.Lys14Arg) variant was identified in a Chinese family with autosomal recessive sensory-dominant axonal neuropathy and static encephalopathy characterized by dysaesthesia, limb weakness, dysarthria and cognitive deficits.<span></span><a><sup>15</sup></a> The clinical presentations of these patients showed some overlaps in central and peripheral nervous systems. However, our patients presented predominant proprioceptive sensory loss and sensory ataxia rather than a multisystem neurological impairment. Preferential expression of <em>COX20</em> in proprioceptive neurons may provide a potential explanation for the tissue-specific manifestation. However, the mechanism underlying the phenotypic variability between our patients and Turkish or American patients is still unclear. Notably, it seems that expression level of COX20 has little correlation with the interfamilial differences in phenotypes, because we have observed that almost no COX20 protein expression in our patients with homozygous c.41A&gt;G (p.Lys14Arg) variant, consistent with previous reports.<span></span><a><sup>14</sup></a><sup>,</sup><span></span><a><sup>15</sup></a> Further investigation is needed to confirm whether the expression level of COX20, race specific modifier genes or epigenetic factors play a role in the determination of phenotypes.</p><p>To date, several genes related to mitochondrial functions have been identiﬁed as causative genes of sensory neuronopathies or mitochondrial disorders with sensory neuronopathy as a key feature. For example, pathogenic variants in <em>POLG</em> have been reported to cause sensory neuronopathy by defects in mitochondrial complex I and IV deficiency secondary to mtDNA deletion and depletion.<span></span><a><sup>7</sup></a> COX20, as a nuclear-encoded assembly factor, plays an important role in mitochondrial complex IV function. Previous studies have shown that TALENs-mediated knockout of COX20 in HEK-293T cells results in a profound decrease in complex IV levels and activity by destabilizing COX2 and subsequently blocking the incorporation of COX2 into the complex IV assembly S2 intermediate.<span></span><a><sup>11</sup></a> Consistent with these findings, we showed that knockdown of COX20 caused impaired COX assembly and reduced COX activity in ND7/23 cells. In addition, a recent study showed that deletion of COX20 in yeast cells resulted in intolerance to hydrogen peroxide induced oxidative stress and programmed cell death.<span></span><a><sup>35</sup></a> In our cell model, we have also found that COX20 deficiency induced slow cell growth and decreased cell viability under oxidative stress. Thus, we hypothesized that the pathogenic variants of <em>COX20</em> are associated with the disease by affecting the assembly and maturation of COX with a reduction of complex IV activity, mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization, compromised respiration and tolerance to oxidative stress by loss of COX20 protein. We here provided preliminary evidence to support mitochondrial dysfunction having a direct effect on the pathophysiology of peripheral neuropathies.</p><p>Although the precise molecular mechanisms linking COX deficiency to sensory neuronopathy remain to be clarified, studies on complex IV deficiency with neuropathy or Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease causative genes encoding COX associated mitochondrial proteins such as <em>COA3</em>, <em>COA7</em>, <em>SURF1</em>, <em>SCO2</em> and <em>COX6A1</em> suggested that impaired axonal transport, oxidative stress and defective mitochondrial copper homeostasis might be involved.<span></span><a><sup>36–40</sup></a> Further work is needed to clarify the role of <em>COX20</em> in causing the neurodegeneration of the large proprioceptive sensory neurons and to precisely establish the tissue-specific cellular signalling pathways that can be altered by COX deficiency.</p><p>In summary, we identified bi-allelic pathogenic variants in <em>COX20</em> causing autosomal recessive sensory neuronopathy, expanding genotype and phenotype spectrums of <em>COX20</em> gene. Moreover, histopathological and functional data further indicated that loss of COX20 contributed to mitochondrial bioenergetics dysfunction as a loss-of-function mechanism in sensory neuronopathies.</p> <h2>Acknowledgements</h2>
