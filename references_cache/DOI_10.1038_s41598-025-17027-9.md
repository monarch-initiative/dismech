# Novel COL4A3–COL4A5 variants and digenic inheritance in pediatric Alport syndrome from Southwestern China

Alport syndrome is a hereditary glomerular disease driven by pathogenic variants in COL4A3–COL4A5 
that compromise the α3–α4–α5 type IV collagen scaffold, manifesting as persistent hematuria, 
proteinuria, and ultimately end-stage renal disease. Its pronounced phenotypic variability, low 
sensitivity of renal biopsy, and limited response to ACE inhibitors complicate accurate diagnosis and 
therapy. In a cohort of 40 pedigrees from southwest China, we discovered 21 novel COL4A3-COL4A5 
mutations. Notably, two families carried rare digenic COL4A4/COL4A5 variants, providing strong 
evidence for dual-locus pathogenicity. Immunofluorescence of five mutation-positive patients revealed 
allele-specific depletion of α3 and α5 chains in the glomerular basement membrane, with frameshift 
and splice-site mutations eliciting more severe collagen loss than missense. Complementary AlphaFold 
modeling predicted that digenic variants induce greater destabilization of Gly-X-Y triple helices domain 
than monogenic lesions. Over three years, ACEI therapy achieved only sporadic partial remissions, 
underscoring the need for precision-guided treatment strategies. This study not only expands the 
genetic spectrum of Alport syndrome in China’s ethnic minority populations but also elucidates the 
mechanistic impact of digenic inheritance.

Ethnic minorities
Alport syndrome (AS) is an inherited glomerulopathy resulting from mutations in type IV collagen genes 
encoding the α3-4-5 heterotrimer, leading to progressive kidney disease. Clinically, AS is characterized by 
persistent glomerular hematuria, proteinuria, and progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), with potential 
extrarenal manifestations1. As the second most prevalent monogenic nephropathy after autosomal dominant 
polycystic kidney disease, AS accounts for over 1% of kidney transplant recipients and exhibits age and ethnic 
disparities2. It contributes to 1–2% of ESRD cases in Europe and 2.3% in the United States3,4. Recent advancements 
in genetic sequencing have identified digenic mutations in type IV collagen and potential modifier genes in 
AS pathogenesis5–7. Current therapeutic strategies primarily involve angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors 
(ACEIs) to delay ESRD progression 8. However, clinical outcomes remain suboptimal, with limited treatment 
responsiveness and reduced life expectancy observed in ACEI-treated cohorts9.
AS exhibits clinical heterogeneity due to complex gene-environment interactions and unclear molecular 
mechanisms. Although X-linked Alport syndrome (XLAS) was historically considered the primary cause of 
ESRD risk, autosomal recessive AS (ARAS) also leads to complete ESRD penetrance in both sexes, but with 
differing age-dependent progression 10. XLAS patients often present with early, nephrotic-range proteinuria 
and a high ESRD incidence (90% by age 40, median onset: 25 years) 11,12. ARAS typically involves milder 
urinary abnormalities (microscopic hematuria or subnephrotic proteinuria) and later ESRD onset, especially in 
heterozygous carriers13,14. Digenic AS exhibits unique phenotypes intermediate between autosomal dominant 
AS (ADAS) and ARAS 15,16. Notably, significant phenotypic differences exist among genotypes as the disease 
progresses, but the mechanisms behind these temporal disparities are unclear.
To date, hundreds of pathogenic variants causing AS have been identified. With the advancement of Next-
generation sequencing (NGS) technologies, rare digenic mutations and the role of modifier genes have been

Materials and methods
Sample collection
All sample collection procedures were approved by the Kunming Children’s Hospital Ethics Committee 
(Approval No.2022-03-02-K01) and conducted according to institutional guidelines and the Declaration of 
Helsinki. Medical records of pediatric patients diagnosed with Alport syndrome between September 2018 
and December 2024 were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic system. All data were anonymized to ensure 
patient confidentiality. Blood samples from children and parents, clinical leftover specimens, and biological 
samples from Kunming Children’s Hospital were sent to Beijing MyGenostics for whole-exome sequencing 
(WES) sequencing. Residual renal biopsy specimens from diagnostic procedures and archived samples from the 
Children’s Research Institute Biobank, with prior ethical approval, were also used. All samples were collected 
with informed consent from patients and their families.
Patient selection and diagnostic criteria
Patient selection
The study prospectively screened pediatric patients (aged 1–18 years) from ethnic minorities in Southwest China 
(September 2018–December 2024). Inclusion required:
(a) Persistent glomerular hematuria (> 3 months) ± proteinuria (> 150 mg/24 h);
(b) ≥ 1 high-risk feature guidelines23,24:
• Family history of hematuria or kidney failure.
• High-tone sensorineural hearing loss.
• Ocular lesions (anterior lenticonus/fleck retinopathy).
• Biopsy suggesting Alport syndrome (the glomerular basement membrane (GBM) lamellation/type IV colla-
gen defects).
Exclusion criteria:
• Inability to complete genetic validation (NGS/Sanger).
• Loss to critical follow-up (renal/auditory/ocular).
• Confirmed alternative diagnoses: IgA nephropathy, Dent disease, MYH9-related disorders and so on.
Diagnostic and inclusion criteria
Definitive AS diagnosis required ≥ 1 criterion19,24:
• Pathogenic COL4A5 variant (hemizygous/heterozygous).
• Biallelic pathogenic variants in COL4A3/COL4A4.
• Digenic COL4A3-COL4A5 mutations.
• Patients exhibiting any AS pathological or clinical phenotype and harboring a pathogenic variants in COL4A3 
or COL4A4.
Pathological confirmation: (1) Electron microscopy: GBM basket-weave lamellation Irregular bulging’ of the 
GBM; (2) Thinned GBM; (3) Electron-dense deposits in the GBM, different from immune complexes; (4) 
Lucencies in the GBM; 5)Immunofluorescence: Absent α3/α4/α5(IV) collagen in glomeruli/skin.
Clinical criteria 25: (1) Persistent hematuria + family history + high-tone deafness; (2) Persistent 
hematuria + anterior lenticonus.
Phenocopy exclusion mandated:
• Negative COL4A3-COL4A5 sequencing + normal α5(IV) skin staining.
• Pathogenic variants in other genes identified by whole-exome sequencing.
Clinical data collection
Clinical parameters were retrospectively obtained via medical records and telephone interviews. Data included 
demographics, family history (3-generation pedigree analysis), renal function, audiology/ophthalmologic 
evaluations, and urinalysis (hematuria/proteinuria severity).
Gene sequencing
Targeted region capture sequencing
Genomic DNA was fragmented and ligated to Illumina PE adapters for library preparation. Target regions were 
enriched via hybridization to capture probes and amplified by linker-mediated PCR. Libraries were sequenced 
on the Illumina NextSeq 500 platform. Raw data underwent image processing and sample demultiplexing.

DNA library Preparation and sequencing
Genomic DNA extracted from peripheral blood using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen) was quantified using 
a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). One to three micrograms of DNA was sheared to approximately 
150 bp fragments using a Covaris S220 Focused-ultrasonicator. Libraries were constructed using the MyGenostics 
DNA Sample Prep Reagent Set and sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform.
Targeted enrichment and gene panel sequencing
Targeted enrichment was performed using the GenCap skeletal system gene panel (MyGenostics). Biotinylated 
100 bp probes spanning coding exons and ± 50 bp flanking regions were hybridized with 500 ng of DNA library. 
Target regions were captured with streptavidin beads, washed, then PCR-amplified (13 cycles) and purified using 
SPRI beads (Beckman Coulter) prior to sequencing.
Bioinformatics and whole genome CNV analysis
Sequencing reads were adapter-trimmed and filtered with Cutadapt, then aligned to the UCSC hg19 reference 
genome using BW A (Sentieon). Duplicates were removed and base qualities recalibrated. SNPs and InDels 
were called with Sentieon and annotated with ANNOV AR using 1000 Genomes, dbSNP , ExAC, and HGMD 
databases26. Copy number variations (CNVs) were detected with CNVkit.
Verification of family co-segregation
All mutations detected by DNBSEQ-T7 sequencing were validated by Sanger sequencing. Genomic DNA from all 
available family members was collected for Sanger sequencing. Two pairs of primers (F: 5’-XXXXXXXXXX-3’; 
R: 5’-XXXXXXXXXX-3’) were designed to amplify the XXXXX region. PCR amplification was conducted 
using the following protocol: an initial denaturation step at 98 °C for 2 min, followed by 10 cycles of 98 °C for 
10 s, 65 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 10 s. This was succeeded by 25 cycles at 98 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 
72 °C for 10 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 1 min. The purified PCR products (approximately XX bp) 
were sequenced using Sanger sequencing (Biosune), and the results were analyzed with DNASTAR (Madison) 
software (Supplementary Table S1).
Immunofluorescence
Primary reagents
Primary antibodies: mouse anti-human collagen IV α1/α3 [MAB1, MAB3] and rabbit anti-human α5 [MAB5] 
(Wieslab). Also: mouse anti-human α4 polyclonal [AB756P] (Sigma) at 1:50. Secondary antibodies: fluorescein 
isothiocyanate (FITC)-goat anti-rabbit IgG and FITC-rabbit anti-mouse IgG at 1:100. Other reagents: pepsin K 
solution, H₂O₂, Phosphate-buffered saline with Tween 20 (PBST), and anti-fade mounting medium.
Experimental method
Frozen kidney sections  (4–6  μm) were thawed and fixed in acetone for five minutes. Sections were washed 
in PBST three times for five minutes each. Sections were digested with pepsin K for ten minutes. After PBST 
washes, sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 1:50 for two hours or overnight at 4 °C. Sections 
were washed and incubated with secondary antibodies at 1:100 for one hour in the dark. Sections were washed, 
mounted with anti-fade medium, and coverslipped. Fluorescence was observed, and images were captured by a 
microscope.
Treatment efficacy assessment
Longitudinal monitoring included: urinalysis (proteinuria/RBC quantification), renal function (creatinine/
eGFR-Schwartz), ophthalmic/auditory evaluations, with efficacy defined by proteinuria remission (complete: 
<150 mg/24 h + normal albumin; partial: ≥50% reduction or dipstick < 1+) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) 
progression (KDIGO 2024)27.
Results
We analyzed 40 Alport syndrome families and characterized their clinical and molecular features. We identified 
21 novel pathogenic COL4A variants, comprising 12 in COL4A5, 5 in COL4A3, and 4 in COL4A4. These included 
26 XLAS, 12 autosomal AS, and two COL4A5/COL4A4 digenic families. XLAS phenotypes were more severe 
than ARAS and ADAS. Structural effects of novel mutations were predicted by AlphaFold and confirmed by 
immunofluorescence.
Clinical features
Among 40 probands, 26 were male and 14 were female. Mean onset age was 5.74 ± 3.72 years for males and 
4.98 ± 3.47 years for females. No significant age difference was found ( P = 0.52). Age at onset ranged from one 
to fourteen years. Pre-school onset (1–6 years) accounted for 65% of cases. Ethnically, 77.5% were Han Chinese 
and 22.5% were ethnic minorities (mainly Yi, Hui, Miao, and Hani). Hematuria with proteinuria was the main 
phenotype in 34 cases (85%). Pure hematuria occurred in six cases (15%). Six cases had gross hematuria, and 
ten had nephrotic-range proteinuria. One case presented with chronic renal failure. Extrarenal manifestations 
occurred in eight cases (20%). Seven had sensorineural hearing loss, and one had congenital cataract. Positive 
family history was observed in 22 families (55%) (Table 1).
Histopathology
Twenty patients underwent ultrasound-guided renal biopsy. Light microscopy showed seven minimal change, 
nine mesangial proliferation, and four focal segmental sclerosis cases. Electron microscopy revealed eleven

Digenic inheritance evidence
Two families exhibited COL4A5/COL4A4 digenic inheritance. All three probands presented with refractory 
nephrotic syndrome and severe hematuria. Proband 2 had nephrotic-range proteinuria. Proband 14 showed 
a family history of hematuria, proteinuria, and renal impairment. Mutations COL4A5 p.G475D was novel. All 
mutations are localized to collagen’s Gly-XY triple-helical domains. American College of Medical Genetics and 
Genomics (ACMG) classified them as pathogenic or likely pathogenic26.
Genotype–phenotype correlation
We compared XLAS (n = 25) with ADAS/ARAS (n = 12) groups, after excluding three families (P1, P2, and P14) 
with suspected digenic involvement. No significant differences in onset age or eGFR were observed (Mann-
Whitney Test, P > 0.05). Proteinuria levels did not differ significantly between groups (Mann-Whitney Test, 
P = 0.59). Within XLAS (n = 28), patients were stratified by missense versus non-missense variants. Age at onset 
did not differ (Mann-Whitney Test, P = 0.21). The non-missense variant group had higher proteinuria than the 
missense group. Proteinuria was 1.21 ± 1.24 g/24 h versus 0.27 ± 0.16 g/24 h (Mann-Whitney Test, P = 0.01 < 0.05) 
(Fig. 2).
Pathogenicity prediction
All novel variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Pathogenicity was predicted using SIFT, PROVEAN, 
REVEL, MutationTaster, and GERP++. ACMG classified variants as pathogenic or likely pathogenic. PopViz 
predicted most missense variants as deleterious28. Most deleterious missense mutations are localized to Gly-XY 
motifs and the NC1 domain.
Structural modeling by alphafold
AlphaFold predicted structural impacts of digenic COL4A3-COL4A5 mutations. In the wild-type model, 
COL4A3, COL4A4, and COL4A5 chains formed a stable triple-helix structure, with well-aligned Gly-X-Y repeats 
domain (Fig.  3A,B). Among the identified variants, COL4A5 p.P1314S and COL4A4 p.R1226C were located 
in close spatial proximity within the triple-helix domain (Fig.  3C,D). Structural predictions revealed that this 
digenic combination reduced local interchain hydrogen bonding and altered backbone geometry, leading to 
marked conformational destabilization. In contrast, COL4A5 p.G475D introduced a bulky aspartate residue, 
disturbing local helix packing via steric hindrance. The COL4A4 p.A832T variant had a relatively mild effect, 
introducing a polar residue that increased local flexibility but did not disrupt the overall helical configuration 
(Fig. 3E,F). Predicted structural defects correlate with severe clinical phenotypes.
Immunofluorescence findings
Immunofluorescence of five AS patients showed variable collagen IV expression. Frameshift variants COL4A5 
c.2557delC and c.3091delG abolished α3 and α5 expression. Collagen IV α3, α5 is absent in the GBM and tubules. 
Splice variant COL4A5 c.3790 + 1G > T caused segmental α3 and α5 loss. Missense variants COL4A5 c.3535G > T 
and c.1045 C > A showed normal α3/α5 expression. All five patients exhibited hematuria and proteinuria (Fig. 4).
Treatment and follow-up
All 40 AS patients received ACEI therapy; some also received prednisone or immunosuppressants and were 
followed for over three years. Only a few patients achieved laboratory remission after ACEI, steroids, or 
immunosuppressants. Of 21 patients receiving ACEI alone (captopril 1–2 mg/kg d), only three (P6, P12, P19) had 
partial remission (urinalysis <+). The remaining 18 patients had persistent hematuria or proteinuria. Among 12 
patients on ACEI plus prednisone (1–2 mg/kg d), only one (P5) achieved partial remission in the first year. None 
of the other 11 patients achieved remission over a three-year follow-up. In ten patients on ACEI, prednisone, and 
tacrolimus (0.05–0.1 mg/kg d), two (P9, P32) had partial remission after one year. Their proteinuria remained 
< 0.5 g/24 h. However, P32 experienced proteinuria recurrence in the third year. Patient P33 (COL4A3-mutant 
ADAS, FSGS) treated with prednisone and cyclophosphamide, followed by prednisone and tacrolimus, had 
persistent proteinuria and frequent relapses. Rituximab (375  mg/m 2) reduced relapse frequency in year five, 
but proteinuria persisted. Notably, among early-diagnosed AS patients without baseline CKD, 39/40 (97.5%) 
maintained preserved renal function (eGFR ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m2) throughout the 3-year follow-up, with only 
one patient (P28, male, COL4A5 c.81 + 2T>C) progressed to ESRD, demonstrating rapid functional decline 
from diagnosis at age 14 years (baseline creatinine 300 µmol/L, eGFR 32 mL/min/1.73  m 2) to hemodialysis 
dependence within 3 years (pre-dialysis creatinine 857 µmol/L, eGFR 5.94 mL/min/1.73 m2), with post-dialysis 
stabilization at creatinine 281 µmol/L (eGFR 18.2 mL/min/1.73 m2).
Discussion
In this study, we performed a retrospective analysis of 40 genetically confirmed pedigrees of AS from Southwest 
China. We identified 21 novel mutations in the COL4A3-COL4A5 genes, including two previously unreported 
cases of digenic inheritance involving type IV collagen genes. Immunofluorescence assays showed that the newly 
discovered mutations in COL4A5 affect the expression of type IV collagen. This research is one of the few studies 
to explore the clinical manifestations, pathological features, and molecular genetics of AS in children from 
ethnic minority regions in Southwest China. It expands the known mutational spectrum of AS and uncovers 
rare digenic mutations.

Our center found that patients with AS in Southwest China exhibited early disease onset, with a predominance 
of male cases. However, there was no statistically significant difference in the age of onset between genders. 
The clinical phenotypes varied widely, typically presenting as edema, microscopic hematuria accompanied 
by proteinuria, and gross hematuria. Extrarenal manifestations were uncommon, and the diagnostic rates 
using electron microscopy remained low. The study revealed that approximately 38.46% of male AS patients 
developed nephrotic-range proteinuria, compared to only 7.14% of females. Current research indicates that in 
XLAS, females possess two homologous X chromosomes, leading to heterozygous variants due to single-point 
mutations with differential expression29–31. This is further complicated by random X-chromosome inactivation. 
In contrast, males have hemizygous mutations that cause disease regardless of whether the mutations are 
dominant or recessive. Reports indicate that female XLAS patients develop significant proteinuria and reach 
ESRD much later than males 11. Additionally, single-center cohort studies on XLAS genotype-phenotype 
relationships show that nonsense, splicing, frameshift, duplication, and digenic mutations are associated with 
more severe clinical phenotypes compared to missense mutations. These findings highlight the importance of 
early monitoring, routine urinalysis, and genetic screening for male patients and those with severe genotypes in 
AS families, enabling prompt diagnosis and intervention.
Different types of XLAS mutations affect type IV collagen expression in varying degrees. Through 
immunofluorescence staining of five novel AS patients, we found that COL4A5 mutations—specifically 
c.2557delC, c.3091delG, c.3790 + 1G > T, c.3535G > T, and c.1045 C > A—significantly influence the expression 
of type IV collagen. Frameshift and splicing mutations in XLAS exhibit more pronounced deficiencies in α5 
chain expression in the GBM than missense mutations. These mutations are also associated with more severe 
clinical phenotypes. For example, members of the c.2557delC pedigree developed hematuria and proteinuria at

Digenic inheritance has intrigued geneticists since the early 20th century, but only with the advent of high-
throughput sequencing (HTS) has identifying one or multiple pathogenic genes for monogenic disorders become 
more straightforward, similarly simplifying digenic inheritance verification 5,30,39. This study describes rare 
instances of two digenic mutation AS families (P2, P14), providing new evidence of digenic inheritance. Current 
research on AS digenic mutations typically divides into combinations between two type IV collagen-encoding 
genes40. Here, we describe two AS families with COL4A4 and COL4A5 digenic mutation combinations. Using 
the AlphaFold deep learning prediction model, we systematically analyzed the structural impact of COL4A5 and 
COL4A4 digenic mutations (P2, P14) on highly conserved triple-helical Gly-X-Y domains and NC1 domains in 
type IV collagen α3, α4, and α5 chains. This revealed that, compared to single gene mutations, digenic mutations 
cause multiple structural defects—including premature termination of triple helices, disruption of disulfide bond 
networks, and decreased local stability from glycine/alanine residue substitutions—resulting in more severe AS 
clinical phenotypes. All probands inherited digenic mutations from their mothers, featuring co-inheritance of 
two variants. The P14 proband’s mother had already progressed to CKD stage III relatively early at age 30. 
This aligns with previous European AS clinical research indicating correlations between digenic mutations and 
clinical phenotypes 24. For instance, a European AS patient data center study of COL4A3/COL4A4 mutation 
combination pedigrees demonstrated that digenic autosomal mutations on homologous chromosomes present 
more severe phenotypes than autosomal dominant patterns41. This potentially suggests that kidney involvement 
severity correlates with the number of mutant type IV collagen molecules, though validation requires additional 
cases.
In conclusion, our analysis of 40 Alport syndrome pedigrees from Southwest China uncovered 21 novel 
mutations, including two rare COL4A4–COL4A5 digenic variants. Immunofluorescence showed that COL4A5 
mutations differentially alter type IV collagen α3 and α5 expression, while deep learning–based modeling 
revealed distinct structural changes in both single-gene and digenic mutations. These findings broaden the AS 
mutation spectrum and enhance diagnostic strategies.
