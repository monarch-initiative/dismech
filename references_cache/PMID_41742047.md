# Scrotal abscess and Fournier gangrene caused by Klebsiella pneumoniae: a case series and literature review.
**Authors:** Jiang Y, Huang D, Wu H
**Journal:** BMC Infect Dis (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12879-026-12946-y](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-12946-y)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Klebsiella pneumoniae is a common nosocomial pathogen capable of causing invasive disease, including sepsis and meningitis, even in immunocompetent hosts. Involvement of the scrotum, manifesting as scrotal abscess or Fournier’s gangrene, is uncommon and under-recognized. We report three K. pneumoniae scrotal infections and provide microbiological characterization to highlight their clinical relevance and novelty. CASE PRESENTATION: Three elderly male patients (aged 68, 78, and 85 years) with diabetes and a history of rectal or prostate cancer presented with acute scrotal swelling, erythema, and ulceration. Two cases progressed to Fournier’s gangrene (necrotizing fasciitis). All underwent urgent surgical debridement or abscess drainage, combined with susceptibility-directed antimicrobial therapy (cefazolin, cefotaxime, or ciprofloxacin). K. pneumoniae was identified as the sole pathogen in all cases, including one hypervirulent ST23 strain. All patients achieved significant clinical improvement and were discharged with healed or healing wounds. CONCLUSION: Although rare, K. pneumoniae should be considered as a potential pathogen in scrotal infections, especially in patients with diabetes and cancer. Early recognition, prompt surgical source control and susceptibility-directed antimicrobial therapy are crucial for favorable outcomes. CLINICAL TRIAL: Clinical trial number: Not applicable. This study was not registered as a clinical trial because it was a descriptive cross-sectional study involving the analysis of bacterial isolates from clinical specimens, which did not meet the criteria for a clinical trial.

Scrotal abscesses and Fournier gangrene (FG) are infections caused mostly by intestinal flora, such as Escherichia coli, Enterococcus faecalis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae (KP), Proteus mirabilis and Enterococcus faecium [ 1 ], often requiring surgical treatment and even surgical emergencies. K. pneumoniae is a normal intestinal flora found in both healthy humans and animals [ 2 ]. It belongs to the genus Klebsiella in the order Enterobacterales and is distributed in soil, water, and vegetation worldwide [ 3 ]. Hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (HvKP) was first reported in 1986 and is thought to be responsible for liver abscesses, endophthalmitis, pulmonary embolization, purulent meningitis, and prostatic abscesses [ 4 ]. Compared with HvKP, classical K. pneumoniae (cKP) strains exhibit less virulence. While most articles and case series have focused on the clinical characteristics of scrotal abscess or FG, little attention has been paid to the features of cases induced by a specific pathogen species or to virulent types such as HvKP. Herein, we report three cases of scrotal abscess and FG caused by K. pneumoniae, with the aim of increasing awareness of the involvement of the scrotum and shedding light on a diagnosis that is not commonly associated with K. pneumoniae among medical personnel.

On April 24 2023, a 68-year-old patient with type 2 diabetes (uncontrolled blood glucose level for 10 years) was admitted to our hospital for scheduled rectal cancer treatment. He had been diagnosed with rectal cancer more than 7 months prior and had just completed 8 courses of palliative FOLFOXIRI chemotherapy and one course of radiotherapy (February 27 ~ April 14, 2023). He had been diagnosed with K. pneumoniae bacteremia while receiving chemotherapy two months earlier. The pathogen was ESBL negative and resistant to cefazolin and ciprofloxacin, intermediate to levofloxacin, and susceptible to cefazolin, cefuroxime, ceftazidime, cefotaxime, cefepime, ampicillin-sulbactam, piperacillin-tazobactam, cefoxitin, imipenem, meropenem, amikacin, gentamicin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Table 1). The infection was controlled after intravenous cefazolin (1gq8h) administration. Physical examination revealed swelling and skin lesions with exudate on the scrotum. Radiodermatitis was initially considered, and the lesion was treated with mupirocin ointment. As the infection deteriorated (Fig. 1a), intravenous cefazolin (1gq8h) was resumed, and the external application of povidone-iodine therapy was initiated on April 29. On the same day, a secretion sample was collected from the scrotal lesion. Microscopic examination revealed numerous leukocytes and gram-negative bacilli, and bacterial culture yielded K. pneumoniae growth on May 2. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed that the pathogen was resistant to ampicillin and cefazolin, but susceptible to amoxicillin-clavulanate, ampicillin-sulbactam, piperacillin-tazobactam, cefuroxime, ceftazidime, cefotaxime, cefepime, aztreonam, imipenem, meropenem, amikacin, gentamicin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, tetracycline, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Table 1). The antimicrobial therapy was changed to cefotaxime (2 g, qd, until discharge). Despite active infection control, the scrotum swelled significantly, accompanied by ulcerative lesions deep in the fascia, and increased exudation. FG was diagnosed and the patient was transferred to plastic surgery for surgical treatment after ten days of hospitalization in the medical oncology department. On May 9, the patient underwent resection of the scrotal lesion and debridement. Pathological examination of the resected tissue revealed inflammatory granulation tissue, surface erosion, and ulceration (Fig. 2), with a pathological diagnosis of chronic ulcer. The patient had no fever during the entire hospitalization period, and the wound was dry and clean, with no obvious bleeding or exudation after surgical treatment. On May 17, the patient was discharged when the wound was dry and clean, with no obvious bleeding or exudation.

A 78-year-old patient, who complained of six months of left scrotal swelling and pain, came to the hospital seeking medical help on May 16, 2023. The patient had a history of diabetes mellitus and poor blood sugar control. He was diagnosed with prostate cancer and had undergone transurethral laser prostatectomy eight months prior. On admission, physical examination revealed that the left scrotum was enlarged, the abscess was approximately 10 × 8 cm in size, the surface of the scrotum was red and swollen with white pus spots, and the skin temperature increased with high tension. However, the abscess had not ruptured. The patient was diagnosed with diabetes, a left scrotal abscess, and a urinary tract infection. The laboratory results showed a WBC count of 18.284 × 109/L, a neutrophil proportion of 90.3%, a CRP level of 140.81 mg/L (normal: ≤10 mg/L) and a PCT level 0.4 ng/mL (normal: 0 − 0.5 ng/L). Injected insulin aspart (8U before breakfast in the morning and 6U before dinner in the evening) and cefotaxime (IV, 2 g,q8h, until discharge) were prescribed. On May 18th, there was a rupture on the surface of the left scrotum with oozing white pus. Abscess drainage was performed, and pus samples were collected. The smear showed white blood cells ≥ 25/low power field with moderate gram-negative bacteria. Four days later, microbiological testing revealed that K. pneumoniae was resistant to ampicillin, intermediate to cefazolin, and susceptible to amoxicillin-clavulanate, ampicillin-sulbactam, piperacillin-tazobactam, cefuroxime, ceftazidime, cefotaxime, cefepime, aztreonam, imipenem, meropenem, amikacin, gentamicin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, tetracycline, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Table 1). Combined with surgical debridement and intravenous antibiotic treatment, the swelling and pain in the left scrotum improved and pus exudation significantly decreased (Fig. 1b). However, on May 24, the patient had a fever of 38.9 °C. Based on consultation with clinical pharmacists, ciprofloxacin (iv, 0.2 g, q12h, until May 29) was added. On the following days, the patient no longer had fever, and the swelling and pain improved. He was discharged on May 30 when there was no obvious redness, swelling, exudation, or tenderness in the scrotum, and most of the ulcerated surface of the left scrotum healed.

On March 2, 2024, an 85-year-old patient was admitted to the emergency department with 3 days of scrotal skin ulceration. He was diagnosed with rectal adenocarcinoma and underwent radiotherapy and chemotherapy in June 2022 and a double-lumen sigmoid colostomy (with an artificial anus in the lower left abdomen) in December 2023. He had been diagnosed with diabetes one year previously, and the blood glucose control was unsatisfactory.

Three days prior, the family members noticed that the patient’s scrotal skin had ruptured and was an oozing fluid. The patient experienced pain but had no fever. Iodine was administered, but the scrotal swelling was not relieved, and the wound continued to ooze the fluid. Physical examination revealed a wound (approximately 5 cm × 7.0 cm in size) on the left scrotum. The edge of the wound was concave, red, and swollen, and the base contained pale necrotic tissue with slight exudation. The laboratory results showed that the WBC count was 17.44 × 109/L, the neutrophil proportion was 88.2%, the CRP level was 129.52 mg/L (normal: ≤10 mg/L), and the PCT level was 0.12 ng/mL (normal: 0 − 0.5 ng/L). Intravenous cefazolin (1gq8h, until March 26) and povidone iodine emulsifiable paste were administered on March 3 for infection control, and insulin (4U-4U-4U subcutaneously injected before three meals until March 24) was used for glucose control. The debridement of the scrotal ulcer was performed on March 5. Debridement, the base was found to have reached the deep fascia, leading to a diagnosis of necrotizing fasciitis of the scrotum. A pus smear showed a large number of white blood cells and gram-negative bacilli (Fig. 3). The pus, along with perianal swabs and fecal samples, was sent to the laboratory for pathogen detection. On March 16, all three samples exhibited K. pneumoniae growth. AST showed that the three strains presented the same susceptibility pattern, and were susceptible to cefazolin, amoxicillin-clavulanate, ampicillin-sulbactam, piperacillin-tazobactam, cefuroxime, ceftazidime, cefotaxime, cefepime, aztreonam, imipenem, meropenem, amikacin, gentamicin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, tetracycline and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Table 1), and whole genome sequencing (WGS) of the three strains also showed homology (Fig. 4). FG is thought to be related to the replacement of the anal pouch at the fistula site. Therefore, intravenous cefazolin and povidone-iodine emulsifiable pastes were administered. Over the next few days, the scrotal wound decreased and there was no obvious necrotic tissue. The secretion gradually decreased, and there was no redness or swelling at the wound edge (Fig. 1c). On March 26, the patient was discharged with significant improvement. One month later, the patient was readmitted for 10 days with aggravated edema; however, the opening of the scrotal sinus was closed, with no obvious exudation, local redness, or swelling as inflammatory symptoms.

Pathogen identification was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) on the VITEK® MS system (bioMérieux, France) with the associated IVD database. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed using the broth microdilution method. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) results were interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100-Ed34 guidelines [ 5 ], which were the current standard at the time of data analysis and manuscript preparation. The string test was performed to phenotypically screen for hypermucoviscosity, a characteristic associated with HvKP. A standard bacteriological loop was used to stretch a colony on an agar plate; a viscous string of > 5 mm in length was considered a positive result. K. pneumoniae strains were sequenced using Illumina GA IIx (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) which was used to construct two paired-end (PE) libraries with an average insertion length of 350 bp [ 6 ]. The raw data were processed in four steps: removing reads with 5 bp of ambiguous bases, removing reads with 20 bp of low-quality (≤ Q20) bases, removing adapter contamination, and removing duplicated reads. Finally, 100× libraries were obtained from the clean paired-end read data. The assembly was performed using SOAP V2.04, SPAdes-3.11.1 and Aabyss-1.3.7 [ 7 ]. GapCloser (v1.12) was used to connect the scaffolds and a Perl script was used to select the best assembly results for subsequent analysis.

WGS was performed on five K. pneumoniae strains isolated from pus (three patients; Case 1: L1HHH1000128; Case 2: L1HHH1000129; Case 3: Wh-yinnang), perianal swabs (Case 3: WH-gangzhou), and fecal samples (Case 3: WH-fenbian). The results showed that the multilocus sequence typing of the strain isolated from Case 1 was ST23, that from Case 2 was ST267, and that three of the strains from Case 3 were all ST7163 (Table 2). HvKP-associated virulence genes were all positive in the strain isolated from the scrotum in case 1, whereas they were all negative in strains from cases 2 and 3. The resistance genes included ArnT, CRP, and Escherichia coli UhpT, with mutations conferring resistance to fosfomycin, and Escherichia coli soxR, with a mutation conferring antibiotic resistance; H-NS, KP KpnE, KP KpnF, KP KpnG, KP KpnH, KP OmpK37, KP acrA, LptD, OmpA, acrB, acrD, baeR, cpxA, emrR, eptB, marA, mdtC, msbA, oqxA, oqxB and ramA were all positive among patients from the three strains, however, five other resistance genes among the three strains were different, and no ESBL or multidrug resistance genes were detected.

Scrotal abscess is a presentation of various etiologies, including epididymal -orchitis, neglected testicular torsion, spread from intra-abdominal abscess, and the hematogenous route of systemic infection [ 8 ]. Tan reported that patients under 35 years of age are vulnerable to genitourinary abscesses [ 9 ]. FG is a major urological infectious emergency with an incidence of approximately 1.6/100,000 men [ 10 ] and a mortality rate of 12.5% [ 11 ]. Scrotal infection can manifest as swelling, redness, chronic ulcers, or FG and can be easily neglected. In some cases, FG develop rapidly and have severe consequences.

In this case series, we reported three cases of scrotal abscess or FG caused by KP, a silent bowel colonizer [ 12 , 13 ]. All three patients had diabetes and were diagnosed with rectal or prostate cancer. Supplementary Table S1 summarizes the clinical data of our cases and those of six other patients with scrotal abscess or FG caused by K. pneumoniae. It is well known that diabetes increases the risk of infection [ 14 ]. Among the nine patients, five had diabetes [ 15 , 16 ] and one neonate’s mother had diabetes [ 17 ]. Interestingly, except for one neonatal patient [ 18 ], eight of them were from Asia [ 15– 17 , 19 , 20 ] and five elderly patients were from China. Since HvKP was first reported in 1986 [ 4 ], infections caused by HvKP have attracted the attention of clinicians, and many studies have been conducted. Whether this situation is similar to that of HvKP remains to be determined. Therefore, we report these cases to draw attention to KP-induced scrotal infection.

FG is characterized by polymicrobial infection beginning in the genital or perineal region, with an identifiable cause in 95% of cases [ 21 ]. The most frequently identified microorganisms are E. coli, K. pneumoniae and Enterococcus [ 11 , 21 , 22 ]. In case 3, strains from feces, perianal swabs, and scrotum shared the same MLST and susceptibility patterns, and the genome ANI between the strains indicated that they were homologous. This is consistent with the finding that 13–50% of FG cases have colorectal origins and that seventeen to eighty-seven% of the sources are urogenital [ 11 ]. Homology among the strains in this case confirmed that the infection was caused by K. pneumoniae from the intestinal tract. This finding suggested that for immunocompromised patients, if feces carrying pathogens are not wiped thoroughly and promptly, the pathogen is prone to contact with the skin and mucous membranes and cause infection. For K. pneumoniae, virulence should always be considered as it predisposes patients to metastatic infection. The strain in our first case carried multiple hypervirulence-related genes, suggesting that it is HvKP. Tan et al. reported that all K. pneumoniae isolates from genitourinary abscesses were HvKP, with genes rmpA2, ybtS, kfuC, wzi, aerobactin genes (iucABCD and iutA) and type 3 fimbriae genes (mrkAD) [ 9 ] being positive. Although the other two cases did not carry virulence genes, the fact that HvKP can cause scrotal infections should not be ignored, considering both the virulence and vulnerability of patients, especially those with diabetes and cancer.

Early clinical suspicion and timely microbiological sampling are crucial in patients with diabetes, cancer, or recent radiotherapy in the groin region, as scrotal infections can mimic radiodermatitis or other non-infectious conditions. Although K. pneumoniae is reported in approximately 10% of Fournier gangrene cases, our study provides detailed microbiological and genomic characterization rarely documented in such presentations. The use of whole-genome sequencing confirmed fecal–scrotal transmission in one case and identified a hypervirulent ST23 strain in another, underscoring the value of molecular diagnostics in tracing infection sources and recognizing hypervirulent lineages even in non-metastatic presentations.

In terms of antimicrobial resistance, carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae is of “critical concern” according to the World Health Organization [ 23 ]. Although HvKP is often emphasized in metastatic infections, cKP can also cause severe local and systemic disease in immunocompromised hosts, as seen in our Cases 2 and 3. This suggests that virulence typing alone may not fully predict clinical severity in patients with significant comorbidities such as diabetes and malignancy.

A study on male genitourinary abscesses found that multidrug-resistant K. pneumoniae was the dominant microorganism, and 81.8% of the isolates were carbapenem-resistant and belonged to the ST11 type carrying the blaKPC-2 carbapenemase gene [ 9 ]. Fortunately, the strains isolated from three patients were sensitive (Supplementary Table S2). Nevertheless, multidrug-resistant K. pneumoniae should always be considered when initiating antimicrobial treatments. Moreover, surgical management, such as lesion resection or debridement, is necessary to remove the infected foci and control further development of infection. Negative pressure wound therapy is widely used for surgical treatment, and He et al. concluded that it could reduce the operation frequency and shorten the healing time of the wound as an effective adjuvant therapy [ 22 ]. Infections may also be misdiagnosed as radiodermatitis in patients with rectal cancer receiving radiotherapy. Thus, for patients with tumors in the groin area receiving radiotherapy, vigilance against infection should be strengthened, and timely collection of suitable specimens for pathogen detection is essential for rapid diagnosis and treatment.

The most significant limitation is the small sample size of only three cases. This precludes meaningful statistical analysis and hinders the establishment of evidence-based clinical protocols. Consequently, our findings are specific to these patients and may not represent the broader spectrum of scrotal abscess or FG. Second, this is a single-center study, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other healthcare settings with different patient populations, microbiota colonization patterns, or antimicrobial resistance profiles. Third, diagnosis and treatment protocols may not be universally accepted or available in all clinical settings. Fourth, our literature search did not find enough articles specific to scrotal infections caused by K. pneumoniae, which limits our ability to control variables that could influence outcomes and does not allow for the establishment of a cause-and-effect relationship. Finally, we did not preserve all the strains isolated from the patients, especially the K. pneumoniae from the blood of case 1, which hindered us from performing homology analysis and definitively tracing the origin of the scrotal infection in that case. Future studies will prioritize strain preservation for comprehensive molecular epidemiology.

This study highlights K. pneumoniae-induced scrotal infection. Although not frequently reported, this case series emphasizes the necessity for clinicians to suspect infection with K. pneumoniae, rather than neglecting or misdiagnosing it as radiodermatitis in postoperative diabetic patients with colorectal or urogenital cancer presenting with scrotal swelling. Careful nursing care is crucial for the timely detection of scrotal infections, and early collection and submission of specimens for bacterial culture are essential for diagnosis. Successful antibiotic and surgical treatments illustrate the potential for a favorable prognosis with prompt diagnosis and appropriate management.
