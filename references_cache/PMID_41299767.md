# CISD2 ensures adequate ER-mitochondrial coupling, critically supporting mitochondrial function in neurons.
**Authors:** Loncke J, de Ridder I, La Rovere R, Vaarmann A, Fan G, Ahuja K, Serysheva I, Verfaillie C, Kerkhofs M, Parys JB, Kaasik A, Bultynck G, Vervliet T
**Journal:** Acta Neuropathol Commun (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s40478-025-02132-7](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40478-025-02132-7)

## Content

Loss of Cisd2, an iron-sulfur cluster transfer protein, results in type 2 Wolfram syndrome (WS2), a disorder associated with severe impacts on pancreatic β cell and neuronal functions. Cisd2 has been implicated in regulating intracellular Ca2+ signaling. However, the molecular basis and cellular consequences remain poorly understood. In this work, we demonstrate that Cisd2 intersects with intracellular Ca2+ dynamics at different levels, by interacting with the inositol-1,4,5-trisphosphate receptors and as a regulator of ER-mitochondria tethering. As such, loss of Cisd2 in HeLa cells results in reduced ER-mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer while only modestly impacting cytosolic Ca2+ signaling. In HeLa cells, Cisd2 deficiency promotes autophagic flux, yet has minimal impact on mitochondrial function. However, studying the impact of Cisd2 deficiency in human induced pluripotent stem cell -derived cortical neurons revealed a severe loss of glutamate-evoked Ca2+ responses in cytosol and associated uptake in mitochondria due to loss of ER-mitochondria contact sites. Correlating with the profound changes in cellular Ca2+ handling, mitochondrial function (oxygen consumption rate, ATP production, mitochondrial potential maintenance) declined severely, while autophagic flux was increased. Overall, these deficiencies further impact the resilience of Cisd2-deficient cortical neurons to cell stress as Cisd2-KO neurons were highly sensitive to staurosporine, an inducer of apoptosis. Overall, this work is one of the first to decipher the impact of Cisd2 on ER-mitochondria Ca2+ handling in a WS2 disease-relevant cell models, thereby revealing a unique dependence of neurons on Cisd2 for their mitochondrial health and cell stress resilience.

CDGSH iron sulfur domain 2 (Cisd2) (alias: Naf-1) is an iron-sulfur (Fe-S)-cluster domain-binding protein, important for cellular homeostasis [1, 58]. CISD2 together with Wolframin (Wfs1) are the two genes that upon acquiring loss-of-function mutations result in Wolfram syndrome. This rare genetic disease starts during childhood with diabetes mellitus due to loss of functional pancreatic β cells, progressing during adolescence towards neurodegeneration in the central and peripheral nervous system [41]. Physiologically, Cisd2-protein levels have been implicated in longevity and the adequate functioning of β cells and neurons, but also of skeletal and cardiac muscles [16, 64, 72], liver [29–31, 57] and corneal epithelia [60] cells. A reduction of Cisd2 expression (e.g. in heterozygous Cisd2 knockout (KO) mice) evokes early aging and organ dysfunctions [16]. At the pathophysiological level, Cisd2 is upregulated in breast cancer, whereby inhibition of Cisd2 results in breast cancer cell death [28, 36]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, however, haploinsufficiency of Cisd2 results in hepatocarcinogenesis [57, 59], underpinning the complexity of Cisd2 in cancer cell biology.

Fascinatingly, although WFS1 and CISD2 belong to two completely different gene families resulting in two proteins with unrelated protein domains and cellular function, loss-of-function of either WFS1 or CISD2 results in Wolfram syndrome [41]. Therefore, we have postulated that Wfs1 and Cisd2 have converging roles, in for instance regulating intracellular Ca2+ signaling [41]. In virtually all cells, endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-originating Ca2+ signals are mediated by the opening of and Ca2+-flux through inositol (1, 4, 5)-trisphosphate (IP3) receptors (IP3Rs), which arguably form the major class of intracellular Ca2+-release channels [7]. These Ca2+ signals do not only occur in the cytosol but are also transmitted to the mitochondrial matrix via mitochondria ER contact sites (MERCS) through inter-organellar IP3R-glucose regulated protein 75 (GRP75)-voltage-dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1)-protein complexes [9].

Two important clues hinted towards the involvement of Cisd2 in intracellular Ca2+ signaling arising from the ER. First, both Wfs1 and Cisd2 have been reported to form protein complexes with IP3Rs [2, 12]. Wfs1 was found to control IP3R function by operating in a tripartite complex together with neuronal Ca2+ sensor-1 protein (NCS-1) [2]. In contrast, Cisd2 appears to form a complex with IP3Rs involving anti-apoptotic B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) [13, 62]. Fascinatingly, both NCS-1 as well as Bcl-2 are well-established direct interactors of IP3Rs [33, 46, 48]. Furthermore, Bcl-2 and Cisd2 also directly interact with each other [13, 40, 62]. More recently, it was found that Cisd2 alleviated Bcl-2’s ability to reduce the number and extent of MERCS [40]. Second, both Wfs1 and Cisd2 have been found to reside at MERCS [20, 38, 40, 70, 71, 73]. Biochemical isolation of the ER membrane fractions involved in MERCS, so-called mitochondria-associated ER membranes (MAMs), are enriched in both Wfs1 and Cisd2 [20, 40, 41]. Interestingly, in a proteome-wide, proximity biotinylation-based approach (Contact-ID), Cisd2 was among the top hits of proteins resident at MERCS [38]. Loss of Wfs1 resulted in a reduced MERCS integrity and impaired ER-mitochondria Ca2+ transfers, thereby impairing mitochondrial functions such as mitochondrial bio-energetics, particularly impacting neurons [39]. MERCS disassembly and dysfunction is also found in cellular and animal models carrying pathogenic WFS1 mutations identified in Wolfram syndrome patients [2, 17]. Moreover, strategies that restored MERCS organization and Ca2+-signaling function such as NCS-1 overexpression [2, 47] or pharmacological agonists of the Sigma-1 receptor, a MERCS-resident chaperone, also improved mitochondrial function [17].

While our understanding of MERCS controlled by Wfs1 has been majorly advanced the last 5 years, the functional impact of loss of Cisd2 on MERCS remains poorly understood. There has been one report in patient-derived fibroblasts linking CISD2 mutations to an increase of MERCS that also displayed an increase in agonist-induced ER-mitochondria Ca2+ transfers, compared to control fibroblasts obtained from unaffected family members [53]. Nevertheless, as the impact of wild-type Cisd2 on MERCS, particularly in disease-relevant cell types remains elusive, exploring its function in neurons is critical. Furthermore, pathogenic CISD2 mutations may also introduce gain-of-function aspects and thus may not reflect the biological functions of wild-type Cisd2. Finally, MERCS may differ across individuals irrespective of the CISD2 gene status, making it instrumental to study CISD2 using isogenic conditions.

To address this knowledge gap, we set out to study MERCS organization and Ca2+-signaling function in cell models in which CISD2 was knocked out using CRISPR/Cas9. To explore its cell biological function in controlling MERCS, we used HeLa cells, an excellent cell model to study Ca2+ signaling and particularly MERCS and its function in ER-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer due to the efficient coupling of ER and mitochondria in these cells [37]. In addition to this, to explore the cell physiological impact of loss of Cisd2, we developed Cisd2-KO human-induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) that were differentiated to cortical neurons. In both cell systems, we found that loss of Cisd2 resulted in loss of MERCS and impaired ER-mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer, revealing an important role of Cisd2 in regulating MERCS integrity irrespective of the cellular context. Yet, while HeLa cells were rather resilient towards loss of Cisd2 for their survival, mitochondrial metabolism and cell death susceptibility, hiPSC-derived cortical neurons lacking Cisd2 were particularly susceptible to cell stress inducers such as staurosporine. Furthermore, neurons lacking Cisd2 displayed impaired mitochondrial health, including reduced OXPHOS and ATP-production capacity. Of note, while loss of Cisd2 had miminal impact on agonist-evoked cytosolic Ca2+ signals in HeLa cells, it severely impaired such cytosolic Ca2+ signals in cortical neurons. This unique feature may explain why neurons are highly susceptible to lowered Cisd2-protein levels, thereby accounting for Wolfram syndrome symptoms related to Cisd2 dysfunction.

Given the emerging link between Cisd2 and intracellular Ca2+ signaling [39–41, 53, 57, 58, 70] and evidence of Cisd2 being engaged in a macrocomplex with IP3R1 [13], we examined whether Cisd2 could directly interact with IP3Rs, the main intracellular Ca2+-release channels. First, to study the interaction of Cisd2 and IP3R1 in isolation of other IP3R isoforms, we made use of Human Embryonic Kidney (HEK)293 cells TKO cells that re-expressed IP3R1. From cell lysates, IP3R1 was pulled down via immunoprecipitation (IP) with an antibody specifically targeting IP3R1 antibody [49]. Note that the input band of Cisd2 is overexposed in order to show the fainter co-immunoprecipitated Cisd2 band. Vice versa, IP of endougenous Cisd2 resulted in specific co-immunoprecipitated IP3R1 (Fig. 1B). We observed no interaction between CISD2 and purified GST, which acted as a negative control (Supplementary Fig. 1G). The KD values of the different Cisd2CYT/GST-Fragment interactions were determined by calculating and plotting the Fraction bound values (Fig. 1H). We observe the highest affinity for the interaction between Cisd2CYT and GST-ligand binding region (LBD) and GST-Fragment 3 (KD of ~ 500 nM and ~ 1 µM, respectively), marking amino acids 1–604 and 923–1581 of IP3R1 to likely harbor binding sites for Cisd2CYT. To assess functional effects of Cisd2 on IP3R-mediated Ca2+ signaling, we generated Cisd2 KO HeLa cell populations using a CRISPR/Cas9 approach in combination with a gRNA targeting Cisd2 (gRNACisd2), while expressing Cas9 in absence of gRNACisd2 served as control cells (CTRL), as described previously [40]. Note that generating a complete HeLa knockout cell population is challenging due to the potential chromosomal duplications. Nevertheless, our genetic ablation of Cisd2 was successful as almost no Cisd2 could be detected at the protein level in Cisd2-KO cells (Supplementary Fig. 2A and 2B). Additionally, immunofluorescent staining with a monoclonal Cisd2 antibody in HeLa CTRL and Cisd2 KO cells further underpins the absence of endogenous Cisd2 proteins in Cisd2 KO cells (Supplementary Fig. 2C). We evaluated the impact of loss of Cisd2 on resting cytosolic Ca2+ levels by comparing the values of the ratiometric Fura-2 Ca2+ sensor, loaded as Fura-2-AM in HeLa CTRL and Cisd2 KO cells. Fura-2 enables the measurement of resting cytosolic Ca2+ levels in a bleaching- and loading-independent manner making the dye the most suitable choice for these types of experiments. Fura-2 ratios were recorded using a fluorescence microscope for 30 s in single cells (Supplementary Fig. 2D for representative fluorescence acquisitions). Quantification of basal Fura-2 ratios during a 30 s recording revealed no major difference in basal [Ca2+]cyt (Supplementary Fig. 2E).

Furthermore, ER Ca2+ store content was evaluated by addition of thapsigargin, a high-affinity and irreversible inhibitor of SERCA-type Ca2+ pumps, while chelating extracellular Ca2+. Averaged traces of Cal520-loaded cell populations of HeLa CTRL and Cisd2 KO cells are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2F. Quantification of area under the curve (AUC) of the thapsigargin-releasable Ca2+ indicated that absence of Cisd2 did not affect the ER Ca2+-store content (Supplementary Fig. 2G). Moreover, Ca2+ responses to ionomycin were recorded to obtain a measure of total intracellular Ca2+ content (Supplementary Fig. 2J). Similarly, total intracellular Ca2+ content was not affected by absence of Cisd2 (Supplementary Fig. 2K). These results convey that, at least in HeLa cells, Cisd2 is not required for the maintenance of resting Ca2+ homeostasis.

Next, we evaluated the impact of Cisd2 deficiency on IP3R-mediated Ca2+ dynamics and ER-mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer. Both CTRL and Cisd2 KO cells exhibited a robust Ca2+ transient in the cytosol, although loss of Cisd2 resulted in a significantly reduced rate of cytosolic Ca2+ rise (Fig. 2B). The magnitude of cytosolic Ca2+ release of 5 µM ATP responses was quantified by analysis of area under curve (AUC) and was not significantly (p = 0.1), decreased in Cisd2 KO cells (Fig. 2C). In brief, HeLa cells were loaded with Mag Fluo 4, a low-affinity Ca2+ indicator. Extensive loading times allow the dye to be partially taken up in the ER. Finally, we evaluated whether the impact of Cisd2 depletion could sensitize cortical neurons to cell stress treatments such as staurosporine. Therefore, CTRL and Cisd2 KO cortical neurons were subjected to different concentrations of staurosporine and PARP ratios were determined through immunoblotting (Fig. 8C). Volumetric quantification of cleaved PARP over total PARP indicated that Cisd2-KO hiPSC-derived cortical neurons were more sensitive to staurosporine than CTRL hiPSC-derived cortical neurons in two ways: first, the threshold for inducing apoptosis in Cisd2-KO neurons is lower than that of control neurons (cfr 100 nM condition); second, at higher staurosporine concentrations (300 nM-1 µM), Cisd2 KO neurons display more apoptosis than control neurons (Fig. 8D).

Our study identifies a neuron-specific vulnerability to loss of Cisd2, highlighting profound mitochondrial and Ca2+ signaling defects in neurons that are less pronounced in mitotic cells such as HeLa cells. While both HeLa cells and cortical neurons lacking Cisd2 showed disrupted ER-mitochondria contact sites (Figs. 3 and 6) and reduced ER-to-mitochondria Ca2⁺ transfer (Figs. 2 and 7), only neurons displayed severe functional consequences: diminished mitochondrial membrane potential, suppressed oxphos and ATP production, and heightened sensitivity to apoptosis (Figs. 7 and 8). Of note, both Cisd2-deficient neurons and HeLa cells, exhibited an increase in autophagic flux (Figs. 5 and 8). This marked cell-type-specific divergence might suggest that Cisd2 plays a critical role in maintaining mitochondrial function and intracellular Ca2⁺ signaling specifically in post-mitotic neuronal cells. These differences may reflect unique dependencies in neurons on tightly regulated ER-mitochondrial communication and IP3R-mediated Ca2⁺ release, which appear less crucial in proliferative cell lines. Conceivably, compensatory mechanisms in HeLa cells could arise upon loss of Cisd2, whereas in the more stringent neuronal context, this might not be possible. Moreover, IP3R isoform distribution differs greatly in neurons compared to HeLa cells, with IP3R1 being the predominantly expressed IP3R isoform in neurons [56]. The possibility exists that effects of Cisd2 on IP3R, or downstream consequences of aberrant IP3R signaling, are isoform-specific. Additionally, the mitochondrial distribution and dynamics of post-mitotic neurons is fundamentally different than in proliferative cells [24].

Mechanistically, Cisd2 directly interacts with IP3R channels (Fig. 1), which are intracellular Ca2+-release channels not only delivering Ca2+ towards the cytosol but also towards the mitochondria. Our work suggests that the cytosolic Cisd2 primarily binds to the LBD, between amino acids 1–604, with an affinity of ~ 500 nM, and with a lower affinity (1 µM) to the modulatory domain of IP3R1 between amino acids 923–1581. The obtained KD values of the direct interactions were relatively high, suggesting the complex to be of a transient/regulatory nature, and/or to be dependent on one or multiple cofactors. Recently published work corroborated these findings and highlighted cysteine 101 of full length Cisd2 as essential for binding to IP3R1 [27].

In the HeLa and cortical neuron models that we generated, loss of Cisd2 impaired MERCS organization with a lowered ER-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer. Conversely, overexpression of Cisd2 restored ER-mitochondria contact site organization in both Cisd2-deficient cortical neurons and HeLa cells. The rescue was achieved using an N-terminally Flag₃-tagged Cisd2 construct. While the presence of the tag could potentially influence subcellular localization, the C-terminal ER-retention motif remained intact, supporting proper targeting of the expressed protein [5]. Despite the shared loss of MERCS integrity, only neurons, but not HeLa cells, displayed severe perturbations in their cellular functions upon loss of Cisd2, showing decreased mitochondrial potential, decreased oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production, accompanied with increased autophagic flux (Figs. 7 and 8), which is in agreement with previous work [13]. As a consequence, Cisd2-lacking neurons became more susceptible to staurosporine as a chemical cell stress inducer (Fig. 7), while Cisd2-lacking HeLa cells were rather unaffected (Figs. 5). The basis for this difference in apoptosis susceptibility between Cisd2-deficient neurons and HeLa cells is not fully clear. Cisd2 directly binds to anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 proteins [40], which prevent apoptosis by inhibiting Bax/Bak activity at mitochondria [18, 69] and by inhibiting IP3R activity at the ER [10, 32]. The impact of Cisd2 on cell death may therefore be dependent on the abundance of Bcl-2 complexes with Bax/Bak or with IP3Rs, which could be different in post-mitotic neurons versus HeLa cells. Our observation that loss of Cisd2 had more profound impacts on Ca2+ dynamics in neurons compared to HeLa cells, heavily suppressing cytosolic Ca2+ signals in neurons (Fig. 6) but not in HeLa cells exposed to physiological agonists (Fig. 2), supports the hypothesis that altered Ca2+ dependent cell death regulation may underlie the difference in apoptosis sensitivity between the neurons and HeLa cell model. Moreover, in this study, we have used staurosporine as an inducer of apoptosis, which can act via both IP3R-dependent and -independent mechanisms [6, 52]. Hence, further work should explore a broader scala of cell death triggers to better understand the role of Cisd2 in apoptotic and non-apoptotic cell death in neurons versus cancer cells. In that respect, different cancer cell models may display different dependencies on Cisd2 to prevent apoptosis, as Cisd2 knockdown resulted in apoptosis activation in epithelial breast cancer cells [28]. Moreover, expression of a dominant negative Cisd2 evoked ferroptosis in breast cancer cells by rapidly promoting the accumulation of mitochondrial labile iron and ROS with changes in Ca2+ homeostasis following only later on [36].

Of note, autophagic flux appeared increased in both HeLa (Fig. 5) and in neurons lacking Cisd2 (Fig. 8), thus suggesting that this process may be (in part) unrelated to impaired metabolism. This hints towards other—more direct—functions for Cisd2 in control of autophagy. In fact, Cisd2 has been reported to function as an important co-factor for Bcl-2, which operates as an anti-autophagic protein by scaffolding Beclin 1 [11, 13]. Hence, in absence of Cisd2, it’s possible that Bcl-2, endogenously present in HeLa and neurons, is impaired in suppressing autophagy, thereby explaining the increased autophagic flux in both HeLa cells and neurons. Alternatively, other mechanisms might be at play, since Cisd2 has been reported to prevent starvation-induced autophagy by inhibiting AMPK activity, at least in cardiomyocytes [21]. Given that mitochondrial health and turnover are tightly linked to autophagic processes, particularly through the selective degradation of mitochondria via mitophagy, it is plausible that part of the observed phenotype also involves dysregulation of mitophagy. Neurons in particular are very reliant on mitophagy to sustain bioenergetic needs [44]. Supporting this, the fruit fly protein Cisd, the homolog of Cisd2/Cisd1, has recently been implicated in mitophagy. Reduced Cisd levels led to increased mitophagy and autophagic flux. Moreover, loss of Pink1/Parkin function resulted in an accumulation of Cisd2, suggesting a possible regulatory link of Cisd2 with the mitophagy pathway [43]. The process of mitophagy is an interesting research area to be targeted in future endeavors. These future studies could shed more light on the co-occurrence of increased autophagic flux and the heightened sensitivity to cell death observed in iPSC-derived cortical neurons. In Alzheimer’s disease (AD) models of iPSC-derived neurons, mitophagy was shown to be impaired [23], and in a mouse model of AD, elevated Cisd2 expression conferred neuroprotective effects [15]. Collectively, these findings underscore the significance of mitophagy in cortical neurons and point to a potentially important role for Cisd2 in modulating this process.

A recent unbiased proteome-wide analysis of MAMs using Contact-ID, a Bio-ID-based labeling approach of proteins proximal to ER-mitochondria contact sites revealed 115 MAM-specific proteins of which Cisd2 was ranked among the top candidates [38]. Validating this screen, our findings indicate that, consistent with other reports [38, 40, 70], Cisd2 is a critical component for the adequate organization of MERCS in cells. Being one of the two Wolfram-linked genes, it appears that Wfs1 and Cisd2 execute converging functions at MERCS, as loss of either Wfs1 or Cisd2 results in decreased MERCS and impaired ER-mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer [17]. Moreover, recent work indicated that Wfs1 and Cisd2 interact with each other and that impaired ER Ca2+ release by loss of Wfs1 could be compensated by overexpression of Cisd2 [27, 39]. Furthermore, it’s fascinating that Cisd2 via its cytosolic region can directly interact with IP3Rs, yet this interaction does not seem to functionally control IP3R function as addition of the purified cytosolic Cisd2 fragment did not change the open probability of single IP3R channels [40]. This correlates with our findings in HeLa cells that displayed comparable agonist-evoked Ca2+ signals in the cytosol irrespective of the presence or absence of Cisd2 [40]. Of note, in those conditions, we have used intermediate agonist concentrations, in order to be able to detect both sensitizing and inhibitory effects. Instead, in neurons, loss of Cisd2 severely hampered cytosolic Ca2+ rises evoked by the physiological agonist glutamate. Moreover, these experiments were performed in absence of extracellular Ca2+, thus ensuring the Ca2+ release originated from the ER through IP3Rs. The diverging impact of Cisd2 on intracellular Ca2+ dynamics dependent on the cell type is fascinating and requires further study. Hence, it will be important to unravel the mechanisms responsible for this effect. It’s tempting to speculate that in neurons Cisd2 cooperates with other factors/proteins to support the adequate function of IP3Rs in mediating Ca2+ release. For instance, the impact of Wfs1 on MAMs and ER-mitochondria Ca2+ transfers is executed via NCS-1 [2, 47]. We hypothesize that NCS-1, an IP3R-accessory protein known to enhance IP3R function [48], may also cooperate with Cisd2. Accordingly, the loss of Cisd2 could potentially disrupt NCS-1 association with IP3Rs, thereby impairing its modulatory function. Moreover, the importance of IP3R-Cisd2 complexes and such potentially additional proteins for Cisd2’s impact on ER-mitochondrial integrity requires further scrutiny. Therefore, it will be of interest to assess whether Cisd2 requires the presence of IP3Rs to sustain ER-mitochondrial contact sites. It is indeed well appreciated that the IP3R plays an important role in ER-mitochondria tethering, and that it performs this function via a complex with GRP75 and VDAC1. However, multiple other proteins stabilize the ER-mitochondria tethering by interacting with IP3R1, e.g. pyruvate dehydrogenase lipoamide kinase isozyme 4 (PDK4), IP3R-binding protein released by IP3 (IRBIT), sigma 1 receptor, thymocyte-expressed positive selection-associated gene 1 (TESPA1) and cyclophilin D [19].

Furthermore, putting our findings in context with a previous study performed in patient fibroblasts carrying Cisd2 mutations, it was found that ER-mitochondria Ca2+ was increased in the latter [53]. In that study, it was found that Cisd2-mutated fibroblasts displayed increased ER-mitochondria Ca2+ transfer compared to age-matched controls and that ER-mitochondria contact sites (in terms of number of ER-mitochondria contact points / mm2, number of mitochondria with ER contact sites and length of mitochondria located adjacent to ER) are increased. Of interest, also in these patient fibroblasts, that represent a cell type likely not affected in Wolfram-syndrome type 2, mitochondrial morphology and oxidative phosphorylation function was only marginally affected, thereby resembling our findings obtained in HeLa cells at least with respect to mitochondrial function. Therefore, further work is needed to assess how Cisd2 mutations impact ER-mitochondria contact sites and ER-mitochondria contact site formation.

The differences with our findings are striking but could be due to different reasons. First, in our work, we have used isogenic cell models thereby allowing a careful comparison between wild-type and KO models, while Rouzier et al. [53] used cells derived from closely related individuals. Second, in our study, we have focused on KO/knockdown approaches thus lowering overall Cisd2-protein levels, while Rouzier et al. [53] have studied the impact of Cisd2 patient mutations associated with aberrant Cisd2 function. Hence, it’s certainly possible that loss of Cisd2 impairs ER-mitochondria contacts while some patient-associated Cisd2 mutations display gain-of-function properties for certain aspects. Further work will be needed to explore the impact of Cisd2 expression and mutations on cellular functions. Third, our work also reveals the critical importance of the cell type, e.g. the impact of Cisd2 mutations in the context of neurons, a cell type affected in WS2, could certainly be different than in fibroblasts, a cell type not affected in WS2.

In conclusion, our study highlights the critical role of Cisd2 in ER-mitochondria communication and its impact on cellular function, particularly in neurons. We demonstrate that Cisd2 directly interacts with IP3Rs and is essential for maintaining MERCS integrity and ER-mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer, both in cell lines such as HeLa cells as well as in WS-relevant cell models such as neurons. While loss of Cisd2 in HeLa cells did not majorly impact their function, in neurons, loss of Cisd2 results in profound mitochondrial defects, thereby rendering these cells highly susceptible to cellular stress, underscoring the cell-type-specific functions of Cisd2. Furthermore, loss of Cisd2 in neurons, but much less so in HeLa cells, is accompanied by severely suppressed IP3R-mediated Ca2+ release. These findings raise important questions regarding the molecular mechanisms by which Cisd2 modulates intracellular Ca2+ dynamics, potentially involving interactions with other regulatory proteins such as NCS-1. Using these Cisd2-KO neuronal cell models, future studies studying the impact of WS2-linked Cisd2 mutations on ER-mitochondria contact sites and Ca2+ transfer will be of interest.
