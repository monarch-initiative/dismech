BACKGROUND AND PURPOSE: Multiple acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (MADD) is an 
inherited disorder of fatty acid oxidation that causes lipid storage myopathy 
(LSM). This is the first report on MADD that describes the phenotypic and 
genetic features of a Malaysian cohort.
METHODS: Among the >2,500 patients in a local muscle biopsy database, patients 
with LSM were identified and their genomic DNA were extracted from muscle 
samples and peripheral blood. All 13 exons of the electron-transfer flavoprotein 
dehydrogenase gene (ETFDH) were subsequently sequenced. Fifty controls were 
included to determine the prevalence of identified mutations in the normal 
population.
RESULTS: Fourteen (82%) of the 17 LSM patients had MADD with ETFDH mutations. 
Twelve (86%) were Chinese and two were Malay sisters. Other unrelated patients 
reported that they had no relevant family history. Nine (64%) were females. The 
median age at onset was 18.5 years (interquartile range=16-37 years). All 14 
demonstrated proximal limb weakness, elevated serum creatine kinase levels, and 
myopathic changes in electromyography. Three patients experienced a metabolic 
crisis at their presentation. Sanger sequencing of ETFDH revealed nine different 
variants/mutations, one of which was novel: c.998A>G (p.Y333C) in exon 9. 
Notably, 12 (86%) patients, including the 2 Malay sisters, carried a common 
c.250G>A (p.A84T) variant, consistent with the hotspot mutation reported in 
southern China. All of the patients responded well to riboflavin therapy.
CONCLUSIONS: Most of our Malaysian cohort with LSM had late-onset, 
riboflavin-responsive MADD with ETFDH mutations, and they demonstrated 
phenotypic and genetic features similar to those of cases reported in southern 
China. Furthermore, we report a novel ETFDH mutation and possibly the first ever 
MADD patients of Malay descent.

Lipid storage myopathies (LSMs) are rare autosomal recessive inherited disorders caused by defects in fatty acid metabolism leading to lipid accumulation in muscle fibers.1 There are four main causes of LSM that can be diagnosed through genetic analyses: 1) primary carnitine deficiency, 2) multiple acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (MADD), 3) neutral lipid storage disease with myopathy, and 4) neutral lipid storage disease with ichthyosis.2

MADD (OMIM 231680), also known as glutaric aciduria type II, is a genetic disorder with an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern. Patients with MADD may experience progressive muscle weakness or episodic muscle weakness, myalgia, and rhabdomyolysis, with an onset in infancy, adolescence, or adulthood.3 Specifically, MADD can be categorized into three phenotypes: type I (neonatal onset with congenital anomalies; OMIM 608053), type II (neonatal onset without congenital anomalies; OMIM 231680), and type III (late onset; OMIM 231680).456 Patients with neonatal-onset MADD often succumb during the neonatal period due to nonketotic hypoglycemia, excretion of substantial amounts of fatty-acid- and amino-acid-derived metabolites, and severe metabolic acidosis. Type III (late-onset) MADD is milder, commonly presenting with episodes of hypoglycemia, vomiting, and proximal muscle weakness, usually triggered by metabolic stress.78910

Lipid accumulation in MADD is due to failure of electron transfer from acyl-CoA dehydrogenases to the respiratory chain in β-oxidation process, which results in free fatty acids not being oxidized.511 Electron-transfer flavoprotein (ETF) and ETF-ubiquinone oxidoreductase (ETF-QO) play a major role in this process, and mutations in ETF-α (OMIM 608053), ETF-β (OMIM 130410), or ETFDH (ETF-dehydrogenase gene; OMIM 231675) will lead to defects of ETF (encoded by ETF-α and -β) or ETF-QO.412 A mutation in ETFDH is usually associated with riboflavin-responsive MADD, with most patients either partly or fully recovering after an oral riboflavin supplement and a low-fat diet are initiated.8911121314

Most of the MADD cases reported globally are the type III (late-onset) subtype with ETFDH mutations.15 MADD with ETFDH mutations has been reported to be more prominent in Chinese patients, with especially the c.250G>A (p.A84T) variant being the most common hotspot variant in southern China, while c.770A>G (p.Y257C) and c.1227A>C (p.L409F) are common variants in both northern and southern China.46891617181920 Furthermore, MADD cases with the c.250G>A (p.A84T) variant have also been reported in Taiwan, Hong Kong, Thailand, and Singapore.1016182122 We aimed to provide the first report on MADD in Malaysia that describes the phenotypic and genetic features of a Malaysian cohort.

The University of Malaya muscle biopsy database spans from 1994 to the present and encompasses >2,500 patients including cases referred from other Malaysian healthcare centers nationwide. This database was searched to identify patients with late-onset LSM (symptom onset at an age of ≥1 year). The patients identified were counseled for genetic testing for ETFDH mutations. Their medical history, findings of physical examinations, and results of biochemical, electrophysiological, histopathological, and genetic investigations were obtained through both reviewing medical records and prospective assessments. In addition, symptomatic family members of these identified patients were counseled for genetic testing as well for possible inclusion in this study. Informed consent was obtained from all of the included subjects. This study complied with the principles of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the University of Malaya (MEC ID No: 20146-296).

Clinical assessments included the relevant medical history (including the family history) and physical examinations. Patients underwent electrophysiological studies, namely nerve conduction studies and electromyography (EMG; Synergy EDX, Natus Medical, Pleasanton, CA, USA). Biochemical investigations included serum creatine kinase (CK) levels.

Common muscle biopsy sites included the biceps, deltoid, and quadriceps. Fresh frozen muscle specimens were sectioned at a thickness of 8 µm for use in routine histochemistry staining, including with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), modified Gomori trichrome, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH)-tetrazolium reductase, acid phosphatase, and ATPase (pH 4.6). In addition, Oil Red O (ORO), Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS), and PAS-diastase (PAS-D) staining procedures were performed to confirm the presence of LSM.

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from ten 8-µm-thick frozen muscle sections and whole blood using a QIAamp DNA Mini Kit and QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit, respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). ETFDH was amplified from gDNA using previously described primers and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) conditions,4 except for the primers covering exon 8, which were designed in-house. The primer set for exon 8 was redesigned to cover the coding sequence completely with the flanking intronic region and the forward and reverse primers 5′-GCAAGTTTTATGCATTGTGGTGA-3′ and 5′-ACAGTCTTCAGATTCAAAACCCT-3′, respectively. It was optimized at an annealing temperature of 60℃. All 13 coding exons were covered, including the flanking intronic regions.

All nucleotides and amino-acid references are based on the Ensembl reference sequence ENST00000511912 (https://www.ensembl.org/Homo_sapiens/Transcript/Summary?db=core;g=ENSG00000171503;r=4:158672296-158709623;t=ENST00000511912). The specificity of the PCR products was assessed using 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. Amplified PCR products were purified with a QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Qiagen) and then sent for Sanger sequencing. Sequence Scanner Software (version 2, Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), and Align Plus software (version 4, Scientific & Educational Central, USA) were used for sequence data analysis.

The impact of the mutations on the biological function of the protein was predicted using Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant (SIFT) and Protein Variation Effect Analyzer (PROVEAN) analysis software. Thresholds of a SIFT low-confidence prediction with a median conservation is >3.25, while PROVEAN scores for common polymorphisms are >-2.5 and for disease variants are ≤-2.5.2324 In addition, 100 chromosomes from 50 healthy Malaysian controls were directly sequenced to determine the prevalence of identified mutations in the normal population.

Two of the patients with confirmed ETFDH mutations received additional peripheral-blood gene-panel testing, and another two patients underwent peripheral-blood whole-exome sequencing (WES) in external laboratories. Therefore, these four patients were evaluated for ETF-α and ETF-β mutations as well.

The Statistical Package for Social Sciences (version 28, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for the statistical analyses. Quantitative variables are presented as frequency (percentage) values for categorical data and as median [interquartile range] values for continuous data with a skewed distribution and for ordinal data. The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to assess whether data conformed to a normal distribution.

Fourteen (82%) of the 17 identified patients with late-onset LSM demonstrated ETFDH mutations and hence were diagnosed with late-onset MADD. The clinical, biochemical, electrophysiological, and histopathological features of these 14 patients are summarized in Table 1. These 14 patients included 9 (64%) females. Twelve (86%) were Chinese and two (14%) were Malay siblings. The age at onset was 18.5 [16–37] years. The age at muscle biopsy, and hence the LSM diagnosis, was 24 [16–44] years. The age at MADD diagnosis was 24 [19–47] years. The disease duration was 11 [6–15] years. In particular, the delay from the LSM diagnosis based on a muscle biopsy to the MADD diagnosis was 3 [0–6] years.

The common clinical symptoms in all 14 patients were exercise intolerance and symmetrical proximal muscle weakness with episodic worsening or deterioration. Six (43%) patients had neck weakness and/or head drop, three (21%) complained of dysphagia, and two (14%) experienced myalgia over the proximal muscles. None reported significant gastrointestinal symptoms. Acute severe exacerbation of weakness with respiratory insufficiency occurred in three (21%) patients, who require mechanical ventilation at their first presentation. One of these three patients was further complicated by severe metabolic crisis, rhabdomyolysis, acute renal failure, and died during the same intensive care unit admission. The levels of serum CK were elevated in all 14 patients, ranging from a borderline elevation to >150 times the upper limit of normal (in Patient 9, who presented with severe metabolic crisis and rhabdomyolysis). EMG showed a myopathic pattern in all 14 patients.

In the muscle histopathology analyses, H&E staining revealed mild fiber-size variations and numerous small round vacuoles in the muscle fibers (predominantly in type I fibers) in all patients except Patient 5 who did not undergo muscle biopsy. Patient 5 was the sister of Patient 4 (the Malay sisters) and they presented with similar proximal weakness. ORO staining demonstrated lipid accumulation in the small vacuoles that were seen in H&E staining (Fig. 1), while coarse granular staining was observed in NADH staining. No abnormal glycogen was demonstrated by PAS and PAS-D staining.

Eight different missense mutations and 1 deletion (premature translational stop signal) were found in coding exons of ETFDH of all 14 patients. Notably, 12 (86%) patients carried the common c.250G>A (p.A84T) pathological variant: 8 homozygous and 4 compound heterozygous (Table 1). Sequence analysis of gDNA from Patient 8 revealed a novel heterozygous variant in exon 9: c.998A>G (p.Y333C). This novel variant was predicted as deleterious by the SIFT and PROVEAN analysis software with scores of 2.97 and -7.486, respectively. No carrier of this variant was found in 100 alleles of healthy Malaysian controls. Moreover, the alignment of amino acid sequences revealed that tyrosine at codon 333 was highly conserved in most of the higher eukaryotes.

Upon confirming the MADD diagnosis, all 13 surviving patients were promptly started on riboflavin supplementation at 100 mg/day. All of them improved dramatically, with complete resolution of the proximal weakness and other symptoms along with normalization of serum CK levels. In addition, throughout the follow-up duration of 9.5 [5–14] years and the riboflavin supplementation duration of 8 [6–8] years, all 13 patients remained asymptomatic and in remission with no further deterioration or relapse of symptoms while being on continuous riboflavin supplementation at a standard maintenance dosage of 100 mg/day.

The clinical, biochemical, electrophysiological, and histopathological features of our Malaysia MADD patients were similar to the reported phenotype of late-onset MADD worldwide.2678910161821 Most of these reports involved Chinese patients, and 86% of our Malaysian cohort comprised Malaysian Chinese. Notably, among our 14 Malaysian MADD patients with ETFDH mutations, 86% of them (including the 2 Malay sisters) carried the common c.250G>A (p.A84T) pathological variant that is the hotspot mutation in southern China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong.46891016181920 The frequency of 86% (c.250G>A in MADD with ETFDH mutations) in our cohort is close to the reported high frequencies (up to 81%) in southern China and Taiwan.691920

Historically, southern Min Chinese are believed to have migrated from central mainland China to the southern region and subsequently further migrated to the Southeast Asia region, including Malaysia. Accordingly, the geographical distribution of the c.250G>A variant is similar to the distribution and migration route of the southern Min Chinese over the past few centuries.20 Malaysian Chinese probably originate from southern China, which may explain the genotypic and phenotypic similarities described above. Such postulation might also be applicable to the few reported cases in Thailand and Singapore.2122 Furthermore, we postulate that the two Malay sisters with this hotspot mutation could have had a certain extent of Chinese ancestry from centuries ago that they were not aware of. Regardless, to the best of our knowledge, they are the first reported MADD patients of Malay descent.

Chen et al.20 reviewed the epidemiology of ETFDH mutations among 381 published MADD cases globally. The six most common variants were c.250G>A (28.1%) in Chinese/Asian populations, c.770A>G (12.9%) in Chinese/Asian populations, c.1227A>C (8.9%) in Chinese populations, c.1130T>C (6.3%) in a Turkish populations, c.389A>T (5.8%) in a Chinese populations, and c.1367C>T (2.4%) in Caucasian/Japanese populations. In particular, there have been no reported MADD cases with the c.250G>A variant among Korean, Japanese, Israeli, and Caucasian populations.20252627 Similarly, comparing the population frequencies of the c.250G>A variant across regions in public population databases revealed that the frequency was consistently highest among Asians.2829

Meanwhile, we detected a novel ETFDH mutation, namely c.998A>G (p.Y333C) in exon 9 (ETFDH, exon 9, c.998A>G, p.Y333C, heterozygous, pathogenic, MADD, autosomal recessive). There is no record of this variant in any public population database or disease database. We hypothesize that this novel heterozygous variant is pathogenic/likely pathogenic since 1) it was not detected in the 50 normal controls, 2) it was predicted as deleterious in the SIFT and PROVEAN analyses, and 3) the affected amino acids were highly conserved in higher eukaryotes. This finding might expand the spectrum of ETFDH mutations in MADD reported globally.

Grünert8 reviewed 350 cases of late-onset MADD globally and reported on their clinical features.8 The clinical features of these 350 cases were similar to those of our cohort, namely 1) acute symptoms in 33% vs. 21%, 2) mean age at onset of 19.2 years vs. a median of 18.5 years, 3) mean diagnostic delay of 3.9 years vs. a median of 3 years, and 4) mortality rate of 5.2% vs. 7.1%. In addition, Zhu et al.9 reported clinical features of 148 MADD cases in mainland China that were also similar to those of our cohort, namely 1) proximal limb weakness in 100% vs. 98.7%, 2) neck weakness in 42.9% vs. 54.9%, and 3) dysphagia in 21.4% vs. 21.0%.

All of the patients in our cohort improved dramatically and promptly recovered upon initiation of riboflavin supplementation at 100 mg/day. This encouraging response is consistent with other reports.89 In particular, Grünert8 reported a 98.4% responsiveness to riboflavin among 350 late-onset MADD patients. Moreover, Liu et al.30 suggested that the duration of riboflavin supplementation for patients with late-onset MADD should be longer than 1 month, since almost half of their patients still presented with elevated muscle enzymes and reduced muscle strength after 1 month of riboflavin therapy. This supports our decision to maintain all of our patients on long-term riboflavin supplementation, which is an easily available, accessible, and affordable therapy that keeps these patients in stable remission.

Clinical manifestations of MADD are heterogeneous. Therefore, making or confirming a MADD diagnosis can be especially challenging in resource-limited settings, which applies to most low- and middle-income countries in view of the low availability and accessibility of genetic testing. In particular, genetic testing is not available in the vast majority of Malaysian hospitals, while gene panels, WES, and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) outsourced to laboratories in other countries are too costly for most Malaysians. Correspondingly, the delay from the LSM diagnosis based on muscle biopsy to the MADD diagnosis was 3 [0–6] years in our cohort, since the research grant that supported the ETFDH sequencing from frozen muscle biopsy samples (as part of academic research initiatives instead of routine clinical practice) was only available from 2015 onward. We therefore propose that empirical therapy with riboflavin supplementation can be considered for patients with suggestive clinical features and histologically confirmed LSM even when definitive genetic testing has not been performed, especially in regional resource-limited centers. In addition, a positive response to riboflavin supplementation may also support the working diagnosis of MADD. Still, since ETFDH mutation is the most commonly reported mutation in late-onset MADD worldwide, a targeted genetic analysis of ETFDH, which is probably less costly than other genetic tests (e.g., gene panels, WES, and WGS), is strongly advocated whenever feasible, even in resource-limited settings.

One of the limitations of our study was muscle biopsy being the entry point. MADD patients who did not undergo muscle biopsy were not included in our analyses. These patients might have been asymptomatic or had a relatively mild condition, and hence did not seek medical investigations. Meanwhile, symptomatic patients with more-severe clinical features were assumed to be keener to undergo further investigations (especially muscle biopsy and genetic testing) and management. Therefore, selecting study subjects from a muscle biopsy database might have resulted in selection bias toward patients at the more-severe end of the disease spectrum.

Another limitation of this study was the lack of detailed, comprehensive clinical data of some patients due to its retrospective design. Moreover, in view of the long disease duration, namely 11 [7–14] years, most patients had great difficulty recalling the details of the clinical features (symptoms and medical history) at their disease onset and presentation. Furthermore, since the disease onset occurred at 11 [7–14] years prior to recruitment/inclusion, the attempts by patients to recall the early clinical features might have resulted in significant recall bias and inaccuracy. In addition, most patients presented to or sought consultation at other centers before being referred to our center for a muscle biopsy. This imposed challenges in accessing the earlier clinical data of patients at other hospitals since those records were either incomplete or no longer available. However, throughout the follow-up duration of 9.5 [6–13] years at our center, all assessments were conducted prospectively and clinical data were documented contemporaneously. Therefore, the available clinical data were highly likely to be accurate and reliable.

Other limitations of this study included being performed at a single center and the lack of genetic analyses of ETF-α and ETF-β in 10 of the 14 patients. However, the substantial number of patients in our muscle biopsy database, combined with most of them being cases referred from other centers nationwide, may mean that they were representative of the entire Malaysian population. Furthermore, it has been extensively reported that ETFDH mutation is far more common than mutations of ETF-α, ETF-β, and other genes in late-onset MADD, and so even such a targeted genetic analysis of a single gene may provide a satisfactory detection rate.815 This is supported by 82% of our patients with histologically confirmed LSM having late-onset MADD with ETFDH mutations. No ETF-α or ETF-β mutation was found in the four patients who underwent additional gene-panel testing and WES.

Strengths of this study include: firstly, it being the first to investigate MADD in Malaysia; secondly, it being the first to involve MADD patients of Malay descent; and thirdly, it identified a novel pathogenic variant that may broaden the current spectrum of known ETFDH mutations in MADD globally. Moreover, reports on MADD have been scarce throughout Southeast Asia region, with only case reports of two siblings in Thailand and of one Singaporean Chinese being available previously.2122 Accordingly, our study of a cohort of 14 patients may expand the current body of evidence regionally.

In conclusion, most (82%) of our Malaysian cohort with histologically confirmed LSM were late-onset (type III), riboflavin-responsive MADD with ETFDH mutations. In addition, most (86%) of these patients with ETFDH mutations harbored a common hotspot mutation, namely the c.250G>A (p.A84T) pathological variant. Overall, the phenotypic and genetic features of our cohort were similar to the descriptions of other late-onset MADD cases globally, especially those reported in southern China and Taiwan. Furthermore, we discovered a novel ETFDH mutation, namely c.998A>G (p.Y333C) in exon 9, and possibly the first MADD patients of Malay descent.
