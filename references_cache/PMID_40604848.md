BACKGROUND: The KCNH1 gene (OMIM #603,305) encodes a voltage-gated potassium 
channel primarily found in the central nervous system. Recent discoveries have 
linked pathogenic variations in this gene to Temple-Baraitser syndrome (TMBTS, 
OMIM #611,816) and Zimmermann-Laband syndrome (ZLS, OMIM #135,500). A common 
manifestation of these syndromes is gingival fibromatosis, which may partially 
or completely cover tooth crowns, leading in some cases to functional and 
aesthetic problems, as well as delayed tooth eruption.
CASE PRESENTATION: A four-year-old boy and his parents first consulted for 
delayed primary molars eruption. Shortly after birth, he was diagnosed with a 
developmental encephalopathy caused by a de novo pathogenic variant in KCNH1.
TREATMENT: The first step of oral treatment consisted of myofunctional and 
speech/language therapy to stimulate biting and chewing. It also helped with the 
rehabilitation of proper tongue function. This was followed by a gingivoplasty 
to expose the submerged teeth. We propose a clinical approach to optimize 
disease management. This aims to minimize complications associated with this 
rare disorder.
CONCLUSION: This case illustrates the need for appropriate and early 
gingivoplasty to prevent teeth impaction and restore dental function. 
Additionally, it explores potential complications and provides grounds for a 
comprehensive protocol for managing gingival fibromatosis for patients with 
KCNH1 variant.

Genetic variants, known as alterations in the DNA sequence from the reference genome, play a key role in human diversity and disease. While many are benign polymorphisms, others can disrupt gene function with significant clinical consequences. Among these, pathogenic variants in the KCNH1 gene, which encodes a voltage-gated potassium channel critical for neurodevelopment and cellular excitability, have been implicated in rare syndromes such as Zimmermann-Laband syndrome (ZLS, OMIM #135,500) and Temple-Baraitser syndrome (TMBTS, OMIM #611,816). ZLS is characterized by intellectual disability, epilepsy, morphological facial features, enlarged gingiva, hypertrichosis and hypoplasia or aplasia of nails and terminal phalanges [1, 2]. On the other hand, individuals with TMBTS typically display developmental delay (neonatal hypotonia, delayed developmental milestones), epilepsy, broad thumbs and great toes along with missing or underdeveloped nails. In some cases, morphological facial features are characterized by a pseudo-myopathic appearance [1]. Other facial features such as high anterior or low frontal hairline, flat forehead, ptosis, hypertelorism, down slating palpebral fissures, epicanthal folds, thick helices, long philtrum, high-arched palate, broad mouth with thick vermilion border of the lower lip and downturned corners, were also reported. In addition, all patients experienced seizures, with onset occurring at varying age [3].

ZLS differs from TMBTS by the presence of gingival fibromatosis, coarse facial features, hypertrichosis, hepatosplenomegaly, and more severe digital anomalies [3].

According to the Orphanet database (www.orpha.net), the prevalence of these disorders is less than 1/1,000,000. Around 60 patients have been identified for both diseases (around 50 for ZLS and 9 for TMBTS).

ZLS exhibits genetic heterogeneity. It is associated with heterozygous gain-of-function missense variants in the KCNH1 (1q32.2) and KCNN3 (1q21.3) genes, and less frequently, recurrent missense variants in the ATP6 V1B2 (8p21.3) gene [4]. Additionally, ZLS shares allelic characteristics with TMBTS, wherein gain-of-function variations in the KCNH1 gene are also responsible for the condition [5]. Both syndromes can be caused by the same variants, and some individuals with KCNH1 variants show symptoms that do not fully align with TMBTS or ZLS. This suggests that these conditions may fall within a broader KCNH1-related disorder spectrum [2, 6].

In the oral cavity, the phenotype associated with variants in the gene KCNH1 is characterized by the presence of a progressive and diffuse gingival hypertrophy, often with multiple unerupted teeth [2]. Hereditary gingival fibromatosis is an uncommon condition that may manifest independently, as a gene anomaly, or as a component of various syndromes. This disorder presents a gradual and benign thickening of the keratinized gingiva, either localized or generalized, with slow progression. The excess tissue may cover partially or completely the crowns of the teeth, causing functional, esthetic and periodontal problems. Delayed tooth eruption or primary failure of tooth eruption may occur in some cases [7].

The objective of this paper is to describe the gingival fibromatosis and its surgical management by gingivoplasty of a four-year-old boy with a variant in the KCNH1 gene. Moreover, a clinical management protocol is proposed to optimize the intervention and reduce complications.

A four-year-old patient was referred by his general dentist to the National Reference Center for Rare Oral and Dental Diseases (CRMR O-Rares) in Strasbourg for gingival fibromatosis in June 2023. The primary reason for consultation was a delayed eruption of the primary molars. The patient was unable to bite and chew properly, and his diet was mainly liquid and soft.

The clinical phenotype was detailed and collected in the D[4]/phenodent register (approved by the Commission Nationale de l'Informatique et des Libertés CNIL N°908,416 www.phenodent.org). The patient and his parents agreed to participate in the clinical research project (https://clinicaltrials.gov: NCT02397824) and provided written consent for genetic testing.

The patient is the only child of unrelated parents. Family investigation revealed a sudden death at 44 years old on the father's side of the family. On the mother’s side, a first cousin (son of an aunt) died from a heart malformation, and another first cousin (son of an uncle) has severe intellectual disability. No family members presented with a syndrome similar to the patient’s.

Pregnancy was spontaneous, with normal ultrasound monitoring. The patient was born at full term, weighing 3060 g, measuring 48 cm in length, and with a head circumference of 34 cm. He had congenital torticollis and left plagiocephaly, which were managed with physiotherapy.

The first generalized tonic–clonic seizure happened at the age of 7 months, with recurrence during hospitalization. The patient is currently treated with sodium valproate and has had few recurrences since the first episode, some occurring spontaneously, some fever induced.

In terms of psychomotor development, parents describe him as hypotonic since birth. At 7 months, before his first seizure, he was unable to sit with assistance. After the seizure there was a plateau in his developmental milestones, without any observed regression.

Additional examinations, including brain MRI and metabolic screening, yielded normal results. The pediatrician recommended further evaluation with cardiac and abdominal ultrasounds, both of which were normal, as well as an ophthalmological assessment that revealed fatigue-related strabismus. Optical correction was implemented and well tolerated.

The child has experienced chronic, variable vomiting since shortly after birth, attributed to pathological acid gastroesophageal reflux confirmed by pH monitoring without associated esophagitis, with mild cardia insufficiency in the context of neurologically induced hypotonia. The symptoms were treated with esomeprazole and sodium alginate.

The child presents facial traits such as long eyelashes, as well as anomalies in the extremities including hypoplasia of the thumbnails and big toes. His developmental delay is characterized by initial hypotonia, a myopathy-like appearance (hypotonia and motor delay) in the absence of structural or biochemical muscular abnormalities, motor developmental delay, non-verbal communication, and subsequently severe intellectual disability. Autism spectrum symptoms have also been observed [1].

While epilepsy can manifest in different types ranging from more to less drug-resistant [9], the fact that the child has not had a seizure since the first hospitalization is a very positive element in this context.

This young boy exhibits pediatric feeding disorders, characterized by an impaired ability to consume food in an appropriate manner for his age. His difficulties extend beyond simple feeding challenges. These issues can significantly affect his growth and development, requiring a multidisciplinary approach for assessment and intervention.

The intraoral examination reveals good oral hygiene with a small amount of plaque on the surface of the teeth. The primary dentition does not show visible abnormalities in number or structure. The maxillary second temporary molars are partly covered by thick gums, and only their occlusal surfaces are visible in the mouth, except for the left maxillary second molar (Fig. 1). The excess gingival tissue covers part of or the entire crown and creates diastemas and retention of primary teeth. Bleeding on probing (BOP) is present in all areas, with a gingival index (GI) of 1 [10]. The periodontal probing depth ranges from 4 to 8 mm.

The functional examination reveals that gingival fibromatosis causes masticatory problems in occlusion: during static occlusion, no dental contacts are found but rather gingival and gingivodental contacts.

The intra-oral radiographs reveal no significant resorption of the alveolar bone or ankylosis (Fig. 1).

The analysis method involves capturing coding regions through hybridization (QXT, Agilent) followed by high-throughput paired-end 75 bp sequencing (NextSeq550, Illumina). Then, the genomic data analysis was performed using a bioinformatics pipeline associated with the University of Paris, pipeline from the Institute of Medical Genetics of Alsace, STARK and VaRank [11]. The detection sensitivity is over 99% for nucleotide substitutions, while the rate is lower for indels.

This analysis reveals a de novo variant in the KCNH1 gene, confirmed by parental analysis and identity verification using nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). On chromosome 1, a heterozygous variant has been found in the KCNH1 gene at position NM_172362.2:c.1070G > A, resulting in the amino acid change p.(Arg357Gln). This genetic variation alters an amino acid highly conserved across different species, indicating its functional importance. The software tools SIFT (v6.2.0), MutationTaster (v2021), AlphaMissense and PolyPhen-2 predict that this change is likely deleterious [12, 13].

This variation has been reported several times as pathogenic in individuals with Temple-Baraitser syndrome or Zimmermann-Laband syndrome, both of which are associated with epilepsy, hypotonia and intellectual disability [12, 13]. To our knowledge, it has never been reported in the GnomAD (v4) database that compiles genetic variations from healthy individuals.

The initial step of the oral cavity issues management was through an oral disorder rehabilitation teaching biting and chewing [14]. For infants, learning how to bite and chew is a crucial stage of feeding development. Physiologically, at around 5–6 months of age, they initiate oral exploration using their fingers and mouth, employing a bite and release pattern. The progression of biting and chewing skills advances from this stage onward, with the infant honing the movements of their jaw, tongue, and lips. If infants fail to progress through certain aspects of this developmental process, intervention may be required to facilitate the acquisition of biting and chewing abilities. Chewing dysfunction often results from sensory or motor impairments and may require intervention.

The goal of the oral disorder rehabilitation in this case is to decrease oral hypersensitivity, stimulate a correct chewing pattern, help stimulate tooth re-eruption and reduce risks of fibromatosis relapse after surgery. This rehabilitation is performed by specialized speech therapists in France.

This first step was not sufficient in our patient, and surgery was therefore indicated to reduce the gingival interference.

Given the extent and complexity of the procedure and the lack of cooperation due to the child's intellectual disability, it was decided to perform the surgery under general anesthesia.

Following disinfection with povidone-iodine (Betadine®) and local infiltration anesthesia using articaine and epinephrine, the depth of gingival overgrowth was evaluated using a periodontal probe, and excess gingiva was excised. A subsequent external bevel incision was performed using an electric scalpel (Fig. 5). This surgical device uses high-frequency electrical currents to cut or clot tissues and cauterize them simultaneously.Fig. 5Incision line for gingivectomy using an electric scalpel

The histologic analysis of the lesion revealed epithelial acanthosis with hyperparakeratosis. The rete ridges were elongated getting through the underlying connective tissue. The connective tissue was dense, showing numerous collagen fiber bundles, fibroblasts and a few blood vessels. A mild chronic inflammatory cell infiltrate was also observed. The parents were instructed to massage the gum with choline salicylate, a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory gel, which alleviates pain and inflammation by inhibiting the release of prostaglandins, and cetalkonium chloride, an antiseptic that eradicates bacteria and fungi, guarding against mouth ulcer infection and accelerating healing. The parents could alternate with a chlorhexidine gel (0.2%) to control gum inflammation.

Oral hygiene was maintained with a surgical toothbrush for brushing. No antibiotics were prescribed, only analgesics when needed.

The patient was discharged a few hours after surgery but later reported severe blood loss during 5 days. Profuse bleeding around the gingiva on the upper right side was frequently observed after eating firm food. The screening tests of hemostasis and thrombosis were within the normal range.

Local compression techniques with tranexamic acid were prescribed for 5 days.

At 5 days post-surgery, the gum tissue exhibited normal stages of healing. Most granulation tissue had disappeared, and even if the gum showed some signs of inflammation, the regeneration process was in progress.

The child also showed severe signs of discomfort over 2 weeks after the surgery, despite satisfactory wound healing (Fig. 10).Fig. 10Clinical appearance of the gingiva 10 days after surgery

The parents reported multiple episodes of pain throughout the day. According to them, the child exhibited occasional chin quivering, jaw clenching, restless and tense behavior, accompanied by frequent complaints, making soothing difficult. Even though pain can be difficult to access in children with intellectual disabilities, it was clear the boy was experiencing some sort of discomfort or irritation. In fact, no visible intraoral clinical abnormalities were detected aside from a generalized gingival inflammation. Exploration of the digestive system revealed no clinical evidence of lesions.

Due to his syndrome, the risk of epileptic seizures, the anorexia, and the unclear cause of pain, he was hospitalized for observation and administration of continuous systemic grade II analgesics. The parents were strongly recommended to brush their child's teeth and apply chlorhexidine gel twice daily to eliminate dental plaque and facilitate gingival healing. Before this, they had been unable to carry out systematic oral hygiene procedures due to the risk of bleeding and discomfort.

Over the following days, the pain level decreased and the gum healed completely.

At one-month post-surgery, the child seemed very eager to chew and use his molars. The diet improved with the introduction of a variety of solid foods. Speech and language therapy is still ongoing.

Hereditary gingival fibromatosis is a rare condition marked by a gradual, benign, localized, or widespread overgrowth of the gingival tissues, characterized by the expansion and accumulation of connective tissue along with occasional cell proliferation.

In this case, this disease is linked to a syndrome linked to the presence of a pathogenic variation in the KCNH1 gene causing a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy [7]. This young patient presented generalized gingival hypertrophy that interfered with the normal eruption of primary and permanent teeth in the oral cavity, with a significant impact on his masticatory function. As around 80% of children with developmental disorders, this young boy exhibits pediatric feeding disorders defined as impaired oral intake that is not age appropriate and are associated with feeding skill, medical, nutrition and/or psychosocial dysfunction [16].

Other signs reported, not found in the child, but for which vigilance is necessary, include growth delay, scoliosis, and hepatosplenomegaly. Digestive disorders such as constipation and reflux are also common in early childhood but are not present in this case. Typically, brain MRI is normal, as in the patient’s case. Cardiac or renal malformations are rare and have been ruled out in the patient. There does not appear to be a predisposition to ophthalmological or ENT (Ear, Nose, and Throat) disorders.

The treatment involved oral disorders rehabilitation using chewing toys. It was useful to habituate the patient to the use of the chews as early as possible to ensure the longevity of the postoperative outcome. However, when this proved not to be totally effective, surgical gingivoplasty using an electric scalpel was carried out even though gingival fibromatosis requiring surgical intervention during early primary dentition is a rare occurrence. An intense bleeding and a prolonged painful state followed the surgery. After an appropriate treatment, the long-term evolution was favorable, with a good masticatory function and no relapse of gingival hypertrophy.

Compared with the use of an ordinary scalpel, electrosurgery may result in less blood loss, faster tissue separation and a lower risk of injury to surgeons, although the data in the literature are hardly conclusive [17].

Other studies have noted that the laser diode presents several advantages over the traditional scalpel, including reduced intraoperative bleeding, decreased swelling in the treated area, improved coagulation and wound healing, no need for sutures, shorter surgical time, and decreased postoperative pain [18]. Moreover, the laser provides immediate disinfection of the surgical site, and there are fewer instances of additional mechanical trauma. Similarly, the CO2 laser offers comparable benefits to the conventional scalpel, with Suter et al. reporting reduced collateral thermal damage around the lesion margins [19]. Patients treated with lasers experienced less pain compared to those treated with electric scalpels, possibly due to the analgesic effects of diode lasers, which disrupt sodium and potassium pumps in cell membranes, leading to impaired impulse conduction. Alternatively, this pain reduction may be associated with nerve ending ablation.

Reflecting on this case, considering the patient’s medical history and the profuse post-operative bleeding, the resection would still be more efficient and safer with electrosurgery or a diode laser.

Although KCNH1 functions in organisms are not well known, our results reveal that this gene may play a role in osmoregulation [20]. It is established that this type of excessive and persistent bleeding is out of the ordinary, especially with the use of the electric scalpel. Excessive bleeding can have various causes, including abnormalities in the coagulation processes or issues related to platelets [21]. It is important to note that the patient was treated with sodium valproate to prevent epileptic seizures. Sodium valproate can suppress bone marrow function, resulting in reduced production of red blood cells and platelets, as well as impaired platelet aggregation, which prolongs bleeding times. It has a negative impact on coagulation, potentially causing thrombocytopenia, platelet dysfunction, and altered concentrations of various coagulation factors [22, 23]. Although these hematological abnormalities are rare, they may elevate the risk of significant hemorrhage and increase the likelihood of bleeding complications following surgery. Despite a classic hemostasis assessment showing values within the normal range, the bleeding complications in this case suggest that further investigation into potential platelet function impairment is imperative before the operations if the patient is taking medications similar to sodium valproate. Indeed, standard hemostasis tests do not always reveal all coagulation issues.

Challenges related to recognizing pain are especially troubling among children with intellectual disabilities due to their heightened vulnerability to health problems associated with discomfort [24]. Limited possibilities for pain assessment, the high incidence of comorbidities and the use of co-medication have to be considered. This includes conditions such as gastro-esophageal reflux disease, respiratory diseases, dental problems, and epilepsy [25]. Therefore, pain assessment and pain diagnostics are often difficult. Post-operative gingival healing can be quite distressing, uncomfortable, even aching. For patients with cognitive disabilities, the expression of this sore is often unpredictable and can vary. Post-operative pain assessment is the cornerstone of pain management. Several tools can be used, such as the revised Face Leg Activity Cry Consolability FLACC scale [26, 27]. Close monitoring and adequate analgesics should be planned over the following days after surgery.

While sodium valproate is not entirely without oral side effects, studies have shown it generally carries a lower risk of causing gingival hyperplasia compared to first-line agents like phenytoin. For instance, a review of adverse effects from antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) highlights that, although gingival hyperplasia is a concern with many AEDs, sodium valproate is often preferred when minimizing periodontal complications is essential [28]. Ramírez-Rámiz et al. provide a comprehensive review of the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying drug-induced gingival overgrowth, highlighting the complex interplay between genetic predisposition, inflammatory responses, and fibroblast activity [29]. The article outlines the histopathological features, biochemical pathways, and clinical presentation of gingival enlargement, particularly in relation to medications such as anticonvulsants. It emphasizes that this gingival overgrowth involves an imbalance between collagen synthesis and degradation, modulated by cytokines, growth factors, and matrix metalloproteinases [29].

The recurrence of gingival fibromatosis after gingivectomy remains a significant clinical concern, particularly in syndromic and drug-induced cases. In hereditary forms, such as syndromic gingival fibromatosis, recurrence has been reported in approximately 34% of pediatric cases, even after surgical intervention [7]. Similarly, drug-induced gingival overgrowth, especially associated with medications like cyclosporine A or nifedipine, shows comparable recurrence rates within 18 months post-gingivectomy [30].

Several factors influence recurrence, including poor oral hygiene, persistent gingival inflammation, younger age, and lack of regular dental follow-up. In drug-induced cases, continued exposure to the causative medication also significantly increases the risk.

Some studies have mentioned that discontinuation of treatment can be discussed with the prescribing physician, typically for a period of about one year [31]. However, in our patient’s case, due to the low risk of the drug causing hyperplasia and to maintain better control of his epileptic seizures, we chose not to discontinue treatment. If a recurrence occurs, this option can still be considered later. We recommend a careful balance between managing seizure control and minimizing gingival overgrowth and bleeding complications, especially when valproate is considered as part of the therapeutic regimen, under medical supervision to mitigate seizure risk effectively.

It is important to emphasize the role of parental supervision in maintaining optimal oral hygiene, particularly in controlling bacterial biofilm, as a key strategy to prevent recurrence of gingival overgrowth and reduce the risk of requiring further invasive surgical interventions.

Finally, the surgical aspects are an important part of the treatment, but oral disorders rehabilitation with speech therapist and a careful evaluation of the treatment and the communication and behavior condition of the child are just as important. When properly carried out, they can defer the indication for surgery or, at least, perpetuate the postoperative result and minimize post-operative complications.

This case underscores the importance of early interprofessional collaboration, particularly involving pediatric neurology, speech therapy, and oral surgery, to ensure comprehensive management and optimal outcomes.

The patient's disability makes it difficult for him and his parents to follow post-operative instructions strictly. Difficulty in maintaining good oral hygiene is another factor that may explain the bleeding in this young patient.

This case report suggests that ambulatory care may not be the most appropriate option. Hospital admission and close observation may be necessary for bleeding risk and pain monitoring.
