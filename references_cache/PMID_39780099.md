# Triple-drug antibiotic therapy for disseminated nocardial abscess in the mediastinum and brain of an immunocompetent patient: a case report.
**Authors:** Zhang Y, Qi Z, Li H, Gao H
**Journal:** BMC Infect Dis (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12879-025-10445-0](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-025-10445-0)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Nocardia are widely present in nature and considered opportunistic pathogens. They can result in hematogenous spread infection through the ruptured skin or respiratory tract when the host's immune system is compromised. Currently, 119 species of Nocardia have been identified, with 54 capable of causing infections in humans. Nocardial brain abscesses are a rare intracranial lesion that accounts for only 2% of all brain abscesses, but have a mortality rate of 20-55%. This article reports a successfully cured case of mediastinal Nocardia infection with disseminated brain abscess.

CASE PRESENTATION: The patient presented with intermittent chills, shivering, and fever, with the highest temperature of 39˚C, accompanied by shoulder and back pain, dizziness, and headaches. A chest-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed multiple enhanced nodulars in the bilateral hilum and mediastinum. A head-enhanced CT revealed scattered multiple ring-enhanced nodules in both cerebral hemispheres and the left cerebrum, accompanied by extensive surrounding edema. The mediastinal puncture tissue culture confirmed the growth of Nocardia. After twice discussions with multidisciplinary team (MDT) to rule out the possibility of mediastinal and intracranial metastatic tumors, we promptly initiated treatment with a triple-drug antibiotic regimen consisting of imipenem/cilastatin sodium, linezolid dextrose, and Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX). The patient ultimately achieved complete remission.

CONCLUSIONS: Mediastinal nocardiosis with disseminated brain abscesses is a rare condition that can be difficult to differentiate from brain metastases caused by lung cancer. Bacterial culture results, imaging features, and MDT discussions are crucial for accurate diagnosis and treatment. A triple-drug antibiotic regimen has been found to be effective in treatment with acceptable levels of toxicity.

Nocardia are widely present in nature and are considered opportunistic pathogens [1, 2]. It can lead to hematogenous spread infection through the ruptured skin or respiratory tract when the host’s immune system is compromised. Nocardial brain abscesses are rare intracranial lesions, accounting for only 2% of all brain abscesses [3] and having a mortality rate of 20–55% [4, 5]. However, there is still a lack of comprehensive epidemiological data on cases of nocardial brain abscesses, resulting in difficulties in early diagnosis, non-standardized treatment, and poor prognosis. This article reports a successfully cured case of disseminated brain abscesses with mediastinal nocardial infection, aiming to provide insight into the diagnosis and treatment of this disease.

A 53-year-old male farmer was admitted to our department on October 13, 2023, due to “intermittent chills and fever for over a month, with the discovery of a mediastinal mass 10 days earlier.” In early September 2023, after a cold, the patient developed intermittent chills, shivering, and fever, with the highest temperature of 39˚C, accompanied by shoulder and back pain, dizziness, and headaches. The headaches were continuous, without projectile vomiting, dyspnea, or chest tightness. On October 3, 2023, a chest-enhanced computed tomography (CT) at the local hospital revealed multiple enhanced nodulars in the bilateral hilum and mediastinum, with the largest one measuring approximately 5.6 cm × 6.1 cm. There was no relief after undergoing antibiotic treatment at the local hospital. On October 10, 2023, an outpatient head-enhanced CT revealed scattered multiple ring-enhanced nodules measuring approximately 1.0 cm × 0.9 cm in both cerebral hemispheres and the left cerebrum, accompanied by extensive surrounding edema. These findings suggested intracranial infectious lesions, potentially indicating tuberculosis. The patient had no history of diabetes, tumors, or immune diseases. The preliminary diagnosis for this patient was intracranial and mediastinal masses that required further investigation.

After hospitalization with impaired consciousness and delayed reactions, laboratory findings were as follows: Leucocyte count (6.4 × 109/L), Neutrophil percentage (78.2%), Procalcitonin level (0.1 ng/mL), Alanine transaminase level (136.1 IU/L), Aspartate aminotransferase level (143.2 IU/L), Carbohydrate antigen 125 level (253.7U/ml). Additionally, tests for novel coronavirus, tuberculosis DNA in sputum, Cryptococcal capsule antigen, and India ink staining all came back negative. The sputum culture showed the presence of Pseudomonas putida with a bacterial count of +++, along with a yeast-like fungus. The fiberoptic bronchoscopy showed no abnormalities, except for the external compressive stenosis observed in the bilateral main bronchi and right middle bronchus. On October 16, an endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) of mediastinal lymph nodes revealed the presence of some inflammatory exudative components, but no tumor cells were detected. A chest CT on October 19 revealed the mass had enlarged to 7.7 cm (Fig. 1a), while a cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) still showed multiple circular-enhanced nodulars (Fig. 2a-b). Subsequently, the culture of mediastinal puncture tissue confirmed the growth of Nocardia on October 20 (Fig. 3a-b). The patient was then prescribed 1.44 g of oral Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole(TMP-SMX) three times a day on October 23.

Four days later, the patient no longer had a fever, but the dizziness and headache persisted. On October 27, a multidisciplinary team (MDT) concluded that the patient might have a nocardial brain abscess. However, the patient refused to undergo drainage of the mediastinal abscess or an intracranial biopsy. On October 30, the Pandy’s test of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was weakly positive, with total protein and chloride levels of 0.76 g/L and 113.0 mmol/L, respectively. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) indicated an EB virus infection. The MDT team recommended adding intravenous 1 g imipenem/cilastatin sodium every 8 h and intravenous 600 mg linezolid dextrose every 12 h for 14 days. Two weeks later, the patient’s symptoms of dizziness and headache significantly improved, with clear consciousness. The patient was discharged on November 17, 2023, and continued to take oral TMP-SMX.

On March 8, 2024, the patient’s symptoms had completely resolved. A chest-enhanced CT scan showed the mediastinal abscess had decreased to 2.7 cm (Fig. 1b), and a head-enhanced MRI also revealed decreased nodules (Fig. 2c-d). The patient would continue to take oral TMP-SMX for at least one year. On August 28, 2024, we conducted a follow-up with the patient via telephone and noted that he was in good health. A second re-examination is scheduled for one year following the onset.

Classified based on the location and extent of the lesions, there are four main types of nocardiosis: cutaneous, pulmonary, neurologic, and disseminated. The risk of death from non-disseminated disease is low, and most patients with primary cutaneous nocardiosis can fully recover. The incidence of pulmonary infections can be as high as 80%, while the incidence of patients with disseminated infections ranges from 44 to 85% [2]. Nocardia has a specific affinity for nervous tissue. Central nervous system (CNS) involvement typically occurs as a result of hematogenous dissemination, direct spread from a nearby site of infection, or head trauma. In this case, the patient’s infection might be related to an accidental skin injury during farm work. The delayed diagnosis resulted in the bacteria spreading to the mediastinum and CNS while the patient’s immune system was still functioning normally.

There are few reports on mediastinal nocardiosis. In cases of pulmonary infection, the clinical presentation lacks specificity, with fever, cough, and sputum being common symptoms. Common signs of CT imaging include lobar consolidations, isolated or multiple nodules, peribronchiolar consolidations or central lobular nodules, and patchy shadows of varying shapes [6]. A retrospective study reviewed 75 cases of pulmonary nocardiosis and found that the most prevalent CT manifestations were solitary or multiple nodules and/or masses (36.0%) in the lungs, consolidation (28.0%), and nodular bronchiectasis (26.7%) [7]. Only 13 patients (17.3%) exhibited mediastinal lymphadenopathy without the formation of large masses and necrosis. In this case, the patient had a predominantly large necrotic mediastinal mass with a few small pulmonary nodules, leading us to suspect a tumor more likely.

The diagnosis of nocardiosis usually depends on a bacterial culture of the infected tissue. The positive rate of sputum culture is about 39.4%, whereas tissue culture has a positivity rate of about 85–95%. Therefore, it is recommended to culture multiple clinical samples multiple times [8, 9]. Fortunately, the nocardial infection in this case was confirmed by the first tissue culture obtained through an EBUS-TBNA. Despite the pathogenetic evidence, there was still suspicion of a mediastinal tumor combined with nocardial infection, prompting consideration of either recurrent EBUS-TBNA or surgical exploration. Ultimately, a consensus was reached within the MDT, and single-drug antibiotic therapy was initiated.

There are regional differences in the distribution of Nocardia. Nocardia asteroides is predominantly found in Gansu Province in northwestern China [10], Nocardia brasiliensis is predominantly found in Hunan Province in central China [11], and Nocardia neoformans is predominantly found in Hebei Province in northern China [12]. Furthermore, different species of Nocardia may vary in their susceptibility to antibiotics [13]. This emphasizes the importance of species identification for empirically selecting antibiotics. The preferred method for identifying Nocardia species is through sequence analysis of the 16 S ribosomal ribonucleic acid (rRNA) gene. However, due to technical limitations of our hospital at the time, we were unable to perform typing and drug susceptibility testing for Nocardia infections in this patient. It was not until a matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization (MALDI) time-of-flight (TOF) mass spectrometry (Clin-TOF-II, BIOYANG, Beijing, China) [14] was introduced at our hospital in July 2024 that the frozen specimen was finally identified as Nocardia cyriacigeorgica, a pathogenic bacteria [4].

The symptoms of nocardial brain abscess are varied and nonspecific. Patients may present with headaches, nausea, vomiting, meningeal irritation, or focal neurologic deficits. Most patients (93%) with cerebral Nocardia infection present with annular enhanced lesions indicative of brain abscesses [15]. However, in some patients, brain abscesses may be misdiagnosed as primary or metastatic tumors prior to biopsy. In this case, the patient refused to rundergo a biopsy or surgery for disseminated disease. Therefore, the MDT team recommended a CSF examination with bacterial culture and mNGS for its safety and fast diagnostic capabilities for intracranial infections [16].

However, the bacterial culture of the patient’s CSF was negative, and mNGS showed Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection. The absence of Nocardia may be attributed to the low detection rate in the CSF and the fact that the abscess has not ruptured into the ventricle [17]. The EBV infection was considered to be latent or non-pathogenic [18]. Patients with viral encephalitis frequently exhibit multiple or solitary lesions of low or isointense signal in T1WI phase and high signal on the T2WI phases, with inconspicuous enhancement in the early stage, and diffuse, patchy, or gyrus-like enhancement in the later stage, without a mass effect [19]. All of these signs are markedly different from the intracranial imaging in this case. Therefore, the second multidisciplinary discussion concluded that the patient’s multiple intracranial lesions were still considered to be disseminated nocardial abscesses despite the absence of intracranial pathogenetic evidence. They recommended a triple antibiotic regimen in accordance with the guidelines for disseminated Norcardial infections in immunocompromised patients [20–22].

Sulfonamides have remained a cornerstone of first-line treatment for nocardial infections, with a resistance frequency of ≤ 9% [23]. In China, a TMP-SMX resistance rate for Nocardia ranging from 0 to 53.8% has been reported [24]. Additionally, the mortality rate of nocardial brain abscesses is about 20–55%, significantly higher than the less than 10% of other bacterial brain abscesses [5, 25]. Therefore, combination therapy is recommended for most patients with disseminated infections, while sulfonamides should be administrated for ≥ 12 months in immunocompromised or intracranially infected patients [22]. Meanwhile, in clinical practice, combination or even triple-drug therapy is commonly used to treat disseminated infections in immunocompetent patients [26]. In this case, it was decided to combine three-drug therapy after consultation with the MDT, as his CNS symptoms did not show significant improvement after 8 days of TMP-SMX treatment. Then the triple-drug antibiotic therapy resulted in significant relief of CNS symptoms and a sharp decrease in lesions. Accordingly, the regimen may be sufficient to treat disseminated nocardial brain abscesses with less toxicity, helping to avoid invasive surgical debridement or drainage.

In conclusion, mediastinal nocardial infections complicating disseminated brain abscesses are rare and present challenges in differentiating them from brain metastases caused by lung cancer. Accurate identification of the pathogen and differentiation by imaging is key to timely diagnosis, while early initiation of multidrug antibiotic therapy is critical.
