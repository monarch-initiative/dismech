</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Acromesomelic chondrodysplasias (ACDs) are characterized by disproportionate shortening of the appendicular skeleton, predominantly affecting the middle (forearms and forelegs) and distal segments (hands and feet). Here, we present two consanguineous families with missense (c.157T&gt;C, p.(C53R)) or nonsense (c.657G&gt;A, p.(W219*)) mutations in <em>BMPR1B.</em> Homozygous affected individuals show clinical and radiographic findings consistent with ACD-type Grebe. Functional analysis of the missense mutation C53R revealed that the mutated receptor was partially located at the cell membrane. In contrast to the wild-type receptor, C53R mutation hindered the activation of the receptor by its ligand GDF5, as shown by reporter gene assay. Further, overexpression of the C53R mutation in an <em>in vitro</em> chondrogenesis assay showed no effect on cell differentiation, indicating a loss of function. The nonsense mutation (c.657G&gt;A, p.(W219*)) introduces a premature stop codon, which is predicted to be subject to nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, causing reduced protein translation of the mutant allele. A loss-of-function effect of both mutations causing recessive ACD-type Grebe is further supported by the mild brachydactyly or even non-penetrance of these mutations observed in the heterozygous parents. In contrast, dominant-negative <em>BMPR1B</em> mutations described previously are associated with autosomal-dominant brachydactyly-type A2.</p>
<section id="kwd-group1" class="kwd-group"><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> BMPR1B, acromesomelic chondrodysplasia, Grebe syndrome, genital development</p></section></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>Osteochondrodysplasias are a clinically and genetically heterogeneous group of hereditary skeletal disorders.<sup><a href="#bib1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib1">1</a></sup> Among these, acromesomelic chondrodysplasias (ACDs) constitute a rare subgroup characterized by short stature, severely shortened limbs, and hand/foot malformations. The severity of limb abnormalities increases in a proximo-to-distal gradient with hands and feet being most severely affected, showing brachydactyly and/or rudimentary fingers. The group of ACDs are classified into five subgroups according to the Nosology and Classification of Genetic Skeletal Disorders:<sup><a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2">2</a></sup> Acromesomelic dysplasia-type Maroteaux (MIM 602875), Grebe dysplasia (Grebe type, MIM 200700 and Hunter Thompson type, MIM 201250), Fibular hypoplasia and complex brachydactyly (Du Pan, MIM 228900), acromesomelic dysplasia with genital anomalies (MIM 609441) and Acromesomelic dysplasia Osebold–Remondini type (MIM 112910). Grebe dysplasia and Du Pan syndrome share an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern and are caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations in growth and differentiation factor 5 (<em>GDF5</em>; MIM *601146).</p>
<p>GDF5 is a member of the TGF-<em>β</em> superfamily belonging to the subgroup of bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs). GDF5 binds extracellular to transmembrane serine threonine kinases such as BMPR1A and BMPR1B, with a preference for BMPR1B.<sup><a href="#bib3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib3">3</a></sup> All reported mutations in <em>GDF5</em> associated with ACD are caused by a homozygous functional loss of GDF5, which is caused by either deletions, duplications, or insertions leading to a premature translational stop or caused by point mutations targeting the cleavage site or the mature domain of GDF5, thereby compromising its regular function.<sup><a href="#bib4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib4">4</a>, <a href="#bib5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib5">5</a>, <a href="#bib6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib6">6</a>, <a href="#bib7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib7">7</a>, <a href="#bib8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib8">8</a>, <a href="#bib9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib9">9</a>, <a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10">10</a>, <a href="#bib11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib11">11</a>, <a href="#bib12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib12">12</a>, <a href="#bib13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib13">13</a></sup> Interestingly, one individual with a homozygous mutation in <em>BMPR1B</em> has been reported presenting with a subtype of ACD with additional genital anomalies,<sup><a href="#bib14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib14">14</a></sup> implicating that mutations in the ligand and its receptor can cause similar phenotypes. Heterozygous carriers of dominant-negative mutations in <em>BMPR1B</em> are usually affected by brachydactyly-type A2 (BDA2; MIM number 112600), which is characterized by a shortened middle phalanx of the index finger and variable clinodactyly of the fifth finger.<sup><a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15">15</a></sup> In contrast, carriers of heterozygous mutations in the ligand, <em>GDF5,</em> are usually affected by brachydactyly-type C (MIM number 113100),<sup><a href="#bib16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib16">16</a>, <a href="#bib17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib17">17</a></sup> a condition characterized by brachymesophalangy of index, middle, and little fingers as well as shortening of the first metacarpal. Ring fingers are only mildly affected and hyperphalangy of the index and middle fingers might occur.<sup><a href="#bib9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib9">9</a>, <a href="#bib18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib18">18</a>, <a href="#bib19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib19">19</a>, <a href="#bib20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib20">20</a></sup> However, clinical phenotypes resulting from heterozygous <em>BMPR1B</em> and <em>GDF5</em> mutations are variable and also show overlapping features.<sup><a href="#bib21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib21">21</a>, <a href="#bib22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib22">22</a></sup></p>
<p>Here we present two consanguineous families segregating as yet unreported mutations in <em>BMPR1B</em> (c.157T&gt;C, p.(C53R) and (c.657G&gt;A, p.(W219*)) with a total of three affected individuals carrying the respective mutations in a homozygous state. Both mutations are proposed to cause a loss of function of BMPR1B.</p></section><section id="sec2"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">MATERIALS AND METHODS</h2>
<section id="sec3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Clinical investigation and molecular analyses</h3>
<p>All clinical investigations were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles. Informed consent was obtained for genetic analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples by standard methods. With regard to the ACD phenotype, we first performed mutation screening of <em>GDF5</em> (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/NM_000557.2" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NM_000557.2</a>) and subsequently of <em>BMPR1B</em> (<a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/NM_001256794.1" class="usa-link" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NM_001256794.1</a>). The coding regions of <em>GDF5</em> and <em>BMPR1B</em> as well as the flanking intronic sequences were amplified by standard PCR protocols. The primer sequences and PCR conditions for the molecular testing can be found elsewhere (<em>GDF5</em>;<sup><a href="#bib23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib23">23</a></sup>
<em>BMPR1B</em><sup><a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15">15</a></sup>). Sequencing was done using the ABI Prism BigDye Terminator Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems, Life Technologies, Foster City, CA, USA) with PCR primers used as sequencing primers. Products were evaluated on an automated capillary sequencer (Applied Biosystems 3730).</p>
<p>Obtained sequence variant information was submitted to the Leiden Open Variation Database (<a href="https://www.grenada.lumc.nl/LOVD2/mendelian_genes/home.php?select_db=BMPR1B" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">https://www.grenada.lumc.nl/LOVD2/mendelian_genes/home.php?select_db=BMPR1B</a>).</p></section><section id="sec4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sequence alignment and structural data</h3>
<p>Amino-acid sequences of the ligand-binding domain (LBD) of the seven human TGF-<em>β</em>-type I receptors as well as chicken and mouse orthologs of BMPR1B were aligned using Clustal Omega<sup><a href="#bib24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib24">24</a>, <a href="#bib25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib25">25</a></sup> (<a href="http://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/services/web/toolform.ebi?tool=clustalo" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/services/web/toolform.ebi?tool=clustalo</a>) and colored using CHROMA<sup><a href="#bib26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib26">26</a></sup> (<a href="http://www.llew.org.uk/chroma/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">http://www.llew.org.uk/chroma/</a>).</p>
<p>Modeling of the BMPR1B-mutant C53R was performed using the BMPR1B coordinates of the crystal structure of GDF5 bound to wild-type BMPR1B (PDB entry 3EVS).<sup><a href="#bib27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib27">27</a></sup> First, the disulfide bond involving cysteine residues 32 and 53 of BMPR1B was removed and C53 was replaced by an arginine residue using the ProteinDesign tool of the software package Quanta2008 (Accelrys, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The resulting model of C53R was then refined by energy minimization using the all-hydrogen force field of CHARMM27 and employing only geometrical energy terms. As the charged side chain of the introduced arginine at position 53 is almost completely buried inside the hydrophobic core of the BMPR1B, further energy minimization and short (50 ps) <em>in vacuo</em> molecular dynamics simulations were performed using the CHARMM27 force field described above but employing also electrostatic energy terms using a distance-dependent treatment of the coulomb term. Images of the three-dimensional structure of a BMPR1B–GDF5 complex were based on the PDB-file 3EVS<sup><a href="#bib27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib27">27</a></sup> rendered by PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Schrödinger, LLC (Portland, OR, USA).</p></section><section id="sec5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Cloning of expression constructs</h3>
<p>The coding sequences of murine <em>Bmpr1b</em> and human <em>GDF5</em> were cloned into the shuttle vector pSLAX13.<sup><a href="#bib28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib28">28</a></sup> Murine <em>Bmpr1b</em> in pSLAX13 was used as a template for <em>in vitro</em> mutagenesis to introduce the homologous mutation C53R, to delete the LBD (dLBD), or to insert a hemagglutinin (HA) tag. Primers for mutagenesis are available in <a href="#sup1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Table 1</a>. Inserts were subcloned into the expression vector pCS2+ via the ClaI restriction site.</p></section><section id="sec6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">NIH/3T3 cultivation</h3>
<p>NIH/3T3 cells (American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), Manassas, VA, USA) were cultivated in DMEM 4.5 g/l glucose (Lonza, Verviers, Belgium) supplemented with 10% FBS superior (Biochrom AG, Berlin, Germany), 2 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">M L</span>-glutamine (Lonza) and penicillin/streptomycin (Lonza) in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO<sub>2</sub> at 37 °C.</p></section><section id="sec7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Immunocytochemistry</h3>
<p>NIH/3T3 cells were seeded in black ‘CellCarrier' 96-well plates with an optically clear bottom (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA) and transfected with HA-tagged <em>Bmpr1b</em>-constructs in pCS2+ using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Twenty-four hours after transfection, cells were washed twice and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in serum-free DMEM 4.5 g/l glucose (Lonza). After washing with PBS, cells were fixed for 15 min at room temperature (RT) in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS and washed three more times with PBS. For permeabilization, cells were incubated for 15 min in PBS containing 0.2% Triton-X-100. To block nonspecific binding, cells were incubated in 10% FBS superior (Biochrom AG) in PBS overnight at 4 °C. This was followed by incubation with rabbit anti-HA antibody (H6908, Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) diluted 1:100 in PBS containing 10% FBS superior (Biochrom AG) at RT for 1 h. Detection was achieved after extensive washing by incubation with anti-rabbit-Alexa Fluor 488 antibody (A11008, Molecular Probes Life Technologies, Eugene, OR, USA) diluted 1:2000 in PBS containing 10% FCS (Biochrom AG) and DAPI (Invitrogen, Life Technologies). Fluorescence images were recorded with the ‘Operetta' High Content Imaging System (Perkin Elmer) using × 60 magnification. Wide-field images were deconvolved by iterative restoration (25 steps) using Volocity 3D Image Analysis Software (Perkin Elmer).</p></section><section id="sec8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Luciferase reporter gene assay</h3>
<p>NIH/3T3 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate and co-transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Life Technologies) at about 70% confluency with pCS2+ expression constructs of <em>Bmpr1b</em><sup>wt</sup>, <em>Bmpr1b</em><sup>C53R</sup>, <em>Bmpr1b</em><sup>dLBD</sup>, <em>GDF5</em>, BRE luciferase reporter construct<sup><a href="#bib29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib29">29</a></sup> and the Renilla luciferase vector pRL-TK (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), which served as an internal control for transfection efficiency. Cells were cultivated in DMEM 4.5 g/l glucose (Lonza) supplemented with 1% FBS superior (Biochrom AG), 2 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">M L</span>-glutamine (Lonza), penicillin/streptomycin (Lonza) for 42 h after transfection and subsequently collected and lyzed in potassium phosphate buffer (9 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">M</span> potassium dihydrogen phosphate, 91 m<span class="font-variant-small-caps">M</span> dipotassium phosphate, 0.2% Triton-X-100). Dual luciferase activity was determined as described previously<sup><a href="#bib30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib30">30</a></sup> using Mithras LB 940 (Berthold Technologies GmbH &amp; Co. KG, Bad Wildbad, Germany).</p></section><section id="sec9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Micromass cultures</h3>
<p>The coding sequence of chicken <em>BMPR1B</em> in pSLAX13.<sup><a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15">15</a></sup> was used as a template for <em>in vitro</em> mutagenesis to introduce the homologous mutation C53R and dLBD. Primers for mutagenesis are available in <a href="#sup1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Table 1</a>. Inserts were subcloned into the avian-specific retroviral construct RCASBP(A) via ClaI.<sup><a href="#bib31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib31">31</a></sup></p>
<p>Production and concentration of viral supernatant was performed as described.<sup><a href="#bib32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib32">32</a></sup> Fertilized chicken eggs (VALO BioMedia GmbH, Osterholz-Scharmbeck, Germany) were incubated at 37.5 °C in a humidified egg incubator for ∼4.5 days. Micromass cultures were isolated from limb buds of Hamburger Hamilton stage 23–24<sup><a href="#bib33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib33">33</a></sup> as described, with minor modifications.<sup><a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15">15</a></sup> Micromass cultures were plated at a density of 2 × 10<sup>5</sup> cells per 12 <em>μ</em>l drop in the middle of a 24-well tissue-culture plate. Infection was performed with a titer of 1 × 10<sup>7</sup> plaque-forming units of the concentrated RCAS viral supernatants, as described.<sup><a href="#bib28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib28">28</a></sup> Empty RCAS virus was used as a control. Cells were allowed to attach for 2 h in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO<sub>2</sub> at 39 °C and then complemented with DMEM-F12 (Biochrom AG) culture medium supplemented with 10% FBS superior (Biochrom AG), 0.2% chicken serum (Sigma-Aldrich), <span class="font-variant-small-caps">L</span>-glutamine (Lonza), penicillin/streptomycin (Lonza). The culture medium was replaced every 2 days. Micromass cultures were washed twice with PBS, fixed with Kahles' Fixative (28.9% ethanol, 0.37% formaldehyde, 3.9% acetic acid), and stained with 0.05% Alcian Blue (Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.1 <span class="font-variant-small-caps">M</span> HCl at day 7. Quantification of the staining was achieved by extraction with 6 <span class="font-variant-small-caps">M</span> guanidine hydrochloride (Carl Roth GmbH+Co. KG, Karlsruhe, Germany) overnight at RT. Dye concentration was determined spectrophotometrically at 595 nm.</p></section></section><section id="sec10"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">RESULTS</h2>
<section id="sec11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Family I</h3>

The family history is otherwise unremarkable (pedigree scheme in <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1Ab</a>).</p>

Tibiae were shortened and severely bowed, and fibulae were absent resulting in bilateral dislocation of the tibiotalar joints (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1Aj</a>). Bilateral shortened ulnae, mildly bowed radii, and small ossification centers of the radial epiphyses were observed. One ossified carpal bone was detected (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1Am and n</a>). The patient had absent 1st metacarpals, slender 2nd metacarpals, clumsy and shortened 3rd–5th metacarpals as well as absence of the proximal thumb phalanx and the proximal and middle phalanges of the 5th fingers. Fingers 2–4 appeared to have only two phalangeal bones with very small proximal phalanges (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1Ai</a>). In the feet, talus, calcaneus, and possible ossification centers of the ossa navicularia were visible, all metatarsals were present, and all distal phalanges of the feet were hypoplastic. On the left side, very small ossification centers of the phalanges of the 3rd and 4th toe were visible (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1Ak and l</a>).</p>
<p>Ultrasound examination of the abdomen showed a normal uterus; the ovaries could not be evaluated because of bowel gas superposition. Ultrasound examination of the heart was normal. Hearing and ophthalmologic examination were also normal.</p>
<p>The mother showed mild BDA2 with shortened middle phalanges of the 2nd and 5th fingers and toes (<a href="#sup1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Figure 1Aa–c</a>). The father did not show any abnormalities (<a href="#sup1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Figure 1Ad–f</a>).</p></section><section id="sec12"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Family II</h3>
<p>The second family originates from Pakistan; the parents are healthy first cousins and the two affected brothers are aged 15 (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1Ba</a>) and 19 years (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1Bg</a>), respectively. Both parents presented with short stature (mother: 150 cm; father: 145 cm). The family history is otherwise unremarkable (pedigree <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1Bb</a>).</p>
<p>The patients also presented with disproportionate short stature because of severe acromesomelic limb shortening (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1Ba and g</a>); the younger brother was 119 cm tall (−6SD) and the older brother was 134 cm tall (−5.6SD). The hands of both brothers showed marked shortening of digits and mild radial deviation of fingers with all fingernails being present (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1Bc and j</a>). The fibulae were not palpable. Valgus instability of the ankle joints was evident. The feet were small and broad with all toes being short and functionless with constrictions at their bases and all toenails being present (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1Bd and k</a>).</p>
<p>On X-ray examination, tibiae were shortened but not bowed (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1Bg and k</a>). The fibulae were absent resulting in bilateral dislocation of the tibiotalar joints (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1Bf and h</a>). Bilateral shortened ulnae but no bowing of the radii was observed. Carpal bones were deformed and there were carpal fusions in both affected brothers and additional fusions of the 2nd and 3rd metacarpals in the older brother. Metacarpals were shortened with the absence of 1st metacarpals, and short and deformed proximal and middle phalanges of the fingers were observed with the absence of the proximal phalanx of the index fingers (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1Be</a> and i). In contrast to the affected girl from family I, in both brothers a small proximal phalanx of the thumbs as well as a small middle phalanx of the 5th fingers were present. In the feet, talus, calcaneus, and possible remnants of some ossa navicularia were visible and all metatarsals were present. All distal phalanges of the feet were hypoplastic; some mislocalized middle or proximal phalanges seemed to be present (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1Bf and h</a>).</p>
<p>The parents presented with short stature and the father with mild generalized brachydactyly (<a href="#sup1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Figure 1</a>; mother: Ba–c; father: Bd–f).</p>

</div></section></section><section id="sec13"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Molecular analysis results</h3>
<p>In both families, mutations in <em>GDF5</em> were excluded and subsequent sequencing of the <em>BMPR1B</em> gene revealed a homozygous single-nucleotide exchange in family I: c.157T&gt;C, p.(C53R), which has not been reported in the literature to date. The mutation was detected in a heterozygous state in both parents. In family II, a homozygous nonsense mutation in <em>BMPR1B</em> was detected in both affected brothers: c.657G&gt;A, p.(W219*), which also has not been reported in the literature to date. This mutation was again detected in a heterozygous state in both parents.</p></section><section id="sec14"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">
<em>BMPR1B</em> mutations (p.(C53R); p.(W219*)) are predicted to cause a loss of function by different mechanisms</h3>
<p>The human <em>BMPPR1B</em> gene is located on chromosome 4 and consists of 10 coding exons. The protein consists of 502 amino acids and contains four functional domains: a LBD, a single-pass transmembrane domain, a glycine–serine-rich-box and a kinase domain (KD; <a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure 2a</a>). The nonsense mutation c.657G&gt;A, p.(W219*) introduces a premature stop codon within the KD located in coding exon 6 of 10 of <em>BMPR1B</em> and is therefore predicted to activate the nonsense-mediated mRNA decay machinery.<sup><a href="#bib34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib34">34</a></sup></p>

<p>BMPR1B is a transmembrane receptor mainly located at the cell surface. In order to study whether an altered structure of the LBD caused by the C53R mutation influences its localization, HA-tagged mouse <em>Bmpr1b</em> constructs were expressed in NIH/3T3 cells and stained extracellularly with a HA-specific antibody. Immunofluorescence staining showed that HA-BMPR1B<sup>wt</sup> and HA-BMPR1B<sup>C53R</sup> as well as an artificially designed construct carrying a deletion of the LBD, HA-BMPR1B<sup>dLBD</sup>, are all located at the cell membrane (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure 3a</a>). Notably, fluorescence intensity of the extracellular staining of cells expressing HA-BMPR1B<sup>C53R</sup> was reduced as compared with cells expressing HA-BMPR1B<sup>wt</sup> or HA-BMPR1B<sup>dLBD</sup>, which is not the case when HA-BMPR1B is stained on permeabilized cells, detecting also intracellular proteins (<a href="#sup1" class="usa-link">Supplementary Figure 2</a>). Although HA-BMPR1B<sup>C53R</sup> is processed and can translocate to the cell surface in our overexpression studies, the efficiency of these processes might be reduced.</p>

</h3>
<p>BMPR1B-mediated signaling can be activated by ligands, such as various BMPs and GDF5, which has a very high affinity for BMPR1B.<sup><a href="#bib3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib3">3</a>, <a href="#bib27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib27">27</a></sup></p>
<p>We tested whether BMPR1B<sup>C53R</sup> can mediate GDF5-dependent signaling with a BMP responsive luciferase (BRE) reporter assay. NIH/3T3 cells were transiently co-transfected with a BRE luciferase reporter and expression constructs of either BMPR1B<sup>wt</sup>, BMPR1B<sup>C53R</sup>, or BMPR1B<sup>dLBD</sup>. In the absence of exogenously expressed ligand, a slight activation of the luciferase signal was observed for BMPR1B<sup>wt</sup> (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure 3b</a>). When BMPR1B<sup>wt</sup> was co-expressed with GDF5, there was a prominent increase in the luciferase signal, which was not observed in cells co-expressing BMPR1B<sup>C53R</sup> and GDF5 (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure 3b</a>). Similar to BMPR1B<sup>dLBD</sup>, which lacks the binding site for GDF5, the signal activity of BMPR1B<sup>C53R</sup> did not significantly differ from cells, which were transfected with an empty vector control (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure 3b</a>). Thus, BMPR1B<sup>C53R</sup> was not able to mediate BMP signaling induced by GDF5.</p></section><section id="sec17"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">BMPR1B mutation C53R has no dominant-negative effect on <em>in vitro</em> chondrogenesis</h3>
<p>We further studied the effect of the mutation C53R on BMPR1B by analyzing its effect on chondrogenic differentiation <em>in vitro</em> using the chicken micromass culture system.<sup><a href="#bib35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib35">35</a></sup> Previously reported point mutations in BMPR1B associated with BDA2 showed a dominant-negative effect on chondrogenesis, when overexpressed in this system.<sup><a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15">15</a>, <a href="#bib21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib21">21</a></sup> RCASBP(A) virus expressing the chicken orthologs of BMPR1B<sup>wt</sup>, BMPR1B<sup>C53R</sup>, BMPR1B<sup>dLBD</sup>, and the BDA2-associated dominant-negative mutant BMPR1B<sup>I200K</sup>.<sup><a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15">15</a></sup> or empty virus as a control were used to infect micromass cultures. After 7 days, extracellular matrix production was evaluated by Alcian blue staining of proteoglycans in the formed cartilaginous matrix (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure 4</a>). When comparing the degree of chondrogenic differentiation of micromass cultures expressing BMPR1B<sup>wt</sup>, BMPR1B<sup>C53R</sup>, or BMPR1B<sup>dLBD</sup> with control cultures, no significant differences were observed. As expected, micromass cultures expressing the BDA2-associated mutant BMPR1B<sup>I200K</sup> hardly displayed any Alcian blue staining, which indicates a strong dominant-negative effect on chondrogenic differentiation (<a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure 4</a>). In contrast to the BDA2-associated mutation BMPR1B<sup>I200K</sup>, the ACD-associated mutation BMPR1B<sup>C53R</sup> seemed rather to constitute a loss-of-function mutant and not eliciting a dominant-negative effect.</p>

<p>All three patients with homozygous <em>BMPR1B</em> mutations reported here show a severe form of limb malformation consisting of bilateral aplasia of the fibula, severe brachydactyly, and fusion of carpal/tarsal bones as similarly seen in other ACDs. The female patient with the c.157T&gt;C, p.(C53R) missense mutation additionally presented with bowing of tibiae and radii, which was not present in the two affected brothers with the c.657G&gt;A, p.(W219*) nonsense mutation. For both mutations, we propose a loss-of-function mechanism. The missense mutation C53R disrupts one of five highly conserved disulfide bridges within the extracellular domain of BMPR1B. The disulfide bonds are important for folding and stability of the protein.<sup><a href="#bib27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib27">27</a></sup> Short molecular dynamics simulation of BMPR1B carrying the C53R mutation employing a force field handling with electrostatic energy terms indicates a severe misfolding of the extracellular domain because of the unbalanced charge of the arginine residue buried into the hydrophobic core of the LBD of BMPR1B, which implicates a loss-of-function pathomechanism. This hypothesis was supported by our overexpression studies where membrane localization of the mutated receptor BMPR1B<sup>C53R</sup> was still observed, albeit without activation by the high-affinity ligand GDF5 in reporter gene analysis. It is noteworthy that the point mutation C53R had the same effect as a mutant Bmpr1b construct missing the entire LBD. It is possible that at a lower endogenous expression level, the mutated protein is degraded rapidly, as a free cysteine and a missing central disulfide bond might trigger proteasomal degradation during endoplasmic reticulum control (for review see Ellgaard <em>et al</em><sup><a href="#bib36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib36">36</a></sup>). Moreover, our data indicate that even receptors with membrane localization can be functionally inactive. This is also supported by our overexpression experiment using the micromass culture system, where BMPR1B<sup>C53R</sup> had no negative effect on chondrogenic differentiation, unlike the BDA2-associated dominant-negative BMPR1B<sup>I200K</sup> mutation.<sup><a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15">15</a></sup> BMPR1B<sup>I200K</sup> might act as a ligand sink by binding BMPs without initiation of signal transduction, whereas BMPR1B<sup>C53R</sup> does not bind ligands. Therefore, we propose a loss of function rather than a dominant-negative pathomechanism for the ACD-associated C53R mutation.</p>
<p>The nonsense mutation c.657G&gt;A, p.(W219*) most likely fails to yield functional protein, as the transcripts encoded are likely subject to the nonsense-mediated RNA decay. So far, homozygous mutations in <em>BMPR1B</em> have only once been shown to cause a subtype of ACD with additional genital anomalies caused by a homozygous 8 bp deletion (c.361_368delGGACCTAT), which is located in the extracellular domain of the BMPR1B protein.<sup><a href="#bib14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib14">14</a></sup> This mutation is also proposed to result in a loss of function because of a frameshift and premature stop codon followed by nonsense-mediated RNA decay.<sup><a href="#bib14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib14">14</a></sup> This patient showed uterus hypoplasia and displayed ovarian dysfunction resulting in hypergonadotropic hypogonadism. In contrast, the uterus of our only female patient carrying the homozygous missense mutation c.157T&gt;C, p.(C53R) appeared normal, but ovaries could not be evaluated by ultrasound examination. Therefore, her phenotype does not seem to differ from the other GDF5-associated ACD phenotypes.</p>
<p>Interestingly, heterozygous mutation carriers presented in this paper showed no classical BDA2 phenotype. Only the mother of our female patient with the missense mutation showed a mild form BDA2, whereas the father in family II presented with mild generalized brachydactyly. Moreover, the heterozygous <em>BMPR1B</em> mutation carriers in the family described by Demirhan <em>et al</em><sup><a href="#bib14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib14">14</a></sup> did not show any signs of brachydactyly. Thus, we propose a loss-of-function pathomechanism for homozygous <em>BMPR1B</em> mutations causing ACD, whereas BDA2 is caused by heterozygous dominant-negative mutations in <em>BMPR1B</em>.<sup><a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15">15</a>, <a href="#bib21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib21">21</a></sup></p>
<p>These findings widen the phenotypic spectrum of ACD caused by homozygous <em>BMPR1B</em> mutations and may help in ACD diagnosis.</p></section><section id="ack1" class="ack"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Acknowledgments</h2>
