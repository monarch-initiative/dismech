# Click Chemistry-Based Quantification of Extracellular Matrix Turnover for Drug Screening and Regenerative Medicine.
**Authors:** Porter A, Fan S, Peng Y, Lv M, Zhou Y, Alanazi A, Han L, Wang L, Lu XL
**Journal:** Ann Biomed Eng (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10439-026-04152-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10439-026-04152-3)

## Content

PURPOSE: To develop and validate a bioorthogonal labeling approach for quantifying ECM remodeling in living cell and tissue culture systems.

METHODS: Strain-promoted azide-alkyne (SPAAC) reactions, or copper-free click chemistry, were used to fluorescently label newly synthesized glycan and protein matrix components. ECM synthesis and degradation was quantified in cartilage explants, human mesenchymal stem cells, and SKBR3 breast cancer cells under various external stimuli, including inflammation, mechanical stimulus, and drug treatment.

RESULTS: The click chemistry method reliable quantified ECM turnover across platforms. It detected reduced glycan and protein synthesis after 24-hour inflammatory challenge and enabled longitudinal tracking of ECM degradation in cartilage explants. The technique demonstrated high sensitivity, measuring increased ECM deposition by ~ 10,000 human mesenchymal stem cells in 12-hour intervals and substrate stiffness-dependent synthesis by SKBR3 cells. Additionally, the approach supported osteoarthritis drug screening by identifying compounds that mitigated inflammation-induced ECM degradation.

CONCLUSION: Compared to traditional biochemical or histological assays, the click chemistry-based technique provides higher sensitivity, reduced sample requirements, and improved temporal resolution for quantifying ECM turnover. Its versatility enables broad application in tissue engineering, regenerative medicine, disease modeling, and high-throughput drug evaluation.

Introduction
The extracellular matrices (ECM) of connective tissues 
such as cartilage, bone, and ligament are highly organized 
networks of proteins, proteoglycans, and other biomacro-
molecules. The ECM’s chemical composition, mechanical 
properties, and reservoir of signaling molecules are key in 
controlling the cell niche and cell behaviors [1 ]. Healthy 
ECM is a dynamic structure that undergoes continuous 
modeling and remodeling through the metabolic activities 
of cells [2 ]. Dysregulation of ECM turnover is associated 
with many diseases, such as fibrotic diseases, tumor growth, 
osteoporosis, and osteoarthritis [3 ]. Biomedical studies of 
connective tissues and ECM-driven diseases often require 
precise measurements of ECM dynamics, such as the syn-
thesis and degradation rates of ECM molecules like collagen 
and glycosaminoglycan (GAG) [3]. In such in vitro studies, 
cells are preferably cultured in their native ECM to maintain 
a cell phenotype close to the in vivo state.
Biochemical assays and antibody- and dye-based stains 
are commonly used to characterize the macromolecular 
composition of the ECM. Staining techniques visualize 
macromolecule spatial distributions, whereas biochemical 
assays quantify their content. The sensitivity of biochemical 
assays is a critical component of research design. In tissue 
engineering studies, for example, cartilage cells seeded in 
hydrogels often need to be cultured for weeks to generate

assay techniques offer only “snapshot” measurements, rep-

and its limited biocompatibility for cells makes it unsuitable 
for long-term tracking of ECM synthesis [5].
Click chemistry reactions have recently been explored for 
labeling proteins and ECM macromolecules. Click chemis-
try, coined by K.B. Sharpless in 2001, represents “a set of 
powerful and highly reliable reactions for the rapid synthesis 
of new compounds and combinatorial libraries through het-
eroatom links” [6 ]. Click chemistry reactions are modular, 
high-yielding, and rapid. They produce minimal byproducts 
and need only simple reaction conditions [6 ]. The classic 
click reaction, copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddi -
tion (CuAAC), has been widely used in pharmaceutical 
and material science research [7 , 8]. However, the copper 
catalyst is toxic to mammalian cells at the required reac-
tion concentration, precluding applications in living cells. 
To address this limitation, a new class of click reactions 
was developed, termed “bioorthogonal” by C.R. Bertozzi, 
that can occur in a living system without interfering with the 
native biochemical processes [9 ]. A widely used reaction 
is strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC), 
commonly referred to as copper-free click chemistry, which 
eliminates the need for a copper catalyst by employing ring 
strain release of cyclooctyne derivative to drive the reac-
tion [10]. Ligation products based on bioorthogonal click 
chemistry reactions have been developed for proteomic and 
glycobiology research [11, 12]. These tools hold significant 
promise for cell bioengineering or regenerative medicine 
research. However, they have not yet been widely adopted to 
evaluate ECM dynamics in biomedical studies. For example, 
the DMMB assay remains the most common method for 
quantifying GAG content in cartilage and osteoarthritis stud-
ies, and the hydroxyproline assay is still the first-line option 
for collagen measurement. There remains a strong demand 
for quantitative techniques that assess ECM turnover with 
high sensitivity and specificity, particularly for investigat -
ing how cell metabolism responds to environmental stimuli 
such as mechanical loading, growth factors, inflammation, 
and drug treatment.
In this study, we present a copper-free click chemistry 
(SPAAC)-based method to quantify ECM synthesis in mon-
olayer and native tissue culture by fluorescently labeling nas-
cent matrix macromolecules synthesized by cells. Benefiting 
from the bioorthogonality of the SPAAC reaction, we further 
demonstrate a complementary method to quantify ECM deg-
radation by longitudinally tracking ECM loss from tissue 
during long-term culture. Using these new techniques, we 
quantified cell metabolic activities and ECM dynamics in 
response to mechanical stimuli, inflammatory challenge, and 
drug treatment. The click chemistry-based methods proved 
accurate, convenient, and cost-efficient for evaluating ECM 
turnover in cell and tissue culture, with potential applica-
tions in biology, drug screening, personalized medicine, and 
regenerative medicine research.
Materials and Methods
Cartilage Tissue Harvest
Fresh calf knee joints (1-2 months old) of mixed left or right 
side and sex were obtained from a local abattoir (Green Vil-
lage, NJ). Cylindrical cartilage samples (diameter = 3 mm, 
thickness = 2 mm, tissue wet weight ≈ 15 mg) were har -
vested from the central, load-bearing region of the femoral 
condyles using a biopsy punch and custom-designed cutting 
tool [13]. Cartilage samples were harvested from at least 
three joints for each experiment. Samples were cultured in 
chondrogenic medium (DMEM high glucose supplemented 
with 1% insulin-transferrin-selenium-ethanolamine [ITS-X], 
0.9 mM sodium pyruvate, 50 μg/mL L-proline, and 50 μg/
mL ascorbate 2-phosphate) at 37 °C and 5%  CO2 for 48 
hours before further experiments [13, 14].

Quantification of ECM Synthesis Rates of hMSCs 
Cultured in Monolayer
To quantify the click chemistry methods sensitivity, nascent 
ECM deposition by hMSCs was tracked in 12-hour incre-
ments. hMSCs are widely used in regenerative medicine 
for treating musculoskeletal diseases due to their pluripo-
tency and capacity to undergo chondrogenic differentiation 
[36]. hMSCs were seeded at 10,000 cells/well in 96 well 
plates (n = 8, n = no. of replicates per group) [37]. Follow -
ing overnight attachment, GalNAz or AHA was added to 
the culture medium for 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, or 72 hours. After 
30 min fluorescent dye labeling, cells were enzymatically 
digested, and the fluorescence of the digestion solution was 
read on a fluorescent plate reader to quantify the amount of 
matrix deposition in each well (Fig.  5a). Fluorescent inten-
sity was normalized to the first 12-hour timepoint for group 
comparison.
Quantification of ECM Synthesis by SK‑BR‑3 Cells 
on Substrates of Different Stiffness
To demonstrate the click chemistry method’s sensitivity 
to mechanical cell stimuli, we investigated how substrate 
stiffness affected breast cancer cell ECM deposition, given 
the ECM’s critical role in tumor remodeling, cancer pro-
gression, and metastasis [ 38, 39]. ECM stiffness can alter 
integrin-ECM adhesion and subsequent micromechanical 
cell signals (Fig. 5e) [40, 41], driving downstream changes 
to metabolic pathways [42, 43]. SK-BR-3 breast cells were 
seeded at 10,000 cells/well in 48-well plates (n = 8) contain-
ing thin polyacrylamide films of three different stiffnesses: 
6, 12, or 22 kPa [44]. Substrate stiffnesses were selected 
based on physiological breast tissue stiffness and common 
substrates used in literature [45–48]. After overnight attach-
ment, cells were metabolically labeled with GalNAz or AHA 
for 36 hours, followed by 30 minute dye labeling and enzy-
matic digestion. A 36 hour metabolic labeling period was 
selected based on the minimum amount time required for a 
measurable increase in matrix deposition as demonstrated in 
the hMSC experiment (see Results). For comparison, fluo-
rescent intensity was normalized to the 6 kPa group.
Statistical Analysis
Statistical analyses were conducted in JMP Pro 17 
(SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). All data are presented as 
means ± standard deviation. Before analysis, normality and 
homoscedasticity assumptions were assessed using Shapiro-
Wilks and Q-Q plots, respectively. A p  value of less than 
0.05 was considered statistically significant. To assess the 
correlation between substrate stiffness and ECM synthesis in 
SK-BR-3 cells, a simple linear regression was conducted. All 
other synthesis experiments were assessed using one-way 
ANOVA with Tukey post hoc tests to compare all means and 
adjust for family-wise type I errors. The cumulative loss of 
glycans and proteins was compared using one-way ANOVA 
with Tukey post hoc tests either at each of the tested time 
points (IL-1β dosage study) or only at the final time point to 
compare total glycan and protein loss (drug screening study). 
To evaluate the correlation between the click chemistry and 
traditional chemical assays, simple linear regression on the 
individual, non-cumulative readings from all tested time 
points was performed (glycan n = 45 aggregate data points, 
protein n = 54).

Quantification of ECM Synthesis in Inflammatory 
Challenged Cartilage
To assess the feasibility of using click chemistry to quan-
tify ECM synthesis in tissue, we examined in situ chon-
drocyte matrix synthesis within cartilage under inflamma-
tory stimulation by IL-1β. Exposure to 1 ng/ml IL-1β for 
24 hours (0 Pre + 1 group) reduced the amount of nascent 
glycans (0.76 ± 0.13 vs 1.00 ± 0.14, p = 0.02) and proteins 
(0.50 ± 0.18 vs 1.00 ± 0.20, p < 0.001) synthesized by the 
cartilage samples. A 7-day pre-treatment with IL-1β fur -
ther reduced the 24-hour synthesis (7 Pre + 1 group) of both 
glycans (0.43 ± 0.13, p = 0.002) and proteins (0.18 ± 0.05, 
p = 0.01) compared to the 0 Pre + 1 group. (Fig. 2d).
Quantification of ECM Loss from Inflammatory 
Challenged Cartilage
Copper-free click chemistry reactions are bioorthogonal, 
allowing for longitudinal tracking of cartilage degradation 
without disrupting tissue culture. During long-term culture, 
fluorescent-labeled ECM molecules degrade and diffuse into 
the culture medium. By measuring the fluorescent intensity 
in the medium, we can quantify the degradation of click-
labeled ECM molecules from the same sample over time. 
The loss of labeled glycans became evident after just 4 days 
of exposure to IL-1β and continued to increase during the 
culture period. By day 8, 0.25 ng/ml IL-1β resulted in a 
37 ± 18% total loss of nascent glycans, compared to 9 ± 2% 
in control samples (p < 0.001). Glycan loss increased sig-
nificantly with higher IL-1β doses, with 0.5 ng/ml causing 
a 61 ± 8% total loss (p < 0.001 vs ctrl) and 1 ng/ml inducing 
70 ± 6% loss (p < 0.001 vs ctrl) (Fig.  3b). In contrast, no 
significant loss of the labeled protein was detected during 
the first 12 days of IL-1β exposure (data not shown). After 
18 days of IL-1β exposure, only 1 ng/ml IL-1β induced a 
significant loss of protein compared to control (53 ± 15% vs 
7 ± 6%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3c).
The correlation between nascent and total ECM loss from 
cartilage was assessed by performing traditional sGAG and 
hydroxyproline assays. Spent culture media from control 
and 1 ng/ml IL-1β treated samples was used for both click 
chemistry readings and chemical assay tests. A significant 
correlation was detected between the GAG loss determined 
by the DMMB assay and glycan loss determined using click 
chemistry  (R2 = 0.86, p < 0.001, Pearson’s r = 0.93) (Fig. 3d). 
Similarly, collagen loss estimated by hydroxyproline assay 
correlated with protein loss by click chemistry  (R2 = 0.79, 
p < 0.001, Pearson’s r = 0.89) (Fig. 3e). Therefore, during 
ECM degradation, the loss of newly synthesized ECM was 
proportional to the loss of total ECM. The temporal trends 
of ECM loss detected by the click chemistry and traditional 
chemical assays were also highly correlated across individ-
ual samples (see Supplementary Figure S2).
Click Chemistry‑Based Drug Screening 
for Osteoarthritis
To explore the potential of click chemistry for high-through-
put drug screening, we evaluated the effects of three small 
molecule drugs (lithium chloride, valproic acid, and dexa-
methasone) on inflammation-induced degradation of car -
tilage ECM. Dexamethasone significantly reduced glycan 
loss compared to the IL-1β alone group, 65 ± 6% vs 83 ± 2% 
(p < 0.05). LiCl and VPA had no significant impact on gly -
can loss (Fig. 4b). After 28 days, IL-1β caused a substantial 
loss of nascent protein (86 ± 3%). All three drugs alleviated 
protein loss significantly (p < 0.05 vs IL-1β). Dexametha-
sone almost eliminated inflammation-induced protein loss, 
bringing it to the control group level (13 ± 1% vs 12 ± 2%), 
while VPA reduced protein loss to 25 ± 2% and LiCl reduced 
it to 73± 4% (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4c).
Quantification of ECM Synthesis Rates of hMSCs 
Cultured in Monolayer
Nascent ECM synthesis by monolayer hMSCs was labeled 
using click chemistry in a similar method to cartilage. After 
a 48-hour metabolic labeling period, both nascent glycans 
and proteins were visible around the hMSCs (red) (Fig. 5b). 
Glycans from adjacent cells merged to form networks, unlike 
the new proteins, which remained along the cell surface. 
This pattern differed from that observed in native cartilage 
matrix (Fig.  1c-e). The sensitivity of the click chemistry 
method was demonstrated by tracking hMSC ECM deposi-
tion in 12-hour increments. Enzymatic digestion showed no 
increase in fluorescent intensity during the first 12-24 hours 
for nascent glycans (1.0 ± 0.2 vs 1.1 ± 0.1, p = 0.9) and pro-
teins (1.0 ± 0.2 vs 1.0 ± 0.1, p = 0.9). Significant increase of 
deposited ECM was detected starting at 36 hours for both 
nascent glycans (2.1 ± 0.2, p < 0.001 vs 24 hrs) and proteins 
(1.8 ± 0.2, p < 0.001 vs 24 hrs). Further increases in depos-
ited nascent glycans and proteins were detected every 12 
hours thereafter (p < 0.05 for all comparisons 36-72 hrs) 
(Fig. 5c).

Discussion
We developed a copper-free click chemistry-based technique 
that enables rapid, sensitive, and cost-efficient quantification 
of ECM synthesis and degradation. By selectively labeling 
nascent glycans and proteins, this method provides simul-
taneous spatial visualization and longitudinal measurement 
of ECM remodeling with high temporal resolution. Its scal-
ability and sensitivity allowed detection of matrix deposi-
tion from as few as 10,000 mesenchymal stem cells within 
12-hour intervals, substantially shortening experimental 
timelines relative to conventional biochemical or histo-
logical assays. Given the continued expansion of available 
bioorthogonal probes, this framework can be readily adapted 
to additional ECM components and cell types, positioning it 
as a broadly applicable tool for studying matrix remodeling 
across biological systems (Fig.  6). A detailed protocol is 
included in the Supplementary Material to facilitate wider 
implementation.
Application of this technique to cartilage revealed dis-
tinct patterns of nascent ECM deposition. The most abun-
dant proteoglycan in cartilage is aggrecan, which contains 
up to 100 attached chondroitin sulfate GAG chains [17, 49]. 
These chains consist of alternating amino sugar GalNAc and 
glucuronic acid units [50]. Thus, the supplied azide-modified 
GalNAz can be incorporated into O-linked glycans and fur-
ther into the disaccharide building blocks of chondroitin sul-
fate GAG chains. The click-labeled nascent glycans localized 
preferentially within the PCM, consistent with the known 
behavior of aggrecan and other proteoglycans attaching to 
CD44 on the chondrocyte surface before integrating into the 
territorial-ECM [51–54]. AHA is an azide-modified methio-
nine, analogous to  [35S]methionine method for tracking nas-
cent collagen in cartilage [11, 55]. Collagens contain higher 
methionine content than other ECM proteins and are more 
abundant throughout the cartilage matrix (Supplementary 
Table S1) [26, 56], so most AHA molecules are expected 
to incorporate into collagen. In contrast to GalNAz-labeled 
glycans, nascent AHA-labeled proteins were largely absent 
from the PCM and instead accumulated in the IT-ECM 
within 24 hours of secretion, reflecting their accelerated 
incorporation into new collagen fibrils and the fibrillar net-
work [57]. These spatial dynamics align with our histologi-
cal staining, revealing that areas with accumulated nascent 
glycans or proteins also exhibited higher sulfated GAG and 
collagen contents, respectively. A similar spatial distribution 
of nascent collagen was also noted in engineered hydrogels, 
underscoring the ability of the click chemistry technique to 
resolve ECM dynamics that are otherwise challenging to 
track at short time scales [58].
Compared to traditional methods, such as dye-based 
chemical assays or radio-labeling methods, the click 
chemistry approach offers significant advantages in ana-
lytical-efficiency. The method enables detection of 24-hour 
changes in ECM production from as little as 3 mg of car -
tilage tissue, a level of sensitivity not achievable with 
standard assays that typically require larger samples and 
longer culture durations. Furthermore, the click chemistry 
method offers greater user convenience. Click chemistry 
does not require the use of harsh or dangerous chemicals, 
compared to hydrochloric acid for hydroxyproline assays 
or radioactive metabolic probes [29]. Longitudinal experi-
ments of ECM degradation can be quantified through sim-
ple fluorescent readouts at each time point, eliminating 
the need for repeated chemical assays. Together, these

The click chemistry-based approach, however, does 
have inherent limitations. Because the method relies on 
incorporating modified precursors through endogenous 
biosynthetic pathways, labeling efficiency and specific-
ity may vary with cell type and biochemical composition 
of the tissue. The method is therefore most suitable for 
tissues with matrices dominated by only a few distinct 
macromolecular components, such as cartilage. The use 
of incorporated metabolic precursors also means that the 
assay functions as a proportional reporter of newly syn -
thesized ECM, precluding quantitation of absolute nascent 
ECM content. Additionally, click chemistry only measures 
nascent ECM components, so complementary biochemical 
and histological methods remain essential tools for assess-
ing total matrix content and compositional integrity.
Despite these considerations, the click chemistry plat-
form offers a versatile and scalable tool for tissue engi-
neering, regenerative medicine, and ECM-centered disease 
research. The ability to rapidly quantify ECM synthesis 
at a high temporal resolution enables systemic optimiza-
tion of stem cell niches and refinement of the combination 
of cells, scaffolds, nutrients, and mechanical stimuli for 
artificial tissue fabrication [59, 60]. Beyond engineered 
systems, the method offers a practical solution for analyz-
ing ECM turnover in small clinical samples, such as tumor 
biopsies, which can inform therapeutic decisions and drug 
dosage optimization in oncology. Furthermore, the meth-
od’s low cost makes it well suited for high-throughput drug 
screening applications in conditions characterized by aber -
rant ECM remodeling, such as fibrosis and osteoarthritis.
