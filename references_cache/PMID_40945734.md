# Ascorbate mitigates oxidative stress and hemin cytotoxicity in heme oxygenase-1 deficiency.
**Authors:** Berendes LS, Schulze Westhoff P, Tobinski AM, Narvaez Pardo JA, Wingert V, Fobker M, Biskup S, Seelhöfer A, Van Marck V, Heitplatz B, Drell PT, Wittkowski H, Das AM, Hannibal L, Nowak WN, Józkowicz A, Klotz L, Varga G, Marquardt T, Park J
**Journal:** J Biol Chem (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jbc.2025.110705](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbc.2025.110705)

## Content

Heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) deficiency manifests with severe metabolic abnormalities, yet the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. We investigated metabolic adaptations in HO-1-deficient cells using patient-derived lymphoblastoid cells (LCL) and HEK293T knockout cells. Both cell types exhibited concordant metabolic signatures, characterized by a substantial reduction in intracellular ascorbic acid levels, despite compensatory upregulation of sodium-dependent vitamin C transporter 2 (SVCT2) and downregulation of GLUT1, compared to wild-type cells. Ultrastructural examination revealed abnormal mitochondrial morphology in both models. Both cell types showed higher baseline hydrogen peroxide levels compared to wild-type cells. Treatment with 2-phospho-L-ascorbic acid (AA2P) restored cell viability in both models upon hemin-induced stress, with protection requiring functional SVCT2-mediated uptake. AA2P supplementation attenuated the elevated H2O2 levels without altering glutathione redox homeostasis as measured by GSH/GSSG ratios. These findings illuminate ascorbic acid metabolism as a critical node in HO-1 deficiency pathophysiology and suggest ascorbic acid supplementation as a potential therapeutic strategy for this rare disorder.

Protein-bound heme plays vital roles in physiology that include oxygen transport by hemoglobin, electron transfer in the mitochondrial respiratory chain, and nitric oxide signaling by guanylate cyclase (1, 2). However, heme in its free state is a pro-oxidant molecule that can lead to cytotoxicity. Consequently, free heme is broken down by a highly efficient catabolic pathway initiated by one of two isoforms of heme oxygenase (inducible HO-1 and constitutive HO-2) (3, 4), which convert heme into equimolar amounts of carbon monoxide (CO), ferrous iron (Fe2+), and biliverdin (5). The products of this reaction themselves can exert antioxidant effects (6), while HO-1 activation stimulates various oxidant-responsive transcription factors (7). These properties make heme oxygenases fundamental actors in the response to redox stress.

The absence of HO-1 results in a progressive phenotype in murine models, highlighting the physiological importance of this protein. These animals exhibit impaired fertility, increased prenatal lethality, and reduced stress resistance, accompanied by anemia, inflammation, iron accumulation, and excess oxidative damage (8, 9). Human HO-1 deficiency (OMIM #614034), first described by Yachie and colleagues (10), mirrors this murine phenotype but is much more severe, with hemolytic anemia, chronic inflammation, and recurrent, treatment-resistant episodes of hyperinflammation. These inflammatory episodes cause extensive damage across multiple organ systems, leading to death during childhood (11). It is worth noting that although abnormal iron metabolism was common to human and murine HO-1 deficiency, dysfunction of endothelium and monocytes seemed to be more significant in humans (11, 12).

While macrophage repopulation or bone marrow transplantation have been proposed as potential treatments, the lack of effective, clinically available therapies for HO-1 deficiency remains a significant challenge (9, 13). This unmet need is caused by a lack of mechanistic insight into the condition’s pathophysiology. Although oxidative damage is believed to play a central role (11), the specific molecules and metabolites involved are not well understood. Studies in non-human models have implicated altered mitochondrial function as a downstream consequence of HO-1 deficiency (14, 15, 16, 17, 18). However, the relevance of these changes in human HO-1 deficiency remains unclear. Intriguingly, human cases of HO-1 deficiency frequently present with an initial stable phase, followed by inflammatory exacerbation. This disease course suggests the presence of compensatory mechanisms that could be targeted for new therapeutic approaches.

Here, we investigated metabolic changes and compensatory mechanisms in a patient-derived lymphoblastoid cell line and HEK293T knockout cells. Our findings indicate that HO-1 deficiency is associated with ascorbic acid depletion, leading to increased oxidative damage. HO-1-deficient cells exhibited altered mitochondrial ultrastructure and impaired mitochondrial function. In hemin-stimulated HO-1-deficient cells, 2-phospho-L-ascorbic acid (AA2P) normalized cell viability, an effect inhibited by the ascorbate uptake inhibitor sulfinpyrazone. In conclusion, we propose ascorbic acid as a potential therapeutic agent for HO-1 deficiency.

To investigate the disease mechanisms underlying HO-1 deficiency, we employed two complementary cellular models. First, we utilized lymphoblastoid cells derived from a patient with compound heterozygous truncating variants in HMOX1 [c.55dupG (p.Glu19Glyfs∗14); c.262_268delinsCC (p.Ala88Profs∗51)] (19), designated LCL HO-1 MUT/MUT. Second, CRISPR/Cas9-engineered HEK293T cells lacking HO-1 (HEK293 HO-1 −/−), alongside their respective wild-type controls, were used. The HEK293T model, which originates from a different germ layer than lymphoblastoid cells (20), provided a variant-independent system to investigate the consequences of HO-1 deficiency. Our previous characterization demonstrated the complete absence of HO-1 protein in the patient-derived cells due to nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (19), validating the complementary use of these knockout models to study the cellular phenotype of HO-1 deficiency.

To evaluate the functional consequences of HO-1 deficiency, we challenged both cellular models with increasing concentrations of hemin, the physiological substrate of HO-1. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that patient-derived LCL HO-1
MUT/MUT cells exhibited significantly reduced viability compared to wild-type controls when exposed to 50, 75, and 100 μM hemin (Fig. 1A). Similarly, HEK293 HO-1 −/− cells demonstrated marked sensitivity to hemin, with substantial decreases in viability at concentrations of 30 and 60 μM (Fig. 1B). Beyond sensitivity to hemin challenge, we investigated whether HO-1 deficiency affects basal cellular proliferation. Non-linear regression analysis of exponential growth curves revealed cell type-specific alterations in proliferation kinetics. Patient-derived lymphoblastoid cell lines displayed a different pattern, with LCL HO-1 MUT/MUT cells maintaining proliferation rates (k = 0.01405 h−1, CI 0.01103–0.01816 h−1) comparable to control LCLs (k = 0.01457 h−1; p = 0.9) (Fig. 1C). In contrast, HEK293 cells, HO-1 deficiency significantly reduced the exponential growth rate constant by 34% (k = 0.02353 h−1, CI 0.01663–0.03212 h−1) compared to wild-type counterparts (k = 0.0356 h−1, CI 0.02918–0.04363); p = 0.02) (Fig. 1D). This finding aligns with HO-1's established role in promoting cell cycle progression through modulation of redox status and interaction with growth signaling pathways.

Given HO-1’s ability to generate Fe2+ from heme, we evaluated the cellular iron content but found no significant difference between HO-1 competent or deficient cells in either LCL or the HEK293 model (Fig. 1, E and F).

To characterize the redox status of HO-1-deficient cells, we quantified intracellular hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) levels using a selective boronate-based fluorescent probe. Both LCL HO-1 MUT/MUT and HEK293 HO-1 −/− cells exhibited significantly elevated basal H2O2 concentrations compared to their respective wild-type counterparts (p = 0.002 and p = 0.03, Fig. 2, A and B). Additionally, the GSH/GSSG ratios were significantly reduced in HEK293 HO-1 −/− cells when relative to wild-type controls (p = 0.007), whereas no significant difference was observed in LCL (Fig. 2, C and D). To identify molecular adaptations in HO-1 deficiency, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) comparing patient-derived LCL HO-1 MUT/MUT to wild-type cells (see Supporting Information, including Supplemental methods and raw results). This analysis revealed significant upregulation of SLC23A2, which encodes the principal high-affinity sodium-dependent vitamin C transporter SVCT2 (Fig. S1). Given the critical role of ascorbate in cellular antioxidant defense, we validated this finding at the protein level. Quantitative immunoblotting confirmed significantly increased SVCT2 protein expression in both LCL HO-1 MUT/MUT and HEK293 HO-1 −/− cells compared to HO-1 competent controls (p = 0.004 and p < 0.001, respectively; Fig. 3, A, B, I, and J). Despite this compensatory upregulation of SVCT2, intracellular ascorbic acid levels were significantly depleted in LCL HO-1 MUT/MUT and HEK293 HO-1 −/− cells under standard culture conditions (p = 0.020 and p = 0.030, respectively; Fig. 3, E and M). In contrast, the expression of GLUT1, a major dehydroascorbate (DHA) transporter, was significantly reduced in both HEK293 HO-1 −/− cells (p < 0.001) and LCL HO-1 MUT/MUT (p < 0.001) when compared to wildtype controls (Fig. 3, C, D, K, and L). GLUT1, which serves as a major transporter for both glucose and dehydroascorbate (DHA), showed particularly severe reduction in LCL HO-1MUT/MUT cells, where a characteristic ∼52 kDa GLUT1 band observed in wildtype cells was completely absent in immunoblot analysis, while a smaller, ∼45-kDa band was observed in both cell types.

Despite this reduction in DHA uptake transporter levels, the activity of dehydroascorbate reductase (DHAR, EC 1.8.5.1) in both LCL and HEK293T cells was not significantly different between HO-1 competent and deficient cells (Fig. 3, H, and P).

Given conflicting reports regarding HO-1's impact on mitochondrial biology (16, 21, 22), we conducted comprehensive analyses of mitochondrial morphology and function in our cellular models. Transmission electron microscopy revealed that mitochondrial ultrastructure in LCL HO-1 MUT/MUT was indistinguishable from controls under baseline conditions (Fig. 4, A and C). However, upon exposure to sublethal concentrations of hemin (25 μM), HO-1-deficient lymphoblasts exhibited significant mitochondrial enlargement across all morphometric parameters measured (Fig. 4, A and B). In contrast, HEK293 HO-1 −/− cells displayed pronounced mitochondrial enlargement even at baseline, with significant increases in width, perimeter, and area compared to wild-type controls This mitochondrial swelling was further exacerbated following hemin challenge (Fig. 4, C and D). To determine whether these structural alterations were accompanied by functional impairments, we performed comprehensive bioenergetic profiling. Spectrophotometric analysis of respiratory chain complexes in HEK293HO-1 −/− cells revealed significantly elevated ATP synthase (Complex V) activity (396 nmol/min/mg protein versus 279 nmol/min/mg protein in controls), while activities of other complexes and citrate synthase remained comparable between genotypes (Table 1). Despite the enhanced ATP synthase activity, total cellular ATP content was unchanged in HO-1 deficient cells (Fig. 4E), suggesting compensatory metabolic adaptations. Seahorse extracellular flux analysis demonstrated that HO-1 deficiency significantly compromised both mitochondrial and glycolytic functions, with marked reductions in basal respiration (30.43 ± 0.17 versus 44.07 ± 0.31 pmol/min, p < 0.001), maximal respiratory capacity (18.41 ± 0.75 versus 24.74 ± 1.21 pmol/min, p = 0.01), basal glycolysis, and glycolytic capacity (Fig. 4E). To identify potential therapeutic approaches for HO-1 deficiency, we evaluated whether antioxidant supplementation could mitigate hemin-induced cytotoxicity. We selected clinically relevant antioxidants at concentrations informed by published pharmacokinetic data in humans (Table 2) (23, 24, 25, 26). To achieve stable and consistent levels of ascorbic acid, 2-phospho-L-ascorbic acid (AA2P) was chosen for use in cell culture (27). In LCL HO-1 WT/WT cells, none of the tested antioxidants significantly altered viability (Fig. 1G). In contrast, LCL HO-1 MUT/MUT cells exhibited a striking improvement in survival when pre-treated with 100 μM 2-phospho-L-ascorbic acid (AA2P) before hemin challenge (p < 0.01), while cysteamine (126 μM) unexpectedly reduced viability (p < 0.05). These findings demonstrate that cellular uptake of AA2P via SVCT2 is essential for its protective effects in the context of HO-1 deficiency, ruling out a mechanism based solely on extracellular antioxidant activity. This observation further supports the therapeutic relevance of our findings, as it identifies SVCT2-mediated ascorbate transport as a critical determinant of cell survival in HO-1 deficiency.

Heme oxygenase-1 deficiency is a devastating inborn error of metabolism with limited therapeutic options and poor clinical outcomes despite aggressive immunosuppressive interventions (11). A striking feature of this condition is a silent interval preceding clinical disease manifestations, suggesting the existence of compensatory mechanisms that temporarily mitigate disease progression. We identified three distinct cellular alterations in HO-1 deficiency: changes in mitochondrial structure and function (Fig. 4), dysregulated ascorbate metabolism with compensatory upregulation of SVCT2 (Fig. 3), and cell type-specific impacts on cellular proliferation (Fig. 1). Most importantly, we demonstrated that ascorbate repletion through 2-phospho-L-ascorbic acid (AA2P) administration effectively prevents hemin-induced cytotoxicity in HO-1 deficient cells through an SVCT2-dependent mechanism (Figs. 5 and 6).

Our complementary cellular models—patient-derived lymphoblastoid cells harboring pathogenic HMOX1 variants and CRISPR/Cas9-engineered HEK293T cells—faithfully recapitulated cardinal features of HO-1 deficiency, including heightened sensitivity to hemin exposure and oxidative stress. The consistency of phenotypes across these distinct cell lineages strengthens the validity of our findings and their potential relevance to diverse tissues affected in this multisystem disorder. Lymphoblastoid cells are particularly germane models for investigating HO-1 deficiency, as they originate from bone marrow-derived lymphocytes. The bone marrow represents a critical site of pathology in HO-1 deficiency, with HO-1 playing essential roles in maintaining hematopoietic stem cell function and preventing premature exhaustion (28).

Consistent with established paradigms, we observed significant redox imbalance, with both LCL HO-1 MUT/MUT and HEK293 HO-1 −/− cells exhibiting elevated basal H2O2 concentrations compared to wild-type counterparts (Fig. 2, A and B). GSH/GSSG ratios—a key indicator of cellular redox status—were significantly reduced in HEK293 HO-1 −/− cells relative to controls, whereas LCL cells showed no significant difference in glutathione redox status (Fig. 2, C and D). Of note, cultured LCLs displayed unexpectedly high GSH/GSSG ratios across genotypes. These ratios likely reflect exceptionally effective preservation of the reduced state or specific aspects of our culture conditions (29, 30), rather than indicating any underlying biological abnormality. Importantly, we observed no differences in these ratios between genotypes, suggesting that this phenomenon is methodological rather than genotype-specific. This cell type-specific difference in glutathione homeostasis despite consistent H2O2 elevation suggests differential adaptation of redox systems across cell lineages. This dysregulation was observed consistently across both our cellular models, indicating a fundamental metabolic consequence of HO-1 deficiency rather than a cell type-specific phenomenon. Despite the observed redox imbalance, measurements of cellular iron content revealed no significant differences between HO-1-deficient and control cells across both cellular models. This finding is noteworthy given HO-1's canonical role in heme degradation and subsequent iron release. The absence of iron accumulation suggests that the observed oxidative stress likely stems from the loss of HO-1's direct antioxidant functions rather than iron-mediated Fenton chemistry.

Concurrently, we found substantially altered ascorbate metabolism as a consequence of HO-1 deficiency (Fig. 3). HO-1-deficient cells exhibited significantly reduced ascorbate levels at baseline that were further depleted by stimulation with the substrate hemin. These reductions were accompanied by complex changes of ascorbic acid influx: SVCT2, the principal ascorbic acid uptake transporter was upregulated in both HO-1-deficient HEK293 and LCL cells, suggesting a compensatory increase of expression in the context of intracellular depletion. Simultaneously, the expression of GLUT1, a potent DHA uptake transporter was reduced in both cell types when compared to HO-1 competent controls with no significant differences in DHAR activity. These divergent expression patterns of major regulators of cellular AA content indicate complex compensatory mechanisms. SVCT2 is frequently upregulated in response to increased oxidative stress (31, 32) and likely represents an adaptive response to chronic oxidative stress, although the precise signaling pathways mediating this response remain to be elucidated. Transcriptional regulation of SVCT2 has been linked to Nrf2 signaling in other contexts, and this pathway may be perturbed in HO-1 deficiency given HO-1's role as an Nrf2 target gene.

GLUT1 shows variable and oftentimes cell-type-specific responses to increased ROS but is known to be downregulated as a consequence of prolonged, H2O2-mediated oxidative stress (33, 34), which aligns with our findings of increased H2O2 levels in HO-1 deficiency.

The observed downregulation of GLUT1 in HO-1-deficient cells, particularly the loss of the characteristic ∼52 kDa band in LCL HO-1 MUT/MUT cells, warrants contextual interpretation beyond the direct effects of HO-1 deficiency. Similar GLUT1 expression alterations have been documented in mutations affecting other genes not directly involved in glucose transport. For instance, PURA gene mutations result in reduced GLUT1 expression and hypoglycorrhachia despite intact SLC2A1 (35), while pathogenic variants in SLC9A6, encoding the endosomal Na+/H+ exchanger NHE6, can lead to secondary GLUT1 downregulation (36). Furthermore, large-scale genomic screens have identified over 300 genes whose disruption affects GLUT1 expression, with particular enrichment in G-protein coupled receptor and purinergic signaling pathways (37). Thus, the GLUT1 dysregulation we observed may reflect a common cellular response to oxidative insult rather than a specific effect of HO-1 deficiency, potentially representing a broader pattern of metabolic adaptation that converges on altered glucose and DHA transport capacity.

In parallel with these redox disruptions, we observed significant alterations in both mitochondrial structure and function in HO-1-deficient cells, with distinct patterns emerging between our cellular models. Electron microscopy revealed that LCL HO-1 MUT/MUT maintained normal mitochondrial ultrastructure under baseline conditions but developed significant mitochondrial enlargement upon hemin challenge (25 μM, Fig. 4, A and B). In contrast, HEK293 HO-1 −/− cells exhibited pronounced mitochondrial enlargement even at baseline, with significant increases in width, perimeter, and area compared to controls—effects further exacerbated by hemin exposure (Fig. 4, C and D).

While our data do not directly establish whether these mitochondrial defects are a consequence of increased hemin, oxidative (peroxide) stress, or other factors, it is well established that mitochondrial structure and function are highly sensitive to redox imbalances. Oxidative stress, characterized by excessive production of ROS and impaired antioxidant defenses, is a recognized driver of mitochondrial swelling and dysfunction through mechanisms such as mitochondrial permeability transition (38, 39) and altered membrane potential (40). Moreover, mitochondrial dysfunction itself can further amplify oxidative stress, creating a self-perpetuating cycle.

Bioenergetic profiling demonstrated that these structural changes were accompanied by substantial functional alterations. Spectrophotometric analysis revealed significantly elevated ATP synthase activity in HO-1-deficient cells (396 versus 279 nmol/min/mg protein in controls), while other respiratory chain complexes and citrate synthase remained largely unaffected (Fig. 4E). Despite this upregulation, total cellular ATP content was unchanged, suggesting compensatory metabolic adaptations. Seahorse extracellular flux analysis confirmed compromised bioenergetics in HO-1-deficient cells, with marked reductions in basal respiration (30.43 ± 0.17 versus 44.07 ± 0.31 pmol/min), maximal respiratory capacity (18.41 ± 0.75 versus 24.74 ± 1.21 pmol/min), and glycolytic function (Fig. 4E). These observations align with previous studies in murine models suggesting that HO-1 contributes to mitochondrial quality control through its enzymatic products (14, 16, 41), although the precise mechanisms in human cells remain to be elucidated.

Taken together, while our findings demonstrate a robust association between HO-1 deficiency, altered mitochondrial morphology, and impaired bioenergetics, the specific contribution of oxidative stress or hemin accumulation to these defects cannot be definitively determined from our current data. However, a broader literature supports a central role for oxidative stress in mediating mitochondrial damage across diverse cellular contexts. In turn, mitochondrial dysfunction may further augment reactive oxygen species generation, depleting ascorbate pools and contributing to elevated H2O2 levels.

Having established significant alterations in redox homeostasis and mitochondrial function, we next investigated whether these metabolic disruptions influenced cellular proliferation. Our findings revealed a dichotomy in proliferation phenotypes between our cellular models. HEK293 HO-1 −/− cells exhibited significantly reduced proliferation rates compared to wild-type counterparts (Fig. 1D). In contrast, patient-derived LCL HO-1 MUT/MUT cells maintained proliferation rates comparable to control LCLs (Fig. 1C). This differential impact suggests that HO-1's role in cellular proliferation is context-dependent and influenced by lineage-specific factors. In epithelial-derived cells, HO-1 appears to be intimately linked to proliferation pathways, while lymphoid cells display remarkable resilience to HO-1 deficiency under basal conditions. This resilience likely reflects specialized mechanisms in hematopoietic and lymphoid cells for managing oxidative stress while maintaining proliferative capacity consistent with the clinical observation that hematopoietic tissues can function normally for extended periods before disease manifestation in patients with HO-1 deficiency.

Our previous work revealed an additional critical role for HO-1 in protecting cells from replication stress through prevention of DNA G-quadruplex accumulation (42), demonstrating that HO-1 deficiency disrupts the PARP1-p53-p21 axis and impairs nuclear accumulation of p53, compromising genomic stability. This mechanism may provide further insight into the specific cellular vulnerabilities observed in our HO-1-deficient models, potentially connecting oxidative stress, ascorbate depletion, and genomic integrity through shared regulatory pathways.

Based on our observations of ascorbate deficiency, we demonstrated that supplementation with AA2P effectively prevented hemin-induced cytotoxicity in HO-1-deficient cells, unlike other commonly used antioxidants (Figs. 1 and 5). Dose-response experiments showed significant cytoprotection at AA2P concentrations of 50 to 100 μM, with cell viability restored to near-baseline levels even under hemin challenge (Fig. 5). This protective effect was SVCT2-dependent, as pharmaceutical SVCT2 inhibition with sulfinpyrazone abolished the cytoprotective effects of AA2P treatment (Fig. 6).

Intracellular ascorbate measurements confirmed that AA2P supplementation successfully restored ascorbate levels in HO-1-deficient cells (Fig. 3, G and O), demonstrating effective cellular uptake and conversion to active ascorbate. Of note, the detrimental effects of AA2P in excess concentrations were observed in both cell models, indicating a pro-oxidant effect of AA2P in alignment with previous literature (43, 44). AA2P treatment significantly reduced elevated H2O2 levels in both cell types compared to untreated HO-1-deficient cells (p = 0.02 and p = 0.01 in HEK293T and LCL, respectively, Fig. 2, A and B). Interestingly, despite successfully lowering H2O2 concentrations, AA2P treatment did not alter cellular GSH/GSSG (Fig. 2, C and D), suggesting that AA2P's antioxidant activity may operate primarily through direct reactive oxygen species scavenging rather than by modulating the glutathione system.

Despite AA2P's robust protection against hemin-induced cytotoxicity, it failed to normalize the impaired proliferation kinetics of HO-1-deficient cells. Nonlinear regression analysis of exponential growth curves measured by Hoechst 33,342 nuclear staining over 72 h revealed that ascorbate supplementation had no significant effect on growth rates in either HEK293 HO-1 −/− cells or LCL HO-1 MUT/MUT cells (Fig. 5, C and F). This selective rescue of viability but not proliferation provides compelling evidence for the functional separation of HO-1's redox-regulatory and proliferation-modulatory roles.

Our findings reveal a previously unrecognized metabolic signature in HO-1 deficiency, characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction and ascorbate depletion with compensatory dysregulation of ascorbic acid transporters, and redox imbalance manifesting as elevated H2O2 levels. The demonstration that AA2P supplementation provides SVCT2-dependent protection against hemin-induced cytotoxicity and normalizes H2O2 levels establishes a promising therapeutic approach that addresses a specific metabolic vulnerability in this devastating disorder. These results provide mechanistic insights into the cellular pathophysiology of HO-1 deficiency and identify ascorbate repletion as a potential adjunctive therapy deserving further preclinical and clinical evaluation. Future translational studies should evaluate whether ascorbate repletion can attenuate the inflammatory and hematological manifestations of HO-1 deficiency in preclinical models, potentially offering a readily implementable adjunctive therapy for this devastating disorder.

Lysation was performed either with lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 1.0% Triton X-100 (SVCT2), or RIPA buffer (GLUT1) and protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (#ab201119, Abcam), pH 7.6 for 30 min on ice. Adhesive cells were scraped after the addition of the buffer. The suspension was centrifuged at 10,000g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was diluted to a concentration of 2 μg protein/μl in lysis buffer and mixed with LI-COR 4 × protein sample loading buffer (#928-40004, LI-COR Biosciences), followed by denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min. For SVCT2 and GLUT1 detection, 15 μg of protein were separated on 4 to 12% Bis-Tris gels (#NP0322BOX, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total protein visualization using the Revert 700 total protein stain (#926-11021, LI-COR) and transfer onto nitrocellulose membranes was carried out following the manufacturer’s protocol. Membranes were blocked with 5% milk in PBST (SVCT2) or 5% BSA in TBST (GLUT1) for 1 h at room temperature and constant agitation, followed by incubation with primary antibodies in blocking buffer at 4 °C overnight. Anti-SVCT2 Polyclonal Antibody (#27019-1-AP, Proteintech) was used at a dilution of 1:4000 in 5% milk in PBST. Anti-GLUT1 antibody (D3J3A, #12939T, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) was diluted 1:1000 in 5% BSA in TBST. For SVCT2 visualization, IRDye 800 CW conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (#925-32213, LiCor) was used at a dilution of 1:20,000 as a secondary antibody. GLUT1 was visualized using HRP- HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG(H+L) (#SA00001-2, Proteintech). The specificity of the antibodies was verified by the vendors. Blots were visualized on a Chemidoc apparatus. Fluorescence and luminescence intensity analyses were performed using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad) with normalization to total protein as determined by total protein stain.

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.4.1, GraphPad Software, Inc).

For comparisons between two groups, Student's unpaired two-sided t test was applied. For multiple group comparisons with normally distributed data, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's post hoc test (for comparisons to a control group) or two-way ANOVA followed by Šidák's post hoc test (for factorial designs) were used. Non-normally distributed data from multiple groups were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn's post hoc test.

Growth rate analysis was performed using non-linear least-squares regression with an exponential growth model (Y=Y0ekx), where k represents the growth rate constant. Comparison of best-fit values between groups was conducted using the extra sum-of-squares F test. Residuals were examined visually to confirm model appropriateness. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. For all analyses, exact p values are reported where possible.

For normalization of data, raw values were divided by the mean of the respective control group (either solvent-treated or wild-type cells) within each independent experiment. Relative viabilities were calculated as (sample value/mean control value) × 100 and expressed as percentages. Unless otherwise indicated, experiments were conducted with n = 3 biological replicates, each comprising three technical replicates. No outliers were excluded from the analyses.
