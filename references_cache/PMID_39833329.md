Cerebral proliferative angiopathy (CPA) is a rare subtype of cerebral 
arteriovenous malformation, characterized by unique angiographic features and 
clinical presentations. Although the clinical and angiographic characteristics 
of CPA have been well described, their impact on the surrounding tissues remains 
underexplored. Herein, we investigated the presence of calvarial thickening in 
patients with CPA, and discuss its potential pathogenesis. This retrospective 
multicenter cohort study enrolled 16 CPA patients from our institutions. 
Patients were identified by a search of the hospital medical records for key 
words related to "cerebral proliferative angiopathy." Data on demographics, CPA 
characteristics, clinical symptoms, and calvarial thickening were collected and 
analyzed using CT and MRI. Statistical analyses were performed using t-tests and 
Fisher's exact tests, with significance set at P < 0.05. Patients with CPA 
ranged from 4 to 69 years of age and underwent skull evaluation at a median age 
of 26.5 years. Calvarial thickening was present in 43.8% of cases, predominantly 
affecting the frontal bone, and extending bilaterally. A trend toward calvarial 
thickening was observed in patients with concomitant signs of cerebral venous 
congestion (p = 0.06), while no significant association with age, sex, or 
symptoms was found. Calvarial thickening displayed centripetal proliferation 
with trabecular formation extending from the inner table. This study identified 
Calvarial centripetal proliferation in a significant proportion of patients with 
CPA. We hypothesized that this may be related to elevated vascular endothelial 
growth factor levels due to the proangiogenic nature of CPA.

Cerebral proliferative angiopathy (CPA) is a rare and poorly understood vascular disease that accounts for approximately 3.4% of all cerebral arteriovenous malformations (AVMs)1. Since Lasjaunias et al. first described CPA as a clinical entity in 2008, fewer than 100 additional cases have been reported in the literature2. CPA is angiographically characterized by the absence of dominant feeders to a large nidus, a small draining vein size in relation to the size of the arteriovenous shunting zone, the presence of a transdural supply, stenosis of feeding arteries, and the presence of intermingled brain tissue between the vascular spaces, which can be observed on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)1. In terms of clinical characteristics, CPA predominantly affects young females, and primarily presents as headaches, seizures, or transient ischemic attacks (TIA)2. Hemorrhage is uncommon; however, rebleeding rates are high1,2. No standardized treatment method has yet been indicated; however, most patients are treated conservatively, while a small number are treated with endovascular embolization, indirect revascularization, or radiosurgery2–4. With regard to its clinical course, ongoing development/appearance of additional vessels and disturbances in cerebral blood flow (CBF) over time have been reported, with neurological deterioration occurring in approximately 10% of cases2.

Calvarial thickening can be observed in patients with primary or secondary metabolic bone disorders (e.g., hyperparathyroidism and renal osteodystrophy), chronic hemolytic disorders (e.g., thalassemia and sickle cell disease), Paget’s disease, phenytoin medication, and various overgrowth disorders such as acromegaly5. These conditions has also been observed in patients with reactive bony changes to extra-axial tumors6,7, Sturge-Weber syndrome8 and Dyke-Davidoff-Masson syndrome9, but has not yet been described in CPA patients. In this study, we (a) investigate the occurrence of calvarial thickening in patients with CPA, (b) present this novel imaging finding, and (c) discuss its potential underlying pathogenesis.

This retrospective multicenter cohort study aimed to explore the calvarial and intracranial imaging characteristics of CPA. Patients diagnosed with CPA at our quaternary referral centers were enrolled. Patients were identified through a systematic computer-assisted search of radiology reports using the following keywords: cerebral proliferative angiopathy, computed tomography (CT), and MRI. Patients were excluded if no diagnostic skull imaging datasets were available, or if an alternative diagnosis other than CPA was made. Overall, 16 patients were identified and included in the secondary review. All neuroimaging was performed in accordance with departmental protocols, including multiplanar soft tissue and bone algorithm CT reconstructions, as well as multisequence and multiplanar MRI. The imaging protocols for each imaging modality depended on the standards of each facility and were not standardized. All imaging data were systematically reviewed by three experienced neuroradiologists (TK, TPK and TH) with 13–33 years of experience in adult and pediatric neuroimaging. The three neuroradiologists evaluated independently, and only cases with concordant evaluations were included.

Data on the following patient characteristics were collected: age at the initial digital subtraction angiography (DSA), sex, clinical presentation, CPA characteristics (location, feeding arteries, transdural supply, nidus, draining vein, and venous congestion), calvarial thickening on the latest skull imaging, and treatment modality. Calvarial thickening was qualitatively evaluated by the presence of “centripetal proliferation” and “trabecular formation” according to morphology. Centripetal proliferation” was defined as the formation of new bone structures centripetally from the original inner table, whereas ‘trabecular formation’ was defined as the presence of a trabecular structure within the new bone.

This study was approved by the research ethics board of our hospital (ID-number: 23-5609.0) in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. Patients were not contacted regarding the study. With the approval of the research ethics board in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Age was expressed as the median (range) and was evaluated using the t-test. Other factors were expressed as percentages, and were evaluated using the Fisher’s exact test. Due to the small sample size, the t-test was used for continuous variables and the Fisher exact test for categorical variables. In addition, Pearson’s chi-square test was used to evaluate the validity of venous congestion. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro 15 (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, United States), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Table 1 presents the characteristics of the study participants. The age at diagnosis of patients with CPA ranged from 4 to 69 years, with a median age of 17.5 years, while the median age at neuroimaging evaluation was 26.5 years (range: 6–69 years). There was a slight female predominance in the cohort (56.3%). TIA was the most common clinical presentation (50.0%), followed by seizures (37.5%) and headaches (31.3%). Most CPAs (93.8%) were located in the supratentorial region, with the majority (81.3%) being diffuse (> 6 cm), while 62.5% showed watershed zone involvement. There were no significant differences in lesion laterality. Approximately half of patients (56.3%) experienced venous congestion.

Calvarial thickening was present in seven cases (43.8%), and showed no association with age, sex, or clinical presentation. There were further no significant differences in lesion localization. Lesions in both groups were diffuse with watershed zone involvement (P = 0.63). In the calvarial thickening group, all lesions were > 6 cm in size (P = 0.21). A trend toward calvarial thickening was observed in patients with concomitant signs of cerebral venous congestion (P = 0.06, Pearson’s chi-square test: P = 0.04).

Figure 1 presents a summary all cases of calvarial thickening. All thickening resulted in a centripetal proliferative pattern, characterized by an expanded inner table, with no changes observed in the outer table. Specifically, the inner border of the original inner table was identifiable in many cases, with bony tissue formation occurring inside and creating a new layer. The expansion of the inner table was primarily accompanied by trabecular formation, with some cases showing thickening of the inner table.

Figure 2 presents the illustrative case of a female patient who was diagnosed with CPA at 4 years of age. Clinical symptoms at presentation included seizures and multiple episodes of TIA, characterized by recurrent right-sided weakness, numbness, facial droop, and slurring of speech, with each episode lasting up to 15 min. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) revealed a left-sided CPA with hyperproliferation of the left anterior cerebral artery (ACA) and middle cerebral artery (MCA) branches, and very slow arteriovenous shunting (Fig. 2A). The venous phase of DSA demonstrated mild venous congestion, as well as relatively small, but dilated cortical veins and dural venous sinuses (Fig. 2B). An external carotid angiogram showed multiple dilated branches of the left middle meningeal artery anastomosed with the left ACA branches (Fig. 2C). MRI revealed intermingled brain tissue between the diffuse and loosely spaced vascular channels (Fig. 2D), with involvement of the watershed zone. Overall, these findings supported the diagnosis of CPA. The initial CT scan at the age of 4 years showed normal skull width (Fig. 2E and F); however, the most recent CT scan at the age of 12 years revealed bilateral calvarial centripetal proliferation with trabecular formation (Fig. 2G and H).

In this study, we identified calvarial thickening in approximately half of patients diagnosed with CPA, a rare entity (3.4%) of cerebral AVMs. Calvarial thickening was bilateral and exhibited a centripetal pattern, expanding the inner table of the skull with a trabecular formation. Regarding the frequency of skull involvement, the frontal bones were affected in all cases, followed by the parietal and temporal bones, and then the rest of the skull. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that calvarial thickening was initiated in the frontal bone and extended posteriorly. Although no statistically significant clinical or imaging findings were associated with calvarial thickening in our study, the observed trend indicated that calvarial thickening may be more common in patients with angiographic signs of venous congestion.

Many diseases which can result in calvarial thickening have been reported5, for which the underlying mechanisms can be classified into three main categories: external factors, hematopoietic demand, and osteocyte remodeling. External factors include neoplasia, inflammation, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) hypotension, and brain atrophy. These include primary and metastatic tumors, meningitis10 and hematoma calcification, post-shunt surgery11 or spontaneous intracranial hypotension12, and Dyke-Davidoff-Masson syndrome9, respectively. The morphological characteristics of calvarial thickening associated with external factors vary depending on the location of the factor, which affects the outer, diploic, and inner tables. Among these, CSF hypotension has been reported to bilaterally increase the thickness from the inner table to the diploic space11,12.

Hematopoietic demand is defined as an erythropoietin-mediated change, which may include hemolytic disorders, such as thalassemia13 and sickle cell disease, as well as congenital cardiovascular and respiratory diseases in pediatric patients14,15. The expansion of the diploic space, described as a “hair-on-end” appearance, is a representative morphological feature of calvarial thickening associated with hematopoietic demand. In such cases, membranous ossification is not enhanced; instead, calvarial thickening is caused by ballooning of the diploic space and is not layered.

Osteocyte remodeling is also a major cause of calvarial thickening which is thought to result from a disturbance in the balance between osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Parathyroid hormones are considered as factors which can affect both cell types16, while prior studies have reported that primary hyperparathyroidism17 and renal osteodystrophy18 can cause bilateral diploic fibrosis and thickening. Additionally, osteoclast dysfunction has been reported to cause thickening of the cortical bone, triggering diseases related to receptor activator of NF-κB ligand (RANKL), which is important in the osteoclast signaling pathway. Paget’s disease has further been shown to involve abnormalities in the RANKL pathway19. The RANKL pathway can also be suppressed via growth hormone in acromegaly20,21 and via transforming growth factor β in meningioma7, as well as long-term use of phenytoin22,23. The morphological hallmark of these diseases is thickening of the outer table.

Finally, remodeling associated with osteoblast hyperactivity has been reported, and is thought to be mediated by vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)24. Adjacent hyperostosis has been observed in hemangiomas25 and orbital AVM26, for which VEGF is considered to be the primary contributing factor. Additionally, hyperostosis frontalis interna (HFI), characterized by hyperostosis in the bilateral frontal bones of the skull27, is believed to be driven by humoral factors such as VEGF and estrogen28. Furthermore, studies have reported that thickening occurs in the bone ipsilateral to the port-wine stain in Sturge-Weber syndrome8. Thus, VEGF-mediated remodeling is characterized by calvarial thickening in accordance with the spread of VEGF in response to pathological conditions.

VEGF is thought to promote angiogenesis and osteogenesis directly24. VEGF is produced both physiologically and pathologically under hypoxic conditions, and functions as a potent angiogenic factor to support the sprouting of vascular endothelial cells. Lesions associated with hypoxia, such as Moyamoya disease, AVMs, and dural arteriovenous fistulas (DAVFs)29,30, exhibit high levels of VEGF in the lesion and surrounding tissue. It has further been speculated that VEGF establishes dural-to-pial or pial-to-dural blood supply routes. In CPA, the primary cause of vessel proliferation is believed to be local hypoxemia, resulting in a secondary increase in VEGF levels31,32. Indeed, one study identified elevated VEGF levels in the CSF33.

Additionally, studies have shown that VEGF directly stimulates osteoblasts and enhances membranous ossification, particularly in the calvarium34. As such, VEGF acts on the inner tablet, causing centripetal osteogenesis. The characteristic feature of this osteogenic change is trabecular formation, observed as an increase in the diploic space25, accompanied by the formation of new bone structures containing trabecular structures inside the inner table28. This finding is consistent with the fact that osteogenesis is associated with vascular construction.

This study is the first to describe the existence of calvarial thickening in a significant proportion of patients with CPA. Our findings indicate that calvarial thickening associated with CPA occurs bilaterally, primarily in the inner table of membranous ossification, and is in contact with the CSF. This finding supports the hypothesis that elevated levels of VEGF in the CSF affect the osteogenic dura, enhancing angiogenesis and osteogenesis in the calvarium24. In fact, in one case in our cohort, bone remodeling was observed to have occurred during the clinical course (Fig. 2). Further, the original inner table was recognized in many cases, suggesting that this bone morphology results from remodeling due to CPA. Morphologically, many of our cases showed centripetal proliferation accompanied by trabecular formation, resulting in a layered structure parallel to the inner table. Thus, calvarial centripetal proliferation with trabecular formation was consistent with the VEGF-induced morphological changes in the calvarium. However, calvarial thickening has not been reported in other intracranial vascular lesions that also exhibit elevated tissue VEGF levels, such as AVMs and DAVFs29,30. CPA has further been shown to cause more pronounced tissue ischemia than AVMs, suggesting potential differences in VEGF levels31. Our study further suggests that calvarial thickening is more common in patients with CPA, particularly in the presence of venous congestion, which has been associated with decreased CBF in cerebral hemodynamic assessments of DAVF35, indicating the potential for tissue hypoperfusion. Similarly, CPA with venous congestion may lead to profound tissue hypoperfusion, increasing VEGF levels. Impaired venous return results in cranial fusion in premature calvarial fusion, whereas venous congestion may cause calvarial thickening in shunt disease. Furthermore, we found no significant association between the transdural supply and calvarial thickening in this study (5/7 (71.4%); without calvarial thickening: 4/9 (44.4%); p = 0.38). As VEGF may be involved in both transdural supply and calvarial thickening and the ratio was higher in the calvarial thickening group in this study, a larger study may reveal a significant relationship.

Interestingly, significant skull thickness has also been reported in patients with Moyamoya disease. In one analysis of 50 patients with Moyamoya disease, Nagaishi et al. reported significant frontal and occipital bone thickness (10 and 11 mm vs. 6.5, 7.7 mm, respectively, p < 0.05)36. Although there was no mention of prominent osteogenesis in the inner table, a similar mechanism may be involved. AVMs and DAVFs are typically treated early upon symptom presentation, potentially resulting in shorter periods of tissue exposure to VEGF. This suggests that calvarial centripetal proliferation with trabecular formation induced by CPA and Moyamoya disease may be attributed to prolonged exposure to high levels of VEGF. However, few studies have focused on calvarial thickening associated with cerebrovascular disease, and it is not fully clear whether calvarial thickening is more common to CPA. To verify these mechanisms, prospective studies examining VEGF levels and duration of exposure to shunt disease and moyamoya disease, including CPA, are warranted.

Our observation of calvarial centripetal proliferation with trabecular formation associated with CPA represents a novel finding that may be significant in elucidating the pathogenesis of CPA. However, this study had several limitations. Firstly, this was a retrospective study, which, due to the rarity of the disease, enrolled only a small number of patients without genetic analysis, leading to a potential selection bias and difficulty in interpreting statistical studies. Also, only univariate analysis was performed, so potential confounders could not be excluded. Although our cohort was small, it is potentially generalizable due to the multicenter study design, the wide range of patient ages, and a background consistent with previous reports1,2. On the other hand, because this is a multicenter, long-term, retrospective study, the imaging protocols are not standardized and biases associated with technological advances cannot be eliminated. Our lack of direct assessment of the VEGF levels was another limitation, which inhibited our ability to verify the proposed mechanism. Future prospective studies are needed to validate the mechanism involving VEGF. Further, the clinical impact remains unclear. Additionally, only one case of progressive calvarial changes was observed during the clinical course. Future prospective studies are needed to clarify calvarial changes over time. Despite these limitations, CPA-associated calvarial centripetal proliferation with trabecular formation may be a novel imaging finding for prolonged VEGF exposure. Larger prospective studies are needed to clarify the prevalence and clinical significance of these findings.

In conclusion, this novel study identified calvarial centripetal proliferation with trabecular formation in a notable population of patients with CPA for the first time. Our findings suggest that elevated levels of VEGF may be related not only to vascular proliferation in the CPA, but also to bilateral calvarial centripetal proliferation with trabecular formation. As such, we propose that CPA-associated calvarial centripetal proliferation could potentially serve as an imaging finding for prolonged VEGF exposure. However, this observation requires validation through further investigation in larger collaborative cohort studies to clarify its prevalence and clinical implications.
