Yaws, a neglected tropical disease caused by Treponema pallidum subsp. pertenue, 
primarily affects children in impoverished rural areas. It is spread through 
direct skin contact. The disease progresses through clinical stages. In the 
primary stage, patients develop a, usually painless, papilloma or ulcer, often 
on the lower extremities, which is highly contagious. If untreated, this lesion 
heals spontaneously but may progress to secondary yaws, characterised by 
disseminated skin lesions, scaly papules and painful hyperkeratotic plaques on 
the palms and soles. Tertiary cases occur in 10% of untreated individuals and 
can cause severe disfigurement, but with the wider availability of treatment 
this is rare today. Though yaws can be treated with benzathine penicillin, oral 
azithromycin has become the preferred treatment due to its ease of 
administration. Currently, it is known to be endemic in 16 countries, with the 
majority of cases found in the Western Pacific, followed by West Africa and 
parts of South East Asia. The World Health Organization has renewed eradication 
efforts, targeting global eradication by 2030 through mass drug administration 
(MDA) campaigns and enhanced diagnostics. However, challenges such as emerging 
azithromycin resistance, difficulties in achieving high MDA coverage, and 
potential zoonotic transmission from nonhuman primates threaten progress. 
Accurate diagnosis is critical, especially in distinguishing yaws from similar 
skin conditions and ensuring all endemic communities are identified and offered 
treatment. Strengthening surveillance and enhancing laboratory capacity for 
molecular diagnostics is essential for the success of the eradication campaign. 
With sustained effort and innovation, the global eradication of yaws by 2030 
remains achievable.

Background  16 
Yaws is a neglected tropical disease (NTD), caused by the spirochete bacterium Treponema pallidum 17 
subsp. pertenue (Tpe)  1,2. It is one of eleven diseases classified by the World Health Organization (WHO) 18 
as a skin-NTD.  Tpe is closely related to T. pallidum subsp. pallidum, the pathogen responsible for the 19 
sexually transmitted infection, syphilis, but unlike syphilis, yaws is not spread sexually. Yaws, alongside 20 
bejel (T. pallidum subsp. endemicium) and pinta (T. pallidum subsp. carateum), make up a group of 21 
diseases referred to as the endemic or non-venereal treponematoses  3,4. It is most common in children 22 
aged under 15 years of age and is spread through direct skin-to-skin contact, facilitated by 23 
microabrasions in the skin4. Yaws is a disease of poverty, predominantly affecting people living in rural 24 
areas, with little access to healthcare services5. Currently it is known to be endemic in 16 countries, with 25 
the majority of cases found in the Pacific Islands, followed by West and Central Africa and Asia6. It is one 26 
of only two diseases targeted for eradication by 20307. 27

Clinical features 30 
Yaws is a chronic illness that occurs in multiple stages (table 1). During the initial stage of infection 31 
(primary yaws), a person presents with a papilloma or exudative ulcer (Figure 1a &b). These tend to be 32 
solitary and painless. Primary lesions are most commonly found on the lower extremities as this is the 33 
most common site of infection and regional lymphadenopathy is common. This primary lesion, also 34 
known as a “mother yaw” is teaming with bacteria and is highly contagious2. If left untreated the 35 
primary lesion will spontaneously heal but the individual will go on to develop secondary yaws, which is 36 
also highly infectious. This usually happens within weeks, but can take up two years after the initial 37 
infection. In 9-15% of cases, the primary papule may still be present, meaning the primary and 38 
secondary stages of yaws are not distinct4,8. During the secondary infection the patient may present with 39 
disseminated lesions, similar to the mother yaw, or present with scaly papules. Palmar and plantar, 40 
hyperkeratotic plaques on palm and soles of feet, can also develop of which can cause a painful crab like 41

prevented the development of this disability forming stage of disease and today it is rarely seen. Unlike 7 
syphilis, neurological and cardiological involvement and congenital transmission are not thought to be 8 
major sequalae of yaws4. 9 
Diagnosis  10 
As with many of the NTDs, in recent years a focus on diagnostics is gaining momentum and diagnostics 11 
are now considered vital to aid with the targets for many NTDs programmes. At the patient level, access 12 
to sensitive and specific diagnostic tests are important to receiving the correct diagnosis and 13 
appropriate treatment. At a programmatic level they are key in generating data to guide interventions. 14 
For yaws eradication to be successful endemic communities must be rapidly identified to allow them to 15 
receive azithromycin, it is also vital non-endemic communities are not falsely identified and treated, 16 
wasting money and resources.  17 
Clinical diagnosis of yaws is challenging as yaws like lesions can be easily confused with other conditions 18 
such as lesions caused by Haemophilus ducreyi, a bacterium found in 30-40 % of suspected yaws 19 
lesions10–13. Lesions caused by other co-endemic pathogens such as Corynebacterium diphtheriae or 20 
Fusobacterium spp. as well as idiopathic cutaneous ulcers are also often mistaken for yaws. Therefore, 21 
diagnostic tests are needed to avoid misdiagnosis based on clinical manifestations alone.  22 
Serological tests have long been the mainstay of diagnosis for yaws. A major challenge is these tests 23 
cannot distinguish between yaws and syphilis14. However demographic data as well as geographical 24 
location can sometimes help identify which treponemal disease is responsible for a positive test. Two 25 
types of serological markers are used: treponemal and non-treponemal antibodies. Treponemal tests 26 
detect antibodies to Treponemal cell wall components and are highly specific. They persist throughout 27 
life once exposed so detection of these only indicates exposure at some point in time15. Non-treponemal 28 
tests detect antibodies produced damage to cell walls caused by treponemes, but similar antibodies can 29 
be detected at a low level in other acute infections. High levels of ‘non-treponemal’ antibodies are seen 30 
in current infection15,16. Point of care (POC) lateral flow tests are available for both antibodies and allow 31 
for detection of syphilis and yaws in a field setting. However, these tests lack both sensitivity and 32 
specificity and therefore are of limited use in eradication campaigns, especially when cases of yaws 33 
become rarer. WHO recommends that there should also be access to molecular tests to provide 34 
sensitive and specific diagnosis of yaws.  35 
There are ongoing evaluations of new diagnostic tools which may be useful at providing a sensitive and 36 
specific diagnostic of yaws, close to the patient, but currently only PCR is recommended. There are also 37 
ongoing efforts to strengthen laboratory capacity for yaws diagnostics in endemic countries such as the 38 
establishment of the WHO supported SKIN-NTD LABNET, a collaborating laboratory network established 39 
for the diagnosis of yaws, Buruli ulcer, leprosy, cutaneous leishmaniasis and mycetoma. 40

in yaws are highly distinctive and rarely mistaken for lesions caused by bacteria, fungi, or parasites. 4

Treatment and eradication  7 
Yaws can be easily treated using single dose benzathine penicillin, which is delivered intramusularly23,24. 8 
The discovery of penicillin’s effectiveness at treating the disease was the key driver behind WHO’s 9 
original yaws eradication campaign in the 1950-60’s. Prior to this, yaws was widespread, was found in 96 10 
countries and thought to affect over 50 million people. This campaign was so successful that worldwide 11 
prevalence was reduced by over 95%25,26. However, a lack of political will and early discontinuation of 12 
the campaigns led to a resurgence of the disease. Today, only India has reported and been certified as 13 
having interrupted transmission27,28.  14 
In 2012, it was demonstrated that that 30mg/kg oral azithromycin was as effective as penicillin at 15 
treating yaws and, due to its simplicity of administration, azithromycin was recommended as the first-16 
line treatment29. This discovery also led to renewed eradication efforts, known as the Morges strategy30. 17 
The use of an orally administered drug avoids some of the logistical and operational challenges of an 18 
injectable drug, facilitating large scale administration in rural settings. A donation by EMS 19 
pharmaceuticals of over 150 million azithromycin tablets specifically for yaws has also provided impetus 20 
to the eradication campaign7. 21 
The Morges strategy centres on treating all endemic communities with azithromycin, known as total 22 
community treatment (TCT) and then revisiting these communities and treating any residual cases and 23 
their close contacts, known as total targeted treatment (TTT)31. Improvements in sanitation as well as 24 
enhancing access to health care and community engagement are also considered to be important 25 
measures in supporting eradication. In 2022 the results of a trial investigating an improved eradication 26 
strategy were published. This trial evaluated the use of three rounds of TCT at 6 monthly intervals 27 
compared with the approach set out in the Morgues strategy (one round of TCT followed by two rounds 28 
of TCT). The new approach was found to be superior, suggesting that in most settings it will be 29 
preferable to conduct more than one round of TCT32. 30 
Although oral azithromycin is highly effective at treating yaws and avoids many of the difficulties of 31 
using an injectable drug such as benzathine penicillin, there have been a small number of cases of 32 
antibiotic resistance reported that have occurred following mass treatment. It is vital that resistance 33 
cases are promptly detected and people are offered an alternative treatment to prevent the spread of 34 
these strains19,20. 35 
In the recently publish NTD road map just yaws and Guinea worm have been targeted for eradication7. 36 
For yaws this is hoped to be accomplished, primarily through the use of mass drug administration 37 
campaigns followed by stringent post-MDA surveillance. As well as focusing efforts on known endemic 38 
countries there are many countries that were either previously endemic, or non-endemic that require 39 
certification.  WHO supports countries in achieving yaws eradication by providing a structured process 40

Since the inaugural Morges strategy meeting in 2012 considerable progress has been made towards the 3 
eradication of yaws. MDA campaigns are underway in Cameroon, Central African Republic, Congo, 4 
Papua New Guinea, and Vanuatu. Studies have looked at integration of yaws with other NTDs for 5 
example a pharmacokinetic and safety study of co-administration of albendazole, diethylcarbamazine, 6 
Ivermectin and azithromycin for the integrated treatment of yaws and lymphatic filariasis in Papua New 7 
Guinea. This study showed there to be no significant adverse events associated with combining the 8 
drugs regimes and highlights how control programmes for different diseases can be integrated to save 9 
resources. Integration can also take place during surveillance for the disease and would allow for 10 
mapping of multiple diseases, again using shared resources. There are also ongoing investigations into 11 
alternative treatments for yaws, such as linezolid, which has been shown to be potentially effective at 12 
treating both syphilis and yaws.  13 
Aside from funding, there are a number of obstacles standing in the way of the eradication target for 14 
yaws. As mentioned above, cases of disease relapse have been seen in the context of emerging 15 
azithromycin resistance34,35. It will be key to educate and inform communities and health care workers 16 
about this possibility to allow for prompt detection of resurgent cases. Highly sensitive diagnostic tools 17 
need to be developed to confirm resistance and alternative treatments will need to be readily available 18 
in post-MDA settings to prevent widespread transmission or potentially resistance Tpe strains. 19 
Although MDA is the primary strategy for the eradication campaign, achieving higher than 90% coverage 20 
is not always straightforward, and without it there are numerous opportunities for disease resurgence. 21 
A high coverage is particularly challenging to achieve in hard-to-reach areas or in mobile populations 22 
where, some individuals may not be present during treatment campaigns and thus may serve as sources 23 
of reinfection. The Baka people, who are native to the Congo Basin and are known for their frequent 24 
movements through the forest, provide a specific example. Furthermore, difficult -to-reach locations 25 
may restrict the scope of treatment or case identification. 26 
Many nations still report diagnosis data based on clinical examination alone or with the use of 27 
serological POC tests.  These tests lack sensitivity and could therefore miss cases leading to entire 28 
communities missing MDA campaigns. Conversely, relying solely on a clinical diagnosis could lead to 29 
non-endemic communities being incorrectly labelled as endemic, and so would receive unnecessary 30 
interventions.  A 2022 survey of yaws endemic countries highlighted that only four of fifteen endemic 31 
countries had access to molecular testing for yaws and only one of these had an established assay for 32 
detecting azithromycin resistance36. It is necessary to improve surveillance systems for case detection 33 
and follow-up in all endemic countries and this should be achieved using more sensitive and specific 34 
molecular diagnostic tools.  35 
Although yaws is primarily considered a human disease, nonhuman primates have been identified as a 36 
reservoir for the causative bacterium, Tpe, even in countries no longer endemic for yaws in humans37. 37 
While there is currently no evidence of transmission from nonhuman primates to humans, the ongoing 38 
encroachment of human populations into the habitats of these animals raises the potential for zoonotic 39 
transmission, posing a continued threat of disease resurgence. 40

Conclusion  2 
The global eradication of yaws by 2030 is an ambitious target which at the time of writing is just five 3 
years away. Achieving this target will require a coordinated effort across multiple fronts. The success of 4 
this initiative hinges on overcoming several key challenges, including the emergence of antibiotic-5 
resistant strains, and the difficulties in achieving high coverage during mass drug administration (MDA) 6 
campaigns and the potential for zoonotic transmission from nonhuman primates. Additionally, accurate 7 
diagnosis is crucial to ensure that endemic communities are properly identified and treated, while non-8 
endemic communities are not subjected to unnecessary interventions. Strengthening surveillance 9 
systems and enhancing diagnostic capabilities with sensitive molecular tools will be essential to meeting 10 
these goals. Through sustained commitment, innovation in diagnostic and treatment strategies, and 11 
comprehensive community engagement, the eradication of yaws can be realised.  12
