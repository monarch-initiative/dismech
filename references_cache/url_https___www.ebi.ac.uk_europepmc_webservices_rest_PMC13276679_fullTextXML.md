id="kwd-group1" sec-type="kwd-group" disp-level="2"><p><bold>Keywords:</bold> epilepsy, Lennox–Gastaut syndrome, TELO2, You‐Hoover‐Fong syndrome</p></sec></notes></front><body><p>
<italic>TELO2</italic> (OMIM 611140) encodes the telomere maintenance 2 (TEL2) protein. Despite the name, human TEL2 is not involved in telomere maintenance, but instead has a role in modulation of the MTOR pathway.<xref rid="epd270179-bib-0001" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>1</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="epd270179-bib-0002" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>2</sup>
</xref> Individuals with biallelic pathogenic variants in <italic>TELO2</italic> have TELO2‐related disorder, also known as You‐Hoover‐Fong syndrome, with clinical features including intellectual disability, microcephaly, dysmorphic facial features, cortical blindness, strabismus, cataracts, nystagmus, movement disorders, and hypertonia.<xref rid="epd270179-bib-0003" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>3</sup>
</xref> Of the 26 individuals thus far described, 4 have had seizures reported, with only very limited clinical detail available.<xref rid="epd270179-bib-0003" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>3</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="epd270179-bib-0004" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>4</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="epd270179-bib-0005" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>5</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="epd270179-bib-0006" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>6</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="epd270179-bib-0007" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>7</sup>
</xref> Age of onset has ranged from “early childhood” to 15 years, with reported seizure types including febrile convulsions, absence, focal impaired consciousness, and generalized tonic–clonic (details from each report in Appendix <xref rid="epd270179-supitem-0002" ref-type="supplementary-material">S1</xref>). Here, we describe a female with TELO2‐related disorder and an epilepsy presentation consistent with Lennox–Gastaut syndrome.</p><p>A 9‐year‐old girl had significant developmental impairment, intellectual disability, microcephaly, hypothyroidism, and failure to thrive. She first presented with tonic seizures at age 1.5 years, in the context of a febrile illness; from that point on, she had daily events. She has never clearly had any other seizure types. At present, she has 2–3 tonic seizures per day, despite receiving rufinamide (42 mg/kg/day), levetiracetam (53 mg/kg/day), valproic acid (33 mg/kg/day), and clobazam (0.8 mg/kg/day). Duration of the tonic seizures can be up to 1 min. Her parents did not think either levetiracetam or valproic acid had meaningful benefit, but both rufinamide and clobazam elicited some partial improvement in seizure control. She experienced status epilepticus on at least two occasions, having clusters of frequent tonic seizures without return to baseline between events and was treated with intravenous phenobarbital and midazolam.</p><p>From a developmental perspective, she walked before 1.5 years and could say 2 words before 1.5 years; however, she had global developmental regression at the time of seizure onset. At present, she can crawl but cannot walk independently and is unsteady if brought to a standing position. She is not toilet‐trained and is dependent on caregivers for all self‐care. She can speak about 10 words and can follow simple one‐step commands. Her parents are both from Afghanistan; there is no known consanguinity. There is no known family history of epilepsy or other neurologic disease. On examination, she has microcephaly (head circumference 46.9 cm at age 7 years) but no clear dysmorphic features. Her neurological examination is abnormal, including generalized spasticity, axial hypotonia, hyperreflexia, clonus in the lower limbs, and bilateral upgoing plantar responses.</p><p>Her interictal EEG in wakefulness at ages 7 and 8 years showed diffusely slow and disorganized background activity composed of primarily medium amplitude 3–5 Hz theta rhythms; in sleep, no clear sleep spindles or vertex waves were seen. There are frequent‐to‐abundant multifocal and bilateral spike–wave discharges, often occurring in runs at 2–2.5 Hz (Figure <xref rid="epd270179-fig-0001" ref-type="fig">1A</xref>). With tonic seizures, there is a diffuse slow wave followed by low amplitude beta fast activity (Figure <xref rid="epd270179-fig-0001" ref-type="fig">1B</xref>). Brain MRI at age 8 years showed mild diffuse atrophy (Figure <xref rid="epd270179-supitem-0002" ref-type="supplementary-material">S1</xref>).</p><fig id="epd270179-fig-0001" position="float"><?disp-level 1?><label>FIGURE 1</label><caption><p>EEG findings: at age 7 years, the interictal EEG (A) shows diffuse slowing and abundant bilateral spike–wave discharges occurring in runs at ~2 Hz. During a tonic seizure (B), the ictal rhythm involves a high amplitude bilateral slow wave (arrow), followed by diffuse low amplitude beta fast activity.</p></caption><alternatives><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" content-type="image" id="jats-graphic-1" xlink:href="EPD2-28-893-g001.jpg"><?cloudpmc-path blobs/df5d/13276679/85238e1f55ff/EPD2-28-893-g001.jpg?><?cloudpmc-bucket cdn?><?image-server-status NEED_LOADING?><?original-height 1145?><?original-width 1064?><?scaled-height 763?><?scaled-width 709?></graphic><graphic xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" content-type="thumb" xlink:href="EPD2-28-893-g001.gif"><?cloudpmc-path blobs/df5d/13276679/4bbc1ed15864/EPD2-28-893-g001.gif?><?cloudpmc-bucket cdn?></graphic></alternatives></fig><p>A clinical gene panel (Autism/ID Xpanded Panel, GeneDx) identified a homozygous missense variant in <italic>TELO2</italic> (<ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/NM_016111.3" ext-link-type="uri">NM_016111.3</ext-link>; c.2312 T&gt;C, p.(Leu771Ser)); both parents were subsequently found to be heterozygous carriers. The variant is extremely rare in population databases (gnomAD allele frequency ≈7 × 10<sup>−5</sup>; no homozygotes) (American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criterion PM2).<xref rid="epd270179-bib-0008" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>8</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="epd270179-bib-0009" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>9</sup>
</xref> This variant has been observed with a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant on the opposite allele (in trans) in 3/26 previously published patients with TELO2‐related disorder.<xref rid="epd270179-bib-0004" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>4</sup>
</xref>, <xref rid="epd270179-bib-0005" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>5</sup>
</xref> The variant affects a highly conserved leucine residue and is predicted to have a deleterious effect on protein structure and function by multiple in silico tools (ACMG criterion PP3).<xref rid="epd270179-bib-0008" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>8</sup>
</xref> The patient's clinical phenotype, including global developmental impairment, intellectual disability, microcephaly, failure to thrive, severe epilepsy, and abnormal neurological findings, matches the spectrum of TELO2‐related syndrome described in the literature (ACMG criterion PP4).<xref rid="epd270179-bib-0008" ref-type="bibr">
<sup>8</sup>
</xref>
</p><p>In summary, this patient's presentation illustrates that biallelic <italic>TELO2</italic> pathogenic variants can cause a severe infantile‐onset developmental and epileptic encephalopathy consistent with Lennox–Gastaut syndrome. Further study is needed to clarify the full epilepsy spectrum in this genetic condition and determine whether certain treatments are more or less likely to be effective.</p><sec id="epd270179-sec-0006" disp-level="1"><title>AUTHOR CONTRIBUTIONS</title><p>Melissa Odabassian was involved in data collection and wrote the initial manuscript draft.
