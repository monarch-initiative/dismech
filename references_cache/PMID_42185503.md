# A decade of clinical course and prognostic determinants in type 1 autoimmune pancreatitis: insights from a 10-year longitudinal multicenter retrospective study.
**Authors:** Miyazawa M, Takatori H, Nishitani M, Komura T, Kaji K, Yano M, Kitamura K, Nakai R, Matsukawa H, Takemura N, Yanagi M, Nagai K, Kido H, Takata N, Hayashi T, Seki A, Nio K, Yamada S, Iida N, Shimakami T, Yamashita T
**Journal:** J Gastroenterol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00535-026-02448-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00535-026-02448-8)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Type 1 autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP) is a unique form of pancreatitis characterized by IgG4-related inflammation and corticosteroid (CS) responsiveness, but long-term outcomes remain unclear. We evaluated 10-year relapse, malignancy, metabolic/nutritional changes, and survival.

METHODS: We retrospectively analyzed 83 CS-treated patients with type 1 AIP followed for ≥ 10 years or until death, assessing: relapse and risk factors; standardized incidence ratios (SIRs) and risk factors for malignancies; 10-year changes in HbA1c and serum albumin; CS-related adverse events; and overall survival (OS).

RESULTS: Cumulative relapse rates at 1, 3, 5, 7, and 10 years were 11.0%, 26.9%, 38.3%, 50.0%, and 56.9%. CS discontinuation independently predicted relapse, whereas serum IgG4 normal at diagnosis or normalized after CS was protective. Overall malignancy risk was not increased (SIR 1.00; 95%CI 0.59-1.42), while pancreatic cancer (PC) showed a numerically higher SIR (1.98 overall; 2.96 ≥ 5 years postdiagnosis). In 72 patients with 10-year laboratory follow-up, HbA1c improved at 3 years but tended to worsen at 10 years, and serum albumin declined by 10 years (mean - 0.27 g/dL), exceeding age-related norms. Preexisting diabetes attenuated albumin decline. Ten-year survival was 85.5%, and CS maintenance was associated with better OS.

CONCLUSIONS: Type 1 AIP shows progressive relapse and metabolic deterioration over a decade, while CS maintenance and IgG4 normalization confer long-term protection. Although the overall risk of malignancy was not increased, the incidence of PC during late follow-up was slightly higher. These findings highlight the need for continuous monitoring for relapse, PC, and nutritional decline in AIP management.

enlargement, irregular narrowing of the main pancreatic 
duct (MPD), and a marked response to corticosteroid 
(CS) therapy. In 2001, Hamano et al. demonstrated that 
elevated serum IgG4 levels are a useful diagnostic bio-
marker for AIP [2 ], supporting its recognition as a pan-
creatic manifestation of IgG4-related disease (IgG4-RD). 
The International Consensus Diagnostic Criteria (ICDC), 
proposed in 2011, classified AIP into two subtypes—type 
1 (IgG4-related) and type 2 (idiopathic duct-centric) 
AIP—based on clinical, histopathological, and imaging 
features [3 ]. Type 1 AIP, the predominant form in Asian 
populations, represents the pancreatic lesion of systemic 
IgG4-RD [3 , 4].
Although short-term outcomes are generally favorable 
with CS therapy [4 , 5], the long-term prognosis of AIP 
remains incompletely defined. Relapse [4 , 6, 7], malig-
nancy [8 , 9], pancreatic endocrine or exocrine dysfunc-
tion [10, 11], and CS-related adverse events [12, 13] may 
influence long-term outcomes. However, prior cohort stud-
ies have typically reported median follow-up of approxi-
mately 5 years [4 , 13], and systematic evaluations beyond 
10 years are lacking.
The aim of this study was to comprehensively ana-
lyze relapse rates, incidence of malignancies, pancre-
atic functional changes, CS-related adverse events, and 
overall survival in patients with type 1 AIP followed for 
more than 10 years, to clarify key challenges in long-term 
management.
Methods
Study design and ethical considerations
This multicenter retrospective cohort study was conducted 
at Kanazawa University Hospital and seven affiliated insti-
tutions. The study was performed in accordance with the 
Declaration of Helsinki (revised 2024) and was approved 
by the Institutional Review Board of Kanazawa University 
Hospital (approval number: 2016-191). Because the study 
used previously collected clinical data without new patient 
enrollment, the Institutional Review Board approved a 
waiver of written informed consent under an opt-out pol-
icy. The study information was publicly disclosed on the 
websites of participating institutions, and patients were 
given the opportunity to decline participation.
Study population
Between January 1999 and February 2015, 159 patients 
diagnosed with type 1 AIP (definite or probable according 
to ICDC) [3] were identified. Of these, the following were 
excluded:
1. 40 patients who survived but were followed for less than 
10 years,
2. 25 patients who did not receive CS therapy within 
6 months after diagnosis, and
3. 11 patients with visit intervals of ≥ 6 months and insuf-
ficient data for analysis.
Finally, 83 patients (71 men; mean age, 66.3 years) were 
included in the analysis.
Patients were considered eligible for long-term follow-
up if they had clinic visits at least every six months and 
were evaluated for at least 10 years or until death by clinical 
examinations and at least one imaging modality, including 
ultrasonography (US), endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS), 
computed tomography (CT), or magnetic resonance imag -
ing (MRI). Missing data were not imputed; only analyzable 
cases were included. A flow diagram of patient selection is 
shown in Supplementary File 1.
Data collection
The following clinical data were retrospectively extracted 
from medical records:
• baseline characteristics (age, sex, comorbidities, medical 
history, physical findings),
• laboratory data (bilirubin, albumin, glycated hemoglobin 
[HbA1c], IgG, IgG4),
• imaging findings (US, EUS, CT, MRI, endoscopic retro-
grade cholangiopancreatography [ERCP]),
• pathological findings (EUS-guided fine-needle aspiration 
[EUS-FNA] or percutaneous biopsy),
• treatment course (biliary drainage, initial prednisolone 
(PSL) dose, CS maintenance therapy, relapse events),
• complications (diabetes mellitus [DM], malignancies, 
cardiovascular events, fragility fractures), and
• survival outcomes (last follow-up, date of death, cause 
of death).
Treatment strategy
CS therapy was initiated in patients with obstructive jaun-
dice, abdominal pain, or clinically significant other organ 
involvement (OOI). Obstructive jaundice was defined as a 
total bilirubin level > 1.2 mg/dL with radiologic evidence of 
bile duct dilation. Owing to the retrospective design, indi-
cations for biliary drainage were not standardized. In some 
cases, ERCP was not feasible and CS therapy was initiated 
without drainage; in others, prophylactic drainage was per -
formed even in mild cholestasis to prevent postbiopsy biliary

obstruction. CS therapy was administered in accordance 
with the Japanese consensus guidelines for AIP [14]. Pred-
nisolone (PSL) was started at 0.6 mg/kg/day for 2–4 weeks 
and then tapered by 5 mg every 2–4 weeks based on clinical 
and radiological response to a maintenance dose of approxi-
mately 5–10 mg/day. The decision to continue maintenance 
therapy and its duration was left to the attending physician. 
After CS initiation, patients were followed every 3–6 months 
with laboratory testing and abdominal imaging (US, EUS, 
CT, or MRI), with additional imaging performed when new 
symptoms developed or serum IgG4 levels reelevated to 
assess for relapse as needed. Relapse was treated by restart-
ing or increasing PSL.
Definitions
In accordance with the ICDC, serum IgG4 ≥ 135 mg/dL was 
considered elevated [3]. OOI comprised the following IgG4-
related lesions defined by the ICDC [3 ]: IgG4-related scle-
rosing cholangitis, retroperitoneal fibrosis, dacryoadenitis 
or sialadenitis, and IgG4-related kidney disease [15]. His-
topathologic confirmation was based on surgical resection 
or core biopsy specimens, and EUS-FNA samples were also 
accepted when applicable. Remission was defined as clinical 
improvement with resolution of pancreatic and/or extrapan-
creatic lesions within 3 months after CS initiation [16]. CS 
maintenance therapy was defined as continuous administra-
tion for > 6 months. Relapse was defined as recurrence or 
worsening of symptoms or imaging abnormalities, regard-
less of changes in serum IgG4 [16]. During follow-up, serum 
IgG4 was measured at least monthly until remission and 
thereafter every 3–6 months at each participating institution. 
Serum IgG4 normalization was defined as either a normal 
IgG4 level at diagnosis or normalization within 6 months 
after CS initiation, irrespective of subsequent reelevation. 
DM was diagnosed according to American Diabetes Asso-
ciation criteria [17]; DM diagnosed or treated ≥ 6 months 
before AIP diagnosis was classified as preexisting, and all 
others were classified as new onset. HbA1c was reported in 
National Glycohemoglobin Standardization Program units, 
with Japan Diabetes Society values converted by adding 
0.4% [18]. MPD dilatation was defined as an MPD diam-
eter ≥ 3 mm on imaging. CS-related adverse events included 
fragility fractures (non-traumatic fractures after minimal 
external force) and macrovascular events (coronary, cerebro-
vascular, or peripheral arterial disease). Serum albumin was 
used as a surrogate marker of pancreatic exocrine function 
and was compared with age-related physiological decline 
reported in Japanese population studies.
Evaluation items
The following endpoints were evaluated:
• relapse rate of AIP and risk factors,
• standardized incidence ratio (SIR) and risk factors for 
malignancies,
• changes in HbA1c and serum albumin levels over 
10 years,
• CS-related adverse events and associated factors, and
• overall survival (OS) and causes of death.
Statistical analysis
Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square 
test, and continuous variables were summarized as mean 
(standard deviation [SD]) or median (interquartile range 
[IQR]) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U  test. 
Survival and relapse-free survival were estimated by the 
Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank 
test. A landmark analysis was performed for patients who 
remained relapse-free for ≥ 3 years after CS initiation; the 
landmark time zero was set at 36 months, and predictors of 
relapse beyond 3 years were evaluated during the subsequent 
postlandmark period.
Cox proportional hazards regression was used to identify 
independent predictors of relapse, overall survival (OS), and 
malignancy development. For OS and malignancy analyses, 
time-to-event was calculated from the date of AIP diagnosis 
to death, malignancy diagnosis, or last follow-up (censor -
ing). For relapse analyses, time-to-event was calculated from 
the date of CS initiation to the first documented relapse or 
last follow-up; in the landmark analysis, follow-up started 
at 36 months. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariate analyses 
were entered into multivariate Cox models using the forced-
entry method, with age and sex included as adjustment vari-
ables irrespective of univariate significance. Missing data 
were handled by pairwise deletion. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 
95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.
For CS-related adverse events (fragility fractures and 
macrovascular events), variables with p < 0.10 in univariate 
analyses were entered into multivariate logistic regression 
models, with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs calculated.
Standardized incidence ratios (SIRs) were calculated as 
observed-to-expected malignancies, with expected numbers 
derived from Japanese population cancer incidence rates and 
cohort person-years stratified by calendar period, sex, and 
5-year age groups [19]. Multiple malignancies in a single 
patient were counted as separate events; statistical signifi-
cance was defined by a 95%CI excluding 1.0.
Normality of HbA1c and serum albumin distributions 
was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As both were 
non-normally distributed, changes at baseline, 3 years, and 
10 years were evaluated using the Friedman test, with post 
hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and Bonferroni correction 
(three pairwise comparisons). ΔHbA1c and ΔAlb from 3 
to 10 years were calculated; univariate analyses used the

Results
Clinical characteristics of the patients
A total of 83 patients with type 1 AIP were enrolled (mean 
age at diagnosis, 66.3 ± 10.5 years), with a marked male pre-
dominance (85.5%) (Table  1). Diffuse pancreatic enlarge-
ment was observed in 67.5%. OOI was present in 54.2%, 
including sialadenitis/dacryoadenitis (37.3%), kidney dis-
ease (25.3%), retroperitoneal fibrosis (8.4%), and proximal 
bile duct stricture (13.3%). Biliary drainage before CS initia-
tion was performed in 25.3%.
During follow-up, DM was present in 66.3% of patients 
(24.1% preexisting and 39.8% new onset). The median initial 
PSL dose was 30 mg/day (IQR 30–37.5). CS maintenance 
therapy was administered in 95.1%, and 43.9% discontin-
ued CS during follow-up. The distribution of CS therapy 
duration and individual follow-up patterns are shown in 
Supplementary File 2. Overall, most patients received pro-
longed CS therapy; 78.8% of patients followed for ≥ 3 years 
received CS therapy for ≥ 3 years, and 90.0% had cumulative 
CS administration exceeding 3 years, including retreatment 
after discontinuation.
Serum IgG4 was normal at diagnosis or normalized after 
CS therapy in 51.3% of evaluable patients. Relapse occurred 
in 57.3% (pancreatic 37.8%; OOI 25.6%). Malignancies 
developed in 25.3%, including pancreatic cancer (PC) in 
two patients (2.4%). During observation, 21.7% developed 
MPD dilatation and 8.4% developed pancreatic stones. CS-
related adverse events included fragility fractures in 14.5% 
and macrovascular events in 19.3%. The median follow-up 
duration was 138 months (IQR 124–177).
Relapse rate of AIP and risk factors
During long-term follow-up, cumulative relapse rates at 1, 
3, 5, 7, and 10 years were 11.0%, 26.9%, 38.3%, 50.0%, and 
56.9%, respectively (Fig.  1). The median relapse-free sur -
vival was 6.69 years (95%CI 3.68–9.70), and Kaplan–Meier 
analysis showed a steady increase in relapse incidence, 
affecting more than half of patients by 10 years.
In univariate analyses, retroperitoneal fibrosis (p = 0.01), 
CS discontinuation ( p = 0.02), and serum IgG4 normal at 
diagnosis or normalized after CS therapy (p  < 0.01) were 
significantly associated with relapse (Table 2). In multivari-
ate Cox regression, CS discontinuation was an independent 
risk factor (HR = 2.68; 95%CI 1.29–5.59; p = 0.01), whereas 
serum IgG4 normal at diagnosis or normalized after CS ther-
apy was protective (HR = 0.38; 95%CI 0.20–0.74; p < 0.01).
To evaluate whether serum IgG4 status modifies relapse 
risk after CS discontinuation, relapse-free survival was 
further stratified into four groups according to IgG4 status 
(normal at diagnosis or normalized after CS therapy [N/N] 
vs persistently elevated) and CS continuation/discontinua-
tion (Supplementary File 3). Pairwise log-rank tests demon-
strated that relapse-free survival was significantly longer in 
the IgG4 N/N + CS continuation group than in the N/N + CS 
discontinuation group ( p < 0.01), the IgG4 elevated + CS 
continuation group (p  < 0.01), and the IgG4 elevated + CS 
discontinuation group (p  < 0.01). In addition, relapse-free 
survival was significantly longer in the IgG4 N/N + CS dis-
continuation group than in the IgG4 elevated + CS discontin-
uation group (p = 0.02), whereas other pairwise comparisons 
were not significant.
In a landmark subanalysis of 48 patients who remained 
relapse-free for ≥ 3 years on continuous CS therapy, relapse 
beyond 3 years occurred in 19 patients (39.6%) over a 
median postlandmark follow-up of 103 months (time zero 
set at 36 months after CS initiation). On univariate analy -
sis, younger age at diagnosis (p  = 0.04), higher serum IgG 
(p = 0.03), higher serum IgG4 (p  = 0.01), retroperitoneal 
fibrosis (p < 0.01), and CS discontinuation (p  = 0.04) were 
associated with relapse (Table 3). In multivariate Cox regres-
sion, younger age (HR = 0.89; 95%CI 0.83–0.96; p < 0.01), 
higher serum IgG (HR = 1.00; 95%CI 1.00–1.00; p = 0.03), 
and CS discontinuation (HR = 6.52; 95%CI 1.90–22.33; 
p < 0.01) remained independent predictors.
Furthermore, to evaluate the impact of the timing of CS 
discontinuation, a Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed 
using landmark analysis at 3 years after the start of CS initia-
tion (Supplementary File 4). Among patients who remained 
relapse-free at 3 years (n = 60), relapse-free survival tended 
to be shorter in those who had already discontinued CS by 
the 3-year landmark ( n = 12; median 92.4 months, 95%CI 
29.3–155.5) than in those still receiving CS at the landmark 
(n = 48; median not reached), although this difference was 
not statistically significant (log-rank, p = 0.21). We then ana-
lyzed the 48 patients who were relapse-free and receiving 
CS at the 3-year landmark. During subsequent follow-up, 12 
patients discontinued CS and 36 continued CS throughout 
follow-up. Relapse-free survival was significantly longer in 
patients who continued CS compared with those who dis-
continued CS after the landmark (median not reached vs 
80.3 months, 95%CI 44.7–115.9; log-rank, p = 0.03).

with malignancy development (Table  5). In univariate 
analysis, higher serum IgG (p  = 0.02) and retroperitoneal 
fibrosis (p  = 0.03) were associated with malignancy. In 
multivariate Cox regression, higher serum IgG (HR = 1.00;

We also evaluated whether IgG levels after CS initia-
tion contribute to malignancy risk. Of the 69 patients for 
whom data were available, only 3 had IgG levels exceeding 
1700 mg/dL within 6 months of CS initiation, indicating 
that IgG levels returned to near-normal levels early after 
CS initiation. Furthermore, there was no significant differ -
ence in IgG levels after CS initiation between patients who 
developed malignancy and those who did not.
Changes in HbA1c and serum albumin levels 
over 10 years

data for HbA1c and serum albumin, values at baseline (AIP 
diagnosis), 3 years, and 10 years were compared (Fig.  2).

significantly improved by 3 years (p  < 0.01) but tended to 
worsen by 10 years ( p = 0.02). Serum albumin showed a 
modest increase up to 3 years (p  = 0.04) followed by a sig-
nificant decline by 10 years (p  < 0.01). In Japanese popula-
tion-based cohorts of older adults, physiological albumin 
decline averages approximately 0.10–0.15 g/dL per decade 
[20, 21]. In contrast, our AIP cohort showed a mean albumin 
decrease of 0.27 g/dL over 10 years, exceeding that expected 
from normal aging.

CS‑related adverse events and associated factors
During long-term follow-up, fragility fractures were 
observed in 14.5% and macrovascular events in 19.3% 
of patients receiving CS therapy. In the univariate analy -
sis, older age at AIP diagnosis (p  < 0.01) and female sex 
(p = 0.04) were significantly associated with fragility frac-
tures. Multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed 
that older age (OR 1.13, 95%CI 1.03–1.24, p  = 0.01) and 
female sex (OR 5.56, 95%CI 1.12–33.33; p  = 0.04) were 
independent risk factors for fractures (Supplementary File 
6). Osteoporosis prophylaxis with bisphosphonates and/or 
calcium/vitamin D was more frequently administered in 
patients with longer CS exposure, including those receiving 
CS therapy for ≥ 3 years (79.4% vs 41.2%, p = 0.005) and 
those with cumulative CS administration exceeding 3 years 
(76.4% vs 25.0%, p = 0.006), but was not significantly asso-
ciated with fracture development, suggesting possible con-
founding by indication. For macrovascular events, coexisting 
DM during follow-up was the only factor showing a sig-
nificant association (univariate p = 0.05), and it remained an 
independent predictor in the multivariate model (OR 4.77, 
95%CI 1.01–23.37, p  = 0.05). Other factors, including CS-
related factors, relapse, or malignancy development, were 
not significantly related to either adverse event.

Discussion
This multicenter retrospective study provides a comprehen-
sive assessment of long-term outcomes in patients with type 
1 AIP who were followed for more than 10 years or until 
death. Our findings demonstrate that, although AIP generally 
responds well to CS therapy and carries a favorable overall 
prognosis, the disease exhibits a chronic relapsing course 
and is associated with long-term metabolic and nutritional 
alterations as well as CS-related adverse events that require 
lifelong clinical vigilance.
Relapse rate and CS management
In this cohort, the cumulative relapse rate reached 56.9% at 
10 years, indicating that relapse remains common beyond 
the 3-year maintenance period recommended in the Japanese 
consensus guidelines [14]. This extends findings from earlier 
cohorts with shorter follow-up [4 , 6, 9, 13] and highlights 
the chronic, relapsing course of AIP. Notably, a large Japa-
nese retrospective study also reported substantial relapse 
after cessation of glucocorticoid maintenance therapy, sup-
porting extended low-dose CS in selected patients [22].
In multivariate analysis, CS discontinuation was the 
strongest independent predictor of relapse, consistent with 
prior reports showing lower relapse rates with prolonged 
maintenance than with early withdrawal [7 , 23]. A system-
atic review and meta-analysis similarly supports mainte-
nance therapy to reduce relapse risk [24]. In this study, CS 
maintenance therapy was operationally defined as continu-
ous CS administration for > 6 months, a definition also used 
in a previous AIP cohort study [7]. Although this definition 
is broader than the conventional 3-year maintenance strat-
egy, it was intended to capture the real-world transition from 
CS initiation to maintenance therapy in this retrospective 
cohort. Landmark analyses conducted at 3 years to clarify 
the clinical significance of long-term treatment provided 
clinically interpretable estimates of relapse risk after sus -
tained remission. Although relapse-free survival did not 
differ significantly between patients who had already dis -
continued CS by 3 years and those still receiving CS at that 
time, subsequent CS discontinuation after the 3-year land-
mark was associated with a significantly higher relapse risk 
than continued maintenance. While these results should be 
interpreted cautiously, because CS discontinuation was not 
randomized and may be influenced by clinical decision-mak-
ing, they support the concept that continued low-dose CS 
maintenance can reduce late relapse risk in selected patients 
and underscore the need for individualized withdrawal strat-
egies. On the other hand, long-term CS exposure carries 
toxicity concerns; therefore, steroid-sparing approaches such 
as B-cell depletion with inebilizumab—recently evaluated 
in IgG4-RD—may be a promising option warranting further 
study in AIP [25].
Serum IgG4 normalization was also independently asso-
ciated with a lower relapse risk, consistent with observations 
that persistent IgG4 elevation predicts recurrence [22]. Our 
four-group Kaplan–Meier analyses of relapse-free survival 
stratified according to IgG4 status (N/N vs persistently ele-
vated) and CS continuation/discontinuation provide clini-
cally interpretable context for CS withdrawal decisions. 
Importantly, the lowest relapse risk was observed in patients 
with IgG4 N/N status who continued CS, whereas CS dis-
continuation was associated with higher relapse risk even 
among patients with IgG4 N/N status. At the same time, 
among patients who discontinued CS, those with IgG4 N/N 
status had a lower relapse risk than those with persistently 
elevated IgG4, suggesting that serologic response may help 
stratify relapse risk after withdrawal. However, the subgroup 
size is limited, and these findings should be interpreted cau-
tiously as hypothesis-generating. Importantly, our data do 
not support routine CS discontinuation solely based on 
IgG4 normalization. Rather, IgG4 normalization should be 
interpreted as one component of relapse risk stratification, 
and continuing low-dose maintenance therapy should be a 
reasonable default strategy unless patient-specific factors 
strongly favor withdrawal.
In conditional survival analysis of patients relapse-free 
for ≥ 3 years on continuous CS therapy, younger age at diag-
nosis predicted late relapse, suggesting sustained immune 
reactivity in younger patients, in line with immunopatho-
genic evidence of lymphoplasmacytic infiltration in AIP 
[26]. Notably, total IgG—rather than IgG4—remained an 
independent predictor of relapse in our landmark analysis 
focused on late relapse beyond 3 years. Although serum 
IgG4 has traditionally been regarded as the key serological 
marker in AIP and IgG4-RD, several previous studies have 
also suggested the clinical relevance of total IgG for relapse

prediction [27– 29]. Our finding is consistent with prior 
evidence and suggests that total IgG may reflect broader 
immune activation not fully captured by IgG4 alone. One 
plausible explanation is that patients who remain relapse-
free for ≥ 3 years represent a clinically “controlled” sub-
group in whom overt IgG4-skewed inflammation has already 
been suppressed by CS therapy. In such patients, persistently 
elevated IgG4 may not directly translate into relapse risk, 
whereas total IgG may better reflect residual polyclonal 
immune activation or chronic organ-specific inflammation. 
Therefore, regular monitoring of both IgG and IgG4 may 
be useful for detecting late relapse risk during long-term 
follow-up. Nevertheless, given the limited sample size and 
conditional nature of the landmark cohort, these findings 
should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger 
cohorts.
Malignancy and SIR analysis
In this study, the SIR for all malignancies was 1.00 (95%CI 
0.59–1.42), indicating no overall excess cancer risk com-
pared with the general population. Furthermore, no asso-
ciation was found between CS-related factors and malig-
nancy development. In contrast, a large-scale Japanese 
multicenter collaborative study that reported the protective 
association of CS maintenance therapy in AIP patients had 
an SIR of 1.21 for all malignancies [9 ]. Although it is bio-
logically plausible that adequate control of chronic inflam-
mation might reduce malignancy risk, our ability to detect 
treatment-related effects was limited by the small number 
of malignancy events and potential confounding inherent 
to retrospective treatment decisions. Therefore, our results 
should be interpreted cautiously and should not be consid-
ered evidence to negate the protective role of CS mainte-
nance therapy. Asian cohorts have reported elevated SIRs 
for gastrointestinal cancers, particularly within the first year 
after diagnosis (e.g., gastric and colorectal cancers) [30]. In 
addition, an association between IgG4-RD and non-Hodgkin 
lymphoma has been suggested [31], indicating that risk may 
differ by cancer type.
Two cases of pancreatic cancer (PC) occurred in our 
cohort, yielding an overall SIR of 1.98, which was numeri-
cally elevated but statistically inconclusive. Both devel-
oped > 5 years after AIP diagnosis, and the late-period SIR 
was higher at 2.96. This partially aligns with a multicenter 
study reporting an increased PC SIR of 3.22 in AIP [9 ], 
although that study also noted excess PC risk within 2 years, 
suggesting heterogeneity in the timing of risk. Given the 
small number of PC events, robust risk estimation requires 
substantially larger cohorts and longer follow-up. Nonethe-
less, the late occurrence of PC in our cohort supports the 
possibility that chronic inflammation and fibrosis contrib-
ute to carcinogenesis in susceptible individuals. These data 
underscore the need for vigilance not only for relapse but 
also for PC during long-term follow-up [30].
Shiokawa et al. reported higher malignancy risk in AIP 
patients with elevated immunoglobulins and retroperitoneal 
fibrosis [30], which is consistent with our findings. In our 
study, high baseline IgG levels emerged as an independ-
ent risk factor for malignancy, suggesting that patients with 
greater systemic inflammatory burden may be more sus-
ceptible; however, this association does not appear to be 
mediated by persistent hypergammaglobulinemia after CS 
initiation. Indeed, IgG levels generally normalized within 
6 months after CS initiation, and post-treatment IgG lev -
els were not significantly different between patients with 
and without malignancy. Larger cohorts with standardized 
immunologic assessments are needed to clarify whether 
malignancy risk is driven by pre-treatment inflammatory 
phenotype, residual inflammation despite CS initiation, or 
unrelated factors.
Metabolic and nutritional changes
This study is among the first to track both HbA1c and serum 
albumin over 10 years in AIP, demonstrating a biphasic met-
abolic course and progressive nutritional decline. Prior long-
term work has largely emphasized relapse and shorter-term 
glycemic outcomes [7]. In our cohort, HbA1c improved up 
to 3 years—likely reflecting resolution of pancreatic inflam-
mation due to CS therapy—but then tended to worsen by 
10 years, consistent with gradual progression of pancreatic 
endocrine insufficiency. Although MPD dilatation showed 
only a non-significant trend toward greater HbA1c worsen-
ing, this pattern is in line with reports linking morphological 
progression (e.g., pancreatic atrophy and ductal abnormali-
ties) to deterioration of diabetes control [32, 33]. However, it 
should be noted that HbA1c values remained < 6.5% in many 
patients, and modest increases over a 10-year period may 
not always translate into clinically meaningful deterioration.
Serum albumin rose slightly early but declined signifi-
cantly by 10 years, suggesting gradual nutritional deteriora-
tion. Because direct exocrine function testing was not feasi-
ble in this long-term cohort, we used albumin as a surrogate 
marker. A recent machine-learning approach suggested that 
exocrine pancreatic insufficiency in chronic pancreatitis and 
AIP can be predicted without direct tests using BMI and 
total protein [34]. Given the tendency for hypergammaglobu-
linemia in AIP, albumin may be a more robust surrogate than 
total protein alone. Nevertheless, albumin is influenced by 
extrapancreatic factors, such as age-related decline, chronic 
comorbidities, and CS-related catabolism; these must be 
considered when interpreting longitudinal trends. Even 
so, the 10-year albumin decline in our cohort exceeded the 
physiological range reported in community-dwelling Japa-
nese older adults, supporting an additional disease-related

component that may reflect progressive pancreatic fibrosis 
and exocrine insufficiency over time. Consistently, 50–80% 
of AIP patients reportedly develop exocrine insufficiency in 
the long term, particularly those with pancreatic atrophy or 
MPD dilatation [35, 36].
Interestingly, preexisting DM was independently associ-
ated with attenuated albumin decline at 10 years. This find-
ing may appear inexplicable, as DM is often linked to poorer 
nutritional status. However, absolute albumin levels were 
comparable between groups at baseline and at 3 years, argu-
ing against a simple “floor effect,” and albumin tended to be 
higher in patients with preexisting DM at 10 years. Moreo-
ver, the association persisted after additional adjustment for 
baseline albumin, suggesting that the observed effect was 
not explained by lower pre-treatment albumin values. A 
plausible explanation is that patients with preexisting DM 
may have received closer metabolic and nutritional monitor-
ing and earlier therapeutic intervention (including insulin 
counteracting CS-induced proteolysis in some cases), which 
could mitigate long-term catabolic effects and help preserve 
nutritional status. Experimental data indicate that insulin 
promotes protein synthesis and suppresses proteolysis via 
eIF5A-dependent mechanisms [37], providing biological 
support for this observation. Overall, albumin decline in 
long-standing AIP likely reflects a multifactorial signal inte-
grating aging, systemic catabolic burden, and progressive 
exocrine dysfunction, underscoring the need for long-term 
monitoring of both glycemic control and nutritional status. 
Nevertheless, given the observational design, this finding 
should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger 
cohorts with standardized nutritional assessments.
CS‑related adverse events
Fragility fractures occurred in 14.5% of CS-treated patients, 
and macrovascular events occurred in 19.3%. Older age 
and female sex were independent predictors of fractures, 
consistent with the well-established susceptibility of these 
groups to glucocorticoid-induced bone loss and with similar 
observations in the large Japanese AIP cohort [9]. Postmeno-
pausal bone loss further increases vulnerability in women, 
underscoring the need for early bone health assessment and 
prophylactic therapy. To contextualize fracture risk, we 
compared our crude incidence with Japanese population-
based data. In our cohort, 12 fragility fractures occurred over 
approximately 1019 person-years, corresponding to 1178 per 
100,000 person-years. Sakuma et al. reported an incidence 
of 322.4 per 100,000 person-years for major osteoporotic 
fractures in an adjusted general population of Japan [38]. 
Thus, fracture incidence appeared higher in our AIP cohort, 
although this comparison is limited by differences in fracture 
definitions, unavailable fracture timing, older age, and long-
term CS exposure.
The occurrence of macrovascular events was associ-
ated with coexisting DM, consistent with the known links 
between dysglycemia and cardiovascular risk. Macrovas-
cular events occurred in 16 patients over approximately 
1019 person-years, corresponding to 1570 per 100,000 
person-years. Epidemiological studies based on the gen-
eral population of Japan have reported a crude incidence 
of approximately 458 macrovascular events per 100,000 
person-years [39]. Therefore, macrovascular event inci-
dence also appeared higher in our AIP cohort. However, 
this should be interpreted cautiously, because event defi-
nitions differed, event timing was unavailable, and our 
cohort included older CS-treated patients with a high 
prevalence of DM.
Although cumulative CS dose is an important deter -
minant of steroid-related toxicity, it could not be reliably 
reconstructed in this study because of heterogeneous taper -
ing schedules, temporary dose escalation, retreatment after 
relapse or discontinuation, incomplete longitudinal dose 
documentation across centers, and uncertainty in older pre-
electronic medical records generated more than 10 years 
ago. Therefore, we used duration-based metrics, including 
CS therapy for ≥ 3 years and cumulative CS administration 
exceeding 3 years, as surrogate indicators of CS exposure. 
In our cohort, these duration-based CS exposure variables 
were not significantly associated with fragility fractures or 
macrovascular events. However, this finding should not be 
interpreted as evidence that cumulative steroid exposure 
is unrelated to toxicity, because previous large-scale AIP 
studies have suggested that higher total steroid dose may be 
associated with serious steroid-related adverse events [40].
Osteoporosis prophylaxis with bisphosphonates and/
or calcium/vitamin D was more common in patients with 
longer CS exposure but was not associated with fewer frac-
tures, likely reflecting confounding by indication. Impor -
tantly, the frequencies of fragility fractures and macrovas-
cular events were not significantly lower among AIP patients 
who discontinued CS. This likely reflects the strong influ-
ence of baseline vulnerability—particularly age, sex, and 
DM—on fracture and cardiovascular risk, as well as the 
potential impact of preventive measures implemented dur -
ing routine follow-up. Therefore, this finding should not 
be interpreted as evidence that CS exposure is harmless; 
rather, it suggests that discontinuing CS solely because of 
toxicity concerns does not necessarily translate into fewer 
long-term complications. Because reliable data on glucose 
intervention, lipid management, cardiovascular prevention, 
healthcare utilization, and medical costs were unavailable, 
the trade-off among prolonged CS maintenance, relapse pre-
vention, adverse events, preventive care, and cost requires 
prospective evaluation. Taken together, our results support 
a risk-stratified approach that balances relapse prevention 
against toxicity risk and emphasizes proactive complication

prevention throughout long-term follow-up, regardless of 
whether CS is continued or discontinued.
OS and its association with AIP
The median OS in this AIP cohort was 252 months, with a 
10-year survival rate of 85.5%, indicating a generally favora-
ble prognosis. No deaths directly attributable to AIP were 
observed. The median age at death in this study cohort was 
84.5 years (IQR 78.3–88.0), and the mode was 90 years. This 
is almost equivalent to the mode of age at death based on the 
2024 Japanese Life Table, suggesting that the prognosis for 
AIP is not poor. Nevertheless, these comparisons should be 
interpreted cautiously, because the life-table modal age does 
not fully account for differences in baseline age structure, 
comorbidities, or selection related to long-term follow-up 
in our study.
In multivariate analysis, advanced age at diagnosis was 
an independent predictor of mortality, and maintenance CS 
therapy was associated with improved survival. Similar 
associations have been reported previously; notably, a mul-
ticenter cohort study in Japan showed that CS maintenance 
therapy was linked to better prognosis even after multivari-
able adjustment and propensity score matching for age and 
sex [9 ]. Nevertheless, our finding should be interpreted 
cautiously, because confounding by indication is difficult 
to eliminate in retrospective cohorts. In particular, patients 
who did not receive CS maintenance therapy might have 
had comorbidities, frailty, or other contraindications lim-
iting their ability to tolerate long-term CS therapy, which 
could bias survival estimates. At the same time, the consist-
ent protective signal across studies raises the possibility that 
sustained control of AIP and OOIs may reduce long-term 
organ damage and complications, thereby contributing to 
improved survival. Additionally, although biliary drainage 
before CS initiation showed a trend toward poorer OS, this 
may reflect more severe baseline disease or difficult manage-
ment cases rather than a direct causal effect; several patients 
experienced complex biliary management challenges that 
could have contributed to shorter survival [41].
Study limitations
First, this was a retrospective, single-country cohort study, and 
variability in CS regimens, tapering strategies, and follow-up 
intensity among centers may have introduced treatment hetero-
geneity and residual confounding. In particular, the decision 
to continue or discontinue CS maintenance therapy was not 
randomized and may have been influenced by disease sever-
ity, comorbidities, frailty, physician preference, and concerns 
regarding steroid toxicity; therefore, associations between CS 
strategy and relapse, adverse events, or survival should not be 
interpreted as causal. Second, although both patients who were 
followed for ≥ 10 years and those who died within the 10-year 
period were included, the study population still represented 
long-term managed cases, and the findings may not be fully 
generalizable to all patients with type 1 AIP, including those 
lost to follow-up early after diagnosis. Third, while relapse 
was defined according to standardized clinical, serologic, and 
imaging criteria, management after relapse—such as timing 
and method of biliary drainage or retreatment—was not stand-
ardized across centers. Fourth, serum IgG and IgG4 levels 
were measured regularly during follow-up, and the definition 
of IgG4 normalization was uniformly applied as either a nor-
mal serum IgG4 level at diagnosis or normalization within 
6 months after CS initiation, regardless of subsequent reeleva-
tion. However, minor variations in measurement timing and 
assay methods across centers may have introduced residual 
bias. Fifth, direct measures of exocrine pancreatic function 
were unavailable, and albumin was used as a surrogate marker, 
which could have been affected by extrapancreatic factors, 
including aging, comorbidities, and CS-related catabolism. 
Sixth, cumulative CS dose could not be reliably reconstructed 
because of incomplete longitudinal dose documentation across 
centers and uncertainty in older pre-electronic medical records 
generated more than 10 years ago. Therefore, treatment-dura-
tion metrics were used as surrogate indicators of CS exposure, 
which may not fully capture the relationship between steroid 
burden and toxicity. Seventh, although data on osteoporosis 
prophylaxis were available, detailed information on glucose/
lipid intervention, cardiovascular risk reduction strategies, and 
medical costs could not be systematically collected. There-
fore, the overall trade-off among prolonged CS maintenance, 
relapse prevention, adverse events, and cost-effectiveness 
could not be fully assessed. Eighth, the timing of fragility frac-
tures and macrovascular events was unavailable. Accordingly, 
comparisons with Japanese population-based data were lim-
ited to crude contextual comparisons using total follow-up per-
son-years. Differences in event definitions between this study 
and reference population studies should also be considered. 
Finally, the relatively small number of malignancy and mortal-
ity events limited statistical power for subgroup analyses, and 
calendar-time effects across the long enrollment period could 
have influenced diagnostic and therapeutic practices.
Despite these limitations, this study provides one of the 
most comprehensive long-term evaluations of type 1 AIP to 
date, integrating relapse, malignancy, metabolic and nutri-
tional changes, CS-related adverse events, and survival over 
more than a decade of follow-up.
Conclusion
Type 1 AIP demonstrates generally favorable long-term 
survival under CS therapy; however, relapse continues to 
occur even beyond typical maintenance periods. Serum IgG4

normalization may help inform relapse risk stratification, 
but it should not be regarded as sufficient justification for 
CS withdrawal. Continued low-dose maintenance should 
be considered even in patients with IgG4 normal/normal-
ized status, because CS discontinuation was associated 
with increased relapse risk despite serological remission. 
Therefore, routine CS discontinuation is not supported by 
our data; if withdrawal is considered, it should be reserved 
for carefully selected patients with compelling reasons to 
avoid prolonged CS exposure and should be accompanied by 
close surveillance. Outcomes should be optimized through 
an integrated management approach that addresses relapse 
prevention, CS-related bone health and cardiovascular risk, 
and emerging biologic therapies as potential alternatives to 
prolonged CS therapy in selected patients.
