Rosacea, an inflammatory skin disorder with complex pathogenesis, remains poorly 
understood. Through integrative bioinformatics and experimental approaches, we 
identified 304 differentially expressed genes in erythrotelangiectasia rosacea 
(ETR), primarily enriched in lipid metabolism pathways. Support vector machine 
(SVM), linear regression analyses and network analysis revealed ACADVL and ACSL5 
as potential therapeutic targets. Immunological profiling demonstrated 
distinctive immune cell infiltration, with elevated M0 and M1 macrophages in 
patients with ETR. Immunofluorescence validation confirmed significant ACSL5 
upregulation and increased M1 macrophage infiltration in the rosacea mouse 
model. The co-localization of ACSL5 with M1 macrophage markers suggests a 
mechanistic link between lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses. These 
findings provide new insights into ETR pathogenesis and highlight ACSL5 as a 
promising therapeutic target for inflammatory skin disorders.

Rosacea is a chronic, recurrent inflammatory disease that commonly affects the central area of the face. It primarily involves facial blood vessels and nerves, and its etiology remains unknown while its pathogenesis is complex1,2. It is believed that neurovascular dysregulation and abnormalities in both the adaptive and innate immune systems predispose individuals to this condition, leading to aberrant and persistent vasodilation of the facial skin3. Th1 and Th17 cell-mediated immune responses may impact adaptive immunity among individuals afflicted with an abnormal increase in ROS levels4,5. And neuromodulators contribute to an intricate network that culminates in inflammation, vasodilation, and angiogenesis associated with rosacea6. But the molecular background of the normal central face and the transcriptional profiling and immune cell composition of rosacea lesions remain largely unknown.

Based on the diverse clinical presentations, rosacea can be categorized into four subtypes: erythematotelangiectatic rosacea (ETR), papulopustular rosacea (PPR), phymatousPhR), and ocular rosacea (OR)7. ETR is characterized by skin sensitivity symptoms such as dryness, a sensation of heat, or a tingling sensation in the central area of the face. This can be attributed to the relatively thin protective layer on the cheeks, reduced sebaceous gland activity, and an abundance of blood vessels in the deeper layers of the skin, leading to occasional flushing and redness8. Persistent redness and flushing of the face are frequently reported and represent the most distressing manifestations of this condition. The daily lives and professional activities of these individuals are often significantly impacted by a persistent ailment that proves challenging to resolve9,10.

Conventional approaches to address erythema and flushing associated with rosacea encompass the utilization of systemic medications, topical medications, and physical therapy11. Physical therapy encompass modalities such as laser and light therapy, photodynamic therapy, and other targeted physical treatments12. However, the efficacy of these strategies can vary among patients, with some individuals experiencing inadequate symptom relief despite attempting these techniques. Given the recurrent nature of paroxysmal flushing episodes, it is imperative to establish effective interventions for this condition, investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying rosacea can serve as a foundation for innovative therapeutic interventions. Therefore, the objective of this study is to investigate the molecular mechanism underlying erythrocytic rosacea using bioinformatics methods, elucidate the role of immune cells, and establish a molecular foundation for novel therapeutic approaches.

After searching in Gene Expression Omnibus database with the inclusion criteria of patients with rosacea; 52 skin samples from GSE65914 were obtained (14 from patients with erythematotelangiectatic rosacea (ETR) and 20 normal controls). We first performed background correction and data normalization after collecting the dataset, and the results are given in box plots (Fig. 1A).

After data preprocessing, DEGs were identified with the setting of cutoff at P adj.value< 0.05 and |log2 (FC)| ≥ 1. As shown in the PCA plot (Fig. 1B), there was a significant difference between the two groups. As shown in the volcano plots, totally 1058 DEGs were obtained, 387 were down regulated and 669 were upregulated (Fig. 1C). The top 50 DEGs were illustrated in heatmap plots (Fig. 1D).

To explore differences in immune cell infiltration between the control and ETR groups, we utilized CIBERSORT to estimate the relative proportions of immune cells (Fig. 5A and B). In the ETR group, we observed higher proportions of resting and activated CD4 memory T cells, as well as M0 and M1 macrophages, while regulatory T cells (Tregs), activated NK cells, resting dendritic cells, and resting mast cells showed reduced proportions compared to the control group. Pearson correlation analysis further revealed significant differences in immune cell relationships between the two groups (Fig. 5C). Particularly, M1 macrophages exhibited a strong network of correlations with other immune cells in the ETR group, highlighting their potential role in ETR pathogenesis. In addition, we analyzed the correlation between hub genes and immune cell infiltration. A heatmap (Fig. 5D) demonstrated that both ACSL5 and ACADVL are closely associated with M1 macrophages, further suggesting their potential involvement in M1 macrophage-driven inflammatory pathways in ETR. These findings underscore the importance of M1 macrophages and their interaction with ACSL5 and ACADVL in the immune microenvironment of ETR.

 Immunofluorescence staining revealed a significant upregulation of ACSL5 expression in the rosacea mouse model compared to the control group (Fig. 6 A and B). In the inflammatory regions of the dermis, ACSL5 expression was markedly increased. The proportion of M1 macrophages among total macrophages was significantly higher in the model group compared to the control group (Fig. 6 C). Dual immunofluorescence staining for ACSL5 and M1 macrophage markers (iNOS and CD68) showed that the proportion of ACSL5+/iNOS+ cells among total macrophages was significantly increased in the model group (Fig. 6D) (p < 0.05). Moreover, the proportion of ACSL5+/iNOS+ cells among M1 macrophages was also significantly elevated in the model group (Fig. 6E). These results suggest a potential association between ACSL5 and M1 macrophage polarization in the inflammatory microenvironment of rosacea.

Rosacea is a chronic inflammatory skin disease characterized by centro-facial erythema, telangiectasia, papules, and pustules. Oral medication or light-based therapies for persistent erythema and flushing in rosacea may be ineffective in some cases. However, the efficacy of erythema rosacea is still not satisfactory, so more in-depth mechanism research and therapeutic target mining are still necessary.

In this study, a total of 304 differentially expressed genes associated with erythematotelangiectatic rosacea (ETR) were identified through differential gene analysis and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). Enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were primarily involved in metabolic pathways, PPAR signaling pathway, fatty acid metabolism, and lipid metabolic processes. While comprehensive lipidomic studies on rosacea skin lesions remain limited, one previous small-scale analysis reported no significant differences in major lipid components, including cholesterol, triglycerides, and squalene, between patients with rosacea and healthy controls13. This discrepancy may reflect limitations in the resolution and sensitivity of conventional lipidomic analyses. In contrast, our transcriptomic data revealed marked alterations in genes associated with lipid metabolism, particularly those involved in fatty acid pathways such as ACSL5 and ACADVL. These transcriptional changes indicate potential metabolic dysregulation that may not be reflected at the bulk lipid level. Such molecular alterations could influence enzyme function, lipid signaling, or metabolic fluxes, which may escape detection by untargeted lipid profiling. To address this gap, advanced approaches—such as targeted or spatial lipidomics—could complement transcriptomic findings and offer deeper insight into lipid pathway disturbances in rosacea. This integrative strategy may help uncover subtle metabolic shifts relevant to disease pathogenesis and expand the current understanding of rosacea at the molecular level. Previous research has indicated that inflammatory processes, triggered by oxidative stress and lipid oxidation, contribute to the development and progression of rosacea14. In addition, a study demonstrated that doxycycline, an antibiotic, can reduce cell number and increase cell lipid content in vitro, suggesting that doxycycline may treat rosacea by affecting sebaceous gland function and lipid metabolism15. Considering the close association between the differentially expressed genes and lipid metabolism observed in our study, it is plausible to speculate that lipid metabolism may represent a novel research avenue for investigating the pathogenesis of rosacea. However, further confirmatory experiments are necessary.

Subsequently, to further explore the potential therapeutic targets, two key genes (ACSL5, ACADVL) were identified by PPI network construction and machine learning. In recent years, the role of ACSL5 in fatty acid metabolism has received much attention. ACSL5 has different metabolic regulatory effects on fatty acids in different environments, which may be related to the tissue distribution and intracellular localization of ACSL516. ACSL5 has been reported not only involved in fatty acid uptake, mitochondrial metabolism but also associated with decreased Wnt activity and increased expression in CD8 and CD4 T-cell17. ACADVL is involved in the fatty acid degradation pathway and the fatty acid beta-oxidation biological process18. These literatures further confirm the potential role of dysregulation of lipid metabolism in the pathogenesis of rosacea. The association between the two genes and rosacea is still rare, and may become new research targets, but further in vitro verification experiments are needed.

Recently, an increasing number of studies have revealed the critical role of the innate immune system in the development of rosacea. Biopsies from patients with rosacea show perivascular and pilosebaceous infiltration of a large number of innate immune cells such as macrophages, mast cells and neutrophils. Among them, macrophages have been recognized as important coordinators of skin immunity19. Similarly, in the present study, we also found that ETR group did present more immune cell infiltration, including macrophages (M0 and M1), activated CD4 memory T cells.

M0 macrophages are dormant macrophages, which can be induced to be polarized into M1 macrophages or M2 macrophages. M1 macrophages are characterized by the expression of a broad spectrum of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines. M2 polarization of macrophages leads to the secretion of a range of anti-inflammatory factors4. Although it has been reported that the number of macrophages is increased in the skin lesions of rosacea patients, the exact role of macrophages in the pathogenesis of rosacea has not been clearly elucidated. In fact, macrophage and metabolic disorders have been found to co-occur in different metabolic diseases, including acne, atherosclerosis, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, etc20–22. Our findings revealed differential expression of lipid metabolism-related genes and increased M1 macrophage infiltration in ETR lesions, with ACSL5 potentially serving as a critical bridging gene connecting these two aspects. Our immunofluorescence results demonstrated a significant upregulation of ACSL5 expression and M1 macrophage markers in the rosacea mouse model. The enhanced ACSL5 expression and its co-localization with M1 macrophage markers suggest a potential association between lipid metabolism dysregulation and increased M1 macrophage polarization in the pathogenesis of rosacea. These findings provide new insights into the disease mechanism and highlight ACSL5 as a potential therapeutic target.

Although ACSL5 has not been directly targeted in dermatological settings, inhibitors of the ACSL family, such as Triacsin C, highlight the potential for pharmacological intervention23. Due to limited isoform specificity and off-target effects, there is a need to develop selective ACSL5 inhibitors. Improved understanding of ACSL5’s molecular characteristics may facilitate this process. Given its role in both lipid metabolism and macrophage polarization, ACSL5 may serve as a therapeutic target in rosacea. Further experimental studies are required to validate its functional relevance and therapeutic potential.

In summary, this study revealed the potential role of lipid metabolism dysregulation and M1 macrophage infiltration in the pathogenesis of ETR through bioinformatics analysis, and identified ASCL5 as key lipid metabolism regulatory genes, providing new insights into the pathogenesis of rosacea. Nevertheless, several limitations should be noted. The absence of in vitro functional validation limits the ability to establish a direct causal link between ACSL5, ACADVL, and disease progression. Further studies using cellular models and gene perturbation assays are needed to confirm their roles. Additionally, the use of publicly available transcriptomic data and a relatively limited sample size may introduce bias and affect the generalizability of the results. Addressing these issues in future research will be important for enhancing the translational significance of these findings.

Gene expression datasets were collected from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo).

Microarray data’s normalized expression matrix was taken from the GSE dataset. The annotation files from the dataset were then used to annotate the probes. We initially eliminated the batch effects with “sva R” package24. The differential expressed genes (DEGS) between the comparison group and the control group in the dataset were then obtained using the “limma R” package (|Log2 FC| > 1, p adj value< 0.05)25. Additionally, the R software’s “heatmap” and “ggplot2” packages26 were used to create a heat map and a box plot.

We first estimated the MAD (Median Absolute Deviation) of each gene individually using the gene expression profile, then we deleted the top 50% of genes with the smallest MAD, and last, we excluded the outlier genes and samples using the goodSamplesGenes function of the “WGCNA R” package27 and built a scale-free co-expression network. A power of β = 12 was chosen. To further investigate the module, we estimated the dissimilarity of module eigen Genes, selected a cut line for the module dendrogram, and merged modules with a distance less than 0.25 and sensitivity set to 3. 15 non-grey modules were obtained in the end. In addition, we estimated the expression correlation of module eigenvectors with genes in order to attain MM, as well as the expression correlation of genes with genes to acquire GS. Genes with high connectivity in the clinically significant module were identified as hub genes based on the cutoff criterion (|MM| > 0.8 and |GS| > 0.1).

GO annotations of genes from the “org R” package was used for the functional enrichment analysis of gene sets. Genes were mapped to the background set using Hs.eg.db (version 3.1.0) and KEGG annotation from the rest API (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/rest/keggapi.html) as the background, and results for gene set enrichment were obtained using enrichment analysis using the “clusterProfiler” R package28. Set the minimum and maximum gene sets to 5 and 5000, respectively. Significant enrichment was defined as p value of 0.05 or lower. CBNplot29, which exhibited a Bayesian network inference approach, was employed to explore molecular regulatory relationships. We set the parameter R (the number of bootstraps) to 10,000 to ensure that the gene or pathway regulatory network can be stably inferred.

We created PPI networks with a minimum needed interaction score of 0.4 using the web program STRING (https://string-db.org/) to characterize the critical DEGs. The download interaction data was used with Cytoscape software30 for additional analysis. The cytoHubba plugin identified significant genes as hub genes. Using the MCODE plugin, the PPI network’s major clusters were chosen. Next, the iRegulon plugin was used to identify master regulators that target these DEGs from major clusters. In this experiment, TFs with normalized enrichment score and normalized enrichment score (NES) ≥ 5 were set as the parameters to build the regulatory network.

The DEGs from hub clusters were screened by the LASSO and SVM algorithms. To filter the characteristic genes, the “glmnet” package31was used to perform regression analysis on the target genes. At the same time, support vector machine (SVM) was utilized to construct classification analysis by the “e1071”32, “kernlab”33, and “Caret” packages34. Finally, the “Venn” package was used to determine the intersection of the characteristic genes in the two algorithms.

An immune infiltrating cell score was calculated for each sample based on expression profiles using the CIBERSORT method selected from the “IOBR R” package35. The comparison regarding the proportion of diverse types of immune cells between ETR and control groups was visualized via the box plot. A heatmap depicting the correlation of 22 types of infiltrating immune cells was carried out using the Pearson analysis. Gene expression profiles were processed, and results with a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess relationships between immune cell types in both groups, with correlation coefficients visualized in a correlation matrix to identify immune-regulatory interactions specific to ETR development. To further explore the role of key genes, the correlations between hub genes and immune cell infiltration were investigated using Pearson correlation analysis and visualized with heatmaps. All visualizations, including immune cell proportions, correlation matrices, and heatmaps, were generated using R software to elucidate immune dynamics and the interplay between hub genes and the immune microenvironment in ETR.

The rosacea mouse model was established using SPF-grade female BALB/c mice, aged 6 to 8 weeks, with an average body weight of (28 ± 2) g. A total of ten mice were used in this study, with five assigned to the experimental group and five to the control group. The antimicrobial peptide LL- 37 was dissolved in physiological saline to prepare a solution with a concentration of 320 µmol/L, which was stored for use. The dorsal skin, spanning from the nape of the neck to the midpoint of the tail, was shaved to expose an area approximately 3 cm x 4 cm in size. Each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of 40 µl of LL- 37 solution via an insulin syringe. The injections were repeated every 12 h at the time points of 0, 12, 24, and 36 h, resulting in a total of four injections administered to the central region of the shaved skin. Twelve hours after the final injection, the injection sites were assessed for erythema with well-defined borders, which indicated successful model establishment.

Skin tissues from experimental mice were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 4 h, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Section (5 μm) were cut, mounted on slides, and blocked with 5% normal goat serum and 0.3% Triton X- 100. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight, followed by secondary antibody staining. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Slides were mounted with antifade medium and analyzed using fluorescence microscopy to evaluate the expression of specific biomarkers.

The mice were kept in the animal facility of Wenzhou Medical University.
