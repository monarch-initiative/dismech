# Cerebellar Heterogeneity and Selective vulnerability in Spinocerebellar Ataxia Type 1 (SCA1).
**Authors:** Hamel K, Moncada EL, Sheeler C, Rosa JG, Gilliat S, Zhang Y, Cvetanovic M
**Journal:** Neurobiol Dis (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.nbd.2024.106530](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nbd.2024.106530)

## Content

Heterogeneity is one of the key features of the healthy brain and selective vulnerability characterizes many, if not all, neurodegenerative diseases. While cerebellum contains majority of brain cells, neither its heterogeneity nor selective vulnerability in disease are well understood. Here we describe molecular, cellular and functional heterogeneity in the context of healthy cerebellum as well as in cerebellar disease Spinocerebellar Ataxia Type 1 (SCA1). We first compared disease pathology in cerebellar vermis and hemispheres across anterior to posterior axis in a knock-in SCA1 mouse model. Using immunohistochemistry, we demonstrated earlier and more severe pathology of PCs and glia in the posterior cerebellar vermis of SCA1 mice. We also demonstrate heterogeneity of Bergmann glia in the unaffected, wild-type mice. Then, using RNA sequencing, we found both shared, as well as, posterior cerebellum-specific molecular mechanisms of pathogenesis that include exacerbated gene dysregulation, increased number of altered signaling pathways, and decreased pathway activity scores in the posterior cerebellum of SCA1 mice. We demonstrated unexpectedly large differences in the gene expression between posterior and anterior cerebellar vermis of wild-type mice, indicative of robust intraregional heterogeneity of gene expression in the healthy cerebellum. Additionally, we found that SCA1 disease profoundly reduces intracerebellar heterogeneity of gene expression. Further, using fiber photometry, we found that population level PC calcium activity was altered in the posterior lobules in SCA1 mice during walking. We also identified regional differences in the population level activity of Purkinje cells (PCs) in unrestrained wild-type mice that were diminished in SCA1 mice.

Neuronal heterogeneity across brain regions is an important hallmark of a healthy brain. Selective vulnerability, often defined as a severe loss of neurons in a specific brain region, is a key pathophysiological feature of neurodegenerative diseases (Fu et al., 2018; Pandya and Patani, 2021; Saxena and Caroni, 2011; Subramaniam, 2019). Whether heterogeneity contributes to selective vulnerability in diseases, and how disease impacts heterogeneity remains little understood (Pandya and Patani, 2021).

The cerebellum contains the majority of the brain’s neurons (Herculano-Houzel, 2009), and is well-known for its role in movement and balance, cognition, affect, and reward. Anatomically, the cerebellar cortex is composed of two lateral hemispheres and a central vermis that is further divided by the primary fissure into the anterior and posterior areas (D’Mello and Stoodley, 2015). While cerebellar circuitry looks almost identical across these regions, several studies have demonstrated differences in gene expression, activity, behavioral role, and connectivity across the cerebellum (Buckner et al., 2011; Choe et al., 2018; Kozareva et al., 2021; Middleton and Strick, 1994; Shakkottai et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2014) indicative of intracerebellar heterogeneity.

Spinocerebellar ataxia type-1 (SCA1) is an inherited neurodegenerative disease that severely affects the cerebellum (Klinke et al., 2010; Koeppen, 2005; Seidel et al., 2012; Zoghbi et al., 1988) . SCA1 is caused by the abnormal expansion of glutamine (Q) encoding CAG repeats in the ATAXIN1 (ATXN1) gene (Orr et al., 1993, Zoghbi and Orr, 2009). While mutant ATXN1 is expressed throughout the brain, SCA1 is characterized by a severe loss of cerebellar Purkinje cells (PCs) in the posterior vermis of patients with SCA1 (Reetz et al., 2013). A recent MRI study of SCA1 patients demonstrated that while cortical thickness of anterior lobules was not different from controls, cortex of posterior lobules was significantly thinner in SCA1 patients (Nigri et al., 2022). These results suggest that during early phases of SCA1, posterior cerebellar lobules atrophy earlier and faster. As the substrates of increased vulnerability in posterior cerebellum remain unknown, we investigated the mechanisms underlying increased vulnerability of PCs in the posterior vermis and hemispheres and relative resistance of anterior vermis.

While mouse models of SCA1 have been extensively used to study SCA1 pathogenesis, most of the studies examined only the primary fissure of the vermis (that divides anterior lobules IV/V and posterior lobule VI) or used whole-cerebellum RNA and protein analyses (Driessen et al., 2018, Handler et al., 2022, Ruegsegger et al., 2016). Consequently, little is known about the selective vulnerability in SCA1 mouse models and how SCA1 impacts cerebellar intrinsic heterogeneity. To address these gaps in knowledge, we first used immunohistochemistry to examine neuronal and glial pathology in cerebellum along the anteroposterior and mediolateral axes in SCA1 knock-in mouse model Atxn1154Q/2Q mice, in which 154 CAG repeats were knocked into one endogenous mouse Atxn1 allele (Watase et al., 2002). We then used region-specific RNA-sequencing to explore the molecular underpinnings of increased PC pathology in posterior vermal lobules of SCA1 mice. We also explored heterogeneity in gene expression across cerebellar regions in healthy wild-type mice to identify genes and pathways that may contribute to regional vulnerability in disease. We determined how SCA1 impacts intrinsic intracerebellar heterogeneity of gene expression seen in wild-type mice. Fiber photometry was employed to investigate how is the activity of PCs altered in the anterior and posterior lobules in freely moving SCA1 mice. Finally, we examined whether there are differences in PC activity across cerebellar regions in wild-type mice and if those physiological differences are altered in SCA1.

The creation of the Atxn1154Q/2Q mice was previously described (Watase et al., 2002). These knock-in mice express mutant protein under the endogenous promoter and as such have physiologically relevant expression levels and spatial patterning of mutant ATXN1 expression. As CAG repeats are unstable, we periodically sequence the CAG region to evaluate the number of repeats present in our experimental population. At the time of these experiments, the average number of CAG repeats in our colony was 166. Experimental groups were balanced with an equal number of male and female Atxn1154Q/2Q and wild-type (WT) mice. In all experiments, investigators were blinded to the genotype.

For fiber photometry experiments, we utilized Pcp2-Cre;Ai95 mice. Ai95 line has a floxed-STOP cassette preventing transcription of the GCaMP6f fast variant calcium indicator. In Pcp2-Cre mice, Cre expression is limited to PCs in the cerebellum, resulting in selective expression of GCaMP6f in PCs in Pcp2-Cre;Ai95 mice and allowing us to record calcium activity specifically from PCs. Pcp2-cre;Ai95mice were crossed with Atxn1154Q/2Q mice to allow us to record from PCs of SCA1mice.

Animal experimentation was approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of University of Minnesota and was conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health’s (NIH) Principles of Laboratory Animal Care (86–23, revised 1985), and the American Physiological Society’s Guiding Principles in the Use of Animals (National Research Council (US) Committee for the Update of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, 2011).

Cerebellar anterior and posterior cortex of vermis and hemispheres were dissected as previously described (Hamel and Cvetanovic, 2021). Briefly, brains were placed into a brain matrix with dorsal side up, and one razor blade was placed along the sagittal midline, making sure that the blade pushes all the way to the bottom of the matrix. Six additional razor blades were positioned 1 mm apart from each other (three on each side of the brain). In total there were six sagittal brain sections with the four most lateral sections containing hemispheres where we separated deep cerebellar nuclei from the cortex. Two midline vermal sections were cut just posterior to the primary fissure separating the anterior lobes (I/II-IV/V) from the posterior lobes (VI-X). We used 12 Atxn1154Q/2Q and wild-type (WT) mice for RNA seq experiments and 8 Atxn1154Q/2Q and wild-type (WT) mice for RT-qPCR validation. Mice were around 18 weeks of age and balanced for sex.

RNA was extracted from dissected regions using TRIzol Reagent (Life Technologies). RNA was sent to the University of Minnesota Genomics Center for quality control, including fluorometric quantification (RiboGreen assay, Life Technologies) and RNA integrity with capillary electrophoresis (Agilent BioAnalyzer 2100, Agilent Technologies Inc.). All submitted samples with RNA integrity numbers (RINs) 8.0 or greater proceeded to RNA sequencing on an Illumina NextSeq 550 using a 100-nt paired-end read strategy (Friedrich et al., 2018). Data is stored on University of Minnesota Supercomputing Institute Servers. To analyze data raw, paired short reads were analyzed through CHURP (Collection of Hierarchical UMII-RIS Pipelines, PEARC ‘19 Proceeding, Article No. 96), which includes data quality control via FastQC, data preprocessing via Trimmomatic (Bolger et al., 2014), mapping via HiSat2 (Kim et al., 2015) against reference mouse genome and expression quantification via Subread (Liao et al., 2014). Resulting count matrix of gene expression was used as input of R (https://www.r-project.org/) package: EdgeR (Chen et al., 2016; Love et al., 2014) (v3.32.1), which tested the differential gene expression and visualized the behavior of expected invariant control genes and published positive control genes. Genes with resulting FDR values less than 0.05 were considered significant. EdgeR was used to reveal sample outliers through Multi-Dimensional Scaling (MDS) and Principle Component Analysis (PCA). Pathway analysis and dot plot creation was preformed using the clusterProfiler package (v3.18.1) using the top 500 DEGs based on absolute value of LogFC (Yu et al., 2012). Heatmaps were created using pheatmap package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=pheatmap)(v1.0.12). Volcano plots were created using BiocMananger (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=BiocManager) (v1.30.16) and EnhancedVolcano (https://github.com/kevinblighe/EnhancedVolcano) (v1.8.0) packages. Pathway activity score was calculated using R package GSVA, we then averaged the score across samples from the same brain region with the same genotype.

Total RNA was extracted from dissected mouse cerebellar regions using TRIzol (Life Technologies), and RT-qPCR was performed as described previously (Rosa et al., 2022). We used IDT Primetime primers for the Grid2, Gli1, and Cck. GAPDH Universal Probe Library from Roche was used for normalization in the Cck experiments. 18S RNA (Forward: AGTCCCTGCCCTTTGTACACA, Reverse: CGATCCGAGGGCCTCACTA) was used for normalization in the Grid2 and Gli1 quantification.

We used six littermate and sex-matched mice of each genotype (N = 5-8 each of Atxn1154Q/2Q and wild-type (WT)), at each age (18- and 12-weeks of age) and balanced for sex. IF was performed on a minimum of four different floating 40-μm-thick brain slices from each mouse (four technical replicates per mouse per region or antibody of interest) in four different cohorts of mice derived from four litters. Experimenter performing staining, imaging and analysis were blinded for the genotype until all quantification was completed. All mice within a cohort were stained at the same time. We used primary antibodies against Purkinje cell marker calbindin (rabbit, Sigma-Aldrich, C9848), vesicular glutamate transporter 2 (VGLUT2) (guinea pig, Millipore, AB2251-I), astrocytic marker glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) (chicken, Millipore, AB5541), and microglial marker ionized calcium binding adapting molecule 1 (Iba1) (rabbit, Abcam, AB107159) as previously described (Asher et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2018).

Confocal images of min of four different slices from each mouse were acquired using an Olympus FV1000 microscope using an oil 20X objective. Z-stacks consisting of twenty non-overlapping 1-μm-thick slices were taken of each stained brain slice per brain region (i.e., four z-stacks per mouse per region, each taken from a different brain slice). The laser power and detector gain were standardized and fixed between mice within a cohort, and all images for mice within a cohort were acquired in a single imaging session to allow for quantitative comparison. Fluorescent images were acquired using an epifluorescent microscope (Leica DM6000) using a 20X objective. Single 20X images were stitched together to capture one plane of the entire mouse cerebellum.

Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ (NIH) as described previously. To quantify relative intensity of staining for calbindin we measured the average signal intensity at three different areas in the region of interest (minimum of 12 measurements for each region of interest per each mouse) and normalized it to that of the WT sex-matched littermate control mouse of that cohort. To quantify atrophy of the cerebellar molecular layer we took three measurements per image of the distance from the base of the Purkinje soma to the end of their dendrites, the average being the molecular layer width for that image (min of 12 measurements per each region of interest/mouse). Extension of climbing fibers was quantified as a ratio of the width of VGLUT2 staining to the width of the molecular layer. To quantify relative intensity of staining for GFAP we measured the average signal intensity in the region of interest and normalized it to that of the WT littermate mouse (minimum of 16 measurements per each mouse). We counted the number of Iba1 positive microglia in regions of interest (minimum of 16 measurements per each mouse) and divided it by the area to quantify microglial density. IHC

Atxn1154Q/2Q and wild-type (WT) Pcp2-Cre;Ai95 mice aged 14 to 15-weeks-old were implanted with a photometry cannula. Briefly, mice were induced and anesthetized using isoflurane at 2%. To manage pain, buprenorphine was subcutaneously (SQ) injected 3 hours previous to the surgery, carprofen was delivered SQ right before the start of the surgery, and lidocaine topically applied to the scalp before making the incision. An incision was made to expose the skull, bregma was located, and holes were drilled to implant the fiber photometry cannulas (1.25 mm ferrule, 400um core, 0.37 NA, Neurophotometrics San Diego, CA) into the Lobule 4 (AP: −5.7, ML: −1, and DV: −1.2 from Bregma) and Lobule 6 (AP: −6.6, ML: 1, and DV: −1.4 from Bregma) of the cerebellum. Once the cannula was implanted, it was fixed using dental resin ionomer (Geristore, DenMat). Fiber photometry experiments were not conducted until at least 1 week after the surgery to ensure sufficient time for recovery.

Fiber photometry recordings took place on a 20-inch by 20-inch arena allowing the mice to move freely for 10 minutes while attached to the patch cord. Using a TDT photometry rig (RZ10X, Tucker-Davis Technologies) the signal from GCaMP6f expressed in Purkinje cells was excited and recorded using a 465 nm LED and a 405 nm LED for isosbestic signal sampled at 1 kHz.

Raw files from the photometry recording were processed using a custom MATLAB (2022 B) script. This script removes the photobleaching and subtracts the 405 nm signal (isosbestic) from the 465 nm signal (GCaMP6f). Once the file has been processed, OriginPro 2021 (OriginLab) was utilized to normalize the traces to Z-Score. Using the built-in tool Peak Analyzer and the Asymmetric Least Square Smoothing method, the baseline of the trace is determined, and artifacts are removed. Calcium events are identified based on the normalized amplitude of the traces with a threshold of 2 standard deviations in 2 second windows. Once the activity peaks were identified for all the traces, they were grouped and frequency as well as amplitude were compared using a one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test.

Wherever possible, sample sizes were calculated using power analyses based on the standard deviations from our previous studies, significance level of 5%, and power of 90%. Statistical tests were performed with GraphPad Prism 7.0. Data from immunohistochemistry imaging was analyzed using either a Students t-test or one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc test depending on whether the comparison was between genotypes or within genotype. Data for RTqPCR was analyzed by One-way ANOVA with the Tukey’s post-hoc test or Welch’s t-test depending on whether comparison is between genotypes or cerebellar region. DEGs for RNA sequencing data were identified by a FDR threshold of less than 0.05. Fiber photometry data was analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by the Tukey’s post-hoc test.

To determine whether SCA1 mice exhibit enhanced pathology in the posterior cerebellum as patients with SCA1 do, we analyzed Purkinje cell (PC) pathology and glial reactivity across different regions of the cerebellum. This included four lobules of the cerebellar vermis: anterior lobule II, the primary fissure (lobules V/VI), and posterior lobules VII and X, and four lobules of the hemispheres: Crus I and II, the paramedian, and the copula pyramidis of Atxn1154Q/2Q mice (Figure 1A). All measurements of cerebellar pathology were performed at 18 weeks as Atxn1154Q/2Q mice have already developed motor deficits but have limited PC loss at this age (Watase et al., 2002).

Atxn1154Q/2Q mice exhibit stereotypical Purkinje cell pathology prior to cell loss which is quantified as atrophy of PC dendrites in the molecular layer of the cerebellar cortex, decreased calbindin expression (a calcium binding protein that is highly expressed in PCs), and loss of vesicular glutamate transporter 2 positive (VGLUT2+) climbing fiber synapses on PC dendrites (Ebner et al., 2013; Kim et al., 2018) (Figure 1A). We found significantly reduced width of the molecular layer (ML), indicative of PCs dendritic atrophy, only in posterior lobules VII and X of the vermis and in Crus 1 of the hemispheres (Figure 1B). Calbindin expression was slightly but significantly decreased only in the PCs in posterior vermis- specifically lobules VI (~21% decrease), VII (~33%) and X (~ 39%) in Atxn1154Q/2Q mice relative to wild-type controls (Figure1C). It is difficult to evaluate biological relevance of these relatively small differences in calbindin expression. Notably, we did not see ML atrophy nor reduced calbindin expression in anterior lobe II.

Intriguingly, a significant loss of climbing fiber synapses was detected in all SCA1 lobules examined (Figure1D), indicating that loss of synapses precedes atrophy and decrease in calbindin expression. Notably, synaptic loss in SCA1 cerebellar vermis was graded along the anterior to posterior axis with lobule II, and primary fissure having smallest (10% decrease), lobule VII having a 17% decrease, and most posterior lobule X having the largest (~28% reduction) synaptic loss (Supplementary Figure 1). There was a significant amount of synaptic loss across the four hemisphere lobules (Figure 1D), though, unlike the vermis, the loss was consistent across each hemisphere lobules (about 10%, Supplementary Figure 1).

To determine whether regional differences are present at an earlier disease stage, we investigated pathology at 12 weeks (Supplementary Figure 2). We found significant reduction in calbindin intensity only in the posterior lobules VII and X (Supplementary Figure 2D) and a significant synapse loss in the lobules V/VI, VII, and X (Supplementary Figure 2C and E). Notably, anterior lobule II showed no significant changes in any of our PC pathology measures at the 12-week time point.

These results demonstrate that PC pathology presents earliest and most severely in the lobules of posterior vermis in Atxn1154Q/2Q mice, and that there is relative sparing of PC degeneration in the anterior vermis. This is consistent with the increased vulnerability of posterior cerebellar PCs observed in human SCA1 patients.

To examine intracerebellar differences in SCA1 glial pathology we evaluated expression of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and the density of ionized calcium binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba1) labeled microglia as a simple measure of reactive astrogliosis and microgliosis. Reactivity of Bergmann glia (BG), a special type of cerebellar astrocytes, was quantified as an increase in the expression of GFAP in the molecular and Purkinje cell layers (Kim et al., 2018; Rosa et al., 2022). While there are many different types of glia in the cerebellum, we chose to focus on BGs because of their close morphological and functional association with PCs in the molecular layer. BG processes closely interact with PC dendrites and are critical to ongoing PC health and function (Bellamy, 2006; Farmer et al., 2016; Luttik et al., 2022). Importantly, we and others previously demonstrated that BG contribute to SCA1 pathogenesis (Kim et al., 2018; Luttik et al., 2022; Edamakanti et al., 2023). We found a slight (~37%) but significant increase in BG GFAP intensity only in lobule X of Atxn1154Q/2Q mice compared to WT controls (Figure 2A and B). Finally, we found that density of Iba1+ microglia was increased only in the lobule X and Crus 1 in SCA1 mice compared to controls (Figure 2C and D).

Assessment of gliosis at the earlier disease time point demonstrated increased GFAP staining intensity only in lobule X (Supplementary Figure 2F and G) and a trending increase in microglial density in lobule X of the 12-week Atxn1154Q/2Q mice (Supplementary Figure 2H and I).

Intriguingly, in the wild-type mice we found significant differences in the relative BG GFAP expression across anterior to posterior axis. In particular GFAP expression was higher in BG in lobule X (~ 66%) and Copula Pyramidis (~35%) of wild-type mice compared to other lobules (Figure 3B), indicating a regional heterogeneity of BG GFAP expression in the cerebella of wild-type mice. Biological relevance of these slight, but significant, increases in GFAP expression remains to be determined. We did not find significant difference in either VGLUT2/ML or Iba1 density across cerebellar regions in the wild-type mice (Figure 3A, C).

Taken together, these results indicate earliest and most severe pathology of PC neurons, astrocytes (BG), and microglia in the posterior lobule X of Atxn1154Q/2Q mice consistent with increased thinning of posterior lobules in patients with SCA1.

Our next goal was to provide insight into molecular mechanisms of increased SCA1 pathogenesis in posterior cerebellum. We recently developed and quick and reproducible dissection method (Hamel and Cvetanovic, 2021) to isolate three distinct cortical regions of the cerebellum- cerebellar cortex of the anterior vermis (CCAV), cerebellar cortex of the posterior vermis (CCPV), and cerebellar cortex of the hemispheres (CCH).

First, to confirm that increased vulnerability is detected when combining posterior lobules, we compared the average ML thickness from each cerebellar region (Supplementary Figure 3A). Similar to analysis per lobule, we found a significant reduction in the ML width in the posterior lobules of the vermis (average of lobules VI-X) and in the hemispheres of Atxn1154Q/2Q mice but not in the anterior vermal lobules (average of lobules I-V) (Supplementary Figure 3B), validating that posterior to anterior lobule vulnerability is detectable at the regional level.

To provide insight into regional molecular mechanisms of SCA1 pathogenesis, we compared transcriptomes of dissected anterior vermis, posterior vermis, and hemispheres in Atxn1154Q/2Q mice to wild-type littermate controls (Supplementary Figure 4, black arrows). We identified a similar number of DEGs in each of these regions (CCAV: 1655, CCPV: 1715 and CCH: 1444) (Figure 4A). Consistent with previous findings from whole cerebellar RNA sequencing (Driessen et al., 2018; Ingram et al., 2016), the majority of the DEGs identified in each region were downregulated in the Atxn1154Q/2Q mice (Figure 4B). Approximately 42% (683) DEGs were altered in all three regions of interest, constituting a shared effect of SCA1 pathogenesis. These shared pathogenesis genes included the Magenta module, previously identified to correlate with SCA1 cerebellar pathology (Ingram et al., 2016). While the expression of the Magenta genes was impacted in all regions, it was most perturbed in the posterior vermis (Figure 4C) consistent with its exacerbated pathology.

In keeping with this high proportion of shared gene expression changes across regions, Gene Ontology (GO) pathway analysis found that pathways regulating ion transmembrane transport, voltage gated channel activity, and potassium ion transmembrane transport were consistently changed across all regions (Supplementary Figure 5). Notably, many of these pathways have been identified in bulk cerebellar transcriptomic studies (Driessen et al., 2018; Ingram et al., 2016).

While a large proportion of our transcriptomic data is consistent with and supported by previous bulk analysis of the SCA1 cerebellum, we also found a surprisingly large number of unique differential gene expression changes in each cerebellar region of SCA1 mice (CCAV: 673, CCPV: 533 and CCH: 281). In fact, most of the DEGs with the greatest log fold change (LogFC) were unique to each region, further supporting difference in disease pathogenesis across cerebellar regions (Figure 4D). Importantly, Atxn1 expression did not differ across regions, indicating that regional differences in SCA1 pathogenesis across cerebellum are not a consequence of differences in Atxn1 expression (Supplementary Figure 6).

Genes that are differentially impacted across SCA1 cerebellar cortical regions could reflect and contribute to region specific pathology of PCs and BG (Supplementary Figure 7A, B). For instance, PC gene Grid2 is significantly decreased in the posterior vermis and hemispheres but not in the anterior cerebellum (Supplementary Figure 7A, C). Grid2 (Glutamate Ionotropic Receptor Delta Type Subunit) encodes a glutamate ionotropic receptor GRID2 which is highly expressed in PCs. Loss of GRID2 expression through homozygous deletion causes spinocerebellar ataxia type 18 (Sarna and Hawkes, 2003; Selimi et al., 2003)(Hills et al., 2013; Utine et al., 2013). We confirmed the region-selective decrease of Grid2 expression in the posterior vermis using an independent set of RNA isolated from these three regions and RTqPCR (Supplementary Figure 7D).

Following our observation that glial reactivity was enhanced in the posterior vermis, we also found reduced expression of genes important for BG function in the posterior vermis and hemispheres. This includes a notable downregulation of Gli1, transcriptional factor known for its role in maintaining BG identity in response to PC via Sonic hedgehog (Shh) signaling (Farmer et al., 2016) (Supplementary Figure 7B, E). The enhanced decrease of Gli1 was confirmed with RT-qPCR (Supplementary Figure 7F). Gli1 regulates expression of Slc1a3 BG gene that is responsible for regulating homeostasis of glutamate, and that we previously found to be decreased in SCA1 (Djukic et al., 2007; Rothstein et al., 1996). Consistent with decreased Gli1 expression in posterior vermis and hemispheres we found that expression of Slc1a3 is significantly altered in the posterior vermis and hemispheres but not anterior vermis (Supplementary Figure 7B). Together, these results indicate regional impairment of Shh signaling that may contribute to the exacerbated BG dysfunction in posterior vermis of SCA1 mice.

To KEGG pathway analysis identified difference is the pathways disrupted across the regions. Notably, the largest number of disrupted pathways was in the posterior cerebellum (CCAV: 24, CCPV: 46, CCH: 9) (Figure 4E). This result may indicate that in addition to the relative importance of altered signaling pathways, the sheer number of altered pathways may also be a significant contributor to increased vulnerability in SCA1. Additionally, activity scores for the altered pathways are more decreased in CCPV than in other cerebellar regions (Figure 4F).

ATXN1 is thought to regulate gene expression by binding to the transcriptional factors (TF). We used TF enrichment analysis ChEA3 to aid in identifying the TF responsible for the observed regional changes in SCA1 cerebella (Keenan et al., 2019). Four of the top 15 TF, NACC2, KCNIP3, ARNT2 and RORB were identified in each cerebellar region (Supplementary Figure 8), while the rest of TF were either unique to each region (CCAV:8, CCPV: 6, CCH: 5), or shared between two regions (CCAV/CCPV:1, CCAV/CCH: 2, CCPV/CCH: 4). Intriguingly, none of these TF were identified as ATXN1 interactors in the previous studies (Chen et al., 2022; Lim et al., 2006; Rocha et al., 2019; Suter et al., 2013).

We reasoned that a transcriptomic study of cerebellar regions could provide crucial insight into cerebellar heterogeneity that may contribute to selective PC vulnerability in disease.

To provide insight into physiological cerebellar heterogeneity we performed a comparative analysis in wild-type animals to identify the genes and pathways enriched in each cerebellar region (Supplementary Figure 3B, yellow arrows). Strikingly, we found a large number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with total of 1229 and 774 DEGs between anterior and posterior vermis and anterior vermis and hemispheres respectively (Figure 5A). Only 365 DEGs between the posterior vermis and hemispheres indicates a higher degree of molecular similarity between those two regions (Figure 5). Top 15 region-enriched genes further illustrate unique molecular characteristics of each intracerebellar region (Figure 5B).

Using KEGG analysis we identified several signaling pathways that differ across intracerebellar regions, including calcium and cAMP signaling (Figure 5C). KEGG analysis also identified neuroligand signaling as significantly enriched in the anterior vermis compared to both posterior vermis and hemispheres (Figure 5C). To explore this pathway further, we plotted expression of the genes in the neuroligand signaling pathway in each of the cerebellar cortical regions of wild-type mice (Supplementary Figure 9). Among neuroligand genes enriched in anterior vermis is Cck, which encodes for Cholecystokinin (CCK) that was recently found to be neuroprotective in SCA1 and SCA2 (Ingram et al., 2016; Wozniak et al., 2021). We confirmed increased Cck expression in the anterior vermis by RT-qPCR (Figure 5D).

Together, these results indicate significant intracerebellar heterogeneity in the expression of genes and signaling pathways in wild-type mice.

We next asked how disease impacts cerebellar heterogeneity by comparing gene expression (Supplementary Figure 3B, pink arrows) and PC activity across cerebellar regions in Atxn1154Q/2Q mice.

Based on the number of differentially expressed genes found per comparison, the regional distinctions between the anterior and posterior vermis shrank while the hemispheres remained transcriptionally unique. In contrast to the 1229 genes that were differentially expressed between the anterior and posterior vermis in the wild-type mice, this comparison yielded only 507 DEGs in Atxn1154Q/2Q mice (Figure 6A). Moreover, the number of region-unique DEGs dropped from 671 genes to 186 in Atxn1154Q/2Q mice in the comparison of the anterior and posterior vermis (Figure 6B). Similarly, the number of DEGs between posterior vermis and hemispheres was slightly decreased - from 360 DEGs in wild-type mice to 213 in Atxn1154Q/2Q mice (Figure 6B). Intriguingly, this trend did not extend to the comparison between the anterior vermis and hemispheres. The number of DEGs identified between the SCA1 anterior vermis and hemispheres was 705, similar to 743 DEGs found in wild-type mice. This large reduction of number of DEGs between posterior and anterior vermis or hemispheres suggests significant loss of heterogeneity during disease progression in the Atxn1154Q/2Q mice.

Comparing the top 15 differentially expressed genes (up and down regulated) from each region further reveals impact of SCA1 on intrinsic heterogeneity (Supplementary Figure 10A-C and Figure 5). Furthermore, out of the top ten pathways identified by KEGG pathway analysis differing anterior and posterior vermis in WT mice (Figure 5), only one (Hippo signaling) remained different between anterior and posterior vermis in SCA1 mice (Supplementary Figure 10D). These results indicate that SCA1 profoundly dissipates intrinsic heterogeneity of gene expression between anterior and posterior vermis.

Changes in disease may either promote pathogenesis or be compensatory. Indeed, previous studies indicated that PC’s dendritic atrophy in SCA1 may be compensatory change to preserve PC’s firing rate (Dell’Orco et al., 2015). In addition, previous studies used cerebellar slice electrophysiology to reveal reduced regularity of PC firing rate, measured as increased coefficient of variation of the inter-spike interval Atxn1154Q/2Q mice (Bushart et al., 2021), but how is PC activity altered in freely moving Atxn1154Q/2Q mice was unknown.

To quantify population level PC activity in anterior and posterior lobules of the cerebellar vermis in freely moving mice we employed fiber photometry and PC-specific expression of the genetically encoded calcium indicator (GCAMP6f). While fiber photometry cannot provide single cell resolution, we chose this technique due to its lightweight that minimizes the impact on balance, relatively minimal damage, and because it allows for long-term monitoring of PC population activity in freely moving mice. Specific expression of GCaMP6f in PCs was achieved by crossing the Ai95 line, which has a floxed-STOP cassette preventing transcription of the GCaMP6 fast variant calcium indicator, with Pcp2-Cre which expresses Cre recombinase only in Purkinje cells (Chen et al., 2013; Madisen et al., 2015; Sługocka et al., 2017). Fiber photometry cannulas were implanted into anterior lobule IV and the contralateral posterior lobule VI (Figure 7A, Supplementary Figure 11) which are as far anterior and posterior as we could access while minimizing the damage to the cerebellum and surrounding tissue. Post-surgery, mice were allowed to recover for one week after which we recorded mouse movement along with the PC calcium activity while mice were exploring open field arena for ten minutes (Figure 7A).

Over a ten-minute period of open field self-initiated walking, we found that the average amplitude of population level PC calcium events was significantly decreased in the anterior lobule IV of Atxn1154Q/2Q mice compared to wild-type mice (Figure 7B). Surprisingly, none of the PC activity measures were significantly altered in lobule VI (Figure 7B).

SCA1 manifests as decreased balance and movement. Indeed, during the ten minutes of recording Atxn1154Q/2Q mice spent significantly less time walking than wild-type mice (Atxn1154Q/2Q: 102.9 ± 15.5, WT: 214.9 ± 23.1, Supplementary Figure 12).

Therefore, to minimize the impact of how much mice were moving and to gain deeper understanding of activity changes in SCA1, we separately analyzed PC activity while mice were walking and when they were stationary.

During walking only lobule VI was significantly altered in Atxn1154Q/2Q mice compared to wild-type mice. In particular, we found that AUC was significantly increased in lobule VI while frequency of PC calcium events was trending towards increase in lobule VI of Atxn1154Q/2Q mice compared to wild-type littermate controls (P= 0.053)(Figure 7C). When mice were stationary, amplitude of PC calcium events was decreased in both lobule IV and VI of Atxn1154Q/2Q mice compared to wild-type mice. In lobule IV we also found a decrease in the AUC when Atxn1154Q/2Q mice were not walking compared to wild-type mice (Figure 7D). The opposite changes, increase and decrease in PCs activity when Atxn1154Q/2Q mice were respectively ambulatory and stationary (Figure 7 C and D), likely resulted in no change observed when total calcium activity was analyzed (Figure 7B).

It is important to interpret these results with consideration of the population level nature of the measurements and the possibility that the data might be affected by small differences in the placement of the photometry cannula.

While cerebellum has been known for its uniform anatomy and circuitry (Diedrichsen et al., 2019), previous studies demonstrated differences in PC activity across the cerebellum in wild-type mice. For instance, zebrin positive PC that predominate in the posterior cerebellum have a lower firing rate compared to more anteriorly prevalent zebrin negative PCs (Zhou et al., 2014). It is possible that these physiological differences in PC’s activity may contribute to regional changes in PCs activity in SCA1 mice.

Therefore, we next investigated regional PC activity in vivo during self-motivated and unrestrained walking of wild-type mice. Total average amplitude of PC calcium events over a ten -minute period was larger in the anterior lobule IV compared to posterior lobule VI of wild-type mice, while the frequency and area under the curve (AUC) were not statistically different (Figure 8A). Together, these results indicate differences at the population level PCs calcium activity, with increased activity in the anterior vermis compared to the posterior vermis, consistent with previous studies.

We next analyzed PC population activity during periods when mice were walking as well as when they were stationary. We found that during walking PC calcium activity is increased in a lobule-specific manner. In lobule IV we found statistically increased frequency of PC calcium events during walking, while in lobule VI we found an increase in the amplitude and in the AUC (Figure 8B). This result indicates that the mechanism of increased PC activity during walking may be region-specific, with more frequent PC activity in anterior lobules, and larger and longer events in posterior lobules.

We then investigated whether this physiological regional pattern of PC activity is impacted in SCA1 (Figure 8C-D). When comparing total PC activity, we found significant increase in the AUC in lobule VI compared to lobule IV, indicating increased PC activity in posterior lobule VI in SCA1 (Figure 8C). This is in contrast to decreased PC activity in lobule VI compared to lobule IV in wild-type mice (Figure 8A).

When comparing PCs activity during ambulation and when mice were stationary, in both lobule IV and VI we found increased frequency, amplitude and AUC when Atxn1154Q/2Q mice were walking (Figure 8D). This similar increase in PCs activity is in contrast to lobule-specific changes in PC calcium activity in wild-type mice (Figure 8B). We also found that increase in PC activity during walking was larger in Atxn1154Q/2Q mice compared to wild-type mice, indicating that larger PC activity is needed to produce movement in SCA1 than in wild-type condition.

Together these results indicate that lobule-specific PCs activity changes during walking in healthy mice are not present in SCA1 mice.

The advancement of sequencing techniques, including single-cell and spatial transcriptomics has raised the awareness of the regional heterogeneity of gene expression in the healthy brain. Our results further increase awareness of the intraregional heterogeneity and provide insight into a complex interplay between regional heterogeneity and increased vulnerability in neurodegeneration.

We identified a pattern of exacerbated pathology of PCs, astrocytes and microglia across the anterior to posterior axis of the cerebellar vermis of SCA1 mice, consistent with observations of earlier and more severe PC vulnerability in the posterior vermis of patients with SCA1. While we have performed rigorous staining and analysis, including normalizing results to wild-type controls, and performing blinded staining and analysis, it is important to note the limitations of quantifying immunohistochemistry, such as semiquantitative nature of measuring intensity of GFAP and calbindin staining.

We found that while many genes and pathways were altered in both regions, the posterior cerebellum exhibited larger number of affected pathways. This result may indicate that the number of affected pathways contributes to increased regional vulnerability. We identified a surprising number of DEGs in a comparison of the anterior and posterior regions in the wild-type cerebellum, a result supportive of substantial intracerebellar heterogeneity. We found that the anterior vermis is enriched in genes negatively regulating neuroinflammation including anti-inflammatory neuropeptide Y (NPY), NPY receptors 1 and 5 (NPY1R and NPY5R) (Li et al., 2019), and dopamine 2 receptor (DRD2) (Du et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2020) (Figure 5A). Increased expression of these anti-inflammatory genes in the anterior vermis could reduce neuroinflammation in the anterior vermis during cerebellar disease. Conversely, increased expression of genes regulating inflammation (IL17Re (Interleukin 17 Receptor), NFAT 5 (nuclear factor of activated T cells 5) and Sp1), cell death (Cas2 (caspase-2)), and dysregulated synapse (Snca (αSynuclein)) genes could predispose posterior vermis to the increased neuroinflammation and loss of synapses in disease, as we observed in SCA1 (Figure 5A).

Notably, our results indicate that disease dissipates intracerebellar heterogeneity of gene expression. We found a striking decrease in region-specific genes and pathways in SCA1 mice compared to wild-type controls. It is possible that the reduction in heterogeneity may decrease resistance and/or increase vulnerability in anterior cerebellum allowing for disease pathogenesis to eventually spread throughout cerebellum.

Intracerebellar differences in degeneration have been described in other cerebellar diseases. In Spinocerebellar Ataxia type 5 (SCA5) posterior lobules of the cerebellum preferentially undergo degeneration (Perkins et al., 2016). However, the posterior cerebellum is not universally more vulnerable. In fact, the opposite pattern of cerebellar degeneration is seen in Niemann-Pick disease, type C1 (NPC1), where the anterior vermis is more affected (Martin et al., 2019). Thus, it is likely that the region-specific effect of a disease is not simply a question of one region being generally more vulnerable than another, but a factor of the disease-specific mechanism and the underlying molecular signature of that cerebellar region. The results of these studies and the work presented here highlight the importance of assessing future disease-associated molecular changes in a region-specific manner, as bulk RNA sequencing is likely to mask any differences across regions that might confer vulnerability.

Increased vulnerability in disease is usually defined as most severe neuronal pathology in particular brain region. As studies have shown that some pathological changes, such as neuronal atrophy, may be compensatory measures aimed to preserve function in disease (Dell’Orco et al., 2015), it is important to determine whether increased pathology in fact correlates with decreased function. Using fiber photometry and measuring PC calcium signaling at the population level, we found that PC function is impacted in live, freely behaving SCA1 mice. In particular, our results demonstrate that population level PC activity is increased only in the lobule VI when SCA1 mice were walking compared to wild-type mice, while lobule IV was not significantly altered during ambulation. These results indicate that functional impairments correlate with region specific PC pathology and gene expression in SCA1.

While these studies for the first time demonstrate in vivo functional impairment of PC in behaving SCA1 mice and show regional differences that correlate with pathology and molecular changes, these results are limited by population level measurements and possibility that slight differences in the position of cannula may affect our measurements. Future studies using wide-field 2 photon imaging could further explore PC activity changes in SCA1 at the level of individual cells and processes.

Additionally, we found that while both SCA1 and wild-type mice increase PC activity during ambulation, SCA1 mice do not exhibit region-specific changes in PC activity seen in wild-type mice. In ambulating wild-type mice PCs in anterior lobule IV exhibited increased frequency of calcium events, while PCs in lobule VI had increased amplitude and AUC of calcium events compared to when mice were stationary. While functional importance of the lobule IV- and VI -specific changes in PC activity during ambulation is unclear, it is possible that it contributes to balancing and coordination during ambulation.

While these results indicate an exacerbated change in the cerebellar transcriptome and PCs activity in posterior lobules, they are limited to one stage of a progressively worsening disease. Our histological analyses show that the posterior cerebellum worsens with time and that the anterior cerebellum begins to show signs of declining PC health at later time points. Thus, it is likely that transcriptomic analysis would demonstrate evidence of PC dysfunction and pathology in anterior lobules with aging. Future transcriptomic work following the progression of SCA1 in both regions over time would thus be useful for understanding both how the disease uniquely affects these two regions and what mechanisms may be useful for delaying cerebellar dysfunction in vulnerable regions.

Our findings advance understanding of cerebellar physiology and neurodegeneration in several ways. First, we characterized cerebellar heterogeneity at the level of the gene expression and PC activity during unrestrained walking in the wild-type mice. These results increase our understanding of cerebellar physiology and provide a foundation on which to test genes and pathways that could contribute to the observed differences in PC activity and underlie increased vulnerability in cerebellar disease. Second, our results show that intraregional analysis may be needed to identify differences in disease susceptibility and underlying molecular processes that may mitigate disease. Third, our work provides targets for further exploration of cerebellar heterogeneity and the impact the loss of heterogeneity may have on progression of cerebellar disease. We hope that the database of genes and pathways altered in a regional manner will inspire studies of increased disease vulnerability or disease resistance, to provide targets for successful treatments for SCA1 and other cerebellar diseases.
