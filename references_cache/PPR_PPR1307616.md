Abstract
Background
Tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) is a rare multisystem disorder resulting from pathogenic mutations in
the TSC1 and TSC2 genes, which induce constitutive hyperactivation of the mammalian target of
rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway. As a ﬁrst-line targeted therapeutic agent for TSC, the mTOR
inhibitor sirolimus is characterized by a narrow therapeutic window and substantial interindividual
variability in pharmacokinetic proﬁle.
Case Presentation:
This paper reports a 26-day-old female neonate diagnosed with TSC who harbored the CYP3A5 *3/*3
genotype associated with the poor metabolizer phenotype. Following administration of sirolimus at a
standard dose normalized by body surface area, the patient developed supratherapeutic sirolimus blood
concentrations accompanied by clinical toxicity manifested as persistent somnolence. Guided by the
results of pre-treatment pharmacogenomic testing, subsequent dose adjustment via reduction
successfully normalized the trough blood concentration of sirolimus and completely resolved the clinical
toxic symptoms.
Literature Review:
We conducted a systematic review of current clinical management strategies for TSC and sirolimus-
based pharmacotherapy, with a speciﬁc focus on the pharmacogenomic characteristics of this agent.
Genetic polymorphisms of CYP3A5, particularly the CYP3A5 *3 allele which exhibits a high carrier
frequency among East Asian populations, are identiﬁed as the primary genetic factor contributing to
interindividual variability in sirolimus metabolism. The integration of pre-treatment pharmacogenomic
testing, population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) modeling and therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) into a
precision medicine framework can signiﬁcantly improve the safety and therapeutic eﬃcacy among
patients with tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC), particularly in neonatal and infant populations.
Conclusion
The present case report combined with systematic literature review collectively demonstrates that
pharmacogenomic testing of CYP3A5 and CYP3A4 is crucially required for the implementation of
individualized precision dosing of sirolimus in pediatric tuberous sclerosis complex.
Introduction
Page 2/12

Tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) is an autosomal dominant neurocutaneous disorder with an
estimated incidence ranging from 1/6,000 to 1/10,000[1], and is classiﬁed as a rare disease. The
pathogenesis of TSC is attributed to loss-of-function pathogenic variants in either the TSC1 or TSC2
gene, which disrupts the assembly and function of the tuberous sclerosis protein complex, abolishes the
physiological inhibitory effect on mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), and ultimately
leads to hyperactivation of the mTOR signaling pathway in multiple tissues. This sustained
hyperactivation promotes aberrant cell proliferation and differentiation, resulting in the development of
multifocal hamartomas [2]throughout the body. TSC presents a highly heterogeneous clinical phenotype
and typically involves multiple organ systems, including the central nervous system (epilepsy, intellectual
disability, autism spectrum disorder), skin (hypomelanotic macules), kidneys, and heart (rhabdomyoma)
[3].
Over the past decade, the clinical application of mTOR inhibitors represented by sirolimus has
fundamentally transformed the clinical management paradigm of TSC, the paradigm has shifted from
purely symptomatic management to etiology-oriented targeted therapy[4]. Nevertheless, sirolimus is
characterized by a narrow therapeutic window and signiﬁcant interindividual pharmacokinetic
heterogeneity; suboptimal dosing can easily lead to treatment failure or severe adverse toxicity[5].
As summarized in Table 1, the metabolic elimination of sirolimus is primarily mediated by CYP3A5. Loss-
of-function genetic variants (e.g., *3) are the predominant genetic factor contributing to elevated
interindividual variation in blood drug concentrations, and such variants exhibit a relatively high
prevalence among East Asian populations[6]. Although the association between CYP3A5 variants and
sirolimus pharmacokinetics has been well-validated by numerous studies and systematic reviews in the
context of renal transplantation[6], relevant data are extremely limited for tuberous sclerosis complex
(TSC), especially among neonatal populations. Critically, CYP3A4 activity is negligible in the neonatal
stage, such that sirolimus clearance is almost completely dependent on CYP3A5[7] function. This
developmental characteristic substantially exacerbates the clinical risk conferred by a poor metabolizer
genotype in neonates. Accordingly, the TSC-affected neonate reported in this study, who developed
sirolimus toxicity secondary to the CYP3A5*3/*3 genotype, represents a high-risk case located at the
intersection of an evidence gap deﬁned by a speciﬁc age group, a speciﬁc disease, and a high-risk
genotype. This case highlights the critical clinical signiﬁcance of preemptive pharmacogenomic testing
for this vulnerable population.

1. Case Presentation
1.1 Case Introduction
A 26-day-old female infant was admitted to our hospital due to 22 days of hypomelanotic macules and
12 days of recurrent seizures. The infant was delivered via cesarean section at 38 + 1 weeks of gestation
with a birth weight of 3.15 kg, and the indication for cesarean section was abnormal fetal heart
monitoring results (arrhythmia and bradycardia). During neonatal hospitalization, echocardiographic
examination detected cardiac rhabdomyomas, and funduscopic examination identiﬁed retinal nodules.
Hypomelanotic macules were observed on the 4th day after birth, and cranial magnetic resonance
imaging (MRI) revealed the presence of cortical tubers.
Page 4/12

1.2 Diagnostic Evaluation
A clinical diagnosis of Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC) was formulated based on the pathognomonic
clinical manifestations of the disease, including hypomelanotic macules, cardiac rhabdomyomas,
subependymal nodules, cortical tubers and retinal nodules. Genetic analysis via whole-exome
sequencing (WES) conﬁrmed a pathogenic mutation in the TSC2 gene (NM_000548.5: c.2713C > T,
p.Arg905Trp), which provided a deﬁnitive genetic diagnosis of TSC. The patient presented with recurrent
seizures, and video-electroencephalography (vEEG) recording detected abnormal epileptiform
discharges corresponding to focal seizure activity, thus an additional diagnosis of TSC-associated focal
epilepsy was established.
1.3 Treatment, Adverse Events, and Dose Adjustments
Targeted therapy with oral sirolimus solution was initiated at a standard starting dose of 0.2 mg once
daily. After the initiation of treatment, the infant developed central nervous system depression
characterized by somnolence and diminished responsiveness. Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM)
detected signiﬁcantly elevated sirolimus trough concentrations of 28.1 ng/mL and 29.4 ng/mL, which
substantially exceeded the recommended therapeutic target range of 5–15 ng/mL.
To elucidate the underlying etiology of this drug toxicity, pharmacogenomic genotyping was conducted.
No pathogenic variants were identiﬁed in the CYP3A4 gene, while the patient was conﬁrmed to be a
homozygous carrier of the rs776746 AA genotype (CYP3A5 *3/*3 genotype). This poor metabolizer
phenotype markedly impaired the metabolic clearance of sirolimus in the patient.
1.4 Individualized Dosage Adjustment and Clinical Follow-
up
Based on the pharmacogenomic detection results, the sirolimus dosage was gradually reduced to 0.1
mg once daily. Follow-up TDM showed that the trough concentration of sirolimus successfully decreased
to 10.1 ng/mL, and the patient’s somnolence completely resolved. This clinical case demonstrates the
determinant role of CYP3A5 genotype in regulating systemic sirolimus exposure.
During subsequent follow-up, despite continuous sirolimus administration, the patient developed drug-
resistant epilepsy and underwent resection of the right frontal epileptogenic focus. The patient remained
seizure-free for a period after surgery, but seizures recurred later, especially during episodes of febrile
illness. The anti-seizure regimen was adjusted via sequential addition of valproic acid and lacosamide,
after which seizure frequency reduced and seizure control was achieved. The patient also exhibited ﬁne
motor impairment of the left hand, and the patient is currently receiving standardized systematic
physical rehabilitation, with a gradual recovery of motor function. This clinical course demonstrates that
even with precision pharmacotherapy, a proportion of TSC-associated epilepsy still requires a
multimodal and comprehensive management strategy.
1.5 Discussion
Page 5/12

This patient represents a clear clinical example of neonatal sirolimus toxicity induced by the CYP3A5
poor metabolizer genotype. The occurrence of severe drug toxicity under the standard dosing regimen
based on body surface area directly veriﬁes the signiﬁcant effect of the CYP3A5 3/*3* genotype on drug
metabolism in neonates, and emphasizes the direct clinical application value of genotype-guided drug
dosing. The ﬁndings of this study strongly support the implementation of preemptive CYP3A5
genotyping before sirolimus administration, especially for neonates and infants with immature metabolic
systems and highly variable drug clearance [6][7]capacity.
2. Overview of TSC and Sirolimus Therapy
2.1 The mTOR Signaling Pathway and Paradigm Shift in
TSC Treatment
TSC is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder caused by pathogenic mutations in the TSC1 or TSC2
gene. These mutations lead to loss of function of the TSC protein complex, which acts as a key negative
regulator of mTORC1 under physiological conditions. Inactivation of the TSC protein complex results in
persistent aberrant hyperactivation of the mTOR signaling pathway in multiple tissues, thereby
promoting abnormal cell proliferation and lesion formation of multisystem hamartoma development.
Accordingly, TSC is recognized as the canonical paradigm of "mTORopathy". Accumulating evidence
indicates that TSC2 mutations typically confer a broader spectrum of clinical involvement and a more
severe phenotypic presentation compared with TSC1 mutations[8].
In-depth elucidation of this core molecular pathogenic mechanism has driven a fundamental paradigm
shift in the clinical management of TSC: the ﬁeld has transitioned from conventional symptomatic
interventions (i.e., traditional anti-seizure medications, surgical resection of lesions) to a new era of
etiology-directed therapy centered on mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors, including
sirolimus and everolimus[4]. By directly inhibiting the hyperactivated mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1)
signaling, these agents block disease progression at the molecular origin. Nevertheless, full realization
of their clinical utility requires not only the availability of the therapeutic agents themselves, but also the
implementation of precision management strategies and multidisciplinary collaboration across the
entire disease trajectory.
2.2 Applications and Challenges of Sirolimus in TSC
Clinical Management
As a prototypical mTORC1 inhibitor, sirolimus has exhibited consistent and substantial therapeutic
eﬃcacy against a wide range of TSC-associated complications, and has become a core component of
comprehensive TSC management. Its clinical indications in TSC treatment are extensive, including:
reducing the volume of or eliminating renal angiomyolipomas to reduce the risk of hemorrhage[9];
promoting the regression of cardiac rhabdomyomas to ameliorate hemodynamic obstruction[10]; and
inhibiting the progression of subependymal giant cell astrocytomas (SEGAs), thereby enabling avoidance
Page 6/12

or delay of neurosurgical intervention[4]. Comprehensive management strategies for TSC-associated
epilepsy—encompassing current systematic reviews have elaborated extensively on existing therapeutic
approaches, including mTOR inhibitors[11], ketogenic diet and surgical intervention.
Although sirolimus delivers [12]signiﬁcant therapeutic beneﬁts, its clinical application is constrained by a
narrow therapeutic window and substantial interindividual pharmacokinetic variability[5][13]. As a result,
achieving desirable eﬃcacy and safety is heavily dependent on precise individualized dosing. In essence,
sirolimus therapy is a typical application scenario of precision medicine, which requires clinical practice
to break through the limitation of conventional ﬁxed-dose administration regimens. To optimize dosing
strategy and maintain blood drug concentration within the safe therapeutic window, it is necessary to
actively incorporate therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM), pharmacogenomics (e.g., CYP3A5 genotype)
and population pharmacokinetic models into clinical decision-making. Long-term follow-up data in
pediatric populations show that standardized drug administration does not cause adverse effects on
physical development, but close monitoring of adverse reactions such as stomatitis and hyperlipidemia
is still necessary[14].
2.3 Early screening biomarkers to assist diagnosis and
comprehensive management
Early identiﬁcation and accurate diagnosis of tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) are prerequisites for
implementing effective intervention. Driven by in-depth research on the pathogenesis and progression of
TSC, diagnostic strategies have transformed from a single pattern relying on clinical manifestations to a
comprehensive scheme based on updated international consensus criteria[1]. This scheme integrates
clinical features, advanced imaging techniques (such as brain MRI and echocardiography) and genetic
testing, among which the detection of pathogenic
Pathogenic TSC1/TSC2 mutations have been established as an independent diagnostic criterion to
enable early and accurate diagnosis[3] of tuberous sclerosis complex. On this basis, the identiﬁcation of
predictive biomarkers has become a key research direction for improving clinical prognoses of patients.
2.3.1 Neurophysiological Biomarkers
Longitudinal video electroencephalography (vEEG) monitoring demonstrates that epileptiform
discharges often occur prior to the onset of clinical seizures in infant patients with TSC. Existing studies
show that the early occurrence of such discharges (especially within the ﬁrst several months after birth),
particularly multifocal epileptiform discharges, is a robust predictor of subsequent drug-resistant
epilepsy and adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes[15]. This ﬁnding provides a valuable therapeutic
window for implementing "preemptive" or preventive interventions in the pathophysiological process of
epileptogenesis.
2.3.2 Neuroimaging and Genetic Biomarkers
Page 7/12

Quantitative analysis of brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can accurately measure the volume
and lesion burden of cortical tubers and subependymal nodules, and has conﬁrmed a quantitative
correlation between these indicators and seizure severity as well as neurodevelopmental outcomes.
Meanwhile, speciﬁc genotypes such as TSC2 mutations can provide support for clinical risk stratiﬁcation
and prognosis prediction[8].
The comprehensive management of TSC is far from limited to monotherapy. Although mTOR inhibitors
act as a fundamental treatment option, their clinical application must be integrated into a lifelong
multidisciplinary collaborative management framework, which should cover departments including
pediatric neurology, epilepsy subspecialty, neurosurgery and nephrology, cardiology, dermatology,
genetic counseling, and rehabilitation psychology[16]. Treatment regimens require stratiﬁed stratiﬁcation
and multi-dimensional complementation: surgical intervention, neuromodulation therapy or ketogenic
diet is indicated for drug-resistant epilepsy; laser therapy or surgical resection is adopted for cutaneous
lesions; and behavioral intervention combined with educational support is implemented for TSC-
associated neuropsychiatric disorders (TAND)[17]. This individualized full-course management model,
with precision targeted therapy as the core and all necessary clinical modalities integrated, is an
inevitable approach to improving the long-term prognosis and quality of life of TSC patients.
3. Precision Sirolimus Therapy: Focus on Pharmacogenomics and
Pharmacokinetics
3.1 The Core Role of CYP3A5 Genetic Polymorphisms
Precise administration of sirolimus is heavily dependent on its pharmacokinetic characteristics. This
drug has a narrow therapeutic window and signiﬁcant inter-individual variability in blood drug
concentration[5]. Supratherapeutic exposure will induce adverse toxic reactions such as stomatitis,
myelosuppression, hyperlipidemia and central nervous system depression (consistent with the
somnolence symptom observed in this case), which often necessitates temporary treatment
interruption[13][14]. The metabolic clearance of sirolimus is mainly mediated by the CYP3A enzyme
system, among which CYP3A5 acts as the primary metabolic enzyme, while CYP3A4 plays a secondary
role[18].
Polymorphisms of the CYP3A5 gene determine the functional activity of the encoded enzyme: individuals
carrying the *3/*3 genotype are deﬁned as "poor metabolizers",exhibits a marked reduction in drug
clearance and increased blood drug concentration, which indicates that lower maintenance doses[6] are
necessary for this population. Notably, the allele frequency of this variant exceeds 70% in East Asian
populations, meaning this population has a high baseline risk of initial drug toxicity, a ﬁnding with
important clinical implications[6].
Page 8/12

Furthermore, although rare loss-of-function variants of CYP3A4 (such as *8 and *20) have extremely low
incidence, they can also severely impair the metabolic process of sirolimus. Existing case reports show
that patients carrying compound heterozygous loss-of-function variants of CYP3A4 can develop a
signiﬁcant increase in blood drug concentration and prolonged drug half-life under conventional dosage
regimens, eventually leading to severe drug toxicity[19]. This indicates that when patients develop severe
adverse drug reactions that cannot be explained by the CYP3A5*3/*3 genotype, more comprehensive
genetic detection and analysis for CYP3A4 should be performed.
3.2 Developmental Pharmacology and Drug Interaction
After oral absorption, sirolimus is extensively metabolized by cytochrome P450 (CYP) 3A enzymes in the
liver and intestinal mucosa, among which CYP3A5 and CYP3A4 are the core functional isoenzymes[18].
Developmental pharmacology further ampliﬁes the effect of these genotypes: CYP3A7 is the dominant
isoenzyme during the fetal period, and is gradually replaced by CYP3A4 after birth; CYP3A5 is the
primary functional CYP3A enzyme during the neonatal period, while the activity of CYP3A4 matures
gradually within the ﬁrst few months after birth[7][20]. Therefore, The neonate harboring the CYP3A5 *3*
genotype presents a dual impairment in sirolimus clearance: functional CYP3A5 is absent, and a mature
CYP3A4-mediated compensatory pathway is concurrently unavailable. At present, the majority of
existing pharmacogenomic evidence regarding sirolimus originates from studies of adult renal
transplant recipients[6], which leaves a signiﬁcant knowledge gap in the neonatal population. However,
TSC can be diagnosed in utero or during the neonatal period, and there is a clear therapeutic demand for
early targeted mTOR inhibition in this population[3][10]. Against this backdrop, our patient demonstrates
that CYP3A5 is the critical driver of sirolimus metabolism in neonates with TSC, where the *3/*3
genotype precipitated a clear toxicity event under standard dosing. To our knowledge, no identical
clinical scenario has been previously reported.
This ﬁnding emphasizes the need for prospective studies in the neonatal TSC population to establish
age- and genotype-stratiﬁed dosing algorithms, alongside a precision framework combining preemptive
genetic testing with TDM[21]. Furthermore, clinicians must remain vigilant regarding drug-drug
interactions, as various concomitant medications can alter sirolimus concentrations by inducing (e.g.,
rifampicin, carbamazepine) or inhibiting (e.g., ketoconazole, macrolide antibiotics) CYP3A enzymes[18]
[18]. PopPK model-driven dose optimization and remedial strategies offer additional validated support
for managing compliance-related dosing deviations[22] .
Conclusion
The diagnosis and management of Tuberous Sclerosis Complex have entered the era of precision
medicine. As the core targeted agent, maximizing the eﬃcacy of sirolimus while minimizing its toxicity
depends on a thorough understanding and proactive management of a patient's individual genetic proﬁle
and pharmacokinetic characteristics.
Page 9/12

This case report and literature review demonstrate that CYP3A5 genetic polymorphisms are key,
predictable drivers of interindividual variability in sirolimus exposure. Implementing an individualized
dosing strategy—led by preemptive CYP3A5 genotyping and supported by both therapeutic drug
monitoring (TDM) and population pharmacokinetic modeling—is vital to ensure safety and therapeutic
success for all patients with TSC, particularly neonates and infants with fragile drug clearance
capacities.
Moving forward, as more predictive biomarkers are validated and therapeutic options expand, TSC
management will become increasingly reﬁned, individualized, and systematic, ultimately achieving the
goal of improving long-term patient prognosis and quality of life. Although mTOR inhibitors are the
cornerstone of TSC therapy, comprehensive care extends far beyond a single drug. A complete
therapeutic framework must integrate multidisciplinary strategies, requiring close collaboration among
pediatric neurology, neurosurgery, rehabilitation, and psychiatry.
