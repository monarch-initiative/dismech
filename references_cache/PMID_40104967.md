INTRODUCTION: Spinal cord infarction has various clinical presentations,
depending on the vascular territory involved at the spinal cord level. The most
common symptom is bilateral weakness. However, unilateral hemiparesis can occur
if the sulcal artery is involved. Stroke is the most common and serious
cerebrovascular complication associated with kidney transplantation. Clinically
asymptomatic renal transplant recipients may experience increased intima-media
thickening, which is significantly associated with an increased prevalence of
cerebrovascular diseases.
CASE REPORT: Herein, we report the case of a 64-year-old adult male with a
history of kidney transplantation who presented with sudden-onset right-sided
hemiparesis. The patient presented with right shoulder pain, right fingertip
paresthesia, and recent dysuria, necessitating catheterization. T2-weighted
magnetic resonance imaging at the C2-C3 level revealed hyperintensity
corresponding to the right sulcal artery. Diffusion-weighted imaging revealed
hyperintensities corresponding to T2 signal abnormalities, with a decreased
apparent diffusion coefficient. The patient was diagnosed with cervical spinal
cord infarction involving the right sulcal artery at the C2-C3 level.
Subsequently, pulsed intravenous methylprednisolone and dual antiplatelet
treatment (aspirin and clopidogrel) were administered. The patient showed a
rapid and marked improvement in neurological function within 3 days.
CONCLUSION: The patient in this case report initially presented with unilateral
hemiparesis. The symptoms mimicked those of stroke, making accurate diagnosis
challenging. This case highlights the need to consider spinal cord infarction in
the differential diagnosis of patients presenting with unilateral hemiparesis.
In addition, our findings suggest that spinal cord infarction may be a long-term
complication in kidney transplant recipients.

The spinal cord receives blood from the arteries and is characterized by various anastomoses and variants. Spinal cord infarction is a condition characterized by various clinical presentations, depending on the vascular territory of the invaded spinal cord level. The most affected vessel is the anterior spinal artery, which may present with bilateral motor weakness. Unilateral weakness, an initial clinical manifestation of spinal cord infarction, is relatively rare and occurs less than half as often as bilateral weakness or quadriparesis.1 In a patient presenting with unilateral weakness, a brain lesion is initially suspected before a spinal cord lesion in the differential diagnosis, which makes diagnosis and treatment difficult.

Therefore, further studies are warranted to fully understand the etiology and mechanisms of spinal cord infarction, as they remain unclear. However, several studies have reported the likelihood of various neurological complications after kidney transplantation.2,3 Of these complications, cerebrovascular disease is the most common and life-threatening in renal transplant recipients. This report presents the case of a patient who developed an idiopathic spinal cord infarction after renal transplantation. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the possibility of spinal cord infarction as a long-term complication of kidney transplantation.

A 64-year-old man presented to a local community hospital with right-sided unilateral weakness that had developed 9 days before hospital visitation. The patient exhibited weakness in the right arm and leg, while facial function remained normal.

In addition, on the same day, the patient presented with right shoulder pain and right fingertip paresthesia, which had a sudden onset and progressively worsened. Three days after the onset, paresthesia developed in the fingertips of the left hand. The patient experienced dysuria on the eighth day of symptoms and was catheterized to address the urinary issue. The patient had a history of kidney transplantation due to chronic renal failure 4 years prior, and was receiving oral tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and prednisone for maintenance. Furthermore, the patient was a nonsmoker, with no medical history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, or diabetes. In addition, the patient had no history of recent trauma or viral infection. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain did not reveal any abnormalities; therefore, the patient was transferred to our facility for further evaluation of the sudden unilateral weakness. The patient’s vital signs measured at our facility were as follows: blood pressure, 107/75 mm Hg; heart rate, 83 beats/min; respiratory rate, 20 breaths/min; and body temperature, 36.9°C. The patient was alert and well-oriented; cranial nerve function was intact. Although neurological examination showed that the patient’s left side was normal, the Medical Research Council (MRC) grades of the right arm and leg were 3/5, indicating motor weakness. Pain, temperature sensation, vibration, and proprioception were preserved; however, slight hypoesthesia was observed in both the fingertips and soles of the feet. Reflexes were bilaterally 2+ in the biceps, triceps, brachioradialis, patella, and Achilles tendons. Hoffman sign, Babinski sign, and ankle clonus test results were negative. A digital rectal examination revealed a slightly decreased anal tone, and the patient developed urinary incontinence; therefore, a urethral catheter was inserted. Hematological and biochemical findings were within normal ranges. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed the following results: white blood cells, 0 cells/mm3; red blood cells, 0 cells/mm3; total protein, 62.2 (normal: 15–45) mg/dL; and glucose, 58 (normal: 70–110) mg/dL. The CSF bacterial and virological assays were negative. Oligoclonal bands were not detected, and IgG synthesis did not increase. Autoimmune antibodies against vasculitis, including antinuclear antibody (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), complement levels, cardiolipin, and lupus antibodies, were unremarkable.

Sagittal T2-weighted imaging revealed a pencil-like hyperintensity extending from the C2 to the C3 level. Diffusion-weighted image (DWI) showed hyperintensity corresponding to T2 signal abnormalities, with a decreased apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC). On the axial T2-weighted image at the C2–3 level, ventrolateral paramedian hyperintensity was predominantly observed in the gray matter, corresponding to the territory of the sulcal artery on the right side (Fig. 1). Therefore, the patient was diagnosed with a cervical spinal cord infarction involving the right sulcal artery at the C2–3 level. Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) of the brain and aorta revealed no abnormalities. The carotid arteries were also assessed, showing no significant stenosis or other issues. In addition, transthoracic echocardiography and 24-hour Holter monitoring results were normal, and intima-media thickness was evaluated with no signs of thickening. Autonomic nervous system testing revealed classic orthostatic hypotension and postganglionic sudomotor dysfunction, suggesting a sympathetic pathway dysfunction. After diagnosis, pulsed intravenous methylprednisolone was administered for 3 days to protect the spinal cord and promote neurological recovery. The patient’s right shoulder pain and bilateral fingertip paresthesia resolved during this time. Afterward, dual antiplatelet treatment(aspirin and clopidogrel) was initiated to prevent further vascular events. Motor weakness in the patient’s right limbs gradually improved to 5-/5 in the right arm and 5-/5 in the right leg, based on the MRC grade; however, urinary incontinence remained. The patient was discharged on the 21st day of hospitalization and continued rehabilitation with consistent bladder training.

Spinal cord infarction has various clinical manifestations, depending on the invaded vascular territory.3 The spinal MRI findings of the patient in this case suggested right branch infarction of the sulcal artery. The sulcal artery is a small vessel that originates from the anterior spinal artery and travels along the anterior median fissure, alternating between the left and right sides. Patients with unilateral sulcal artery infarction present with ipsilateral hemiparesis below the level of infarction and contralateral dissociated sensation deficits, referred to as sulcal artery syndrome.3 In the present case, the patient initially presented with ipsilateral limb weakness, with bilateral fingertip hypoesthesia and dysuria. The initial presentation posed a diagnostic challenge because it resembled that of a stroke, which may present with an ischemic lesion and weakness on either the ipsilateral or contralateral side. In the present case, a brain MRI was performed as an initial examination to rule out other possible etiologies of ischemic stroke. After confirming the absence of a significant lesion, an additional spinal cord workup was performed, leading to the diagnosis of spinal cord infarction. Therefore, spinal cord infarction should be considered a differential diagnosis in patients with unilateral weakness.

Spinal cord infarctions have several causative factors.2 The most common causes are disease or surgery involving the aorta and vertebral arteries; however, the exact etiology in ∼23% of the cases remains unknown.2 The patient in the present case report had no major vascular risk factors besides old age (64 y), no MRA findings suggestive of dissection or embolism, and no history of trauma or infection. However, the patient had a history of kidney transplantation, which is of significance. Among the various neurological complications that can occur after kidney transplantation, stroke is the most common and dangerous cerebrovascular complication.4 Oliveras et al5 reported that renal transplant recipients developed stroke ∼49 months after transplantation, with a higher prevalence in renal transplant recipients than in the general population with normal renal function. Other studies have shown that renal transplant recipients with no cardiovascular symptoms and normal blood pressure may present with noticeable intima-media thickening, despite normal blood pressure levels, and that the prevalence of cerebrovascular disease increases significantly with increasing intima-media thickening.6,7 Therefore, renal transplant recipients without clinical cardiovascular symptoms, as in this case, have a risk of cerebrovascular events, such as spinal cord infarction. However, the pathophysiology of the transition to post-transplant cerebrovascular events in renal transplant recipients remains largely unknown.

In conclusion, the present report describes the case of a patient with a history of kidney transplantation who presented with acute unilateral weakness as an initial symptom. Spinal cord infarction and stroke should be considered as differential diagnoses in patients with unilateral weakness as an initial symptom. Furthermore, this case report revealed that unexplained spinal cord infarction may develop as a long-term complication of kidney transplantation. However, further studies are needed to understand its mechanism and pathophysiology.
