# Overlap Diagnostic Odyssey and Full Mouth Rehabilitation of a Juvenile Patient With IFIH1-Related Disorder: A Case of Aicardi-Goutières and Singleton Merten Syndromes Overlap.
**Authors:** Friedman C, Ari T, Ünsal G, Balci T, Morrison M, Ari N
**Journal:** Spec Care Dentist (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1111/scd.70026](https://doi.org/10.1111/scd.70026)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: This case report describes the prosthetic rehabilitation of a 15-year-old female patient with a rare IFIH1-related interferonopathy, presenting features from both Aicardi-Goutières Syndrome (AGS) and Singleton-Merten Syndrome (SMS). The report highlights the unique dental and maxillofacial challenges, and the multidisciplinary treatment approach required in such patients.

METHODS: The patient underwent a comprehensive prosthetic rehabilitation, which included two stages of extractions, followed by the fabrication of a complete upper denture and a two-implant-retained mandibular overdenture. Pre- and post-treatment assessments, including panoramic radiographs and cone beam computed tomography (CBCT), were utilized to guide treatment.

RESULTS: Initial assessments revealed root formation anomalies, multiple impacted teeth, internal resorption, and associated dental anomalies. Following extractions and ridge augmentation, two freestanding implants were successfully placed in the anterior mandible, with no complications during osseointegration. The patient reported satisfaction with the function, esthetics, and stability of her dental prostheses.

CONCLUSIONS: This case underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach for managing dental anomalies and bone fragility in patients with IFIH1-related disorders. Early genetic diagnosis and thorough treatment planning are critical for successful prosthetic rehabilitation and optimal patient outcomes.

Aicardi‐Goutières Syndrome (AGS, OMIM# PS225750) is a rare, multisystem genetic disorder that primarily affects the brain, immune system, and skin. It often presents with early‐onset neurological symptoms such as irritability, fever, abnormal muscle contractions, and developmental delays. AGS is typically diagnosed in infancy and can present with a range of severity, from mild to severe intellectual and physical disabilities. Pathogenic variants in several different genes can cause this condition (ADAR, IFIH1, TREX1, RNASEH2A, and others), in autosomal dominant or autosomal recessive inheritance patterns, mostly via an inappropriate immune response and damage to various tissues, particularly the brain's white matter [ 1 ]. Although AGS is typically known as an early‐onset encephalopathy, variants in IFIH1 have been seen in about 3% of individuals with AGS and generally show a later‐onset form, sometimes with remarkably preserved neurological function. Pure spastic paraparesis or childhood‐onset paraparesis with normal intellect has been described with IFIH variants.

The maxillofacial implications of AGS are significant, given the disorder's impact on neurological function and overall development. Patients with AGS often exhibit microcephaly, which can influence craniofacial growth and development. Additionally, the presence of chronic neurological symptoms and potential muscle spasticity can complicate oral health maintenance, leading to increased risks of dental caries, periodontal disease, and difficulties in receiving dental treatment. Furthermore, additional risks are created for procedures carried out under anesthesia because of dystrophic skeletal mass [ 2 ]. Overall, the structural and functional abnormalities in the jaws and teeth necessitate specialized dental care and intervention strategies tailored to the individual needs of these patients [ 3 ].

Singleton‐Merten Syndrome (SMS, OMIM #182250 #616298) is another rare genetic disorder that primarily affects the skeletal system, teeth, and skin. The syndrome can be caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in the IFIH1 or DDX58 genes, both of which lead to dysregulation of the immune response and subsequent systemic effects [ 4 ]. SMS is characterized by progressive calcification of the aorta and heart valves, and skeletal abnormalities such as osteoporosis. In the maxillofacial region, SMS can result in severe dental anomalies. Patients often experience delayed or failed dental eruption, early onset of periodontitis, and extensive root resorption, ultimately leading to the premature loss of teeth.

These dental issues, compounded by bone fragility, complicate both surgical and prosthetic rehabilitation. Effective management requires a comprehensive and multidisciplinary approach to address both the dental and skeletal manifestations of the syndrome [ 5 ].

IFIH1 encodes interferon‐induced helicase C domain‐containing protein 1(IFIH1) which induces a type I interferon‐mediated antiviral response. Recent studies have characterized IFIH1 gain‐of‐function variants to various interferonopathies; conditions characterized by an excessive and dysregulated immune response. It was initially thought that SMS was a distinct disorder related to IFIH1. However, further reports indicated that SMS and AGS phenotypes seen associated with IFIH1 gain‐of‐function variants are indeed part of the same disease spectrum, with features from either condition [ 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 ]. The phenotype in these individuals would have features from either condition, complicating the clinical diagnostic process, unless unbiased genome‐wide sequencing techniques, such as exome sequencing, are utilized. Besides SMS, IFIH1 mutations are also associated with disorders like Systemic Lupus Erythematosus, which can lead to oral health issues such as periodontitis and tooth loss. Chronic Atypical Neutrophilic Dermatosis with Lipodystrophy and Elevated Temperature (CANDLE) syndrome is another related condition, presenting with symptoms like skin lesions and joint pain, and may include oral manifestations such as mucosal ulcerations. These syndromes also illustrate the wide‐ranging effects of IFIH1 mutations, impacting both systemic and oral health [ 1 , 4 , 7 , 10– 12 ]. The management of multiple impacted permanent teeth often involves a comprehensive treatment strategy, particularly when associated with syndromes, metabolic disorders, or hormonal imbalances. In such instances, a meticulous, multidisciplinary strategy is essential for optimal patient care.

The treatment paradigm for these complex cases encompasses a systematic examination of primary dentition, the extraction of any supernumerary teeth, and facilitating the eruption of permanent teeth through orthodontic traction or surgical exposure [ 13 ]. Should any impacted tooth remain unerupted despite these interventions, extraction followed by appropriate replacement options will be considered, ensuring not only an aesthetically pleasing outcome but also the functional integrity of the patient's dentition.

Loss of teeth ends up in loss of function, and lack of normal alveolar growth, in conjunction with unpleasant esthetics that hampers the psychosocial development of the young child. Traditionally, the management of single tooth loss in the young child has done by conservative means such as Maryland Bridge, resin‐bonded restorations, or removable prosthesis in cases of multiple missing teeth.

Implant placement in a young child would be an ideal method of treatment for the absence of teeth. They restore the function, preserve the alveolar bone and give excellent esthetics, restoring the child's confidence, and social acceptability. Parents are usually overzealous and keen to get this treatment done as soon as offered the suggestion. However, dental implant placement in children has special consideration, the impending growth, which needs to be understood before commencing on the treatment plan.

The primary concerns of implants in young patient are danger of them becoming embedded, relocated, or displaced as the jaw grows [ 14 ]. As transverse growth in the region of the lower incisors and canines ceases early, mandibular anterior implants have better prognosis in young patients than those placed in other areas of the mandible [ 15 ].

Successful implant treatment in children has been achieved by several clinicians when they incorporated a multidisciplinary approach in their treatment plan [ 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 ].

This case report aims to present a unique and extreme case of a juvenile patient diagnosed with IFIH1‐related disorder with exome sequencing, who exhibited features from both AGS and SMS. The dual presence of these syndromes poses significant challenges for maxillofacial and dental treatment. This report focuses on the diagnostic phase and detailed prosthetic rehabilitation of the patient, which involved the use of a maxillary complete denture, and a two‐implant‐retained mandibular overdenture. To our knowledge, this is the first documented case report addressing both the diagnostic challenges in a case of overlap between AGS and SMS.

The following section delineates the specific hurdles encountered during the medical diagnostic phase, as well as the obstacles faced in the dental treatment phase, in pursuit of achieving a favorable outcome.

A 15‐year‐old female patient was referred to a private prosthodontics clinic primarily for esthetic concerns related to her dental condition, as well as for the rehabilitation of missing teeth and improvement of her chewing function. This case report focuses on clinical care provided to the patient, with no experimental procedures conducted. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient's guardian for the publication of all relevant medical and dental information, including clinical photographs and radiographs, in accordance with ethical reporting standards.

The patient exhibited distinctive facial brown macules, a common feature in syndromic conditions such as AGS and SMS. These hyperpigmented macules were predominantly located on her cheeks (Figure 1).

Extraoral image of the patient showing small pinpoint brown macules (lentigines), predominantly on the cheeks. Lentigines are characteristic dermatological features of syndromic conditions such as Singleton‐Merten Syndrome (SMS).

This patient was first seen by a dental specialist around 7.5 years of age referred for pediatric dental care following prior evaluation in Montreal, where she had received a diagnosis of cerebral palsy. Upon referral, the primary concerns included extreme anxiety and delayed dental eruption. A panoramic radiograph revealed multiple dental anomalies characterized by delayed eruption of several teeth and abnormal root formation in numerous permanent teeth. Under general anesthesia, the patient underwent extraction of several primary teeth and diode laser exposure of unerupted permanent anterior and posterior teeth. She was subsequently referred to the Thames Valley Children's Center and a periodontist for further assessment of her medical and dental conditions, alongside a psychologist for her generalized anxiety. The implementation of desensitization techniques and intensive behavioral support enabled the administration of future dental care, occasionally assisted by nitrous oxide sedation. Approximately 6 months post‐initial intervention, an orthodontic consultation recommended the orthodontic extrusion of upper anterior teeth. However, continued delayed dental development prompted further evaluation by a pediatric endocrinologist, who found no identifiable underlying cause and did not pursue genetic testing. The patient's condition was further complicated by the emergence of severe, treatment‐resistant psoriasis necessitating involvement with a pediatric dermatologist (Figure 2a,b). Given the potential ectodermal connections between her dermatological and dental issues, a referral for a second opinion was sought. By this time, extensive periodontal breakdown had necessitated the extraction of her anterior lower teeth, likely exacerbated by her occlusal trauma and overarching medical condition. Despite continued dermatological interventions, her skin condition remained uncontrolled. Eventually, referral to a pediatric rheumatologist facilitated a genetic consultation, leading to a diagnosis after 4 years of navigating the medical system. This case underscores the critical importance of early diagnosis and coordinated care, as the patient endured extensive, potentially preventable, medical and dental interventions.

(a) A photograph shows the extent of the patient's severe psoriasis, characterized by thick, scaly plaques and erythema on the lower limbs. (b) A photograph showcasing extensive, chronic psoriasis affecting the arm.

When seen in Medical Genetics at age 12, given her multisystem issues, the patient underwent exome sequencing as a trio, which identified a heterozygous (on one copy), de novo (new, not inherited from parents), likely pathogenic (disease causing) variant in the IFIH1 gene. The specific variant was reported as c.995 G>C, p. Gly332Ala. Although this exact variant had not been reported before, the characteristics of the variant (de novo, missense, affecting a Glycine residue) were sufficient to qualify for the likely pathogenic classification, as per the ACMG Criteria [ 19 ]. Based on this finding, she received a diagnosis of IFIH1‐related disorder with features from mild AGS (pure spastic paraplegia) and SMS (skin and dental findings).

Upon initial assessment, a panoramic radiograph (Figure 3) revealed several significant findings indicative of a complex dental syndrome. Anomalies in root formation were observed in all permanent teeth. Impacted second and third molars, with shorter roots, were observed in all four quadrants. Additionally, teeth 34, 35, 44, and 45 were also impacted. There was no sign of periodontal pathology, so the initial plan was to retain the teeth. However, the patient later developed pain due to internal resorption, complicating the conservative treatment plan and extending the treatment duration. At this point, a referral was made to an oral and maxillofacial surgeon (OMS) for consultation.

Panoramic radiograph revealing root formation anomalies and impacted teeth. The image highlights the complex dental anomalies associated with the patient's syndromes, including impacted second and third molars with shortened roots and impactions in all four quadrants.

The OMS initially hoped to remove only symptomatic teeth in an effort to preserve bone structure and avoid damage to adjacent maxillary and mandibular vital structures, such as the maxillary sinus and the inferior alveolar neurovascular bundle, respectively. As the patient's condition progressed, however, the remaining teeth became symptomatic one by one, necessitating a shift toward complete dental clearance. Following re‐evaluation of the patient's condition, considering both AGS and SMS, this approach was deemed more appropriate. A skeletal age assessment, using a wrist radiograph, revealed that the patient's skeletal maturity was delayed by approximately 4 years. A multidisciplinary consultation, including a pediatrician, confirmed that the mandibular anterior region had matured sufficiently, justifying the decision for complete clearance and subsequent placement of two freestanding implants in the anterior mandible. This decision was further supported by similar cases in the literature, where implant treatment was successfully implemented in patients with ectodermal dysplasia [ 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 ].

The patient's family was informed about the current literature regarding mandibular growth and implant placement in juvenile patients. Research shows that the transverse growth of the mandible completes early, around the first year of life, with limited changes occurring afterward through remodeling [ 20 , 21 , 22 ].

This makes the anterior mandible an optimal site for osseointegrated implants before skeletal maturation, as it presents fewer growth variables [ 16 , 20 , 23 ].

After reviewing this information, the patient's family accepted the treatment plan for the placement of two freestanding implants in the anterior mandible, and informed consent was obtained prior to the procedure.

The initial treatment plan involved preserving teeth 55, 11, 21, and partially erupted 26 and 12, along with all the impacted upper teeth, to support a maxillary overdenture (Figure 4). For the mandible, the plan was to extract all erupted and impacted lower anterior teeth followed by immediate ridge augmentation with allogeneic bone to prepare for future implant placement. The impacted mandibular posterior teeth were to be retained for both surgical and prosthetic considerations: to prevent damage to the inferior alveolar neurovascular bundle, and to preserve bone, as the mandibular surface‐bearing area was minimal. The case was classified as Class IV complexity based on the Complete Edentulous Prosthodontic Diagnostic Index with presence of both type D Maxilla and Mandible. The teeth 44 and 34 were planned to be retained due to their deep positioning in the bone and close proximity to the inferior alveolar nerve/mental foramina. Interestingly, there was a fistula tract extending from impacted teeth 44 and 34 to the surface of the alveolar ridge at each site (Figure 5a–d).

Coronal (a, b) and sagittal (c, d) cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) images. IAN, inferior alveolar nerve; FT, fistula tract; MN, mental nerve.

The first extraction session took place with the OMS in July 2023 under intravenous (IV) sedation, and ridge augmentation with allograft was performed in the anterior mandible (Figures 6 and 7). During the 4‐month healing period, several remaining teeth became symptomatic due to either internal resorption or fistula tract formation leading to their sequential removal. At the request of the patient and her parents, complete dental clearance was ultimately pursued. This was initially avoided due to the many impacted teeth in close proximity to the inferior alveolar nerve and maxillary sinus. After thorough assessment and planning with preoperative cone beam computed tomography (CBCT), the final extraction session was completed in February 2024. Fortunately, there were no complications involving oro‐antral communication or nerve paresthesia. Fabrication of conventional complete upper and lower dentures (CUD and CLD) was initiated after the first extraction appointment, but treatment was paused to allow post‐extraction healing of the alveolar ridges.

Panoramic radiograph taken after the first extraction session, showing the remaining teeth and augmented anterior mandibular ridge.

CBCT images taken after the first extraction session, showing the remaining teeth and augmented anterior mandibular ridge.

Given the complexity of the case, printed trial dentures were fabricated to finalize tooth positions before processing (Figure 8). These were also used while the dentures were sent to the dental lab for a denture reline procedure. A follow‐up panoramic radiograph revealed healthy surrounding bone structures, as evidenced by the visible lamina dura of the extracted teeth, indicating that bone healing had occurred uneventfully despite the IFIH1‐related disorders and the associated complex tooth impactions (Figure 9).

Intraoral image of printed trial upper and lower complete dentures (CDs) used for determining final tooth positions before processing.

Post‐extraction panoramic radiograph showing the patient in an edentulous state after full mouth extraction. The image displays healthy surrounding bone structures, as indicated by the visible lamina dura of the extracted teeth.

The prosthodontic plan involved placing two freestanding implants in the anterior mandible to support an implant‐retained overdenture. Periapical radiographs confirmed successful osseointegration of these implants (Straumann NC 3.3 × 12 mm), with no complications observed (Figure 10). After a 4‐month healing period, osseointegration was confirmed by the OMS, and prosthetic treatment proceeded. Healing abutments were removed, tissue heights measured, and appropriate Novaloc abutments (Straumann) were selected. The lower denture was then sent for hard reline and attachment of titanium housings corresponding to the Novaloc abutments. During the delivery appointment, the patient expressed satisfaction with the increased retention of the lower overdenture after insertion of the white retention inserts (Figures 11 and 12). A 1‐month follow‐up appointment revealed healthy peri‐implant soft tissues with no complications (Figure 13), and the patient stated that she was satisfied with the esthetics, stability, and retention of her dentures.

This case report highlights the presence of root formation anomalies and early tooth loss in a patient with IFIH1‐related condition with features of AGS and SMS. Research shows that mutations in the IFIH1 and DDX58 genes activate the type I interferonopathy (IFN‐I) signaling pathway, a key factor in the dental anomalies seen in these syndromes. Elevated IFN‐I scores in children with these mutations underscore the genetic basis of these conditions [ 1 ].

Novel genetic technologies, such as next generation sequencing has revolutionized clinical genetics practice by allowing robust, unbiased, and accurate genome‐wide sequencing to uncover Mendelian disorders in a timely manner. The diagnostic odyssey of individuals with rare genetic disorders, especially ones with multisystem issues, have significantly shortened since the introduction and availability of genome‐wide sequencing. However, access to genetic services is still limited by lack of awareness or expertise in many countries, including Canada [ 22 ]. Genetic testing is crucial for accurately diagnosing and managing rare conditions such as AGS and SMS, as illustrated in our case and many instances in literature, including a child with AGS type 7, who exhibited severe neurological and developmental delays due to an IFIH1 mutation [ 3 ]. Emralino et al. [ 3 ] explored the role of inflammation in these syndromes, suggesting that inflammatory processes could exacerbate dental issues like root resorption [ 4 ]. The systemic manifestations of these syndromes, such as glaucoma, also highlight the need for comprehensive care, including early dental evaluations to prevent long‐term complications [ 5 ].

A case reported by Hasegawa et al. [ 11 ] with a novel IFIH1 variant showed overlapping features of SMS and AGS, including premature tooth loss. Further studies, such as those by Gosu et al. [ 24 ] and Xiao et al. [ 24 ], emphasized the dental challenges posed by these mutations, suggesting potential therapeutic targets. Onizawa et al. [ 26 ] developed a mouse model that supports targeting microglial activation to manage symptoms in AGS.

The variability in clinical presentations, as noted by Rice et al. [ 9 ], underlines the need for accurate diagnosis and individualized management approaches. Potential treatments like Janus kinase inhibitors and reverse transcriptase inhibitors could help manage dental symptoms in AGS and SMS [ 8 , 27 , 28 ]. Yu et al. [ 27 , 28 ] and others have provided structural insights into these mutations, which could inform targeted therapies. The genetic overlap between AGS and SMS, as highlighted by Buers et al. [ 8 , 10 ] and Bursztejn et al. [ 7 ], underscores the importance of early genetic assessment and testing in getting to a timely diagnosis and guiding the clinical management and treatment planning for such complex cases [ 6 ].

Tooth loss in children and adolescents, whether due to trauma or extraction of impacted teeth in syndromic patients, can result in the absence of some or all permanent teeth. This situation can lead to various challenges, including cosmetic concerns, functional difficulties, problems with mastication, and speech impairments. Such issues can significantly impact the social and psychological well‐being of children and adolescents during their developmental years. Therefore, it is crucial to address the oral rehabilitation needs of these young patients who are experiencing edentulism.

Osseointegration with dental implants is well‐documented as a safe and predictable method of tooth replacement. However, all scientific studies about this have been performed in adults, when the dynamics of growth and development is not an issue.

Several clinicians have successfully achieved implant treatment in children by integrating a multidisciplinary approach into their treatment plans [ 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 ].

A case reported by Montanari et al. [ 15 ] involving a child with ectodermal dysplasia demonstrated successful implant‐supported overdenture treatment, with no adverse effects on implant positioning despite continued mandibular growth. After 3 years of follow‐up, the mandibular implant‐supported overdenture was well accepted by the patient who reported excellent masticatory and esthetic improvements.

Guckes et al. [ 16 ] described a case of 3‐year‐old patient with ectodermal dysplasia in which dental implants located in the mandible and maxilla have not moved despite growth. During the 5‐year follow‐up, the relative position of four implants within the anterior mandible remains unchanged as mandibular growth takes place in the rami and condyles, not in the anterior mandible.

The treatment outcome of multiple implants placement in a patient with oligodontia was analyzed by Becktor et al. [ 17 ] 12 years after the treatment. At age 8 years, 8 months, five implants were placed between the mental foramina and implant supported fixed prosthesis was provided. They concluded that only forward rotation of mandible had an effect on the implants by changing their angulation during the 12‐year follow‐up which can be compensated by revising the denture.

This case report illustrates the successful application of a multidisciplinary treatment strategy for a patient with an IFIH1‐related condition, specifically AGS and SMS. After comprehensive evaluations, the patient was deemed suitable for implant placement in the anterior mandible and for an implant‐retained mandibular overdenture. Through careful preoperative analysis and precise execution of the treatment plan, we were able to fabricate the dentures that were not only aesthetic but also functional and stable.

Significantly, the healing process following mandibular ridge augmentation and the osseointegration of the dental implants occurred without complications related to the patient's syndromic condition. The positive results of this intervention extended beyond physical improvements; they included enhanced self‐esteem, improved eating capabilities, and better speech. These outcomes underscore the considerable psychosocial benefits derived from a coordinated surgical and prosthodontic approach for patients with complex syndromic conditions, highlighting the importance of tailored treatment plans in enhancing overall quality of life.

This case report highlights not only the challenges of diagnosing rare genetic disorders but also the difficulties of treatment, as well as the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration. We hope that our experience will serve as a learning opportunity for healthcare professionals and emphasize the need for continuous education and collaboration in complex cases.

The challenges faced in this case exemplify the difficulties inherent in multi‐disciplinary caring for pediatric patients with complex medical histories. The inability to directly consult a medical specialist was one of the most significant challenges we faced during the diagnostic phase of this case. The initial diagnosis of cerebral palsy likely contributed to the reluctance in further exploring the genetic testing. A more proactive stance in seeking additional evaluations like genome sequencing could have expedited diagnosis and treatment, ultimately reduced the burden of care, and improved the patient's quality of life.

Given the complexity and variability of these cases, this report does not aim to provide a generalized care plan for all AGS‐SMS patients; however, in cases with similar presentations, a treatment plan leaning more toward dental clearance rather than conservative approach might be considered. With this case, it has been shown that ridge augmentation followed by the placement of dental implants can be successfully accomplished despite the multisystem effects of the IFIH1‐related disorder. The absence of specific dental clinical guidelines for these syndromes can lead to suboptimal care, increased costs, and prolonged treatment times. Developing standardized guidelines will help improve patient outcomes and provide clear treatment pathways for managing complex syndromic conditions like AGS and SMS.
