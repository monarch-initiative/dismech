With the near eradication of poliovirus due to global vaccination campaigns,
attention has shifted to other enteroviruses that can cause polio-like paralysis
syndrome (now termed acute flaccid myelitis)1-3. In particular, enterovirus D68
(EV-D68) is believed to be the main driver of epidemic outbreaks of acute
flaccid myelitis in recent years4, yet not much is known about EV-D68 host
interactions. EV-D68 is a respiratory virus5 but, in rare cases, can spread to
the central nervous system to cause severe neuropathogenesis. Here we use
genome-scale CRISPR screens to identify the poorly characterized multipass
membrane transporter MFSD6 as a host entry factor for EV-D68. Knockout of MFSD6
expression abrogated EV-D68 infection in cell lines and primary cells
corresponding to respiratory and neural cells. MFSD6 localized to the plasma
membrane and was required for viral entry into host cells. MFSD6 bound directly
to EV-D68 particles through its extracellular, third loop (L3). We determined
the cryo-electron microscopy structure of EV-D68 in a complex with MFSD6 L3,
revealing the interaction interface. A decoy receptor, engineered by fusing
MFSD6 L3 to Fc, blocked EV-D68 infection of human primary lung epithelial cells
and provided near-complete protection in a lethal mouse model of EV-D68
infection. Collectively, our results reveal MFSD6 as an entry receptor for
EV-D68, and support the targeting of MFSD6 as a potential mechanism to combat
infections by this emerging pathogen with pandemic potential.

Enteroviruses (EVs) are a large genus of single-stranded, positive-sense RNA viruses in the picornavirus family and are among the most common infectious agents. Recent global outbreaks of EV-D68 were associated with respiratory disease and severe neuropathogenesis in children1, yet there are no therapeutic options. Cellular receptors play an important role in EV pathogenesis6. Sialic acid-containing oligosaccharides were shown to mediate attachment and infection by EV-D687,8. In addition, ICAM-5 was identified as a functional entry receptor for EV-D689. However, ICAM-5 expression is limited to the telencephalon, a part of the brain not implicated in EV-D68 pathogenesis10, which suggests the existence of other EV-D68 proteinaceous receptors.

To identify a receptor required for EV-D68 infection, we performed genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screens (Fig. 1a) in the human lung adenocarcinoma A549 cell line and in the human glioblastoma U87-MG cell line. After mutagenesis with a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 library11, cells were infected with EV-D68 (USA/IL/2014-18952). To identify gene knockouts that are protective against EV-D68 infection, guide RNA sequences (gRNAs) were amplified and sequenced from surviving cells. We then calculated the statistical enrichment12 of gRNAs present in the resistant cell population compared to the uninfected population. Besides genes involved in sialic acid biosynthesis (e.g. SLC35A1 and C1GALT1), another gene, MFSD6, stood out as highly enriched in both cell lines (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Tables 1-2). MFSD6 is a multipass membrane protein that belongs to the Major Facilitator Superfamily Domain (MFSD) group of solute transporters13. The function of MFSD6 is largely unknown but it is widely expressed including in tissues of relevance for EV-D68 pathogenesis such as the lungs and spinal cord (Extended Data Fig. 1a). ICAM-5 was not enriched in the screens (Fig. 1b).

To validate the function of MFSD6 across diverse human cell types relevant for EV-D68 infection, we focused on respiratory and neural cells. We used CRISPR-Cas9 to generate knockout mutations and verified knockout frequencies in pooled cell populations (Extended Data Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 2a-b). Cells were infected with EV-D68 and viral RNA replication was determined 24 hours post-infection using RT-qPCR. Compared to control cells (non-target gRNA or wild-type), MFSD6 knockout resulted in strongly reduced viral RNA levels in all cell lines including A549, BEAS-2B (immortalized normal human bronchial epithelium), U87-MG, SK-N-SH (neuroblastoma), and HEK293FT (embryonic kidney) (Fig. 1c). ICAM-5 knockout affected EV-D68 replication in HEK293FT cells but not in other tested cell lines. In primary normal human bronchial epithelium (NHBE) cells, and in neuronal cells derived after differentiation from human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), MFSD6 knockout similarly resulted in reduced EV-D68 infection (Fig. 1d-e).

To characterize the role of MFSD6 in the viral life cycle, we generated isogenic, clonal knockouts of MFSD6 (ΔMFSD6) in A549 cells and U87-MG cells (Extended Data Fig. 3a). Additionally, we generated knockout cell lines trans-complemented with Flag epitope-tagged MFSD6 (ΔMFSD6+). Immunoblotting showed endogenous MFSD6 expression in A549 cells, while in U87-MG cells it was too low to detect with the available antibody (Extended Data Fig. 1c). No MFSD6 was detected in ΔMFSD6 cells, and trans-complementation resulted in high levels of expression. In both cell lines, MFSD6 deficiency resulted in severely reduced EV-D68 infection, decreasing viral RNA levels more than 1,000-fold at 24 hours post-infection (Fig. 1f). Likewise, viral capsid protein expression was strongly reduced as measured by immunofluorescence microscopy (Extended Data Fig. 1d). Trans-complementation of MFSD6 partially restored or enhanced infection compared to wild-type A549 and U87-MG cells, respectively. Similar results were obtained when virus particle production was measured during a time-course of infection (Fig. 1g). Infection with additional EV-D68 strains representative of different clades showed that the dependence on MFSD6 is conserved (Extended Data Fig. 4a-c). In contrast, replication of the related enteroviruses – enterovirus A71 (EV-A71), coxsackievirus A10 (CV-A10), and rhinovirus B14 (RV-B14) – was not affected in U87-MG ΔMFSD6 cells (Extended Data Fig. 4d). Taken together, unbiased, genome-scale screens identified the orphan membrane transporter MFSD6 as a host protein required for infection by multiple strains of EV-D68 in a diverse selection of cellular backgrounds.

MFSD6 is phylogenetically related to MFSD813 (also named CLN7), a putative lysosomal chloride channel14. MFSD8 is transiently localized to the plasma membrane, where it is internalized along the endocytic pathway and delivered to lysosomes15. To determine the subcellular localization of MFSD6, we fused MFSD6 to the bright green fluorescent protein mNeonGreen (MFSD6-mNeon)16. This protein was stably expressed in ΔMFSD6 A549 cells through lentiviral transduction. Similar to unfused MFSD6, MFSD6-mNeon was able to restore EV-D68 infection in the ΔMFSD6 background, suggesting proper localization and function (Extended Data Fig. 4e).

Using fluorescence microscopy and staining with the plasma membrane marker wheat germ agglutinin (WGA), we observed that MFSD6-mNeon localized partly to the plasma membrane (Fig. 2a). To further investigate plasma membrane localization biochemically, cell surface biotinylation experiments were performed using wild-type, ΔMFSD6, and ΔMFSD6+ A549 cells. After biotinylation and precipitation using streptavidin resin, samples were immunoblotted for MFSD6 and for Na+/K+ channels, which served as a control for plasma membrane localization. In the biotinylated samples, we observed immunoreactive bands in wild-type and ΔMFSD6+ lanes, indicative of the presence of endogenous and trans-complemented MFSD6, respectively, at the plasma membrane (Fig. 2b). Besides plasma membrane localization, MFSD6-mNeon was observed intracellularly in fluorescent puncta, which colocalized with markers of the lysosome (LAMP2) and late endosome (RAB7), but not the Golgi apparatus (GM130) (Fig. 2c-d). Thus, analogous to the related MFSD8 transporter15, MFSD6 is located both at the plasma membrane and in organelles of the late endocytic pathway, suggesting a potential role in viral entry.

To pinpoint the step in the viral life cycle that depends on MFSD6, we constructed an EV-D68 replicon, replacing the structural proteins VP4, VP2, and part of VP3 with Renilla luciferase (Extended Data Fig. 4f). Electroporation of in vitro transcribed replicon RNA bypasses viral entry but allows viral translation and RNA replication to occur. In a time-course experiment using A549 cells and U87-MG cells, we observed initial translation followed by an exponential increase of luciferase activity (Fig. 2e). This increase was not observed upon treatment with the replication inhibitor guanidine hydrochloride (GuHCl), indicating the increase is due to viral RNA replication. We noted a difference in luciferase activity at initial time points, but at later time points the levels of luciferase activity in wild-type cells and ΔMFSD6 cells were similar. While we cannot formally exclude that MFSD6 may contribute to early translation, these results suggest that MFSD6 does not contribute to viral RNA replication, which points to a role in entry (Fig. 2e).

Next, we determined whether MFSD6 is important for EV-D68 cellular attachment and subsequent internalization. As a control, we generated clonal ΔSLC35A1 cells (Extended Data Fig. 3b), which are deficient in the synthesis of sialylated glycans, known attachment factors for EV-D687. While less cellular attachment was observed in ΔSLC35A1 cells, ΔMFSD6 cells were not (in U87-MG) or only modestly (in A549) affected in their ability to bind EV-D68 (Fig. 2f). In contrast, loss of MFSD6 resulted in internalization defects comparable to those seen in ΔSLC35A1 cells (Fig. 2g). In A549 ΔMFSD6+ cells, EV-D68 internalization was partially restored. Overexpression of MFSD6 in U87-MG cells resulted in strong increases in viral attachment and internalization (Fig. 2f-g). To exclude that MFSD6 acts by affecting glycan synthesis, we stained ΔMFSD6 A549 and ΔMFSD6 U87-MG with the lectin SNA. We observed levels of lectin-positive sialylated glycans at the cell surface of ΔMFSD6 cells similar to those of wild-type cells (Extended Data Fig. 3c). Altogether, we conclude that MFSD6 is important for internalization of EV-D68 but has little to no contribution to initial cellular attachment.

A defining feature of viral receptors is their ability to directly bind to viral particles. MFSD6 is a membrane transporter with twelve predicted transmembrane domains (TMDs) and cytoplasmic N- and C-terminal regions13. The trans-complemented cells express MFSD6 with a Flag epitope on the C-terminus (MFSD6-Flag), facilitating affinity purification. As a specificity control, we used MFSD8, which was similarly tagged (MFSD8-Flag). Purified MFSD6-Flag but not MFSD8-Flag bound to purified EV-D68 particles in an immunoprecipitation assay (Fig. 3a). To pinpoint the domain responsible for interaction, we engineered MFSD6 mutant proteins that focus on the large, extracellular loop between TMDs 3 and 4 (residues 153-285, herein termed loop 3, L3), hypothesizing that this is the most likely protein domain to mediate an interaction with EV-D68 during virus entry (Fig. 3b). Expression of MFSD6 mutants with stable trans-complementation in ΔMFSD6 cells was validated through western blot (Extended Data Fig. 5a). As expected, MFSD6 but not MFSD8 rescued EV-D68 infection (Fig. 3c-d). Deletion of the L3 domain and replacement of it with the HA epitope tag (ΔL3) abrogated MFSD6’s function in EV-D68 infection (Fig. 3c-d). Conversely, MFSD8 gained the ability to act as a host factor for EV-D68 infection in a loop swap (LS) variant, in which the largest MFSD8 extracellular loop, located between TMDs 9 and 10 (residues 359-412, herein termed loop 9, L9), was replaced with the MFSD6 L3 domain (Fig. 3c-d).

Having established the essentiality of the MFSD6 L3 domain for EV-D68 infection, we next determined whether this domain could interact directly with EV-D68 virions. To that end, we generated and purified a soluble Fc fusion protein, Fc-MFSD6(L3), along with Fc-MFSD8(L9) as a control protein (Fig. 3e, Extended Data Fig. 5b). We found that purified EV-D68 interacts directly and specifically with Fc-MFSD6(L3) but not with Fc-MFSD8(L9) using an immunoprecipitation assay and using biolayer interferometry (BLI) (Fig. 3f-h). Neither EV-A71 nor CVB3 interacted with Fc-MFSD6(L3) (Extended Data Fig. 5c-d).

MFSD6 was previously identified as a mucin-domain glycoprotein with the sites for O-linked glycosylation present in the L3 domain17. To determine whether the interaction of MFSD6 L3 with EV-D68 is dependent on O-linked glycosylation, we produced Fc-MFSD6(L3) in HEK293T cells lacking O-linked glycosylation machinery18 and confirmed the specific reduction of O-glycans by mass spectrometry (MS) and western blot (Extended Data Fig 6a-c). Ablation of O-linked glycosylation did not substantially decrease binding to EV-D68, suggesting that O-linked glycosylation is not required for interaction between MFSD6 and EV-D68 (Fig. 3f, h).

To determine how EV-D68 engages with its receptor, we performed single particle cryo-EM on purified EV-D68 alone and EV-D68 bound with Fc-MFSD6(L3) (Fig. 4a, Extended Data Fig. 5b, e-i) and solved the corresponding structures to 2.0 Å and 2.1 Å, respectively (Extended Data Figs. 7-8, Extended Data Table 1). To avoid potential challenges due to mucin glycan heterogeneity, we used Fc-MFSD6(L3) deficient in O-linked glycosylation (Extended Data Fig. 5b). The Fc-MFSD6(L3)-bound structure displayed an additional density present in each of the 60 asymmetric units (VP1-4, chains A-D) of the EV-D68 particle (Fig. 4b). We generated models for each capsid protein and for the additional density. We found that this density corresponds to a portion of Fc-MFSD6(L3), matching MFSD6 residues 200-208 (chain E), along with a complex type N-glycan located at N207 (chain F) (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Video 1). This suggests that the full loop exhibits flexibility that limits its resolvability and that the resolved region of the loop is stabilized by its interaction with EV-D68. MS-based proteomics19,20 was employed to characterize the various glycoforms present at N207 and quantify their compositional abundances (Fig. 4c, Extended Data Fig. 6d-e). It was found that all identified glycan structures at N207 share a common core, which matches the cryo-EM map (Fig. 4b, Extended Data Fig. 8k). The distal ends of these glycoforms cause heterogeneity21,22, likely explaining why the corresponding density is less resolved away from the core.

Further structural analysis of the binding interface of our map and models identified hydrogen bonds and a salt bridge between MFSD6 and surface exposed amino acid residues of the EV-D68 virion (Extended Data Fig. 9a-b, Supplementary Video 1). In particular, MFSD6 residues N200, L202, E203, and R205 (chain E) make contact with residues in VP1 and VP3, while the N207 glycan moiety (chain F) interacts with residues in VP2. Focusing on these residues, we found that MFSD6-N200 forms hydrogen bonds with VP1-T1094 and VP3-Q3247 (Fig. 4d). Residue MFSD6-L202 forms a hydrogen bond with VP1-T1216 and embeds in a hydrophobic pocket formed by its interactions with VP1-T1094, VP1-T1216, VP1-I1262, and VP3-A3245 (Fig. 4e). Residue MFSD6-E203 engages in three interactions: a salt bridge with VP1-K1092 and two hydrogen bonds with VP1-G1151 and VP1-N1219 (Fig. 4f). Other interactions (Extended Data Figure 9a-b) include hydrogen bonding of MFSD6-R205 to VP1-L1208 (Extended Data Fig. 9b-c, Supplementary Video 1) and two GlcNAc monosaccharides linked to N207 that engage in hydrogen bonding with residues VP2-T2137, VP2-T2139, and VP2-S2140 (Extended Data Fig. 9b,d, Supplementary Video 1).

Other enteroviruses interact with their cognate Ig-like receptors in a virion surface depression termed the canyon, which initiates the uncoating process23. These interactions often involve residues in the VP1 loop between beta-strands G and H (GH loop)7. While MFSD6 does not contain Ig-like domains, the interaction interface is similarly mapped to the canyon region of EV-D68 (Extended Data Fig. 9e-f). MFSD6 contact sites VP1-L1208 and VP1-T1216 are part of the GH loop and VP1-N1219 is the first residue of the H beta-strand (Extended Data Fig. 9a-b).

To investigate which interacting residues in MFSD6 are most critical to EV-D68 binding, we generated alanine mutants (Fig. 4g). We introduced these into Fc-MFSD6(L3) and produced mutant proteins in wild-type cells, allowing for both O- and N-linked glycosylation. Alanine substitution of the entire 200-208 region or the four contact residues together (N200, L202, E203, and R205) resulted in loss of EV-D68 binding, which supports our structural interpretation. Single mutants in N200, L202, and E203 each lost binding, identifying them as key residues contributing to binding. The R205 mutant displayed binding comparable to that of wild type Fc-MFSD6(L3). Given the hydrogen bonding of GlcNAc monosaccharides linked to N207 (Extended Data Fig. 9b,d, Supplementary Video 1), we also mutated this residue to prevent glycosylation. This mutant retained its ability to bind to EV-D68.

We assessed whether Fc-MFSD6(L3) can function as a decoy receptor to prevent EV-D68 infection. After pre-incubating EV-D68 particles with Fc-MFSD6(L3), we observed a dose-dependent decrease of EV-D68 infection in A549 cells and in primary normal human bronchial epithelial (NHBE) cells (Fig. 5a-b). No such inhibition was observed with Fc-MFSD8(L9). We used C57BL/6 mice deficient in expression of the type I interferon receptor (Ifnar) as a mouse model that is highly susceptible to EV-D68 infection24-26 (Fig. 5c). Intraperitoneal (IP) administration of EV-D68 pre-incubated with Fc-MFSD8(L9) resulted in systemic neurotropic infection as evidenced by the accumulation of infectious virus particles in diaphragm, leg muscle, and spinal cord (Fig. 5d), and death of all challenged mice (Fig. 5e). In stark contrast, pre-incubation of EV-D68 with Fc-MFSD6(L3) markedly decreased the accumulation of infectious virus particles in tissues (Fig. 5d), and strongly increased survival, with all but one of the challenged mice surviving (Fig. 5e). To assess the ability of Fc-MFSD6(L3) to prevent lethal EV-D68 infection when administered systemically, we injected mice intraperitoneally with Fc-MFSD6(L3) 6 hours prior to EV-D68 infection (Fig. 5c). This prophylactic treatment strongly increased survival, nearly completely preventing lethality observed in control mice treated with Fc-MFSD8(L9) (Fig. 5f).

Our studies assign a function to the orphan membrane transporter MFSD6 as an entry receptor that is required for EV-D68 infection. MFSD6 is broadly expressed, has a poorly defined role in regulating neural circuit activity, and is required for axon regrowth in C. elegans27,28. We found that MFSD6 is critical for viral infection in a wide range of human cell types including neural and respiratory cells. Mechanistically, we show that MFSD6 binds to EV-D68 particles and is required for EV-D68 cell entry while initial attachment to cells is mainly mediated by interactions with sialic acid. While this manuscript was in revision, another group reported on the independent identification of MFSD6 as a receptor for EV-D68, with findings in agreement with ours29. Altogether, these findings indicate a general function of MFSD6 in viral infection that likely contributes to EV-D68 pathogenesis.

Picornaviruses have evolved to utilize diverse receptors, including single pass transmembrane proteins with Ig-like domains (e.g. PVR and ICAM-1) and multipass transmembrane proteins (e.g. SCARB2 and KREMEN1)6,30. Our structural analysis revealed that, analogous to Ig-like receptors, MFSD6 interacts with EV-D68 in the canyon region through contact sites in the VP1 GH loop (Extended Data Figure 9 and Extended Data Figure 10a-b). Sialic acid binds EV-D68 in the canyon at a distinct site not involving the GH loop7 (Extended Data Figure 10c). Comparison with structures of EV-D68 in complex with monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) identified two mAbs31 with binding sites proximal to the MFSD6 interaction site and one mAb32 adjacent to it (Extended Data Figure 10d-f). This suggests that blockage of EV-D68 interaction with MFSD6 may contribute to the neutralizing activity of these mAbs, in addition to the previously suggested block in sialic acid interactions31 and prevention of uncoating32.

MFSD6 belongs to the family of solute carrier transporters in which members have been identified as retrovirus receptors33-35 but not as picornavirus receptors. Because solute carrier transporters are considered druggable therapeutic targets36, development of chemical modulators for MFSD6 could find utility as antiviral therapies. In addition, given the strong protection conferred by Fc-MFSD6(L3) in an immunodeficient lethal model of EV-D68 infection in mice, such decoy-receptor37,38 fusion proteins could be developed as a strategy to prevent EV-D68 pathogenesis.

A549 and U87-MG cells were stably transduced with lenti Cas9-Blast (Addgene, 52962) and selected using blasticidin to generate constitutively expressing Cas9 cells. For each library, 300 million cells expressing Cas9 were transduced (MOI = 0.3) with the lentiGuide-Puro from the Brunello library (Addgene, 73178). Cells were selected using puromycin for two days, then pooled together. For each screen, enough cells were seeded for 600-fold coverage of mutagenized cells, then infected with EV-D68 IL (MOI = 0.1) at 34°C. For the U87-MG screen, 1 mM guanidine hydrochloride (GuHCl) was added to the cells at 3, 4, and 6 days post-infection, and 2 mM GuHCl was added 7 days post-infection to allow the cells to recover without re-infection. For the A549 screen, 2 mM GuHCl was added after 5 days to allow cells to recover without re-infection. Media changes occurred every day for the first 4 days post-infection and at the time of GuHCl addition. Visible colonies of virus-resistant cells were pooled and harvested at 11 days post-infection. The media was changed after most cells were dead, usually 2-5 days post-infection. Uninfected starting populations of mutagenized cells were used as the unselected reference. Total genomic DNA from both virus-resistant and uninfected cells was extracted using a QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, 51306). The inserted gRNA sequences were retrieved from the genomic DNA by PCR amplification. The PCR products were then purified and sequenced using next-generation sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq Lane (Novogene). The sequencing data were processed and analyzed using the MAGeCK algorithm (version 0.5.4)12 to determine the Robust Rank Aggregation (RRA), a measure of ranking for each hit that takes the following criteria into account: (1) the number of sequencing reads for each unique guide; (2) the number of unique gRNAs per gene (that is, 0–4); and (3) the enrichment of a particular gRNA compared with uninfected cell populations.

CRISPR gRNA sequences were taken from the Brunello library and corresponding oligos were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies. The oligos for MFSD6 (F: 5’-CACCGGGTGGTGGTAACAATAACAG-3’, R: 5’-AAACCTGTTATTGTTACCACCACCC-3’) and SLC35A1 (F: 5’-CACCGATAAAGTTATTGCTAAGTGT-3’, R: 5’-AAACACACTTAGCAATAACTTTATC-3’) were cloned into the Cas9-expressing pX458 gRNA plasmid (Addgene, 48138) by first phosphorylating and annealing the oligos using T4 polynucleotide kinase (PNK) (NEB, M0201L). Next the oligos were digested and ligated using BpiI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, ER1012) and T7 DNA ligase (NEB, M0202L) along with pX458. The resulting plasmids were treated with PlasmidSafe exonuclease (Lucigen, E3101K) before electroporation into ElectroMAX DH10B E. coli (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 18290015). Colonies were picked and DNA was prepared using a Qiagen miniprep kit. Sequences were verified through Sanger sequencing through Genewiz using their U6 promoter primer.

The cloning products were transfected into A549 and U87-MG cells using Transit-X2 (VWR, 10766-888) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. At 48 hours post-transfection, the cells were single-cell sorted based on GFP into 96-well plates using a BD FACSAria II or BD FACSFusion sorter at the Stanford Shared FACS Facility. Clonal cell lines were allowed to expand from a single cell, and genomic DNA was isolated using QuickExtract (Lucigen, QE09050) for sequence-based genotyping of the targeted allele. After protein expression and purification, the protein samples were further buffer exchanged using a methanol/chloroform/water precipitation and resolubilization method50. Briefly, 300 μL of cold LC/MS-grade water (4°C) was added to 100 μL of protein solution followed by 400 μL of cold methanol (−20°C). The protein solution was vortexed for 30 s followed by addition of 100 μL of cold chloroform (−20°C) and an additional 30 s of vortexing. The sample was centrifuged for 10 min at 18,000g at 4°C after which the top layer of the solution was discarded without disturbing the protein pellet. Then, 400 μL of cold methanol (−20°C) was added to the sample, gently vortexed, and centrifuged for 10 min at 18,000 g at 4°C. After which, the supernatant was discarded. Protein pellets were resolubilized with 4 μL of 80% formic acid (−20°C) and diluted to 1% formic acid with 80:20 water: acetonitrile. Top-down LC-MS was carried out using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system coupled to an Agilent 6230 ToF LC/MS (Agilent Technologies). Samples were injected onto an Agilent PLRP-S column (2.1 × 50 mm, 5 μm particle size, 1,000 Å pore size) using a gradient of 10% to 90% mobile phase B (0–5 min, 10% B; 5–15 min, 20%–65% B; 15–18 min, 65%–90% B, 18–22 min, 90% B; 22–25 min, 10% B; mobile phase A set to 0.2% formic acid in water; mobile phase B set to 0.2% formic acid in acetonitrile). Flow rate was set to 200 μL/min with a column temperature of 60°C. Mass spectra were taken at a scan rate of 1 Hz over a 200–3200 m/z scan range with the ToF set to extended dynamic range. The mass spectrometer was operated using a dual Agilent jet stream (AJS) high-sensitivity ion source with the following instrument parameters: gas temperature (275°C), drying gas (12 L/min), nebulizer (40 psi), sheath gas temperature (400°C), sheath gas flow (12 L/min), VCap(3000 V), nozzle voltage (2000 V), fragmentor (250 V), skimmer (65 V), and Oct 1 RF Vpp (750 V).

Protein samples were processed using a suspension trapping (S-Trap) microcolumn (Protifi, C02-micro-80, ≤ 100 μg) following vendor protocols as described by Protifi (Fairport, NY, USA) with slight modifications. Briefly, protein samples were first reduced using 20 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) at 37°C for 15 min and then alkylated using 40 mM 2-chloroacetamide for 20 min at room temperature. The denatured, non-digested proteins were then acidified using phosphoric acid (85 % w/v) to a final phosphoric acid concentration of 2.5 % w/v before being loaded on the S-trap microcolumn. The bound proteins were washed by repeated centrifugation using 100 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) in 90% methanol to remove MS incompatible buffer salts and detergents. The protein samples were then digested on the S-trap using 50 μL of a 1:10 enzyme to substrate mass ratio of Trypsin/Lys-C mix (Promega, V5072) at 37°C overnight for 16 h. The resulting peptides were then eluted from the S-trap using 40 μL of 50 mM TEAB, followed by 40 μL of 0.2% formic acid (FA), and finally with 40 μL of 50% acetonitrile (ACN). The pooled elution mixtures were then dried using a Labconco CentriVap vacuum concentrator (Kansas City, MO, USA). Dried peptides were resuspended in 12 μL of 0.2% FA, 2% ACN and peptide concentrations were measured by absorbance at 205 nm using a Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific) prior to LC/MS analysis.

Reconstituted peptides were injected and separated using a trap-and-elute nanoflow method over an Aurora Ultimate TS 25 cm nanoflow UHPLC column (25 cm x 75 μm ID, 1.7 μm C18; IonOpticks, AUR3-25075C18-TS) analytical column connected to a Dionex Ultimate 3000 RPLC nano system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with an integrated loading pump using mobile phases A (water with 0.2% FA) and B (ACN with 0.2% FA). Peptides were loaded onto a trap column (Acclaim PepMap 100 C18, 5 μm particles, 20 mm length, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 164213) at a flow rate of 5 μL/min under 100% A which was then put in line with the analytical column 5.5 min into the acquisition. The peptides were then separated at 300 nL/min using the following elution gradient: 2 % B for 6 min, 2% to 5 % B from 6 to 6.5 min, 5 % to 28 % B from 6.5 to 66.5 min, 28 % to 90 % B from 66.5 to 105 min, isocratic flow at 95 % B from 105 to 115 min, and a re-equilibration at 2 % B for 5 min for a total analysis time of 120 min. Eluted peptides were analyzed on an Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid MS system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Precursors were ionized using an EASY-Spray ionization source (Thermo Fisher Scientific) set at 1.85 kV compared to ground, and the column was held at 45 °C. The inlet capillary temperature was set at 325 °C. Data-dependent acquisition (DDA) included MS1 scans acquired over a mass range of 400-1450 m/z, with a resolution of 120,000 at 200 m/z, normalized automatic gain control (AGC) target of 750% with absolute AGC value of 3 E6, a maximum injection time (IT) of 50 ms, and RF lens at 60%. DDA MS2 scans were then collected with an isolation window of 2 m/z, a normalized AGC target of 100%, with absolute AGC value of 5 E4, a maximum IT of 120 ms, Orbitrap resolution of 60,000 at 200 m/z, normalized stepped HCD collision energy of 28, 32, 36%, and a scan range of set to first mass at 120 m/z. Monoisotopic precursor selection was enabled for peptide isotopic distributions, precursors of z = 2 to 8 were selected for data-dependent MS/MS with 10 dependent scans per duty cycle, and dynamic exclusion was set to 30 s with a ± 10 ppm window set around the precursor monoisotopic ion.

To model the loop density, a Cα backbone was first built, and side chains with the best visual fit were placed to identify the protein sequence, which was validated by Q-score analysis (Extended Data Fig. 8a-k).
