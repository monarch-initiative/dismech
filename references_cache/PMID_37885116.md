# Norepinephrine transporter and vesicular monoamine transporter 2 tumor expression as a predictor of response to (131) I-MIBG in patients with relapsed/refractory neuroblastoma.
**Authors:** Batra V, Gikandi A, Pawel B, Martinez D, Granger MM, Marachelian A, Park JR, Maris JM, Vo KT, Matthay KK, DuBois SG
**Journal:** Pediatr Blood Cancer (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1002/pbc.30743](https://doi.org/10.1002/pbc.30743)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Prior studies suggest that norepinephrine transporter (NET) and vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) mediate meta-iodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) uptake and retention in neuroblastoma tumors. We evaluated the relationship between NET and VMAT2 tumor expression and clinical response to 131 I-MIBG therapy in patients with neuroblastoma.

METHODS: Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was used to evaluate NET and VMAT2 protein expression levels on archival tumor samples (obtained at diagnosis or relapse) from patients with relapsed or refractory neuroblastoma treated with 131 I-MIBG. A composite protein expression H-score was determined by multiplying a semi-quantitative intensity value (0-3+) by the percentage of tumor cells expressing the protein.

RESULTS: Tumor samples and clinical data were available for 106 patients, of whom 28.3% had partial response (PR) or higher. NET H-score was not significantly associated with response (≥PR), though the percentage of tumor cells expressing NET was lower among responders (median 80% for ≥PR vs. 90% for <PR; p = .0014). VMAT2 H-score was not significantly associated with PR or higher versus less than PR, though patients with PR or higher had lower VMAT2 staining intensity (p = .005). VMAT2 H-score was significantly lower in patients with complete response (median 40 vs. 210 for patients with <complete response; p = .0049). VMAT2 H-scores were significantly higher in ganglioneuroblastoma (vs. neuroblastoma; p = .037), differentiated/poorly differentiated tumors (vs. undifferentiated; p = .0047), and tumors lacking MYCN amplification (vs. MYCN amplified; p = .0011).

CONCLUSIONS: Markers of lower NET and VMAT2 protein expression are associated with higher likelihood of response to 131 I-MIBG therapy in patients with relapsed/refractory neuroblastoma. Increased VMAT2 protein expression is associated with a more differentiated disease phenotype.

Despite being used in neuroblastoma diagnostic imaging and therapy since the 1980s, there are are few predictors of response to 131I-metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) [1,2]. With its expanding role in neuroblastoma treatment, identifying biomarkers associated with response to 131I-MIBG is essential for optimal patient selection. Transporters mediating the import or efflux of radiolabeled MIBG may be candidate predictive biomarkers of 131I-MIBG therapy response. Our group previously demonstrated a positive association between norepinephrine transporter (NET; SLC6A2) and vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) protein expression and MIBG avidity in patients with intermediate-risk and high-risk neuroblastoma [3,4].

Given that higher expression of NET and VMAT2 expression is associated with MIBG avidity, we now hypothesize that expression levels of these transporters may predict response to 131I-MIBG therapy. The primary aim of the current study was therefore to evaluate the association between NET and VMAT2 protein expression with response to therapeutic 131I-MIBG in patients with relapsed or refractory neuroblastoma. Secondary aims included evaluating the association between tumor NET and VMAT2 protein expression with clinical and biological features of neuroblastoma as well as with whole-body radiation exposure.

The analytical cohort includes two subsets of patients with relapsed or refractory MIBG-avid high-risk neuroblastoma who received 131I-MIBG as greater than or equal to their second line of therapy. The first subset includes patients who were treated with their first course of 131I-MIBG as part of clinical trials or expanded access programs at the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF, NCT01370330) or the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP, NCT01163383). Eligible patients received a first dose of 131I-MIBG ≥15 mCi/kg as monotherapy or in combination with other agents and did not receive prior 131I-MIBG outside of UCSF or CHOP [5,6]. Patients with available archival tumor material from diagnosis or relapse were included in this analysis. The second subset includes patients with relapsed or refractory disease who were treated as part of a recent randomized phase II trial comparing 131I-MIBG as monotherapy to 131I-MIBG in combination with vincristine / irinotecan or vorinostat (NANT11–01 trial, NCT02035137) [7]. All patients on NANT11–01 received 18 mCi/kg 131I-MIBG per course, with up to two courses allowed. There were no restrictions on the number of lines of prior therapy before enrollment on NANT11–01 and previous 131I-MIBG monotherapy was allowed if patients did not progress while on prior 131I-MIBG, if at least 6 months had elapsed since last treatment, and if the cumulative lifetime dose did not exceed 18 mCi/kg. Evaluation of tissue markers, including NET and VMAT2, was included as a specific optional correlative biology aim of this trial. Patients who provided additional consent and had available archival tumor material from diagnosis or relapse were included in this analysis.

Each 131I-MIBG trial and evaluation of archival tumor material was approved by local institutional review boards (IRBs) with appropriate consent from all patients or legal guardians.

The primary outcome variable was objective response to first course of 131I-MIBG. Response was typically assessed 6–8 weeks post-treatment. Patients were categorized overall as responders (≥partial response, PR) or non-responders (<PR) using response in each neuroblastoma disease domain (soft tissue, bone, bone marrow). Response on CHOP/UCSF clinical trials and expanded access protocols was graded by International Neuroblastoma Response Criteria v1 (INRC) [8]. Response on NANT11–01 was assessed by NANT Response Criteria v1.2 (NANTRC) and included central review of response as previously described [7,9].

In this analysis, the “relapse” category includes patients with any prior relapsed or progressive disease, while the “refractory” category includes patients with refractory or persistent disease without a prior episode of disease progression.

Clinical features were extracted from the medical record or obtained directly from the NANT11–01 clinical trial database. Whole body radiation dose was calculated as previously described [10].

NET-17 (Mabtechnologies, NET17–1, 1:1000) and VMAT2 (Origene, TA500506, 1:1000) antibodies were used to stain formalin fixed paraffin embedded tissue slides (see Supplemental Figure 1 for representative images and additional methods) [3,4]. Tumor characteristics and immunohistochemical staining were evaluated by a pediatric pathologist (BP) with expertise in pediatric peripheral neuroblastic tumors. Slides that did not contain an interpretable neuroblastic component were excluded from analysis. Immunohistochemical stains were evaluated for staining percentage and intensity (0/none to 3/intense) of staining. A staining H-score was calculated (intensity × % cells; 0 to 300) for each tumor. Immunohistochemistry staining and scoring were performed blinded to patient clinical and biological features, including response to 131I-MIBG.

Wilcoxon rank sum tests were used to compare the distribution of continuous protein expression variables (staining H-score or percent positive cells) between binary groups. Fisher exact tests were used to compare the categorical protein intensity variables between groups. Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the correlation between continuous protein expression score and continuous whole body radiation dose. All statistical analyses were performed in STATA BE v17 (College Station, TX).

The cohort began with 137 unique patients with available samples. Of these, 14 patients had no clinical data available and were excluded. An additional 17 patients were excluded as tissue material was inadequate due to crush artifact, necrotic tumor, or insufficient tumor. The analytic cohort therefore consists of 106 unique patients whose characteristics are shown in Table 1. There were 30 responders (≥PR) and 76 patients with <PR. Patient characteristics are generally as expected for a population of patients with relapsed/refractory neuroblastoma, including 22.6% of patients with MYCN amplified tumors. Forty-six (43%) patients were treated on NANT11–01 and the remainder were treated on earlier trials or with compassionate access 131I-MIBG. Fifty-nine (56%) patients had first-course whole body dosimetry data available.

We first evaluated whether NET protein expression composite H-score was associated with response to MIBG (Table 2). There was no statistically significant difference in the distribution of NET composite H-score between responders (≥PR) and non-responders (<PR; median 180 vs. 180; p = 0.08; Figure 1A). There was also no association between NET composite H-score and response status when the analysis was restricted to the patients who received MIBG monotherapy (median 170 vs. 190; p = 0.13; Table 3). Composite NET protein expression H-score was also not significantly different in complete responders vs. patients with less than a complete response (median 140 vs. 180; p = 0.32) or between patients coded as having ≥PR by MIBG scan criteria vs. <PR by MIBG scan criteria (median 180 vs. 200; p = 0.12).

We next evaluated whether intensity of NET protein expression or percent of tumor cells with NET protein expression were associated with response (≥PR vs. <PR). There was no statistical association between NET expression intensity on 0–3+ scale and response (p = 0.54). However, percent of tumor cells with NET protein expression was significantly lower in patients with ≥PR compared to <PR (Table 2). Specifically, responders had a median percent NET protein expression of 80% (range 0–100%) compared to 90% (range 30–100%) in non-responders (p = 0.0014; Figure 1B).

We next evaluated the association between VMAT2 protein expression and response to MIBG therapy (Table 2). VMAT2 composite protein expression H-score was not significantly different between patients with ≥PR versus <PR (median 210 vs. 210; p = 0.32). However, VMAT2 composite H-scores were significantly lower in patients who had a complete response (median 40 vs. 210; p = 0.0049; Table 3 and Figure 1C).

There was no statistically significant difference in median percent cells positive for VMAT2 protein expression between patients with ≥PR vs. <PR (70 vs. 80; p = 0.27). However, there was a significant difference in the distribution of VMAT2 protein intensity, with patients with ≥PR having lower VMAT2 staining intensity (p= 0.005; Table 2). Interestingly, only 4% of tumors from patients with <PR lacked VMAT2 staining compared to 18.5% of tumors from patients with ≥PR.

We next evaluated the association between NET and VMAT2 composite protein expression H-score and MYCN amplification status, histopathological subtype, and grade of tumor differentiation (Table 4). NET composite expression H-score was not statistically associated with histopathological subtype (p = 0.45), tumor grade (p = 0.60), or MYCN status (p = 0.82).

In contrast, VMAT2 protein expression was associated with each of these features (Table 4). Tumors with ganglioneuroblastoma had significantly higher VMAT2 composite protein expression H-scores than tumors with neuroblastoma histology (median 270 vs. 180; p = 0.037). Composite VMAT2 protein expression H-score was significantly higher in differentiated/poorly differentiated tumors than undifferentiated tumors (median 225 vs. 100; p = 0.0047). MYCN nonamplified tumors showed significantly higher VMAT2 protein expression than MYCN amplified tumors (median 210 vs. 60; p = 0.0011). The proportion of responders with MYCN nonamplified tumors and MYCN amplified tumors were 22/72 (30.6%) and 6/21 (28.6%), respectively (p = 1.0).

There was no correlation between NET2 protein expression composite H-score and whole-body radiation dose (correlation coefficient 0.12; p = 0.38; Figure 2A). However, there was a weak positive correlation between VMAT2 expression composite H-score and whole-body radiation dose (correlation coefficient 0.32; p = 0.014; Figure 2B).

We show for the first time that lower NET and VMAT2 protein expression are associated with higher likelihood of response to 131I-MIBG therapy in patients with relapsed/refractory neuroblastoma. In particular, we observed a striking association between lower VMAT2 protein expression and likelihood of a complete response to 131I-MIBG. We further show that higher VMAT2 protein expression is associated with a more differentiated disease phenotype (including ganglioneuroblastoma and lack of MYCN amplification). Future work will be needed to determine if these associations with degree of differentiation may help to explain the observed inverse association between VMAT2 expression and likelihood of response to 131I-MIBG. Finally, consistent with the role of VMAT2 in mediating 131I-MIBG retention, we observed a weak positive correlation between VMAT2 protein expression and whole-body radiation dose after 131I-MIBG therapy.

There are few studies identifying neural differentiation markers predictive of MIBG response. Neuroblastoma originates from neural crest cells with underlying genomic and epigenetic impairments in differentiation. Much progress has been made in identifying the developmental origins of neuroblastoma including the recognition of two super enhancer associated differentiation states. Transcriptome profiling has led to the classification of neuroblastoma into two divergent transcriptional states: Mesenchymal (MES) and Noradrenergic (ADRN) clusters [11,12]. ADRN-cluster cases were further subclassified into MYCN and ATRX clusters, characterized by MYCN amplification and ATRX alterations, respectively. Importantly, SLC18A2 (VMAT2) was highly expressed in the ATRX cluster. VMAT2 is a member of the solute carrier superfamily that has been localized to monoaminergic neurons of the central and peripheral sympathetic nervous systems. VMAT2 has been shown to be expressed in chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla, which share an ADRN signature with low-risk neuroblastoma cells [11,13]. Our work supports the observation that tumors enriched in VMAT2 expression are non MYCN amplified and that there is higher expression of VMAT2 in differentiated tumors and in ganglioneuroblastomas.

Previously, we observed that MYCN amplified tumors had significantly lower levels of NET and VMAT2 protein expression compared to MYCN nonamplified tumors, and that MIBG non-avid tumors have higher rates of MYCN amplification compared to MIBG avid tumors [3,4]. Together, these findings suggest an inverse correlation between MYCN amplification and neural differentiation, supporting data from prior studies that have established an association between MYCN signaling and undifferentiated or poorly differentiated neuroblastoma [14,15]. We extend these findings in the current analysis. Specifically, in this analysis focused exclusively on patients with MIBG avid tumors (and therefore candidates for 131I-MIBG therapy), there was no association between NET expression and MYCN status, while VMAT2 expression remained strongly associated with MYCN status. Similar to prior studies, the present analysis did not find an association between MYCN status and response to 131I-MIBG therapy. Together, our findings suggest that additional work is needed to understand the interplay between MYCN status, VMAT2 expression, and response to 131I-MIBG therapy [16].

We acknowledge certain limitations in our analysis. First, tumor material was not available from a subset of patients, and it is possible that those with available tumor material were not representative of the broader population of patients with relapsed/refractory neuroblastoma. Second, archival tumor material was used, which may not have reflected expression level and cell state at the time of 131I-MIBG treatment. Data on whether specific samples were from initial diagnosis or from biopsy at time of relapse were unavailable. Third, while a cohort of >100 patients is a relatively large number with this rare disease, it is possible that some null statistical findings reflect our modest sample size. Similarly, the subgroup analyses need to be interpreted in the context of these limitations. Fourth, our work emphasizes that the expression levels of NET and VMAT2 are only part of the equation for predicting response to 131I-MIBG therapy, with overlapping expression levels across groups defined by response. Despite careful standardization, immunohistochemistry is only semi-quantitative and may be limited by inherent variables such as heterogeneity of expression as well as necrotic areas in the tumor specimen limiting interpretation. It is also important to note that there are likely several other important contributing factors mediating radiation responsiveness as well as 131I-MIBG uptake, retention, and efflux. For example, the role of multidrug resistance protein 1 and 4 (MRP1/4; ABCC1/4) in efflux has recently been reported [17]. Moreover, other transporters play a role in renal excretion of MIBG, including human organic cation transporter 2 (hOCT2) and multidrug and toxin extrusion proteins 1 and 2-K (hMATE1/2-K) [17]. A better understanding of these mediators of 131I-MIBG efflux and clearance may lead to an improved prediction of clinical outcomes. Finally, while it is tempting to try to link NET and VMAT2 protein expression mechanistically with response to 131I-MIBG therapy, it is important to note that our findings represent statistical associations and cannot speak to the mechanism of the findings. Indeed, one might have hypothesized that higher NET and/or VMAT2 levels would result in increased uptake and retention of 131I-MIBG and therefore higher likelihood of response, but we instead observed an inverse relationship. This unexpected finding highlights the need for additional work to understand this association.

Identifying predictors of response to 131I-MIBG therapy is critical for several reasons. This therapy involves inpatient radiation isolation in young children, radiation exposure to caregivers, significant expense, and risk of significant late effects. Identifying patients unlikely to benefit from 131I-MIBG therapy may spare them these risks. Moreover, the role of 131I-MIBG is expanding to include patients earlier in the course of their disease, such that more patients may be treated with this modality in coming years. Newer trials are also evaluating the use of 131I-MIBG combination strategies, including some agents that may modulate the expression of catecholamine transporters or differentiation markers. For example, the HDAC inhibitor vorinostat increases NET protein expression and was recently shown to be associated with higher response rates when added to 131I-MIBG therapy in the NANT11–01 trial [7,18,19]. Before being used to guide clinical care, it will be important to validate our current findings in the context of frontline trials and in new combination trials conducted in patients with relapsed/refractory disease, while also investigating potential other genomic, transcriptomic, and/or proteomic predictors of clinical outcomes after 131I-MIBG therapy.
