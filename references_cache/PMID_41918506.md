# Homozygous PTRHD1 Mutation in Intellectual Disability and Atypical Parkinsonism.
**Authors:** Bölükbaşı EY, Mumtaz S, Afzal M, Shabbir RMK, Tolun A, Malik S
**Journal:** Yale J Biol Med (2026)
**DOI:** [10.59249/RIBG4986](https://doi.org/10.59249/RIBG4986)

## Content

Five different homozygous PTRHD1 mutations, two of them in more than one family, have been reported as responsible for intellectual disability (ID) and parkinsonism. In all 10 families, onset of ID was early childhood, and in parkinsonism, later childhood to the fourth decade. We report on a family with four siblings presenting with mild to moderate ID and mildly ataxic gait without spasticity or hemiparesis that is not consistent with parkinsonism. The signs of parkinsonism such as bradykinesia, tremor, slow to response, dementia, and gait problems appeared in the fourth decade. There was no muscle rigidity and postural instability, but there were unusual features of exotropia, pectus excavatum, and prominent clavicles. Linkage analysis using SNP genotyping followed by exome sequencing led to the discovery of PTRHD1 c.155G>A (p.Cys52Tyr), already reported in an Iranian sibling pair. Our findings reveal that not all PTRHD1 mutations manifest with muscle rigidity and postural instability and confirm that gait problems may not be evident until towards the end of the fourth decade. Early onset behavioral problems in presented patients include attention deficit, hyperactivity, aggressive behavior and seclusion, apraxia of speech, stuttering, and somniloquy. Gait ataxia, exotropia, pectus excavatum, and prominent clavicles further widen the clinical phenotype. PTRHD1 is expressed in many organs, and we found widespread expression in the adult brain. The association of PTRHD1 dysfunction with both cognitive and motor phenotypes highlights the potential role of the protein in neurodevelopment and neurodegeneration.

Intellectual disability (ID) has wide clinical variability and genetic heterogeneity.
This neurodevelopmental disorder is commonly syndromic. On the basis of severity, ID
is classified into mild, moderate, severe, and profound forms [1]. Application of current genetic technologies such as exome
sequencing have facilitated the identification of a large number of disease genes
for recessive ID, some of which are in small families [2,3]. One pitfall in
disease gene search in small families is that more than one of the numerous
mutations in a sibling pair can be contributing to the disease. In addition,
identification of the causative mutation is easier in consanguineous families. In
literature, cohorts of consanguineous families from Pakistan and the Middle East
including Saudi Arabia and Iran show a diagnostic yield up to 90% in some genetic
studies performed [4,5].

We present the results of clinical and genetic investigations in a family with four
siblings afflicted with mild to moderate ID and one unaffected sibling. The signs of
parkinsonism bradykinesia, tremor, slow to response, and dementia plus ataxia
appeared very late, at ages of 34 to 48 years. A hallmark of muscle rigidity,
postural instability for parkinsonism did not develop even in the eldest patient
aged 48 years. This contrasts with the earlier onset of third decade parkinsonism
reported in the sibling pair with the same homozygous mutation as well as the
sibling pair with a mutation that alters the adjacent amino acid [6,7].
Patients have exotropia and the three patients examined in detail have pectus
excavatum and prominent clavicles. Our findings widen the clinical phenotype.

The family originates from a rural region of Southern Punjab, Pakistan. Parents
are first cousins, and four of five siblings are affected (Figure 1). Affected siblings have a poor
socio-economic background with no formal schooling and are engaged in
subsistence farming. Since the primary presentation in patients was ID, a
thorough physical and medical examination of all seven family members was
carried out with the help of physicians and neurologists at Nishtar Medical
Hospital in Multan, Pakistan, using the DSM-5 instrument [1]. The DSM-5 diagnostic criteria incorporate the number of
symptoms, duration, age of onset, and functional impairment to ensure a
standardized case definition of a neurodevelopmental, behavioral, and
psychiatric disorder. The family was initially recruited in 2015, and the
development of symptoms was monitored in several visits, until the last clinical
investigation in March 2024. Parkinsonism was defined by the presence of
bradykinesia in combination with at least one additional extrapyramidal feature,
including resting tremor or muscle rigidity. The study protocol was approved by
the Ethical Review Committee of Quaid-i-Azam University and the Boğaziçi
University Institutional Review Board, and all material was acquired in
accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

All seven members of the core family participated in the genetic study. SNP
genotyping was performed for the four affected members using the Illumina Human
OmniExpress-24 BeadChip with approximately 700 000 markers, and the generated
data were processed using the Illumina Genome Studio (v.1.02) Genotyping Module.
Multipoint logarithm of odds (LOD) scores were calculated with Merlin and
ALLEGRO implemented in easyLINKAGE (v.5.08), in an autosomal recessive model
with full penetrance and assuming a disease allele frequency of 0.01. Genomic
deletions and duplications were detected through cnvPartition (v3.2.0) CNV
Analysis Plug-in for Genome Studio in the identified region. To assess whether
the genotypes in affected siblings and not the unaffected sister were the same
and thus whether the homozygosity was possibly due to identity by descent, the
genotype data were aligned on MS-Excel and compared. Haplotypes were constructed
by ALLEGRO.

Exome sequencing was performed for patient 402. The exome library created using
the Agilent SureSelect Target Enrichment System was sequenced on the Illumina
HiSeq2000 platform, and reads were aligned to the reference sequence hg19 (build
GRCh 37) using BWA0.5.9. Variants were called with SAMtools-0.1.14, and
annotation of the variants was performed by using ANNOVAR. Variants that were
not rare (ie, allele frequencies >0.01) and possibly not homozygous (ratios
of the number of reads with the variant to the number of reads with the
reference sequence <60%) were eliminated. The single candidate variant found
at the identified disease locus was validated by Sanger sequencing. Segregation
of the mutation in the family and testing of 205 Pakistani controls were
performed by single strand confirmational analyses (SSCP). Pakistani samples
were not included in public databases when we launched the study.

Relative quantification of PTRHD1 transcripts was performed
using cDNA generated from commercial total RNA extracted from 17 different
tissues: frontal cortex, parietal cortex, occipital cortex, corpus callosum,
cerebellum, brainstem, substantia nigra, pons, putamen, spinal cord, liver,
blood, skeletal muscle, adipose, bone marrow, and testis. Total RNA samples
(Clontech Laboratories, Inc. Palo Alto, CA, USA) were used as templates to
synthesize cDNA using a RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Fermentas,
Inc. Hanover, Maryland, USA). Real-time PCR was performed using a SYBR Green I
Master Kit on a LightCycler 480 (Roche). Housekeeping gene BETA
TUBULIN (TUBB) was used as the reference gene.
For real-time PCR reactions, a forward primer specific to exon 1 and a reverse
primer specific to the junction of exons 1 and 2 of PTRHD1 and
a forward specific to exon 1 and a reverse primer specific to exon 3 of
TUBB were used. The sequences of the primers, respectively,
are AAAGGATCTATCACAAGCTCCG, ATCTGGGGCCTCGAGGAC, ACATCCAGGCTGGTCAGTG, and
AAAGGACCTGAGCGAACAGA.

Pathogenicity of the detected variants was assessed through online bioinformatics
tools. For homology modeling, PTRHD1 reference sequence (NP_001013685.1) was
retrieved from NCBI. Tertiary structures of both reference and mutant proteins
were predicted first through SWISS-MODEL and then I-TASSER server
(https://aideepmed.com/I-TASSER/) [8].
Tertiary structures with best scores were selected for comparison. The
structures were visualized through VMD tool
(http://mordred.bioc.cam.ac.uk/~rapper/rampage.php) and Chimera
(https://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/chimera/).

Reportedly, pregnancy and delivery were unremarkable for all affected siblings,
who have ID and poor communication skills. They are able to perform simple tasks
and personal care but require some aid and supervision in more complex daily
activities. They are trained to perform simple manual work alongside with
unaffected family members in agricultural fields and animal farms. Hence, their
condition is concordant with mild or moderate forms of ID [1]. Other notable findings are mild developmental delay
manifesting with learning disability, language and speech delay, exotropia,
pectus excavatum, prominent clavicles, poor social-emotional skills, and
inability to communicate (Table 1). The
behavioral symptoms range from hyperactivity, aggressive behavior, attention
deficit, social isolation, and somniloquy (sleep-talking). The eldest brother
(401), aged 50 years, is the least affected of all siblings. He performs better
than his siblings in fieldwork and generally peaceful in contrast to his
affected brothers. Brothers 402 (aged 41 years) and 403 (aged 37 years) are
hyperactive, impulsive, and exhibit aggressive behavior, whereas their sister
404 (aged 34) is peaceful, socially isolated, and spends most of her time at
home. Brother 403 is the most severely affected of all. He stutters, has unclear
language, and is hyperactive with recurrent episodes of aggressive behavior. The
results of multiecho and multiplanar brain MRI were grossly unmarkable, but fine
detail of hippocampal morphology could not be reliably evaluated due to
suboptimal coronal coverage (Appendix A: Supplemental Figure 1). None of the
patients has facial dysmorphism. Symptoms of parkinsonism such as bradykinesia,
tremor, slowness to respond, and gait problems appeared in the fourth decade in
all three affected males. Gait examination revealed a mildly ataxic gait
characterized by impaired balance and coordination, without features of
spasticity or hemiparesis. There was no muscle rigidity or postural instability.
The appearance and worsening of symptoms were evident during subsequent visits
to the family. Affected female was at age 34 years during the last examination
and had not yet developed tremor or gait problem, yet showed signs of dementia
and slow response.

Parents and all five siblings participated in the genetic study. We first
performed linkage analysis using SNP genotype data to map the disease gene
locus, which yielded a single locus at 2p24.1-p23.3 with a maximal multipoint
LOD score of 3.25 (Appendix A: Supplemental Figure 2). The maximal region of
shared homozygosity is 1 909 724 bp, between rs2723137 (23 577 934 bp) and
rs6546045 (25 487 658 bp). Homozygosity was shared by affected siblings, but not
unaffected sibling and was compatible with identity by descent from a recent
common ancestor. At the time, Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) did not
report any relevant phenotype at the locus, and thus we launched exome
sequencing to search for the causative mutation.

In the linked region, the exome sequence file of patient 402 listed
PTRHD1 (NM_001013663) nonsynonymous c.155G>A
(p.Cys52Tyr; rs781442277) as the only candidate to underlie the pathology in the
family (Appendix A: Supplemental Table 1; Supplemental Figures 2, 3). The
variant was validated by Sanger sequencing (Figure
2) and segregated with the disease in the family. None of the 205
control samples from the Pakistani population tested positive for the variant,
and gnomAD reports only two heterozygotes worldwide; both are in the over 43 128
South Asian exomes that include many Pakistani samples. Mutation has a high
Combined Annotation-Dependent Depletion (CADD) score (29.9), indicating that it
is damaging to the protein. The only other rare or novel variant possibly
damaging was linked ITSN2 (NM_006277) c.2245G>A
(p.Glu749Lys; rs751750609), which was reported in one allele among the 82 838
alleles in gnomAD South Asian samples and one allele among the 1 169 828
European alleles. It has a very low CADD score (4.68), indicating that it is
predicted as benign.

PTRHD1 p.Cys52Tyr is predicted to lead to the substitution of
cysteine, which is nonpolar and neutral with polar and neutral tyrosine, most
probably affecting protein folding. PTRHD1 has 140 amino acids whose sequence is
totally conserved between human and chimpanzee, and Cys52 is conserved across
all species except for C. elegans according to HomoloGene
(Figure 1).

Two of the patients (402 and 403) were relatively more severely affected than the
other two. To search for a locus possibly harboring a homozygous mutation that
exacerbates the clinical phenotype in those patients, we performed linkage
analysis in a recessive model assuming those two as affected and the latter two
as unaffected. Maximal multipoint LOD score of 2.05 was obtained in many small
regions; however, none of those regions harbored a candidate variant.

The gene is reported with wide tissue expression in Expression Atlas
(http://www.ebi.ac.uk/gxa). We investigated its expression in the adult brain
and found expression in all regions assayed (Figure 1).

The majority of the in silico tools predicted the variant as
damaging or deleterious and CADD score is very high (Appendix A: Supplemental
Table 2). 3D models of the wild type (C-score=-0.53; Exp. TM-score = 0.65±0.13;
Exp. RMSD = 5.7±3.6Å) and mutant protein (C-score=-0.33; Exp. TM-score =
0.67±0.13; Exp. RMSD = 5.3±3.4Å) were predicted (Appendix A: Supplemental Figure
4; Supplemental Table 3). Mutated residue 52 falls in a helix. Superimposed
tertiary structure of the protein did not reveal much difference in the position
and number of α-helices and β-sheets. The structures were further analyzed for
potential differences in the binding of ligand, which revealed that one of the
three ligand-binding sites was abolished in the mutant protein (the putative
mutation falls close to a key ligand-binding site H53). Likely, the
aminoacyl-tRNA hydrolase activity is abrogated or compromised in the mutant
protein.

During the initial investigation of the presented family in 2015, patients were much
younger than the four patients with PTRHD1mutation reported at the
time, and thus an assessment of the early phase of the disease and subsequent follow
up was possible in the presented family. Patients initially presented with mild to
moderate ID with no remarkable associated anomalies except for the skeletal findings
of pectus excavatum and prominent clavicles. Finding the same p.Cys52Tyr mutation
reported in an Iranian sibling-pair verified that the mutation underlies ID in both
families [7].

We found expression of PTRHD1 in different brain parts including the
striatum and putamen, which are involved in degenerative neurological disorders. In
addition, deletions at 2p23 that encompass the gene have been associated with
several ID syndromes [9-11]. The PTH2 (peptidyl-tRNA hydrolase 2) domain is common in
the protein family to which PTRHD1 belongs. Yeast Pth2 is ubiquitin-like (UBL)
domain-binding protein known to participate in the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway.
Overexpression of yeast Pth2 has been shown to retard
ubiquitin-mediated degradation [12]. The
ubiquitin proteasome system plays a role in brain development, synaptic plasticity,
and long-term memory formation [13].

To date, ten families have been reported with homozygous PTRHD1
mutation (Table 2) [6,7,14-18]. An Iranian family carried the same p.Cys52Tyr
mutation as the family we present, three families carried p.Ala57Argfs*26, a large
kinship comprising three related families carried p.Arg122Gln, and one family
carried p.His53Tyr. With the presented family, the seven known families have in
total four mutations. The majority of the families are from the Middle East, and all
have parental consanguinity.

We had launched our study on the presented family before the first family with
PTRHD1 mutation was reported [6]. Since at the time there were a large number of loci and genes
involved in ID and parkinsonism, we performed linkage analysis and subsequent exome
sequencing but did not publish our findings because patients were too young to
manifest parkinsonism. We followed up with the family until patients were 34 to 50
years old, facilitating the observation of early signs of the PTRHD1
mutation as well as the late onset symptoms.

Early onset and nonprogressive ID in the four siblings were assessed as mild to
moderate whereas signs of parkinsonism such as bradykinesia, resting tremor, slow
body movements, gait problems, and dementia were found only in the recent clinical
re-evaluation, after the affected siblings reached their fourth decade. It is worth
mentioning that extrapyramidal features including bradykinesia and resting tremor
are characteristics of parkinsonism whereas gait abnormality was presented as mild
ataxic rather than a typical parkinsonian feature. Yet, the affected female at age
34 years had not developed these symptoms but only showed signs of dementia and slow
response. Remarkably, patients have unusual features of exotropia, pectus excavatum,
and prominent clavicles, which have not been reported in the 10 families afflicted
with the PTRHD1-related disease. Furthermore, the presence of mild
gait ataxia suggests that the neurological phenotype associated with
PTRHD1 mutation may extend beyond pure parkinsonism to involve
cerebellar or cerebellar network dysfunction, a feature increasingly recognized in
complex genetic movement disorders.

This study confirms that biallelic mutation in PTRHD1 causes early
onset ID, behavioral problems, and parkinsonism. However, symptoms of parkinsonism
could be atypical. Hence, elderly individuals with parkinsonism, particularly
accompanied with behavioral problems and/or low IQ or borderline ID, could be tested
for PTRHD1 mutation. We propose also that testing of children
afflicted with idiopathic ID with behavioral problems could benefit families.
