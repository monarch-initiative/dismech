# Efficacy and safety of marstacimab prophylaxis in hemophilia A/B with inhibitors: results from the phase 3 BASIS trial.
**Authors:** Matino D, Acharya SS, Taylor CT, Sun P, Agathon D, Raje S, Gould T, Palladino A, Mahlangu J, Lissitchkov T, Todorova M, Chan A, Carcao M, Sun J, Yang R, Wu R, Jin C, Zeng X, Boban A, Bilic E, Frenzel L, Fung Chan GC, Kong Li C, Apte S, Choraria N, Chistolini A, Marchesini E, Peyvandi F, Fujii T, Matsushita T, Kaneda M, Lyu CJ, Park YS, Kim S, Martinez LV, Adrian JM, Wali Y, Al Khabori M, Kurtov I, Alzahrani H, Zaher G, Micic D, Miljic P, Kostic G, Vucic M, Djurdjevic P, Hidalgo OB, Porras JG, Jiménez-Yuste V, Calvo Villas JM, Lopez Fernandez MF, Albayrak C, Okan V, Balkan C, Sahin F, Antmen A, Unal E, Visweshwar N, Sharathkumar A, Kruse-Jarres R

**DOI:** [10.1182/blood.2025031065](https://doi.org/10.1182/blood.2025031065)

## Content

Marstacimab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits tissue factor pathway inhibitor, is approved for prophylactic use in individuals with hemophilia A or B without inhibitors. We present efficacy and safety for individuals with inhibitors. The open-label, single-arm, phase 3 study evaluated once-weekly subcutaneous flat-dose marstacimab in males aged 12 to <75 years with severe hemophilia A or moderately severe to severe hemophilia B. Participants with inhibitors received bypassing agents (on-demand or routine prophylaxis) during a 6-month observational phase (OP) before entering a 12-month active treatment phase (ATP) with marstacimab. Primary end points were annualized bleeding rate (ABR) of treated bleeds and safety. Of 60 participants with inhibitors in the OP, 51 entered the ATP and received marstacimab. In the on-demand group (n = 48), mean estimated ABR declined from 19.78 (95% confidence interval [CI], 16.12-24.27) in the OP to 1.39 (95% CI, 0.85-2.29) during the ATP (ABR ratio, 0.07 [95% CI, 0.042-0.118]; 2-sided P< .0001). Results were consistent by hemophilia type (ABR ratio, 0.05 [hemophilia A, n = 40]; 0.13 [hemophilia B, n = 8]). Participants reported significant improvements in health-related quality of life. Adverse events were common but mostly mild; 1 treatment-related grade 3 skin rash led to discontinuation. Antidrug antibodies were detected in 19.6% of participants, with no apparent effect on efficacy or safety. In participants with inhibitors, marstacimab was associated with reduced bleeding rates and an acceptable safety profile, with no thromboembolic events. Marstacimab may be a viable treatment option for people with hemophilia A or B with inhibitors. This trial was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov as #NCT03938792. ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03938792.

In recent decades, replacement of deficient coagulation factors using clotting factor VIII (FVIII) or FIX concentrates has been the mainstay of treatment and prevention of bleeding episodes in people with hemophilia A and B without inhibitors.1,2 The efficacy of clotting factor concentrates is reduced by the generation of inhibitory antibodies in ∼20% to 35% of people with hemophilia A and up to 10% of people with hemophilia B, rendering replacement therapy ineffective.1,3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 People with hemophilia with inhibitors are at an increased risk of serious, organ- or life-threatening bleeding episodes, as well as joint disease, disabilities, and premature death.9,10 In this population, bypassing agents that generate thrombin despite a lack of FVIII/FIX activity are typically used to treat acute bleeding episodes.1,2,11,12 However, the effectiveness of these agents is often suboptimal and inconsistent, and their use is associated with the risk of thrombotic events.2 Routine prophylaxis (RP) with bypassing agents is particularly challenging, requiring frequent intravenous infusions while being less effective than standard prophylactic factor replacement therapy in people without inhibitors.13,14 Immune tolerance induction aims to eradicate inhibitors via regular administration of high-dose infusions of the deficient factor to induce tolerance.11,15,16 However, immune tolerance induction necessitates frequent, long-term administrations, at times necessitating the use of a central venous catheter, which can be complicated with risks of infection and thrombosis and, in hemophilia B, may be associated with a high risk of anaphylactic reactions and nephrotic syndrome.11,15, 16, 17 These treatment burdens often result in poor adherence, which is associated with increased bleeding risk, resulting in structural joint damage, worsening function, and reduced health-related quality of life (HRQoL).18 Overall, therefore, improved management of hemophilia in people who develop inhibitors is needed to reduce morbidity and mortality in this population.15,19

Treatment challenges associated with factor replacement therapy and bypassing agents have led to the development of various long-acting, subcutaneously administered nonfactor alternatives with novel mechanisms of action independent of FVIII/FIX and, thus, inhibitor status.11,16 These include emicizumab, which mimics FVIII cofactor activity, and is approved for the prophylactic treatment of people with hemophilia A, with or without inhibitors.19, 20, 21 Additionally, concizumab, an anti–tissue factor pathway inhibitor (anti-TFPI) antibody, and the antithrombin small interfering RNA fitusiran are approved for hemophilia A and B, with or without inhibitors.22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27 However, all of these agents have been associated with serious thrombotic and/or thromboembolic adverse events (AE) in clinical trials, and the US labels for emicizumab, concizumab, and fitusiran include warnings for thrombotic/thromboembolic events.16,20,24,28, 29, 30, 31 Thus, there remains an unmet need for safe and effective treatment options that can be used prophylactically across all hemophilia subsets, that is, those with hemophilia A or B, with or without inhibitors.

Marstacimab is an anti-TFPI monoclonal antibody approved for the prophylactic treatment of people with hemophilia A or B without inhibitors.32,33 Marstacimab targets the Kunitz 2 domain of TFPI to prevent inhibition of the activated FX/FVII–tissue factor complex, resulting in increased thrombin generation and improved hemostasis, independent of FVIII and FIX.34 A favorable risk-benefit profile was observed in a phase 1b/2 study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02974855)35 and its long-term phase 2 follow-up (NCT03363321)36 evaluating multiple ascending doses of marstacimab in participants with hemophilia A or B with or without inhibitors. The initial approval of marstacimab was based on results from the noninhibitor cohort of the phase 3 BASIS trial (NCT03938792), which assessed the safety and efficacy of marstacimab in people with hemophilia A or B, with or without inhibitors.37 Marstacimab treatment led to a significant reduction in the annualized bleeding rate (ABR) of treated bleeds compared with previous factor replacement therapy in participants without inhibitors.37 We present the efficacy and safety results from the inhibitor cohort of the pivotal phase 3 BASIS trial.

BASIS (www.ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03938792) was a 1-way crossover, open-label, multicenter phase 3 trial in adolescent and adult participants with severe hemophilia A or moderately severe to severe hemophilia B with or without inhibitors (supplemental Figure 1, available on the Blood website). Participants were enrolled into 1 of 2 cohorts depending on the presence of inhibitors. Full details of the BASIS study design and results of the noninhibitor cohort have been published previously.37 Herein, we report the results for the inhibitor cohort.

BASIS, conducted at 52 centers across 19 countries, began on 9 March 2020 and was completed by 29 April 2025. The study consisted of an ∼45-day screening period, a 6-month observation phase (OP) during which participants received either on-demand (OD) or RP treatment, followed by a 12-month active treatment phase (ATP) and a 1-month follow-up phase. Participants who completed the parent study could continue to receive marstacimab in an open-label extension (OLE) study (NCT05145127); these participants were not required to complete the 1-month follow-up phase.

The study was conducted in accordance with the International Council for Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines, the Declaration of Helsinki, and other applicable laws and regulations. Participants or their legally authorized representative provided written informed consent. The protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board or independent ethics committees at each participating site, and an independent data monitoring committee oversaw the safety and conduct of the trial. Information on data handling, quality assurance, and the role of the sponsor have been published previously.37

Eligible participants were males, aged 12 to <75 years, with severe hemophilia A (FVIII <1%) or moderately severe to severe hemophilia B (FIX ≤2%) and a body weight ≥35 kg at screening. Participants enrolled in the inhibitor cohort had documentation of a current high-titer (≥5 BU/mL) or low-titer (<5 BU/mL) inhibitor refractory to FVIII/FIX replacement, with FVIII/FIX recovery <60% of expected within the 6 months before enrollment. Participants with documented inhibitors not meeting these criteria (eg, condition precluding rechallenge with FVIII/FIX) could be considered for enrollment on a case-by-case basis. The OD cohort enrolled participants who had experienced ≥6 (hemophilia A) or ≥4 (hemophilia B) spontaneous or traumatic bleeding episodes requiring treatment during the 6 months before enrollment and were willing to continue OD treatment during the OP. Participants on RP (defined as treatment with bypassing agent to prevent bleeding) who demonstrated ≥80% compliance with prophylaxis regimen during the previous 6 months were considered for enrollment on a case-by-case basis.

Exclusion criteria included known hemostatic defect other than hemophilia A/B, abnormal renal or hepatic function, abnormal hematology values, corrected QT interval above acceptable values, hypersensitivity or allergic reaction to the study intervention, and previous/current treatment for or a history of coronary artery diseases, ischemic disease, or venous or arterial thrombosis. Acceptable laboratory values for prothrombin time/international normalized ratio and fibrinogen (obtained at or after the enrollment visit) were required before dosing.

During the 12-month ATP, participants received a single loading dose of 300-mg subcutaneous marstacimab administered as 2× 150-mg injections, followed by a once-weekly dose of 150 mg with a prefilled syringe. Dose escalation to 300 mg was allowed at the investigator’s discretion after day 180 for participants who met protocol-specified criteria based on breakthrough bleeding. Participant treatment adherence was assessed by a review of participant- or caregiver-completed eDiary at each study visit. Participants were required to maintain ≥80% adherence.

The primary efficacy end point was ABR for treated bleeding events with marstacimab treatment compared with previous OD therapy during the OP. ABR was calculated as the number of bleeds requiring treatment divided by (days on treatment period divided by 365.25). If a participant did not complete a treatment period, the days on treatment ended at the last dosing day plus 6 days. Key secondary end points included ABR for specific bleed types (joint bleeds, spontaneous bleeds, target joint bleeds [in baseline target joints identified at screening], and total bleeds [treated and untreated]), change in Hemophilia Joint Health Score (HJHS), and patient-reported HRQoL outcomes (Haemophilia Quality of Life Questionnaire for Adults [Haem-A-QoL] and EuroQol 5 Dimensions 5 Level [EQ-5D-5L]). Safety assessments included AE of special interest (including thrombotic events, injection site reactions, hypersensitivity, and anaphylactic reactions), laboratory tests, and immunogenicity (antidrug antibodies [ADA] and neutralizing antibodies [nAb]). Exploratory end points included pharmacokinetic (PK)/pharmacodynamic (PD) parameters, number of target joints, and bypassing agent consumption. Additional details are provided in supplemental Section 1.

Details of the study assessments have been published previously.37 Briefly, bleeding episodes, infusions of medications to treat bleeds, and study treatment injections were recorded by the participant or caregiver using the eDiary, which was reviewed by the investigator at study visits. Joint health was assessed using HJHS version 2.1. HRQoL assessments were collected at scheduled office visits using an electronic device (tablet) preloaded with the instruments. Safety data were collected from the time of informed consent to ≥28 days after last study drug administration. ADA, nAb, and PK/PD parameters were determined using validated laboratory-developed assays. The number of target joints occurring during the OP and ATP was derived from the eDiary by counting joints that met the definition (ie, major joints into which ≥3 spontaneous bleeds occurred in a 6-month period) during the given treatment phase.

Type I error across primary and key secondary end points was controlled through a predetermined hierarchical testing approach at the 1-sided 0.025 level (supplemental Section 2). All reported P values are 2-sided. A sample size of 25 participants was required to provide at least 90% power to demonstrate superiority of marstacimab vs OD, which was declared when the upper limit of the 2-sided 95% confidence interval (CI) for the ratio of the ABR for treated bleeds (marstacimab:OD) was <0.5. For the OD cohort, data were analyzed using repeated-measures negative binomial models using the log link, with the number of bleeds as a response variable, treatment as a factor, and time on treatment in log years as an offset variable to account for different duration on treatment. Any data collected after dose escalation were not included in the primary end point assessment to avoid overestimation of the effect of the intended dose. Data after discontinuation of treatment, if collected, were not included. Participants on prior RP were allowed to enroll to supplement the safety evaluation of the inhibitor cohort. The efficacy in these participants was summarized separately using descriptive statistics without any hypothesis testing. For HRQoL end points, change from baseline at 6 months was compared between the OP and ATP using the Wilcoxon signed rank test (within-participant comparison), with treatment effect estimated as the median difference.

Efficacy assessments were performed in the modified intention-to-treat population, defined as all participants who completed the OP and received ≥1 dose of marstacimab in the ATP (excluding participants on RP in the OP). Safety analyses included (1) all participants in the RP group who received ≥1 prophylactic or OD treatment during the OP and all participants in the OD group who completed any OP baseline procedures (all safety population); and (2) all participants who received ≥1 dose of marstacimab in the ATP (marstacimab safety population). See supplemental Section 2 for further statistical methods.

Of 107 screened male participants with hemophilia with inhibitors, 60 (OD, n = 57; RP, n = 3) entered the 6-month OP, and 51 (OD, n = 48; RP, n = 3) entered the 12-month ATP and received ≥1 dose of marstacimab (Figure 1; supplemental Table 1). Across all participants who entered the OP, 47 (78.3%) had hemophilia A, and 13 (21.7%) had hemophilia B (Table 1). The median age was 23 years (range, 12-75); 16 participants (26.7%) were adolescents, and 44 (73.3%) were adults. Most participants were Asian (53.3%) or White (31.7%). The median marstacimab treatment duration was 364 days (range, 259-406; supplemental Table 2), and adherence was 98.2%. Overall, 4 participants in the OD group (3 with hemophilia A and 1 with hemophilia B) met the protocol-defined criteria and had their dose escalated to 300 mg once weekly. Participants who had their dose escalated had a median age of 30.5 years (range, 22-50) and mean body mass index of 21.6 kg/m2 (standard deviation [SD], 2.05).

The mean ABR of treated bleeds was reduced to 1.39 (95% CI, 0.85-2.29) with marstacimab in the ATP from 19.78 (95% CI, 16.12-24.27) with prior OD in the OP (estimated ABR ratio, 0.07; 95% CI, 0.042-0.118; P < .0001), demonstrating the superiority of marstacimab over OD therapy (Figure 2A; supplemental Table 3). The median ABR was 0.00 (range, 0.00-14.91) with marstacimab in the ATP vs 16.42 (range, 0.00-69.10) in the OP. Numerical reductions in mean ABR for treated bleeds were reported for the 3 RP participants (1.03 [SD, 1.02] during the ATP vs 7.61 [SD, 2.95] during the OP). In the OD group, 26 of 48 participants (54.2%) completed the ATP and had no treated bleeds vs 1 of 48 (2.1%) in the OP. Results were consistent by hemophilia type, age, and geographic subgroups in the OD group (supplemental Figure 2; supplemental Table 4). The estimated ABR ratio was 0.05 (95% CI, 0.032-0.094) for participants with hemophilia A (n = 40) and 0.13 (95% CI, 0.049-0.363) for participants with hemophilia B (n = 8). In general, participants who had their dose escalated had fewer bleeds after dose escalation (supplemental Table 5). ABRs for treated bleeds improved over time in the ATP (supplemental Table 6; supplemental Figure 3), and reductions were observed regardless of the number of target joints participants had at baseline (supplemental Figure 4). Although the estimated median difference in HJHS was small (supplemental Table 7), the mean number of target joints decreased from the OP (0.6 [SD, 0.91]) to the ATP (0.0 [SD, 0.20]; supplemental Figure 6). During the ATP, 48 participants with prior OD therapy had a mean bypass agent exposure of 3 days (range, 0-35) per participant. Similar amounts of bypassing agents were used to treat bleeding episodes between the OP and ATP (activated prothrombin complex concentrate, 39.83 vs 34.24 IU/kg per dose; recombinant activated factor VII, 111.41 vs 94.20 μg/kg per dose); however, the total annualized amount of bypassing agents used was numerically lower in the ATP (activated prothrombin complex concentrate, 1356.78 vs 106.80 IU/kg per year; recombinant activated factor VII, 5760.83 vs 1094.37 μg/kg per year; supplemental Table 8; supplemental Figure 7).

During the ATP, 38 participants (74.5%) experienced AE, most of which were mild to moderate in severity; the most frequently reported were COVID-19 (the timing of study conduct coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic), upper respiratory tract infection, fibrin D-dimer increased, and headache (Table 2). Five participants reported serious AE (SAE) during the OP. One participant reported an SAE during the ATP (treatment-related skin rash [grade 3] occurring ∼8 months after the first dose, which had resolved by the last follow-up), which also led to study discontinuation. One other participant discontinued from the study due to an AE (during the OP). Ten participants had their marstacimab dose temporarily interrupted (8 due to confirmed/suspected COVID-19 and 1 due to skin rash) or reduced (1 due to abdominal pain). No fatal or thrombotic events were reported in this cohort of participants. No clinically significant laboratory abnormalities were observed. ADA were detected in 10 of 51 participants (19.6%; supplemental Table 9); titers were low, and 9 of 10 cases resolved by the end of study. The presence of ADA had no impact on ABR (supplemental Table 10), incidence of AE (supplemental Table 11), or PK or PD end points, including thrombin generation (supplemental Figures 8-12). nAb were detected in 2 participants and resolved in both by the end of study.

Marstacimab plasma concentrations reached steady state by ∼60 days. Treatment-related increases within physiological ranges were observed for all PD assessments (median range at steady state in adults, 251-293 ng/mL [total TFPI], 49-70 nM [peak thrombin], 523-690 pmol/L [prothrombin fragment 1+2], and 0.3 μg/mL [D-dimer]). There were no clinical findings suggesting that PD biomarker changes were reflective of a pattern of excessive coagulation. There was no clinically relevant impact of hemophilia type or presence of ADA on marstacimab PK or PD. Additional details are provided in supplemental Section 4.

In participants aged 12 to <75 years with hemophilia A or B with inhibitors, prophylactic treatment with subcutaneous marstacimab 150 mg once weekly significantly reduced the ABR of treated bleeds in the ATP vs prior OD therapy in the OP, demonstrating the superiority of marstacimab compared with prior OD bypassing agent treatment. Participants receiving prior RP also had a lower mean ABR for treated bleeds in the ATP vs the OP; due to the small sample size, no statistical comparisons were made. ABR reductions were generally consistent across subgroups, including hemophilia type, age, and geographic region; however, these results should be interpreted with caution given the small sample sizes, broad CIs for some subgroups, and lack of sufficient power to assess superiority. Notably, among the 4 participants (8%) who had their dose escalated to marstacimab 300 mg, improvements in ABR for treated bleeds were observed without any notable treatment-related increases in AE, suggesting dose adjustments could be considered by physicians to improve outcomes in individuals who require better bleeding control based on clinical criteria. Bleeding rates with marstacimab prophylaxis in this study were comparable to those reported in clinical trials of other nonfactor therapies (ie, concizumab and fitusiran) in participants with hemophilia A or B with inhibitors,29,38 although these studies used doses of rebalancing agents that were modified after study pauses due to safety events.30,31 For participants with hemophilia A with inhibitors, bleeding rates with marstacimab were lower than those reported in a phase 3 trial of emicizumab28; however, improvements in bleeding rates have been reported with longer-term use of emicizumab,39 similar to time-dependent improvements observed with marstacimab in participants without inhibitors.37 Without a direct comparison, any conclusions about the relative hemostatic efficacy of prophylactic agents remain uncertain. For participants with inhibitors, the long-term impact of marstacimab prophylaxis on bleeding rates will be further explored in the BASIS OLE study.

Hemophilia is associated with a substantial detrimental impact on the physical, social, and emotional well-being of affected individuals.40,41 HRQoL is also negatively affected by treatment burden.40 That said, several studies have identified a significant association between treatment adherence and better HRQoL.18 Although there is a paucity of data on whether people with hemophilia show better adherence to subcutaneous nonfactor therapy than IV factor replacement or bypassing agent therapy, high rates of adherence (87%-90%) with emicizumab have been reported in a real-world setting.42 Moreover, systematic/literature reviews have reported that patients generally prefer subcutaneous over IV administration.18,43 Overall, therefore, well-tolerated treatments with simple administration and reduced dosing frequencies may improve HRQoL by both enhancing adherence to prophylaxis and alleviating concerns associated with treatment administration. In this study, adherence was high in the OD group (98.1%), and marstacimab prophylaxis demonstrated superiority in all secondary HRQoL end points except EQ-VAS score. Together, these data suggest that, in addition to reducing bleeding events, marstacimab prophylaxis also improves HRQoL and reduces hemophilia treatment burden. Follow-up of HRQoL measures throughout the OLE study will provide further insights into the long-term impact of marstacimab.

Marstacimab was well tolerated over 12 months, with an acceptable safety profile, consistent with findings from the noninhibitor cohort of the BASIS trial37 and earlier marstacimab studies.35,36 One participant experienced an SAE of grade 3 skin rash, considered related to marstacimab, and discontinued from the ATP. The SAE was resolved at last follow-up. Overall, there was a low incidence of injection site reactions (n = 4 [7.8%]), most of which were transient and mild in severity. The timing of the study coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, and COVID-19 was one of the most frequently reported AE. COVID-19 infection has been associated with increased thrombotic risk.44,45 To mitigate potential additional thrombotic risk, marstacimab prophylaxis was suspended for any participant with confirmed/suspected symptomatic COVID-19. A total of 8 participants had their marstacimab dose temporarily interrupted for this reason. No participant discontinued the study due to COVID-19. No treatment-related increases in PD biomarkers were reflective of a pattern of excessive coagulation, and there were no thromboembolic events. To date, 1 thrombotic event has been reported with marstacimab. A non–life-threatening deep vein thrombosis was reported in the OLE study in a participant without inhibitors with multiple prothrombotic risk factors who had been treated with marstacimab for 3 years.46 In this cohort of participants with inhibitors, <20% developed antibodies to marstacimab, similar to the incidence of ADA reported with marstacimab (19.8%) in participants without inhibitors37 and with concizumab (26%) in participants with inhibitors.29 ADA resolved in the majority of participants (90%) by study completion and did not have a clinically relevant impact on efficacy, safety, PK, or PD. After marstacimab administration, total plasma TFPI levels increased, indicative of the formation of a marstacimab-TFPI complex, consistent with previous observations in healthy volunteers47 and participants with hemophilia without inhibitors.37 Of note, peak thrombin values in this study (49 nM-70 nM) were within the range observed for nonhemophilic participants in a phase 1 study of marstacimab.47 Extended follow-up in the OLE study will continue to investigate the long-term safety of marstacimab in participants with inhibitors.

Limitations of this study include the open-label, 1-way crossover study design. Comparisons were made between the OP and ATP, before and after marstacimab administration, without randomization or blinding. This design may introduce biases that confound the interpretation of results, including regression to the mean, placebo effects, or increased clinical attention during the ATP. The study was designed to compare the efficacy of marstacimab with prior OD therapy with bypassing agents, limiting the ability to directly compare efficacy outcomes with existing nonfactor or bypassing agent prophylactic regimens. Second, the sample size was small, with a particularly limited number of participants with hemophilia B. Subgroup analyses, particularly across age, hemophilia type, and geography, were not powered for demonstrating superiority and should be treated as hypothesis generating rather than confirmatory. Only 4 participants underwent dose escalation; more data, especially from the OLE study, are needed to confirm the efficacy and/or safety of higher doses of marstacimab. No thrombotic events or deaths were observed in participants with inhibitors during the ATP; however, the sample size was insufficient to fully characterize thrombotic events. Third, the short duration of the follow-up period may limit understanding of the long-term efficacy and safety of marstacimab, including the durability of bleeding control. Nearly 20% of participants developed ADA, although most resolved, and the presence of ADA did not correlate with reduced efficacy or increased AE. Nevertheless, longer-term immunogenicity surveillance is needed, particularly with repeated or escalated dosing. Limitations of the study assessments should also be considered. Reported improvements in HRQoL, although statistically significant, were based on within-subject comparisons without a placebo or active comparator group, and improvements in EQ-VAS score did not reach statistical significance. Interpretability is also limited by the potential for recall bias in self-reported instruments. The absence of meaningful improvement in HJHS is unsurprising given the short duration of follow-up and participants’ advanced arthropathy at baseline. Although reductions in the number of target joints are encouraging, the clinical relevance of bleeding reduction without demonstrable joint benefit remains uncertain over the long term. Finally, although high adherence was reported, the controlled trial environment and use of eDiaries likely overestimates real-world adherence. Whether these high levels of compliance and efficacy can be replicated outside of trial settings remains to be demonstrated.

Overall, these findings demonstrate the efficacy and safety of fixed-dose once-weekly subcutaneous injections of marstacimab up to 12 months in adolescents and adults with hemophilia A or B with inhibitors. Together with findings from the noninhibitor cohort,37 these results suggest that marstacimab may help to address an unmet need for treatment options that can be used by people with hemophilia regardless of hemophilia type or inhibitor status.
