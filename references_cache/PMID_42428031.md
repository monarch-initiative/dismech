# Metastatic Testicular Germ Cell Tumor in a Patient With Neurofibromatosis Type 1: Treatment With Trametinib Based on NF1 Gene Mutation.
**Authors:** Goto K, Kijima T, Sato Y, Watanabe K, Kage H, Oda K, Shimoi T, Ishida K, Kume H, Kamai T
**Journal:** IJU Case Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1002/iju5.70223](https://doi.org/10.1002/iju5.70223)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is a genetic disorder that increases the risk of various tumors. However, its association with testicular germ cell tumors (GCTs) is rare. We report a case of metastatic GCT in an NF1 patient treated with precision medicine.

CASE PRESENTATION: A 40-year-old male with NF1 presented with a left testicular non-seminomatous GCT and lung metastases. The tumor showed progressive disease after chemotherapy (VIP, TGP) and surgical removal of metastases. Comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) test found a pathogenic NF1 frameshift mutation (p.Q514fs*43). Based on this finding, the patient received the MEK inhibitor trametinib under patient-proposed healthcare services (BELIEVE Trial). Treatment resulted in normalization of serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) for 6 months before the tumor eventually progressed. The patient died at 39 months after diagnosis.

CONCLUSION: This is the first report of trametinib for a metastatic GCT with an NF1 mutation, suggesting a possible but limited treatment effect.

TRIAL REGISTRATION: The prospective trial of patient-proposed healthcare services with multiple targeted agents based on the results of gene profiling by multigene panel test (BELIEVE), NCCH1901/jRCTs031190104.

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is a genetic disorder caused by mutations in the NF1 tumor suppressor gene. Patients with NF1 have a higher risk of developing various malignancies [ 1 , 2 ]. The most common malignancies in NF1 patients are malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs) and central nervous system tumors [ 3 ]. However, the association between NF1 and testicular germ cell tumors (GCTs) is rare, with only a few cases reported [ 4 , 5 ].

The NF1 gene produces neurofibromin, a protein that controls the Ras signaling pathway. Loss of neurofibromin leads to hyperactivation of the Ras/MEK/ERK pathway, driving cell growth and survival [ 6 , 7 ]. This pathway is a key therapeutic target in NF1‐related tumors.

Treatment of platinum‐resistant metastatic GCTs remains difficult, with few effective options beyond salvage chemotherapy and surgery [ 8 ]. Cancer genomic medicine has opened new possibilities for precision medicine by identifying actionable mutations in individual tumors.

Here, we present a case of an NF1 patient with a chemotherapy‐resistant metastatic GCT harboring a pathogenic NF1 frameshift mutation treated with the MEK inhibitor trametinib.

A 40‐year‐old Japanese male with NF1 (von Recklinghausen's disease) presented with a left testicular tumor. He had no family history of NF1. Three months prior, he had presented to the dermatology department with pain and swelling of the right thigh. Physical examination revealed multiple café‐au‐lait macules and cutaneous neurofibromas. A subcutaneous tumor (9 × 10 cm) in the right thigh was excised with suspicion of MPNST. Pathology confirmed malignant triton tumor (MPNST with rhabdomyoblastic differentiation). Based on multiple café‐au‐lait macules, cutaneous neurofibromas, and MPNST, the patient was clinically diagnosed with NF1 per the NIH diagnostic criteria. Adjuvant radiotherapy was administered to the right thigh for MPNST. During radiotherapy, he developed left testicular swelling and pain, leading to the discovery of testicular cancer.

He underwent a left high orchiectomy. Pathological examination showed a non‐seminomatous mixed GCT (70% teratoma, 20% embryonal carcinoma, 10% yolk sac tumor, < 1% seminoma) (Figure 1). CT showed lung metastases in the right lung. Initial serum AFP was elevated at 349 ng/mL; human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) was less than 0.1 mIU/mL. After orchiectomy, AFP decreased to 36 ng/mL. The patient was classified as good prognosis per IGCCCG criteria based on the post‐orchiectomy AFP level.

He received four cycles of VIP (etoposide, ifosfamide, and cisplatin) chemotherapy. AFP decreased to 12.3 ng/mL but plateaued above normal. CT showed progressive disease (PD) of the right lung metastasis; as this was the only metastatic site, surgical resection was chosen. A right S3 partial lung resection was performed at 4 months after diagnosis. After surgery, AFP increased again and a new left lung metastasis appeared. He received three cycles of TGP (paclitaxel, gemcitabine, and cisplatin) chemotherapy. AFP decreased but again plateaued above normal, and as this remained a solitary metastasis, a left upper lobe partial resection was performed at 10 months. Pathology of both lung specimens showed metastatic teratoma (Figure 2). Immunohistochemical analysis of the primary testicular tumor and lung metastases confirmed their GCT origin and excluded MPNST (Figures S1 and S2).

Pathological findings of the lung metastases. (A) Right lung metastasis showing cells resembling immature rhabdomyoid tissue (H&E, × 150). (B) Left lung metastasis showing cells resembling immature rhabdomyoid tissue (H&E, × 150). Scale bars: 100 μm.

At 11 months after diagnosis, comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) using FoundationOne CDx was performed on the primary testicular tumor. The test found a pathogenic frameshift mutation in the NF1 gene (c.1541_1542del, p.Q514fs*43). No other actionable mutations were found (Table S1). As the patient had a clinical diagnosis of NF1, the detected NF1 variant was considered likely to be of germline origin. After discussion with the patient, confirmatory germline testing was not performed. Following a recommendation from a multidisciplinary expert panel at 13 months, the patient was enrolled in patient‐proposed healthcare services (the BELIEVE Trial; NCCH1901, jRCTs031190104) to receive the MEK1/2 inhibitor trametinib.

Trametinib was started at 17 months after diagnosis at a dose of 2 mg/day. The dose was reduced to 1.5 mg/day due to skin rash and elevated creatine kinase. AFP gradually normalized by 5 months after starting trametinib (Figure 3). CT at 4 months showed a slight increase in lung metastases (Figure 4), but the change was within stable disease (SD) by RECIST, and AFP continued to decrease, so trametinib was continued. However, CT at 6 months showed clear PD, and trametinib was discontinued. The patient was transferred to palliative care and died 39 months after the initial diagnosis.

This case report is, to our knowledge, the first to describe the use of a MEK inhibitor for a metastatic GCT in a patient with NF1. The association between NF1 and testicular cancer is rare [ 4 ]. Existing reports mainly describe seminomas or teratomas, making this case of a chemotherapy‐resistant, mixed non‐seminomatous GCT particularly important [ 4 , 5 , 9 ].

Given the patient's history of MPNST, distinguishing metastatic teratoma from MPNST was critical. The rhabdomyoid cells in both the primary tumor and lung metastases were desmin‐positive and SALL4‐negative. The lung lesions were negative for S‐100 and SOX10, and retained H3K27me3 expression. Loss of H3K27me3 is highly specific for MPNST [ 10 ]; its retention strongly excluded MPNST and confirmed metastatic teratoma.

The pathogenic NF1 frameshift mutation provided a strong rationale for targeting the downstream MEK/ERK pathway. Loss‐of‐function mutations in NF1 lead to constitutive Ras activation, driving tumor growth in various NF1‐related tumors [ 6 ]. MEK inhibitors, such as trametinib, have shown clinical activity in other NF1‐related malignancies, including low‐grade gliomas and plexiform neurofibromas [ 11 , 12 ]. While trametinib has shown variable results in NF1‐mutated melanoma, its role in GCTs has not been studied [ 13 ].

The clinical course showed both the potential and the limitations of this targeted approach. Although imaging showed eventual PD, AFP normalized during trametinib treatment, suggesting a biological effect of MEK inhibition. This is consistent with cytostatic rather than cytotoxic effects often seen with MEK inhibitors in other solid tumors [ 14 , 15 ]. The discrepancy between tumor marker response and imaging findings may reflect the heterogeneous nature of GCTs, where the AFP‐producing component responded to treatment while other components, such as teratoma, continued to grow.

However, the eventual progression highlights the complexity of GCT biology and likely resistance mechanisms. While the NF1 mutation was a clear driver, other signaling pathways may have enabled escape from MEK inhibition [ 8 , 16 ]. Future strategies for NF1‐driven tumors may require combination therapies targeting multiple signaling molecules [ 17 ]. A limitation of this report is the lack of confirmatory germline testing, although the clinical diagnosis of NF1 strongly suggests the germline origin of the mutation.

This case demonstrates the value of CGP test in identifying treatable targets in rare and resistant cancers. While trametinib provided temporary clinical benefit with AFP normalization, it was insufficient for a durable response. This report adds to the limited literature on GCTs in NF1 patients and highlights the need for combination targeted therapies.
