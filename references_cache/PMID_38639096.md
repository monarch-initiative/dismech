BACKGROUND: While our understanding of the single-cell gene expression patterns 
underlying the transformation of vascular cell types during the progression of 
atherosclerosis is rapidly improving, the clinical and pathophysiological 
relevance of these changes remains poorly understood.
METHODS: Single-cell RNA sequencing data generated with SmartSeq2 (≈8000 
genes/cell) in 16 588 single cells isolated during atherosclerosis progression 
in Ldlr-/-Apob100/100 mice with human-like plasma lipoproteins and from humans 
with asymptomatic and symptomatic carotid plaques was clustered into multiple 
subtypes. For clinical and pathophysiological context, the advanced-stage and 
symptomatic subtype clusters were integrated with 135 tissue-specific 
(atherosclerotic aortic wall, mammary artery, liver, skeletal muscle, and 
visceral and subcutaneous, fat) gene-regulatory networks (GRNs) inferred from 
600 coronary artery disease patients in the STARNET (Stockholm-Tartu 
Atherosclerosis Reverse Network Engineering Task) study.
RESULTS: Advanced stages of atherosclerosis progression and symptomatic carotid 
plaques were largely characterized by 3 smooth muscle cells (SMCs), and 3 
macrophage subtype clusters with extracellular matrix organization/osteogenic 
(SMC), and M1-type proinflammatory/Trem2-high lipid-associated (macrophage) 
phenotypes. Integrative analysis of these 6 clusters with STARNET revealed 
significant enrichments of 3 arterial wall GRNs: GRN33 (macrophage), GRN39 
(SMC), and GRN122 (macrophage) with major contributions to coronary artery 
disease heritability and strong associations with clinical scores of coronary 
atherosclerosis severity. The presence and pathophysiological relevance of GRN39 
were verified in 5 independent RNAseq data sets obtained from the human coronary 
and aortic artery, and primary SMCs and by targeting its top-key drivers, FRZB 
and ALCAM in cultured human coronary artery SMCs.
CONCLUSIONS: By identifying and integrating the most gene-rich single-cell 
subclusters of atherosclerosis to date with a coronary artery disease framework 
of GRNs, GRN39 was identified and independently validated as being critical for 
the transformation of contractile SMCs into an osteogenic phenotype promoting 
advanced, symptomatic atherosclerosis.

Atherosclerosis is an underlying cause of myocardial infarction and stroke.

The plasticity of cells of the arterial wall during the progression of atherosclerosis is essential for understanding how stable atherosclerotic plaques become unstable, causing clinical events.

We generated an RNA sequence dataset of atherosclerosis obtained from atherosclerotic progression in Ldlr−/−Apob100/100 mice and from symptomatic and asymptomatic carotid plaques in humans.

We reidentified and markedly expanded the gene content of 3 inflammatory- and osteogenic-related smooth muscle cell (SMC) subtypes, and 3 M1-type proinflammatory and Trem2-high lipid-associated-like macrophage subtypes critical for the development of advanced and symptomatic atherosclerotic plaques.

We provided pathophysiological and clinical context to these 6 subcellular clusters by integrative analysis with a CAD framework of 135 human gene-regulatory networks (GRNs) identifying 3 arterial wall GRNs; the MP-specific GRN33 and GRN122, and the SMC-specific GRN39, of which GRN39 was reconfirmed in 5 independent human datasets and experimentally in human vascular smooth muscle cells.

We show that GRN39 is critical for the transformation of contractile SMCs into an osteogenic phenotype promoting advanced-stage and symptomatic atherosclerotic plaques.

Atherosclerosis, a late-onset disease of the arterial wall, is the underlying cause of myocardial infarction and stroke.1 The main cell types of the atherosclerotic arterial wall are endothelial cells (ECs), smooth muscle cells (SMCs), and different forms of CD45+ immune and inflammatory cells. However, in recent single-cell RNA sequence (scRNAseq) studies, it has become increasingly clear that these major cell types are highly plastic, transforming into subtypes in parallel with the progression of atherosclerosis. For instance, arterial wall SMCs have been shown to transform into osteogenic-like cells during late-stage atherosclerosis, both in mice2 and humans.3 This SMC plasticity seems to have multipotent differentiation capacity including generation of proatherogenic macrophage-like,4 and fibrochondrocyte-like cells, or alternatively, reversal back to a more native atheroprotective contractile form of SMCs.5

Besides SMCs, the single-cell atherosclerosis landscape of CD45+ immune cells and in part ECs have been studied both in humans6 and mice.2,4,7,8 In the atherosclerotic mouse aortas, mainly 2 populations of macrophage have been detected; lipid-poor proinflammatory macrophages9 and potentially less inflammatory lipid-laden TREM2-expressing macrophages.4,10 Furthermore, using lineage tracing, a portion of lesion foam cells have been shown to derive from SMCs3,4 that may become more atherogenic in the form of osteogenic-like cells.2 Overall, these observations have been supported by studies of human carotid plaques,11 which in addition have provided evidence of endothelial-mesenchymal transitions paralleled by a decline in CD4+ and CD8+ cell cytotoxicity.11 A decline that was not observed in an earlier study characterizing symptomatic carotid plaques where instead a subset of differentiated CD4+T-cells was found to be activated.6

To date, the design of scRNAseq studies has typically involved a relatively high number of sequenced cells isolated from a limited number of patients or mice.3,5,6,10,12 This study design prevents meaningful inference of potential clinical relevancies of identified cell subtypes. To compensate, some studies have sought to provide clinical context to scRNAseq-derived cell subtypes by examining their contents of candidate genes identified in genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of coronary artery disease (CAD). To this end, TCF21, a CAD GWAS candidate gene, was proposed to regulate SMC plasticity in an atherosclerosis-protective role.13 In addition, by combining scRNAseq and scATAC-seq (assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing), the CAD GWAS candidate gene, ATF314 was identified as a repressor of SMC transitioning into proatherosclerotic vascular inflammation.15 More recently, we found that CAD-associated genetic variants were particularly enriched in EC- and SMC-specific open chromatin regions in which causal roles for at least 30 GWAS CAD genes using functional fine-mapping were identified.16 Other studies have sought to establish the clinical relevance of cell subtypes by examining their associations with established CAD pathways. For instance, the state of intermediate SMCs in carotid plaques was shown to be regulated by the retinoic acid pathway, which previously had been associated with symptomatic atherosclerosis in humans.5

Besides highlighting cell-level mechanisms of isolated CAD candidate genes in GWAS risk loci, a more comprehensive understanding of the pathophysiological role and clinical relevance of cellular subtypes identified in scRNAseq data is largely missing. Gene-regulatory networks (GRNs) represent pathophysiological processes and molecular functions involved in complex disease etiology, like CAD.1,17 Whereas physiological GRNs typically act within tissues, disease-relevant GRNs frequently span across tissue borders.18 Similarly, pathophysiological processes driving atherosclerosis in the arterial wall are not restrained within given cell types but emerge because of migrating and transforming cells within the arterial wall. Bulk RNAseq data obtained from the entire arterial wall captures the overall variation associated with atherosclerosis but also contains noise related to cell-type composition rather than actual changes in cellular transcription. scRNAseq data, on the other hand, captures relatively noise-free cellular transcription but suffers from selective enrichment of certain cell types and shallow sequencing (≈3000 genes/cell) in a low number of individual samples. Thus, by integrating bulk and scRNAseq, cell-specific and disease-relevant GRNs with clinical implications essential for the development of advanced and symptomatic atherosclerosis should be identifiable. Herein, we generated Smart-Seq2 scRNAseq data yielding on average ≈8000 genes/cell from 16 588 single cells isolated from human symptomatic and asymptomatic carotid plaques and the arterial aortic arch during progression of atherosclerosis in a mouse model with human-like apoB100-containing lipoproteins.19,20 We then integrated these data with 135 tissue-specific GRNs inferred from bulk RNAseq data of 6 arterial wall and metabolic tissues in 600 CAD patients of the STARNET (Stockholm-Tartu Atherosclerosis Reverse Network Engineering Task) study.18,21 Through this process, 1 SMC- and 2 macrophage-dominant arterial wall GRNs critical for the development of advanced stage and symptomatic atherosclerosis were identified whereof the SMC-dominant GRN39 was validated in 5 independent human arterial wall and SMC RNAseq data sets and experimentally in human coronary artery SMCs (Figure 1).

To study the vascular cell transformation taking place during atherosclerosis progression, we performed Smart-Seq2 scRNAseq in a total of 8898 cells isolated from the healthy aortic arch in C57BI6/J mice at baseline (10 weeks) and from atherosclerotic aortic arch in Ldlr−/−Apob100/100 mice (C57BI6/J) at early (20 and 30 weeks) and advanced (45 and 60 weeks) stages of atherosclerosis progression (Figure 2A through 2C). Like humans, the Ldlr−/−Apob100/100 mouse model carries most of its plasma cholesterol in Apob100/100-containing LDL (low-density lipoprotein), resulting in rapid progression of human-like advanced atherosclerotic lesions even when fed a regular chow diet19,20,22 (Figure 2D). Fluorescent-activated cell sorting of SMCs, ECs, and inflammatory white blood cells was performed using antibodies against Pdgfrβ (platelet-derived growth factor receptor-β), Cd31, and Cd45 followed by RNAseq and clustering using the Seurat Algorithm23 resulting in a range of vascular cell subtypes (Figure 2A through 2C) indicated by cell-type specific markers (Figure 2E; Table S2; Figure S2), which were further verified in independent data (Figure S3).

Compared with baseline, early atherosclerosis was characterized by the de novo formation of macrophage and myofibroblast clusters, one separate EC cluster, and several new SMC subtype clusters (Figure 2B). In the advanced stages of atherosclerosis, the cellular subtype patterns became more distinct in accordance with a significant expansion of lesion size (Figure 2C and 2F). In contrast, baseline clusters of B- and T-cells remained largely unaffected during the progression of atherosclerosis progression (Figure 2A through 2C). Cell-type–specific differentially expressed genes between baseline/early and advanced stages of atherosclerosis were largely unique to SMCs, ECs, and macrophages (Figure 2G) and paralleled by distinct biological processes according to gene ontology (GO) analysis (Figure 2G; Tables S3 and S4).

To map the cellular composition of human atherosclerotic lesions, Smart-Seq2 scRNAseq was performed on 7690 cells isolated by fluorescent-activated cell sorting using corresponding human antibodies against SMC, EC, and CD45+ cells applied to carotid plaques obtained from 15 patients during endarterectomy. Clustering of the scRNAseq data revealed 3 main clusters corresponding to SMCs, ECs, and macrophages, as well as 2 smaller T-cell and pericyte clusters (Figure 3A) as indicated by cell-type–specific markers (Figure 3B; Figure S2), which were further verified in independent data (Figure S4). Other than a slightly higher contribution of ECs from asymptomatic patients, the single-cell contribution from symptomatic (n=8) versus asymptomatic (n=7) patients was similar in SMCs, macrophages, and pericytes (Figure 3C). Cell-specific differentially expressed genes between symptomatic and asymptomatic carotid plaques were largely unique to macrophages but substantially shared between SMCs and ECs (Figure 3D). According to GO analysis, the dominant biological processes of these differentially expressed genes were extracellular matrix disassembly (GO:0022617) in macrophages, extracellular matrix organization (GO:0030198) in SMCs, cytokine signaling (GO:0019221) in ECs, and mitochondrial ATP synthesis/coupled electron transport (GO:0042775) shared between ECs/SMCs (Figure 3D; Tables S5 and S6).

SMCs were the most common cell-type both during atherosclerosis progression in mice (n=3778) and in human carotid plaques (n=3235). During atherosclerosis progression, clustering23 resulted in 7 SMC clusters (mSMC1-7; Figure 4A; Table S7). Trajectory-pseudotime analysis showed that baseline mSMC1 and mSMC2 evolved into mSCM3 and, mSMC4 whereafter the trajectory was divided into 2 separate paths, one leading to mSMC5, the other to mSMC6 and mSMC7 (Figure 4B). Examining the expression levels of known markers for SMC-related pathophysiological functions (contraction, immune responses, and inflammation/osteogenesis), mSMC6 and mSMC7 showed high levels of expression for gene markers associated with inflammation and osteogenesis (Figure 4C; Figure S5). hSMC6 and hSMC7 were characterized by low expression levels of Calponin1 (CNN1), a marker for SMC contractility, and high expression levels of Decorin (DCN), a marker for collagen assembly (Figure 4F). In addition, the relative contributions of cells isolated from symptomatic carotid plaques (Figure 4G) were the highest in hSMC7 (n=653 cells) and the smaller hSMC8 (n=166 cells). In hSMC7, the switching from a contractile to an osteogenic phenotype was most evident (Figure S5; Table S8).

To examine possible commonalities between the SMC clusters predominantly originating from the advanced stages of atherosclerosis progression and the symptomatic carotid plaques, we compared genes belonging to mSMC6/mSMC7 and hSMC7 (Figure 3H). Out of a total of 998 genes, 351 (35%) were shared between at least 2 of these clusters, and out of the 407 human genes in hSMC7, 176 genes (43%) were shared with mSMC6/mSMC7 (Figure 4H, higher) suggesting these clusters shared pathophysiological functions. A notion that was further substantiated by GO analysis, revealing that the dominant biological process of these 3 clusters was extracellular matrix organization (GO:0030198; Figure 4H, lower; Figures S6 and S7; Tables S9 and S10), including several markers for inflammation and osteogenesis.

The combination of genes included in the mSMC6/mSMC7/hSMC7 clusters contained close to all SMC genes identified by earlier advanced-stage atherosclerosis scRNAseq studies3,13 (Figure S8; https://sicof.medh.ki.se/athero). Importantly, however, the mSMC6/mSMC7/hSMC7 clusters also expanded the number of genes belonging to the advanced-stage osteogenic and inflammatory phenotype by nearly 3-fold (n=564/176). As a result, previously unknown signaling and complement activation pathways were added to this overarching phenotype (Figure 4I).

Macrophages were the second-most common cell type both during atherosclerosis progression in mice (n=1218) and in human carotid plaques (n=2888). During atherosclerosis progression, clustering24 resulted in 6 macrophage subtype clusters (mMP1-6, Figure 5A; Table S7). Pseudotime analysis formed a trajectory originating from mMP1 evolving into mMP2, mMP5, and mMP6, whereas mMP3 and mMP4 were largely outside this trajectory (Figure 5B). Examining GO and the expression levels of known markers for macrophage-related pathophysiological functions, mMP1-mMP4 consisted mostly of monocyte-like macrophages whereas mMP5 and mMP6 genes were compatible with an M1-type proinflammatory (mMP5) and Trem2-high lipid-associated phenotype (mMP6), respectively (Figure 5C; Figure S9). Corroborating the trajectory analysis (Figure 5B), the highest relative contribution of cells isolated from the advanced stages of atherosclerosis (weeks 45 and 60) was observed in mMP5 and mMP6 (Figure 5D).

scRNAseq analysis provides important information about the cell-type dynamics of disease processes like atherosclerosis. However, despite many RNA sequenced cells (in the current study, n=16 588), scRNAseq studies to date3,6,11,13,16 have relied on a small number of individuals/samples making inferences regarding pathophysiological and clinical relevance of the identified subtype clusters difficult. To understand the pathophysiological relevance and possible clinical implications of the 6 advanced-stage and symptomatic-associated SMC and macrophage clusters (Figures 4 and 5), we examined their individual enrichments in GRNs inferred from the STARNET study18,21 (Figure 6). STARNET is a comprehensive genetic-of-gene expression human cardiometabolic database that includes multiomics data obtained from blood, 2 arterial wall, and 4 metabolic tissues isolated from 582 well-characterized patients with CAD and 225 CAD-free controls. As we compare atherosclerosis from several different arterial wall sample types (ie, human and mouse aorta and carotid plaques), integrative dimension reduction plots to visualize these data sets together were performed. Generally, these plots established that these atherosclerotic arterial wall samples are valid surrogates for coronary atherosclerosis (Figure S14A). Particularly, we noted that the 6 macrophage and SMC subclusters isolated from advanced and symptomatic atherosclerosis co-localized between humans and mice (Figure S14B).

Out of 135 tissue-specific GRNs identified in STARNET,18 12 were significantly enriched in genes from 1 or more of the 3 advanced-stage and symptomatic-associated SMC clusters (Figure 6A, left). Eight of these 12 GRNs originated from the atherosclerotic arterial wall. Among these 8, GRN82, GRN49, and GRN39 ranked the highest whereof GRN39 was strongly associated with the extent of clinically significant coronary atherosclerosis indicated by Synergy Between Percutaneous Coronary Intervention With Taxus and Cardiac Surgery (SYNTAX) score (Figure 6B). Moreover, according to the carotid scRNAseq data, GRN39 consisted of genes predominately expressed in SMCs (Figure 6C, left) as opposed to GRN49 and GRN82, which were of a cross-cell type nature (Figure S15). GRN39, containing 182 genes, whereof 41 were key drivers, contributed to 1.79% of broad sense CAD heritability (H2). GRN39 also contained 8 CAD GWAS candidate genes (CDH13, HTRA1, FN1, PGF, ITGB5, BCAP29, TMEM204, and TGA1) and was enriched for the biological process of extracellular matrix organization (GO:0030198, Bonferroni corrected P=1.0397×10−33). In addition to SYNTAX score, GRN39 was also associated with body mass index (BMI) and blood levels of HbA1c (hemoglobine A1c), LDL, and HDL (high density lipoprotiens) cholesterol (Figure 6C, right). The SMC expression levels of the mouse orthologs to the GRN39’s top key drivers FRZB (frizzled-related protein) and ALCAM (activated leukocyte cell adhesion molecule) gradually increased during the progression of atherosclerosis, paralleling the SMC cluster trajectory (Figure 6C, middle), suggesting FRZB and ALCAM may be suitable targets to modify GRN39 activity in the arterial wall.

Of the 135 tissue-specific GRNs,18 10 GRNs were significantly enriched in genes from 1 or more of the 3 advanced-stage, and symptomatic macrophage subtype clusters, whereof the top 4 originated from the atherosclerotic arterial wall (Figure 6A, right). Of these, GRN33 and GRN122 were strongly associated with the extent of coronary atherosclerosis as indicated by the Duke score (Figure 6B). According to the carotid scRNAseq data, like GRN3 (Figure S16), GRN33 and GRN122 consisted of genes predominately expressed in macrophages (left, Figure 6D and 6E, respectively).

GRN33 (n=182 genes including 41 key drivers) represented the biological processes, and response to lipid/cytokines (GO:0033993 and GO:0034097, Bonferroni corrected P=6.7×10−52 and 4.9×10−41, respectively) and contributed to 1.14% of CAD H2. GRN33 was also highly enriched in arterial wall genes differentially expressed in CAD, and in addition to Duke score, was strongly associated with blood levels of LDL cholesterol (Figure 6D, right). The CAD GWAS candidate genes in GRN33 were LDLR, CXCL8, RASGEF1B, MAT2A, MCL1, TRIB1, OSM, YBX3, and SLC22A4 (Figure 6D, right). The macrophage expression levels of the GRN33 top key drivers gradually increased (ATF3 [activating transcription factor 3]) or peaked during early atherosclerosis (FOS [Fos Proto-Oncogene]; Figure 6D, middle), suggesting ATF3 and FOS may be suitable early targets to modify GRN33 activity.

GRN122 (n=766 genes, including 30 key drivers) represented the biological processes of regulation of immune response (GO:0050776, Bonferroni corrected P=2.1×10−174), contributed a remarkable 4.1% of CAD H2, and was, besides Duke score, strongly associated with BMI and plasma LDL cholesterol levels (Figure 6E, right). The CAD GWAS candidate genes in GRN122 were SERPINA1, C2, ARHGAP15, PLCB2, MITF, TBXAS1, SCARB1, ZNF831, EDEM2, CTSS, NPC1, PID1, VAMP8, ZC3H12D, CFAP161, and TARID (Figure 6E, right). The macrophage expression level of the seventh- and eighth-ranked GRN122 key drivers, CTSB (cathepsin B) and CAPG (capping actin protein), increased along with atherosclerosis progression (Figure 6D, middle) suggesting they may be suitable early targets to modify GRN122 activity.

Out of GRN39, GRN33, and GRN122, GRN39 had the highest fraction of single-cell annotated genes (>95%) and was the sole SMC-specific GRN (Figure 6C). We, therefore, sought to validate GRN39 in 5 independent atherosclerosis-relevant gene expression data sets: (1) coronary artery bulk, (2) single-cell RNAseq and snATAC data from Stanford/University Virginia,26 (3) aortic, (4) coronary artery bulk RNAseq data from GTEx,27 and (5) cell type–specific human vascular SMC bulk RNAseq data from University of Virginia28 (Figure 7). Using the Net-rep application,29 comparing with the original STARNET data, GRN39 replicated in the 2 RNAseq data sets of the coronary artery (n=139 and 118), and in the aortic wall (n=197) bulk RNAseq data sets (Figure 7A). In a subset of the coronary artery bulk RNAseq data sets, the biopsy location (lesion versus no lesion, n=28/26) and the ischemic status of the donors (ischemic versus not ischemic, n=38/23) were provided.26 Although not significantly enriched for lesion-status alone, GRN39 genes were enriched for ischemic status as well as for combined lesion/ischemic status (Figure 7B). Moreover, according to coronary artery snATACseq/scRNAseq data, GRN39 was reconfirmed to be of SMC origin (Figure 7C). Next, given the SMC-specificity of GRN39, we queried a large human vascular SMC RNAseq data set28 reconfirming the gene-gene interactions of GRN39, particularly in proliferative SMCs (Figure 7D). Furthermore, most GRN39 genes were associated with at least 1 vascular SMC atherosclerosis-related phenotype (Figure 7E). Taken together, by successfully evaluating GRN39 in 5 independent human vascular RNAseq data sets, it appears that GRN39 plays a critical role in transforming SMCs from a contractile to an osteogenic phenotype, ultimately promoting advanced and symptomatic atherosclerosis.

To provide experimental proof of GRN39, we perturbed its top key drivers FRZB and ALCAM in human coronary artery smooth muscle cells (HCASMCs). Based on high ALCAM and low FRZB basal expression levels in these cells, we selected to knock down ALCAM but to overexpress FRZB. The HCASMC media was either untreated or treated with TGFβ and cholesterol, the latter to mimic a contractile (TGFβ) or synthetic (cholesterol) SMC phenotypes. We observed a marked upregulation of FRZB (Figure S17) and achieved 60% to 70% reduction in ALCAM mRNA levels (Table S12).

Compared with random GRN39-sized groups of coding genes within each RNAseq data set (ie, Mock, siALCAM, or oeFRZB), the GRN39 genes were significantly more connected both in mock-treated and FRZB/ALCAM perturbed HCASMC across cell media treatment (Figure 8A). However, FRZB overexpression significantly increased the connectivity of GRN39 whereas knockdown of ALCAM lowered it (Figure 8B). Gene set enrichment analysis further revealed that both the FRZB and ALCAM perturbations, especially under cholesterol-loading conditions, exhibited strong associations with genes in GRN39 (Figure 8C). Furthermore, GO enrichment demonstrated a significant effect of FRZB on cell proliferation, chromatin remodeling, and ossification (Table S13). In line with this, FRZB overexpression was confirmed to modulate HCASMC proliferation and induce calcification (Figure 8D and 8E). Among the top genes regulated by FRZB in multiple treatment conditions were the transcription factors ZEB1 and KLF3 and the chromatin-associated protein HMGB1 (Table S12). Conversely, knockdown of ALCAM was associated with increased expression of inflammation-related pathways (Table S13) and the induction of INHBA, IL1B (interleukin 1 beta), and MMP3 expression (Table S12). Overall, the FRZB and ALCAM perturbations confirmed the presence of GRN39 in HCASMCs and emphasized a distinct pathophysiological role of GRN39 in transforming contractile SMCs toward an osteogenic phenotype.

Technological advances in characterizing molecular heterogeneity at the single-cell level have resulted in a deeper understanding of the biological diversity of cells that transform healthy tissues into diseased ones, including the formation of atherosclerotic plaques in the arterial wall.30,31 Here, by applying Smart-Seq2, we provide the deepest multi-species scRNAseq data set of atherosclerosis to date, which identified and markedly expanded3,7,10,13 3 inflammatory- and osteogenic-related SMC subtypes, and 3 M1-type proinflammatory and Trem2-high lipid-associated-like macrophage subtypes critical for advanced and symptomatic atherosclerosis. Next, we provided pathophysiological and clinical context to these 6 subcellular clusters by integrative analysis with a CAD framework of 135 tissue-specific GRNs18 identifying 3 arterial wall GRNs; the macrophage-specific GRN33 and GRN122, and the SMC-specific GRN39, of which GRN39 was successfully validated in independent data and experimentally.

The clustering of scRNAseq data has proven critically important in delineating the subtypes of vascular cells promoting atherosclerosis. However, scRNAseq data have yet to be proven useful for network inference. Critical components of -omics data sets to allow network inference are (1) gene (RNA) expression data reflecting the full spectra of variation associated with disease including lowly expressed genes and (2) expression data coupled to genetic diversity representing at least hundreds of individuals. Currently, scRNAseq data sets do not fulfill these criteria. Moreover, given that the primary goal of scRNAseq is to define disease-relevant subtypes of cells, subsequent network inference within these subtypes is simply untenable as the subtype data lack sufficient depth. Furthermore, fluorescent-activated cell sorting introduces cell-type biases. Thus, although a coexpression network method using scRNAseq data has been proposed,32 the lack of genetic diversity prevents the inference of directional coexpression networks (ie, GRNs), which in turn prevents the identification of key disease drivers and causal-inference of networks with clinical phenotypes. On the other hand, the human cardiometabolic-focused multiomics STARNET study data sets, obtained from hundreds of living patients with and without obstructive coronary atherosclerosis, meet all the abovementioned criteria. Hence, by integrating STARNET GRNs, we could provide key drivers, clinical contexts, heritability contributions, and pathophysiological mechanisms to the subcellular clusters inferred from our scRNAseq data of advanced and symptomatic atherosclerosis.

The top-ranked key driver of the macrophage-dominated GRN33 (Figure 6), KLF4 (Kruppel-like factor 4 (KLF2 and KLF6 were also top key drivers]) has previously been implicated in atherogenesis as a deciding factor for SMC-switching into an macrophage-like osteogenic phenotype contributing to both plaque calcification and destabilization.3 In addition, KLF4 has been reported to be an important regulator within macrophages.11 Besides KLF2/4/6, other top key drivers in GRN33 were also compelling: ATF3 (a negative regulator of inflammation-promoting cholesterol metabolism in macrophages33), CEBPD (a promoter of lipid accumulation in M1-type macrophage in the atherosclerotic lesion34), EGR1-3 (early growth response transcription factors regulating the expression of proteins such as IL1B and CXCL2 [C-X-C Motif Chemokine Ligand 2]), FOS/FOSB/FOSL1&2 and JUN/JUNB/JUND (FOS and JUN [Jun Proto-Oncogene] are involved in formation of foam cells35,36 and the FOS and JUN families form the transcription factor complex AP-1 (Transcription Factor Subunit) a key regulator of cell proliferation, differentiation, and transformation), MYC/MXD1 (forming the MAX transcription factor involved in regulating cellular transformation), NFIL3 (activates ATFs [see ATF3 above]), and NR4A1-3 (the 3 family members of nuclear receptor subfamily 4 group, which is part of the steroid-thyroid hormone-retinoid receptor superfamily). Taken together, the overarching picture of these key drivers suggests that in the advanced stages of atherosclerosis, GRN33 promotes the formation of M1-type macrophages, resulting in symptomatic plaques likely through lipid accumulation and increased inflammation.

While GRN33 has in part already been implicated in atherogenesis,3 the macrophage-dominated GRN122 has not but stands out as a major contributor to CAD heritability (H2=4.1%; Figure 6E). Although GRN122 contains 16 candidate genes assigned by GWAS of CAD, the 4.1% H2 contribution cannot be explained by these risk genes alone, as the contribution per GWAS risk locus on average is <0.1%.37 Instead, the genetic regulation of GRN122 genes (ie, the expression quantitative trait locus [eQTLs] in GRN122) carries substantial heritability for CAD. GRN122 is also interesting given that out of its 766 genes, only 30 were identified as key drivers (Figure 6E). Moreover, out of these, only seven were top-ranked, namely BCL11B (enhances IL2 expression), HLA-DPA1/HLA-DQA1 (class II molecules expressed in antigen-presenting cells like macrophages), IKZF3 (involved in regulating BCL2 expression and controlling apoptosis in an IL2-dependent manner), STAT4 (regulating the differentiation of T helper cells), TRAF3IP3 (promotes inflammation by stimulating translocation of toll-like receptor 4 to lipid rafts, and ZNF831 (unknown function). Taken together and consistent with its major GO representation, the importance of GRN122 as a major contributor to CAD heritability paralleled by its strong association with the degree of coronary atherosclerosis (Figure 6B) appears to be explained by an immune-regulatory role essential for advancing the atherosclerotic plaque to become symptomatic.

Notably, unlike GRN33, which was only enriched in genes from mMP5, representing lipid-poor proinflammatory macrophages,23 GRN122 was the only macrophage GRN enriched in genes from mMP6, representing TREM2-high, lipid-rich suppositively atheroprotective macrophages.23 However, GRN122 was also partly enriched in mMP5 genes (Figure 6A). Thus, GRN122 may have a decisive immune-regulatory role in determining whether lesion macrophages diversify into becoming proatherogenic or atheroprotective.

WNT (wingless-related integration site) signaling is believed to act through 3 main pathways: the canonical Wnt pathway affecting transcription, the noncanonical planar cell polarity pathway modulating cytoskeleton and cell appearance, and the noncanonical Wnt/calcium pathway regulating intracellular calcium levels. While the ECM (extracellular matrix) has been reported to modify WNT signaling,38 WNT signaling itself alters cell adhesion,39 inflammation,40 calcification,41 and even atherosclerosis.42 GRN39 contained 30 ECM-related genes (including the top key drivers; ABHD2, ADAMTSL3, CHST3, ECM2, FMOD, IGFBP2, GALNT5&10 and QSOX1; other key drivers; AEBP1, ECM2, COL13A1, and SMOC2; and among regular network nodes; ANOS1, ANTXR1, COL1A2, COL8A1, COL8A2, COL10A1, COL14A1, ENG, FKBP10, FN1, KAZALD1, LAMB2, MMP2, P3H4, SLC2A10, TNFRSF11B, and VCAN), 7 genes involved in proliferation/differentiation (top key driver; AEBP1, IGFBP2, and TBX2; other key drivers; CFH, SMOC2, and PDGFD; nodes; FGF1), 34 genes involving intracellular calcium regulation (including the top key driver, AHNAK2, EPDR1, PCDH10, SHC4, and TBX2; other key drivers; CAMK1D, NELL2, PDGFD, and TNS3; and among regular nodes; AQP1, BCL21, CACNB4, CACNA1H, CCND1, CD55, CDH13, CPNE4, CRIP2, ETV5, FGF1, FN1, FZD7, GAS6, HTRA1, MEGF10, MMP2, PDGFA, PDGFC, PGF, SCG2, SERPINE1, ST8SIA1, SYT12, and WWTR1), and 28 genes involved in cell adhesion (including the top key drivers; ALCAM, ECM2, EPDR1, ITGB5, and ITGBL1; other key drivers; COL13A1, SMOC2, and TNS3; and among regular nodes; ATRNL1, CDH11, CDH13, CERCAM, COL8A1, FN1, FZD7, GA6, GAS-AS2, ITGA1, ITGA10, ITGA11, ITGA3, JAM3, LAMB2, PARVA, PEAK1, SERPINE1, SPRY4, and TJP2). Taking these genes together, GRN39's involvement in ECM organization and intracellular calcium regulation suggests it modifies Wnt signaling through the noncanonical Wnt/calcium pathway, which in turn activates SMC proliferation and cell adhesion, thus promoting atherosclerosis. In support of this notion, GRN39 top key drivers; DKK3, FRZB, and FZD1 are well-established modifiers of Wnt signaling and ablation of DKK3 has been shown to attenuate the development of atherosclerosis in mice.43

Our study has limitations: first, the number of extracted T-cells in the atherosclerotic lesions was low although their role in advanced stage and symptomatic atherosclerosis is likely more important6 than suggested by our study. For instance, it is evident that the macrophage-dominated GRN122 takes part in regulating cellular immunity. Similarly, the single-cell isolation protocol in our study yielded few luminal ECs relative to ECs from the microvasculature. Carotid plaques isolated by endarterectomy contain few luminal ECs. In contrast, carotid neovascularization has previously been implicated in plaque rupture.30 In our study, however, among 4 mouse (3 from vasa vasorum) and 6 human (3 from microvasculature) subtypes of ECs, none were enriched in cells isolated during the advanced stages of atherosclerosis, or from symptomatic carotid plaques (Figure S3). Albeit using a combination of human and mouse atherosclerosis tissue sources for our study rather than relying on a singular model system as most prior studies, we recognize that, as surrogates for atherosclerosis in the coronary arteries (ie, CAD), our data from human and mouse aorta and carotid plaques may have limitations. Nevertheless, our combined analyses of bulk and single-cell RNA sequence data suggest that the mechanisms of atherosclerosis at these different arterial wall locations are largely overlapping (Figure S14). We also note that since Smart-seq2 is a fluorescent-activated cell sorting–based technique relying on extracting atherosclerosis-relevant cell types using antibodies some cells may not be recognized and therefore lost in the subsequent analysis. Additionally, although the cultured primary vascular SMCs exposed to serum-free medium for 24 hours have previously been used to model contractile SMCs,44,45 establishing the exact phenotypic nature of SMCs in vitro is difficult. Last, like published scRNAseq studies,3,6,7,10,11 we relied on a relatively small but compared with earlier studies larger number of carotid plaques and mouse aortic arches.

Driven by the successes of genetic studies of rare disorders, the scientific focus of studies aiming to understand the genetics of complex traits, like atherosclerosis, rely largely on identifying isolated target pathways and genes, most notably studies of individual candidate genes in risk loci identified by GWAS. Besides unraveling new biology, the underlying motivation for this one-by-one gene strategy leans heavily on the idea that there are disease-central pathways that if targeted alone will substantially mitigate the development of atherosclerosis. However, recent insights from the field of genetics tell another story suggesting the one-by-one strategy is suboptimal. Namely, the sheer number of risk alleles (regardless of their individual nature in terms of genomic location, target genes, and pathways) determines the development of clinically significant atherosclerosis.46 Thus, risk genes are interchangeable as their corresponding pathways do not act in isolation but jointly within larger biological systems of hundreds, or even thousands of connected genes (ie, within GRNs) that together constitute the pathophysiological processes that drive complex diseases like atherosclerosis. In our study, by integrating subtypes of vascular cells inferred from the deepest scRNAseq data set to date with a CAD framework of GRNs, we identified 3 largely cell-specific GRNs essential for the development of advanced and symptomatic atherosclerosis. Finding new therapies targeting the top key drivers of these GRNs is a realistic strategy to substantially mitigate the development of symptomatic atherosclerosis and its deadly consequences, like myocardial infarction and stroke.
