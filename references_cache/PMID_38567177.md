Keywords:HMG-CoA synthase, HMG-CoA lyase, ketogenesis, exon array, hypoketotic hypoglycemia, tandem mass spectrometry, gas chromatography-mass spectrometry

Ketone bodies play a pivotal role as an energy source for extrahepatic tissues during periods of fasting and lipolytic stress. These are derived from fatty acids and, to a lesser extent, from ketogenic amino acids mainly leucine.1An inborn error of ketogenesis can result in acute, fulminant, and potentially fatal decompensation during lipolytic stress, particularly in infants and children. Defects in ketogenesis include mitochondrial 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA (HMG-CoA) synthase (HMGCS) deficiency and HMG-CoA lyase (HMGCL) deficiency. Both the disorders present as hypoketotic hypoglycemia during periods of fasting and stress. HMGCL deficiency, in addition, causes the accumulation of intermediates of the leucine catabolic pathway.2Early identification and management of these disorders are vital as acute episodes can be catastrophic and can result in permanent neurological sequelae. In this case series, we describe the clinical, biochemical, and molecular characteristics of four Indian patients with an inborn error of ketogenesis (two each of HMGCS and HMGCL deficiency). We also briefly describe the management including dietary interventions and clinical outcomes on follow-up of these patients.

A 15-month-old female child, second born at term via cesarean section with a birth weight of 2,250 g. Antenatal and postnatal periods were uneventful. Her parents had a nonconsanguineous marriage. She presented with an acute episode of fever, vomiting, fast breathing, and lethargy. She had one healthy sibling. There were similar complaints in her paternal uncle at the age of 28 years, who is currently asymptomatic. However, further clinical details are unavailable. The development and growth parameters of the proband were normal. She presented with generalized tonic-clonic seizures, fast breathing, and acute encephalopathy and was in shock. Hepatomegaly was present. Hypoglycemia, severe metabolic acidosis, hyperammonemia with normal lactate, and absence of urinary ketones were noted (Table 1). She was mechanically ventilated and intravenous 10% dextrose and intravenous phenytoin were initiated. Severe metabolic acidosis, hyperammonemia, hypoglycemia with normal lactate, and absence of urinary ketones were detected (Table 1). Complete blood count, coagulation test, creatine kinase, and lipid profile were normal. Transaminitis was evident. Intravenous sodium benzoate and arginine were started due to high ammonia levels. Because of escalating ammonia levels and refractory metabolic acidosis, hemodialysis was initiated and continued for 48 hours following which blood gas and ammonia levels normalized. She was extubated after 7 days.

Tandem mass spectrometry (TMS) was nonspecific, with elevated free carnitine and normal acylcarnitine levels. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GCMS) revealed increased excretion of 3-hydroxybutyrate. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed restricted diffusion involving the gray and subcortical white matter of the left occipital lobe suggestive of an acute infarct. Clinical exome sequencing on genomic DNA extracted from peripheral venous blood revealed a homozygous missense variant, c.520T > G (p. Phe174Val) in theHMGCS2gene (ENST00000369406.3, GRCh37), which was further validated by Sanger sequencing. This variant is novel but a different amino acid substitution at the same position has been previously reported in patients with HMGCS deficiency.3Both the parents were found to be carriers for the same variant (Fig. 1a, b). This variant is classified as likely pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG-AMP) criteria (PM2, PM5, PP2, and PP3).

The family was counseled regarding avoidance of prolonged fasting with regular daytime feeding and an additional bedtime snack or uncooked starch. A sick-day protocol regimen was provided stressing the urgent need for intravenous glucose during periods of illness. Dietary restriction of very high-fat content foods was emphasized with supplementation of L-carnitine. With adherence to dietary therapy, she had another episode of fever, poor feeding, and vomiting after 2 months. Laboratory investigations showed evidence of hypoketotic hypoglycemia. She was immediately admitted, and this episode was managed well with intravenous fluids leading to the reversal of catabolic stress. There was no clinical deterioration, and she was discharged after 2 days. The patient is currently 41 months old with normal growth and neuromotor development.

A consanguineously married couple came to us for preconceptional counseling because of two infantile deaths. Medical records of their second child were available for review. He was born full-term via cesarean section with a birth weight of 2,500 g. His elder sibling expired at 10 months of age due to a similar illness and was not evaluated. At 6 months of age, he developed fever, vomiting, loose stools, and generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Medical records revealed that he had altered sensorium, respiratory distress, and poor peripheral circulation. He was given oxygen, intravenous fluids, antiepileptics (phenytoin and later levetiracetam), antibiotics, and vasopressor support. Blood investigations showed anemia, leukocytosis, and thrombocytosis. Liver transaminases and coagulation profiles were deranged. Metabolic workup was suggestive of high anion gap metabolic acidosis, hyperammonemia, hypoglycemia with normal lactate, and absence of urine ketones. He was electively intubated on day 1 due to poor hemodynamic status. Despite supportive management, acute liver failure worsened, and he developed oliguria with persisting metabolic acidosis for which peritoneal dialysis was initiated on day 2 of illness. The infant succumbed to illness on day 3 of hospitalization.

TMS was nonspecific and showed raised glutamine, leucine/isoleucine, and malonylcarnitine/hydroxybutyrylcarnitine levels. Urine GCMS was suggestive of dicarboxylic aciduria (elevated levels of glutamic acid, glutaconic acid, 3 hydroxyadipic acid, 5 hydroxycaproic acid, and adipic acid). Due to the nonavailability of the blood sample of the index case for molecular analysis, carrier screening of the parents was performed by clinical exome sequencing after counseling. Both parents were found to be carriers of a nonsense variant, c.965C > G (p. Ser322Ter) in theHMGCS2gene (ENST00000369406.8, GRCh37). This variant is novel and pathogenic according to ACMG-AMP criteria (PVS1, PM2, and PP3).

The couple was counseled regarding the nature of the illness and the availability of preventive measures when identified early and a good outcome when managed appropriately and promptly. They were also given an option for prenatal testing.

A 5-year-old girl was born full-term via normal vaginal delivery with a birth weight of 3,000 g. Her parents had a nonconsanguineous marriage and had an elder son who was developmentally normal and healthy. She presented with chief complaints of cough with coryza, fever, vomiting, and generalized convulsive status epilepticus. The proband had a past history of severe dengue fever at 4 years of age that required hospitalization for 5 days. She also underwent arthrotomy and lavage of the right hip joint for septic arthritis at 4 years 4 months of age. However, there was no documented history of metabolic decompensation during these hospitalizations. During her current illness, she was found in an encephalopathic state. She was initially administered midazolam followed by a loading dose of valproate at 20 mg/kg. A second loading dose of valproate failed to control the seizures, and hence, she was administered phenytoin at 20 mg/kg following which the seizure was controlled. Intravenous dextrose was also started for hypoglycemia, and glucose infusion rate (GIR) requirement was up to 6 mg/kg/min for stabilization of blood sugar levels. Infectious workup was negative. Electroencephalogram was normal. Brain MRI revealed T2/fluid-attenuated inversion recovery hyperintensities in left putamen, head of left caudate nucleus, and left corona radiata. Metabolic workup was suggestive of high anion gap metabolic acidosis, hyperammonemia with increased lactate level, absence of urine ketone.

TMS was suggestive of increased 3-OH isovaleryl carnitine. Urine GCMS revealed metabolites of leucine degradation (high levels of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaric acid, 3-methylglutaconic acid, and 3-methylglutaric acid). Clinical exome sequencing (GRCh37) identified compound heterozygosity for the missense variants, c.494G > A (p. Arg165Gln) and c.620G > C (p. Gly207Ala) in theHMGCLgene (ENST00000374490.3). The c.494G > A variant has previously been reported in patients affected with HMGCL deficiency (Clinvar variant ID 501099), while a different amino acid substitution at the same position has also been previously reported in patients with HMGCL deficiency.4Hence, this variant is likely pathogenic (PM1, PM2, PP2, PP3, and PP5). The second variant c.620G > C (p. Gly207Ala) is of uncertain significance (PM2 and PP3) according to ACMG-AMP criteria. Segregation analysis by sangar sequencing revealed the mother and father to be carriers for the c.494G > A and c.620G > C variants in theHMGCLgene, respectively (Fig. 1C,D). To explore the significance of c.620G > C on the structure and function of HMGCL protein, energy minimization molecular modeling and docking were performed. The mutant version has slightly higher binding energy (–3.36) when compared with wild version (–3.97). This indicates that the mutation reduces the binding affinity of the protein, thereby reducing its efficacy with the substrate.

The patient responded to supportive management. She was started on oral carnitine while continuing oral phenytoin. Her parents were provided with a sick-day regimen and were counseled regarding the avoidance of prolonged fasting and frequent feeding during episodes of intercurrent illness. Avoidance of very high fat intake was also advised. Because of a less severe and delayed presentation, protein restriction was not advised. Her repeat brain MRI at 6 years of age was normal, and oral phenytoin was discontinued. She is currently 7 years 6 months old with normal growth and neurological function with no further episodes of metabolic decompensation.

A 4-month-old was born full-term via vaginal delivery with a birth weight of 2,500 g. Parents had a nonconsanguineous marriage. There was a history of the demise of an elder female sibling with a similar illness at 15 months of age. Her growth and development were normal. She presented with an acute history of fever, vomiting, seizures, altered sensorium, and circulatory failure. She was mechanically ventilated and started on intravenous fluids, antiepileptic levetiracetam at 15 mg/kg, and broad-spectrum antibiotics. The sepsis workup was negative. Baseline investigations revealed leukocytosis and deranged liver function with transaminitis. In addition, she had hypoglycemia, high anion gap metabolic acidosis, hyperammonemia, and absence of urine ketones. TMS showed increased levels of methylmalonyl/hydroxyisovaleryl carnitine. Biotinidase assay was normal. Urine GCMS showed metabolites of leucine degradation (increased levels of 2-hydroxyglutaric acid, 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaric acid, 3-hydroxyisovaleric acid, 3-methylcrotonoylglycine, 3-methylglutaconic acid, and 3-methylglutaric acid). Whole exome sequencing (GRCh38) revealed a heterozygous frameshift variant, c.545dup (p.Ala183SerfsTer2) in theHMGCLgene (ENST00000374490.8). This variant is novel and pathogenic according to ACMG-AMP criteria (PVS1, PM2, and PP3). This variant was confirmed in the proband by Sanger sequencing and was inherited from the mother (Fig. 2A–C). Copy number variation (CNV) analysis on the exome data pointed toward a suspected large heterozygous deletion involving exon 2 of theHMGCLgene. To confirm the heterozygous CNV in theHMGCLgene, chromosomal microarray analysis was performed using a CytoScan XON array (Affymetrix, United States). This revealed a heterozygous loss of 700 bp involving exon 2 of theHMGCLgene (GRCh37) (Fig. 2D). Because of the absence of markers in the immediate intronic regions and doubtfulness of precise breakpoints, primers were designed targeting exon 1 and intron 2. Long-range polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed on the DNA extracted from the proband and her parents using the LA Taq PCR kit (Takara Bio, United States). Following PCR amplification, samples were analyzed on agarose gel electrophoresis along with a 1 kb DNA ladder. A single band of size 7.6 kb was visible on the lane of the mother's sample. The detection of two bands of size 7.6 and 6 kb confirmed the heterozygous deletion in both proband and father's samples (Fig. 2E).

With conservative management, her metabolic crisis resolved and she was weaned off ventilation after 3 days. Because of early and severe presentation, she was started on a low protein and low-fat diet, and this included a combination of standard infant formula and leucine-free special formula (Pristine IVA 1 diet) providing calorie, 100 kcal/kg/d; protein,1.5 g/kg/d; fat, 3.5 g/kg/d. This diet was provided in 3 to 4 hours intervals including adequate night-time feeding. Carnitine along with multivitamin supplementation and oral levetiracetam was initiated. Parents were counseled regarding the condition and were provided a sick-day regimen. Later, she had two more episodes of acute illness in the form of viral upper respiratory tract infection and an acute gastroenteritis. Both the illnesses were well managed with the early institution of intravenous dextrose. Following the introduction of complementary feeds at 6 months of age, parents were advised to continue a low-protein and low-fat diet and were provided with a list of food products containing low leucine content to target a leucine level of 100 mg/kg/d. She is currently 21 months of age. Her feeding regimen involves one night-time feed at present. Her growth and development are normal at present.

In the present study, we have summarized the clinical, biochemical, and molecular characteristics of four Indian patients with inborn errors of ketogenesis. All the individuals had an acute metabolic decompensation in the early infancy period except case 3. All the affected individuals exhibited hypoketotic hypoglycemia, and the disorder was life-threatening in three individuals with one succumbing to the illness. However, with the institution of appropriate dietary therapy along with the restriction, no long-term neurological sequelae were observed and the affected individuals (patients 1, 2, and 4) were thriving well. On molecular evaluation by exome sequencing, five out of the six variants detected in theHMGCS2andHMGCLgenes were novel and were orthogonally confirmed by Sanger sequencing. This included a complete deletion of exon 2 and the flanking intronic region of theHMGCLgene that required microarray and long-range PCR for validation.

Both HMGCS and HMGCL deficiency are underrecognized ketogenic disorders. Less than 40 cases of HMGCS deficiency5and less than 150 cases of HMGCL deficiency are reported worldwide till date.6Both these disorders are clinically heterogeneous ranging from the most severe neonatal form having a potentially fatal outcome to a milder presentation in adulthood. While neonatal onset has been seen in 50% of cases with HMGCL deficiency, onset in HMGCS deficiency ranges from 3 months to 6 years of age.7Both these disorders present with hypoketotic hypoglycemia, metabolic acidosis, hepatomegaly, and encephalopathy precipitated by catabolic stress. The distinguishing feature between these two disorders is the presence of characteristic metabolites derived from leucine degradation in HMGCL deficiency due to which it is also considered an organic acidemia.6

The classic clinical presentation was observed in all the affected individuals. However, patient 1 demonstrated ketonuria (3 hydroxybutyrylcarnitine) during an episode of acute illness. Although HMGCS deficiency is a defect of ketogenesis, considerable ketonuria has been reported during metabolic decompensations in some patients with a possible source of ketone bodies from leucine or elevation of the L-enantiomer, L-3-hydroxy-n-butyric acid that is indistinguishable from D-enantiomer by conventional GCMS analysis of urinary organic acids.2

Hyperammonemia is another common finding observed in these disorders. In our patients, serum ammonia levels ranged between 221 and 773 μmol/L. Ketone bodies normally decrease muscular proteolysis as well as the output of lactate, pyruvate, and alanine from muscle thereby decreasing gluconeogenesis. In the absence of ketone bodies, gluconeogenesis is triggered to maintain normoglycemia resulting in a higher output of lactate, pyruvate, and alanine from muscle.8This alteration in metabolism along with proteolysis promotes hyperammonemia. Coenzyme A derivatives that accumulate due to the block in the leucine degradation pathway may probably inhibit enzymes involved in the urea cycle. Though energy depletion is the main pathogenesis in HMGCS deficiency, few patients like patient 1 from our series had worsening of the clinical status despite energy repletion thereby requiring hemodialysis. Hyperammonemia can, therefore, be one of the contributing factors to the severity of the illness. It has also been postulated that the accumulation of acetyl-CoA and acetoacetyl-CoA in liver mitochondria under conditions of ketogenic stress can cause such life-threatening episodes.9

HMGCS has no definite and established metabolite marker that can be identified by TMS. However, elevated levels of acetylcarnitine as a marker have been recently reported. Dicarboxylic aciduria is frequently encountered in urine GCMS owing to the omega oxidation in peroxisomes peroxisomes and, a block in the beta-oxidation of fatty acids by accumulated HMG-CoA.10In patient 1, we observed increased free carnitine level and ketonuria, and in patient 2, we observed dicarboxylic aciduria. 4-Hydroxy-6-methyl-2-pyrone and crotonylglycine are other novel markers recently reported to be invaluable for specific diagnosis of HMGCS deficiency.11However, we could not evaluate these novel biomarkers in the affected individuals. HMGCL deficiency, on the contrary, has characteristic metabolites derived from the leucine degradation in urinary organic acids (3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaric, 3-methylglutaric, 3-methylglutaconic acids, and 3-methylcrotonylglycine) along with elevated levels of 3 hydroxyisovaleryl carnitine (C5OH) and adipylcarnitine in acylcarnitine profile.6Patients 3 and 4 exhibited increased levels of C5OH in TMS and leucine metabolites in urine GCMS. With this characteristic acylcarnitine profile, other close differentials of hypoketotic hypoglycemia like fatty acid oxidation disorders were ruled out.

Genomic testing of parents for carrier status in putative genes, as demonstrated in family 2, is increasingly being exploited for diagnosis where the proband is no longer alive. A thorough review of available records is, therefore, essential for accurate interpretation of the results in such cases. In our study, molecular analysis revealed 5 novel variants that included a missense (c.520T > G) and a nonsense variant (c.965C > G) in theHMGCS2gene and a missense (c.620G > C), frameshift (c.545dup), and whole exon deletion in theHMGCLgene. Deletion involving single or multiple exons has previously been reported in HMGCL deficiency. The humanHMGCLgene is an Alu element-rich gene containing 23 Alu repeats within introns.12In patient 4, exome sequence analysis identified only a heterozygous missense variant, an intragenic deletion caused by nonequal homologous recombination between Alu elements was suspected. We performed XON array analysis that detected an approximate loss of 700 bp involving the entire exon 2 and flanking intronic region. The presence of the deletion was orthogonally verified using long-range PCR in both the proband and the father.

With limited clinical experience in the management of these patients along with clinical heterogeneity, optimal dietary treatment remains ill-defined and the stringency in management depends upon the severity of the disorder. Avoidance of fasting and immediate carbohydrate administration at times of lipolytic stress remains the mainstay of management, and most of these individuals recover quickly without further deterioration. A special diet is usually not required in HMGCS deficiency, and these cases can be managed by moderation of fat intake.13For HMGCL deficiency, reported literature has shown variable treatment strategies in the form of no dietary restriction, moderate leucine, protein and fat restriction, and leucine-free special diets based on the severity of the disorder.6While patient 3 with a late presentation was managed with moderate fat restriction, patient 4 required an initial leucine-free diet due to an early and a severe presentation. She was later started on a restricted leucine, protein, and fat diet. Hence, recognizing the severity of the disorder at an early stage is vital in improving the clinical outcome.

To conclude, we describe the clinical, biochemical, and molecular findings in four Indian patients with an inborn error of ketogenesis with special emphasis on their dietary interventions. We also describe five novel variants, thus expanding the molecular spectrum of these disorders. Inborn errors of ketogenesis should be strongly considered as a differential in patients presenting with hypoketotic hypoglycemia to avert acute fulminant crisis and long-term neurological sequelae.
