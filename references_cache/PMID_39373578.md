# The Type 1 Diabetes-Associated Single Nucleotide Polymorphism rs1990760 in IFIH1 Is Associated with Increased Basal Type I IFNs and IFN-stimulated Gene Expression.
**Authors:** Taylor JP, Blum SI, Graffeo HC, Shang Q, Qiu S, Green TJ, Botta D, Lund FE, Tse HM
**Journal:** J Immunol (2024)
**DOI:** [10.4049/jimmunol.2400344](https://doi.org/10.4049/jimmunol.2400344)

## Content

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is a chronic autoimmune disease that is caused by a combination of genetic and environmental risk factors. In this study, we sought to determine whether a known genetic risk factor, the rs1990760 single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) (A946T) in IFIH1, resulted in a gain of function in the MDA5 protein and the effects of this mutation on the regulation of type I IFNs during infection with the diabetogenic virus coxsackievirus B3. We found that in cell lines overexpressing the risk variant IFIH1946T there was an elevated level of basal type I IFN signaling and increased basal IFN-stimulated gene expression. An investigation into the mechanism demonstrated that recombinant MDA5 with the A946T mutation had increased ATPase activity in vitro. We also assessed the effect of this SNP in primary human PBMCs from healthy donors to determine whether this SNP influenced their response to infection with coxsackievirus B3. However, we observed no significant changes in type I IFN expression or downstream induction of IFN-stimulated genes in PBMCs from donors carrying the risk allele IFIH1946T. These findings demonstrate the need for a deeper understanding of how mutations in T1D-associated genes contribute to disease onset in specific cellular contexts.

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is characterized by hyperglycemia that results from the autoimmune-mediated destruction of the β-cells in the pancreas. It is estimated that approximately 1.5 million people in the United States have T1D and the incidence rate is growing (1). Although T1D affects fewer individuals worldwide than type 2 diabetes, it remains the most common form of diabetes in children under 15 years of age. It is currently estimated that over 500,000 children under the age of 15 have T1D and that number is rising by 3% each year (2). Despite treatment with exogenous insulin injections, individuals with T1D may still experience comorbidities, including nephropathy, neuropathy, retinopathy, and cardiovascular disease (3). The resulting financial burden to the healthcare system is estimated to be $237 billion a year and steadily climbing (4).

T1D is a polygenic disease with a strong genetic component (5) with the highest risk associated with HLA class II haplotypes HLA-DR3-DQ2 and HLA-DR4-DQ8 (3). However, polymorphisms in the INS gene, which codes for insulin, and many immune-related genes, such as IFIH1, also contribute to genetic risk (3). Although genetic susceptibility is required for T1D, it is not sufficient for disease development, pointing to the role of environmental factors. Indeed, low concordance rates of T1D in monozygotic twins support the hypothesis that environmental triggers along with genetic susceptibility are necessary for the development of T1D (6, 7). For a cure to be realized, the interplay between genetic susceptibility and environmental risk factors needs further study.

It has been proposed that dietary antigens, the microbiota, or infections could be environmental triggers that initiate autoimmunity and the destruction of β-cells (8). Enteroviruses, such as Coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3) have been associated with T1D for decades (9, 10). Patients with T1D are more likely to have detectable enterovirus and elevated IFN-α levels in the pancreas and peripheral blood compared to healthy controls (11–13) and enteroviruses have also been recovered from the intestines of T1D patients (13). Our group and others have shown that CVB3 infection can accelerate the onset of T1D in genetically susceptible non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice (14–17) demonstrating the interplay between genetics and an environmental factor. However, it has not been conclusively demonstrated that the same is true in humans.

Immune activation by CVB3 occurs when dsRNA intermediates generated during CVB3 replication are detected by the cytosolic sensor, MDA5, which is encoded by the IFIH1 gene. Interestingly, genome-wide association studies have identified single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the IFIH1 gene that are associated with an increased risk for T1D (18, 19). However, it is not known whether SNPs in IFIH1 enhance or dampen the response to CVB3 infection and how this contributes to an increased risk for T1D. The SNP rs1990760 is a non-synonymous mutation in IFIH1 that results in an amino acid change at position 946 from alanine to threonine (A946T). The A946T SNP is highly associated with T1D and has also been associated with several other autoimmune diseases including rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus (20–22). However, the functional consequences of this mutation on MDA5 function remain unknown.

Rare gain-of-function mutations in the IFIH1 gene may increase in vitro RNA binding avidity resulting in increased IFN signaling at baseline as well as after ligand stimulation (23). It has been proposed that the A946T SNP results in a gain-of-function since poly(I:C) stimulation of PBMCs from individuals homozygous for this SNP have increased production of type I IFN and upregulation of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) (24). However, another study demonstrated that CVB3 infection of human islets from individuals homozygous for the A946T SNP for 48 hours resulted in reduced upregulation of IFN-stimulated genes contradicting the PBMC findings (25).

Rare loss-of-function mutations in IFIH1 associated with T1D protection (26) include E627* (rs35744605), which results in a premature stop codon and protein truncation, and I923V (rs35667974) that result in decreased IFN signaling after ligand stimulation. Experiments in NOD mice with only one functional copy of Ifih1 (Ifih1+/−) have shown that decreased MDA5 expression is protective for spontaneous and virus-induced T1D (27). Similar experiments published by our group have shown that NOD mice with a five amino acid deletion in the Helicase 1 domain of Ifih1 are also protected from spontaneous and virus-induced diabetes and that this correlates with decreased ATPase activity of MDA5 in cell-free in vitro assays (28). Collectively, these data suggest that altered MDA5 expression or function can lead to a dysregulated type I IFN response.

Dysregulated type I IFNs are associated with T1D in humans and NOD mice. Expression of ISGs has been detected in insulitic islets from patients recently diagnosed with T1D (29), and elevated levels of IFN-α are expressed in pancreata from T1D patients compared to healthy controls (12). Transgenic mice that express IFN-α from the insulin promoter develop spontaneous diabetes at an incidence greater than 50% by four weeks of age (30). Conversely, blockade of the type I IFN receptor with a neutralizing antibody in the NOD mouse delays the onset of T1D (30). These studies provide evidence that inappropriate IFN synthesis can contribute to T1D pathogenesis.

In the current study, our goal was to determine if the A946T SNP resulted in a gain-of-function in MDA5 and if this affected the regulation of type I IFNs. Specifically, we wanted to assess how this SNP affected the response of immune cells to infection with diabetogenic viruses such as CVB3. We hypothesized that the A946T SNP resulted in a gain-of-function and a concomitant exacerbated type I IFN response after infection with CVB3. Our results demonstrated that the A946T SNP caused an elevated level of basal type I IFN signaling and increased basal ISG expression. However, after stimulation with CVB3 or a dsRNA mimetic poly(I:C), there was no difference in IFN induction in cells carrying the A946T SNP. We provide evidence that the A946T SNP resulted in increased ATPase activity of MDA5 which may be the mechanism responsible for increased basal type I IFN expression.

It has been previously shown that patients with recent onset T1D have a type I IFN signature. There is strong evidence in both human studies and animal models that type I IFNs play a role in T1D pathogenesis (35). We hypothesized that part of this effect could be due to the genetic background of the host and that IFIH1 SNPs could play a role. Therefore, we first wanted to demonstrate that PBMCs from T1D patients had a differential response to stimulation with MDA5 ligands. To test this, we treated PBMCs from control and T1D donors with high molecular weight poly(I:C)/LyoVec to stimulate MDA5 for 24 hours. Using RT-qPCR, we measured the expression of type I IFNs and ISGs and found that PBMCs from T1D patients had a significantly higher expression of IFNA2 and IFNB1 (Figure 1A-B). They also had increased expression of ISGs downstream of type I IFN signaling, which included IFIT1, IFIT2, ISG15, USP18, OAS2, and MX2 (Figure 1C-H).

Previous research has shown that individuals homozygous for the IFIH1946T allele are at a higher risk of developing autoimmunity, including type 1 diabetes (18, 19). Studies by Gorman and colleagues (24) demonstrated that PBMCs from individuals homozygous for the risk allele produced more IFNB1 transcripts and had increased expression of ISGs. They also demonstrated that overexpression of IFIH1946T resulted in an increased basal level of IFNB1 transcripts in the HEK293T cell line. We wanted to confirm their findings and explore the mechanism that leads to increased basal IFN activity.

First, we developed overexpression constructs that allowed us to overexpress either IFIH1946A or IFIH1946T on the same transcript as an eGFP reporter. To determine how overexpression of these variants affected type I IFN production and signaling, we utilized the HEK Blue IFN-α/β reporter cell line. This HEK 293-based cell line expresses all the components necessary for a functional type I IFN receptor and has a secreted alkaline phosphatase reporter driven by an ISG54 promoter. We transfected HEK Blue IFN-α/β cells with our constructs to overexpress MDA5 and titrated poly(I:C)/LyoVec to induce MDA5 signaling. We found that at baseline or very low levels (less than 50 ng/mL) of poly(I:C)/LyoVec stimulation the cells overexpressing IFIH1946T had more type I IFN activity than those expressing the IFIH1946A construct (Figure 2A).

We next wanted to determine if this difference persisted over time. We found that at each time point measured there was increased basal type I IFN activity in cells overexpressing the IFIH1946T construct (Figure 2B). To confirm that this finding was not due to differences in transfection efficiency or an artifact of the transfection process itself, we used flow cytometry to measure eGFP expression after transfection with the overexpression plasmids. There was no difference in the geometric mean fluorescence intensity (gMFI) of eGFP+ cells or the percentage of eGFP-positive cells between the cells transfected with the IFIH1946A or IFIH1946T overexpression constructs at all the time points tested (Figure 2C-D). We also found that when we repeated the same experiment in an NFκB reporter cell line there was no difference in NFκB reporter activity, suggesting that this was specific to type I IFN signaling and that secreted alkaline phosphatase activity was not due to transfection with plasmid DNA (Supplemental Figure 1).

Given that IFIH1946T overexpression led to increased basal type I IFN activity we measured basal expression of ISGs by transfecting our IFIH1946A and IFIH1946T overexpression plasmids in the β-cell-derived cell line β-Lox5 (31). We found that in this cell line, there was a difference in the transfection efficiency between the IFIH1946A and IFIH1946T constructs as determined by MDA5 and eGFP expression by Western blot (Figure 3A). We found a similar result when we assessed expression of IFIH1 at the transcript level by RT-qPCR (Figure 3B).

When we assessed the expression of IFNA2 and IFNB1 (Figure 3C-D) we found that only IFNB1 was expressed significantly higher in the IFIH1946T-transfected cells, whereas IFNA2 was unchanged. In our previous HEK Blue assays, we had no way to separate the effects of IFN-α vs IFN-β since they both induce reporter activity in this cell line (Figure 2). We also observed increased expression of the ISGs ISG15, USP18, IFIT1, IRF1, CCL2, and CCL5 downstream of IFN signaling suggesting that β-cells expressing the risk allele exhibit a heightened type I IFN response at baseline (Figure 3E-J). However, it is unknown how the A946T SNP in IFIH1 contributes to this phenotype.

To determine the mechanism behind increased basal type I IFN signaling, we designed constructs to produce a recombinant MDA5 to be used for in vitro functional assays. It is very difficult to express full-size and functional recombinant MDA5 protein in bacteria. It has been previously reported that recombinant MDA5 can be used for in vitro functional assays with the N-terminal CARD domains removed, resulting in a smaller protein construct with robust expression and purification using a bacterial expression system (32). We have previously used this method to produce recombinant murine MDA5 for in vitro functional assays (28). Utilizing bacterial expression vectors, we expressed and purified recombinant MDA5 with the A946A and A946T protein variants. The purity of the recombinant protein was confirmed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting for MDA5 protein showing the expected protein size without CARD domains. (Supplemental Figure 2).

It has been previously shown that the E627X mutation within MDA5 results in decreased in vitro ATPase activity (36). Since this mutation is associated with protection from T1D, we hypothesized that the A946T mutation, which is associated with an increased risk for T1D, would increase ATPase activity of MDA5. Indeed, ATPase assays showed that MDA5 with the A946T mutation had increased ATPase activity in the presence of poly(I:C), a mimetic of dsRNA (Figure 4). These data suggest that the A946T-specific increase in basal type I IFN signaling may be, at least in part, due to increased ATPase activity (i.e., a gain of function), which may result in increased responses to endogenous dsRNA. However, whether the difference in ATPase activity is evident during a viral infection when dsRNA levels are high remains to be determined.

The data presented in this paper so far demonstrate a difference in the type I IFN response to poly(I:C)/LyoVec in cells expressing the A946T variant. However, it has yet to be determined if there is a similar phenotype present during infection with a diabetogenic virus like CVB3. To determine if there was a differential response to infection with CVB3, we infected PBMCs previously isolated from healthy controls or patients with T1D and assessed their type I IFN response. There was no response to CVB3 (data not shown). However, if we used freshly isolated PBMCs that were not previously frozen, we could elicit a strong type I IFN response. Indeed, if we stimulated PBMCs that had previously been frozen and compared antiviral gene expression to PBMCs from the same donors that had been stimulated immediately after isolation, we could no longer elicit a strong antiviral response with CVB3 (Supplemental Figure 3). We have made similar observations with fresh versus frozen monocytes that were differentiated into macrophages and infected with HIV-1 (unpublished data; data not shown). Therefore, to address whether the type I IFN pathway is stimulated in PBMCs in response to infection with CVB3 we performed all subsequent PBMC experiments with PBMCs freshly isolated from buffy coats from LifeSouth Community Blood Center.

We wanted to see what the effect of the A946T gain-of-function mutation was in primary cells expressing endogenous MDA5 levels (without overexpression). We collected fresh PBMCs from 81 healthy donors. Our cohort had 44 male donors with a median age of 47 ± 14.31 and 37 female donors with a median age 45 ± 16.62 (Supplemental Figure 6A). In our cohort there were 62 Caucasian donors, 16 African American donors, 2 Asian donors, and 1 biracial/other donor (Supplemental Figure 6B). PBMCs were treated with poly(I:C)/LyoVec or CVB3 for 24 hours to assess the type I IFN response. We found that CVB3 induced many of the same antiviral genes induced by poly(I:C)/LyoVec, including IFIH1, IFIT1, IFIT2, ISG15, MX2, IFNB1, IFNA2, IRF1, USP18, OAS1, and DDX58 (Figure 5A-K). However, the expression level of IFNB1 and IFNA2 was significantly lower than that induced by poly(I:C)/LyoVec treatment alone (Figure 5F-G). This may be due to differing kinetics between a CVB3 viral infection and transfection with poly(I:C)/LyoVec. Furthermore, viral proteases have been shown to interfere with the type I IFN pathway through by cleaving MAVS and TRIF (37, 38). Therefore, it is expected that there would be a dampened IFN response with infectious virus compared to a synthetic dsRNA mimetic.

While we only saw a difference in type I IFN activity in HEK293 cells at lower doses (1–100 ng/mL), we wanted to determine if we could reproduce the findings made by Gorman and colleagues (24). Thus, we used the same dose of poly(I:C) (1 μg/mL) and collected our samples at the same time point (24 hours post treatment). In our PBMC experiments only endogenous levels of MDA5 protein are present at baseline and may not be as sensitive to low doses of poly(I:C) as HEK293 cell which are overexpressing the MDA5 protein. We added an additional treatment, CVB3 infection to compare the effects of an actual virus to the dsRNA mimetic poly(I:C), which may not accurately mimic all aspects of a viral infection.

We also measured type I IFN activity in the supernatants using a HEK Blue IFN-α/β reporter assay. We observed a significant increase in type I IFN activity in the supernatants from PBMCs treated with CVB3 compared to poly(I:C)/LyoVec (Figure 5L). To confirm that the type I IFN activity was due to secreted type I IFN and not due to residual virus in our supernatant samples infecting the reporter cells and causing IFN production, we UV-inactivated any leftover virus in the supernatant before assaying type I IFN activity.

A previous report by Domsgen and colleagues (25) showed that type III IFNs may be produced in the pancreatic islets in response to CVB3 infection. To see if type III IFNs played a role in the ISG response in our PBMC experiment, we performed an additional reporter assay using HEK Blue IFN-lambda cells. In contrast to type I IFNs, we found no type III IFN activity amongst any of the groups (Figure 5M). These data suggest that type III IFNs may not play a direct role in immune cells during CVB3 infection but do not preclude the possibility that they may play a role in the pancreatic islets or gut epithelial cells.

To confirm our RT-qPCR findings, we also analyzed the supernatants from our PBMC experiments by multiplex immunoassay to quantify secreted cytokines/chemokines. We found that IFNα2 was significantly upregulated by poly(I:C)/LyoVec and CVB3 treatment and that there was significantly more IFNα2 present in the supernatants of CVB3-treated cells compared to poly(I:C)/LyoVec-treated cells (Figure 6A). Interestingly, this did not correlate with our RT-qPCR findings, which showed that poly(I:C)/LyoVec-treated cells expressed significantly more IFNA2 transcripts than CVB3-infected cells. This may be due to the time the samples were collected, since the response may have been winding down at the transcriptional level but overall had been higher in terms of total secreted cytokines. Both CVB3 and poly(I:C) treatments upregulated the secretion of many other cytokines associated with inflammatory responses including IFNγ, IL-1RA, IL-1β, IL-8, CXCL10, CCL4, and CCL5 (Figure 6B-H and Supplemental Figure 4).

A previous study by Gorman and colleagues (24) suggested that PBMCs from healthy donors with the T1D-associated IFIH1 SNP, rs1990760, exhibited stronger antiviral responses compared to PBMCs from healthy donors without the risk-associated SNP. This study looked at the effects of poly(I:C) transfection on PBMCs and showed that IFNB1 transcripts as well as the transcripts of several downstream ISGs were significantly increased when the donor was homozygous for the IFIH1946T allele. However, in their study, they did not measure the response to infectious viruses. We wanted to see if we could reproduce their findings with poly(I:C) and if the same phenotype could be observed following infection with CVB3.

To do this, we used banked gDNA from all of the donors we analyzed in Figure 5 and determined their genotypes for the following IFIH1 SNPs: rs1990760 and rs3747517. We had 20 non-risk/non-risk (CC), 40 risk/non-risk (CT), and 22 risk/risk (TT) donors for the rs1990760 SNP (Supplemental Figure 5). We also determined the genotypes for the following rare SNPs associated with protection from T1D: rs35744606 and rs35667974 (Supplemental Figure 5). We found that two donors carried a single allele with the rs35755605 SNP and one donor carried a single allele with the rs356667974. However, even if we removed these donors from the data analysis it had no effects on the final results (data not shown). We reanalyzed the RT-qPCR data we previously reported in Figure 5 and separated the data by rs1990760 genotype to see if there were any significant differences in gene expression between donors carrying the risk allele or those who did not. Contrary to the findings by Gorman and colleagues (24), we found no statistically significant difference in the expression of any gene analyzed in response to CVB3 infection, except for IFNA2. IFNA2 expression was higher in donors homozygous for the risk version of the IFIH1 gene (TT allele), however, it is unclear if this is biologically significant. We also did not find any statistically significant differences in gene expression in response to poly(I:C)/LyoVec, failing to reproduce the findings made by Gorman and colleagues (24) (Figure 7) Notably, we looked at some of the same genes as Gorman and colleagues, namely USP18, OAS1, and DDX58 and did not see IFIH1 genotype-dependent differences in the expression of these genes (24).

We also considered that the genetic background or ethnicity of our donors could influence whether there was MDA5-dependent gene expression differences in the presence of the A946T allele. To assess this, we separated the data from Figure 7 into one group with Caucasian donors only and one group with African American donors only. Even after separating the data by ethnicity (Supplemental Figures 7 and 8) there was no significant differences caused by the rs1990760 SNP. Notably, there were only 16 African American donors in our cohort and none of them was homozygous for the risk allele. With only 16 African American donors we cannot make any meaningful conclusions about the data.

Considering that differences might manifest at the protein level rather than at the transcript level, we grouped the secreted cytokine/chemokine data shown in Figure 6 in order to compare results across the different rs1990760 genotypes. The Milliplex data was consistent with the RT-qPCR data and showed that genotype had no significant effect on the secretion of any of the cytokines or chemokines in the panel (Figure 8). These data suggest that the IFIH1 SNP alone has no effect on the antiviral response at least in immune cells and that the increased expression of ISGs in islets from individuals homozygous for the non-risk IFIH1 allele (CC) seen by Domsgen and colleagues (25) may be intrinsic to pancreatic islets.

The objective of this study was to assess if the A946T SNP in IFIH1 resulted in a gain-of-function and if this affected the regulation of type I IFNs during infection with CVB3. Viral infections have long been proposed as putative triggers for T1D and it has also been demonstrated that dysregulated type I IFNs play a role in T1D pathogenesis (35). In this study, we sought to assess whether a known genetic risk factor, the A946T SNP in IFIH1, resulted in dysregulated IFN responses to T1D-associated viruses, thus finding a unifying mechanism linking these factors to an elevated risk for T1D.

There is strong evidence that dysregulated type I IFNs are associated with T1D onset. A recent report by Rodriguez and colleagues (2020) demonstrated an increased type I IFN gene signature in ex vivo whole blood from T1D patients after stimulation with poly(I:C) compared to whole blood from non-diabetic controls (39). Another study by Ferreira and colleagues showed that prior to the onset of T1D, there is a detectable type I IFN signature in peripheral blood immune cells (40). An increased type I IFN signature has also been observed in the islets of individuals with recent onset of T1D (12, 29, 41). Our study builds on previous research examining whether immune cells from patients with T1D exhibit an exaggerated IFN response in vitro to a known MDA5 ligand, transfected high molecular weight poly(I:C). Our results are consistent with previous findings reported by others but are novel in that they show that direct stimulation of MDA5 can produce the same phenotype. However, the cause of this increased type I IFN signature remains unknown.

Rice and colleagues previously demonstrated that the functional consequences of several rare mutations in IFIH1 (R337G, D393V, G495R, R720Q, R779H, and R779C) were an increase in the expression of type I IFN-stimulated genes in HEK 293 cells after poly(I:C) treatment and at steady state. Interestingly, this increased type I IFN activity correlated with increased ATP hydrolysis and dsRNA binding (23). These data suggest that mutations in IFIH1 may be sufficient to result in dysregulated type I IFN signaling. However, the SNPs assessed by Rice and colleagues are rare and have not been previously associated with T1D. We sought to determine if the more common T1D-associated SNP (A946T) was also a gain-of-function mutation and if it caused a similar increase in type I IFN signaling.

Using a similar methodology, we showed that overexpression of the IFIH1946T variant in HEK 293 cells also resulted in an increase in type I IFN activity at baseline. However, post transfection with high molecular weight poly(I:C), we did not detect a difference in reporter activity between cells overexpressing IFIH1946A or IFIH1946T. Similar to Rice and colleagues, we demonstrated an increase in baseline type I IFN reporter activity suggesting that the A946T mutation is also a gain-of-function mutation. Given the previous observations that increased IFN-α expression can be detected in the islets of recent onset T1D patients, we wanted to determine if the increased basal type I IFN signaling could also be detected in a β-cell-derived cell line. To test this, we utilized β-Lox5 cells (31) and demonstrated the same phenotype we observed in HEK 293 cells.

The differences in the type and delivery method of poly(I:C) likely account for why we did not also see an increase in signaling in the cells overexpressing IFIH1946T after stimulation. In our experiment, we transfected cells with high molecular weight poly(I:C) which has been shown to directly stimulate MDA5 (42). Rice and colleagues reported the use of poly(I:C) without the use of a transfection agent and did not specify whether they used high or low molecular weight. The lack of transfection reagent likely results in primarily TLR3 signaling.

To determine if the A946T mutation was indeed a gain-of-function mutation, we performed ATP hydrolysis assays with human Δ2CARD MDA5 constructs. We observed that MDA5 with the A946T mutation had increased ATPase activity in the presence of high molecular weight poly(I:C) compared to control. This is the first study to demonstrate a functional change in MDA5 ATPase activity as a result of the rs1990760 SNP. While previous studies have examined the effect of the SNP on expression of ISGs downstream of MDA5 signaling, we are the first to show that the function of MDA5 is altered because of this SNP. This is an important finding which provides insight into the mechanism of how the SNP may be contributing to autoimmunity.

ATPase activity in MDA5 is related to its function and downstream signaling. ATPase hydrolysis has been proposed to facilitate the release of dsRNA from the MDA5 protein (43). For self RNAs, the ATPase activity is rapid and dissociation from the RNA happens before the CARD domains can oligomerize and signal through MAVS. Conversely, when there is tight binding to foreign dsRNA, the slower ATPase hydrolysis results in longer binding and oligomerization and better signaling through the CARD domains with MAVS. However, our results demonstrated that the A946T mutation is associated with increased ATPase activity which correlates with increased signaling. This corroborates the findings of Rice and colleagues in their study of other gain-of-function mutations in IFIH1 (23).

Our group has also demonstrated that in the NOD mouse model, mice with a five amino acid deletion in the helicase 1 domain of MDA5 are protected from T1D which correlated with decreased ATPase activity after stimulation with high molecular weight poly(I:C) (28). Additional experiments testing whether the A946T variant of MDA5 has increased filament formation and disassembly or whether the effect is strictly on signaling are still needed. Our finding that increased ATPase activity is correlated with increased type I IFN activity may offer a future therapeutic target for treating autoimmune diseases like T1D.

Collectively, these data support the conclusion that the A946T mutation, previously associated with T1D risk, is a gain-of-function mutation that results in dysregulated basal IFN activity. However, it remains unclear how increased basal levels of IFN contribute to increased risk for T1D. One possibility is that increased type I IFNs in the islets enhance or modify antigen presentation. Previous studies have shown that increased IFN-α in the islets of recent onset T1D patients correlates with hyperexpression of HLA-I and that this is associated with increased infiltration of cytotoxic T cells (41, 44). The increased presentation of self-antigens or neoantigens may be a precipitating event in the initiation of autoimmunity (45). IFN-β has been shown to upregulate the immunoproteasome subunits in islets and is associated with T1D onset (46). The immunoproteasome subunits alter the processing of cytosolic proteins and the peptides available for presentation on HLA, potentially increasing the presentation of neoantigens (47, 48). MHC is also upregulated in the presence of type I IFNs (35). Increased basal expression of type I IFNs may be the catalyst for the presentation of self-peptides or neoantigens to auto-reactive T cells in genetically susceptible individuals.

Previous studies used poly(I:C) to stimulate MDA5 in PBMCs from healthy individuals with or without the A946T SNP (24). We sought to reproduce and expand on these findings by using an infectious virus to activate MDA5. Interestingly, we found that in previously frozen cells, we could not induce an antiviral response to CVB3 infection. Consequently, our experiments required that all cells were collected fresh. This presents a challenge for large studies of immune cells from healthy controls and patients with T1D since most PBMCs used in these studies come from frozen cells in biorepositories. It is unclear why cryopreservation of immune cells impacts their ability to support viral replication and warrants further investigation.

In the current study, we collected fresh buffy coats for each experiment. We were able to demonstrate that these cells responded to transfected high molecular weight poly(I:C) and CVB3 infection with a significant upregulation of type I IFN genes and downstream ISGs. We observed a concomitant increase in the secretion of inflammatory cytokines suggesting that the increased gene expression was of functional consequence. Unfortunately, when we divided the data into groups based on the A946T SNP, we observed no differences in gene regulation. We also evaluated whether there were differences in cytokine secretion when accounting for the presence or absence of the A946T SNP. However, we failed to detect any significant differences in cytokine expression related to the A946T SNP. These results contradict previous findings by Gorman, et al. that demonstrated that in primary human PBMCs, there was a significant upregulation of ISGs at the transcript level after transfection with poly(I:C) in cells from donors homozygous for the A946T SNP (24).

Several factors may explain our inability to replicate the findings of Gorman, et al. (24) including differences in donor populations, sample size, transfection reagents used, prior cryopreservation of cells, and statistical analyses used. Our healthy control donors came from the Montgomery, Alabama area and may have distinctly different genetic backgrounds from the donors recruited by Gorman, et al, who did not provide ethnicity data in their report. When we analyzed our data based on the ethnicity data provided by LifeSouth Community Blood Center we found that there were no statistically significant differences in gene expression between Caucasian donors and African American (Supplemental Figures 7 and 8). One caveat to analyzing our data by race/ethnicity is that the sample size for each genotype is smaller and lacks statistical power. Future studies should focus on including healthy donors and patients with T1D from a variety of races to account for haplotype differences and HLA-related differences.

Another factor that may account for our inability to replicate the findings reported by Gorman, et al. is sample size. Both our study and the study reported by Gorman, et al. had at least twenty donors for each genotype analyzed. Yet, despite similar sample sizes, we obtained different results suggesting that both studies may be underpowered. The effect size observed by Gorman, et al. is very small and it is likely that any impact of the A946T SNP is very small by itself. We suggest that a larger, multi-center study is performed that controls for ethnicity and HLA haplotypes to resolve this scientific question.

We only assessed the SNPs related to IFIH1 and did not select for specific HLA haplotypes. It is well known that no single SNP in IFIH1 is likely sufficient to result in the development of T1D but rather a combination of SNPs. The highest risk is carried by HLA haplotype which was not controlled for in our study. We created our own high molecular weight poly(I:C) transfection complexes using the LyoVec transfection reagent from Invivogen, whereas Gorman, et al. reported using the TransIT-mRNA transfection kit from Mirus Bio, which may also account for the differences observed.

The differences in gene expression between IFIH1946A- and IFIH1946T-expressing cells reported by Gorman et al. (24) were statistically significant for the following genes: USP18, RSAD2, OAS1, IDO1, IRF9, and STAT2. However, the fold change increases were very small and may not be biologically significant. Their statistical analysis also omitted the untreated samples from both genotypes so they could use a separate one-tailed Mann-Whitney U-test for some genes or a separate Welch’s t-test for other genes, which makes the statistical analysis less stringent. This increases the likelihood of obtaining false positives due to a lack of correction for multiple comparisons.

In our study, we used two-way-ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison test. However, we had a stringent p-value threshold that accounted for the 9 simultaneous comparisons (3 genotypes x 3 treatments) we were making per gene. With this number of comparisons, we set our corrected p-value threshold to 0.0056 (0.05/number of comparisons) to be considered significant. With these criteria, we did not find any statistically significant differences in mean gene expression between PBMCs expressing IFIH1946A or IFIH1946T except when comparing IFNA2 expression between donors heterozygous or homozygous for the A946T SNP and only when treated with CVB3. Whether this result is biologically significant remains unknown without repeating the study with a much larger cohort. Interestingly, in our overexpression experiments with β-Lox5 cells we found that only IFNB1 and not IFNA2 transcripts were significantly higher in cells overexpressing IFIH1946T.

In contrast, the results we observed in our overexpression experiments with HEK 293 and β-Lox5 cells do recapitulate with the findings reported by Gorman, et al. (24). Overexpression of IFIH1946T in immortalized cells results in increased basal type I IFN signaling. This is likely because overexpression experiments are more likely to have a bigger effect size which is easier to detect and replicate. This also corroborates the findings previously reported by Rice and colleagues, (23) supporting the conclusion that the A946T SNP is in fact a gain-of-function mutation.

Studies by Domsgen, et al. showed that human islets carrying the A946T SNP had decreased antiviral responses compared to controls (25). This contrasts with what we observed in our overexpression experiments with immortalized cell lines, suggesting that cellular context is important. Interestingly, their study suggested a role for type III IFNs in the context of infected islets. We did not see any type III IFN activity in response to poly(I:C) or CVB3 in ex vivo immune cells in our study. It has been shown previously that the capsid protein, VP1, from the T1D-associated virus CVB3 can be detected in β-cells but not α-cells in human islets (13, 49). However, it is unclear if this is because α-cells are resistant to infection or just better equipped to restrict viral protein production after infection.

It has been previously shown that α- and β-cells reacted differently to CVB5 infection (50, 51). Specifically, β-cells mounted a weak type I IFN response after infection compared to α-cells which resulted in decreased viral clearance. This is likely an adaptation to protect β-cells from damage during viral infections but a gain-of-function in MDA5 could counteract this protective adaptation. This could mean that in the context of β-cells, a gain-of-function in MDA5 activity could lead to a stronger type I IFN response. This may result in quicker viral clearance but at the cost of increased β-cell stress or damage and presentation of autoantigens or neoantigens to the immune system. This could explain why the rs1990760 SNP is associated with decreased type I IFN activity in the islets in the study by Domsgen and colleagues, whereas other studies show an association of the SNP with increased IFN activity.

Future studies should focus specifically on cells from individuals with T1D to determine if the effects of the rs1990760 SNP are restricted to certain genetic backgrounds including T1D-associated HLA haplotypes or T1D-associated SNPs like in the INS gene. These studies should be done with larger sample sizes with a wider variety of donor backgrounds. The effects of this mutation are likely different in pancreatic cell subsets and antigen presenting cells. A critical next step would be to assess the effect of this SNP in macrophages, dendritic cells, β-cells, α-cells, and exocrine cells. The advent of new technologies like spatial transcriptomics may offer an approach to solving the pleiotropic effects associated with this SNP.
