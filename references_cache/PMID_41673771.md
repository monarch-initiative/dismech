OBJECTIVES: Blau syndrome (BS) is a rare autoinflammatory disorder characterized 
by the clinical triad of uveitis, dermatitis, and arthritis. While the clinical 
spectrum is well-documented, data on the longitudinal efficacy and durability of 
tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) inhibitors remain limited. This study aimed to 
evaluate the longitudinal treatment trajectories, safety, and long-term outcomes 
of TNF-α inhibitors in a Chinese cohort. METHODS: This longitudinal 
observational cohort study analyzed clinical data from 13 Chinese patients 
diagnosed with BS at Peking Union Medical College Hospital between 2015 and 
2025. Whole-exome sequencing was performed in all cases. The cohort was 
comprehensively evaluated in terms of demographic characteristics, clinical 
manifestations, genetic findings, genotype-phenotype correlations, and treatment 
outcomes (specifically focusing on the long-term durability and treatment 
switching of TNF-α inhibitors). Statistical analyses were performed to assess 
long-term efficacy and safety. RESULTS: The cohort included 7 (53.8%) females 
and 6 (46.2%) males. The median age of onset was 2 years (IQR: 1–5 years), while 
the median age at diagnosis was 23 years (IQR: 18.5–26.5 years). The substantial 
diagnostic delay (median: 19 years, range: 7–29 years, IQR: 18.5–21 years) 
likely contributed to the high prevalence of clinical manifestations, including 
arthritis (13,100%), ocular involvement (12, 92.3%), joint deformity (10, 
76.9%), and the full classical triad (9, 69.2%). Genetic analysis revealed 8 
NOD2 substitutions, with p.R334W being the most recurrent (6, 46.2%). Regarding 
treatment, 12 (92.3%) patients received TNF-α inhibitors, either as monotherapy 
or in combination with conventional DMARDs or corticosteroids. These agents 
demonstrated robust initial efficacy: complete remission was consistently 
achieved with infliximab (4 patients, 30.8%), etanercept (3 patients, 23.1%), 
and adalimumab (3 patients, 23.1%). However, long-term follow-up revealed the 
complexity of management. While 6 (46.2%) patients achieved sustained stability, 
relapse or secondary loss of efficacy occurred in 6 (46.2%) patients, 
necessitating treatment adjustments. Additionally, alternative therapies 
(tofacitinib, canakinumab) showed promise in refractory cases. Safety was 
generally manageable, with only one patient (7.7%) experiencing adverse effects 
from TNF-α inhibitors. Stratified analysis based on the NOD2 genotype revealed 
no significant differences in clinical severity or treatment efficacy. 
CONCLUSIONS: This study provides real-world observations from a single-center 
cohort of Chinese patients with Blau syndrome. Our findings suggest that early 
recognition and timely therapeutic intervention are essential to prevent 
irreversible organ involvement. TNF-α inhibitors represent a feasible 
therapeutic option for achieving symptom control, particularly in cases 
refractory to conventional treatments. However, observed variability in 
therapeutic responses and the occurrence of secondary loss of efficacy highlight 
the complexity of long-term management. These preliminary results underscore the 
need for individualized treatment strategies and call for larger, prospective, 
controlled studies to validate the potential benefits of early biologic 
intervention.

Blau syndrome (BS) is a rare autosomal dominant autoinflammatory disorder caused by variants in thenucleotide-binding oligomerization domain containing 2(NOD2) gene. It is clinically characterized by a triad of early-onset dermatitis, chronic symmetric arthritis, and recurrent uveitis [1,2,3,4], with the latter being a leading cause of blindness in children. Cutaneous manifestations such as scaly erythematous plaques and lichenoid papules are often the earliest signs, while arthritis may progress to camptodactyly and restricted mobility. Uveitis, typically presenting as granulomatous panuveitis, is the most severe complication and a major determinant of visual prognosis [5,6]. Although uncommon, systemic involvement of visceral organs has been reported, further contributing to morbidity [7,8,9,10,11]. However, substantial heterogeneity exists, as many patients initially present with only partial features, complicating early recognition [12] and making BS easily misdiagnosed as juvenile idiopathic arthritis. Diagnosis is based on recognition of the clinical triad and histopathological evidence of non-caseating granulomas, with genetic confirmation byNOD2variants [13,14]. Timely diagnosis and adequate control of ocular and articular inflammation are essential to prevent irreversible damage and improve long-term outcomes.

Despite advances in clinical care, management of BS remains challenging. Current treatments rely largely on empirical use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroids, and conventional immunosuppressants. In recent years, biologics targeting tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), Interleukin (IL)-1, and IL-6 pathways have demonstrated promising efficacy in refractory cases [15,16,17]. However, the response to biologic therapy in BS has been heterogeneous, with varying results across patients [15]. Nevertheless, most patients continue to experience persistent or relapsing disease activity, and no standardized therapeutic guidelines are available.

Given the rarity of BS, long-term cohort studies on Chinese patients with BS are lacking [17]. This study investigates the clinical characteristics, genetic findings, therapeutic responses, and 10-year experience of BS patients managed at a tertiary care center in China, providing insights into disease heterogeneity and treatment strategies in this population.

This longitudinal, observational, single-center study included 13 patients with BS referred to the Department of Rheumatology at Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH) between January 2015 and September 2025.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of BS based on the classical clinical triad (arthritis, dermatitis, and uveitis) and/or the identification of a pathogenic or likely pathogenicNOD2gene variants according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics guidelines; (2) availability of valid medical records supporting the diagnosis [1,18,19].

Exclusion criteria were strictly limited to patients with other confirmed concomitant rheumatic or autoimmune diseases that mimic BS. Due to the rarity of the disease, no minimum follow-up duration was mandated to maximize the cohort size, and patients were included regardless of their prior treatment history before referral.

Data were obtained through a comprehensive review of electronic medical records and continuous longitudinal follow-up. The following variables were collected:

Demographic and Clinical Data: Age at onset, age at diagnosis, family history, and comprehensive clinical manifestations.

Genetics: Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood. Whole exome sequencing was performed either under the Peking Union Medical College Hospital - public welfare project for Rare Disease service Improvement or at external certified genetic testing centers. Sanger sequencing was conducted in probands and available family members to confirm identifiedNOD2variants.

Treatment Details: Detailed treatment regimens were recorded, including the type of medication, dosage, administration route, and duration. There was no strict requirement for a specific duration of standard therapy before biologic initiation. Therapeutic strategies were individualized based on disease severity and treatment response (Details provided in Supplementary TableS1). In brief, biologic agents were frequently administered in combination with conventional synthetic DMARDs (e.g., Methotrexate [MTX] 5–15 mg/week, Leflunomide [LEF] 20 mg/day, or Cyclosporine A [CsA] 50–100 mg/day) and/or corticosteroids (Prednisone, tapering from 20 mg/day or maintained at 5 mg/day). For biologic agents, administration regimens varied as follows: (1) Infliximab (n= 4): Administered intravenously at 3–5 mg/kg or a fixed dose of 200 mg, with maintenance intervals ranging from 8 to 16 weeks; (2) Adalimumab (n= 3): Administered subcutaneously at a standard dose of 40 mg every 2 weeks; (3) Etanercept (n= 3): Administered subcutaneously with dosages ranging from 25 mg every 2 weeks to 50 mg weekly; (4) Canakinumab (n= 1): Administered subcutaneously at 150 mg every 6–8 weeks. 2 patients received conventional therapy alone (combinations of MTX, LEF, CsA, and corticosteroids).

Safety Screening: Before initiating TNF inhibitor therapy, all patients underwent standardized safety screening, including evaluation for latent tuberculosis infection using interferon-gamma release assays or the tuberculin skin test (PPD), as well as serological screening for hepatitis B and C viruses. Complete blood counts and liver function tests were also assessed at baseline.

Disease Control: Defined as the absence of BS-associated clinical manifestations combined with the normalization of inflammatory indicators.

Disease Flare: Defined as the recurrence or worsening of typical clinical manifestations (arthritis, dermatitis, or uveitis) necessitating therapeutic escalation or re-initiation, often accompanied by elevated inflammatory markers.

Treatment Response: Categorized based on clinical remission and the percentage reduction in inflammatory markers: Good response (> 80% improvement); Partial response (20%-80% improvement); and No response (< 20% improvement).

Laboratory Measurements & Cut-off Values: Serum IL-6 and TNF-α levels were measured using a standardized solid-phase chemiluminescent immunometric assay at the Department of Clinical Laboratory in accordance with the manufacturer’s standard operating procedures and daily quality control protocols. Laboratory abnormalities were defined based on the following institutional reference ranges: white blood cell count > 9.5 × 109/L or < 3.5 × 109/L; Hypersensitive C-reactive protein (CRP) > 8 mg/L; Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) > 20 mm/h; Ferritin > 307 ng/mL OR < 14ng/mL; IL-6 > 5.9 pg/mL; and TNF-α > 8.1 pg/mL.

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 25.0) and GraphPad Prism (Version 9.0). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with range/interquartile range (IQR). In graphical representations, data points represent the mean, and error bars indicate the standard error of the mean (SEM) to visualize precision. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. Comparative analyses were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. AP-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking Union Medical College Hospital on January 1, 2022 (Ethical No: ZS-3272) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consents were obtained from all participants.

Of 13 patients assessed, 6 (46.2%) were male, and 7 (53.8%) were female. All patients were Han Chinese. A positive family history was identified in 6 (46.2%) cases. The median age of disease onset was 2 years (IQR: 1–5 years), with 10 (76.9%) patients experiencing onset before 4 years of age. The median age at diagnosis was 23 years (IQR: 18.5–26.5 years), and the median diagnostic delay was 19 years (IQR: 18.5–21 years). Demographic and clinical features of BS patients in our center are shown in Table1. The complete classical triad (arthritis, dermatitis, and uveitis) was observed in 9 (69.2%) patients during the disease course, while 4 (30.8%) presented with an incomplete triad. Specifically, 3 (23.1%) manifested only arthritis and skin rash, and 1 (7.7%) exhibited arthritis and uveitis. At disease onset, 11 (84.6%) patients presented with single-system involvement: 8 (61.5%) had initial symptoms of arthralgia/arthritis, 2 (15.4%) presented with skin rash, and 1 (7.7%) had iritis as the initial manifestation.

However, despite this variation at onset, articular involvement ultimately proved to be universal. During the disease course, arthritis developed in all 13 (100%) patients, making it the most common clinical manifestation (Fig.1A). Joint deformities developed in 10 (76.9%) patients, and all patients had polyarthritis. The median age at onset of joint symptoms was 3 years (IQR: 2-6.5 years). The arthritis was typically boggy, symmetrical, and predominantly involved the knees (11 patients, 84.6%), wrists (10 patients, 76.9%), and ankles (9 patients, 69.2%). The elbow joints were affected in 6 (46.2%) patients, and the shoulder joints in 2 (15.4%). Small joints, such as the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) and metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints, were also involved. Camptodactyly was observed in 10 (76.9%) patients. In 8 (61.5%) cases, arthritis preceded the appearance of skin lesions. In 2 (15.4%) cases, arthritis developed after the onset of skin lesions but before ocular involvement, while in another 2 (15.4%) cases, it occurred after both skin and ocular manifestations.

Skin lesions were observed in 12 (92.3%) patients. The clinical presentation was heterogeneous, with erythematous papules being the most common phenotype (5 patients, 38.5%). Other manifestations included ichthyosiform eruptions (4 patients, 30.8%), folliculitis-like eruptions (2 patients, 15.4%), and erythema nodosum (1 patient, 7.7%). These lesions were typically symmetrically distributed on the trunk and extremities (Fig.1B). The median age of onset for skin rash was 36 months (IQR: 8-204 months). Skin biopsies were performed in 3 (23.1%) patients. Histopathological findings included panniculitis-like changes with diffuse lymphocytic, histiocytic, and scattered neutrophilic infiltration (Fig.1B), as well as classical non-caseating granulomatous inflammation characterized by multinucleated giant cells.

Ocular involvement was observed in 12 (92.3%) patients. Bilateral disease was present in 11 (84.6%) patients, and unilateral disease in 1 (7.7%) (Fig.1C). Consequently, a total of 23 affected eyes were included in the detailed analysis. The median age at onset of ocular symptoms was 13 years (IQR: 7–18 years). Uveitis was the predominant manifestation, diagnosed in 9 (69.2%) patients (18 eyes). Other ocular manifestations included end-stage ocular sequelae (secondary glaucoma, cataracts, and globe atrophy) in 1 (7.7%) patient (2 eyes), recurrent conjunctival hyperemia in 1 (7.7%) patient (2 eyes), and transient visual disturbances in 1 (7.7%) patient (1 eye). The most frequently reported symptoms included red eyes (8 patients, 61.5%; 15 eyes), vision loss (6 patients, 46.2%; 12 eyes), blurred vision (5 patients, 38.5%; 9 eyes), ocular pain (3 patients, 23.1%; 6 eyes), and photophobia (1 patient, 7.7%; 2 eyes). Structural complications were severe among the affected eyes. Secondary glaucoma and cataracts were observed in 3 (23.1%) patients (6 eyes). Ocular atrophy occurred in 2 (15.4%) patients (4 eyes). Notably, 4 (30.8%) patients (7 eyes) met the criteria for blindness at baseline. This high prevalence of severe visual impairment is attributed to the prolonged disease duration and significant diagnostic delay before referral to our center.

Fever occurred in 8 (61.5%) patients. The median age of fever onset was 6 years (IQR: 1–14 years). Regarding the fever pattern, 5 (38.5%) patients presented with periodic fever, while 3 (23.1%) patients exhibited episodic fever. Low-grade fever (< 38 °C) was the predominant manifestation in 5 (38.5%) patients, whereas 3 (23.1%) patients developed high-grade fever, with a maximum temperature of 41 °C. Additionally, Vascular involvement was identified in 3 (23.1%) patients, all presenting with hypertension. In 2 (15.4%) cases, hypertension was secondary to large-vessel vasculitis: one patient exhibited abdominal aortic stenosis at the renal level, and the other showed bilateral renal artery stenosis with diffuse carotid thickening. The third patient had no demonstrable vascular anomalies. Gastrointestinal involvement was observed in 2 (15.4%) patients, including one with abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting, and another with liver cirrhosis and hepatosplenomegaly, with liver biopsy showing granulomatous inflammation. Renal involvement was identified in 1 (7.7%) patient presenting with CKD stage 3. Renal biopsy revealed chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis characterized by diffuse fibrosis, mononuclear cell infiltration, and focal granulomatous changes consistent with BS. None of the patients showed cardiac, central nervous system, or hearing involvement.

Baseline laboratory results showed a relatively normal white blood cell count of 6.87 ± 2.01 × 10⁹/L (reference 3.5–9.5 × 10⁹/L), neutrophil count of 3.47 ± 1.28 × 10⁹/L (reference 2.0-7.5 × 10⁹/L), platelet count of 304.4 ± 115.6 × 10⁹/L (reference 100–350 × 10⁹/L), and hemoglobin level of 122.6 ± 27.1 g/L (reference 110–150 g/L). Elevated inflammatory markers included CRP at 22.67 (1.96, 63.60) mg/L (reference < 8 mg/L), ESR at 16.50 (9.50, 18.75) mm/h (reference 0–20 mm/h), ferritin at 66.53 ± 22.70 ng/mL (reference 14–307 ng/mL), IL-6 at 65.28 ± 51.77 pg/mL (reference < 5.9 pg/mL), and TNF-α at 106.1 ± 102.0 pg/mL (reference < 8.1 pg/mL).

All patients in our study underwentNOD2(NM_022162) genetic analysis, which identified 8 different variants in 13 patients. Detailed pedigrees for all 13 patients included in this study are now provided in Supplementary Fig.1. The most frequent variant was p.R334W, present in 6 (46.2%) patients, followed by p.R334Q in 2 (15.4%) patients. The variants p.R311W, p.R587C, p.M513T, and p.M491L were each identified in a single sporadic case, while one patient carried both p.R471C and p.H496P variants. The novel variant p.H496P was classified as Likely Pathogenic according to ACMG guidelines (criteria: PM1, PM2, PM5, PP3). This classification is supported by the presence of a known pathogenic variant at the same codon (p.H496L) in the Infevers database, its location within the NACHT domain, and its confirmedde novostatus (details provided in Supplementary TableS2).

A total of 13 patients were included in the treatment analysis. Therapeutic strategies were primarily guided by the dominant clinical manifestations. In general, conventional synthetic DMARDs (primarily Methotrexate) and corticosteroids were initiated as first-line therapies, specifically targeting arthritis and cutaneous lesions. However, for cases with vision-threatening uveitis, polyarthritis refractory to conventional regimens, or disease flares during corticosteroid tapering, treatment was escalated to biologic therapy to facilitate steroid sparing and minimize glucocorticoid-related adverse effects. The medications used in our study included NSAIDs, glucocorticoids (prednisolone or methylprednisolone), synthetic DMARDs (methotrexate, leflunomide, salazosulfapyridine, or cyclosporine), and biologics (TNF-α inhibitors and IL-1 inhibitors). The TNF-α inhibitors administered were mainly infliximab, adalimumab, and etanercept.

Biologic therapy was the dominant treatment modality. The Infliximab Group comprised 4 (30.8%) patients, all of whom received combination therapy (concomitant use with methotrexate, salazosulfapyridine, and prednisone). This agent was specifically selected for patients presenting with severe, sight-threatening uveitis and extensive articular inflammation, given its rapid efficacy in controlling acute flares. Notably, Infliximab consistently induced Complete Response (CR) in all treated patients in this group. Adalimumab was preferentially chosen for managing ocular and articular symptoms, while Etanercept was primarily utilized for articular dominance. The Etanercept Group and the Adalimumab Group both included 3 (23.1%) patients (1 monotherapy and 2 combination therapy), all of whom achieved CR.

Additionally, one patient was treated with the IL-1 inhibitor Canakinumab (switched from Adalimumab) due to safety concerns associated with severe hepatic comorbidity following a transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shut procedure, maintaining sustained CR without hepatotoxicity. Conventional therapy alone was used as the primary regimen for 2 (15.4%) patients, directed towards managing mild-to-moderate musculoskeletal and cutaneous symptoms. The restriction to conventional therapy in one case was due to a prolonged lack of definitive diagnosis, while in the other, it was adopted following the primary failure of Adalimumab treatment. Outcomes in this group were variable, with one patient achieving CR via triple therapy (Prednisone+Leflunomide+Methotrexate) after primary biologic failure, while the other showed No Response. Complex treatment courses, including switches due to secondary failure, adverse events, or economic reasons, are detailed in Table2.

Disease activity improved across multiple domains following treatment. Clinical manifestations were markedly alleviated, with all febrile patients achieving normalization of body temperature. Arthritis was controlled in 11 (84.6%) patients, 10 (76.9%) of whom received TNF-α inhibitors; skin lesions resolved in 5 (38.5%) patients, all treated with TNF-α inhibitors; and ocular symptoms (pain, photophobia, tearing, uveitis) resolved in 5 (38.5%) patients, 3 (23.1%) with TNF-α inhibitor therapy.

TNF-α inhibitors demonstrated rapid and durable efficacy (Fig.2). The median follow-up period was 24 months (IQR: 4.5–46.5 months). The median disease course before initiation of TNF-α inhibitors was 228 months (IQR: 204–303 months), and the median duration of TNF-α inhibitor treatment was 21 months (IQR: 9.0–52.0 months). At the last follow-up, the median patient age was 25 years old (IQR: 22.3–32.5 years). Regarding therapeutic response, the median time to achieve disease control was 4.5 months (IQR: 2.6–8.3 months). The median duration of long-term disease stability on TNF-α inhibitors was 20.5 months (IQR: 9–42 months). Notably, 6 patients maintained long-term disease stability on TNF-α inhibitors. Concomitant therapies were successfully reduced, with three patients discontinuing additional agents (MTX, Thalidomide [THD]), and corticosteroids tapered or discontinued in 3 cases (final dosages reduced to 5 mg daily or every other day). Inflammatory markers decreased, including CRP (Fig.2B), ESR (Fig.2C), and IL-6 (Fig.2D). Furthermore, VAS (Fig.2F) and PGA (Fig.2G) scores improved, and SF-36 scores increased (Fig.2H), indicating better disease control and quality of life.

Treatment was generally well tolerated, with only 1 patient developing mild adverse effects (facial flushing and lip swelling). One Etanercept-treated patient developed unexplained fever progressing to septic shock, requiring temporary interruption. The TNF inhibitor was successfully re-initiated after the infection resolved. Otherwise, no severe opportunistic infections, active tuberculosis, significant cytopenias, or hepatotoxicity were detected, and no patients discontinued treatment permanently. Regarding cytokine monitoring, analysis of the subset of patients with paired samples (n= 4, 30.8%) revealed heterogeneous responses. While distinct suppression of TNF-α was observed in 2 (15.4%) patients, a paradoxical increase was noted in the other 2 (P1: 114 to 188 pg/mL; P4: 10.1 to 75.7 pg/mL). Crucially, this elevation was determined to be a purely biological finding rather than a sign of treatment failure. This conclusion is supported by the fact that patients with elevated TNF levels maintained clinical remission, and concurrent inflammatory markers (WBC, ESR, and CRP) remained within normal limits. Thus, these elevations likely reflect the accumulation of inactive drug-cytokine complexes and do not warrant treatment discontinuation.

Despite initial responses, 6 (46.2%) patients experienced relapse. In cases of inadequate response, alternative agents were explored. One patient switched to canakinumab and achieved marked improvement in arthritis and skin rash, with reduced fever frequency and sustained disease stability for 2 years. Another patient treated with tofacitinib achieved favorable control of skin rash for 9 months.

The substantial diagnostic delay (median: 19 years) likely contributed to the high prevalence of clinical manifestations, including arthritis (13, 100%), ocular involvement (12, 92.3%), and joint deformity (10, 76.9%). Stratification by median delay ( < = 19 years vs. > 19years) showed no significant difference in the prevalence of clinical manifestations including fever, skin rash, joint deformity, vision loss, and blindness or treatment outcomes including complete remission rates and relapse (Supplementary TableS3). This suggests that even the “shorter” delay group had already surpassed the critical window for preventing structural damage. However, the true benefit of early intervention is illustrated by the intrafamilial comparison of Patient 1 and 2 in our cohort. While Patient 2 (delay 7 years) received anti-TNF therapy shortly after onset and preserved full function, his mother (Patient 1, delay 25 years) suffered irreversible blindness and deformities despite a comparable response to the therapy. This contrast confirms that while biologics effectively control inflammation at any stage, preventing structural disability necessitates intervention before cumulative damage occurs.

To explore the impact of specific genotypes, we stratified the cohort into patients carrying the recurrent p.R334W variant (6 patients, 46.2%) and those with non-R334W variants (7 patients, 53.8%). We observed no statistically significant differences between the two groups regarding demographic features (age of onset, age at diagnosis) or the duration of diagnostic delay. Similarly, the prevalence of key clinical manifestations-including fever, skin rash, arthritis, joint deformities, and ocular complications (vision loss/blindness)-was comparable between groups. Crucially, therapeutic outcomes were consistent across genotypes. The rate of complete remission did not differ significantly between R334W carriers and non-carriers, nor did the frequency of relapse (details provided in Supplementary TableS4).

Our study provides a comprehensive analysis of the long-term clinical trajectory in a cohort of Chinese patients with Blau syndrome. Among our observations, the most distinctive finding of our study is the severe clinical phenotype driven by prolonged untreated disease. The significant diagnostic delay (median delay: 19 years) observed in our cohort is largely attributable to the disease’s clinical overlap with other rheumatic conditions. In our cohort, 69.2% of patients presented with the complete classical triad, which is comparable to the prevalence reported in larger cohorts (71–85%) [6,20] but significantly higher than the 31% reported in our previous study [21]. This increase is likely attributable to the extended follow-up duration and expanded pedigrees in the current analysis, confirming that the BS phenotype is dynamic and cumulative. The prolonged interval without effective biologic therapy not only allowed the full triad to manifest but also likely contributed to the high rate of irreversible damage, such as joint deformities and blindness. Thus, while sufficient observation confirms that the clinical profile in Chinese patients mirrors the classical triad seen in other ethnicities, it also underscores the critical need for earlier diagnosis to intervene before this cumulative damage occurs.

From a genetic perspective, our study identified 8NOD2genetic variants, including 1 novel variant (p.H496P). Genotype-phenotype correlations were analyzed by comparing patients carrying the recurrent p.R334W variant with those carrying non-R334W variants. We observed no statistically significant differences in clinical manifestations, diagnostic delay, or treatment outcomes between the two groups. This lack of correlation suggests that the specificNOD2genotype may not be the primary determinant of disease severity or therapeutic response in our cohort. Instead, as noted earlier, clinical outcomes appear to be driven more by the duration of untreated disease rather than the specific underlying mutation.

Regarding therapeutic management, our data highlights a strategy of personalized biologic selection tailored to organ involvement and severity. Infliximab (used in 4 patients, 30.8%) was the predominant choice for patients presenting with severe, vision-threatening uveitis and extensive articular inflammation, given its rapid efficacy in controlling acute flares. Adalimumab (3 patients, 23.1%) was preferentially utilized for managing combined ocular and articular symptoms, while Etanercept (3 patients, 23.1%) was primarily restricted to patients with articular dominance. Notably, complete response (CR) was achieved in all patients across these three TNF-inhibitor groups. In terms of safety and complications, biologic treatments were generally well tolerated, with no permanent discontinuations due to toxicity. Mild adverse effects, such as facial flushing and lip swelling, were observed in only one patient. Only one etanercept-treated patient developed an unexplained fever progressing to septic shock, requiring temporary interruption of therapy. The TNF inhibitor was successfully re-initiated after the infection resolved. These findings underscore that close monitoring for infectious complications is mandatory.

Furthermore, we highlight the importance of safety-guided decision-making in complex cases: the use of the IL-1 inhibitor Canakinumab in one patient was specifically prompted by the need for a liver-safe profile following a TIPS procedure, successfully maintaining remission. Conversely, the restriction to conventional synthetic DMARDs in one patient-due to diagnostic ambiguity-resulted in suboptimal outcomes, reinforcing that conventional therapy alone is often insufficient for preventing progression in classical BS.

However, it is crucial to note that while anti-TNF agents are initially highly effective, their long-term efficacy may diminish, necessitating vigilance for secondary failure. In our cohort, we observed a challenging case of sequential secondary failure: one patient initially achieved long-term remission with Infliximab but experienced loss of efficacy; a subsequent switch to Adalimumab successfully re-induced remission, yet efficacy subsequently waned. Notably, this patient ultimately achieved clinical remission following a switch to Tofacitinib. This highlights that for patients who experience secondary failure or intolerance to TNF inhibitors, switching to agents such as IL-1 inhibitors [22,23] or emerging options like Janus kinase inhibitors [24,25,26] represents a vital therapeutic strategy. The critical importance of timing is underscored by the contrasting trajectories of Patient 1 and 2 described in our results. While biologics successfully controlled inflammation in both the late-stage (mother) and early-stage (son) cases, the mother’s irreversible damage highlights that preventing disability necessitates timely intervention before structural changes occur.

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged, primarily stemming from its observational nature of the study and the rarity of the condition. These include a relatively small sample size, incomplete clinical data, and the lack of a control group. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of concomitant therapies precludes definitive causal inferences regarding efficacy. Notably, routine assessment of anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) was not performed; consequently, the potential role of immunogenicity in the observed paradoxical cytokine changes or secondary loss of efficacy could not be definitively evaluated. Future multicenter collaborations incorporating prospective ADA profiling are warranted to enlarge the cohort and elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying these clinical and serological phenomena.

Our real-world observations suggest that early diagnosis and early initiation of appropriate therapy-including timely escalation to biologic agents-are critical to prevent irreversible organ involvement. TNF-α inhibitors appear to be a feasible therapeutic option for achieving symptom control, particularly in cases refractory to conventional treatments. However, it is equally important to acknowledge that therapeutic responses may vary over time and between individuals. The observation of relapse and secondary loss of efficacy highlights the complexity of long-term management. These findings underscore the need for individualized treatment strategies and support the continued exploration of alternative targeted therapies.
