# Family vs Individual Treatment for Children With Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder: A Randomized Clinical Trial.
**Authors:** Lock J, Matheson B, Jo B, Bohon C, Datta N, Whyte A, Boyce H, Gurcan HY, Cogburn AE, Kim B
**Journal:** J Am Acad Child Adolesc Psychiatry (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jaac.2026.04.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaac.2026.04.007)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: To examine the efficacy of family-based treatment for avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (FBT-ARFID) compared with individual psychoeducational motivational therapy (PMT) for underweight children with ARFID between 6 and 12 years of age. The main outcome evaluated was the difference between groups on change in percent estimated body weight (%EBW) from baseline to end of treatment (EOT).

METHOD: A total of 98 children with ARFID were randomized to 14 sessions of telehealth FBT-ARFID or PMT conducted over 4 months. Assessments of weight/height, eating-related cognitions, and behaviors associated with ARFID were collected online at baseline, 1 month, 2 months, and EOT by assessors masked to treatment condition.

RESULTS: FBT-ARFID was superior to PMT at EOT in promoting increased %EBW. There were no differences between groups regarding improvements in overall severity of ARFID symptoms or other related ARFID symptoms; however, baseline severity of ARFID symptoms moderated the effect, with children who were most symptomatic improving significantly more in FBT-ARFID than in PMT (exploratory analyses).

CONCLUSION: FBT-ARFID is superior to PMT for promoting weight gain in low-weight children with ARFID, especially children with greater severity of ARFID symptoms.

CLINICAL TRIAL REGISTRATION INFORMATION: Confirming the Efficacy/Mechanism of Family Therapy for Children With Low Weight ARFID. https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04450771.

Avoidant restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) is a relatively recent psychiatric disorder added to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual 5th Edition (DSM-5) in 2013.1 ARFID has an estimated prevalence of 2–6% of children and adolescents2–4 making it a significant mental health and physical health concern. ARFID is characterized by a range of dysfunctional eating behaviors, including a lack of interest in eating (LOI), sensory-related eating concerns (such as taste, color, or texture) (SS), and/or a fear of adverse consequences of eating (i.e., fear of choking or vomiting) (FAC). Young people with ARFID often present with a combination of these symptoms.5–7 Unlike anorexia nervosa (AN) or bulimia nervosa (BN), eating behaviors in ARFID are not driven by body image and weight concerns. Nonetheless, the cognitions, emotions, and restricted eating behaviors associated with ARFID lead to significant medical and psychosocial problems including malnutrition, nutritional deficiencies, growth and developmental delays, family conflicts, and social developmental disruptions.8,9

While there is as yet no evidence-based treatment for ARFID in pediatric populations,10 the first-line treatment for other child and adolescent eating disorders, such as AN and BN, is a specific form of manualized family therapy called family-based treatment (FBT).11–13 Preliminary data from a pilot randomized clinical trial (RCT) exploring an adapted version of FBT for children 5–12 years old with ARFID that compared it to usual care provided evidence that manualized FBT-ARFID is feasible and possibly effective.14 The current report describes the first study to assess the efficacy of FBT-ARFID in the context of an adequately powered RCT. The study involved children between the ages of 6 and 12 years of age with DSM-5 ARFID as assessed by parent report on the Pica, ARFID, Rumination Disorder Interview (PARDI)15 and who were ≤ 88% of expected percent body weight (%EBW) or 10th percentile for BMI weight at study entry—an indication of physical health impact of the disorder.16,17 The study compared FBT-ARFID to psychoeducational motivational therapy (PMT) as an active control individual therapy with no overlapping interventions.

We hypothesized that participants randomized to FBT-ARFID would have significantly greater change in %EBW at EOT than those in PMT. We also predicted that decreases in severity of restrictive eating behaviors and attitudes would be greater in FBT-ARFID than PMT as measured by the standard outcome measure for ARFID-- the pica, ARFID, rumination disorder interview (PARDI).15 Although we did not have other specific a priori hypotheses in this initial RCT, we planned to explore whether baseline participant age, parental self-efficacy,18 ARFID severity, psychiatric co-morbidity, parenting style, and family structure19 would moderate treatment effect on the main outcomes (%EBW and change in severity of ARFID symptoms at EOT).

Consent and consenting procedures were approved by our institution’s IRB and reviewed and re-approved annually. Parents consented and children assented to participate in the study.

Participants were English-speaking children living with their parents/guardians between the ages of 6 and 12 years of age with DSM-5 ARFID with baseline weight ≤ 88% EBW or ≤10th percentile for BMI weight and medically stable for outpatient treatment.20 While there is no definitive cut-point for unhealthy low weight in children, clinical reports and a systematic review suggest that children below 90% of expected mean body weight to be at moderate risk for health impacts so we used this an indicator that DSM-5 criteria A (significant weight loss or indicator of nutritional deficiency) for ARFID was met.16,17 Participants were included if parents reported ARFID behaviors and all diagnostic exclusions were met (DSM-5 B, C, D criteria) on the PARDI combined with low weight (DSM-5 criteria A). Potential participants were excluded if any of the following were present: current psychotic illness or mental retardation; current dependence on drugs or alcohol; physical conditions (e.g. diabetes mellitus) that accounted fully for current low weight or eating behaviors; previous FBT or PMT; currently taking psychiatric medication that could not be safely discontinued or that had been prescribed for less than 2 months. The trial was pre-registered and then subsequently updated to include a complete list of secondary outcomes prior to enrolling the first participant (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04450771). Full details of the research design were previously published.21

The main outcome for this study was change in %EBW from BL to EOT. We chose this outcome because all profiles of ARFID were eligible for inclusion but all participants were also required to be underweight (≤ 88 percent expected weight for height and age). Thus weight gain was a clinical target for all participants regardless of ARFID profile because all were at risk for faltering growth, pubertal delays, and other related developmental harms. The secondary outcome was the severity scale of the PARDI (see description below). The severity scale was chosen because it can be applied to all ARFID profiles, while individual profile scale scores cannot be combined across all profiles.

Nation-wide (US) recruitment of participants for this study was accomplished through colleagues, organizations, social media posts/ads (e.g., Instagram, Facebook), referrals from clinics, and by advertising in local media. Recruitment and treatment took place from April 2021 to July 2025.

There were two treatments offered in the study. FBT was chosen because preliminary studies suggested it could be effective for children with ARFID to improve weight and behavioral symptoms. PMT was chosen as an active comparison condition that focused on promoting individual understanding and motivation related to ARFID while decreasing family conflict over eating by asking parents to refrain from trying to change eating behaviors.22–24 It was reasonable to consider this approach as an active comparison because similar therapies involving play and recommending decreasing parental management over eating have been used and show preliminary effects.22–24 Thus, PMT controlled for the presence of the whole family during treatment sessions and parental behavioral approach of FBT as well as offering a credible alternative intervention. Participants were randomized to one of the treatments below in this parallel group study design. Randomization occurred using a block randomization with block size of 8 sequentially numbered and conducted by data coordination center personnel not involved in assessments or treatment. All sessions and assessments were conducted online on a secure HIPAA-compliant platform. Treatments were provided by PhD-level clinicians.

Family-based treatment for ARFID (FBT-ARFID) is a manualized treatment based on the model of FBT25,26 that employs the same interventions as standard FBT for AN and BN. However, unlike FBT for AN and BN, FBT-ARFID for children 12 and under consists of 2 phases, as the 3rd phase of standard FBT focuses on adolescence. The first phase of FBT-ARFID focuses on parents taking charge and changing any eating behaviors (e.g., a highly limited range of food, disinterest in and insufficient intake, and fear that eating would lead to choking, vomiting, or an allergic reaction, etc.) that are maintaining ARFID. The second phase focuses on the child taking up age-appropriate self-management of eating consistent with the changes parents employed in phase one. Fourteen 1-hour sessions were conducted approximately weekly over 4 months.

Psychoeducational motivational therapy (PMT) is a manualized therapy that consists of 14 one-hour sessions over 4 months.21,27,28 Nine of these are individual sessions with the child, and five are separate sessions with the parent/s or guardian/s without the child present. This therapy aims to encourage non-behavioral exploration (through education about the effects of ARFID, imaginative play in younger children, and cognitive reflection in older children) about how ARFID is affecting the young person’s life and consider ways they might wish to change their current eating behaviors. In PMT, parents are asked not to directly encourage behavioral change, but to support decisions related to eating that the child undertakes on their own and thereby reduce conflicts over eating.

Assessors were trained by one of the developers of the PARDI and masked to treatment allocation. Weekly assessment meetings were held to resolve any question in scoring by consensus ratings to adjudicate any differences. Assessments occurred online at baseline (BL), 1-month in treatment, 2-months in treatment, and EOT (approximately 4-months later).

Weight and Height: Weight/height measurements were reported by parents and %EBW was calculated using the Center for Disease Control metrics in children and adolescents.29

The Pica, ARFID, Rumination Disorder Interview (PARDI).15 The PARDI is a validated measure designed to diagnose and evaluate symptom severity of ARFID and phenotypes (sensory sensitivity, lack of interest in eating, and fear of adverse consequences).15 Parents completed the PARDI on behalf of their child at all major assessment time points.

Parents versus Avoidant Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (PvARFID) is a slight wording modification (change of AN to ARFID in the scale items) of the Parents versus Anorexia Nervosa Scale,18 a measure of parental self-efficacy at changing eating behaviors. It was administered at BL and bi-weekly during the first two months of treatment to parents, and at all major assessment time points.

Parenting Style Questionnaire (PSQ).30 This PSQ is a validated measure of parenting style assessing authoritative and permissive style. The PSQ was administered at BL only.

Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale for Children (CES-DC).31,32 This is a validated measure of childhood depression and was completed by children.

Revised Children’s Manifest Anxiety Scale (RCMAS).33,34 This is a validated measure of childhood anxiety and was completed by children..

Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II).35,36 Parents completed the 21-item BDI to assess parental depression.

Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI).37 Parents completed the 21-item BAI to assess parental anxiety.

Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Aged Children (6–18 years) (K-SADS).38 The K-SADS-PL is a validated interview for detecting psychiatric disorders in children and adolescents. Parents were interviewed on behalf of their child. The K-SADS was administered only at BL to ascertain co-morbid psychiatric diagnoses.

Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ).39 The SDQ assesses conduct, social, and peer behaviors in children.

Adverse events. Parents were asked to report hospitalizations, outside treatment, and harms at all major assessment times points.

We used Cohen’s d=0.5 at EOT as a clinically meaningful effect size in our primary power estimation for Aim 1. We assumed intraclass correlation (ICC) of 0.6 for the correlation across repeated measures of %EBW, which is a conservative estimate based on good reliability observed in our previous eating disorder studies.19,40–42 We assumed attrition gradually increasing to 25% by EOT. Under this scenario, we estimated power based on the proposed linear mixed effects modeling. With planned recruitment of approximately 100 participants (50 in each condition), the estimated power to detect the difference in %EBW at EOT between the FBT-ARFID and PMT conditions is 0.84 (alpha=.05, two-tailed).

The core data analytic strategy for this study was longitudinal linear mixed effects (LME) modeling43,44 in line with the intent-to-treat (ITT) principle. In this framework, we fully utilized repeatedly measured primary and secondary outcomes including all individuals who had outcome data at one or more assessments. Specifically, we used a random intercept model allowing for individual variation at baseline. We modeled the change from baseline and EOT and its difference between the two treatment conditions as the key fixed effects of interest. We used restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation for our LME models. The R software (R version 4.4.1) was used throughout the analyses. To examine potential moderators of treatment effect (exploratory analyses), we employed the MacArthur framework for moderator analysis,45 following the eligibility and analytical criteria for determining moderators. Following eligibility and analytic criteria for moderation, we also included BL weight and ARFID severity score as potential moderators of the main outcomes. This allowed us to examine whether BL weight moderated outcome for decreases in severity, and whether BL ARFID symptom severity moderated weight gain.

We recruited 98 of the planned 100 participants (98%; Figure 1 consort diagram). In this sample, children with ARFID were 59% male, 20% Hispanic, 8% Asian, 2% African-American, 65% white, and 19% more than one race. The mean age of participants was 9.7 (2.0) years. The mean BL %EBW was 84.6 (4.6)%. The most common co-morbid conditions identified were: attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD; n=24; 24.5%), oppositional defiant disorder (n=12; 12.2%), and generalized anxiety disorder (n=9; 9.2%). Most participants were from intact families (n=85; 86.7%). The main reason for exclusion of potential participants was not being underweight (n=205, 42%) defined by >88% EBW or >10th percentile for BMI weight (see Consort Figure 1).

We defined membership in ARFID phenotypic groups as follows: scores of >0.6 on sensory sensitivity (SS) and scores of >1.1 on lack of interest (LOI) in eating PARDI subscales in line with a data-driven approach suggested in prior research.46 Given the instability of the fear of adverse consequences (FAC) subscale that have been reported,46 we individually assessed all items on this subscale per participant and consensus coded membership in this phenotypic presentation if participants scored at least a 4/6 (suggested clinical severity cut-off) on any item or endorsed multiple subscale items with some degree of impairment. Mixed presentations included participants with scores above the cut-points noted above on more than one subscale. Using these definitions (See Table 1), we found the following: SS=2%, LOI=6%, FAC=1%, mixed=90%, with most of the mixed presentations clustered in the combined LOI and SS group (64%).

A total of 7 participants dropped from treatment during the study (n=3 from FBT-ARFID and n=4 from PMT; 7% of total sample). Study attrition (i.e., refusal to provide data regardless of treatment status) by EOT was also low at 2%, with no differences related to treatment allocation (n=1 in FBT-ARFID and n=1 in PMT). Participants in FBT-ARFID completed an average of 12.98 (SD: 2.71) of 14 planned treatment sessions (93% of planned sessions), and participants in PMT completed 13.24 out of 14 planned treatment sessions (SD: 2.59) (95% of planned sessions).

Two assessors trained by the second author and masked to treatment allocation assessed differential fidelity to FBT-ARFID compared to PMT using video or audiotapes on a random sample of 20%, employing a standardized fidelity measure focused on early FBT treatment.47 Sessions were double-coded, and kappa agreement was calculated. Adherence in each arm was high (100%) with perfect agreement between raters.

The participants in the FBT-ARFID arm increased mean %EBW from 85.7 (5.11)% to 90.5 (5.51)% by EOT. This within-treatment effect was statistically significant and associated with a clinically meaningful large ES of 0.88. The participants in the PMT arm showed little change from a mean %EBW of 86.7 (6.13)% to 86.5 (6.64)% by EOT. Between-group differences in change in %EBW were statistically significant, favoring FBT-ARFID and associated with a clinically meaningful medium/large ES of 0.77.

The participants in the FBT-ARFID arm demonstrated decreased overall severity of ARFID symptom ratings from 3.02 (0.77) to 2.37 (0.97) from BL to EOT, which was statistically significant and associated with a clinically meaningful large ES (0.85). The participants in the PMT arm demonstrated decreased overall severity from 3.15 (0.59) to 2.66 (0.98) from BL to EOT, which was statistically significant and associated with clinically meaningful large ES (0.81). There was no overall statistical difference between the randomized groups in decreasing ARFID symptom severity (Table 2 and Figure 2).

We analyzed 26 BL variables as potential moderators of treatment effects on change in %EBW, including PARDI subscales and severity scores, parenting style, parental depression, parental anxiety, demographic factors (child age, sex, race, ethnicity), previous hospitalizations, child psychiatric comorbidity, child neurodivergence (ADHD and/or ASD), and family status (intact, separated, divorced, single). These analyses were exploratory. We analyzed these potential moderator variables in relation to decreases in ARFID symptom severity (secondary outcomes) at EOT. We found that those with higher severity scores at BL did significantly better in FBT-ARFID than PMT (Figure 3). PSQ score was also identified as a moderator of treatment effect for %EBW change, suggesting that children of parents who reported a greater use of an authoritarian parenting style (i.e., high demand, low responsivity) did worse when randomized to PMT (Figure 2).

Other than parental self-efficacy improving significantly more in the FBT-ARFID arm, there were no significant differences between groups (Table 3).

Descriptive changes (ES) in PARDI subscales by ARFID profiles can be found in Table 4. Results suggest overall similar improvements on these subscales regardless of treatment allocation. These improvements in ES ranged from small for SS (ES~0.3) to medium for FAC and LOI (ES~0.5). For mixed profiles with more than 10 participants, ES changes overall suggest medium-to-large improvements in ARFID-related clinical symptoms. ES for those in in the mixed profile group of SS and LOI that comprised 64% (63/98) varied somewhat by randomized group. For those in PMT, SS change was associated with a small-medium ES = 0.44 and change in those in FBT had an ES=0.59. In this mixed group, change in LOI in PMT was associated with a medium ES=0.56 and those in FBT with a small-medium ES=0.42. For the other mixed group that included all three profiles that comprised 18% (18/98) of the participants, change in FAC was associated with a large-very large ES in both conditions (FBT=1.15, PMT=0.92). This was also the case for the SS profile in this group where change was associated with a large ES in FBT=0.97 and in PMT=0.74. However, for those in the LOI profile in this group, those in FBT demonstrated a very large ES= 1.17, while those in in PMT demonstrated changes associated with a small-medium ES=0.45. The mixed profile combining FAC (N=3) and SS (N=3) was deemed too small to accurately calculate a meaningful ES. This was also the case for the small number of participants who fell only into a single profile (FAC (N=1), SS (N=2), and LOI (N=6)). We also report changes in ARFID symptoms in groups that include all participants with any significant clinical problems (as defined in the methods) related to SS, FAC, or LOI regardless of whether they also had other ARFID symptom subscale scores as well (see Table 5). Changes based on clinical change in these groupings did not differ by group and were associated with medium to large effect sizes.

No ARFID-related hospitalizations or other treatment-related serious adverse events occurred in either treatment arm.

This is the first powered RCT examining outpatient treatments for children with ARFID. It examined differential effects related to weight gain and severity of eating behaviors in a group of underweight children between the ages of 6 and 12 with ARFID. The two treatments had different treatment targets and modalities (family therapy vs individual therapy). FBT-ARFID is a family treatment that aims to help parents directly change their child’s restrictive eating patterns, while PMT is focused on helping the child understand ARFID and explore motivation to change their eating behaviors through imaginative play and reflection while their parents are asked not to demand behavioral changes in eating during treatment. The results of the study have a range of implications for future treatment of children with ARFID and designing future treatment studies for this serious but understudied condition.

Both treatments were exceptionally well-tolerated, and families completed treatment at high rates. However, FBT-ARFID is more effective than PMT in promoting weight gain in underweight children with ARFID. This finding is in line with preliminary studies and supports the main hypothesis that FBT-ARFID would outperform PMT in helping low-weight children gain weight. Because some of the most serious problems associated with ARFID in children are related to the impact the disorder has on growth, bone health, and puberty, the differential benefit of FBT-ARFID for weight gain is clinically important. On average, the children increased by 5% of expected mean body weight percentage over 14 weeks, going from 85% at BL to 90% at the EOT.

However, both PMT and FBT-ARFID had similar and large and significant effects (both between and within treatment conditions) on decreasing the degree of severity of ARFID symptoms; thus, our hypothesis that FBT-ARFID would show overall greater improvements in the severity of symptoms was not supported. However, a planned exploratory moderator analysis of outcome found that children who were most severely symptomatic at BL showed greater reduction of symptom severity in FBT-ARFID, and this effect was statistically significant and associated with a large ES. In addition, there was a moderator identified related to improving %EBW in PMT that suggested that children of parents who used a more authoritarian parenting style did not do as well in PMT. On the other hand, parents whose child was randomized to FBT-ARFID experienced large and differential improvements compared to those in PMT in parental self-efficacy (PSE) related specifically to helping address their child’s eating problems as measured by the Parents vs ARFID scale. Improvements on PSE is a putative mechanism of treatment effects in FBT for AN.48–50 Theoretically, these improvements in PSE increase confidence and persistence in making needed changes to help their child make needed behavioral changes about what they eat (e.g., more calorically dense foods) and how much they eat in order to increase overall the quality and amount of food needed to gain weight. A planned future hypothesis generating mediator/mechanism study will systematically explore how FBT-ARFID might work—including whether PSE is a possible mechanism--when used with children with ARFID.

Taken together, these findings suggest that for children who are underweight, highly symptomatic (i.e., greater severity), and whose parents use a more authoritative parenting style, FBT-ARFID should likely be the treatment of choice compared to PMT. At the same time, there was a substantial improvement in most other ARFID-related behaviors other than weight in PMT. In this regard, it is important to highlight that a principal reason that many otherwise eligible participants (42%) were excluded from this study was that they were not underweight. Thus, many children with ARFID who do not require weight gain and who are not severely symptomatic might do well with PMT, but because we did not include non-low weight children with ARFID in our sample, a future study is required to examine this possibility.

In the present study, demographic characteristics and psychiatric comorbidities did not impact treatment outcome as moderators of treatment effect. The study included only a small number n=5; 5%) of children with ASD (according to symptom report by parents on the KSADS), and as such, treatment outcomes among children with and without ASD could not be compared systematically. However, children with and without broadly defined neurodivergence in the sample (i.e., ASD or ADHD, or both) did not appear to differentially respond to either treatment. Future studies might explore FBT-ARFID and PMT within an explicit subset of children with neurodivergence to better understand what modifications, if any, are needed to promote ARFID symptom improvement for individuals with neurodivergence.

Strengths of the current study include diverse, nationally recruited participants, low treatment and study attrition rates yielding a sufficient sample size to test our main hypothesis. Our analytic procedures allowed us to capture and utilize all data from multiple time points to estimate treatment effects accurately. Limitations of our study include the focus on only young children who were underweight which limits generalizability of treatment findings to children with ARFID across the weight spectrum. This study also only included one standardized measure of behavioral symptom change (PARDI). Use of the PARDI limited the ability to examine changes in specific ARFID profiles because most participants (90%) reported combined profiles and the scales do not allow for combining profile scores. In addition, reliance on parental reports for weight, height, and behavioral outcomes is a significant limitation, but if there was bias in parental reports it applies equally to both randomized groups. It is also a limitation that the clinical population scores on the PARDI were lower than suggested thresholds thus it is possible the study group may have a mild form of ARFID. While this may be the case, all participants were nonetheless at moderate risk of growth faltering because of low weight.16,17 It is noteworthy in this regard that other studies of clinical populations of children with ARFID reported lower scale scores than currently suggested by the scoring rubric of the PARDI.15,51,52 Future studies might conduct systematic studies of these thresholds to better understand their accuracy. Further, the PARDI scaling system (0–6) allows for only a limited change and although we detected statistically and clinically significant changes on change in severity of ARFID, these changes appear to be meaningful while also small in degree. In addition, while we had generally excellent treatment and study retentions, there were still 7% of participants who did not complete treatment and while this may have effected results, because there were not differences between randomized groups, these effects would be unlikely to affect the comparative outcome results. All assessments were conducted online which may also be a limitation of the study. As with all randomized studies, the effect of time alone may contribute to changes in outcomes, though any time effects would be applicable to both conditions. Also, in determining the presence of co-morbid psychiatric diagnoses we used parent reports only on the K-SADS which does not conform to standard use of this measure and may limit the reliability of diagnostic co-morbidity in the sample. We also included only within and end-of-treatment data.

It is of interest also that both treatments in this study were delivered via telehealth and associated with high rates of attendance. Using telehealth to deliver ARFID treatments has the potential to increase rates of dissemination of treatments and improve access to families.

Future studies testing FBT-ARFID and PMT in other ARFID populations, including across the age and weight spectrum, are needed. For example, it is unknown how adolescents compared to young children with ARFID might respond to FBT-ARFID or PMT given different developmental considerations for these age groups. In addition, studies that include profile specific methods to evaluate outcome are needed. Given the prevalence of ARFID, studies that evaluate efficient delivery of treatments (e.g., dose, duration, level of family involvement, and self-help models) are also needed. Additional studies using data from the current sample will potentially allow the exploration of the durability of treatment effects post treatment and mechanisms of treatment effect. Currently, there are no other empirically supported treatments with ARFID that have been tested with adequately powered RCTs, and the development and testing of other approaches is sorely needed.
