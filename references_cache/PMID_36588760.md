# Rare Transient Infantile Hypertriglyceridemia with Hypoglycemia and Insulin Resistance Caused by a Novel GPD1 Mutation.
**Authors:** Tan Y, Ouyang W, Ma Y, Jiang T, Tang L, Zhang H, Kang Z, Qin X, Yu Y, Li S
**Journal:** Mol Syndromol (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1159/000523764](https://doi.org/10.1159/000523764)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Transient infantile hypertriglyceridemia (HTGTI) is a rare autosomal recessive disease. At present, only 20 cases of HTGTI have been reported worldwide. Hence, it is necessary to further assess the phenotypic and genetic variation spectra of HTGTI.

CASE PRESENTATION: A 10-month-old male infant was diagnosed with hypertriglyceridemia, hepatomegaly, liver injury, fasting hypoglycemia, and insulin resistance. Trio-whole exome sequencing (trio-WES) was performed on the patient and his parents. Bioinformatics software was used to analyze the suspected genes and potential pathogenicity of the resulting mutant proteins. The results of trio-WES demonstrated that the patient was homozygous for a novel mutation in the GPD1 gene (NM_005276.3; c.805C>T/p.Arg269Trp), whereas his parents were heterozygous for the same mutation. Bioinformatics prediction results demonstrated that the mutation might affect the protein function, and crystal simulation results showed that the mutation might affect the protein-binding ability of the enzyme.

CONCLUSION: Our results indicated that the novel homozygous mutation in GPD1 could be the pathogenic factor in the patient. Our report highlights the value of genome sequencing in the diagnosis of infant liver disease with low phenotypic heterogeneity.

Transient infantile hypertriglyceridemia (HTGTI) is a rare genetic disease caused by GPD1 gene mutations. Only 20 cases have been reported worldwide, of which 3 occurred in Asia.

The main clinical features of HTGTI are abnormal phenotypes of lipid metabolism such as infantile hypertriglyceridemia, liver injury, persistent fatty liver, and hepatic fibrosis.

Only 2 patients with HTGTI showed the extended phenotype of fasting hypoglycemia or insulin resistance.

A novel mutation was identified in the GPD1 gene, which resulted in severe HTGTI phenotype in a 10-month-old infant, including fasting hypoglycemia and insulin resistance.

The novel mutation of the GPD1 gene (c.805C>T/p.Arg269Trp) is located in the core region of protein catalytic activity. Patients with the reported adjacent variants (p.Arg269Gln/p.Ala274Thr) also have extended phenotypes such as fasting hypoglycemia or insulin resistance.

This study provides important case support for abnormal glucose metabolism in HTGTI.

The GPD1 gene encodes human liver cytoplasmic reduced nicotine adenine dinucleotide (NADH)-dependent glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 1, a 37.5-kDa protease consisting of 349 amino acids. GPD1 catalyzes the reversible redox reaction of dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) and NADH to glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P) and NAD+, thereby playing a crucial role in sugar and lipid metabolism [Menaya et al., 1995]. Basel-Vanagaite et al. [2012] first reported in 2012 that a defect in GPD1 could lead to transient infantile hypertriglyceridemia (HTGTI; OMIM #614480). At present, only 20 patients with HTGTI have been reported worldwide, including 3 Asian patients [Basel-Vanagaite et al., 2012; Joshi et al., 2014; Dionisi-Vici et al., 2016; Li et al., 2017, 2018; Matarazzo et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2021]. HTGTI is a rare autosomal recessive disorder that is characterized by moderate to severe hypertriglyceridemia in infants and tends to normalize with age. HTGTI is also related to liver enlargement, an elevated transaminase level, persistent fatty liver, and hepatic fibrosis [Basel-Vanagaite et al., 2012]. Li et al. [2017] reported additional phenotypes, such as fasting hypoglycemia and insulin resistance, in a 13-year-old boy. However, the correlation between these phenotypes and GPD1 variants remains to be further studied.

In this study, we report a case of HTGTI caused by a novel mutation in GPD1 that was accompanied by phenotypes such as fasting hypoglycemia and insulin resistance. This case report expands the diversity of HTGTI etiology and GPD1 variants and provides evidence for the phenotypic correlation of insulin resistance in infants with GPD1 mutations.

A 10-month-old male infant who experienced diarrhea, vomiting, and a low fever was hospitalized at the Department of Hepatopathy Center of Hunan Children's Hospital. Physical examination revealed a dry skin and sunken eyes, thick breathing sounds and wet rales in both lungs, no uplift in the precardiac region, and a heart rate of 139 beats/minute. Computed tomography of the chest revealed bronchopneumonia. Furthermore, the abdomen of the infant was swollen 6 cm below the right rib cage, near the liver, and was soft. The spleen was not enlarged, shifting dullness was negative, and the bowel sounds were active. The patient's parents were consanguineous, and the parents and siblings were healthy.

Biochemical tests revealed increased levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), suggesting liver damage. Fasting blood lipid and glucose tests showed that the triglyceride levels were increased and the blood glucose level was decreased. Insulin release tests showed that insulin and C-peptide levels were decreased, indicating insulin resistance (Table 1). Liver fibrosis tests showed mild abnormalities, with a possibility of hepatic fibrosis. The results of Doppler ultrasound showed hepatomegaly (48 mm below the right rib cage); however, because bone marrow cytology was normal, the possibility that hepatomegaly was caused by blood cell disease was dismissed. The results of serological tests for related viruses, including Epstein-Barr virus, hepatotropic viruses, cytomegalovirus, and HIV, were negative. Liver biopsy showed a disordered liver tissue structure; the central vein was visible, but the hepatic lobule structure was incomplete; most hepatocytes were degenerated, and some were dissolved and necrotic. Low levels of inflammatory cell infiltration and Kupffer cell proliferation were observed in the manifold area. However, there was no obvious proliferation of vascular endothelial cells. Glycogen levels in some hepatocytes were higher than the normal range, and the number of fat droplets in some hepatocytes substantial increased (Fig. 1a).

This study was approved by the ethics committee of the Hunan Children's Hospital review board (HCHLL-2021-78) and was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. After obtaining informed consent from the patient's parents, trio-whole exome sequencing (trio-WES) was performed for the patient and his parents. Peripheral blood (3 mL) was collected from the patient and family members, and genomic DNA was extracted using the blood genomic column medium volume extraction kit (CoWin Biosciences, Inc., Beijing, China), as per the manufacturer's protocol. The xGen Exome Research Panel v1.0 (Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc., Coralville, IA, USA) was used to capture the probe and conduct liquid hybridization with the genomic DNA library. The DNA fragments in the target region were enriched by WES analysis, and a whole exome library was established. An Illumina NovaSeq 6000 series sequencer (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used for high-throughput sequencing. The sequencing depth ranged from 100× to 150×, and the average coverage was >99.5%. These high-quality and reliable data were assessed for variation minimum allele frequencies using major variation distribution frequency databases (dbSNP, 1000 Genomes, ExAC, and ExAC East Asia). Additionally, we used protein structure prediction software, such as Provean, SIFT, Polyphen2-HVAR, Polyphen2-HDIV, and MutationTaster. Subsequently, the pathogenicity of the patient's related variants was rated according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines published in 2015 [Richards et al., 2015]. For the selected variants, primers were designed according to the Ensemble database, and Sanger sequencing was performed using an ABI 3730XL sequencer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The results were analyzed using the DNASTAR 5.0 software (DNASTAR, Inc., Madison, WI, USA).

The structures of the protein (PDB: 6E90) and small molecule ligands were downloaded from the RCSB database (https://www.rcsb.org/structure/6E90). The Arg269Trp variant structure was constructed on the basis of the crystal structure using the PyMOL software. The molecular dynamic simulation of the wild-type and variant proteins was carried out using the GROMACS 5.1.4 package under constant pressure and constant temperature, and the structural parameters were optimized using the Amber FF99SB force field. Finally, PyMOL was used to analyze the interactions.

The results of the gene mutation detection revealed that the patient presented with a homozygous variation in the GPD1 gene (NM_005276.3; c.805C>T/p.Arg269Trp), whereas both parents were heterozygous carriers of the same mutation. This variant has not been recorded in the dbSNP, 1000 Genomes, ExAC, and ExAC East Asia databases and has not been reported in the HGMD and ClinVar databases and is thus a novel variant of GPD1. Some bioinformatics software predicted that the variant might lead to protein structure changes, and the ACMG guidelines rated the variant as “likely pathogenic”. Finally, Sanger sequencing confirmed the existence of the mutation and revealed that both the elder brother and elder sister of the patient were heterozygous carriers of the same GPD1 mutation (Fig. 1b).

The variation observed by protein molecular dynamic simulation analysis suggested that the variant might not affect the secondary structure of the protein (PDB code: 6E90). Further, interaction analysis showed that the wild-type Arg269 formed H-bonds with the DHAP substrate, NAD coenzyme, and Gln295 residue. By contrast, the mutation-type Trp269 had a shorter side chain and formed water bridges with DHAP and NAD, although the H-bond with the Gln295 residue was retained (Fig. 2). However, further calculation of the binding free energy between the protein, substrate, and coenzyme indicated that the interaction between the protein and the substrate was weakened in the variant, which may have led to the occurrence of the disease.

In this case report, we describe a patient with HTGTI caused by a novel GPD1 mutation. His condition was mainly characterized by severe hepatomegaly, hepatic damage and fibrosis, and elevated aminotransferase and triglyceride levels but also by fasting hypoglycemia and insulin resistance phenotypes, which are rare in reported cases of HTGTI. The pathogenic mutation and phenotypic spectrum data of HTGTI were further assessed. Prior studies have reported that the main clinical features of HTGTI are hypertriglyceridemia, fatty liver, hepatic fibrosis, hepatomegaly, and growth retardation (Table 2). Additionally, some patients have demonstrated splenomegaly, insulin resistance, acanthosis nigricans, and hirsutism [Dionisi-Vici et al., 2016; Li et al., 2017]. The extensive overlapping of phenotypes between HTGTI and GSD makes the diagnosis and treatment difficult. The patient in this study was initially suspected of having GSD because of hepatomegaly, a decreased fasting blood glucose level, and impaired liver function. After oral cornstarch treatment and other corresponding treatments, the AST, ALT, and fasting blood glucose levels returned to normal. However, further liver pathological examination suggested the possibility of hepatic fibrosis, and the results of trio-WES revealed that the patient had a novel homozygous mutation in GPD1, c.805C>T/p.Arg269Trp. Finally, we diagnosed the patient with HTGTI according to the clinical characteristics and results of gene sequencing.

Analysis of the simulated protein structure with the Arg269Trp mutation revealed that this variation may reduce the GPD1 interaction with DHAP and NAD+ and affect the protein function. Ou et al. [2006] reported that Arg269 can form a ternary complex structure with DHAP and NAD+ and determined that Arg269 was the key residue of GPD1 protein recognition cofactor binding. Dionisi-Vici et al. [2016] also reported a GPD1 mutation at the Arg269 locus in a child with HTGTI; however, the base replacement was different from that in our case (c.806G>A/p.Arg269Gln). Interestingly, the process of diagnosis and treatment of the patient was very similar to that in our case, including similar clinical characteristics, such as the onset time, fasting hypoglycemia, hepatomegaly, and hypertriglyceridemia. However, previous studies have reported that the clinical phenotypes of the affected children are relatively similar in cases when the GPD1 protein function is downregulated (missense variation) or has a single dose deficiency (nonsense mutation or classical splicing variation). Only a few cases have presented with phenotypes such as cholestasis or hirsutism, which may be related to the potential pathogenesis of HTGTI. According to existing studies, GPD1 defects can limit the conversion of G3P to DHAP and thereby indirectly increase the content of G3P. This excess G3P can be used for triglyceride synthesis, thereby making patients prone to hypertriglyceridemia and resulting in a disease phenotype of abnormal lipid metabolism with concentrated phenotypes [Basel-Vanagaite et al., 2012; Dionisi-Vici et al., 2016; Li et al., 2017].

In addition to the extensively abnormal lipid metabolism phenotype, only 2 patients with HTGTI have been previously reported to show an insulin resistance or abnormal blood glucose phenotype [Dionisi-Vici et al., 2016; Li et al., 2017]. The patient in our case also exhibited insulin resistance, as revealed by the insulin release test. Although the mechanism underlying the development of such phenotypes is not clear, evidence suggests that GPD1 deficiency can lead to nonalcoholic hepatic steatosis and increase the risk of metabolic syndrome, such as insulin resistance or diabetes [Marchesini et al., 2001; Dionisi-Vici et al., 2016]. It should be noted that the symptoms of abnormal blood glucose levels in patients with HTGTI are mild and can be easily ignored clinically. Sato et al. [2016] confirmed that the loss of GPD1 protein function could inhibit glycerol-induced gluconeogenesis and also induce an active compensatory amino acid pathway, which may explain why patients with HTGTI are usually not affected by abnormal blood glucose levels.

According to current studies, the HTGTI cases due to GPD1 mutations are rare; thus, more cases are needed to assess the phenotypic and genetic variation spectra of the disease. Further basic research of disease pathogenesis, especially of the regulatory mechanism leading to insulin resistance or abnormal blood glucose levels, needs to be performed. In conclusion, we report a case of an infant with HTGTI that was caused by a novel GPD1 mutation (p.Arg269Trp), which is located in the core region of the GPD1 protein and is associated with its catalytic activity. Our case not only provides reference data for family genetic counseling and subsequent prenatal diagnosis but also provides clinical data for further mechanism-based research of GPD1 mutations.
