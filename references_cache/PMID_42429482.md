# FLVCR2 Regulation of Intracellular Heme Modulates Vascular Tip/Stalk Specification During Retinal Angiogenesis.
**Authors:** Goncalves A, Stanley A, Santander N, Mukherjee D, Liu X, Cleuren A, Arnold T, Antonetti D
**Journal:** Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1167/iovs.67.8.32](https://doi.org/10.1167/iovs.67.8.32)

## Content

PURPOSE: Fowler syndrome is an embryonic lethal disease with proliferative vasculopathy and hydranencephaly-hydrocephaly of the central nervous system caused by mutations in feline leukemia virus subgroup C receptor-related protein 2 (FLVCR2) characterized by large regions of hypovascularization along with glomeruloid formation of poorly organized vascular endothelial cell masses. The purpose of the current study is to elucidate the mechanisms by which FLVCR2 regulates retinal angiogenesis.

METHODS: Recent animal models have characterized reduced brain vascularization in gene deletion models of Flvcr2. Here, we use conditional knockout of Flvcr2 in mice and knockdown in primary bovine retinal endothelial cell culture to explore retinal development and associated mechanisms of angiogenic regulation by FLVCR2.

RESULTS: Endothelial cell-specific deletion of Flvcr2 or chemical inhibition of heme synthesis during post-natal retinal vascularization led to reduced vascular expansion in both primary (superficial) and secondary (deep) vascular plexuses, and a more severe but partially penetrant phenotype with attenuation of retinal vascular development and formation of abnormal vascular glomeruloids. Mechanistically, Flvcr2 knockdown in retinal endothelial cells led to reduced heme content and promoted NOTCH signaling and hyperproliferation that could be reversed with addition of a heme precursor. Similar effects were observed in cells treated with inhibitors of heme synthesis and reversed with addition of cell-permeable hemin. Furthermore, we found that FLVCR2/heme signaling attenuates NOTCH1 activation and affects downstream tip/stalk cell specification, while also preventing VEGF-induced proliferation.

CONCLUSIONS: Collectively, these data indicate a newly identified role for FLVCR2 and heme in the regulation of tip/stalk specification in angiogenesis.

Angiogenesis in the central nervous system (CNS) is a highly specialized and tightly regulated process that differs from vascular development in other tissues. This distinction arises from unique features, including the blood-retinal barrier (BRB) and blood-brain barrier (BBB), both of which are characterized by well-developed tight junctions and limited transcellular permeability. The BBB and BRB create selective permeability frameworks essential for maintaining the neural microenvironment.1 Unique cellular interactions, and CNS-specific genetic pathways further contribute to the regulation of vascular integrity and function.2,3 These unique characteristics are particularly evident when examining genetic disorders of CNS vascular development such as Norrie disease, caused by mutations in the NDP gene, which disrupts retinal vascularization.4 Similarly, cerebral cavernous malformations, associated with mutations in the CCM1, CCM2, or CCM3 genes,5 or β-catenin gene deletion6 compromise vascular stability in the brain and retina. Understanding CNS-specific vascular development and BBB/BRB formation will provide valuable insight into targeting diseases that involve neovascularization such as diabetic retinopathy, age-related macular degeneration, brain tumors, stroke, and others.

Genetic studies have linked human mutations in the FLVCR2 gene (aka major facilitator superfamily domain 7C [MFSD7C]) to Fowler syndrome,7–9 a rare, prenatal lethal, autosomal-recessive disorder characterized by cerebral proliferative vasculopathy and hydranencephaly-hydrocephaly. Diagnosis of Fowler Syndrome relies on cerebral histological characterization describing the brain as reduced to a thin mantle, with aberrant perforating thick vessels growing in every direction and ending in glomeruloid structures with inclusion-bearing ECs.8,10–12 The formation of glomeruloids, consisting of proliferative ECs and capillary vessels, has been observed in the inner layer of the developing retina.10 These structures are accompanied by a mass of cellular glial tissue and fibrin deposits, while the area anterior to the glomeruloids remains avascular. Importantly, even though a variety of tissues express the FLVCR2 gene, the only observable vascular defects present in Fowler syndrome are restricted to the CNS, affecting brain and retina.13

Gene deletion of Flvcr2 in mice recapitulates the altered vascular development in the central nervous system observed in Fowler syndrome. FLVCR2 was shown to be necessary for angiogenic sprouting in the brain, with reduced tip cell formation leading to loss of vascular sprouting. However, Flvcr2 is dispensable for maintaining the blood-brain barrier.14,15 FLVCR2 belongs to the major facilitator superfamily (MFS) of transporters16 and recent studies have uncovered that FLVCR2 is a choline transporter especially relevant in the context of the BBB.17,18 Structural studies demonstrated that FLVCR2 actively binds and transports choline, revealing specific residues critical for choline recognition and transport.17 This choline transport is believed to be essential for maintaining CNS-specific choline levels, supporting acetylcholine synthesis and membrane integrity.18

FLVCR2 was originally proposed as a cell surface heme importer with induced expression or knockdown regulating heme import.13 Although FLVCR2 acts as a choline importer, the data in this study shows that changes in FLVCR2 expression also impact cellular heme content. While the precise relationship between FLVCR2 function and cellular heme remains to be fully elucidated, the studies here reveal a role for heme in regulation of tip/stalk specification.

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Michigan and the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF). All mice were maintained on a C57BL6 background in specific pathogen-free conditions. Animals were housed in ventilated caging on a standard light/dark cycle and proper ambient temperature and humidity with food and water provided as desired.

To identify endothelial cell-specific genes uniquely expressed in the brain and retina, conditional Rpl22fl mice (MGI:4355967; JAX strain no. 011029)19 were crossed to hemizygous Tek-Cre males (MGI:2136412; JAX strain #004128)20 to generate Rpl22fl/fl;Tek-Cre mice, as previously described.21 To evaluate the effect of the heme synthesis inhibitor succinylacetone (SA) in vivo, C57BL/6 pups were given an intraperitoneal injection of SA (320 mg/kg) or saline solution on P0, 1 and 2 for analysis at P6, or injected on P6, 7 and 8 for analysis at P15. Retinas were collected at P15 and prepared for whole-mount staining of the vasculature as described below.

Mice with a hemagglutinin (HA) tag on the C-terminus of endogenous Flvcr2 (Flvcr2HA MGI:7567913) were previously generated.17 These mice were then bred to homozygosity with no apparent effect on fertility, frequency of live birth, or health of mice, indicating no apparent effect of attaching a HA tag to Flvcr2 on the protein's function.

In order to bypass embryonic lethality observed in Flvcr2 constitutive knockouts,14 we studied mice with tamoxifen-inducible endothelial cell specific conditional knockout (cKO) of Flvcr2, generated by crossing Flvcr2fl mice22 (kind gift of Jianzhu Chen, Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Cambridge, MA, USA) with Cdh5CreERT2 mice (kind gift from Ralf Adams, Max Planck Institute for Molecular Biomedicine, Muenster, Germany). High degrees of cellular recombination were detected based on CreER recombination reporters and whole-brain quantitative PCR. Postnatal gene deletion was induced by 3 intragastric injections of 100µg of tamoxifen diluted in corn oil (C8267; Sigma-Aldrich Corp., St. Louis, MO, USA) on P0, P1, and P2 for analysis at P6, or injected on P5, P6, and P7 for analysis at P10.

Bovine retinal endothelial cells (BREC) were isolated as previously described.23 For experimentation, BREC were used from passages 5 to 9. For knockdown studies, cells were transfected with 75nM of either scrambled RNA, small inhibitory (si)FLVCR2 RNA (no. sc-92360A; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), or overexpression (oe)FLVCR2 (MR208748; OriGene Technologies, Inc., Rockville, MD, USA) using Amaxa Nucleofection kit (Lonza Group, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were treated with reagents from Supplementary Table S1 for 24 hours.

To identify genes that may contribute specifically to the vasculature of the blood-brain and blood-retinal barrier, TRAP-seq was performed using Tek-Cre endothelial specific expression of a tagged ribosomal protein in adult mice. Deep sequencing of the co-precipitated mRNA from brain and retina vascular beds was conducted and results were compared to previously published data21 from heart, liver, lung, and kidney. We identified several genes of interest, including Mfsd2a, a lipid transporter that was recently shown to be required for blood-brain and blood-retina formation, acting as a suppressor of transcytosis.24,25 FLVCR2 was identified from the same MFS category, exhibiting higher expression in brain and retinal endothelial cells as compared to kidney, liver, lung, and heart samples (Fig. 1A). Flvcr2 expression measured by qRT-PCR in whole brain and retinal lysates revealed a higher expression in the retinal tissue when compared to brain (Fig. 1B). Flvcr2 expression in the vasculature was confirmed through fluorescent in situ hybridization at P7, showing colocalization with Pecam1. Strong Flvcr2 expression was detected in the retinal pigmented epithelium (RPE) (Fig. 1E). Expression of Flvcr2 and Pecam1 over developmental time course was confirmed, revealing an increase of Flvcr2 commensurate with increase in the endothelial cell marker (Fig. 1C). However, Flvcr2/Pecam1 ratio decreased at P7 and P12, when the vasculature undergoes active angiogenesis (Fig. 1D). To confirm FLVCR2 expression at the protein level and determine its subcellular localization, a mouse model expressing a hemagglutinin (HA) tag at the C terminus of FLVCR2 was used.17 Flat-mount analysis of retinas from P6, P21 and P30 Flvcr2HA/HA mice (Figs. 1F–I) indicated that FLVCR2 is found in endothelial cells of all three vascular plexuses, and the RPE (Fig. 1F). In the superficial vascular plexus (SVP) of P6 mice lower FLVCR2 expression was observed at the proliferating vascular front as compared to higher FLVCR2 levels in the more central arteriovenous region. Lower expression was observed at the growing front both in superficial tip (S Tips) and proliferating stalk cells (Fig. 1H). Higher expression was observed in the already stabilized vasculature of the central arteriovenous region. Interestingly, diving tip cells (D Tips) exhibit relatively high FLVCR2 expression as they descend to form the deep vascular plexus (DVP) (Fig. 1I). Immunogold labeling of Flvcr2HA/HA mice followed by transmission electron microscopy revealed FLVCR2 localized largely to the luminal surface in brain capillary endothelial cells (Fig. 1G) and retinal vein endothelial cells (Supplementary Fig. S1). A clear concentration of FLVCR2 was observed in luminal projections.

We observed a severe, partially penetrant, vascular phenotype in Flvcr2ECko mice at P15. A proportion of the retinas (2/14) displayed large avascular regions bordered by masses of poorly organized vessels, and glomeruli throughout the SVP while the intermediate and deep plexuses did not form (Fig. 2M). We combined SA treatment with gene deletion over the same time course and observed the same partial penetrance of this phenotype at about ten percent (2/19) (data not shown).

In order to explore in more detail the role of FLVCR2 and heme on the angiogenic processes, BREC were transfected with either scramble RNA or three distinct siRNAs specifically targeting FLVCR2. FLVCR2 expression was decreased by 75% with all 3 siRNAs (Fig. 3A). Knockdown of Flvcr2 using two siRNAs induced a significant increase in cell proliferation and the third siRNA trended toward the same increase (Fig. 3B). Tube formation was assessed using Matrigel matrix containing growth factors, knockdown of FLVCR2 led to an increase in master junctions, master segments and mesh density compared to scramble controls (Fig. 3C). FLVCR2 knockdown had no effect on retinal cell permeability to 70kDa dextran or electrical resistance (Supplementary Fig. S2) similar to effects on BBB permeability.14 Additionally, siFLVCR2 had no effect on expression of a related choline/heme transporter FLVCR1a, but did decrease heme catabolism enzyme HMOX1 expression compared to controls (Fig. 3D). FLVCR2 knockdown reduced intracellular heme content by 50% (Fig. 3E) and reduced uptake of the zinc mesoporphyrin nearly 25% (Fig. 3F). Importantly, both the increase in cell proliferation and tube formation induced by siFLVCR2 were reversed by adding the heme synthesis precursor aminolevulinic acid (5ALA) (Figs. 3G, 3H). Inhibition of heme biosynthesis with SA decreased intracellular heme content by 30% (Fig. 3I) and increased endothelial cell proliferation to a similar degree as FLVCR2 knockdown, which was reversed by supplementing a cell-permeable derivative of heme26 (Fig. 3J). Tube formation was also significantly increased following SA treatment (Fig. 3K). FLVCR2 knockdown or SA treatment did not alter electron transport chain activity and ATP synthesis as observed by Seahorse analysis. Addition of NMPP to inhibit ferrochelatase (FECH1) does reduce ATP production as previously reported27 (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Recent findings demonstrate that brain endothelial cells of Flvcr2 knockout mice reduce tip cells.14 Loss of FLVCR2 increased notch intracellular domain (NICD) production in BREC (Fig. 4B). The γ-secretase inhibitor DAPT was able to block siFLVCR2 driven increase in proliferation and tube formation (Figs. 4C, 4D). An antibody directed to the extracellular domain of NOTCH1 reduced siFLVCR2 driven proliferation to control levels (Fig. 4E). DAPT also prevented the increase in cell proliferation induced by heme synthesis inhibition with SA (Fig. 4F). The effect of heme on vascular sprouting was further assessed in ex vivo retinal explants. Explants were treated with SA at d0 or starting on d8 (after initial sprouts have formed) with analysis on d13 and compared to untreated controls. SA treatment starting on d0 showed no effect on explant vessel formation, but treatment beginning on d8 doubled vessel outgrowth (Fig. 4G).

We explored the relation of Flvcr2/heme regulation of cell proliferation with the well-established angiogenic cytokine VEGF. Knockdown of Flvcr2, or treatment with SA in BREC showed no effect on VEGF receptor phosphorylation or ERK activation (Supplementary Fig. S4). Treatment with VEGF led to a progressive (∼30%) decrease in FLVCR2 mRNA expression (Fig. 5A) with no change in heme levels observed (Fig. 5B). Knockdown of FLVCR2 decreased expression by ∼75% while VEGF treatment reduced FLVCR2 mRNA by ∼30% without a statistically significant additive effect when combined with the siRNA (Fig. 5C). Both treatments increased cell proliferation to a nearly identical degree. Combining knockdown and VEGF did not increase proliferation more than either treatment alone (Fig. 5D). Overexpression of FLVCR2 by plasmid transfection reduced BREC proliferation by ∼25% and suppressed VEGF-induced proliferation (Fig. 5E). VEGF induced proliferation was completely inhibited by addition 5ALA or hemin (Fig. 5F).

This study reveals a novel role for FLVCR2 and intracellular heme in regulating retinal angiogenesis. We demonstrate in vivo that FLVCR2 in the SVP of the developing retina is dramatically reduced at the peripheral angiogenic front and elevated in the central, more developed vasculature with D-Tip cells showing strong expression of FLVCR2. Endothelial-specific deletion of Flvcr2 impaired retinal vascular development of the SVP at P6 and DVP at P10. These vascular phenotypes were recapitulated by inhibiting heme synthesis with SA treatment over a similar time course. A dramatic phenotype, showing highly aberrant retinal angiogenesis with SVP glomeruloids, was observed with a partial penetrance of ∼15%. siFLVCR2 in BREC or heme synthesis inhibition resulted in a hyperproliferative stalk cell phenotype. Providing heme precursor or soluble hemin reversed these increases in BREC proliferation, strongly supporting a role for heme in regulating endothelial proliferation. The proliferation changes observed in Flvcr2 knockdown and heme synthesis inhibition are likely mediated by Notch signaling since the gamma-secretase inhibitor DAPT prevented this response in cells. Addition of soluble hemin or FLVCR2 overexpression can block VEGF-induced endothelial proliferation. Collectively, the data demonstrates that higher FLVCR2 promotes D Tip cell character, whereas reductions in FLVCR2 and intracellular heme promote stalk cell specification (Fig. 6).

The expression of FLVCR2 is notably enriched in retinal endothelial cells compared to endothelial cells in other tissues, including the brain, suggesting a critical and tissue-specific role of this transporter in retinal vascular development. Previous reports have shown that mice with germline deletion of Flvcr2 in ECs exhibit disrupted CNS angiogenesis and are embryonic lethal.14,15 This study used an inducible, endothelial specific, deletion of Flvcr2 allowing us to specifically target retinal endothelial development postnatally, bypassing effects on initial development of the brain vasculature. This approach enabled us to extend previous observations to the retina, revealing vascular abnormalities similar to those seen in Fowler syndrome.10 Indeed, deletion of endothelial Flvcr2 resulted in impaired retinal vascular development, characterized by reduced angiogenic sprouting and hypovascularized retinal tissue. This disruption affected both superficial angiogenesis and vascular sprouting into the deeper layers of the retina. A small subset of Flvcr2ECko mice displayed a more severe phenotype, suggesting variable penetrance and the possibility of compensatory mechanisms that are engaged upon Flvcr2 deletion. The nature of these compensatory responses is unknown and warrants further investigation. Importantly, the retinal vascular phenotype was replicated by inhibiting heme synthesis. These findings highlight the essential roles of FLVCR2 and heme in ensuring proper retinal vascular development.

Heme has been shown to have direct effects on endothelial gene expression. Overabundance of extracellular heme content leads to endothelial cell activation, increasing the expression of adhesion molecules and release of cytokines promoting leukocyte recruitment.29,30 In vitro, silencing the plasma membrane heme exporter FLVCR1a in endothelial cells causes defective tube formation and poor viability because of intracellular heme accumulation.31 Endothelial-specific Flvcr1a knockout mice show aberrant angiogenesis leading to hemorrhage and embryonic lethality.31 These Flvcr1a mutant embryos are characterized by reduced vasculature development, particularly in the limbs and tail where vessels do not form properly, and branching is compromised. The formation of a functional microvascular network is prevented by intracellular heme accumulation triggering EC death by paraptosis.31

Previous studies have investigated the role of heme in controlling retinal angiogenesis. Both chemical and genetic targeting of ferrochelatase (FECH1), the terminal enzyme in heme biosynthesis, effectively inhibit endothelial proliferation and neovascularization. This inhibition appears to result from two main factors: increased free radical production and specific blockage of the electron transport chain at complex IV, which collectively lead to reduced mitochondrial metabolic function.27,32,33 Our findings present an intriguing contrast to these previous studies since FLVCR2 knockdown, or SA treatment did not reduce electron transport chain activity and ATP synthesis in a magnitude comparable to FECH1 inhibition (Supplementary Fig. S3). This key difference suggests that the mechanism by which FLVCR2 knockdown regulates angiogenesis is likely through reduction of cytoplasmic heme or transcriptional mechanisms controlling tip/stalk cell fate determination rather than metabolic effects. Transcription factors BACH1/2 might contribute to this regulation. In cancer, heme levels have been shown to modulate BACH1 activity, controlling cell growth and metastasis through pathways that could similarly impact angiogenic processes.34,35 Interestingly, BACH1 is known to antagonize Nrf2 binding, which in-turn regulates tip/stalk selection via NOTCH signaling.36 Inhibiting endothelial protein C receptor (Epcr) was found to prevent pathological neovascularization. Epcr activates heme oxygenase (HO), reducing heme. Knockdown or inhibition of HO activity also inhibited pathological neovascularization.37 Proper retinal angiogenesis requires expression of the heme exporter Flvcr1a.38 5Ala was able to reduce human dermal vascular endothelial cell proliferation associated with protoporphyrin production.39

During vascular development DLL4/Notch mediates tip to stalk signaling. High VEGF concentration induces expression of the notch ligand in cells that specify as tip cells. DLL binding to Notch on neighboring cells induces intracellular γ-secretase mediated cleavage of the notch receptor to form the NICD, which translocates to the nucleus, activating transcriptional regulation of stalk cell specification and proliferation.40 DLL4 is itself a target of Notch/NICD, leading to a feed-forward signaling loop, whereas Jagged-1 (Jag1), an alternative Notch ligand, suppresses Notch signaling. Loss of DLL4 or inhibition of Notch signaling leads to increased tip cell formation, hyper-sprouting and increased branching.41,42 Conversely, Jag1 plays an opposing role to DLL4, promoting tip cell formation and increased vascular density. Interestingly, endothelial-specific Jag1 deletion results in reduced sprouting and decreased vessel branching and a significant decrease in the number of tips and filopodia.43 The similarity of our in vivo phenotype to that of Jag1 knockout mice suggests that FLVCR2 could act as an inhibitor of Notch signaling and promote tip cell specification. This hypothesis is congruent with in vitro data here, showing that FLVCR2 deficiency or heme depletion promotes a hyperproliferative stalk cell phenotype.

We further examined the role of heme in VEGF-induced proliferation. VEGF treatment did not have an apparent change in endothelial heme content despite a modest decrease in FLVCR2 mRNA. However, the ability to force FLVCR2 expression or enhanced heme content to blunt VEGF-induced proliferation supports the notion that FLVCR2 and intracellular heme act as negative regulators of endothelial cell proliferation and are important for proper tip/stalk specification.

Several questions remain to be addressed in future studies. FLVCR2 has been clearly shown to import choline in endothelial cells. Exactly how FLVCR2 regulates intracellular heme content, and how choline may be involved in this process, remains to be determined. Furthermore, the current data does not rule out an independent contribution of choline import in regulation of developmental angiogenesis. The precise mechanisms by which intracellular heme levels influence Notch signaling and tip-stalk cell specification also warrant further investigation. It is noteworthy that knockdown of Flvcr2 expression by 75% in endothelial cells using siRNA may impact heme content in a manner distinct from in-vitro constitutive knockout of Flvcr2. Finally, the interplay between heme signaling, VEGF pathways, and other angiogenic regulators should be further explored to build a comprehensive understanding of the complex regulatory networks governing retinal vascular development.

Our study demonstrates that FLVCR2 and heme contribute critical roles in controlling retinal vascular development by modulating endothelial cell proliferation and tip/stalk cell specification. These findings provide mechanistic insight into the vascular defects associated with Fowler syndrome and support a previously unrecognized regulatory role for heme in retinal vascular development, with potential therapeutic relevance for retinal disorders characterized by aberrant angiogenesis.
