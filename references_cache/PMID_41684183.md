Arginase 1 deficiency (ARG1D) is the least common urea cycle disorder. Neonatal
onset is rarely described, and hyperammonemic coma is less common in patients
with ARG1D compared to other urea cycle disorders. In recent years, we diagnosed
a high number of ARG1D patients in Mayotte, an insular (Comoro Islands)
department of France. This study aimed at retrospectively analyze and compare
our patients' data with those diagnosed and followed in Paris. We present a
series of 27 subjects included either in the Center Hospitalier de Mayotte
(CHM), Mamoudzou, France (n = 17), or in Hôpital Necker-Enfants Malades (NEM),
Paris, France (n = 10). Median age at diagnosis (n = 27) was 2 years (ranging
from birth to 15 years). Patients diagnosed at CHM were generally older than
those diagnosed at NEM, which may reflect a longer diagnostic delay in CHM. The
main clinical features at diagnosis included hyperammonemic coma (with or
without liver failure) and neurodevelopmental delay with spastic diplegia. Brain
MRI could be either normal or showed severe lesions in patients with overt
hyperammonemia at diagnosis. Eight different ARG1 variants were identified,
including two variants (c.466-2 A>G and c.766G>A) that were found exclusively in
subjects originating from the Comoro Islands. Despite no significant differences
in laboratory parameters, clinical outcomes remained better in NEM versus CHM
possibly ascribable to a longer diagnostic delay in CHM. These findings revealed
a new cluster of ARG1D associated with two severe variants in Mayotte,
suggesting that this region could possibly benefit from targeted newborn
screening for ARG1D due to its overrepresentation.

Arginase 1 is an enzyme that catalyzes the final step of the urea cycle, hydrolyzing arginine to ornithine and urea [1]. Pathogenic variants in ARG1, the gene encoding arginase 1, cause arginase deficiency (ARG1D), an autosomal recessive disorder belonging to urea cycle defects (UCD). Lack of arginase leads to impaired arginine catabolism with subsequent hyperargininemia and disruption of ureagenesis. As a result, nitrogen accumulates as ammonia (NH3). More importantly than hyperammonemia, increased arginine and neurotoxic guanidino compounds are implicated in ARG1D pathophysiology [2, 3]. ARG1D is the least common UCD. Its incidence was estimated between 1:350 000 and 1:1 000 000 [4, 5]. However, recent studies based on pathogenic variant frequencies in population databases suggest that ARG1D may be more prevalent than previously thought, with cases often remaining undiagnosed or misdiagnosed [6, 7]. More specifically, a recent and comprehensive analysis of allele frequency of ARG1 genetic variants gives one in 650 000 as the most likely estimate for the worldwide birth prevalence [6].

The clinical manifestations typically begin in infancy or early childhood and include progressive spastic paraparesis, developmental delay, cognitive decline, epilepsy, growth impairment, and episodes of acute or chronic hyperammonemia, usually without overt liver disease [1, 5]. Although ammonia concentrations are generally lower than in other UCDs [5], hyperammonemic decompensations remain an important cause of morbidity and mortality, including in our cohort. Neonatal onset is rare, and hyperammonemic coma occurs less frequently than in other UCDs [8]. Increased levels of arginine are thought to be involved in the pathophysiology, but other factors may be responsible for the neurological symptoms observed in ARG1D [5]. Among the proposed mechanisms, the accumulation of guanidino compounds derived from arginine appears particularly relevant and may contribute to the neurological manifestations of the disorder [2, 3]. Mouse model studies further suggest corticospinal tract dysmyelination, aligning with patient observations [9].

Standard of care (SOC) relies on a strict low‐protein diet supplemented with essential amino acids and nitrogen‐scavenging agents [10]. The objective is to reduce plasma arginine and ammonia levels, and current guidelines recommend maintaining plasma arginine below 200 μmol/L [10]. In practice, achieving these targets is often challenging, and long‐term neurocognitive impairments may persist even with adequate biochemical control [3, 11]. Several well‐documented cases identified by newborn screening or familial screening have demonstrated excellent outcomes with very early treatment [11, 12]. Pegylated human arginase 1 (pegzilarginase) was evaluated in two clinical trials, both showing significant lowering of plasma arginine and improvements in functional mobility in ARG1D patients [13, 14]. The therapy has now been approved by European Medicines Agency and Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency. Although long‐term studies are needed to confirm its long‐term clinical efficacy, these initial results are very promising for ARG1D where SOC failed to meet requirements.

Mayotte, formerly part of the Union of the Comoros, is now an overseas department of France, while remaining socially and geographically interconnected with the Comoro Islands. Socioeconomic challenges in Mayotte limit access to food, education, and healthcare, which directly affects management of disorders requiring strict dietary treatment such as ARG1D. Over the last decade, an unexpectedly high number of ARG1D cases was diagnosed in the Mayotte Pediatrics Department, prompting this retrospective analysis. Our primary objective was to describe the clinical presentation, laboratory parameters, neuroimaging and genetics features, and outcomes of the first documented ARG1D cluster in Mayotte (Comoro Islands). Our secondary objective was to compare these patients with ARG1D cases diagnosed over the last 25 years at the Paris Necker‐Enfants Malades Reference Center for Inborn Errors of Metabolism.

A multicentric retrospective study was conducted in the Center Hospitalier de Mayotte, Mamoudzou, France (CHM) and in Hôpital Necker‐Enfants Malades, Paris (NEM, Paris) between January 1st 1997 and May 1st 2022.

All patients diagnosed or hospitalized with ARG1D in one of the two hospitals during the time of analysis were included. Five patients in the present study population were also included in a trial on pegylated human arginase conducted by NEM (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03921541). For the present study, their follow‐up data was censored at the time of their inclusion in the trial.

Clinical data, anthropometric measurements and prescriptions were collected from the patients' medical and dietary files, in written or computerized format.

Outcome in living subjects with ARG1D was evaluated in terms of cognitive, motor skills and growth retardation, and presence or absence of epilepsy. Unfortunately, formal neurocognitive evaluation data were not available. We therefore distinguished severe cognitive impairment defined as a child who does not speak and is unable to reproduce simple tasks and moderate cognitive impairment defined as a child who can speak and/or reproduce simple tasks.

The determination of ammonia blood levels was performed locally. Plasma arginine concentrations and urinary orotic acid concentrations were measured centrally in the NEM laboratory. All laboratory parameters were retrieved from the medical database of the CHM or NEM.


ARG1 molecular genetic testing was performed to confirm diagnosis in all but 1 patient either by direct Sanger sequencing or by next‐generation sequencing of all exons and exon‐intron junctions of the ARG1 gene. Arginase enzyme activity was not measured except for patient 18 (P18), who was reported in 2009 [15].

Modelisation of Arginase 1 variants was performed with the Swiss‐Pdb viewer software using the RCSB PDB 6V7C protein structure.

GraphPad Prism version 9.0 was used for statistical analysis (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Continuous variables were presented as median and range (minimum, maximum). Categorical variables were presented as number of patients with available data. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney test.

Our study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review boards at each site. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of each patient prior to genetic testing.

During the period 1997 to 2022, 27 patients in 23 families were included either in the Center Hospitalier de Mayotte, Mamoudzou, France (CHM, n = 17, 63%), or in the Hôpital Necker‐Enfants Malades, Paris, France (NEM, n = 10, 37%). All 17 patients followed in Mayotte originate from the Comoro Islands (Mayotte or Comoros). Amongst the 10 patients followed up in Paris, 2 siblings (P18 and P19) originated from the Comoro Islands (but were not duplicated and counted only in NEM as they were followed‐up in NEM) and 8 had a mixed background.

Estimating the incidence and prevalence of ARG1D in Mayotte is challenging due to continuous movement of families between Mayotte and the other Comoro islands. Nevertheless, despite a smaller annual birth rate in Mayotte (~10 000) compared to the Paris region (~170 000), more patients were diagnosed in CHM than in NEM (n = 17, 63%), versus (n = 10, 37%), respectively (Figure S1). This observation supports the existence of a cluster of ARG1D within the Comoro Islands, suggesting a probable founder effect.

Eight different variants were identified, including 1 splice variant (c.466‐2A>G), one nonsense variant (c.61C>T), two missense variants (c.766G>A/p.Glu256Lys; c.532G>C/p.Gly178Arg), and four small insertion/deletions (c.470_486del; c.703_707delins; c.272_273ins; c.791_793del), among which five were never reported to date. Only two variants, c.466‐2A>G and c.766G>A, were found in all patients of Comorian origin whether diagnosed in CMH or in NEM. Indeed, the c.466‐2A>G represented 63% of pathogenic variants while c.766G>A/p.Glu256Lys represented 37% of pathogenic variants in these patients. The variant c.766G>A/p.Glu256Lys had not been described before and was predicted to be deleterious in silico (CADD, MetaSVM, Polyphen2). Arginase 1 is a trimeric binuclear manganese metalloenzyme. The variant p/Glu256Lys is located on the periphery of the peptide and may disrupt interactions between the three subunits by interfering with the interaction between the amino acids Glutamate256 and Arginine255 that allows interaction between the peptides two by two (Figure 5).

Two out of 17 patients in CHM had no overt cognitive impairment observed at the ages of 12 months and 13 months (P7 and P8). Three patients (P8, P16, and P19) had not reached the age of walking. Fifteen patients underwent a brain MRI at diagnosis (seven patients in CHM and eight patients in NEM) and/or during for follow‐up (one patient in CHM and five patients in NEM). P24 exhibited acute unilateral lesions with cytotoxic oedema (Figure 6E,F). In this study, we describe 27 patients with ARG1D. Seventeen were followed in Mayotte (CHM), all of Comorian origin, while 10 were followed in Paris (NEM), including two siblings of Comorian origin. The predominance of patients from the Comoro Islands supports a cluster of ARG1D cases, likely due to a founder effect. Genetic analysis revealed eight ARG1 variants in the cohort. Hence, the estimated prevalence of ARG1D was greater in the Comoro Islands than in the Paris region. Two variants, c.466‐2A>G and c.766G>A/p.Glu256Lys, were exclusively found in patients of Comorian origin, supporting the presence of a genetic founder effect. These variants were associated with severe phenotypes, with most patients exhibiting early symptoms (before 2 years of age). Other variants were found in non‐Comorian patients, mainly null variants, consistent with previously reported diversity of ARG1 mutations [1, 18].

Nineteen patients presented with symptoms before the age of 2 years, which is earlier than typically reported in the literature [11]. Two patterns were identified: (1) patients with spastic diplegia or pyramidal signs without overt hyperammonemia [19] experienced a longer delay to diagnosis and (2) patients presenting with acute hyperammonemic crises [18] were diagnosed earlier with a shorter diagnosis delay, highlighting the impact of acute episodes on early disease identification.

These data allowed estimation of diagnostic delay, revealing that patients in CHM, particularly those with subtle early symptoms, had a substantially longer delay compared to NEM patients.

Age at diagnosis was greater for patients in CHM compared to patients in NEM. This could be related to several factors. First, all patients were not born in Mayotte (n = 3). They came from the nearby Comoro Islands, and were often over 3 years of age when they arrived in Mayotte. Second, some subjects could have presented mild symptoms before age 2 years, without medical evaluation. Finally, some patients were misdiagnosed. Seven had a history of unexplained epilepsy, with multiple emergency visits before ARG1D diagnosis was made. Four patients presented with acute respiratory distress, and were initially diagnosed as having bronchiolitis despite respiratory alkalosis which could have pointed toward UCD. These patients were screened for hyperammonemia only when exhibiting a low level of consciousness. As clinicians in CHM became more aware of ARG1D, the disease was identified more often and at a lower age with time (Figure 2). Possibly due—though not formally demonstrated owing to data lacking—to a greater delay in diagnosis, CHM patients exhibited a worse outcome than the NEM ones.

The single patient with persistent cholestasis was later diagnosed with genetic sclerosing cholangitis due to a pathogenic DCDC2 variant, confirming an alternative genetic cause of liver disease. This case highlights the importance of careful differential diagnosis in ARG1D patients with liver abnormalities as liver disease including cholestasis is reported in ARG1D [1].

All symptomatic patients showed elevated plasma ammonia and arginine at diagnosis. Acute hyperammonemia, typically considered uncommon in ARG1D [11], was observed in 83% of symptomatic patients in this cohort. No differences were noted between CHM and NEM patients regarding ammonia, arginine, glutamine, or urinary orotic acid. Our described cohorts stand out with respect to higher mortality and higher prevalence of hyperammonemia than observed in other ARG1D patients. That may be ascribable to a more sever genotype in these two cohorts. During follow‐up, arginine concentrations decreased under therapy, with most patients achieving target levels (< 200 μmol/L). Four patients enrolled in a pegylated human arginase (pegzilarginase) trial had their follow‐up censored at the time of inclusion.

Clinical outcomes were generally poorer in CHM compared to NEM. Growth, cognitive, and motor outcomes were more severely affected in CHM, likely due to a combination of genetic factors, delayed diagnosis, and difficulties with implementing and maintaining dietary therapy. Importantly, acute hyperammonemic decompensation was the major cause of death in this cohort, particularly in CHM (six/seven deaths), despite ARG1D typically being considered less prone to acute crises compared to other urea cycle disorders. This emphasizes the role of disease severity, genetic factors, and challenges in treatment adherence in determining outcomes. Two patients diagnosed through family screening (P19 and P21) were treated from birth and exhibited normal neurocognitive outcomes, demonstrating the benefit of early detection and treatment.

Brain MRI findings reflected the clinical severity and timing of diagnosis. Patients with delayed diagnosis and severe hyperammonemia displayed diffuse cortical and subcortical lesions, while early‐diagnosed patients (P19 and P21) exhibited normal neuroimaging. Corticospinal tract lesions, previously associated with ARG1D [21], were observed in patients with abnormal imaging albeit in a broader pattern of diffuse and severe cortical and subcortical abnormalities, but not in those with early intervention.

This study underscores the importance of early recognition and intervention in ARG1D. The identification of early‐onset patients with subtle pyramidal signs highlights the need for increased awareness among clinicians. Moreover, pegzilarginase therapy holds promise for reducing arginine plasma concentrations and potentially preventing acute hyperammonemic crises, complementing standard dietary and nitrogen‐scavenger treatments [14]. Given the high mortality in this cohort due to hyperammonemia, our findings support the inclusion of ARG1D in targeted newborn screening programs, particularly in populations with identified founder variants.

Limitations include the retrospective nature of the study and incomplete data for some patients. Estimation of age at onset was sometimes challenging, particularly in patients with subtle early symptoms. Nonetheless, the available data allowed identification of subgroups with different diagnostic delays and clinical courses.

We report a series of 27 patients with ARG1D, highlighting a novel Comorian founder effect with two recurrent variants associated with early‐onset, severe disease. Our data demonstrate that acute hyperammonemia remains a major cause of death in this cohort, contrasting with prior reports. Early diagnosis, family screening, and innovative treatments such as pegzilarginase may significantly improve outcomes. These findings support targeted newborn screening in high‐risk populations to prevent severe complications.
