Abstract
Background Stiff-Person Syndrome is a rare autoimmune disease of the central nervous system characterised by 
progressive stiffness of the axial muscles, due to the inadequate inhibition of excitatory neurotransmitters. The 
pathophysiology is considered to be a dysfunction of GABAergic (γ-aminobutyric acid) inhibitory pathways, leading to 
hyperexcitability at spinal interneuron and cortical levels, which causes impaired muscle relaxation (Hadavi et al. in Pract 
Neurol, 2011). The natural history of SPS is understudied and symptoms range from mild to severe progressive illness. 
Considering the rarity and different spectrum of presentation, diagnosis is difficult and often missed (Espay and Chen 
in Muscle Nerv 34:677–90, 2006).  
Case presentation We present a 60 year-old Asian-origin gentleman with a 10 year history of right lower limb weakness 
and paraspinal muscle spasms, and a 2 month history of left lower limb weakness with jerky, intermittent, and painful 
spasms that left him bedridden. The absence of clinical improvement despite multiple negative workups elsewhere 
and an inconclusive diagnosis indicates a need for further evaluation and consideration of an alternative diagnosis. The 
clinical syndrome of insidious-onset spastic paraparesis, paraspinal muscle spasms, and myoclonic jerks without bladder 
involvement led to localization to the spinal cord. After a preliminary etiological screen and strong clinical localization 
alongside antibody testing, Stiff-Person Syndrome was suspected as an autoimmune disorder. The electromyogram 
response to benzodiazepines played a pivotal role in promptly confirming the diagnosis. The patient was commenced on 
appropriate therapy with muscle relaxants, immune modulation and rehabilitation, significantly improving symptoms.  
Conclusion Stiff Person Syndrome, a severe and disabling autoimmune disease, is often underdiagnosed or misdiagnosed. 
This case underscores the critical importance of early diagnosis based on clinical evaluation, immune markers and the 
prompt initiation of treatment to achieve significant clinical improvement. Diazepam response to Electromyography 
(EMG) played a crucial role in confirming the diagnosis of Stiff-Person Syndrome, reinforcing its significance as a diagnostic 
tool alongside antibody testing.

1  Background
SPS is a rare autoimmune disorder, first described in 1956 by Moersch and Waltman in a case series of 14 patients 
who presented with fluctuating diffused and truncal stiffness and intermittent spasms [3 ]. SPS presents various clinical 
manifestations ranging from isolated limb stiffness, thoracolumbar paraspinal stiffness, and generalized muscle spasms 
with an exaggerated startle, with severe variants causing dysautonomia and brain stem dysfunction which leads to delay 
in diagnosis and mismanagement. The main pathology proposed is the reduction of the inhibitory neurotransmitter 
γ-aminobutyric acid. This is elucidated in a study where the levels of GABA was checked using MR spectroscopy, showing 
lower levels of GABA in the motor cortex in SPS patients[4 ].The activities of daily living become severely impaired over 
time however timely diagnosis can lead to significant clinical improvement. We report a case of a patient with 10 years 
history of progressive spastic weakness of lower limbs, with multiple negative workups in the primary care leading to 
a bed-bound state. Once the diagnosis was confirmed, immunosuppression was initiated and the patient achieved 
functional capacity within one week of treatment.
2  Case presentation
We report a 60 year-old gentleman of Asian origin, who is a known case of diabetes, who presented with stiffness of 
the lower back and right lower limb for the last 10 years, which was insidious in onset and progressive. He was initially 
noted to have difficulty while walking and climbing stairs and had recurrent falls. However, there was no history of 
tripping on objects or dragging of feet, no slowness of activities, imbalance or worsening in the dark. There was no loss 
of consciousness or light-headedness in the past. He later progressed to have difficulty in bending down to lifting objects 
or wearing his footwear. He presented to multiple hospitals and has had multiple investigations done including imaging, 
which was inconclusive. He was managed conservatively at a local centre as Idiopathic Parkinson’s disease and stroke 
and there was no improvement in the symptoms.
The symptoms worsened over the past 2 months when he developed painful intermittent involuntary jerky movements 
of the right lower limb with preserved consciousness. These episodes were sudden, aggravated by the initiation of 
movement, tactile stimuli, and loud noises, and subsided partially during sleep. He was also observed to have stiffness 
in his left lower limb, with difficulty bending his knees and hip. Due to these symptoms, he had been confined to bed for 
the past week and required assistance with daily activities. He did not have any associated weakness of his upper limbs, 
sensory symptoms or bladder and bowel involvements. There was no history of fever, weight loss, seizure or any drug or 
toxin exposure in the past. There was no history of any constitutional symptoms.
On examination, his vital signs were within normal limits and no abnormal skin lesions, or thyroid swelling were noted. 
Neurological examinations showed normal cognitive functions, cranial nerve assessment and speech assessment. He 
did not have any associated sensory loss or band-like sensation. Motor examination revealed a flexed attitude of the 
lower limb with normal bulk of all limbs. His power on upper limbs were 5/5 proximally and distally with normal reflexes 
however proximal lower limb weakness could not be assessed due to increased pain and spasm. The distal power was 
noted to be 4/5 with increased hip, knee and ankle tone bilaterally. He was noted to have severe neck and paraspinal 
muscle spasms superimposed with involuntary arrhythmic jerky movements of the entire right lower limb precipitated 
by a startle. There were co-contractions of agonist and antagonist muscles in the right lower limb with posturing. They 
were short-lasting and disappeared gradually on removal of the trigger or at rest. There was hyperlordosis of the lumbar 
spine and a woody feel of the axial muscles. His anal tone was normal.
In summary, a 60 year-old diabetic, with chronic gradual onset lower back and right lower limb stiffness with gait 
dysfunction for 10 years with previous extensive evaluation which was non contributory, presenting with recent 
worsening of left lower limb weakness and painful intermittent jerky movements. There was no gross motor or sensory

3  Discussion
Stiff person syndrome is a rare autoimmune neurological disease first described by Moersch and Waltman in 1956 
in patients who presented with stiffness and spasms after their patients used descriptions such as “falls as a wooden 
man, ” “falls like a wax dummy, ” “stiff, ” or “board like” . The characteristic signs and symptoms were muscle rigidity, 
hyperreflexia and truncal spasms with excellent response to diazepam [2 ]. The incidence is estimated to be 1 to 2 
per million per year and more common among women in the 4th decade [3 ]. The incidence is likely underestimated 
due to underdiagnosis. Patients are usually misdiagnosed as different neurological syndromes, including vascular 
strokes and extrapyramidal syndromes.
The proposed pathophysiology is an autoantibody Glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) mediated inhibition of 
inhibitory neurotransmitter γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA). GAD is a pyridoxal 5’-phosphate-dependent enzyme, which 
is involved in catalyzing the decarboxylation of GABA, which is an inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous 
system that decreases neuronal excitability in the brain and plays an important role in muscle tone regulation. GAD 
is normally expressed in neurons, pancreatic islet β-cells, oviduct mucosal epithelial cells, and spermatocytes and 
spermatids in the testes. There are two isoforms of GAD (GAD 65 and 67) and antibodies in SPS are mainly against 
GAD-65. Anti-GAD antibodies cause altered levels of GABA leading to a loss of neural inhibition of skeletal muscles 
and excessive unintentional muscle contractions [1 ]. Other GAD-Antibody spectrum disorders include Autoimmune 
epilepsy, Cerebellar Ataxia, Limbic encephalitis and PERM.
Other less commonly identified antibodies are Anti-Amphiphysin antibodies and Glycine-receptor antibodies 
[6, 7]. Autoimmune association of GAD-antibody is Type-1 Diabetes mellitus(30%) and thyroid disorders and Anti-
Amphiphysin antibodies are strongly associated with paraneoplastic syndromes, mainly breast carcinoma [7 –9] 
(Table 3).
Classical SPS presents with axial muscle stiffness, exaggerated lumbar lordosis and episodic spasms, triggered by 
external stimuli. Stiffness improves or disappears during sleep or with Diazepam [10, 11]. Among the other variants, 
focal SPS presents asymmetric stiffness of limbs, progressive encephalitis with rigidity(PERM) presents with subacute 
encephalitis like nystagmus, opsoclonus, dysarthria, dysphagia, ophthalmoparesis and cognitive decline [12– 14]. 
Paraneoplastic SPS, comprising 5% of patients, manifests as cervical stiffness and is associated with breast, colon, 
lung, thymus malignancies and in Hodgkin’s lymphomas [15].
Stiff person syndrome is mainly a clinical diagnosis with no specific diagnostic tests.
There is no routine laboratory abnormalities associated with SPS. Serum creatine kinase and Glycated Haemoglobin 
will be elevated in cases of Type 1 Diabetes. HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQB1 genes are associated with SPS. Oligoclonal 
bands auto-antibodies, including GAD in CSF, have also been observed in cases with very high anti-GAD titres.

Electrophysiology plays a major role in diagnosing SPS with subtle symptoms. A comprehensive study involving 
three patients with Stiff-Person Syndrome (SPS) utilized simultaneous video-EEG-surface EMG recordings alongside 
routine EMG to characterize key electrophysiological features [16]. Findings included continuous muscle activity that 
varied with sleep–wake states, voluntary and passive movements, and medication effects. Additionally, superimposed 
intermittent generalized contractions persisted into drowsiness, interfering with sleep onset. The study also noted 
abnormal co-contractions of antagonistic muscles and identified a distinct motor response resembling stimulus-induced 
truncal myoclonus, observed consistently across all patients. IN SPS, antibodies target the GAD enzyme, crucial for GABA 
synthesis, resulting in reduced GABA levels and impaired inhibitory muscle control. Benzodiazepines like diazepam and 
clonazepam enhance GABAergic activity by potentiating GABA receptor function and strengthening inhibitory circuits. 
Therefore, suppression of continuous abnormal motor unit activity in the agonist and antagonist muscles with acute 
diazepam administration serves as a key diagnostic criterion, reinforcing the presence of GABAergic dysfunction, which 
underlies the pathophysiology of SPS. This finding not only supports the diagnosis but also highlights the clinical utility 
of EMG in differentiating SPS from other neuromuscular disorders.
The progressive nature along with variability in its clinical presentation poses challenges for diagnosis as well [1]. The 
average time to diagnosis from onset of symptoms was 3.5 years and the time to treatment was 5 years [7 ]. Given the 
prolonged nature of the symptoms, autoimmune disorders are often considered lower in the list of differentials. However, 
Anti-GAD antibody disorders stand out as an example of chronic autoimmunity.
While spasticity, rigidity, and myoclonus are characteristic of Stiff-Person Syndrome, differentiating it from other 
conditions with similar presentations is crucial for effective management. The differential diagnosis should include non-
compressive myelopathy, hereditary spastic paraparesis, primary lateral sclerosis, multiple sclerosis, and motor neuron 
disease. Less frequent considerations include Parkinson’s disease, myopathies, and neuromyotonias [1].
It is crucial to timely initiate therapy to prevent or lessen progression and avoid long-term complications. Most 
of the patients improve with medications, however, some patients show disease progression over time, leading to 
permanent orthopaedic abnormalities, inability to walk, and disability. SPS patients are prone to lumbar hyperlordosis, 
joint deformities, and muscle atrophy, causing abnormal posturing, gait abnormalities and recurrent falls [17].
Treatment of SPS aims at symptom relief and immunomodulation. Therapies target the two key pathogenic 
mechanisms: GABAergic dysfunction and autoimmunity.
[18]. Benzodiazepines augment GABA-dependent pathways and cause profound muscle relaxation. The initial drug of 
choice is diazepam, starting at 5 mg four times daily, with gradual dose increases over several weeks to control stiffness 
and improve mobility. Daily doses of diazepam needed to control the disease can be as high as 100 to 200 mg/day in 
some patients. GABA-B agonist Baclofen can be used to treat spasticity along with benzodiazepines. Intrathecal baclofen 
is used to treat severe spasticity due to poor CSF bioavailability and can greatly improve the symptoms [1]. Newer options 
like Levetiracetam and Pregabalin has been studies and has shown adequate improvement in symptoms [19]. Dantrolene, 
Clonazepam, Tizanidine, Gabapentin and Valproate are other options.
In patients with insufficient symptomatic relief from GABAergic agents such as benzodiazepines or baclofen, or 
those with severe disease impacting daily function, immune-modulating therapy will be considered. Intravenous 
immunoglobulins are considered the first-line option for immunomodulation. IVIG is typically administered at a total 
dose of 2 g/kg, either as 1 g/kg over two consecutive days or 0.4 g/kg daily for five days. IVIG has shown promising 
efficacy in both adults and children with Stiff-Person Syndrome (SPS), with evidence suggesting it may neutralize serum 
anti-GAD antibodies [1]. When administered at 2 g/kg over two days for three months, IVIG significantly reduced stiffness 
scores and improved mobility, fall frequency, and daily function in 11 out of 16 patients compared to placebo [20]. 
A retrospective analysis of 34 patients receiving home IVIG infusions reported that 57% of patients felt “better, ” 19% 
remained the “same, ” and 13% felt “worse” compared to before treatment [21]. However, cost remains the main deciding 
factor in developing countries.
B-cell depletion with Rituximab or plasma exchange, which has good efficacy and a lesser side effect profile is 
used when there is poor response to IVIG. Rituximab, a chimeric B cell-depleting anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, is 
administered at a dose of 375 mg/m 2 given weekly for four consecutive doses and can be repeated every four to eight 
months, depending upon the patient’s clinical course. This may increase remission in stiff person syndrome patients 
refractory to standard care, but more studies are needed to guide therapy for this rare neurological disorder [22]. Plasma 
exchange has not been extensively investigated, but it has demonstrated improvement in mobility and minimize 
disability in SPS. The mechanism of action is thought to be removal of key components of the immune system, including 
complements, cytokines, and other modulatory factors. However, the evidence supporting its effectiveness remains 
insufficient [23]. Studies suggest it may be useful as adjuvant therapy, in patients with no IVIG regimen response. In an

While glucocorticoids have demonstrated efficacy and was once considered as first line agent in controlling symptoms 
of Stiff-Person Syndrome, they are not considered first-line treatment due to their potential side effects, especially in 
patients with diabetes mellitus.
The prognosis of patients with SPS remains unpredictable and most patients experience a progressive decline in 
function, leading to significant disability, if not treated effectively. SPS significantly impacts the quality of life (QoL) of 
patients, with many experiencing severe depression and anxiety. The presence of psychiatric comorbidities is common 
and can further complicate the prognosis. Addressing these psychiatric symptoms is crucial for improving overall 
patient outcomes [24].
Our patient presented with 10-year history of progressive axial muscle stiffness with recent worsening jerky movement 
of the right lower limb with no long tract signs or cerebral signs suggesting an SPS. His Anti-GAD antibodies were elevated. 
EMG showed classical continuous motor unit electrical activity which was abolished by intravenous benzodiazepines. 
Elevated CPK levels were considered to be due to the constant muscle spasm, which showed significant improvement 
in the levels in subsequent follow-up after therapy was initiated (9458 µ/L to 118 µ/L).
Symptomatic relief was achieved with oral Benzodiazepines and limb physiotherapy. Rituximab was given for 
immunomodulation along with IV Methylprednisolone due to financial concerns. The patient was clinically better, and 
adequate ambulation was achieved on 6 month follow up. Prompt clinical diagnosis with the help of EMG helped in 
quick diagnosis and appropriate therapy initiation in this case.
4  Conclusion
In conclusion, Stiff person syndrome is a rare disease with severely disabling stiffness with spasms which can impair 
daily physical activity. It is often misdiagnosed or underdiagnosed leading to delayed diagnosis, progression of disease 
and poor quality of life. It is a diagnosis of exclusion making it a diagnostic challenge in the real-world scenario, hence a 
prompt clinical localization, EMG with response to benzodiazepines and autoimmune and paraneoplastic workup, will 
help in accurate diagnosis and appropriate management. SPS is a classic example of chronic autoimmunity disorder, 
and it responds well to immunotherapy and early initiation of the same assists in adequately controlling the disease. The 
prognosis of SPS is highly variable and depends on several factors, including the subtype of SPS, the presence of specific 
antibodies, and the timeliness and effectiveness of treatment. Early diagnosis and appropriate management are critical 
for improving outcomes and quality of life for patients with SPS. Increased awareness and understanding of diseases 
among medical professionals are essential for better diagnostic rate and prompt management.
