# Identifying NDUFA12 mutation in a Saudi family: An unusual presentation of mitochondrial Complex I deficiency mimicking as idiopathic intracranial hypertension in a patient with papilledema and visual loss.
**Authors:** Alshamrani FJ, Alajmi MS, Almuslim NI, Alsubaie MM, Fardan GM, Alabdali MM
**Journal:** J Family Community Med (2026)
**DOI:** [10.4103/jfcm.jfcm_322_25](https://doi.org/10.4103/jfcm.jfcm_322_25)

## Content

Mitochondrial diseases are considered one of the most common groups of neurogenetic diseases. Complex I (CI) deficiency is the most encountered single enzyme deficiency of the mitochondrial diseases. The mutation of the NDUFA is linked to Leigh syndrome and CI defects. This article reports on a patient with mutation in NDUFA12 that was initially perceived as idiopathic intracranial hypertension, calling attention to the importance of considering NDUFA12 mutations in optic atrophy and dystonia diagnoses, particularly in young patients with new onset headache and progressive bilateral visual impairment. In addition, it emphasizes the need to explore other nonneurological features in diagnosing mitochondrial disease in those who do not fit into a defined syndrome. Further research on NDUFA12 variants is essential for a better understanding of their wide phenotypic spectrum.

Mitochondrial diseases are among the most prevalent inborn errors in metabolism.[1] They are a heterogeneous group of disorders with multisystem involvement, particularly affecting organs that demand high energy, such as the brain, eyes, heart, and muscles.[23] This explains why, in most cases, mitochondrial disorders result in neurodegeneration and/or muscle weakness.[4]

Mitochondria play a major role in adenosine triphosphate production through oxidative phosphorylation. This process depends on the transport of electrons from nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH), which are sequentially converted into molecular oxygen through enzymatic respiratory chain complexes (Complexes I–V) located in the inner mitochondrial membrane.[1] Complex I (CI) deficiency is the most common respiratory chain complex deficiency in childhood, accounting for 20%–30% of all mitochondrial diseases. This condition exhibits considerable heterogeneity and manifests a wide range of clinical presentations, including fatal infantile lactic acidosis, encephalomyopathies, leukoencephalopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and exercise intolerance.[15] Usually, the onset of symptoms occurs after birth, typically after a normal developmental period. Frequently encountered neurological signs include dystonia, failure to thrive, spasticity, ataxia, optic atrophy, nystagmus, and hypotonia, leading ultimately to death. However, there are cases of long-term survival and adult-onset, although rare.[5] Several ophthalmic manifestations have been identified, with external ophthalmoplegia, such as chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia, Kearns–Sayre syndrome, and Pearson syndrome as some of the most common clinical manifestations found in various mitochondrial syndromes. In addition, isolated painless bilateral optic neuropathy is seen in Leber hereditary optic neuropathy and dominant optic atrophy.[6]

In this study, we report on a patient who presented with subacute headache followed by visual impairment. She was initially given a possible diagnosis of idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) and was found to have a silent ischemic stroke on brain imaging. Notably, we identified an unusual variant associated with recognized mitochondrial syndromes.

Our patient was a 30-year-old right-handed Saudi female, born to consanguineous Saudi parents. She was delivered full-term following an uneventful pregnancy and achieved developmental milestones at the appropriate ages. She graduated from nursing college and currently works as a nurse. Her past medical history was unremarkable.

The patient’s younger sister exhibited right-sided spasticity and hemiplegia at the age of 9, was undiagnosed, and was conservatively managed with extensive physiotherapy sessions. Her nephew developed hemiplegia and dysarthria at the age of 10, and is still under investigation. During visiting hours, it was observed that the patient’s sister was short in stature, had a very thin body, and intentional tremor with limping gait.

The patient began to experience a holocephalic headache one month before hospital admission. The headache was initially moderate in intensity, aching in nature, nonpositional, and partially relieved by simple analgesics (paracetamol) taken at home and a few hours later the headache recurred. There were no identifiable triggers.

On August 6, 2023, she presented to the Emergency Department (ED) with worsening headache severity, which was then only partially relieved by analgesics. She was discharged with painkillers.

On August 14, 2023, she returned to the ED with the same complaint. Furthermore, her vision in both eyes was blurred, but worse in the right eye (OD), and she had black spots in her visual field. She was referred to Ophthalmology Department for further evaluation and management.

The initial ophthalmology assessment showed best-corrected visual acuity of 20/40 in the OD and 20/25 in the left eye (OS) with impaired color vision bilaterally 4/15, 14/15, respectively. Fundoscopy revealed elevated, blurred optic disc margins bilaterally, more pronounced on the right, which lead to a diagnosis of papilledema.

On August 16, 2023, the patient was evaluated by a neuro-ophthalmologist, who gathered some more history. The patient reported that 2 weeks earlier, her headache had localized to the left frontoparietal area, changed from aching sensation to pulsatile nature, and progressively worsened to an intensity of 8/10 on the pain numeric scale. The pain did not radiate, and was only partially relieved by paracetamol. It worsened when she lay down. This was accompanied by nausea and recently, bilateral blurred vision resulting in concerns of total visual loss. On examination, visual acuity and color vision were unchanged from previous assessments. Pupils reacted sluggishly but without afferent pupillary defect. Eye movements were full, with intact saccades and pursuits. Visual field testing revealed central scotomas in both eyes [Figure 1]. Fundoscopy revealed a flat retina and bilateral optic nerve head (ONH) hyperemia with nasal elevation (OD > OS). Therefore, bilateral optic neuritis and IIH were suspected.

Noncontrast head computed tomography revealed hypodensity in the right putamen [Figure 2]. The patient was referred to the neurology team for further assessment.

Plain CT head axial cut showed hypodensity of right putamen

Neurology assessment on August 16, 2023, noted the patient’s short stature and thin build. Multiple hypopigmented patches were observed on the left cheek, right temporal area near the hairline, and the volar aspect of the left forearm. Neurological examination was otherwise unremarkable except for decreased bilateral visual acuity with impaired color perception (right worse than left) and bilateral papilledema (Grade 2 right, Grade 1 left). A lumbar puncture was performed, and opening pressure was 30 cm H2O. Based on the papilledema and right putamen hypodensity, the differential diagnoses were IIH and a stroke in a young adult.

On August 18, 2023, optical coherence tomography revealed ONH elevation in both eyes (disc area in mm2: 2.77 OD, 2.47 OS). Retinal nerve fiber layer thickness measurements are shown in Figure 3.

Fundus fluorescein angiography showed delayed filling of the right disc without bilateral disc leakage, suggestive of pseudoedema. In addition, visual evoked potential using LED goggles revealed evidence of optic neuropathy, with prolonged latencies across all waveforms and reduced amplitudes in both eyes, but with more pronounced effects in the OS [Figure 4], indicating axonal damage. These findings necessitated further investigation into underlying etiologies capable of producing such severe optic involvement at presentation. As part of the stroke workup, noncontrast brain magnetic resonance imaging [Figure 5] confirmed a chronic silent infarction in the right dorsal putamen, with otherwise unremarkable vascular imaging.

Given the complex clinical picture, several differential diagnoses were considered, including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pressure disorders, young-onset stroke, Demyelinating diseases, neurocutaneous syndromes (phakomatoses), and mitochondrial disorders. Notably, the patient’s short stature, positive family history, skin findings suggestive of phakomatosis, and elevated CSF opening pressure, in the absence of a clear structural lesion, prompted the decision to pursue genetic testing early in the diagnostic workup. Of the mitochondrial disorders, Leber’s hereditary optic neuropathy is normally the first to be considered in young adults with severe subacute bilateral visual loss.

Genetic analysis was performed using mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and nuclear mitochondrial gene panel sequencing, including next-generation sequencing-based CNS gene analyses. The results revealed a homozygous nonsense mutation in the NDUFA12 gene: c.4G>T (p.Glu2*), confirming a diagnosis of autosomal recessive mitochondrial CI deficiency, nuclear type 23.

On August 31, 2023, the patient was discharged home on Aspirin 100 mg orally once daily. There was a marked improvement of her headache and static visual impairment. She was followed up regularly at a neurology and neuro-ophthalmology clinic.

CI deficiency is the most common single-enzyme deficiency of mitochondrial disorders. Identifying the variable genotype phenotypes associated with CI deficiency is challenging. In cases of isolated CI deficiency, the underlying genetic cause may include mutations in mtDNA that affect one of the seven structural subunits, nuclear DNA mutations, involving any of the 37 nuclear-encoded structural subunits, mutations in ancillary proteins essential for proper assembly and biogenesis of CI. This extensive genetic diversity significantly complicates the diagnostic process for affected individuals.[78]

Here, we report on a patient whose symptoms began in her 30’s, in contrast to the majority of previously reported cases, which typically present early in life and often have poor outcomes, particularly in patients with nuclear-encoded mutations.[910] In a 2022 case series involving 18 patients with NDUFA12 variants, the age at the onset of symptoms ranged from birth to 28 years. Notably, a Turkish male in that study presented with visual impairment as his initial symptom at 28 years of age.[5]

NDUFA12 mutations have been associated with a broad clinical spectrum. In one study involving seven patients, dystonia, either focal or generalized, was the predominant feature in four individuals, together with other neurological signs.[10] These observations were consistent with another study in which approximately 61% of the recruited cases (11 patients out of 18) had dystonia, followed by pyramidal features in 44.4% (8 out of 18 patients) and visual impairment in 38.9% (7 out of 18 patients).[5] In our case, visual impairment was the primary presenting symptom, accompanied by migraine-like headaches.

Notably, two Saudi brothers with the same NDUFA12 variant c.4G>T (p.Glu2), as in our patient, were described in the same 2022 study. One of these two brothers exhibited visual impairment and focal dystonia, whereas the other, despite carrying identical variant, presented with spastic quadriplegia without visual symptoms.[5]

This case expands the phenotype of the NDUFA12 mutation and underscores the importance of paying attention to other nonneurological features when considering mitochondrial diseases, especially those that do not fit into a defined syndrome. Furthermore, it recommends the inclusion of NDUFA12 variants in the workup of unexplained visual impairment with optic atrophy.
