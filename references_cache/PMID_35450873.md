# Case of GPD1 deficiency causing hypertriglyceridaemia and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis.
**Authors:** Polchar L, Vallabhaneni P
**Journal:** BMJ Case Rep (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1136/bcr-2021-246369](https://doi.org/10.1136/bcr-2021-246369)

## Content

We report a case of a young girl of South Asian descent presented with faltering growth, hepatomegaly, hypertriglyceridaemia and raised transaminases. Subsequent ultrasound scans identified fatty infiltration in her liver, and a liver biopsy showed fibrosis and steatosis. The patient's serum triglycerides normalised without intervention by the age of 28 months. At age 6, whole-exome sequencing of the patient's genome identified novel homozygous variants in the glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 1 (GPD1) gene, GPD1 c.500G>A (p.Gly167Asp), leading to a diagnosis of GPD1 deficiency.

We report a case of a young girl of South Asian descent presented with faltering growth, hepatomegaly, hypertriglyceridaemia and raised transaminases. Subsequent ultrasound scans identified fatty infiltration in her liver, and a liver biopsy showed fibrosis and steatosis. The patient’s serum triglycerides normalised without intervention by the age of 28 months. At age 6, whole-exome sequencing of the patient’s genome identified novel homozygous variants in the glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 1 (GPD1) gene, GPD1 c.500G>A (p.Gly167Asp), leading to a diagnosis of GPD1 deficiency.

Keywords: Gastrointestinal system, Nutrition, Liver disease, Nonalcoholic steatosis

Glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 1 (GPD1) deficiency is a very rare diagnosis, and currently, fewer than 30 cases exist in the literature.1–7 The collaboration of multiple teams, in different medical specialities, over a prolonged period was required to reach this diagnosis, highlighting the importance of multidisciplinary teamwork.

The GPD1 protein catalyses the production of glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P) and NAD+ from dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) and NADH in a reversible redox reaction.1 Deficiency of this protein typically causes liver steatosis and fibrosis, hepatomegaly, elevated liver enzymes and infantile hypertriglyceridaemia.1 The first cases of GPD1 deficiency were reported in 2012,1 and most cases report the condition to be a benign one that does not require intervention,1–6 although one source reports the use of fenofibrate to reduce triglyceride levels.7 The GPD1 genetic variant present in this patient has not been reported in the literature before. This case report furthers the emerging body of knowledge about this disease.

A 23-month-old girl of South Asian descent presented to the hospital with gastroenteritis, dehydration and faltering growth due to feeding difficulties. Her weight on admission was 8.69 kg (0.4th-2nd centile). On admission, it was noted that the patient had new-onset hepatomegaly, palpable to 8 cm below the costal margin. The tip of her spleen was also palpable. She did not present with jaundice or any other peripheral stigmata of liver disease, her abdomen was soft and non-tender, and the patient did not have any dysmorphic features.

Her medical history was significant for faltering growth and global developmental delay; the patient had generally tracked on the second centile before her admission, and at a 15-month review, she had a developmental age of 6 months. Due to oligohydramnios, the patient was born at 37 weeks by planned caesarean section. She suffered from intrauterine growth restriction, and her birth weight was 2.32 kg. She was born with a Volkmann’s ischaemic contracture of her left forearm musculature. The patient’s parents were first cousins, and her mother and elder siblings had learning difficulties.

The patient’s blood tests on admission revealed raised liver enzymes: alanine transaminase (ALT), 98 IU/L (normal reference range <33 IU/L); aspartate transaminase (AST), 122 IU/L (normal reference range <32 IU/L); g-glutamyl transferase (GGT), 149 IU/L (normal reference range <44 IU/L); and raised triglycerides, 8.9 mmol/L (normal reference range <2 mmol/L). Albumin, prothrombin and glucose concentrations were within normal ranges. An ultrasound scan of the liver showed a greatly enlarged, echogenic liver without a focal abnormality during her admission. Previous investigations for the developmental delay had shown raised serum biotinidase concentrations.

A working diagnosis of glycogen storage disorder was considered in light of the patient’s presentation of a combination of faltering growth, hepatomegaly, hypertriglyceridaemia and raised biotinidase concentrations. Genetic analysis was undertaken, but no pathogenic variants suggestive of glycogen storage disorder were found. Normal female karyotype was also identified postgenetic analysis. Infective and neoplastic causes were also ruled out after further investigations.

With the input of metabolic specialists, further differential diagnoses were considered, and more investigations were performed. These included investigations to rule out galactosaemia and mucopolysaccharidosis. Transferrin glycoforms showed no congenital defects of glycosylation. A leucocyte enzyme panel was performed, which showed normal enzyme activity. Acylcarnitine measurement showed normal free carnitine and acylcarnitine concentrations. Serum lactate, serum amino acids, urine organic acids, glycosaminoglycans and homocysteine concentrations were normal. The patient also had a normal comparative genomic hybridisation array.

A liver biopsy was performed when the patient was 3 years old. This showed portoportal bridging fibrosis with partial nodularity, suggesting metabolic liver disease. In addition, mixed macrovesicular and microvesicular steatosis with mild activity and periportal copper-binding protein depositions were also noted. Further differential diagnoses of mitochondriopathy, citrin deficiency, fatty acid oxidation defect disorders and lysosomal acid lipase deficiency were ruled out. A cholestasis gene panel was performed to rule out progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis and other genetic disorders that can cause multisystem disease in light of the liver biopsy results. Genetic testing for Wilson’s disease was carried out, which showed no variant in the ATP7B gene.

A muscle biopsy was also taken, which did not show any apparent pathological abnormality or mitochondrial change but showed mild excessive type 1 fibres with one small group of slow-myosin positive fibres indicative of neurogenic change.

The progression of the patient’s liver disease remained stable. After her initially high serum AST, ALT and GGT concentrations at 23 months, they remained above the normal reference ranges but did show a general downward trend (values plotted in figures 1 and 2). Notably, however, her serum triglyceride concentrations, which were 8.9 mmol/L aged 23 months, dropped to 7.8 mmol/L a month later, and by the age of 2½ years, they measured 2.5 mmol/L. Subsequent ultrasound scans of the liver showed little progression from the patient’s initial scan, aged 23 months. The most recent scan when the patient was aged 6 showed a bright, enlarged liver consistent with fatty infiltration but with no focal abnormality.

At age 6, after her extensive testing, the patient and her parents underwent whole-exome sequencing as part of the ‘100 000 Genomes Project’. This identified homozygous variants in the GPD1 gene: GPD1 c.500G>A (p.Gly167Asp). The patient’s parents were heterozygous for this variant. As this gene variant had not been reported in the literature before, it was classified as a variant of uncertain significance. However, due to the link between GDP1 deficiency and liver disease, it was determined that this genetic variant was most likely to have caused the patient’s transient infantile hypertriglyceridaemia, fatty liver and hepatic fibrosis.

After the patient’s admission, aged 23 months, she continued with intermitted nasogastric feeding until2½ years of age. She was subsequently switched to nutritional supplement drinks. She continued to track on the second centile, although she has experienced an improvement in weight gain over the recent years. The patient’s developmental delay has also become less pronounced, mirroring the patient’s gain in weight. Her most recent review noted that she has been attending a mainstream school achieving regular grades. In addition, the patient’s Volkmann’s ischaemic contracture improved over time, and by the age of 4, her wrist movements were within normal range. The hepatomegaly settled by age 7.

At her most recent follow-up, aged 7, the patient has been in good health. No intervention has been required to date for the patient’s liver disease, with elevated liver enzymes and triglycerides resolving spontaneously. The latest monitoring ultrasound scan has not shown any progression of the initial fibrotic changes noted in earlier scans. However, it is planned for the patient to have a regular paediatric follow-up with annual ultrasound and fibrosis scans to monitor her liver disease progression.

The GPD1 gene is mapped on chromosome 12q12-q13 and encodes cytoplasmic NADH/NAD+-dependent GPD1, essential in the metabolism of both lipids and carbohydrates. GPD1 catalyses the production of G3P and NAD+ from DHAP and NADH in a reversible redox reaction (figure 3).1 However, the mechanistic role of GPD1 deficiency in hypertriglyceridaemia is currently unclear.5
GPD1 sequence variants were first found to be a cause of infantile hypertriglyceridaemia by Basel-Vanagaite et al. They identified 10 patients presenting with early-onset hepatomegaly, hypertriglyceridaemia, moderately raised transaminases, hepatic steatosis and hepatic fibrosis in the first year of life.1

In total, fewer than 30 cases of GPD1 deficiency exist in the literature,1–7 and the GDP1 gene variant reported in this patient is unique. The majority of the reported patients with GPD1 deficiency share the pathological features found by Basel-Vanagaite et al.1–7 However, the faltering growth described in this patient has not been as widely reported. The 10 patients reported by Tesarova et al all experienced ‘low-normal growth’,6 and one of the 10 patients described by Basel-Vanagaite et al had ‘poor weight gain’.1 However, no other cases in the current literature report difficulties with poor weight gain as marked as those described in this patient, which was severe to the extent that she required nasogastric feeding. One case of GPD1 deficiency contrasts with this one, as the patient in the report presented with obesity and insulin resistance.4

It appears that the disease course in GPD1 deficiency is non-progressive and relatively benign,5 although one case report describes a patient whose hypertriglyceridaemia persisted at the age of 30 years.3 In the patient presented in this report, her serum triglyceride concentrations dropped without intervention, as seen in the case reported by Jia-Qi et al.5 However, the patient reported by Matarazzo et al had persistent hypertriglyceridaemia until the age of 15, for which he was treated with fenofibrate.7 This effectively reduced his serum triglyceride concentrations.

The commonly shared features of glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase 1 (GPD1) deficiency are early-onset hepatomegaly, hypertriglyceridaemia, raised transaminases, hepatic steatosis and hepatic fibrosis. Therefore, GPD1 deficiency should be considered in a child or adolescent presenting with these features without a clear cause.

It is less clear what effect GPD1 deficiency has on weight gain. However, with the number of cases of GPD1 deficiency increasing in the literature, hopefully, more light will be shed on this in the future.

A multidisciplinary team was vital in reaching this diagnosis, including input from paediatric metabolic disorders, genetic specialists, paediatric hepatology who undertook the liver biopsy and the 100 000 Genomes Project. This case highlights the importance of multidisciplinary working when diagnosing a patient with a rare condition.
