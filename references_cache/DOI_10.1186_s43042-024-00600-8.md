# Molecular characterization of Ebola virus, immune response, and therapeutic challenges: a narrative review
**Authors:** Martin Ndayambaje, Callixte Yadufashije, Thierry Habyarimana, Theogene Niyonsaba, Hicham Wahnou, Patrick Gad Iradukunda, Cedrick Izere, Olivier Uwishema, Pacifique Ndishimye, Mounia Oudghiri
**Journal:** Egyptian Journal of Medical Human Genetics (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s43042-024-00600-8](https://doi.org/10.1186/s43042-024-00600-8)

## Content

AbstractThe Ebola virus (EBOV) remains a major public health challenge due to its complex structure and the lack of appropriate and effective vaccines and therapies. This review characterizes the Ebola virus, its immune response, and its therapeutic challenges. Structural EBOV proteins include the envelope glycoprotein, nucleoprotein, RNA polymerase L, and viral proteins VP30, VP24, VP35, and VP40. The proteins play a role in the virus’s pathogenesis by evading the host's immune response. The immune system evasion mechanisms of EBOV are critical in its pathogenesis. Some vaccines, such as the recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus-Zaire Ebola virus (RVSV-ZEBOV), have proven to be very effective and have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) additionally, four other vaccines have been approved including Gam Evac-Combi (licensed in Russia), ad5-EBOV (approved in China), Zabdeno and Mvabea (approved in Europe). However, some challenges remain in developing effective vaccines, such as the selection of immunogens, cross-protecting immunity, long-term protection, mechanism of protection, and rapid response vaccination. Despite the progress made, there is still a need for an effective vaccine that offers durable and broad protection against multiple strains of the Ebola virus. This will be achieved through the collaboration of various organizations and government and Non-Governmental Organization (NGO) agencies.

a highly contagious virus, with transmission occurring 
from infected humans or animals, and causes a mostly 
fatal illness known as Ebola virus disease (EVD). Firstly, 
the virus was discovered in 1976 during unconnected 
outbreaks in northern Zaire, the Democratic Republic 
of the Congo, and southern Sudan. Since its discovery,

Ebola virus species and transmission
Ebolavirus and Marburgvirus are hemorrhagic viruses 
that belong to the family of Filoviridae most likely 
descended from a common ancestor [4]. The EBOV is 
classified into six species and each species is associ -
ated with a particular outbreak. All ebolaviruses belong 
to the genus of ebolavirus and the family of Filoviridae. 
Four of the six species of ebolaviruses are known to cause 
hemorrhagic fever or EVD [3]. Ebola virus (EBOV, Zaire 
ebolavirus sp) was first identified in 1976, during an out -
break of Yambuku in the Democratic Republic of the 
Congo(DRC) [5]. In the same year during an epidemic in 
Nzara, Sudan, the Sudan virus (SUDV; sometimes known 
as the Sudan ebolavirus) was initially isolated [6]. Since 
then, other epidemics have been connected to EBOV 
and SUDV and were recognized as severe diseases and 
fatal illness [3]. After its introduction to a primate facil -
ity in Reston, Virginia, macaques (Macaca fascicularis) 
that consumed crabs from the Philippines were affected. 
Ten years later, the Reston virus (RESTV; also known as 
Reston ebolavirus) was identified. Remarkably, all other 
known filoviruses were found in Africa, except RESTV, 
which is the only virus with an Asian origin. Although 
RESTV may infect pigs and monkeys, no instances of the 
virus have been recorded in humans [3]. In 1994, an eth -
nologist who conducted an autopsy on a deceased chim -
panzee was found to have the Taï Forest virus (TAFV; 
also known as Taï Forest ebolavirus) that had hemor -
rhagic features, this is a single case identified since the 
first identification [7]. Following an outbreak in Bundibu-
gyo, Uganda, in 2007, the fifth member, the Bundibugyo 
virus (BDBV; sp: Bundibugyo ebolavirus), was given its 
retroactive name. Two minor outbreaks were connected 
to BDBV, and since 2012, no new cases have been docu -
mented [4]. In 2018, Mops condylurus, an Angolan free-
tailed bat, and Chaerephon pumilus, a tiny free-tailed 
bat, were the sources of the sixth ebolavirus species [8]. 
The Bombali ebolavirus (BOMV) was the name given 
to this new strain. It is the second species of ebolavirus 
after RESTV that is thought to not cause symptoms in 
humans, and it has not been connected to any outbreaks 
[3].
Most epidemics of EBOV may be traced back to a 
singular introduction of the virus into the human pop -
ulation as a result of zoonotic spillover events that origi -
nated from an unknown reservoir and were spread in 
unknown ways. After this first introduction, the virus 
spreads via direct human contact, which is mostly caused 
by non-aerosol routes, or by contact with infected tis -
sues, body fluids, or objects (fomites) that have been 
contaminated by infectious materials. The importance 
of human-to-human transmission channels in the spread 
and continuation of EBOV epidemics is highlighted by 
this mechanism [9]. According to historical records, 
EBOV may have only seldom caused illness in humans 
by being transferred from its natural reservoir host(s), 
however, a small number of EVD outbreaks were likely 
missed or not recorded. Governance, communications, 
isolation, infrastructure, health care, and international 
connection have all been taken into account when esti -
mating the likelihood of an index case being infected and 
then spreading both locally and worldwide [10]. These 
forecasts are essential for determining which areas need 
more funding and surveillance. It can be difficult to track 
EBOV in humans following a zoonotic transmission 
event, particularly since there isn’t a single known natural 
reservoir [10].
Factors that influence EBOV disease outbreaks
Environmental factors that can predispose to EBOV 
outbreaks include changes in land use, encroachment 
into forested areas, and hunting of wildlife, which can 
increase human contact with the natural reservoirs of 
the virus, such as fruit bats [3]. Immune suppression 
can indeed affect the severity and outcome of EBOV 
infection. A weakened immune system, whether due to 
preexisting conditions, malnourishment, stress, or con -
current infections, may not be able to mount an effective 
defense against the virus [11]. Additionally, immune sup -
pression can lead to an impaired response to the EBOV, 
potentially resulting in more severe disease outcomes or 
increased mortality rate [11]. It is important to note that 
the EBOV itself causes significant immune suppression 
by targeting immune cells and evading the host’s immune 
response [11].
The EBOV is a profoundly contagious and lethal patho-
gen responsible for inducing severe hemorrhagic fever in 
humans. The vulnerability of individuals to EBOV infec -
tion is affected by numerous host factors [12]. Several 
host factors can impact susceptibility to the EBOV and

Molecular characterization
The EBOV, a deadly pathogen that causes severe hem -
orrhagic fever in humans and non-human primates, 
encodes seven structural or multifunctional proteins in 
its genome. The genome of the EBOV consists of around 
19,000 nucleotides and encodes seven structural proteins 
(Fig.  2) [14]. These proteins include the envelope glyco -
protein, nucleoprotein, RNA polymerase L, and viral pro-
teins VP30, VP24, VP35, and VP40 [15].
Nucleoprotein (NP)
The configuration of the Ebola nucleoprotein is cru -
cial for understanding its role in the virus’s replication 
and pathogenesis. The nucleoprotein of the EBOV is 
an essential component of its structure and plays a cru -
cial role in viral replication and pathogenesis [16]. NP 
is responsible for binding and protecting the viral RNA 
genome, as well as regulating viral gene expression. It 
forms a complex with the viral RNA to form the nucle -
ocapsid, which is essential for the packaging of the viral 
genome during virus assembly. The nucleoprotein also 
interacts with other viral proteins, such as the VP30 and 
VP35 proteins, to facilitate viral transcription and rep -
lication [17]. Overall, the structure and function of the 
Ebola nucleoprotein are critical for viral replication, gene 
expression, and immune evasion. The nucleoprotein (NP) 
of the EBOV plays a crucial role in host–pathogen inter -
actions and the pathogenesis of the virus. NP is involved 
in the regulation of EBOV replication, including tran -
scription and capsid formation. In summary, NP plays a 
critical role in EBOV replication, host-cell interactions, 
and the pathogenesis of the virus [18].

VP35 protein
The VP35 protein of the EBOV plays a crucial role in 
the replication and pathogenesis of the virus. VP35 
protein is a multifunctional protein and is essen -
tial for the replication, transcription, and evasion of 
the host immune response [19]. The structure of the 
VP35 protein is composed of an N-terminal coiled-coil 
domain followed by a central basic patch, an unstruc -
tured linker region, and a C-terminal interferon inhibi -
tory domain. The N-terminal coiled-coil domain is 
involved in protein–protein interactions, which are 
vital for the function of VP35. Additionally, the cen -
tral basic patch of VP35 is responsible for binding to 
viral RNA, which is essential for viral replication. The 
structure of the VP35 protein allows it to interact with 
host factors and viral RNA, contributing to its role as 
an important virulence determinant that aids in evad -
ing the host immune response and promoting viral 
replication. VP35 is implicated in inhibiting the activa -
tion of IRF-3 and protein kinase R, serving as an RNA-
silencing suppressor to inhibit RNA interference, and 
inhibiting the type I interferon response, all of which 
are crucial for the virus’s ability to propagate and sur -
vive within the host environment [19]. Moreover, VP35 
is also involved in the suppression of innate immune 
responses, as it has been demonstrated to function as 
an IFN antagonist that inhibits type I IFN responses in 
EBOV-infected cells. VP35’s role in impairing nuclear 
accumulation of tyrosine-phosphorylated STAT1 is 
a critical mechanism for evading the antiviral effect. 
The multifunctional nature of VP35 is further high -
lighted by its involvement in the formation of the viral 
nucleocapsid, where it interacts with other viral pro -
teins, such as NP and L, to form the viral replication 
complex [20]. VP35 also acts as an RNA polymerase 
cofactor in the transcription and replication complex, 
playing a crucial role in viral RNA synthesis [21]. The 
VP35 protein’s multifunctional nature underscores its 
significance in the virus life cycle [22].
VP40 protein
The structure of the EBOV VP40 protein plays a pivotal 
role in its functional versatility throughout the viral life 
cycle. The crystal structure, biochemistry, and cellular 
microscopy have revealed that VP40 rearranges into dif -
ferent structures with distinct functions crucial for the 
ebolavirus life cycle [23]. The unaltered, unmodified, 
wild-type VP40 polypeptide can assemble into distinct 
structures, such as a dimeric precursor, a hexameric 
structural component, and a non-structural RNA-bind -
ing ring structure [24]. Each of these arrangements has 
been demonstrated to possess unique and indispensable 
functions within the EBOV life cycle.
The VP40 protein’s ability to assume different shapes to 
perform various functions provides insights into how the 
virus accomplishes a multi-step life cycle with a minimal 
number of genes [25]. The EBOV VP40 protein is primar-
ily known for its role in the budding of the virus from 
the plasma membrane [26]. The VP40 protein forms the 
viral matrix that provides structure to the virion and is 
crucial for the assembly and release of virus-like particles 
The EBOV VP40 protein, also known as the matrix pro -
tein, is crucial for viral assembly and release. The VP40 
protein also entraps the host ESCRT pathway through 
several modular domains, which increases the robust -
ness of the viral life cycle. Studies have shown that the 
VP40 protein interacts with multiple functionally related 
host proteins, demonstrating its ability to accommodate 
multiple functions and structural rearrangements dur -
ing the viral life cycle [25]. Additionally, the VP40 protein 
has been found to regulate viral transcription and plays 
a significant role in virion assembly. The conformational

Glycoprotein
GP on the surface of the virus
Ebola glycoproteins are essential components of the 
EBOV, playing a crucial role in its structure and function. 
They are located on the surface of the virus and consist of 
two subunits—the GP1 and GP2 subunits [27]. The GP1 
subunit is responsible for binding to host cells and initiat-
ing the process of viral entry. Upon binding to host cells, 
the GP1 subunit undergoes a conformational change, 
exposing the GP2 subunit [28]. This allows the virus to 
fuse with the host cell membrane and release its contents 
into the cell. This fusion process is facilitated by the high 
glycosylation of the Ebola glycoproteins, which not only 
aids in stabilizing the protein structure but also contrib -
utes to the viral evasion of the host immune response 
[28]. The Ebola glycoproteins also play a role in the for -
mation of the spike-like projections on the surface of the 
virus. These spikes, composed of trimers of the glyco -
proteins, are responsible for the attachment of the virus 
to host cells and play a crucial role in the virulence and 
pathogenicity of the EBOV [29].
Soluble secreted glycoprotein (sGP)
EBOV also produces a soluble glycoprotein known as 
soluble secreted glycoprotein (sGP) [30]. This soluble gly-
coprotein is released into the surrounding environment 
and has been found to have immunomodulatory effects. 
It has been suggested that sGP may help the virus evade 
the host immune response by inhibiting the produc -
tion and function of certain immune cells. Additionally, 
the Ebola glycoproteins have been a focus of research in 
vaccine development. Scientists have been studying the 
Ebola glycoproteins in hopes of using them as targets for 
vaccine development [31].
∆‑peptide
The ∆-peptide a non-structural derived from the Ebola 
glycoproteins is a potential peptide that has been identi -
fied as a target for vaccine development to prevent viral 
entry or its interaction with host cells [32]. The ∆-pep -
tide, derived from the Ebola glycoproteins, specifically 
targets the region responsible for mediating attachment 
and fusion of the virus to host cells [33].
Shed GP
Shed GP refers to the truncated glycoprotein that is 
secreted in large quantities during Ebola infection. This 
shed GP acts as an “antigen sink” or “antigen decoy” by 
absorbing specific antibodies and modulating the host 
immune response to promote immune evasion [34]. 
Additionally, it’s thought that shed GP lessens the cyto -
toxicity that GP causes to cells. Furthermore, shed GP 
stimulates macrophages and non-infected dendritic cells, 
which results in large-scale cytokine production and 
heightened vascular permeability [33].
Soluble small secreted glycoprotein
The soluble small secreted glycoprotein (ssGP) is another 
protein produced by the EBOV [35]. In addition to the 
membrane-bound glycoprotein, the EBOV also produces 
a soluble small secreted glycoprotein (GP). This soluble 
glycoprotein serves as a potential diagnostic marker for 
EBOV infection, as it is present in the bloodstream of 
infected individuals. The ssGP has been found to have 
immunomodulatory effects, potentially inhibiting the 
immune response and aiding in immune evasion [36].
VP30 protein
Ebola VP30 is a vital protein in the structure of the EBOV 
and plays a crucial role in its function [37]. VP30 is a 
transcription factor for the EBOV, meaning it is respon -
sible for regulating the initiation of viral transcription. 
In addition to its role in viral transcription, VP30 also 
has an essential function in viral replication and regula -
tion [38]. Suppression of VP30 from the EBOV genome 
leads to a replication-incompetent virus that is non-path-
ogenic in animal models. This highlights the importance 
of VP30 in the life cycle of the EBOV and its potential 
as a target for drug design to combat Ebola infections. 
VP30, the transcription factor for the EBOV, is a crucial 
protein in the structure of the virus [37]. It regulates the 
initiation of viral transcription and plays a vital role in 
viral replication and regulation [38]. Furthermore, VP30 
interacts with other proteins such as NP , VP35, and L, as 
well as viral RNA, to form the viral replication complex. 
This complex is necessary for efficient viral replication 
and transcription. VP30 is also highly phosphorylated 
and serves as a co-factor for transcription activation [32]. 
Overall, VP30 is a multifunctional protein that is essen -
tial for the replication and transcription of the EBOV.
VP24 protein
VP24 constitutes around 7.5% of the collective EBOV 
proteins and is identified as one of the five proteins 
engaged in the formation of EBOV nucleocapsid (NC).

tial for NC formation and the successful completion of

causes the NC to undergo advantageous conformational 
changes, VP24, VP35, and NP are in charge of packing 
NC into virions. Additionally, this structural alteration

methods to suppress the host immune response. Func -

viral life cycle of the EBOV such as modulation of the 
host immune response. Particularly, VP24 is implicated 
in the inhibition of interferon (IFN) responses which is 
crucial for the mechanism against viral infection [41]. It 
can also prevent the activation of NF-κB, which targets 
several downstream IFN-responsive genes [42]. By block-

demonstrated to be mediated by several other aa. Fur -
thermore, it was shown that oxidative stress influences 
the host response’s VP24 regulation, which helps infected 
cells recover from stress [32]. It observed that VP24 plays 
a key role in host immune evasion, which it does by pre -

ways. VP24 is implicated in budding viral initiation and 
appears to be essential for viral replication. As a result, 
focusing on these residues while developing vaccines may 
prevent viral replication.
L protein
L protein is the biggest, multi-subunit, multifunctional 
EBOV protein with 2212 aa L, which is a component of 
the RNP complex. L protein consists of five domains: First 
polyribonucleotide transferase (PRNTase) with tran -
scription/replication and polyadenylation activity; sec -
ond is the capping domain third is the connector domain 
with an organizational role; fourth methyltransferase 
domain with MTase activity; and fifth a small C-terminal 
domain [32]. In addition, a homooligomerization domain 
is present in EBOV L residues 1–450. L homooligomeri -
zation is not necessary for the non-competitive L-VP35 
interaction, which involves L proteins 1–380 [43]. The 
relocalization of L into viral inclusion bodies is made 
possible by the binding of L-VP35. The RESTV L pro -
tein was demonstrated to interact with VP30 in addition 
to VP35 [44]. The L protein of the EBOV is an essential 
part of its replication machinery. It functions as the viral 
polymerase, which is responsible for synthesizing viral 
RNA during the replication process. The L protein of the 
EBOV plays a critical role in the replication and propa -
gation of the virus. It catalyzes the viral RNA synthesis, 
allowing the virus to produce copies of its genome and 
propagate within infected cells. The L protein interacts 
with other viral proteins, such as NP , VP35, and VP30, to 
form the RNA synthesis machinery. To further confirm 
the importance of the L protein in RNA synthesis, viral 
mini-replicon assays were performed. In these assays, 
plasmids expressing the minimal EBOV protein compo -
nents for RNA synthesis were transfected into cells [17].
Host immune response to Ebola
Innate immune response
Innate immunity plays a crucial role in the initial defense 
against EBOV infection. Upon entry into the body, 
the EBOV interacts with various innate immune sys -
tem components. This interaction triggers a cascade of 
immune responses aimed at containing and eliminating 
the virus [45]. Innate immune cells, such as dendritic 
cells, macrophages, and monocytes, play a significant 
role in the response to EBOV infection. These cells are 
some of the first to encounter and be infected by the 
virus [45]. They contribute to the production of cytokines 
and chemokines that induce an antiviral state, activating 
the innate immune system (Fig.  3). Additionally, these 
cells are involved in the clearance of infected cells and 
debris, helping to control the spread of the virus [46]. 
Macrophages are crucial in the response to EBOV infec -
tion. They serve as early detectors of invading pathogens

not directly infected by the virus but undergo apoptosis, 
a form of programmed cell death. This can impair the 
immune response as NK cells are important for eliminat -

innate immune cells.
Adaptive immune mechanism
T cell activation
There is a growing body of research on T cell activation in 
the context of EBOV infection (Fig.  3), as scientists seek 
to uncover the mechanisms by which the immune sys -
tem responds to the virus and identify potential targets 
for therapeutic interventions [52]. In both fatal and sur -
vivor instances, multiparametric flow cytometry analysis 
has demonstrated a strong activation of Tc CD8 + T-cells 
followed by significant growth [52]. An average of 45% 
of CD8 + T cells were discovered to display the activa -
tion markers HLA-DR, Ki-67, and CD38. These sub -
sets included HLA-DR + /CD38 + , Ki-67 + /granzyme 
B + , and Ki-67 + /PD-1 + [53]. Sakabe et  al. [54] showed 
that memory CD8 + T cells secreted IFN-γ and TNF-α 
in about 80% of surviving cases. This was particularly 
evident when the cells were activated by proteins other 
than GP , such as EBOV nucleoprotein, virion protein 24 
(VP24), or VP40 [54]. Speranza and colleagues analyzed 
samples taken during the West African epidemic (2014–
2016) and found that survivors had higher levels of T 
-cell immunity transcripts (RANTES, CD40L, CD28, 
etc.) than fatal cases did. This finding confirmed the idea 
that survivors had a strong and sustained T cell response 
[55]. Nonetheless, compared to fatal instances, the sur -
vivors’ immune responses were more robust in terms 
of both quantity and variety. T cell fatigue may be the 
cause of T cell proliferation and its inability to adequately 
guard against EVD in lethal instances [56]. Furthermore, 
through the PD-1 inhibitory pathway, stimulation of the 
Ki-67 + /PD-1 + CD8 + T cell fraction appears to be the 
cause of a reduced adaptive immunological response [57]. 
The high viral titer and T cell fatigue may be explained 
by the oligoclonal response and increased expression of 
CTLA-4 and PD-1 in CD8 + and CD4 + T cells observed 
in the fatal patients. It is unclear if increased inflam -
mation and virus titers in fatal instances might lead to 
increased CTLA-4 and PD-1 expression in T cells [57].
Lymphocyte apoptosis
During the EBOV, there was a massive decreased of lym -
phocytes including CD4 + , CD8 + , and NK cells, and that 
reduction appeared in fatal cases [58]. Programmed cell 
death of NK cells is believed to involve the reduction in 
lymphocyte levels [59]. Most lymphocyte cells affected by 
Ebola include CD4 + , CD8 + and their level is reduced at 
one-fourth in fatal cases compared to the survivors [12]. 
The apoptosis of lymphocytes in EVD may be caused by 
the deregulation of interactions between dendritic cells 
(DCs) and the upregulation of apoptosis-related genes 
such as Fas, Fas Ligand (FasL), and tumor necrosis factor-
related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL), or by direct 
lysis by EBOV GP [12, 58, 60]. T cells play an important 
role in host survival during Ebola infection, as shown by 
their protective effect in mice and increased fatality rate 
in NHP models with complete T cell depletion [61, 62]. 
A similar result was shown utilizing a mice model lacking 
cytotoxic T cells, the experimental mice were dying upon 
experimental EBOV infection [63].
Ebola and antibodies production
While cell-mediated immunity is thought to be the pri -
mary means of defense against EBOV infection [64]. It 
has been proposed that the humoral immunity response, 
which includes the secretion of antibodies in conjunction 
with Tc activation (via the Fas/FasL or perforin pathway), 
is a reliable indicator of survival (Fig.  3) [12, 53]. IgM 
and IgG antibodies specific to EBOV are found between 
12–31 and 23–42  days after infection, respectively [53, 
65]. Since humoral response had little effect on the sus -
ceptibility to infection or recovery in mouse models, it is 
believed that humoral response is less important for EVD 
survival [63]. Furthermore, throughout the epidemic, 
there was no difference in the antibody titers of survivors 
and fatal cases [66]. Neutralizing antibodies (NAb) have

Mechanism of EBOV pathogenesis
Several studies demonstrated that the EBOV has vari -
ous mechanisms used to evade the host immune 
response such as viral proteins, VP24, VP3, and VP40, 
evasion of the immune system by the EBOV is indicated 
by the rapid multiplication of the virus in the host cells 
[69]. VP35 and VP24 may impede early IFN synthesis 
and signaling, which can compromise the host anti -
viral defense mechanism, innate response antagonist 
domains (IRADs) are the areas of VP35 and VP24 that 
shield the virus from host antiviral action [57].VP24 
can bind to specific proteins of the karyopherin alpha 
(KPNA) family implicated in the nuclear translocation, 
inhibiting the JAK-STAT pathway and IFN response 
(Fig.  4) [70].VP35 is known as a central contributor to 
host immune evasion through inhibition production 
type I IFN production [69].VP35 effectively shields 
dsRNA from detection during both stages of replica -
tion and transcription by capping dsRNA and prevent -
ing host sensors from identifying viral dsRNA, and the 
interferon inhibitory domain (IID) of VP35’s carboxy-
terminal domain is responsible for capping the ends of 
dsRNA, VP35-dsRNA contact, and VP35-PACT inter -
action, which protects dsRNA from host cellular sen -
sors (Fig.  5). Moreover, VP35 inhibits IRF-3 and IRF-7 
phosphorylation, which compromises IFN synthesis 
and RLR signaling [57]. Impaired VP35 suppresses 
innate immune response which leads to the DC matu -
ration, cell activation, and production of cytokines, 
this eventually suppresses the T cell response and 
might cause the adaptive immune system as a whole 
to fail [71, 72]. For anti-EBOV antibodies, secreted 
glycoprotein (sGP) serves as a ruse to stop them from 
interacting with viral glycoprotein. Glycosylation of 
sGP and GP1 facilitates viral attachment to host cells 
and inhibits immunological recognition, both of which 
aid in immune evasion. By steric blockage, EBOV GP 
obstructs the immune system’s ability to recognize 
HLA class I and II molecules. Additionally, EBOV GP 
inhibits viral budding stopping and immune response 
activation through its anti-tetherin function. By using

Ebola prevention measures
Therapeutic advancements
While still developing vaccines, some therapeutic strat -
egies have shown to be very effective in fighting against 
EBOV spreading, for example, EBOV entry inhibitors 
(such as Clarithromycin, posaconazole, and Amiloride) 
the endosomal antagonists are designed to disrupt EBOV 
trafficking, infection, and maturation (Fig.  6). Proteins 
like NPC1 receptors, VP35, VP40, VP20, VP24, GP pro -
teins, TIM-1, TIM-4, and Axl (a receptor tyrosine kinase) 
with clathrin-dependent endocytosis in the entry of the 
EBOV are targeted and inhibited [73, 74].
Another group is convalescent blood transfusion 
(Including ZMapp and MIL-77E cocktails) consisting of 
collecting antibodies (Monoclonal antibodies) and used 
as passive immunization as neutralizing antibodies from 
individuals suffering from EBOV [73]. The anticoagu -
lant therapy, specifically with the recombinant nematode 
anticoagulant protein c2 (rNAPc2) illustrated in Fig.  4, 
has received approval from the FDA as a stimulant for 
anticoagulation. This particular therapy is advisable for 
individuals post-exposure, due to its capacity to provide 
protection against organ failure related to thrombin [57]. 
Also, the use of interferons even though they result in 
some side effects they shown to be effective as they help 
in the elimination of EBOV from the bloodstream and 
stop the symptoms propagation [73].
Research on EBOV explains its escape mechanism by 
highlighting how it disrupts the host cell’s signaling path-
ways. EBOV and host gene expression inhibitors embody 
an alternative therapeutic strategy, aiming to target spe -
cific genes within the infected host cell. This strategy 
involves the disruption of the MAP-Kinase signaling 
pathway or interference with the viral genetic material. 
Approaches such as oligomer-mediated inhibition and 
small interfering RNA (siRNA), commonly utilized for 
gene expression regulation in cancer cells, can also be 
directed toward various EBOV genes. For instance, the 
use of siRNA to target the RNA polymerase L protein can 
impede microRNAs that play a role in viral infection [73, 
75].
As it was shown that EBOV enters the host through ion 
channels, drugs like amiodarone or verapamil or others 
are used to inhibit the viral entry as ion Channel inhibi -
tors, Fig.  4 and some drugs made for other diseases or 
repurposed drugs help in different mechanisms like the 
viral entry inhibition, replication inhibition or provoke 
immune responses. Some antiparasitic drugs like chloro -
quine, antibiotic drugs like Teicoplanin, antifungal drugs 
like Terconazole, psychoactive drugs like chlorproma -
zine imipramine, and antidepressant drugs like Sertra -
line shown to be helpful during EBOV infection [73]. 
Recently therapeutic modalities have been described by 
El Ayoubi et  al., 2024. Novel anti-EBOV medications, 
such as combination therapy like toremifene-mefloquine-
posaconazole, show promise in addressing viral repli -
cation by targeting it through GP inhibitors and host 
factors. AAK1 inhibitors, broad-spectrum mAbs, and 
bispecific antibodies against EBOV’s GP and NPC-1 are 
examples of advancements. Preclinical and clinical exper-
iments demonstrate the potential for these strategies in 
the fight against Ebola [76].

To curb the spread of the EBOV public health measures 
toward prevention are essential such as enhancement of 
the surveillance for early case detection, hand hygiene, 
and safe burial practice, and promoting education and 
awareness initiatives to disseminate correct information 
about the EBOV disease. Additionally, establish travel 
restrictions and isolation measures to limit the move -
ment from infected areas. Decentralization of screening 
measures mainly at the airport and borders and strength-
ening healthcare infrastructure [86].

Future Ebola research
EBOV research has made significant progress in recent 
years, with advances in understanding the virus’s patho -
genesis, transmission, and potential treatment options. 
However, there are still many important areas that 
require further exploration to better prepare for future 
outbreaks and improve clinical management. One crucial 
future direction in Ebola research is the development of 
effective vaccines that can provide long-term protection 
against the virus [78]. While progress has been made in 
this area, there is still a need for improved vaccine can -
didates that offer durable and broad protection against 
multiple strains of the EBOV. Another area that requires 
attention in Ebola research is the development of antivi -
ral drugs with potent activity against the virus [87]. These 
drugs can be used both for the treatment of infected indi-
viduals and as a prophylactic measure for those at high 
risk of exposure [88]. Additionally, further research is 
needed to better understand the immune response to 
EBOV infection and the factors that contribute to either 
successful clearance of the virus or progression to severe 
disease [89]. Furthermore, there is a need to study the 
potential long-term effects of Ebola infection on survi -
vors, as well as the risk of viral persistence and relapse 
[89]. Another important avenue for future research is the 
study of viral reservoirs and animal hosts to better under-
stand the natural ecology of the EBOV. This knowledge is 
essential for predicting and preventing spillover events in 
human populations [90].
Role of NGOs and international agencies 
collaboration and preparedness
Collaboration of Non-Governmental organizations and 
international agencies is the key step for Ebola preven -
tion, commonly known are WHO, CDC, and Medecin 
sans frontiers. The collaboration of these agencies 
enhances coordination among research institutions, gov -
ernments, and pharmaceutical companies to promote 
equitable access to treatment and vaccines in countries 
with limited infrastructures. Moreover, these organiza -
tions are contributing to raising technical standards, 
logistic support, and providing community education 
and awareness programs and funding [91].
Conclusion
Various studies have been conducted on the EBOV and 
provide an understanding of its dynamic, immunological 
response, and implication of innate and adaptive immu -
nity during infection and immune evasion viral proteins 
play a key role in immune response evasion. While the 
development and approval of vaccines offer signifi -
cant progress, ensuring equitable access and address -
ing vaccine hesitancy remain paramount. Furthermore, 
ongoing surveillance and adaptive strategies are essential 
to respond to the evolving nature of the EBOV. Inter -
national collaboration is crucial to navigate the chal -
lenges posed by EVD and to promote global response 
capabilities.
