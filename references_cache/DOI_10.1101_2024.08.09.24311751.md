# A Literature Review and Pooled Case Analysis of Cardiofaciocutaneous Syndrome to Estimate Cancer Risk
**Authors:** Jazmyn Bess, Toniya Brown, Sonia Bhala, Anaqa Faizer, Muzzammil Ahmadzada, Alicia A Livinski, Alex Pemov, Jung Kim, Philip S Rosenberg, Gina M Ney, Douglas R Stewart
**DOI:** [10.1101/2024.08.09.24311751](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.08.09.24311751)

Abstract

Background

                    Cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome (CFC) is a rare disorder with multiple congenital anomalies including macrocephaly, failure to thrive, and neurocognitive delay. CFC is part “RASopathy” syndromes caused by pathogenic germline variants in
                    BRAF, KRAS, MAP2K1,
                    and
                    MAP2K2.
                    To estimate cancer risk in CFC we conducted a systematic review using case reports and series.
                  


Methods
We reviewed articles and abstracted CFC cases to form a retrospective cohort based on PRISMA guidelines. Genotype-pphenotype (cancer) correlations, standardized incidence ratios (SIR), cumulative incidence and cause-specific hazard rates for cancer and cancer-free in CFC were calculated.


Results

                    This study includes 198 publications reporting 690 patients. Only 1.6% (11) had cancer, including acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). Six cancer patients harbored pathogenic variants within
                    BRAF, MAP2K1
                    , and
                    MAP2K2
                    . Cumulative incidence by age 10 was 5% for cancer or cancer-free death. Hazard Ratio (death) was 1-2% until age 3 and declined thereafter. Significant SIRs were found for all sites (SIR=4.96) and ALL (SIR=24.23).
                  


Conclusions
This is the largest investigation of cancer in CFC to date. Cancer risk in the CFC population is elevated but appears limited to earlier childhood. Modest case and cancer numbers could pose limitations to accurately assess cancer risk in CFC and more studies are needed.

Introduction 66 
Cardiofaciocutaneous syndrome (CFC) is a rare disorder first described in 1986 and is 67 
characterized by multiple congenital anomalies including heart defects, distinctive craniofacial 68 
anomalies (including macrocephaly and bi-temporal narrowing) , and cutaneous abnormalities. 69 
Most individuals also have some form of intellectual disability.1,2 The overall prevalence of CFC 70 
was last reported in 2012 as 1:810,000,3 but current RASopathy studies are seeking to better 71 
define and update this prevalence.4 CFC arises due to a pathogenic germline variant in BRAF, 72 
KRAS, MAP2K1, or MAP2K2, and is part of a larger group of syndromes termed RASopathies.  73 
RASopathies are a set of clinical syndromes with phenotypic overlap which result from 74 
pathogenic variants in genes involved in the RAS/Mitogen Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) 75 
pathway signaling.5 76 
 77 
The RAS/MAPK pathway is integral to normal development and is involved in nearly every 78 
aspect of a cell's activity. Dysregulation of this pathway, therefore, is critical to disease 79 
development. Illustratively, somatic variants in RAS/MAPK genes result in cellular dysfunction 80 
and are among the most common oncogenes in all human cancer.5 It is therefore plausible that 81 
individuals with pathogenic germline variants in a RAS/MAPK gene, such as individuals with 82 
CFC, are also at increased risk of cancer.6-12 Thus, estimation of cancer risk is integral to 83 
understanding disease processes and risk management for this population.2 However, due to the 84 
rarity of many of the RASopathies, genotype-cancer correlates have been difficult to calculate 85 
and interpret.  86 
 87 
A previous literature review was conducted on 226 reported CFC cases with calculations of 88 
cumulative incidence and hazard rates of cancer and cancer-free death in 2011.12 Because genetic 89 
testing is more readily available and technologies have improved since previous report, we 90 
sought to update and more accurately quantify cancer risk and incidence in CFC in this 91 
systematic literature review and pooled case analysis. This was completed by creating a 92 
retrospective cohort of CFC cases through systematic evidence review (SER) of the pertinent 93 
global biomedical literature. These cases were analyzed by determining the following: 1) a 94 
description of the types and frequencies of cancer; 2) a list of BRAF, KRAS, MAP2K1 and 95 
MAP2K2 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants detected in patients when available; 3) genotype-96

Materials and Methods 101 
Systematic Literature Review 102 
We followed the PRISMA system, including the checklist and flowchart. The review was 103 
registered using PROSPERO under the identification tag CRD42023405823 104 
(https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/display_record.php?RecordID=405823) 105 
A biomedical librarian from the National Institutes of Health library assisted with identifying 106 
articles from Embase, LILACS, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases between 1986 107 
and Feb 2023. Although CFC was first named and identified in 1986, the timeline was extended 108 
to include any potential retroactively identified cases included in literature. The diagnostic 109 
criterion set by Reynolds and co-authors;1 was used to include/exclude any cases identified. We 110 
searched published qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods studies, however “grey 111 
literature” (i.e. dissertations and conference abstracts/proceedings) was not included 112 
(Supplementary Figure 1). Information regarding databases, search strategies, study selection, 113 
details of full text review, data extraction, risk of bias,13,14, and statistical analysis is shown  in 114 
the Supplementary Methods. 115

or without cancer phenotype from a study. Type of study designs included were case reports, 120 
case series, and cohort studies. A total of 22 studies in languages other than English were 121 
excluded. Title and abstract screening as well as full text review were done using the Covidence 122 
systematic review software tool (https://www.covidence.org/) (Veritas Health Innovation, 123 
Melbourne, Australia). A pilot of both screening steps was conducted with all reviewers on a 124 
sample of the same 15 records. Title and abstract screening were performed by four reviewers 125 
independently (TB, JB, SB, MA). Full text review was proceeded by four reviewers for articles 126

Statistical Analyses 164 
The total number of cases available for qualitative analyses was 690. A subset of cases was used 165 
for sensitivity analysis that included only tier 1 criteria (pathogenic/likely variants). Cumulative 166 
incidence curves and cause-specific hazards were calculated, for overall cohort and for 167 
sensitivity analysis, to examine competing risks for cancer and death with outcomes of interest 168 
being cancer or death12.  169 
Genotype-phenotype (cancer) associations were not calculated for the overall cohort due to an 170 
insufficient number of cases for analysis. However, we sought to assess genotype-phenotype 171 
(cancer) correlations found in the cohort by reporting frequency and percentages of cancers and 172 
variants associated with cancer types.  173 
Standard incidence ratio calculations were performed using SAS v9.4. Observed cancer 174 
incidence and person-time at risk were derived from literature reports, assuming that each person 175 
was at risk from birth until publication date and including all reported cancers excluding non-176 
melanoma skin cancer (which is not available in the expected rates). Population cancer incidence 177 
rates were created using SEER*Stat 8.4.3 based on cancer registry data from 8 registries for 178 
1975-2020.20 Expected values for the calculations were calculated as a time-dependent weighted 179 
sum of person-time and population cancer incidence rates, stratified by sex, attained age, and 180 
attained calendar year. 181

Incidence and cause-specific hazards 224 
 225 
We aimed to understand the cumulative incidence for competing risks for cancer and death prior 226 
to cancer onset within the CFC cohort. There was a cumulative incidence, after accounting for 227 
competing risk, of 3% (95% CI:1.1-5.5) for cancer and 2% (95% CI:0.4-3.7) for death prior to 228 
cancer by age 10 (Figure 3-A). Therefore, 5% of CFC patients developed cancer or died by 10 229 
years of age. The cause-specific hazard of death was 1-2% until 3 years of age and declined 230 
thereafter (Figure 3-B).  231 
We performed a sensitivity analysis for competing risk of cancer and death prior to cancer using 232 
tier 1 data only (P/LP variants) on patients with a reported age (n=258). The estimated 233 
cumulative incidence and cause-specific hazard was similar despite only using the subset with 234 
molecular data (Supplementary Figure 2). To estimate the cancer risk associated with CFC, we 235 
calculated SIR using the six of eleven cancers that included demographic data across 4,913 236 
person-years (Table 3). All sites (SIR=4.96) and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (SIR=24.23) 237 
were significantly higher than in the general population (p<0.05). Incidence rates were stratified 238 
by attained age (0-4, 5-9,…,85+). For SIR calculations, the observed time in the cohort was 239 
matched with the appropriate attained age category to calculate expected cancers. Person-year 240 
contribution was based on when a person entered and exited. To calculate overall SIR, a sum of 241 
all the component expected cancer values were obtained to determine the overall number of 242 
expected cancers. 243

Discussion 245 
This study enhances and confirms previous analyses through a comprehensive SER by creating a 246 
retrospective cohort of individuals with CFC. Our global literature cohort is currently the largest 247 
to date including 690 individuals with a clinical and/or molecular diagnosis of CFC. Despite the 248 
rarity of CFC, we were able to collect a substantial amount of CFC cases from a total of 578 case 249 
series (3 were cancers) and 112 case reports (8 were cancers). Although this is a 33% increase in 250 
case numbers from the previous systematic review in 2011, cancer cases remained similar in 251 
proportion.12 Since the previous systematic review, new cancer cases have been reported, 252 
including disseminated oligodendrogliallike leptomeningeal tumor 21and pediatric ALL.22-24  253 
Exploratory analyses and descriptive statistics were conducted to understand trends in the data, 254 
finding common pediatric cancers, including ALL, most frequent in the CFC cohort. Previously 255 
reported CFC cases with cancer often provided molecular diagnoses information6-10, but four 256 
new cancer cases reported since the previous systematic review had no molecular diagnosis 257 
information available.21-24 . Nonetheless, we sought to understand genotype-phenotype (cancer) 258 
correlations by examining the landscape of reported variants and correlated cancers. Six of the 259 
11 cancers reported pathogenic variants found in the CFC cohort including MAP2K2 260 
p.Pro128Gln; BRAF p.Gly469Glu; and MAP2K1p.Tyr130Cys, reported in ALL, non-Hodgkin’s 261 
lymphoma, and hepatoblastoma, respectively. This molecular information is critical and can 262 
provide medical providers with an insightful clinical application tool for improved cancer risk 263 
assessment and counseling for families impacted by CFC.  In addition to confirming a low 264 
cumulative incidence found in the previous study,12 we captured significant SIRs for all sites and 265 
ALL compared to the general population. These results suggest that cancer risk is elevated in this 266 
population, however, may be limited to early childhood. 267 
 268 
A major limitation in this study was sparseness of data, including a modest number of cancer 269 
cases, molecular variant information, and demographics. Insufficient and/or missing data 270 
reduced our ability and statistical power to perform more in-depth analyses of genotype-271 
phenotype associations, survival rates, and stratification by age, race/ethnicity, and other 272 
demographic factors. The reliance on published reports also introduced publication bias 273 
including possible duplicate cases, missing cases, and possible enrichment of cancer cases versus 274

Our review highlights the need for further studies in this rare population to be conducted and 282 
capture the most up-to-date information regarding CFC prevalence and cancer risk. Currently, 283 
RASopathy studies are being conducted through NCI-sponsored intramural and extramural 284 
collaborations seeking to understand cancer risk throughout the lifetime of individuals with CFC.  285 
These studies will impact screening recommendations for cancer and, perhaps, better define what 286 
genotypes are most commonly associated with cancer.4 Together, they may improve diagnostics, 287 
clinical care management, screening guidelines, targeted therapies, and risk support for patients 288 
with RASopathies including CFC within and beyond childhood developmental stages of life.  289
