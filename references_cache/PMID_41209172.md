Membranous nephropathy (MN) is a leading cause of nephrotic syndrome in adults.
In most cases, its etiology is unknown (idiopathic), while ∼30% of cases are
secondary to other diseases, infections, or exposure to drugs and toxic agents.
Up to 70% of patients with idiopathic MN have circulating antibodies against the
M-type phospholipase A2 receptor, a podocyte surface protein. The formation of
immune complexes on podocytes triggers complement activation, leading to the
activation of both the C5b-C9 complex and C5a. Recently, several novel podocyte
antigens have been identified in both idiopathic and secondary MN. Autophagy, a
cellular mechanism that removes and recycles damaged cytoplasmic material, may
have a dual role in MN pathogenesis. It can regulate innate and adaptive immune
responses, promoting the production of autoantibodies against podocyte antigens.
However, autophagy also protects podocytes from apoptosis and injury. In
addition, it plays a role in the progression of tubular atrophy and interstitial
fibrosis, which can lead to chronic kidney disease and kidney failure. This
article explores the mechanisms that activate and regulate macroautophagy and
examines its role in MN. The potential therapeutic implications of autophagy
regulation in MN, based on preliminary studies, are also discussed.

Membranous nephropathy (MN) is a glomerular disease which is usually associated with nephrotic syndrome (NS). Histologically, it is characterized by uniform thickening of the glomerular capillary due to the presence of immunoglobulin containing deposits on the outer or subepithelial aspect of the glomerular basement membrane. In most cases the etiology is unknown, and the disease is called idiopathic MN. However, it is possible that in civilized countries pollutants, such as fine particulate matter (PM) PM2.5 - PM10 or nitrogen oxide,1, 2, 3 may play an etiological role in genetically predisposed individuals.4 In about 30% of cases MN may be associated with other autoimmune diseases (ie systemic lupus erythematosus), infections (viral hepatitis), malignancies (carcinoma), or exposure to drugs (lithium, anti-inflammatory drugs etc) or cytotoxic drugs (heavy metals, hydrocarbons). The outcome of MN is variable. Some patients may enter a spontaneous remission of proteinuria; others may slowly progress to end-stage kidney disease or die from NS-related complications.5,6 Although several studies have attempted to define the percentages of patients with different outcomes, the results are difficult to interpret because they largely depend on variations in treatment methods, patient selection criteria, and definitions of remission and relapse. Patients with severe and un-remitting NS may also suffer from disabling and even life-threatening extrarenal complications, such as thromboembolic disease and cardiovascular events.7

Much of our understanding of the pathogenesis of human MN comes from Heymann models of experimental glomerulonephritis in rats. However, despite extensive investigations, the search for potential antigen(s) remained elusive for many years. In 2009, Beck et al8 reported that up to 70% of adults with idiopathic MN exhibited circulating IgG4 antibodies against M-type phospholipase A(2) receptor (PLA(2)R) expressed on podocyte surface.

Other reports confirmed the presence of anti-PLA2R antibodies in 65%-80% of patients with MN and outlined also the prognostic role of anti-PLA2R antibodies.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 However, in the recent past, a score of new antigens have been discovered in 2%-10% of patients with MN, most of them being localized on podocyte surface.16,17 It has been confirmed that the immune-complex formation in subepithelial space activates the complement cascade, mainly through the lectin pathway,18 although alternative and classic pathways may also be involved.19,20 The final formation of the membrane attack complex C5b-C9 eventually leads to podocyte effacement, disruption of the actin cytoskeleton, and proteinuria. The inflammatory stimulators C3a and C5a may also be implicated in the complement-mediated damage in MN.21

In recent years, attention has been addressed to the possible involvement of autophagy in the pathophysiology of MN.

Autophagy is a complex cellular process that removes and recycles dysfunctional cytoplasmic material. It occurs in 3 main forms: macroautophagy, microautophagy, and chaperone-mediated autophagy. However, the term autophagy typically refers to macroautophagy. In response to endogenous or exogenous stimuli, a double membrane structure called a phagophore sequesters unwanted material (cargo), elongates, and forms spherical vesicles known as autophagosomes. These autophagosomes then fuse with lysosomes, forming autolysosomes, where the cargo is degraded by hydrolytic enzymes and recycled. The resulting macromolecules are released back and recycled in the cytosol for reuse. Autophagy is regulated by 2 kinases: the adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR).22

The AMPK stimulates autophagy by phosphorylating Unc-51-like kinase 1 (ULK1), which is part of a protein complex containing the focal adhesion kinase family-interacting protein of 200 kD protein and autophagy-related proteins encoded by genes (Atg). The ULK1 complex phosphorylates and activates the phosphathydilinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) complex composed by Beclin 1, the enzymes vacuolar protein sorting 34 and VPS15, and Atg14L. Once activated, PI3K generates the phosphatidylinositol-3 phosphate, which is essential for autophagy nucleation of the autophagosome, activation of downstream autophagy components and other membrane trafficking processes.23 The coordinated phosphorylation is critical for regulating autophagy induction.24 Multiple mechanisms may modulate autophagosome elongation, the phase of the protein synthesis pathway. Among several Atg-related proteins, the C-terminal glycine of light-chain protein 3-I (LC3-I) is modified by the addition of phosphatidylethanolamine to generate LC3-II, which is recruited to autophagosomal double membranes. Once the autophagosome closes, it fuses with the lysosome, where LC3-II is eventually degraded.25 Thus, detecting LC3 by immunoblotting can allow to monitor autophagic activity. P62, a classic autophagy receptor, can bind to LC3 promoting autophagy and degradation of ubiquitin proteins.26 Because p62 accumulates when autophagy is inhibited, and decreased levels can be observed when autophagy is induced, p62 may be used as a marker to study autophagic flux.27 Some members of the phosphatidylinositol family may act as fine tuners and regulators in autophagosome formation. Rab proteins are small guanine triphosphatases that act as key regulators of intracellular membrane trafficking and fusion. Of critical relevance is the presence of phosphatidylcholine in the autophagosome membrane to orchestrate the fusion of the autophagosome.28 In most cases, the construction of the double-membrane vesicles (autophagosomes) is formed through the canonical action of several Atg that are recruited to the site of autophagosome biogenesis. The lysosome is the destination for degradation of the intracellular components (endocytic cargo) sequestered by the autophagosome. Fusion of the autophagosome with the lysosome depends on the enzyme Rab7 and several adaptor proteins.29 The lysosome supplies acid hydrolases that break down the inner membrane from the autophagosome and degrade the cargo. The mTOR is a subunit of 2 molecular complexes: mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1), also known as the raptor complex, and mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2), also called the rictor complex. mTORC1 consists of a serine-threonine kinase, the regulatory protein GTPase (Rheb), and other associated proteins. mTORC1 complex regulates cell growth (size), proliferation, translation, and autophagy, while mTORC2 controls the actin cytoskeleton and apoptosis.30 The mTOR complex inhibits autophagy by preventing the activation of ULK1,24 which plays a pivotal role in ensuring the precise functionality of cellular response mechanisms. There is a reciprocal inhibition between AMPK and mTOR: AMPK downregulates mTOR, whereas mTOR inhibits AMPK. This regulation is essential to maintain AMPK in an inactive state under normal physiological conditions. Moreover, mTORC1 may directly control autophagosome formation via phosphorylation and degradation of WIPI2 (WD repeat domain, phosphoinositide-interacting 2), a critical protein in isolation membrane growth and elongation.31 Reactive oxygen species can regulate autophagy through both mTOR-dependent and independent mechanisms.

The autophagic machinery can also be involved in various processes that do not imply the entire repertoire of Atg or lysosomal delivery of cytosolic constituents. These functions are defined as noncanonical autophagy. For example, macroautophagic machinery can also occur in the absence of some of these key autophagy proteins. In noncanonical autophagy LC3 is directly conjugated on the membrane of phagosomes by elements of autophagy. LC3-associated phagocytosis and LC3-associated endocytosis have been found to be implicated in the regulation of multiple processes, including immunity and inflammation.32 Lipid droplets can also play a crucial role in forming autophagosomes, possibly by providing lipid moieties to proteins (lipidation). It has been demonstrated that lipidated forms of LC3 (LC3II) can concur to the formation of mammalian autophagosomes in different diseases.33

Autophagic flux may play a dual role in the pathogenesis of MN. On the contrary, autophagy regulates signals that activate both innate and adaptive immunity, and increased autophagic activity can promote the production of autoantibodies against podocyte antigens. On the contrary, autophagy helps protect podocytes from apoptosis and injury, meaning that impaired autophagy may have deleterious effects on podocyte morphology and function.

Substantial evidence has accumulated indicating that the pattern recognition receptors of the innate immunity recognize not only pathogen-associated molecular patterns but also danger associated molecular pattern, including masked hidden antigens. Autophagy has a critical role in this phase, since it may deliver danger associated molecular pattern to toll-like receptors, NOD-like receptors, and inflammasomes.34,35 The pattern recognition receptors promote a signal, amplified by adapter molecules (MyD88, Tirap, Trif, Tram) and autophagy proteins (Atg16). This signal is transmitted by a chain of kinases (IRAK1, IRAK4, TBK1, and IKK) to inflammatory transcription factors like NFκB, activator Protein-1, and Interferon-3 that regulate the inflammatory response.36,37 In the inflammatory microenvironment, dendritic cells mature and migrate to lymph nodes, where they present the intercepted podocyte protein as an antigen to T cells equipped with specific receptors. These antigen-presenting cells require a second signal to activate T cells. This costimulatory signal involves B7 molecules (CD80 and CD86) on antigen-presenting cells and their receptor on T cells, CD28. Calcineurin, a phosphatase complex, dephosphorylates the nuclear factor of activated T cells family of proteins, enabling their translocation into the nucleus. There, nuclear factor of activated T cells contributes to the synthesis of IL-2, which binds to its receptor and, along with IL-15, activates Janus kinase 3 (JAK3). This activation triggers growth signals through PI3K and protein kinase B (Akt). A key regulator role in protein translation is played by mTOR. Several interleukines (ILs) generate different types of T helper cells: Th1 (IL-12), Th17 (IL-17), or Th2 (IL-4). The collaboration between T and B cells activates B cells to produce plasmablasts and antibodies.38 By activating innate and adaptive immunity, autophagy can enhance the production of autoantibodies directed against the podocyte targets.

Normally, podocytes have a high level of autophagy that can protect from injury.39 In podocytes of patients with MN, the complex C5b-C9 can upregulate the number of LC3-positive puncta and the expression of p62/SQSTM1, the protein Sequestosome 1, a classical selective autophagy receptor that can also have roles in the ubiquitin-proteasome system.40 These findings indicate that the formation of autophagosomes is unable to degrade lysosomes. The complex C5b-9 can abrogate the autophagic pathway by increasing lysosomal membrane permeabilization, reducing acidification, and impairing the lysosome degradation.41 It has been demonstrated that LC3-positive puncta become larger in cells even when LC3-II is not formed. This phenomenon is due to LC3-I and p62 accumulation when autophagy is impaired.42 Experimental studies showed that defective autophagy can increase the susceptibility of podocytes to injury, leading to podocytopenia, foot effacement, and increased expression of podocyte proteins.43, 44, 45 Defective autophagy in genetically predisposed individuals may make podocytes more vulnerable to exogenous and endogenous factors, which could explain why multiple podocyte antigens, beyond PLA2R, are expressed in MN. Of note, MN patients with nephrotic proteinuria have higher level of serum PLA2-IB (a secretory group B of PLA2R) and fewer autophagosomes than those with nonnephrotic-range proteinuria.46 These data show that serum PLA2-IB can downregulate autophagy in podocytes and promote podocyte injury caused by PLA2R autoantibodies. Abrogating PLA2-IB-induced dysregulated autophagy resulted in inhibition of mTOR/ULK1, suggesting that autophagy is inhibited by activation of the mTOR/ULK1 signaling pathway.47 These data indicate that autophagy inactivation is one of the important mechanisms underlying the podocyte injury in idiopathic MN and demonstrate that the complex C5b-9 can trigger podocyte injury, at least partially by inhibiting the lysosomal-dependent autophagic pathway. On the contrary, it has been reported that in MN podocytes there is an increased expression of C3a receptor associated with a downregulation of synaptopodin, an actin-associated protein playing a protective role in injured podocytes.48 It has been demonstrated that complement receptors C3aR/C5aR expressed on dendritic cells can suppress mitophagy (an autophagic mechanism that degrades damaged mitochondria) and promote dendritic cell survival.49 These data suggest that C3a anaphylatoxin has a crucial role in complement-mediated podocyte damage, and the C3aR antagonist may be a potentially viable treatment for MN.50 One may wonder why so many podocyte antigens are involved in MN. Many of the novel autoantigens in MN are normally not expressed. It is possible to speculate that in the presence of defective autophagy, some intracellular podocyte proteins are more exposed to injury with consequent overexpression of different podocyte antigens triggering autoantibody production. Accumulation of autophagic vacuoles in podocytes of patients with idiopathic MN might be an indirect proof of this mechanism.41

Autophagy is also involved in the pathogenesis of tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis. In normal conditions, proteins that pass the glomerular barrier51 are reabsorbed into proximal tubular cells by a multireceptor complex formed by megalin, cubilin, amnionless, and Dab2.52 After endocytosis, autophagy may protect tubular epithelial cells by degrading and eliminating unwanted proteins.53 However, in NS the large amount of filtered proteins exceeds both the proximal tubular uptake and autophagic degradation capacities, resulting in proteinuria and tubular damage.54,55 Defective autophagy can increase apoptosis leading to tubular atrophy,56 and promote the release of transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1), which can exert different functions by promoting interstitial fibrosis or inducing protective autophagy.57 However, TGF-β1 may also collaborate with C3a and the C3a receptor in promoting epithelial–mesenchymal transition.58

Preliminary studies showed that regulators of autophagic flux may play a role in the treatment of MN. Promotion of autophagy by treatment with rapamycin protected podocytes from sublytic complement attack-induced injury in vitro.59 Pharmacological inhibition of PI3k/AKT pathway in cationic bovine serum albumin-induced MN could activate renal autophagy and reduce cell proliferation and inflammation of MN.60 In rats with passive Heymann nephritis, curcumin, an anti-inflammatory agent, could significantly alleviate the development of MN by inducing autophagy, as demonstrated by the increased number of podocytes and autophagic vacuoles compared with the passive Heymann nephritis control rats.61 The sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor canagliflozin could restore autophagy in podocytes of rats with MN. Co-culture of B-cells derived from MN rats triggered the phosphorylation of mTOR and ULK1 in podocytes, inhibited podocyte autophagy, and resulted in podocyte injury. B-cells derived from canagliflozin-treated rats restored autophagy and reversed the imbalance in Th1/Th2 cells in MN rats.62 Metformin can induce autophagy by interfering with different signal pathways.63 Female Sprague-Dawley rats with cationic bovine serum albumin-induced MN were randomly assigned to 4 groups: metformin, rapamycin, metformin + rapamycin, and a control group. The combination of metformin and rapamycin significantly reduced urinary protein excretion and mitigated inflammation, renal fibrosis, and podocyte foot process fusion. These findings were associated with an increased autophagic flux in podocytes as demonstrated by the enhanced expression of LC3-II/I and autophagosomes in podocytes and decreased mTOR expression.64 Given that podocyte autophagy serves as a crucial protective mechanism and its impairment contributes to the progression of podocytopathies such as minimal change disease and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, targeting autophagy with specific regulators represents a promising therapeutic strategy in these conditions as well.65,66

Autophagy is a dynamic process that regulates cellular turnover by degrading and removing damaged or unwanted material while recycling the resulting molecules for reuse in the cytosol. In MN, autophagy can enhance the processing and presentation of podocyte antigens, potentially contributing to the production of autoantibodies, complement activation, glomerular filtration disruption, and proteinuria. Conversely, defective autophagy may exacerbate podocyte injury and stress, suggesting that maintaining proper autophagic function could protect podocytes and reduce proteinuria in patients with severe NS. Clinical trials are needed to assess the efficacy and safety of targeting autophagy at different stages of MN.
