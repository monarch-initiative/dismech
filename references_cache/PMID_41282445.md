BACKGROUND: Noma is a severe, rapidly progressing necrotizing infection that
predominantly affects children in resource-limited settings and can lead to
devastating facial disfigurement. Despite its burden, limited data exist on its
clinical profile and surgical outcomes in Ethiopia.
METHODS: We conducted a retrospective study of 103 noma survivors treated during
the Facing Africa surgical missions in Ethiopia from 2007 to 2020. Data were
collected on sociodemographics, clinical presentation, anatomical involvement,
surgical procedures, complications, and follow-up outcomes. Descriptive
statistics were used, and comparisons were made with regional data.
RESULTS: Of the 103 patients, 51.5% were men, with a mean age of 27.4 years.
Most (74.8%) lived in rural areas, and more than 60% lacked formal education.
Common clinical issues included eating difficulties (77%), speech challenges
(54.4%), and trismus (38.8%). Submental flap (49.5%) and radial forearm free
flap (20.4%) were the most common procedures. Postoperative complications
included wound infection (22.3%) and flap failure (16.5%). Secondary procedures
such as scar revision (66%) and commissuroplasty (32%) were frequently needed.
CONCLUSIONS: Noma continues to disproportionately affect vulnerable populations
in Ethiopia. Although surgical reconstruction is effective, high complication
rates and the need for multiple surgical procedures underscore the importance of
comprehensive long-term care, including rehabilitation and psychological
support.

Question: What are the sociodemographic factors, clinical features, surgical outcomes, and long-term challenges of noma (cancrum oris) in Ethiopia’s underserved populations?

Findings: A retrospective review of 103 noma patients treated by Facing Africa (2007–2020) revealed that most patients were rural with low socioeconomic status and severe functional impairments. Surgical treatments, including local and free flaps, showed favorable outcomes despite notable complications, emphasizing the importance of integrated care and ongoing follow-up.

Meaning: Noma remains a critical public health concern in Ethiopia, necessitating early diagnosis, multidisciplinary surgical care, and psychosocial support to enhance patient recovery and quality of life.

Noma, or cancrum oris, is a rapidly progressive infectious disease predominantly affecting malnourished children younger than 10 years old in impoverished regions of sub-Saharan Africa, Asia, and Latin America.1 It causes gangrenous destruction of facial tissues, leading to severe disfigurement and functional impairments such as difficulties in eating, speaking, and breathing.2 The disease typically initiates as a gingival infection that quickly advances to necrosis involving the cheeks, lips, and nose.3

Malnutrition, immunosuppression, poor hygiene, and limited access to healthcare are central factors in the pathogenesis of noma, with infections by Fusobacterium necrophorum commonly implicated.4,5 The rapid progression of the disease contributes to a high mortality rate of 80%–90% among affected children.6 Survivors face lifelong physical disabilities and social stigma, which profoundly affect their psychological well-being and quality of life.7,8 Despite being largely preventable through improved nutrition, sanitation, and healthcare access, noma remains neglected in many low-resource settings.9

Current interventions focus on early detection, prevention, and surgical reconstruction. However, these remain insufficient due to socioeconomic barriers and weak health infrastructures, particularly in rural areas.10 Additionally, little is known about the long-term rehabilitative needs of survivors, including psychosocial support essential for community reintegration.11

Current strategies to reduce the incidence of noma emphasize community-based interventions focusing on improved nutrition, hygiene, and early case detection. Nutritional supplementation, particularly vitamin A, along with the promotion of oral hygiene, significantly lowers disease risk.3,12 Measles and other childhood infectious diseases reduce immunosuppression, a key factor in noma development.4,13 Programs involving local and international partnerships, such as Facing Africa, provide essential surgical reconstruction, rehabilitation, and psychosocial support to survivors.14,15

Facing Africa is a UK-based nongovernmental organization focused on the surgical rehabilitation of noma survivors, primarily aiming to correct facial disfigurements and restore both functional and aesthetic outcomes. Although the organization also promotes community awareness for noma prevention and early identification, its primary mission is to treat individuals already affected by the disease.16

To achieve this, Facing Africa deploys outreach teams across Ethiopia to identify and recruit patients from remote and underserved regions. Selected patients are brought to a central rehabilitation facility in Addis Ababa, where they receive comprehensive preoperative care—including nutritional support, treatment of malnutrition and comorbidities, oral hygiene, and dental care. Patients are typically rehabilitated for about 1 month before surgery.16,17

Surgical missions are conducted biannually, each lasting approximately 2 weeks. Multidisciplinary teams composed of surgeons, anesthetists, nurses, and support staff perform the reconstructive procedures. Postoperative recovery takes place in the same facility, where patients are monitored for wound healing, early complications, and functional outcomes. Continued follow-up is managed through phone calls and periodic photograph updates. Patients needing further procedures or experiencing complications are reassessed during future missions.16,17

Despite these efforts, inadequate healthcare infrastructure, poverty, and limited access to preventive services remain critical barriers.18,19 Integration of vaccination campaigns, community education, and nutrition programs is vital for sustainable control and prevention of noma in endemic regions.20

The management of noma begins with early identification based on clinical signs such as gingival ulceration, facial swelling, and necrosis, which allows prompt intervention to halt disease progression.20,21 The treatment algorithm involves an initial clinical assessment focusing on characteristic oral lesions and systemic symptoms. Although no bacteriologic studies have been conducted in Ethiopia, research from other regions implicates anaerobic bacteria, such as F. necrophorum, in the pathogenesis of noma. This information guides the use of prophylactic and therapeutic antibiotics, typically penicillin combined with metronidazole.20,21 Medical treatment includes immediate administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics, nutritional support, and hydration to control infection and improve immune status. Following stabilization, surgical intervention is undertaken to address functional impairments and aesthetic deformities through procedures such as scar release and flap reconstruction.20,22 Facing Africa’s surgical treatment algorithm begins with evaluating the patient’s functional impairment, especially trismus, and assessing the extent of soft tissue and bony loss. Defects are categorized by location and severity, including cheek, lips, nose, periorbital region, and jaw. Trismus correction is prioritized using bilateral coronoidectomy or condylectomy with temporalis muscle interposition, followed by early postoperative physiotherapy to prevent recurrence. Soft tissue defects are reconstructed with regional flaps such as submental, forehead, and Abbe flaps, as well as free flaps (Figs. 1A, B). Nasal reconstruction is performed in stages, typically using a forehead flap combined with cartilage grafts. Mandibular loss is managed with reconstruction plates, and free fibula flaps are considered when resources permit. Surgical treatment is staged, starting with trismus release and scar contracture correction, followed by defect reconstruction and later secondary refinements. Postoperative care includes multidisciplinary support with nutrition, physiotherapy, and psychological counseling.17,23

This study analyzed data from patients treated at Yekatit 12 Hospital Medical College, MCM Hospital, CURE Hospital, and Nordic Medical Center between 2007 and 2020. The data, collected with the help of Facing Africa, were entered prospectively into a database, including demographic details, clinical findings, management, outcomes, and follow-up information for all noma patients. These patients, often malnourished with complications such as locked jaw, were rehabilitated pre- and postoperatively at a rehabilitation center in Addis Ababa. Data from these patients were used as the primary source for the study.

A hospital-based retrospective cross-sectional study design was used. The source population included all noma patients treated with Facing Africa’s support, and the study population consisted of those with complete records. The study included patients with complete data, excluding those with incomplete records or deferred surgery. Convenience sampling was used to include all treated patients with complete data.

Dependent and independent variables were examined, with noma as the dependent variable and marital status, occupation, literacy, income, ethnicity, diet, and mental illness as independent variables. Data were collected through standardized questionnaires, and data quality was ensured through regular checks and supervision. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the English version of the questionnaires used for sociodemographic data, risk factor, clinical presentation, surgical management, and outcomes assessment of noma patients, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E468.) The data were manually verified for consistency and completeness before being analyzed using SPSS version 25.0, with descriptive statistics used to summarize the findings.

Ethical approval was obtained from Addis Ababa University’s Surgical Department and the research ethics review committee. A data-sharing agreement was also signed with Facing Africa. The results of this study will be disseminated through conferences, workshops, and scientific publications, and shared with relevant stakeholders. Limitations include the retrospective design, which may have resulted in missing or incomplete data due to the original purpose of the chart reviews.

A total of 103 patients with noma were analyzed, with a nearly equal distribution of men (51.5%) and women (48.5%). The majority (45.6%) were aged 16–29 years, with a mean age of 27.43 years. Most patients originated from rural areas (74.8%), predominantly from the Amhara (37.9%) and Oromia (21.4%) regions. Religious affiliations were mainly Muslim (38.8%) and Orthodox Christian (37.9%). A significant proportion had no formal education (60.2%) and earned less than 500 Ethiopian birr per month (60.2%). Farming was the most common occupation (41.7%). Socially, more than half (54.4%) were single, and the largest family size category was 4–6 members (57.3%). (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays the sociodemographic characteristics of noma patients treated by the Facing Africa mission in Ethiopia (2007–2020), https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E469.)

Regarding water sources, 47.6% of patients relied on tap water, 40.8% used river water, and 11.7% used wells. Most patients (83.5%) reported no addictions; however, 10.7% consumed alcohol, 3.9% used khat, and 2% smoked. Comorbidities were uncommon, with 86.4% reporting none, though malaria (5.8%) and retroviral infections (1.9%) were noted. Immunization status was largely unknown (66%), with 21.4% vaccinated and 12.6% unvaccinated (Table 1).

This study provided important insights into the sociodemographic, clinical, and surgical characteristics of noma patients in Ethiopia, highlighting significant socioeconomic and healthcare access challenges. The predominance of rural patients (74.8%) with low income and limited formal education reflects the socioeconomic vulnerability associated with noma, consistent with prior studies that link poverty, low literacy, and restricted healthcare access to increased disease risk.1–9 The majority coming from Amhara and Oromia regions aligns with known geographic disparities in health resources and poverty in Ethiopia.6,7

Functionally, noma survivors experience severe impairments in eating, speaking, and breathing, underscoring the profound impact of orofacial destruction on basic physiological functions.1,18,23 These impairments not only reduce quality of life but also contribute to social isolation, as reflected in the high rates of dissatisfaction with appearance and experiences of stigma and bullying. These findings underscore the psychological burden of noma, which previous research has documented as a critical aspect requiring integrated psychosocial care.8,15,24

The surgical interventions performed, predominantly local flap reconstructions, demonstrate practical approaches suitable for resource-limited settings, consistent with literature advocating their effectiveness and feasibility.1,14 However, the notable rates of postoperative complications such as wound infections and flap failures (22.3% and 16.5%, respectively) highlight ongoing challenges in surgical management, particularly in low-resource environments where infection control and specialized care are limited.13,25 These persistent difficulties are consistent with historical reports that associate noma with poverty, neglect, and high complication.26

Secondary procedures such as scar revision and commissuroplasty, commonly needed for functional and aesthetic optimization, point to the complex, staged nature of noma reconstruction. These findings emphasize the importance of long-term follow-up and comprehensive surgical planning. Overall, this study illustrated that addressing noma’s physical and psychosocial sequelae requires a multidisciplinary approach involving surgical expertise, psychological support, and social reintegration efforts.

Noma remains a severe public health challenge in Ethiopia, predominantly affecting socioeconomically disadvantaged rural populations with limited access to healthcare. The disease causes significant functional impairments and facial deformities, leading to substantial psychosocial distress and social exclusion.

Although surgical interventions improve function and aesthetics, postoperative complications and the need for secondary procedures remain substantial challenges, underscoring the complexity of noma management. A comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach that integrates early detection, surgical care, psychosocial support, and socioeconomic empowerment is essential to treat noma.

Based on the findings of this retrospective study, we suggest several areas of focus that may improve the care of noma patients in Ethiopia. Raising awareness about the early signs of noma through community education, particularly in rural and underserved areas, could support earlier detection and prevention. Attention to nutrition, oral hygiene, and immunization may help reduce the risk of disease development.

Improving access to surgical and rehabilitative services remains critical. The establishment of specialized centers and the use of mobile outreach teams could help reach populations in remote areas who are disproportionately affected. Addressing socioeconomic challenges through supportive programs, such as financial assistance or skills training, may reduce barriers to care and support long-term recovery.

Given the significant psychosocial burden faced by many survivors, integrating mental health support into care programs could assist with stigma, distress, and social reintegration. Investing in training and equipping surgical teams, especially in advanced reconstructive techniques, could also contribute to better surgical outcomes and reduce complication rates. Furthermore, establishing reliable systems for postoperative follow-up would help manage complications early and allow for timely secondary procedures when needed. Finally, fostering collaboration among governmental bodies, nongovernmental organizations, and international partners may strengthen support systems, improve awareness efforts, and contribute to more coordinated care for those affected by noma.
