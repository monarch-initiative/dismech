BACKGROUND: Haemolytic disease of the foetus and newborn (HDFN) is an immune 
disorder driven by maternal alloimmunisation against foetal/newborn red blood 
cell antigens. HDFN can cause significant morbidity and mortality, with symptoms 
in the foetus ranging from mild anaemia to hydrops fetalis. While in newborns, 
HDFN can lead to severe forms of neonatal hyperbilirubinaemia and kernicterus. 
This systematic review (SR) aimed to identify and summarise real-world evidence 
(RWE) related to the patient burden/experience and economic burden of HDFN.
METHODS: Electronic database searches supplemented by handsearching of grey 
literature, were conducted to identify studies that reported the clinical 
patient burden/experience, and economic burden of HDFN in Europe, the Middle 
East, and Africa (EMEA). Data from eligible studies were summarised in a 
narrative synthesis due to heterogeneity between studies.
RESULTS: A total of 26 relevant publications were identified for inclusion in 
the SR, consisting of one study that directly measured Health Related Quality of 
Life, 9 studies reporting on proxy outcomes for patient burden and 18 studies 
reporting on economic burden (this includes two double-counted studies reporting 
more than one outcome type). Neurodevelopment, academic development, behaviour 
and personality were assessed as proxy outcomes for patient burden given the 
limited identification of patient-reported outcome data. These studies suggested 
potential neurodevelopmental impairments in children with HDFN. Despite these 
indirect insights into patient burden, identified data were limited and results 
should be interpreted with consideration of the inherent heterogeneity in design 
and endpoints assessed across RWE studies. Economic burden data were primarily 
limited to healthcare resource use outcomes, with limited reported data on 
healthcare costs, it is difficult to draw notable conclusions on the true 
economic burden of HDFN.
CONCLUSIONS: The current SR provides a clear summary of the available evidence 
for the patient experience and economic burden of HDFN. While the limited 
evidence indicates that HDFN does confer a significant burden on patients, the 
review identifies the need for further well-powered and representative 
observational studies using well-defined outcome measures to aid a greater 
understanding of the burden and experience of HDFN.
TRIAL REGISTRATION: The protocol for this systematic review was registered in 
PROSPERO CRD42022328444.

Background
Haemolytic disease of the foetus and newborn (HDFN) 
is an immune disorder driven by a red blood cell (RBC) 
incompatibility in which maternal alloantibodies attack 
foetal or newborn RBCs [ 1]. HDFN is estimated to 
affect 3–80 in 100,000 live births per year in developed 
nations and 252–529 in 100,000 live births per year in 
developing nations [ 2]. HDFN may affect foetuses posi -
tive for an alloantibody target such as RhD or K, when 
the mother’s RBCs are negative for that same target [ 3]. 
A systematic review (SR) published in 2023 indicated that 
the global prevalence of Rh(D)-mediated HDFN affected 
around 0.047% of all pregnancies, and the global preva -
lence of K-mediated HDFN affected around 0.006% of 
all pregnancies [ 4]. Following the introduction of RhD 
immunoglobulin, the incidence of RhD alloimmunisa -
tion in developed nations has significantly decreased [ 5, 
6]. However, anti D remains a common antibody in preg-
nancy within developing nations due to limited avail -
ability of anti-D immunoglobulin [ 5, 7]. These under 
resourced nations lack the universal screening pro -
grams necessary for timely intervention, contributing to 
increased HDFN incidence [5].
HDFN can be unpredictable with a non-specific clini -
cal presentation [ 3]. In the foetus HDFN can lead to a 
range of symptoms from mild anaemia to hydrops feta -
lis in most severe cases. Hydrops fetalis is characterised 
by severe oedema in the foetal skin and serous cavities, 
and is usually fatal, either in utero or soon after birth [ 3, 
8]. In newborns, severe HDFN can result in acute forms 
of neonatal hyperbilirubinaemia and kernicterus [ 9]. The 
development of kernicterus results in irreversible dam -
age to the central nervous system through a buildup of 
bilirubin in the tissues [ 3]. Newborns who experience 
kernicterus often have developmental disabilities, hypo -
tonia, and hearing loss [1], highlighting the importance of 
prompt recognition and treatment [3, 9].
During pregnancy, monitoring of mid-cerebral artery 
peak systolic velocity (MCA-PSV) by Doppler ultra -
sound, after early detection of maternal alloantibod -
ies via screening, allows for intervention by intrauterine 
transfusion (IUT) to treat diagnosed foetal anaemia and 
prevent hydrops fetalis [ 1, 3]. IUTs have become a cor -
nerstone in prenatal management of HDFN however, as 
an invasive procedure it still presents risk of complica -
tions [ 10]. IUT complications can include puncture site 
bleeding, bradycardia and cord occlusion, all of which 
can result in maternal morbidity. This can result in the 
need for an emergency caesarean section, related pre -
term birth morbidity and even foetal or neonatal death 
(0.6% IUT-related foetal demise rate per procedure) [ 10]. 
Additional, non-invasive treatment options include intra-
venous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and plasma exchange 
(PLEX) to prevent foetal anaemia and can delay the 
need for IUT intervention [ 4, 11, 12]. Post-birth, haemo-
lytic disease of the newborn is predominantly managed 
through the treatment of hyperbilirubinemia with photo-
therapy and, in rarer cases, with exchange transfusion [7]. 
Exchange transfusion (ET) consists of replacing the new -
born’s blood mass to reduce bilirubin and the quantity of 
antibodies [ 9]. Anaemic infants may also require blood 
transfusions with ABO-matched packed RBCs [ 13]. IVIG 
has been recently explored as a further treatment option 
in newborns. However, whilst its administration to moth-
ers has been shown to be somewhat efficacious prior to 
delivery, it is still not currently recommended [9, 14].
Advancements in foetal and post-birth care have 
resulted in a dramatic decrease in mortality rates (< 2%) 
however, reported morbidity rates remain as high as 74% 
[8]. Current disease management is not curative and 
focuses on preventing associated complications of HDFN 
such as hydrops fetalis and hyperbilirubinemia [ 9]. Evi -
dence for frequency of use and effectiveness of current 
management strategies on disease severity and the pre -
vention of foetal anaemia is limited [ 4]. This is coupled 
with differing treatment availability and disease manage -
ment across developed and developing countries [5].
A previously published SR highlighted the lack of 
therapy options and the high clinical burden of HDFN, 
as well as identifying evidence gaps that require future 
studies to improve clinical care [ 15]. Further research 
is needed to assess the impact of HDFN on the foetus/
newborn and their parents from a patient perspective. A 
better understanding of HDFN burden in patients will 
aid the development of improved therapeutic strategies. 
The objective of this SR was to identify and summarise 
real-world evidence (RWE) related to the patient bur -
den/experience and economic burden of HDFN, whereby 
the ‘patient’ is defined as the foetus/newborn as no data 
were identified for the mother. RWE is defined as medi -
cal evidence generated during routine patient care [ 16]. 
RWE benefits the assessment of HDFN burden through 
continual observation of disease epidemiology, treatment 
patterns, and outcomes in the real world with a patient-
centric focus [17].

Electronic databases
Electronic searches of the following databases were con -

Medline® Epub Ahead of Print, Medline® Daily, EBM

of the primary search strategy and an additional targeted 
search for the following disease terms: alloimmune anae -

included as synonymous terms for HDFN. The full search 
strategy is provided in the supplementary information.
In addition to the electronic searches, handsearching 
of the following sources was performed to identify any 
other relevant evidence: reference lists of eligible stud -
ies, conference proceedings, global health technology 
assessment (HTA) body websites, and additional relevant 
sources (e.g. advocacy group websites).
Eligibility criteria
Observational studies reporting RWE in patients with 
HDFN in EMEA were eligible for inclusion. Studies con -
ducted in a controlled clinical setting and single case 
studies were excluded. Full eligibility criteria are provided 
in Table 1.
Citation screening
Two independent reviewers screened the title and 
abstract of citations against the pre-defined inclusion/
exclusion criteria (Table  1). The full text of citations 
included at abstract screening stage were then screened 
by two independent reviewers. At both screening stages, 
any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved 
through discussion or the intervention of a strategic 
advisor. Full publication screening highlighted a lack of 
evidence for pre-specified patient burden and experi -
ence outcomes. In the absence of such data, a post-hoc 
analysis of the existing search results was conducted to 
identify studies reporting the following ‘proxy’ outcomes: 
neurodevelopment, academic development, behaviour, 
and personality. Academic development was defined as 
any assessment of academic achievement, required sup -
port, intelligence, or skills. Proxy outcomes were deter -
mined from full paper mapping of available data prior 
to exclusion of any publications based on outcomes. 
Since a broad database search was conducted, post-hoc 
alteration of relevant outcomes at this stage did not 
have any implications on the systematic methodology. 
Agreed proxy outcomes were reported in more than one 
study and deemed as relevant indicators of patient’s life 
experience.
Assessment of study bias
A formal quality assessment (QA) was conducted by a 
single reviewer using the Critical Appraisal Skills Pro -
gramme (CASP) cohort study checklist (Table  2). A 
second reviewer then checked the assessment, and any 
discrepancies were resolved through discussion or inter -
vention of a third reviewer. However, due to the study 
and population heterogeneity across the included stud -
ies, reviewers also considered potential characteristics 
impacting the validity of the results (e.g. patient sample 
size, patient withdrawal and study design perspective). 
These were summarised and noted as limitations to assist 
in establishing the robustness of the results in individual 
studies.
Results
Study selection
An overview of the study selection process is shown in 
the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig.  1). The electronic data -
base search identified 3,158 articles in the original review 
(March 2022) and 506 articles in the October 2023 
update. Following the removal of 633 duplicates, 3,031 
articles were screened for relevance based on title and 
abstract. Across the March 2022 original review and the 
October 2023 update, a total of 26 relevant publications 
(covering 26 unique analyses) were identified for inclu -
sion from the electronic database and supplementary 
handsearching.
A list of the included studies and their key characteris -
tics are detailed in Table  3. Almost all studies were con -
ducted using a cohort study design; however, 15 were 
retrospective [19–33], five were prospective [ 34–38], and 
the remaining six were cross-sectional [ 39–44]. Most 
studies were conducted in Europe (n = 20) [21–23, 25, 26, 
28–31, 33, 35–38, 40–44]. Of the remaining studies, five 
were conducted in the Middle East [19, 24, 27, 32, 34] and 
one in Africa [ 20]. With regard to outcomes reported, 
nine studies reported on patient burden and experience 
[26, 36–38, 40–44] and 18 studies reported on economic 
burden and resource use [ 19–28, 30–35, 39, 41] (one 
study reporting patient and economic burden data) [26].

Proxy outcomes for patient burden included neuro -

personality outcomes, which were reported across nine 
studies [ 26, 29, 36–38, 40, 42, 43] (Table  5). These out -

available. However, one study also assessed the effect 
of prior IUT treatment on these proxy outcomes and 
reported no overall difference in neurodevelopmental, 
academic developmental, and behavioural outcomes 
between patients with or without prior IUT treatment 
[37]. While the effect of IUT treatment from this study 
has been reported across several developmental out -
comes, including motor disorder and intelligence exami -
nations, this was not a focus of the current review.
Neurodevelopment
Two studies investigating the neurodevelopmental 
impact of HDFN reported satisfactory motor and neu -
rodevelopmental progress in children with HDFN, with 
further five studies reporting a low incidence of neuro -
logical impairment [26, 36–38, 40, 42, 43] (Table 5). Rates 
of impairment ranged from 0 to 11.2%, assessed by vari -
ous tools and definitions. Two studies compared children 
with HDFN to the general population and reported no 
statistically significant differences in psychomotor or per-
ceptual-motor development [37, 40]. However, one study 
reported a numerically higher proportion of disabilities 
in the HDFN cohort versus the general Dutch popula -
tion (10.1% vs. 6.0%; p = NR) [ 26]. While most studies 
reported a low incidence of neurological impairment, 
four of these studies explored factors associated with 
deficits in the neurodevelopment of children with HDFN 
[26, 36, 38, 43] including; increased HDFN severity [ 38, 
43], short gestation period [ 36, 38, 43], low haemoglobin 
(Hb) level [ 26, 36, 43] and a higher number of IUTs per -
formed during pregnancy [36] (Table 5).
Academic development
Six studies reported on the impact of HDFN on academic 
development [29, 36–38, 42, 43] (Table 5). Of these, two 
studies reported significant differences in infants with 
HDFN versus the population norm [ 29, 36]. In one of 
these studies, patients with HDFN exhibited significantly 
higher grade repetition (19.0% vs. 10.3%, respectively; 
p = 0.020) but a significantly higher achievement of grade 
A in mathematics (44% vs. 25%, respectively; p = 0.020) 
compared with the population norm [ 29]. Conversely, 
one study reported a moderate (14.4%) or severe (3.1%) 
cognitive impairment in infants with HDFN which was 
reportedly greater than the Dutch population norm (cog-
nitive impairment rates not reported) [36]. The incidence 
of severe developmental delay in infants with HDFN 
(3.1%) was in line with the Dutch population norm 
(2.3%) [ 36]. Two studies reported that factors includ -
ing decreased HDFN severity [ 38, 43], high birth weight 
[38, 43] low parental social class [ 38], and female sex [43] 
were associated with inferior academic development [ 38, 
43] (Table 5).
Personality outcomes
Differing personality outcomes were reported in HDFN 
patients, with two studies reporting an association 
between certain personality traits (high ergic tension; 
increased excitability and anxiety; decreased sentimen -
tality and conscientiousness) and HDFN disease sever -
ity [38, 43] (Table  5). Increased HDFN severity [ 38, 43], 
high and low Hb level [ 43] and prior neonatal treatment 
[43] were highlighted as factors impacting personality 
(Table 5).

Economic burden and resource use
Economic burden data were primarily limited to neonatal 
healthcare resource use (HRU) outcomes, with the excep-
tion of one study in Israel that reported the mean annual 
HRU cost in the first year of life of HDFN newborns to be 
approximately US$5,988.70 [24]. The study also reported 
that the average HRU costs through pregnancy, first year 
of life, and up to 18 years of age were estimated to be 
US$26,689.10 [24].
Length of stay
Length of stay (LOS) was the most frequently reported 
HRU outcome, reported across 13 publications with a 
high degree of inter-study heterogeneity [ 19–23, 25, 26, 
28, 29, 31, 33–35] (Table  6). The size of HDFN popula -
tions ranged from 11 to 438 patients [ 29, 33]. The aver -
age LOS for newborns generally ranged from 6 to 7 days 
across eight post-2010 publications [19–21, 23, 28, 29, 34, 
35]. One study reported that median neonatal intensive 
care unit (NICU) LOS was significantly higher in new -
borns with moderate HDFN compared with mild HDFN 
(6 vs. 0 days, respectively; p < 0.01) [35]. One further 
study reported significantly higher median NICU LOS in 
neonates with HDFN receiving postnatal adjunct IVIG 
with phototherapy versus phototherapy alone (10 vs. 6 
days, respectively; p < 0.001) [23]. Despite this, no sig -
nificant differences in LOS were reported in comparisons 
between other treatment regimens including postnatal 
ET, postnatal IVIG, or IUT [ 19, 21, 28, 29]. One study 
assessed antenatal factors as predictors of HDFN sever -
ity [27]; positive correlations between increased maternal 
pre-delivery anti-D antibody levels and a longer length of 
neonatal admission, as well as increased middle cerebral 
artery peak systolic velocity (MCA-PSV) and a longer 
duration of phototherapy were identified.
Neonatal intensive care unit visits
Neonatal unit admissions and readmissions in patients 
with HDFN were reported across six publications, with 
the size of HDFN populations ranging from 19 to 531 
patients [ 19, 22, 27, 30, 32, 34] (Table  6). One study 
reported that 97% of neonates who had received IUT for 
HDFN were admitted to the NICU and, of these, 28% 
were readmitted following discharge with the majority of 
babies requiring additional blood transfusion at routine 
six-week test [ 22]. Two studies reported a high propor -
tion of NICU admissions in HDFN patients at 78.0% and 
79.8%, respectively [30, 34]. A further study reported low 
NICU readmission rates for blood transfusion or pho -
totherapy (< 4%), regardless of whether adjunct postna -
tal IVIG treatment had been received [ 19]. Two studies 
reported the rate of readmission for neonates with HDFN 
after receiving postnatal IVIG treatment as 14.6% and 
7.9%, respectively [27, 32].
Specialist visits
Only one study reported on visits of HDFN children 
(n = 450) and pregnant mothers ( n = 73) to specialist phy -
sicians [ 24] (Table  6). The study reported that 27.4% of 
mothers were hospitalised, with 91.8% visiting a gynae -
cologist and 56.2% visiting a general practitioner during 
pregnancy. During the first year of life, more than 20% 
of HDFN newborns visited an ophthalmologist or an ear 
nose and throat (ENT) specialist. It was further reported 
that from 1 to 18 years of age 62.6% of children with 
HDFN were treated by an ENT, 72.5% were treated by an 
ophthalmologist (72.5%), and a quarter consulted with a 
neurologist. This study suggests that children with HDFN 
experience long-term audiovisual complications.
Access to care
One study reported an overview of HDFN management 
and prevention, based on recorded data from transfu -
sion structures (39). Of the 176 responding centres, 46% 
reported only performing immunohaematological tests 
on the mother and newborn, with 29% recording that 
immunoprophylaxis had been given. In total, 64 of the 
1,661 cases (3.8%) of clinically relevant HDFN required 
transfusion treatments, such as IUT and ET.
Discussion
Summary of study findings
The objective of this SR was to identify the currently 
available evidence for the patient burden/experience, and 
economic burden of HDFN. Our review was conducted 
using gold-standard, independent screening and data 
checking methods to limit reviewer bias. In addition, 
extensive literature searches across multiple databases 
were undertaken to maximise the retrieval of relevant 
studies, a clear inclusion criteria was agreed for transpar -
ency and consistency between reviewers plus a formal 
QA performed contextualise results on the basis of risk 
of bias. In the assessment of economic burden, the cur -
rent SR shows that a substantial proportion of HDFN 
patients are admitted to the NICU [19, 22, 27, 30, 32, 34]. 
The review also provided an indirect insight into patient

by a lack of conventional PRO data, for which findings 
were restricted to a single study [ 44]. These study find -
ings were further restricted by a lack of transparency 
surrounding the acquisition of the population norm data 
and limited reporting of baseline characteristics. As the 
‘patient’ was defined as the foetus/newborn, the lack of 
comprehensive burden data was also driven by difficulty 
in the assessment of patient experience within a very 
young population. To circumvent this, proxy long-term 
outcomes, including neurodevelopment, academic devel -
opment, and behaviour/personality were assessed, and 
suggested potential neurodevelopmental impairments in 
children with HDFN [ 26, 29, 36–38, 40, 42, 43]. Despite 
these indirect insights into patient burden, identified data 
were limited and results should be interpreted with con -
sideration of the inherent heterogeneity in study design, 
healthcare systems and endpoints assessed across RWE 
studies. Our review therefore highlights key evidence 
gaps regarding patient burden and experience in HDFN.
The lack of PRO data in mothers of foetuses/new -
borns with HDFN is a key evidence gap. Future studies 
are needed to assess the impact of HDFN on mothers in 
pregnancy and family members. With the suggestion of 
neurodevelopmental impairment through assessment 
of proxy outcomes, the consequent psychological effect 
of HDFN, lifestyle adaptations required, and impact on 
family planning or breastfeeding need to be assessed to 
understand the implications of the disease on parents/
caregivers. HDFN may also be detrimental to parental 
mental health leading to increased anxiety, in part due to 
its potential to drive economic pressures, such as out-of-
pocket treatments, work capability, and burden on fam -
ily/day care of other children.
Economic burden data were primarily limited to health-
care resource use outcomes, with just one publication 
reporting the impact of HDFN on healthcare resource 
costs [ 24]. The study reported the mean annual HRU 
cost in the first year of life of HDFN newborns in Israel 
to be approximately US$5,988.70; however, no compara -
tive data for the general population were provided [ 24]. 
This study was also limited in that it did not report the 
economic impact of having a child with HDFN on par -
ents/caregivers. Six publications highlighted a high rate 
of NICU admission among neonates with HDFN [ 19, 22, 
27, 30, 32, 34]. LOS was reported across 10 publications 
[11–13, 15, 18, 20, 21, 25–27]. However, a high degree 
of inter-study heterogeneity was observed regarding the 
relevant populations and reason for hospitalisation, mak -
ing robust synthesis of data challenging. Specialist vis -
its [ 24], access to care [ 39], and factors associated with 
increased resource use [ 22] were only reported in single 
publications. With limited reported data, it is difficult to 
draw notable conclusions on the true economic burden 
of HDFN. Reasons for limited cost data could include 
small HDFN patient populations within healthcare facili -
ties and the geographical restriction to the EMEA region. 
Further assessment of these resource outcomes could 
help to improve healthcare system efficiency for patients 
with HDFN by identifying gaps in healthcare services. 
With reported high rates of NICU admission in neonates 
with HDFN [19, 22, 27, 30, 32, 34], further studies inves -
tigating the associated cost of NICU admission versus 
regular nursery or mother/baby unit admission could be 
beneficial to highlight how healthcare system efficiency 
could potentially be improved. Healthcare resource use 
outcomes could also act as proxies to help combat chal -
lenges in measuring the true burden of HDFN, such as 
complications associated with IUT treatment which car -
ries a reported 1–3% risk of foetal adverse events [45].

impact of IUT treatment on future development and dis -
ability as well as mortality. While one study found that 
IUT treatment was associated with high perinatal mor -

Limitations of the evidence
While the review was conducted according to robust 
methodology, the results of the SR should be interpreted 
with caution, as there was substantial variation in sample 
sizes, patient populations (particularly regarding disease 
definitions and treatment received), and study design 
across the included studies. Such inter-study heterogene-
ity precluded the conduct of a quantitative meta-analysis 
for any of the outcomes of interest, which would have 
allowed for a formal assessment of the extent of hetero -
geneity between studies. Future trials with more focused 
entry criteria, representative populations, and aligned 
study designs are essential to minimise heterogeneity and 
fully assess the burden of HDFN. Limitations of the avail-
able evidence were further highlighted during the QA 
where all studies were criticised in at least one domain. 
Consistent adjustment for such factors is vital when com-
paring datasets to minimise bias and gain clear insights, 
particularly when dealing with heterogeneous data. This 
highlights a need for further analysis of outcome data 
with adjustments for key prognostic factors and/or treat -
ment effect modifiers as the impact of observed inter-
study heterogeneity on study conclusions is currently 
unclear.
Evidence was further limited by substantial data gaps, 
identified in the assessment of patient experience and 
burden in HDFN, including a lack of generic and dis -
ease-specific PRO data. This SR relied on inconsistent 
outcome measures, primarily from studies where the 
outcome of interest was not the primary aim of the publi-
cation, which further limits robust comparisons and con-
clusions. Therefore, while technological advances may 
appear to improve clinical outcomes, the true burden of 
HDFN remains elusive due to the lack of available tools 
to accurately assess HRQoL. The true burden of HDFN 
may be considerably higher and future reviews could be 
expanded to assess how the range of HDFN-associated 
disabilities and symptoms impact patient quality of life. 
Additionally, robustness of our conclusions is impacted 
by small patient populations, enrolment of patients at a 
single study site, publication dates and insufficient or 
unclear follow-up (for the developmental studies). The 
latter was particularly notable for patient burden proxy 
studies as six of the eight studies identified were pub -
lished pre-2000. Considering the known technological 
advances in HDFN management since 2000 (Doppler 
monitoring, IVIG, PLEX, and NICU innovations), out -
come definitions and results from these publications 
are unlikely to accurately reflect current practice. Geo -
graphical restriction to the EMEA region may also have 
reduced the generalisability of findings. Despite the 
limitations, the findings of this SR may help to improve 
awareness and understanding of the HDFN burden. This 
aligns with a recently published study that highlighted 
a need to promote awareness of HDFN burden to the 
public by producing actionable information online [ 46]. 
The current SR supports this recent study in helping to 
understand the burden of HDFN on patients, which in 
turn improves public awareness and communication of 
its potential impacts.
Despite the evident data gaps identified, our SR pro -
vides a valuable synthesis of the available evidence for 
patient and economic burden of HDFN. The challenges 
of HDFN treatment were highlighted in a recently pub -
lished SR, which reported that prenatal burden and the 
need for treatment remains high, with a lack of therapy 
options [ 4]. While this recent SR focused on treatment 
efficacy and clinical trial outcomes, the current SR differs 
in that it provides an insight into the available data on 
patient burden and economic outcomes in HDFN using 
RWE.
Conclusion
The current SR provides a clear summary of the available 
evidence for the experience, and patient and economic 
burden of HDFN. While the limited evidence indicates 
that HDFN does confer a burden on patients, the review 
identifies the need for further well-powered and repre -
sentative observational studies using well-defined out -
come measures to address the evidence gaps highlighted. 
This future research will provide a greater understanding 
of the patient burden and experience of HDFN compared 
with the general population, which will in turn support 
the development of treatments to improve the standard 
of care for both children with HDFN and their parents 
via updated clinically validated guidelines.
