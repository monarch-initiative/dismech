# Early-onset autosomal dominant distal renal tubular acidosis caused by a de novo SLC4A1 R589C variant with growth failure and rickets.
**Authors:** Ito H, Nagata M, Sakuraya K, Mayumi R, Matsuda A, Sakaguchi K, Shoji H
**Journal:** CEN Case Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s13730-026-01192-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13730-026-01192-5)

## Content

Autosomal dominant (AD) distal renal tubular acidosis (dRTA) generally has a later onset and milder clinical course than autosomal recessive forms. However, delayed diagnosis may result in prolonged metabolic acidosis and severe growth impairment. Here we report the case of a Japanese boy with a de novo SLC4A1 (NM_000342.4):c.1765C > T (p.Arg589Cys) variant who presented with marked growth failure from 6 months of age and was diagnosed with AD-dRTA at 27 months. Because severe short stature was the predominant clinical feature, venous blood gas analysis was performed and revealed metabolic acidosis (pH 7.197, HCO3- 11.5 mmol/L). Additional investigations demonstrated hyperchloremia, hypokalemia, hypercalciuria, bilateral nephrocalcinosis, rickets, an inappropriately high urine pH, and a positive urinary anion gap. Targeted gene panel analysis using next-generation sequencing, followed by Sanger confirmation, established the genetic diagnosis. After initiation of alkali therapy, metabolic acidosis progressively improved; serum bicarbonate first reached the normal range at 9 months and remained within or close to the normal range thereafter. Radiographic improvement of rickets was observed within 3 months, before cholecalciferol supplementation was initiated, and substantial catch-up growth occurred during 5 years of follow-up, with the height standard deviation score improving from - 4.1 to - 2.3, while renal function remained preserved. Prolonged auxological and biochemical follow-up of early-onset SLC4A1-associated AD-dRTA has rarely been reported. This case illustrates that AD-dRTA may present during infancy and that assessment of acid-base status may facilitate timely diagnosis in children with otherwise unexplained growth failure.

Keywords: Distal renal tubular acidosis, SLC4A1, R589C, Growth failure, Nephrocalcinosis

Distal renal tubular acidosis (dRTA) is characterized by impaired urinary acid secretion, leading to normal anion gap metabolic acidosis, growth failure, nephrocalcinosis, and rickets [1–3]. Because chronic metabolic acidosis adversely affects skeletal growth and bone mineralization during infancy and childhood, early recognition and treatment are essential to optimize long-term outcomes [1]. However, the diagnosis may be delayed when growth failure is the predominant presenting feature and obvious renal manifestations are absent.

Autosomal dominant (AD) dRTA caused by SLC4A1 variants is generally considered to have a later onset and milder clinical course than autosomal recessive disease [4, 5]. Nevertheless, considerable phenotypic variability exists, and severe manifestations, including growth failure, rickets, and nephrocalcinosis, may occur even in patients with R589 variants [5–7]. Studies have demonstrated that early diagnosis and appropriate alkali therapy are associated with improved growth and preservation of renal function [1, 8]. More recently, registry data have linked higher serum bicarbonate levels and earlier diagnosis with better growth outcomes [9].

Here, we report a boy with a de novo SLC4A1 (NM_000342.4):c.1765C > T (p.Arg589Cys) variant who presented primarily with severe growth failure during infancy. Venous blood gas analysis prompted further evaluation, leading to the diagnosis of AD-dRTA and allowing prompt initiation of alkali therapy. Five years of longitudinal follow-up demonstrated overall catch-up growth, accompanied by sustained biochemical improvement following correction of chronic metabolic acidosis, highlighting the importance of early recognition and intervention in children with unexplained growth failure.

A 2-year-and-3-month-old boy was referred to our hospital for evaluation of marked growth failure that had been apparent since 6 months of age. He was the fourth child of healthy, non-consanguineous Japanese parents and was born at 33 weeks of gestation. His birth weight was 2264 g (+ 0.64 SDS) and birth length was 44.5 cm (+ 0.34 SDS), both appropriate for gestational age. His head circumference was 30.1 cm (− 0.03 SDS). No apparent feeding difficulty or inadequate nutritional intake was noted, and his developmental milestones were otherwise appropriate for his age. There was no relevant family history.

At presentation, his height was 74.8 cm (− 4.1 SDS) and weight was 8.12 kg (− 3.1 SDS), indicating severe growth failure (Fig. 1). His head circumference was 46.5 cm, between the 3rd and 10th percentiles. Physical examination revealed bilateral genu varum. Given the patient’s marked growth failure without an apparent cause, venous blood gas analysis was performed and revealed metabolic acidosis (pH 7.197, HCO3− 11.5 mmol/L, base excess − 15.2 mmol/L) (Table 1). Serum chemistry demonstrated hyperchloremia (Cl 116 mEq/L) and hypokalemia (K 3.1 mEq/L), consistent with normal anion gap hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis. Serum calcium was normal (8.8 mg/dL), and renal function was mildly decreased (eGFR 85.3 mL/min/1.73 m2; creatinine 0.29 mg/dL, calculated using the creatinine-based equation for Japanese children [10]). Urinary β2-microglobulin (37,000 μg/L) and serum alkaline phosphatase (1632 U/L) were elevated. Urinalysis showed highly alkaline urine (pH 7.5), hypocitraturia (urine citrate < 20 mg/L), hypercalciuria with a urine calcium-to-creatinine ratio of 0.64 mg/mg, and a positive urinary anion gap of 16.1 mEq/L, suggesting impaired renal acid excretion (Table 1). Although the clinical and biochemical findings strongly supported overt dRTA, a furosemide loading test (1 mg/kg [8 mg] intravenously, without fludrocortisone) was additionally performed as part of the diagnostic evaluation before genetic confirmation was available. Urinary acidification remained impaired, with the urine pH remaining ≥ 6.5. Based on these clinical and biochemical findings, the patient was diagnosed with dRTA. Given the severe short stature at presentation, a growth hormone (GH) stimulation test was performed. The peak GH level was 20.2 ng/mL on the clonidine stimulation test, excluding growth hormone deficiency. Serum zinc was mildly decreased (70 μg/dL); however, zinc supplementation was not administered, and subsequent catch-up growth occurred following correction of metabolic acidosis.

Renal ultrasonography revealed bilateral medullary nephrocalcinosis (Fig. 2). Knee radiographs demonstrated characteristic rachitic changes, including cupping, fraying, and splaying of the metaphyses (Fig. 3). Targeted gene panel analysis using next-generation sequencing detected a heterozygous c.1765C > T (p.Arg589Cys) variant in exon 14 of SLC4A1 (NM_000342.4), which was subsequently confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Fig. 4). Parental Sanger sequencing demonstrated that the variant was absent in both parents, confirming its de novo origin. No pathogenic variants were identified in ATP6V1B1 or ATP6V0A4. The variant was classified as pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology guidelines [11]. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with SLC4A1-associated AD-dRTA.

Alkali therapy with potassium sodium citrate was initiated immediately after diagnosis at a dose of 1 mEq/kg/day and gradually increased to 4 mEq/kg/day, with the aim of maintaining serum bicarbonate within the age-appropriate normal range, consistent with the recommendations available at the time [1]. One month after treatment initiation, urinary calcium excretion had improved, with the urine calcium-to-creatinine ratio decreasing to 0.17 mg/mg; serum potassium had normalized to 3.7 mEq/L, and acid–base balance had begun to improve. Follow-up radiographs obtained 3 months after treatment initiation showed improvement of the rachitic changes (Fig. 3). The urine calcium-to-creatinine ratio transiently increased to 0.45 mg/mg at 4 months, likely reflecting variability inherent to spot urine sampling during active dose titration, but subsequently normalized. Six months after treatment initiation, acid–base status had improved substantially (pH 7.374, HCO3− 18.2 mmol/L, base excess − 5.8 mmol/L), and urinary β2-microglobulin had decreased to 2430 μg/L. Metabolic control continued to improve thereafter. Serum bicarbonate reached the normal range (22.9 mmol/L) 9 months after treatment initiation, increased further to 24.9 mmol/L at 1 year, and remained within or close to the normal range throughout the subsequent follow-up. Longitudinal changes in biochemical parameters, growth, renal function, and alkali dosage are summarized in Table 2. Because correction of severe metabolic acidosis was initially prioritized in the management of rickets associated with dRTA, vitamin D supplementation was not initiated at diagnosis. As serum 25(OH)D remained low at 9.2 ng/mL at 4 months, cholecalciferol supplementation (600 IU/day) was initiated after the 4-month assessment and before the 6-month assessment. Native vitamin D was selected because renal function was preserved and there was no evidence of impaired renal vitamin D activation. Serum 25(OH)D increased to 18.2 ng/mL at 6 months and 21.4 ng/mL at 9 months. During the interval between the 3-year and 5-year follow-up visits, alfacalcidol was temporarily used because of difficulty taking the liquid cholecalciferol preparation; after hypercalcemia, hypophosphatemia, and suppressed iPTH were observed, native vitamin D supplementation was resumed.

At 5 years after treatment initiation, when the patient was 7 years old, the dose of potassium sodium citrate had been reduced to 3 mEq/kg/day, reflecting adequate metabolic control with increasing body weight, and cholecalciferol was being administered at an average dose of 1500 IU/day (1000 and 2000 IU on alternate days). Serum potassium was 4.0 mEq/L, and acid–base balance was nearly normalized (pH 7.396, HCO3− 26.6 mmol/L). The urine calcium-to-creatinine ratio had decreased to 0.13 mg/mg, indicating resolution of hypercalciuria. Urinary β2-microglobulin further decreased to 153 μg/L (Table 2). Nephrocalcinosis remained stable, and renal function was well preserved (eGFR 92.9 mL/min/1.73 m2) (Fig. 2). Genu varum gradually improved and was markedly ameliorated by approximately 1 year after treatment initiation, while height SDS improved from − 4.1 to − 2.3, indicating substantial catch-up growth (Fig. 1).

Studies have characterized the clinical and genetic features of SLC4A1-associated dRTA [5, 12, 13], whereas detailed longitudinal outcomes after early treatment remain limited. In the present patient, early recognition and sustained correction of metabolic acidosis were accompanied by substantial overall catch-up growth over 5 years of follow-up.

An important clinical message from this case is the potential value of early acid–base assessment in children presenting with otherwise unexplained growth failure. Structured diagnostic approaches for pediatric growth failure include screening for renal tubular acidosis among potential secondary causes [14]. In the present patient, venous blood gas analysis prompted further evaluation, ultimately leading to the diagnosis of dRTA and initiation of treatment. Because growth failure preceded overt renal manifestations, the diagnosis could easily have been overlooked without blood gas analysis. Thus, assessment of acid–base status, including serum bicarbonate measurement or venous blood gas analysis, may facilitate timely diagnosis in such patients.

The age at clinical presentation in this patient was considerably earlier than in previous cohorts of SLC4A1-associated dRTA: the mean age at onset was 3.7 ± 2.6 years in a pediatric genotype–phenotype cohort [13], the median age at diagnosis was 4.6 years among patients with dominant SLC4A1 variants in the European dRTA Registry [9], and the median age at onset was 5 years in a systematic review including both dominant and recessive forms [12]. Although severe manifestations have been reported in patients with R589 variants, including early-childhood presentation with growth retardation and rickets [7], direct quantitative comparison of height SDS was not possible because previous SLC4A1 cohorts did not consistently report height SDS at diagnosis. Growth retardation was reported in 31.4% of patients with available clinical data [12], while a height SDS ≤ − 2 at the last follow-up was observed in 3 of 10 pediatric patients with SLC4A1 variants; however, two of these patients had only short follow-up periods [13]. Accordingly, the height SDS of − 4.1 in the present patient indicates clinically marked early growth failure. These observations suggest that, in some patients with SLC4A1-associated dRTA, clinically significant acidification defects may become apparent much earlier than traditionally recognized.

The European dRTA Registry demonstrated that height SDS was positively associated with serum bicarbonate levels and earlier diagnosis and supported a therapeutic target of approximately 24 mmol/L [9]. In our patient, serum bicarbonate first reached ≥ 22 mmol/L at 9 months and remained between 23.8 and 26.6 mmol/L thereafter (Table 2), accompanied by overall catch-up growth, improvement in biochemical abnormalities, and progressive resolution of rickets, with a gain of 1.8 SD in height over 5 years [8, 15]. Although correction of metabolic acidosis was initially prioritized as a central component of the management of skeletal manifestations associated with dRTA [1, 16], cholecalciferol supplementation was subsequently introduced because serum 25(OH)D remained low at 4 months despite early radiographic improvement of rickets. Collectively, the radiographic improvement observed before vitamin D supplementation suggests that correction of metabolic acidosis contributed substantially to the early improvement in rickets. Nevertheless, the contribution of subsequent vitamin D supplementation to later skeletal recovery and catch-up growth cannot be excluded.

Urinary β2-microglobulin markedly decreased following alkali therapy, from 37,000 μg/L at diagnosis to 153 μg/L at 5 years (Table 2), suggesting that at least part of the tubular dysfunction was reversible. Low-molecular-weight proteinuria in dRTA may reflect secondary proximal tubular dysfunction associated with metabolic abnormalities, particularly hypokalemia [17]. Age-related variation in urinary low-molecular-weight protein indices has been reported in children [18]; however, the marked decline observed in this patient was temporally associated with correction of metabolic acidosis. Despite improvement in hypercalciuria, nephrocalcinosis persisted, whereas renal function remained preserved during the 5-year follow-up. Nevertheless, continued surveillance remains warranted because chronic kidney disease (CKD) may develop later in life in patients with primary dRTA [8]. The European dRTA Registry also reported a higher risk of CKD stage > 1 among patients with dominant SLC4A1 variants [9].

Although the present patient exhibited a clinically marked presentation in early childhood, the factors underlying this phenotype remain uncertain. Functional studies have shown that p.Arg589His causes intracellular retention of kidney AE1 and exerts a dominant-negative effect on co-expressed wild-type kAE1 through heterodimer formation [19]. Other substitutions at the same residue, including p.Arg589Cys, have also been shown to impair cell-surface targeting of kAE1 [19]. However, considerable phenotypic variability has been documented even among patients carrying the same Arg589 substitution, ranging from incomplete dRTA to overt disease with skeletal manifestations [6, 20]. Therefore, the unusually early presentation in our patient cannot be attributed to the p.Arg589Cys variant alone, and other genetic or environmental modifiers may also have contributed. The de novo occurrence of p.Arg589Cys in our patient is consistent with the previous report of a de novo p.Arg589Cys case and with the proposal that codon 589 represents a CpG mutational hotspot in SLC4A1 [7]. Genetic counseling is also important in de novo cases because affected individuals have a 50% risk of transmitting the variant to their offspring.

This report has some limitations. Prematurity and vitamin D deficiency may have contributed to the severity of the initial clinical presentation, making it difficult to determine the extent to which each factor contributed to the observed phenotype. Nevertheless, the characteristic clinical features, biochemical findings, and identification of a de novo pathogenic SLC4A1 variant strongly support the diagnosis of SLC4A1-associated AD-dRTA.

In summary, this case illustrates that SLC4A1-associated AD-dRTA may present during infancy with clinically marked growth and skeletal manifestations, despite typically being recognized later in childhood. Assessment of acid–base status, including serum bicarbonate measurement or venous blood gas analysis, may facilitate timely diagnosis in children with otherwise unexplained growth failure and enable treatment before prolonged metabolic acidosis further compromises growth.
