# Predominant Symptom in Gastroparesis: Relationships to Quality of Life, Treatments, and Outcome.
**Authors:** Parkman HP, Wilson LA, Hasler WL, Grover M, Chumpitazi BP, Burton-Murray H, Sarosiek I, Moshiree B, Preidis GA, Shulman RJ, Miriel LA, Shade D, Kuo B, McCallum RW, Abell TL, Pasricha PJ, NIDDK Gastroparesis Clinical Research Consortium
**Journal:** Neurogastroenterol Motil (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/nmo.70287](https://doi.org/10.1111/nmo.70287)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Patients with gastroparesis (Gp) can have a variety of symptoms. Apart from use of prokinetics, symptomatic therapies for the predominant symptom (PrS) are used.

AIMS: (1) To classify Gp patients based on their PrS; (2) To determine differences in Gp symptoms, gastric emptying (GE), quality of life (QOL), treatments, and symptom improvement among Gp patients with different PrS.

METHODS: Gp patients were enrolled in a multicenter registry and followed for 96-weeks. Patients recorded their PrS and completed questionnaires assessing symptoms and QOL at enrollment and at follow up visits.

RESULTS: Of 1013 Gp patients (361 diabetic, 607 idiopathic, 45 post-fundoplication), the PrS were nausea (31%), vomiting (20%), abdominal pain (20%), bloating (9%), fullness (4%), GERD (4%), constipation (3%). Abdominal pain was more often a PrS in idiopathics, whereas vomiting was more often a PrS in diabetics. QOL scores were lower in the abdominal pain PrS group. PrS was associated with greater use of directed symptomatic treatment (antiemetic agents and gastric electrical stimulation for nausea and vomiting, prokinetic agents for vomiting and fullness, and constipation medications for constipation). Of 555 patients followed over 48 weeks, GCSI improved by ≥ 1 in 26% overall, more often in patients with initial PrS of nausea (32%) and bloating (35%) and less often for initial PrS of fullness (13%) or abdominal pain (15%).

CONCLUSIONS: In Gp patients, the most common PrS were nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain. PrS is associated with QOL, treatments utilized, and patient outcomes. Thus, the PrS of a patient with Gp provides useful information about their present condition and future outcome.

Patients with gastroparesis (Gp) can have a variety of symptoms with different severities of the different symptoms associated with Gp (nausea, vomiting, fullness, abdominal pain, bloating) [1]. Thus, some patients have vomiting as their main symptom; other patients may have early satiety, whereas others may have abdominal pain.

Treatment of Gp is difficult in part because of the uncertainty of the pathophysiological basis of symptoms [2]. Apart from the use of prokinetics, symptomatic therapies are the mainstay. An understanding of the symptoms in Gp and its relationship to other clinical parameters as well as clinical outcomes is important. Our Gastroparesis Clinical Research Consortium (GpCRC) has previously investigated many of the symptoms of Gp. There is a high symptom burden in patients with Gp with decreased QOL [3]. The disease burden of Gp remains high with only 28% of patients symptomatically improving over time [4]. Nausea is present in nearly all Gp patients (95%) [5]. Vomiting is more prevalent in diabetic than in idiopathic patients. Abdominal pain is also prevalent in Gp, often associated with idiopathic etiology [6].

Many clinicians approach patients by asking about their predominant symptom (PrS) to guide therapy. Classifying patients with Gp based on their PrS has been suggested; one approach divides patients into vomiting‐predominant Gp, dyspepsia‐predominant Gp, and regurgitation‐predominant Gp [7]. A symptom‐based approach suggests selecting treatment based on the PrS [2, 8]. In some studies, the primary endpoint has been patient self‐report of adequate relief of symptoms [9].

The aims of this study were: (1) To classify Gp patients based on their PrS; (2) To determine differences in overall Gp symptoms, gastric emptying (GE), quality of life (QOL), treatments, and symptom improvement among Gp patients with different PrS. This study seeks to provide evidence for a symptom‐based approach to patients with gastroparesis. Perhaps reduction in PrS severity may be a better way to assess clinical improvement rather than improvement in the average of all symptoms, as done with current outcome measures.

Patients in this study were enrolled in one of the first three GpCRC multicenter registry studies (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifiers: NCT00398801, NCT01696747, NCT03680859); observational studies of patients with symptoms of Gp with entry criteria being 18 years or older with symptoms of Gp (nausea, vomiting, early satiety, postprandial fullness) for at least 12 weeks, gastric emptying scintigraphy (GES) within 6 months of enrollment, and no structural abnormality on upper endoscopy. This study's cohort consists of patients who have symptoms of Gp with delayed gastric emptying of diabetic, idiopathic, or postfundoplication etiologies.

The studies were approved by the Institutional Review Board at each center. Informed consent was obtained from each participant. All authors had access to study data and reviewed and approved the final manuscript.

At enrollment into the registry, patients completed case report forms and questionnaires capturing symptoms and symptom onset, body weight, disease profile, associated medical conditions including diabetes, medication and supplemental therapies, and prior surgeries.

While in the registry, patients continued to receive treatments for their clinical care.

At the 48 and 96‐week follow up visits, patients completed similar questionnaires and were again asked to record their current PrS and treatments for their Gp.

Upon enrollment in the registry, patients filled out a baseline history form enquiring about the PrS that prompted the evaluation for Gp. The PrSs were grouped for similar types of symptoms that may lead to specific treatments: nausea, vomiting, early satiety/postprandial fullness, bloating, abdominal pain, constipation, loss of appetite, GERD (heartburn), and other.

Patient Assessment of Upper Gastrointestinal Disorders‐Symptoms (PAGI‐SYM) is a patient self‐reported questionnaire for symptoms of Gp, dyspepsia, and gastroesophageal reflux disease [10]. Severity of each symptom over the past 2‐weeks is scored from 0 (none) to 5 (very severe). Gastroparesis Cardinal Symptom Index (GCSI) [11] is contained in PAGI‐SYM.

SF‐36 Health Survey (SF‐36) is a 36‐item, self‐report measure designed to assess quality of life (QOL) in patients providing two summary scores (physical and mental health) [12].

Patient Assessment of Upper Gastrointestinal Disorders‐Quality of Life (PAGI‐QoL) is a patient questionnaire assessing QOL in patients with dyspepsia, GERD, or Gp [13].

Rome IV Diagnostic Questionnaire was used to help assess for functional gastrointestinal disorders (FGIDs) [14]. Rome IV modules for (1) nausea, vomiting, and belching, (2) functional dyspepsia, and (3) functional bowel disorders were used. In the first registry, the Rome IV scoring algorithms were used for Rome III questions.

GES was performed after an overnight fast. Medications affecting gastrointestinal motility were stopped 72 h prior to the study. In subjects with diabetes, low blood sugar (hypoglycemia < 70 mg/dL) or high blood sugar (hyperglycemia > 270 mg/dL) were corrected or the test rescheduled. Following ingestion of a technetium‐99 m labeled low‐fat egg white sandwich meal, imaging was performed at 0, 1, 2, and 4 h [15]. Gastric retention of the meal (> 60% at 2 h and/or > 10% at 4 h) was considered delayed gastric emptying.

Baseline patient characteristics and changes over time were compared across different PrS groups (nausea, vomiting, fullness, bloating, abdominal pain, constipation, GERD symptoms, other). Data are presented as means (standard deviation) or number (percent). Comparisons across PrS groups were made using chi‐square tests for categorical measures and ANOVA for continuous measures. Changes in PAGI‐SYM, SF‐36, and PAGI‐QOL over 48 weeks are presented as means (95% CI), and the standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated to quantify the effect size of between‐group differences in changes. SMD values of approximately 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 were interpreted as small, moderate, and large effects, respectively. These analyses included adjustment for the baseline value, and in a supplementary analysis, were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and HbA1c in multivariable linear regression models. p‐values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 and Stata 15.1.

1013 Gp patients (361 diabetic, 607 idiopathic, 45 post‐fundoplication; age 44 ± 14 (SD) years, 85% female, BMI 28 ± 8 kg/m2) were enrolled. At baseline, the PrSs were nausea (N = 315 [31.1% of total]), vomiting (N = 205 [20.2%]), abdominal pain (N = 200 [19.7%]), bloating (N = 96 [9.5%]), fullness (N = 45 [4.4%]), GERD (N = 45 [4.4%]), constipation (N = 29 [2.9%]), and other (78 [7.7%]) (Table 1). For the PrSs, the corresponding PAGI‐SYM symptom often had the highest mean severity score. In patients with PrS of nausea, the most severe mean PAGI‐SYM symptom was nausea (3.8 ± 1.2). In patients with PrS of abdominal pain, the most severe PAGI‐SYM symptom was upper abdominal pain (3.9 ± 1.3).

GCSI severity (3.0 ± 1.1) and gastric emptying rates (66% ± 18% retention at 2 h and 34% ± 22% retention at 4 h) were similar regardless of the PrS.

In diabetic gastroparesis, the two top PrS were nausea and vomiting, whereas in idiopathic gastroparesis, the two top PrS were nausea and abdominal pain. Abdominal pain was identified more often as PrS in idiopathic Gp, whereas vomiting was identified more often as PrS in diabetic Gp (p = 0.009).

Gp patients were also classified by Rome IV criteria (Table 1). Functional dyspepsia (FD) was present in 85% and chronic nausea and vomiting syndrome (CNVS) was present in 36%. Postprandial distress syndrome (PDS) subtype of FD was present in 80%, more so in patients with PrS of fullness (96%; p = 0.06). Epigastric pain syndrome (EPS) subtype of FD was present in 58% of patients, more so in patients with PrS of abdominal pain (69%; p = 0.02). Chronic nausea and vomiting syndrome (CNVS) was present in 36%, more so in patients with PrS of vomiting (57%; p < 0.01).

Quality of life was impaired overall in the patients with Gp (SF‐36 physical score of 33.6, SF‐36 mental score of 39.4, and PAGI‐QOL of 2.6) (Table 1). The abdominal pain PrS group showed lower PAGI‐QOL scores (2.4) compared to other PrS groups (p = 0.03). The abdominal pain and constipation PrS groups had lower SF‐36 physical subscale scores (31.9 and 29.4, respectively; p < 0.001) compared to other PrS groups.

The PrS was associated with greater use of directed symptomatic treatment (Table 1). Antiemetic agents were used in 59% of the total 1013 patients, more often for patients with PrS of nausea (75% of patients) and vomiting (67% of patients) (p < 0.001). Prokinetic agents were used in 46% of patients, more often for patients with PrS of vomiting (54% of patients) and fullness (60% of patients) (p = 0.005). Constipation medications were used in 45% of total patients, more often for patients with PrS of constipation (79% of patients) (p = 0.004). Gastric electrical stimulation was used in 6% of the total patients, more often for patients with PrS of nausea (8% of patients) and vomiting (10% of patients) (p = 0.01).

Of 1013 patients in the study population, 555 had follow up information at 48 weeks (Table 2). Patients often had a greater improvement in the PAGI‐SYM symptom severity corresponding to their PrS compared to patients who did not have that particular symptom as their PrS. For example, of patients with nausea as the PrS, their nausea severity improved by 0.7 (95% CI: −1, −0.5) compared to improvement in nausea of 0.5 in patients with vomiting as their PrS, 0.7 in bloating PrS group, and 0.4 in abdominal pain PrS group (p = 0.09), and although small, this group had the largest SMD of 0.21 compared to other PrS groups. Of patients with vomiting as their enrollment PrS, vomiting improved by 0.6 (95% CI: −0.9, −0.3), compared to improvements in vomiting of 0.3 for nausea PrS group, and 0.3 for abdominal pain PrS group (p = 0.09), and the SMD was 0.22. Of patients with abdominal pain as the PrS, upper abdominal pain improved by 0.6 (95% CI: −0.9, −0.4) compared to 0.6 for the nausea PrS group, 0.5 for vomiting PrS, and 0.6 for bloating PrS group (p = 0.03) with a small SMD of 0.05. Adjusting for age, sex, BMI, and HbA1c resulted in similar findings (Table S1).

At 48‐week follow up, GCSI improved by ≥ 1 in 26% of patients overall, more often in patients with initial PrS of nausea (32% of patients) and bloating (35%). In contrast, improvement in GCSI was less frequent for initial PrS of fullness (13%), abdominal pain (15%) or constipation (8%) (p = 0.01) (Table 2). A modified GCSI score (averaging the symptom severities for nausea, vomiting, early satiety, postprandial fullness, and upper abdominal pain) was used in order to factor in abdominal pain; when this was done, improvement in GCSI by ≥ 1 increased from 15% to 26%, but still lower than the average improvement of ≥ 1 in the modified GCSI of 31% in all patients.

There was a numerical increase in the percentage of patients with symptom improvements in their PrS, defined as an improvement in the corresponding PAGI‐SYM symptom score by ≥ 1 point, associated with improvements in their overall GCSI. Of the patients with baseline PrS of nausea, 32% of patients had improvement of GCSI ≥ 1 over 48 weeks, with 39% of patients having improvements of N/V subscore ≥ 1, 37% of patients having improvements of fullness subscore ≥ 1, and 37% of patients having improvements in the bloating subscore ≥ 1 (Table 2). Of the patients with baseline PrS of bloating, for which 35% had improvement of GCSI ≥ 1, 30% of patients had improvements in the N/V subscore ≥ 1, 40% of patients had improvements in the fullness subscore ≥ 1, and 44% of patients had improvements in the bloating subscore ≥ 1. Thus, improvement in the PrS, presumably by directed treatment at PrS, may contribute to overall GCSI improvement.

We also assessed symptoms and symptom improvement by etiology of gastroparesis (Table S2). Vomiting severity was greater in diabetic patients than in idiopathic patients at enrollment (2.2 vs. 1.7; p < 0.01). Upper abdominal pain was greater in idiopathic patients than in diabetic patients at enrollment (3.1 vs. 2.7; p = 0.04). Over the 48‐week follow‐up, upper abdominal pain improved slightly more in idiopathic compared to diabetic Gp (−0.7 ± 1.6 vs. −0.3 ± 1.6; p = 0.35) with more patients with idiopathic patients having improved upper abdominal pain ≥ 1 compared to diabetic patients (53% vs. 40%; p = 0.02). However, total GCSI improved to a similar extent in diabetic (1.2), idiopathic (1.0), and post‐fundoplication patients (1.1) (p = 0.53).

The PrSs of the 555 patients at 48 weeks were nausea (N = 193 [34.8% of total]), abdominal pain (N = 99 [17.8%]), bloating (N = 74 [13.3%]), fullness (N = 44 [7.9%]), vomiting (N = 36 [6.5%]), constipation (N = 35 [6.3%]), GERD (N = 23 [4.1%]), and other (51 [9.2%]) (Figure 1). The PrS remained the same at enrollment and at 48 weeks in only 209 of the 555 patients (38% of patients), with the PrS changing in 62% of patients, particularly when it was initially vomiting, with 83% of patients changing PrS. Figure 2 depicts each group of patients with initial PrS of nausea, vomiting, bloating and abdominal pain at baseline with their resultant PrS at 48 weeks.

Of the 189 patients initially with nausea as PrS, 107 (57%) had nausea as PrS at 48 weeks whereas the others switched their PrS to abdominal pain (20 patients), bloating (18 patients), fullness (10 patients), or other symptom (16 patients) (Figure 1, Figure 2 and Table S3). Patients who had PrS of nausea at baseline and changed to another symptom at 48 weeks (N = 82) had a significant decrease in the nausea score on the PAGI‐SYM compared to the 102 patients who stayed with their PrS of nausea at both time points (−1.2 ± 1.5 vs. −0.4 ± 1.6, p < 0.001) (Table S4).

We assessed medication use over 48 weeks. Among patients initially with nausea PrS, 74% were on antiemetic agents (Table S3). Over the 48 weeks, 61% stayed on antiemetics, 13% stopped, 13% started, and 14% of patients stayed off antiemetics (Table 3, Figure 3). At baseline, 47% were on prokinetic agents. Over the 48 weeks, 19% stayed on prokinetic agents, 28% stopped, 8% started, and 44% of patients stayed off prokinetic agents (Table 3 and Figure 3). When comparing patients who had nausea PrS at both baseline and 48 weeks to those with nausea PrS at baseline but another PrS at 48 weeks, there was no significant difference according to medication usage at either time point, or starting/stopping medications, including antiemetics, prokinetics, opiates, and neuropathic modulators (Table S3).

Change in medication use from baseline to 48 weeks.

We also assessed changes in QOL over the 48 weeks (Table S3). Compared to the slight improvement in QOL if patients remained with nausea as the PrS (+0.5 ± 9.5 on SF‐36 physical), those crossing over from nausea to abdominal pain had a numerical decrease in QOL (−1.8 ± 7.7) (p = 0.24 adjusting for baseline SF‐36 physical score), and those crossing over to vomiting as a main symptom, their SF‐36 physical increased by 2.7 ± 5.6.

Of the 115 patients initially with vomiting as PrS, only 20 patients had vomiting as PrS at 48 weeks, whereas others had nausea (37 patients), abdominal pain (21 patients), fullness (11 patients), or another symptom (9 patients) (Figures 1 and 2 and Table S5). For patients with initial PrS of vomiting, there were decreases in vomiting severity in both patients in whom the PrS switched away from vomiting (−0.6 ± 1.8) compared to those patients that remained with vomiting as the PrS at week 48 (−0.8 ± 1.7) (p = 0.53) (Table S6). This occurred with similar use of antiemetic agents and prokinetics in both these groups. Of the 115 patients initially with vomiting as PrS, 68% of the patients were on antiemetic agents at baseline. Over the 48 weeks, 51% stayed on antiemetics, 17% stopped, 15% started, and 17% of patients stayed off antiemetics (Table 3, Figure 3). 59% of the patients were on prokinetic agents at baseline. Over the 48 weeks, 29% stayed on prokinetic agents, 30% stopped, 5% started, and 36% stayed off prokinetic agents.

We assessed changes in QOL over the 48 weeks (Table S5). In the 20 patients that continued to have vomiting as the PrS, there was a decrease in the SF‐36 physical QOL by 4.6 ± 9.5 points; whereas if patients changed from vomiting to nausea as their PrS, the SF‐36 physical QOL increased slightly by 0.9 ± 7.2 points (p = 0.03, adjusting for baseline SF‐36 physical score). If patients changed to a PrS of abdominal pain at 48 weeks, the SF‐36 physical increased slightly by 1.7 ± 6.7 (p = 0.20 compared to persistent vomiting as PrS).

Of 103 patients initially with abdominal pain as PrS at enrollment, only 42 had abdominal pain as the PrS at 48 weeks, whereas others had nausea (19 patients), bloating (16 patients), vomiting (5 patients) (Figures 1 and 3 and Table S7). For patients with initial PrS of abdominal pain, the improvement in abdominal pain at 48 weeks tended to be greater in patients that changed to another PrS (−0.7 ± 1.5) compared to patients that remained with abdominal pain as their PrS at 48 weeks (−0.5 ± 1.1; p = 0.16) (Table S8).

There was a numerical increase in neuromodulators added in patients changing their PrS from abdominal pain to another PrS (13% vs. 5%); p = 0.24. Of 103 patients initially with abdominal pain as PrS at enrollment, 33% were on neuropathic pain modulators (e.g., duloxetine, gabapentin, pregabalin, divalproex, topiramate) at baseline. Over the 48 weeks, 22% stayed on neuropathic pain modulators, 11% stopped, 7% started, and 60% of patients stayed off neuropathic pain modulators (Table 3, Figure 3). 33% were on symptom modulators (e.g., tricyclic or tetracyclic antidepressants) at baseline. Over the 48 weeks, 12% stayed on symptom modulators, 11% stopped, 10% started, and 68% of patients stayed off symptom modulators. 43% were on opiates at baseline. Over the 48 weeks, 32% stayed on opiates, 10% stopped, 11% started, and 48% of patients stayed off opiates.

Patients who had persistence of abdominal pain as PrS had slight improvement in SF36 physical (0.8 ± 8.0) compared to the slight decrease in SF36 physical if they crossed over to nausea as the PrS (−1.2 ± 7.6; p = 0.91 adjusting for baseline SF‐36 physical score) (Table S6). If the patients crossed over to vomiting as a PrS, their SF36 physical increased by 3.8 ± 2.7 points (p = 0.31, adjusting for baseline SF‐36 physical score).

Of 277 patients followed over 96 weeks on treatment for their Gp, the PrSs at 96 weeks were nausea (N = 98 [35.4%]), abdominal pain (N = 45 [16.2%]), bloating (N = 45 [16.2%]), constipation (N = 23 [8.3%]), GERD (N = 16 [5.8%]), fullness (N = 15 [5.4%]), vomiting (N = 15 [5.4%]), and other (20 [7.2%]) (Figure S1). For the 277 patients with follow up at 96 weeks, their predominant symptom at each of the baseline, 48 week, and 96 weeks is shown in Figure 4. There was a decrease in the PrS of vomiting from 23% at enrollment to 5% at weeks 48 and 96. Similar to the baseline to 48 week data, the 48 week to 96 weeks data also showed a change in the PrS in many patients. Of the 99 patients with nausea as the PrS at 48 weeks, 57 patients (56%) had nausea as the PrS at 96 weeks. Of the 46 patients with abdominal pain as the PrS at 48 weeks, only 23 (50%) had abdominal pain as the PrS at 96 weeks (Figure S2).

Predominant symptom by weeks of follow‐up.

Overall, stability of the PrS over the 96 week follow up was evaluated (Table S9). 57 of 246 (23.6%) patients with follow up data at enrollment, 48, and 96 weeks had a similar PrS at each of the three time points. 40% of patients with initial PrS of nausea had this similar PrS at 48 and 96 weeks, compared to 5% for vomiting, 24% for abdominal pain, 19% for bloating, and 15% for fullness. Thus, PrS of nausea at baseline enrollment was more likely to persist as PrS at subsequent timepoints than other PrS.

Our study characterized patients with Gp based on their PrS and observed them over time in order to develop a better understanding of the symptoms in Gp. In our cohort of 1013 Gp patients, the main PrS were nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain. The degree of impaired QOL varied with patients' PrS, with abdominal pain associated with poorer QOL. PrS was associated with greater use of directed symptomatic treatment: antiemetic agents and gastric electrical stimulation for nausea and vomiting, prokinetic agents for vomiting and fullness, and constipation medications for constipation. Over 48 weeks, GSCI improved ≥ 1 in a minority of patients, more often in patients with PrS of nausea and bloating, and less often for fullness and abdominal pain. Thus, PrS of Gp patients influenced their QOL, treatments given, and their clinical improvement.

Symptom improvement over time was assessed when patients were in the registry, during which treatments for their condition were given by their physicians. In our study, at 48 weeks followup, GCSI improved by ≥ 1 in 26% of patients. GCSI improved ≥ 1 was present more often in patients with initial PrS of nausea and bloating, and less often for initial PrS of fullness and abdominal pain. Thus, patients with PrS of abdominal pain and fullness appear more difficult to treat as these patients had a lower percentage of patients decreasing their GCSI by one or more points. This finding has implications for drug development for gastroparesis, as patients with PrS of abdominal pain are less likely to improve over time with treatment. It should be noted that a limitation of the GCSI score is that it excludes abdominal pain, and this might explain why the score is less improved in the patient with abdominal pain as PrS. Recent modified versions of the GCSI daily diary (ANMS GCSI‐DD) now include abdominal pain as one of the gastroparesis symptoms. We analyzed the data using a modified GCSI, taking into account abdominal pain, resulting in a slightly greater percentage of patients improving, from 15% to 26% in patients with abdominal pain PrS, but still below the 31% of all patients.

The patients in this study were followed over 96 weeks. Interestingly, the PrS changed in many patients over time. Of 555 patients followed over 48 weeks on treatment for their Gp, the PrS changed in 62%. Vomiting was the PrS that most often changed. Improvement in the vomiting severity with treatment appeared to “bring out” abdominal pain as the PrS. We suspect that treatments given to these patients might have influenced changes in PrS from baseline to 48 weeks; this was seen in those patients with vomiting as the initial PrS; a reduction of the vomiting severity occurred presumably by antiemetic treatments allowing the transition to abdominal pain as the PrS at 48 weeks. This might be due to having a number of treatments for nausea and vomiting whereas only a few, and often ineffective, treatments for abdominal pain. Our hypothesis that treatments drove PrS transitions remains speculative, in part, as we were not able to quantify the treatment exposure intensity by looking at the dosage and duration of treatments as this data was not available. Alternatively, the change in the PrS over time could be due to the Gp disorder itself with natural fluctuations of symptoms reflecting the multidimensional nature of symptoms of gastroparesis. A change in type of functional GI disorders has been reported in patients with functional GI disorders, now termed Disorders of Gut Brain Interaction [16]. Interestingly, our Gp consortium has shown that the disorders of gastroparesis and functional dyspepsia can fluctuate over time, a finding that suggests gastroparesis and functional dyspepsia represent a spectrum of gastric sensorimotor disorders [17]. The labile nature of the PrS may impose some difficulty using the PrS‐based classification scheme to classify patients with gastroparesis. However, the PrS can be viewed as the “PrS at that moment in time”; such as during an office visit, this will help the clinician focus on treatments targeting that specific PrS. This study underscores the heterogeneity and dynamic symptomatology of Gp and the need for multidimensional phenotyping of patients with Gp.

Baseline abdominal pain as PrS is associated with a poor outcome in terms of overall symptom improvement. The question we then asked is if someone did not have abdominal pain as PrS at baseline but at 48 weeks changed to abdominal pain, does their subsequent outcome follow the pattern that we saw for abdominal pain as a baseline PrS? We found that the 48 week PrS, if different from the initial one, appeared to have a similar impact on these metrics in subsequent follow up. For example, patients crossing over from nausea to abdominal pain had a decrease in QOL compared to the slight improvement if patients remained with nausea as the PrS. In the patients with vomiting as their initial PrS, those that continued to have vomiting as the main symptom had a decrease in the QOL; whereas if they switched to nausea or abdominal pain PrS at 48 weeks, QOL increased slightly. Patients that had persistence of abdominal pain as PrS had slight improvement in QOL compared to the slight decrease if they crossed over to nausea as PrS. Thus, baseline or emergent abdominal pain PrS was associated with poorer QOL and symptom improvement. This is consistent with prior literature on visceral hypersensitivity and central sensitization. Our current data does not integrate the pathophysiologic abnormalities found with poor outcomes in our patients as this data was not available in our patient registry but this remains a future focus of research.

Through this study, we have a better understanding of the symptoms of patients with Gp, primarily their PrS. The main PrS of patients with gastroparesis are nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain. This teaches the significance of these major symptoms of gastroparesis in terms of unmet needs of patients and potential pharmacologic targets. More frequent monitoring of symptoms using the GCSI‐DD rather than the two‐week recall of symptoms using the PAGI‐SYM could better quantify temporal variability and patient improvement. Reduction in PrS severity may be a better way to assess clinical improvement in patients rather than looking for an improvement in the average of multiple symptoms, as done with the GCSI, which represents an average of nine symptoms of gastroparesis.

Nevertheless, there are some limitations in this registry study. Patients were recruited and enrolled from tertiary referral practices from centers that see a number of Gp patients; our registry may not be representative of primary GI practices. This was an analysis of the PrS in a large longitudinal registry with data collected prospectively; some of the data obtained is retrospective based on patient recall, such as PrS at the beginning of Gp. Symptom classification relies on self‐report by the patients, without physician adjudication. Of note, each of the PrS for nausea and abdominal pain had their respective symptoms from the PAGI‐SYM at the highest severity. Our registry had a low follow‐up rate for the 48 and 96 week follow‐up visits. Some of the low follow up rate was due to COVID‐19 infection with travel restrictions and avoidance of in person visits but most of it appeared to be due to nature of registry studies especially for a chronic disease with few therapies, symptom improvements no longer needing follow up, and patients returning to original local providers because they are closer. Our study still had a large number of patients for each of our follow‐up time points: 1013 at enrollment, 555 at 48 weeks, and 277 at 96 weeks. Given the numerous subgroup analyses, there is a risk of false‐positive associations; p values were corrected for multiple comparisons. One possible explanation for the PrS change as a result of treatment response is that we did not analyze treatment exposure or dose‐response relationships; thus, the causal inferences remain speculative. A mixed‐effects model linking therapy classes to PrS transitions and QOL trajectories might have provided stronger mechanistic insight. Gastric electric stimulation is a directed symptomatic treatment; however, this therapy is not widely available and is limited to a few medical centers with surgical and gastroenterology expertise. Prospective studies of patients undergoing treatment for their symptoms will help further determine a better understanding of treatment and the change in symptoms.

In conclusion, this study has evaluated the PrS of Gp patients. The most common PrS of our Gp patients were nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain. The PrS of the patient is associated with QOL, treatments utilized, and outcomes. Interestingly, the PrS can change over time; this could be due to treatments given for the gastroparesis or to the underlying waxing and waning nature of the chronic disorder of gastroparesis. We found that when the 48‐week PrS was different from the initial PrS, this finding had a similar impact on these metrics in subsequent follow‐up. Thus, the PrS of a patient with gastroparesis appears to provide useful information about their present condition and future outcomes.
