# A mouse model of chondrocyte-specific somatic mutation reveals a role for Ext1 loss of heterozygosity in multiple hereditary exostoses.
**Authors:** Matsumoto K, Irie F, Mackem S, Yamaguchi Y
**Journal:** Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A (2010)
**DOI:** [10.1073/pnas.0914642107](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0914642107)

## Content

Multiple hereditary exostoses (MHE) is one of the most common skeletal dysplasias, exhibiting the formation of multiple cartilage-capped bony protrusions (osteochondroma) and characteristic bone deformities. Individuals with MHE carry heterozygous loss-of-function mutations in Ext1 or Ext2, genes which together encode an enzyme essential for heparan sulfate synthesis. Despite the identification of causative genes, the pathogenesis of MHE remains unclear, especially with regard to whether osteochondroma results from loss of heterozygosity of the Ext genes. Hampering elucidation of the pathogenic mechanism of MHE, both Ext1(+/-) and Ext2(+/-) heterozygous mutant mice, which mimic the genetic status of human MHE, are highly resistant to osteochondroma formation, especially in long bones. To address these issues, we created a mouse model in which Ext1 is stochastically inactivated in a chondrocyte-specific manner. We show that these mice develop multiple osteochondromas and characteristic bone deformities in a pattern and a frequency that are almost identical to those of human MHE, suggesting a role for Ext1 LOH in MHE. Surprisingly, however, genotyping and fate mapping analyses reveal that chondrocytes constituting osteochondromas are mixtures of mutant and wild-type cells. Moreover, osteochondromas do not possess many typical neoplastic properties. Together, our results suggest that inactivation of Ext1 in a small fraction of chondrocytes is sufficient for the development of osteochondromas and other skeletal defects associated with MHE. Because the observed osteochondromas in our mouse model do not arise from clonal growth of chondrocytes, they cannot be considered true neoplasms.

Keywords:bone development, heparan sulfate, osteochondroma

Multiple hereditary exostoses (MHE) is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by the formation of multiple cartilage-capped bony protrusions (osteochondroma). It is one of the most common skeletal dysplasias in humans, with a prevalence of 1 in 18,000 (1,2). In MHE, osteochondromas occur in almost all types of bones, including flat bones, rib bones, and vertebrae, although they most typically form at the distal end of long bones. MHE patients also exhibit various deformities of the skeletal system, such as short stature, limb length inequalities, bowing of the limb bones, and scoliosis (3–5). Malignant transformation into metastatic chondrosarcoma is the most serious complication of MHE, but its frequency is not particularly high (6). Genetic linkage analysis has identified two genes as being associated with the vast majority of MHE:EXT1, located on chromosome 8q24.1, andEXT2, located on chromosome 11p11 (7–11). It has been established thatEXT1andEXT2jointly encode a glycosyltransferase essential for heparan sulfate (HS) synthesis (12,13).

Heparan sulfate (HS) is a highly sulfated linear polysaccharide with a backbone of alternatingN-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) and glucuronic acid (GlcA) residues. The EXT1 and EXT2 proteins form an oligomeric complex that catalyzes the copolymerization of GlcNAc and GlcA residues, thereby elongating the HS backbone.

The current consensus is that the copolymerase activity resides in the EXT1 protein, and that the association of the EXT2 protein with the EXT1 protein is necessary for the proper localization of EXT1 in the Golgi apparatus (14,15). Genetic ablation of either gene results in essentially complete abrogation of HS production in cells and tissues (16,17). The majority of cases of MHE carry frameshift or missense mutations inEXT1orEXT2(18,19).

Despite the unambiguous identification of causative genes and their function, the pathogenic mechanism of MHE remains elusive. It was originally hypothesized thatEXT1andEXT2are tumor suppressor genes, and the loss of heterozygosity (LOH) at these loci plays a key role in osteochondroma formation (8,9). However, the data from genetic analysis of osteochondromas are equivocal (20–23). It is also uncertain whether osteochondroma is a true neoplasm, which by definition is derived from clonal expansion of progenitor cells, or whether it represents a focal developmental malformation (20). Further confounding the issue is the fact that bothExt1+/−andExt2+/−heterozygous mutant mice, which mimic the genetic conditions of human MHE faithfully, develop osteochondromas only in rib bones with a low penetrance (17,24).

To address these unsolved issues, we created a mouse model based on stochastic, tissue-specific inactivation ofExt1. We show that mice with inactivation ofExt1in a minor fraction of chondrocytes develop multiple osteochondromas and bone deformities in a pattern almost identical to human MHE, whereas mice carrying monoallelic inactivation do not, suggesting a role for LOH in the process. Surprisingly, we found that osteochondromas do not develop in these mice by clonal growth of mutant chondrocytes. Our results provide answers to long-standing questions concerning the pathogenic mechanism of this disorder, and furthermore, indicate that, despite the likely contribution of LOH in its initiation, osteochondroma in MHE is not a true neoplasm in its strictest sense.

To test the hypothesis that inactivation ofExt1occurring in a small fraction of chondrocytes is the pathogenic mechanism of MHE, we used a method of stochastic inactivation of loxP-flankedExt1alleles (Ext1F) using a tamoxifen-dependent Cre transgene driven by the Col2a1 promoter (Col2-CreERT) (25). We originally intended to control the level of recombination using different doses of tamoxifen. Unexpectedly,Col2-CreERT;Ext1F/Fmice developed multiple osteochondromas and other MHE-like skeletal defects without tamoxifen treatment (Fig. 1). As described below, this turned out to be due to a low-level leakiness of the transgene, which causes random deletion ofExt1in a small fraction of chondrocytes (see below). For convenience,Col2-CreERT;Ext1F/Fmice that are raised without tamoxifen treatment are calledExt1-SKO mice (“SKO” representing “stochastic/sporadic knockout”) in this article.

InExt1-SKO mice, multiple bony protrusions involving the wrist, fibula, shoulder, and rib were identified by radiographic and macroscopic observations (Fig. 1AandB). Histological examination of these lesions revealed bony tuberosities with a cartilage cap (Fig. 1C), which is consistent with the histological features of osteochondromas.Table S1summarizes the occurrence of osteochondromas at different locations inExt1-SKO mice at P28. The penetrance of the long bone exostosis phenotype (defined as the presence of at least one conspicuous osteochondroma in long bones) inExt1-SKO mice was 100%. This is in stark contrast to the observation that mice carrying the same genotype as human MHE patients (i.e.,Ext1+/−andExt2+/−) never develop osteochondromas in long bones (17,24) (Table S1).

Individuals with MHE frequently exhibit skeletal defects other than osteochondromas, such as short stature, bowing deformity of the forearm, subluxation/dislocation of the radius, and scoliosis.Ext1-SKO mice had short stature [wild type, 8.04 ± 0.13 cm (n= 16);Ext1-SKO, 5.57 ± 0.15 cm (n= 12); at P28]. The bowing deformity of the radius and the subluxation/dislocation of the radial head were observed in 91.7% ofExt1-SKO mice, whereas scoliosis was found in 58.3% (Fig. 1DandE, andTable S1). Together with the pattern of osteochondroma formation mentioned above, these results show thatExt1-SKO mice phenocopy the skeletal defects of MHE quite faithfully. In comparison, heterozygous SKO mice (Col2-CreERT;Ext1F/+) never developed osteochondromas or other skeletal defects (Table S1). The same is true for another conditional heterozygousExt1mutant model in whichExt1is monoallelically inactivated in the entire limb bud mesenchyme (Prx1-Cre;Ext1F/+) (Table S1). These results strongly suggest a requirement for biallelic inactivation ofExt1in the development of MHE-like phenotype. In addition to the development of osteochondromas,Ext1-SKO mice display mild abnormalities in the growth plate and joint cartilage (Fig. S1).

TheCol2-CreERTtransgene used in this study has been reported to exhibit a low-level leakiness (25). Thus, we hypothesized that the phenotypes observed inExt1-SKO mice are caused by leaky Cre activity. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed the spatiotemporal recombination pattern of theCol2-CreERTtransgene using the Rosa26-lacZ reporter (26) in whole-mount skeleton (Fig. S2) and sections through the growth plate (Fig. 2A). In the whole-mount forelimb at E14.5, a small number of lacZ+clusters in developing limb skeleton were observed (Fig. S2, E14.5), indicating that stochastic recombination occurs as early as E14.5. Postnatally, a small number of lacZ+clusters were found in the femoral head cartilage at P0, and the number of lacZ+clusters showed no significant increase during the period from P0 to P28 (Fig. S2, P0–P28). In the growth plate, lacZ+cells form columnar clusters, corresponding to the longitudinal growth of chondrocytes (Fig. 2A). As is the case with the whole-mount analysis, the frequency of lacZ+cells was low both in P7 and P28 growth plates. No lacZ+cells were found in the perichondrium, including the area known as the groove of Ranvier (27). As an independent measure for recombination efficiency, we also performed an allele-specific PCR analysis (Fig. 2BandC). Consistent with the specificity of theCol2a1promoter, no recombined allele (Ext1ΔF) was detected in tissues other than cartilage (Fig. 2B). Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that 6% of theExt1allele is recombined in tibial cartilage ofCol2-CreERT;Ext1F/Fmice, whereas the ratio in rib cartilage was 15% (Fig. 2C). Together, these results demonstrate that inExt1-SKO mice,Ext1is stochastically inactivated in a minor fraction of chondrocytes.

The development of osteochondromas was examined in the wrist joint area (Fig. 3), where osteochondromas develop at 100% penetrance. The first sign of abnormality was found at P14, when the width of the distal radius and ulna became increased and cartilage was formed in abnormal locations (Fig. 3A, arrowheads in P14). At P21, the overgrowth and deformity of cartilage progressed (arrowheads in P21), and at the same time, small cartilaginous islands were formed in the epiphysis (arrows in P21). At P28, multiple protrusions that closely resemble osteochondromas in MHE were established (arrows in P28). Histologically, microscopic exostoses could be detected at P14 (Fig. 3B, arrows in P14), as well as the overgrowth of cartilage tissues between the radius and ulna (arrowheads in P14). Similar microscopic osteochondromas were also seen in the distal femur at P14 (Fig. 3C). In rib bones, the development of osteochondromas occurs during the period of P7 to P28, slightly earlier than in long bones (Fig. S3). These results demonstrate that osteochondromas develop quickly during the early postnatal period corresponding to rapid bone growth, rather than at the time of gene ablation or the onset of HS expression. TheCol2-CreERT-dependent recombination commences as early as E14.5 and does not change during early postnatal period (see above), and HS is continuously expressed in developing limbs from the early limb bud stage onwards (28,29).

To gain insight into the mechanisms of osteochondroma development, histological and molecular properties of osteochondromas were examined. Safranin O/Fast Green staining showed that osteochondromas inExt1-SKO mice have the growth plate-like cellular architecture as seen in human MHE (SafO/FGinFig. 4AandB). Expression domains ofCol2a1,Ihh, andCol10are organized rather orderly as in the normal growth plate (Fig. 4A). Moreover, the frequency of replicating cells in osteochondromas is not significantly different from that in the normal growth plate (Fig. 4B). These results suggest that osteochondromas inExt1-SKO mice do not possess many typical neoplastic properties, such as disorganized and unregulated growth; rather, they look more like ectopic growth plates.

The fact that the inactivation ofExt1in a small fraction of chondrocytes leads to the formation of multiple osteochondromas led us to examine whether osteochondromas are clonal growth ofExt1-null chondrocytes. To address this issue, we first analyzed DNA samples isolated from the cartilage cap region of osteochondromas by laser capture microdissection (LCM) (Fig. 5A Upper). Because the amount and quality of DNA that could be obtained from fixed osteochondroma tissues were not sufficient for the quantitative PCR protocol, we performed semiquantitative analysis using the standard allele-specific PCR protocol (Materials and Methods). Surprisingly, this analysis revealed that all 15 osteochondromas examined were heterogeneous, containing both the recombined and intact alleles (Fig. 5A Lower). The ratio between the recombinedExt1ΔFand intactExt1Falleles was variable, and in most samples examined, theExt1ΔFallele represented a minor proportion of the totalExt1alleles present. To further define the genetic makeup of osteochondromas, we performed a fate mapping analysis usingR26R;Col2a1-CreERT;Ext1F/Fcompound mice and determined the contribution ofExt1-null chondrocytes to osteochondromas. We found that the cartilage cap of osteochondromas contained variable numbers of lacZ+chondrocytes, but were never comprised 100% of the lacZ+cells (Fig. 5B), which is consistent with the result from the genotyping analysis. Together, these results demonstrate that osteochondromas are not simple clonal growth ofExt1-null chondrocytes.

In this article, we show thatExt1-SKO mice recapitulate many of the key features of human MHE. Most importantly, this mouse model develops multiple osteochondromas in long bones, which is the hallmark of MHE and which could not be reproduced previously inExt1orExt2heterozygous mutant mice (17,24). It has been debated that whether LOH plays a role in the pathogenesis of MHE (20–23). Our present findings lend strong support to the LOH hypothesis regarding the mechanism of osteochondroma formation. Although biallelic inactivation ofExt1in individual lacZ+cells could not be specifically verified in our model, its likely importance in osteochondroma formation is further suggested by the total absence of osteochondromas in two control mutant mice—namely,Col2-CreERT;Ext1F/+andPrx1-Cre;Ext1F/+—in which biallelic inactivation cannot occur (Table S1).

It is surprising that osteochondromas that developed inExt1-SKO mice invariably contain bothExt1null and wild-type chondrocytes. Moreover, wild-type cells constitute a major population of chondrocytes in most of the osteochondromas examined. Thus, it is unlikely that wild-type cells are just an incidental component of osteochondromas. This heterogeneous composition of osteochondromas may be a part of the reason why the previous studies with human osteochondroma specimens have generated somewhat conflicting views concerning LOH as the genetic mechanism of MHE (20–23). Together with the nearly normal expression pattern of differentiation markers and the level of cell proliferation similar to that in the normal growth plate (Fig. 4), osteochondroma developed inExt1-SKO mice resembles an ectopic growth plate more than a neoplasm.

This study has provided insight into the cellular origin of osteochondroma. It has been reported that transgenicCol2a1promoters tend to be expressed not only in chondrocytes but also in early perichondrial progenitors (30). However, activation of theCol2a1-CreERTtransgene used in this study in perichondrial cells does not occur unless the transgene is induced with tamoxifen before E13 when chondrogenic and perichondrial precursors are not yet segregated (25). Indeed, we have never observed lacZ+labeling in the perichondrium ofR26R;Col2-CreERTmice (seeResults). Thus, osteochondromas inExt1-SKO mice are likely to originate from chondrocytes in the growth plate. It should be noted, however, that this conclusion does not mutually exclude the possibility that perichondrial cells can also be an origin of osteochondromas. This latter possibility will need to be examined by studies using different Cre systems.

Although our results represent a significant development toward understanding the pathogenesis of MHE, there are a number of remaining questions. For instance, how does the inactivation ofExt1in a small fraction of chondrocytes result in the formation of osteochondromas, which we now know are nonclonal? Our data suggest that the presence ofExt1null chondrocytes is required for theinitiationof osteochondromas in these mice, but the subsequent growth of osteochondromas is not directly due to the unregulated proliferation of mutant cells. In other words, despite the role of LOH in its development, the pathogenesis of MHE is probably not fully explained by the classical two-hit model. An interesting possibility, although speculative at this point, is that mutant chondrocytes lacking HS exert some sort of cell nonautonomous effect on wild-type chondrocytes. Another key question is whether the same genetic mechanism as we found in this study applies to human MHE. Considering the present findings regarding the importance of biallelic inactivation, it should be of interest to revisit the issue of LOH in human osteochondromas. If LOH does indeed underlie human MHE, it is then important to determine how LOH occurs in human chondrocytes. Alternatively, it is possible that mouse and human chondrocytes do not respond in the identical manner to heterozygous levels ofExt1, in such a manner that mouse cells tolerate heterozygous levels ofExt1whereas human cells begin to behave aberrantly at that level. It is also possible that mouse cells have a mechanism to compensate for an inactiveExt1allele whereas human cells cannot. In any event, this mouse model should be useful to address these questions concerning the pathogenesis of MHE as well as to explore therapeutic strategies for this important bone disorder.

For whole-mount analysis of skeletons, mice were eviscerated and fixed in 95% ethanol overnight. Radiographic examination was performed on a Faxitron MX-20 DC4 (Faxitron X-Ray LLC) using an energy of 26 kV and an exposure time of 10 s.

Tissue specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS, embedded in paraffin, decalcified in EDTA, and sectioned at 5 mm. For Safranin O/Fast Green staining, deparaffinized, rehydrated sections were stained in Weigert's Iron Hematoxylin (Sigma) and 0.02% aqueous Fast Green (Sigma), followed by a rinse in 1% acetic acid and 0.1% aqueous Safranin O (Sigma). For immunohistochemistry, fixed and decalcified tissue specimens were embedded in OCT compound and cryosectioned at 5 mm. After blocking with 5% goat serum, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the anti-phospho-histone H3 (pHH3) antibody (Upstate Biotechnology), followed by detection with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen) and counterstaining with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI) (Invitrogen). Images of sections were taken using a TE300 Nikon fluorescence microscope. The number of pHH3-positive cells and the number of DAPI-positive nuclei were counted in two 100 × 100-μm sampling windows (per section) in the cartilage cap region of osteochondromas or in the growth plate. For each osteochondroma or growth plate specimens, counting was made in two adjacent sections, and the percentage of pHH3-positive cells to the total number of cells was determined for each specimen. Four osteochondroma and four growth plate specimens were examined.

Whole-skeleton X-gal staining was performed as described in ref.34with some modifications. Specimens were then incubated overnight at 4 °C in the dark in 0.1% X-gal reaction buffer at pH 7.5, rinsed in PBS, and postfixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C. Specimens were placed in 1% KOH for 1–2 wk with fresh KOH every 2 d; the cleared samples were then stored in 70% ethanol.
