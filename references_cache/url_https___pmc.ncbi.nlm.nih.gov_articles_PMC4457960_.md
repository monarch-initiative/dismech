</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>Autosomal-dominant lateral temporal epilepsy (ADLTE) is a genetic epilepsy syndrome clinically characterized by focal seizures with prominent auditory symptoms. ADLTE is genetically heterogeneous, and mutations in <em>LGI1</em> account for fewer than 50% of affected families. Here, we report the identification of causal mutations in reelin (<em>RELN</em>) in seven ADLTE-affected families without <em>LGI1</em> mutations. We initially investigated 13 ADLTE-affected families by performing SNP-array linkage analysis and whole-exome sequencing and identified three heterozygous missense mutations co-segregating with the syndrome. Subsequent analysis of 15 small ADLTE-affected families revealed four additional missense mutations. 3D modeling predicted that all mutations have structural effects on protein-domain folding. Overall, <em>RELN</em> mutations occurred in 7/40 (17.5%) ADLTE-affected families. <em>RELN</em> encodes a secreted protein, Reelin, which has important functions in both the developing and adult brain and is also found in the blood serum. We show that ADLTE-related mutations significantly decrease serum levels of Reelin, suggesting an inhibitory effect of mutations on protein secretion. We also show that Reelin and LGI1 co-localize in a subset of rat brain neurons, supporting an involvement of both proteins in a common molecular pathway underlying ADLTE. Homozygous <em>RELN</em> mutations are known to cause lissencephaly with cerebellar hypoplasia. Our findings extend the spectrum of neurological disorders associated with <em>RELN</em> mutations and establish a link between <em>RELN</em> and <em>LGI1</em>, which play key regulatory roles in both the developing and adult brain.</p></section><section id="sec1"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Main Text</h2>
<p>Temporal-lobe epilepsy is the most common type of focal epilepsy. It is sometimes associated with structural brain lesions, but genetic forms have also been described. Familial temporal-lobe epilepsy comprises two genetically distinct syndromes: familial mesial temporal-lobe epilepsy (FMTLE [MIM: <a href="https://omim.org/entry/611630" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">611630</a>])<a href="#bib1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib1"><sup>1</sup></a> and autosomal-dominant lateral temporal epilepsy (ADLTE [MIM: <a href="https://omim.org/entry/600512" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">600512</a>]), also named autosomal-dominant partial epilepsy with auditory features (ADPEAF).<a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2"><sup>2</sup></a> ADLTE is a well-defined epileptic syndrome clinically characterized by focal seizures with prominent auditory and/or aphasic symptoms, normal brain MRI, and relatively benign evolution.<a href="#bib2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib2"><sup>2,3</sup></a> Its inheritance pattern is autosomal dominant with reduced penetrance (around 70%). Mutations associated with ADLTE are found in leucine-rich, glioma inactivated 1 (<em>LGI1</em> [MIM: <a href="https://omim.org/entry/604619" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">604619</a>])<a href="#bib4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib4"><sup>4–6</sup></a> in 30%–50% of ADLTE-affected families.<a href="#bib3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib3"><sup>3,7,8</sup></a> Other genes harboring ADLTE-causing mutations are unknown. <em>LGI1</em> is expressed mainly in neurons, particularly in the neocortex and limbic regions,<a href="#bib4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib4"><sup>4,9</sup></a> and its protein product, LGI1, is secreted.<a href="#bib9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib9"><sup>9</sup></a> LGI1 has been implicated in the transmission of K<sup>+</sup> and AMPA synaptic currents<a href="#bib10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib10"><sup>10,11</sup></a> and in the regulation of post-natal maturation of cortical excitatory synapses and dendrite pruning.<a href="#bib12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib12"><sup>12</sup></a> However, it is not known which of these functions underlies ADLTE. Identification of additional genes whose mutations cause ADLTE will help to clarify the pathogenic mechanism leading to this disorder. Here we report the identification of ADLTE-causing heterozygous mutations in <em>RELN</em> (MIM: <a href="https://omim.org/entry/600514" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">600514</a>), encoding the protein Reelin.</p>
<p>To identify genes harboring ADLTE-causing mutations, we performed linkage analysis of 16 ADLTE-affected families in whom Sanger sequencing had failed to detect <em>LGI1</em> mutations; we did so by using a SNP array followed by whole-exome sequencing to identify candidate variants in the linkage peaks. Most of these families have been described previously.<a href="#bib7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib7"><sup>7</sup></a> We reassessed the epilepsy phenotypes of affected members to confirm eligibility for linkage analysis on the basis of clinical features and intrafamilial clinical homogeneity. Each family contained two or more affected individuals with a history of focal epilepsy with auras characteristic of the syndrome, i.e., auditory (ringing, humming, sounds, voices, or music) and/or receptive-aphasic symptoms, and absence of identified structural or metabolic insults to the CNS. Written informed consent was obtained from all family members participating in the study. The study was approved by local, Italian League Against Epilepsy (LICE) or Columbia University ethics committees. Affected and unaffected members of these families (143 DNAs in total, 50 affected persons) were genotyped with the high-density HumanOmni1-Quad v.1.0 (Illumina) beadchip, and genome-wide linkage analysis was performed with the Merlin program,<a href="#bib13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib13"><sup>13</sup></a> under the assumption of autosomal-dominant inheritance with 70% penetrance, a disease-allele frequency of 0.001, and a phenocopy rate of 0.0. To avoid inflated linkage values, we performed two independent analyses by using two different SNP subsets selected on the basis of different linkage-disequilibrium parameters, r<sup>2</sup> &lt; 0.4 (180,047 SNPs) and r<sup>2</sup> &lt; 0.2 (62,887 SNPs). In both analyses, the highest linkage peak (heterogeneity logarithm of odds [HLOD] = 2.349; alpha = 0.289) was at chromosome 7q22.1 (<a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">Figure S1</a>), a region encompassing 5.70 Mb (chr7: 101,977,695–107,685,645) between SNPs rs803118 and rs1990158. Two relatively large families, shown in <a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>, were the main contributors to this HLOD peak: F31 (LOD score = 2.03) and F14 (LOD score = 1.92).</p>

Whenever possible, we chose second- or third-degree relatives to reduce the fraction of the genome inherited by chance. Exome sequencing was performed at IGA Technology Services with the SureSelect 50-Mb v.2.0 Capture Kit (Agilent Technologies). Enriched libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq2000. Reads were aligned to the UCSC Genome Browser hg19 reference sequence with the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner,<a href="#bib14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib14"><sup>14</sup></a> and variant calling and genotyping were performed with the Genome Analysis Toolkit.<a href="#bib15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib15"><sup>15</sup></a> Variants were annotated by ANNOVAR<a href="#bib16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib16"><sup>16</sup></a> and filtered with NCBI dbSNP v.135, the 1000 Genomes Project catalog, and AVSIFT.<a href="#bib17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib17"><sup>17</sup></a> An average of 4.545 Gb of sequence was generated per affected individual; 99.8% of the total bases passed quality assessment and were aligned to the human reference sequence, and 80% mapped to the targeted exons with a mean coverage of 39×. At this depth of coverage, 95% of the targeted bases were sufficiently covered to pass our threshold for variant calling (≥10×). We considered only heterozygous nonsynonymous variants, splice-site changes, and short indels causing frameshifts that were shared by the two affected individuals analyzed in each family, and then filtered out those occurring in the 1000 Genomes Project catalog and the NHLBI Exome Sequencing Project Exome Variant Server (EVS) with frequency &gt; 0.01, those in genome segmental duplications, and those classified as benign, tolerated, or not damaging by different prediction software tools, such as SIFT and PolyPhen-2 (see examples in <a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">Tables S1</a> and <a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">S2</a>). Finally, we extracted the gene variants lying in the chromosome 7q22.1 linkage interval from the annotation file of potential candidate variants that passed the filtering steps. Three families (F31, F33, and F14) exhibited only one heterozygous mutation in the 7q22.1 candidate region (<a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">Table S1</a>), and all three missense mutations, c.2392C&gt;A (p.His798Asn), c.2531C&gt;T (p.Pro844Leu), and c.8347G&gt;T (p.Gly2783Cys), occurred in <em>RELN</em> (GenBank: <a href="http://ncbi-n:NM_005045.3" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NM_005045.3</a>). These mutations were validated by Sanger sequencing (<a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">Figure S2</a>), and each of them co-segregated with the disease in the corresponding family (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>). Subsequently, we performed whole-exome sequencing on the probands of four additional ADLTE-affected families who had not been analyzed by genome-wide linkage and found an additional heterozygous <em>RELN</em> mutation, c.2288A&gt;G (p.Asp763Gly), in one of them. Moreover, massive parallel sequencing of <em>RELN</em> exons in the probands of 11 small ADLTE-affected families, performed on the Illumina MiSeq System with the MiSeq Reagent Kit v.3, revealed three additional missense mutations—c.2015C&gt;T (p.Pro672Leu), c.2168A&gt;G (p.Tyr723Cys), and c.9526G&gt;A (p.Glu3176Lys)—each in a single family. These mutations, all validated by Sanger sequencing, were also detected in the additional available affected members of the families (<a href="#fig1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>). In total, we identified seven <em>RELN</em> mutations, each in a different ADLTE-affected family. The main clinical features of the families affected by <em>RELN</em> mutations are summarized in <a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>. None of the seven mutations was detected in 270 healthy control individuals of matched ancestry, in 174 simplex-case individuals with lateral temporal epilepsy, or in the 1000 Genomes Project catalog or EVS. The c.2015C&gt;T (p.Pro672Leu) mutation, which was detected by targeted <em>RELN</em> sequencing, was present in dbSNP v.138 and the ExAC Browser (frequency = 0.0001), whereas c.2168A&gt;G (p.Tyr723Cys) was present in the ExAC Browser only (frequency = 0.000024). All amino acid substitutions affect highly conserved residues (<a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">Figure S3</a>) and are predicted to be deleterious by the PolyPhen-2, SIFT, and MutationTaster prediction tools (<a href="#tbl1" class="usa-link">Table 1</a>). Many potentially harmful <em>RELN</em> variants, most of which await Sanger confirmation, are reported in the EVS. However, all mutations identified in this study affect structurally important amino acids and/or result in amino acid changes that most likely perturb protein folding (see below). In the families we studied, 20/33 (60%) of individuals carrying one of these mutations have epilepsy, suggesting a relatively low penetrance of <em>RELN</em> mutations, and there are no affected persons who do not carry one of these mutations. All together, the above findings support a pathogenic effect of the reported mutations. In our cohort of Italian ADLTE-affected families, <em>RELN</em> mutations account for disease in 7/40 (17.5%) families, a frequency comparable with that of <em>LGI1</em> mutations (12/40 families; 30%). We detected no <em>RELN</em> mutations in 17 FMTLE-affected families, suggesting that mutations in this gene might specifically cause ADLTE.</p>

</div></section><p><em>RELN</em>, first identified in the homozygous-null mutant <em>reeler</em> mouse strain,<a href="#bib18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib18"><sup>18</sup></a> comprises 65 exons and is mostly expressed in the brain. It encodes Reelin (a gigantic secreted protein of 3,460 amino acids), which consists of an N-terminal signal peptide, an F-spondin-like domain, a unique region, and eight tandem repeats of 350–390 residues termed “Reelin repeats” (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>A).<a href="#bib18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib18"><sup>18,19</sup></a> Each Reelin repeat comprises a central epidermal growth factor (EGF) or EGF-like module flanked by two subrepeats (A and B), each containing a 14-amino-acid Asp-box (bacterial neuraminidase repeat) motif<a href="#bib20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib20"><sup>20</sup></a> (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figures 2</a>A and 2B). Both the EGF-like and Asp-box modules support the compact, “horseshoe-like” structural arrangement of each Reelin repeat<a href="#bib21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib21"><sup>21</sup></a> (<a href="#fig3" class="usa-link">Figure 3</a>). Some ADLTE-related <em>RELN</em> mutations affect amino acid residues that are important for proper folding of Reelin repeats. The p.Pro672Leu substitution affects the first residue of the EGF-like module in Reelin repeat 1, a proline that most likely facilitates the abrupt bend of the protein main chain at the subrepeat A-EGF boundary,<a href="#bib21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib21"><sup>21</sup></a> and its replacement with a leucine might result in local unfolding of repeat 1. Two amino acid substitutions, p.His798Asn and p.Gly2783Cys, affect residues in Asp-boxes 2 and 13, respectively (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figures 2</a>B and 2C), most likely resulting in structural abnormalities. Particularly, the p.Gly2783Cys substitution replaces an invariant Gly residue at position 8 in the Asp-box consensus sequence<a href="#bib20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib20"><sup>20</sup></a> with a cysteine (<a href="#fig2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>C), most likely altering the conformation of Asp-box 13. To evaluate the potential impact of the ADLTE-related amino acid substitutions on the structure of Reelin repeats, we generated a 3D model of the three Reelin repeats harboring these amino acid changes. To do this, we manually aligned the Reelin sequence (UniProt: <a href="http://uniprotkb:P78509" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">P78509</a>) according to its component repeats by using Jalview.<a href="#bib23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib23"><sup>23</sup></a> Two crystal structures (PDB: <a href="http://pdb:2DDU" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2DDU</a>, <a href="http://pdb:2E26" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2E26</a>)<a href="#bib24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib24"><sup>24</sup></a> were used as starting points for modeling with the HOMER server. The models were analyzed with the ConSurf<a href="#bib25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib25"><sup>25</sup></a> server, and figures were drawn with ESPript<a href="#bib26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib26"><sup>26</sup></a> for sequence alignments and with PyMol (DeLano Scientific) for structures. This 3D model supports the deleterious effects of p.Pro672Leu, p.His798Asn, and p.Gly2783Cys substitutions on the organization of Reelin repeats (see <a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">Figure S4</a>). In addition, it predicts structural consequences for the p.Tyr723Cys replacement, which is due to exposure of an unpaired cysteine to the solvent, and for the p.Glu3176Lys substitution, which disrupts a structurally important electrostatic interaction between the negatively charged Glu3176 and the positively charged Arg3409 (<a href="#mmc1" class="usa-link">Figure S4</a>). This latter effect, in particular, might not be dramatic in the intracellular environment, where pH is neutral, but might destabilize Reelin repeat 8 when Reelin is exported to the extracellular milieu. Finally, the p.Asp763Gly substitution is predicted to perturb the horseshoe-like arrangement of Reelin repeat 1.</p>

In the serum, the 310-kDa isoform accounts for most of the signal (&gt;80%), whereas only trace amounts of the 160-kDa isoform are present.<a href="#bib27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib27"><sup>27</sup></a> We analyzed blood sera samples from affected individuals carrying the Reelin substitutions p.Asp763Gly, p.His798Asn, p.Gly2783Cys, and p.Glu3176Lys by immunoblot. As shown in <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure 4</a>A, the amount of the 310-kDa Reelin isoform was remarkably lower in the sera from these affected individuals than in the sera from control individuals, and there was no increase in the 420-kDa isoform. This trend was significant in four different experiments (p = 0.028; <a href="#fig4" class="usa-link">Figure 4</a>B). Notably, in persons carrying the p.Asp763Gly, p.His798Asn, and p.Gly2783Cys substitutions, the amount of serum Reelin was approximately 50% of that in control individuals. Because missense mutations are unlikely to affect protein synthesis, lower levels of serum Reelin most likely result from impaired secretion of the altered proteins from hepatocytes, and this mechanism could also decrease the amount of extracellular Reelin in the brain. Serum Reelin levels might be influenced by a polymorphic GGC repeat located immediately 5′ of the <em>RELN</em> initiation codon. In vitro studies showed that compared to 8- to 10-repeat alleles, rare, “long” 12- to 14-repeat alleles might result in 50%–60% less Reelin expression,<a href="#bib31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib31"><sup>31</sup></a> and subjects with at least one long allele were found to have an average of 25% less serum Reelin expression than were individuals with common 8- to 10-repeat alleles.<a href="#bib29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib29"><sup>29</sup></a> Together, 8- and 10-repeat alleles account for &gt;95% of all alleles in the Italian population overall.<a href="#bib32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib32"><sup>32</sup></a> We detected only these two common alleles in our affected individuals and control individuals (data not shown), ruling out any influence of this polymorphism on serum Reelin levels in the study subjects.</p>

Particularly, we show that four amino acid substitutions—p.Asp763Gly, p.His798Asn, p.Gly2783Cys, and p.Glu3176Lys—significantly reduce serum levels of Reelin, suggesting a loss-of-function effect due to impaired secretion of altered proteins. Support for this suggestion comes from our 3D protein model, which predicts that these amino acid changes result in misfolding of Reelin repeats and most likely lead to degradation of the altered proteins by the endoplasmic reticulum quality-control machinery. Reduced levels of serum Reelin are observed in heterozygous <em>reeler</em> mice,<a href="#bib27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib27"><sup>27</sup></a> which have apparently normal brains but display functional and molecular defects at the synapse.<a href="#bib34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib34"><sup>34</sup></a> Similar synaptic defects might also be associated with the heterozygous <em>RELN</em> mutations detected in our subjects and might eventually give rise to ADLTE.</p>
<p>Previous work revealed that homozygous <em>RELN</em> mutations cause lissencephaly with cerebellar hypoplasia (LCH [MIM: <a href="https://omim.org/entry/257320" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">257320</a>]), a recessive disorder characterized by severe and widespread neuronal-migration defects, delayed cognitive development, and seizures,<a href="#bib28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib28"><sup>28</sup></a> a phenotype similar to that of homozygous <em>reeler</em> mice.<a href="#bib35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib35"><sup>35</sup></a> Three small consanguineous LCH-affected families in whom <em>RELN</em> mutations segregate with disease have been reported so far; heterozygote individuals in these families exhibited reduced levels of Reelin in their sera and were reported as clinically normal.<a href="#bib28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib28"><sup>28,30</sup></a> The apparent normal phenotype of these individuals is consistent with the relatively low penetrance of <em>RELN</em> mutations observed in this study and the possible occurrence of subtle epilepsy signs, not infrequent in ADLTE, which might remain undiagnosed in some individuals. Thus, as in other genetic epilepsy syndromes,<a href="#bib36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib36"><sup>36</sup></a>
<em>RELN</em> mutations might result in different clinical phenotypes, depending on mode of inheritance: heterozygous mutations might cause ADLTE, and homozygous mutations might result in the more severe LCH.</p>
<p>Reelin is localized almost exclusively in GABAergic interneurons in the post-natal brain,<a href="#bib37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib37"><sup>37</sup></a> and two types of neurons have been identified by immunofluorescence analysis, i.e., neurons with intense Reelin immunoreactivity and neurons with weak immunoreactivity.<a href="#bib37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib37"><sup>37,38</sup></a> The former neurons produce and secrete Reelin; the latter are targeted by Reelin molecules released from other cells.<a href="#bib38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib38"><sup>38</sup></a> LGI1 is localized mainly in pyramidal neurons, but a detailed analysis of its distribution in the brain is missing. Notably, our results show that some cortical and hippocampal neurons intensely labeled by Reelin antibodies are also heavily immunoreactive for LGI1, suggesting that these neurons might synthesize and secrete relatively high levels of both proteins, which might act in a coordinate manner to exert particular functions possibly related to epilepsy. Future work aimed at characterizing these cell types will help elucidate the cellular bases underlying ADLTE.</p>
<p>Reelin is an extracellular glycoprotein that is secreted by Cajal-Retzius cells during embryogenesis and by GABAergic neurons in the post-natal brain.<a href="#bib39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib39"><sup>39</sup></a> Reelin serves a dual purpose in the mammalian brain: it is crucial to the correct cytoarchitecture of laminated structures during embryonic development<a href="#bib18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib18"><sup>18,40</sup></a> and modulates dendritic growth and synaptic plasticity at post-natal and adult stages.<a href="#bib41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib41"><sup>41,42</sup></a> These activities are, at least in part, mediated by binding to apolipoprotein E receptor 2 (ApoER2) and very low-density lipoprotein receptor (VLDLR),<a href="#bib43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib43"><sup>43–45</sup></a> resulting in phosphorylation of the intracellular adaptor protein protein disabled homolog 1 (DAB1).<a href="#bib46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib46"><sup>46–48</sup></a> Phosphorylated DAB1 activates several different signaling pathways involved in formation and plasticity of neuronal networks (for review, see ref.<a href="#bib49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib49"><sup>49</sup></a>). Recent work on conditional knockout mice suggests that LGI1, like Reelin, could serve different functions during brain development and adulthood.<a href="#bib50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="bib50"><sup>50</sup></a> Given that Reelin and LGI1 co-localize to distinct neurons in both the immature and mature rat brain, it is conceivable that a functional interplay between the two proteins is necessary for proper regulation of various neuronal processes during development and in adult life.
