# Identifying molecular signatures of parapneumonic effusion reveals HMGB2 as the disease progression biomarker.
**Authors:** Wu KA, Chen M, Wu KL, Lai CH, Chang IY, Jan FW, Chin CY, Tasi PC, Wu CC, Kuo YH, Shih LJ, Wang WL, Kao YH, Yang CY
**Journal:** Respir Res (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12931-026-03730-2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03730-2)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Parapneumonic effusion (PPE) exacerbation is associated with high morbidity and mortality in pneumonia patients. However, the molecular signatures underlying the progression and severity of PPE remain poorly understood. We therefore employed whole-transcriptome profiling to comprehensively characterize the molecular landscape associated with PPE exacerbation and its clinical manifestations.

METHODS: In the discovery cohort, 26 PPE patients underwent capture-based targeted RNA sequencing and were stratified by disease severity into non-severe PPE (UPPE, n = 9) and severe PPE (n = 17), comprising CPPE (n = 12) and empyema (n = 5). Bioinformatic functional annotation was performed to delineate gene expression signatures associated with disease progression from non-severe to severe PPE. Integrated analyses of the transcriptome and secretome of pleural effusions identified high-mobility group box 2 (HMGB2) as a novel biomarker for severe PPE. In the validation cohort, 220 patients with different pleural effusion etiologies were enrolled for enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analysis to evaluate HMGB2 levels. The functional relevance of HMGB2 in PPE progression was characterized in neutrophils and macrophages.

RESULTS: In pneumonia patients, severe PPE was associated with higher expression of 232 genes and lower expression of 212 genes compared with non-severe PPE. Upregulated gene signatures associated with disease progression in severe PPE were enriched in neutrophil degranulation, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, HIF-1 signaling, and inflammatory response pathways. Elevated HMGB2 levels in severe PPE enabled accurate discrimination from other pleural effusion cases, with an AUC of 0.93. In vitro infection with Streptococcus pneumoniae significantly increased HMGB2, IL1β, and IL8 expression in neutrophils and macrophages. Moreover, HMGB2 overexpression enhanced NF-κB activation as well as IL-1β and IL-8 levels in PGN-stimulated macrophages. These results suggest that HMGB2 regulates IL-1β and IL-8 via the NF-κB signaling pathway.

CONCLUSIONS: Our study delineates molecular signatures driving PPE exacerbation and highlights HMGB2 as a novel biomarker and therapeutic target with potential to prevent severe PPE.

Pneumonia is the most common respiratory infectious disease caused by pathogen infection worldwide [1, 2]. According to World Health Organization (WHO) statistics, pneumonia causes life-threatening illness across all age groups, with the highest mortality observed among children under five years of age and adults aged 70 years and older [1, 2]. These epidemiological data underscore the profound burden of the disease on public health and its substantial contribution to global mortality [3]. Thus, a careful examination of the molecule signatures involved and their associations with complications of pneumonia might help in the diagnosis, determining the prognosis, and eventually in reducing the death rate from pneumonia.

A parapneumonic effusion (PPE) is one of the major complications of pneumonia that results from interstitial fluid leakage caused by increased visceral pleural permeability, inflammation of the lung parenchyma, local cellular damage, and microbial infection [4]. In turn, the formation of intercellular spaces between pleural mesothelial cells is due to increased neutrophil and macrophage infiltration and the release of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα) in pleural effusions. When inflammation is prolonged, PPE progresses to the organizing stage with pleural peel formation that restricts the normal expansion of the lung. The progression of PPE can range in severity from uncomplicated PPE (UPPE) to complicated PPE (CPPE) and empyema. The majority of UPPE resolves with appropriate antibiotic therapy, with or without adjunctive therapeutic thoracentesis. As pathogens proliferate rapidly within the pleural cavity, they elicit extensive inflammatory responses, immune cell infiltration, and tissue remodeling, collectively driving the progression of UPPE to CPPE and empyema [4]. If a patient develops PPE, the course of pneumonia will become serious and complicated, increasing the patient’s mortality. Prolonged hospitalization and the need for invasive treatments increase the medical costs of patients with PPE [5, 6]. If a patient progresses to CPPE and empyema, management requires appropriate antibiotic therapy along with invasive interventions such as chest drainage or surgical procedures [5, 6]. Accordingly, comprehensive characterization of the molecular mechanisms underlying PPE exacerbation may offer valuable insights for early diagnosis, risk stratification, and the development of targeted therapies to prevent PPE progression in patients with pneumonia.

Several biomarkers for differentiating pleural effusions in PPE patients have been reported [7–9]; however, most studies relied on quantification of specific proteins rather than comprehensive proteomic or transcriptomic profiling. Recent next-generation sequencing–based transcriptomic studies have significantly advanced our understanding of the molecular landscape of pleural effusions. Bulk RNA sequencing has revealed distinct gene expression profiles between malignant and infectious pleural effusions, highlighting differential biological processes such as enhanced protein synthesis in malignant fluids and increased ATP production pathways in infectious effusions [10]. Single-cell RNA sequencing studies have further provided high-resolution insights into the immune microenvironment of pleural effusions, demonstrating marked heterogeneity of immune cell populations and functional immune states within malignant pleural effusions [11]. In addition, dual RNA sequencing approaches in bacterial pneumonia have revealed compartmentalized host immune responses and host–pathogen transcriptional interactions within the pleural space [12]. Despite these advances, transcriptomic studies specifically focusing on parapneumonic effusions and their progression remain limited. Using an iTRAQ-based quantitative proteomics and ELISA analysis, we identified BPI, NGAL, AZU1, and calprotectin as biomarker candidates to distinguish between UPPE and CPPE. Furthermore, the combination of serum calprotectin and CRP can be used for the differential diagnosis of CPPE and empyema [13, 14]. In addition, changes in pleural cytokines MIF and MIP-3α were found to distinguish between CPPE and empyema [15]. Other inflammatory-related cytokine levels in pleural fluid, including IL-1β, IL-1ra, IL-8, IL-10, VEGF, TNF sRI, and TNF sRII, were able to discriminate complicated effusions [7, 8]. Although the high mobility group box (HMGB) family, a group of nonhistone DNA-binding proteins, was able to regulate the process of damage-associated molecular pattern signalling in inflammatory response and cytokine production, no reports were demonstrated whether HMGB2 acts as a biomarker for pneumonia patients with PPE to distinguish CPPE from UPPE. Despite the demonstrations from proteomic profiling that certain protein biomarkers were able to distinguish CPPE and empyema from UPPE, the detailed molecular mechanisms driving PPE progression remain largely uncharacterized, representing a critical knowledge gap in understanding disease severity. Accordingly, an integration of transcriptomic dataset on RNA expression with proteomic profiling in pleural fluids should help classify the progression of PPE.

In this study, we classified PPE into two groups: non-severe PPE (UPPE) and severe PPE (CPPE or empyema). We employed RNA sequencing to comprehensively profile the gene expression landscape in PPE, aiming to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying disease progression. We found that a total of 232 genes were upregulated in patients with severe PPE relative to those with non-severe PPE, and such genes were mainly involved the pathways of neutrophil activation, glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, HIF-1 signaling, and inflammatory cytokines. We demonstrated that elevated HMGB2 levels in severe PPE enabled accurate discrimination from other pleural effusion cases. We indicated that infection with S. pneumoniae induced increases in levels of HMGB2, IL1β and IL8 mRNA expression in neutrophils and macrophages. We showed that transient HMGB2 overexpression stimulated expressions of IL-1β and IL-8 at both mRNAs and proteins in PGN-stimulated macrophages, as well as activating NF-κB promoter-directed luciferase expression. Thus, this study delineates molecular signatures driving PPE exacerbation, and highlights HMGB2 as a novel biomarker in pneumonia patients for distinguishing severe PPE from non-severe PPE, as well as a possible therapeutic target for the prevention of pneumonia subjects with PPE progression.

This prospective study enrolled a total of 246 patients with pleural effusion who were admitted to two hospitals in Taiwan: Taoyuan Armed Forces General Hospital and Chang Gung Memorial Hospital. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Tri-Service General Hospital and Chang Gung Memorial Hospital. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and regulations, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians prior to sample collection. Eligible participants were patients aged ≥ 20 years who were diagnosed with pleural effusion by pulmonologists based on chest radiography and ultrasound, and subsequently underwent ultrasound-guided thoracentesis upon admission for both diagnostic and sampling purposes. Exclusion criteria included: (1) tuberculous pleural effusion; (2) coagulation disorders, defined as prothrombin time (PT) or activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) > 1.5 times the upper limit of normal, or platelet count < 50,000/µL; (3) pregnancy; (4) pleural effusion depth < 1 cm on ultrasound; and (5) inability to tolerate pleural fluid aspiration despite local anesthesia. All patients underwent ultrasound-guided thoracentesis upon admission for both diagnostic and sampling purposes. Pleural effusions were classified as transudative or exudative according to Light’s criteria [9]. The exudative pleural effusions included PPEs, malignancy, and other exudates. PPEs were defined as exudative pleural effusions associated with pneumonia, lung abscess, or bronchiectasis, and were further stratified into UPPE, CPPE, and empyema. CPPE was diagnosed when at least one of the following criteria was met: (1) pleural fluid pH < 7.2, (2) glucose < 60 mg/dL, (3) lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) > 1000 U/L, or (4) a positive Gram stain or bacterial culture of pleural fluid. Empyema was defined as the presence of gross pus in the pleural cavity. For both clinical management and analytical purposes, UPPE was categorized as non-severe PPE, while CPPE and empyema were classified as severe PPE, reflecting the increased need for invasive interventions and intensive monitoring in severe cases. Malignant pleural effusion was diagnosed based on the identification of malignant cells in pleural fluid cytology.

The inclusion criteria for the discovery cohort were predefined as confirmed parapneumonic effusion, availability of high-quality RNA samples, and complete clinical metadata. Baseline clinical characteristics, including pleural levels of LDH, glucose, and pH, as well as comorbid conditions, were collected for all patients. In the discovery cohort, the UPPE group included 2 patients with valvular heart disease and 2 with a history of cerebrovascular accident; the CPPE group included 1 patient with coronary artery disease and 2 with diabetes mellitus; and the empyema group included 1 patient with asthma. A total of 26 PPE patients undergoing RNA sequencing were stratified into two groups according to disease severity: 9 non-severe PPE (UPPE, n = 9) and 17 severe PPE (CPPE, n = 12; empyema, n = 5) (Table 1).

Management of PPE adhered to established clinical guidelines. Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgical decortication (VATSD) was considered in patients demonstrating clinical or radiographic evidence of treatment failure. Chest computed tomography (CT) scans were performed in all PPE patients to assess for the presence of loculated effusions prior to surgery. Information on antibiotic exposure was collected as part of the clinical metadata. All PPE patients received empirical antibiotic therapy prior to pleural fluid sampling, consistent with routine clinical management of suspected pleural infection. Although antibiotic use was not standardized, sampling was generally performed at an early stage of clinical evaluation, which may reduce but not eliminate treatment-related effects on inflammatory and transcriptomic profiles.

Following collection, pleural fluid samples were centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min. The acellular supernatant and cellular pellet fractions were then separated and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analyses.

RNA extraction, RNA sequencing, and transcriptome data analysis were conducted as previously described [16]. Briefly, total RNA was extracted from pleural effusion cells using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were initially assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and RNA integrity was evaluated by the percentage of RNA fragments > 200 nucleotides (DV200) using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. For RNA sequencing, 100 ng of total RNA was enzymatically fragmented and reverse transcribed into first-strand cDNA using random hexamer primers. Second-strand cDNA synthesis, end repair, adenylation of 3’ ends, and adaptor ligation were performed according to the TruSeq RNA Exome Library Preparation protocol (Illumina, USA). Libraries were subsequently enriched by PCR amplification, purified, and quantified using Qubit dsDNA HS Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and size distribution confirmed by Agilent Bioanalyzer. Twenty-six libraries were quantified, pooled, and subsequently sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 500 system with 2 × 75 bp paired-end reads, generating a minimum coverage of 25 million reads per sample.

Reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using STAR (version 2.7.3a). Gene-level count matrices derived from the aligned reads were used as input for differential expression analysis with DESeq2. Library size and RNA composition were normalized in DESeq2 using the median-of-ratios method (size-factor normalization), and DESeq2 normalized counts were used for downstream expression reporting. Differential expression testing was performed in DESeq2 using the default Wald test framework, and p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). No fixed manual sequencing-depth threshold (prefilter) was imposed for gene inclusion; genes with zero counts across all samples were excluded, and DESeq2’s default independent filtering (independentFiltering=TRUE) was applied during results extraction to automatically filter very low-abundance genes based on mean normalized counts (baseMean) to optimize detection power at the specified FDR level. The lack of FDR-significant genes may reflect limited statistical power under stringent multiple testing correction, as well as increased biological heterogeneity, both of which are known to reduce sensitivity in transcriptomic analyses [17]. To avoid missing biologically relevant signatures in the PPE cohort, we employed a more inclusive threshold of nominal p-value < 0.05 and fold change ≥ 2 for exploratory purposes, given that no features passed the multiple testing correction (FDR < 0.05). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with an absolute fold change ≥ 2 (|log2FC| ≥1) between the two groups, with a p-value < 0.05. These potentially differentially expressed genes were subjected to functional annotation to identify significantly enriched biological pathways. Functional enrichment and pathway analyses of DEGs were performed using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) and the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) v6.8, enabling the annotation of biological processes, molecular functions, and signaling pathways relevant to PPE progression. Using IPA, the top 12 canonical pathways with a -log10 p-value > 4.5 and a z-score > 1 were identified as the most significantly enriched and activated pathways, and the bar charts were plotted using GraphPad Prism. The top 12 significantly enriched KEGG pathways and biological processes (p < 0.01, fold enrichment > 2.5) were selected and visualized using bubble plots.

Human HL-60 promyelocytes were cultured in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (IMDM; Gibco, USA) containing 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C in a humid atmosphere containing 5% CO2. HL-60 cells were treated with 1.3% DMSO (Sigma, USA) and 1 nM all-trans-retinoic acid (ATRA; MEC, USA) for 24, 48, 72, 96 h to induce differentiation into neutrophil-like cells. The neutrophil-like cells were stained with Liu’s stain, and the characteristic morphological features of differentiated HL-60 cells were quantified using a light microscope. Human THP-1 monocytes were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (RPMI 1640; Gibco, USA) containing 10% FBS (Gibco, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C in a humid atmosphere containing 5% CO2. THP-1 cells were induced to differentiate into macrophages with 150 ng/ml phorbol myristoyl acetate (PMA; Sigma, USA) for 48 h. Murine Raw 264.7 macrophages were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, USA). The bone marrow cells of 8–10-week-old C57BL/6 mice were isolated and stained with FITC-conjugated anti–Gr-1 antibodies (clone RB6-8C5, BioLegend) and PE-conjugated anti–CD11b antibodies (clone M1/70, BioLegend), followed by cell sorting for Gr-1⁺CD11b⁺ cells. The murine primary neutrophils (Gr-1⁺CD11b+) were then cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (RPMI 1640; Gibco, USA) containing 10% FBS (Gibco, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C in a humid atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

2 × 106 THP-1 cells were cultured in a 6-well plate and then transfected with either a control plasmid or a wild-type HMGB2-Flag plasmid for 48 h using Lipofectamine® 2000 Reagent (Lipo2000, Invitroge, USA). The transfected THP-1 cells were then treated with 1–5 µg/ml peptidoglycan (PGN-SA, Invitrogen, USA) for 6 h, followed by RNA extraction from cell lysates. The RNA samples were subjected to qRT-PCR experiments. The culture supernatants were collected for ELISA assay. 1 × 107 HL-60 cells were transfected with either a control plasmid or a wild-type HMGB2-Flag plasmid in 100 µl resuspension buffer using Neon transfection system (Invitrogen, USA) and then cultured in 6-well plate with 2 ml RPMI/10%FBS for 48 h.

Commercial sandwich ELISA kits were used to measure the levels of HMGB2 (LifeSpan Biosciences, USA and Fine Biotech, China), IL-1β (BioLegend, USA), and IL-8 (BioLegend, USA) in pleural effusions or cell culture supernatants. The assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Total RNA was extracted from treated cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of RNA were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The 20 µl reverse transcription reaction contained 1 µg of total RNA, 4 µl of 5X RT buffer, 1 µl of oligo(dT) primer (50 µM), 1 µl of random hexamer primer (100 µM), 1 µl of reverse transcriptase, and nuclease-free water to a final volume of 20 µl. The reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by 85 °C for 5 s to inactivate the enzyme. The synthesized cDNA was stored at − 20 °C until further use in qRT-PCR experiments. The qRT-PCR reactions were prepared with 0.5 µl of nuclease-free water, 2 µl of cDNA (5 ng/µl), 5 µl of 2X SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche, Switzerland), and 2.5 µl of primer mix (0.8 µM). The qRT-PCR reactions carried out under the following cycling conditions: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 60 s. The primer sequences were as follows: human HMGB2 forward 5′- CACCATCTGCCTTCTTCC-3′, reverse 5′- TTTGG CTGACTGCTCAGAC-3′; human IL1β forward 5′- AGAAGAACCTATCTTCTTCGAC-3′, reverse 5′- ATATGGACCAGACATCACCAA-3′; human IL8 forward 5′-CCAGCTGTGTTGGTAGTG-3′, reverse 5′-ACAAGTTTCAACCAGCAAGA-3′; human EEF1A1 forward 5′-CAATGTGGGCTTCAATGTCAA-3′, reverse 5′-CATAGCCGGCGCTTATTTG − 3′; mouse Hmgb2 forward 5′-GACTGGTCTGGTCAAGTTG-3′, reverse 5′-GCGTCTGTCTACCTGCT-3′; mouse Il1β forward 5′-AGCTGGAGAGTGTGGAT-3′, reverse 5′-TACCAGTTGGGGAACTC-3′; mouse Il8 forward 5′-CCGTCCCTGTGACACTCAAG-3′, reverse 5′-ACAGAAGCTTCATTGCCGGT-3′; and mouse Actin forward 5′-CCTTCTTGGGTATGGAATCC-3′, reverse 5′-CAGGAGGAGCAATGATCTTG-3′. EEF1A1 was used as the reference gene for human cell lines. Actin was used as the reference gene for murine cell lines. ΔCt for each sample was calculated by subtracting the Ct value of the reference gene from that of the target gene. The relative quantification was then determined as the fold change using the formula 2^-ΔCt.

Pleural tissue biopsies for histopathological analysis were collected from 4 patients with non-severe and 6 patients with severe PPE who underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgical decortication (VATSD). The pleural tissues were formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE), and tissue sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Tissue Sect.  (5-µm thick) were subjected to antigen retrieval using Bond Epitope Retrieval Solution 2 in a Bond-Max automated immunostainer (Leica Biosystems) and stained with antibodies against HMGB2 (Abcam, ab11973, UK). The procedures were performed in accordance with standard protocols.

Streptococcus pneumoniae (TIGR4, ATCC BAA-334) was grown on blood agar plates (Becton Dickinson, USA). The growth of S. pneumoniae was determined by optical density at OD600 = 0.2 ~ 0.3, and the bacteria were refreshed for 2 to 3 h in Todd Hewitt Broth (Becton Dickinson, USA). The bacteria were suspended in IMDM containing 5% FBS, and their growth was monitored by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀ = 0.6). HL-60 cells, THP-1 cells, or primary murine neutrophils were infected with S. pneumoniae at the indicated concentrations (MOI: 2, 10, 20) for 1–3 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2. RNA samples were extracted from treated cells and subsequently subjected to qRT-PCR analysis.

Cell lysates were prepared and subjected to 8% to 12% denaturing SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and the separated proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membranes were blocked with 5% milk in TBST (0.1% Tween 20) at room temperature for 1 h. Then, the membranes were incubated with antibodies against HMGB2 (Abcam, ab11973) and GAPDH (Bioworkd, ap 0063) at 4 °C overnight. After washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse (NEF822001EA) or anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (NEF812001EA) at room temperature for 1 h. Bands were visualized with ECL (Millipore) and exposed to X-ray films. The immunoblot images were analysed by ImageJ software.

Differentiated HL-60 neutrophils (~ 1 × 10⁶ cells) were suspended in 100 µL of staining buffer (1× PBS with 2% BSA) containing 1 µL of FITC-conjugated anti-CD11b antibody (clone M1/70, BioLegend) and incubated at 4 °C for 15 min. The cells were washed with 1× PBS containing 2% (w/v) BSA and resuspended in 500 µL of staining buffer. The cells were acquired using an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and analyzed with FlowJo software (Tree Star, USA).

Raw 264.7 macrophages (2.5 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates and co-transfected with an NF-κB luciferase reporter and Renilla plasmids, along with either a control vector or HMGB2-Flag plasmid, for 48 h using Lipofectamine 2000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, the cells were treated with 10 µg/ml bacterial-derived peptidoglycan (PGN) for 24 h. After the treatment, we lysed cells with reporter lysis buffer (Promega, USA), added equal volumes of luciferase substrate to each well, and detected the luciferase activity using the GloMax 20/20 Luminometer (Promega, USA). Luciferase activity was normalized to the transfection efficiency by determining the Renilla luciferase activity generated from a co-transfected Renilla expression vector.

All statistical data are expressed as the mean ± s.e.m. Between-group comparisons were performed with Mann-Whiteny U test or two-tailed Student’s t test. Multiple group comparisons were performed with 1-way ANOVA Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. The levels of target proteins in pleural effusions were compared and plotted using GraphPad Prism. ROC curves were generated by plotting sensitivity versus 1-specificity. The area under the curve (AUC) was reported with the 95% confidence interval to estimate diagnostic usefulness. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was applied to examine the correlation among the parameters. All statistical tests were performed using SPSS software version 20 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) or GraphPad Prism. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The RNA sequencing data generated in this study have been deposited in the NIH Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject accession number PRJNA1357382.

For the discovery cohort, 26 PPE patients were enrolled for RNA sequencing, with their clinical characteristics and pleural biochemical profiles detailed in Table 1. The mean ages of the patients were 64.9 ± 6.8 years in the non-severe PPE group (UPPE, n = 9) and 60.4 ± 5.2 years in the severe PPE group (CPPE/empyema, n = 17), respectively. Blood levels of pH and glucose were significantly lower in severe PPE patients when compared with those in non-severe PPE patients, while LDH levels were significantly higher. No significant differences of blood protein levels and the number of white blood cells, such as neutrophils, lymphocytes, and mesothelial cells, were observed between non-severe and severe groups of pneumonia patients.

To identify disease-associated signatures and potential biomarkers in severe PPE, we performed capture-based RNA sequencing of pleural effusion cells as indicated in Fig. 1 to reveal stage-specific molecular pathways driving disease progression, as well as comparing with those observed in non-severe PPE. The transcriptomes of 26 patients with PPE, including non-severe PPE and severe PPE, were analysed using bulk RNA sequencing. Our PPE transcriptome analysis revealed 31,459 genes per sample; the mean read count was 46.9 million. Differential expression analysis was performed to compare cellular gene expression between severe and non-severe PPE. Genes exhibiting a ≥ 2-fold change in expression with a p-value < 0.05 in severe PPE relative to non-severe PPE were defined as differential expression genes (DEGs). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed significant differences in severe PPE and non-severe PPE transcriptome profiles (supplementary Fig. 1). The volcano plot was generated by plotting the log₂-transformed fold changes in gene expression against the –log₁₀-transformed nominal p-values to compare severe and non-severe PPE (Fig. 2A). We found that the respective 232 and 212 genes were upregulated (log2 fold change ≥ 1, nominal p-value < 0.05) and downregulated (log2 fold change ≤ 1, nominal p-value < 0.05) in the severe PPE group compared with the non-severe group (Fig. 2A). To further investigate the biological relevance of the 232 upregulated genes identified in severe PPE relative to non-severe PPE, we performed Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) to analyse canonical pathway (Fig. 2B). We found that neutrophil degranulation (gene count = 30, -log10 p-value = 16.1, z-score = 5.47), glycolysis (gene count = 7, -log10 p-value = 8.71, z-score = 2.44), and HIF-1α signaling (gene count = 12, -log10 p-value = 6.35, z-score = 2.33) were the top enriched pathways predicted to be activated in severe PPE relative to non-severe PPE (Fig. 2B). Consistent with the IPA results, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis — visualized via a bubble plot where numerical gene counts are labeled within each circle — revealed that glycolysis (gene count = 8, p < 0.001, fold enrichment = 8.86), HIF-1 signaling (gene count = 9, p < 0.001, fold enrichment = 6.07), autophagy (gene count = 9, p < 0.01, fold enrichment = 3.95), NF-κB signaling (gene count = 7, p < 0.01, fold enrichment = 4.94), TNF signaling (gene count = 7, p < 0.01, fold enrichment = 4.36), and necroptosis (gene count = 8, p < 0.01, fold enrichment = 3.73) were the significantly enriched pathways in severe PPE (Fig. 2C). In addition, the Gene ontology (GO) analysis demonstrated that glycolytic process (gene count = 10, p < 0.001), inflammatory response (gene count = 20, p < 0.001), and response to hypoxia (gene count = 13, p < 0.001) were significantly enriched in severe PPE (Fig. 2D, Supplemental Table 1). Conversely, GO analysis of the 212 downregulated genes in severe PPE revealed a significant downregulation of complement activation pathways (Supplemental Table 2).

We also found that several upregulated differential expression genes in severe PPE belonged to the category of extracellular space in the cellular component pathway analysis (data not shown). The finding suggests that these dysregulated proteins may be secreted from cells to the extracellular space. Previously, we generated a secretome dataset from PPE patients using LC–MS/MS, which identified 45 proteins significantly upregulated in severe PPE [13]. To systematically identify novel contributors to PPE progression and candidate biomarkers, we integrated transcriptome dataset (232 upregulated genes) with secretome dataset (45 upregulated proteins) from severe versus non-severe PPE. We identified two potential candidates—HMGB2 and ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) — that were consistently highly expressed in patients with severe PPE across both transcriptome and secretome datasets (Fig. 4A). As HMGB2 is an HMGB family member participated in physiological processes of DNA repair, cell death, and damage-associated molecular pattern signalling in inflammatory responses [18, 19], we visualized HMGB2-associated networks in severe PPE, focusing on inflammatory response pathway (GO:0006954) in which HMGB2 is involved (Supplemental Table 1). Among the 20 upregulated genes in the inflammatory response pathway, HMGB2, CXCL8 (IL8), and IL1β showed potential associations in GeneMANIA analysis (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, pleural HMGB2 levels were integrated with 10 upregulated cytokines identified from our previously published cytokine profiling dataset, which measured 40 proteins using Bio-Plex multiplex assays in PPE patients [15]. Interestingly, two cytokines, CXCL8 (IL-8) and IL-1β, showed the higher positive correlation coefficients with HMGB2 in the correlation analysis (Fig. 4C). These results may highlight the role of HMGB2 in mediating CXCL8 (IL-8) and IL-1β cytokine production. The expression of HMGB2 protein was then assessed by immunohistochemistry in pleural effusion samples from patients with severe (n = 6) and non-severe (n = 4) PPE (Fig. 4D), the results showed that HMGB2 protein was significantly higher in severe than in non-severe PPE (Fig. 4D).

To further validate HMGB2 as a potential biomarker, we quantified its protein levels in a large cohort of 220 patients using ELISA assays. In the validation cohort, a total of 220 patients with different pleural effusion etiologies were included and classified in two approaches. First, patients were divided into two groups: 120 non-PPE patients, including other exudates, transudates, and malignant pleural effusions, and 100 PPE patients, comprising both non-severe and severe PPE. The pleural fluid concentrations of HMGB2 protein were 72.43 ± 5.15 pg/mL in the non-PPE group and 536.90 ± 68.58 pg/mL in the PPE group (mean ± s.e.m.) (p < 0.0001), demonstrating a significant elevation of HMGB2 levels in PPE compared with non-PPE (Fig. 4E). Second, patients were further categorized into five subgroups: other exudates (n = 40), transudates (n = 40), malignant pleural effusions (n = 40), non-severe PPE (n = 50), and severe PPE (n = 50). Notably, HMGB2 protein levels were significantly higher in severe PPE (876.20 ± 117.1 pg/mL) than in non-severe PPE (206.20 ± 24.23 pg/mL) (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4F). We then characterized the diagnostic value of HMGB2 for severe PPE and other pleural effusions. We found that the area under the curve (AUC) value for distinguishing severe PPE from other pleural effusion PPE was 0.93 (p < 0.0001) for pleural HMGB2 (Fig. 4G). The diagnostic performance of pleural HMGB2, LDH, and glucose levels in distinguishing severe PPE from other PEs was calculated (Supplementary Table 5). Pleural LDH and glucose levels are established as conventional markers for diagnosing PPE; specifically, LDH > 1000 U/L and glucose < 60 mg/dL are typically used as criteria to define severe PPE (including CPPE and empyema). At an HMGB2 cutoff value of 155 pg/mL, the sensitivity was 94.0% and the specificity was 84.7%. In comparison, using a pleural fluid LDH cutoff of > 1000 U/L yielded a sensitivity of 74.0% and a specificity of 94.7%. Similarly, a pleural fluid glucose level < 60 mg/dL showed a sensitivity of 76.0% and a specificity of 94.1%. Regarding diagnostic performance, HMGB2 yielded a higher sensitivity (94.0%) than both LDH and glucose, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for identifying severe PPE. In addition, pleural HMGB2 protein levels were observed in PPE patients to positively correlate with pleural LDH levels (r = 0.32, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4H).

When PPE occurs in pneumonia patients, innate immune cells, such as neutrophils and macrophages, are recruited and activated. Thus, we assessed whether HMGB2 levels change during neutrophils activation (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). First, HL60 cells were differentiated into neutrophil-like cells (dHL-60) under ATRA and DMSO stimulation for four days. The cell morphology and the percentage of differentiated HL-60 cells were determined by morphological observation using Liu’s staining (Supplementary Fig. 3). HL60 cells were induced to differentiate into neutrophil-like cells, and samples were collected daily from day 1 to day 4 of differentiation for analysis of HMGB2 expression. The protein and mRNA levels of HMGB2 were examined by Western blotting and qRT-PCR, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 4B). Our data showed that HMGB2 expression progressively increased during HL60 cell differentiation, indicating that HMGB2 upregulation may be associated with neutrophil maturation and functional activation. To further link the association of HMGB2 to neutrophil differentiation, HL-60 cells were transfected with either a control or HMGB2-Flag plasmid for 48 h, followed by ATRA/DMSO stimulation. The efficiency of HMGB2 overexpression was confirmed by qRT-PCR 48 h post-transfection (Supplementary Fig. 4C). As CD11b, an integrin αM subunit (also known as ITGAM), is a well-established marker of neutrophil differentiation and plays a critical role in mediating cell adhesion, migration, and phagocytosis during immune responses, we assessed whether CD11b protein expression is altered during differentiation of neutrophils. Indeed, differentiated neutrophils expressed higher CD11b protein levels than undifferentiated neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. 4D). Our data also showed that transient overexpression of HMGB2 enhanced the CD11b protein level.

S. pneumoniae is a gram-positive opportunistic pathogen that colonizes the upper respiratory tract of humans and is the common cause of pneumonia with PPEs. When its infection occurs in pneumonia patients to cause the progression from UPPE to severe PPE, innate immune cells, such as neutrophils and macrophages, are the first immune cells to be recruited and activated. Thus, we used HL60 neutrophil-like cells, murine primary neutrophils, and human THP1 macrophages to assess the possibility that S. pneumoniae infection may alter HMGB2 expression in neutrophils and macrophages (Fig. 5). We found that HMGB2 mRNA levels were significantly increased when differentiated HL60 cells were infected with S. pneumoniae at various MOIs (2–20) for 1 h (Fig. 5A). In addition, the mRNA levels of the inflammatory cytokines IL1β and IL8 significantly increased in S. pneumoniae-infected dHL60 cells (Fig. 5A). In different experiments using murine primary neutrophils, infection with 2 ~ 20 MOIs of S. pneumoniae also activated Hmgb2, Il1β, and Il8 mRNA expression (Fig. 5B). When differentiated human THP1 macrophages were infected with 2 and 10 MOIs of S. pneumoniae for 3 h, all HMGB2, IL1β, and IL8 mRNA levels were significantly increased after infection (Fig. 5C). These results suggest that S. pneumoniae infection can induce HMGB2, IL8, and IL1β gene expression in neutrophils and macrophages.

Elevated inflammatory cytokine production in bacterial pneumonia is crucial for the induction phase of the immune response. However, an exaggerated systemic release of cytokines in the acute phase can cause a cytokine storm, leading to tissue damage and multiorgan failure. However, no reports have demonstrated the expression and regulation of HMGB2 in PPEs and its association with the production of inflammatory cytokines. Accordingly, we investigated whether HMGB2 overexpression promotes inflammatory cytokine production upon bacterial infection in macrophages (Fig. 6). First, we confirmed the overexpression of HMGB2 mRNA in THP-1 macrophages transfected with an HMGB2-Flag plasmid for 48 h using a qRT–PCR assay (Fig. 6A). When compared with control cells transfected with empty vector, HMGB2-overexpressing THP-1 cells exhibited significantly higher levels of IL1β and IL8 mRNAs upon PGN stimulation (Fig. 6B). Consistently, the levels of IL-1β and IL-8 proteins in the culture medium were significantly higher in HMGB2-overexpressing THP-1 cells than in control cells following PGN stimulation. (Fig. 6C). Next, we examined whether HMGB2 enhanced NF-κB promoter activity in macrophages upon PGN stimulation, thereby promoting the production of IL-1β and IL-8. Cells were co-transfected with either control or HMGB2-Flag plasmids along with an NF-κB luciferase reporter plasmid for 48 h. Then, the transfected cells were stimulated with PGN for 24 h, followed by the luciferase activity assay. Compared with control cells, NF-κB promoter activity was significantly increased in HMGB2-overexpressing cells following PGN stimulation (Fig. 6D). Our results indicate that S. pneumoniae infection stimulates HMGB2 expression in neutrophils and macrophages, and that HMGB2 promotes inflammatory cytokine production possibly through activation of NF-κB (Fig. 6E).

In this study, we attempted to identify molecular signatures of pneumonia patients with severe PPE in relation to physiopathological pathways. Pneumonia patients with severe PPE revealed a significant upregulation of 232 genes expression when compared to those subjects with non-severe group. In addition, these transcriptomic alterations indicate enhanced activation of immune (e.g., CXCL8, CCL20, IL1β) and metabolic (e.g., GPI, PFKFB3, PGK1, HK2) pathways in response to the more advanced disease state. Notably, neutrophil-mediated immune responses appear to play a central role, particularly through mechanisms such as neutrophil activation and degranulation. Degranulation involves the release of antimicrobial peptides, proteolytic enzymes, and other effector molecules, which contribute to both pathogen clearance and the amplification of inflammation [20]. Glucose metabolic pathways, such as GPI, PFKFB3, TPI1, PGK1, ALDOC, ALDOA, ENO2, GAPDH, HK2, IER3, are simultaneously upregulated to meet the heightened energy demands of immune cells. These pathways include glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, which provide rapid ATP production and confer metabolic flexibility. Our findings were consistent with those reports indicating that neutrophils depended on gluconeogenesis and glycogenesis to generate intracellular glycogen stores, which are critical for their survival and antimicrobial activity under metabolically restrictive conditions [21]. Notably, Kanellakis et al. also reported that pleural infection patients exhibit diverse responses of neutrophil-mediated immunity, glycolysis and pentose phosphate activation [22].

As the infection progresses, both hypoxia and oxidative stress responses are triggered, including BNIP3, SOD2, and MYB. In severe PPE, the HIF-α signaling pathway (e.g., ERO1A, BNIP3L, BNIP3, HILPDA, PGK1, ADAM8, PTGS2, NDRG1, VEGFA) was found to be enriched (Fig. 2B and C), suggesting a role in facilitating cellular adaptation to hypoxic conditions and promoting metabolic reprogramming. HIF-1 is a key transcription factor that regulates the cellular response to low oxygen environments. Beyond its classical role in maintaining oxygen homeostasis, HIF-1 plays a crucial role in the immune system by modulating immune cell metabolism and function under both hypoxic and normoxic conditions. In inflamed or infected tissues—where hypoxia is commonly observed—HIF-1 enhances the antimicrobial activity of macrophages, neutrophils, and other immune cells. Emerging evidence further indicates that HIF-1 activation may exert either protective or detrimental effects during infections, depending on the specific context and pathogen involved [23, 24]. Concurrently, the inflammatory cytokine network is activated, with signals from cytokine-producing pathways contributing to local inflammation [25]. In severe PPE, we also observed enrichment of pathogen-induced cytokine storm signaling (Fig. 2B), reflecting amplification of the immune response; however, excessive cytokine release can result in tissue damage [26]. Mechanistically, TNF signaling further increases vascular and pleural permeability, promoting exudative effusion formation and immune cell infiltration [27]. In parallel, enrichment of necroptosis-related pathways suggests the involvement of regulated necrotic cell death, resulting in the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and amplification of inflammation [28]. These processes collectively contribute to fibrin deposition, pleural thickening, and loculation.

Collectively, these findings support a model in which disease severity in PPE is driven by a sequential and self-reinforcing immunometabolic cascade. In the initial phase, inflammatory cytokine signaling orchestrates immune activation and shapes the local inflammatory milieu. As pleural infection progresses, inflammatory exudate accumulation, immune cell infiltration, and impaired fluid drainage collectively establish a hypoxic pleural microenvironment, which serves as a critical driver of disease progression and promotes HIF-1 activation [29, 30]. In response to hypoxic stress, HIF-1–dependent signaling induces metabolic reprogramming, enhancing glycolytic flux and functionally coupling oxygen sensing to immune activation and inflammatory amplification [31]. Importantly, this shift is closely associated with neutrophil metabolic reprogramming, enabling sustained effector functions, including degranulation, ROS production, and cytokine release, particularly under hypoxic and inflammatory conditions [32, 33]. These interconnected processes ultimately form a self-amplifying feedback loop, in which inflammatory cytokine signaling, hypoxia, and metabolic adaptation synergistically sustain immune activation and drive persistent inflammatory escalation. This reinforcing cycle provides a mechanistic framework linking microenvironmental stress to neutrophil functional persistence and progressive disease severity in PPE.

Pneumonia patients with empyema had 76 higher and 35 lower than those with CPPE and the functional pathway annotations from upregulated genes indicated neutrophils involved in the regulation of both intracellular mechanisms (e.g., pyroptosis signaling) and extracellular defenses (e.g., neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation). Pyroptosis represents an inflammatory programmed cell death characterized by caspase-1 activation and gasdermin D–mediated membrane pore formation, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory mediators that amplify the local immune response [34]. Concurrently, NET formation facilitates immobilization and killing of extracellular bacteria through chromatin extrusion; however, excessive NETs contribute to tissue damage, endothelial dysfunction, and thrombotic complications [35, 36]. Enrichment of the macrophage classical activation and interferon signaling pathways was observed in empyema (Fig. 3B), suggesting a prominent pro-inflammatory immune activation. Classically activated macrophages (M1) are induced by interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and bacterial components such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and play a pivotal role in host defense against intracellular pathogens by producing high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines [37, 38]. This M1 polarization is essential for effective pathogen clearance, but excessive or prolonged activation may contribute to tissue damage and disease exacerbation [37, 38]. In parallel, interferon signaling pathways—including both type I (IFN-α/β) and type II (IFN-γ) responses—were significantly upregulated in empyema (Fig. 3B), underscoring their central role in shaping innate and adaptive immunity. IFN-γ enhances the antimicrobial capacity of macrophages and promotes antigen presentation via MHC class I and II pathways [39, 40]. Type I interferons activate a broad spectrum of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), many of which exert direct or indirect antibacterial effects [41, 42]. Notably, ISGylation—a type I IFN-induced post-translational modification—was also enhanced in empyema. This modification regulates protein stability and modulates downstream signaling cascades involved in immune defense [43]. Together, these findings suggest that the combined activation of interferon and macrophage classical activation signaling contributes to a potent but potentially tissue-damaging immune response in empyema. Notably, significant activation of the cytokine storm signaling pathway was observed in empyema. Cytokine storm, characterized by excessive and dysregulated release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, is a hallmark of systemic inflammatory responses during severe bacterial infections. This aberrant immune activation is implicated in extensive tissue injury, increased vascular permeability, and subsequent multiorgan dysfunction [44]. Collectively, these signaling pathways represent critical nodes in the host immune response to severe bacterial infection. Their coordinated activation ensures effective pathogen clearance; however, their dysregulation underlies the pathogenesis of immune-mediated tissue injury and systemic complications, underscoring the importance of tightly regulated immune homeostasis in determining infection outcomes.

Following integration of PPE transcriptome with secretome profiling, this study highlighted HMGB2 and FTH1 as potential biomarkers for disease severity in pneumonia patients with PPE. FTH1 is frequently upregulated during the inflammatory response, a change that restricts iron availability to pathogens and mitigates oxidative stress, thereby implicating FTH1 in host immune defense and contributing to disease severity [45]. Serum ferritin is a well-known marker of inflammation [46]. Hyperferritinaemia is being increasingly recognized as a marker of critical illness [47, 48]. Ferritin is increased in children with septic shock, and high ferritin levels are associated with poorer outcomes [49]. HMGB2 was reported to possess multiple biological functions. It can function as a potential diagnostic marker and therapeutic target for liver fibrosis and cirrhosis [50], regulate the differentiation and stemness of exhausted CD8+ T cells during chronic viral infection and cancer [51], and associate with inflammatory processes [18, 19]. Despite the demonstrations that HMGB2 modulated cellular processes of immune cells in association with inflammation, the functional roles of HMGB2 in lung inflammation or pneumonia with PPE are still unclear. In this study, HMGB2 was identified as a specific biomarker capable of distinguishing severe PPE from other pleural effusions. It was evident that HMGB2 expression was significantly elevated in severe PPE compared with other pleural effusions and was associated with increased levels of IL-8, IL-1β, and LDH. LDH is a glycolytic enzyme involved in carbohydrate metabolism and serves as a nonspecific marker of cellular injury and inflammation [52]. In pleural effusions, elevated LDH levels reflect increased vascular permeability or tissue damage, and changes in its levels can act as one of the criteria for CPPE [53] and a marker for indication of intense local inflammatory activity [54].

In support of these findings, our cellular experiments indicated that transient overexpression of HMGB2 enhanced IL-1β and IL-8 expression in PGN-stimulated macrophages. Furthermore, S. pneumoniae infection in neutrophils and macrophages induced HMGB2, IL1β, and IL8 expression. Notably, S. pneumoniae can translocate from the parenchyma to the pleural space via visceral mesothelial cells, thereby stimulating inflammatory cell and cytokine responses associated with pleural infection [55]. In addition, the acute inflammatory response is marked by the formation of pleural effusions containing abundant inflammatory cells in pneumonia patients with PPEs in associations with the bacterial infection and the recruitments and activation of neutrophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes from the circulation into the pleural cavity. The dysregulated immune response can lead to a hyperinflammatory condition (cytokine storm); excessive inflammation leads to dysregulated immune cell activation and may also lead to organ dysfunction. Understanding the cytokine profile or immune-associated gene signatures in pneumonia patients with PPEs may be helpful in disease diagnosis, severity assessments, and mortality prediction, thereby better informing the therapeutic approach. Accordingly, our findings suggest that HMGB2 is an important regulator in the inflammatory process of PPEs and that an increased HMGB2 expression may contribute to cytokine production in pneumonia patients.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the transcriptomic analysis was conducted in a relatively small discovery cohort (PPE patients = 26), which may limit statistical power. However, this limitation was partially mitigated by subsequent validation in an independent cohort of 220 patients with different pleural effusion etiologies, which supports the robustness and clinical relevance of the identified biomarker. Second, although predefined inclusion criteria were applied, the potential for selection bias cannot be completely excluded. In addition, underlying comorbid conditions were present in a subset of patients and may have influenced host immune responses and gene expression profiles. Third, most patients received empirical antibiotic therapy prior to pleural fluid sampling, reflecting real-world clinical practice. While samples were generally obtained at an early stage of clinical evaluation, antibiotic exposure was not standardized and may have affected inflammatory and transcriptomic signatures. As antibiotic administration was clinically indicated and not randomized, its impact could not be fully controlled in this study. Finally, the use of cell line models represents a limitation of this study, as they may not fully recapitulate the complexity of in vivo conditions. In the confined pleural space, sustained neutrophil activation may establish a self-amplifying inflammatory loop that exacerbates tissue injury and perpetuates local immune activation. Our previous studies have shown that PPE severity is associated with elevated IL-6, IL-8, and IL-1β levels, reflecting a neutrophil-dominant inflammatory milieu that drives pleural injury and disease progression [14]. In this study, HL-60–derived neutrophil-like cells were used to investigate the mechanistic role of HMGB2 in neutrophil-associated inflammatory signaling. Although this model does not fully recapitulate the biology of primary pleural neutrophils, it is widely used and provides a reproducible platform for mechanistic analysis. Our findings suggest that HMGB2 participates in regulating neutrophil-like inflammatory pathways consistent with the cytokine environment observed in severe PPE, thereby supporting its relevance in pleural inflammation. To further validate the physiological relevance of these findings, future studies incorporating primary PBMCs or primary neutrophils will be necessary to confirm these observations in more physiologically relevant systems.

We conclude that pneumonia patients with severe PPEs have 232 higher and 212 lower genes than those with non-severe PPEs. The HMGB2 stimulation of IL-1β and IL-8 protein expression in PGN-stimulated macrophages is likely mediated through activation of NFκB pathway. Moreover, bioinformatic pathway analysis indicated that the progression of PPE in pneumonia patients is accompanied by the formation of a distinct pleural microenvironment as characterized by dynamic cross-talk among immune cells, inflammatory cytokines, glucose metabolism, and HIF-1 signaling pathways (Fig. 7). These results may offer valuable insights for early diagnosis, risk stratification, and the development of targeted therapies to prevent PPE progression in patients with pneumonia.
