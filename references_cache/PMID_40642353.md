PURPOSE: Human leukocyte antigen-A29 (HLA-A29) birdshot retinochoroiditis (BRC) 
is a noninfectious uveitis affecting independently the retina and the choroid. 
While the choroidal involvement is characterized by a stromal choroiditis, the 
retinal features of BRC were less well defined until a recent study determined a 
specific pattern of retinal involvement with seven features including profuse 
posterior retinal leakage, thick fluorescein angiography sheathing/staining, 
profuse disc hyperfluorescence, macular edema with foveal sparing, and 
circulatory arteriovenous pseudo delay. The aim of this study was to determine 
whether the specific pattern of retinal involvement was diagnostic for BRC 
distinguishing it from other causes of retinal vasculitis.
METHODS: A retrospective comparative study comparing patients diagnosed with BRC 
to patients with other causes of retinal vasculitis. A score based on seven 
retinal features was calculated. The maximum possible score was 16. If the score 
reached ≥ 9, the vasculitis was considered to be compatible with BRC.
RESULTS: Eighteen BRC and 18 non-BRC vasculitis were included in the study. The 
mean age of BRC patients was 49.5 ± 9.1 years, 11/18 females and 7/18 males 
(female 61%/male 39%). HLA-A29 antigen was present in all patients (100%). The 
mean age of patients in the control group with non-BRC vasculitis was 37 ± 17, 
11/18 females and 7/18 males (female 61%/male 39%). The control group included 
nine cases of sarcoidosis chorioretinitis, six cases of intermediate uveitis of 
the pars planitis type, two cases of Behçet uveitis, and one case of 
tuberculosis chorioretinitis. The mean scores for the BRC group were 10.5 ± 1.7, 
while in the control group, it was 3.9 ± 2.8 (P < 0.0001, t-test). Using 
Fisher's exact test, the probability of the scores ≥ 9 to correspond to BRC was 
significantly different and higher than the scores of the group of non-BRC 
vasculitis (P < 0.0001). The positive predictive value was high amounting to 
88.9%.
CONCLUSION: Retinal vasculitis related to BRC has specific findings leading, if 
present, to the diagnosis of BRC that should be ascertained by the search of 
indocyanine green angiography lesions with the ultimate confirmation by the 
presence of the HLA-A29 antigen.

Human leukocyte antigen-A29 (HLA-A29) birdshot retinochoroiditis (BRC) is a bilateral predominantly posterior uveitis characterized by dual albeit independent retinal vasculitis/retinitis and choroiditis.[1] Early descriptions of the disease focused on the typical rice-shaped, cream-colored, depigmented choroidal fundus lesions called “birdshot lesions” in one of the initial articles and vitiliginous chorioretinitis in the other.[23] Thus, the choroidal depigmented lesions were considered the hallmark of the disease and represented the main point of attention for many years, being classified in the group of primary stromal choroiditis entities.[4] This was reinforced when indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) became available showing the typical hypofluorescent dark dots (HDDs) which became a key element in early diagnosis as HDDs precede the apparition of clinical “birdshot lesions.”[567] Following the preponderant importance taken by ICGA and choroidal lesions, the retinal component of the disease remained somewhat in the background after initial fluorescein angiography (FA) descriptions in early articles by Ryan and Maumenee, Gass, and Kaplan and Aaberg.[238] Ryan and Maumenee described profuse retinal leakage including capillaries and macular edema and even thought to name the disease diffuse capillary vasculopathy but then put forward the choroidal component and called the disease birdshot retinochoroidopathy.[2] Six months later, Kaplan and Aaberg published on a group of four patients and also described diffuse retinal vascular leakage and macular edema.[8] Finally, another 10 months later, Gass published his article entitled “Vitiliginous Chorioretinitis,” giving a more detailed overview on retinal involvement.[3] He described macular edema in most of his 11 cases and pronounced retinal capillary leakage. However, more interestingly, he described increased retinal circulation time, which we now know not to be real circulatory delay but pseudo delay due to the massive fluorescein exudation not reaching veins in sufficient quantity to mark the vessels.[9]

Although these articles described some characteristics of retinal vasculitis found in BRC, none of them comprehensively analyzed BRC retinal vasculitis and thereafter retinal vasculitis continued to be neglected. We found only one study systematically analyzing retinal vasculitis in BRC that showed that thick FA sheathing/staining of large posterior vessels, called “contiguous, perineural retinal vascular leakage” by the authors, had a good positive predictive value (PPV) for BRC.[10] Another recent article reported disc edema in all BRC patients with profuse disc hyperfluorescence.[11]

We recently published the first detailed study, to the best of our knowledge, on retinal vasculitis in BRC.[12] We have shown the crucial importance that the appraisal of retinal vasculitis in BRC represents possibly indicating that the pattern of vasculitis in BRC potentially has a strong diagnostic value. We identified seven particular vasculitis features which, together, appeared to have a diagnostic potential for BRC.

The aim of this study was to assess whether the pattern of BRC retinal vasculitis described[12] could be statistically associated with BRC by comparing BRC vasculitis cases to nonbirdshot vasculitis cases.

In our previously published article,[12] FA characteristics of BRC vasculitis comprised the following features: (1) profuse posterior retinal exudation in 81% of cases [Figure 1], (2) macular edema in 81% of cases with (3) foveolar sparing in 82% of cases presenting macular edema [Figure 2], (4) thick FA sheathing/staining of large posterior pole vessels in 62% of cases [Figure 3], (5) profuse disc hyperfluorescence in 100% of cases [Figure 1], (6) a specific feature was the so-called arteriovenous pseudocirculatory delay found in 81% of cases [Figure 4], and (7) A less frequently found but remarkable feature was the presence of thick vitreous strands in 37% of cases [Figure 5]. In this retrospective comparative study, the FA scores of patients diagnosed with BRC and non-BRC were compared.

Inclusion criteria for BRC comprised typical HDDs on ICGA and HLA-A29 positivity. Inclusion criteria of non-BRC are mentioned for each entity in the list of non-BRC cases in the result section. A score was calculated by attributing 2 points, whether present or not, to profuse leakage, macular edema, foveal sparing, profuse disc hyperfluorescence, and thick vitreous strands. Three points were attributed to circulatory pseudo delay and thick FA sheathing/staining as these features were considered more specific findings for BRC vasculitis. The maximum possible score was 16. If the score reached ≥ 9, the vasculitis was considered to be compatible with BRC [Table 1]. The cutoff point of 9 was chosen arbitrarily, based on the authors’ preference, as it had shown the optimal yield in previous studies.[1213]

Scoring of birdshot retinochoroiditis vasculitis pattern

A group of 18 BRC patients and a control group of 18 nonbirdshot vasculitis cases were evaluated according to the BRC vasculitis scoring system including the seven features listed in Table 1. The number of scores ≥ 9 in the BRC group was compared to a group of 18 non-BRC vasculitis patients with a known diagnosis. Scores were attributed on the basis of the presence or not of the features. Evaluation of FA features was performed by two independent investigators not knowing the diagnosis and was averaged among them. The numbers of scores ≥ 9 in both groups were compared statistically using Fisher’s exact test, and the sensitivity, specificity, and PPV were calculated.

Eighteen patients following the exposed strict BRC inclusion criteria were assigned to the birdshot group. The mean age of patients was 49.5 ± 9.1 years, including 11/18 females and 7/18 males (female 61%/male 39%). HLA-A29 antigen was present in all patients (100%, inclusion criterion).

Eighteen patients diagnosed with vasculitis entities, excluding BRC were assigned to the control group. The mean age of patients in the control group was 37 ± 17 including 11/18 females and 7/18 males (female 61%/male 39%). The control group included nine cases of sarcoidosis chorioretinitis diagnosed following the IWOS criteria,[14] six cases of intermediate uveitis of the pars planitis type (bilateral vitritis, retinal vasculitis, snowballs and snowbanks, absence of synechiae, and negative uveitis workup), two cases of Behçet uveitis (bilateral retinal vasculitis and recurrent oral aphthae), and one case of tuberculosis (TB) chorioretinitis (compatible chorioretinitis with positive QuantiFERON test that responded to anti-TB therapy).

The scores of patients in both the groups are indicated in Table 2. The mean scores for the BRC group were 10.5 ± 1.7, significantly higher than in the control group, amounting to 3.9 ± 2.8 (P < 0.0001, t-test). Comparing the two groups using Fisher’s exact test, the probability that the scores ≥ 9 corresponded to BRC was significantly different and higher than the scores of the group of non-BRC vasculitis (P < 0.0001).

In the BRC group, there were 16 true positives and 2 false negatives. In the control group, there were 16 true negatives and 2 false positives. Thus, the BRC pattern of vasculitis had a sensitivity of 44.4%, a specificity of 88.9%, and a PPV of 88.9% to correspond to HLA-A29 BRC.

It is well established that BRC presents dual independent retinitis and choroiditis.[13] Retinal involvement in BRC, in particular retinal vasculitis, has been cited in most articles on BRC but has, to the best of our knowledge, never been addressed comprehensively, except in a recent article that focused on one feature of BRC retinal vasculitis, thick FA sheathing/staining of retinal vessels which the authors called “FA contiguous, perineural retinal vascular leakage.” Another fact that has been published was the profuse disc hyperfluorescence indicating that disc oedema was a prominent feature in BRC.[11] In a recent study, we wanted to go further and study retinal vasculitis in BRC globally. We identified seven FA features found in BRC vasculitis, including some less specific signs such as profuse posterior pole leakage, macular edema, profuse disc hyperfluorescence, and thick vitreous strands, as well as more specific signs such as foveolar sparing of macular edema, circulatory pseudo delay, and FA thick sheathing/staining of large posterior vessels.[12] Having identified these features, our aim in this study was to identify whether this pattern of vasculitis could be associated with BRC in a statistically significant fashion. Comparing a group of 18 BRC cases to a control group of 18 non-BRC retinal vasculitis, the probability of a given “BRC” vasculitis pattern to be able to diagnose BRC was statistically highly significant using Fisher’s exact test. The positive predictive (PPV) of such a vasculitis pattern was high for HLA-A29 BRC.

Our results put retinitis in the center of clinical investigation of BRC rather than choroidal signs. For many years, the choroidal clinical signs were in the center of attention probably because they were very demonstrative. However, when “birdshot choroidal fundus lesions” are noted, this means that the disease is already fairly advanced in its chronic stage characterized by irreversible lesions.[15] As for Vogt–Koyanagi–Harada (VKH), early diagnosis is crucial to prevent irreversible lesions which can be achieved if early diagnosis is obtained.[515] The advent of ICGA already made it possible to diagnose BRC before “birdshot choroidal fundus lesions” are detected and initiate early treatment with the consequence that the development of clinical birdshot lesions could be avoided[15] in the same fashion that sunset glow fundus can be avoided if initial-onset VKH is treated without delay with dual steroidal and nonsteroidal immunosuppression.[16] Following our findings, if the focus is placed on retinal involvement, paying attention to the BRC pattern of vasculitis, this will speed up the diagnosis of BRC even more, leading the clinician to perform ICGA, confirming the strong probability of BRC that is definitively ascertained by the presence of the HLA-A29 antigen.

Our study showed that only sound facts can define disease characteristics rather than consensus meetings and vague opinion surveys, the harmfulness of which appeared also for BRC with a consensus meeting that misled the medical community[17] and that took more than 15 years to be rectified.[18] Indeed, the “research” diagnostic criteria of BRC published in 2006 after a consensus meeting[17] have since been revised and corrected because of the many shortcomings inherent to such a process where opinions prime over objective facts.[17] The facts that were ignored, among others, were (1) the crucial role of ICGA in BRC putting “birdshot lesions” in the background no more essential for diagnosis, (2) the essential diagnostic role of the HLA-A29 antigen, and (3) ignoring the importance of retinal vasculitis in the appraisal of BRC.

In summary, our purpose here was to give retinal vasculitis the preponderant place it deserves as the early manifestation of BRC and systematically define the characteristics and the diagnostic usefulness of the retinal component of the disease that was proven to be statistically significant for the diagnosis of BRC. The BRC vasculitis pattern include key retinal features such as profuse vascular leakage, macular oedema often sparing of the fovea, retinal arterio-venous circulatory “pseudo-delay”, thick FA sheathing/staining of large posterior pole vessels and pronounced disc hyperfluorescence. Among the more specific features of BRC, retinal vasculitis was the retinal arteriovenous circulatory “pseudo delay” present in over 80% of cases, which is explained by the massive leakage of large and small vessels into the retina with the consequence that there is not sufficient fluorescein reaching the veins with delayed or absent marking on FA. If ICGA arteriovenous circulation time is considered, the transit time is, however, normal as the circulating large ICG-protein molecular complex is not extravasating from retinal vessels.[39] This phenomenon of circulatory delay was described by Gass in his article on vitiliginous chorioretinitis and was erroneously considered a true perfusion delay as ICGA was not available at that time.[3] Another relatively specific feature was the thick sheathing/staining of large vessels of the posterior arcades merging into the disc, as reported previously.[10] These features allowed us to propose a scoring system that assigned a statistically significant diagnostic value to the BRC pattern of vasculitis. However, these findings are still preliminary and need to be confirmed by a larger series to be conducted in a completely blinded fashion.

According to our findings, the main diagnostic steps for BRC are to identify retinal vasculitis with a pattern compatible with BRC, as put forward in our study, followed by the search for stromal choroiditis using ICGA and when present, perform search of the HLA-A29 antigen to confirm the diagnosis.

Practically, the diagnosis of BRC should be considered (1) when facing any bilateral compatible retinal vasculitis on FA showing a severe retinal vasculitis of a certain type, (2) completed by typical choroidal involvement explored by ICGA, and (3) HLA-A29 positivity.
