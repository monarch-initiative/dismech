Cholangiopathies comprise a spectrum of chronic intrahepatic and extrahepatic 
biliary tract disorders culminating in progressive cholestatic liver injury, 
fibrosis, and often cirrhosis and its sequela. Treatment for these diseases is 
limited, and collectively, they are one of the therapeutic "black boxes" in 
clinical hepatology. The etiopathogenesis of the cholangiopathies likely 
includes disease-specific mediators but also common cellular and molecular 
events driving disease progression (eg, cholestatic fibrogenesis, inflammation, 
and duct damage). The common pathways involve cholangiocytes, the epithelial 
cells lining the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts, which are central to 
the pathogenesis of these disorders. Current information suggests that 
cholangiocytes function as a signaling "hub" in biliary tract-associated injury. 
Herein, we review the pivotal role of cholangiocytes in cholestatic 
fibrogenesis, focusing on the crosstalk between cholangiocytes and portal 
fibroblasts and HSCs. The proclivity of these cells to undergo a 
senescence-associated secretory phenotype, which is proinflammatory and 
profibrogenic, and the intrinsic intracellular activation pathways resulting in 
the secretion of cytokines and chemokines are reviewed. The crosstalk between 
cholangiocytes and cells of the innate (neutrophils and macrophages) and 
adaptive (T cells and B cells) immune systems is also examined in detail. The 
information will help consolidate information on this topic and guide further 
research and potential therapeutic strategies for these diseases.

Cholestatic liver diseases, characterized by impaired bile formation and excretion of compounds into bile, are a diverse group of acute and chronic disorders of the biliary tract. Cholangiocytes, the specialized epithelial cells lining the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts, are central to the pathogenesis of chronic cholestatic liver diseases. These cells serve to sense and modify bile through complex mechanosensory, chemosensory, and osmosensory mechanisms that, in turn, affect secretion and absorption of electrolytes, bile acids, water, and other molecules (1).

A unique feature of cholangiocytes is that their apical membrane is constantly exposed to bile that contains millimolar concentrations of hydrophobic, toxic bile acids, other noxious constituents and pathogens. Hence, cholangiocytes provide an important barrier function protecting the liver parenchyma from damage by biliary constituents and must be able to respond to pathogenic affronts by inducing an inflammatory reaction to confine liver injury. Also, to overcome cholestasis conditions, intrahepatic cholangiocytes proliferate within the portal tract and into the hepatic parenchyma, perhaps as a compensatory attempt to increase bile drainage (2, 3). This increase in intrahepatic cholangiocyte proliferation, referred to as the ductular reaction, also elicits an inflammatory wound healing response with fibrogenesis. Cholangiocytes also have a propensity to undergo senescence (a state of irreversible cell cycle arrest) in pathophysiologic situations, resulting in an inflammatory state known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). In all these pathophysiologic scenarios, these activated cholangiocytes appear to amplify similar transcriptional programs promoting secretion of a variety of cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and matrix molecules that promote cholestatic fibrogenesis. The intrinsic ability of cholangiocytes to drive this cholestatic fibrogenesis is evident in genetic diseases affecting the cholangiocytes. For example, a disease such as cystic fibrosis (caused by mutations in the epithelial cell chloride ion channel termed cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulatory, CFTR), and variants of the cilia gene polycystic kidney disease 1 like 1 (PKD1L1) associated with syndromic biliary atresia can lead to chronic progressive liver fibrosis and chronic liver failure. The term “cholangiopathy” appeared in the medical literature in the 1950’s to refer to various abnormalities of the biliary tract (4, 5), but since the mid-1990’s there has been a parabolic explosion of interest and publications on cholangiopathies (6) (Figure 1). The modern definition of cholangiopathies includes a diverse group of progressive, chronic, cholestatic liver diseases emanating from a wide variety of pathogenic mechanisms (Table 1) in which the cholangiocyte suffers a primary insult that ignites a pathobiological cascade of disease progression(1). This cascade of events includes activation of cholangiocytes toward a secretory phenotype that promotes local inflammation, immune cell infiltration, and activation of hepatic stellate cells and portal fibroblasts, all culminating in chronic biliary damage, cholestatic fibrogenesis, and stricturing of the larger bile ducts. End-stage cholangiopathies remain essentially untreatable without liver transplantation. Although many of these diseases are thought to be immune-mediated, and clearly immune reactions are ubiquitous and prominent in these syndromes, conventional immunosuppressive therapy has not proven useful (7). Indeed, one of these diseases, primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), is often associated with inflammatory bowel disease for which immunomodulatory agents are successfully employed; yet, to date, none of these therapies has been efficacious for the associated PSC. Furthermore, PSC can recur after liver transplantation in the face of systemic immunosuppression. These observations suggest that cholangiocyte-specific pathologies initiate and promote these diseases (8, 9).

A vast landscape of potential treatment modalities is being aggressively investigated around the globe for cholangiopathies, including therapeutics targeting the bile acid milieu, immunomodulatory therapies, microbiome manipulation, senolytics, epigenetic agents, and antifibrotics. However, few of these therapies directly target the pathophysiologic dysfunction occurring within activated cholangiocytes. Therefore, to encourage attention on cholangiocyte dysfunction, it is timely and topical to emphasize and review what is known regarding cholangiocyte-driven, disease promoting mechanisms. A prior publication on cholangiocyte pathobiology reviewed current pathways mediating cholangiocyte proliferation, including the ductular reaction, and their role in liver regeneration and transport functions and, therefore, these topics will not be reviewed here (1).

Cholestatic fibrogenesis comprises a complex series of pathophysiological processes, involving multiple cell types, which begins in the periportal areas, propagates to include bridging fibrosis between the portal tracts, and culminates in the characteristic jigsaw pattern of biliary fibrosis involving the entire hepatic lobule. This process is incited by various pathological events centered on or within the cholangiocytes and is perpetuated by epithelial-mesenchymal crosstalk with fibrogenic cell types including portal fibroblasts (PF) and hepatic stellate cells (HSC) (10). These activated cell types then produce the matrisome of fibronectin-1 (FN1), collagen, laminin, and other extracellular matrix (ECM) molecules (11). These excessive matrix molecules drive stricturing of the bile ducts, formation of fibrotic septa within the liver parenchyma, and ultimately loss of hepatic synthetic function and end-stage liver disease. Recent research has shed light on the complex molecular events involved in the development of biliary fibrosis. Studies have highlighted the key role for various paracrine mediators, emanating from cholangiocytes, including cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and matrix molecules that promote the activation of PF and HSC (Figure 2). Many of these mediators are members of the SASP and are sometimes collectively referred to as “cholangiokines” (12). Some prototypical examples include interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), FN1, platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), among many others. We are also beginning to unravel the signaling pathways, epigenetic events, and transcriptional programs intrinsic to cholangiocytes that coordinate this aberrant wound healing response (13, 14). Below, we review some of the well-established and emerging cholangiocentric fibrosis mechanisms that will be crucial for the design of targeted therapeutics.

TGF-β signaling is a master regulator of fibrogenesis in various tissues, including the liver (15). The primary cellular sources of TGF-β in biliary fibrosis include tissue-resident macrophages (Kupffer cells), infiltrating macrophages, and activated cholangiocytes(16). Other liver cell types can also secrete TGF-β, including hepatocytes, liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, and HSC (17). These various cellular sources of TGF-β in the liver underscore its multifaceted roles in normal hepatic physiology including proliferation (18, 19), differentiation (20), and tissue repair (21), as well as its pathological roles in apoptosis and ECM production (18). While TGF-β signaling clearly plays a vital role in orchestrating the fibrogenic response within the biliary epithelium, the field is only beginning to unravel the intricate mechanisms underlying signaling in cholangiocytes during biliary fibrosis.

Dysregulation of these TGF-β-dependent pathways can lead to aberrant fibrogenic responses, including secretion of inflammatory mediators, fibrogenic activators of PF and HSC, ECM molecules, and autocrine stimulators of cholangiocytes, including TGF-β itself. Indeed, TGF-β prominently activates transcription of genes such FN1, SERPIN1, CCN2, ACTA2, PDGF, and TGFB1 in H69 cholangiocytes (10, 14, 22). Pharmacologic (CPTH6, Bortezomib) or genetic interference (EZH2 knockout or KAT2A knockdown) with TGF-β signaling at various levels can abolish these responses and blunt fibrosis in murine models of cholestatic injury (3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine (DDC)-diet and Abcb4−/−) (14, 22).

Given its significant role in the pathogenesis of biliary fibrosis, TGF-β signaling clearly represents an attractive therapeutic target for the treatment of biliary fibrosis. Strategies aimed at modulating TGF-β activity, such as small molecule inhibitors, neutralizing antibodies, gene therapy approaches, and epigenetic pharmacology hold promise for mitigating fibrogenesis and ameliorating liver fibrosis. However, the complex and pleiotropic nature of TGF-β signaling necessitates a comprehensive understanding of its cell type-selective and context-dependent effects and potential off-target effects of therapeutic interventions. Indeed, several toxicities have been noted with systemic inhibition of TGF-β, including cardiac valve problems, autoimmunity, and carcinogenesis (23).

Wnt pathways are highly conserved and play critical roles in embryonic development, tissue homeostasis, and regeneration (24). Dysregulation of Wnt signaling has been implicated in biliary fibrosis as a critical regulator of cholangiocyte activation, inflammation, and fibrogenesis (25). Canonical Wnt signaling begins with binding of Wnt ligands to Frizzled (Fzd) receptors (or its co-receptors, LRP5/6), on the cell surface. This binding event activates Disheveled (Dvl), which inhibits β-catenin destruction. In the absence of Wnt, β-catenin is phosphorylated and targeted for degradation at the proteasome. Upon Wnt stimulation, β-catenin accumulates in the cytoplasm and undergoes nuclear translocation. Nuclear β-catenin associates with certain transcription factors (T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer factor), which then leads to the transcriptional activation of Wnt target genes (26).

Wnt signaling is a potent regulator of cholangiocyte proliferation during liver injury and biliary fibrosis (27). Thus, activation of Wnt signaling promotes cholangiocyte proliferation, contributing to the ductular reaction. Several studies have demonstrated upregulation of Wnt ligands and receptors in cholangiocytes during cholestasis and biliary fibrosis (28), while knockout experiments suggest a role for Wnt signaling in driving the expansion of the biliary epithelium (29). Crosstalk between Wnt signaling and other fibrogenic pathways, such as TGF-β and Hedgehog signaling could further amplify the fibrogenic response in biliary fibrosis.

Integrins are a family of transmembrane receptors that mediate cell-cell and cell-ECM interactions (30). In the context of biliary fibrosis, integrin signaling contributes to the activation of HSC (31), the deposition of ECM (32), and the regulation of cholangiocyte behavior (33). Integrins are heterodimeric receptors composed of α and β subunits, with each subunit having multiple isoforms. Through their extracellular domains, integrins interact with specific ECM components and are classified into collagen-binding, laminin-binding, and RGD-binding families (34). Upon ligand binding, integrins undergo conformational changes that trigger intracellular signaling cascades.

Integrins expressed on cholangiocytes play a role in modulating their behavior and response to injury. Integrin-mediated adhesion to ECM components regulates cholangiocyte contributions to fibrogenesis. αVβ6 integrin is a specific heterodimeric transmembrane receptor that plays a crucial role in regulating cell-ECM interactions. In cholangiocytes, the role of αVβ6 integrin has gained attention due to its involvement in biliary fibrosis (34). Under normal conditions, αVβ6 integrin expression in cholangiocytes is low. However, its expression can be induced in response to injury, inflammation, profibrotic stimuli, and mechanical stress. Activation of αVβ6 integrin in cholangiocytes facilitates adhesion to specific ECM ligands, such as FN1 and latent TGF-β complexes, leading to the activation of downstream signaling pathways. One of the unique functions of αVβ6 integrin is its ability to activate these latent TGF-β complexes, thereby promoting TGF-β signaling in an autocrine or paracrine manner, ultimately leading to the development and progression of liver fibrosis. Targeting αVβ6 integrin or its downstream signaling pathways represents a potential therapeutic approach for mitigating biliary fibrosis and liver disease progression. Inhibitors of integrin-ligand interactions, such as RGD (Arg-Gly-Asp) peptide mimetics, small molecule inhibitors, and monoclonal antibodies targeting specific integrin subunits, have been explored as potential antifibrotic agents in preclinical studies and clinical trials.

Bile acids, synthesized from cholesterol in the liver, are amphipathic molecules with important roles in digestion, absorption of lipids, and cholesterol homeostasis. In addition to their classical functions, bile acids have emerged as signaling molecules that regulate various cellular processes through activation of nuclear and membrane-bound receptors. Dysregulation of bile acid signaling has been implicated in the pathogenesis of liver diseases, including biliary fibrosis (35).

Bile acids serve as ligands for several nuclear receptors, such as the farnesoid X receptor (FXR), pregnane X receptor (PXR), vitamin D receptor (VDR), and liver X receptor (LXR) (36). Cholangiocytes express various bile acid receptors and respond to bile acids by regulating bile secretion, cell proliferation, inflammation, and fibrogenesis. Bile acid signaling in cholangiocytes influences biliary epithelial cell function and integrity, modulating the progression of biliary fibrosis. Pharmacological agonists or antagonists targeting bile acid receptors, synthetic bile acids, bile acid sequestrants, and inhibitors of bile acid synthesis enzymes are being explored as potential treatments to ameliorate liver fibrosis and cholestasis. We refer the readers to a recent detailed review on the role of bile acids in biliary fibrosis (35).

While genetic studies have been ongoing for many years, simple genetic causes of PSC and other cholangiopathies have not been identified. The pathogenesis appears to involve many of the complex and multifactorial mechanisms and signaling pathways described in this review. Increasingly, it appears that these dysregulated signaling pathways in biliary fibrosis are likely integrated at the level of the epigenome (13). Epigenetic mechanisms consist of reversible modifications of DNA or histone proteins that regulate gene expression without altering the underlying DNA sequence. These mechanisms play critical roles in responses to environmental cues. Dysregulation of epigenetic processes have been implicated in the pathogenesis of many diseases, including biliary fibrosis.

In biliary fibrosis, aberrant DNA methylation patterns have been reported in cholangiocytes in the context of cholestatic liver diseases. A methylome-wide association study of cholestatic liver diseases, namely PSC and primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), demonstrated differential methylation patterns associated with unique pathways (37). Another study implicates DNA hypomethylation as a specific factor in mediating overexpression of genes associated with biliary atresia, including PDGF (38). Epigenetic regulation of forkhead box A2 via DNA methylation has been implicated in cholestatic liver injury in mice (39). Interestingly, epigenetic remodeling of the methylome and hydroxymethylome also appears to allow ductal cells to form organoids and activate a regenerative phenotype in vitro (40). A variety of DNA methylation changes have been noted in PBC, suggesting epigenetically induced secretory failure (41). A variety of studies that evaluated DNA methylation changes in the context of cholangiocarcinoma will not be reviewed in detail here.

Dysregulated histone modifications are increasingly implicated in biliary fibrosis, affecting the transcriptional regulation of genes involved in the DR and fibrogenesis (13). These transcriptional changes can be affected by activation though deposition of activating histone marks (14) or by de-repression through loss of silencing histone marks (22). For example, Gli-1 binding in key cholangiocyte gene promoters overcomes an epigenetic barrier conferred by the polycomb protein, enhancer of zeste homologue 2 (EZH2), a silencing histone methyltransferase. EZH2 KO mice fed the choline-deficient ethanolamine-supplemented diet have an exaggerated DR and more robust peri-portal fibrosis (42). Likewise, cholangiocyte-selective KO of EZH2 exacerbates bile duct ligation (BDL)-induced fibrosis (22). Furthermore, TGF-β signaling promotes proteasomal degradation of EZH2 and activates transcription of a fibrogenic gene program in cholangiocytes. Indeed, the proteasomal inhibitor, bortezomib is protective against biliary fibrosis in Abcb4−/− (also known as Mdr2−/−) mice, a cholestatic liver disease model (22). In addition to its role in de-repression, TGF-β signaling also activates transcription of an HSC-activating gene program in cholangiocytes via SMAD-mediated, KAT2A-dependent H3K9ac (14), demonstrated by a combination of RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, and ATAC-seq. Several studies have also implicated histone modifications in cholangiocyte senescence. In PSC, H3K4me3 marks the active promoter of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A downstream of ETS1 (43). ETS1 also activates transcription of the anti-apoptotic Bcl-xL via H3K27ac (44). The transcriptional activation is mediated by the BET protein and epigenetic reader, bromodomain containing 2 (BRD2). Inhibition of BET proteins reduced inflammation and fibrosis in mice fed the DDC-diet, an in vivo model of cholestatic disease, and in Abcb4−/− mice (45). Epigenetic silencing of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) occurs via H3K9me3 to promote cholangiocyte senescence. Cholangiocyte-selective TERT KO exacerbated biliary fibrosis while androgen therapy with danazol rescued biliary fibrosis in Abcb4−/− mice (46). Interestingly, sex-specific chromatin dynamics also appear to drive cyst formation in polycystic liver disease. Male cystogenesis is driven by a switch from H3K9me3 to H3K9ac while female cystogenesis is associated with H3K27ac (47).

Inflammation is an integral component of cholestatic liver diseases (48, 49). Inflammatory processes are initiated to repair the injury or stress to the liver cells. However, long term inflammatory processes can activate liver myofibroblasts (hepatic stellate cells, portal fibroblasts) resulting in deposition of collagen and other matrix constituents, culminating in fibrosis and eventually cirrhosis (50).

The etiologies of cholestatic liver diseases such as PSC, PBC, and biliary atresia are thought to include an immune-mediated component that can precipitate injury by inducing damage to the biliary tree. Bile acids synthesized in hepatocytes are excreted into the bile and then enter the enterohepatic circulation. A seemingly straightforward process that, when altered, can result in disposition and retention of bile acids resulting in alteration of bile acid synthesis, uptake, and transport (51). These alterations, mediated by gene expression changes, serve to remove bile acids from the body to alleviate cholestasis. With obstruction, one can imagine that these processes can no longer accommodate the clearance leading to sustained injury (52). Accumulation of bile acids can be a double-edge sword in cholestasis; induce apoptosis of hepatocytes and cholangiocytes as well as be the proinflammatory molecules themselves driving the inflammation (53, 54).

Inflammation is a healthy response to hepatic injury and is necessary for proper wound healing. However, when unresolved, it can further liver injury through local tissue damage due to release of inflammatory cytokines and/or chemokines. These molecules are the mediators of inflammatory cell infiltration in the liver. Upon infection or any tissue insult, cholangiocytes can become activated and release pro-inflammatory cholangiokines such as CCL2, IL-6, TNF-α and others (12, 55). These cytokines can act on cholangiocytes in an autocrine/paracrine fashion, but also are key in recruitment of immune cells to the injury site. In disease conditions, injurious stimuli caused either by mechanotransduction, genetic abnormalities, epigenetic modulation, etc., can lead to conversion of quiescent cholangiocytes to either a proliferative or a senescent state. As discussed above, the signaling pathways implicated in initiating the fibrogenic pathways also intertwine with the inflammatory response instigated by signaling in cholangiocytes themselves.

The family of NF-κB transcription factors are involved in diverse biological processes, including inflammation (56). Signaling through NF-κB can be through the canonical pathway which relies on I B degradation, which is rapid and transient. The non-canonical pathway is slow, persistent, and relies on NF-κB-inducing kinase (NIK) (57). Studies have suggested dysregulation of non-canonical NF-κB signaling in transcription of proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules, promoting inflammation in various liver diseases including cholangiopathies. Increased nuclear levels of the RELB proto-oncogene NF-κB subunit as well as increased lymphotoxin B levels were detected in bile ducts of liver tissues from cholestatic liver diseases compared to control tissues (58). These observations correlated mechanistically with increased cytokine expression, ductular reaction, and fibrosis. Interestingly, increased NF-κB activation along with inflammation and fibrosis was also observed in mice lacking β-catenin and CFTR (59), suggesting an interaction between these pathways. Small molecule inhibitor NIK inhibitor 46 (XT2) (60) or B022 (61) have been shown to effectively reduce drug-induced liver injury and inflammation. However, the role of this inhibitor and its impact in cholangiopathies remains to be tested. Similarly, IKK inhibitors (in clinical trials for malignant melanoma) remain to be tested in cholestatic liver diseases (62).

Toll-like receptors recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and trigger innate immune responses (63). Cholangiocytes express TLR2, 3, 4, 5, and 9 receptors. As the biliary epithelium is in contact with the bacterial products coming from intestine, there are checkpoints in place to keep the TLR4 signaling repressed and maintain the “LPS tolerance”(64). Upon changes in the checkpoints or over stimulation during injury/disease, there is a decrease in the tolerance. TLR4 then responds to microbial stimuli and LPS thereby activates N-Ras to produce proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6(65). TLR4 also engages the NF-κB pathway via the myeloid differentiation protein 88 (MyD88) and activation of the IL-1 receptor associated kinase to produce cholangiokines (66). These allow for cholangiocytes to interact with the innate immune system (discussed below). For example, in a mice model lacking CFTR, exposure to experimental colitis activates the TLR4-NF-κB pathway in the peribiliary area, mounting an inflammatory response characterized by CD45+ inflammatory cells (67). This response is blunted in the wild-type littermates. Primary cholangiocytes isolated from PSC patients exhibited upregulation of TLR4, as well as enhanced activation of the MyD88 signaling, contributing to hyper-responsiveness to endotoxins that further perpetuate the innate immune response and inflammation in PSC (68). The TLR-NF-κB pathway is also implicated in the upregulation of inducible nitric oxide synthase 2 (iNOS2) (69), which results in formation of reactive nitrogen oxygen species and nitric oxide in cholangiocytes. These then parlayed into disruption of the cholangiocyte secretion of bicarbonate via the Cl− anion exchanger as well as the cAMP-dependent fluid secretion by cholangiocytes that affects the alkalinity of the bile acids mitigating bile acid toxicity (70). Activation of TLRs in cholangiocytes thus induces the production of inflammatory mediators, exacerbating cholangiopathy-associated inflammation.

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is an important regulatory axis in liver as a promoter of regeneration and repair (71). During biliary diseases upregulation of specific Wnts has been observed, especially Wnt7b (27). Congruently, active β-catenin is also observed in animal models of cholestatic liver diseases, suggesting activation of canonical Wnt signaling (72). A recent study revealed a tripartite complex of β-catenin, NF-κB and CFTR in cholangiocytes (59). Interfering with the complex lead to profound activation of NF-κB along with the increased expression of its pro-inflammatory gene targets. These data suggest a protective role of β-catenin in dampening NF-κB signaling in cholangiocytes, maintaining their homeostasis. Along similar lines, another study suggests that Wnt inhibitors could have a beneficial role in cholangiopathies by preventing their activation and the downstream initiation of the inflammatory cascade (25).

Disruption of bile acid (BA) metabolism and flow leads to accumulation of BAs in the liver and increased circulatory BAs in cholestatic liver diseases (73). The elevation in hepatic BAs disturbs the cholangiocyte tight junctions, thereby initiating an inflammatory and fibrotic response (74–76). The apical sodium-dependent BA transporter (ASBT) localized on the apical membrane of cholangiocytes uptake the BAs (77). ASBT inhibitors are potential therapeutic targets for cholestatic liver diseases like PBC (53). Sphingosine1-phosphate receptor 2 (S1PR2), another receptor predominantly expressed by biliary epithelia, mediates BA-induced proliferation and cholestasis, which was attenuated with pharmacological and genetic deletion in the BDL mouse model (78). Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5), also known as GPBAR1, is a BA receptor found on biliary epithelial cells where it has been shown to promote secretion, proliferation, and tight junction integrity (79, 80). Interestingly, TGR5 is downregulated in biliary epithelial cells in PSC (81). This effect was attributed to increased IL-8 levels observed in PSC. The protective role for TGR5 was confirmed in the Abcb4−/− mice where overexpression of Tgr5 was sufficient to reduce the reactive phenotype of biliary cells and associated inflammation and cholangitis. The therapeutic benefit of ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) is also accredited to increased TGR5 levels, which further increased the biliary bicarbonate secretion (82). 24-norursodeoxycholic acid (norUDCA), a shortened homologue of UDCA, through its cholehepatic shunting ability and stimulation of bicarbonate secretion showed better therapeutic benefits in PSC patients in a phase II clinical study (83). BA have a heterogeneous effect on intracellular signaling in cholangiocytes that can result in differential effects on secretion, proliferation, and survival. This provides an avenue for therapeutic opportunities to modulate bile acid signaling in cholangiocytes.

Cells are exposed to cytotoxic stressors throughout their lifespan. In response, cells initiate signaling cascades that converge on transcriptional machinery and alter their proteomic profile and phenotype; they also may initiate processes aimed at eliminating damaged or aged cells. Among these protective processes are programmed cell death (apoptosis) and cell cycle arrest (senescence). Although apoptosis of cholangiocytes does not appear to be prominent in cholangiopathies, senescence occurs frequently perhaps as a mechanism to prevent apoptosis and maintain the biliary epithelia. Senescence is characterized by withdrawal from the cell cycle, apoptosis resistance, and proinflammatory SASP (84). Cell cycle arrest prevents proliferation of aged or damaged cells and thus plays a protective role, including tumor suppression. However, persistence of senescent cells can also contribute to disease (85). While much of the focus has been on age-related pathologies, senescence also contributes to non-age-related diseases, including cholestatic liver disease. PBC, PSC, and biliary atresia are cholangiopathies in which senescent cholangiocytes have been implicated. Indeed, correlative studies have demonstrated increased numbers of senescent cholangiocytes in each of these diseases (86–91). Recently, studies have focused on pathways driving the senescent phenotype, and therapeutic approaches targeting cholangiocyte senescence.

The potential pathophysiologic relevance of cellular senescence was first proposed by Hayflick and Moorhead when they observed permanent cell cycle withdrawal of fibroblasts following repeated passages in culture (92). They proposed that these differentiated cells had finite replicative capacity, and that cellular senescence may contribute to tissue/organismal aging. We now know that this fibroblast senescence was likely due to telomere erosion caused by extensive proliferation in the absence of telomerase activity, a process known as replicative senescence. While telomere erosion and the resultant initiation of a DNA damage response remains a significant cause of cellular senescence, additional causes include mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative and oncogenic stress, and inflammation (93–95). The characterization of a cell as senescent requires detection of multiple markers indicative of cell-cycle withdrawal including upregulated expression of the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors, p21 (CDKN1A) and p16 (CDKN2A), senescence-associated beta galactosidase activity (SA-β-GAL), and increased detection of DNA double strand breaks [e.g., phosphorylation of the histone mark gamma-H2A, γH2A.x). Increased detection of these markers in cholangiocytes over the progression of PBC, PSC, and biliary atresia has been demonstrated (Figure 3).

In tissues from PBC patients, senescence markers p21 and p16 are increased in small bile ducts compared to healthy controls. Additionally, cholangiocyte expression of p21 and p16 was increased in late compared to early stage PBC, suggesting a correlation of cholangiocyte senescence with disease progression (90, 96). Additionally, p16 expression in bile ductules significantly correlated to failed patient outcomes after UDCA treatment, suggesting that cholangiocyte senescence is important both in PBC progression and potentially in preventing an adequate response to pharmacologic intervention (97). Senescent cholangiocytes are also a prominent feature in PSC. Indeed, p21 and p16 expression, as well as the detection of γH2A.x, are elevated in cholangiocytes from PSC patients compared to healthy controls (46, 86). Moreover, increased numbers of senescent cholangiocytes correlate with disease severity and patient outcomes (87). While the potential role of cholangiocyte senescence in the pathology of biliary atresia is less well studied, patients with biliary atresia exhibit decreased telomere length in hepatic tissues, suggesting that telomere erosion, or replicative senescence, may be involved (98). Also, cholangiocyte p16 expression is elevated in explant livers of biliary atresia patients (88). Finally, liver samples from biliary atresia patients undergoing hepatoportoenterostomy exhibited increased senescence markers in cholangiocytes compared to non-disease controls (89). Thus, cholangiocyte senescence correlates with disease severity in these three cholangiopathies.

Unlike apoptosis, where cells die without initiating a profound immune response, senescent cells rely on a proinflammatory SASP for immune cell recruitment and efficient targeted killing (e.g., via NK and CD8+ T-cells,) and clearance (via macrophage-dependent effero/phagocytosis) of damaged cells (99). Chronic senescence, and prolonged exposure to a proinflammatory SASP can have deleterious consequences to surrounding tissue. The SASP from senescent cholangiocytes can induce senescence in surrounding cells and result in a persistent pro-fibroinflammatory state (86, 100, 101). Why the accumulation of senescent cholangiocytes correlates with disease severity in PBC, PSC, and biliary atresia has not been resolved; however, several hypotheses have been proposed including the persistence of undefined cytotoxic stressors, senescent cholangiocyte-mediated evasion of immune elimination, immune cell-mediated ineffective clearance of senescent cholangiocytes, or the induction of an immunosuppressive state (102). While cumulative observations have supported that accumulation of senescent cholangiocytes correlate with cholestatic liver disease progression, more recent animal studies support a direct causal role for cholangiocyte senescence in pathogenesis. One such study addressed whether the induction of cholangiocyte senescence resulted in pathologic features consistent with sclerosing cholangitis. In this study, the authors knocked out the double minute 2-proto-oncogene (Mdm2) in cholangiocytes and found that upregulated expression of p53 promoted increased p16, p21, and γH2A.x in cholangiocytes. Moreover, the senescent cholangiocytes exhibited increased expression of SASP factors, and promoted increased hepatocyte senescence and biliary fibrosis, and decreased liver regeneration (103). These results support a causative role of cholangiocyte senescence in driving features of cholestatic liver diseases.

Additional studies have addressed cause vs. consequence of senescent cell accumulation in pathogenic processes. A breakthrough in the beneficial effects of senescent cell removal in aging comorbidities came with the development of a transgenic INK-attac mouse (104). This mouse allows for the targeted elimination of p16INK-positive senescent cells. Using a progeroid mouse model (BubR1) containing the p16INK-attac transgene, the authors showed that elimination of senescent cells delayed the onset of age-related (senescence-associated) pathologies. This important study supported the role of senescent cells in causation and established that removal of senescent cells improved features of age-related diseases. Mouse models of cholestasis, including BDL, the Abcb4−/− mouse, and the DDC-fed mouse, all exhibit increased markers of cholangiocyte senescence (39, 45, 105–107). This supports the idea that a persistent cytotoxic insult promotes cholangiocyte senescence. Using the p16INK-attac mouse crossed with the Abcb4−/− mouse, it was shown that the induced elimination of p16-positive cells resulted in diminished inflammation and fibrosis and correlated with a decrease in the number of senescent cholangiocytes (106). Additionally, crossing the Abcb4−/− mouse with a p16-deficient mouse or morpholino-mediated depletion of p16 in the Abcb4−/− mouse also improved the fibroinflammatory phenotype of the Abcb4−/− mouse (106, 108). Similarly, Abcb4−/− mice treated with drugs that selectively kill senescent cells (senolytics), including the BCL inhibitors navitoclax and A1331, or the flavonoid fisetin, showed a similar reduction in inflammatory markers and fibrosis along with a reduction in the expression of cholangiocyte p16 and p21(105, 106), thus providing preclinical data supporting the use of senolytics in cholestatic liver disease. Together, as in the progeroid p16INK-attac model, these results support a role for senescent cholangiocytes in the causation of cholestatic liver disease and establish that removal of these senescent/proinflammatory cells improve features of disease.

While data continue to support a prominent role of senescence in cholestatic liver diseases, questions remain. Primary among these is whether senescent cholangiocytes drive disease progression and whether therapies targeting senescent cholangiocytes improve parameters of human cholestatic liver disease. The demonstration that removal of senescent cholangiocytes improves disease outcomes in pre-clinical studies (senotherapies) should be expanded into early clinical studies. Another unanswered question is why senescent cholangiocytes accumulate over cholestatic liver disease progression. While ineffective clearance is a reasonable explanation, an alternative is that cholestatic liver disease patients are predisposed to cholangiocyte senescence. Interestingly, in support of this, skin fibroblasts collected from patients with PSC, when reprogrammed to pluripotency and then differentiated to cholangiocytes exhibit features of senescent cholangiocytes (109). This suggests that the genomic or epigenomic background of patients with PSC favors the induction of cholangiocyte senescence. Each of these explanations merit further experimentation. Additionally, it is generally accepted that cytotoxic stressors can drive cholangiocytes to alter their phenotypic profile and proliferate, undergo apoptosis, or senesce. However, a specific driver, or drivers of the cholangiocyte transition to senescence in cholestatic liver disease has not been clarified. Hence, cholangiocyte senescence is a targetable feature in cholangiopathies and the potential for therapeutic interventions targeting this cellular fate should be pursued aggressively.

As the body’s first line of defense against pathogens, the innate immune system is involved in recognizing and killing infectious agents. However, tissue damage in the absence of infection can also trigger an innate immune response (“sterile inflammation”). This is elicited by the release of intracellular DAMPs into the extracellular space following non-physiologic, lytic cell death, or the generation of apoptotic bodies following apoptotic cell death, or in response to released pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. The innate immune system comprises most cells of the myeloid lineage (macrophages and their precursors, monocytes, mast cells, dendritic cells, neutrophils, basophils, and eosinophils) and the lymphoid progenitor-derived natural killer (NK) cells. All myeloid cells have phagocytic capacity, in particular macrophages and neutrophils. Among these cells, the contribution of macrophages and neutrophils in cholangiopathies is well-recognized and amply studied, as both are found to be recruited in large numbers in the peribiliary areas in PSC and PBC patients, as well as in animal models of experimental cholestasis (10, 110–114).

Intracellular signaling pathways initiated by the engagement of TLRs are observed in several cholangiopathies and result in the activation of transcription factors such as NF-κB, which regulates the transcription of a vast range of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines as part of the initial injury repair process (115, 116). Indeed, these reactive cholangiocytes display increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNFα, IL-6) and chemokines (CCL2, IL-8, C-X-C chemokine ligand 10 [CXCL10] and 12 [CXCL12], C-X3-C motif chemokine ligand 1 [CX3CL1]) that recruit and activate immune cells, including monocytes/macrophages, neutrophils, and T cells to the peribiliary areas (Figure 4). However, in pathologic conditions associated with repair mechanism dysregulation and chronic injury, this process is not restrained, resulting in the accumulation of immune cells, perpetuation of inflammation, and increased tissue damage and fibrosis. Consistently, CCL2, CX3CL1, and IL-8 are overexpressed in cholangiocytes of PSC and PBC patients (102).

In cholestasis, there is a complex interplay between cholangiocytes and macrophages, which influences the progression and resolution of the disease. Hepatic macrophages consist of the tissue resident Kupffer cells (KC) located in the space of Disse, which originate from the yolk sac, and monocyte-derived macrophages (MoMF) originating from the bone marrow. Despite previous implications of KC in the pathogenesis of cholestatic liver injury, recent studies have demonstrated that depletion of KC has no effect on myeloid cell infiltration or liver injury in mouse models of sclerosing cholangitis (117). In fact, KC are depleted in the early stages of cholestatic liver diseases (117, 118). In contrast, cholangiocyte-released CCL2 recruits pro-inflammatory MoMF from the bloodstream into the liver, and blocking monocyte recruitment by targeting CCR2, the receptor for CCL2, attenuated liver injury and fibrosis in Abcb4−/− mice (110). Owing to their innate plasticity, macrophages are uniquely poised to respond to cues in the microenvironment by switching from a pro-inflammatory phenotype in the injury phase to a restorative one during the recovery phase. Therefore, depletion of macrophages or inhibition of their specific functions can be beneficial or detrimental depending on the stage or the nature of the injury (119). Indeed, infiltrating MoMF have been shown to switch to a regenerative phenotype in response to bile acids and to promote bile duct repair by secretion of cholangiocyte mitogens, including tumor necrosis factor-like weak inducer of apoptosis (TWEAK). They also induce expression of integrin αVβ6 on cholangiocytes and initiate the fibrotic response via secretion of pro-fibrotic cytokines such as TGFβ and PDGF (120, 121). In addition, as MoMF are also pivotal for the removal of apoptotic bodies and cell debris to promote tissue repair, impaired MERTK-mediated efferocytosis of cholangiocyte apoptotic bodies by peribiliary macrophages promotes cholestatic liver injury (121). Recent studies aimed to characterize the macrophage population in the cholestatic milieu have determined that MoMF accumulating in desmin-rich, periductal/periportal areas in PSC and mouse models of fibrosing cholangitis resemble previously described lipid-associated macrophages (LAMs) with high expression of Spp1 (encoding osteopontin) and Trem2, and their enrichment correlate with the degree of fibrosis. Genetic deficiency of Spp1 in macrophages resulted in increased macrophage inflammatory potential and exacerbation of liver inflammation and injury (118). Finally, a recent seminal work by Stankey et al. has identified the transcription factor ETS2 as a central regulator of human inflammatory macrophages in several autoimmune and/or inflammatory diseases, including PSC. Inflammatory macrophages with high expression of ETS2 and ETS2-regulated genes were found adjacent to the diseased bile ducts in PSC. Consistently, targeting of upstream regulators of ETS2 reduced the release of inflammatory cytokines from cultured intestinal biopsies of IBD patients (122). Thus, understanding the crosstalk between cholangiocytes and macrophages is essential for developing therapeutic strategies to treat cholestatic liver diseases.

Neutrophils are known to infiltrate the liver with the purpose of repairing the injury (123). However, in chronic liver diseases, the tissue microenvironment perturbs the repair mechanism leading to aberrant signaling and inflammation by neutrophils. Bile duct brushings from human PSC patients reveal an increased number of liver neutrophils in the biliary tract (112). These neutrophils were proposed to be infiltrating through the production of CXCL8 by the tissue resident T cells. Murine models of biliary fibrosis also revealed portal accumulation of neutrophils which were annotated as “Ductular-reaction associated neutrophils (DRANs)”(113). Peripheral depletion of neutrophils or interference with the infiltration mitigated biliary fibrosis and ductular reaction observed in DDC-fed mice, suggesting a feedback loop between activated cholangiocytes and infiltrated neutrophils. In this study, the authors proposed the CXCL ligands as recruiters of neutrophils, as the pharmacological inhibition of the CXCL-CXCR axis reduces neutrophil infiltration (113). The authors used SCH-527123, a CXCR1/2 inhibitor to block the migration of neutrophils both in vitro and in vivo to further show that neutrophils exacerbate the disease by promoting cholangiocyte proliferation. The role of CXCL ligands from cholangiocytes to recruit neutrophils have also been reported in alcoholic liver disease (124).

Adhesion molecules also play a significant role in neutrophil chemotaxis to liver. In the BDL model of cholestasis increased portal accumulation of neutrophils and associated oxidative stress was dependent on the CD18 receptor or ICAM1 as the way to extravasate from the sinusoids into the parenchyma (125, 126). Interestingly, in alcoholic liver disease, crosstalk between cholangiocytes and neutrophils is also dependent on ICAM-1 expression on neutrophils which bind to integrin β1 on diseased cholangiocytes (127). Recruited neutrophils impair biliary bicarbonate secretion, thereby exacerbating alcohol-induced cholestasis. Mechanistically, ICAM-1 complexes with cytoskeletal protein, ezrin-radixin-moesin (ERM) and the Na(+)/H(+) exchange factor 1 (NHERF-1) to mediate the migration of neutrophils across the endothelium (128).

Biliary atresia patient liver tissues also show higher number of neutrophils around the biliary tracts compared to controls (129). The damaging effects of neutrophils is exerted through the production of ROS that can induce cell death as well as production of hypochlorous acid that can bind DNA and proteins to produce toxic chloramines. Augmenting the liver’s antioxidant capability or treatment with antioxidants may be possible therapeutic options for coping with the oxidative stress. Mice models of biliary atresia with increased neutrophil infiltration display CD177+ neutrophils as neutrophil extracellular traps (NET) producers by single cell RNA-seq. Consistent with this observation CD177−/− mice exhibited reduced morbidity and mortality (130). A pilot study with N-acetylcysteine treatment in biliary atresia patients reduced CD177+ cells and ROS levels, demonstrating a beneficial effect (130). However, a long-term clinical trial is warranted to test the side effects and the success in reducing the disease burden on patients.

Together, these studies suggest a model whereby an initial insult to cholangiocytes releases inflammatory molecules to recruit neutrophils as first responders. Upon extravasation into the parenchyma, neutrophils can adopt an activated phenotype which may further reduce their phagocytic ability and augment the cytoxic effects through ROS production that propagates the neutrophil-induced cell damage in cholestasis. With the advent of single cell and spatial technologies, future studies phenotyping neutrophil clusters as the disease progress would provide further insight into the subsets that could be beneficial or harmful. Employing the spatial distribution of neutrophils in diseased liver tissue can provide crucial insights into cellular connections and impact on other immune cell function as the disease progresses. These findings can lead to the discovery of targeted therapeutics for treatment of inflammation and subsequent fibrosis in cholestasis.

Dendritic cells (DCs) are the sentinel cells of the immune system that reside in the hepatic portal triads (131). Normally, DCs promote immune tolerance, but can become inflammatory upon damage. There are three DC subtypes: conventional DCs 1 and 2 (cDC1s and cDC2s), and plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs). Conventional cDCs are antigen presenting cells and activate CD8+ T cells, as well as Th1 cells (cDC1-mediated) and CD4+ T cells (cDC2-mediated) (132). Type I interferon response is mediated by pDCs. Interestingly, in PSC (humans and mouse models), cDC2s selectively expand in the liver (133). These expanded cDC2s support and maintain Th17 responses in infiltrating T cells, which are known to contribute to PSC pathogenesis (134). Similarly, an IL-17 signature around the damaged bile ducts has been observed in PBC, which could play a role in disease pathogenesis (135). Single cell transcriptomics further determined that the expanded cDC2s acquired proinflammatory monocyte-like features. A prominent role for these innate cells in cholangitis was further confirmed by the increased expression of CCL2 and CXCL2, which are key mediators in recruitment of macrophages and neutrophils seen in PSC pathogenesis (133). In PBC, CCL20 released by biliary epithelial cells recruits circulating immature dendritic cells (136). Thus, the recruitment and the local expansion of dendritic cells are involved in the inflammatory response seen in cholestasis. Whether targeting the selective expansion of cDC2s could help reduce the inflammation seen in cholangitis remains to be tested. It is also becoming clear that the innate immune cells crosstalk with the adaptive immune system shapes the microenvironment and affects the disease pathogenesis. Therefore, dual targeting of the immune system may be more beneficial for taming the proinflammatory response seen in cholestatic livers.

The adaptive immune system consists of lymphocytes (T and B cells), their antigen specific receptors T cell receptor (TCR) and B cell receptor (BCR), antigen presenting molecules (MHC), and myriad signaling molecules (chemokines and cytokines) (137). The collective response of the adaptive immune system leads to cell-mediated (T cell) and humoral immunity (B cell) (138). The liver is uniquely positioned to receive 25% of the body’s cardiac output every minute. A majority (80%) of the blood enters the liver from the gastrointestinal system via the portal vein along with arterial input from the hepatic artery (139). Blood entering from the portal vein and hepatic artery irrigate the liver through a complex network of sinusoidal vessels that permeate the liver parenchyma such that resident cells of the liver (including cholangiocytes) come into close contact with the components of the systemic circulation, particularly by-products of the gastrointestinal system (140). Therefore, the liver is a homeostatically tolerogenic organ, where there is a constant state of low-grade immunosuppression, and it is simultaneously poised to provide immune surveillance against pathogenic molecules. Within the immune terrain of the liver, there are cell types that are passing through and others that are exclusively tissue-resident, a critical characteristic of intrahepatic lymphocytes (141). Because of the paucity of B cells in the liver (5% of all lymphocytes) compared to T cells, this section will primarily focus on the crosstalk between cholangiocytes and T cells. T cells are the second most abundant population of the immune system in the liver, second only to cells of the monocytic lineage, and intrahepatic T cells are predominantly tissue-resident. Tissue residency of T cells in the liver is marked by CD69 (142, 143) and peribiliary T cells additionally express CD103 (112).

There are two mechanisms that make the cholestatic liver immune microenvironment a rich site to facilitate cholangiocyte crosstalk with adaptive lymphocytes. First, there is an enrichment of T cells in periportal areas in mouse and human cholestatic liver (9, 112, 134, 144–146). Second, cholangiocytes produce molecules that enhance infiltration and interaction with T cells. Cholangiocytes in cholestatic mouse models and in PSC and PBC livers secrete CCL20 and CXCL9-11 (147–149) which respectively attract CCR6+ and CXCR3+ CD4+ helper-T (Th) cells to the portal tract (148). Certain chemokines exhibit dual functions as chemoattractant and cell-adhesive molecules and are produced by cholangiocytes in cholestasis. Fractalkine (CX3CL1), the ligand for CX3CR1, is upregulated on cholangiocytes in human cholestatic livers, and cholangiocytes exposed to inflammatory stimuli in vitro. This then mediates infiltration of CX3CR1+ T cells to periportal areas and indeed promotes direct contact between T cells and cholangiocytes in cholestasis (150). Another chemokine produced by cholangiocytes, CXCL16, exists in membrane (lipid scavenger and cell-adhesive) and soluble (chemoattractant) forms and binds to CXCR6+ T cells, thereby promoting liver infiltration and direct adherence of cholangiocytes to T cells (151).

Several studies highlight the reciprocal functional impact of crosstalk between adaptive immune cells and cholangiocytes on each other and on disease pathogenesis. Recently, a causal gain-of-function mutation in CD100 (CD100 K849T), a molecule important in T cell activation, was identified in a familial subtype of PSC (152). Subsequent examination demonstrated that cholangiocytes crosstalk with CD100-mutated CD4+ T cells and cause them to adopt a Th17 phenotype (8). The cholangiocyte secretome contains Th17 polarizing cytokines, and Th17 cells provide a proliferative signal to cholangiocytes, suggesting a bidirectional relationship (149). Furthermore, cholangiocyte organoids derived from PSC patients secrete robust amounts of IL-7 (153), which supports the survival of IL-17 producing T cells (154–156). PSC- and non-PSC-derived cholangiocyte organoids also exhibit increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines (including CCL20, CXCL1, and CXCL5) in response to IL-17A treatment, with a stronger effect observed in PSC-derived organoids (153). IL-17-producing CD8+ T cells lead to IL-17-dependent upregulation of PD-L1 on cholangiocytes, thereby restraining sustained CD8+ T cell activation and protecting from progressive liver damage in cholestasis (157). Cytotoxic CD8+ T cells cause Granzyme-B-dependent apoptosis of cholangiocytes in the Abcb4−/− model of cholestasis, thereby aggravating liver injury and fibrosis (158). An extreme example of T cell-cholangiocyte interaction is the internalization of the T cell into the cholangiocyte. The earliest observation of this phenomenon was made by electron microscopy (159). CD8+ T cells are internalized by cholangiocytes in the setting of PBC (160), a disease where cytotoxic CD8+ T cells are major drivers. Mechanistically, the internalization occurs through the formation of adherens junctions mediated by E-cadherin (CD8+ T cell) and β-catenin (cholangiocyte) interactions, and this phenomenon, though not exclusive, is most prominent in PBC livers (161).

The role of cholangiocytes as antigen presenting cells (APCs) to engage with T cells via their TCR has also been explored. Levels of MHC I increase in cholangiocytes in response to viral infections (162). Unlike other epithelial cells, cholangiocytes express MHC II which is upregulated in the setting of cholestasis or by inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and IFN-γ) in vitro (162–166). Cholangiocytes isolated from patients with PBC and normal livers upregulate their MHC II gene expression when exposed to IFN-γ and IL-1β (163). Cytokine-induced upregulation of MHC II is more pronounced in cholangiocytes than hepatocytes (164). The expression of co-stimulatory molecules, which include the B7 ligands CD80 and CD86, has not been reported in cholangiocytes, although they express other members of the B7 family, namely PD-L1 and CD40 (157, 164). Interestingly, following an upregulation of MHC I expression, CMV infected cholangiocytes can stimulate CD8+ T cell proliferation in vitro, suggesting a co-stimulatory molecule is expressed on cholangiocytes that enable cytotoxic T cell activation (162). In contrast, cholangiocytes were unable to activate CD4+ T cells without the addition of CD28 agonistic molecule (165). Therefore, the ability of cholangiocytes to express peptide antigens have not been established. However, cholangiocytes can present non-peptide antigens which are uniquely recognized by unconventional T cells. Although not strictly adaptive immune cells, unconventional T cells (iNKT and MAIT cells in particular), are significantly enriched in the liver (167–169). iNKT and MAIT cells possess semi-invariant T cell receptors that recognize non-peptide antigens. Cholangiocytes (in both mice and human) express the antigen presenting molecule CD1d which displays the glycolipid α-galactosylceramide, a highly potent activator of iNKT cells (170). Interestingly, CD1d expression on cholangiocytes is downregulated in PSC and PBC. The expression of the MAIT cell antigen presenting molecule, MR-1, on cholangiocytes was indirectly confirmed with in vitro experiments that demonstrate an attenuated activation of MAIT cells when co-cultured with cholangiocytes in the presence of anti-MR1 blocking antibody (171). Furthermore, this study also confirmed the presence of cholangiocyte-specific chemokine receptors on human MAIT cells (CXCR3, CXCR6 and CCR6). Recently, crosstalk between cholangiocyte and MAIT cells has been dissected in BA whereby MAIT cells drive cholangiocyte proliferation in an AREG dependent pathway (172).

Much remains to be understood regarding the mechanisms of cholangiocyte dysfunction in disease. Future advances will need to exploit new technical advances and address conceptual and experimental gaps in our knowledge. The field of cholangiocyte biology requires advanced in vitro models to mimic human physiology and disease employing human cholangiocytes, immune cells and fibroblasts. Although three dimensional organoids have been developed, the field needs reliable and reproduceable multicellular organoids to dissect the crosstalk occurring between cell types. This technology may be aided by bioprinting and microfluidic devices. The ability to employ modifiable hydrogels to serve as a scaffold for human cholangiocytes may permit the development of bile duct-like structures with luminal flow of human bile and addition of other cell types (173). The ultimate goal would be to reproduce advanced “periductular” fibrosis and strictures to mimic human disease.

Experimentally, several pressing questions need to be addressed. The stiffness and/or viscoelasticity effects of the cholangiocyte extracellular matrix likely influences cholangiocyte biology. One could envision a feed-forward scenario where a stiff extracellular matrix promotes a cholangiocyte pro-fibrogenic and -inflammatory phenotype, further increasing fibrosis and inflammation. Hence the cholangiocyte-driven fibrogenic process could be self-sustaining, explaining why in biliary atresia the biliary fibrosis progresses despite drainage of the extrahepatic obstruction by a successful surgical hepato-portoenterostomy known as the Kasai procedure. The mode of cell death occurring in cholangiocytes in various disease processes is simply unknown in vivo. Cholangiocytes may undergo apoptosis and necroptosis (174, 175). However, whether cholangiocytes undergo pyroptosis or ferroptosis is unclear, and more likely these cell death pathways may lead to sub-lethal injury, causing aberrant signaling pathways stimulating inflammatory cascades (e.g., apoptotic stress causes mitochondrial DNA release activating inflammatory cascades via the cGAS-STING pathway, etc.) (176). Regarding senescence, we do not understand the propensity of cholangiocytes to undergo senescence and what is the molecular switch driving this phenotype. Recently, many bacterial-modified bile acids have been identified (177, 178). We need further information regarding the presence of these bile acids in bile, their transport and engagement of known bile acid receptors in cholangiocytes, and their effects on cholangiocyte signaling processes. In addition to bile acids, bile may contain many other immune mediators and toxic compounds. For example, bile from patients with PSC contains MAIT cells antigens (179), and bile may also contain other inflammatory mediators, such as oxysterols (180). The question remains: Are selected cholangiopathies driven by constituents in bile and therefore be considered a “bileopathy”?

Many patients with PSC have an intergenic haplotype on chromosome 21q22 which is associated with enhanced expression of ETS2, a central regulator of human inflammatory macrophages (122). ETS2 expression is regulated by mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase enzymes termed MEK1 and MEK2. MEK inhibitors, which are FDA-approved to treat human cancers, may be useful in attenuating macrophage-associated inflammation in cholestatic liver diseases; a concept which needs to be explored in preclinical studies.

The relationship between the microbiome and cholangiocyte pathobiology is also inadequately understood despite evolving literature on this topic (181). The microbiome consists of bacteria, fungi and viruses, and all will need to be taken into account; fungal species and viruses are often neglected and not profiled. There are also four microbiomes to consider, including: 1) cross talk of microbial metabolites and products from the colon entering the portal circulation and transported into bile; 2) the microbiome of the bile, likely due to upper gut microbial species migrating by extension into the bile duct lumen; 3) intracellular bacteria, which have been identified in tumors, but likely exist in diseased cells as well (182); and 4) the tissue resident microbiome, which has also been described in liver (183). As mentioned above, bacteria in the gastrointestinal lumen generate many species of bile acids, but also bile acids reciprocally alter the microbiome (184). In particular, the microbiome in bile and perhaps within cholangiocytes needs to be further explored as mediators of aberrant cholangiocyte structure and function.

This list is not exhaustive, but instead highlights relevant knowledge gaps that can be addressed with current methodologies. We hope these questions will guide future research priorities and underscore their importance to human health for funding agencies. We eagerly anticipate future answers to these and other questions.

Despite making up a minority cell population in the liver (approximately 5% of liver cells), cholangiocytes are crucial for health. Extensive information exists about their regulated transport functions, contributions to bile composition and flow, and regenerative capacity (1). Despite this progress, our knowledge of cholangiopathies remains insufficient regarding disease mechanisms. Current scientific research, therefore, is focused on the role of cholangiocytes in liver immunobiology, inflammation, and fibrosis - a critical area of inquiry for unraveling the pathogenic mechanisms contributing to initiation and progression of the cholangiopathies. Without such information, the rational development of therapies has been impeded. We encourage further research on these processes, which we hope will lead to improved therapies for these diseases.
