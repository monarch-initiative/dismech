# Keratin 9 regulates mechanosensitive YAP1 signalling in palmoplantar epidermis.
**Authors:** Steiner SN, Horst EN, Chiu ES, Athaiya M, Johnson CN, Shen JY, Kerns ML, Kirma JD, McCarthy RL, Shah SFH, Sarig O, Samuelov L, Sprecher E, O'Toole EA, Gudjonsson JE, Mehta G, Iglesias-Bartolome R, Coulombe PA
**Journal:** Nat Cell Biol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41556-026-02087-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41556-026-02087-4)

## Content

The palmoplantar epidermis, in adapting to the exceptional mechanical strain it bears during locomotion, is histologically and molecularly distinct from other skin body sites. The mechanisms specifying and maintaining its unique identity remain incompletely defined. Here we identify the type I keratin 9 (KRT9/K9), a protein uniquely expressed in palmoplantar epidermis, as a key modulator of mechanosensitive YAP1 signalling in response to postnatal mechanical compression. K9 loss-of-function variants driving KRT9-pEDD, a palmoplantar epidermal differentiation disorder, result in aberrant YAP1 subcellular partitioning and elevated levels of the stress-induced keratin 16 (KRT16/K16). Krt9 null mice recapitulate these molecular phenotypes as early as postnatal day 3. We further identify dynamic, YAP1-dependent regulation of Krt16/K16, upstream of and necessary for Krt9/K9 expression, during early postnatal development in situ and in response to mechanical compression of keratinocytes ex vivo, highlighting the role of mechanical stress in epidermal specification. Mechanistically, K9 interacts with the YAP1 binding protein 14-3-3σ and sequesters YAP1 in the cytoplasm, inhibiting its transcriptional activity; these functions are disrupted by KRT9-pEDD-causing variants in K9. Finally, we show that genetic or pharmacological inhibition of YAP1 ameliorates palmoplantar keratoderma in Krt9 null mice. These findings reveal a role for mechanical cues in specification and maintenance of palmoplantar skin and suggest new therapeutic interventions for inherited palmoplantar epidermal differentiation disorders.

Specification of gene expression, cell fate and tissue function is orchestrated by dynamic interplay between developmental cues and environmental factors. The epidermis offers a notable example of this complexity; as the body’s primary barrier, it mediates essential and multimodal protective functions while adapting to diverse cues and pressures 1 . The epidermis of palmoplantar skin, located on the ventral aspect of the hands and feet, endures a substantial mechanical burden during locomotion and has unique histological and molecular adaptations 2 , 3 , among them the expression of keratin 9 (gene KRT9; protein K9) and keratin 16 (KRT16/K16) in its differentiating layers 2 .

Keratins are an abundant and diverse family of intermediate filament (IF)-forming proteins expressed primarily in epithelial cells across the body 4 . The regulation of keratin protein expression is tightly choreographed depending on body site, differentiation and context, including stress 5 . Two distinct types of keratins, I and II, interact in an obligatory fashion to yield mature intermediate filaments 6 . K9 (KRT9/K9) is a type I keratin that is uniquely expressed in the suprabasal (differentiating) layers of human and mouse epidermis 2 , 3 , 7 , 8 . K9’s presumptive type II polymerization partners are keratin 1 (KRT1/K1) and keratin 2 (KRT2/K2), respectively at early and late stages of differentiation 2 , 9 . Pathogenic variants in KRT9/K9 cause KRT9 palmoplantar epidermal differentiation disorder (KRT9-pEDD), formerly epidermolytic palmoplantar keratoderma (EPPK), an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by fragility of suprabasal, differentiating keratinocytes, acanthosis, and hyperkeratosis in palmoplantar skin 10 , 11 , suggesting a loss of mechanical integrity and defects in tissue homeostasis 10 . Krt9−/− mice recapitulate a clear KRT9-pEDD-like phenotype 12 , developing macroscopic lesions in the footpad skin by 3.5 weeks of age, tissue fragility and hyperkeratosis. There is no successful clinical treatment for KRT9-pEDD, and the molecular mechanisms underpinning the aetiology of the palmoplantar lesions are not well understood.

While KRT9-pEDD is a rare disease with an estimated worldwide incidence of 2.2–4.4 per 100,000 live newborns 13 , the clinical presentation of PPK arises from pathogenic variants in over 50 different genes 11 . For example, pathogenic variants in K16 (KRT16/K16), a type I keratin associated with stress response in interfollicular epidermis 14 , result in pachyonychia congenita (KRT16-pEDD-PC), a condition that entails severe, non-epidermolytic PPK 15 . Incidentally, studies have illuminated a potential link between KRT16/K16 and KRT9/K9 in palmoplantar skin. KRT9/K9 levels are decreased in PPK lesions of individuals with pEDD-PC 16 and in Krt16−/− mice 17 , which spontaneously develop PPK-like lesions. K16, on the other hand, is strongly upregulated in Krt9−/− mice 12 . The dynamic interplay between these two keratins in palmoplantar skin is underappreciated and its importance unresolved.

Here, we identify a role for K9 in negatively modulating YAP1 signalling in human and mouse palmoplantar epidermis, suggesting a pathogenic mechanism relevant to KRT9-pEDD and other palmoplantar epidermal differentiation disorders (pEDDs). We show that loss of K9 function in humans through dominant-negative missense alleles disrupts YAP1 subcellular partitioning and induces upregulation of K16. Krt9−/− mice recapitulate these molecular phenotypes, starting in early postnatal development. We demonstrate that, in mice, Krt9/K9 expression increases after a transient spike in Krt16/K16 in early perinatal life. This spike is YAP1-dependent, and ex vivo mechanical compression is sufficient to induce KRT16 and then KRT9 in a YAP1-dependent manner, revealing a link between mechanotransduction and body site-specific identity in the palmoplantar epidermis. We also show that K9 is sufficient to regulate the partitioning of YAP1 between the cytoplasm and nucleus in vitro, whereas KRT9 pathogenic variants fail to do so. Crucially, we find that pharmacological or genetic inhibition of YAP1 rescues the KRT9-pEDD-like lesions and reduces Krt16/K16 expression in the Krt9−/− mouse. Our findings provide mechanistic insight into the specification and maintenance of palmoplantar keratinocyte identity, illuminate the crosstalk between mechanical cues and development, and suggest potential clinical interventions for KRT9-pEDD and possibly other pEDDs.

Previous studies have identified mechanical fragility in the clinically-involved palmoplantar skin of KRT9-pEDD patients 10 . We investigated whether KRT9-pEDD lesions exhibit defects in YAP1 signalling, a mechanotransducive pathway of relevance to skin 18 , 19 . YAP1that has been implicated in the balance of proliferation and differentiation in keratinocytes of the interfollicular epidermis 20 , 21 . Punch biopsies obtained from lesional skin of patients with KRT9-pEDD, and trunk and plantar skin from healthy volunteers, were collected and stained via immunofluorescence (IF) for YAP1 and K9 (Fig. 1a). Variants in KRT9 were confirmed (p.R163W, p.N161S and p.M156V). Using keratin 14 (KRT14/K14) to mark all progenitor keratinocytes, we quantified the relative occurrence of nuclear YAP1 in progenitor (K14+) and differentiating (K14−) cells (Fig. 1b). Consistent with previous reports in trunk epidermis 22 , 23 , a strong IF signal for YAP1 occurs in both the nucleus and cytoplasm in basal keratinocytes of the trunk and palmoplantar epidermis, and otherwise YAP1 occurs as a weaker and diffuse signal in the cytoplasm of suprabasal keratinocytes in healthy skin (Fig. 1a). A small population of K14−, nuclear YAP1+ keratinocytes occurs in the terminally differentiating layers of both trunk and plantar human epidermis (Fig. 1a–c). By contrast, lesional skin from patients with KRT9-pEDD exhibited strong nuclear YAP1 signal in the suprabasal epidermis (Fig. 1a–c), expanding the K14−, nuclear YAP1+ population by almost tenfold. These YAP1+ nuclei occurred significantly further from the basal epidermis compared with either healthy trunk or plantar epidermis, correlating with the increased thickness of the K14− suprabasal layers (Fig. 1c,d). In keratinocytes of healthy plantar epidermis, high K9 fluorescence intensity was negatively correlated to nuclear YAP1 intensity (Pearson r = −0.2615; P = 0.0015). In KRT9-pEDD palmoplantar epidermis, however, higher K9 expression instead positively correlated to nuclear YAP1 fluorescence intensity (Pearson r = 0.2954, P = 0.0016) (Fig. 1e). These data reveals aberrant suprabasal nuclear YAP1 as a new characteristic of lesional skin in KRT9-pEDD. Unlike trunk skin, K16 is constitutively expressed in healthy plantar skin epidermis 2 , 17 , but this is significantly increased in samples from patients with KRT9-pEDDs (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b). In samples from patients with KRT9-pEDD, K16 and K9 fluorescence intensity do not have any meaningful correlation, and K16 fluorescence intensity is high irrespective of the K9 level in individual cells (Extended Data Fig. 1c). K9 and K16 staining display a significant negative correlation in individual cells of healthy palmoplantar epidermis (Extended Data Fig. 1c), a finding corroborated on the transcript level through single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis (Fig. 1d; ref. 7 ). In samples from patients with KRT9-pEDD, however, K16 and K9 fluorescence intensity do not have any meaningful correlation, and K16 fluorescence intensity is high irrespective of the K9 level in individual cells (Extended Data Fig. 1c). This contrasts with other differentiation-specific keratins; for instance, KRT9 and KRT10 exhibit a strong positive correlation (Extended Data Fig. 1d), as do KRT9 and its presumptive type 2 partner KRT1, and KRT16 and its type 2 partner KRT6A (Extended Data Fig. 1d). The top 10% of KRT9-expressing cells exhibit enrichment in Gene Ontology terms relating to formation of the cornified envelope, programmed cell death and keratinocyte differentiation, while the top 10% of KRT16-expressing cells enrich terms relating to innate immune activation and wound healing 5 (Extended Data Fig. 1e). In summary, patients with KRT9-pEDD experience both dysregulation of YAP1 subcellular portioning and increased K16 expression. These observations suggest that K9 may negatively modulate YAP1 nuclear localization and K16 expression in healthy human plantar epidermis.

To test the molecular mechanism(s) underpinning the formation of KRT9-pEDD keratodermas, we set out to identify potential YAP1 defects in a mouse model of KRT9-pEDD. Krt9−/− mice, as related above, develop clear KRT9-pEDD-like manifestations by 2.5–3 weeks of age 12 (Extended Data Fig. 2), and exhibit abnormally thickened palmoplantar epidermis by postnatal day 10 (P10) (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). Furthermore, cross-sectional cellular area was increased in Krt9−/− palmoplantar keratinocytes at 3.5 weeks of age (Extended Data Fig. 2c,d). Accordingly, we focused our attention on the first 10 days of life to detect early molecular alterations. At postnatal day 0 (P0) wild-type (WT) and Krt9−/− mice have similar YAP1 localization throughout the palmoplantar epidermis (Fig. 2a,d). As early as P3, however, Krt9−/− mice exhibit aberrant nuclear-localized YAP1 in the suprabasal layers as observed by immunofluorescence (Fig. 2b,d). This phenotype has worsened by 3.5 weeks of age, when macroscopic lesions are observable in Krt9−/− mice (Fig. 2c,d). While overall levels of YAP1 are unaltered (Fig. 2e), we find that the ratio of phosphorylated (Ser127) YAP1 to total YAP1 is significantly attenuated in Krt9−/− animals at P3 via western blot (WB) (Fig. 2e). Phosphorylated YAP1 is the form sequestered in the cytoplasm and therefore inactive 24 ; the reduction of phospho-YAP1 in the footpad skin of Krt9−/− mice correlates to the increase in its nuclear localization. The YAP1 binding protein 14-3-3σ also shows significantly elevated levels in Krt9−/− animals by P3 (Fig. 2e). We next examined, via bulk RNA-seq, transcriptional patterns altered in the Krt9−/− mice relative to WT littermates at P0 and P3 (Fig. 2f,g). Notably, Krt19, previously identified as a YAP1 target gene 20 , 25 , was significantly upregulated in Krt9−/− mice at both P0 and P3. In this setting, we also identified that levels of K16 are similar in WT and Krt9−/− littermates at P0 but are elevated in Krt9−/− animals, relative to WT, at P3 (Extended Data Fig. 2e). These findings mirror those in adult human KRT9-pEDD lesions, provide insight into the developmental onset of KRT9-pEDD-like phenotypes, and suggest that an increase in nuclear YAP1 may be one of the earliest molecular alterations in the aetiology of KRT9-pEDD-like lesions. Given the early postnatal emergence of the Krt9−/− phenotype, we next examined the postnatal expression patterns of keratins in the developing palmoplantar epidermis of WT mice. Mice were collected at embryonic day 18.5 (E18.5), postnatal day 0 (P0), P1, P3 and P10. Whole paws were subjected to TMT-labelled quantitative mass spectrometry (TMT-MS) analysis. Upon examination of all intermediate filament species, only five displayed dynamic regulation between E18.5 and P10: keratin 16 (K16), keratin 6 isoform b (K6B), keratin 17 (K17), keratin 2 (K2) and K9 (Fig. 3a). These dynamics include a shift from K6/K16 dominance at P0–P1 to K2/K9 dominance at P3–P10. The dynamic regulation of K16 was independently confirmed via WB of total protein extracts prepared from whole paws of WT animals at E18.5, P0, P1, P2, P3, P10 and 3.5 weeks of age (Extended Data Fig. 2f,g). We further examined these temporal patterns at the transcriptional level via bulk RNA-seq of whole paws collected from WT animals at P0 and P3. Mirroring the protein-level data, Krt16, Krt6a and Krt6b transcripts are enriched in P0 animals, whereas Krt2 and Krt9 transcripts are enriched in P3 samples (Fig. 3b). To test whether the postnatal dynamics of Krt9/K9 and Krt16/K16 are YAP1-responsive in normal development, we utilized a recently described mouse line in which the expression of a synthetic TEAD inhibitor (TEADi) is placed under dual doxycycline (ROSA26LSL-rtTA; ref. 26 ) and tamoxifen (K14CreERT; ref. 27 ) control in basal epidermal keratinocytes 20 (Extended Data Fig. 3a). YAP1 binding to TEAD factors is essential for its transcriptional effects 28 ; hence, blocking YAP1 binding to TEAD should eliminate its transcriptional activity while leaving the localization, phosphorylation and expression of endogenous YAP1 intact 20 . TEADi+ males (K14CreERT +/+; ROSA26LSL-rtTA +/+; tetO-TEADi +/−) were crossed with WT C57Bl/6J females. Pregnant females were transferred to doxycycline chow at E17.5 and intraperitoneal injection of 60 mg kg−1 tamoxifen was performed at E18.5 (Extended Data Fig. 3b). Pups were collected and analysed at P1, when Krt16/K16 is transiently elevated in WT contexts, and at P2, when Krt9 levels begin to increase in WT palmoplantar skin. At P1, TEADi+ mice exhibited significantly reduced K16 staining via immunofluorescence (Fig. 3c,e), reduced K16 protein via WB (Extended Data Fig. 3d,e), and reduced Krt16 transcript via quantitative PCR with reverse transcription (RT–qPCR) relative to their TEADi− littermates (Fig. 3f). This delayed Krt16/K16 spike at P1 in TEADi+ perinatal mice resulted in a later Krt9 onset: Krt9 transcript levels were attenuated in P2 TEADi+ mice relative to WT littermates (Fig. 3g). Furthermore, Krt16/K16 levels were elevated in TEADi+ mice relative to their TEADi− littermates at P2 (Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 3f, g). These data together suggest that the observed Krt16/K16 dynamics in early perinatal development are partially YAP1-dependent and are important for subsequent initiation of Krt9 expression.

To examine whether YAP1-dependent regulation of KRT16 occurs via direct binding upstream of the transcription start site (TSS), we utilized a modified N/TERT2G culture protocol 29 , 30 to generate a post-confluent, uniformly bilayered epithelium in submerged culture. Under such conditions, expression of keratinocyte differentiation markers, for example, K10 and K16, is exclusive to the upper layer of keratinocytes 30 . The KRT16 locus has three candidate TEAD binding sites within 1 kb upstream of the TSS, whereas KRT10 has only one (Extended Data Fig. 3h). ChIP–qPCR assays confirmed binding of YAP1 to two of three candidate KRT16 TEAD sites, while it does not bind to the candidate TEAD site upstream of KRT10 (Fig. 3h). Taken together, we observed that Krt16/K16 is transiently upregulated, in a YAP1-dependent fashion, in the postnatal palmoplantar epidermis. Delaying or reducing this YAP1-responsive increase in Krt16/K16 negatively impacts the onset of Krt9 expression. Based on ChIP assays, KRT16 is a likely YAP1 target gene in differentiating keratinocytes. As Krt16/K16 is typically considered a stress-responsive keratin 5 , 31– 33 , we hypothesized that this phenomenon occurs in response to mechanical compression during the first use of the developing paw pads, for example, nursing and early locomotion.

We employed a compression bioreactor 34 to assess whether KRT16 and KRT9 are responsive to mechanical compression in keratinocyte cultures ex vivo. In brief, cells were suspended in an agarose/ collagen type 1 hydrogel and subjected to mechanical force; this three-dimensional (3D) hydrogel culture removed the contribution of cell–cell contacts to mechanosensing. N/TERT keratinocytes, selected as a substitute for human primary keratinocytes 29 , 35 , were suspended in a 1% agarose/1 mg ml−1 collagen type I hydrogel, approximating the Young’s modulus of viable epidermis of the plantar surface 36 , 37 , and subjected to 10 kPa of static compression for 24 h. N/TERT cells were also cultured in parallel under identical conditions but without compression (Fig. 4a and Extended Data Fig. 4a). To examine whether compression-induced transcriptional changes were YAP1-dependent, we further utilized the YAP1 inhibitor verteporfin (VERT). VERT inhibits YAP1 transcriptional activity by re-localizing it to the cytoplasm, though the specific mechanism involved may depend on biological context 38– 40 . Inhibition efficiency was evaluated via transcription of CYR61, a canonical YAP1 target gene 21 , 41 . In N/TERT keratinocytes post-compression, key keratin transcripts normally expressed in the palmoplantar epidermis—KRT9 (Fig. 4b), KRT17 (Fig. 4d) and KRT16 (Fig. 4e)—were significantly upregulated, suggesting that their expression is responsive to mechanical stress. The same genes did not respond to compression in cultures treated with 2.5 µM VERT (Fig. 4b,e). Similarly, CYR61 was induced by mechanical compression and inhibited by VERT treatment (Fig. 4c). By contrast, Actin was upregulated in response to compression but not significantly inhibited by VERT treatment (Fig. 4f), and nor was RPS6, a control gene (Fig. 4g). To test the interplay between KRT16 and KRT9 under mechanical compression, we employed KRT16 knockout N/TERT keratinocytes 30 . Notably, N/ TERT cells null for KRT16 failed to upregulate KRT9 while maintaining an increase in KRT6 and Actin in response to compression (Fig. 4h). For additional characterization, human A431 epidermal carcinoma cells were selected for their epithelial character and the absence of both KRT9 and KRT16 expression at baseline. We further examined whether mechanical compression impacts transcription of other genes involved in epidermal homeostasis (Extended Data Fig. 4e). No other tested keratin transcripts displayed a compression-responsive upregulation; of the other genes tested, only KLF4 and WNT5A displayed upregulation in post-compression A431 cells (Extended Data Fig. 4e). Both WNT5A and KLF4 have been implicated in KRT9 regulation in vivo 42– 44 . Taken together, these data suggest that palmoplantar-enriched keratins are responsive to mechanical compression in a YAP1-dependent fashion, and that KRT9 induction under compression is dependent on KRT16.

Like most cytoplasmic partners that anchor YAP1 in the cytoplasm (for example, catenins 22 , 49 ), keratin proteins are unlikely to regulate YAP1 via direct binding. Instead, cytoplasmic anchors such as catenins or K14 rely on 14-3-3 family isoforms, which bind both phosphorylated YAP1 and a phosphorylated residue on the cytoplasmic partner to form a ternary complex 22 , 47 . Phosphorylation of keratins is well-known to modulate their organization, solubility and interactions with signalling effectors 50– 53 . 14-3-3σ is a 14-3-3 family protein enriched in stratified epithelia 23 and previously shown to bind K14 (ref. 47 ) and promote keratinocyte differentiation in epidermis 23 . To test whether K9 interacts with 14-3-3σ, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) in HeLa cells co-transfected with HA-tagged 14-3-3σ and WT K9−GFP (Extended Data Fig. 5b). Upon HA pulldown, we observed co-IP of both WT K9−GFP and of EGFP−K14, a known 14-3-3σ interactor 47 , indicating that these proteins physically interact. For many type I keratins, including K18 (ref. 54 ), K14 (refs. 47 , 55 ), K17 (ref. 56 ) and K16 (ref. 30 ), 14-3-3 binding occurs on phosphoserines located in the N-terminal head domain. For human and mouse K9, however, computational algorithms 57 predict that 14-3-3 binding most likely occur at phosphoserines in the central α-helical rod domain (Extended Data Fig. 5c). Importantly, co-transfection of WT, K9 R163Q, or K9 C406A and its type 2 partner K1 in HeLa cells did not change the overall YAP1 or 14-3-3σ protein levels (Extended Data Fig. 5d,e) or alter the nuclear or cellular area of HeLa cells (Extended Data Fig. 5f).

To assess whether the interaction between K9 and 14-3-3σ holds in other cell culture systems and is impacted by KRT9-pEDD-causing variants, we performed proximity ligation assay (PLA) between transfected keratins and endogenous 14-3-3σ in A431 cells (Fig. 5d,e). A431 cells expressing WT K9−GFP showed an average of 7.7 PLA ± 2.1 puncta per cell, a level comparable with cells expressing WT EGFP−K14 (Fig. 5d,e). Cells expressing either the K9 R163Q (3.9 ± 1.8 PLA puncta per cell) or K9 C406A (4.5 ± 1.2 PLA puncta per cell) variants showed significantly reduced physical proximity with 14-3-3σ (Fig. 5d,e). Transfection of WT or mutant K9 did not affect total 14-3-3σ protein levels as shown via WB (Extended Data Fig. 5d). These data show that the property of cytoplasmic sequestration of YAP1 is conserved in K14 and K9 but not in K16, and that the introduction of pathogenic variants in K9 disrupts its ability to form a ternary complex with 14-3-3σ/YAP1 and thereby negatively regulate the transcriptional activity of YAP1.

To that end, we first employed a pharmacological approach using verteporfin (VERT). Optimization studies showed that treatment 2.5 µM VERT significantly reduced the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio of YAP1 (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b) and restored YAP1 cytoplasmic localization in transfected cells expressing K9 R163Q and K9 C406A (Extended Data Fig. 6c–e). By contrast, no enhancement of YAP1 cytoplasmic sequestration was observed in cells transfected with WT EGFP−K14 or WT K9−GFP and treated with VERT (Extended Data Fig. 6c–e). VERT treatment of HeLa cells concurrently increased the phosphorylated-to-total YAP1 ratio (Extended Data Fig. 7a,b), whereas the steady-state levels of total YAP1 or 14-3-3σ proteins remained normal under these treatment conditions (Extended Data Fig. 7a, b).

YAP1 and the pro-differentiation transcription factor KLF4 have been reported to interact and oppose one another in epidermal differentiation 20 . Of note, WT animals treated with VERT experience an increase in Krt9 transcription (Extended Data Fig. 8d), suggesting a negative relationship between YAP1 activity and Krt9/K9 expression. Given previous reports that Krt9 is a KLF4 target gene 42 , we further examined KLF4 localization in WT and Krt9−/− mice treated with VERT. The lesional epidermis of vehicle-treated Krt9−/− mice exhibited a significantly increased fraction of KLF4+ nuclei, relative to their WT littermates, whereas Krt9−/− animals treated with VERT experienced a normalization of the fraction of KLF4+ nuclei (Extended Data Fig. 8e,f). These differences are likely not due to YAP1’s direct effect on the proliferative capacity of epidermal keratinocytes, as determined via Ki67 staining; indeed, aberrant nuclear YAP1 occurs primarily in the suprabasal layers, while Ki67 staining remains confined to the basal layers (Extended Data Fig. 8g–i). These data suggest that dysregulation of YAP1 subcellular partitioning in the Krt9−/− mice participates in formation of KRT9-pEDD-like lesions, and that pharmacological inhibition of YAP1 is a promising avenue to resolve these PPKs.

In this study, we uncover a signalling function for K9 in safeguarding homeostasis during intense mechanical stress in the palmoplantar epidermis by driving the cytoplasmic sequestration of YAP1, a notable mechanotransducive transcriptional effector. We illuminate the YAP1-dependent postnatal regulation of Krt16/K16 and Krt9/K9 in this specialized skin site, likely in response to mechanical forces. Mechanistically, we find that K9 forms a complex with 14-3-3σ to sequester and transcriptionally inactivate YAP1. Our findings establish a causative link between loss of proper YAP1 regulation and hyperkeratosis of the palmoplantar skin, suggesting a therapeutic target for KRT9-pEDD and other disorders of palmoplantar epidermal differentiation.

While KRT9-pEDD is an individually rare condition, the clinical presentation of PPK is not. Variants in more than 50 genes result in disorders of differentiation and homeostasis in palmoplantar epidermis 11 . Accordingly, a better understanding of how homeostasis is established (and disrupted) in this specialized skin site stands to provide insight with potential clinical relevance for a wide variety of disorders. Krt9/K9’s temporal dynamics in the developing palmoplantar epidermis distinguish it from related members of the keratin family. We find that, unlike Krt1 (K9’s presumptive assembly partner) or Krt10, Krt9/K9 expression does not become robust until a few days postnatally, correlating with dramatic changes to mechanical demands placed on palmoplantar skin. The development-, differentiation- and stress-dependent co-regulation of type I and type II keratin genes as pairs (for example, Krt1/Krt10) is a prominent feature in epidermis 5 , 60 , making Krt9/K9 stand out through its dynamic regulation. Keratin proteins, including K9, are widely appreciated for their contribution to mechanical resilience 12 , 61 , 62 , and are also known to impact nuclear mechanosensing via interaction with, and force transmission through, plectins 63 . The living layers of the palmoplantar epidermis also possess remarkably different mechanical properties relative to thin epidermis 36 . While our results suggest that Krt9/K9 expression may occur in response to mechanical stress, whether and how the K9 protein conveys increased mechanical resilience to keratinocytes remains to be explored.

Previous studies of the specification of palmoplantar skin have identified factors involved in KRT9/K9 regulation, particularly secreted factors modulating palmoplantar identity in the epidermis and underlying mesenchyme. Ex vivo co-culture of non-palmoplantar keratinocytes with palmoplantar fibroblasts highlighted the ability of mesenchymal cues to induce KRT9 expression 8 , 64 , 65 . Fibroblasts in the distal limb exhibit a unique HOX code that is instrumental in enabling the secretion of WNT5A 44 , which is sufficient to induce KRT9 expression in non-palmoplantar human keratinocytes 8 , 66 . Furthermore, keratinocyte-intrinsic factors play a key part in modulating palmoplantar epidermal differentiation and expression of KRT9/K9. For example, variants in WNT10A result in loss of KRT9/K9 expression and the manifestation of PPK, caused in part by hypoactivation of the transcription factor KLF4 in the differentiating layers of the palmoplantar epidermis 42 . Such factors most likely mediate the competency of the palmoplantar epidermis to express Krt9/K9 in response to mechanical cues over developmental time. The situation differs in interfollicular epidermis, where these mesenchymal cues are absent and mechanical compression is unlikely to initiate Krt9/K9 expression.

We identify Krt16/K16, responding to early postnatal mechanical stress, as an important contributor to robust Krt9/K9 expression in the early postnatal period in mice. We have previously reported that Krt16−/− mice fail to robustly express Krt9/K9 during postnatal development, although the mechanism remained undefined 17 . Here, we observed a transient rise and fall of Krt16/K16 before the robust increase of Krt9/K9 in normal developing palmoplantar epidermis; however, this elevation of Krt16/K16 persists and is amplified in Krt9−/− mice. YAP1-induced expression of Krt16/K16 results in a yet-to-be-identified signal that triggers expression of Krt9/K9 during the establishment of normal palmoplantar homeostasis. As we report here, YAP1 inhibition directly reduces the abnormally high Krt16/K16 expression in footpad skin lesions of Krt9−/− mice, suggesting that this signalling axis is relevant to disease contexts. Previous work has indicated the Krt16/K16 is also elevated in the Krt10−/−/Krt2−/− double-null mice, which experience compensatory Krt9 upregulation 67 . Our data suggest that while Krt16 is directly responsive to YAP1, its K16 protein product is not intrinsically competent to modulate its subcellular distribution and, consequently, its transcriptional activity. Instead, Krt9/K9 expression is required to inactivate YAP1 in the palmoplantar epidermis, dampening Krt16/K16 expression and mediating the tissue’s adaptation to the experience of mechanical stress. We describe here the interdependency between two keratin proteins in maintaining tissue homeostasis, providing context for examining diverse epithelial diseases.

YAP1 is a crucial effector of mechanotransduction 18 , and its role in epidermal homeostasis is well established 21 , 68 . Consistent with previous literature, we find that YAP1 occurs at low levels in the nucleus of differentiating keratinocytes 22 , 23 , 68 , 69 , while it prominently localizes to the nucleus in keratinocytes within the basal layer 70 , 71 . Our previous work has identified keratins as crucial regulators of this phenomenon; we have proposed a ‘gating’ effect in the progenitor compartment that depends on the occurrence of keratin species with conserved cysteines in the stutter motif of the coil 2 domain 47 . Our original model argues that disulfide-bonded, K14-containing keratin filaments bind 14-3-3σ in a phosphorylation-dependent manner and sequester YAP1 in the cytoplasm, after which keratinocyte differentiation can proceed. As keratinocytes differentiate, however, K14 ceases to be made, and K10 and, in the palmoplantar epidermis, K9, become the predominant type I keratin species featuring stutter cysteine residues. We therefore predicted the involvement of a ‘handoff’ mechanism whereby cytoplasmic sequestration of YAP1 would necessitate, as differentiation proceeds, the involvement of a differentiation-specific keratin that features the stutter cysteine and can bind 14-3-3 isoforms 47 . Notably, K16 is differentiation-specific, contains a stutter cysteine (Extended Data Fig. 5a), and interacts with 14-3-3 proteins 30 ; yet, it is not competent to sequester YAP1 (Fig. 5a) and its strong expression in KRT9-pEDD (Extended Data Fig. 1) and Krt9−/− plantar skin 12 does not rescue the nuclear YAP1 localization resulting from K9 loss. Such findings provide compelling evidence that K9 fulfils the specialized role of YAP1 sequestration in differentiating keratinocytes of palmoplantar epidermis. The KRT9 C406 → R variant is causative for KRT9-pEDD and represents the only known instance of a pathogenic variant altering the stutter cysteine in a keratin gene 46 . The data we report here suggest that, in addition to potentially causing fragility of the keratin cytoskeleton, KRT9-pEDD-causative pathogenic variants in KRT9 crucially fail to sequester YAP1 during differentiation.

An expanded model that incorporates recent findings on K15 (see 55 ) and the current findings on the differentiation-specific K9 is presented in Fig. 8. The model proposes that (1) expression of K15, which lacks a stutter cysteine, at sufficiently high levels promotes the progenitor state in the relevant subset of keratinocytes in the basal layer; that (2) the progressive buildup in K14 levels overcomes the influence of K15 and, once specified through post-translational modifications 47 , 51 , K14 promotes the entry of progenitor keratinocytes into differentiation; and that (3) either K9 (palmoplantar epidermis; Fig. 8) or K10 (interfollicular epidermis) is required to maintain keratinocytes into the differentiation programme. In circumstances of high mechanical strain, such as in palmoplantar epidermis, YAP1-induced K16 in the suprabasal layer provides a signal engaging the expression of Krt9/K9 (Fig. 8). The sequential expression of specific type I keratins in the epidermis (first K15; then K14, then K9 or K10) thus has a profound impact on the regulation of the balance between the progenitor and differentiating status of keratinocytes and their normal progression through terminal differentiation. Of note, our model does not propose that keratins regulate proliferation or differentiation per se; instead, the expression of specific type I keratins promotes cellular states along the homeostatic continuum by modulating effectors of progenitor identity, such as YAP1 (ref. 72 ). While the current model relies on the ability of select type I keratins to sequester YAP1 in the cytoplasm, several open questions remain. These include the precise mechanisms through which keratins engage 14-3-3 proteins (which are bivalent adaptor proteins that can function as heterodimers 49 , 73 ;), the role of site-specific phosphorylation on keratins, which is typically required for 14-3-3 interaction 49 , 57 , and the role of YAP1 when nuclear-localized in post-mitotic, differentiating keratinocytes. Fig. 8 Model of keratin-dependent regulation of YAP1 subcellular localization in palmoplantar epidermal differentiation. The sequential expression of type I keratins K15, K14 and K9 in palmoplantar epidermal keratinocytes plays important roles at three stages: (1) promoting progenitor identity in basal-layer keratinocytes (K15; ref. 55 ); (2) regulating the balance between proliferation and differentiation (K14; ref. 47 ); and (3) maintaining normal keratinocyte differentiation (K9; this study). The transcription of KRT15 and KRT14 is regulated by YAP1 (refs. 47 , 55 ), whereas KRT9 is not (this study). K14 (ref. 47 ) and K9 (this study), but not K15 (ref. 55 ), can effectively mediate the cytoplasmic sequestration of YAP1. This property is conferred by the presence of a cysteine at the second position of the four-residue stutter motif in coil 2 along with phosphorylation-dependent binding to 14-3-3σ. The model also proposes that a transient, YAP1-mediated increase in K16 expression occurs shortly after birth in mice and is required for the subsequent upregulation of Krt9/K9 as the footpad epidermis matures toward an adult-like state. Several aspects remain unresolved, including how K15 antagonizes K14 activity in this pathway and/or promotes the progenitor state, and how transient K16 expression leads to KRT9 expression (indicated by question marks). See Discussion for additional details.

The relationship between K9 and YAP1 in the palmoplantar epidermis may be consequential in diseases beyond KRT9-pEDD. Some diseases that manifest with PPK correlate with non-functional or low levels of KRT9/K9; most notably, a subset of individuals with pachyonychia congenita 16 have low levels of K9 in the lesional palmoplantar epidermis. Other palmoplantar epidermal differentiation disorders, such as those arising from variants in desmosome genes, interrupt the mechanical linkage of the keratin filament network to the periphery of the cell 74 , 75 . Additionally, individuals with WNT10A variants 42 develop PPK due to defects in KLF4 and the subsequent failure to robustly express KRT9/K9, and patients with variants directly in KLF4 also develop PPK 76 . Given the findings reported here, it is possible that a subset of the PPKs converge on the aetiology of failed K9 expression, leading to dysregulated YAP1. A survey of KRT9 expression and YAP1 localization and activity in various pEDDs may allow pharmacological inhibition of YAP1 to be a promising intervention for a subset of PPKs.
