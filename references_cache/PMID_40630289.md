INTRODUCTION: Primary membranous nephropathy (pMN) is a frequent cause of
nephrotic syndrome in adult patients without diabetes. Recognizing the major
shift in the classification of pMN based on target antigen and the management of
patients with pMN with more widespread use of rituximab (RTX), we sought to
better characterize the clinical course and risk factors in adults and children
with pMN.
METHODS: We used the Cure Glomerulonephropathy (CureGN) prospective cohort of
patients with pMN diagnosed using biopsy between 2010 and 2023. We report time
to kidney outcomes using adjusted Cox proportional hazards models.
RESULTS: In total, 591 patients (537 adults and 54 children) were evaluated with
9% reaching kidney failure. Anti-B cell therapy was used in 44% of patients. Age
< 18 years, self-reported Black/African American race, proteinuria > 3 g/g, and
lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) at enrollment were associated
with worse kidney survival. Black race (adjusted hazard ratio [HR]: 1.8; 95%
confidence interval [CI]: 1.1-2.9) and age < 18 years (adjusted HR: 3.7; 95% CI:
2.0-7.1) were associated with an increased risk of kidney failure and/or > 40%
eGFR decline after adjusting for exposure to immunosuppression. Latinx ethnicity
was associated with a lower likelihood of reaching complete proteinuria
remission (adjusted HR: 0.4; 95% CI: 0.2-0.7).
CONCLUSION: This study unveils self-reported Black race, young age (aged < 18
years) and Latinx ethnicity as potential risk factors associated with worse
kidney outcomes.

pMN is a leading cause of nephrotic syndrome in the adult population worldwide.1 It is characterized by accumulation of immune deposits on the outer (subepithelial) aspect of the glomerular basement membrane.2 Understanding of the pathophysiology and treatment of MN has advanced considerably in the past decade, particularly since the discovery that M-type phospholipase A2 receptor autoantibodies (antiPLA2R) are implicated in about 70% to 80% of pMN cases.3 PLA2R antigen is expressed on the podocyte, where it serves as target for antiPLA2R autoantibodies. Moreover, antibodies against other podocyte and nonpodocyte antigens (e.g., thrombospondin type-1 domain-containing 7A, neural epidermal growth factor-like 1 protein) have been detected in variable proportions of adults with pMN with clinical significance that continues to be characterized.4,5

Tangible progress in clinical care, patient outcomes, and novel therapies for pMN occurred slowly before the discovery of antiPLA2R. However, its discovery has sparked new interest in B-cell targeted immunosuppressive strategies for pMN. RTX has been shown in the MENTOR trial to be noninferior to cyclosporine in inducing remission in patients with pMN and superior in maintaining remission for up to 2 years.6
Post hoc analysis of this trial also suggests the importance of combining circulating antiPLA2R levels with other clinical risk factors (e.g., serum albumin) when assessing treatment response.7

The risk of progressive loss of kidney function influences decisions on immunosuppressive therapy (IST) initiation.1,2,8 Several “traditional” risk factors for disease progression have been described over the past decades and are reported in the 2021 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes guidelines.9 These include baseline level of proteinuria, eGFR, and antiPLA2R titers. However, no risk score model has been proposed in the literature and the role of emerging risk factors (e.g., presence of high risk APOL1 alleles in patients with West African ancestry) remains to be characterized. Finally, most large natural history studies of MN were performed before the antiPLA2R and RTX eras.10,11

Recognizing this major shift in the classification of pMN based on target antigen and the management of patients with pMN with more widespread use of RTX, this study aimed to better characterize disease trajectory, and novel risk factors for clinically meaningful outcomes in the large, contemporary pMN cohort in the CureGN study.

CureGN is an ongoing, multicenter prospective, observational cohort study of children and adults with biopsy-proven pMN or 3 other primary glomerular diseases (IgA nephropathy, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis and minimal change disease). Initial diagnostic kidney biopsies were performed within 5 years before study enrollment. Institutionalized patients; or those with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) at screening, diabetes, a previous organ or hematopoietic transplant, hepatitis B or C, HIV, malignancy, or systemic lupus erythematosus at time of biopsy, were excluded.12

For this analysis, all children and adults with pMN enrolled in CureGN from December 2014 to June 2023 were considered for inclusion. We restricted our study population to patients with available follow-up data for outcomes analyses. Whenever available, laboratory values measured between the time of symptom onset and the time of enrollment were retrospectively collected and recorded. All study subjects provided written informed consent. This study was approved by each institutional review board in agreement with the Declaration of Helsinki.

At study enrollment, data were collected regarding all ISTs used to treat glomerular disease before study enrollment, including start and stop dates. Thereafter, medication lists were updated at each study visit. We categorized IST as follows: glucocorticoid (GC) alone, calcineurin inhibitor (CNI) alone, CNI with GC, RTX-based therapy, mycophenolate mofetil–based therapy, or other. If GC were prescribed concurrently with another IST, we considered this to be therapy based on the other IST. Therapies started within 30 days of one another were considered part of the same regimen.

(i) Kidney failure was defined as 2 consecutive measures of eGFR < 15 ml/min per 1.73 m2 more than 1 month apart, or kidney transplant or chronic dialysis among patients with a completed enrollment visit. (ii) eGFR decline > 40% was based on decline from enrollment using regression method13 among patients with a completed enrollment visit. (iii) Composite outcome of kidney failure and >40% eGFR decline (outcome i and/or ii). (iv) Complete remission was defined as urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR) < 0.3 g/g, a negative or trace dipstick, or a reported remission. The complete remission analysis was only performed among incident patients enrolled within 6 months postkidney biopsy and who were not in complete remission at enrollment. Partial remission (defined as at least 50% reduction in UPCR to a level < 3.5 g/g, with improving or stable eGFR) was reported but not included in the primary analyses.

Adult was defined as age ≥ 18 years at biopsy. eGFR was calculated using CKiD-U25 formula14 if aged < 25 years or the Chronic Kidney Disease–Epidemiology Collaboration 2021 formula15 for older patients. Laboratory values at the time of biopsy were missing for up to 27% of participants, whereas laboratory values at the time of enrollment were missing for up to 10% participants.

Kidney biopsies underwent standardized semiquantitative evaluation (core scoring) of digitized whole slide images from the light microscopy slides, electron microscopic digital images, and using immunofluorescence findings taken from the reports.16 The following pathological features were studied as potential candidate predictors of kidney outcomes: (i) semiquantitative number of global glomerular sclerosing or obliterative lesions, (ii) semiquantitative number of segmental glomerular sclerosis lesions, (iii) semiquantitative interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (IFTA), (iv) dominant Ehrenreich and Churg membranous stage,17 (v) arteriosclerosis (0–3+) based on the most severely affected artery, and (vi) arteriolar hyalinosis or arteriolosclerosis (0–3+) based on the most severely affected arteriole.12 The range of Ehrenreich and Churg stages in a single biopsy was not assessed.

Descriptive analyses were performed using frequencies with percentages for categorical variables and medians with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables. Unadjusted comparisons across groups (e.g., Black vs. nonBlack) were made using chi-square and Kruskal-Wallis testing, as appropriate. Incidence rates were calculated using Poisson regression models where the denominator was unit time of participation to the CureGN cohort in participant-years. Cox proportional hazards models were constructed to assess the association between various potential risk factors of disease progression and time to kidney outcomes. All models were adjusted for self-reported race, UPCR, and eGFR at enrollment, and time from biopsy to enrollment. Models investigating the association between composite outcome of kidney failure and > 40% decline in eGFR were also adjusted for exposure to IST before enrollment and after enrollment. The IST exposure after enrollment was allowed to change over time (time varying), which was not the case for IST given before enrollment (nontime varying). PLA2R status was not included in the primary analyses (only in supplemental analyses) because 28% of patients had no biopsy staining for PLA2R and/or no antiPLA2R measurement during active disease (defined as UPCR > 1 g/g or 24-h proteinuria > 1 g/d within 30 days of enrollment). In a supplemental analysis, we explored the association between the presence of high-risk APOL1 alleles and kidney outcomes using our regression models. All analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc. Cary, NC).

During their disease course, 491 participants (83%) were exposed to any IST, with similar exposure between Black versus non-Black patients (87% vs. 83%, respectively). Similar proportions of patients were exposed to CNIs and RTX: 47% and 44%, respectively. Initially, RTX was received mostly (78% of treated patients) as two 1 g doses, 2 weeks apart. Cyclophosphamide was initiated in 152 (26%) patients, rarely in combination with GC (3 patients treated with a modified Ponticelli’s protocol). GCs alone was used in 57 patients (9.6%) during study follow-up.

Complete remission was reached at any time during follow-up in 349 of 591 patients (59%), and median time to complete remission was 13.7 months (IQR: 4.8–27.8 months). Partial remission was reached at any time during follow-up in 183 of 591 patients (31%). Ten percent of patients never achieved a complete or partial remission. Among the participants with available study follow-up, proteinuria < 1 g/g was achieved in 51% of patients exposed to RTX, 57% exposed to CNI, and 14% exposed to cyclophosphamide.

With respect to kidney end points, 51 patients (9%) reached kidney failure (8 children and 43 adults at the time of biopsy), 99 (17%) had a > 40% decline in eGFR and 115 (19%) experienced composite event of disease progression (kidney failure and 40% decline in eGFR). Kidney survival was worse in self-reported Black/African American patients (crude survival curves are presented in Figure 3): 19% experienced kidney failure (vs. 7% of non-Black patients), 28% reached > 40% eGFR decline (vs. 15% for non-Black patients) and 34% attained the composite outcome (vs. 17% for non-Black patients). Among patients with nephrotic-range proteinuria at enrollment, 14% reached kidney failure and 29% achieved the composite outcome. Patients with high-risk APOL1 genotype had a prevalence of ESKD of 58% (Table S1) whereas 58% also reached the composite outcome. Overall, kidney failure rate was 0.020 per participant-year. Kidney failure was higher in children (0.033 vs. 0.019 per participant-year for adults, P = 0.1610), in patients with self-reported Black/African American race (0.050 vs. 0.016 per participant-year for White patients, P < 0.0001) and in patients who experienced no complete remission (0.034 vs. 0.008 per participant-year for those who reached complete remission, P = 0.0083). The rate of eGFR decline > 40% was 0.042 per patient-year for both children and adults. Patients with self-reported Black/African America race and those with a history of disease relapse had significantly higher rates of eGFR decline > 40%.

Twenty-two patients died during their follow-up, with a median age of 74 (IQR: 56–93) years. The death rate for the overall cohort was 0.009 per participant-year with a higher rate for patients with eGFR ≤ 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 at enrollment (0.019 per participant-year vs. 0.003 for those with eGFR > 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2, P < 0.0001).

Patients with self-reported Black race had a decreased likelihood of reaching complete proteinuria remission in our unadjusted Cox survival model. This association was no longer significant after controlling for immunosuppression exposure (Table 3). Latinx ethnicity was associated with lower adjusted hazards of achieving complete remission (HR: 0.4; 95% CI: 0.2–0.7). Children had an increased likelihood of reaching complete remission in both unadjusted and adjusted Cox survival models (HR: 2.8 for the latter; 95% CI: 1.5–5.0). Exposure to immunosuppression after enrollment was associated with higher hazards of achieving complete proteinuria remission (adjusted HR: 1.9; 95% CI: 1.0–3.6). pMN is a frequent cause of nephrotic syndrome in adult patients without diabetes.1 In this large international cohort of patients with pMN, potential risk factors for kidney disease progression were assessed, adjusting for treatment (> 40% of patients were exposed to anti B-cell therapy) and traditional baseline measures of disease severity. This study contributes new information to the influence of emerging risk factors on disease trajectory. When compared with nonBlack race, patients who self-reported Black/African American race were more likely to have worse kidney outcomes (19% reached ESKD vs. 7%) despite similar exposure to IST than non-Black patients. Patients aged < 18 years had a worse kidney survival despite a higher likelihood of achieving complete proteinuria remission. We observed that Latinx participants had increased risk of not reaching complete remission after adjusting for treatment and proteinuria at baseline.

Historically, cyclosporine was preferred in patients with high-risk proteinuria and progressive kidney disease, with approximatively 70% reaching either complete or partial remission. CNIs have however been associated with > 30% of relapse rate within two years after drug cessation.18 Use of cytotoxic agents is limited by their side-effect profile and should be restricted to patients with high-risk of kidney failure, despite the fact that they have been less studied in the high-risk population. Monthly cycling of corticosteroids and cyclophosphamide was associated with better long-term kidney survival than controls in the randomized trial by Jha and colleagues.19 In this study, the 10-year dialysis-free survival was 89% in patients treated with cyclophosphamide versus 65% in the control group (without exposure to antiproteinuric therapy or corticosteroids). A relatively recent cohort study in Spain reported a prevalence of ESKD of 13% for >5 years of follow-up.10 In our cohort, 9% of patients reached ESKD when exposed to contemporary therapies (47% exposed to CNIs, 44% to anti–B-cell therapy, and 26% exposed to cyclophosphamide), with a median follow-up of 4.6 years. Reasons for a relatively higher rate of kidney failure despite exposure to immunosuppression in our cohort remain unclear; however, they may be related to the presence of patients of Latinx ethnicity and self-reported Black/African American race. Patients enrolled in the study by Jha et al. were younger. Furthermore, >40% of our patients reported persistent nephrotic-range proteinuria for a period ranging from 30 days to 1 year.

In the MENTOR controlled trial,6 RTX has been proven to induce a response (i.e., partial or complete remission) similar to CNIs after 12 months of therapy but showed superiority in maintaining remission after 24 months of follow-up. RTX is now considered as a first-line therapy20 for patients with moderate or high-risk of progressive loss of kidney function, with an increase in use over the past decade.21 Our cohort has clinical characteristics (with respect to baseline proteinuria and eGFR) similar to those found in the MENTOR trial population, but was not designed to investigate the specific association between RTX use and meaningful clinical outcomes. We observed a similar association between exposure to immunosuppression after enrollment and complete remission as seen in the MENTOR study, with > 50% of patients reaching proteinuria remission. However, exploring the impact of RTX use on kidney failure will require further analyses and was not the aim of our study.

The prognostic value of histopathologic lesions has been previously described in kidney biopsy cohort studies.22, 23, 24 Tubulointerstitial lesions and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis were mainly associated with worse kidney survival. The more recent cohort study by Srivastava et al.25 included only 47 patients with MN. Analyses presented here provide interesting insights into potential pathologic features associated with long-term kidney outcomes. Segmental glomerular sclerosing lesions and IFTA were related to functional kidney decline (HRs of 1.8 and 7.7, respectively) in our unadjusted analyses. Given our limited statistical power, we were not able to verify those associations with multivariable adjustment for covariates. This study is a first step toward a more comprehensive risk model integrating not only clinical data, but also pathological and genetic characteristics.

Our findings are consistent with previously reported associations26 with respect to the relationship between high-risk APOL1 genotype and progressive kidney disease in patients with pMN, which included patients from the CureGN cohort. Patients with high-risk APOL1 genotype expressed more severe chronic injury (IFTA, global and segmental glomerulosclerosis) on kidney biopsies. This observation concurs with previous reports,26,27 including the study by Larsen and colleagues in PLA2R-positive patients with MN. We were not able to validate the potential increased risk of concomitant collapsing glomerulopathy in patients with pMN with high-risk APOL1 genotype (only 1 patient was diagnosed with concomitant collapsing glomerulopathy).

Although this is a large cohort with longitudinal follow-up, there are some limitations to this study. Patients in our prospective cohort were enrolled after kidney biopsy (median time from biopsy to enrollment was 0.8 years). Participants were often previously exposed to IST; thus, disease activity was not as severe as at initial presentation closer to biopsy time. This may account for the lower overall percentage of PLA2R positivity (41%) when measured at study enrollment compared with the expected 70% to 80% of pMN cases.3 It also should be noted that only 72% of patients had PLA2R status available at the time of active disease and 65% of kidney biopsies stained positively for PLA2R when this was done. Systematic longitudinal antiPLA2R autoantibody quantification has not yet been performed on the CureGN biospecimens, precluding integration of PLA2R status in our main Cox survival models. We were not able to establish a correlation between PLA2R status and kidney failure progression in our exploratory analysis.

The strengths of our study include the following: (i) sample size, with this being perhaps the largest pMN cohort worldwide, with a diverse patient population reflective of contemporary treatment practices (allowing good generalizability); (ii) adequate follow-up time to study clinical kidney outcomes; and (iii) the comparatively large group of pediatric patients, for whom kidney disease prognosis remained undefined in the literature. We confirm the prognostic value of traditional clinical predictors (proteinuria and eGFR at baseline) using the CureGN cohort and assess the association between PLA2R positivity and a lower likelihood of proteinuria remission as previously described.28

This study confirms the traditional predictors of kidney disease progression such as proteinuria, kidney function at baseline, and presence of IFTA in a large contemporary cohort of patients with pMN. It also unveils an association, after controlling for exposure to IST, between self-reported Black race and young age (aged < 18 years) with worse kidney outcomes, and lower chance of achieving complete remission among patients of Latinx ethnicity. Whether those observations are driven by genetic factors or social determinants of health remains to be determined in further studies.
