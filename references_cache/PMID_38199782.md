# The diagnostic odyssey of a patient with dihydropyrimidinase deficiency: a case report and review of the literature.
**Authors:** Albokhari D, Alharbi O, Blesson A, Jain M
**Journal:** Cold Spring Harb Mol Case Stud (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1101/mcs.a006319](https://doi.org/10.1101/mcs.a006319)

## Content

Dihydropyrimidinase (DHP) deficiency is an autosomal recessive metabolic disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic variants of DPYS Patients with DHP deficiency exhibit a broad spectrum of phenotypes, ranging from severe neurological and gastrointestinal involvement to cases with no apparent symptoms. The biochemical diagnosis of DHP deficiency is based on the detection of a significant amount of dihydropyrimidines in urine, plasma, and cerebrospinal fluid samples. Molecular genetic testing, specifically the identification of biallelic pathogenic variants in DPYS, has proven instrumental in confirming the diagnosis and facilitating family studies. This case study documents the diagnostic journey of an 18-yr-old patient with DHP deficiency, highlighting features at the severe end of the clinical spectrum. Notably, our patient exhibited previously unreported skeletal features that positively responded to bisphosphonate treatment, contributing valuable insights to the clinical characterization of DHP deficiency. Additionally, a novel DPYS variant was identified and confirmed pathogenicity through metabolic testing, further expanding the variant spectrum of the gene. Our case emphasizes the importance of a comprehensive diagnostic approach using genetic sequencing and metabolic testing for accurate diagnosis.

A growing number of metabolic and genetic disorders are associated with symptoms and signs resembling cerebral palsy (CP), marked by prominent spasticity. Examples include hereditary spastic paraplegia, Aicardi–Goutières syndrome, leukodystrophies, and Sjörgen–Larsson syndrome (Hakami et al. 2019). Inherited metabolic diseases include homocysteine remethylation defects, arginase deﬁciency, biotinidase deficiency, dopamine synthesis defects, sulﬁte oxidase deﬁciency, molybdenum cofactor deﬁciency, dihydropyrimidinase (DHP) deficiency, cerebral folate deficiency syndrome, cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis, peroxisomal disorders, and lysosomal diseases (Murala et al. 2021).

DHP deficiency, an autosomal recessive metabolic disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in the DPYS gene (MIM #613326; https://omim.org) on Chromosome 8q22, affects the second enzyme in the pyrimidine degradation pathway, which converts dihydrouracil (DHU) and dihydrothymine (DHT) to N-carbamyl-β-alanine and N-carbamyl-β-aminoisobutyric acid, respectively. This alteration leads to the accumulation of DHT and DHU along with thymine (THY) and uracil (UR), as well as low levels of N-carbamyl-β-amino acids, which can be identified by the analysis of pyrimidines and dihydropyrimidines in urine samples. The clinical features of DHP deficiency vary among affected individuals, ranging from severe neurological and gastrointestinal involvement to asymptomatic cases. To date, 34 patients have been reported to have DHP deficiency. Our contribution adds a newly identified patient to this cohort, presenting with severe neurological involvement, profound intellectual disability (ID), seizure disorder, movement disorder, spastic quadriplegia, and abnormal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings. Later, the patient developed feeding problems, requiring gastrostomy during early adulthood. Multiple skeletal involvements including scoliosis, arthrogryposis, bilateral bony deformities of the wrist, and osteoporosis, which are unreported features, have expanded the clinical spectrum of this disease. Furthermore, we identified a novel homozygous DPYS variant, which was confirmed using metabolic testing.

Although molecular diagnosis based on sequencing of DPYS has identified a considerable number and spectrum of variants, metabolic testing is still required to provide evidence of pathogenicity for the novel variants in metabolic diseases, such as in our case. Identifying patients with DHP deficiency, even when they are asymptomatic, is warranted to prevent neurotoxicity while administering pyrimidine analog drugs, such as 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), to patients with cancer.

Our patient is an 18-yr-old male born at 36 wk gestation to healthy, nonconsanguineous parents.

Maternal preeclampsia and fetal bradycardia complicated the pregnancy, and though born with a nuchal cord, no resuscitation was required. A 5-d stay in the neonatal intensive care unit was necessary because of prematurity. Developmental milestones were gradually achieved, with developmental delay and spasticity first noted at ∼9 mo of age. At 3.5 yr old, a rapid regression occurred in all developmental areas, rendering the patient unable to walk or speak. No known infection or injury preceded this loss. Shortly after the regression, the patient developed convulsions and was diagnosed with spastic quadriplegic CP. Despite physical therapy and oral medications, spasticity intensified with age, necessitating the implantation of a baclofen pump at ∼18 yr of age. At 18 yr, because of risk factors for low bone density, including reduced mobility, seizure disorder, treatment with antiepileptic medications, and a history of a ketogenic diet, bone mineral density was evaluated. Osteoporosis was diagnosed based on low bone mineral density measured by dual X-ray absorptiometry. The patient has undergone 3 yr of bisphosphonate treatment (zoledronate), resulting in improved bone mineral density. Initial measurements in the osteoporosis range (lumbar spine 0.57 g/cm2 and left total hip 0.53 g/cm2) have shown improvement in the most recent assessments (lumbar spine 0.83 g/cm2 and left total hip 0.75 g/cm2). Additionally, the patient developed gastritis and feeding difficulties in the form of dysphagia, vomiting, and gastroesophageal reflux (GERD), which required multiple admissions. Eventually, a gastrostomy tube was inserted, which alleviated the patient's symptoms.

Normal brain MRI results were observed at 1.5 and 3.5 yr of age. However, a subsequent brain MRI at 16 yr revealed a mild increase in FLAIR signal in the parietal periventricular white matter, accompanied by volume loss and periventricular leukomalacia. Around 5 yr of age, abdominal ultrasound and echocardiogram studies yielded normal results.

Biochemical testing, encompassing general metabolic screening such as plasma amino acids, acylcarnitine profile, carnitine, methylmalonic acid (MMA), homocysteine, and B12, revealed unremarkable results. Negative findings were also noted for mucopolysaccharide and oligosaccharide electrophoresis, as well as lysosomal enzyme disease screening. Urine studies, including those for organic acids, showed no significant abnormalities. Molecular testing, including karyotype, comparative genomic hybridization (CGH) chromosomal microarray, MECP2 sequencing, and deletion/duplication studies, as well as chromosomal methylation studies, all returned negative results. Subsequently, research-based whole-genome sequencing (WGS) on genomic DNA isolated from whole blood was performed and returned negative results.

The patient, evaluated at 16 yr and 5 mo, presented with macrocephaly (>98 percentile) and a low anterior hairline. Coarse facial appearance was noted (Fig. 1A,B). Furthermore, the patient exhibited other dysmorphic features, including low-set, posteriorly rotated ears; a short and broad neck; widely spaced teeth; high-arched palate; and inverted nipples. Physical abnormalities included scoliosis, bilateral arthrogryposis, and bony wrist deformities, with camptodactyly observed in the fingers and toes (Fig. 1C,D). Neurological examination revealed dystonia and spasticity in the upper and lower extremities, an abnormal deep tendon reflex, and ankle clonus. The patient was nonverbal, wheelchair-dependent, and profoundly cognitively impaired. Ophthalmological and auditory evaluation results were normal.

Facial features included coarse facial appearance, low anterior hairline, deeply set eyes, thick coarse eyebrows with medial flare, short nose with high prominent nasal bridge, prominent lips, and short and broad neck (A), as well as protruded mandible, retruded midface, and low-set posteriorly rotated ears (B). (C) Bony wrist deformities and camptodactyly. (D) Wheelchair dependence, macrocephaly, and bilateral arthrogryposis.

Considering the patient's medical history and physical findings, genetic causes were strongly suspected. Chromosome microarray analysis, using single-nucleotide polymorphism technology, identified total uniparental isodisomy across Chromosome 8. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) demonstrated a homozygous variant in DPYS (NM_001385.3:c.502T > C; p.Tyr168His) (Table 1). Subsequent analysis of pyrimidines and dihydropyrimidines in urine revealed extremely high levels of DHU and DHT, mildly elevated THY, and normal UR, consistent with DHP deficiency (Table 2). We applied the current ACMG/AMP guidelines in variant classification with specifications provided by ClinGen. The variant was absent from gnomAD v4.0 (PM2_Supporting), in silico predictors suggested a deleterious effect (PP3), and the patient's biochemical results were a match for the phenotype (PP4). This resulted in a variant classification of uncertain significance (VUS). It should be noted that research-based WGS returned negative results. This sequencing was completed at an outside facility with no access to raw data. The variant was also not orthogonally confirmed by any method. However, the biochemical data and clinical features support the presence and pathogenicity of the variant. Many factors can affect the accuracy of variant calls in WES and WGS, such as genomic region, variant type, and read depth, which vary according to the analytical pipeline. A previous study found that most false negative WGS calls result from filtering, whereas most false negative WES variants are related to poor coverage (Goldfeder et al. 2016).

A comprehensive review of the literature was conducted through a PubMed search using the terms “dihydropyrimidinase deficiency” and “DHP.” Articles published between 1990 and the present were reviewed, focusing on phenotypic variability. The review included 34 patients from 14 published reports, incorporating our patient and culminating in a total of 35 patients (Table 3; Duran et al. 1990; Henderson et al. 1993; Assmann et al. 1997; Putman et al. 1997; van Gennip et al. 1997; van Kuilenburg et al. 2007, 2010; Yeung et al. 2013; Akai et al. 2015; Nakajima et al. 2016, 2017; Tsuchiya et al. 2019; Mirzaei et al. 2020). Among the 35 patients, nine patients were asymptomatic at the time of diagnosis (Ohba et al. 1994; Hamajima et al. 1998; Sumi et al. 1998; Kuhara et al. 2003; Hishinuma et al. 2017), with two adult siblings (36 and 37 yr old, respectively) (Sumi et al. 1998). However, one of these asymptomatic individuals developed a developmental delay at 1 yr of age and IDs at 11 yr of age (Hishinuma et al. 2017). Notably, two asymptomatic siblings were diagnosed with DHP deficiency after their sister exhibited clinical features and received a diagnosis (van Kuilenburg et al. 2010). The most prominent features identified were neurological abnormalities including IDs (16 out of 35, 46%), seizures (12 out of 35, 36%), and hypotonia (10 out of 35, 29%). Seven patients (20%) had unspecified developmental delay, and five displayed abnormal MRI findings, including white matter volume loss (three out of five), periventricular leukomalacia, cortical atrophy, and porencephaly in individual cases. Three patients were diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder, and two were diagnosed with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Three patients exhibited movement disorders, including chorea, ataxia, and dyskinesia, and two presented with spastic quadriplegia. Coarse and/or dysmorphic features were noted in four patients, while three patients had microcephaly, and two had macrocephaly. Common gastrointestinal symptoms included feeding problems (eight out of 35) and failure to thrive (six out of 35). One patient was asymptomatic until the age of 22 yr, when signs of liver dysfunction emerged, progressing to liver cirrhosis. Two patients showed evidence of cardiac abnormalities, including VSD and cardiomyopathy. The main ophthalmologic symptom was cataract, with hypermetropia identified in two patients. Hematological symptoms included protein S deficiency complicated by cerebral thrombophlebitis, which was identified in only one patient. One patient had type II tyrosinemia and was asymptomatic at the time of diagnosis. Additionally, one patient with a history of ADHD and borderline IQ, incidentally, diagnosed with DHP deficiency through urine metabolome analysis when he experienced muscle cramps and rhabdomyolysis. Interestingly, there were no reported patients of scoliosis, osteoporosis, arthrogryposis, or bony deformities similar to the findings of our patient. One patient faced severe respiratory problems leading to death at 4 wk, with limited information available, whereas our patient developed a chronic cough with no other symptoms. All patients exhibited characteristic biochemical findings of increased UR, DHU, THY, and DHT levels in bodily fluids.

Patients grappling with complicated medical health issues often navigate multiple clinical evaluations, imaging studies, and laboratory tests during their diagnostic journey. The integration of next-generation sequencing (NGS) with metabolic testing emerges as a promising approach to abbreviate or conclude this diagnostic odyssey, as exemplified in our patient. In this case, we present a patient with DHP deficiency harboring a homozygous variant (c.502T > C; p.Tyr168His) in exon 3 in DPYS. This variant, previously unreported and absent in large population cohorts (Lek et al. 2016), denotes a nonconserved amino acid substitution; however, it is likely to impact the secondary protein structure, as these residues differ in polarity, charge, and size. In silico analyses, including protein predictors and evolutionary conservation, support the deleterious effects of this substitution. The biochemical diagnosis of DHP deficiency was corroborated by the significant concentrations of DHT and DHU in urine, as confirmed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). The initial microarray, using CGH technology, detects only copy-number changes, whereas copy-neutral changes like uniparental disomy (UPD) can remain undetected, which explains the initial negative result in our patient.

The initial case of DHP deficiency, reported in an infant experiencing convulsions with subsequent normal development until follow-up was lost at 19 mo, marked the beginning of a growing body of knowledge (Duran et al. 1990). Currently, 35 documented patients including our patient reveal a wide array of clinical manifestations, with neurological abnormalities being the most prevalent. The role of elevated pyrimidines and their derivatives or decreased concentrations of alanine and aminoisobutyric acid in neurological features remains uncertain (van Gennip et al. 1998). Although the gastrointestinal system is the second most common organ involvement in DHP early in childhood, our patient developed gastrointestinal symptoms at 18 yr, underlining the potential for delayed onset and emphasizing the importance of regular screening for complications that may emerge or intensify at any age. A similar late-onset manifestation, liver dysfunction leading to cirrhosis at 22 yr, was reported in a previous case (Mirzaei et al. 2020). These findings highlight the need for ongoing screening and examination for anticipated complications. The skeletal manifestations observed in our patient further expand the known DHP phenotype, underscoring the importance of scoliosis screening and serial bone density measurements.

Although it is possible that the expanded phenotype may be related to other genetic variants, including homozygous variants on Chromosome 8, clinical exome sequencing identified only the homozygous variant in DPYS in our patient.

Routine biochemical laboratory examinations, including amino acid, organic acid, and acylcarnitine analyses, typically yield normal results in patients with DHP disorder. However, GC–MS can be used to measure pyrimidine metabolites in urine, though interlaboratory variations may result in inconsistent findings (van Gennip et al. 1993; Kuhara et al. 2003).

Molecular diagnosis based on sequencing DPYS identified a considerable number and spectrum of variants. To date, most of the identified variants are missense variants, with at least 19 pathogenic variants reported. Other pathogenic alterations have also been identified, including three nonsense variants, three deletions, one insertion, and two splice site variants (Hamajima et al. 1998; van Kuilenburg et al. 2007, 2010; Yeung et al. 2013; Nakajima et al. 2016; Hishinuma et al. 2017; Tsuchiya et al. 2019; Mirzaei et al. 2020). Twenty-six percent of the variants were distributed in exon 1, and 59% were spread between exons 5 and 8. Fourteen patients had at least one variant in exon 6, and their symptoms ranged from asymptomatic to severe. Seven patients had at least one variant in exon 1. Six patients each had at least one variant in exons 7, 8, and 9. The impact of specific variants on phenotype varied, as illustrated by different outcomes for patients with the same variants (Supplemental Table S1). For example, the homozygous variant (p.Trp360Arg; exon 6) in one patient revealed a severe phenotype, whereas compound heterozygosity (p.Trp360Arg/p.Arg412Met; Ex6/Ex7) in two siblings resulted in a mild phenotype. Another variant (p.Gln334Arg; Ex6) manifested differently in six patients, ranging from asymptomatic (four patients) to mild (two patients). It is possible that the symptoms in the two patients were related to the second variant (c.1443 + 5G > A; intron 8). However, two of the asymptomatic patients presented another variant besides p.Gln334Arg—a frameshift insertion (812–814InsA) leading to a stop codon—and another missense variant (p.Gly435Arg; Ex8). The variant (p.Arg490Thr; Ex9) was found in five patients. When inherited in a homozygous manner, patients developed a mild phenotype, whereas compound heterozygotes showed variable phenotypes ranging from asymptomatic (second variant, either p.Thr68Arg; Ex1 or p.Met250Ile; Ex4) to mild (p.Val59Phe; Ex1) to moderate to severe (p.His295Arg; Ex5). Homozygous (p.Ser379Arg; E7) caused a mild phenotype in three patients, whereas when compounded with (p.Val364Met; E6) it caused severe disease. In the case of the compound heterozygous p.Leu7Val/p.Met70Thr in three siblings, there were variable intrafamilial phenotypes, with one sibling showing mild to moderate symptoms and two being entirely asymptomatic. A similar pattern emerged in two siblings with the homozygous variant 1092 + 3delG. One sibling experienced ID, hypotonia, feeding problems, and GERD, whereas the other experienced feeding problems, mild ID, severe autism, and mild facial coarsening. Furthermore, other reported sibling pairs featured cases in which one sibling had only seizure disorders, whereas the other had both seizure disorders and developmental delays.

Our observations from previous reports proved that, despite the undetectable enzyme activity of DHP in several variants, many affected individuals remained asymptomatic or revealed milder features, whereas other patients with DHP deficiency experienced a severe form. Additionally, reports describing intrafamilial variability in clinical presentation within families further highlight the complex nature of DHP deficiency (van Kuilenburg et al. 2007, 2010; Nakajima et al. 2016). The mechanisms underlying severity and intrafamilial variability may be related to more complex factors, which warrant further investigation.

A major concern in cancer treatment is the development of neurotoxicity when using pyrimidine analog drugs, such as 5-FU, for patients with defects in the pyrimidine degradation pathway. DPD catabolizes >85% of administered 5-FU (Lu et al. 1992). Typically, DHP catalyzes the reversible hydrolysis of 5,6-dihydro-5-FU to α-fluoro-β-reidopropionate. Patients with DHP deficiency are prone to accumulating 5,6-dihydro-5-FU in the plasma. DPD can convert this back to 5-FU, reaching toxic levels rapidly (Thomas et al. 2007). Furthermore, severe 5-FU toxicity has been associated with partial DHP deficiency resulting from DHP heterozygosity (van Kuilenburg et al. 2004).

DHP deficiency manifests with diverse clinical presentations, predominantly centered around neurological symptoms that can overlap with other disorders. However, DHP deficiency is not detected by routine biochemical tests, such as amino acid, organic acid, and acylcarnitine analyses, making DHP cases likely to be missed or underdiagnosed. The limited number of reported cases hinders a comprehensive understanding of the DHP phenotype. Therefore, screening for pyrimidine degradation defects is crucial in patients presenting with nonspecific neurological symptoms. In addition, more studies are required to gain further insight into the clinical phenotype. Biochemical and molecular testing are essential for diagnosis, direct anticipatory guidance, and genetic counseling. Identifying variants holds significance for prenatal diagnosis and determining carrier status in family members, crucial for averting toxic reactions to 5-FU. Our case report defines osteoporosis in the context of DHP deficiency and advances our understanding of DHP deficiency in clinical practice. Furthermore, it contributes to the expansion of the variant spectrum of DPYS with the inclusion of a novel variant confirmed by metabolic testing, emphasizing the vital role of metabolic testing in the era of NGS.
