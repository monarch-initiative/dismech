ABSTRACT
COX4I1 gene encodes cytochrome c oxidase subunit 4 isoform 1, involved in the early assembly stages of
mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV. To date, COX4I1 pathogenic variants have been reported in only a
few cases, each exhibiting heterogeneous clinical phenotypes and limited functional data. Here, we describe the
fourth reported case of COX4I1 deficiency associated with human disease, expanding the phenotypic and genetic
spectrum of this rare mitochondrial disorder and providing novel clinical, molecular, and functional data. The
herein reported individual presented with progressive deterioration of motor skills, intellectual disability and
brain imaging abnormalities compatible with Leigh syndrome. Genetic studies combining short and long read
next generation sequencing uncovered a peculiar genetic combination in this patient, harboring a de novo
COX4I1 nonsense substitution in trans with an inherited deep intronic variant (c.[64C > T];[73 + 1511A > G]; p.
[Arg22Ter];[Glu25ValfsTer9]). Functional studies performed in patient ’ s tissues and transiently transfected cell
lines demonstrated that the identified variants mainly exert their pathogenic effect by targeting COX4I1 protein
levels, thereby impairing the proper assembly and activity of complex IV. Additionally, proteomic data in pa -
tient ’ s fibroblasts suggested an underlying pathomechanism that involves not only the regulation of complex IV
function but also the levels of mitoribosomal proteins. In summary, our findings shed light to clarify some of the
main clinical features associated with COX4I1 deficiency and the molecular mechanisms involved in the path -
ogenesis of this disorder.

1. Introduction
Mitochondrial disorders are a large and heterogeneous group of
inherited metabolic diseases that present with a wide variety of clinical
symptoms and biochemical abnormalities. Genetic alterations affecting
components of the oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) system are
among the most common defects associated with these diseases, which
can be caused by mutations in genes encoded by the nuclear genome
(nDNA) or in genes encoded by mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) ( Gorman
et al., 2016, Parikh et al., 2019, Thompson et al., 2020 ).
The OXPHOS system consists of five heteropolymeric complexes
(complexes I-V) embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane, whose
main function is the generation of most of the cellular energy in form of
ATP. Complexes I-IV couple the oxidation of substrates with electron
transport and the formation of an electrochemical gradient that triggers
ATP synthesis, a reaction catalysed by ATP synthase or complex V
( Tim ´on-G ´omez et al., 2018, Signes and Fernandez-Vizarra, 2018 ). Cy -
tochrome c oxidase (COX) or complex IV (CIV) is the terminal enzyme of
the electron transport chain, and consists of fourteen subunits, three of
which encoded by mtDNA and the remaining eleven by the nDNA
( Brischigliaro and Zeviani, 2021, Kadenbach, 2021 ). In addition, a large
number of assembly factors, all of them encoded by the nDNA, are
required for the correct assembly and function of this complex ( Signes
and Fernandez-Vizarra, 2018, Tim ´on-G ´omez et al., 2018, Vidoni et al.,
2017 ).
To date, mutations in more than 30 genes encoding either complex
IV subunits or regulatory proteins have been reported to be associated to
human disease ( Brischigliaro and Zeviani, 2021, Fernandez-Vizarra and
Zeviani, 2021, Ronchi et al., 2023 ). In addition to primary defects,
complex IV deficiency can also be secondary to other mitochondrial
alterations, such as those affecting mitochondrial transcription and
translation ( Brischigliaro and Zeviani, 2021 ). Clinical manifestations of
complex IV deficiencies are also highly heterogeneous and include
growth retardation, myopathic features associated with mitochondrial
dysfunction in skeletal muscle and heart, liver alterations, and Leigh
syndrome, among others (OMIM: 220110 ) ( DiMauro et al., 2012, Bri -
schigliaro and Zeviani, 2021 ). The onset of the disease generally occurs
in infancy and the outcome is usually poor. As typically occurs in
mitochondrial disorders there is no clear genotype-phenotype correla -
tion and, regardless of the improvement made by the implementation of
next generation sequencing technologies, the diagnosis of these patients
is still challenging ( Schlieben and Prokisch, 2023 ). Remarkably, most
cases with mitochondrial complex IV deficiency of nuclear origin are
caused by mutations in assembly or accessory factors, whereas muta -
tions in COX subunits are rare and often even private ( Fernandez-Vizarra
and Zeviani, 2021, Brischigliaro and Zeviani, 2021 ). This is the case of
COX4I1 gene, which encodes cytochrome c oxidase subunit 4 isoform 1
(OMIM: *123864). Pathogenic variants in this gene have only been
described in three families, each showing very different clinical pre -
sentations, ranging from failure to thrive, mild dysmorphia, and signs of
Fanconi anaemia to severe brain abnormalities, developmental delay,
intellectual disability, and seizures ( Abu-Libdeh et al., 2017, Pillai et al.,
2019; Liu et al., 2025 ).
In the present study, we provide further evidence of COX4I1
involvement in human disease by reporting novel clinical, genetic and
functional data in a patient presenting with progressive deterioration of
motor skills and brain imaging compatible with Leigh syndrome. The
patient had a peculiar genetic combination, harboring a de novo
nonsense substitution in trans with an inherited deep intronic variant.
Functional studies demonstrated that the identified variants importantly
affect COX4I1 protein levels, complex IV function and also the levels of
mitoribosomal proteins. Our study expands the knowledge of the
phenotypic features and the molecular pathomechanisms associated
with this rare disorder.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Whole exome sequencing
Genomic DNA was isolated following the manufacturer ’ s recom -
mendations (QiAmp DNA Mini Kit, QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Short-
read whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed using Nextera
DNA exome Kit (Illumina). Analysis of the primary data was performed
using a self-developed pipeline (coreBM-EX 1.0.0; Hospital Clinic of
Barcelona, Barcelona, Spain). Variant calls were analyzed using Var -
iantStudio software (Illumina). Variants were filtered and prioritized
using different databases (1000 Genomes, gnomAD, ClinVar, and
HGMD), pathogenicity predictive models and patient ’ s phenotypic
traits. Raw data was further reanalyzed using the pipeline developed by
CNAG-CRG and the URDCAT genome-phenome analysis platform
( https://rdcat.cnag.crg.eu/ ) ( Laurie et al., 2016 ).
2.2. Long read sequencing
Targeted long read sequencing (LRS) was performed using nanopore
technology. DNA was obtained from the three available patients ’ tissues:
blood, skin fibroblasts, and muscle. We amplified by PCR the COX4I1
genomic region encompassing the two identified variants. PCR products
were sequenced with nanopore sequencing technology using the Flongle
flow cell in a MinION platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, ONT).
Briefly, PCR products were prepared in the sequencing library using the
ONT Native Barcoding Kit following the manufacturer ’ s protocol with
slight modifications. The bioinformatic analysis of LRS data was per -
formed with a pipeline from Longseq Applications that consisted of: i)
basecalling using Dorado basecaller, which is integrated within the
MinKNOW software (ONT); ii) alignment to the human reference as -
sembly (GRCh38) using Minimap2; iii) variants calling and phasing with
Clair3 software for SNPs.

3. Results
3.1. Case report
The patient is a 10-year-old Spanish boy with a progressive motor
disorder since the age of two. He was born from non-consanguineous
healthy parents, and he has two healthy siblings. Pregnancy and de -
livery were uneventful. In the neonatal period he underwent surgery for
aortic coarctation without remarkable complications. During the first
years of life he exhibited normal psychomotor development, achieving
independent ambulation at one year of age. However, by the age of two,
his parents noticed a right-footed gait and a gradual decline in motor
coordination. He struggled with climbing up and down stairs and had
difficulty jumping. By the age of four, he was referred to a neuro -
pediatrician for evaluation. In the following years, he experienced a
progressive deterioration of motor skills, with increasing spasticity of
the lower limbs despite botulinum toxin treatment. Although he was
able to walk, he needed support for long distances. He also showed
spastic-dystonic triparesis, mainly affecting right extremities, along with
difficulties in fine motor skills. In addition, he showed orofacial invol -
untary movements, including blinking and dyskinesias, which were
associated with pronunciation difficulties, tongue movement and swal -
lowing. Initially, no significant cognitive deterioration was observed
compared to his peers; he was able to learn, to understand instructions
and was integrated into the social sphere.
Over the years, his deterioration has become increasingly evident,
affecting both motor and cognitive functions. Kaufman Brief Intelligence
Test at 7 yo and 10 yo showed total IQ:75 + /-12 and 54 + /-9, respec -
tively. Despite retaining some ability to walk, he now finally required a
wheelchair for mobility. His language skills also declined, prompting the
use of an eye-tracking system to facilitate communication, with very
positive results. Brain MRI revealed T2 hyperintensities in both caudate
and lenticular nuclei, without spinal cord involvement or changes in
follow-up MRIs despite ongoing psychomotor deterioration   findings
that are consistent with Leigh syndrome ( Fig. 1 ). Visual and somato -
sensory evoked potentials were normal. Ophthalmological and cardio -
logical evaluations showed no abnormalities. However,
electroencephalogram evidenced diffusely slow waking background
brain activity without epileptiform abnormalities.
Biochemical studies showed high levels of plasma lactate, with
normal pyruvate and consistently increased levels of medium- and long-
chain acylcarnitine species ( Table 1 ). Plasma alanine, proline and
glycine levels were elevated, although these measurements were per -
formed only once ( Table 1 ). These biochemical findings were suggestive
of a mitochondrial disorder or multiple acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (MAD)
deficiency. The latter suspicion was ruled out, as no mutations were
found in ETFA , ETFB , ETFDH , SLC52A1 , SLC52A2 , and SLC52A3 , and no
response to riboflavin treatment was observed. Mitochondrial respira -
tory chain analysis in muscle tissue revealed a marked reduction of
complex IV activity together with high citrate synthase activity, further
supporting the diagnosis of a mitochondrial disorder ( Table2 ).
3.2. Identification of a de novo and an inherited COX4I1 recessive
variants
Short read WES analysis ruled out variants in MAD-associated genes
but identified a heterozygous nonsense variant (c.64C > T, p.Arg22Ter)
in COX4I1 gene (NM_0001861), which codifies a subunit of mitochon -
drial respiratory chain complex IV ( Fig. 2A ). The variant had an
extremely low frequency in gnomAD 4.1 (1.865e-6, accessed April
2025) and was not present in the parents, suggesting that it was a de novo
substitution. As COX4I1 mutations had been already associated with an
autosomal recessive disease, cDNA studies were performed in fibroblasts
to search for a second variant in this gene. RT-PCR followed by agarose
gel analysis revealed a faint higher molecular weight migrating band
that was present in patient ’ s fibroblasts but not in control individuals.
The abnormal band was detected in both cycloheximide treated and
untreated cells ( Fig. 2B ).
To clarify the nature of this alteration, the PCR product was cloned
and transformed into E. coli . Colony PCR and Sanger sequencing iden -
tified three different COX4I1 cDNA populations: a subset of fragments
corresponded to wild-type COX4I1 transcripts, a second population
corresponded to transcripts harbouring the nonsense de novo mutation
identified by WES (c.64C > T), and finally, a third group of transcripts
had a 82 bp insertion between COX4I1 exon 2 and exon 3 together with a
deletion of the first two nucleotides of exon 3 (c.74_75delin -
s73 + 1512_73 + 1593), which was compatible with the higher molecular
weight migrating band detected by RT-PCR ( Fig. 2B , Supplementary
figure 1 ). Remarkably, this transcript did not have the c.64C > T variant,
suggesting that transcripts with these variants were likely transcribed
from separate alleles. Further analysis of genomic DNA identified a
heterozygous deep intronic rare variant in intron 2 (c.73 + 1511A > G)
adjacent to the retained sequence ( Fig. 2C ). The variant was not anno -
tated in gnomAD 4.0. In silico analysis using Splice AI ( Jaganathan et al.,
2019 ) revealed a moderate impact on the splicing process (0.41),
whereas Human Splice Finder ( Desmet et al., 2009 ) suggested no sig -
nificant impact on splicing signals. Therefore, the variant was classified
as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) and functional studies were
required to demonstrate its potential pathogenic effect.

3.3. Long read sequencing corroborated that the identified COX4I1
variants segregated separately
Since COX4I1 deficiency is a recessive disorder, targeted LRS was
performed to fully clarify whether the de novo c.64C > T substitution and
the inherited c.73 + 1511A > G variant segregated in separate alleles.
Phasing analysis of long sequencing data showed that the two variants
were in trans , as they were mutually excluded in long reads encom -
passing the genomic region including both substitutions ( Fig. 3A ). The
fact that one of the variants was a de novo substitution prompted us to
analyze different tissues at high sequencing depth to investigate the
possibility of mosaicism. Results showed that both variants were found
in approximately 50% of the reads in all three tissues analyzed (blood,
skin derived fibroblasts and muscle), suggesting that the de novo sub -
stitution might have originated in early stages of embryonic develop -
ment or during maternal gametogenesis ( Fig. 3B ).

site activation and intronic retention

WT) produced the expected products, indicating unaltered splicing
events. On the contrary, cells expressing the plasmid containing the
c.73 + 1511A > G variant (pET01-MUT) showed a higher molecular

4. Discussion
To date, COX4I1 pathogenic variants have only been reported in
three families presenting with different clinical phenotypes ( Abu-Libdeh
et al., 2017, Pillai et al., 2019, Liu et al., 2025 ). Here we describe a new
patient with COX4I1 deficiency and discuss their clinical, biochemical
and genetic characteristics, compared with those of previously reported
individuals, while also providing functional data. The first description of
COX4I1 deficiency was a patient presenting with failure to thrive, short
stature, microcephaly, minor dysmorphic features and Fanconi anaemia
( Abu-Libdeh et al., 2017 ). Two years later, Pillai and co-workers re -
ported two siblings with COX4I1 variants showing different phenotypic
traits, consisting of significant regression of motor skills development in
the first months of life, intellectual disability, seizures and brain MRI
resembling Leigh syndrome ( Pillai et al., 2019 ). Recently, during the
preparation of this manuscript, a new COX4I1 patient presenting
developmental regression, epilepsy, low body weight, microcephaly,
hypotonia, and progressive cerebral atrophy was also reported ( Liu
et al., 2025 ). Due to the limited availability of phenotypic data and the
clinical heterogeneity observed in these descriptions, the phenotypic
picture of this disorder remains controversial, and more descriptions
will be needed to better clarify the major phenotypic characteristics and
the clinical spectrum of the disease. In this regard, the patient described
here exhibited some symptoms similar to those of previously described
individuals but also presented notable differences. Our patient showed
progressive deterioration of motor skills, cognitive impairment, and
brain imaging abnormalities consistent with Leigh syndrome, but he had
normal stature and weight gain, and did not showed evidence of
microcephaly, seizures, dysmorphia or Fanconi anemia. Remarkably,
our patient displayed an altered profile of acylcarnitines. Interestingly,
acylcarnitines were only measured in one of the previously reported
patients and were unaltered ( Pillai et al., 2019 ).

COX4I1 deficiency is an autosomal recessive disorder. Interestingly,
our patient showed a peculiar genetic combination harbouring two
COX4I1 heterozygous variants, being one of them a paternally inherited
deep intronic variant (c.73 + 1511A > G) and the other one a de novo
nonsense substitution (c.64C > T, p.Arg22Ter). This is relevant since such
findings are not frequent in inherited metabolic disorders, which are
mostly recessive and usually present the typical biallelic inheritance
pattern in which both parents are germline heterozygous carriers for the
pathogenic variants. Although long read sequencing is not yet fully
implemented as a routine diagnostic method, the use of this technology
has been determinant to demonstrate that the identified COX4I1 vari -
ants were in separate alleles, as expected for a recessive disorder. These
results highlighted the diagnostic potential of long read sequencing not
only to improve the detection of structural variation and repeat ex -
pansions but also to phase candidate de novo variants ( Miller et al., 2021;
Conlin et al., 2022 ). The presence of a de novo substitution also raised the
question whether this individual could be a mosaic. Analysis of long
reads at high sequencing depth showed that this possibility was unlikely
since the proportion of alleles with the inherited variant and the de novo
substitution in different tissues was close to 50%, suggesting that the de
novo variant may have been originated early in patient ’ s embryonic
development or even during maternal gametogenesis. The latter possi -
bility is remarkable considering that de novo mutations are mostly
originated during paternal gametogenesis ( Kong et al., 2012 ). Curiously,
one of the previously reported COX4I1 individuals also had a de novo
genetic alteration in heterozygosity ( Liu et al., 2025 ). COX4I1 is located
in chromosome 16, which is composed of segmental copies that pre -
dispose this chromosome to rearrangements via recombination ( Atli
et al., 2022 ). Indeed, genomic studies suggest that although de novo
variation occurs along all chromosomes, regions with higher recombi -
nation have more de novo mutations ( Kessler et al., 2020 ). Although
genetic data on COX41 deficiency is still very limited, these observations
raise the possibility that the genomic region containing COX4I1 could be
prone to suffer de novo variation, but further studies will be needed to
support such a hypothetical scenario.

transcripts, likely generated by the allele carrying the c.73 + 1511A > G

of functional COX4I1 protein in patient ’ s cells. The pathogenic effect of
the c.64C > T nonsense variant was plausible, as it was predicted to
introduce a premature termination codon within the coding sequence of
the protein. However, the impact of the c.73 + 1511A > G substitution on
mRNA processing and protein function was uncertain and therefore
required further demonstration. Transient expression of a minigene
vector containing the c.73 + 1511A > G variant in HAP1 cells revealed the
same leaky splicing alteration observed in patient ’ s fibroblasts. This
functional assay clarified the causal effect of this variant by demon -
strating that it was responsible of the intronic retention, and also by
providing a molecular explanation for the different COX4I1 mRNA
populations observed in patient ’ s fibroblasts. Consistent with the effect
predicted for both variants, COX4I1 protein levels were found to be
importantly reduced, albeit not completely absent, in both fibroblasts
and muscle biopsy. Additionally, low steady state levels of other com -
plex IV subunits and reduced amounts of fully assembled complex IV
were observed. These defects were particularly evident in muscle, where
complex IV assembly, although it was still detectable, was extremely
low. The milder deficiency observed in fibroblasts may rely on the fact
that COX4I1 protein levels appear to be proportionally higher in this
tissue than in muscle, and may therefore be sufficient to allow the for -
mation of relevant amounts of properly assembled complex IV. Previous
studies showed that complete complex IV deficiency, either caused by
COX4I1 ablation or by other defects in the early assembly steps, also
affects complex I levels, suggesting some degree of interdependency
between these complexes (
ˇ
Cun ´atov ´a et al., 2021, Diaz et al., 2006, Li
et al., 2007, Hornig-Do et al., 2012 ). In our patient, the presence of re -
sidual amounts of both COX4I1 and assembled complex IV likely over -
come such defect and may explain why the assembly of complex I
appeared unaltered. This scenario is also supported by experiments
performed in COX4I1 knock-down HEK293 cells, which showed that
residual complex IV levels are sufficient to prevent concomitant complex
I defects ( Fornuskova et al., 2010 ).
Complex IV regulation involves multiple mechanisms, including the
differential expression of subunit isoforms in different tissues and in
response to specific environmental stimuli ( Sinkler et al., 2017 ). In the
particular case of COX4, COX4I1 is the main isoform and is ubiquitously
expressed in normoxic conditions. In contrast, COX4I2 is a less prevalent
isoform, and shows a more restricted expression pattern, primarily in
lung tissue and under hypoxic or other stress-related states ( Fukuda
et al., 2017 ). Previous studies proposed that COX4I2 could partially
compensate for COX4I1 deficiency as it was found to be upregulated in
COX4I1 patient fibroblasts and upon severe shRNA-mediated down -
regulation of COX4I1 mRNA ( Douiev et al., 2021 ). In our patient,
COX4I2 mRNA could not be reliably detected by quantitative PCR,
suggesting that such compensatory mechanism, while it cannot be
completely ruled out, is at least less evident than in the previously re -
ported individual. Notably, the clinical presentation of the reported
patient was relatively milder than the patient reported here ( Abu-Libdeh
et al., 2017 ). Furthermore, we speculate that the residual levels of wild
type COX4I1 mRNA and protein detected in our patient could potentially
explain why COX4I2 upregulation is not clearly observed or is below the
sensitivity threshold of the method employed. To fully elucidate this
issue, further studies involving additional COX4I1-deficient individuals
and using more sensitive methodologies will be essential to clarify
whether isoform 2 upregulation is a unique feature of the patient
described by Abu-Libdeh et al, or if it represents a broader compensatory
mechanism associated with COX4I1 deficiency.
Current knowledge of the pathomechanisms associated with COX4I1
deficiency in human disease remains limited, as functional studies in
previously reported patients are scarce and essentially restricted to
analysis performed in a single case ( Abu-Libdeh et al., 2017; Douiev
et al., 2018; Douiev et al., 2022 ). Therefore, at present, this knowledge
largely relies on observations made in genetically modified cellular
models. Here we expand the knowledge of this disorder at the molecular
level by providing the first proteomic study performed in COX4I1 in -
dividuals. As expected, proteomic data revealed a significant reduction
of complex IV structural subunits but also highlighted a strong signature
consisting on the downregulation of mitoribosomal proteins and other
factors involved in mitochondrial ribosome assembly. Our data is
consistent with previous observations made in COX4I1 knock out cell
lines and support, for the first time in patient ’ s samples, the hypothesis
that a dual mechanism involving both complex IV and mitochondrial
ribosome function could be on the bases of the pathogenic cascade of
this disorder (
ˇ
Cun ´atov ´a et al., 2021 ). Although the precise molecular
events underlying COX4I1 deficiency are not yet fully understood, it is
plausible to speculate that the mitoribosomal defects associated with
this disease could result from deregulated levels of at least a subset of
mitoribosomal assembly factors. However, further studies will be
required to elucidate the precise mechanistic issues, which are over the
scope of the present manuscript.
5. Conclusions
In summary, our study reports the fourth description of COX4I1
deficiency associated with human disease. We provided functional data
demonstrating that the identified variants exert their pathogenic effect
by targeting COX4I1 protein levels and subsequently the activity of
mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV. Moreover, our proteomic
approach suggests a pathomechanism — still to be fully clarified — that
involves not only the regulation of complex IV function but also the
formation of mitochondrial ribosomes. Lastly, although our data shed
light to clarify some of the main clinical and biochemical features
associated with COX4I1 deficiency further descriptions are still needed
to fully delineate the phenotypic spectrum of the disease and the un -
derlying molecular mechanisms involved in its pathogenesis.
