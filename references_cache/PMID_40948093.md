# Primary ciliary dyskinesia phenotypes and correlation with genotype.
**Authors:** Horani A, Wee W, Omran H, Ferkol T
**Journal:** Curr Opin Pulm Med (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1097/MCP.0000000000001212](https://doi.org/10.1097/MCP.0000000000001212)

## Content

PURPOSE OF REVIEW: Primary ciliary dyskinesia is a rare, inherited disease, and over 60 genes have been linked to motile ciliopathies. During the past quarter century, our understanding of the complex genetics and biological function of motile cilia has greatly advanced.

RECENT FINDINGS: Our growing knowledge of genetics and pathophysiology of primary ciliary dyskinesia has yielded insights into novel clinical features and genotype-phenotype relationships in motile ciliopathies. Children with biallelic CCDC39 or CCDC40 mutations have greater lung disease, related to both cilia motility-dependent and motility-independent effects. Pathogenic variants in genes involved in cilia generation, like CCNO , are also associated with more severe lung disease. Conversely, people who have defects in other genes, like DHAH11 and RSPH1 , have less severe lung disease, possibly related to residual ciliary motility. Finally, a growing number of primary ciliopathies are associated with abnormal motile cilia ultrastructure and function, and specific pathogenic variants can lead to distinct clinical presentations, best illustrated by structure-function studies in TUBB4B .

SUMMARY: These findings have yielded new insights into the clinical heterogeneity of motile ciliopathies, thus broadening their clinical spectrum. Additional research to elucidate the underlying pathophysiology in these overlapping conditions is warranted.

Motile and primary (sensory) cilia are specialized organelles present on most cells and have wide ranging functions. Our understanding of the genetics, pathophysiology, and clinical manifestations of motile ciliopathies, like primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD), has greatly advanced since the disease was linked to ciliary ultrastructural defects over half-century ago and led to unexpected findings. In this manuscript, we will review our expanding knowledge of the genetics and pathophysiology of PCD and other motile ciliopathies, their clinical features, and genotype-phenotype relationships, including overlapping conditions and syndromes.

Cilia are highly conserved organelles that project from the cell surface and have specialized functions in the body, generally classified by microtubular structure and function. Usually, in humans they are segregated into three main types of cilia: motile 9+2 motile cilia (multiple motile cilia of airway, ependymal, fallopian tube and efferent ductule cells; sperm cilia), embryonic 9+0 motile cilia (nodal cilia), and 9+0 nonmotile, primary cilia (present on most fully differentiated cell types) [1] (Fig. 1).

Multiple motile cilia are complex structures composed of hundreds of proteins [2]. They are evolutionarily conserved organelles on the epithelial surface of the upper and lower respiratory tract, specific parts of the male and female reproductive tracts, and ependymal cells lining the brain ventricles within the central nervous system. There are hundreds of cilia per ciliated epithelial cell. These multiciliated cells are clustered atop the airway epithelium, and the cilia move synchronously in the same direction to achieve mucociliary clearance, vectorially clearing mucus, trapped inhaled particles, and pathogens from the airways [3]. The sperm have a single cilium (also known as flagellum), structurally analogous to the motile cilium, which is responsible for propulsion.

The motile cilia axoneme, a microtubular structure, is composed of nine doublets encircling a central pair, forming a characteristic “9+2” configuration recognizable on axonemal cross-sections seen using transmission electron photomicrography. Inner (IDA) and outer dynein arms (ODA) are composed of dynein motors that extend from the outer doublet and repeat at regular intervals. The ODA power ciliary beating, producing a coordinated, wave-like beat followed by a recovery stroke. The IDA emanate from microtubule doublets and are linked to radial spokes (RS). The RS also anchor the outer doublets to the central pair and interact with the nexin-dynein regulatory complex (N-DRC), a structure that regulates motor activity. Additionally, the RS coordinate dynein arm activity, sending signals from the central apparatus (CA) to the dynein arms [3,4].

Motile cilia also exist transiently in the ventral node of the gastrula during embryonic development. Nodal cilia are monocilia that have a circular array of nine microtubule doublets with IDA and ODA but lack the CA, resulting in a 9+0 configuration. They rotate in a whirling motion, generating leftward flow of extracellular fluid across the surface. This directional flow induces asymmetric deformation of sensory cilia along the dorsoventral axis, activating calcium signaling and a cascade of transcriptional changes to establish body sidedness [5■■]. Without leftward flow, left-right organ laterality becomes a random event.

A third type of cilia, known as primary or sensory, are ubiquitous, single organelles on the surface of most nondividing cells. Typically, they also have a 9+0 configuration. Unlike motile cilia, primary cilia lack the dynein motors, rendering them nonmotile. These organelles have vital functions during embryogenesis, and are central to development, growth, and repair functions, often mediated through surface receptors. Moreover, primary cilia play important roles in sensory organs, serving as chemoreceptors, mechanoreceptors, and osmoreceptors. For instance, they form the photoreceptors in the retina. Interestingly, the inner ear kinocilia is the only sensory cilia that have a CA (9+2 configuration) [6,7]. Genetic defects in primary cilia can affect many organ systems such as polycystic kidney diseases, Jeune thoracic dystrophy, and Joubert syndrome, retinal disease along with others, collectively known as ciliopathies.

Any disruption of airway cilia function can lead to impaired mucociliary clearance, leading to airway infections and inflammation. Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a rare, genetically heterogeneous ciliopathy in which motile cilia function is impaired. Recent genetic epidemiological studies have calculated a prevalence to 1:7500 worldwide [8]. The cardinal features of PCD include neonatal respiratory distress in a term birth and daily “wet” or productive cough beginning in the first 6months of life [9]. Bronchiectasis is common, typically involving the morphological right middle lobe and lingula, and nearly universal in adults with the disease. Sinonasal disease is another common feature and contributes greatly to disease morbidity. Daily nonseasonal nasal congestion and rhinitis are present in over 80% of patients [9], beginning in infancy and persisting into adulthood [10].

Motile cilia line the middle ear and Eustachian tubes, and their dysfunction causes impaired clearance. Most people with PCD have middle ear involvement, including persistent effusions and recurrent ear infections [9]and conductive hearing loss is common. Increasingly, sensorineural hearing loss has been described in adults with PCD [10,11], though the mechanism remains unclear. A recent study suggested that sensorineural hearing loss may be related to progressive cochlear damage [12]. Management varies but can include hearing aids or insertion of tympanostomy tubes, though the latter can lead to recurrent, prolonged otorrhea and persistent membrane perforations [9,10].

While PCD is usually associated with upper and lower respiratory tract involvement, there is a spectrum of clinical manifestations that extend beyond the airways; left-right laterality defects, subfertility, and central nervous system involvement. Roughly half of people with PCD have complete mirror-image arrangement of their organs, that is, situs inversus totalis. Other organ laterality defects can also occur, broadly categorized as situs ambiguus and heterotaxy, a syndrome in which organs are mislocalized and sometimes abnormal, such as congenital heart defects, pulmonary isomerism, intestinal malrotation, liver abnormalities, asplenia and polysplenia. Studies suggest that heterotaxy can occur in 12% of individuals with PCD [13,14].

In the central nervous system, cilia are found on the ependymal cells and play a role in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) transport. Hydrocephalus is frequently described in various animal models with motile cilia defect, and ventriculomegaly is increasingly recognized in people with PCD [15■]. The exact mechanism is unknown, but several hypotheses have been proposed, including impaired CSF circulation related to abnormal ependymal cilia leading to dilation of the ventricles, choroid plexus hyperplasia with CSF over-production, or aqueduct stenosis as seen in individuals with de novo autosomal dominant mutations in FOXJ1 defect [16]. The latter maybe due to effects on NOTCH-1 signaling which regulates cell proliferation and differentiation within the ependymal layer of the brain’s ventricles. The clinical and long-term neurodevelopmental implications of hydrocephalus in PCD are largely unknown.

In the male and female reproductive systems, cilia have important functions. In men, some genetic defects can impair sperm cilia function and thus motility. In addition, some genetic defects can impact multiple motile cilia lining the efferent ductules of the testes that when defective cause sperm agglutination and luminal stasis [17]. Moreover, pathogenic variants in some genes, like DNAH11, have been associated with asthenoteratozoospermia [18,19].

In women, ciliary dysfunction affects cilia in both fallopian tubes and endometrium, resulting in impaired ovum transport, reduced fertility, and modestly increased risk of ectopic pregnancies. It is hypothesized that lung disease severity does not predict fertility outcomes, but variability in fertility in patients with PCD may be related to the differences in genotype penetrance across different organ systems [20].

Through collaborative international research efforts, 64 confirmed or putative genes encoding proteins involved in cilia biogenesis, assembly, structure, and function have been implicated in PCD (Table 1), and this number will continue to rise. It is estimated that current genetic approaches identify 70% and 80% of clinically diagnosed cases of PCD [1,21]. Mostly transmitted as an autosomal recessive trait, PCD has also been documented through X-linked and hemidominant inheritance. Therefore, careful family history for consanguinity or presence of other affected relatives should be elicited. Interestingly, PCD phenotypes, albeit milder, can also occur in female carriers of X-linked PCD variants (e.g. DNAAF6) due to skewed X-inactivation [22]. Recently, de novo dominant mutations in FOXJ1 and TUBB4B have been reported in PCD individuals [13,24].

Many disease-associated genes have been linked to specific ultrastructural defects and ciliary dysmotility, including those that encode proteins in the ODA, IDA, N-DRC, RS, and CA, historically identified by changes in cilia ultrastructure by transmission electron microscopy. However, there are an increasing number of gene variants that are associated with normal or near normal cilia ultrastructure [1,3,24], reflecting the limitations of transmission electron microscopy as a diagnostic test.

Genes associated with PCD can be classified as those encoding the structural components of the cilia. These include variants affecting the ODA proteins, such as DNAH5 and DNAI1[1], which account for approximately 30% of PCD cases, the largest subgroup of PCD. Pathogenic variants in these genes often result in truncated or absent ODA and dysmotile cilia. Other gene variants may have preserved though abnormal cilia motility, such as variants in the ODA protein DNAH11. Individuals with variants in DNAH11 have rapid and hyperkinetic cilia beat patterns, normal axonemal ultrastructure, and often have fewer neonatal manifestations and less lung disease [25■,26,27].

Genes encoding RS proteins, like RSPH4A and RSPH9, and CA associated proteins, like SPEF2, STK36, CFAP46, CFAP54, CFAP74, and CFAP221, are not associated with laterality defects, since they do not appreciably impair nodal cilia motility [1]. In addition, people with RSPH1 defects reportedly have less pronounced lung disease and less middle ear involvement [28].

Defects in the CA protein, HYDIN, account for at least 7% of confirmed PCD cases, and cause subtle ultrastructure changes with a near normal cilia motility phenotype. Pathogenic HYDIN variants pose a diagnostic challenge due to the presence of a pseudogene (HYDIN2). Combined approaches, involving long-read sequencing and immunofluorescence microscopy, using antibodies targeting the interacting protein SPEF2, can help to establish the correct diagnosis [29,30]. Another protein, growth arrestspecific protein 2-like 2 (GAS2L2), maintains basal body orientationand pathogenic GAS2L2 variants have been found to cause ciliary disorientation and asynchronous beating [31].

Pathogenic variants in genes encoding coiled-coil domain containing proteins, CCDC39 and CCDC40 [32,33], produce shortened cilia with IDA defects and microtubular disorganization, related to abnormal spacing of IDA and CA within the axoneme. Notably, studies have shown that people with biallelic CCDC39 and CCDC40 variants have more severe lung disease when compared with individuals with dynein arm protein defects [25■,34,35]. Recent analysis demonstrated that the heterodimer CCDC39 and CCDC40 function as a scaffold to support the assembly of a network of ciliary proteins, and genetic defects lead to loss of over 90 cilia structural proteins and ciliary address recognition proteins. Thus, respiratory disease caused by variants in CCDC39 and CCDC40 are likely related to both motility-dependent and motility-independent effects of the extensive loss of these proteins [36■■]. Another gene, NEK10, encodes a ciliated cell-specific kinase that regulates cells length, and when mutated causes shortened motile cilia and impaired mucociliary clearance [37].

Not all forms of PCD are associated with structural cilia proteins. There are 12 nonaxonemal proteins that are expressed in the cell cytoplasm critical for the assembly of axonemal structures before transport into the cilia [1,24]. Defects in these dynein axonemal assembly factors are associated with truncated or absent ODA and IDA. The precise function of many of these proteins is not known, but evidence points to close interactions between several assembly factors, dynein arm proteins, and heat-shock proteins within specialized cytoplasmic organelles called dynein axonemal particles (DyNAPs), suggesting that they may function as chaperons during dynein arm protein assembly [38,39].

Variants in cilia regulatory factors have also been identified in PCD patients. Cyclin O (CCNO) regulates centriole amplification and ciliogenesis, and defects are associated with reduced numbers of normally appearing motile cilia, leading to a form of PCD termed reduced generation of multiple motile cilia disease. Affected people present with sinopulmonary disease and hydrocephaly in some [40]. A recent large genotype-phenotype study of over 1200 PCD individuals demonstrated that CCNO mutations had greater reduction of FEV1 when compared to other gene defects [25■]. Similarly, de novo variants in FOXJ1, a cilia master transcription regulator, cause oligocilia and associated with airway disease and hydrocephaly [16].

Motile cilia share many structures with primary cilia. For example, PKD1 and PKD2 encode polycystin 1 and polycystin 2, respectively, localize to the renal primary cilium [41] and are essential for modulating mechanical signals that regulate renal morphogenesis. Defects are associated with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD). There is evidence that polycystins are also expressed on motile cilia, and clinical studies have shown that people with ADPKD have an increased prevalence of bronchiectasis [42], but without many features often associated with PCD. Patients with other primary ciliopathies, such as Usher syndrome, a genetic disease that affects the retinal photoreceptors and cochlear cilia, can also rarely present with motile cilia dysfunction [43].

Skeletal dysplasia is a group of disorders that affect the axial skeleton and ribs cage and may be associated with secondary respiratory insufficiency [44], often caused by variants in genes affecting intraciliary transport in primary cilia. While most syndromes linked with skeletal dysplasia do not have primary lung disease, there are recent reports suggesting that some affected individuals have motile cilia dysfunction [45]. For instance, specific IFT74 defects, which encodes an intraciliary transport protein, can cause clinical features of PCD with bronchiectasis. The motile cilia in these people are reportedly shortened with disorganized microtubules [46]. Indeed, novel genotype-phenotype relationships are increasingly recognized in these syndromes, and variants within specific domains may result in different clinical presentations.

Several genes encoding proteins within the cilia transition zone, an area at the proximal end of cilia important for intraciliary transport, have also been implicated in syndromic PCD. RPGR is a GTPase regulator localized to the transition zone of primary and motile cilia, and pathogenic variants cause X-linked retinitis pigmentosa. Affected individuals can also have features consistent with PCD, combining the eye and airway disease [47]. Likewise, people with oral-facial-digital syndrome type 1 and Joubert syndrome, caused by OFD1 defects that encodes a protein central to basal body biogenesis, can present with primary ciliopathy or syndromic PCD [48,49], depending on the variant and level of residual protein function. TALPID3 mutations also cause defects of the biogenesis of primary and respiratory cilia and Joubert syndrome as well as PCD [50].

Recent reports have described markedly variable clinical presentations in individuals with different variants in the same affected gene, best illustrated by tubulin beta 4B class IVb (TUBB4B) defects. Structure-function studies revealed that different TUBB4B variants affect distinct tubulin interfaces, which leads to three distinct, clinical presentations. Affected individuals can present with PCD alone, primary ciliopathy without PCD features, or syndromic PCD [23■■].

Primary ciliary dyskinesia is a rare, inherited disease of motile cilia and is among a growing collection of genetic disorders known as ciliopathies. Usually, people with PCD present early in life with neonatal respiratory distress, left-right laterality defects, persistent sinonasal and middle ear disease, chronic daily cough with eventual development of bronchiectasis. Our understanding of the genetics in PCD has greatly advanced, and this knowledge has translated into new insights about the clinical heterogeneity of motile ciliopathies, including extra-pulmonary manifestations. Indeed, motile ciliopathies have a broad clinical spectrum, and atypical forms are still to be discovered. As our understanding of PCD and other motile ciliopathies grow, it is certain that more overlapping cases will be identified.
