# Hypo-osmotic swelling test guided sperm selection followed by intracytoplasmic sperm injection in two men with multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella associated with SPEF2 and CFAP251 variants: two case reports.
**Authors:** Deng H, Zheng T
**Journal:** AME Case Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.21037/acr-2026-0186](https://doi.org/10.21037/acr-2026-0186)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella (MMAF) is a rare but severe form of asthenozoospermia. It is characterized by multiple abnormalities in sperm flagella, manifesting as various combinations of short, absent, coiled, curved, and/or irregularly diameter flagella. For MMAF patients with no motile sperm in their semen, it is necessary to identify viable immotile sperm for assisted conception via intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). The hypo-osmotic swelling test (HOST) technique is one method used to identify viable immotile sperm.

CASE DESCRIPTION: Both patients were young men diagnosed with MMAF and had no motile sperm in their semen. Genetic testing revealed a sperm flagellar 2 (SPEF2) gene mutation in patient A and a cilia and flagella associated protein 251 (CFAP251) gene mutation in patient B. Live immotile sperm were successfully identified in both patients using the HOST, and ICSI was performed. They underwent ICSI successfully and achieved pregnancy. Both couples achieved live births following ICSI treatment.

CONCLUSIONS: For MMAF patients lacking motile sperm, the HOST is a viable method for identifying viable sperm for ICSI. These two cases, caused by SPEF2 and CFAP251 mutations, provide clinical evidence supporting the potential for successful assisted conception via ICSI in MMAF patients with different genetic etiologies.

• This study describes the use of hypo-osmotic swelling test (HOST) to identify viable sperm for the treatment of multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella (MMAF).

• This study reports successful intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) cases in patients with MMAF caused by these two genetic mutations in this study.

• Men with MMAF cannot conceive a child through natural conception. ICSI is currently the primary treatment option for fertility in these patients.

Multiple morphological abnormalities of the sperm flagella (MMAF) is a rare but severe subtype of asthenozoospermia. It is characterized by multiple abnormalities in sperm flagella, manifesting as various combinations of short, absent, coiled, curved, and/or irregularly diameter flagella (1). MMAF leads to asthenozoospermia. In most cases, the percentage of motile sperm in MMAF patients ranges from 0% to 10% (2). Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) is an assisted reproductive technology that helps MMAF patients conceive genetically related offspring. For MMAF patients with no motile sperm, live immotile sperm must be identified for ICSI. The hypo-osmotic swelling test (HOST) is one method used to identify live immotile sperm. HOST evaluates sperm tail membrane integrity via osmosis: intact membranes allow hypotonic fluid to enter the relatively hypertonic sperm tail, causing swelling, while damaged membranes show no such response (3). We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://acr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/acr-2026-0186/rc).

Patient A: male, 26 years old. He married in April 2022. Since then, he has had regular sexual intercourse and has been able to achieve normal erections and ejaculation. He has not used contraception and his partner has not become pregnant. He presented to Yichang Central People’s Hospital in November 2023. Two semen analyses revealed no motile sperm, with normal semen volume and concentration, sperm viability of 40% and 48%, and normal sperm morphology of 0% in both analyses. Sperm viability in both patients was assessed using the eosin-alone staining method. Both patients underwent Papanicolaou staining for morphological evaluation. No characteristic defects were observed in the sperm head or neck morphology of either patient; the main abnormalities were concentrated in the tail region. The quantitative data on tail defects from the two semen analyses of patient A were as follows: absent flagella 33.5% and 41.4%, short flagella 45.5% and 39.3%, coiled flagella 3.6% and 2.7%, bent flagella 0% and 1.5%, and irregular-caliber flagella 22.5% and 30.8%. Both patients underwent whole-exome sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq platform, targeting all protein-coding exons and flanking splice regions (±20 bp). Reads were aligned to the GRCh37/hg19 reference genome. Variants were annotated using population databases including gnomAD, ExAC, and 1000 Genomes, as well as disease databases including ClinVar, HGMD, and OMIM. Filtering criteria excluded common polymorphisms with East Asian allele frequencies >1%, synonymous variants, non-splice-site intronic variants, and variants with low sequencing quality. Candidate variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) guidelines. All reported variants were validated by Sanger sequencing. For patient A, the target regions achieved a mean sequencing depth of 207.48×, with 99.66% of bases covered at ≥20× and a Q30 quality score of 97.22%. Whole-exome sequencing revealed a homozygous stop-gain variant c.2203C>T (p.Q735*) in the sperm flagellar 2 (SPEF2) gene. This variant replaces the amino acid glutamine at position 735 with a stop codon, causing premature termination of protein translation. This variant is classified as pathogenic.

Patient B: male, 26 years old. He married in January 2023. Since then, he has maintained a regular sexual life, with normal erections and ejaculation, and has not used contraception, resulting in no pregnancy. He presented to Yichang Central People’s Hospital in November 2023. Two semen analyses revealed no motile sperm, with normal semen volume, sperm concentrations of 6.8×106/mL and 20.9×106/mL, sperm viability of 40% and 38%, and normal sperm morphology of 0.5% and 0.3%, respectively. The quantitative data on tail defects from the two semen analyses of patient B were as follows: absent flagella 2.5% and 4.3%, short flagella 65.5% and 72.8%, coiled flagella 23.4% and 31.5%, bent flagella 0% and 0%, and irregular-caliber flagella 31.3% and 22.5%. The sperm deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) fragmentation index was 8.95%. For patient B, the whole-exome sequencing target regions achieved a mean sequencing depth of 187.48×, with 99.72% of bases covered at ≥20× and a Q30 quality score of 96.39%. Whole-exome sequencing revealed two heterozygous frameshift mutations in the cilia and flagella associated protein 251 (CFAP251) gene. Variant 1: a heterozygous frameshift mutation in the CFAP251 gene [c.202del (p.E68Rfs*31)], resulting in the replacement of glutamic acid at position 68 with arginine, followed by a frameshift and the premature generation of a stop codon, leading to premature termination of protein translation. This variant is classified as a likely pathogenic variant. Variant 2: a heterozygous frameshift mutation in the CFAP251 gene [c.1930_1931del (p.R644Gfs*3)] results in the replacement of arginine at position 644 with glycine, followed by a frameshift and the premature generation of a stop codon, leading to premature termination of protein translation. This variant is a pathogenic mutation.

The spouses of both patients are in good health, and their examination results showed no significant abnormalities. The basic information for the two patients and their spouses is presented in Table 1.

Prior to ovarian stimulation, our embryology laboratory staff performed an HOST preliminary test on the patient’s sperm. The purpose was to determine whether live sperm could be easily identified using the HOST, and secondly, to familiarize the embryology staff with the patient’s sperm to facilitate subsequent ICSI procedures. For the HOST preliminary test, we used the same HOST solution as that used for subsequent ICSI, prepared by mixing G-MOPS-plus (Vitrolife, Sweden) medium and purified water in a 1:1 ratio. Table 2 shows the HOST results for the two patients calculated using the method described above.

The ICSI procedures were performed as follows. The patient’s spouse underwent ovarian stimulation treatment according to a predetermined protocol. Ovarian stimulation was initiated on days 2–3 of the menstrual cycle. When a dominant follicle reached a diameter of ≥20 mm, or when three follicles reached a diameter of ≥18 mm, ultrasound-guided oocyte retrieval was performed 36 hours after the trigger injection. On the day of oocyte retrieval, the male partner provided a semen sample via masturbation, and the collected sperm was washed and optimized using the direct wash centrifugation method. After the retrieved oocytes were washed and cultured, ICSI fertilization was performed. During ICSI, we selected spermatozoa with normal head morphology for HOST confirmation of sperm viability. However, these spermatozoa did not necessarily have normal neck or tail morphology. We used the following HOST to select viable sperm for fertilization. Three droplets were prepared in the ICSI dish: the first contained G-MOPS-plus medium; the second contained a 1:1 mixture of G-MOPS-plus medium and distilled water; and the third contained G-MOPS-plus medium. All three microdrops had a volume of 5 μL, and the working temperature was maintained at 37.0±0.5 ℃. The osmolality of the first and third microdrops ranged from 270–280 mOsm/kg. Due to the addition of purified water, the osmolality of the second micro-drop was approximately 140 mOsm/kg. The washed and prepared sperm were added to the first droplet. Using the ICSI microinjection needle, we selected a spermatozoon and placed it into the second droplet to observe whether the sperm flagellum swells. Once swelling was observed, the spermatozoon was immediately transferred to the third droplet. After the swelling subsides, the spermatozoon was injected into the oocyte using the microinjection needle. We repeated the above process to complete ICSI fertilization. The fertilized eggs were cultured in an incubator for 3–7 days, and well-developed cleavage-stage embryos or blastocysts were selected for embryo transfer. We defined normal fertilization as two-pronuclear fertilization. Embryo grading was performed according to the Istanbul Consensus on embryo assessment and the Gardner blastocyst grading system (4).

The ICSI procedure, embryonic development, blastocyst culture and formation, as well as pregnancy outcomes of the two patients are summarized in Table 3. No artificial oocyte activation was performed on any of the retrieved oocytes. Patient A received a fresh transfer of two cleavage-stage embryos on day 3. Patient B did not undergo fresh embryo transfer due to the risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome. One 4BB blastocyst was thawed and transferred. Both patients had positive pregnancy tests 14 days post-transfer. Their subsequent prenatal follow-ups were uneventful, and they delivered healthy babies at term. Please see Table 4 for the timeline of the two patients. All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yichang Central People’s Hospital (No. 2026-244-01). Written informed consent was obtained from the patients for publication of this case report. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

The innovation of the present case report lies in describing an ICSI treatment protocol relying on the HOST technique, through which MMAF patients with entirely immotile sperm achieved successful assisted reproduction and delivered healthy infants. Although numerous studies have confirmed that variants in the CFAP251 and SPEF2 genes lead to MMAF. Liu et al. reported that patients with pathogenic CFAP251 variants obtained healthy offspring after ICSI (5). However, relevant reports on healthy births following ICSI among patients carrying SPEF2 mutations are scarce. This case report supplements clinical evidence for ICSI application in patients with these two types of gene mutations.

HOST experiments conducted for assisted reproductive technology require a thorough assessment of the safety of assisted reproductive technology. Studies have shown that short-term hypotonic exposure does not impair sperm motility or survival. Electron microscopy has confirmed that swelling does not affect contractile activity or motility, has no significant effect on sperm head disaggregation or spermatid nucleation, and results in a chromosomal aneuploidy rate similar to that of motile sperm (6). Hypotonic solutions for ICSI can be prepared using the following method: a mixture of 50% culture medium and 50% distilled water (7,8). The World Health Organization manual only states that sperm should not be kept in hypotonic solutions for more than 5 minutes, but does not provide specific operational protocols (9).

The sperm tail contains at least 900 proteins (10), and mutations in approximately 62 genes have been reported to cause MMAF (11). The SPEF2 gene is located on chromosome 5p13.2 and consists of 46 exons. SPEF2 is critical for sperm flagellar biogenesis and function; mutations in this gene adversely affect sperm motility, leading to MMAF (11). CFAP251 is located on chromosome 12q24.31 and consists of 23 exons. The CFAP251 protein acts as a platform for protein complex assembly or a mediator of short-term interactions between proteins, playing a crucial role in flagellar structure and beating (12).

The limitations of this report include the lack of transmission electron microscopy examination of the spermatozoa and the inability to provide images of sperm morphology. For patient B, we did not obtain parental samples for segregation analysis. The phase of the two heterozygous frameshift variants in the CFAP251 gene therefore remains unconfirmed. Consequently, whether these variants are in trans or in cis could not be experimentally verified, and the biallelic inference is based solely on the disease inheritance model.

MMAF due to asthenozoospermia, the HOST is a feasible, safe, and effective method for selecting viable immotile sperm. It can be used for assisted reproduction via ICSI. Both cases of MMAF patients with SPEF2 and CFAP251 gene mutations reported in this study successfully achieved pregnancy and delivered healthy offspring through HOST combined with ICSI.
