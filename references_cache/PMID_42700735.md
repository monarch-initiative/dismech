# Dinutuximab beta with TEMIRI chemotherapy in relapsed/refractory neuroblastoma: Real-world insights into the open question of GD2 expression.
**Authors:** De Ioris MA, Del Bufalo F, Quintarelli C, Bertaina V, Fabozzi F, Cefalo MG, Alessi I, Grimaldi C, Villani MF, D'Andrea ML, Stracuzzi A, Altini C, Malaspina C, Sinibaldi M, Di Cecca S, Iaffaldano L, Geraci C, Becilli M, De Angelis B, Crocoli A, Cassanelli G, Margaritora S, Mastronuzzi A, Locatelli F
**Journal:** Transl Oncol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.tranon.2026.103013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tranon.2026.103013)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: Prognosis of Relapsed/Refractory (R/R) neuroblastoma is still dismal. Chemoimmunotherapy has been reported to improve the response rate.

PATIENTS AND METHODS: Dinutuximab beta added to chemotherapy was used in an off-label setting. Treatment was administered on a 21-day schedule: on day 1 Irinotecan was administered at the dose of 50 mg/m2/day for 5 days, together with Temozolomide, at the dose of 100 mg/m2/day for 5 days. Dinutuximab beta was administered from day 2 at the dose of 17,5 mg/m2/day for 4 consecutive days.

RESULTS: Twenty-five children received chemo-immunotherapy. Hematological toxicity was the most common adverse event. The best overall response rate (ORR) was 52% (95% CI:31-72), with an ORR of 62% (95% CI: 38-82) at metastatic sites. Responses were higher at bone and bone marrow sites (ORR 67% and 92%, respectively). SIOPEN skeletal score declined during treatment, the best response being observed after the first 2/3 courses. Primary tumors and soft-tissue lesions were less responsive, though metabolic activity often decreased. Negative or low GD2 expression was observed in 10% and in 35% of tumor tissue, respectively at baseline and after treatment, with mean GD2-positive tumor cells decreasing from 88% to 62% in tumor tissue, mainly associated with differentiating histology.

DISCUSSION: This real-world experience confirms that a short schedule is a feasible and effective option in R/R neuroblastoma. The dynamic and heterogeneous expression of GD2 deserves further evaluation, particularly in relation to selection of patients and subsequent GD2-directed treatment strategies.

Neuroblastoma (NB) is the most frequent extracranial solid tumor of childhood, with a course ranging from spontaneous regression without any treatment to a poor outcome despite intensive multimodal therapy [ 1 ]. Children with refractory/relapsed (R/R) disease present a grim prognosis with an event-free survival (EFS) < 20% [ 2 , 3 ]. Different second-line strategies have been proposed with similar limited efficacy [ [4] , [5] , [6] ]. The addition of immunotherapy, namely the monoclonal antibody dinutuximab directed against the disialogangloside GD2 antigen, to chemotherapy improves the effectiveness of standard backbone treatments [ 7 , 8 ]. In Europe, the BEACON-Immuno trial based on dinutuximab beta completed the recruitment in February 2021 and the results have been published recently, showing a response rate of 30% in the chemoimmunotherapy arm, versus 18% in the chemotherapy alone arm [ 9 ]. Nevertheless, a long-term disease control is still difficult to be reached.

Herein, we report a real-life case series of 25 patients with R/R NB treated with Dinutuximab beta plus temozolomide/irinotecan (TEMIRI). The GD2 expression was evaluated whenever possible before and after treatment.

Considering the preliminary report of BEACON Immuno on dinutuximab beta [ 10 ] and the COG experience with dinutuximab in RR NB [ 7 , 8 ], the chemo-immunotherapy was included in our institutional algorithm for RR patients. In detail, chemoimmunotherapy was considered as a first option in relapsed high-risk (HR) NB, in refractory NB not included in NBHR2 [ 11 ] and in relapsed low/intermediate NB children who failed a salvage treatment with standard chemotherapy. TEMIRI was used as backbone chemotherapy. Treatment was administered every 21 days according to the following scheme: on day 1 Irinotecan was administered at the dose of 50 mg/m2/day for 5 days, together with Temozolomide, at the dose of 100 mg/m2/day for 5 days. Dinutuximab beta, used in an off-label setting, was administered from day 2 for 4 consecutive days at the dose of 17,5 mg/m2/day (Supplemental Table S1). The chemoimmunotherapy was administered up to 6 courses depending on patient’s response and tolerability. We included all consecutive patients who received TEMIRI plus dinutuximab beta during the study period.

Data on demographics, disease status, previous treatment, antitumor activity, toxicity and safety for each patient treated were reviewed for preparing this report. A full disease evaluation was performed before treatment and after the second or the third course and every 2–3 courses and before any other treatments through MR/CT scan, metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) scan or FDG-PET scan for MIBG non-avid tumor and bone marrow evaluation. The bone marrow evaluation was performed through aspirates and trephines. Response was assessed according to modified International Neuroblastoma Response Criteria (INRC) [ 12 ]. Bone response was monitored by MIBG scintigraphy using the SIOPEN score [ 13 ], while a quantitative assessment of MIBG uptake in the primary tumor and in any soft tissue lesions was performed using xSPECT/CT system, defining the ratio lesion SUV max/liver SUV average. Two thresholds in SUV variation (ΔSUV) have been defined at 20% and 30%, respectively.

Bone marrow biopsies and aspirates were routinely performed with morphological, immunohistochemistry (IHC) and flow-cytometry (FCM) evaluation. FCM evaluation on fresh bone marrow and tumor samples from primary or metastatic disease was employed to study the expression of GD2. GD2 expression was evaluated by a dedicated multiparametric flow cytometry panel including CD45, CD56, CD81, CD99, B7-H3 and GD2 (see Supplemental file for more details). GD2 expression was assessed on bone marrow cells which were classified as either positive or negative, while tumor tissue was classified as negative/low expression when less than 50% of tumor cells expressed GD2. The 50% cut off was exploratory. Bone biopsies were performed when clinically indicated and not specifically for GD2 expression.

The overall response rate (ORR) was defined as the sum of complete (CR), partial response (PR) and minimal response (MR) according to the INRC [ 14 ]. The MIBG scintigraphy for this report were reviewed by two senior nuclear medicine experts (MFV, CA), while CT/MR scan were reviewed by two senior radiologists (MLD, CM). Clinical and laboratory adverse events occurring during treatment are reported according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0 (CTCAE v5.0) [ 15 ].

The Internal Review Board (IRB) of the Bambino Gesù Children’s Hospital approved the administration of the proposed schedule in each patient, and a specific informed consent (IC) was signed by the patient parents or legal guardians. All investigations were conducted in accordance with the principles expressed in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data was analyzed by using both descriptive and inferential statistics. Continuous variables were summarized and reported as median. Categorical variables (including dichotomous variables) were summarized and reported as counts and/or percentages. The independence between two given variables of interest was assessed by exploiting Chi-square (for large-sized samples) or Fisher’s exact tests (for small-sized samples). Continuous variables were reported as median (interquartile range, IQR). Comparisons between independent, non-paired groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. All statistical analyses were performed by using Easy R statistical software [ 16 ] and, in these analyses, p-values ≤ 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

From June 2023 to May 2025, 25 patients with R/R NB were treated with dinutuximab beta plus TEMIRI for a total of 103 courses. Patients' characteristics including the disease status are summarized in Table 1. Median age at time of treatment was 5.6 years (range 2.4–18.4 years; IQR 3.3–6.9 years); 18 patients were male and 7 females. Eleven patients had previously received ≥ 2 lines of treatment, while in 14 patients chemoimmunotherapy represented the second-line approach after relapse or for a refractory disease. Previous immunotherapy with dinutuximab beta had been administered in 9 patients. Out of 25 patients, 9 (36%) presented a primary refractory disease. A metastatic dissemination at time of treatment was detected in 21/25 patients; overall, 18 patients presented a MIBG-avid disease at metastatic site with a median SIOPEN Skeletal score of 25 (range 4–64; IQR 11–35). Nineteen patients have bone/bone marrow metastasis (one of them with negative MIBG scan), 2 patients an isolated lymph-node involvement, while combined metastasis were reported in 6 cases. Out of 25 patients, 20 patients presented a primary tumor/residual primary tumor (in 9 patients with a maximum diameter lower than 5 cm).

All patients received the same schedule for a total of 103 course. Toxicity information was available for all patients and courses. Chemotherapy reduction was performed in 6 out of 25 patients for grade 4 hematological toxicity, associated with a delay of more than 7 days to start a new course. None reduced the dosage of or discontinued dinutuximab beta.

As shown in Table 2, the main toxicities observed were hematological with neutropenia in 60% (grade 4 in 13%) and thrombocytopenia in 51% (grade 4 in 7%) of courses. Grade 1–2 fever was observed in 45% of patients. To note, grade 1–2 neuropathic pain was observed in only 10/103 (10%) courses and always during the first course. There were no toxic deaths. Major toxicities were observed in the first course with a reduced toxicity in the subsequent courses.

At last follow-up on January 15st 2026, 14 patients were alive and 11 had died of tumor progression/relapsed disease.

The best ORR was 52% [95%CI: 31–72], with progression during treatment recorded in 4 patients. In details, two patients presented a metastatic progression soon after two courses, the third child, who was in PR after the first two courses, progressed at metastatic site after four courses and the last patient with localized MYCN amplified tumor in SD after two courses, subsequently progressed after 4 courses. The SIOPEN Skeletal score decreased to 0 in 8/18 patients (44%) after treatment with ORR on bone of 67% [95%CI: 41–87]. Overall, baseline GD2 expression was evaluable in 19 of 25 patients (76%), based on bone marrow and/or tumor tissue from the primary tumor or a metastatic site. Bone marrow assessment was available in 14 patients and consistently identified a GD2-positive neuroblastoma cell population. Among 12 tumor tissue samples assessed, 9 were evaluable, of which 2 showed negative/low GD2 expression (Table 4). Notably, one patient showed heterogeneous GD2 expression across disease sites, with GD2-negative neuroblastoma cells in a bone lesion and GD2-positive cells in the bone marrow. Overall, negative/low GD2 expression was documented in 2 of 19 evaluable patients (10%). One of these two patients experienced progressive disease during chemoimmunotherapy, compared with 3 of 17 patients with GD2-positive disease. After chemoimmunotherapy, GD2 expression was assessed in 22 tumor tissue samples obtained from 17 patients, of which 18 samples (82%) were evaluable. Low/negative GD2 expression was observed in 6 of 17 patients (35%), all of whom showed evidence of tumor differentiation on histological assessment. Notably, one patient showed heterogeneous GD2 expression across disease sites, with GD2-negative cells in an abdominal lymph-node lesion showing ganglioneuroma histology and a GD2-positive population in the primary tumor showing differentiating neuroblastoma histology. Overall, GD2 expression tended to decrease after chemoimmunotherapy, with the median percentage of GD2-positive tumor cells decreasing from 88% (IQR 56–93; range 0–100) at baseline to 62% (IQR 32–92; range 1.8–97) after treatment, although this difference was not statistically significant. Low/negative GD2 expression after treatment was not significantly associated with response, with an ORR of 50% compared with 55% in patients with GD2-positive disease. Histological findings are summarized in Table 4. Notably, all four patients with subsequent relapse for whom GD2 evaluation was available remained GD2-positive, providing no evidence of preferential selection of GD2-negative clones during chemoimmunotherapy.

R/R neuroblastoma remains an unmet medical need. In recent years, immunotherapy has opened new perspectives for potentially curative approaches; among the most promising strategies, there are GD2-targeted therapies, including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells and HLA-haploidentical stem-cell transplantation followed by anti-GD2 monoclonal antibody therapy, both showing encouraging results with impressive long-term outcomes [ [17] , [18] , [19] , [20] ].

Achieving and maintaining tumor burden reduction is essential to maximize the efficacy of immune-based consolidation strategies, and the Neuroblastoma Drug Development Strategy Forum emphasized the importance of GD2-directed approaches [ 21 ]. Within this evolving therapeutic landscape, chemoimmunotherapy plays a central role as reinduction for R/R patients. The BEACON—Neuroblastoma trial (2013), designed to identify the most effective chemotherapy backbone in a randomized setting, reported an ORR of 26% in the bevacizumab arm versus 18% in controls, underscoring the limited efficacy of chemotherapy alone [ 22 ].

COG studies showed ORRs of 41.5–49% in multicenter prospective trials combining irinotecan, temozolomide, and dinutuximab with GM-CSF [ 7 , 9 ]. In recent years, off label chemoimmunotherapy has become the standard of care for R/R neuroblastoma in the United States [ 23 ], whereas its implementation in Europe has progressed more slowly. In detail, the European experience remains limited, with few retrospective series reporting outcomes with dinutuximab beta plus chemotherapy [ [24] , [25] , [26] ], while only recently the BEACON—Neuroblastoma Immuno trial results were published [ 9 ].This latter trial showed an ORR of 30.2% in the immune arm versus 18.2% in the non-immune arm, providing a signal of activity that warrants further investigation [ 9 ].

In the retrospective European analyses, the Sacha study documented that the chemotherapy backbone has little impact on efficacy of chemoimmunotherapy, reinforcing the central role of the anti-GD2 component and suggesting a negative influence of MYCN amplification on response [ 26 ]. Moreover, the best response was observed after two courses, with no substantial benefit from additional courses [ 26 ]. Similarly, in our cohort, the major response was achieved within the first 2–3 courses, with no significant gains from treatment prolongation. This finding has important implications for the design of re-induction and subsequent consolidation strategies based on chemo-immunotherapy. At our institution, dinutuximab beta was administered as a condensed 4-day infusion every 3 weeks to improve treatment feasibility, reduce hospitalization, and enhance patient compliance. The schedule was well tolerated, with no dinutuximab beta dose reductions or treatment discontinuations. The best ORR was 52%, with an ORR at metastatic sites of 62%. The most striking responses were observed in bone and bone marrow metastases, whereas primary tumors and soft tissue/lymph-node disease were largely unmodified in terms of tumor masses although a SUV reduction was recorded. A quantitative software analysis of MIBG uptake in neuroblastoma has not been reported, except in a study by Wang et al., where xSPECT was used to evaluate bone lesions [ 27 ]. Nonetheless, reductions in MIBG avidity together with histological evidence of tumor differentiation support the clinical relevance of metabolic responses. This finding is in line with the Spanish experience, where two-thirds of patients with residual lesions after immunotherapy showed histological differentiation, highlighting the potential limitations of conventional imaging in assessing response to immunotherapy [ 28 ].

GD2 expression appeared to mirror this pattern, with low expression in differentiating tumors, underscoring biological complexity and implications for treatment planning. Building on the long-held assumption that GD2 is broadly expressed in neuroblastoma, earlier clinical-pathological series already demonstrated meaningful heterogeneity: in a large German cohort (NB97/NB2004), Schumacher-Kuckelkorn et al. reported either complete or partial GD2 loss in about 12% of cases—already 7.8% at diagnosis and more frequent at relapse—suggesting the emergence of GD2-negative subclones under treatment pressure [ 29 ]. Similarly, Terzić et al. found GD2 expression in 96% of tumors, but wide variation in the fraction of GD2-positive cells; notably, lower baseline GD2 positivity correlated with relapse after chimeric monoclonal antibody ch14.18, and in vitro natural killer (NK)-mediated antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) efficacy correlated with GD2 density, indicating that the level of expression rather than the mere presence of GD2 is clinically relevant [ 30 ].

Recently, in pre-clinical setting, both Mabe et al. and Di Matteo et al. [ 31 , 32 ] conclude that GD2 expression is a critical determinant of response to GD2-targeted therapy, though different mechanisms of GD2 modulation were hypothesized. In the former study, epigenetic lineage switching that reduces GD2 via ST8SIA1 down-regulation and is reversible with EZH2 inhibition,was proposed [ 31 ], while in the latter, immune-escape–driven resistance in primary mesenchymal cultures, where GD2 is preserved or increased and sensitivity is restored by dinutuximab-mediated ADCC, was reported [ 32 ]. However, in both COG and European studies, tumor samples were not routinely tested for GD2 expression before therapy. As noted by Irwin and Goldsmith [ 33 ], although anti-GD2 antibodies—either alone or combined with chemotherapy—show substantial efficacy in HR neuroblastoma, validated biomarkers for patient selection are still lacking. Prospective evaluation of GD2 has been hindered by assay standardization issues and by the difficulty/complexity of obtaining serial sampling, though emerging blood- and bone marrow–based assays offer promising non-invasive options to monitor GD2 dynamics and optimize treatment strategies [ [33] , [34] , [35] , [36] , [37] , [38] ].

This study provides a first real-world evaluation of GD2 expression in the setting of chemoimmunotherapy. GD2 expression was assessed, whenever feasible, before and after treatment in bone marrow and/or tumor tissue. Our findings suggest that GD2 expression may vary across disease sites and over time, highlighting its spatial and temporal heterogeneity. Low/negative GD2 expression was observed in approximately 10% of evaluable patients at baseline and in 35% after chemoimmunotherapy, with an overall trend toward reduced GD2 expression in tumor tissue after treatment. Notably, low/negative GD2 expression after treatment was consistently associated with histological evidence of tumor differentiation, suggesting that changes in GD2 expression should be interpreted in the context of tumor phenotype. At baseline, one of the two patients with low/negative GD2 expression experienced disease progression, raising the hypothesis that reduced target expression may influence response to GD2-directed therapy. However, the very small number of patients precludes any conclusion regarding its predictive value. Importantly, the 50% threshold used to define low GD2 expression in tumor tissue was exploratory and does not represent a validated biological or clinically relevant cut-off; bone marrow assessment was performed separately and classified as GD2-positive or -negative.

Overall our findings are encouraging and consistent with previous reports in terms of efficacy and toxicity, despite the use of a condensed 4-day infusion schedule. The clinical benefit was largely achieved within the first two treatment courses, with no meaningful incremental responses, thereafter, thus supporting a careful optimization of re-induction timing and subsequent consolidation strategies. Our exploratory findings also bring the complexity of GD2 expression into focus, highlighting its heterogeneity and relationship with tumor differentiation. Rather than defining a predictive biomarker or clinically relevant cut-off, our data raise an open question regarding the potential role of longitudinal GD2 assessment in informing GD2-directed treatment strategies. Given the central role of GD2-targeted antibody and cellular therapies in neuroblastoma, prospective studies incorporating standardized methodologies and longitudinal GD2 evaluation are warranted to better define the clinical relevance of GD2 expression.
