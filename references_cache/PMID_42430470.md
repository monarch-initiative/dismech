# The MEK inhibitor trametinib incurs mitochondrial injury and induces innate immune responses in the mouse heart.
**Authors:** Fisher-Wellman KH, Lutze RD, Kirkland LG, Beak JY, Goyal M, Nagalingam R, Morrissey SM, Sandroni PB, Huang W, Bailon JD, Schroder MA, Albrecht LA, Ofei E, Chin AL, Green TD, McClung JM, Montgomery MM, Hagen JT, Chrest BR, Franczak EM, Krassovkaia P, Aruleba RT, Zawistowski JS, Stuhlmiller TJ, Gomez SM, Prathumsap N, Zhang Q, Zhang J, Xu W, Zhang L, Meier JA, Carey LA, Schisler JC, Johnson GL, Jensen BC
**Journal:** Sci Adv (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1126/sciadv.aeb2695](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aeb2695)

## Content

Trametinib (Trm) is a highly selective mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase (MEK) inhibitor that potently and persistently abrogates extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 activation. Trm initially was used to treat BRAF Val600→Glu (V600E)-mutated melanoma, but its Food and Drug Administration-approved indications are expanding rapidly. Trm generally is well tolerated, but it can cause dose-limiting cardiomyopathy and heart failure. Here, we characterize a mouse model of Trm cardiotoxicity using complementary in vitro approaches to show that Trm induces mitochondrial dysfunction in cardiomyocytes and some cancer cell types. In vivo, Trm caused contractile dysfunction within 3 days and heart failure within 2 weeks. High-resolution respirometry using isolated cardiac mitochondria revealed that Trm compromises oxidative metabolism, in part, through blunted activity of electron transport system complexes. Trm-mediated mitochondrial injury led to the release of mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns including mitochondrial DNA in both mice and humans, triggering activation of canonical innate immune pathways including cGAS-STING. In multiple rodent and human cardiomyocyte platforms, Trm diminished mitochondrial respiratory capacity at nanomolar concentrations, but this lesion was reversed by expression of a phosphomimetic signal transducer and activator of transcription 3-S727 construct. We also found that Trm induced mitochondrial dysfunction in some but not all cancer cell lines, identifying a previously unrecognized effect that could contribute to Trm's anticancer efficacy.

Persistent hyperactivation of the RAS–RAF–mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) kinase (MEK)–extracellular signal–regulated kinase (ERK) pathway commonly contributes to carcinogenesis. Many recently developed cancer therapies target this pathway, including inhibitors of MAPK kinase 1 and 2 (MEK 1 and MEK 2). Trametinib (Trm) is a highly selective allosteric MEK inhibitor (MEKi) that potently and persistently inhibits ERK1/2 activation (1). In 2013, Trm received Food and Drug Administration (FDA) clearance for treatment of BRAF V600E/K-mutant melanoma, becoming the first MEKi approved for any indication. The FDA subsequently approved the MEKi’s binimetinib and cobimetinib for melanoma and added non–small cell lung cancer to the indications for Trm. The clinical indications for Trm are almost certain to expand further, as ClinicalTrials.gov lists more than 100 active clinical trials of Trm in malignancies including colorectal cancer, prostate cancer, and leukemia.

Trm generally is well tolerated but can be associated with potentially serious cardiotoxicity (2–6). A meta-analysis of all published clinical trials found a relative risk increase of 4.9-fold for new cardiomyopathy after MEKi treatment (7), and a postmarketing toxicological study identified incident cardiomyopathy in 11% of Trm-treated patients (6). A more recent prospective study found an even higher incidence of MEKi-induced cardiomyopathy, with 46% of patients developing contractile dysfunction early in their treatment course (8). Although many patients recover normal cardiac function after discontinuation of Trm treatment (9), a pharmacovigilance study found 19% mortality in patients with cardiovascular adverse effects due to MEKi therapy (4). The 2022 European Society of Cardiology Cardio-oncology guidelines contain a two-page section on MEKi cardiovascular toxicity (10), further reinforcing its clinical importance. These adverse effects also occur in association with selumetinib and cobimetinib (9, 11), suggesting that they may represent a class effect of MEKi’s. The cardiotoxicity of combination BRAF-MEK inhibition appears to be driven almost entirely by the MEKi component (3, 4).

The mechanisms underlying Trm-associated cardiotoxicity are unclear. The MEK-ERK axis plays numerous important roles in the heart, collectively promoting cardiomyocyte survival after injury (12). Mice lacking ERK1 and ERK2, the exclusive immediate downstream signaling targets of MEK1, display extensive cardiomyocyte apoptosis and enhanced susceptibility to heart failure after chronic pressure overload (13). Hence, the cardiotoxicity of Trm may arise entirely from on-target inhibition of the cardioprotective MEK-ERK axis. However, we (14) and others (15) have shown that kinase inhibitors also cause adverse cardiovascular responses through unforeseen off-target effects.

We undertook these studies to elucidate the mechanisms through which Trm causes cardiotoxicity in mice. Using in vivo and in vitro approaches, we found that Trm induces reversible cardiac dysfunction characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction that is exacerbated by an unanticipated induction of an innate immune response. We also show that Trm blunts mitochondrial oxidative capacity in multiple cancer cell types. None of these findings would have been predicted by extant knowledge of MEK-ERK functions in the heart or cancer.

Trm cardiotoxicity typically manifests as reversible contractile dysfunction in affected patients with cancer (8, 16, 17). To determine whether rodents provide a suitable model for studying this adverse effect, we gavaged female mice with Trm (3 mg/kg) (1) or vehicle daily for 14 days (Fig. 1A), leading to complete abrogation of ERK1/2 phosphorylation in heart lysates (Fig. 1B). Conscious echocardiography revealed that contractile function (baseline fractional shortening: 54 ± 1%) was reduced after 1 week (48 ± 2%; P < 0.0001 versus baseline) and 2 weeks (47 ± 1%; P < 0.0001 versus baseline) of Trm treatment (Fig. 1C). In a subset of mice, echocardiograms demonstrated that fractional shortening returned to levels statistically indistinguishable from baseline within 1 week of withdrawing Trm treatment (Fig. 1A, “washout”), and this recovery persisted to 2 weeks after withdrawal (Fig. 1C) (P < 0.001 versus day 14).

Compared to vehicle treatment, Trm treatment was associated with 8 ± 2% lower heart weight indexed to body weight (P = 0.0004). Indexed lung, liver, and spleen weights were 26 ± 3% (P < 0.0001), 15 ± 3% (P < 0.0001), and 119 ± 11% (P < 0.0001) higher, respectively, suggesting that Trm induced pulmonary edema and splanchnic venous congestion consistent with biventricular heart failure that resolved with restoration of normal contractile function in the washout period (Fig. 1D).

To investigate the unanticipated Trm-associated decrease in indexed heart weight, we measured cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area in the mouse hearts after 2 weeks of Trm treatment using immunofluorescent staining for the plasma membrane marker wheat germ agglutinin (Fig. 1E). The mean cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area in vehicle-treated mice was 305 ± 3 μM2 (n = 4 mice; 618 myocytes measured). In Trm-treated mice, the mean cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area was 214 ± 4 μM2 (n = 4 mice; 705 myocytes measured; Fig. 1F). Trm exposure did not induce cardiac fibrosis at any time point, as measured by Masson trichrome staining (fig. S1).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to characterize the subcellular architecture of Trm-exposed hearts. Qualitative assessment was most notable for pathological alterations in mitochondrial ultrastructure including linearization of cristae and electron dense inclusion bodies. Autophagosomes were also more prominent in the Trm-exposed hearts than the vehicle-exposed hearts, perhaps indicative of enhanced mitophagy (Fig. 1G). Mitochondrial morphology was quantified by two independent reviewers who were blinded to treatment conditions. No differences in mitochondrial perimeter or area were detected. There was a statistically significant but very small difference in mitochondrial circularity between the Trm and vehicle-treated groups (fig. S2).

Together, these results indicated that Trm induces a reversible cardiomyopathy with biventricular heart failure in mice, consistent with its cardiotoxic effects in some humans. In mice, this cardiomyopathy is characterized by cardiomyocyte atrophy and abnormalities in mitochondrial ultrastructure.

To investigate potential mechanisms underlying these findings, we carried out transcriptomic profiling of vehicle- and Trm-treated mouse hearts (n = 3 and 4 per group) using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq; fig. S3A). We found that Trm induced broad transcriptional changes, with 5269 significantly different transcripts (DESeq2 adjusted P < 0.01) between the two groups (Fig. 2A). Functional annotation [Gene Ontology (GO)] of transcripts >2-fold less abundant in the Trm group than control group revealed a notable enrichment of GO Cellular Component terms related to mitochondria, such as mitochondrial part (4.8-fold enriched; P = 4.3 × 10−67) and mitochondrial respiratory chain (10.6-fold enriched; P = 4.7 × 10−30; Fig. 2B). GO Biological Processes such as oxidative phosphorylation (7.5-fold enriched; P = 6.2 × 10−12) were disproportionately represented in the twofold less abundant transcripts (Fig. 2C). Complementary gene set enrichment analyses [GSEA; Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB)] yielded consistent findings, with marked and highly statistically significant enrichment in hallmark oxidative phosphorylation [normalized enrichment score (NES) = 3.1, false discovery rate (FDR) = 0] and hallmark fatty acid metabolism (NES = 2.1, FDR < 0.0001; Fig. 2D). We next turned our attention to the signal from the transcripts that were twofold more abundant in the mouse heart after 14 days of Trm exposure. Unexpectedly, we found pronounced enrichment of GO biological processes related to an immune response (Fig. 2E) that would not be predicted by our current understanding of MEK-ERK biology in the heart. The most highly statistically significant of these pathways were response to cytokine and response to interferon-β (IFN-β) (fig. S3D), suggesting that a type 1 interferon response might be induced by Trm exposure. GSEA functional annotation revealed highly significant differential expression of transcripts in the hallmark interferon-β response (NES = –3.3, FDR = 0) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) Janus kinase signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling (NES = −2.1, FDR < 0.0001) pathways (Fig. 2F), both of which are classical innate immune response pathways. The NES for the GSEA term MEK_UP.V1_DN (genes up-regulated by MEK activation) was −2.2 (FDR = 0), validating the on-target transcriptomic effects of MEK inhibition by Trm.

As mitochondrial dysfunction and immune activation are central to the pathobiology of heart failure of most etiologies, we sought to determine whether the molecular signature we identified in the failing hearts of Trm-treated mice was unique. We compared RNA-seq datasets [National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) GSE96566] from mice that developed heart failure after myocardial infarction (MI) or transverse aortic constriction (TAC) (18) with our Trm RNA-seq dataset using DESeq2 (19) and found that the Trm transcriptomic signature was distinct from TAC or MI (Fig. 2G). We then applied unsupervised hierarchical clustering to Trm-induced differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that overlapped with TAC or MI DEGs (2850 transcripts; Fig. 2, G and H, and table S1) to assess the degree of similarity in the three datasets. Pairwise comparisons of the three datasets found significant overlap between the TAC and MI datasets (1.64-fold enrichment; P = 2.2 × 10−226), with modest overlap between Trm and TAC (1.06-fold enrichment; P = 4.6 × 10−7) and no demonstrable overlap between Trm and MI (Fig. 2I). We then applied functional annotation (PANTHER v15) to characterize the 2847 DEGs unique to Trm exposure (Fig. 2G). Among the uniquely Trm–down-regulated genes, the most statistically significant GO Cellular Component term was “mitochondrion” (5.0-fold enrichment; FDR 1.1 × 10−62; table S2). “Inflammation mediated by cytokine” was the most statistically significant up-regulated GO Biological Process pathway (3.9-fold enrichment; FDR 2.9 × 10−3) in Trm–up-regulated transcripts. Together, these analyses demonstrate that the Trm transcriptomic profile is distinct from other forms of heart injury, particularly with respect to transcripts related to mitochondria and the immune response.

We then sought to determine whether these marked Trm-induced alterations in the mitochondrial ultrastructure and the transcriptome corresponded to changes in mitochondrial physiology. Here, we treated mice with vehicle or Trm for 3 days (Fig. 3A) rather than 14 days (as in Fig. 1 or 2). We intentionally chose an earlier time point for these experiments with the aim of defining whether alterations in mitochondrial function were a primary cause—rather than merely an epiphenomenon—of cardiomyopathy and heart failure. Conscious echocardiography revealed that contractile function in the Trm-treated animals declined to a similar extent at 3 days (Fig. 3B) as it did at 7 and 14 days (Fig. 1C). Echocardiography in lightly sedated mice demonstrated a decrease in both the E wave/A wave (E/A) ratio and E′ velocity, consistent with diastolic dysfunction (fig. S6). However, in contrast to the 14-day time point (Fig. 1C), we did not observe any statistically significant difference in indexed heart weight or indexed lung weight (Fig. 3C) between vehicle and Trm-treated mice. Collectively, these data suggest that 3 days of Trm treatment is sufficient to induce mild contractile dysfunction but does not cause either cardiac atrophy or heart failure (pulmonary congestion).

Having established day 3 of treatment as a suitable time point for these mitochondrial physiology experiments, we then sought to characterize the effects of Trm on cardiac mitochondrial abundance. We used two broadly accepted orthogonal methods: citrate synthase activity and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) abundance. We found that citrate synthase activity was 16 ± 8% (P = 0.02) lower in isolated cardiac mitochondria from Trm-treated than vehicle-treated mouse hearts (Fig. 3D). Aggregate abundance of three mtDNA genes (D-loop, COX-1, mtND2) relative to nuclear reference DNA was 36 ± 4% lower (P = 0.001) in Trm-treated than in vehicle-treated hearts (Fig. 3E).

We then sought to determine whether Trm exposure affected mitochondrial oxidative metabolism. To evaluate the effect of Trm on cardiac bioenergetics, we applied high-resolution respirometry [Oroboros Oxygraph-2K (O2K) respirometer] to isolated cardiac mitochondria. Using the same isolated mitochondria samples, following tryptic digestion, label-free quantitative nLC-MS/MS was performed. Identified proteins were then searched against the MitoCarta 3.0 database, a comprehensive list of mitochondrial proteins. We then divided the summed abundance of all proteins that mapped to MitoCarta 3.0 by the total protein abundance to generate a mitochondrial enrichment factor (MEF), which essentially reflects the fraction of total protein that consists of validated mitochondrial protein (summarized in Fig. 3F schematic) (20). In subsequent experiments, MEF was used to confirm and standardize mitochondrial content and preparation purity. As seen in Fig. 3F, there was no difference in MEF between groups, indicating that the purity of each mitochondrial prep did not differ across groups. We then used the MEF to standardize our respiration data, which are first normalized to total protein and then to mitochondrial protein only.

Following our published mitochondrial diagnostics protocol (21, 22), mitochondria (3 μg per sample) were first subjected to a freeze fracture to allow direct access to the electron transport system (ETS) and then loaded in the O2K respirometers. The respiratory system was then reconstituted by providing cytochrome c and exogenous nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH). Rotenone [Rot; complex I inhibitor], saturating levels of succinate [complex II substrate], and then antimycin A (Ant A; complex III inhibitor) were added sequentially. Representative traces of the assay demonstrate compromised respiratory capacity in Trm-exposed cardiac mitochondria (Fig. 3G). Maximal NADH-supported respiration reflects complex I–supported flux. Ant-corrected oxygen consumption rates (OCRs) were plotted separately and demonstrated significant decreases in both NADH-supported (complex I) and succinate-supported (complex II) oxidative capacity (Fig. 3H). Data were then normalized to total protein and then corrected for MEF.

To further identify potential nodes of dysregulation that might account for this compromise in energetics, we carried out activity assays for the distal enzyme complexes of the ETS and normalized the results to mitochondrial protein content. Results revealed 26 ± 14% lower complex III activity (P = 0.0149) and 29 ± 11% lower complex IV activity (P = 0.0113) in Trm-exposed heart mitochondria (Fig. 3I). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that Trm compromises cardiac mitochondrial oxidative function in vivo and that this bioenergetic injury occurs at least, in part, due to intrinsic defects in respiratory flux through the ETS.

As the heart is a pluricellular organ, we next undertook in vitro studies in neonatal rat ventricular myocytes (NRVMs) and human-induced pluripotent stem cell–derived cardiomyocytes (h-iPSC–CMs) to define whether the in vivo alterations in cardiac mitochondrial abundance and oxidative function were driven by cardiomyocytes. We found that Trm inhibited NRVM MEK activation at concentrations as low as 10 nM (Fig. 4A). Trm exhibited only modest effects on cell death at nanomolar concentrations after 24 hours of exposure [lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay; Fig. 4B] but did promote proapoptotic processes (annexin V– and propidium iodide (PI)–costained NRVMs; Fig. 4C and fig. S6C) after 48 hours of exposure. We identified a Trm concentration-dependent reduction in citrate synthase activity in NRVMs that was evident within 4 hours and persisted at least through 24 hours of exposure (Fig. 4D), suggesting a diminution in functional mitochondrial abundance that was disproportionate to NRVM attrition. We then used a Seahorse bioanalyzer to determine mitochondrial OCR under basal, adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP)–linked (oligomycin added), and maximal [carbonyl cyanide p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP) added] conditions and found that Trm induced a dose-related decrease in ATP-linked and maximal OCR. Maximal respiration was 14 ± 6% (P < 0.05) lower in the presence of Trm (10 nM) and 30 ± 4% (P < 0.01) lower in the presence of Trm (100 nM) when compared with vehicle-exposed NRVMs (Fig. 4E).

To further characterize the effects of Trm on NRVM metabolism, we assayed fatty acid oxidation (FAO), as fatty acids are the primary substrate for oxidative metabolism in cardiomyocytes and functional annotation of our RNA-seq data indicated down-regulation of transcripts related to FAO (Fig. 2D and fig. S4A). We found that 14C-oleate oxidation was reduced by Trm (10 nM; 23 ± 5%; P < 0.05) and Trm (100 nm; 27 ± 6%; P < 0.01), whereas the positive control, the carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 inhibitor etomoxir, was associated with a 75 ± 2% reduction (Fig. 4F).

To discern whether the suppression of mitochondrial abundance and function might be a class effect of MEKi’s, we exposed NRVMs to selumetinib, finding near-complete inhibition of MEK activity at 100 nM (Fig. 4G). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) revealed a dose-related decrease in mtDNA normalized to nuclear DNA, consistent with depletion of mitochondria (Fig. 4H). We then asked whether compromised mitochondrial function might be a class effect of MEKi’s and whether that effect would apply in human and rodent systems. To that end, we exposed h-iPSC–CMs to the MEKi’s selumetinib and cobimetinib in a Seahorse Mito Stress assay and found that both agents impaired maximal mitochondrial oxidative capacity, similar to Trm (Fig. 4I).

In RAS mutant cancer cell lines, MEK-mediated ERK activation is essential for mitochondrial STAT3 phosphorylation at the serine-727 site (S727), which is thought to be a central signaling event in Ras-Raf-MEK-ERK–mediated oncogenesis (23). Furthermore, it is known that mitochondrial STAT3 potently regulates cellular respiration in the heart through enhancing the activation of both ETS complexes I and II (24–26) and that phosphorylation at S727 is required for mitochondrial STAT3 localization (27). We found that Trm (10 nM) exposure reduced the presence of total STAT3 and almost completely eliminated phospho-STAT3 (pSTAT3)–S727 in NRVM mitochondria (Fig. 4J). Trm exposure in vivo actually increased total cardiac STAT3 and phosphorylation at the proinflammatory tyrosine-705 residue in whole heart lysates (fig. S7A) but almost completely eliminated STAT3 and pSTAT3-S727 in cardiac mitochondria (fig. S7B) at day 3. We then sought to determine whether Trm-mediated abrogation of STAT3-S727 phosphorylation might contribute to the observed respiratory deficits. To that end, we cultured NRVMs with vehicle, Trm (10 nM) or Trm (100 nM) in the presence or absence of a phosphomimetic, mitochondrially targeted STAT3-S727 construct that replaces the serine at residue 727 with an aspartic acid to lock STAT3 in its active state [mitochondrial STAT3 (mtSTAT3)–S727D, courtesy of A. Larner and J. Meier] (24) and found that the mtSTAT3-S727D construct localized to mitochondria (fig. S7C) and rescued the Trm-induced injury to OCR (Fig. 4K).

Together, these findings suggest that the in vivo signal of Trm-induced mitochondrial injury is driven at least, in part, by cardiomyocyte effects and that this injury likely represents a class effect of MEKi’s on both rodent and human cardiomyocytes. Abrogation of mitochondrial STAT3 translocation contributes to these deficits.

These abnormalities in oxidative metabolism prompted us to ask whether Trm affected redox status in the heart. Both immunofluorescence (Fig. 5A) and immunoblotting (Fig. 5B) indicated that Trm exposure increased the abundance of 3-nitrotyrosine, a commonly used marker of in vivo oxidative stress (28).

To ascertain whether the in vitro energetic deficits induced by Trm (Fig. 4) were associated with other evidence of mitochondrial injury such as oxidative stress, we exposed h-iPSC–CMs to vehicle or Trm for 24 hours. Automated quantification of Amplex Red staining for mitochondrial hydrogen peroxide showed a 2.2-fold increase (P = 0.03) in Trm (10 nM) and a 2.7-fold increase in Trm (100 nM; P = 0.003) relative to vehicle (Fig. 5C). Quantification of mitochondrial superoxide demonstrated a fourfold increase in MitoSOX staining induced by both Trm (10 nM) and (100 nM) (P < 0.0001) compared to vehicle (Fig. 5D). We then used 2,7-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCF) and thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reactive substances (TBARS), widely used although nonspecific assays of oxidative stress, for orthogonal substantiation of our findings in NRVMs (fig. S6, A and B). To define whether these effects were unique to Trm or more broadly representative of MEKi’s, we measured superoxide production using a Mitochondrial Superoxide Assay Kit (Abcam, ab219943) on NRVMs exposed to Trm, cobimetinib, or selumetinib. All three MEKi’s induced a roughly 50% increase in superoxide production (Fig. 5E).

To further characterize the effects of these insults to energetics and redox status on mitochondrial fitness, we assayed for mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm). Using the mitochondrial membrane permeant dye 5,5′,6,6′-tetrachloro-1,1′,3,3′-tetraethylbenzimidazolylcarbocyanine iodide (JC-1) that fluoresces red when ΔΨm is intact and green when it is compromised, we found that Trm induced marked alterations in the JC-1 red/green ratio (Fig. 5F) indicative of loss of ΔΨm.

Together, these findings demonstrate that Trm compromises cardiomyocyte energetics and ΔΨm and enhances oxidative stress in vitro and in vivo. These findings further suggest that the adverse effects of Trm on the mouse heart in vivo likely arise from cardiomyocyte mitochondrial injury.

Our RNA-seq data demonstrated both a loss of transcripts related to mitochondria and a gain of transcripts related to immunity (Fig. 2), leading us to hypothesize that Trm induces mitochondrial DAMP (mtDAMP) release to activate an innate immune response. To probe that hypothesis, we first carried out quantitative PCR for mtDNA in the medium of NRVMs exposed to vehicle or Trm (10 nM) for 24 hours. We found that Trm-exposed NRVMs released 2.5-fold more mtDNA into culture medium than vehicle-treated NRVMs (P = 0.007) (Fig. 6A). We then measured mtDNA in the serum of mice exposed to vehicle or Trm (3 mg/kg per day) and found a threefold increase in mtDNA in the serum of Trm-treated mice (P = 0.016) (Fig. 6B).

To extend our rodent model findings and establish potential clinical relevance, we used quantitative PCR to assay circulating mtDNA in serum samples from subjects with triple negative breast cancer enrolled in a window-of-opportunity trial (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT01467310) (29). Blood was drawn at baseline (pretreatment) and after seven consecutive days of Trm administration (posttreatment). Using validated primers for human mitochondrial genes [tRNALeu(UUR),16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA), and ND1] with reference to a nuclear gene (β2-microglobulin) (30, 31), we found that serum mtDNA abundance reliably increased after Trm exposure. When we indexed each individual result to the pre-Trm mean for each gene, we found an aggregate 4.8-fold increase [P = 0.029 by repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA)] in circulating mtDNA (Fig. 6C). Although these findings cannot be directly attributed to cardiac mitochondrial injury as the tissue origin of mtDNA cannot be identified, circulating mtDNA is a known marker of mitochondrial dysfunction in the mouse heart (32) and correlates with incident heart failure in humans (33).

The cGAS-STING pathway is a key transducer of the innate immune response and can be activated by the release of mtDNA. In light of our findings that Trm induces mtDNA release both in vitro and in vivo, we sought to determine whether Trm exposure activated the cGAS-STING pathway in the mouse heart in vivo. We found that Trm (3 mg/kg) for 14 days led to up-regulation of TBK1, cGAS, and pSTING in whole heart lysates compared to vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 6D). Given the broad transcriptomic changes observed in our RNA-seq results, we then asked whether STAT1 (34), STAT3 (35), and nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) (36), classical regulators of the transcriptional response downstream of cGAS-STING, were activated by Trm exposure. Immunoblotting revealed robust up-regulation and activation of all three (Fig. 6E). Activation of NF-κB was also identified in NRVMs treated with selumetinib, suggesting that this finding was a class effect of MEKi’s (fig. S6D). We then selected representative targets of these transcription factors for confirmatory quantitative reverse transcription PCR and found that 14 days of Trm exposure increased IFN-α transcript abundance 3.7-fold, IFN-γ 4.6-fold, STAT1 4.9-fold, SOCS3 4.4-fold, CXCL10 4.8-fold, and IL-17 4.2-fold relative to vehicle exposure. None of these transcripts was significantly increased (one-way ANOVA) after 3 days of Trm (Fig. 6F).

Next we carried out enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) for IL-6, IL-1β, and tumor necrosis factor–α (TNFα), critical effector molecules of the mtDAMP-induced, STING-mediated innate immune response (36). In heart lysates, we found marked up-regulation of IL-6 (Fig. 6G) following the 14-day Trm treatment. In the serum of mice treated with Trm for 14 days, we identified similar increases in IL-1β, IL-6, and TNFα (Fig. 6H). These changes were not evident on day 3 of treatment and had completely resolved after a 14-day washout period in which mice did not receive Trm (as in Fig. 1A).

Immunoblotting revealed no evidence of STING activation after 3 days of Trm exposure (fig. S9A). We then carried out immunohistochemical staining for NLRP3 (representative images in fig. S9B), a downstream target of STING, and used QuPath (37) for automated quantification of “specks” in the left ventricle. We identified low speck levels in vehicle-treated hearts and no significant increase in speck abundance after 3 days of Trm. After 14 days of Trm, however, there was a marked increase in NLRP3 specks of low, medium, and high intensity (thresholds set by QuPath; Fig. 6I). Together, these findings suggest that activation of the innate immune system occurs between days 3 and 14, later than the induction of mitochondrial injury, which occurs between days 1 and 3.

To test whether STING activation is critical for the hypocontractile phenotype of Trm cardiotoxicity, we gavaged mice with vehicle or Trm (3 mg/kg per day), administered intraperitoneal injections of either vehicle or the STING antagonist H-151 (750 nmol/day) (38), and then performed conscious echocardiography on days 7 and 14 of treatment. H-151 alone did not affect contractile function (Fig. 6J). On day 7, there was no difference in fractional shortening between the Trm + H-151 (51.1 ± 0.8%) and Trm + vehicle (49.5 ± 0.4%). On day 14, the fractional shortening in the Trm + H-151 (52.5 ± 0.7%) group was higher than in the Trm + vehicle group (48.0 ± 0.5%; P = 0.0035) although lower than the vehicle-treated control group (57.7 ± 1.5%; P < 0.0001).

Collectively, these data suggest that Trm induces mitochondrial injury that leads to the release of mtDAMPs, including mtDNA, which activates the cGAS-STING pathway and its canonical downstream effectors. STING activation contributes to, but does not entirely account for, Trm-induced cardiac injury.

There has been considerable recent interest in therapeutically modulating cancer cell metabolism (39, 40), but the direct effect of MEK inhibition on cancer cell bioenergetics has not been studied. To evaluate whether MEK inhibition incurs mitochondrial injury in cancer cells as it does in cardiomyocytes, we exposed a variety of cancer cell lines to Trm (100 nM) for 24 hours. In the acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cell line MV4-11, Trm exposure induced cytostatic effects (Fig. 7A), consistent with on-target inhibition of the progrowth MAPK signaling axis. Despite these effects on cell growth, cell viability was unaffected by Trm exposure (Fig. 7B), similar to our findings in cardiomyocytes at 24 hours (Fig. 4B). In intact cells, Trm lowered both basal and maximal respiration after normalization to cell counts (Fig. 7C). To explore the direct impact of Trm on oxidative phosphorylation (OxPhos) kinetics, we permeabilized MV4-11 cells with digitonin, supplied with carbon substrates, and OxPhos was stimulated using our customized creatine kinase (CK) clamp technique to more accurately simulate in vivo physiology (21, 22). Across a range of physiological ATP free energies (−54.16 to −61.49 kJ/mol), Trm lowered OxPhos flux (Fig. 7D). Following inhibition of ATP synthase with oligomycin, FCCP-stimulated respiration was also suppressed in Trm-exposed cells (Fig. 7D), consistent with localization of Trm-mediated OxPhos lesions to the respiratory complexes, as we found in cardiac mitochondria (Fig. 3, G to I).

Using a different AML cell line, HL-60, we observed nearly identical Trm-mediated suppression of both basal and maximal respiration, as well as OxPhos kinetics (Fig. 7, E and F). To determine whether the effects of Trm were consistent in another clinically relevant cancer cell line, we performed 24 hours of Trm exposure experiments in the melanoma cell line A375. For these experiments, we assessed both maximal respiratory capacity and OxPhos kinetics in digitonin-permeabilized cells. Consistent with our results in AML cell lines, basal respiration before digitonin was lower in Trm-exposed cells (Fig. 7, G and H; “basal’). Unlike results in AML cell lines, Trm exposure in A375 cells actually increased maximal respiratory capacity and had no impact on OxPhos kinetics (Fig. 7, G and H). These results were unexpected and suggest that the impact of Trm on mitochondrial bioenergetics is cell-type dependent.

Aside from representing distinct cancers, the AML cell lines differ from the A375 melanoma line insofar, as both MV4-11 and HL-60 cells are BRAF wild type, whereas A375 cells have the BRAF V600E mutation, the oncogenic driver mutation for which Trm is most commonly used clinically. To ascertain whether the effect of Trm on mitochondrial bioenergetics is determined by BRAF V600E, we established isogenic MV4-11 cells by infecting cells with lentiviral vectors expressing a control zsGreen construct or mutated BRAF V600E. The presence of the V600E mutation increased cell proliferation (Fig. 8A), as well as basal respiration (Fig. 8B). Consistent with increased MAPK signaling, phosphorylated ERK (p-ERK) was higher in BRAF mutant MV4-11 cells, but 24 hours of exposure to Trm (100 nM) abrogated ERK activation similarly in both cell lines (Fig. 8, C and D). Trm exposure diminished maximal respiratory capacity and respiratory kinetics to a similar extent in both BRAF wild-type and V600E mutant cells (Fig. 8, E to G) in a dose-related manner (fig. S10, A to C), suggesting that the bioenergetic effects of Trm are cell-type dependent but independent of the BRAF V600E mutation. Together, these findings expand our understanding of the effects of a common oncogenic mutation on oxidative metabolism and provide further evidence that MEK inhibition compromises OxPhos in clinically relevant in vitro systems.

In this study, we demonstrated that the commonly used MEKi Trm induces cardiomyocyte mitochondrial dysfunction and provokes a robust immune signaling response in the heart, substantially expanding our understanding of the mechanisms underlying Trm cardiotoxicity. To our knowledge, ours is the first report that Trm induces mitochondrial injury in the heart and the most extensive characterization of the bioenergetic response to Trm across multiple cancer cell types. Collectively our findings explicate the mechanistic underpinnings of MEKi cardiotoxicity and reveal previously unidentified functional and translationally relevant roles for the MEK-ERK axis in cardiomyocytes and cancer cells.

We chose the dose of Trm (3 mg/kg per day) for our experiments based on the initial publication using in vivo administration of the compound (1). This dose was tolerated well while effectively suppressing xenografted tumor growth over a 14-day time course. In our experience, this dose was associated with minimal attrition (>90% survival) and <5% loss of body weight relative to vehicle-gavaged animals. Our cardiac phenotype very closely resembled the human condition with a relatively mild and completely reversible decrease in contractile function—as evidenced in a recently published study demonstrating that 46% of patients receiving a MEKi-developed cardiomyopathy that was early onset, mostly mild, and largely reversible (8). Hence, we believe that the dose we chose for our experiments is translationally relevant.

The MEK-ERK axis mediates numerous well-documented cardioprotective effects [reviewed in (41) and (42)], participating in adaptive hypertrophy (12), protection against oxidative stress (43), and antiapoptotic defense against cytotoxic insults such as anthracyclines (44). Global ERK2 null mice exhibit embryonic lethality (45). ERK1−/− ERK2+/− mice have normal basal cardiac size and function but are more susceptible to cardiomyocyte apoptosis and develop larger infarcts after coronary artery ligation (46). Pressure loading the hearts of ERK1−/− ERK2+/− mice with TAC leads to accelerated cardiac hypertrophy and more severe heart failure than wild type controls (13), but these defects are rescued by overexpression of MEK1 (13, 46). There are no mitochondrial or bioenergetic phenotypes for any of these MEK-ERK mice and no other published demonstration that the MEK-ERK axis regulates mitochondrial function in the heart.

One previous publication studied Trm cardiotoxicity in a mouse model (47). The authors used transcriptomic, functional, and histopathological analysis of Trm-exposed mouse hearts and complementary studies in human organoids to show that Trm induces contractile dysfunction and cardiomyocyte atrophy, as we show in Fig. 1. Further similarities with our findings include the identification of immune activation in transcriptomic pathway analysis. Here, we expand upon those findings by identifying and extensively characterizing the mitochondrial dysfunction that underlies the cardiotoxic injury, providing a physiologically mechanistic basis for our findings.

The central finding of the current work is the demonstration that MEK inhibition directly compromises cardiomyocyte oxidative metabolism. We found that Trm exposure decreases OCR in ex vivo isolated cardiac mitochondria at least, in part, through compromising ETS complexes I, II, III, and IV activity (Fig. 3, F and G). In vitro studies in NRVMs and h-iPSC–CMs (Fig. 4) indicate that these findings arise from cardiomyocyte mitochondrial injury and are consistent across multiple cardiomyocyte platforms and species, although the variable use of these distinct cell types could be construed as a limitation.

Although the MEK-ERK signaling axis has not been implicated previously in regulation of cardiomyocyte oxidative metabolism, its activation has been reported to regulate mitochondrial dynamics. In particular, multiple studies have found that ERK phosphorylates Drp1 to promote mitochondrial fission in the context of septic cardiomyopathy (48, 49), heart failure (50), and ischemia reperfusion (51). We found no evidence that Trm suppressed Drp1 phosphorylation (fig. S8A), and our TEM data demonstrated no meaningful difference in mitochondrial size or shape after Trm exposure, further arguing against accelerated fission. One study found that Trm actually increased PGC-1α and transcript abundance of its mitochondrial biogenesis targets in the kidney (52), but we found no difference in PGC-1α in Trm-treated compared to vehicle-treated hearts (fig. S8B). This apparent discrepancy is expected given that mitochondrial physiology differs substantially across somatic cells (20) and cancer cell types (53). We did identify an increase in the abundance of canonical markers of mitophagy using immunoblotting for Pink1, Parkin, and LC3BII (fig. S8A) and quantitative reverse transcription (qRT)–PCR (fig. S8, C and D). We attribute the apparent up-regulation of the mitophagy machinery to the clearance of mitochondria damaged by Trm consistent with the accumulation of autophagosomes in the TEM images of Trm-exposed hearts (Fig. 1G).

Our findings suggest that Trm may compromise mitochondrial function through multiple mechanisms rather than one discrete pathway, likely a result of the fact that ERK1/2 has over 100 known downstream targets (54). Here, we found that MEK inhibition abrogated the phosphorylation of STAT3 at S727 and prevented the translocation of STAT3 to mitochondria in cardiomyocytes. We also showed that Trm abrogated phosphorylation of STAT3-S727 and reduced respiratory capacity in multiple AML cell lines (fig. S10, D to G), suggesting that this putative mechanism may apply across multiple tissue types. Introduction of a phosphomimetic STAT3-S727 construct rescued the cardiomyocyte mitochondrial injury, the first demonstration that this MEK-ERK-STAT3 pathway regulates oxidative metabolism in the heart. We chose to pursue this potential mechanism of injury given that (i) in RAS mutant cancer cell lines, MEK-mediated ERK activation is essential for mitochondrial STAT3 phosphorylation, which is a critical element in Ras-Raf-MEK-ERK–mediated oncogenesis (23), and (ii) mitochondrial STAT3-S727 potently regulates cellular respiration in the heart through enhancing the activation of both ETS complexes I and II (24). In association with cyclophilin D, mitochondrial STAT3 also was shown to reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in mouse embryonic fibroblasts after oxidative injury (55). Here, we show that this cytoprotective property of mitochondrial STAT3 also is functional in cardiomyocytes exposed to Trm.

The effects of MEK inhibition on mitochondrial physiology have been studied to a somewhat greater extent in the context of cancer biology, particularly in light of the fact that there is intense interest in developing cancer therapies that intentionally target cellular metabolism, including OxPhos (56–60). One study found that structurally distinct MEKi tool compounds (U0126, MIIC, and PD98059) impair ETS complex I activity in HL-60 cells (61). Here, we demonstrated that the first-in-class MEKi therapeutic Trm compromises oxidative metabolism in some, but not all, cancer cell types and that this effect is not dependent on the BRAF V600E oncogenic driver mutation. To our knowledge, ours is the first study to characterize the effects of Trm on OxPhos in cancer cell lines with either wild-type or mutant BRAF although BRAF inhibitors, which are very commonly used in combination with MEKi’s, are known to augment OxPhos in BRAF V600E-mutated melanoma (62, 63). Enhanced OxPhos also represents one mechanism through which BRAF-V600E mutated melanoma can achieve resistance to MEK inhibition (64). Collectively, these published data, coupled with our findings, suggest that inhibition of OxPhos may contribute to the therapeutic efficacy of Trm in multiple types of cancer.

Differentiation of h-iPSCs into cardiomyocytes was performed following a small-molecule–based protocol adapted from Burridge et al. (69). Briefly, h-iPSCs were seeded on Matrigel-coated plates and cultured until colonies reached ~80% of confluency. On day 0, differentiation was initiated by replacing the medium with RPMI 1640 containing 2% B27 supplement minus insulin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A1895601, USA), activin A (60 ng/μl; PeproTech, 120-14P-50UG, USA), 12 μM CHIR-99021 (STEMCELL Technologies,100-1042, Canada), and l-ascorbic acid (50 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich, A5960, USA). On days 1 and 2, cells were fed daily with fresh RPMI 1640 supplemented with 2% B27 supplement minus insulin, 5 μM IWR-1 (STEMCELL Technologies, 72562, Canada), and l-ascorbic acid (50 μg/ml). On day 4, the medium was replaced with RPMI 1640 containing 2% B27 supplement minus insulin and 5 μM IWR-1. From day 6 onward, cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 with 2% B27 supplement with insulin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 17504044, USA), refreshed every other day. Spontaneous contractile activity was typically observed between days 7 and 9 of differentiation.

Abundance of selected cytokines was measured in mouse serum according to the manufacturer’s protocol using a Luminex-based MILLIPLEX MAP Cytokine panel (EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA) in the UNC Center for Gastrointestinal Biology and Disease Advanced Analytics Core (70, 71).

Female Sprague-Dawley rats were from Charles River. NRVMs were isolated as previously described (72). Briefly, hearts from 1- to 2-day-old rat pups were minced, digested serially in collagenase (Worthington)–containing solution, filtered, and then preplated to exclude nonmyocytes. NRVMs were then plated on laminin-coated dishes in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with 5% fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich, F2442) for 24 hours. Experiments were carried out after 36 to 72 hours of serum starvation in the presence of 5-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine. LDH assays (Sigma-Aldrich, MAK066-1KT) were carried out in 1% fetal bovine serum (FBS).

NRVMs were seeded onto 96-well plates and exposed to vehicle or Trm, and then 50 μl of culture medium from each well was collected and transferred to another 96-well culture plate. A total of 50 μl of LDH reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C20300) was then added to each well, and the solution was incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After the reaction was complete, absorbance at 490 nm was measured by an ELISA reader.

After 24 hours of serum starvation, NRVMs were exposed to vehicle or Trm for 48 hours. Cells were washed with cold PBS two times and then incubated with 200 μl of binding buffer [10 mM Hepes, 140 mM NaCl, and 2.5 mM CaCl2 (pH 7.4)] including 10 μl of fluorescein isothiocyanate–labeled annexin V, 2 μl of PI, and Hoechst 33342 (1 μg/ml; Molecular Probes, USA) for 15 min at room temperature. Cells were then examined under an epifluorescence microscope (Olympus IX81 Inverted Light Microscope, UNC Microscopy Core) by an observer blinded to experimental condition. Images were analyzed using ImageJ. The experiment was carried out three independent times in duplicates of each treatment condition.

The extracellular OCR was determined using Seahorse XF24 extracellular flux analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience) in the UNC Lineberger Cancer Center or the UNC GEMM core. NRVMs or iPSC-CMs were seeded into an XF24 cell culture microplate 24 hours before the assay. OCR was measured in extracellular flux analyzer XFe96 (Agilent) with pyruvate (Sigma-Aldrich, #P2256) as substrate following the manufacturer’s protocol. A total of 1.5 μM oligomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, #495455), 0.5 μM FCCP (Sigma-Aldrich, #C2920), 5 μM Rot (Sigma-Aldrich, #45656), and 5 μM Ant A (Sigma-Aldrich, #A8674) was used for the assay protocol (73). Quantification of basal and maximal OCR was performed according to the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit User Guide. OCR was normalized to DNA content in each well measured by CyQUANT Cell Proliferation Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C7026) (74). For each experiment, equal cell numbers suspended in 1 ml of respiration buffer were pipetted into the calibrated oxygen electrode chamber, and the temperature was maintained at 37°C (75). Experimental conditions were assayed in duplicate, and the experiment was repeated two independent times.

FAO assays used 500 μM sodium oleate (Sigma-Aldrich, O-7501) with BSA 0.5% (Sigma-Aldrich, 05470) and carnitine. 14C-oleate (Perkin-Elmer, NEC317050UC) was added to the medium and incubated for 2 hours, and then media were transferred to a 48-well CO2-trapping plate with 200 μl of NaOH (1 N). A total of 70% perchloric acid was added to each well, and CO2 was trapped while agitating on an orbital shaker. One aliquot of medium was transferred to a scintillation vial for labeled CO2 counting. Cells were scraped on ice and incubated overnight, and then acid soluble metabolites (ASMs) were counted in the cell supernatant. Total cellular protein was quantitated using the Bradford reagent. Total oxidation = (dpm from CO2 trap – blank) × (200/150) × specific activity + (dpm from ASM – blank) × (550/300) × specific activity (14).

Mitochondrial membrane potential in NRVMs was determined by JC-1 (Abcam, Cambridge, MA) reduction according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, serum-starved NRVMs were incubated with either vehicle control or Trm for 48 hours. JC-1 (2 μM) was added to each well for 30 min. Cells were washed once with medium and then analyzed by plate reader (CLARIOstar, BMG LABTECH, Germany). JC-1 green fluorescence was excited at 488 nm, and emission was detected using a 530 ± 40 nm filter. JC-1 red fluorescence was excited at 488 nm, and emission was detected using a 613 ± 20 nm filter.

iPSC-CMs were treated with vehicle, 10 nM Trm, or 100 nM Trm for 24 hours or MitoPQ (Abcam, ab146819), as a positive control, for 18 hours. Cells were washed with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS), and 500 nM MitoSOX Red reagent (Invitrogen, M36008) in Hanks’ balanced salt solution was added and incubated for 30 min. Cells were then imaged with the Texas Red channel on an EVOS M700 microscope. Three to six images were taken from each well. For analysis, total area of positive MitoSOX staining was calculated for each image. The percentage from each image was averaged for each well with four to six wells used for each treatment condition. Each group was normalized to vehicle alone to obtain final values. Analysis was performed using QuPath.

iPSC-CMs were treated with vehicle, 10 nM Trm, or 100 nM Trm for 24 hours. The Amplex Red Hydrogen Peroxide/Peroxidase Assay Kit (Invitrogen, A22188) was used to measure hydrogen peroxide production. A total of 20 mM H2O2 was used as a positive control. Cells were incubated in Amplex Red reagent for 30 min, and absorbance was read at 560 nm. All groups were normalized to vehicle.

For detection of ROS formation, NRVMs were exposed to vehicle or Trm for 24 hours and then incubated with 5 mM H2DCFDA (Abcam) for 30 min or 75 μl of TBA reagent (R&D/Bo-Techne, Minneapolis, MN) for 2 hours. Fluorescent intensity was measured at 525 or 532 nm (TBARS) with a microplate reader at room temperature (DCF). Superoxide: ROS production was measured at 2-MIN intervals over a 60-min time period using the Mitochondrial Superoxide Assay Kit (Abcam, ab219943) with MitoROS 580 dye using a plate reader (BMG Labtech, CLARIOstar). Fluorescence (Excitation/Emmission 540/590) was measured (60 min at 37°C), and ROS production was calculated. Plating density was optimized to produce a linear result, and color change was visually confirmed.

High-resolution O2 consumption measurements were conducted using the Oroboros O2K (Oroboros Instruments, Innsbruck, Austria) in intact and digitonin-permeabilized cells. For each intact cell experiment, cells were centrifuged at ×300g for 7 min at room temperature, washed in PBS, centrifuged once more, and then suspended in assay medium at a cell concentration of ~1 × 106 viable cells/ml. Assay medium was RPMI 1640, IMDM, or DMEM, without bicarbonate, supplemented with 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/ml), and streptomycin (100 μg/ml). All experiments were carried out at 37°C in a 1-ml reaction volume. For permeabilized cell experiments, cells were centrifuged at ×300g for 7 min at room temperature, washed in respiration buffer, centrifuged once more, and then suspended in respiration buffer at a cell concentration of ~1 × 106 viable cells/ml. Respiration buffer consisted of potassium-MES (105 mM, pH 7.2), KCl (30 mM), KH2PO4 (10 mM), MgCl2 (5 mM), EGTA (1 mM), and BSA (2.5 g/liter). After recording basal respiration, cells were permeabilized with digitonin (20 μg/ml), energized with various carbon substrates (pyruvate, malate, glutamate, succinate, octanoyl-l-carnitine; P, M, G, S, and O; 5, 2, 5, 5, and 0.2 mM), and flux was stimulated across a physiological ATP free energy demand using the CK clamp as previously described (20). For all experiments, nonmitochondrial respiration was controlled for by adding Ant A (0.5 μM). Data were normalized to viable cell count and expressed as pmol/s/million cells.
