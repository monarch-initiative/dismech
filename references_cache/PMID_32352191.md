Tonsillar metastasis from distant primary tumours has not been described in 
dogs, and reports are rare in humans. The aims of this study were to determine 
whether tumour metastasis occurs to the canine palatine tonsils and evaluate 
whether afferent lymphatics drain to the palatine tonsils via skull indirect 
computed tomography lymphangiography (ICTL). Retrospective review of the 
diagnostic laboratory tonsillar histopathology submissions was performed. ICTL 
studies (n = 53) were retrospectively reviewed by a single radiologist. Of 882 
total tonsil histopathology samples, 492 (56%) were considered neoplastic, with 
8% of them benign. Of the primary malignant neoplasms of the tonsil, squamous 
cell carcinoma (55%), lymphoma (17%) and melanoma (12%) were most common. 
Tonsillar metastasis was confirmed in 41 cases; histopathologic evaluation 
revealed melanoma (25), carcinoma (10), hemangiosarcoma (2) and one each of 
fibrosarcoma, malignant histiocytosis, basal cell tumour and undifferentiated 
sarcoma. A total of 53 tonsillar melanoma cases were identified, of which 25 
represented metastasis from a known distant primary tumour and 28 were solely 
tonsillar, with 9 of these cases having concurrent lymph node metastasis. No 
ICTL cases had lymphatic contrast drainage to the palatine tonsil. The palatine 
tonsil is an important site of metastasis for a variety of primary tumour types 
from many locations. ICTL did not reveal lymphatics draining to the palatine 
tonsil, which is highly supportive of hematogenous route of metastasis to the 
tonsil. Tonsillar metastasis may be more common than previously thought. It is 
recommended to perform a thorough oral examination, including the tonsils, for 
all oral tumours and melanoma cases.

Primary tonsillar neoplasia is rare, with a reported incidence of 8–25% of all oropharyngeal tumors in dogs.1–3 The most common malignant tumor reported is squamous cell carcinoma, with rare reports of melanoma and lymphoma.2–5 Lymphoma is reported to classically consist of bilateral tonsil involvement along with diffuse lymphadenopathy.1 Individual tonsillar neoplasia case reports in dogs include benign polyps, cysts, a plasmacytoma, and a mast cell tumor (MCT).6–10 Only melanoma has been mentioned to metastasize to the tonsils in dogs, without citation to literature.11 Specific to human tonsillar melanomas, the presence or absence of junctional activity in the adjacent mucosa on histopathology can differentiate between primary versus metastatic melanoma, respectively.12

Unless incidentally found, tonsillar masses are typically large, readily causing clinical signs, including: dysphagia, coughing, ptyalism and dyspnea. This can be accompanied by palpable cervical enlargement, blood-tinged saliva, and anorexia. Given that many tonsillar neoplasms are found late in the course of disease, metastasis at diagnosis is extremely common. Lymph node metastasis, including ipsilateral and contralateral mandibular and medial retropharyngeal lymph nodes, have been reported in up to 97% of cases.1 Other metastatic sites reported include the thyroid gland, lungs, liver, spleen, kidney, pericardium and heart.1 Therefore, at evaluation for tonsillar neoplasia, it is recommended to perform staging tests, including thoracic imaging (radiographs or computed tomography[CT] scan), abdominal imaging (ultrasound or CT scan), and evaluation of local lymph nodes (ultrasound or CT scan +/− indirect lymphangiography of the head and neck lymph nodes with lymph node cytology and/or biopsy).13

Tonsils are part of the hemolymphatic system, consisting of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue with primary and secondary lymphoid follicles surrounded by a common capsule. Tonsil anatomy varies by the individual species, with palatine, lingual, and pharyngeal tonsils commonly present in dogs. The palatine tonsil is the only tonsil readily visible on examination, located in the tonsillar fossa within the lateral oropharynx. The lingual and pharyngeal tonsils are more diffuse.14,15,16 In humans and dogs, it has been noted that the palatine tonsil does not receive afferent lymphatics, but does contain multiple efferent lymphatics to the medial retropharyngeal lymph nodes.16,17 No data can be found to support the statement that the palatine tonsil does not receive afferent lymphatics in dogs. Despite increasing use of skull indirect computed tomography lymphangiography (ICTL) for sentinel lymph node mapping for oropharyngeal tumors, contrast drainage to the tonsils has not been previously documented but, at the same token, this has not been a primary goal of these studies.18–20 The route of metastasis to the palatine tonsil is not well-documented or understood. Given the traditional route of metastasis to a lymph node is from its afferent lymph vessels, specifically evaluating the presence of afferent vessels to the palatine tonsil is warranted.

The aims of this study were to report the histologic types of neoplasia of the canine palatine tonsil and their incidence from a single large database and to attempt to determine whether tumor metastasis occurs to the palatine tonsils in dogs. Secondary aims were to evaluate whether afferent lymphatics drain to the palatine tonsils via skull ICTL and determine if the presence or absence of junctional activity in the adjacent mucosa on histopathology can differentiate between primary versus metastatic melanoma in dogs.

The diagnostic laboratory records, including external and internal submissions, were searched for all cases with canine tonsillar histopathology from January 2005 through May 2018. Cases of normal tonsillar histopathology were excluded from further analysis. Additional exclusion criteria consisted of benign changes, including tonsillar lymphoid hyperplasia, tonsillitis, fibrosis, and necrosis. For comprehensive analysis of tonsillar neoplasia, all remaining cases were included. Primary tonsillar squamous cell carcinoma and tonsillar lymphoma were excluded from further analysis of malignant tonsillar neoplasia, as these tumor types have been well-established as canine primary tonsillar neoplasms. Presumed tonsillar metastasis was defined as those cases with the same histopathologic diagnosis confirmed within the tonsil and an additional primary tumor, simultaneously or historically, within the same patient. Tonsillar melanoma cases were additionally reviewed for histopathologic changes that could potentially differentiate primary versus metastatic melanoma (see histopathology below).

Medical records were searched to include all skull ICTL performed through August 2018, the first of which was performed November 2012. Cases were excluded if cytology or histopathology of the primary tumor was unavailable.

Routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained slides from prior tonsil melanoma histopathology cases were re-evaluated by a single board-certified pathologist (DR) when available. The pathologist was blinded to the clinical history. Histological evaluation included confirmation of the original melanoma histopathology diagnosis and assessment of junctional activity of the overlying and adjacent mucosa of the tonsil, as previously detailed in human melanoma cases.12 Specifically, junctional activity in this study was defined as the primary presence of single cells to intra-epithelial nests of proliferating neoplastic melanocytes within the basal layer of the squamous mucosa adjacent to the underlying submucosa/tonsillar neoplastic mass, regardless of the presence or absence of intra-epithelial cells in the mucosa immediately overlying the neoplastic mass. Those cases in which the majority of the tonsillar mucosa was significantly eroded or ulcerated were excluded from analysis.

The skull ICTL protocol varied slightly with the individual radiologist’s preference. The patients were positioned in sternal recumbency with the forelimbs extended cranially and the maxilla in a bite-block with the head supported by a towel. All CT scans were extended through the entire cranial cervical region to the level of the thoracic inlet. ICTL consisted of approximately 0.25 mL Iohexol (350 mg/ml) injected in each quadrant surrounding the primary tumor, followed by CT scans most commonly at 1, 3, 5, 9, 12, and 15 minutes. Scanning was typically discontinued if contrast reached a lymph node on the 3 minute or later scan, or at 15 minutes, if no contrast enhancement was present within the lymphatics on previous images. All ICTL studies were retrospectively reviewed by a single board-certified radiologist (EKR) for lymphatic contrast drainage, sentinel lymph node contrast drainage and change in tonsillar appearance, including size and contrast enhancement. Lymphatic contrast drainage was defined as contrast from the ICTL within lymphatic vessels extending from the primary tumor. Lymph node and tonsil contrast drainage was defined as contrast from the ICTL present within the lymph node or tonsil itself.

Data were assessed using descriptive statistics. Fisher’s exact test was used to determine if an association was present between the presence and absence of junctional activity and the melanoma being primary versus metastatic.

No validation testing was performed as no cell lines were utilized for this research.

The diagnostic laboratory database search resulted in 1008 total canine tonsillar histopathology cases. After exclusion of normal tonsillar histopathology, 882 total tonsillar histopathology cases remained, with 390 (44% of the total) considered benign changes, including lymphoid hyperplasia (92% of all benign changes), tonsillitis (5%), fibrosis (1.8%), and necrosis (0.5%). Following exclusion of these benign changes, 492 tonsillar neoplasia histopathology cases remained, including 39 (8%) benign neoplasia and 453 (92%) malignant neoplasia (Tables 1 and 2).

Excluding primary tonsillar squamous cell carcinoma and lymphoma from the malignant cases, 123 cases (25% of all malignant tonsillar histopathology cases) met the inclusion criteria for other types of tonsillar malignant neoplasia (Table 2). Histopathology of the tonsil matched that of a previously or concurrently diagnosed primary tumor in 41 cases (33% of those that met the inclusion criteria for malignant neoplasia other than squamous cell carcinoma and lymphoma; 9% of all malignant tonsillar histopathology cases). Of the cases with confirmed tonsillar metastasis, histopathology revealed melanoma (25), various carcinomas (10), hemangiosarcoma (2) and one each of fibrosarcoma, malignant histiocytosis, basal cell tumor, and undifferentiated sarcoma. Primary tumors arose from various locations within the body, including outside of the head and neck region (Table 3). A total of 53 tonsillar melanoma cases were identified, with 25 presumed metastasic from a known primary tumor located elsewhere (5 mandibular, 2 maxillary, 1 palatal, 5 lip, 3 tongue, 1 shoulder, and 8 unspecified oral gingival locations). Twenty-eight melanoma cases were noted solely within the tonsil with no known additional tumor location documented. Nine of these suspected primary tonsillar melanoma cases additionally had confirmed lymph node involvement on histopathology.

Of 53 total identified tonsillar melanoma cases, 38 cases (72%) had formalinfixed, paraffin embedded tissue blocks or H&E stained slides available for retrospective review. Of these cases, 19 (50%) demonstrated definitive junctional activity (Figure 1A), 10 (26%) did not have junctional changes (Figure 1B), and 9 (24%) did not have adequate mucosal architecture to be evaluated accurately. Of the 38 melanoma cases reviewed, 18 were considered primary and 11 were considered metastatic. The relationship between primary versus metastatic and presence or absence of junctional activity is reported in table 4. There was no association between the presence and absence of junctional activity and whether the melanoma was primary versus metastatic (p=0.43).

Review of hospital medical records revealed 53 skull ICTL studies. None of the studies had a known tonsillar tumor. The primary tumor identified was variable and included: melanoma (18), squamous cell carcinoma (9), MCT (7), osteosarcoma (6), undifferentiated sarcoma (3), fibrosarcoma (3), acanthomatous ameloblastoma (2), and one each of peripheral odontogenic fibroma (POF), plasma cell tumor, salivary adenocarcinoma, myxosarcoma, and a granulomatous lesion. The location of the primary tumor within the head and neck was highly variable: mandibular (22), maxillary (14), lip (8), nasal planum (3), tongue (2), and one each of ear, muzzle, cervical region, and palate.

One case with primary right mandibular squamous cell carcinoma had a single enlarged, contrast-enhancing tonsil present. The left mandibular lymph node was confirmed metastatic on histopathology in this case, while histopathology was not performed for the tonsil. No tonsils contained contrast drainage on ICTL; however, there was a lymphatic vessel that coursed around the tonsil in 4 cases (Figure 2). There were 7 ICTL studies (13%) with no contrast drainage within the lymphatics and 19 ICTL studies (36%) that did not have contrast within a sentinel lymph node itself. Of 34 cases with sentinel lymph node contrast drainage, 25 (74%) were to an ipsilateral lymph node, with the majority being solely to the ipsilateral mandibular lymph node (23 cases). In two cases, the sentinel lymph node identified was the ipsilateral superficial cervical lymph node and in one of these, the sentinel lymph node was considered ipsilateral for a primary midline tongue tumor. Of the bilateral sentinel lymph node contrast drainage cases, the majority involved bilateral mandibular lymph nodes (6), while two cases involved a combination of ipsilateral and contralateral mandibular and medial retropharyngeal lymph nodes identified with contrast drainage (Table 5).

The results of our study are consistent with prior reports that tonsillar squamous cell carcinoma is the most common primary tonsillar tumor in dogs in our hospital population.3 Following squamous cell carcinoma and lymphoma, tonsillar melanoma was the next most common neoplasm identified within the tonsil. This is consistent with a recent tonsil CT imaging study, that found the majority of tonsil tumors in dogs to be carcinoma, followed by 2 cases of melanoma, and a single lymphoma case.4 In our study, melanoma was found to be much more common than previously noted, at 12% of all malignant tonsillar neoplasms, and the most common malignant neoplasm after squamous cell carcinoma and lymphoma (55% and 17%, respectively). Carcinoma variants and undifferentiated and anaplastic sarcomas were the next most common. It is possible that some of these other tumor types observed in our study were mis-diagnosed and could represent amelanotic melanomas, or in cases diagnosed as carcinoma variants, actually represent poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinomas lacking features of squamous differentiation such as keratinization and intercellular bridges. Regarding amelanotic melanomas, these can be easily distinguished by immunohistochemical labeling for a panel of markers including PNL-2, Melan A, and tyrosinase.21 In cases of poorly differentiated carcinomas which lack morphologic features of squamous or glandular differentiation, this distinction between squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma can be difficult. Molecular methods to aid in this diagnosis specifically within the tonsil have not been investigated in veterinary species; however, similar to other anatomical locations such as the mammary gland, it is possible that positive labeling for cytokeratin19 in glandular carcinomas of the tonsil may differentiate them from squamous cell carcinoma.22 Salivary carcinomas and oral squamous cell carcinomas with suspected metastases to the tonsil were included when histopathology was consistent with tonsillar tissue. Cases with invasion and/or extension into the tonsil may not be able to be differentiated from separate lesions in the mucosa or glandular tissue. One internal case of salivary carcinoma tonsillar metastasis was later confirmed, with history of a prior cervical carcinoma suspected metastatic from a previous salivary carcinoma. Plasma cell tumors were also noted to be a common tumor type within the tonsil (15% when squamous cell carcinoma and lymphoma were excluded), which has not been previously documented, aside from a single report of a benign plasmacytoma.9

In humans, 0.8% of tonsillar neoplasia is considered metastatic.23 Human tonsillar metastasis most commonly occurs secondary to pulmonary neoplasia, gastrointestinal neoplasia, renal neoplasia, melanoma, breast cancer, and rarely colorectal cancer.24–30 In our study, metastasis to the tonsils from distant primary tumors was also uncommon in dogs, with only 9% of tonsillar neoplasia in this study being presumed metastatic. This was most common with melanoma, followed by carcinomas, hemangiosarcoma, and a variety of other tumor types. The metastatic carcinoma cases were primarily metastatic from the head and neck region, with a single case originating from a mediastinal mass. Of the two tonsillar hemangiosarcoma cases, one had a primary hepatic tumor and the other a body wall tumor. Approximately half of the tonsillar melanoma cases had a confirmed primary tumor located elsewhere, majority of which originated from within the oral cavity. A single case had a concurrent shoulder cutaneous melanoma. While possible, it is considered unlikely that all of these would be multiple de novo tumors within the same patient.

Of the remaining tonsillar melanoma cases, 21 cases (40%) solely involved the tonsil, suggestive of primary tonsillar melanoma. Histopathology consistent with concurrent lymph node involvement was found in 13% of tonsillar melanoma cases. This may be underreported given inability to confirm whether the lymph nodes were initially evaluated in all cases. Oral melanoma is considered a common diagnosis in dogs, and early melanoma lesions can be difficult to detect due to normal oral pigmentation in this species, thus it is challenging to differentiate a primary versus metastatic tumor. There have been less than 30 documented cases of cutaneous melanoma metastasizing to the tonsil in people, majority of which occur late in the course of disease and are associated with a guarded prognosis.28 In humans, immunohistochemistry stains have been used to confirm whether a melanoma diagnosis is consistent with primary or metastatic disease, especially coupled with the lag time to evidence of metastasis and patient presentation.31 Specific to human patients with cutaneous melanoma with oropharyngeal metastasis, histopathologic features were able to be utilized to aid in differentiation of primary and metastatic melanoma using junctional changes.12 In those cases, metastatic melanoma lesions were consistently covered by an intact layer of mucosa in surrounding and/or overlying mucosa and were void of junctional changes. Thus, tonsil melanoma cases in our population were re-evaluated by a single board-certified pathologist (DR). Based on this evaluation, the presence or absence of junctional changes was not reliable in predicting primary versus metastatic tonsillar melanoma in dogs (Table 4). While it is very possible that this lack of correlation is due to species-specific differences between canine and human melanoma, other possible explanations for the lack of correlation in our study could be because our classification of primary versus metastatic was incorrect or the tissue samples available for histopathology review were incomplete. Another limitation that could potentially account for the lack of correlation is that in certain cases of melanoma, junctional activity can be difficult to discern on routine H&E stain, potentially reducing our sensitivity and specificity in accurately classifying our case set. Ultimately, given an overall small sample size, including few cases with a known previous or concurrent melanoma available for review, definitive conclusions cannot be made at this time regarding the ability to differentiate between primary versus metastatic melanoma of the palatine tonsil in dogs based on the presence or absence of junctional activity in the adjacent mucosa on histopathology. These histopathologic features should be further evaluated prospectively for canine melanoma in the future, as it may offer clarification of primary versus metastatic lesions, which could assist with patient clinical staging.

Review of skull ICTL cases for a variety of head and neck tumors through August 2018 revealed no evidence of afferent lymphatic drainage to the palatine tonsil, while having an overall success rate of identifying afferent lymphatic drainage in 87% and a specific sentinel lymph node(s) in 64%. An afferent lymphatic vessel with drainage that coursed around the tonsil was inconsistently present; however, the tonsil itself never contained contrast drainage from ICTL. The absence of afferent lymphatic vessels to the palatine tonsil in dogs is consistent with the literature.16 These findings further suggest that metastasis to the palatine tonsil likely occurs via hematogenous spread. Prior studies with skull ICTL for sentinel lymph node mapping for veterinary oropharyngeal tumors have not previously documented contrast drainage of the tonsils; however, it is unclear if the tonsils were specifically evaluated in past studies.18–20

Four main theories have been proposed in human medicine for tonsillar metastasis. Traditional hematogenous spread via systemic venous drainage from the lungs, liver, or portal circulation remains the main theory behind tonsillar metastasis.24,32 Secondly, hematogenous spread has been proposed via retrograde flow from increased intrathoracic or intraabdominal pressure, through the Batson paravertebral plexus, bypassing the lungs.33 In our study, 5 primary tumors that metastasized to the tonsil were not located on the head or neck (12%, Table 3). The most likely route of metastasis for these tumors is arguably hematogenous spread, particularly with respect to hemangiosarcoma. Another theory describes retrograde lymphatic spread from the thoracic duct and/or right cervical duct—the conduit of lymph flow from the right side of the head, via cervical lymph node metastasis;12,24 however, there are documented cases without the presence of simultaneous lymph node metastasis. Iatrogenic metastasis (or seeding) can occur from direct inoculation or spread from instrumentation during a procedure, such as endoscopy or bronchoscopy or, in the case of gastrointestinal neoplasia, from regurgitation or vomiting of gastric contents containing neoplastic cells.24,34,35 Lymphatic hypertrophy and lymphangiogenesis have been shown to occur with tonsillar inflammation.17 We propose the possibility of an additional theory for tonsillar metastasis from the development of aberrant lymphatic afferents to the tonsil in the case of inflammation secondary to neoplasia or infection. Twenty-two primary tumors that metastasized to the tonsil were located in the intraoral or pharyngeal cavity (54%, Table 3). The majority of these (19) were melanomas, a tumor known to readily metastasize regionally via the lymphatic route. Unfortunately, none of the ICTL cases in our study were considered primary or metastatic tonsillar neoplasia, and only a single case had an enlarged tonsil. The ICTL cases were delineating one or more lymphatic pathways leading to a sentinel lymph node(s) having predominant tumor drainage and were not delineating regional lymphatic anatomy of those dogs. Additional prospective ICTL studies with tonsillar metastatic neoplasia are indicated to further evaluate the theory that aberrant lymphatic afferents to the tonsil can develop.

The limitations of this study include its retrospective nature, including external submissions, that prohibited the ability to assess patient treatments and outcomes. Given the inclusion of outside histopathology admissions to the diagnostic laboratory, complete assessment of medical records and evidence of lymph node metastasis was not feasible. Some metastatic cases may have been included as primary tonsillar neoplasia cases based upon incomplete historical information and unknown staging by the submitting veterinarian. Lymph node metastasis is likely underrepresented. None of the cases with histopathologic diagnoses of tonsillar metastases had concurrent ICTL performed in this study. Given the small case numbers for tonsillar melanoma, conclusions could not be drawn for histopathologic differences between primary and metastatic cases.

Differential diagnoses for tonsillar neoplasia should include malignant melanoma, plasma cell tumor, and metastatic neoplasia, in addition to squamous cell carcinoma and lymphoma that are commonly considered. This paper supports that tonsillar metastasis likely occurs via a hematogenous route from a variety of primary tumor types, originating from various anatomic locations, rarely including tumors located outside of the head and neck region. Therefore, a thorough oral examination, especially for melanoma patients, should include assessment of the tonsillar crypts when patients are being evaluated for metastasis. Early detection of tonsillar neoplasia, including metastatic lesions, may afford surgical excision as an option to eliminate gross disease, especially prudent for melanoma. Additional prospective ICTL studies should be performed for tonsillar neoplasia specifically. Additional prospective melanoma histopathology studies should be performed in dogs to further evaluate the ability to differentiate primary and metastatic lesions.
