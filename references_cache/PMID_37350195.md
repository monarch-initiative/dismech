MYC amplifications have been frequently detected in radiation (RT)-associated 
angiosarcomas (ASs) by low-resolution molecular methods. However, large-scale 
next-generation sequencing (NGS) studies to investigate the genomic landscape of 
RT-AS are scarce, particularly compared with other RT-associated sarcomas. We 
performed a detailed comparative genomic investigation of RT-AS versus other 
RT-associated histotypes, as well as sporadic sarcomas with similar histologies. 
Our institutional targeted DNA-NGS assay database was searched for RT-associated 
sarcomas. Clinical outcome data, pathologic diagnosis, and the types and 
frequencies of genomic alterations, including single nucleotide variants (SNVs) 
and copy number alterations (CNAs), were analyzed. The cohort consisted of 82 
patients, 68 (83%) females and 14 (17%) males, aged 37-88 (mean 64) years. 
Forty-four RT-ASs (38 from breast) and 38 RT sarcomas of other histologies, 
including 12 malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (RT-MPNSTs), 14 
undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcomas (RT-UPSs), and 12 osteosarcomas (RT-OSs), 
were included. Median time intervals from radiation to initial diagnosis in 
RT-AS (8.0 years) were significantly lower than those in RT-MPNST and RT-UPS 
(12.5 and 18.5 years), respectively. Each RT-sarcoma histotype harbored distinct 
mutations and CNAs. RT-associated AS had more frequent MYC, FLT4, CRKL, HRAS, 
and KMT2D alterations than sporadic AS (enriched in TP53, KDR, ATM, ATRX), 
whereas the mutational landscapes of MPNST, UPS, and OS were similar in both RT 
and non-RT settings. CDKN2A/B deletions and TP53 alterations were infrequent in 
RT-AS compared with other RT sarcomas. Among RT sarcomas, RT-AS harbored the 
lowest fraction of genome altered (FGA), while RT-MPNST showed the highest FGA. 
RT-AS had the lowest insertion:SNV and deletion:SNV ratios, while RT-UPS had the 
highest. The predominant mutational signatures were associated with errors in 
DNA repair and replication. In conclusion, RT-AS has a distinct genomic 
landscape compared with other RT sarcomas and sporadic AS. Potential molecular 
targets for precision medicine may be histotype-dependent. © 2023 The

Approximately 50% of patients with malignancies receive radiation therapy (RT) at some point as part of their treatment [1]. Due to the carcinogenic effects of ionizing radiation, a late complication of RT is the development of secondary malignancies. Although RT-associated sarcomas are rare among patients who received RT (< 1%), they account for approximately 5% of all sarcomas [2–4]. RT-associated sarcomas represent a heterogeneous group of highly aggressive mesenchymal neoplasms associated with various histologies and clinical presentations. Our understanding of this subset remains limited not only due to their rare incidence and pleiotropic phenotypes, but also due to a lack of comprehensive genomic studies across variable histologies as well as histology-matched comparisons with non-RT sarcomas. A high incidence of MYC amplifications has been previously demonstrated in RT-associated angiosarcomas (AS) by low resolution molecular methods such as FISH and array CGH [5–7]. However, large scale NGS studies to investigate the genomic landscape of mutations and other copy number alterations (CNA) are scarce, particularly in comparison with other RT-associated sarcomas. Moreover, the prognostic impact of histologic types on the outcome of RT sarcomas remains controversial, with a current study showing that RT-associated AS have a less aggressive outcome compared to RT-associated malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNST) and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS) [8].

Additionally, certain RT-associated histologies are strongly associated with specific clinical presentations and the type of radiation regimen delivered. For example, most sarcomas arising in the RT field for breast cancer are AS, a histology that otherwise rarely occurs at other sites of the body or are seldom associated with other cancer types. In contrast, UPSs and osteosarcomas (OSs) are the most common RT-associated histotypes for sarcomas that arise in the bone, while RT-associated MPNSTs show a wide distribution across various soft tissue sites and are associated with a variety of cancer types [2]. It remains unclear if these histotype associations with anatomic sites and cancer types are related to underlying differences in the genomic landscape of each RT-associated histology. Another long-standing controversial issue is whether the RT setting is associated with an unfavorable outcome within the same sarcoma histotype and if the survival differences are indeed related to distinct intrinsic molecular alterations between RT- and non-RT histology [8–10].

In this study, we sought to perform a detailed comparative genomic investigation of AS versus other histotypes occurring in the RT field for better understanding of their pathogenesis and potential histology-tailored therapeutic approaches. Additionally, we performed histotype-specific genomic comparisons with non-RT control groups to further delineate similarity and differences for each sarcoma entity.

This study was approved by the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Institute Institutional Review Board. Our institutional targeted DNA-NGS assay database was searched for RT-associated sarcomas and sporadic, non-RT-associated sarcomas with matched histologies. Clinical history, clinical outcome data, pathologic diagnosis, and immunoprofile were documented in each case and reviewed to verify that the cases were correctly annotated. The minimum time interval between radiation therapy and onset of radiation-associated sarcoma among the cohort was 2 years.

FISH on interphase nuclei from paraffin embedded 4-micron sections was performed applying custom probes using bacterial artificial chromosomes (BACs), covering MYC (RP11–440N18). The BAC clones were obtained from BACPAC sources of Children’s Hospital of Oakland Research Institute (CHORI) (Oakland, CA) (http://bacpac.chori.org). Details for FISH testing have been described previously [7].

Detailed descriptions of MSK-IMPACT (Memorial Sloan Kettering-Integrated Mutation Profiling of Actionable Cancer Targets) workflow and data analysis, a matched tumor–normal hybridization capture-based targeted DNA next-generation sequencing (NGS) assay for solid tumors, have been provided previously [11]. All mutational and copy number calls were generated by the standard MSK-IMPACT pipeline. The reference genome was homo sapiens genome assembly GRCh37 (hg19). Intermediate variant call format (VCF) files containing somatic point mutations and small insertions/deletions (indels) called by Mutect [12] and Vardict [13] in matched tumor-normal mode, prior to filtering of silent mutations, were retrieved. Genetic alterations included copy number alterations (CNA), nonsense mutation, frameshift insertion/deletion, splice site mutations, missense mutations and in-frame insertions/deletions (insdel). Only somatic alterations were included. The types and frequencies of genomic alterations, including single nucleotide variants (SNV) and CNA, were analyzed for each category of RT-associated and non-RT-sarcomas. Copy number calling was determined by the GATK Depth of Coverage tool by comparing to a pool of FFPE normal specimens [14]. The fraction of genome altered (FGA) was defined as the fraction of genome with log2 copy number gain >0.2 or loss < −0.2 relative to the size of the genome for which copy number was profiled. Structural variants (SVs), including deletions, duplications, inversions, and translocations, were called using DELLY [15].

Data analysis was performed using R version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Mutations and gene-level copy number alterations were visualized and summarized using the R package ‘ComplexHeatmap’ version 2.8.0 [16]. A mixed linear effects model was implemented using the R package ‘lme4’ version 1.1.31 to assess for statistically significant differences in structural variant types across the different RT-sarcoma histologies [17]. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the Pathway Commons database [18].

Mutational signatures were extracted using non-negative matrix factorization with analysis performed using the R package “sigminer” version 2.1.9 using VCF files as input [19–21]. De novo mutational signature discovery for single base substitution (SBS), doublet base substitution (DBS) and small insertions and deletions (ID) was performed using the sig_unify_extract function with default parameters. Mutational signatures were then matched to SBSv3, DBS and ID reference mutational signatures in the Catalog of Somatic Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC) database using the get_sig_similarity function [19,20].

Survival analysis by comparison of hazard ratios using log–rank p testing and visualization of Kaplan–Meier curves were performed using the R packages ‘survminer’ version 0.4.9 and ‘survival’ version 3.2.13 [22,23]. Clinical charts were manually reviewed to document date of radiation therapy, initial presentation of RT-sarcoma, disease progression, and survival status. Median time (in years) to disease progression was defined as the time interval between initial presentation (presence of tumor seen radiographically or on physical examination) and the first instance of tumor recurrence or distant metastases after initial surgical resection and/or chemoradiation therapy with radiographically negative evidence of residual tumor.

The entire radiation-associated cohort consisted of 82 patients, 68 (83%) females and 14 (17%) males, aged 37–88 (mean 64) years. In terms of histologies, 44 (54%) were RT-AS (38 from breast/chest wall) and 38 were sarcomas of other histotypes, including 12 (15%) RT-MPNSTs, 14 (17%) RT-UPSs, and 12 (15%) RT-OSs. The RT-AS cohort consisted of 43 (98%) females and 1 (2%) male, aged 38–88 (mean 70) years. The RT-MPNST cohort consisted of 7 (58%) females and 5 (42%) males, aged 37–81 (mean 60) years. The RT-UPS cohort consisted of 10 (71%) females and 4 (29%) males, aged 37–74 (mean 58) years. The RT-OS cohort consisted of 8 (67%) females and 4 (33%) males, aged 46–83 (mean 61) years. There were significant differences in mean age among the different RT-sarcoma histologies (ANOVA p < 0.0001), with the mean age of RT-AS patients significantly older than those of the RT-MPNST, RT-OS, and RT-UPS patients after multiple comparison corrections (Table 1). Compared with sporadic sarcomas, radiation induced sarcomas showed higher female-to-male ratios but affected patients of similar age ranges (supplementary material, Table S1).

Among RT-AS, the most common prior (original) malignancy and site were carcinoma of the breast (86%) and chest wall (89%), respectively. Among RT-MPNST, the most common prior malignancy and site were breast or other carcinomas (50%) and pelvis (33%), respectively. Among RT-UPS, the most common prior malignancy and site were carcinoma of the breast (50%) and chest wall (57%), respectively. Among RT-OS, the most common prior malignancy and site were carcinoma (others) (58%) and chest wall (42%), respectively (Table 1).

RT-AS and RT-OS had a shorter interval since radiation to development of RT-sarcoma. Median time intervals (and range) from radiation to initial diagnosis of radiation-associated sarcoma were 8.0 (3–22), 12.5 (7–39), 18.5 (2–36), and 9.0 (2–39) years for RT-AS, RT-MPNST, RT-UPS, and RT-OS, respectively (global log-rank p-value = 0.0047). After correction for multiple comparisons, the pairwise log-rank p-values remained significant for AS versus MPNST (log-rank p = 0.017) and AS versus UPS (log-rank p = 0.0065) (Figure 1).

The genomic landscapes of radiation-associated angiosarcomas (RT-AS) were compared to other RT-sarcomas with available NGS results from the same institutional MSK-IMPACT platform.

Among RT-AS, the most common alterations other than MYC amplification (33, 75%) were alterations in FLT4, CRKL, HRAS, and KMT2D (Figure 2A). All 33 RT-AS cases that were MYC amplified were from the breast/chest wall with a history of breast carcinoma (87% of 38 cases). Of the RT-AS from other sites, none were MYC amplified. Among the 11 RT-AS cases without MYC amplification, 5 cases were also tested by FISH for MYC gene alterations (4 from breast, 1 from head and neck), and no discrepancies were identified. There were no recurrent non-MYC alterations among the cases without MYC amplifications. CRKL, FLT4, HRAS, and MYC are involved in stress-activated MAP kinase cascade. HRAS, FLT4, and MYC are involved in the Hippo–Merlin signaling pathway. Additionally, HRAS, FLT4 and KDR are part of the VEGF and VEGFR signaling network. Both HRAS and MYC are involved in response to ionizing radiation. The RT-AS cases with MYC amplifications had a significantly shorter time interval since radiation to development of RT-sarcoma compared to RT-AS cases without MYC amplifications (log-rank P = 0.0083, supplementary material, Figure S1).

In contrast, among RT-MPNSTs, the most frequent alterations were loss-of-function alterations in CDKN2A/B, NF1, TEK, SUZ12, TP53 and PTPRD (Figure 2B). CDKN2A, NF1 and TEK are involved in regulation of cell-matrix adhesion. CDKN2A, SUZ12 and TP53 are involved in oxidative stress induced senescence and SUMOylation. On the other hand, RT-UPS and RT-OS shared similar genomic alterations. Among RT-UPS, the most common alterations occurred in TP53, CDKN2A/B and KMT2D (Figure 3A). Similarly, among RT-OS, the most common alterations occurred in TP53 and CDKN2A/B (Figure 3B). CDKN2A and TP53 are involved G1/S transition of mitotic cell cycle and negative regulation of cell growth.

The genomic landscapes of RT-sarcomas were then compared to other sarcomas with similar histologies arising de novo. In this control group were included 135 sporadic AS, 64 sporadic MPNST, 273 sporadic UPS, and 76 sporadic OS.

Compared to sporadic (non-RT-associated) AS, alterations in MYC (amplifications, 75% versus 13%), FLT4 (mostly amplifications, 16% versus 8%), CRKL (amplifications, 14% versus 9%), HRAS (missense, in-frame insdel, 14% versus 2%), and KMT2D [mostly truncating (nonsense, frameshift) and some missense mutations, 11% versus 1%] were more common in RT-AS. The HRAS mutations were all hotspots mutations at the Ras GTPase domain, e.g., A59T, Q61R/L, S65_A66insDS, G60S65_dup. Interestingly, among RT-AS, MYC amplifications appeared to co-occur with FLT4, CRKL, HRAS, KMT2D and KDR. Whereas among sporadic AS, MYC amplifications tended to be mutually exclusive with KMT2D and KDR.

On the other hand, alterations in TP53 (loss-of-function missense and truncating mutations, 9% versus 23%), KDR (amplification, gain-of-function point mutations, 11% versus 24%), ATM [mostly inactivating mutations (frameshift, nonsense, splice site), 2% versus 10%], ATRX [mostly inactivating mutations (frameshift, nonsense, splice site), 2% versus 7%], CDKN2A/CDKN2B (predominantly deletions, 2% versus 5–7%) were less common in RT-AS compared to sporadic AS. Alterations in KIT (amplification), PDGFRA (amplification), ARID1A, POT1 and TERT were exclusively detected in sporadic AS but not RT-AS. Additionally, with the exception of one deletion event in CDKN2A/CDKN2B, the alterations in these genes were variants of uncertain significance (missense mutations, in-frame insdel), whereas most of the alterations in the same genes among sporadic AS were either inactivating alterations (deletion, frameshift insdels, nonsense mutations, splicing) in tumor suppressor genes (ATM, ATRX, CDKN2A/2B, ARID1A) or amplifications in oncogenes (KDR, KIT, PDGFRA) (Figure 2A).

Compared to sporadic MPNST, CDKN2A/B deletions (92% versus 44%), NF1 inactivating mutations/deletions (67% versus 36%), TEK deletions (42% versus 6%), SUZ12 inactivating mutations/deletions (33% versus 17%), TP53 inactivating mutations/deletions (25% versus 14%) and PTPRD mutations/deletions (25% versus 5%) were more common in RT-MPNST. In contrast, alterations in PREX2 (amplifications, 0% versus 8%), PRKAR1A (amplifications, 0% versus 9%), ATRX (mostly truncating mutations, 0% versus 8%), and SMARCB1 (truncating mutations or deletion, 0% versus 6%) were only observed in sporadic MPNST but not RT-MPNST (Figure 2B).

Compared to sporadic UPS, the frequencies of TP53 (deletions and loss-of-function mutations, 50% versus 57%), CDKN2A/B (mostly deletions, 29% versus 20%) and RB1 (truncating mutation or deletions, 14% versus 19%) were similar to RT-UPS (Figure 3A). On the other hand, KMT2D (truncating mutations, 21% versus 4%) and NOTCH3 (missense mutations, 14% versus 4%) were more frequent in RT-UPS compared to sporadic UPS. Alterations in ATRX (mostly truncating mutations, 19%), GRIN2A (missense mutations, 5%), PIK3CA (hotspot gain-of-function mutations, 5%) and TSC2 (deletions, inactivating mutations, 5%) were detected only in sporadic UPS but not RT-UPS (Figure 3A).

Compared to sporadic OS, the frequencies of TP53 (deletions and loss-of-function mutations, 42% versus 54%), CDKN2A/B (deletions, 33% versus 21%) and PTEN (truncating mutations or deletion, 17% versus 14%) were similar to RT-OS. On the other hand, MYC (amplifications, 17% versus 7%), FGFR1 (amplifications, 17% versus 7%) and PTPRT (missense mutations, 17% versus 7%) were more frequent in RT-OS compared to sporadic OS. Alterations in RB1 (truncating mutation or deletions, 25% versus 8%), NF1 (loss-of-function mutation or deletion, 14% versus 0%), TERT (promoter mutations, 14% versus 8%), and ATRX (loss-of-function mutation or deletion, 14% versus 8%) were detected more frequently in sporadic OS compared to RT-OS (Figure 3B).

Compared to other RT-associated sarcomas, RT-AS harbored significantly lower fraction of genome altered (FGA): 9% (0–35%) compared to 51% (7–85%) in RT-MPNST, 25% (0.3–67%) in RT-UPS, and 32% (0–72%) in RT-OS (ANOVA, P < 0.0001) (Figure 4A). RT-MPNST has the highest FGA followed by RT-OS and then RT-UPS. This trend is supported by the greater percentage of copy number alterations across the genome, as shown in the heatmap in supplementary material, Figure S2.

Not only did RT-MPNST harbor the highest FGA among RT-histologies, but the average FGA was also significantly higher in RT-MPNST compared to sporadic MPNST (51% versus 31%, p = 0.014). On the other hand, the average FGA showed no significant differences in RT-AS, RT-UPS or RT-OS compared to their non-RT-associated counterparts (Figure 4B). Of note, since the average FGA for sporadic pediatric OS (age <18 years) was significantly higher than sporadic OS in older adults (age ≥ 50 years) (supplementary material, Figure S3), we only included sporadic OS in older adults as the control group in this FGA analysis.

The abundance of small insertions and deletions relative to single nucleotide variants (SNV) was calculated for all four RT-sarcomas. RT-AS has the lowest insertion:SNV and deletion:SNV ratios among RT-sarcomas, while RT-UPS has the highest (adjusted p-value < 0.05) (Figure 5A,B). On the other hand, the four RT-sarcomas showed significantly different frequencies of structural variant types (large deletions, duplications, inversions, translocations): large deletions being most frequent among RT-UPS and non-existent in RT-OS (mixed linear effect model p = 0.00095), translocations being more frequent among RT-MPNST and RT-OS and non-existent in RT-AS (mixed linear effect model p = 0.04012) (Figure 5C).

Next, de novo analysis of single base substitution (SBS), doublet base substitution (DBS) and small insertions and deletions (ID) mutational signatures for RT-AS, RT-MPNST, RT-OS and RT-UPS was performed. Thereafter, these mutational signatures were compared to reference COSMIC mutational signatures. The predominant mutational signatures across all four RT-sarcoma histotypes were associated with errors in DNA repair and replication, i.e., defective DNA mismatch repair, slippage during DNA replication (Figure 6, supplementary material, Figures S4–6, and Table S2).

The median overall survival (OS) was 5.1, 2.1, 4.8 and 2.8 years, respectively, among RT-AS, RT-MPNST, RT-UPS, and RT-OS (supplementary material, Figure S7A). The median progression-free survival (PFS) from the time of diagnosis was 1.7, 2.3, 1.6, and 1.0 years, respectively, among RT-AS, RT-MPNST, RT-UPS, and RT-OS (supplementary material, Figure S7B). There were no statistically significant differences in OS or PFS among the four RT-sarcoma categories in this small cohort. However, this analysis did not take into consideration stage at diagnosis or treatment (e.g., chemotherapy).

The risk stratification and subsequent clinical management for de novo/sporadic sarcoma are based on established clinicopathologic factors, such as tumor grade, size, depth, location, histologic type, and surgical margins [24]. However, similar criteria for RT-sarcomas have not been well characterized, where most if not all are considered histologically high grade and are associated with aggressive presentation and poor overall outcomes. Moreover, debate still persists about whether survival outcome in RT-sarcomas is histology-specific, or if the observed poorer outcomes in RT- versus non-RT sarcomas are instead related to intrinsic biological differences. In this study, we undertake a comprehensive comparative genomic investigation of the four most common RT-sarcoma histotypes to better characterize the mutational landscape across histologies and to draw parallels with the same sarcoma histotypes in the non-RT (sporadic) setting.

A recent large clinical study focusing only on surgically resectable, nonmetastatic RT-associated sarcomas managed at a single tertiary center showed that their aggressive behavior is histology-dependent at least in certain sarcoma types [8]. Specifically, survival of patients with myxofibrosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma and angiosarcoma were comparable with patients presenting with the same respective histologies in the sporadic setting, even before matching for stage. In contrast, RT-associated MPNST and UPS were associated with roughly double the mortality rate of patients presenting with these malignancies in the sporadic setting. In the current study, RT-MPNST was highly enriched for genome-wide arm level copy number changes, even more so than sporadic MPNST. This may account for the observation that disease-specific death (DSD) only differed between RT-MPNST and sporadic MPNST (83% versus 46% 5-year DSD) but not for other RT-sarcoma histologies in this study of histology-specific prognostication for RT-sarcoma.

The overwhelming majority of RT-associated AS are high grade, occur in the chest wall as cutaneous nodules, and are secondary to irradiation for breast cancer. Studies have reported controversial results on the impact of RT on survival, with some studies showing an unfavorable outcome compared with sporadic cases, while others have not [25–29]. Additionally, in one large study of RT-sarcomas [9], differences in latency have been shown to be histotype-dependent, with the shortest documented in liposarcoma, and the longest in leiomyosarcoma. Although most sarcomas occur after 3 years, a small subset may show an earlier onset, and in the same study UPS was the prevalent histology. In the current study, RT-AS, specifically, RT-AS cases with MYC amplifications showed the shortest latency from radiation to development of disease compared to RT-MPNST, RT-UPS and RT-OS. This may be related to the increased genomic instability, as evidenced by the higher number of co-existing genetic alterations, among the MYC-amplified RT-AS compared to the non-MYC-amplified cases.

In contrast to other cancers associated with known carcinogens (tobacco, u-v exposure) that harbor a high tumor mutation burden (TMB), RT-associated sarcomas show a relatively low number of mutations that can be directly linked to ionizing radiation. This observation may explain the relatively low absolute risk of developing secondary malignancy in the RT field (0.03–0.2%) [30,31], with additional driver mutations likely being required. There have been only a handful comparative genetic studies between RT versus non-RT sarcomas, mostly limited by a very small number of cases for each sarcoma histotype. When conventional cytogenetic methods were used on ten cases of RT sarcomas of various histologies, the results showed relatively complex karyotypes with multiple, mostly unbalanced, structural rearrangements, similar to the abnormalities detected in non-RT tumors of the same histotypes [32]. A subsequent study using comparative genomic hybridization (CGH) showed gains at 7q being frequent in both post-irradiation and sporadic UPS, while losses were more frequent in RT-OS compared with sporadic cases: specifically, losses at 1p (57% in RT versus 3% in non-RT OS) [33]. Regarding radiation-associated deletions and chromosomal rearrangements, using chromosome band painting technique: Intrachromosomal rearrangements or deletions, which could be visualized by chromosome band painting techniques, are produced much more efficiently by densely ionizing radiation compared with chemical mutagens [34].

In the NGS era, one study investigated 12 RT-associated secondary malignancies of four different tumor types (OS, spindle cell sarcoma, AS, breast cancer) by whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and obtained catalogues of somatic mutations, in comparison to 319 RT-naive breast cancers and sarcomas processed by the same sequencing and bioinformatics pipeline [35]. Overall, the RT-associated secondary malignancies showed an excess of balanced inversions and small deletions. The indel/substitution ratio was significantly increased in RT associated tumors (P=0.0003), with a significant excess of deletions relative to insertions in RT setting. Interestingly, deletions in RT-associated cancers showed almost no variability across the genome and minimal correlation with genomic properties such as replication timing, sequence complexity, or GC content. Since small deletions were the predominant read-out of this damage, there was no association with genomic features that influence other mutational processes. Moreover, the pathways for repairing radiation-associated DNA damage (i.e., small deletions) were either microhomology-mediated (at the breakpoint junction) or through non-homologous end-joining, rather than homologous recombination.

Additionally, based on a prior study, a high proportion (58%) of the RT-associated sarcomas exhibited a somatic inactivating mutation for one allele of TP53, systematically associated with a loss of the other allele [35]. The data we presented in this study do not address allele-specific inactivation status of TP53, which is beyond the scope of our investigation. On the other hand, the high frequency (52%) of short deletions observed in the mutation pattern of RT-associated sarcomas may be related to the induction of DNA breaks by ionizing radiation. The lack of hyper-reactivity of CpG dinucleotides and the presence of recurrent sites of mutation at codons 135 and 237 also seem to be specific for radiation tumorigenesis [36]. For structural variants, RT-associated second malignancies showed a high prevalence of an exceedingly rare type of rearrangement, in the form of balanced inversions (ranging from a few hundred base pairs to nearly 100 megabases), irrespective of histotype. Furthermore, these two genomic imprints of ionizing radiation, small deletions, and balanced inversions were also noted in a cohort of radiation-exposed prostate cancer lesions [35].

Among the RT-sarcomas in our study, we compared the ratios of indels to SNVs in the different RT-sarcomas and found that they are lowest in RT-AS and highest in RT-UPS. Further, we showed that the four RT-sarcomas showed significantly different frequencies of structural variants, with large deletions being most frequent among RT-UPS and non-existent in RT-OS, and translocations being more frequent among RT-MPNST and RT-OS and non-existent in RT-AS. We also found that the mutational signatures involving SNVs and small indels were predominantly attributed to DNA damage mismatch repair and DNA slippage during replication regardless of the RT-histotype.

In the whole-genome sequencing study of 12 RT-associated secondary malignancies, RT-AS harbored PTPRB and PLCG1 mutations, while spindle cell sarcoma and OS driver alterations included TP53 and CDKN2A [35]. Compared to other RT-sarcomas, among RT-AS, PTPRB mutations were identified only in tumors that were either known to be secondary to RT and / or have MYC amplification, a biomarker of radiation-associated secondary AS (p = 0.005). In this group, the prevalence of PTPRB mutations was 45% [35]. Our results showed that RT-associated sarcomas have overall limited genomic overlap, with each histotype harboring distinct mutations and CNA. For example, CDKN2A/B alterations were almost non-existent in RT-AS but occurred in the majority of RT-MPNST cases (2% versus 92%). Similarly, TP53 alterations were infrequent in RT-AS compared to RT-UPS and RT-OS (9% versus 42–50%). Additionally, the mutational landscapes of MPNST and UPS/OS are similar in both RT and non-RT settings, whereas RT-associated AS has distinct MYC, CRKL, HRAS and KMT2D alterations compared to their non-RT-associated counterparts. Consistent with previous findings, MYC amplification was seen in 87% of RT-AS from the breast/chest wall in patients with a history of breast carcinoma, while no amplifications were detected in RT-AS occurring at other sites. CRKL, FLT4, HRAS, and MYC are involved in stress-activated MAP kinase cascade. Activation of this pathway has been shown previously in AS in a study using a targeted NGS panel [37]. Although our study provided another proof-of-concept that a targeted NGS panel of sufficient size can provide reasonable information on most cancer-relevant genes, mutational signatures, and some structural variants, WGS is still the optimal technique to truly examine the spectrum and scope of these genomic alterations, specifically those involving noncoding regions.

In conclusion, we conducted a comprehensive comparative genomic investigation of the four most common RT-sarcoma histotypes in comparison to sarcomas of the same histotypes in the non-RT (sporadic) setting. Our results indicate that RT-AS has distinct genomic landscape compared to other RT-sarcomas and sporadic AS. In our opinion, from a therapeutic standpoint, our findings that RT-sarcomas demonstrate histology-specific genomic profiles support the current approach of treating RT-sarcomas based on histology as opposed to this group of sarcomas as a homogenous category. For instance, both primary and RT-AS are treated with taxane-based chemotherapeutic regimens [38]. Further, investigations on the use of small molecular inhibitors of DNA methyltransferase (DNMT1) in sporadic/NF1-related and RT-MPNSTs harboring polycomb repressive complex (PRC)-inactivating mutations are ongoing [39]. Thus, potential molecular targets for precision medicine may be histotype-dependent.
