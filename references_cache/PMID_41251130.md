BACKGROUND: Acute transverse myelitis (ATM), acute flaccid myelitis with
polio-like myeloradiculitis (AFM) and Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) are severe
neuroimmune disorders associated with high morbidity. First-line treatments
include corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIg), but they may be
insufficient. Therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) and immunoadsorption (IA) are
second- or third-line options in pediatrics, despite their recognized efficacy
in adults. Data on pediatric use remain limited. This study aimed to evaluate
the efficacy and safety of apheresis in children with ATM, GBS or AFM.
METHODS: We conducted a retrospective monocentric study at Necker University
Hospital, including pediatric patients diagnosed with ATM, GBS or AFM who
underwent TPE/IA between 2014 and 2024. Functional outcomes were assessed using
the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at five time points, from peak disease severity
to last follow-up. Safety was evaluated based on severe adverse events.
RESULTS: Among 23 children (6 years [Q1-Q3]), 20 received TPE and 3 IA. 17 of 23
patients (74%) showed significant improvement by the end of treatment. The
median mRS was 5 (IQR 4-5) before apheresis, improving to 4 (IQR 2-4) at the end
of TPE/IA, and decreasing to 2 at 6 months. Brainstem function fully recovered
in all surviving patients. Among 14 children with sphincter dysfunction, 5 (36%)
still required intermittent catheterization. No treatment discontinuations
occurred, but two patients died, one from venous air embolism, one after
life-sustaining treatment limitation.
CONCLUSION: TPE and IA appear effective in pediatric ATM, AFM and GBS. Larger
studies are needed to confirm long-term efficacy and refine treatment
guidelines.

Acute transverse myelitis (ATM) and Guillain‐Barré syndrome (GBS) are inflammatory and immunological disorders affecting respectively the central and peripheral nervous systems. They are involved in severe motor deficits and life‐threatening complications in adults as well as in children [1, 2]. First‐line treatments rely on immunomodulatory therapies, such as corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulins [3]. However, they can be insufficient whereas the rapid progression of ATM and GBS can critically affect both functional and vital prognoses.

Most often, therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) (extracting plasma and replacing it) and immunoadsorption (IA) (removing targeted immune components) are considered second‐ or third‐line options in children, whereas the American Society for Apheresis (ASFA) categorizes therapeutic apheresis (AT) as a first‐line therapy for GBS and a second‐line therapy for neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders (NMOSD) in adult [4]. However, there is no clear ASFA recommendation for its use in ATM, and data on its application in pediatric patients remain limited [4, 5, 6, 7].

Despite increasing recognition of therapeutic apheresis's potential role in neuroimmune conditions, its use in pediatric patients presents unique challenges. Children have lower circulating blood volumes, a higher risk of hemodynamic instability, and more frequent complications related to vascular access and anticoagulation management [8].

The aim of the study was to assess the benefit and safety of therapeutic apheresis in the treatment of ATM, AFM and GBS in children through a retrospective monocentric analysis of pediatric patients who were treated at Necker University Hospital, a French resource Center.

This retrospective, monocentric study was conducted at Necker University Hospital in Paris. Pediatric patients were included if they were diagnosed with ATM, with or without associated optic neuropathy, GBS or an AFM characterized by concomitant spinal cord and peripheral axonal involvement and underwent apheresis before the age of 18, between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2024. Cases with associated supratentorial acute demyelinating syndromes were excluded to assure homogeneity. Data were collected retrospectively from electronic medical records by one pediatrician (AF). Collected data details are reported in supplemental methods.

The primary outcome was the modification in the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) for patients aged ≥ 2 years and in the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) for those < 2 years. Scores were assessed at five time points: disease peak, the start of TPE/IA (J0) (corresponding to the first TPE/IA session), immediately after the last TPE/IA session, at 6 months post‐treatment, and at last follow‐up. The mRS (0 = no symptoms, 5 = total dependence) quantified global disability. Scores were retrospectively determined from medical records. EDSS allowed specific assessment of pyramidal, brainstem, and sphincter functions when initially affected.

Safety was assessed through the systematic collection of severe adverse events, defined as any treatment‐related complication causing death, prolonged hospitalization, or need for extended intravenous treatment. Specific complications investigated included catheter‐related infections, thrombosis, and mortality.

Due to limited sample size, statistical analyses were descriptive, reporting ratios, medians, and interquartile ranges.

This study complied with ethical guidelines and institutional regulations for research involving human participants. The authors declare no conflicts of interest and received no funding for this study.

A total of 23 patients underwent TPE or IA for the treatment of ATM, GBS or AFM (Figure S1). Patient characteristics are presented in Table 1 and Table S1.

Initial Characteristics (Before Treatment With Plasma Exchange or Immunoadsorption) of a Series of 23 Patients With ATM, GBS, and AFM.

TPE/IA were initiated at an advanced stage, in median 9 days after disease peak was reached and 10 days after the start of first‐line therapies. All patients had received first‐line therapies. Among the 23 patients, 20 received TPE, and 3 received IA, with a median of 9 sessions per patient (20 days [11–23]). All required central venous catheter placement (Table 1).

Compared to the 9% improvement observed before TPE/IA, 17 of 23 patients (74%) showed significant improvement at the end of TPE/IA, defined as a decrease of at least 1 point on the mRS for patients over 2 years old and on the EDSS for those under 2 years old. (Tables S3 and S4). Before apheresis, the mRS was assessed at disease peak and at the start of TPE/IA (Figure 1). At both stages, the median mRS score was 5 (IQR 3–5), indicating no clinical improvement despite first‐line therapies. The median mRS improved from 5 to 4 (IQR 2–4) during apheresis and, 6 (34%) had an mRS ≤ 2, indicating regained ambulation and autonomy. At six months, the median mRS decreased to 2 (IQR 1–3), with 10 patients (54%) achieving mRS ≤ 2. At the last follow‐up (median 35 months, IQR 16–60), the median mRS remained stable, with continued individual improvements (Tables S2 and S3). Similar results were obtained regarding the motor EDSS score including all patients regardless of age (Figure 1). Finally, 7 patients (30%) had a full motor recovery (Table S3).

Brainstem function, particularly cranial nerve involvement affecting swallowing and speech, was evaluated using the brainstem score of the EDSS. The median score before apheresis was 5 (IQR 5–5), which decreased by 2 points at treatment end. At final follow‐up, all surviving patients had fully recovered (Figure 1, Tables S2 and S3).

Sphincter dysfunction was assessed using the EDSS sphincter score. At the end of TPE/IA, 5 of 14 patients (36%) had recovered completely, while 7 (50%) still required catheterization. At final follow‐up, 6 (43%) had fully recovered. (Figure 1, Tables S2 and S3).

Regarding safety, two patients died during follow‐up: one following an air embolism, one following a limitation of life‐sustaining treatments. Two central line infections and one jugular thrombosis were reported, all with a favorable outcome. No apheresis was discontinued due to poor tolerance.

TPE and IA are increasingly recognized as effective short‐term interventions for severe neuroimmune disorders, particularly when the response to immunomodulatory and anti‐inflammatory treatments is inadequate or too slow. In our study, the median mRS and EDSS scores just before treatment were both 5, indicating severe disability, with no improvement with first‐line immunotherapy. Following TPE/IA (9 sessions [7, 8, 9, 10]), 17 of 23 patients (74%) showed significant improvement by the end of treatment. These findings are consistent with the existing pediatric literature [5, 6, 7, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Nolan et al. reported that 15 of 19 (79%) children diagnosed with severe ATM improved after 4–7 TPE sessions [9]. In GBS, two studies comparing children with severe forms treated with either IVIg or TPE showed that TPE reduced mechanical ventilation duration (11 vs. 13 days, p = 0.037) [10], shortened hospital stays (15 vs. 29 days, p < 0.001) and lowered intubation rates (4 vs. 20, p < 0.001) [13]. Eyre et al. observed a median mRS improvement from 5 to 4 after TPE/IA in 6 GBS and 14 ATM children [7]. These results support the role of apheresis in controlling disease progression and enhancing recovery in severe cases, reinforcing its value as a key therapeutic option for short‐term efficacy.

The long‐term efficacy of apheresis remains a question. In our cohort, 6 (34%) of 18 patients had an mRS ≤ 2 after TPE/IA, increasing to 10 (54%) at 6 months and 12 (67%) at last follow‐up, suggesting a potential sustained benefit. Few studies assess long‐term outcomes. Eyre et al. reported a 2‐point mRS improvement in GBS and a 1‐point improvement in ATM [7] at last follow‐up, while Nolan et al. found children with mRS ≤ 2 increased from 4 (21%) at discharge to 12 (80%) at follow‐up [9]. As 19 (90%) of our patients received post‐TPE immunotherapy, mainly rituximab, cumulative treatment effects should also be considered.

Over 10 years at Necker Hospital, TPE/IA was used only in the most severe cases—20% of myelitis, 75% of AFM and fewer GBS. Historical data before apheresis show 35%–40% full recovery in all myelitis cases, including mild cases. GBS usually recovers well, except in severe forms: in Saad et al., 23% of severe GBS cases treated with IVIg alone fully recovered [13]. In our cohort, which included only very severe cases, 30% recovered completely suggesting that TPE/IA may offer comparable or better outcomes in severe pediatric cases than those treated with first‐line therapies alone. (Table S4).

An often‐overlooked aspect in TPE/IA studies is sphincter function. In our cohort, only 5 of 14 patients (35%) with initial bladder dysfunction fully recovered by TPE/IA end, increasing to 6 (43%) at final follow‐up. Unlike motor recovery, early incomplete sphincter recovery appears associated with persistent deficits.

Overall, TPE/IA sessions were well tolerated. Our findings align with existing literature [5, 9, 13]. Two deaths occurred: one from disease severity—a known risk in myelitis with estimated 5.5% mortality [14]—and one from fatal air embolism during central line removal, highlighting the need for strict procedural protocols and cautious patient selection for this second‐line invasive therapy [15].

Despite encouraging results, this study has limitations. The small sample reflects the rarity of these disorders, and its single‐center retrospective design may limit generalizability. The absence of a control group and the frequent use of adjunctive therapies, such as rituximab, complicate the interpretation of long‐term outcomes. Future randomized controlled trials, even if difficult because of the rarity of the diseases, are essential to define TPE/IA's precise role in pediatric neuroimmune disorders and its optimal integration into standard protocols.
