Introduction 
Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) are characterized 
as a clonal proliferation of hematopoietic stem/progenitor 
cells, which cause an increase in one or more mature 
myeloid lineage cells. MPNs consist of multiple 
subgroups: chronic myeloid leukemia (CML);  
polycythemia vera (PV); essential thrombocythemia 
(ET); prefibrotic primary myelofibrosis (PMF); overt 
PMF; chronic neutrophilic leukemia (CNL); chronic 
eosinophilic leukemia (CEL); and unclassifiable MPN, 
not otherwise specified [1]. 
 Essential thrombocythemia (ET) is a chronic 
myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN)c characterized by an 
elevated platelet count, resulting from clonal expansion of

megakaryocytes. ET is a member of the BCR -ABL 
negative myeloproliferative neoplasms, which share a 
common pathophysiology marked by acquisition of a 
JAK-STAT signaling mutation in the hematopoietic stem 
cell, leading to excessive proliferation of one or mo re 
mature myeloid cell lineages [2]. 
The JAK2V617F mutation is the most common disease -
causing mutation in MPN, including ET. This mutation 
leads to constitutive JAK -STAT signaling and increased 
gene expression. While JAK2V617F is present in over 
95% of polycythemia vera cases, it is found in only around 
50% of ET patients. Approximately one -third of ET 
patients have a CALR gene mutation, which encodes the 
calreticulin protein. Mutations in the MPL gene, which 
encodes the TPO receptor, are reported in less than 5% of 
ET patients, often in olde r individuals. Roughly 10% of 
ET patients lack identifiable JAK-STAT driver mutations 
and are considered "triple-negative" [3] 
 
Epidemiology and natural history 
Essential thrombocythemia is a rare disease, with an 
estimated incidence of around 1 -5 cases per 100,000 
population and a prevalence of 38 -57 per 100,000. The 
disease is more common in females. ET typically affects 
older individuals, with an onset between 50-60 years of 
age. However, a substantial cohort of younger patients are 
also diagnosed with ET. Compared to the general 
population, overall survival in ET patients is mildly 
reduced. Nevertheless, the prognosis in ET is more 
favorable than in other myelo proliferative neoplasms, 
such as polycythemia vera or primary myelofibrosis. 
Notably, younger ET patients, particularly those under 60 
years old and with a platelet count less than 1500 × 
10^9/L, seem to have a more indolent disease course. 
Moreover, these younger ET patients exhibit a thrombotic 
risk comparable to the general population and better 
overall survival [4]. 
 
Genetic and molecular pathways 
Mutations in JAK2, MPL, and CALR are frequently 
observed in BCR -ABL negative MPN, and detecting 
these driver mutations has become crucial for diagnosis. 
Comprehensive genetic analyses have identified 
additional mutations associated with prognosis in these 
disorders. Single-cell studies have provided insights into 
the effects of mutation order and the pathogenesis of 
BCR-ABL negative MPNs. Furthermore, overexpression 
of CREB3L1 has been observed in the megakaryocytes 
and platelets of these patients [5]. 
The JAK2 mutation was a key diagnostic factor in the 
2008 World Health Organization (WHO) classification for 
BCR-ABL negative MPN. Since then, mutations in MPL 
and CALR have also been identified as important 
diagnostic markers, leading to a revised 2022 WH O 
classification that relies on detecting these three driver 
gene mutations. However, some patients with ET and 
PMF lack mutations in all three genes and are considered 
"triple-negative." [6] For these triple -negative cases, 
excluding non-cancerous causes of high blood cell counts 
is important, and a bone marrow (BM) biopsy is needed 
to confirm the MPN diagnosis based on the characteristic 
BM features. ET, along with PV and PMF, make up the 
classical Philadelphia -negative MPNs, which share 
common clinical and molecular traits. While ET generally 
has a good prognosis, it can be variable, and some patients 
may progress to more serious conditions like 
myelofibrosis or acute leukemia [4,7]. 
 
Clinical Characteristics 
ET reflects the transformation of a multipotent 
hematopoietic stem cell, but its precise molecular 
pathogenesis remains unclear. It is a rare, heterogeneous 
neoplasm with the potential to progress to MF or leukemic 
transformation, presenting with a wide sp ectrum of 
symptoms ranging from asymptomatic thrombocytosis to 
increased thrombotic and hemorrhagic conditions [8]. 
In under a half of ET patients, the most frequent 
symptoms are asthenia, weight loss, fever and early 
satiety.  The onset and/or progression of ET may be 
complicated by thrombotic events and/or the progression 
to myelofibrosis and/or acute myeloid leukemia  (AML). 
The most common arterial events reported in ET are 
stroke (10.7%) and acute coronary syndromes (6.1%), 
whereas the most frequent venous events are deep vein 
(3.4%), pulmonary (0.9%), splanchnic (1.4%) and 
cerebral (0.7%) thrombosis, respectively.  The prevalence 
of hemorrhagic events in ET is evaluated at 7.3% (5.3 –
10.0), the most common being cutaneous/mucosal and/or 
gastrointestinal bleedings [1,9].  
Thrombotic risk estimation is based on the history of 
thrombosis, presence of the JAK2 V617F mutation, age, 
and cardiovascular risk [9].   
The risk of progression to MF at 15 years varies between 4 
and 11%, and that to AML varies between 2 and 5% [10]. 
 
Diagnostic Criteria and Testing 
Thrombocytosis, defined by a persistently elevated 
platelet count ≥450 × 10^9/L, may be due to a wide range

of conditions with divergent management strategies, 
expected clinical courses, and prognoses. The accurate 
diagnosis and appropriate management of ET require an 
integrated, comprehensive approach [11]. This involves 
addressing both the major and minor crit eria outlined in 
the WHO diagnostic guidelines, as well as thoroughly 
excluding secondary causes of thrombocytosis [1]. 
The diagnostic workup for ET typically includes a 
detailed medical history, complete blood count, peripheral 
blood smear analysis, assessment of iron status, C -
reactive protein levels, and genetic testing to detect driver 
mutations such as JAK2V617F, CALR, and MPL. If these 
initial investigations fail to identify a secondary cause for 
the thrombocytosis, further evaluation with bone marrow 
aspiration and biopsy is warranted. In cases where the 
JAK2V617F, CALR, or MPL mutations are not detected, 
it is important to perform a more comprehensive myeloid 
gene panel to assess for the presence of clonal markers in 
other, non -JAK-STAT pathway genes [12,13]. This 
multifaceted diagnostic approach is crucial for ensuring 
accurate classification, appropriate risk stratification, and 
the development of an optimal management plan for 
patients with suspected ET.

Differential diagnosis 
Investigation of reactive causes for thrombocytosis 
requires a comprehensive assessment, including the full 
blood count, blood film, iron status, C -reactive protein, 
and bone marrow trephine biopsy, which may reveal 
features such as increased macrophage activity and few or 
no megakaryocytes with typical ET morphology [11]. 
ET must be differentiated from other MPN that can mimic 
it, such as PV , PMF, BCR -ABL1-positive CML, 
myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasm 
(MDS/MPN) overlap syndromes, and MDS with isolated 
del(5q) or chromosome 3 abnormalities, all of which can 
present with thrombocytosis.

Iron deficiency, a feature of PV , can mask the elevated 
hemoglobin and hematocrit levels of PV in a patient 
presenting with normal red cell indices and an elevated 
platelet count, thus mimicking ET [14,15]. This MPN 
subtype, known as "masked PV ," has been reported to 
have a higher risk of myelofibrotic transformation and 
thrombosis than ET, and its management includes 
maintaining a hematocrit below 45% [16]. The 2016 
WHO criteria adjusted the hemoglobin range to better 
identify these cases when diagnosing PV [6]. 
 
Risk Stratification 
Vascular complications, including arterial and venous 
thrombosis, are the primary drivers of morbidity and 
mortality in patients with ET. Thrombosis can be the 
presenting symptom that leads to the diagnosis of ET. The 
incidence of thrombosis in ET is estim ated to be around 
14% at 10 years, with a prevalence at diagnosis of 10% -
35%. Additionally, microvascular complications are 
observed, manifesting as symptoms such as 
erythromelalgia, migraine, and paresthesia [17–19]. 
Hemorrhagic episodes may occur in ET, particularly in 
cases with extreme thrombocytosis. The bleeding in these 
patients is primarily due to an acquired von Willebrand 
syndrome (vVWS), a complex and not fully understood 
condition. The proposed mechanism inv olves the loss of 
high molecular weight multimers of von Willebrand 
factor (vWF), which can occur through increased 
proteolysis by the enzyme ADAMTS-13 and/or increased 
adsorption of vWF onto the surface of the elevated 
number of platelets. This platelet c ount-dependent 
depletion of functional vWF leads to impaired platelet 
adhesion and increased bleeding risk in these patients 
[20]. The development of this acquired vVWS is a key 
factor contributing to the hemorrhagic complications 
observed in ET, particula rly when the platelet count is 
markedly elevated [21,22]. 
Risk stratification is crucial in ET to identify patients at 
higher risk of thromboembolic events, which significantly 
contribute to morbidity and mortality. 
  The conventional risk stratification approach for ET 
relies on a system that categorizes patients as either high-
risk or low -risk, as derived from the landmark study by 
Cortelazzo et al [23]. This risk assessment takes into 
account factors such as age an d prior thrombosis. 
Moreover, the presence of the JAK2 V617F mutation, one 
of the most common driver mutations in BCR -ABL-
negative myeloproliferative neoplasms, has been shown 
to independently increase the risk of vascular events in 
ET, underscoring the importance of molecular profiling in 
the comprehensive evaluation and management of these 
patients [17,23]. 
Recent studies suggest that factors such as mutation type, 
leukocytosis, and anemia may also influence prognosis, 
outcomes, and survival in ET. Around 50% of ET patients 
experience systemic manifestations that can negatively 
impact their quality of life, d espite some being 
asymptomatic [24]. Common symptoms include 
constitutional symptoms and those associated with 
microvascular complications, such as headache, 
dizziness, and erythromelalgia [24]. 
Additionally, a multivariable retrospective analysis led to 
the development of the International Prognostic Score of 
Thrombosis for Essential Thrombocythemia (IPSET), 
which incorporates several factors into a single prognostic 
score for thrombosis risk [25 ,26]. This model classified 
patients into 3 risk groups based on age >60 years (1 
point), cardiovascular risk factors including tobacco use, 
hypertension, and diabetes mellitus (1 point), previous 
thrombosis (2 points), and JAK2 V617F positivity (2 
points). Patients with 2 points are considered high risk 
(thrombosis risk 3.56% of patients per year).  A revised 
IPSET-thrombosis score leading to a 4 -tier model where 
patients were classified as very low risk (no thrombosis, 
age 60, JAK2 unmutated), and high ri sk (thrombosis 
history; or age >60 and JAK2 mutated); cardiovascular 
risk factors were no longer a part of the model. The 
revised IPSET -thrombosis score has been validated by 
Haider et al and its use has been adopted by European 
Leukemia Net and National C omprehensive Cancer 
Network guidelines [25–27]. 
 
Therapeutic options 
Therapeutic strategies 
Individualized management is essential for patients with 
ET. 
Contemporary therapies for ET, such as antiplatelet agents 
and cytoreductive therapies aimed at reducing the platelet 
count, primarily target the management of thrombotic risk 
[24]. However, these approaches do not fully address the 
risk of disease progres sion, such as the potential 
transformation to more advanced MPD like myelofibrosis 
or acute leukemia. Therefore, treatment decisions for 
patients with ET require a careful consideration of various 
factors. These include the specific mutation profile of the  
disease, the patient's overall prognosis, their personal 
preferences and priorities, the potential drug toxicity 
profiles, the mode of drug administration, the impact on

can develop an optimal management pla n to address the 
complex needs of patients with ET. 
 
Conventional Cytoreductive Therapies 
Hydroxycarbamide (HC) is the cytoreductive agent 
recommended by expert consensus for ET. As the most 
widely utilized cytoreductive therapy, HC is particularly 
preferred for the treatment of elderly patients, who 
constitute the majority of the ET population [28,29].  
However, HC may not be the optimal choice for all 
patients. Some literature data argue that the use of HC 
should be approached with caution. Therefore, alternative 
cytoreductive therapies, such as interferon -alpha (INF-a) 
or anagrelide, may be more suitable for certain elderly ET 
patients, as they can provide effective platelet reduction 
with potentially fewer adverse effects. The choice of 
cytoreductive agent should be carefully considered based 
on the individual patient's risk factors, comorbidities, and 
overall prognosis [30]. 
Cytoreductive treatment is recommended for high-risk ET 
patients, such as those aged 60 or older, with a history of 
thrombosis or bleeding, or with a platelet count of 150 × 
10^9/L or higher. Cytoreductive treatment may also be 
appropriate for some low-risk ET patients with persistent 
symptoms [30].  
The results of clinical trials have demonstrated that 
treatment with HC in combination with low -dose aspirin 
is superior to treatment with anagrelide plus low -dose 
aspirin in reducing the risk of thrombotic events in 
patients with ET. Patients treated with  anagrelide had 
similar rates of thrombosis compared to those on other 
cytoreductive agents, but they experienced higher rates of 
hemorrhage and progression to myelofibrosis. Therefore, 
anagrelide is more frequently and appropriately utilized in 
the second-line setting, rather than as a first -line therapy. 
In the context of the emergence of novel targeted therapies 
for myeloproliferative neoplasms, the European

The management of intermediate -risk ET lacks clear 
evidence, and the majority of these patients may not 
benefit from cytoreductive treatment. For younger 
patients, consideration of an alternative first -line agent, 
such as INF-a, is recommended. Excellent responses have 
been observed in patients treated with pegylated -
interferon-alpha-2a (PEG -INF-2a). Although older INF 
preparations had tolerability issues, the appearance of 
pegylated formulations has improved this issue [34]. 
 
Targeted Molecular Therapies 
The first clinically approved JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor, 
Ruxolitinib (RUX), has shown promise in the treatment 
of ET as well. RUX is currently approved for the 
management of patients with symptomatic MF and with 
PV , where it has demonstrated excellent responses in 
terms of a reduction in spleen size, achievement of 
hematocrit control, and improvements in patients' 
symptoms and quality of life. Building on these successes, 
RUX has also been investigated in clinical trials for 
patients with ET who are HC -resistant or HC-intolerant, 
or as a potential third -line therapy option for this 
condition[35]. The results of these trials suggest that RUX 
may offer an effective targeted treatment approach for 
certain subsets of ET patients, particularly those who have

Non-cytoreductive treatment 
Antiplatelet therapy is considered a cornerstone of ET 
management and is widely adopted as the standard of 
care. However, there are certain exceptions where 
antiplatelet agents may not be appropriate, such as in 
patients with adverse reactions or young individuals 
deemed to have a low thrombotic risk, where the risks 
may outweigh the benefits. Additionally, antiplatelet 
treatment is contraindicated in certain situations, when an 
increased bleeding risk attributed to acquired von 
Willebrand syndrome is possible [24]. Aspirin, an 
irreversible COX -1 inhibitor, has demonstrated efficacy 
in reducing the risk of thrombotic events and mitigati ng 
vasomotor symptoms in ET. Consequently, low -dose 
aspirin is recommended for the management of most ET 
risk groups, with some exceptions. 
Interestingly, a retrospective study reported an increased 
bleeding risk in low -risk CALR -mutated ET patients, 
without a corresponding reduction in thrombotic risk. In 
the second -line setting, various targeted therapeutic 
approaches are currently under inv estigation in ET, with 
promising emerging signals [37]. 
 
Considerations for specific populations 
Young Patients 
The driver mutation profile observed in the younger 
cohort of ET patients was notably distinct from the more 
typical pattern seen in older ET patients. This younger 
group exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of 
CALR and MPL mutations, along with a larger proportion 
of triple -negative cases. Interestingly, the JAK2V617F 
mutation, which is the most common driver in ET overall, 
was present in only 31.7% of these younger patients, 
markedly lower than the proportion typically observed in 
the broader ET population [38]. 
This unique mutational landscape observed in younger ET 
patients suggests that the underlying molecular 
pathogenesis may differ from that of their older 
counterparts. The specific driver mutations present can 
have important implications for disease behavio r, 
thrombotic risk, and response to targeted therapies. Given 
the rarity of ET in younger individuals, the optimal 
management approach to effectively prevent thrombotic 
complications in this patient subgroup remains uncertain 
[39]. 
The majority of these younger ET patients would be 
classified as low -risk according to current risk -
stratification guidelines, and may not require intensive 
cytoreductive treatment [38]. However, a recent 
comprehensive review found that over 60% of the 
younger ET patients included were managed with 
cytoreductive therapies, despite most not meeting high -
risk criteria. This disparity highlights the need for further 
research and the development of tailored risk -
stratification and treatment strategies that acco unt for the 
distinct molecular and clinical features observed in 
younger ET patients. Elucidating the precise drivers of 
disease in this population and understanding their impact 
on thrombotic risk and therapeutic responsiveness will be 
crucial to optimizi ng management and improving 
outcomes for these individuals [38–40]. 
 
Triple-Negative Thrombocytosis 
This subgroup of ET patients with distinct 
clinicopathological features warrants further investigation 
to elucidate the presumptive underlying pathophysiology. 
They are characterized by a younger age and increased 
prevalence in females. Before confidently assigning a 
triple-negative molecular status, it is important to consider 
the possibility of inherited or reactive etiologies [41]. 
While hereditary thrombocytosis is rare, it has been 
described in families with mutations in JAK -STAT 
signaling genes. Exploring the potential presence of low -
level driver mutations, noncanonical JAK-STAT signaling 
mutations, or non -JAK-STAT driver clonal markers may 
be warranted in this patient subgroup. Long -term follow-
up of these triple -negative patients will be interesting  to 
determine their outcomes and whether intervention is ever 
required for modest thrombocytosis without 
complications [11]. 
Given the increased incidence  ET in women, including 
those of reproductive age, understanding the potential 
complications of ET during pregnancy and implementing 
appropriate clinical management strategies is crucial. 
Maternal morbidity and pregnancy compl ications are 
elevated in ET, including thrombosis, hemorrhage, 
miscarriage, stillbirth, intrauterine growth restriction, 
preeclampsia, and premature labor. However, successful 
pregnancies occur more often in ET patients than in those 
with PV . Importantly, the JAK2V617F mutation status has 
not been associated with pregnancy complications or 
outcomes. Low -dose aspirin is considered safe in 
pregnancy and has been shown to reduce rates of 
preeclampsia and fetal mortality. In the setting of ET, 
aspirin use is associated with improved maternal and fetal

is widely used for thromboprophylaxis in pregnancy when

If cytoreductive therapy is required, interferon is the only 
safe option, and observational data suggest it may be 
associated with a higher live birth rate [42]. Management 
strategies for thrombosis and bleeding in the context of 
ET and pregnancy are also discussed. 
 
Conclusion 
While ET is a relatively indolent MPN, the disease can be 
associated with significant morbidity and mortality due to 
thrombotic and hemorrhagic complications. Accurate 
diagnosis, comprehensive molecular profiling, and risk -
adapted management strategies are essential to optimizing 
outcomes for patients with ET. Treatment is primarily 
focused on reducing the risk of thrombotic events, and the 
selection of specific cytoreductive and antiplatelet 
therapies should be informed by individualized risk 
assessment based on factors such as age, prior thrombosis, 
cardiovascular risk factors, and the presence of driver 
mutations like JAK2 V617F. Emerging therapies, 
including novel targeted agents, hold promise for 
expanding the therapeutic arsenal and further improving 
the care and quality of life for patients with ET [43 –45]. 
Moreover, a multidisciplinary approach involving close 
collaboration between hematologists, primary care 
providers, and other relevant specialists is crucial for 
providing comprehensive, patient -centered care for this 
complex condition.
