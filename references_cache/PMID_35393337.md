# FXR1-related congenital myopathy: expansion of the clinical and genetic spectrum.
**Authors:** Mroczek M, Longman C, Farrugia ME, Kapetanovic Garcia S, Ardicli D, Topaloglu H, Hernández-Laín A, Orhan D, Alikasifoglu M, Duff J, Specht S, Nowak K, Ravenscroft G, Chao K, Valivullah Z, Donkervoort S, Saade D, Bönnemann C, Straub V, Yoon G
**Journal:** J Med Genet (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1136/jmedgenet-2021-108341](https://doi.org/10.1136/jmedgenet-2021-108341)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Biallelic pathogenic variants in FXR1 have recently been associated with two congenital myopathy phenotypes: a severe form associated with hypotonia, long bone fractures, respiratory insufficiency and infantile death, and a milder form characterised by proximal muscle weakness with survival into adulthood.

OBJECTIVE: We report eight patients from four unrelated families with biallelic pathogenic variants in exon 15 of FXR1.

METHODS: Whole exome sequencing was used to detect variants in FXR1.

RESULTS: Common clinical features were noted for all patients, which included proximal myopathy, normal serum creatine kinase levels and diffuse muscle atrophy with relative preservation of the quadriceps femoris muscle on muscle imaging. Additionally, some patients with FXR1-related myopathy had respiratory involvement and required bilevel positive airway pressure support. Muscle biopsy showed multi-minicores and type I fibre predominance with internalised nuclei.

CONCLUSION: FXR1-related congenital myopathy is an emerging entity that is clinically recognisable. Phenotypic variability associated with variants in FXR1 can result from differences in variant location and type and is also observed between patients homozygous for the same variant, rendering specific genotype-phenotype correlations difficult. Our work broadens the phenotypic spectrum of FXR1-related congenital myopathy.

FXR1 encodes the Fragile X mental retardation syndrome-related protein 1 (FXR1P), which has RNA binding properties and a high degree of similarity to two other genes encoding proteins from the same family, FXR2 and FMR1.[1,2] FXR1 undergoes alternative splicing which is both tissue-specific and dependent on developmental stage. There are at least seven different genetic isoforms of FXR1 deriving from alternative splicing of exons 12, 13, 15 and 16.[1,3,4,5,6] Longer isoforms expressed in mature cardiac and skeletal muscles contain an additional exon 15 (81bp). Myoblasts express short and medium FXR1 isoforms (ranging from 70 to 80 kDa), whereas myotubes mainly express longer isoforms (82–84 kDa).[4,7] The balance of FXR1 isoforms is altered in a number of neuromuscular disorders, among them myotonic dystrophy[8] and facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy.[4] On the subcellular level FXR1 is located in the Z-band and in costameres.[3]

Pathogenic variants in exon 15 of FXR1 are associated with two congenital myopathy phenotypes in humans: proximal myopathy with multi-minicore lesions (MIM# 618823) and a more severe myopathy with respiratory insufficiency, multiple fractures and death in infancy (MIM# 618822).[9] Herein, we provide detailed clinical characterization of eight patients from four families of different ethnic origins and expand the genetic and clinical spectrum of patients with bi-allelic pathogenic variants in FXR1.

Two siblings from Family A were evaluated at the Queen Elizabeth University Hospital in Glasgow, United Kingdom. A single patient from Family B was identified at the Hospital de Basurto in Bilbao, Spain. Two siblings from Family C were evaluated at Hacettepe University Children’s Hospital in Ankara, Turkey. Three siblings from Family D were evaluated at the Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto, Canada and were previously reported.[9]

Whole exome sequencing (WES) for AII:2 and BII:1 was performed at the Broad Institute (Cambridge, USA) using Standard Germline Exome version 5, with Sample prep (Illumina Nextera), hybrid capture (Illumina Rapid Capture Enrichment 37Mb target), sequencing (Illumina, 150bp paired end reads) and confirmation of variants and segregation was performed by Sanger sequencing. WES for the proband in Family C was performed at the Australian Genome Research Facility (Illumina, SureSelect V6, 150bp paired end reads). WES for Family D was carried out as previously described.[9]

The proband, AII:2, was born naturally at term to healthy first-degree cousins of British origin following an uneventful pregnancy. Developmental history was significant for gross motor delay; she walked well after 1 year and was always slower than her peers at school. Her course remained stable until her late teens when she developed left foot drop and lost the ability to run and jump. At last evaluation at age 45 years she could walk 100 m and climb stairs using one handrail. She had mild joint laxity (elbow, thumbs, distal interphalangeal joints) and bilateral scapular winging, but no contractures, spinal rigidity or scoliosis. There was hypertrophy of the anterior thigh and the calf muscles, and she had marked weakness of the neck flexors, extensors and the tibialis anterior muscles in addition to generalized weakness of her upper extremities.

AII:1 is the elder brother of AII:2, who has a longstanding history of being slower to run and jump than his peers. His clinical course was similar to his affected sister; it was stable until his 40s when climbing stairs became more difficult. At age 58 years he was still ambulatory and able to climb stairs independently. He had no respiratory symptoms.

BII:1 is a 53 year old female born at term following an uneventful pregnancy to healthy non-consanguineous parents from Galicia, Spain. She was hypotonic at birth, sat at 1 year, and walked after 2 years. During childhood she was able to walk and run with difficulty, and at 8 years she was noted to have flat feet, progressive scoliosis and kyphosis, prominent proximal muscular atrophy with limb girdle distribution of weakness, scapular winging, calf hypertrophy and joint laxity of her ankles and wrists. During late childhood, initial respiratory symptoms of exertional dyspnea were observed. She developed severe respiratory dysfunction at 20 years and required temporary ventilatory support, and was subsequently started on non-invasive motion ventilation (NIMV) with bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP). Physical examination revealed generalized neck and proximal upper and lower limb weakness, with involvement of the tibialis anterior muscles bilaterally. Additionally, she had scapular winging, prominent scoliosis and a rigid spine.

CIII:1 was a 17 year old girl born to consanguineous Turkish parents at 42+3 weeks following an uneventful pregnancy. She was hypotonic at birth and initially required oxygen due to respiratory distress. Reduced antigravity movements were also observed. At 7 months she was noted to have prominent axial hypotonia and cervical and proximal muscle weakness. She sat at 2 years and at 5 years she was able to walk short distances with support. At the age of 6 years, thoracic scoliosis was noted. Her clinical course was characterized by slow deterioration, and although she continued to be mobile with support for shorter distances, from the age of 7 years she became increasingly reliant on a wheelchair for longer distances. She had poor head control, axial hypotonia and proximal weakness predominantly in her lower limbs. She had mild contractures of her elbows and ankles bilaterally. She had frequent respiratory infections requiring hospitalization (4–5 times/year) and developed a progressive thoracic scoliosis, for which she had scoliosis surgery at 12 years. She passed away at age 17 years due to a respiratory infection complicated by bacterial sepsis.

CIII:3 is the younger brother of CIII:1. He was born at 38+1 weeks with a birth weight of 3,550 g following an uneventful pregnancy. He was hypotonic and cyanotic at birth, and required nasal oxygen due to respiratory distress in the first week of life. He sat independently at 12 months and requires assistance to stand, he is unable to walk. On physical examination at 2 years 3 months, he had normal extraocular movements, weakness of the neck muscles and axial hypotonia with reduced lower extremity movements and absent deep tendon reflexes. There was no ptosis, facial weakness or joint contractures.

DII:3 is a 28 year old male with longstanding proximal muscle weakness. He was born at 26 weeks following a normal pregnancy to healthy parents of mixed Argentinian/Turkish origin. His birth weight was 2.6 kg. He had bilateral cryptorchidism and hypotonia at birth. He had two episodes of lower respiratory tract infection and apnea in the first month of life, but otherwise was well. Developmental history was significant for gross motor delay; he sat at 1 year and 9 months and walked at 2 years and 3 months. He had proximal muscle weakness and difficulties getting up from a chair, transferring from a standing position to the couch and climbing stairs. He had moderate obstructive sleep apnea and is treated with nocturnal BiPAP, with variable compliance. He has been diagnosed with depression but has declined treatment with anti-depressants. On examination he had weakness of his neck flexors and extensors, proximal upper and lower extremity weakness, calf hypertrophy, mild thoracolumbar scoliosis, but no spinal rigidity.

DII:4 is a 26 year old male with proximal muscle weakness. He was born following a normal pregnancy at term via Caesarean section for prolonged labour. He was hypotonic at birth, but otherwise had a normal neonatal course and there were no feeding or respiratory difficulties. He sat at 8–9 months, crawled after 12 months, and walked after 18 months. Currently he is independent for activities of daily living including cutting food, handling utensils, dressing and personal hygiene, but is slow. He has been diagnosed with depression and is treated with cognitive behavioural therapy after failing multiple trials of anti-depressants. Physical examination revealed proximal lower and upper extremity weakness, mild calf hypertrophy, a positive Gowers sign and a Trendelenburg gait. He had no ocular involvement, facial weakness, scoliosis or scapular winging.

DII:5 is a 24 year old female. She was born following a normal pregnancy via elective Caesarean section. She was hypotonic at birth but otherwise had no feeding, cardiac or respiratory issues. Her development was characterized by gross motor delays; she sat at 8 months, walked at 22 months, and was able to climb stairs at 4–5 years. Currently, at age 24 years, she has difficulty getting up and out of bed, climbing upstairs, or rising from the floor or from chairs. She is unable to walk quickly, run, jump, or skip. She had mild obstructive sleep apnea but never required BiPAP treatment. She has been diagnosed with bipolar disorder, social anxiety disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, attention deficit disorder, has a past history of bulimia and is under the care of a psychiatrist. Brain MRI and MRS were normal. Physical examination revealed weakness of the neck and proximal muscles of the upper and lower extremities, a positive Gowers sign and a mild Trendelenburg gait.

Further clinical details of all patients are presented in Figure 1 and Table 1.

Both individuals from family A and the patient from Family B were found to be homozygous for a single nucleotide duplication in exon 15 of FXR1 Chr3:180971083 G>GA (hg 38), (XM_005247813.3):c.1707dupA; p.Pro570Thrfs*7. Patients CIII:1 and CIII:3 were homozygous for a frameshift variant in exon 15 of FXR1 Chr3:180971082 T>TG (hg 38), (XM_005247813.3):c.1699dupG; p.(Glu567Glyfs*10). In family D, a homozygous single nucleotide deletion also in exon 15 of FXR1 Chr3:180971083 GA>G (hg38), (XM_005247813.3): c.1707delA; p.Lys569Asnfs*57 was found in the three affected siblings. For all variants, no homozygous individuals have been reported in control populations (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/, Supplemental Figure 2) and the asymptomatic parents, when available, were confirmed to be heterozygous carriers of the variants (Figure 1, Supplemental Figure 4). Additional details regarding the variants are available in Supplemental Figures 1–5.

A muscle biopsy was performed in individuals from all four families; AII:2, BII:1, CIII:1 and DII:4. In all patients the muscle biopsy analysis revealed prominent myopathic changes with multi-minicores, increased internal nuclei and type I fibre predominance. Additionally, in the muscle biopsy of AII:2, CIII:1 and DII:4, multiple core-like areas in some fibres had irregular outlines and were eccentric, resembling moth-eaten fibres. The muscle biopsy of BII:1 showed marked fatty replacement with mild endomysial fibrosis and many ring fibres. There was mild variability in fibre size and internal nuclei, but no necrotic or regenerative fibres. In the biopsy from CIII:1 there was prominent fatty replacement, fibre size variability and an increase in perimysial fibrous tissue. Electron microscopy (EM) for BII:1 and DII:4 revealed focal disruptions in the myofibrillar striation pattern with Z-band streaming and absence of mitochondria, consistent with multi-minicores.

Muscle MRI was performed for 3 individuals, AII:2, BII:1 and DII:5, and demonstrated variable muscle involvement at the advanced stage of disease, with the sartorius muscle being more severely affected than the gracilis muscle. Relative sparing of the quadriceps femoris muscle was noted (Supplemental Figure 6).

Bi-allelic variants in exon 15 of FXR1 were initially reported in two unrelated families and were associated with two distinct myopathic phenotypes: one severe leading to neonatal death and the other a much milder proximal myopathy. This variability was due to different variants having differential effects on protein expression and stability, generation of abnormal FXR1 isoforms and accumulation of abnormal granular structures, sequestration of mRNA, interruption of normal transport, transcriptional handling, and translation of mRNAs, and altered gene expression.[9] In addition to detailed clinical descriptions of patients with previously reported p.Lys569Asnfs*57 FXR1 variants, we report three patients from two families with a novel homozygous p.Pro570Thrfs*7 variant and two siblings with a novel homozygous p.Glu567Glyfs*10 variant. All variants are frameshifts predicted to cause premature truncation of the protein and are localized to the 5’ end of exon 15 of the muscle isoform of FXR1.

We suggest that FXR1-related congenital multi-minicore myopathy is a clinically recognizable entity, with the following core clinical features: neonatal hypotonia, weakness of the neck muscles, proximal myopathy with a predominantly limb girdle distribution of muscle weakness, normal serum creatinine kinase levels, myopathic EMG, multi-minicores and internalized nuclei on muscle biopsy and diffuse muscle atrophy with relative preservation of the quadriceps femoris muscle on MRI. Respiratory involvement was variable, and while some patients with FXR1-related myopathy had respiratory insufficiency requiring BiPAP support, this was not consistent even between patients homozygous for the same pathogenic FXR1 variant. Other clinical features observed to varying degrees among the cohort were weakness of the tibialis anterior muscle, calf hypertrophy, scoliosis, joint laxity, contractures, and psychiatric symptoms. No patient had weakness of the face, including extra ocular muscles, or cardiac involvement.

A recent study suggests a role of FXR1 in the pathophysiology of both sporadic and C9orf72-and FUS-linked forms of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Both FXR1 and FXR2 were found to interact directly with ALS-related microRNAs via GDCGG motif-specific binding, and FXR1 was found specifically to co-aggregate with FUS in motor neurons. This implies a broader role for the family of Fragile X-related proteins in neuromuscular diseases beyond structural myopathies.[10] Variants in FXR1 have been associated with several neuropsychiatric diseases, notably insomnia, bipolar disorder and schizophrenia.[11, 12] In addition, differential expression of FXR1 was reported in patients with schizophrenia.[13] All three patients from family D had significant psychiatric manifestations (depression, bipolar disorder, social anxiety disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, attention deficit disorder, bulimia), however, the association of psychiatric manifestations with FXR1 pathogenic variants requires further study.

Molecular testing for FXR1 should be considered for patients with a clinical diagnosis of congenital myopathy, especially if cores are observed on muscle biopsy. The inclusion of FXR1 in neuromuscular gene panels will contribute to establishing the diagnosis in more patients. Clinical evaluation of a larger number of patients with pathogenic variants in FXR1 will further our understanding of the function of the gene and the role of the muscle-specific isoforms in human disease.
