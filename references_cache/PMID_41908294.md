# Possible association between the microbiota in subgingival and atherosclerotic plaque in a cohort of patients with carotid stenosis.
**Authors:** Schulz S, Börner S, Bitter K, Gheit H, Partsakhashvili J, Ukkat J, Misiak D, Reichert S
**Journal:** J Oral Microbiol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1080/20002297.2026.2648325](https://doi.org/10.1080/20002297.2026.2648325)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Periodontal pathogens have been implicated in systemic diseases, including atherosclerosis. This study investigates the relationship between subgingival and microbial colonization of carotid plaque in patients undergoing carotid endarterectomy (DRKS00021598).

METHODS: Microbial samples from both subgingival and carotid plaques were collected from 25 hospitalized patients. Preoperative all patients underwent periodontal examination. The microbial composition was assessed using metagenomics sequencing of 16S rRNA gene (V3/V4 regions).

RESULTS: Bacterial DNA was detected in both subgingival and carotid plaque samples. The microbial composition differed between both communities. Alpha diversity was significantly higher in subgingival samples than in carotid plaques (p = 0.039). Beta diversity analysis, (including unweighted UniFrac (p < 0.001), linear discriminant analysis, principal component analysis) confirmed significant differences between subgingival and carotid plaque microbiota. The study revealed overlaps in a few individual cases.

CONCLUSIONS: These findings show that carotid plaque microbiota differ from subgingival communities and are not exclusively of oral origin, suggesting additional systemic sources.

Epidemiological evidence indicates a relationship between cardiovascular diseases and periodontitis, a link that is further substantiated by biological plausibility [1–3].

Internal carotid artery stenosis develops primarily as a consequence of atherosclerotic plaques at the carotid bifurcation. Similar to atherosclerosis, established risk factors for carotid stenosis include high blood pressure, age, male gender, dyslipidaemia, diabetes mellitus and smoking [4,5]. Furthermore, chronic inflammation plays a central role in the development and progression of carotid stenosis.

Microorganisms have been proposed as potential inflammatory triggers: bacterial DNA and viable bacteria have been detected in human carotid atherosclerotic plaques, including well-known oral periodontopathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans [6]. These findings suggest that recurrent transient bacteremia, e.g. those associated with periodontal disease, may allow oral microbes to translocate into the vascular system and contribute to plaque colonisation [7,8]. However, comparative sequencing studies demonstrate that microbial DNA is present in both subgingival biofilm and carotid plaques, though the communities differ in composition, suggesting selective translocation or enrichment rather than direct overlap [8,9]. Consensus statements and meta-analyses further support an epidemiological and biological link between periodontitis, systemic inflammation and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, providing plausibility for microbial involvement in carotid atherosclerosis [2,3].

Taken together, these considerations provide a strong biological plausibility for microbial involvement in the pathogenesis of carotid atherosclerosis and underscore the importance of studying oral–vascular microbial interactions in patients with carotid stenosis.

Although initial studies have investigated a potential association between the oral microbiota and the bacterial presence within atherosclerotic plaques, these studies were limited either by very small sample sizes (n = 5 [8], n = 4 [9], n = 4 [10], n = 15 [11], or by analysing only the microbiota within atherosclerotic plaques without a direct comparison to the subgingival environment [7,12].

Therefore, in the present study, we investigated the bacterial composition of both carotid artery plaques and subgingival biofilm samples. The novelty of our work lies not merely in detecting bacteria in carotid plaques, but in the ecological and quantitative comparison of paired subgingival and carotid microbiota within the same individuals undergoing endarterectomy (n = 25). Using 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing (V3–V4 region), we intended to investigate whether the oral microbiota and the microbiota of the internal carotid artery stenosis exhibit similarities.

Patients were enrolled in the study between February 2019 and June 2023 at the Department of Visceral, Vascular and Endocrine Surgery, University Hospital Halle (Saale). Eligible patients were required to present with internal carotid artery stenosis fulfilling the surgical indication for operative therapy, defined according to NASCET as ≥70% stenosis in asymptomatic cases or ≥50% in symptomatic cases [13]. In addition, patients were required to have at least four natural teeth or intraosseous implants to allow for appropriate dental examination. Exclusion criteria comprised fewer than four teeth or implants, receipt of subgingival periodontal therapy within the previous six months, antibiotic treatment within the last three months, or pregnancy. Anamnestic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, smoking status and body mass index, as well as medical history (e.g. diabetes mellitus, hypertension, peripheral arterial disease and dyslipoproteinemia), were documented (Table 1).

The classification of periodontitis was recognised according to the criteria of the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) working group [14] and in accordance with the consensus report of the 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions [15].

According to the 2017 World Workshop classification, periodontitis is graded based on the evaluation of primary criteria and modifiers, with progression classified as A, B or C. Long-term data on attachment loss within five years were unavailable. For ethical reasons, X-rays could not be taken, meaning that the bone loss index could not be determined. In addition, HbA1c values were rarely available for patients with diabetes. For these reasons, it was not possible to reliably assign the periodontitis cases to a progression grade. Therefore, periodontal classification was restricted to staging (I–IV), which can be reliably determined based on clinical attachment loss, pocket depth and tooth loss alone.

In line with CDC classification, severe periodontitis was defined when at least two interproximal sites with clinical attachment loss (CAL) ≥ 6 mm (not on the same tooth) and at least one interproximal site with probing depth (PD) ≥ 5 mm were present. Moderate periodontitis was diagnosed when at least two interproximal sites with CAL ≥ 4 mm (not on the same tooth) or at least two interproximal sites with PD ≥ 5 mm (not on the same tooth) could be determined. Cases not meeting these criteria were classified as mild or absent periodontitis.

According to the new classification framework, disease stages I to IV were assigned based on severity (defined by the extent of interdental clinical attachment loss and tooth loss), complexity, and extent and distribution. Since radiographs were not available, radiographic bone loss could not be assessed, and grading (A–C) of periodontitis could therefore not be applied (Table 1).

The clinical dental examination involved determining the plaque index and bleeding index at six sites around each tooth. Measurements of maximal probing depth (PD, defined as the distance from the gingival margin to the base of the pocket) and maximal clinical attachment loss (CAL, defined as the distance from the cemento-enamel junction to the base of the pocket) and bleeding on probing (BOP) were obtained at six sites per tooth using a pressure-sensitive periodontal probe UNC 15 (0.2 N; Aesculap, Tuttlingen, Germany). In addition, the proportion of non-bleeding shallow sites (PPD ≤ 4 mm without BOP), as defined by the current classification of periodontitis (Papapanou et al., 2017), was recorded. A single calibrated examiner conducted all periodontal assessments.

Subgingival plaque samples were collected from all teeth with a probing depth of at least 4 mm, using sterile curettes. Prior to sampling, the supragingival biofilm was removed from all test teeth. Prior to sampling, the supragingival biofilm was removed from all of the test teeth. This was followed by relative drying using cotton rolls. Carotid plaque specimens were obtained intraoperatively under sterile conditions immediately during endarterectomy, placed in sterile tubes and frozen at –80 °C until DNA processing.

Subgingival and carotid artery samples were disrupted using the gentleMACS™ Octo Dissociator. Microbial DNA was isolated from both samples with the AllPrep® Bact. DNA/RNA/Protein Kit (Qiagen) according to manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, bacterial cell walls were digested using a lysozyme-based enzymatic lysis buffer, followed by treatment with proteinase K and a chaotropic lysis buffer to completely release nucleic acids and inactivate nucleases. The resulting lysate was transferred onto an AllPrep DNA spin column, which selectively binds DNA under high-salt conditions. After several washing steps to remove proteins, lipids and other contaminants, genomic DNA was eluted in nuclease-free buffer.

DNA quantity and integrity were assessed using Qubit fluorometry and Bioanalyzer electrophoresis before sequencing (shipment to Novogene; Cambridge, UK).

Sequencing was performed by Novogene Co. Ltd. (Cambridge, UK) on the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform (250 bp paired-end reads) (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

The V3/V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified and purified, followed by library preparation with the NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

The data analysis was done by Novogene Co. Ltd. (Cambridge UK) bioinformatics service as described below.

Pre-processing and quality filtering of raw sequence reads were performed using the QIIME2 workflow (version 2022.02; http://qiime2.org, accessed 03 September 2025). Raw paired-end reads were quality filtered, trimmed, denoised and dereplicated using the DADA2 plugin (version 1.16), which resolves sequences at single-nucleotide resolution and generates amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) rather than operational taxonomic units.

Taxonomic assignment of ASVs was performed using a Naïve Bayes classifier trained on the SILVA 138.1 reference database. Taxonomic classifications are considered reliable at the genus level and above, while species-level assignments were reported only where sufficient confidence was achieved, in accordance with current limitations of V3–V4 16S rRNA gene sequencing.

Relative abundances of major taxa were calculated.

Alpha diversity (Shannon index) and beta diversity (UniFrac distances) were analysed in QIIME2, visualised in R (ggplot2) (Version 4.0.0) and compared with LEfSe (Galaxy platform: http://galaxy.biobakery.org/root?tool_id=LEfSe_run, 03.09.2025).

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 28.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Normality was assessed with Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Alpha (Shannon index) and beta diversity (UniFrac) were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction (5%).

However, rare overlaps of individual species were detected in both sites (Fusobacterium nucleatum (3 patients), Prevotella oris (1 patient), Treponema socranskii (1 patient), Capnocytophaga gingivalis (1 patient), Candidatus Saccharimonas aalbogensis (formerly TM7, 3 patients), Schaalia odontolytica (1patient) Veillonella parvula (1 patient) and Actinomyc. sp. oral taxon (2 patients)). Nevertheless, bacterial abundance in carotid plaque was much lower, indicating these overlaps are isolated events.

We compared the microbiota of carotid plaques and subgingival sites in patients undergoing carotid endarterectomy. 16S rRNA sequencing revealed detectable bacterial DNA at both sites, with subgingival samples showing higher α-diversity and Bacteroidota dominance, while carotid plaques had lower α-diversity and were enriched in Proteobacteria. β-diversity analyses confirmed clear separation, indicating distinct microbial communities in oral and vascular tissues.

Unlike previous reports, our study provides several key advances that extend current knowledge on oral-vascular microbial relationship. By analysing paired subgingival and carotid plaque samples from 25 patients undergoing carotid endarterectomy, we enabled direct within-patient comparisons of oral and vascular microbiota, overcoming a major limitation of earlier studies that often focused on plaque samples alone or relied on very small paired cohorts. Beyond descriptive taxonomic profiling, we applied a comprehensive ecological framework—including alpha diversity metrics, UniFrac-based beta diversity analyses, LEfSe and cladogram-based approaches—to demonstrate not only compositional differences but also clear community-level ecological separation between oral and vascular niches. Importantly, we further quantified the frequency and abundance of shared taxa and show that oral bacteria appear in carotid plaques only sporadically and at very low relative abundance. This quantitative assessment provides strong support for the concept that vascular microbial signatures do not simply reflect passive spill-over from periodontal pockets but instead represent selective and infrequent colonisation events. Together, these contributions clearly distinguish our findings from previous studies [16–18] and highlight the added value of a cohort-based, paired ecological analysis of oral and vascular microbiota. The predominance of Bacteroidota in subgingival samples is consistent with previous studies on periodontal microbiota, where genera such as Porphyromonas, Tannerella and Prevotella are considered major drivers of periodontitis [19]. In contrast, carotid plaques were dominated by Proteobacteria, which are commonly associated with systemic infections and vascular colonisation [11,19]. This distinct enrichment suggests that while oral pathogens may occasionally translocate into the bloodstream, the vascular microenvironment selectively favours taxa that are adapted to survival under oxidative and inflammatory conditions [20].

The observed reduction in α-diversity, reflecting lower within-sample richness and evenness, within carotid plaques parallels earlier findings reporting that atherosclerotic lesions harbour less complex microbial communities compared to oral or gut microbiota [21]. This restricted diversity may reflect ecological filtering within vascular tissue, where only a subset of bacteria can persist. In contrast, the subgingival niche supports a highly diverse and structured biofilm community. Similar reductions in α-diversity have been reported in coronary atherosclerosis, where microbial DNA signatures differed markedly from oral profiles and showed dominance of Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria [21]. Together, these findings reinforce the hypothesis that atherosclerotic plaque colonisation is not a direct mirror of oral dysbiosis but involves selective enrichment and adaptation to a distinct ecological niche.

The distinct β-diversity, which captures between-sample differences in community composition, further supports the notion that oral and vascular microbiota represent separate microbial ecosystems. As illustrated in Figure 3C, the weighted UniFrac–based PCoA shows a tight clustering of carotid plaque samples, reflecting low within-group variability, reduced diversity and a conserved community structure across patients. In contrast, subgingival samples display wide dispersion, consistent with high interpersonal and site-specific variability characteristic of the oral biofilm. The clear spatial separation between oral and vascular samples indicates systematic ecological divergence rather than random overlap between the two microbiota. While transient bacteremia originating from periodontal pockets remains a plausible mechanism of microbial entry to circulation [22], the limited compositional overlap suggests that additional systemic sources, including gut translocation or respiratory microbiota [23], may contribute to the vascular microbial signal. Moreover, microbial components such as lipopolysaccharide may promote vascular inflammation via activation of Toll-like receptors and the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby contributing to plaque progression and instability independent of persistent bacterial colonisation [24,25].

Our findings are in line with epidemiological studies linking periodontitis to an increased risk of cardiovascular disease [26,27], but they also highlight that vascular microbiota are not simple reflections of oral microbial profiles. Instead, atherosclerotic lesions appear to harbour distinct ecological niches, shaped by systemic exposure and local conditions. These results support the concept that microbial involvement in carotid stenosis is complex, involving contributions from multiple sources rather than exclusively from the oral cavity.

Occasional overlaps were observed in a few individuals. The taxa we observed in both sites are biologically plausible candidates for episodic translocation. Fusobacterium nucleatum and Prevotella oris key drivers of periodontitis, were sporadically detected in carotid plaques, supporting episodic translocation [8,28,29]. Capnocytophaga gingivalis and Candidatus Saccharimonas aalborgensis (TM7) act as opportunistic taxa enriched in dysbiotic biofilms; while their role in vascular lesions is unclear, their presence may reflect stochastic translocation rather than stable colonisation [30,31]. T. socranskii has been associated with periodontitis severity, but its vascular role remains poorly characterised [32].

Therefore, the observed sporadic overlap pattern is best explained by rare, random translocation events rather than stable, widespread colonisation of vascular tissue by subgingival microbiota [12].

The relatively small sample size limits the generalisability of the results, and the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. With regard to the rare overlaps, one consideration mitigates strong causal conclusions. A bias in the results may be due to low biomass. Atherosclerotic tissue often contains very little bacterial DNA, making the sequence data susceptible to contamination from reagents/kits and sample carryover. This may lead to an exaggerated apparent correlation between oral and vascular disease [33].

16S rRNA sequencing detects DNA but not microbial viability or activity. Because sequencing was based on the V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene, species-level identification cannot be guaranteed with high confidence, particularly for closely related taxa, as previously shown by Johnson et al. [34]. Therefore, most interpretations were made at phylum-to-genus level, and species-level overlaps must be interpreted cautiously. Future studies should complement amplicon sequencing with targeted qPCR or shotgun metagenomics to validate and quantify key taxa such as Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas spp., which cannot be reliably resolved at species level by V3–V4 16S rRNA sequencing alone. Longitudinal studies using these approaches are needed to clarify functional roles and causal links.

In summary, this study demonstrates clear microbial differences between subgingival sites and carotid plaques, with Bacteroidota dominating the oral microbiota and Proteobacteria enriched in vascular tissue. However, inflammation without persistent colonisation remains a plausible mechanism for carotid artery atherosclerosis: epidemiological and mechanistic syntheses (e.g. the AHA statement) support a link between periodontitis and atherosclerotic vascular disease via systemic inflammatory pathways, but do not conclude that vascular plaques typically harbour a stable, oral-like microbiota [35].

These findings support the notion that the carotid plaque microbiota is not solely derived from oral sources but may also reflect systemic microbial translocation and local ecological selection. Understanding the origins and functions of the vascular microbiota may provide new insights into the pathogenesis of carotid stenosis and offer potential avenues for preventive or therapeutic interventions.
