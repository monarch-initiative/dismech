# Tertiary Syphilitic Aortitis Manifesting as Ascending Aortic Aneurysm and Dissection.
**Authors:** de Almeida Gripp E, Araujo E Silva AE, Jácome BZ, Lourenço BN, Pires de Vasconcelos SM, Carestiato LR, Serafim da Silveira J, Navarro VR, Pereira Dantas CE, Zakhia de Seixas A
**Journal:** JACC Case Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jaccas.2026.108453](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaccas.2026.108453)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Aortic dissection in tertiary syphilis is rare, typically precluded by the dense medial fibrosis characteristic of syphilitic aneurysms.

CASE SUMMARY: A 42-year-old woman presented with dyspnea, weight loss, and syncope. Echocardiography revealed a large ascending aortic aneurysm associated with dissection and intramural hematoma, with severe aortic regurgitation and a tricuspid valve. Computed tomography confirmed the dissection. Serological screening was positive for both HIV and syphilis. The patient underwent surgical intervention, with a favorable clinical outcome.

DISCUSSION: This case illustrates the resurgence of syphilis, a life-threatening cardiovascular complication. Progression to dissection represents an atypical clinical trajectory for syphilitic aortitis. The active inflammatory phase may paradoxically weaken the aortic wall and facilitating dissection. Syphilis prevalence is increasing worldwide, necessitating clinical vigilance.

TAKE-HOME MESSAGE: The identification of an aortic aneurysm in young patients, particularly in the presence of a tricuspid aortic valve, should prompt immediate screening for syphilis and other infectious etiologies.

A 42-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with worsening exertional dyspnea and fatigue, which had progressed over the preceding months. Three months before admission, she experienced an episode of acute precordial pain associated with nausea and syncope; although emergency care was sought at that time, no diagnostic investigations were performed. She also reported a significant unintentional weight loss of 6 kg over the past 6 months.

Upon physical examination, the patient exhibited a wide pulse pressure (110/40 mm Hg) and a heart rate of 78 beats/min. Precordial palpation revealed a sustained apical impulse, displaced inferiorly and laterally. Cardiac auscultation demonstrated a loud (grade 4/6) holodiastolic murmur, loudest at the aortic area, accompanied by a palpable thrill. Neurological assessment was unremarkable, with no evidence of focal deficits, aphasia, or sensory disturbances.

The patient had no significant past medical history.

Given her presenting symptoms, aortic syndromes were considered. However, in view of her young age, potential causes included large-vessel vasculitis such as Takayasu arteritis and giant cell arteritis.

Doppler analysis demonstrated severe aortic regurgitation, characterized by an eccentric, anteriorly directed jet. This was attributed to significant distortion of the aortic root architecture. To further characterize the anatomy and the mechanism of regurgitation, transesophageal echocardiography was performed. The examination confirmed a tricuspid aortic valve and a large ascending aortic aneurysm complicated by dissection and hematoma (Videos 5 and 6, Figures 6A and 6B). The mechanism of aortic regurgitation was attributed to impaired cusp coaptation resulting from distortion of the aortic annulus and commissures caused by a large ascending aortic aneurysm, combined with restriction of one of the cusps, explaining the eccentric jet (Video 7, Figure 7). (B) Midesophageal short-axis view confirming a trileaflet aortic valve morphology. Cardiac computed tomography demonstrated a massive ascending aortic aneurysm, measuring 8.1 × 7.5 cm, complicated by an acute dissection and partial thrombosis of the false lumen. The dissection flap originated at the sinotubular junction and extended distally to the iliac arteries (Video 8, Figures 8A and 8B). Multiple reentry tears (fenestrations) between the true and false lumens were identified, with the false lumen causing significant hemodynamic compression of the true lumen throughout the thoracic and abdominal segments. Three-dimensional volume rendering reconstruction was performed to further delineate the spatial relationship of the lumens and the extent of the aneurysm (Figure 9). Serological evaluation confirmed HIV infection and syphilis, with a venereal disease research laboratory test titer of 1:128 and positive treponemal testing (fluorescent treponemal antibody absorption test), substantiating a diagnosis of tertiary syphilitic aortitis. Laboratory work-up revealed significant immunosuppression, with a CD4+ T-cell count of 158 cells/mm3 and an HIV-1 viral load of 145,000 copies/mL. Inflammatory markers were also elevated, including a C-reactive protein of 48 mg/L, consistent with active large-vessel vasculitis.

Therapy with benzathine penicillin G was promptly initiated. Concomitantly, antiretroviral therapy was started despite the low CD4+ count, in accordance with current guidelines for advanced HIV infection.

Given the diagnosis of a Stanford type A aortic dissection—a life-threatening emergency—urgent surgical intervention was indicated.

Surgical access was obtained via median sternotomy. Upon entry, the mediastinum presented significant inflammatory changes, characterized by extensive adhesions and fibrotic scarring. Meticulous dissection of the heart and the ascending aorta followed, revealing an aneurysmal vessel with an associated intramural hematoma.

The resection encompassed the entire ascending aorta and the aortic arch up to zone 1, terminating at the origin of the left common carotid artery. The sinotubular junction served as the proximal margin; although the aortic root was nondissected and maintained a preserved diameter, the tissue appeared macroscopically diseased.

Intraoperative exploration revealed marked induration and thickening of the ascending aorta, alongside extensive fibrosis involving the coronary arteries. Ostial coronaries were scarred and thickened. A 30-mm straight Dacron graft was used to replace the segment between the aortic arch (zone 1) and the sinotubular junction. Subsequently, the brachiocephalic trunk was reimplanted using a 10-mm straight Dacron interposition graft, restoring cephalic perfusion (Figures 10A and 10B). (B) Gross specimen showing significant mural thickening of the ascending aorta and the massive dimensions of the aneurysmal sac.

Despite the surgical complexity, the patient's postoperative course was unremarkable. She was discharged on postoperative day 20 to complete a prolonged course of intravenous antibiotic therapy for tertiary syphilis. Predischarge echocardiography demonstrated a well-functioning aortic graft with no residual aortic regurgitation. At the 6-month follow-up, she remained clinically stable with significant improvement in functional status.

Gross examination of the explanted specimen revealed a significantly thickened aortic wall with the classic “tree-bark” appearance of the intimal surface, secondary to patchy subintimal fibrosis. Histopathological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of syphilitic aortitis, demonstrating hallmark obliterative endarteritis of the vasa vasorum. This was associated with a dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate within the tunica adventitia and extensive fragmentation and destruction of the medial elastic lamellae.

Syphilis, a systemic infection caused by the spirochete Treponema pallidum, remains a significant pathogen among sexually transmitted infections. 1 Despite the availability of effective pharmacotherapy, the global burden remains substantial, with rising prevalence and incidence worldwide. 2 Syphilis facilitates the transmission and acquisition of HIV, while HIV coinfection has been documented to accelerate the natural progression of syphilis. 3

The infection progresses through 4 distinct stages (primary, secondary, latent, and tertiary) and can virtually affect any organ system. Clinical hallmarks include the painless chancre of primary disease or the characteristic maculopapular rash involving the palms and soles in secondary syphilis. 1 , 4 While these early lesions typically resolve spontaneously, leading to a latent phase detectable only through serological testing, approximately one-third of untreated patients progressed to tertiary syphilis in the preantibiotic era. 1 , 4

Tertiary syphilis may manifest as cardiovascular syphilis, specifically syphilitic aortitis. The pathogenesis results from invasion of the aortic adventitia and vasa vasorum. 5 This leads to obliterative endarteritis, medial ischemia, fibrosis, and aneurysm formation, most commonly in the ascending aorta. 5

The progression to aortic dissection in the present case represents an atypical clinical trajectory. 4 Syphilitic aortitis classically results in a fibrotic, calcified vessel wall that is resistant to delamination. 4 Although transverse scarring (the “tree-bark” appearance) usually tethers the aortic layers, the active inflammatory phase, characterized by the deposition of mucopolysaccharides replacing collagen, may paradoxically weaken the structural integrity of the media, facilitating dissection. 6

In this patient, the episode of precordial pain, nausea, and syncope 3 months earlier likely marked the initiation of the intramural hematoma and subsequent dissection. Although the primary cause of death in approximately 80% of untreated cases is the rupture of a saccular aneurysm, our patient presented with a Stanford type A aortic dissection, a life-threatening emergency requiring emergent surgical intervention. 5

Given the global resurgence of syphilis, a high index of clinical suspicion is required. The identification of an aortic aneurysm or dissection in relatively young patients, particularly those with a tricuspid aortic valve and no traditional cardiovascular risk factors, should prompt immediate screening for T pallidum. Early recognition is vital, as the pathogen remains sensitive to penicillin G. Furthermore, in cases of syphilitic aortitis, timely surgical referral for dissection or symptomatic aneurysms is lifesaving and remains the cornerstone of management for advanced cardiovascular manifestations.
