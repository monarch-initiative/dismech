# CIROZ is dispensable in ancestral vertebrates but essential for left-right patterning in humans.
**Authors:** Szenker-Ravi E, Ott T, Yusof A, Chopra M, Khatoo M, Pak B, Xuan Goh W, Beckers A, Brady AF, Ewans LJ, Djaziri N, Almontashiri NAM, Alghamdi MA, Alharby E, Dasouki M, Romo L, Tan WH, Maddirevula S, Alkuraya FS, Giordano JL, Alkelai A, Wapner RJ, Stals K, Alfadhel M, Alswaid AF, Bogusch S, Schafer-Kosulya A, Vogel S, Vick P, Schweickert A, Wakeling M, Moreau de Bellaing A, Alshamsi AM, Sanlaville D, Mbarek H, Saad C, Ellard S, Eisenhaber F, Tripolszki K, Beetz C, Bauer P, Gossler A, Eisenhaber B, Blum M, Bouvagnet P, Bertoli-Avella A, Amiel J, Gordon CT, Reversade B

## Content

Four genes-DAND5, PKD1L1, MMP21, and CIROP-form a genetic module that has specifically evolved in vertebrate species that harbor motile cilia in their left-right organizer (LRO). We find here that CIROZ (previously known as C1orf127) is also specifically expressed in the LRO of mice, frogs, and fish, where it encodes a protein with a signal peptide followed by 3 zona pellucida N domains, consistent with extracellular localization. We report 16 individuals from 10 families with bi-allelic CIROZ inactivation variants, which cause heterotaxy with congenital heart defects. While the knockout of Ciroz in mice also leads to situs anomalies, we unexpectedly find that its targeted inactivation in zebrafish and Xenopus does not lead to observable LR anomalies. Moreover, CIROZ is absent or obsolete in select animals with motile cilia at their LRO, including Carnivora, Atherinomorpha fish, or jawless vertebrates. In summary, this evo-devo study identifies CIROZ as an essential gene for breaking bilateral embryonic symmetry in humans and mice, whereas we witness its contemporary pseudogenization in discrete vertebrate species.

Keywords: CIROZ, C1orf127, evo-devo, laterality, heterotaxy, left-right organizer, CIROP, MMP21, DAND5, PKD1L1, gene loss, pseudogenization, motile cilia, ZP-N, ZP2

During evolution, genes can become obsolete and disappear. Here, we characterize CIROZ, an ancestral gene repeatedly lost in some species of fish, birds, reptiles, and mammals. Despite that, we find that its inactivation in humans causes heterotaxy, indicating that CIROZ is still essential for left-right patterning of the human embryo.

Studies in model vertebrate organisms have established that left-right (LR) asymmetry is initiated during early embryonic development in a transient organ, the LR organizer (LRO; also known as the posterior notochord [PNC]1 in mice or Kupffer’s vesicle [KV]2 in zebrafish). The LRO in mice is a small epithelial structure on the ventral surface of the embryo. Cells in its central region contain motile cilia, which rotate in a clockwise manner to generate a leftward fluid flow, while cells at its periphery contain primary immotile cilia, which are thought to receive and interpret this flow.3,4,5 The molecular mechanisms by which the leftward flow is interpreted are not precisely understood, but several studies indicate the involvement of a mechanosensitive calcium channel composed of PKD2 (polycystic kidney disease 2 or polycystin-2)6,7 and PKD1L1 (PKD protein 1-like 1).8,9 These two transmembrane proteins are localized to immotile cilia, and their stimulation by fluid flow is believed to trigger an influx of calcium on left-specific cells.5,10,11 The key consequence of fluid flow sensing is the left-sided post-transcriptional down-regulation of Dand5 (Dan domain BMP antagonist family member 5), which encodes a secreted inhibitor of the transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) family of ligands, including NODAL.12,13,14 This left-specific repression of DAND5 enables NODAL, whose mRNA is initially symmetrically expressed around the murine LRO, to specifically initiate a signaling cascade on the left side of the embryo. NODAL forms heterodimers with the related molecule GDF1 (growth differentiation factor 1), and the disinhibition of NODAL on the left results in signaling via its cognate TGF-β receptors. NODAL signaling leads to the upregulation of Nodal itself and the Pitx2 (paired like homeodomain 2) transcription factor in the left lateral plate mesoderm (LPM). This signaling cascade establishes a morphogenetic program that results in asymmetrically defined organogenesis referred to as situs solitus.5

Heterotaxy (HTX) is a disorder of laterality in which thoracic and/or abdominal viscera lack their typical pattern of asymmetry (also known as situs ambiguus). HTX is often accompanied by complex congenital heart defects (CHDs), which are the main cause of morbidity and mortality in affected individuals. Situs inversus totalis refers to a complete mirror-image version of the normal anatomical arrangement of thoracic and abdominal viscera, which is referred to as situs solitus. Laterality defects have an estimated prevalence of approximately 1 in 10,000 live births15 but can be underdiagnosed since situs inversus totalis, for example, is asymptomatic.

Chronic interstitial lung disease (CILD; previously known as primary ciliary dyskinesia [PCD]), characterized by chronic respiratory tract infection and infertility, is caused by the bi-allelic alteration of genes implicated in the function of motile cilia, including DNAI1 (MIM: 604366) (CILD1 [MIM: 244400]),16,17,18

We previously noted that MMP21, which is necessary for LR patterning in humans, mice, and zebrafish,44,45,46 displays evidence of pseudogenization in one branch of placental mammals, the Cetartiodactyla, which groups the cetaceans and even-toed ungulates, as well as in birds and reptiles.44 This appears to correlate with the absence of motile cilia in the LRO of these animals,55,56 suggesting alternative mechanisms for the generation of LR asymmetry. Extending this observation to a systematic, genome-wide search for genes with a pattern of evolutionary loss similar to that of MMP21, we recently established that PKD1L1 and DAND5, along with two previously uncharacterized genes, CIROP and CIROZ (previously referred to as C1orf127 [MIM: 619700]), are also absent or show signs of open reading frame (ORF) decay in Cetartiodactyla, birds, and reptiles.54
CIROP, which encodes a metallopeptidase acting downstream of the leftward fluid flow but upstream of DAND5 repression in model organisms with motile cilia at their LRO, was also found to be frequently mutated in individuals with HTX.54

In this study, we report bi-allelic pathogenic variants in CIROZ among 14 individuals with HTX. CIROZ belongs to a syn-expression group containing the four other genes DAND5, PKD1L1, CIROP, and MMP21. CIROZ which stands for ciliated LRO protein containing zona pellucida (ZP)-N domains, encodes a protein that bears homology to the ZP glycoprotein 2 (ZP2). While existing mouse knockout (KO) lines of Ciroz validate its role in LR patterning in rodents,57 we found that its targeted inactivation in zebrafish and Xenopus was without consequence on laterality. Further analysis of the evolutionary trajectory of this gene indicated that it has undergone selective obsolescence in additional vertebrate species compared to the other four members of the group.

The study included 40 individuals from 10 families, of which 16 individuals were affected with LR asymmetry defects; 14 of them harbor bi-allelic loss-of-function variants in CIROZ, and 2 could not be genotyped. Additionally, 2 asymptomatic family members harbor bi-allelic loss-of-function variants in CIROZ. The families originated from the UK (family 1), Lebanon (family 2), Saudi Arabia (families 3–5 and 8), UAE (family 6), Syria (family 7), and Guatemala (families 9 and 10) (see Figure 1 and Table S2). The study protocol and genetic analyses performed in Singapore were approved by A∗STAR IRB (2019-087). Genetic analyses performed in France (Comité de protection des personnes Ile-de-France II) and Saudi Arabia (IBEC protocol 23IBEC090) were done in accordance with local bioethics laws. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their parents.

We searched for ciroz orthologs in the genomic interval between casz1 and tardbp (or tardbpl) in 49 teleost fish species, representing over 20 orders (Table S5). For this table, we used assemblies listed at https://hgdownload.soe.ucsc.edu/hubs/fish/index.html. In each genome, the interval between casz1 and tardbp/tardbpl was inspected for ciroz-related sequences in the "NCBI RefSeq genes, curated and predicted sets'' track, or if a RefSeq annotation was absent, then we used the "Augustus gene predictions" track. Although a ciroz ORF could be identified in most fish genomes analyzed, it was absent in this interval in all members of Atherinomorpha (eight species studied), a clade that is comprised of the orders Atheriniformes, Beloniformes (including Medaka), and Cyprinodontiformes. For the genomes with an absence of ciroz in the casz1-tardbp/tardbpl interval, tBLASTn searches using the zebrafish Ciroz protein sequence as a query did not identify significant matches elsewhere in the genome of each.

We previously suggested that CIROZ, along with DAND5, PKD1L1, MMP21, and CIROP, might be absent or undergoing ORF decay in species lacking motile cilia at their LRO, i.e., cetartiodactyl mammals, birds, and reptiles (Figures 1 and S1).54 Closer inspection of CIROZ exonic sequences in all mammalian genomes, available by Multiz alignment at the UCSC Genome Browser, indicates broader decay across mammals as compared to the other four genes. In several CIROZ coding exons, we found frameshift-inducing insertions, deletions, or premature stop codons in all representatives of Laurasiatheria, which includes cetartiodactyl mammals but also species believed to possess motile cilia at their LRO, including carnivores, bats, and odd-toed ungulates (Figures 1A, 1B, and S1). The CIROZ ORF is intact in the other major groups of mammals: Euarchontoglires (including mice and humans), as well as Afrotheria and marsupials (Figures 1A, 1B, and S1). This distribution suggests that within mammals, the pseudogenization of CIROZ began at the origin of Laurasiatheria, recently estimated to be ∼75 million years ago (mya).75

Analysis of PhyloP-defined vertebrate conservation76 at the CIROZ locus indicated that, surprisingly, the most conserved elements do not correspond to exons but rather to several intronic regions, while the conserved regions in the MMP21 and CIROP loci are exonic regions as expected (Figure S2A). The interval between TARDBP and CASZ1, the 2 genes flanking CIROZ, is contiguous in vertebrate genomes even when CIROZ is no longer present (Figure 1C). Examination of publicly available genome-wide predictions for enhancer-promoter contacts in developing mouse embryos indicates that one of the highly conserved intronic Ciroz elements corresponds to a region that is predicted to contact the Casz1 promoter (Figure S2B). This intronic CIROZ region is conserved in the TARDBP-CASZ1 interval in several bird species, including chicken and zebra finch (Figure S2B), even though the CIROZ ORF itself is absent (Figure S1A). In human cells, CASZ1 and CIROZ have been reported to fall within a topologically associated domain.77 If the conserved non-coding elements within CIROZ introns are necessary for the regulation of neighboring genes, then the CIROZ locus may have been resistant to gross alterations throughout evolution, even in species in which the intact ORF itself is no longer present.

This phylo-genomic analysis reveals that CIROZ, as opposed to the other 4 genes of the module, is absent or mutated not only in birds, reptiles, and cetartiodactyl mammals but also in non-cetartiodactyl laurasiatherian species that are believed to possess motile cilia at their LRO. This suggests that CIROZ may be under less stringent negative selection and may be dispensable in a larger group of vertebrates.

We report herein 16 individuals from 10 independently ascertained families with LR anomalies with or without CHDs, consistent with HTX. Family 1 is of European ancestry, and families 9 and 10 are from Central America, while all remaining families were of Middle Eastern background and known to be consanguineous (families 2–8) (Figure 2A). Family 6 was previously reported, albeit without a pedigree, allele segregation, or accurate number of affected individuals.78 Family 7 was mentioned in supplemental data by Petrovski and colleagues.61 Of these 16 affected individuals, three were affected fetuses subjected to termination of pregnancy, and two died in the first year of life due to complex cardiac phenotypes. The phenotypes in these 16 individuals range from HTX without CHD (5/16, including asymptomatic situs inversus totalis in individual F4-I:2 or horseshoe kidney with situs solitus in individual F4-II:4) to HTX with severe CHD (11/16, for example in F1-II:3, hypoplastic left heart syndrome, mitral and aortic atresia, bilobed lungs, and malrotation of the bowel) (see Table S2 and supplemental note for detailed clinical reports).

Through research-based or diagnostic exome/genome sequencing of individuals with HTX in independent centers, we identified causative bi-allelic variants in CIROZ (GenBank: NM_001170754.2) in these ten families, with cohort collection facilitated through GeneMatcher.79

In family 1, affected fetuses were compound heterozygous for the variants c.56C>T (p.Thr19Met) (paternal) and c.577C>T (p.Arg193∗) (maternal) (Figure 2A). The p.Thr19Met variant in exon 2 has a gnomAD (v.4.1.0) minor-allele frequency (MAF) of 4.32e−4 (664/1,536,352), while the p.Arg193∗ variant has been seen 4 times out of 1,613,530 alleles (MAF = 2.48e−6). Notably, both variants have not been seen in homozygosity. Although p.Thr19Met affects a residue with poor conservation across vertebrate species, it occurs within the predicted signal peptide of CIROZ and could affect its secretion (Figure 2B). Moreover, this variant creates a new ATG downstream of the wild-type start codon, independent of frame, and could thus lead to an alternative translation start site, which would lead to the production of a protein lacking its native signal peptide. The stop-gained variant p.Arg193∗ occurs in exon 7 and would be expected to undergo nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) leading to a protein-null allele (Figure 2B).

In seven additional families (F2–F8), the affected individuals were homozygous for another stop-gained variant, c.337C>T (p.Arg113∗), occurring in exon 4 and therefore also predicted to induce NMD (Figures 2A and 2B). Two affected individuals (F3-II:2 and F6-II:8) born to heterozygous parents could not be genotyped due to the unavailability of DNA samples. In the latest gnomAD (v.4.1.0), this c.337C>T (p.Arg113∗) variant occurs at a frequency of 2.73e−5 (42 out of 1, 536, 258 alleles) and has been reported once in the homozygous state in a Saudi individual with HTX (ClinVar: RCV003989718.2). Suspecting a founder effect for the p.Arg113∗ variant in these 7 Middle Eastern families, we analyzed neighboring SNPs in the exome or genome sequencing data from seven individuals to assess whether a common haplotype exists (Table S3). No shared haplotype across all 7 individuals could be identified, suggesting that this variant did not arise in a recent common ancestor. Interrogation of 14,000 genomes from the Qatar Genome Program80,81 revealed that this allele has been seen 24 times in heterozygosity (MAF = 8.57e−4). Although this is approximately 30 times higher than that of the global frequency in gnomAD, it suggests that this variant remains rare in the Middle East. It is expected that additional affected individuals homozygous for this p.Arg113∗ variant will be identified in Middle Eastern populations where endogamy is prevalent.

Families 9 and 10, both of Guatemalan origin, segregate a distinct germline allele with a gnomAD (v.4.1.0) MAF of 5.62e−6, which had not been seen as homozygous. This deletion of one base c.1210del (p.Gln404Argfs∗84) is also expected to be pathogenic, either by triggering NMD or by making a nonfunctional protein with an altered C terminus. Both affected children presented with situs inversus (see Table S2 and supplemental note for detailed clinical reports).

Notably, there were two individuals with bi-allelic CIROZ variants and non-phenotypic penetrance: F1-II:4 with situs solitus on antenatal ultrasound, postnatal echocardiogram, and clinical examination and F2-II:2 with situs solitus on clinical examination and normal abdominal ultrasound (Figure 2A; Table S3). Interestingly, significantly fewer individuals with bi-allelic pathogenic variants in CIROZ presented with CHD (5/16) compared to affected individuals for the other 4 genes of the module (MMP21, PKD1L1, CIROP, and DAND5) (chi-squared test, p = 0.006; Figure 2C; Table S4), indicating that CIROZ might have a different mode of action. We also compared the situs phenotype in individuals with inactivation of any of the 5 genes of this functional module (MMP21, PKD1L1, CIROP, CIROZ, and DAND5) to those with mutations in genes causing CILD due to impaired cilia motility. Significant differences are observed, with a much higher proportion of situs ambiguus in the former compared to the latter group (chi-squared test p < 0.00001; Figure 2D; Table S4). These clinical and genetic data support an active role for CIROZ in establishing situs solitus in humans. This was independently confirmed by W. Dong and colleagues, whose analysis of 5,497 exomes from individuals affected by CHD found CIROZ to be a genome-wide risk gene for laterality-associated defects and ascertained 5 families segregating bi-allelic loss-of-function CIROZ variants, including the recurrent variant encoding the stop-gained allele p.Arg113∗.82

The zebrafish ciroz homolog (Genbank: XM_009296969) maps to chromosome 23 and is flanked by the tardbp and casz1 genes, which are syntenic to its chromosomal location in humans and mice (Figure 1C). No other paralogs were found by BLAST search. Its exon-intron structure is similar to that of humans with the exception of the twelfth and thirteenth exons, which are absent in zebrafish. The predicted protein is thus lacking more than 360 amino acids in its C-terminal region relative to the human version (Figures 1B and S1A). By WISH, endogenous ciroz was found to have a restricted spatiotemporal expression pattern in cells that form the zebrafish LRO, the KV, starting at the 3-somite stage (Figure 3A). Relative to other markers of the LRO, ciroz was expressed after cirop, which starts to be expressed around the bud stage,54 but earlier than mmp21, whose expression only debuts at the 6-somite stage.44

We next assessed whether Xenopus laevis, an amphibian species that possesses a homolog of CIROZ, would similarly express ciroz in its LRO. The X. laevis ciroz gene is on chromosome 7 and is syntenic to the human gene but encodes a shorter protein, similar to zebrafish (Figures 1B and S1A). WISH revealed that ciroz was first expressed in stages 12–16 in central-flow-generating LRO cells with noto- and hypochordal fates. (Figure 3B). This spatiotemporal expression is analogous to what we observed in zebrafish.

Finally, we performed WISH in mouse embryos. The homolog of Ciroz on chromosome 4 of Mus musculus is syntenic to that of humans and exhibits an identical exon-intron structure, apart from the absence of human exon 13, a 3′ non-coding exon (Figure S3B). Its endogenous spatiotemporal expression was confined to the LRO, with transcription beginning at the late bud stage and ending by the 6-somite stage (Figures 3C and S3A). Its expression appeared symmetric and enriched in pit cells of the ciliated LRO (Figure 3C). Unlike Cirop, no bias expression in crown cells of the LRO or apparent down-regulation on the prospective left side in response to the flow could be recorded.54

Altogether, these expression analyses in three distinct animal models with functional LROs with motile cilia revealed that Ciroz is indeed transcribed specifically in the LRO, consistent with Ciroz playing a role in the process of LR patterning.

Two publicly available mutant mouse lines of Ciroz exist. The first one was previously published57 and described in the Mouse Genome Informatics (MGI) database as Gm572b2b1167Clo. This ENU-induced variant represents a 5′ splice donor mutation c.26+1G>A that is expected to yield a protein-null allele p.Trp9∗ (Figure S3B). When homozygous, E15.5 mutant Cirozc.26+1G>A embryos display laterality defects such as situs inversus totalis, CHDs, right pulmonary isomerism, or abnormal liver positioning (see Figure S3C and the MGI database MGI:5431499). Another mutant line was generated by the International Mouse Phenotyping Consortium (IMPC)83 and described in the MGI database as Gm572tm1b(EUCOMM)Hmgu. In this line, a LacZ cassette was introduced in lieu of exon 4, creating an out-of-frame allele (Figure S3B and MGI:5635119). This LacZ-KO transgenic line was noted to be partially embryonic lethal in the homozygous state, with less than 6% making it to birth (Figure S3D). Some of the available X-rays of E18.5 mutant embryos reveal defects compatible with HTX, such as situs inversus phenotypes affecting the lungs, heart, liver, and stomach (Figure S3D). These two distinct KO alleles confirm that Ciroz is required for the stereotypical positioning of organs along the LR axis in rodents. These Ciroz mutant mice, which were not generated nor characterized by our groups, appear to phenocopy the human disease and, as such, may provide a tractable mammalian model for understanding the exact pathogenesis of this disease of laterality.

We next set out to examine the role of ciroz in Xenopus laevis, a classical vertebrate species with motile cilia in its LRO, located at the posterior end of the gastrocoel roof plate (GRP), centered at the dorsal midline.84 In frogs, dand514 and cirop54 have been shown to be expressed in the LRO and essential for LR patterning. The annotated start codon of the Xenopus ciroz transcript (Genbank: XM_041569138.1) is situated in exon 2. The theoretical protein (XP_041425072) produced from this transcript is predicted to contain a signal peptide at its N terminus (aa 1–23) and a DUF4556 domain (aa 144–359) (Figure 4A), similarly to its human counterpart. To examine the anticipated extracellular localization of CIROZ, mRNA encoding HA-tagged CIROZ, CIROP, or FLAG-tagged MMP21 was microinjected into 8-cell stage embryos. Following an 18 h culture period, the supernatant of 10 dissociated animal caps was subjected to western blotting. Consistent with its predicted secretion, CIROZ, like MMP21 and CIROP, was readily detectable within the spent media (Figure 4B). Interestingly, we notice that secreted CIROZ seems to form oligomers, as evidenced by the presence of a strong signal at higher molecular weight (Figure 4B). To achieve a complete KO, zygotes were microinjected with targeted guides against the introns 2 (gRNA1) and 11 (gRNA2) of ciroz, along with recombinant Cas9, with the aim to create large deletions spanning most of the gene. Proper genomic editing at both gRNA locations, as well as a complete genomic deletion between the two gRNAs, was documented in injected embryos (Figures S4A–S4C). To our surprise, however, no discernable situs defect could be recorded when crispants were compared to uninjected wild-type siblings as assessed by heart, intestine, and gallbladder positions (Figures 4C and 4D). To make sure that no genetic compensation could explain the lack of phenotype in ciroz crispants, we also depleted Ciroz by injecting TBMOs or SBMOs (Figures 4A, S4D, and S4E). The absence of LR patterning defects was confirmed in the morphants (Figures 4C and 4D). Altogether, these results indicate that ciroz is apparently dispensable for LR patterning in Xenopus.

We next set out to examine the role of ciroz in zebrafish, an appropriate vertebrate model with motile cilia in its LRO, which is referred to as the KV.85 In this species, dand5,86
mmp21,44,46
pkd1l1,87 and cirop54 have been shown to be expressed in the KV and essential for LR patterning. The annotated start codon of the zebrafish ciroz transcript (ENSDART00000156512.2) is situated in exon 2, while it is embedded in exon 1 in humans and mice. The theoretical protein produced from this transcript is not predicted to contain a signal peptide at its N terminus. Suspecting that the N-terminal-encoding portion of zebrafish ciroz may be incorrectly annotated, we searched for other tentative translation start sites within the first intron of ciroz. In species with an intact CIROZ, the first exon typically encodes less than 15 aa, forming part of the signal peptide. We therefore selected all ATGs within the first intron of zebrafish ciroz that are followed by a short ORF and bounded by a candidate donor splice site (gt), with the potential to maintain the phase if spliced to exon 2 (Figure S5A). We identified five regions meeting these criteria (colored columns in Figure S5A). Examination of publicly available RNA-seq data from zebrafish embryos at stages corresponding to the presence of the KV did not indicate a significant signal within the predicted exon 1 of ENSDART00000156512.2 but did disclose a signal spanning one of the alternative translation start sites (light blue column in Figures S5A and S5B). Using a forward primer at the 5′ limit of this RNA-seq peak (ex 1∗) and a reverse primer in exon 11, we successfully amplified a PCR product using 12 hpf zebrafish embryo cDNA that corresponds precisely to the exon-exon boundaries predicted by RNA-seq (Figures S5B–S5D). This transcript extends the N terminus of the annotated Ciroz protein by 30 aa (Figure S5C) and confers a possible signal peptide (Figure S5E). These results suggest that the current exon-intron structure of ciroz in zebrafish might be inaccurate. We propose that an alternative exon 1, which encodes the signal peptide, exists and is yet to be annotated.

To achieve a complete KO, Danio rerio zygotes were microinjected with targeted guides against the exon 6 of ciroz along with recombinant CRISPR-Cas9 protein. Three out-of-frame alleles, c.414_423del (p.Pro169Ilefs∗11), c.415delinsTGTAT (p.Pro169Cysfs∗4), and c.414_417del (p.Ile168Metfs∗14) were selected, outcrossed for multiple generations, and then bred to homozygosity (Figure 5A). The mutant ciroz transcripts were targeted for NMD as evidenced by WISH and qPCR analyses. Alleles carrying premature termination codons were undetectable by WISH at the 4-somite stage and significantly reduced at the 12-somite stage by qPCR relative to the wild-type transcripts (Figure 5B). Together with targeted Sanger sequencing of the locus and analysis of the endogenous mutant transcripts, we are confident that these 3 independent mutant lines represent deleterious alleles. We next analyzed zygotic (Z) null embryos for potential situs anomalies. To our surprise, no discernable heart looping defects could be recorded when compared to wild-type or heterozygous siblings (Figure 5C). To unmask a potential maternal compensation, we next generated MZ null ciroz fish. These fish, like their Z null parents, had no heart looping defects (Figure 5C). This was confirmed in homozygous mutants of the 3 alleles as well as in compound heterozygous crosses. Similarly, no defect was observed in homozygous ciroz KO fish for liver and pancreas positioning, as assessed with the LiPan transgenic line (Figure 5D). Likewise brain asymmetry was unaltered as assessed by WISH using the leftover (lov) marker which is normally asymmetrically expressed in the diencephalon (Figure 5E). Additionally, lefty2 was properly expressed in the heart primordium on the left (Figure 5F), confirming the absence of LR patterning defects. Altogether, these results indicate that ciroz is also apparently dispensable for LR patterning in zebrafish, which is consistent with MZ mutants being viable and fertile (Figure 5G).

In order to independently validate the lack of phenotype in the absence of Ciroz and to rule out any genetic compensation in the KO,88,89 we designed an SBMO targeting the junction between exon 4 and intron 4 (Figure S6A). Its efficiency was confirmed by RT-PCR in embryos injected with increasing concentrations of SBMO, showing that 2 nL was sufficient to significantly induce missplicing (Figure S6B). The predominant transcript had undergone exon 4 skipping, resulting in a frameshift with an early stop codon (Figure S6C). In SBMO-injected embryos, cardiac looping defects were seen both in wild-type and ciroz KO animals, indicating a non-specific effect (Figure S6C). Moreover, SBMO-injected embryos displayed overt morphological defects, including heart edema, which may confound the interpretation of the observed cardiac looping defects. These results using MO-mediated depletion are ambiguous, while those relying on MZ ciroz KO fish argue against an active role played by Ciroz during zebrafish LR specification.

This unexpected outcome prompted us to take a deeper look at the conservation of CIROZ in other related fish species. In the ray-finned fish medaka, whose LR asymmetry establishment is otherwise similar to that of zebrafish and Xenopus,12,90,91 the corresponding locus in chromosome 7 is bereft of a recognizable ciroz homolog (Figure 1C). Questioning whether this may reflect an incomplete genome assembly, we looked into the genomes of other fish species. To our surprise, no CIROZ homologs were found in species belonging to the clade of Atherinomorpha fish, which includes medaka, a model organism with intact CIROP, MMP21, PKD1L1, and DAND5 genes (Figures 1A and S1A; Table S5). In addition, CIROZ was also absent in cartilaginous fish (such as the elephant shark), jawless vertebrates (such as the Japanese lamprey), and the ancestral amphioxus (Cephalochordata) (Figure S1A). The absence of CIROZ in certain groups of fish suggests that this gene may not be essential in some vertebrates even though they have a functional ciliated LRO.

No published information is available regarding the molecular function of CIROZ. BLAST analysis using its primary amino acid sequence (UniProt: G8JLG8) did not reveal clear similarities to other proteins besides its orthologs in other species. The UniProt database suggests that the longest isoform of human CIROZ (G8JLG8 at 823 aa) contains a predicted N-terminal signal peptide, followed by a domain of unknown function (DUF4556) spanning approximately 214 aa (Figure 6A). Toward the C-terminal half of the protein, several large, disordered regions and small segments of compositional bias (proline- or polar residue rich) are annotated.

In an attempt to gain insights into the domain architecture of CIROZ, an HHpred analysis was performed to identify remote protein homology and three-dimensional (3D) structure prediction (Figure S7A; Table S6). This revealed a homology with a 96% probability between the N-terminal region of CIROZ (aa 50–365) and the ZP2 (Q05996) protein. Conversely, a homology to CIROZ is also predicted for ZP2, indicating that these two proteins may share common domains (Figures 6A and S7). The ZP is composed of 4 glycoproteins (ZP1–4 in humans) that form an extracellular matrix surrounding mammalian oocytes, where they serve to mediate sperm-egg interactions.92,93 ZP proteins contain a ZP module that consists of two immunoglobulin-like folds, the ZP-N-terminal (ZP-N) and ZP-C-terminal (ZP-C) domains.94 After its signal peptide, ZP2 also contains three sequential ZP-N domains, which correspond to the region of significant homology to CIROZ (Figure 6A). AlphaFold95 prediction for either mouse or human CIROZ annotates three adjacent, well-structured subdomains within the N-terminal half of the protein (between residues 45 and 368 of CIROZ G8JLG8), which match with the resolved structure of human ZP2 between its residues 39 and 365 with an e value of 2.75e−10 (Figure 6B). Close comparison of the known structure of the ZP-N1 domain from mouse ZP2 (PDB: 5II6)96 to that of the predicted mouse CIROZ structure revealed compelling similarities, including two pairs of properly spaced cysteines forming intramolecular disulfide bridges, which is a signature of ZP-N domains (Figure 6B, insets). HHpred analysis against the PDB confirms the presence of a ZP-N1 domain in CIROZ (aa 50–165, e value: 2.1e−3, probability: 96%). Additionally, HHpred analysis against the protein family (Pfam) database reveals that CIROZ and ZP2 share a DUF4556 domain (aa 151–365 in CIROZ, aa 137–330 in ZP2), which corresponds to the regions of ZP-N2 and ZP-N3 in ZP2 (Figure S7). The unsupervised annotation of a DUF4556 within ZP2 indicates that this domain of unknown function may, in reality, represent a pair of ZP-N domains (Figures 6A and 6B). Hence, we propose that CIROZ contains three adjacent ZP-N domains at its N terminus such that the delineation of a DUF4556 is moot.

Based on these in silico predictions, we used HEK293T cells to express full-length human CIROZ tagged with HA and compared it to C-terminally truncated constructs encompassing the ZP-N1-2-3 and ZP-N1-2 domains and the ZP-N1 subunit alone (Figure 6C). Each recombinant protein was readily secreted in the conditioned medium. The soluble proteins were found to spontaneously oligomerize as revealed by western blotting and manifested as discrete bands with each monomeric form and a ladder of bands of molecular weight corresponding to dimers, trimers, and oligomers thereof (Figure 6C). These multimers were seen despite harsh denaturation consisting of boiling in presence of SDS and DTT. This autonomous multimerization is analogous to that of other ZP-N subunits from ZP proteins,97,98 supporting our predicted annotation of three functional ZP-N domains within the N-terminal portion of CIROZ.

It has long been recorded that in CILD, the clinical outcome is a 50% chance of situs inversus or situs solitus, with situs ambiguus being an exception. Mutated genes in this CILD group encode proteins of the periphery of the axoneme, such as dynein proteins, which are key for generating the flow at the LRO. By contrast, when disruption of the lateralization cascade does not impact cilia motility, situs ambiguus is a more frequent outcome than situs inversus or situs solitus. Although our current affected individuals’ numbers are relatively small (n = 16), the significantly lower rate of situs ambiguus for CIROZ deficiency among the 5 genes of the syn-expression module could reflect a permissive, but dispensable, role of this protein for the situs solitus conformation. Additionally, almost 40% of these affected individuals were born without any CHDs, an observation not seen for the other 4 genes of the module, including CIROP54 and MMP21.44,45,46,99,100,101 More affected individuals will be needed to confirm whether this trend holds.

Of the five genes in the ciliated LRO-specific module, DAND5 and PKD1L1 have been the most extensively studied at the functional level. DAND5 is a secreted TGF-β antagonist, necessary for the direct inhibition of NODAL/GDF1 activity on the right side. DAND5 down-regulation on the left side allows propagation of the NODAL cascade specifically on the left, leading to proper asymmetric morphogenesis. PKD1L1 is a cell-surface channel that complexes with PKD2 on immotile cilia, where they are thought to sense fluid flow, leading to an influx of calcium in peripheral cells on the left side of the LRO.91 This ultimately causes degradation of DAND5 mRNA through a recently proposed intracellular mechanism in mice involving BICC1 and the CCR4-NOT deadenylase complex.102 In zebrafish, cirop is necessary for dand5 repression on the left side, yet KV formation and fluid flow are unaffected in cirop KOs, suggesting it may play a role in flow sensing on the left side, upstream of dand5 post-transcriptional down-regulation.54 Therefore, PKD1L1, PKD2, CIROP, and DAND5 may be closely linked in the circumferential crown cells of the LRO, where immotile cilia are believed to sense the flow. The functional relationship of these 4 proteins with MMP21 and CIROZ, which also belong to this “functional operon,” remains to be explored. While our investigations of CIROZ in ancestral vertebrates spanning species of fish and amphibians argue that this gene is undergoing obsolescence, the exact role played by CIROZ in the mouse and human LRO remains to be addressed. It will be important to examine whether cilia motility, cilia-driven flow, or LRO cell/tissue morphology are affected and how it can be interpreted in relation to the activity of MMP21, CIROP, and PKD1L1.

The three consecutive ZP-N domains delineated in CIROZ may mirror those found in ZP2. We note that zebrafish do not have homologs of ZP2, which exist in Lepisosteus oculatus (spotted gar).103 This configuration of three ZP-N domains seen in CIROZ and ZP2 could have a common ancestral origin and may have been deployed to serve in the blockage of polyspermy in ZP2 and for LR specification in CIROZ. Further, if the homology of CIROZ to ZP2 can serve as a cue to its mode of action, one may envisage that CIROZ’s function could also be dependent on its cleavage by an extracellular protease. Like ZP2, which is cleaved by ovastacin,104 CIROZ may need to be similarly processed by a metalloproteinase to facilitate or terminate its activity. This could serve as an irreversible switch to lock in nascent LR asymmetry in cells of the LRO. It will be important to determine if CIROZ could be a substrate of MMP21 and/or CIROP, two zinc-dependent metallopeptidases that have partly co-evolved with CIROZ for establishing the LR axis.

A major difference in the pattern of evolutionary loss between CIROZ and the other four genes of the ciliated LRO-specific gene module is the absence of an intact CIROZ in all sequenced laurasiatherians, whose last common ancestor was ∼75 mya, while for MMP21, PKD1L1, CIROP, and DAND5, pseudogenization is only evident within Cetartiodactyla (i.e., one subgroup of laurasiatherians), whose last common ancestor was ∼60 mya.75 That non-cetartiodactyl laurasiatherians, believed to possess motile cilia at their LRO, lost CIROZ yet have retained intact versions of the other four genes argues against a domino effect whereby the loss of CIROZ would lead to inactivation of the other genes of the module. Instead, this phylogenomic analysis lends support to the notion that non-cetartiodactyl laurasiatherians have evolved alternative mechanisms to compensate for the loss of CIROZ while still requiring functional MMP21, PKD1L1, CIROP, and DAND5.

We undertook to inactivate Ciroz in zebrafish and Xenopus because of its restricted expression at the LRO. We assumed that as for dand5, cirop, and mmp21, whose KOs lead to laterality defects in these species,44,54,105
ciroz loss would also model HTX, as is seen in humans and mice. To our surprise, this was without effect: ciroz KO zebrafish or frogs had normal situs and were indistinguishable from wild-type animals. This unexpected finding prompted us to examine its conservation in other species. CIROZ homologs were found in other Cyprinidae species (such as the golden-line barbel) but were conspicuously absent in Atherinomorpha (including medaka), whose last common ancestor was ∼80 mya.106 We are also unable to identify an ortholog in elephant shark (cartilaginous fish), Japanese lamprey (jawless vertebrate), or amphioxus (Cephalochordata), suggesting that CIROZ may have first appeared in Teleostomi rather than Deuterostomia.

In Xenopus, zebrafish and other ray-finned fish species, including fugu and tilapia, ciroz encodes a shorter protein. We question whether the addition of a long C terminus in higher vertebrates may have shifted its role toward LR patterning, as is the case in rodents and humans. In summary, here we describe 10 families with bi-allelic pathogenic CIROZ variants in 14 affected individuals presenting with congenital HTX. This, we find, is phenocopied in mice lacking Ciroz but not in KO zebrafish or Xenopus. Our findings highlight how, across vertebrate groups, there is a diversity of molecular mechanisms that ensure the correct development of LR asymmetry and how the genes involved in this process are under contemporaneous evolution.
