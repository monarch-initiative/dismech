# Long-term efficiency of pallidal DBS and the role of Levodopa treatment in DYT-GNAL and 18p deletion syndrome associated dystonia: an observational study and review of literature.
**Authors:** Reimer J, Schumann F, Lohmann K, Riemer TG, Krauss JK, Schneider GH, Kühn AA, Krause P
**Journal:** J Neural Transm (Vienna) (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s00702-026-03109-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00702-026-03109-z)

## Content

Dystonia due to loss-of-function variants in the GNAL gene in DYT-GNAL and 18p-deletion (18pdel) syndrome has been reported to respond well to pallidal deep brain stimulation (GPi-DBS) occasionally, but long-term data is scarce. GNAL is implicated in dopamine receptor function, which may explain anecdotal reports of hypokinesia in DYT-GNAL and 18pdel-associated dystonia, a phenomenon that has not yet been systematically reviewed. We retrospectively evaluated a cohort of three patients with GNAL variants treated with GPi-DBS, documenting individual long-term outcomes spanning up to 25 years. Dystonia severity was assessed using the Toronto Western Spasmodic Torticollis Rating Scale (TWSTRS) and the Burke - Fahn - Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMDRS). We also documented bradykinetic symptoms and levodopa response. A literature review was added focusing on DBS outcomes and the effects of levodopa in DYT-GNAL and 18pdel-associated dystonia. In our patients, TWSTRS severity subscale I was reduced by 63% in early (≤ 12months; 20.7 to 7.7 points) and 81% in late follow-up (> 12months; 20.7 to 4 points) after GPi-DBS χ. BFMDRS decreased by 67% and 44%; each with a Kendall's coefficient of 0.78, indicating a high degree of concordance in improvement trajectories. Two patients exhibited bradykinesia, which was levodopa-responsive in one. GPi-DBS responses have been reported for another ten DYT-GNAL and two 18pdel-patients. Bradykinesia prompted levodopa challenges in 15 patients, resulting in improvement in five. Long-term follow-up data from three DYT-GNAL patients treated with DBS showed sustained improvement in dystonia, particularly in cervical symptoms. Bradykinesia may be an inherent clinical feature of GNAL-related dystonia, warranting further investigation.

Introduction
Pathogenic variants in the G Protein Subunit Alpha L
(GNAL) gene cause adult-onset primary torsion dystonia
(DYT-GNAL). Since its initial identification (Fuchs et al.
2013; Vemula et al. 2013), approximately 100 DYT-GNAL
patients have been reported (Lohmann et al. 2025). The
inheritance pattern is usually autosomal dominant with high,
but not full, penetrance in affected families. Onset typically
occurs in early to late adulthood, with a median age at onset
(AAO) of 38 years (Lange et al. 2021). In most patients,
symptoms begin in the neck and craniocervical region, often
progressing to segmental or multifocal involvement, and in
some patients, generalized dystonia  (   h t t p s : / / w w w . m d s g e n e
. o r g     ) . GNAL is located on the short arm of chromosome 18
(18p11.21). Variable deletions of chromosome 18p result
in the 18p deletion (18pdel) syndrome, which is clinically
characterized by cognitive impairment, facial dysmorphia,
and short stature. Additional features may include spino -
cerebellar ataxia, facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy,
and dystonia (Hasi-Zogaj et al. 2015). A deletion of GNAL
is considered the primary driver of dystonia in 18pdel syn -
drome (Crosiers et al. 2019).
Despite the increasing recognition of DYT-GNAL, the
relationship between genetic variants, clinical expression,
and treatment responses, particularly to therapies like deep
brain stimulation (DBS), remains to be defined, given the
limited number of case reports and therapeutic data.
DBS of the globus pallidus internus (GPi) is an effec -
tive treatment option for focal, segmental, and generalized
dystonia (Kupsch et al. 2006, Krause et al. 2025). Detailed
phenotyping and genetic profiling play an increasingly
important role in predicting treatment response, aiming to
optimize individual DBS outcomes. Previous reports on
DBS effects in DYT-GNAL are mostly limited to single case
studies, with the largest series from a single center involving
three patients undergoing GPi-DBS (Sarva et al. 2019). In
a review of eight published DYT-GNAL GPi-DBS patients,
significant improvement of cervical dystonia was observed
with mixed responses for limb, laryngeal, and truncal dys -
tonia. Compared to DYT-THAP1 mutation carriers, patients
with DYT-TOR1A and DYT-GNAL dystonia demonstrated
higher early response rates to DBS, while long-term out -
comes were comparable across all three genotypes (Ahn
et al. 2019). Additionally, two patients have been reported
with DBS treatment in 18pdel-associated dystonia (Vela-
Desojo et al. 2019; Duma et al. 2019).
A growing body of evidence implicates the role of dopa-
mine in the pathogenesis of dystonia. The GNAL gene
encodes the protein Gαolf, which plays a critical role in
dopaminergic signal transduction within the striatum.
Pathogenic variants in GNAL are primarily loss-of-function
variants, resulting in reduced Gαolf activity. This leads to
an imbalance of the direct and indirect pathways within the
basal ganglia, contributing to the pathophysiology of dysto-
nia (Thomsen et al. 2024).
Interestingly, one patient with 18pdel-syndrome with
dystonia-parkinsonism and evidence of nigrostriatal
degeneration on a dopamine transporter (DaT) scan has
been reported. This patient showed partial improvement
of bradykinesia and gait disturbances with levodopa treat -
ment (Di Rauso et al. 2024). In our cohort, bradykinesia
was observed in two patients, with freezing of gait (FOG)
present in one. Although bradykinesia has been described
as a stimulation-related side effect in patients treated with
chronic GPi-DBS (Huebl et al. 2015; Schrader et al. 2011),
in our cohort, symptoms were present prior to DBS treat -
ment. Additionally, the patient reported by Di Rauso et al.
(2024) did not receive DBS, suggesting that bradykinesia
might be an additional motor feature of GNAL-associated
dystonia rather than solely a consequence of DBS treatment.
To address this hypothesis, we here systematically evalu-
ate the responses to DBS and medical treatment, including
levodopa, alongside a detailed review of motor and non-
motor phenotypes in DYT-GNAL and 18pdel-associated
dystonia, with a particular focus on bradykinesia. Addi -
tionally, we present long-term observational data (up to 25
years) on the effects of bilateral GPi-DBS in three patients
with GNAL variants treated at our center, contextualizing
these findings within the existing literature.
Methods
The study was approved by the local ethics committee of
Charité – Universitätsmedizin Berlin (EA1/120/24) and
has therefore been performed in accordance with the ethi -
cal standards outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki
and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent
was obtained from all participants. All data supporting the
findings of this study are available within the paper and its
supplementary information.
Patients: We retrospectively analyzed a cohort of three
patients with variants in GNAL with bilateral GPi-DBS and
individual long-term results of up to 25 years of treatment
at our center. Patients presented at regular intervals for DBS
adjustment and battery check (at least once per year). Video
documentation was conducted at baseline and about one
year postoperatively, as well as sporadically at later time
points, for example, following genetic confirmation or when
clinical changes were reported. Dystonia was rated using
the Toronto Western Spasmodic Torticollis Rating Scale
(TWSTRS) I (severity scale) as well as the Burke-Fahn-
Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMDRS) (movement

scale) according to video documentation before DBS (base-
line), at an early follow up (FU) time point within the first
year of treatment and the longest available postoperative
FU (late FU; >12 months). TWSTRS II (disability subscale)
and III (pain subscale) scores, as well as Unified Parkinson’s
Disease Rating scale (UPDRS) motor examination (part III),
could not be assessed retrospectively but were sporadically
assessed during clinical routine follow-ups. Furthermore,
we evaluated bradykinetic symptoms, DaT scan results, and
levodopa response wherever data were available, including
a review of clinical records and video documentation.
Genetics: The genetic variants were detected either in a
research setting (DYSTRACT consortium) through micro -
array analyses (unpublished data, Patient 1 and 2) or by
diagnostic testing using gene panel analysis (Patient 3).
Review of literature : Data and publications included in
our literature review were collected and analyzed up to
March 31st, 2025. The PubMed database was searched for
literature on DYT-GNAL- and 18pdel-associated dystonia
with the focus on published cases with given information on
additional phenotyping and/or applied therapies. Use of the
key words “GNAL dystonia”, “DYT 25”, “DYT GNAL”
and “18p syndrome AND dystonia” provided 115 results
for DYT-GNAL and seven results for 18pdel-syndrome-
associated dystonia. We retrieved 21 DYT-GNAL patients
fulfilling the criteria on phenotypical description (written/
disease-specific motor scales or video documentation) and/
or given information on applied therapies, as well as 19
patients with 18pdel-associated dystonia.
Statistics: Statistical analysis of age, disease duration,
and dystonia scores was conducted using SPSS (IBM Corp.
Released 2023. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Ver -
sion 29.0.2.0 Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Data are given as
mean ± standard deviation (SD) according to Cohen’s unless
specified otherwise. Changes from baseline TWSTRS I
and BFMDRS motor subscale (pre-DBS) at early and late
follow-up were analyzed using non-parametric Friedman
test for repeated measures. A p-value < 0.05was consid -
ered to be significant. Kendall’s coefficient (W) was used
to quantify the effect size and calculated using the equation
W = X2/(k*[n-1]) (X 2 = Friedman test statistic, k = number
of patients, n = number of timepoints). Values between 0.6
and 0.8 are generally interpreted as a strong level of agree -
ment indicating consistent patterns of change over patients,
whereas values above 0.8 indicate a very strong effect.
Results
Patient cohort from our center
Demographics: Patients (video documentation available in
the Supplementary Online Resource Videos 1, 2, 3) treated

Patient 1: This male patient, with a positive family his -
tory for dystonia, developed spasmodic torticollis at the age
of 36. Genetic testing revealed a 1.3 Mb deletion including
the GNAL gene (DYSTRACT, unpublished data) in both
the patient and his affected father (who presented with cer -
vical dystonia and parkinsonism). Treatment with trihexy -
phenidyl, clonazepam, baclofen and cervical botulinum
toxin injections provided only partial and transient benefit,
leading to evaluation for bilateral GPi-DBS at the age of
44 years. Preoperatively, TWSTRS I was 23/35 points and
BFMDRS motor score 27.5/120 points. In addition to dys -
tonic features, both patient and father presented with bra -
dykinesia and slight freezing of gait (FOG). The patient´s
DaT-Scan was unremarkable, but a standardized levodopa
challenge prior to DBS markedly improved bradykinesia
and FOG, with UPDRS III decreased by 61% (from 26/132
points to 10/132 points). Oral medication and botulinum
toxin injections were discontinued postoperatively due to
the sufficient DBS effect. After initial improvement during
the first year, secondary generalization of dystonia devel -
oped, affecting the trunk, left upper limb, and both legs.
Adjustment of stimulation parameters improved cervical
and truncal dystonia and gait (TWSTRS I DBS-OFF 20/35;
DBS-ON 6/35, BFMDRS DBS-ON 9/120). Eleven years
post implantation, benefit for cervical dystonia remained
stable, though refractory painful dystonia of the left upper
limb persisted. Transient stimulation-induced side effects
included transient dysarthria, paresthesia, and gait distur -
bance. Under 300 mg/day of levodopa, the patient reported
a smoother gait and increased walking speed. Gait and bra -
dykinesia worsened during (short-term) DBS-OFF, while
higher stimulation frequencies additionally increased FOG.
Long-term DBS-OFF was not tolerated due to exacerbating
neck and back pain. After 11 years, TWSTRS I remained
improved (8/35). Botulinum toxin treatment for the upper
left limb was reintroduced 13 years post-surgery due to pro-
gressive dystonia and pain (BFMDRS 16/120).

and limbs and intermittent phasic head movements. Family

Bradykinesia and Levodopa treatment in DYT-GNAL
and 18pdel-associated dystonia
All reviewed patients received guideline-based medical
treatment (AWMF 2021). Botulinum toxin injections were
highly effective for cervical dystonia, orofacial dystonia,
and limb dystonia (compare Table 2).
Along with our own observation of bradykinesia and
FOG in one of our DYT-GNAL patients, Parkinson’s Dis -
ease (PD)-like symptoms in both DYT-GNAL and 18pdel-
associated dystonia prompted a levodopa challenge in 15
patients, with some improvement seen in five. Only one
18pdel-associated dystonia patient showed improvement in
dystonia itself.
Bradykinesia and FOG have been reported as potential
stimulation-associated side effects of chronic bipallidal
neurostimulation (Huebl et al. 2015; Schrader et al. 2011).
Since one or both symptoms were evident in preoperative
video recordings of our patient and persisted during the
stimulation off-state, we do not consider these symptoms
to be stimulation-induced. We can only speculate whether
patients with DYT-GNAL may be more susceptible to a
worsening of preexisting bradykinesia under chronic neuro-
stimulation of the GPi. Since most published patients did
not receive GPi-DBS, we interpret these PD-like features as
a potential additional motor manifestation of DYT-GNAL
dystonia, rather than solely being a DBS side effect.
The functional impairment of Gαolf due to pathogenic
GNAL variants and deletions may underlie the observed
dopaminergic dysregulation. Gαolf plays a crucial role in
striatal signaling by directly coupling dopamine 1 (D1R)
and adenosine type 2 (A2A) receptors to adenylyl cyclase,
leading to an activation of the cAMP pathway (Corvol et
al. 2001). Simultaneously, Gαolf interacts with dopamine 2
receptors (D2R), inhibiting adenylyl cyclase and modulat -
ing dopaminergic signaling through the indirect pathway (El
Atiallah et al. 2024). Oral levodopa intake may increase pre-
synaptic dopamine release, compensating for the decreased
responsiveness of D1R and D2R by increasing ligand con -
centration and receptor saturation, thereby maximizing
residual receptor activity. Furthermore, levodopa modulates
cholinergic and glutamatergic neurons in both the direct and
indirect pathways, which are essential for motor control (De
Deurwaerdere et al. 2017).
As previously noted, levodopa-response was observed in
only one-third of patients. We hypothesize that variations
in the response to dopaminergic therapy might differ due to
varying residual function of the D1R and D2R.
PD-like symptoms led to DaT scans in four patients, which
were normal despite clinically relevant bradykinesia and
FOG in two patients. Only one DYT-GNAL patient showed
DaT scan results suggestive of nigrostriatal degeneration,
and clumsiness. Patient 32 showed improved lower limb
dystonia and gait after 6 months of 300 mg levodopa per
day. No positive effects were noted in the remaining five
patients.
Discussion
In this study, we report long-term follow-up data on three
dystonia patients with pathogenic GNAL variants treated
with GPi-DBS at our center and contextualize these findings
with the existing literature on pharmacological management
and DBS outcomes in ten DYT-GNAL and two 18pdel-
associated dystonia. Given our observations of bradyki -
nesia and freezing of gait (FOG) in DYT-GNAL patients,
we also present a systematic evaluation of pharmacological
treatment, including levodopa, alongside a comprehensive
review of additional motor and non-motor phenotypes in
both dystonia subtypes. To our knowledge, this is the first
study to systematically assess levodopa responsiveness and
bradykinetic symptoms in DYT-GNAL and 18pdel-associ -
ated dystonia.
DBS effects in DYT-GNAL and 18pdel-associated
dystonia
Across all 15 patients with GNAL variants and 18pdel-asso-
ciated dystonia treated with bilateral pallidal DBS, sustained
improvement in cervical dystonia was observed over fol -
low-up periods of up to 25 years with comparable patterns
of improvement across our three patients indicated by a high
Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W = 0.78). While cer-
vical dystonia consistently showed a good response to DBS,
outcomes for limb dystonia and speech were more variable.
Furthermore, (secondary) generalization is common in the
analyzed DBS cohort with only two patients with report -
edly focal distribution (patient IDs: 14 (Sarva et al. 2019),
18 (Carecchio et al. 2016)). However, due to the lack of
standardized motor scores such as TWSTRS I and BFM -
DRS, for most reviewed patients, no formal analysis could
be performed for the DBS cohort.
In the reviewed cohort, including our three patients,
AAO did not differ significantly between DYT-GNAL and
18pdel-associated dystonia patients who did or did not
receive DBS. Pain, present in all three of our patients and
mentioned in Patients 13 and 14 (Sarva et al. 2019), may
be an important factor to pursue DBS. Available patient
histories indicated a relevant reduction in pain; however,
symptom-specific scores, such as TWSTRS III for pain
quantification, were unavailable for most reviewed patients
and for our patients.

Strengths and limitations of the study
This study provides a comprehensive phenotypic and treat -
ment-related characterization of two rare genetic forms of
dystonia, DYT-GNAL and 18pdel-associated dystonia,
through detailed clinical observation and structured treat -
ment evaluation. It offers an in-depth analysis of medical
treatment regimens and their effects, including botulinum
toxin and levodopa. Notably, we present long-term follow-
up data of GPi-DBS for up to 25 years in DYT-GNAL
dystonia, reporting outcomes from three patients being reg-
ularly treated in our center. In addition, we describe a novel
observation of PD-like features, such as bradykinesia and
FOG, in DYT-GNAL dystonia patients, with preliminary
evidence suggesting potential levodopa responsiveness in at
least a subset of patients. These findings may expand clini -
cal understanding and management of GNAL-related move-
ment disorders.
Due to the retrospective design and the nature of this
study as a review of multiple case reports on a rare genetic
form of dystonia, the dataset lacks consistency and homo -
geneity, particularly in terms of TWSTRS I, II and III scores
and the BFMDRS motor and disability scales. Additionally,
the small sample size in our cohort limits statistical analysis
of motor scales to non-parametric tests. Kendall’s coeffi -
cient of concordance (W) was used to assess the consistency
and extent of change across patients, since it is less depen -
dent on sample size compared to p-value based significance
testing. Furthermore, the reviewed sample is highly het -
erogeneous in terms of age at onset, age at surgery, genetic
variants, and dystonia distribution, making it challenging to
generalize these results to other cohorts.
Conclusions
Genetic testing of dystonia patients is increasingly impor -
tant due to growing evidence of variable outcomes follow -
ing DBS. Combining our own cases with those from the
literature, the results support the use of DBS as a safe and
long-lasting treatment option for medically refractory DYT-
GNAL dystonia patients, particularly for cervical dystonia.
However, the suboptimal effects of DBS on truncal and limb
dystonia should be addressed during individual counseling
to set realistic expectations for treatment outcomes in DYT-
GNAL and 18pdel-associated dystonia.

non-responsive to levodopa, PD-like features were com -
monly observed in both DYT-GNAL and 18pdel-associated
