Introduction
The epidemiology of leprosy is a complex subject. The modes, course and population
distribution of infection with Mycobacterium leprae remain poorly understood, due to the
paucity of data on infection. Risk factors for different clinical presentations have yet to be
defined. The effectiveness of the various control interventions differs between populations and
remains controversial.
Global trends in leprosy have been much discussed over recent years, stimulated in particu-
lar by the WHO target, set in 1991, to “eliminate leprosy as a public health problem” (defined
as reduction of prevalence below 1 per 10,000 population). 1 That target stimulated major
changes in programme priorities, case finding, diagnosis, classification, treatment regimens
and data collection, all of which complicate comparisons of data over time. The emphasis on
prevalence meant that major reductions could and did occur as a result of shortened treatment
duration, despite constant incidence. The declaration of “elimination” at the global level ,
announced by WHO in May 2001,2 was misinterpreted by some ministries of health as effective
eradication, with the consequence that some national programmes were wound down despite
continued transmission and disease in their populations. All this has stimulated argument and
controversy, which have further obscured the actual epidemiological situation in much of the
world.3–6
That said, there is much evidence that incidence of the disease has declined in many
populations. Not only did leprosy disappear from northern Europe over recent centuries,

but there is also evidence that transmission of M. leprae (or at least autochthonous disease)
has recently stopped in several populations (e.g. Japan, South Korea) which were endemic
throughout most of the 20th century. 7–10 Though the earlier declines were associated with
major social and environmental changes, including improved hygiene, nutrition and housing,
and may have been influenced by the increase of tuberculosis, the more recent declines are
more difficult to explain, being influenced by case finding and treatment programmes, and
widespread BCG vaccination, in addition to improvements in socio-economic and living
conditions. These factors have occurred together in most endemic populations, confounding
one another, and making it difficult to estimate the contribution of any particular intervention.
Ironically, it may be that autochthonous leprosy is actually increasing in at least one population
in the world, the USA, due to local zoonotic ecology and the increase in range of a reservoir
host, the nine-banded armadillo. 11 Whether this is true in any other nations in the Americas
is at present unknown. In several other countries the new case detection statistics show no
convincing decline in recent years, though the actual underlying trends are unclear. 12
Trends in Africa are particularly obscure. It is apparent that there have been real declines, but
much of the region remains endemic for the infection and disease. Given the ongoing burdens
of malaria, tuberculosis, HIV , etc, leprosy is not a high priority for national health systems in
Africa; and several countries have downsized their leprosy programmes and some have stopped
regular reporting to WHO.13,14 In this context it is of interest to examine one particular African
population, where leprosy has been subject to detailed scrutiny for several decades. Karonga
District, a rural area in northern Malawi, was recognised in the 1970s to have a sizable leprosy
burden, and for that reason was selected for epidemiological studies in the early years of the
WHO Tropical Disease Research (TDR) initiative. A control programme had been introduced
into the district in the early 1970s, by the British Leprosy Relief Association (LEPRA), and a
major leprosy research programme began there in 1979, involving two large total population
surveys, known as the Lepra Evaluation Project (LEP), and a vaccine trial, known as the
Karonga Prevention Trial (KPT). The epidemiology and trial projects together became known
as the Karonga Prevention Study (KPS). A series of publications described disease patterns
and risk factors, as well as treatment and vaccine effects. Coincident with the vaccine trial
intervention, leprosy declined dramatically in the population, and the research programme
ultimately evolved to focus upon a variety of other health problems in the population. It now
forms a core part of the Malawi Epidemiology and Intervention Research Unit (MEIRU). 15
As a result of this history, Karonga District is among the most thoroughly studied areas in
the world with respect to leprosy. This report traces the story of leprosy work in the district over
the past five decades, describing its contributions to our understanding of the natural history
of the disease as well its changing pattern as it declined to very low level. It concludes by
comparing these patterns and trends to observations in other leprosy endemic populations.
K A RO NGA D I ST R I C T
Karonga is the north-eastern-most district in Malawi, located between latitudes 9.5° and
10.5° south of the equator. It measures approximately 110 km north-to- south, by 20–50 km
east-to-west, bordered on the east by Lake Malawi, on the north by the Songwe River (the
international boundary with Tanzania), and to the west and south by the central African
plateau (Map, Figure  1). The area has been recognised as a district since before Malawi ’s
independence in 1964, with its administrative centre in Karonga Town (the “Boma”). Health
services have been centred at the Karonga District Hospital (KDH), in the Boma, since 1932.

The main ethnic groups are Kyangonde-speaking people, who live north of Mlale, and

Like many areas in rural Africa, Karonga District has experienced immense socio-economic
change over the period covered by this report. Primary education was introduced and expanded
throughout the district after independence in 1964. The opening of the road link to the
rest of the country in 1980 brought increased commerce and all manner of manufactured
goods. Electricity supplies were introduced by small hydro-electric schemes and ultimately
by connection to the national grid in the mid-1990s, though most villages in the district are
still without power. Telephones were introduced in late 1970s and replaced by a mobile phone
network in the early 2000s. These socio-economic changes are evident in many indicators,

H I ST O RY O F L E P RO SY A N D L E P RO SY WO R K I N K A RO NGA D I ST R I C T
It is likely that leprosy has existed in Karonga district for a very long time. The disease
was seen by David Livingstone in southern Nyasaland (the old name for Malawi) in 1860. 19
A mission hospital was founded in Livingstonia, immediately west of Karonga District in
1875, and its services extended to Karonga in 1894 by Dr David Cross. The first (colonial)
government medical officer arrived in Karonga in 1919, and was involved in providing
hydnocarpus (Chaulmoogra) oil to mission stations for the treatment of leprosy. 20,21 Though
precise numbers are not known, at least several hundred leprosy patients were identified in
Karonga by the government and missionary health services during the first half of the twentieth
century, many of whom were sent to leprosaria set up first at Makete, in Tanzania, and at
Malamulo in Malawi’s southern region, and later at Kochirira, established in 1956 in Malawi’s
Central Region. It has been estimated that there were as many as 80,000 leprosy patients in
Malawi in 1965 (This was approximately 2% of the total population, but it should be noted
that the disease was considered life-long at that time). 22
The first “vertical” leprosy control programme, based initially upon dapsone monotherapy
distributed by mobile clinics, was introduced into Malawi by Lepra as a pilot project in 1965.
The work expanded to virtually the entire country (excluding the southern region, which was
covered by a Seventh Day Adventist mission), including Karonga district in 1973.23–25 Records
are available from the introduction of this Lepra Control Project (“LCP”). Case finding during
this period was largely passive, supplemented by some active detection in school surveys.
T uberculoid (TT/BT) patients received dapsone alone during this period, whereas borderline
and lepromatous patients received also thiacetazone, starting in November 1974.24 Analysis of
data from Karonga during the 1970s in comparison to data from elsewhere in Malawi suggested
that rates were higher in lowland districts, such as Karonga than in highland districts, and
particularly high in Karonga itself. 25
The recognition of considerable numbers of leprosy patients in Karonga District in the
1970s coincided with major advances in leprosy basic science. The 1971 discovery that
armadillos could be infected with M. leprae provided large numbers of bacilli for research,
and was an important factor leading to the founding in 1974 of the WHO TDR programme,
whose first steering committee was devoted to Immunology of Leprosy (IMMLEP). 26 This
led, in turn, to the development of the first serological assays, soluble antigen skin tests and
combined vaccines for leprosy. There was a need to evaluate these new tools, and Karonga
District provided a suitable site. The consequent research programme, known at first as the
Lepra Evaluation Project (LEP), thus began in 1979, with support from Lepra and other
members of the International Federation of Anti-Leprosy Organisations (“ILEP”).
The LEP project began with a first (thus “LEP-1”) house-to-house survey of virtually the
entire district (the southern tip of the district was omitted as it was evident that there was very

little leprosy in that area), beginning with a pilot study in 1979 and continuing until August
1984.
The pilot study was carried out between March and September 1979, in three villages near
Mwenilondo, in the centre of the district. 3018 individuals were interviewed and examined.
T wenty-one leprosy patients known to the LCP were found, and another 30 were newly
discovered by the LEP team, including 8 long-standing cases who had received some treatment
before the LCP had arrived in the district. In addition to confirming the high prevalence of
leprosy, the pilot study allowed initial training of staff and field testing of forms and procedures
employed in the later fieldwork.
The full LEP-1 survey was one of the most intensive case finding exercises ever carried
out for leprosy. Though a few individuals self-reported to health services over these years,
the vast majority of cases were detected actively. Not only was there an enumeration of the
total population, but great effort was made to trace and examine all individuals, regardless of
age, including by repeated visits to households in order to examine initially-absent individuals.
Details of the field procedures implemented by trained interviewers and paramedical leprosy
control assistants (LCAs) were published in 1987. 27 Much emphasis was placed upon the
diagnosis of leprosy, including medical officer review of all suspects, 4 mm punch biopsy
of lesions, and development of an algorithm to assess the certainty of leprosy diagnosis. 28
There was a major effort to evaluate new tools for diagnosis of M. leprae infection based
upon new skin test antigens and serological assays. This survey provided detailed estimates
of leprosy prevalence in Karonga District, broken down by age, sex, BCG scar status and
treatment history, as described below. Responsibility for leprosy treatment in the district was
also taken over by the LEP, with the introduction of rifampicin for borderline and lepromatous
patients in January 1981 and then the full WHO/MDT regimen for all patients in May 1983. 24
A second survey (“LEP-2”) was implemented between late 1985 and 1989, again a total
population house-to-house survey covering the same area as LEP-1, and with equal intensity.
Its primary aim was to measure leprosy incidence as a function of risk factor information
collected in LEP-1. These data were analysed to show incidence rates by age, sex, BCG
scar status, household and dwelling contact with cases, schooling and housing conditions,
and geography within Karonga District, as described below. In addition, this second survey
provided the platform for a total population randomised controlled vaccine trial, known as the
Karonga Prevention Trial (KPT). The trial was associated with the WHO/IMMLEP initiative,
and designed to test two hypotheses: (1) that a second BCG vaccination would increase
protection against leprosy and tuberculosis induced by the first (an important question in
that many countries had introduced repeat BCG policies but this had never been evaluated
in a controlled trial) and (2) that addition of M. leprae antigens to BCG vaccine would
increase protection against leprosy (which had emerged as a major recommendation of the
WHO IMMLEP programme). 26,29 Several preliminary studies were carried out in 1985 and
1986 to decide upon the appropriate BCG strain and dosage of M. leprae, based upon their
reactogenicity and ability to induce delayed-type hypersensitivity to antigens ofM. leprae.30,31
More than 120,000 individuals over three months of age were ultimately randomised to receive
either a second BCG vaccination or a placebo (if they already had scar evidence of a first
BCG vaccination) or else BCG with or without killed M. leprae (if they had no BCG scar).
Field methods and quality control procedures of the trial have been described in detail. 32 By
agreement with the Ministry of Health, routine BCG vaccination was discontinued in Karonga
District during the intake phase of the trial, to ensure that all vaccinations were recorded in
detail. Routine BCG was continued again after the trial intake, but restricted only to infants.

As it was evident by 1990 that the frequency of leprosy was declining rapidly in the
population, it was decided that another house-to-house survey of the entire district would not
be appropriate as a means of finding cases. Instead, follow-up of the vaccine trial population
was based upon a mixture of “enhanced” passive and targeted active case detection. Project
LCA staff were posted at the three hospitals and nine health centres in Karonga District, where
they examined all individuals who came either as patients or as accompaniers of patients. In
addition, house-to-house surveys were carried out each year in selected areas where 5000–
10,000 trial participants were known to have lived. Furthermore, all trial participants aged
10–60 years who were known to have lived in household contact with a leprosy patient since
1980, or who were first degree relatives of current or past leprosy patients, were traced and
examined. By May 1995, more than 64% of the trial population had been examined at least
once after vaccination, including 18,038 individuals (14.9% of the trial population) examined
in the active case detection surveys. The accumulated data were then assembled for an initial
evaluation of the vaccines.
Starting in 1996, the Karonga research programme received major funding from the
Wellcome Trust, and expanded to cover a variety of issues beyond leprosy, in particular
relating to tuberculosis, immune responses to mycobacterial vaccines, genetics, HIV , and
demographic surveillance. In association with this, leprosy case detection reverted after 1995
to more traditional passive case detection procedures, focussing only upon individuals who
self-presented to health centres or who were recognised by chance by staff involved in a variety
of other research projects. Because of its history of leprosy work in the district, and associated
expertise, project staff were repeatedly involved in reviewing suspects who self-reported at
health centres. Project medical officers were often involved in case reviews, and continued to
biopsy suspects until 2000. However, the number of new leprosy cases identified continued to
decline, and the project ’s major attention naturally turned to its other research projects.
These several stages in the project ’s leprosy activities are summarised in Table  1 and
Figure 3A. This report describes procedures and presents data on all cases identified in
Karonga District since 1973. Emphasis is placed here upon results. Full details of methods,
and full tabulations of data, are to be found in the cited publications.
Materials and methods
DATA CO L L E C T I O N F O R M S
Basic data were recorded by the LCP on leprosy patients identified during the 1970s, on
individual “control cards”, including: personal identifiers, description of lesions and sensory
loss, a body chart on which lesions were drawn, and a record of treatment. These LCP data
were later captured in detail (on computer) for all cases who were actually seen by the LEP
research programme, starting in 1979.
As the LEP was conceived from the start as a longitudinal research project, the project
developed and implemented a set of forms designed to capture data which might be relevant
for the natural history and control of the disease.
(1) Household form : Households are fundamental to the social structure of the Karonga
District population, and are defined by local convention as a group of people living
together (typically a nuclear or extended family, as it is a polygamous society), and
acknowledging the same person as “head”. The form recorded the household ’s location,
name of household head, language spoken, year of establishment, presence of a pit
latrine, major assets (such as cattle or fishing boats) and a list of household members.

Staff were trained to include recording of the BCG scar, along with birthmarks and
skin lesions of any sort, as a routine part of a general skin exam, with no discussion
of potential implications for leprosy. The form also recorded the collection of blood
(typically finger-prick capillary blood dropped on to Whatman #6 filter paper), skin snips,

(6) Detailed exam form : This was completed only for individuals considered leprosy sus-

or passive detection), details of skin lesions, nerve thickening, sensory loss (diminished
pain sensation, thermo-sensation, and anaesthesia) and disabilities, as well as a record of
slit skin smears or biopsies, all drawn on a body chart. The examiner (typically a medical
officer reviewing the subject) rated his or her “clinical certainty” that the individual had

tively the same from 1979 until 1995, when the personal (AP) and general exam forms were
combined into a “GP” (general purpose) form, which incorporated the essential data of the two
previous forms. The detailed exam form has continued to be used to the present day, although
a small number (six, by end 2023) of recent cases have been registered by the district health
services in recent years without a detailed exam form having been filled when the patient was
first seen. Information on these individuals has been extracted from the district leprosy register
for this review. All the information on the various forms has been entered – and continues to be
entered – into a computer database for analysis. These data provide the basis for the analyses
presented in this paper.
I D E N T I F I E R S
Reliable identification of individuals over time was essential for the Karonga research pro-
gramme, as for any large field survey. The fact that individuals could move, and be seen (and
hence have a “personal [alive and present] form” filled) more than once, by different field
teams, or they could be just heard about, for example named as a parent, by different field
teams (and hence have one or more “personal [dead or absent] form”), meant that individuals
could be given different identification numbers in the field (as the ident numbers were pre-
printed on the AP and DA forms). Their identify had to be recognised by careful scrutiny
of data in the coding office, on every AP and DA form (in particular name, date and village
of birth, parents ’ names, and extending to other variables, such as birthmarks recorded on
the general exam form). Procedures and computerised listings of critical data were developed
early in the project to facilitate such recognition in the project coding office. 27 This was a
critical task, throughout the programme, to ensure that each individual ended up with one
agreed ident number and that all his or her information, collected over many years, could be
accessed through that number. These identifications have been reliable and allowed tracing of
individuals and families over more than four decades.
L E P RO SY D I AG NO S I S
Given the widely recognised (but less often acknowledged in publications) problems of
diagnosing leprosy, in particular before the development of classic signs associated with nerve
damage, much effort was devoted to rigorous assessment of the certainty that any individual
had “leprosy” (clinical signs attributable to M. leprae infection). This began in the field, with
a critical assessment of “clinical certainty”, using a six-point scale from 0  = “not leprosy”, to:
1  = “possibly”; 2  = “to be considered seriously”; 3  = “most likely”; 4  = “extremely likely”;
to 5  = “certain”. 28 This assessment was typically made by a medical officer reviewing the
patient, using criteria outlined in Table  2, and recorded on the detailed exam form.

up period of 4.5 years, 35 of the 277 (13%) of the suspects were diagnosed with what was
considered to be unequivocal leprosy, and 3 of the 35 had developed disabilities. In 211 of the
277 (76%), all signs had disappeared completely. These data were discussed with reference
to other published studies of diagnostic accuracy and self-healing rates, and it was estimated
that the overall sensitivity and specificity of each clinical examination in the Karonga context
were on the order of 90% and 95% respectively.
The project was explicitly conscious of the important theoretical and practical implications
of case definitions diagnostic uncertainty, in particular the need to avoid bias (thus particular
effort was placed to blind clinical examiners to vaccination scars and the histopathologist to
clinical description of biopsied lesions), and to maximise specificity in order not to bias relative
risk measures towards unity. 40–42 Risk factor analysis showed that the M category is highly
specific, as shown in the BCG section in Results, below. Results of analyses have thus typically
been presented for N and M cases together, and then separately for “N” cases, given that the
specificity of this group is expected to be as high as possible.

We note here that, over these 50 years, some (195) patients were registered and treated

the infection and disease. Thus some individuals were found with active disease, requiring

A note on stigma
Among the initial concerns when designing the Karonga research programme was whether
there might be any stigma associated with the disease in the population, which could affect
data collection and hence bias ultimate results of the studies. A KAP (“knowledge-attitude-
practice”) questionnaire was thus included in the 1979 pilot study, including questions such
as whether individuals would be concerned to marry someone from a family known to have
vibuntu or mbuga va ukoma (local words for leprosy in Tumbuka and Nkhonde, respectively).
This exercise revealed no evidence of stigma, and the issue was not explored further in the
LEP-1 survey. Over the course of many years none of the medical officers came upon evidence
of particular fear or sensitivities to the disease in the Karonga population. 24

Environmental factors. The issue of environmental determinants across the district was
addressed by examining leprosy incidence as a function of location and population density,
as well as distance from the main road, rivers and the lake shore. 66 Incidence rates were
between two and three times higher in the north compared to the south of the district, and
lowest in the semi-urban district capital, Karonga Boma. The most obvious environmental
difference between these regions is the north ’s higher rainfall and more fertile soil. There was
no overall association between leprosy incidence and population density, although the highest
rates were observed in the least densely populated areas (rate ratio 2.57 [1.2–5.5] compared to
the most densely populated semi-urban area, adjusted for age, sex, BCG, schooling, housing
and other geographic variables). Looking at the entire district, incidence rates increased with
increasing distance from a main road, but declined with increasing distance from a river
or from the shore of Lake Malawi. It was recognised that the association with proximity
to rivers may reflect the larger number of rivers in the north of the district. Differences in
incidence rates between groups speaking different languages (e.g. higher among Kyangonde
than Tumbuka speakers) were difficult to interpret, due to confounding by area of residence,
as most Kyangonde speakers live in the northern, wetter, part of the District.
Household and dwelling contact. The implications of household and dwelling contact with
a known leprosy case were examined in detail, by examining incidence rates as a function of
contact status recorded in LEP-1.67 The main finding was that living in the same household, or
same dwelling, as a multibacillary (BI >1) case in LEP-1 was associated with three-to-six-fold
or eight-to-nine-fold increased risk of incident leprosy, respectively (Table  9). In contrast,
living with a paucibacillary case was associated with a two-fold increased risk, the same
for household or dwelling contact. Among incident cases identified in the LEP-2 survey, 15
percent had been recorded as living with known leprosy cases in LEP-1. These overall results
harboured several important details and implications.
First, it was recognised that contact status is itself subject to considerable misclassification,
given that households evolve and change membership over time. Figure  18 shows the distri-
butions of the number of individuals per household, the number of dwellings per household
(it was a polygamous society) and the number of individuals per dwelling during the LEP-1
survey. Approximately 20% of children, 30–40% of young adults and 10% of older adults
changed household over the five years between LEP-1 and LEP-2 (Figure 19). There was thus
considerable opportunity for individuals not recorded as having household or dwelling contact
in LEP-1 to have in fact shared a household or dwelling with a leprosy case at some time. The
effect of such misclassification is to reduce considerably the observed relative risk associated
with a recorded history of contact with a known leprosy case. 68

household contact with a multibacillary case makes biological sense (as it reflects intensity

with a paucibacillary case per se, the observed two-fold increase in risk being a reflection of
the likelihood that the presence of paucibacillary index cases reflected unrecorded presence or
prior contact of some household members with multibacillary cases.

Studies of M leprae infection and immunological responses
There was a major effort to study the pattern and natural history of M. leprae infection,
employing serological and skin test assays developed through the WHO/IMMLEP programme,
and later PCR. It was hoped that these studies could provide insights on two major questions:
what is the relation between patterns of infection and of disease in an endemic population, and
what determined the geographic trend of more disease in the north compared to the south of
Karonga District.
Humoral immunity–antibody assays. Early versions of the phenolic glycolipid (PGL-1)
and synthetic disaccharide glycoconjugate antigen ELISA assay were evaluated on sera from
Karonga leprosy patients, stratified by BI and clinical activity, and compared with sera from
United Kingdom resident controls with and without known exposure to mycobacteria other
than M. leprae.75 UK resident controls were used initially because of the concern that many
residents of Karonga District might have been exposed and hence either sensitised to or
actually infected with M. leprae. Antibody levels were correlated with BI and activity level
in leprosy patients, but levels in paucibacillary patients (BI = 0) were only marginally higher
than in UK controls (Figure  21). Interestingly, among the UK resident controls, the lowest
levels were observed in children below 4 years of age, and the highest levels in adults with
M. tuberculosis or M. avium-intracellulare infections, indicating an accumulation with age of
antibodies which cross react with PGL of M. leprae in a non-leprosy-endemic population.
Given that the vast majority of leprosy patients in Karonga were paucibacillary, it was
recognised that these assays were not likely to be useful in monitoring M. leprae infection in
such a population. Any threshold low enough to identify a high proportion of paucibacillary
patients (and hence of individuals with paucibacillary infections but without visible lesions),
would have very low specificity – a problem likely to be exacerbated by the presence of various
other mycobacteria (some of which might well have cross reacting antigens) in the Karonga
environment (Figures 22, 23).

antigen.76 Antibody levels had a unimodal distribution, thus showing no obvious cut-off for
“positivity”. Patterns by age and sex were similar for different cut-off levels, with levels

Molecular and genetic studies of M. Leprae. By historical coincidence, the WHO IMMLEP
programme came to an end in the early 1990s, just as leprosy was declining rapidly from
Karonga, and just when a new generation of molecular and genetic tools was introduced
and applied to leprosy. 26 Prominent among these innovations were the development and
introduction of polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and the sequencing of the M. leprae genome
in 2001.93 The Karonga programme employed these new technologies in three studies.
It was reported in 1993 that it was possible to identify M. leprae by PCR in nasal swabs of
leprosy patients and contacts, and early results suggested that the prevalence of nasal carriers
in endemic communities might be quite high. 94 An initial study was carried out in Karonga to
compare two laboratories in their ability to identify M. leprae and M. tuberculosis in nasal
swabs collected from leprosy and tuberculosis patients, along with positive and negative
controls (e.g. swabs dipped in positive sputum and swabs from nasal cavities of healthy
Europeans). The results showed that both labs were able to identify M. tuberculosis in positive
sputum and in nasal swabs of smear positive tuberculosis cases, and M. leprae in nasal swabs
of most MB cases. However, there was considerable inter-lab variation and one lab reported
finding M. leprae in several samples from tuberculosis patients. 95 The experience illustrated
the need for blind controls in such studies. This was not pursued further in Karonga.
Soon after the genome of M. leprae was sequenced it was suggested that polymorphisms
in several short tandem repeat sequences might be used to trace transmission chains. A study
was organised of 43 biopsies from 17 Karonga patients, including biopsies collected from
different sites and tissues (skin or nerve) at the same time, and from the same patient at different
times.96 The results showed considerable heterogeneity both between patients and within the
same patient between sites and over time. It was concluded from these results that the seven
repeat sequences included in this study evolved so rapidly that it would be difficult to infer
transmission links (by genetic similarity of the M. leprae) unless transmission had occurred
very recently.
Finally, the Karonga programme contributed several biopsies to an international study
which attempted to trace the origin and spread of M. leprae around the world on the basis
of comparative genomic analysis of rare single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). The study
included genetic analysis of M. leprae from 21 countries around the world, and concluded
that the species probably arose first in East Africa (represented by samples from Karonga and
from Ethiopia), from where it spread to Asia, Europe, North and West Africa and ultimately
to the Americas. 97 On the basis of these data and analyses, it appears that leprosy had been in
Karonga for a very long time indeed.

The WHO/MDT regimens were first designed by a study group organised by the WHO in 1981,
and promoted in a WHO Technical Report Series document in 1982. 44 They had never been
evaluated in a randomised controlled trial. Three efforts were made to evaluate these regimens
after their introduction in Malawi in 1983.
A preliminary evaluation was performed by comparing the development or regression of
disabilities in paucibacillary patients between two cohorts: 863 patients registered in 1983 and
placed on MDT (600 mg rifampicin supervised at 4–6 week intervals plus 100 mg dapsone
self-administered daily for 6 months; half doses in children below 15 years) versus an historical
control group of 859 patients who had been registered in 1975 and treated with dapsone
monotherapy.98 It was recognised that the comparison was not ideal, as the catchment areas
for the cohorts was not identical, and the treatment for T ype I reactions was probably more
vigorously instituted in the eighties than in the seventies. Nevertheless, it was found that
the percentage of patients who developed new disabilities or experienced a deterioration in
disabilities was broadly similar between the cohorts. The outcomes one year after completion
of MDT (31∕545 = 5.7% with new or worse disabilities) were similar to that (23∕526 = 4.4%)
at the end if at least three years of dapsone monotherapy. However, there was some evidence
that a higher percentage of patients with potentially reversible disabilities improved after MDT
than after dapsone monotherapy (48∕89 = 52% versus 18∕80 = 23%). It was commented that
ideally there should be a formal randomised comparison between these regimens but that this
was probably no longer feasible.
A subsequent study examined the risk of T ype 1 reactions and of relapses in 480 pau-
cibacillary patients treated with the WHO-MDT regimen, including 153 ascertained by active
case detection by the LEP in Karonga, and 327 who self-reported to the LCP in Karonga
and elsewhere in Malawi. 99 At the end of the treatment period the lesions had completely
disappeared in 24.7% of the patients but were judged to be still active in 4.3%. During one year
after completion of treatment, two patients were identified with new active skin lesions, giving
a relapse rate of 4.2 per 1000 person years (95% CI: 1.1–15.0 per 1000 PYs). The incidence rate
of marked type 1 reactions (renewed inflammation in previously inactive lesions) during the
first year after completion of treatment was 47.8 per 1000 person years among self-reporting
patients but zero among patients identified by active case finding. There was evidence that
the risk of type 1 reactions is closely related to the classification and stage of the disease
at detection. Four year follow-up of these patients revealed a total of 12 relapses (6.5 per
1000 person years [95% confidence interval 3.4–11.4]). 100 A relative lack of cell-mediated
immunity, as suggested by number of lesions, clinical classification and lepromin test results,
and poor compliance with the dapsone component of WHO/MDT, appeared to be associated
with a marginally increased risk of relapse.
Treatment of multibacillary patients in Malawi followed WHO ’s 1982 MDT recommenda-

Immunological studies on mycobacterial vaccine action in Karonga
The variation in effectiveness provided by BCG vaccination between different populations
and different diseases poses some of the most difficult, longstanding and important enigmas in
immunology and public health. Data from Karonga illustrate these puzzles: the same (Glaxo)
BCG which imparts high efficacy against tuberculosis in countries such as the UK was found
to provide very little protection against tuberculosis in Karonga but appreciable protection
against leprosy. Why?!

64 LEP – KPS Team
To address these questions, a major series of studies was set up to examine immune
responses to BCG vaccination in Malawi compared to responses to the same vaccine in the
UK. Responses were measured primarily in terms of in vitro interferon-gamma (IFNγ) and
other cytokine responses to various antigens, a standard method for assessing cellular immune
responses. These studies built upon the considerable evidence that natural exposure to various
different mycobacteria in the environment can impart some protection against tuberculosis and
can be responsible for population differences in the behaviour of BCG. 84,86 The interference
with BCG’s protection might involve either “blocking” of infection and sensitisation by BCG
bacilli, or “masking”, by providing a degree of heterologous protection upon which BCG
cannot significantly improve.84 The classic evidence was based largely upon studies of DTH
rather than in vitro measures of cellular immunity.
Initial studies evaluated IFNγ responses to 12 antigens from 9 species of mycobacteria
among 616 adults in Karonga, and found that the prevalence of sensitivity was higher to
antigens of M. avium, M. intracellulare and M. scrofulaceum (known as the “MAIS complex”)
than to antigens of M. tuberculosis, M. bovis, M. marinum , M. kansasii , M. fortuitum or M.
vaccae. Comparisons pre and post BCG vaccination suggested that the IFNγ responses to
M. tuberculosis PPD (“purified protein derivative”), post vaccination, increased more among
individuals with low than with high initial responses to the MAIS antigens. 92
It was found that IFNγ responses to Mtb PPD correlated with DTH responses to RT23
tuberculin, but with interesting exceptions, as 13 (5%) of 270 individuals with zero DTH
showed IFNγ responses greater than 500 pg/ml, and 7 (13%) of 53 individuals with greater
than 10 mm induration showed IFNγ responses less than 62 pg/ml 108. These exceptions were
not explained, but may indicate individuals with particular immunological deficits and with
particular susceptibility or resistance to infection.
A comparison between 483 young adults in Karonga and 180 adolescents in the UK revealed
dramatic differences between the two populations: much higher initial prevalence of IFNγ
responses to M. tuberculosis PPD in Karonga than in the UK, but much greater increases in
these responses post BCG in the UK. These results paralleled classic work on DTH responses
to BCG, and suggested that magnitude of BCG-attributable change in IFNγ might be a better
correlate of protective immunity induced by the vaccine, at least against tuberculosis, than
any other currently available measure. 109 It was not possible to test whether those with no
environmental exposure in Karonga, prior to BCG vaccination, had equivalent protection to
the UK vaccinees.
Further analysis of these data found that the higher BCG-attributable IFNγ response to M.
tuberculosis PPD among individuals with low than those with high initial IFNγ response to
MAIS antigens, which was suggested in Malawi, did not occur in the UK. It was recognised
that repeated exposure to environmental mycobacteria in Karonga was different from the less
frequent exposures in the UK context, and that it was “not possible to test directly the masking
or blocking hypotheses in humans by such methods without much larger cohort studies which
may need to include protection against tuberculosis as an outcome”. 110
These studies were followed by an investigation of IFNγ responses to particular recombinant
and purified antigens of M. tuberculosis (38KDa, MPT64, ESAT-6), M. bovis (MB70), M.
bovis BCG (Ag 85) and M. leprae (18KDa, 35 KDa, 65KDa) in young non-BCG-vaccinated
adults in Karonga. 111 The prevalence of responsiveness was highest to the M. leprae 18KDa
and 35 KDa and the M. bovis BCG Ag85 antigens, though infection with M. leprae and M.
bovis are believed to be far less prevalent than infection with M. tuberculosis in Karonga. It
was found that the prevalence of M. leprae antigen responsiveness was higher in the south

Leprosy in Malawi, 1973-2023 65
than the north of Karonga District, in contrast to the known pattern of clinical leprosy. It
was further noted that the M. leprae 18KDa antigen has close homologues in M. avium , M.
intracellulare and M. habana, and the M. leprae 35KDa antigen has a close homologue in M.
avium. It is thus possible that the IFNγ response patterns reflected prevalence of one or another
environmental mycobacterium which shares antigens with M. leprae, and could be providing
protection against leprosy in the southern half of the district, much as was inferred from the
skin test studies discussed above.
Similar studies were carried out to investigate measures of innate immunity, in terms of in
vitro responses (TNFα, IL1b, IL10) to eight mycobacterial antigens. 112 Again the prevalence
of responsiveness was far higher among adolescents in Malawi (19 to 57% responders) than
in the UK (less than 5% responders). This raised a question of whether the high prevalence
of anti-inflammatory IL10 response to mycobacteria in Malawi might be somehow related to
poor effectiveness of BCG against tuberculosis in such an environment.
Applying yet another approach, an effort was made to compare T cell clonal repertoires pre
and post BCG vaccination between Karonga and the UK, on the hypothesis that Malawians
would have more clones attributable to exposure to environmental mycobacteria and hence
have a secondary-type response to vaccination. 113 However, the magnitude of the clonal
response did not correlate well with other assays and this line of investigation was abandoned.
Several of these results were consistent with arguments that prior exposure to environmental
mycobacteria can influence to effectiveness of BCG vaccination. This led to an hypothesis that
responses to vaccination in very early infancy, at an age prior to exposure to environmental
antigens, should be similar between Malawi and the UK. A direct test of this hypothesis pro-
vided a surprising result – that responses were significantly stronger among infants in the UK
than in Malawi. Furthermore, the responses appeared to vary by season, in both populations,
being stronger among infants born in the hot and dry months (October–December) than in
those born at other times of the year in Malawi ( P = 0.002), and stronger in those born in
spring and summer than in autumn or winter in the UK ( p = 0.04). No technical reason was
found for these differences, but the seasonal relationship raised questions of whether other
infections (e.g. malaria in Malawi, respiratory infections in the UK) or nutritional factors could
be involved.114 This was followed by additional studies comparing responses to BCG in infants
in Karonga, The Gambia and the UK examining vitamin D, zinc and inflammatory markers
but no explanation was found for the differences by area and season of birth. 115,116 We note
that seasonality differences have been observed in immune responses to several vaccines. 117
Follow-up studies of vaccinated cohorts in the UK led to the recognition that BCG-
attributable IFNγ responsiveness wanes between 3 and 36 months after vaccination, but lasts
at least 14 years.118 This waning observation may help explain why the effectiveness of infant
BCG vaccination can be high in children, against systemic forms of tuberculosis, yet very
low against adult forms of tuberculosis in tropical settings. 83 And it may be relevant to our
observation that protection from the second BCG appeared to decline after 10 years. It thus
lends support to prime-boost protocols which are being explored in the context of some
mycobacterial vaccines.119,120
In addition to these studies of in vitro responses to different mycobacteria and to BCG, there
was a series of in vivo studies in mice, involving 6 species of environmental mycobacteria
from Karonga (3 species of M. fortuitum and one of M. chelonae from soil, and two strains
of M. avium from sputum). 121 Only the M. avium proliferated in the mice. Prior exposure to
the M. avium isolates was found to inhibit BCG growth and ablate IFNγ responses to BCG,

and presumably to reduce the vaccine ’s protective ability, providing a direct analogue of the
hypothesised “blocking” mechanism.
Related to these studies of immunological responses to BCG vaccine, a survey of helminth
infections was carried out among 501 schoolchildren and among 301 adolescents participating
in the BCG investigations. 122 Hookworm, Schistosoma mansoni and S haematobium were
detected in 64%, 27% and 20% of the schoolchildren and in 55%, 40% and 25% of the
immunology study subjects respectively. The prevalence of “at least one” helminth infection
was 76% among schoolchildren and 79% among the immunology study subjects. There was
no evidence for any association between the presence of a BCG scar and presence or intensity
of infections with helminths in the schoolchildren, nor evidence that BCG vaccination of
adolescents and young adults had any effect on the prevalence of helminth infections one year
later. These data testify to the very high frequency of helminth infections in this population,
a circumstance common in rural Africa at this period. We note that several authors have
postulated the helminths might predispose to leprosy, particularly MB disease, by inducing
a shift Th2 immune responses. 123,124 We have no further data on this hypothesis in Karonga
but note that helminths are ubiquitous in such environments, yet MB leprosy is rare in the
Karonga population.
This series of studies contributed importantly to the complex literature on mycobacterial
immunology, adding to arguments favouring subunit vaccines, which may avoid the growth
inhibition effects of prior environmental mycobacterial exposure, as well as prime boost
strategies to extend the duration of protection.119 The differential effectiveness of BCG versus
leprosy and tuberculosis remains unexplained, but may relate to different quantitative thresh-
olds required for immunity to the two diseases, or (more likely) to complex patterns of antigen
sharing between the many mycobacteria to which people are exposed in leprosy endemic
regions. The large number and variety of such bacteria in different environments poses a hugely
difficult problem for vaccinology and for immunological studies of mycobacteria.

population of Karonga increased substantially, as evident in the census figures shown in
Figure 2. One can use these numbers to estimate crude new case detection rates for the total
Karonga population over time, which come out to be on the order of 1 per thousand per
annum in the 1970s to 3 per million over the past decade. Of course such estimates should
be interpreted with caution, as they were associated with different methods and intensity of
surveillance, but the trend is clear.
Along with the decline in numbers, there was a substantial increase in the average age
of cases over time. One reason for this age increase is a cohort effect of the introduction of
BCG in this population 50 years ago. Another important factor is the decline in infection risk
which must have occurred, associated with the decline in source cases. The lower the risk of
infection, the longer on average it takes for infection to occur in successive birth cohorts. As
a result, the average age at infection, and of consequent disease onset, increases. Along with
this trend there must have been a tendency for a relative increase, over time, in cases with
longest incubation period, typically multibacillary disease. This has been described in other
populations with declining incidence, in particular Norway, Korea and Spain.8,9,125,126 We are
unable to describe rigorously the increase in multibacillary cases in Karonga because of the
changes in ascertainment methods and in classification criteria over time (and discontinuation
of biopsy and histopathology since 2000), which in effect meant more and more cases were
considered to be multibacillary, according to changes in treatment policies. This is a general
problem with leprosy statistics, as an expected increase in multibacillary cases (expected
as a consequence of declining incidence) is confounded by the loosening of the criteria for
considering any case to be multibacillary. However, an increase in proportion multibacillary
would fit with the observed increases in proportion disabled and proportion of males, and also
the increased average age observed in these data.

When interpreting these trends it is important to appreciate that leprosy case finding was far
less intensive in these other populations than it was in Karonga District, especially between
1979 and 1995. In addition, leprosy control programmes in this area of Africa were wound
down over the past two decades, in part as a consequence of WHO ’s declaration that leprosy
had been “eliminated as a public health problem”, globally.2 As a consequence of this “winding
down” some countries failed to report to WHO in some years, as reflected in the blank years
in Figure 34B.
Given the patchiness of the data, the problems confronted by leprosy programmes in
southern Africa, and changes in ascertainment, one must be cautious in generalising from
such data. One the other hand, it is evident that leprosy has declined substantially in this region
of Africa in recent decades, and that it appears to have declined more rapidly, and to lower
levels, in Karonga than in other populations in the country and region. Given the intensity of
the control activities in Karonga District, this makes sense.
Summary interpretation of leprosy in Karonga
The Karonga research programme documented a remarkable decline of leprosy over the past
50 years, from a high incidence and prevalence (up to 5% cumulative incidence among older
individuals) to very low levels, near to “disappearance”. Explaining this decline raises a variety

Genetic analyses of M. leprae from the district indicate that the infection has been present
in this part of Africa for a very long time, at least hundreds of years. 97
The Karonga population was noted for its high frequency of leprosy in the 1970s, an
observation which led to its selection as a research site in 1978. As in most leprosy endemic
populations, the disease was not distributed uniformly, but was concentrated in certain villages
in particular in the north of the district. The reasons for this distribution could in theory involve
genetic, behavioural, social or environmental factors. Or chance. We doubt that human genetics
plays a major role in determining local heterogeneity, despite the several hints of genetic
associations described above, 69–74 and the several observations of family clustering, which
we are more inclined to attribute to close physical contact and other shared risk factors within
the home environment. And we are aware of no behavioural differences which could explain
the distribution of the disease. There is the fact that the northern part of the district has more
rivers and somewhat different agriculture, as a consequence, opening the possibility of some

environmental factor which could have favoured survival of the leprosy bacillus in the northern
part of the district. We should also acknowledge that there is increasing appreciation that
clusters of acute infectious diseases are often attributable to chance “superspreader” events,
when circumstances happen to favour a large number of transmissions. There is no reason why
this should not occur also with leprosy, but such “events” are particularly difficult to ascertain
with a disease which has a long incubation period.
Accepting these caveats, a variety of observations suggest that local variation in exposure
to different mycobacterial antigens may have influenced the geographic distribution of lep-
rosy in the district. Environmental mycobacteria are varied and plentiful, with distributions
determined by factors such as soil type and pH, and perhaps vegetation. All humans are
exposed to these bacteria in water, food and dust. They have and share many antigens, to
which people develop sensitivity identifiable by skin tests with specific reagents and the IFNγ
assays described above. Certain patterns of sensitivity are in turn associated with differences
in leprosy risk. This mechanism was first demonstrated in classic studies of tuberculosis (e.g.
low incidence associated with prior sensitisation toMycobacterium avium-intracellulare).84,86
Its possible relevance for leprosy is suggested by patterns of skin test sensitivity antigens in
Karonga District, discussed above in the Cellular immunity - skin tests section. Given the
number and variety of environmental mycobacteria (only a minority of which have yet been
identified), around the world, this remains an extremely difficult problem to tackle, especially
as the extensive antigenic cross-reactivity between mycobacteria means there are few if any
antigens perfectly specific to the various environmental mycobacteria. This difficulty does not
mean it is not important.
Why did leprosy decline so rapidly from Karonga District (Figures  3B, 31, 32)? Three
factors obviously contributed to this: the intensive case finding and treatment programme
inherent in the two total population surveys (LEP-1 and LEP-2); the wide total-population
application of BCG vaccination; and improvements in socio-economic standards of living in
recent decades. Is it possible to weight these explanations, given the data available?
Few populations in the world have been subject to such rigorous case finding as was
implemented in Karonga District between 1979 and 1989. Not only was there systematic
censusing of the population, with scheduled visits to every household, twice during the 1980s,
but special effort was devoted to find individuals who were absent on the appointed day of
the field visit. Furthermore, paramedical staff were trained to have a high index of suspicion,
and more than 4000 biopsies were taken of suspect lesions in the field in order to maximise
the sensitivity of the case finding operation. In this context we note that many early and
“mild” cases must have been ascertained, some of which might have self-healed on their own,
without treatment. It is appropriate to bear this in mind when interpreting the numbers of
cases registered between 1979–1989. Along with this intensive case finding effort, project staff
delivered MDT to all patients in their homes, which maximised compliance and meant that
patients received their drugs even if they travelled or moved. The continued contact between
project staff and patients in their homes meant that reactions or relapses were identified and
treated early, and increased the likelihood that any additional cases in the homes of patients
on treatment would be recognised early. All these efforts must have reduced the intensity of
infection transmission in the Karonga population, and are surely one reason for the dramatic
decline during the 1990s.
The wide use of BCG vaccine was a second factor behind the rapid decline. BCG vaccina-
tion had been introduced into the district by means of campaigns in schools, in the mid-1970s,
and continued with routine infant vaccination which reached approximately 60% coverage by

74 LEP – KPS Team
1980, rising up to 90% in subsequent years (see Figure  12).128 Both case control and cohort
analyses indicated that these BCG vaccinations were providing at least 50% protection against
leprosy. The fact that BCG was selectively provided first to school children and then infants
means that its effects were selectively in the young, such that most individuals born after 1965
(for school campaigns) or 1975 (for infant vaccination) were at substantially lower risk of
disease.
The observation of 50% effectiveness of BCG in preventing leprosy meant that there
could be no placebo control in the KPT, for those without a BCG scar; and thus very few
individuals would be left without at least a single BCG vaccination when the Karonga vaccine
trial was implemented between 1986 and 1989. Virtually the entire population of all ages
(more than 122,000 individuals) were recruited into the trial, which provided BCG or BCG
+ killed M. leprae vaccine to all who had no scar, and a second BCG vaccination to 58% of
those 54,865 individuals with scar evidence of having already received a vaccine from the
national programme. The fact that the second BCG vaccine was found to induce an additional
50% protection indicated that these doubly-vaccinated individuals received 75% protection.
Though the trial indicated that this additional protection may have lasted only some 10 years,
this meant substantial protection of the doubly-vaccinated in the population until 2000. The
dramatic decline of leprosy in the 1990s is consistent with this effect.
Finally there is the effect of socio-economic improvements in the population over recent
decades. Poverty and its correlates have long been recognised to be important risk factors for
leprosy, and this was demonstrated clearly in the Karonga data, which showed poor housing
and low education achievement to be associated with highly elevated relative risks of the
disease (Table  8). The precise mechanism underlying these associations remains unclear,
but may involve closeness of physical contact, which facilitates both respiratory and skin-to-
skin transmission (thus the higher risks among dwelling- compared to household- contacts of
cases), as well as different levels of hygiene, nutrition and perhaps intercurrent infections (e.g.
with skin pathogens such asSarcoptes scabei, and various dermatophytes, or helminths), which
may influence susceptibility to either infection or to disease.122,129–132 Karonga remains a rural
population, but the road connection to the rest of the country built 43 years ago, universal
education, and modern telecommunications (mobile phones are now ubiquitous) have brought
immense changes. Among the health implications of these changes has been the considerable
decline in infant mortality from 123 to 29 per thousand live births, as recorded in the latest
census, and a dramatic decline in fertility as evident in Figure 2C.17 Though we may not
understand the precise biological mechanisms triggered by these socio-economic factors, e.g.
the way in which they affect transmission of M. leprae, or reduce the likelihood that infection
leads to manifest disease, there is no doubt that they have important implications for leprosy,
as shown in many populations.
Any discussion of the observed decline of leprosy in Karonga must acknowledge that
ascertainment efficiency has changed importantly over time, from a very high level associated
with house-to-house total population active case finding in the 1980s to a mixture of active
and passive ascertainment 1990–1995, to passive ascertainment over the past 25 years. The
absence of active surveillance, and the high prevalence of disabilities among the few cases
ascertained in recent years make it likely that a small number of cases, probably with only mild
manifestations, have escaped detection. We note that a majority of the cases now identified
in Karonga come from outside the district, but expect a few more locally born cases to be
identified in the years to come.

Each of these factors must have contributed to leprosy ’s decline in Karonga. The fact that
the decline appears to have been more rapid in Karonga than in neighbouring populations
(Figure 34), may be taken as evidence that the intensive case finding and total population
vaccination initiatives were important, as at least routine BCG vaccination and improvements
in socio-economic conditions were widespread in this region of Africa.
The Karonga project was initially conceived in association with the WHO IMMLEP
programme, in the 1980s, with an intention to study the epidemiology of M. leprae infection,
as well as of clinical leprosy. But the ascertainment of M. leprae infection on a population
scale has proven thus far unachievable, and this discussion of leprosy patterns and trends has
had to be based upon clinical case data, rather than on information on infection patterns in
the community. It is a moot point whether widespread application of any of the newer assays
for M. leprae infection would have been able to reveal underlying patterns of infection. That
remains a holy grail of leprosy epidemiology. 133
Discussion and implications
Karonga is one district in rural Africa. How representative it may be of the rest of the region
and continent is unclear, as there are few comparable data from elsewhere in Africa with which
to compare the observations described above. That said, there are several patterns evident in
these data which appear consistent, or inconsistent, with other observations and which point
to intriguing differences between leprosy in Karonga, or in Africa, and elsewhere in the world.
Though comparisons of clinical leprosy presentations between populations are complicated,
because criteria for classification have changed repeatedly over time, and have been applied
differently in different programmes, the proportion of paucibacillary cases in Karonga appears
high compared to populations in Asia and the Americas. A high proportion of PB disease
was in the past conjectured be a consistent feature of leprosy in Africa. 125 There have been
suggestions that it could be attributable to the relative ease of seeing hypopigmented patches
against dark skin. In the Karonga circumstances it might have been influenced by the intensity
of case finding, which favoured identification of early paucibacillary lesions, some of which
would have self-healed on their own, had they not been identified and put on treatment.
Whether there is truly a regional or racial difference is difficult to confirm.
A second prominent feature in Karonga has been the higher incidence of disease in
females, compared to males. This is in contrast to most populations of the world, which show
higher rates in males. Interestingly, a female excess has been reported in some other African
populations.134 While the female excess is evident for all leprosy in Karonga, it was not found
for multibacillary disease, and is thus linked to the very high proportion of paucibacillary
disease in the Karonga population. It has been commented that one reason for the excess
in males in some populations is related to differential ascertainment in many populations,
especially in Asia, where females may be less thoroughly examined because of social taboos
– females being more covered by clothes in many societies, a factor which is exacerbated by
the fact that most field workers in most programmes are male. Furthermore, social stigma
is likely to be a particularly important factor among females in many populations, insofar as
the presence of leprosy may influence marriage opportunities. A relatively high proportion of
leprosy cases among females in Karonga, and some other African populations may thus be
related to the relative absence of stigma compared with many other populations. In addition
to social taboos, there is the general observation that males are more likely than females to
contract multibacillary disease in all populations, with associated implications for severity,
skin slit smear positivity and disabilities – for this reason cases in males are in general more

76 LEP – KPS Team
likely to be ascertained than those in females. Three special characteristics of Karonga are all
relevant here: the intensity of field examinations including involvement of female paramedical
staff, the virtual absence of social stigma and the very high proportion of paucibacillary
disease.
This sex difference points to another peculiarity of leprosy, which has received little
attention. Multibacillary disease has been more prevalent among males than females in
Karonga, as in all populations of the world. There is an irony in this sex difference, insofar
as multibacillary leprosy is associated with low cellular but high humoral immune responses,
classically negative in the lepromin test, but with high levels of anti-PGL-1 antibodies. Why is
it, then, that cellular responses to mycobacteria, as evaluated by skin tests (and also reflected
in BCG scar size), are universally stronger among males than among females, after puberty (as
illustrated in Figure  25 and Table 7C in this report), whereas anti-PGL-1 titres are generally
higher among females (as shown in Figure  24). This may reflect that anti-PGL antibodies
are predominantly IgM, which is generally higher in females, 77 but could suggest some
peculiarity of a specific M. leprae antigen. It may be that some of the immunological assays
recently developed in leprosy research may be able to explain this apparently inconsistent
immunological pattern.133
Another feature of leprosy in Karonga, which may be different from some other populations
has been the distribution of lesions on the body. As shown in Figure  13, initial or single
leprosy lesions in Karonga were found predominantly on the face, back and upper arms and
much less frequently on the anterior trunk and the legs, regardless of age. Interestingly, this
pattern is similar to that reported in Uganda 135 but not in Myanmar, and in other Asian
populations, where lesions were more likely to be identified on the buttocks and lower
limbs.136–138 Might this reflect another difference between leprosy in Africa and in other,
perhaps Asian populations? 53 Might this reflect different patterns of ‘local’ environmental or
behavioural factors, perhaps skin temperature, micro-trauma, or insect exposure,.. or biotypes
of M. leprae… between populations? We have no good explanation for the observation.
The Karonga leprosy experience may have some unique features, but it is probably broadly
representative of populations in which leprosy was once frequent and from which it has
virtually disappeared in recent time. The drivers of the decline, a combination of case finding
and treatment, BCG vaccination and socio-economic improvement, are shared to different
degrees by virtually all endemic populations. Hopefully the Karonga experience described here
can provide guidance as to how best to design and apply these several interventions, perhaps
supplemented by chemoprophylaxis, or new vaccines, or attention to animal reservoirs, to
optimise future control of this ancient disease in other populations. 139
This review has brought together 50 years of observations on leprosy in a particular
population, Karonga District in northern Malawi. It provides a detailed description of the
disease, its peculiarities and its dramatic decline in this population. We wish to close by
acknowledging the foresight and support of Lepra and the WHO IMMLEP programme, along
with the other ILEP member organisations, in establishing the Karonga research initiative
long ago. Though leprosy has now virtually disappeared from the population, the project
continues to contribute to many aspects of public health, as the foundation structure of the
Malawi Epidemiology and Intervention Research Unit, MEIRU. 15
