# FLNB and TTC26 regulate ciliary Hedgehog signaling to maintain intervertebral disc matrix homeostasis in adolescent idiopathic scoliosis.
**Authors:** Jiang H, Gao Y, Lai B, Wu J, Liao T, Zhang Z, Zhou X, Zhao J, Meng Y, Cai Z, Zhang C, Lai S, Gao R, Wang C, Li J, Yang F, Ding Y, Zhou X
**Journal:** Genome Biol (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13059-026-04107-w](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-026-04107-w)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is a complex spinal deformity with evidence of contributions from multiple genetic factors, but the molecular basis of gene-gene interactions in its development remains unclear. Filamin B (FLNB) and tetratricopeptide repeat domain 26 (TTC26) have both been implicated in the regulation of primary cilia and extracellular matrix homeostasis. However, whether combined variation in these genes contributes to spinal instability has not been established.

RESULTS: We generated mice carrying heterozygous mutations in both Flnb and Ttc26 and find that these double heterozygous mice develop progressive spinal curvature during puberty, whereas single gene mutants show only mild susceptibility. We observed reduced production of collagen type II and aggrecan in the intervertebral disc, accompanied by increased expression of matrix degrading enzymes. Transcriptomic and cellular analyses show that simultaneous loss of Flnb and Ttc26 suppresses Sonic hedgehog (Shh) signaling in the nucleus pulposus, due to impaired methylation and nuclear accumulation of the transcription factor GLI2. We find that TTC26 is required for the localization of protein arginine methyltransferase 7 (PRMT7) to the primary cilium, enabling methylation of GLI2, while FLNB binds methylated GLI2 to promote its nuclear import. Restoring Shh signaling in vivo improves extracellular matrix composition in mutant discs.

CONCLUSIONS: These findings show that FLNB and TTC26 act together to regulate Shh-GLI2 signaling and maintain extracellular matrix homeostasis in the intervertebral disc. Their combined disruption compromises spine stability and may contribute to the pathogenesis of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis.

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is the most common type of pediatric spinal deformity affecting up to 4% of adolescents worldwide [1]. Most AIS patients present with cosmetic deformities such as shoulder imbalance and rib hump, and cases with severe curves, paraplegia or cardiopulmonary deficits might need intensive surgical treatment [2]. The diagnosis of AIS exclusively depends on clinical examination and radiographical evaluation. Currently, the etiology and histopathology of AIS remain unclear in part due to its genetic complexity [3]. Breakthroughs from recent genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and next-generation sequencing (NGS) of human AIS cohorts have identified at least 30 genetic loci with significant association with the onset or progression of AIS [4, 5]. However, none has shown sufficient power to cover the majority of its physiopathologic mechanisms [2]. Our previous study suggested that AIS is an oligogenic disease; nonetheless, the molecular mechanism remains elusive [6].

Spine stability depends on the integrity of a delicate system of stabilizers, including intervertebral discs (IVDs), tensile muscles and ligaments [7]. Some studies have proposed AIS as a mechanical, rotatory decompensation attributed to the deformation of the discs [8]. Radiographic studies of the spine in AIS patients showed that the wedging of the discs preceded the wedging of the vertebrae during scoliosis progression, suggesting IVD malformation to be a driver factor in AIS pathogenesis [9]. An early study using the Col2a1-Cre; Gpr126fl/fl mice [10] to model idiopathic scoliosis, which was the first bona fide model of AIS derived from loss of an AIS candidate gene, provided proof of concept for contributions from cartilaginous and dense tissues of the spine. And a later study [11] using the Cre/loxP system to create several tissue-specific knockout of Gpr126 mouse models demonstrated that alterations in Gpr126 signaling, specifically in dense connective tissues, are key drivers of scoliosis. Moreover, genetic studies in patient cohorts and animal models revealed a series of loci involved in the development and homeostasis of the extracellular matrix (ECM) of the IVD (paired box 1 (PAX1) [12, 13], GPR126 [11, 14], SRY-box transcription factor 6 (SOX6) [15, 16], etc.), which supports this hypothesis. However, the mechanism and pathways of these variations, which regulate IVD and ECM, are poorly studied.

The relationship between ciliary defects and AIS has been emphasized [2, 17]. Cilia can be classified as motile and non-motile (primary) cilia. Studies utilizing zebrafish models of motile cilia defects demonstrated that impaired cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow leads to idiopathic scoliosis (IS)-like spine curvatures [18, 19]. Additionally, the Reissner fiber regulated by cilia in the CSF was found to be necessary for spinal straightening [20]. Despite these advantages, key differences in the physiology of the zebrafish make it difficult to translate these research findings to AIS patients, since the Reissner fiber has not been identified in humans [17]. Although mutations in some genes associated with primary cilia are also reported to be associated with AIS (kinesin family member 7 (KIF7) [21], POC5 centriolar protein homolog (POC5) [22], etc.), the role of primary cilia in AIS is unclear. The primary cilium is a microtubule-based organelle that projects from the cell surface and plays important roles in mechano- and chemo-sensation and transduction during organ development and is found in almost all eukaryotic cells, including those in IVDs [23].

Our previous study discovered the digenic inheritance of filamin B (FLNB) and tetratricopeptide repeat domain 26 (TTC26) mutations in some AIS patients [6]. FLNB is an actin-binding protein that mediates the structure and function of the actin cytoskeleton and was identified at the basal body of the cilia, suggesting its role in cilia-associated signal transduction [24]. TTC26 (also known as intraflagellar transport 56 [IFT56]) is a highly conserved intraflagellar transport (IFT) B protein that localizes to the cilia [25]. The IFT56hop mouse mutant (MUT) develops polydactyly, hydrocephalus and male sterility characteristic of ciliopathy. Mechanically, the IFT56hop MUT disrupts the integrity of the IFTB complex and cilia-dependent hedgehog (Hh) signaling transduction without affecting cilia biogenesis [25, 26]. However, whether altered cilia-dependent cell signaling pathways contribute to disease pathogenesis in AIS patients carrying double mutations of FLNB and TTC26 remains unclear.

Here, we generated a novel mouse model of AIS carrying double heterozygous mutations of Flnb and Ttc26. Flnb−/+; Ttc26−/+ mice exhibited puberty-onset progressive curves, while mice with only one mutation showed minor susceptibility to scoliosis. Further analysis of Flnb−/+; Ttc26−/+ mice demonstrated that changes in the matrix metabolism of IVDs might contribute to the IS-like phenotype. Additionally, loss of both FLNB and TTC26 down-regulated the Hh signaling pathway in the nucleus pulposus (NP), leading to ECM disorders of IVDs. FLNB and TTC26 synergistically regulated GLI family zinc finger 2 (GLI2) accumulation in the nucleus, with TTC26 regulating GLI2 methylation through protein arginine methyltransferase 7 (PRMT7) in cilia and FLNB binding directly to GLI2 to facilitate transport of GLI2 into nucleus. Moreover, the up-regulation of Hh signaling by patched 1 (Ptch1) or suppressor of fused protein (Sufu) knockdown restored ECM metabolism of Flnb−/+; Ttc26−/+ IVDs. The elucidation of such mechanisms may provide a better understanding of the oligogenic nature of AIS and help develop new and effective therapies for treating AIS.

We focused on the digenic inheritance of FLNB and TTC26 as this is the most frequent combination in our previous study, and were the two top-ranked genes respectively in our gene-based burden test [6]. Mouse androgenetic haploid embryonic stem cells (AG-haESCs) were injected into oocytes (intracytoplasmic AG-haESC injection, ICAHCI) to produce semi-cloned (SC) animals since this system allows the production and analysis of sufficient numbers of SC offspring carrying multiple heterozygous MUT genes in one generation [27–30]. SC mice carrying double mutations of Flnb (Flnb c.376_382del) and Ttc26 (Ttc26 c.4014_4015insAT) (referred to as Flnb−/+; Ttc26−/+ or DKD) and SC mice carrying single mutation of Flnb or Ttc26 (referred to as Flnb−/+ and Ttc26−/+, respectively) were generated (Fig. 1a and Additional file 1: Fig. S1a). In each experiment, approximately 160 oocytes were microinjected, and around 90% of the injected embryos developed to the two-cell stage by the next day. These two-cell embryos were then transferred into the oviducts of ICR surrogate mothers (20 embryos per oviduct). Because the AG-haESCs carried Flnb– and Ttc26– mutations, all resulting offspring were Flnb+/– and Ttc26+/– biallelic heterozygotes. Each experiment typically yielded approximately ten female heterozygotes. Over 90% of DKD, Flnb−/+ and Ttc26−/+ SC pups grew up to adulthood and showed similar growth profiles to those of wild-type (WT) SC mice (Additional file 1: Fig. S1b). An X-ray analysis was then performed at postnatal day 30 (P30) and P90 (Fig. 1b). A Cobb angle greater than 10 ° in the coronal plane was defined as scoliosis. Spine curvatures were observed at P30 (onset of puberty) in 6 of 8 DKD SC mice, while only 1 and 2 mice showed scoliosis in Flnb−/+and Ttc26−/+ SC mice, respectively (Fig. 1c, d). Although there was a slight increase in MMP13 expression within the growth plate of P30 Ttc26−/+ IVDs, no significant difference was observed between Flnb−/+; Ttc26−/+ and WT SC mouse groups Additional file 1: Fig. S3b). SP7 IHC staining showed a similar trend Additional file 1: Fig. S3c). We also investigated whether Flnb and Ttc26 overexpression could restore the ECM metabolic process in DKD NPs. Overexpression of either Flnb or Ttc26 failed to restore the expression of Gli1 and Ptch1 in DKD NPs (Fig. 3i). However, combined transfection of DKD NPs with Flnb- and Ttc26-overexpression plasmids rescued the activation of Hh signaling and protein expression of COL-II and ACAN (Fig. 3i, j and Additional file 1: Fig. S4c), suggesting that FLNB and TTC26 synergistically regulate the Hh-Gli pathway to regulate the NP ECM metabolic process.

Furthermore, the expression of GLI1 and PTCH1 was significantly decreased in primary NP cells of AIS patients compared with those of WT healthy individuals after Shh treatment (Fig. 3k). Consistently, RT-qPCR analysis showed that gene expression of COL2A1 and ACAN was significantly down-regulated, whereas gene expression of MMP3 was significantly up-regulated in primary NP cells of AIS patients compared with controls (Fig. 3l), validating Hh signaling dysregulation and ECM metabolic imbalance in NPs of AIS.

The activation and transduction of vertebrate Hh signaling depends on primary cilia [34]. To elucidate the role of FLNB and TTC26 in Hh signaling, we first investigated whether primary ciliogenesis was affected in DKD NP cells. The spine, including IVDs, was stained for cilia markers ADP-ribosylation factor-like 13B (ARL13B) and α-Tubulin and it was confirmed that the ciliary ratio of NP cells was unaffected in DKD mice (Additional file 1: Fig. S5a, b). We also performed immunofluorescence staining for cilia in NP cells from the groups of SC mice. About 83.3% and 78.7% (p = 0.422) of the cells in WT and DKD groups had normal cilia structures, respectively (Additional file 1: Fig. S5c, d). Meanwhile, DKD NP cells exhibited a slightly decreased cilia length compared with WT NP cells after serum starvation (Additional file 1: Fig. S5c). Collectively, these data indicate that the dysregulated Hh signaling of Flnb−/+; Ttc26−/+ NP cells is independent of cilia formation.

After Shh bound to the receptor PTCH1, the Smoothened (SMO) receptor was activated and enriched in the primary cilium, which in turn activated the transcriptional effectors of the Hh pathway. Then, GLI2 accumulates in primary cilia, dissociates from the negative regulator SUFU and translocates to the nuclear, leading to downstream target gene expression [35]. The SMO assay showed that SMO localization in cilia was unaffected in DKD NP cells following Shh stimulation (Additional file 1: Fig. S5e, f), indicating that the loss of FLNB and TTC26 causes Hh signaling dysregulation downstream of SMO accumulation in cilia.

Since SOX9 regulates the transcription of COL2A1 and ACAN genes [36, 37], we first used the online JASPER database to identify putative GLI2-binding sites (GBS) in the genomic sequence adjacent to the transcription start site of the SOX9 gene and found that no putative site was predicted. Whether GLI2 binds to the ACAN promoter and activates its transcription was also explored using the aforementioned experiments. A nonsense mutation of the Ttc26 gene is harbored in the hop mouse, characterized by a hopping gait, polydactyly, hydrocephalus and male sterility, which might be attributed to the impaired dissociation of the GLI-SUFU complex [26]. Since TTC26 could not directly interact with GLI2 or SUFU [26, 38, 39], we speculated that other proteins might be involved. Additional TTC26-associated components that regulate the Hh signal pathway were identified using co-immunoprecipitation coupled with mass spectrometry (Co-IP/MS). HEK 293 T cells stably expressing GFP-TTC26 (WT) or GFP-TTC26R50C MUT were utilized. The present study has made several key discoveries: (1) mice heterozygous for Flnb and Ttc26 genes are more predisposed to progressive scoliosis during puberty, confirming the hypothesis that AIS is an oligogenic disease. (2) The expression of COL-II and ACAN was down-regulated in discs from DKD SC mice, which showed no obvious malformation of the bone structure. Loss of FLNB and TTC26 genes collaboratively suppressed the Shh signaling pathway in NP cells, thereby inhibiting the expression of COL-II and ACAN. (3) TTC26 regulates GLI2 methylation through PRMT7 binding and ciliary localization, and FLNB imports methylated GLI2 into the nucleus through its direct interaction with GLI2. Taken together, these observations suggested that FLNB and TTC26 co-regulated Hh-GLI2 signaling, playing a pivotal role in the postnatal hemostasis of ECM and IVD, and might be a therapeutic target for the treatment of AIS.

AIS is a multifactorial condition with variable recurrence risk and expression in curve severity, type and location [47]. The inability to identify a specific disease-causing gene suggests a polygenic inheritance model [48]. Our previous exome sequencing study of both AIS trios and sporadic patients identified about 20% of oligogenicity in AIS patients and the number of rare damaging variants as an independent prognostic factor for curve progression [6]. Recent studies also revealed a polygenic burden of rare variants in cytoskeletal [49] and ECM-related processes [50] in AIS patients. Meanwhile, the polygenic risk score used for the prediction of the onset and progression of scoliosis showed promising results and might be beneficial for screening and early intervention [51]. A better understanding of the mechanism underlying the oligogenic nature of AIS may aid in the identification of new and effective strategies for better treatment in the future.

The role of IVD, especially the composition and hemostasis of ECM, has been highlighted in the etiology of AIS [8, 52, 53]. The vertebrate IVD is composed of fibrocartilaginous tissues encapsulating a gel-like NP tissue, which arises from the notochord tissue [54]. Cells in the NP are described as “chondrocyte-like” cells expressing mainly COL-II. During the postnatal development of the NP, notochordal-like cells are gradually replaced by less metabolic cells in the first decade, resulting in a change in the ECM composition and the strength and integrity of the IVD [53]. Meanwhile, increased loading of the spine due to the rapid pubertal growth puts high mechanical demands on the IVD, and IVD disorders may lead to compromised spine stability and scoliotic deformity [8]. Previous clinical studies found no obvious malformation of the bony structures in AIS patients but some subtle changes in IVDs, such as wedged IVDs with shifted NP to the convexity of the curve [55], reduced proteoglycan contents in NP [56] and altered distribution of collagen fibers in the AF [9]. Interestingly, the present study found reduced COL-II expression in Flnb−/+; Ttc26−/+ SC mice, consistent with the IHC findings of the NP tissues from AIS patients. Altogether, these findings support the critical role of the ECM hemostasis of the IVDs in the onset of AIS.

The Hh signaling pathway plays a crucial role in embryonic patterning and postnatal tissue development. The Hh family consists of Shh, desert hedgehog (Dhh) and Indian hedgehog (Ihh) [35]. Shh has been reported to play a unique role in regulating IVD formation and postnatal maintenance [57]. The expression of Shh was detected in the postnatal NP cells, and blockage of the Shh signaling pathway resulted in dramatic histological and molecular changes in NPs of the neonatal mouse IVD [57, 58]. The current study identified a down-regulated Shh signaling pathway in NP tissues of the Flnb−/+; Ttc26−/+ SC mice through RNA-sequencing and RT-qPCR assays. In addition, down-regulated Shh signaling resulted in reduced expression of a differentiation marker Sox9 and ECM components, including Col2a1 and Acan. Furthermore, the simultaneous addition of FLNB and TTC26 to DKD NP cells restored the expression of Hh targets Gli1 and Ptch1 and ECM components COL-II and ACAN to a normal level, validating the role of FLNB and TTC26 in Shh signaling. Besides, we found that GLI2 bound directly to the promotor of both COL2A1 and ACAN genes to activate gene transcription, underscoring the importance of the Hh-GLI2-COL2A1/ACAN axis in regulating the growth and differentiation of postnatal IVD.

The GLI family was first identified as the downstream effector of the Hh signaling pathway and consists of three proteins: GLI1, GLI2 and GLI [41]. As the major activator of Hh signaling, GLI2 dissociates from the inhibitor SUFU, accumulates in the nucleus and induces target gene expression after Shh stimulation. However, the mechanism by which GLI2 was activated in the cilia and transported into the nucleus is unclear [36]. Our data demonstrate that TTC26 is indispensable for PRMT7-mediated GLI2-methylation. Although no direct interaction was identified between TTC26 and GLI2 [26], TTC26 and PRMT7 form complexes, which further stabilize their interaction. TTC26 deficient cells showed decreased ciliary localization of PRMT7 and cilia-targeted PRMT7 overexpression rescued GLI2 methylation and Hh signaling activation, suggesting that TTC26 is necessary for the intraflagellar transport of PRMT7 and the cilia is a major regulatory site for PRMT7.

In addition to crosslinking and anchoring actin filaments, filamins serve as signaling scaffolds by interacting with other binding partners, including intracellular signaling molecules and transcription factors [43, 59]. In mice, homozygous deletion of Flnb impairs the differentiation of chondrocyte precursors [60], heterotopic ossification of the disc tissues due to the cell fate change of the AF cells into chondrocytes, and severe skeletal malformations [33]. The skeletal defects of Flnb deficient mice strongly resemble the human skeletal disorders associated with Flnb mutations, suggesting that FLNB can regulate disc development [33]. In this study, our data revealed that FLNB participates in Shh signaling by modulating GLI2 nucleus accumulation. FLNB interacted directly with GLI2, and this interaction was abolished in the GLI2 mutant of the methylation sites (GLI2 R225/227 K). Prior studies have suggested that the interaction of GLI2 with SUFU inhibits its nuclear translocation, with the GLI2 mutant R225/227 K showing enhanced interaction with SUFU [29]. Our findings indicate that under Shh stimulation, FLNB facilitates the import of methylated GLI2 into the nucleus, thereby activating the Shh signaling pathway.

The synergistic effects of FLNB and TTC26 in vitro is consistent with more severe deformity of the digenic heterozygous mice, suggesting FLNB and TTC26 epistasis [61]. Adopting the original definition of epistasis [61], we discovered two patients from AIS trios carrying trans heterozygous variants in FLNB and TTC26. In mice, homozygous deletion of Flnb results in congenital scoliosis-like phenotype [60]. In our study, heterozygous mutation of Flnb or Ttc26 leads to decreased incidence of scoliosis than digenic heterozygous mice. We hypothesized that the function of the NP cells is severely affected by these two variants, which impaired the biomechanical stability of the spine leading to spine curvature, because both proteins function together at the cilia-dependent HH-Gli2 signaling and are necessary for the growth and differentiation of postnatal IVD.

This study has several limitations. First, we only used the tail disc microinjection model to explore the therapeutic effects of ECM metabolism on Hh signaling. However, its effect on scoliosis needs to be further investigated. Second, our study solely examined the downstream molecular mechanism of digenic inheritance of FLNB and TTC26. In future, other variant combinations need to be investigated to identify a common pathway involved in the development of spinal deformity. We shall continue to study these issues further in future experiments.

In this study, we found that loss of both FLNB and TTC26 inhibited the Hh signaling pathway in NP cells, causing an imbalance in IVD ECM metabolism, and inducing spinal instability and scoliosis. GLI2 served as a downstream molecular target of FLNB and TTC26 mediating their effects on NP ECM homeostasis. Administration of a small-molecule agonist of the Hh pathway rescued the metabolic disorder of Flnb
−/+; Ttc26−/+ discs. In brief, our findings demonstrate that the FLNB/TTC26–Hh–GLI2 axis participates in AIS pathogenesis and may be a new therapeutic strategy for treating AIS.
