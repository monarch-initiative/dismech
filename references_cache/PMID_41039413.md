Goltz syndrome, also known as focal dermal hypoplasia, is an X-linked dominant 
genetic disorder caused by mutations in the PORCN gene, mainly characterized by 
developmental impairments affecting the skin, hair, bones, teeth, and eyes. This 
study reports a case with extensive skin dysplasia, limb malformations, and 
short stature. The genomic DNA whole exome sequencing of the child at 1 month of 
age revealed a mutation in the PORCN gene, confirming the diagnosis of Goltz 
syndrome. When she was 3 years old, she suffered from weight loss (W<-3SD) and 
was short for her age (H<-3SD). The growth hormone stimulation test confirmed 
growth hormone deficiency. She was treated with long-acting growth hormone 
(0.2 mg/Kg/week) for 2 years and 9 months, leading to a significant increase in 
height, with an average annual growth rate of 9.4 cm, without any side effects 
after three years of follow-up. Goltz syndrome with short stature may be 
associated with growth hormone deficiency, and long-term growth hormone therapy 
can achieve clinical benefit.

Focal dermal hypoplasia (FDH), also known as Goltz syndrome, is a rare X-linked dominant disorder caused by mutations of the PORCN gene [1]. It presents with developmental abnormalities in the skin, bones, teeth, eyes, and various systems due to defective mesoderm and ectoderm. Many children with Goltz syndrome are shorter than average because of skeletal issues and a lack of growth hormone. In November 2024, the Indian endocrinologist Namburi Divyasri [2] reported a patient with Goltz syndrome who exhibited a satisfactory response to treatment with growth hormone for short stature. So far, there have been no other reports in this regard either from China or other countries. This study presents a case with Goltz syndrome and short stature who was treated with long-acting growth hormone at our hospital in 2021. We followed the case for three years to assess the patient’s growth and development. The written informed consent was obtained from the child and her family.

The case was a 3-year-old girl who was admitted for multiple scar-like lesions on her skin all over her body, along with slow growth of her height and weight over the past 3 years. The girl was finally diagnosed with Goltz syndrome with short stature. The child was born to a second gravida mother, via cesarean section at 37 weeks, with a birth weight of 2.6 kg (P25 ~ P50), length of 49 cm (P50 ~P75), and head circumference of 32 cm (P25 ~ P50). There was no history of asphyxia or resuscitation. After birth, the dermatologist found that the child’s facial skin showed an orange peel-like change accompanied by laxity, subcutaneous tissue atrophy, and syndactyly of the second and third fingers of the right hand. At 1 month of age, whole exome sequencing of the child and parents revealed a mutation in the PORCN gene (NM_203475:exon3:c.178G > A(p.G60R)) in the child, which confirmed Goltz syndrome. At 2 years of age, the patient underwent surgery to separate her right hand fingers at Fudan University Children’s Hospital in Shanghai. She was breastfed after birth and started eating solid foods at 6 months. She received timely vaccinations. Her height and weight gain were slow falling behind her peers. Her height was 71 cm at 1 year (−2SD~−1SD, 69.7 cm ~ 72.3 cm), 79 cm at 2 years (−3SD~−2SD, 77.3 cm ~ 80.5 cm), and 84.5 cm at 3 years (<−3SD, 85 cm). At 2 years, the girl could say simple sentences, and she started walking independently at 1.5 years. The whole-exome sequencing of both parents were normal. The girl had an older sister who was 6 years old and healthy. There was no family history of hereditary diseases. The patient’s father was 170 cm tall, and her mother was 155 cm tall, with a target height of 156 cm.

On physical examination at 3 years old and 1 month, her weight was 9.5 kg (<−3SD, 10.5 kg), her height was 84.5 cm (<−3SD, 86 cm), looking smaller for her age (P50: 14.4 kg, 96.6 cm), and her language skills were at par with her peers. She presented with extensive skin dysplasia, characterized by spotty depigmentation and atrophic erythema distributed along the Blaschko lines, with some areas merging into patches resembling scar-like changes (Fig. 1A). The child had sparse, dull hair, large area of skin dysplasia on her forehead and around her lips, a flat nasal bridge, a fissure on the left ala nasi, and asymmetry in her lip line and both nostrils (Fig. 1B). The child had oligodontia, accompanied by misalignment and enamel hypoplasia (Fig. 1C). Her neck and thyroid examination showed no abnormalities and cardiac and pulmonary examinations were normal. The child’s abdomen was soft, with no palpable enlargement of the liver or spleen. No abnormalities were seen in the external genitalia and no spinal deformities were observed. The right hand showed a lobster-like appearance. The right index and middle fingers exhibited syndactyly which was corrected after surgery. The child had poorly developed nails, with absent nail in the 5th finger. The second and third toes on the left foot were fused (Fig. 1D), and neurological examination detected no abnormalities.

Laboratory examination at 3 years old: blood count, urinalysis, liver and kidney function tests, electrolyte levels, fasting blood glucose, glycated hemoglobin, thyroid function, adrenal cortex hormones, and gonadal hormones were all within the normal range for her age group. Immunoglobulin levels were normal, and the total 25-hydroxyvitamin D level was 58 ng/ml. β-hCG was < 0.100 U/L, IGF1 was 132 ng/ml (49–289 ng/ml), and IGFBP3 was 3.71 µg/ml (0.9–4.3 µg/ml). The karyotype was 46XX. She underwent stimulation tests with arginine and clonidine, with blood samples taken at 30, 60, 90, and 120 min to measure growth hormone levels. A peak < 5 ng/ml indicates complete deficiency, and a peak < 10 ng/ml indicates partial deficiency (Table 1).

Treatment and follow-up process: While ensuring enough sleep, some exercise, and a balanced diet, long-acting growth hormone was prescribed subcutaneously at a dose of 0.2 mg/kg per week from 3 years and 1 month old. Follow-ups were conducted every three months. The patient received long-acting growth hormone for 2 years and 9 months, and her height increased from 84.5 cm to 108.8 cm. There were no side effects, and the average annual growth rate was 9.4 cm. The treatment stopped because of financial constraints (Table 2,Fig. 4).

Goltz syndrome is an extremely rare X-linked dominant genetic disorder. It was first described by Goltz in 1962 [3]. In 2007, researchers identified mutations of the PORCN gene as the etiology. The PORCN gene is located on chromosome Xp11.23 [1, 4]. More than 200 PORCN variants have been identified so far [5]. A study from Denmark and Northern Europe reported that Goltz syndrome affects approximately 1.2 subjects per million people [6]. To date, nearly 300 patients have been identified worldwide [7]. Nearly 90% of patients with FDH are female. They are either heterozygous or mosaics for pathogenic variants of PORCN, of whom 95% carry de novo mutations and 5% inherited the mutant variant from one parent [7]. This article reported a patient with PORCN gene mutation, with no abnormalities in the parents’ genetic tests. Male patients with FDH might be mosaics for de novo pathogenic mutations of PORCN [8] or have Klinefelter syndrome with a 47,XXY karyotype [9], since hemizygous male fetuses with pathogenic variants of the PORCN gene may not survive in utero.

Goltz syndrome lesions involve multiple organs throughout the body, with dermatologic manifestations being the most common features. These can include congenital patchy underdevelopment of skin, congenital nodular lipomatosis, congenital skin pigmentation, facial capillary malformations [10, 11], trunk, limbs and nail abnormalities [8, 12], such as defects, alopecia, diffuse hair thinning, and fine hair [10], as well as papillomas and vascular fibromas [8]. Craniofacial abnormalities in Goltz syndrome comprise microcephaly, facial asymmetry, nasal wing depression, and cleft lip/palate [13]. Approximately 70% of subjects suffer from oral soft tissue defects, and more than 90% of the affected population suffer from oral hard tissue defects, with more than half of the patients exhibiting enamel grooves, peg-shaped teeth, and/or enamel hypoplasia [14]. Skeletal deformities primarily manifest with syndactyly, polydactyly, oligodactyly and ectrodactyly [10]. Some cases also exhibit lymphedema [15]. Additionally, some patients may experience cataracts, strabismus, eyelid and conjunctival papillomas [16], sensorineural hearing loss, hydrocephalus, agenesis of the corpus callosum, Arnold-Chiari malformation [17], growth and developmental impairments [18, 19], behavioral and cognitive problems [4], and gynecological abnormalities [20].

Nearly 3/4 of patients with Goltz syndrome have short stature and low body weight [21]. The reasons for short stature are complex and may include feeding, swallowing, and chewing difficulties, as well as gastroesophageal reflux and nutritional deficiencies [22]. Although the function of PORCN is not yet clear, it is currently believed to be involved in encoding the regulation of wingless protein (Wnt). Goltz syndrome is known to be caused by developmental abnormalities of the mesoderm and ectoderm, and Wnt signaling has been confirmed to play an important role in ectoderm development [21]. The pituitary gland originates from ectodermal tissue and is involved in the Wnt signaling pathway. Growth hormone, secreted by the pituitary gland, is a key determinant of linear growth during childhood [23]. Therefore, the short stature observed in some patients with Goltz syndrome may be related to abnormal pituitary development and insufficient growth hormone secretion.

Under physiological conditions, the synthesis and secretion of growth hormone are regulated by hypothalamic growth hormone-releasing hormone and somatostatin together. To stimulate growth hormone secretion and mimic physiological conditions, this study selected arginine and clonidine as pharmacological stimulants. Normally, the peak growth hormone concentration should be greater than 10 µg/L. The child’s peak growth hormone level is less than 10 µg/L, indicating growth hormone deficiency, which may be related to underdevelopment of the anterior pituitary. The Wnt signaling pathway may influence the expansion of the Rathke’s pouch, the primordial tissue of the anterior and intermediate lobes of the pituitary gland [24–26], as well as promote the postnatal expansion of the pituitary gland, thereby affecting the secretion of growth hormone [27].

The child’s bone age did not show significant delay, but the child had gradually fallen increasingly behind peers in height development since birth, with growth rates between 1 and 2 years and 2–3 years both below the 25th percentile (9.9 cm and 8.2 cm, respectively). At the age of 3, the height was below the − 3 SD level compared to peers. Since the child had multiple congenital abnormalities besides short stature, idiopathic short stature was a likely possibility. Moreover, short stature can negatively affect children’s mental well-being and social interactions. Considering the child’s low serum growth hormone levels, the child was considered eligible for growth hormone treatment. To reduce the frequency of injections and improve treatment compliance, the parents chose a long-acting growth hormone at a dose of 0.2 mg/kg per week for subcutaneous injection. The patient’s height significantly increased after 2 years and 9 months of treatment, resulting in an increase from 84.5 cm to 108.8 cm, with an average growth rate of 9.4 cm per year. Compared to peers of the same age, the patient’s height percentile rose from below the − 3 SD level to above the − 2 SD level. After the child received growth hormone treatment, the increase in height promoted the child’s social and psychological health development, and her parents are very satisfied with the height benefits. However, due to the financial difficulties, the child was unable to continue receiving growth hormone injections. After one year of follow-up, the child only grew 2.9 cm and no side effects were detected during follow-ups, which indicate the growth hormone is beneficial to the short stature in Goltz syndrome.

Children with Goltz syndrome typically present with skin, limb deformities, and facial deformities, but there are also individual case reports of tumors, specifically a 16-year-old patient with papillary thyroid carcinoma [28] and a 38-year-old patient with multiple basal cell carcinoma [29]. The PORCN gene is involved in the secretion and signal transduction of Wnt proteins, with the classical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway playing a key role in cancer development [28, 30]. However, the relationship between Goltz syndrome and tumor susceptibility is still unclear and requires further in-depth research. The growth hormone receptor is expressed in various tissues throughout the body, and growth hormone promotes cell growth and proliferation through its receptors [31]. Growth hormone can serve as nutritional support during the perioperative period [32], but some studies have shown that growth hormone can promote the proliferation of malignant tumors [33, 34], thus the effects of growth hormone on malignant tumors remain unclear. For children with Goltz syndrome, if there is still insufficient growth despite adequate nutrition, further evaluation should be conducted to determine whether there is growth hormone deficiency. Meanwhile, it is also necessary to further investigate the long-term side effects of growth hormone replacement therapy and the final height outcomes.
