# Treatment for primary avascular necrosis of the lower limb in childhood.
**Authors:** Ball S, Thomas R, Galloway AM, Davies LM, Gray K, Brennan S, Johnston RV, Williams N, Munns CF, Pacey V

## Content

To assess the benefit and harm of pharmacological interventions for paediatric primary lower limb AVN on function, pain, patient-reported outcomes, and radiological outcomes. The secondary objective for all four of the reviews is to evaluate health equity by assessing the effects across different geographical regions, gender, and age groups.

Paediatric primary lower limb avascular necrosis (AVN) is a rare, self‐limiting, multifactorial bone disorder characterised by disruption to the vascular supply to bone tissue, leading to ischaemic damage, joint collapse, or deformity [1, 2]. It predominantly affects weight‐bearing joints and significantly impacts physical activity, participation, and quality of life [3, 4]. The aetiology and pathogenesis have yet to be fully understood, limiting the development of consistent preventative and treatment strategies [2]. Typically, primary lower limb AVN affects the epiphysis of long bones, the femoral head (Legg‐Calve‐Perthes disease, herein known as Perthes disease) being the most common site affected (0.4 to 29.0 per 100,000 children and adolescents) [4]. Other skeletal regions affected by AVN include the distal femoral metaphysis, femoral condyles, tibial metaphysis and diaphysis, talus, navicular (Kohler’s disease), and metatarsal heads (the second metatarsal head being the most commonly affected, referred to as Freiberg’s disease) [5, 6]. The prevalence rates at other locations are unknown. Similarities are seen with age and sex differences, with Perthes disease and Kohler’s disease predominantly affecting males aged between 4 and 8 years and 2 and 10 years respectively, whilst Freiberg’s disease frequently affects females typically aged 11 to 17 years [4, 7, 8].

Children/adolescents with primary AVN frequently present with or without limping gait of insidious onset, pain localised at or referred from the necrotic site, or a reduction in range of motion (ROM) at the affected joint [1]. Primary lower limb AVN is typically diagnosed using a combination of clinical examination and radiography and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Diagnostic classifications to diagnose, classify stage, and monitor progress have been established for Perthes disease. A recent Delphi study (manuscript in preparation) by the authors of this protocol reached consensus for diagnostic classifications with modified Waldenström classification [9] being ranked the most important for clinical use, followed by modified Herring (lateral pillar) classification [10] and perfusion MRI [11]. Freiberg’s disease is graded using the Smillie classification [12], whilst no other location utilises formal diagnosing classifications, with diagnosis based on clinical symptoms and plain radiographs [6].

The precise pathophysiology remains unknown for all locations. However, it does appear linked to ischaemic attacks disrupting blood supply to the bone [2][13]. Various studies have investigated contributing causes such as environmental risk factors, genetic factors, and overuse mechanical factors; however, there remains no consensus for any location [6, 14].

A common feature amongst all primary lower limb AVN locations is that they appear to be self‐limiting in nature, with revascularisation frequently occurring without treatment [4, 5, 6]. However, varying time periods of disease resolution are seen between AVN locations: as short as 3 to 12 months in milder cases, or upward of 2 to 5 years for more severe cases or those with Perthes disease [5, 6, 9, 15]. A consequence of primary lower limb AVN and the lengthy healing and resolution phase is that symptomatically, children and adolescents frequently develop pain both acute and chronic, negatively affecting participation in usual activities and reducing quality of life [16, 17]. Given the self‐limiting nature of the condition, clinical decision‐making is inherently complex, particularly in determining whether intervention is warranted and, if so, identifying the most appropriate type.

Recommended interventions vary for each location. However, similarities are seen: initial active watching and reduced weight‐bearing, followed by use of orthotic devices with or without physical interventions, and/or surgical interventions [18, 19, 20, 21]. The absence of standardised non‐surgical intervention protocols coupled with variability in surgical timing and methods contribute to inconsistent management approaches and clinical outcomes [20, 22]. Despite extensive research, particularly in Perthes disease, no consensus or clinical guideline exists for managing paediatric primary lower limb AVN.

Non‐surgical interventions are typically adopted to reduce the mechanical load upon the bones, thereby minimising the risk of further microdamage, alleviating symptoms, and preventing secondary complications such as reduced muscle length or strength [15, 23, 24]. Similarities in methods of non‐surgical interventions between all primary lower limb AVN sites are seen within the literature [12, 15, 25, 26]. These techniques have been studied at varying lower limb AVN locations in different trial designs; variable success rates are noted. Variations in methods of providing non‐surgical interventions, both in frequency, type, and dosing, are evident [19].

Surgical interventions for primary lower limb AVN aim to establish new blood supply to the bone tissue, bone grafting, debridement, joint containment or pressure‐relieving methods, and/or joint replacement [12]. At the hip joint, studies of different designs demonstrate conflicting results for surgery type and timing, and whether it is superior to non‐surgical interventions for certain patients [27, 28, 29]. Other lower limb locations utilise surgical interventions after non‐surgical methods have failed and demonstrate good results in small case studies; no larger trials have been conducted [30, 31].

Pharmacological interventions aim to reduce the symptoms of pain (oral/topical analgesics) and inflammation (non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)), and slow/halting of osteoclastic resorption to allow time for revascularisation and reossification to the necrotic location (bisphosphonates) [31, 32]. Limited studies regarding pharmacological interventions for paediatric patients are evident, with adult and animal data providing most of the clinical information. A currently unpublished prospective randomised controlled trial [33] investigating bisphosphonate treatment (zoledronate) for Perthes disease found no significant improvement in radiographic follow‐up at 12 and 24 months, but a significant improvement in pain and hip ROM at one‐year follow‐up for those treated with bisphosphonates. Evidence supporting pharmacologic management for acute pain in children is well‐established [34, 35]; however, no studies have been conducted specific to primary lower limb AVN.

There are no similar Cochrane reviews on interventions for primary lower limb AVN. Recently published systematic reviews on Perthes disease are limited by type of intervention investigated and methods of assessing outcomes [20, 22, 36, 37, 38]. No systematic reviews have been conducted evaluating paediatric populations with other primary lower limb AVN sites.

Paediatric primary lower limb AVN are rare, but known to greatly affect the quality of life of patients and their families [16]. The length of time from diagnosis to fully healed may be up to five years [39] for Perthes disease, whilst other lower limb locations can also take several months to years [26, 30]. During this time period, children/adolescents will be prevented from participating in many physical activities, potentially limiting physical and social development and their ability to participate in particular sports and recreation [40]. Pain is a frequent concern for this condition and typically increases with physical activity and throughout the management period. Further, a demonstrated reduction in quality of life and negative effects on school attendance, family dynamics, and social emotional effects of managing the longevity and pain associated with Perthes disease has recently been established [17].

Intervention variations are evident between and within different countries worldwide [29, 41]. The wide‐ranging intervention approaches within the literature and in clinical practice, many not supported by robust studies, indicate that children and adolescents may be receiving ineffective and inefficient interventions. Further, a recent scoping review and subsequent Delphi study identified lower limb conditions resulting in chronic pain in children and adolescents, and ranked primary lower limb AVN as a top 10 condition in which health professionals and caregivers believed were important for clinical guideline development [42, 43]. Prescribing certain interventions may incur excess costs to both the healthcare system and affected families. This underscores the importance of synthesising existing evidence and integrating all primary lower limb AVN sites and their corresponding interventions into comprehensive reviews.

We will follow the Methodological Expectations for Cochrane Intervention Reviews (MECIR) when conducting the reviews [44], and the PRISMA 2020 for reporting [45].

We will include randomised controlled trials (RCTs) (parallel and cluster‐randomised only) and quasi‐RCTs (studies that use methods of allocation that are not strictly random, e.g. hospital numbers or date of birth). There will be no restrictions on date or language. We will exclude cross‐over trials, as this design is inappropriate for primary lower limb AVN, a progressive condition in which the disease course changes over time [46].

We will include published and unpublished studies, provided they are reported in full‐text, thesis, or dissertation format. Unpublished data and ongoing trials will be described, and the results will not be included in the synthesis of results. Trials yet to be completed will be classified as ‘ongoing trials’ and unpublished trials or those published as abstracts only as ‘studies awaiting classification’. We will describe the characteristics of these studies in tables in each review.

We will include trials recruiting children and adolescents aged < 18 years diagnosed with primary AVN in any location in the lower limbs. Alternate terminologies include Legg‐Calve‐Perthes disease, Kohler disease, or Freiberg disease. Studies reporting data on conditions in addition to primary lower limb AVN, or from both paediatric and adult participants, will be eligible if data for children and adolescents (< 18 years) can be individually extracted, or study authors provide the desired results upon request.

We will use a subject‐specific search strategy incorporating titles, abstracts, keywords, and relevant MeSH terms. We will use a validated paediatric filter [49]. See Supplementary material 1 for the MEDLINE search strategy, which will be adapted for other databases as indicated.

We will examine the reference lists of included studies and contact study authors for unpublished or ongoing studies and participant‐level data where appropriate. We will search for errata or retractions from included studies published in full text on PubMed (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and report the date this was done within each review. We will search the grey literature with a combined approach of structured database searches and targeted website searches to ensure we capture regulatory information (e.g. Drugs@FDA), trial registries, abstracts, and conference papers (e.g. Embase or CENTRAL, or both). We will search theses and dissertations via ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global (about.proquest.com/en/products‐services/pqdtglobal/). We will carefully document search processes to demonstrate comprehensiveness and ensure reproducibility [50].

If any eligible trials include review authors as investigators, those authors will be excluded from all aspects of the review process for their respective studies, including study eligibility assessment, data extraction, risk of bias evaluation, and GRADE assessment [51].

For all four reviews, we will import search results into EndNote software [52] to eliminate duplicates. We will then import the results into Covidence software [53] to streamline the study selection process.

In all four reviews, pairs of review authors (from SBa, RT, KG, LD, AG) will independently screen the titles and abstracts identified by the search. We will classify the studies as potentially eligible, unclear, or not eligible. Two review authors (from SBa, RT, KG, LD, AG) will retrieve the full texts of potentially eligible and unclear publications and independently screen them against the inclusion criteria. We will record the reasons for exclusion of the ineligible studies in the 'Characteristics of excluded studies' table. We will resolve any disagreements through discussion or in consultation with a third review author (from VP, NW, CM) if required. If any eligible trials include review authors as investigators, those authors will not be involved in the data extraction for their own study.

We will identify and remove duplicates and collate multiple reports of the same study so that each study, rather than each publication, is the unit of interest in the review. Where necessary to determine study eligibility, we will contact study authors to obtain additional information, such as missing details on study methods or results. We will classify studies for which data remain incomplete or unobtainable as ‘awaiting classification’ and list them in the ‘Characteristics of studies awaiting classification’ table.

We will record the selection process in sufficient detail to complete a PRISMA flow diagram [45] and 'Characteristics of excluded studies' table for each review.

We will use a standard data collection form across all reviews to record study characteristics and outcome data in Covidence [53]. Two review authors (from SBa, LD, RT, KG) will independently extract data, with any discrepancies in data extraction resolved through discussion or in consultation with a third review author (from VP, NW, AG) if required. If any eligible trials include review authors as investigators, those authors will not be involved in the data extraction for their own study.

Where study authors report multiple measures for a given outcome, the scales or assessments provided in each category of outcomes (as above) will be prioritised as per the hierarchical order specified above (e.g. for pain outcome, VAS (child‐reported) will be extracted before any other pain scale such as the Wong‐Baker FACES scale). In all categories where participants can self‐report, we will extract self‐reported measures before any other measure (e.g. child‐reported or parent as proxy reporter for PROMIS). We will extract change‐from‐baseline data for any of the same measures. When extracted data include only total scale scores without corresponding subscale scores, we will contact the authors of the relevant trials to obtain subscale data (e.g. PROMIS mobility and/or pain interference) to clarify which subscale primarily contributes to the overall score and report the outcomes accurately. Where data are reported as both adjusted and unadjusted values for the same outcome, we will extract the unadjusted values. We will extract intention‐to‐treat (ITT)‐analysed data if reported, or the number of participants analysed at that time point where data are unavailable for missing participants.

Data will be primarily stratified by primary lower limb AVN anatomical location (e.g. hip, knee, ankle, foot, other (long bones)).

In all four reviews, two review authors (from SBa, CM, AG, RT) will independently assess risk of bias for each included study, as specified by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [59]. Any disagreements between authors will be resolved by consensus or in consultation with a third review author (from VP, NW, SBr, RJ). If any eligible trials include review authors as investigators, those authors will not be involved in the risk of bias assessment for their own study.

We will assess risk of bias using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool (www.riskofbias.info) [54]. We will use the RoB 2 tool extension for cluster‐randomised trials to judge risk of bias based on the additional considerations associated with cluster‐randomised trials [46].

We will judge risk of bias for each domain as 'low risk of bias', 'some concerns', or 'high risk of bias' using responses to signalling questions and algorithms within the RoB 2 tool.

In each of the four reviews, outcomes for primary lower limb AVN anatomical location will be stratified, outcomes presented separately, and, where possible, analyses will be based on ITT data. Summary data for each intervention group will be presented in forest plots, including the number of participants and events for dichotomous outcomes, and the mean, SD, and number of participants for continuous outcomes. All data analysis will be performed in RevMan [58].

For continuous outcomes (global physical function, mobility), we will calculate mean differences (MD); when outcome measures differ across studies, we will use standardised mean differences (SMD) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to facilitate quantitative pooling [60][61]. For dichotomous outcomes (pain, limp, leg length discrepancy, evidence of arthritic changes, missing days from school, treatment failure, treatment success), we will report results, where applicable, as risk ratios (RR) or risk differences (RD), each accompanied by 95% CIs. We will calculate the absolute per cent change from the difference in the risks between the intervention and control group using GRADEpro GDT and express it as a percentage [62]. SMDs will be back‐translated to a representative scale (e.g. 0 to 10 for pain) by multiplying the SMD by a typical between‐person SD, such as the baseline SD of the control group from the most representative trial [59].

Where trials use different measures for the same outcome or construct, we will use the reported outcome measure that is highest on our predefined hierarchy as the index measure [63]. Means and SDs will be transformed from other outcome measures to the scale of the index measure; data will be pooled using the MD as the summary estimate (e.g. for pain outcome, we will assume self‐reported VAS as the index measure).

We will enter data presented as a scale with a consistent direction of effect across trials. Where trials use different scales to measure the same conceptual outcome (e.g. physical function), we will calculate SMDs, with corresponding 95% CIs. SMDs will be back‐translated to a typical scale (e.g. 0 to 10 for pain) by multiplying the SMD by a typical amongst‐person SD (e.g. the SD of the control group at baseline from the most representative trial) [61].

We will provide the absolute per cent change and the SMD (change from baseline) in the 'Synthesis of results' section of the Results and the 'What happens' column of the summary of findings table in each review.

We will use the following minimal clinical important differences (MCIDs) for interpreting our results as described in Chapter 15 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [64]. In the absence of published thresholds for pain and function for paediatric primary lower limb AVN, we will use consensus thresholds and assume the MCID of 1.5 points on a 10‐point scale for pain and 10 points on a 100‐point scale for function and health‐related quality of life. For measurement instrument PROMIS, a difference of 2 to 6 T‐score points indicates meaningful change; a threshold of 3 T‐score points will be used in the reviews [65]. As guided by clinically meaningful change in other paediatric conditions for mobility measures, we will use a change of > 2.30 seconds for the timed up and go test, and > 30 metres for the 6‐minute walk test [66][67]. For global assessment of treatment success measured on a 7‐point scale, we will use an MCID of 1.5 [68].

If data from an RCT are not reported in a format that can be entered directly into a meta‐analysis, we will convert the data to the required format as described in Chapter 6 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [61].

In all four reviews, trial outcomes will be assessed per participant, not per joints or bones affected. If data are not presented per participant, we will contact the study authors for this information. Where a participant is affected bilaterally, and data are available for review, we will use the more severely affected AVN site in analysis.

Where multiple trial arms are included, only the relevant data will be analysed in the reviews, but all data will be reported in the ‘Characteristics of included studies’ table. In trials including multiple eligible intervention arms that share a common control group, we will retain the intervention groups as separate comparisons and divide the shared control group proportionally between them to avoid double‐counting of participants, in accordance with Cochrane methodological guidance [46]. For dichotomous outcomes, both the sample sizes and the numbers of people with events can be summed across groups. For continuous outcomes, means and SDs can be combined using the methods described in Chapter 7 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [51].

If we identify cluster‐randomised trials, we will extract the available statistical data needed to account for the implications of clustering and apply appropriate statistical adjustments as described in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. This will include calculating the effective sample size by accounting for the intracluster correlation coefficient (ICC), and whether the study adjusted results for the correlations in the data [46]. In instances where the ICC is not reported, we will consider external estimates, and conduct sensitivity analyses to assess the impact of varying ICC values.

If we identify co‐interventions or multiple‐component intervention trials, we will extract available data and group the data by the ‘main’ intervention component as per Chapter 3 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [46] (e.g. surgical intervention with strength training would be grouped into surgical intervention). Additionally, we will document where this occurs in the summary of findings table and address their potential influence on outcomes in the Discussion section of each review.

In all four reviews, we will contact study authors to request missing numerical data (when data are not available for all participants or mixed‐age or mixed‐diagnoses cohorts) or to verify study characteristics. If it is not feasible to obtain the necessary information, we will deem the missing data likely to introduce substantial bias, and will report the results of the affected studies narratively.

For dichotomous data (e.g. proportion of participants with global treatment success), we will calculate the RR using the number of participants randomised to the group as the denominator. For dichotomous outcomes assessing possible harms of treatment (e.g. proportion of participants withdrawing due to adverse events), we will calculate the RR using the number of participants randomised to the group and receiving at least one treatment as the denominator.

For continuous outcomes (e.g. mean change in pain score), we will calculate the MD or SMD based on the number of participants analysed at the corresponding time point. Where participant numbers are not reported at the relevant time points, we will use the number of participants randomised to each group at baseline.

Where feasible, we will calculate missing SDs from other reported statistics, such as standard errors, CIs, or P values, in accordance with the methods outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [60]. If SDs cannot be derived, we will impute them using data from other studies included in the meta‐analysis. We will examine and discuss the potential impact of missing data on the findings in the Discussion section of each review.

In all four reviews, we will assess publication bias using a funnel plot and Egger’s test, provided that the meta‐analysis includes a minimum of 10 studies [69]. If we detect asymmetry in the funnel plot, we will review the trial characteristics to assess whether the asymmetry is due to publication bias, methodological factors, or clinical diversity of the trials. Where pooling of data can occur, we will perform formal statistical tests to investigate funnel plot asymmetry, following the guidance in Chapter 13 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [70].

To assess outcome reporting bias, we will compare trial protocols with corresponding published reports. For studies published after 1 July 2005, we will search the WHO ICTRP (trialsearch.who.int) to identify the priori trial protocol. We will evaluate the presence of selective outcome reporting by examining discrepancies between prespecified and reported outcomes.

In each of the four reviews, where sufficient data are available, we plan to pool outcomes to estimate benefit and harm of interventions stratified by anatomical location: hip, knee, ankle, foot, other (long bones).

We plan to synthesise effect estimates using a random‐effects model, reflecting anticipated clinical diversity of interventions and the likelihood that different trials are estimating different intervention effects. We will use the inverse‐variance method for meta‐analysis of continuous outcomes and the Mantel‐Haenszel method for meta‐analysis of dichotomous outcomes. We will use the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimator to estimate between‐trial variance. We will use the Hartung‐Knapp‐Sidik‐Jonkman method to calculate a CI for the meta‐analysis effect estimate when there are at least three studies and the estimate of heterogeneity is greater than zero. In other scenarios (i.e. in pooled analyses of two studies, or where the estimate of heterogeneity is equal to zero), we will use the Wald‐type method [60].

Where pooling of data is not possible (e.g. if only a single trial contributes to a given outcome), we will still present effect estimates and 95% CIs of each trial in forest plots and summarise the results in the text. If multiple trials for a comparison are not suitable for inclusion in a meta‐analysis, we will adopt the approach outlined in the Synthesis Without Meta‐analysis (SWiM) reporting guideline [71].

We will use RevMan to present the data in forest plots [58].

We will assess statistical heterogeneity through visual inspection of forest plots to identify apparent discrepancies in study results, as well as by calculating the I² statistic and conducting the Chi² test.

We will interpret the I² value as follows, according to the recommendations in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [60]:

0% to 40%: might not be important;30% to 60%: may represent moderate heterogeneity;50% to 90%: may represent substantial heterogeneity;75% to 100%: represents considerable heterogeneity.

Interpretation of the I² statistic will consider both the magnitude and direction of effect estimates, as well as the strength of the evidence for heterogeneity [60].

We will interpret the Chi² test for heterogeneity using a significance threshold of P ≤ 0.10, which will be considered indicative of statistical heterogeneity. If we identify substantial heterogeneity, we will report it and explore potential sources in accordance with the guidance provided in Section 10.10 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [60].

We will utilise GRADE methodology to assess the certainty of evidence and strength of recommendations, as recommended in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [73].
