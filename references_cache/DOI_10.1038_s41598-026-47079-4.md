Abstract: PLS1, encoding an actin -bundling protein Plastin 1, is one of the 
pathogenic genes responsible for autosomal dominant non -syndromic hearing loss. 
The cellular damage caused by mutant plastin proteins may arise from loss of function 
and/or dominant -negative effects. In this study , we investigated the pathogenic 
mechanism of PLS1 mutations by establishing both in vitro cellular models and in 
vivo zebrafish models. Immunoprecipitation demonstrated that the ΔExon8 mutation 
disrupts the intramolecular interaction between the ABD1 and ABD2 domains, while 
immunofluorescence colocalization revealed that the mutation alters the binding 
pattern of PLS1 to actin bundles and disturbs cytoskeletal organization. Through 
zebrafish FM 1-43 uptake assays, we showed that the mutant protein impaired the 
functional opening and permeability of the stereociliary mechanoelectrical 
transduction (MET) channel. Although both PLS1 -knockout zebrafish and zebrafish 
expressing ΔExon8-PLS1 exhibited behavioral signs of hearing impairment, defects in 
MET channel u ptake were observed only in the mutant -expressing fish. Notably, 
neither PLS1 deficiency nor re -expression of human wild -type PLS1 in knockout 
zebrafish reproduces this MET defect. Thus, the mutation not only reduces native 
PLS1 function but also interferes with normal MET channel activity. In summary, our 
findings indicate that the DFNA76 phenotype caused by the PLS1ΔExon8 mutation is 
likely due to a dominant-negative effect with partial loss-of-function.  
Keywords: DFNA76; PLS1; hearing loss; ACTG1; MYO1C 
Hearing loss is one of the most common hereditary sensory disorders, affecting 
approximately one in every 500 newborns 1. More than 100 causative genes have been 
identified, with non -syndromic hearing loss accounting for the majority of cases 2. 
Among the non -syndromic forms, 128 autosomal recessive genes have been 
characterized, which comprise approximately 20% of all cases; the remaining cases 
follow X -linked or mitochondrial inheritance 3. Autosomal recessive hearing loss 
typically presents early with rapid progression, whereas autosomal dominant forms 
usually progress more slowly and are often influenced by environmental or peripheral 
factors 4.  
Although many pathogenic variants associated with autosomal dominant 
non-syndromic hearing loss (ADNSHL) have been reported, most studies  have 
focused on clinical phenotypes. Many animal studies rely primarily on gene knockout 
models, with limited in-depth functional evaluation of the mutant proteins themselves 
5 With the rapid development of adeno-associated virus (AA V)-mediated gene therapy, 
new therapeutic avenues have emerged for patients carrying loss -of-function variants 
6,7. Therefore, it is essential to define the  pathogenic mechanism of each mutation , 
particularly distinguishing between loss -of-function and dominant -negative 
mutations. 
PLS1 encodes Plastin-1, an actin-binding protein belonging to the plastin family 
that includes PLS1, PLS2, and PLS3 8. PLS1 is predominantly expressed in intestinal 
epithelial microvilli and stereocilia of cochlear and vestibular h air cells 9-12. Plastin 
proteins contain two N -terminal EF -hand calcium -binding domains and two 
C-terminal actin -binding domains (ABD1 and ABD2), each consisting of a pair of 
calponin homology (CH) domains 13,14. ABD2 has a higher affinity for actin than

ABD1, but ABD1 imposes an autoinhibitory constraint on ABD2. When ABD1 first 
engages actin bundles, autoinhibition is relieved, allowing ABD2 to initiate effective 
cross-linking15. 
PLS1 is a well-known ADNSHL protein. The first reported PLS1 pathogenic 
variants were three missense mutations—c.805G>A (p.E269K), c.713G>T (p.L238R), 
and c.383T>C (p.F128S) —identified in three unrelated European families with 
autosomal dominant deafness16. Schrauwen et al. later iden tified a p.L363F variant in 
a Hungarian Roma family 17, and the p.E269K mutation was also detected in a 
Turkish family 18. In animal models, Pls1-knockout mice displayed shorter and 
thinner stereocilia and developed moderate progressive hearing loss, suggesting that 
PLS1 contributes to mild or progressive hearing impairment in humans 14,19,20. 
We recently identified a Chinese family with ADNSHL carrying a splice-altering 
PLS1 variant that leads to exon 8 skipping 21. Affected individuals exhibit progressive, 
unilateral or bilateral, moderate -to-severe, high-frequency hearing loss that begins in 
childhood and worsen s with age. However, the mechanism by which this PLS1 
mutation causes severe hearing impairment in humans remains unclear. In this study, 
we further investigated the pathogenic effects of the previously reported PLS1 
c.981G>A (ΔExon 8) variant. Using cellular and zebrafish models, we found that 
exon-8 deletion disrupts the ABD1 domain and impairs the ABD1–ABD2 interaction. 
Given prior studies showing that ABD1 exerts autoinhibitory control over ABD2, our 
results are consistent with th e possibility that ΔExon8 weakens this intramolecular 
restraint. This may perturb PLS1 –actin organization and contribute to abnormal 
localization of actin - and myosin -associated structures, ultimately impairing MET 
channel function. 
Together, these findings provide important insights into the pathogenic 
mechanism of PLS1ΔExon8-associated hearing loss and offer potential clues for 
therapeutic intervention. 
Results 
The PLS1 Mutation Alters Its Intrinsic Structural Interactions 
Based on the findings of Schwebach et al. 15, PLS1 contains two actin -binding 
domains, ABD1 and ABD2. ABD2 exhibit ed a substantially higher affinity for actin 
than ABD1 in vitro, and the two domains interact intr amolecularly such that ABD1 
suppresses ABD2-mediated actin binding. Exon 8 encodes the α18 and α19 helices 
within the ABD1 domain (Fig. 1A), leading us to hypothesize that exon -8 deletion 
disrupts ABD1 structural integrity. 
To test this, we co-transfected HEK293T cells with Flag-WT-ABD1, HA-ABD2, 
or Flag-ΔExon8-ABD1 and HA-ABD2 (Fig.1E-F). Lysates were immunoprecipitated 
using anti -Flag magnetic beads, followed by western blotting with anti -Flag and 
anti-HA antibodies to assess  the ABD1–ABD2 interaction. Only WT -ABD1 
successfully pulled down HA -ABD2, whereas ΔExon8 -ABD1 failed to capture 
HA-ABD2 (Fig. 1C).

The ΔExon8-PLS1 mutation alters auditory responses in zebrafish 
We evaluated  the auditory-sensory behavioral responses of zebrafish larvae. 
Figure 6A shows the full 15 -minute swimming trajectory of one representative larva 
from each group, recorded over the entire experimental period, whereas the 
quantitative analyses in Fig. 6B –C include all 15 larvae per group, each treated as

The ΔExon8 -PLS1 mutation induces MET defects in zebrafish hair 
cells 
Previous studies have shown that MYO1C regulates MET function in stereocilia 
in cochlear hair cells, and this phenotype can be assessed using FM1 -43 dye uptake. 
In light of  our Co -IP results that implicates MYO1C’s association with PLS1 , we 
examined whether the ΔExon8 -PLS1 mutation affects  MET channel activity in hair 
bundles using FM1 -43 labeling. FM1-43 is a vital dye that enters hair cells through 
non-selective cation -permeable MET channels and emits fluorescence upon 
internalization, thereby serving as an indicator of MET channel activity22. 
Quantification of FM1-43 fluorescence intensity based on the mean fluorescence 
intensity of the neuromast region showed that ΔExon8-PLS1 larvae exhibited 
significantly reduced fluorescence compared with wild-type larvae (Fig. 6D-E), 
indicating impaired MET channel function. In contrast, FM1-43 uptake was increased 
in Pls1-KO larvae. This pattern suggests that the MET channels in Pls1-KO fish tend 
to remain abnormally open, whereas the mutant protein impairs the normal opening of 
the MET channels, resulting in decreased permeability.

Discussion 
This study elucidates the pathogenic mechanism by which exon 8 deletion in the 
PLS1 gene leads to non -syndromic hearing loss. PLS1 localizes predominantly to the 
stereociliary shafts of auditory and vestibular hair cells, where it plays a critical role 
ARTICLE IN PRESS
ARTICLE IN PRESS

 
in bundling and stabiliz ing actin filaments 23. Wild-type PLS1 maintains a dynamic 
regulatory balance through an intramolecular interaction, in which ABD1 suppresses 
the high actin -binding affinity of ABD2 24. When ABD1 first engages actin and 
undergoes conformational changes, this inhibition is relieved, enab ling ABD2 to bind 
with high affinity and promote actin cross -linking13,15. This tightly regulated process 
ensures proper dynamic remodeling of actin filaments 25. However, our findings 
demonstrate that the deletion of exon 8 generates a mutant PLS1 protein that disrupts 
this regulatory mechanism. 
IP-MS analysis revealed that ΔExon8 -PLS1 capture d markedly more ACTG1 
and MYO1C than WT-PLS1. We hypothesize that loss of exon 8 disrupts the 
structural integrit y of ABD1 and weakens its interaction with ABD2. In light of 
previous studies showing ABD1-mediated autoinhibition of ABD2, this finding is 
consistent with a model in which ΔExon8 reduces intramolecular restraint and thereby 
perturbs PLS1 –actin organization. However, direct measurements of ABD2 
actin-binding affinity or full-length PLS1 conformational changes were not performed 
in the present study.  PLS1 is primarily known as an actin -binding protein, and no 
direct biochemical evidence supports its direct interaction with myosin. Therefore, we 
examined the relationship between PLS1, ACTG1, a nd MYO1C. We found that 
MYO1C and ACTG1 exhibit a strong physical interaction, and this interaction 
remains intact in Pls1-KO cells. However, immunofluorescence colocalization 
revealed that their spatial correlation was markedly reduced compared with the 
WT-PLS1 rescue group. Consistent with our 3D quantitative analysis of γ -actin 
bundles architecture, ΔExon8-PLS1 expression cau sed substantial reductions in actin 
bundle volume, bundle length, and bundle branch points , indicating that the actin 
tracks used by MYO1C26 become fragmented. Under these disrupted cytoskeletal 
conditions, MYO1C binds to abnormally reorganized actin bundles . Together, these 
results suggest  that ΔExon8-PLS1 may alter the organization of actin -associated 
structures in a manner that affects the apparent spatial relationship between ACTG1 
and MYO1C under the rescue conditions tested, while the overall ACTG1 –MYO1C 
interaction remained detectable by Co-IP. 
These findings suggest that mutant PLS1 shows enhanced association with 
ACTG1 and that its association with MYO1C is likely ACTG1 -dependent, as 
depletion of ACTG1 reduced MYO1C co -precipitation with Δ Exon8-PLS1. 
Therefore, our data support the interpretation that ΔExon8-PLS1 likely exerts a 
dominant-negative effect, while also showing features consistent with partial loss of 
function.  
Quantitative confocal analyses revealed significant abnormalities in cell 
morphology in bo th Pls1-KO and ΔExon8-PLS1–rescued cells. However, 
ΔExon8-PLS1–expressing cells exhibited smaller and shorter F -actin bundles with 
fewer branch points, which may contribute to the weakened MYO1C–ACTG1 
interaction. Although MYO1C normally interacts with ACTG1, the excessively strong 
binding of ΔExon8 -PLS1 to ACTG1 likely creates a competitive effect on F -actin, 
thereby disrupting the mechanical coupling interface normally mediated by MYO1C 
and ACTG1. This raises the possibility that ΔExon8-PLS1 alters the actin scaffold in

ARTICLE IN PRESS

 
a way that may affect MYO1C positioning or function.  Notably, unlike the 
pronounced structural defects induced by ΔExon8 -PLS1, Pls1-KO cells showed only 
a reduction in ACTG1 -positive volume of actin bundles  but no major defects in  the 
length of actin bundles  or bundle branch points . This may reflect compensation by 
other actin -bundling proteins (Espin, Fascin, Villin) 20, which exhibit overlapping 
functions and can partially substitute one another in the event of loss 14,27,28. 
MYO1C is a single -headed class I myosin that acts as a tension regulator at 
stereocilia tips, maintaining optimal sensitivity of MET channels 29,30. As an 
ATP-dependent molecular motor, M YO1C specifically binds to actin and generates 
force via ATP hydrolysis, contributing to cellular contractility, vesicle trafficking, and 
motility31-33. Its functions extend across diverse cellular contexts  by linking 
membrane structures to the actin cytoskeleton 34-36. MYO1C’s involvement in MET 
has been demonstrated in mouse cochlear hair cells using FM1-43 uptake assays37.  
Notably, our FM1-43 uptake assays revealed distinct effects of PLS1 deficiency 
and ΔExon8-PLS1 expression on hair -cell dye uptake. FM1 -43 uptake was slightly 
increased in Pls1 -knockout larvae but reduced in the ΔExon8-PLS1 group. These 
contrasting patterns suggest that PLS1 deficiency and ΔExon8-PLS1 expression may 
perturb MET channel function through different mechanisms. However, whether these 
differences reflect altered bundle mechanics, tip-link tension, or channel gating cannot 
be determined from the present data and will require direct physiological and 
ultrastructural analyses. Because Espin and Fascin are also expressed in zebrafish 
stereocilia and can partially compensate for the absence of PLS1 38-40, FM1-43 uptake 
increases only modestly. In contrast, ΔExon8 -PLS1 makes the actin bundles stiffer 
and abnormally arranged, hindering proper MET cha nnel opening and resulting in a 
pronounced reduction in FM1-43 uptake. 
Biochemical analyses further showed that ΔExon8 -PLS1 ha d a shorter protein 
half-life than WT-PLS1. Previous studies have reported a ~24-hour half-life for PLS3 
41. CHX-chase experiments revealed rapid degradation of ΔExon8 -PLS1 within 12h, 
whereas WT-PLS1 remained stable, indicating that the mutation substantially reduce d 
protein stability. According to UniProt, the ubiquitination site (K333) is located only 
six amino acids downstream of the ΔExon8 deletion region 42. Together with our 
ubiquitination as says showing a marked increase in ΔExon8 -PLS1 ubiquitination, 
these findings indicate that the mutant protein is more susceptible to 
ubiquitin-mediated degradation and therefore accumulates at lower levels in cells. 
Unlike the dominant-negative effects described above, this aspect of the phenotype is 
more consistent with a dosage-insufficient, partial loss-of-function mechanism. 
In zebrafish embryos, the human ΔExon8-PLS1 variant results in clear auditory 
deficits, including abnormal otic morphology and reduced acoustic –evoked 
swimming responses. Pls1-KO larvae exhibited enlarged otic vesicles and altered 
otolith diameters, both of which were rescued by WT-PLS1 expression. In contrast, 
ΔExon8-PLS1 failed to rescue these abnormalities and produced phenotypes 
comparable to those of the knockout, suggesting that the mutation disrupt ed the 
essential functions of PLS1 in otolith formation and cytoskeletal homeostasis. 
Behavioral assays further demonstrated that both Pls1-KO and ΔExon8-PLS1 larvae

ARTICLE IN PRESS

 
exhibited reduced sound -evoked locomotor responses, whereas WT-PLS1 partially 
restored auditory sensitivity. We did not directly assess the expression of other plastin 
isoforms (e.g., pls3) in the Pls1-KO lines. Although genetic compensation has been 
reported in some stable CRISPR/Cas9 zebrafish models 43,44, the robust phenotypes 
observed in hair-cell morphology and FM1 -43 uptake suggest that any compensatory 
expression of other pla stin isoforms did not obscure the Pls1-KO phenotypes. Future 
studies could investigate potential transcriptional adaptation in more detail. These 
findings indicate that the PLS1 mutation not only disrupts structural integrity  but also 
causes functional impairment of hearing, with effects comparable to those of the null 
mutant. Although our results suggest that ΔExon8 -PLS1 exerts a dominant -negative 
effect, this conclusion is inferred from the functional divergence between Pls1-KO 
and ΔExon8-PLS1 rescue models. We did not directly assess the co -expression of 
wild-type and mutant PLS1, and future studies are needed to confirm  the 
dominant-negative mechanism under physiologically relevant conditions. 
Taken together, our results provide new insights into how PLS1 mutations 
disrupt cytoskeletal dynamics and actin –myosin complex assembly. Further 
investigation of hair  cell electrophysiology and stereociliary ultrastructure in 
PLS1-mutant zebrafish will de epen our understanding of the mechanism of 
PLS1-associated hearing loss.

Our animal experiments comply with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.

Zebrafish Acoustic Stimulation Behavioral Assay 
To further evaluate the effect of the Pls1 gene on zebrafish auditory function, 5

the distance move d during the 30 -second stimulus period versus the 5 -minute rest 
intervals. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, version 10.0. 
For visualization, the full 15 -minute swimming trajectory of a single representative 
larva from each grou p was recorded and presented in Fig. 6A. Quantitative analyses 
(Figs. 6B –C) included all 15 larvae per group, each treated as one biological 
replicate.
