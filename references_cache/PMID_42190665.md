# CSNK2B gene replacement rescues autism-related phenotypes and establishes translational EEG biomarkers.
**Authors:** Ding C, Wang X, Yuan Y, Zhang Y, Hu Y, Wang C, Du A, Qiu Z
**Journal:** Cell Rep Med (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.xcrm.2026.102832](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2026.102832)

## Content

Variants in CSNK2B cause neurodevelopmental disorders with autism and epilepsy, but therapeutic evidence and translatable biomarkers remain limited. We generate Csnk2b haploinsufficient mice that recapitulate key disease features, including social and cognitive deficits, anxiety-like behavior, spontaneous seizures, cortical abnormalities, and reduced inhibitory interneurons. Early postnatal, brain-wide gene replacement improves survival and rescues structural, behavioral, and seizure phenotypes. Treatment also normalizes brain activity signatures linked to circuit function, including theta and gamma power, power ratios, interregional coherence, and gamma-band directional connectivity. These findings show that reduced Csnk2b dosage disrupts cortical development and network synchronization but can be corrected after birth. They also identify a compact set of noninvasive brain activity biomarkers with potential value for target engagement, dose selection, and longitudinal monitoring in future gene therapy studies.

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), including autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and developmental epileptic encephalopathies, arise from disruption of genes that regulate neuronal proliferation, differentiation, and synaptic maturation. 1 , 2 Increasingly, exome sequencing studies have revealed that a subset of NDDs results from dosage-sensitive genes whose partial loss of function perturbs critical developmental pathways. 3 , 4 Understanding how reduced gene dosage alters neural circuit formation is essential for identifying mechanistic convergence and developing targeted therapies.

CSNK2B, which encodes the β regulatory subunit of casein kinase II (CK2), has emerged as one such dosage-sensitive gene. 5 The β subunit stabilizes the holoenzyme complex and modulates substrate specificity. 6 , 7 Complete Csnk2b deletion in mice results in embryonic lethality, 8 underscoring its essential developmental role, while conditional disruption impairs cortical expansion and glial lineage progression. 9 CK2 is a constitutively active serine/threonine kinase that forms a heterotetramer consisting of two catalytic (α/α′) and two regulatory (β) subunits. 10 During brain development, CK2 regulates neural progenitor proliferation, neuronal differentiation, cytoskeletal organization, and synaptic signaling. 11 , 12 , 13 At the circuit level, CK2 influences NMDA receptor-dependent signaling and synaptic plasticity, processes fundamental to network maturation and cognitive function. 14

Human genetic data support a critical requirement for precise CSNK2B dosage. In one well-characterized cohort, 25 individuals with heterozygous CSNK2B variants were identified through exome sequencing. 15 The majority of variants arose de novo and included 14 predicted loss-of-function (LoF) alleles (nonsense, frameshift, canonical splice-site, or start-loss), in addition to missense variants. 15 Affected individuals presented with early-onset epilepsy, developmental delay, intellectual disability, and ASD-related features, with variable severity. 15 Population sequencing datasets further demonstrate marked intolerance of CSNK2B to LoF variation (gnomAD pLI ≈1.00; loss-of-function observed/expected upper bound fraction (LOEUF) ≈0.24; observed/expected LoF ≈0.08), strongly supporting haploinsufficiency as the principal disease mechanism. In addition, clustering of certain missense variants within domains required for β-subunit dimerization and interaction with catalytic α/α′ subunits suggests that altered holoenzyme assembly may contribute to pathogenicity in selected cases. 15

Beyond rare monogenic presentations, integrative genomic analyses combining genome-wide association studies (GWASs) with brain expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) data have implicated CSNK2B as a candidate risk gene for schizophrenia, and its expression is reduced in postmortem brains of affected individuals. 16 Functional studies demonstrate that Csnk2b knockdown in neural stem cells disrupts progenitor homeostasis, impairs neuronal differentiation, and alters neuronal morphology and synaptic transmission. 16 Together, these findings indicate that disruption of CSNK2B-dependent pathways may represent a shared mechanism across multiple NDDs.

Despite strong genetic and cellular evidence implicating CSNK2B in neurodevelopment, several fundamental questions remain unresolved. It is unclear whether reduced CSNK2B dosage alone is sufficient to drive ASD- and epilepsy-relevant phenotypes in vivo, how gene dosage affects cortical circuit synchronization, and whether restoring gene expression after birth can reverse established deficits. Furthermore, translational biomarkers capable of linking preclinical models to future therapeutic trials are lacking.

Here, we generated Csnk2b haploinsufficient (Csnk2b+/−) mice to test reduced gene dosage. These mice recapitulate core clinical features of patients, including ASD-related behavioral abnormalities, spontaneous epileptiform activity, growth impairment, cortical network disruption, and reduced survival (only ∼60% survive to P150). Early postnatal adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated CSNK2B gene replacement restored neuronal and circuit deficits, normalized electrophysiological signatures, improved survival, and ameliorated behavioral phenotypes. Together, these findings establish dosage-sensitive CSNK2B deficiency as a driver of neurodevelopmental pathology and provide preclinical proof-of-concept for gene replacement therapy, with translatable biomarkers.

Four independent studies have reported de novo CSNK2B variants in patients with intellectual disability, epilepsy, and ASD. 15 , 17 , 18 , 19 All variants described in these studies were included (Figure 1A). To explore the functional effects of CSNK2B mutations, we first used the CRISPR-Cas9 system to knock out the Csnk2b gene in mice. After designing single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) flanking exon 3 of Csnk2b, we successfully deleted 841 base pairs in this region (Figures S1A and S1B). Subsequently, we detected the mRNA expression of Csnk2b in the brains of Csnk2b+/− mice and found that it was significantly reduced by approximately 50% compared to that in wild-type (WT) mice (p = 0.001; Figure S1C). Consistently, its protein level was also significantly decreased by about 50% relative to WT controls (p < 0.001; Figures S1D and S1E). Staining of mouse brain sections revealed that Csnk2b is expressed in multiple brain regions, including the cortex and hippocampus (Figure 1B). Within the cortical region, Csnk2b is highly expressed in the dendrites and axons of neurons (Figure 1B). Quantification of neuronal staining in the primary somatosensory cortex (S1Tr) demonstrated a significant reduction in cortical thickness in Csnk2b+/− mice compared with WT littermates (p = 0.001; Figures 1C and 1D).

To further investigate the role of Csnk2b in development, we measured the body weights of Csnk2b+/− mice and WT mice at postnatal day 21 and day 60. The results showed that Csnk2b+/− mice had significantly lower body weights at both time points compared to WT mice (D21, p = 0.003; D60, p = 0.002; Figure S1F). Additionally, the brain weights of Csnk2b+/− mice were significantly lower than those of WT mice at these two stages (p = 0.009; p = 0.004; Figure S1G). Consistent with these growth deficits, we found that haploinsufficiency of Csnk2b significantly reduced the survival rate of mice, with Csnk2b+/− mice having a survival rate of only 60% at 150 days postnatal (p = 0.007; Figure 1E). Together, these findings indicate that Csnk2b haploinsufficiency impairs the growth and viability of mice.

To evaluate the social ability of Csnk2b+/− mice, we first conducted the three-chamber test. During the sociability test, we found that Csnk2b+/− mice exhibited similar social abilities to WT mice, spending more time interacting with a stranger mouse rather than exploring an empty cage (WT: p < 0.001; Csnk2b+/−: p = 0.001; Figures 1F and 1G). However, when confronted with both a stranger and a familiar mouse, Csnk2b+/− mice did not display the same social preference as WT mice, instead allocating approximately equal time to exploring both mice (WT: p = 0.002; Csnk2b+/−: p = 0.624; Figures 1H and 1I).

Subsequently, we investigated the ability of Csnk2b+/− mice to explore and recognize novel and familiar objects. During the habituation phase, identical empty cages were placed in both side chambers, and no significant difference in exploration time between the left and right chambers was observed (WT: p = 0.601; Csnk2b+/−: p = 0.398; Figures S1H and S1I), indicating no inherent side preference. When the right chamber was empty and the left chamber contained a novel object, Csnk2b+/− mice, similar to WT mice, spent more time exploring the novel object (WT: p = 0.005; Csnk2b+/−: p = 0.001; Figures S1J and S1K). Additionally, when the right chamber contained a novel object and the left chamber contained a familiar object, Csnk2b+/− mice, like WT mice, exhibited a preference for exploring the novel object on the right side (WT: p = 0.043; Csnk2b+/−: p = 0.009; Figures S1L and S1M).

We further examined the repetitive and stereotyped behaviors in Csnk2b+/− mice through the marble burying test and self-grooming recording. The results of the marble burying test showed no significant difference in the number of marbles buried between WT and Csnk2b+/− mice within the same time period (p = 0.309; Figures 1J and 1K). The self-grooming test also revealed no significant difference in self-grooming time between the two groups of mice (p = 0.759; Figure 1L). Additionally, the open field test showed that Csnk2b+/− mice spent significantly less time in the central area compared to WT mice (p = 0.015; Figures 1M and 1N), while their total locomotor distance was significantly increased (p = 0.036; Figure 1O). This behavior suggests hyperactivity combined with increased anxiety-like behavior.

We assessed the spatial learning and memory abilities of Csnk2b+/− mice using the Barnes maze test. During the first 4 days of training, there was no significant difference in the latency to locate the escape hole between Csnk2b+/− and WT mice (Figure 1P), indicating comparable acquisition of spatial learning. Similarly, no significant difference was observed on the fifth day of testing (p = 0.115; Figure 1P). However, on the twelfth day, Csnk2b+/− mice required significantly more time to locate the escape hole compared to WT mice (p = 0.003; Figure 1P), suggesting impaired long-term spatial memory retention rather than a deficit in initial learning.

To further confirm the robustness of these behavioral phenotypes, we performed the same behavioral tests in an independent cohort of WT and Csnk2b+/− mice. Consistent with the primary cohort, Csnk2b+/− mice exhibited impaired social novelty preference in the three-chamber test, showing no significant preference for the novel stranger compared with WT mice (WT, p < 0.001; Csnk2b+/−, p = 0.307; Figures S2A and S2B). No significant differences were observed between WT and Csnk2b+/− mice in the marble burying (p = 0.630; Figures S2C and S2D) or self-grooming tests (p = 0.935; Figure S2E). In the open field test, Csnk2b+/− mice spent less time in the center area (p < 0.001; Figures S2F and S2G) and displayed increased locomotor activity compared with WT mice (p = 0.007; Figure S2H). In the Barnes maze, no difference was detected during the acquisition phase, whereas Csnk2b+/− mice showed significantly increased escape latency on day 12 (p < 0.001; Figure S2I), indicating impaired long-term spatial memory retention. These findings from an independent cohort further support the reproducibility of the behavioral abnormalities observed in Csnk2b+/− mice.

To investigate whether Csnk2b+/− mice exhibit epileptic symptoms analogous to those observed in clinical patients, we continuously monitored their behavioral manifestations over a 24-h period while simultaneously performing electrocorticography (ECoG) recordings. A subset of adult Csnk2b+/− mice (7 out of 10) displayed overt behavioral features of epileptic seizures, including wild running, myoclonic seizures, and generalized tonic-clonic seizures (Video S1), accompanied by prominent epileptic discharges (p = 0.003; Figures 1Q and 1R). In contrast, no spontaneous epileptic seizures were detected in WT mice (Figures 1Q and 1R). The frequency of spontaneous seizures in Csnk2b+/− mice was 2.30 ± 2.13 times/day, with a total daily seizure duration of 2.21 ± 2.17 min (Figures 1S and 1T).

To further explore whether Csnk2b affects the development of specific inhibitory neurons, we analyzed its spatiotemporal expression patterns. We first utilized the Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE) data available on the NCBI website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and found that Csnk2b is expressed in both embryonic and adult mouse brains, with peak expression observed at embryonic day 14 (Figure S3A). Then, by consulting single-cell sequencing data from the Allen Brain Map database (https://portal.brain-map.org/), we discovered that Csnk2b is expressed in various inhibitory neurons, such as parvalbumin (PV)- and somatostatin (SST)-positive neurons (Figure S3B). Staining for these two types of inhibitory neurons revealed a significant reduction in PV + interneurons in the cortex of Csnk2b+/− mice (retrosplenial granular cortex [RSG], p = 0.039; retrosplenial agranular cortex [RSA], p < 0.001; medial parietal association cortex [MPtA], p = 0.033; lateral parietal association cortex [LPtA], p = 0.004; Figures 2I and 2J), whereas PV + interneuron density in the hippocampus was unchanged (p = 0.92; Figure 2K). Additionally, SST+ interneuron density in both the cortex and hippocampus remained comparable between Csnk2b+/− and WT mice (RSG, p = 0.736; RSA, p = 0.561; MPtA, p = 0.288; LPtA, p = 0.539; hippocampus, p = 0.598; Figures S3C–S3E).

Since PV-positive neurons are pivotal for generating gamma oscillations and maintaining excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) balance, we reasoned that their loss would be reflected in aberrant network activity. Cortical ECoG recordings pointed to network-level hyperexcitability in Csnk2b+/− mice. The E/I balance was fundamentally altered, as evidenced by a significant elevation in gamma-band (30–80 Hz) relative power (p = 0.025; Figures 2L and 2M). This disinhibited state was further confirmed by a significant reduction in both the theta/gamma ratio and the beta/gamma ratio (theta/gamma, p = 0.008; beta/gamma, p < 0.001; Figures 2N and 2O).

We next asked whether this local disinhibition impaired large-scale network coordination. We found that long-range gamma synchrony, measured by magnitude-squared coherence between bilateral prefrontal sites, was severely compromised in Csnk2b+/− mice (p = 0.035; Figures 2P and 2Q). To probe the functional impact of this desynchronization, we assessed the efficiency of directional information transfer using Granger causality. In both groups, the net information flow between channels was balanced (i.e., the difference between channel 1→2 and channel 2→1 was near zero). However, the analysis revealed a significant attenuation in the overall strength of this bidirectional, gamma-band information flow in mutants (p = 0.036; Figure 2R).

We selected AAV-PHP.eB, a serotype known for its efficient transduction across the blood-brain barrier. Additionally, we compared the therapeutic effects of the neuron-specific hSyn promoter and the non-cell-specific CAG promoter. The human CSNK2B-expressing AAV was delivered to the mouse brain via retro-orbital injection (Figure 3A). One month after AAV injection, quantification in the S1Tr region revealed significantly higher transduction efficiency for AAV-CAG-CSNK2B compared with AAV-hSyn-CSNK2B (p = 0.001; Figures 3B and 3C). Subsequent weight measurements showed that both AAV-hSyn-CSNK2B and AAV-CAG-CSNK2B injections significantly increased the body weight of Csnk2b+/− mice (hSyn, p = 0.003; CAG, p < 0.001; Figure 3D). Notably, AAV-CAG-CSNK2B resulted in a significantly greater increase in body weight compared to AAV-hSyn-CSNK2B (p = 0.048; Figure 3D). Quantification of protein levels showed that both AAVs significantly increased CSNK2B protein abundance in the cortex of Csnk2b+/− mice (both p < 0.001; Figures 3E and 3F), with AAV-CAG-CSNK2B producing a significantly greater increase than AAV-hSyn-CSNK2B (p = 0.028; Figure 3F). In the hippocampus, both AAV treatments also significantly elevated CSNK2B protein levels in Csnk2b+/− mice (hSyn, p = 0.045; CAG, p = 0.006; Figure 3G). However, no significant difference was observed between AAV-CAG-CSNK2B and AAV-hSyn-CSNK2B (p = 0.18; Figure 3G).

Fluorescence staining indicated that both AAV treatments significantly increased cortical thickness in Csnk2b+/− mice (hSyn, p = 0.003; CAG, p < 0.001; Figures 3H and 3I), although no significant difference was observed between the two treatments (p = 0.11; Figure 3I). Finally, both AAV-hSyn-CSNK2B and AAV-CAG-CSNK2B treatments significantly enhanced the survival rate of Csnk2b+/− mice (hSyn, p = 0.034; CAG, p = 0.049; Figure 3J), increasing it from 50% at 150 days postnatal to 80%, although no significant difference was detected between the two treatment groups (p = 0.72; Figure 3J).

Additionally, treatment with either AAV also significantly increased the neuronal density in cortical layer 6 of Csnk2b+/− mice (hSyn, p = 0.019; CAG, p = 0.002; Figures 4E and 4F), with no significant difference between AAV-hSyn-CSNK2B and AAV-CAG-CSNK2B (p = 0.086; Figure 4F). Finally, we assessed the development of PV-positive interneurons in the cortex of Csnk2b+/− mice. Both AAV-hSyn-CSNK2B and AAV-CAG-CSNK2B treatments significantly increased the density of PV-positive neurons compared with the control group (both p < 0.05; Figures 4G and 4H). Furthermore, no significant differences were observed between the two AAV treatments except in the RSG brain region (both p > 0.05; Figure 4H). Because PV-interneuron loss in the Csnk2b+/− cortex predicted oscillatory disruption, we asked whether neonatal AAV-PHP.eB–CSNK2B would correct ECoG-detectable network signatures that could serve as translational biomarkers. A two-way ANOVA on band-limited relative power revealed a significant groups × frequency band interaction (F (8, 118) = 2.922, p = 0.005), indicating frequency-specific rescue. Post hoc tests showed selective normalization of two prespecified endpoints: the pathological gamma elevation was reduced, and the suppressed theta power was restored (gamma, p = 0.047; theta, p = 0.024; Figures 5A and 5B). While gamma peak frequency and bandwidth exhibited only trends toward reversal (p > 0.1; Figures 5C and 5D), composite ratios that index cortical E/I balance were robustly corrected—most notably, the theta/gamma ratio, which was reduced in Csnk2b+/− mice, returned to WT levels after treatment (p = 0.003; Figure 5E). The beta/gamma ratio showed a concordant, though nonsignificant, tendency toward normalization (p > 0.1; Figure 5F). Together, these data nominate theta and gamma band power, and their ratios, as sensitive ECoG biomarkers of target engagement and physiological efficacy for CSNK2B-linked NDDs. We next evaluated whether local circuit repair generalized to macroscopic communication. Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA of magnitude-squared coherence demonstrated a strong main effect of group (F (2, 23) = 14.15, p < 0.0001) and a significant groups × frequency band interaction (F (8, 92) = 3.513, p = 0.0014), consistent with band-specific network impairment and rescue. Gene therapy increased inter-regional synchrony and restored coherence to WT levels across key bands, including gamma (both p < 0.05; Figures 5G and 5H), positioning band-limited coherence as a second quantitative biomarker class.

Finally, to probe effective connectivity, we performed Granger causality focused on gamma (a PV-dependent rhythm). Although net directionality remained near zero in all groups (balanced bidirectional flow; Figure 5I), the absolute strength of bidirectional gamma-band influence—a measure of information transfer efficiency—was significantly reduced in Csnk2b+/− mice and was restored by gene therapy to WT levels (p = 0.036; Figure 5J). Notably, all analyses replicated in light-phase data, indicating state-independent robustness of these ECoG endpoints (Figures S4A–S4J).

Collectively, these results define a compact, translatable ECoG biomarker panel—(i) theta and gamma relative power, (ii) theta/gamma (±beta/gamma) ratios, (iii) band-limited coherence, and (iv) gamma-band Granger causality—that reports AAV target engagement, E/I rebalancing, and network-level rescue. These signatures offer objective efficacy readouts to guide dose optimization and early clinical decision-making in gene therapy for NDDs.

During the social novelty test, compared to the control group (Csnk2b+/− mice injected with AAV-GFP), both treatment groups showed a significant social preference, spending significantly more time interacting with the stranger mouse than with the familiar mouse (hSyn, p = 0.032; CAG, p < 0.001; Figures 6A and 6B). Subsequently, we evaluated anxiety-like behavior using the open field test. Following injection with either AAV-hSyn-CSNK2B or AAV-CAG-CSNK2B, Csnk2b+/− mice spent significantly more time exploring the central area of the open field (hSyn, p = 0.012; CAG, p = 0.01; Figures 6C and 6D), indicating a rescue of the anxiety-like phenotype. No significant difference was observed between the two treatment groups (Figure 6D). Importantly, the total locomotor distance did not differ significantly between treated and control groups, nor between the two treatment groups (Figure 6E), suggesting that the improvement in center exploration was not secondary to alterations in overall locomotor activity.

We then investigated whether the long-term spatial learning and memory abilities of Csnk2b+/− mice were improved using the Barnes maze test. We found that after injection of AAV-hSyn-CSNK2B or AAV-CAG-CSNK2B, the time required for Csnk2b+/− mice to locate the escape hole in the test on day 12 was significantly reduced (both p < 0.001; Figure 6F). However, no significant difference was observed in the time taken to find the escape hole between the two treatment groups (Figure 6F). In addition, during the test on day 5, there was no significant difference in the time taken to locate the escape hole among the four groups of mice (Figure 6F), which indicates that the short-term spatial learning and memory abilities of Csnk2b+/− mice were not impaired.

Finally, we evaluated the 24-h seizure profiles of Csnk2b+/− mice in adulthood. The results demonstrated that injection of either AAV-hSyn-CSNK2B or AAV-CAG-CSNK2B reduced the incidence of spontaneous seizures in Csnk2b+/− mice (hSyn, p = 0.02; CAG, p = 0.07; Figure 6G). The seizure incidence decreased from 70% (7 out of 10) in the control group to 10% (1 out of 10) in the AAV-hSyn-CSNK2B group and 20% (2 out of 10) in the AAV-CAG-CSNK2B group, with no significant difference between the two therapeutic groups (Figure 6G). Furthermore, relative to the control group, both therapeutic groups exhibited marked reductions in seizure frequency (hSyn, p = 0.002; CAG, p = 0.004; Figure 6H) and total seizure duration (hSyn, p = 0.003; CAG, p = 0.006; Figure 6I), whereas no significant differences were detected between the two AAV-treated groups (Figures 6H and 6I). Specifically, the daily seizure frequency of Csnk2b+/− mice decreased from 3.70 ± 3.21 times/day (control group) to 0.30 ± 0.95 times/day (AAV-hSyn-CSNK2B group) and 0.40 ± 0.84 times/day (AAV-CAG-CSNK2B group) (Figure 6H). Correspondingly, the total daily seizure duration was reduced from 3.57 ± 3.43 min per day to 0.39 ± 1.23 min per day and 0.30 ± 0.59 min per day (Figure 6I).

Csnk2b+/− mice closely mirror clinical manifestations of CSNK2B-associated disorders. 20 , 21 They exhibited impaired social novelty recognition and heightened anxiety, consistent with ASD features. 22 Social novelty recognition is a complex cognitive process that depends on the coordinated function of brain regions such as the prefrontal cortex and basolateral amygdala, as well as specialized neuronal circuits, including the PV-positive interneuron network. 23 , 24 The reduction in cortical layers 2/3 and 6 neurons and the specific loss of PV interneurons may disrupt these circuits, contributing to social impairments. Moreover, Csnk2b+/− mice showed long-term memory deficits, epileptic seizures, and lower body weight, analogous to patients with Poirier-Bienvenu neurodevelopmental syndrome (POBINDS). 20 These findings suggest that Csnk2b+/− mice represent a valuable model for studying diseases linked to CSNK2B mutations.

Our findings revealed that Csnk2b haploinsufficiency led to cortical thinning. Previous research has shown that Csnk2b deficiency impairs the proliferation and differentiation of neural stem cells, 9 , 16 which may be the underlying cause of the observed defects in brain development. Beyond neural stem cells, CSNK2B also modulates proliferation of diverse cell types, such as hepatocytes and colorectal cells. 25 , 26 This suggests that Csnk2b can regulate proliferation across multiple cell types, contributing to systemic growth retardation in Csnk2b+/− mice. The reduced body weight observed in Csnk2b+/− mice may therefore reflect both central and systemic mechanisms. In particular, broader weight improvement with the CAG-driven vector than with the neuron-specific hSyn vector raises the possibility that non-neuronal or peripheral CSNK2B expression also contributes to growth regulation. Because we did not directly assess feeding behavior, metabolic parameters, or peripheral tissue involvement, the mechanism underlying this phenotype remains to be determined.

Importantly, we observed a significant reduction in neurons within the superficial and deep layers of the cortex, as well as a specific loss of PV-positive inhibitory interneurons. This selective vulnerability may be attributed to the unique signaling dependencies of these neuronal populations. For instance, the mTOR and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, both of which are regulated by CSNK2B, are known to play critical roles in the development and function of PV-positive interneurons. 25 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 An intriguing finding is that PV-positive interneuron density was reduced in the cortex but not the hippocampus. Our data do not explain this regional difference. Cortical and hippocampal PV interneurons may differ in developmental timing, circuit integration, or sensitivity to reduced CSNK2B signaling. Given that CSNK2B is linked to the mTOR and Wnt-β-catenin pathways, which are important for interneuron development, cortical PV neurons may be more vulnerable to Csnk2b haploinsufficiency. Alternatively, hippocampal PV interneurons may be less affected at this stage or may engage compensatory mechanisms that preserve cell density. Moreover, our whole-hippocampus analysis may have missed subtle subregion-specific changes.

The reduction of PV-positive inhibitory interneurons is particularly noteworthy, as these cells are crucial for maintaining the E/I balance in cortical circuits. 31 Our data also confirm the cortical E/I imbalance in Csnk2b-deficient mice, characterized by increased gamma power and a decreased theta/gamma ratio. PV-positive neurons are crucial for fast inhibition and gamma rhythm generation. 32 , 33 This microcircuit dysfunction not only leads to local hyperexcitability but also impairs global brain communication. This impairment not only constitutes a potential mechanism contributing to autism-like behaviors but also provides a plausible explanation for the increased seizure susceptibility observed in the model. 34 , 35 , 36 , 37

Electroencephalogram (EEG) abnormalities have been reported in individuals with CSNK2B-related neurodevelopmental syndrome, including interictal epileptiform discharges, generalized spike-and-wave or polyspike-and-wave activity and, in some cases, background slowing. 38 , 39 However, existing studies are largely based on clinical EEG interpretation rather than systematic quantitative analyses of frequency band-resolved spectral power. To our knowledge, detailed band-based spectral comparisons, similar to those performed in the present mouse study, have not yet been conducted in patient cohorts. Therefore, while our findings raise the possibility that specific EEG parameters may represent candidate translational readouts of network dysfunction, their clinical applicability remains speculative and will require validation in future prospective studies using standardized EEG acquisition and analytical pipelines.

We initiated treatment at P3. In mice, the peak of embryonic neurogenesis occurs before birth, 40 and AAV-mediated transgene expression requires several days to reach functional levels. Thus, the observed therapeutic effects are unlikely to result from renewed production of prenatally generated neurons. Instead, postnatal CSNK2B restoration likely acts on ongoing postnatal processes: neuronal survival, maturation, dendrite/axon growth, synaptogenesis, and circuit refinement. In this framework, the rescue of cortical neurons may reflect improved survival or maturation of prenatally generated neurons or recovery of marker expression, rather than de novo neurogenesis. This intervention rescued multiple deficits, supporting a role for CSNK2B in postnatal development.

Regarding serotype selection, we opted for the AAV-PHP.eB variant, which was engineered from AAV9. 41 , 42 Its enhanced ability to cross the blood-brain barrier enables broad CNS delivery via retro-orbital injection, thereby avoiding direct needle penetration into the neonatal brain. We note, however, that neonatal intracranial or intracerebroventricular delivery has been widely used and can achieve robust transduction in many experimental settings. 43 , 44 The AAV-PHP.eB also has certain limitations. For example, its high efficiency is strain-dependent in mice, 42 , 45 and in non-human primates (such as macaques), its transduction efficiency does not exhibit a significant advantage. 46 , 47 These factors may restrict its broad applicability across different animal models or human populations.

Although AAV-CAG-CSNK2B showed higher transduction and protein expression than AAV-hSyn-CSNK2B in bulk tissue, the two treatments did not differ significantly in cortical neuron development, behavior, epilepsy, or survival. Because we lacked cell-type-specific transduction or non-CNS expression data, the basis for this comparable efficacy remains unclear. Low-level peripheral expression from hSyn cannot be excluded. Future studies should assess biodistribution and tissue-specific expression to define the relative contributions of central versus peripheral transduction. Moreover, because CSNK2B deficiency is dosage-sensitive, expression level is likely critical for therapeutic development. Although we observed no overt toxicity, our experiments were not designed to evaluate overexpression or ectopic expression. Thus, the apparent efficacy of AAV-mediated rescue does not guarantee that supraphysiological or off-target CSNK2B expression is safe. Future studies should systematically define the therapeutic window and safety margin through dose dependence, cell-type specificity, and long-term outcomes.

First, our focus was primarily on macroscopic cortical and hippocampal structures and specific neuronal types. Future research should investigate how Csnk2b deficiency affects neural circuits, synaptic plasticity, and downstream pathways. Second, our model only recapitulates heterozygous deletion, whereas patients carry diverse mutations that may have different functional impacts. Third, intervention was at P3, simulating early treatment. Because transgene expression requires several days, our results do not indicate full reversal of prenatal defects but rather that a substantial part of the phenotype remains modifiable during early postnatal maturation. Our histological analyses cannot distinguish whether the rescue reflects recovery of cell number, improved survival of prenatally generated neurons, or restoration of neuronal identity. Therapeutic efficacy for individuals with already apparent symptoms remains to be investigated. Subsequent studies should elucidate refined molecular mechanisms, explore treatment efficacy across broader time windows, and advance translational research.

After 24-h cable habituation, continuous ECoG signals were acquired across a full 24-h circadian cycle using a Pinnacle Technology setup, sampled at 1000 Hz with online band-pass filtering (0.5–500 Hz) and 50/60 Hz notch filtering. Synchronized video was recorded throughout for behavioral correlation. Data from all groups were processed through a standardized MATLAB pipeline: raw EDF signals were downsampled to 250 Hz and segmented into 60-min epochs. The first two ECoG channels from the 9th nocturnal segment (hours 8–9) were selected for core analysis, followed by multi-notch filtering (50 Hz harmonics elimination), 4th-order Butterworth band-pass filtering (0.5–100 Hz), and Z score normalization to ensure signal quality.

Power spectral density was computed using Welch’s method (256-point window, 128-point overlap, 256-point FFT) across five key frequency bands: δ (1–4 Hz), θ (4–8 Hz), α (8–13 Hz), β (13–30 Hz), and γ (30–80 Hz). Both absolute power and relative power (normalized to total power in 1–100 Hz range) were analyzed for each band, providing fundamental data for subsequent E/I imbalance metric calculations.

Magnitude-squared coherence was employed to quantify the linear dependence and frequency-specific synchronization between the two ECoG channels. The coherence estimate, Cxy(f), between signals x(t) and y(t) at frequency f was calculated using the following formula:

Cxy(f)=|Pxy(f)|2Pxx(f)Pyy(f) where Pxx(f) and Pyy(f) are the power spectral densities of x and y, and Pxy(f) is the cross-power spectral density. Calculations were based on Welch’s periodogram method (256-point Hamming window, 50% overlap), yielding coherence values ranging from 0 (no connectivity) to 1 (perfect synchronization). The mean coherence within each pre-defined frequency band was subsequently computed for group-level statistical comparisons.

Directional information flow was investigated using Granger causality analysis. We fitted a bivariate vector autoregressive (VAR) model with an order of 8 to the preprocessed signals. Conceptually, a signal X is said to Granger-cause a signal Y if the prediction of Y is improved by incorporating past values of X. By comparing the predictive power of models that included or excluded the historical information from one channel to another, we quantified the unidirectional causal influence (Granger causality) in both directions (GCX→Y and GCY→X). The net causal flow was subsequently defined as the difference between these two directional influences (GCX→Y − GCY→X), providing a measure of the dominant direction of information exchange.

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 software and are presented as mean ± SEM, with the sample size (n) specified in each figure legend. Prior to statistical analyses, data were evaluated for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. We also carefully inspected the data for potential outliers. however, to maintain the integrity of biological variance, no data points were excluded from the final analyses. Statistical analyses were selected according to the experimental design and data distribution, and are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. For comparisons between two independent groups with normally distributed data, a two-tailed Student’s t test was used. For comparisons among more than two groups with normally distributed data, one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test was applied, as appropriate. For datasets that did not pass the normality test, non-parametric tests were utilized, such as the Mann-Whitney U test (for two groups) or the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test (for more than two groups). Paired two-tailed Student’s t test was used for within-group comparisons where applicable. Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical data, and the log rank (Mantel–Cox) test was used for survival analysis. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant and is indicated as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001; NS denotes not significant.
