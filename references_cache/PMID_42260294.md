Abstract
Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is caused by mutations in the Survival Motor Neuron 1 (SMN1) gene, leading to 
reduced SMN protein levels and widespread disruption in RNA metabolism. Despite of disease-modifying 
therapies, which remarkably improve patient outcomes, the long-term effects remain unknown. Dysregulated 
small non-coding RNAs, including microRNAs, have been reported in SMA, but their contribution to the disease 
remains unclear. We previously showed that flunarizine could improve the phenotype of an SMA mouse model; 
however its mode of action is incomplete. Here, we showed that flunarizine modulates the expression of 
numerous microRNAs. Using small-RNA sequencing of a flunarizine-treated SMA patient fibroblast cell line, 
we identified several microRNAs, which are also dysregulated in the brains and/or spinal cords of SMA mouse 
models at early disease stages and corrected with flunarizine. Transfection of the miR-128-3p inhibitor interferes 
with the flunarizine-induced neurite outgrowth in the murine neuronal NSC34 cells. Among the mRNAs 
modulated by flunarizine, homeodomain interacting protein kinase 2 (Hipk2) transcripts were revealed as novel 
miR-128-3p targets using either the mimic or its inhibitor. Our findings suggest that an early microRNA 
accumulation in spinal cords of SMA models can contribute to molecular dysfunctions and may represent an 
initiating event in pathogenesis.

Introduction 
Neurodegenerative diseases, including age-related neurological disorders and their associated loss of 
muscle mass, remain incurable, highlighting a need for strategies that might accelerate therapeutic discoveries. 
Drug repurposing provides a valuable approach to identify new indications for compounds whose biochemical 
properties and clinical safety have already been partially established. However, when applied to previously 
untested pathological contexts, their mechanisms of action must be reassessed. Flunarizine was recently 
examined in the setting of motor neuron disease (MND) spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) models 1. Further 
analyses could define its molecular profile and support a potential for repositioning in neuroprotection.
SMA is a genetic neurodegenerative disease primary affecting spinal MNs and skeletal muscles they 
innervate, caused by mutations or deletions of the survival motor neuron 1 (SMN1) gene 2. The SMN2 gene, a 
near-identical gene of SMN1 located in close proximity on chromosome 5q13 2-3, and retained in all SMA 
patients 4, provides only partial compensation for SMN1 loss. Only a few nucleotide variations differentiate 
SMN2 from SMN1, the variation in SMN2 exon7 (E7) promotes E7 skipping, reducing SMN protein levels 4-6. 
The number of SMN2 copies varies in human genome and a higher copy number is preferentially associated with 
a milder SMA phenotype 7. Therefore, the most direct therapeutic strategy for SMA was to increase SMN 
protein levels either by promoting SMN2 E7 inclusion or through SMN1 gene replacement therapy. Three 
innovative treatments are now available for clinical use, namely nusinersen (Spinraza, Biogen), onasemnogene 
abeparvovec (Zolgensma, Novartis) and risdiplam (Evrysdi, Roche) 8-10. Nusinersen is an antisense 
oligonucleotide administered by repetitive lumbar punctures that raises SMN2 E7 inclusion, increasing SMN 
protein levels. Onasemnogene abeparvovec is an AAV9-mediated SMN1 gene replacement therapy given as a 
single intravenous, or intrathecal, injection. Finally, the oral medication risdiplam, taken daily, is a splicing 
modifier promoting SMN2 E7 inclusion. Although these therapies have extended patient life expectancy, their 
variable effect on motor function underscores the need for a better understanding of disease mechanisms.
SMN protein is ubiquitously expressed and localizes in the cytoplasm and nucleus where it concentrates 
in nuclear ribonucleoprotein (RNP) condensates named Cajal bodies (CBs) 11. This was the first indication of a 
role for SMN in RNA metabolism. Indeed, CBs play a role in the assembly of numerous RNP particles involved 
in splicing, transcription and ribosome biogenesis 12. Its best studied function is an essential role for SMN in the 
assembly of the splicing small nuclear (sn)RNPs 13. In SMA, reduced SMN levels lead to tissue- and snRNP-
specific changes in the snRNP repertoire that is responsible for widespread splicing defects, including intron 
retention 14-15. The expression of other small non-coding RNAs is also impaired in SMA, such as small nucleolar 
(sno)RNAs and microRNAs 16-17. 
MicroRNAs are abundant regulators of nervous system development, controlling patterning, cell 
specification, and axonal pathfinding 18. Maturation steps are regulated by many factors that ultimately modulate 
entire gene networks 19. These ~22-nucleotide non-coding RNAs form a major class of post-transcriptional 
regulators that fine-tune gene expression programs 18-21. Their biogenesis is tightly regulated and begins with 
RNA polymerase II–mediated transcription of primary microRNA (pri-miRNA) transcripts, which are cleaved in 
the nucleus by the Drosha–DGCR8 Microprocessor complex to generate precursor microRNAs (pre-miRNAs). 
Pre-miRNAs are exported to the cytoplasm by Exportin-5 and processed by Dicer into microRNA duplexes, 
from which one strand is loaded onto an Argonaute protein to form the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), 
while the passenger strand is degraded, resulting in strand asymmetry for many microRNAs 22. The mature 
microRNA guides RISC to target mRNAs via partial or complete complementarity, leading to translational 
repression or mRNA degradation. In addition to the canonical pathway, non-canonical mechanisms also 
contribute to microRNA biogenesis, including nuclear processes such as splicing-dependent maturation of 
intronic microRNAs 23. The microRNA databases contain 1879 mature microRNAs, but only 30-40 microRNAs 
are highly expressed in the central nervous system (CNS) 24. 
Maintenance of microRNA homeostasis emerged as a key determinant of MN survival. Indeed, a cell-
specific ablation of Dicer induces MN cell death 25. Deregulated microRNA expression is observed in SMA and 
in another MND, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) 25-30. Indeed, ALS-associated RNA-binding proteins 
TDP43 (encoded by TARDBP) and FUS, and the SMN-complex protein Gemin5 regulate microRNA biogenesis 
25,30-33. Most microRNAs act as post-transcriptional regulators that bind to the non-coding 3’UTR of mRNA 
targets. Bioinformatics analyses reveal that a single microRNA can have thousands of candidate targets, but 
experimental validations result in only a very few being true targets 34. Interestingly, levels of mature

microRNAs are dynamically and reversibly modulated under physiological signals 18,20, making them relevant 
therapeutic targets and/or biomarkers in human diseases.
A number of reports substantiate the connection between SMA and microRNA dysregulation. Smn-
knockdown in embryonic rat cortical neurons revealed that miR-183 expression was up-regulated while its mTor 
mRNA target was down-regulated upon SMN depletion 35. However, the injection of a miR-183 sponge to 
inhibit the microRNA showed modest impact on an SMA mouse model 35. Others revealed a marked increase of 
miR-431 levels in embryonic rat MNs upon SMN reduction together with reduced levels of miR-431 target 
chondrolectin (Chodl) mRNA 36. Of note, the CHODL protein can regulate MN axon outgrowth 37. In another 
study, MIR23a replacement therapy extended lifespan of an SMA mouse model 38. The neuroprotective effect of 
miR-23a was through SMN-independent mechanisms that did not prevent the loss of proprioceptive VGLUT1 
synapses. Moreover, dysregulation of miRNA expression in SMA appears to be context dependent. miR-34a, 
which regulates genes involved in axonal growth and neuroprotection, is reduced in spinal MNs of SMA mice, 
where its restoration improves neuromuscular function 3 9 , yet is increased in non-neural tissues that may 
contribute to systemic disease features 40, underscoring context-specific roles of miRNAs in SMA pathogenesis 
41. Recent studies showed that miR-140-3p is progressively upregulated in the spinal cord of SMA mice, and its 
inhibition leads to improved behavioral outcomes 42.
In biofluids from SMA patients, the detection of microRNAs such as miRs 1, 7, 9, 15a, 15b, 23a, 34, 
107, 126, 128-2, 130, 132, 133a/b, 142, 181a, 206, 324, 335, 378a, 423, 431, 660, has been correlated with 
clinical responses to current therapies 43-50. However, miR-146a remains upregulated in biofluids of treated SMA 
patients 47. Astrocyte-derived miR-146a can impair MN survival and function, suggesting that sustained miR-
146a levels may contribute to disease progression through inflammatory and glial-mediated mechanisms 51. 
Consistent with their involvement in MN biology, miR-1, 133a/b, 181 and 206 have also been detected in 
biofluids of ALS patients 52-55. Collectively, these findings underline microRNAs as contributors to MN 
vulnerability and disease progression, underscoring the broader relevance of their dysregulation in MN 
pathology. The roles of microRNAs in disease mechanisms and their use as biomarkers or therapeutic targets 
remain to be further addressed.
To identify pharmacological compounds that could be possible adjunct therapy, we performed a 
repositioning screen. Compounds, including flunarizine, were found to facilitate the reestablishment of SMN 
protein in CBs of fibroblasts derived from SMA patients 1. Flunarizine is primarily indicated for the treatment of 
migraine, vertigo and epilepsy and can change in vitro splicing in cancer cells 56. Moreover, the drug can 
improve lifespan, survival of spinal cord MNs, VGLUT1 synapses and neuromuscular junctions of an SMA 
mouse model 1. We confirmed that flunarizine exhibits slow, indirect inhibition of calcium channels rather than 
classical rapid channel blockade 57, suggesting its therapeutic effects might arise through other mechanisms. 
Along this line, HIPK2 and the trimethylation of lysine 27 on histone H3 protein (H3K27me3) demethylase 
KDM6B were increased with flunarizine in murine neuronal NSC34 cells, which correlates with neurite 
outgrowth, a process impaired in MN diseases 58. Both genes have important roles for MNs. Kdm6b is 
responsible for spinal cord MN specification during development 59. Hipk2 regulates neuronal survival during 
the perinatal period 60 and influences endoplasmic reticulum stress in an ALS mouse model 61, suggesting critical 
roles in MN biology. However, the extent to which flunarizine could potentially modulate microRNA profile and 
whether this might contribute to the neuroprotective effects remained to be determined.
We show here that flunarizine modifies microRNA expression in an SMA patient fibroblast cell line 
and spinal cord of an SMA mouse model. We reveal tissue- and age-dependent alterations of microRNA profiles 
during disease that are normalized by flunarizine. The drug has also a profound impact on the microRNA 
signature of murine neuronal NSC34 cells, with some microRNAs promoting neurite outgrowth. The expression 
of the neuron-specific microRNA miR-128-3p increases in NSC34 cells with flunarizine and miR-128-3p 
inhibition increases the levels of Hipk2 mRNAs and interferes with the flunarizine-induced neurite outgrowth. 
Flunarizine does not induce overall modifications of histone H3 protein marks within NSC34 cells. Instead, 
changes in microRNA signatures by flunarizine appear to be dependent on cellular/tissue context.

Materials and Methods
All methods were carried out in accordance with approved institutional biosafety and radiation guidelines for 
Université Paris Cité (UPC), Institut National de la Santé Et de la Recherche Médicale (INSERM) and Ministère 
de l’Enseignement supérieur, de la Recherche et de l’Espace and adhered to European and French regulations. 
All experimental protocols were approved by INSERM and UPC.
Cell Cultures, flunarizine treatment, and preparation for immunodetection
SMA and control fibroblast cell lines, and murine motoneuron-like NSC34 cells were grown on TPP dishes in 
Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM)-Glutamax supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/ml) 
and streptomycin (100 mg/ml) at 37°C with 5% CO2 and treated with flunarizine as previously described 1,58. 
The NSC34 cell line was kindly provided by Neil R. Cashman 62 as previously indicated 58. Briefly, flunarizine 
was dissolved in DMSO (10 mg/mL). Cells are grown at 10 000 cells/cm 2 and treated with flunarizine (10 
μg/mL) or diluent (0.1% DMSO) for different timepoints. Treated cells were washed with cold PBS, scraped off, 
centrifuged, and pellets frozen at −80°C for immunoblotting. Immunofluorescence was performed as previously 
described [1,57-58]. Briefly, cells were fixed in 4% formaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and 
immunostained with primary and secondary antibodies (Supplementary Table S1). Nuclei were stained with 
bisbenzimide, and samples mounted in Fluoromount-Gold. 
Primary skin fibroblasts derived from an SMA patient of the French SMA cohorts previously established in 
1990s 63 under the grant n°88-C0179 (Ministère de la Recherche et de la Technologie) were kindly provided by 
Pr J Melki (INSERM, France). Samples were anonymised and informed consent was obtained from patients or 
their legal guardians. Fibroblasts from a type I SMA patient were immortalized by transfection with an SV40 
large T antigen-expressing plasmid and G418 selection as previously described 64. The resulting cell line was 
maintained under standard culture conditions. Pr B Parfait kindly provided the control fibroblast cell line 65. All 
work with human cells was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines from INSERM and 
UPC, and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.
Transfection Assay
Freshly plated NSC34 cells were transfected using DharmaFect Transfection Reagent III according to the 
manufacturer (Horizon Discovery) with either miR mimic or inhibitor at a final concentration of 15 or 30 nM 
(Table S2). Controls were transfected cells with a miR inhibitor negative control. 48 h post-transfection, cells 
were either stored at -80°C as pellets, lysed in TRIzol for RNA extraction or fixed for immunofluorescence.
Immunoblotting analysis
Frozen pellets were resuspended in cold Tris–NaCl buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl) containing 
EDTA-free protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined, and 30 µg were separated by SDS–PAGE, 
and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were incubated with the No-Stain protein labeling solution 
(Invitrogen) for total protein normalization, blocked in 10% nonfat milk, incubated with primary (Table S1) and 
HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (CleanBlot), detected with ECL, and imaged with ImageQuant LAS 4000 
or Fuji films. Membranes were stripped with Restore (Thermo Scientific) when required for repeated probings. 
Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.
RNA preparation, and qPCR analysis
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and treated with RNase-free DNase (Promega). cDNA was 
synthesized from 1 µg RNA using either SuperScript III (Invitrogen) or miScript II RT (Qiagen) for mRNA and 
microRNA analysis, respectively. Quantitative PCR was performed in triplicates with SYBR Green ROX mix 
(Thermo Scientific) on Bio-Rad CFX384. Normalized expression was calculated according to the ∆∆Ct method 
using reference genes as indicated in figure legends. Primers were designed using a free primer design tool 
(eurofinsgenomics.eu) and validated following the MIQE guidelines (Table S3).

Total RNA quality (RIN 8.6–9.2) and microRNA content (63–70%) were assessed on an Agilent 2100 
Bioanalyzer using RNA 6000 Pico and Small RNA kits. RNA-seq libraries were prepared from 500 ng of total 
RNA. Briefly, Small RNAs were purified using the PureLink miRNA Isolation Kit (Ambion). Libraries were

Animal Procedures, and spinal cord preparation
The severe Taiwanese mouse model of SMA (Smn ko/ko; huSMN2 tg/0) and control heterozygous mice (Smn ko/wt; 
SMN2tg/0) were maintained as previously described [1,57-58], and were from a cross between the transgenic mild 
SMA-like males (strain 005058, Smn ko/ko; huSMN2 +/+ four copies, FVB.CgSmn1 tm1HungTg(SMN2)2Hung/J), and 
heterozygous Smn knock-out females Smn ko/+ (FVB.129P2(B6)-Smn1tm1Hung/J) generated from our cross between 
transgenic mild SMA-like males and FVB/NRj females, as we previously described [57]. The FVB/NRj females 
are from Janvier (Le Genest-St-Isle, France). Daily injections were administered from birth with either 
flunarizine (500 μg/mL, 1 μl/g) or vehicle (2% DMSO, 1 μl/g) in saline solution as previously described 1. Each 
animal was assigned a unique identifier. Mouse models and controls from the same liter were included, as the 
SMA phenotype is not visible at birth. No exclusion criteria were applied, both sexes were considered, 
experiments were blinded, and group allocation was revealed only for analyses. Groups of 3–6 mice were used 
to minimize animal numbers, and genotyping was performed as described 57. Animal were anesthetized by 
intraperitoneal injections of pentobarbital (20 mg/kg) and decapitated, and tissues were dissected, snap-frozen, 
and stored at −80°C for RNA extraction. Immunofluorescence was performed as previously described 1. Briefly, 
animal were perfused with PBS, spinal cords were dissected, post-fixed for 24 h, embedded in agarose, and 
sectioned. Sections (50 µm) were pre-incubated with 0.01 M glycine, blocked with 5% donkey serum and 3% 
BSA, and incubated for 48h at 4°C with primary antibodies and with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room 
temperature (Table S1). After staining with bisbenzimide, sections were mounted with Fluoromount Gold.
Microscope image acquisition, processing, and analysis
Samples were imaged with an ORCA Flash camera (Hamamatsu Photonic) mounted on an epifluorescence

analyzed with ZEN or ImageJ. The normalized integrated density (NID) = (Integrated density – background 
density) / (area of the region of interest x slice number of the Z-stack)
Statistical Analysis
At least three independent experiments were performed and presented as the mean ± SD. Statistical analyses 
were conducted using Graphpad Prism 9.5.0 or Excel. We have analyzed the data for neurite outgrowth by 
computing mean values of the length of the longest neurite for each independent experiment (n3) and used 
means of means for the statistical analysis (see supplemental information). A normality test was conducted, and 
statistical tests were indicated in figure legends. p-values ≤ 0.05 were considered significant.

Results
Small-RNA sequencing of a flunarizine-treated SMA patient fibroblast cell line
We previously profiled transcriptional changes by RNA sequencing after a 4-hour flunarizine treatment 
of an SMA patient fibroblast cell line used to identify drug candidates including flunarizine 1. This cell line 
(hereafter referred to as SMA cells) was derived from a severe type I SMA patient carrying two SMN2 copies. 
Accordingly, a reduction of exon 7 in SMN2 transcripts was observed (Supplementary Fig. S1), leading to a 
residual SMN protein level of ≈25-30% 68. The STRING analysis with the dataset (GSE145146) revealed an 
enriched interaction network among the top differentially expressed (DE) genes. Although the network remained 
broadly distributed, a small number of proteins exhibited connectivity (Fig. S2). An enrichment analysis using 
MOAL software identified miR-128-3p as the top-ranked term among the predicted microRNA seed targets (Fig. 
1a). To explore whether flunarizine can mediate microRNA changes, we analysed their expression by an 
unbiased method. The RNA was isolated from SMA cells following a 4-hour treatment compared to its diluent, 
and cDNA libraries were generated for sequencing. The reads were mapped to annotated human microRNAs 
from MSigDB, revealing that flunarizine changes the expression of multiple microRNAs (Fig. 1b).
A subset of 30 microRNAs was compared by RT-qPCR between SMA and control cells (hereafter 
referred to as non-SMA) treated with the drug or its diluent (Fig. 1c). Flunarizine preferentially reduced miR 
levels in the SMA cells, with distinct profiles for SMA and non-SMA cells. Four microRNAs (miR-145-5p, 
miR-146a-5p, miR-149-5p, 320a-3p) were upregulated in SMA compared with non-SMA cells (DE1.5) 
whereas 11 microRNAs (miR-24-3p, miR-29b-3p, miR-92a-3p, miR-125b-5p, miR-128-3p, miR-181a-5p, miR-
191-5p, miR-324-5p, miR-378a-3p, miR-4521) were downregulated by at least 1.5-fold (DE<0.67). Among the 
15 DE microRNAs, eleven (miR-24-3p, miR-29b-1-5p, miR-92a-3p, miR-125b-5p, miR-145-5p, miR-146a-5p, 
miR-146b-5p, miR-181a-5p, miR-193a-5p, miR-324-5p, miR-378a-3p) were previously reported as altered in 
SMA conditions 36,38,39,43,46,50. We also observed 4 additional DE microRNAs (miR-128-3p, miR-149-5p, miR-
191-5p, miR-320a-3p). Notably, the divergent differential expression between RNA-sequencing and RT-qPCR is 
likely due to differences in isoform resolution. Indeed, RNA-sequencing captures the diversity of isoforms (pre-, 
pri- and mature) whereas RT-qPCR is designed to detect the mature isoforms. Although the limited sample size 
precluded definitive conclusions on disease-specificity, dysregulation of miR-128-3p was also reported with 
fibroblasts derived from ALS patients carrying C9orf72 mutations or sporadic ALS 69. 
miR-128-3p inhibition affects flunarizine-mediated neurite outgrowth in NSC34 cells
Given the impact of flunarizine on microRNA levels in human fibroblasts, we explored whether the 
drug could have similar effects in NSC34 cells. This hybrid cell line was produced by the fusion of a 
neuroblastoma cell line with MN-enriched cells from murine embryonic (E12–E14) spinal cords 62. E12 is an 
early stage of MN differentiation, with post-mitotic MNs being generated between E9 and E11 70. Our previous 
studies showed that flunarizine promotes neurite outgrowth in NSC34 cells, and in SMA patient iPSCs-derived 
MNs 58. We therefore examined by RT-qPCR the effects of 5- and 16-hour treatments on the expression level of 
the 30 microRNAs, several of which have been implicated in neurite outgrowth. Flunarizine produces changes 
on the microRNA profile (Fig. 2a). At 5-hour treatment, a decrease was observed in miR-138-5p, miR-181a-3p, 
and miR-320a-5p levels (DE≤0.70) while miR-29b-1-5p, miR-125a-5p and miR-146a-5p were upregulated 
(DE≥1.29). After a 16-hour treatment, microRNA levels were either unchanged or upregulated (DE>1.5), 
including miR-23a-5p, miR-27a-5p, miR-29b-1-5p, miR-125a-5p, miR-125b-5p, miR-145-5p, miR-146a-5p, 
miR-193a-5p and miR-378a-3p. We also noted a significant increase (DE=1.26) in miR-128-3p levels after a 16-
hour treatment.
We next evaluated whether some of those microRNAs could regulate neurite growth as flunarizine does 
(Fig. 2b-c). miR-92a-3p and miR-199a-5p were used as positive and neutral effector, respectively. The 
microRNA-17–92 cluster promotes axonal growth 71 [67] while miR-199a-5p has no effect on neurite growth 72. 
At 48-hour post-transfection, the cells were fixed and immunolabelled with an antibody against beta 3-tubulin, a 
neuron-specific tubulin isoform encoded by Tubb3 that is a marker of morphological maturation used to measure 
the length of the longest neurite per cell. Cells transfected with miR-92a-3p mimic showed an increased 
proportion bearing a longer neurite while those transfected with miR-199a-5p mimic were not different from the 
negative control (NEG). A significant increase of neurite lengths was considered growth promoting while

transfection, the length of the longest neurite was measured (Fig. 2e). We found that reduced miR-128-3p levels 
significantly reduced flunarizine impact on neurite length, suggesting a role in the drug mechanisms. 
Hipk2 transcripts are targets of miR-128-3p in NSC34 cells
To identify potential miR-128-3p targets under flunarizine treatment, we compared the prediction miR-
binding site databases and our previous RNA-seq dataset 68 and found candidates (Fig. 3a). We first selected a 
gene subset and evaluated by RT-qPCR their expression in NSC34 cells. The Zfp36l1, Arrdc4, Adamts5, and 
Hic1 mRNAs were not detected while Hipk2 was expressed and increased by flunarizine [58]. To validate that 
Hipk2 could be a miR-128-3p target, we performed transfection assays with miR-128-3p mimic or inhibitor. 
Modulations of miR-128-3p levels were confirmed in transfected cells (Fig. 3b). We also evaluated whether the 
levels of other microRNAs could have been altered with the mimic or inhibitor, confirming their specificity (Fig. 
S5). The effects of microRNAs on targets being highly context-specific, we evaluated known targets such as 
Casc3, Upf1, Txnip and Isl1. Casc3 and Upf1 encode components of the nonsense-mediated mRNA decay 
pathway that are regulated by miR-128-3p during neuronal differentiation 73,77,80 and Casc3 mRNA levels were 
transiently increased by flunarizine (Fig. S6). Txnip and Isl1 mRNAs have been described as miR-128-3p targets 
81,82, and are modulated by flunarizine 58,68. Moreover, miR-128 has been shown to repress H3K27me3 marks 83. 
Kdm6a and Kdm6b encode H3K27me3-specific demethylases, and flunarizine modulates Kdm6b mRNA and 
protein levels 58. Two days post-transfection, a reduction of mRNA levels for Casc3, Hipk2, and Kdm6a was 
observed with the mimic while the inhibitor caused an increase in mRNA levels for Txnip, R3hdm1, and Hipk2 
(Fig. 3c). No significant changes were observed in the expression of other known targets, such as Upf1 and Isl1, 
further indicating context-specific regulation. The fact that miR-128-3p mimic decreased Hipk2 mRNA levels 
and its inhibitor increased them indicated that Hipk2 behaves like a direct target.
Immunoblot analysis revealed that miR-128-3p mimic caused a modest reduction in KDM6A levels 
whereas no changes were observed for KDM6B, and HIPK2 isoforms (Fig. 3d-e). These results are consistent 
with additional post-transcriptional regulation, as Kdm6a lacks predicted miR-128-3p binding sites in the 3′-
UTRs and that protein interaction might stabilize HIPK2. We also evaluated the H3K27 methylation and 
acetylation status in transfected cells, and detected no significant changes (Fig. 3d-e), indicating that a modest 
reduction of KDM6A levels were insufficient to induce detectable changes in H3K27 methylation.
Because flunarizine was shown by immunoblotting of total protein extracts to increase H3K9K14 
acetylation in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cell lines 84, we assessed histone H3 marks at different 
timepoints of flunarizine treatment in NSC34 cells (Fig. 3f-g). Transient changes of H3K27me3, H3K4me3, and 
KDM6A were detected at early timepoints whereas a trend towards reduced H3K4K19 acetylation was observed 
over the timeline. In contrast with what was previously reported for NSCLC, our findings showed that 
flunarizine had distinct effects on the overall H3K9K14 acetylation level in a neuronal cell type.

Attenuation of microRNA dysregulation by flunarizine in the spinal cord of an SMA mouse model
To determine whether flunarizine can modulate in vivo any of the above-identified microRNAs, their 
expression was examined in the brain and spinal cord of control and SMA mouse models at post-natal day 5 (P5, 
early symptoms), P10 and/or P11 (Fig. 4). Results from males and females were combined because no 
significant sex-dependent differences were observed. RT-qPCR analyses revealed that their expression strongly 
changes from P5 to P11 in SMA spinal cords compared to controls whereas no major differences were detected 
in the brain at different neonatal stages. In spinal cord of SMA model at P5, flunarizine normalized the elevated 
microRNA levels; including miR-146a-5p which up-regulation correlates with MN loss in SMA 51 and in ALS85. 
At P11, the drug produced a reversal of expression patterns in SMA spinal cord; this was shown by the 
upregulation of downregulated microRNAs and the downregulation of those upregulated. Our data showed that 
flunarizine normalizes tissue- and development-specific microRNA levels in an SMA mouse model.
Genotype- and sex-dependent regulation of Hipk2 expression by flunarizine in mouse spinal cord
Given the role of HIPK2 in neuronal biology 60-61, we analyzed by RT-qPCR whether Hipk2 mRNA 
levels were modulated by flunarizine in the spinal cord from males and females of control (heterozygous) and 
SMA mouse models at P5, and P8. One qPCR primer pair targets the mRNA central Hipk2 mRNA region while 
the other pairs target either the 5’ or 3’end (Fig. 5a). The 5’ end primers corresponded to transcripts from the 
first alternative promoter. At P5, control females showed a modest response to flunarizine whereas females from 
the SMA model did not. In males at P5, no differences were observed between treatments and genotypes (Fig. 
5b-c). However, at P8, differences in flunarizine responses were observed between males and females from 
controls and SMA mouse models, and revealing a sex- and genotype-dependent response to flunarizine (Fig. 5d-
e). In females, the drug increased Hipk2 expression in controls but had no effects in the disease model. In males, 
the opposite pattern was observed: the disease model responded to treatment whereas controls showed no 
changes. These findings demonstrate that flunarizine can elicit different effects depending on sex and phenotype, 
underscoring the need to consider both in therapeutic applications.
As MNs account for ≈1% of spinal cord cells, HIPK2 protein labeling was assessed by the co-
immunolabeling of MNs with anti-choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) in spinal cord sections from P8 control and 
disease mouse models treated with vehicle or flunarizine (Fig. 5f-i). The normalized integrated density (NID) for 
the HIPK2 immunoreactivity was higher in ChAT-positive cells (MNs) than in ChAT-negative cells (non-MNs) 
from all 8 conditions (males, females, vehicle- or drug-treated animals). In vehicle-treated males and females, 
the NID of ChAT-positive cells differed in SMA compared with controls, the distribution of NID values being 
discontinuous in SMA compared with controls. No significant effect of flunarizine on NID values was observed 
in ChAT-positive cells in female animals from either SMA or control models, whereas NID values were 
significantly reduced in males. Moreover, the drug significantly decreased NID values in chat-negative cells in 
SMA and control animals. Our findings showed that differences are detectable at the protein level, even though 
similar mRNA levels were measured between females and males, suggesting post-transcriptional regulation(s) of 
Hipk2 expression in a genotype- and sex-dependent manner.

Discussion
A better understanding of rare MN diseases is essential to improve treatments. Current therapies for 
SMA that aim to increase SMN protein levels have remarkably improved patient outcomes, however, they do not 
provide a definitive cure. In this study, we used microRNA sequencing in SMA patient fibroblasts to investigate 
the expression of microRNAs and their potential mRNA targets. Many important conclusions arise from this 
study. A subset of microRNAs dysregulated in SMA patient cells was modulated by flunarizine in murine 
neuronal NSC34 cells and in spinal cord of Smn-deficient mouse models. The function of this microRNA subset 
in MN is unknown.
Our microRNA profiling showed that isoforms of approximately 30 microRNAs were markedly 
modulated in SMA patient fibroblasts by flunarizine. Remarkably, the drug appeared to accumulate pri- and/or 
pre-miRNAs without corresponding increases in mature microRNAs in patient fibroblasts as observed by RT-
qPCR (Fig. 1). This pattern pointed to a bottleneck in microRNA processing that might implicate directly or 
indirectly components of the microRNA biogenesis machinery such as Dicer (in the cytoplasm) or 
Drosha/DGCR8 Microprocessor complex (in the nucleus). The Microprocessor complex rapidly processes co-
transcriptionnally the pri-microRNAs, resulting in short lifetime, while pre-microRNAs have intermediate 
lifetime and mature microRNAs are highly stable once loaded into Argonaute-containing RISC complexes 86. 
About half of the flunarizine-modulated microRNAs are intronic microRNAs (mirtrons, Table S4). In the case of 
mirtrons, pre-microRNAs are directly produced through splicing, debranching, trimming and also exported by 
exportin 5, before Dicer processing. In addition to the mirtrons, other pre-microRNAs are microprocessor-
independent, rather a 7-methylguanosine (m7G)-cap is added to be exported via the PHAX-exportin 1 (CRM1) 
pathway, such as miR-320a and miR-484 87.
Interestingly, most of the 30 flunarizine-modulated microRNAs were also dysregulated in spinal cord of 
SMA mouse models at post-natal day P5 and P11 and normalized with flunarizine (Fig. 4). SMA disease altered 
the expression of those mature microRNAs in both directions at P11, with some being upregulated and others 
downregulated compared with controls. At P5 (early symptomatic time point), the microRNAs were 
preferentially upregulated compared with controls and decreased by the drug. This is in agreement with the 
reduced protein translation observed in this SMA mouse model at early timepoints, often before obvious motor 
signs 88-89. These authors also reported RNAs identified with reduced translation efficiency, among which 
3’UTR-mediated translation regulation was the most highly enriched term, pointing to a potential contribution of 
microRNA-dependent mechanisms. Our previous report that flunarizine can upregulate mRNA levels without 
the corresponding increases in protein levels was also suggesting post-transcriptional regulations 58. Our findings 
that the drug corrects both microprocessor-dependent and -independent microRNAs suggest a cytoplasmic site 
of action, potentially through the modulation of Dicer-mediated processing. Although the direct interactors of 
flunarizine remain unknown, the drug behaves in a pre-clinical model as expected for a treatment, normalizing 
expression levels towards levels found in controls.
The analysis of the flunarizine-modulated microRNAs provided us the opportunity to identify novel 
microRNA alterations during disease progression in a mouse model. Indeed, 4 additional microRNAs were 
identified all related to the CNS. miR-128-3p, miR-149-5p, miR-191-5p, and miR-320a-3p found here can 
regulate neuronal survival, plasticity, and stress responses by modulating synaptic maturation, dendritic 
complexity, apoptosis, and microenvironmental homeostasis, highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets in 
neurodegenerative and ischemic disorders 90-93. Additional studies have highlighted other microRNAs as 
protective factors for neuron growth and development. Increases of either miR-23a-3p miR-27a-3p, miR-29b or 
reduction of miR-134, miR-181 or miR-494 can have neuronal protective effects 94-96. They contribute to 
neuroprotection by convergently regulating apoptosis, synaptic stability, oxidative stress responses, and 
inflammatory signaling. Interestingly, miR-100-5p, which is markedly downregulated in neural stem cells from 
another SMA mouse model 97, was also restored to normal levels by the drug. Among the microRNAs 
normalized by flunarizine, we also found miR-23a-3p, miR-27a-3p, miR-29b, miR-134, miR-181, and miR-494, 
indicating that the drug can function as a modulator of different gene expression programs towards 
neuroprotection. However, the finding that the neuronal miR-128-3p was not modulated by flunarizine in vivo, 
except in brains of SMA mouse models at post-natal day P5, was intriguing as it suggested a different link with 
the drug action.

We previously revealed flunarizine-induced neurite outgrowth in NSC34 cells 58 pointing to 
mechanisms that may explain its beneficial effects in vivo 1. Indeed, morphological change, such as neurite 
outgrowth, is widely regarded as a neuroprotective sign because it reflects not only differentiation and the 
capacity to extend axons/dendrites but also commitment of pro-survival and plasticity pathways. Our 
transfection study investigating neurite outgrowth showed that several of the flunarizine-regulated microRNAs 
were promoting neurite growth in NSC34 cells (Fig. 2e). The miR-128-3p depletion was the only condition to 
cause retraction of neurites (Fig. 2c), as previously reported in other neuronal cells 73. The role of miR-128 is 
highly context-dependent; miR-128 tends to limit neurite growth if expressed too early or too highly during the 
developmental cell migration phase. Indeed, it contributes to timing mechanisms that switch neurons from 
migration to growth and maturation. Under stress conditions or in more mature neurons, miR-128 seems to 
protect neurite structure, helping to prevent damage-induced retraction, also protecting neurons from apoptosis 
during spinal cord injury 98. We had further arguments to focus the study on miR-128-3p. Despite the capacity of 
the 30 identified microRNAs to target thousands of transcripts, they were associated with only ~200 
differentially expressed genes at the same timepoint in a flunarizine-treated SMA patient fibroblast cell line, 
indicating some selectivity. Notably, miR-128 ranked highest in our molecular signature analysis (Fig. 1a), and 
one differentially expressed gene, HIPK2, contained predicted binding sites for 25 out of the 30 microRNAs 
(Fig. S7), motivating our prioritization for further analyses.
HIPK2, the most studied kinase of the HIPK family, was initially identified as a co-repressor of 
homeodomain transcription factors 99. Its activity is tightly regulated through dynamic subcellular localization 
and mechanisms controlling synthesis and degradation, such that mRNA levels do not necessarily reflect protein 
abundance (Fig. 3), indicating additional regulatory mechanisms. HIPK2 critically influences the development 
and specification of neuronal subtypes by modulating key signaling pathways, transcription factors, and 
epigenetic regulators involved in neurogenesis and neuronal differentiation 100. Interestingly, Hipk2 knockdown 
mice exhibit neuronal loss and structural abnormalities across the CNS 101. They exhibit a myopathic phenotype 
with altered fiber size and neuromuscular junctions, accompanied by degenerative changes in ventral horn 
neurons, including large presumptive MNs. Another study reported similar observations 102. In contrast, in 
SOD1G93A ALS mice, HIPK2 drives ER stress–dependent MN death, while its inhibition delays onset, preserves 
neurons, and extends survival, highlighting it as a potential therapeutic target whose timing may be critical 61. 
Finally, HIPK2 can modulate chromatin through substrates such as HDACs and acetyltransferases, and may 
promote chromatin decompaction by reducing H3K27me3 levels, potentially facilitating the recruitment of 
H3K27me3 demethylases to target loci 103. Regulation of miR-128/HIPK2 axis might represent an adjunct to the 
future therapeutic avenues in the context of motor neuron diseases, however, the timing of intervention may be 
critical, and further investigations are required.
In conclusion, transcriptional profiling of a SMA patient fibroblast cell line at early timepoint of 
treatment revealed flunarizine-induced changes in the expression of genes and microRNAs implicated in 
neuronal homeostasis. Elucidating specific microRNA-mRNA interactions and their associated biological 
pathways may facilitate the identification of novel therapeutic targets to develop adjunct therapies in MN 
diseases, and restrain disease progression.
