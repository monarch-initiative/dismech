# Management and survival outcomes of desmoplastic small round cell tumor: a retrospective cohort study from a tertiary cancer center

Desmoplastic small round cell tumor (DSRCT) is a highly aggressive and uncommon sarcoma with a dismal clinical outcome. Western cohorts have reported clinical outcomes, but there is minimal evidence from the Indian context. This study examined the clinical features, management, and outcomes of DSRCT patients at a tertiary cancer center in India.

We retrospectively reviewed medical records of 25 patients with pathologically confirmed DSRCT treated between 2014 and 2023 at Regional Cancer Centre (RCC), Trivandrum. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and prognostic factors by Cox regression analysis.

The median age at diagnosis was 29 years (range 14–53 years), with a male predominance (N= 24, 96%). Vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide plus ifosfamide, and etoposide (VDC + IE) was the first-line regimen in 84% (N= 21) and was associated with the longest median overall survival (OS) of 25 months. Surgery was performed in 36% (N= 9) (all R2 resection) of patients and significantly improved OS (28.0 vs. 13.5 months;p= 0.0093). Median OS for the cohort was 22.0 months, with 1-, 2-, and 3-year survival rates of 63.0%, 44.2%, and 14.7%, respectively. Median progression-free survival (PFS) was 16.0 months after first-line and 5.5 months after second-line therapy. Increasing age was independently associated with poorer survival (hazard ratio [HR] 1.09;p= 0.0165).

Although DSRCT may initially respond to chemotherapy, the benefit diminishes with successive lines, and long-term outcomes remain poor. Surgical resection, when feasible, offers a survival advantage, but there is an urgent need for evidence-based novel therapeutic strategies.

DSRCT is a rare and aggressive sarcoma that predominantly affects adolescents and young adults, with a strong male predisposition. The tumor most often arises from the serosal surfaces of the abdominal cavity and usually presents with widespread intra-abdominal disease at diagnosis [1,2]. Originating from mesenchymal tissue, DSRCT is characterized by multiple peritoneal deposits and bulky masses, which are frequently associated with symptoms such as abdominal pain, distension, fatigue, and weight loss [3,4].

The age-specific incidence of DSRCT is approximately 0.3 cases per million. Notably, racial differences have been reported, with incidence rates of 0.5 per million in Blacks compared with 0.2 per million in Whites, a statistically significant disparity (p= 0.037) [5]. Other population-based analyses have estimated the incidence at around 0.2 per million, further reinforcing its rarity [6,7].

At the molecular level, DSRCT is defined by a characteristic chromosomal translocation, t (11;22) (p13; q12), which results in the EWSR1–WT1 fusion gene. This translocation serves as a diagnostic hallmark of the disease [8]. Because of these molecular features, combined with late and nonspecific symptoms, early diagnosis is often very difficult [9].

Currently, there is no universally accepted standard of care for DSRCT. Treatment is usually multimodal, involving intensive multi-agent chemotherapy, aggressive cytoreductive surgery, and postoperative whole abdominopelvic radiotherapy (WAP-RT) [10,11,12]. Other strategies, such as high-dose chemotherapy with autologous stem cell transplant, hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC), targeted therapies, and maintenance regimens, have also been attempted [13]. These approaches usually provide only temporary disease control, and outcomes remain poor. Reported median overall survival (OS) ranges from 17 to 60 months. 3-year OS rate is between 30 and 40%, while 5-year survival is below 15% [11,14,15]. About 60–70% of patients die within three years of diagnosis, highlighting the poor prognosis. Death most often results from progressive intra-abdominal disease leading to bowel obstruction, cachexia, or multi-organ failure [11].

Despite the availability of comprehensive multimodal therapy, recurrence is prevalent and sustained remissions are infrequent. The lack of standardized treatment protocols and limited access to services in specialized centers exacerbate the situation [12]. However, the data from Western cohorts have provided valuable insights, there is a lack of evidence from the Indian subcontinent, resulting in gaps in the comprehension of regional disease patterns, treatment methodologies, and survival outcomes.

In light of these findings, the current study aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics, treatment strategies, and survival outcomes of DSRCT patients treated over a decade in a tertiary care facility. The study aims to assess the prognostic markers and treatment patterns with the goal of developing more effective and standardized treatments for this rare and aggressive tumor.

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted at the Regional Cancer Centre (RCC), Trivandrum. All patient data were retrieved from the hospital medical records of DSRCT patients from January 2014 to December 2023. Patients who were diagnosed with DSRCT and initiated on treatment at RCC Trivandrum were included. Patients were excluded if their medical records were incomplete, or if they had received prior anti-cancer therapy elsewhere. The treatment response was evaluated based on the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (RECIST 1.1).

The primary outcomes were the response rate to induction chemotherapy and the likelihood of achieving resectability, categorized as R0/R1 versus R2 resections. The secondary outcomes included progression-free survival (PFS) 1 and 2 and OS. OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause. PFS was defined separately for each line of chemotherapy as the interval from initiation of that line until disease progression or death, whichever occurred first. Additional secondary analyses included the correlation of survival outcomes with known prognostic factors namely age, extent of surgical resection, stage of disease, and performance status.

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) of RCC Trivandrum. Informed consent was waived off due to the retrospective nature of the study and confidentiality was maintained throughout the study by de-identifying the patient’s information.

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Survival distributions were estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Prognostic factors were assessed using Cox proportional hazards models. Treatment response was summarized as proportions. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

A total of 25 patients were included in the study, with majority being male (N= 24, 96%). The mean age at diagnosis was 29 ± 9 years (range 14–53 years) and 22 (88%) patients had good performance status (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status [ECOG-PS] 0–1). The abdomen plus pelvis was the primary tumor site in 24 patients (96%), and abdominal pain (N= 17, 68%) was the most common presenting symptom. All patients were confirmed histopathologically. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) most frequently showed desmin positivity (N= 24, 96%) and cytokeratin positivity (N= 17, 68%), with other markers being less common. The diagnostic translocation EWSR1–WT1 was evaluated by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) in 72% (N= 18) of the patients. In cases without FISH confirmation, diagnosis was established through classical histomorphology and confirmatory immunoprofile (desmin dot-like positivity, cytokeratin/EMA co-expression, nuclear WT1 positivity), which reliably distinguishes DSRCT from other small round cell sarcomas and. Absence of RNA-based fusion confirmation in a subset of patients is a limitation. At presentation, all patients had multiple peritoneal masses, and four (16%) showed diffuse peritoneal disease. Imaging further demonstrated the presence of intra-abdominal deposits in 24 (96%) patients. Distant metastases were present in 11 (44%) patients, most commonly in the liver, followed by the spleen and mediastinal lymph nodes. Most patients received vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide plus ifosfamide and etoposide (VDC + IE) as first-line chemotherapy (N= 21, 84%), with a median of 16.5 cycles (IQR 1–17) administered. Surgery was performed in 36% (N= 9) (R0/R1 resection: 0%, R2 resection: 36%), All had diffused peritoneal involvement at baseline and were ineligible for curative cytoreduction. The operative intent was palliative or cytoreductive, primarily for relief of bowel obstruction, bleeding, or abdominal distension. Typical procedures included omentectomy, segmental bowel resection, peritonectomy, and excision of large nodules. None of the patients were referred to an external peritonectomy centre, as formal CRS/HIPEC services were not available at our institution during most of the study period. Intraoperative descriptions corresponded to extensive peritoneal carcinomatosis (estimated PCI > 20) and incomplete cytoreduction (CC-3). No HIPEC was administered and there were no perioperative deaths and Three patients (12%) received radiotherapy – two for palliation (30 Gy delivered in 10 fractions) and one as postoperative consolidation (45 Gy in 25 fractions using 3D CRT). These reflects the predominance of unresectable R2 disease and limited feasibility of WAP-RT during the earlier study years. Adjuvant chemotherapy post definitive surgery was given to only one patient (4%), rest all had disease progression. Liver metastases were present in 10 patients (40%) and were managed non-surgically. None underwent hepatic metastasectomy or local ablative therapy, as disease was multifocal and accompanied by extensive peritoneal spread. Management was systemic, using the same chemotherapy backbone; no patient received liver-directed radiotherapy. The major first-line outcome was progressive disease (PD) (N= 15, 60%), while a partial response (PR) was achieved in 20% (N= 5) and a complete response (CR) in 4% (N= 1). Although nearly half of the patients achieved an early radiologic response to induction VDC/IE, most experienced disease progression by the end of first-line therapy, reflecting the transient chemosensitivity characteristic of DSRCT. For second-line treatment, topotecan plus cyclophosphamide was most commonly used (N= 4, 16%). Only one patient proceeded to third-line chemotherapy (etoposide). No patients received maintenance chemotherapy after first-line treatment. Patients who achieved response or stable disease were monitored with periodic imaging until progression, reflecting the absence of standardized or validated maintenance strategies for DSRCT during the study period. In the univariable analysis, increasing age was associated with significantly shorter OS (P= 0.0086) and shorter PFS-1 (P= 0.0075). By contrast, patient who were eligible to undergo surgical resection as a part of first line treatment was associated with longer OS (28.0 vs. 13.5 months;P= 0.0093) and longer PFS-1 (23.0 vs. 11.5 months;P= 0.0264), with a trend toward longer PFS-2 (7.0 vs. 1.0 months;P= 0.0641). In multivariable Cox regression, increasing age remained independently associated with inferior OS (hazard ratio [HR], 1.09; 95% CI, 1.02–1.16;P= 0.0165) and inferior PFS-1 (HR, 1.08; 95% CI, 1.01–1.15;P= 0.0188). Surgical resection as a part of first line treatment was independently associated with improved OS (HR, 0.20; 95% CI, 0.06–0.67;P= 0.0091) and longer PFS-1 (HR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.10–0.97;P= 0.0435). Given the small sample size and limited number of events, the multivariable Cox regression should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. In this retrospective study, we found that patients with advanced DSRCT achieved transient responses to first-line chemotherapy, but these were not durable and the benefit declined with subsequent treatments. The median PFS was 16.0 months for first-line chemotherapy and reduced to 5.5 months with second-line chemotherapy treatment. The overall response rate (ORR) to first-line therapy was limited, with PR and CR noted in only 24% of patients, whereas 60% experienced disease progression despite treatment. The median OS for the cohort was 22.0 months, demonstrating the aggressive natural history of this disease. The median OS is slightly lower than recent Western series, likely reflects the predominance of unresectable disease, lack of HIPEC/WAP-RT consolidation in most patients, and real-world treatment limitations in a resource-constrained setting.

A multimodal approach that integrates chemotherapy, surgery, and adjunctive methods such as radiotherapy or HIPEC is widely regarded as the optimal management strategy for DSRCT. Several studies have demonstrated a significant improvement in survival in patients undergoing surgical cytoreduction. Our findings are consistent with prior data demonstrating the prognostic value of maximal cytoreduction. In a large prospective series, Hayes Jordan et al. (J Clin Oncol 2018) reported a 3-year overall survival of 71% in patients achieving CC-0/1 cytoreduction combined with HIPEC and postoperative WAP-RT, compared with 26% in those with incomplete resection [16]. Similarly, Subbiah et al. (Eur J Cancer 2018) confirmed improved median survival of 44 months with complete CRS/HIPEC versus 16 months with non-surgical management [17]. These studies underscore that long-term survival in DSRCT is largely dependent on achieving complete macroscopic cytoreduction within a multimodality framework Honoré et al. reported a median OS of 37.7 months in patients treated with surgery, chemotherapy, and whole abdominopelvic radiotherapy [18]. Similarly, Scheer et al. observed a three-year OS of 56.9% in patients achieving remission after complete resection and multimodal therapy [19], while Jeong et al. demonstrated longer OS in patients undergoing surgery with intended curative resection (43.5 vs. 20.1 months;p= 0.027) [20]. Zhang et al. also confirmed complete resection as an independent predictor of survival in their series of 48 patients [21]. Consistent with these findings, surgery in our cohort was associated with significantly prolonged OS (28.0 vs. 13.5 months;p= 0.0093) and PFS (23.0 vs. 11.5 months;p= 0.0264) and remained an independent predictor of survival on multivariable analysis. However, as shown in above studies, complete resection was rarely feasible given the extensive tumor burden at diagnosis [18,19,20].

In patients where complete resection was not possible, systemic chemotherapy remained the mainstay of treatment. Jeong et al. reported median time-to-progression (TTP) of 9.9, 3.5, and 2.5 months after first-, second-, and third-line therapy, respectively, with disease control rates (DCR) exceeding 75% across lines [20]. Wong et al. similarly observed progressively shorter responses with successive regimens, with TTP of 3.9, 2.3, and 1.1 months [22]. Our results are in agreement, with median PFS decreasing from 16.0 months in the first-line setting to 5.5 months in second-line therapy. The VDC + IE regimen was the most commonly used in our cohort and was associated with the longest median OS (25 months), reinforcing its role as the backbone of systemic therapy. The subsequent lines of therapy were individualized based on patient performance status, prior toxicities, drug availability, and evolving institutional practices over the study decade. This heterogeneity reflects real-world clinical decision-making in this ultra-rare tumor. Despite the lack of uniformity, our data still provide comparative outcome signals—for example, four patients treated with topotecan plus cyclophosphamide achieved an OS of 48 months and a PFS of 41 months—suggesting that selected second-line regimens may offer durable benefit in a small subset, potentially guiding future prospective evaluation of these regimens. The favorable outcomes observed with topotecan–cyclophosphamide in our series are consistent with prior evidence supporting the activity of topoisomerase inhibitors in DSRCT. TheEWSR1–WT1fusion transcript shares transcriptional dysregulation features withEWSR1–FLI1in Ewing sarcoma, conferring relative susceptibility to topoisomerase I/II inhibition. This biological rationale underlies the clinical use of irinotecan–temozolomide, which achieved meaningful disease control in the pilot trial by Magnan et al. [23]. Our observation mirrors findings in relapsed Ewing sarcoma, where topotecan–cyclophosphamide produced higher response rates than other second-line regimens. High-dose ifosfamide remains the best-performing salvage regimen in Ewing sarcoma, but was not routinely used in our cohort owing to temporal patient selection. While these results highlight the potential utility of topoisomerase-based combinations in refractory DSRCT, extrapolation from Ewing sarcoma data should be made cautiously pending disease-specific prospective trials. The lower 3-year survival in our cohort likely reflects the predominance of unresectable disease, limited access to HIPEC/WAP-RT, delayed referrals, and socioeconomic barriers affecting treatment continuity. Nonetheless, consistent with the above series, the majority of patients experienced progression despite therapy, underscoring the limited efficacy of chemotherapy alone [20,22].

Prognostic markers in DSRCT remain poorly defined. Our study also identified age as an important prognostic factor, with each additional year associated with a 9% higher risk of death (HR 1.09; 95% CI, 1.02–1.16;p= 0.0165) and an 8% higher risk for progression (HR 1.08; 95% CI, 1.01–1.15;p= 0.0188). This finding complements prior research describing other adverse features. A study including 14 DSRCT patients demonstrated that patients with fewer metastatic sites had better OS (27.0 vs. 14.7 months;p= 0.024) [20], while another study reported that the absence of thrombosis and normal C-reactive protein (CRP) were favorable prognostic markers [19]. Campos et al. confirmed the overall poor long-term survival of this disease, with a median OS of 27 months and a five-year OS of only 12% despite multimodal therapy [24]. Population-based SEER data reinforce the demographic profile, showing 192 reported cases with an age-adjusted incidence of 0.3 per million, a peak in the 20–24-year age group, and a clear male predominance (0.4 vs. 0.1 per million;p< 0.001) [5]. Taken together, these findings indicate that host factors such as age, baseline disease burden are critical determinants of outcome in DSRCT.

Given these findings, survival in advanced DSRCT remains limited, with the majority of previously cited studies, including ours, indicating a median OS of 20–30 months. This points out the importance of more novel treatment strategies. A pilot study using the combination of irinotecan, temozolomide, and bevacizumab demonstrated a three-year OS rate of 61%, implying a possible advantage of integrating anti-angiogenic therapy [23]. Pazopanib, an oral VEGF receptor antagonist, has shown disease control in refractory cases, with a median PFS of 9.2 months and a DCR of 78% [25]. Immunotherapy has thus far produced limited results, indicating the low immunogenicity of DSRCT. However, various innovative strategies such as anti-B7-H3 radioimmunotherapy, antibody-drug conjugates (ADC), and CAR-T cell therapy are currently undergoing clinical evaluation [26,27,28]. Emerging data presented at the 2025 ASCO Annual Meeting demonstrated promising antitumor activity of the antibody–drug conjugate trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) in refractory DSRCT, with responses observed irrespective of HER2 expression. These results highlight the potential of ADCs as a treatment platform in this disease and warrant dedicated clinical evaluation of payload-based therapies and radioconjugates in future trials [29]. Further clinical trials will be crucial to validate these techniques and enhance long-term results in this rare and aggressive sarcoma.

This study’s main strength lies in its thorough characterization of DSRCT patients in a real-world context, including a rigorous evaluation of treatment results across various therapeutic lines. The identification of age as an independent predictive variable, along with the surgical resectability, offers therapeutically significant insights that enhance the evidence-based data on this rare sarcoma.

The retrospective design, small sample size, and heterogeneity of treatment approaches beyond first-line chemotherapy limit the study. Being a single-center analysis, the findings may not be fully generalizable to broader populations.

Although DSRCT may initially respond to chemotherapy, the durability of the response declines with subsequent lines, and OS remains poor. Surgical resection, when feasible, continues to offer a survival advantage, but the rarity and aggressive biology of this tumor demand collaborative efforts and the development of novel systemic therapies. In resource-limited settings where HIPEC or WAP-RT are not routinely available, treatment should prioritize dose-intense VDC + IE–based induction chemotherapy and maximal feasible cytoreductive surgery. Selected patients may benefit from low-cost, oral metronomic regimens or repurposed targeted agents such as pazopanib for palliation. Early multidisciplinary evaluation and referral to regional sarcoma networks can optimize outcomes even in the absence of high-resource modalities.
