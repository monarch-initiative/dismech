BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVE: The impact of germline pathogenic variants (PVs) in
cancer predisposition genes on risk of prostate cancer (PCa) remains
understudied in large populations of African ancestry. This study aims to
characterize the range of genetic risk of PCa and aggressive disease phenotypes
in men of African ancestry.
METHODS: We analyzed 7176 PCa cases and 4873 controls from seven countries
across North America and Africa to assess the association between PVs in 37
cancer predisposition genes and the risk of overall, aggressive, and metastatic
PCa. Genes significantly associated with PCa risk were used to estimate lifetime
absolute risk based on family history, polygenic risk score (PRS), and PV
carrier status.
KEY FINDINGS AND LIMITATIONS: PVs in ATM, BRCA2, CHEK2, HOXB13, and PALB2 were
presented in 4% of aggressive/metastatic PCa cases and were significantly
associated with an increased risk of aggressive PCa (odds ratio 2.18-5.96,
p < 0.05). Lifetime absolute risk varied widely depending on PV carrier status,
PRS, and family history, ranging from 3.0% to 74% for overall PCa, 0.6% to 41%
for aggressive PCa, and 0.2% to 37% for metastatic PCa. PV carriers with a
positive family history and a PRS in the 90th percentile had seven, 18, and 34
times the risks of overall, aggressive, and metastatic PCa, respectively,
compared with average-risk individuals. Oversampling of aggressive cases may
limit the generalizability of these findings to screening populations.
CONCLUSIONS AND CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS: Integration of PV status, PRS, and family
history enables more refined PCa risk estimates. The wide range of PCa risk
observed among men of African ancestry in our study supports future prospective
studies in the development of risk-stratified cancer screening programs to
identify high-risk individuals who may benefit from screening at an earlier age.

Prostate cancer (PCa) is highly heritable, with genetic factors estimated to account for 58% of variability in risk [1]. A multiancestry polygenic risk score (PRS) of 451 common variants has been shown to effectively stratify PCa risk across populations and differentiate between the risks of aggressive and nonaggressive disease in men of African ancestry [2–5]. Rare germline pathogenic variants (PVs) within DNA repair and cancer predisposition genes are also associated with an increased risk of PCa, as well as early age onset [6,7], aggressive disease [8–13], and overall survival [14]. BRCA2, HOXB13, and ATM are the most consistently reported genes [6,8–12,15–21], with evidence also reported for BRCA1 [8,11], CHEK2 [11,20], NBN [22], PALB2 [10,19], and TP53 [23]. Studies have shown a more than ten-fold gradient in PCa risk due to the wide variability of PRSs among carriers of PVs in cancer predisposition genes, indicating that assessment of both rare germline PVs and PRSs of common variants will improve PCa risk prediction [24,25]. The prevalence of PVs and associated risks for overall PCa, aggressive, and metastatic disease have been examined extensively in European ancestry populations, while their impact in men of African ancestry is not as well defined [10,12,13].

Defining the spectrum of genetic risk is important for risk stratification and to inform risk-based screening strategies. This is particularly important for men of African ancestry, given the higher PCa incidence and mortality in this ancestry group [26]. Current recommendations for when to start prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing are based on one’s risk determined by family history, germline mutation status, or race, with Black/African American men recommended to begin PSA testing 5 yr earlier than men from other racial and ethnic groups [27]. In this study, we aimed to characterize the range of genetic risk of PCa and aggressive disease phenotypes in >12 000 men of African ancestry from North America and Africa. We (1) evaluated the impact of rare germline PVs in DNA repair and cancer predisposition genes on the risk of PCa and aggressive disease, and (2) estimated the lifetime absolute risks across percentiles of PRS for PV carriers and noncarriers, and men with and without a family history of PCa.

This analysis consists of 7176 PCa cases and 4873 controls of African ancestry from the African Ancestry Prostate Cancer Consortium and the Men of African Descent and Carcinoma of the Prostate Network (Supplementary Table 1) [4,28]. PCa cases were selected irrespective of family history of PCa, age at diagnosis, or PV carrier status. Controls were individuals without a diagnosis of PCa. In most studies, controls were selected without knowledge of their PSA level and were frequency matched to cases by age. In certain studies, controls were sampled from PCa screening programs (Supplementary Table 1). While tumor stage (mostly pathological) and grade were available for the majority of cases from the USA, most cases from African countries had Gleason score and/or PSA level measured at the time of diagnosis. Aggressive cases were defined as men meeting at least one of the following criteria: tumor stage T3/T4, metastasis (M1), a Gleason score of ≥8, or death from PCa. Nonaggressive cases were defined as men diagnosed with a stage T1/T2 tumor and a Gleason score of 6, while intermediate-risk cases were defined as men diagnosed with a stage T1/T2 tumor and a Gleason score of 7. PSA at diagnosis was used to define nonaggressive (<10 ng/ml), intermediate-risk (10–20 ng/ml), aggressive (>20 ng/ml), or metastatic (>100 ng/ml) cases if other clinical information was unavailable. From each study, aggressive and metastatic cases were oversampled to improve power in analyses of aggressive disease. Informed consent was obtained from all study participants, and study protocols were approved by the respective institutional review boards.

Whole-exome sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) using the Twist Human Core Exome plus CIDR Custom panel (TwistBioscience), which covers 36.5 Mb of human protein-coding regions. Genetic variants were identified using the Genome Analysis Toolkit. High-quality sequence data (read depth of >20×) were obtained for 93.8% of target exon regions with a mean coverage of 73×. Germline PVs were defined as rare variants (minor allele frequency [MAF] <1% in controls) that had either (1) a variant effect predictor impact score of “high” [29], representing loss-of-function variants with protein-truncating or splice-altering consequences, or (2) a pathogenic or likely pathogenic ClinVar classification [30], representing known PVs. We excluded known PCa low-risk variants c.9976A>T (rs11571833) in BRCA2 and c.1100del (rs555607708) in CHEK2 from the primary analysis. We estimated genetic similarity to individuals in the 1000 Genomes African panel using 81 003 common (MAF ≥0.01) and independent (r2 < 0.1) variants from an unsupervised (K = 2) ADMIXTURE analysis [31], which included the 1000 Genomes Project phase 3 European and African individuals [32] as reference samples. The same set of variants was used to perform a principal component analysis.

Our analysis focused on 37 DNA repair and cancer predisposition genes, including 16 genes commonly included in clinical gene testing panels for PCa and 21 genes with previous association evidence with overall or aggressive PCa in men of European ancestry [13,33]. For overall, aggressive, metastatic, intermediate-risk, and nonaggressive PCa, gene-based associations were assessed by comparing PV carrier frequencies by disease status using logistic regression models, adjusting for age and the top ten principal components (to account for differences in genetic ancestry) in case-control and case-case analyses. Association tests were performed in the overall study and stratified by country, limited to genes with at least three carriers in each comparison group. Since each of these genes has prior biological or statistical evidence for PCa, we considered a p value of <0.05 to be statistically significant.

We defined a set of “prioritized genes” that included genes with a significant risk-increasing (odds ratio [OR] >1) association in analyses of aggressive or metastatic cases versus controls. We conducted gene-set analyses to estimate the association of PVs aggregated across the prioritized genes, the panel genes (n = 16), and a subset of panel genes not included among the prioritized genes.

We estimated the lifetime absolute risk of PCa (by age 85 yr) by fitting multivariable logistic regression models with overall, aggressive, or metastatic PCa as the outcome and age (in years), first-degree family history of PCa (negative and positive), carrier status of PVs in prioritized genes (no and yes), PRSs (continuous), and the top ten principal components of ancestry as the joint predictor variables. The PRS was constructed by summing weighted allelic dosages from 451 PCa risk variants using the weights from the most recent multiancestry genome-wide association study [2]. As described previously [3,5,25], the estimates of risk from this model were combined with age- and population-specific (ie, non-Hispanic Black) incidence rates for overall PCa and metastatic disease from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results program and mortality rates from the National Center for Health Statistics to estimate lifetime absolute risk. The incidence rates used to estimate the lifetime absolute risk of aggressive PCa were approximately 17% of the incidence rates of overall PCa [34]. Absolute risk confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using 10 000 Monte Carlo samples. This analysis was conducted in 4567 cases and 3137 controls with genome-wide array data from the USA, with the data from African countries excluded, given the limited available information on family history, age-specific PCa incidence, and mortality.

The 7176 PCa cases and 4873 controls in our study were recruited from the USA (66%), Uganda (12%), Ghana (10%), South Africa (6.4%), Nigeria (1.9%), Senegal (1.5%), and Guadeloupe (2.5%; Supplementary Fig. 1). On average, participants from African countries were genetically more similar to individuals in the 1000 Genomes African panels (Ghana 99%, Nigeria 99%, Senegal 92%, South Africa 99%, and Uganda 95%) than those from the USA (76%) and Guadeloupe (78%). Of the 7176 cases, 1074 had metastatic disease (15%), 1793 had a Gleason score of ≥8 (25%), and 1124 had stage T3 or T4 disease (16%), with 3283 (46%) classified as aggressive, 1752 (24%) as nonaggressive, and 2141 (30%) as intermediate-risk cases (Table 1).

Of the 37 genes examined, a significant association with the risk of overall PCa (cases vs controls) was observed for BRCA2 (OR = 1.86, 95% CI = 1.20–2.90, p = 0.006), CHEK2 (OR = 3.20, 95% CI = 1.09–9.43, p = 0.03), HOXB13 (OR = 2.32, 95% CI = 1.36–3.94, p = 0.002), and PALB2 (OR = 3.46, 95% CI = 1.18–10.1, p = 0.02; Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2). Five genes were significantly associated with an increased risk of aggressive PCa (vs controls): ATM (OR = 2.18, 95% CI = 1.23–3.88, p = 0.008), BRCA2 (OR = 2.66, 95% CI = 1.64–4.31, p < 0.001), CHEK2 (OR = 3.50, 95% CI = 1.05–11.7, p = 0.04), HOXB13 (OR = 3.16, 95% CI = 1.76–5.68, p < 0.001), and PALB2 (OR = 5.96, 95% CI = 1.94–18.3, p = 0.002). A suggestive association was also found for MSH5 (OR = 1.79, 95% CI = 0.99–3.24, p = 0.05). For all genes significantly associated with aggressive PCa, their associations with metastatic PCa (vs controls) were similar or slightly greater in magnitude (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2), while positive nonsignificant associations (OR >1.5) were observed for nonaggressive and intermediate PCa (vs controls) for CHEK2, HOXB13, and PALB2. Of the 37 genes, the differences in risk of aggressive versus nonaggressive PCa in case-case analyses were significant for ATM (OR = 2.64, 95% CI = 1.15–6.04, p = 0.02) and BRCA2 (OR = 2.39, 95%CI = 1.16–4.91, p = 0.02), and was suggestive for HOXB13 (OR = 1.94, 95% CI = 0.95–3.99, p = 0.07) and PALB2 (OR = 2.99, 95% CI = 0.83–10.7, p = 0.09; Supplementary Table 2).

Additional genes with PV frequencies that were at least two-fold greater among overall, aggressive, or metastatic cases than controls included EPCAM (0.06% vs 0.02%), FAM175A (0.28% vs 0.12%), MLH1 (0.06% vs 0%), MUTYH (3.0% vs 1.3%), NBN (0.24% vs 0.12%), NEIL2 (0.28% vs 0.14%), and TP53 (0.28% vs 0.06%; Supplementary Table 2). Of these genes, evidence for significant or suggestive risk-increasing associations or two-fold or greater PV frequencies in aggressive or metastatic PCa was reported for NBN, TP53, and NEIL2 in our previous analysis in >17 000 men of European ancestry [13].

Five genes were selected into the prioritized gene set given their significant risk-increasing associations with aggressive or metastatic PCa in case-control analyses: ATM, BRCA2, CHEK2, HOXB13, and PALB2 (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). In gene-set analyses, PVs in these five genes were found in 1.5% of controls and 2.1% of nonaggressive, 2.3% of intermediate-risk, 4.0% of aggressive, and 3.9% of metastatic cases (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 5). Compared with controls, PV carriers had a nonsignificant 1.3-fold risk of nonaggressive PCa (95% CI = 0.85–1.93, p = 0.25), a 1.5-fold risk of intermediate-risk PCa (95% CI = 1.04–2.16, p = 0.03), a 2.9-fold risk of aggressive PCa (95% CI = 2.19–3.95, p < 0.001), and a 3.3-fold risk of metastatic PCa (95% CI = 2.11–5.01, p < 0.001), with significantly increased risks observed for aggressive (OR = 2.26, 95% CI = 1.52–3.35, p < 0.001) and metastatic PCa (OR = 2.36, 95% CI = 1.28–4.35, p = 0.006) disease relative to nonaggressive disease.

The associations with aggressive or metastatic PCa (vs controls) for PVs aggregated across the 16 genes commonly included in gene panels were weaker than those observed for the prioritized genes (OR 1.69 vs 2.94–3.25) and were null upon excluding prioritized genes from the 16 panel genes (n = 11; OR from 0.89 to 0.94; Supplementary Table 5).

Using external incidence and mortality data and the association estimates from multivariable models, including family history, PRS, and carrier status of PVs in the five prioritized genes (Supplementary Table 6), we estimated the lifetime absolute risk of overall, aggressive, and metastatic PCa in African Americans. The estimated lifetime absolute risks of aggressive and metastatic PCa (by age 85 yr) were greater for PV carriers than for noncarriers, particularly for those with a higher PRS and with a family history of PCa (Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2). For noncarriers, the lifetime absolute risk of aggressive PCa increased from 0.6% in the 10th percentile of PRS to 8.3% in the 90th percentile for men without a family history of PCa, and from 1.4% to 18%, respectively, for men with a positive family history. For carriers in the 10th and 90th PRS percentiles, the estimated lifetime absolute risks of aggressive PCa were 1.6% and 21%, respectively, for men without a family history, and increased to 3.7% and 41%, respectively, for men with a positive family history. A similar trend was observed for metastatic PCa, where the lifetime risks were tripled in carriers of PVs compared with noncarriers, especially among men with a higher PRS (90th PRS percentile: 20% vs 6.0% for men without a family history and 37% vs 12% for men with a positive family history; Table 2).

The lifetime risks for noncarriers with a positive family history of PCa in the 50th PRS percentile were ~20% for overall PCa, ~5% for aggressive PCa, and ~2% for metastatic PCa (Table 2). We considered these as “risk thresholds” that reflect current screening guidelines for Black/African American men and men with a strong family history. Among men with a positive family history, 51% of noncarriers (>49th PRS percentile) and 82% of carriers (>18th PRS percentile) had a lifetime risk of aggressive PCa that exceeded the risk threshold (Table 3). Even for men without a family history, the lifetime risk of aggressive PCa exceeded the risk threshold for 26% of noncarriers and 56% of carriers. PV carriers with a positive PCa family history in the 90th percentile of PRS reached the risk threshold for aggressive PCa at the age of 56 yr, which is at least 29 yr earlier than men with average risk in the population (noncarriers without a family history of PCa in the 50th percentile of PRS; >85 yr of age; Table 4). The lifetime risk of metastatic PCa exceeded the risk threshold for 36% of noncarriers and 66% of carriers without a family history of PCa, and for 54% of noncarriers and 83% of carriers with a positive family history (Table 3). PV carriers with a positive family history of PCa in the 90th percentile of PRS reached the threshold risk of metastatic PCa at the age of 55 yr, which is 30 yr earlier than men with average risk in the population (>85 yr of age; Table 4).

Findings from this large study in men of African ancestry provide robust evidence that rare germline PVs in ATM, BRCA2, CHEK2, HOXB13, and PALB2 are significant risk factors for overall, aggressive, and/or metastatic PCa. We showed that PVs in these genes, in combination with PRS and family history, can markedly differentiate the risks of overall, aggressive, and metastatic PCa. Importantly, the ability to assess an individual’s risk of developing aggressive disease more accurately, particularly in African ancestry populations, may inform screening guidelines that prioritize personalized risk over race-based recommendations.

Studying rare PVs remains challenging, particularly in non-European ancestry populations, due to limited sample sizes and reduced statistical power. As the largest study of PVs in men of African ancestry to date, our study provides compelling statistical evidence for associations between PVs in ATM, BRCA2, CHEK2, HOXB13, and PALB2 and the risk of overall and/or aggressive PCa, findings that align with those of prior studies in both European and African ancestry populations [8,9,12,13]. We also observed suggestive evidence for MSH5 and notable frequency differences (more than two-fold higher PV frequencies in aggressive or metastatic cases than in controls) for NBN, NEIL2, and TP53, despite their extreme rarity (fewer than ten carriers in controls) in our study population of African ancestry men. These genes have similarly been implicated in European ancestry studies [13], reinforcing their potential as candidate genes for aggressive PCa.

Current National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines recommend initiating PCa screening at the age of 45 yr for men at an average risk and 40 yr for men at a high risk, including (1) Black/African American men, (2) men with a concerning family or personal history (eg, multiple affected relatives and/or family history of early-onset cancers), or (3) carriers of germline mutations in PCa susceptibility genes [27,35]. Our findings reveal substantial gradients in the lifetime risk of PCa and aggressive disease among African American men based on PV carrier status, PRS, and family history, ranging from 3.0% to 74% for overall PCa, 0.6% to 41% for aggressive PCa, and 0.2% to 37% for metastatic disease. Notably, some individuals classified as having a high risk by current guidelines (ie, PV carriers with a positive family history) had lifetime risks below the risk thresholds if their PRS was low, while others classified as having an average risk (ie, noncarriers without a family history) had risks above thresholds if their PRS was high. This suggests the potential for misclassification in PCa risk when PRS is not considered. Furthermore, men in the extremely high-risk group (ie, carriers with a positive family history in the 90th PRS percentile) reached the PCa risk thresholds in their 50s, approximately 30 yr earlier than those in the average-risk group (ie, noncarriers without a family history in the 50th PRS percentile).

These findings underscore that not all men of African ancestry have an elevated PCa risk and that incorporation of comprehensive genetic information reveals a wide spectrum of risks. Our results support the integration of PRS and germline PVs with a family history into risk prediction models to enable more personalized PCa risk estimates. In the context of PSA screening, shared decision-making between patients and physicians should be informed by individualized risk profiles rather than race alone. This study adds to the growing evidence in favor of risk-tailored cancer screening strategies, advocating for earlier and more frequent screening for high-risk individuals, and delayed and less frequent screening for those at a lower risk, to improve early detection of aggressive PCa while minimizing overdiagnosis [24,36]. The effectiveness of PRS-based screening has recently been demonstrated in the BARCODE 1 study, which showed improved identification of clinically significant PCa at younger ages [37,38]. Future research, such as the TRANSFORM trial, is needed to evaluate the clinical utility of integrating PRSs and PVs into genetic risk assessment frameworks for both primary and secondary prevention [37,39].

In this study, we conducted case-control analyses to estimate effect sizes for the association of PVs in specific genes and gene sets, stratified by different PCa phenotype groups compared with controls. While the strongest associations were observed for metastatic and aggressive PCa patients (vs controls), positive nonsignificant associations (OR >1.5) were also observed for nonaggressive and intermediate-risk groups (vs controls) for genes CHEK2, HOXB13, and PALB2. The prioritized gene set was also associated with a 1.5-fold risk of intermediate-risk PCa (p = 0.03), which suggests that PVs in certain genes may contribute to the development of less aggressive forms of PCa. These stratified effect size estimates from the case-control analyses also enabled modeling of the absolute risk for different PCa phenotypes. Complementary case-case analyses were performed to specifically test whether PV frequencies differed significantly between PCa severity groups, which helped establish the over-representation of PVs in the five prioritized genes in more aggressive compared with less aggressive cases.

Our study has a number of strengths and limitations. This study represents the largest effort to date to characterize PVs in high-risk genes for PCa among men of African ancestry, with ~34% of the study population from Western, Southern, and Eastern Africa, and the Caribbean. Clinical information about grade, stage, and/or PSA at diagnosis was available for most cases, and oversampling of aggressive or metastatic cases was performed to improve the statistical power of testing rare PVs for PCa subgroups. While the oversampling was unlikely to bias the association of PVs with aggressive or metastatic PCa, it may inflate the frequencies of PVs and the association estimates for overall PCa for genes associated with aggressive disease. For a subset of studies missing stage and Gleason score, mainly those from African countries, PSA was used to define PCa subgroups, which could lead to misclassification and biased associations toward the null. However, the PV frequencies observed in the non-US countries were generally consistent with the trend observed in the USA (ie, higher PV frequencies among more aggressive cases), suggesting that any impact of misclassification was not substantial. Despite the large sample size of this study, the statistical power was still limited for many genes with rare PVs (eg, TP53). For the non-US countries, the data were too scarce to generate stable estimates of the association and to evaluate potential effect heterogeneity across countries. Lastly, the lack of reliable incidence and mortality data from African countries prevented us from estimating the absolute risk of PCa in these countries, limiting the generalizability of our findings.

Findings from this large study provide strong evidence to prioritize ATM, BRCA2, HOXB13, CHEK2, and PALB2 as candidate genes impacting the risk of aggressive and/or metastatic PCa in men of African ancestry. Our findings also support the incorporation of PRSs, PVs, and family history of PCa in risk prediction models to improve risk stratification. The identification, early detection, and treatment of men at a high risk of aggressive PCa may lower PCa mortality, while patients at the lowest risk may be spared from the overdiagnosis and overtreatment of indolent disease. A screening program that includes genetic information needs to be evaluated in large multiancestry clinical studies.
