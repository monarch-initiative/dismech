# Clinical course, genetic etiology, and visual outcome in cone and cone-rod dystrophy.
**Authors:** Thiadens AA, Phan TM, Zekveld-Vroon RC, Leroy BP, van den Born LI, Hoyng CB, Klaver CC, Writing Committee for the Cone Disorders Study Group Consortium, Roosing S, Pott JW, van Schooneveld MJ, van Moll-Ramirez N, van Genderen MM, Boon CJ, den Hollander AI, Bergen AA, De Baere E, Cremers FP, Lotery AJ
**Journal:** Ophthalmology (2012)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ophtha.2011.10.011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ophtha.2011.10.011)

## Content

OBJECTIVE: To evaluate the clinical course, genetic etiology, and visual prognosis in patients with cone dystrophy (CD) and cone-rod dystrophy (CRD).

DESIGN: Clinic-based, longitudinal, multicenter study.

PARTICIPANTS: Consecutive probands with CD (N = 98), CRD (N = 83), and affected relatives (N = 41 and N = 17, respectively) from various ophthalmogenetic clinics in The Netherlands, Belgium, and the United Kingdom.

METHODS: Data on best-corrected Snellen visual acuity, color vision, ophthalmoscopy, fundus photography, Goldmann perimetry, and full-field standard electroretinogram (ERG) from all patients were registered from medical charts over a mean follow-up of 19 years. The ABCA4, CNGB3, KCNV2, PDE6C, and RPGR genes were analyzed by direct sequencing in autosomal recessive (AR) and X-linked (XL), respectively. Genotyping was not undertaken for autosomal-dominant cases.

MAIN OUTCOME MEASURES: The 10-year progression of all clinical parameters and cumulative lifetime risk of low vision and legal blindness were assessed.

RESULTS: The mean age onset for CD was 16 years (standard deviation, 11), and of CRD 12 years (standard deviation, 11; P = 0.02). The pattern of inheritance was AR in 92% of CD and 90% of CRD. Ten years after diagnosis, 35% of CD and 51% of CRD had a bull's eye maculopathy; 70% of CRD showed absolute peripheral visual field defects and 37% of CD developed rod involvement on ERG. The mean age of legal blindness was 48 (standard error [SE], 3.1) years in CD, and 35 (SE, 1.1; P<0.001) years in CRD. ABCA4 mutations were found in 8 of 90 (9%) of AR-CD, and in 17 of 65 (26%) of AR-CRD. Other mutations were detected in CNGB3 (3/90; 3%), KCNV2 (4/90; 4%), and in PDE6C (1/90; 1%). The RPGR gene was mutated in the 2 XL-CD and in 4 of 5 (80%) of XL-CRD. ABCA4 mutations as well as age of onset <20 years were significantly associated with a faster progression to legal blindness (P<0.001).

CONCLUSIONS: Although CD had a slightly more favorable clinical course than CRD, both disorders progressed to legal blindness in the majority of patients. Mutations in the ABCA4 gene and early onset of disease were independent prognostic parameters for visual loss. Our data may serve as an aid in counseling patients with progressive cone disorders.

Cone dystrophy (CD) and cone–rod dystrophy (CRD) are
the most common hereditary cone disorders with a fre-
quency of 1:30 000 to 40 000 worldwide.
1–3 Both diseases
are characterized by loss of cone photoreceptors and pro-
gressive visual decline, but CD and CRD are also distinct
disease entities with considerable variability in genetic
causes and clinical consequences.
Patients with CD present with poor visual acuity and
severe color vision disturbances. The macular appearance
ranges from normal to a bull’s eye maculopathy, and the
optic disc may show a variable degree of temporal pallor.
The visual ﬁeld generally shows a central scotoma. Cone
amplitude responses, as registered by full-ﬁeld electroreti-
nogram (ERG), deteriorate progressively, whereas rod re-
sponses are initially normal. All modes of Mendelian inher-
itance have been reported, but the autosomal-recessive (AR)
form is by far the most common. The genetic causes of the
autosomal-dominant
4–6 and X-linked (XL) 7,8 inheritance

have been elucidated to a large extent, but the genetic causes
of the AR form are mostly unknown. 9 –13
Cone–rod dystrophy can be distinguished from CD by
the early involvement of rod photoreceptors. Symptoms
resemble those of CD, but patients may also experience
nyctalopia owing to rod dysfunction. The macular appear-
ance resembles that of CD, but patients may also develop
retinal vascular attenuation and peripheral pigment deposits.
The visual ﬁeld shows a central scotoma with a variable
degree of peripheral involvement. The ERG shows reduced
cone and rod responses, with cone responses more severely
affected. As in CD, all modes of inheritance are present, and
currently, 17 genes and some additional loci have been
reported.
3,14 ABCA4 seems to be the most prominent causal
gene; however, the etiologic fraction for this gene varies
widely between studies (24%– 65%).
15–21 Hence, a large pro-
portion of AR-CRD patients still has an unknown etiology.
Patient management and counseling are currently challeng-
ing for clinicians who encounter CD and CRD patients. Age of
onset, decline of visual acuity and visual ﬁelds, progression to
legal blindness, genetic etiology, and genotype–phenotype
correlations have not been studied thoroughly. Currently
available literature on cone disorders only describes the
phenotypes of CD and CRD in relation to their genetic
defect.
1–3 Therefore, how the majority of CD and CRD
progress, at what age patients will not be able to drive or
read anymore, and at what age legal blindness occurs are
questions that remain largely unanswered.
This study evaluates clinical parameters as a function of
age in a large series of CD and CRD patients, and studies
the relative contribution of known causal genes (i.e., the
ABCA4, CNGB3, KCNV2, PDE6C, and RPGR genes). Pro-
bands and relatives were ascertained from multiple ophthal-
mogenetic clinics, were included consecutively without
knowledge of genetic defect, and were diagnosed according
to standardized protocols. Disease parameters were system-
atically evaluated from medical charts, and complemented
by clinical and genetic workups. This enabled us to study
the causes and consequences of both CD and CRD.
Patients and Methods
Study Population
All patients were ascertained from eight ophthalmogenetic centers
located in The Netherlands, Belgium, and the United Kingdom.
Most patients (/H1101170%) were selected from ERG databases using
the terms “reduced” or “absent” cone amplitude responses. The
remaining patients (/H1101130%) were ascertained as consecutive pa-
tients who visited one of the clinics during the study period
(2006 –2009). In total, we included 98 CD patients with 41 af-
fected relatives, and 83 CRD patients with 17 affected relatives.
We retrieved all clinical data from medical charts, and 2 clinical
graders (A.A.H.J.T. and C.C.W.K.) made a ﬁnal diagnosis based
on a standard protocol. Discrepancies were discussed and solved
by consensus. Inclusion criteria for CD were progressive decline of
visual acuity, color vision disturbances, and reduced cone ampli-
tude responses on ERG, with normal rod responses for /H113505 years.
Inclusion criteria for CRD were a progressive decline of visual
acuity, color vision disturbances, and reduction of both cone and
rod responses on ERG, with cones equally or more severely
reduced than rods. Exclusion criteria were congenital nystagmus,
typical fundus ﬂavimaculatus, and syndromic disorders. We sub-
classiﬁed CRD into central visual ﬁeld involvement (cfCRD), and
central as well as peripheral visual ﬁeld defects at diagnosis
(cpfCRD).
The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of
Erasmus Medical Center and adhered to the tenets of the Decla-
ration of Helsinki. All participants provided signed, informed
consent for participation in the study, retrieval of medical records,
and use of blood and DNA for research.
Clinical Examination
We collected all ophthalmologic data that had been registered
during patients’ lifetime. These data included Snellen visual acu-
ity, color vision (Hardy–Rand–Rittler color vision test or Lanthony
Panel D-15 tests), slit-lamp and fundus examinations, perimetry,
and ERGs. Patients with incomplete or unavailable data from the
past 5 years were invited for an additional ophthalmologic exam-
ination, color vision and visual ﬁeld testing, and ERG. Color vision
defects were classiﬁed as “mild” or “severe” in accordance with
the criteria provided in the manuals. Visual ﬁeld testing was
executed with Goldmann perimetry; depth of scotomata was
scored as relative or absolute; location of scotomata was scored as
central or peripheral. The ERGs were in accordance with the
recommendations of the International Society for Clinical Electro-
physiology of Vision.
22 We used standard United States criteria to
deﬁne low vision (best-corrected visual acuity /H110210.50 [20/40] in
the better eye) and legal blindness (best-corrected visual acuity
/H113490.10 [20/200] in the better eye).
Molecular Genetic Analysis
Blood samples were obtained from probands, affected relatives,
and parents. DNA was isolated from peripheral blood lymphocytes
by standard procedures. For all patients with AR-CD, the coding
region and intron/exon boundaries of the CNGB3, KCNV2, and
PDE6C genes were ampliﬁed by polymerase chain reaction, fol-
lowed by direct sequencing. X-linked–C(R)D probands were
screened for mutations in the RPGR gene. Segregation analysis
was performed when mutations were detected in autosomal genes
or 1 mutation was found in an XL gene. ABCA4 gene mutations
were analyzed in AR-C(R)D patients with microarray screening
performed by Asper Biotech (Tartu, Estonia) according to previ-
ously described methods.
23 When only 1 ABCA4 allele was af -
fected, the pathogenicity of mutations was calculated with various
online prediction programs including SIFT, Polyphen, Grantham
score, SMART, and BLOSUM62.
24 When only 1 pathologic af -
fected allele was found, denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis
was performed to detect a second disease allele. Novel variants
were screened in 100 ethnically matched control subjects.
Statistical Analysis
Frequency differences between CD and CRD patients were com-
pared using the Student t test for continuous variables and the
Pearson chi-square test for categorical data. Cumulative risks of
visual loss were studied with Kaplan–Meier product-limit survival
analysis, with low vision or legal blindness as outcomes. Analyses
were stratiﬁed for the diagnosis CD or CRD, and for mode of
inheritance (XL, AR, or autosomal dominant). Cumulative risks
were compared between groups using the log-rank test. Cox re-
gression survival analyses were performed for early onset ( /H1102120
years) and presence of ABCA4 mutations with low vision and legal

Results
Clinical Characteristics and Visual Outcomes
The average duration of follow-up was 19 years (standard devia-
tion, 15). The most common mode of inheritance was presumably
AR in both disease groups (92% and 90%). The ﬁrst symptom was
visual loss in 88% (123/139) of CD and in 85% (85/100) of CRD
cases. Other symptoms at onset were color vision disturbances
(CD, 7/139; CRD, 8/100), nyctalopia (CRD, 2/100), photophobia
(CD, 7/139; CRD, 5/100), or hemeralopia (CD, 2/139). Baseline
characteristics of all probands are summarized in Table 1. The
mean age of disease onset was signiﬁcantly earlier in CRD (12 vs
16 years; P /H110050.02). High myopia (spherical equivalent /H11349/H110026
diopters) was observed in 17% (17/98) of CD, and in 17% (14/83)
of CRD patients (P /H110050.53); all XL patients had high myopia.
High refractive errors occurred slightly less often in patients with
ABCA4 mutations; however, differences were not statistically sig-
niﬁcant (high myopia 3/26 [12%]; P /H110050.30).

Figure 2 shows the cumulative risk of visual decline as a function
of age for ABCA4 mutations versus other genetic causes. In
AR-CD probands with ABCA4, low vision was present in 50% at
age 8 (SE, 1.0) and legal blindness in 50% at age 12 (SE, 3.8). The

We investigated whether early onset of disease ( /H1102120 years)
caused a faster progression to low vision and legal blindness. In
early onset CD, low vision was present in 50% of patients at age
12 (SE, 2.5) and legal blindness was present in 50% at age 40. For
early onset CRD, these ages were 8 and 22 years (SE, 4.2),
respectively. With Cox regression analysis, ABCA4 carriership and
early age at onset were independent prognostic factors for progres-
sion to low vision and legal blindness (both P/H110210.001).
Discussion
We investigated a large series of CD and CRD patients, and
carefully evaluated their clinical and genetic characteristics.
The average affected patient had an onset within the ﬁrst 2
decades, followed by a gradual visual decline, ending in
legal blindness before middle age. For both CD and CRD,
ABCA4 was an important genetic cause associated with a
remarkably poor visual outcome. Despite these similarities,
differences between the 2 diseases were also noticed. Over-
all, CRD seemed to be a more severe disease: The mean age
of onset was 4 years earlier (12 vs 16 years; P /H110050.02), all
clinical parameters showed a higher proportion of progres-
sion, and mean age of legal blindness was considerably
lower (23 vs 48 years; P/H110210.001).
In this study, ERG was the gold standard for diagnosis, and
diminished cone amplitudes were obligatory for all patients.
Intact rod responses for/H113505 years after diagnosis were classi-
ﬁed as CD, rod involvement within 5 years as CRD. Despite
these diagnostic criteria, a considerable proportion of CRD
patients (31%) did not show peripheral visual ﬁeld defects at
onset. These CRD patients had a milder course of disease,
more comparable with CD (Fig 1). The functionally delayed
rod impairment in these patients may imply a diagnosis of CD
rather than CRD. On the other hand, a proportion of CD
patients (37%; 36/90) had diminished rod responses on ERG at
10 years of follow-up (Table 2), and these patients may be
more appropriate for a diagnosis of CRD. Despite these diag-
nostic considerations, our classiﬁcation allowed for detection
of considerable differences in clinical course. A limitation of
the study is that not all patients underwent a complete oph-
thalmic examination at 10 years after diagnosis. To evaluate
potential selection bias, we examined whether the CD
(90/139; 65%) and CRD (81/100; 81%) patients at 10 years of

A large proportion of the causal genes in AR-CD and
AR-CRD could not be identiﬁed using conventional genetic
screening techniques. It is likely that new methods such as
next-generation sequencing, retinal gene chips (300-kb Ret-
inal Resequencing Chip), and whole-genome sequencing
will facilitate identiﬁcation of a large proportion of addi-
tional causal mutations.
3 8–4 0This will add to the knowledge
of the genetic etiology of retinal disorders, and facilitate the
potential for future gene-based intervention methods. In
addition, complete genetic data will enable studies of geno-
type–phenotype correlations, and will help to improve our
current prognostic model. It is likely that the unknown
genes, analogous to the ABCA4 gene, have their own spe-
ciﬁc effects on visual prognosis.
In conclusion, we found that CD and CRD are distinct
cone disorders with each speciﬁc characteristics and clinical
course. Nevertheless, both seem to have a relatively early
onset and an ominous visual prognosis. ABCA4 is an im-
portant genetic cause of both disorders, and serves as a
prognostic factor for severe visual decline. Independently,
early onset also negatively affects visual outcome. Our
results can be used to aid in the clinical and genetic coun-
seling of CD and CRD patients.
