# Daily Outpatient Physical Therapy for a Toddler With a Neurodegenerative Disease: A Case Report.
**Authors:** Bican R, Ferrante R, Hendershot S, Byars M, Lo W, Heathcock JC
**Journal:** Pediatr Phys Ther (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1097/PEP.0000000000000884](https://doi.org/10.1097/PEP.0000000000000884)

## Content

PURPOSE: This case report highlights the potential value of delivering a high-dose physical therapy (PT) intervention for a child with a neurodegenerative disease. We include developmental outcomes for a 23-month-old toddler with biallelic TBCD gene mutations following daily outpatient PT.

SUMMARY OF KEY POINTS: The child had clinical improvements in gross and fine motor, cognition, expressive and receptive language, socioemotional, and adaptive behavior function as determined through Goal Attainment Scaling, Gross Motor Function Measure, and Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development following daily PT intervention.

STATEMENT OF CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS FOR CLINICAL PRACTICE: High-dose outpatient PT may be beneficial for a child with a neurodegenerative disease at some time frames. In selected cases, if the neurodegenerative disease slowly progresses, high-dose PT may be a treatment option to promote motor change.

Physical therapy (PT) improves motor skills, maximizes independence, and increases quality of life for children with developmental delays.1 When a child has a rare progressive neurodegenerative disorder, there is uncertainty on what is effective treatment. These children are often excluded from larger, randomized, clinical trials due to the rarity and progression of their disease; however, children with rare neurodegenerative disorders should receive evidence-based rehabilitation interventions.2 Case studies and other single-subject design methods can inform the evaluation and treatment of children with rare progressive neurodegenerative disorders.3

It is important for physical therapists to consider the dose of the PT intervention when developing a plan of care for a child with developmental delays. Dose of PT services can be defined using the FITT Model: Frequency: how often, Intensity: child effort or repetitions, Time: duration, and Type: specific treatment.4 There is an emerging body of evidence that supports the efficacy of high intensity (most days of the week for several weeks) PT interventions to improve motor function for young children with developmental delays.5–8 This frequency of therapy is thought to mimic typical motor skill development in which a child learns a new skill and then masters it within their natural environment, such as their home, school, or community.7 We use frequency to define how many times per week the intervention occurred and intensity as the duration of each treatment session. These 2 components are important when considering principles of motor learning, which include repetition of whole and part skills with shaping and feedback of child-initiated movements. Increased repetition can be afforded in a PT intervention that is delivered at a higher frequency, or more days per week.

The child in this case report has biallelic mutations in the TBCD gene, a very rare neurodegenerative disorder. No studies have reported a suggested, or optimal, therapeutic dose for PT interventions to maximize functional mobility. We examined PT frequency for 2 other rare, recessively inherited, progressive neurological diagnoses: Batten disease and spinal muscular atrophy (SMA). For children with Batten disease CLN2 type, consensus management guidelines propose PT services to be delivered 2–3 times per week early in life to promote functional mobility and maximize independence.9 Similarly, consensus statements for standard of care for children with SMA promote PT interventions including standing, stretching, strengthening, and aerobic exercise to be performed between 3–5 days/week. 10,11 Dunaway et. al. reported that younger children with SMA ages 3–5 years old received, on average, 9.6 PT sessions per month, or nearly 2 sessions per week.12 For children with cerebral palsy (CP) (a non-progressive neurological diagnosis), there is robust literature supporting the efficacy of high intensity interventions (several times/week) for motor change.13,14 Specifically, constraint-induced movement therapy (a high dose PT intervention) is a “green light”13 intervention in a recent systematic review by Novak et. al..8,15–17,17–19 Gannotti et. al. also suggests when considering models of care for rehabilitation services for children with CP, providers should consider massed practice, or high intensity rehabilitation interventions, early in life to optimize neuroplasticity of the brain.7,20 We hypothesized that a high intensity, or daily, PT model of care may benefit the child presented in this case report.

Children with neurodegenerative disorders present unique challenges when considering dose of PT interventions in the clinical setting. Specifically, when deciding whether a high dose intervention may benefit a child, the physical therapist must consider child, family, and intervention being delivered for optimal motor outcomes to maximize functional independence.4 For child characteristics, clinician’s should consider the child’s age, severity, and comorbidities to determine the child’s ability to participate in skilled, high dose PT intervention. Family characteristics, such as family-centered goals, availability, and commitment to attend multiple therapy sessions during the week should be considered. In this case report, we also propose that when deciding on a high dose intervention in the clinic, institutional characteristics should also be considered, such as therapist availability, scheduling, and insurance coverage for PT services.

This case report presents a child (23 months old) with an extremely rare (around 20 documented cases), neurodegenerative disorder caused by biallelic mutations in the TBCD gene resulting in seizures, developmental delays, and spastic quadriplegia.21–26 Several case series have been reported in the literature with varying clinical presentations and timelines for decline.21–25,27 Although overall prognosis is poor, the rate of motor decline is not well understood and the condition is so rare that a full appreciation of the clinical phenotype is unknown.27 PT services are important for optimizing motor abilities and function for the child. We present child, family, and institutional characteristics that informed our clinical decision for a high dose PT intervention and outcomes following the intervention. This case demonstrates that a daily high dose intervention may benefit a child with a neurodegenerative disorder.5–8,14–19

The child is a male toddler with a diagnosis of early-onset progressive encephalopathy secondary to a biallelic mutation in the TBCD gene, which is responsible for encoding the tubulin-specific chaperone D (TBCD).27 TBCD is 1 of 5 tubulin co-chaperones that is vital in microtubule dynamics and is critical for survival.26,27 This is a recessively inherited disorder that is life-limiting with no known cure.21–26 At the beginning of the intervention, the child was 23 months and 12 days old.

A timeline of significant medical events is in Table 1. The child had an unremarkable birth history. He was born at 38 3/7 weeks gestation at a birth weight of 8 lb. 11.8 oz. via spontaneous vaginal delivery. At 9 months old, the child had surgery for a spontaneous hip dislocation (spontaneous hip dislocation may have been a first indictor of an underlying medical condition). Following surgery, the child had a PT evaluation and scored extremely delayed in gross motor skills on the Gross Motor Subtest of the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, 3rd Edition (Raw score: 27; Scaled score; 1; Age equivalent: 7 months). As a result of this scoring, extensive diagnostic testing were performed, including Whole Exome Sequencing (WES), and at age 23 months the biallelic mutations in the TBCD gene28 were identified.

The specific clinical features of biallelic mutations of the TBCD gene include early-onset cortical atrophy, microcephaly, thin corpus collosum, developmental delays, epileptic seizures, and spastic quadriplegia.21–26 This child had all these features in the first year of life and hyperreflexia, hip dysplasia, and proximal hypotonia.

Goal attainment scaling (GAS)29–31, the Gross Motor Function Measure, 88-item (GMFM-88)32,33, and the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, 3rd Edition (Bayley-III)34 were used to quantify gross motor and developmental skills before and after the intervention. For GAS, a trained therapist retrospectively created the GAS scaling measurements, based on the child’s baseline function and family-centered goals (Supplementary Digital Content). GAS goals were completed pre- and post-intervention. Although the GMFM-88 was designed to be used for children with CP, it has been used as an outcome measure in other pediatric populations.35–41 For this child, only dimensions (A) lying and rolling, and (B) sitting were used as these were the skills he was able to complete. The GMFM-88 was administered pre-intervention, post-intervention, and at follow-up (1-month after post-intervention) (Figure 1). Finally, all subtests of the Bayley-III were administered pre-intervention, post-intervention, and at follow-up (Figure 1).

Prior to the child receiving a diagnosis of the biallelic mutations of the TBCD gene, the family enrolled him in a randomized clinical trial (DRIVE, R01HD083384–01A1). The child was randomized to a high dose (2 hours/session, 5 sessions/week, for 1 month) arm of the study. Baseline assessments were completed, and the child was scheduled to start the intervention but prior to his start date, he was diagnosed with the biallelic mutations of the TBCD gene. Since a neurodegenerative disorder was an exclusion criterion for the study, he was withdrawn.

Although the child was withdrawn from the study, the family wanted to complete the high dose PT through outpatient health services. At Nationwide Children’s Hospital (NCH), high dose PT interventions have been identified as promising and used as a current model of care.5,6,18,42 We considered logistical issues to successful delivery of high dose interventions including (1) child characteristics (patient tolerance and ability to improve throughout a high dose PT intervention), (2) family characteristics (family engagement and commitment to attending therapy sessions), (2) institutional characteristics (therapist availability as it is a clinical recommendation for the child to have no more than 2 therapists for continuity of care). In coordination with the Developmental PT Program Manager at NCH, these considerations were addressed: (1) based on the diagnosis, a neurologist approved the child for participation in daily high dose PT intervention, meaning the neurologist determined there would be no medical reason that the child would not be able to participate and that the intervention would not cause harm to the child, (2) the child was continuing to make progress in all developmental domains, (3) the family stated their preference and commitment to the intervention, and (4) 2 therapists were identified to deliver care to ensure continuity.

The daily high dose intervention used the same protocol as the clinical trial.6 The child was scheduled for 2 hours per session, 5 sessions per week, for 1 month (20 treatment sessions) (Figure 1). Goals were developed in collaboration with the family (Table 2).43 Therapy services were based on principles of motor learning and infant development, specifically focusing on repetition of child-initiated movements and included massed practice, and feedback in active, task-oriented functional mobility.6,13 The interventions were focused in four main categories: (1) proximal strengthening, (2) transitions (i.e. floor transitioning from sitting to/from prone, sitting to/from standing), (3) supported standing, and (4) supported walking using gait trainer (Mustang). Therapeutic activities could be performed in any order for any given length of time; however, a typical session would begin on the mat with proximal strengthening (~20 minutes per session) and transition activities (~40 minutes per session), followed by standing (~20 minutes per session) and walking activities (~40 minutes per session). Examples of the activities completed during a 2-hour intervention are in Table 3. Following the completion of the high dose PT intervention, the child had no PT services for 1 month.

Throughout the intervention, the family was provided a home exercise program for the child.13 The family was educated to incorporate exercises into their everyday life, optimizing therapeutic opportunities in daily activities that were meaningful to the family and child.13,30,43 Home exercises were based on activities performed during the therapy session and included: (1) proximal strengthening; sitting on the parent’s lap and leaning side to side, (2) transitions; encouraging transitions during floor play at home, during sit to/from stand transfers during the day, (3) supported standing; during dressing, play activities, and (4) supported walking using gait trainer; performed at school in the classroom and on the playground. These exercises were practiced during the therapy sessions to ensure the family could perform handling techniques, appropriate level of support, and appropriate level of feedback for the child. At the end of the high dose PT intervention, the family was provided a document of these exercises and progressions.

The child completed 17 of 20 therapy sessions, meeting 85% of the prescribed frequency. The mean treatment time was 92.8±10.9 minutes/session out of the scheduled 120 minutes/session (2 hours), meeting on average 77.3% of the prescribed intensity. 64.8% of therapy was therapeutic exercise and 35.2% was gait training.

The child’s goals, GAS, and outcomes are in Table 2. The child made progress in all goals throughout the intervention and progressed more than expected in 2 of the 3 functional goals set.

The child made improvements in dimensions (A) lying and rolling, and (B) sitting on the GMFM-88. From baseline to immediately post-intervention, he improved 9 points on dimension (A) and 1 point on dimension (B). These improvements continued through follow-up, as he improved 6 points on dimension (A) and 3 points on dimension (B). Outcomes are in Table 3.

All Bayley-III scores are reported as the raw score, as the child was seriously delayed, and scaled scores were not appropriate for use. He made improvements in the 5 domains tested on the Bayley-III from baseline to post-intervention (8-point improvement on fine motor, 4 points on gross motor, 3 points on cognition, 1 point on expressive language, and 3 points on receptive language). He also had improvements in 6/7 domains from post-intervention to 1-month post intervention (1-point improvement on fine motor and cognition, 2 points on expressive language and receptive language, 24 points on socio-emotional, and 62 points on adaptive behavior). He had a decreased score on the gross motor domain (1-point decrease). Outcomes are in Table 4.

A daily high dose PT intervention may be feasible and beneficial for a child with a neurodegenerative disorder. Children with neurodegenerative diseases, especially if they are rare, are often excluded from clinical trials: which is what occurred to the child described in this case report. Exclusion from clinical trials greatly limits the amount of available literature to inform evidence-based clinical care and the generalizability of high dosing models of care.2 Children with neurodegenerative disorders, such as biallelic mutations in the TBCD gene, experience delays in all developmental domains. These children and their families should be afforded evidence-based PT2, underpinning the importance of single subject design and case reports for children with rare diseases that includes a rehabilitation component as described in this case report.21–26

High dose PT interventions for other progressive and non-progressive neurological diagnoses are emerging and promising for improvements in motor skill development and quality of life.5,6,9,18 In addition, consensus guidelines for children with Batten disease and SMA have recommended physical activity interventions, such as strengthening and stretching, to be performed at a minimum of 2–3 times per week.9,10,12,44 Although this may not be considered “high frequency”, it is a higher frequency than the child in this case report was previously receiving (1 time/week).45 In addition there are many studies supporting the efficacy of high dose PT interventions (several times/week) for motor change for children with CP, a non-progressive neurological diagnosis.5,6,13,14 With this information and improvements during and after the treatment period we determined that a high dose PT intervention may be beneficial for the child and family in this case report.

Successful implementation of this daily and high dose PT intervention required careful consideration of child, family, and institutional characteristics utilizing a family-centered care approach. The child in this case was medically cleared by a neurologist and was evaluated by a clinical physical therapist who determined that he would be able to tolerate and participate skilled PT daily for 2 hours each day based on reports from previous physical therapy sessions. Through communication and collaborative goal setting between the family and medical team, it was clear the family was committed to and seeking out a high dose model of care, even after no longer being eligible for the clinical trial. The Developmental PT Program Manager provided institutional logistical support to ensure that the child could participate in a high dose intervention and would be seen by 2 therapists to promote continuity of care. Meeting the needs of a family whose child was recently diagnosed with a progressive and terminal illness was paramount.

The child in this case report made improvements in gross and fine motor skills as captured through personalized and collaborative family goals using GAS, the GMFM-88, and the Bayley-III, surpassing the minimum clinically important difference on the GMFM-88.46 Based on the on these outcome measures, motor skill change was likely more rapid during the high dose intervention and was maintained throughout the 1-month break from outpatient PT through delivery of an HEP. The only item he did not maintain from pre-intervention to follow-up was on the Bayley-III, gross motor item #33: ability to support his own weight in standing for 2 seconds, which may be related to maturation/growth of the child.

Gross and fine motor improvements were expected, as the intervention was focused on motor abilities; however, he also had improvements on dimensions of the Bayley-III that were not targeted through the intervention. Specifically, he made improvements in cognition, expressive language, and receptive language. This may be attributed to natural development of the child, but may also be influenced by improvements in motor skills (fine and gross) as he was able to increase interaction with his environment.47,48 In addition, parents reported improvements on the Bayley-III Socioemotional and Adaptive Behavior subtests from pre-intervention to follow-up. These subtests ask questions about daily life tasks. Improvements noted by the parent in these subtests highlights the carryover of the intervention from the clinic to home.

Identifying efficacious PT interventions for children with neurodegenerative disorders and poor long-term prognoses presents unique challenges, as there is scarce available literature to inform evidence-based clinical care. It is important for physical therapists to (1) meet the needs of the child and their families, and (2) maximize motor skills and independence through therapeutic interventions and family education in coordination with the Family-Centered Care for best outcomes.49 When selecting a high dose model of care, physical therapists should consider the child, family, and institutional characteristics as described throughout this case. This case report demonstrates that a daily and high dose PT intervention was likely beneficial for a child with a progressive, neurodegenerative disease and met the goals of his family.
