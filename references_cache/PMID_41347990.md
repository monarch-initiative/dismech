Bleeding disorder of unknown cause (BDUC) is increasingly recognized as a common diagnosis among adult patients with mild to moderate bleeding symptoms and is reflective of unremarkable hemostatic tests despite a clinically relevant bleeding tendency. While extensive clinical and basic research has been conducted on the bleeding phenotype and underlying mechanisms of BDUC, great uncertainty remains regarding the long-term risk for spontaneous bleeding and the bleeding risk after hemostatic challenges. Currently available data suggest that a substantial portion of BDUC patients continue to experience spontaneous bleeding symptoms, such as easy bruising, epistaxis, and heavy menstrual bleeding. Risk factors associated with the persistence of these symptoms include the presence of the respective symptom prior to the BDUC diagnosis and a higher baseline bleeding score. Furthermore, a large proportion of BDUC patients report bleeding complications after surgery or child delivery prior to initial investigations. Patients with prior bleeding complications after such events, patients with blood group O, and patients who did not receive hemostatic prophylaxis demonstrate an increased risk for postinterventional bleeding during follow-up. Although BDUC remains a diagnosis of exclusion, accumulating data underscore the need to recognize it as a potentially persistent clinically relevant condition. Future studies should focus on refining diagnostic algorithms, investigating underlying mechanisms, and developing predictive tools for bleeding risk to improve individualized management strategies.

A 34-year-old woman is referred to the hemostasis outpatient department for the evaluation of a suspected bleeding disorder. During the structured interview based on the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis Bleeding Assessment Tool (ISTH-BAT),1the patient reported extensive spontaneous bruises, prolonged bleeding from minor wounds, and abnormal uterine bleeding, which had been treated unsuccessfully with antifibrinolytics, iron supplementation, hormonal therapies, and a curettage prior to this visit. Furthermore, the patient reported prolonged bleeding after a tooth extraction, which required resuturing, as well as postsurgical bleeding complications after 5 out of 7 prior surgeries, some requiring surgical hemostasis and blood transfusions. Additional postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) occurred after the delivery of the patient's only child, which did not require medical intervention. In total, the patient presented with a clinically relevant bleeding tendency, scoring 14 points in the ISTH-BAT (above the upper limit of normal of 5 in women aged 31-51 years),2,3warranting an extensive hemostatic evaluation. After thorough laboratory investigations, including a complete blood count, blood chemistry including thyroid hormones, von Willebrand disease diagnostics, coagulation factor activity measurements, and platelet function analyses as outlined inTable 1, the common disorders associated with mild to moderate bleeding symptoms were ruled out, resulting in the diagnosis of a bleeding disorder of unknown cause (BDUC).4During the following year, the patient underwent total hysterectomy due to her heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) at the age of 35, after which she also experienced prolonged vaginal bleeding. Five years after the initial presentation, the patient is to undergo a planned hemithyroidectomy due to adenoma, and a statement about her risk for bleeding complications during and after this procedure, as well as recommendations for optimal perioperative hemostatic management, have been requested by the surgical team.

Individuals experiencing chronic bleeding symptoms, such as heavy menstrual bleeding, recurrent epistaxis, or spontaneous large hematomas, frequently consult health care professionals to investigate potential underlying causes. Following initial assessments and the exclusion of life-threatening causes of bleeding, a series of laboratory investigations is typically conducted, with both patients and physicians seeking clarification regarding a potential bleeding disorder. Unfortunately, patients and their physicians are often left disappointed when no underlying bleeding disorder can be identified. In recent years, it has become clear that the most common outcome in patients with mild to moderate bleeding tendencies is the absence of any detectable abnormality. This phenomenon is now recognized as “bleeding disorder of unknown cause”, reflecting normal results across all hemostatic tests. As a diagnosis of exclusion, BDUC is diagnosed when a patient's bleeding tendency cannot be explained by any identifiable hemostatic disorder or secondary cause.

While data on the life-long prevalence and severity of bleeding manifestations in the general population, including individuals across all adult age groups, have been investigated using the ISTH-BAT,3,5prospective data on the incidence and cumulative risk of bleeding events in broadly representative, otherwise healthy cohorts are, to our knowledge, lacking. Such data could serve as a comparator for the bleeding risk in patients with BDUC. Prospective data on major bleeding events have been collected in certain diseases, such as cancer or hemodialysis. In those patient populations, the bleeding risk is very high: 7 per 100 patient-years in patients with cancer and 6 per 100 patient-years in those with hemodialysis.6,7To obtain more information on the bleeding risk in individuals without anticoagulation or severe disease, incidence rates from patients with a history of venous thromboembolism enrolled in the placebo arms of long-term anticoagulation trials can be analyzed. The incidence of major bleeding from those studies in patients on placebo was 0.17 to 0.72 per 100 patient-years—and thus not negligible.8While the risk of major bleeding events is of great concern to patients and their physicians, mild to moderate bleeding can also significantly impact a patient's quality of life (QoL), as has been shown in patients from the Vienna Bleeding Biobank (VIBB).9

The most frequently asked questions of patients diagnosed with BDUC and their doctors are as follows:

(1) What is the risk of developing a severe bleed in the future?

(2) Will there be dangerous bleeding during surgery or childbirth?

(3) Which treatments can be applied in the long term to decrease the bleeding tendency?

(4) Which treatment is effective to prevent or treat bleeding after a surgical procedure or during childbirth?

Currently, there is no objectively correct and evidence-based answer to these questions. However, there is consensus that urgently needed evidence can only be gained in prospective observational studies or interventional trials.

Studies that investigate patients with BDUC prospectively are still scarce; this is even the case in established mild to moderate bleeding disorders. In fact, to our knowledge only 2 prospective studies that investigated the occurrence of bleeding in patients with BDUC have been published.10,11Additionally, a few retrospective studies evaluating bleeding events following the diagnosis of BDUC have been published.12-17These studies are presented and discussed in detail in this review.

Spontaneous bleeding symptoms such as easy bruising, prolonged bleeding from minor wounds, or epistaxis are common among patients with mild to moderate bleeding disorders and BDUC, affecting between 20% and 50%.16,18,19Even though these symptoms are usually not life-threatening, they can negatively affect a patient's overall QoL and require additional medical attention, including diagnostic assessments and interventions.9,20

In a retrospective analysis of 90 BDUC patients (98% women), Relke et al aimed to identify predictive parameters for spontaneous bleedings.13Fifty-two patients (57.8%) reported at least 1 spontaneous bleeding symptom, with HMB as the most common type of bleeding. The baseline ISTH-BAT score was slightly higher in patients who reported subsequent spontaneous bleeding, although it did not reach statistical significance. However, in multivariable logistic regression a higher baseline bleeding score was significantly associated with an increased risk for the occurrence of any spontaneous bleeding in the future, with an odds ratio of 1.25 (95% CI, 1.05-1.49). Contrarily, both a higher platelet count and higher age at study inclusion were associated with a decreased risk for future bleeding, with odds ratios of 0.99 (95% CI, 0.99-1.00) and 0.95 (95% CI, 0.92-0.99), respectively.

To date, 2 prospective studies have been conducted to investigate the risk of bleeding among patients with mild to moderate bleeding disorders and BDUC.10,11Fasulo et al investigated a total of 136 patients, of whom 92 (68%) had BDUC, for a mean follow-up period of 2.2 years.10During follow-up, 1 major bleed (MB) and 10 clinically relevant nonmajor bleeds (CRNMB) were documented in 11 patients (8.1%), of whom 4 had BDUC (36%), which resulted in an overall incidence rate of 3.7 per 100 person- years for MB and CRNMB.21There was no difference in the baseline ISTH-BAT (≤5 vs >5) between the patients with bleeding episodes and the rest of the cohort, and the incidence rate was similar between both groups. However, Cox regression analysis, shown inFigure 1(adjusted for age at study inclusion), revealed a significantly higher incidence rate of 7.5 in patients with laboratory-confirmed diagnoses of a bleeding disorder in comparison with BDUC patients (incidence rate, 1.9), resulting in an adjusted hazard ratio of 3.5 (95% CI, 0.9-13.0).

The most comprehensive study to date on the persistence of spontaneous bleeding symptoms in patients after the diagnosis of BDUC has been reported by Mehic et al.11In this study, follow-up interviews were conducted with 392 patients with mild to moderate bleeding disorders, 246 of whom (62.8%) were classified as having BDUC. During the follow-up period (median. 4.3 years), the most commonly reported spontaneous bleeding symptoms were easy bruising and hematoma in 47.9% and prolonged bleeding from minor wounds in 26.9% of BDUC patients, followed by HMB in 24.3% of female BDUC patients. Further data are outlined inTable 2. In accordance with the results of the previously discussed study, a higher baseline bleeding severity (assessed in this study using the Vicenza bleeding questionnaire) was significantly associated with the occurrence of any bleeding symptom during the follow-up period (adjusted odds ratio, 1.14 per increase of the bleeding score by 1 point; 95% CI, 1.04-1.25).22However, this analysis also included nonspontaneous bleeding symptoms, such as postpartum or postsurgical bleeding. Upon analysis of all individual bleeding symptoms, the authors described an association between symptoms reported at initial study inclusion and symptom recurrence at follow-up, indicating that the majority of symptoms tend to persist over longer periods of time (Figure 2). Importantly, this trend of symptom persistence was the strongest in easy bruising and hematoma, as 59.6% of patients with this symptom during follow-up had also reported it at study inclusion. Similarly, 48.9% of patients who reported prolonged bleeding from minor wounds during the follow-up period had also experienced it prior to initial study inclusion.

HMB is frequent in women at childbearing age. A recent study from the United Kingdom and Australia revealed a prevalence of 33.7% and anemia in 13% of women aged 18 to 50 years (median age, 28.5 years).23It is known that in at least 10% of women with HMB an inherited bleeding disorder can be diagnosed.24However, after excluding gynecologic causes of HMB, a large number of women with unexplained HMB remain, and those women might also present with other bleeding manifestations, such as mucocutaneous bleeding at other sites or postsurgical bleeding. Some of these patients may meet the criteria for BDUC following a thorough evaluation of their medical and bleeding history, comorbidities, and individual characteristics. Investigating recurrent bleeding in patients included in the prospective observational VIBB study revealed that 67 in 188 (35.6%) of those who had HMB at inclusion also had HMB during follow-up, whereas this was the case in only 20 in 113 (17.7%) women who had no HMB at study inclusion. This difference was highly statistically significant. Thus, it can be expected that women with BDUC and HMB are affected long term by this bleeding manifestation, with consequences for QoL and iron status, including iron deficiency anemia. Although no specific studies exist on the treatment of HMB exclusively in women with BDUC, it can be expected that the recommended treatments prescribed for patients with established diagnoses of a mild to moderate bleeding disorder will show similar efficacy in BDUC patients. Those treatments include tranexamic acid and/or hormone therapy.24

Postsurgical bleeding, PPH, and bleeding after tooth extractions are a frequent cause of referral of patients for hemostatic investigations and are common in patients diagnosed with mild to moderate bleeding disorders and BDUC.19

Castle et al conducted a retrospective case review of 36 women with BDUC and assessed a total of 60 deliveries over a 10-year period after the initial BDUC diagnosis.14In 51 deliveries with documented estimated blood loss, PPH occurred in 23 cases (45%), of which 18 were primary (within 24 hours after delivery) and 5 were secondary (24 hours to 6 weeks after delivery). No cause for the bleeding complication was found in 3 of the 9 (33%) primary PPH cases with blood loss exceeding 1000  ml and in 3 of the 5 cases of secondary PPH (60%). Analysis of the 54 deliveries with documentation regarding primary bleeding prophylaxis (tranexamic acid, desmopressin, or platelet transfusions) revealed 6.3-fold higher risk for PPH in women who did not receive prophylaxis.

Similarly, Berkowitz et al investigated the efficacy of hemostatic prophylaxis in 48 major surgeries, 70 minor procedures, and 19 births in a cohort of 127 BDUC patients.12In this study 90% of patients undergoing major surgery and 76% undergoing minor procedures received hemostatic prophylaxis. Ninety percent of the pregnant women received tranexamic acid alone or in combination as prophylaxis for childbirth. Bleeding occurred in 5 in 48 (10%) major surgeries, in 1 in 70 (1%) minor procedures, and in 5 in 19 births. While the prevalence of bleeding was lower in patients who received any hemostatic prophylaxis (9% vs 25% in major surgery, 0% vs 6% in minor procedures, and 24% vs 50% in childbirth), no significant association was found between the occurrence of bleeding and the use of prophylaxis in a regression analysis adjusting for age, bleeding score, procedure classification, and study site (P= .051).

The aforementioned study by Mehic et al separately analyzed bleeding after tooth extractions, PPH, and postsurgical bleeding.11During follow-up, 85 patients underwent tooth extractions, of whom 23 (27.1%) reported prolonged bleeding or bleeding complications. Similarly, 39 of 173 patients (22.5%) who underwent surgical procedures during follow-up reported bleeding complications. Of these, 70% were classified as CRNMB and 25% as MB. No PPH was reported in 17 deliveries in the BDUC-patient cohort. In a multivariate regression model, blood group O, previous postsurgical bleeding complications, and the diagnosis of an established bleeding disorder (in contrast to BDUC) were identified as significant predictors for postsurgical bleeding, postpartum bleeding (which only occurred in patients with established diagnoses of a bleeding disorder), or bleeding after tooth extractions during the follow-up period.

Having thoroughly discussed the risk of future bleeding in BDUC patients, we now return to the clinical case, who requires a statement on her individual bleeding risk for a planned hemithyroidectomy and, perhaps, recommendations for hemostatic bleeding prophylaxis. She presents with 2 major characteristics identified in Mehic et al as independently associated with an increased risk of postsurgical bleeding complications: She has blood group O and initially presented a significant history of surgery-related bleeding, ranging from minor to major complications.11However, as she has not been diagnosed with a specific bleeding disorder, her bleeding risk could be lower than in those with a diagnosed bleeding disorder, as Mehic et al and Castle et al showed a lower risk for bleeding after medical interventions in patients with BDUC.11,14

Nevertheless, her bleeding history and characteristics, as well as the site of the planned surgery, warrant pharmacological bleeding prophylaxis as recommended by the ISTH, which suggests tranexamic acid for most invasive surgery, with the addition of desmopressin in interventions with a higher bleeding risk. Furthermore, these management plans should be tailored to the individual patient and devised by multidisciplinary teams.2Obaji et al nicely summarized the outcome of 78 surgeries performed on 33 BDUC patients over the course of 16 years.15Most commonly, tranexamic acid (alone or in combination with desmopressin) was used as primary prophylaxis. This treatment showed an overall success rate of 90%, as 70 surgeries were performed without bleeding-related complications. Hence, peri- and postsurgical prophylaxis with tranexamic acid (1000  mg 3 times per day for 3 days) and desmopressin (0.3 microgram/kg of body weight intravenously before surgery) was recommended in our case.25Postinterventional antithrombotic prophylaxis should follow current guidelines while taking the individual bleeding risk into account and was not prescribed in our patient. Under this treatment, the planned surgery was conducted without significant bleeding complications or prolonged bleeding, and thus a hemostatically satisfactory result could be achieved.

It has only recently become clear that a significant proportion of patients with clinically relevant bleeding tendencies— particularly women—show no abnormalities in extensive hemostatic investigations.26Furthermore, it could even be questioned whether subtle abnormalities in routine homeostatic panels that lead to a formal diagnosis (eg, slightly decreased von Willebrand factor activity and antigen levels) are indeed the underlying reason for the bleeding tendency.27,28Risk and fear of future bleeding, specifically during invasive procedures, are a significant concern for patients and their treating physicians. At present a prediction of future bleeding is difficult, as prospective studies are scarce and have revealed somewhat discrepant results. In the currently published literature, including that of the larger VIBB study, a higher baseline bleeding score at initial evaluation and a prior history of spontaneous bleeding symptoms were associated with more unprovoked bleeding during follow-up, whereas prior bleeding complications, a lack of prophylactic treatment, and blood group O, but not the ISTH-BAT score, were found to be risk factors for future bleeding after hemostatic challenges. As personalized treatment is not possible in patients in whom no diagnosis of a bleeding disorders could be made, treatment with tranexamic acid and desmopressin is likely to decrease the amount of blood loss during acute bleeding events and invasive procedures.

In persons with hemophilia, novel rebalancing agents, such as monoclonal antibodies against tissue factor pathway inhibitors, have been introduced and proven to be effective in preventing bleeding episodes.29It can be assumed that these agents may also decrease the bleeding rate in patients with BDUC. Of note, such a novel approach must be investigated in appropriately designed interventional trials and would only be designated for the most severe cases of BDUC.
