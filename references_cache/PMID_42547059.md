# Thanatophoric dysplasia: prevalence, clinical characteristics, and molecular findings in a public hospital from Western Mexico.
**Authors:** Cruz-Cruz JP, Robles-Espinoza K, Magaña-Torres MT, Peña-Padilla C, Bustos-Rodríguez FJ, Barrios-Prieto E, Bobadilla-Morales L, Corona-Rivera A, Corona-Rivera JR
**Journal:** Gac Med Mex (2025)
**DOI:** [10.24875/GMM.M26001074](https://doi.org/10.24875/GMM.M26001074)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Thanatophoric dysplasia (TD) is a lethal FGFR3 chondrodysplasia characterized by micromelia and bowed femurs (TD1), or straight femurs and cloverleaf skull (TD2).

OBJECTIVE: To determine the prevalence and clinical and molecular characteristics of patients with TD in a public hospital in Western Mexico.

MATERIALS AND METHODS: Observational, retrospective and descriptive study. Ten live births with TD were included from a total of 118,154 births between 2009 and 2024 at the Hospital Civil de Guadalajara Dr. Juan I. Menchaca (Mexico). The FGFR3 gene was analyzed by PCR, followed by direct sequencing of exons 4-6 and 8.

RESULTS: The prevalence of TD was 0.85 per 10,000 births (95% CI: 0.32-1.37). All presented TD1 (4 men and 6 women), sporadic occurrence and 4/10 advanced paternal age. Phenotype: short stature, macrocephaly, hypotonia, distinctive facial features, narrow thorax, prominent abdomen, micromelia, redundant skinfolds, trident hand, short ribs, rhizomelic shortening, irregular metaphyses, platyspondyly and bowed femurs. In seven, pathogenic variants in FGFR3 were identified: p.Arg248Cys (n = 3), p.Ser371Cys (n = 2), p.Gly370Cys (n = 1) and p.Try373Cys (n = 1).

CONCLUSIONS: One in 11,815 infants was born with T1D. Molecular analysis allows to distinguish it from other lethal chondrodysplasias and to provide appropriate genetic counseling.

Introduction
Thanatophoric dysplasia (TD) is a short-limbed 
skeletal dysplasia characterized by relative macro -
cephaly and a small thorax, and is usually lethal at 
birth.1 Its occurrence is sporadic, and most cases are 
explained by de novo mutations with autosomal dom -
inant inheritance. 2,3 The most common form, TD 
type 1 (TD1), is characterized by micromelia, bowed 
femora (“telephone receiver-like”), and occasionally 
craniosynostosis (MIM 187600). In contrast, TD type 2 
(TD2) is distinguished by straight femora and moder -
ate-to-severe craniosynostosis with cloverleaf skull 
deformity (MIM 187601). Both types also present with 
short ribs, distinctive facial features, brachydactyly, 
hypotonia, redundant skin folds of the extremities, and 
severe respiratory insufficiency. Radio graphically, 
findings include rhizomelic shortening of long bones, 
irregular metaphyses, platyspondyly (H-shaped verte -
brae), a small foramen magnum with brainstem com -
pression, and central nervous system anomalies. 1,4,5 
Early death occurs due to respiratory insufficiency 
caused by a restrictive thorax and pulmonary hypo -
plasia, although exceptional cases with prolonged 
survival up to 28 years have been reported. 6
TD belongs to the group of FGFR3-related chondro -
dysplasias (fibroblast growth factor receptor 3). 1 
FGFR3 is a transmembrane protein with tyrosine 
kinase activity, essential for the regulation of cell 
growth and endochondral ossification. Pathogenic 
variants causing TD result in gain-of-function of 
FGFR3, leading to ligand-independent constitutive 
activation and inhibition of chondrocyte proliferation, 
maturation, and differentiation. 1,7
Prenatal ultrasono graphic evaluation and postnatal 
clinical-radiographic assessment are commonly used for 
diagnosis and may even allow distinction between TD 
subtypes.1,8 However, in a minority of cases, differentia -
tion between TD and other lethal skeletal dysplasias may 
be impossible based solely on clinical findings. 9
TD is estimated to affect approximately 1 in 20,000 
live births. 1,10 Although there are anatomopathologic 
descriptions of patients with TD in Mexico, 11 no studies 
have reported its prevalence or molecular findings. 
Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the 
prevalence and the clinical and molecular character -
istics of a series of patients with TD in a public hos -
pital in Western Mexico.
Materials and methods
Study design and sample characteristics
We conducted an observational, retrospective, 
descriptive study in a cohort of patients with a diag -
nosis of TD, all born between January 2009 and 
December 2024 at  Hospital Civil de Guadalajara 
Dr. Juan I. Menchaca, Guadalajara, Jalisco, Mexico. 
The prevalence of TD was estimated by dividing the 
number of live births with TD by the total number of 
live births at the hospital during the study period. The 
total number of births was obtained from the hospital 
information system as the primary data source. Our 
hospital is a referral center in western Mexico that 
provides tertiary care to an open population, predom -
inantly urban and of low socioeconomic status, with 
approximately 7000 births per year (about 5% of 
annual births in the state of Jalisco).
Clinical evaluation of cases
Patients with TD were evaluated at the Centro de 
Registro e Investigación sobre Anomalías Congénitas 
(CRIAC), a hospital-based program for active surveil -
lance of congenital defects that includes all live births 
of ≥ 22 weeks’ gestation and birth weight > 500 g. 
All parents of participant infants were of mestizo origin 
from Western Mexico. Among osteochondrodysplasia 
cases recorded in CRIAC (ICD-10 codes Q77 and 
Q78), only those with a clinical diagnosis of TD (ICD-10 
code Q77.1) were included. The diagnosis of TD was 
established by a certified clinical geneticist and was 
considered in the presence of micromelia, short bowed 
or straight femora, with or without craniosynostosis, in

Molecular diagnosis
Genomic DNA was extracted from a blood sample 
using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Ger -
many) and subsequently stored at –20 °C in the Cyto -
genetics Unit of the  Hospital Civil de Guadalajara 
Dr. Juan I. Menchaca  until molecular analysis. The 
search for pathogenic variants in the FGFR3 gene 
was performed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) 
and Sanger sequencing. The molecular study was 
carried out at the Centro de Investigación Biomédica 
de Occidente del Instituto Mexicano del Seguro 
Social as part of a collaborative project registered on 
skeletal dysplasias (R-2022-785-001). Since all 
patients had a clinical diagnosis of TD1, and consid -
ering that more than 90% of cases are associated with 
the p.Arg248Cys and p.Tyr373Cys variants, located in 
exons 6 and 8 of the FGFR3 gene, respectively,¹ only 
2 fragments including these regions were amplified. 
Primers were designed using Oligo ®.net Primer Anal -
ysis Software v.6 (https://www.oligo.net/downloads.
html). The first fragment, 873 bp in length, comprised 
exons 4 to 6 (forward 5’TCCTACACAGGACGG -
GAAACTG3’ and reverse 5’ACCCAAATCCTCACG -
CAACC3’), and the second fragment, 382 bp, included 
exon 8 (forward 5’CCCTTCCGCTCCCAGTGGTG3’, 
reverse 5’CACAGCCCAGGACCAGCGTG). PCR reac -
tions were performed in a final volume of 10 µL, with 
50–100 ng of DNA, 1X PCRx buffer (PCRx Enhancer 
System, Thermo Fisher), 2.0 mM MgCl 2, 5% DMSO, 
2.5 pmol of each primer, and 0.5 U of Platinum Taq 
polymerase (Invitrogen) for amplification of exons 4 to 
6. For exon 8, 0.02 U of recombinant Taq polymerase 
(EP0404 Thermo Fisher) was used. The thermocy -
cling program consisted of a touchdown protocol with 
3 different annealing temperatures: 66 °C (6 cycles), 
64 °C (6 cycles), and 62 °C (26 cycles). PCR products 
were visualized on 6% polyacrylamide gels stained 
with silver nitrate. For sequencing, the amplicons were 
purified with 0.5 µl of ExoSAP-IT™ (Affymetrix, Santa 
Clara, CA, USA), incubated at 37 °C for 15 minutes 
and then at 80 °C for 15 minutes. Sequencing reac -
tions were performed in a volume of 10 µl, with 100–
300 ng of DNA, 1.5 µl of 5X buffer, 2.5 pmol of primer, 
and 0.5 µl of the Big Dye Terminator kit v.3.1 (Applied 
Biosystems, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The thermocy -
cling program consisted of an initial denaturation at 
96 °C for 4 minutes, followed by 25 cycles of 96 °C 
for 10 seconds, 55 °C for 5 seconds, and 60 °C for 
2 minutes. Sequencing reactions were purified using 
Centrisep columns filled with Sephadex ® G-50 medium 
(Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA), and capillary 
electrophoresis was performed on a SeqStudio sys -
tem (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). 
The obtained electropherograms were compared with 
the reference sequence NM_000142.5. Variant 
nomenclature followed the recommendations of the 
Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS; varnomen.
hgvs.org).

Results
Prevalence
During the 15-year period, the hospital-based study 
cohort included 10 newborns with a clinical diagnosis 
of TD registered at the CRIAC, out of a total of 118,154 
live births. Therefore, the prevalence of TD obtained 
was 0.85 per 10,000 live births (95% CI, 0.32-1.37), 
equivalent to 1 case per 11,815 births. By clinical sub -
type, all patients had TD1; 0 patients with TD2 were 
identified.
Molecular diagnosis
In 7 of the 8 patients analyzed at the molecular 
level, variants causing TD1 were identified in the 
FGFR3 gene: c.742C>T (p.Arg248Cys) (n = 3), 
c.1111A>T (p.Ser371Cys) (n = 2), c.1108G>T (p.Gly -
370Cys) (n = 1), and c.1118A>G (p.Tyr373Cys) (n = 1). 
All corresponded to missense mutations with gain-of-
function effects. In one patient, no pathogenic variant 
was detected in the four analyzed exons, and it was 
not possible to analyze the remaining regions of the 
gene. Based on these data, we estimated a minimum 
prevalence of molecularly confirmed TD1 of 0.59 per 
10,000 births (95% CI, 0.15-1.03).
Clinical evaluation of cases
Clinical, radiological, and molecular data identified 
in the 10 cases are presented in Table 1  and Fig. 1  
(patients A to J). All cases were sporadic, and no 
family history of consanguinity or congenital diseases 
was identified. The mean paternal age was 34.7 ± 
8.3 years, and in 4/10 cases it was greater than 
35 years. The male/female ratio was 0.66. Mothers 
underwent at least 1 ultrasound during prenatal care, 
reported by maternal–fetal medicine as TD (n = 9) or 
achondroplasia (n = 1). Only four cases had a more 
detailed ultrasound description, which included find -
ings such as macrocephaly, frontal bossing, depressed 
nasal bridge, restrictive thorax (bell-shaped chest 
sign), pulmonary hypoplasia, platyspondyly, and short, 
curved femurs ( Fig. 2 ). In 3/10 pregnancies, polyhy -
dramnios was reported. The mean gestational age 
was 34.7 ± 2.7 weeks, and in 8/10 cases birth was 
preterm. The Z-score for weight-for-gestational age 
was normal in 9/10 patients (mean –0.26 SD). All 
patients had short stature (mean Z-score for 
length-for-gestational age –3.7 SD), and macroceph -
aly was observed in 6/10 (mean Z-score for head 
circumference-for-gestational age 2.6 SD). The clini -
cal and radio graphic features of TD1 shown in Table 1 
were consistently present in nearly all patients. The 
components that define the distinctive facial appear -
ance of a patient with TD1 were prominent forehead, 
flat facies, and depressed nasal bridge. The most 
severe micromelia was observed in patient I. Femoral 
bowing was moderate in patients A, B, E, and H, 
whereas in patients A, C, F, G, I, and J it was very 
marked (Fig. 1). As unique findings, ventricular septal 
defect (patient D) and severe clubfoot (patient F) were 
identified. In all patients, survival was less than 
5 days, with a mean age at death of 1.7 ± 1.1 days. 
Autopsy studies in two patients additionally identified 
pulmonary hypoplasia and chondrocyte disorganiza -
tion (patients A and B), and splenomegaly (patient A).
Discussion
The observed prevalence of TD (0.85 per 10,000 
live births) appears higher than the range most fre -
quently reported in other countries (0.21-0.30 per 
10,000 births). 2,16-19 In those studies, prevalence esti -
mates were based solely on clinical diagnosis and, 
unlike our study, often included late fetal deaths and 
elective terminations. 2,17,18 Therefore, our prevalence 
estimate based on molecularly confirmed TD1 cases 
(0.59 per 10,000 births; 95% CI, 0.15-1.03) may serve 
as a useful reference for future studies involving larger 
populations with molecular characterization.
As in other autosomal dominant disorders, de novo 
mutations causing TD have been associated with 
advanced paternal age. 1,3 The mean paternal age and 
the proportion of fathers older than 35 years in our 
cohort suggest a paternal age effect. Moreover, pater -
nal age in our cohort (34.7 ± 8.3 years) contrasts with 
the mean paternal age of 27.8 ± 7.7 years reported in 
893 fathers of unaffected newborns in our hospital. 20 
This phenomenon has been attributed to mutations in 
tyrosine kinase receptor genes such as FGFR3, which 
are highly expressed in spermatogonial stem cells and 
act as driver mutations, promoting ligand-independent 
receptor activation. This confers a selective advan -
tage, leading to clonal expansion of mutated cells with 
increasing paternal age. 3

gestation. Given its perinatal lethality, early diagnosis 
allows consideration of pregnancy termination as part 
of prenatal management. 1,8 In our cohort, no pregnan -

permitted in the state of Jalisco until 2024.

cohort, characteristic abnormalities affecting the skull, 
thorax, spine, pelvis, and long bones were present in

This may be explained by the relatively small sample size 
or may reflect population differences or underdiagnosis

Other reported central nervous system abnormalities, 
such as temporal lobe malformations, corpus callosum

Perinatal lethality is also observed in other FGFR3-re-
lated chondrodysplasias, as it is common in homozy -
gous achondroplasia 1,9 and rare, but reported, in 
heterozygous achondroplasia. 22 SADDAN syndrome 
(severe achondroplasia with developmental delay and 
acanthosis nigricans), although severe, does not usually 
result in death at birth, except in cases requiring but not 
receiving ventilatory support. 1 Additionally, isolated 
reports describe patients with a TD-like phenotype whose 
molecular diagnosis corresponded to Pfeiffer syndrome 
type 223 or cartilage-hair hypoplasia/anauxetic dysplasia 
spectrum (autosomal recessive). 24 This highlights the 
importance of confirming the molecular diagnosis in all 
cases to provide appropriate genetic counseling.
In this study, the most frequent variants were p.Arg -
248Cys and p.Ser371Cys, which account for approxi -
mately 90% of TD1 cases, whereas p.Gly370Cys and 
p.Tyr373Cys variants are reported in <  2% of cases. 4,7 
Although all of these variants are associated with the 
classic TD1 phenotype, the p.Tyr373Cys variant has been 
associated with a more severe phenotype, as observed 
in patient I (Figure 1), in whom micromelia was more pro-
nounced.4 The negative molecular result in patient D illus-
trates one of the limitations of our study, as full sequencing 
of the gene was not performed in that case.
Conclusions
The prevalence of TD in our setting was 0.85 per 
10,000 live births. All cases corresponded to TD1, and 
no cases of TD2 were identified. We confirm that pre -
natal findings, physical examination at birth, and 
radiographic evaluation allow accurate diagnosis of 
TD in most cases. However, the importance of obtain -
ing molecular confirmation in all cases is emphasized 
to exclude other lethal skeletal dysplasias with over -
lapping phenotypes.
