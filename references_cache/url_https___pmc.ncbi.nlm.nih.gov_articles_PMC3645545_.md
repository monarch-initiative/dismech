</div></section></section><section><section><section><h2>Abstract</h2>
<p>The sense of hearing is remarkable for its auditory dynamic range, which spans more than 10<sup>12</sup> in acoustic intensity. The mechanisms that enable the cochlea to transduce high sound levels without damage are of key interest, particularly with regard to the broad impact of industrial, military, and recreational auditory overstimulation on hearing disability. We show that ATP-gated ion channels assembled from P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor subunits in the cochlea are necessary for the development of temporary threshold shift (TTS), evident in auditory brainstem response recordings as sound levels rise. In mice null for the <em>P2RX2</em> gene (encoding the P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor subunit), sustained 85-dB noise failed to elicit the TTS that wild-type (WT) mice developed. ATP released from the tissues of the cochlear partition with elevation of sound levels likely activates the broadly distributed P2X<sub>2</sub> receptors on epithelial cells lining the endolymphatic compartment. This purinergic signaling is supported by significantly greater noise-induced suppression of distortion product otoacoustic emissions derived from outer hair cell transduction and decreased suprathreshold auditory brainstem response input/output gain in WT mice compared with <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice. At higher sound levels (≥95 dB), additional processes dominated TTS, and <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice were more vulnerable than WT mice to permanent hearing loss due to hair cell synapse disruption. <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice lacked ATP-gated conductance across the cochlear partition, including loss of ATP-gated inward current in hair cells. These data indicate that a significant component of TTS represents P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor-dependent purinergic hearing adaptation that underpins the upper physiological range of hearing.</p>
<section><p><strong>Keywords:</strong> noise-induced hearing loss, acoustic overstimulation, permanent threshold shift, auditory neurotransmission, sound transduction</p></section></section><hr/>
<p>Sensory systems are characterized by adaptation processes that sustain transduction as stimulus intensity increases. The mammalian auditory system operates across an acoustic power range of ~120 dB, measured on the logarithmic decibel scale. The mechanism for the extraordinary acuity of the cochlea (recalling the age-old adage of “hearing a pin drop”) arises from the commitment of 75% of the sensory hair cells, the outer hair cells, to electromechanical (reverse) transduction, driving a “cochlear amplifier.” The nonlinear outer hair cell reverse transduction provides an ∼40-dB gain at hearing threshold, reducing to zero as sound levels rise (<a>1</a>). A major challenge for auditory physiology is to understand how hearing is preserved in the face of acoustic overstimulation, as noise can damage the cochlea and can greatly exacerbate hearing loss with aging (<a>2</a>). Given the recent propensity for direct delivery of high-level recreational sound to the ear canals by personal music players and, more broadly, the impact on our hearing of noise from industrial and military environments, there is an imperative to better understand the intrinsic mechanisms that enable the cochlea to accommodate loud sound.</p>
<p>Known mechanisms by which the cochlea adjusts its sensitivity to loud sound include the middle-ear muscle reflex and efferent feedback to the outer hair cells. The middle-ear muscle reflex (<a>3</a>) is largely driven by vocalization or intense low-frequency sound, and fatigues after a few minutes. Efferent neuronal adaptation is even more rapid (milliseconds to seconds) and provides dynamic modulation that enables the cochlea to unmask sounds of attentive interest from background noise. The olivocochlear efferent system is to some extent otoprotective against noise damage (<a>4</a>), and contributes to “conditioning,” where sustained moderate sound exposure toughens the cochlea against subsequent acoustic overstimulation (<a>5</a>). However, this efferent feedback to the cochlea rapidly adapts at sound levels well below safe upper hearing limits (85 dB LAeq [equivalent continuous A-weighted sound pressure level (dB)]), as reflected in workplace legislation (<a>6</a>). In this study, we investigated the hypothesis that purinergic signaling contributes to cochlear adaptation to elevated sound levels and protection from overstimulation. A complementary report by Yan et al. (<a>7</a>) shows that a dominant-negative point mutation in the ATP-gated ion channel P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor subunit underlies the autosomal-dominant nonsyndromic progressive hearing loss locus DFNA41. This study included data using the P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor-encoding gene knockout (<em>P2RX2</em>-null) mouse model, which demonstrated that noise exposure over a significant fraction of the animals’ life caused selective high-frequency hearing loss.</p>
<p>The P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor is abundantly expressed by cells lining the cochlear partition, including the sensory hair cells of the organ of Corti, Reissner’s membrane epithelial cells, and spiral ganglion neurons (<a>8</a>–<a>10</a>). The cochlear partition maintains the positive endocochlear potential (EP; ∼+100 mV) that, along with the negative membrane potential of the hair cells, provides the driving force for sound transduction (<a>11</a>). Both EP and the hair cell membrane potential are reduced by activation of P2X<sub>2</sub>-like ATP-gated nonselective cation channels (<a>12</a>, <a>13</a>). In the guinea pig cochlea, noise stress causes release of ATP into the K<sup>+</sup>-rich endolymphatic compartment, where P2X<sub>2</sub> receptors are concentrated (<a>14</a>). Thus, a role for ATP regulation of cochlear function via P2X<sub>2</sub> receptors is well-established at the cellular and tissue level, but analysis of the potential contribution of this pathway to the regulation of hearing has previously lacked an appropriate animal model. Here we provide evidence that as the sound floor is elevated, ATP is released into the cochlear partition, activating P2X<sub>2</sub> receptors, which reduces sound transduction and synaptic transmission from the hair cells. This purinergic regulation of hearing sensitivity was revealed by the absence of temporary threshold shift (TTS) in <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice. The mechanism is otoprotective, as <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice are highly vulnerable to noise-induced hearing loss with more extensive acoustic overstimulation.</p>
<section><h2>Moderate Noise TTS Is P2X<sub>2</sub> Receptor-Dependent</h2>
<p>Auditory brainstem response (ABR) thresholds to tone pips (4–32 kHz) were initially compared between <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice and background wild-type (WT) strain (C57BL/6J) mice at 6 mo of age. Baseline thresholds were comparable (<a>Fig. S1</a>). Remarkably, the <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice lacked the pronounced increase in threshold (loss of hearing sensitivity) to a moderately high, closed-field noise exposure [30 min; 85 decibel sound pressure level (dB SPL); 8–16 kHz band-pass noise] evident in the WT controls when measured using click and 16-kHz tone pip stimuli within ∼30 min of the noise exposure (<a>Fig. 1 <em>A</em> and <em>B</em></a>). The difference in mean thresholds post- versus prenoise in WT mice was highly significant (<em>P &lt;</em> 0.001, ranked ANOVA with Holm–Sidak, all pairwise multiple comparisons; <em>n</em> = 9), whereas <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice thresholds were not affected by the noise exposure (<em>P &gt;</em> 0.05; <em>n</em> = 7). TTS was calculated by subtracting the immediate postnoise threshold from the prenoise (baseline) threshold for each mouse. The difference between <em>P2RX2-</em>null and WT TTS was correspondingly highly significant (<em>P &lt;</em> 0.001; WT TTS vs. <em>P2RX2-</em>null TTS: click, <em>P =</em> 0.007 and 16 kHz, <em>P =</em> 0.002). TTS in WT mice measured by click stimulus was 10.6 ± 1.4 dB (<em>P &lt;</em> 0.001, single-sample <em>t</em> test) compared with 2.1 ± 0.9 dB (<em>P =</em> 0.045) in <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice; WT 16-kHz TTS was 15.6 ± 4.8 dB (<em>P =</em> 0.012) compared with 1.4 ± 2.6 dB (<em>P =</em> 0.596) in <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice.</p>

<em>P2RX2-</em>Null Mice Have Greater Hearing Loss in High-Level Noise</h2>
<p>We titrated the noise level to determine the contribution of cochlear P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor signaling to TTS and permanent threshold shift (PTS) at higher sound levels, levels that would be expected to induce more extensive TTS, but no PTS, in WT mice. Noise at 95 dB SPL for 30 min (8–16 kHz octave band, closed-field) caused substantial immediate threshold shifts in strain-matched WT mice (<em>n</em> = 11) (≥45 dB from 16 kHz) as well as <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice (<em>n</em> = 11) (10- to 13-wk-old) (<a>Fig. 3<em>A</em></a>). This PTS was associated with a significantly greater immediate DPOAE threshold shift in <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice compared with WT (<a>Fig. S6</a>; <em>P =</em> 0.026, 16–28 kHz, two-way ANOVA), suggesting that in the absence of P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor activation at this high noise level, outer hair cells located basal to the tonotopic place for the noise band are overdriven.</p>

<p><em>P2RX2-</em>null mice failed to exhibit the initial 15 dB of threshold shift produced when sound level was elevated. WT mice developed this TTS with a 20-min time constant. The impact of moderate noise on cochlear function was also reflected in the substantial decrease in suprathreshold ABR amplitude in WT mice compared with the preserved gain of the ABR input/output function of <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice. Recovery from the P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor-dependent TTS had a 12-h time constant, which is similar to the situation with human subjects exposed to TTS-level sound (<a>25</a>). In the absence of P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor signaling, the cochlea was more vulnerable to damage from excessive noise, evident as PTS in the <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice at the higher presented sound levels. Given that perceived loudness halves every 10 dB and sound intensity halves every 3 dB, this P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor-dependent hearing modulation provides a substantial downward adjustment of the operational point of sound transduction and auditory neurotransmission in the cochlea at high sustained sound levels. These characteristics provide strong support for the postulate that sound-induced activation of P2X<sub>2</sub> receptors in the cochlea underlies a local autocrine/paracrine purinergic adaptation mechanism that sustains the upper physiological range of hearing.</p>
<p>Although it is appreciated that TTS (reversible within hours to days) and PTS are not a continuum of cochlear pathophysiology due to acoustic overstimulation, noise-induced threshold changes are multifactorial, as revealed by histological, physiological, proteomic, and transcriptional studies of the cellular and molecular responses of the cochlea to loud sound (e.g., <a>17</a>, <a>25</a>–<a>29</a>). The mechanisms underlying TTS have remained speculative, although studies suggest that oxidative stress at the inner hair cell afferent synapse is significant (<a>30</a>). Even low-level noise stress (producing TTS) can have long-term downstream consequences for hearing—evident in earlier development of hearing loss with aging (<a>31</a>).</p>
<p>The identification that purinergic signaling drives TTS as the background sound level rises is key evidence that such TTS is fundamentally adaptive. Neural regulation via the olivocochlear efferent innervation to the outer hair cells can certainly protect the cochlea from noise-induced hearing loss (<a>5</a>, <a>32</a>), but the kinetics for efferent regulation of cochlear function, such as contralateral suppression via the medial olivocochlear bundle, have fast time constants (<a>4</a>) inconsistent with TTS. The most parsimonious explanation arising from our study is that P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor activation, as a result of noise-induced ATP release in the cochlea, represents the primary upstream signaling element causing the dominant component of TTS at moderate sustained noise levels, and this reflects physiological adaptation to noise rather than an injury response. Certainly, the preservation of hearing sensitivity in the face of sustained 85-dB noise exposure in <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice attests to the lack of immediate tissue damage as an underlying cause of the TTS in WT mice at this sound level. This adaptation process would enable cochlear hair cells to encode the upper component of the physiological hearing range in background noise without damage. A potential caveat to our results is that the <em>P2RX2</em>-null mice lacked P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor expression in the cochlea from early development. However, underlying hearing physiology was normal (<a>Fig. S1</a>; see also ref. <a>7</a>). Older <em>P2RX2</em>-null mice do exhibit selective hearing loss, evident as exacerbated presbycusis compared with wild-type controls at 18 mo of age raised in the quiet (<a>7</a>).</p>
<p>The mechanism of the P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor-dependent reduction in hearing sensitivity surely involves the movement of cations (including a high Ca<sup>2+</sup> conductance) through P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor homomeric ATP-gated channels (<a>24</a>). At a tissue level, it has been shown that release of ATP into the endolymphatic compartment causes a rapid shunt of K<sup>+</sup> entering the ATP-gated ion channels (<a>12</a>) (confirmed here as exclusively P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor-mediated), which, via the associated fall in EP, and parallel depolarization of the hair cells, would contribute to regulation of sound transduction. However, extracellular ATP is rapidly hydrolyzed by ecto-ATPases (<a>33</a>). Because TTS arising from 30 min of 85-dB noise persisted for hours, well beyond the likely period of elevated extracellular ATP, a more sustained effect must underlie P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor-dependent TTS. A clue may be found in the observation that outer hair cell electromotility (reverse transduction) in WT mice was compromised with TTS (DPOAE threshold was elevated and DPOAE amplitude was suppressed; <a>Figs. S2</a> and <a>S3</a>), alongside the increase in ABR threshold (decreased neural output). EP recovers rapidly after cessation of noise, and direct action of K<sup>+</sup> efflux is also unlikely to account for the TTS, as clearance of sound-induced K<sup>+</sup> buildup around the inner hair cells is too rapid (<a>34</a>). The hair cell transducer conductance (forward transduction) is relatively resistant to acoustic overstimulation, where, despite considerable increases in cochlear nerve thresholds (TTS), cochlear microphonic output (a population measure of outer hair cell receptor potential) is sustained (<a>35</a>). Based on reduced tuning of basilar membrane displacement associated with this TTS, it has been proposed that outer hair cell reverse transduction (rather than forward transduction) was affected (<a>35</a>). Ca<sup>2+</sup>-mediated adaptation affecting outer hair cell electromotility (<a>36</a>) and supporting cell micromechanical compliance, as well as Ca<sup>2+</sup>-regulated adaptation at the inner hair cell ribbon synapse (<a>37</a>, <a>38</a>), are all processes likely to be impacted by the distributed cochlear P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor signaling.</p>
<p>Our findings generate an apparent paradox in that moderate noise fails to affect hearing in <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice (speaking to the robust nature of forward and reverse transduction without P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor-dependent adaptation) but loud noise causes enhanced PTS in these animals. This dichotomy in sensitivity to noise in <em>P2RX2</em>-null mice can be reconciled if the TTS-level adaptation arising from noise-activated P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor signaling protects transduction and transmission at the hair cells from excessive acoustic stimulation, whereas PTS, focused at the inner hair cell–spiral ganglion synapse, arises from overdrive of these processes in the absence of this purinergic adaptation mechanism. Noise-induced glutamate excitotoxicity is well-established in the cochlea (<a>17</a>, <a>27</a>), and is correlated with loss of the spiral ganglion punctate synapses, atrophy of the neuron soma, and, presynaptically, loss and dislocation of the CtBP2-labeled ribbon synapses (<a>18</a>). All of these characteristics match the cochlear structural profile of <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice with PTS.</p>
<p>The identification of the P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor as a key element of hearing adaptation suggests that factors that compromise this process, such as age-related muting of P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor-dependent regulation of cochlear partition conductance (<a>39</a>), or polymorphisms within the <em>P2RX2</em> gene, contribute to variation in susceptibility to noise-induced hearing loss in society. Indeed, the present study clearly complements our recent report (<a>7</a>), which showed that the absence of cochlear P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor signaling in two Chinese families, attributable to a dominant-negative mutation P2RX2 c.178G&gt;T (p.V60L), removed intrinsic purinergic otoprotection from the hearing organ and precipitated the DFNA41 autosomal-dominant progressive hearing loss. DFNA41 subjects with a history of noise exposure had exacerbated high-frequency hearing loss, an effect that was modeled in <em>P2RX2</em>-null mice exposed to long-term noise exposure. In identifying a key molecular element of hearing adaptation to sustained noise stressors, the present study provides a timely reinforcement of the consequence of exceeding the capacity of our hearing organ to cope with loud sound. These experiments open a path for analysis of the mechanisms for sound-evoked ATP release into the cochlear tissues associated with this P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor signaling, including potential contributions from P2Y receptors linked to ATP release (<a>40</a>), and cellular elements that sustain dynamic adaptation to changes in sound level over a lifetime.</p></section><section><h2>Materials and Methods</h2>
<p>Additional details of methodology are provided online in <a><em>SI Materials and Methods</em></a>.</p>
<section><h3>
<em>P2RX2</em>-null mouse model.</h3>
<p>This study used <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice (<em>B6.129-P2rx2</em><sup><em>tm1Ckn</em></sup><em>/J</em>), which have previously enabled elucidation of the role of P2X<sub>2</sub> receptors in a range of sensory modalities, including pain sensation and urinary bladder reflexes (<a>41</a>). These mice, and strain-matched C57BL/6J WT mice, were obtained as breeding stock from The Jackson Laboratory. <em>P2RX2-</em>null mice and WT littermates were generated using heterozygous (<em>P2RX2</em>(<sup>+/−</sup>)) breeding pairs derived from crossing these lines. The C57BL/6J strain is commonly used for transgenic mouse line development and has been extensively studied for hearing function, including the identification of a mutation in the <em>Cdh23</em> gene that impacts age-related hearing loss (<a>42</a>). The studies were approved by the University of Auckland Animal Ethics Committee, the local animal subjects committee of the San Diego Veterans Affairs Healthcare System, and the University of New South Wales Animal Care and Ethics Committee. The mice were maintained in individually ventilated cages (VentiRack; BioZone).</p></section><section><h3>ABR and DPOAE.</h3>
<p>Sound levels are presented as dB sound pressure level (SPL). Click and puretone ABR thresholds and cubic DPOAEs (2f<sub>1</sub>-f<sub>2</sub>) were measured under ketamine/xylazine/acepromazine (K/X/A) anesthesia as previously described (<a>43</a>). P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor-dependent TTS was produced using 85 dB SPL noise (8–16 kHz or 4–32 kHz), delivered via the ear probe (closed-field). PTS was generated in conscious mice using 2-h 100-dB SPL open-field broadband noise (8–16 kHz band-pass), or under K/X/A anesthesia (4–32 kHz, 100 dB SPL, 1 h, closed-field).</p></section><section><h3>Endocochlear Potential and Cochlear Partition Resistance.</h3>
<p>EP and CoPR measurements were made under urethane anesthesia as previously described (<a>12</a>), with the minor modification of a 500-ms 1-µA current pulse at 50% duty cycle.</p></section><section><h3>Cellular Physiology.</h3>
<p>Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings from Reissner’s membrane epithelial cells and hair cells were made in situ as previously described (<a>22</a>). Cells were voltage-clamped at −60 mV and ATP pressure-applied to the cells via a glass micropipette.</p></section><section><h3>Immunolabeling.</h3>
<p>Immunofluorescence imaging of P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor expression used an anti-P2X<sub>2</sub> receptor polyclonal antiserum (Alomone). CtBP2 immunolabeling of the hair cell ribbon synapses used an anti-CtBP2 antisera (BD Biosciences). Neurofilament 200 immunolabeling of spiral ganglion neurite projections to the hair cell synapses used an anti-neurofilament 200 antibody (Sigma). After mounting, the immunolabeling was imaged using a Zeiss 710 confocal microscope.</p></section><section><h3>Data Analysis.</h3>
<p>Parametric and nonparametric data comparisons were performed using two-way ANOVA, Student <em>t</em> tests, Mann–Whitney rank-sum tests, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests after assessment of normality and variance (SigmaPlot version 11; Systat Software). Threshold for significance α = 0.05, with Holm–Sidak all pairwise multiple comparison, where appropriate. Data are presented with SE (SEM).</p></section></section><section><h2>Supplementary Material</h2>
