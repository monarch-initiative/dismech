BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES: Paraneoplastic neurologic syndrome (PNS) diagnostic 
criteria were first proposed in 2004 and updated in 2021. The PNS-CARE score, 
derived from the updated criteria, is a composite model for assigning likelihood 
for patients with suspected PNS. In this study, we evaluated the utility and 
applicability of the 2021 PNS-CARE score and present our PNS cohort.
METHODS: This is a retrospective study. We identified Mayo Clinic patients 
suspected to have PNS (1/2005-12/2020) and collected relevant information 
including demographics, PNS presentation, and clinical outcomes. Inclusion 
criteria were the following: (1) patients with a syndrome consistent with PNS 
and (2) patients with sufficient information available in charts. Exclusion 
criteria were the following: (1) evaluation only before 2005, (2) patients not 
evaluated by neurology, (3) presentation after immune checkpoint inhibitors, and 
(4) syndromes not included in 2021 criteria. All patients were evaluated for the 
2021 and 2004 PNS criteria.
RESULTS: We identified 484 patients suspected to have PNS at initial 
presentation, of whom 212 (44%) were considered to have PNS after completion of 
evaluation. Among these 212 patients, the most common autoantibodies were PCA1 
(Yo)-IgG (17%), KLHL11-IgG (16%), and CRMP5-IgG (14%) and the most common 
phenotypes were rapidly progressive cerebellar syndrome (29%), brainstem 
encephalitis (14%), and limbic encephalitis (8%). The 2021 PNS criteria 
definite/probable categorization (PNS-CARE score ≥ 6) had a sensitivity and 
specificity of 93% and 100%, respectively, while the 2004 PNS criteria definite 
categorization had a sensitivity and specificity of 67% and 99%, respectively. 
We found 15 patients with a PNS-CARE score ≤5 who likely had PNS on our review. 
The most common presentation among these patients was KLHL11-IgG brainstem 
encephalitis (7/15, 47%) with likely burned-out testicular tumor.
DISCUSSION: Our study validates the PNS-CARE score. A clearer understanding of 
typical PNS presentation and common underlying malignancies and autoantibodies 
can aid in earlier and more accurate diagnosis, which is crucial for downstream 
clinical decisions. Some patients with an intermediate-risk phenotype do not 
meet probable/definite criteria despite the presence of high-risk antibodies 
and/or underlying malignancy.

Paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes (PNSs) are a diverse group of immune-mediated neurologic disorders associated with systemic neoplasms.1 Diagnosis of these patients can often be difficult because of varied neurologic presentation and the broad differential for neurologic manifestations other than paraneoplastic autoimmunity including direct tumor infiltration, side effects of chemotherapy, increased risk of infections secondary to immunosuppressive nature of chemotherapy, toxic-metabolic derangements from cancers or their therapies, and/or a combination thereof. Because of this, expert consensus diagnostic criteria can serve as a valuable tool for clinical/neurologic practice.

The first proposal of PNS diagnostic criteria was published in 2004.2 This criterion attempted to propose possible or definite PNS diagnosis based on a combination of the presenting syndrome, underlying malignancy, and well-established neural-specific antibodies. Our understanding of PNS has advanced significantly in the past 20 years, with the identification of numerous new autoantibodies and improved understanding of the association between specific malignancies, syndromes, and relevant antibodies.3-7 Thus, in 2019, a group of international experts (PNS-CARE panel) convened to develop revised diagnostic criteria, later published in 2021 incorporating a new clinical scoring system (PNS-CARE Score).8 We sought to evaluate the validity of the 2021 PNS-CARE diagnostic criteria and describe our experience with PNS at the Mayo Clinic hospitals and health system.

This study was approved by the Mayo Clinic institutional review board. All patients consented to the use of their medical records for research purposes.

This was a retrospective study performed between January 2005 and December 2020 at 3 Mayo Clinic sites (Minnesota, Florida, or Arizona) with data identified from 2 sources. Through our neuroimmunology database, we first identified patients with a known high-risk autoantibody per 2021 diagnostic criteria. Next, we searched Advanced Cohort Explorer, an electronic retrieval system that interrogates the electronic medical record, with key words for paraneoplastic evaluation (Figure 1). Our inclusion criteria were the following: (1) patients whose records indicated a clinical presentation consistent with a paraneoplastic disease at presentation and (2) patients with sufficient clinical documentation for chart extraction. We excluded patients (1) who were only evaluated by a Mayo Clinic neurologist before 2005; (2) who were not evaluated by neurology in their disease course; (3) whose presentation was believed to be secondary to immune checkpoint inhibitors; and (4) whose syndromes were not included in the 2021 criteria (for example, dermatomyositis and cancer-associated retinopathy). Details of our patient identification process are presented in Figure 1.

Search key words included the following: paraneoplastic neuropathy; paraneoplastic antibody level; paraneoplastic encephalomyelitis; paraneoplastic peripheral neuropathy; paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration; paraneoplastic autonomic dysfunction; paraneoplastic antibody; paraneoplastic neuromyopathy and neuropathy; perineoplastic/paraneoplastic; paraneoplastic/perineoplastic condition; autoimmune encephalopathy; and myopathy autoimmune non-inflammatory. We excluded phenotypes that were not included by the updated 2021 article, including inflammatory myopathies (dermatomyositis, polymyositis, and necrotizing myopathies); myasthenia gravis; polyneuropathies associated with monoclonal gammopathies; and paraneoplastic retinopathy, optic neuritis, and cochlea-vestibulopathy.8

We collected data on demographics (age, sex, and ethnicity) and clinical characteristics including presenting neurologic syndrome, associated malignancy (if any), and autoantibody found (if any); treatments; and both neurologic and cancer outcomes.

All testing was historically performed in the Mayo Clinic Neuroimmunology Laboratory by standardized methodologies and was available through the medical records.9,10 First, serum samples of patients were tested by indirect tissue-based immunofluorescence on composite substrate of the adult mouse cerebellum, midbrain, basal ganglia, thalamus, cerebral cortex, hippocampus, stomach, and kidneys to detect IgG autoantibodies.111213 When a characteristic staining pattern was recognized, an antigen-specific confirmatory testing was performed. Amphiphysin and collapsin response mediator protein 5 (CRMP5)-IgG were confirmed by Western blot analysis using full-length recombinant human proteins.11,14 Kelch-like protein 11 (KLHL11)-IgG autoantibody was confirmed by the HEK293 cell overexpression assay.3,15 Radioimmunoprecipitation assays were used for glutamic acid decarboxylase 62 (GAD65) and P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channel (CCPQ) autoantibodies.161718 Anti-neuronal nuclear antibody (ANNA)1 (Hu), ANNA2 (Ri), ANNA3, Purkinje cell antibody (PCA)1 (Yo), PCA2, PCA-Tr, and SRY-Box transcription factor 1 (SOX1)-IgG, also known as anti-glial nuclear antibody, autoantibodies were confirmed by commercial protein blots.16 N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor (NMDAR), α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptor (AMPAR), γ-aminobutyric acid-B receptor (GABABR), glycine receptor (GlyR), and contactin-associated protein-like 2 (CASPR2)-IgG autoantibodies were confirmed by cell-based assays on HEK293 cells.19,20

Many patients who were initially evaluated for PNS did not ultimately have a syndrome consistent with PNS. Examples of these include length-dependent peripheral neuropathies ultimately attributed to chemotherapy and encephalopathies ultimately attributed to toxic metabolic encephalopathies. Because there is no gold standard for PNS diagnosis, all patients were reviewed and diagnosed to have PNS (PNS+) by 3 neurologists (H.H.Z., M.R., and D.D.) and the original evaluating neurologist who saw them in the clinical setting. The diagnosis of PNS began with reasonable exclusion of the wide differential of neurologic manifestation of malignancies and their treatments. We then evaluated each patient based on neurologic syndrome, paraclinical studies (including antibody, MRI features, and CSF results), and an expected malignancy. In rare cases of disagreement, we reached a consensus through majority agreement after review by 2 additional co-authors (A.Z., P.G.). Subsequently, patients were categorized by both the 2004 and 2021 criteria.

Next, we identified commonly associated malignancies in our cohort (Figure 2B). We found that the most associated malignancies were small-cell lung carcinoma (17%), breast adenocarcinoma (14%), and seminoma (13%), followed by ovarian adenocarcinoma (10%), non–small-cell lung carcinoma (6%), lymphoma (5%), thymoma (2%), and GI cancers (2%). We found 15 patients in whom malignancy was strongly suspected, but not found. These were most commonly patients with KLHL11-IgG whose orchiectomy showed atrophic fibrous changes and/or calcification/microlithiasis suspicious for burned-out germ cell tumor (n = 10). In 5 patients, lung cancer was suspected but workup was either declined by the patient or the patient was lost to follow-up. We also found 4 patients in whom a biopsy of local mass or lymph node showed sarcoma or carcinoma, but a primary source was not found. Gender stratification of our cohort revealed that the most common malignancies in women were breast adenocarcinoma and ovarian adenocarcinoma while seminoma was common in men (Figure 2B).

The most common presenting syndromes were rapidly progressive cerebellar syndrome (29%), brainstem encephalitis (14%), and limbic encephalitis (8%, Figure 2C). In our PNS+ cohort, 16 patients were diagnosed with multiple syndromes. Three patients were diagnosed with both brainstem encephalitis and limbic encephalitis, 7 patients were diagnosed with rapidly progressive cerebellar syndrome and another syndrome (2 encephalomyelitis, 3 brainstem encephalitis, 1 limbic encephalitis and sensory neuronopathy, and 1 stiff-person syndrome), 5 patients had GI pseudo-obstruction with another syndrome (2 Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome [LEMS], 1 brainstem encephalitis, 1 myeloneuropathy, and 1 sensory neuronopathy), and 1 additional patient had cerebellar ataxia/LEMS. We also found 17 patients with other syndromes in low frequencies including motor neuron disease (n = 2), autonomic neuropathy (n = 2), LEMS (n = 2), cranial neuropathy (n = 1), Morvan syndrome (n = 1), neuromyotonia (n = 1), and others. Gender stratification revealed that women most commonly had rapidly progressive cerebellar syndrome while men most commonly had brainstem encephalitis (Figure 2C). We looked at our whole cohort of 484 patients, with disease positive as patients who were deemed PNS+ based on our evaluation (Methods section provides further details on how patients were determined to be PNS+). For the 2004 criteria, we defined “definite” as a test positive, yielding a sensitivity of 67% and specificity of 99%. For the 2021 criteria, when we used “definite” as a test positive, the sensitivity was 55% with 100% specificity. When we combined “definite” and “probable” as test positive, the sensitivity was 93% and the specificity continued to be 100%. The area under the curve for the 2021 criteria was 0.99 (95% CI 0.992 to 0.999) by ROC analysis when both “definite” and “probable” were combined as test positive. In this study, we present our experience applying both the 2004 and 2021 PNS criteria among patients evaluated for PNS. Of our cohort of 484 patients included in the study, we found 212 patients believed to have PNS by autoimmune neurology experts at Mayo Clinic, a tertiary center with expertise in diagnosing and treating patients with neurologic autoimmunity. The demographics of our whole cohort and patients with PNS were similar. In our cohort, because specificity was already quite high for the 2004 criteria (99%), the 2021 criteria did not significantly improve specificity (100%). However, because of inclusion of several new autoantibodies and syndromes described since the publication of 2004 criteria, sensitivity was dramatically improved (93% vs 67%). The most common antibody specificities encountered were PCA1 (Yo)-IgG, KLHL11-IgG, and CRMP5-IgG. The most common cancers were small-cell lung cancer, breast adenocarcinoma, and seminoma.

When comparing the 2 criteria, one of the most prominent changes was the inclusion of several antibodies discovered after the publication of the 2004 criteria. This includes SOX1-IgG in 2005,21 NMDAR-IgG in 2007,22 AMPAR-IgG in 2009,23,24 GABABR-IgG in 2010,4,25 CASPR2-IgG in association with Morvan syndrome in 2012,5 and KLHL11-IgG in 2019.3,15 Other antibodies, such as P/Q voltage-gated calcium channel IgG, were re-visited. This antibody was found to be associated with malignancy in 50% of patients with LEMS 26 and even a higher proportion of patients with rapidly progressive cerebellar syndrome and,27 therefore, was included as an intermediate-risk antibody.

The updated criteria also emphasized the importance of autoantibody testing methodology because certain techniques and autoantibodies are prone to generate false-positive results. In many laboratories, paraneoplastic autoantibody detection starts with screening with commercial immunoblot assays. However, these assays can generate both false-positive28 and false-negative results29 for specific autoantibodies. A study evaluating the diagnostic yield of commercial immunoblot assay28 demonstrated that only 30%–36% of positive assay results could be orthogonally confirmed by an independent assay such as indirect murine tissue immunofluorescence assay.28 Most patients that were negative on orthogonal assays did not have underlying tumor, and the clinical phenotype was also incongruent.

As more antibodies are inevitably discovered, more entries into the high-risk or intermediate-risk autoantibody categories are not only possible, but likely. When this scenario arises, the novel antibody should be carefully validated and characterized regarding the expected clinical syndrome and demonstration of a high association with cancer. In addition, demonstration of the autoantigen expression in the tumor is needed for establishing potential paraneoplastic pathogenesis.

Certain nonclassical syndromes in the 2004 criteria were excluded from the 2021 criteria. For example, Guillain-Barre syndrome, brachial neuritis or neuropathy with vasculitis, subacute/chronic sensorimotor neuropathies, and motor neuron disease were not included as PNS phenotypes in the 2021 criteria. Of note, dermatomyositis that was considered a classical syndrome in the 2004 criteria was excluded altogether in the 2021 criteria because of presence of alternative diagnostic criteria.30 However, axonal polyradiculoneuropathy and encephalitis other than well-defined LE, such as anti-NMDAR encephalitis or anti-GABAAR encephalitis, were included in the intermediate-risk category (as long as they fulfill the criteria for autoimmune encephalitis).8

Compared with the 2004 criteria, the PNS-CARE score of the 2021 criteria provided an objective scoring system that can potentially make categorization of patients with PNS more replicable. The 2004 criteria required the clinician to categorize based on subjective determinations. Examples of this include the following: (1) in the absence of a tumor or onconeural antibody in a patient presenting with a classical syndrome, a clinician has to determine whether there is high risk of cancer to determine whether it is possible or not the patient has PNS; (2) in the presence of a nonclassical neurologic syndrome with a tumor, the clinician has to determine whether the symptoms improved with treatment of the malignancy. This flexibility reduces the accuracy of the criteria to the clinical expertise of the clinician—an experienced practitioner may accurately apply the criteria, whereas an inexperienced practitioner may not. By contrast, the 2021 criteria use the PNS-CARE score, which assigns a numerical value to different aspects of the patient—syndrome, malignancy, and antibody—and categorizes the patient as PNS or not based on objective findings, minimizing user error.

Most common cancers identified in our cohort were similar to what have been previously reported in paraneoplastic neurologic disorders,31,32 with small-cell lung carcinoma and germ cell tumors being most common. A malignancy was not discovered in only 22% (46/213) of our PNS+ group. In 15 of these patients, malignancy was highly suspected. Subsequent workup most commonly revealed evidence of burned-out testicular tumor, and the patient was lost to follow-up or declined workup because of rapidly declining clinical course. When able, thorough malignancy screening sequentially is recommended for at least the first 2 years after neurologic presentation in patients highly suspicious for PNS.8

A small subset (4% of PNS+ patients and 2% of total cohort) had pathology suspicious for burned-out germ cell tumor in conjunction with KLHL11-IgG rhombencephalitis. This is consistent with what has been previously reported.3 Although previous studies have demonstrated that in some patients, evidence of metastatic seminoma can be found in lymph nodes despite regression of the primary tumor,33 we did not see these patients in our cohort. In these patients we would be inclined to score them for the cancer portion of the PNS-CARE score, thus fulfilling the “definite” categorization. In future iterations of the criteria, an intermediate score can be proposed for these scenarios where burned-out germ cell tumors have been found in association with KLHL11-IgG or Ma2-IgG seropositivity.

It is important to note that we stratified our cohort by presenting PNS, providing detailed description of our experience with commonly found antibodies, malignancies, and outcomes. We also provided typical MRI changes than can be found with different autoantibody presentations. Not all clinicians tasked with management of PNS have easy access to antibody diagnostics, and this is especially true of clinicians practicing in resource-poor regions of the world. An understanding of potential malignancies to look for with a characteristic paraneoplastic neurologic syndrome, especially with typical MRI findings, is helpful in further diagnostics and patient counseling.

Potential limitations of this study include the retrospective nature of the study. In addition, because there currently is no “gold standard” for diagnosis of PNS, our PNS+ cohort is based on evaluation by neurologists with expertise in diagnosis of these disorders. The patients in this group are categorized with varying degrees of certainty, ranging from high certainty in patients with well-characterized syndrome, antibody, and malignancy combinations, to moderate certainty when one or more of the categories is missing or atypical. Although we included all patients suspected with PNS in our whole cohort, we did exclude patients whose syndromes were not included in the 2021 diagnostic criteria. Because of this, our data may be affected by verification bias, in particular with the high specificity. Another major limitation of the study is the referral bias at Mayo Clinic. As a tertiary referral center, our experience is biased towards previously undiagnosed patients or those with conditions that our group helped describe.3,15 This bias is likely at least partially responsible for our relatively high percentage of KLHL11-IgG brainstem encephalitis patients. Our group has previously published our experience with frequency of various autoantibodies in autoimmune encephalitis.20 Other centers have also recently published autoantibody frequencies.34 The referral bias could also be contributing, at least in part, to certain syndromes, such as limbic encephalitis, and autoantibodies, such as ANNA1 (Hu)-IgG, being underrepresented in our cohort compared with published epidemiologic data.12,35-37 In addition, cases may have been overlooked during electronic medical record searches because of inaccurate diagnostic code associations, which could potentially affect the observed frequencies of autoantibodies and cancers in our cohort. Future epidemiologic studies and/or multicenter studies would be helpful to elucidate more accurate disease and autoantibody frequencies. Because the 2004 criteria are 2-level criteria (possible and definite) and the 2021 criteria are 3-level criteria (possible, probable, and definite), there are some limitations in direct comparison of the 2. To address this, we have in practice converted the 2021 criteria into 2-level criteria by combining “definite” and “probable” into the same category when performing our sensitivity/specificity analysis. Finally, an important consideration in the era of immune checkpoint inhibitors is the possible autoimmune neurologic conditions that can result from these medications. In this study, we excluded patients whose syndromes were believed to be likely secondary to immune checkpoint inhibitors, but these patients are also important to study for the future.

Overall, we studied a large cohort of potentially PNS patients, using both the 2004 and 2021 PNS-CARE criteria. Our study validates the utility and applicability of the PNS-CARE score. The 2021 PNS-CARE model performed well with much improved sensitivity compared with the 2004 criteria without much decrease in specificity. Further iterations of PNS diagnostics can focus on certain intermediate-risk phenotypes that do not fulfill probable/definite criteria despite having high-risk antibodies and/or underlying malignancies. We also outlined our experience in PNS neurologic presentations and their common associated malignancies and autoantibodies. Understanding these associations could lead clinicians to better diagnose and treat PNS.
