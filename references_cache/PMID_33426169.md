# Secondary Dystonia in a Novel Mitochondriopathy Responsive to Deep Brain Stimulation Therapy.
**Authors:** Sharma VD, Buetefisch CM, Kendall FD, Gross RE, DeLong MR, Juncos JL
**Journal:** Mov Disord Clin Pract (2021)
**DOI:** [10.1002/mdc3.13075](https://doi.org/10.1002/mdc3.13075)

## Content

View Supplementary Video 1

Basal ganglia are particularly vulnerable to mitochondrial dysfunction leading to a wide range of movement disorders of which dystonia and parkinsonism are the most common.
1
 Management of these conditions remains a challenge, because their response to all available treatments is poor. MEGDEL syndrome (3‐MEthylGlutaconic aciduria, Deafness, Encephalopathy, and Leigh‐like syndrome) is one such rare mitochondriopathy that presents in early childhood.
2
,
3
 We report a patient with novel mutations in SERAC1 gene who presented with secondary dystonia and had a meaningful and sustained improvement in her condition with bilateral deep brain stimulation (DBS) therapy of the globus pallidus interna (GPi).

Case Report

This 16‐year‐old girl was evaluated for a neurologic syndrome with prominent dystonia. Developmentally, she presented with mild psychomotor retardation but met milestones appropriately otherwise. She was diagnosed with inattentive attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) at the age of 7 years and developed anxiety with repetitive obsessive–compulsive behaviors (OCBs) by the age of 9 years. She also had intermittent motor tics with shoulder shrugging and repetitive touching of the face. For these symptoms, she was treated with escitalopram and risperidone that controlled the OCBs and tics. Attentional problems were controlled with stimulants. By her early teens, cognitive symptoms stabilized and she maintained good school performance and socialization.

At the age of 14 years, she developed dystonic symptoms in left arm with intermittent tightness and elbow flexion. Over time, symptoms progressed with intorsion of the left foot, prominent axial symptoms (scoliosis and intermittent lateral flexion of the trunk), and ataxia (Video S1). Family history was negative for neurologic disorders in parents and siblings. Her maternal grandmother had Parkinson's disease and a seizure disorder.

Pertinent exam findings include dystonic posturing with adduction of left arm and elbow flexion, scoliosis, and intermittent but prominent rocking movements of the trunk with flexion at the waist to the left and mild torticollis (Video S1). She had fidgety‐athetoid movements throughout. Sensory exam showed diminished distal pinprick sensation in all extremities. Vibration and joint position sense were normal. She walked without assistance despite the truncal and foot dystonia that accentuated with action. Burke‐Fahn‐Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMDRS) total score was 30.5.

Work‐up including serum acylcarnitine profile, amino acids, lactate and pyruvate, and transaminases level were normal. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of brain showed T2 hyperintensity in both caudate and putamen (Fig. 1). Urine organic amino acids showed elevated 3‐methylglutaconic acid and methylglutaric acid. A methylglutaconic aciduria nuclear gene panel was ordered that showed a novel heterozygous missense mutation (R639P – p.Arg639Pro [CGT > CCT]: c.1916 G > C in exon 17) and a novel partial deletion of exons 9–10 of the SERAC1 gene.

Dystonia was treated with baclofen, trihexyphenidyl, and botulinum toxin in left arm without significant benefit. She was considered a reasonable candidate for DBS therapy and underwent bilateral intraoperative MRI‐guided GPi‐DBS lead placement without any complications. Details of the procedure have been described previously.
4
 At 1‐year DBS follow‐up assessment, she had very good improvement in truncal and the left upper extremity dystonia. The rocking (athetoid) movements of the torso resolved over time (Video S1). Her improvement is highlighted by a 2‐year follow‐up BFMDRS score of 7. Importantly, following DBS, the scoliosis did not change, and there was no worsening of cognition, attention, or school performance anxiety.

Discussion

The better known MEGDEL syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive mitochondriopathy because of mutations in SERAC 1 gene. Although the exact function of the encoded protein is not clear, it is thought to play crucial role in mitochondrial function and intracellular cholesterol trafficking.
5
 In the majority of reported cases, clinical manifestations began during the first year of life with feeding problems and truncal hypotonia. Over time they also develop psychomotor retardation, loss of motor skills, and extrapyramidal symptoms with prominent dystonia. Sensorineural hearing loss and spasticity are often also present.
2
,
3
 The diagnosis is suspected in patients with Leigh‐like neuroradiologic findings on brain MRI involving basal ganglia and is confirmed by elevated urinary concentration of 3‐methylglutaconic acid and 3‐methyl glutaric acid plus the identification of a pathogenic mutation in SERAC1 gene.
2
,
3

Our patient developed prominent hemi‐dystonia as a teenager with associated slow athetoid movements of extremities and rocking, thrusting movements of the torso. These movements started before use of risperidone and did not improve after a month off the drug. There were no other signs to suggest Tardive dyskinesia (eg, tongue movements and finger chorea). Any further attempt to eliminate risperidone resulted in a worsening of tics, OCBs, and anxiety. Reductions in the dose of stimulants also produced no change in the movements and caused worsening of attention and school performance. It remains unclear whether the neuropsychiatric symptoms were directly related to the mitochondrial dysfunction. The phenotype of this case appears to be a milder variant of MEGDEL syndrome. Compared to the reported cases, our patient had delayed onset of dystonia, no significant developmental delays, and no deafness. The imaging findings were consistent with Leigh disease‐like, and the biochemical findings and genetic tests were consistent MEGDEL syndrome.

We report novel mutations in the SERAC1 gene that were considered pathogenic. Finally, this is the first reported DBS intervention in MEGDEL syndrome and one of the few that have been reported in patients with other mitochondriopathies and with variable responses (Table 1).
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
 Consideration for DBS therapy in these disorders is difficult because of the scarcity of outcome literature, particularly in patients with multisystem involvement. From a surgical standpoint, targeting GPi can be challenging because of the common involvement of basal ganglia in these disorders. Our patient had a significant improvement in her dystonia and athetoid movements with GPi‐DBS therapy. Although the reported results are limited by the case format and lack of controls, it provides a measure of hope and realistic expectations in carefully selected cases with mitochondriopathies and movement disorder that retain good cognition into adolescence and beyond. This report further expands the genotype–phenotype spectrum of MEGDEL syndrome.
