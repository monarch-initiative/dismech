# Unexpectedly high levels of normally spliced transcripts from the pathogenic SLC10A7 alleles in a recessive form of skeletal dysplasia.
**Authors:** Zhao XC, Zhang ZC, Ye WL, Ye YY, Wang LT, Zheng XQ, Chang YB, Chen C
**Journal:** HGG Adv (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.xhgg.2025.100545](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xhgg.2025.100545)

## Content

Bi-allelic loss-of-function (LoF) variants in SLC10A7 cause short stature, amelogenesis imperfecta, and skeletal dysplasia with scoliosis (SSASKS). Here, we report findings from an individual of Chinese ancestry with SSASKS carrying compound heterozygous splice-altering SLC10A7 variants: a previously reported pathogenic variant (NM_001029998.6:c.722-16A>G, paternal) and a de novo splice site variant (NM_001029998.6:c.472-1G>T, maternal). In silico predictions, minigene assays, and analyses of RNA and protein from the affected individual revealed that c.472-1G>T causes in-frame exon 7 skipping and c.722-16A>G induces out-of-frame exon 9 skipping. RNA sequencing of blood-derived cells showed that ∼32% residual SLC10A7 function in the affected individual, consistent with the 43% protein accumulation observed by western blot analysis of muscle tissue. These findings indicate that a previously presumed complete LoF allele instead results in partial LoF, prompting a refinement of the genotype-phenotype framework for SLC10A7-related SSASKS. This study highlights the challenges of predicting partial LoF effects, the value of RNA and protein analyses from affected individual-derived tissues, and the importance of distinguishing partial from complete LoF variants in the diagnosis and counseling of recessive disorders.

In 2018, bi-allelic loss-of-function (LoF) variants in SLC10A7 (solute carrier family 10 member 7 [MIM: 611459]) were identified as the genetic basis of a rare skeletal dysplasia syndrome known as short stature, amelogenesis imperfecta, and skeletal dysplasia with scoliosis (SSASKS [MIM: 618363]).1,2 This disorder is primarily characterized by disproportionate short stature, enamel hypoplasia, skeletal dysplasia with progressive scoliosis, along with variable additional features such as facial dysmorphism, moderate hearing loss, and mild intellectual disability.

SLC10A7, located on chromosome 4q31.2, comprises 12 exons3 and is broadly expressed.3,4,5 Its representative isoform, GenBank: NM_001029998.6—the originally characterized 12-exon transcript3 and the only isoform conserved between human and mouse4—encodes a 340-amino-acid transmembrane protein involved in calcium homeostasis and glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis. Functional disruption of SLC10A7 impairs intracellular calcium signaling and extracellular matrix organization, contributing to the multisystemic manifestations of SSASKS.1,2,5,6,7,8 Since 2018, only nine pathogenic SLC10A7 variants have been reported: two nonsense, four missense, and three splice-altering variants.1,2,7,9 These variants have generally been considered to cause complete or near-complete LoF of the affected allele—supporting the notion that SSASKS arises from substantial SLC10A7 deficiency.

Here, we report findings from an individual of Han Chinese ancestry with compound heterozygous splice-altering SLC10A7 variants. Notably, RNA analyses using material derived from the affected individual revealed splicing outcomes that differed from those obtained through minigene assays. In particular, two splice site variants—NM_001029998.6:c.722–16A>G, classified as pathogenic in ClinVar (accessed May 19, 2025) and NM_001029998.6:c.472–1G>T—were found to result in approximately 32% normally spliced transcripts. This observation, together with supporting evidence from protein analysis in the same individual, suggests that relatively high residual levels of normal SLC10A7 function may still be compatible with disease manifestation in SLC10A7-related SSASKS.

A boy of Han Chinese ancestry was referred to the Spine Department at Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital at age 9.5 years for evaluation of progressive scoliosis (Cobb angle >80°) and localized kyphosis (55°). In addition to these spinal deformities, he exhibited clinical features consistent with SSASKS (see case report in supplemental information and Figure S1).

Exome sequencing identified two rare heterozygous variants in SLC10A7. The first, NM_001029998.6:c.722–16A>G (chr4:146292996T>C [GRCh38]), was inherited from the father (Figure 1A). This variant lies immediately upstream of the 13-bp polypyrimidine tract (PPT) in intron 8 (Figure 1B) and was previously reported in a sibling pair with SSASKS.1 In that report, c.722–16A>G was maternally inherited and found in trans with NM_001029998.6:c.335G>A p.(Gly112Asp), a missense variant inherited from the father. RNA analysis using skin fibroblasts from the affected individuals demonstrated that c.722–16A>G results in skipping of exon 9 (52 bp), leading to a frameshift at codon 241 and a premature termination codon (p.Ile241Argfs30∗). The second variant, NM_001029998.6:c.472–1G>T (chr4:146306010C>A [GRCh38]), affects the canonical splice acceptor site of intron 6 (Figure 1C) and was not detected in either parent, indicating a de novo origin (Figure 1A). This variant has not been previously reported and is absent from both ClinVar (accessed May 19, 2025) and gnomAD v.4.1.0. The two intronic variants are located 13,014 bp apart at the genomic level, making phasing by allele-specific PCR technically challenging. Nevertheless, based on the clinical diagnosis consistent with autosomal recessive SSASKS, we presumed that c.472–1G>T resides on the maternal allele.

We used SpliceAI10 to predict the splicing consequences of the two SLC10A7 intronic variants. For c.722–16A>G, SpliceAI predicted concurrent loss of the canonical intron 8 splice acceptor site (ΔAL = 0.70) and intron 9 splice donor site (ΔDL = 0.73) (Figure 1B), consistent with the previously observed skipping of exon 9.1

For c.472–1G>T, SpliceAI predicted complete loss of the canonical intron 6 splice acceptor site (ΔAL = 1.00), a moderate gain of an alternative acceptor site within exon 7 (ΔAG = 0.61), and a low donor loss score for the canonical intron 7 splice donor site (ΔDL = 0.25) (Figure 1C). These predictions suggest complex effects, potentially leading to both complete and partial skipping of exon 7.

We performed minigene assays to experimentally assess the splicing effects of c.722–16A>G and c.472–1G>T. Assays were conducted using wild-type (WT) and variant constructs based on the pcMINI vector (Figures 2A and 2C). Reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR) analysis following transfection into HEK293T and HeLa cells showed that, for c.722–16A>G, the WT construct generated a major transcript with normal exon 9 inclusion and a minor transcript lacking exon 9. For c.472–1G>T, the WT construct produced a single normally spliced transcript, while the variant construct generated two aberrant products: one lacking the entire exon 7 (p.Leu158_Gln185del) and one lacking the first 45 bp of exon 7 (p.Leu158_Leu173del) (Figure 2D). These results are consistent with the SpliceAI prediction (Figure 1C) and demonstrate that c.472–1G>T disrupts normal splicing by promoting both complete and partial exon 7 skipping.

We also tested both variants using the pcMINI-N vector and observed similar splicing patterns in both cell lines (Figure S2).

To further investigate these observations, we performed two additional RT-PCR and RNA sequencing analyses. One of the RT-PCR used primers spanning exons 8–11 to focus on exon 9 skipping. The single RT-PCR product from the affected individual corresponded to the exon 9-included transcript (Figure 3B), confirming the absence of exon 9-skipped transcripts from the c.722–16A>G allele. The second RT-PCR analysis used primers spanning exons 4–8 to focus on exon 7. This confirmed the presence of both exon 7-skipped and normally spliced transcripts, but again showed no evidence of the partial exon 7-skipped product observed in the minigene assay (Figure 3B). Further splice pattern analysis for SLC10A7 splicing variants via RNA sequencing (Figure 3C) revealed an intriguing finding: after excluding common transcript variants, we identified exon 8 to exon 10 junction reads (reflecting exon 9 skipping)—although this splicing pattern is relatively rare (Figure 3D). Additionally, we observed exon 7-skipped transcripts, in which exon 6 is directly connected with exon 8 (Figure 3D).

These findings, taken together, support four key conclusions.1.Consistent with the minigene assay, the complete (84 bp) exon 7-skipped transcript associated with the c.472–1G>T allele was observed. This aberrant transcript is in-frame.2.By contrast, the partial (45 bp) exon 7-skipped transcript associated with the c.472–1G>T allele—detected in the minigene assay—was not observed in blood-derived RNA, despite also being in-frame.3.Neither variant produced normally spliced transcripts in the minigene assay. In contrast, a normally spliced transcript was observed in blood cells.4.Exon 9-skipped transcripts associated with c.722–16A>G were observed in the minigene assay, RNA sequencing of blood cells (Figures 2B and 3D), and in fibroblasts from affected individuals in the study by Ashikov et al.,1 but not in the RT-PCR analyzed in this study. This absence may be attributed to several factors: (1) exon 9 skipping is out-of-frame, rendering the resulting transcript susceptible to nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD) in vivo11; (2) sequence context differences between the minigene construct and the endogenous genomic setting; (3) tissue-specific differences in SLC10A7 expression or splicing regulation; (4) RNA sequencing is a more sensitive assay than RT-PCR to explore splicing patterns.

Exon 9-skipped transcripts associated with c.722–16A>G were observed in the minigene assay, RNA sequencing of blood cells (Figures 2B and 3D), and in fibroblasts from affected individuals in the study by Ashikov et al.,1 but not in the RT-PCR analyzed in this study. This absence may be attributed to several factors: (1) exon 9 skipping is out-of-frame, rendering the resulting transcript susceptible to nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD) in vivo11; (2) sequence context differences between the minigene construct and the endogenous genomic setting; (3) tissue-specific differences in SLC10A7 expression or splicing regulation; (4) RNA sequencing is a more sensitive assay than RT-PCR to explore splicing patterns.

Altogether, these analyses support that the two variants are located in trans. If the variants were in cis, a shorter transcript lacking both exons 7 and 9 may have been detected in RT-PCR assays using primers spanning exons 4–11—but this was not observed.

The RT-PCR results of blood RNA provided strong evidence for incomplete LoF of SLC10A7, it is necessary to detect the expression of SLC10A7 transcripts to assess the residual function of SLC10A7 in vivo. RNA sequencing analysis revealed that the expression level of total SLC10A7 transcripts in the affected individual was lower than that in control sample (TPM: 2.65[affected] vs. 3.10 [control]) (Figure S3A). Further analysis of the proportion of different transcript variants revealed that SCL10A7-201 (GenBank: NM_001029998.6) accounted for up to 32% of total transcripts in the affected individual, whereas in the control sample SCL10A7-201 accounted for 77%. In the affected individual, the exon 7-skipped transcript and the exon 9-skipped transcript accounted for 52% and 6%, respectively (Figure S3B).

It remained important to evaluate this finding in an additional tissue to strengthen its biological relevance. To this end, we accessed frozen muscle tissue collected from the affected individual during surgical intervention. RT-PCR analysis using RNA extracted from this sample failed to detect SLC10A7 expression, consistent with previous studies that also did not identify SLC10A7 transcripts in human muscle.3,5 Despite the absence of detectable mRNA, immunohistochemistry using a commercial rabbit polyclonal antibody against human SLC10A7 revealed clear protein accumulation in control muscle tissue. Importantly, SLC10A7 was also detectable in the affected individual’s muscle, albeit with markedly reduced staining intensity (Figure 4A). We next performed western blot analysis. As shown in Figure 4B, the lowest band corresponds to GAPDH, while the SLC10A7 antibody detected two specific bands. The stronger band migrated at just under 45 kDa, consistent with the predicted molecular weight of the full-length 340-amino-acid SLC10A7 protein (37.44 kDa). The weaker, higher-molecular-weight band likely represents either a dimerized form of SLC10A7 (Noppes et al.12) or a post-translationally modified isoform, such as a glycosylated product. Notably, a distinct band corresponding to the predicted protein product from the exon 7-skipped transcript and other less common isoforms were not detected. This may reflect insufficient antibody recognition due to conformational changes caused by the 28-amino-acid deletion or degradation of the aberrant protein due to structural instability. Using the <45 kDa band as a reference and normalizing against GAPDH, SLC10A7 protein accumulation in the affected individual was estimated at ∼43% of control levels. This result was broadly consistent with the RNA sequencing-based results. Compared with the control sample, RNA sequencing screened out 693 downregulated genes and 81 upregulated genes in the affected individual (Figure 4C). GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes revealed that the functions of these genes were mainly concentrated in oxidative phosphorylation (Figure 4D), suggesting that the pathogenic mechanism of SSASKS caused by the decreased expression of SLC10A7 may be related to this pathway.

Interpreting clinically detected variants remains a major challenge in medical genetics, especially when variants lie outside coding regions or exert subtle effects on gene function. This is particularly true for splice-altering variants that occur beyond the canonical GT donor or AG acceptor sites.13 Even some GT>GC substitutions at canonical splice donor sites can result in normal splicing, further complicating variant interpretation.14,15

In this study, we provide evidence that the previously reported pathogenic variant SLC10A7 c.722–16A>G represents a partial LoF, or hypomorphic, allele. In the prior report,1 c.722–16A>G was inherited in trans with a missense variant (p.(Gly112Asp)), and normally spliced transcripts from the missense allele may have masked the functional effect of c.722–16A>G. Moreover, tissue-specific NMD of exon 9-skipped transcripts may affect estimates of the relative expression abundance of normal transcripts in the affected individual, thereby affecting the assessment of the actual functional threshold of SLC10A7.

While RT-PCR analysis of RNA from blood cells of the affected individual corroborated SpliceAI predictions and minigene assay results regarding exon 7 and exon 9 skipping, RNA sequencing analysis also revealed that approximately 45% of transcripts from the c.472–1G>T and c.722–16A>G alleles were normally spliced. This discrepancy highlights the limitations of current in silico tools and minigene models15,16,17 and underscores the value of analyzing RNA directly from affected individual-derived tissues.

Given that SSASKS is thought to result from bi-allelic complete or near-complete SLC10A7 LoF, the observation that c.472–1G>T and c.722–16A>G produce substantial levels of normally spliced transcript may seem unexpected. In retrospect, this is consistent with the intermediate SpliceAI scores (ΔAL = 0.70; ΔDL = 0.73) of c.722–16A>G, both below the high-confidence threshold of 0.8.10,13,15,18 Biologically, the variant lies upstream of the 13-bp PPT but does not disrupt either the PPT or the branchpoint sequence (Figure 1B), suggesting a modest effect on spliceosome recognition.

The finding that substantial levels of normally spliced transcript was further supported by western blot analysis of muscle tissue, which showed approximately 43% protein accumulation relative to control. Together, these results suggest that considerable residual SLC10A7 function may still be compatible with disease manifestation. This refinement of the genotype-phenotype relationship in SLC10A7-related SSASKS will have important implications for both variant classification and genetic counseling.
