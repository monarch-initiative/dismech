# Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis as presenting manifestation of profound combined immunodeficiency due to an ORAI1 mutation.
**Authors:** Klemann C, Ammann S, Heizmann M, Fuchs S, Bode SF, Heeg M, Fuchs H, Lehmberg K, Zur Stadt U, Roll C, Vraetz T, Speckmann C, Lorenz MR, Schwarz K, Rohr J, Feske S, Ehl S
**Journal:** J Allergy Clin Immunol (2017)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jaci.2017.05.039](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaci.2017.05.039)

## Content

Activation of lymphocytes, in particular of T-cells, is a key and eponymous feature of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) and provides the rationale for lymphocyte directed therapies including anti-thymocyte globulin or alemtuzumab1. However, the observation of HLH in a few infants with severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) with few circulating T-cells has raised the possibility that the hyperinflammatory HLH syndrome, as currently defined by clinical criteria, may not always depend on activated T-cells2. Here, we report the first case of HLH in a patient with a severe defect in lymphocyte activation due to a novel mutation in ORAI1, providing further evidence for an additional pathway of pathogenesis of the HLH syndrome, independent of T- and NK-cell activation.

The index patient was the second child of consanguineous Turkish parents, who presented at 6 weeks of life with recurrent episodes of diarrhea and fever. At 3 months, he was hospitalized for bronchiolitis and splenomegaly. Primary post-natal CMV infection was diagnosed based on of blood virus loads >100.000 IU/ml, a negative CMV PCR in the neonatal screening card and a positive CMV PCR in maternal breast milk. Despite initiation of ganciclovir, the patient developed the full clinical picture of HLH. In addition to persistent fever and profound splenomegaly, he had pancytopenia (minimum hemoglobin 8.4g/dl, platelets 32x103/µl, leukocytes 1.4 x 103/µl), hyperferritinemia (5,103ng/ml), hypertriglyceridemia (371 mg/dl = 4.1mmol/l), hypofibrinogenemia (1.4g/l) and elevated soluble CD25 (max. 4,022U/l). Expression of perforin in NK-cells was normal, but the patient had absent degranulation of resting and PHA/IL-2 activated NK-cells (Fig. 1A), suggesting primary (1°)HLH due to a genetic disorder of cytotoxicity (Fig.1B). Bone marrow puncture for demonstration of hemophagocytosis was not considered necessary for the clinical diagnosis, but the patient had significant monocyte activation comparable to a patient with perforin deficiency (Fig. 1C). Overall, 7/7 tested criteria for HLH were positive. However, perforin, SAP and XIAP were expressed normally and no mutations were found in UNC13D, STXBP2, and RAB27a. However, the patient had a heterozygous mutation in Syntaxin11 p.Glu270Lys (STX11:c.808G>A), which has not been reported associated with 1°HLH previously and is predicted to be benign. Treatment with dexamethasone improved the HLH symptoms, but the patient relapsed upon tapering and the CMV infection remained poorly controlled.

When respiratory failure developed, the patient was transferred to our Center for further diagnosis and treatment. In addition to his CMV infection, the patient was diagnosed with Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia by immunofluorescence of his BAL fluid and PCR, prompting further diagnostic work-up for SCID. However, the patient had increased serum immunoglobulins levels (IgG 1,610, IgA 177, IgM 1,820mg/dl) and had recovered normal leukocyte counts with 3,958/µl T-cells, 306/µl B-cells, and 193/µl NK-cells. His TCR Vβ-chain distribution was normal. The percentage of CD4+CD45RA+ naïve T-cells was moderately reduced (33%), but despite ongoing CMV infection, 80% of CD8+ T-cells had a naïve CD45RA+CD27+CCR7+ phenotype. Moreover, HLA-DR expression on CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells was low with 7% and 8%, respectively. T-cell proliferation to PHA was mildly reduced, but completely absent after anti-CD3 or anti-CD3/CD28 stimulation (Fig.1D). While CD25 (not shown) and CD69 upregulation upon anti-CD3/CD28 stimulation were nearly normal, the patient's CD4+ T-cells failed to upregulate CD40L expression (Fig.1E). Upregulation of CD40L occurs quickly within 1–2 hours after TCR engagement and is dependent on Ca2+ influx via the Ca2+ release-activated Ca2+ (CRAC) channel and NFAT pathway activation, while CD69 up-regulation is a result of CRAC-independent RAS activation3. Switched memory B cells were absent, indicating a helper cell defect. Overall, these findings were consistent with a T-cell activation defect and a genetic disorder of CRAC channel function.

CRAC-channelopathy is characterized by a severe defect of T-cell function and a SCID-like immunodeficiency with normal T-cell numbers, consistent with the patient's phenotype. Other signs of CRAC-channelopathy, such as ectodermal dysplasia with anhidrosis4 were not obvious in our patient, but it was noted that his fever in the course of the CMV infections was particularly responsive to physical measures, while antipyretics had only little effect. While muscular hypotonia, another feature of CRAC-channelopathy, was difficult to assess on mechanical ventilation, the mother reported reduced fetal child movements and hypotonia after birth. Moreover, the patient showed mydriasis with poor contraction of the pupils to light. Further studies confirmed absent Ca2+ influx in the patient's T-cells after TCR stimulation or after depletion of ER Ca2+ stores with thapsigargin (TG), which triggers the opening of CRAC channels (Fig.2A). Genetic analysis revealed that the patient is homozygous for a novel missense mutation in ORAI1 (Exon2 c.443T>G; p.Ile148Ser), which encodes the pore-forming subunit of the CRAC channel (Fig.2B)5. Multiple sequence alignments (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) showed that the site of p.Ile148Ser mutation was highly conserved among all species. The variation was not listed in SNPbase (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp), 1000 Genome-Database (http://browser.1000genomes.org/Homo_sapiens/Info/Index), EVS-Database (http://evs.gs.washington.edu), Kaviar-Database. (http://db.systemsbiology.net/kaviar/) and ExAC-Database (http://exac.broadinstitute.org/). Both parents and the brother were heterozygous for the mutation. Flow cytometric analysis of the patient's PBMC showed that the mutant ORAI1 protein was expressed (Fig.2B). Thus, this is the first human mutation described in the II-III intracellular loop of ORAI1 resulting in sustained protein expression4, 6. Srikanth et al. had previously demonstrated that engineered mutations of ORAI1 residues P146 and E149 neighboring I148 abolish SOCE in vitro7. These results suggest that residues 146, 148 and 149 are essential for CRAC channel activation, potentially by mediating the binding of STIM1 to ORAI1, although further experiments are required to understand the mechanisms underlying the effects of the p.Ile148Ser mutation.

The patient underwent HSCT with unmanipulated bone marrow containing 20x106 CD34+ stem cells/kg from his healthy HLA identical, CMV+ brother, who showed normal results in all immunological assays. Due to his recurrent HLH episodes, the patient was conditioned with treosulfan and fludarabin. Despite continuous antiviral therapy with ganciclovir and foscarnet and early evidence of engraftment, the patient died 30 days after transplantation due to severe, CMV-associated pulmonary inflammatory complications.

Development of HLH in an infant born to a consanguineous family that is associated with complete absence of lymphocyte degranulation and cytotoxicity is usually highly predictive of a genetic disorder of granule exocytosis. In our patient, however, no disease-causing mutations were detected in genes so far associated with 1°HLH. Instead, the patient had clinical, genetic and functional evidence of ORAI1 deficiency that results in a defect of lymphocyte activation. Since lymphocyte cytotoxicity is dependent on CRAC-channel function8, it is possible that ORAI1 deficiency directly caused the impaired immune regulation characteristic of HLH as in other diseases with impaired cytotoxicity. However, HLH has never been reported in a patient with ORAI1 or STIM1 deficiency and our patient lacked the typical signs of T-cell activation characteristic of 1°HLH or virus-induced secondary (2°)HLH9. We therefore favor the interpretation that the clinical picture of HLH was an indirect consequence of impaired immunological control of CMV infection due to the severe T-cell activation defect. A similar 2°HLH syndrome has been reported in a few patients with lymphopenic SCID variants and viral infections2. Our patient illustrates that the clinical syndrome currently defined as HLH includes hyperinflammatory states clinically indistinguishable from 1°HLH that are not dependent on hyperactivation of T-cells. Furthermore, we confirm impaired pupillary light reflex, NK-cell degranulation and CD40L upregulation as rapid and informative assays for the diagnosis of genetic disorders of CRAC channel function.
