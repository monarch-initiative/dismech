# Large-scale analyses of the X chromosome in 2,354 infertile men discover recurrently affected genes associated with spermatogenic failure.
**Authors:** Riera-Escamilla A, Vockel M, Nagirnaja L, Xavier MJ, Carbonell A, Moreno-Mendoza D, Pybus M, Farnetani G, Rosta V, Cioppi F, Friedrich C, Oud MS, van der Heijden GW, Soave A, Diemer T, Ars E, Sánchez-Curbelo J, Kliesch S, O'Bryan MK, Ruiz-Castañe E, GEMINI Consortium, Azorín F, Veltman JA, Aston KI, Conrad DF, Tüttelmann F, Krausz C
**Journal:** Am J Hum Genet (2022)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ajhg.2022.06.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajhg.2022.06.007)

## Content

Although the evolutionary history of the X chromosome indicates its specialization in male fitness, its role in spermatogenesis has largely been unexplored. Currently only three X chromosome genes are considered of moderate-definitive diagnostic value. We aimed to provide a comprehensive analysis of all X chromosome-linked protein-coding genes in 2,354 azoospermic/cryptozoospermic men from four independent cohorts. Genomic data were analyzed and compared with data in normozoospermic control individuals and gnomAD. While updating the clinical significance of known genes, we propose 21 recurrently mutated genes strongly associated with and 34 moderately associated with azoospermia/cryptozoospermia not previously linked to male infertility (novel). The most frequently affected prioritized gene, RBBP7, was found mutated in ten men across all cohorts, and our functional studies in Drosophila support its role in germ stem cell maintenance. Collectively, our study represents a significant step towards the definition of the missing genetic etiology in idiopathic severe spermatogenic failure and significantly reduces the knowledge gap of X-linked genetic causes of azoospermia/cryptozoospermia contributing to the development of future diagnostic gene panels.

Keywords: male infertility, genetics, genomics, azoospermia, cryptozoospermia, X chromosome, next-generation sequencing, spermatogenesis, genes, RBBP7

Riera-Escamilla et al. provided a comprehensive analysis of all X chromosome-linked protein-coding genes in 2,354 azoospermic/cryptozoospermic men from four independent cohorts. While updating the clinical significance of known genes, they propose 21 novel recurrently mutated genes strongly associated with and 34 moderately associated with azoospermia/cryptozoospermia.

Male factor contributes to infertility in about 50% of couples and affects 7% of men in the general population.1 Its most severe forms are azoospermia or cryptozoospermia, the absence or extreme reduction of spermatozoa in the ejaculate, respectively. These conditions account for about 10%–15% of male factor infertility and may be due to acquired diseases (approximately 40% of men) or, in nearly 20%–25%, can be the consequence of known genetic factors, such as karyotype anomalies and Y chromosome microdeletions.2 In a relatively high proportion of infertile men (40%), the etiology cannot be established with the currently available diagnostic tools; this condition is termed as “idiopathic” crypto-/azoospermia. It has been predicted that yet unknown, monogenic factors are likely to play a major role in idiopathic non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA). In the last 5 years, whole-exome sequencing (WES) has been highly successful in identifying mainly recessive monogenic anomalies in this pathological condition.3,4 However, the majority of recent findings lack strong/definitive evidence for their gene-disease relationship (GDR) and only explain approximately 1% of men with idiopathic crypto-/azoospermia.3

The X chromosome is a sub-metacentric chromosome that is in a state of rapid evolution. About 17% of its gene content violates Ohno’s law, which states that the gene content of the X chromosomes is conserved among placental mammals.5,6 Strikingly, most of the independently acquired X chromosome genes show high expression in the testis and little to no expression in other tissues,6 implying a specialized role in male reproductive fitness. This and the fact that a single pathogenic variant is sufficient to alter or ablate protein function (i.e., the absence of a compensatory X allele) make the X chromosome an ideal target for discovering the missing genetic etiology in idiopathic spermatogenic failure. Based on previous studies however, validated X chromosome-linked monogenic causes of NOA/cryptozoospermia are surprisingly rare.7 According to the last systematic review by Houston and colleagues (2021),3 only AR (MIM: 313700), TEX11 (MIM: 300311), and USP26 (MIM: 300309) reach sufficiently high clinical evidence for their inclusion in diagnostic testing for NOA or oligozoospermia.

In this study, we performed a systematic evaluation of all X chromosome protein-coding genes in 2,354 infertile men belonging to four distinct cohorts. The dual purposes of our study were as follows: (1) identify men carrying variants in genes previously linked to NOA/cryptozoospermia and (2) identify recurrent X chromosome linked genetic causes not previously linked to male infertility. We identified men carrying variants in known NOA/oligozoospermia genes, in support of previous studies, and in some instances, the additional evidence provided by this study significantly elevated clinical evidence for specific variants. Furthermore, we discovered 55 recurrently mutated genes, not previously associated with human spermatogenic failure, with strong or moderate supporting data for a role in spermatogenesis. Seventeen of the strong candidate genes were immediately replicated when additional men carrying variants were identified in more than one independent cohort.

A total of 2,354 idiopathic NOA/cryptozoospermic men from four different cohorts who were sequenced before March 2021 were enrolled for the present study. Individuals with known acquired (e.g., chemotherapy, orchitis, testicular torsion, etc.) and genetic (karyotype aberrations, Y chromosomal AZF deletions) causes of azoospermia were excluded.

Barcelona cohort (BCN): 129 NOA men attending the Fundació Puigvert (Barcelona). A customized gene panel including all X chromosome RefSeq protein-coding genes was sequenced in 50 infertile men through a NextSeq platform. The remaining 79 men underwent exome sequencing, carried out as a service by Macrogen (Republic of Korea) with the Agilent SureSelect_V6 enrichment and a NovaSeq6000. Men with X-linked candidate variants identified through the X chromosome gene panel were exome sequenced to rule out other autosomal candidate genes.

MERGE cohort (Male Reproductive Genomics, Münster, Germany): 1,056 unrelated NOA/cryptozoospermic men. Exome sequencing was performed as described in Wyrwoll et al., 2020.8

GEMINI cohort (Genetics of Male Infertility Initiative): 903 unrelated NOA-affected men, predominantly of European ancestry (91%), recruited across seven countries. Exome sequencing was performed as described in Nagirnaja et al., 2021.9

Nijmegen/Newcastle (NIJ/NCL) cohort: 266 infertile men, 225 affected by azoospermia and 41 by cryptozoospermia. Exome sequencing was performed as described in Oud et al., 2021.10

Replication cohorts: 265 NOA/cryptozoospermic men from MERGE and 54 NOA-affected men from Barcelona, sequenced after March 2021, were enrolled for a targeted look up for variants in genes with predicted strong relationship with male infertility.

Exome data from 209 normozoospermic control individuals were used for comparison with the cohorts of affected men, and we excluded (1) variants found in both groups of infertile men and controls, (2) genes in which loss-of-function (LoF) variants were found in the normozoospermic control individuals, and (3) non-LoF variants located in the same protein domain in both affected men and control individuals in the prioritized genes (see below). The 209 control individuals were recruited from three cohorts: Barcelona cohort, 15 normozoospermic individuals affected by testicular germ cell tumor; MERGE study, 66 normozoospermic men; and GEMINI, 128 normozoospermic control individuals.

All individuals provided written informed consent to be included in the analyses. The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committees (Fundacio Puigvert: 2014/04c, MERGE: 2010-578-f-S, GEMINI: 201502059; Nijmegen: NL50495.091.14 version 5.0, Newcastle: REC ref. 18/NE/0089).

We selected non-synonymous exonic and splicing variants and filtered out polymorphisms (≥1% in the general population) on the basis of the maximum allele frequency observed in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD). Variants reported to be in heterozygosis and/or with a depth of coverage < 10 and/or found in normozoospermic control individuals were automatically discarded. Recurrent variants were discarded if (1) found in >3 unrelated men/cohort or (2) present in >1% in the Münster in-house database (for the MERGE cohort). All filtered variants predicted to be pathogenic by CADD11 (CADD ≥ 20) and PolyPhen12 and/or MutationTaster13 were considered for further analysis (i.e., variants predicted to be benign/neutral according to both tools were excluded from further analysis). Finally, variants were classified following the ACMG criteria14 and those classified as likely benign/benign were excluded.

Variants in prioritized genes (see gene prioritization below) were further characterized by (1) evaluating the presence of homozygosity women in gnomAD, suggesting the variant likely does not cause NOA, and (2) presence of predicted-as-pathogenic variants (non-LoF) in the normozoospermic control individuals in the same protein domain. Variants found in homozygosis in gnomAD or located in the same protein domain as controls were removed.

In order to identify novel causes of NOA/cryptozoospermia, we selected all genes mutated in two or more infertile men and excluded those in which LoF variants were found in normozoospermic control individuals. In order to prioritize the selected genes (n = 377), we established a scoring system based on the following three criteria: (1) level of mRNA and protein expression in the testis, based on The Human Protein Atlas; (2) the presence of reproductive defects in the mutated mice according to the Mouse Genome Informatics database (MGI); and (3) data from PubMed published through December 2021 (see Table S7). For this latter criterion, we established four different scores based on the level of evidence for or against the involvement of a gene in NOA/cryptozoospermia. The highest score is 3, which implies strong supporting data, and was given to those genes that met one of the following criteria: (1) previously reported in the phenotypic spectrum from NOA to oligozoospermia; (2) data from male mice showing secretory azoospermia reported in the literature but not in MGI; (3) indirect evidence or in vitro experiments indicate their involvement in spermatogenesis (e.g., role in meiosis, DNA repair-related functions, germ cell differentiation/proliferation); and (4) physical interaction with other NOA candidate genes (according to PubMed and further checked in String). The second score is 1 and refers to limited support for a gene disease-relationship, and it was given to those genes that met one of the following criteria: (1) pathogenic variants described as causal of other male infertility phenotypes; (2) reported to be removed in a block with other X chromosome genes in women with premature ovarian insufficiency (POI); (3) gene variants lead to a severe syndromic phenotype that among its features includes small testes or cryptorchidism, but no other data with respect to male infertility has been reported; (4) Multicopy/ampliconic cancer testis antigen (CTA) genes without any other relevant information. The third score, corresponding to 0, reflects no evidence, meaning that no relevant information suggesting a role for spermatogenesis or human fertility has been found in PubMed. Finally, the fourth score, −4, was given when suspected pathogenic variants were reported to be transmitted from father to son.

By applying this scoring system, the maximum score for a given gene is 7 and the minimum score is −4. Genes were selected for further investigation and classified in two main categories: (1) genes previously reported to be associated with male infertility and (2) candidate genes for NOA/cryptozoospermia not previously linked to male infertility. For the latter category, we sub-classified genes on the basis of their predicted relationship with NOA/cryptozoospermia as follows: (1) strong (score ≥ 5), (2) moderate (score 3–4), and (3) limited/no relationship (score ≤ 2).

A comprehensive list of genes, previously reported in severe spermatogenic quantitative defects (NOA and oligozoospermia), was generated on the basis of (1) genes with at least limited evidence for GDR reported in the systematic review from Houston et al., 2021,3 which includes data published until July 1, 2020, and (2) genes reported to be associated with NOA/oligozoospermia after July 1, 2020. We crossed this list with our list of variants to identify those who carried previously reported X chromosome-linked genetic causes.

In order to exclude men who presented with autosome-linked causes of NOA, we generated a comprehensive list of autosomal genes previously reported in NOA on the basis of (1) the systematic review from Houston et al., 2021,3 including those with at least moderate evidence, and (2) those not included in the systematic review and previously discovered in our laboratories based on a smaller study population10 (L.N., unpublished data).

Predicted hemizygous deletions (exons with coverage close to 0) were evaluated and annotated in all cohorts. Deletions present in more than 1% of the samples of the Database of Genomic Variations were excluded from the analysis. Deletions removing the same gene (with the same or different breakpoint) in more than three unrelated infertile men from the same cohort were also excluded.

Hemizygous deletions partially or completely removing a gene were confirmed by +/− PCR with a set of primers within the deleted region and another set complementary to a region outside of the deleted region. Deletions were confirmed when no amplification was observed with the internal primers but was observed with the external primer pair.

We queried the gnomAD v2.1.1 to retrieve all variants within the 377 genes of interest identified in the exomes of the 76,702 men presently included in the database. Variants identified uniquely in females were immediately excluded from our analysis. Variants were then fully annotated as described above, then coding variants were filtered for rarity (minor allele frequency [MAF] ≤ 0.01) and predicted to be pathogenic by CADD (CADD ≥ 20) and PolyPhen and/or MutationTaster. Having identified several likely pathogenic, rare LoF, and non-LoF variants in these 377 genes of interest in both the infertile group and in the men present in the gnomAD database, we performed a gene-based burden test comparing the number of individuals carrying only LoF variants or only non-LoF variants or both types of variants against men in the gnomAD control cohort. For each independent analysis, the proportion of individuals with pathogenic variants in each of the genes of interest was statistically evaluated with two-tailed Fisher’s exact tests; individual p values were then corrected with Bonferroni correction to adjust for performing 377 consecutive statistical tests and reduce the risk of type I errors. An adjusted p value of 0.05 was set as the threshold for significance after Bonferroni correction. All statistical analyses were performed with R statistical software.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis with STRING v11.515 was performed on the 41 genes with score ≥ 5 and on the 21 strong candidate genes. As interaction sources provided by STRING, we only included experiments and databases.

A total of 2,354 NOA/cryptozoospermic men belonging to four cohorts (BCN = 129, GEMINI = 903, MERGE = 1,056, and NIJ/NCL = 266) were enrolled in this study. We identified 2,091 infertile male-specific, rare, protein-altering, and predicted-to-be-pathogenic single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) or indels in 543 genes. These variants were classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUSs), likely pathogenic or pathogenic according to the American College Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines14 (BCN, n = 161; GEMINI, n = 643; MERGE, n = 1,063; and NIJ/NCL, n = 224), and were distributed among the cohorts as reported in Figure S1. In addition to SNVs and indels, we also identified seven rare, partial/complete gene deletions that we classified as LoF variants.

According to our literature search, variants in 14 X chromosome genes have been previously reported as potential causes of severe spermatogenic quantitative defects (NOA or oligozoospermia). 11 out of 14 genes (AR, BCORL1 [MIM: 300688], FAM47C [MIM: 301067], H2BW1 [MIM: 300507], HAUS7 [MIM: 300540], MAGEB4 [MIM: 300153], MAP7D3 [MIM: 300930], TEX11, USP26, WNK3 [MIM: 300358], and ZFX [MIM: 314980]) were already systematically analyzed in the recently published review by Houston et al. (2021)3 and were classified as at least limited evidence, whereas the remaining three genes (GCNA [MIM: 300369], STAG2 [MIM: 300826], and TBC1D25 [MIM: 311240]) were published after the systematic review.19, 20, 21, 22 Because we identified a partial gene deletion (exons 16–17) in MAP7D3 in 1/209 of our normozoospermic controls, this gene is not likely to be sensitive to LoF variation. In addition to the 14 published genes, we considered as “known candidate genes” for NOA, four additional genes that were previously reported by our laboratories: DCAF12L1, HUWE1 (MIM: 300697), LUZP4 (MIM: 300616), and YY2 (MIM: 300570)10 (L.N., unpublished data). In these genes, we found men carrying variants in our additional cohorts (see Tables S1, S2, and S3). A total of 83 VUSs/likely pathogenic variants were identified in the 17 previously reported known and candidate genes in the cohort of 2,354 infertile men. This finding further validates the importance of these genes in male infertility. For these 83 variants, no homozygous women have been reported in gnomAD, nor were the same protein domains mutated in control individuals, and men with these variants did not carry other variants predicted to be pathogenic in autosomal candidate genes. On the basis of the additional men carrying variants, we calculated the level of GDR for each gene, and we were able to increase the score of clinical evidence in four instances (BCORL1, DCAF12L1, GCNA, and ZFX) (Table 1).

397 out of 543 genes were found to be mutated in more than one infertile man across cohorts, 20 of which were discarded because LoF variants were also found in the normozoospermic cohort. The final number of candidate variants was 1,723 in 377 genes. We subjected these variants and genes to further analysis to determine their potential involvement in male infertility (see Tables S1 and S2 and Figure 1). Of the 377 genes, 24 were mutated in all four cohorts, 104 in at least three cohorts, 180 in two cohorts, and finally 69 genes were mutated in more than one man belonging to a single cohort (see Figure S2). 15 genes were previously described as “known NOA/oligozoospermia candidate genes,” whereas the remaining 362 genes were not previously linked to human NOA/oligozoospermia in any respect.

We employed the method described by Mueller et al. (2013)6 to further classify the mutated genes according to their evolutionary and structural features. The X chromosome-linked genes were divided into (1) single copy, ampliconic, and multicopy genes and (2) shared with mouse/newly acquired genes. A total of 306 out of 377 recurrently mutated genes were single copy genes (295 shared with mouse; 11 newly acquired); 59 were multicopy genes (39 shared with mouse; 20 newly acquired); and 12 genes were classified as ampliconic (nine shared with mouse and three newly acquired). As expected, for 17 of the 34 genes classified as newly acquired (ATXN3L [MIM: 300920], CBLL2, CT47B1 [MIM: 300790], DDX53 [MIM: 301079], FAM9A [MIM: 300477], FAM47B, GLUD2 [MIM: 300144], H2BW1 [MIM: 300507], MAGEA12 [MIM: 300177], MAGEC2 [MIM: 300468], MAGEC3 [MIM: 300469], SCML1 [MIM: 300227], SPANXN1 [MIM: 300664], TFDP3 [MIM: 300772], VCX [MIM: 300229], VCX3A [MIM: 300553], and VCX3B [MIM: 300981]) gene expression is known to be enriched in testis (Table S1). In fact, 50% of newly acquired X chromosome genes in humans are enriched in testis, in contrast to 19% of genes shared between human and mouse.

After applying our in-house scoring system (see material and methods), we classified the 362 genes according to their potential relationship with NOA/cryptozoospermia (strong, moderate, limited, or absent).

The most promising genes are those that received the highest score (score ≥ 5) and were therefore classified as strong candidates. After removing gnomAD variants reported in homozygosity in women, those that occurred in the same functional domain as controls and excluding variants from men carrying stronger candidate variants in known NOA-associated genes (see supplemental information), we obtained a list of 70 variants in 21 candidate genes (APEX2 [MIM: 300773], CMC4, CUL4B [MIM: 300304], DCAF12L2, FAM9A [MIM: 300477], FANCB [MIM: 300515], FATE1 [MIM: 300450], KIF4A [MIM: 300521], MSL3 [MIM: 300609], NONO [MIM: 300084], PASD1 [MIM: 300933], POLA1 [MIM: 312040], RBBP7 [MIM: 300825], SCML1 [MIM: 300227], SCML2 [MIM: 300208], TAF1 [MIM: 313650], TAF9B [MIM: 300754], THOC2 [MIM: 300395], TIMP1 [MIM: 305370], USP9X [MIM: 300072], and ZMYM3 [MIM: 300061]) (see Figures 1 and 2 and Table S4). It is noteworthy that no homozygous LoF variants have been described in these 21 genes in the thousands of females included in gnomAD.

Based on our literature/database search, these 21 candidate genes were found to play a role in cell proliferation/differentiation, meiotic cell cycle, DNA repair, chromatin remodeling, and cell communication. We divided them in two major groups: (1) genes with supporting evidence from model organisms and (2) genes with supporting evidence from expression and published data.

Model organisms were available for nine of the 21 candidate genes. All these models suggest that damaging variants in the gene of interest affect the early phases of spermatogenesis and meiosis. RBBP7 is the most frequently mutated gene in our four cohorts: nine NOA-affected men carry variants not reported in gnomAD, three of them LoF. The encoded protein is involved in chromatin regulation and metabolism. To test the role of RBBP7 in male fertility, we performed RNAi-mediated knockdown experiments in Drosophila (see material and methods). The Drosophila orthologue of RBBP7 is Caf1-p55. We observed that 94% (n = 36) of knockdown nos > Caf1-p55RNAi testes were very underdeveloped (Figure 3B), showing a prominent reduction in size (Figures 3A, right, and 3C). To define where the loss of RBBP7/Caf1-p55 was impacting spermatogenesis, we analyze the presence of early germ cells in nos > Caf1-p55RNAi testes by using antibodies against Vasa, a specific marker for germ cells, and dBigH1, a linker histone variant expressed specifically in germ line stem cells (GSCs), gonoblasts (GBs), and spermatocytes.18,24 In wild-type testes, dBigH1 is detected at the tip in GSCs and GBs. It is not observed in spermatogonia (Vasa-positive and dBigh1-negative cells with intense DAPI staining), and it is strongly detected in spermatocytes (Vasa- and dBigH1-positive cells with diffuse DAPI staining)23 (Figure 3D, left). We observed a complete lack of dBigH1- and Vasa-expressing cells at the tip of nos > Caf1-p55RNAi testes, revealing that RBBP7/Caf1-p55 is necessary for GSCs maintenance (Figure 3D) and that the lack of GSCs causes the strong reduction in Vasa-positive cells observed in nos > Caf1-p55RNAi testes, which include more differentiated cell types including spermatocytes (Figures 3C and 3D). Considering the high degree of similarity (94%) between Caf1-p55 and RBBP7 at a protein level, the results obtained in Drosophila strongly support that RBBP7 most likely has a role in human spermatogenesis and variants in this gene can cause azoospermia.

Similar to RBBP7, the Fancb-null male mice display a progressive loss of primordial germ cells.25 Such a testis phenotype is compatible with the Sertoli cell-only phenotype observed in the individual carrying a variant in FANCB. FANCB encodes a member of the Fanconi anemia protein family, which is involved in interstrand cross-link DNA repair. Variants in other members of this family, such as FANCA, XRCC2, BRCA2, and FANCM, have already been reported in idiopathic NOA-affected men men3,26 (L.N., unpublished data).

Three other genes (ZMYM3, KIF4A, and SCML2) are involved in meiosis. Zmym3-null mice are infertile because of meiotic arrest at metaphase of the first meiotic division. According to Hu et al. (2017),27 this gene has an essential role in the metaphase to anaphase transition during male meiosis by regulating the expression of diverse families of genes. KIF4A is an ATP-dependent microtubule-based motor protein involved in chromosome segregation and condensation. Experiments with morpholino oligo-depletion of Kif4 in mouse oocytes showed abnormal MII spindle formation, chromosome misalignment, and an increase in symmetrical cytokinesis instead of the normal asymmetrical cytokinesis.28 Interestingly, variants in the Drosophila orthologue klp3a results in male infertility as a result of aberrant contractile ring assembly during meiotic cytokinesis.29
SCML2 is a testis-enriched component of the Polycomb complex. In mice, it is essential for the global silencing of somatic/progenitor genes and for activation of late-spermatogenesis-specific genes. In fact, the null mice are infertile with small testis, impaired differentiation in spermatogenesis, and apoptosis of differentiating male germ cells.30 We have also identified six infertile men carrying pathogenic variants in its paralog SCML1 for which no model organism is available (see below).

For five genes (POLA1, USP9X, CUL4B, THOC2, and MSL3) with infertile animal models, the associated human phenotype is syndromic. For two of the genes (Pola1 and Usp9x), mouse models clearly indicate their involvement in early stages of spermatogenesis,31,32 whereas Cul4b is a key regulator of post-meiotic sperm development.33
THOC2 has been studied both in Caenorhabditis elegans and Drosophila, and in both models meiosis is affected.34,35
MSL3 encodes a protein that in Drosophila is a master regulator of meiosis, which coordinates the expression of factors required for recombination, and GSC differentiation in the female.36 In mice, Msl3 is expressed in differentiating spermatogonia and in spermatocytes.37 The reported human syndromic phenotypes of these genes include developmental delay and borderline-mild or moderate intellectual disability, and for some, hypergonadotropic hypogonadism or small testes were also described (VEODS [MIM: 301030], PDR [MIM: 301220], XLID99 [MIM: 300919], MRXS99F [MIM: 300968], MRXSC [MIM: 300354], XLID12 [MIM: 300957], and MRXSBA [MIM: 301032]). Unfortunately, the intellectual performance of men with variants in these genes was not formally tested. It is also worth noting that the majority of the above-described syndromes in the literature are caused by deletions, which may include more than one gene.

For 11 genes (SCML1, CMC4, TAF1, TAF9B, FAM9A, APEX2, FATE1, NONO, PASD1, DCAF12L2, and TIMP1), testis expression and published data strongly suggest a role in male fertility, but functional validation is currently lacking.

We report variants in three genes, each of which has previously been described to be deleted in block with other contiguous X chromosome genes in men affected by syndromic azoospermia. On the basis of the expression pattern and their function, we propose these three genes as the most likely cause for the observed reproductive phenotype. SCML1 encodes a protein enriched in spermatocytes. In 2020, Milunsky et al.38 reported a man with NOA and cataract carrying a deletion of SCML1-NHS-RAI2 (NHS [MIM: 302350]). The authors suggested that NHS deletion could be the cause of cataracts, whereas the SCML1 deletion is most likely the cause of NOA. Taking into consideration the expression pattern and the testicular phenotype of our men carrying variants in SCML1 (maturation/meiotic arrest), we propose SCML1 could play a role in human male meiosis. Xq.28 deletions, encompassing among others CMC4, cause Moyamoya disease (MYMY4 [MIM: 300845]), a rare, progressive cerebrovascular disorder that may involve two or more of the following genes: CMC4, FUNDC2, MTCP1, BRCC3, and F8. A common feature of these men is that they are affected by hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, and the only gene deleted in all families showing infertility is CMC4 (Deng et al.39 and reference therein). A similar reproductive phenotype was described by Deng et al.39 in a man with CMC4 and FUNDC2 deletion. The authors suggested that the most likely cause of the phenotype was the absence of CMC4 because it is highly expressed in the fetal testis and involved in cell proliferation.39
FAM9A is a testis-enriched, newly acquired gene and contains a Cor1/Xlr/Xmr-conserved region, which is shared with SYCP3, a structural component of the synaptonemal complex and a known NOA-associated gene. Deletions encompassing both FAM9A and KAL1 have already been reported in men with Kallmann syndrome.40,41 Although KAL1 deletion is the cause for the hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, in both studies affected men were azoospermic, and spermatogenesis was not induced after hormonal treatment,41 which could be explained in part by the absence of FAM9A.

Four of these genes interact with other known NOA-associated genes. TAF1 and TAF9B interact with TAF7L and TAF4B.3 Interestingly, through the analysis of expression datasets, Gura et al.42 determined that, in mice, Taf9b mRNA is coordinately upregulated with Taf4b mRNA during meiosis and that both Taf1 and Taf9b 3ʹ UTRs are directly bound by DAZL in leptotene spermatocytes.42 APEX2 is an evolutionarily conserved protein with multiple nuclease activities that play a critical role in genome and epigenome integrity (Lin et al., 202043 and reference therein). It interacts with multiple other DNA repair genes (BRCA1, BRCA2, RAD51, CHEK1, and BARD1) with essential function in spermatogenesis, both in human and animal models. DCAF12L2 interacts with its paralog DCAF12L1 and shows the same expression pattern, i.e., it is enriched in testis (early/late spermatids) and epididymis. Therefore, it is plausible that DCAF12L2, similar to DCAF12L1 (see paragraph on genes previously described in NOA/cryptozoospermia), plays a role during spermatogenesis and variants in this gene can cause NOA.

Finally, for four additional genes, comprehensive testis expression and protein function strongly suggest essential roles in spermatogenesis. FATE1 encodes a testis-specific protein that is involved in the onset of gonadal sex determination and is co-expressed with SRY in the testis.44 In adulthood, it is strongly expressed in spermatogonia, primary spermatocytes, and Sertoli cells. On the basis of this expression profile, Olesen et al. (2003)45 sequenced this gene in a cohort of 144 unselected infertile men without reporting pathogenic variants. This result is most likely to be due to the small sample size and the highly heterogeneous semen phenotypes (including even normozoospermic men). NONO encodes an RNA-binding protein involved in transcriptional regulation and RNA splicing. It is expressed in Sertoli cells, and it mediates transcription and the coactivation of the androgen receptor.46
PASD1 is a cancer testis antigen (CTA) that acts to repress the circadian clock CLOCK-ARNTL/BMAL1. Although no circadian rhythm is observed in spermatogenesis (Lu et al., 201747 and references therein), the deletion of Piwil2, another CTA, which represses the circadian clock CLOCK/BMAL1, causes azoospermia in mice and humans.21,48 Therefore, PASD1 variants could also lead to defects in spermatogenesis through the same mechanism. TIMP1 encodes an inhibitor of the matrix metalloproteinases. In rodents, FSH induces the secretion of TIMP-1 in Sertoli cells, and it has been predicted to be involved in testicular extracellular matrix remodeling during both testicular development and spermatogenesis as well as in primordial germ cell migration49,50

We performed a targeted investigation for rare coding pathogenic variants in the 21 strong candidate genes in 265 NOA/cryptozoospermic and 54 NOA newly recruited infertile men sequenced after March 2021 in MERGE and Barcelona, respectively. After applying the same variant filtering criteria used for the initial cohorts, we identified nine additional men carrying variants in DCAF12L2 (n = 2), PASD1 (n = 2), CUL4B (n = 2), RBBP7 (n = 1), POLA1 (n = 1), and TAF1 (n = 1). Detailed description of the variants and the phenotypes is described in Figure 2 and Table S4. With the replication study, the total number of infertile men carrying variants increased in RBBP7 (n = 10), TAF1 (n = 6), PASD1 (n = 5), POLA1 (n = 5), CUL4B (n = 4), and DCAF12L2 (n = 4). We have calculated the GDR for each of the 21 genes, and three of them (RBBP7, PASD1, and SCML1) immediately reach the level of moderate evidence and can be added to a diagnostic gene panel (supplemental information).

We identified 434 variants in 100 genes with moderate supporting data in relationship with male infertility (score 3 or 4; Table S1 and supplemental information). Among these 100 genes, we consider as the most promising those showing enriched expression in testis (n = 51 genes) and those for which at least moderate supporting data for a role in spermatogenesis were found in PubMed (n = 4 genes). The 55 genes were subjected to the same exclusion criteria as the strong candidates, resulting in 34/55 genes (123 variants) (Table S5). Among the genes with enriched expression in testis GLUD2 (MIM: 300144) and ATXN3L (MIM: 300920) stood out because they are single-copy, newly acquired genes that were found mutated in six and two men, respectively. Concerning the second group, we identified 15 variants mapping to CENPI, RGN, TMEM255A, and ZNF185. In rat, Cenpi mRNA and Fshr mRNA (a key hormone receptor in spermatogenesis) follow a similar expression pattern during postnatal development as well as at different germ cell stages.51 Moreover, Cenpi is required for the localization of MAD2L2 to kinetochores. In mice, variants in Mad2l2 led to primordial germ cell apoptosis.52 RGN encodes an androgen-target gene in rat testis that may play a role in male reproductive function.53
TMEM255A regulates meiosis and quality of mouse oocytes through both canonical and non-canonical WNT5a signaling pathways.54
ZNF185 (MIM: 300381) encodes a zinc finger protein, and its depletion interferes with actin cytoskeleton remodeling and cell polarization.55 Moreover, studies with antigenic peptides derived from ZNF185 induced fertility suppression in mice without damaging reproductive organs.56

We performed a protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis on the 41 genes with a score ≥ 5 by using STRING. We obtained a significant PPI enrichment in this set of genes (p value < 1.35e−7); the number of expected edges was 3 and the observed was 16 (Figure 4), indicating that among these proteins there are more interactions than would be expected for a random set of proteins of similar size, strongly suggesting that these proteins are at least partially biologically connected. Interestingly, such significant enrichment was also observed with the 21 genes strongly associated with NOA/cryptozoospermia (p value = 0.0123) with 5 observed edges, compared with an expected 1 edge (Figure S3)

In order to provide further evidence for our findings, and to identify further candidate genes not fulfilling our in-house score criteria, we performed burden testing. We compared the number of individuals carrying predicted-to-be-pathogenic variants in our cohort of 2,354 infertile men versus the 76,702 XY individuals represented in gnomAD (Table S6). We initially identified 104 genes enriched with pathogenic variants in infertile men compared to the gnomAD population. However, only 17 genes reached significance after conservative adjustment for multiple testing (adjusted p values ≤ 0.05, Table S6). Of note, ten of the 17 genes were classified as having a “moderate/strong relationship with NOA/cryptozoospermia” according to our in-house gene prioritization. Nevertheless, the majority of genes classified as “moderate/strong,” including some well-validated NOA/oligozoospermia genes such as TEX11 and AR, did not reach formal significance after Bonferroni correction. The remaining seven genes with significant p values were classified as limited or no evidence. For instance, a fertile father has been found with a pathogenic variant in MAGEA3, prompting questioning of its role in NOA/cryptozoospermia. The association with male infertility remains unclear for the other six genes because the currently available data on testicular expression, model organisms, and PubMed do not suggest any role in spermatogenesis (Table S6). The most significant adjusted p values (p values < 10−9) were obtained for three genes with limited (AFF2 [MIM: 300806], SUPT20HL1, and SUPT20HL2) and one gene with moderate (ARMCX4 [MIM: 301046]) supporting data according to our in-house prioritization. All these four genes are of potential interest for future studies.

Until now, the proposed “masculinization” of the human X chromosome based on the observed evolutionary aspects has lacked clinical evidence due to the identification of very few X chromosome genes with clinical relevance.3,7 Thanks to our large study based on 2,354 NOA/cryptozoospermic men belonging to four cohorts, this gap is substantially reduced. The clinical relevance of a gene for a given disease depends on a number of criteria, among them the recurrence of men carrying variants and the subsequent identification of variants in independent study populations. Therefore, as the first step, we searched for variants mapping to genes already reported to be responsible for NOA/cryptozoospermia. We identified a total of 83 infertile men carrying predicted pathogenic variants in 17 genes, corresponding to a detection rate of 3.5% in our study population. Interestingly, 15/17 genes were mutated in more than one man and 13/17 in more than one cohort.

In this study, we identified 362 recurrently mutated genes in NOA/cryptozoospermic men not previously associated with infertility. The most relevant genes are the 21 recurrently affected genes for which we could predict a strong relationship with a reproductive phenotype. We performed a targeted look up of variants in these genes in two independent cohorts and identified nine additional men carrying variants in six of the 21 genes. The frequency of potential disease-causing variants in these newly discovered 21 candidate genes was 2.6%, and most importantly, >80% the genes were mutated in more than one cohort. The majority of these genes are involved in meiosis, supporting our previous observation of a relatively higher incidence of monogenic forms in men with NOA with meiotic arrest compared to other testicular phenotypes.57 By adding the 21 strong candidate genes discovered in our study, we double the number of X chromosome genes associated with severe spermatogenic quantitative defects.

As stated above, currently only three X chromosome genes (USP26, AR, and TEX11) reached high enough evidence (GDR) to be proposed for a diagnostic NOA gene panel.3 As a result of our study, seven additional genes reach moderate/strong GDR evidence, increasing the number of genes to be included in the diagnostic setting from three to ten (see Table 1 and Table S8).

In addition, we propose 34 potentially relevant genes that currently present a “moderate relationship with NOA/cryptozoospermia,” which will require further validation in other large-scale studies.

Our most significant discovery is the role of RBBP7 variants in severe spermatogenic impairment. This gene was mutated in ten men affected by early spermatogenic failure, belonging to all cohorts (including the replication cohort). To further support its relevance, the burden test showed that pathogenic variants in RBBP7 were significantly more common in infertile men than in gnomAD control individuals (adjusted p value = 5.07E−6). Since the orthologous gene in Drosophila, Caf1-p55, shows a high degree of homology with this gene, we performed RNAi-mediated knockdown experiments in this model organism. We demonstrated that Caf1-p55 is necessary for GSCs maintenance, reflecting the human phenotype. Interestingly it has been shown that RBBP7, and CUL4B, which was found mutated in four infertile men, may form a complex. This complex, called CRL4BRBBP7, is involved in HUWE1 degradation.58 Importantly, variants in HUWE1 were recently identified as a cause of NOA10. Interestingly, HUWE1 has been shown to be involved in BRCA1 degradation. This fits with the observation that knockdown of RBBP7 through siRNA in cell culture leads to a shortened BRCA1 half-life as a result of the lack of HUWE1 inhibition.58 On the basis of these interactions, we speculate that RBBP7, CUL4B, or HUWE1 may lead to both spermatogenic failure and cancer predisposition. In this regard, it is worth noting that epidemiological studies reported significantly increased risk for malignancies in NOA-affected men.59 Among others, genetic factors, especially variants in genes encoding proteins involved DNA repair, have already been proposed to explain this phenomenon.4,26,57

By burden testing, we identified 17 genes significantly enriched (after Bonferroni correction) with predicted-as-pathogenic variants in infertile men compared to the gnomAD population. Four of these genes (AFF2, SUPT20HL2, SUPT20HL1, and ARMCX4) reach the highest adjusted p values for significance (<10−9), warranting future investigation. In fact, support for a putative role of these genes in severe spermatogenic failure is currently scarce. AFF2 encodes for an RNA-binding protein that might be involved in alternative splicing regulation. The expression of this gene is enhanced in bone marrow, brain, epididymis, and placenta, and AFF2 variants are associated with intellectual developmental disorders (XLID109 [MIM: 309548]). Both SUPT20HL2 and SUPT20HL1 are predicted to encode for proteins involved in transcription coregulator activity. However, according to the Human Protein Atlas, no expression has been detected in any adult tissue. Moderate supporting data are available for ARCMX4. This gene encodes a ubiquitously expressed protein with unknown function. Although Lu et al.60 proposed a potential role of this gene in NOA, the reported variant was classified as likely benign by Houston et al.3 In our study we describe four ARCMX4 VUSs in men affected by NOA and cryptozoospermia. On the other hand, well-validated NOA/oligozoospermia-associated genes, such as TEX11 and AR, did not reach significance after Bonferroni correction. This could be in part due to the fact that the gnomAD control population is not an ideal cohort for our study because sperm parameters and fertility status of these individuals were not analyzed.

In conclusion, thanks to the efforts of four centers and their many contributing partners, we provide comprehensive genomic data on the X chromosome in what we believe to be the largest group of NOA/cryptozoospermic men available to date. We propose 21 novel genetic etiologies for severe spermatogenic failure with strong supporting data, including ten genes for which model organisms recapitulate the human phenotype. Our analysis represents a substantial step toward reducing the gap in our understanding of X-linked genetic causes of spermatogenic failure.
