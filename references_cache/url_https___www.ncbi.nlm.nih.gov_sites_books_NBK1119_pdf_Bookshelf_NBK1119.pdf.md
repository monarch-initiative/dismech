Summary
Clinical characteristics
The dystrophinopathies comprise a spectrum including Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) and Becker
muscular dystrophy (BMD), which predominantly affect males and involve skeletal and cardiac muscle, and
DMD-related cardiomyopathy, characterized by cardiac muscle involvement typically without symptomatic
skeletal muscle involvement that affects both males and females. DMD is characterized by early onset and
progressive weakness, motor delays, increased risk of cognitive issues, neurobehavioral and psychiatric
manifestations, progressive respiratory issues, decline in cardiac function, constipation, osteopenia, and
Increased mortality. BMD is characterized by later onset and slower progression, a more heterogeneous cardiac
phenotype, and longer survival. Longevity in those with dystrophinopathies has increased with development of
improved therapies.
Diagnosis/testing
The diagnosis of a dystrophinopathy is established in a proband with suggestive clinical and laboratory findings
and a hemizygous or heterozygous pathogenic variant in DMD identified by molecular genetic testing.

conceiving or as soon as pregnancy is recognized; women with dilated cardiomyopathy should be managed by a
cardiac specialist with experience in treatment of heart failure and/or neuromuscular disorders and a high-risk
obstetrician.
Genetic counseling
Dystrophinopathies are inherited in an X-linked manner.
Male proband: The risk to sibs depends on the genetic status of the mother. If the mother of the proband is
heterozygous for a DMD pathogenic variant, the chance of the mother transmitting the pathogenic variant in
each pregnancy is 50%. Males who inherit the DMD pathogenic variant will be affected and will typically have
clinical manifestations similar to those of the proband. Females who inherit the pathogenic variant will be
heterozygotes and at risk for DMD-related cardiomyopathy. Heterozygous females are mostly free of severe
skeletal muscle disease but may have milder muscle involvement or, rarely, a DMD or BMD phenotype resulting
from non-random X-chromosome inactivation.

Female proband: The risk to sibs depends on the genetic status of the parents. If the mother of the proband has a
DMD pathogenic variant, the chance of the mother transmitting it in each pregnancy is 50%. If the father of the
proband has a DMD pathogenic variant, he will transmit it to all his daughters and none of his sons.
Once the DMD pathogenic variant has been identified in an affected family member, identification of female
heterozygotes and prenatal testing are possible. Depending on the familial DMD pathogenic variant,
preimplantation genetic testing may be an option for some families but can be challenging if phasing is
necessary to confirm test results.
Diagnosis
For the purposes of this GeneReview, the terms "male" and "female" are narrowly defined as the individual's
biological sex at birth as it determines clinical care [Caughey et al 2021].
Clinical and molecular genetic diagnostic criteria for the dystrophinopathies have been published [Birnkrant et
al 2018a, Birnkrant et al 2018b, Birnkrant et al 2018c, Fratter et al 2020].
The dystrophinopathies comprise a spectrum including Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) and Becker
muscular dystrophy (BMD), which predominantly affect males and involve skeletal and cardiac muscle, and
DMD-related cardiomyopathy, characterized by cardiac muscle involvement typically without symptomatic
skeletal muscle involvement that affects both males and females.
Suggestive Findings
A dystrophinopathy should be suspected in a proband with any of the following clinical and laboratory findings
and family history.
Clinical Findings
Skeletal muscle involvement typically in a male proband:
• In children age two to three years, delay in the age of onset of independent walking; frequent falls; inability
to keep up with peers [Duan et al 2021]
• In adolescence to adulthood, onset of progressive symmetric muscle weakness (proximal > distal) often
manifesting as calf hypertrophy, weakness of quadriceps femoris, and preservation of neck flexor muscle
strength
Cardiac muscle involvement in a male or female proband:
• Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), defined as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) <50%. In individuals
with DMD, 17% have late gadolinium enhancement in cardiac MRI prior to age ten years.
Neurodevelopmental delay (e.g., speech-language delays, motor impairments) and cognitive dysfunction (IQ
deficits, learning disabilities) typically in a male proband [Banihani et al 2015, Ricotti et al 2016, Geuens et al
2024, Baldi et al 2025]:
• Speech-language delays often precede motor manifestations [Baldi et al 2025].
• Deficits in verbal memory, attention, and executive function are common [Banihani et al 2015, Ricotti et
al 2016, Geuens et al 2024, Baldi et al 2025].
• Neurobehavioral/psychiatric manifestations include autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention-deficit/
hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), depression, anxiety disorders, and obsessive-compulsive disorder in
adolescents/adults [Banihani et al 2015, Ricotti et al 2016, Geuens et al 2024, Baldi et al 2025].

Laboratory Findings
Serum creatine phosphokinase (CK) concentration. When CK concentrations are 10 times above normal, this
can be the best initial screening test for the diagnosis of a dystrophinopathy. Note: (1) CK can also be a screening
test for a dystrophinopathy in boys who initially present with neurobehavioral/psychiatric manifestations such
as ASD. (2) Due to its high variability, CK concentration cannot be used for continuous monitoring of
therapeutic disease progression [Wang et al 2021].
Note that newborn screening (NBS) for dystrophinopathies using various methods (e.g., blood CK
concentrations, multigene panel, genome sequencing) is now being performed (or being considered) in several
states in the United States.
Family History
Family history may suggest X-linked inheritance (e.g., no male-to-male transmission) or the proband may
appear to be the only affected family member (approximately one third of DMD pathogenic variants occur de
novo in the proband). Absence of a known family history does not preclude the diagnosis of a dystrophinopathy.
• A family history consistent with X-linked transmission may not be apparent when there are no maternal
uncles or male sibs, as dystrophinopathy-related findings are most noted in males; however, a female may
be the proband in some families.
• Heterozygous females are typically asymptomatic or may exhibit weakness, fatigue, and cardiac
involvement. DCM is the most common cardiac manifestation in females heterozygous for a DMD
pathogenic variant [Novelli et al 2025].
Establishing the Diagnosis
Male proband. The diagnosis of a dystrophinopathy is established in a male proband with suggestive findings
and a hemizygous pathogenic (or likely pathogenic) variant in DMD identified by molecular genetic testing (see
Table 1).
Female proband. The diagnosis of a dystrophinopathy is usually established in a female proband with
suggestive findings and a heterozygous pathogenic (or likely pathogenic) variant in DMD identified by
molecular genetic testing (see Table 1). Note: On rare occasions, an affected female could have biallelic DMD
pathogenic (or likely pathogenic) variants.
Note: (1) Per American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics / Association for Molecular Pathology
variant interpretation guidelines, the terms "pathogenic variant" and "likely pathogenic variant" are synonymous
in a clinical setting, meaning that both are considered diagnostic and can be used for clinical decision making
[Richards et al 2015]. Reference to "pathogenic variants" in this GeneReview is understood to include likely
pathogenic variants. (2) Identification of a hemizygous or heterozygous DMD variant of uncertain significance
does not establish or rule out the diagnosis.
Molecular genetic testing approaches can include a combination of gene-targeted testing (single-gene testing,
multigene panel) and comprehensive genomic testing (chromosomal microarray analysis, exome sequencing,
genome sequencing). Gene-targeted testing requires that the clinician determine which gene(s) are likely
involved (Option 1 and Option 2), whereas genomic testing does not (Option 3).
Option 1
When the phenotypic and laboratory findings suggest the diagnosis of a dystrophinopathy, molecular genetic
testing approaches can include a step-by-step approach from the European Molecular Genetics Quality Network
(EMQN) [Fratter et al 2020, Figure 1]:

Option 2
Recently, assays designed to detect both CNVs and sequence variants (Levels 1 and 2 in Option 1) have become
available. These assays use next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based assays of the entire DMD gene that include
full sequence analysis as well as deep intronic variant detection with CNVs and single-nucleotide variant (SNV)
calling [Zhao et al 2024]. These NGS-based single comprehensive assays are suitable for diagnostic testing,
heterozygote detection in females, and prenatal testing [Nallamilli et al 2021].
If no DMD pathogenic variants are identified, the following is recommended: perform RNA/cDNA analysis on
the involved muscle or RNA analysis of muscle tissue, myogenic cells from urine-derived stem cells, or
extracellular RNA analysis from urine.
Note: Males with somatic mosaicism for a DMD pathogenic variant have also been reported, though very rarely.
It can be difficult to identify these affected males by routine diagnostic methods because their mild clinical
manifestations often differ from those of the classic dystrophinopathy phenotypes. If somatic mosaicism is
suspected, use of high diagnostic sensitivity methods (e.g., NGS with a high sequence read depth) is
recommended to detect DMD mosaicism.
Option 3
When the diagnosis of a dystrophinopathy has not been considered because an individual has atypical
phenotypic features, some clinicians opt for multigene panel testing.
• A multigene panel that includes DMD and other genes of interest (see Differential Diagnosis) is most
likely to identify the genetic cause of the condition while limiting identification of pathogenic variants and
variants of uncertain significance in genes that do not explain the underlying phenotype. A multigene
panel may also be most appropriate for individuals with less severe clinical presentations. Males with later-
onset dystrophinopathy and most females may not have findings clinically distinct enough to suggest
single-gene testing of DMD as the initial diagnostic test. Note: (1) The genes included in the panel and the
diagnostic sensitivity of the testing used for each gene vary by laboratory and are likely to change over
time. (2) Some multigene panels may include genes not associated with the condition discussed in this
GeneReview. (3) In some laboratories, panel options may include a custom laboratory-designed panel
and/or custom phenotype-focused exome analysis that includes genes specified by the clinician. (4)
Methods used in a panel may include sequence analysis, deletion/duplication analysis, and/or other non-
sequencing-based tests.

Clinical Characteristics
The dystrophinopathies comprise a spectrum including Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) and Becker
muscular dystrophy (BMD), which predominantly affect males and involve skeletal and cardiac muscle, and
DMD-related cardiomyopathy, characterized by cardiac muscle involvement typically without symptomatic
skeletal muscle involvement that affects both males and females.
Clinical Description
Skeletal Muscle Involvement
Skeletal muscle findings range from severe to mild skeletal muscle involvement (e.g., myalgia and/or cramps) to
absence of skeletal muscle involvement.
At the most severe end of the dystrophinopathy spectrum – classically defined as DMD – the earliest phase of
skeletal muscle manifestations begins shortly after birth and lasts until age five years, when subtle motor delays
such as late walking, clumsiness, frequent falls, and difficulty running occur. Parents may notice a longer time to
achieve motor milestones and Gowers sign, a hallmark feature of DMD, reflecting skeletal muscle weakness

often accompanied by pseudohypertrophy of the calf muscles. Albeit not a classic finding, hypertrophy can be
noted in the forearm and the tongue that can lead to difficulties in fine motor tasks and speech, respectively.
Muscle degeneration typically advances from proximal muscles such as pelvic and hip girdle muscles to trunk
and axial muscles [Birnkrant et al 2018b, Venugopal & Pavlakis 2025]. With age, proximal weakness becomes
functionally limiting. In early adolescence, progressive decline in ambulation and the risk of contractures and
scoliosis increase. Loss of independent ambulation historically clusters in the early teens. From adolescence
onward generalized weakness affects the trunk and upper limbs, with progressive loss of upper body function.
More moderate skeletal muscle manifestations (e.g., BMD) may be characterized by onset in adolescence to
adulthood of progressive symmetric muscle weakness (proximal > distal) often with calf hypertrophy; weakness
of quadriceps femoris (sometimes the only sign); activity-induced cramping (present in some individuals);
preservation of neck flexor muscle strength; and flexion contractures of the elbows (if present, late in the course).
Wheelchair dependency typically occurs after age 16 years, with some individuals remaining ambulatory into
their 30s and even into their 40s and beyond.
Cognitive Abilities
Dystrophinopathies are associated with a distinctive neuropsychological profile that reflects both direct central
nervous system involvement and secondary developmental influences. While many individuals demonstrate
overall intellectual functioning within the broadly average range, there is an elevated risk for global cognitive
delay (intellectual developmental disorder) [Pascual-Morena et al 2023], particularly in those who have early
motor delays [Mirski & Crawford 2014]. Individuals who have broadly age-appropriate intellectual functioning
can have variable cognitive abilities, with greater difficulties in language processing, attentional regulation, and
executive functions. Verbal abilities, including expressive language, verbal working memory, and phonologic
processing, are more vulnerable than visuospatial skills, contributing to academic difficulties in reading, writing,
and higher-order language tasks [Darmahkasih et al 2020]. Executive function weaknesses, including difficulties
with attentional regulation, cognitive flexibility, planning, and organization, are also more common and can
persist across development, even in individuals without global intellectual impairment [Battini et al 2018, Fee et
al 2019, Gillenstrand et al 2026].
These cognitive features are frequently observed in the absence of cognitive regression and appear largely
independent of motor decline [Chieffo et al 2023], supporting their classification as central to
dystrophinopathies rather than secondary to medical treatment or physical disability. Consistent with this,
variation in cognitive outcomes has been linked to genotype, with increasing risk and severity associated with
distal pathogenic variants affecting brain-expressed isoforms, particularly Dp140 and Dp71. Individuals lacking
these isoforms show higher rates of intellectual disability, language impairment, and difficulties with attention
regulation and executive functions, which may manifest in the context of diagnosed attention-deficit/
hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [Darmahkasih et al 2020, Pascual-Morena
et al 2023]. However, cognitive and behavioral vulnerabilities exist along a continuum, and many individuals will
exhibit subclinical weaknesses that still have meaningful functional impact on learning, independence, and
medical care. As such, proactive screening for neurodevelopmental differences, followed by developmentally
informed assessment, is increasingly emphasized as important in medical care [Colvin et al 2018, Colvin et al
2022, Baldi et al 2025, Truba et al 2025, Miranda et al 2026].
Neurobehavioral and Psychiatric Manifestations
Dystrophinopathies are associated with an increased prevalence of neurobehavioral and psychiatric conditions
[Birnkrant et al 2018c, Pascual-Morena et al 2023, Brogna et al 2025]. In addition to elevated rates of ADHD and
ASD, there may also be anxiety disorders, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, and mood disturbances associated
with substantial diagnostic comorbidity [Darmahkasih et al 2020, Pascual-Morena et al 2023]. Problems with
emotional regulation, including irritability, low frustration tolerance, rigidity, and oppositional behaviors, are

frequently noted in childhood, while internalizing symptoms such as anxiety and depression become
increasingly salient across adolescence and early adulthood [Latimer et al 2017, Brogna et al 2025]. There is
likely an interaction between neurobiological vulnerability to these conditions and the impact of
dystrophinopathies on physical functioning and independence, with suicidality becoming more likely around
the loss of ambulation [Birnkrant et al 2018c]. However, psychiatric symptoms are not fully explained by disease
severity or physical disability, supporting that these symptoms are likely manifestations of altered
neurodevelopmental trajectories arising from central nervous system involvement [Brogna et al 2025, Vaillend et
al 2025].
The neurobehavioral and psychiatric manifestations of dystrophinopathies are intertwined with cognitive
variability. Weaknesses in attention, executive functions, and language processing can contribute to difficulties
with emotional control and social cognition, which in turn amplify the cognitive vulnerabilities. Secondary
factors such as sleep disturbance, chronic fatigue, corticosteroid exposure, and psychosocial stress associated
with living with a progressive medical condition are also important modifiers in neurobehavioral and psychiatric
functioning [Birnkrant et al 2018c, Pascual-Morena et al 2023]. From a clinical perspective, neurobehavioral and
psychiatric symptoms exist along a continuum, ranging from subclinical vulnerabilities to diagnosable disorders,
and are frequently underrecognized in routine neuromuscular care. Proactive screening, developmentally
informed assessment, and integrated behavioral support to mitigate downstream effects on quality of life,
functional independence, and treatment adherence are critical [Baldi et al 2025, Truba et al 2025].
Respiratory Issues
The progression of respiratory muscle weakness leads to hypoventilation and increase in the rapid shallow
breathing (RSB) index due to increased fatigue, reduced muscle action, paradoxical diaphragmatic movement
(inward, upward motion of the diaphragm and abdomen during inhalation), and pseudohypertrophy of the
diaphragm with connective tissue. Overall spirometry values of an individual are lower, including inspiratory
and expiratory pressures that lead to a pattern of restrictive lung disease, hypercapnia, oxygen desaturation, and
ultimately respiratory failure [Lo Mauro & Aliverti 2016].
Nocturnal hypoventilation, which is often the earliest manifestation of respiratory issues, is manifest by sleep-
disordered breathing, morning headaches, and poor sleep quality. Ineffective cough and impaired airway
clearance increase the risk for infection. Reduced spirometry values, such as decreasing forced vital capacity,
lower maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures, and reduced peak cough flow, are also key clinical
manifestations [Hnaini et al 2023].
Cardiomyopathy
Individuals with dystrophinopathies are at high risk of progressive cardiac disease. This is secondary to the lack
of dystrophin expression in the myocardium, which leads to fatty-fibrotic replacement of healthy tissue and
dilated cardiomyopathy [Bourke et al 2022]. Individuals with DMD exhibit a decline in cardiac function that
correlates with age and skeletal muscle decline [Spurney et al 2014]. Cardiac involvement usually becomes
evident in the second decade of life. However, early evidence of cardiac dysfunction can be seen in up to 17% of
individuals younger than age ten years [Hor et al 2013].
Individuals with BMD have a more heterogenous cardiac phenotype. Only a small proportion of children and
adolescents with BMD exhibit early signs of cardiac involvement, but those who do develop cardiomyopathy
often face a severe and rapidly progressive decline [Connuck et al 2008]. The risk of developing heart failure
increases with age, with up to 70% of individuals with BMD developing symptoms of heart failure by age 40
years; heart failure is the primary cause of mortality in this population [Kamdar & Garry 2016].
Individuals with dystrophinopathies are at increased risk of arrhythmias due to an increase in fibrotic changes of
the myocardium with disease progression [Villa et al 2016].

Gastrointestinal (GI) Issues
GI issues in individuals with dystrophinopathies result from enteric dysmotility, with marked delay in gastric
emptying and prolonged orocecal transit time and colonic transit time. Constipation, the most common GI
issue, persists in two thirds of individuals, despite laxative use. GI dysmotility has not been correlated with
respiratory difficulties, suggesting the cause is intrinsic neuromuscular dysfunction rather than secondary to
respiratory difficulties [Lo Cascio et al 2016, Blokhuis et al 2024].
Bone Fractures
Individuals with a dystrophinopathy, particularly DMD, have a high fracture burden due to the use of
corticosteroid (see Table 5), with nearly half experiencing one or more fractures; the probability increases to 64%
by age 20 years. Vertebral fractures were associated with longer steroid exposure (greater than three years) and
lower lumbar bone mineral density z scores, with body mass index (BMI) paradoxically trending higher in those
with non-vertebral fractures. Additionally, individuals' functional decline accelerated post fracture, with
ambulatory individuals showing a 12.5% greater annual North Star Ambulatory Assessment (NSAA) score
deterioration (0.44 vs 0.27 points/year) versus fracture-free peers [Liaw et al 2023].
Osteopenia often develops in childhood due to immobility, muscle weakness, and suboptimal mechanical
loading of the skeleton. Osteoporosis is common in adolescents and young adults, particularly those with long-
term corticosteroid therapy, delayed puberty, vitamin D insufficiency, and/or nutritional deficiencies [De Ford et
al 2025].
Although vertebral fractures may be asymptomatic, they can lead to progressive kyphosis, height loss, and back
pain, whereas long bone fractures often result in immobilization and functional decline, affecting ambulation,
transfers, and overall independence. Cumulative fractures and osteoporosis contribute to increased disease
burden, hospitalization, and reduced quality of life in adolescents and young adults with DMD [Phung et al
2023].
Prognosis
DMD. A longitudinal analysis of 40,274 individuals revealed a median survival age of 22 years, with survival
probabilities decreased from 99.8% at age ten years to 13.3% at age 40 years. Despite these observations,
longevity has increased from age 18.3 years for those born before 1970 to an average of 28.1 years for those born
after 1990, likely a reflection of advancing therapeutics. Mortality rates have significantly dropped from those
born before 1970 to after 1990 (265 vs 27.6 deaths/1,000/year, respectively) [Broomfield et al 2021].
BMD. Life span averages 40 to 50 years but is highly variable.
DMD-related cardiomyopathy. Life span is highly variable, ranging from adolescence to later adulthood.
Without early intervention, life expectancy can be shortened due to rapid heart failure. However, individuals
diagnosed early who respond well to heart therapies or undergo transplantation can achieve a normal or near-
normal lifespan.
Heterozygous Females
Heterozygous females (also referred to "manifesting heterozygotes") are at risk for DMD-related cardiomyopathy
but can also manifest skeletal muscle symptoms such as muscle cramps, fatigue, pain, and overt muscle
weakness.
Cardiomyopathy. DMD heterozygotes are at risk for developing cardiomyopathy, even in the absence of skeletal
manifestations, with an estimated lifetime risk of 7.3%-16.7%. Pregnancy leads to an increased volume load on
the heart, and there is an increased risk of developing clinically significant peripartum cardiomyopathy in DMD
heterozygotes.

Nomenclature
Dystrophinopathy. The term dystrophinopathy encompasses all phenotypes (in both males and females)
associated with a pathogenic variant in DMD, including Duchenne muscular dystrophy, Becker muscular
dystrophy, and DMD-related dilated cardiomyopathy.
Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) was clinically defined in the past by inability to walk by age 16-18
months; positive Gowers sign or toe walking (at any age, especially younger than age 5 years); progressive
symmetric muscle weakness (proximal > distal) often with calf hypertrophy; and wheelchair dependency before
age 13 years.
Intermediate dystrophinopathy refers to dystrophinopathy with severity between that associated with DMD
and Becker muscular dystrophy and wheelchair dependence between ages 13 and 16 years.

Prevalence
One in 3,600 live-born males are affected with dystrophinopathies worldwide, and two of every 10,000 live-born
males in the United States [Venugopal & Pavlakis 2025].
Genetically Related (Allelic) Disorders
DMD-related intellectual disability without muscle weakness has been described in several individuals with
pathogenic variants in DMD [Juan-Mateu et al 2013, de Brouwer et al 2014]. Additional data are needed to
confirm the association of DMD pathogenic variants with isolated intellectual disability.
Contiguous Gene Deletions
Xp21 deletion (previously referred to as complex glycerol kinase deficiency) is a contiguous gene deletion
disorder caused by deletion of NR0B1 (causing X-linked adrenal hypoplasia congenita) and GK (causing glycerol
kinase deficiency) and, in some instances, deletion of DMD. The clinical findings in Xp21 deletion vary and can
include metabolic crisis during starvation, hypoglycemia, seizures, growth restriction, and developmental delay.
Developmental delay has been reported in males with Xp21 deletion when the deletion extends proximally to
include DMD or when larger deletions extend distally to include IL1RAPL1 and DMD.

and the disorders caused by their deletion include the following:
• XK (McLeod neuroacanthocytosis syndrome)

Prednisone. In a systematic review of 12 randomized or quasi-randomized controlled trials, Matthews et al
[2016] concluded that daily prednisone at 0.75 mg/kg/day significantly improved muscle strength and motor
function over a six- to 12-month period compared to placebo. Improvements were noted in stair climbing,
walking/running speed, time to complete Gowers sign, and respiratory metrics. Comparative studies reported
weekend-only treatment (i.e., two consecutive days per week) with 5 mg/kg of prednisone to be equally effective
as daily dosing during a 12-month period. Adverse effects of corticosteroid treatments included significant
weight gain, behavioral changes, excessive hair growth, and cushingoid features. The side effects were more
frequent with higher doses and daily administration, although generally manageable.
Deflazacort. In some studies, individuals treated with deflazacort showed a slower decline in motor function
and longer maintenance of key milestones (e.g., rising from supine) compared to individuals treated with
prednisone [McDonald et al 2020]. In a real-world outcomes study on long-term prednisone and deflazacort use
in individuals with DMD, Marden et al [2020] reported median age at loss of ambulation of 15.6 years and 13.5
years for deflazacort and prednisone, respectively. Deflazacort was also associated with a reduction in the hazard
of loss of ambulation and risk of scoliosis by 53% and 59%, respectively, compared to prednisone. Dosing of 0.75
mg/kg/day and 0.9 mg/kg/day for prednisone and deflazacort, respectively, showed lower frequency of weight
gain with deflazacort [Bonifati et al 2000].
Vamorolone is associated with a more favorable safety profile compared with prednisone, with clinical trial data
showing improved growth trajectories, fewer behavioral problems, and reduced cushingoid appearance and
metabolic side effects. Vamorolone also functions as a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, thus providing
additional cardiac and metabolic advantages, while maintaining motor efficacy similar to that of prednisone in
boys with DMD, including comparable improvements in timed motor tests, and demonstrating better
tolerability. A disadvantage of vamorolone treatment (compared to treatment with deflazacort and prednisolone)
is the association with central nervous system side effects. In a study assessing the depression side effects of
corticosteroids using the force swim test and tail suspension test, deflazacort did not increase immobile time,
whereas high-dose prednisone and all doses of vamorolone increased immobile time significantly [Liu et al
2024].

In GeneReviews, a targeted therapy is one that addresses the specific underlying mechanism of disease causation
(regardless of whether the therapy is significantly efficacious for one or more manifestation of the genetic condition);
would otherwise not be considered without knowledge of the underlying genetic cause of the condition; or could lead
to a cure. —ED
Histone Deacetylase Inhibitor Therapy
Givinostat (Duvyzat®), a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, is approved for individuals with DMD age six
years and older. FDA approval was based primarily on a Phase III randomized, placebo-controlled trial showing

that givinostat significantly slowed functional decline over a 48-week period [Mercuri et al 2024]. The primary

Exon skipping therapies use antisense oligonucleotides (AONs) (short, synthetic pieces of nucleic acid) to
modify how the dystrophin pre-mRNA is spliced. AONs bind to specific sequences on dystrophin pre-mRNA,
masking the exon from the splicing machinery [Chwalenia et al 2025]. The spliceosome consequently removes
the faulty exon during mRNA formation, resulting in mRNA that restores the reading frame of the gene, thus
allowing the muscle cell to produce a shortened but partially functional dystrophin protein [Leckie et al 2024].
Exon-skipping therapies can only be given to individuals whose DMD pathogenic variant is compatible with the
exon-skipping therapy. Another major limitation is the requirement for repeated dosing, because AONs are
rapidly cleared and do not integrate into the genome. Infusion-related reactions, such as fever, chills, and rash as
well as kidney toxicity and injection site complications, are some of the safety concerns associated with this form
of therapy [Chung Liang et al 2022].
Gene transfer therapy introduces a functional version of DMD into muscle cells to compensate for the defective
DMD allele. Because the complete gene is too large (2.4 Mb) to fit into standard delivery systems, adeno-

associated virus (AAV)-mediated gene therapies use a shortened but functional copy of DMD (micro-dystrophin
gene) encoding micro-dystrophin, which retains key domains. The minigene is delivered using a viral vector,
most commonly AAV [Zhao et al 2025]. The virus acts as a carrier, delivering the minigene into skeletal and
cardiac muscles. The minigene remains as an episome in skeletal and cardiac muscle cells and expresses micro-
dystrophin. The goal is to reduce muscle damage, improve the durability of muscle membranes, and slow disease
progression [Berling et al 2023, Shimizu-Motohashi 2025].
There are several broad limitations of gene therapy. Micro-dystrophin is a truncated protein, so it does not fully
restore normal muscle function. Individuals who have undergone AAV-mediated gene therapy cannot generally
be re-dosed with AAV because of the strong, long-lasting immune responses. Safety concerns include immune
reactions to AAV , which manifest as elevated liver enzymes (hepatitis), increased risk of severe inflammatory
responses, pre-existing neutralizing antibodies, and yet-unknown adverse outcomes. Gene therapy
administration requires multidisciplinary expertise and a gene therapy team to safely manage adverse events
[W olff et al 2025, Mendell et al 2026, Shieh et al 2026].

Agents/Circumstances to Avoid
Corticosteroids are the mainstay of treatment; however, abrupt discontinuation of steroids must be avoided
[Matthews et al 2016]. There is a risk of adrenal crisis due to chronic steroid use, and stress dosing is
recommended [Birnkrant et al 2018c].
Statins may exacerbate muscle weakness and cause rhabdomyolysis, despite reduction of creatine kinase levels
and lowered damage with increased muscle function using simvastatin in dystrophic mice [Whitehead et al
2015].
Anesthetics such as succinylcholine can trigger malignant hyperthermia [Birnkrant et al 2018a].
Evaluation of Relatives at Risk
Male relatives. It is appropriate to evaluate at-risk male relatives of an affected individual in order to identify as
early as possible those who would benefit from prompt initiation of supportive care and targeted therapy. Such
testing can also allow for correct diagnosis of early (and often nonspecific) neurologic, behavioral, and/or
cognitive signs and symptoms.
Female relatives. It is appropriate to evaluate at-risk female family members (i.e., first-degree relatives of a
proband with a DMD pathogenic variant) to identify as early as possible those who would benefit from cardiac
and skeletal muscle evaluation and surveillance.
Evaluations of at-risk relatives can include:
• Molecular genetic testing if the DMD pathogenic variant in the family is known;
• Molecular genetic testing of the at-risk relative if an affected family member is not available for testing: by
deletion/duplication analysis first and, if no pathogenic variant is identified, by sequence analysis.
See Genetic Counseling for issues related to testing of at-risk relatives for genetic counseling purposes.
Pregnancy Management for Heterozygous Females
Symptomatic heterozygous women should undergo an evaluation for DMD-related cardiomyopathy and skeletal
muscle involvement ideally prior to conceiving a pregnancy or as soon as the pregnancy is recognized.
Asymptomatic women who are heterozygous for a DMD pathogenic variant should consider undergoing a
cardiac evaluation prior to conception or when a pregnancy is recognized. It is recommended that women with
evidence of dilated cardiomyopathy should be treated and/or monitored by a cardiac specialist with experience
in the treatment of heart failure and/or neuromuscular disorders and a high-risk obstetrician [Riguzzi et al

Genetic Counseling
Genetic counseling is the process of providing individuals and families with information on the nature, mode(s) of
inheritance, and implications of genetic disorders to help them make informed medical and personal decisions. The
following section deals with genetic risk assessment and the use of family history and genetic testing to clarify genetic
status for family members; it is not meant to address all personal, cultural, or ethical issues that may arise or to
substitute for consultation with a genetics professional. —ED.
Mode of Inheritance
Dystrophinopathies – comprising Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD),
and DMD-related cardiomyopathy – are inherited in an X-linked manner.
In most families, an affected male is the index case; rarely, a female is affected and represents the index case.
Non-random X-chromosome inactivation (XCI) is a long-recognized cause of clinically manifesting
heterozygous females. Other genetic mechanisms that can explain the rare occurrence of DMD/BMD in females
include a deletion involving Xp21.2, an X-chromosome rearrangement involving Xp21.2 or complete absence of
an X chromosome, uniparental disomy (UPD) of the X chromosome, and biallelic DMD pathogenic variants.
Note: Genetic mechanisms underlying DMD/BMD in females other than non-random XCI are not discussed
further in this section.
Risk to Family Members
Parents of a male proband
• The father of an affected male will not have the disorder, nor will he be hemizygous for the DMD
pathogenic variant; therefore, he does not require further evaluation/testing.
• In a family with more than one affected individual, the mother of an affected male is an obligate
heterozygote. (If a woman has more than one affected child and no other affected relatives and if the DMD
pathogenic variant cannot be detected in her leukocyte DNA, she most likely has gonadal mosaicism.)
Dystrophinopathies 21

• If a male is the only affected family member (i.e., a simplex case), the mother of an affected male proband:
⚬ May be heterozygous for the DMD pathogenic variant identified in the proband (approximately two
thirds of mothers of affected males with no family history of dystrophinopathies are heterozygotes);
⚬ May not be heterozygous and the affected male has a dystrophinopathy as the result of a de novo
DMD pathogenic variant;
⚬ May have gonadal mosaicism for a DMD pathogenic variant (maternal gonadal mosaicism for a
DMD pathogenic variant has been reported in multiple families [Wang et al 2017, Verebi et al
2025]).
• Molecular genetic testing of the mother is recommended to confirm her genetic status, allow reliable
recurrence risk assessment, and determine her need for cardiac and skeletal muscle evaluation and
surveillance.
• If the DMD pathogenic variant identified in the proband is not identified in the mother, the following
possibilities should be considered:
⚬ The proband has a de novo DMD pathogenic variant.
⚬ The proband inherited a DMD pathogenic variant from a mother with gonadal mosaicism. Note:
Testing of maternal leukocyte DNA may not detect all instances of somatic mosaicism and will not
detect a pathogenic variant that is present in the germ (gonadal) cells only.
Parents of a female proband
• A female proband may have inherited a DMD pathogenic variant from either her mother or her father, or
the pathogenic variant may be de novo.
• Detailed evaluation of the mother and review of the extended family history may help distinguish
probands with a de novo DMD pathogenic variant from those with an inherited pathogenic variant.
Molecular genetic testing of the parents can determine if the DMD pathogenic variant was inherited. Note:
Testing of parental leukocyte DNA may not detect all instances of somatic mosaicism and will not detect a
pathogenic variant that is present in the germ (gonadal) cells only.
Sibs of a male proband. The risk to sibs depends on the genetic status of the mother.
• If the mother of the proband is heterozygous for a DMD pathogenic variant, the chance of the mother
transmitting the pathogenic variant in each pregnancy is 50%.
⚬ Males who inherit the DMD pathogenic variant will be affected and will typically have clinical
manifestations similar to those of the proband.
⚬ Females who inherit the DMD pathogenic variant will be heterozygotes. All females with a DMD
pathogenic variant are at risk for DMD-related cardiomyopathy and should undergo cardiac
evaluation and surveillance. Heterozygous females are mostly free of severe skeletal muscle disease
but may have milder muscle involvement. Rarely, heterozygous females may have a DMD/BMD
phenotype resulting from non-random XCI. (See Clinical Description, Heterozygous Females.)
• If a male proband represents a simplex case and if the DMD pathogenic variant cannot be detected in the
leukocyte DNA of the mother, the risk to sibs is greater than that of the general population because of the
possibility of maternal gonadal mosaicism. The estimated recurrence risk in a male fetus in these
circumstances is estimated to be 4.3%-11% (mean: 5.8%) because of the possibility of maternal gonadal
mosaicism [Verebi et al 2025].
Sibs of a female proband. The risk to sibs depends on the genetic status of the parents.
• If the mother of the proband has a DMD pathogenic variant, the chance of the mother transmitting it in
each pregnancy is 50%. (See Sibs of a male proband.)
• If the father of the proband has a DMD pathogenic variant, he will transmit it to all of his daughters and
none of his sons.

• If the proband represents a simplex case (and the DMD pathogenic variant cannot be detected in the
leukocyte DNA of either parent), the risk to sibs is greater than that of the general population because of
the possibility of parental gonadal mosaicism [Verebi et al 2025].
Offspring of a male proband. Affected males transmit the DMD pathogenic variant to all of their daughters and
none of their sons.
Offspring of a female proband. Females with a DMD pathogenic variant have a 50% chance of transmitting the
pathogenic variant to each child.
Other family members. The risk to other family members of a male proband depends on the status of the
proband's mother: if the mother of the proband has the DMD pathogenic variant, maternal family members may
be at risk of having a DMD pathogenic variant (see Evaluation of Relatives at Risk).
Heterozygote Detection
Molecular genetic testing. Identification of female heterozygotes requires either prior identification of the DMD
pathogenic variant in the family or, if a family member with a dystrophinopathy is not available for testing,
molecular genetic testing first by sequence analysis, and if no pathogenic variant is identified, by gene-targeted
deletion/duplication analysis. Note: Targeted molecular genetic testing can be used to determine the point of
origin of a de novo pathogenic variant. This information is important for determining which branches of the
family are at risk of having a DMD pathogenic variant.
Females with a DMD pathogenic variant may have a range of clinical manifestations (see Clinical Description,
Heterozygous Females). Females who are identified as heterozygous for a DMD pathogenic variant should
undergo cardiac and skeletal muscle evaluation and surveillance.
Serum creatine phosphokinase (CK) concentration can be normal in heterozygous females and is not reliable for
heterozygote detection. In a cohort of 53 individuals known to be heterozygous for a DMD pathogenic variant,
CK concentration was elevated in only 57% of heterozygotes [Fornander et al 2021].

Family planning
• The optimal time for determination of genetic risk and discussion of the availability of prenatal/
preimplantation genetic testing is before pregnancy.
• It is appropriate to offer genetic counseling (including discussion of potential risks to offspring and
reproductive options) to young adults who have – or are at risk of having – a DMD pathogenic variant. See
Pickart et al [2025] for a review of reproductive options and other considerations related to genetic
counseling for dystrophinopathies.
• Heterozygous females should consider undergoing a cardiac evaluation prior to conception or when a
pregnancy is recognized. It is recommended that women with evidence of dilated cardiomyopathy be
treated and/or monitored by a cardiac specialist with experience in the treatment of heart failure and/or
neuromuscular disorders and a high-risk obstetrician [Riguzzi et al 2025].
• The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics includes DMD/BMD among those disorders for
which expanded carrier screening should be offered to all pregnant individuals and individuals planning a
pregnancy [Gregg et al 2021].

Prenatal Testing and Preimplantation Genetic Testing
Once the DMD pathogenic variant has been identified in an affected family member, prenatal testing is possible.
Depending on the familial DMD pathogenic variant, preimplantation genetic testing may be an option for some
families but can be challenging if phasing (i.e., determining which maternal X chromosome has the DMD
pathogenic variant and if the fetus has inherited this chromosome) is necessary to confirm test results [Pickart et
al 2025].
Differences in perspective may exist among medical professionals and within families regarding the use of
prenatal and preimplantation genetic testing. While most health care professionals would consider use of
prenatal and preimplantation genetic testing to be a personal decision, discussion of these issues may be helpful.

Molecular Pathogenesis
DMD encodes dystrophin, a membrane-associated protein present in muscle cells and some neurons. The N-
terminal domain binds to actin. A large rod domain includes 24 homologous repeats forming an alpha-helical
structure, a cysteine-rich calcium-binding region near the C terminus, and a C-terminal domain that binds with
other membrane proteins. Dystrophin is therefore part of a protein complex that links the cytoskeleton with
membrane proteins that in turn bind with proteins in the extracellular matrix.
More than 5,000 pathogenic variants have been identified in persons with Duchenne muscular dystrophy
(DMD) or Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD) [Aartsma-Rus et al 2006, Flanigan et al 2009, T uffery-Giraud et al
2009]. Disease-causing alleles are highly variable, including deletion of the entire gene, deletion or duplication of
one or more exons, and small deletions, insertions, or single-base changes. In both DMD and BMD, partial
deletions and duplications cluster in two recombination hot spots, one proximal at the 5' end of the gene,
comprising exons 2-20 (30%), and one more distal, comprising exons 44-53 (70%) [Den Dunnen et al 1989].
Dystrophinopathies 25

Duplications cluster near the 5' end of the gene, with duplication of exon 2 being the single most common
duplication identified [White et al 2006].
Pathogenic variants that lead to lack of dystrophin expression tend to cause DMD, whereas those that lead to
abnormal quality or quantity of dystrophin lead to BMD. In DMD-related cardiomyopathy, functional
dystrophin is absent in the myocardium but may be normal or mildly abnormal in skeletal muscle [Ferlini et al
1999, Neri et al 2007]; DMD-related cardiomyopathy is associated with specific types of DMD pathogenic
variants that have a differential response to tissue-specific transcription or alternative splicing in cardiac versus
skeletal muscle.
Mechanism of disease causation. Loss of function (genetic null allele disorder)
DMD-specific laboratory technical considerations. If somatic mosaicism for a DMD pathogenic variant is
suspected, molecular genetic testing using high diagnostic sensitivity methods (e.g., NGS with a high sequence
read depth) is recommended.
