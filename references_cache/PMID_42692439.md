Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycar­
dia (CPVT) is a rare, life ­threatening, inherited arrhyth­
mia syndrome characterized by adrenergically stimulat­
ed ventricular tachyarrhythmias that cause syncope
and sudden cardiac death.1) While most cases result from
autosomal dominant variants of the cardiac ryanodine
receptor (RYR2) gene, approximately 2%–5% involve ho­
mozygous or compound heterozygous variants of the
cardiac calsequestrin (CASQ2) gene.2) CASQ2­mediated
CPVT often presents a more malignant clinical course,
including earlier onset and significant sinus node dy ­
sfunc tion.3,4) To the best of our knowledge, this is the first
Korean CASQ2­mediated CPVT case where sleep distur­
bances uniquely aggravated malignant arrhythmias re­
quiring an implantable cardioverter­defibrillator (ICD)
for refractory arrhythmias and severe drug ­induced
bradycardia.
An 8­year­old girl with no relevant family history pre­
sented with exertional syncope. Electrocardiography
revealed sinus bradycardia (57 bpm) and normal QTc, and
echocardiography confirmed a structurally normal heart.
Holter monitoring revealed bidirectional ventricular ta­
chycardia (VT) associated with reported chest discomfort.
Treadmill testing induced nonsustained bidirectional
VT at stage 3 (Fig. 1), confirming the CPVT diagnosis. The
initial nadolol dose (15 mg) was decreased to 10 mg for
bradycardia­related chest tightness and dizziness, and
then switched to propranolol for recurrent exertional
syncope. Escalating beta­blockers to control VT­induced
syncope exacerbated bradycardia­related symptoms (sleep
disturbance, constipation, and abdominal pain), causing
noncompliance and worsening syncope. Addition of
flecainide did not alter this pattern; therefore, left cardiac
sympathetic denervation (LCSD) was performed at 9 years
of age.
Post­LCSD, the premature ventricular contraction (PVC)
heart rate (HR) threshold increased from 108 to 120 bpm.
Notably, bidirectional PVCs previously observed at 130
bpm were completely suppressed after the procedure.
During adolescence, the patient experienced frequent
headaches, dizziness, and insomnia. Worsening insom­
nia and circadian rhythm disruption increased VT­related
syncope. Medication­exacerbated dizziness prompted fre­
quent self­discontinuation. Sleep actigraphy confirmed
irregular sleep onset (midnight to 7:00 AM). Concurrently,
serial treadmill testing demonstrated a progressively
lower HR threshold for PVCs (110 to 83 bpm). To regulate
circadian rhythms, melatonin (2 mg) was administered
at age 11. Actigraphy confirmed corrected sleep onset
before midnight, with reduced syncope and a temporarily
improved quality of life. However, symptomatic brady­
cardia perpetuated poor patient compliance, causing
recurrent syncope.
To prevent fatal ventricular fibrillation (VF), a dual ­
chamber ICD was implanted at the age of 14 years ac ­
cording to European Society of Cardiology Class IIa re­
com mendation.5) Pacing at 60 bpm (AAIR↔DDDR mode)
controlled bradycardia­related symptoms, improving
compliance and stabilizing her condition without further
syncope on nadolol 40 mg daily (Fig. 2).
Genetic analysis revealed a novel biallelic nonsense
CASQ2 variant (c.220G>T, p.(Glu74*)). Family screening
revealed an asymptomatic heterozygous father (T/WT)
and variant­free mother (WT/WT).
Given its definitive phenotype, the CASQ2 variant is
likely to be disease ­causing.6) Although heterozygotes
occasionally exhibit mild proarrhythmic phenotypes,
CASQ2­mediated CPVT is fundamentally an autosomal
recessive disease manifesting in homozygotes. 3,7) Her
apparent homozygosity suggests maternal exon deletion,
paternal uniparental isodisomy, or polymerase chain
reaction allele dropout. Confirmatory testing, such as

The coexistence of intrinsic and drug­induced brady­
cardia, which reduces patient compliance, is well­docu­
mented in RYR2­related CPVT.10) Recent CASQ2 deletion
studies have suggested that loss of function increases
spontaneous calcium release while causing sinus node
fibrosis and calcium cycling dysfunction. 4) Her low
resting HR suggested that intrinsic sinus node dysfunc­
tion aggravated drug­induced bradycardia. Many pati ents
with CPVT exhibit poor medication adherence owing to
bradycardia, fatigue, or dizziness, directly link ing non­
adherence to life ­threatening breakthrough arrhyth  ­
mias.10) ICD implantation in CPVT is generally controver­
sial because of the risk of shock­induced catecholamine
storms, and is not indicated solely for pacing.1) However,
our primary indication was life ­threatening refractory
syncope, despite maximal pharmacological therapy and
LCSD. In addition, atrial pacing resolved symptomatic
bradycardia, enabling optimal beta­blocker therapy while
preventing sudden cardiac death.
In conclusion, to the best of our knowledge, this is the
first report of a Korean case of CASQ2­mediated CPVT
that provides valuable clinical insights into the malignant
features exacerbated by sleep deprivation and drug in­
tolerance.
CASQ2 variants create a therapeutic paradox; life ­
threatening VT/VF coexists with profound bradycardia,
limiting essential antiarrhythmic use. Active pacing is a
vital secondary benefit when ICDs are used for refractory
syncope. This dual function resolves the therapeutic
paradox and offers a practical strategy to ensure phar­
macological compliance and arrhythmic stability.
Question
Which of the following is not a common finding of
CASQ2­mediated CPVT?
(A) Clinical presentation requires a homozygous geno­
type.
(B) Patients often exhibit profound resting bradycardia
and sinus node dysfunction.
(C) Ventricular arrhythmias are exclusively triggered by
high­intensity physical exercise.
(D) It tends to present with a more malignant clinical
course compared to RYR2 variants.
