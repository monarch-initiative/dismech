# Current Concepts in Periprosthetic Joint Infection: Modern Biomarkers, Microbiome Insights, Molecular Diagnostics, and Emerging Prevention Strategies.
**Authors:** Saadeh JE, Saad R, Assaf F, Osman R, Chami AA, Harb BA, Ghanem W, Badra M, Moucharafieh R
**Journal:** Orthop Res Rev (2026)
**DOI:** [10.2147/ORR.S634292](https://doi.org/10.2147/ORR.S634292)

## Content

AIM: Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) remains one of the leading causes of revision arthroplasty and is associated with substantial morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. Diagnosis remains challenging because of heterogeneous clinical presentations, the limited sensitivity of conventional inflammatory markers in chronic and low-grade infections, culture-negative cases, and biofilm-mediated pathogen persistence. This narrative review summarizes recent advances in the diagnosis and prevention of PJI, with a particular focus on synovial and serum biomarkers, microbiome-related susceptibility, molecular diagnostics, artificial intelligence, and emerging anti-biofilm and implant-surface technologies. Synovial biomarkers, including alpha-defensin, leukocyte esterase, calprotectin, and D-lactate, have demonstrated improved diagnostic performance, particularly in equivocal and culture-negative cases. However, none has sufficient evidence to replace established diagnostic criteria, and current data support their use as adjunctive tools in selected clinical scenarios rather than as standalone tests. Serum biomarkers beyond erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein, such as fibrinogen and protein fraction alterations, may provide additional diagnostic value but require further validation and standardized diagnostic thresholds. Molecular approaches, particularly metagenomic next-generation sequencing, improve pathogen detection in complex infections but remain limited by cost, protocol variability, and challenges in distinguishing clinically relevant pathogens from contaminants. Preventive strategies targeting bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation through local antibiotic delivery, surface modification, and nanotechnology-enabled implants are promising, although long-term efficacy, safety, and cost-effectiveness remain to be established. Overall, PJI management is evolving toward integrated, multimodal diagnostic strategies and biologically informed prevention. Current evidence supports the selective clinical use of synovial biomarker testing as an adjunctive tool within established diagnostic criteria, while continued research is needed before widespread routine implementation can be recommended.

STATEMENT OF CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE: Modern synovial biomarkers provide valuable adjunctive information that may improve diagnostic confidence in challenging periprosthetic joint infection cases when incorporated into a multidisciplinary diagnostic approach involving clinical assessment, laboratory evaluation, microbiological analysis, and established consensus criteria. Emerging preventive strategies targeting biofilm formation and host-microbe interactions represent promising approaches, although their ability to reduce revision rates and improve patient outcomes requires further clinical validation.

This narrative review was based on a focused literature search of PubMed/MEDLINE and relevant medical databases using combinations of keywords related to periprosthetic joint infection, biomarkers, synovial diagnostics, microbiome, molecular diagnostics, artificial intelligence, biofilm prevention, and implant surface technologies. Publications including original studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and relevant expert consensus documents were considered. Studies were selected based on their relevance to current diagnostic challenges, emerging technologies, and clinical applicability. Given the narrative nature of this review, no quantitative synthesis or formal risk-of-bias assessment was performed.

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) continues to be one of the most devastating complications of joint arthroplasty. Although advancements in surgical technique and perioperative care have reduced infection rates, the increasing volume of hip and knee arthroplasty worldwide means the total burden of PJI continues to rise.1 Diagnosis remains challenging due to the limitations of traditional markers such as ESR and CRP, the presence of culture-negative cases, and the complexity of biofilm-forming organisms.1 In recent years, accelerated progress has been made in identifying modern biomarkers, understanding the influence of the microbiome, and developing new prevention strategies.1–3 This review synthesizes current evidence on modern diagnostic biomarkers, microbiome-driven insights into susceptibility and pathogenesis, and emerging preventive approaches including biofilm-targeted technologies, nanotechnology-enabled implant strategies, and data-driven precision medicine pathways.

Current diagnostic approaches for PJI rely on established consensus definitions, including the Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) criteria and the International Consensus Meeting (ICM) framework, which integrate multiple domains such as clinical findings, serum inflammatory markers, synovial fluid analysis, microbiological results, and intraoperative findings.4,5 Although these criteria have significantly improved diagnostic standardization, important limitations remain. Diagnosis can be particularly challenging in chronic infections, infections caused by low-virulence organisms, culture-negative cases, and biofilm-associated infections, where conventional markers and microbiological methods may demonstrate reduced sensitivity.4,5 These diagnostic challenges have driven the investigation of novel biomarkers and molecular approaches aimed at improving diagnostic confidence while complementing, rather than replacing, current diagnostic frameworks.

It is important to emphasize that the role of the microbiome in PJI remains an emerging and investigational field. While mechanistic studies, animal models, and evidence from other inflammatory conditions provide biological plausibility for interactions between dysbiosis, immune regulation, intestinal permeability, and infection susceptibility, direct evidence establishing causation in human PJI remains limited. Therefore, the gut–immune–joint axis should currently be considered a potential hypothesis-generating framework rather than an established clinical pathway for PJI risk prediction or prevention.

The relationship between the microbiome and PJI can be considered from three distinct perspectives: (1) the potential origin of microorganisms causing PJI from host microbial reservoirs such as the skin, gut, oral cavity, or urinary tract; (2) the possible influence of dysbiosis on systemic immune function and susceptibility to infection; and (3) whether microbiome modulation can reduce the risk of PJI. Although these concepts are related, they are supported by different levels of evidence and should be considered separately.

Synovial fluid biomarkers, particularly alpha-defensin and leukocyte esterase, have gained increasing attention as reliable adjuncts for the diagnosis of PJI, addressing several limitations of conventional diagnostic methods.2 Alpha-defensin is an innate antimicrobial peptide released by activated neutrophils in response to bacterial pathogens and is largely unaffected by prior antibiotic exposure or organism virulence.2 Leukocyte esterase, a neutrophil-derived enzyme detected using a simple colorimetric reagent strip, reflects local inflammatory activity within the joint.2 In a 2016 systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating synovial biomarkers for PJI diagnosis, the alpha-defensin immunoassay demonstrated a pooled sensitivity of 1.00 (95% CI, 0.82–1.00) and a pooled specificity of 0.96 (95% CI, 0.89–0.99), indicating excellent diagnostic performance with near-complete sensitivity.2 In contrast, the leukocyte esterase test showed a pooled sensitivity of 0.81 (95% CI, 0.49–0.95) and specificity of 0.97 (95% CI, 0.82–0.99), reflecting high specificity but more variable sensitivity. While alpha-defensin offers superior diagnostic accuracy, its cost and limited availability may restrict widespread use.2 Conversely, leukocyte esterase testing remains an attractive point-of-care option due to its low cost, rapid turnaround time, and ease of implementation, particularly in intraoperative or resource-limited settings.2 Together, these biomarkers provide valuable complementary tools that enhance diagnostic confidence when used alongside established clinical, serologic, and microbiologic criteria.2

While skin flora remain the predominant source of organisms causing PJI, increasing attention has been directed toward the potential contribution of other host microbial reservoirs, including the gut microbiome, although direct evidence remains limited.6,7

In addition to that, calprotectin, released predominantly by activated neutrophils during inflammatory responses, has recently emerged as a promising synovial fluid biomarker for the diagnosis of PJI. A recent meta-analysis including 618 patients reported a pooled sensitivity of approximately 92% and a pooled specificity of 93% for synovial calprotectin, highlighting its strong discriminative capacity between infected and aseptic joints.3 Additional advantages of this biomarker include its relatively low cost and rapid processing time, and early investigations of a lateral flow, point-of-care calprotectin assay have demonstrated encouraging diagnostic accuracy in clinical settings.

Additionally, D-lactate, a metabolic byproduct produced by bacteria, represents another synovial biomarker with growing relevance in PJI diagnosis. In a study involving more than 200 cases, synovial D-lactate achieved a sensitivity of approximately 92% and a specificity of 89%, supporting its role as a useful screening tool for infection.8 The appeal of D-lactate lies in its rapid turnaround time and minimal synovial fluid requirements; however, its diagnostic performance may be influenced by factors such as bacterial virulence and biofilm-related metabolic activity.8

Beyond these, numerous other synovial candidates are under investigation. In PJI, elevated synovial interleukin-6 reflects intense local pro-inflammatory signaling that distinguishes infected from aseptic joints with high diagnostic accuracy (eg, IL-6 ≥30,750 pg/mL demonstrating Area-under-the-curve ≈ 0.96, sensitivity 90%, and specificity ~ 95%) and works in concert with IL-1 pathways to amplify neutrophil recruitment and inflammatory mediator release at the site of infection, underscoring the central role of these cytokines in modulating the host response to bacterial invasion in PJI.9

Growing evidence has drawn attention to additional synovial biomarkers such as soluble urokinase-type plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR), toll-like receptor-2 (TLR-2), osteopontin, and cysteine-rich with EGF-like domains 2 (CRELD2) as potential tools in the evaluation of PJI. Recent investigations have demonstrated significantly elevated levels of these markers in infected joints compared with aseptic failures, suggesting their involvement in host immune activation during infection 6. In particular, increased synovial TLR-2 expression is thought to reflect innate immune recognition of gram-positive pathogens, while elevated suPAR concentrations have shown strong associations with PJI and in at least one cohort, demonstrated higher sensitivity than conventional markers such as CRP or IL-6.10

When comparing available synovial biomarkers, alpha-defensin currently demonstrates the most consistent diagnostic performance, particularly in chronic and culture-negative infections, although its cost and limited availability may restrict widespread use. Leukocyte esterase offers a rapid and inexpensive point-of-care alternative but has lower sensitivity and may be affected by sample quality. Calprotectin has emerged as a promising biomarker with favorable diagnostic accuracy and practical advantages, including rapid processing and potential bedside applicability; however, further validation is required before widespread adoption. D-lactate provides pathogen-related metabolic information with rapid turnaround but may be influenced by bacterial characteristics and biofilm activity. Other emerging biomarkers, including cytokines and immune-response markers such as IL-6, suPAR, and TLR-2, remain investigational and require additional multicenter validation. Therefore, current evidence supports the use of synovial biomarkers as complementary tools within a multimodal diagnostic framework rather than as replacements for established PJI criteria.3,8–12

Although these biomarkers improve diagnostic confidence, particularly in equivocal cases, evidence demonstrating that their incorporation into EBJIS/ICM-based diagnostic pathways improves treatment decisions, revision strategies, or long-term patient outcomes remains limited.2–5 Furthermore, biomarker thresholds should not be interpreted as universally applicable, as diagnostic performance may vary according to infection chronicity, organism virulence, joint-specific factors, underlying inflammatory diseases, prior antibiotic exposure, and the overall clinical context.2–5

The interpretation of synovial biomarker thresholds may vary according to the clinical context, particularly between acute postoperative and chronic low-grade infections. In acute PJI, intense inflammatory responses may result in higher synovial leukocyte counts and biomarker levels, whereas chronic infections caused by low-virulence organisms or biofilm-associated pathogens may demonstrate lower inflammatory activity, potentially reducing biomarker sensitivity. Therefore, fixed cutoff values should be interpreted cautiously and in conjunction with infection chronicity, timing from index arthroplasty, and the overall diagnostic framework. Furthermore, underlying inflammatory or autoimmune conditions may alter synovial inflammatory profiles and increase the risk of false-positive results, limiting the specificity of individual biomarkers.2,3,9 These considerations highlight the importance of integrating synovial biomarkers with clinical findings, microbiological data, and established consensus criteria rather than relying on isolated threshold values.

Beyond synovial fluid analysis, increasing attention has been directed toward circulating biomarkers to enhance the diagnostic evaluation of PJI beyond traditional CRP and ESR. Among these, D-dimer a marker of fibrin turnover and systemic fibrinolysis was introduced as a serum criterion in the 2018 International Consensus Meeting (ICM) definition for PJI. Early investigations suggested that serum D-dimer could differentiate septic from aseptic failure with reported sensitivities approaching 80–90% and moderate specificity, in some cases equaling or exceeding the performance of CRP and ESR.11 Nevertheless, subsequent studies have yielded inconsistent findings, revealing limitations in its clinical applicability. In particular, D-dimer appears less reliable in the early postoperative period and may be influenced by recent surgery, thromboembolic disease, or underlying coagulation abnormalities, thereby reducing its diagnostic specificity.

In contrast, fibrinogen a key coagulation protein that also functions as an acute-phase reactant has demonstrated more consistent diagnostic utility. A systematic review reported higher specificity for fibrinogen in confirming infection, and several analyses have suggested that fibrinogen may outperform CRP in overall diagnostic performance.11 Notably, comparative data from a single cohort demonstrated a substantially higher area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for fibrinogen (approximately 0.80–0.85) compared with D-dimer (approximately 0.66), highlighting fibrinogen’s relative stability even in the presence of confounding clinical variables.12 Although these fibrinolysis-related markers are inexpensive and widely available, interpretation should be individualized, as factors such as anticoagulant therapy or concurrent thrombotic conditions may influence serum levels.

An emerging serum-based strategy for diagnosing PJI involves the application of serum protein electrophoresis (SPE) to identify alterations in globulin fractions associated with chronic infection. Recent evidence has demonstrated that the α1-globulin fraction is significantly increased in patients with chronic PJI. In a 2024 study examining late chronic infections classified as Tsukayama type IV, an elevated α1-globulin proportion on SPE achieved diagnostic performance comparable to traditional inflammatory markers, with reported sensitivity and specificity of approximately 80%.12 Notably, when the α1-globulin fraction was combined with CRP and ESR, the overall diagnostic accuracy improved substantially, yielding an area under the curve of approximately 0.90, suggesting an additive value beyond standard serum tests.12 Beyond initial diagnosis, α1-globulin levels also appeared useful for assessing infection control prior to reimplantation, indicating a potential role in guiding the timing of second-stage revision procedures.12 These findings reinforce the concept that shifts in acute-phase protein profiles, including albumin and globulin fractions, may reflect underlying infectious activity. Other serum biomarkers under investigation include interleukin-6, which has demonstrated high sensitivity in select studies but remains limited by availability, and procalcitonin, which has generally shown suboptimal sensitivity for PJI.

Whether modifying the microbiome can reduce the incidence of PJI remains unproven, and current preventive strategies should be considered investigational pending prospective clinical trials.7,13

Recognizing that no single test can definitively diagnose PJI, researchers are increasingly turning to multiplex strategies and advanced algorithms to enhance diagnostic accuracy. Multi-analyte panels combining serum and synovial biomarkers, including CRP, D-dimer, WBC count, α-defensin, and calprotectin, enhance sensitivity without sacrificing specificity.3 In parallel, point-of-care (POC) technologies have been developed for rapid intraoperative or clinic use. Lateral flow devices now exist for markers like alpha-defensin and calprotectin, enabling surgeons to get results from synovial fluid within minutes.3 Combined multi-marker panels and AI represent the next-generation diagnostic approach, enabling real-time, personalized, and more accurate PJI detection.14

Despite their promise, multiplex biomarker platforms and machine learning–assisted diagnostic models face important barriers to routine clinical implementation. Most machine learning algorithms have been developed using retrospective datasets and require prospective external validation before widespread clinical adoption. In addition, successful implementation depends on standardized data collection, integration with electronic health records, and adequate computational infrastructure. Cost also remains an important consideration, particularly for multiplex assays and artificial intelligence–enabled platforms, which may not be readily available in resource-limited settings.14 Consequently, these technologies should currently be viewed as decision-support tools with promising future applications rather than replacements for established diagnostic pathways.

Despite increasing interest in modern serum and synovial biomarkers for PJI, important limitations remain before routine clinical adoption. Many biomarkers have been evaluated in heterogeneous patient populations using differing diagnostic definitions, which limits reproducibility and complicates the establishment of universal cutoff values. Real-world factors such as infection chronicity, low-virulence organisms, prior antibiotic exposure, and joint-specific differences further influence biomarker performance and may result in false-negative or equivocal findings.4 Conversely, systemic inflammation or recent surgery may reduce specificity.5

Practical barriers include cost, limited availability, and reliance on specialized platforms. Consequently, biomarkers should be interpreted within a multimodal diagnostic framework. Further large-scale, prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate thresholds and define their incremental clinical value.4,5

At present, insufficient evidence exists to recommend different threshold values according to acute versus chronic infection, joint type, causative organism, or inflammatory comorbidities. Further prospective studies are needed to establish standardized, context-specific cutoff values.4,5,15 Future prospective studies should evaluate not only diagnostic accuracy but also whether incorporating these biomarkers into established multidisciplinary diagnostic pathways improves clinical decision-making, patient outcomes, and cost-effectiveness.4,5

Emerging evidence suggests that PJI susceptibility may extend beyond local contamination to include systemic immune modulation potentially influenced by the gut microbiome. Although the proposed “gut–immune–joint axis” is biologically plausible and supported by preclinical and observational studies, its role in PJI pathogenesis remains incompletely understood and requires further clinical validation.6,7,16–18

Several biological mechanisms potentially link gut microbiota dysregulation to increased PJI risk. These mechanisms have been proposed based on evidence from microbiome research, immune studies, and preclinical models.

The gut microbiota plays a central role in educating and regulating the host immune system. Commensal microbes stimulate immune cell maturation and maintain a balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory pathways. Dysbiosis, which is characterized by reduced diversity and imbalance in key phyla such as Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria, may impair this regulatory function.6

This mechanism aligns with broader immunological understanding: dysbiosis can skew cytokine profiles, decrease regulatory T cell function, and promote systemic inflammation, all of which may impair effective antimicrobial responses at surgical sites.16

One proposed mechanism linking gut dysbiosis to systemic pathology is disruption of mucosal barrier integrity. Dysbiosis may increase intestinal permeability, allowing translocation of microbial products such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which may contribute to systemic immune activation.17 Gut epithelial tight junctions normally limit translocation of microbes and bacterial products such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS). Dysbiosis has been proposed to disrupt this barrier, potentially increasing circulating microbial products and contributing to immune activation.17

In patients undergoing arthroplasty, markers of gut permeability, including zonulin and soluble CD14, were significantly elevated in those who developed PJI compared to aseptic controls, suggesting an association between increased gut permeability and PJI that supports, but does not establish, the proposed gut–immune–joint axis in humans.18

One proposed mechanistic model is the Trojan Horse hypothesis, whereby gut microbes or microbial products are internalized by leukocytes and transported via the bloodstream to distant sites, including prosthetic joints. Once at the joint, these microbes or microbial antigens may contribute to local inflammation or infection. This mechanism remains experimental and is supported primarily by preclinical studies, with currently limited evidence in humans.7,19

Beyond translocation, gut dysbiosis alters the profile of microbial metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, and other small molecules, which influence systemic inflammation and immune cell activation. While direct evidence in PJI is still emerging, studies in arthritis demonstrate that microbiome-derived metabolites can modulate joint immune responses, potentially affecting local susceptibility to infection and inflammation.20

Furthermore, systemic inflammatory modulation may affect local tissue environments in ways that influence biofilm formation on implants, a major challenge in PJI management, by altering host antimicrobial peptide expression and extracellular matrix composition.21

Antibiotic exposure represents another potential contributor to microbiome disruption in patients undergoing arthroplasty. Although perioperative antibiotics remain essential for preventing surgical site infections, repeated or prolonged antibiotic exposure may alter gut microbial composition, reduce microbial diversity, and interfere with normal host–microbiome interactions. These alterations may influence immune homeostasis through changes in microbial metabolites, inflammatory signaling, and intestinal barrier function, potentially affecting host susceptibility to infection. However, while experimental and emerging clinical data suggest an association between microbiome disruption and infection susceptibility, the direct contribution of antibiotic-induced dysbiosis to PJI development remains incompletely established and requires further investigation.7,17,20

Understanding the gut–joint axis has important implications for both risk assessment and potential intervention strategies in PJI.

Current preoperative risk models for PJI focus on clinical factors such as diabetes, obesity, and prior joint infection. However, these conditions are themselves associated with gut microbiome alterations and chronic low-grade systemic inflammation.17 Incorporating microbiome-related biomarkers, such as measures of gut permeability or microbial diversity, could enhance risk stratification beyond traditional clinical variables. For example, a history of severe microbiome disruption, such as prior Clostridium difficile infection, appears to convey an additional risk for postoperative infection, making it a potential factor and should be seriously considered when planning arthroplasty.22

If dysbiosis contributes to PJI susceptibility, microbiome modulation may represent a future preventive strategy. However, evidence supporting interventions such as probiotics, prebiotics, dietary modification, and microbiome-preserving antibiotic strategies remains limited, and these approaches are discussed further in Section 4.2.13,17,23

Culture-negative PJI, a substantial clinical challenge, may sometimes reflect atypical pathogen profiles or altered host microbiome interactions rather than purely diagnostic failure.24 Improved understanding of microbiome influences could support novel diagnostic approaches, including microbial DNA profiling and host immune response signatures.24

Periprosthetic joint infections continue to lead as causes of revision arthroplasty and, in turn, increase morbidity and mortality risks on patients. The complex interplay between biofilm formation, immune response strength and diagnostic delay pose major limitation to recovery time. With that, new research is focused on studying the contributing factors to generate targeted, patient-specific preventive measures.

The formation of biofilm, a complex microorganism-generated matrix of polysaccharides, proteins, and nucleic acids, represents a major mechanism of bacterial persistence on prosthetic surfaces and contributes to antimicrobial tolerance. Although rifampin-based combination therapy has demonstrated clinical benefit in the treatment of established staphylococcal PJI, particularly in implant-retention strategies, it represents a therapeutic rather than a preventive approach.25 Therefore, current preventive strategies focus primarily on reducing initial bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation through antimicrobial coatings, surface modifications, and local anti-biofilm technologies.

Active prosthetic surface modification aims to reduce bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation through antimicrobial or anti-adhesive coatings.26,27 These strategies decrease local bacterial burden, reduce reliance on systemic antibiotics, and may limit antimicrobial resistance. Novel strategies also use nanopatterning, modifying the prosthetic surface at a nanometer scale, to create nanopores that interfere with the creation of the biofilm. Other strategies include the use of antimicrobial hydrogel carriers to deliver short-term, higher doses of antibiotics locally; therefore, decreasing systemic exposure.26

Nanotechnology offers promising strategies for preventing PJI by targeting early bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation on implant surfaces. Nanostructured coatings, particularly those incorporating silver or metal-oxide nanoparticles, exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity through membrane disruption, reactive oxygen species generation, and controlled ion release, thereby inhibiting biofilm maturation while preserving implant biocompatibility.27 In addition, antibiotic loaded nanocarriers enable localized, sustained drug delivery at the bone–implant interface, achieving high antimicrobial concentrations without systemic toxicity.27 These systems may enhance penetration into biofilms, a major limitation of conventional antibiotic therapy. Emerging multifunctional nanocoatings combine antibacterial properties with osteogenic or immunomodulatory effects, promoting osseointegration while reducing infection risk.28 Although most evidence remains preclinical, early studies demonstrate significant reductions in bacterial burden and improved implant integration. Translation into routine clinical practice remains limited by manufacturing complexity, regulatory approval requirements, production costs, long-term safety concerns, and uncertainty regarding the durability of nanocoatings under physiological conditions, highlighting the need for further clinical validation before widespread implementation.27,29

Additional emerging anti-biofilm strategies include bacteriophage therapy, quorum-sensing inhibitors, and antimicrobial peptides. Bacteriophages offer pathogen-specific bacterial killing with the potential to penetrate biofilms and may provide an alternative for infections caused by multidrug-resistant organisms.26 Quorum-sensing inhibitors aim to disrupt bacterial communication pathways involved in biofilm formation and virulence, whereas antimicrobial peptides enhance innate host defense through broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity.26 Although these approaches have demonstrated encouraging preclinical and early translational results, clinical evidence remains limited, and further studies are required to establish their efficacy, safety, and role in routine PJI prevention and management.

Constant communication between the multiple microorganisms in the gut microbiome and the host allows for a symbiotic relationship and is necessary for a proper immune response. Patients with chronic diseases, dietary restrictions or recurrent antibiotic use, are more likely to have an impaired immune regulation. This increases the likelihood of dysbiosis and, in turn, increases the risk of infections.30

Modulating the microbiome was therefore suggested as a novel preventive method for infections, including PJIs. Recent evidence shows the inter-connected relationship between the nutritional status, immune system and commensal microflora. Accordingly, possible interventions include the use of preoperative prebiotics, dietary modifications to improve microbial diversity and methods that improve or preserve the gut barrier integrity.31 Additionally, proper use of perioperative antibiotics can be used to minimize disruption of microbiota while maintaining efficient prophylaxis against infections.32

The variable nature of PJI development risk has led to an increased interest in strategies that integrate clinical, biological and molecular data. Traditional assessments rely primarily on comorbidities and fail to incorporate individual factors, including immune system strength and previous microbial exposures. Accordingly, recent studies have focused on the personalization of treatment methods to generate patient-specific infection risk profiles.33–35

With the continued development of artificial intelligence, new predictive techniques are developing which could potentially replace the “one-size-fits-all” paradigm for PJI prevention. Such approaches may enable tailored prophylactic and surveillance protocol development to personalize preventive methods.34,35

mNGS has re-shaped the approach to diagnosis in PJI, especially when conventional culture methods fail due to low sensitivity, prior antibiotic exposure or presence of fastidious or polymicrobial organisms. Recent meta-analyses and prospective studies have shown promising results for mNGS in these scenarios, offering high sensitivity reaching 89–95% and specificity of approximately 92–95% for pathogen detection in PJI, thus proving to be superior to culture in many scenarios, especially culture negative, biofilm-associated or rare pathogens. mNGS can be applied to a variety of specimen types, including synovial fluid, periprosthetic tissue, prosthetic fluid, with the latter being generally of the highest diagnostic value since the process allows the breakage of bacterial biofilm. mNGS is also able to detect circulating microbial cell-free DNA from the plasma in some cases, thus adding to its versatility.37,38

Key advantages that allow mNGS to stand out as a diagnostic tool of high value are the rapid turnaround time which is often 1–2 days, as compared to 5 days for cultures,37,39 a broader, unbiased pathogen detection spectrum, including bacteria, fungi or viruses.37,38

Furthermore, targeted NGS (tNGS) and multiplex PCR-based NGS approaches have also been studied, having to offer comparable diagnostic accuracy to mNGS, with tNGS being a better cost-efficient option with faster turnaround time, however at the cost of having a narrower pathogen panel, yet may be of benefit in some clinical scenarios.39

Despite its promise, mNGS remains constrained by high cost, lack of standardized protocols, inter-laboratory variability, and challenges in interpreting results. Detection of contaminating organisms, residual DNA from nonviable microorganisms, colonizing species, or polymicrobial signals may complicate differentiation between clinically relevant pathogens and incidental findings. Consequently, mNGS results should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical presentation, conventional microbiology, and established diagnostic criteria rather than in isolation.40

Artificial intelligence(AI) is increasingly becoming a vital key player in the diagnosis and management of PJI through multimodal approaches that integrate clinical, laboratory, radiologic and molecular data. AI algorithms are being fed information that combine synovial biomarkers, serum markers, clinical presentations, radiologic imaging, microbiome signatures and molecular study results in order to synthesize AI models able to aid in diagnosis, risk stratification and therapeutic decisions.34,35 The integration of the multiplex of biomarkers enhances sensitivity and specificity beyond single-marker approaches.34

Although non-invasive radiologic imaging is not included in the current mainstream diagnostic criteria of PJI, there is potential for AI-based automation in the detection of features that are suggestive of infection, such as fluid collection, implant loosening, or periosteal reaction, in combination with clinical and laboratory findings, further improving on the potential diagnostic performance.35 Beyond that, AI is being used to interpret the complex molecular outputs of multiplex PCR, mNGS and tNGS, facilitating rapid pathogen identification, especially in culture-negative cases, and also identifying AMR genes, thus guiding therapeutic decision-making.34,37 Not only that, but with advanced sequencing techniques being developed, such as 16S rRNA, shotgun metagenomics and metatranscriptomics, we are able to reveal distinct microbial environments that are found in infected versus aseptic joints, a promising predictive tool that AI is beginning to be integrated in.41

Although AI has shown considerable promise, most available models remain in the early stages of development and require prospective multicenter validation before clinical implementation. AI is therefore best regarded as a decision-support tool with the potential to enhance diagnostic accuracy and individualized decision-making, rather than as a replacement for clinician judgment or an established standard of care.34,35

Even though diagnostic and preventive strategies for PJI have expanded considerably in recent years, important limitations remain that continue to impact daily clinical practice. Perhaps the most fundamental challenge is the absence of a single investigation that can be relied upon as a definitive diagnostic test for PJI. Current diagnostic criteria, including those proposed by the Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) and later refined through ICM, represent a significant advance in standardizing diagnosis; however, they are ultimately based on the integration of multiple parameters, each of which is imperfect when considered in isolation.15

The increasing availability of synovial and serum biomarkers including calprotectin, alpha-defensin, D-lactate, and various inflammatory cytokines has improved diagnostic confidence in many cases. Nevertheless, their adoption remains inconsistent and highly center-dependent. Acute versus chronic infection, the presence of metallosis, and underlying inflammatory joint disease appear to influence biomarker accuracy in distinct ways. This lack of standardization explains why many of these tests continue to serve as adjuncts rather than replacements for established diagnostic approaches.15

Despite advances in culture techniques, including optimized sampling strategies and extended incubation protocols, culture-negative PJI remains relatively common. False-negative results are frequently attributed to biofilm formation on implant surfaces, prior antibiotic exposure, and low bacterial burden. Molecular diagnostic tools, such as multiplex polymerase chain reaction and metagenomic sequencing, have been introduced to address some of these limitations, particularly in complex or recurrent cases. However, these techniques introduce new uncertainties, as interpretation can be difficult when multiple organisms or low-level microbial signals are detected. As such, molecular findings must be carefully integrated with clinical, laboratory, and imaging data to avoid overdiagnosis or misclassification.42

From a therapeutic standpoint, biofilm-associated infection remains one of the most formidable obstacles in PJI management. Although innovative strategies such as antimicrobial implant coatings, nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems, and bacteriophage therapy are under active investigation, the majority of available evidence remains experimental.43,44

An additional challenge to the widespread adoption of emerging diagnostic and preventive technologies is their economic impact. Advanced molecular diagnostics, multiplex biomarker platforms, and novel implant technologies may be associated with substantial costs, limited reimbursement, and restricted availability, particularly in low-resource healthcare settings. Consequently, future implementation strategies should consider not only diagnostic performance but also cost-effectiveness, accessibility, and equitable integration into diverse healthcare systems.34,37,40,45

Future progress in PJI will depend on validation and integration of diagnostic tools rather than reliance on any single biomarker. Large multicenter studies are needed to establish reproducible cutoff values and clarify performance across infection phenotypes and patient populations.15,46 Molecular diagnostics require standardization of sampling, sequencing platforms, and reporting to support clinical interpretability and cross-institution comparability.15,45

Future progress will also depend on collaborative research initiatives that facilitate robust validation of emerging diagnostic technologies. Multicenter biomarker registries could provide large, diverse datasets to establish reproducible diagnostic thresholds and evaluate performance across different patient populations and clinical settings. In parallel, adaptive clinical trial designs may accelerate the evaluation of novel biomarkers and molecular diagnostic platforms while accommodating rapidly evolving technologies. The development of standardized reporting frameworks for biomarker studies and molecular diagnostics would further improve reproducibility, facilitate comparison across studies, and support evidence-based integration into future clinical guidelines.15,45–47

The gut microbiome remains a promising but investigational area. Randomized trials are required to determine whether perioperative dietary strategies, probiotics/prebiotics, or microbiome-preserving pathways reduce PJI risk.7 AI is likely to provide greatest value as a decision-support tool integrating multimodal data, but must be externally validated and assessed for generalizability and bias before adoption into routine care pathways.47 Finally, implant surface engineering and anti-biofilm technologies, including nanopatterning and local delivery platforms, must demonstrate durable clinical benefit, safety, scalability, and cost-effectiveness before widespread clinical adoption can be recommended.26,27,29,44

Beyond current surface modification and local antimicrobial delivery strategies, several emerging biofilm-targeted technologies are being investigated as potential adjuncts in PJI management. Engineered antimicrobial peptides, such as PLG0206, have demonstrated rapid broad-spectrum antibiofilm activity against organisms implicated in PJI and have shown encouraging results in ex vivo studies using infected prosthetic components.48 PhotothermAA gel represents another novel approach, combining photothermal and antimicrobial effects; in a rabbit model of knee PJI, its addition to debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention significantly reduced implant biofilm burden compared with debridement and antibiotics alone.49 Additionally, alternating magnetic field (AMF)-based technologies have emerged as a noninvasive strategy to disrupt biofilms on metallic implants and enhance antibiotic activity, with promising results in experimental implant infection models.50 Although these approaches represent important advances in biofilm-directed therapy, their clinical translation remains limited by the need for further validation, optimization of treatment parameters, regulatory approval, manufacturing scalability, and demonstration of long-term safety and clinical benefit.48–50

Periprosthetic joint infection remains one of the most consequential complications in arthroplasty, driven by diagnostic uncertainty, biofilm-mediated persistence, and substantial patient and system-level burden. While ESR, CRP, and standard cultures remain foundational, their limitations particularly in chronic or culture-negative cases have accelerated adoption of modern synovial biomarkers and multi-test strategies. In contemporary practice, synovial biomarker assays including alpha-defensin, leukocyte esterase, calprotectin, and D-lactate provide clinically useful adjuncts that improve diagnostic confidence when interpreted within established consensus frameworks.2,3,8

The next phase of progress will be defined by integrated precision diagnostics rather than any single transformative test. Molecular platforms such as metagenomic sequencing may improve pathogen identification and guide antimicrobial selection in complex or culture-negative cases, but broader adoption will depend on standardized workflows and clinically interpretable reporting.37–40 Artificial intelligence offers promise for integrating clinical, biomarker, imaging, and molecular data to reduce diagnostic variability and support individualized decision-making, yet must remain a validated decision-support tool rather than a replacement for clinician responsibility.34,35

Although modern biomarkers, molecular diagnostics, artificial intelligence, and biofilm-targeted strategies have expanded the diagnostic and therapeutic landscape, none is sufficient as a standalone solution. Future progress will depend on rigorous validation, standardization, and integration of these complementary approaches into established multidisciplinary diagnostic pathways. Ultimately, the future of PJI diagnosis lies not in identifying a single superior biomarker but in combining biological, molecular, and computational tools to support accurate, timely, and individualized patient care. Future research should focus on validating context-specific biomarker thresholds across different infection phenotypes and patient populations while integrating these tests into established multidisciplinary diagnostic pathways.15
