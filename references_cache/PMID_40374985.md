# Newborn Screening Followed By Early Treatment is Essential to Improve Survival in SCID.
**Authors:** Goebel GA, Cunha LAO, Minafra FG, Pinto JA
**Journal:** J Clin Immunol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10875-025-01887-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-025-01887-x)

## Content

Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) is a heterogeneous genetic disease characterized by severe T-cell lymphopenia with a profound impairment of T- and B-cells' function and, in some types, also NK cells. Hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) is the only curative treatment currently available in Brazil. Late diagnosis and treatment are the main factors affecting the survival of these children. This study aims to describe the demographic, phenotypic, genotypic, and clinical characteristics of twenty SCID patients (including typical SCID, leaky-SCID, and Omenn Syndrome) followed at a Brazilian referral center and correlate these data with their clinical outcome. The children were analyzed into two groups: patients diagnosed early by newborn screening (NBS) or family history, n = 7, and patients with late diagnosis, by clinical presentation, n = 13. The 2-year overall survival (OS) of the late group was 29.2%, in contrast to the 2-year OS of the early diagnosis group of 71.4% (p = 0.053). However, despite early diagnosis in the first group, timely access to HCT was delayed, with a median of 11 months. This research reveals that survival depends not only on timely diagnosis but also on early definitive treatment. To improve SCID survival rates, developing countries need public policies that allow rapid access to curative treatment for these patients.

Severe Combined Immunodeficiency Disease (SCID) is an Inborn Error of Immunity (IEI) that includes a heterogeneous group of genetic conditions. It is characterized by the absence of T lymphocytes, which is associated with the absence or dysfunction of B lymphocytes. Some forms of the disease also show impairment of Natural Killer (NK) cells [1]. Generally, patients are asymptomatic at birth. However, if they do not receive early diagnosis and treatment, they may develop severe infections with high mortality in the first year of life.

Since 2010, the United States of America (USA) has implemented universal newborn screening (NBS) for SCID through the quantification of TREC (T-cell receptor excision circles) [5–7]. In the following years, other countries [8–13] also formalized NBS for SCID. TRECs are DNA sequences excised after the rearrangement of the α and β T-cell receptor (TCR) during their maturation, reflecting the thymic production of naive T cells [7, 14]. The number of TREC copies is determined from dried blood spots on filter paper, where quantification is performed by real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)[15]. Four years after the implementation of NBS in the USA, a considerable increase in the incidence of SCID was described, from about 1 in 100,000 births [16, 17] to 1 in 58,000 [7, 14]. The incidence of SCID with autosomal recessive inheritance may be even higher in cultures where consanguineous marriage is common, as demonstrated in a pilot NBS study in Saudi Arabia that found an incidence of 1:2,906 births [18]. In 2021, the city of São Paulo, Brazil, implemented NBS and identified five patients with SCID out of 80,000 newborns screened [19–21]. From 2020 to 2021, the Nucleus of Actions and Research in Support of Diagnosis developed the first Brazilian population-based pilot program at the Federal University of Minas Gerais (NUPAD-UFMG) in Minas Gerais. Two hundred two thousand, seven hundred seventy two children were screened, and a prevalence of SCID 1:33,795 [7, 14] was estimated. In January 2024, NBS for SCID was universally implemented by the Unified Health System in Minas Gerais state.

Unfortunately, NBS for SCID is not yet universal in most countries. The diagnosis of SCID occurs mainly from a positive family history or, in most cases, a clinical history, contributing to maintaining a high mortality rate of the disease in the first years of life [22–27]. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HCT) is the most used curative treatment for SCID worldwide and the only treatment currently available in Brazil. Research showed that age > 3.5 months at the time of HCT, mainly when associated with active infection, relates to lower survival after the procedure [5, 28, 29]. Therefore, performing HCT earlier reduces the risk of infections during HCT, leading to decreased morbidity and mortality and a better prognosis for patients.

Many publications describe large cohorts assessing the survival of patients with SCID, but most are from developed countries [5, 30–35]. Fewer and more minor descriptions are found in developing countries [22–27]. In Brazil, only two studies have been conducted addressing the clinical, phenotypic, genotypic profile, and survival of patients diagnosed with SCID (platforms: Scopus, Web of Science, and PubMed) [21, 25]. This research aimed to analyze data from 20 patients with a confirmed diagnosis of SCID followed at a single Brazilian Immunology Referral Center. The authors evaluated the impact of implementing a pilot NBS program on the overall survival (OS) of SCID patients and the influence of the delay in timely access to HCT on the survival of the population studied.

A retrospective and prospective analysis of medical records of patients with a confirmed diagnosis of SCID (including typical SCID, leaky-SCID, and Omenn Syndrome) was conducted over the last two decades in the Immunology service of UFMG’s Hospital das Clínicas, one of the largest referral institutions for the treatment of IEI in Brazil. The data was collected from November 2021 to July 2023. Descriptions of 23 cases of the disease were found during this period, including 18 typical SCID, four leaky SCID, and one Omenn Syndrome. Two instances of typical SCID diagnosed in 2004 and 2005 were excluded because it was impossible to access their medical records, and one probable leaky SCID from 2013 was excluded because it was impossible to confirm the diagnosis with the available data in the medical record. Most patients received definitive treatment (HCT) at this Reference Centre (all with identical HLA donors). Transplants with grafts from HLA-haploidentical donors were carried out in other transplant centers in Brazil. For data analysis, the children were separated into two groups: 1) early diagnosis, by NBS or positive family history; 2) late diagnosis, by clinical presentation.

The phenotypic evaluation of lymphocyte subgroups for the absolute and relative quantification (CD3 +, CD4 +, CD8 +, CD16 +/56 +, CD19 +, CD3 CD4 CD45RO + (memory T helper lymphocyte), and CD4 CD45RA + (naive T helper lymphocyte) was performed through lymphocyte immunophenotyping by flow cytometry (Cytometer Couter-Epics XL-MCL). Genetic mutation research through next-generation sequencing was also performed whenever available. The normal ranges for lymphocyte quantification were determined based on references from the Brazilian population [36]. Lymphocytes were phenotypically classified as T- (CD3 < 300 cells/µL), B + (CD19 > 400 cells/µL), Blow (CD19 from 50 to 400 cells/µL), or B- (CD19 < 50 cells/µL), and NK + (> 100 cells/µL), NKlow (from 40 to 100 cells/µL), or NK- (< 40 cells/µL) according to the work of Pai and colleagues [28]. Patients diagnosed through NBS also had their TREC levels measured from dried blood spots on filter paper at NUPAD-UFMG laboratories. TREC analysis was carried out by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) using TREC-specific TaqMan-MGB primers and probes [37, 38]. The"normal"cut-off point was greater than or equal to 25 copies/μL [15, 39]. Patients P11, P13, and P14, despite not being diagnosed through NBS, also had their TREC levels measured.

The Primary Immune Deficiency Treatment Consortium (PIDTC)[41, 42]updated the criteria for diagnosing SCID in 2014 and 2022. These updates classified patients into typical SCID, leaky-SCID, and Omenn Syndrome. However, these criteria were not used to confirm the diagnosis of all patients because most of them were diagnosed before these publications.

A descriptive analysis of patient characterization was performed. Demographic variables related to the disease and transplantation were described using frequencies for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges for quantitative variables. Variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and a non-normal distribution was detected. Fisher's Exact Test was used to compare proportions, and the Mann–Whitney test was used to compare medians. Survival probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Differences with p ≤ 0.05 were considered significant. The software used for the analyses was SPSS version 25.0.

The institutional review board of UFMG approved the study.

Twenty patients with confirmed diagnoses of SCID were included for data analysis, of which sixteen were typical SCID, three were leaky-SCID, and one had Omenn Syndrome (Table 1). A higher number of diagnoses were observed in 2020–2021 (n = 7), coinciding with the period of the NUPAD-UFMG pilot NBS program for SCID.

Consanguinity was present in more than half of the families for which these data were available (8 out of 15 patients). A history of early deaths in the family was found in 9 of 18 patients (50%), with only one family (P5) having received genetic and immunological counseling.

All patients received the Bacillus Calmette-guérin (BCG) vaccine at birth, except for patient P5. Eighteen of the twenty patients had records in their medical files regarding the presence or absence of alterations secondary to vaccination. Eight of these eighteen patients (44.4%) experienced some complication related to the BCG vaccine, with 5 (62.5%) having a loco-regional reaction (BCGitis) and 3 (37.5%) with disseminated disease (BCGosis). The six patients from the NBS started prophylaxis with Rifampicin and Isoniazid upon diagnosis, and only one (0.17%) patient presented a loco-regional reaction, treated with a quadruple regimen (Rifampicin, Isoniazid, Ethambutol, and Clarithromycin), without complications. The group of patients who were diagnosed later presented more severe complications after vaccination, including disseminated BCGosis. Three patients in this group have BCG complications associated with their death’s causes (Table 3). Patients with later diagnoses had also received other live agent vaccines, such as rotavirus and yellow fever, according to the vaccination schedule established by the Brazilian National Immunization Program. After this vaccination, one patient (P14) had severe encephalitis from Yellow Fever.

Fifteen out of nineteen patients (78%) presented severe absolute lymphopenia in the blood count with lymphocytes < 2,500 cells/μL of blood at the time of diagnosis. One patient did not have a record of the absolute lymphocyte count. Three patients (P1, P7 and P8) had CD3 + lymphocytes > 300 cells/μL. They were included in this historical series because they met the minimum criteria for the diagnosis of SCID established in 2009 [21, 40], and were diagnosed before 2015 [21, 40]. Six out of nineteen patients (31.5%) presented hypogammaglobulinemia associated with lymphopenia at diagnosis, and one patient did not have a record of the immunoglobulin G (IgG) value. Out of the nine patients who underwent TREC screening, 8 had undetectable values, and one presented a result of 2.3 copies/μL of peripheral blood (normal range > 25 copies/μL) (Table 1).

The most prevalent phenotype was T-B + NK + with seven patients (35%), followed by T-BlowNK + with four patients (20%), T-B-NK + with three patients (15%), T-B-NK with two patients (10%), and T-BlowNK- with one patient (5%). In three patients (15%), B lymphocyte and NK cell counts were not performed due to the unavailability of the service and the severity of the condition at diagnosis.

Genetic analysis through next-generation sequencing revealed mutations in hemizygous in the IL2RG gene (4 patients), in homozygous in RAG1 (2), CD3δ (2), IL7R (1), and in compound heterozygous in ADA (1) and PNP (1). Nine patients did not have a genetic diagnosis. Three of them showed mutations in heterozygous (JAK3 (1), LIG4 (1), and RMRP (1)) that do not explain the disease. One did not present a mutation in the genetic panel of 17 genes for SCID available at the service (IL2RG, JAK3, ORAI1, GATA2, RAG1, RAG2, LCK, ADA, PNP, CD3D, CD3G, CD3Z, CD3E, IL7R, LIG4, DCLRE1 C, NHEJ1). Five of these patients were not submitted to genetic analysis due to the unavailability of the test at the time. Despite not having a genetic diagnosis, all of them met the minimum criteria of clinical and immunophenotyping for Severe Combined Immunodeficiency (SCID) (Tables 1, 3) [21, 40].

Several patients did not receive a genetic diagnosis. Nonetheless, they met the clinical and immunophenotyping criteria [21, 40] for Severe Combined Immunodeficiency (SCID) (Tables 1, 3). Specifically, patients P12, P14, and P20 presented with heterozygous mutations in the JAK3, LIG4, and RMRP genes, respectively. P12 presented with BCGitis and acute otitis media requiring intravenous antibiotic therapy. P14 had yellow fever encephalitis (post-vaccination) and died at the time of diagnosis. Despite undergoing genetic counseling, this family had another baby with the same mutation and identical immunophenotyping, but in homozygous form. This infant was not included in this analysis as they were born after the data collection period for this study. P20 had a TREC of zero copies in NBS and CD3 < 300 cells/μL. He started prophylactic therapy promptly after diagnosis and had no infections until HCT (Tables 3, 5). Patient 1 (P1) was admitted to the hospital at 3.5 months of age due to gastrointestinal sepsis, disseminated candidiasis, failure to thrive, and BCGitis. She was diagnosed at 5 months of age, but despite all medical interventions, she passed away within one month of her diagnosis (Table 1).

The patients were divided into two groups, early and late diagnosis, to assess the impact of early diagnosis on the disease outcome. The timing of diagnosis was also considered to evaluate whether the infection status before HCT at different ages would directly impact the survival of these patients after HCT. After the diagnostic confirmation with lymphocyte immunophenotyping, antimicrobial prophylaxis were started according to the Latin American Consensus on managing patients with SCID of 2019 [43].

Out of the seven patients diagnosed early, only 1 (P16) had been hospitalized before diagnostic confirmation at 30 days of life due to difficult-to-treat acute otitis media, requiring intravenous antibiotic therapy. During follow-up, even with appropriate prophylaxis, 5 of 7 patients (71.4%) were admitted to the ward to treat infectious events (Table 2), requiring parenteral antibiotic therapy without serious complications. Five of the seven also presented confirmed infection by RT-PCR for COVID-19 with mild symptoms. Patient 18 developed drug-induced hepatitis at 7 months. It was impossible to return to antimicrobial prophylaxis after repeated attempts, opting for rigorous observation and maintaining prophylaxis only with intravenous human immunoglobulin until HCT was performed (Table 2).

All patients in this group underwent curative treatment with HCT. The median age of patients at HCT was 380 days (IQR: 251–571; ranging from 185 to 623 days), and the median time between diagnosis and transplantation was 330 days (IQR: 226–524; ranging from 167 to 582 days) (Table 4). The earliest HCT was performed at 6 months of life (185 days), and the latest at 1 year and 8 months (623 days). One patient (P19) had failed to thrive since birth and had been hospitalized on two occasions previously. This patient underwent HCT at 9 months but succumbed to complications in the immediate post-HCT period. As the patient underwent HCT in another service, it was impossible to access clinical evolution details (Table 2). Patient P15 underwent HCT at 11 months of life (348 days) after treatment for acute CMV gastroenteritis, and the procedure was successful. However, a year after the transplant, he presented with an acute respiratory infection without a defined agent, which evolved into respiratory failure and death at 1 year and 11 months. Therefore, in this group of patients with early diagnosis, 5 out of 7 (71.4%) were successful in HCT and are alive and well, all with more than 3 years of follow-up. Although no patient underwent transplantation before 3.5 months of life, it was observed that the two patients who did not present infectious episodes before the procedure were alive at more than 3 years of age. Only three of the five patients who had infectious episodes before HCT survived (60%) (Table 2).

The median age of patients at HCT was 539 days (IQR: 400–1022; ranging from 328 to 3294 days), with no significant difference from the early diagnosis group (380 days; p = 0.234). (Table 4). The earliest HCT was performed at 12 months of life (335 days) and the latest at 9 years (2851 days) (Table 4). The percentage of survivors in the study population was 47.3% (9 out of 19 patients, excluding one patient who was lost to follow-up), with a median follow-up time of 7 years (ranging from 3 to 15 years). As no patient in this cohort underwent HCT before 3.5 months of age to assess the direct impact of early transplantation on survival, we evaluated the effect of early diagnosis (at birth or by positive family history) on survival.

The 2-year overall survival (OS) of the whole group was 45.3% (95% CI 22.3–68.2%) (Fig. 2a). The percentage of survivors among patients who had the opportunity to undergo HCT was 69.2% (9 out of 13 patients – Table 2). Comparing the 2-year OS of this group of patients, we observed very similar rates between the two groups, 71.4% (95% CI 37.8–100%) for patients in the early diagnosis group vs. 60% (95% CI 17–100%) for patients in the late diagnosis group (p = 0.774), suggesting that early diagnosis alone may not directly impact survival after HCT (Fig. 2c). While waiting for HCT, many patients had infections, which may have contributed to the authors not finding a difference in OS between the two groups (p = 0.774).

Assessing the impact of prior infections on clinical outcomes before HCT, we found a significant difference in the percentage of survivors between patients who underwent transplantation without prior infection and those who underwent transplantation after experiencing infectious conditions. In patients who did not present an infection before HCT, we found a survival percentage of 100% (2 out of 2 patients). In contrast, in those who had an infectious episode before HCT, we found a survival percentage of 60% in the early diagnosis group and 66.6% in the late diagnosis group. However, a statistical comparison was impossible due to the small number in each group, and the late diagnosis without infection group had no representatives.

SCID is a fatal condition that can be cured if adequately diagnosed and treated. However, its management is particularly challenging in developing countries, where, in many cases, sufficient HCT centers do not exist, and BCG vaccination and infections are issues. The NBS hopes to provide assertive health care to these patients. This study addresses whether only the early diagnosis is sufficient to change the scenario of this condition.

There was a significant increase in SCID diagnoses in 2020 and 2021 in Minas Gerais state, coinciding with the implementation of the NBS pilot program. Considering the estimated incidence of SCID from this pilot program of 0.29 cases per 10,000 live births (1:33,795) and an average of 250,000 live births per year in the state, about 145 patients were expected to be diagnosed in the last two decades. Since only 23 patients were documented in this referral center, it can be inferred that, although some patients may have been diagnosed and followed up in other centers, most cases occurred without proper diagnosis and follow-up. Being a rare disease, few healthcare professionals can recognize it by clinical presentation, which delays the correct diagnosis.

Most patients in this cohort were diagnosed from family history and/or clinical suspicion (14 out of 20 patients). The overall median age at diagnosis was 164 days (5.4 months), lower than the median found in the last two Brazilian studies, 6.5 months [21, 25], and similar to those found in Turkey, India, and Japan [32, 44, 45]. However, excluding patients diagnosed through NBS, the median rises to 190 days (6.3 months), approaching the national median and showing the influence of neonatal screening on the early diagnosis of these patients.

In Brazil, BCG vaccination is mandatory due to the high incidence of tuberculosis in the country, with more than 78,000 new cases per year, according to the Brazilian Ministry of Health. It is administered in the first 5 days of life for term babies and preterm infants when they reach more than 2 kg in weight. Of the 20 patients in the study, 19 received the vaccine at birth, and only one did not receive it due to a prior family history of SCID. Vaccine-related complications were described in 44.4% of the patients, mostly being loco-regional reactions. Compared to other Brazilian studies, Barreiros et al. [21] found 28.9%, also with a predominance of loco-regional reactions. Mazzucchelli et al. [25] found a 65% rate of vaccine-related complications, with 48.3% disseminated reactions. This last higher rate could be related to the data collection period (2009–2011) when knowledge was more limited, especially in developing countries. Among the six patients diagnosed through NBS and who started early prophylaxis with Rifampicin and Isoniazid, only one patient developed BCGitis, which was treated with a quadruple regimen (Rifampicin, Isoniazid, Ethambutol, and Clarithromycin) and did not develop complications—the study conducted by Marciano et al. [46] assessed the complications of BCG vaccination in 349 SCID patients from 28 centers in 17 countries and found a low incidence of vaccine-related complications in patients who received anti-mycobacterial therapy while asymptomatic (only 2 of 78 patients). BCG-associated complications in this group also did not result in death [46].

However, based only on these data, it is not possible to ensure that, with the implementation of NBS in countries where BCG vaccination is mandatory in the first days of life, the incidence of vaccine-related complications will remain low due to the early start of prophylactic antimicrobial therapy in asymptomatic infants. It is also not possible to predict the frequency of adverse effects of these medications starting at such an early age. More studies should be developed to assess the safety of maintaining BCG vaccination at birth in countries with NBS for SCID.

The percentage of survivors in this study’s population was 47.3%, like other Brazilian studies [21, 25]. A significant difference in 2-year OS was observed between patients diagnosed early and those diagnosed late (p = 0.053), corroborating the importance of early diagnosis for the survival of these patients. For patients who underwent HCT, it was observed that the median time between diagnosis and HCT was approximately 11 months in both groups. Many studies from developed countries have already demonstrated better outcomes in patients who underwent transplantation before 3.5 months of age and without a history of prior infections, with an average survival rate of 85–96% [5, 28, 30, 32, 34, 47–50]. In our study, despite patients from the NBS pilot program or with a positive family history of SCID receiving the diagnosis with a median of 41 days, no one underwent HCT before 3.5 months of age. The earliest transplant was at 6 months, and most of the patients had already had an infection during this period. These factors may partly explain the lack of statistical difference in survival rates after transplantation between the early and late diagnosis groups. In the literature, for those patients who were transplanted under the same conditions found in our population, late transplants, and/or after infectious episodes, survival rates ranged from 50–69% [28, 32, 34, 49, 51]. These values align with those found in our population, at 69.2%. The rates also resemble the values found in the most recent Brazilian studies, 70% and 68%, [21, 25, 52] and those from other developing countries like Iran [24] and Turkey [45].

Despite increasing knowledge about the disease in recent years, with the implementation of NBS in some Brazilian locations, there is still a shortage of human and financial resources, contributing to the delay in definitive treatment for these patients. Currently, the main challenge to overcome this delay is to increase the number of beds in transplant centers and open new transplant centers in Brazil. Due to the lack of supplies and technology for haploidentical transplants, our referral center only performs HLA-identical transplants, which include matched related donor or matched unrelated donor transplants. In addition, few beds are available once the transplant hospital ward receives adult and child patients for hematological, kidney, liver, and heart transplants. Only two public health centers perform haploidentical transplants in SCID patients in Brazil. The authors also observed a delay in identifying and locating compatible unrelated donors nationally and internationally. During this study, NBS was only a pilot program. NBS was officially implemented across the state of Minas Gerais in January 2024. Several strategies to address the problem of the delay until curative treatment have already been put into action: 1) the creation of an outpatient clinic to receive only screened patients with a trained medical team; 2) the organization of a flow of specific test requests and availability of a genetic test within one month for patients with a phenotypic diagnosis; 3) collection of samples for rapid HLA typing of parents and siblings and, if not compatible, immediate enrollment in the bone marrow bank; 4) establishment of a referral flow for the patient to the bone marrow transplant team immediately after the confirmed diagnosis. However, despite all the measures already taken, we still see that the main challenge that delays the definitive treatment of these patients is the lack of beds and staff with the capacity to absorb the demand from all over the country. To solve this problem, it is imperative to implement public policies ensuring suitable transplant centers are equipped with the necessary tools to carry out HCT in children.

This research shows that early diagnosis using NBS can help more children receive appropriate care from specialists until they undergo curative treatment with HCT. This can prevent the development of severe infections and reduce the overall mortality of the disease. However, in addition to early diagnosis, shortening the time between diagnosis and HCT is crucial to improve patients'survival after HCT.

One limitation of this study is that it is observational, with most patients being assessed retrospectively. However, this limitation was addressed by using standardized medical records. Another challenge is the rarity of the disease, resulting in a small number of patients analyzed; only 20 diagnoses were made over the past two decades. This limited sample size may also impact the statistical analysis regarding the effects of early diagnosis on patient survival and the incidence of infections on outcomes following HCT.
