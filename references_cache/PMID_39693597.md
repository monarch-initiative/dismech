BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES: Susac syndrome (SuS) is a rare disorder characterized 
by encephalopathy, branch retinal artery occlusion, and sensorineural hearing 
loss, often accompanied by vertigo. Recent updates to diagnostic criteria and 
treatment guidelines have been made. This study examines clinical 
manifestations; disease activity; and risk factors of disability, dependency, 
and return to work in patients with SuS.
METHODS: A retrospective multicenter study was conducted on 20 consecutive 
patients with SuS with at least 2 years of follow-up. Clinical and paraclinical 
activities were assessed and rated according to the severity at onset and the 
end of follow-up. Cognitive function was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive 
Assessment while disability and dependence in daily activities were measured 
using the modified Rankin Scale. Employment status was graded.
RESULTS: The mean age at onset was 38.9 years, with a mean follow-up of 55.9 
months. The female-to-male ratio was 1.86, and 45% of patients had the complete 
clinical triad. Severe cerebral involvement at onset was associated with a 
higher risk of cerebral exacerbations within the first year and with an 
increased long-term disability and dependency. Cognitive function improved in 
75% of patients during follow-up. At disease onset, hearing loss excluding low 
frequencies occurred in 46.7%. Relapse of hearing loss was associated with 
greater impairment in daily activities. Male sex and elevated CSF protein levels 
were linked to poorer prognosis. Cerebral and inner ear exacerbations were most 
common in the first year while retinal exacerbations occurred more frequently, 
mainly within the first 2 years. Approximately 50% of patients resumed 
employment while 25% did not return to work.
DISCUSSION: Current treatment strategies for SuS do not fully prevent relapses. 
Severe brain manifestation at onset, male sex, and high CSF protein levels are 
risk factors of a worse prognosis of disability and dependence, indicating the 
need for intensive treatment. High-frequency hearing loss does not exclude SuS 
diagnosis.

Susac syndrome (SuS) is a rare immune-mediated occlusive microvascular endotheliopathy, characterized by encephalopathy, hearing loss, and visual disturbances.1–6 First described in 1979, it is an autoimmune disease targeting blood vessel endothelium, primarily mediated by CD8 T cells leading to ischemic lesions in the brain, inner ear, and retina.3,5

Clinical manifestations vary widely, with the complete triad present in fewer than 20% of patients at onset.5–10 The European Susac Consortium has proposed diagnostic criteria to enhance early diagnosis, facilitate earlier treatment, and reduce diagnostic errors.11

The clinical triad consists of brain involvement, which may present as headache, cognitive dysfunction, confusion, and behavioral changes, often with typical MRI findings. Inner ear involvement is marked by hearing loss (typically in low frequencies), tinnitus, and/or vertigo if vestibular function is impaired. The eye is affected by branch retinal artery occlusion (BRAO), with fluorescein angiogram (FA) showing BRAO, arterial wall hyperfluorescence (AWH), and microaneurysms in later stages. Patients may experience vision loss and visual field defects secondary to retinal artery occlusion.1–6,11–13

There are no randomized controlled trials or prospective studies supporting current treatment approaches. Recommendations are based on empirical evidence from retrospective case series, expert opinions, and therapeutic experiences with juvenile dermatomyositis, because of its pathologic similarity to SuS and anti-neutrophilic cytoplasmic antibody-associated vasculitis.12,14,15 Treatment is based on the severity of encephalopathy and expected disease-related disabilities. Long-term outcomes vary widely, from mild to extremely severe permanent functional deficits.16–20

There is limited knowledge on predictive factors and treatment needs for SuS. Early sufficiently aggressive immunosuppressive treatment may improve prognosis21 and reduce severe hearing loss risk.22 However, brain lesion load during the acute phase was not associated with disability risk.23

The study objectives were to describe the clinical manifestations of SuS at onset and during follow-up and to identify prognostic risk factors of developing disability and dependency from demographic, clinical, and laboratory parameters. In addition, we evaluated the treatment responses based on current guidelines.12

This observational retrospective study was conducted in 3 major Israeli medical centers: Tel Aviv Sourasky Medical Center, Rabin Medical Center, and Shamir Medical Center.

The study cohort comprised 20 consecutive adult patients with probable or definite SuS according to Kleffner criteria,11 diagnosed between July 2017 and August 2023 and followed for at least 2 years. Treatment followed the immunotherapy guidelines12 (Table 1).

SuS diagnosis was based on typical clinical symptoms evaluated by neurologists, rheumatologists, ophthalmologists, and otorhinolaryngologist specialists, combined with typical paraclinical findings on brain MRI; BRAO and AWH on FA; and vestibulocochlear involvement confirmed by audiogram, video head impulse test, and cervical vestibular-evoked myogenic potentials.24 A follow-up period of at least two years was required because of the monocyclic course of SuS observed in the first 1–2 years.25

Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were obtained from the institutional medical records. All patients received treatment per guidelines immediately after diagnosis,12 which continued throughout the follow-up period. The patients came for routine follow-up visits at least once every 3 months and whenever new symptoms were suspected. The patients were hospitalized in neurology departments or treated in infusion units during an exacerbation. The ongoing parenteral treatment was given in the infusion units. All patients remained under follow-up after their diagnosis, and there were no cases of discontinuation of follow-up.

To evaluate the impact of SuS clinical manifestations on disability and dependency, we graded symptom severity at disease onset and the end of the follow-up. Manifestations were ranked by their effect on organ functions, with cerebral damage severity assessed using the encephalopathy classifications.12 Mild encephalopathy was defined as mild or noncognitive and executive dysfunction and/or personality changes at levels that may not be obvious to nonfamily members, mild or no language dysfunction, fully independent in activities of daily living (ADL), independent ambulation, and possible new headache onset. Moderate encephalopathy was defined as mild confusion, mild-to-moderate cognitive and/or language dysfunction, variable neurologic symptoms, somewhat dependent in some ADL, and fully ambulatory but may have some difficulty with strength or balance. Severe encephalopathy was defined as abnormal thought processing, moderate-to-severe cognitive and/or language dysfunction, multifocal neurologic symptoms, considerable dependency in most ADL, and ambulatory but may require assistance. Extremely severe encephalopathy was defined as extensive cognitive and language dysfunction, multifocal neurologic symptoms, very dependent in all ADL, inability to control sphincters, bedridden, requiring constant nursing care, and inability to cooperate with tests such as FAs or audiograms.

A clinical cerebral relapse was defined as a symptomatic exacerbation occurring at least 1 month after disease onset or a previous relapse. Cognitive impairment was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)26 at disease onset and the end of follow-up.

Audiometric frequencies were categorized as low (250–500 Hz), mid (1,000–2000 Hz), and high (4,000–8,000 Hz). Hearing impairment severity was assessed in the more affected ear using the World Health Organization definition.27 Mild hearing loss was defined as a 26–40 dB loss or symptomatic impairment below 26 dB, moderate as a 41–60 dB loss, and severe as a 61–80 dB loss or hearing aid use.

Retinal involvement was assessed based on the extent of visual field impairment in the more affected eye. Visual field defects were categorized as mild, characterized by the presence of scotomas; moderate, involving up to 1 quadrant of the visual field; and severe, affecting more than one quadrant.28

Overall disability and dependency in ADL at the end of follow-up were assessed using the modified Rankin Scale.29 This scale categorizes outcomes as follows: grade 0 denotes no symptoms; grade 1 indicates no significant disability despite symptoms, with full capacity to perform usual duties; grade 2 reflects slight disability, characterized by the inability to perform all previous activities but with the ability to manage personal affairs independently; grade 3 signifies moderate disability, requiring some assistance yet allowing for independent ambulation; and grade 4 represents moderately severe disability, marked by an inability to walk independently, hear without a hearing aid, or manage personal needs without help. Employment status was classified into 3 categories: no return, partial return, and full return to employment.

This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki principles and received ethics committee approval from each participating medical center. Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design and anonymized nature of the study.

Statistical analysis and data graphical representation were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.3.0). Descriptive statistics reported clinical variables, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test compared nonparametric dependent variables, the Fisher exact test assessed associations between categorical variables, and the Spearman rank correlation coefficient evaluated correlations of nonparametric variables. Results were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Sixteen patients presented with cerebral involvement/encephalopathy at disease onset. The severity of the cerebral involvement/encephalopathy at disease onset was mild or moderate clinical manifestations in 10 patients (50.0%) and severe or extremely severe in 6 patients (30.0%, Table 2).

Among the patients with cerebral manifestations at the disease onset, most of the patients (n = 14, 87.5%) presented with cognitive dysfunction and/or confusion and/or behavioral changes. Two patients (12.5%) who had mild cerebral manifestations presented solely with headache as a SuS-related symptom that was accompanied by typical findings of SuS in brain MRI.

During follow-up, 4 patients (20.0%) experienced symptomatic cerebral relapse (3 with cognitive exacerbation and 1 with focal neurologic signs), accompanied by new brain MRI findings, all within the first year. These patients had cerebral symptoms at disease onset. In addition, 3 other patients had new asymptomatic cerebral lesions on MRI, 2 occurred in the first year and 1 in the fifth year. One of these patients had no cerebral symptoms at onset. The likelihood of cerebral symptomatic relapse was higher in patients with more severe cerebral symptoms at onset (grades 2–4) while those with milder symptoms were at lower risk (p = 0.014).

Among the 8 patients with moderate or greater cerebral manifestations at disease onset, 4 experienced cerebral symptomatic relapse within the first year. Of these, 1 received a moderate, 1 a severe, and 2 an extremely severe treatment protocol that included CTX. The other 4 patients, who did not relapse, all received extremely severe treatment protocols.12 These patients also had no additional asymptomatic brain manifestations in MRI imaging (Table 3).

Throughout treatment and follow-up, cerebral manifestations improved partially, leaving 12 patients with mild impairment, none with moderate, and 4 with severe impairment due to cerebral involvement at the end of follow-up (Table 2). Overall, 13 patients (65.0%) remained stable, including 4 without clinical cerebral symptoms, while 7 patients (35.0%) showed improvement, and no patients experienced deterioration (Table 2).

Cognitive improvement was also reflected in the MoCA test scores. The MoCA test was performed in 12 of the 16 patients who displayed a cerebral clinical manifestation at disease onset. The median MoCA test scores at onset was 24 (range 12–29) and improved in 9 patients (75.0%) by at least 2 points with a median score of 28 (range of 22–29) at the end of follow-up (p = 0.003, Figure 1).

Twelve patients with cerebral involvement at SuS onset were assessed with MoCA at disease onset and follow-up, showing a significant improvement in scores over time. MoCA = Montreal Cognitive Assessment.

Hearing loss was present in 15 patients (75.0%) at disease onset and in 16 patients (80%) over the disease course. At onset, 12 patients (60.0%) had mild hearing loss, including 1 asymptomatic; 1 had moderate loss (5.0%); and 2 had severe loss (10.0%). During follow-up, 3 patients (15.0%) experienced symptomatic exacerbation of hearing loss, 2 within the first year and 1 in the fourth year. This worsening led to severe impairment in 2 patients, who required hearing aids. Of these, 2 were on severe disease protocols and 1 on a moderate disease protocol.12 In addition, 3 patients had asymptomatic worsening detected by audiogram at the 1st, 2nd, and 6th years. At the end of follow-up, 4 patients (20.0%) had severe hearing loss needing a hearing aid, 2 (10.0%) had moderate loss, and 9 (45.0%) had mild loss, including 5 being asymptomatic. Vestibular symptoms, including vertigo, were noted in 1 patient at onset and another in the second year.

At disease onset, 15 patients (75.0%) had retinal involvement: 6 (30.0%) with mild visual field defects, 5 (25.0%) with moderate defects, and 4 (20.0%) with severe defects. Two patients (10.0%) had asymptomatic BRAO and/or AWH detected by FA (Table 2). During follow-up, 14 patients (70.0%) experienced additional retinal activity: 3 (15.0%) had symptomatic BRAO, 5 (20.0%) had both symptomatic and asymptomatic BRAO/AWH, and 6 (30.0%) had asymptomatic BRAO/AWH. The frequency of new symptomatic and/or asymptomatic retinal activity (event) varied, with 7 patients having 1 event, 4 patients having 2 events, 2 patients having 3 events, and 1 patient having 5 events.

Retinal events during follow-up occurred in 10 patients (50.0%) in the 1st year, 7 patients (35.0%) in the 2nd year, 2 patients in the 3rd and 4th years, 3 patients in the 5th year, and 1 patient each in the 6th and 7th years (Table 3). Visual field damage worsened in 3 patients (15.0%) and improved in 5 patients (25%). By the end of follow-up, no patients had severe visual defects, 7 patients (35.0%) had moderate, 11 patients (55.0%) had mild, and 2 patients (10.0%) were asymptomatic (Table 2).

Treatment was based on the severity of cerebral manifestations at onset: 9 patients (45.0%) received mild-to-moderate protocols, 4 patients (20.0%) received severe protocols, and 7 patients (35.0%) received extremely severe protocols (Table 1). Disease activity persisted across all protocols. In the first year, 13 patients showed ongoing activity. Brain activity was present in 6 patients (30.0%) in the first year but decreased significantly in the second year, with a significantly higher frequency of disease flares in the first year compared with the subsequent 3 years (p = 0.027). Inner ear activity was relatively infrequent, occurred in 3 patients (15.0%) in the first year and 2 patients (10.0%) in the second year, declining thereafter. Retinal activity, seen in 10 patients (50.0%) in the first year and 7 patients (35.0%) in the second year, also decreased, with fewer occurrences in years 3 and 4 (p = 0.041). Retinal activity remained slightly more frequent than activity in the other target organs (Table 3).

We used the modified Rankin Scale to evaluate functional impairment and dependence in daily activities at the end of follow-up. The median score was 1, with a range of 0–4. Fifteen patients (75.0%) had minimal disability (score ≤1) and could perform all daily tasks, including 4 who were completely asymptomatic (score = 0). One patient (5.0%) had slight disability (score = 2) and could not perform all previous activities, 2 patients (10.0%) had moderate disability requiring some assistance (score = 3), and 2 patients (10.0%) had moderate-severe disability needing constant help in daily activities (score = 4). Regarding employment, 10 patients (50.0%) returned to full-time employment, 5 (25.0%) returned to part-time employment, and 5 (25.0%) did not return to employment.

We studied the extent to which the sex, age at onset, and clinical parameters of the disease served as a prognostic marker for the later degree of disability or dependence in daily activities.

All the 5 patients with mild, moderate, and moderate-severe disability (modified Rankin scores = 2–4) were men (71.4% of the men, p = 0.001 compared with women). No significant association was found between age at SuS onset, disease duration, and disability due to SuS.

We investigated whether the initial manifestations of the disease predict later disability or dependence in daily activities. We found a significant positive correlation between the severity of initial cerebral manifestations and the degree of disability or dependence at follow-up (r = 0.733, p< 0.001, Figure 2). Six patients had severe and extremely severe cerebral manifestations at the onset of the disease. Severe or extremely severe cerebral manifestations at onset were associated with an increased degree of disability or dependence in daily activities at the end of the follow-up (Rankin scores 3–4, p = 0.003) while mild or no cerebral manifestations at onset, found in 14 patients, were associated with lower degree of disability or dependence scores (Rankin scores 0–1, p < 0.001). There was no correlation between the severity of initial hearing loss and subsequent disability or dependence (r = 0.233, p = 0.323). The damage to the retina did not lead to severe visual dysfunction and did not correlate with overall disability or dependence (r = −0.380, p = 0.098).

A significant positive correlation was found between the severity of cerebral manifestations at SuS onset and disability and dependency at follow-up, as measured using the modified Rankin Scale. SuS = Susac syndrome.

Because the disease affects various organs with different degrees of influence on disability, we examined the relationship between the damage to each organ and the return to full and partial employment or no return to employment. The severity of the cerebral manifestations and the severity of hearing loss at the end of follow-up negatively correlated with the return to employment (r = −0.525, p = 0.018, and r = −0.447, p = 0.048, respectively) while there was no significant correlation between the severity of the visual damage and the return to employment (r = 0, p = 1).

The total protein and white blood cell (WBC) concentrations in the CSF measured at disease onset were available for 16 of the 20 patients and are presented in Table 1. The total protein concentration (mean ± SD) was 86.6 ± 35.3 mg/dL (normal value ≤45.0 mg/dL). The CSF protein concentration was above the upper normal limit in 14 patients (87.5%). The CSF protein levels and the degree of disability or dependence in daily activities at the end of the follow-up correlated positively (r = 0.669, p = 0.002, Figure 3), and a protein level above 100 mg/dL was associated with a modified Rankin score ≥3 (p = 0.014). The mean WBC concentration was 5.9 ± 4.6 cells/μL (normal count ≤4 cells/μL). WBC concentration values above the upper normal limit were found in 8 patients (50.0%). No correlation was found between the WBC concentration in the CSF and the degree of disability or dependence in daily activities (r = −0.045, p = 0.864). Intrathecal synthesis of oligoclonal bands (OCBs) were tested in 13 patients during the diagnostic evaluations and found in 3 patients (2 men and 1 woman). There was no significant association between the presence of intrathecal OCBs and the degree of disability or dependence in daily activities (p = 0.202).

A significant positive correlation was found between CSF total protein levels at diagnosis and disability and dependency at the end of the follow-up, as measured using the modified Rankin Scale.

We conducted a multicenter retrospective study to describe SuS clinical manifestations at onset and follow-up, to identify prognostic risk factors of disability and dependency, and to evaluate therapeutic responses to recommended treatments. Consistent with previous reports, the full clinical triad was present at onset in a minority of patients.1,6,7,15,18

The female-to-male prevalence ratio of SuS was 1.86, which is lower than the usually reported ratios of 2.2–3.5.11,12,17,18,30 This indicates a relative increase in prevalence among men in our cohort. Variations in the ratio may reflect differences between populations, as a previous study of 10 patients with SuS in Israel described 6 women to 4 men19 and a ratio of 1.13 was found in an Australian cohort.31 Rennebohm and Susac defined 3 disease courses: (1) monocyclic, with dominant cerebral activity in the first 1–2 years followed by resolution; (2) polycyclic, with initial retinal or inner ear symptoms and relapses occurring beyond 2 years; and (3) chronic, with ongoing worsening.30 All 20 patients in our study had either the monocyclic or polycyclic course. We did not find a case of chronic course. It is worth noting that the course of the disease in part of the patients may not fit the definitions of monocyclic or polycyclic or chronic disease, and it was suggested to consider instead a classification of relapsing-remitting disease.32

Consistent with previous reports, most patients did not experience significant disability.17 However, some patients had substantial impacts on daily functioning and did not return to their predisease level.

Our study identified prognostic factors of disability and dependence in SuS and found that more severe cerebral manifestation at disease onset was associated with worse prognosis. This contrasts with the findings of Aubart-Cohen et al.,17 who found no such association in their 9 cases.

In addition, an increased disability and dependency were associated with male sex and high CSF total protein levels (especially above 100 mg/dL). Because our cohort is not large and represents a certain population, the possibility that high CSF protein levels at onset comprise a biomarker of more severe disease warrants further investigation.

One aim was to assess the therapeutic effect of recommended treatment protocols. Owing to the lack of a controlled study, we could not perform a definitive analysis. Our study found that disease activity continued in some patients despite treatment. Most patients did not experience worsening of cerebral or inner ear symptoms after onset. When relapses did occur, they were primarily during the first 2 years of the disease, indicating a need for better prevention of ongoing disease activity in these early years.

The tendency toward cerebral worsening was found mainly among patients with a cerebral manifestation of at least moderate degree or worse at disease onset. We observed cognitive improvement among our patients during the follow-up as had been reported by others.10,33

The occurrence of auditory relapse was uncommon but was found to be associated with an increased risk of functional deterioration and reduced likelihood of returning to full employment.

Retinal activity in SuS typically persists for the first 2 years but can continue longer in some cases. We observed 1 patient with retinal involvement lasting over 7 years. While retinal relapses were more frequent than cerebral or inner ear relapses, they did not cause disability or affect daily functioning or employment. Continued retinal activity despite immunosuppressant and immunomodulatory treatments was also reported in 10 patients not treated with the updated protocol.19

The relatively high incidence of retinal activity in the first 2 years suggests a delayed therapeutic response in the retina compared with the brain and inner ear. This variation in disease activity across organs may indicate different pathophysiologic mechanisms or intrinsic differences in affected tissues.

The typical hearing loss in SuS is usually described as being in the low-medium hearing frequencies.2,4,7,11,14,15,17,19,22,34-36 Indeed, we found that the low-frequency loss is the most common at the end of the follow-up (81.3% of the patients with hearing loss). However, hearing loss of the high frequencies was also common at onset (60.0%), and there were cases where the hearing loss at disease onset was only in the high-frequency range (20.0%). Furthermore, in 46.7% of the cases at disease onset, no low-frequency loss was observed. Therefore, we suggest that diagnosis of SuS should not be ruled out when the hearing loss is not in the low frequencies.

In summary, SuS is most active in the first 2 years. The current treatment guidelines did not completely prevent disease activity, especially during the first 1–2 years, thus raising an unmet therapeutic need. Severe initial cerebral manifestation was associated with significant risk of disability and dependence, as well as the risk of cerebral relapse within the first year of the disease. In addition, male sex and elevated CSF protein levels were associated with more severe disease outcomes, suggesting that these factors may warrant the initiation of more aggressive treatment at disease onset. While most patients do not experience meaningful disability, 25% of our cohort were left with some degree of disability and functional dependence. Return to work was notably affected, with only half of the patients resuming full employment, and a quarter were unable to return at all. This study summarizes findings regarding 20 patients; therefore, validation is recommended, as well as continued research in larger cohorts.
