Mantle Cell Lymphoma (MCL) is a B-cell neoplasm with a high incidence of 
relapse, even after the introduction of novel targeted therapies. MCL cells 
develop in specialized tissue microenvironments such as bone marrow and 
secondary lymphoid organs, where the pathological cells interact with several 
microenvironmental components through a complex network of soluble factors and 
adhesion molecules. This dynamic and complex system, including the activation of 
B-cell receptor signaling (BCR), promotes survival, immune evasion, and 
therapeutic resistance. Given the central role of the BCR signaling pathway in 
proliferation and survival of neoplastic B lymphocytes, in the last decades 
several biological agents against the key BCR proteins, i.e. Bruton Tyrosine 
Kinase inhibitors (BTKi), have been developed, and they represent the most 
effective treatment for MCL management. Importantly, BTKi response is influenced 
by the type of BCR signaling preferentially adopted by MCL cells and the 
composition of the tumor microenvironment. The present review summarizes and 
elucidates the mechanisms by which MCL cells and their microenvironments 
interact and how MCL cells integrate these interactions with the BCR signaling, 
highlighting the involvement of these external cues on MCL pathogenesis, 
progression, and treatment.

Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is an uncommon subtype of lymphoid malignancies that accounts for approximately 5–7% of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) in Western Countries, with a reported incidence of 1–2 per 100,000 persons. MCL is more common in males than in women with a 3:1 ratio [1]. MCL is clinically heterogeneous, with some patients having an indolent disease, not requiring treatment for several years, while others experiencing a highly aggressive disease with dismal outcome [2]. Patients typically present with lymphadenopathy at several sites. Extranodal dissemination occurs in 90% of patients, including infiltration of the bone marrow (BM), dissemination into the peripheral blood (PB), liver and gastrointestinal (GI) tract [1, 3].

The genetic hallmark of MCL is the t(11;14) translocation that juxtaposes the cyclin D1 gene (CCND1) to the immunoglobulin heavy or light chain loci, leading to the overexpression of CYCLIN D1 [4]. According to the ICC/WHO 2022 update, MCL is subdivided in two categories [5, 6]. The most common form of MCL (Nodal MCL, 80–90% of cases) is characterized by sex-determining region Y-box 11 (SOX11) overexpression, generalized lymphadenopathy, and a more aggressive clinical course [5–7]. Acquisition of additional molecular/cytogenetic abnormalities can lead to even more aggressive pleomorphic or blastoid MCL [5, 6, 8]. Leukemic non-nodal (LNN) MCL (10–20% of cases) mainly involves PB and spleen but not lymph nodes (LNs) in early stages; typically displays SOX11 negativity, and presents an indolent clinical behavior, although the acquisition of tumor protein P53 (TP53) alterations and genomic complexity confer an adverse prognosis [5–7].

Although median overall survival (OS) has doubled in the last decades, due to notable therapeutic advances, OS curves never reach a plateau, all patients virtually relapse, and disease remains incurable [9, 10]. Approximately 15% of MCL patients are resistant and progress during induction therapy, due to chemoimmunotherapy (CIT) refractoriness. Therapy for relapsed MCL patients primarily involves Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors (BTKi)—such as ibrutinib—the most established agents targeting the B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling, clinically available for MCL [11–14].

In this review, we summarize the current knowledge on mechanisms by which MCL cells and their microenvironments interact and how MCL cells integrate these interactions with the BCR signaling, highlighting the involvement of these external cues on MCL pathogenesis, progression, and treatment.

MCL is one of the malignant lymphoid neoplasms with a higher level of genomic instability. It is driven by a complex network of genetic alterations that contribute to disease progression and treatment resistance. The initial and characteristic event is the chromosomal translocation t(11;14) (q14;q32). This event leads the CCND1 gene under the transcriptional control of the immunoglobulin heavy-chain variable (IGHV) region gene, which is constantly active in mature peripheral B cells, thus resulting in the constitutive overexpression of CYCLIN D1 and subsequent dysregulation of the cell cycle at the G1-S phase transcription [15]. However, in rare cases, CYCLIN D1 is not expressed, and instead, CYCLIN D2 (CCND2), D3 (CCND3), and E1 (CCNE1) are expressed as a consequence of unusual chromosomal translocations. The t(2;12)(p11;p13)/IGK-CCND2 translocation is found in CYCLIN D2-positive cases, the t(6;14)(p21;q32)/IGH-CCND3 translocation is found in CYCLIN D3-positive cases, and a novel cryptic t(2;14)(p24;q32) translocation targeting V-Myc avian myelocytomatosis viral oncogene neuroblastoma (MYCN) is detected in cases expressing CYCLIN E [16]. Although t(11;14) is an oncogenic event in most MCLs, the primary differentiating element into Nodal and LNN MCLs is the expression of SOX11, which is usually overexpressed in the Nodal subtype. Unlike CCND1, no genetic lesions were associated with SOX11 overexpression in MCL. Rather, its upregulation seems to be driven by hypomethylation of a distant enhancer region, which induces an alteration in the three-dimensional chromatin pattern, leading to the activation of SOX11 transcription [17]. SOX11 acts in MCL biology as oncogene, regulating several mechanisms that contribute to tumor progression and malignancy. It increases the BCR signaling activity through repression of the transcription of B-cell lymphoma 6 (BCL6), thus preventing the entry of lymphocytes into the germinal center [18, 19]. In addition, SOX11 induces the block of mature B-cell differentiation, modulation of cell cycle, apoptosis, and stem cell development, mainly through upregulation of paired box 5 (PAX5), a transcription factor active during early B-cell development [20–22].

In addition to the t(11;14) primary oncogenic event, MCLs carry at least one secondary driver alteration, including gene mutations, copy number variations (CNVs), and structural variants (SVs). Although MCL is characterized by substantial genetic heterogeneity, several genes are recurrently mutated, impacting crucial signaling pathways involved in DNA damage response, cell proliferation, cell survival, chromatin modification, telomere maintenance, and RNA regulation. Among DNA repair genes, cell cycle and survival regulators the most frequent mutated gene is ataxia telangiectasia (ATM) followed by CCND1, TP53, retinoblastoma transcriptional corepressor 1 (RB1), the proto-oncogene BHLH Transcription Factor (MYC) and dependent kinase inhibitor 2 A and 1B (CDKN2A, CDKN1B) [23–29]. Among genes involved in the epigenetic regulation, recurrent mutations have been described in histone-lysine N-methyltransferase 2D/C (KMT2D/C), the BCL6 corepressor (BCOR), Wolf–Hirschhorn syndrome candidate 1 protein (WHSC1), SWI/SNF related, matrix associated, actin dependent regulator of chromatin A4/B1 (SMARCA4 and B1), myocyte enhancer factor 2B (MEF2B), Tet methylcytosine dioxygenase 2 (TET2) [25, 30–32]. Other aberrant somatic mutations include the telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), the protein ligase ubiquitin protein ligase E3 component N-recognin 5 (UBR5) [32, 33], the protection of telomeres 1 (POT1), and the heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein H1 (HNRNPH1) gene, implicated in RNA processing [23, 34]. Common mutations are also found in genes involved in nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling: baculoviral IAP repeat containing three (BIRC3), caspase recruitment domain-containing protein 11 (CARD11), tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor-associated factor 2 (TRAF2) [35, 36]; in neurogenic locus notch homolog protein (NOTCH) signaling: NOTCH 1/2 and spen family transcriptional repressor (SPEN) [25, 37]; in BCR signaling: Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK), mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase 14 (MAP3K14) [38, 39]; Toll-like receptor signaling: Toll Like Receptor 2 (TLR2) [23, 25].

CNV and SV profiles in MCL are frequently characterized by losses of 1p22-p13, 1p32, 6q/TNFAIP3, 9p21/CDKN2A and CDKN2B, 9q, 11q22/ATM and BIRC3, 13q14/RB1, 13q33-q34, 17p/TP53, 10q21.1 and 15q14-q21.1, and gains of 3q25-q29, 7p22/CARD11, 8q24/MYC, 10p12/BMI1, 12q13/CDK4, 13q31/MIR17HG, 18q21/BCL2 [26, 33, 40, 41].

The BCR is a multimeric complex located on the surface of B lymphocytes, composed by the antigen-specific surface immunoglobulins (sIg) and the Iga/Igb hetero-dimers (CD79A, CD79B). It is crucial for normal B cell development and adaptive immunity, recognizing a variety of antigens [42]. Activation of BCR by antigen encounter induces a network of signaling events that promote proliferation, survival and differentiation of B cells. Antigen binding to sIg leads to conformational changes in the BCR complex, allowing the Src kinase Lck/yes-related protein tyrosine kinase (LYN) to phosphorylate CD79A, CD79B and spleen associated tyrosine kinase (SYK), which in turn activates other upstream kinases, including BTK, and downstream pathways, comprising calcium signaling, phospholipase C gamma 2 (PLCγ2), mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), protein kinase Cβ (PKCβ). NF‐κB signaling is activated by PLCγ2, PKCβ and CARD11, inducing recruitment of the B cell CLL/Lymphoma 10 (BCL10) and mucosa‐associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma translocation protein 1 (MALT1) into the CARD11–BCL10–MALT1 (CBM) complex, which activates IκB kinase (IKK) [43–45]. LYN also induces phosphorylation of the BCR co‐receptor CD19, which leads to phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase delta (PI3Kδ) recruitment and activation. PI3Kδ, through the second messenger phosphatidylinositol‐3,4,5‐triphosphate (PIP3), induces BTK recruitment, PLCγ2 activation, and downstream activation of the protein kinase B (AKT) and mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase (mTOR). Moreover, BTK also contributes to AKT and mTOR activation. Combined BTK‐dependent and PI3K‐dependent signaling pathways regulate B cell metabolism, proliferation, DNA repair, survival, cytoskeleton remodeling, and migration [46, 47]. Besides the BCR signaling activated in response to antigen binding, a continuous, low-strength signal is generated by BCR and is thought to be independent of antigen binding. This termed “tonic” BCR signaling, is mediated by PI3Kα and PI3Kδ pathways and provides survival signals to B cells [48]. Interestingly, B cell subsets may differ in their survival dependence on specific signaling pathways [49, 50].

The duration and intensity of the BCR signaling is tightly governed by a dynamic equilibrium between activating and inhibitory mechanisms. Negative regulation of BCR signaling involves transmembrane receptors such as CD22, CD5, CD72 and Fc gamma receptor II B (FcγRIIB). Their inhibitory function resides on the immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibition motif (ITIM) domain, which is phosphorylated by LYN upon BCR stimulation. In turn, phosphorylated ITIMs recruit phosphatases, such as Src homology 2 (SH2) domain-containing phosphatase-1 (SHP-1) or SH2 domain-containing inositol phosphatase (SHIP), thus arresting the BCR signaling [51].

The BCR is commonly maintained in malignant B cells and increasing evidence suggests that its signaling plays a relevant role in their pathogenesis (Fig. 1). The impact of BCR signaling in malignant B cells is best characterized in CLL where the number of somatic mutations in the IGHV genes distinguishes patients with unmutated IGHV (U‐CLL, with 98% or more sequence homology with the germline sequence) from those with mutated IGHV (M‐CLL, with less than 98% sequence homology). Patients with U‐CLL present with aggressive disease and shorter survival, whereas patients with M‐CLL typically have slower disease progression and longer survival [52]. These differences in clinical behavior are considered to result from higher responsiveness to BCR stimulation that promotes survival and proliferation [53–55]. In more recent years, the level of IGHV somatic mutations has been shown to identify two MCL subtypes that parallel the two main CLL subgroups [27, 41, 52]. Nodal MCL derives from mature B cells that have bypassed the germinal center and carry no or a limited number of IGHV mutations. As for U-CLLs, this form of MCL has a more aggressive clinical course. Conversely, LNN MCL derives from cells that have experienced the germinal center and carry a higher number of IGHV mutations. Similarly to the M-CLL subgroup, this MCL subtype presents an indolent clinical behavior. BCR stimulation by microbial antigens or autoantigens in tissue microenvironment during transformation can favor the development and expansion of malignant B cell clones in several B-cell malignancies [56] Fig. 1). In addition, autoantigens can stimulate polyreactive BCRs in CLL, MCL, and activated B cell-type (ABC) diffuse large B cell (DLBCL) [27, 57–59]. Overall, the use and expression of stereotyped IGHV in CLL and MCL [27, 60], although the gene used are often distinct in the two cancers [61], argues in favor of antigen-dependent BCR activation as a preeminent mechanism in CLL and MCL. Evidence that the antigen-dependent activation of BCR is crucial for proliferation and survival of malignant B cells derives from studies on CLL, DLBCL, and MCL. In CLL, it has been shown that the degree of functional response to BCR stimulation correlates with proliferation and survival [53, 62] and a higher BCR responsiveness identifies patients with a faster progression and a shorter survival [63, 64]. In MCL, BCR signaling strength correlates with tumor proliferation and identifies a subset of patients with inferior survival after cytotoxic therapy [65]. Moreover, we and others showed that a higher BCR signaling degree identifies MCL patients with a poor outcome and inversely correlates with susceptibility to BTK and SYK inhibitors [66, 67].

Antigen-dependent chronic stimulation of the BCR can be augmented by oncogenic mutations within components of the BCR or its downstream signaling pathways. Studies in CLL and MCL showed that somatic BTKC481S mutation enhances BCR activation and tumor cell proliferation [38, 68, 69]. Furthermore, Rahal et al. reported that genetic lesions in non-canonical NF-κB pathway signaling components underline activation of this pathway in ibrutinib-insensitive MCL cell lines [36]. Moreover, SOX11 promotes the activation of BCR signaling in mouse models, increasing also BCR kinase responses to external stimulation [18, 22, 70].

Multiple mechanisms underlie an antigen-independent tonic BCR signaling that supports survival of malignant B cells primarily through the PI3K–AKT–mTOR, but not NF-κB, pathway [71, 72] Fig. 1). Inactivation of the PI3K negative regulator phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) has been identified as a common mechanism in different types of lymphomas to maintain tonic BCR signaling in the absence of a functional BCR signaling. Constitutive activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, possibly due to loss of PTEN expression, has been detected in aggressive blastoid MCL variants and has been proposed to contribute to the pathogenesis of MCL [73]. Moreover, we have recently shown that higher constitutive AKT activity is associated with an inferior response to ibrutinib in MCL [66].

An active microenvironment is a defining driver of lymphoma progression and drug resistance, and MCL is no exception. Several studies were performed to decipher the composition of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and its bidirectional crosstalk with malignant B cells, thereby opening new therapeutic approaches. Saleh and colleagues recently reviewed the current knowledge on the biology of the TME in MCL [74]. In the present review, we focus on SOX11‑mediated interactions between MCL cells and the TME and on the crosstalk between immune cells and MCL cells.

SOX11 governs not only the intrinsic biology of the malignant clone but also its interaction with the surrounding microenvironment [19, 22, 75, 76]. Beyond orchestrating B‑cell differentiation, the SOX11 transcriptional program profoundly reshapes the MCL ecosystem (Fig. 2). Palomero et al. first described a SOX11–platelet‑derived growth‑factor subunit A (PDGFA) axis: SOX11 activates PDGFA transcription, thereby increasing micro‑vessel density and tumor growth, migration and angiogenesis in cell lines and xenografts [75]. Balsas et al. more recently showed that SOX11 up‑regulates C‑X‑C motif chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4) and protein tyrosine kinase 2 (PTK2), triggering ERK1/2, focal‑adhesion kinase (FAK) and PI3K/AKT signaling [76]. Collectively, these observations help to explain the biological divergence between nodal MCL and LNN disease. SOX11 also modulates the immune landscape of nodal MCL (Fig. 2). Using the NanoString PanCancer Immune Profiling Panel on FFPE lymph‑node biopsies, Balsas et al. found a “cold” TME with scant immune infiltration and downregulated MHC‑I/II expression in SOX11‑positive tumors [77]. In addition, SOX11 drives CD70 over‑expression, which correlates with CD27+ regulatory‑T‑cell infiltration, high proliferation of MCL B cells and more aggressive clinical courses [77].

SOX11 is also involved in the modulation and augmentation of BCR signaling activity. Kuo et al. showed that the overexpression of SOX11 in mice B cell leads to BCR signaling activation and development of disease that mimics human MCL [18]. Recent work by Dutta et al. identified an indirect yet critical mechanism through which SOX11 amplifies BCR signaling in MCL by transcriptionally upregulating PAX5, leading to increased expression of CD19, a key BCR co-receptor. Notably, pharmacologic inhibition of SOX11 reduced PAX5/CD19 levels, suppressed BCR signaling, and induced cell death in ibrutinib-resistant models. Targeting the SOX11–PAX5–CD19 axis thus offers a promising strategy to overcome BTK inhibitor resistance in MCL [78].

Taken together, these findings suggest that SOX11 and its downstream pathways could be potential targets that may overcome stromal-mediated resistance in MCL. A recent study reported that small SOX11 inhibitors exhibit cytotoxic activity in SOX11 positive MCL cells in combination with ibrutinib, inducing a blockage of BCR signaling and modulation of SOX11 targets [79]. In xenograft models, FAK or CXCR4 inhibitors impair SOX11-positive MCL cell engraftment in the bone marrow. Moreover, specific FAK and PI3K inhibitors reduce SOX11-mediated cell migration and stromal interactions [76].

A high‑resolution view of the MCL microenvironment has revealed pronounced heterogeneity. Bulk RNA‑seq of 41 lymph‑node samples delineated four ecotypes ranging from immune‑enriched to immune‑depleted [80]. The immune‑depleted pattern predicts BTKi resistance and poor prognosis. At the opposite extreme, an “immune‑hot” pattern—marked by checkpoint‑gene up‑regulation and limited stromal signatures—suggests susceptibility to PD‑1/PD‑L1 blockade and may partly explain the activity of lenalidomide [81]. A third, “mesenchymal” subgroup is dominated by stromal genes and pro‑tumor cytokines and frequently acquires BTKi resistance, whereas a fourth cluster resembles normal lymph‑node tissue [81]. A recent study reported an in-depth reference map of T-cells in lymph nodes from patients with DLBCL, MCL, follicular (FL) and marginal zone lymphoma (MZL). Across all entities, malignant LNs are characterized by a variable expansion of PD1-positive cytotoxic T cells lacking the transcription factor 7 (TCF7), converging into terminally exhausted T cells [82]. Such TCF7‑negative cells cannot be restored by checkpoint blockade [83, 84], an observation that explains the limited efficacy of checkpoint inhibitors in B‑cell NHL [85]. Moreover, higher proportions of exhausted TCF7-negative T cells have been associated with inferior survival in FL and DLBCL [82, 86, 87]. In MCL specifically, TIGIT/PD‑1 double‑positive T cells secrete less interferon‑γ (IFN‑γ), tumor‑necrosis‑factor‑α (TNF‑α) and interleukin‑2 (IL‑2), underscoring functional exhaustion [88].

The pivotal contribution of T cells to MCL growth and drug resistance makes them compelling therapeutic targets. However, checkpoint inhibitors as single agents have given uncertain responses in MCL, while encouraging results derive from bispecific antibodies and cellular therapies based on chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cells aiming at restoring T-cell mediated cytotoxicity [89]. Combination strategies are under investigation: vidutolimod (CMP‑001) plus pembrolizumab (NCT03983668) in R/R lymphoma; ibrutinib plus pembrolizumab (NCT03153202); and nivolumab plus lenalidomide (NCT03015896) in MCL and other lymphoma subtypes. In a patient‑derived three‑dimensional MCL model, ibrutinib plus nivolumab overturned BTKi resistance [90]. The extent to which such regimens bypass tumour‑intrinsic or niche‑dependent resistance mechanisms remains to be clarified [91].

Macrophages, although numerically fewer than T cells, exert a strong pro‑tumor influence [92]. High densities of CD163+ macrophages predict tumor progression and poor outcome independently of the MCL international prognostic index (MIPI), the marker of proliferation Ki-67 (Ki67) and TP53 status [93]. Tissues rich in CD163+ cells display elevated V‑domain immunoglobulin suppressor of T‑cell activation (VISTA) and B7 homolog 3 (B7‑H3) expression, together with macrophage survival genes that activate MAPK pathways in nearby tumor cells [94]. Conversely, MCL cells are able to reprogram monocytes into pro-tumoral macrophages via cytokines such as colony stimulating factor 1 (CSF1), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and interleukin-32 (IL-32). These macrophages enhance tumor growth, notably through B-cell activating factor (BAFF) secretion, a process regulated by NF-κB signaling. Specifically, IL-32 and BAFF are controlled by the non-canonical NF-κB pathway, whereas CSF1 and IL-10 are regulated by the BCR/NF-κB1 axis. In BTKi responders, early decreases in CSF1, IL-10, and CD163-positive monocytes suggest that BTKi disrupts pro-tumoral microenvironmental interactions [95–97]. In this light, macrophages appear to be potential targets for developing novel therapeutic approaches in MCL. Moreover, the activity of tumor immune microenvironment cells and macrophages has been suggested to be potentially mirrored by peripheral blood cell subpopulations (i.e. monocytes) and monocyte count at MCL presentation has been proposed to have a prognostic impact [98, 99].

Because proliferation and survival of malignant B lymphocytes depend on BCR signaling, kinases along this axis have become prime therapeutic targets. Over the past decades, multiple BCR‑associated kinase inhibitors (BAKi) have shown preclinical and clinical efficacy [100]. Critically, the type of BCR signaling preferentially adopted by a particular lymphoma subtype influences the sensitivity to different BAKi: tumors driven by chronic‑active signaling are highly susceptible to BTK inhibitors, whereas those relying on tonic signaling respond better to inhibition of SYK or PI3K‑AKT [101] [102]; Fig. 1). Dissecting these mechanisms helps identify suitable patients and design resistance‑escape strategies. Although inhibitors of SYK [103], BTK [104] and PI3Kδ [105, 106] have been developed, BTK‑ and PI3K‑targeted agents are the most thoroughly characterized and are discussed below.

BTK is one of the most intensively targeted kinases within the BCR cascade because of its essential role in propagating antigen‑dependent and chronic‑active signaling. Several BTKi have reshaped the therapeutic landscape of B-cell malignancies thanks to their efficacy and more favorable toxicity profile compared with chemo‑immunotherapy (reviewed in Liu et al. and Burger et al.) [107, 108] Fig. 1). Ibrutinib, the first‑in‑class covalent inhibitor, irreversibly binds Cys 481 in the BTK catalytic domain [12, 109]. It has been approved for the treatment of CLL [110, 111], MCL [12], Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia (WM) [112–114] and MZL [115] with activity confirmed in both treatment‑naïve and R/R settings [116] [117]. Beyond blocking BTK, ibrutinib suppresses AKT and ERK activation from both intrinsic and extrinsic sources [118]. It also remodels the TME: by interfering with CXCL12/CXCL13‑driven adhesion and chemotaxis, ibrutinib disrupts stromal engagement of MCL cells [119] [120]. Off‑target inhibition of interleukin‑2‑inducible T‑cell kinase (ITK) and B‑lymphocyte kinase (BLK) further modulates T‑ and B‑cell responses [121].

Second‐generation BTKi, such as acalabrutinib [122–124] and zanubrutinib [125, 126] show higher BTK selectivity and fewer off-target effects compared to ibrutinib, translating into lower adverse‑event rates [127–129]. Non-covalent inhibitors, such as pirtobrutinib, are even more effective and able to overcome resistance to ibrutinib, with fewer side effects [130, 131], although their microenvironmental impact and resistance pathways are still being defined [132]. Finally, several BTK degraders have been synthesized to overcome ibrutinib resistance, potentially targeting both the mutant and wild-type BTK, but only a limited number of clinical evidence are available for MCL patients [133, 134].

Despite the considerable clinical response to ibrutinib, resistance inevitably emerges and remains a clinical challenge [135]. Multiple mechanisms contribute to acquired resistance involving primary intrinsic mechanisms – such as gene mutations—and activation of biological processes that uncouple the tumor from its BCR addiction [31, 136, 137]. The mechanisms responsible for BTKi resistance in B-cell malignancies have been recently reviewed by Liu et al., and Nakhoda et al. [107, 138]. Herein, we focus on BTKi resistance in MCL that stems from four main intertwined biological processes. First, point mutations acquired in the BTK signaling axis—classically in BTK, PLCγ2 or CARD11—directly abrogate drug binding or create constitutively active downstream effectors [139]. The presence of mutations in BTK and/or PLCγ2 genes could lead to emerging resistance also to the second-generation BTKi [140]. In addition, MCL selects missense and truncating CCND1 lesions that stabilize CYCLIN D1 allowing G1/S transit even when BTK is blocked. A second genetic route involves loss-of-function mutations in TRAF2 and BIRC3 that induce the activation of the non-canonical NF-κB pathway bypassing the canonical BCR-NF-κB signaling, which is sensitive to BTKi, thus permitting proliferation under BTKi pressure [36]. TME provides a complementary escape mechanism. BTKi resistance is mediated by direct interactions between stromal cells and MCL cells involving the very late activation antigen 4 (VLA-4) [141]. Furthermore, stromal cells sustain MCL cell growth upon ibrutinib withdrawal, dumping suppression of PI3K–AKT signaling induced by ibrutinib [107, 142]. Dual targeting with the PI3K-α inhibitor BYL719 and ibrutinib amplifies pAKT downregulation and overrides stromal protection [142]. Functional protein re-programming supplies a third layer of resistance. Over-expression of the transcription factor MYC is associated with high levels of BTK, PLCγ2, ERK and AKT phosphorylation, conferring resistance to ibrutinib; MYC stability in this setting depends on heat shock protein 90 (HSP90), and the HSP90 inhibitor PU-H71 restores apoptosis in resistant cell lines [143, 144]. Separately, overexpression of receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 1 (ROR1)-normally silent in adult tissues-forms a ROR1/CD19 complex that lead to persistent activation of PI3K–AKT and MEK–ERK cascades independently of BCR/BTK [136]. MALT1 amplifies both canonical and non-canonical NF-κB activity and strengthens integrin/PI3K signaling; inhibition of MALT1 synergizes with BTKi in vitro, representing a promising approach to overcome ibrutinib resistance [145, 146]. Finally, the de-ubiquitinase ubiquitin-specific protease 14 (USP14) stabilizes the exportin 1 (XPO1), sequestering p53 and other tumor suppressors in the cytoplasm [147, 148]. Accordingly, Ming et al. demonstrated that inhibiting XPO1 leads to the consequent NF-κB inhibition and IκB retaining in the nuclei, pointing at this axis as possible target to overcome ibrutinib resistance [149]. Whereas sensitive MCL cells globally suppress energy metabolism after ibrutinib treatment, resistant clones retain glutaminolysis and become dependent on glutamine-fueled oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) for adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production. An autocrine loop—in which MYC up-regulates the glutamine transporter alanine-serine-cysteine transporter 2 (ASCT2) and glutaminase (GLS), and GLS-driven glutaminolysis further elevates MYC—locks cells into this dependency. Consequently, resistant cultures are hypersensitive to the GLS inhibitor BPTES or the complex-I inhibitor IACS-010759, agents that collapse mitochondrial respiration not affected by BTKi alone [150, 151].

PI3K activation has an important role in tumor cell biology, and a great effort has been performed to target PI3K and/or downstream kinases such as AKT and mTOR (reviewed in Fruman et al., He et al. and Yang et al.; [152–154]. Outside CLL, however, single‑agent activity at tolerable doses has been modest. Idelalisib, a selective inhibitor of the PI3Kδ isoform, successfully abrogates PI3K downstream pathway, blocking CLL cell proliferation, survival, migration and homing and influencing the interaction between tumor cells and microenvironment [155]. Furthermore, idelalisib induces an increased expression of apoptosis markers in DLBCL, FL, and MCL cell lines [156] and shows preclinical activity alone or in combinations across B‑cell malignancies [157]. Although idelalisib induces a good overall response rate in R/R CLL patients [158], it is currently used for R/R CLL only if other options are unavailable or considered unsuitable for the patient, due to its side effects, particularly of infectious nature [159]. Given the pronounced PI3K/AKT activation in MCL, this pathway would represent a good therapeutic target [73]. Although a phase 1 study showed an overall response rate of 40% with an acceptable safety profile in 40 R/R MCL patients [160], idelalisib alone or in combination with other agents that target this pathway, i.e. mTOR inhibitors, has not entered clinical practice for MCL.

A distinguishing feature of lymph node resident MCL cells is the expression of PI3K gamma (PI3Kγ), a PI3K isoform that is not highly expressed in other B cells or B-cell malignancies. Duvelisib, a dual PI3Kδ/γ inhibitor, has a greater effect than PI3Kδ- and PI3Kγ-selective inhibitors in blocking the proliferation of primary MCL cells and MCL cell lines, and is critical for migration of primary MCL cells and cell lines [161]. Parsaclisib is a potent and highly selective PI3Kδ inhibitor that has demonstrated meaningful clinical benefits in MCL patients but has shown limited clinical benefits in patients who had previously failed BTKi treatment [162].

MCL is a biologically and clinically heterogeneous disease shaped by the convergence of intrinsic genetic lesions and extrinsic microenvironmental cues. Central to its pathogenesis, which is driven by the founding t(11;14) translocation and other secondary mutations, are the aberrant activation of BCR signaling and the complex and dynamic interactions between malignant B cells and their microenvironment. Stromal support, immune evasion, and metabolic reprogramming collectively contribute to disease progression, therapeutic resistance, and clonal evolution.

The therapeutic landscape of MCL has been transformed by the development of targeted agents—including covalent and non-covalent BTK inhibitors, PI3Kδ/γ antagonists, bispecific antibodies, and CAR-T cells—which have validated BCR signaling and microenvironmental interactions as clinically actionable vulnerabilities. However, the emergence of resistance to these agents highlights the limitations of monotherapy and underscores the need for more sophisticated, mechanism-driven treatment strategies.

Moving forward, therapeutic success will depend on the integration of functional biomarkers that reflect tumor biology in real time, enabling more precise patient stratification and therapy selection. Comprehensive profiling of the tumor microenvironment—particularly stromal and macrophage niches—and its interplay with malignant cells will be critical to identify targets that can disrupt protective compartments or convert them into therapeutic allies. In parallel, longitudinal monitoring of patients during therapy will allow early detection of resistance mechanisms and dynamic adaptation of treatment regimens.

In summary, the intertwined nature of BCR signaling and microenvironmental support is both a challenge and an opportunity in MCL therapy. By embracing this complexity through rational combination strategies, functional precision medicine, and dynamic disease monitoring, it may be possible to move beyond disease control and toward curative approaches for patients with this historically difficult-to-treat lymphoma.
