Prolonged fever accompanied by systemic inflammation remains one of the most challenging diagnostic problems in internal medicine and rheumatology. Among the autoinflammatory disorders presenting as fever of unknown origin (FUO), adult‐onset Still's disease (AOSD) represents a rare but important diagnostic consideration owing to its heterogeneous clinical manifestations, lack of specific diagnostic biomarkers, and potential for severe complications if diagnosis is delayed [1, 2].

AOSD is a systemic inflammatory disorder classically characterized by quotidian high‐spiking fevers, inflammatory arthritis or arthralgia, evanescent salmon‐colored rash, leukocytosis with neutrophilia, and marked hyperferritinemia [3]. However, not all patients present with this complete constellation of features. Wissler–Fanconi syndrome (also called subsepsis allergica or subseptic Still's disease) is characterized predominantly by prolonged spiking fevers and migratory inflammatory arthritis but notably lacks the typical salmon‐colored rash [4, 5, 6]. First described by Wissler in 1943 and later by Fanconi in 1946, this phenotype represents an uncommon and diagnostically challenging variant within the AOSD spectrum. Such incomplete or atypical presentations substantially increase the risk of misdiagnosis as infection, malignancy, or other systemic inflammatory disorders, particularly in settings where AOSD awareness is limited.

The diagnosis of AOSD, including its Wissler–Fanconi variant, remains fundamentally one of exclusion. The Yamaguchi classification criteria, which emphasize fever pattern, duration of arthralgia, neutrophilic leukocytosis, and negative autoantibodies, remain the most sensitive diagnostic tool in clinical practice [3]. Early recognition of AOSD is clinically crucial, as timely initiation of immunomodulatory therapy often leads to rapid symptom resolution and may prevent long‐term sequelae such as chronic inflammatory arthritis or the development of macrophage activation syndrome, a potentially life‐threatening complication [2, 5]. Awareness of atypical phenotypes such as Wissler–Fanconi syndrome is therefore essential, particularly in young adults presenting with FUO and inflammatory arthritis.

We report the case of a young adult male who presented with several weeks of high‐grade quotidian fever and migratory inflammatory arthritis, accompanied by markedly elevated inflammatory markers and extreme hyperferritinemia. Extensive evaluation excluded infectious, malignant, and autoimmune causes, and the patient fulfilled the Yamaguchi classification criteria for AOSD. The clinical presentation and disease course were consistent with Wissler–Fanconi syndrome, an atypical subseptic variant of AOSD.

A 22‐year‐old previously healthy man presented with a three‐week history of persistent high‐grade fever and progressive joint pain. The fever was daily, sudden in onset, and documented up to 40°C (104°F), frequently accompanied by chills and rigors, with no clear diurnal variation. There was no associated cough, sore throat, urinary symptoms, gastrointestinal complaints, or localizing features suggestive of a focal infection.

The febrile illness was accompanied by migratory inflammatory joint symptoms. He initially developed pain and swelling of the right knee with restriction of movement, followed by involvement of the left wrist in an additive pattern. The joint pain was partially relieved with non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs but recurred and progressed, with subsequent involvement of the left knee. At the time of presentation, symptoms in the right knee and left wrist had largely subsided, while the left knee remained painful and swollen.

There was no associated rash, sore throat, oral or genital ulcers, ocular redness, urethral discharge, or constitutional symptoms such as weight loss or night sweats. There was no past history of chronic kidney disease, gout, or connective tissue disease, and no relevant family history. He had no recent travel, animal exposure, or known contact with tuberculosis, and was not taking any regular medications, including corticosteroids or immunosuppressive agents.

On physical examination, the patient appeared acutely unwell and was febrile with a temperature of 40°C, pulse rate of 102 beats per minute, and blood pressure of 116/70 mmHg. There was no pallor, icterus, lymphadenopathy, or peripheral edema. Skin examination revealed no rash, including no evanescent or salmon‐colored eruptions.

Musculoskeletal examination demonstrated a swollen, warm, and tender left knee with restricted range of motion and no crepitus. Examination of the right knee, wrists, elbows, and small joints of the hands was unremarkable at the time of assessment. There was no clinical evidence of synovitis in other joints. Sacroiliac joint stress tests, including the FABER test, were negative bilaterally, and spinal mobility was preserved, with a normal Schober test.

Cardiovascular, respiratory, abdominal, and neurological examinations were unremarkable, with no organomegaly or signs of systemic involvement.

Given the patient's presentation with prolonged high‐grade fever and inflammatory arthritis, a broad differential diagnosis was initially considered. Infectious causes including tuberculosis, bacterial endocarditis, brucellosis, malaria, viral hepatitis, and human immunodeficiency virus infection were prioritized, given the systemic inflammatory features. Malignancy, particularly hematologic malignancies, was also considered in view of persistent fever and leukocytosis.

Autoimmune and inflammatory conditions were systematically evaluated, including systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, spondyloarthritis, reactive arthritis, rheumatic fever, and vasculitis. The absence of inflammatory back pain, morning stiffness, psoriasis, dactylitis, enthesitis, or features of inflammatory bowel disease argued against seronegative spondyloarthropathies. Additionally, there was no history of preceding infection or urogenital/gastrointestinal symptoms to suggest reactive arthritis. Crystal‐induced arthritis, including gout and pseudogout, was considered less likely due to the absence of characteristic clinical features and risk factors. There were no clinical features suggestive of inflammatory bowel disease–associated arthritis or connective tissue disease.

The absence of rash, sore throat, or lymphadenopathy initially obscured consideration of AOSD, particularly its atypical presentations.

Initial laboratory investigations revealed marked systemic inflammation, with neutrophilic leukocytosis and significantly elevated acute‐phase reactants. Liver function tests showed a hepatocellular pattern of transaminase elevation, while renal function and serum electrolytes were within normal limits. Autoimmune serology, including antinuclear antibodies and rheumatoid factor, was negative. A comprehensive infectious workup, including blood cultures and serologic testing for common bacterial, mycobacterial, and viral pathogens, was unremarkable.

Radiologic evaluation was performed to assess for occult infection, inflammatory arthropathy, or structural joint disease. Chest radiography showed no evidence of pulmonary infection or malignancy (Figure 1). Plain radiographs of the sacroiliac joints were normal, with no features of sacroiliitis (Figure 2). X‐ray imaging of the knee demonstrated preserved joint spaces and no erosive or degenerative changes (Figure 3).

Given persistent fever, inflammatory arthritis, negative infectious and autoimmune studies, and markedly elevated inflammatory markers, serum ferritin was measured and found to be profoundly elevated, supporting consideration of AOSD. Following exclusion of infectious and neoplastic etiologies and establishment of a working diagnosis of AOSD with a Wissler–Fanconi phenotype, the patient was initiated on high‐dose systemic corticosteroid therapy with oral prednisolone at a dose of 1 mg/kg/day. This decision was based on the severity of systemic symptoms, marked inflammatory response, and absence of contraindications to immunosuppression.

Following initiation of high‐dose oral prednisolone at 1 mg/kg/day, the patient demonstrated a rapid clinical response. The fever resolved completely within 72 h of starting corticosteroid therapy, and there was marked improvement in joint pain and swelling. Over the subsequent days, the patient became systemically well and was able to mobilize without significant discomfort.

Serial laboratory monitoring showed a progressive decline in inflammatory markers, with improvement in leukocytosis and normalization of liver transaminases over the first week of treatment. Given the favorable clinical response and to facilitate corticosteroid tapering, methotrexate was introduced at a dose of 15 mg once weekly, along with folic acid supplementation, as a steroid‐sparing disease‐modifying agent. The choice of methotrexate over biologic agents such as IL‐1 or IL‐6 inhibitors was guided by several factors: the patient's favorable initial response to corticosteroids suggesting less aggressive disease, the unavailability and prohibitive cost of biologic therapies in our resource‐limited setting, and the well‐established efficacy and safety profile of methotrexate as first‐line steroid‐sparing therapy in AOSD [7].

A gradual taper of oral corticosteroids was initiated after 1 week, guided by clinical response and laboratory parameters. At three‐month follow‐up, the patient remained afebrile and asymptomatic, with no recurrence of joint symptoms. Inflammatory markers and serum ferritin levels had normalized, and liver function tests were within reference ranges. No features suggestive of macrophage activation syndrome or other systemic complications were observed during follow‐up.

The patient continues to be followed regularly in the rheumatology clinic and remains clinically stable on methotrexate therapy.

AOSD is a rare systemic autoinflammatory disorder with a highly variable clinical presentation, often posing significant diagnostic challenges. Although classically defined by the triad of quotidian fever, inflammatory arthritis, and evanescent rash, incomplete or atypical presentations are increasingly recognized and may account for substantial diagnostic delay [2, 5]. The present case illustrates Wissler–Fanconi syndrome, an uncommon subseptic phenotype of AOSD, characterized predominantly by prolonged fever and inflammatory arthritis in the absence of the typical rash.

Several recent case reports have described similar atypical presentations of AOSD presenting as fever of unknown origin, particularly in young adults. Cases reported from Asia and the Middle East frequently describe prolonged diagnostic pathways, with extensive infectious workups preceding consideration of autoinflammatory disease [8, 9]. In keeping with these reports, our patient presented with high‐grade quotidian fever and migratory inflammatory arthritis, without rash, sore throat, or lymphadenopathy, features that are absent in up to 20%–30% of patients with AOSD [1, 10].

Marked hyperferritinemia, a hallmark of AOSD, played a pivotal role in establishing the diagnosis in this case. Ferritin levels exceeding 10,000 ng/mL have been repeatedly associated with active disease and are particularly useful in distinguishing AOSD from infectious or malignant causes of FUO [2, 11]. Similar reliance on ferritin and hematological markers (esp. mean platelet volume) as a diagnostic discriminator has been described in studies, especially where access to advanced immunologic or imaging modalities is limited [12, 13].

The diagnostic process in this case was further shaped by the realities of a resource‐limited healthcare setting in Pakistan, where infectious diseases such as tuberculosis, brucellosis, and enteric fever are endemic and appropriately prioritized in the differential diagnosis of prolonged fever [14, 15]. This epidemiological context necessitates exhaustive exclusion of infectious causes before immunosuppressive therapy can be safely initiated [16]. As described in other studies from low‐ and middle‐income countries, such settings often experience delayed recognition of AOSD due to limited awareness of rare autoinflammatory diseases and restricted access to specialized tests [9, 10, 14].

Despite these constraints, this case demonstrates that careful clinical assessment combined with judicious use of accessible investigations, including ferritin measurement and plain radiography, can allow accurate diagnosis. The Yamaguchi criteria, which rely largely on clinical and routine laboratory parameters, remain particularly valuable in such contexts and were instrumental in guiding diagnosis in our patient [3].

Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of initial therapy for AOSD, with reported response rates of 60%–70% in systemic disease [5]. The rapid defervescence and clinical improvement observed in our patient within 72 h of initiating high‐dose corticosteroids is consistent with outcomes reported in multiple contemporary case series [8, 9, 12, 14, 17]. Early introduction of methotrexate as a steroid‐sparing agent aligns with current recommendations and has been shown to reduce relapse rates and long‐term steroid exposure [1].

While biologic therapies targeting IL‐1 and IL‐6 have transformed the management of refractory AOSD in high‐resource settings, their availability and cost remain prohibitive in many low‐ and middle‐income countries [10, 18]. In this context, conventional disease‐modifying antirheumatic drugs continue to play a central role [19]. Our case supports existing evidence that favorable outcomes can still be achieved with early diagnosis and timely initiation of conventional immunosuppressive therapy, even in the absence of biologics.

Long‐term management of AOSD requires vigilant monitoring for potential complications and disease evolution. Macrophage activation syndrome, a life‐threatening complication characterized by sudden deterioration with high fever, cytopenias, hepatic dysfunction, coagulopathy, and hyperferritinemia, occurs in approximately 10%–15% of AOSD patients and necessitates prompt recognition and aggressive treatment [20]. Additionally, evolution to chronic articular disease occurs in a significant proportion of patients, with some developing destructive polyarthritis requiring escalation of disease‐modifying therapy [21]. Regular clinical assessment and serial monitoring of inflammatory markers, ferritin levels, and joint examination remain essential components of long‐term follow‐up to guide therapeutic decisions and prevent complications.

Several limitations of this case report merit acknowledgment. Advanced imaging modalities such as MRI or PET‐CT, which can provide additional diagnostic information in evaluating FUO and detecting subclinical inflammation, were not available in our setting. Similarly, glycosylated ferritin measurement, which has shown higher specificity for AOSD compared to total ferritin, could not be performed due to resource constraints. Cytokine profiling (IL‐1β, IL‐6, IL‐18) and assessment for HLA‐B27, HLA‐DRB1, or other genetic markers that may contribute to disease pathogenesis and phenotypic expression were also not accessible. Furthermore, we did not perform a bone marrow examination to definitively exclude occult hematologic malignancy, though the clinical course and response to therapy argued strongly against this diagnosis. Despite these limitations, our case demonstrates that AOSD can be accurately diagnosed and effectively managed using careful clinical assessment and judicious use of available laboratory and radiographic investigations, an important consideration for clinicians practicing in resource‐limited settings.

This case adds to the growing body of literature emphasizing that AOSD should remain a diagnostic consideration in young adults presenting with FUO and inflammatory arthritis, even when classical features are absent. Recognition of Wissler–Fanconi syndrome is particularly important, as its atypical presentation may mimic infection or malignancy and lead to unnecessary investigations and delays in treatment. In regions where infectious diseases are prevalent and healthcare resources are constrained, heightened awareness of this entity may facilitate earlier diagnosis and improve patient outcomes.
