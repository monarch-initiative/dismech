# Odevixibat treatment for recurrent cholestasis in a patient with biallelic UNC45A variants causing osteo-oto-hepato-enteric syndrome.
**Authors:** Imhann F, Zhou Z, Malcolm CFS, ‘Support and Supervision Team’
**Journal:** JHEP Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.jhepr.2025.101710](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhepr.2025.101710)

## Content

To the Editor:

With great interest, we read the recent publication by Di Giorgio et al. covering odevixibat treatments for cholestatic pruritus.1 Here, we wish to report a case of osteo-oto-hepatic-enteric syndrome (O2HE), successfully treated with odevixibat. O2HE is a rare, difficult to diagnose syndrome characterized by congenital cholestatic pruritus, diarrhea, sensorineural hearing loss and/or bone fragility. O2HE was first described in 2018 in association with UNC45A gene variants.2 There is no cure or effective treatment, and the pathogenic mechanisms are not known.3

We report on a 33-year-old male patient who has had episodes of unexplained severe pruritus, diarrhea and weight loss throughout his life. Within 10 days after birth, he developed jaundice and completely white-colored diarrhea. Blood results at day 28 indicated a low gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT) cholestasis: aspartate aminotransferase 35 U/L (reference value (ref): 0−30), alanine aminotransferase 30 U/L (ref: 0−30), alkaline phosphatase 902 U/L (ref: 100−250), GGT 27 U/L (ref: 0−40), total bilirubin 175 μmol/L (ref: 1−15) and direct bilirubin 137 μmol/L (ref: 0−5). A liver biopsy at day 32 showed severe histological cholestasis with enlarged portal fields and bile duct proliferation. Surgical exploration of the bile ducts revealed patent bile ducts and therefore no biliary atresia. At age 2, the patient was diagnosed with benign recurrent intrahepatic cholestasis based on the recurrent episodes of jaundice, elevated serum bile acids and pruritus since birth. He has been treated with cholestyramine and simvastatin and later with rifampicin, which resulted in partial relief of the pruritus and did not change episode frequency. Blood results were normal between episodes. The patient also continued to experience loose stools up to six times a day, accompanied by multiple hospital admissions due to dehydration and weight loss. Endoscopic examinations revealed idiopathic villous atrophy. At age 10, the patient experienced a profound increase in pruritus and cholestasis, with bilirubin levels up to 270 μmol/L for a sustained period of 7 months. A new liver biopsy showed normal architecture of the liver parenchyma, with canalicular cholestasis and bile pigments in the cytoplasm of the hepatocytes. At age 18, the patient transitioned from pediatric to adult gastroenterology and hepatology care. Treatment with cholestyramine, rifampicin and oral nutritional supplements including fat-soluble vitamins remained unchanged. Episodes of jaundice, pruritus, diarrhea and weight loss continued to occur 2−3 times a year on average, lasting several weeks before partly resolving. Molecular genetics at age 20 revealed no pathogenic variants in the ABCB11 and ATP8B1 genes. At ages 14 and 25, DEXA-scans showed osteoporosis. Zoledronic acid and calcium/vitamin D supplements improved bone mineral density of the lumbar spine but less so for the proximal femur.

At age 30, whole-exome sequencing was performed. An analysis of the exomes of the 116 genes in our cholestasis and liver failure panel revealed compound heterozygous variants in the UNC45A gene: a maternally-derived missense variant, c.292C>T p.(Arg98Trp), that has been previously reported in another patient with O2HE,4 and a paternally-derived novel variant leading to a premature stop codon, c.592C>T p.(Gln198∗). Both variants have been reported as heterozygous variants in the genetic population database gnoMAD. In agreement with the association of biallelic UNC45A variants with O2HE, moderate bilateral sensorineural hearing loss was diagnosed in our patient by the ear, nose, and throat specialist, for which he received a hearing aid. Immunofluorescence microscopy of the liver specimen archived at age 10 showed reduced expression of the UNC45A protein and reduced expression and aberrant localization of the bile salt export pump (BSEP) in hepatocytes (Fig. 1A). All prior treatments for his pruritus were insufficiently effective. We considered ursodeoxycholic acid, but did not start this drug because of the phenotype of concomitant diarrhea and the very limited effects reported for ursodeoxycholic acid in low GGT cholestasis. We did not consider using bezafibrate because it is primarily used for cholestatic pruritus and would not be expected to treat diarrhea. Instead, we decided to treat the patient with the ileal bile acid transporter (IBAT) antagonist odevixibat. Odevixibat has been shown to decrease pruritus and serum bile acids in patients with cholestatic Alagille syndrome, ATP8B1 deficiency and BSEP deficiency,5,6 and odevixibat received regulatory approval from the European Medicines Agency and the US Food and Drug Administration in 2021.7 Odevixibat for our patient was obtained through compassionate use from Albireo Pharma Inc (now Ipsen). At age 31, odevixibat treatment (38.5 μg/kg/day) was started once the patient’s serum bile acids and bilirubin levels had returned to normal following an episode. The patient was monitored using standard liver laboratory tests, serum bile acid analyses and regular qualitative assessment of complaints (Fig. 1C-F). Since the start of odevixibat treatment in 2023, the patient has not experienced any new episode of pruritus or weight loss (follow-up: 2.5 years). Laboratory tests showed transient moderately elevated levels of serum aminotransferases (Fig. 1C), a side effect previously associated with odevixibat use.8 Bilirubin and total bile acid levels remained low (Fig.1D). The cholic acid-to-chenodeoxycholic acid (CA/CDCA) ratio did not return to normal levels (ref >0.6) after odevixibat treatment (Fig. 1E). The glycine-to-taurine conjugated bile acid (G/T) ratio was transiently reduced after the start of odevixibat treatment (Fig. 1F).

To date, 14 patients with O2HE have been reported in the medical literature (source: MEDLINE), including our patient. While O2HE remains rare in the literature, the presence of pathogenic UNC45A variants in population databases suggests that O2HE may be underdiagnosed. A review of all case reports revealed that, while not all patients experience all O2HE-associated symptoms, cholestatic pruritus emerges as the most consistent clinical feature across cases (Fig. 1B). The strong association between UNC45A variants and cholestatic pruritus is supported by observations from patients diagnosed with Aegenaes syndrome, which is characterized by intense cholestatic pruritus and lower limb edemas and biallelic UNC45A variants with at least one obligatory UNC45A variant in the 5’ untranslated region.9 Our study highlights the need to continue molecular testing in cases of idiopathic cholestasis. It also demonstrates the broader applicability of odevixibat for treating cholestatic pruritus with various underlying genetic etiologies, including prevention of new episodes of UNC45A-associated pruritus. While long-term follow-up and treatment of more patients is awaited, our results indicate that the poorly understood O2HE-associated pruritus can be placed among other odevixibat-responsive cholestatic liver diseases, pointing to a shared disease mechanism involving portal and/or systemic bile acid-mediated toxicity or pruritogenic pathways.
