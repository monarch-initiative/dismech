# Irreversible Ocular and Systemic Damage in ROSAH Syndrome.
**Authors:** Fabiani C, Caggiano V, Sbalchiero J, Gaggiano C, Cardamone C, De Bernardo M, Giudice V, Moscato P, Rosa N, Lopalco G, Cantarini L, Vitale A
**Journal:** Ophthalmol Ther (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s40123-026-01356-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40123-026-01356-7)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: This study aimed to describe the clinical, genetic, and pathophysiologic features of four Italian patients from two unrelated families with retinal dystrophy, optic nerve edema, splenomegaly, anhidrosis, and headache (ROSAH) syndrome, highlighting novel ocular and systemic manifestations and therapeutic considerations.

METHODS: We report detailed phenotypic, ophthalmologic, and systemic findings in four genetically confirmed cases (eight eyes). Special attention was given to ocular and systemic manifestations and complications, inflammatory markers, systemic complications, and treatment responses.

RESULTS: All eyes exhibited bilateral optic disc and peripapillary involvement, cone-rod retinal dystrophy confirmed by full-field electroretinography and multimodal retinal imaging, low-grade uveitis and retinal vasculitis. Novel findings included keratoconus in one patient and dry eye in all patients. Acute angle-closure glaucoma was also observed in four eyes (two patients) with short axial lengths. Systemically, in addition to the previously described features of fever, splenomegaly, headache, dental anomalies, and anhidrosis, we report the first case of reactive amyloidosis in ROSAH syndrome, leading to severe renal failure and dialysis dependence. Splenectomy resolved recurrent fever in two patients. Conventional immunosuppressants were largely ineffective, whereas interleukin-6 inhibition provided systemic benefit but limited ocular improvement.

CONCLUSIONS: ROSAH syndrome presents with distinctive ocular features, and recognition of ocular surface, corneal and anterior segment anomalies further broadens its phenotypic spectrum. Reactive amyloidosis is a possible complication of ROSAH syndrome, as in other autoinflammatory disorders. Early diagnosis is critical to prevent irreversible ocular and extraocular organ damage, and to guide timely, targeted therapeutic interventions.


Why carry out this study?Retinal dystrophy, optic nerve edema, splenomegaly, anhidrosis, and headache (ROSAH) syndrome is a rare autosomal dominant autoinflammatory disease caused by gain-of-function alpha-kinase 1 (ALPK1) variants, characterized by early optic disc involvement, retinal degeneration, intraocular inflammation, and systemic manifestations, but its full clinical spectrum and long-term course remain incompletely defined.Additional ophthalmic features, including dry eye disease, keratoconus, and angle-closure glaucoma in eyes with short axial length, as well as rare systemic complications, namely amyloidosis leading to kidney failure, have been reported, and responses to interleukin (IL)-6 inhibition have been described, indicating an unmet need for better phenotypic characterization and targeted treatment strategies.This case series aimed to further delineate the ocular and systemic phenotype of ROSAH syndrome, providing data supporting that ALPK1 gain-of-function variants are associated with a broader and more severe clinical spectrum than previously recognized.What was learned from the study?The study expands the ROSAH phenotype by identifying novel anterior segment and ocular surface abnormalities and by reporting the first observed case of reactive AA amyloidosis with severe renal involvement.Ocular inflammation and retinal degeneration may progress independently, and visual recovery may be limited despite treatment.Findings from this study support the need for early diagnosis, multidisciplinary monitoring, and development of more targeted therapies to reduce the risk of irreversible visual and systemic damage.

ROSAH syndrome (retinal dystrophy, optic nerve edema, splenomegaly, anhidrosis, and headache) is a rare autosomal dominant disorder caused by pathogenic gain-of-function variants in the alpha-kinase 1 (ALPK1) gene, most commonly p.Thr237Met, but also p.Tyr254Cys, p.Ser277Phe, and p.Thr237Ala [1–3]. Some variants are associated with fully syndromic phenotypes, whereas others present predominantly with ocular manifestations [4].

Increasing evidence supports the classification of ROSAH syndrome as an autoinflammatory disease, belonging to the expanding group of “relopathies”, resulting from dysregulation of nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling. In affected individuals, interferon-γ (IFNγ) amplifies this pathway, enhancing NF-κB-dependent cytokine release, including interleukin (IL)-6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and IL-1β, thereby promoting chronic, low-grade inflammation that contributes to neuroinflammation and organ damage [5, 6].

Ocular involvement represents the earliest and most distinctive manifestation of ROSAH syndrome, driven by both genetic and inflammatory mechanisms. ALPK1 localizes to the photoreceptor connecting cilium, retinal pigment epithelium, and centrosomes. Fibroblasts derived from affected individuals exhibit defective ciliogenesis, and microglial infiltration of the outer retina has been observed in animal models, suggesting that ALPK1 dysfunction contributes to retinal degeneration through structural and inflammatory pathways [1]. Clinically, ROSAH syndrome typically presents in infancy or adolescence with bilateral optic disc elevation progressing to atrophy, followed by progressive cone-rod dystrophy (CORD), low-grade uveitis, and retinal vasculitis [7].

However, extraocular involvement, including splenomegaly, periodic fever, arthritis, anhidrosis, dental anomalies (such as abnormal tooth morphology, delayed eruption, caries, or enamel defects), and skeletal abnormalities, further underscores the potential multisystemic nature of the disorder [1, 5, 8]. To date, fewer than 80 cases of ROSAH syndrome have been reported in the literature.

The objective of this study is to describe four Italian patients from two distinct families affected by ROSAH syndrome and to discuss their clinical, genetic, and pathophysiologic features in the context of the current understanding of the disease.

We present four patients (eight eyes) with genetically confirmed ROSAH syndrome, illustrating the systemic and ocFlar manifestations of the disease and its potential complications. Three patients (cases 1, 2, and 3) belonged to the same family (mother and two sons), while one patient (case 4) represented the first identified case in a separate family.

Inclusion criteria consisted of the identification of a pathogenic ALPK1 mutation along with systemic and ophthalmologic clinical features supporting the diagnosis of ROSAH syndrome. In this specific context, apart from the absence of signed informed consent, no specific exclusion criteria were established. Genetic testing by whole exome sequencing (WES) leading to the identification of ALPK1 mutation was performed at multiple centers; in silico algorithms were employed to assess their pathogenicity. Of note, WES was performed at accredited diagnostic laboratories using validated clinical WES pipelines. Although sequencing platforms and bioinformatic pipelines differed across centers, all analyses included standard steps of read alignment, variant calling, annotation, and clinical classification according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria [9]. Variant pathogenicity was supported by commonly adopted in silico prediction tools, such as Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant (SIFT), Polymorphism Phenotyping v2 (PolyPhen-2), and Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD), as implemented in the local diagnostic pipelines.

The patients were followed at the multidisciplinary European Reference Network for Rare Immunodeficiency, Autoinflammatory and Autoimmune Diseases (ERN-RITA) center of the University Hospital of Siena, Italy. Patients underwent ophthalmologic evaluations at least every 3–6 months, or more frequently when clinically indicated in the case of disease flares. Evaluations included laboratory tests and instrumental examinations, tailored to the patients’ clinical presentation.

Comprehensive ophthalmic assessments were conducted, including best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), ocular surface and anterior chamber assessment, tonometry, fundus photography (FP), biometry, electroretinography (ERG), optical coherence tomography (OCT), fluorescein angiography (FA), fundus autofluorescence (FAF), and non-invasive break-up time (niBUT).

N. 14951; NCT05200715) approved the study, which was performed according to the Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants for both study participation and the publication of any potentially identifying information. The study protocol was also approved by the Ethics Committees of the other two participating centers, namely the University of Bari and the University of Salerno. Clinical data are kept in accordance with the EU General Data Protection Regulations (GDPR), or other counterparts, on the processing of personal data and the protection of privacy (2016/679/EU).

All patients reported headache and anhidrosis since late adolescence. In cases 1, 2, and 3, a dermoscopic study performed during follow-up revealed complete sclerosis of the axillary sweat glands. One patient (case 1) developed reactive amyloidosis involving both the kidneys and the gastrointestinal tract. Renal involvement resulted in kidney failure, necessitating dialysis.

All patients experienced splenomegaly and episodes of fever during the course of the disease. In two cases (cases 2 and 3), splenectomy resulted in resolution of febrile episodes, whereas in case 1 and 4, treatment with tocilizumab led to resolution of fever. Three patients (cases 1, 2, and 4) received treatment with tocilizumab during the last year, whereas case 3, the mother, declined treatment due to already severe and irreversible visual impairment concomitant with normal inflammatory markers, and a clinically stable systemic condition over several years. Tocilizumab led to normalization of acute phase reactants and resolution of headache and arthralgia in all cases under treatment. In a patient (case 2) with low-grade anterior chamber intraocular inflammation (cells +) and tiny endothelial precipitates (Fig. 4, panel E), tocilizumab treatment resulted in long-term remission of uveitis. Regarding the posterior segment, case 1 showed persistent areas of optic disc and perivascular leakage in the posterior pole and mid-periphery on FA (Fig. 1, panel G and H), indicating ongoing retinal vasculitis activity. None of these patients showed evidence of intraretinal edema on OCT or progression of retinal dystrophy on autofluorescence imaging. Cases 1, 2, and 4 reported subjective improvement in vision that was not accompanied by objective improvement in BCVA, but was associated with a slight reduction in retinal thickness on OCT.

The ocular phenotype remains the most distinctive and diagnostic hallmark of ROSAH syndrome, placing it within the group of monogenic uveitis entities associated with autoinflammatory diseases, as highlighted in the recent International Uveitis Study Group (IUSG)/AutoInflammatory Disease Alliance (AIDA) Network survey on monogenic uveitis [7, 10].

Notably, in our cohort considerable variability in disease severity and age at onset is observed, even among affected members of the same family, consistent with previously reported studies. Such discrepancies likely reflect a combination of genetic factors and possibly environmental or inflammatory triggers influencing disease penetrance and severity.

Regarding ocular involvement, while optic disc elevation is the most common and earliest ocular sign, it is typically asymptomatic in the initial years and may go unnoticed without detailed ophthalmologic assessment [1, 7]. Patient-reported visual symptoms generally arise during the adolescence or even later when progressive CORD and ocular inflammation coexist, as observed in our cohort [7]. Recognizing that optic disc elevation may remain asymptomatic and therefore easily overlooked, its detection remains crucial for achieving an early diagnosis. Given the autosomal dominant inheritance, first-degree relatives should be screened; however, ROSAH syndrome should also be considered in pediatric patients with characteristic optic disc elevation even in the absence of family history, due to the possibility of de novo mutations, as observed in one patient from our cohort.

Up to half of patients affected with ROSAH syndrome may report headache, making a thorough evaluation to exclude intracranial hypertension essential before attributing optic disc elevation to less common etiologies. Notably, in our cohort and prior reports, optic disc elevation in ROSAH syndrome lacks the typical intense fluorescein leakage characteristic of true optic disc inflammation, a finding observed in other inherited optic neuropathies such as Leber hereditary optic neuropathy, or lysosomal storage disorders. Unlike mucopolysaccharidoses, where optic disc edema is frequently associated with intracranial hypertension, optic disc elevation in ROSAH syndrome occurs independently of raised intracranial pressure, despite headache being a frequent symptom, likely mediated by alternative mechanisms. Several non-mutually exclusive mechanisms may be hypothesized, including primary central nervous system involvement, ALPK1 accumulation, retinal ganglion cell dysfunction, and axoplasmic flow disruption [7]. Therefore, a comprehensive ophthalmologic and systemic assessment is recommended in at-risk children to allow timely monitoring and intervention before irreversible retinal degeneration occurs.

On the other hand, in our cohort, the oldest patient with the longest disease duration exhibited a stable clinical course despite long-term discontinuation of immunosuppressive therapy, with no evidence of active ocular or systemic inflammation, complete symptom resolution, and normalization of inflammatory markers. However, ocular involvement progressively evolved into diffuse retinal degeneration and bilateral optic disc atrophy with severe visual impairment, underscoring the need for longitudinal studies to clarify the inflammatory and degenerative components and the natural history of ROSAH syndrome.

Beyond the classic features, less common ocular manifestations are beginning to emerge. In our case series, one patient had bilateral keratoconus raising the hypothesis that ALPK1 dysregulation might influence corneal stromal remodeling and structural integrity during ocular development, though a direct mechanistic link remains unproven. Two related patients developed angle-closure glaucoma with bilaterally short axial lengths, and three additional cases have been reported [7, 11], supporting a role for ALPK1 in ocular growth and anterior segment development and underscoring the need for thorough ophthalmologic evaluation in ROSAH syndrome.

The association between ALPK1 mutations and impaired sweating appears biologically plausible, given the high expression of ALPK1 in sweat gland myoepithelial cells [1]. Consistent with this, all patients with ROSAH syndrome in our series exhibited complete sclerosis of the sweat glands. In addition, all patients had dry eye, potentially due to lacrimal and meibomian glands dysfunction. Further studies are needed to better characterize the structure and function of these glands, as has already been demonstrated for the sweat glands. Notably, ocular surface inflammation with blepharitis and dry eye has been reported in other autoinflammatory conditions, such as cryopyrin-associated periodic syndrome (CAPS) and VEXAS (vacuoles, E1 enzyme, X-linked, autoinflammatory, somatic) syndrome, suggesting that ocular surface involvement may be a common feature in this group of disorders [12–16].

Febrile episodes in ROSAH syndrome are abrupt and self-limited, typically resolving within 24 h. Notably, recurrent fever in cases 2 and 3 resolved completely after splenectomy, suggesting that the spleen may act as an immunologic amplifier of hyperacute cytokine surges. These observations point to a critical, yet unexplored, role of splenic immune modulation in the systemic autoinflammatory activity of ROSAH syndrome.

Although it is still early to draw definitive genotype–phenotype correlations, current evidence suggests that specific ALPK1 mutations may influence the severity and spectrum of ROSAH syndrome. In particular, the p.Thr237Met variant, identified in all four patients reported in this study, is associated with the most complete and severe phenotype [1], whereas the p.Thr237Ala variant appears to result in a milder presentation, characterized predominantly by ocular involvement with minimal or absent systemic manifestations [3]. The p.Tyr254Cys variant has also been linked to the full ROSAH phenotype, although data remain limited [5, 17]. Finally, the p.Ser277Phe mutation exhibits broader and stronger activation by human metabolites than T237M, suggesting a potentially more severe or distinct inflammatory profile [2].

To our knowledge, the occurrence of renal and intestinal amyloidosis in case 1 represents the first documented instance of reactive amyloidosis in ROSAH syndrome, highlighting the amyloidogenic potential of chronic systemic inflammation in this condition. In autoinflammatory diseases, serum amyloid A (SAA) deposition is a typical pathogenic hallmark, and its sustained elevation can drive irreversible organ dysfunction. Given the severity of this complication, early recognition and prompt initiation of targeted systemic anti-inflammatory therapy are imperative to prevent irreversible organ damage. Unfortunately, SAA was not systematically assessed during follow-up and no data were available in this regard. Conversely, CRP values typically do not reflect the degree of inflammation in patients treated with anti-IL-6 therapy; therefore, we chose not to consider them for this study purpose [18].

Corticosteroids and conventional immunosuppressive agents have generally proven ineffective. In contrast, IL-6 inhibition has shown efficacy in controlling systemic and ocular inflammation across some reported cases, amelioration of systemic symptoms and resolution of retinal vasculitis and macular edema [5, 7]. While awaiting more specific therapies, such as DF-003, a selective ALPK1 inhibitor that has been shown to effectively suppress pro-inflammatory signaling in vitro and in a mouse model of ROSAH syndrome in a recent preclinical study [19], our patients were treated with the IL-6 inhibitor tocilizumab. Indeed, tocilizumab has been used successfully in various non-infectious types of ocular inflammation, particularly for cases refractory to corticosteroids, conventional immunosuppressants, and tumor necrosis factor inhibitors. In clinical trials and multicenter cohorts, tocilizumab improved BCVA, reduced vitreous haze, decreased central macular thickness, and allowed steroid sparing. Response rates ranged from 50 to 90% for macular edema resolution and 60% to 80% for control of uveitic activity, depending on the subtype (e.g., juvenile idiopathic arthritis-associated uveitis, Behçet panuveitis, birdshot chorioretinopathy) [20–23]. In ROSAH syndrome, although the number of treated patients is small, preliminary evidence suggests partial to complete responses in ocular inflammation, mirroring the therapeutic trends observed in other types of refractory uveitis. While direct comparisons are limited by cohort size and disease heterogeneity, these data support the biologic rationale for IL-6 blockade in ROSAH and provide context for interpreting tocilizumab outcomes in this rare condition.

Notably, in our case series, tocilizumab appeared to be more effective in controlling systemic manifestations of the disease than ocular involvement, as it did not significantly reduce retinal vasculitis activity in one case nor improve visual function across all cases. Our experience reinforces the idea that visual recovery remains limited once structural retinal damage has occurred, highlighting the importance of early systemic diagnosis and intervention. Regarding this, Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors may represent a rational therapeutic approach, as they can interfere with both IFNγ-driven ALPK1 activation [24]. Moreover, the emergence of selective ALPK1 inhibitors (such as DF-033) opens the possibility for targeted, disease-modifying treatments that directly address the underlying molecular defect [19].

This study has several limitations. In particular, the small sample size, driven by the rarity of the disease, limits the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, follow-up durations were inconsistent, ranging from 2 years for most patients to 11 years for one patient, reflecting the real-world nature of data collection in this ultra-rare condition. Genetic testing was performed at multiple centers using different WES platforms, variant-calling pipelines, and in silico prediction tools, which may introduce some variability in variant detection and interpretation. Moreover, rare complications observed in small case series, such as amyloidosis, may be relatively overrepresented and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Larger registry-based studies, such as those conducted within international collaborative registries, such as the international AIDA Network registries dedicated to monogenic autoinflammatory diseases and inflammatory ocular diseases [25, 26], are needed to better define the true prevalence, spectrum, and clinical weight of these complications.

In summary, while bilateral optic disc involvement, CORD-like picture, low-grade uveitis, and retinal vasculitis are the most frequent and defining ocular manifestations, recognition of associated ocular surface, corneal and anterior segment anomalies is critical for an optimal management. Systemic manifestations range from potentially severe complications, such as reactive amyloidosis, to intriguing phenomena like the interplay between splenic function and recurrent fever. Timely identification of ROSAH syndrome is essential not only to preserve vision but also to prevent irreversible extraocular organ damage, including renal injury due to reactive amyloidosis.

ROSAH syndrome is a rare monogenic autoinflammatory disease in which ocular involvement is the principal diagnostic feature. This case series expands the phenotypic spectrum by identifying novel anterior segment and ocular surface findings and reporting the first case of reactive amyloidosis with severe renal involvement. Early recognition and multidisciplinary management are essential to prevent irreversible ocular and systemic damage. Current immunomodulatory therapies do not always provide satisfactory control of ocular manifestations, suggesting that new treatment alternatives are required, including selective ALPK1 inhibitors, which are currently under investigation.
