Schizophrenia is a chronic, severe mental disorder associated with significant 
disability, caregiver burden, and public health impact, particularly in low- and 
middle-income countries such as India. While multiple international clinical 
practice guidelines (CPGs) exist, contextual differences in illness 
presentation, sociocultural factors, health-care infrastructure, and resource 
availability necessitate region-specific guidance. This CPG provides a 
comprehensive, stage-based overview of the assessment and management of 
schizophrenia, with an emphasis on applicability to the Indian context. The 
document synthesizes available evidence, existing guideline recommendations, and 
expert clinical consensus to outline pragmatic approaches across the illness 
trajectory, including the prodromal phase, first-episode psychosis, multiepisode 
relapsing-remitting course, and treatment-resistant schizophrenia. Detailed 
recommendations are provided for clinical assessment, use of standardized rating 
scales, physical and laboratory investigations, and identification of comorbid 
medical and psychiatric conditions at each stage. Pharmacological strategies 
emphasize rational antipsychotic use, including early intervention principles, 
maintenance treatment, long-acting injectable antipsychotics, therapeutic drug 
monitoring-particularly for clozapine-and evidence-based augmentation approaches 
in treatment resistance. The guideline also addresses emergency management, 
antipsychotic switching strategies, and the judicious use of combination and 
adjuvant psychotropic therapies. Equally, it highlights the critical role of 
psychological and psychosocial interventions, family involvement, 
community-based care, and rehabilitation services in achieving functional 
recovery. Special attention is given to implementation challenges and 
adaptations relevant to Indian clinical settings, including cost, accessibility, 
family structures, and public mental health systems. Overall, this guideline 
aims to support clinicians in delivering individualized, evidence-informed, and 
contextually appropriate care for persons with schizophrenia, thereby improving 
long-term outcomes and quality of life.

In this clinical practice guideline (CPG), we summarize the approach principles and treatment overview for patients with schizophrenia. Given the availability of several CPGs, this manuscript aims to provide an overview of the relevant literature and suggest potential options based on the consensus among the clinicians who authored this manuscript. To an extent, issues pertinent to the Indian context are emphasized.

Schizophrenia develops/progresses in at least three stages, that is, prodrome, first episode, and the chronic phase.[1] Some authors have also suggested a multiepisode relapsing–remitting phase in addition to the three phases/stages or included it in the course of schizophrenia illness.[2] There is usually a premorbid phase before the prodrome (with soft neurological signs, early neurodevelopmental delay, etc.).[1] The treatment approach varies as per the stage/phase of schizophrenia with regard to medications and nonpharmacological strategies. The present CPGs aim to update and contextualize existing recommendations by integrating internationally accepted standards with the realities of Indian clinical practice. These guidelines seek to bridge the gap between global evidence-based practices and local sociocultural, economic, and health-system considerations, thereby facilitating personalized and pragmatic care for individuals with schizophrenia in India. They provide a comprehensive framework for the assessment and management of schizophrenia across various clinical stages and care settings. However, given the inherent heterogeneity of clinical presentations and service contexts, not all patient-specific factors can be fully addressed within a guideline framework. Accordingly, these recommendations should be interpreted as broad, evidence-informed guidance rather than prescriptive or legally binding directives. Clinicians are encouraged to apply their professional expertise, clinical judgment, and contextual understanding to adapt these recommendations to the individual needs, preferences, and circumstances of patients and their families, as well as to the resources available within their practice setting. These guidelines are intended to complement, rather than replace, sound clinical knowledge, competence, and individualized decision-making in routine psychiatric care.

To facilitate clinical application, certain sections of the guidelines recommend a stage-specific approach to assessment and treatment.

In many patients, the onset of psychotic symptoms is preceded by varying periods of sub-syndromal clinical symptoms associated with distress and/or dysfunction. It is easy to diagnose the prodrome retrospectively. However, due to heterogeneous presentation and a smaller proportion (10%–50%) of individuals who get converted to schizophrenia, diagnoses of the prodrome lack specificity during cross-sectional or prospective evaluations. Some of the experiences may be part of the normal developmental process and may not be pathological. Hence, prodrome is not currently recognized as a distinct diagnosis in the major diagnostic systems.

DSM-5-TR, however, includes “Attenuated psychosis syndrome” (APS) as “conditions for further study” while incorporating the same in “other specified schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorder.” APS is characterized by noticeable changes in experiences and/or behaviors associated with attenuated forms of the positive symptoms. The “attenuated” form is based on lack of sustenance of symptoms (i.e., intermittent transient presentation), lesser severity (causing only modest impairments), and presence of insight (on perceptual deviations and delusional-like ideas). ICD-11 does not recognize APS, and the WHO working group recommended that it be coded within the chapter on “Factors influencing health status and encounters with health services” (Z) or the chapter on “Symptoms, signs and abnormal clinical and laboratory findings” (R).[8]

Other related terms include “Ultra-high risk,” “clinical high risk” (CHR), and “At-risk mental states” (ARMS), which are operationalized in clinical research settings using combinations of symptom frequency, intensity, and functional decline, often within a defined age group (typically late adolescence to young adulthood). As prodrome is usually characterized by attenuated psychotic symptoms, functional/academic decline, mood symptoms, and social withdrawal, early identification is crucial for preventive early interventions (discussed later). A comprehensive psychiatric, physical, and neurological assessment is necessary. Rating scales that can be used to document and monitor anomalous experiences and prodromal symptoms include (1) Scale of Prodromal Symptoms (SOPS),[9] (2) Structured Interview for Prodromal Syndromes,[10] (3) Bonn Scale for the Assessment of Basic Symptoms,[7] (4) Multidimensional assessment of psychotic prodrome,[11] (5) The Comprehensive assessment of At-Risk Mental States,[12] and (6) Basel Screening Instrument for Psychosis.[13] Box 2 mentions the assessment to be done in patients presenting in the prodrome stage of schizophrenia.

A detailed and comprehensive psychiatric assessment, including age of onset, pattern, duration of untreated psychosis (DUP), psychosocial context, comorbidity (substance use, mood/anxiety, obsessive–compulsive symptoms/disorder, etc.), and physical/neurological examination, in a structured format, is mandatory. Assessment may include the use of a standardized rating scale to document and monitor psychopathology at baseline and during follow-up visits. Commonly used ones are the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale, Scale for Assessment of Positive Symptoms, Scale for Assessment of Negative Symptoms, and the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale. The Global Assessment of Functioning or the Socio-Occupational Functioning Assessment Rating Scale can be used to assess functioning. In India, a longer DUP is usually seen (median 6–12 months) as compared to Western settings, partly due to stigma, magico-religious beliefs, and limited mental health literacy.[14]

In many patients, the course of schizophrenia, spanning more than 5 years, would be characterized by long-term persistence of symptoms, functional decline, and multiple relapses. About 70% of patients with first episode schizophrenia achieve symptom remission in the first 2 years of treatment, with improvements most pronounced in the first 6 months. However, 60%–80% relapse within the first 5 years. Functional remission and quality of life may lag behind symptomatic recovery. Clinical recovery, meaning both symptomatic and functional remission, occurs in 20–30% of patients over long-term follow-up periods (5–10 years), with meta-analyses indicating a pooled recovery rate of around 21%. The outcome of the first episode of schizophrenia may be better in India compared to these estimates, which are predominantly Western. Early treatment, faster response, good premorbid functioning, and absence of psychoactive substance use would generally predict a good outcome. Delaying the relapse from the first episode may determine a better long-term recovery.[15]

Clinical assessment in multiepisode relapsing–remitting or chronic schizophrenia requires a comprehensive, multidimensional approach, functional assessments, and risk evaluations over time. Integrating standardized symptom rating scales may enhance the care. Along with a thorough psychiatric history and clinical evaluation, there is a need to review the history of relapses, adherence, psychosocial functioning, medication side effects, and comorbid medical conditions. Assessment of factors leading to relapse needs to be documented as they can be used to prevent further relapse and can be included in tailored individualized psycho-education, such as looking for stressors, substance use, medication adherence, medication side effects, belief system regarding medications, and therapeutic alliance. No investigations are diagnostic and absolutely needed for the diagnosis and management of the prodromal state. Structured clinical interviews, examinations (such as minor physical anomalies and neurological soft signs), and comprehensive neuropsychological assessments may enhance the clinical decision-making process. Basic blood investigations like complete blood count (CBC), nutritional evaluations (Vitamin B12, Vitamin D, folic acid), metabolic profiles (blood glucose, serum electrolytes like sodium, potassium, and chloride levels, calcium levels, liver enzymes and renal function test including blood urea and serum creatinine), and thyroid profile (primarily thyroid stimulating hormone) are generally helpful as screening for contributing comorbidities that influence the mental status.

Additional investigations like toxicology screening for psychoactive substances, HIV/Syphilis serology, and tests for chronic infections like tuberculosis may be considered, based on the individual’s presentation and prevalence of these conditions in the population. Neuroimaging and neurophysiology are not clinically necessary unless there is a strong clinical suspicion of a neurological disorder. Computed tomography scans, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (both structural and functional), electroencephalography (including evoked responses such as mismatch negativity, P300, and P50), and heart rate variability have been extensively used in research for detecting group-level risk. According to current evidence, these do not have much clinical utility.

Autoimmune profile (serum and cerebrospinal fluids) should be restricted to only those cases with atypical features, rapid progression, or neurological signs (“red flags”).[17] The “red flags” include altered consciousness, seizures, focal neurological deficits, movement disorders, cognitive decline, catatonia, autonomic instability, or evidence of underlying systemic autoimmune disease. A few basic laboratory investigations need to be done in every patient with schizophrenia as patients with schizophrenia have one or more physical health conditions. These include CBC, liver function tests, renal function tests, fasting blood glucose, and lipid profile. Wherever indicated, HbA1c, thyroid profile, and electrocardiogram (ECG) would be beneficial. Nutritional status and lifestyle are generally poorer in patients with schizophrenia, more so in India, with different dietary habits and preferences. Assessments for nutritional deficiencies, such as Vitamin B12, folic acid, and Vitamin D levels, would be helpful, particularly in individuals experiencing negative symptoms and cognitive deficits.[181920] Anthropometric assessment (weight and body mass index) may be recorded in every patient at baseline and follow-up visits. Investigative monitoring for metabolic syndrome/side effects should be done during the early phase (1–3 months) of new antipsychotic initiation and annually thereafter. More frequent monitoring may be necessary when clinical signs of metabolic syndrome are present. Neuroimaging, EEG, toxicology screening, screening for chronic infections, and autoimmune screening are advised in specific clinical conditions as described above. Severe, intolerable adverse effects (especially neurological) with lower doses of antipsychotics may be an additional indicator for neuroimaging.

Regular investigations and monitoring would be similar to those in the first episode of schizophrenia. Close monitoring of metabolic syndrome, prolactin levels (especially with antipsychotics causing hyperprolactinemia in both sexes, menstrual disturbances in females), ECG (>40 years, on injectable antipsychotics or antipsychotics at high-risk of increasing QTc), and nutritional status would be needed. Therapeutic drug-level monitoring and psycho-pharmacogenomic studies (focusing on Cytochrome P450 enzymes) may be helpful in patients with treatment-resistant positive symptoms, antipsychotic intolerance, and frequent relapses despite reported fair medication adherence. The clinical utility of psycho-pharmacogenomics is disputable due to the lack of larger, standardized trials and cost-effectiveness evaluations. When conducted, decisions must always be integrated with other clinical factors.

Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) of psychotropic drugs, more importantly for clozapine, has a strong basis for optimizing treatment outcome[21] as its efficacy is affected by pharmacokinetic interactions (with smoking, drug interactions with serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors [SSRIs]); it has certain dose-related side effects (seizures, drowsiness, hypersalivation, tachycardia, postural hypotension, and constipation), and it can have insufficient clinical response (nonresponders achieve clinical effects when serum levels are above 350 ng/ml).[22] The Consensus Guidelines for TDM in Neuropsychopharmacology (AGNP) has recommended TDM monitoring of clozapine with the highest possible evidence level.[23] The guidelines define 350 ng/ml as the lower target concentration limit for achieving a satisfactory clinical response to clozapine and 600 ng/ml as the upper target concentration limit as this level is associated with an increased risk of clozapine-induced tonic-clonic seizures.[23] Overall, it has been recommended that with a ratio of 1.7 between upper and lower target concentrations, clozapine exhibits a narrow therapeutic index window, further supporting the need for TDM on a case-to-case basis when side effects or inadequate responses are suspected despite good adherence and minimal drug interactions or smoking.[23] Box 4 mentions the usual conditions under which TDM of clozapine is recommended.[24]

The goal of treatment during prodrome is to delay or prevent full psychosis, reduce distress, and improve functioning. Pharmacological intervention remains controversial, and psychosocial interventions have been recommended as the mainstay of treatment.[25] Antipsychotics are not routinely recommended unless functional deterioration is severe or an imminent risk of psychosis exists. Low-dose second-generation antipsychotics (e.g., Risperidone 1–2 mg/day, olanzapine 5–10 mg/day) may be considered, under close monitoring, for ultra-high-risk individuals.[26] Recent systematic reviews suggest limited long-term benefit of antipsychotic use during prodrome,[27] with a stronger case for psychological interventions such as cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT),[28] cognitive training,[29] omega-3 fatty acids,[29] and psychoeducation. However, the research evidence is of low quality and further investigation is required to confirm the efficacy of these interventions. We recommend starting with nonpharmacological interventions first (CBT and psychoeducation), followed by low-dose 2nd generation antipsychotics only if there is a severe functional decline. The antipsychotic trial duration should be short, and the dose needs to be escalated if frank psychosis occurs. Access to CBT/psychological interventions is limited in India, and proper psychoeducation and regular monitoring of symptoms in follow-up visits are recommended. The goals of treatment should be rapid symptom control, achieving remission with minimal side effects, improving adherence, and attaining functional recovery. Systematic reviews have revealed that although second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs) and first-generation antipsychotics (FGAs) are both effective for positive symptoms, SGAs tend to have lower extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), better subjective tolerability, and possibly better outcomes for negative and cognitive symptoms.[3031] A comparative review of guidelines for first-episode schizophrenia indicates a consensus that antipsychotics should be started promptly, monotherapy preferred, side effect profile to guide choice, and maintenance for 1–2 years after remission.[32] SGAs are preferred as the 1st line drug for the management of acute psychosis episodes (mostly Risperidone, Olanzapine, Aripiprazole, Amisulpride). FGAs can be used as a first-line treatment when there are cost/tolerance issues with SGAs, with careful monitoring of extrapyramidal side effects. Start with a low to moderate dose (avoid high initial dosing) and then titrate till response is achieved; however, always aim for the minimal effective dose.[33] It is suggested that once remission is achieved, medications should be continued for at least 1 to 2 years to reduce relapse risk with periodic follow-ups (in which residual psychopathology, functioning, and cognitive deficits, if any, need to be assessed). Discontinuation should be gradual and hyperbolic reduction (10%–25% decrease every 3–6 months with the smallest possible dose before stopping) with close monitoring for withdrawal symptoms or any minor relapse of symptoms. Throughout the duration, monitoring should be conducted for metabolic side effects (weight gain, glucose, and lipids), extrapyramidal side effects, serum prolactin levels, and sexual side effects as early detection improves management and adherence.

The goals of treatment should be to prevent relapses, maintain functioning, address cumulative side-effect burden, improve adherence, and minimize residual symptoms. Studies have proven that relapse rates are high when treatment is stopped or poorly adhered to, and maintenance therapy in patients with multiepisode schizophrenia is always warranted.[34] Most of the comparative evidence had favored older SGAs, with clozapine, olanzapine, and risperidone superior on more outcomes than other newer SGAs.[34] Long-acting injectables (LAIs) have been shown in several studies to reduce relapse, rehospitalization, and improve adherence in relapsing populations.[3536] Treatment recommendations in multiepisode/relapsing–remitting schizophrenia should be to continue antipsychotic medication (usually the same or switched based on tolerability and efficacy) long-term; many should be advised to continue indefinitely if the risk of relapse is high. Nonetheless, there is emerging evidence that not every patient needs lifelong treatment with antipsychotics;[3738] furthermore, patients with lower doses or no antipsychotics have shown improved social functioning.[39] In every patient who has maintained stable remission of symptoms for a reasonable period, the possibility of gradual dose reduction to reach a minimum effective dose and, in select cases, a careful approach (balancing the risk versus benefits of continued antipsychotics) toward stopping antipsychotics may be considered.[3740] Further systematic research is required in this area.[4142]

Discuss LAI antipsychotics with all patients who require ongoing antipsychotic treatment, and consider their use particularly in those with a history of nonadherence, frequent relapse, poor social support, poor insight, or a clear preference for an injectable formulation. The antipsychotic dose should be optimized so that it is sufficient for symptom control but the lowest possible to reduce side effects. Regular review/monitoring of side effects is necessary, and consideration should be given to switching to agents with better tolerability if metabolic, sexual, or motor adverse effects are compromising quality of life. Proactive evaluation for comorbid psychiatric conditions and their management is essential too (for example, mood stabilizers if mood features that have not responded to antipsychotics, antidepressants if persistent depression, lorazepam or electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) for threatening situations such as severe aggression, high suicidal risk, catatonic signs, agents for negative or cognitive symptoms, and so on, when primary antipsychotics are ineffective in managing them, based on evidence, along with an antipsychotic monotherapy trial).

According to systematic reviews, the rate of TRS in first-episode cohorts ranges from 22% to 25%.[43] A systematic review and network meta-analysis of 150 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (11,375 patients) have shown clozapine to have superior efficacy over haloperidol, chlorpromazine, quetiapine, and sulpiride, among others.[44] Combined options (e.g., clozapine + amisulpride, topiramate) have shown some promise in reducing both positive and negative symptoms. However, many estimates have low certainty and increased risk of adverse effects. Clozapine is the gold standard for TRS. It should be initiated once criteria are met, that is, persistence of more than moderate severity of psychotic symptoms causing significant dysfunction or distress after at least two failed antipsychotic trials, including one with amisulpride, olanzapine, or risperidone of adequate dose/duration with adequate and confirmed adherence. Optimization of clozapine dosing (gradual titration, monitoring blood levels where possible) is essential to get adequate symptom control. Augmentation strategies should be used when residual symptoms persist despite an adequate clozapine dose or when side effects restrict the increment of clozapine dose, that is, combining clozapine with other antipsychotics (amisulpride, aripiprazole), mood stabilizers, or other agents shown in trials (memantine, topiramate, etc.), balancing the risk profile.[45] ECT is suggested when clozapine response is partial or in severe cases, according to some guidelines. Many guidelines suggest ECT may improve positive symptoms in TRS when added to clozapine.[46]

For treating predominant negative symptoms in schizophrenia, the first line is to address the possible secondary contributors like depression, anxiety symptoms, medical comorbidities (including nutritional deficiencies), medication adverse effects (reduce antipsychotic, anticholinergic, and sedative burden), and substance use disorders. Evaluation of residual positive symptoms and optimizing antipsychotic monotherapy should be considered. Amisulpride (50–300 mg) is the only antipsychotic to have evidence of superiority over placebo.[47] Cariprazine has shown promise for primary negative symptoms in a manufacturer-sponsored trial. Switching to an SGA shall be attempted if the patient is on FGA. A meta-analysis suggested clozapine, amisulpride, and olanzapine, followed by zotepine and risperidone, to reduce negative symptoms significantly more than other drugs. But this is based on studies in acute treatment of multiepisode schizophrenia, where the predominant negative symptom group was excluded.[48] It will be important to note that the optimal antipsychotic dose of most SGAs for negative symptoms is observed nearer to the lower-to-medium range of the licensed dose.[49] Adjunctive pharmacotherapy augmentations like antidepressants (SSRIs are commonly tried) can be attempted but should be discontinued if found ineffective.[50] Some studies have studied pimavanserin, stimulants like modafinil, armodafinil and 3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine, 5HT3A antagonist like ondansetron, minocycline, topiramate, and estrogens with limited evidence.[51] There is some evidence of cannabidiol worsening the negative symptoms of schizophrenia, hence not recommended unless more data are available.[52]

However, pharmacotherapy is found to have lesser efficacy than psychosocial interventions—such as CBT for psychosis (CBTp), social skills training (SST), and motivational interviewing toward sustained gains. Guidelines on predominant negative symptoms recommend offering rehabilitative measures, including supportive employment and supportive housing, in this population, as no single approach fully remits symptoms.[50] Neuromodulation options, including add-on repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), have yielded small-to-moderate reductions beyond sham in RCTs, with tDCS particularly feasible for resource-limited settings like India.[535455] The adjuvant yoga therapy in improving negative symptoms may be considered.[5657] Multidisciplinary monitoring with a multipronged approach may ensure functional gains. At times, rapid tranquilization is necessary for immediate management of acute agitation/aggression/violence for the safety of the patient as well as the caregivers/treating team. Several guidelines (e.g., WFSBP, NICE, and country-level CPGs) include recommendations for the management of acute agitation with the use of IM formulations of antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, or combinations.[5859] Systematic reviews on rapid tranquilization have examined the efficacy and tolerability of various agents (e.g., IM haloperidol, IM olanzapine, IM lorazepam), although head-to-head evidence is variable.[585960] Recommended strategies include doing a complete and quick assessment to rule out or identify any medical cause/substance intoxication/substance withdrawal, along with risk assessment of harm to self and others. In non-TRS patients, switching is often employed: (1) to manage adverse effects (e.g., from olanzapine or clozapine to aripiprazole to reduce weight/metabolic burden), (2) in case of partial symptom control despite adherence, and (3) to improve adherence through LAI formulations. Evidence exists that switching to LAIs (such as risperidone, olanzapine, or paliperidone) may improve adherence and reduce relapse risk compared to continuing oral formulations.[273738] Switching between SGAs, for instance, from risperidone to lurasidone or cariprazine, may provide better tolerability or improvements in cognitive or negative symptom domains.[61]

In TRS, where two adequate trials of different antipsychotics have failed, switching to clozapine remains the evidence-based approach.[62] Clozapine demonstrates superior efficacy for positive symptoms and suicide prevention but requires careful monitoring due to hematological and metabolic adverse effects. If clozapine is contraindicated or not tolerated, switching to another serotonin-dopamine agonist (e.g., olanzapine or amisulpride) may offer modest symptomatic improvement, although the evidence is limited.[63]

One should adopt either cross-titration (gradual reduction of the current antipsychotic while introducing the new one, which is the most widely recommended method to reduce relapse and withdrawal risk) or abrupt switch (which may be used in cases of severe adverse effects such as neuroleptic malignant syndrome). A recent meta-analysis suggested no significant differences between the two approaches; however, it is essential to consider patient and caregiver comfort when selecting a strategy.[64] Plasma level monitoring can help identify poor metabolizers or nonadherence, thereby optimizing individualized dosing.[64]

It should be used only after failure of or partial response to monotherapy. However, combination therapy requires clear documentation of the rationale and ongoing reevaluation of necessity. Furthermore, individuals on combination therapy should be regularly monitored for metabolic, cardiac, and drug–drug interaction issues.[65] Table 5 depicts the switching and combination strategies.

Adjuvant psychotropic medications play an important role in the comprehensive management of schizophrenia, complementing antipsychotic therapy to address residual, comorbid, or treatment-emergent problems. While antipsychotics remain the cornerstone of management, they may not sufficiently control affective, anxiety, obsessive–compulsive, impulsive–aggressive, or sleep-related symptoms.[76] In the Indian setting, where patient presentations are often complex and multidimensional, rational and judicious use of adjuvants can improve functional outcomes and quality of life.

The most common adjuvants used include antidepressants, mood stabilizers, benzodiazepines, beta-blockers, and cognitive enhancers. Among antidepressants, SSRIs and SNRIs are used for comorbid depressive or anxiety symptoms, persistent negative symptoms, or secondary or comorbid obsessive–compulsive symptoms/disorder.[7778] Their use requires close monitoring for potential pharmacokinetic interactions; for example, fluvoxamine can increase clozapine plasma levels by CYP1A2 inhibition. In patients with schizoaffective disorder or prominent mood lability, mood stabilizers such as valproate, lithium, or lamotrigine may be effective as adjuncts.[798081] Valproate is particularly helpful in controlling impulsivity, irritability, and aggression.[79] Lithium, though effective, needs close monitoring of renal and thyroid function, and adherence may be challenging in resource-limited Indian contexts.

Benzodiazepines (lorazepam, clonazepam) are often prescribed short-term for acute agitation, insomnia, or catatonia, but their long-term use should be avoided due to tolerance and dependence risk. In catatonia, the lorazepam challenge test serves both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Beta-blockers, such as propranolol, can be beneficial for antipsychotic-induced akathisia or anxiety symptoms. Anticholinergic agents (trihexyphenidyl, benztropine, promethazine) are sometimes necessary for managing extrapyramidal symptoms, but they should be used at the lowest effective dose and for the shortest duration.

Adjunctive cognitive enhancers, such as memantine, donepezil, and omega-3 fatty acids, have been explored for alleviating negative and cognitive symptoms, with modest evidence but no additional clinical benefits.[8283] NAC and sarcosine are emerging as glutamatergic modulators with potential benefits in domains related to negative symptoms.[84] In practice, Indian clinicians must carefully weigh the cost, availability, and feasibility of monitoring before initiating such agents. Modafinil or bupropion may also be considered for prominent cognitive slowing or antidepressant effects, though evidence remains limited, and pose a risk of exacerbation of psychosis.[85]

Nutraceuticals and adjuvants, cannabidiol: Diets rich in some nutrients, including short-chain fatty acids (omega-3 fatty acids), phytochemicals, probiotics, and fibers, seem to have an anti-inflammatory and protective action on the nervous system. They are widely used as supplements in the management of schizophrenia.[86] A recent meta-analysis showed that only three of the 23 nutraceuticals/phytochemicals – sarcosine, citicoline, and N-acetylcysteine – may have some role as augmenting agents, but with a low to very low quality of evidence.[87] Studies with cannabidiol are mixed, with a recent randomized controlled trial reporting exacerbation of memory impairments and psychotic symptoms when used as a pretreatment in schizophrenia patients with comorbid cannabis use disorder.[52]

The overarching principle is that adjuvant psychotropics should be used only after optimization of the primary antipsychotic regimen, ensuring adequate dose, duration, and adherence. Polypharmacy increases cost, pill burden, side effects, and nonadherence; therefore, the addition of these agents must be done only after proper discussion and documentation. Clinicians should also counsel patients and caregivers about the rationale, expected benefits, and risks in culturally appropriate terms. The treating psychiatrist should recommend a clear indication, trial duration, and review for continuation and periodically assess the beneficial effects, if any, and decide whether to continue treatment. Monitoring for drug interactions, metabolic parameters, and emergent side effects is essential. In summary, adjuvant psychotropics can address specific unmet needs in schizophrenia treatment, affective instability, anxiety, aggression, or cognitive deficits but must be employed selectively, in an evidence-based and time-limited manner. Their use should never substitute for optimizing antipsychotic therapy or psychosocial interventions. The summary of the use of adjuvant medications in schizophrenia is described in Table 7.

LAI antipsychotics play a crucial role in enhancing adherence, preventing relapse, and ensuring stable plasma drug levels in patients with schizophrenia, particularly those with poor insight or irregular follow-up. Nonadherence rates to oral antipsychotics in Indian settings are reported between 30% and 50%, mainly due to poor illness awareness, side effects, socioeconomic constraints, and inadequate family supervision.[888990] Meta-analyses and real-world studies consistently demonstrate that LAIs reduce relapse and hospitalization rates by 30%–40% compared to oral formulations.[3583]

The usual indications for LAIs[35] are (1) Documented nonadherence or poor insight, (2) Frequent relapses despite adequate oral therapy, (3) Preference of patient/family for reduced pill burden, (4) History of violence or aggression linked to relapse, (5) Poor family supervision or unstable psychosocial environment, and (6) Difficulty accessing regular outpatient follow-up (common in rural and difficult terrain/hilly regions). Recent data[919293] suggest LAIs can be started in the early phase or first episode of schizophrenia to improve patient outcomes and can also be used as an augmentation strategy with clozapine in cases of clozapine-resistant schizophrenia.[949596]

Infrastructure limitations, particularly in smaller centers, pose significant challenges to the use of certain LAI antipsychotics in India. For instance, maintaining the cold chain required for storage and ensuring postinjection observation facilities, particularly for olanzapine pamoate, which carries a risk of postinjection delirium/sedation syndrome, may not be feasible in resource-constrained settings.[9798] Consequently, depot formulations such as haloperidol decanoate, fluphenazine decanoate and flupenthixol decanoate, and zuclopenthixol decanoate continue to remain more pragmatic and widely used choices in such contexts.[98] Further, cost and accessibility also play a major role in LAI utilization. Agents like paliperidone palmitate and aripiprazole monohydrate LAIs remain expensive and are not routinely available under most public health schemes. Finally, adequate training of psychiatric nursing personnel in the correct techniques of LAI administration, recognition and management of injection-related adverse events, and patient counseling are essential for safe and effective implementation of LAI programs within community and hospital settings.[98] Table 8 depicts the commonly used LAIs in the Indian context.

Inpatient admission offers the advantage of comprehensive evaluation, safe environment, pharmacological stabilization, and initiation of psychosocial therapy. It also facilitates family counseling, discharge planning, and community reintegration. However, due to limited bed availability and costs, long-term hospitalization is discouraged unless absolutely necessary. The focus should be on early stabilization and planned transition to outpatient follow-up.

Outpatient management is the mainstay for the majority of individuals with schizophrenia in India. It is suitable when patients are clinically stable, have reasonable insight and cooperative family support, and can adhere to medications. The clinician should ensure structured follow-up, preferably within 2–4 weeks after discharge or initiation of treatment, and may consider using community-based mental health services or telepsychiatry where possible to enhance the accessibility of care. The District Mental Health Programme (DMHP) has enabled such continuity through outreach clinics, ensuring access in semi-urban and rural settings. Family members play a critical role in medication supervision, identifying early relapse, and supporting vocational rehabilitation.

Certain hybrid models, such as day-care centers, partial hospitalization, and community-based rehabilitation homes, can bridge the gap between inpatient and outpatient care. These are increasingly being implemented by NGOs and tertiary institutions. Studies such as the Community-based Intervention for People with Schizophrenia and their caregivers in India trial have demonstrated that community-based care combined with pharmacotherapy yields comparable outcomes to facility-based care, reinforcing that home-based rehabilitation is feasible and effective in India.[99]

In all stages, it is recommended to follow a structured decision-making criterion: (a) assess clinical stability, (b) evaluate risk, (c) consider family financial burden and access to healthcare services, and (d) choose the least restrictive safe environment. Ethical and legal safeguards, including obtaining informed consent, maintaining accurate documentation, and adhering to the provisions of the Mental Healthcare Act 2017, must be observed during admissions.[100] Discharge planning should include relapse prevention strategies, prescription reconciliation, psychoeducation, and scheduling of the first outpatient follow-up. The goal is to ensure continuity, community reintegration, and family involvement while preventing avoidable institutionalization.

These interventions, in general, aim to improve social, communication, coping, and problem-solving skills and self-esteem and reduce addictive behaviors.

Pharmacotherapy, though essential, is insufficient for comprehensive recovery in schizophrenia. Psychological and psychosocial interventions address the cognitive, behavioral, emotional, and social dimensions of the illness, thereby improving adherence, insight, functional outcome, and quality of life. In India, such interventions must be tailored to local cultural and resource contexts, while leveraging the country’s strong family structures and community networks. Psychosocial interventions remain crucial in the long-term recovery process. They should be initiated early and integrated with pharmacotherapy.

Psychoeducation forms the foundation. It involves structured sessions for patients and caregivers that cover the nature of the illness, the rationale behind treatment, potential side effects, and early warning signs of relapse. Indian studies indicate that psychoeducation significantly improves medication adherence and reduces relapse rates.[102] It should be repeated at each phase of treatment using local language and culturally relevant examples. Written handouts and family meetings can reinforce understanding.

Family interventions are crucial, given that most Indian patients live with family. The focus is on enhancing communication, reducing high expressed emotions, improving coping skills, and fostering collaborative problem-solving. Family members are trained to support adherence, recognize prodromal signs, and manage stress. Studies from India have shown that family-based care reduces relapse and improves functioning.[103]

SST is another evidence-based approach for improving interpersonal functioning. SST utilizes behavioral techniques to teach interpersonal and independent living skills, including conversation, making friends, and handling social situations. It is an effective way to improve social functioning and negative symptoms by breaking complex skills into smaller parts using a structured approach that includes modeling (role-playing), practice, and feedback. Incorporating SST into day-care or rehabilitation programs has yielded positive results.[104105] Although the evidence in favor of SST is very limited, with data rated as very low quality, it is worth trying in patients with residual/predominant negative symptoms.[104]

CBTp helps patients challenge distressing delusional beliefs, cope with voices (persistent or treatment-resistant auditory hallucinations), and reduce anxiety. In Indian tertiary settings, CBTp is feasible when delivered, with structured manuals, by trained psychologists or psychiatrists.[106] It improves insight and coping even in partially remitted patients.

Cognitive remediation therapy (CRT), which focuses on attention, memory, and executive functions, enhances functional outcomes and employability, although access remains limited. CRT has shown small to moderate improvements in cognition and function in schizophrenia.[107]

Vocational rehabilitation and supported employment are vital for long-term recovery. Rehabilitation centers and community-based NGOs in India have demonstrated that structured vocational programs reduce disability and caregiver burden. Partnerships with local industries and micro-enterprise models can be encouraged under the framework of the Rehabilitation Council of India. Several models have been developed and found beneficial.[108]

Lifestyle and wellness interventions are gaining importance, given the high metabolic burden of antipsychotics. Structured exercise, yoga, mindfulness, dietary counseling, and smoking cessation programs help mitigate cardiometabolic risks. The NIMHANS yoga module and yoga-based interventions can be incorporated into maintenance care.[109]

Community-based rehabilitation is particularly relevant in India’s public-health context. The DMHP, community health workers (ASHAs), and peer-support networks can deliver basic psychoeducation and relapse monitoring in rural areas. Telepsychiatry platforms have expanded access to psychological follow-ups and psychosocial interventions, thereby enhancing the delivery of mental health care.[110]

In summary, psychological and psychosocial interventions are indispensable complements to pharmacotherapy. When culturally adapted and delivered within India’s family-centric and community-oriented health framework, they transform treatment from symptom control to recovery, restoring dignity, social participation, and meaningful life for persons with schizophrenia. Combination of clozapine with ECT as an augmentation strategy in clozapine-resistant schizophrenia has shown enhanced efficacy and faster symptom reduction and is widely used.[4667125]

The Mental Healthcare Act (MHCA) 2017[166] has set an important legal framework for healthcare management of patients with severe mental illness, including schizophrenia. It aims to protect and promote the rights of patients during service delivery.

It calls for treatment in the least restrictive environment with the consent of the individuals. The act provides provisions for treating patients who lack the capacity to make treatment decisions and have high support needs in appropriate settings based on the request of family members (or by the person nominated by the patient, while having the capacity, for the purpose of making decisions) with ascertainment by designated mental health professionals. The act mandates the responsibility of securing and caring for the homeless mentally ill person or one without a caregiver but with high support needs onto the government bodies, including the police and social justice/welfare services, along with the mental health professionals. It enables emergency management by clinicians in the community or general clinical settings for not more than a stipulated period before shifting to a mental health establishment. Supported (involuntary) admission is subject to strict criteria, review by MHRB, and time limits, balancing patient autonomy and clinical need. Documentation and reporting of such admissions are the responsibility of the mental health care providers and establishment. Any further restrictive care, like physical restraints, should be periodically reported to MHRB.

While we have attempted to summarize several of the relatively well-established treatment components for schizophrenia, several newer developments are evolving and are likely to gather more evidence base in the coming years. Some of these include the utility of polygenic risk score in predicting risk for psychosis as well as prognosis, gene polymorphism assay to map the drug-metabolizing status of the patients, the role of technology in schizophrenia management, and the availability of medications that work through newer mechanisms (for example, combination of Xanomeline and Trospium). We hope that these advances will complement the existing armamentarium for treating this challenging disorder in the future.

| Suicidality[ 135 136 137 ] | Risk of suicide is markedly elevated in schizophrenia Suicide is more common during the early phases of schizophrenia and the early period of recovery from psychotic symptoms Employ dynamic suicide risk formulations integrating long-term risk status, acute risk state, available resources, and foreseeable stressors The risk assessment and suicide risk formulation are recommended during initial consultation, and repeated whenever there are clinical changes or new stressors Long-term risk factors: younger age, adverse childhood experiences, high socioeconomic status, premorbidly high scholastic achievement, high intelligence, overambitious and higher aspirations, emotional dysregulation, early age of onset, posthospitalization, chronic and deteriorating course with multiple relapses, past attempts, family history of suicide, inadequate treatment, poor medication adherence, poor response to treatment, comorbid substance use disorder, internalized stigma, and insufficient family support/family conflicts Acute risk factors: Recent suicidal attempt/ideations, psychopathology – command hallucinations and delusions related to death during acute psychotic phases; higher illness awareness (greater insight) along with demoralization, feeling of isolation and hopelessness, more so during early recovery phase; agitation/restlessness and drug-induced akathisia (and other extrapyramidal symptoms), insomnia, recent loss/grief, unemployment, medication changes Important resources to assess and strengthen: Coping strategies, resilience, social support, and good patient–clinician collaboration Immediate safety measures: Hospitalization for imminent risk, removal of means, family and social support mobilization, and active safety planning. Electroconvulsive therapy reduces the imminent risk of suicide/self-harm, with early response Early and close involvement of family caregivers in the therapeutic process, psychoeducating them about the warning signs of suicide Adequate treatment of psychotic symptoms with antipsychotic medication and of depression, when present, with appropriate antidepressant and psychotherapy Clozapine has the strongest evidence to reduce the longer-term risk for suicidal behaviors and may be initiated even in the absence of treatment resistance in those with a very high risk of suicide, like multiple suicide attempts or persistent suicidal ideations Comprehensive biopsychosocial strategies prioritizing risk factor identification, close monitoring, structured safety planning, and evidence-based interventions can better reduce suicide attempts and mortality than any modality administered alone |
| Violence and aggression[ 138 139 140 141 142 ] | Violence and aggression in schizophrenia require systematic evaluation and a tiered intervention strategy Risk of harm to self or others would warrant inpatient care Integration of risk assessment, multi-modal management, and staff training is needed in psychiatric emergency settings Factors that heighten risk for violence include dynamic factors like active psychosis (delusions, hallucinations commanding harm, agitation), substance use, prior history of violence, nonadherence to treatment, and certain personality traits (like impulsivity), and static factors like male sex, adolescence and early adulthood, childhood abuse, past or family history of aggressive behaviors Assess for the possibility of substance intoxication, withdrawal, delirium, and catatonic excitement to choose an appropriate management strategy Use a tiered approach in the acute management of aggression/violence related to psychosis: • Always attempt verbal de-escalation • Antipsychotics and benzodiazepines in oral or parental form would reduce the imminent risk when verbal de-escalation fails • A combination of antipsychotic, specifically haloperidol, with promethazine or benzodiazepine (midazolam/lorazepam) is more effective, faster, and safer • Olanzapine and ziprasidone can be alternatives, but the need for reinjections may be higher • Physical restraint should be used only if necessary, with regular safety monitoring and for the shortest necessary time. The rationale should be documented, and every physical restraint event should be reported as per MHCA 2017 Avoid seclusions as a management of aggression/violence as it is prohibited in India by MHCA 2017 Debriefing should be attempted after coercive measures Electroconvulsive therapy is indicated for faster response and reducing the risk in individuals with persisting violent or aggressive behavior Adequate antipsychotic treatment, use of a mood stabilizer like valproate, may reduce the long-term agitation/aggression risk, especially in those with affective symptoms Clozapine has strong evidence to reduce the longer-term risk for aggression and may be initiated even in the absence of treatment resistance in those with a persistent and/or refractory aggression, agitation, and/or homicidal behavior Management of substance craving and family psychoeducation and involvement are critical for prevention and support Timeout procedures may be needed in some patients, especially those with intellectual deficits, and must be used as a comprehensive behavioral management program |
| Comorbid substance use disorder[ 143 ] | Dual disorder of schizophrenia with substance use is associated with poor family/social support, unemployment, homelessness, legal encounters, treatment nonadherence, frequent relapses and hospitalizations, risk of suicide/self-harm, aggression, treatment-resistance, physical comorbidities, and all-cause mortality Schizophrenia and substance use reinforce manifestations of each other Multidisciplinary integrated care for both conditions is preferable to managing them sequentially (one after the other) or parallel care (two separate care systems) Antipsychotics with long half-life and simple dosing regimes enhance adherence and outcome. LAI antipsychotics have greater benefits Antipsychotics with weaker D2 antagonism (like clozapine, olanzapine, ziprasidone) and partial agonism (like aripiprazole) seem to have superiority in reducing substance-related outcomes compared to strong D2 antagonists (like haloperidol, risperidone) Pharmacological agents effective for the management of detoxification and pharmaco-prophylaxis for different substance use disorders may be considered if required Psychosocial interventions like motivational interviewing, motivation enhancement treatment, relapse prevention counselling, and cognitive behavioral interventions may be included in the treatment plan |

| Catatonia[ 144 145 ] | Syndrome of Catatonia is often an emergency and needs prompt management Evaluate for secondary catatonia: Epilepsy, encephalitis, substance-induced, structural brain lesions, endocrine and metabolic causes, antipsychotic-induced, benzodiazepine withdrawal, clozapine withdrawal Evaluate for malignant catatonia, neuroleptic malignant syndrome (which requires urgent inpatient admission and may require ICU setup) Appropriate investigations may be carried out, and any other underlying causes are to be managed Appropriate hydration, electrolyte supplementation, nutrition, and supportive treatments for physical health need to be provided Initial treatment of choice is lorazepam If catatonia does not respond to benzodiazepines, consider ECT Evidence for Zolpidem, other benzodiazepines (midazolam, clonazepam), transcranial magnetic stimulation, memantine, amantadine, lamotrigine, valproate, and atypical antipsychotics is minimal and can be considered as third-line treatments if the above two are not effective or feasible Dantrolene and dopamine agonists like bromocriptine are considered in refractory malignant catatonia Bromocriptine to be considered in case of NMS and ICU admission if worsening of clinical condition; may require ECT Antipsychotics may prevent catatonic relapse in schizophrenia |
| Depression[ 146 147 148 ] | Depression can be part of prodrome, acute psychotic episodes, and post-psychotic depression Depressive symptoms have significant overlap/similarities and hence need to be differentiated from negative symptoms, medication side effects (including dysphoria), psychoactive substance-related symptoms, demoralization due to stigma, incomplete functional recovery, family burden, and coping difficulties due to psychosocial issues. These issues should be appropriately addressed (like dose reduction of EPS/dysphoria-inducing antipsychotics, switching to safer antipsychotics or antipsychotics with antidepressant properties, adding anticholinergics, and psychosocial interventions) before considering pharmacotherapy to reduce depression Antidepressants (SSRIs and SNRIs) may be helpful in post-psychotic depression and the syndromal depressive symptoms in the prodromal stage Antipsychotics with antidepressant properties (quetiapine, lurasidone, amisulpride, aripiprazole, olanzapine, clozapine) are to be considered first-line in managing depression during the acute psychotic phase. Benzodiazepines can be useful in associated agitation and insomnia. There are concerns about antidepressants worsening psychosis in acute psychotic phases, especially with TCAs Emerging evidence suggests certain antidepressants (sertraline, fluoxetine, escitalopram, venlafaxine, mirtazapine) used as adjunctive to antipsychotics may be safe and superior to antipsychotics alone. Mood stabilizers, including lithium and valproate, may have a role in reducing depression in patients with bipolar type of schizoaffective disorder rTMS and ECT are generally considered when antipsychotic monotherapy is insufficient or rapid symptom control is needed (e.g., high suicidal risk) |
| Mania[ 149 150 ] | Manic symptoms in schizophrenia may be syndromal or nonsyndromal. Manic symptoms may be present in a large proportion of patients, but only in a few are they persistent with significant severity over a longer duration to qualify for schizoaffective disorder. The possibility of manic symptoms secondary to psychoactive substances like cannabis, catatonic excitement, and hyperthymic personality should be considered Manic symptoms can predict worse outcomes, higher risk behaviors, and increased complexity in treatment; early and aggressive intervention is recommended Atypical antipsychotics (olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine) are frequently used for both acute agitation and longer-term antimanic properties Mood stabilizers (lithium, valproate and carbamazepine) can moderate manic episodes and prevent recurrence, especially important in schizoaffective disorder, but evidence is limited Benzodiazepines may be added for sedation if agitation is severe, but they should not be used alone Antidepressants should generally be avoided or tapered off during manic episodes as these may worsen mania or psychosis Clozapine may be an effective treatment for both psychotic and affective symptoms, regardless of an acute or maintenance phase There is some evidence that second-generation long-acting injectable antipsychotics are effective in schizoaffective mania |
| Obsessive-compulsive disorder/symptoms[ 151 152 ] | Syndromal or subsyndromal OC symptoms are common in schizophrenia. They may manifest as a prodromal symptom of schizophrenia, may be part of the clinical manifestation of schizophrenia, may be treatment-emergent (antipsychotic-induced), or may be a comorbidity preceding the onset of psychotic symptoms Hence, assessment of OC symptoms includes evaluating their independence (i.e., not merely part of delusional content), persistence over time, and if they cause separate distress/impairment beyond psychosis OCS, if not severe and later in onset, can be separately addressed if they persist after psychotic symptoms subside Data on anti-obsessional agents are limited, but SSRIs and clomipramine added to antipsychotics are generally effective CBT in the form of exposure and response prevention may be considered when adding SSRI/Aripiprazole or changing antipsychotics is not feasible or ineffective |
|  | Treatment-emergent OCS are commonly reported with SGAs like clozapine, olanzapine, and risperidone. Reduction of dose followed by switching to an antipsychotic with minimal influence on the serotonergic system (Aripiprazole, amisulpride, ziprasidone, and first-generation antipsychotics) or addition of SSRIs/clomipramine or addition of aripiprazole can be considered Possibility of manic switch, worsening of psychosis, and pharmacokinetic interactions with antipsychotics (e.g., fluoxetine and fluvoxamine with clozapine) should be weighed before choosing, starting, and optimizing SSRI/clomipramine |
| Perinatal women[ 153 ] | Schizophrenia is associated with a higher risk of pregnancy-related complications and disorders Proactive collaborative coordinated care between obstetric, psychiatric, and primary care teams with regular monitoring for metabolic side effects and pregnancy-associated complications is needed The risk of unplanned pregnancy is also higher in schizophrenia Antipsychotic drugs with a long track record of safety are preferred, at the lowest effective dose, due to the unavailability of rigorous evidence Risperidone and paliperidone are currently not considered first-line agents due to some concerns of malformations In women with a higher risk of gestational diabetes, antipsychotics with fewer metabolic adversities should be chosen However, an effective antipsychotic on which the patient has been stable should not be discontinued or changed as relapse poses a greater risk to the mother–baby than the potential adverse effects Dose should not be reduced just because of pregnancy as the antipsychotic metabolism may increase during pregnancy and may lead to subtherapeutic levels (except clozapine which may increase by 3 rd trimester). Doses should always be individualized Postpartum psychosis is considered an emergency and treated preferably with hospitalization due to the increased risk of infanticide and suicide Antipsychotics are not contraindicated during breastfeeding. Clozapine may be present in breast milk, and its potential risks to the infant need to be considered and monitored Psychosocial interventions, including supportive interventions, should be offered Electroconvulsive therapy is safe and can offer a rapid treatment response for acute psychosis in the pregnancy and postpartum period |
| Children and adolescents[ 154 ] | Early-onset schizophrenia is considered to have a poorer outcome, especially with longer duration of untreated psychosis Individualized care, regular reassessment for response and side effects, and developmentally appropriate interventions should be provided. Family involvement is crucial in this population |
| Older adults[ 155 156 ] | Late-onset (40–60 years) and very late-onset (>60 years) schizophrenia are rare and need to be cautiously diagnosed after ruling out other causes Thoroughly evaluate for affective psychosis, delirium, dementia (Alzheimer’s, fronto-temporal, Parkinson’s, Lewy body, vascular or mixed), medication-induced (higher doses of anti-histaminergics or anticholinergics, steroids, dopamine agonists, antimalarials, antiretrovirals, opioid analgesics, fluoroquinolones, ACE inhibitors and beta-blockers) and psychoactiv substance-induced psychosis (cannabis, stimulants, hallucinogens, opioids and dissociatives), severe nutritional and endocrine disorders Neuroimaging is indicated, especially if presenting with the first episode at an elderly age (>60 years) Comorbidities are common, and the choice of antipsychotics would be based on their side-effect profiles First-line antipsychotics will be the ones with lower anticholinergic burden and lower sedative property (to prevent falls) Typically started at a lower dose (25%–50% lower than that in adults) Avoid anticholinergic co-prescription to the possible extent Monitoring for metabolic adverse effects should be more frequent Psychosocial challenges would be unique and often need appropriate psychosocial interventions |
| Epilepsy[ 157 158 159 ] | Comorbidity of schizophrenia and epilepsy is more frequent than expected by chance Rule out ictal and post-ictal psychosis. They are self-remitting with supportive treatment. Preventing seizures would suffice without the need for prophylactic antipsychotic treatment Inter-ictal psychosis and para-ictal psychosis would have a course similar to schizophrenia and often need antipsychotic treatment When needed, choose antipsychotics with lower seizure propensity (Avoid or use with caution: Clozapine, chlorpromazine, loxapine, zotepine, quetiapine, and olanzapine) Consider substantial drug interactions with antiepileptics while dosing |
| Hepatic Impairment[ 160 161 ] | Higher risk of liver disease in persons with mental illness. Possible causes include poor nutrition, smoking, viral hepatitis, antipsychotics, and concurrent substance use Patients with schizophrenia and chronic viral hepatitis have a higher risk of severe hepatic outcomes (e.g., liver failure, cancer) Balance risk–benefit carefully; mild transaminase elevations may have to be accepted if psychosis requires treatment, but discontinue if severe liver injury occurs Avoid phenothiazines like chlorpromazine and fluphenazine due to the risk of cholestatic hepatic injury Most other antipsychotics can cause hepatocellular injury with increased transaminases, but it is mostly self-resolving and rarely needs discontinuation Steatosis and mixed types of liver injuries are also seen over months to years, and are often associated with metabolic syndrome and may progress to chronic fibrosis Olanzapine and clozapine pose a greater risk of hepatotoxicity; risperidone and quetiapine pose a moderate risk. Lurasidone, aripiprazole, cariprazine, amisulpride, paliperidone, lurasidone, brexpiprazole, ziprasidone, haloperidol, and quetiapine have a lower risk In patients with viral hepatitis, choose antipsychotics with lower hepatic metabolism when possible (e.g., paliperidone, amisulpride) Aripiprazole, paliperidone, and ziprasidone have relatively safer profiles; no dosage adjustment is needed in mild to moderate hepatic impairment Most other antipsychotics that get metabolized in the liver should be started at lower doses and uptitrated slowly in mild to moderate hepatic impairment |
| Renal impairment[ 162 ] | Most FGAs are considered safe in CKD without dose adjustment, except phenothiazines, which may increase hypotension risk Among SGAs, amisulpride and paliperidone are excreted unchanged in urine, need dose consideration, and are better avoided in end-stage renal disease Aripiprazole and asenapine are generally safe, with caution warranted for olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, and clozapine Long-acting injectables are better avoided in renal impairment |
| Metabolic syndrome (Obesity, Diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia)[ 163 164 ] | Syndromal and subsyndromal metabolic syndrome is higher in schizophrenia owing to genetic and lifestyle factors and medications contributing to it. This increases the risk of medical morbidity and early mortality Regular monitoring for metabolic syndrome at baseline and periodically, as per the individual’s risk, is needed Population-specific criteria should be followed when assessing metabolic syndrome, given the higher body fat content and risk profile in Indians Metabolic syndrome includes: Obesity (waist circumference males: ≥90 cm and for females: ≥80 cm), hypertriglyceridemia (≥150 mg/dl), low HDL (males<40 mg/dl and females<50 mg/dl), hypertension (systolic blood pressure≥130 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure≥85 mmHg), and dysglycemia (fasting blood sugar≥100 mg/dl). Additional measures include: HbA1c>5.6, body mass index>22.5 kg/m 2 , serum cholesterol>200 mg/dl, LDL>130 mg/dl Lifestyle modifications are primary: diet quality improvement, caloric intake reduction, increased physical activity, and smoking/alcohol abstinence Olanzapine and clozapine have the highest metabolic risk, followed by other SGAs like risperidone, quetiapine On identifying metabolic changes, wherever feasible, lower the doses of these antipsychotics or switch to antipsychotics with favorable metabolic profiles (e.g., aripiprazole, ziprasidone, lurasidone) However, controlling psychotic symptoms with an effective antipsychotic should not be compromised, which will reflect in improving the metabolic profile and physical health Antipsychotics like clozapine should not be unduly withheld in those with treatment resistance where other antipsychotics are not effective Pharmacological management using agents such as metformin, GLP-1RAs, and aripiprazole augmentation would improve metabolic parameters There is evolving evidence of the use of glucagon-like peptide agonist (Semaglutide) for antipsychotic induced weight gain[ 165 ]; however, safety data and effectiveness are limited |
