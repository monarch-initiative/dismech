Last Update: <span>July 27, 2023</span>.</p><p><em>Estimated reading time: 40 minutes</em></p></div><div><div><h2>Summary</h2><div><h4>Clinical characteristics.</h4><p>Megalencephalic leukoencephalopathy with subcortical cysts (MLC) is characterized by two phenotypes: classic MLC and improving MLC.</p><ul><li><div>Individuals with <b>classic MLC</b> present with macrocephaly, often in association with seizures, gradual onset of ataxia, spasticity, and sometimes extrapyramidal findings, mild gross motor developmental delays, and late-onset cognitive deterioration. Macrocephaly, observed in most affected individuals, may be present at birth but more frequently develops during the first year of life. The degree of macrocephaly is variable, with head circumferences reaching four to six standard deviations greater than the mean. After the first year of life, head growth trajectory typically normalizes and growth follows a line parallel to, although several standard deviations above, the 98th centile. Initial mental and motor development is normal in most individuals. Walking is often unstable, followed by ataxia of the trunk and extremities, pyramidal dysfunction, and brisk deep tendon reflexes. Early-onset seizures are common, and approximately 60% of individuals have epilepsy that is typically well controlled with anti-seizure medication, but status epilepticus occurs relatively frequently. Cognitive deterioration occurs later in the course of the disease and is usually mild in severity. Overall disease severity varies, with some individuals being able to ambulate independently for only a few years from disease onset to other individuals continuing to independently walk in the fifth decade of life.</div></li><li><div>Individuals with <b>improving MLC</b> have a similar initial presentation with delayed cognitive or motor development, followed by an improving clinical course: macrocephaly usually persists, but some children become normocephalic; motor function improves or normalizes; hypotonia and clumsiness may persist in some or neurologic examination may become normal. Some individuals have intellectual disability that is stable, with or without autism spectrum disorder. Epilepsy is much less frequent than in classic MLC.</div></li></ul></div><div><h4>Diagnosis/testing.</h4><p>The diagnosis of classic MLC is established in individuals with suggestive clinical findings and characteristic abnormalities identified on brain MRI examination, including abnormal and swollen cerebral hemispheric white matter and subcortical cysts in the anterior temporal and often frontoparietal regions; and/or <a>biallelic</a> <a>loss-of-function</a> variants in <i>MLC1</i> or <i>HEPACAM</i> or a <a>heterozygous</a> <a>pathogenic variant</a> in <i>GPRC5B</i> identified by <a>molecular genetic testing</a>.</p><p>The diagnosis of improving MLC is established in individuals with suggestive clinical findings and a <a>heterozygous</a> <a>gain-of-function</a> variant in <i>HEPACAM</i> or <a>biallelic</a> pathogenic variants in <i>AQP4</i> identified by <a>molecular genetic testing</a>.</p></div><div><h4>Management.</h4><p><i>Treatment of manifestations:</i> Physical therapy to improve motor function; speech therapy as needed; special education; anti-seizure medication to control seizures.</p><p><i>Agents/circumstances to avoid:</i> Contact sports and other activities associated with a high risk of head trauma should be avoided.</p></div><div><h4>Genetic counseling.</h4><p>MLC is inherited in an <a>autosomal recessive</a> or an <a>autosomal dominant</a> manner.</p><ul><li><div><b>Classic MLC</b> is most commonly caused by <a>biallelic</a> pathogenic variants in <i>MLC1</i> or <i>HEPACAM</i> and is inherited in an <a>autosomal recessive</a> manner. If both parents of a child with <i>MLC1</i>-related classic MLC or biallelic <i>HEPACAM</i>-related classic MLC are known to be <a>heterozygous</a> for an <i>MLC1</i> or <i>HEPACAM</i> <a>pathogenic variant</a>, each sib of an affected individual has at conception a 25% chance of inheriting biallelic pathogenic variants and being affected, a 50% chance of being heterozygous, and a 25% chance of inheriting neither of the <a>familial</a> pathogenic variants. Once the autosomal recessive MLC-related pathogenic variants have been identified in an affected family member, <a>carrier testing</a> for at-risk relatives is possible. Rarely, classic MLC occurs as an <a>autosomal dominant</a> disorder caused by a heterozygous <i>GPRC5B</i> pathogenic variant. All individuals with <i>GPRC5B</i>-related classic MLC reported to date have had the disorder as a result of a <a><i>de novo</i></a> pathogenic variant<i>.</i></div></li><li><div><b>Improving MLC</b> most commonly occurs as an <a>autosomal dominant</a> disorder caused by an inherited or <a><i>de novo</i></a> <a>pathogenic variant</a> in <i>HEPACAM</i>. Approximately 20% of individuals with <a>heterozygous</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related improving MLC have the disorder as a result of a <i>de novo</i> pathogenic variant. Each child of an individual with heterozygous <i>HEPACAM</i>-related improving MLC has a 50% chance of inheriting the pathogenic variant. Rarely, improving MLC is due to <a>biallelic</a> pathogenic variants in <i>AQP4</i> and is inherited in an <a>autosomal recessive</a> manner.</div></li></ul><p>Once the MLC-related <a>pathogenic variant</a>(s) have been identified in an affected family member, prenatal and <a>preimplantation genetic testing</a> for MLC are possible.</p></div></div><div><h2><i>GeneReview</i> Scope</h2><div><div><p>Megalencephalic Leukoencephalopathy with Subcortical Cysts (MLC): Included Phenotypes</p></div><p><span><a>View in own window</a></span></p><div><table><thead><tr><th colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="col">Phenotype</th><th colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="col">Gene <sup>1</sup></th><th colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="col">Locus Name</th><th colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="col">MOI</th><th colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="col">Clinical Characteristics</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td colspan="1" rowspan="3" scope="row">

<b>Clinical findings</b>
</p><ul><li><div>Macrocephaly with onset that is either <a>congenital</a> or within the first year of life</div></li><li><div>Normal or mildly delayed early development</div></li><li><div>Slow deterioration of motor functions with cerebellar ataxia and mild spasticity</div></li><li><div>Seizures</div></li><li><div>Dysarthria</div></li><li><div>Decline in cognitive function (occurs later and is typically milder than motor decline)</div></li><li><div>Behavioral problems in some individuals</div></li><li><div>Temporary exacerbation of signs and symptoms after minor head trauma</div></li></ul><p><b>Imaging findings.</b> Brain MRI features include the following abnormalities (see <a>Figure 1</a>):</p><div><a><img/></a><div><h4><a>Figure 1. </a></h4><p>Brain images of an individual with MLC (A, C) and an unaffected individual (B, D) A. Transverse T<sub>2</sub>-weighted image of a child age nine years with MLC, showing diffusely abnormal and mildly swollen white matter</p></div></div><ul><li><div>Diffusely abnormal and mildly swollen cerebral hemispheric white matter</div></li><li><div>Subcortical cysts are almost invariably present in the anterior temporal region and often in the frontoparietal regions.</div></li><li><div>Over time, white matter swelling decreases and cerebral atrophy ensues. The subcortical cysts may increase in size and number. In some individuals, the cysts become very large, occupying a large part of the frontoparietal white matter. In others, the white matter abnormalities decrease over time, and signal intensities become less abnormal.</div></li><li><div>Diffusion-weighted imaging reveals increased diffusivity of abnormal white matter [<a>Itoh et al 2006</a>, <a>van der Voorn et al 2006</a>].</div></li></ul><p><b>Family history.</b> Family history is most often consistent with <a>autosomal recessive</a> inheritance (e.g., affected sibs and/or parental <a>consanguinity</a>). Rarely, a <a>proband</a> has a <a><i>de novo</i></a> <a>pathogenic variant</a> and represents a <a>simplex</a> case. Absence of a known family history does not preclude the diagnosis.</p></div><div><h4>Improving MLC</h4><p>Improving MLC should be suspected in individuals with the following clinical and brain imaging findings and family history.</p><p>
<b>Clinical findings</b>
</p><ul><li><div>Macrocephaly with onset that is either <a>congenital</a> or within the first year of life; macrocephaly may persist or head size may be in the normal range in older individuals</div></li><li><div>Normal or mildly delayed early development</div></li><li><div>Motor function improves after the first year of life (clumsiness and hypotonia may persist)</div></li><li><div>Seizures in some individuals</div></li><li><div>Intellectual disability (with or without autism spectrum disorder) or normal cognitive function</div></li><li><div>No regression of mental or motor functions</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Imaging findings</b>
</p><ul><li><div>Brain MRI abnormalities within the first year of life are similar to those seen in the classic MLC <a>phenotype</a>, but cerebellar white matter is usually normal.</div></li><li><div>Striking improvement occurs over time. Brain MRI may appear normal within a few years and no longer diagnostic of MLC. In some individuals, minor frontal and temporal subcortical white matter abnormalities and anterior temporal cysts persist.</div></li></ul><p><b>Family history.</b> Family history often suggests <a>autosomal dominant</a> inheritance (e.g., affected males and females in multiple generations) or, less commonly, a <a>proband</a> has a <a><i>de novo</i></a> <a>pathogenic variant</a> and represents a <a>simplex</a> case. Rarely, the family history may suggest <a>autosomal recessive</a> inheritance. Absence of a known family history does not preclude the diagnosis.</p></div></div><div><h3>Establishing the Diagnosis</h3><p>The clinical diagnosis of megalencephalic leukoencephalopathy with subcortical cysts (MLC) can be <b>established</b> in a <a>proband</a> based on the presence of clinical and imaging findings detailed in <a>Suggestive Findings</a>.</p><p>The molecular diagnosis of <b>classic MLC</b> is <b>established</b> in a <a>proband</a> with <a>suggestive findings</a> and <a>biallelic</a> pathogenic (or <a>likely pathogenic</a>) variants in <i>MLC1</i> or <i>HEPACAM</i> or a <a>heterozygous</a> pathogenic (or likely pathogenic) variant in <i>GPRC5B</i> identified by <a>molecular genetic testing</a>.</p><p>The molecular diagnosis of <b>improving MLC</b> is <b>established</b> in a <a>proband</a> with <a>suggestive findings</a> and a <a>heterozygous</a> pathogenic (or <a>likely pathogenic</a>) variant in <i>HEPACAM</i> or <a>biallelic</a> pathogenic (or likely pathogenic) variants in <i>AQP4</i> identified by <a>molecular genetic testing</a>.</p><p>Note: (1) Per ACMG/AMP variant interpretation guidelines, the terms "<a>pathogenic variant</a>" and "<a>likely pathogenic</a> variant" are synonymous in a clinical setting, meaning that both are considered diagnostic and can be used for clinical decision making [<a>Richards et al 2015</a>]. Reference to "pathogenic variants" in this section is understood to include likely pathogenic variants. (2) Identification of variant(s) of <a>uncertain significance</a> does not establish or rule out the diagnosis.</p><p>Molecular genetic testing approaches can include a combination of <b><a>gene</a>-targeted testing</b> (<a>multigene panel</a>) and <b>comprehensive</b>
<b><a>genomic</a> testing</b> (<a>exome sequencing</a>, <a>genome sequencing</a>). Gene-targeted testing requires that the clinician determine which gene(s) are likely involved (see <a>Option 1</a>), whereas comprehensive genomic testing does not (see <a>Option 2</a>).</p><div><h4>Option 1</h4><p><b>A <a>multigene panel</a></b> that includes some or all of the genes listed in <a>Table 1</a> and other genes of interest (see <a>Differential Diagnosis</a>) is most likely to identify the genetic cause of the condition while limiting identification of variants of <a>uncertain significance</a> and pathogenic variants in genes that do not explain the underlying <a>phenotype</a>. Note: (1) The genes included in the panel and the diagnostic <a>sensitivity</a> of the testing used for each <a>gene</a> vary by laboratory and are likely to change over time. (2) Some multigene panels may include genes not associated with the condition discussed in this <i>GeneReview</i>. (3) In some laboratories, panel options may include a custom laboratory-designed panel and/or custom phenotype-focused <a>exome</a> analysis that includes genes specified by the clinician. (4) Methods used in a panel may include <a>sequence analysis</a>, <a>deletion/duplication analysis</a>, and/or other non-sequencing-based tests.</p><p>Note: If no <a>pathogenic variant</a>(s) are identified in any of the genes listed in <a>Table 1</a> in an individual with a clinical diagnosis of MLC, <a>cDNA</a> analysis of <i>MLC1</i> should be considered next to detect deep <a>intronic</a> variants (see <a>Chapter Notes</a> for research testing options).</p><p>For an introduction to multigene panels click <a>here</a>. Gene-targeted deletion/duplication testing will detect deletions ranging from a single exon to the whole gene; however, breakpoints of large deletions and/or deletion of adjacent genes (as seen with <i>MLC1</i>) may not be detected by these methods. Exome and <a>genome sequencing</a> may be able to detect deletions/duplications using breakpoint detection or read depth; however, <a>sensitivity</a> can be lower than gene-targeted deletion/duplication analysis.</p></div></dd><dt>6. </dt><dd><div><p>Data derived from the subscription-based professional view of Human Gene Mutation Database [<a>Stenson et al 2020</a>]</p></div></dd><dt>7. </dt><dd><div><p><i>De novo</i> <a>heterozygous</a> <i>GPRC5B</i> variants were identified in three unrelated individuals with classic MLC, and <a>biallelic</a> variants in <i>AQP4</i> were identified in one family with improving MLC [<a>Passchier et al 2023</a>].</p></div></dd><dt>8. </dt><dd><div><p>A fraction of these variants are in a splice junction (most are within the canonical <a>splice site</a>, although deep <a>intronic</a> variants have also been reported).</p></div></dd><dt>9. </dt><dd><div><p>No large intragenic deletions/duplications have been reported in individuals with <i>HEPACAM-</i>related MLC [<a>Stenson et al 2020</a>]. To date, three individuals with heterozygous pathogenic variants in <i>GPRC5B</i> and classic MLC as well as two individuals with a biallelic <a>pathogenic variant</a> in <i>AQP4</i> and improving MLC have been identified [<a>Passchier et al 2023</a>]. The following description of the phenotypic features associated with this spectrum is based on these reports.</p><div><h3><span>Table 2. </span></h3><div><p>Megalencephalic Leukoencephalopathy with Subcortical Cysts (MLC): Comparison of Phenotypes by Select Features</p></div><p><span><a>View in own window</a></span></p><div><table><thead><tr><th colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="col">Feature</th><th colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="col">Classic MLC</th><th colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="col">Improving MLC</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="row">Macrocephaly, typically persistent</td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">~95%</td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">~55%</td></tr><tr><td colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="row">Motor delays, initial</td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">~30%</td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">~15%</td></tr><tr><td colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="row">Ataxia</td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">~80%</td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">~20%</td></tr><tr><td colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="row">Pyramidal dysfunction</td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">~20%</td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">~5%</td></tr><tr><td colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="row">Cognitive delays</td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">~70%</td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">~30% (mild ID)</td></tr><tr><td colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="row">Autism spectrum disorder</td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">~10%</td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">~25%</td></tr><tr><td colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="row">Seizures</td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">~65%</td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">~10%</td></tr><tr><td colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="row">Brain MRI abnormalities</td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">100%</td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">Initially 100%, but typically followed by improvement or normalization</td></tr></tbody></table></div><div><div><dl><dt></dt><dd><div><p>Data based on <a>Hamilton et al [2018]</a></p></div></dd><dt></dt><dd><div><p>ID = intellectual disability; MLC = megalencephalic leukoencephalopathy with subcortical cysts</p></div></dd></dl></div></div></div><div><h4>Classic MLC</h4><p><b>Macrocephaly.</b> Macrocephaly can be present at birth but more frequently develops during the first year of life. The degree of macrocephaly is variable; it may be as great as four to six standard deviations greater than the mean in some affected individuals. After the first year of life, head growth rate normalizes, and growth follows a line above and parallel to the 98th centile.</p><p><b>Motor development.</b> Prior to age one year, motor development is normal in most infants and mildly delayed in some. Apart from progressive macrocephaly, the first clinical sign is usually delayed walking. Walking is often unstable, and the child falls frequently. However, most children can walk independently. Muscle tone tends to be low, apart from some ankle hypertonia.</p><p>Slow deterioration of motor function occurs over years with development of ataxia of the trunk and extremities. Signs of pyramidal dysfunction are late and minor and largely dominated by signs of cerebellar ataxia. Speech becomes increasingly dysarthric and dysphagia may develop. Deep tendon reflexes become brisk and Babinski signs become apparent. Some individuals display extrapyramidal movement abnormalities with dystonia and athetosis. Some individuals also develop tics [<a>Sugiura et al 2006</a>].</p><p>Gradually, the ability to walk independently is lost and many children become completely wheelchair dependent at the end of the first decade or in the second decade of life. Some children have a more severe clinical course and maintain their ability to walk independently for only a few years, or never achieve independent walking. Others maintain the ability to walk independently into the fifth decade of life.</p><p><b>Cognitive function.</b> Initial cognitive development is normal in most children and mildly delayed in some. Intellectual deterioration is late and often mild, but in 15% of individuals the decline is severe. Decreasing school performance becomes evident during later years of primary school. Behavioral problems are reported in 25%-30% of individuals. Autism or autistic features are seen in 5%-10% of individuals [<a>Sugiura et al 2006</a>, <a>Hamilton et al 2018</a>].</p><p><b>Seizures.</b> Approximately 75% of individuals with classic MLC experience at least one seizure before age 20 years [<a>Dubey et al 2018</a>, <a>Hamilton et al 2018</a>]. Seizure onset is typically early, often within a few years after birth. Approximately 60% of individuals develop epilepsy, which is usually easily controlled with anti-seizure medication [<a>Dubey et al 2018</a>]. Affected individuals (15%-20%) may experience one or more episodes of status epilepticus, the first of which typically occurs within a few years after seizure onset [<a>Dubey et al 2018</a>]. Seizures and status epilepticus are frequently precipitated by minor head trauma.</p><p><b>Minor head trauma</b> may induce temporary deterioration in some individuals, most often observed as seizures or status epilepticus, prolonged unconsciousness, or acute motor deterioration with gradual improvement [<a>Bugiani et al 2003</a>, <a>Dubey et al 2018</a>].</p><p><b>Prognosis.</b> Some children have a more benign clinical course and, even as teenagers, have macrocephaly only. Individuals who are ambulatory with or without support at age 15 years are most likely to remain ambulatory for decades [Authors, personal communication]. There are limited data regarding overall life span in this disorder. Some individuals have died in their teens or twenties; others are alive in their fifties.</p><p><b>Brain imaging findings.</b> Brain MRI findings seen in affected individuals include diffusely abnormal and mildly swollen cerebral hemispheric white matter. Subcortical cysts are almost invariably present in the anterior temporal region and often the frontoparietal region. Over time, white matter swelling decreases and cerebral atrophy ensues. Subcortical cysts may increase in size and number. In some individuals, the cysts become very large, occupying a large part of the frontoparietal white matter. In others, white matter abnormalities decrease over time, and the signal intensity becomes less abnormal. Diffusion-weighted imaging reveals increased diffusivity of abnormal white matter [<a>Itoh et al 2006</a>, <a>van der Voorn et al 2006</a>]. Notably, central white matter structures, including the corpus callosum, internal capsule, and brain stem, are better preserved than other structures, although they are not usually entirely normal. Cerebellar white matter usually has a mildly abnormal signal and is not swollen.</p><p><b>Neuropathologic examination.</b> Brain biopsy shows numerous vacuoles between the outer lamellae of myelin sheaths, suggesting splitting of these lamellae or incomplete compaction [<a>van der Knaap et al 1996</a>]. In addition, small vacuoles are observed in astrocytic endfeet [<a>Duarri et al 2011</a>].</p></div><div><h4>Improving MLC</h4><p>In children diagnosed with improving MLC due to <a>heterozygous</a> <a>gain-of-function</a> variants in <i>HEPACAM</i>, the initial disease course is the same as that in children with the classic <a>phenotype</a>. Early cognitive and motor development is normal in most individuals and mildly delayed in some.</p><p><b>Macrocephaly</b> is present at birth or (more commonly) develops within the first year of life in 90% of individuals. After the first year of life, head growth trajectory usually either decreases or follows a line above and parallel to the 98th centile. In 40%-50% of affected children, the head circumference normalizes [<a>Hamilton et al 2018</a>].</p><p><b>Motor development.</b> Apart from macrocephaly, the first clinical sign is usually delayed walking. Walking is often unstable, and the child falls frequently. After the second or third year of life, motor function improves or normalizes in most, and all children eventually achieve independent walking. Neurologic examination may become normal, but some children have persistent hypotonia and clumsiness. Regression does not occur.</p><p><b>Cognitive function</b> is normal in approximately 75% of individuals; 25% have mild intellectual disability [<a>Hamilton et al 2018</a>]. Behavioral problems are observed in 30% and autism spectrum disorder in 25% of individuals. Developmental regression has not been observed to date.</p><p><b>Seizures.</b> Epilepsy and status epilepticus may occur, but 90% of individuals have no history of seizures [<a>Hamilton et al 2018</a>].</p><p><b>Prognosis.</b> Information regarding average life span is very limited. One child died in status epilepticus at age three years [<a>Hamilton et al 2018</a>].</p><p>In families with multiple affected individuals in more than one generation, the <a>proband</a> is usually the child and the affected parent is subsequently diagnosed. Parents with a <a>heterozygous</a> <a>pathogenic variant</a> in <i>HEPACAM</i> often have macrocephaly or a history of childhood macrocephaly but normal motor and cognitive function. Some parents have cognitive or behavioral problems or motor clumsiness. Considering the normal health of many parents, it does not appear that heterozygous <i>HEPACAM</i>-related improving MLC is associated with a shortened life span; however, no formal long-term studies have been performed to date.</p><p>Only two sibs have been reported to date with <a>biallelic</a> pathogenic variants in <i>AQP4</i>, and limited prognosis information is available [<a>Passchier et al 2023</a>].</p></div></div><div><h3>Phenotype Correlations by Gene</h3><p><b>Classic MLC.</b> A review of 17 individuals with <a>biallelic</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related classic MLC revealed no phenotypic differences from individuals with <i>MLC1</i>-related classic MLC [<a>Hamilton et al 2018</a>].</p><p>The three individuals with <i>GPRC5B</i>-related classic MLC are phenotypically indistinguishable from individuals with classic MLC due to <a>biallelic</a> pathogenic variants in <i>MLC1</i> or <i>HEPACAM</i> [<a>Passchier et al 2023</a>].</p><p><b>Improving MLC.</b> Only two individuals (sibs) have been described to date with <a>biallelic</a> pathogenic variants in <i>AQP4</i> [<a>Passchier et al 2023</a>], so it is currently unclear whether <a>heterozygous</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related improving MLC and <i>AQP4</i>-related MLC are phenotypically distinguishable.</p></div><div><h3>Genotype-Phenotype Correlations</h3><p><b><i>AQP4</i>.</b> Two sibs with <a>biallelic</a> <i>AQP4</i> pathogenic variants have been reported with improving MLC similar to <a>heterozygous</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related improving MLC [<a>Passchier et al 2023</a>].</p><p><b><i>GPRC5B.</i></b> Three unrelated individuals with <a><i>de novo</i></a> <a>heterozygous</a> pathogenic variants in <i>GPRC5B</i> have been described [<a>Passchier et al 2023</a>]. For these individuals, brain MRI presentation and clinical course are indistinguishable from <i>MLC1</i>-related classic MLC and <a>biallelic</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related classic MLC.</p><p><b><i>HEPACAM</i>.</b> Most known <a>biallelic</a> and <a>heterozygous</a> <i>HEPACAM</i> pathogenic variants affect the extracellular or transmembrane domains of the protein [<a>López-Hernández et al 2011</a>, <a>Elorza-Vidal et al 2020</a>].</p><ul><li><div>In <a>biallelic</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related classic MLC, pathogenic variants in <i>HEPACAM</i> are widely distributed over the entire extracellular and transmembrane region of the protein, whereas in <a>heterozygous</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related improving MLC, pathogenic variants are clustered in the first extracellular immunoglobulin-like <a>domain</a> [<a>López-Hernández et al 2011</a>, <a>Elorza-Vidal et al 2020</a>].</div></li><li><div><i>HEPACAM</i> pathogenic variants associated with <a>autosomal recessive</a> classic MLC and <i>HEPACAM</i> pathogenic variants associated with <a>autosomal dominant</a> improving MLC do not overlap, although they may affect the same residue [<a>López-Hernández et al 2011</a>]. At present, it is unclear why some <i>HEPACAM</i> pathogenic variants are associated with autosomal recessive classic MLC and others with autosomal dominant improving MLC. Notably, only <a>missense</a> variants in <i>HEPACAM</i> have been reported in improving MLC [<a>López-Hernández et al 2011</a>, <a>van der Knaap et al 2012</a>, <a>Elorza-Vidal et al 2020</a>].</div></li></ul><p><b><i>MLC1</i>.</b> A review of 187 individuals with <a>biallelic</a> <i>MLC1</i> pathogenic variants revealed that disease severity and clinical course can vary significantly in individuals from the same family [<a>Hamilton et al 2018</a>]. There are no known <a>genotype-phenotype correlations</a> to date.</p></div><div><h3>Nomenclature</h3><p>Subtypes of megalencephalic leukoencephalopathy with subcortical cysts (MLC) were previously named according to the genetic loci associated with each subtype:</p><ul><li><div>

The penetrance of <a>biallelic</a> pathogenic <i>AQP4</i> variants is unknown.</p></div><div><h3>Prevalence</h3><p>There are several populations where founder variants in MLC-associated genes have been identified (see <a>Table 8</a>).</p><p>Founder variants in <i>MLC1</i> have been identified in East Indian individuals from the Agrawal community [<a>Leegwater et al 2002</a>, <a>Singhal et al 2003</a>, <a>Gorospe et al 2004</a>], Libyan and Turkish Jewish individuals [<a>Ben-Zeev et al 2002</a>], Korean individuals [<a>Choi et al 2017</a>], and Egyptian individuals [<a>Abdel-Salam et al 2016</a>].</p></div></div><div><h2>Genetically Related (Allelic) Disorders</h2><p>Associations of <i>MLC1</i> variants with catatonic schizophrenia have been reported. For some individuals with Jacobsen syndrome, MLC-like white matter abnormalities on MRI have been described [<a>Yamamoto et al 2015</a>]. In the US, early intervention is a federally funded program available in all states that provides in-home services to target individual therapy needs.</p><p><b>Ages 3-5 years.</b> In the US, developmental preschool through the local public school district is recommended. Before placement, an evaluation is made to determine needed services and therapies and an individualized education plan (IEP) is developed for those who qualify based on established motor, language, social, or cognitive delay. The early intervention program typically assists with this transition. Developmental preschool is center based; for children too medically unstable to attend, home-based services are provided.</p><p><b>All ages.</b> Consultation with a developmental pediatrician is recommended to ensure the involvement of appropriate community, state, and educational agencies (US) and to support parents in maximizing quality of life. Some issues to consider:</p><ul><li><div>IEP services:</div><ul><li><div>An IEP provides specially designed instruction and related services to children who qualify.</div></li><li><div>IEP services will be reviewed annually to determine whether any changes are needed.</div></li><li><div>Special education law requires that children participating in an IEP be in the least restrictive environment feasible at school and included in general education as much as possible, when and where appropriate.</div></li><li><div>PT, OT, and speech services will be provided in the IEP to the extent that the need affects the child's access to academic material. Beyond that, private supportive therapies based on the affected individual's needs may be considered. Specific recommendations regarding type of therapy can be made by a developmental pediatrician.</div></li><li><div>As a child enters the teen years, a transition plan should be discussed and incorporated in the IEP. For those receiving IEP services, the public school district is required to provide services until age 21.</div></li></ul></li><li><div>A 504 plan (Section 504: a US federal statute that prohibits discrimination based on disability) can be considered for those who require accommodations or modifications such as front-of-class seating, assistive technology devices, classroom scribes, extra time between classes, modified assignments, and enlarged text.</div></li><li><div>Developmental Disabilities Administration (DDA) enrollment is recommended. DDA is a US public agency that provides services and support to qualified individuals. Eligibility differs by state but is typically determined by diagnosis and/or associated cognitive/adaptive disabilities.</div></li><li><div>Families with limited income and resources may also qualify for supplemental security income (SSI) for their child with a disability.</div></li></ul></div><div><h4>Motor Dysfunction</h4><p>
<b>Gross motor dysfunction</b>
</p><ul><li><div>Physical therapy is recommended to maximize mobility and to reduce the risk for later-onset orthopedic complications (e.g., contractures, scoliosis, hip dislocation).</div></li><li><div>Consider use of durable medical equipment and positioning devices as needed (e.g., wheelchairs, walkers, bath chairs, orthotics, adaptive strollers).</div></li></ul><p><b>Fine motor dysfunction.</b> Occupational therapy is recommended for difficulty with fine motor skills that affect adaptive function such as feeding, grooming, dressing, and writing.</p><p><b>Communication issues.</b> Consider evaluation for alternative means of communication (e.g., <a>augmentative and alternative communication</a> [AAC]) for individuals who have expressive language difficulties. An AAC evaluation can be completed by a speech-language pathologist who has expertise in the area. The evaluation will consider cognitive abilities and sensory impairments to determine the most appropriate form of communication. AAC devices can range from low-tech, such as picture exchange communication, to high-tech, such as voice-generating devices. Contrary to popular belief, AAC devices do not hinder verbal development of speech, but rather support optimal speech and language development.</p><p><b>Social/behavioral difficulties.</b> Children may qualify for and benefit from interventions used in treatment of autism spectrum disorder, including applied behavior analysis (ABA). ABA therapy is individualized therapy targeted to each child's behavioral, social, and adaptive strengths and weaknesses and is typically performed one on one with a board-certified behavior analyst. Consultation with a developmental pediatrician may be helpful in guiding parents through appropriate behavioral management strategies or providing prescription medications, such as medication used to treat attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, when necessary. Concerns about serious aggressive or destructive behavior can be addressed by a pediatric psychiatrist.</p></div></div><div><h3>Surveillance</h3><p>To monitor existing manifestations, the individual's response to supportive care, and the emergence of new manifestations, the evaluations summarized in <a>Table 6</a> are recommended.</p><div><h3><span>Table 6. </span></h3><div><p>Megalencephalic Leukoencephalopathy with Subcortical Cysts: Recommended Surveillance</p></div><p><span><a>View in own window</a></span></p><div><table><thead><tr><th colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="col">System/Concern</th><th colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="col">Evaluation</th><th colspan="1" rowspan="1" scope="col">Frequency</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td colspan="1" rowspan="3" scope="row">

Note: There may not be clinical trials for this disorder.</p></div></div><div><h2>Genetic Counseling</h2><p><i>Genetic counseling is the process of providing individuals and families with information on the nature, mode(s) of inheritance, and implications of genetic disorders to help them make informed medical and personal decisions. The following section deals with genetic risk assessment and the use of family history and genetic testing to clarify genetic status for family members; it is not meant to address all personal, cultural, or ethical issues that may arise or to substitute for consultation with a genetics professional</i>. —ED.</p><div><h3>Mode of Inheritance</h3><p><b>Classic megalencephalic leukoencephalopathy with subcortical cysts (MLC)</b> is most commonly caused by <a>biallelic</a> pathogenic variants in <i>MLC1</i> or <i>HEPACAM</i> and inherited in an <a>autosomal recessive</a> manner. Rarely, classic MLC occurs as an <a>autosomal dominant</a> disorder caused by a <a><i>de novo</i></a> <a>heterozygous</a> <i>GPRC5B</i> <a>pathogenic variant</a>.</p><p><b>Improving MLC</b> most commonly occurs as an <a>autosomal dominant</a> disorder caused by an inherited or <a><i>de novo</i></a> <a>heterozygous</a> <a>pathogenic variant</a> in <i>HEPACAM</i>. Rarely, improving MLC is caused by <a>biallelic</a> pathogenic variants in <i>AQP4</i> and inherited in an <a>autosomal recessive</a> manner.</p><p><b>Note:</b>
<i>HEPACAM</i> pathogenic variants associated with classic MLC and <i>HEPACAM</i> pathogenic variants associated with improving MLC do not overlap, although they may affect the same residue [<a>López-Hernández et al 2011</a>]. In a few individuals with a <a>pathogenic variant</a> associated with <a>autosomal dominant</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related improving MLC on one <a>allele</a> and a pathogenic variant associated with <a>autosomal recessive</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related classic MLC on the other allele, classic MLC was observed [Authors, personal communication]. In these individuals, risk to offspring depends on the transmitted pathogenic variant: offspring who inherit the allele associated with <a>heterozygous</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related improving MLC may be affected and offspring who inherit the allele associated with <a>biallelic</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related classic MLC will be asymptomatic carriers.</p></div><div><h3>Autosomal Recessive Inheritance – Risk to Family Members</h3><p>
<b>Parents of a <a>proband</a></b>
</p><ul><li><div>The parents of a child with <a>autosomal recessive</a> MLC (i.e., a child with <i>MLC1</i>-related classic MLC, <a>biallelic</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related classic MLC, or <i>AQP4</i>-related improving MLC) are typically <a>heterozygous</a> for an autosomal recessive MLC-related <a>pathogenic variant</a>.</div></li><li><div>If a molecular diagnosis has been established in the <a>proband</a>, <a>molecular genetic testing</a> is recommended for the parents of the proband to confirm that both parents are <a>heterozygous</a> for an <a>autosomal recessive</a> MLC-related <a>pathogenic variant</a> and to allow reliable <a>recurrence risk</a> assessment.</div></li><li><div>If a <a>pathogenic variant</a> is detected in only one parent and parental identity testing has confirmed biological maternity and paternity, it is possible that one of the pathogenic variants identified in the <a>proband</a> occurred as a <a><i>de novo</i></a> event in the proband [<a>López-Hernández et al 2011</a>] or as a <a>postzygotic</a> <i>de novo</i> event in a mosaic parent. If the proband appears to have <a>homozygous</a> pathogenic variants (i.e., the same two pathogenic variants), additional possibilities to consider include:</div><ul><li><div>A single- or multiexon <a>deletion</a> in the <a>proband</a> that was not detected by <a>sequence analysis</a> and that resulted in the artifactual appearance of homozygosity;</div></li><li><div>Uniparental isodisomy for the parental <a>chromosome</a> with the <a>pathogenic variant</a> that resulted in homozygosity for the pathogenic variant in the <a>proband</a>.</div></li></ul></li><li><div>Individuals who are <a>heterozygous</a> for an <i>MLC1</i> <a>pathogenic variant</a> are typically asymptomatic.</div></li><li><div>Individuals who are <a>heterozygous</a> for a <i>HEPACAM</i> <a>pathogenic variant</a> associated with classic MLC are asymptomatic. <i>HEPACAM</i> pathogenic variants associated with <a>autosomal dominant</a> improving MLC do not overlap with <i>HEPACAM</i> pathogenic variants associated with <a>autosomal recessive</a> classic MLC (see <a>Genotype-Phenotype Correlations</a>; also see <b>Note</b> under <a>Mode of Inheritance</a>).</div></li><li><div>Individuals who are <a>heterozygous</a> for an <i>AQP4</i> <a>pathogenic variant</a> are asymptomatic to date.</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Sibs of a <a>proband</a></b>
</p><ul><li><div>If both parents are known to be <a>heterozygous</a> for a <a>pathogenic variant</a> associated with <a>autosomal recessive</a> MLC, each sib of an affected individual has at conception a 25% chance of inheriting <a>biallelic</a> pathogenic variants and being affected, a 50% chance of being heterozygous, and a 25% chance of inheriting neither of the <a>familial</a> pathogenic variants.</div></li><li><div>Intrafamilial variability has been observed in <i>MLC1</i>-related classic MLC. A review of 187 individuals with <a>biallelic</a> <i>MLC1</i> pathogenic variants revealed that in individuals from the same family, disease severity and clinical course can vary significantly [<a>Hamilton et al 2018</a>].</div></li><li><div>Individuals who are <a>heterozygous</a> for an <i>MLC1</i> <a>pathogenic variant</a> are typically asymptomatic.</div></li><li><div>Individuals who are <a>heterozygous</a> for a <i>HEPACAM</i> <a>pathogenic variant</a> associated with classic MLC are asymptomatic. <i>HEPACAM</i> pathogenic variants associated with <a>autosomal dominant</a> improving MLC do not overlap with <i>HEPACAM</i> pathogenic variants associated with <a>autosomal recessive</a> classic MLC (see <a>Genotype-Phenotype Correlations</a>; also see <b>Note</b> under <a>Mode of Inheritance</a>).</div></li><li><div>Individuals who are <a>heterozygous</a> for an <i>AQP4</i> <a>pathogenic variant</a> are asymptomatic to date.</div></li></ul><p><b>Offspring of a <a>proband</a>.</b> The offspring of an individual with <a>autosomal recessive</a> MLC (i.e., <i>MLC1</i>-related classic MLC, <a>biallelic</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related classic MLC, or <i>AQP4</i>-related improving MLC) are obligate heterozygotes (carriers) for a <a>pathogenic variant</a>.</p><p><b>Other family members.</b> Each sib of the <a>proband</a>'s parents is at a 50% risk of being a <a>carrier</a> of a <a>pathogenic variant</a> associated with <a>autosomal recessive</a> MLC.</p><div><h4>Carrier Detection</h4><p>Carrier testing for at-risk relatives requires prior identification of the MLC-related pathogenic variants in the family.</p><p><b>Note:</b> The parents of many individuals with classic MLC are <a>consanguineous</a>. Carrier testing for reproductive partners of known carriers is appropriate, particularly if <a>consanguinity</a> is likely.</p></div></div><div><h3>Autosomal Dominant Inheritance – Risk to Family Members</h3><p>
<b>Parents of a <a>proband</a></b>
</p><ul><li><div>Many individuals with <a>heterozygous</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related improving MLC have a parent with macrocephaly or a history of childhood macrocephaly. Given the neurologic improvement that is observed in this <a>phenotype</a>, the parent is not at risk of neurologic regression. Whether having a heterozygous <a>gain-of-function</a> <i>HEPACAM</i> <a>pathogenic variant</a> leads to other manifestations late in life has not been investigated; however, to date no evidence suggests disease progression later in life.</div></li><li><div>A <a>proband</a> with <a>heterozygous</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related improving MLC may have the disorder as the result of a <a><i>de novo</i></a>
<i>HEPACAM</i> <a>pathogenic variant</a>. The proportion of individuals with <i>HEPACAM</i>-related improving MLC caused by a <i>de novo</i>
<i>HEPACAM</i> pathogenic variant is approximately 20%.</div></li><li><div>In the three individuals reported to date with <i>GPRC5B</i>-related classic MLC, the <a>heterozygous</a> pathogenic <i>GPRC5B</i> variant arose <a><i>de novo</i></a>.</div></li><li><div>If the <a>proband</a> appears to be the only affected family member (i.e., a <a>simplex</a> case), <a>molecular genetic testing</a> is recommended for the parents of the proband to confirm their genetic status and to allow reliable <a>recurrence risk</a> counseling.</div></li><li><div>If the <a>pathogenic variant</a> identified in the <a>proband</a> is not identified in either parent and parental identity testing has confirmed biological maternity and paternity, the following possibilities should be considered:</div><ul><li><div>The <a>proband</a> has a <a><i>de novo</i></a> <a>pathogenic variant</a>.</div></li><li><div>The <a>proband</a> inherited a <a>pathogenic variant</a> from a parent with <a>germline</a> (or somatic and germline) <a>mosaicism</a>. Note: Testing of parental leukocyte DNA may not detect all instances of <a>somatic mosaicism</a> and will not detect a pathogenic variant that is present in the germ cells only.</div></li></ul></li><li><div>The family history of some individuals diagnosed with <a>heterozygous</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related improving MLC may appear to be negative because of failure to recognize the disorder in family members, reduced <a>penetrance</a>, or milder phenotypic presentation. Therefore, an apparently negative family history cannot be confirmed unless <a>molecular genetic testing</a> has demonstrated that neither parent is heterozygous for the <a>pathogenic variant</a> identified in the <a>proband</a>.</div></li></ul><p><b>Sibs of a <a>proband</a>.</b> The risk to the sibs of the proband depends on the genetic status of the proband's parents:</p><ul><li><div>If a parent of the <a>proband</a> is affected and/or is known to have the <a>autosomal dominant</a> MLC-related <a>pathogenic variant</a> identified in the proband, the risk to the sibs of inheriting the variant is 50%.</div></li><li><div>If the <a>heterozygous</a> <i>HEPACAM</i> or <i>GPRC5B</i> <a>pathogenic variant</a> found in the <a>proband</a> cannot be detected in the leukocyte DNA of either parent, the <a>recurrence risk</a> to sibs is estimated to be 1% because of the theoretic possibility of parental <a>germline mosaicism</a> [<a>Rahbari et al 2016</a>].</div></li><li><div>If the parents have not been tested for the <a>heterozygous</a> <i>HEPACAM</i> or <i>GPRC5B</i> <a>pathogenic variant</a> but are clinically unaffected, the sibs are still at increased risk for heterozygous <i>HEPACAM</i>-related improving MLC or <i>GPRC5B</i>-related classic MLC because of the possibility of reduced <a>penetrance</a> in a heterozygous parent or the theoretic possibility of parental <a>germline mosaicism</a>.</div></li></ul><p><b>Offspring of a <a>proband</a>.</b> Each child of an individual with <a>autosomal dominant</a> MLC (i.e., <a>heterozygous</a> <i>HEPACAM</i>-related improving MLC or <i>GPRC5B</i>-related classic MLC) has a 50% chance of inheriting the <a>pathogenic variant</a>.</p><p><b>Other family members.</b> The risk to other family members depends on the status of the <a>proband</a>'s parents: if a parent has the <a>heterozygous</a> <i>HEPACAM</i> or <i>GPRC5B</i> <a>pathogenic variant</a>, the parent's family members may be at risk.</p></div><div><h3>Related Genetic Counseling Issues</h3><p>
<b>Family planning</b>
</p><ul><li><div>The optimal time for determination of genetic risk and discussion of the availability of prenatal/<a>preimplantation genetic testing</a> is before pregnancy.</div></li><li><div>It is appropriate to offer <a>genetic counseling</a> (including discussion of potential risks to offspring and reproductive options) to young adults who are affected or are carriers (or are at risk of being carriers) of MLC-related pathogenic variants.</div></li></ul><p><b>DNA banking.</b> Because it is likely that testing methodology and our understanding of genes, pathogenic mechanisms, and diseases will improve in the future, consideration should be given to banking DNA from probands in whom a molecular diagnosis has not been confirmed (i.e., the causative pathogenic mechanism is unknown). <a>613926</a></td><td colspan="1" rowspan="1">MEGALENCEPHALIC LEUKOENCEPHALOPATHY WITH SUBCORTICAL CYSTS 2B, REMITTING, WITH OR WITHOUT IMPAIRED INTELLECTUAL DEVELOPMENT; MLC2B</td></tr></tbody></table></div></div><div><h3>Molecular Pathogenesis</h3><p><b><i>MLC1.</i></b> Membrane protein MLC1 (MLC1) is highly expressed in the human brain, as well as in leukocytes. Within the human brain, MLC1 is almost exclusively expressed in astrocytic endfeet in the perivascular, subependymal, and subpial regions. The localization of the protein, combined with the clinical <a>phenotype</a> of megalencephalic leukoencephalopathy with subcortical cysts (MLC), suggests a role for MLC1 in brain ion and water homeostasis [<a>van der Knaap et al 2012</a>]. MLC1 shares its endfeet localization with members of the dystrophin glycoprotein-associated complex, including aquaporin-4 [<a>Boor et al 2007</a>]. MLC1 has been shown to interact with a variety of proteins potentially involved in brain ion and water homeostasis. These include the Na<sup>+</sup>/K<sup>+</sup>-ATPase, TRPV4, caveolin-1, and Kir4.1, among others [<a>Brignone et al 2015</a>].</p><p><i>MLC1</i> pathogenic variants show a low steady-state expression of MLC1 with a consequent reduction in surface expression [<a>Teijido et al 2004</a>, <a>Montagna et al 2006</a>]. Electrophysiologic studies reveal a reduction of volume-regulated anion channel (VRAC) activity in lymphoblasts from individuals with <a>biallelic</a> <i>MLC1</i> pathogenic variants. This results in impaired regulatory volume decrease upon hypotonic cell swelling [<a>Ridder et al 2011</a>]. Cell lines overexpressing normal MLC1 show enhanced VRAC activity, which is absent upon overexpression of mutated MLC1, supporting the role of MLC1 in VRAC regulation [<a>Ridder et al 2011</a>].</p><p><b><i>HEPACAM</i></b> encodes hepatic and glial cell adhesion molecule (called either hepaCAM or glialCAM), a single transmembrane protein with two extracellular immunoglobulin domains that is predominantly expressed in the brain. GlialCAM colocalizes with MLC1 in astrocyte endfeet. However, glialCAM is also expressed in axons, on the outside of myelin sheaths, and in oligodendrocytes [<a>Favre-Kontula et al 2008</a>, <a>López-Hernández et al 2011</a>]. GlialCAM acts as a chaperone for MLC1, ensuring the localization of both proteins at astrocyte-astrocyte junctions in endfeet and in cultured astrocytes [<a>López-Hernández et al 2011</a>, <a>Capdevila-Nortes et al 2013</a>]. GlialCAM also acts as an auxiliary subunit for the chloride channel ClC-2. It increases ClC-2 mediated currents, changes their functional properties, and is necessary for ClC-2 localization to astrocyte-astrocyte junctions [<a>Jeworutzki et al 2012</a>, <a>Hoegg-Beiler et al 2014</a>]. GlialCAM also interacts with the gap junction subunit connexin 43 [<a>Wu et al 2016</a>].</p><p>All MLC-causing variants in <i>HEPACAM</i> affect the extracellular region of glialCAM. In cultured rat primary astrocytes, mutated glialCAM with either a dominant or a recessive <a>pathogenic variant</a> disrupts localization of MLC1 and glialCAM at astrocyte-astrocyte junctions. Coexpression of <a>wild type</a> glialCAM rescues the detrimental effect of glialCAM with a recessive variant on MLC1 localization, but it does not rescue the effect of the dominant variant of glialCAM [<a>López-Hernández et al 2011</a>]. Biochemical analysis shows that some dominant and recessive pathogenic variants disrupt the ability of glialCAM to homo-oligomerize [<a>Arnedo et al 2014</a>]. However, what causes a <i>HEPACAM</i> variant to be dominant vs recessive is still unclear.</p><p><b><i>GPRC5B</i></b> encodes G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR) family C group 5 member B (GPRC5B). In the brain, GPRC5B is highly expressed in perivascular astrocyte endfeet, a location shared with MLC1 and glialCAM [<a>Alonso-Gardón et al 2021</a>, <a>Passchier et al 2023</a>]. GPCRs can be activated by different types of chemical or mechanical stimuli, or by interaction with other proteins. GPRC5B is considered an orphan receptor since its ligand has not been identified. An interaction of GPRC5B with MLC1 and glialCAM has been described [<a>Alonso-Gardón et al 2021</a>]. Volume regulation in lymphoblasts from GPRC5B patients is disrupted [<a>Passchier et al 2023</a>]. Knockdown of GPRC5B in astrocytes reduces VRAC activity [<a>Alonso-Gardón et al 2021</a>], while overexpression in astrocytoma cells increases VRAC activity [<a>Passchier et al 2023</a>]. Therefore, expression patterns and cellular mechanisms linked to GPRC5B overlap with those of MLC1 and glialCAM. The two dominant pathogenic variants that have been identified in <i>GPRC5B</i> are duplications of two neighboring amino acids in the fourth transmembrane region of the receptor (p.Ile176dup and p.Ala177dup) [<a>Passchier et al 2023</a>]. How these variants alter the function of the receptor is currently unknown.</p><p><b><i>AQP4</i></b> encodes the ubiquitous brain water channel aquaporin-4. Like the other proteins involved in MLC, aquaporin-4 is highly expressed in perivascular astrocyte endfeet and at other brain-fluid interfaces. At these locations, aquaporin-4, similar to MLC1, is part of the dystrophin glycoprotein-associated complex [<a>Boor et al 2007</a>]. The presence of large numbers of water channels at astrocyte endfeet enables fast water exchange between the extracellular space and the interconnected network of astrocytes and other glial cells [<a>Nagelhus &amp; Ottersen 2013</a>]. These combined features increase the ability of astrocytes to rapidly adjust cell volume and increases their capacity for ion and water homeostasis, but simultaneously makes them more prone to pathologic swelling in situations of acute edema formation. Modulation of aquaporin-4 function or localization is being investigated as potential therapeutic strategy for the treatment of acute brain edema [<a>Kitchen et al 2020</a>].</p><p>The recessive <a>pathogenic variant</a> p.Ala215Thr in aquaporin-4 alters the second of two highly conserved Asn-Pro-Ala (NPA) motifs that form the narrow central part of the water channel. These motifs are crucial for selective water permeability and for localization of the channel to the plasma membrane [<a>King et al 2004</a>]. The p.Ala215Thr variant leads to protein misfolding, resulting in loss of membrane localization and water channel function [<a>Passchier et al 2023</a>].</p><p>
