# A systematic review and meta-analysis of air pollution and increased risk of frailty.
**Authors:** Jafari Z, Andrew M, Rockwood K
**Journal:** Age Ageing (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1093/ageing/afaf129](https://doi.org/10.1093/ageing/afaf129)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Environmental air pollution is increasingly recognised as a potential contributor to frailty. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesise existing evidence on the associations between environmental air pollution and frailty in middle-aged and older adults, providing insights into the impact of air pollution on public health.

METHODS: The systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Statement 2020. Four electronic databases were searched without restrictions on language, publication status, or year of publication.

RESULTS: Of the 145 publications identified through the systematic search, 18 were included. Meta-analyses indicated a 19% increased risk of frailty due to air pollution (fine particulate matter ≤2.5 microns) [n = 9 studies; pooled odds ratio (OR) 1.19; 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.10-1.27], a 28% increase with exposure to household solid fuels (n = 4 studies; OR 1.28; 95% CI 1.16-1.40) and a 59% increase due to exposure to secondhand smoke (n = 3 studies; OR 1.59; 95% CI 0.46-2.72). Except for the meta-analysis on air pollution, no heterogeneity or risk of publication bias was observed amongst the included studies. The Joanna Briggs Institute checklist confirmed high methodological quality across all included studies.

CONCLUSIONS: Environmental exposures, including air pollution, the use of unclean household fuels and exposure to secondhand smoke, significantly increase the risk of frailty. These findings underscore the urgent need to raise awareness and establish effective public health strategies to reduce these environmental risks and associated frailty, particularly in light of population ageing.

Ageing is a multifaceted process characterised by a gradual decline in physiological functions, accumulation of molecular and cellular damage and increased susceptibility to health problems [1]. Measuring frailty reveals how ageing affects bodily systems, emphasising the interplay of biological and environmental factors. Frailty, linked to heightened vulnerability in older adults [2, 3], can be defined either as a specific clinical syndrome or biological phenotype [4] or as a graded health state reflecting accumulated age-related deficits [5]. Frailty can be assessed in various ways, typically categorised as robust, prefrail and frail [2, 3]. Robust individuals show no signs of frailty and maintain high functional ability, whilst pre-frail individuals exhibit some frailty criteria, indicating early signs of vulnerability without meeting full frailty criteria [4]. The ‘prefrailty’ stage is critical as it represents an opportunity for interventions to prevent progression to frailty. Those who are frail meet the cutoff criteria and are at significantly higher risk for adverse health outcomes, including disability, hospitalisation and mortality [4].

Common clinical tools for measuring frailty include the frailty index (FI) [5] and the Fried frailty phenotype (FP) [4]. The FI is the ratio of accumulated deficits to all measured deficits, whilst the FP identifies frailty when at least three of the following five criteria are met: weight loss, exhaustion, weakness, slowness and lack of activity [2]. The prevalence of frailty amongst older adults varies widely based on the population and assessment criteria, estimated globally at 10%–15%, with higher rates in those aged 80+ years [6]. In a systematic review (SR) and meta-analysis (MA) published in 2021, including 240 studies from 62 countries and territories representing 1,755,497 participants, the pooled prevalence of frailty using FP and FI was reported at 12% and 24%, respectively [7]. For prefrail status, the prevalence was 46% for FP and 49% for FI. In short, nearly half of the older adult population is at an intermediate stage of vulnerability (prefrail), which can progress to frailty without effective prevention.

Frailty is influenced by various factors, with age being the primary risk, particularly after 65 [2, 3]. Women are more susceptible due to muscle mass, metabolism and hormonal differences [8]. Chronic conditions like diabetes, cardiovascular disease and arthritis further increase frailty risk by reducing physiological reserves [9]. Poor nutrition, particularly protein deficiency [8] and physical inactivity contribute to muscle loss and weakness, key elements of frailty [2, 3]. Socioeconomic factors, such as lower socioeconomic status (SES) and poor living conditions, also elevate the risk [10]. Psychological issues like depression and anxiety exacerbate frailty by affecting health behaviours [11]. Emerging evidence suggests that environmental factors, such as air pollution, play a significant role in the development of frailty [12]. Air pollution, which includes a mix of particles and gases like particulate matter (PM), nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and ozone (O3), has been linked to various adverse health outcomes, particularly affecting the respiratory and cardiovascular systems [12, 13]. Amongst PMs, PM2.5 (fine particulate matter ≤2.5 microns) is extensively studied for its role in cardiopulmonary illnesses and its significant health risks [14]. High levels of air pollution, including NO2 and PM, are identified as modifiable risk factors for dementia [15], suggesting they could also be modifiable risk factors for frailty. Studies indicate that older adults residing in high air pollution areas are more likely to experience frailty, emphasising the importance of environmental health concerning ageing and vulnerability [16, 17].

The primary aim of this SR and MA is to examine the impact of exposure to air pollution, particularly PM2.5, on frailty, with a focus on frailty as assessed by the FI and/or the FP. Compared to a recent SR and MA on the effects of air pollution exposure on frailty risk [18], our review spans three distinct exposures—air pollution, household solid fuel use and secondhand smoke (SHS)—providing a broader assessment of environmental determinants of frailty and a more detailed statistical approach. By synthesising existing research through MA, we aim to quantify the extent to which air pollution contributes to frailty. We hypothesise that exposure to air pollution is associated with an increased risk of frailty in middle-aged and older adults.

We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Statement 2020 (Appendix 1). Four electronic databases were searched up to August 2024, updated in February 2025: PubMed, Ovid Medline, Scopus and the Cochrane database (Figure 1). The search had no restrictions on language, publication status, or date. The combined search term ‘air pollution’ AND ‘frailty’ AND ‘human’ was used for the systematic search. References of included studies were manually checked to identify any missing articles. Both published reviews and preprints were examined, if applicable. The SR protocol was established before the review and registered (CRD42024561510) with the PROSPERO International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews.

The PRISMA flow diagram details the search and selection process applied during the systematic review. Publications that did not meet PICOS’s criteria were excluded.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined using the PICOS framework (Participants, Interventions, Comparators, Outcomes and Study designs) [19] as follows: (P) peer-reviewed research articles on middle-aged and/or older adults seeking the potential association between air pollution and the risk of frailty, (I) exposure to air pollution assessed by self-report surveys and/or national/regional air quality data registries, (C) a control group without frailty or no control group, (O) frailty as measured by self-report checklists and/or indirect measures such as gait speed, grip strength or overall physical function and (S) Observational studies. Reviews, books, case reports and editorials were excluded.

Duplicates were eliminated using EndNote software. The final papers were selected through a two-stage process: title and abstract screening and full-text screening. Papers that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded at each stage. Two reviewers independently conducted the systematic search and assessed evidence quality, aiming to resolve disagreements by consensus [20].

The following data were extracted from the included studies: author, year, study design, participants, frailty measures, air pollution exposure, statistical analyses and primary findings.

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Analytical Studies [21] was used to assess the quality of the included articles.

The MA was carried out using Jamovi 2.3.28, MAJOR Library. A P-value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. The pooled estimate of the odds ratios (OR) of frailty was quantified by MAs. Forest plots depict the pooled estimate of ORs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) (Figure 2). The I-square (I2) test was performed to quantify the degree of heterogeneity in MAs. I2 statistics of 0, 25, 50 and 75% correspond to no, low, medium and high heterogeneity, respectively [22]. For I2 ≥ 50%, indicating high heterogeneity, a random-effects model was carried out [23]. Funnel Plots and Egger’s regression test were used to assess potential publication bias [24].

The database search yielded 173 publications: 67 from PubMed, 7 from Ovid Medline, 99 from Scopus and none from the Cochrane database. Duplicate records, totalling 61 publications, were removed (Figure 1). After screening titles and abstracts, 112 more records were eliminated. One further paper was removed because air pollution was not amongst the environmental pollutants studied [25], leaving 18 articles for full-text review. Tables 1–3 summarise the characteristics of the included articles: 10 on environmental air pollution and frailty [16, 17, 26–33], five on household air pollution from solid fuels and frailty [34–38] and three on SHS and frailty [39–41]. Hand-searching these reference lists did not yield any additional related articles for inclusion. There was complete agreement between the reviewers on the included papers.

In all but one included study [36], statistical analyses were adjusted for various sociodemographic and clinical potential confounding factors (Tables 1–3). The gender-stratified analysis in two studies [17, 32] showed a stronger association between air pollution and frailty in men than in women.

Based on the rating criteria, all studies reviewed here were determined to have high methodological quality (Appendix 2).


Figure 2A represents a random effect model performed on nine studies [16, 17, 26–32] reporting the association between air pollution exposure (PM2.5) and the risk of frailty in middle-aged and older adults (n = 513 609). Amongst common pollutants found in the air, PM2.5 was the pollutant reported in all studies; therefore, it was used for MAs. Figure 2A1 represents the details of descriptive and analytic quantifications, indicating a statistically significant 19% higher OR of frailty in those exposed to high levels of PM2.5 (OR = 1.19, 95%CI 1.10–1.27, Z = 27.20, P < 0.001). Figure 2A1 also exhibits a Forest plot, where each horizontal line represents a separate study included in the MA. The diamond in the Forest plot indicates the pooled estimate of ORs with its horizontal tips displaying the 95% CI. The I2 test demonstrates a high heterogeneity in the results of the included studies (I2 = 97.06%, P < 0.001). Figure 2A2 exhibits publication bias. Data points outside the Funnel plot (Egger’s Regression Test results: t = 2.820, P = 0.005) suggest potential publication bias across the included studies.


Figure 2B displays an MA using a fixed effect model on four studies (n = 22 571) [34–36, 38] that evaluated frailty risk from exposure to household air pollution. Figure 2B1 summarises the details of descriptive and analytic quantifications. A pooled estimate of OR of frailty equal to 1.28 indicates 28% higher odds of frailty in those exposed to household air pollution compared to those without exposure, which was statistically significant (OR = 1.28, 95%CI 1.16–1.40, Z = 20.60, P < 0.001). The I2 test results indicate no heterogeneity in the results of the included studies (I2 = 0.0%, P = 0.756). Figure 2B2 shows the analysis of publication bias. The Funnel plot, displaying the study points evenly distributed in the funnel, and the Egger’s Regression Test results (t = 1.009, P = 0.313) demonstrate no publication bias in the included articles.


Figure 2C demonstrates the results of an MA using a fixed effect model on three studies [39–41] investigating the relationship between SHS and increased risk of frailty in older adults (n = 6010). Figure 2C1, representing the details of descriptive and analytic quantifications, indicates a significantly high pooled estimate of ORs of frailty (59%) in those exposed to SHS (OR = 1.59, 95%CI 0.46–2.72, Z = 2.76, P = 0.006). The I2 test results exhibit no heterogeneity in the results of the included studies (I2 = 0.0%, P = 0.535). Figure 2C2 shows the analysis of publication bias. The Funnel plot, demonstrating the study points distributed in the funnel, and the Egger’s Regression Test results (t = 1.097, P = 0.237) exhibit no publication bias.

Of the 18 selected studies, 16 that reported the risk of developing frailty due to air pollution exposure were included in the meta-analyses. The pooled effect size estimates of ORs using standard errors and 95% CIs indicate a 19%, 28% and 59% increased risk of frailty associated with long-term exposure to PM2.5, household use of polluting fuels and SHS, respectively. Notably, aside from the MA assessing the impact of PM2.5 on frailty, other MAs exhibited no heterogeneity or risk of bias.

All ten studies identified in the systematic search [16, 17, 26, 27, 29–33, 42] focused on middle-aged and older adults based in China [16, 26, 27, 31, 42], Taiwan [32], Israel [33], as well as two joint studies: one from the UK and Sweden [29] and another from six middle-income countries [17]. All studies employed either the FI [16, 17, 26, 27, 29, 31, 33, 42] or FP [30, 32] to assess frailty. Except for one study focusing on patients with myocardial infarction [33], other studies reported findings on community-dwelling individuals. The MA of the pooled estimate of ORs demonstrated a 19% increased risk of frailty in those with a history of exposure to ambient PM2.5.

The data aggregated here highlight the substantial adverse impact of prolonged exposure to PM2.5 on frailty risk amongst middle-aged and older adults. This is consistent with an expanding body of research indicating that air pollution, specifically PM2.5, poses a significant public health threat, particularly for vulnerable populations. PM2.5 particles are small enough to penetrate deep into the lungs and enter the bloodstream, triggering systemic inflammation and oxidative stress [14]. Systemic inflammation can accelerate ageing and contribute to the deterioration of multiple organ systems [1, 43], thereby increasing susceptibility to frailty [31]. Elevated levels of inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) have been observed in individuals exposed to high levels of PM2.5, and these inflammatory markers have also been correlated with an elevated risk of frailty [44].

Exposure to PM2.5 is also strongly linked to cardiovascular diseases, stroke and respiratory illnesses. In two studies [17, 32], men showed a stronger association between air pollution and frailty, consistent with higher hazard ratios for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality from PM2.5 and PM10 in men than women [45]. PM2.5-induced cardiorespiratory stress heightens the risk of transitioning to a frail state. Moreover, PM2.5 exposure accelerates cellular ageing through mechanisms like telomere shortening and mitochondrial dysfunction. These changes decrease cellular repair capacity and increase apoptosis, leading to the deterioration of multiple organ systems [14]. This biological ageing process is a fundamental aspect of frailty, characterised by reduced physiological reserves and increased vulnerability to stressors [46]. Recent studies highlight that the complexity of frailty cannot be fully understood by examining individual biomarkers in isolation. In the 'omics era, it is imperative to consider combinations of biomarkers and their interactions to grasp the multifaceted nature of frailty fully. Oxidative stress resulting from PM2.5 exposure exemplifies this complexity, as it involves multiple pathways that damage cells and tissues, impairing bodily functions and contributing to frailty [29]. This perspective aligns with the recognition that numerous pathways lead to frailty, necessitating a comprehensive approach that integrates various biological markers and their synergistic effects [46].

Additionally, emerging research suggests that PM2.5 can also affect the central nervous system, potentially exacerbating frailty through cognitive decline and neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Specifically, PM2.5 particles can cross the blood–brain barrier, leading to neuroinflammation characterised by increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species (ROS), which contribute to neuronal damage and synaptic dysfunction. Over time, chronic exposure to PM2.5 may accelerate the accumulation of beta-amyloid plaques and tau protein tangles, hallmark pathologies of AD, thereby increasing the risk of cognitive impairment and frailty [47]. Indeed, exposure to air pollution has been identified as a potentially modifiable risk factor for AD, contributing to 2.3% of the worldwide burden. These neurobiological effects underscore how PM2.5 exposure not only affects physical health but also exacerbates cognitive decline, further predisposing individuals to frailty as they age.

Despite the heterogeneity across the included studies, as evidenced by some data points falling outside the funnel plot—potentially due to publication bias, small-study effects or methodological differences [24]—our finding underscores the importance of addressing environmental pollution as a key factor in frailty prevention and management. Social context is crucial, as communities with lower SES are more likely to be in areas of higher air pollution [48]. Although the source studies tried to adjust for SES, this adjustment was likely incomplete due to the complexity of social conditions. These limitations necessitate caution when interpreting the results and indicate the need for further research to validate and extend our findings.

Of the five included studies [17, 34–37], four were based in China, and one was an international study with datasets from six countries [35]. The FI and/or FP were utilised to assess frailty, and the information regarding household air pollution due to the use of solid fuels was based on self-report questionnaires included in the large datasets used in these studies. Of these five studies, four [34–36, 38] reported similar analyses and were included in MAs. Our results indicate that using solid fuels, commonly for cooking, can significantly increase frailty in older adults by 28% compared to clean fuels. The results of the analysis showed no heterogeneity in the data analysed and no risk of bias across the included studies. Of note, despite the source studies adjusting for social factors with available variables in their datasets, the social and SES context of the use of household air pollution remains a relevant consideration and may have influenced these results [35].

Solid fuel combustion generates harmful pollutants, including fine PMs, carbon monoxide and volatile organic compounds, which contribute to respiratory issues like Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) and asthma [37], as well as cardiovascular diseases (e.g. hypertension and ischemic heart disease) through systemic inflammation and oxidative stress [49]. This inflammation can lead to reduced blood flow to the brain and muscles, worsening physical and cognitive decline and increasing mortality risk [35, 50]. Inhaling pollutants from biomass fuels also damages cellular structures, such as DNA and proteins, leading to dysfunction and apoptosis and heightening frailty vulnerability [34]. These findings stress the need for cleaner household fuels, improved ventilation and public awareness to address health risks from indoor air pollution.

We identified three studies from Japan [39], Spain [40] and the USA [41] that employed self-report questionnaires and/or serum cotinine concentration to measure the impact of SHS on frailty. The studies used FP [40, 41] and/or some physical measures such as grip strength, lower-extremity performance and mobility limitations [39, 40] to assess frailty. Our MA showed a 59% increased risk of frailty due to SHS exposure, with no observed heterogeneity or publication bias. This indicates that SHS is a more potent risk factor for frailty compared to air pollution (19%) and household solid fuels (28%), likely due to its higher concentration of toxic substances. SHS contains a complex mixture of toxic and carcinogenic chemicals such as nicotine, benzene, formaldehyde and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. These substances have been shown to induce systemic inflammation and oxidative stress in exposed individuals, as evidenced by elevated levels of markers such as CRP and IL-6. Chronic exposure to these chemicals can lead to persistent inflammatory responses, which are known to contribute to the development and progression of frailty [39, 44]. Moreover, SHS exposure has detrimental effects on cardiovascular health, increasing the risks of hypertension, atherosclerosis and other cardiovascular diseases. These conditions impair physical function and mobility, reducing the body's capacity to perform physical activities [41].

SHS exposure compromises respiratory health, leading to chronic COPD and other conditions that limit physical function and worsen frailty. Reduced respiratory function lowers tissue oxygenation, contributing to physical decline and frailty risk [40]. SHS toxins also disrupt protein synthesis and muscle repair, resulting in sarcopenia, which is characterised by a loss of muscle mass and strength [39]. Emerging evidence indicates that long-term exposure to PM2.5 [51] and smoking habits [52] are associated with an increased risk of sarcopenia, potentially due to systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, which can accelerate muscle degradation. Furthermore, SHS triggers neuroinflammation and oxidative stress in the brain, impairing cognitive function and contributing to cognitive frailty [41]. Social determinants such as lower socioeconomic status and higher social vulnerability are closely linked to both increased SHS exposure and smoking behaviour, which in turn elevate frailty risk through chronic inflammation and other related health complications​ [41].

Our SR and MAs possess several notable strengths and weaknesses. A significant strength is the inclusion of multiple primary studies with large datasets, offering a comprehensive overview of the link between environmental exposures and frailty. Utilising recognised frailty indices, such as the FI with cutoffs predominantly set at >0.2 or 0.25, and the FP ensures consistent frailty assessment across studies, thereby enhancing result comparability. Furthermore, the lack of observed heterogeneity and minimal risk of bias in two out of three MAs underscores the robustness of our findings.

However, the heterogeneity observed in the MA linking air pollution and frailty may arise from publication bias, small-study effects and methodological differences, warranting caution in interpreting the findings [24]. Furthermore, the observational nature of the included studies limits the establishment of causal relationships.

The measures of air pollution included here all occur in social contexts and would be strongly influenced by SES and living conditions, which may have been incompletely accounted for in the source studies. Indoor cooking fuel, predominantly used in low- and middle-income countries, particularly by poorer households, is a significant source of household air pollution, directly impacting frailty in these communities [53]. Low SES neighbourhoods are often located near polluting industries and highways, increasing exposure to environmental pollutants [48]. Smoking, and thus SHS exposure, is also strongly socially determined, with higher prevalence amongst lower SES groups, further contributing to health disparities [54]. Social context, social vulnerability and SES are risk factors for frailty [55], complicating adjustments in observational studies. Moreover, our analyses primarily relied on studies conducted in specific geographic regions, predominantly China, which may limit the generalisability of our findings to other populations with different environmental and socioeconomic conditions.

Given these limitations, there is a need for more longitudinal studies to provide stronger evidence of causality between environmental air pollution, household air pollution from unclean solid fuels, SHS and frailty. Frailty is a critical health outcome that demands urgent attention, particularly due to the ageing population. It is closely linked to heightened vulnerability to health issues, diminished functional independence and substantial increases in healthcare expenditures [56]. Notably, frailty is not a permanent state of decline; it can be improved or even prevented through appropriate interventions. Studies have shown that lifestyle modifications, including increased physical activity, nutritional support and social engagement, can significantly enhance frailty outcomes and potentially reverse the condition [57]. Future research should aim to include diverse populations from various regions to improve the generalisability of findings and understand the global impact of these exposures on frailty. It is also crucial to explore the underlying biological mechanisms in greater detail to elucidate how these environmental factors contribute to the development and progression of frailty. Additionally, there is a need for preventive interventional studies to evaluate the effectiveness of strategies aimed at reducing exposure to these pollutants and mitigating their impact on frailty. Such interventions could include promoting the use of cleaner cooking technologies and fuels, improving home ventilation, implementing stricter air quality regulations, addressing underlying poverty and conducting public awareness campaigns about the health risks associated with indoor and outdoor air pollution.

In this article, we investigated the relationship between various forms of air pollution—ambient air pollution, household air pollution and SHS—and the risk of frailty amongst middle-aged and older adults. Our primary findings reveal significant associations: a 19% increased risk of frailty with PM2.5 exposure, a 28% increased risk with household air pollution from solid fuels, and a 59% increased risk with SHS exposure. Whilst our findings demonstrate robustness in two of the three MAs, the heterogeneity observed in air pollution and frailty analysis highlights the need for cautious interpretation and more rigorous longitudinal research. Additionally, social contextual factors may have been challenging to fully account for in source studies. The implications of these results are vital for public health policies aimed at reducing air pollution exposure and its impact on frailty. Future research should target diverse populations, examine the detailed health effects of these pollutants and evaluate intervention effectiveness.
