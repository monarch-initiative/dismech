Background
Kidney transplantation is the treatment of choice for end-
stage kidney disease (ESKD) in children, due to lower 
mortality rates and fewer cognitive and learning impair -
ments when compared to long-term kidney replace -
ment therapy [ 1]. Kidney transplantation in pediatric 
patients also has the benefit of avoiding exacerbation of 
bone disease and cardiovascular morbidity and mortal -
ity that may appear while on dialysis [ 2]. However, care-
ful consideration must be given to types of grafts used 
(deceased and living donor adult kidneys) and transplant 
indications. Rare genetic kidney diseases associated with 
other systemic manifestations can be the primary cause 
for chronic kidney disease in the pediatric population,

Abstract
Background Kidney transplantation is the gold standard treatment for end-stage kidney disease in children. Rare 
genetic systemic diseases associated with cystic kidney disease such as COL4A1-related disorder and oral facial 
digital syndrome type 1 could contribute to end-stage kidney disease in the pediatric population but there is scarce 
evidence in the literature regarding kidney transplant outcomes in these cases.
Case presentation We report a case of a 5-year-old male with COL4A1-related disorder who received a living-related 
donor kidney transplant from his mother. To our knowledge this is the first reported kidney transplant in a pediatric 
recipient with COL4A1-related disorder. We also present a case of a 16-year-old female with oral facial digital syndrome 
type 1 who received a deceased donor kidney transplant.
Conclusions In this case series, we discuss surgical technique, indication for kidney transplant, influence of 
comorbidities and intellectual disability in transplant outcomes.

At our institution, two pediatric patients with rare 
genetic diseases causing ESKD (COL4A1-related disorder 
and oral-facial-digital syndrome type 1) were consecu -
tively evaluated for kidney transplant. We report these 
two cases together in efforts to discuss the challenges 
that may arise in the treatment and management of 
ESKD patients due to rare genetic diseases. We address 
the distinctive features of both diseases and challenges 
for kidney transplantation in each case.
Case presentation
Case 1
This patient is a 5-year-old male with COL4A1 collagen 
mutation with bilateral cystic dysplastic kidneys and 
global developmental delay. He was born at full term with 
a weight of 2.3  kg. At one month of age, he was admit -
ted to the pediatric intensive care unit for dehydration, 
emesis and failure to thrive.
At six months of age, he was admitted for peritoneal 
dialysis catheter and gastro-jejunostomy (GJ) tube place -
ment. During surgery, the patient developed a stroke and 
seizure attributed to hypertension. He was maintained on 
peritoneal dialysis from February 2019 until kidney trans-
plant, with one episode of peritonitis, which required 
transitional hemodialysis. A voiding cystourethrogram 
(VCUG) excluded vesicoureteral reflux. Ultrasound scan 
and CT scan revealed small kidneys without identifiable 
renal parenchyma (Fig. 1).
Neurologic assessment revealed the diagnosis of agen -
esis of the corpus callosum with brain microbleeding due 
to COL4A1 collagen disease affecting the small vessels of 
the brain (Fig.  2). He had hypotonia and developmental 
delay. Cataract surgery on the left eye was done at the age 
of three. Other comorbidities included: obstructive sleep 
apnea secondary to adenoidal hypertrophy and hypothy -
roidism on thyroxine treatment. To our knowledge, there 
are no reports of hypothyroidism as part of the COL4A1 
mutation-associated disease and we consider this an inci-
dental finding.
Rapid exome testing was performed on the patient with 
COL4A1-related disorder, which identified that he was 
heterozygous for a de novo p.G888R pathogenic variant 
in the COL4A1 gene consistent with COL4A1-related 
disorder, as well as biallelic cystic fibrosis transmem -
brane conductance regulator ( CFTR) variants. The test -
ing showed a CFTR pathogenic variant in one allele and a 
variant of varying clinical consequence in the other allele. 
Individuals who are heterozygous for a single pathogenic 
variant in CFTR are generally asymptomatic and there -
fore the CFTR gene variant was not considered conse -
quential in this patient.

When the patient was evaluated for transplantation, his 
nutrition was maintained exclusively through a G-J tube. 
He was non-verbal, hypotonic and had cognitive and

tube feeds and is diaper dependent.
After extensive evaluation and information given to the 
parents about treatment alternatives for ESKD, living-
related kidney transplant was offered. Patient’s mother 
was evaluated as a donor. She was 39 years old and had 
no history of arterial hypertension, diabetes mellitus 
or any other comorbidities that could prevent her from 
being accepted as a living-donor. Her left kidney had no 
abnormalities and her right kidney had a large extra-renal 
pelvis with no obstruction and good function. Therefore, 
the right kidney was chosen for donation.
Donor nephrectomy was completed via robotic-
assisted laparoscopic approach with no intraoperative 
complications. Open kidney transplantation was per -
formed in the pediatric recipient following standard 
surgical technique used in pediatric patients at our 
institution.

reflected medially and liver and native right kidney mobi-

COL4A1 mutations have been linked to an autoso -
mal dominant hereditary systemic disease with reduced 
penetrance, which includes a broad spectrum of lesions 
affecting small vessels of the brain, eyes, kidney and 
muscles [ 5, 8–10]. The phenotype of the disease result -
ing from COL4A1 mutation is widely variable, with broad 
disparity in disease onset (from antenatal period until late 
adulthood) and clinical features even in the same family. 
Common phenotypes of the disease include intracerebral 
hemorrhages and porencephaly [11], muscle cramps, ret-
inal arterial tortuosities and/or hemorrhages, congenital 
or juvenile cataract [ 10]. Reported clinical presentations 
of kidney disease are hematuria, proteinuria, bilateral 
kidney cysts and varying degrees of renal insufficiency [ 8, 
12, 13].
Case 2
Our patient is a 16-year-old girl with mild growth delay, 
clinically diagnosed with oral-facial-digital syndrome 
type 1. Her clinical manifestations included polycystic 
kidney disease (Fig. 3), cleft lip and palate, agenesis of the 
corpus callosum, syndactyly and intellectual disability. 
Her kidney disease progressed and she developed ESKD, 
requiring hemodialysis at 13 years of age. Family his -
tory is remarkable for her mother also being diagnosed 
with OFD type 1 syndrome with a similar phenotype as 
described for our patient, including the need for chronic 
hemodialysis for ESKD. However, the mother does not 
have intellectual disability. She has two sisters with a 
milder phenotypic expression of OFD type 1 syndrome.
Given the strong family history of the disease with 
a previous diagnosis of the mother and two sisters, no 
genetic testing was performed in this patient.
Before kidney transplantation was offered, extensive 
medical and psychosocial evaluation of the patient was 
performed. Her BMI was 19 and she was on the 38th per-
centile for age, gender and height. Blood pressure and 
cardiac evaluation was normal. The length of her right 
native kidney was 17.9 cm and the left was 19.7 cm (See 
Fig.  2A and B). The patient was unable to swallow pills 
because of her cleft palate and preferred soft-textured 
food. She had a gatrostomy tube to facilitate nutritional 
supplementation and medication (liquid) intake. Her 
mother appeared to keep an adequate adherence to 
treatment. The patient required a wheelchair because of 
fatigue when walking long distances. She was able to per -
form simple activities of daily living but was limited with 
performing complex tasks such as making decisions for 
her health and well-being. After explaining risks and ben-
efits associated with kidney transplant to her mother, the

At age 16, the patient received a left kidney transplant

An extended right lower quadrant incision (modified 
Gibson incision) was made. An extraperitoneal approach 
was used in order to minimize the morbidities associated 
with intraabdominal operations. Due to size mismatch 
between the recipient and the allograft (donor to recipi -
ent body surface area ratio was 1.3) and the large size of 
the recipient’s native kidney, complete liver and native 
right kidney mobilization was required to create suffi -
cient space in the retroperitoneum for the adult allograft. 
Therefore, nephrectomy of the native kidney was avoided. 
Venous anastomosis was performed to the vena cava fol -
lowed by the arterial anastomosis to the common iliac 
artery. The allograft ureter was anastomosed to the blad -
der as described in Case 1. No ureteral stent or Jackson-
Pratt drain were used in both cases.
The postoperative period was uncomplicated and 
the patient had immediate graft function. No signs of 
abdominal compartment syndrome appeared during the 
first postoperative days. Ultrasound scan 24 h after sur -
gery showed a normal sized kidney with adequate vascu -
lar perfusion and no abnormal findings. Serum creatinine 
was 1.1 mg/dl at two weeks post-transplant.
Oral-facial-digital (OFD) syndrome is a rare X-linked 
dominant ciliopathy characterized by malformations of 
the face, oral cavity and fingers that are often accompa -
nied by cerebral abnormalities. OFD type 1 syndrome 
occurs from defects in the OFD1 gene protein. The most 
frequent form of kidney involvement in the OFD syn -
drome is polycystic kidney disease in approximately 
15–50% cases [ 14–16] and mutations in the C-terminal 
part of the OFD1 protein at exons 20–21 are associated 
with kidney involvement in the disease [17–19].
Immunosuppression
Our induction immunosuppression protocol for both 
patients [20, 21] included antithymocyte globulin 1 mg/
kg (1 dose) intraoperatively (day 0), two doses of basilix -
imab 10 mg on day 0 and day 4 post-transplantation and 
methylprednisolone starting intraoperatively at 10  mg/
kg and tapered to 0.3  mg/kg/day of prednisone in Case 
1. Case 2 was on a steroid-free maintenance regimen 
after the first post-transplant week. Oral mycophenolate 
mofetil is introduced on the second postoperative day at 
a dose of 600 mg/m2 twice daily. Dose adjustment is per -
formed according to white blood cell count and gastroin -
testinal tolerance. Oral tacrolimus was introduced when 
serum creatinine was < 2  mg/dl in the first patient and 
< 3 mg/dl in the second patient. Tacrolimus trough levels 
were targeted to 6-8 ng/mL. This is the standard immu -
nosuppression regimen used in pediatric kidney trans -
plant recipients at our institution, which did not require 
any modification for both of our patients.
Discussion and conclusions
Surgical challenges
While pediatric kidney transplantation has become the 
treatment of choice for ESKD in children, it entails sev -
eral surgical challenges that need to be addressed. Space 
limitations associated with small pediatric recipient and 
adult donor allograft have been shown to be overcome by 
using an intraperitoneal approach, but this approach pro-
longs the recovery time and is subject to complications 
such as post-operative adhesions. Numerous studies 
have proved that extraperitoneal kidney transplantation 
in children is feasible and safe [ 22]. Native nephrectomy 
may be avoided in these cases if a careful mobilization of 
the native liver and kidney is performed to create space 
in the retroperitoneum [ 23]. The patient in Case 2 pre -
sented with polycystic kidney disease (PKD) and large 
native kidneys. However, native nephrectomy was not 
required after an adequate mobilization of the liver and 
right kidney.
Challenges in the diagnosis of genetic disease
A common challenge in the investigation of rare genetic 
diseases arises from the types of genetic mutations which 
range from large scale gene alterations to single gene 
mutations (point mutations, missense, nonsense, splicing 
mutations). The methodologies utilized for their detec -
tion and characterization vary depending on single organ 
or multiple organ involvement. It is challenging when 
multiple organ systems are involved and there is varia -
tion in the clinical expression of the disease. OFD type 1 
is an X-linked dominant ciliopathy of primary cilia. The 
latter are present in multiple organs such as the central 
nervous system (CNS), skeletal system, eyes, kidney and 
liver. Variability in phenotypic expression in the same 
family may be partly explained by a phenomenon known 
as X chromosome inactivation which occurs in variable 
degrees in each tissue. The presence of modifiers and 
enhancers further contribute to variability in clinical 
expression. The OFD1 gene is located on the short arm 
of the X chromosome ( Xp22.2) and its mutations are 
responsible for OFD type 1 syndrome. To date, 184 dif -
ferent and apparently pathogenic sequence variants have 
been detected in the OFD1 gene. An accurate diagnosis 
of OFD type 1 syndrome can be made through next gen -
eration sequencing-based gene panel [ 24]. Gene panels 
associated with congenital anomalies of the kidney and 
urinary tract are usually employed. However, in some

cases with a strong family history of the disease and a 
clear clinical diagnosis, genetic testing can be avoided.
COL4A1 mutations have been linked to an autoso -
mal dominant hereditary systemic disease with reduced 
penetrance, which includes a broad spectrum of lesions 
affecting small vessels of the brain, eyes, kidney and mus -
cles, as described by Vahedi et al. [ 8]. Type IV collagen 
is formed by a heterotrimeric helix of two α1 (COL4A1) 
chains and one α2 (COL4A2) chain. This molecule is 
an essential component of the basement membranes of 
multiple tissues including vascular endothelia. COL4A1 
gene is mapped on the telomeric region of chromosome 
13q34. The phenotype of the disease resulting from 
COL4A1 mutation is widely variable, with broad dis -
parity in disease onset (from antenatal period until late 
adulthood) and clinical features even in the same family. 
Common phenotypes of the disease include diffuse small 
vessel disease of the brain, causing intracerebral hemor -
rhages and porencephaly, arterial retinal tortuosities, ret -
inal hemorrhages, congenital or juvenile cataract, muscle 
cramps and elevated serum creatine kinase, Raynaud’s 
phenomenon, supraventricular arrhythmia, alterations 
of the basement membranes of the Bowman’s capsule, 
tubules and interstitial capillaries in the kidney, resulting 
in hematuria, bilateral renal cysts and renal insufficiency 
[8, 12, 13].
The diagnosis of a COL4A1-related disorder is estab -
lished by molecular testing, which can range between 
single-gene testing, multigene panel sequencing and 
exome sequencing. The patient in case 1 had rapid exome 
sequencing as a diagnostic method because he had no 
previous family history of the disease and the broad dif -
ferential diagnosis did not allow for targeted testing 
[13]. He had all of the clinical features described with 
COL4A1-related disorder, which primarily affects type 4 
collagen structure and basement membranes in various 
tissues. Clinical features include small vessel disease of 
the brain leading to susceptibility to intracerebral bleed -
ing (which results in leukoencephalopathy), hypotonia, 
nephropathy, congenital dysgenesis of the iris and cor -
nea, cataracts, optic nerve hypoplasia, congenital myopa -
thy and severe developmental delay.
These two cases demonstrate the challenges that the 
diagnosis of rare genetic diseases entail. Multiple genetic 
testing techniques that may be used in these type of 
patients are available to clinicians, ranging from single-
gene targeted testing to whole genome sequencing. Each 
case must be individually evaluated and the most ade -
quate genetic testing technique must be chosen taking 
into account clinical suspicion and previous family his -
tory of the disease.
Ethical challenges
Additionally, indication for transplant surgery can be 
complex in children with rare diseases, considering the 
limited evidence regarding long-term results in this pop -
ulation. Kidney transplantation has been described as a 
successful treatment for ESKD in patients with Alport 
syndrome (caused by variants in COL4A3, COL4A4 and 
COL4A5 genes [25]), but to our knowledge, kidney trans-
plantation in a patient with COL4A1-related disorder has 
not been reported in the literature. We report a case of 
kidney transplantation in a 5-year old child with COL4A1 
mutation that was complicated by poor bladder drainage 
and a functional ureteropelvic junction obstruction from 
the large extra-renal pelvis in the transplanted kidney. 
Both these abnormalities manifested after transplanta -
tion when the renal pelvis and bladder were subjected to 
high urine volumes.
There are some cases of kidney transplantation 
reported in OFD type 1 syndrome. Feather et al. [ 26] 
reported a three-generation kindred with OFD1 and PKD 
affecting five females, of which one underwent kidney 
transplantation as an adult, but subsequently died from 
myocardial infarction. Along these lines, other authors 
have reported cases of adult kidney transplant in patients 
with OFD type 1 syndrome [ 16, 27], but pediatric kid -
ney transplant has only been described by Sakakibara et 
al. [24] who reported 4 cases of male children with OFD 
type 1 syndrome. Of these, 2 patients developed ESKD 
and underwent kidney transplant at 8 and 14 years of age. 
However, follow up is vaguely reported.
Kidney transplantation in children with multiple 
comorbidities including intellectual disability has been 
thoroughly debated [ 28–30]. Questions arise regarding 
outcomes of kidney transplantation in this population 
and the ethical dilemma of organ allocation to patients 
with multiple comorbidities and profound cognitive 
impairment. Limited data from case reports and small 
cohort studies suggests that pediatric kidney transplant 
reciepients with an intellectual disability have compara -
ble graft and recipient survival outcomes when compared 
to other pediatric recipients [ 28]. Wightman et al. per -
formed a large-scale retrospective cohort study of all chil-
dren (n = 2076) receiving a first kidney alone transplant in 
the UNOS dataset from 2008 to 2011. No significant dif -
ference in graft or patient survival between patients with 
definite or probable intellectual disability and those with-
out disability were observed up to three years post-trans -
plantation [29]. Also, in 2020 the American Academy of 
Pediatrics issued a statement recommending that pedi -
atric patients should not be excluded from consideration 
for solid organ transplant solely on the basis of an intel -
lectual or developmental disability [30].
An important screening criterion when considering 
kidney transplant in pediatric patients with significant

comorbidities and intellectual disability is family sup -
port. This is a key point, given that parents are typically 
responsible for correct medication taking [ 31]. Ohta et 
al. found positive effects of kidney transplantation on 
quality of life (QOL) among kidney transplant recipients 
with intellectual disability and a reduction in the bur -
den of care on families compared to dialysis [ 32]. In the 
two cases that we have reported, family support played a 
fundamental role in compliance with medication intake 
and no modifications in the conventional drug schedules 
were needed, with adequate maintenance of immunosup-
pression targets.
In conclusion, we report two cases of pediatric kid -
ney transplantation from living and deceased donor in 
children with rare genetic diseases that involve various 
degrees of intellectual disability. We highlight surgi -
cal technique, challenges associated with rare genetic 
diseases and ethical dilemmas that may arise when 
approaching these complex cases.
