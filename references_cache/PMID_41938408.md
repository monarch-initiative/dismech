# A case report and literature review of pediatric multifocal melorheostosis in a unilateral limb.
**Authors:** Na X, Fuyong Y, Xiushu T, Lijun W, Lingjia Y, Kai T, Yisheng X
**Journal:** Int J Surg Case Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1097/RC9.0000000000000277](https://doi.org/10.1097/RC9.0000000000000277)

## Content

INTRODUCTION AND IMPORTANCE: Melorheostosis (Léri-Joanny syndrome) is a rare, nonhereditary, congenital sclerotic bone dysplasia with an estimated prevalence of 0.9 per million. Its enigmatic etiology and distinctive "melting wax" radiological appearance warrant detailed case reporting to enhance understanding of diagnosis and management, particularly in pediatric populations.

CASE PRESENTATION: A 9-year-old male presented with incidental right upper limb bone abnormalities discovered post-trauma. Asymptomatic without pain/swelling, he had no relevant family history. Laboratory tests (CBC, CRP, ESR) were normal. Imaging revealed unilateral cortical sclerosis with increased density in the right scapular coracoid process, humeral head, distal humerus, ulnar head, wrist, and metacarpals, exhibiting a pathognomonic "melting wax drip" pattern. Histopathology confirmed irregular endosteal bone proliferation, densely arranged lamellar bone, and enlarged/distorted Haversian canals .

CLINICAL DISCUSSION: Melorheostosis predominantly affects limbs unilaterally; multifocal involvement (as here) is uncommon. Radiography remains the diagnostic cornerstone, supplemented by CT/MRI for spatial extent. This case was classified as endosteal subtype. Management is symptom-directed: asymptomatic cases require monitoring only. Surgical correction (e.g., osteotomy) is reserved for severe pain/deformity refractory to analgesics/physiotherapy. Long-term follow-up is critical due to theoretical malignant transformation concerns, though unreported clinically.

CONCLUSION: This pediatric case underscores melorheostosis' incidental presentation and diagnostic reliance on classic imaging. The patient remained asymptomatic over 6 months, obviating intervention. Longitudinal surveillance is planned to elucidate natural history, contributing to management paradigms for this rare entity.

Melorheostosis, also known as Léri-Joanny syndrome, is a rare and enigmatic bone dysplasia characterized by benign, sclerotic bone overgrowth. First described by Leri and Joanny in 1922[1], it is classically known for a distinctive radiological pattern resembling “melting wax dripping down” the bone surface[2]. However, its radiographic presentation is now recognized as a spectrum, with this classic pattern representing only one of several subtypes, as highlighted by the Freyschmidt classification[3]. Despite its intriguing clinical and radiological features, melorheostosis remains poorly understood due to its rarity, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 0.9 per million individuals[4]. The etiology and pathogenesis of melorheostosis are not fully elucidated, and it is generally considered a non-hereditary, congenital disorder. The condition predominantly affects the limbs, with less frequent involvement of the spine and ribs. Treatment options are primarily symptomatic, focusing on pain management and surgical intervention when necessary. In this case report, we present a unique instance of pediatric multifocal melorheostosis in a unilateral limb, and discuss the clinical management implications of this rare entity. Through a comprehensive review of the literature, we aim to contribute to the understanding of melorheostosis and its implications for clinical practice.

The information in this case report is obtained from retrospective chart review and is reported in line with the SCARE guideline[5]. Patient’s informed written consent was obtained to use data for publication of this case report.

A 9-year-old boy presented to a local hospital with a chief complaint of “pain and mild swelling on the dorsum of the right hand for 4 days following a car accident.” After obtaining an X-ray of the right upper limb, bone abnormalities were noted. However, due to limited diagnostic capabilities at the local facility, the specific nature of these abnormalities was not detailed in the report, and neither the radiology report nor the original images were available for our review. No definitive diagnosis was made at that time. The patient received symptomatic treatment for pain and swelling (specific medication unknown), after which the symptoms completely resolved. To obtain a definitive diagnosis, the patient was referred to our higher-level hospital for further evaluation.

Upon admission to our hospital, the patient exhibited no tenderness in the right upper limb. There was no pain or swelling on the dorsum of the right hand, no obvious local abnormalities, and no restrictions in movement. The patient’s voice was strong, breathing was normal, and he reported no discomfort. Findings from the specialized physical examination were as follows: Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were unremarkable. Vital signs were stable with a heart rate of 72 bpm, respiratory rate of 20 breaths/min, and blood pressure of 80/106 mmHg. There was no swelling in the right upper limb. No tenderness was elicited in the right shoulder, elbow, wrist, or hand. No bony crepitus was palpable or audible. Distal circulation was adequate. No skin abnormalities were noted. Sensation in the limb was normal. All other physical examination findings were normal.

After admission, a repeat X-ray revealed nodular and striated hyperdense opacities in the right coracoid process, humeral head, distal humerus, capitate, lunate, and the second and third metacarpophalangeal bones. No significant alteration in bone morphology was observed. No abnormal soft tissue densities were seen surrounding the lesions. Joint congruency was maintained. No other significant abnormalities were noted. The final diagnosis, integrating the clinical presentation, imaging, and pathological results, was melorheostosis. The patient was in good general health, denied any family history of similar conditions, and reported a history of allergies to mangoes and amoxicillin.

Imaging revealed sclerotic cortical hyperplasia with increased density in the right scapular coracoid process, humeral head, distal humerus, ulnar head, wrist joint, and metacarpal bones. We have formulated a long-term follow-up plan for this patient, which includes clinical evaluations of the affected limb at 6, 12, and 24 months. Based on the findings, imaging re-examination will be performed if clinically indicated, to monitor for any disease progression or delayed symptoms. The patient was followed up for 6 and 12 months, during which no significant pain or deformities were observed. As a result, no specific treatment was provided. We plan to continue long-term follow-up of this case to further understand the progression of melorheostosis.

Melorheostosis, also known as Leri’s disease or Leri-Joanny syndrome, derives its name from its distinctive radiological appearance[1]. It has been referred to as flowing wax hyperostosis, striated hyperostosis of the limb bones, striated bone dysplasia of the limb, undulating osteosclerosis, or flowing osteosclerosis[2]. The condition was first identified and described by Leri and Joanny in 1922, over a century ago, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 0.9 per million individuals[4]. Due to its rarity, large-scale clinical data collection for research has been challenging, and the precise pathogenesis of the disease remains unclear. Current literature suggests that melorheostosis is not hereditary and is classified as a congenital, benign, and sclerotic bone dysplasia. Some researchers have hypothesized potential links with abnormalities in periosteum development, vascular growth, local inflammation, or infection, but no reliable clinical evidence supports these theories. Reported cases indicate that melorheostosis can affect limb bones, the spine, ribs, and pelvis, with unilateral limb involvement being the most common presentation[6]. Involvement of vertebrae and ribs is rare[7]. In this case, the patient exhibited lesions involving the unilateral scapula, humeral head, distal humerus, carpal bones, and metacarpal bones.

The radiological presentation of melorheostosis encompasses a spectrum of patterns, with X-ray imaging remaining the primary diagnostic modality. The most recognized and pathognomonic hallmark is the appearance of irregular, flowing, high-density cortical hyperostosis resembling “melting candle wax” along the bone surface. However, it is crucial to note that this classic pattern is not universal. The disease exhibits radiographic heterogeneity, which has been systematically categorized into subtypes such as the classical “flowing wax,” osteoma-like, osteopathia striata-like, and myositis ossificans-like forms, among others (e.g., according to the Freyschmidt classification)[3]. Therefore, while the “candle wax” sign is diagnostic, other distinct patterns are recognized and important for identifying atypical cases. The disease typically affects a single bone or multiple bones on one side of the body. In cases involving multiple bones, the lesions are often distributed in a continuous pattern, though occasionally they may appear irregularly scattered. In this case, the X-ray findings were consistent with those reported in the literature, showing unilateral upper limb involvement (Fig. 1). Notably, based on the Freyschmidt system, which categorizes melorheostosis into classical (flowing wax), osteoma-like, osteopathia striata-like, myositis ossificans-like, and mixed types, the imaging findings in our patient are most consistent with the osteopathia striata-like subtype[3]. This precise classification moves beyond a simple description of rarity and situates our case within the recognized phenotypic spectrum of the disease.

Yu et al have classified melorheostosis into different subtypes based on its radiological characteristics and the direction of lesion spread: endosteal type (lesions extending into the inner cortex), periosteal type (lesions spreading outward from the cortex), and parosteal type (lesions flowing into adjacent soft tissues). Cases exhibiting features of two or three of these types are categorized as mixed type[8]. This case is classified as the endosteal type. Additionally, CT imaging can provide a clearer definition of the three-dimensional spatial extent of the lesions, while MRI can help determine whether surrounding soft tissues are involved. Histopathological examination lacks specific diagnostic features but typically shows active osteoblasts, prominent bone proliferation and sclerosis, and tightly arranged lamellar bone. The findings in this case are consistent with those reported in the literature. If melorheostosis leads to skeletal deformity and severely impacts the patient’s daily activities, it is advisable to perform a CT scan to further delineate the extent of the lesions and assess the specifics of the deformity, which can aid in preoperative planning for a more tailored surgical approach. If the patient’s melorheostosis does not cause significant symptoms or the discomfort does not interfere with daily life, a CT scan is generally not necessary. When the diagnosis of melorheostosis can be confirmed by X-ray, histological examination is not mandatory. Histopathological examination is primarily indicated to rule out other diseases that may present with similar features.

The management of melorheostosis is primarily symptomatic. For patients without significant pain or deformities, no specific treatment is necessary. For those with pain but no deformities, symptomatic pain management is recommended, including oral analgesics or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [9,10]and local physiotherapy[11]. For patients with pain and deformities, surgical intervention is indicated when conservative treatments are ineffective or when deformities are severe. Corrective surgeries have been shown to significantly alleviate pain and improve functional mobility. For instance, Li Shengjie et al[12] and Yao Shuyuan et al[13] reported cases of patients with severe deformities due to melorheostosis who underwent successful corrective surgeries. Yao Shuyuan et al[13] additionally conducted a 19-year follow-up, showing no symptoms or functional impairments, enabling the patient to participate in sports activities, with no progression of bone sclerosis observed. Iordache et al[14] speculate that melorheostosis may have a potential for malignant transformation or progression, although no definitive clinical evidence has been reported. As a rare disease, the understanding of melorheostosis remains limited, highlighting the importance of long-term follow-up for affected patients.This will provide valuable clinical evidence to enhance the systematic knowledge of this rare condition.

This case illustrates an incidental diagnosis of pediatric multifocal melorheostosis in a unilateral upper limb, confirmed by its pathognomonic “melting wax” radiological appearance and histopathological features of sclerotic endosteal hyperplasia. The asymptomatic presentation over 12 months of follow-up obviated therapeutic intervention, aligning with current management guidelines for nonprogressive cases. Given the rarity of this condition and theoretical concerns about long-term complications (e.g., contractures or malignant transformation), sustained surveillance is essential. Continued documentation of such cases will enhance understanding of melorheostosis’ natural history and refine evidence-based management strategies.
