INTRODUCTION: Holocarboxylase synthetase deficiency (HLCS deficiency, OMIM 
#253270) is an exceedingly rare metabolic disorder resulting in multiple 
carboxylase deficiencies owing to impaired biotin cycle. Clinical manifestations 
include severe metabolic acidosis, hyperammonemia, tachypnea, skin rash, 
alopecia, feeding problems, hypotonia, developmental delay, seizures, and, in 
severe cases, death.
METHODS AND RESULTS: An 8-day-old female neonate presented with severe lactic 
acidosis, necessitating sedation and mechanical ventilation. Despite receiving 
supportive care, no evident clinical improvement was observed, accompanied by 
the onset of generalized ichthyosis. Genetic analysis of actionable metabolic 
disorders revealed compound heterozygous variants of HLCS (NM_000411.8), 
specifically c.[710T>C (p.Leu237Pro)]; [1544G>A (p.Ser515Asn)], prompting the 
initiation of biotin mega-dose therapy (10 mg/day). Remarkably, dramatic 
clinical improvement in lactic acidosis was observed the day after initiating 
biotin administration, leading to the discontinuation of mechanical ventilation 
within 6 days. The patient remained in stable condition during follow-up, 
exhibiting normal growth and development along with consistently stable 
laboratory findings up to 18 months of age.
CONCLUSION: Our case highlights the significance of early genetic testing in 
neonates with unexplained metabolic disorders to enable timely diagnosis and 
therapy initiation. Biotin therapy has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in 
improving the clinical condition of patients with HLCS deficiency, leading to 
favorable outcomes.

Holocarboxylase synthetase deficiency (HLCS deficiency, OMIM #253270) is a rare but treatable inherited metabolic disorder characterized by ineffective utilization of vitamin biotin. It follows an autosomal recessive manner with an estimated prevalence ranging from 1 in 100,000 to 930,000 live births (Donti, Blackburn, and Atwal 2016; Hong et al. 2017; Suzuki et al. 2005). Despite its rarity, there have been no reported cases of HLCS deficiency in Korea.

HLCS deficiency arises from biallelic pathogenic variants in the HLCS gene, leading to the loss of HLCS function. HLCS is responsible for covalently linking biotin, also known as Vitamin H, to various biotin‐dependent carboxylases. Biotin, a water‐soluble vitamin from the B complex, serves as an essential coenzyme for five human carboxylases: propionyl‐CoA carboxylase (PCC), 3‐methylcrotonyl‐CoA carboxylase (MCC), pyruvate carboxylase (PC), and acetyl‐CoA carboxylase (ACC) (Zempleni et al. 2019). Biotinylation of these carboxylases is crucial for their enzymatic activities, and these biotin‐dependent carboxylases also play a critical role in the intermediate metabolism of gluconeogenesis, fatty acid synthesis, and amino acid catabolism (León‐Del‐Río 2019; Said 2012).

HLCS deficiency can be screened through the detection of elevated hydroxypentanoylcarnitine (C5‐OH) levels in neonatal screening tests. Additionally, the urinary organic acid profile may reveal the elevated levels of various organic acid derivatives, including lactic acid, 3‐OH isovaleric acid, 3‐OH propionic acid, 3‐methylcrotonylglycine, methylcitric acid, and tiglylglycine, stemming from the loss of function of multiple biotin‐dependent carboxylases. The clinical manifestations of HLCS deficiency, such as tachypnea, skin rash, hyperammonemia, severe metabolic acidosis, feeding problems, alopecia, hypotonia, developmental delay, and seizures, may manifest as early as a few hours after birth until approximately 15 months of age. Without treatment, infants may progress to profound metabolic acidosis, cerebral edema, coma, and even death (León‐Del‐Río 2019). Supplementation with high dose of free biotin can improve the clinical status of symptomatic HLCS‐deficient patients and prevent most clinical symptoms and signs (Van Hove et al. 2008).

Here, we present the first case of a symptomatic female neonate diagnosed with HLCS deficiency in Korea. She initially presented with severe lactic acidosis and tachypnea on the eighth day of life and exhibited a dramatic response to high‐dose biotin therapy.

A female infant was delivered vaginally at 39 + 5 weeks of gestation without any prenatal or perinatal complications. At birth, her height, weight, and head circumference measured 49.0 cm (30th percentile), 2880 g (15th percentile), and 34.5 cm (49th percentile), respectively. She was the first child of healthy parents with no family history of inherited metabolic diseases. However, her respiratory rate was elevated, reaching 65 breaths/min in the newborn nursery immediately after birth. Despite oxygen supplementation, her respiratory difficulties persisted and were accompanied by lethargy. Consequently, she was transferred to a regional hospital where mechanical ventilation was initiated. Initial capillary blood gas analysis revealed metabolic acidosis and lactic acidosis with respiratory compensation, alongside hyperammonemia (pH 7.25, pCO2 27.8 mmHg, base excess −13.0 mmol/L, lactic acid 16.0 mmol/L, and ammonia 273 μg/dL). During the 7 days of mechanical ventilation support, lactic acidosis and tachypnea recurred intermittently based on the level of sedation. She was subsequently transferred to our hospital at the age of 8 days for further diagnostic evaluation and management.

Upon admission to our hospital, the initial vital signs were as follows: blood pressure of 78/51 mmHg, heart rate of 140 beats per minute, and a body temperature of 37.5°C. The patient was intubated, with a respiratory rate was 60 breaths per minute, with an oxygen saturation of 97% and an FiO2 of 0.21. Physical examinations did not reveal any congenital or dysmorphic anomalies. Although chest wall retraction was observed during respiration, her breathing sounds were clear. Her venous blood gas analysis revealed a pH of 7.426, pCO2 of 24.2 mmHg, pO2 of 49 mmHg, HCO3 of 15.9 mmol/L, base excess of −8, and a high level of lactic acid at 10.09 mmol/L, indicating severe metabolic acidosis and lactic acidosis with compensatory hypocapnia. Additionally, the patient's blood ammonia level increased to 392 μg/dL. Treatment included administration of sodium bicarbonate for metabolic acidosis, and intravenous sodium benzoate, arginine, and L‐carnitine were administered for hyperammonemia management.

Due to an elevated level in 3‐hydroxyisovalerylcarnitine (C5‐OH, 3.6955 μmol/L) on her newborn screening test performed on the third day of life, urine organic acid analysis was performed. It revealed excessive excretion of lactic acid (6419.9 mmol/mol Cr) and 3‐hydroxyisovaleric acid (1956.4 mmol/mol Cr; Figure 1). The patient was placed under deep sedation with vecuronium bromide, mechanical ventilation was continuously provided to manage severe tachypnea from metabolic acidosis. However, the patient's condition did not show significant clinical improvement. On the 23rd day of life, multiple pustules first appeared on the face, followed by the development of generalized erythematous scaly patches extending to the neck. These skin manifestations rapidly progressed, resulting in ichthyosis‐like desquamative eruptions covering her entire body, along with hair loss. To assess the possibility of metabolic encephalopathy and potential damage to the central nervous system associated with persistent and non‐improving lactic acidosis, she underwent brain MRI and MR spectroscopy on the 33rd day after birth. The MRI and spectroscopy revealed T2 hyperintensity (Figure 2) and an increased lactic acid peak in the cerebrospinal fluid. She did not exhibit any symptoms suggestive of seizures during admission, and electroencephalography findings were normal. However, the initial automated auditory brainstem response test showed abnormalities in both ears.

The NeoSeq action panel, a next‐generation sequencing panel designed for the early diagnosis of actionable diseases in infants, including treatable metabolic diseases, was used (Kim et al. 2023). The analysis released two likely pathogenic variants on HLCS (NM_000411.8), c.[710T>C (p.Leu237Pro)]; [1544G>A (p.Ser515Asn)]. To confirm the diagnosis of HLCS deficiency, parental screening was conducted, revealing that each parent was heterozygous for the two identified variants (Figure 3).

Based on the clinical manifestations and genetic testing results, her condition was deemed consistent with HLCS deficiency. High‐dose oral biotin supplementation (10 mg/day) was initiated on the 47th day of life. Biotin therapy demonstrated immediate effects, with plasma lactic acid levels decreasing from 10.76 to 2.87 mmol/L on the day after treatment initiation and remaining within the normal range from the third day onward (Figure 1). Furthermore, metabolic acidosis and hyperammonemia rapidly improved, resulting in successful extubation after 6 days of biotin supplementation. The skin conditions and abnormal brain MR findings also improved swiftly (Figure 2), and the follow‐up automated auditory brainstem response test showed normal results.

After 22 days of biotin therapy, the patient was discharged with continued biotin treatment (10 mg/day). She achieved developmental milestones, such as rolling over and crawling at 6 months of age, and walking independently at 13 months of age. At 17 months of age, cognitive and motor development using the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development showed average levels, while language development was borderline (approximately 6 months delayed). Her growth parameters were within the normal range, with a height of 77 cm (25–50th percentile), weight of 10 kg (50–75th percentile), and head circumference of 47.5 cm (75–90th percentile) at 16 months of age. Biochemical examination revealed normal plasma lactic acid levels and improved plasma C5‐OH levels (1.113 μmol/L; Figure 1). Urinary 3‐hydroxyisovaleric acid, 3‐methylcrotonylglycine, and 3‐hydroxypropionate were undetected.

Biotin is indispensable for the metabolism and viability of all organisms as it serves as a cofactor for biotin‐dependent carboxylases (León‐Del‐Río, Valadez‐Graham, and Gravel 2017). These enzymes are pivotal in gluconeogenesis, fatty acid synthesis, and amino acid catabolism. Initially, they exist in inactive apo‐forms, which are transformed into active holo‐forms through biotinylation facilitated by HLCS, a process essential for their function (Said 2012).

HLCS deficiency is a genetic disorder affecting biotin metabolism, characterized by reduced or absent activity of the HLCS enzyme. Patients with this disorder often present with respiratory and feeding difficulties, along with symptoms such as hypotonia, seizures, skin rash, and alopecia. Developmental delay or coma may also occur. Metabolic acidosis, organic aciduria, and hyperammonemia are common findings. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in the HLCS gene have been identified, resulting in impaired enzyme function, primarily due to reduced affinity (Said 2012; Yang et al. 2001).

HLCS deficiency can potentially be diagnosed during fetal development by evaluating fetal organic acid levels, assessing mitochondrial carboxylase activity in fetal cells, and performing mutational analyses (Said 2012). However, considering the extremely rare frequency of HLCS deficiency, prenatal diagnosis is realistically challenging in cases like our patient without a family history. In most reported cases, infants experiencing metabolic crises after birth undergo conventional tests such as tandem mass spectrometry and urine organic acid analysis. HLCS deficiency can then be inferred through appropriate interpretation, even though initial biochemical tests reveal abnormalities across a wide range of areas, and diagnosis may be challenging. The NeoSeq action panel, a next‐generation sequencing (NGS) test developed for relatively common genetic metabolic disorders with available treatments, offers the advantage of providing a relatively rapid and clear diagnosis (Kim et al. 2023). In our case, the patient could promptly begin a definitive diagnosis and treatment after receiving the test results.

According to the ClinVar database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar), approximately 100 sequence variations related to the HLCS gene are classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic. Our patient exhibited two likely pathogenic variants: c.710T>C (p.Leu237Pro) and c.1544G>A (p.Ser515Asn). Notably, c.710T>C (p.Leu237Pro) is one of the most frequently reported variants among Japanese patients. Haplotype analysis revealed that c.710T>C is a founder variant, along with c.780del (Aoki et al. 1995; Yang et al. 2001). The variant, c.1544G>A (p.Ser515Asn), was frequently found in Chinese patients, with an allele frequency of 4.4%. Other commonly identified variants include c.1522C>T (p.Arg508Trp) and c.1088T>A (p.Val363Asp) with an allele frequency of 45.6% and 14.7%, respectively (Guo, Jiang, and Xu 2023; Ling et al. 2023). Although the correlation between these genetic variants and resulting phenotypes has not yet been clarified, enzymes containing variants located outside the biotin‐binding region (Gly466‐Val674) showed normal to higher affinity for biotin compared to variants inside the biotin‐binding region (Suzuki et al. 2005). The variant c.710T>C (p.Leu237Pro) is known to abolish enzyme activity (Yang et al. 2001) but is located outside the biotin‐binding region. Conversely, the variant c.1544G>A (p.Ser515Asn) is present within this region, and there have been no reports on the residual enzyme activity of this variant. The severe clinical manifestations exhibited by our patient were severe, particularly the neonatal form responsive to biotin may be associated with the c.710T>C variant (p.Leu237Pro).

HLCS deficiency can be treated with oral pharmacological doses of biotin (10–20 mg/day), particularly in patients with biotin‐responsive variants, leading to significant improvements in clinical outcomes (Said 2012). Similarly, our patient demonstrated remarkable improvement in her condition shortly after initiating biotin supplementation. Presently, she is progressing well in her overall development and enjoying quality time with her parents.
