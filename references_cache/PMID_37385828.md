# Using imlifidase to elucidate the characteristics and importance of anti-GBM antibodies produced after start of treatment.
**Authors:** Tyrberg L, Andersson F, Uhlin F, Hellmark T, Segelmark M
**Journal:** Nephrol Dial Transplant (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1093/ndt/gfad132](https://doi.org/10.1093/ndt/gfad132)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Autoantibodies are common in glomerulonephritis, but the clinical benefit of rapid elimination has not been determined, even in anti-glomerular basement membrane (GBM) disease. Even less is known about the importance of autoantibody characteristics, including epitope specificity and immunoglobulin G (IgG) subclass distribution. We aimed to address this by characterizing the autoantibody profile in anti-GBM patients: we utilized samples from the GOOD-IDES-01 (treating GOODpasture's disease with Imunoglobulin G Degrading Enzyme of Streptococcus pyogenous) (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03157037) trial , where imlifidase, which cleaves all IgG in vivo within hours, was given to 15 anti-GBM patients.

METHODS: In the GOOD-IDES-01 trial, plasmapheresis was (re)started if anti-GBM antibodies rebounded. Serum samples were collected prospectively for 6 months and analyzed for anti-GBM epitope specificity using recombinant constructs of the EA and EB epitopes, IgG subclass using monoclonal antibodies, and anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA). The results were correlated with clinical data.

RESULTS: Patients with a rebound (n = 10) tended to have lower eGFR at 6 months (11 vs 34 mL/min/1.73 m2, P = .055), and patients with dialysis at 6 months had a higher EB/EA ratio at rebound (0.8 vs 0.5, P = .047). Moreover, two patients demonstrated increasing epitope restriction and several patients displayed a shift in subclass distribution at rebound. Six patients were double positive for ANCA. ANCA rebound was seen in 50% of patients; only one patient remained ANCA positive at 6 months.

CONCLUSIONS: In this study, rebound of anti-GBM antibodies, especially if directed against the EB epitope, was associated with a worse outcome. This supports the notion that all means should be used to eliminate anti-GBM antibodies. In this study ANCA was removed early and long-term by imlifidase and cyclophosphamide.

What was known:

Autoantibodies are common in glomerulonephritis, but the clinical benefit of rapid elimination is undetermined, even in anti-glomerular basement membrane (GBM) disease, and even less is known about the importance of autoantibody characteristics such as epitope specificity and immunoglobulin G (IgG) subclass distribution, or double positivity for other autoantibodies.

The pharmaceutical agent imlifidase degrades all IgG in vivo within a few hours and was used in the GOOD-IDES-01 trial, offering a unique possibility to clarify the role of autoantibodies in many autoimmune diseases.

The prognosis of anti-GBM nephritis with current standard therapy is disappointing, with less than 30% renal survival after 6–12 months, hence, elucidating the importance and benefit of removing autoantibodies is needed to establish treatment recommendations to improve outcome.

This study adds:

The ongoing production of anti-GBM antibodies despite standard treatment, resulting in a rebound of anti-GBM antibodies, especially if directed against the EB epitope, was associated with a worse outcome in this study.

The subclass distribution of anti-GBM autoantibodies appeared to be dynamic, supporting the notion that the anti-GBM antibody producing plasma cells are relatively short-lived.

In this study anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) were removed both early and long-term by the combination of imlifidase and cyclophosphamide.

Potential impact:

Our findings provide a rationale for using plasma exchange to curb rebound of anti-GBM autoantibodies, at least until more data become available.

This study strengthens the rationale for setting up trials with imlifidase in other renal diseases with pathogenic autoantibodies such as ANCA-associated vasculitis.

Autoantibodies are common in glomerulonephritis and have been described in all major histological forms, most recently anti-nephrin antibodies in minimal change nephropathy and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis [ 1 , 2 ]. The role of autoantibodies in pathogenesis is variable and insufficiently elucidated. Even in anti-glomerular basement membrane (GBM) disease, which by definition is a small vessel vasculitis driven by anti-GBM autoantibody deposition along the GBM [ 3 ], it is not excluded that other mechanisms may operate alongside the autoantibodies. Experimental autoimmune glomerulonephritis (EAG) could be induced by immunization with GBM in animals unable to produce antibodies [ 4 ]. The prognosis of anti-GBM nephritis with current standard therapy is disappointing, with less than 30% overall renal survival after 6–12 months, with variation across subgroups and treatment regimens [ 5 , 6 ]. It is, however, not evident whether this is because current therapy does not remove the autoantibodies efficiently enough, damage at diagnosis is beyond a point of no-return or other mechanisms continue to operate when autoantibodies have been removed by plasma exchange (PLEX). There are reports of an association between anti-GBM antibody levels and outcome [ 7–10 ], but only one recently published study also takes into account the established risk factor renal histology [ 11 ]. Furthermore, two major epitopes on α3(IV)NC1 have been identified and denoted EA and EB [ 12 , 13 ]. It is unclear whether autoantibodies against each of them provide the same risk and should be removed with the same urgency. Anti-GBM antibodies are, in most cases, predominantly of subclass immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) and IgG3 [ 14–17 ], but the importance of subclass distribution is largely unknown.

The pharmaceutical agent imlifidase, which is based on the Immunoglobulin-degrading enzyme of Streptococcus pyogenes (IdeS), degrades all IgG in vivo within a few hours [ 18–20 ]. Imlifidase thus offers a unique possibility to clarify the role of autoantibodies in many autoimmune diseases. In the recently published GOOD-IDES-01 (treating GOODpasture's disease with Imunoglobulin G Degrading Enzyme of Streptococcus pyogenous) (ClinicalTrials .gov identifier: NCT03157037) trial, 15 patients with anti-GBM disease were given imlifidase in addition to standard treatment [ 20 ]. In some patients, anti-GBM antibodies returned after 1–3 weeks [ 20 ] indicating that autoantibody production was still ongoing at diagnosis.

A systematic literature review found that 34% of anti-GBM patients were double positive for anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic autoantibodies (ANCA) [ 21 ]. The benefit of removing ANCAs is not established, possibly in part due to the limited efficacy of PLEX [ 22 ]. As ANCAs are usually of class IgG, they might be subject to cleavage by imlifidase, presenting an opportunity to investigate the tendency to rebound, and the outcome of double-positive patients after treatment with imlifidase.

The aim of this study was to investigate the importance of ongoing autoantibody production at time of diagnosis and the pathogenicity of recently produced autoantibodies. A second aim was to study the characteristics of rebounding anti-GBM antibodies such as epitope specificity, possible epitope spread and changes in subclass distribution. Additionally, we wanted to study the clinical course of patients double positive for anti-GBM antibodies and ANCA or anti-nuclear autoantibodies (ANA) after treatment with imlifidase, and their tendency to rebound.

GOOD-IDES-01 is a single-arm phase II trial of imlifidase in anti-GBM disease (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03157037). In the trial, 15 anti-GBM patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <15 mL/min/1.73 m2 were treated with a single dose of imlifidase in addition to standard therapy and monitored for 6 months [ 20 ]. Blood samples were collected before administering imlifidase and prospectively for 6 months. The samples were frozen at –80°C at the local hospital and sent to the central laboratory for further analyses. Serum samples were analyzed for ANCA and ANA antibody levels at the Department of Clinical Immunology and Transfusion Medicine, Region Östergötland, Linköping, Sweden. eGFR were calculated based on serum creatinine using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease formula. For all calculations in this study, eGFR was set to 0 mL/min/1.73 m2 for dialysis-dependent patients and the patient who died anuric during the study. However, for one patient who only required two dialysis sessions per week at 6 months and could stop dialysis at 8 months, eGFR was arbitrarily set to 5 mL/min/1.73 m2. According to the protocol, treatment with PLEX should be (re)started if anti-GBM antibodies, according to local laboratories, became detectable again after imlifidase treatment. Rebound was defined as such an event. One patient in the study received one PLEX session without a positive anti-GBM antibody test after imlifidase and was thus not categorized as a rebound. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the trial was approved by independent ethics committees and regulatory authorities in all participating countries. All patients in the GOOD-IDES-01 trial provided written informed consent, including exploratory analyses of central laboratory material, which were approved by the Regional Ethical Approval Board in Linköping (dnr 2018/518-32).

For analysis of epitope specificity, recombinant constructs containing the epitopes EA and EB on the NC1-α1 backbone were produced as described by Yang et al. [ 23 ]. Purified recombinant proteins of the NC1-α3 and the NC1-α1 with only the EA or the EB epitope were used as the solid phase in enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analyses of epitope specificity with NC1-α1 as a negative control, as described in Supplementary data. The same positive control was used on all plates to assess interplate variability.

Analysis of subclass distribution was performed in a similar way using ELISA with the NC1-α3 recombinant protein as the solid phase and subclass-specific mouse anti-human secondary antibodies [IgG1 clone no HP6069 (Sigma Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA); IgG2 clone no HP6200 (Mabtech, Stockholm, Sweden); IgG3 clone no HP6050 (Sigma Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA); IgG4 clone no 6025 (Sigma Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA)] as described in the Supplementary data.

For all ELISAs, double samples were analyzed, and their mean value calculated in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft 365MSO, Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Descriptive statistics and calculations were performed in RStudio version 2021.09.2 (Posit Software, Boston, MA, USA). Statistical analyses were performed using Fisher's exact test or Chi2-test for dichotomous data and for continuous data using Mann–Whitney U-test or Kruskal–Wallis test. Plots were created using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

A ratio between reactivity [optical density (OD)] towards the EB and the EA recombinant proteins (EB/EA ratio) was calculated for each patient before treatment and at rebound. Based on the EB/EA ratio the patients were divided into three groups: EA > EB (EB/EA ≤ 0.50), EA ≈ EB (EB/EA 0.51–1.49) and EA < EB (EB/EA ≥ 1.50). Linear regression was used to investigate the relationship between the EB/EA ratio and total level of anti-GBM antibodies represented by the NC1-α3 recombinant protein.

Ratios between the four IgG subclasses were calculated before treatment and at rebound in the same manner as the EB/EA ratio.

According to the GOOD-IDES-01 protocol, PLEX should be restarted if autoantibodies became detectable again after depletion by imlifidase, defined as a rebound in this study. There were 10 patients with a rebound of anti-GBM antibodies, and they did not differ from the five without rebound with respect to age but were more often male (Table 1). In the group with no rebound, all patients were alive and free of dialysis at 6 months while only 50% in the rebound group were dialysis-free at 6 months. When assigning an eGFR of 0 to patients in dialysis or dead at 6 months, the median eGFR was lower in the rebound group (10.7 vs 33.8 mL/min/1.73 m2, P = .055).

There were no differences in outcome between patients with early rebound (Day 4–7, n = 5, 40% renal survival) compared with patients with a late rebound (Day 10–29, n = 5, 60% renal survival). Similarly, there was no difference in outcome between those with a weak rebound (requiring two to four PLEX sessions, n = 5, 40% renal survival) compared with patients with a strong rebound (9–18 PLEX sessions, n = 5, 60% renal survival). Out of six patients in the trial who were double positive for anti-GBM antibodies and ANCA (DP-ANCA), five had a rebound of anti-GBM antibodies.

There was no indication that rebound was affected by treatment such as dose of methylprednisolone or whether cyclophosphamide was given as intermittent intravenous pulses or daily oral doses (Table 1). In the group without rebound, 80% were treated with intravenous cyclophosphamide and in the rebound group the corresponding figure was 60% (P = .7).

Next, we investigated epitope specificity before treatment and at rebound by calculating a ratio of the reactivity with the EA and EB recombinant proteins. Linear regression revealed no relation between the EB/EA ratio and the total level of anti-GBM autoantibodies, expressed as reactivity towards the NC1-α3 recombinant protein (r2 = 0.11). Three patterns of epitope specificity appeared: more reactivity towards EA, more reactivity towards EB or similar levels of reactivity (Fig. 1A–C). Only two patients changed to a different pattern group at rebound; both displayed similar reactivity towards both epitopes before treatment. However, at rebound, one displayed more reactivity towards EA and one displayed more reactivity towards EB. Autoantibodies towards EB were associated with a worse outcome when the EB/EA ratio was compared between patients on and off dialysis at 6 months (0.8 vs 0.5, P = .047, Mann–Whitney U-test). Both patients with EB predominance at rebound remained dialysis dependent (Table 2). In the group with similar reactivity towards both epitopes at rebound, 50% were dialysis dependent or dead at the end of the trial while in the EA group 25% were dialysis dependent at 6 months.

We then analyzed the autoantibody subclass distribution towards the NC1-α3 recombinant protein in rebounding patients before treatment and at rebound. Most patients (60%) had autoantibodies of predominantly subclass IgG1 before treatment, but 30% had predominantly autoantibodies of subclass IgG4 and 10% of subclass IgG2 (Fig. 1D–F). Most patients (70%) displayed similar subclass distribution both before treatment and at rebound. In three cases the reactivity of IgG1 was lower at rebound compared with before treatment, resulting in a different predominating subclass. Ratios of OD measured at rebound for IgG4 and the other three IgG subclasses, respectively, were compared between patients who were free of dialysis at 6 months and patients who remained dialysis dependent (Fig. 2). There was a tendency towards a larger proportion of IgG4-anti-GBM antibodies in dialysis dependent patients, but the difference was not statistically significant for any single subclass.

We compared the relation between epitope restriction and the subclass distribution. There was a tendency for IgG4 predominance to be associated with reactivity towards the EB epitope. Both patients in the EB group and one patient in the group with similar reactivity towards both epitopes had predominantly autoantibodies of subclass IgG4, while none of the patients in the EA group displayed this pattern (Table 2).

We also studied the course of ANCA in the six DP-ANCA patients, of whom four had myeloperoxidase-ANCA (MPO-ANCA) and two proteinase 3-ANCA (PR3-ANCA). The DP-ANCA patients did not differ with respect to age but five of six were males (Table 3). Two and 24 h following administration of imlifidase, no ANCA could be detected in the circulation of any of the patients (Fig. 1G-I). After 2 weeks ANCA had rebounded above the reference level in three of six DP-ANCA patients, two MPO-ANCA-positive patients and one PR3-ANCA-positive patients. However, at 3 and 6 months only the PR3-ANCA-positive patient remained above the reference level.

At 6 months, three of the six DP-ANCA patients were on dialysis treatment; two of them had a rebound of ANCA and all three had a rebound of anti-GBM antibodies. One patient had a rebound of MPO-ANCA but not anti-GBM antibodies, displayed pauci-immune findings on renal biopsy and recovered renal function with an eGFR of 59.1 mL/min/1.73 m2 at 6 months.

Double positivity for anti-GBM antibodies and ANA (DP-ANA) was present in three patients, two of whom were also MPO-ANCA positive (Table 3). All three DP-ANA were male and had a rebound of anti-GBM antibodies. ANA rebounded in two patients at 3 and 6 months, respectively. The two triple-positive patients were dialysis dependent at inclusion and remained so at the end of the trial, but one of them could leave dialysis at 8 months after treatment with imlifidase. The third DP-ANA patient had a renal biopsy showing pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis and was dialysis dependent at diagnosis. This patient was included in the study a month later during a rebound of anti-GBM antibodies, and at that time, he was dialysis-free and remained so during the trial.

In this study we have explored the importance of rebound of circulating autoantibodies after treatment with imlifidase. The GOOD-IDES-01 trial was the first clinical trial where this novel approach to treating autoantibody-mediated diseases was tested, making it possible to distinguish between autoantibodies present at diagnosis and those produced after commencement of treatment. Two thirds of the patients had a rebound of autoantibodies after 4–29 days. As imlifidase has been shown to degrade both circulating and tissue-bound IgG within hours [ 19 , 24 , 25 ], this reappearance of anti-GBM autoantibodies is likely the result of ongoing autoantibody production by plasma cells in the bone marrow at diagnosis, which persisted after treatment with pulse-methylprednisolone and cyclophosphamide. None of the patients in the group without rebound of autoantibodies needed dialysis at 6 months, while 50% of the patients who had a rebound were dialysis dependent then. Furthermore, eGFR at 6 months tended to be lower in the rebound group. Hence, it seems prudent to treat the rebound of anti-GBM after imlifidase with PLEX, to keep autoantibody levels down, until more data become available. This is in line with previous studies on the benefit of rapid autoantibody removal [ 26 , 27 ]. It should be noted, however, that we did not see any association between the magnitude of rebound, measured as the total number of PLEX given, and outcome. The need for pulse-methylprednisolone has never been evaluated in anti-GBM disease, and the optimal dose is not established. All patients but one in the GOOD-IDES-01 trial received pulse-methylprednisolone but doses varied. We saw no tendency for higher doses to better protect from rebound, nor did daily oral cyclophosphamide seem to be superior to intermittent pulse-doses in this respect.

The importance of autoantibody characteristics is largely unknown. We found an association between more reactivity towards the EB epitope at rebound and poor renal outcome. Previous reports on the importance of epitope specificity are inconsistent. In an animal study, only immunization with protein constructs of EA alone induced EAG, while immunization with both constructs augmented disease [ 28 ]. In a cohort of sera from Swedish anti-GBM patients, higher levels of autoantibodies reacting with a recombinant protein containing only EA were found in patients with poor renal outcome [ 8 ]. But, in a Chinese study, high levels of EB autoantibodies were identified as an independent risk factor for oliguria or anuria [ 7 , 29 ] while EA autoantibodies were associated with the percentage of glomerular crescents [ 7 ]. As we have only measured circulating autoantibodies, it is possible that the epitope specificity of kidney-bound autoantibodies differs from our results. Perhaps EA autoantibodies are more often kidney bound resulting in a larger proportion of EB autoantibodies left in the circulation. However, Pedchenko et al. found that both EA and EB were targets of kidney-bound anti-GBM antibodies [ 12 ].

We found no indication of epitope spreading, our results rather suggest an epitope narrowing towards either EA or EB at rebound. This is somewhat in contrast to previous studies showing intramolecular epitope spreading in EAG [ 30 ] and intermolecular epitope spreading between the α-chains [ 31 ]. It is possible that epitope spreading occurs earlier in the pathogenesis before presentation of symptoms and diagnosis.

Consistent with previous literature [ 14–16 ], most patients in our study had a predominance of IgG1 before treatment. Interestingly, at rebound, IgG1 was less clearly predominant, resulting in a different predominant subclass in three patients. Our results do not reveal in which direction this change in subclass distribution occurs, but the dynamic could indicate that anti-GBM antibody producing plasma cells are relatively short-lived.

The patients in our study with more reactivity towards EB also displayed a predominance of subclass IgG4, making it difficult to identify the more important factor. Previous studies of anti-GBM patients with predominantly IgG4-anti-GBM antibodies describe cases with mild renal involvement but more prominent lung engagement [ 32–34 ]. However, in these reports, autoantibodies were directed towards the α3,4,5NC1 hexamers and non-reactive with α3(IV)NC1 monomers exhibiting the EB epitope, and might consequently represent a separate set of IgG4 autoantibodies. Still, as IgG4 is typically formed after prolonged exposure to an antigen, it is possible that patients with predominant IgG4-anti-GBM antibodies have had a longer disease duration before presentation of symptoms and start of treatment, resulting in more chronic changes and an epitope specificity narrowing on EB.

We have also studied the clinical course of patients double positive for anti-GBM antibodies and ANCA and/or ANA. There were six DP-ANCA patients, four being MPO-ANCA positive, hence similar frequencies as presented in the recent literature review [ 21 ]. Following administration of imlifidase, ANCA was rapidly removed in all DP-ANCA patients and while ANCA rebounded within 2 weeks in 50%, long-term clearance of ANCA was observed in five of six patients. At 6 months 50% of DP-ANCA patients remained dialysis dependent. All DP-ANCA and DP-ANA patients had a rebound of at least one autoantibody and rebound of anti-GBM antibodies was more common than in patients single positive for anti-GBM antibodies [6/7 (86%) and 4/8 (50%), respectively]. It has previously been suggested that there are connections between the production of ANCA and anti-GBM [ 21 ], but our study does not provide new data on this relationship. Immunofluorescence did not reveal a linear staining pattern in three of the patients. All three were double positive, two for MPO-ANCA and one for ANA. Two were dialysis dependent at inclusion and all three were dialysis-free at 6 months. Hence, trials of imlifidase in other autoantibody-associated renal diseases could be of great interest. Enghard et al. reported a case of severe AAV where imlifidase treatment was attempted and resulted in improvement of symptoms [ 35 ]. Unfortunately, the patient died, but postmortem autopsy revealed no vasculitic damage indicating that the rapid degradation of ANCA could halt vasculitis progression, but this must be confirmed by larger studies.

This study has several limitations. Most importantly, the sample size is very small, and no statistically sound conclusions can be made. There is also a risk for type II errors, where associations or differences that are actually present are missed. The findings must be interpreted as hypothesis generating, to be addressed in future studies of larger number of patients. Furthermore, we have not calculated standardized ELISA units of reactivity towards the different epitope constructs and there is no standard for IgG subclass determination, and it is therefore not possible to calculate concentrations in standardized units, making it difficult to compare results with other studies. However, our study also has several merits. Samples were taken prospectively according to a fixed protocol during a 6-month period and collected along with clinical data and registration of adverse events. To our knowledge this type of standardized sampling has never been accomplished before in anti-GBM disease.

In conclusion, we have, for the first time, been able to study the importance of ongoing autoantibody production in anti-GBM disease, as treatment with imlifidase rapidly degrades all IgG. In this study, patients with ongoing autoantibody production at diagnosis, persisting despite treatment, displayed a worse renal outcome compared with those where autoantibody production had ceased. This provides a rationale for using PLEX to curb rebound, until more data become available. We also observed that the predominance of IgG1 before treatment with imlifidase was less clear at rebound, indicating that plasma cells producing anti-GBM antibodies are relatively short-lived. Furthermore, we found a possible association between autoantibodies towards the EB epitope at rebound and poor renal outcome, and perhaps also between EB autoantibodies and IgG4. Finally, imlifidase rapidly removed ANCA and ANA in eight double-positive patients in this study, and although 50% of DP-ANCA patients had a short-term rebound, long-term clearance of ANCA was observed in most patients.
