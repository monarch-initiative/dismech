# WNK2 oncogenicity revealed by the discovery and functional characterization of novel gene isoforms in MYD88 mutated Waldenström's Macroglobulinemia.
**Authors:** Guerrera ML, Liu X, Tsakmaklis N, Peachey AL, Kofides A, Hatcher J, Ramirez Gamero A, Guijosa A, Yang G, Sun H, Liu S, Patterson CJ, Meid K, Flynn C, Carrasco RD, Branagan AR, Sarosiek SR, Castillo JJ, Anderson KC, Munshi N, Treon SP, Hunter ZR
**Journal:** Blood Cancer J (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41408-026-01554-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41408-026-01554-3)

## Content

WNK2 is a known tumor suppressor in a set of solid tumors, including glioblastoma multiforme and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. WNK2 functions are largely unknown beyond the kinase domain (KD)-dependent inhibition of ERK1/2 signaling. Waldenström's Macroglobulinemia (WM) is an indolent, yet incurable, B cell lymphoma characterized by highly recurrent MYD88 (MYD88MUT) and CXCR4 (CXCR4MUT) mutations that trigger sustained NF-κB and ERK1/2 signaling. Although not expressed in healthy B cells, WNK2 is a top dysregulated gene in MYD88MUT WM. To study WNK2 regulation and signaling, we performed multi-omics analyses, including bulk and PacBio Iso-Seq RNA-Seq and methylome, in 264 untreated WM and functional studies in cell lines and primary WM cells. Aberrant expression of WNK2 emerged as a near universal feature of early-stage WM and a hallmark of plasma cell-like MYD88MUT WM. We identified novel isoforms that carried a shared aberrant splicing event, either contained or lacked the KD and were highly expressed by the tumor cells, unlike the canonical full-length isoforms. Functionally, WNK2/S-NK1, the most expressed of the KD-lacking isoforms in WM, triggered a pro-inflammatory cascade that activated ERK1/2 and NF-κB signaling. We observed a similar, cancer-specific upregulation of WNK2 in a set of solid tumors, including the highly aggressive cholangiocarcinoma. Our findings reveal an undocumented oncogenic function for WNK2 driven by novel, cancer-specific isoforms and provide a framework for its further investigation as a determinant of disease progression in MYD88MUT WM and a novel therapeutic target in hematological and solid tumor oncology.

Waldenström’s Macroglobulinemia (WM) is a lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma consisting of a bone marrow (BM) infiltrate of IgM-secreting lymphoplasmacytic cells [1], bearing somatic mutations in MYD88L265P (MYD88MUT) and CXCR4 (CXCR4MUT) and heterozygous deletions in chromosome 6q (del6q), in 93–97%, 30–40% and ~50% of cases, respectively [2–8]. MYD88MUT alone is insufficient for lymphomagenesis [9, 10]. CXCR4MUT arise early in the MYD88MUT clone and tend to be subclonal to MYD88MUT; del6q are associated with the transition from the precursor stage of IgM monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) to WM [4, 8]. We previously showed that WNK2 was upregulated in MYD88MUTCXCR4WT, downregulated in MYD88MUTCXCR4MUT and MYD88MUTdel6q, and silenced in MYD88 wild-type CXCR4WT (hence, MYD88WT) cases; no mutations or copy number alterations were observed [6, 7]. Among the key prosurvival pathways in MYD88MUT WM, the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) is constitutively activated by MYD88MUT signaling [2, 11]; the activation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) induces the release of inflammatory cytokines and promotes ibrutinib resistance [12–15].

WNK2 is one of the four members of the With-No-Lysine (WNK) serine/threonine kinase family, characterized by bearing an essential catalytic lysine residue in subdomain I, instead of subdomain II as most other kinases [16]. The WNK2 gene spans about 136 kb on chromosome 9q22.31 and has 15 annotated splice variants. The canonical transcript encodes a large protein carrying a proximal kinase domain as the only structured domain, several disordered regions, and a distal coiled-coil domain [17]. The limited literature published on WNK2 focuses on the kinase domain’s function and related signaling. WNK2 negatively regulates ERK1/2 activation in a kinase-dependent manner through RHOA/RAC1 modulation of PAK1, thus inhibiting cell cycle progression and proliferation [18, 19]. WNK2 acts as a tumor suppressor in several solid tumors, including glioblastoma multiforme and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, where it is primarily silenced by promoter methylation [20–25].

WNK2 is not expressed in healthy hematopoietic cells, except for low expression in plasma cells (PC), while is a top dysregulated gene in MYD88MUT WM. We therefore sought to study WNK2 transcriptional regulation and signaling in MYD88MUT WM. We functionally characterized WNK2/S-NK1, the most abundant of the newly identified isoforms that lack the ERK-inhibitory kinase domain in WM.

Patient samples with a confirmed clinicopathological diagnosis of WM [1] were analyzed (Supplemental Table 1). Samples were collected following informed consent and Dana-Farber Cancer Institute Institutional Review Board approval. Patient CD19+ B cells or CD138+ PC cells were genotyped [3, 5, 26, 27] and used for genomic and validation studies. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for WNK2 was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded BM biopsies of 30 WM and 3 healthy donors (HD) according to standard protocols. Native MYD88MUT(L265P) BCWM.1, MWCL-1 and MYD88MUT(S243N) BCWM.2 (WM); MYD88MUT(L265P) TMD-8, HBL-1, OCI-LY3 and MYD88MUT(S222R) SU-DHL-2 (activated B-cell subtype of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, ABC-DLBCL); MYD88WT OCI-LY7 and OCI-LY19 (germinal center B-cell like DLBCL, GCB-DLBCL); Ramos (Burkitt lymphoma); RPMI-8226 and MM.1S (multiple myeloma, MM), NCBI-BL2126 (normal EBV-transformed B cells) cell lines were used for gene expression studies. Additional information is provided in Supplemental Methods and Table 2.

Transcriptomic data from 264 untreated BM CD19+ WM cells, including 4 MYD88WT WM, and 13 paired HD peripheral blood B cells (HDPB, CD19+CD27-) and memory B cells (HDMB, CD19+CD27+) from an ongoing multi-omics study were analyzed for WNK2 expression [26–28]. Salmon [29] was used to quantitate hg38 Gencode (v46) isoforms augmented by the novel WNK2 isoforms identified in an ongoing PacBio Iso-Seq analysis [30] (Supplemental Table 3 and Data 2-3). WNK2 expression using the augmented gene reference was assessed in brain, colon (n = 50 and n = 13, randomly selected from SRA SRP319543 and SRA SRP450263, respectively) and paired intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma/adjacent non-tumor liver tissues (n = 15, SRA SRP159264). WNK2 expression across multiple solid and hematological tumors vs the relative non-tumor counterparts was profiled using the GEPIA database [31]; a cohort of 200 non-IgM MM was also analyzed (data provided by Dr. Nikhil Munshi). Additional information is provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Target-specific TaqMan qRT-PCR on primary cells and/or cell lines cDNA were performed in triplicate using the QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems; Supplemental Methods and Table 2). The observed recurrent exon skipping event was validated by a multiplex RT-PCR assay (Supplemental Methods and Table 4).

Based on an ongoing multi-omics study [26–28], 111 CpGs along the WNK2 gene were identified and studied. A subset enhanced reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (ERRBS) analysis of DNA from 44 MYD88MUT WM, 6 HDMB and 8 BM CD138+ HD plasma cells (HDPC) was performed. ERRBS findings were validated by bisulfite sequencing PCR (BSP; Supplemental Methods and Table 4). Spearman’s correlation between % DNA methylation at the differentially methylated CpGs (dmCpGs) and WNK2 expression per isoform family was performed for all WM samples.

The WNK2/S-NK1 sequence was cloned from a BM CD19+ WM sample with high WNK2 expression; the artificial WNK2/S-NK1e construct was obtained through the Genewiz gene synthesis service (Supplemental Table 4). Stable WNK2 cell lines were obtained by lentiviral transduction of WM cell lines followed by single-cell clone expansion (Supplemental Methods).

Immunoblotting was performed in primary BM CD19+ WM cells, CD19+ HDPB and WNK2 stable cell lines (Supplemental Table 2). Importantly, freshly collected protein lysates were used per cell line. The nuclear activity of 48 transcription factors (TFs) in WNK2/S-NK1 stable cells vs vector controls was analyzed using the TF Activation Profiling Plate Array I (Signosis Inc.) following the manufacturer’s protocol (Supplemental Methods).

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.1) and R.

We first assessed WNK2 transcriptional levels by RNA-Seq in 264 untreated WM patients. We recently showed that MYD88MUT WM can be subclassified into three distinct subtypes. The Early WM subtype is enriched in early/smoldering WM; as the disease becomes more symptomatic, patients progress from the Early WM into either the B cell-like (BCL) or plasma cell-like (PCL) subtype. In line with recent publications [32, 33], CXCR4MUT are enriched in BCL, del6q in PCL subtyped WM [26, 27]. As expected, healthy B cell controls and MYD88WT WM showed no WNK2 expression in our cohort. Within MYD88MUT patients (n = 260), nearly all Early WM exhibited aberrant expression of WNK2 (median 7.6, range 0-152 Transcripts per Million, TpM) compared to HDMB (median and range 0 TpM, p ≤ 0.0001). In more symptomatic/advanced subtypes, WNK2 expression was either suppressed in the majority of BCL (median 0.2, range 0-27 TpM) or further upregulated in PCL WM (median 22.9, range 0.1-108.6 TpM). Within each subtype, no differences in WNK2 expression were observed based on patient CXCR4 mutational status (Fig. 1A). To contextualize the degree of WNK2 dysregulation observed, we compared WNK2 rank to that of MYD88 as a reference gene in WM. The rank of WNK2 (3,222nd of 22,229 expressed genes) was similar to MYD88 (2,741st of 22,229) in PCL, unlike BCL WM (rank 15,222nd and 2,052nd of 22,310 genes for WNK2 and MYD88, respectively). We validated the RNA-Seq findings in 46 BM CD19+ WM samples by qRT-PCR (Fig. 1B). No expression differences were found in paired BM CD19+ B cells/CD138+ PC from 3 patients (Fig. 1C). By IHC, WNK2 was detected in both lymphoplasmacytic cells and PC in WM, and in HDPC. WNK2 protein levels were concordant with transcriptional levels per patient subtype and genotype (Fig. 1D). Our findings showed that, in contrast to healthy B cells that lack WNK2 expression, nearly all early-stage MYD88MUT WM (Early WM subtype) showed intermediate WNK2 expression levels. Because WNK2 upregulation was exclusively observed in MYD88MUT WM and MYD88MUT is an early event in disease pathogenesis [3, 9, 10, 34, 35], we hypothesized a role for WNK2 in the progression of MYD88MUT WM and sought to investigate whether its expression was driven by MYD88MUT. We quantified WNK2 transcriptional levels in 13 MYD88MUT and MYD88WT lymphoma and MM cell lines by qRT-PCR. Unlike WM samples, all MYD88MUT WM and DLBCL lines showed suppression of WNK2 vs those MYD88WT (Fig. 1E). WNK2 expression was 9-fold higher in a representative CXCR4WT PCL WM sample vs either OCI-LY7 or Ramos, the highest WNK2 expressors (p < 0.0001, Fig. 1F). WNK2 levels did not increase following either MYD88MUT overexpression in endogenously MYD88WT OCI-LY7 and Ramos or time course stimulation of native OCI-LY7, Ramos and RPMI-8226 with LPS, CpG ODNs or IL-6 (Supplemental Fig. 1).

The aberrant expression of WNK2 in tumor B/PC cells from early-stage MYD88MUT WM and the differential transcriptional regulation with disease progression contrast with the notion of WNK2 acting as a tumor suppressor [20–25]. We therefore profiled its expression in a broader spectrum of hematological and solid cancers. In a cohort of 200 non-IgM MM patients, no significant changes in WNK2 expression were observed across non-hyperdiploid, hyperdiploid and not otherwise specified MM and compared to normal PC; only a small fraction of cases showed WNK2 upregulation (Supplemental Fig. 2A). Using the GEPIA database [31], DLBCL tumors showed no significant differences in WNK2 expression vs non-tumor samples; a cluster of high WNK2 expressing tumors was observed (Supplemental Fig. 2B). Moreover, while WNK2 was typically silenced in the previously reported solid tumors [20–25], it was instead upregulated in cholangiocarcinoma, esophageal carcinoma, lung squamous cell carcinoma, ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma, and thymoma vs the respective non-tumor tissues, similarly to WM (Fig. 1G). These findings suggested a dimorphic nature of WNK2 able to act as either a tumor suppressor or an oncogene in a tissue and cancer-specific fashion. Promoter methylation is a primary mechanism of WNK2 silencing in MYD88WT WM and several solid tumors [6, 20–23]. Our group and others recently reported two distinct DNA methylation-based subtypes of WM [28, 36] and aberrant RNA splicing and intron retentions in WM [30, 37]. We therefore sought to study CpG methylation and alternative splicing as transcriptional regulators of WNK2 in WM.

We first investigated whether WNK2 CpG methylation differed within MYD88MUT WM and vs HD by ERRBS. We recently reported that the CpG methylation profile of BCL WM clustered with HDMB, while PCL WM clustered with IgM MM [28]. Out of 111 CpGs along the WNK2 gene, 17 were differentially methylated CpG (dmCpGs, Supplemental Table 5). CpGs 1-4 were located in the WNK2 promoter region, CpGs 5-17 in an annotated enhancer in intron 1 (GeneHancer GH09J093200, Fig. 2A). PCL WM showed significant intronic enhancer hypomethylation in 13/13 (100%), 6/13 (46.1%) and 7/13 (53.8%) dmCpGs vs HDPC, HDMB and BCL WM, respectively (Fig. 2B-D). At the promoter, PCL WM revealed similar methylation levels of HDPC and significant hypomethylation in 4/4 (100%) dmCpGs vs BCL (Fig. 2D, Supplemental Fig. 3A). BCL WM showed similar methylation levels at the promoter region and enhancer hypomethylation in 2/13 (15.4%) and hypermethylation in 1/13 (7.7%) dmCpGs vs HDMB (Supplemental Fig. 3A-D). We validated the ERRBS-predicted methylation status at CpGs 7-9 in 10 WM patients via BSP (Fig. 2E). By hierarchical clustering analysis, the dmCpGs clustered based on their location. As expected, HDPC formed a unique cluster bearing high intronic enhancer methylation levels, and HDMB co-clustered with BCL WM. WM samples clustered predominantly based on subtype, with PCL showing prevalent enhancer hypomethylation and BCL a methylation pattern intermediate between HDPC and PCL. Early WM clustered with either PCL or BCL WM, thus suggesting that methylation of WNK2 dmCpGs may not be directly linked to the progression into either subtype (Fig. 2F). Our findings suggested that sustained WNK2 CpG hypomethylation, particularly at an intronic enhancer, is a hallmark of PCL WM vs BCL WM and HD. We used the standard Gencode reference augmented by the 11 novel isoforms discovered by PacBio Iso-Seq analysis to study WNK2 alternative splicing in our cohort of MYD88MUT WM. We grouped the WNK2 transcripts into four families and focused on the first three: i) the Canonical Kinase domain (C-K) family contains the 4 Gencode protein-coding transcripts that encompass the gene full-length; ii) the Spliced Kinase domain (S-K) family contains 4 novel transcripts whose sequences differed from the C-K isoforms only for the aberrant skipping of two distal exons (exons 27-28 using the canonical transcript as reference); iii) the Spliced Non-Kinase domain (S-NK) family contains 1 Gencode and 6 novel transcripts characterized by the skipping of exons 27-28 and an internal transcription start site resulting in short transcript length and lack of the kinase domain; iv) the Other family is a highly heterogenous group that contains the remaining 11 transcripts (Supplemental Table 3, Supplemental Fig. 4 and 5, Supplemental Data 2 and 3). We observed that the skipping of exons 27-28 was recurrent in all 10 novel isoforms encompassing that genomic region and absent in the 4 C-K isoforms (Fig. 3A, Supplemental Table 3). To determine the relative expression of the different isoform families, we focused on the 111/260 (43%) WM bearing high WNK2 levels ( > 5 TpM). In this subset of patients, the novel S-K and S-NK isoforms jointly accounted for 42% of total WNK2 expression vs 6.1% of C-K-related transcription, regardless of disease subtype (p < 0.0001); the ‘Other’ isoforms accounted for about 50% of gene expression (Fig. 3B-C). The RNA-Seq raw read coverage confirmed that WNK2 expression in WM was significantly skewed towards exons 27-28 skipping vs inclusion in the majority of transcripts (Fig. 3D). We validated this finding in 28 WM patients via multiplex RT-PCR. High expression of transcripts skipping exon 27-28 was detected in Early WM and increased in PCL WM (p = 0.028). Despite being less expressed, a similar transcriptional pattern was observed for the control WNK2 transcript per patient subtype (p = 0.0009, Fig. 3E). Next, we sought to comparatively study the WNK2 isoform landscape across normal and diseased tissues by leveraging public RNA-Seq datasets of normal brain and colon, and paired cholangiocarcinoma/adjacent non-tumor liver tissues. In brain samples, median WNK2 expression was 55.1 TpM (Fig. 3F); the C-K family was significantly more expressed than both S-K and S-NK (p < 0.0001, Fig. 3G). In normal colon, median expression of WNK2 was 57.1 TpM (Fig. 3F); the C-K and S-K families were minimally/not expressed (Fig. 3G). Similarly to healthy B vs WM cells, WNK2 expression was upregulated in cholangiocarcinoma vs paired normal liver tissues (18.1 vs 0.4 median TpM, p = 0.002, Fig. 3F); in 9/15 (60%) tumors bearing high WNK2 ( > 5 TpM), the relative expression of C-K, S-K, and S-NK was 22.3%, 13.5% and 20.5%, respectively (Fig. 3G). When comparing tissue-specific relative expression of WNK2 isoform families vs normal brain, C-K expression was significantly decreased in normal colon and WM (p < 0.0001); S-K expression was cancer-specific in both WM and cholangiocarcinoma (p < 0.0001) and absent in normal colon; and S-NK expression was significantly increased in WM, cholangiocarcinoma and normal colon tissues (p < 0.0001, Fig. 3G). Within the S-NK family, the isoform usage varied across tissues in line with the heterogeneous cell types. In the cancer setting, WNK2/S-NK1 was the predominant transcript in WM and WNK2/S-NK3 in cholangiocarcinoma (Fig. 3H). Our findings suggest a role for the non-kinase S-NK isoforms in colon physiological function, in contrast to their cancer-specific upregulation and usage in WM and cholangiocarcinoma. We hypothesized that spliced WNK2 may contribute to MYD88MUT WM oncogenesis and therefore sought to functionally characterize the effects of WNK2/S-NK1 overexpression in WM cell lines.

Unlike the S-K isoforms, S-NK isoforms are suitable for overexpression by lentiviral transduction in B cells and therefore a suitable model to study the functional effects of skipping exons 27-28 in the absence of the kinase domain in BCWM.1 and MWCL-1. We generated stable cell lines overexpressing either WNK2/S-NK1 or an artificial WNK2 transcript, obtained by inserting exons 27-28 into the backbone sequence of WNK2/S-NK1 (hence, WNK2/S-NK1e), as a control (Fig. 4A). Compared to a representative CXCR4WT PCL WM, WNK2 expression levels were higher in BCWM.1WNK2/S-NK1 (p = 0.02) and lower in MWCL-1WNK2/S-NK1 cells (p = 0.009, Fig. 4G), thus suggesting that BCWM.1WNK2/S-NK1 may more closely model WNK2 biology in PCL WM. Overexpression of either WNK2/S-NK1 or WNK2/S-NK1e generated multiple bands of different molecular weight, thus suggesting that different post-translational modifications (PTMs) may be at play and differentially affect WNK2 signaling in the two cell models (Fig. 4B-C). Stable BCWM.1WNK2/S-NK1 and MWCL-1WNK2/S-NK1 cells showed increased phosphorylation of ERK1/2 and the downstream target p90RSK; BCWM.1WNK2/S-NK1 also showed increased activation of NF-κB. Phosphorylation of ERK1/2 reverted to vector control levels in both BCWM.1WNK2/S-NK1e and MWCL-1WNK2/S-NK1e cells; decreased phosphorylation of NF-κB was also observed in BCWM.1WNK2/S-NK1e (Fig. 4B-C). To validate our findings, we profiled the nuclear activation of 48 TFs and observed a significant enrichment of ERK1/2 and NF-κB-related TFs, including AP1, AR, CREB, PPAR, in both BCWM.1WNK2/S-NK1 and MWCL-1WNK2/S-NK1 cells vs their vector controls; BCWM.1WNK2/S-NK1 cells showed a stronger TF signature in line with the higher transduction efficiency achieved (Fig. 4D-E, Supplemental Fig. 6A-B). In both models, overexpression of either WNK2/S-NK1 or WNK2/S-NK1e did not significantly impact cell proliferation vs vector controls (data not shown). PAK1 has been described as the mediator of WNK2-related inhibition of ERK1/2 [18, 19]. We observed increased phosphorylation of ERK1/2 and PAK1 in primary WM BM CD19+ cells expressing WNK2 (Fig. 4F). Compared to vector controls, BCWM.1WNK2/S-NK1 cells showed upregulation of CXCL13 (mean log2 fold change 9.2), S100A9 (4.5), S100A8 (4.1), CXCL10 (3.4), FCER1G (3.5), CCL3 (2.3), AGER (1.3), CCL5 (1.2), and STAT3 (0.8) and downregulation of C5AR1 (−3.6), CCL22 (−2.1), and LGALS9 (−0.5). BCWM.1WNK2/S-NK1e transcriptional changes trended in the same direction as in BCWM.1WNK2/S-NK1 with a greater degree of interreplicate variability (Fig. 4G-H). MWCL-1WNK2/S-NK1 cells showed downregulation of CXCL10 (−3.8), S100A9 (−1.4), S100A8 (−1.3) vs vector controls (Supplemental Fig. 6C); the observed transcriptional differences between BCWM.1WNK2/S-NK1 and MWCL-1WNK2/S-NK1 may be due to the different genomic background, including monoallelic chr17p13 deletion and TP53MUT in MWCL1 [38]. Taken together, our data suggested that the novel and cancer-specific S-NK isoforms drive WNK2 oncogenicity in WM by triggering a pro-inflammatory cascade that activates the ERK1/2 and NF-κB signaling. These effects are kinase domain-independent and are mediated by the observed aberrant exon skipping event (Supplemental Fig. 7). The tumor-driven inflammation likely promotes a dysfunctional BM microenvironment, in turn supportive of the tumor growth, which is a hallmark of IgM MGUS, the precursor stage of WM [39]. Because of their extensive length, the functional characterization of the cancer-specific S-K isoforms in B cells remains at this time limited by the lack of suitable technologies. The current lack of commercially available drugs specifically targeting WNK2 prevents in vitro studies to assess the cytotoxic effects of its blockade in primary WM samples. Further studies are warranted to develop WNK2-specific inhibitors and test their B cell-selectivity.

In this study, we sought to delineate the role of aberrantly expressed WNK2 in MYD88MUT WM by investigating mechanisms of transcriptional regulation and signaling. WNK2 is a so-called dark serine/threonine protein kinase that acts as a tumor suppressor in several solid tumors, where it is primarily silenced by promoter methylation [20–25]. The limited literature on this target revealed that the protein kinase domain is required for the negative regulation of ERK1/2 [18, 19]. In contrast with the tumor suppressor function and the absence of expression in healthy B cells, we described a high selective pressure to dysregulate WNK2 expression from early stages of MYD88MUT WM via i) a MYD88-independent mechanism likely linked to aberrant methylation changes and ii) the high expression of previously undocumented and cancer-specific gene isoforms, associated with the iii) concurrent low expression of canonical full-length C-K isoforms with putative tumor suppressor function. Based on the WM subtypes we recently described [26, 27], WNK2 is aberrantly upregulated in nearly all Early WM, regardless of genotype, and, as patients progress into either the BCL or PCL subtype with increasingly symptomatic disease, WNK2 gets either silenced or further upregulated, respectively. Because MYD88MUT is an early event in WM pathogenesis and a second hit is likely needed for lymphomagenesis [3, 9, 10, 34, 35], WNK2 may be a crucial determinant of disease progression and cell fate determination. Aberrant methylation caused generalized hypomethylation, particularly at an intronic enhancer, in PCL subtyped WM and promoter hypermethylation, similarly to HDMB, in BCL. While sample CpG methylation status negatively correlated with WNK2 expression, it did not explain the preferential expression of the novel vs the canonical full-length isoforms. We recently showed that changes in methylation and chromatin accessibility underlie subtype classification and disease evolution in WM [26–28]. Further studies are warranted to extrapolate the WNK2-specific gene signature, study WNK2 signaling in MYD88MUTCXCR4WT
vs MYD88MUTCXCR4MUT with detectable gene expression and investigate the role of chromatin accessibility on differential WNK2 expression across subtypes and disease stages.

Here, we reported for the first time novel, cancer-specific WNK2 isoforms discovered by combined RNA-Seq and PacBio Iso-Seq analyses, that jointly accounted for 42% of total gene expression in contrast to the significant downregulation of canonical full-length isoforms with putative tumor suppressor function in WM. The novel isoforms bear a common aberrant exon skipping event and either contain (S-K) or lack (S-NK) the ERK-inhibitory kinase domain. Isoforms of the highly heterogenous ‘Other’ family account for about 50% of WNK2 transcription. Regardless of WM subtype, our data showed the predominance of ERK-enabling vs ERK-inhibitory WNK2 isoforms. Cancer-associated RNA splicing dysregulation can sustain oncogenesis by contributing to increased cell proliferation, decreased apoptosis, enhanced migration and metastatic ability, chemoresistance and immune evasion [40]. In WM, aberrant splicing and intron retentions have been described for genes regulating splicing, NF-κB, ERK1/2 and TP53 in WM [30, 37]. Our isoforms’ findings were particularly compelling considering that WNK2 is an intrinsically disordered protein (IDP) [41] that carries the kinase domain as the solely structured domain. Unable to fold spontaneously into stable globular three-dimensional structures, IDPs dynamically fluctuate rapidly through various conformations and interact with different binding partners with high specificity and modest affinity, thus representing master regulators of signaling pathways and key cellular processes, including transcription, translation, and cell cycle [42, 43]. Here, we described a novel oncogenic function for WNK2 in WM related to the overexpression of the WNK2/S-NK1 isoform, an enabler of the ERK1/2 and NF-κB prosurvival signaling via the aberrant skipping of exons 27-28. Despite not directly affecting cell proliferation, WNK2/S-NK1 overexpression transcriptionally modulated genes involved in inflammation and neutrophil chemotaxis, including upregulating the S100A8 and S100A9 alarmins and the pro-inflammatory chemokines CXCL13, CXCL10, CCL3, CCL5. Among their many functions, the S100A8 and S100A9 alarmins induce chemotaxis and activation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), a potent immunosuppressive cell type, in inflammatory diseases and cancers [44, 45]. MDSCs play a critical role in suppressing T cell response and inducing T cell tolerance in cancer [45, 46]. A recent study has described the expansion of polymorphonuclear-MDSC in WM [47]. Prosurvival and inflammatory cytokines play a key role in WM biology, of which IL-6 is the most appreciated [12, 14, 48]. An inflammatory type of WM characterized by high CRP levels has been recently described [49, 50]. For the first time, our study showed WM cells being able to express alarmins and pro-inflammatory chemokines, including CXCL13, atypical for B cells, thus potentially promoting an inflammatory BM microenvironment that may both support the malignant clone and lead to immune exhaustion. Our findings are particularly compelling given that WNK2 is aberrantly upregulated from early-stage WM and a recent single-cell multi-omics study described myeloid inflammation and immune dysfunction, particularly of the T cell compartment, as a hallmark of IgM MGUS, the precursor stage of WM [39]. Interestingly, about 50% of IgM MGUS are MYD88MUT and at higher risk of progression to WM vs those MYD88WT [3, 34, 35]. As for the cell lines, BCWM.1 and MWCL-1 independence from the microenvironment may account for their native silencing of WNK2.

Finally, our study suggested a broader oncological relevance of our findings, including in cholangiocarcinoma, esophageal carcinoma, lung squamous cell carcinoma, ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma and thymoma, where WNK2 is upregulated in the tumor vs non-tumor tissues, similarly to WM. Our comparative RNA-Seq studies of WM and cholangiocarcinoma, a highly aggressive and chemotherapy-refractory tumor of the biliary tract with a dismal prognosis [51], vs normal brain and colon samples showed a similar transcriptional dysregulation of WNK2 and the cancer-specific upregulation of the novel S-K and S-NK isoform families in both WM and cholangiocarcinoma. Normal colon tissues expressed WNK2/S-NK isoforms and lacked canonical full-length isoforms, thus suggesting a physiological role for short transcripts lacking the kinase domain in this organ, as opposed to their aberrant upregulation and usage in WM and cholangiocarcinoma. WNK2 upregulation was observed only in a small subset of MM and DLBCL samples. A recent study defined a clinically and biologically distinct subgroup of GCB-DLBCL with poor outcomes after standard R-CHOP immunochemotherapy, characterized by a unique 104-gene double-hit signature, that includes WNK2 as one of the upregulated genes. Outcomes for this subgroup were comparable to those of ABC-DLBCL patients [52]. Unfortunately, pharmacological targeting of WNK2 is currently not achievable due to the lack of WNK2-selective inhibitors. Further studies are warranted to develop such drugs and test the effects of WNK2 blockade in tumor tissues.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrated for the first time the dimorphic nature of WNK2 that can act as an oncogene or a tumor suppressor based on isoform expression in a tissue and cancer-specific fashion and underscored the complex biology that can arise from systemic isoform dysregulation of key regulatory genes. In WM, we showed that the aberrant transcriptional upregulation of WNK2 is universally occurring in early-stage MYD88MUT WM and represents a hallmark of PCL WM, a more symptomatic/advanced subtype of WM. For the first time, we described a novel oncogenic role for WNK2 driven by the cancer-specific expression of novel and aberrantly spliced gene isoforms, of which the dominant S-NK isoform promotes inflammation and myeloid chemotaxis, thus likely contributing to the myeloid inflammation and immune dysfunction occurring from the precursor stage of IgM MGUS [39]. Our study establishes the foundation for further investigation of WNK2 as a novel determinant of disease progression in MYD88MUT WM and therapeutic target in WM and other hematological and solid cancers where its expression is aberrantly upregulated.
