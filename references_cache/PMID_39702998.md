# Transfer RNA and small molecule therapeutics for aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase diseases.
**Authors:** Samuels TN, Wu F, Mahmood M, Abuzaid WA, Sun N, Moresco A, Siu VM, O'Donoghue P, Heinemann IU
**Journal:** FEBS J (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1111/febs.17361](https://doi.org/10.1111/febs.17361)

## Content

Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases catalyze the ligation of a specific amino acid to its cognate tRNA. The resulting aminoacyl-tRNAs are indispensable intermediates in protein biosynthesis, facilitating the precise decoding of the genetic code. Pathogenic alleles in the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases can lead to several dominant and recessive disorders. To date, disease-specific treatments for these conditions are largely unavailable. We review pathogenic human synthetase alleles, the molecular and cellular mechanisms of tRNA synthetase diseases, and emerging approaches to allele-specific treatments, including small molecules and nucleic acid-based therapeutics. Current treatment approaches to rescue defective or dysfunctional tRNA synthetase mutants include supplementation with cognate amino acids and delivery of cognate tRNAs to alleviate bottlenecks in translation. Complementary approaches use inhibitors to target the integrated stress response, which can be dysregulated in tRNA synthetase diseases.

Aminoacyl‐tRNA synthetases (aaRS) are highly conserved and essential enzymes that play a vital role in protein synthesis in every living cell [1]. Each aaRS catalyzes the ligation of a specific transfer RNA (tRNA) with its corresponding or cognate amino acid to form the aminoacyl‐tRNA substrates that the ribosome uses to decode the genetic code and synthesize proteins according to the genetic instructions in messenger RNAs (mRNAs) [2]. The aaRSs catalyze tRNA aminoacylation in a two‐step adenosine triphosphate (ATP)‐dependent reaction. The first reaction occurs following the binding of the amino acid to the active site along with ATP, and catalysis results in the formation of an aminoacyl‐adenylate complex and the release of pyrophosphate (PPi). In a second step, the aaRS ligates the activated amino acid to the 3′ end of the bound tRNA, yielding the final aminoacyl‐tRNA and releasing adenosine monophosphate (AMP) as a byproduct (Fig. 1).

The aaRS binds ATP and the amino acid to form an aminoacyl adenylate intermediate, releasing pyrophosphate. Subsequently, the cognate tRNA is bound, and the transesterification reaction is catalyzed releasing aminoacylated tRNA and AMP. To ensure translational fidelity, each aaRS discriminates against non‐cognate tRNAs utilizing so‐called identity elements in their cognate tRNAs as well as anti‐determinants found in non‐cognate tRNAs [3]. Similarly, aaRSs specifically accommodate their substrate amino acid in the active site by using the residues that line the amino acid binding pocket to generate steric and electrostatic complementarity with the cognate amino acid. Structurally, synthetases are organized in two independently evolved enzyme families of class I and class II aaRSs [4] (Fig. 2). The class I aaRSs evolved with a Rossman fold catalytic core domain of parallel beta‐sheets flanked by alpha‐helices, while the class II aaRSs include a rare biotin synthetase‐like catalytic core domain of anti‐parallel beta‐sheets surrounded by alpha‐helices. The two classes approach the tRNA from opposing sides. Except for tryptophanyl‐(WARS) and tyrosyl‐tRNA synthetases (YARS), the class I aaRSs bind to the minor groove side of the tRNA. In contrast, class II aaRSs approach the major groove side of the tRNA. Each class accounts for about half of the 20 proteinogenic amino acids; however, many fascinating exceptions exist [5], such as lysyl‐tRNA synthetase (KARS) which is found in independently evolved versions, one from class I and the other version from class II [6].

While many aaRSs can accurately distinguish the cognate amino acid from the cellular amino acid pool, others can discriminate against most, but not all competing amino acids in the active site resulting in mis‐aminoacylation of the tRNA [7]. To ensure only correctly charged tRNAs are released from the synthetase, these aaRSs include an editing domain, where certain non‐cognate amino acids are recognized and cleaved from the mis‐aminoacylated tRNA. Amino acid and tRNA recognition, as well as tRNA editing, have been previously reviewed in detail [8]. These proofreading activities help maintain an accurate aminoacyl‐tRNA pool for the ribosome to decode each codon in the mRNA and incorporate the correct amino acid into the growing polypeptide chain. Together, the accurate and selective aminoacylation of tRNAs, as well as the anticodon‐codon pairing on the ribosome ensure fidelity of the proteome [9, 10]. In terms of nomenclature, the aaRSs are often referred to in a 3‐letter amino acid abbreviation, e.g. HisRS for histidyl‐tRNA synthetase; however, studies in human genetics often rather use a one‐letter code abbreviation, e.g. HARS for histidyl‐tRNA synthetase. Since this review focuses on human genetic diseases, we will use the latter nomenclature for all aaRSs.

In eukaryotes, protein synthesis occurs in both the cytosol and the mitochondria, and for 17 out of the 20 standard amino acids, two separate copies of the corresponding aaRSs are encoded in the nuclear genome and localized to either the cytosol (aaRS1) or the mitochondria (aaRS2) after translation [11] (Fig. 2). Fascinatingly, cytosolic glutamyl and prolyl‐tRNA synthetases are produced as a glutamyl‐prolyl‐tRNA synthetase (EPRS) fusion protein with dual activity [12], whereas the mitochondrial counterparts are expressed as separate proteins. Both KARS and glycyl‐tRNA synthetase (GARS) are encoded as bifunctional proteins transcribed from the same gene (Fig. 2). Two isoforms of GARS are generated by alternative translation initiation [13], while KARS isoforms are produced by alternative splicing for KARS [14].

Cytosolic glutaminyl‐tRNAGln (Gln‐tRNAGln) is synthesized by a dedicated glutaminyl‐tRNA synthetase (QARS), while mitochondrial glutaminyl‐tRNAGln is synthesized indirectly using a glutamate intermediate, in a pathway prevalent in bacteria. In the indirect aminoacylation mechanism, tRNAGln is first charged with glutamic acid (Glu) by mitochondrial glutamyl‐tRNA synthetase (EARS2). Glu‐tRNAGln is then transamidated to Gln‐tRNAGln, by the glutamyl‐tRNAGln amidotransferase protein complex (GATCAB) using free glutamine as an amide donor [15, 16]. Such an indirect pathway for aminoacyl‐tRNA synthesis is also used in the cytosol for the synthesis of the 21st genetically encoded amino acid, selenocysteine. The tRNASec is ‘mis‐aminoacylated’ by seryl‐tRNA synthetase (SARS) with serine (Ser) to produce Ser‐tRNASec. Ser‐tRNASec is subsequently phosphorylated by O‐phosphoseryl‐tRNASec kinase (PSTK), which is finally converted to Sec‐tRNASec by O‐phosphoseryl (Sep)‐tRNA:Sec tRNA synthase (SepSecS) [17].

In the cytosol, nine of the 20 aaRSs are integrated into the cytoplasmic multi‐tRNA synthetase complex (MSC) along with three non‐synthetase proteins. The MSC includes the aaRS complex‐interacting multifunctional proteins (AIMP‐1, ‐2 and ‐3) [18, 19], the dual activity EPRS, arginyl‐tRNA synthetase (RARS), aspartyl‐tRNA synthetase (DARS), glutaminyl‐tRNA synthetase (QARS), isoleucyl‐tRNA synthetase (IARS), leucyl‐tRNA synthetase (LARS), KARS, and methionyl‐tRNA synthetase (MARS). The MSC spatially coordinates tRNA aminoacylation activity with translation at the ribosome and is thought to increase protein synthesis efficiency, but is also thought to play a role in other biological processes, such as RNA processing, protein homeostasis and intracellular signaling [20]. Valyl‐tRNA synthetase (VARS) is not a part of the MSC, but forms the VARS‐EF‐1H complex with the four subunits of elongation factor 1 (EF‐1H) [21]. It is thought that co‐localization of the proteins is achieved through coordinated mRNA localization and translation [22], where components of translation machinery are spatially organized to coordinate protein synthesis with other biological processes [23, 24].

Outside of the canonical aminoacylation function of synthetases, many aaRSs have additional non‐canonical or moonlighting functions. For example, KARS, which is usually part of the MSC, dissociates from the MSC upon phosphorylation and associates with microphthalmia transcription factor (MITF) to activate transcription of Ap4A in mast cells [25]. The same KARS phosphorylation‐dependent pathway also up‐regulates HIV‐1 transcription in HEK 293T cells [26]. KARS also associates with upstream transcription factor 2 (USF2), suggesting that KARS and Ap4A may be involved in general regulation of gene transcription [27]. Similarly, the bifunctional EPRS can also be released from the MSC upon phosphorylation and can then become part of the interferon (IFN)‐gamma‐activated inhibitor of translation (GAIT) complex, specifically silencing ceruloplasmin (Cp) mRNA translation, resulting in inflammation‐responsive gene‐specific translational silencing [28]. A recent study showed that TARS2 interacts with the SARS‐CoV‐2M protein during infection and showed deregulated expression of HARS2 and EARS2 during COVID‐19 infection, indicating a role of some aaRS in viral infection [29]. Other non‐canonical functions of TARS in the activation or inhibition of intracellular signaling pathways were recently reviewed in detail [30]. These examples of synthetases moonlighting in transcriptional regulation and other pathways indicate that we can anticipate the discovery of many more non‐canonical aaRS functions in the future that may represent relevant therapeutic targets for infectious disease treatments [31].

Mutations in both mitochondrial and cytosolic aaRS are associated with genetic diseases in humans. Mutations in multiple aaRSs cause a multitude of disorders that can affect the nervous system, skeletal muscles, liver, and heart, or can cause developmental delays and multisystem disorders [32]. Genetically, these mutations are categorized as monoallelic or biallelic. Monoallelic mutations in several aaRSs are associated with axonal Charcot–Marie‐Tooth type 2 (CMT2) disease, while biallelic mutations in aaRS genes are often associated with multi‐tissue syndromes [32]. Many comprehensive reviews of disease‐causing mutations in cytoplasmic [32, 33, 34, 35, 36], bifunctional [37] and mitochondrial aaRSs [35, 38, 39] have enriched our understanding of the role of aaRS mutations in disease. We will, therefore, focus on the most prevalent diseases caused by aaRS mutations and review the progress towards therapeutic approaches, including new treatments and clinical trials to cure ARSopathies.

Mutations in several aaRS genes are associated with CMT disease [40]. CMT is a genetic disorder with heterogenous modes of inheritance that results in progressive chronic neuropathy. CMT has the highest incidence of any neurological disease in humans, affecting 1 in 2500 people. While CMT differs in severity depending on the affected allele and specific disease‐causing mutation, disease onset usually occurs within the first two decades of life [41]. The major CMT subtypes CMT1 and CMT2 can be distinguished using electrophysiological studies, such as testing nerve conduction velocity (NCV), which measures the speed at which an electrical signal is propagated through the nerves [42]. CMT1 causes demyelination and is characterized by slower median or ulnar NCV. CMT2 patients present with a normal NCV but reduced muscle action potential, which is the first neuromuscular event that ultimately results in muscle contraction [42]. CMT disease presentation is characterized by weakness and loss of muscle stretch reflex, which begins in the distal limbs and progresses to the body, and bone deformities often arise [43]. In addition, patients also present with loss of sensation to peripheral stimuli such as pain, temperature, or vibration in the legs [44]. Seven different aaRS have been associated with CMT2, with most of the mutations found in GARS [45, 46]. I addition, several mutations in YARS [47], AARS [48], HARS [49], MARS [50], and WARS [51] have been well characterized as CMT causative. Most recently, dominant mutations in asparaginyl‐tRNA synthetase (NARS1) were identified as causative for a sensorimotor axonal neuropathy phenotype [52].

Most of the CMT‐associated aaRS variants are functionally dimeric and are not part of the MSC [18], with MARS presenting an exception, as this synthetase is both monomeric and part of the MSC. Nonetheless, none of the CMT‐associated MARS mutations are thought to impact the formation of the MSC. Biochemical assays and studies in several model systems showed that both loss‐of‐function and toxic gain‐of‐function mutations found in different aaRSs can all lead to CMT phenotypes [46].

Biallelic disease‐causing variants are found in both mitochondrial and cytoplasmic aaRS genes as recently reviewed [36]. In most cases, parents of individuals with biallelic (homozygous or compound heterozygous) variants are asymptomatic as they are carrying only one of the pathogenic variants. For example, two findings uncovered biallelic YARS1 variants that lead to an autosomal recessive multisystemic disorder. YARS1 Pro213Leu and Gly525Arg occurred in two siblings with asymptomatic parents [53] while compound heterozygous YARS1 Arg367Trp and Leu261Arg was causative for multisystemic disorder in a separate patient with partially symptomatic parents that each carry one pathogenic YARS allele [54]. This rare multisystem disorder is characterized by failure to thrive, hypertriglyceridemia, developmental delay, liver dysfunction, lung cysts, and MRI evidence of abnormal subcortical white matter.

A second example of biallelic variants is found in NARS1. No systematic genotype–phenotype study for pathogenic NARS1 alleles has been carried out; however, the literature describes 33 individuals with clinical characteristics including neurodevelopmental disorder with microcephaly, language delay and gait abnormalities (NDMILG). For example, an Arg545Cys mutation in NARS1 was identified as a causative allele, leading to reduced NARS abundance and aminoacylation activity in patient‐derived fibroblasts [55]. A dominant nonsense mutation (Arg534X) leads to severe global developmental delay [55]. Wang et al. [56] characterized three disease‐causing NARS1 mutants: Thr17Met, Asp356Ala, and Arg545Cys. Cells from patients with NDMILG had reduced cytoplasmic NARS1 transferase activity and patient‐derived organoids modeled microcephaly, revealing defects in radial glial cell proliferation. Despite known pathogenicity of these NARS1 mutations, the molecular basis of disease in patients remains unclear for most variants. Finally, homozygous and compound heterozygous mutations in Alanyl‐tRNA synthetase (AARS) were identified as causative for severe early‐onset infantile epileptic encephalopathy with persistent myelination defect and peripheral neuropathy [57].

While CMT type 2W is associated with several dominant heterozygous mutations in HARS, autosomal recessive Usher syndrome type 3B (Ush3B) is caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous HARS variants [58, 59]. Ush3B, linked to homozygous Tyr454Ser variants, is also referred to as HARS syndrome and leads to progressive hearing and vision loss with occasional sudden death during acute illness [58, 60]. Another example of an autosomal recessive ARSopathy is found with MARS, where pulmonary alveolar proteinosis can be caused by homozygous or compound heterozygous variants in the cytoplasmic MARS1 gene [61, 62, 63]. Disease‐causing mutations were found in two homozygous missense variants Ala393Thr and Ser567Leu, as well as a homozygous Asp605Val variant, and the disease manifests in severe, early‐onset symptoms resulting in early death before the age of 2 years in 30% of patients. Of note, heterozygous carriers are unaffected [61, 62, 63]. Studies in both patient‐derived cells and yeast models demonstrated decreased aminoacylation activity of the disease‐linked MARS1 variants.

Many aaRS alleles associated with CMT have been characterized using biochemical and molecular approaches in model systems. From a biochemical perspective, gain‐of‐function and loss‐of‐function mutations in aaRSs can be differentiated by examining the impact on aminoacylation catalysis, protein structure, and thermostability (Fig. 3). Loss‐of‐function mutations include loss of aaRS stability and function due to thermal instability [58] or deficient aminoacylation activity [64]. Other mutations were shown to modify dimerization or structural flexibility of the aaRS protein [65], or alter protein–protein interactions, such as an AARS Arg329His mutation resulting in the pathogenic binding of neuropilin 1 (NRP1) [66]. Some deficient aaRS variants may lead to reduced overall activity, insufficient aminoacyl‐tRNA levels and subsequent accumulation of uncharged tRNAs in the cell [67]. For example, CMT‐causing variant Cys201Arg in GARS has been shown to increase GARS binding to tRNAGly and fails to release it, which impairs enzymatic turnover and overall reduces Gly‐tRNAGly production [68]. Reduced aminoacyl‐tRNA production and accumulation of uncharged tRNAs activates the integrated stress response (ISR) through the general control nonderepressible 2 (GCN2) regulator and subsequent phosphorylation of eIF2α [68] (Fig. 3). Activation of the integrated stress response is primarily a survival pathway that can also trigger apoptosis [69], which may contribute to disease manifestation.

Cellular consequences of pathogenic aaRS alleles and current treatment approaches. Gain‐of‐function phenotypes often arise from non‐specific substrate recognition, leading to mistranslation, or altered protein–protein interactions. Global mistranslation leads to protein aggregation, activating the heat shock and unfolded protein response. Loss‐of‐function in tRNA synthetases can be caused by for example reduced aminoacylation activity or tRNA binding, loss of structural integrity or dimerization. This results in the accumulation of uncharged tRNA and factors such as GCN2 and ATF4 are activated as part of the integrated stress response. Cognate amino acids and tRNAs supplementation can rescue mutant tRNA synthetases. On the other hand, variants in the tRNA binding domain can lead to new activities with off‐target tRNAs, while variants in the amino acid binding site can cause mis‐aminoacylation of the cognate tRNA [70, 71]. Because the ribosome cannot distinguish correctly charged tRNAs from mis‐charged tRNAs [72], mis‐aminoacylation can lead to subsequent mistranslation across the proteome [73, 74]. Mistranslation caused by mutations in tRNAs [73, 75, 76, 77, 78, 79], aaRSs [75], or other components of the protein quality machinery is associated with neurodegenerative diseases [73, 80]. Mistranslation by mutant tRNA is known to cause cytotoxicity in mammalian and neuronal cells [78] and disruption of protein homeostasis [81, 82]. Mistranslation caused by tRNA mischarging due to pathogenic aaRS variants would have a similar effect on the proteome. Indeed, we found that mistranslation resulting from CMT‐causing HARS variants led to a significant growth defect and insoluble protein aggregation in a yeast model system due to proteome‐wide errors in protein synthesis that generated dysfunctional, mis‐folded, and aggregated proteins, which may contribute to CMT disease [70].

As multi‐gene panels for genetic conditions become more cost‐effective and, therefore, more sequencing data becomes available, many new aaRS variants are being identified and classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS) [American College of Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) Category 3] [83]. Very few variants in aaRS genes have been demonstrated to be pathogenic in cellular, animal or patient‐derived models of disease, but such studies are essential to demonstrate a deleterious effect at the cellular or whole organism level. The value of these studies to support a diagnosis cannot be overestimated. For example, patients with symptoms of CMT and a VUS in a CMT‐associated gene remain in a state of limbo, lacking a definitive diagnosis. To date, several variants in the cytoplasmic HARS1 have been classified as pathogenic [60]. Most of these variants are associated with CMT, whereas Y454S is associated with autosomal recessive Ush3B (i.e., HARS syndrome) [60]. In the HARS gene. there are currently 335 unique variants listed at Clinvar [84]; six entries have been defined as likely pathogenic or pathogenic, encompassing seven single amino acid variants causing Ush3B and 20 mutations associated with CMT. An additional six variants are listed as likely benign or benign, 18 with conflicting classification, while most of the identified HARS variants (302) remain classified as variants of uncertain significance.

Because of the rarity of individual aaRS mutations, re‐categorization of VUSs as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, likely benign, or benign remains an important and motivating challenge for future studies that will directly impact patients. MacArthur et al. [85] established a guideline on summarizing confidence in variant pathogenicity, relying on genome variation data and experimental results, but concluded that ‘unambiguous assignment of disease causality for sequence variants is often impossible, particularly for the very low‐frequency variants underlying many cases of rare, severe diseases’. This poses a significant burden on patients, who may be in urgent need to identify a genetic cause for their symptoms and put a name to the disease, which may also open opportunities for patient care and therapy to help address symptoms that are not accessible without a diagnosis [86]. Therefore, comprehensive guidelines to re‐define VUSs [83] and natural history studies that track disease progression will hold significant value in directing research efforts and interpretation of new biochemical studies and model system development focussing on VUS reassignment.

Until recently, therapeutic approach for ARSopathies was restricted to symptomatic treatments [87]. A few recent studies hold great promise in an allele‐specific treatment using cognate amino acid or tRNA supplementation to resolve the root cause of ARSopathies by improving or restoring normal aaRS function, while small molecule inhibitors often aim to restore physiological changes.

The main function of each aaRS is to provide sufficient levels of accurately aminoacylated tRNA substrates for translation. While pathogenic alleles may result in various biochemical consequences, reduced availability of aminoacyl‐tRNA is a common consequence and emerging theme in ARSopathies. Both recessive and dominant alleles must retain sufficient aminoacylation function to support viability. Supplementation with one or both substrates, tRNA or amino acid, may promote sufficient aminoacylation activity, as increasing amino acid levels may indeed aid in bypassing the bottleneck in aminoacyl‐tRNA synthesis caused by the pathogenic aaRS allele [88]. Similarly, alleles leading to reduced specificity and aminoacylation of non‐cognate amino acids or tRNA may be ameliorated by supplying a surplus of cognate substrates.

Amino acid supplementation is generally regarded as safe, with several studies showing that prolonged, high‐dose alanine [89], histidine [90] and glycine [91] supplementation do not exhibit toxicity. Prolonged high‐dose methionine [92], tyrosine [93], and tryptophan [94] can have toxic effects, yet methionine was successfully used as a treatment for pathogenic MARS alleles. Despite potential toxic effects of high doses of methionine [92], amino acid supplementation has been strikingly successful in the phenotypic rescue for MARS1‐related syndrome with pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (see above), where methionine supplementation of 20–30 mg·kg−1 increased methionine plasma levels by approximately 10‐fold, completely alleviating symptoms of pulmonary alveolar proteinosis and preventing recurrence [61, 95]. Measurable patient improvements included an improved respiratory and growth status, resolved anemia and cholestasis and improved albumin plasma levels. Since then, a pharmaceutical oral formulation for l‐methionine supplementation has been developed [96], and consistent methionine supplementation enabled three successful lung transplantations in patients with alveolar proteinosis/lung fibrosis that would usually not be considered for lung transplants [97]. Similarly, initial studies in patients with homozygous HARS Tyr454Ser mutations show that histidine supplementation may be useful as a treatment. Here, histidine supplementation is being used in an ongoing clinical trial to address HARS1‐related hearing and vision loss (NCT02924935) [60]. Histidine supplementation was also tested in humanized yeast models for several CMT‐associated HARS mutations, where increased histidine successfully resolved the phenotypes of some, but not all HARS mutations, indicating that the benefits of histidine supplementation may need to be tested for each allele [70, 98]. Excitingly, amino acid supplementation has also proved to be beneficial in fibroblasts derived from patients with mutant alleles for multiple other aaRSs. Patient‐derived fibroblasts with pathogenic variants in IARS, LARS, the beta‐subunit of phenylalanyl‐tRNA synthetase (FARSB), and SARS all showed reduced aminoacylation activity. Symptoms of these pediatric patients between 11 months and 8 years ranged from dysmaturity, failure to thrive requiring tube feeding and oxygen supply, anemia, liver disease, global developmental delay, and interstitial lung disease. Patients were subsequently treated with the amino acid (l‐isoleucine, l‐leucine, l‐phenylalanine or l‐serine) cognate to the defective aaRS, and all patients improved over the course of the treatment, with noticeable improvements in growth, head circumference, development, and oxygen dependency [88].

These studies demonstrate the strong potential for amino acid supplementation to be beneficial to patients with pathogenic aaRS alleles and could provide a first line of treatment for at least some ARSopathies.

As with amino acid supplementation, the delivery of wild‐type cognate tRNA as a therapeutic for ARSopathies aims to increase substrate availability for the defective aaRS mutant. We recently reviewed mechanisms and clinically relevant delivery approaches for tRNA therapeutics in detail [71]. The advent of tRNA medicine as a viable therapy emerged with landmark studies demonstrating tRNA delivery to treat mouse models of cystic fibrosis [99] and mucopolysaccharidosis type I [100]. Thus, tRNA therapeutics have become an exciting new development with many potential applications to ARSopathies. Excitingly, wild‐type tRNAGly delivery was successfully applied in mice with GARS mutations [101]. Mouse models harboring CMT‐causative GARS alleles that sequester tRNA, as outlined above, were successfully rescued by supplementation with tRNA. Here, increasing cognate tRNA concentrations ameliorated symptoms in a mouse model for GARS Cys201Arg, and this approach was also successful in yeast models for HARS alleles where histidine supplementation was not successful [70, 98, 110]. While tRNA therapeutics are certainly more challenging to deliver to patients than amino acid supplementation, cognate tRNA therapies may become a preferred treatment option for aaRS alleles that do not respond to amino acid supplementation [70].

Small molecule inhibitors for ARSopathies generally do not target the aaRS but aim to alleviate downstream effects of aaRS pathogenic alleles. Examples include inhibition of the integrated stress response and anti‐inflammatory agents. In some cases, changes or defects in aaRS activity can lead to integrated stress response (ISR) activation via the protein kinase general control nonderepressible 2 (GCN2). GCN2 activation is usually associated with amino acid starvation [102] and results from uncharged tRNA accumulation in the cytoplasm. GCN2 encodes a regulatory domain that is homologous to the tRNA binding domain of HARS, enabling GCN2 to bind uncharged tRNAs. Following tRNA binding, GCN2 undergoes a conformational change that subsequently promotes GCN2‐dependent phosphorylation of the eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α), activation of the ISR, and inhibition of translation initiation [103]. Similar to amino acid starvation, reduced aaRS activity caused by aaRS deficient alleles can also lead to the accumulation of uncharged tRNA in the cytoplasm. The activation of GCN2 and the ISR was shown to contribute to disease pathology, where GCN2‐mediated ISR activation promotes axonal degeneration. Excitingly, pharmacological inhibition of GCN2 reduces disease phenotypes of CMT‐GARS mouse models [68]. Recent reports, however, also caution that GCN2 inhibitors need to be carefully assessed before implementation, since some GCN2 inhibitors reduced GCN2 activity but also activated the ISR via an alternate route through protein kinase R (PKR)‐like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) [104, 105]. Another interesting therapeutic development focuses on replenishing a splice variant of HARS itself, which moonlights as an anti‐inflammatory factor. Treatment of patients with sarcoidosis with a fusion protein consisting of the N‐terminal 60 amino acids of HARS fused to a human immunoglobulin 1 (IgG1) led to favorable improvements in quality of life and stable to improved pulmonary function [106]. Several CMT‐causative GARS variants cause an aberrant interaction with histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6) to stimulate deacetylase activity on α‐tubulin, subsequently leading to deficits in axonal transport in mice models [107]. HDAC6 inhibition has been proposed as a potential treatment venue and was successful in initial studies [107], but further genetic analysis did not confirm the role of HDAC6 in CMT development [108]. Other components of the integrated stress response pathways can also be targeted for pharmacological intervention, such as PERK inhibitors that were recently reviewed in detail [69]. These inhibitors remain to be tested in aaRS‐related disease models and may reveal new avenues for symptomatic treatment.

Mutations in aaRSs can cause a variety of biochemical and cellular consequences and manifest in many different diseases. Given the rarity of individual aaRS alleles and the diversity of disease pathologies, the possibility for a single therapy for all ARSopathies is not anticipated. Fascinatingly, cognate amino acid and cognate tRNA supplementation have emerged as recurrent themes to address ARSopathies. The recent advances towards new therapeutic approaches for ARSopathies demonstrate that the development of personalized medicines for aaRS patients may require a careful evaluation of treatment options. Where some patients may benefit from amino acid supplementation, for others with different aaRS mutants, tRNA supplementation may prove the more effective therapy. For aaRS patients with an activated ISR, GCN2 inhibitors or other ISR inhibitors may relieve symptoms and increase quality of life. Future studies in model systems, including patient‐derived models, will be essential to evaluate and engineer novel therapeutic and allele‐specific approaches for ARSopathies as well as to define the pathogenic nature of the aaRS variants of unknown significance. New therapies and the resolution of pathogenic variants will provide important benefits to patients with aaRS diseases. We hope and anticipate that the many model systems for aaRS‐related diseases and the dedicated research teams attempting to identify new approaches for a cure for aaRS‐related diseases, will succeed in developing generalizable or allele‐specific interventions.
