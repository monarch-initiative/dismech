We evaluated long-term outcomes of 288 children with refractory-Langerhans cell 
histiocytosis (R-LCH) from 26 countries prescribed off-label mitogen-activated 
protein kinase inhibitors (MAPKis) according to clinical indications. MAPKi 
indications were: 148 R-risk-organ-positive (R-RO+), 67 R-RO-negative (R-RO-), 
13 lung destruction (lung), 9 sclerosing cholangitis (SC), 49 neurodegeneration 
(ND) and 2 diabetes insipidus (DI). Median ages at diagnosis and MAPKi onset 
were 1.3 and 2.3 years, respectively, with median follow-up of 3.7 years (1166 
person-years). Agents were mostly prescribed as monotherapies: 184 vemurafenib, 
115 dabrafenib, 3 encorafenib, 42 cobimetinib, 45 trametinib and/or 1 
binimetinib; followed 51 times by various chemotherapies or hematopoietic 
stem-cell transplantation, or combined anti-BRAF-anti-MEK 28 times. Short-term 
responses (<8 weeks) ranged from 98% (R-RO+ and R-RO-), to 30% (lung) to none 
(ND, DI and SC), although long-term lung and ND responses could be observed. 
Skin rash was the most frequent adverse event (~55%); 7 others were 1 
cardiomyopathy and 6 retinitis. Five developed MAPKi-unrelated tumors. Nine 
patients died. Five-year survival was 98%. After 113 patients with R-LCH 
discontinued MAPKi, 69 experienced disease reactivation. None of the various 
empirical maintenance therapies were able to prevent secondary reactivation. 
Among the 143 assessable patients without ND-LCH at MAPKi onset, 60 developed 
ND: 45% 5-year risk. MAPKis appeared to be safe and effective in children with 
R-RO+/RO-LCH, while other indications' responses were less frequent or occurred 
later. Further studies are needed to find effective maintenance-therapy 
approaches, particularly to prevent frequently observed secondary ND.

Ten years after the first report of vemurafenib (VEM) treatment in refractory-Langerhans cell histiocytosis (R-LCH), MAPK inhibitors (MAPKis) became a frequent therapeutic option for R-LCH.1 MAPKis, such as VEM2 or dabrafenib (DAB),3,4 achieved very rapid disease control with fewer complications than chemotherapy.5 However, because of R-LCH rarity, most patients are treated off-label. Over the last 10 years, only 2 clinical trials including 44 patients,6,7 and case reports and small series including ≤150 patients have been published worldwide (supplemental Table 1). Notably, information on long-term outcomes is sorely lacking, as are evaluations of various indications, for example lung involvement with parenchymal destruction (lung), sclerosing cholangitis (SC), and neurodegeneration (ND)-LCH.2,8,9 These gaps in knowledge prompted us to analyze this series of cases compiled under the aegis of the European Consortium for Histiocytosis (https://www.echo-histio.net).

This study included 288 children MAPKi-treated for biopsy-proven LCH that was refractory to standard therapy or presented with life-threatening symptoms at onset.10 Patients were enrolled if they had received at least 1 of the 6 MAPKis available off-label, including anti-v-RAF murine sarcoma viral oncogene homolog B (BRAF) such as VEM, DAB, or encorafenib, and anti-MAPKs such as cobimetinib (COB), trametinib (TRA), or binimetinib (BIN). This patient series, established after the publication of 54 LCH cases in 2019,2 provides long-term follow-up of those initial patients who were observed until 31 December 2018, and added 234 new patients. In addition, 28 patients had been reported in small series11, 12, 13 or case reports,8,9,14, 15, 16 and follow-up has been extended for all those previously reported cases. None of those patients were ever enrolled in a clinical trial for MAPKis, which were always prescribed off-label herein. Patients were classified according to their main MAPKi indications10 such as refractory risk-organ–positive (R-RO+; liver, spleen, and blood cytopenia10) or refractory risk-organ–negative (R-RO–), according to the LCH extent, SC,17 central nervous system ND with clinical symptoms (cND),18,19 and/or diabetes insipidus (DI). Failure of first-line therapy was characterized by disease progression in one or more risk organs (ROs), after vinblastine administration (once per week) and prednisolone (minimum dose, 40 mg/m2 per day), with the adjunction of a third drug or not. RO+ patients had R-LCH when 1 or more RO(s) showed no attenuation after first-line therapy. Therapeutic failure can occur at the first LCH episode or during its evolution in a patient whose disease initially responded but was then followed by reactivation in 1 or more RO(s). In addition to the primary MAPKi indications, patients could also have secondary indications at MAPKi onset. Symptomatic cND complications or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-detected ND (mND) abnormalities, which can be asymptomatic, were defined according to published criteria.18,20,21

Patients were entered into their respective national LCH registries (Eudract 2015-000403-6)22 and informed consent was obtained from their parents for inclusion in this observational study or they had been enrolled in the LCH-4 protocol (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02205762). Finally, data entry into the French National Histiocytosis Registry was approved by the national watchdog authority (Commission Nationale Informatique et Liberté 909207). Inclusion of laboratory findings was approved by 2 ethics committees, Île-de-France III (no. 2011-A00447-34; 2011-2020) and Sud-Ouest et Outre-Mer II (no. 2019-A01814-53; since 2020). In addition to data-collection authorization, after explaining to the parents the use of an off-label medication in the absence of an effective therapeutic alternative, informed consent was obtained before prescribing MAPKis. This procedure was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study participants’ origins by country are summarized in supplemental Table 2.

Disease activity was assessed by applying LCH-II and LCH-III trial criteria,23,24 and the Disease Activity Score (DAS).25 Permanent consequences were not considered in such scoring methods. Reactivation was defined as disease-activity reappearance after complete resolution. Poor response, that is, refractory to first-line chemotherapy at week 6 or 12, was defined as more intense disease activity according to histiocyte society criteria, or DAS increase or persistent DAS >8. Notably, a patient’s LCH may become refractory to chemotherapy later during disease evolution.

The set of evaluation criteria (supplemental Table 3) reflects the main MAPKi indications.

Steady-state MAPKi residual concentrations were evaluated in 138 patients and the dosage methods were reported elsewhere.26 The circulating cell-free BRAF DNA (ccf-BRAFV600E) load, performed on cell-free plasma, was determined in peripheral blood mononuclear cell DNA (n = 7), with most being tested using a droplet-based digital polymerase chain reaction (n = 124), according to previously described techniques15,27,28, and the results were pooled. Safety for all patients was evaluated according to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5. Time-to-remission was defined as the MAPKi day-1 to null-DAS25 day interval or Histiocyte Society criteria-defined nonactive disease.23,24 For patients from whom MAPKis were withdrawn, any clinical reactivation after discontinuation was recorded, as was the first instance of brain mND.20,21 Between-group differences were compared with the Mann-Whitney U test for quantitative variables and Fisher exact test for qualitative variables. Survival-analysis end points included any type of clinical reactivation, the date of the first mND, and death. Survival rates were estimated with the Kaplan-Meier method, and subgroups were compared with log-rank tests and Cox regression analyses. All participating patients started MAPKis before 31 December 2024. The cut-off date for this analysis was 30 August 2025.

A total of 288 patients were enrolled in the study (Table 1; supplemental Table 4). MAPKi indications included 148 cases of RO+, 67 of RO–, 13 of lung, 9 of SC, 49 of cND, and 2 cases of DI. In addition to the main MAPKi indications, SC was also associated in 12 patients, mND in 13, DI in 62, and lung in 10 patients. A MAPKi was prescribed as first-line therapy in light of reports indicating a likely poor response to standard chemotherapy (eg, 12 macrophage-activation syndromes cases in newborns,29 3 cases of cND, 4 of lung, 1 of DI, and 1 case of SC), whereas 267 patients (93%) had LCH which was refractory to at least 1 standard-therapy line. Median (interquartile range [IQR]) age at diagnosis was 1.3 years (IQR, 0.6-2.4; range, 0-15.3). Median age at MAPKi onset was 2.3 years (IQR, 1.4-5.6; range, 0.1-21), with median follow-up at 3.7 years (IQR, 1.8-6.1; for 1191 patient-years), and median treatment duration lasted 2.1 years. BRAFV600E mutation, sought for all patients, was detected in 250 patients (87%). Many parameters such as demographic data, mutation status, and disease activity, reflected the primary MAPKi indication. RO+ was the most frequent indication, and RO+ group had the highest DASs, was younger, and 92% were BRAFV600E carriers. A total of 322 distinct adverse events (AEs) that occurred in 160 patients are detailed in Table 3. The remaining 128 patients had no reported AEs. Skin rash was the predominant AE (215 events; 66%), and was seen most frequently with VEM (147; 68%). The most worrying skin AEs that impaired patients’ quality of life were photosensitivity while on VEM, and paronychia occurring most often while on COB, TRA, or BIN. We observed a few cardiac abnormalities, including 4 events in patients while they were on VEM with associated QT corrected elongation and 1 transient cardiomyopathy while on a combination COB–DAB. Four patients experienced retinitis, with any MAPKi, and 2 developed cataracts on DAB. All those AEs were successfully managed by dose reduction or agent withdrawal. Over the long term, no growth retardation was reported but several patients developed community-acquired infections, such as chicken pox, flu, or COVID, with benign outcomes. The 33 grade 3 or 4 AEs observed were mostly dermatological (supplemental Table 6). No agent-related renal or endocrine dysfunctions were seen, and growth was normal except for LCH-related pituitary dysfunction. One patient who took VEM for 2.5 years stopped treatment 3 months before becoming pregnant and gave birth to a normal newborn. Five patients developed secondary tumors, including 2 patients with post-transplant Epstein-Barr virus lymphomas (among 13 transplanted) and 2 cases of myelodysplastic syndromes (MDSs) with cytogenetic abnormalities were documented in patients who had received MAPKi and chemotherapy with etoposide, clofarabine, or 2′-chlorodeoxyadenosine–cytarabine (2-CdA–AraC). No MDS was reported in patients who had received only MAPKi monotherapy or combined with the classic vinblastine regimen. One patient developed botriomycoma, considered unrelated to the prescribed VEM. Among 215 patients with active disease (RO+/−) at MAPKi initiation, median DAS ranged from 6 (IQR, 5-13) at diagnosis to 0 (IQR, 0-1) at 6 to 8 weeks (P < .0001). Evaluations at the same time yielded 157 complete responses (CRs; non-active diseases), 57 partial responses (active disease better [ADB]), and 1 failure (Table 1). Among those 57 partial responses, 2 never achieved a CR. The median time to CR was 41 days (range, 5-167) and was slightly shorter for patients whose MAPKi indication was RO– (37 days) than RO+ (46 days) (P = .01) (supplemental Figure 3). The rapidity of response and CR rates corresponded well with the initial DASs and was 58 days for DAS ≥10, 44 days for DAS ≥5, and <10 and 36 days for DAS <5 (P < .001).

No change was observed in 2 patients with DI taking MAPKi for this indication. In addition to those 2 patients, 62 others had DI before starting MAPKi and no DI regression was observed after therapy, whereas 12 of the 224 initially DI-free patients developed DI after MAPKi initiation.

Among 21 patients who had received a MAPKi for SC-LCH, 9 received it as a primary indication and 12 patients for simultaneously active LCH and SC-LCH. Fifteen patients had no measurable change of SC-LCH on MRI or liver-disease progression; however, the SC appeared to regress after starting MAPK inhibition in 3 patients without liver cirrhosis. MAPKi controlled the systemic LCH in 5 RO+ patients, allowing liver transplantation. Eleven patients received liver grafts. The major complication of that surgery was post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease in 1 patient and fatal sepsis in another, 3 years post-engraftment.

Lung was the MAPKi indication for 13 patients but was also present in 10 others with RO+ involvement. One patient who received a lung transplant within the first 30 days of starting MAPKi was not evaluated. Two patients had no sustainable responses to MAPKi, of which 1 died because of mechanical complications and the other underwent lung transplantation.30 Thus, 20 out of 22 assessable patients with lung had good clinical responses, with decreased numbers of nodules/cysts. Supplemental Figure 4 illustrates an example of good response to MAPKi.

Lastly, cND-LCH was the MAPKi indication for 49 patients. This group, always BRAFV600E carriers, was older at MAPKi onset (median age, 11.7 years) that occurred later during disease evolution when cND symptoms appeared. Four cND patients developed severe skin rashes that led to agent withdrawal for 3 patients, whereas 4 others stopped MAPKi within 3 months because of no improvement. Clinical neurological responses could be assessed in only 42 of these cND patients with prolonged therapy, among whom stabilization was achieved in 28 patients (66%) and mild but clear regression in 14 patients (33%). When it occurred, symptom attenuation was observed during the first 12 months of therapy. The latter 14 who improved, had benefited from treatment earlier during their cND evolution than those without improvement (median ND-LCH to MAPKi interval was 33 vs 50 months, respectively). Patients’ morbidity scores,19 determined at MAPKi start and last follow-up, indicated small but significant score reduction (median difference, 1; P = .01).

Once the disease was controlled, to limit unnecessary long-term therapy, their treating physicians decided, in the absence of guidelines, when to discontinue MAPKi. Seventy-nine patients experienced reactivations after a median of 0.38 years (range, 0.1-0.9) following discontinuation. Among the 226 patients ND-free at diagnosis and who underwent MRI at least once during follow-up, mND could be evaluated for 147 patients. MRI was obtained routinely for 124 symptom-free patients and 23 with symptoms suggestive of cND, for example, 5 with recurrent falls or cerebellar syndrome, but also symptoms like fatigue or clumsiness compatible with early ND-LCH stages for 18 patients. mND was detected in 60 patients. The median (IQR) time from MAPKi day 1 to the last ND-negative MRI was 3 years (2.9-5), which is shorter than the median time to ND diagnosis. That time difference might suggest that additional follow-up could detect additional ND complications among such ND-free patients.

The 3-year ND risk was 18% (95% CI, 13%-27%) and increased to 45% (95% CI, 35%-55%) after 5 years (Figure 3). Univariate risk analysis failed to identify prognostic factors (data not shown). Indeed, neither the first MAPKi proposed (VEM, DAB, COB, or TRA), combined chemotherapy–MAPKi, or combination anti-BRAF–anti-MEK, nor HSCT (n = 5) was able to attenuate the risk of secondary ND. Now, 10 years after the first reported MAPKi use in children with R-LCH,31 it has been confirmed that this class of agents achieves a therapeutic response in numerous situations previously deemed hopeless. Disease complexity, rarity, and the very young age of the patients are the origin of many difficulties. That context underlies why only 2 clinical trials on R-LCH,6,7 enrolling ≤44 patients, had been undertaken and reported since 2015, whereas the quasi-totality of patients were treated off-label with MAPKi(s) (supplemental Table 1). Here, we analyzed the different primary unmet medical needs of R-LCH, largely exceeding the inclusions of previous studies and providing long-term follow-up data.

Among the patients with active R-LCH (ie, RO+ and RO–; 215/288), MAPKis induced rapid and dramatic clinical improvement. These results agree with those of previous studies (supplemental Table 1) and can now be considered established. Analysis of the MAPKi effect is more complex for indications other than R-RO+ and R-RO–. The main role of MAPKis in patients with SC is to obtain good disease control before liver transplantation, which remains the only therapeutic option. MAPKis had no effect on DI, neither reversing pituitary involvement nor preventing it. Considering only life-threatening lung-LCH, the final response depended on the extent of the underlying parenchymal destruction and, despite disease control, lung transplant may be necessary.30 ND complications, for which only limited recovery is expected, represent the most difficult situation, where the main MAPKi contribution was merely stabilization. For those specific indications, future studies will have to clarify the impact of MAPKis.

Even though the MAPKi-safety profile is better than that of high-dose chemotherapy, most of the patients with R-LCH required prolonged and chronic MAPKi administration, which represents a specific difficulty. Though mild AEs such as skin rashes and paronychia are easily managed, the relatively rare but more severe events, like retinitis and cardiomyopathy, are limiting factors. Compared with the high risk of secondary skin tumors seen in adults with melanoma (∼30%),32,33 the absence of any MAPKi-related secondary tumors in 288 pediatric patients is reassuring. Noteworthy, the 2 cases of MDSs, observed in patients after high-dose chemotherapy, are in agreement with a case report34 and general data about LCH–malignancy associations.35

As reported previously,2 we also observed a very high reactivation rate (63% at 3 years) after therapy was withdrawn. Reactivation dynamics and patterns appeared to be determined by the initial LCH clinical and molecular characteristics. RO+ patients and those with high ccf-BRAFV600E loads were at a higher risk of reactivation. MAPKis appear to have an on/off effect, inducing very rapid clinical responses but not eradication of the pathogenic clone.

Unfortunately, we did not observe any effect of classic chemotherapies such as vinblastine, 6-mercaptopurine, and methotrexate, or other more toxic chemotherapies, like 2-CdA–AraC, clofarabine, anti-BRAF–anti-MEK combinations, or HSCT, as none seemed to prevent long-term reactivation. Since those results were obtained from small numbers of patients, the potential contribution of combined chemotherapy within structured clinical trials remains an option.6

Lastly, the rate of ND complications in our series was high. That phenomenon was mentioned in a preliminary report on 5 patients.11 Its frequency was much higher than expected, reaching 45% of MAPKi-treated R-LCH patients after 5 years on therapy. However, the ND-LCH pattern under MAPKis differs somewhat from classic ND, which occurs spontaneously or after chemotherapy alone,18,36 as the patients were much younger at ND-LCH onset and 1 of 3 patients was already symptomatic before the age of 5 years. Even the 10-year cND rate before the MAPKi era among patients with hematological involvement was 11%, with an mND risk of 33.6%29; our series’ 45% mND rate was even higher. That high rate could be partially explained by the prolonged survival of patients with aggressive systemic LCH and is more likely associated with disease sequestration in the brain microglia,37,38 highlighting a new potential severe threat requiring further studies to limit it.

In conclusion, off-label MAPKi use was confirmed to be highly effective in a very large series of children with R-LCH. AEs were frequent but usually readily manageable, whereas the long-term risk of ND emerged as a serious concern.
