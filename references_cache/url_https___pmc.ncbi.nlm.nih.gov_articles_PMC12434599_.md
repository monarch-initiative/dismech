</div></section></section><section aria-label="Article content"><section class="body main-article-body"><section class="abstract" id="abstract1"><h2>Key Points</h2>
<section id="ab-noi250064-1"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Question</h3>
<p>Is home high-intensity aerobic training, defined as training 5 times/week at greater than 80% maximum heart rate, a safe way to reduce ataxia symptoms compared to home balance training in people with cerebellar ataxias?</p></section><section id="ab-noi250064-2"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Findings</h3>
<p>In this randomized clinical trial that included 62 individuals with various cerebellar ataxias, home high-intensity aerobic training appeared safe and effective in improving ataxia symptoms. Improvements in ataxia symptoms, fitness, fatigue, balance, and gait were maintained for individuals in the aerobic group who continued to hit training goals at 1 year, but trended back to baseline for those who did not.</p></section><section id="ab-noi250064-3"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Meaning</h3>
<p>Home high-intensity aerobic training appears to be an effective and safe treatment option for individuals with cerebellar ataxias for ataxia symptoms, fatigue, and aerobic fitness; further investigation is warranted to establish methods to promote training adherence.</p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="abstract2"><h2>Abstract</h2>
<section id="ab-noi250064-4"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Importance</h3>
<p>Clinical practice guidelines advise balance training for cerebellar ataxia, but little is known regarding high-intensity aerobic exercise.</p></section><section id="ab-noi250064-5"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Objective</h3>
<p>To compare home high-intensity aerobic training to home balance training on improvements of ataxia symptoms using the Scale for the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia (SARA).</p></section><section id="ab-noi250064-6"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Design, Setting, and Participants</h3>
<p>This assessor-masked randomized clinical trial was conducted between January 1, 2021, and September 1, 2024, through home training, with in-person assessments at a single ataxia care center in a large urban US city. Individuals with various cerebellar ataxia types were eligible for inclusion. Data analysis was performed from November 2024 to February 2025.</p></section><section id="ab-noi250064-7"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Interventions</h3>
<p>Individuals in the aerobic group trained for 30 minutes/session, 5 times/week, at up to 85% predicted maximum heart rate. Individuals in the balance group performed 30 minutes of balance exercises of varying difficulty 5 times/week. Participants were given study support of biweekly phone calls for only the first 6 months of this 12-month study.</p></section><section id="ab-noi250064-8"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Main Outcomes and Measures</h3>
<p>The primary outcome was SARA score to measure ataxia symptoms (range, 0-40 points, with higher scores indicating more ataxia). Secondary outcomes included number of adverse events, training adherence, balance measures, gait speed, quality of life, fatigue, and fitness levels (assessed via V̇<span class="font-variant-small-caps">o</span><sub>2</sub>max). Assessments were conducted at baseline and at 6, 9, and 12 months.</p></section><section id="ab-noi250064-9"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h3>
<p>A total of 114 individuals with various cerebellar ataxia types were approached: 52 individuals declined participation or did not meet inclusion criteria, while 62 individuals were enrolled. The 62 participants included 29 women (46.8%), with a mean (SD) age of 54.4 (12.9) years and mean (SD) SARA score of 12.1 (4.1) points. Linear mixed-effects model analysis revealed that the home aerobic group had significantly larger improvement in outcomes than the balance group, particularly for SARA score (β, −1.53; 95% CI, −2.44 to −0.61; <em>P</em> = .001), fatigue (β, −9.38; 95% CI, −15.1 to −3.7; <em>P</em> = .001), and V<span class="font-variant-small-caps">o</span><sub>2</sub>max (β, 4.26; 95% CI, 2.1-6.4; <em>P</em> &lt; .001). At 6 months, the aerobic and balance groups had changes in SARA scores of −2.4 points (95% CI, −3.1 to −1.80) and −0.9 points (95% CI, −1.5 to −0.2), respectively. For the aerobic group, individuals who continued training maintained benefits in SARA score (change from baseline, −3.81 points; 95% CI, −2.2 to −5.4), whereas those who limited or stopped training had benefits trend back to baseline levels (change from baseline, 0.4 points; 95% CI, −0.4 to 1.2) at 1 year.</p></section><section id="ab-noi250064-10"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions and Relevance</h3>
<p>In this randomized clinical trial, home high-intensity aerobic training improved ataxia symptoms, fatigue, and aerobic fitness more than dose-matched home balance training among individuals with cerebellar ataxias. Individuals in the aerobic group who continued to train regularly maintained benefits at 1 year.</p></section><section id="ab-noi250064-11"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Trial Registration</h3>
<p>ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: <a href="https://www.clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05002218?cond=NCT05002218&amp;rank=1" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">NCT05002218</a></p></section></section><section class="abstract" id="abstract3"><hr class="headless">
<p>This randomized clinical trial compares home high-intensity aerobic training to home balance training for improvements of ataxia symptoms among individuals with various cerebellar ataxia types.</p></section><section id="H1-1-NOI250064"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Introduction</h2>
<p>Cerebellar ataxias are a heterogenous group of disorders characterized by progressive disability due to loss of coordination.<sup><a href="#noi250064r1" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r1">1</a></sup> Approximately 150 000 US individuals live with these diseases,<sup><a href="#noi250064r2" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r2">2</a></sup> and the mean annual health care cost exceeds $18 000 per person.<sup><a href="#noi250064r3" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r3">3</a></sup> Although trials are ongoing, there is a need to find treatments that slow disease progression and improve individuals’ functional abilities.</p>
<p>Aerobic training is a possible treatment for ataxias. Research indicates that ataxic mice have increased lifespan and larger cerebellums when they perform regular aerobic exercise compared to sedentary controls.<sup><a href="#noi250064r4" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r4">4</a>,<a href="#noi250064r5" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r5">5</a>,<a href="#noi250064r6" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r6">6</a></sup> Animal studies also document that aerobic training enhances neuroplasticity mechanisms.<sup><a href="#noi250064r7" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r7">7</a></sup> In human studies, aerobic training slows progression in various neurodegenerative disorders, including Parkinson disease,<sup><a href="#noi250064r8" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r8">8</a>,<a href="#noi250064r9" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r9">9</a>,<a href="#noi250064r10" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r10">10</a></sup> Huntington disease,<sup><a href="#noi250064r11" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r11">11</a>,<a href="#noi250064r12" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r12">12</a>,<a href="#noi250064r13" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r13">13</a></sup> and Alzheimer disease.<sup><a href="#noi250064r14" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r14">14</a>,<a href="#noi250064r15" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r15">15</a>,<a href="#noi250064r16" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r16">16</a></sup></p>
<p>Despite these encouraging data, human studies in ataxias have primarily focused on balance training.<sup><a href="#noi250064r17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r17">17</a>,<a href="#noi250064r18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r18">18</a>,<a href="#noi250064r19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r19">19</a>,<a href="#noi250064r20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r20">20</a>,<a href="#noi250064r21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r21">21</a>,<a href="#noi250064r22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r22">22</a></sup> Although conflicting results have been reported, most studies suggest that balance training improves ataxia symptoms for individuals with ataxia if training is adequately challenging.<sup><a href="#noi250064r23" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r23">23</a></sup> In these studies, improvements in ataxia symptoms ranged from 1.0 to 2.8 points on the Scale for the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia (SARA), the most common primary outcome measure, with a minimal clinically significant difference of 1.0 point.<sup><a href="#noi250064r17" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r17">17</a>,<a href="#noi250064r18" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r18">18</a>,<a href="#noi250064r19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r19">19</a>,<a href="#noi250064r20" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r20">20</a>,<a href="#noi250064r21" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r21">21</a>,<a href="#noi250064r22" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r22">22</a></sup> However, most experts agree that balance training helps individuals compensate for deficits but does not impact disease progression.<sup><a href="#noi250064r24" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r24">24</a>,<a href="#noi250064r25" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r25">25</a></sup></p>
<p>Given the lack of data regarding aerobic exercise on ataxia, our group first devised a study showing high-intensity aerobic training was safe and feasible for individuals with ataxia.<sup><a href="#noi250064r26" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r26">26</a>,<a href="#noi250064r27" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r27">27</a></sup> We then conducted a small trial comparing home aerobic training to dose-matched balance training for a 6-month period.<sup><a href="#noi250064r28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r28">28</a></sup> Results indicated that home aerobic training caused greater improvements in ataxia symptoms than home balance training. However, there were several limitations to this study. First, it was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, precluding cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET). Second, recruitment was largely limited to individuals with multiple system atrophy–cerebellar type and idiopathic cerebellar ataxia, as individuals with other types of ataxia were recruited for other competing trials. Third, there was a small sample size, and fourth, training adherence relied on training logs, with no objective measures. Fifth, there was no long-term follow-up, and sixth, there were no measures of quality of life, fatigue, or structural changes that might occur within the brain.</p>
<p>To overcome these issues, we conducted another study comparing home aerobic training to balance training with a larger sample size over a longer duration. We were able to recruit individuals with a wide range of diagnoses and used heart rate watch monitors to track training adherence. We also added secondary measures of quality of life and fatigue for this study. Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were also performed, but these results are still being analyzed and will be presented in another article. As in our previous study, we hypothesized that individuals randomized to home aerobic training would have more improvements in ataxia symptoms compared to individuals randomized to balance training.</p></section><section id="H1-2-NOI250064"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Methods</h2>
<section id="H2-1-NOI250064"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Study Design</h3>
<p>This study was an assessor-masked, single-center randomized clinical trial of individuals with ataxia that compared home balance training to home aerobic training. The setting was at the outpatient clinic at Columbia University Medical Center in New York, New York. The institutional review board for Columbia University approved the trial protocol, which is available in <a href="#note-NOI250064-1-s" class="usa-link">Supplement 1</a>. All participants provided written informed consent, and all data were anonymous. This trial followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (<a href="http://www.equator-network.org/reporting-guidelines/consort/" class="usa-link usa-link--external" data-ga-action="click_feat_suppl" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CONSORT</a>) reporting guidelines.</p></section><section id="H2-2-NOI250064"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Study Participants</h3>
<p>Participants with cerebellar ataxia were enrolled using convenience sampling from January 1, 2021, to September 1, 2023. Participants were recruited from an ataxia clinical care center at Columbia University Medical Center and the Coordination of Rare Diseases at Sanford registry. Participants were eligible for inclusion if they had a clinical diagnosis of ataxia (SARA score &gt;3), were older than 18 years, had cerebellar atrophy on MRI, could stand unaided and walk with or without an assistive device (SARA walking subscore ≤6), and had the ability to safely ride a stationary exercise bicycle (SARA sitting subscore ≤2). Exclusion criteria included poorly controlled or unstable cardiovascular disease, Montreal Cognitive Assessment score less than 23/30,<sup><a href="#noi250064r29" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r29">29</a></sup> disorders that would interfere with the performance of endurance exercise (eg, stroke, respiratory problems, traumatic brain injury, or neuromuscular disease), regular participation in vigorous endurance exercise (defined as &gt;2 days/week for at least the past 4 months at maximum heart rate &gt;65%),<sup><a href="#noi250064r30" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r30">30</a></sup> inclusion in another clinical trial that could impact SARA score, and/or evidence of serious arrhythmias or ischemic heart disease. Individuals with various forms of ataxia were allowed to participate to reach recruitment goals, and no ataxia types were enriched for effect.</p></section><section id="H2-3-NOI250064"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Screening, Baseline Testing, and Randomization</h3>
<p>Prescreening was done via telephone in the ataxia clinic at the Neurological Institute. Participants who met criteria underwent baseline assessments. Participants were randomly allocated 1:1 to home balance or home aerobic training through a computer-based permuted block randomization with a block size of 6 or 8. The randomization sequence was generated by an independent statistician, and the details of the group allocation were concealed on cards placed inside sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes.</p></section><section id="H2-4-NOI250064"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Interventions</h3>
<section id="H3-1-NOI250064"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Aerobic Training for Ataxia</h4>
<p>Participants randomized to the aerobic group were given a stationary exercise bike (ProGear 225 Folding Magnetic Upright Exercise Bike with Heart Pulse [ProGear]) for home use. They were instructed to exercise 5 times/week. The regimen included a 5-minute warm-up, 30 minutes of exercise at target heart rate, and 5-minute cooldown. Participants conducted leg cycling only. No balance exercises or arm cycling was included. Participants were instructed to monitor heart rate and adjust exercise to remain in target heart rate range (ie, by changing bike resistance or pedaling at different speeds). During the exercise, participants wore a Fitbit Charge 5 (Fitbit) or Apple Watch Series 3 (Apple) that captured heart rate throughout the exercise session.</p>
<p>Individuals were instructed to start training at 65% maximum heart rate achieved during baseline CPET testing. Participants were then called every 2 weeks for the first 6 months of training. If individuals were unable to achieve heart rate goals, intensity was decreased by 5%. If participants achieved goals, but training was considered moderate or hard, participants maintained the same exercise intensity. If training was easy, participants increased heart rate goal by 5%, up to 85% of age-predicted maximum heart rate.</p>
<p>At the completion of 6 months, participants were encouraged to continue training, but study support was withdrawn. The biweekly phone calls stopped, and the research assistant stopped monitoring training logs. However, participants were instructed to continue recording training sessions on their watch, which was checked at the 9- and 12-month assessments. Participants were considered adherent to training if they achieved 80% of goals (training ≥24 minutes/session, at ≥65% maximum heart rate, 4 times/week).<sup><a href="#noi250064r31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r31">31</a>,<a href="#noi250064r32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r32">32</a>,<a href="#noi250064r33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r33">33</a></sup></p></section><section id="H3-2-NOI250064"><h4 class="pmc_sec_title">Home Balance Training for Ataxia</h4>
<p>The balance training protocol has been published previously.<sup><a href="#noi250064r28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r28">28</a></sup> Participants were given 3 levels of exercises: easy, moderate, and hard. At the initial assessment, the exercise physiologist evaluated the participants, instructed them on safe exercise performance, and recommended an exercise starting level. Participants were then instructed to perform 30 minutes of exercise, 5 times/week. Specifically, participants were asked to perform 6 different exercises, each for 5 minutes.</p>
<p>Participants recorded training sessions on either a Fitbit Charge 5 or Apple Watch Series 3. The exercise research assistant monitored training on biweekly phone calls where they asked participants regarding balance challenge (1-10 scale, with 10 being highest difficulty) and adverse events.</p>
<p>After 6 months, training support was withdrawn. Participants were adherent to training if they achieved 80% of their goals (training ≥24 minutes/session, with balance challenge score ≥6, 4 times/week).<sup><a href="#noi250064r31" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r31">31</a>,<a href="#noi250064r32" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r32">32</a>,<a href="#noi250064r33" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r33">33</a></sup></p></section></section><section id="H2-5-NOI250064"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Outcome Measures</h3>
<p>Outcome assessors were trained and masked to group allocation. All assessments were administered at baseline, 6 months, 9 months, and 12 months.</p>
<p>The primary outcome was SARA score, a validated clinical measure of ataxia severity.<sup><a href="#noi250064r34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r34">34</a></sup> Scores range from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating more ataxia symptoms. This test has a sensitivity of 0.99 and specificity of 0.96.<sup><a href="#noi250064r35" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r35">35</a></sup> Interrater reliability, test-retest reliability, and internal consistency are also high.<sup><a href="#noi250064r36" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r36">36</a></sup> The minimal clinically significant difference is approximately 1.0 point.<sup><a href="#noi250064r34" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r34">34</a></sup> Each SARA test was video-recorded, and the order was scrambled to blind researchers to group assignment and sequence of testing.</p>
<p>Secondary outcome measures for this study included maximal oxygen consumption (V<span class="font-variant-small-caps">o</span><sub>2</sub>max), Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS), World Health Organization Quality of Life (WHOQOL-BREF), Timed Up and Go: balance (TUG), Dynamic Gait Index: balance (DGI), and gait speed. V<span class="font-variant-small-caps">o</span><sub>2</sub>max is a validated measure of aerobic fitness. Results indicate that a V<span class="font-variant-small-caps">o</span><sub>2</sub>max increase of 1 point decreases the relative risk of all-cause mortality by 9%.<sup><a href="#noi250064r37" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r37">37</a></sup> The measure was determined by a breath-by-breath measurement of V<span class="font-variant-small-caps">o</span><sub>2</sub> with a Vmax Encore metabolic system (VIASYS Healthcare) while participants performed a progressive ramped exercise test using an electronic-braked lower body cycle ergometer (CareFusion).<sup><a href="#noi250064r38" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r38">38</a></sup> FSS is a widely used measure of fatigue severity. Prior studies indicate a change of 1.9 points reflects clinically important change.<sup><a href="#noi250064r39" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r39">39</a></sup> WHOQOL-BREF is a validated assessment of quality of life,<sup><a href="#noi250064r40" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r40">40</a></sup> while TUG is a validated measure of balance, walking ability, and fall risk.<sup><a href="#noi250064r41" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r41">41</a></sup> DGI is a validated measure to assess dynamic balance and functional mobility; scores less than 20 on this 24-point scale indicate increased fall risk.<sup><a href="#noi250064r42" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r42">42</a></sup> Gait speed is used to measure walking ability. A change of 0.1 to 0.2 m/s is clinically meaningful.<sup><a href="#noi250064r43" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r43">43</a></sup></p></section><section id="H2-6-NOI250064"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Adverse Events</h3>
<p>Participants were instructed to inform the research team if they encountered any adverse events during the study. Adverse events were also identified during biweekly phone calls by asking participants about pain, discomfort, problems, or concerns regarding the training.</p></section><section id="H2-7-NOI250064"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Sample Size</h3>
<p>Using the data from our prior study comparing home aerobic to balance training on ataxia symptoms using SARA, we found a large effect size of 0.84 in favor of aerobic training.<sup><a href="#noi250064r28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r28">28</a></sup> Assuming an attrition rate of 25%,<sup><a href="#noi250064r28" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r28">28</a></sup> a sample size of 48 participants, with 24 in each arm, was required to provide a 2-arm trial with 80% power to detect an effect size of at least 0.80 at a 5% level of significance. However, we were able to recruit more participants in this study over a 2-year period. Sample size was capped at 62 participants due to budgetary constraints.</p></section><section id="H2-8-NOI250064"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Statistical Analysis</h3>
<p>Before the specific statistical techniques were applied, all variables were examined for illegitimate values, outliers, and inconsistencies. The distributions of variables were also examined and described in terms of means and standard deviations for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables. All raw test scores were converted to <em>z</em> scores referenced to baseline scores. The primary analysis was on the intent-to-treat principle. We accounted for dropouts by examining the primary outcome variables using (longitudinal) linear mixed-effects models (MEMs) for continuous outcomes. MEMs do not require complete measurement data to estimate the outcome variable. The inferences from analyses with missing data are valid under the missing-at-random assumption (ie, the missing mechanism does not depend on the value of the unobserved outcome), which is not testable in this study. Thus, we performed a sensitivity analysis by comparing the results from those with missing data to those of completers. We also performed 2-sample <em>t</em> tests on change scores from baseline to 6 months for all primary and secondary outcome measures to corroborate findings in longitudinal analyses. For all analyses, we reported standardized effect sizes to guide future studies and meta-analyses. <em>P</em> &lt; .05 was considered statistically significant; <em>P</em> values were 2-tailed.</p></section></section><section id="H1-3-NOI250064"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Results</h2>
<p>Of 114 participants screened, 18 did not meet eligibility criteria and 32 declined to participate (enrollment rate: 54.4%) (<a href="#noi250064f1" class="usa-link">Figure 1</a>). Of 62 participants randomized, 31 were randomized to home aerobic training and 31 were randomized to home balance training. The overall dropout rate was 6 of 62 participants (9.7%) at 6 months (aerobic: 2/31 [6.5%]; balance: 4/31 [12.9%]), 10 of 62 (16.1%) at 9 months (aerobic: 4/31 [12.9%]; balance: 6/31 [19.4%]), and 11 of 62 (17.7%) at 12 months (aerobic: 5/31 [16.1%]; balance: 6/31 [19.4%]). There were no statistical differences in terms of age, sex, disease duration, use of riluzole, and baseline SARA scores between those who completed the study and those who dropped out (eTable 1 in <a href="#note-NOI250064-1-s" class="usa-link">Supplement 2</a>).</p>

</div></div></section></section><section id="H2-11-NOI250064"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Primary Outcomes</h3>
<p>Compared to the balance group, the aerobic training group demonstrated significantly better improvements in SARA (time-by-group interaction, 6 months: β, −1.53; 95% CI, −2.44 to −0.61; <em>P</em> = .001; 9 months: β, −1.34; 95% CI, −2.29 to −0.38; <em>P</em> = .006; and 12 months: β, −1.40; 95% CI, −2.37 to −0.43; <em>P</em> = .005) (<a href="#noi250064t3" class="usa-link">Table 3</a>; eTables 2 and 3 in <a href="#note-NOI250064-1-s" class="usa-link">Supplement 2</a>; <a href="#noi250064f2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>A). At 6 months, the aerobic and balance groups had changes in SARA scores of −2.4 points (95% CI, −3.1 to −1.80) and −0.9 points (95% CI, −1.5 to −0.2), respectively. Results remained significant after correcting for multiple comparison corrections (<em>F</em> = 4.6; <em>P</em> = .02) (eTable 4 in <a href="#note-NOI250064-1-s" class="usa-link">Supplement 2</a>). For the aerobic group, individuals who continued to train regularly maintained benefits (SARA score, −3.81 points; 95% CI, −2.2 to −5.4), whereas those who limited training had ataxia symptoms trend back to baseline (SARA score, 0.4 points; 95% CI, −0.4 to 1.2) at 1 year (<a href="#noi250064f2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>D). In SARA subgroup analysis, sit and finger chase scores were most likely to improve with aerobic training compared to balance training (eFigure in <a href="#note-NOI250064-1-s" class="usa-link">Supplement 2</a>).</p>

<p>Compared to the balance group, the aerobic training group demonstrated significantly better improvements in fatigue (6 months: β, −9.38; 95% CI, −15.1 to −3.7; <em>P</em> = .001; 9 months: β, −6.94; 95% CI, −12.8 to −1.0; <em>P</em> = .02; and 12 months: β, −8.61; 95% CI, −14.6 to −2.6; <em>P</em> = .005) and V<span class="font-variant-small-caps">o</span><sub>2</sub>max (6 months: β, 4.26; 95% CI, 2.1-6.4; <em>P</em> &lt; .001; 9 months: β, 1.93; 95% CI, −0.33 to 4.2; <em>P</em> = .09; and 12 months: β, 2.2; 95% CI, −0.16 to 4.52; <em>P</em> = .07) (<a href="#noi250064t3" class="usa-link">Table 3</a>; eTables 2 and 3 in <a href="#note-NOI250064-1-s" class="usa-link">Supplement 2</a>; <a href="#noi250064f2" class="usa-link">Figure 2</a>B and C). Results remained significant after correcting for multiple comparison corrections (fatigue: <em>F</em> = 4.3, <em>P</em> = .02; V<span class="font-variant-small-caps">o</span><sub>2</sub>max: <em>F</em> = 5.0, <em>P</em> = .02) (eTable 4 in <a href="#note-NOI250064-1-s" class="usa-link">Supplement 2</a>). Although participants in the aerobic group showed improvements in DGI, gait speed, and TUG, results were not significantly different when compared to the balance group. Measures of quality of life were not improved with either training. We repeated the statistical analysis in completers only and found similar results.</p></section><section id="H2-13-NOI250064"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Adverse Events</h3>
<p>There were no serious adverse events related to training in either group. The most common minor adverse event in the aerobic group was mild pain, which did not impact training. The most common minor adverse event in the balance group was falls, but no injuries were reported (eTable 5 in <a href="#note-NOI250064-1-s" class="usa-link">Supplement 2</a>).</p></section></section><section id="H1-4-NOI250064"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Discussion</h2>
<p>Home-based high-intensity aerobic training led to greater improvement in ataxia symptoms, fitness, and fatigue compared to balance training in individuals with ataxias. Both interventions were safe and feasible, with high adherence to training at 6 months and no serious adverse events reported, suggesting that structured, remotely delivered exercise programs are implementable in this population.</p>
<p>These findings align with prior work demonstrating the potential for aerobic exercise to promote functional plasticity in individuals with cerebellar degeneration. Preclinical and human studies have suggested that aerobic activity may enhance cerebellar output, modulate sensorimotor integration, and delay the progression of motor decline in ataxia syndromes.<sup><a href="#noi250064r19" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r19">19</a>,<a href="#noi250064r46" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r46">46</a>,<a href="#noi250064r47" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r47">47</a></sup> Notably, our trial suggests that even in the context of progressive neurodegeneration, individuals retain capacity for functional gains when appropriately challenged through high-intensity paradigms.</p>
<p>Although balance training is commonly recommended for cerebellar ataxias, its effects are believed to be due to compensation for deficits.<sup><a href="#noi250064r48" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r48">48</a>,<a href="#noi250064r49" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r49">49</a>,<a href="#noi250064r50" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r50">50</a></sup> In our trial, balance group participants experienced modest improvements in ataxia symptoms, but the magnitude of change was smaller than in the aerobic group. These findings support the hypothesis that aerobic training may have broader systemic effects, potentially by improving cardiorespiratory fitness, neuromuscular coordination, or task endurance.</p>
<section id="H2-14-NOI250064"><h3 class="pmc_sec_title">Limitations</h3>
<p>Improvements from aerobic training were maintained at 9 months and 1 year compared to balance training. However, these results are limited by participant dropout. This issue is common with the use of aerobic training as a treatment modality, with numerous reports citing that 50% of people who start an exercise program will drop out within 6 months.<sup><a href="#noi250064r51" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r51">51</a>,<a href="#noi250064r52" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r52">52</a>,<a href="#noi250064r53" class="usa-link" aria-describedby="noi250064r53">53</a></sup> The presence of dropout can lead to attrition bias and an overestimation of treatment effect. Although small, there was a higher dropout rate in the balance group compared to the aerobic group. Our statistical methods handled dropout by using the missing-at-random assumption, which may not be accurate. Higher dropout in the balance group may indicate lower acceptability, reduce power, and impact the interpretability of efficacy. To help mitigate dropout, we showed that individuals who completed the study were statistically like those who dropped out.</p>
<p>Another limitation was the lack of training adherence when study support was withdrawn at 6 months. Although 63% of individuals in the aerobic group continued to train, only 38.5% hit all training goals at 1 year, with most participants training less frequently or with lower intensity. The reduced adherence likely contributed to training benefits reducing after 6 months. In future studies, we plan to implement a virtual coaching program to help limit this issue.</p>
<p>Other limitations of the study include several factors. First, regarding home exercise, although remote delivery enhances real-world generalizability, it limits our ability to confirm intensity and technique fidelity across participants. Second, regarding inclusion of various cerebellar ataxias, while this heterogeneity may reduce statistical power to detect disease-specific effects, it increases the relevance of our findings to general clinical practice. Third, regarding lack of imaging, MRI results will be published as soon as the data are analyzed. Fourth, the intervention period may not be long enough to assess durability of benefits. Indeed, biomarkers like neuroimaging may require a minimum of 1 year to detect differences, especially for slower progressing ataxia types. Ongoing follow-up may help clarify the trajectory of change beyond the active intervention window.</p></section></section><section id="H1-5-NOI250064"><h2 class="pmc_sec_title">Conclusions</h2>
<p>In conclusion, this randomized clinical trial suggests that home-based high-intensity aerobic training is feasible and may offer greater functional benefits than balance training in individuals with cerebellar ataxias. These findings support the inclusion of aerobic exercise in rehabilitation strategies for ataxia.</p></section><section id="note-NOI250064-1"><section class="sm xbox font-sm" id="note-NOI250064-1-s"><div class="caption p">
