## Content

Background
Tatton-Brown-Rahman syndrome (TBRS; OMIM 615879), also known as DNA methyltransferase 3 alpha (DNMT3A)-overgrowth syndrome (DOS), was first described by Tatton-Brown in 2014. This syndrome is characterised by overgrowth, intellectual disability and distinctive facial features and is the consequence of germline loss-of-function variants in DNMT3A, which encodes a DNA methyltransferase involved in epigenetic regulation. Somatic variants of DNMT3A are frequently observed in haematological malignancies, including acute myeloid leukaemia (AML). To date, 100 individuals with TBRS with de novo germline variants have been described. We aimed to further characterise this disorder clinically and at the molecular level in a nationwide series of 24 French patients and to investigate the correlation between the severity of intellectual disability and the type of variant.


Methods
We collected genetic and medical information from 24 individuals with TBRS using a questionnaire released through the French National AnDDI-Rares Network.


Results
Here, we describe the first nationwide French cohort of 24 individuals with germline likely pathogenic/pathogenic variants in DNMT3A, including 17 novel variants. We confirmed that the main phenotypic features were intellectual disability (100% of individuals), distinctive facial features (96%) and overgrowth (87%). We highlighted novel clinical features, such as hypertrichosis, and further described the neurological features and EEG results.


Conclusion
This study of a nationwide cohort of individuals with TBRS confirms previously published data and provides additional information and clarifies clinical features to facilitate diagnosis and improve care. This study adds value to the growing body of knowledge on TBRS and broadens its clinical and molecular spectrum.

INTRODUCTION 1 
 2 
Tatton-Brown-Rahman syndrome (TBRS, OMIM #615879) was first described in 3 
2014 by Katrina Tatton-Brown and Nazneen Rahman [1]. In a second publication, 55 4 
individuals were clinically evaluated, including 13 previously reported individuals [2]. 5 
Numerous subsequent reports have confirmed the original findings [3 –26]. TBRS is 6 
characterized mainly by overgrowth (defined as height and/or head circumference at 7 
least 2 standard deviations above the population mean); intellectual disability ranging 8 
from mild to severe; and distinctive facial features, including coarse features, narrow 9 
palpebral fissures, and heavy and horizontal eyebrows. In addition to these three 10 
main characteristics, other features, such as hypotonia, behavioral disorders, 11 
seizures, joint hypermobility, scoliosis, dental abnormalities, and predisposition to 12 
malignant hemopathies, are more inconsistently described. The vast majority of 13 
reported individuals are children. Most cases are sporadic and occur in individuals 14 
with de novo heterozygous germline variants in DNMT3A . However, parent -to-child 15 
transmission in some families has demonstrated autosomal dominant inheritance 16 
with variable expressivity, and inheritance from unaffected mosaic parents has been 17 
reported [5, 6, 16, 22]. An increasing number of case reports converge toward 18 
phenotypic variability and multisystem involvement in this syndrome. 19 
DNA methyltransferase 3 alpha (DNMT3A) is one of three active human 20 
methyltransferases, with the others being DNMT3B and DNMT1. DNMT3A is 21 
involved in de novo  DNA methylation during development. This enzyme also plays a 22 
role in the maintenance and remodeling of DNA methylation patterns, especially at 23 
CpG sites and in histone proteins. In addition to this central role in epigenetic 24 
regulation and chromatin configuration, a role in gene regulatory functions such as 25

transcriptional silencing and activation has been reported [27, 28] . DNMT3A is also 1 
essential for parental imprinting [29] and XIST repression, which is involved in X 2 
chromosome inactivation [30]. 3 
DNMT3A contains three functional domains: i) a proline- tryptophan-tryptophan-4 
proline (PWWP) domain present in transcription factors involved in the regulation of 5 
developmental processes, ii) an ATRX-DNMT3-DNMT3L (ADD) domain implicated in 6 
protein‒protein and protein‒DNA binding interactions, and iii) a methyltransf erase 7 
(MTase) domain essential for methyltransferase activity. 8 
In individuals with TBRS, DNMT3A  germline variants lead to protein loss of function. 9 
Germline gain -of-function variants in the DNMT3A PWWP domain have been 10 
implicated in Heyn -Sproul-Jackson syn drome (HESJAS, OMIM #618724), which is 11 
characterized by a mirrored phenotype to TBRS, i.e., microcephalic dwarfism. 12 
In addition to DNMT3A constitutional variants, somatic mutations have been reported 13 
in acute myeloid leukemia (AML, OMIM #601626) and other hematopoietic 14 
malignancies. More than half of DNMT3A  somatic variants occur at amino acid 15 
p.Arg882, which is located in the MTase domain [31, 32]. However, the spectrum of 16 
germline variants overlaps with that observed in hematological malignancies [15].  17 
Furthermore, TBRS is associated with perturbed hematopoiesis and the development 18 
of hematopoietic malignancy in children and young adults [2, 10, 11, 13, 33]. 19 
Approximately one hundred individuals have been reported in the literature to date; 20 
however, with the increasing availability of next -generation sequencing, the number 21 
of individuals diagnosed with germline mutations in DNMT3A  is growing, making it 22 
necessary to improve our knowledge of the disease, its evolution and its individual 23 
management. 24

SUBJECTS AND METHODS 4 
 5 
Individual recruitment. A national collaboration through the French National AnDDI -6 
Rares ( Developmental Abnormalities with or without Intellectual Deficiency of Rare 7 
Causes) health network made it possible to collect 24 individuals with TBRS between 8 
January 2021 and January 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) the 9 
presence of a germline likely pathogenic or pathogenic variant in DNMT3A  and 2) 10 
available molecular and clinical data. A DNMT3A -specific clinical proforma was sent 11 
to genetic specialists (including clinical elements present in overgrowth and 12 
intellectual disability syndromes and clinical symptoms already reported in the 13 
literature in individuals with TBRS). When available, we collected photographs, 14 
growth curves, and imaging data. Brain imaging was reinterpreted by a local 15 
specialist for seven individuals with available radiological data.  DNMT3A variants 16 
were detected via gene panel, exome, or genome sequencing. The reference 17 
transcript used for variant designation was NM_022552.5. Variants were interpreted 18 
according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) 19 
guidelines [34]. All individuals or legal guardians provided written informed consent 20 
for genetic analyses. As required by French law, we informed all participants of their 21 
right to oppose the use of their data. Individuals or legal guardians have given their 22 
consent for the use of their photographs in the publication. This study was approved 23 
by the ethics committee of Nancy University Hospital (referral n°429) and was 24 
conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. 25

DISCUSSION 20 
 21 
Katrina Tatton-Brown and Nazneen Rahman clinically evaluated a total of 55 TBRS 22 
individuals [2]. Tlemsani et al.  [18] and Tenorio et al. [8]  reported 7 individuals, Balci 23 
et al. [5]  reported 6 individuals, and Xin et al. [6] reported 6 individuals from two 24 
families. To our knowledge, the present series of 24 French TBRS individuals is the 25 

Accepted manuscript
16 
 
second largest series described to date. Our study confirmed the main features of the 1 
syndrome and provided further molecular and clinical data on this rare overgrowth 2 
condition. We confirmed that the phenotypic spectrum is very broad, ranging from 3 
nonsyndromic ID for one of our individuals (Patient 19) to very severe and syndromic 4 
ID for two individuals (Patients 5 and 15). 5 
 6 
We analyzed the mutational landscape of DNMT3A . Seventeen pathogenic or likely 7 
pathogenic variants observed in our cohort have never been described before, 8 
including an in- frame duplication affecting six highly conserved amino acids in 9 
proximity to the PWWP domain for which the DNA methylation profile showed a 10 
concordant episignature [35] . Furthermore, these positions have been shown to be 11 
crucial in the DNMT1 -DNMT3A interaction [36]. The majority of the variants are 12 
missense variants localized in one of the three described functional domains of the 13 
DNMT3A protein. Importantly, DNMT3A  is one of the most common genes 14 
associated with clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) [37] . Indeed, 15 
some somatic mutations in DNMT3A  provide a preneoplastic growth advantage that 16 
can result in the accumulation, over a period of years, of lymphocytes bearing the 17 
somatic variant. Therefore, some pathogenic germline variants are common in the 18 
general population due to age- related clonal hematopoiesis, thus limiting the utility of 19 
databases such as gnomAD in the pathogenicity stratification of DNMT3A germline 20 
variants [2] since the gnomAD-derived variants were largely derived from lymphocyte 21 
DNA and many gnomAD participants were middle- aged or older. GnomAD missense 22 
variants occurring in the three main DNMT3A functional domains are represented by 23 
histograms in figure 1. 24

As previously shown [19] , we found no significant correlation between the type of 1 
variant and ID severity. 2 
 3 
There is relatively little pregnancy and neonatal data in the literature. We collected 4 
only 10 TBRS individuals with abnormalities during the fetal period, including 4 with 5 
macrosomia (one individual in our cohort) and 4 with preeclampsia (absent in our 6 
cohort). In comparison, preeclampsia was described in 17% of individuals with Sotos 7 
syndrome, another genetic overgrowth syndrome [18]. In conclusion, pregnancy and 8 
neonatal data in patients with TBRS vary and are nonspecific. 9 
 10 
The analysis of antenatal ultrasound data, birth parameters and their evolution favors 11 
overgrowth with constitutional  macrocephaly present from birth or early childhood, 12 
with an evolution in the range of +2 SDs consistent with the literature [2, 38]. 13 
Physical examination revealed facial features already described in the literature, 14 
including a round face with coarse feat ures, thick horizontal eyebrows, narrow 15 
palpebral fissures and dental abnormalities. As previously described, facial features 16 
are more apparent in early adolescence and less specific in early childhood and 17 
adulthood [2, 38]. Among dental abnormalities, malocclusion, caries and double teeth 18 
have been reported [38], and a recent work highlighted the importance of appropriate 19 
dental care for individuals with TBRS [12]. 20 
We observed hair abnormalities in 11 individuals with hypertrichosis  in our cohort. 21 
This manifestation has not been previously noted and could aid in the clinical 22 
diagnosis of TBRS. The early onset of puberty observed in 4 female individuals in our 23 
cohort could also be a new feature of TBRS syndrome. 24

In our cohort, psychomotor development was delayed, with regression in 4 1 
individuals. One hundred percent of individuals had mild to severe ID, with moderate 2 
ID being most common, and 29% of individuals had ASD. These data are consistent 3 
with the literature [38, 3 9]. We noted compulsive eating and/or hyperphagia in 6 of 4 
our individuals, as previously reported [2, 5] . This could contribute to the obesity 5 
observed in this syndrome. 6 
At least one epileptic event occurred in 42% of our individuals; most were isolated 7 
febrile seizures, with afebrile events occurring in only 4 individuals. EEG profiles have 8 
rarely been described in previous studies. We collected EEG data for 12 TBRS 9 
individuals, with focal abnormalities in 3 symptomatic individuals, generalized 10 
abnormalities in one symptomatic individual, and focal abnormalities in 1 individual 11 
without clinical seizures. 12 
Fourteen individuals (58%) had neurological features, with hypotonia, cerebellar 13 
syndrome, a decrease in fine motor coordination, tremor  and symptomatology of 14 
peripheral neuropathy being the most frequent findings. Neurological findings are 15 
poorly described in the literature, highlighting the need for systematic examination of 16 
TBRS individuals by an experienced neurologist to confirm and validate these 17 
findings in further studies. Recently, the description of an individual with congenital 18 
myopathy and rhabdomyolysis and another individual with axonal polyneuropathy led 19 
to the extension of the TBRS phenotype to include neuromuscular features and 20 
polyneuropathy [24, 26]. 21 
On brain imaging, we found the same various nonspecific abnormalities as previously 22 
described in other overgrowth syndromes. However, corpus callosum abnormalities 23 
were present in 8 individuals (44%). This finding is consistent with a recent s tudy 24 
suggesting that structural neuroimaging abnormalities have been previously 25

underestimated in TBRS patients and revealing the presence of corpus callosum and 1 
brain tract alterations, likely caused by microstructural cortical defects and/or primary 2 
anomalies in connection development  [23]. In contrast to the findings of previous 3 
studies, ventriculomegaly or Chiari malformation were rarely observed in our cohort. 4 
 5 
We observed a cardiovascular phenotype in 25% of our individuals. Cardiovascular 6 
phenotypes include congenital malformations diagnosed early in life and aortic root 7 
dilatation, dilated cardiomyopathy or arrythmia diagnosed in late childhood or 8 
adulthood; periodic surveillance with echocardiography is needed to assess aortic 9 
root size [2, 4, 6, 11, 15, 17, 18, 40]. Among respiratory abnormalities, sleep apnea is 10 
the most common manifestation, as previously described [2, 5] . Musculoskeletal 11 
pathologies are common and include joint hypermobility and scoliosis. 12 
 13 
Although neoplasia was rarely observed in our cohort, several cases of cancer of 14 
various types have been described in the literature, including acute myeloid leukemia 15 
and other hematologic or solid tumors [10, 14, 41] . As there are no consensus 16 
guidelines regarding screening for hematologic malignancy in individuals with TBRS 17 
[33, 38, 42], we recommend that hematological malignancies such as easy bruising 18 
and petechiae, recurrent bleeding, pallor, persistent fatigue, fever and recurrent 19 
infections be assessed at each follow-up visit [2]. Furthermore, TBRS individuals and 20 
their families should be educated about the signs and symptoms that would warrant 21 
medical evaluation. Complete blood count and further investigations should be 22 
performed in the event of signs of hematologic malignancy. In addition, any other 23 
change in signs and symptoms in these patients should be investigated further to 24 
exclude the development of tumors. Natural history studies of a larger number of 25

TBRS individuals would be needed to enable surveillance guidelines for 1 
malignancies particularly hematological malignancies to be established. 2 
 3 
In conclusion, this national collaborative study included the second largest cohort of 4 
individuals with TBRS. Our findings are consistent with known clinical features 5 
described in the literature, including ID of varying severity, overgrowth and 6 
recognizable facial features. It is essential to keep in mind the limits induced by 7 
reporting bias, which can lead to probable overestimation of the frequency and 8 
severity of neurodevelopmental disorders.  However, we have highlighted the lack of 9 
specific antenatal signs of Tatton- Brown-Rahman syndrome. Constitutional 10 
overgrowth, although not typically detected in the antenatal period, seems to be 11 
present at birth or in early childhood. We broadened the phenotype by reporting 12 
localized congenital hypertrichosis and early onset of puberty in females. We 13 
highlight the neurological phenotype and specific EEG abnormalities. Although 14 
malignancies seem to be rare, monitoring blood parameters is essential. We did n ot 15 
identify any phenotype‒genotype correlations. 16 
This study may contribute to improving the care of TBRS individuals. Furthermore, 17 
given the involvement of the DNMT3A  gene in methylation, current therapeutic 18 
advances targeting chromatin marks may benefit TBRS patients in the future. 19
