# Impact of Growth Hormone Treatment in Children From an Extended Family With ACAN -Related Short Stature.
**Authors:** Shalev-Goldman E, Asaly A, Ityel H, Pode-Shakked B, Modan-Moses D, Levy-Shraga Y
**Journal:** Clin Genet (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1111/cge.70050](https://doi.org/10.1111/cge.70050)

## Content

Heterozygous variants in the ACAN gene are a recognized cause of short stature. We aimed to evaluate the response to growth hormone (GH) treatment in members of an extended family carrying an ACAN variant. Clinical and laboratory data of five related children were retrospectively collected. GH therapy was initiated at a mean age of 3.2 ± 2.1 years and treated for 6.4 ± 5.0 years. The height standard deviation scores (SDS) increased from -3.3 ± 1.0 to -2.0 ± 1.1 (p = 0.001), with a mean gain of 1.3 ± 0.3 SDS. The most notable increase in growth velocity SDS occurred during the first year of treatment (-0.5 ± 0.6 to 2.1 ± 1.2; p = 0.04). No adverse effects were observed, and bone age did not advance significantly. Three children entered puberty between the ages of 10 and 11.6 years and received adjunctive gonadotropin releasing hormone analog therapy. Genetic testing identified a heterozygous nonsense variant in the ACAN (c.2023C>T; p.Arg675*). Our findings suggest that GH therapy can improve growth velocity in children with ACAN-related short stature, though the degree of response varies even within the same family. The long-term impact on final adult height remains to be elucidated.

Over the past two decades, several single‐gene mutations have been identified as causes of short stature in patients initially classified as idiopathic short stature (ISS) [1]. One such cause is heterozygous variants in the ACAN gene [2]. Aggrecan, encoded by the ACAN gene, is a key proteoglycan component of the extracellular matrix in both the growth plate and articular cartilage. Deficiency in aggrecan disrupts the organization and maturation of chondrocytes, leading to premature hypertrophic differentiation and early cessation of growth [3]. Classically, individuals with heterozygous ACAN variants have been described as having disproportionate short stature and advanced bone age (BA) [2, 4, 5]. More recently, reported clinical findings include macrocephaly, brachydactyly, midface hypoplasia, a flat nasal bridge, hyperlordosis, and scoliosis. Aggrecan deficiency may also impair articular cartilage function, resulting in early‐onset osteoarthritis or osteochondritis dissecans [6]. However, advanced BA is not a consistent feature in all patients. In approximately 20% of cases, short stature may be the only notable finding [5, 7].

The impact of recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) in children with heterozygous ACAN variants has been studied in recent years, but results remain inconclusive [5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. A recent prospective study of 10 short‐statured children over 3 years demonstrated increased linear growth during treatment, with diminished response in those entering puberty before rhGH initiation [15]. Long‐term efficacy data remain limited.

We report an extended family with multiple members across four generations carrying a heterozygous ACAN variant. Five individuals began rhGH treatment for the indication of ISS or growth hormone deficiency (GHD) before the genetic diagnosis of the ACAN variant was established. The focus of the current report was on assessing their response to rhGH therapy.

Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data of the five patients were collected from the patients' medical records. BA was assessed according to the Greulich and Pyle method. IGF‐1 levels were measured using a chemiluminescent immunometric method (Liaison, DiaSorin, Italy, or Immulite 2000, Siemens Medical Solutions Diagnostics, USA). The IGF‐1 reference intervals were consistent across both immunoassay systems. IGF‐1 SDS was calculated based on age and sex. GH status was evaluated by stimulation tests. A normal response to the test was defined as GH > 10 mcg/L until the year 2010, and as GH > 7.5 mcg/L thereafter, according to national guidelines.

Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations (SD) or as medians (ranges). The paired t‐test was applied to compare between variables before and after rhGH initiation. Results were considered significant if the two‐sided p value was < 0.05.

Five children were evaluated at a mean age of 2.0 ± 1.1 years, with baseline height and weight SDS of −3.1 ± 1.0 and −1.4 ± 1.0, respectively. The children underwent a comprehensive evaluation for short stature in accordance with clinical guidelines, including growth hormone stimulation testing. Laboratory investigation showed normal thyroid function and a negative chronic disease screen. In two of the children, patients III.1 and IV.11, isolated GHD was diagnosed based on peak GH levels below the cut‐off in two consecutive GH stimulation tests. These two children started rhGH treatment at ages 10 months and 2.6 years, respectively (Table 1). Three children exhibited normal responses to the stimulation test and commenced rhGH therapy between the ages of 1.8 and 5.4 years.

Growth hormone (GH) peak was assessed via stimulation testing. Prior to 2010, a normal response was defined as GH > 10 mcg/L; this threshold was later revised to > 7.5 mcg/L. At the time, a GH level of 7.9 mcg/L was considered subnormal.

The mean height SDS at the initiation of rhGH treatment was −3.3 ± 1.0. The initial rhGH dose was 0.03 mg/kg, and it gradually increased to 0.05 mg/kg based on height velocity and IGF‐1 levels. The most prominent increase in growth velocity (GV) SDS occurred during the first year (pre‐treatment: −0.5 ± 0.6 vs. GV SDS during the first year: 2.1 ± 1.2, p = 0.04). Mean treatment duration was 6.4 ± 5.0 years (range: 1.6–14.5). At last follow‐up, mean age was 9.6 ± 4.5 years, and height SDS had significantly improved from baseline to −2.0 ± 1.1 (p = 0.001), with a mean gain of 1.3 ± 0.3 SDS (range: 0.8–1.5). At baseline, BA was not significantly advanced, with a mean bone age‐to‐chronological age (BA/CA) ratio of 1.1 ± 0.3. This ratio remained stable throughout the follow‐up period (Table 1). Changes in height SDS, GV SDS, and IGF‐1 SDS before treatment and during the first 3 years of therapy are illustrated in Figure 1.2.

During the study, three boys (III.1, IV.3, IV.4) entered puberty at ages 11.6, 10.8, and 10 years, respectively. All received gonadotropin releasing hormone (GnRH) analog therapy to arrest puberty and extend the growth period. Patient III.1 discontinued treatment at age 14, while the others remained on therapy at study end. The growth charts of the five patients are presented in Figure 2.

The proband (III.1) was referred for genetic evaluation at 13.6 years of age due to a strong family history of short stature and clinical features consistent with disproportionate short stature. Whole exome sequencing identified a heterozygous ACAN nonsense variant (NM_001369268.1: c.2023C>T; p.Arg675*). This variant introduces a premature stop codon, predicted to result in loss of function through nonsense‐mediated decay. The variant has been previously described [16]. Subsequent Sanger sequencing confirmed the variant in other affected family members.

We describe an extended family with a nonsense variant in exon 10 of the ACAN gene, located within the G2 domain. The proband was diagnosed at 13.6 years, over 12 years after initiating rhGH therapy for suspected GHD. Advances in molecular genetics have enabled the reclassification of patients previously labeled as isolated GHD or ISS. The diagnosis of GHD typically relies on two GH stimulation tests, though these tests are known to have low specificity, which can result in false‐positive diagnoses [17]. In a study involving 52 children with isolated GHD and a familial pattern of short stature, a pathogenic variant in a gene associated with growth was identified in 15 children (29%). Notably, in 10 cases, a primary growth plate disorder—including ACAN deficiency—was identified [17]. In another study of 1005 short stature patients, ACAN variants were present in 1.2% of the cohort, rising to 14.3% among those with advanced BA and 35.7% in those with both advanced BA and a family history of short stature [4]. In our study, two patients were diagnosed with GHD and three with ISS; all were subsequently found to carry the same pathogenic ACAN variant.

The therapeutic outcome of rhGH treatment in children carrying pathogenic ACAN variants remains uncertain. In our cohort, the mean improvement in height SDS during treatment with rhGH was 1.3 ± 0.3 (range: 0.8–1.5), which is consistent with findings from two recent studies evaluating the 3‐year response to rhGH therapy, reporting mean height gains of 1.0 SDS (0.9–1.4) [14] and 1.2 SDS (range: 0.8–1.9) [15], respectively. A broader literature review comprising 48 children treated with rhGH demonstrated a more modest mean improvement in height SDS of 0.5, with height SDS increasing from a baseline of −2.7 ± 0.9 to −2.2 ± 1.0 following therapy [18]. Several factors may account for the variability in treatment response, including age at treatment initiation (with initiation during puberty appearing less effective [14]), rhGH dosage, treatment adherence, and the specific ACAN variant involved [19]. The greatest increase in growth velocity was observed during the first year of therapy, followed by a more modest response in subsequent years [15]. Although early treatment results are promising, the long‐term impact on final adult height has yet to be determined.

A distinctive feature of our study is the inclusion of three patients who initiated rhGH therapy at an early age, with an extended follow‐up period. The onset of puberty occurred between 10 and 11.6 years of age, at which point GnRH analog therapy was introduced. Notably, patient III.1 experienced a decline in height SDS during the pubertal years, from −2.7 to −3.3. Nevertheless, by the end of the study, at near‐final adult height, he had achieved a net gain of 0.8 SDS relative to baseline. The efficacy of combined treatment with rhGH and GnRH analogs remains inconclusive. While van der Steen et al. [7] reported a potential benefit in terms of final adult height with this combination therapy, other studies have not demonstrated a significant advantage [11, 19].

An increased BA/CA ratio was observed only in 2 of the 5 patients. While individuals with ACAN mutations have traditionally been characterized by advanced BA [2], more recent studies have reported that only approximately 24%–50% of patients exhibit this feature [8, 14, 18]. Notably, our findings demonstrated that the BA/CA ratio remained stable during rhGH therapy, consistent with previous observations [15].

GH therapy was well tolerated, consistent with previous studies. However, two safety concerns warrant attention. In a previous study, scoliosis developed in 2 of 36 children during treatment [14]. Scoliosis is a known feature of ACAN deficiency, reported in ~2% of cases [18]. It remains unclear whether scoliosis onset is part of the natural disease course or related to rhGH therapy. Routine scoliosis screening is recommended during rhGH follow‐up [20].

Another concern is the potential development of osteochondritis dissecans (OCD). A case report described a boy with ISS who developed multi‐joint OCD 6 months after starting rhGH therapy, with MRI confirming lesions [6]. Subsequent WES identified a de novo heterozygous pathogenic ACAN variant. Individuals with pathogenic ACAN variants are known to be at increased risk for developing multi‐joint OCD. The authors of the case report hypothesized that rhGH therapy may have triggered or exacerbated OCD [6]. However, there is currently no established evidence that rhGH treatment alone increases the risk of OCD in the general population.

In summary, this report adds to the current understanding of rhGH therapy in ACAN‐related short stature, demonstrating its efficacy and safety. Notable inter‐individual variability in treatment response was observed. The long‐term impact on final adult height remains to be elucidated, and further longitudinal studies are warranted to address this question.
