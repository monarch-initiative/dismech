progressive neurodegeneration. The majority of cases are caused by variants in
the gene encoding the PDC subunit E1α. The molecular effects of the variants,
however, remain poorly understood. Using yeast as a eukaryotic model system, we

The majority of cases are caused by variants in the gene encoding the PDC subunit E1α. The molecular effects of the variants, however, remain poorly understood. The E1α A189V substitution located in the heterodimer interface showed a more compact conformation with significant underrepresentation of E1 in PDC and impaired overall PDC activity. The E1α M230V substitution located in the tetramer and heterodimer interface showed a relatively more open conformation and was particularly affected by low thiamin pyrophosphate concentrations. The E1α R322C substitution located in the phosphorylation loop of E1α resulted in PDC lacking E3 subunits and abolished overall functional activity. Furthermore, we show for the E1α variant A189V that variant E1α accumulates in the Hsp60 chaperonin, but can be released upon ATP supplementation. Our studies suggest that pathogenic E1α variants may be associated with structural changes of PDC and impaired folding of E1α.

Pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC) deficiency is a severe genetic disorder that leads to complex brain abnormalities and metabolic disturbances. The disease manifests as a neurodevelopmental disorder of varying severity and is associated with mental and psychomotor retardation accompanied by lactic acidosis [1,2]. Structural abnormalities found in the brains of patients with PDC deficiency include ventriculomegaly, microcephaly, subacute necrotizing encephalomyelopathy (Leigh syndrome), dysgenesis and agenesis of the corpus callosum, as well as structural abnormalities of the basal ganglia, brain stem and cerebellum [2]. Hypotonia or, less frequently, hypertonia, as well as seizures, are regularly seen in patients with PDC deficiency [2,3]. At present, there is no cure for PDC deficiency, but several therapeutic approaches have been proposed, including ketogenic and high-fat diets, as well as the use of dietary supplements, such as thiamine and dichloroacetate [4–6]. More recently, preclinical studies have suggested phenylbutyrate, a pan-histone deacetylase inhibitor, as a treatment option for PDC deficiency [7,8].

PDC deficiency is caused by pathogenic variants in genes coding for PDC components [2]. PDC is a multi-megadalton macromolecular assembly primarily found in the mitochondria. It comprises three enzymatic components, namely the thiamin pyrophosphate (TPP)-dependent pyruvate dehydrogenase subunit E1 composed of α2β2heterotetramers, the dihydrolipoamide acetyltransferase subunit E2, and the dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase homodimeric subunit E3 [9]. Together, the enzymatic activities of PDC catalyze the decarboxylation of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA [9]. Thus, PDC links anaerobic glycolysis and the citric acid cycle.

Structurally, the eukaryotic PDC comprises a roughly pentagonal pseudo-dodecahedral core, which is surrounded by a complex peripheral shell [10]. The pseudo-dodecahedral PDC core comprises multiple copies of the E2 protein and E3-binding protein (E3BP); multiple copies of E3 homodimers and E1α2β2heterotetramers surround the inner core complex [11]. The exact stoichiometry of PDC is a matter of debate [12] and several models have been put forward [9]. According to the ‘addition’ model, 12 E3BP are bound to 60 E2 (12 E3BP + 60 E2) [13], while the ‘substitution’ models propose that either 12 E3BP are bound to 48 E2 (12 E3BP + 48 E2) [14] or 20 E3BP are bound to 40 E2 (20 E3BP + 40 E2) [15]. Thus, up to 60 copies of E1α2β2heterotetramers and 24 copies of E3 homodimers bind to the pseudo-dodecahedral PDC core. Of note, the enzymatic activity of PDC is regulated by phosphorylation [9]. Phosphorylation of E1 inactivates PDC, while dephosphorylation activates the enzyme complex [16,17].

Pathogenic variants were reported for all genes encoding PDC subunits [2]. However, the vast majority of PDC deficiencies can be attributed to pathogenic variants in the gene encoding the E1α subunit [2,18], while variants in the genes coding for E1β and E3BP subunits are a rare cause of PDC deficiency [19,20]. Of note, pathogenic variants in theDLDgene encoding the E3 subunit may lead to a complex overlap syndrome with neurological, hepatic, myopathic, and cardiac involvement, as E3, in addition to PDC, is an integral component of two further multi-enzyme complexes, namely α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase and branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase [21].

Although pathogenic variants in the gene encoding the E1α subunit are the predominant cause of PDC deficiency [2,18], it is poorly understood how these variants affect PDC. In this respect, the yeastSaccharomyces cerevisiae(S. cerevisiae) has been established as an effective system to model human disease mechanisms that involve mitochondria [22,23]. Human and yeast PDC are highly conserved (Fig.1a, b). AsS. cerevisiaepreferentially uses pyruvate for ethanol production rather than importing the pyruvate into the citric acid cycle, PDC is, in contrast to human, not essential for survival ofS. cerevisiaeunder standard high-glucose conditions. Thus, yeast is viable despite the introduction of a (potentially) lethal mutation in the gene encoding the E1α subunit (i.e.,PDA1). Moreover, as yeast is a favorable system for genetic tagging by homologous recombination,S. cerevisiaeenables to work with endogenous PDC complexes (i.e., all genes are at their original gene loci under the control of their native promoters), so the risk of accumulating partially or artificially assembled complexes that could be formed due to non-physiological or variable expression levels of individual components in foreign expression hosts is minimized. In this context, it has been shown in a previous study that human PDC cores produced inE. colican adopt a stoichiometry different from native bovine PDC cores [24].

Recently, we described a yeastS. cerevisiaemodel system in which wild-typePDA1was tagged with a C-terminal TwinStrep-tag [25]. This system allowed us to purify endogenous PDC efficiently from cell extracts for molecular characterization. In this study, to address how PDC deficiency-related variants affect PDC, we mutated severalE1αgene sites. We herein selected point mutations from different functional regions of the E1α protein [2] for the analysis and studied the variants in the yeast model: the position mutated in the E1α A189V variant (A169V in human) is attributed to the heterodimer interface, the position of the E1α M230V variant (M210V in human) is attributed to an overlap of heterodimer and tetramer interface, and the position of the E1α R322C variant (R302C in human) is attributed to the phosphorylation loop region. Our biochemical and structural studies suggest that pathogenic E1α variants may be associated with specific structural changes of PDC and suggest a role of impaired folding of variant E1α.

Pyruvate dehydrogenase complex is a multi-megadalton macromolecular complex that assembles into an E2–E3BP core surrounded by an E1–E3 shell at a distanced(Fig.1a). Genetic studies of PDC deficiency patients indicated that the observed variants in thePDHA1gene that encodes the E1α subunit are distributed throughout the gene including several regions where pathogenic variants cluster [2,18]. In this study, we selected three representative single amino acid residue substitutions within the main clusters of pathogenic E1α variants described for PDC deficiency: A169V, M210V, and R302C [2]. The variants in human E1α correspond to A189V, M230V, and R322C, respectively, in yeast (numbering including the mitochondrial transit peptide). These sites are located in different functional regions of the E1α subunit: the A169V variant is found in the heterodimer interface of human E1α, the M210V variant is attributed to the heterodimer-tetramer interface overlap region of human E1α, and the R302C variant has been suggested to reside in the phosphorylation loop of human E1α [2]. All variants are located within regions of the E1α protein that are conserved betweenHomo sapiensandS. cerevisiaeE1α (Fig.1b).

We introduced the corresponding mutations into a haploid yeast strain expressing E1α with a C-terminal TwinStrep-tag using site-directed mutagenesis of thePDA1cassette and homologous recombination (Fig.1c). After selection, we isolated colonies that harbored the intended mutation within thePDA1gene and confirmed the mutation by sequencing (Fig.1d). Of note, the resulting yeast strain produces only variant protein, due to its haploid nature. Moreover, the protein is expressed under its native promoter. Using quantitative western blotting, we tested whether or not the protein level of E1α is altered in the variant strains in comparison to the control strain. We found a trend towards higher E1α-TwinStrep protein amounts for the variants A189V and M230V and a significant increase in E1α-TwinStrep levels in the R322C variant (p< 0.05; Fig.1e) suggesting different degrees of functional impairment.

Next, we assayed the functional activity of purified E1α A189V variant PDC by measuring the absorption at 340 nm upon addition of substrate as indicator for the formation of NADH. This assay measures the overall functional activity of PDC and yields NADH production only if all three PDC activities are functional. Lineweaver–Burk plotting revealed a Michaelis constantKmof 0.256 mM (Fig.2b; the kinetic data are summarized in Supplementary Table 2), which is higher than theKmvalue obtained for control PDC under otherwise identical purification conditions (0.114 mM; Supplementary Fig. 1c). The specific activity of E1α A189V variant PDC decreased to 5% of the control PDC (0.32 vs. 6.0 U/mg, respectively), demonstrating that E1α A189V variant isolates are less active than control PDC.

Taken together with the somewhat slower migration of the E1α A189V variant PDC in density gradient ultracentrifugation, we reasoned that the architecture of the variant complexes was altered. To support this conclusion, we imaged the peak gradient fractions by electron microscopy. Individual PDC adopted a polygonal shape, to which small globular domains were attached closely (Fig.2c). We determined class averages of the PDC by single-particle image processing [33]. These class averages revealed particles composed of a pseudo-symmetrical inner core and single, small, globular domains in the periphery (Fig.2d). Few globular, peripheral domains were attached to the core, and these domains surrounded the core at a close distance. The class averages (Fig.2d) were in full agreement with the individual raw particle images (Fig.2c).

To provide further evidence for structural alterations of E1α A189V variant PDC, we determined the size distribution and performed difference mapping. For the inner PDC core, we found no significant difference between the E1α A189V variant and the control particles (p′ = 0.626; diameter of 184.6 ± 13.5 Å for the E1α A189V variant, and 183.8 ± 8.8 Å for the control; Fig.2e, left; the statistical data are summarized in Supplementary Table 3). In contrast, the dimension of the outer shell of the E1α A189V variant PDC was smaller (282.2 ± 19.9 Å for the E1α A189V variant and 329.4 ± 10.6 Å for the control;p′ = 1.3e−46; Fig.2e, right).

In line with the smaller overall diameter of the E1α A189V variant PDC shell, we found predominantly differences in the shell (Fig.2f). In particular, the shell of the E1α A189V variant PDC was incomplete in comparison to control PDC (compare red areas in Fig.2f). While the outer shell of control PDC comprises a (near) complete layer of densities surrounding the core, a few single densities are bound to the E1α A189V variant core. Moreover, these single, globular densities are attached at a closer distance to the core, while a darker gap of lower density between the core and the shell (distancedin Fig.1a) is seen in control PDC. This gap grants the control PDC a typical appearance that is not seen in the E1α A189V variant. In contrast, there was good agreement between the E1α A189V variant and control PDC cores. We conclude that the functional impairment of E1α A189V variant PDC is accompanied by significant structural alterations resulting in a smaller, more compact architecture of the E1α A189V variant PDC.

In ultracentrifugation, E1α M230V variant PDC complexes recovered by StrepTactin affinity purification peaked in fraction 5 and were likewise slower migrating than control PDC [25] (Fig.3a). All five PDC components comigrated in this gradient fraction and were clearly separated in the gel (Fig.3a). In the functional assay, the E1α M230V variant PDC had a slightly higherKm(0.163 mM; Fig.3b; Supplementary Table 2) than control PDC (0.114 mM) and exhibited a slightly lower specific activity at 5 mM pyruvate compared to control PDC (5.3 vs. 6.0 U/mg, respectively).

As these functional parameters were strikingly similar between E1α M230V variant and control PDC at saturating pyruvate and TPP concentrations, we also tested the dependence on TPP at low pyruvate concentrations. To this end, we varied the TPP concentration at a constant pyruvate concentration of 0.133 mM and performed activity assays. At this relatively low pyruvate concentration, the specific activities of the control and E1α M230V variant PDC were 2.65 and 0.88 U/mg, respectively (i.e., 33% of control PDC). The enzymatic activity of both control and E1α M230V variant PDC was dependent on the TPP concentration; however, E1α M230V variant PDC responded more strongly to decreasing TPP concentrations (Fig.3b, inset). While the relative specific activity of control PDC decreased to about 79% at the lowest TPP concentration tested, it dropped to 53% for the activity of the E1α M230V variant PDC in comparison to the standard 0.3 mM TPP concentration (p< 0.01). Thus, the functional effect of the E1α M230V variant PDC is most pronounced at non-saturating pyruvate and TPP concentrations.

Electron microscopy of E1α M230V variant PDC revealed a particle with a compact core to which globular small domains appear to be attached at a certain distance (Fig.3c). In comparison to the more compact appearance of E1α A189V variant PDC (Fig.2c), E1α M230V variant PDC particles exhibited a looser appearance (Fig.3c). In accordance with the individual particle images (Fig.3c), class averages of the E1α M230V variant PDC (Fig.3d) revealed a core indistinguishable from the core structure observed for control and E1α M230V variant PDC (Fig.2d). Shell densities incompletely surrounded the core of E1α M230V variant PDC. In comparison to control PDC [25], we found a higher distancedbetween core and shell densities for the E1α M230V variant PDC (Fig.3d).

To corroborate the data, we determined the size distribution of E1α M230V variant PDC cores and shells in comparison to control PDC. Again, there was no significant difference between E1α M230V variant and control PDC cores (p′ = 0.434; diameter of 185.0 ± 11.7 Å for the E1α M230V variant and 183.8 ± 8.8 Å for the control; Fig.3e, left; Supplementary Table 3). However, the diameter of the outer shell was larger in the E1α M230V variant PDC than in control PDC (390.1 ± 27.3 Å for the E1α M230V variant and 329.4 ± 10.6 Å for the control,p′ = 9.9e−43; Fig.3e, right).

In addition, difference mapping confirmed the high similarity of the E1α M230V variant and control PDC cores (Fig.3f). However, relative to those of control PDC, the shell densities of E1α M230V variant PDC were attached to the core at a greater distance (Fig.3f) and did not match control PDC densities (red areas in Fig.3f, bottom). Instead, the outer densities of the E1α M230V variant PDC were associated with the core more peripherally (blue areas in Fig.3f, bottom). Thus, the E1α M230V variant induces a more open, loose overall architecture and impairs PDC function.

When the same set of experiments was performed for E1α R322C variant PDC, E1α R322C variant PDC sedimented slightly slower than the control during ultracentrifugation (i.e., fraction 6; Fig.4a). The banding pattern differed from the one observed for the other samples: only four clear bands out of the expected five PDC bands were reproducibly visible in the SDS-PAGE (see below for protein identification). Strikingly, we could not detect functional activity of the E1α R322C variant PDC (Fig.4b; Supplementary Table 2), demonstrating that the particles are devoid of measurable overall catalytic activity.

To test whether or not the absence of functional activity is associated with alterations in the quaternary structure, we imaged the peak gradient fraction of E1α R322C variant PDC using electron microscopy. Notably, individual particle images of E1α R322C variant PDC were basically indistinguishable from control PDC (Fig.4c). Single-particle image processing corroborated this observation. The resulting class averages were reminiscent of those observed for control PDC and comprised a core and an outer shell at the same position, as observed for control PDC (Fig.4d). However, we noticed that the shell densities of E1α R322C variant PDC were incomplete, resulting in the appearance of gaps within the shell.

In line with these observations, the dimensions of the E1α R322C variant and control PDC cores were not significantly different (p′ = 0.077; dimensions of 181.5 ± 9.9 Å for the E1α R322C variant, 183.8 ± 8.8 Å for the control; Fig.4e, left). The outer shell of E1α R322C variant PDC was slightly smaller than that of control PDC (314.3 ± 13.2 Å for the E1α R322C variant and 329.4 ± 10.6 Å for control;p′ = 4.2e−16; Fig.4e, right; Supplementary Table 3), though this difference was less pronounced than in the E1α A189V variant PDC (282.2 ± 19.9 Å). Difference mapping of E1α R322C variant PDC indicated minor differences in shell densities, which were sometimes underrepresented and broken (red areas in Fig.4f). We conclude that the E1α R322C variant renders the PDC inactive, but the structural changes that occurred in this variant were least pronounced of all variants studied herein.

Based on the fact that the E1α R322C variant complexes were primarily functionally inactive (Fig.4b), we hypothesized that one or more components of the complex might be absent in the E1α R322C variant complexes. Along these lines, we observed only four clear protein bands in the SDS-PAGE of the E1α R322C variant PDC (Fig.4a). We quantified the peak sucrose gradient fractions of control and E1α variant PDC proteins by SDS-PAGE (Fig.5a). Using peak sucrose gradient fractions prevents the modulation of band intensities by the presence of individual affinity-selected proteins (e.g., E1α) or smaller E1α-containing subcomplexes. In contrast, unfractionated PDC eluates may contain mixtures of multiple E1α (sub-) particles and thus do not allow for reliable quantification. While control, E1α A189V, and E1α M230V variant PDC showed bands for all five PDC proteins, only four visible bands were observed for the E1α R322C variant PDC (Fig.5a).

Using densitometry, we estimated the abundance of the respective proteins relative to the E2 protein, which appears to be the most stable element of control and E1α variant PDC. In comparison to control PDC, E3 was significantly reduced in E1α R322C variant PDC (p< 0.05; Fig.5b). The E1α A189V variant showed significantly decreased levels of E1α and E1β (p< 0.05; Fig.5b). In addition, we observed a tendency towards decreased levels of E1α and E1β also for the E1α M230V variant (p≤ 0.1). To confirm the identity of observed protein bands, we performed mass spectrometry-based protein identification. E1α, E1β, E2, and E3BP were identified in all samples (Fig.5c). In contrast, E3 was readily identified in the control, E1α A189V variant, and E1α M230V variant PDC, but not in the E1α R322C variant PDC. Thus, the E1 and E3 subunits are destabilized in E1α A189V and E1α R322C variant PDC, respectively. As the effect of an E1α variant on E3 was unexpected, we subjected the E1α R322C variant strain to complete sequencing of all five PDC genes, which confirmed that the introduced mutation was the only deviation from the S288C wild-type sequence for all PDC genes.

In our elution fractions, we noticed the coelution of PDC with an approximately 60 kDa band, which was more pronounced in E1α variant PDC isolates than in control PDC isolates. In gradient ultracentrifugation fractions optimized for the fully assembled PDC, the 60 kDa proteins sedimented together with E1α and E1β to a lower S value than did the fully assembled PDC (Figs.2a,3a,4a, fractions 7–9). We, therefore, centrifuged the eluates at higher speed to achieve a better separation in the 15–25 Svedberg (S) region and analyzed the fractions by SDS-PAGE. The 60 kDa protein comigrated with E1α, and to a lower extent with E1β at an apparent S value of approximately 20S (peak in fraction 17), but not with the other PDC constituents (Fig.6a). Thus, under both centrifugation conditions, the 60 kDa protein comigrated with E1, indicating that the proteins form a complex.

Using mass spectrometry, we identified the 60 kDa protein as mitochondrial heat-shock protein 60 (Hsp60). Hsp60 assists proteins in obtaining their native fold in the mitochondria [34]. As Hsp60 can collaborate with the cochaperonin Hsp10 [35], we also evaluated the low-molecular weight area of the SDS-PAGE for the presence of a 10 kDa protein. We did not observe a band for the Hsp10 protein for any of the PDC studied herein, indicating that E1 predominantly forms a complex with Hsp60 alone in the slower migrating gradient fractions.

To assess whether or not E1 formed a defined complex with the chaperonin Hsp60 and whether or not E1α could be released from Hsp60, we analyzed E1α A189V variant isolates and incubated the peak Hsp60 fraction in the absence or presence of supplemented ATP and analyzed the samples by BN-PAGE. We selected a high concentration of ATP to ensure that sufficient ATP amounts were available. In the absence of ATP, we observed a rather broad band for the Hsp60 complexes at an estimated molecular weight of 480–800 kDa (Fig.6b, lanes 2–6). In addition, we observed a weak signal ≥ 1 MDa. These sizes are in line with a mixture of heptameric and tetradecameric Hsp60 complexes [36] in addition to somewhat variable high-molecular weight (HMW) complexes. Upon incubation with ATP, the majority of the Hsp60 complexes in the 480–800 kDa range were converted to a sharply migrating form of ~ 460 kDa (Fig.6b, lanes 7–10). However, the HMW complexes remained visible upon ATP incubation.

To analyze substrate release of these complexes, we transferred the BN-PAGE proteins to a membrane and probed for E1α using streptactin. Samples incubated in the absence of ATP produced a broad band in the molecular weight area, where the Hsp60 complexes were observed as well (compare Fig.6c with6b, lanes 2–6). In contrast, this band disappeared upon incubation with ATP, suggesting that the E1α substrate was released upon ATP addition (Fig.6c, lanes 7–10). The HMW complexes, however, were shown to contain E1α independent of ATP treatment. We conclude that the Hsp60 complexes can release non-aggregated E1α upon supplementation with ATP, whereas especially larger aggregates containing E1α do not effectively disintegrate during the tested period of time.

To corroborate these data, we subjected the peak glycerol gradient fractions to electron microscopy. In the absence of supplemented ATP, we observed Hsp60 complexes that bound rather amorphous aggregates; in addition, many Hsp60 complexes were attached to each other (Fig.6d and Supplementary Fig. 2). Upon incubation with ATP, Hsp60 complexes appeared more dispersed with less aggregation; however, some of the amorphous material remained visible (Fig.6e and Supplementary Fig. 2). With and without ATP, the Hsp60 complexes occurred as a mixture of double rings, incomplete double rings and single rings (Fig.6d, e); these findings are in line with the BN-PAGE data (Fig.6b, c). Thus, variant E1α accumulates in Hsp60 complexes and can be partially released upon the addition of ATP.

Pyruvate dehydrogenase complex is a central metabolic enzyme complex that links anaerobic glycolysis in the cytoplasm with the citric acid cycle in mitochondria [9]. Functional impairment in PDC as observed in PDC deficiency affects brain development [1,2]. Here, we used a model system to study the structural effects of three pathogenic E1α variants described in human, A169V, M210V, and R302C representing different forms of PDC deficiency. In line with the previous results in patients [18,37–40], we show here that all three analyzed E1α variant PDC complexes exhibited compromised enzymatic function. The E1α A169V variant, E1α M210V variant, and E1α R302C variant were suggested previously to disturb the E1 heterodimer interface, the E1 tetramer interface, and the phosphorylation loop, respectively [2].

Herein, the most prominent effect was introduced by the E1α R322C variant and resulted in PDC devoid of any measureable overall enzymatic activity. This observation is consistent with previous reports using cell lines established from a PDC deficiency patient carrying the E1α R302C variant as well as a cell line engineered to express the E1α R302C variant, only [37,38]. Accordingly, the E1α R302C variant has been stratified into the PDC deficiency group (0–25% PDC activity) affected most severely by the disorder [2]. However, a reduced level of PDH activity was reported in a female patient carrying a heterozygous E1α R302C variant [18]. As X-inactivation data were not reported in that study [18], the contribution of the wild-type E1α subunit to the overall activity of PDC detected cannot be deduced in this case. Of note, the E1α R302C variant has so far only be observed in females [18,38,41] as well as a male with somatic mosaicism for the E1α R302C variant [18], which furthermore supports the severity of the E1α R302C variant.

Despite the severe phenotype, our E1α R322C variant PDC were remarkably similar in their overall architecture to control PDC [25]. We found minor differences in the morphology of the particles, resulting in small gaps in the outer shell. In line with this observation, we demonstrated that the E3 enzyme, which is located in the outer shell, is strongly underrepresented. Thus, the lack of functional activity is caused by the absence of one of the PDC enzymes required for NADH formation. This observation was unexpected as, in contrast to α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex (OGDC) [42], there are no known direct interactions between E1 and E3 in PDC. E3 is recruited to PDC via E3BP with a dissociation constant (Kd) of 0.78 nM [43,44]. However, as arginine-302 is located on the outside of the E1 heterotetramer (Fig.7a, green residue), a weak interaction between E1 and E3 may be possible. Alternatively, the variant may influence post-translational modifications of E3BP that decrease its affinity to E3. As the activity of E1 was not assessed separately, the E1α R322C variant PDC may still exhibit functional defects in its E1 subunit additional to its lack of E3.

The E1α A169V variant (Fig.7a, orange residue) is located within the E1 heterodimer interface (yellow residues). For the yeast E1α A189V variant PDC, we observed a significant underrepresentation of both E1α and E1β, suggesting that the variant destabilizes the binding of E1 to the core complex. Note that E1α does not bind directly to E2 but rather depends on the interaction with E1β for integration into the PDC [45]. This compositional difference was also reflected by a lower number of protuberances in the outer shell, in line with the E1α2β2heterotetramers contributing the major portion of outer shell densities [46,47]. The E1 underrepresentation as well as the lower core–shell distance gave the E1α A189V variant PDC particles an overall more compact appearance. The E1α A189V variant PDC exhibited impaired functional activity and possessed a higherKm. In line with our results, a severely reduced overall activity of PDC was observed in a male patient [18], and the E1α A169V variant has likewise been grouped into the PDC deficiency class with 0–25% PDC activity [2].

Based on patient data, where a severe reduction of the PDC activity was observed in skin fibroblasts and lymphocytes of a male patient [39], also the E1α M210V variant has been suggested to belong to the PDC deficiency class with 0–25% PDC activity [2]. Here, the yeast E1α M230V variant PDC function was primarily affected at non-saturating pyruvate and TPP concentrations. In line with our data, a study using a TPP analogue suggested that the E1α M210V variant influences TPP binding [40]. Methionine-210 has been reported to be located in the conserved TPP-binding motif [39,48]; however, methionine-210 is found at a certain distance to TPP (Fig.7a, red residue) and thus does not directly bind TPP [49]. It is, therefore, possible that this residue has an indirect influence on the structural integrity of the TPP-binding domain and thus can interfere with the TPP interaction. In addition to impaired TPP binding, we demonstrated that the M230V substitution induces an overall structural change in the PDC, resulting in a more open conformation. In this respect, it has been suggested that the core–shell distance is optimized in wild-type PDC, allowing one lipoyl arm to reach multiple E1 subunits [46]. Accordingly, a larger distance would restrict the possibilities of a given E2 lipoyl residue to contact and transport substrate from E1 to E2. Moreover, the somewhat lowerdfvalues obtained for E1α M230V variant shell and E1α A189V variant shell compared to the theoretical maximum may indicate that there may be a somewhat higher degree of heterogeneity (i.e., statistical variance) in the E1α M230V variant and E1α A189V variant samples compared to control.

For E1α variant PDC, we further observed an accumulation of E1α in the Hsp60 chaperonin complex (Fig.7b). Mitochondrial Hsp60 and the related bacterial GroEL complex are ATP-dependent chaperonins that facilitate protein folding in mitochondria and bacteria, respectively [34,50]. They are composed of 7–14 (Hsp60) and 14 (GroEL) identical subunits, forming a barrel-like structure [51,52]. By sequence comparison, yeast Hsp60 shows 57% identity to human HSP60, and the functional conservation of the yeast Hsp60 system and its suitability to model human disease is well established [22,23,53]. The yeast Hsp60 system was even shown to be capable to facilitate folding of human substrates and has been used in multiple studies to analyze folding of human wild-type and mutated proteins [54]. Our data show that even single amino acid variants in critical residues hamper the refolding of E1α upon import into the mitochondria, which may lead to the accumulation of aggregates that cannot be integrated into PDC (Fig.7b). Note that in our study, all PDC proteins were endogenous proteins expressed under their native promoters, which excludes the induction of aggregation caused by forced overexpression. In this regard, the fact that yeast PDC proteins were studied in relation to yeast’s own mitochondrial folding machinery also rules out any artifacts related to inter-species incompatibilities.

Hsp60 barrels can collaborate with the homoheptameric Hsp10 or GroES cochaperonin complex [50]. The heptameric Hsp10 or GroES complex thereby forms a lid-like structure enclosing the folding chamber [52,55]. Of note, some substrates can also be folded in the absence of a cochaperonin [56–58]. The variant E1α-Hsp60 chaperone complexes studied herein were not associated with the Hsp10 cochaperonin but, rather, required ATP for substrate release. An additional, though less well-described, function of Hsp60 that has been put forward is an assembly function for macromolecular complexes [54,59]. The comigration of both E1α and E1β with Hsp60 might point to a role of Hsp60 in E1 heterotetramer assembly, given that only E1α was TwinStrep-tagged. Of note, patients with reduced Hsp60 amounts suffer from an impairment of multiple mitochondrial enzymes including PDC [60], highlighting the importance of Hsp60 for PDC.

In this study, we provide evidence for the notion that single amino acid substitutions in the E1α subunit impair PDC through diverse mechanisms including folding impairments as well as global alteration of PDC architecture. All variants analyzed herein resulted in the assembly of PDC that differed in their structural organization and composition (Fig.7b) and showed impaired functional activity. Hence, our results support the idea that architectural changes and impaired folding can be seen alongside lowered catalytic activity of PDC and may thus reflect the varying severity of PDC deficiency in patients.
