# Prevalence and complications of single-gene and chromosomal disorders in craniosynostosis.
**Authors:** Wilkie AO, Byren JC, Hurst JA, Jayamohan J, Johnson D, Knight SJ, Lester T, Richards PG, Twigg SR, Wall SA
**Journal:** Pediatrics (2010)
**DOI:** [10.1542/peds.2009-3491](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2009-3491)

## Content

OBJECTIVES: We describe the first cohort-based analysis of the impact of genetic disorders in craniosynostosis. We aimed to refine the understanding of prognoses and pathogenesis and to provide rational criteria for clinical genetic testing.

METHODS: We undertook targeted molecular genetic and cytogenetic testing for 326 children who required surgery because of craniosynostosis, were born in 1993-2002, presented to a single craniofacial unit, and were monitored until the end of 2007.

RESULTS: Eighty-four children (and 64 relatives) had pathologic genetic alterations (86% single-gene mutations and 14% chromosomal abnormalities). The FGFR3 P250R mutation was the single largest contributor (24%) to the genetic group. Genetic diagnoses accounted for 21% of all craniosynostosis cases and were associated with increased rates of many complications. Children with an initial clinical diagnosis of nonsyndromic craniosynostosis were more likely to have a causative mutation if the synostoses were unicoronal or bicoronal (10 of 48 cases) than if they were sagittal or metopic (0 of 55 cases; P = .0003). Repeat craniofacial surgery was required for 58% of children with single-gene mutations but only 17% of those with chromosomal abnormalities (P = .01).

CONCLUSIONS: Clinical genetic assessment is critical for the treatment of children with craniosynostosis. Genetic testing of nonsyndromic cases (at least for FGFR3 P250R and FGFR2 exons IIIa/c) should be targeted to patients with coronal or multisuture synostoses. Single-gene disorders that disrupt physiologic signaling in the cranial sutures often require reoperation, whereas chromosomal abnormalities follow a more-indolent course, which suggests a different, secondary origin of the associated craniosynostosis.

The skull vault grows at narrow seams of unossified tissue, termed cranial sutures, which separate the individual bones. Craniosynostosis, the premature fusion of one or more cranial sutures, affects one in 2,100-2,500 children1,2 and has diverse presentations and causes. It can involve a single suture (most commonly the sagittal, followed in decreasing frequency by the coronal, metopic and lambdoid sutures) or multiple sutures; can have a mechanical contribution (for example external pressure on the fetal skull as a result of malposition, or reduced internal expansive force from suboptimal signalling of brain growth) or genetic predisposition; and may be isolated (non-syndromic), or associated with additional clinical signs as part of a syndrome.3-5 Although a single corrective surgical procedure is usually sufficient in non-syndromic, single suture synostosis, complex cases are challenging to manage. In the UK a multidisciplinary team approach is used.6 Here we explore the impact that a genetic diagnosis associated with craniosynostosis has on clinical outcome, evaluated at a single unit over a 15-year period starting in 1993.

During this same period, advances in molecular genetics have transformed diagnostic precision in the common craniosynostosis syndromes. Heterozygous mutations in the genes FGFR2 (fibroblast growth factor receptor 2, causing Crouzon, Apert and Pfeiffer syndromes), TWIST1 (twist homolog 1, causing Saethre-Chotzen syndrome) and EFNB1 (ephrin-B1, causing craniofrontonasal syndrome) are characteristic of their respective disorders (clinical features are summarized in Table 5, supporting information),4,5 and in the case of FGFR2 and TWIST1, can occasionally occur in individuals with an apparently non-syndromic clinical presentation.7-9 Most important clinically has been the discovery of a previously poorly delineated condition, associated with a heterozygous P250R mutation encoded by FGFR3 (fibroblast growth factor receptor 3), that is a common cause of coronal synostosis.10-12 This entity (now termed Muenke syndrome), has rather non-specific clinical features, craniosynostosis does not always occur, and the diagnosis can only be confirmed by molecular genetic testing.13

Although there have been previous surveys of the genetic contribution to craniosynostosis,12,14-17 none has comprehensively evaluated a large prospectively ascertained cohort of unselected patients. We had four major aims in undertaking this work. First, to quantify the contribution of identifiable genetic lesions to craniosynostosis; second, to derive objective criteria to guide a cost-efficient strategy for genetic testing of this heterogeneous entity; third, to examine the implications of a genetic diagnosis for management and prognosis; and finally, to gain insights into pathogenesis.

The study was approved by the Oxfordshire Research Ethics Committee B (C02.143) and written informed consent was obtained from the child’s parent or guardian. We included all 326 children born in the years 1993-2002, presenting to Oxford from anywhere in the UK, who underwent at least one major craniofacial surgical procedure (at Oxford or elsewhere) for craniosynostosis. The diagnosis was confirmed by either standard or 3-dimensional computed tomography of the skull. From January 1995 all procedures were undertaken according to uniform surgical protocols by a single team; 22 children (6.7%) had their first surgical procedure elsewhere (more information on surgical protocols18-20 is provided in Additional Methods, supporting information). Major craniofacial procedures included all transcranial surgery and major subcranial osteotomies (Le Fort III), but not ventricular shunting or additional procedures related to surgical complications. The first craniofacial procedure was performed at less than 5 years in 306 (93.9%) cases, indicating that our minimum 5 year follow-up from birth will have captured most children who eventually required surgery.

All children were assessed by a multispecialty team including a clinical geneticist. Most were followed up at least biennially, but 26 were lost to follow-up and 6 died; hence 294 (90.2%) continued to be reviewed at Oxford unit when the study ended (31 December 2007). Medical records were examined for significant events (Additional Methods). The average duration of follow-up from the first craniofacial procedure was 7.04±3·30 (SD) years.

Blood was obtained from 244 children for genetic analysis, including G-banded karyotype and/or DNA extraction. Molecular genetic testing included previously documented mutation hotspots16 in the FGFR1, FGFR2, FGFR3, TWIST1, EFNB1 and several additional genes, and array comparative genomic hybridization (CGH) in selected individuals (Additional Methods).

We developed two diagnostic classifications, one based on initial diagnosis (before genetic testing), the other on final diagnosis (after genetic testing). Children in whom a genetic diagnosis was considered to be readily apparent clinically (based either on the referral diagnosis, or after assessment of the family history and phenotype at Oxford) were excluded from analysis of the diagnostic utility of genetic testing; in these cases the proposed diagnosis was confirmed by appropriate molecular or cytogenetic testing. After all testing had been completed, children were classified into three major categories of final diagnosis: (1) proven genetic, where a causative chromosome abnormality or pathogenic single gene mutation was identified; (2) other syndrome, where additional malformations and/or a developmental quotient <70 (without obvious secondary cause) and/or a positive family history of craniosynostosis were present, but no specific gene mutation, chromosome abnormality or other predisposition (such as prenatal exposure or extreme prematurity) was identified; and (3) non-syndromic, where neither (1) nor (2) was applicable. The relationship between the initial and final diagnoses is summarized in Fig 4, supporting information.

Statistical testing employed χ2 tests for data on annual case recruitment, heteroscedastic t-tests (2-tailed) for comparison of surgical outcomes and Fisher’s exact tests (2-tailed) for comparison of 2×2 numerical data.

Referral Rates and Genetic Classification of Craniosynostosis in the Cohort The cohort comprises 326 children with radiologically proven craniosynostosis. These were classified (after genetic analysis) into proven genetic (n = 84, including 3 sib pairs), other syndrome (n = 49, including 5 sib pairs) and non-syndromic (n = 193) categories, as defined above. In the last category, a predisposing factor was identified in 20 children (see Additional Methods).

The number of children in each birth year by final diagnosis is shown in Fig 1. Whereas there was no long-term change in the annual number of referrals in the proven genetic and other syndrome categories, the non-syndromic cases showed a marked step-change increase (P<0.001) in children born in 1998 onwards. This coincided with a revised definition of the craniofacial surgery service (operative from 1 April 1998), which stated that the designated craniofacial units were funded for the assessment of all patients having a suspected congenital craniofacial condition;21 hence, the lower number of non-syndromic cases prior to 1998 is likely to reflect under-referral (confirmed by known changes in referral criteria used by specific surgical colleagues). To estimate the proportions of children in the three major diagnostic categories, and those with different non-syndromic synostoses, we used only data for those born 1998-2002 (n = 215; Fig 2A and Table 6, supporting information). The relative prevalence of different single suture synostoses, including marked sex biases in presentation, broadly accords with previous findings.1,2,22,23 However to sub-classify the proven genetic cases we included children born in all years (Fig 2B), as there was no evidence for a change in these referrals over time (Fig 1 and Table 6).

Twenty-one percent (46/215; 95% CI 16-27%) of children born 1998-2002 had a proven genetic cause for their craniosynostosis (Fig 2A), and, based on all birth years, the major contributions to the proven genetic cases (n = 84) were made by chromosome abnormalities (15%) and mutations in FGFR2 (32%), FGFR3 (25%), TWIST1 (19%) and EFNB1 (7%) (Fig 2B). Single mutations in FAM20C and LMX1B were identified, which represent novel associations with craniosynostosis.24,25 There were no clearly pathogenic mutations in several other genes previously implicated in craniosynostosis (Additional Methods), including FGFR1, MSX2, EFNA4 or in the Twist box encoded by TWIST1.

The 13 chromosome abnormalities identified were very diverse (Table 1). The t(7p,8q) translocation was associated with deletion of TWIST1 at 7p21.126 and the deletions of 9p and 11q represent known associations with metopic synostosis.3,5 Multiple suture synostosis in the child with trisomy 6p21→pter might be attributable to excess dosage of the key osteogenic regulator RUNX2.16 Two of the chromosome abnormalities were submicroscopic, being detected by array CGH: both rearrangements (deletion of 17q21.31; duplication of 22q11.21-11.23) have been described previously in multiple individuals, but no previous case has had craniosynostosis.27,28 Molecular analysis of the inv(22), present as a de novo change in two brothers, revealed a more complex rearrangement with at least 4 breaks (C. Babbs, unpublished data). A causal relationship to the craniosynostosis (as a low frequency complication) was judged likely in these last 3 cases, because of the relatively large extent of the rearrangements and associated clinical problems, including learning disability. Overall, synostosis of the midline (metopic/sagittal) sutures predominated in association with chromosome abnormalities (11/13 cases), and was exclusively present in 7 cases. None of the aneuploid chromosomal regions overlapped between cases, indicating marked causal heterogeneity. The frequent association of metopic synostosis with chromosomal abnormalities was demonstrated in a separate study from our unit.29

In contrast to the rarity of individual chromosome abnormalities, some single gene mutations were present in multiple patients (Table 2); of these, the FGFR3 P250R substitution was the most common (24% of the genetic cases, 95% CI 15-34%; and 5.6% of all cases born 1998-2002, 95% CI 2.9-9.6%). In addition to the 20 children with this mutation who had craniosynostosis, a further 5 (counting only those born in 1993-2002) with FGFR3 P250R were first degree relatives of affected individuals, but did not themselves have clinically significant craniosynostosis. Assuming an overall prevalence of craniosynostosis in childhood1 of 1 in 2,100 and 80% penetrance, we estimate from these data that the population prevalence of the FGFR3 P250R mutation is ~1 in 30,000.

To evaluate critically the added diagnostic value provided by genetic testing, we assessed in each case whether the clinical features and/or a family history of a known mutation implied an obvious diagnosis. This was so for 45/84 children with a proven genetic cause (Table 3, footnote a; Fig 4). We then analysed how the remaining 39 children with a positive genetic test result (11 with chromosome abnormalities, 27 with single gene mutations, and 1 with both) had presented (Table 3). All children with chromosome abnormalities had a syndrome, comprising 12/77 (16%) of cases initially placed in this diagnostic group. A further 16/77 (21%) children with a syndrome had a single gene mutation; these included 9 FGFR3 P250R mutations (5 had originally been misdiagnosed as Crouzon or Saethre-Chotzen syndrome and the remainder had a family history of unusual head shapes). Single gene mutations were also present in 11/204 (5.4%) of cases originally classified as having non-syndromic synostosis (Table 3); again FGFR3 P250R was the most common individual mutation (7 cases). In contrast to the predominance of midline suture synostosis in children with chromosome abnormalities, 10/11 of the apparently non-syndromic cases with single gene mutations had unicoronal or bicoronal synostosis (Table 3). Children with non-syndromic unicoronal synostosis and a single gene mutation were more likely than mutation-negative children to require squint correction (4/7 versus 5/35; P=0.005) and hearing aids (2/7 versus 0/35; P= 0.025). The high prevalence of strabismus associated with FGFR2 and FGFR3 mutations was noted previously.30

The data presented in Table 3 may be used to formulate rational criteria for cytogenetic and molecular genetic testing in craniosynostosis. Cytogenetic analysis should clearly be considered for all syndromic cases (although it may be deferred when a single gene mutation is suspected and subsequently confirmed), but no clinically significant chromosome abnormalities were identified in 92 mutation-negative, non-syndromic children known to have been tested; these figures include negative array CGH studies on 14 individuals with sagittal synostosis and 13 with metopic synostosis. Array CGH was performed on 30 syndromic individuals without a known genetic basis, and identified microdeletions judged to be clinically contributory in 2 (6.7%; 95% CI 1-22%), a rate significantly lower (P=0.05) than a recently published figure of 28% for the prevalence of submicroscopic abnormalities in syndromic craniosynostosis of unknown cause.31

Molecular genetic analysis is also indicated in syndromic cases; in non-syndromic cases, all children with sagittal (n = 27), metopic (n = 28) or lambdoid (n = 7) synostosis had negative tests, in contrast to 3/8 (37.5%) bicoronal cases and 7/40 (17.5%) unicoronal cases, in whom a single gene mutation was identified. The majority of the non-syndromic cases with mutations had FGFR3 P250R (n = 7), but mutations in FGFR2 (n = 3) and TWIST1 (n = 1) were also identified. Hence the positive diagnostic yield from molecular genetic testing in non-syndromic synostosis is substantially higher in children with fusion of the coronal suture(s) only, compared to those with fusion of the midline sutures (P=0.0003).

The number of children who had undergone 1, 2, 3 or 4 major craniofacial procedures (as defined above) was 246, 62, 13 and 5, respectively; the timing of these procedures is shown in Fig 5, supporting information. There are several reasons why a child with craniosynostosis might require more than one corrective procedure (Additional Methods). In severe turribracycephaly our operative protocol involves an initial 2-stage procedure comprising posterior release followed by definitive fronto-orbital advancement and remodelling.18,20 Additional unplanned revisions may be necessary because of secondarily raised intracranial pressure or progressive calvarial deformity.18-20 Later on, children with significant midface hypoplasia and exorbitism may require midface advancement,32 and those with craniofrontonasal syndrome may require hypertelorism correction.33

We tabulated the number of major craniofacial procedures that each child had undergone for each completed half-year (up to 5 years) or year of life. Using the average number of procedures as an index of craniofacial severity, Fig 3A shows that the proven genetic cases consistently had a more severe surgical course than the other two groups (significant at P<0.05 from ages 2-7 years). We were interested to determine whether this greater severity was consistent for different underlying genetic diagnoses. Figure 3B shows that children with mutations in FGFR2, FGFR3 (P250R) and TWIST1 all exhibited more severe surgical courses than those without a genetic diagnosis (P<0.05 for each gene from ages 4.5-7 years). By contrast, children with chromosome abnormalities had surgical courses similar to those without a genetic diagnosis, repeat surgery being required in only 17% compared to 58% with a single gene mutation (P=0.01).

In addition to major craniofacial procedures, children may have additional problems related either to the presence of craniosynostosis, or to the underlying syndrome. We tabulated the more common complications and found that, for every complication measured, children with a proven genetic diagnosis fared worse than those in the two other diagnostic categories combined (summarized in Table 4 for those measures reaching statistical significance, see Table 7, supporting information, for detailed data). Requirements for midface procedures, ventricular shunting, and tracheostomy were predominantly associated with FGFR2 mutations, whilst the need for ptosis correction, fitting of hearing aids and upper limb surgery largely represent associations with particular syndromes (Saethre-Chotzen, Muenke and Apert syndromes, respectively). Significant learning disability (developmental quotient <70), present in 21/81 (26%) of the proven genetic cases, was most commonly associated with chromosome abnormalities (8/11) and Apert syndrome (5/9). The six children in the cohort who died (1.8%) all had syndromes: either Pfeiffer syndrome with an identified FGFR2 mutation (n = 2), chromosome abnormality (n = 1) (Tables 1 and 2) or of unknown genetic basis (n = 3). Four deaths (at ages 0.5, 1.2, 2.1 and 3.9 years) were directly attributable to complications of craniosynostosis, which involved multiple sutures in all cases. This included one perioperative death at 0.5 years, in a child with an unbalanced t(6;7) chromosome translocation and uncorrected tetralogy of Fallot, who required emergency intervention for severe raised intracranial pressure.

This series provides the most accurate demographic data available to estimate the relative prevalence of proven genetic causes in craniosynostosis (Fig 2) and demonstrates the substantial additional medical and surgical burden implied by a genetic diagnosis (Fig 3, Table 4). As well as the 84 proven genetic cases, additional mutation positive individuals were identified in 36 families and totalled 64 affected relatives (Tables 1 and 2), indicating the need for extended genetic counselling in many families.

For clinical management, a particularly important category comprises those cases in whom the initial clinical diagnosis was uncertain. By combined cytogenetic and molecular genetic analysis we achieved a new and specific diagnosis in 39/326 (12%) of the cohort (Table 3). About two-fifths (n =16) of these children had just one mutation (FGFR3 P250R) and 7 of these cases presented with apparently non-syndromic coronal craniosynostosis. A further 6 children with uncertain initial diagnoses harboured mutations in FGFR2 (Table 3). The identification of causative mutations is important for clinical management, as it indicates a higher risk for repeat craniofacial procedures (Fig 3),18-20,34,35 as well as having implications for genetic counselling of the extended family, and future strategies for targeted pharmacological inhibition of genetically determined craniosynostosis.36 By contrast the evidence does not support routine testing of children with non-syndromic sagittal, metopic or (with less confidence) lambdoid synostosis (Table 3), which together comprise 48% of cases (Fig 2A). The striking difference in molecular epidemiology of coronal synostosis, compared to synostosis of other cranial sutures, probably reflects the distinctive origin of coronal sutures at the boundary between two different embryonic tissues, the neural crest and the cephalic mesoderm.37,38

Our data provide trajectories for the surgical outcome of different classes of craniosynostosis. Whilst variations occur in the surgical management of children with FGFR2, FGFR3, and TWIST1 mutations (Table 7), all are associated with a greater number of repeat craniofacial procedures at most ages compared to cases without identified genetic cause (Fig 3B). This reflects the important biological roles of these genes in the development and maintenance of the cranial sutures.37 Surprisingly, children with chromosome abnormalities tended to present later and with a different pattern of suture involvement (metopic or sagittal), compared to those with single gene mutations. They rarely required repeat craniofacial surgery (Fig 3B, Table 7), suggesting that intrinsic signalling abnormalities in the cranial sutures were less likely to be present. As the associated abnormal karyotypes are very diverse (Table 1)3,31 and impaired cognitive development is frequent, an alternative possibility in these chromosomal cases is that the craniosynostosis has a biomechanical origin (reduced transduction of stretch forces exerted by the growing brain). We conclude that although a few recurrent chromosome abnormalities [such as deletions in 7p21 (TWIST1), 9p22-p24 and 11q23-q24, and duplication of 5q35 (MSX2)] clearly predispose genetically to craniosynostosis, attempts to identify specific craniosynostosis “genes” at many other putative loci will fail.

Our data also provide insight into the etiology of craniosynostosis in children with other syndromes. This group is highly heterogeneous; amongst proposed diagnoses, only Kabuki syndrome39 was present recurrently (2 cases). The cumulative craniofacial procedures were significantly fewer in the other syndrome group than in the subgroups with identified single gene disorders, and similar overall to the non-syndromic children (Fig 3A,B). This relatively benign surgical course suggests that only a minority have intrinsic abnormalities of biogenesis or signalling in the cranial sutures.

This study highlights the added value that a clinical genetic assessment and, where indicated, cytogenetic and molecular genetic testing, can bring to the management of a child with craniosynostosis – informing both the surgical prognosis and the estimation of recurrence risks – and provides a benchmark for future surgical series. We propose that molecular genetic testing, as a minimum for mutations in FGFR3 (P250R) and FGFR2 exons IIIa and IIIc, should be essential in children with bicoronal, unicoronal or multisuture synostosis. Research aimed at identifying new genes mutated in craniosynostosis requires careful choice of patients, as many with chromosomal abnormalities or other syndromes may have secondary causes.
