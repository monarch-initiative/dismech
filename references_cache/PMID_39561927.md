ultra-rare, progressive genetic disease, characterised by immune deficiency and
dysregulation, affecting individuals from birth. In a 12-week phase III
randomised placebo-controlled trial, leniolisib, a selective PI3Kδ inhibitor,
was well-tolerated and met both co-primary endpoints (change from Baseline in
log10-transformed sum of product of diameters of index lymph nodes and
percentage of naïve/total B cells at Day 85). Here, prespecified subgroup
analyses are reported in adolescents aged 12-17 years (leniolisib, n = 8;
placebo, n = 4) and adults aged ≥18 (leniolisib, n = 13; placebo, n = 6). In

increased the percentage of naïve B cells (least squares mean change:
adolescents, 44.5 versus -16.5; adults, 28.4 versus -1.1). Leniolisib was
well-tolerated in both adolescents and adults. These results show leniolisib is

Activated phosphoinositide 3-kinase delta (PI3Kδ) syndrome (APDS) is an ultra-rare inborn error of immunity (IEI), characterised by early hallmarks of both lymphoproliferation and immune dysfunction.1 It is a genetic disease, which arises from hyperactive signalling within the PI3Kδ pathway due to pathogenic variants in the genes encoding the p110δ and p85α PI3Kδ subunits: the PIK3CD (APDS1) and PIK3R1 (APDS2) genes, respectively.1, 2 The PI3Kδ enzyme complex is a central component in regulating a wide range of fundamental cellular processes, and is primarily expressed in lymphocytes.1 Therefore, hyperactive PI3Kδ signalling can result in a broad range of symptoms experienced by individuals with APDS, which can lead to irreversible end organ damage and early mortality as the disease progresses.3, 4

Immune dysregulation in APDS is reflected by abnormal lymphocyte counts, with common identifying features being a reduction in naïve and memory B cells, increase in transitional B cells and an inverted CD4+/CD8+ T cell ratio.5–8 Furthermore, impairments in B cell immunoglobulin (Ig) class switching can result in reduced production of higher affinity antibodies such as IgG and IgA and elevated levels of lower affinity IgM, impairing the adaptive immune response.1, 9–11 Immune dysregulation can in turn lead to lymphoproliferation, manifesting as lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly and/or hepatomegaly, which have been observed in 70–89% of people with APDS.5, 6, 9 Individuals with APDS also have increased susceptibility to malignancies such as lymphoma, reported in 8–25% of patients.5, 6, 9 This underlying dysregulation of the immune system can affect individuals from an early age, with progressive symptoms developing from early childhood, leading to an accumulation of severe and recurrent infections, autoimmune complications, and gastrointestinal (GI) tract symptoms up to and throughout adulthood.5 Moreover, the wide range of debilitating symptoms in APDS can have a substantial impact on an individual’s quality of life.12–15

Currently, the range of treatments available is limited. These include supportive therapy (e.g. Ig replacement therapy [IRT], B cell-depleting molecules such as rituximab, and antimicrobials), surgical interventions (e.g. reduction of lymphadenopathy) and unlicensed empirical immunomodulatory therapies (e.g. mammalian target of rapamycin [mTOR] inhibitors).5, 12, 15, 16 Although these treatments may alleviate some symptoms of APDS, none of them target all symptoms, or the underlying cause of disease, and some treatments can be associated with severe adverse events (AEs).16 Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) may be curative for some people with APDS, however, this is not the treatment of choice for the majority of affected individuals, due to multiple potential side effects.13, 16 With early diagnosis and intervention being paramount in individuals with APDS, due to the progressive nature of the disease, there is a clear need for effective licensed therapies, with minimal safety concerns, to enable the treatment of younger individuals with APDS.17

Leniolisib is a novel, oral, small molecular inhibitor which selectively targets PI3Kδ, reducing hyperactive signalling of the enzyme.18, 19 In a phase III, 12-week, triple-blinded, placebo-controlled, randomised controlled trial (RCT), 31 participants aged 12–54 years from the United States (US), Europe and Russia received 70 mg of leniolisib twice daily.19 Leniolisib was well-tolerated and met both co-primary efficacy endpoints, correcting immune dysregulation as measured by increasing the percentage of naïve B cells out of total B cells, and reducing lymphoproliferation as measured by a reduction in lymphadenopathy compared with placebo.19 The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the United Kingdom Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) and Israel Institute for Standardisation and Control of Pharmaceuticals subsequently approved leniolisib as the first treatment for APDS in individuals ≥12 years of age.20–22 Furthermore, an ongoing open-label extension (OLE) study has suggested that leniolisib treatment for individuals with APDS has long-term tolerability and durable clinical impacts.8

The median age at diagnosis for APDS is 12 years, with a median diagnostic delay of 7 years.9 Adolescence is a critical period for disease progression and intervention, as virtually all patients will experience respiratory infections and lymphoproliferative disease upon reaching adulthood.3, 9 Considering this alongside the progressive nature of APDS and accumulation of active symptoms with age, it may be expected that providing treatments earlier in adolescence might provide additional benefits compared with treatment during adulthood.17, 23 Early treatment may aid in decreasing long-term sequelae and irreversible organ damage, with this being the cornerstone of good practice.17, 23 Therefore, understanding whether the efficacy and safety profiles of leniolisib are consistent in younger patients is key.

Here, we compared leniolisib versus placebo in adolescent and adult subgroups across key efficacy and safety endpoints of the first phase III RCT in APDS. This pre-specified subgroup analysis will assess the impacts, if any, of age on the effects of leniolisib.

Full details of the design of the 12-week, phase III RCT assessing the efficacy and safety of leniolisib 70 mg twice daily versus placebo (NCT02435173) have been published previously.19 A pre-specified subgroup analysis split by age was conducted for patients enrolled in the RCT, to examine treatment effects of leniolisib in adolescent and adult populations.

Patients aged 12–75 years with pathogenic variants in PIK3CD or PIK3R1 and ≥1 measurable lymph node on computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging scan were eligible for RCT enrolment.19 Full eligibility criteria have been reported previously.19 For this pre-specified subgroup analysis, participants were grouped into adolescents (aged 12–17 years) and adults (≥18 years).

A washout period for antiproliferative, immunosuppressive agents, including rapamycin, and B cell-depletion therapies, lasting from six weeks to six months, depending on the treatment in question, was performed prior to RCT entry.19 Glucocorticoid doses equivalent to ≤25 mg/day of prednisone were allowed within two weeks before first dose of study treatment and throughout the study.19

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics were recorded a day before study treatment was initiated, and participant treatment response to leniolisib was compared from Baseline up to Day 85.19 To observe the effect of leniolisib versus placebo in correcting immune system dysregulation, the first co-primary endpoint of the RCT, change from Baseline in the proportion of naïve B cells out of total B cells, was recorded at Day 85 in adolescents and adults with a reduced percentage of naïve B cells at Baseline (<48% naïve B cells out of total B cells).24 A sensitivity analysis was also performed, including patients irrespective of percentage of naïve B cells at Baseline. The effects of leniolisib on the immune profiles of the adolescent and adult subgroups was further explored through observing change from Baseline to Day 85 in various B and T cell populations (cell lineage markers used for identification are defined in Supplementary Table 1), alongside serum IgM levels.

The other co-primary endpoint was change from Baseline to Day 85 in the log10-transformed sum of product of diameters (SPD) of up to six of the participant’s largest lymph nodes, defined as index lymph nodes, to assess whether leniolisib treatment reduced lymphoproliferation in APDS patients. Lymph nodes were measured by magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography scan and selected per Cheson criteria.25 Further secondary endpoints were change from Baseline to Day 85 in the log10-transformed SPD of non-index lymph nodes and the three-dimensional (3D) volume of the spleen. Lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly “response” parameters were also assessed on an individual patient basis (Supplementary Methods).

The absorption phase of leniolisib was assessed after first dose over an 8-hour period, using geometric mean (CV%) maximum concentration (Cmax), and over the course of the study, on Day 29, 57 and 85 using steady-state trough concentrations following multiple doses of leniolisib.

Safety and tolerability were also assessed, reported as AEs recorded during and up to 30 days after the end of the trial. Common AEs (reported in ≥2 patients overall within the trial), sinopulmonary infection AEs, AEs suspected to be related to study drug, serious AEs, AEs leading to discontinuation, and AEs by severity (Grade 1–5), according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, were also reported.26

Full details of the statistical analyses in the RCT have been published previously.19 Efficacy endpoints are reported for the pharmacodynamic (PD) analysis set, which included patients who received any study drug and experienced no protocol deviations that would impact PD data. Change from Baseline to Day 85 for percentage of naïve B cells out of total B cells, log10-transformed SPD of index and non-index lymph nodes and spleen 3D volume are presented using least squares (LS) means, for which data were analysed using an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model with treatment as a fixed effect and Baseline value as a covariate. The use of glucocorticoids and IRT at Baseline were both included as categorical (Yes/No) covariates.

Mean change from Baseline to Day 85 is presented for other efficacy endpoints. Individual patient values for change from Baseline to Day 85 are also presented for naïve B cells, as a proportion of total B cells (all patients with available data), SPD of index lymph nodes and 3D spleen volume. Pharmacokinetic (PK) analyses included participants that had at least one PK concentration measurement, received any study drug and had not experienced any protocol deviations that would affect PK data. Safety data are presented for all patients who received at least one dose of study treatment.

A total of 31 participants were enrolled in the RCT, 12 adolescents (12–17 years) and 19 adults (≥18 years). Of these, eight adolescents and 13 adults were randomised to the leniolisib group, and four adolescents and six adults were randomised to placebo. Their Baseline characteristics are summarised in Table 1.

One of the main differences between subgroups was the median weight and height of adolescents (60.0 kg, 160.9 cm) being lower than the adult group (72.1 kg, 167.0 cm), which is expected. At Baseline, glucocorticoids and sirolimus were used more commonly in adolescents (glucocorticoids: 75.0% adolescents and 47.4% adults; sirolimus: 33.3% adolescents and 15.8% adults), whereas IRT use was more common in adults (58.3% adolescents and 73.7% adults) (Table 1). History of asthma and lymphoproliferation were more common in adolescents, history of pneumonia was more common in adults and history of bronchiectasis was similar between subgroups.

The LS mean change from Baseline to Day 85 in percentage of naïve B cells out of total B cells (in participants with <48% naïve B cells at Baseline) overall was 37.3% greater in leniolisib-treated participants compared with placebo (p=0.0002, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 24.1%, 50.5%) (Figure 1a).19 In adults, the difference was 29.5% in favour of leniolisib (95% CI: 16.0%, 43.0%) and in adolescents, the difference was 61.1% in favour of leniolisib (95% CI: 12.6%, 109.5%).

When including all patients, irrespective of percentage of naïve B cells at Baseline, overall LS mean change from Baseline was also greater for leniolisib compared to placebo (Supplementary Figure 1). Both the adolescent and adult subgroups showed a similar trend, with LS mean change from Baseline being greater for leniolisib than placebo. The percent changes from Baseline at Day 85 in the proportion of naïve B cells out of total B cells for individual patients (all patients irrespective of naïve B cells at Baseline) are shown in Figure 1b.

The results of exploratory post hoc analyses, exploring changes in other immune cell subsets with leniolisib versus placebo in adolescent and adult subgroups are presented in Table 2. Overall, leniolisib tended to promote favourable changes in the immune profiles of both adolescent and adult participants with APDS, compared with placebo.

Mean serum IgM levels showed a greater decrease from Baseline to Day 85 in leniolisib-treated patients versus placebo among both adolescents and adults (Supplementary Figure 2).

Leniolisib reduced lymphadenopathy in the overall population, meeting the second co-primary endpoint, with the difference in LS mean change from Baseline in log10-transformed SPD of index lymph nodes between leniolisib and placebo being −0.3 (reductions indicate improvement) (p=0.0006, 95% CI: −0.4, −0.1).19 For adolescents, the difference in LS mean change from Baseline in log10-transformed SPD of index lymph nodes between leniolisib and placebo was −0.3 (95% CI: −0.6, 0.0). For adults, the difference was also in favour of leniolisib at −0.3 (95% CI: −0.5, −0.1) (Figure 2a). The percent changes from Baseline at Day 85 in SPD of index lymph nodes for individual patients are presented in Figure 2b.

In post hoc responder analyses, higher proportions of leniolisib-treated adolescents and adults achieved a ≥30% reduction and ≥50% reduction in the SPD of their index lymph nodes from Baseline to Day 85, compared with those treated with placebo (Supplementary Figure 3a).

When analysing the LS mean change from Baseline to Day 85 in spleen 3D volume, the difference between leniolisib and placebo treatment showed a similar trend, favouring leniolisib overall (−186.4 cm3, p=0.0020, 95% CI: −296.5, −76.2), for adolescents (−104.2 cm3, 95% CI: −338.7, 130.2) and for adults (−259.1 cm3, 95% CI: −429.5, −88.6) (reductions indicate improvement) (Figure 2c). The percent changes from Baseline to Day 85 in spleen 3D volume for individual patients are presented in Figure 2d.

In a post hoc responder analysis, a higher proportion of adolescents in the placebo-treated group achieved a ≥35% reduction in spleen 3D volume compared to the leniolisib-treated group (Supplementary Figure 3b). On the other hand, a higher proportion of leniolisib-treated adults achieved a ≥35% reduction compared to placebo-treated adults.

Lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly response parameters assessed the presence or absence of lesion enlargement and splenomegaly. Larger percentages of leniolisib-treated adolescents and adults achieved complete or partial response in their index lymph nodes and spleen compared with placebo (Supplementary Table 2).

The LS mean change from Baseline to Day 85 in the sum of the log10-transformed 3D volumes of non-index lymph nodes was also assessed. Adolescents (–2.3, 95% CI: – 4.5, –0.1) and adults (–1.7, 95% CI: −3.5, 0.0) showed a difference in favour of leniolisib (Supplementary Figure 4). Lymphadenopathy response parameters were also assessed in non-index lymph nodes. A larger proportion of leniolisib-treated adolescents and adults achieved complete or partial response compared to placebo-treated adults (Supplementary Table 2).

Together, these data suggest that leniolisib reduces lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly in both adolescent and adult subgroups.

Following a single dose of leniolisib 70 mg, both adolescents and adults demonstrated a similar absorption phase, characterised by geometric mean (CV%) Cmax of 2,070 ng/mL (25%) and 2,090 ng/mL (27%) respectively; however, adolescents reached this slower, at a median Tmax of 3.0 hours, compared to 1.2 hours in adults (Figure 3). No food restrictions were in place in the study. After multiple doses of leniolisib, both subgroups showed similar trends in arithmetic mean trough leniolisib plasma concentrations (Figure 3).

The safety profile of leniolisib was similar in adolescents and adults, with similar rates of study drug-related AEs for both subgroups (Table 3). AEs were reported by 75.0% of leniolisib- and 100.0% of placebo-treated adolescents. Within the adult subpopulation, 92.3% leniolisib- and 83.3% placebo-treated participants reported AEs. Most AEs experienced by adolescents and adults were between Grade 1 and 3 (mild to severe); only two adult patients in the leniolisib treatment group experienced Grade 4 (life-threatening/disabling) AEs.

Study drug-related AEs were reported by 25.0% of leniolisib- and 50.0% of placebo-treated adolescents and 23.1% of leniolisib- and 16.7% of placebo-treated adults.

In total, 12.5% of adolescents and 15.4% of adults in the leniolisib-treated group reported serious AEs, however, none of the serious AEs in either subgroup were related to study drug. All serious AEs are reported in Supplementary Table 3. Furthermore, no AEs led to study discontinuation and no deaths were reported within 30 days of the end of the trial (Table 3).

The most common AEs are reported in Supplementary Table 3. For leniolisib-treated adolescents, the most common AE was sinusitis (three participants, 37.5%), and in adults it was headaches (four participants, 30.8%). Leniolisib-related AEs reported by adolescents included alopecia (two participants, 25.0%) and increased weight (one participant, 12.5%), and for adults, aphthous ulcer, taste disorder and vomiting (one participant, 7.7%, for each).

When considering AEs that have been associated with other PI3Kδ inhibitors within an oncology setting, both adolescent and adult leniolisib-treated groups reported a higher frequency of transient low neutrophil counts compared with placebo. More leniolisib-treated adults (38.5%) experienced GI AEs compared to adolescents (12.5%), and neither subgroup reported pneumonitis (Supplementary Table 4).

In patients with APDS, 70 mg of leniolisib given orally twice a day over a 12-week period demonstrated clinical benefit over placebo in both adolescents and adults with respect to both coprimary endpoints, increasing naïve/total B cell proportions and reducing index lymph node size at Day 85. Similar trends were seen in the secondary and exploratory endpoints, including normalisation of B and T cell immune profiles, alongside reductions in sizes of the spleen and non-index lymph nodes, with leniolisib for both adolescents and adults.

As APDS is a rare disease with an estimated worldwide prevalence of 1–2 per 1,000,000 people, clinical trials investigating new treatments have challenges regarding patient numbers.23 Given the small number of adolescent participants, it is difficult to draw strong statistical conclusions for this subgroup, which in turn explains the wide 95% CIs. The small size of the adolescent cohort may explain why a higher proportion of adolescents in the placebo group achieved ≥35% reduction in spleen 3D volume compared to the leniolisib group, with only one participant from each treatment group achieving ≥35% reduction. This emphasises the importance in observing both overall data trends and individual patient data to understand the effect of leniolisib treatment in adolescents. In this analysis, individual patient data supported the trends observed in the summary data in both groups, strengthening the reliability of the results when using smaller patient cohorts. Future studies using larger patient cohorts may be required to confirm these trends.

Individuals with APDS may be immunocompromised, as PI3Kδ is expressed predominantly in lymphocytes resulting in dysregulation of the immune system, primarily in B and T cell subsets, which can lead to lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly.1, 13 Given the progressive nature of APDS and the expected benefit of early treatment, understanding the effects of leniolisib treatment on the immune systems of both adolescents and adults is key. Here, leniolisib treatment improved the profiles of immune cells and reduced lymphoproliferation compared with placebo in both adolescent and adult subgroups, indicating that leniolisib may improve trafficking, development and maintenance of B and T cells.19 In turn, this suggests an improved defence against pathogens, which may reduce the recurrence of infections commonly seen in APDS and therefore subsequently reduce symptoms such as coughing, congestion, muscle aches and high temperatures.5, 12, 19, 27

APDS is a progressive disease, with the proportion of patients affected by infectious, lymphoproliferative and gastrointestinal manifestations and cytopenias increasing with age.3 The characteristics of the overall group of participants included in this study are representative of the general APDS population. However, the characteristics of the adolescent and adult subgroups reported here may be more homogenous than in an unselected population, which may contain a higher frequency of APDS manifestations in adults compared to younger patients. This is likely due to the trial inclusion criteria, stating that participants must have at least one measurable lymph node and clinical findings consistent with APDS, and the exclusion criteria, which prevent individuals who are not able to undergo washout periods or receive limited immunosuppressant medications from participating. Combined, these factors may potentially exclude non-severe and severe APDS patients from this study, including patients with clinically significant autoimmune cytopenias.8 In line with this consideration, similar proportions of participants had a history of bronchiectasis in both age groups in this study, indicating accumulation of end-organ damage prior to adolescence. This is consistent with contemporary data from the European Society for Immunodeficiencies registry, reporting median age of bronchiectasis onset of 7 years.3 Differences in prior and baseline medication use were apparent between age groups, including more frequent prior use of sirolimus, higher Baseline use of glucocorticoids and lower use of IRT in adolescents compared to adults. Importantly, any imbalances in prior sirolimus use between groups are anticipated to have been mitigated by the protocol-mandated 6-week washout for sirolimus, prior to starting randomised treatment.19 As would be expected, the adult subgroup had a higher median weight than the adolescent subgroup. Age and increased adipose weight can result in important PK and PD changes, such as reduced renal and hepatic clearance in older individuals and reduced drug clearance in individuals with a larger adipose body weight.28, 29 Despite the weight difference, all participants received the same dose of leniolisib and observed similar absorption rates, although median Tmax was quicker in adults compared to adolescents. However, by three hours post-exposure, plasma concentrations were similar between subgroups. These results suggest that variation in age and weight did not greatly influence leniolisib absorption rates in this study and demonstrates that a similar level of leniolisib exposure was experienced by both adolescents and adults, with no implications for leniolisib dose in either subgroup.

Current treatments do not specifically target the underlying pathophysiology of APDS, they are only able to reduce certain symptoms, and can be associated with multiple side effects.6, 13, 23, 30 IRT provides benefits for many patients but is unable to prevent the development of lymphoproliferative complications and, in some patients, the progression to bronchiectasis or lymphoma.16 Rituximab treatment is often complicated by sustained B cell lymphopenia, and sirolimus treatment, although effective in decreasing non-neoplastic lymphoproliferation, is less effective in treating APDS-related cytopenias and GI disease.16 Meanwhile, HSCT is associated with side effects such as graft-versus-host disease, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia and encephalitis.16

Leniolisib, as a selective PI3Kδ inhibitor, has the potential to offer a targeted treatment option with fewer side effects, compared with other PI3K inhibitors.16, 31 Early treatment with leniolisib may reduce the need for these other therapies, and in turn the incidence of related side effects among individuals with APDS. Here, leniolisib was well-tolerated in both adolescent and adult subgroups, with comparable rates of AEs in both leniolisib-treated groups, and with no AEs leading to discontinuation or serious AEs related to leniolisib. Study drug-related AEs were also only experienced in a low percentage of adolescents and adults treated with leniolisib; study drug-related transient alopecia AEs were reported in 2 patients.8 Low neutrophil counts (0.5–<1.0 × 109/L) at a single visit within the 12-week trial were observed in three (37.5%) adolescents and one (7.7%) adult in the leniolisib-treated subgroup. Neutrophil counts increased back above 1.5 × 109/L within 2–6 weeks in all participants. PI3K inhibitors have previously been associated with AEs which have limited their clinical uptake,31 and therefore understanding the tolerability of leniolisib in both adolescent and adult subgroups is key to enabling confident treatment of individuals with APDS from an early age to prevent accumulation of symptoms and reduce the potential for irreversible organ damage. There were no leniolisib discontinuations due to AEs; this contrasts with other PI3K inhibitors such as copanlisib, alpelisib, idelalisib, duvelisib and umbralisib, used in clinical trials for patients with haematological malignancies where rates of AE-related discontinuations ranged from 15–35%. These trials, however, had larger patient cohorts and longer treatment duration.31 Future data from the OLE will provide further evidence for the long-term safety of leniolisib in adolescent and adult patients.8

The limitations of this phase III trial have been discussed previously by Rao et al.19 Leniolisib being administered over only a 12-week period reduced the ability to assess other longer-term clinically meaningful measures, such as infection frequency, hospitalisation, antimicrobial use and continued need for IRT, in comparison to placebo. The 12-week period was selected for this study for ethical reasons, to reduce the amount of time that participants were receiving placebo. With the washout period for antiproliferative, immunosuppressive or B cell-depletion therapies prior to the trial lasting from six weeks to six months, individuals in the placebo group may have experienced significant periods with no treatment. The long-term effects of leniolisib are being evaluated in the ongoing OLE study. At a 2.0-year interim analysis, leniolisib continued to be well-tolerated and maintained durable efficacy.8 Within this interim OLE analysis, IRT usage had decreased in 37% of leniolisib-treated participants who were receiving IRT at the time of screening, with six participants stopping using IRT, and four of these six being IRT-free for 1–2.5 years as of the data cut-off date.8 Additionally, there was a statistically significant decrease in annualised infection rates with each additional year of leniolisib treatment.8 However, these published data sets from the OLE do not provide a comparative analysis between adolescent and adult subpopulations; further long-term analyses in adolescent and adult subpopulations may be of interest.

Finally, individuals with APDS typically begin showing symptoms at a median age of 1.7 years.9 Treatment of younger children may be beneficial, and therefore, ongoing paediatric studies are investigating leniolisib safety and efficacy in children with APDS aged 1–6 (NCT05693129) and 4–11 years (NCT05438407).32, 33

In this pre-specified subgroup analysis of a phase III RCT, the clinical benefits of leniolisib were observed in both co-primary endpoints in both adolescents and adults with APDS, with reductions in lymphoproliferation and immunophenotype correction. With leniolisib being well-tolerated by both groups, these data show that 70 mg of leniolisib twice daily is an effective treatment for APDS in both subpopulations. These findings are important due to the progressive nature of the condition and, therefore, the requirement for early treatment. Trials for leniolisib in younger children with APDS (1–11 years of age) are currently ongoing and recruiting.
