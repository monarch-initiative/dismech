# Breast cancer in gene mutation carriers: Does family history matter?
**Authors:** Sporn M, Sharma AR, Jain K, Wu J, Bicasan IJ, DiMaggio C, Guth A, Hiotis K, Shapiro R, Axelrod D, Gemignani ML, Schnabel FR
**Journal:** Cancer Genet (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.cancergen.2026.10.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cancergen.2026.10.007)

## Content

BACKGROUND/OBJECTIVE: As genetic testing becomes routine, more individuals carrying pathogenic variants in breast cancer susceptibility genes in the absence of suggestive family history have been identified. Mutations in BRCA 1, BRCA 2, ATM, CHEK2, and PALB2 account for approximately 7-10% of breast cancers. Our aim was to identify differences in demographic factors, tumor characteristics, and surgical treatment between mutation carriers with and without a reported first-degree affected relative.

METHODS: We retrospectively reviewed our Institution's IRB approved breast cancer database to identify patients enrolled between 1/2010 and 8/2025 who tested positive for BRCA1, BRCA2, ATM, CHEK2, or PALB2. Patients with prior breast cancer were excluded. Demographic, clinical, tumor, and surgical data were collected. Continuous variables were compared using t-tests and categorical variables using chi-square tests.

RESULTS: Among 199 mutation carriers with breast cancer, 128 (64%) reported no first-degree affected relative (N1D) and 71 (36%) did (Y1D). At diagnosis, N1D patients were younger (p=0.001), more often presented with a palpable mass (p=0.003) and had higher rates of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) (p= 0.023). Mutation distribution did not differ, nor did overall histology, although N1D patients trended to have more invasive disease. Surgery type did not differ between groups (p=0.607). 55% of N1D patients had an affected second-degree relative.

CONCLUSION: N1D patients presented at a younger age and were more likely to have clinically apparent invasive disease, along with higher rates of NAC. These findings likely reflect differences in screening rather than more aggressive tumor biology, as receptor subtype, triple-negative rates, and tumor grade were comparable across groups. Despite these differences, surgical management was similar, suggesting that operative planning is influenced more by genetic risk rather than by family history alone. Many N1D patients had an affected second-degree relative, indicating the need for comprehensive evaluation of family history. These findings support expanding genetic testing beyond family-based criteria to better identify high-risk individuals.
