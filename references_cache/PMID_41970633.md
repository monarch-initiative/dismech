# Quantification of glycosaminoglycans in dried blood spots, and evaluation of its usefulness as a secondary newborn screening test for mucopolysaccharidoses.
**Authors:** Oboshi W, Hirakiyama A, Miura M, Omachi H, Tanaka M, Seo JH, Ohtake A, Osawa Y, Ishige M, Kosuga M, Munenaga T, Migita O, Nyuzuki H, Sasai H, Fukuda T, Sano S, Ito T, Bo R, Takeshima Y, Esaki H, Inoue T, Nagamitsu S, Watanabe Y, Nakamura K, Sawada H, Maruyama S, Murayama K, Okuyama T
**Journal:** Biochem Biophys Rep (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.bbrep.2026.102569](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bbrep.2026.102569)

## Content

Mucopolysaccharidoses (MPS) are a group of lysosomal disorders characterized by pathological accumulation of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs). Enzyme-based newborn screening (NBS) for MPS often yields high false-positive rates because of carriers and pseudodeficient newborns. To improve screening specificity, we developed an LC-MS/MS-based enzymatic method to quantify GAG-derived disaccharides in dried blood spots (DBSs) and evaluated its utility as a second-tier test. Using DBS samples MPS-positive newborns, we quantified dermatan sulfate- (DS), heparan sulfate- (HS), and keratan sulfate-derived disaccharides. Under the primary positivity rule (DS or HS ≥ the 95th percentile cutoff), the method achieved 100% sensitivity and specificity for MPS I, completely eliminating false-positive results among MPS I carriers. For MPS II, the same rule yielded 100% sensitivity and 84.3% specificity. Reanalysis using a stricter rule requiring both DS and HS to exceed the cutoff improved specificity for MPS II to 100% without loss of sensitivity (100%). All confirmed MPS I and II patients exceeded both DS and HS cutoffs, whereas HS-only values above the cutoff occurred exclusively in MPS II pseudodeficiency, reflecting analytical and cutoff-related overlap rather than pathological GAG accumulation. Based on these findings, we recommend the stricter dual-marker rule for laboratory implementation, particularly for improving specificity in MPS II. Implementation of this approach as a second-tier test may substantially reduce false-positive referrals, eliminating all false positives for MPS I carriers and improving specificity for MPS II pseudodeficiency from 84.3% to 100%. This method may serve as a practical complementary screening tool within expanded NBS programs.

Mucopolysaccharidoses (MPS) are a family of disorders caused by deficiencies of specific lysosomal enzymes involved in glycosaminoglycan (GAG) degradation, resulting in progressive accumulation of GAGs within cells and tissues throughout the body [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. MPS comprises multiple distinct disorders, each defined by a specific enzyme deficiency and a characteristic pattern of GAG storage. The clinical manifestations are heterogeneous and type dependent and may include skeletal abnormalities, cardiopulmonary involvement, hepatosplenomegaly, and, in some subtypes, neurological impairment [8].

In Japan, newborn screening (NBS) is conducted as a publicly funded municipal program, and screening for lysosomal storage disorders, including MPS, is implemented through various expanded NBS initiatives. Primary NBS for MPS is based on measurement of lysosomal enzyme activities using dried blood spots (DBSs), most commonly by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) or fluorometric methods [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Newborns with markedly reduced enzyme activity are classified as screen positive. However, most infants identified by enzyme-based screening do not have clinical MPS but are carriers or pseudodeficient individuals, resulting in high false-positive rates, particularly for MPS I and MPS II [13].

Infants who screen positive are referred for confirmatory testing, which may include repeat enzyme assays, urinary GAG analysis, and molecular genetic testing. Although modern mass spectrometry-based methods enable quantification of urinary GAGs using small sample volumes, access to these assays and their implementation vary among screening laboratories. Consequently, follow-up testing can impose substantial logistical, psychological, and economic burdens on families and healthcare systems, underscoring the need for more specific second-tier screening strategies that can be performed directly on residual DBS samples.

Abnormal accumulation of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) is a fundamental biochemical feature of MPS. GAGs are linear polysaccharides composed of repeating disaccharide units consisting of an amino sugar and a uronic acid or galactose and are classified as dermatan sulfate (DS), heparan sulfate (HS), keratan sulfate (KS), chondroitin sulfate (CS), and hyaluronan (HA) [[14], [15], [16], [17]].

Historically, various analytical approaches have been developed to measure GAGs, including thin-layer chromatography, gas chromatography, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays. These methods played important roles in the biochemical characterization and diagnosis of MPS but were limited by sensitivity, specificity, and sample volume requirements [[18], [19], [20], [21]].

More recently, LC–MS/MS-based methods have become the dominant analytical platforms for GAG analysis in both clinical diagnostics and newborn screening applications because of their high sensitivity, specificity, and capacity to quantify multiple GAG-derived analytes using small sample volumes [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. In addition to enzymatic digestion and butanolysis approaches, methanolysis-based LC–MS/MS methods using stable-isotope dilution have also been developed and applied to GAG quantification [[26], [27], [28], [29]]. These methods allow sensitive measurement of specific GAG-derived species, although they differ in analyte coverage and suitability for DBS workflows.

Among these approaches, analysis of endogenous non-reducing end (NRE) glycosaminoglycan fragments in dried blood spots by mass spectrometry has emerged as a highly specific second-tier strategy for newborn screening of MPS [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that disease-specific NRE GAG markers effectively discriminate affected infants from carriers and pseudodeficient cases, achieving near-complete elimination of false-positive results in MPS I, MPS II, and other MPS subtypes [[30], [31], [32]]. Accordingly, NRE-based assays are increasingly regarded as the current and future gold standard for second-tier newborn screening for MPS [33].

In parallel with NRE-based methods, enzymatic digestion of accumulated GAGs into characteristic disaccharide units followed by LC–MS/MS quantification has been explored as an alternative analytical strategy. This disaccharide-based approach does not rely on detection of NRE fragments but instead measures GAG-derived disaccharides generated by specific lyases. Although this method has been shown to detect pathological GAG accumulation in patients with MPS, its analytical performance, particularly with respect to specificity in MPS II, has been reported to be inferior to that of NRE-based assays [34].

Despite these limitations, enzymatic disaccharide-based GAG quantification may remain relevant in expanded NBS settings where implementation of NRE-based methods is constrained by analytical platform availability, technical complexity, or laboratory readiness. Because all MPS subtypes currently targeted by expanded NBS programs are characterized by accumulation of DS, HS, and/or KS, quantification of these GAG species in DBSs may still provide clinically useful information as a pragmatic or transitional second-tier screening option.

In the present study, we developed and evaluated an LC–MS/MS-based enzymatic method for quantifying DS-, HS-, and KS-derived disaccharides in DBS samples. Using DBSs obtained from newborns who screened positive for MPS in expanded NBS programs in Japan, we assessed the ability of this approach to distinguish true MPS patients from carriers and pseudodeficient newborns. The aim of this work was not to supersede NRE-based assays but to evaluate the practical utility and limitations of enzymatic GAG disaccharide quantification as a complementary or interim second-tier screening strategy within current newborn screening infrastructures.

The study was conducted with approval from the Institutional Review Board of Saitama Medical University Hospital (approval number 2022-091). Samples were collected after obtaining opt-out consent or written informed consent from the parents or legal guardians of the newborns.

DBS samples were obtained from (i) 131 newborn controls who screened negative in expanded NBS; (ii) 13 MPS I carriers and 51 MPS II pseudodeficient newborns who screened false positive; and (iii) 3 MPS I patients, 8 MPS II patients, 1 MPS IVA patient, and 1 MPS VI patient who screened true positive and were diagnosed with MPS. The diagnosis of MPS was confirmed based on a combination of enzyme activity assays, molecular genetic testing, urinary GAG analysis, and clinical findings.

A single 3.2-mm disk punched from a DBS was placed into a 0.5-mL deep-well microplate (TreffLab, Degersheim, Switzerland). After addition of 100 μL ultra-pure water (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan), extraction was performed at 400 rpm and 37 °C for 2 h. Twenty microliters of the supernatant was transferred into each of three wells of an Acroprep Advance 96-well filter plate (Pall Corporation, NY, USA), followed by addition of 5 μL Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7) containing 0.01% bovine serum albumin. For disaccharide generation, 5 μL of reaction solution was added to each well: 0.3 μIU/mL chondroitinase B (Iduron, Manchester, UK) for DS analysis, 40 U/mL heparinase (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for HS analysis, or 0.2 mU/mL keratanase II (GlycoSyn, Wellington, New Zealand) for KS analysis. Five microliters of 5 μmol/L chondrosine (Biosynth, Staad, Switzerland) was added as an internal standard. Enzymatic digestion was carried out at 700 rpm and 37 °C for 18 h. To terminate the reaction, samples were frozen at −80 °C for 30 min then thawed. After addition of 80 μL methanol (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), samples were centrifuged at 3000×g and 4 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 80 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a new Acroprep Advance 96-well filter plate and subjected to centrifugal filtration at 2500×g and 4 °C for 15 min to prepare the samples for analysis.

A DS-derived disaccharide (ΔDi-4S), HS-derived disaccharides (ΔDiHS-0S and ΔDiHS-NS), and KS-derived disaccharides (Gal-GlcNAc(6S) and Gal (6S)-GlcNAc(6S)) generated by enzymatic digestion were analyzed. GAG-derived disaccharides were quantified using LC–MS/MS. The LC system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) was equipped with a Shim-pack GIST Amide column (2.1 mm I.D. x 100 mm L., 3.0 μm; Shimadzu) and a Shim-pack GIST-HP (G) Amide guard column (2.1 mm I.D. x 10 mm L., 1.9 μm; Shimadzu).

Chromatographic separation was achieved using a binary gradient consisting of 5 mmol/L ammonium formate in water (Solution A; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) and 5 mmol/L ammonium formate in acetonitrile/water (95:5, v/v) (Solution B; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation). The column oven temperature was maintained at 60 °C, and the flow rate was 0.3 mL/min. The gradient program was as follows: 0–1 min, 95% B; 1–4 min, 95%–70% B; 4.0–5.5 min, 70%–10% B; 5.5–8.0 min, 10% B; 8–13 min, 95% B. MS/MS analysis was performed using an LCMS-8060NX triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Shimadzu) equipped with an electrospray ionization interface in negative-ion mode. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) conditions were optimized using the automatic MRM optimization function. Quantification of target analytes was performed using LabSolutions software (Shimadzu).

Quantification was based on calibration curves generated from authentic GAG standards that were enzymatically digested into the corresponding disaccharides using the same procedure as that for the samples. Chondrosine was used as an internal standard for normalization. A limitation of this enzymatic disaccharide-based approach is the absence of stable isotope–labeled internal standards for the target disaccharides, which may affect the accuracy of absolute quantification compared with isotope dilution–based LC–MS/MS methods.

Non-parametric statistical analyses were performed using Dunn's test. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 31 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

As a secondary screening method for MPS I, GAG quantification achieved 100% sensitivity, 100% specificity, a positive predictive value of 100%, and a negative predictive value of 100%. For MPS II, the method achieved 100% sensitivity, 84.3% specificity, a positive predictive value of 50%, and a negative predictive value of 100%. Screening for both MPS I and MPS II yielded a false negative rate of 0%. Compared with enzyme activity measurement alone, the second-tier GAG analysis reduced false positives by 100% for MPS I and by 84.3% for MPS II. KS-derived Gal-GlcNAc(6S) and Gal (6S)-GlcNAc(6S) values above the cutoff were observed in the MPS IVA patient. In the MPS VI patient, KS-derived Gal-GlcNAc(6S), Gal (6S)-GlcNAc(6S), and DS-derived ΔDi-4S exceeded the cutoff values. Both patients exhibited pathological accumulation of GAGs.

Among 51 MPS II pseudodeficiency samples, 8 screened positive under the primary rule (DS or HS ≥ 95th-percentile cutoff). All eight were negative for DS-derived ΔDi-4S; four showed elevation of ΔDiHS-0S alone, and four showed elevations of both ΔDiHS-0S and ΔDiHS-NS. When a stricter criterion requiring elevation of both DS and HS was applied, all eight pseudodeficiency positives reverted to negative, improving specificity for MPS II from 84.3% to 100% with no loss of sensitivity (100%). These results are summarized in Table S1.

Given that all patients with confirmed MPS I and II exceeded both DS and HS cutoffs and that HS-only cutoff exceedances occurred exclusively in MPS II pseudodeficiency, the strict dual-marker rule (“DS and HS ≥ cutoff”) provides superior specificity without loss of sensitivity. We, therefore, recommend the strict rule as the preferred algorithm for laboratories implementing disaccharide-based second-tier testing.

In expanded NBS programs in Japan, infants with markedly reduced lysosomal enzyme activity identified through primary screening undergo confirmatory testing, which typically includes repeat enzyme assays, urinary GAG analysis, and molecular genetic testing. Previous studies have demonstrated that enzyme activity-based screening alone is associated with high false-positive rates for MPS I and MPS II, largely due to the presence of carriers and pseudodeficient alleles [13]. Consequently, many infants without clinical disease are subjected to unnecessary follow-up testing, placing substantial logistical and psychological burdens on families and healthcare systems. These challenges underscore the need for effective second-tier screening strategies that improve specificity while remaining compatible with existing NBS workflows.

In this study, we evaluated an LC–MS/MS-based enzymatic disaccharide method for quantifying DS-, HS-, and KS-derived disaccharides in DBS samples as a potential second-tier screening approach for MPS. Using residual DBS samples from infants who screened positive by enzyme activity measurement, we demonstrated pathological accumulation of GAG-derived disaccharides in all confirmed MPS patients included in this cohort. In contrast, most MPS I carriers and MPS II pseudodeficient newborns did not exhibit abnormal GAG accumulation, resulting in complete elimination of false positives for MPS I and a substantial reduction for MPS II. However, a limitation of this enzymatic disaccharide-based approach is the absence of stable isotope-labeled internal standards, which may affect the accuracy of absolute quantification compared with isotope dilution-based LC–MS/MS methods.

Despite these encouraging findings, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of the enzymatic disaccharide-based approach, particularly for MPS II. Although sensitivity for MPS II was 100% in this cohort, specificity was 84.3%, with a positive predictive value of 50%. These results indicate that a subset of pseudodeficient newborns cannot be reliably distinguished from affected individuals using this method alone. Importantly, in our cohort, all confirmed MPS I and MPS II patients exceeded the cutoff for both DS- and HS-derived disaccharides, whereas none of the 13 MPS I carriers exceeded any cutoff. In contrast, single-analyte elevations (HS-only) occurred exclusively in MPS II pseudodeficiency (4 with ΔDiHS-0S alone and 4 with ΔDiHS-0S and ΔDiHS-NS). Thus, the residual false positives originated from the positivity rule (“DS or HS ≥ cutoff”) rather than from insufficient GAG elevation in affected patients. Consistent with this interpretation, applying a stricter rule requiring both DS and HS elevations eliminated all false positives without reducing sensitivity (Table S1).

This limitation is consistent with previous reports showing that disaccharide-based GAG quantification does not fully resolve MPS II pseudodeficiency [34]. Importantly, MPS II pseudodeficiency does not involve pathological GAG accumulation; therefore, the residual overlap observed in our cohort cannot be attributed to partial degradation of accumulated GAGs. Rather, the overlap likely reflects limitations of disaccharide-based biomarkers.

In recent years, analysis of endogenous NRE GAG fragments in DBS samples by mass spectrometry has emerged as a highly specific second-tier strategy for newborn screening for MPS. NRE-based assays directly measure disease-specific GAG fragments generated by incomplete lysosomal degradation and have been shown to effectively discriminate affected infants from carriers and pseudodeficient cases, achieving near-complete elimination of false-positive results across multiple MPS subtypes. Accordingly, NRE-based methods are increasingly regarded as the current and future gold standard for second-tier newborn screening for MPS.

Compared with NRE-based approaches, the enzymatic disaccharide-based method evaluated in the present study demonstrates inferior analytical performance, particularly with respect to specificity for MPS II. The objective of this study was therefore not to propose this method as a replacement for NRE-based assays, but rather to assess its practical utility as a complementary or transitional option within current expanded NBS infrastructures. In many screening laboratories, implementation of NRE-based assays may be limited by analytical platform availability, technical complexity, or laboratory readiness. Under such circumstances, enzymatic disaccharide-based GAG quantification may still provide clinically useful information when applied as a second-tier test using residual DBS samples, thereby reducing false positives compared with enzyme activity measurement alone.

A large cohort study similarly demonstrated that second-tier LC–MS/MS GAG testing markedly reduced false positives, with GAG–enzyme combinations achieving zero false positives, further supporting the utility of GAG-based second-tier approaches within NBS workflows [35].

Elevated KS-derived disaccharides were observed in patients with MPS I, II, IVA, and VI in this study. In MPS IVA, KS accumulation is a well-established primary biochemical feature [36,37]. In contrast, KS elevation in MPS I and II is not directly related to the underlying enzyme deficiencies and likely reflects secondary accumulation, as previously reported [38]. The absence of KS accumulation in MPS I carriers and MPS II pseudodeficient newborns further supports the association between KS elevation and pathological lysosomal dysfunction rather than pseudodeficiency alone [39].

CS was not evaluated in the present study. Although CS-derived biomarkers may be informative for certain MPS subtypes, including MPS IVA and VI, quantification of CS using the enzymatic disaccharide-based DBS approach is technically challenging and was considered of limited incremental value for second-tier screening in the context of expanded NBS programs primarily targeting MPS I and II. Alternative analytical strategies, including methanolysis-based and NRE-based methods, may be more suitable for CS analysis and should be considered in future studies.

Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgement. First, the number of confirmed MPS patients was small, reflecting the rarity of these disorders, and no false-positive cases were available for MPS IVA or VI. Continued accumulation of DBS samples from newborns with diverse MPS subtypes will be necessary to further evaluate the performance of this approach. Second, cutoff values were derived from a limited number of newborn controls, and refinement of reference intervals will require larger population-based datasets. Third, this study assumes that all infants with MPS born during the study period would have been identified through clinical follow-up. Late-onset or attenuated forms of MPS may not yet have come to clinical attention, raising the possibility of undetected false-negative cases.

In conclusion, enzymatic disaccharide-based quantification of GAGs in DBS samples can reduce false-positive results when applied as a second-tier screening test following enzyme activity-based newborn screening for MPS. Although this approach does not match the specificity of NRE-based assays and should not be considered a substitute for them, it may serve as a pragmatic and readily implementable complementary strategy within current expanded NBS frameworks, particularly in laboratories where differences in analytical platforms, technical requirements, or implementation readiness influence method selection.

Taken together, our findings indicate that the strict dual-marker rule (“DS and HS ≥ cutoff”) is the most appropriate algorithm for laboratories implementing this disaccharide-based second-tier test, as it maximizes specificity without compromising on sensitivity.

Using an LC–MS/MS-based method, we analyzed GAGs in DBS samples from newborns who screened positive for MPS in NBS. Abnormal GAG accumulation was detected in all confirmed MPS patients, and false positives were reduced by 100% among MPS I carriers and by 84.3% among MPS II pseudodeficient newborns. Implementation of GAG quantification as a secondary screening method in expanded NBS programs is expected to substantially reduce false-positive results, thereby enabling newborns to avoid unnecessary detailed examinations and genetic testing.
