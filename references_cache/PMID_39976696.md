# X-linked Deficiency in ELF4 in Females with Skewed X Chromosome Inactivation.
**Authors:** Zhao R, Zhang Z, Mei S, Sun L, Zhang Q, Lv Q, Zhou F, Sun G, Zhou L, Tang X, An Y, Liu Z, Zhao X, Du H
**Journal:** J Clin Immunol (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s10875-025-01866-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10875-025-01866-2)

## Content

Deficiency in ELF4, X-linked (DEX) is a newly identified monogenic autoinflammatory disease. Most reported cases are male, leading to the recognition of DEX being primarily limited to male patients. Here we described 3 pediatric female patients with DEX from 3 unrelated families, who are all heterozygous for ELF4 mutations (c.320_c.321insA, c.329delA and c.685 A > G). Similar to reported male DEX patients, the main clinical features include recurring oral ulcers, abdominal pain and diarrhea with colonoscopy showing ulcers in the colon. Meanwhile, novel and effective treatment strategies, such as the use of the biologic vedolizumab and exclusive enteral nutrition (EEN), have provided additional options for the treatment of DEX. Finally, we observed skewed X chromosome inactivation patterns in all three female patients, with over-inactivation of the X chromosome carrying the wild-type allele confirmed in two of them.

The ELF4 deficiency disorder, known as Deficiency in ELF4, X-linked (DEX) or X-linked familial Behcet-like autoinflammatory syndrome-2 (AIFBL2) (OMIM: 301074), has been recently identified and belongs to the group of inborn errors of immunity (IEIs) [1–4]. DEX primarily affects males and is characterized by recurrent inflammation involving the skin, mucosal tissues (including skin ulcers or rashes, oral ulcers, IBD (Inflammatory bowel disease)-like intestinal inflammation, and perianal ulcers), and joints, which may be accompanied by recurrent fever. Only one case exhibited defined autoimmune manifestations [4], and two case showed increased susceptibility to infections, and cancer [5].

The gene, mutations of which are responsible for the phenotype, is E74 like ETS transcription factor 4 (ELF4), located on the X chromosome (Xq26.1) and highly expressed in a variety of hematopoietic cells [6]. The protein encoded by this gene is ETS-related transcription factor Elf-4 (ELF4), which belongs to the ETS domain transcription factor family. ELF4 is involved in the differentiation and function of various immune cells and plays a crucial role in the regulation of the immune system [7].

According to the latest classification of IEIs by the IUIS (International Union of Immunological Societies), there are currently 27 causative genes located on the X chromosome in IEIs [8], some of which have been reported to cause disease in female carriers via the mechanism of skewed inactivation of X Chromosome [9–11]. X chromosome inactivation (XCI) is a process by which one of the two X chromosomes in female mammals is randomly silenced to achieve dosage compensation between males and females. This inactivation ensures that females do not produce twice the amount of X-linked gene products as males [12].

Females who are carriers of a defective ELF4 gene are usually asymptomatic. However, the mother of one DEX patient was recorded with a history of typical Behçet’s disease [4]. Additionally, a report on an atypical case of DEX mentioned a female carrier who exhibited clinical manifestations similar to those of male patient [2]. It remains to be clarified whether female carriers can also develop the disease, which has significant implications for avoiding missed diagnoses.

Here, we report 3 female patients from 3 unrelated families, who presented with symptoms resembling the DEX phenotype. The patients were heterozygous for an ELF4 mutation whose pathogenicity was confirmed bioinformatically and experimentally. Finally, we showed all 3 patients had a nonrandom pattern of X chromosome inactivation in the PBMCs (Peripheral blood mononuclear cells). Our findings confirmed that females carrying pathogenic ELF4 mutations may develop DEX phenotype due to non-random X chromosome inactivation.

Informed consent was obtained from all participating patients and their parents, following protocols approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, Children’s Hospital of Fudan University, and Children’s Hospital Affiliated to Zhengzhou University.

HEK 293T cells (ATCC, CRL-11268) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from freshly drawn heparin-treated blood by Ficoll density gradient centrifugation and cultured in 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FCS.

As previously reported [4]: whole cell lysates were prepared using 1× RIPA buffer, supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Beyotime). As previously reported [3] and briefly: HEK293T cells were plated and transfected with various WT and mutant 7.1-pCMV-3×Flag expression plasmids. 24 h later, cycloheximide was added (1ug/ml) and cells were collected at different time point for western blot detection.

The human androgen receptor gene (AR) is located on the long arm of the X chromosome at position Xq13. Its first exon contains a CAG repeat sequence with high polymorphism. Within 100 bp of this polymorphic site, there are two HpaII and two HhaI restriction sites. These sites are methylated on the inactive X chromosome and demethylated on the active X chromosome. For P1 and P3: AR gene based method was used as we previously reported [10]. For P2: The CAG repeat sequences on the maternal and paternal X chromosomes of the AR gene are identical, preventing their differentiation. We then used the XCIFiler kit (fluorescent PCR-capillary electrophoresis) from Shanghai JingZhun Biotech, following the manufacturer’s instructions. This kit contains 4 polymorphic genetic markers on the X chromosome (XpB-XpA-XpC-XqA, XqA is the AR loci). Approximately 99% of female individuals exhibit heterozygosity at least at one locus. When multiple loci are heterozygous before enzyme digestion, the XCI value for each locus is calculated individually, and their arithmetic mean is used as the final XCI result for the sample.

Samples were compared using two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-test with GraphPad Prism 7.00. Error bars were represented by SD (Standard deviation).

Patient 1 (P1), born to non-consanguineous parents, initially presented with recurrent oral ulcers and intermittent fevers at 6 years and 7 months old, poorly responding to antimicrobial therapy. Ulcers appeared on the tonsils, tongue, lips, and buccal mucosa, occasionally with Candida albicans infection. At 7 years old, she began experiencing recurrent abdominal pain, moderate anemia, and was underweight (-2SD). Her mother had recurrent oral ulcers with no treatment and further disease progression. Her brother died at 6 years old after recurrent fever, oral ulcers, and bloody stools. She has 2 healthy younger sisters. Immunological tests were normal. At 7 years and 1 month old, colonoscopy revealed ulcers in the terminal ileum and colon, leading to a diagnosis of Crohn’s disease. She was treated with glucocorticoids, thalidomide, and infliximab, showing sustained remission. After more than 20 doses of infliximab, unexplained abdominal pain prompted switching to vedolizumab, which eased her pain. She continues maintenance treatment with vedolizumab and oral ulcers did not recur.

Patient 2 (P2), a 7-year-old girl, began showing symptoms at 3 years of age, with recurrent oral, vulvar, and perianal ulcers. Despite symptomatic treatment, she frequently relapsed and also experienced recurrent fevers and erythema nodosum-like rashes. Elevated CRP (C-reactive protein), ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate), and transient hip synovitis were noted. Her uncle had recurrent oral ulcers, and her maternal grandmother was suspected of having Sjögren’s syndrome. Immunological tests showed normal Ig (Immunoglobulin) levels and negative autoantibodies, but elevated anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibodies and lupus anticoagulant. Colonoscopy revealed a terminal ileum ulcer and rectal erosions. She was treated with mesalamine, steroids, and adalimumab, achieving sustained remission.

Patient 3 (P3), a 12-year-old girl, started showing symptoms at 9, including recurrent oral ulcers, diarrhea, weight loss, and fever, with elevated CRP and ESR (Fig. 1A) and poor response to antimicrobials alone. Her father was suspected of having irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). Stool tests revealed pyocytes. Immunological tests showed elevated T and B cells, and increased TNF-alpha, IL-1beta, and IL-6 in the serum. Fecal calprotectin was over 1500ug/g (<50 ug/g) (Fig. 1A). Colonoscopy revealed deep ulcers from the cecum to the sigmoid colon and pathological biopsy of the corresponding sites revealed inflammatory changes with ulcer formation (Fig. 1B and C). Diagnosed with Crohn’s disease, P3 was treated with EEN (Exclusive enteral nutrition), and later mesalamine, 6-mercaptopurine, and finally adalimumab, achieving sustained remission (Fig. 1D and E, Supplemental Figs. 1–2).

Theoretically, the frameshift mutations in P1 and P2 would lead to the production of a truncated ELF4 protein of over 100 amino acids in length. We constructed mutant expression plasmids and found no wild-type ELF4 protein but rather the truncated ELF4 protein was detected (Fig. 3A). Additionally, using the previously reported IFN-b dual-luciferase reporter assay, we showed the truncated ELF4 proteins failed to activate the IFN-b promoter, likely due to the failure to express important domains such as the NLS and ETS domains (Fig. 3B). The I229V missense mutation carried by P3 is located in the ETS domain. This variant does not occur in the 1000 Genomes database, ExAC database, or in-house database. This amino acid site is highly conserved across species and among ETS domain transcription factor superfamily members [3]. Pathogenicity prediction using multiple methods classified this variant as “Damaging” (Supplementary Table 1). The AlphaMissense pathogenicity score of this variant is 0.948 (likely pathogenic) [13]. Structurally, various atomic contacts and bonds are predicted to be disrupted by the mutation [14, 15] (Fig. 3C). Similarly, the I229V ELF4 failed to activate the IFN-b promoter (Fig. 3B), although it has little impact on protein expression levels (Fig. 3A, D and E).

According to previous reports, DEX tends to follow an X-linked recessive inheritance pattern, where female carriers typically do not develop symptoms [16]. Skewed X-chromosome inactivation is one of the main mechanisms causing female carriers to develop symptoms in X-linked recessive diseases [17, 18]. Therefore, we examined the X-chromosome inactivation in the three probands. We found that P1’s paternal X-chromosome (carrying the wild-type ELF4 genotype) had an inactivation rate of approximately 82%, indicating moderate skewing of inactivation (> 70%, < 90%) (Fig. 4A), leading to overexpression of the mutant ELF4. In contrast, her second younger sister, who also carries the mutation but does not exhibit symptoms, did not show X-chromosome inactivation skewing (53% <70%) (Fig. 4A). P3’s maternal X-chromosome (carrying the wild-type ELF4 genotype) had an inactivation rate of about 83%, also indicating moderate skewing of inactivation (Fig. 4B). The polymorphic locus of the AR gene cannot distinguish between the paternal and maternal X chromosomes of P2. Therefore, we employed another method (with additional 3 polymorphic genetic markers), which revealed that P2’s paternal X-chromosome had an inactivation rate of approximately 33.7% identified as skewed in this system (≥ 25%) (Fig. 4C). Since the ELF4 mutation carried by the proband is De novo, we cannot determine whether it occurred on the maternal or paternal X-chromosome.

According to previous reports, DEX primarily affects males. In this study, we identified four affected female carriers across three unrelated families and provided a detailed summary of the clinical history of three pediatric patients, including their treatment experience. The onset age for these three patients ranged from 3 to 9 years, similar to previously reported male patients, indicating that regardless of gender, DEX diagnosis does not require a very early onset of age. Oral ulcers and colonic ulcers remain the most common manifestations, with recurrent fever of unknown origin accompanied by elevated inflammatory markers also being frequent. No recurrent infections or autoimmune manifestations were observed. While reduced NK cell counts are a common immunological feature in male DEX patients, all three female patients in this study had normal NK cell counts, normal or increased total B cell counts, and no reduction in IgG or IgM levels, although memory B cells were not tested.

P1 and P2 both carry ELF4 frameshift mutations, resulting in a truncated ELF4 lacking several important domains, such as the NLS and ETS domains (Fig. 3A). This likely explains the loss of function nature of the mutations. P1’s mother and second younger sister are also carriers. The mother has a history of recurrent oral ulcers, while the sister is currently healthy. In addition, her brother exhibited similar symptoms but died of this condition at an early age, so it could not be confirmed whether he carried this mutation. Neither of P2’s parents carry the mutation, suggesting it is a De novo mutation. However, P2’s uncle has a history of recurrent oral ulcers, and her maternal grandmother shows autoimmune manifestations, though their genotypes are unknown. Further research is needed to investigate potential association of these presentations. P3 carries a missense mutation which locates in the ETS domain, close to previously reported site [3]. This may also affect ELF4’s binding to target gene promoters. P3’s father carries this mutation but only exhibits symptoms of IBS. It is not uncommon that family members with the same genotype are affected variably by IEIs [19].

Treatment for female DEX patients is similar to males, with corticosteroids and biologics commonly used to induce and maintain remission. Adalimumab is currently the most common biologic used to control inflammation in DEX patients, showing good efficacy in both previously reported patients and in P1 and P3 from this study. However, P1 relapsed on adalimumab and required corticosteroids again and a switch to vedolizumab for sustaining remission, indicating close monitoring of DEX patients on adalimumab is necessary. Vedolizumab is a recombinant humanized IgG monoclonal antibody targeting the α4β7 integrin, currently the only gut-selective biologic. It specifically binds to α4β7 integrin, which is primarily expressed on the surface of memory T lymphocytes [20] and a subset of monocytes [21]. Through interaction with its ligand, mucosal addressin cell adhesion molecule-1 (MAdCAM-1), it specifically mediates lymphocyte homing to Peyer’s patches, mesenteric lymph nodes, and other gut-associated lymphoid tissues, playing an important role in inflammatory bowel diseases [22]. Vedolizumab’s effectiveness suggests significance of T lymphocytes in DEX pathogenesis. Indeed, ELF4 has been shown to be important in T cells [23], but connection between these findings has not been uncovered. P3 achieved short- and intermediate-term targets according to the standards set by the International Organization for the Study of Inflammatory Bowel Diseases (IOIBD) [24], with EEN instead of corticosteroids, highlighting a potential new approach for DEX treatment [25]. In short, our report offers new insights into treatment strategies for DEX.

The X chromosome contains 867 identified genes, and at least 533 disorders are associated with genes on the X chromosome [26]. The most common explanation for female carriers of X-linked recessive disorders being symptomatic is skewed XCI, as seen in many genetic disorders, including IEIs [9–11, 17]. In P1, the inactivation ratio of the paternal wild-type X chromosome is significantly higher than that of the maternal mutated X chromosome (Fig. 4A). Her second younger sister, who is also a carrier but asymptomatic, was found to have random XCI (Fig. 4A). In P3, the maternal wild-type X chromosome showed a significantly higher inactivation ratio than the paternal mutated X chromosome (Fig. 4C). These findings suggest that female carriers of ELF4 mutations exhibit symptoms presumably due to skewed XCI. In P2, the inactivation ratio of the paternal X chromosome was significantly lower than that of the maternal X chromosome, leading to the speculation that the ELF4 mutation occurred on the paternal X chromosome. Skewed XCI is reported to be somewhat common in the general female population [27], and the skewness of X-chromosome inactivation increases with age [28]. Additionally, female carriers of X-linked diseases often have milder clinical manifestations compared to affected males. Heterozygous female Elf4 knock-out mice exhibited susceptibility to colitis with severity milder compared to homozygous knockout mice (our unpublished data). Based on this information, while definitive evidence is lacking, it is reasonable to infer that the clinical manifestations in these female patients are likely due to skewed XCI. This further predicts that female patients, especially adult women, could be an important subset of DEX patients [29]. Therefore, as with some other IEIs, the diagnosis and treatment of DEX require collaboration between pediatricians and adult physicians.

In summary, we have described three pediatric female DEX patients from unrelated families, all heterozygous carriers of pathogenic ELF4 mutations. Their clinical manifestations are similar to those of male DEX patients. For treatment, P1 maintained remission with vedolizumab, while P3 achieved initial remission with EEN (though sustained remission requires combining other interventions), providing new treatment options for DEX patients. The X-chromosome inactivation skewing observed in these symptomatic female carriers may explain their disease onset. It is likely that more female DEX patients will be diagnosed in the future, and we hope this study will raise awareness among both pediatric and adult physicians.
