Tay-Sachs disease (TSD) is a fatal neurodegenerative disease caused by a
deficiency of the enzyme β-N-acetylhexosaminidase A (HexA). TSD naturally occurs
in Jacob sheep is the only experimental model of TSD. TSD in sheep recapitulates
neurologic features similar to juvenile onset and late onset TSD patients. Due
to the paucity of human literature on pathology of TSD, a better natural history
in the sheep TSD brain, which is on the same order of magnitude as a child's, is
necessary for evaluating therapy and characterizing the pathological events that
occur. To provide clinicians and researchers with a clearer understanding of
longitudinal pathology in patients, we compare spectrum of clinical signs and
brain pathology in mildly symptomatic (3-months), moderately symptomatic
(6-months), or severely affected TSD sheep (humane endpoint at ~9-months of
age). Increased GM2 ganglioside in the CSF of TSD sheep and a TSD specific
biomarker on MRS (taurine) correlate with disease severity. Microglial
activation and reactive astrocytes were observed globally on histopathology in
TSD sheep with a widespread reduction in oligodendrocyte density. Myelination is
reduced primarily in the forebrain illustrated by loss of white matter on MRI.
GM2 and GM3 ganglioside were increased and distributed differently in various
tissues. The study of TSD in the sheep model provides a natural history to shed
light on the pathophysiology of TSD, which is of utmost importance due to novel
therapeutics being assessed in human patients.

Tay-Sachs disease (TSD) is a fatal neurodegenerative disease caused by a deficiency of the enzyme β-N-acetylhexosaminidase (Hex), which is responsible for the breakdown of GM2 ganglioside in the cell. Hex consists of a heterodimer with an alpha and beta subunit; TSD is caused by an alpha subunit mutation (HEXA gene mutation) whereas a beta subunit mutation causes Sandhoff disease (SD; HEXB gene mutation). These two diseases are nearly clinically indistinguishable. GM2 accumulation is ubiquitous within the affected individual, but most prominently in the central nervous system because gangliosides are most abundant in the nervous tissue. As a result, TSD and SD are dominated by progressive degeneration of neurologic function. TSD and SD have three recognizable clinical presentations (1). Infantile onset is the most common and severe, with children perceived to be normal at birth followed by progressive developmental regression starting commonly around 6 months, seizures, and a semi-vegetative state before death by 5 years of age (2–5). Juvenile onset patients are often diagnosed in childhood with clumsiness, learning disabilities and speech problems and eventually develop spasticity and seizures with death commonly in their teens (6). Adult-onset patients are often misdiagnosed for years, but the first clinical signs often manifest in the second decade of life and can include psychiatric disease, ataxia and muscle weakness, peripheral neuropathy, dysarthria, and incoordination that progresses to loss of ambulation. These patients can have a near-normal lifespan (7–10).

There are currently four GM2 gangliosidosis laboratory animal models: the TSD sheep, a newly described Tay-Sachs pig, the SD mouse, the SD cat, and the SD zebrafish {Cork, 1977 #81;Torres, 2010 #192;Sango, 1995 #477;Bertani, 2021 #1303}. The mouse model for TSD has an alternate catabolic pathway for GM2 ganglioside and is asymptomatic (14). Although multiple mutations in the feline HEXB gene (Sandhoff) have been described, there is currently no feline models for Tay-Sachs disease. A missense mutation in HEXA cDNA leads to TSD in Jacob sheep. A single nucleotide change (G-to-C transition) at the end of exon 11 induces an amino acid change from Gly444 to Arg (G444R mutation) leading to loss of HEXA activity due to skipping of exon 11 (54). Therefore, the TSD sheep are the only fully characterized animal model that shares similar genetics, biochemical pathology, and disease progression as TSD patients. Coupled with a central nervous system that is more similar in size and complexity to humans, evaluation of therapeutic modalities in sheep as a large animal model of TSD is critical for translation to human medicine (15). AAV gene therapy has been described in the mouse and cat models of SD, with a >four-fold increase in lifespan and marked attenuation of neurological disease (16–22). Similar intracranial administration of AAV monocistronic vectors in TSD sheep showed delayed symptom onset, reduction of acquired symptoms, attenuation of microglial activation, HexA distribution, and ganglioside clearance (23).

Success in these large animal models has led to the first tests of AAV gene therapy for Tay-Sachs and Sandhoff disease in humans (IND#16225; clinicaltrials.gov
NCT04669535). This is an exciting milestone for patients as no effective therapy has reached to patients since its discovery in 1880. Despite knowing about Tay-Sachs for 140 years, surprisingly little is known about the pathophysiology of this disease. To elucidate underlying neuropathology and further the understanding of how disease progression relates to biomarkers, we performed in-life assessments including MRI, MRS, maze testing, CSF collection and neurological examinations.

Animals were then euthanized, and post-mortem samples were evaluated for HexA activity and GM2 ganglioside content and correlated with histopathologic changes in cell populations to give insight into the brain areas with the earliest and most severe neurodegeneration. Our goal is to provide a reference for physicians when evaluating the effects of disease modifying therapies in patients with Tay-Sachs and Sandhoff disease, in a way that is not possible in patients.

All animal procedures were approved by the Auburn University Institutional Care and Use Committee and the Cummings College of Veterinary Medicine at Tufts University. TSD sheep were randomly allocated into groups. Anesthesia for MRI and CSF collection was induced with a combination of midazolam (2 mg/kg) and ketamine (2mg/kg) and maintained in manually ventilated animals with isoflurane (0.5–2%). In animals that developed tremors during MRI, we administered dexmedetomidine as a constant rate infusion at a dose of 0.01mg/kg/hour. Animals were humanely euthanized by pentobarbital (100mg/kg) overdose.

Sheep that underwent maze testing were separated from flock located at the end of the maze and placed at the beginning of the maze. The sheep undergoing the testing is allowed to navigate the maze and the time is recorded. Separate cohorts were used for one time maze testing at 6 months of age (TSD n=9, Control n=14) and monthly maze testing at 1 (TSD n=3, Control n=2) 2 (TSD n=3, Control n=1), 3 (TSD n=3, Control n=2), 5 (TSD n=3, Control n=2) and 7 (TSD n=3, Control n=1) months of age. Individual neurological exams were scored as 2=normal, 1.5–1=delayed, and 0=absent.

Brain and spinal cord sections were frozen in OCT and 40 μm sections were cut from the block and extracted as previously described in 50mM of citrate phosphate buffer containing 0.1% Triton X-100 and 0.05% bovine serum albumin (23). HexA activity (4MU/mg/hr) was measured in using a 4MU fluorogenic substrate (4MU-6-sulfo-2-acetamido-2-deoxy-β-D-glucopyranoside; MUGS).

The sheep CSF samples for ceramide and mono-acyl ceramide (MC) were injected directly into the LC-MS/MS system. To the sheep CSF samples (50 μL) for GM2 were added with d3-GM2 18:0 (internal standard for GM2) in methanol (50 μL), and the mixtures were injected into the LC-MS/MS system. The sheep tissues were homogenized in aqueous 2% (w/v) 3-[(3-cholamidopropyl) dimethylammonio]-1-propanesulfonate (CHAPS) solution (10 mL/g wet tissue). Protein precipitation was used to extract lipids from 50 μL of homogenate with 200 μL of methanol containing d3-GM1 18:0, d3-GM2 18:0, and d3-GM3 18:0 as internal standards for GM1, GM2, and GM3, respectively. The supernatants were injected into the LC-MS/MS system. The lipids were separated with reversed phase column on a Shimadzu Prominence UFLC system coupled with an AB Sciex 4000QTRAP mass spectrometer and two Valco switching valves. The multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) was used to detect lipids. The data processing was performed with Analyst 1.5.3. The relative quantification data of ceramide and MC were reported as peak areas, and GM1, GM2, GM3 as peak area ratios of analytes to their internal standards.

The sheep cohort used for the pathology consisted of a total of 16 sheep with 8 clinically normal controls and 8 TSD affected sheep. The necropsy timepoints were 3 months (n=3), 6 months (n=3), and 9 months (n=2) for each group. H&E and Luxol fast blue (LFB) staining were performed on all sections (24). Selected 5-um sections of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue were subjected to immunohistochemical staining using an automated system (Autostainer Link, Dako) with a polymer-based horseradish peroxidase-conjugated detection kit with 3, 3′ diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride as the chromogen (EnVision™ FLEX high-pH kit, Dako). Antigen retrieval was performed by heating in citrate buffer (pH 6.1) and quenching of endogenous peroxidase activity was via immersion in a phosphate buffer containing H2O2, 15 mmol/l NaN3 and detergent (EnVision™ FLEX Peroxidase-blocking Reagent, Dako). Slides were counterstained with haematoxylin. For negative reagent controls, slides in which the step of incubation of the primary antibody was removed from the protocol were used. Negative internal tissue controls in immunolabelled slides consisted of existing tissue elements which would not be expected to demonstrate any immunoreactivity for the antibody being evaluated.Sections were pre-incubated in MAP2 for 4 hours (Sigma-Aldrich, HM-2; 1:1000 dilution), Olig2 (Abcam; EPR2678, 1:200 dilution) was incubated for 30 min, GFAP (IR 52461–2; no dilution; Dako-Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was incubated for 20 min, and Iba1 (Biocare Medical, CP290A; 1:100 dilution) was incubated for 30 minutes. Detection was performed using the Dako EnVision HRP detection with DAB chromogen and hematoxylin counterstain. For quantification, slides were digitally scanned at ×80 using an Aperio1 Scan Scope (Leica Biosystems, Buffalo Grove, IL, USA). Algorithms were written to quantify area of diaminobenzidine (DAB) for each individual stain using Visiopharm quantitative digital histopathology software (Visiopharm, Hoersholm Denmark) and applied to all slides of an individual stain (Suppl. Fig. 1). The thalamus, hippocampus, parietal cortex, and cerebellar hemispheres were evaluated with all stains. Cervical and lumbar intumescences were evaluated with Iba1 and Luxol fast blue.

Anatomical MR images and MRS data were collected a 3 time points throughout the study on a 7T MAGNETOM scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). A 32-channel head coil (Nova Medical, Boston, MA) was used for all scans. The imaging protocol included 3D T1-weighted sequence and 2D T2-weighted TSE sequence. Single voxel spectroscopy was acquired using the stimulated echo acquisition mode with variable pulse power and optimized relaxation delays water suppression, TE/TR= 4.6/10,000ms and 32 averages. In all animals, a 10mm· 10mm· 17mm voxel was placed in the striatothalamic region that was well defined on the high-resolution anatomical images. Sheep were premedicated with buprenorphine (0.02 mg/kg), acepromazine (0.05 mg/kg), and glycopyrrolate (0.01 mg/kg) intramuscularly. Sheep were anesthetized using midazolam (0.3 mg/kg) and ketamine (10 mg/kg) intravenously, intubated, and anesthesia was maintained using isoflurane gas during the procedure. Sheep were imaged in dorsal recumbency.

Statistics were performed using Graphpad PRISM. Maze data was analyzed using unpaired T-test for latency to complete the first maze trial (p < 0.001). Latency to complete maze for multiple trials was analyzed with a mixed-effects analysis and no statistical significance was determined for latency to complete maze between TSD or control sheep. CSF lipidomics and MRS data were analyzed using a one-way ANOVA followed by a parametric unpaired T-test assuming Gaussian distribution. Enzyme assay and ganglioside storage statistical analysis included a one-way ANOVA followed by multiple unpaired T-tests assuming unequal variances using a false discovery approach with a two-stage up method of Benjamini, Krieger and Tekutieli with a Q=1%. Histopathology data was presented as mean ± standard deviation and range (minimum-maximum). Level of significance was set at two-tailed P-value level of 0.05.

Wild-type and TSD sheep were followed from birth until predetermined endpoint (3 months and 6 months) or they lost the ability to walk (~9 months). Animals were evaluated longitudinally using neurological examinations, maze testing, CSF analyses, ultra-high field MRI and MR spectroscopy. We elected to harvest mildly symptomatic (3-months), moderately symptomatic (6-months) or severely affected TSD sheep (~9 months of age) to correlate gradations of clinical signs with brain pathology. We quantified microgliosis, astrogliosis, neuronal density, myelination and oligodendrocyte number in the thalamus, hippocampus, parietal cortex, and cerebellar hemispheres to better understand disease progression. GM2 ganglioside and HexA activity are included to provide insight as to the severity of GM2 storage in the brain.

We previously reported the survival of the TSD sheep to 9.4+/− 0.8 months (23), when they lose the ability to stand. To better understand the disease phenotype in the TSD sheep, we performed biweekly serial neurological exams (Fig. 1). The first clinical sign observed (present by 3 months of age) in the TSD sheep is positional strabismus of the eyes, where one or both eyes deviates when the head is lifted (Fig. 1A). Proprioceptive deficits are noted around 4 months of age and manifest as abnormal posture or proprioceptive ataxia (Fig. 1B). Reduced knuckling response of the metacarpophalangeal joint is also noted around 4 months of age and progresses rapidly after 6 months of age with disease (Fig. 1C). The ability to hemi-hop (right or left side) decreases first in the pelvic limbs by 4 months of age, followed by the thoracic limbs (Fig. 1D). The TSD sheep lose the menace (blink) response starting around 7–7.5 months of age (Fig. 1A) and around the same time ataxia worsens to include a cerebellar phenotype (truncal sway, wide based stance; Fig. 1B). The ataxia is thought to be primarily due to loss in proprioception since muscle tone does not reliably decrease until end stages of disease (Fig. 1E). Based on these findings, we developed a clinical rating score that encompasses the aforementioned neurological signs (Fig. 1F), revealing an initial peak around 4 months of age, followed by a plateau until 7–8 months of age where a rapid deterioration occurs.

To better understand the effects of TSD on cognition and memory, we developed a maze test (Fig. 2A). This test utilizes the natural flocking instinct of sheep, with one animal isolated from the flock, which is kept at the end of the maze. The animal cannot see the flock but can hear and smell them. When sheep are put in maze for the first time at 6 months of age, there is a clear delineation between the time it takes the TSD sheep to navigate the maze as compared to normal controls (Fig. 2B). We selected 6 months of age because this is the latest time point where all TSD sheep are able to walk without difficulty. Interestingly, if the TSD sheep are tested monthly, they retain the ability to navigate the maze, suggesting they are able to remember the maze (Fig. 2C).

To quantify the storage products in a minimally invasive way, we performed targeted lipidomics of cerebrospinal fluid. Ceramide was elevated in CSF by 4 months of age after which it plateaued (Fig. 3A). Monohexosylceramide (MC) trends towards significance at 4 months and increased further at endpoint (Fig. 3B). GM2 (18:0 and 20:0) were both increased by 3 months of age and stayed elevated until endpoint (Fig. 3 C&D).

MRI is the modality of choice to evaluate neurological disease and here we use the highest field strength (7 Tesla) clinically approved for patient use (Fig. 4). On T2-weighted TSE MRI, the TSD sheep brain undergoes a progressive demyelination with loss of white matter that is most evident at the borders of white and gray matter starting at 3 months of age. At 9 months of age the white matter loses its normal hypointensity due to demyelination (Fig. 4, top). MR spectroscopy of brain metabolites identified a progressive increase in glutamine and reduction of the neurotransmitter glutamate in TSD sheep, representing a disequilibrium of these two neurotransmitters (Fig. 4, bottom). Myoinositol, a marker of gliosis, increased starting at 6 months. N-acetyl aspartate, a maker of neuron and axon health, dropped dramatically between 6 months and humane endpoint (~9 months). NAAG (N-acetyl aspartylglutamate) also decreased at humane endpoint. Taurine increased progressively starting at 6 months, which is thought to be conjugated to GM2 as a compensatory mechanism to increase solubility of the ganglioside (25). Markers of demyelination glycerophosphocholine and phosphocholine also increase starting at 6 months, as do creatine and phosphocreatine, which are thought to be markers of demyelination.

Pathology of Tay-Sachs was evaluated across all major regions of the brain, as well as cervical and lumbar sections of spinal cord. Tissues from multiple animals per time point at three and six months of age, as well animals at either nine months or the humane endpoint were available for evaluation by light microscopy with previously described histochemical and immunohistochemical stains. Cortex, thalamus, substantia nigra, hippocampus, and cervical and lumbar spinal cord were available for at least two animals in each group, and cerebellum was available for at least one animal in each group. Oculomotor nuclei and red nuclei were represented in sections from animals from at least two time points. The pathology in these animals is consistent with what has been reported previously (51). Briefly, neurons in all examined areas of the brain and spinal cord were moderately to markedly expanded by fine foamy to granular clear to lightly eosinophilic cytoplasmic material (Suppl. Fig. 3). There was a progression of astrocytic changes already present at three months and worse at six and nine months consisting of reactive astrocytes characterized by increased cytoplasmic visibility (with occasional frank gemistocytic change), increased branching of cytoplasmic processes, enlarged nuclei with vesicular or marginated chromatin, and occasionally formation of large, bizarre nuclei with irregular nuclear silhouettes as well as nuclei present in doublets or triplets. These changes were especially prominent in the subcortical white matter and glia limitans. There was a diffuse mild to moderate increase in the number of visible microglial nuclei. There was scattered vacuolation of the neuroparenchyma and formation of rare small spheroids, with both of these changes increasing with each time point and most prominent in the white matter of the telencephalon. In the cerebellum, Purkinje cell bodies were markedly distended at all time points with storage material, with more subtle expansion of the cytoplasm of the granule neurons and an associated time-dependent mild decrease in cellularity of the granular layer. Scattered cells interpreted as basket, stellate, and Golgi cells were also distended by storage material, and reactive astrocytes with large, open-faced nuclei were present at all time points.

The neuroinflammatory response in TSD sheep was also evaluated by immunohistochemistry for astrocytes (glial fibrillary acidic protein [GFAP]) and microglia (Iba1; Fig. 6). GFAP immunolabeling area was highest in the thalamus of TSD sheep starting at 6 months of age and trending to increase at 9 months of age (Fig. 6 A&B). In the parietal cortex, and to a lesser extent the cerebellar hemispheres, GFAP labeling area appeared to increase later in the disease process at 9 months of age in TSD sheep. Interestingly, the hippocampus does not show evidence of increased GFAP, which may be related to the fact that astrocytes in the cortex express ionotropic glutamate receptors, but not in the hippocampus (26). As estimated by Iba1 immunolabeling, evidence of a microgliosis was suggested early in disease presentation in the thalamus and parietal cortex (Fig. 6 C&D). Microglial densities are high in these areas early in life in affected animals; however, they remain static throughout disease progression. Although the cerebellum shows apparent increases in Iba1 labeling area, the microglial density is less than in the thalamus and parietal cortices. The absence of Iba1 labeling area increases in the spinal intumescences indicates a neuroinflammatory process largely limited to the brain (data not shown).

Microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2) functions to stabilize microtubules in neurons and was used as an indicator of neuronal loss and/or loss of dendritic process area in TSD sheep (Fig. 7 A&B). Microscopic evaluation of the thalamus shows equivalent MAP2 labeling area at 3 months compared to normal controls. Neuronal density as estimated by MAP2 staining trended towards a decrease in the thalamus, with significant reductions in the hippocampus and cerebellum at 6 months of age with relative sparing of the parietal cortex.

Although there was no significant decrease, Luxol fast blue (LFB) staining for myelin trended toward a reduction in TSD sheep in all brain areas evaluated (Fig. 7 C&D). CNS areas with higher density of white matter such as the parietal cortex and thalamus may serve as a better representation of hypomyelination. As with Iba1 staining, the spinal cord intumescences remained unaffected (Suppl. Fig.1. The results of LFB staining suggest decreased myelination of the thalamus and parietal cortex with preserved myelination of the spinal cord. Oligodendrocyte density was evaluated and quantified based on DAB area (Olig2 IHC) in an effort to determine an etiology for the demyelination (Fig. 7 D&F). Oligodendrocyte staining (Olig2+ area) was significantly reduced in the thalamus, hippocampus, parietal cortex, and cerebellar hemispheres by 3 months of age in affected sheep, illustrating a persistent widespread reduction in oligodendrocyte density early in disease process.

HexA enzyme activity was well below normal in all brain areas, however notable increases in residual activity were noted in the caudal-most area of the cerebellum and in aspects of the spinal cord (Fig. 8A-C). Targeted lipidomics were performed on GM1 ganglioside (16:0, 18:0 and 20:0), GM2 ganglioside (16:0 18:0 and 20:0) and GM3 ganglioside (16:0 18:0 and 20:0) throughout the CNS (Fig. 8D). Mild to no increases in GM1 ganglioside were noted in all brain areas (data not shown). GM2 gangliosides 18:0 and 20:0 were increased to a similar degree in all brain areas, however GM2 20:0 had much higher levels in the thalamus (Th), brainstem (BS) cerebellum (CB) and cervical (CI) and lumbar intumescences (LI) as compared to the frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital cortices. GM3 species were elevated in the forebrain to a greater degree than the cerebellum and spinal cord.

TSD sheep have relatively high residual HexA activity, reach sexual maturity prior to endpoint and develop a hybrid of neurological signs, cognitive dysfunction, and distribution of pathological changes that is consistent with an attenuated form of TSD. These findings suggest that this model most closely represents juvenile or late onset Tay-Sachs. Here we performed an in-depth natural history study of untreated TSD sheep to shed light on pathophysiology of TSD, which is of special importance now because novel therapeutics are being administered to patients where post-mortem samples can be difficult to obtain.

The clinically relevant biomarkers outlined here are the same metrics used in patients with neurological diseases. CSF provides a minimally invasive way of inferring brain pathology and here we report increases in GM2 ganglioside in the CSF of TSD sheep. GM2 is elevated in TSD patients (Cynthia Tifft, personal communication) and, since it directly relates to the disease etiology, GM2 may qualify as a surrogate marker for future human clinical trials. Similar MRI and MRS findings have been well documented in all forms of human TSD and include demyelination (on structural MRI and an increase in choline on MRS), neuroaxonal loss (reduction in NAA), and increased markers of gliosis (myoinositol) (27–33). We also detected increased taurine on MRS, which has been previously shown in TSD patients to represent taurine conjugated GM2, a novel metabolite in TSD (25). These biomarkers clearly correlate with disease severity and histopathological findings, supporting their use in future preclinical and human clinical trials.

The clinical presentation of infantile Tay-Sachs disease is characterized by a perceived normal at birth that fails to reach or maintain developmental milestones (1, 4). Often the first clinical signs are weak muscles and inability to appropriately track with eyes, exaggerated startle, followed by development of profound hypotonia, seizures and progression to a semi-vegetative state (1, 4). In contrast, the juvenile form is highly variable in presentation and often starts with “clumsy” children with speech and mild cognitive impairments who can develop increased muscle tone, hypermetria, ataxia, and seizures that is followed by a semi-vegetative state (1, 6). In LOTS (late onset TSD), patients predominantly experience dysarthria, psychosis, ataxia, distal limb weakness, peripheral nerve dysfunction, mild cognitive disabilities, and oculomotor abnormalities, but patients can have a near normal lifespan (1, 7, 8, 34, 35). The TSD sheep split their neurological features between those of juvenile onset (cognitive dysfunction, ataxia, seizures) and late onset patients (ataxia, distal limb weakness, oculomotor changes and cerebellar features).

Infantile TSD patients exhibit diffusely enlarged neurons throughout the forebrain with loss of angular configuration, reduced Nissl substance, and eccentric nuclei (36). These neurons are so distended with ganglioside storage that it displaces/degenerates neurofibrils (36). In contrast, LOTS patients show little cortical atrophy with most neurons exhibiting a mildly swollen appearance (37), but in the cerebellum there is overt granular cell layer loss, Purkinje cell dropout, swollen dendrites, and microgliosis (37). Neurodegeneration was also noted to a lesser degree in the subcortical areas (37). We and others have previously described neuropathology in TSD sheep (38, 39), and observe changes similar to infantile in the forebrain, with less severe changes in the cerebellum.

Reduced myelination in infantile TSD was reported as patchy in the cerebral white matter, without evidence of breakdown, suggestive of hypomyelination (36). On MRI, reduced myelination appears profound and near complete in infantile patients (31–33), whereas in LOTS, reduced myelination is mild but diffuse in the cortex and cerebellum (37). Additional characteristic MRI findings of infantile GM2 gangliosidosis are mild T2 hyperintensity of the putamen and caudate nucleus with diffuse forebrain hypomyelination (53). In TSD sheep, myelination is moderately reduced primarily in the forebrain, which is again intermediate to infantile and LOTS. Hypomyelination is a predominant finding in infantile GM2 and likely relevant to the poor myelination seen in TSD sheep. Interestingly, oligodendrocyte number was also reduced, suggesting that oligodendrocyte dysfunction/loss may be a contributing factor in reduced myelination. To our knowledge there are no reports of reduced oligodendrocyte number in TSD patients, but this has been observed in infants and cats with GM1 gangliosidosis (40, 41). Reactive astrocytes are also noted diffusely in TSD patients (36) and this change is reflected in the TSD sheep. This differs from microglial activation, which primarily occurs in the gray matter of infantile TSD patients (36) and in cerebellum of LOTS patients (37). In TSD sheep microglial activation is observed globally, albeit to a greater degree in the forebrain.

In the TSD fetus, abnormal pathology is present in the neurons of the spinal cord and dorsal root ganglia but is not noted in the cerebrum (42, 43). We hypothesize that this happens because the cerebrum is the last to develop. Spinal cord degeneration is also a hallmark feature of LOTS, with the most prominent GM2 storage in the anterior horns and less in the brainstem, basal ganglia, and cortex (the cerebellum was not evaluated) (44). Spinal cord fiber loss is greatest in the fasciculus gracilis in LOTS, which receives sensory information from the lower limbs (44), but not present in the TSD sheep (Suppl. Fig 2). TSD sheep spinal cord pathology was noted at 3 months of age but was not as progressive as in the forebrain. It remains unknown if a similar slower progression of spinal cord pathology occurs in infantile or juvenile patients.

Both infantile TSD and more prominently LOTS patients have peripheral nervous system dysfunction with storage filled dorsal root ganglia, denervation atrophy of skeletal muscle cells, and reduced large fiber density (7, 45–47). Dorsal root ganglia in TSD sheep are also enlarged with intracytoplasmic storage material (44). Peripheral nervous system pathology was not evaluated in the TSD sheep in this study, but TSD sheep nerve conduction velocities and electromyography are within the normal range at humane endpoint (data not shown).

Storage of GM2 and other ganglioside metabolites in the brain of TSD patients has been thoroughly reported (37, 46, 48). In infantile TSD, the level of GM2 storage in the cortex and cerebellum is an order of magnitude higher than controls (46), and in the 22-week-old fetus GM2 is nearly double that of the control fetus and by death at 3 years and 5 years increases to 7 times or 380 times greater than control tissues, respectively (42, 49). In LOTS, there is a reported moderate 30% increase in the gray matter of the cerebral cortex, which corelates with the reported attenuated histopathological changes in that brain region compared to the infantile form (37). While the authors could not find GM2 quantification in the cerebellum and spinal cord for LOTS, a detailed histopathologic table of ganglioside distended neurons in the cerebellum varies from 5% - 20% in 4 adult onset GM2 patients (50). We have included a similar table for comparison (Suppl. Fig 3). Here we report the first spatial analysis of GM2 and GM3 ganglioside storage throughout the TSD brain. Elevations in GM2 and GM3 subspecies were distributed differently in various tissues. For example, GM2 18:0 is ubiquitously distributed in the TSD brain whereas GM2 20:0 appears to be at higher levels in the spinal cord. This may reflect differences in myelin content of the tissues assayed, but further spatial evaluation is needed. The differential distribution of ganglioside storage may prove to uncover aspects of lysosomal disease that are likely much more complicated than previously thought, as highlighted by our findings and the differential cellular distribution of ganglioside subspecies and other lipids by MALDI imaging in the SD mouse (51). These new data may prove to be important in future therapeutic testing.

Due to the incomplete information in the literature regarding the pathology of TSD in humans, and ongoing or upcoming new treatments for TSD, effective evaluation of therapeutic effect will require a better understanding of the pathological events occurring in the TSD brain. Here we provide longitudinal quantification of a subset of post-mortem changes in an intermediate form of TSD. These data are associated with enzymatic activity and ganglioside storage, to provide clinicians a better understanding of ongoing pathological events in their patients based on minimally invasive clinical tests like those described here.
