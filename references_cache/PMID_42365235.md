# Lessons in the Wake of a Lassa fever case in the Midwest U.S: epidemiology, management and preparedness gaps.
**Authors:** Sebastian SL, Maria Susana CP, Huber Said PZ, Karen B
**Journal:** BMC Infect Dis (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s12879-026-13875-6](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13875-6)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Lassa fever (LF) is a zoonotic viral hemorrhagic disease endemic to West Africa that remains persistently neglected despite substantial morbidity, mortality, and outbreak potential. A fixed burden estimate is not available due to the absence of systematic surveillance across endemic regions; passive case detection, limited laboratory capacity, and significant underreporting make precise quantification impossible. Contemporary modeling now projects approximately 2.7 million Lassa virus infections each year across West Africa, with an estimated 3,900 deaths and 2.0 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) over a decade - figures that likely still underestimate the true burden (6,86). While most infections are mild or asymptomatic, severe disease is associated with high case-fatality rates, nosocomial transmission, and long-term sequelae. The most recent travel-associated case in the Midwest U.S highlights the ongoing risk of international spread and exposes critical gaps in recognizing and responding to rare but high-consequence pathogens, even in high-resource settings.

METHODS: This narrative review synthesizes epidemiologic data, clinical studies, surveillance reports, and outbreak investigations to examine Lassa fever through the lens of outbreak detection, response, and preparedness.

RESULTS: Early clinical recognition remains difficult due to non-specific presentations and, in non-endemic countries, ignoring travel and exposure history. Non-specific presentation and limited diagnostic tools access contributes to delayed diagnosis and extensive contact investigations. Molecular diagnostics, particularly RT-PCR, remain central to early diagnosis but are influenced by viral genetic diversity and sampling timing. Ribavirin continues to be widely used for treatment and post-exposure prophylaxis despite limited and heterogeneous evidence. Emerging antivirals, monoclonal antibodies, and vaccine candidates show promise but are not yet available for routine use. Outbreak control is further complicated by the complex ecology of Lassa virus, including rodent reservoirs, environmental drivers, and potential involvement of additional host species.

CONCLUSIONS: Lassa fever remains a high-threat pathogen at the intersection of endemic disease and global outbreak preparedness. Travel-associated cases, like the most recent one in Iowa, consistently reveal gaps in infection prevention and control, exposure-risk assessment, and post-exposure management. Early suspicion, followed by immediate isolation, rapid engagement of public health authorities, and timely initiation of infection control and clinical measures is central to limiting downstream consequences. Addressing these gaps will require strengthened laboratory capacity, harmonized surveillance and response frameworks, accelerated development of medical countermeasures, and sustained investment in preparedness strategies applicable to both endemic and non-endemic settings.

Healthcare in 2019 was the last year we operated in a pre-pandemic climate. By 2020, a seemingly low-risk pathogen had forever changed the way we respond to outbreaks, serving as a cautionary tale of the widespread disruption that healthcare systems, economic and political structures, and above all, entire populations can face when unprepared [ 1 ]. This renewed awareness has placed neglected viral threats back into the spotlight, particularly those with zoonotic origins, limited treatment options, and the potential for international spread [ 1 , 2 ]. Among these threats is Lassa virus. Endemic to West Africa, and responsible for an estimated 100,000 to 300,000 infections annually (traditional estimates, pre-2020 modelling era), Lassa fever remains vastly underdiagnosed due to nonspecific clinical features and limited diagnostic capacity in resource-constrained regions [ 3 , 4 ]. The high case-fatality rate among hospitalized patients, potential for nosocomial outbreaks, and inclusion as a Category A bioterrorism agent by the U.S. Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) elevates its relevance in the context of post-pandemic threat preparedness [ 2– 5 ].

In the fall of 2024, Lassa virus once again crossed an ocean within a single patient: a man returning from construction work in rural Liberia arrived at an Iowa hospital with fever. This was the United States’ ninth imported case since 1969 [ 6– 8 ]. Despite the small number and its spanning five decades, these cases consistently expose the same faulty lines including delayed clinical suspicion, challenges in accessing diagnostic tools, and large contact tracing operations to protect healthcare workers and communities. This narrative review aims to examine Lassa as a high-threat pathogen at the intersection of endemic disease and global health security. Using the most recent U.S. travel-associated case in Iowa as a contemporary anchor, we synthesize historical experience with imported cases, summarize current approaches to outbreak detection and control, and highlight key gaps in diagnostics, clinical management, post-exposure prophylaxis, and preparedness frameworks that are relevant to future responses to rare but high-consequence pathogens.

This narrative review has four main objectives: (i) describe Lassa fever as a high-threat pathogen in the context of global health security; (ii) to synthesize available evidence on travel-associated Lassa fever cases, with particular attention to the 2024 Iowa case as the most recent example from a high-resource, non-endemic setting; (iii) examine key elements of outbreak detection, clinical management, and infection prevention and control (IPC); and (iv) to review the current and potential future roles of ribavirin for treatment and post-exposure prophylaxis, identifying remaining gaps in preparedness for Lassa virus and other rare, high-consequence pathogens.

This article is a narrative review that combines published evidence on Lassa fever with experiential insight derived from the management of an imported case diagnosed in the United States in October 2024.

We performed a non-systematic literature search in PubMed/MEDLINE and Embase for articles published January 1969 up to November 28th of 2025, using combinations of keywords and MeSH terms including “Lassa fever”, “Lassa virus”, “Arenaviridae infections”, “viral hemorrhagic fever”, “outbreak response”, “disease outbreaks”, “travel-associated infection”, “imported infection”, “post-exposure prophylaxis”, “ribavirin”, and “preparedness”. PubMed/MEDLINE and Embase were selected as the primary databases because they provide the most comprehensive coverage of biomedical and infectious disease literature relevant to this review’s clinical and epidemiologic scope, including indexed grey literature from major public health agencies. Additional references were identified by hand-searching the reference lists of key articles and relevant reviews retrieved through the database search, as well as through authoritative grey literature sources (e.g. WHO, Africa CDC, CDC, and national public health agency guidance documents). We included peer-reviewed publications (original research, case reports/series, observational studies, clinical trials, and narrative or scoping reviews) as well as authoritative grey literature (e.g. WHO, Africa CDC, CDC, and national public health agency guidance) that addressed at least one of the following domains: (i) Lassa fever epidemiology in endemic regions; (ii) imported or travel-associated cases; (iii) outbreak investigation and infection prevention and control; (iv) clinical management and supportive care; (v) prophylactic or therapeutic use of ribavirin and other agents; or (vi) vaccine and preparedness initiatives. Articles not available in English, conference abstracts without sufficient methodological detail, and reports without clear relevance to outbreak control or preparedness were excluded.

In addition to the literature review, this manuscript incorporates experiential insight from one of the authors, who worked closely with the clinical and public health teams involved in the assessment and management of the 2024 travel-associated Lassa fever case in Iowa. Insights from this encounter were used to contextualize published evidence on outbreak recognition, risk stratification of contacts, infection prevention and control, and coordination between hospital-based and public health personnel in a high-resource setting.

Lassa fever (LF) is a zoonotic viral hemorrhagic fever endemic to West Africa, with the highest documented burden in Nigeria, Sierra Leone, Liberia, and Guinea. In 2024, according to the WHO and Africa’s CDC, traditional estimates suggest that 100,000–300,000 infections and approximately 5,000 deaths occur each year [ 3 , 4 ]. However, recent transmission and vaccine-impact models[ 6– 8 ] indicate that the true burden is likely substantially higher, with some projections approaching up to 900,000 infections and over 200,000 hospitalizations annually [ 9– 11 ]. These findings, highlighted in the critical review by Besson [ 12 ], underscore the extent to which limited surveillance, underdiagnosis, and incomplete reporting have historically obscured the true scale of LF in endemic regions. The accuracy and completeness of existing surveillance data remain constrained: passive case detection, restricted laboratory capacity, and weak health information systems result in substantial underreporting of both cases and deaths. A recent analysis estimated that reported cases represent only approximately 58% of expected severe cases, highlighting the magnitude of undetected burden [ 11 , 12 , 86 ]. Lassa virus is maintained in nature primarily by Mastomys natalensis, the multimammate rat, which sheds viruses in urine and feces and brings infection into human households through contamination of food, surfaces, and dust [ 11 , 12 ]. Person-to-person transmission also occurs, via direct contact with fluid or secretions from infected persons. Across endemic regions, human infection risk is strongly shaped by housing quality, food storage practices, and rodent infestation of dwellings, as well as environmental change, such as deforestation, agricultural expansion, and climate variability that alters rodent abundance and brings reservoir species into closer contact with communities [ 12 , 13 ].

Within this endemic belt, Nigeria currently accounts for most reported Lassa fever cases, with recurrent outbreaks and a marked increase in laboratory-confirmed cases over the last decade. From 2020 to 2023, more than 4,000 confirmed cases and over 700 deaths were reported across 34 of the country’s 37 states, underscoring both the breadth and severity of transmission [ 14 ]. In Sierra Leone, a recent case–control study of 37 laboratory-confirmed cases reported a 62% case-fatality rate and identified recent household rodent presence and housing conditions as major risk factors, with households keeping cats reporting fewer rodents and a trend toward lower Lassa fever occurrence [ 15 ].

The ecology of LASV transmission is shaped by its rodent reservoirs and surrounding environmental change. Although Mastomys natalensis remains the principal reservoir, recent field investigations have identified LASV or LASV antibodies in additional rodent hosts such as Mastomys erythroleucus and Hylomyscus pamfi, as well as in domestic animals in some settings, suggesting a broader ecological niche than previously appreciated [ 13 , 16 ]. Systematic reviews of environmental drivers further link LF risk to deforestation, land-use change, agricultural expansion, and climate-sensitive factors such as rainfall patterns, which influence rodent population dynamics and the frequency of human–rodent contact [ 12 , 13 ]. In Guinea, longitudinal rodent and environmental studies demonstrate that Lassa virus circulation is tightly linked to seasonal rainfall, Mastomys natalensis abundance, and village-level land-use patterns, with higher rodent infection prevalence during the rainy season and persistent high-risk foci in rural communities [ 13 ].

Modelling studies synthesizing seroprevalence and outbreak data indicate that tens of millions of people in West Africa live in areas environmentally suitable for LASV transmission, with Nigeria bearing the highest projected burden [ 9 , 10 , 13 ]. Estimates of the basic reproductive number (R₀) for LF are modestly above 1, consistent with sustained but geographically focal transmission that is highly sensitive to local ecological and social conditions [ 10 ].

In parallel, LF has increasingly been framed as a priority pathogen for medical countermeasure development and pandemic preparedness. Recent research-agenda papers and coalition roadmaps have emphasized its combination of substantial endemic burden, high case fatality among hospitalized patients, potential for nosocomial spread, and lack of licensed vaccines, leading to its prioritization by WHO and the Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations (CEPI) for accelerated development of diagnostics, therapeutics, and vaccines, including within the “100 Days Mission” framework [ 11 , 12 ]. This prioritization reflects not only its regional impact but also its potential for international spread through travel and its relevance as a prototype high-threat zoonotic pathogen.

Although the overwhelming majority of LF cases occur in West Africa, travel-associated infections have been sporadically reported in non-endemic regions, including Europe, North America, and, most recently, Asia. These imported cases, which often present as nonspecific febrile illnesses in returning travelers, have triggered intensive public-health responses because of concerns about delayed recognition and health-care–associated transmission. The first imported case of LF in China, reported in a traveler returning from West Africa, and since 1969, nine travel-associated cases have been confirmed in the United States, involving varied clinical settings, routes of exposure, and outcomes [ 15 , 17 , 85 ]. These examples across the globe provide a historical backdrop against which the most recent case in Iowa cannot be understood as an isolated event, but as part of a recurring pattern that continues to test outbreak recognition and illustrate how even a single imported infection can strain diagnostic capacity, infection-prevention systems, and contact-tracing infrastructure in high-resource settings [ 8 , 17 ].

Lassa fever has a broad clinical spectrum, ranging from asymptomatic infection to fulminant multi-organ failure. It is estimated that up to 80% of infections are mild or clinically silent, whereas roughly one in five progresses to severe disease with involvement of the liver, kidneys, and central nervous system. The incubation period for LF typically ranges from 2 to 21 days, with symptoms beginning at a median of 7–10 days after exposure. Symptoms start gradually with non-specific prodrome of fever, malaise, and generalized weakness that is often indistinguishable from other febrile illnesses [ 3 , 26 , 27 ]. As LF progresses, patients may develop sore throat, arthralgias, chest pain, cough, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea, with varying combinations of respiratory and gastrointestinal symptoms [ 26 , 27 ].

In severe cases, LF is characterized by signs of endothelial damage, vascular leak syndrome and end-organ dysfunction. Progressive hypotension, facial and neck edema, pleural effusions, and pulmonary edema are frequently reported, alongside laboratory evidence of hepatic injury, coagulopathy, and renal impairment [ 26– 28 ]. Hemorrhagic manifestations including mucosal bleeding, hematemesis, melena, or vaginal bleeding occur in a minority of patients but are consistently associated with poor prognosis [ 26 , 27 , 29 ]. Neurologic complications such as confusion, seizures, tremor, and encephalopathy generally emerge in later stages of illness and are markers of advanced disease Among hospitalized patients with severe Lassa fever, reported case-fatality rates commonly range from around 10% to 30%, depending on the cohort, clinical stage at presentation, quality of supportive care, and access to timely antiviral therapy [ 3 , 26 , 28 ].

Children and pregnant women represent particularly vulnerable groups. Classical and contemporary cohorts show that pediatric Lassa fever spans the full spectrum from mild febrile illness to shock and multi-organ failure, with the highest mortality in the very young [ 30– 32 ]. In the LASCOPE pediatric cohort in Nigeria, 124 hospitalized Reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) confirmed cases, fever and gastrointestinal symptoms predominated, and death was concentrated in infants, with a case-fatality rate of ~ 22% in children < 12 months compared with ~ 3% in older children [ 32 ]. In pregnancy, Lassa fever is consistently associated with very poor fetal and maternal outcomes: Third-trimester infection frequently results in fetal loss rates approaching 80–95% and higher maternal case-fatality than in non-pregnant adults [ 33 , 34 ] underscoring the need to include pregnant women and infants in clinical management algorithms and future therapeutic and vaccine trials.

Long-term sequelae distinguish Lassa fever from some viral hemorrhagic fevers. Sensorineural hearing loss is one of the most characteristic complications, reported in roughly one-third of survivors in some cohorts, including individuals who experienced relatively mild acute illness Hearing loss may be unilateral or bilateral, is frequently permanent, and carries substantial educational, social, and economic consequences in endemic communities [ 29 , 35 , 36 ]. Other reported sequelae include persistent asthenia, neurocognitive complaints, psychological distress, and residual neurologic deficits, although systematic data remain limited [ 26 , 27 , 29 ].

From an infection prevention and control perspective, the clinical profile of Lassa fever presents several challenges. The early phase is dominated by non-specific symptoms in settings where febrile illness is ubiquitous, which can delay clinical suspicion, testing, and appropriate infection-prevention measures. Diagnostic delay, particularly in returning travelers to non-endemic healthcare settings who may be less familiar with the disease, also occurs [ 26 ].

The early phase of Lassa fever is clinically indistinguishable from other common febrile illnesses such as malaria, typhoid, and other viral hemorrhagic fevers. This non-specific presentation makes clinical diagnosis alone unreliable, or at least greatly delays it. This reinforces the need for early suspicion and recognition, by obtaining adequate travel and exposure history, followed by laboratory confirmation to guide individual patient care and deploy appropriate infection-prevention and surveillance measures [ 37 ].

RT-PCR remains the cornerstone of acute Lassa fever diagnosis, allowing direct detection of viral RNA in blood and other body fluids [ 37– 39 ]. In published evaluations, Lassa virus (LASV) RT-PCR assays have specificity ≥ 98% and sensitivity approximately 80–90% in early acute-phase samples with adequate viral load when compared with reference assays or composite clinical/serologic standards [ 39 , 40 ]. In routine practice, clinical samples are inactivated before testing, permitting RT-PCR to be performed safely in Biosafety (BSL) level 2 and 3 facilities, while working with live virus itself generally requires BSL-4 containment [ 39 ]. The substantial genetic diversity of Lassa virus across West Africa can impair assay performance through primer–template mismatches, particularly for lineage-specific assays. This has driven the development of pan-LASV RT-PCR platforms designed to maintain sensitivity across multiple lineages [ 37 , 39 ]. Beyond simple detection, RT-PCR also provides prognostic information. Analyses from the LASCOPE cohorts have shown that lower cycle threshold (Ct) values are strongly associated with mortality in both adults and children, supporting the use of Ct as a surrogate for viral load and as a tool for risk stratification in clinical and research settings [ 32 , 41 ]. At the health-system level, Nigerian surveillance data have identified clinical predictors such as fever combined with gastrointestinal or neurologic symptoms that increase the pre-test probability of a positive RT-PCR result, offering a pragmatic basis for targeted testing algorithms in resource-limited settings [ 42 ].

Serologic assays based on ELISA remain important for both clinical and epidemiologic purposes. IgM antibodies can be detected 9 to 12 days post-infection, indicating recent infection, whereas IgG detection reflects prior exposure. Prolonged IgM persistence and delayed seroconversion limit the utility of using antibody results in the earliest phase of illness for diagnostic purposes [ 39 , 43 ]. ELISA testing detects circulating viral nucleoprotein, and positive antigen results then provide more timely evidence of active infection. ELISA in combination with RT-PCR are commonly used in diagnostic algorithms at reference centers [ 39 , 44 ]. A systematic review of diagnostic accuracy studies found that combined antigen/IgM ELISA formats substantially outperformed IgM-only assays, with pooled sensitivity and specificity around 88% and 90%, respectively, compared with 31% and 95.7% for IgM alone [ 40 ]. These findings support the use of multiplex or combined antigen/antibody platforms where available, particularly for triage and outbreak investigation in endemic settings.

Point-of-care rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), including lateral flow immunoassays such as ReLASV® Pan-Lassa antigen rapid diagnostic test (formerly Zalgen Labs, Frederick, MD, USA; now discontinued following the company’s cessation of operations) have been developed to enable near-patient testing in resource-limited settings [ 37 ]. While early evaluations reported promising performance, subsequent field data have highlighted variability in sensitivity, especially for certain viral lineages and in patients with lower viral loads [ 37 , 40 ]. These limitations underline the need for continuous evaluation of RDTs against circulating strains and for their use as part of tiered diagnostic algorithms, rather than as stand-alone confirmatory tools.

Higher-throughput platforms such as magnetic bead-based multiplex assays (e.g., the Luminex MAGPIX® system) have been used in reference laboratories to simultaneously measure LASV antigens and antibodies, supporting serosurveillance and research on immune responses with greater sensitivity and multiplexing capacity than conventional ELISA [ 37 ]. However, these technologies remain largely confined to specialized research centers and are not yet widely available for routine clinical use in endemic settings.

Evidence from national surveillance data in Nigeria shows that certain clinical constellations, especially fever combined with neurologic manifestations and proteinuria, carry particularly high odds of RT-PCR positivity, and could be incorporated into refined testing algorithms to optimize use of limited resources [ 42 ]. At the same time, historical and contemporary analyses of imported cases demonstrate persistent diagnostic delays: a systematic review of 50 years of travel-associated Lassa fever reported a median interval of approximately two weeks between symptom onset and diagnosis, with viral RNA often still detectable in urine and post-mortem samples [ 45 ]. Outbreak investigations in Kenema, Sierra Leone, similarly describe delays in recognizing Lassa fever despite classic signs, with late diagnosis contributing to high case fatality [ 46 ].

Across these studies, a recurring theme is the lack of standardized clinical documentation and outcome measures, which hampers comparison of diagnostic tools across sites and time. A systematic review of clinical Lassa fever literature highlighted substantial heterogeneity in symptom recording, case definitions, and endpoints, and called for harmonized core data sets to support future diagnostic and therapeutic trials [ 47 ]. Integrated algorithms combining RT-PCR, antigen detection, and context-specific case definitions supported by external quality assurance and linked to surveillance systems have been shown to reduce time to diagnosis and improve outbreak responsiveness but remain unevenly implemented [ 11 , 44 , 48 ].

In addition to currently available RT-PCR and ELISA-based tools, several newer platforms are being developed for Lassa fever diagnosis (Table 2), ranging from improved rapid antigen tests and multiplex syndromic panels to Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-based assays and host-derived biomarkers [ 37 ].

Lassa fever control remains a major challenge in endemic countries, where limited health-system capacity, and gaps in IPC efforts amplify disease spread in health-care settings and households [ 12 , 29 , 55 ]. Human-to-human transmission is generally less efficient than zoonotic spillover, but once severe cases reach hospitals, high viral loads, invasive procedures, and inconsistent use of personal protective equipment (PPE) can drive nosocomial clusters. In this context, the World Health Organization and other expert groups emphasize a multifaceted approach to outbreak control, combining early case detection, strengthened surveillance, rigorous IPC practices, and targeted rodent- and environment-focused interventions [ 12 , 29 , 56 ]. In several Nigerian outbreaks, community-based risk communication, social mobilization, and active case finding have also been central to interrupting transmission chains and improving care-seeking behavior, particularly in rural settings with limited formal health coverage [ 12 , 55 ]. Two illustrative examples from endemic settings demonstrate these principles in practice. In Kenema District, Sierra Leone, a 2019 outbreak involving two pediatric cases was initially unrecognized due to non-specific febrile presentations and was only confirmed following hemorrhagic decompensation; the investigation that followed reinforced community sensitization and improved clinical index of suspicion at local health facilities [ 44 ]. In Nigeria, where annual LF outbreaks are managed through a coordinated national framework involving the Nigeria Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (NCDC), state rapid response teams, and designated Lassa fever treatment centers, key lessons have consistently included the importance of early case identification, standardized contact tracing protocols, and sustained healthcare worker training in PPE use and infection control [ 11 , 54 ].

Rodent and environmental control remain central but difficult components of LF outbreak response. Mastomys natalensis and related reservoir species are highly prolific, adapt well to human dwellings, and exploit poor housing quality, unprotected food storage, and inadequate waste management [ 13 ]. Structural vulnerabilities such as porous walls, open eaves, and limited rodent-proof food storage mean that simple recommendations (e.g. trapping, improved storage) are often hard to implement consistently in resource-constrained communities. Recent work has expanded the ecological picture: detection of LASV infection or antibodies in additional rodent hosts and domestic animals such as goats, cattle, and lizards suggest a broader multi-host system that complicates control strategies focused solely on Mastomys spp [ 13 , 16 ]. Environmental and climatic drivers, including deforestation, agricultural expansion, and rainfall-linked changes in rodent abundance, further modulate transmission risk over time and space [ 13 ].

Mathematical models have been used to explore how different interventions could alter outbreak trajectories. A recent fractional-order model incorporating a dual-dosage vaccination strategy showed that high coverage with a two-dose human vaccine could substantially reduce the basic reproduction number and prevent outbreaks from becoming entrenched, supporting the idea that vaccination could become a cornerstone of LF control once safe and effective vaccines are licensed [ 57 ]. Although these findings are theoretical and no vaccine is currently available for routine use, they complement empirical observations that earlier diagnosis, isolation, strict IPC, and timely access to ribavirin, for both treatment of confirmed cases and post-exposure prophylaxis in high-risk contacts, materially reduce secondary transmission in hospital and community settings [ 12 , 29 , 55 ].

Taken together, these data reinforce that effective Lassa fever outbreak control cannot rely on a single intervention. Sustained progress will require long-term investment in laboratory and surveillance capacity, standardized IPC training and supplies, and community-engaged risk communication, embedded within a One Health framework that explicitly integrates human, animal, and environmental surveillance [ 56 ]. These same structural and coordination challenges have implications beyond endemic regions where imported cases of Lassa fever into high-resource settings, still require rapid recognition, clear IPC protocols, and close coordination between hospitals, public health agencies, and experts in the field. This is repeatedly demonstrated in travel-associated cases in Europe, North America, and Asia, including the most recent fatal case in Iowa [ 8 , 17 , 29 , 45 ].

Controlling LF spread in a high-resource setting focuses less on environmental controls and more on limiting person-to-person transmission. At the core of mitigation efforts is the 3-step approach to “identify, isolate, and inform” (Fig. 1). To identify a patient with LF requires eliciting a detailed travel and exposure history: arguably the single most important and most frequently overlooked step in non-endemic settings. For any patient presenting with unexplained febrile illness, clinicians must ask specifically about travel to West Africa, including country visited, rural or forested areas, housing conditions, and potential contact with rodents or ill individuals. In returning travelers, the median interval from symptom onset to diagnosis has been approximately two weeks, with delayed or incomplete travel history elicitation identified as a major contributing factor [ 45 ]. Details of what the patient did and where they went while in an endemic region: occupational exposures, attendance at funerals, healthcare contact, are equally critical for exposure-risk stratification. Isolating the person under investigation (PUI) requires an airborne infection isolation room (AIIR) or at minimum, separation from others in a private space. These precautions are relevant in endemic regions as well, where airborne isolation is often unavailable and improvised barriers are necessary. Healthcare personnel (HCP) caring for a PUI or confirmed case should don PPE appropriate to the patient’s clinical status per CDC VHF PPE guidance: for a clinically stable patient, this includes gown, gloves, eye protection, and face mask; for a clinically unstable patient (e.g., rapid deterioration, hemodynamic instability, or requirement for intubation), an N95 respirator replaces the face mask, and omission of any component results in a high-risk exposure classification [ 8 ]. This level of protection substantially exceeds standard contact or droplet precautions and is an important educational point for clinicians in both endemic and non-endemic settings. Informing others addresses the importance of clear communication when an infectious agent is a potential threat. This includes the patient and setting their expectations of care, their caregivers and loved ones on any policies that may impede their visitation, hospital/facility leadership, IPC, and notification to public health officials.

In the imported Iowa case, once the patient became a PUI for LF, the Level 1, or Regional Emerging Special Pathogen Treatment Center (RESPTC) was informed and the team from Nebraska traveled to the patient’s bedside to assist local management. The dynamic between Iowa’s Level 2 and the RESPTC center allowed for mitigation efforts that ultimately protected HCP at a time that the patient’s viral load was highest. The patient was managed under “wet” PPE precautions and in a dedicated special pathogen unit that was essential to the workflow and safety of the HCP. Ultimately, no subsequent LF cases were reported [ 8 ].

Germany’s experience in handling an imported LF case relates a similar story but provides a deeper understanding by a serologic survey [ 58 ]. One physician who had not been wearing PPE, had skin to skin exposure while starting an intravenous line and performing phlebotomy, as well as respiratory and mucosal exposure during a cough that occurred while examining the patient’s throat. This physician received ribavirin prophylaxis approximately 36 h after exposure. They converted to positive serology but remained asymptomatic. A second experience in Germany during which an LF patient was only cared for approximately 10 h, resulted in only one secondary case [ 59 ]. A mortician who handled the index patient’s remains developed symptoms, tested positive for LASV by PCR, and received ribavirin. The mortician recovered without complications, highlighting both the occupational risks associated with post-mortem exposure and the critical role of timely recognition and infection prevention measures in limiting severe outcomes.

In the current panorama, acute LF management rests on two pillars: aggressive supportive care and, where available, antiviral therapy with ribavirin. Supportive care includes hemodynamic monitoring, fluid and electrolyte management, oxygen supplementation, treatment of secondary bacterial infections, and management of complications such as acute kidney injury, shock, and bleeding diathesis [ 26 , 29 , 55 ]. In observational cohorts, improved access to intensive care, early recognition of organ dysfunction, and protocolized supportive management have been associated with lower in-hospital mortality, even in the absence of major changes in antiviral options [ 41 ].

Since the 1980s, intravenous ribavirin has been considered the standard antiviral treatment for Lassa fever, based largely on data from McCormick and colleagues in Sierra Leone, who reported reduced mortality among patients with high aminotransferase levels or high viremia treated early in the course of illness [ 60 ]. Subsequent observational studies and re-analyses have suggested that ribavirin may confer benefit when initiated within the first 6–7 days of symptom onset, particularly in patients with markers of severe disease, but the magnitude and consistency of this effect remain uncertain [ 61– 63 ] since a recent systematic re-evaluation of the original ribavirin data highlighted substantial risk of bias, non-randomized comparison groups, and heterogeneity in dosing regimens, raising important concerns about the strength of the evidence base [ 62 , 63 ].

Despite the paucity of data and no other better alternatives, ribavirin continues to be recommended in many national and international guidelines for patients with confirmed or strongly suspected Lassa fever, especially those with severe disease [ 3 , 11 , 12 ]. Viral load kinetics studies from Nigeria have shown that higher baseline viral RNA levels and slower decline during treatment are associated with poor outcomes, suggesting that combining ribavirin administration with early virologic assessment may help therapeutic decision-making [ 64 ].

In children, ribavirin use is largely extrapolated from adult experience, and pediatric-specific evidence remains limited. In the LASCOPE prospective cohort of Nigerian children with RT-PCR–confirmed Lassa fever, mortality was highest in infants under 12 months, particularly those with hypotension and multi-organ dysfunction [ 32 ]. These findings emphasize the importance of early diagnosis and aggressive supportive care in young infants, but optimal pediatric dosing, safety monitoring, and long-term outcomes of ribavirin remain major evidence gaps [ 26 , 32 ].

Ribavirin is generally avoided in early pregnancy because of teratogenicity concerns from animal models and experience in other indications, yet in practice is often used given high maternal risk [ 33 , 62 ]. Recent observational reports from West Africa describe third-trimester use of ribavirin as part of intensive management, but robust comparative data on benefits versus harms in pregnancy are lacking [ 65 ]. Current expert opinion therefore favors individualized, multidisciplinary risk–benefit decision-making, usually prioritizing maternal survival in severe disease while acknowledging the very low quality of evidence to guide therapy [ 11 , 26 , 29 ].

After four decades of depending almost exclusively on ribavirin, Lassa fever therapeutics are finally entering a transition phase, with new antivirals and monoclonal antibodies moving from the laboratory into clinical evaluation. Favipiravir, a broad-spectrum RNA polymerase inhibitor, has shown protective effects in animal models and is being studied in clinical trials comparing oral favipiravir with standard ribavirin regimens in West Africa [ 67 , 68 ]. Preliminary data suggest acceptable safety profiles, but definitive evidence of superiority or non-inferiority in reducing mortality is still pending [ 11 ].

Monoclonal antibody therapies targeting the Lassa virus glycoprotein complex have demonstrated potent protective efficacy in non-human primate models, even when administered several days after infection [ 69 , 70 ]. A three-antibody cocktail (Arevirumab-3) and other candidate mAb combinations are progressing through preclinical development and early-phase clinical planning and are highlighted in recent research roadmaps as promising future options for both treatment and post-exposure prophylaxis [ 71 , 72 ]. However, issues of manufacturing capacity, cost, cold-chain requirements, and deployment in fragile health systems remain major obstacles to their near-term implementation [ 29 ].

In addition, anti-inflammatory adjuncts such as corticosteroids have been explored in small case series, where dexamethasone was co-administered with ribavirin in severe disease with apparent clinical improvement, but these observations are insufficient to support routine use and require confirmation in controlled studies [ 73 ]. Overall, the therapeutic landscape is in transition: ribavirin remains the pragmatic frontline option in endemic settings, but its role will likely evolve as new antivirals and immune-based therapies are formally evaluated [ 11 , 62 ].

Ribavirin has long been considered a candidate agent for post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) following high-risk exposures to Lassa virus, based largely on extrapolation from treatment data and limited observational evidence [ 60 , 61 ]. Early CDC and expert recommendations suggested short oral courses of ribavirin for laboratory and healthcare worker exposures with substantial risk of infection, although no randomized trials of PEP have been conducted and the balance of benefit versus toxicity remains uncertain [ 11 , 45 , 74 ]. Subsequent reviews have emphasized gastrointestinal side effects, teratogenic concerns, and low adherence as major barriers to widespread use of ribavirin PEP [ 62 , 63 ].

Recent experiences during outbreak responses and imported-case investigations illustrate these challenges. In the 2024 Iowa travel-associated Lassa fever case, 105 high-risk contacts without contraindications were offered oral ribavirin prophylaxis, yet only five initiated PEP and four discontinued early because of adverse effects such as nausea, leaving just one individual who completed the full 10-day course [ 8 ]. Similar patterns of low uptake and early discontinuation have been reported in Nigerian outbreak settings, where risk perception, concerns about side effects, pregnancy status, and limited counseling time all influence acceptance of ribavirin PEP among healthcare workers and household contacts [ 12 , 42 ].

Current national guidelines in several endemic countries recommend considering ribavirin PEP for clearly defined high-risk exposures, such as mucous membrane contact with infectious body fluids or unprotected participation in aerosol-generating procedures on confirmed cases while discouraging routine use after low-risk contacts [ 3 , 4 ]. However, implementation rates are highly variable, and many facilities lack standardized exposure-risk assessment tools, clear counseling materials, and mechanisms to monitor adherence and adverse events [ 11 , 56 ]. There is also no consensus on the optimal oral dosing regimen, duration of PEP, or the role of laboratory monitoring during prophylaxis [ 62 , 63 ].

From a preparedness standpoint, the continued dependence on ribavirin for both treatment and PEP exposes several therapeutic readiness gaps. Access to parenteral and oral formulations is uneven across endemic regions, with unavailability and reliance on emergency imports during large outbreaks [ 11 , 12 , 29 ]. The evidence base for PEP is particularly weak, and new agents, such as favipiravir and monoclonal antibodies, are not yet available for routine use and will require careful evaluation of feasibility, cost, and equity once clinical efficacy is established [ 70 , 71 ]. Taken together, these limitations underscore the need to strengthen standardized treatment protocols, secure reliable ribavirin supply chains, develop clear and ethically grounded PEP policies, and accelerate trials of new antivirals and antibody-based interventions as core components of Lassa fever preparedness in both endemic and non-endemic settings.

Despite decades of research, no Lassa fever vaccine has yet been licensed for human use. A 2023 scoping review by Sulis and colleagues identified four candidates that have entered clinical evaluation: INO-4500 (DNA vaccine), MV-LASV (measles-vectored), rVSVΔG-LASV-GPC (recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus vector), and EBS-LASV (inactivated platform) but most remain in early-phase trials with immunogenicity, rather than efficacy, as the primary endpoint (75). This landscape, while encouraging, underscores how LF continues to lag other priority pathogens in terms of advanced clinical development and licensure.

The most clinically advanced candidate to date is the rVSVΔG-LASV-GPC vaccine, developed in partnership with the International AIDS Vaccine Initiative and regional African collaborators. Preclinical work has shown rapid and durable protection in non-human primates against lethal challenge with genetically diverse LASV strains. Phase 1 studies have demonstrated an acceptable safety profile and robust humoral and cellular responses, and a Phase 2 program in West Africa (e.g. the LEAP4WA platform) is now underway, supported by CEPI and other partners. It will assess immunogenicity across broader age groups and in endemic settings [ 76 , 77 ]. Outcomes like severe disease, or mortality are still lacking, and the optimal dosing strategy (single-dose vs. booster) remains under evaluation.

Other vaccine candidates are progressing more slowly but add valuable platform diversity. The INO-4500 DNA vaccine has completed first-in-human testing, with early data suggesting that it is well tolerated and capable of inducing LASV-specific cellular responses [ 75 ]. A measles-vectored LASV vaccine (MV-LASV) and the EBS-LASV inactivated candidate have also entered phase 1 evaluation, and updated reviews highlight additional preclinical platforms including live-attenuated, viral-vector, and emerging mRNA approaches, now being supported under CEPI’s Lassa portfolio [ 75 , 78 , 79 ]. Collectively, these programs reflect a deliberate strategy to balance speed (leveraging proven outbreak platforms such as rVSV) with longer-term goals of safety, durability, and suitability for endemic country use.

Across this pipeline, several scientific gaps remain critical. First, correlates of protection for Lassa fever are not yet fully defined: neutralizing antibody titers, non-neutralizing Fc-mediated responses, and T-cell immunity all appear important, but their relative contributions to clinical protection in humans are unknown [ 75 , 79 ]. Second, the marked genetic diversity of LASV across lineages raises questions about cross-protection; most candidates target the glycoprotein complex, but real-world performance against divergent strains and across different endemic foci has not been established [ 75 ]. Third, data on duration of protection and the need for boosters especially in populations with repeated exposure risk such as healthcare workers, laboratory staff, and residents of hyperendemic areas are still very limited.

Finally, equity and access considerations are central to the remaining gaps. The 2025 Lassa Vaccine Coalition technical brief, led by Africa CDC and partners, emphasizes that any successful vaccine must be affordable, thermostable, and deliverable through existing immunization infrastructure in endemic countries, with sustainable manufacturing and procurement mechanisms to avoid the inequities seen during COVID-19 [ 4 ]. Regulatory preparedness, pathways for emergency use listing, and the integration of Lassa vaccines into broader outbreak-response architectures (including stockpiling and regional allocation) are still in early stages of development[ 80 ].

From a policy implementation perspective, recent work defined “use cases” and projected demand for future Lassa vaccines. Expert consultations with West African stakeholders suggest that early deployment will likely focus on targeted groups (e.g. healthcare workers, high-risk communities in hyperendemic districts, and possibly outbreak-responsive ring vaccination), rather than immediate mass vaccination of entire national populations [ 80 ]. However, modelling and demand-forecasting exercises also highlight major uncertainties, including baseline incidence estimates, health-system capacity to deliver adult vaccines, and community acceptance in settings where Lassa fever is feared but often poorly understood.

Taken all together, the Lassa vaccine field is at an inflection point: for the first time, several clinically advanced candidates exist, supported by coordinated regional and global initiatives, yet key questions about breadth of protection, priority populations, delivery models, and long-term access remain unresolved. For high-threat pathogens such as Lassa virus, vaccines will be central to future outbreak management, but their impact will ultimately depend on whether scientific advances are matched by sustained investment in trial infrastructure, regulatory alignment, implementation research, and mechanisms that ensure that populations most affected by Lassa fever are the first to benefit from any licensed product.

Lassa virus occupies an unusual position at the intersection of neglected tropical disease and biodefense concern. In U.S. and European preparedness frameworks, viral hemorrhagic fevers (VHF) are explicitly listed among the highest-priority biological threats. CDC guidance on bioterrorism groups arenaviruses (e.g. LF) together with filoviruses (Ebola, Marburg) in the Category A tier which implies a high potential to cause severe disease, public panic, and major health-system disruption if deliberately released [ 81 , 82 ]. Features that are undesirable in nature but attractive from a development of biologic weapons standpoint include the ability to spread via aerosols, a relatively low infectious dose, an incubation period compatible with travel and geographic spread of disease, and the absence of a licensed vaccine [ 27 ]. More recent analyses from military and disaster-medicine perspectives describe Lassa virus as a candidate bioterrorism agent capable of seeding focal outbreaks in non-endemic regions, particularly in unprepared health systems, and emphasize that even a small number of deliberate or accidental introductions could trigger intensive contact-tracing and disruption of hospital operations [ 83 ].

At the same time, contemporary biodefense and emerging-pathogen experts caution against sensationalizing this risk. There is no evidence that Lassa virus has ever been successfully weaponized or deliberately used, and significant technical barriers such as need for specialized laboratories, biosafety constraints, and environmental fragility outside controlled conditions limit its feasibility as a large-scale weapon compared with other bacterial agents [ 84 ]. Instead, Lassa virus is increasingly framed as a prototype high-threat zoonotic pathogen whose combination of persistent endemic transmission, potential for international spread, high case fatality among hospitalized patients, and capacity to overwhelm local health systems justifies its inclusion in global “high-consequence pathogen” agendas. From this perspective, its bioterrorism relevance lies less in speculative scenarios of deliberate misuse and more in the reality that any failure to invest in surveillance, diagnostics, medical countermeasures, and outbreak-response capacity for Lassa fever leaves the world vulnerable not only to naturally occurring epidemics, but also to the consequences of laboratory accidents or intentional release in a globally connected society.

LF is included on the WHO list of priority pathogens with epidemic and pandemic potential. Aims to accelerate the development of diagnostics, therapeutics, and vaccines for such agents remains a research and development priority[ 85 ]. In parallel, initiatives such as CEPI’s 100 Days Mission and its associated implementation scorecards treat Lassa virus as a prototypical arenavirus threat, using it to benchmark progress in platform vaccines, clinical-trial networks, regulatory pathways, and equitable access mechanisms [ 78 ]. This moves LF into a category where advancement in development of countermeasures is framed as a core element of global health security rather than a niche research interest [ 11 , 12 ].

In West Africa, Africa CDC and regional partners emphasize Lassa fever within broader VHF agendas that focus on strengthening surveillance, field epidemiology capacity, and public-health emergency operations, with explicit attention to cross-border coordination and One Health approaches in rodent-to-human transmission zones [ 4 ]. At a national level, Nigeria has developed dedicated Lassa fever case-management guidelines and VHF preparedness and response plan that outline structures for coordination, event-based and indicator-based surveillance, laboratory networks, risk communication, and infection-prevention and control across tiers of care [ 86 ].

Imported LF cases, such as the ones summarized earlier in this review provide an additional lens on preparedness, particularly for high resource but non-endemic settings. Systematic reviews of fifty years of imported cases have repeatedly highlighted delays in eliciting travel histories, diagnostic uncertainty, inconsistent use of personal protective equipment, and ad hoc approaches to post-exposure management of healthcare workers [ 45 ]. The recent fatal travel-associated case in Iowa, which required rapid coordination between frontline clinicians, state health authorities, national reference laboratories, and federal high-consequence pathogen teams, underscores both the value and the fragility of these systems: pre-existing VHFs plans, access to specialized diagnostics, and clear chains of command made rapid confirmation and large-scale contact tracing possible, but gaps in PPE use and low uptake of ribavirin post-exposure prophylaxis revealed persistent challenges in translating written protocols into practice [ 8 ].

Across these experiences, several preparedness lessons emerge that are relevant to the “High-threat pathogens and outbreak response” agenda. First, policy frameworks must bridge endemic-country realities with imported-case scenarios. It is not sufficient to invest only in high-resource settings to strengthen surveillance, clinician awareness, and IPC capacity when resource-constrained settings remain underserved in its control efforts. Second, Lassa fever illustrates the need to align research roadmaps (for diagnostics, ribavirin alternatives, and vaccines) with regulatory and financing mechanisms that allow rapid deployment once products are shown to be safe and effective [ 11 , 78 ]. Third, national and regional plans benefit from embedding Lassa fever into broader One Health and climate-sensitive policies, given the strong links between rodent ecology, land-use change, and human risk [ 13 , 56 ].

Finally, imported cases repeatedly show that preparedness is ultimately tested not in strategy documents but in emergency departments, isolation wards, and laboratories. Sustained investment in training, exercises, and after-action reviews is essential if Lassa-specific policies are to function as living documents for high-threat pathogen response rather than static guidance.

This narrative review has several limitations. The literature search, while broad in scope and date range, was non-systematic and confined primarily to PubMed/MEDLINE and Embase; relevant studies indexed in other databases may not have been captured. Inclusion was restricted to English-language publications, which may have excluded relevant reports from French-language endemic-country settings. The experiential perspective incorporated in this manuscript reflects the observations of a single clinician involved in the 2024 Iowa case and cannot be generalized to other high-consequence pathogen events. Finally, the rapidly evolving landscape of Lassa fever diagnostics, therapeutics, and vaccines means that some information may have become outdated between search completion and publication.

The 2024 Iowa case is not an anomaly—it is the ninth iteration of a recurring pattern that has now played out across five decades and eleven countries. Each imported case exposes the same fault lines: delayed clinical suspicion, diagnostic uncertainty, extensive and resource-intensive contact investigations, and gaps in translating written protocols into real-time practice. The common thread is not a failure of knowledge, but a failure of preparedness infrastructure. Lassa fever remains both a persistent regional burden and an unpredictable global threat. Closing the gaps identified in this review—through strengthened laboratory and surveillance capacity, accelerated evaluation of new antivirals and vaccines, harmonized post-exposure prophylaxis frameworks, and sustained investment in clinician education and IPC training—is not only a matter of regional necessity, but a prerequisite for meaningful global health security in an era of increasing zoonotic spillover and international travel.
