BACKGROUND: No nationally representative cut-offs of serum uric acid (SUA) have 
been proposed for screening high SUA levels in children and adolescents in 
China. We aim to develop percentile cut-offs for SUA among children and 
adolescents based on the China national survey.
METHODS: We used SUA data from 42,227 normal-weight children and adolescents 
aged 6-17 years from the China National Nutrition and Health Survey of Children 
and Lactating Mothers conducted in 2016-2017. Age- and sex-specific percentile 
values for SUA were established using the General Additive Model for Location 
Scale and Shape (GAMLSS). Appropriate SUA cut-offs were selected according to 
the association between high SUA levels and the clustering of metabolic syndrome 
(MetS) risk factors. Furthermore, we assessed the effectiveness of the 
established cut-offs in screening high SUA levels compared with the adult 
cut-offs.
RESULTS: We determined smoothed age- and sex-specific SUA percentiles (P3-P97) 
using a normal-weight sample, showing a sharp increase in SUA levels in boys and 
a steady rise in girls from age 10, with both genders leveling off between ages 
14 and 17. Based on the association of high SUA levels with ≥ 2 or ≥ 3 MetS risk 
factors, the P90 level of SUA could be a potential threshold for screening high 
SUA. Furthermore, we suggest simplified SUA cut-offs based on P90 values: for 
boys, > 360 μmol/L (ages 6-9), > 400 μmol/L (ages 10-12), and > 500 μmol/L (ages 
13-17); for girls, > 350 μmol/L, > 380 μmol/L, and > 400 μmol/L. In the 
2016-2017 Chinese national survey, the prevalence of high SUA was 13.2% with P90 
cut-offs, 12.9% with simplified cut-offs, and 15.9% with adult cut-offs. 
Overweight and obese children and adolescents showed higher rates: 18.2% and 
29.7% (P90), 17.6% and 28.5% (simplified), and 21.7% and 29.9% (adult).
CONCLUSIONS: The newly established SUA cut-offs, including the P90 values by age 
and sex and the simplified cut-offs, could be useful for screening high SUA 
levels or hyperuricemia among children and adolescents in China. The prevalence 
of high SUA in Chinese children and adolescents is concerning, particularly 
within overweight and obese populations.

Uric acid is the end-product of dietary and endogenous purine metabolism, resulting from the balance between hepatic production and renal excretion [1]. Serum uric acid (SUA) is not only a cause of gout and nephrolithiasis but also contributes to the development of cardiovascular diseases such as hypertension, atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, heart failure, coronary artery disease, and cardiovascular mortality [1]. Hyperuricemia and gout are currently recognized as global health issues affecting joints and beyond [2]. Recent reports on the prevalence and incidence of gout vary widely across the studied population and methods employed, with prevalence estimates ranging from < 1% to 6.8% and an incidence rate of 0.6 to 2.9 per 1,000 person-years [3]. The Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 reported that 55.8 million people worldwide had gout in 2020, representing a 22.5% increase since 1990. Projections suggest that the number of individuals with gout will rise by more than 70% from 2020 to 2050 [4]. Current guidance documents differ in their recommendation for SUA cut-offs to diagnose hyperuricemia in adults [5]. Considering the developmental challenges presented by age-related differences in youths, the adult SUA cut-offs may also be inappropriate to identify children and adolescents with high SUA or hyperuricemia. Therefore, several countries have explored and constructed age- and sex-specific percentile cut-offs of SUA in children and adolescents [6–9].

The rapidly increasing prevalence of hyperuricemia in China has been witnessed in the last two decades [10]. A pooled analysis conducted between 2009 and 2019 indicated that the estimated overall prevalence of hyperuricemia among children and adolescents was as high as 23.3% (26.6% in boys and 19.8% in girls) [11], according to the adult SUA cut-offs of > 420 μmol/L in males and > 360 μmol/L in females [12, 13]. Several studies have attempted to establish reference values for SUA in children and adolescents in China [14–17]. However, these studies are often limited to localized areas, small sample sizes, or clinical records of patients. In addition, the SUA have not been included in the reference intervals of clinical biochemistry tests commonly used for children, as outlined in the China national health standards [18]. Therefore, it is imperative to establish nationally representative reference values of SUA in Chinese children and adolescents to enhance screening for high SUA levels or hyperuricemia in youths.


We aim to develop SUA cut-offs for defining high SUA levels or hyperuricemia in children and adolescents aged 6–17 based on a large-scale, population-based national survey in China. In addition, we investigated the national prevalence estimates of high SUA levels in youths in China in 2016—2017, particularly in overweight and obese children and adolescents.

Data were obtained from the China National Nutrition and Health Survey of Children and Lactating Mothers, conducted in 2016–2017. A complex, multistage, stratified cluster random sampling procedure was used to provide nationally representative sample from 31 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities. The details of the study design have been described elsewhere [19]. After excluding participants who could not provide key information, such as anthropometric measurements and SUA data, as well as those with unreasonable data, a total of 60,392 children and adolescents aged 6–17 years were included in this study. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Institute for Nutrition and Health, Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (No. 201614). All participants, or their guardians, provided written informed consent prior to the commencement of the study.

Anthropometric measurements, including weight, height, waist circumference (WC), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), were conducted by trained investigators. Weight and height were measured without shoes or coats to the nearest 0.1 kg using an electric scale (G&G tc-200 k, Shanghai, China) and to the nearest 0.1 cm using a stadiometer. WC was measured twice with a soft, non-elastic tape at the midpoint between the bottom of the rib cage and above the top of the iliac bone, and the mean of WC was calculated and recorded for further analysis. After resting for at least 5 min, SBP and DBP were measured three times at one-minute intervals by an electronic sphygmomanometer (Omron HBP 1300, Tokyo, Japan) with an accuracy of 1 mmHg, and the mean of SBP and DBP was calculated and recorded for further analysis.

Laboratory examinations included triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), fast blood glucose (FBG), and SUA. In this study, participants provided 6 mL fasting venous blood samples in the morning for laboratory testing. TG and HDL-C were tested using enzyme colorimetry (Roche Cobas C701 automatic biochemical analyzer series). FBG was assessed using the glucokinase method (Roche P800 automatic biochemical analyzer). SUA was measured using colorimetry (Roche C702 automatic biochemical analyzer).

Firstly, to address potential distortions caused by extreme weight conditions (either high or low) when establishing population-level weight-related reference values [20–23], we compared metabolic risk across three weight-status subgroups: normal-weight, overweight, and obese. Secondly, considering the potential impact of the onset of puberty on SUA levels, we compared SUA levels among three groups: those who have entered puberty, those who have not, and a mixed data group. Thirdly, considering the developmental challenges associated with age-related differences in children and adolescents, we ultimately established age- and sex-specific SUA percentile values to determine percentile cut-offs. Fourth, we selected appropriate SUA cut-offs based on the association between high SUA levels and clustering of MetS risk factors. Fifth, we assessed the ability of the established cut-offs to screen for high SUA levels or hyperuricemia compared with the adult cut-offs in this Chinese national survey conducted from 2016 to 2017.

We used the age- and sex-specific cut-offs of body mass index (BMI) (kg/m2), with BMI calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m2), to classify individuals as underweight, normal-weight, overweight, and obese according to the China national health standards [24, 25]. We defined the MetS as the presence of three or more of the five components (i.e., central obesity, elevated blood pressure, high TG, low HDL-C, and high FBG [26]. That is, central obesity was defined as waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) ≥ 0.46; elevated blood pressure was defined as SBP/DBP ≥ 130/80 mmHg for adolescents aged 13–17 years and ≥ 120/80 mmHg for children aged 6–12 years; high TG was defined as TG ≥ 130 mg/dL at age 10–17 years or ≥ 100 mg/dL at age 6–9 years, or low HDL-C as HDL-C < 40 mg/dL; and high FBG was defined as FBG ≥ 100 mg/dL. In addition, we defined elevated metabolic risk as having ≥ 2 or ≥ 3 out of the above five components. In light of the potential influence of pubertal development on SUA levels in children and adolescents, participants were stratified into three age-groups roughly corresponding to prepubertal (ages 6–9), pubescent (ages 10–12), and adolescent (ages 13–17) stages.

We selected the normal-weight group as a relatively optimal reference sample given its lowest risk of MetS at the population level. A sensitivity analysis was additionally conducted by comparing the impact of the onset of puberty on the SUA levels by age and sex. We used the generalized additive model for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS) model for the Box-Cox power exponential (BCPE) distribution, with cubic spline smoothing, to construct smoothed age- and sex-specific percentile curves of SUA based on the normal-weight sample [27, 28]. The BCPE distribution has four parameters, including μ, σ, ν, and τ, which represent location (median), scale (approximate coefficient of variation), skewness (power transformation to symmetry), and kurtosis (degrees of freedom or power exponential parameter), respectively. The GAMLSS models were performed using the GAMLSS 5.4.22 library, which runs under R 4.4.1. The goodness of fit for the models was assessed by the Bayesian Information Criterion and Q-Q plots [29].

We used the medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) to describe continuous variables and numbers and proportions to describe categorical variables. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for high SUA levels associated with ≥ 2 or ≥ 3 MetS risk factors using a logistic regression model adjusted by age and sex. Based on the proposed P90 cut-offs, the proposed simplified cut-offs, and the adult cut-offs for high SUA or hyperuricemia, we compared and assessed the prevalence estimates of high SUA in the entire population of 60,392 participants from the China National Survey 2016–2017. Fundamental data analysis, Chi-square test, and logistic regression were performed using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

SUA serum uric acid

To our knowledge, this is the most extensive population-based study to present age- and sex-specific SUA cut-offs for screening high SUA or hyperuricemia among children and adolescents aged 6–17 years, based on normal-weight data. The newly established cut-offs of SUA, which include the P90 values by age and sex, as well as the simplified cut-offs, could be helpful to accurately and timely identify hyperuricemia among youths in China and potentially in other parts of the world.

Elevated SUA levels in children and adolescents have been positively correlated with cardiovascular risk factors, including insulin resistance, elevated C-reactive protein levels, and increased carotid intima-media thickness [31]. Moreover, elevated SUA concentrations are recognized as reliable biomarkers for several significant pediatric conditions, such as pulmonary hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and essential hypertension [32, 33]. Given the substantial pathophysiological role of SUA, hyperuricemia has been proposed as an additional diagnostic criterion for metabolic syndrome in children and adolescents, thereby facilitating the early identification of individuals at risk [34, 35]. Currently, there is no universally accepted definition of hyperuricemia. One definition, based on urate solubility in body fluids, considers hyperuricemia to be serum urate levels above 420 µmol/L (7 mg/dL) [36]. Another practical definition sets the threshold at levels above 360 µmol/L (6 mg/dL), aligning with the threshold for lifelong risk and the recommended target for gout management [37]. Definitions may also vary by gender and age, with thresholds over 420 µmol/L for men and over 360 µmol/L for women and children under 15 [5]. Establishing reference values for hyperuricemia in the pediatric population presents a complex challenge. Influenced by weight and dietary purine intake and varying with age owing to altered renal urate reabsorption, SUA levels rise from birth: they remain low (131–149 µmol/L; 2.2–2.5 mg/dL) in infancy due to high uric acid excretion (> 10% fractional excretion of uric acid, FE-UA), then increase to 208–268 µmol/L (3.5–4.5 mg/dL) by age one as FE-UA declines to ~ 8%, and after puberty, boys exhibit a further FE-UA reduction, leading to higher SUA levels than girls [38]. Overall, in these younger populations, SUA levels are significantly influenced by age and gender, particularly the onset of puberty, leading to dynamic changes throughout development [9, 39]. Applying the fixed thresholds used for adults to children and adolescents may lead to inaccurate estimates of hyperuricemia prevalence across age groups. It is therefore crucial to consider age, sex, and pubertal status when developing reference values for hyperuricemia in children and adolescents, ensuring applicability across developmental stages. Additionally, the impact of overweight and obesity on SUA levels must be accounted for, as these factors significantly affect SUA concentrations in the pediatric population.

Research shows varying prevalence of hyperuricemia rates among children and adolescents globally: 16.6% in U.S. adolescents (12–19 years), 9.4% in South Korean children (10–18 years), 23.3% in Chinese children (3–9 years), and 12.6% in those under 19 in the Czech Republic [11, 40, 41]. These findings indicate that hyperuricemia among children and adolescents may represent a global public health issue. Nevertheless, caution is warranted when comparing prevalence rates across countries and regions, given the heterogeneous diagnostic criteria for hyperuricemia used in these studies. Currently, there is no global consensus on reference cut-off values for hyperuricemia in children and adolescents. Research conducted in Brazil and Korea has proposed age- and sex-specific reference ranges for SUA in these populations based on their respective general populations [8, 9]. The cut-off values we propose are significantly higher than those reported in the Brazilian study and slightly exceed those from the Korean study across all age groups. Furthermore, our cut-off values surpass the upper limit of the normal range for SUA in corresponding age groups as outlined by the Mayo Clinic in the United States [30]. This variation may be due to differences in statistical methodologies employed across studies and SUA levels influenced by factors such as ethnicity, dietary habits, and lifestyle [42]. Given that previous studies in the United States and Chinese Taiwan have utilized lower fixed thresholds to define hyperuricemia, resulting in an unusually high prevalence among children and adolescents and potentially overestimating the condition [43, 44], our proposed reference values may provide a more stringent criterion for application in other countries, especially within East Asia. Despite the implementation of more stringent criteria for defining hyperuricemia, its prevalence among children and adolescents remains substantial, at 12.9%, which indicates that there could be millions of potential hyperuricemia cases within this demographic in China. Our findings serve as a wake-up call for public health policymakers to pay closer attention to the high prevalence of hyperuricemia among Chinese children and adolescents. Apart from developing age- and sex-specific references for SUA, we also proposed simplified fixed thresholds of SUA for children and adolescents. Although these simplified criteria yield slightly lower prevalence rates compared with age- and sex-specific cut-offs, the use of fixed cut-offs significantly enhances screening efficiency. It is potentially helpful for the early detection of high SUA or hyperuricemia, risk stratification, and targeted interventions in clinical and primary healthcare practice.

The dynamic balance among urate production, the sophisticated secretion and reabsorption processes in the renal proximal tubule, and intestinal excretion sustains urate homeostasis. Genome-wide association studies and meta-analyses have identified over 30 common genetic variants associated with serum urate levels, most of which involve urate transporters [45]. Only up to 10% of hyperuricemia cases are attributed to Mendelian disorders of purine metabolism, such as the hyperactivity of phosphoribosylpyrophosphate synthetase and the deficiency of hypoxanthine–guanine phosphoribosyltransferase. Three urate transporters, namely URAT1/SLC22A12, GLUT9/SLC2A9, and ABCG2/BCRP, play pivotal roles in regulating serum urate (SU); their dysfunction can lead to urate transport disorders, resulting in either hyperuricemia or hypouricemia. Renal hypouricemia is a rare, heterogeneous inherited disease caused by the dysfunction of two key renal urate transporters—URAT1 (encoded by SLC22A12, OMIM #220,150) and GLUT9 (encoded by SLC2A9, OMIM #612,076)—which impairs tubular urate transport, resulting in insufficient urate reabsorption and/or excessive secretion. Notably, the heritable secretory component of urate homeostasis is primarily mediated by the protein product of the ABCG2/BCRP gene. Dysfunction of ABCG2 reduces extrarenal urate excretion, which is a common underlying mechanism of hyperuricemia.

Although genetic factors predominantly influence serum uric acid levels, the swift rise in hyperuricemia prevalence within the Chinese population may be more significantly attributed to dietary and other lifestyle-related factors. Over recent decades, China's rapid urbanization has led to a marked shift in dietary habits, transitioning from traditional to Western-style patterns. This transition has led to a substantial rise in the consumption of animal-based foods [46], thereby increasing the intake of purines derived from animal sources, which is directly associated with an elevated risk of hyperuricemia [47]. Furthermore, the intake of sugary beverages among children and adolescents in China increased more than tenfold between 2003 and 2014 [48]. Fructose, prevalent in sugar-sweetened beverages, contributes to an elevated risk of hyperuricemia by facilitating the catabolism of adenine nucleotides and augmenting purine synthesis [49]. In addition to dietary factors, insufficient physical activity and a sedentary lifestyle may contribute to the high prevalence of hyperuricemia among Chinese children and adolescents. Regular moderate exercise enhances insulin sensitivity, which may improve SUA levels, whereas a sedentary lifestyle exerts the opposite effect [50]. Evidence from extensive cross-sectional and retrospective studies conducted in China reveals a concerning trend: fewer than 25% of schoolchildren participate in physical activity lasting at least 60 min daily, while 85.8% of students remain sedentary for more than two hours each day [51]. Considering that most adolescents with hyperuricemia do not exhibit obvious symptoms, there are currently no clear guidelines for medication use in this population. Therefore, lifestyle improvements, particularly in diet and exercise, should be prioritized to prevent and treat future hyperuricemia among children and adolescents.

Our study has four main strengths. First, we utilized a large-scale population-based dataset of normal-weight individuals to construct age- and sex-specific percentile values of SUA in children and adolescents, which strengthens the representativeness of our findings relative to other studies. Second, we explored and selected relatively optimal cut-offs based on the minimum risk of clustering of MetS risk factors, which further strengthens the reliability of our findings in comparison to other studies. Third, we developed the validated P90 cut-offs by age and sex, as well as the simplified cut-offs, which could be more practical in clinical and public health settings. Fourth, we further assessed the national prevalence estimates of high SUA or hyperuricemia among children and adolescents in China, according to different SUA cut-offs. However, several limitations should also be noted. Firstly, we proposed age- and sex-specific P90 cut-offs of SUA based on the increasing risk of clustering of MetS from cross-sectional data. Future research should seek to validate the efficacy of the proposed cut-off values by examining more robust outcomes, including definitive endpoint events such as the development of gout and kidney disease, in children and adolescents using longitudinal data. Secondly, although our study considered the effects of puberty and performed pertinent sensitivity analyses, the absence of an objective assessment of pubertal status may introduce potential bias into the uric acid reference ranges established herein. Caution is advised when applying these reference ranges to children and adolescents experiencing atypical pubertal development. Thirdly, SUA exhibits high day-to-day variability, which raises concerns regarding classification based on a single specimen; thus, repeated validation on non-consecutive days may also be necessary in clinical or public health practice.

In this study, the newly established cut-offs of SUA, including the P90 values by age and sex, and the simplified cut-offs, could be helpful in screening high SUA levels or hyperuricemia among children and adolescents in China. There was an alarming level of high SUA in children and adolescents in China, particularly in overweight and obese groups. Early screening for hyperuricemia using appropriate criteria and personalized health promotion, including healthy diets, physical activity, and weight control, should be prioritized.
