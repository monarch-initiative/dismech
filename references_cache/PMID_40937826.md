# Epileptic drop attacks: More than just atonic seizures.
**Authors:** Ono T, Sato K, Honda R, Otsubo H
**Journal:** Epilepsia Open (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1002/epi4.70136](https://doi.org/10.1002/epi4.70136)

## Content

"Drop attacks" are not officially defined by the International League Against Epilepsy. Seizures are characterized by a sudden loss of control over the trunk and posture, leading to falls and injuries, and resolving within a few seconds. Accurately diagnosing the type of seizure is usually difficult due to limitations in clinical documentation based on witness or physician inspections. Historically, epileptic drop attacks have been considered primarily-and almost exclusively-atonic seizures. However, numerous studies using simultaneous video-electroencephalography and electromyography recordings have shown that drop attacks are rarely pure atonic seizures but are often epileptic spasms or other types of seizures with increased muscle tone. Some medical treatments reduce the frequency of epileptic drop attacks; however, their effectiveness has not been evaluated based on the type of seizure or pathophysiology. Corpus callosotomy is an effective surgical option that reduces the frequency and severity of epileptic drop attacks, especially in patients with Lennox-Gastaut syndrome. Recent studies suggest that epileptic spasms are cortically or hemispherically generated seizures and that disconnecting the corpus callosum potentially interrupts bilaterally synchronous seizure activities, supporting the rationale for surgery in patients with epileptic drop attacks. The effectiveness of each treatment should be reevaluated based on seizure type and the pathophysiology of epileptic drop attacks, properly distinguished in the future. PLAIN LANGUAGE SUMMARY: Epileptic drop attacks are sudden falls caused by seizures, often leading to injuries. While these attacks were once thought to be mainly due to atonic seizures, recent studies using video-EEG-electromyography recordings showed that they are more often caused by other types of seizures, such as epileptic spasms. Diagnosing the exact seizure type is complicated but important, as it affects treatment options including surgical approaches like corpus callosotomy. Further studies based on the specific underlying pathophysiology of epileptic drop attacks are needed.

Epileptic drop attacks have historically been and are currently considered synonymous with atonic seizures.

Numerous studies using simultaneous video‐EEG and electromyography recordings have shown that epileptic drop attacks are rarely pure atonic seizures but are often epileptic spasms or other types of seizures with increased muscle tone.

Clinically, the efficacy of medical and surgical treatments for epileptic drop attacks should be reexamined based on the appropriate seizure type and pathophysiology.

 Therefore, we reconsidered the status of epileptic drop attacks, which are challenging epileptic seizures that remain officially unrecognized.

Importantly, the epileptic drop attacks discussed here generally do not include partial epileptic seizure phenomena. For example, focal impaired awareness seizures, which are preceded by an interruption of consciousness and behavior and followed by a relatively “slow fall” due to difficulty maintaining body balance, are not included. Unilateral drop arm, hand, or leg collapses were also excluded. Instead, we focused on seizures where the main symptom is a “sudden” fall.

Reports of epileptic drop attacks appear in literature published two centuries ago, but similar cases were reported much earlier.
18
 Tissot documented an 11‐year‐old patient who experienced 40 falls per day, occasionally resulting in a complete collapse to the floor, in a case report in 1770 (quoted from Seino 1988).
18
 In 1922, Hunt introduced the term “static seizure” to describe seizure‐induced falls.
18
, 
19
 A “static” seizure refers to a sudden loss of postural control, often causing the patient to fall forward and sustain facial and head injuries, closely resembling what we now call epileptic drop attacks (quoted from Seino 1988).
18
 Hunt acknowledged that myoclonic movements may precede a fall but implicitly denied the possibility that muscle tonicity could also be involved (quoted from Seino 1988).
18
 Lennox first described the term “akinetic” seizure and later introduced “astatic” seizure.
20
, 
21
 Curiously, although they described the same phenomenon, Hunt and Lennox adopted the opposite expressions of “static” and “astatic” seizures. Lennox characterized these attacks as involving a rapid loss of postural control, not only due to pure decreased muscle tone but also due to increased muscle tone or preceding myoclonus (quoted from Seino 1988).
18
 Today, this symptom is recognized as a characteristic seizure of LGS, as well as myoclonic seizures, and atypical absence.

Despite the above terminological changes, epileptic drop attacks are currently considered roughly synonymous with atonic seizures, even in various clinical studies and review articles focusing on epileptic drop attacks. For example, a meta‐analysis published in 2017 by Chan et al. on the outcomes of corpus callosotomy for epileptic drop attacks used the expression “drop attacks (i.e., atonic seizures leading to sudden falls).”
23
 Similarly, a systematic review article published in 2015 by Rolston et al. uses the phrase “atonic seizures, often called drop attacks.”
24
 Both are valuable studies with potentially significant impacts on clinical epilepsy, and we certainly do not believe that these quotes reflect their actual interpretation; however, describing drop attacks this way could lead to the misunderstanding that “epileptic drop attacks are exclusively atonic seizures with very rare exceptions.”

While the persistent viewpoint that epileptic drop attacks almost always indicate atonic seizures remains, physicians observing seizures through long‐term video‐electroencephalography (EEG) recordings may find this assumption problematic. A definitive diagnosis of epilepsy and accurate seizure classification are priorities for rational treatment, and their importance has been historically recognized, leading the ILAE to update its terminology and classification. Video‐EEG monitoring, which began expanding globally around 1980,
25
 enabled physicians to diagnose seizures by directly comparing EEG findings with behavioral symptoms that are not usually witnessed. However, particularly when analyzing epileptic drop attacks, identifying the precise pathophysiology through visual inspection alone may be impossible. In such cases, the simultaneous recording of electromyograms (EMGs) is recommended for video‐EEG monitoring of seizures. It allows for identifying the behavioral onset of a seizure and distinguishing whether epileptic drop attacks are caused by a loss or increase in muscular tone (i.e., atonic or tonic/spasms).
26
 Therefore, simultaneous video‐EEG and EMG recording is commonly performed in major epilepsy centers. Physicians familiar with modern study techniques may be uncomfortable with the viewpoint that epileptic drop attacks almost always involve atonic seizures. In our clinical experience from our epilepsy center, where video‐EEG is recorded in 300–500 patients annually, EMG findings suggestive of pure atonic seizures are infrequent. Even when detected, they are often accompanied by a brief muscle contraction preceding electrical attenuation on EMG (Figure 2). Other complex seizure types that can cause drop attacks include myoclonic–tonic, myoclonic–atonic, and tonic–atonic seizures, depending on the study, with interpretations being limited by differences in terminology and definitions of seizure types, as well as the heterogeneity of patient profiles (e.g., most studies included patients with LGS, whereas others focused on those with myoclonic‐astatic epilepsy). CC is an important surgical option for patients with epileptic drop attacks, particularly those with LGS.
38
 It involves severing the corpus callosum, which connects the two hemispheres of the brain, thereby preventing synchronization and facilitation rather than the transfer of bihemispheric seizure activity.
39
, 
40
, 
41
 Numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of this procedure in reducing the frequency and severity of drop attacks. A meta‐analysis by Chan et al. in 2017 showed that CC significantly decreased the occurrence of drop attacks in most patients, with a complete disappearance rate of 55.3%.
23
 Furthermore, Rolston et al. reinforced these findings, stating that patients were significantly more likely to achieve a high response rate in drop attack frequency with CC than with vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) (85.6% vs. 57.6%, respectively).
24
 VNS is also considered to be a good treatment option for drop attacks, mainly in patients with LGS. Okanishi et al. reported that VNS was less effective for epileptic and tonic spasms confirmed by video‐EEG–EMG recording than for other focal‐onset seizures, which may explain why it is less effective than CC.
42
 Graham et al. also reported a systematic review of CC outcomes in a pediatric population and concluded that drop attacks improved with CC more than with other generalized seizure types.
43
 However, the therapeutic effects on epileptic drop attacks have not been verified according to seizure type or pathophysiology in those major studies.

Epileptic drop attacks have been recognized as one of the main symptoms of LGS, and the aforementioned clinical studies have included various patients with this disorder. Many patients with LGS, up to 40%–50%,
44
 have a history of preceding infantile epileptic spasms syndrome, and approximately one‐third of these patients develop LGS.
45
 Considering that epileptic spasms in infantile epileptic spasms syndrome continue even after the transition to LGS,
1
 the finding that epileptic spasms are the most frequent cause of drop attacks is clinically significant. CC has also been adopted for controlling epileptic spasms, not atonic seizures, with an increasing number of studies on its efficacy since 2010.
46
 Although few patients achieve seizure freedom after CC, Baba et al. reported on the effectiveness of surgery in patients with infantile epileptic spasms syndrome, finding that 43% of 56 patients achieved cessation of epileptic spasms.
47
 Kanai et al. also reported on 17 patients with EEG–EMG‐confirmed epileptic spasms who received CC, 41% of whom achieved seizure freedom.
48
 These results suggest that CC is relatively effective for drop attacks in patients with LGS, provided that epileptic spasms are the most frequent pathology of the epileptic drop attacks.

In recent years, clinical findings supporting that epileptic spasms are cortical focal‐onset seizures have increased. Ono et al. reported that in patients with refractory epileptic spasms clinically diagnosed preoperatively with infantile epileptic spasms syndrome or LGS, the responsible focus or hemisphere became unilateral after CC, and the epileptic spasms disappeared after subsequent cortical resection or lobar disconnection.
49
 Furthermore, Uchida et al. reported that in patients with symmetric epileptic spasms who underwent CC, the spasms became asymmetric after surgery. In this case, the hemisphere responsible for seizure symptoms was ipsilateral to the direction in which the neck flexed or the head turned and contralateral to the predominant muscle contractions of the upper and lower extremities.
50
 Nariai et al. analyzed simultaneous EEG–EMG recordings with a high sampling rate during epileptic spasms and found that ictal gamma or beta activity localized to one hemisphere frequently preceded motor symptoms.
51
 Kanai et al. reported a subtype of epileptic spasms with biphasic muscular contractions and a potential mechanism of asynchronous epileptic excitation in the bilateral hemispheres, regardless of whether the epileptogenesis is focal or not.
52
 In the past, generalized seizures, attributed to cortical and subcortical activity,
53
 were considered the mechanism of epileptic drop attacks. There are considerable cases in which CC is ineffective for drop attacks; therefore, the possibility of other types of seizures, including atonic seizures and pathological conditions in which the cortex interacts with subcortical structures, cannot be ignored. However, considering that epileptic drop attacks can be suppressed by more than 60% by disconnection of the corpus callosum, even if only temporarily, which is involved in the connections between the bihemispheric cortices, it is quite easy to accept that most drop attacks are epileptic spasms of exclusively cortical origin or subcortical origin with the enhancement of the corpus callosum.
54



In summary, the term “epileptic drop attack” has often been used synonymously with atonic seizures, and this traditional description persists today. However, clinical studies using video‐EEG and EMG monitoring have shown that many of these are epileptic spasms, while pure atonic seizures—those not preceded by tonic or myoclonic components—are much rarer. Epileptic drop attacks are particularly problematic in LGS, where they are often treatment‐resistant. Medical treatments may help reduce their frequency; however, they seem ineffective in terms of their complete disappearance. Clinical results indicate that CC is often effective against epileptic drop attacks, which aligns with its action against epileptic spasms. Many patients with epilepsy syndromes associated with developmental epileptic encephalopathy, such as infantile epileptic spasms syndrome and LGS, experience severe delays in intellectual and motor development, with many eventually becoming bedridden. In this situation, restricting this effective treatment only to drop attacks, that is, to patients who can stand, collapse, and fall due to seizures, would be unreasonable.

The main reason the ILAE and its committees/task forces have never used “epileptic drop attacks” as an official term may be the diverse and heterogeneous nature of their pathophysiology and causes. Concerns may exist that adopting such uncertain terminology could lead to misunderstandings about the validity of diagnosis and treatment. Naturally, this does not mean that “epileptic drop attacks” should be formally added as an independent seizure type, but efforts should focus on accurately distinguishing the pathophysiology of epileptic drop attacks (i.e., epileptic spasms, tonic seizures, atonic seizures, etc.). We would like to emphasize that classification should not solely rely on whether a fall occurs but that it should instead be based on careful consideration of both the electrophysiological and clinical characteristics that reflect the underlying pathophysiology. Fortunately, the latest ILAE seizure classification has adopted an operational list of “motor phenomena of seizure semiology,” which provides a flexible framework for more precisely characterizing seizure symptoms.
8
 As a proposal, incorporating “epileptic drop attacks” into this list may be a viable option worthy of further consideration. In that case, using combined terminology such as “epileptic spasms with/without drop attacks” or “tonic seizures with/without drop attacks” better captures the essential nature of each seizure while also accounting for individual patient backgrounds. It is sometimes difficult to distinguish epileptic spasms from tonic seizures solely based on their duration. In certain cases, tonic seizures may follow epileptic spasms, and the reverse pattern may also occur. In such cases, increased and sustained muscle tones may simply differentiate seizures epileptic spasms from tonic seizures.
64
 Furthermore, the pattern of attenuation and amplification of muscle tones on EMG can easily differentiate atonic seizures from epileptic spasms and tonic seizures.

Finally, the effectiveness of existing and new treatments should also be re‐examined based on the appropriate seizure type and pathophysiology in the future. In particular, epileptic spasms should be further emphasized as the most important and frequent seizure type causing epileptic drop attacks, and investigations of the treatment options must be prioritized. If more specific treatments for epileptic spasms and/or other seizures causing drop attacks are established, management of epileptic drop attacks could be further optimized for each patient. Such optimization may also include behavioral seizure protection strategies as well as medical and surgical treatments. For example, if we can clarify which situations and types of injuries patients with epileptic spasms are most vulnerable to, it will become possible to implement more individualized protective measures.
