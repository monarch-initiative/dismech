# Severe Myocardial Inflammation and Necrosis in Juvenile Mice Compared with Adult Mice with Coxsackievirus B3 Myocarditis

Abstract

Background
Viral myocarditis presents a significant burden of disease, particularly among children and young adults. However, clinical guidelines and treatment strategies for pediatric patients are derived from those for adult patients due to a lack of pediatric data. Current animal models of viral myocarditis use adult mice, so conclusions from these models cannot necessarily be extrapolated to the pediatric population. We sought to develop a juvenile mouse model of myocarditis to examine differences between these two distinct clinical populations.


Methods

                    Male and female BALB/c 3-4-week-old “juvenile” and 8-week-old “adult” mice were infected intraperitoneally with 10
                    3
                    PFU of heart-passaged coxsackievirus B3. Sera was used to evaluate testosterone and estradiol levels. Cardiac histological evaluations included overall inflammation, fibrosis, and specific cell-type infiltration. RNA was extracted from cardiac tissue and evaluated for changes in gene expression of cell-type markers, complement components, and NLRP3 inflammasome components.
                  


Results
Juvenile mice exhibited more severe inflammation than adult mice but no sex differences in overall inflammation. Juvenile mice demonstrated iancreased infiltration of CD11b+ cells, F4/80+ cells, and CD3+ T-cells vs. adults. Inflammasome genes NLRP3 and caspase-1 were significantly increased in juvenile compared with adult myocarditis.


Conclusions
This paper is the first to describe a juvenile mouse model of coxsackievirus B3 myocarditis and provides a direct comparison to a translational adulat mouse model. Juvenile mice had greater cardiac inflammation than adults. This model replicates clinical populations and provides a valuable tool to study age as a factor in the pathogenesis of myocarditis.

BACKGROUND 49 
Myocarditis, defined as inflammation of the myocardium, remains an important cause of 50 
sudden cardiac death among children and young adults.1,2 Myocarditis is characterized 51 
by immune cell infiltration into the heart and is primarily caused by viral infections, 52 
including coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3), among patients in North America.3-8 Within this 53 
patient population, roughly 30% of patients progress to dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) 54 
and chronic heart failure (HF).9,10 Currently, there are no disease-specific medications for 55 
myocarditis, and guidelines recommend treatment of symptomatic heart failure when 56 
indicated.11-13 The spectrum of presentation and outcomes of myocarditis in both children 57 
and adults varies significantly.14 Though it is well established that myocarditis is 58 
particularly deadly in newborns and infants, specific studies focusing on age remain 59 
scarce.15-17 Children tend to present with a more severe or fulminant myocarditis, 60 
progress rapidly, and develop persistent cardiac dysfunction as well as an increased risk 61 
of death or need for transplantation.16-18 Despite differences in presentation, clinical 62 
progression, and outcomes of myocarditis across age and sex, limited research has 63 
specifically examined these factors in juvenile and adult animal models. 64 
Among adults, males tend to develop myocarditis more often, with a ratio upwards of 65 
3.5:1.19,20 Moreover, studies have shown that men are twice as likely to present with 66 
evidence of cardiac fibrosis on magnetic resonance imaging, progress from myocarditis 67 
to DCM more frequently, and have lower transplant-free recovery.9,21 However, the 68 
mechanisms underlying this sex difference remains poorly understood. 69

Animal models have been critical for understanding the pathogenesis of myocarditis and 71 
its associated sex differences.22,23 From these models, it has been shown that pathogenic 72 
inflammation is associated with upregulation of specific innate and adaptive pathways. 73 
Particularly, the NOD-, LRR- and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) 74 
inflammasome activation via Toll Like Receptor (TLR) signaling is activated in response 75 
to coxsackievirus infection and is critical to the development of myocardial 76 
inflammation.24,25 Furthermore, this pathway has been shown to have greater activation 77 
in males compared with females at multiple points within the pathway.25,26 However, these 78 
experiments have focused on adult mice. To this point, comparison of responses in young 79 
mice in other established mouse models of myocarditis have not been conducted. 80 
Therefore, the goal of this work was to develop and describe a new animal model of 81 
juvenile myocarditis. 82

and male (Figure 1c) mice, myocardial inflammation was greater in juvenile than adult 207

Discussion 284 
In this study, we report the first description of a translational mouse model of juvenile 285 
coxsackievirus B3 myocarditis, compared with the established adult model from the 286 
Fairweather group.23 Overall, we observed that 3-4-week-old mice exhibited more severe 287 
cardiac inflammation and necrosis than sex-matched adult mice. Importantly, we also did 288 
not observe sex differences in cardiac inflammation in juvenile mice as observed in 289 
adults3,39 replicating findings in clinical populations. These findings suggest that age plays 290 
a critical role in the overall immune response in viral myocarditis and that models specific 291 
to the juvenile population are necessary to study disease pathogenesis in this population.  292

To date, studies of myocarditis in animal models have primarily used mice older than 5-8 293 
weeks.29,40,41 These models have identified and investigated sex differences in the adult 294 
population and reinforced the role of sex hormones in disease pathogenesis.42 295 
Additionally, clinical studies and trials in patients with myocarditis have focused on adult 296 
populations.9,21 Therefore, extrapolation of the findings of these studies to juvenile, 297 
prepubescent populations should be done with caution. There remains a lack of studies 298 
utilizing juvenile animals to study viral myocarditis. The only example that attempted to 299 
develop a juvenile viral myocarditis mouse model did not result in cardiac inflammation 300 
but instead led to neuroinflammation and paralysis.43 However, juvenile animal models 301 
for other diseases, such as Pertussis, Respiratory Syncytial Virus, and Influenza 302 
(although not specifically investigating myocarditis), exist and demonstrate that the 303 
immune response is dynamic across the lifespan.44-46 Furthermore, studies comparing 304 
the immune response in juvenile and adult mice have been conducted. In a model of 305 
MRSA pneumonia, infection in neonatal mice resulted in increased bacterial burden and 306 
decreased macrophage and neutrophil recruitment compared with infected adult mice.47 307 
In the context of the viral immune response, a model of paramyxovirus infection showed 308 
that neonatal mice had lower inflammation, decreased cytokine release, and delayed 309 
activation of pattern recognition receptors compared with adults.48 In Pneumovirus 310 
infection, comparisons across ages show that, despite similar viral replication rates, the 311 
overall magnitude and type of inflammation vary, with older mice showing the most 312 
prominent immune cell recruitment.49 Studies using mouse models of influenza have 313 
shown that neonatal immune cells tend to have decreased capacity to secrete interferon 314

Our model shows both similarities and differences to other investigations of immune 317 
responses in juvenile and adult mice. In response to CVB3 infection in adult mice, we 318 
observe an expected increase in macrophage infiltration into the heart, as previously 319 
reported.54 Additionally, our results regarding expression levels of the complement 320 
pathway and NLRP3 inflammasome components agree with previously published results 321 
in CVB3 infection.55,56 However, these processes have never been explored in a juvenile 322 
myocarditis model. Our model not only shows that myocardial inflammation is worse in 323 
juvenile mice but also that this translates into differences in immune cell infiltration, 324 
immune cell marker expression, and innate immunity pathways. Overall, this reinforces 325 
the need to characterize this response in juvenile mice and to understand better the 326 
differential severity of disease in the juvenile population.  327 
In clinical populations, most pediatric myocarditis research focuses on outcomes. 328 
However, some comparative studies have been published. Mainly, it has been shown that, 329 
compared with pediatric patients hospitalized with dilated cardiomyopathy, those 330 
hospitalized with acute myocarditis exhibit increased expression of terminal NLRP3 331 
inflammasome components at both the gene and protein levels.57 However, this study did 332 
not include adult patients with myocarditis or control patients. As expected, our model 333 
showed increased expression of inflammasome components in the myocarditis groups 334 
compared with the control group and revealed differences between pediatric and adult 335 
myocarditis groups.  336

One limitation of our study is that our ability to precisely age the mice was limited by the 337 
method we used to acquire them. While puberty checks allowed us to estimate age based 338 
on puberty status, future experiments using timed pregnancies would provide even more 339 
accurate ages. Additionally, although we report differences by sex, these results do not 340 
confirm true sex differences by age, as our sample size, particularly for juvenile females, 341 
is small. Thus, while our comparisons of juvenile and adult groups regardless of sex are 342 
appropriately powered, further investigation through additional experiments is needed to 343 
establish the validity of our preliminary findings of age differences within each sex. 344 
Another limitation of this study is that we are assessing only one time point; future 345 
directions should investigate sex and age differences at earlier time points during 346 
myocarditis development and progression to dilated cardiomyopathy using our 347 
translational animal model of viral myocarditis. Finally, our model utilized the same viral 348 
dose across all groups. This may lead to differences in relative dose between pediatric 349 
and adult mice due to the inherent differences in size. It is possible that this could explain 350 
the differences seen. Future studies will explore the use of a viral dose normalized to 351 
body weight to better control for potential dosing inconsistencies. 352 
Perspectives and Significance 353 
Overall, myocarditis continues to present a significant burden of disease. Treatment is 354 
primarily focused on symptomatic relief of associated heart failure rather than treatment 355 
of the disease itself. Moreover, treatment strategies in pediatric populations are derived 356 
from that of adult populations.17 However, it has been well established that the immune 357 
response in children differs from that in adults. Despite this, no studies have compared 358 
responses in these two populations. Here, we present the first report of an animal model 359
