# PKHD1L1 is required for stereocilia bundle maintenance, durable hearing function and resilience to noise exposure
**Authors:** Olga S. Strelkova, Richard T. Osgood, Chunjie J. Tian, Xinyuan Zhang, Evan Hale, Pedro De-la-Torre, Daniel M. Hathaway, Artur A. Indzhykulian
**DOI:** [10.1101/2024.02.29.582786](https://doi.org/10.1101/2024.02.29.582786)

## Content

Abstract
Sensory hair cells of the cochlea are essential for hearing, relying on the mechanosensitive stereocilia bundle at their apical pole for their function. Polycystic Kidney and Hepatic Disease 1-Like 1 (PKHD1L1) is a stereocilia protein required for normal hearing in mice, and for the formation of the transient stereocilia surface coat, expressed during early postnatal development. While the function of the stereocilia coat remains unclear, growing evidence supports PKHD1L1 as a human deafness gene. In this study we carry out in depth characterization of PKHD1L1 expression in mice during development and adulthood, analyze hair-cell bundle morphology and hearing function in aging PKHD1L1-defficient mouse lines, and assess their susceptibility to noise damage. Our findings reveal that PKHD1L1-deficient mice display no disruption to bundle cohesion or tectorial membrane attachment-crown formation during development. However, starting from 6 weeks of age, PKHD1L1-defficient mice display missing stereocilia and disruptions to bundle coherence. Both conditional and constitutive PKHD1L1 knock-out mice develop high-frequency hearing loss progressing to lower frequencies with age. Furthermore, PKHD1L1-deficient mice are susceptible to permanent hearing loss following moderate acoustic overexposure, which induces only temporary hearing threshold shifts in wild-type mice. These results suggest a role for PKHD1L1 in establishing robust sensory hair bundles during development, necessary for maintaining bundle cohesion and function in response to acoustic trauma and aging.

Introduction 35 
Auditory hair cells are highly specialized sensory cells located in the cochlea. Essential for our sense of 36 
hearing, they transduce the mechanical stimulus of sound into an electrical signal. Hair cells of the mammalian 37 
cochlea are terminally differentiated and do not regenerate once lost; therefore, hair-cell death or irreparable 38 
cochlear dysfunction leads to permanent sensorineural hearing loss. Hair-cell function is susceptible to 39 
disruption by genetic mutations and environmental factors such as noise exposure, ototoxic drug treatment, 40 
and aging.   41 
We previously reported that the expression of Polycystic Kidney and Hepatic Disease 1-Like 1 (PKHD1L1), 42 
also known as fibrocystin like (Fibrocystin-L), is highly enriched in cochlear hair cells (Wu et al., 2019). 43 
PKHD1L1 is a very large, predominantly extracellular, membrane protein consisting of 4249 amino acids, with 44 
a single-pass transmembrane domain and a very short 6-amino acid intracellular cytoplasmic domain. 45 
PKHD1L1 has been identified as a biomarker or implicated in many cancers (Kang et al., 2023, 2023; Saravia 46 
et al., 2019; Song et al., 2021; L. Wang et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2019; Zou et al., 2022); 47 
and has been linked to schizophrenia (Shang et al., 2024), and anxiety-like traits (Chitre et al., 2023).  48 
There is increasing evidence supporting PKHD1L1 as a human deafness gene. We previously showed that 49 
PKHD1L1-deficient mice develop delayed-onset progressive hearing loss (Wu et al., 2019). Similarly, knockout 50 
of pkhd1l1 zebrafish paralogs, pkhd1l1α and pkhd1l1β, reduces auditory-evoked startle responses in zebrafish 51 
at larval stages, indicative of an early-onset hearing loss. (Makrogkikas et al., 2023). In humans, we recently 52 
reported biallelic variants of PKHD1L1 in four unrelated families with congenital non-syndromic sensorineural 53 
hearing loss (Redfield et al., 2024). These PKHD1L1 variants were predicted to be damaging in silico, and 54 
some of the putative disease-causing mutations were shown to reduce the thermodynamic stabilities of 55 
PKHD1L1 protein fragments in vitro (Redfield et al., 2024). Furthermore, a large-scale exome study of 532 56 
individuals recently identified the PKHD1L1 gene as a possible contributor to hearing outcomes, with a higher 57 
variant load in older adults with hearing loss compared to the non-hearing loss group (Lewis et al., 2023). 58 
However, little is known about PKHD1L1 in the inner ear. In this study we aim to comprehensively characterize 59 
in mice the expression, localization, and roll of PKHD1L1 in hearing function and maintenance. 60 
Within the cochlea there are two types of hair cells: one row of inner hair cells (IHC), responsible for sound 61 
transduction; and three rows of electromotile outer hair cells (OHC), which amplify sound-evoked vibrations 62 
within the cochlea. Hair cells derive their name from the actin-rich stereocilia they carry at their apical poles, 63 
which collectively form the mechanosensitive sensory hair bundle. Hair bundles are composed of three rows of 64 
stereocilia arranged in a staircase pattern. The mechanoelectrical-transducer (MET) machinery is located at 65 
the tips of the two shorter rows of stereocilia, linked to the adjacent taller row by fine protein filaments called tip 66 
links. Tip links mechanically gate the MET channels in response to bundle deflection, initiating the perception 67 
of sound and forming the basis of hearing (Beurg et al., 2009; Pickles et al., 1984). The tallest row of OHC 68 
stereocilia are embedded in the tectorial membrane (TM), a gelatinous extracellular matrix essential for hearing 69 
sensitivity and frequency selectivity (Legan et al., 2000, 2005; Sellon et al., 2019). The tips of the tallest row 70 
stereocilia are linked to the tectorial membrane by attachment crowns (Verpy et al., 2011). There are additional 71 
links between adjacent stereocilia, maintaining cohesion in the mature hair bundle. These inter-stereociliary 72 
links include horizontal top connectors in OHCs, and shaft connectors in IHCs (Goodyear et al., 2005; Nayak et 73 
al., 2007).  74 
During the establishment of the mature hair bundle in early postnatal development there is a rapidly changing 75 
array of additional, temporary, links on the surface of the stereocilia. Identified by electron microscopy, these 76 
include transient lateral links, ankle links, and OHC shaft connectors; in addition to a dense surface coat on the 77 
stereocilia and apical membrane of the cell, visualized by staining with tannic acid (Goodyear et al., 2005). We 78 
previously found that PKHD1L1 localizes to the surface of stereocilia during early postnatal development. 79 
Using Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ mice, we established that it is required for the formation of the stereocilia surface 80 
coat, especially towards the tip of stereocilia (Wu et al., 2019). We showed that Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ mice 81

The functions of the stereocilia coat, and specifically the role of PKHD1L1 on the surface of stereocilia during 86

cochlear hair cells, it has been suggested to play a role in bundle maturation (Goodyear et al., 2005). We 88

The sensory cells and structures of the cochlea, established during development, are not regenerated once 93 
lost. Hair cells must therefore survive a lifetime in order to maintain hearing function. In the present study, we 94 
provide evidence suggesting that PKHD1L1 is required for the establishment of robust sensory bundles, 95 
resilient to varied environmental stresses during aging. Experimentally, high noise levels can induce 96 
permanent (irreversible) threshold shifts (PTS), or temporary (reversible) threshold shifts (TTS) depending on 97 
exposure level and duration (Kujawa & Liberman, 2006; Ryan et al., 2016; Y. Wang et al., 2002). 98 
Administration of moderate noise insult in an experimental setting therefore allows us to assess the sensitivity 99 
of PKHD1L1-deficient hair cells to environmental insult compared to wildtype littermates. 100 
In this study we expand our understanding of PKHD1L1 in the inner ear, and address the previously observed 101 
temporal disparity between PKHD1L1 expression and the onset of hearing loss in its absence. We 102 
comprehensively characterize Pkhd1l1 gene and protein expression during development and in adult mice. 103 
Morphological analysis in PKHD1L1-deficient mice revealed no developmental disruption to bundle cohesion or 104 
attachment-crown formation. However, progressive bundle disruption and missing stereocilia are observed in 105 
mice deficient in PKHD1L1 from 6 weeks of age onwards, with concurrent high frequency hearing loss 106 
progressing to lower frequencies with age in both conditional and constitutive Pkhd1l1 knock-out mice. We find 107 
that PKHD1L1-deficient mice have a higher susceptibility to permanent hearing loss following moderate 108 
acoustic overexposure. Here, therefore, we demonstrate a role for PKHD1L1 in establishing robust sensory 109 
hair bundles during development, which are required in later life to maintain function in response to acoustic 110 
insult and aging.  111

Results 112 
Pkhd1l1 mRNA is expressed in outer and inner hair cells during development  113 
Previously, we reported active transcription of Pkhd1l1 in hair cells during early postnatal development (Wu et 114 
al., 2019). To investigate the discrepancy in the timeframe between the detection of PKHD1L1 protein and the 115 
physiological effects of its deletion on hearing function in later life, we first elected to directly, and more 116 
precisely, evaluate the Pkhd1l1 mRNA levels within inner and outer hair cells during development and aging. 117 
Using a commercially available probe targeting the 1061-2157 bp region (~8% of the 13049 bp transcript) of 118 
Pkhd1l1 mRNA, we carried out in-situ hybridization by RNAscope in acutely dissected organ of Corti from P1 119 
to 9-month-old mice. Pkhd1l1 mRNA was visualized by fluorescence imaging in apical, middle, and basal turns 120 
of the cochlea. Pkhd1l1 expression was observed as diffuse, as well as bright punctate labeling, in both IHCs 121 
and OHCs (Fig. 1a, and Sup. Fig. 1). 122 
To quantify Pkhd1l1 mRNA labeling intensity levels in individual cells, we utilized Cellpose, a deep-learning-123 
based generalist algorithm for cellular segmentation (Stringer et al., 2021). Briefly, the DAPI-stained hair-cell 124 
nuclei were detected using Cellpose, and their ROIs transferred to imageJ for single-cell intensity 125 
measurements of mRNA labeling in maximum intensity z-projections (Fig. 1b).  126 
The intensity of mRNA labeling varied in an age- and cochlear region-dependent manner. Considering first, 127 
OHCs: Pkhd1l1 mRNA fluorescence labeling intensity is highest in the basal region at P1 and drops rapidly 128 
during early postnatal development (Fig. 1a & c). Peak mRNA expression in the middle and apical regions 129 
occurs later, at P4 (Fig. 1c & Sup. Fig. 1a). As hair cells towards the base of the cochlea develop before those 130 
at the apex, and peak fluorescence intensity is similar between the regions, differences in mRNA expression 131 
between regions at a given time point are likely developmental rather than tonotopic. In all 3 regions, OHC 132 
Pkhd1l1 mRNA fluorescence intensity reduces to almost undetectable levels by P11 (comparable to negative 133 
control dapB, Sup. Fig.1b), before the onset of hearing, and remains undetectable in mature hair cells at P24 134 
and 9-month time points (Fig. 1 & Sup Fig. 1a). 135 
Surprisingly, given that little PKHD1L1 was previously detected at the surface of IHCs at early postnatal time 136 
points (Wu et al., 2019), Pkhd1l1 mRNA is also detected at comparatively high levels in inner hair cells. As with 137 
expression in OHCs, fluorescence intensity peaks first in early postnatal development (P7), although later than 138 
that observed in OHCs (P1-4). More curiously, however, Pkhd1l1 mRNA is also detected at high levels in IHCs 139 
at the 9-month time point, (Fig. 1 & Sup. Fig. 1a, with no signal detected in dapB negative control, Sup. Fig.1b). 140 
These findings suggest a possibility of PKHD1L1 protein expression in IHCs in the adult cochlea.  141

PKHD1L1 is transiently expressed in cochlear hair cells during early postnatal development  143 
The abovementioned Pkhd1l1 mRNA expression labeling results do not correlate well in timing or location with 144 
our previous findings of protein detection (Wu et al., 2019). Therefore, to more fully characterize the expression 145 
pattern of PKHD1L1, we carried out anti-PKHD1L1 immunofluorescence labeling in the cochlea throughout 146 
development and in adulthood (Fig. 2). Labeling was carried out in P4–9 month old Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre- mice 147 
(Fig. 2 top 7 panels), facilitating the use of previously reported Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ mice as a negative 148 
labeling control (Fig. 2, bottom panel).  149 
In agreement with our previous report, PKHD1L1 was detected in hair cells throughout the first postnatal week 150 
(Fig. 2 P4-P8) along the length of the cochlea (Sup. Fig. 2a). PKHD1L1 labeling was observed on the hair 151 
bundle stereocilia, with no specific signal detected in hair cell bodies (Sup Fig. 2c). Anti-PKHD1L1 labeling 152 
intensity gradually decreased with development, was largely gone by P10, and completely cleared in mature 153 
hair cells at P21.  154

PKHD1L1 expression is not redistributed on OHC hair bundle stereocilia during development  169 
During early postnatal development, a transient surface coat, and several types of transient stereocilia links are 170 
present on the developing stereocilia bundle (Goodyear et al., 2005). PKHD1L1 was previously demonstrated 171 
to localize with the surface coat at P4 (Wu et al., 2019). Several key stereocilia components including the tip-172 
link proteins cadherin-23 (CDH23) (Michel et al., 2005) and protocadherin-15 (PCDH15) (Indzhykulian et al., 173 
2013; Kazmierczak et al., 2007); horizontal top connector and attachment crown protein stereocilin 174 
(STRC)(Verpy et al., 2011); and transient ankle link component Vlgr1 (Goodyear & Richardson, 1999), have 175 
been demonstrated to be spatially refined, concentrated, or redistributed, following their initially more diffuse 176 
expression on the developing bundle. To determine the distribution pattern of PKHDlL1 on OHC bundles 177 
during this developmental period, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) immunogold labeling of PKHD1L1 was 178 
carried out on cochleae between P4 - P10 (Fig. 3).  179 
Since EDTA decalcification is required for tissue dissection to enable immunogold SEM analysis in cochleae 180 
older than P6, we first evaluated the effect of EDTA treatment on immunogold labeling efficiency. There was 181 
no significant difference in the number of gold particles observed on stereocilia bundles in P4 mouse cochleae, 182 
processed with and without decalcification buffer (Sup. Fig. 3).  183 
In agreement with immunofluorescence data (Fig 2), immunogold labeling on OHC bundles was observed 184 
between P4–P10. Labeling is greatest at P4 with almost no labeling observed by P10 (Fig. 3). Very little gold 185 
labeling was detected in IHCs, unlike that observed in immunofluorescence (Fig. 3b compared to Fig. 2.). This 186 
is likely as a result of reduced labeling efficiency of the immunogold technique. 187 
Upon close inspection of gold beads at the surface of stereocilia bundles, PKHD1L1 did not appear to localize 188 
to any specific areas or links prior to the cessation of its expression at P10 (Fig. 3a). In agreement with 189 
previously reported data at P4 (Wu et al., 2019), PKHD1L1 is found on all three stereocilia rows throughout 190 
development. To evaluate the distribution of PKHD1L1 on specific stereocilia rows of OHC bundles during its 191 
expression, we quantified the proportion of gold particles located on the tallest, middle and short stereocilia 192 
rows at each time point (Fig. 3d,e). There is a slight increase in the proportion of gold beads located on the 193 
tallest row, and decrease on the shortest row, between P4 and P6. However, this does not represent a 194 
substantive change in the overall distribution of PKHD1L1 between the stereocilia rows. We therefore conclude 195 
that PKHD1L1 is not spatially refined or redistributed on the surface of OHC hair bundles during development. 196

PKHD1L1 contains potential proteolytic cleavage sites 201 
To reconcile our findings of Pkhd1l1 mRNA expression in adult IHC with the lack of antibody labeling of the 202 
PKHD1L1 protein, we looked more closely at the structure of PKHD1L1 and antibody epitope (Fig. 4). We 203 
previously reported that PKHD1L1 labelling, unlike tip links and horizontal top connectors, is sensitive to 204 
treatment with protease subtilisin (R. J. Goodyear et al., 2005; Wu et al., 2019). To investigate the possibility of 205 
PKHD1L1 cleavage further we carried out cleavage site prediction on the protein sequence of Pkhd1l1 206 
orthologs from 10 species (Sup. Table 1). Using the ProP 1.0 online prediction tool (Duckert et al., 2004), we 207 
identified three potential cleavage sites in the PKHD1L1 sequence (numbered based upon the mouse 208 
sequence): p.R3566, p.R3976, and p.R3986 (Sup. Fig. 4).  209 
We utilized Alphafold2 to model the structure of a PKHD1L1 fragment (p.3011-p.4249) and mapped the 210 
potential cleavage sites (Fig. 4a-d). Site 1 (p.R3566), with a confidence score below threshold in mice but 211 
present in other species, is located in a parallel beta-helix repeat (PbH1) rich region with structural homology to 212 
the human TMEM2 protein ectodomain (Fig. 4a-c). While site 2 (p.R3976), not identified in other species, and 213 
with a confidence score below threshold, and site 3 (p.R3986, with the highest confidence score in all species) 214 
are located within a domain with structural homology to the PCDH15 MAD12 domain (Fig. 4a,b & d). Notably, 215 
cleavage site 3 is highly conserved across 10 different orthologs and lies within an exposed loop of PKHD1L1 216 
MAD-like domain (Fig. 4e). Proteolytic cleavage at site 3 would release an extracellular protein fragment 217 
containing the NBP2-13765 antibody epitope (Fig. 4 lime), thus hindering the labeling of the remaining 218 
membrane-tethered portion of the PKHD1L1 peptide by the NBP2-13765 antibody used for immunolabeling in 219 
this study. The remaining membrane-bound peptide would have an extracellular domain of just 236 amino 220 
acids. 221 
Although detected Pkhd1l1 mRNA and protein levels correlate well during early-postnatal development, this 222 
analysis suggests that protein cleavage could explain the discrepancy in mRNA detection and protein labeling 223 
in the IHCs of 9-month-old mice.  224

Tectorial membrane attachment crowns and imprints retain normal morphology in PKHD1L1-deficient 226 
mice 227 
The progressive hearing loss phenotype we previously observed in PKHD1L1-deficient mice (Wu et al., 2019) 228 
is similar to that observed in mice with tectorial membrane deficits, such as in STRC and CEACAM16-deficient 229 
mice (Goodyear et al., 2019; Verpy et al., 2008). Interaction with the tectorial membrane, established during 230 
early postnatal development, is critical for OHC function. We therefore sought to investigate the establishment, 231 
and maintenance of OHC attachment with the tectorial membrane in PKHD1L1-deficient mice as a possible 232 
source of pathology. 233 
STRC plays a key role in attachment crown formation between OHC tall row stereocilia and tectorial 234 
membrane (Verpy et al., 2011). We detected STRC at the tips of stereocilia in Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ mice, as 235 
in Pkhd1l1fl/fl littermate controls, by immunofluorescence at P35 – a time point when bundles and the tectorial 236 
membrane are fully developed (Fig. 5a,b). Immunogold SEM of bundles demonstrates the correct localization 237 
of STRC, primarily in halos around the tip of the tallest stereocilia, in addition to between stereocilia tips, in 238 
both PKHD1L1-deficient mice and littermate controls (Fig. 5c). We therefore conclude that PKHD1L1 is not 239 
required for the localization of STRC to attachment crowns or horizontal top connectors. Furthermore, OHC 240 
bundle imprints in the tectorial membrane, observed by SEM, still form in PKHD1L1-defficient mice, and were 241 
still present after 6 months (Fig. 5d). Together these data suggest that the establishment of OHC links with the 242 
TM are not disrupted in PKHD1L1-defficient mice.  243

Loss of PKHD1L1 induces late-onset progressive hearing loss and stereocilia disorganization 249 
Despite the restricted temporal expression of PKHD1L1 to early postnatal development, our previous study 250 
revealed progressive hearing loss in PKHD1L1-deficient mice, tested up to 6 months. However, no gross  251 
morphological changes were found during adolescence (Wu et al., 2019). We sought to further evaluate the 252 
progression of hearing loss in Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ conditional hair-cell-specific knock out (cKO) and 253 
constitutive Pkhd1l-/- KO mice as they age, in addition to conducting comprehensive stereocilia analysis by 254 
SEM in PKHD1L1-deficient mice during aging.  255 
The Atoh1-Cre+ line expresses Cre as early as embryonic day 10.5. In juveniles Cre expression, and in turn 256 
Pkhd1l1 KO in Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ mice, is limited to sensory hair cells, as well as some supporting cells 257 
and cochlear and vestibular sensory neurons (Matei et al., 2005). Hearing deficit was observed by ABR 258 
recordings in cKO Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ mice compared to Pkhd1l1fl/fl (Cre-) littermates (Fig. 6a). ABR 259 
thresholds were elevated at 6 weeks in the high-frequency region (32 kHz, 45 kHz), progressing to lower-260 
frequency regions (16 kHz, 22 kHz) by 9 months of age. Consistent with the predominant localization of 261 
PKHD1L1 to OHC bundles, hearing deficits were mirrored in DPOAE thresholds. Thresholds were elevated 262 
first at 32 kHz at 6 weeks, progressing in severity and frequency range by 9 months (Fig. 6a), indicative of 263 
progressive OHC dysfunction.  264 
The use of hair-cell-specific Pkhd1l1 KO in Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ mice is advantageous to mitigate against 265 
phenotypes related to possible unknown developmental or central processing functions of PKHD1L1 in other 266 
tissues and cell types. However, elevated ABR thresholds have been reported in mice with hair cells 267 
expressing Cre (Matern et al., 2017). In order to confirm that hearing deficit was not as a result of Cre 268 
expression, we generated constitutive KO Pkhd1l-/- mice. Constitutive KO of PKHD1L1 was not embryonically 269 
lethal; Pkhd1l-/- mice appear to develop normally and breed well. Elevated ABR and DPOAE thresholds were 270 
found in Pkhd1l-/- mice from 12 weeks, comparable to those observed in Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ mice, again 271 
progressing to lower frequencies with age, tested up to 15 months (Sup. Fig. 5). These data confirm 272 
progressive hearing deficits as a result of PKHD1L1 KO.  273 
The morphology of OHC stereocilia bundles was examined using SEM in Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ cKO hair cells 274 
during the progression of hearing deficit (Fig. 6b). Mild stereocilia loss (Fig. 6b, yellow asterisks) was observed 275 
after 6 weeks, becoming more pronounced over time. To analyze the progression of OHC stereocilia loss from 276 
as a result of aging, we calculated the number of missing stereocilia from each row of the bundle. Notably, 277 
stereocilia loss was most significant in the shortest row, progressing to the taller rows with age; most 278 
prominently in, but not limited to, the high frequency basal region (Fig. 6c - 32 kHz region, Sup. Fig. 6 - 22 kHz 279 
and 16 kHz regions). 280

Acoustic overexposure leads to permanent hearing loss in PKHD1L1-dificent mice 289 
Moderate noise trauma paradigms have been shown to induce temporary threshold shifts (TTS) in hearing 290 
function, which are subsequently recovered to normal levels (Jensen et al., 2015; Y. Wang et al., 2002). 291 
Despite the expression of PKHD1L1 being restricted to early postnatal development, PKHD1L1-deficient mice 292 
display delayed onset progressive phenotypes in ABRs, DPOAEs and OHC bundle morphology (Fig. 6 & Sup. 293 
Fig. 5). This could be indicative of a protein required for the establishment of more durable hair bundle 294 
morphology and function. To test this hypothesis, we assayed bundle and hearing heath and durability 295 
following noise overexposure. 296 
Exposure to 94 dB SPL 8-16 kHz bandpass noise for 2 hours was carried out at 6 weeks of age in Pkhd1l 297 
fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ mice and Pkhd1l1fl/fl littermate controls – a time point and frequency range at which there is no 298 
hearing deficit in Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ mice (Fig. 6a). Higher noise levels were initially trialed in wild-type 299 
mice, however these lead to permanent threshold shifts (PTS), and were therefore considered too damaging 300 
for BL6 background mice to recover from.  301 
ABRs and DPOAEs were measured at 1 day, 2 days, 2 weeks, and 8 weeks, after noise exposure and 302 
compared to baseline levels collected prior to the noise insult, in order to determine threshold shifts resulting 303 
from the noise exposure (Fig. 7a). In both Pkhd1l1fl/fl and Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ mice ABR and DPOAE 304 
thresholds were elevated in the 16-32 kHz region 1 day post noise exposure with some recovery after 2 days 305 
(Fig. 7b). Thresholds recovered back to baseline levels by 2 weeks post-noise exposure in Pkhd1l1fl/fl mice, 306 
consistent with TTS. Thresholds did not recover to baseline levels in Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ mice however, 307 
with threshold shifts maintained at 2 and 8 weeks following noise insult (Fig. 7b).  308 
In the absence of noise trauma (Sup. Fig. 7) threshold shifts of <10 dB were observed at high frequencies 309 
between the 6-week evaluation time point and an additional 8-week ‘recovery’ time point in both genotypes, as 310 
a result of aging in these mice. This does not however account for the large, pan-frequency, threshold shifts 311 
observed in PKHD1L1-deficient mice alone following noise exposure. These data therefore show the noise 312 
exposure, normally only sufficient to cause TTS in normal mice, leads to PTS in PKHD1L1-deficient mice. 313 
We examined OHC bundle morphology in Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ mice and Pkhd1l1fl/fl  littermates 2 and 8 314 
weeks following noise exposure, time points at which TTS were recovered in control mice but not PKHD1L1-315 
defficent mice (Fig. 8). In control Pkhd1l1fl/fl mice, bundles remained intact and well-organized at 2 and 8 weeks 316 
post-noise exposure. In contrast, in Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ mice, bundle disorganization was observed 2 317 
weeks following noise exposure (Fig. 8a), particularly in tallest row stereocilia, in addition to some loss of short 318 
row stereocilia, as expected at this age in PKHD1L1-defficient mice (comparison to Fig. 6b). By 8 weeks post-319 
noise exposure, bundles were further disrupted, with instances of tall row stereocilia loss and disorganization 320 
(Fig. 8b). These findings demonstrate that deficiency in PKHD1L1 increases susceptibility to bundle disruption 321 
during moderate acoustic overexposure, leading to permanent threshold shifts.  322

Discussion 327 
In this study we aim to better understand the role of PKHD1L1 in the sensory hair bundle and hearing function. 328 
Genetic perturbation of Pkhd1l1 does not cause overt morphological and functional disruption during 329 
development, however, still causes serious hearing-related deficit in adulthood.  330 
Here, we first fully characterized Pkhd1l1 gene and protein expression during development and in adult mice. 331 
We confirmed developmentally restricted expression of PKHD1L1 on the sensory hair bundles of inner and 332 
outer hair cells. We further demonstrated by immunogold SEM that PKHD1L1 does not localize or redistribute 333 
to specific stereocilia bundle structures during this developmental period. Furthermore, we observe progressive 334 
bundle disruption and missing stereocilia in mice deficient in PKHD1L1 from 6 weeks of age onwards. We 335 
report hearing threshold shifts, initially restricted to high frequencies, subsequently progressing to lower 336 
frequencies, in both conditional hair-cell specific and constitutive Pkhd1l1 KO-out mice. Finally, we show that 337 
PKHD1L1-deficient mice have an increased susceptibility to permanent hearing loss following moderate 338 
acoustic overexposure, implicating the expression of PKHD1L1 during development in the establishment of 339 
stable bundles that are robust to environmental insult in later life.  340

PKHD1L1 expression, localization, and potential cleavage 342 
PKHD1L1 protein was detected during the first 10 days of postnatal hair bundle development, primarily 343 
localized on the stereocilia of OHC, with some expression observed in IHC (Fig. 2&3, and Sup. Fig. 2). Gene 344 
expression analysis using RNAscope revealed corresponding early-postnatal Pkhd1l1 gene expression. 345 
However, unexpectedly, transcription of Pkhd1l1 was also observed in the IHCs of 9-month-old adult mice (Fig. 346 
1) despite the absence of corresponding protein detection (Fig. 2). This discrepancy suggests that PKHD1L1 347 
protein detection could be absent due to lack of mRNA translation in IHC at this time point, or that the 348 
translated protein was not detectable at the IHC stereocilia by immunolabeling. 349 
PKHD1L1 is predicted, in silico, to have a very short cytoplasmic domain consisting of only 6 amino acids, a 350 
single-pass transmembrane domain, and a very large 4202 amino acid extracellular domain (Fig. 4 models C-351 
terminal residues p.3011-p.4249). In our previous study we demonstrated that PKHD1L1 labeling (by antibody 352 
NBP2-13765, also used in this study, with an epitope between p.3670-p.3739 Fig. 4a lime) is sensitive to the 353 
protease subtilisin (Wu et al., 2019). Furthermore, PKHD1L1 has been identified in the ‘sheddome’ of activated 354 
blood platelets by mass spectrometry (Fong et al., 2011). The presence of PKHD1L1 following activation was 355 
substantially reduced by inhibitors of the protease ADAM17. These reports suggest that PKHD1L1 is 356 
susceptible to proteolytic cleavage in vitro and in vivo. 357 
Here, we identified potential proteolytic cleavage sites in PKHD1L1 by in silico analysis across Pkhd1l1 358 
orthologs. R3986 is highly conserved across 10 PKHD1L1 orthologs, and scores highly with the ProP cleavage 359 
site prediction tool (Duckert et al., 2004) (Sup. Fig. 4). This cleavage site is located in the MAD-like domain, 360 
with structural homology to the PCDH15 MAD12 domain (De-la-Torre et al., 2018). Referred elsewhere as the 361 
SEA domain, this domain occurs in many cell surface proteins, in which it undergoes proteolytic cleavage or 362 
autoproteolysis(Anderson et al., 2024; Pei & Grishin, 2017). Cleavage at R3986 would release an extracellular 363 
protein fragment containing the NBP2-13765 antibody epitope, preventing detection of the remaining 263 364 
amino acid PKHD1L1 fragment with this antibody. Therefore, PKHD1L1 protein cleavage could explain the 365 
discrepancy in Pkhd1l1 mRNA detection and protein labeling in the IHCs of 9-month-old mice, if Pkhd1l1 366 
mRNA is translated at this time point. In addition, PKHD1L1 splice variants lacking the NBP2-13765 epitope 367 
region in adult IHCs cannot be ruled out. However, to our knowledge, there have been no verified alternate 368 
PKHD1L1 isoforms reported in humans or mice in publicly accessible databases. 369 
Pkhd1l1 mRNA (Fig. 1) and protein (Fig. 2) detection in early development correlate well and cease before P24 370 
and P21 timepoints, respectively. We have not observed PKHD1L1 labeling on the tectorial membrane of 371

The role of PKHD1L1 and the stereocilia coat 378 
The function of the stereocilia coat during bundle maturation in early postnatal development remains unclear. 379 
Although often considered together, distinct from the developmentally transient surface coat labeled with tannic 380 
acid, the stereocilia and apical surface of hair cells are covered by a glycocalyx coat, which can be visualized 381 
by cationic dyes such as Alcian blue (Santi & Anderson, 1987). This glycocalyx coat persists in mature hair 382 
cells, and has been suggested to contribute to bundle cohesion during stimulation and/or preventing stereocilia 383 
fusion (Dolgobrodov et al., 2000a, 2000b; Karavitaki & Corey, 2010). However, PKHD1L1 expression is limited 384 
to early postnatal development (Fig. 2 & 3) and is primarily localized to the external face of the stereocilia 385 
rather than between them (Wu et al., 2019), suggesting a distinct role.  386 
Deficits in key mechanotransduction machinery or stereocilia link proteins often cause morphological disruption 387 
of the hair bundle during development, resulting in a profound hearing loss (reviewed by Petit and Richardson 388 
(Richardson & Petit, 2019)). In contrast, PKHD1L1-deficient mice undergo normal bundle maturation (Fig. 2 & 389 
3), and exhibit only high-frequency hearing loss at younger ages (Fig. 6 & Sup. Fig. 5). This indicates that 390 
PKHD1L1 expression during development is likely not essential for the gross maturation of the hair bundle, or 391 
the localization and function of the MET complex. 392 
We previously proposed that PKHD1L1 may play a role in establishing connection between the OHC 393 
stereocilia with the tectorial membrane. The late-onset mild hearing loss phenotype, and increased DPOAE 394 
thresholds, observed in PKHD1L1-deficient mice is similar to that of previously reported in CEACAM16-395 
deficient mice with TM deficits (Goodyear et al., 2019). Furthermore, PKHD1L1 is primarily located on OHC, 396 
towards the tips of the stereocilia (Fig. 3)(Wu et al., 2019), where TM attachment crowns form. Here, however, 397 
we present data that suggests the role of PKHD1L1 is neither (1) critical to, or (2) limited to, TM attachment 398 
crown formation.  399 
(1) PKHD1L1 is not critical for attachment crown formation. We observed normal localization of key attachment 400 
crown protein stereocilin in PKHD1L1-defficient mice (Fig. 5). Minor disruptions to bundle coherence are 401 
observed during development in animals with mutated attachment crown proteins stereocilin, otogelin, 402 
otogelin-like or tubby (Avan et al., 2019; Han et al., 2020). Similar disruption is also observed in PKHD1L1-403 
difficient mice, however after initially normal development. Furthermore, unlike in mice deficient in attachment 404 
crown proteins (Avan et al., 2019; Han et al., 2020), stereocilia imprints are still present in the tectorial 405 
membrane of PKHD1L1-defficient mice (Fig. 5d). TM imprints formed during development are likely permanent 406 
however (Morisaki et al., 1991), therefore these data do not preclude the possibility of TM attachments 407 
weakening in PKHD1L1-defficient mice as they age and/or are exposed to noise insult.  408 
(2) PKHD1L1 function is likely not limited to attachment crown formation. PKHD1L1 is observed on the surface 409 
of IHCs (Fig. 2), which do not form attachment with the TM. Attachment crowns only form on the tallest row of 410 
OHC stereocilia, and as such stereocilin redistributes to OHC tall row stereocilia during development (Verpy et 411 
al., 2011). PKHD1L1, on the other hand, is expressed on all stereocilia rows and no major redistribution or 412 
refinement in the localization of PKHD1L1 to the tallest row of stereocilia is observed during its expression 413 
period (Fig. 3). Finally, stereocilia loss is observed in PKHD1L1-deficient mice, initially primarily in short-row 414 
stereocilia (Fig. 6b & c). This phenotype is unlikely to be caused by disruption of tall row stereocilia attachment 415 
to the TM. 416

PKHD1L1 is required for the formation of robust stereocilia bundles in noise trauma and aging 432 
As hair cells in the cochlea cannot regenerate once lost, the development of cells and structures resilient to 433 
damage during a lifetime, and mechanisms to absorb and repair damage, are essential for hearing longevity. 434 
Moderate acoustic overexposure induces temporary shifts in hearing thresholds (Wang et al., 2002). Upon 435 
recovery, normal mice exhibit no disruption to hair bundle morphology (observed Fig. 7 Pkhd1l1fl/fl controls), 436 
however, TTS-levels of acoustic overexposure can induce other lasting neuronal pathologies outside the scope 437 
of this study (Fernandez et al., 2020; Kujawa & Liberman, 2009; Liberman & Dodds, 1987). In the absence of 438 
PKHD1L1, threshold shifts in response to moderate noise insult are not reversed, concurrent with 439 
morphological disruption of stereocilia bundles (Fig. 7 & 8). We propose that PKHD1L1 expression during early 440 
postnatal bundle maturation is required for the establishment of robust sensory bundles, resilient to damage 441 
and disruption in response to noise exposure and aging. 442 
We previously reported elevated ABR and DPOAE thresholds in Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ mice as early as 3 443 
weeks of age in high frequency regions, progressing across all tested frequencies by 6 months (Wu et al., 444 
2019). In the present study we again report progressive hearing loss, initially at high frequencies, however, 445 
elevations in ABR and DPOAE thresholds were less pronounced and progressed to lower frequencies more 446 
slowly than observed previously (Fig. 6). We further verified these findings by generating and evaluating the 447 
hearing function in constitutive Pkhd1l-/- KO mice, in which a similar phenotype is observed (Sup. Fig. 5).  448 
The ABR and DPOAE recordings in these two studies were carried out in different institutions, however in both 449 
instances PKHD1L1-deficient mice were compared to littermate controls. As we have determined that 450 
PKHD1L1-defficient bundles are more susceptible to noise-induced damage, the variability in the acoustic 451 
environment between the animal facilities experienced by PKHD1L1-defficient mice used in these studies may 452 
account for the differences seen in hearing thresholds. Interestingly, audiogram profiles of human patients with 453 
non-syndromic hearing loss and biallelic PKHD1L1 variants were also diverse (Redfield et al., 2024). As 454 
PKHD1L1 variants may render hair cells more vulnerable to damage due to environmental factors, it is likely 455 
that in addition to the potential differences in the damaging effect of distinct PKHD1L1 variants at the protein 456 
level, hearing loss profiles in affected probands may also reflect differing levels of acoustic exposure 457 
throughout their lives. 458

2024), the function of PKHD1L1 requires further study. The role of the developmental stereocilia coat remains 461 
unclear. Although PKHD1L1 does not localize to any known link structures during its expression period, 462

In conclusion, although the specific molecular function of PKHD1L1 remains elusive, in the present study we 475 
build on our current understanding of the role of PKHD1L1 in hearing and deafness. After comprehensive 476 
characterization of Pkhd1l1 gene and protein expression during development and in adult mice, we find 477 
PKHD1L1 protein expression on the stereocilia bundle is restricted to early postnatal development. PKHD1L1-478 
deficient mice display no disruption to bundle cohesion or tectorial membrane attachment-crown formation 479 
during development. However, progressive bundle disruption and missing stereocilia are later observed in mice 480 
deficient in PKHD1L1, with concurrent delayed onset progressive hearing loss. Furthermore, we show that 481 
PKHD1L1-deficient mice are susceptible to permanent hearing loss following moderate acoustic overexposure, 482 
at noise levels that induce only temporary thresholds shifts in wild-type mice. Here therefore, we propose that 483 
PKHD1L1 expression is necessary for the establishment of robust sensory bundles that are required for 484 
prolonged hearing function and resilience to noise exposure.   485

Generation of Knockout Mice 487 
We transferred Pkhd1l1fl/fl::Atoh1-Cre+ mice from Corey Lab at Harvard Medical School to Mass Eye and Ear 488 
Animal Care Facility. These mice exhibit PKHD1L1 deficiency selectively in hair cells, triggered by Cre 489 
recombinase expression driven by the Atoh1 promotor. As previously reported (Wu et al., 2019), the Pkhd1l1fl/fl 490 
allele contains 2 loxP sites flanking exon 10, encoding part of the second IPT domain of PKHD1L1. Upon 491 
Atoh1 expression in hair cells, Cre recombinase deletes exon 10 (71 bp), leading to a frameshift and a 492 
premature stop codon downstream of the excised region (Wu et al., 2019). The resulting PKHD1L1 peptide is 493 
258 amino acids (including signal peptide), and consists of a single complete non-membrane-bound IPT 494 
domain, an incomplete IPT domain, and 11 new amino acids before a the premature stop codon (Fig. 4a). 495 
To generate a constitutive KO model, Pkhd1l1fl/fl mice were crossed with an EIIa-Cre mouse line (The Jackson 496 
Laboratory, B6.FVB-Tg(EIIa-cre)C5379Lmgd/J, #003724) to delete exon 10 from germline. Once homozygous 497 
Pkhd1l1-/- alleles were established, the EIIa-Cre allele was bred out of the colony. Genomic PCR implemented 498 
the following primer pairs: Pkhd1l1fl-F (TGA CAC AAC ATA CTG AGC AT) and Pkhd1l1fl-R (GGA AAC TCC 499 
TGT TGA AAC AA), yielding a 624 bp fl band and 438 bp WT band; Pkhd1l1KO-F 500 
(TGACACAACATACTGAGCAT) and Pkhd1l1KO-R (TTTGAAGACCACACTGAGAG), yielding a 422 bp KO 501 
band and 828 bp WT band; EIIA-Cre-F (ATTTGCCTGCATTACCGGTC) and EIIa-Cre-R 502 
(ATCAACGTTTTCTTTTCGGA), yielding a 349 bp Cre band. 503

Protein sequence analysis and prediction of PKHD1L1 cleavage sites 586 
To predict potential cleavage sites in the PKHD1L1 protein sequence, we compared 10 different PKHD1L1 587 
ortholog sequences extracted from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) protein database 588 
(Sup. Table S1). Species were chosen based on sequence availability and taxonomical diversity. To analyze 589 
the complete sequences, each was split into two protein fragments with < 4,000 amino acids and uploaded into 590 
the ProP 1.0. online server tool (Duckert et al., 2004). In Sup. Figure 7 the cleavage site prediction for the 591 
Human PKHD1L1 is showed comprising the residue p.R3012 to its C-terminal ending residue. The equivalent 592 
protein fragments were selected after protein sequence alignment for the other 9 orthologs (See Sup. Table S1 593 
for NCBI residue numbering including signal peptides). Cleavage sites were predicted using the ProP 1.0. 594 
default values. This online tool predicts arginine (R) and lysine (K) pro-peptide cleavage sites using an 595 
ensemble of neural networks. Scores of > 0.5 indicate that the residue is predicted to be followed by a pro-596 
peptide cleavage site. The higher the score the more confident the prediction.  597 
For sequence identity analysis of the PKHD1L1 cleavage site regions sequences were aligned with the 598 
ClustalW algorithm (Larkin et al., 2007) on Geneious using the default parameters and were color-coded by 599 
similarity (Kearse et al., 2012). 600

ABR and DPOAE recordings 615 
ABR and DPOAE thresholds were measured using an EPL Acoustic system in an acoustically and electrically 616 
shielded soundproof chamber, as described previously (Kujawa & Liberman, 2009). Mice were anesthetized 617 
via intraperitoneal injection of 100 mg/kg ketamine and 10 mg/kg xylazine. Artificial tears were applied to 618 
protect the animal’s eyes. The outer ear canals were exposed and the tympanic membranes were examined to 619 
ensure normal appearance before calibrated earphone placement. Three subdermal needle electrodes were 620 
inserted between the ears (reference), behind the pinna (recording), and near the tail (ground). Upon 621 
completion, anesthetized animals were placed on a 37°C heating pad for recovery.  622 
Tone-pip ABR stimuli were varied from 4 to 45 kHz, each had a 5 ms duration, 0.5 ms rise-fall time, and of an 623 
alternating polarity at 30 Hz. For each pure tone frequency tested, the sound pressure level (SPL) ranged from 624 
20-80 dB. Responses were amplified (10,000x), filtered (0.3-3 kHz), and averaged (512x) by the Cochlear 625 
Function Test Suite. ABR thresholds were determined by the lowest SPL at which a recurring 5-peak waveform 626 
was observed. Thresholds were initially assessed by the experiment manually, then confirmed by an 627 
independent researcher by automated analysis with ABR Peak Analysis software (Eaton-Peabody 628 
Laboratories(Suthakar & Liberman, 2019)). Thresholds were averaged from the analysis of 2 independent 629 
researchers at any points of disagreement.  630 
DPOAEs were detected in response to 2 simultaneous pure tones, f1 and f2 (f2/f1 = 1.2; f2 was varied from 4 631 
to 45 kHz in half-octave steps and 10-80 dB SPL in 5 dB increments) with the level ratio L1=L2+10. The 632 
algorithm to determine the DPOAE threshold for each frequency was an estimate of the crossing point of two 633 
curves, 2f1-f2 (dB) and 2f1-f2Nse (dB). No distinction was made between a precisely 80 dB threshold and one 634 
that was above the testing limit. All data were plotted and analyzed in GraphPad Prism. 635

Acoustic overexposure 637 
Following baseline ABR and DPOAE analysis (carried out as described above), awake 6-week-old mice were 638 
placed unrestrained in small cages and exposed to an octave-band noise (8–16 kHz) for 2 hours at 94 dB SPL, 639 
which is identified as the highest non-neuropathic noise level associated with temporary threshold shifts 640 
(Jensen et al., 2015). Sound exposure was created by a white-noise source, filtered, amplified, and delivered 641 
through a JBL compression driver coupled to an exponential horn attached to the top of the exposure booth. 642 
Exposure levels were measured in each cage with a 0.25 inch Brüel and Kjær condenser microphone. ABR 643 
and DPOAEs were measured 1 day, 2 days, 2 weeks, and 8 weeks after noise exposure. Threshold shifts from 644 
baseline were calculated for each mouse at each frequency and time point. Control mice received no noise 645 
exposure. All data were plotted and analyzed in GraphPad Prism.  646
