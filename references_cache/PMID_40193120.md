IMPORTANCE: Invasive group A Streptococcus (GAS) infections are associated with 
substantial morbidity, mortality, and economic burden.
OBJECTIVE: To update trends in invasive GAS disease incidence rates in 10 US 
states between 2013 and 2022.
DESIGN, SETTING, AND PARTICIPANTS: Clinical, demographic, and laboratory data 
for invasive GAS cases were collected as part of population-based surveillance 
in the Active Bacterial Core surveillance network covering 34.9 million persons 
across 10 US states. A case was defined as isolation of GAS from a normally 
sterile site or from a wound in a patient with necrotizing fasciitis or 
streptococcal toxic shock syndrome between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 
2022. Demographic and clinical data were collected from medical record review. 
From 2013 to 2014, available isolates were emm typed and antimicrobial 
susceptibilities determined using conventional methods; from 2015 onward, 
whole-genome sequencing was used.
MAIN OUTCOMES AND MEASURES: Incidence rates by sex, age, race, and selected risk 
factors; clinical syndromes, outcomes, and underlying patient conditions; and 
isolate characteristics, including antimicrobial susceptibility.
RESULTS: Surveillance in 10 US states identified 21 312 cases of invasive GAS 
from 2013 through 2022, including 1981 deaths. The majority of cases (57.5%) 
were in males. Among case-patients, 1272 (6.0%) were aged 0 to 17 years, 13 565 
(63.7%) were aged 18 to 64 years, and 6474 (30.4%) were 65 years or older; 5.5% 
were American Indian or Alaska Native, 14.3% were Black, and 67.1% were White. 
Incidence rose from 3.6 per 100 000 persons in 2013 to 8.2 per 100 000 persons 
in 2022 (P < .001 for trend). Incidence was highest among persons 65 years or 
older; however, the relative increase over time was greatest among adults aged 
18 to 64 years (3.2 to 8.7 per 100 000 persons). Incidence was higher among 
American Indian or Alaska Native persons than in other racial and ethnic groups. 
People experiencing homelessness, people who inject drugs, and residents of 
long-term care facilities had substantially elevated GAS incidence rates. Among 
tested isolates, those nonsusceptible to macrolides and clindamycin increased 
from 12.7% in 2013 to 33.1% in 2022.
CONCLUSIONS: Invasive GAS infections increased substantially in 10 US states 
during a surveillance period from 2013 to 2022. Accelerated efforts to prevent 
and control GAS are needed, especially among groups at highest risk of 
infection.

This population-based surveillance study investigates trends in the incidence, characteristics, antimicrobial resistance, and outcomes of invasive group AStreptococcusinfections among individuals in 10 US states.

Group AStreptococcus(GAS) causes a spectrum of clinical syndromes, from noninvasive diseases, such as pharyngitis and impetigo, to invasive infections with high mortality, such as sepsis, necrotizing fasciitis, and streptococcal toxic shock syndrome. In the US, health care costs from invasive GAS are estimated to exceed $3.8 billion annually.1

Since 1995, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has conducted surveillance for invasive GAS infections through Active Bacterial Core surveillance (ABCs) sites of the Emerging Infections Program network.2From 1995 to 2012, rates of invasive GAS disease in ABCs remained relatively stable at 3.2 to 4.3 cases per 100 000 persons3,4,5; however, incidence of invasive GAS began to rise in 2014,6a trend also documented in geographically disparate countries.7,8,9,10Along with rising incidence, resistance among ABCs GAS isolates to macrolides and clindamycin also increased.11

Certain populations are disproportionately affected by invasive GAS. Adults 65 years and older and residents of long-term care facilities are at elevated risk for both disease and death.12GAS outbreaks are increasingly reported among people experiencing homelessness and people who inject drugs.13,14Incidence rates among Indigenous populations are several times higher than rates among non-Indigenous persons in Alaska,15Australia,7Canada,16and New Zealand.17

This study reviewed 10 years of laboratory- and population-based data to evaluate trends in the incidence, patient and strain characteristics, antimicrobial resistance, and outcomes associated with invasive GAS infections in 10 US states from 2013 to 2022.

Invasive GAS cases from January 1, 2013, through December 31, 2022, were identified through active surveillance for invasive bacterial pathogens in 10 US states, covering 34 991 238 persons in 2022 (~10.5% of the US population) (eTable 1 inSupplement 1). Surveillance sites included the areas around San Francisco, California (3 counties), Denver, Colorado (5 counties), Atlanta, Georgia (20 counties), Baltimore, Maryland (6 counties), Portland, Oregon (3 counties), and Albany and Rochester, New York (15 counties); 20 urban counties in Tennessee; and the states of Connecticut, Minnesota, and New Mexico (eTable 2 inSupplement 1). An invasive GAS case was defined as isolation of GAS or detection of GAS-specific nucleic acid from a normally sterile body site or from a wound accompanied by a diagnosis in the medical record of necrotizing fasciitis or streptococcal toxic shock syndrome in a resident of the catchment areas. Patient demographic and clinical information were extracted from medical records, and audits of all microbiology laboratories in the catchment areas were conducted at least yearly to ensure completeness of case ascertainment. Because risk of invasive GAS infection may vary by race and ethnicity, as part of the demographic information, race and ethnicity were obtained from medical records. Race and ethnicity determinations were made by health care staff not involved in ABCs and categorized by ABCs staff during data extraction using predefined race and ethnicity categories in accordance with Office of Management and Budget standards. Surveillance methods for GAS in ABCs have been previously described4(eAppendix inSupplement 1).

ABCs categorized persons as experiencing homelessness if the medical record documented that at the time of first positive culture, they were without permanent housing (eAppendix inSupplement 1). Residence in a long-term care facility was determined via documentation in the medical record that at the time of culture, a person resided in a nursing home, skilled nursing facility, inpatient hospice, or physical rehabilitation facility. Persons were categorized as injecting drugs if injection drug use within the past 12 months was documented in the medical record at the time of microbiologic culture. Postpartum infections were any infection occurring within 30 days following delivery or miscarriage. Underlying health conditions, risk factors, clinical syndromes, and hospital-acquired and pregnancy-related infections were defined similarly to previous ABCs publications.2,3,4,5This activity was reviewed by the CDC, deemed not to be human participants research, and was conducted in accordance with applicable federal law and CDC policy.18,19,20,21,22ABCs sites obtained ethics approval from their respective state health department and academic partner institutional review boards as needed.

Available isolates were characterized at the CDC’sStreptococcuslaboratory after GAS confirmation. Variations in the surface M protein were used to classify GAS isolates into subtypes. Classically, this was done by serological testing but more recently by sequencing of the 5′ end of the M protein (emm) gene. Isolates collected from 2013 to 2014 wereemmtyped by sequencing the M serotype-specific region of theemmgene; isolates collected from 2015 to 2022 were characterized through whole-genome sequencing (WGS).23Through 2015, isolates were tested for antimicrobial susceptibility using broth microdilution, and minimum inhibitory concentrations were interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines. Starting in 2015, antimicrobial phenotypes were predicted from WGS data; clindamycin nonsusceptibility predicted from WGS data based on presence ofermfamily genes did not discriminate between inducible and constitutive resistance.11All isolates in 2015, and 10% of isolates from 2016 to 2022, were also tested via broth microdilution as previously described.23,24

Missing data for race, age, sex, and hospitalization status were multiply imputed using sequential regression methods. We used regional and national postcensal population estimates for each year as denominators. All incidence rates are expressed as cases per 100 000 persons per year (hereafter,per 100 000). Case fatality rates were calculated using outcome at hospital discharge. Trends were assessed using the Cochran-Armitage test (2-sidedP< .05 considered significant); the Mann-Kendall test or percentage change in proportion over time were reported when data were not linear.

As in a previous publication,25the percentage of case-patients identified by ABCs with documented injection drug use, homelessness, or long-term care facility residence was multiplied by the annual estimate of invasive GAS cases to estimate numerator data. Incidence among people who inject drugs was calculated for 2018 using a published denominator26for persons 18 years or older. Incidence among people experiencing homelessness was calculated for 2013 to 2022 using the national annual point-in-time estimate from the US Department of Housing and Urban Development27as the denominator. The number of long-term care facility residents in the US for denominators in 2018 and 2020 came from the National Center for Health Statistics28,29(eAppendix inSupplement 1).

From 2013 through 2022, surveillance in 10 US states identified 21 312 cases of invasive GAS (2013, n = 1190; 2022, n = 2875). Most patients (95% [n = 20 247]) were hospitalized, 39.6% (8014) were admitted to an intensive care unit, and 9.3% (1981) died. Of case-patients, 57.5% were male; 6.0% (1272) were aged 0 to 17 years, 63.7% (13 565) were aged 18 to 64 years, and 30.4% (6474) were 65 years or older; and 5.5% were American Indian or Alaska Native, 2.5% were Asian, 14.3% were Black, less than 1% were Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, 67.1% were White, and race and ethnicity data were missing for 9.6% of patients (eTable 3 inSupplement 1).

Incidence of invasive GAS in ABCs areas increased from 3.6 per 100 000 in 2013 (Figure 1A) to 8.2 per 100 000 in 2022 (P< .001 for trend). Local increases in incidence ranged from 3.3 per 100 000 in 2013 to 4.0 per 100 000 in 2022 in Connecticut (incidence rate ratio [IRR], 1.2 [95% CI, 0.9-1.5]) and 3.5 to 16.2 per 100 000 in Oregon (IRR, 4.7 [95% CI, 3.5-6.2]) (eTable 4 inSupplement 1). The case fatality rate ranged from 8.2% in 2017 to 10.4% in 2014.

Annual incidence of invasive GAS from 2013 through 2022 among children younger than 18 years ranged from 0.6 to 2.1 per 100 000, with no sustained trend over time; however, during the COVID-19 pandemic, incidence rates declined by 73% between 2019 (2.0 per 100 000) through 2021 (0.6 per 100 000) (Figure 1B). Incidence was highest among children younger than 2 years (3.7 per 100 000) and lowest for those aged 5 to 17 years (1.2 per 100 000). Annual incidence was higher in males (1.9 per 100 000) than females (1.4 per 100 000; IRR, 1.3 [95% CI, 1.2-1.5]) and in Black children (2.0 per 100 000) than White children (1.6 per 100 000; IRR, 1.2 [95% CI, 1.1-1.4]). Incidence rates were higher for American Indian or Alaska Native children (3.6 per 100 000), particularly for children younger than 2 years (IRR, 3.2 per 100 000 [95% CI, 2.1-5.0]) compared with other racial groups.

Cellulitis accompanied by bacteremia (21.4%), bacteremia without focus (20.5%), and abscess (19.3%) were the predominant syndromes observed among children (eTable 5 inSupplement 1). Only 30.1% of children younger than 18 years with invasive GAS had an underlying condition. The case fatality rate of pediatric cases was 2.7%.

From 2013 through 2022, incidence rates in ABCs sites increased from 9.1 to 15.2 per 100 000 among those 65 years and older (IRR, 1.7 [95% CI, 1.5-1.9]) and from 3.2 to 8.7 per 100 000 among those aged 18 to 64 years (IRR, 2.7 [95% CI, 2.4-2.9]) (Figure 1B). Incidence was highest among individuals 85 years or older (19.6 per 100 000) followed by those aged 75 to 84 years (14.4 per 100 000). Compared with 2019, incidence during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020-2021) declined by 33% among persons 65 years or older and 3% among those aged 18 to 64 years.

Incidence was higher among males (8.9 per 100 000) than females (6.2 per 100 000; IRR, 1.4 [95% CI, 1.4-1.5]). Although they comprised only 5.6% of overall cases, incidence rates among American Indian or Alaska Native adults (33.3 per 100 000) were more than 4 times higher than for White (7.7 per 100 000) or Black (7.2 per 100 000) adults and more than 10 times higher than for Asian adults (2.5 per 100 000). Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander adults had the second-highest incidence rate (19.5 per 100 000). Incidence rates among adults increased for all racial and ethnic groups from 2013 through 2022 (Figure 1C), except for Asian persons. The incidence rate difference between American Indian or Alaska Native (39.0 per 100 000) and White adults (10.9 per 100 000) in 2022 was 28.1 and between Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander (28.1 per 100 000) and White adults was 17.2.

Bacteremic cellulitis (44.6%) was the most common syndrome, followed by septic shock (19.2%), bacteremia without focus (18.0%), and pneumonia (13.4%) (eTable 5 inSupplement 1). Cellulitis increased from 41.2% to 47.8% of cases (P< .001) between 2013 and 2022 and was more prevalent among people experiencing homelessness, people who inject drugs, and persons 65 years or older. The highest case fatality rates were observed among patients with nonfocal infections (bacteremia, septic shock, streptococcal toxic shock syndrome; 19.5%), necrotizing fasciitis (17.8%), pneumonia (15.7%), abdominal or peritoneal infection (13.7%), and meningitis (13.7%). Case fatality rates increased from 3.5% in persons aged 18 to 34 years to 21.9% in adults 85 years or older.

Of the 19 956 cases in adults with available data, 6.5% of patients resided in a long-term care facility. There was no significant change in the percentage of cases in long-term care facility residents between 2013 and 2022 (Table). Incidence for long-term care facility residents was 101.1 per 100 000 in 2018 and 75.4 per 100 000 in 2020; the case fatality rate for long-term care facility residents was 17.7%, without significant changes between 2013 and 2022.

Of the 19 515 case-patients with available data on substance use, the number of cases among persons with documented injection drug use (Table) increased from 59 in 2013 to 515 in 2022. The number of cases among people experiencing homelessness increased from 52 in 2013 to 493 in 2022; concomitant injection drug use was documented in 45.6% of case-patients experiencing homelessness. The estimated US rate of invasive GAS among people 18 years or older who inject drugs was 97.0 per 100 000 in 2018.

The percentage of adult patients with 1 or more underlying conditions increased from 86.6% (937 of 1082) in 2013 to 93% (2567 of 2759) in 2022 (Table). The most common underlying conditions were obesity (36.2%), smoking (33.4%), diabetes (29.9%), and acute skin breakdown (28.7%) (Table). Diabetes (40%), acute skin breakdown (36.3%), and liver disease (19%) were more prevalent among American Indian adults with invasive GAS than among Asian, Black, or White patients. Among people who inject drugs, the most common underlying conditions included acute skin breakdown (59.6%) and chronic hepatitis C (37%); for people experiencing homelessness, smoking (66.8%) and acute skin breakdown (46%) were the most common underlying conditions. Among women aged 15 to 44 years, 6.9% (n = 205) of invasive GAS infections were related to pregnancy (1.0% of all cases) and 9.5% (n = 282) were in postpartum individuals (1.3% of all cases).

Streptococcal isolates were available for 18 358 cases (86.1%). The number of isolates available foremmtyping per year increased from 952 in 2013 to 2469 in 2022. GAS isolates were predominantly from blood (89%), joint space (6%), muscle (2%), and pleural fluid or tissue (2%). Eighteenemmtypes accounted for 83.2% of isolates from 2013 to 2022. Among pediatric cases, 3emmtypes (1, 12, 4) accounted for 57% of isolates.

Emmpattern A to C isolates (ie,emmtypes 1, 12), having a stronger association with throat infection than skin infection,30comprised 31% to 44% of the most common isolates from 2013 to 2019 but disproportionately decreased from 2020 through 2021 (Figure 2) (eFigure inSupplement 1). Conversely, isolates from severalemmtypes more frequently associated with skin infections increased in this period, includingemm49(from 113 in 2019 to 279 in 2021) andemm77(from 67 in 2019 to 140 in 2021). Formerly uncommonemmtypes (43, 49, 60, 83), which collectively accounted for 0.3% (n = 3) of isolates in 2013, expanded before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic to account for 26.9% (n = 517) of isolates in 2022. Otheremmtypes, which substantially expanded between 2013 and 2022, included 11, 81, 82, and 92 (Figure 2) (eFigure inSupplement 1). Theseemmtypes were particularly prevalent among people experiencing homelessness, people who inject drugs, long-term care facility residents, and American Indian or Alaska Native persons (data not included).

All streptococcal isolates were susceptible to β-lactam antibiotics (eg, ampicillin, penicillin, cefotaxime) and vancomycin; 1.3% (228 isolates) were nonsusceptible to levofloxacin. Tetracycline nonsusceptibility increased from 16.2% in 2013 to 45.1% in 2022. The percentage of isolates that were nonsusceptible to both macrolides and clindamycin increased from 12.7% (120 isolates) in 2013 to 33.1% (814 isolates) in 2022 (Figure 3).

The number of invasive GAS cases and deaths in active, population-based surveillance covering nearly 35 million people in 10 US states more than doubled from 2013 to 2022. Steadily increasing GAS incidence rates during this period followed stable incidence from the initiation of surveillance in 1995 through 2012.3,4,5The increase reflects substantially elevated rates in relatively small population groups and increasing cases in the much larger number of adults with underlying health conditions.

Prior genomic analyses from ABCs have demonstrated that cases among residents of long-term care facilities, people experiencing homelessness, and people with injection drug use, in addition to being more frequent, are more likely to occur in clusters.31However, these genomic clusters consist of only 15 cases on average, often with an extended duration between cases,32making it challenging to separate small outbreaks from unlinked community transmission. Large outbreaks or clonal expansion of a single strain do not primarily account for the increase in invasive GAS cases between 2013 and 2022, which instead reflect the emergence and expansion of multiple lineages among at-risk populations.

Incidence of invasive GAS remains highest in adults 65 years or older. However, since 2017, adults aged 18 to 64 years have surpassed children younger than 2 years as the age group with the second-highest incidence. In prior ABCs data, invasive GAS incidence was higher in Black than White persons3,5; greater increases in disease incidence among White adults have eliminated this difference. American Indian or Alaska Native persons have significantly higher incidence compared with other racial groups among both children and adults, with the absolute rate difference increasing from 2013 to 2022. Increased GAS cases among Indigenous persons have been similarly noted elsewhere.7,15,16,17,33,34,35Strain variation, community-level socioeconomic factors, and differences in underlying conditions have been suggested as reasons for these disparities.16,36Study data suggest roles for both certain predisposing health conditions, such as diabetes and skin breakdown, and an association with expanding GAS strains with a propensity for skin infection.

Residents of long-term care facilities are particularly vulnerable to GAS-associated disease and death due to increasing age; prevalence of underlying medical conditions, including skin breakdown; and the occurrence of GAS outbreaks in congregate facilities.12Early detection and response to outbreaks in these facilities have the potential to reduce the high incidence of GAS infections in this population.37An increasing prevalence of underlying health conditions associated with invasive GAS likely contributed to rising incidence. The prevalence of underlying conditions among adult patients with invasive GAS increased over the study period, with obesity and diabetes being the most common. Chronic hepatitis C was documented in more than 12% of identified cases, although estimated to be present in 2% to 3% of the US population.38An Australian publication reported a 6-fold increased incidence of invasive GAS among persons with chronic hepatitis C and an 18-fold higher incidence in persons with hepatitis C who also inject drugs.39

Invasive GAS incidence in 2018 among people who inject drugs markedly increased from a previous ABCs estimate for 2011.25The rising invasive GAS incidence in people who inject drugs occurred over a period when injection drug use,26particularly illicitly manufactured fentanyl, substantially increased in the US.40The relatively short duration of effect of fentanyl has been linked to more frequent injections and higher-risk injection practices, including syringe sharing.41The association between illicitly manufactured fentanyl use and hepatitis C virus seroconversion has been documented42and a similarly elevated risk with illicitly manufactured fentanyl use may exist for invasive GAS as for hepatitis C virus, which saw a contemporaneous increase in incidence since 2013. Skin breakdown, abscess, and cellulitis are common among people who inject drugs or experience homelessness.43,44,45,46People who inject drugs presenting to emergency departments with skin and soft tissue infections are at elevated risk of subsequent hospitalization and death.47,48Reducing barriers to timely access to wound care and harm reduction services, such as medication-assisted treatment and syringe service programs, and prompt treatment of minor skin infections may help prevent the development of invasive GAS. Understanding the different risks for GAS infection among sheltered and unsheltered homeless populations and the potential impact of sanctioned encampments with access to portable hygiene facilities and other innovative housing approaches could be further explored.49

An increasing diversity ofemmtypes was isolated from invasive GAS cases over time. Formerly rare serotypes, such as 43, 49, 60, 81, and 83, were responsible for approximately 40% of the increase in infections over the past 10 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic,emmtypes primarily associated with pharyngeal infection declined and isolates associated with skin infection continued to increase. The changingemmtype distribution contributed to an almost 3-fold rise in macrolide and clindamycin resistance from 2013 to 2022, continuing a previously noted trend.50US guidelines from 2014 recommend adding clindamycin to β-lactam antibiotics for treatment of severe GAS infections,51and a 2021 propensity-matched retrospective cohort study suggested a survival benefit for adjunctive clindamycin treatment in invasive GAS infections.52Evaluations of the effect of reduced clindamycin susceptibility on clinical outcomes are warranted.

This study has limitations. First, although the surveillance population was large, ABCs sites have several differences relative to the overall US population. ABCs counties include a higher percentage of urban areas and a lower percentage of persons below US census poverty thresholds. Second, ABCs data were collected by medical chart review, which may not include complete information on underlying risk factors, including drug use and homelessness; certain clinical syndromes, such as streptococcal toxic shock syndrome; race and ethnicity; or social determinants of health, such as poverty, access to health care, and employment. Data on race are missing in approximately 10% of cases and may also be misclassified in the medical record. This could disproportionately affect incidence rate estimates for groups with smaller population sizes, such as Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander persons or American Indian or Alaska Native persons, who are more likely to have their race and ethnicity misclassified in record-based surveillance systems.53The study investigators were unable to analyze results stratified by ethnicity due to the level of missingness for this variable. Third, obtaining correct denominators for transient conditions, such as injection drug use and experiencing homelessness, is challenging. Denominator estimates for homelessness were collected on a single time point in January, while cases for the numerator occurred throughout the year; seasonal variation in size and behavior of the population experiencing homelessness could affect incidence rates in either direction. Fourth, denominator estimates of people with injection drug use, people experiencing homelessness, and long-term care facility residents were not consistently available at the level of ABCs counties. This necessitated the use of national denominators for these populations, thereby limiting the precision and direct comparability of study estimates for GAS infection rates among these groups compared with the general ABCs population. However, the rates among these populations were so substantially elevated that even large variations in denominator sizes would likely only account for a relatively small percentage of the increased incidence.

The increasing invasive GAS burden, particularly among groups that have been economically and socially marginalized, requires urgent attention. Ongoing surveillance to monitor disease burden, strain distribution, and antimicrobial resistance is essential. Improved understanding of drivers of GAS transmission and increasing disease incidence could guide prevention and control efforts prior to availability of a licensed vaccine. Efforts to reduce risk factors, including underlying skin breakdown and infections, particularly for groups with the highest disease incidence, will be critical to change the trajectory of invasive GAS infections in the US.
