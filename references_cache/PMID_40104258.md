Pulmonary vasculopathy presents as a spectrum of diseases affecting the 
precapillary pulmonary arterioles, the capillaries and the venules. Pulmonary 
veno-occlusive disease (PVOD) is classified under group 1 pulmonary arterial 
hypertension (PAH) as subgroup 1.5 (PAH with features of capillary/venous 
involvement), and represents a progressive and fatal spectrum of pulmonary 
vascular disorders. PVOD and pulmonary capillary haemangiomatosis (PCH) can be 
clinically indistinguishable and often coexist, along with the same risk factors 
and genetic alterations; they are referred to together as PVOD/PCH in the 
literature. For brevity, we use the clinical term PVOD in this article. PVOD 
cannot be distinguished from other forms of PAH based on symptoms and 
haemodynamics. Risk factors include exposure to toxins/chemotherapeutic drugs 
and genetic mutation in the EIF2AK4 gene. Radiographic features such as 
mediastinal adenopathy, centrilobular ground-glass opacities, and interlobular 
septal thickening, along with the presence of hypoxia and reduced diffusion 
capacity of the lung may be required for a clinical diagnosis of PVOD, as lung 
biopsy carries a high risk for bleeding. Characteristic histological findings 
include narrowing/occlusion of small pulmonary veins. The development of 
pulmonary oedema with pulmonary vasodilator therapy limits therapeutic options 
for PVOD. With limited treatment options, lung transplantation remains the only 
curative treatment.

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a heterogeneous disease characterised by pulmonary vascular remodelling, a rise in pulmonary artery pressure and right ventricular (RV) failure. If left untreated, PH is associated with significant morbidity and mortality [1]. The haemodynamic definition of precapillary PH was recently revised to a mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) of >20 mmHg at rest, pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) <15 mmHg, and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) of >2 Wood units (WU) [1]. PH is classified into five major groups based on causative mechanisms that allow us to characterise, phenotype and guide management. Group 1 pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) and its subgroups are classified based on precapillary PH and associated conditions that lead to pulmonary vasculopathy (e.g. idiopathic, hereditary, drug/toxin-induced, infectious, congenital heart disease and PAH with capillary/venous involvement). Group 2 PH is due to left heart disease or mitral/aortic valve disease, group 3 PH is due to hypoxia and chronic lung disease, group 4 PH is due to pulmonary artery obstructions and chronic thromboembolic disease, and group 5 PH is due to undifferentiated and multifactorial mechanisms [1, 2].

Pulmonary veno-occlusive disease (PVOD) is a fatal form of PAH characterised by occlusion of the pulmonary venules due to intimal fibrosis, ultimately leading to PAH and RV failure. The seventh World Symposium on PH classified PVOD (PAH with capillary/venous involvement) under group 1 (1.5), compared with the prior classification under a special subgroup 1' [2]. PVOD and pulmonary capillary haemangiomatosis (PCH) can be clinically indistinguishable and often coexist, along with the same risk factors and genetic alterations; they are referred to together as PVOD/PCH in the literature. For brevity, we use the clinical term PVOD in this article.

The features of PVOD were first described in 1934 by Julius Höra in a 48-year-old baker who died after a year with symptoms of dyspnoea, generalised oedema and cyanosis [3]. However, the term PVOD was first coined in 1966 based on two separate case reports of a 40-year-old male and a 45-year-old female with histological features of PVOD [4, 5]. The exact prevalence of PVOD is unknown, but is estimated to be around 0.1–0.5 cases per million persons in the general population [6]. Based on several case series, 5% to 25% of patients diagnosed with PAH may have underlying features of PVOD [6]. Current studies suggest an equal (1:1) distribution of PVOD prevalence in males and females [7]. Lung biopsy provides the most definitive diagnosis, although this is mostly avoided due to the risk of fatal bleeding. Therefore, diagnosis is often made based on risk factors, genetic testing, clinico-radiological signs and development of pulmonary oedema with PAH-specific therapy [8]. This clinical review highlights aetiology, clinical features, diagnostic and management approaches to PVOD based on the current literature.

The characteristic histopathological features of PVOD include narrowing and occlusion of small venules (particularly the pre-septal venules) (figure 1a and b). Other features include alveolar haemorrhage and congestion due to small capillary rupture in the form of haemosiderin-laden macrophages in the alveolar spaces (figure 1a, d and e). Elastic stains can be useful in the diagnosis of PVOD as occluded pulmonary veins have one elastic lamina compared to two elastic layers in pulmonary arteries (figure 1c and d). Sometimes this finding can be difficult to appreciate on haematoxylin and eosin stains [9]. Coexisting features of PCH, such as capillary congestion and proliferation, may also be seen denoting disease evolution and possibly a spectrum of pulmonary vasculopathy [10–12]. PCH shares many histological features with PVOD like haemosiderin-laden macrophages, thickening of the pulmonary artery and capillary proliferation; however, histopathological changes which favour PCH over PVOD are wide alveolar septae with multilayers of capillaries, eventually forming a sheetlike pattern or nodular lesion [9, 13]. However, due to concomitant findings of PCH and PVOD in most patients (especially with EIF2AK4 mutation) and similar presentation, they are clinically indistinguishable and are classified under a common terminology of PAH with pulmonary capillary/venous involvement (group 1.5) [11].

Multiple genetic, environmental/toxin exposures, chemotherapeutic drugs and connective tissue disorders (CTD) have been associated with PVOD.

PVOD has been described in multiple case reports involving siblings, suggesting a genetic predisposition to this disease [14, 15]. The age of presentation in these reports varied from within a few weeks of birth to adulthood. Initial studies had suggested exon deletions in the BMPR2 (bone morphogenetic protein receptor type II) gene were common in PVOD, similar to hereditary PAH [16]. Hadinnapola
et al. [17] reviewed the genomic sequencing from 864 patients with idiopathic/heritable PAH, identifying 130 patients with BMPR2 mutation all of whom had PAH and not PVOD/PCH. In addition, 19 patients with eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 alpha kinase 4 (EIF2AK4) mutation were clinically diagnosed as PAH (with no initial signs of PVOD/PCH) as some of the imaging features may develop later in the course, highlighting the difficulty in diagnosing PVOD early in the disease course [17, 18].

More recently, a French study evaluated the genealogy of 13 families with PVOD and found that biallelic mutations in the EIF2AK4 gene were found in all the families, along with a demonstration of autosomal recessive transmission [19]. The EIF2AK4 gene encodes for the general control nonderepressible 2 kinase (GCN2) protein that acts as a metabolic stress sensor and regulates protein synthesis and mechanisms to adapt to stressors (nutritional, inflammatory or environmental) [20]. Recessive mutation in EIF2AK4 results in decreased GCN2 expression leading to vascular remodelling and development of PVOD [19, 21]. Both homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations can result in the disruption of EIF2AK4 gene function [19].

The EIF2AK4 mutation was also identified in a severe and aggressive form of PAH in five Iberian Gypsy families, with half of the patients with clinical suspicion for PVOD [22]. Studies also demonstrated similar EIF2AK4 mutations in cases of PCH, suggesting both diseases could be a spectrum of disorders [18]. EIF2AK4 mutation is the primary genetic mutation associated with heritable PVOD and has a high penetrance unlike BMPR2 mutation carriers, most of whom may not develop the disease (PAH). The majority of patients with EIF2AK4 involvement present in the young age group compared to sporadic cases of PVOD without EIF2AK4 mutation who present at an older age (median age of 60 years) [19, 23, 24].

Exposure to a variety of chemicals, such as fenfluramine, benzyl sulfonate and a variety of cleaning agents, has been documented in association with PVOD [25]. Organic solvents such as trichloroethylene are associated with PVOD [25, 26]. Trichloroethylene, a chlorinated solvent, causes an increase in endothelial permeability and vascular injury, which is the likely mechanism for its association with PVOD [27]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis of case–control studies demonstrated a higher risk of developing systemic sclerosis (often associated with PVOD) with occupational exposure to solvents, predominantly in males [28].

Montani
et al. [25] demonstrated that 42% of PVOD patients, compared with 3% of PAH patients, had exposure to trichloroethylene (adjusted OR 8.2, 95% CI 1.4–49.4). Tobacco exposure is also implicated in the development of PVOD. All patients in this study with trichloroethylene exposure had concurrent tobacco exposure, suggesting a potential cumulative effect leading to the development of PVOD [25]. Higher cumulative tobacco exposure was noted in patients with PVOD compared with PAH [26].

Chemotherapeutic agents have often been associated with the development of PVOD. The seventh World Symposium on PH classified the association of drugs/toxins with PAH as “definite” or “possible association” based on current literature and known mechanisms with which drugs may induce such pathological changes [2]. Perros
et al. [29] estimated the prevalence of PVOD in patients with anal cancer undergoing chemotherapy to be around 3.9 per 1000 patients per year which is >5000 times the prevalence of idiopathic PVOD (0.5 per million persons per year). In a French PH network study, alkylating agents were the predominant class of drugs associated with PVOD, with cyclophosphamide and mitomycin C being the most common agents [30]. The same study demonstrated in vitro toxicity to the lung endothelium along with vascular remodelling and septal thickening on exposure to cyclophosphamide [30]. The study also evaluated the sex-susceptibility, kinetics of PH development, dose–response relationship, and prevention of PH in rat models exposed to cyclophosphamide. Female rats developed more severe and homogenous disease compared with males with a direct dose and duration–response effect. Interestingly, concurrent administration of cytoprotective agents, such as amifostine, reduced the development of cyclophosphamide-induced PH and improved haemodynamics, whereas mesna had no significant effect. However, both mesna and amifostine significantly reduced brain natriuretic peptide levels [30]. While cyclophosphamide is associated with the development of PH, its use in conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus-associated PH may improve haemodynamics and functional class [31].

Mitomycin C is associated (definite association) with the development of PVOD in human and rat models [29]. Perros
et al. [29] also identified decreased expression of the EIF2AK4 gene and reduced levels of GCN2 protein on administration of mitomycin C with an inverse dose–response in rat models. Patients receiving mitomycin C along with 5-fluorouracil in squamous cell cancer of the anus were associated with the development of PVOD with poor prognosis despite chemotherapy discontinuation or the initiation of PAH-specific therapy [32]. Other agents associated (probable association) with PVOD include cisplatin, bleomycin and carmustine [30].

PVOD has also been reported as a potential complication of haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) after haematological malignancies. Multiple mechanisms, including graft-versus-host disease and opportunistic infection associated endothelial injury in the pulmonary vasculature, have been postulated for the development of PVOD in HSCT patients [33]. This entity may be distinct from the sporadic/heritable PVOD as it may resolve with immunosuppression/treatment of underlying aetiology based on limited case reports in the literature [34].

Systemic sclerosis is the most common CTD associated with PVOD with histological and radiographic evidence of PVOD being more prevalent than currently estimated [35–38]. Systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, sarcoidosis and pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis are other conditions associated with PVOD; however, PH associated with these conditions is classified under group 5.2 with multifactorial mechanisms [36, 39, 40].

COPD is often associated with mild-to-moderate PH and is typically classified under group 3 PH due to chronic lung diseases. However, a “pulmonary vascular phenotype” of COPD or PAH with a “lung phenotype” has been proposed with a predominant cardiovascular limitation to exercise [41, 42]. Clinically, patients with this phenotype present with severe precapillary PH and severely reduced diffusion capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) [42]. Hoeper
et al. [43] analysed data from the COMPERA (Comparative, Prospective Registry of Newly Initiated Therapies for Pulmonary Hypertension) and ASPIRE (Assessing the Spectrum of Pulmonary hypertension Identified at a REferral centre) registries to evaluate patients with PAH with a “lung phenotype” with reduced DLCO and smoking history. They found that the PAH with lung phenotype had normal spirometry with severe reduction in DLCO with mild parenchymal changes on chest imaging and predominantly occurred in males. These patients had lower therapeutic response compared with idiopathic PAH and worse survival [43]. Trip
et al. [44] identified advanced age and cumulative tobacco exposure as factors associated with PAH with severely reduced DLCO (<45% predicted). These patients also had worse exercise performance despite similar haemodynamic profile compared with PAH patients with DLCO >45% [44]. They excluded suspected PVOD from the analysis based on chest imaging or histologically confirmed PVOD [44]. It is essential to highlight this phenotype as PVOD is considered a differential diagnosis in PAH patients with severely reduced DLCO and smoking history. Molecular profiling has shown functional similarities between PVOD and other chronic lung diseases, including COPD and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, with microtubule-associated serine/threonine kinase 2 and protein-o-mannose kinase SGK196 dysregulation as a key pathway; however, currently there is no evidence to suggest a direct relationship between COPD and PVOD [45]. Chest imaging, risk factors/exposure history and genetic assessment may be required to differentiate PVOD from the PAH with lung phenotype.

PVOD is clinically indistinguishable from other forms of PAH based on symptoms. Both disease entities present insidiously with nonspecific symptoms of fatigue, dyspnoea on exertion, light-headedness and leg swelling, and this often leads to a delay in recognition and diagnosis [46]. PVOD tends to have a more severe and progressive phenotype with a higher degree of dyspnoea and hypoxaemia compared with idiopathic PAH [10, 24]. Signs of RV failure, such as pedal oedema and ascites, are usually seen in the later stages of the disease [47]. Presyncope and syncope are suggestive of an advanced disease state with RV failure and should prompt evaluation for underlying PH [47]. Haemoptysis due to occult alveolar haemorrhage has been noted in PVOD [48, 49]. Clubbing and Raynaud's phenomenon are proportionally documented in PVOD and PAH [48].

On physical examination, a loud pulmonic (P2) of the second heart sound and systolic murmur of tricuspid regurgitation with inspiratory augmentation can be appreciated. Crackles can be rarely heard over lung fields in cases where pulmonary oedema and pleural effusions develop.

PVOD is a diagnosis of exclusion and the evaluation follows the same algorithm as other forms of PAH. Once PVOD is suspected, further genetic evaluation may confirm the diagnosis.

ECG may suggest the presence of PH and RV remodelling but cannot differentiate PVOD from other forms of PAH. An ECG is an easily available point-of-care test that may demonstrate evidence of right atrial dilation, right axis deviation and RV hypertrophy [50]. Deep R waves in V1–V2 leads and deep S waves in V5–V6 leads can be appreciated in PH with RV failure. Occasionally, a right bundle branch block may be noted. S–T segment depression in inferior leads (I, II, aVF) may suggest underlying repolarisation abnormalities associated with PH [51].

There is no specific finding on echocardiography that differentiates PVOD from other causes of PAH. Echocardiography can be used to assess the pre-test probability for PH, and several functional and morphological variables can be used to increase the likelihood of PH (figure 2a–d) [1, 52]. Tricuspid regurgitation (TR) and maximum tricuspid regurgitant jet velocity can be used to estimate the TR gradient and ultimately right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) using Bernoulli's equation [52]. However, due to several patient- and operator-related factors, an accurate TR velocity may not always be detected (figure 2a–d) [53]. A dilated right atrium and ventricle are seen in advanced stages (figure 2a). RV hypertrophy is often seen in long-standing PH [52]. Systolic and diastolic flattening of the interventricular septum is suggestive of RV pressure and volume overload (figure 2b). In addition, the presence of left atrial dilation, reduced left ventricular function and diastolic dysfunction may suggest group 2 PH rather than PAH or PVOD.

Spirometry may demonstrate mild restrictive or obstructive impairment in PVOD [54]. However, normal values of forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), FEV1/forced vital capacity ratio and total lung capacity may also be seen [26]. Pulmonary function test (PFT) abnormalities are often seen in advanced disease states with significant pulmonary vascular remodelling and congestion.

One of the major findings on PFTs in PVOD patients is a significant reduction in DLCO [54, 55]. It is hypothesised that the reduction in DLCO is due to a greater reduction in capillary blood flow and poor diffusion capacity from interstitial oedema [48]. DLCO adjusted for alveolar volume (VA) ratio is also significantly reduced in PVOD patients compared with PAH [26]. Furthermore, a DLCO <55% was associated with 89.5% specificity and 64.3% sensitivity for detection of PVOD [26].

PVOD patients have a higher degree of resting hypoxia compared with PAH [24]. In the study by Montani
et al. [26], PVOD patients had a lower mean partial pressure of arterial oxygen, 61.3±17.3 mmHg versus 75.4±3.8 mmHg in PAH patients. The likely cause for significant hypoxia is pulmonary oedema, occult alveolar haemorrhage and fibrotic infiltration of the pulmonary vasculature, leading to a lower diffusion capacity [26]. In terms of 6-min walk distance (6MWD), some studies do show lower walk distance in the PVOD group compared with PAH [49]. However, in the study by Montani
et al. [26], 6MWD was similar between PVOD and PAH.

Chest radiographs may demonstrate enlarged proximal pulmonary arteries on posteroanterior views. The fullness of the retrosternal space on lateral views may suggest right atrial and ventricular enlargement. In PVOD, interstitial infiltrates, Kerley B-lines and pulmonary oedema can be demonstrated [26, 54].

High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest is a key imaging modality that increases the pre-test probability for a PVOD diagnosis. Characteristic findings on HRCT for PVOD include mediastinal lymph node enlargement, centrilobular ground-glass opacities and smooth interlobular septal thickening (figure 3a and b) [49, 56]. Compared with PAH, the above triad of HRCT findings is more prominently seen in PVOD. The presence of two of the three above HRCT findings was associated with a 75% sensitivity and 84.6% specificity for detecting PVOD [26]. The rate of pleural effusions is similar in both end-stage PAH and PVOD, whereas pericardial effusion is more common in PAH [26, 56].

A ventilation–perfusion (V̇/Q̇) scan is recommended as a part of diagnostic evaluation for PH to rule out chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) [1]. The majority of PVOD patients will have a normal V̇/Q̇ scan [57]. However, a V̇/Q̇ scan may demonstrate a patchy peripheral mismatch and mislead to a diagnosis of CTEPH [58]. The rate of mismatched defects is usually similar between PVOD (7.1%) and PAH (10%) (p=0.72) [57]. It is essential to rule out underlying CTEPH as the cause of an abnormal V̇/Q̇ scan and must be followed up with further imaging of the pulmonary vasculature.

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) can provide useful diagnostic and prognostic information in patients suspected of PH or PVOD. Pérez-Olivares
et al. [59] evaluated the CPET findings in 23 patients with PVOD and 52 control patients with PAH. PVOD patients had significant functional impairment with a reduction in predicted peak oxygen consumption (V̇O2), lower oxygen pulse and earlier ventilatory threshold compared with PAH patients [59]. In terms of ventilatory parameters, patients with PVOD have a higher degree of ventilation inefficiency demonstrated by higher minute ventilation (V̇E)/carbon dioxide production (V̇CO2) at ventilatory threshold (EqCO2VT) and higher V̇E/V̇CO2 slope compared with PAH [59]. Oxygen saturation is also lower in the PVOD group. Similar findings were noted in an earlier study by Laveneziana
et al. [60] evaluating eight patients with PVOD.

Invasive CPET may often be performed to obtain live haemodynamic data with exercise. Predicted V̇O2, V̇E/V̇CO2 and peak end-tidal CO2 (PETCO2) slope in combination with PVR has a higher predictive power for PVOD (area under the curve of 0.97, 95% CI 0.95–1.00) [59]. Interestingly, these parameters did not correlate with haemodynamic severity, suggesting that other cardiopulmonary factors may play an important role in these findings [59].

Right heart catheterisation is the gold standard for the diagnosis and haemodynamic phenotyping of PH. The haemodynamic findings in PVOD are indistinguishable from PAH, with predominant precapillary PH defined [1, 26].

While the site of pathology in PVOD is the smaller post-capillary venules, the PAWP is usually normal (≤15 mmHg) [26, 54]. In PVOD, the disease process is in the smaller pulmonary venules close to the capillary network and does not affect the PAWP (which mostly reflects the pressure in the larger veins, left atrial pressure and indirectly the left ventricular end-diastolic pressure) [48]. A theoretical method of measuring pulmonary capillary pressures is dividing PVR measurement into arterial and capillary venous components, which can help differentiate PVOD from PAH. This is done by looking at the pulmonary artery pressure decay curve after balloon occlusion and using it to measure pulmonary capillary pressures [61–63]. However, these methods need prospective validation to translate into clinical practice and are not routinely utilised.

Vasoreactivity testing is recommended as a part of the diagnostic workup to assess candidacy for calcium channel blocker (CCB) therapy for idiopathic, hereditary and drug-induced PAH [1]. A positive vasoreactivity test is defined as a ≥10 mmHg fall in mPAP to an absolute level of ≤40 mmHg with increased or unchanged cardiac output after challenge with vasodilator therapy (inhaled nitric oxide, intravenous epoprostenol or iloprost) [1]. While the current guidelines make no recommendation on its utility in diagnosing or uncovering PVOD, up to 12.2% of patients with PVOD may demonstrate positive vasoreactivity testing. However, it does not predict therapeutic response to CCB or the risk of pulmonary oedema [26, 64].

In the presence of normal PAWP, the development of pulmonary oedema after vasodilator therapy initiation is highly suggestive of a diagnosis of PVOD and can lead to life-threatening hypoxia and even death [8, 54]. Interestingly, none of the patients with PVOD developed pulmonary oedema during the vasoreactivity testing with inhaled nitric oxide for 5–10 min in the case series by Montani
et al. [26]. This may be either due to the short duration of exposure to inhaled nitric oxide or the need for a more potent/systemic drug to uncover pulmonary oedema. In fact, the median time to develop pulmonary oedema in the abovementioned case series was 24 h to 9 days after therapy initiation [26]. Currently, vasoreactivity testing is not recommended in patients with suspected PVOD; however, development of pulmonary oedema with vasodilators may offer an important clue towards diagnosis and further studies are needed to assess its diagnostic utility.

Bronchoscopy is generally not indicated in PVOD and may be associated with a high risk of haemodynamic or respiratory compromise in patients with underlying hypoxaemia and RV failure. However, it is often performed when evaluating for other causes of symptoms and abnormal chest imaging when an alternate cause is considered prior to a definitive diagnosis of PVOD. Patients with PVOD can present with haemoptysis due to occult alveolar haemorrhage [49]. Bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage, if performed, may demonstrate the presence of haemosiderin-laden macrophages [10, 49]. Haemosiderin-laden macrophages may also be detected in sputum samples of patients with PVOD [65]. However, the absence of alveolar haemorrhage does not rule out PVOD. Transbronchial biopsy is contraindicated due to the risk of life-threatening bleeding due to vascular congestion [48].

The diagnosis of PVOD is ultimately made based on a combination of findings in the aforementioned testing and the development of pulmonary oedema with vasodilator therapy (table 1). Meeting two out of the three characteristic HRCT findings (mediastinal adenopathy, centrilobular ground-glass nodularity and interlobular septal thickening), along with significant resting hypoxaemia (partial pressure of oxygen <70 mmHg), reduced DLCO (<55% of predicted normal), occult haemorrhage on bronchoalveolar lavage, poor exercise capacity and evidence of biallelic mutation of the EIF2AK4 gene or exposure to known causative agents may confirm the diagnosis of PVOD [24]. Genetic evaluation may be necessary in patients meeting clinical criteria for PVOD and their family members for early identification and monitoring [23]. Histopathological confirmation may only be possible post mortem or in explanted lungs post-transplant, as lung biopsy in PVOD carries a significant risk for life-threatening bleeding [48].

PVOD is a rapidly progressive disease and portends a worse outcome compared with PAH [66]. Early referral to a PH expert centre is recommended for patients with suspected PVOD for multidisciplinary treatment and potential for clinical trial enrolment and transplant evaluation. 1-year, 3-year and 5-year survival rates for PVOD are 86%, 50% and 27%, respectively, with a median survival time of only 36 months from diagnosis [66]. Prior studies have suggested a 1-year mortality of 72% [54]. Other case series suggest a time from diagnosis to death or lung transplant of 11.8 months in PVOD compared with 42.3 months for PAH, while the time from first symptom onset to death/lung transplant was found to be 24.4 months in PVOD compared with 57.9 months in PAH [26].

Boucly
et al. [66] evaluated risk-assessment strategies in PVOD and identified the Registry to Evaluate Early and Long-Term PAH Disease Management (REVEAL) risk score, COMPERA, the French Pulmonary Hypertension Registry, European Society of Cardiology (ESC)/European Respiratory Society (ERS) three-strata and four-strata scores to accurately predict prognosis in PVOD. ESC/ERS four-strata risk score had better discrimination of prognosis in high-risk patients with PVOD at baseline and follow-up [66]. It is important to note that the c-statistics to discriminate between different risk-stratification methods ranged between 0.64 and 0.74 for PVOD, clinical utility of the risk stratification tool should be individualised and incorporated along with the entire clinical picture and progression of the disease [66]. Interestingly, they found no difference in transplant-free survival or overall survival between patients undergoing treatment with PH therapy versus not receiving any treatment [66]. While risk-assessment is important to understand disease severity and prognosis, early lung transplant referral in all patients with PVOD is recommended due to limited pharmacotherapy.

General supportive measures in PVOD include oxygen supplementation, symptomatic treatment of chronic RV failure with diuretics, immunisation and cardiopulmonary rehabilitation. Diuretics may be instituted if there is evidence of RV failure and pulmonary oedema for symptomatic relief [10]. Since any infection can exacerbate respiratory failure and RV failure, it is essential to ensure guideline-based immunisation against SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19), influenza and Streptococcus pneumoniae [1, 67]. There is currently no evidence of any benefit from routine anticoagulation therapy in PVOD unless there is an alternate indication, such as venous thromboembolism or atrial fibrillation.

Currently, PAH therapy targets one of four pathways that include the nitric oxide pathway, endothelin receptor antagonism, prostacyclin pathway and inhibition of transforming growth factor-β (restoring the balance of vascular proliferation versus inhibition) mediated by the BMPR2 gene. However, the role of PAH-specific therapy is currently limited in PVOD, with most patients excluded from major randomised controlled trials. Data are largely limited to case reports and series (table 2) [54, 68]. One of the major concerns with treating PVOD with PAH-specific therapy is the risk of pulmonary oedema [8, 48].

Montani
et al. [69] evaluated 12 patients with PVOD on intravenous epoprostenol as a bridge to lung transplant and noted functional and haemodynamic improvement over 3–4 months, leading to the successful transplantation of nine patients. Interestingly, only one patient developed mild pulmonary oedema. All patients had severe haemodynamic and clinical impairment prior to initiation of therapy and were started on low doses of epoprostenol with slow up titration to maximum dose, median value of 13 ng·kg−1·min−1. Patients were further treated with diuretics, with three patients requiring inotropic support in the intensive care unit [69]. Ogawa
et al. [70] performed a systematic review of 20 publications with 64 PVOD patients treated with intravenous prostacyclin and endothelin receptor antagonist therapy. While there was an improvement in the 6MWD and haemodynamics, nearly 30 patients reportedly developed pulmonary oedema [70]. Earlier case series reports show that the prevalence of pulmonary oedema is ∼70% with CCB usage and 20–50% with PAH therapy [24, 26, 54]. The decision to consider PAH-specific therapy should be individualised on a case-by-case basis and at PH expert centres under close supervision. The long-term impact of PAH-specific therapy is currently unknown in PVOD due to a lack of prospective, randomised controlled trials, which can be difficult to perform in such rare diseases. Longitudinal data from global registries may offer insights into their potential use.

Cytotoxic injury, immune-complex deposition and inflammation have been implicated in the development of PVOD. Furthermore, CTDs such as scleroderma, systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren syndrome and sarcoidosis-associated PVOD may benefit from immunosuppressive treatment [71]. Bergbaum
et al. [72] demonstrated clinical, haemodynamic and gas transfer stabilisation with mycophenolate and prednisone in three patients with hereditary or idiopathic PVOD, suggesting a potential role in the treatment. In a case series by Certain
et al. [32], three patients with mitomycin C-induced PVOD received concomitant corticosteroid and PAH-specific therapy, and two of them demonstrated transient improvement in functional status and haemodynamics.

Imatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, has antiproliferative activity in the pulmonary vasculature and is currently under study as a potential therapy for PAH [73]. Case reports on the use of imatinib in PVOD have demonstrated clinical and haemodynamic improvement, warranting further studies to evaluate its role as a therapeutic option [74, 75]. Ogawa
et al. [76] studied nine patients with PVOD and PCH who were treated with imatinib and showed improvement in World Health Organization functional classification, mPAP and mean survival time compared with patients not treated with imatinib. Another retrospective study of five patients with PVOD/PCH treated with imatinib demonstrated improvement in partial pressure of oxygen and haemodynamics [77].

As PVOD is a rare disease, the data are limited to case reports and series. There are no guideline recommendations on the use of immunosuppressive therapy in PVOD. Decision to initiate immunosuppressive therapy needs to be individualised and depends on the underlying aetiology (e.g. CTD, HSCT).

PVOD is a rapidly progressive disease, and early referral for lung transplantation remains the most definitive and life-saving treatment [48]. Wille
et al. [78] studied the progression of 49 PVOD and 647 PAH patients enlisted for lung transplants. The proportion of transplants between the PVOD and PAH groups was similar (50.0% versus 47.6%, p=0.60). However, after 6 months, 22.6% of patients in the PVOD group, compared with 11% in the PAH group, died before getting transplanted [78]. Recurrence of PVOD in transplanted lungs has been reported in the literature [79, 80]. Furthermore, PVOD associated with chemotherapeutic drugs poses a unique challenge as a history of underlying malignancy excludes candidacy for lung transplantation. It is, therefore, imperative to initiate early lung transplant referral for any patient suspected of PVOD for a timely evaluation and enlistment. For patients who are not candidates for a lung transplant and who demonstrate progressive disease, early involvement of palliative care specialists may help with symptom control and establish goals of care for treatment.

PVOD remains a progressive fatal disease and requires expedited clinical evaluation for early diagnosis. History of exposure and associated conditions, along with classic radiographic findings and out-of-proportion hypoxaemia, should raise suspicion for PVOD. Genetic evaluation for EIF2AK4 mutation in suspected patients can assist with the diagnosis of a hereditary form of PVOD and help screen asymptomatic family members as well. Therapeutic options are currently limited with a high risk of pulmonary oedema with PAH-specific therapy, which must be trailed cautiously in an experienced PH centre with the capability for early transplant listing. The role of immunosuppression is currently limited, with case series demonstrating temporary benefits in select cases, but this needs to be explored in prospective studies. Lung transplantation remains the only definitive treatment for PVOD.
