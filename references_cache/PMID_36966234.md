# Cancer in Costello syndrome: a systematic review and meta-analysis.
**Authors:** Astiazaran-Symonds E, Ney GM, Higgs C, Oba L, Srivastava R, Livinski AA, Rosenberg PS, Stewart DR
**Journal:** Br J Cancer (2023)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41416-023-02229-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41416-023-02229-7)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Costello syndrome (CS) is a cancer-predisposition disorder caused by germline pathogenic variants in HRAS. We conducted a systematic review using case reports and case series to characterise cancer risk in CS.

METHODS: We conducted a systematic review to identify CS cases to create a retrospective cohort. We tested genotype-phenotype correlations and calculated cumulative incidence and hazard rates (HR) for cancer and cancer-free death, standardised incidence rates (SIR) and survival after cancer.

RESULTS: This study includes 234 publications reporting 621 patients from 35 countries. Over nine percent had cancer, including rhabdomyosarcoma, bladder, and neuroblastoma. The rate of cancer and death associated with p.Gly12Ser were lower when compared to all other variants (P < 0.05). Higher mortality for p.Gly12Cys, p.Gly12Asp, p.Gly12Val and p.Gly60Val and higher malignancy rate for p.Gly12Ala were confirmed (P < 0.05). Cumulative incidence by age 20 was 13% (cancer) and 11% (cancer-free death). HR (death) was 3-4% until age 3. Statistically significant SIRs were found for rhabdomyosarcoma (SIR = 1240), bladder (SIR = 1971), and neuroblastoma (SIR = 60). Survival after cancer appeared reduced.

CONCLUSIONS: This is the largest investigation of cancer in CS to date. The high incidence and SIR values found to highlight the need for rigorous surveillance and evidence-based guidelines for this high-risk population.

Subject terms: Cancer genetics, Genetics research

In 1977, Dr. JM Costello described two unrelated children with a syndrome characterised by short stature, intellectual disability, redundant skin, curly hair, and papillomata [1]. Subsequently, additional cases with the same phenotype were reported and “Costello syndrome” (CS, OMIM 218040) was coined to describe this condition [2].

Costello syndrome is caused by activating variants in conserved amino acid positions in the HRAS gene and is a part of the set of clinical syndromes termed RASopathies. HRAS belongs to the Ras family of proteins, along with KRAS and NRAS. The RAS family of proteins have an essential role in many aspects of mammalian development. Given their critical role, dysregulation of the RAS/Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) pathway leads to a constellation of developmental syndromes and symptoms [3–5]. This association was initially reported in 2005 when heterozygous germline variants in the HRAS gene were identified in 12 individuals with a clinical diagnosis of CS [6]. Importantly, the ClinGen RASopathy Gene Curation Expert Panel has designated HRAS as the only gene with a “definitive” clinical validity classification for association with CS [7].

The HRAS gene was first cloned from a bladder cancer cell line and was shown to be sufficient to transform normal cells. Perhaps unsurprisingly, individuals who harbour a germline pathogenic variant (GPV) in HRAS have increased lifetime risk for malignant tumours, including rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS), neuroblastoma, and transitional cell carcinoma of the bladder [8]. Other common non-cancer clinical features include failure to thrive, short stature, developmental delay and intellectual disability, hypotonia, characteristic facial features (such as full lips, large mouth and full nasal tip), macrocephaly, curly and sparse hair, loose skin with deep palmar and plantar creases, papillomata of the face and perianal region and cardiac disease (hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, pulmonic valve stenosis and arrythmia) [6, 8–11].

Previous studies have reported an increased risk of cancer associated with specific germline variants in CS. However, due to the small number of individuals with this rare disorder in most individual studies, genotype–cancer correlates remain uncertain. Illustratively, the prevalence of CS has been estimated at 1:380,000 in the United Kingdom [12] and at 1:230,000 in Japan [13], and about 80% of patients with a molecular diagnosis of CS share the same germline variant (p.Gly12Ser) [14], making meaningful conclusions around genotype–phenotype associations difficult. While some expert groups have recommended surveillance protocols for patients with CS due to the apparent increased risk of malignancy, there is currently no evidence demonstrating clinical benefit from such monitoring on mortality.

Kratz et al. [15] performed a literature review of 268 individuals with CS and calculated cumulative incidence and hazard rates of cancer and cancer-free death. In the subsequent decade, it became feasible for clinicians to pursue a molecular diagnosis for CS with the widespread use of next-generation sequencing technologies and advances in cancer detection. Accordingly, this increased the numbers of patients identified with and without cancer. In this study, we have updated and expanded upon the findings from Kratz to better quantify cancer risk. To do this, a diverse retrospective cohort of CS literature cases was identified and assembled through a comprehensive systematic evidence review (SER) of the relevant global literature. The data were analysed by determining the following: (1) a description of the types and frequencies of cancer; (2) a list of HRAS GPVs detected in these patients; (3) genotype–cancer correlations; and (4) a pooled analysis of individual case data with estimations of cumulative incidence and hazard rates for cancer and death, standardised incidence ratios (SIRs) of each cancer type, and survival after cancer.

We wrote our protocol and registered it at PROSPERO (CRD42020201812). We followed the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and checklist for reporting this review [16].

Articles were included when individuals with a diagnosis (clinical or molecular) of CS with or without cancer were reported in a study. Cross-sectional studies, case-control studies, cohort studies, longitudinal follow-up studies, case reports, case series, randomised control trials, and clinical trials on CS were all included. Studies published from 1971 to present and in English, Spanish, German, Italian, Portuguese, and French were included. Original research, case reports, case series, dissertations/theses and conference abstracts/proceedings/papers were included.

A biomedical librarian (AAL) searched eight citation and abstract databases, six dissertation and theses databases, and one conference proceedings database in September 2020 and August 2022. For the citation and abstract databases, a combination of keywords and controlled vocabulary terms (i.e., MeSH for PubMed/MEDLINE, EMTREE for Embase) identified by the biomedical librarian and reviewed by the team were used to describe Costello Syndrome. In addition, the following limits were applied using the filters available in each database: publication year (1971–2020 and 2020–2022), language (English, French, German, Italian, Portuguese, Spanish), and document type (original research, case reports/series, conference abstracts/proceedings/papers, dissertations/theses). This time limit was used even though CS was first reported in 1977, as we hoped to retroactively identify earlier reported cases, and we limited to the specified languages due to the language limitations of the review team. The databases searched were: Embase (Elsevier), Embase/MEDLINE (Elsevier), Global Health Index Medicus (World Health Organization), LILACS (Pan American Health Organization), PubMed/MEDLINE (US National Library of Medicine), Scopus (Elsevier), Web of Science: Core Collection (Clarivate Analytics), and Web of Science: Scielo (Clarivate Analytics). See Supplementary File 1 for all final search strategies used.

The six dissertation/theses databases were searched using the basic search field and individual phrases selected from the citation and abstract database search strategies. The filters available in each database were used to apply the same limits as for the citation and abstract databases for publication year and language. The dissertation/theses databases searched were: DART-Europe E-Theses, Dissertations & Theses Global database (ProQuest), Open Dissertations (Ebscohost), Networked Digital Library of Theses and Dissertations (NDLTD), Theses Canada, and Open Access Theses & Dissertations (OATD).

For conference proceedings, the Web of Science: Conference Proceedings Citation Index: Science and Social Sciences & Humanities (1990–present) (Clarivate Analytics) was searched using the same search strategy used for the citation and abstract databases but limited to the Topic field. Using the filters in this database, the search results were limited by (1971–2020 and 2020–2022), language (English, French, German, Italian, Portuguese, Spanish), and document type (meeting abstract and conference paper only).

All results from the citation and abstract, dissertation, and conference proceedings databases were exported into EndNote 20 (Clarivate Analytics) to identify duplicates and unique records. All unique records were exported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovations, Ltd.) by the biomedical librarian.

Covidence was used for screening. Two levels of screening were conducted: first the titles and abstracts were screened using the established eligibility criteria, and then those articles included after title and abstract screening were further screened by reviewing their full text and applying the eligibility criteria. For title and abstract screening, two reviewers (CH, RS) independently completed the screening, and conflicts were resolved by a third reviewer (EAS). Full-text review was performed independently by five reviewers (CH, RS, GN, LO, EAS), and conflicts were resolved by discussion with a different third reviewer.

Data extraction was conducted by four reviewers (CH, RS, GN, LO), and discrepancies were resolved by EAS. The following information was extracted from the included articles: first author, publication year, country of origin (if not reported for the case, the country of affiliation of first author was used), publication source, study type, number of patients with CS, and number of patients with cancer. Furthermore, the following individual participant data were extracted from the studies in which these data were provided: gender, ethnicity/race/nationality, participant age at publication, participant age at diagnosis of CS, diagnostic method, genetic testing modality used, specific variants when available, type of cancer reported, age at cancer diagnosis, vital status, age at death and last known age alive.

We used two Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal checklists for (1) case reports [17] and (2) case series [18]. The case report checklist was used for studies reporting one patient, and the case series checklist for those reporting more than one patient. We adapted both checklists by removing questions related to interventions and outcomes not applicable to our review (see Supplemental Materials for adapted checklists). Working in pairs, four reviewers (RS, CH, LO, EAS) independently completed the assessment for each study, and one reviewer (EAS) reconciled all disagreements.

HRAS variants reported in the relevant literature to be causative of CS were collected. Variants in the included articles were classified using the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) Standards and Guidelines for the Interpretation of Sequence Variants and the ClinGen RASopathy Expert Panel Consensus Methods for Variant Interpretation [19, 20]. The MANE select transcript (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/refseq/MANE/) NM_005343.4 was used for variant nomenclature and classification.

The total number of cases used for qualitative analysis was 621. A subset of cases with sufficient data points (n = 573) was used to perform quantitative pooled analyses. As described previously, cause-specific hazards and cumulative incidence curves accounting for competing risks for cancer and death prior to cancer were calculated [21]. The outcomes of interest were cancer or death. To assess the robustness of the results, three different sensitivity analyses were performed using different subsets of cases: (1) excluding articles for which only an abstract was available; (2) excluding cases without a molecular diagnosis; and (3) excluding cases who have variants of uncertain significance (VUS).

For genotype–phenotype associations, we assigned variants to two groups that were hypothesised in previous reports to have different rates of cancer and death based on a series of attributes (i.e., codon position, variant type, and observations) [14]. We then compared the frequency distribution of variants between groups using chi-square analysis. Statistical significance (P < 0.05) was assessed based on the permutation distribution of the chi-square statistic estimated from 10,000 permutations.

We calculated the SIR comparing the incidence of cancer in cases in the literature with the expected incidence of cancer in the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program Database using SEER*Stat statistical software (Version 8.3.9.2). Birthdates were inferred based on the date of publication and age at publication. Briefly, a SEER*Stat-ready file was prepared using SEER*Prep, and an incidence rate file was created using SEER data for each type of cancer reported in the literature cases. SIR analyses were performed overall and stratified by birth cohort and attained age. An Excel report with the SIR results was then created. These analyses were run as a multiple outcome analysis with a zero-latency exclusion period.

We calculated overall survival curves following cancer for all cases, rhabdomyosarcoma and bladder cancer, and all cases stratified by HRAS variant.

The literature searches, after removal of duplicates, resulted in 1166 unique articles, of which 777 were excluded after title and abstract screening, for a total of 389 articles. After full-text screening of 389 articles, an additional 155 were excluded and 234 were included (Fig. 1). Supplementary Table 1 describes the different subsets of cohorts used for each analysis.

Costello syndrome, similar to the other RASopathies, can be suspected clinically based on medical conditions and associated physical traits or diagnosed through genetic testing. Of the 621 individuals reported to have Costello syndrome, clinical data were available for 573 and a molecular diagnosis was established for 429. Of those with a molecular diagnosis of CS, variants were reported for 391 individuals, with 26 variants reported as unique amino acid changes and 1 copy-number variant. From the variants reported, 23 unique variants were harboured by 386 individuals and determined to be pathogenic or likely pathogenic while four variants (harboured by five individuals) were classified as VUS, according to ACMG/AMP recommendations. The most common GPVs were p.Gly12Ser (n = 240, 61.4%) p. Gly12Ala (n = 34, 8.7%), and p.Gly13Cys (n = 27, 6.9%). All of them are well-established causative variants located in gene hotspots. One individual was reported to have a copy-number variant [duplication at 11p15.5 (dup11p15.5;1,922,452 bp)], including HRAS. Figure 2 presents the complete set of reported variants.

Lollipop plot of the HRAS gene with the variants reported in Costello syndrome literature cases.

Cancer data were collected to evaluate cancer incidence in individuals with a clinical or molecular diagnosis of CS within this cohort. Among the 573 individuals with clinical data, 53 (9.2%) had a history of malignancy, similar to what has been previously reported [15]. The most common cancer reported was RMS (n = 30, 5.2%), followed by transitional cell bladder cancer (n = 13, 2.2%), and neuroblastoma (n = 5, 0.9%). In addition, two individuals with a liver neoplasia (one with hepatoblastoma and one with an unspecified liver neoplasia, 0.3%) and one individual (0.2%) with the following neoplasias were reported: fibrosarcoma (incidence of 0.9 per 100,000 people in the general population), ganglioneuroblastoma (incidence of ~5 cases per one million children in the general population) and unspecified heart neoplasia (Supplementary Fig. 2). No individuals were reported to have more than one primary cancer.

Given the high incidence of early-onset cancers, we sought to quantify the risk of death and cancer within this cohort. To complete this analysis, 107 of the 621 individuals were included, as these individuals had either developed cancer or died. The age at which these individuals had an event (cancer or death) was plotted with their specific HRAS variant and the type of cancer, if any (Supplementary Fig. 3A). In addition, Supplementary Fig. 3B outlines the counts per variant of the 372 individuals who did or did not have an event and whose molecular diagnosis was available. Collectively, 9.2% of individuals carrying the common p.Gly12Ser (21/228) developed cancer (Supplementary Fig. 3B), compared to 38.7% of individuals with a p.Gly12Ala variant (12/31) and 37.5% (6/11) with a p.Gly12Cys variant.

Subsequently, the genotypic data were analysed in relation to available clinical data. Genotype associations with malignancy and death are summarised in Supplementary Fig. 4. We assessed the rates of cancer and death between reported variants and observed a lower rate of cancer and death in patients with the p.Gly12Ser variant compared to: (1) all other variants in the same codon (H0 1); (2) all other missense variants (H0 2); and (3) all other variants (H0 3) (P < 0.05). This analysis also supported a higher mortality for variants p.Gly12Cys, p.Gly12Asp, p.Gly12Val (H0 6), a lower mortality for variants p.Gly13Cys, p.Gly60Asp, p.Glu63_Asp69dup, p.Ser89Cys and p.Ala146Thr (H0 7) and p.Gly60Val (H0 6) (P < 0.05) and a higher malignancy rate for p.Gly12Ala (H0 8) (P < 0.05), when compared to all other variants.

With enhanced understanding of RASopathies and increased availability of clinical sequencing, medical providers strive to provide the most accurate information and offer counselling to patients and families regarding risks associated with a CS diagnosis. We therefore sought to understand the cumulative incidence and risks for individuals reported in 573 CS individuals with available clinical data. We found the cumulative incidence by age 20 years accounting for competing risks was 13% (95% CI: 9–16) for cancer and 11% (95% CI: 8–14) for death prior to cancer. Thus, 24% of CS patients developed cancer or died by age 20 years (Fig. 3a). The cause-specific hazard of death was 3–4% until the age of 3 years and then fell markedly thereafter (Fig. 3b).

We performed sensitivity analyses and excluded the following: (1) articles without a full text available (only abstracts, n = 39); (2) cases without a molecular diagnosis (n = 201); and (3) cases that harboured a VUS (n = 5). Reassuringly, the cumulative incidence of cancer and death as well as the cause-specific hazard of death remained consistent across these different cohorts of patients.

Given many parents seek information on cancer risk associated with CS, we estimated SIRs (Table 2). All reported cancer types in CS occurred at statistically significantly higher rates than expected in the general population. The SIR was 1240 for RMS, 1971 for bladder, and 60 for neuroblastoma. We also calculated SIRs stratifying cases by age group (Supplementary Table 2). The SIR for RMS remained elevated from birth until age 14, at which point no additional cases were identified. For bladder cancer, the highest SIR of 34,129 was observed at ages 10–14 but remained elevated throughout life. The SIR for neuroblastoma was 70 at ages 0–4, after which no more cases were reported. Notably, the elevated SIR in CS correlates with the ages at which neuroblastoma is most often diagnosed in childhood [22]. When stratified by year of birth, the only tumour identified in CS cases born before 1985 was bladder cancer. As shown, this study includes eight additional cases with cancer, which were born after the Kratz et al. analysis in 2010 (Supplementary Table 3).

A relevant clinical question that remains is whether germline pathogenic variants portend a worse cancer prognosis, compared to those individuals without a germline variant. While this could be from a range of factors, including medical comorbidities and response to cytotoxic chemotherapy, survival after cancer was analysed in these individuals. For those individuals with cancer, the overall survival was 62% (95% CI: 50– 80%) after 3.5 years (Fig. 4a). When stratified by cancer type (Fig. 4b), the 3.5-year survival was 54%% for RMS, and 50% after 0.6 years with neuroblastoma. In RMS cases, 2-year survival was 34% with p.Gly12Cys and p.Gly12Ala variants, while 3.5-year survival was 66% for RMS cases with the most common variant p.Gly12Ser (Fig. 4c). The 0.5-year survival after bladder cancer was 50% for cases with the variant p.Gly12Ser while no deaths were reported for cases with the variant p.Gly12Ala (Fig. 4d).

This study expands on the findings from previous analyses by performing a comprehensive SER to retrieve individuals with CS, a rare RASopathy described 46 years ago. Our global literature cohort is the largest to date and includes 621 individual cases of CS. This is a 55% increase in the number of cases from previous analyses, with a proportional increase in cancer cases [15]. We confirmed that the cumulative incidence and hazard rates for cancer and cancer-free death are notably high. Previous work has suggested some correlations between HRAS genotype and cancer incidence [14], which we tested and confirmed, while also identifying variants associated with higher mortality and cancer rates. Similarly, significantly elevated SIRs for all cancers were observed, which has only been previously reported in RMS [23]. Notably, SIR estimations stratified by age revealed important variation in cancer risk throughout life and differed among tumours. In contrast, survival after cancer was estimated and appeared relatively poor across all cancer types. Although specific cancer information was not available, survival after cancer in CS trends with the general population and will likely be directly related to cancer stage at diagnosis. Together, these data provide crucial information to medical providers and their patients to better understand and predict risk and outcomes in cancer for CS.

Analysis of genotype–phenotype correlation is a powerful tool that permits medical providers to better counsel families on risks associated with a particular germline (or somatic) variant. This study, consistent with others, found p.Gly12Ser to be the most prevalent GPV identified in CS patients. It was associated with a milder phenotype (lower mortality and malignancy rates) when compared to other variants, which could contribute to its higher prevalence. On the other side of the spectrum, variants such as p.Gly12Cys, p.Gly12Asp, p.Gly12Val and p.Gly60Val were less frequent and were associated with higher malignancy and mortality. Interestingly, the p.Gly12Ala and p.Gly12Cys variants are commonly detected somatically. However, they were less frequent in individuals with CS. This suggests that particular genetic variation, such as p.Gly12Ala or p.Gly12Cys, may have a high likelihood of embryonic lethality and not ultimately captured in the CS population. Indeed, as a general principle, activating variants that are frequently observed as somatically mutated are not usually detected in the germline setting and may be associated with severe perinatal phenotypes (cardiac, endocrine, respiratory, etc.) [6]. While mortality is likely linked to multiple factors, one possible hypothesis is that specific altered amino acid functionality within genes, such as augmented redox capabilities or modified steric hindrance, alter disease biology and response to chemotherapy [24]. Since inhibitors of specific RAS variants are now clinically utilised, it is imperative to better understand genotype–phenotype correlations, as targeted clinical intervention may be feasible for poor-risk cases.

Our SIR estimates confirm findings from a previous smaller cohort of CS patients that focused on RMS risk [23]. In addition to RMS, neuroblastoma was the most common cancer in children with CS, while bladder tumours were more common in adult life. Notably, we observed an earlier age of presentation of bladder tumours in patients with CS when compared to the general population. Collectively, SIR for all cancers, including neuroblastoma and bladder cancer, were increased.

While cancer survival is typically reported as 5-year survival, data were very limited in this cohort. Thus, it is difficult to draw definitive conclusions for this time interval. However, our analyses suggest the 3.5-year and 0.6-year survival after cancer are reduced with RMS (54%) and neuroblastoma (50%), respectively. Overall, the 5-year survival rate for children with RMS younger than 15 is 70% [American Cancer Society website and [25]]. Although previous studies reported that somatic RAS gene variants have a significant association with high-risk and intermediate-risk embryonal RMS, a more recent, larger study found no association [26, 27]. This suggests that complications related to phenotype and response to chemotherapy might play a role in the reduced survival after cancer in these patients. It is also difficult to make a direct comparison of survival without detailed staging information since RMS survival differs dramatically based on anatomic location and stating. The 5-year survival rate in children under the age of 15 with neuroblastoma at any stage, and without considering any other prognostic factors, is 82% [American Cancer Society website and [25]]. Although the high mortality early in our cohort would seem atypical (two-thirds of cases died before age 2), a larger sample would be necessary to draw definitive conclusions. Unfortunately, not enough data were reported in the studies included to stratify our analyses by tumour location, stage, or risk group. Due to the reliance on available published data, the results of this study are subject to literature biases (i.e., reports of the most severe cases, duplicate, missing or extremely rare cases). Moreover, the paucity of cases with informative vital status data prevented estimations regarding survival rate for other types of cancers. Future population-based studies, such as biobanks with genotypic and EHR-linked data with available information regarding age, vital status, and cancer diagnoses and staging might further help elucidate these associations.

Given the elevated cancer risk in CS, physical exams and abdominal and pelvic ultrasounds are recommended every 3 months until the age of 8–10 for RMS [28]. Our SIR values for RMS corroborate the need for this surveillance. In addition, the risk of RMS in our cohort continued to be elevated until age 14. This suggests that extending surveillance for this tumour might be appropriate. Similar to RMS, bladder cancer screening in CS is recommended through abdominal ultrasound and urinalysis [28] beginning at age 10. Although our findings support this strategy, the calculated SIR for bladder cancer was also elevated from age 0–9 years, with 15% of individuals with bladder cancer developing this cancer in the younger age group. This suggests that the expansion for bladder cancer screening should be considered. In contrast, neuroblastoma screening has not been proven to be beneficial in the general population. A recent controlled trial found no reduction in incidence of Stage 4 neuroblastoma nor mortality when using urine catecholamines as a screening strategy [29]. However, CS is a distinct, high-risk disorder and thus may benefit from targeted surveillance, as has been successful with other tumour predisposition disorders. Furthermore, new advancements on prognostic and diagnostic biomarkers of solid cancers could have clinical applications in CS. In recent years, detection of circulating cell-free DNA has become an attractive and minimally invasive method for cancer screening, therapeutic evaluation, and monitoring disease progression [30–32]. Moreover, the advent of whole-body (WB) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) coupled with metabolic or function-based techniques has recently led to the established use of WB MRI as part of the management of patients with other cancer-predisposition syndromes such as Li-Fraumeni syndrome, where it is used for detection of sarcomas and adrenal cortical carcinomas [33]. However, the limitations of this imaging technique, such as cost, high false-positive rate, anaesthesia requirements, and potential to lead to unnecessary interventions and complications should be considered [34].

This review identified five cases of unusual cancer types for which SIR was also significantly elevated: “heart neoplasia”, fibrosarcoma, ganglioneuroblastoma and two liver neoplasias (one hepatoblastoma and one unspecified liver neoplasia). Despite the low frequency of cases, it is conceivable these neoplasias represent an expansion of the Costello syndrome phenotype.

The findings of this study indicate the need for prospective cohort studies for CS and the RASopathies to be developed. An example of this is the NCI-sponsored intramural and extramural collaboration for the study of RASopathies [35], which aims to assess the impact of screening in cancer incidence and mortality in these patients using both established and novel tools. Moreover, the genotype–cancer correlates provided can serve to stratify patients by risk groups and identify who would benefit the most from screening and variant-specific therapies.
