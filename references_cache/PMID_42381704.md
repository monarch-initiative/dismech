# Therapeutic rescue of pathogenic asparaginyl-tRNA synthetase alleles.
**Authors:** Samuels TN, Kakadia JH, Ward C, Abdullatif F, Mendes MI, Smith DE, Heilmann R, Wu F, Swiercz JM, Salamons GS, Hoffman K, Gebregiworgis T, O'Donoghue P, Heinemann IU
**Journal:** Mol Ther Nucleic Acids (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.omtn.2026.102979](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.omtn.2026.102979)

## Content

Pathogenic alleles in the cytoplasmic asparaginyl-tRNA synthetase (NARS1) are associated with infant- and juvenile-onset disease, with no current disease-specific treatments. We developed a tractable human cell system to study disease-causing NARS1 alleles that can be adapted to investigate NARS1 and other aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase (ARS) alleles. We found that two dominant NARS1 nonsense alleles, R534X and R522X, cause a cytotoxic phenotype and elicit the integrated stress response (ISR). Proteomic and phenotypic changes were rescued by asparagine supplementation in the human cell model. Asparagine supplementation completely restored cell proliferation defects in patient-derived fibroblasts and prevented activation of the ISR. We also tested therapeutic cognate transfer RNA (tRNA) supplementation, which reduced the cytotoxicity of pathogenic NARS1 alleles but did not ameliorate activation of the ISR. A general control nonderepressible 2 (GCN2) inhibitor suppressed ISR activation and reduced cytotoxicity but did not restore changes to the proteome caused by the NARS1 nonsense alleles. The data reveal molecular and cellular defects caused by premature termination codons in NARS1 alleles. Our data also indicate asparagine supplementation as a feasible therapeutic approach to address the underlying cause of NARS1 disease, a rare disease for which currently no treatment is available.

Cytoplasmic asparaginyl-tRNA synthetase (NARS1) disease is an inherited disorder with broad effects on the central nervous system (CNS) and peripheral nervous system (PNS). The prevalence of NARS1 disease is extremely low, with 54 cases across 30 distinct families currently reported.1 NARS1 disease can be inherited in an autosomal-dominant (AD), autosomal-recessive (AR), or de novo fashion.2 Generally, dominant NARS1 alleles cause Charcot-Marie-Tooth (CMT) disease, while autosomal-recessive variants are typically associated with developmental delay and microcephaly. Bi-allelic and homozygous variants present distinct neuropathological symptoms in NARS1 patients, with the homozygous alleles being subcategorized as NARS1-related hereditary neuropathy. This subtype manifests in adolescence and early adulthood with mostly motor and sensory impairment involving weakness of predominantly the distal lower limbs and foot deformities without prominent muscle atrophy.1,3

Interestingly, dominant heterozygous nonsense alleles R522X and R534X causing premature protein termination in the C-terminal domain result in a non-CMT phenotype, NARS1-related neurodevelopmental disorder (NARS1-NDD), which presents neonatal and early-childhood symptoms including global developmental delay, intellectual disability, microcephaly, ataxia, seizures, and, rarely, neurobehavioral/psychiatric manifestations.1 These individuals also possess difficulty coordinating movements, speech problems, and a decline in intellectual function.4 Currently, no disease-specific treatment exists, and symptoms are managed with supportive measures and surgeries.1

NARS1 is a member of a family of highly conserved, essential enzymes that play a vital role in protein synthesis, known as aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (ARSs). In eukaryotes, each of the 20 proteinogenic amino acids has its own specific ARS enzyme5 that ligates amino acids to their cognate transfer RNAs (tRNAs). A high degree of selectivity in the ARS active site is critical, as the ribosome cannot distinguish a correctly charged from a mischarged tRNA. Disease-causing mutations in ARS proteins can lead to a multitude of molecular and cellular consequences,6,7,8 including decreased ARS stability, deficient aminoacylation activity,9,10,11 or toxic gain of function, since some alleles in the catalytic site result in mis-aminoacylation and proteome-wide mistranslation.12 Disease-causing mechanisms that are independent of aminoacylation have also been described.13,14

In humans, the cytoplasmic (NARS1) and mitochondrial (NARS2) NARS proteins are encoded by distinct genes.15 Several single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the NARS1 gene have been identified as disease causing.2,4,16 Two early-onset cases are caused by nonsense mutations of arginine (R, Arg) codons (R522X and R534X), leading to premature termination codons in NARS1 transcripts associated with NARS1-NDD. These NARS1 Arg residues (R522 and R534) are conserved across eukaryotes and are localized in the C-terminal catalytic domain of the protein, which contains the active site2 (Figure 1A). Several ARS mutations, including those found in the alanyl-tRNA synthetase (AARS1) editing active sites19,20 and disease-causing variants,21 are known to induce the integrated stress response (ISR).22 The ISR is stimulated by stress-responsive kinases that are activated by disruptions in protein homeostasis, including uncharged tRNA accumulation (Figure 1B) due to amino acid starvation.23,24 Uncharged tRNAs are recognized by the general control nonderepressible 2 (GCN2) kinase, which then phosphorylates eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) at serine 51.24 Phospho-eIF2α inhibits eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2B (eIF2B), preventing guanine nucleotide exchange activity that is required for the delivery of the initiator tRNA to the ribosome at the start of protein synthesis. As a result, global translation is attenuated and activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) is expressed, favoring the subsequent expression of genes involved in cellular adaptation and amino acid synthesis.24 ISR activation is thought to contribute to disease phenotypes and is activated in glycyl-tRNA synthetase (GARS1) mouse models of CMT disease.22 Pharmacological inhibition of GCN2 is efficacious in improving a GARS1 mouse model’s neuropathological phenotype, highlighting the potential of this treatment approach.22

Here, we investigated the molecular and cellular consequences of two pathogenic NARS1 alleles, R522X and R534X, to test the hypothesis that NARS1 disease-associated mutations reduce aminoacylation and cause proteomic perturbations that can be rescued by supplementation with asparagine (Asn), cognate tRNAAsn, or GCN2 inhibitors. We found that the nonsense alleles elicit a cytotoxic phenotype and activate the ISR in an HEK293T cell model, which was then rescued using Asn or a GCN2 inhibitor. Using mass spectrometry (MS), we identified proteomic perturbations from overexpression of the R522X mutant, with pathways like the ISR, and vascular endothelial growth factor signaling-associated proteins returning to wild-type (WT) levels when treated with Asn. Further, we used patient-derived primary fibroblasts from an individual with the NARS1 R534X variant and found that decreased cell viability is rescued with Asn supplementation.

Since R534X is a dominant allele and has only been identified in heterozygous patients, we next assessed if an equimolar mixture of the WT and R534X proteins causes a shift in the Tm. We found no difference in Tm when comparing the mixture to either WT or R534X alone, but the WT/R534X mixture showed significantly increased Tm upon addition of ATP compared to the heterodimer apoenzyme (Figure 2A). This suggests that the WT/R534X heterodimer adopts a more stable conformation upon binding ATP. R522X was not assessed for thermal stability, as we were unable to purify enough of the recombinant protein due to low levels of protein production, which indicates either a significant change in stability or a protein folding defect of the recombinant protein in E. coli.

Subtle changes in ARS structures can be assessed by limited tryptic digest to probe for differences in surface accessibility, secondary structure, and susceptibility to degradation.9,28 Proteins were incubated with trypsin, with the reaction quenched at the indicated time points (Figure 2B). The products were separated by SDS-PAGE (Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate Polyacrylaminde Gel Electrophoresis), which shows that the R534X mutant is more rapidly digested compared to the WT protein (Figure 2B) as evidenced by a differential digestion pattern of R534X compared to WT with additional lower molecular weight bands appearing in the R534X digest (Figure 2B, black arrows). These data show that the WT and R534X proteins have distinct tryptic fingerprints and surface accessibility. Overall, our findings indicate that R534X alters protein structure and has a dominant effect on thermal stability in the heterodimer.

To establish the cellular response of NARS1 nonsense variants in human cells, we generated an overexpression model in HEK293T cells. HEK293T cells were transfected with plasmids expressing either NARS1 WT, NARS1 R522X, or NARS1 R534X. NARS overexpression was achieved at levels comparable to endogenous NARS for the WT allele, as demonstrated by western blotting for NARS. We can clearly distinguish the overexpressed V5-tagged NARS WT from the endogenous NARS, showing almost equal abundance of the overexpressed and endogenous alleles (Figure S1). For the truncated nonsense variants, the V5-tagged R534X and R522X variants run at approximately the same relative molecular weight as the V5 increases the molecular weight to that of endogenous NARS. We then assessed cytotoxicity and cell viability of these cell lines (Figures 3A and 3B). We measured cell viability and cytotoxicity in two separate assays. The cell viability assay (CCK8) measures metabolic activity as a proxy for the number of viable cells, which reflects cell survival. In contrast, the cytotoxicity assay directly measures cell death. No impact on cell viability was observed for overexpression of any of the pathogenic alleles compared to WT NARS1-expressing cells (Figure 3A), but we observed a clear and significant phenotypic defect for the dominant alleles R522X and R534X when measuring cytotoxicity (Figure 3B). For R522X, a significant increase in dead cells over live cells was observed, where the proportion of dead/live cells was increased from 16% to 21%. Similarly, the proportion of dead cells was significantly increased from 16% to 22% in R534X-expressing cells (Figure 3B), indicating toxicity of R534X. Previous studies have shown that pathogenic ARS alleles can cause the activation of the ISR.22 The ISR can be activated by the accumulation of uncharged tRNA in the cell, which binds to GCN2, and in turn, phosphorylates eIF2α, initiating transcriptional changes to adapt to nutrient starvation. It has also been shown that ISR activation can arise independently of the interaction of uncharged tRNA with GCN2, where it is mediated through the interaction of the ribosomal P-stalk with GCN2 instead.29,30 Uncharged tRNA can accumulate either due to amino acid starvation or due to ineffective ARS aminoacylation, even in the presence of sufficient amino acid substrates.21,23,24,28,31 We, therefore, probed for eIF2α abundance and phosphorylation. We indeed found that relative phosphorylation of eIF2α was significantly increased in R522X and R534X compared to WT NARS1-expressing cells (Figures 3D and 3E). The abundance of eIF2α was not changed (Figure S2A), but significantly increased phosphorylation was evident for the nonsense NARS1 mutants (Figure S2B). We also assessed changes in the expression of transcription factors known to be regulated by the ISR. We found no significant changes in transducin beta-like 2 (TBL2), activating transcription factor 3 (ATF3), or ATF4 RNA abundance (Figures 3F and S1), which may indicate that ISR signaling is not fully activated at 24 h. In addition, we probed for RNA abundance of neuropilin 1 (NRP1), a receptor which is involved in nervous system development and shown to interact with CMT-causing variants of AARS1 and GARS1,32,33 and found no changes in NRP1 mRNA abundance (Figure 3F). Overall, these data indicated that dominant NARS1 nonsense alleles elicit cytotoxicity and activate the ISR in human cells.

To assess if overexpression of pathogenic NARS1 alleles alters the composition of the proteome, we carried out a proteomic analysis of cells overexpressing either WT NARS1 or NARS1 R522X. We found that several proteins were differentially abundant in R522X-overexpressing cells (Figure 4A), and a principal-component analysis showed that the proteomes of NARS1 and NARS1 R522X-overexpressing cells are distinct (Figure 4B). When using a 2-fold cutoff, we found that 4 proteins were significantly reduced in R522X compared to WT cells (Figure 4A). When decreasing the cutoff to 1.8-fold, we found that 49 proteins are not detected or significantly reduced in R522X-expressing cells compared to WT, and 57 proteins are increased in abundance (Figures 4C and 4D; Table S2, Supplementary File 1). A STRING and Gene Ontology (GO)-term analysis of the up- or downregulated proteins separately did not identify enrichments in specific pathways (Figure S3). Combining both up- and downregulated proteins into a STRING search identified mRNA processing/splicing (KW:0507, mRNA splicing, count in network 8/349, false discovery rate [FDR] 0.0137) and RNA binding proteins (GO:0003723, RNA binding, count in network 24/1627, FDR 0.0113) as significantly enriched pathways and cellular functions (Figure 4D). Notably, several proteins associated with the 60S ribosomal subunit are dysregulated. Ribosome biogenesis protein BRX1 homolog (BRX1) and ribosomal protein L3-like (RPL3L) are not detected in the WT NARS1-expressing samples but are detected abundantly in all three R522X-expressing cell samples (Table S2). Conversely, several other ribosome-associated proteins are downregulated in R522X cells. A subunit of the 60S ribosome, ribosomal protein L24 (RPL24), was downregulated 4-fold, and the regulator of ribosome synthesis 1 (RRS1) was downregulated by 3-fold in R522X cells. Interestingly, the transcription factor specificity protein 1 (SP1), which is involved in regulating cell differentiation, growth, apoptosis, response to DNA damage, and chromatin remodeling, is also undetectable in WT cells but abundant in two of the three R522X replicates (Table S2). The proteomic data indicate that in response to NARS1 R522X, RNA processing and protein synthesis pathways are most impacted, showing a dysregulation of cellular and protein homeostasis. We previously showed that supplementation with cognate amino acid and tRNA substrate can rescue pathogenic phenotypes in a humanized Saccharomyces cerevisiae model of CMT disease arising from mutations in the histidyl-tRNA synthetase (HARS1).9,11,12 We, therefore, investigated the ability of Asn or tRNAAsn supplementation to rescue the cytotoxic phenotype of NARS1 R534X and NARS1 R522X alleles.

At 24 h post-transfection, R522X and R534X elicit a cytotoxic phenotype compared to WT (Figure 5A). In untreated control cells, Asn supplementation did not affect cell viability or cytotoxicity (Figures 5A and 5B). Surprisingly, tRNA supplementation alone had a cytotoxic effect in control experiments, increasing the proportion of dead cells from 9.5% to 14% in cells treated with tRNAAsn compared to transfection reagent only controls (Figure S4). Since the expression levels of tRNAAsn were not tested, there may be a dose-dependent effect that may affect toxicity and can be tested in future experiments. We next tested whether Asn or tRNAAsn supplementation is cytotoxic to WT NARS1-expressing cells, but no change in cell viability or cytotoxicity was observed (Figures 5A and 5B). Excitingly, when supplemented with 200 mg/L Asn, we found reduced cytotoxicity for each of the nonsense variants, with R522X and R534X showing pronounced rescue after 24 h to levels indistinguishable from WT NARS-expressing cells (Figure 5A). Similarly, when co-expressing tRNAAsn from the same plasmid as the NARS1 allele, we also found cytotoxicity to be significantly suppressed in cells expressing the R522X and R534X alleles. Activation of the ISR itself is associated with CMT and other disease phenotypes.22,24,35 To determine whether substrate supplementation impacts stress response signaling, levels of total eIF2α and phosphorylated eIF2α (p-eIF2α) were assessed by western blotting. As mentioned earlier, expression of the R534X mutant led to elevated levels of p-eIF2α, consistent with activation of the ISR (Figures 5C and 5E). Western blots were also performed following supplementation of the transfected cells with Asn or co-expression of tRNAAsn to assess whether these would reduce eIF2α or p-eIF2α abundance and relative phosphorylation levels. We also treated the control cells with halofuginone to demonstrate a full activation of the ISR,36 which led to a 2-fold increase in p-eIF2α (Figures 5C–5F). Under all conditions, no change in eIF2α abundance was observed relative to a tubulin loading control (Figures 5C–5F; Figures S1C and S1E). R534X cells show a 2-fold increase in absolute and relative p-eIF2α phosphorylation, and we observed a reversal of eIF2α phosphorylation upon Asn supplementation (Figures 5D and S2D) to levels indistinguishable from cells expressing WT NARS1. The data show that Asn supplementation effectively suppresses NARS1 R534X-induced ISR stimulation.

For the R522X variant, the effect on the activation of the ISR is less clear. While the ISR is significantly activated in R522X cells, we found that the therapeutic rescue approaches with tRNAAsn and Asn led to eIF2α phosphorylation levels that were not significantly different from either the WT or the R522X cells (Figures 5E, 5F, and S2F). Together, these results demonstrate that while NARS1 nonsense variant cytotoxicity can be rescued by providing exogenous Asn or tRNAAsn, the ISR stimulation is only rescued by Asn supplementation in both nonsense variants.

Previous work, as well as our results here, demonstrates that the ISR is activated in ARS-related disease models.22 ISR inhibitors, including those that target GCN2, would serve as a potent treatment option if proven efficacious.37,38 We tested a newly developed GCN2 inhibitor provided by Alesta Therapeutics (ALE2) in our NARS1 cell models. We found that the NARS1 R522X and R534X-induced cytotoxicity was dramatically reduced after 24 h following ALE2 treatment, with the proportion of dead cells reduced from 21% to 10% for R522X and 22% to 13% for R534X (Figure 5A).

Since GCN2 phosphorylates eIF2α, we probed eIF2α abundance and phosphorylation by western blotting but found no significant reduction in p-eIF2α levels (Figures 5C and 5D). Similar to the tRNAAsn and Asn supplementation for the R522X model, a reduction in p-eIF2α levels was observed upon ALE2 treatment (Figures 5E and 5F), which reduced p-eIF2α phosphorylation to a level indistinguishable from both WT and R522X. Lastly, to assess whether our proposed therapeutic interventions impact the transfection efficiency, and subsequent recombinant NARS expression in our model, western blotting was performed, probing for V5-tagged NARS post-treatment. No changes in transgenic NARS abundance were observed across treated and untreated conditions for the WT (Figures 5G–5I) and R534X variant (Figures 5H and 5J), with the exception of WT NARS co-expression with tRNAAsn, which resulted in a 40% reduction in V5-NARS abundance.

We found several pathways impacted by the R522X allele. For example, ribosome biogenesis is impacted in R522X-expressing cells (Table S2), as exemplified by the downregulated RRS1 and the upregulated ribosomal protein S17 (RPS17). Interestingly, RRS1 levels are restored to WT levels using all three therapeutic approaches (Figure 7A). RPS17, a protein component of the 4Ss subunit, is upregulated in R522X cells and, interestingly, is further upregulated in GCN2 inhibitor-treated or Asn-supplemented cells, while tRNA supplementation reduced RPS17 levels below WT levels (Figure 7B). Peptidyl-tRNA hydrolase 2 (PTRH2) releases tRNAs from the ribosome during protein synthesis and is upregulated by 2.5-fold in R522X cells, with levels restored to WT levels in Asn or GCN2 inhibitor-treated cells (Figure 7C). In addition to translation-associated proteins, we also identified proteins with a function in cell structure and adhesion to be deregulated in cells expressing mutant NARS1. Catenin delta-1 (CTNND1) encodes the p120-catenin (p120ctn) protein with a function in cell adhesion,40 while F-actin-capping protein subunit beta (CAPZB) is a member of the F-actin capping protein family and regulates actin filament growth.41,42 Both proteins are upregulated in R522X cells, and abundance is restored to WT levels in Asn-supplemented cells (Figures 7D and 7E). Interestingly, tRNA supplementation further increases CTNND1, while GCN2 inhibition reduces CAPZB.

We found several proteins that are associated with neurodevelopmental disorders, including the VEGF signaling pathway, which is altered in response to R522X expression. The VEGF pathway regulates both blood vessel formation (angiogenesis) and neuron development (neurogenesis),43,44 and its disruption is known to contribute to developmental disorders.44,45 In addition, the VEGF pathway has shown to be indirectly linked to ISR activation, specifically through increased expression of the stimulatory VEGF ligand in response to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and amino acid deprivation, which are conditions synonymous with the ISR.46 Examples of proteins in the VEGF pathway include dead-box protein 3 (DDX3X) and X-ray repair cross complementing 5 (XRCC5). DDX3X is an RNA helicase involved in splicing and associated with DDX3X-related neurodevelopmental disorder, which presents with similar symptoms to NARS1 disease, including microcephaly, movement disorders, and seizures.47 XRCC5 encodes the Ku80 protein, an integral part of the Ku70/80 heterodimer that is part of non-homologous end joining and DNA repair and is indirectly involved in VEGF signaling.48 Both XRCC5 and DDX3X are downregulated in R522X cells, but abundance is restored in all three therapeutic approaches (Figures 7F and 7G). Angiomotin (AMOT) is a crucial suppressor of cancer metastasis and interacts with the VEGF signaling pathways.49 AMOT is upregulated by 2-fold in R522X cells and returned to WT levels in Asn-supplemented cells. Interestingly, tRNA supplementation of R522X-expressing cells further increases AMOT abundance to 8-fold compared with WT. GCN2 inhibition treatment of NARS R522X cells, on the other hand, reduces AMOT to levels below WT (Figure 7H). Together, these data show that VEGF signaling is perturbed in the R522X cells and that the proteomic changes can consistently be reversed with Asn supplementation, while tRNA supplementation and GCN2 inhibition only restore some of the changed protein levels.

We also checked for changes in heat-shock protein abundance and ARS levels, as changes in these have been observed in other model systems for ARS diseases,2,9,11,12 but found no significant changes related to these pathways in the detected proteins between WT- and R522X-expressing cells. Interestingly, we found increased abundance of two ARS proteins, valinyl-tRNA synthetase and bifunctional glutaminyl-prolyl tRNA synthetase, in tRNA-supplemented cells, indicating a proteomic response to the perturbation of the tRNA pool (Table S2; Figure S5).

While our overexpression model in HEK293T cells revealed a defined phenotypic defect, we next sought to confirm the efficacy of Asn supplementation in patient-derived fibroblasts derived from a patient with a heterozygous R534X allele. Similar to other case reports of individuals with the R534X variant,2 the individual who donated the fibroblast cells displayed symptoms of developmental delay, microcephaly, intellectual disability with coordination issues and delayed speech development, an abnormal electroencephalogram (EEG) with seizures, and mixed hypotonia and hypertonia within the first year of life. At the time of the fibroblast sample acquisition, the patient’s seizures had resolved; however, peripheral neuropathy was diagnosed. Additional symptoms in this individual not previously documented in the literature include ongoing issues with eating-related gagging and coughing, as well as autism with stereotypes, hyperactivity, and problems with appropriate communication, along with sleep disruption with nighttime awakenings.

R534X was previously suggested to lead to reduced aminoacylation activity.2,17 We therefore measured NARS1 activity in the R534X patient-derived cells, comparing aminoacylation under high and low (stressed) conditions.52 Asn concentrations for the stressed condition were set at 0.075 mM. Reference plasma values in patients are usually between 0.03 and 0.085 mM. The experimental Asn concentration was set at 0.3 mM Asn, and even under these high Asn conditions, NARS1 activity was reduced to 25% in the patient-derived cells compared to control fibroblasts, indicating starkly reduced aminoacylation in NARS R534X cells. Under stress conditions, using increased temperature and reduced Asn availability, the disparity became even more evident, reducing NARS1 R34X aminoacylation activity to 8% of the control fibroblasts (Figures 8C and 8D). These data highlight that Asn availability is crucial for efficient aminoacylation in patient-derived cells. These findings suggest that Asn supplementation improves cell viability in the patient-derived fibroblasts and increases growth and turnover to a rate comparable with unaffected cells, suggesting Asn supplementation as a potential therapeutic approach for these patients.

To assess whether the ISR is activated in patient-derived fibroblasts as it was in the HEK293T overexpression model, western blotting and RT-qPCR were performed on fibroblast lysates and RNA extracts. Untreated control fibroblast mRNA extraction yielded slightly more mRNA, and we normalized all values to beta-actin (Figure S6). In contrast to the HEK293T overexpression model, p-eIF2α and eIF2α levels were not suggestive of activation of the ISR, with quantification showing that the abundance of both eIF2α proteoforms in the R534X fibroblasts was not significantly different compared to the control fibroblasts (Figures 8E and 8F). Nonetheless, quantification of the downstream transcription factors that are regulated during the ISR showed that ATF3 and ATF4 transcripts were significantly increased by 2.3- and 2.6-fold compared to control cells (Figure 8G), indicating an overall upregulation of the ISR. Upon supplementation with Asn, ATF3 and ATF4 levels returned to levels indistinguishable from control cells, indicating a rescue of the ISR activation (Figure 8G).

Transcripts with premature stop codons are often subject to nonsense-mediated decay (NMD).53,54 We therefore quantified NARS1 mRNA abundance by RT-qPCR and surprisingly found a 2.2-fold increase in NARS1 transcript abundance (Figure 8G), suggesting that the NARS1 R534X mRNA is not a substrate for NMD. Quantitative PCR cannot distinguish between the WT allele and the R534X allele, and it is possible that transcription of the WT NARS1 allele is upregulated, perhaps in a compensatory attempt to stabilize NARS1 protein levels. Interestingly, the NARS transcript abundance returned to WT levels upon Asn supplementation, indicating that increased NARS expression is a compensatory effect in the patient-derived cells. Since no increase in NARS1 protein abundance was found in previous studies for R534X fibroblasts relative to control cells,2 these data indicate that the increase in mRNA abundance may indeed compensate for reduced R534X protein abundance we observed in R534X-overexpressing cells (Figure 2C).

One of the concerns with using Asn as a treatment for a neuropathological disease, such as NARS1 disease, is its metabolization into aspartate and conversion in the Asn synthetase (ASNS) pathway into glutamate. Glutamate is an excitatory neurotransmitter that can worsen seizures and patient outcomes when found in excess or when supplemented orally.55,56,57 We, therefore, employed nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy to determine if Asn is readily converted to glutamate or other metabolites in the ASNS pathway and secreted to the media to act as a potential neurotransmitter. Spent media from HEK293T cells overexpressing WT or R522X NARS1 were collected 24 h post-transfection with or without treatment with 200 mg/L Asn and analyzed by NMR. As expected, the Asn peak intensity shows a significant increase from the spectra generated from the treated WT and R522X samples compared to the untreated samples (Figure 8H). No significant difference in peak intensity was observed in the spectra of glutamate, glutamine, or aspartate, across all treated and untreated groups (Figures 8J and 8K), indicating no increased turnover of Asn into aspartate, glutamate, or glutamine. Amino acid levels should nonetheless be monitored if amino acid supplementation is considered in patients. Overall, these findings reveal that the R534X mutation results in decreased aminoacylation activity and cellular viability in patient-derived fibroblasts. These NARS1 phenotypic defects can be rescued by Asn but not by cognate tRNA supplementation or GCN2 inhibition.

The consequences of pathogenic NARS1 alleles on patients are severe and have recently been compiled into a comprehensive disease description.1 While progress has been made in identifying the genetic cause, the underlying molecular mechanism for most of these alleles remains unclear, and there is currently no disease-specific treatment available. We here investigated the molecular and cellular mechanisms of two dominant NARS1 alleles R522X and R534X and tested potential therapeutic treatments, using cell-based and biochemical assays.

Previous in vitro studies have also shown that the recombinant R534X NARS1 protein exhibits reduced aminoacylation activity and leads to dysfunctional heterodimer formation and that substrate binding may destabilize dimer formation as we also observed here.17 Our data show that both nonsense variants R522X and R534X lead to a cytotoxic phenotype in HEK293T cells, and we found reduced cell viability in patient-derived fibroblasts. It should be noted that the observed phenotypes between HEK293T cells and patient-derived fibroblasts were not always consistent, where e.g., in contrast to fibroblasts, no defect in cell viability is observed in HEK293T cells, but we instead observed cytotoxicity. This discrepancy can likely be attributed to the differences in genetic backgrounds between HEK293T cells and fibroblasts lines, as well as diverse genetic backgrounds of patient and control lines. In addition, HEK293T cells retain multiple (up to three due to chromosomal instability) WT NARS alleles. Nonetheless, we found that the dominant-negative nonsense alleles showed a phenotype in both models. In addition, a previous study showed that the R534X allele demonstrates a dominant-negative growth phenotype in a yeast model, which aligns with the dominant phenotype we observed here.16 Our data from patient-derived fibroblasts confirm that heterozygous NARS1 R534X fibroblasts retain less than 25% NARS1 aminoacylation activity, which is further reduced under stress conditions. Overexpression of NARS1 R534X in human cells shows reduced protein abundance compared to the WT NARS1, indicating reduced stability of NARS1 R534X in the context of human cells. Interestingly, we also observed reduced protein abundance for WT NARS1 when co-expressed with tRNAAsn. This may be attributed to NARS being actively degraded to prevent the production of excessive Asn-tRNAAsn, although this remains speculative. We previously showed that some pathogenic variants in HARS1 cause mistranslation,9,12 but we found no indication of mistranslation generated by mutant NARS1 in our proteomic experiments. This, as well as previously published data,2,4,17 indicated that a dominant loss of function may be the cause of the observed phenotypic and cytotoxic effects of the dominant NARS1 alleles.

Our proteomic data show several changes in the proteome of NARS1 R522X-overexpressing cells. Some of these changes, and the lack thereof when compared to our treatment conditions, may be attributed to variation between replicates. Western blotting probing for specific proteins associated with our observed proteomic perturbations may be performed in the future to further validate these findings. It is interesting to note that a more dramatic effect was observed when comparing the transcriptome of patient-derived fibroblasts with bi-allelic NARS variants to control fibroblasts.2 These sequencing data showed increased expression of several other ARSs in homozygous and compound heterozygous patient-derived fibroblasts, including cytoplasmic glutamyl-tRNA synthetase (DARS1), GARS1, cytoplasmic arginyl-tRNA synthetase (RARS1), cytoplasmic seryl-tRNA synthetase (SARS1), cytoplasmic threonyl-tRNA synthetase (TARS1), cytoplasmic tryptophanyl-tRNA synthetase (WARS1), and cytoplasmic tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase (YARS1).2 In contrast, we observed no significant changes in ARS proteins in the proteome of the dominant NARS R522X allele. Despite larger transcriptomic changes in the bi-allelic variants, we found that the deregulated pathways largely overlap with our proteomic observations. Like our data, these transcriptomic analyses of bi-allelic NARS1 variants revealed upregulation of VEGF-associated pathways, upregulation of the ISR (e.g., ATF4), and changes in ribosomal homoeostasis.2 The ISR is also activated in other synthetase-associated diseases, such as GARS disease models in mice21,22,38 and HARS disease models in rat cells.58 These data show that while each ARS and even each specific ARS allele can cause a variety of different phenotypes and diseases, some of the cellular responses may reveal common themes, such as ISR activation.

Mechanistically, it is thought that supplementation with cognate amino acid or cognate tRNA will increase enzyme activity of an aminoacylation-deficient ARS to a level that is sufficient to sustain translation or outcompete non-specific binding events that lead to mistranslation. Reduced ARS activity can lead to uncharged tRNA accumulation, which activates the ISR.59 Uncharged tRNA can directly stimulate ISR activation through binding to GCN2 via its HARS-like domain, or, as discovered more recently, can act indirectly by allowing GCN2 to interact with the ribosomal P-stalk, which also serves as a potent trigger of this stress response.29,30 Restoring sufficient and correct aminoacylation activity would prevent the activation of the ISR, which can itself be pathogenic.21,22,23,35

Over the past few years, amino acid supplementation has gained increasing attention in the treatment of ARS-associated diseases.60,61,62 For example, methionine supplementation led to phenotypic improvement in an infant and children affected with cytoplasmic methionyl-tRNA synthetase (MARS1)-associated diseases,62,63,64 allowing for a successful lung transplantation in three methionine-supplemented patients with MARS-related pulmonary alveolar proteinosis.65 In another success story, patients with bi-allelic variants in Isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase (IARS), leucyl-tRNA synthetase (LARS), phenylalanyl-tRNA synthetase subunit B (FARSB) and seryl-tRNA synthetase (SARS) were treated with the corresponding amino acids, resulting in overall improvement and management of symptoms.66 A mitochondrial phenylalanyl-tRNA synthetase (FARS2) deficiency responded to phenylalanine supplementation.67 These and other examples indicate amino acid supplementation as a first-line treatment for many ARS-associated diseases.59,66 Indeed, we found that supplementation of Asn rescued not only cytotoxicity but also reversed many of the changes in the proteome of NARS1 R522X-overexpressing cells and rescued cell viability and aminoacylation defects in patient-derived fibroblasts. On the other hand, while ATP stabilized the recombinant protein, increasing glucose concentrations in the media with the intent to increase ATP availability in the cell did not lead to a phenotypic rescue. Together, these findings indicate that Asn should be further studied and clinically evaluated as a potential first-line treatment for NARS1 patients.

While we are hopeful that Asn supplementation will reverse symptoms in NARS1 patients or at least slow progression, as previously observed for other ARS-associated diseases noted earlier, it is also important to consider the development of additional therapeutics. Our previous work in humanized yeast models showed that amino acid supplementation may not be a one-size-fits-all therapeutic approach. For example, histidine was shown to rescue some HARS alleles, while other alleles, even near the active site, can be exacerbated by histidine supplementation.12 These variants were rescued by cognate tRNA supplementation in yeast.9 The cognate tRNA supplementation approach was also successful in restoring a mouse phenotype modeling GARS1-associated disease.68 Indeed, tRNA therapeutics have gained interest, and the development of therapeutic tRNA delivery strategies has brought tRNA medicine closer to clinical applications.59,69 Our data show that cognate tRNA supplementation indeed rescued the cytotoxic effect of pathogenic NARS1 variants but did not ameliorate ISR activation, though the interpretation of the result is complicated by the fact that the level of tRNAAsn overexpression is not known. Perhaps the finding is not surprising, since delivering additional uncharged tRNA facilitated a rescue of NARS1 activity to levels sustaining growth by providing more Asn-tRNA substrate for translation; this approach may also provide additional uncharged tRNA to trigger GCN2 activation. While tRNA supplementation may be less desirable for variants that respond to Asn supplementation, future experiments will show if tRNA supplementation may be a therapeutic approach for other NARS1 alleles. For example, an in silico analysis of the R545C mutant NARS1 protein using a nematode ortholog suggests impaired tRNA binding,2 which may be rescued by tRNA rather than amino acid supplementation. Another interesting approach for the rescue of nonsense alleles such as NARS R522X and R534X, but also many other nonsense ARS alleles, may be found in tRNA nonsense suppressors, allowing for the production of the full-length NARS protein. This approach was shown to be successful for other disease-causing nonsense alleles, and we will explore nonsense suppression in the future.69,70,71

tRNA and amino acid supplementation are highly promising approaches for the treatment of ARS-associated diseases but nonetheless may not be successful for all alleles or require further supporting pharmaceutical intervention. The chronic activation of the ISR has been identified as contributing to ARS-associated and other diseases.22,23,35 Inhibitors of GCN2 have been considered therapeutic options for several diseases, including cancer.36,38,72,73,74 The reduction of ISR markers is a hallmark measurement for the rescue of multiple ARS-related diseases.5,19,21,22,28,31 Previous work in mouse and yeast models of ARS-related diseases has demonstrated that treatment with GCN2 inhibitors, cognate amino acid, or tRNA is efficacious in improving pathogenic phenotypes.9,11,12,22 Our data demonstrate that the GCN2 inhibitor ALE2 can rescue a cytotoxic phenotype caused by pathogenic NARS1 alleles in a HEK293T overexpression model. As expected, the inhibitor was unable to restore the dysregulated proteome caused by NARS1 nonsense variant expression. Nonetheless, considering the chronic activation of the ISR is likely a large contributing factor to NARS1 disease development and progression and has previously been associated with the pathogenicity of CMT-causative ARS alleles,22,23 ISR inhibitors and their continued development represent an important avenue for a small-molecule therapeutic approach for ARS disease.

Genetic diseases, including the extremely rare NARS1 disease, place a large burden on patients and patient caregivers, while drug development is of limited commercial interest to the pharmaceutical industry.75 We investigated the cellular consequences of two nonsense NARS1 alleles and found that the supplementation of Asn leads to a rescue of the cellular phenotypes in both human cell models as well as NARS1 patient-derived fibroblasts. Asn supplementation restored the cellular proteome to closely resemble the WT proteome and prevented activation of the ISR. tRNA supplementation and small-molecule inhibition of the ISR also restored the cellular phenotype but only partially rescued ISR activation and the proteome. We previously found that amino acid supplementation may not be a one-size-fits-all treatment for ARS-related disorders and can indeed exacerbate phenotypes for certain HARS mutants. Indeed, different dominant NARS alleles were shown to complement a yeast NARS deletion strain at various degrees, which indicates that not all disease-causing NARS alleles elicit the same cellular consequences.16 Therefore, personalized medicine approaches need to take allele-specific phenotypes and cellular consequences into account.9,12 Considering that therapeutic tRNA delivery to patients is still in development and GCN2 inhibitors have not passed safety tests yet, the low-hanging fruit of ARS disorders is amino acid supplementation. Nonetheless, we and others believe that this may only be successful in some cases and may not ameliorate all symptoms in patients. It is therefore valuable to assess alternative strategies, such as cognate tRNA supplementation, nonsense suppression for nonsense alleles, inhibition of the ISR, or other supportive treatments that we have not considered here. For the NARS1 alleles (R534X and R522X) characterized in this study, our data suggest that the readily available supplement Asn may point toward a viable therapy.

Limited tryptic proteolysis of NARS1 proteins was performed as previously described.9 Reactions were incubated at 37°C for 5, 30, 60, or 120 min and quenched with 3× SDS dye (6% sodium dodecyl sulfate [SDS], 30% glycerol, 0.006% bromophenol blue, 125 mM Tris-HCl [pH 6.8]), followed by boiling at 95°C. Controls (C0 and C120) were incubated without trypsin for 0 or 120 min, respectively. NARS1 proteins were equilibrated with an equivalent volume of buffer before digestion.

HEK293T cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 300,000 cells and grown in DMEM medium (#11995073, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. The following day, upon reaching ∼70% confluency, cells were transfected, and media were replaced, supplemented with or without 200 mg/L Asn or 1 nM ALE2. Transfected cells were left overnight to allow uptake of lipocomplexes and washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (#14190144, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were then lifted with 0.25% Trypsin- ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (#25200056, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and centrifuged at 700 × g for 15 min at 4°C. Residual media and trypsin were removed in an additional wash with PBS and centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Cells were stored at −80°C until ready to be digested. HEK293T cell pellets were resuspended with 8 M urea and 2% SDS in 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4). Each sample was sonicated at an amplitude of 60% for 5 min with cycles of 30 s on/30 s off. Cell debris was pellet, and soluble protein was quantified using Pierce bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assay kit. An aliquot of 10 μg per sample was reduced with 100 mM tris (2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) and alkylated with 200 mM Iodoacetaminde (IAA). The lysate was then digested with trypsin (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA) at a 50:1, protein:enzyme ratio according to manufacturer’s recommendations. Digested peptides were desalted by C18 clean-up, lyophilized, and stored until analysis.

The digested peptides were resuspended in buffer A (0.1% FA) and injected into the timsTOF Pro (Bruker Daltronics, Bremen, Germany) by nanoflow liquid chromatography using a Thermo Scientific EASY-nLC 1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). Liquid chromatography was performed using a constant flow of 300 μL/min and a 15 cm reversed-phased column with a 75 μm inner diameter filled with Reprosil C18 (PepSEP, Bruker, Germany). Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid and mobile phase B was 99.9% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid (FA). Peptide separation was carried out over 55.5 min as follows: linearly 4% A to 35% B over 50 min with an increase to 100% B over 30 s and held constant for 5 min to clean the column. Column equilibration was done prior to sample loading automatically. The trapped ion mobility spectrometry (TIMS)- time of flight (TOF) Pro was outfitted with a CaptiveSpray source (Bruker Daltronics, Bremen, Germany), operated in parallel accumulation—serial fragmentation (PASEF) mode. Trapped ion mobility separation was achieved using an accumulation time of 100 ms in the first TIMS region and ramps of the TIMS region from 0.85 to 1.30 V.s/cm2, with each ramp lasting 100 ms. MS and tandem MS (MS/MS) scans were limited to 100 to 1,700 m/z, and a polygon filter was applied to the m/z and ion mobility dimensions to select for multiple charged ions most likely to be peptide precursors. Collision energy was applied as a function of ion mobility with a linear regression using the following parameter settings: 0.85 V.s/cm2 → 27 eV, 1.30 V.s/cm2 → 45 eV. TIMS voltage was calibrated using ions from the Agilent Tune Mix (m/z 622, 922, and 1,222). Active exclusion of tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) scans was enabled at a setting of 0.40 min. Quadrupole isolation was set to 2 m/z for m/z less than 700, and 3.0 m/z for ions with an m/z greater than 800. A linear regression calculation was done automatically for ions between m/z 700 and 800.

MS raw data files were processed using PEAKS Studio 12.5 (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc., Ontario, Canada). The data were searched against the Homo sapiens Uniprot database. Precursor ion mass error tolerance was set to 10 ppm, and fragment ion mass error tolerance was set to 0.02 Da. Semi-specific cleavage with trypsin was selected with a maximum of two missed cleavages. A fixed modification of carbamidomethylation (+57.02 Da) on cysteine residues and variable modifications of deamidation (+0.98 Da) on Asn and glutamine, as well as oxidation (+15.99 Da) on methionine, were specified. A 1% false discovery rate (FDR) was set for the database search. After the database search, mutations were manually searched. Only mutations supported by relative fragment ion intensities >2% and having a PEAKS AScore of >15 were considered in the analysis.

For RT-qPCR, cells were collected, and RNA was extracted as described earlier using a commercial RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Further cleanup of RNA was done using phenol-chloroform extraction. For input, 2 μg of RNA was used for cDNA synthesis using the high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (#4368814, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR was performed using PowerTrack SYBR Green Master Mix (#A46012, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and measured with a QuantStudio3 instrument (#A28567, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Negative control reactions containing either no reverse transcriptase or no cDNA were performed in parallel to monitor DNA contamination or primer-primer interactions. Results were quantified using the ΔΔCT method and normalized to beta-actin. Primers used for the experiment are listed in Table S1.

Cells were then lifted with 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (#25200056, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and centrifuged at 700 × g for 15 min at 4°C. Supernatant was aspirated, and the cell pellet was rinsed with PBS, followed by centrifuging at 5,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Cells were then lysed (50 mM Na2HPO4, 1 mM NaP2O7, 20 mM NaF, 2 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid [EDTA], 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM dithiothreitol [DTT], 2 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride [PMSF], and 3.2 mg/mL protease inhibitor). Lysates were suspended in 3× SDS buffer and separated by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) at 100 V (#1658004, Bio-Rad Laboratories), followed by wet transfer onto nitrocellulose membrane (#1620115, Bio-Rad Laboratories) using a Mini Trans-Blot Electrophoretic Transfer Cell (#1703930, Bio-Rad Laboratories). Primary antibodies against the V5 tag (Abcam, #ab206566-1001) to detect NARS-V5 fusion protein, eIF2α (#9722, Cell Signaling Technology), and peIF2αSer51 (Abcam, #ab32157) were used at 1:1,000 dilution in bovine serum albumin (BSA)-Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST). Secondary antibodies IRDye 800CW Donkey anti-rabbit IgG, goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L)-HRP conjugate (#1706516, Bio-Rad), and 600CW goat anti-mouse (LI-COR) were used at 1:5,000 and 1:10,000 dilutions, respectively. Blots were imaged with a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (#734BR5528, Bio-Rad Laboratories) and analyzed using ImageLab software.

To investigate changes in extracellular levels of selected amino acids in conditioned media, 1 mL of growth media was collected from each cell culture flask after 24 h of incubation with treatment and centrifuged for 5 min at 200 × g to pellet cells, followed by a 20-min centrifugation of the supernatant at 21,000 × g to remove remaining debris. Samples were then flash frozen and stored at −80°C until freeze-dried (Labconco, Kansas City, MO, USA). The dried samples were then reconstituted using 600 μL of 50 mM phosphate buffer in 99.8% D2O (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc., Andover, MA, USA) at pH 7.2 (uncorrected) with 50 μM 3-(tetramethysilane) propionic acid-2,2,3,3-d4 (TMSP) (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc., Andover, MA, USA) for spectral referencing. The samples were then centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C to remove any residual debris, and the supernatants were transferred to 5 mm nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) tubes for data acquisition.

NMR experiments were conducted at 298 K using a Bruker Avance III HD 600 MHz spectrometer (Bruker, Bremen, Germany) with a 5 mm triple-resonance (1H, 13C, 15N) room temperature probe. An automated data acquisition was carried out using a SampleJet autosampler and Bruker ICON-NMR software. One-dimensional (1D) 1H-NMR spectra were collected with 32 K data points, a spectrum width of 6,578 Hz, 256 scans, and 16 dummy scans using an excitation sculpting pulse sequence for water suppression.78 MestReNova software v.15.0.1 (Mestrelab Inc., Barcelona, Spain) and Chenomx Profiler v. 17.0.6 (Chenomx, Edmonton, AB, Canada) were used for spectral annotation. Metabolite peak integrals were used to quantify relative metabolite concentration. Relative metabolite compositions were calculated from spectral regions corresponding to Asn (2.89 ppm), aspartate (3.89 ppm), glutamine (2.46 ppm), and glutamate (2.04 ppm) and plotted for each group. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Aminoacylation was assessed by measuring NARS1 activity in cultured patient-derived fibroblasts. Fibroblast lysates (cytosolic fraction) were incubated in triplicate at 37°C for 10 min in a reaction buffer containing 50 mL Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 12 mM MgCl2, 25 mM KCl, 1 mg/mL BSA, 0.5 mM spermine, 1 mM ATP, 0.2 mM yeast total tRNA, 1 mmol/L dithiothreitol, and 0.3 mmol/L [15N2]-Asn. The reaction was terminated using trichloroacetic acid. After washing the sample with trichloroacetic acid, ammonia was added to release the labeled amino acids from the tRNAs. [13C2, 15 N]-glycine was added as an internal standard, and the labeled amino acids were quantified by liquid chromatography MS/MS (LC-MS/MS). To investigate the effect of temperature and lower Asn availability on NARS1 aminoacylation activity, the assay was repeated after incubating the cell lysate for 10 min at 42 °C. The assay was then conducted as described with reduced Asn at 0.075 mM [15N2]-Asn.
