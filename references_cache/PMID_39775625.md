Transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) is a progressive and challenging 
disease characterized by deposition of misfolded transthyretin (TTR) protein in 
the myocardial interstitium. Until recently, treatment options for ATTR-CM were 
limited, with tafamidis emerging as the primary therapeutic agent targeting the 
stabilization of TTR tetramers to prevent amyloid fibril formation. However, 
advancements in understanding the underlying mechanisms of ATTR-CM have led to 
developing novel therapeutic strategies to address various aspects of the 
disease process. This review explores current therapeutic options for treating 
ATTR-CM. It encompasses strategies ranging from inhibiting TTR synthesis using 
RNA silencing methods, such as small interfering RNA (siRNA; e.g., the HELIOS-B 
trial with vutrisiran), antisense oligonucleotides (ASO; e.g., the 
CARDIO-TTRansform trial with eplontersen), and gene-editing technologies such as 
CRISPR/Cas9 (e.g., the MAGNITUDE trial with NTLA-2001). Additionally, it 
explores TTR stabilization approaches beyond tafamidis, such as next-generation 
acoramidis, which showed success in the ATTRibute-CM trial. It also examines 
therapies promoting TTR degradation and removal, which includes the use of 
monoclonal antibodies (e.g., DepleTTR-CM with ALXN2220) and other compounds, 
such as doxycycline or epigallocatechin-3-gallate (a green tea component), 
which, despite long-standing recognition, remain underexplored. Novel 
approaches, such as seeding inhibitors and molecular tweezers, which aim to 
inhibit TTR fibril formation, are also discussed as potential future management 
strategies. The review further highlights the role of ongoing clinical trials in 
evaluating the efficacy and safety of these innovative therapies, emphasizing 
their potential to expand treatment options and improve outcomes for patients 
with ATTR-CM. Advancements in supportive therapies are also discussed to offer a 
comprehensive overview of the evolving therapeutic landscape.

INTRODUCTION
Transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR- 
-CM) with phenotyping overlap between 
hypertrophic and restrictive cardiomyopathy 
results from misfolded transthyretin (TTR) 
deposition in the myocardial interstitium. TTR 
is a tetrameric protein mainly synthesized in 
the liver, responsible for transporting the 
thyroid hormone thyroxine and retinol [1]. 
There are two distinct forms of trans -
thyretin amyloidosis (ATTR): one is variant 
ATTR (ATTRv), caused by mutations in the 
TTR gene and has a prevalence of 1–9 per

the TTR gene is normal and has a prevalence 
of 1–5 per 10 000 individuals (according to Or-
phanet). Established in 2007, the Transthyretin 
Amyloidosis Outcomes Survey (THAOS) is the 
largest global, longitudinal study of ATTR, 
including variant and wild-type forms and as-
ymptomatic carriers. With over 6000 patients 
enrolled over 15 years, THAOS highlights the 
disease’s genotypic and phenotypic diversity, 
with nearly a quarter of symptomatic cases 
showing mixed phenotypes, which empha -

all forms of amyloidosis. These initiatives are expected to

profiles of amyloidosis.
Although, in terms of signs and symptoms, there are 
specific red flags, the broad clinical manifestations of amy-
loidosis make diagnosis challenging and often result in 
a prolonged diagnostic process [7]. Cardiac involvement in 
amyloidosis stands as the primary driver of both morbidity 
and mortality in affected patients, who suffer mainly from 
progressive heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and conduction 
disturbances. Median survival following diagnosis in un-
treated patients is approximately 2.6 years for ATTRv-CM 
caused by the Val122Ile mutation, 5.8 years for ATTRv-CM 
caused by other genetic variants, and 3.6 to 4.8 years for 
ATTRwt-CM [8–10]. 
Therefore, a prompt diagnosis and targeted therapeutic 
intervention are of paramount significance, as conventional 
heart failure treatment is frequently not well-tolerated [11]. 
Tafamidis is no longer the only treatment approved by the 
US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for ATTR-CM, as 
acoramidis was approved on November 22, 2024.
Proposed staging systems for ATTR-CM are essential 
for guiding treatment, as patients should receive therapies 
tailored to their disease stage. Tafamidis and acoramidis are 
highly effective in early-stage disease, while emerging newer 
therapies may offer promising options for patients in more 
advanced stages [9, 12, 13].

The hallmark of ATTR is the extracellular deposition of 
aggregated TTR or TTR fibrils in tissues, although the 
exact process of aggregation and fibril formation re -
mains only partially understood. The discovery of a novel 
mechano-enzymatic mechanism underlying TTR amy -
loidosis, revealing a complex interplay between intrinsic 
amyloidogenicity, environmental factors, and protease 
activity, holds promise and enhances our understanding 
of the disease’s molecular basis, but further examination 
is needed to grasp its implications [14, 15]. Biochemical 
and biophysical evidence suggests that tetrameric TTR 
becomes unstable, dissociating into dimers and mono -
mers with a partially unfolded conformation, which then 
self-assemble into toxic nonfibrillar aggregates and even-
tually amyloid fibrils [16, 17]. The process of TTR amyloid 
formation is presented in Figure 1. Understanding these 
mechanisms helps identify therapeutic targets to prevent 
or halt disease progression.
TARGETED THERAPIES IN TRANSTHYRETIN 
AMYLOID CARDIOMYOPATHY
Various targeted therapies in ATTR-CM have been explored, 
including inhibition of TTR synthesis, TTR stabilization, and 
TTR degradation [11]. The inhibition of TTR fibril formation 
has yet to be thoroughly investigated, but it appears to 
hold considerable potential. Therapies inhibiting TTR 
synthesis include liver transplantation, gene silencing 
therapies (small interfering RNA [siRNA] and antisense ol-
igonucleotides [ASO]), and the revolutionary gene-editing 
technology, CRISPR/Cas9. TTR stabilizers are currently the 
leading therapies for treating ATTR-CM, including selective 
agents that are in the spotlight. Therapies aimed at TTR 
degradation and removal, encompassing disruptors and 
depleters, are viewed with great hopes as they potentially 
offer a chance for remission. Seeding inhibitors and mo -
lecular tweezers are promising new drugs for inhibiting 
fibril formation, but their path to clinical approval remains 
uncertain. Figure 2 illustrates approved, ongoing, and yet-
to-be-fully investigated therapeutic strategies.

INHIBITION OF TRANSTHYRETIN SYNTHESIS 
Silencers include siRNA and ASO. SiRNA is a class of dou-
ble-stranded RNA molecules, 20–25 nucleotides in length. 
Discovered in 1998, RNA interference (RNAi) serves as 
a natural defense mechanism against foreign nucleic acids 
and regulates gene expression. SiRNAs were identified as 
crucial mediators of RNAi in mammalian cells. Delivery of 
siRNAs is essential for inducing gene knockdown, with 
endoribonuclease Dicer processing longer double-strand-
ed RNA into mature siRNA. The siRNA, either synthesized 
chemically or processed by Dicer, is introduced in the 
RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) that comprises 
several distinct proteins, including Argonaute-2 (Ago-2). 
Following activation by removing the “sense” or “passen-
ger” strand, the “antisense” or “guide” strand of the siRNA 
directs RISC to bind with the target messenger RNA (mRNA), 
facilitating cleavage by Ago-2 within the RISC complex. 
Unlike antisense technologies, RNAi operates catalytically, 
enabling efficient gene knockdown with minimal siRNA 
concentrations [19].
ASOs are single-stranded synthetic RNA or DNA se -
quences, typically 15–20 long nucleotides, designed to 
complement specific mRNA sequences. They bind to their 
target mRNA through Watson-Crick base pairing. ASOs can 
function through two main mechanisms: RNA cleavage 
and RNA blockage. In the mechanism of RNA cleavage, 
ASOs form heteroduplexes with mRNA, leading to the 
recruitment of RNase H1, resulting in the degradation of 
the mRNA. This degradation inhibits the translation of the 
protein encoded by the mRNA [20].
While both siRNA and ASO strategies for gene targeting 
have similarities, their differences affect their suitability 
for specific applications. ASOs, being single-stranded, 
offer a cost advantage over siRNA production. However, 
siRNAs are more stable, making them preferable for in 
vitro studies compared to ASOs. Furthermore, ASOs are 
more accessible to deliver in vivo since they do not need 
a vector, and simple chemical modifications can enhance 
their resistance to nucleases. Selecting siRNA and ASO 
strategies depends on factors such as the target gene and 
intended application [21].
Gene-editing therapeutic agents utilizing CRISPR/  
/Cas9 technology have the potential to provide single-dose 
treatments for ATTR.

Emmanuelle Charpentier in 2012, revolutionized the field 
of molecular biology, earning them the Nobel Prize in 
Chemistry in 2020. It enables deliberate insertion, removal, 
or modification of DNA in living cells. Initially discovered 
in prokaryotes as an adaptive immune mechanism against 
viruses, the CRISPR system consists of clustered, regular -
ly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) and 
associated proteins (Cas). Its two main components, the

Liver transplantation
Before 2018, liver transplantation for ATTRv and heart trans-
plantation for ATTR-CM were the primary treatments. How-
ever, they were limited by the challenges of advanced 
patient age and the need for multiorgan transplantation 
in some cases. While still available, these options are sur -
passed mainly by more effective drug therapies, offering 
better outcomes and quality of life [1, 25].

Small interfering RNA (siRNA)
The development of TTR silencing strategies initially tar -
geted hereditary TTR polyneuropathies, with or without 
cardiac involvement. 
Patisiran, formulated as lipid nanoparticles for delivery to 
hepatocytes, selectively binds to a genetically conserved se-
quence in the 3’-untranslated region (3’-UTR) of both mutant 
and wild-type TTR mRNA, leading to direct, sequence-spe-
cific degradation of TTR mRNA in the liver [26]. The findings 
from APOLLO-B indicated that patisiran exhibits superiority 
over placebo, improving quality of life endpoints among  
ATTR-CM patients. However, patisiran, the first FDA-ap -
proved (2019) RNA silencing therapy for ATTRv-related pol-
yneuropathy, faced FDA refusal for ATTR-CM treatment. The 
responsible party announced the discontinuation of efforts 
to seek approval for the extended indication of patisiran in 
the US. Nevertheless, they remain committed to ensuring 
continued access to the product for ATTR-CM patients par-
ticipating in the open-label extension phase of APOLLO-B 
and through the expanded access protocol.
Vutrisiran, the next-generation siRNA, follows a simi lar 
approach to patisiran. However, it differs because it utilizes 
a phosphorothioate backbone instead of the phosphodi-
ester backbone found in patisiran, enhancing stability 
and tolerability. Additionally, vutrisiran is conjugated with 
a triantennary N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) ligand. This 
covalent conjugation of the siRNA with GalNac enhances 
hepatocyte delivery and uptake and stabilizes the mol-
ecule during systemic circulation [27]. Alnylam Pharma-
ceuticals announced at the end of June 2024 that in the 
HELIOS-B study, 655 patients with ATTR-CM who received 
subcutaneous vutrisiran every three months experienced 
a significant 28% reduction in the risk of all-cause mor -
tality and recurrent cardiovascular (CV) events compared 
to those who received placebo over a 36-month period. 
Among the 395 patients who were not on background 
tafamidis, there was a notable 33% reduction in the risk 
of the primary endpoint compared to the placebo group. 
At the European Society of Cardiology Congress in August 
2024, it was presented that vutrisiran showed significant 
advantages over placebo in ATTR-CM patients. Treatment 
with vutrisiran resulted in a reduced risk of death from any

during the phase III ENDEAVOUR clinical trial. Nonclinical

strated no adverse effects on CV or respiratory function in 
monkeys and no neurological effects. Repeat-dose studies 
in mice, rats, and monkeys also indicated good revusiran 
tolerance [29].

Antisense oligonucleotides (ASO)
Inotersen is a second-generation ASO with 2’-O-(2-meth-
oxyethyl) modification targeting transthyretin mRNA [32]. 
In the NEURO-TTR trial, which evaluated neurological 
function in individuals with polyneuropathy due to hATTR, 
significant adverse effects, including glomerulonephritis 
(3%) and thrombocytopenia (2%), were noted in the in-
otersen-treated group. Consequently, the FDA approved 
inotersen in 2019 but implemented a Risk Evaluation and 
Mitigation Program mandating weekly monitoring of 
platelet counts and renal function [33].
Eplontersen, a novel, longer-acting ASO, is currently 
undergoing evaluation in individuals with ATTR-CM. The 
phase III CARDIO-TTRansform study has fully enrolled over 
1400 patients, making it the largest study conducted in 
this patient population to date. Additionally, eplontersen 
received Fast Track designation from the FDA for the treat-
ment of patients with ATTR-CM.

Genetic editing
CRISPR/Cas9
Gene editing holds promising potential as a once-in-a-
lifetime intervention, requiring only a single drug dose. 
This strategy would present an advantage over siRNAs and 
ASOs requiring serial infusions.
NTLA-2001 marks the pioneering use of systemic ad-
ministration for an investigational CRISPR/Cas9 therapy. 
Intellia Therapeutics’ approach employs lipid nanoparticles 
to transport a two-component genome editing system di-
rectly to the liver. This system consists of sgRNA and mRNA 
encoding the Cas9 enzyme, enabling precise gene editing. 
Strong preclinical evidence demonstrates a significant and 
durable reduction in TTR levels [35]. Intellia announced giv-
ing the drug to the first patient in the global pivotal phase 
III MAGNITUDE trial of NTLA-2001 on March 18, 2024. The 
MAGNITUDE trial aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of 
NTLA-2001 as a one-time treatment for patients suffering 
from ATTR-CM.
YolTech Therapeutics received approval from the Chi-
na’s National Medical Products Administration on March 1, 
2024, for its gene-editing drug, YOLT-201, which is under-
going phase I clinical trials to address ATTR-CM. The first 
patient dosed with YOLT-201 in an investigator-initiated 
trial (IIT) by the end of 2023 achieved promising preliminary 
efficacy and safety results. The innovative lipid nanoparticle 
delivery system employed by YolTech sets a notable prec-
edent for advancing gene-editing therapeutics in China.

TRANSTHYRETIN STABILIZATION 
(STABILIZERS)
In the early stages of the disease, a primary focus of 
disease-modifying strategies for ATTR involves targeting 
mechanisms that prevent the disassociation of TTR te -
tramers, ultimately leading to the formation of amyloid 
fibrils. Figure 4 illustrates the mechanism of action of 
TTR stabilizers.
Selective stabilizers
Tafamidis, a first-in-class TTR kinetic stabilizer, revolution-
ized ATTR treatment. Derived from non-selective difluni-
sal, it retains TTR-stabilizing properties while eliminating 
cyclooxygenase inhibition, avoiding NSAID-related side 
effects such as gastrolesivity, cardiotoxicity, and nephro -
toxicity [38].
In the ATTR-ACT trial, tafamidis meglumine showed 
superiority over placebo with a win ratio of 1.7 in a pooled 
approach of both doses at 18 months. Treatment with ta-
famidis was associated with a 30% reduction in all-cause 
mortality compared to placebo (29.5% vs. 42.9%) and a 32% 
lower rate of CV-related hospital admissions. However, in-
dividuals classified as New York Heart Association (NYHA) 
class III at baseline did not experience a similar reduction 
in CV-related hospitalization compared to other subgroups 
(P = 0.78). Secondary outcome measures included a slower 
decline in the 6-minute walk test distance and a slower de-
cline in quality of life assessed by the KCCQ score. Tafamidis 
meglumine was generally well-tolerated, with minor side 
effects such as urinary tract infections [39]. Higher plasma 
concentrations of tafamidis, achieved with doses greater 
than 60 mg of tafamidis meglumine, were necessary for 
maximum TTR kinetic stability. Based on the trial results 
and bioequivalence studies, the FDA and European Med-
icines Agency (EMA) granted marketing authorization for 
a once-daily dose of tafamidis  free acid  61  mg (a single 
capsule bioequivalent to  tafamidis meglumine 80  mg). 
The longer-term survival data and the lack of dose-related 
safety concerns support tafamidis meglumine 80 mg as 
the optimal dose [40]. In the long-term extension study, 
patients who began treatment with tafamidis during the 
ATTR-ACT trial demonstrated significantly improved sur -
vival compared to those who initially received placebo. 
These findings highlight the importance of early diagnosis 
and treatment in managing ATTR-CM [41]. Similar to the 
findings of the aforementioned extension study, real-world 
data show higher survival rates in patients with ATTR-CM 
compared to those observed in the ATTR-ACT trial [42]. 
Moreover, the results confirm that tafamidis is effective in 
treating ATTR-CM patients regardless of whether they are 
under or over 80 years of age [43]. Unfortunately, although 
tafamidis is effective, many patients continue to experience 
disease progression during treatment [44, 45]. A recent 
study offered insights into real-world post-marketing phar-
macovigilance of tafamidis, with extensive data analysis 
indicating a favorable safety profile. Identified potential 
adverse event risks include hearing system damage and 
urinary tract infections, with no signals of potentially fatal 
or disabling adverse reactions [46].
Granted expedited approval by the FDA as a break -
through therapy for a rare condition in May 2019, tafa-
midis was introduced in the US with an annual cost of 
$225 000 [47]. Subsequently, it received marketing autho-
rization in the EU for patients with ATTR-CM in February 
2020. Regrettably, tafamidis is not universally accessible 
or covered by reimbursement in all countries. However, in 
Poland, it has been available through the Drug Program 
B.162 since July 1, 2024.

TTR ELIMINATION (DISRUPTORS 
AND DEPLETERS)
Until recently, options for targeting the breakdown and 
removal of TTR amyloid fibrils have been limited. Elimi-
nating amyloid fibrils is particularly crucial for patients in 
advanced disease stages, as existing strategies, such as 
gene silencing and tetramer stabilization, cannot address 
the accumulation of organ amyloid. Strategies for targeting 
amyloid deposits include disrupting the fibrils or using 
targeted antibodies against misfolded TTR fibrils.
Disruptors
The current data regarding TTR disruptors are insufficient, 
and their use has not been recommended as standard-of-
care [1, 54].
Doxycycline is an affordable and generally well-tol-
erated tetracycline antibiotic, although it may cause 
side effects, including gastrointestinal symptoms, tooth 
discoloration, candidiasis, photosensitivity reactions, and 
headaches. It has been found to eliminate amyloid deposits 
by reducing the deposition of fibrillar TTR when paired 
with the biliary acid tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA), 
which concurrently decreases nonfibrillar TTR deposition. 
The combined administration of these medications exhib-
ited synergistic effects in eliminating amyloid deposits in 
transgenic TTR mouse models [55]. Ursodeoxycholic acid 
(UDCA) demonstrates efficacy comparable to TUDCA [56].
Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), the most abundant 
catechin found in green tea, has some stabilization effect 
on TTR but may also promote amyloid degradation by 
converting existing fibrils into more soluble nonfibril con-
formers in vitro [57, 58].

Uninvestigated treatment strategies for ATTR-CM
Seeding inhibitors
Amyloid seeds are small fibril fragments that induce amy-
loid formation by promoting the adoption of β-sheet-rich 
structures, facilitating fibrillogenesis and spread of amyloid 
deposits. The pathological cascade may begin even years 
before symptom onset; therefore, seeds in tissues may 
serve as early biomarkers for disease stages preceding 
symptoms [67].
TabFH2 is a promising seeding inhibitor therapy de -
signed to block amyloid aggregation by targeting TTR’s 
amyloidogenic segment (F and H β-strands). In vitro studies 
demonstrate its dose-dependent inhibition of TTR aggre-
gation, with complete inhibition at higher doses. It effec -
tively inhibits amyloid formation by both wild-type and 
mutant TTR seeds [68]. In Drosophila models, TabFH2 im-
proves motor parameters and reduces TTR deposition [69]. 
Further research on ATTR-CM is warranted.
Molecular tweezers
Molecular tweezers  are biomimetic receptors inspired 
by the lock-and-key model of enzyme-substrate interac -
tion. CLR01 has been found to hinder the self-assembly and 
toxicity of various amyloidogenic proteins, including TTR, in 
laboratory settings. This inhibition occurs at an early stage 
by disrupting hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions 
that are crucial for the aggregation process. Immuno -
histochemical and Western blot analyses demonstrated 
decreased TTR accumulation in the gastrointestinal tract 
and peripheral nervous system of CLR01-treated mice, 
along with reduced endoplasmic reticulum stress response, 
protein oxidation, and apoptosis. These results suggest 
that CLR01 may be a promising candidate for developing 
disease-modifying therapy for TTR amyloidosis [70, 71].

ATTR-CM treatment poses a significant financial burden 
for many patients due to the high costs associated with 
novel therapies. For instance, vutrisiran and tafamidis are 
priced at approximately $450 000 and $225 000 annually, 
respectively. These costs exceed the typical cost-effective-
ness threshold of $100 000 per quality-adjusted life-year. 
Although actual costs may vary depending on location, 
insurance coverage, and patient assistance programs, the 
overall expense remains a significant barrier to access for 
many patients. As awareness and diagnosis of ATTR-CM 
increase and new therapies continue to evolve, there is 
hope that treatment costs will decrease over time [33].
CONCLUSION
Treatment for ATTR-CM has undergone a remarkable trans-
formation with the advent of targeted therapies. Tafamidis, 
a TTR stabilizer, remains the cornerstone of treatment, 
slowing disease progression by preventing TTR dissociation 
and subsequent amyloid fibril formation. However, disease 
progression can still occur despite its efficacy, underscoring 
the need for additional therapeutic options.
Acoramidis, a next-generation TTR stabilizer, has shown 
promising results in the ATTRibute-CM trial. It offers en-
hanced tetramer stabilization, which has the potential to 
improve clinical outcomes further.
Similarly, RNA silencing therapies, such as vutrisiran, 
have emerged as promising alternatives for early-stage 
patients. The HELIOS-B trial has highlighted their poten-
tial to slow disease progression significantly. ASO, such 
as eplontersen, also show potential in the ongoing CAR-
DIO-TTRANSform trial.
At the forefront of innovation, gene-editing tech-
nologies like CRISPR-Cas9, such as NTLA-2001, offer the 
potential for one-time curative interventions by correcting 
the underlying genetic defect. While still in experimental 
phases, these state-of-the-art approaches could revolu -
tionize disease management and provide lasting solutions 
for patients.
For patients in advanced stages, monoclonal antibodies 
targeting amyloid deposits, such as NI006/ALXN2220 (De-
pleTTR-CM trial) and NNC6019/PRX004, are being de -
veloped to meet their unique needs. One of the most 
promising advancements in anti-amyloid therapy is AT-02, 
a groundbreaking pan-amyloid removal technology.
Innovative therapies, such as seeding inhibitors and 
molecular tweezers, further expand the arsenal against 
amyloid aggregation. 
These emerging treatments are designed to deliver 
improved efficacy and robust safety profiles, making them 
suitable for a broad range of patients. However, the high 
cost of these therapies remains a substantial barrier to 
widespread adoption. Affordable nutraceuticals, such as 
green tea, curcumin, and genistein, may offer supportive 
benefits, enhancing the overall care strategy.
A personalized approach is essential for optimizing 
outcomes in ATTR-CM. For early-stage disease, stabilizers 
such as tafamidis or acoramidis, along with RNA-silencing 
therapies, such as vutrisiran and eplontersen, are highly 
effective. In advanced disease, amyloid-clearing agents 
provide hope by targeting the substantial amyloid burden.
