# Novel SQSTM1 (c.838G>T) Mutation Identified in Two Unrelated Cases of Cerebellar Ataxia and Gaze Palsy.
**Authors:** Hosseini Moshkenani N, Momeni A, Bahreini A
**Journal:** Adv Biomed Res (2025)
**DOI:** [10.4103/abr.abr_308_24](https://doi.org/10.4103/abr.abr_308_24)

## Content

Mutations in the SQSTM1 gene have recently been identified as rare causes of progressive childhood neurodegenerative diseases. To date, only 27 cases have been reported, with 11 different mutations affecting SQSTM1. Here, we describe two new cases of progressive childhood-onset cerebellar ataxia with gaze palsy, in whom clinical exome sequencing identified a novel pathogenic variant. The first patient was a 12-year-old boy of Iraqi and Iranian descent, who presented with a 3-year history of recurrent falls, swaying while walking, and difficulties with writing and speech. Physical examination revealed frozen eye movements, gait ataxia, and dysarthria. The second patient was a 9-year-old Iranian boy, who developed involuntary movements, gait instability, and tremor at the age of 8 years. On examination, cerebellar ataxia and dysarthria were observed. In both cases, initial workup was unremarkable, but whole-exome sequencing revealed a novel homozygous pathogenic truncating mutation, SQSTM1 (c.838G>T, p. Glu280Ter). During 2 years of follow-up, the second patient developed gaze palsy. This report highlights the importance of considering SQSTM1 mutations in the differential diagnosis of patients presenting with both cerebellar ataxia and ophthalmological manifestations.

SQSTM1, also recognized as p62, is a protein with multiple domains that participates in the various cellular functions such as intracellular signaling, response to oxidative stress, and apoptosis.[1]

Heterozygous mutations in SQSTM1 have been linked to Paget disease of bone,[23] amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and frontotemporal dementia.[45] More recently, biallelic inactivating mutations in SQSTM1 have been identified as the cause of a pediatric neurodegenerative disorder characterized by ataxia, dystonia, gaze palsy, and cognitive impairment.[6] Here, we report two novel cases of SQSTM1 mutations from Iran and Iraq, further expanding the spectrum of mutations linked to this rare disorder. Whole-exome sequencing was performed to identify SQSTM1 mutations in both probands.

A 12-year-old boy of Iraqi and Iranian descent presented to our hospital with a 3-year history of recurrent falls and swaying, while walking. He had also experienced difficulties with writing and speech since the age of nine. He was born to consanguineous parents, and both his pregnancy and neonatal period were uneventful. His early developmental milestones were normal, and he achieved independent walking at 13 months. There was no family history of similar neurological disorders. His physical measurements were within the normal range based on World Health Organization standards.

Neurological examination revealed oculomotor apraxia, with difficulty moving his eyes in all directions (frozen eye). However, vision and fundoscopy findings were normal. He exhibited slow movements, gait ataxia (clumsy gait), dysarthria, and mild rigidity in the upper limbs. Deep tendon reflexes were exaggerated, with bilateral extensor plantar responses. Additional findings included dysmetria on the finger-to-nose test and an abnormal tandem gait. Notably, myoclonus, chorea, dystonia, and telangiectasia were absent.

Extensive laboratory investigations, including hematological parameters, renal and liver function tests, serum albumin, thyroid function, cholesterol, triglycerides, alpha-fetoprotein, copper, and ceruloplasmin levels, were all within normal limits. Brain and spinal MRI, EEG, and nerve conduction studies showed no abnormalities.

Given the patient’s clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and imaging results, whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed due to the suspicion of an underlying neurodegenerative disease. WES identified a homozygous pathogenic truncating mutation, p.Glu280Ter (c.838G>T), in the SQSTM1 gene, which is associated with childhood-onset neurodegeneration with ataxia, dystonia, and gaze palsy, inherited in an autosomal recessive manner.

Our department treated a 9-year-old Iranian boy for a movement disorder. His parents were consanguineous. He had normal psychomotor development and achieved independent walking at 14 months. However, at the age of 8 years, he began experiencing involuntary movements, gait instability, head tremors, lip smacking, periodic limb movements during sleep, and intention tremors in his hands.

His height was at the 25th percentile, his weight at the 5th percentile, and his head circumference was within the normal range for his age according to World Health Organization standards.

Clinical examination revealed dysarthria, cerebellar signs with an unsteady gait, dysmetria on the finger-to-nose test, and an abnormal tandem gait. Deep tendon reflexes were normal, and no signs of telangiectasia were present.

Extensive laboratory testing, including hematological parameters, renal and liver function tests, serum albumin, ANA, anti-dsDNA, thyroid function, cholesterol, triglycerides, alpha-fetoprotein, copper, and ceruloplasmin levels, yielded unremarkable results. Brain MRI, EEG, and vision assessments showed no abnormalities.

WES was performed, revealing a homozygous mutation in the SQSTM1 gene (c.838G>T), identical to that found in patient 1.

The patient was initially misdiagnosed with seizures and treated with levetiracetam (250 mg BID) and sodium valproate (300 mg BID), but these medications were ineffective. Due to his sleep disturbances, clonazepam (0.25 mg at night) was prescribed. After confirmation of a neurodegenerative disease, he was treated with coenzyme Q10 (300 mg daily) and vitamin E (every other day).

One year later, he developed gaze palsy with impaired eye movements in all directions. After 2 years, his dysarthria worsened, and his ataxia progressed, though he remained capable of independent walking.

We report two unrelated children with cerebellar ataxia and gaze palsy, both harboring the same homozygous truncating mutation (c.838G>T, p.Glu280Ter) in the SQSTM1 gene. SQSTM1 encodes the p62 protein, which plays a crucial role in various cellular processes, including oxidative stress response, apoptosis, and cell differentiation.[178] Mutations in SQSTM1 are strongly associated with neurodegenerative disorders, as they disrupt autophagy, the ubiquitin-proteasome system, and mitophagy.[79] Further studies in zebrafish models have shown that loss of SQSTM1 function leads to impaired mitochondrial activity, defective clearance of misfolded proteins, and their accumulation, ultimately compromising the structural integrity of the cerebellum and causing atrophy. These findings suggest that SQSTM1 mutations contribute to autophagy defects, impaired protein degradation, and disrupted energy metabolism, all of which drive neurodegeneration.[10]

This childhood neurodegenerative disorder caused by SQSTM1 mutations is rare, with only 27 cases reported to date. These cases have been identified across various regions, including Iran (12 cases), other Asian countries (6 cases), Europe (6 cases), North America (Mexico, 2 cases), and Africa (Tunisia, 1 case).[6710111213] The average age of onset in previously reported cases was 9 years, with a range of 6 to 15 years. The two cases described in our study had onset at 9 and 8 years, respectively, which is consistent with previous reports.

Among the symptoms observed in prior cases, cerebellar ataxia and dysarthria were present in all affected individuals, while cognitive impairment and ophtalmoparesiswere reported in 24 patients, aligning with the clinical presentation of our cases. Additional symptoms previously described include dystonia, choreoathetoid movements, dyskinesia, hearing loss, and hypogonadotropic hypogonadism,[671113] none of which were observed in our patients. Additionally, two patients in previous reports exhibited dysautonomia and other non-motor symptoms, which were also absent in our study.[6]

Brain MRI findings in prior cases revealed cerebellar atrophy in six patients and basal ganglia signal abnormalities with iron accumulation in two patients.[11] However, imaging findings in our patients were normal.

We identified a novel pathogenic variant that has not been previously reported. WES confirmed the presence of a pathogenic truncating mutation in SQSTM1 (NM_003900.5), c.838G>T (p.Glu280Ter), in a homozygous state in both patients. This mutation introduces an early stop codon, likely disrupting the SQSTM1 protein structure through truncation and resulting in a loss of autophagy regulation. To assess the pathogenicity of this mutation, several functional assays are recommended, including protein expression analysis (e.g., Western blot), autophagy function tests (such as the LC3B puncta assay), and the development of knock-in mouse models.

A co-segregation study is a crucial component of genetic research, as it tracks the inheritance pattern of a mutant allele within a family. However, the parents of the described cases did not consent to segregation analysis. Nevertheless, given the parental consanguinity, the homozygous state of the mutation in both patients, and the absence of neurodegenerative diseases in their parents, we conclude that this mutation follows an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern, like other homozygous SQSTM1 gene mutations associated with neurodegeneration, dystonia, ataxia, and gaze palsy.

SQSTM1 mutations are a rare cause of neurodegenerative diseases, but they should be considered in children presenting with both cerebellar ataxia and ophthalmological manifestations. This study underscores the importance of whole-exome sequencing in diagnosing rare neurodegenerative disorders. Identifying new SQSTM1 variants can enhance our understanding of genotype-phenotype correlations, given the diverse clinical presentations observed in affected individuals.
