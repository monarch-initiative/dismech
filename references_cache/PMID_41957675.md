# Meningeal and infiltrating T-cells are associated with repetitive head trauma and tau-mediated neurodegeneration in chronic traumatic encephalopathy.
**Authors:** Calderazzo SM, Butler MLMD, Breen K, Kanner H, Tripodis Y, Stein TD, Holtzman DM, Cherry JD, Huber BR, McKee AC
**Journal:** Acta Neuropathol Commun (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s40478-026-02285-z](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40478-026-02285-z)

## Content

Central nervous system (CNS) associated T-cells are present in the meninges and perivascular spaces of healthy brain tissue, but their recruitment into the brain parenchyma is increased by inflammation and hyperphosphorylated tau (p-tau) accumulation. Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) is a progressive tauopathy associated with exposure to repetitive head impacts (RHI) and definitively diagnosed by the presence of a pathognomonic perivascular p-tau lesion, most commonly at the sulcal depths of the dorsolateral frontal cortex (DLF). Exposure to RHI and CTE is associated with substantial neuroinflammation; however, the involvement of T cells is unknown. Here, we used post-mortem human brain tissue to assess T-cell accumulation in the DLF of 58 individuals exposed to RHI, including 19 with neuropathologically verified Low CTE (stage I-II), 23 with neuropathologically verified High CTE (stage III-IV), and 16 without CTE, as well as 18 controls unexposed to RHI and without CTE. Multiplex immunofluorescence was utilized to label T-cells, microglia, p-tau, and synapses in the leptomeninges, sulcal gray matter, crest gray matter, and white matter. We found that infiltrating T-cells were significantly increased in the sulci across all groups compared to controls, with distinct subtypes in RHI without CTE, compared to Low or High CTE. In addition, T-cell infiltration correlated with the duration of RHI, as measured by years of sports play, and synaptic loss. Meningeal and infiltrating T-cells were elevated in sulci with p-tau depositions and spatially related to MHC2 expressing cells. Meningeal T-cells were also significantly correlated with a younger onset of behavioral symptoms. These data suggest that T-cells may play a role in the chronic inflammation and degeneration associated with RHI and CTE.

Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) is a progressive neurodegenerative tauopathy associated with exposure to repetitive head impacts (RHI) such as those sustained through contact sports, military combat, or interpersonal violence [1,2,3]. CTE presents clinically as Traumatic Encephalopathy Syndrome (TES), which can include cognitive, behavioral, and motor symptoms, most notably explosivity, anxiety, suicidal ideation, and progressive dementia. CTE has been diagnosed in contact sport athletes, including boxers, football, ice hockey, rugby and soccer players, in victims of interpersonal violence, and after military blast or impact injury in individuals as young as seventeen [3]. However, CTE cannot be diagnosed during life and there are no disease-modifying treatments. The neuropathological diagnosis of CTE is defined by the presence of perivascular lesions consisting of hyperphosphorylated tau (p-tau) in neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) and neurites at the depths of cortical sulci (CTE lesions) [3]. In early or low-stage CTE (McKee stages I and II), p-tau CTE lesions are restricted to the cortex and most commonly involve the dorsolateral frontal (DLF) [4]. As CTE pathology progresses (high stage CTE, McKee stages III and IV), p-tau NFTs accumulate diffusely in the medial temporal lobe, diencephalon and brainstem, associated with profound neuronal and white matter loss.

The duration of exposure to RHI, measured by the number of years of participation in sports, is the primary risk factor for CTE. The sites of initial p-tau NFT accumulation (perivascular regions at the cortical sulcal depths) likely reflect the areas of greatest tissue distortion and strain during head impact injury. CTE pathology progresses with age, neuroinflammation, and the duration of RHI exposure, but the precise pathogenetic mechanisms leading to disease progression remain unclear. Previous studies demonstrate that phagocytic microglia increase after RHI and continue to rise with the progression of CTE severity [5]. Bulk gene expression analyses show differentially regulated neuroimmune and neuroinflammatory responses in low-stage CTE compared to high-stage CTE [6]. There is also evidence that a distinctive array of cytokines and neuroimmune proteins are upregulated in CTE compared to other tauopathies [7], including the inflammatory factor CCL21, an important regulator of T-cell recruitment. Yet, the involvement of adaptive immune cells in CTE has not been investigated.

As the brain-resident macrophage, microglia are the prime mediators of neuroinflammation, but there is evidence of both innate and adaptive immune dysregulation in neurodegenerative disease [8,9,10,11]. T-cells are part of the adaptive immune system including the two classical subtypes, CD4 helper cells and CD8 cytotoxic cells, which interact with macrophages and other immune cells to induce distinct cytokine responses. Under normal conditions central nervous system (CNS) T-cells are found in the meninges and perivascular spaces of the brain [12,13] and can influence neuronal and microglia activity [14,15,16]. During chronic inflammation, meningeal lymphocytes can also impact cortical pathologies such as in multiple sclerosis where meningeal T-cells are associated with larger areas of demyelination and more severe clinical symptoms [17,18]. Moreover, activated innate immune cells can recruit T-cells into the brain parenchyma [19,20,21]. In mouse models of tauopathy and in human high-stage Alzheimer's disease, CNS T-cells are associated with p-tau accumulation [22,23,24,25,26]. In addition, murine studies show that T-cells are driven into the brain by microglia-mediated signals [19,22] and mediate neuronal dysfunction, cognitive decline, and atrophy [22,23,27].

Here, we used post-mortem human brain tissue to determine whether the distribution and quantity of T-cells are altered in association with RHI exposure or CTE and to assess the relationship between T-cells, microglia, and markers of neurodegeneration. We examined the DLF cortex (Supplemental Figure S1A), a cortical region that is vulnerable to early CTE lesions [1,2,3], across four groups: RHI exposure with low CTE (McKee stages I-II), RHI exposure with high CTE (McKee stages III-IV), RHI exposure without CTE, and controls without RHI exposure or CTE. We measured the number of meningeal and infiltrating T-cells and assessed their spatial proximity to Iba1 + and MHC2 + cells. We also determined whether more T-cells accumulated around p-tau deposition or were associated with synaptic loss and clinical symptoms.

Consent for brain donation and research participation were provided by the donor's next of kin. Institutional review boards from Boston University and VA Bedford Healthcare System approved brain donation, postmortem clinical record review, neuropathological evaluation, and clinical interviews with donor family members.

Cases were selected from the Boston University Understanding Neurological Injury and Traumatic Encephalopathy (UNITE) and VA Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder brain banks; Brain tissue was collected and processed using identical methods for all [28]. Briefly, one hemisphere is fixed in periodate-lysine-paraformaldehyde (PLP) for at least 3 weeks before the brain is blocked into specific regions, and the blocks are embedded in paraffin for preparation into slides for comprehensive analysis. The pathological evaluation and diagnosis occur without knowledge of the subject’s history. A neuropathologic diagnosis of CTE was made using the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS)/National Institute of Biomedical Imaging and Bioengineering (NIBIB) consensus criteria [1,2]. Individuals with CTE were diagnosed based on the presence, extent, and severity of p-tau deposition through the brain. All evaluations were reviewed by three neuropathologists (BH, TDS, and ACM); a consensus conference resolved any discrepancies in diagnosis. Well-established pathological criteria were used for the diagnosis of all neurodegenerative diseases, including motor neuron disease, Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson’s disease and Lewy body disease, Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration (including progressive supranuclear palsy, corticobasal degeneration, and Pick’s disease) and multiple system atrophy. Exclusion criteria included any significant co-morbid neurodegenerative disease, CNS neoplasm, severe vascular pathology (atherosclerosis or arteriosclerosis), systemic or neurological inflammatory disease, acute head trauma, or post-mortem interval (PMI) > 72 h.

Retrospective clinical evaluation was obtained on each brain donor using a combination of online surveys and telephone calls between researchers, family members, and close friends of the subject as well as medical records. Data were collected through an unstructured interview with a behavioral neurologist, neuropsychologist, or trained research assistant. We chose symptoms across the clinical domains of cognitive, behavioral, and mood commonly associated with CTE including memory loss, explosive behavior, and anxiety. We also included the age of onset of cognitive and behavioral symptoms. Onset of mood symptoms was not reliably reported in our cohort so we chose to exclude it. History of RHI exposure was based on retrospective clinical questionnaires with family and verified with public record databases if the individual played professionally. During a telephone interview, demographics, athletic history (type of sports played, level, position, age of first exposure to sports, and years playing contact sports), military history (branch, location of service, and duration of combat exposure), and traumatic brain injury history (including the number of concussions) were queried.

Seventy-six cases were selected, encompassing 4 groups: 1. Controls with no neuropathological disease and no exposure to RHI (N = 18; Controls), 2. Donors exposed to RHI with no neuropathological disease (N = 16, RHI), 3. Individuals exposed to RHI diagnosed with low CTE (McKee stages I-II) but no other neuropathological disease (N = 19, Low CTE) 4. Individuals exposed to RHI diagnosed with high CTE (McKee stages III-IV) but no other neuropathological disease (N = 23, High CTE). All brain donors were male, aged 22–85 years (mean age 59.08 years, +/− 1.91) (Table1). Most donors were Caucasian, with football as their primary RHI exposure. Four veterans who were exposed to combat and blast injuries were included. The cause of death, mean age, and post-mortem interval (PMI) are listed in Table1.

Paraffin-embedded DLF tissue was cut at a thickness of 10 µm and deparaffinized in a series of xylene and ethanol baths with a 30-min endogenous peroxidase block. All steps were performed at room temperature for the immunofluorescence assay unless stated otherwise. Heat-induced epitope retrieval was performed for each antigen with a BioWave microwave (BioWave Corporation, Norwalk, CT). Subsequently, nonspecific binding was blocked using 3% donkey serum in 0.1% Triton-X PBS. Primary antibody incubation was performed for 1 h at room temperature or overnight at 4 degrees (CD8 1:20 (Dako), CD4 1:100 (Abcam), CD31 1:200 (Abcam), iba1 1:500 (Wako), MHC2 1:500 (Epredia), AT8 1:500 (Invitrogen), PSD95 1:100 (Cell Signaling), VGlut 1:1200 (Millipore), Gephyrin 1:200 (BD Bioscience), VGAT 1:1500 (Synaptic Systems)). Secondary antibodies were incubated for 30 min (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA). Opal dyes 520, 570, 620, 690, or 780 (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA) were applied for 10 min. If another antibody was applied, the slides were placed in the appropriate solution for antigen retrieval, and the previous steps were repeated. After all markers had been labeled, DAPI solution (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA) was applied for 5 min before slides were washed and cover slipped with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mounting Media (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and sealed with clear nail polish (Sally Hansen, New York, NY).

All cases were stained for T-cells, microglia, and p-tau (Supplemental Figure S2-4). A subset of cases (n = 69) with available adjacent tissue sections were also stained for synaptic markers (Supplemental Figure S5).

Slides staining T-cell, microglia, and p-tau were fully imaged in 2D scans using a Perkin Elmer Vectra Polaris slide scanner (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA) at 20 × magnification. Immunofluorescent images were spectrally unmixed through Phenochart and InForm Image Analysis software (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA) to remove autofluorescence. Synaptic slides were sampled with 3D z-stacks on a high-resolution Airyscan confocal microscope (Zeiss 880 Confocal, White Planes, NY) at 60 × objective. Images were deconvolved with Airyscan processing and spectrally unmixed via Zen Blue 3.11 (Zeiss White Planes, NY). HALO (Indica Labs, Albuquerque, NM) software was used to quantify cell densities, percent area, and proximity analyses for T-cells, microglia, and p-tau staining. Halo spatial algorithms were used to label ROIs as positive or negative for p-tau staining and create objects of Iba1 +, MHC2 +, CD8 +, or CD4 + cells. Filters were then applied to detect cellular objects within specific distances of each other or within ROI subsets. Imaris 9.3 (Oxford Instruments, Zurich, Switzerland) software was used to measure synaptic densities on 3D high-resolution images. Four ROIs of the DLF were annotated: leptomeninges, gray matter at the depth of the sulcus (Sulcus), gray matter at the gyral crest (Crest), and white matter (WM) (Supplemental Fig. S1A). The sulcus was defined as the gray matter in the bottom third of the sulcus. The gyral crest was defined as the gray matter in the top third of the gyrus. Finally, we only analyzed meningeal tissue that was present within sulci.

When assessing the total number of T-cells results were reported as a cell density. T-cells in the cortex were further classified as parenchymal (within the cortical tissue) or perivascular (within the glymphatic space) [29,30,31,32]. Due to T-cells being a relatively rare cell type in the brain with low cortical numbers we measured infiltrating T-cells by counting the percentage of total cortical T-cells within the parenchyma, defined as at least 30um away from a CD31 + endothelium (Supplemental Fig. S1B). The distance of 30um was chosen based on the average width of the glymphatic space in the samples.

Spatial associations between T-cells and microglia were determined by counting the number of T-cells within 25um of an Iba1 or MHC2 positive cell. 25um was chosen based on the average length of Iba1 + processes in our samples. Process length was measured from the center of the soma (Supplemental Figure S1C). T-cells within 25 µm of an Iba1 + soma were considered close enough to be in contact with the adjacent cell. Iba1 is a pan-microglia marker that can also label macrophages in the CNS. MHC2 is a marker of microglia reactivity that can also label macrophages as well as dendritic cells. For this reason, we can’t definitively confirm which cells are microglia and will refer to them as Iba1 + or MHC2 + cells. However, based on previous studies in our lab, a large majority of the cells labeled by Iba1 are microglia [33] and a small number of CNS dendritic cells typically reside in the meninges. Although dendritic cells can infiltrate the cortex during inflammation, based on our staining most MHC2 + cells are co-labeled with Iba1 indicating they are largely labeling microglia and macrophage cell types (Supplemental Fig. S4).

AT8 staining was quantitatively assessed by measuring the density of p-tau + neurons and the percent area of AT8 staining. Due to the focal nature of p-tau CTE lesions, spatial relationships between T-cells, MHC2, and AT8 were assessed by measuring the number of T-cells or MHC2 expressing cells in p-tau positive sulci compared to those in p-tau negative sulci.

Synapses were counted in a 3D space as pairs of pre- and post-synaptic markers (Supplemental Fig. S1D and D5) [34]. Excitatory synapses were defined using PSD95 and VGLUT, while inhibitory synapses were defined using Gephyrin (Geph) and VGAT.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). To compare the means between groups, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA, F-test) using age as a covariate was performed and followed by post-hoc Bonferroni correction. Associations between T-cell counts (Parenchymal CD8, Parenchymal CD4, Meningeal CD8, Meningeal CD4) with macrophage markers (Iba1 density, MHC2 density), p-tau burden (AT8 density, AT8 percent area), synaptic densities (Excitatory, Inhibitory), duration of RHI exposure (football years), or age of symptom onset (cognitive, behavioral) were determined by multivariate linear correlations with age as a covariate reported as standardized correlation coefficients (r). Associations between T-cell counts and presence of clinical symptoms (memory loss, explosivity, anxiety) was assessed with binary logistic regression with age as a covariate reported as beta coefficients (B).

Since T-cells were spatially related to MHC2 + cells, we explored whether p-tau was also associated with MHC2 expression and found significantly more MHC2 + cells in p-tau positive sulci compared to p-tau negative sulci (F = 4.126, p = 0.046) and a trend toward increased MHC2 percent area (F = 3.785, p = 0.055).

We next assessed whether CNS T-cells were associated with clinical symptoms of CTE including memory impairment, explosive behavior, and anxiety. We found no significant correlation between T-cells and the presence of clinical symptoms (Sup Table S6). We also compared T-cells with the age of onset of cognitive or behavioral symptoms. We found meningeal CD4 T-cells (R = − 0.240, p = 0.029) were significantly associated with a younger onset of behavioral symptoms.

CTE is a neurodegenerative tauopathy caused by exposure to RHI that largely impacts cortical sulci exposed to high sheering stress during impact injury [14,35]. In low-stage CTE, p-tau pathology is focal and restricted to isolated sulcal depths of the frontal cortex. CTE severity typically progresses to become a diffuse p-tau pathology involving widespread regions of the medial temporal lobe, diencephalon, and brainstem in high stage disease. The cascade of events that promote p-tau deposition after RHI are unclear but is likely initiated by focal tissue distortion and strain during impact injury. The continued progression of p-tau pathology long after the RHI exposure ends suggests that other factors, such as neuroinflammation, play a role in progressive neurodegeneration. Our previous studies show that RHI induces microglial and vascular inflammation that further increases in low and high stage CTE [5,36,37]. Under normal conditions, CNS T-cells typically reside in meninges or perivascular spaces but can further aggregate and infiltrate into the parenchyma when activated in response to inflammation [19,20,21,38] and have been shown to enter the WM following repetitive mild traumatic brain injury (rmTBI) in mouse models [38]. In this study, we found that infiltrating CD8 and CD4 T-cells increased at the sulcal depths in individuals exposed to RHI, and in low- and high-stage CTE, while meningeal T-cells were elevated in high-stage CTE. Overall, T-cells were spatially proximal to microglia and p-tau pathology, and significantly correlated with synaptic loss, p-tau accumulation, and RHI duration. These findings suggest CNS T-cells likely contribute to chronic neuroinflammation in RHI and CTE.

In this study, sulcal CD8 T-cell infiltration was increased in RHI and CTE, predominantly in p-tau + sulci, while CD4 T-cell infiltration was only elevated in RHI without CTE. In the context of CNS viral infections, CD4 T-cells are activated first to help mount inflammatory responses and trigger initial CD8 cytotoxic T-cells [39,40]. Our data indicates that CD4 T-cells may be a part of the cortical injury response in RHI but has less of a role in CTE. However, the CD8 subtype is involved in both RHI and CTE inflammation. Moreover, CD8 and CD4 T cells express unique arrays of cytokines that promote or inhibit differing immune pathways [41]. The differences in infiltrating T-cell subtypes in RHI versus CTE may also denote independent inflammatory pathways occurring at different stages after injury, which is consistent with our previous studies showing distinct signaling pathways across RHI and CTE [6,42]. Notably, as CD8 T-cells are primed to deal with intracellular threats they could serve an important function within the cortex by sensing intracellular stress from p-tau accumulating neurons in CTE. Further analysis of T-cell phenotypes is needed to determine distinct immune responses that may advance tauopathy and neurodegeneration.

Additionally, we observed increased CD8 and CD4 T-cells in the white matter of the RHI and High CTE groups, which is consistent with a previous study on rmTBI in mouse models where T-cells were found in in the WM 1 month after injury [38]. RHI is known to cause axonal injury, and CTE is associated with white matter rarefaction and inflammation [3]. Thus, the infiltration of T-cells into the white matter in RHI may occur in response to the axonal injury. Further inflammation and white matter damage in high CTE may reactivate this response.

Previous studies have shown T-cells can independently mediate neurodegeneration in a human AD culture model [43]. However, when cocultured with microglia they acted synergistically to exacerbate degeneration and were spatially colocalized with microglia adjacent to damaged neurons [43].

This study found that CNS T-cells were significantly correlated with and proximal to Iba1 + cells. There were also significantly more T-cells spatially related to Iba1 + and MHC2 + cells compared to DAPI, suggesting a specific interaction between these cell types. As stated, previous studies in our lab report a large majority of Iba1 + cells are microglia [33] and most MHC2 + cells in our samples are co-labeled with Iba1 indicating microglia and macrophage cell types. Of note many of the Iba1 + cells associated with T-cells expressed MHC2, an antigen presenting receptor that microglia upregulate in response to inflammation. As shown in mouse models, T-cell infiltration can occur after the upregulation of MHC2 in microglia [44] and CNS T-cells also further promote MHC2 expression in macrophages [40,45]. Hence MHC2 + cells represent a generally reactive phenotype that could recruit or interact with T-cells. Here, T-cells adjacent to MHC2 + microglia suggest possible communication between these cell types in the context of RHI and CTE, but further studies of upregulated pathways is needed to fully evaluate T cell and microglia interactions.

Synaptic loss is an early event in many neurodegenerative diseases [46]. Neurodegeneration within the sulcus is also noted in CTE individuals including neuron loss and decreased synaptic proteins [35]. Here, we assessed synaptic density in adjacent tissue sections to determine if T-cells are associated with early CTE neurodegeneration. We found the number of infiltrating T-cells correlated with sulcal synaptic loss, the area most susceptible to impact injury. In other studies of CNS infections, IFNy secreted by T-cells was shown to stimulate excessive microglia-mediated synaptic pruning [15]. Infiltrating T-cells could encourage similar synaptic pruning processes in CTE and further study in their cytokine signaling is warranted.

CNS T-cells have been reported in AD brain tissue [22,24,26,27,47,48] and patrolling the CSF of AD patients [11,49] with specific correlations to p-tau but not a-beta suggesting a role for adaptive immunity in tauopathies. In mouse models of neurodegenerative disease, T-cells recruited by microglia are located near p-tau pathology promoting glial inflammation [22,50], synaptic dysfunction [27], and neuronal loss [22]. However, p-tau-mediated atrophy was ameliorated by blocking T-cells [22]. Whether similar processes also occur in human neurodegeneration is unknown. In this study, cortical and meningeal T-cells accumulated in sulci with p-tau pathology. Moreover, T-cell infiltration was also noted in RHI-exposed individuals without CTE who have no to minimal p-tau aggregates. This suggests cortical T-cells could be involved in RHI triggered inflammation and remain elevated in CTE possibly promoting or responding to p-tau deposition. As defined in multiple immune-mediated disorders, compartmentalized inflammation promotes sustained chronic tissue damage. Such as in multiple sclerosis where many studies have shown organized lymphoid aggregates along the borders of ventricles and meningeal spaces play a significant role in disease progression as they are associated with larger demyelinating lesions and more severe symptoms [51,52]. Here we found meningeal T-cells were spatially related and significantly correlated with p-tau deposits in the sulcus. Therefore, in CTE, meningeal T-cell clusters may release inflammatory signals that stimulate focal cortical inflammation associated with p-tau formation. Specifically, meningeal CD4 T-cells were correlated with p-tau and earlier onset of behavioral symptoms suggesting CD4 mediated meningeal inflammation may be a key factor of CTE. Future examination of T-cell signaling in CTE could influence future biomarker and therapy options.

There are some limitations to this study. High-stage CTE is typically diagnosed in older individuals, whereas low-stage CTE often affects younger individuals. While the four groups were age-matched, all groups had a large overlapping age range, and the mean age of the control group did not significantly differ from the other groups (RHI: p = 1.0, Low: p = 1.0, High: p = 0.54). Furthermore, age was controlled for in all statistical models. Additionally, all data was assessed using 10um tissue sections. This prevents us from knowing what cellular structures were directly above or below our sampled area therefore our spatial classifications are estimates of the larger tissue area. As mentioned synaptic densities were counted in adjacent Sects. 10um apart from T-cell counts, which prevents proof of any direct spatial coupling, but still allows us to determine sufficient associations. While this study focused on specific markers of T-cells, macrophages, and synapses, studies evaluating other measures of immunity and neurodegeneration that are associated with T-cell infiltration will be informative.

Our data shows that RHI exposure and CTE increases T-cells in the CNS where they are adjacent to microglia and associated with early synaptic loss, p-tau deposition, and onset of symptoms. Currently, there is no way to diagnose CTE pre-mortem. Further investigation of meningeal and cortical T-cells in RHI and CTE may aid in defining early CSF or radiological biomarkers that can identify individuals susceptible to disease progression or guide therapeutic options.
