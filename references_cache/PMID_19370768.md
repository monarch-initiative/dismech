# Molecular mechanisms of classical Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS).

Molecular mechanisms of classical Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS).

Mitchell AL(1), Schwarze U, Jennings JF, Byers PH.

Author information:
(1)Department of Pediatrics, Division of Genetics, University of Washington, 
Seattle, WA, USA. alm43@case.edu

Classical Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS) is a heritable disorder characterized by 
joint hypermobility, skin hyperextensibility, and abnormal wound healing. The 
majority of affected individuals have alterations in 1 of the 2 type V collagen 
genes, COL5A1 and COL5A2. The most common mechanism is COL5A1 haploinsufficiency 
due to instability of the transcript of one allele. In dermal fibroblasts from 
our population of 76 individuals with clinical features of classical EDS, there 
were 21 (29.5%) with decreased expression of one COL5A1 allele, consistent with 
published estimates of the frequency of null alleles. We identified the 
causative mutation in nine of these cell strains (mutations for seven others had 
been previously described), and found two nonsense mutations, five splice 
mutations, and two insertion/deletions. The same type of genomic change at 
splice sites can have different effects at the RNA level and the outcome could 
not be predicted from the primary genomic DNA alteration.

Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS) is a heterogeneous group of inherited disorders characterized by soft, hyperextensible skin, hypermobile joints, abnormal scarring, and other systemic involvement. EDS is divided into several types, based on the clinical features, mode of inheritance, and the underlying genetic basis of each (Beighton et al., 1998; Byers and Schwarze, 2002; Steinmann et al., 2002). Classical EDS (MIM#s 130000, 130010), formerly known as EDS types I/II, is characterized by skin hyperextensibility, widened atrophic (cigarette paper) scars, and large and small joint hypermobility (Beighton et al., 1998). Usually classical EDS results from mutations in the COL5A1 (MIM# 120215) and COL5A2 genes (MIM# 120190), which encode the proα1(V) chain and proα2(V) chain respectively, of type V collagen. These chains usually combine in a heterotrimer composed of two proα1(V) and a single proα2(V) chain. Multiple mutations have been identified in both genes, including substitutions for glycine residues in the triple helical domains, nonsense mutations, insertions/deletions leading to frameshifts, splice site mutations, and a 9-X translocation that disrupts the COL5A1 gene (Nicholls et al. 1996; Toriello et al., 1996; Wenstrup et al. 1996; De Paepe et al 1997; Burrows et al. 1998; Michalickova et al., 1998; Richards et al., 1998; Malfait et al, 2005). Approximately 25–30% of individuals with classical EDS are haploinsufficient for the COL5A1 mRNA as a consequence of mutations that lead to nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (Schwarze et al., 2000; Wenstrup et al, 2000; Malfait et al., 2005).

Premature stop codons (PTCs) can appear as a consequence of nonsense mutations, insertion/deletions that alter the reading frame, or splice site changes that lead to the use of cryptic splice sites and an altered reading frame. For nonsense mediated mRNA decay (NMD) to occur, the PTC is generally upstream of the last intron and separated from that intron by at least fifty basepairs (Neu-Yilik et al., 2004).

Because COL5A1 haploinsufficiency accounts for a significant fraction of mutations in classical EDS cases, we identified individuals with decreased expression of one allele, identified the causative mutations in a subset of them, and determined how these mutations altered the stability of the resulting mRNA molecules.

We selected 76 study subjects, which included the 16 previously described (Schwarze et al., 2000), from the University of Washington Collagen Diagnostic Laboratory Repository of individuals referred for evaluation for EDS. Subjects were included if they had all three of the major diagnostic criteria for classical EDS as defined by Beighton et al., (1998): hypermobile joints, hyperextensible skin, and characteristic scars or other evidence of poor wound healing. Samples were obtained with appropriate consent and the Human Subjects Division at the University of Washington approved the study.

A punch skin biopsy or cultured dermal fibroblasts were provided for each subject. If a skin biopsy was received, fibroblasts were cultured as described previously (Bonadio et al., 1985). DNA and RNA were isolated using the appropriate Qiagen kit (QIAamp DNA Mini kit; RNeasy Mini kit; Qiagen, Valencia, CA). Complementary DNA was synthesized from the RNA using the Superscript kit (Gibco BRL).

Collagenous proteins were labeled and studied as previously described (Bonadio et al., 1985).

To determine if both COL5A1 alleles were expressed at equal levels, genomic DNA from each cell strain was screened at four common polymorphic sites in the coding sequence and in the 3′ untranslated region (Schwarze et al., 2000). After identification of a heterozygous site, cDNA was PCR-amplified with primers spanning the variant region, and the product was digested with the appropriate enzyme before analysis by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Schwarze et al., 2000). Eight of the samples with complete or partial haploinsufficiency were selected for analysis of genomic DNA to identify the causative mutation. One cell strain in which the four markers were homozygous was also evaluated at the genomic level.

COL5A1 cDNA was PCR-amplified in six overlapping, large fragments (Schwarze et al., 2000) and the products were analyzed on a 7% polyacrylamide gel. PCR products of the expected size were sequenced using the Big Dye terminator method on an ABI310 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems) to identify missense mutations (reference sequence=RefSeq NM_000093.2; numbered with reference to A of initiation ATG as 1). When a novel band was also detected on the gel, the individual exons within that fragment were amplified from genomic DNA.

Sixty-five of the sixty-six exons of the COL5A1 gene were PCR-amplified from genomic DNA in fifty-seven reactions. Sequences of the primers and amplification conditions used are listed in Supp. Table S1. Buffer composition and times for steps of PCR are as noted in attachment 2. PCR products were sequenced using the Big Dye terminator method and the ABI310 (Applied Biosystems).

The COL5A2 cDNA was PCR-amplified in five overlapping fragments (Schwarze et al., 2000). PCR products were sequenced with the Big Dye terminator method on the ABI310 (Applied Biosystems) (reference sequence= RefSeq NM_000393.2; numbered with reference to A of initiation ATG as 1).

Sixty-five of the 66 exons of the COL5A1 gene (exon 1 was omitted) were amplified in twenty multiplex reactions and three single exon amplifications. Multiplex combinations and amplification conditions are listed in Supp. Table S2. Following amplification, thermocycler conditions were set to favor heteroduplex formation. Products were analyzed on mutation enhancement detection (MDE) gels (BioWhittaker Molecular Applications), and visualized after staining with ethidium bromide. Products with heteroduplexes were individually re-amplified and sequenced with the Big Dye terminator method on an ABI310 to identify the source of the alteration.

Skin fibroblasts were grown to confluence in a 100mm dish, harvested, and centrifuged to isolate the nuclear pellet. Nuclei were lysed and RNA collected following the protocol of the RNeasy Minikit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) (nuclear RNA [nRNA] fraction). RNA was also isolated from the cytoplasmic fraction using the same kit (cytoplasmic RNA [cRNA] fraction). These two RNA fractions were separately treated with RNase-free DNase at 37° for 1 hour prior to being used as templates for cDNA synthesis (Superscript, GibcoBRL).

To identify novel products expressed from the mutant allele, cDNA was synthesized using the nuclear and cytoplasmic RNA templates, with primers located in exons spanning the region of the identified mutations (Supp. Table S3). Products were analyzed on 7% polyacrylamide gels stained with ethidium bromide. Novel bands were cut from the gel, re-amplified with the initial amplification primers, purified with the QIAquick-spin columns (Qiagen, Valencia, CA), and sequenced with the Big Dye terminator method on the ABI310 (Applied Biosystems).

To distinguish normal and mutant products that differed from each other by only one or two basepairs, restriction digestion was done (P6 and P7). Either BsrI (P6) or TspRI (P7) was incubated with PCR product at 65° overnight, followed by analysis on 7% polyacrylamide gel. The mutation in P1 did not affect a restriction site, so a new PCR primer (x49mm; 5′-CTTCTCCCGGAAGGCAGACT-3′) was designed to introduce a HinfI site in the normal COL5A1 sequence. The novel restriction site is present only in the product of the normal allele. These PCR products were digested overnight at 37°.

To screen for the c.1588G>A (p.Gly530Ser) variant in COL5A1, genomic DNA was PCR-amplified using primers in introns 11 (in11S: 5′-CTGGCCTGGTTGCACTCTGACTTG-3′) and 13 (in13A: 5′-GGCTGGGCTCTGTCCCCTTCAGG-3′) (denaturation at 95° for 2 minutes, 35 cycles of 95° for 15 sec, 62° for 20 sec, 72° for 30 sec and a final extension at 72° for 5 minutes). PCR products were digested overnight with restriction endonuclease BbvI at 37°, and analyzed on 7% polyacrylamide gel. There are three constitutive BbvI sites within the PCR product at positions 104 (exon 12), 167 and 181 (intron 12). The site created by the variant is at position 325 (exon 13). Samples were screened from individuals with connective tissue disorders: 72 with classical EDS; 94 with vascular EDS; 82 with osteogenesis imperfecta, as well as anonymous control samples: 49 with neurofibromatosis I; 50 being evaluated for fragile X syndrome; and 51 referred for hemochromatosis testing.

Of seventy-six individuals screened for heterozygosity at each of four polymorphic sites in the COL5A1 gene, seventy (92%) were heterozygous at one or more sites. In cells from twenty of the seventy individuals (28.5%), the mRNA product of one COL5A1 allele was absent or decreased. Cells from eight of these individuals were selected for further study to identify the causative mutation (mutations for 7 of the 20 individuals were reported by Schwarze et al., 2000 and Takahara et al., 2002).

For four cell strains (P1, P8, P11, and P12), amplification of one or more of the COL5A1 cDNA fragments produced a faster-migrating band or a heteroduplex, in addition to the expected band (Figure 1). Sequencing of the genomic DNA in the regions that contained the heteroduplexes led to identification of the genomic mutations: c.2844+1G>A (IVS35+1G>A) (P11); c.2845-2A>C (IVS35-2A>C) (P12); c.3874G>A, p.Glu1292Lys in exon 49 (P1); and exon 62 c.4916_4931del16 (P8). Each of these mutations resulted in creation of a frameshift, a downstream PTC and mRNA instability in the product of the mutant allele (see below).

Heteroduplex analysis of COL5A1 genomic DNA followed by sequence determination of the abnormal fragment from the four cell strains (P5, P6, P9, and P10) with haploinsufficiency, but no abnormality detected by cDNA screening identified four additional mutations: exon 4, c.495G>A; p.Trp165X (P5); exon 13, c.1654C>T; p.Glu552X (P6); c.1989+1G>A (IVS19+1G>A) (P9); and c.2386-1G>A (IVS27-1G>A) (P10).

Heteroduplex analysis of genomic DNA from one individual (P7) uninformative for the markers used in the haploinsufficiency screen led to identification of a mutation (c.3680_3681insT in exon 46), which leads to a premature termination codon in exon 47. The mRNA from the mutant allele was rapidly degraded so that virtually no stable product was present in the cytoplasm (Figure 2A; lane 3).

In P9 cells (c.1989+1G>A; IVS19 +1G>A), the mutant allele used a cryptic splice site four nucleotides downstream in intron 19. The inclusion of four nucleotides from intron 19 altered the reading frame, and resulted in a PTC in exon 20, and a message that should be subject to NMD. A small amount of heteroduplex was visible in the cytoplasmic RNA (lane 2, Figure 3A), which could indicate that not all the message was degraded prior to entering the cytoplasm or that there was incomplete separation of the nuclear fraction from the cytoplasm during isolation of the RNA. A +1G>A alteration in another intron, intron 35 (c.2844+1G>A; IVS35+1G>A; P11), resulted in two splice outcomes- use of a cryptic intronic splice site with partial intron inclusion, and exon 35 skip (Figure 3B). The addition of 118 nucleotides of intron 35 altered the reading frame and created a premature termination codon in exon 39. This product is subject to NMD, while the skip of the 45 nt exon 35 does not alter the reading frame and the mRNA is stable (lane 1 vs. lane 2, Figure 3B).

With a splice acceptor mutation in intron 35 (c.2845-2A>C; IVS35–2A>C; P12), there were also two outcomes- use of a cryptic acceptor site within exon 36 so that 19 nucleotides of coding sequence were lost, and skip of exon 36 (lane 1, Figure 3C). Both products are in relatively low abundance suggesting that, because the exon-skip product is stable, the major outcome of the mutation was the use of the cryptic acceptor site which created a PTC in exon 41, leading to NMD.

With the splice acceptor site mutation c.2386-1G>A (IVS27–1G>A; P10), a shift of the splice site one nucleotide downstream resulted in a PTC within exon 28. A heteroduplex, visible only after amplification of the nuclear RNA (lane 1, Figure 3D), was not seen in the cytoplasmic RNA (lane 2, Figure 3D).

Insertion of a single nucleotide altered the reading frame, and resulted in an unstable message, expected to undergo NMD in cells from P7 (c.3680_3681insT). In the nucleus, there are approximately equal amounts of normal and mutant messages (Figure 2A, lane 2). In the cytoplasm, close to 99% of the product is cut by TspRI, a site present only in the product of the normal allele (Figure 2A, lane 3).

Deletion of sixteen nucleotides from exon 62 (P8) led to a frameshift and PTC in exon 65. The message from the abnormal allele was detected in RNA from the nucleus, but not in the cytoplasmic RNA (Figure 2B, lane 1 vs. lane 2). Again, this demonstrates the degree of message degradation.

In cells from P6, there were no novel bands present after amplification of both nuclear and cytoplasmic RNA fractions. Three different primer sets spanning the region of the mutation were tried and in all cases, the only band visible was that of the expected size. Because the mutation creates a BsrI site, the normal allele can be differentiated from the mutant one by restriction enzyme digestion. In nuclear RNA, approximately 40% of the RT-PCR product was cut twice by BsrI, once at a constitutive site and once at a site introduced by the mutation (lane 1, Figure 2C), while the majority of the product from cytoplasmic RNA was cut at only the constitutive site (lane 2, Figure 2C), consistent with loss of the mutant product by NMD.

In cells from P1 (exon 49 c.3874G>A, p.Glu1292Lys) the missense mutation in exon 49 reduced mRNA stability. RT-PCR of nuclear RNA revealed a novel, smaller product (lane 1, Figure 4A), that resulted from use of a new donor splice site in exon 49 formed by the mutation. The loss of thirty-five basepairs from exon 49 created a PTC in exon 50. This product is not detected in RNA from the cytoplasm, indicating that it has all been degraded (lane 2, Figure 4A). The cryptic exon 49 sequence used for splicing is AGGTAAGC (altered nucleotide underlined), which differs from the consensus splice donor site [AGgt(a/g)agt] by a single nucleotide. The Shapiro and Senapathy score (Shapiro and Senapathy, 1987) for the constitutive sequence (CGgtgagt) is 88.5, while that for the cryptic sequence is 94.2, consistent with the preference for the cryptic site by the splicing machinery.

The second product expressed from the mutant allele is identical in length to the product of the normal allele, but differs by one nucleotide (A vs G). Amplification with a mismatched primer, designed to introduce a HinfI site in the normal sequence, resulted in identical amplification products for the patient and a control. The mismatched reverse primer is located in the part of exon 49 that is deleted by the use of the cryptic splice site so there is no amplification of mRNA containing the deletion. After incubation with HinfI, the control samples are cut completely (lanes 3 and 4, Figure 4B), while the patient samples are only partially digested (lanes 1 and 2, Figure 4B).

COL5A1 cDNA was amplified in six overlapping large fragments and sequenced from two of the eight individuals who did not have null alleles in our previous publication (P9 and P15, Schwarze et al, 2000; P3 and P2, respectively, in this study). Missense mutations were identified in both: P2 c.4465G>A, p.Gly1489Arg, Gly931Arg in the triple helix andP3 c.5504G>C, p.Cys1835Ser in the carboxyl-terminal propeptide. This cysteine is the last cysteine of the carboxyl-terminal propeptide. In all fibrillar collagen proα chains, this residue is important for the stabilization of an intramolecular disulfide bond that is necessary for chain association. Substitution for that cysteine usually results in failure of chain incorporation into trimers. These sequence alterations were confirmed in genomic DNA.

We identified an abnormality of type V collagen by protein screening in only one individual (P4). Theα2(V) band in the cell layer proteins was broadened, consistent with overmodification of the protein (Figure 5). The type V collagen produced by cells from the remaining 75 individuals could not be distinguished from those made by control cells.

A missense mutation (c.3437G>C; p.Gly1149Ala; Gly937Ala in triple helix) was identified by sequencing of COL5A2 cDNA from the individual with the protein gel abnormality. The mutation created an EarI site so its presence was confirmed in genomic DNA, both by sequencing of the relevant exon (46) and by restriction digestion of an exon 46 PCR product. The alteration was not present in DNA from either unaffected parent.

Six of the 72 individuals with classical EDS were heterozygous for the exon 13 c.1588G>A (p.Gly530Ser) variant in COL5A1 (6/144 alleles= 4.2%; Table 1). This is similar to the fraction of the variant allele among individuals with vascular EDS (8/188=4.2%), and individuals with OI (9/164=5.5%). Among the control samples, the frequency of the variant allele ranged from 3.1% to 9.8%. Only one homozygote was detected among the 398 individuals evaluated and it was in an anonymous control sample. This variant is not listed in dbSNP, the reason for which is unclear, given these frequencies.

Mutations in the type V collagen genes, COL5A1 and COL5A2, lead to the phenotype of classical EDS. Previously it has been shown that 25–30% of individuals with classical EDS have mutations that decrease the stability of the messenger RNA expressed from one COL5A1 allele (Schwarze et al. 2000, Wenstrup et al., 2000; Malfait et al., 2005). In our screen of seventy-six subjects with phenotypes consistent with classical EDS, we found that twenty of the seventy who were informative for the markers used had decreased or absent sequences from one COL5A1 allele. In addition, one of the six individuals uninformative for all markers also had a mutation that resulted in a null allele (total nulls=21; 21/71=29.5%). Cells from some individuals were completely lacking mRNA from one COL5A1 allele (nulls), while others had decreased (but not absent) amounts of mRNA from one allele (partial nulls).

We previously described the genomic mutations for seven of the twenty-one individuals with null or partial null alleles (Schwarze et al. 2000, Takahara et al, 2002). We now have identified genomic alterations in nine of the remaining fourteen individuals. Five had mutations affecting splicing (four intronic, one exonic), two had nonsense mutations, and two had insertions/deletions that resulted in reading frame shifts and downstream premature termination codons (Table 2). In each of the nine, the genomic alteration resulted in creation of a premature stop codon, leading to nonsense-mediated decay of the message containing it.

For the three nonsense and insertion/deletion mutations for which samples were available (P6, P7, and P8; P5 not studied), there was only one product expressed from each mutant allele and all message was subjected to NMD.

The effects of splice site mutations were varied. Some resulted in a single outcome and an mRNA with a PTC that underwent NMD (P9 and P10). Other splice site mutations led to two different products (P11 and P12). In these cases, one product contained a PTC and was degraded. The other product of each mutant allele had an intact reading frame, thereby providing stability and protection from NMD.

Outcomes of splice site mutations often cannot be predicted from the change in genomic DNA, as shown by the comparison of outcomes for P9 and P11, both of which have an intronic +1G>A donor site mutation. In one case (IVS19, P9), the only detectable outcome of the mutation was use of a cryptic intronic splice site, leading to partial intron inclusion, frameshift with a PTC in exon 20, and mRNA degradation (Figure 3A). In the other case (IVS35, P11), there were two outcomes. One was partial intron inclusion, as occurred in the previous example and which created an unstable mRNA. The second product resulted from skipping of a forty-five basepair exon, which maintained an intact reading frame (Figure 3B).

These findings indicate that different transcripts of the same allele may adopt different conformations or be processed in different ways. Outcomes of splice site mutations reflect characteristics of the surrounding sequences- the presence of cryptic splice sites that can be activated, and whether the use of a new splice site results in an in-frame or out-of-frame transcript. These possibilities do not account for the presence of more than one outcome, which might be explained, in part, by the local order of intron removal in the region of the mutation (Schwarze et al., 1999). If the intron just prior to one with a donor site mutation is removed more slowly than the one with the mutation, and its removal is dependent upon the splicing of the downstream intron (in this case the affected one), then the cell will retain both introns until the precursor RNA refolds so that splicing results in skipping of the exon adjacent to the mutation. If however, the upstream intron is removed more quickly than the one with the donor site mutation, then exon skipping is not possible and use of a cryptic exonic or intronic splice site downstream of the native one, or whole intron inclusion are the possible outcomes with mRNA stability dependent on whether the product is in or out of frame. The presence of different outcomes from the same allele suggests that in the wild type allele, the folding of the nascent mRNA may differ among transcripts and facilitates the use of different splice pathways. Then, in the presence of a mutation, these splice pathways lead to different outcomes.

Substitutions of glycine residues in the triple helical domain of fibrillar collagens disrupt the propagation of the collagen triple helix and result in abnormal molecules that can be disease-causing. We identified two patients with substitutions for glycine as the mechanism of their classical EDS, one each in COL5A1 and COL5A2. The only previously published example of a substitution for glycine in COL5A2 involved the same glycine residue (1149), but the G>C transversion in the first position of the codon resulted in substitution by arginine (Richards et al., 1998) rather than by alanine, as seen here. This alteration was reflected in the electrophoretic mobility shift seen in the α2(V) chains made by the cultured dermal fibroblasts.

Cysteine residues in the carboxyl-terminal propeptide of fibrillar collagens are involved in inter- and intrachain disulfide bonds. Cysteine is not found in the triple helical domain, presumably because it would increase the likelihood of having unpaired residues. There are eight cysteine residues in the carboxyl-terminal propeptide of the proα1(V) chain, the first four of which form interchain disulfide bonds that stabilize the trimeric structure. The last four form a loop within a loop structure that allows a chain recognition site to appear on each chain. The substituted residue is the last cysteine of the chain and its absence in other fibrillar collagen chains leads to failure of incorporation and chain degradation. This appears to be another mechanism for “haploinsufficiency”, but at the protein level. The abnormal protein would be rapidly degraded.

The c.1588G>A p.Gly530Ser variant in COL5A1 gene has been described previously in individuals with classical EDS (Giunta and Steinmann, 2000; Giunta et al, 2002) and proposed to be disease-modifying in the heterozygous state and disease-causing when homozygous. Although not found in dbSNP, we found that the frequency of the allele with the variant A was similar (about 5%) in groups with classical EDS, vascular EDS, and OI, and in three control groups we tested. Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium predicts the frequency of individuals homozygous for the variant to be 1/400, using an allele frequency of 1/20 (5%). This is consistent with the data obtained here, as there was one homozygote among the 398 individuals screened. If homozygosity for the variant A led to classical EDS, then about 1/400 individuals would be affected. The frequency of the classical form of EDS is thought to be far lower than that (although good epidemiological data are not available) so that homozygosity for the exon 13 variant is unlikely to cause this condition.

From the small number of individuals studied here, it was not possible to determine if there are any significant differences in phenotype among those that have different classes of mutations in the type V collagen genes (Table 2).
