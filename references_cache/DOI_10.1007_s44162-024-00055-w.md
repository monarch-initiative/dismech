# A retrospective review of LMNB1-related autosomal dominant leukodystrophy
**Authors:** Judit M. Perez Ortiz, Karthik Muthusamy, W. Oliver Tobin, Ralitza Gavrilova, Margot A. Cousin, Radhika Dhamija
**Journal:** Journal of Rare Diseases (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s44162-024-00055-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44162-024-00055-w)

## Content

Abstract
Introduction
LMNB1-related autosomal dominant leukodystrophy (ADLD) is a slowly progressive neurodegenerative disorder caused by overexpression of LMNB1. We retrospectively reviewed charts of all ADLD patients seen at Mayo Clinic.

Methods
All available data from molecularly confirmed ADLD patients was reviewed.

Results
Of eight patients identified, three were male. Age at symptom onset ranged from 33 to 64 years. In males, the first symptom was erectile dysfunction (2/3) or neurogenic bladder (1/3) and, in females, weakness (3/5), bladder dysfunction (2/5), or depression (1/5). Diagnostic delay from symptom onset was a median of 6 (IQR 2.3–10) years. Other reported symptoms included cognitive difficulties (8/8), fatigue (7/8), sleep issues (4/8), mood disturbances (5/8), tremor (4/8), and migraine (4/8). Family history was positive in 6. All eight patients had LMNB1 duplication. Eighteen brain MRIs were reviewed from 7 patients. All showed symmetric confluent T2W deep cerebral and periventricular white matter hyperintensities with involvement of the posterior limb of the internal capsule, corpus callosum, corticospinal tract in brain stem, and superior and middle cerebellar peduncles. Seven spine MRIs from six patients showed moderate diffuse atrophy of the spinal cord.

Conclusion
Typical clinical symptoms and characteristic MRI changes should prompt genetic testing for ADLD.

Introduction
LMNB1-related ADLD (autosomal dominant leukod -
ystrophy) is a life-limiting slowly progressive autoso -
mal dominant disease of the central white matter [1]. 
Disease onset is typically in the 4th to 5th decade, with 
early autonomic dysfunction [2] which can include blad -
der dysfunction, constipation, postural hypotension, 
erectile dysfunction, and impaired sweating. Motor and 
coordination deficits follow, with corticospinal tract and 
cerebellar white matter involvement resulting in spastic -
ity, ataxia, and tremor. Cognitive dysfunction is a later 
manifestation in the course of disease [3]. Psychiatric 
comorbidities (anxiety and depression) are also seen [1]. 
Survival can be up to two decades from disease onset [4]. 
LMNB1-related ADLD results in early death, and there is 
no cure [1].
ADLD was first described in 1984 in a kindred with 
symptoms and imaging white matter abnormalities sug -
gestive of progressive multiple sclerosis [5 ]. Two genetic 
alterations have now been identified: LMNB1 gene dupli -
cations or upstream deletions affecting LMNB1expression

is the underlying basis of this disorder [9 ], though the 
exact pathogenesis is still being studied [10, 11].
We performed retrospective chart review of all molec -
ularly confirmed ADLD patients seen at Mayo Clinic to 
summarize our experience. We highlight several unique 
clinical features in our cohort including MRI brain find -

available literature on this ultra-rare disease.
Methods
The Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board granted 
a waiver of consent for this study. No patient included 
in this study declined Minnesota research authoriza -
tion. The Mayo Clinic electronic medical record was 
searched for term “adult leukodystrophy” from 1948 to 
10/2023 using an inhouse text search tool, Advanced 
Text Explorer. All study patients were seen in the Depart-
ments of Clinical Genomics and Neurology at Mayo 
Clinic. Patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and 
testing included in this manuscript were extracted from 
routine clinic visits.
All brain and spine MR images were acquired with 1.5 
Tesla or 3 Tesla clinical MR systems and contained at 
least T1-weighted and T2-weighted sequence(s). In one 
patient, only CT head was available due to implanted 
hardware (bladder stimulator). When available, nerve 
conduction studies (NCS), electromyography (EMG), 
autonomic testing (thermoregulatory sweat test and 
autonomic reflex screen: heart rate to deep breathing, 
Valsalva maneuver, tilt table, sudomotor), urodynamic 
studies, and neuropsychometric testing results were 
reviewed.
Results
A total of 242 patient records were reviewed to identify 
8 unrelated patients with molecularly confirmed ADLD. 
Targeted single gene LMNB1 deletion/duplication testing 
was sent for all patients to confirm diagnosis. All eight 
patients had LMNB1 duplication. Three were male.
Six patients had a positive family history (defined as 
abnormal MRI and/or genetic confirmation in a first-
degree relative). Patient clinical characteristics are shown 
in Table 1.
The median age of first reported symptom was 38.5 
(IQR 35–49.3, range 33 to 64) years. Median age at 
diagnosis was 46 (IQR 40.8–48.5) years, with a median 
diagnostic delay from symptom onset of 6 (IQR 2.3–10) 
years. The first symptoms were bladder dysfunction 
(1/3) or erectile (2/3) dysfunction in male patients and 
bladder dysfunction (2/5), weakness (3/5), or depres -
sion (1/5) in female patients. Increased headache fre -
quency accompanied the first symptoms in patient 
5. Patient 6 was given a diagnosis based on abnormal 
MRI and family history 8 years before symptom onset 
(Fig. 1).
Commonly reported symptoms during disease course 
included neurogenic bladder (8/8), erectile dysfunction 
(3/3), cognitive difficulties (8/8), fatigue (7/8), sleep 
issues (insomnia in 6, daytime sleepiness in 1, obstruc -
tive sleep apnea in 1), neuropathic symptoms (paresthe -
sia and/or pain) (5/8), mood disturbances (5/8), tremor 
(4/8), and migraine (4/8) (Table  1). Psychometric test -
ing was normal except for psychomotor slowing in one 
(1/3) and showed mild cognitive difficulties mainly in 
executive function for two (2/3).
Two patients exhibited unique symptoms.
Patient 1 developed several months of paranoid 
delusions (psychosis) requiring inpatient evaluation, 
with a repeat MRI at time of psychosis showing stable 
ADLD-related changes. He also had more significant 
psychiatric symptoms with ADHD, tics, and anxiety, all 
requiring pharmacologic treatment.
Patient 8 (family history unknown) had SIADH-
related hyponatremia as a prominent early presentation 
with white matter abnormalities initially attributed to 
chronic hyponatremia. This resulted in a 9-year diag -
nostic delay.
Eighteen total brain MRIs were reviewed for 7 
patients. Age at MRI ranged from 33 to 66  years. All 
showed symmetric T2W confluent deep cerebral and 
periventricular white matter hyperintensities with 
involvement of the posterior limb of the internal cap -
sule, corpus callosum, corticospinal tract in brain stem, 
superior and middle cerebellar peduncles, and asso -
ciated volume loss. One head CT available (patient 
7) showed hypodensities in the corresponding areas 
without calcifications (patient’s hardware precluded 
MRI evaluation). None showed contrast enhancement. 
Patient 6 had abnormal MRIs up to 8  years prior to 
symptom onset. Depicted in Fig.  1, her MRIs showed 
progression of subcortical and periventricular white 
matter hyperintensity in the corticospinal tracts, brain -
stem, middle cerebellar peduncles, and cerebellar white 
matter.
Seven spine MRIs from six patients showed moderate 
diffuse atrophy of the spinal cord more pronounced in 
cervical and thoracic regions (Fig. 2).
Electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction stud -
ies (NCS) were available in 4, all of which were nor -
mal. Autonomic reflex screen with tilt table testing and 
quantitative sudomotor axon reflex test (QSART) was 
available in 2. It was abnormal in both showing orthos -
tatic hypotension and anhidrosis. Urodynamic testing 
was available in three patients, and two were abnormal 
(Table 1).

Discussion
LMNB1-related ADLD is a slowly progressive leu -
kodystrophy caused by duplication of LMNB1 [ 4]. We 
performed a retrospective chart review of the eight 
molecularly confirmed ADLD patients seen at Mayo 
Clinic to summarize our experience and expand the 
clinical information of this rare disease.
Our patient cohort exhibited similar symptoms 
onset and evolution as previously described [1 ], with 
commonly early autonomic dysfunction, then ataxia 
and pyramidal symptoms, and later cognitive difficul -
ties. ADLD symptom onset is more commonly seen in 
the 4th to 5th decade [3 , 12]. Half (4/8) of our cohort 
had onset of symptoms in the 4th decade. There was a 
median diagnostic delay of 6 years.
Migraine was seen in 50% of our cohort, which is 
higher than the migraine prevalence in the general pop -
ulation in the United States [13]. Two of our patients 
stood out for prominent neurological symptoms not 
previously reported in the ADLD literature. A younger 
patient (patient 1) experienced debilitating paranoid 
psychosis, apparently without new MRI changes. There 
is a single case report of bipolar type 1 symptoms in a 
patient with suspected, but not genetically confirmed, 
ADLD [14]. There was no documented psychiatric his -
tory in our patient’s family, which limits the interpreta -
tion of whether his prominent symptoms are primary 
psychiatric.
The other patient with unique phenotype in our cohort 
(patient 8) had prominent SIADH-related hyponatremia 
and resulted in diagnostic delay of 9  years due to white 
matter abnormalities attributed to chronic hyponatremia.
One patient had presymptomatic and postsymptomatic 
brain MRIs, which supports that ADLD pathologic white 
matter abnormalities predate disease manifestation. This 
has been described most thoroughly in a 2015 study of

Our study has limitations intrinsic to retrospective 
single-center studies. We extracted data from available 
documentation in office visits without standardized clini-

nomic testing, 3/8 underwent urodynamic testing, and 
only 4/8 underwent EMG/NCS as part of their care. Our

testing, thus excluding patients with likely ADLD but 
without genetic confirmation.
Conclusions
ADLD is an ultra-rare progressive adult-onset leukod -
ystrophy with characteristic neuroimaging [4, 15–18] 
and clinical findings. Recognition of the typical clinical 
symptoms (insidious onset of myelopathy and autonomic 
dysfunction with gradual progression) and characteris -
tic MRI changes (symmetric T2-weighted hyperintensi -
ties in the cerebral white matter extending from below 
the motor cortex, following corticospinal tracts through 
the posterior limb of the internal capsule) should raise

Causative deletions and duplications involving LMNB1
