Munk first described minimal change disease (MCD) as “lipoid nephrosis” because of the presence of lipid in the tubular epithelial cells and urine.[1]MCD is the most common cause of nephrotic syndrome (NS) in children, accounting for 70%–90% of children younger than age 10 years. In adults, MCD is the third most common cause of primary NS seen in 10%–15% of the patients.[2-4]

MCD is a type of NS with no glomerular lesions by light microscopy (LM), no staining on immunofluorescence (IF) microscopy and foot process effacement but no electron-dense deposits on electron microscopy, though there can be minimal mesangial prominence by LM or low-intensity staining for C3 and immunoglobulin (Ig) M by IF.[5]

The course and prognosis of MCD in children and adults differ as in the presence of renal failure (33%), hypertension (HTN) (35%), and microscopic hematuria (47%) in adults with MCD.[6]

Standard treatment for MCD is immunosuppression with multiple agents which are available, such as steroids, cyclophosphamide, cyclosporine (CyA), tacrolimus, and newer drugs like rituximab.

Many studies have shown that MCD is a more common cause for primary NS in India when compared to Western studies.[7]There have even been studies which suggests a difference in incidence of MCD as primary NS in North and South India.[8,9]Hence, this study was taken up to analyze the incidence and course of adult-onset MCD.

A retrospective observational study was carried out and data were collected retrospectively from all biopsy-proven MCD patients between 2012 and 2018. A total of 86 adults were diagnosed to have biopsy-proven MCD. Of these, 32 were excluded due to insufficient data/lost for follow-up. Pediatric MCD cases were excluded from the study. Secondary MCD patients were excluded from the study. Renal biopsy specimen was reported by the Department of Pathology, M. S. Ramaiah Teaching Hospital. Laboratory investigations included complete blood count (CBC), urine routine, renal function test, liver function test, urine proteincreatinine ratio (UPCR)/24-h urine protein.

The Institutional Ethics Committee approval was obtained.

MCD: NS with no glomerular lesions by LM (or only minimal mesangial proliferation), no staining on IF microscopy (or low-intensity staining for C3 and/or IgM) and foot process effacement but no electron-dense deposits on electron microscopy.

Complete remission: Reduction of proteinuria to 0.3 g/day or 300 mg/g urine creatinine and serum albumin >35 g/L (3.5 g/dL).

Partial remission: Reduction of proteinuria to 0.3–3.5 g/day (300–3500 mg/g urine creatinine).

Relapse: Proteinuria >3.5 g/day or >3500 mg/g (4350 mg/mmol) urine creatinine after complete remission has been obtained.

Frequently relapsing NS (FRNS): Four or more relapses within 1 year.

Steroid-dependent NS (SDNS): Two relapses during or within 2 weeks of completing steroid therapy.

Steroid-resistant NS (SRNS): Failure to achieve remission despite at least 16 weeks of prednisolone.

Acute kidney injury (AKI): Rise in serum creatinine by >26.52 pmol/L (0.3 mg/dL) over the baseline or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 h.

Treatment protocol was same for all the patients, and initial treatment included oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg daily for 4 to 16 weeks followed by tapering over the next 6 months. Relapses were treated according to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes guidelines (2012). Cyclophosphamide and CNIs (either of the agents) were used when steroids were contraindicated or in cases of FRNS/SDNS/ SRNS.

The IBM SPSS Statistics Windows version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used to perform statistical analysis.

Descriptive analysis includes expression of all the explanatory and outcome variables in terms of frequency and proportions for categorical variables whereas in terms of mean and standard deviation for continuous variables. Chi-square test was used to compare the age, gender, remission, renal failure and response of different drugs, treatment durations, comorbidity conditions, relapse episodes, and different types of infections based on the degree of proteinuria among study patients. Similarly, a comparison of treatment responses, age, and gender distribution of study patients based on the underlying infection was compared using the same test.

Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare the mean UPCR levels between before and after treatment period.

The level of significance (P-value) was set atP<0.05.

The infections were the most common complication seen in our study population. Respiratory tract infections were the most common at at 38.9% which were both a trigger and consequence of relapses in the patients. Furthermore, the noninfectious complications seen in the study group were mainly secondary to steroid use. These included steroid-induced cataract, hypogonadism, diabetes, and psychosis (Table 8).

In our retrospective analysis of incidence of primary MCD in adults, it was found that MCD was diagnosed in adults in the third and fourth decades of life, with a mean age of 36 years (36.67 ± 14.87 SD). In the study population of 54, 37 (68.5%) were male and 17 (31.5%) were female. Of the total, 47 (87%) of the patients achieved complete remission or partial remission after initial prednisolone therapy. Of the seven patients with SRNS (primary or secondary), four achieved complete remission or partial remission after being treated with immunosuppressive medications. Renal failure was seen in 10 of the 54 patients, and four patients did not recover completely and were lost for follow-up.

Children with MCD rarely manifest the features of HTN or hematuria which is more common in adults. In our study, 20 (37%) of the patients had HTN. Studies on clinical characteristics in the West by Waldman et al[10]and Dias et al[11]show a similar incidence of HTN whereas a study by Keskar et al[12]in a North Indian population shows a lower incidence of HTN at 6.5%.

MCD in children responds well to steroids, whereas in adults, the treatment of MCD is quite challenging. In adults, the response to steroids is often late and steroid resistance is also more common when compared to children.

In the study, steroid resistance was in 7 (13%) of the patients who responded well to immunosuppression.

Two patients achieved spontaneous remission in our study. In the study population, 51 (94.4%) patients achieved complete remission or partial remission which was comparable to studies by Mak et al[13]and Dias et al.[11]Twenty-one patients (38.89%) of the patients had relapses out of which six patients had FRNS who later responded to CNI/rituximab. The mean duration to achieve remission in the study population was 8.8 weeks which was considerably more when compared to the study by Keskar et al,[12]where the time for remission was 5.6 weeks. In patients with SRNS, four out of seven responded to CNI, while three were CNI resistant who received rituximab. Evidence supporting CNI efficacy in FRNS in adults is lacking,[14]and it is also associated with a high incidence of frequent relapses and CNI dependency.[14-16]

One patient did not achieve complete remission or partial remission even with immunosuppression. In our study, two patients received rituximab, one patient achieved remission while the other did not.

Two patients progressed to chronic kidney disease, and these patients also had renal biopsy features suggestive of chronic interstitial nephritis. One patient was initiated on HD in view of severe renal failure, and volume overload was later lost for follow-up. Progression to end-stage renal disease is very uncommon <5% and is usually seen with development of focal segmental glomeruloscle-rosis.[17,18]

Complications commonly encountered were infections, with respiratory tract infections (21%) being the most common. Other infections included urinary tract infections, subacute bacterial peritonitis, and varicella zoster. Noninfectious complications included steroid-induced cataract, hypogonadism, diabetes, and psychosis. There were no episodes of thromboembolism in our study population. However, Nolasco et al[19]in their study found a thrombotic complication in 13% of the patients.

In the same study, a 15-year patient survival was only 40% in patients diagnosed of MCD at >60 years of age and 90% in patients diagnosed of MCD <60 years of age.

Minimal change NS is a type of NS which is highly responsive to steroids with good prognosis in children with adult MCD patients requiring a higher and prolonged course of steroid when compared to children. CNIs and rituximab form a promising second-line drug in patients who are steroid resistant/dependent. However, CNI dependency or relapse after stopping steroids is a concern.
