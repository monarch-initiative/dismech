# TCL1A mediates DNA methylation defects in recurrent hydatidiform mole with NLRP7 pathogenic variants.
**Authors:** Gao Z, Liu Q, Li L, Hu T, Lu X, Wu Y, Qin D, Wang X, Gu C, Li J, Xu C, Zhou D, Zhou F, Bai Y, Kang X, Liu J, Deng D, Li L
**Journal:** Nat Commun (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41467-026-69744-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-69744-y)

## Content

Pathogenic variants in NLRP7, implicated in 55% of recurrent hydatidiform mole characterized by hypomethylation at maternally methylated imprinted regions, are proposed to disrupt de novo DNA methylation in human oocytes. However, the precise mechanism remains unclear. Here, we identify TCL1A, a DNMT3A inhibitor, as an endogenous NLRP7-interacting partner. The cryo-EM structure of the NLRP7-TCL1A complex reveals its fundamental architecture. Comprehensive analysis demonstrates that the majority of recurrent hydatidiform mole-causing NLRP7 variants impair its interaction with TCL1A. Mechanistically, NLRP7 potentially safeguards oocyte methylome by sequestering TCL1A in the cytoplasm, thereby preventing its nuclear entry and subsequent suppression of DNMT3A-mediated de novo methylation. Combining in silico predictions and interaction analysis, we identify L766R as a pathogenic variant. These findings propose a cytoplasmic regulatory mechanism governing nuclear DNA methylation, explaining the hypomethylation pathogenesis in NLRP7 variant-associated recurrent hydatidiform mole.

Hydatidiform mole (HM) is a severe reproductive disease characterized by aberrant embryonic development and trophoblast hyperproliferation1–3. Women with a history of molar pregnancy are at an increased risk of recurrent HM (RHM) and potential progression to malignant gestational trophoblastic neoplasia3,4. As an imprinting disorder, HM is closely related to defective DNA methylation in imprinted germline differentially methylated regions (gDMRs), which primarily results from the loss of the maternal genome, an excess of the paternal genome, or abnormal establishment or maintenance of maternal imprinting5–8. Notably, biallelic pathogenic variants in components of the human subcortical maternal complex (hSCMC), a conserved cytoplasmic complex essential for oocyte and early embryo development, have been implicated in these imprinting disorders1,9–12. The hSCMC consists of four proteins (NLRP5, OOEP, TLE6, and KHDC3L) and three putative proteins (NLRP2, PADI6, and human-specific NLRP7)13–15. Pathogenic variants in NLRP7 and KHDC3L account for the majority of RHM cases, while those in PADI6 and NLRP5 lead to a spectrum of reproductive disorders, including RHM, multilocus imprinting disturbances (MLID), and early embryonic arrest1,9–12.

NLRP7 is a human maternal-effect gene identified from familial RHM (FRHM) and accounts for 55% of patients with RHM1,9. Unlike the most androgenetic HM, NLRP7 variant-related RHMs are typically normal diploid biparental1,9,16. Investigations of molar tissues from FRHM patients with biallelic NLRP7 pathogenic variants have revealed widespread loss of methylation at maternally imprinted gDMRs, while paternally imprinted gDMRs remain relatively unaffected17,18. Furthermore, although the DNA methylation landscape in oocytes carrying NLRP7 pathogenic variants remains to be determined, whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) analysis of the oocytes from a patient with biallelic KHDC3L variants, which account for 5% patients with RHM, revealed genome-wide hypomethylation19. These findings suggest that pathogenic variants in NLRP7/KHDC3L disrupt de novo DNA methylation during human oogenesis. Interestingly, heterozygous variants in NLRP7 have also been observed in mothers of offspring with MLID20–22. However, the mechanism by which cytoplasmically localized NLRP7 influences nuclear DNA methylation remains unclear23,24.

The lack of relevant murine models and scarce human materials hinder the recapitulation of NLRP7 physiological functions in vivo. The identification of its interacting proteins has predominantly relied on in vitro cell systems25–28. Although there may be significant differences in mechanisms and patterns between humans and mice, mouse maternal factors essential for de novo methylation in oocytes, including DNMT3A, DNMT3L, KDM1A, KDM1B, SETD2, HDAC1/HDAC2, SIN3A, CFP1, SALL4, and HIRA, have been identified29,30. Knockout of these factors results in hypomethylation across the entire genome and at maternal gDMRs in oocytes or embryos, similar to that in some patients carrying pathogenic NLRP7/KHDC3L variants17,19. To date, no experimental evidence has confirmed the direct interaction between NLRP7 and these epigenetic factors. In addition, NLRP7 variants are associated with significant phenotypic heterogeneity, manifesting as a spectrum of reproductive and developmental disorders, such as early embryonic arrest, recurrent miscarriage, MLID, and, in rare cases, live births20,21,31–33. This phenotypic variability complicates the genetic diagnosis of NLRP7 pathogenic variants. Therefore, the identification of the in vivo binding partners is critical for understanding the functional mechanism and accurately evaluating the pathogenicity of hSCMC/NLRP7 variants, particularly in cases involving sporadic missense variants.

Human T-cell leukemia/lymphoma 1 A (TCL1A), an oncoprotein of the TCL1 family, drives T-cell leukemias and lymphomas by dysregulating the AKT pathway34,35. Notably, nuclear TCL1A has been reported to disrupt de novo DNA methylation by inhibiting methyltransferase 3 A/3B (DNMT3A/3B) in B-cell chronic lymphocytic leukemia36. Interestingly, TCL1A is also highly expressed in human oocytes37. However, its function and physiological significance remain elusive.

In this study, we screen the binding partners of hSCMC in vivo and in vitro, leading to the identification of a direct interaction between TCL1A and the human-specific NLRP7. Most significantly, our findings reveal that the impaired NLRP7-TCL1A binding represents a potential pathogenic mechanism underlying the NLRP7 variants. This defective interaction facilitates the translocation of hTCL1A into the nucleus, where it likely binds to and inhibits DNMT3A methylation activity, ultimately leading to DNA methylation defects in the RHM associated with NLRP7 pathogenic variants. In addition, the evidence we provide suggests that the NLRP7-TCL1A interaction is a validated molecular indicator to assess the pathogenicity of NLRP7 variants in clinical practice.

We first investigated whether mouse models could recapitulate the defects of de novo DNA methylation establishment observed in human oocytes carrying pathogenic variants in components of the hSCMC. Nlrp5 (encoding one of SCMC core components) and Khdc3 (the ortholog of human KHDC3L) knockout mouse lines were used as experimental models38,39. The genomic DNA methylome in MII-stage oocytes from normal and maternally deficient mice was analyzed using STEM-seq (small-scale TELP-enabled methylome sequencing)40. Compared with the controls, DNA methylation levels in SCMC-defective oocytes only showed minor changes both at the global level and at maternal imprinting control regions (ICRs) (Supplementary Fig. 1a–e). These findings suggest that current SCMC-deficient mouse models inadequately represent the mechanisms of aberrant de novo DNA methylation establishment in the oocytes from patients with pathogenic variants in KHDC3L or NLRP7, highlighting the necessity for human-derived models in such studies.

Co-IP followed by western blotting analysis confirmed that in addition to the four known hSCMC subunits, NLRP7 and NLRP2 were likely the components of the hSCMC in vivo (Fig. 1b). However, PADI6 was not obviously detected. In addition, CCNI2, CDK5, and TCL1A were precipitated by the NLRP7 antibody, while the NLRP5 antibody only pulled down CDK5 and TCL1A, but not CCNI2 (Fig. 1b), suggesting more extensive interactions of NLRP7. To further screen the direct partners of NLRP7, systematic pairwise Co-IP assays were conducted in HEK293T cells overexpressing those candidates. Our Co-IP results were mostly consistent with prior studies (Supplementary Fig. 2a)31. Notably, our assays demonstrated that NLRP7 exhibited distinct interaction strengths with other components in vitro. Weaker interactions of NLRP7 with NLRP5 and PADI6 were observed, while its binding to CDK5 and KHDC3L was minimal. However, our assay showed the strong binding between NLRP7 and NLRP2, CCNI2, itself, TCL1A, and the core hSCMC component TLE6 (Fig. 1c). To further investigate the direct interaction between NLRP7 and TLE6 in living cells, we developed an assay termed VPICONE (Visual Protein-protein Interaction by Co-translocation into Outer Nuclear Envelope) (Supplementary Fig. 2b). In this assay, mCherry tagged an outer nuclear membrane (ONM) anchor (termed as mCDTK) was fused to the C-terminus of bait (bait-mCDTK), thereby recruiting GFP-tagged prey to the ONM for visualization (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). As a validation, direct interaction between TLE6 and OOEP was successfully detected as their co-localization on the ONM in HEK293T cells (Supplementary Fig. 2d). The VPICONE assay revealed that co-expression of NLRP7-mCDTK with GFP-TLE6 induced robust co-localization on the ONM, but was absent in controls (Fig. 1d). The in vivo interaction between NLRP7 and TLE6 was also detected by PLA in the discarded human MI oocytes (Fig. 1e, f). Collectively, these results indicate that human NLRP7 is an authentic component of hSCMC, directly binding to the core protein TLE6.

Among these candidate interactors of NLRP7, CCNI2, a cyclin family protein, binds to CDK5, probably regulating cell cycle in cancer cells41, while TCL1A is linked to DNA methylation via DNMT3 inhibition and thus may be associated with the hSCMC’s function in imprinting disorders36. Thus, we further screened the interactions between TCL1A and those components of the hSCMC through pairwise Co-IP assays in co-expressing HEK293T cells. Those assays showed TCL1A specifically binds to NLRP7 and itself, consistent with its dimeric formation (Fig. 1g)42,43. Additionally, TCL1A interacted with NLRP7 isoforms 2 (NP_996611.2) and 3 (NP_001120727.1), but not isoform 1 (NP_631915.2), in their overexpressing HEK293T or COS-7 cells, indicating that the residues 643-672 of NLRP7 are essential for TCL1A binding (Supplementary Fig. 2e–g). NLRP7-TCL1A interaction remained stable even after washing with high salt buffer (Supplementary Fig. 2h), underscoring the interaction robustness. A variant in TCL1A (residues 36-38: PLT-to-AAA mutation) that was reported to disrupt its dimerization did not dramatically affect its interaction with NLRP7 (Supplementary Fig. 2h)42. Although multiple NLRP family proteins are present in human oocytes44, in vitro Co-IP assays demonstrated that TCL1A specifically interacted with NLRP7, but not directly with other proteins. It also suggests the functional specialization of NLRP7 compared to its most homologous paralog NLRP2 (Supplementary Fig. 2i). The presence of minimal KHDC3L binding did not significantly affect the interaction between NLRP7 and TCL1A (Supplementary Fig. 2j). Notably, the application of the VPICONE assay could visualize the recruitment of GFP-TCL1A to the ONM by NLRP7-mCDTK (Fig. 1h). PLA analysis confirmed the in vivo interaction between NLRP7 and TCL1A in the subcortex and cytoplasm of discarded human oocytes and cleavage embryos (Fig. 1i, j). Taken together, these in vitro and in vivo experiments suggest that TCL1A is a specific and endogenous binding partner of human-specific NLRP7.

We further investigated how the NLRP7-TCL1A complex is incorporated into the core hSCMC45. Our results demonstrated that the core NLRP5 precipitates TCL1A only in the presence of NLRP7 and was also dependent on TLE6/OOEP but not KHDC3L (Fig. 1k). The VPICONE assay further demonstrated that TLE6-mCDTK recruited GFP-TCL1A to the ONM in an NLRP7-dependent manner, supporting the formation of a TLE6-NLRP7-TCL1A complex in cells (Fig.1l). Altogether, these findings suggest that the NLRP7-TCL1A complex is involved in hSCMC through its direct binding to TLE6 (Fig. 1m).

Tcl1, the murine ortholog of TCL1A is a maternal-effect gene crucial for preimplantation embryonic development46. Mouse Tcl1 RNA remained dormant until germinal vesicle breakdown. The protein was undetectable in fully grown oocytes (FGO) (Supplementary Fig. 3a, c, d)47,48. In contrast, consistent with the high translational activity detected at the latest in FGO, human TCL1A was easily detected in the sample of only three human FGO, suggesting its high abundance (Supplementary Fig. 3b–d)37,49. Furthermore, we observed no interaction between exogenous mouse TCL1 and mSCMC core TLE6 in mouse oocytes (Supplementary Fig. 3e). Unlike that of the well-defined SCMC components, the level of mouse TCL1 was also unaffected by maternal NLRP5 knockout at the 2-cell stage (Supplementary Fig. 3f)50–54. These results indicate that the relationship between hSCMC and NLRP7-TCL1A is specialized in humans.

Human NLRP7, akin to other NLRP family members such as NLRP5 and NLRP3, comprises three typical domains: an N-terminal pyrin domain (PYD), a central NACHT domain (NBD, HD1, WHD, and HD2 subdomains), and a C-terminal region composed of 14 canonical leucine-rich repeats (LRR1-14) (Fig. 2a). TCL1A is an all-beta protein consisting of an eight-stranded antiparallel beta-barrel (Fig. 2a)43. Building on our recent structures of the mouse and human SCMCcore (NLRP5/TLE6/OOEP)45,55, we attempted to model an extended hSCMC complex containing NLRP7 and TCL1A. We therefore focused on elucidating the structure of the NLRP7-TCL1A complex. For this purpose, Flag-tagged full-length NLRP7 isoform 3 (residues 1-1037) and Strep-tagged full-length TCL1A (residues 1-114) were co-expressed in HEK293F cells, and the NLRP7-TCL1A complex was purified (Fig. 2b). Single-particle cryo-EM analysis yielded two maps of NLRP7-TCL1A at resolutions of 3.58 Å (state I) and 3.48 Å (state II) (Fig. 2c–f, Supplementary Figs. 4–5, Supplementary Table 2). Interestingly, the NLRP7-TCL1A complex in state I contains two NLRP7 and four TCL1A molecules, while state II contains only one NLRP7 and two TCL1A molecules (Fig. 2c–f). This structural observation suggests that NLRP7-TCL1A adopts a dynamic oligomerization equilibrium, similar to human and mouse SCMCcore45,55. The structural comparison revealed no conformational changes between State I and State II (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Therefore, subsequent analyses focused on the State I complex.

The overall structure of the state I complex forms an approximately 135 Å × 113 Å heterohexamer (Fig. 2c). The two NLRP7 molecules (a and b) embrace each other via extensive interactions with a PYD domain inserted into the cavity between the two dimerized NACHT domains (Fig. 2c, d). NLRP7 molecule (a) adopts a relatively intact structure, with the exception of two disordered regions: a flexible linker between the PYD and NACHT domains (residues 94-114) and a flexible segment within LRR2 (residues 637-655) (Fig. 2c, d). In NLRP7 (b), only the NACHT and partial LRR domains were resolved (Fig. 2c, d). Due to the absence of density for the flexible linker to PYD in both NLRP7s, the identity of the observed PYD domain could not be unambiguously assigned. For clarity in description, we assigned it to the NLRP7 molecule (a) in the current model. Each LRR domain of NLRP7 interacts with one molecule of the TCL1A dimer (Fig. 2c, d). The homo-dimeric forms of NLRP7 and TCL1A in the complex were in agreement with the Co-IP results (Fig. 1c, f).

In the complex, two NLRP7 molecules adopt the nearly identical conformation with a RMSD at 0.85 Å over 653 Cα atoms (Supplementary Fig. 6b). In addition, the structural models between TCL1A(a) in this complex and TCL1A monomer (PDB: 1JSG) are indistinguishable, with a RMSD value at 0.78 Å (Supplementary Fig. 6c), suggesting that the binding of NLRP7 does not significantly alter the conformation of TCL1A.

Further structural comparisons among NLRP7, NLRP3, and mouse NLRP5 suggest that NLRP7 has relatively low ATPase activity. By superimposing the NBD domain of NLRP7 with that of NLRP3 (PDB for inactive form: 7PZC and active form: 8EJ4)56,57, the putative ATP/ADP binding pocket of NLRP7 is different from that in NLRP3 (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b), but similar to that in mouse NLRP5 (PDB: 8H96) (Supplementary Fig. 7c). The NACHT domain of NLRP7 is more extensive than that of NLRP3 (PDB: 7PZC), showing the HD1 and WHD subdomains of NLRP7 shifted more outwardly (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Specifically, in the Walker A and sense 2 motifs, which are critical for ATP binding and hydrolysis, the distances from key residues K184, T185, and H468 of NLRP7 to ADP are much longer than those in NLRP3 (PDB: 7pzc) by superposition (Supplementary Fig. 7d, e). When comparing the NACHT domain of mouse NLRP5 (PDB: 8h96) with that of NLRP7, the conformations of the WHD and HD2 subdomains exhibit a relatively high similarity, except for the HD1 domain, which shows a notable deflection (Supplementary Fig. 7c)45,55. The ATP hydrolysis activity assay further supported the structural analysis (Supplementary Fig. 7f). These data indicate the unique regulations of the conformation in the reproduction-related NLR proteins.

The Q914, R917, K973, and S974 of LRR (a) form the hydrogen bonds with E340, D148, and D144 of NACHT domain in NLRP7 (b). In the NLRP7-TCL1A complex, TCL1A directly interacts with four α-helices of LRR domains of NLRP7 (LRR3-6) (Fig. 3c). Key residues R693, S702, D722, A726, R746, T747, N758, K760 and E780 in NLRP7 directly form hydrogen bonds with the residues W19, M62, T65, L78, Y79, R85, S89, D113 and D114 of TCL1A (a) (Fig. 3c). Substitutions at these interface residues or deletions of the LRR4-5 motif in either protein seriously disrupted the complex stability, with the exceptions of S702A in NLRP7 and W19A/T65A in TCL1A (Fig. 3d, e). Notably, NLRP7 pathogenic variants at R693 and D722 residues linked to RHM and reproductive failure1,58, abolished its TCL1A binding, suggesting that the interaction mediates the pathogenicity of NLRP7 variants.

Sequence alignment of other human reproductive NLRPs, including NLRP2, NLRP4, NLRP5, NLRP7, NLRP9, and NLRP14, identified the residues uniquely conserved in NLRP7 that enable TCL1A binding (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Comparative analysis across mammalian species revealed the conservation in TCL1A-binding interface of NLRP7 (Homo sapiens vs. Macaca: 80%, vs. Pongo: 95%, vs. Sus: 56.7%, vs. Capra: 58.9%) and TCL1A itself (Supplementary Fig. 9b, c). Co-IP assays suggest that this interaction is primate-specific, but is absent in porcine homologs (Supplementary Fig. 9d).

First, we evaluated the impact of these NLRP7 pathogenic variants on protein expression levels in HEK293T cells. Approximately 30.8% (16/52) of the pathogenic variants significantly reduced NLRP7 expression, potentially due to increased protein instability (Supplementary Fig. 9a–e). They were mainly clustered in the NACHT domain (T185del, F250L, K379N, G380R, R390H, L398R) and LRR domain (C668R, R693W, C704Y, P716A, A719V, R721W, D722G, I858Hfs11, G981Rfs19). These findings suggest that the NLRP7 instability resulting from these variants is one of the potential pathogenic mechanisms.

We next evaluated the interaction of NLRP7 pathogenic variants-TCL1A in HEK293T cells. Notably, TCL1A stabilized the expression of normal NLRP7 but had a minor effect on certain mutants when co-expressed (Supplementary Fig. 9d–g). To reduce variability of NLRP7 expression in the experiments, we employed a semiquantitative pull-down assay to quantify the binding affinity of TCL1A to NLRP7 (Supplementary Fig. 9h). Approximately 51.1% (23/45) of nTVs significantly disrupted the NLRP7-TCL1A interaction (Fig. 4c, e), further suggesting that the impaired binding serves as a key pathogenic mechanism. Among these pathogenic variants, about 87.0% (20/23) were localized to the LRR domains, the region with the highest mutational burden observed in patients (Fig. 4c, e)1. Notably, all seven TVs abolished NLRP7-TCL1A binding (Fig. 4d, e). The VPICONE assay confirmed the defective interaction for several RHM-linked variants (Fig. 4f).

When focusing on the major clinical phenotype of those RHM-associated pathogenic variants, ~65.5% (19/29) of nTVs and ~71.4% (25/35) of total tested variants impaired the NLRP7-TCL1A interaction in our assays (Fig. 4g). Given that all five TVs closest to the C-terminus disrupting the interaction (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Table. 3), we reasonably hypothesized that TVs occurring upstream of these sites disrupt TCL1A binding. When incorporating all 46 pathogenic TVs into our analysis, approximately 75.8% (69/91) of total disease-linked variants affected the NLRP7-TCL1A interaction. Notably, when considering only RHM-causing non-synonymous variants, this proportion rose to ~86.5% (64/74) (Fig. 4g). Interestingly, we also observed that multiple pathogenic variants disrupt NLRP7 dimerization (Fig. 4h). Above all, these data demonstrate that NLRP7 pathogenic variants associated with reproductive disorders, especially RHM, predominantly result in protein instability and/or impair TCL1A binding capacity.

We examined whether NLRP7 influences the subcellular localization of TCL1A in HEK293T cells (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Consistent with prior reports36,62, GFP-TCL1A exhibited a diffuse distribution throughout the cells with distinct nuclear localization when co-expressed with mCherry or the non-binding NLRP7 isoform 1-mCherry. In contrast, co-expression of NLRP7 isoform 3, but not most disease-associated variants, markedly reduced TCL1A nuclear localization (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). Furthermore, the proportion of cells exhibiting TCL1A nuclear localization was negatively correlated with the NLRP7/TCL1A expression ratio (Supplementary Fig. 10c–e). A similar pattern was observed in mouse oocytes microinjected with mRNAs encoding normal NLRP7, its mutants, and GFP-TCL1A (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Fig. 10f). These results suggest that NLRP7 prevents the nuclear localization of TCL1A in a dose-dependent manner.

Given the pivotal role of DNMT3A in mediating de novo DNA methylation in oocytes and the association between TCL1A and DNMT3A36,63, we further investigated how nuclear TCL1A functions in DNMT3A methylation activity through several in vitro assays. First, the Co-IP assays confirmed a direct physical interaction between TCL1A and DNMT3A/3B, but not with DNMT1 and DNMT3L (Fig. 5d). Second, in vitro methylation assays using purified DNMT3A and TCL1A demonstrated that TCL1A binding significantly inhibited DNMT3A enzymatic activity (Fig. 5e). Third, although DNMT3L is not expressed in human oocytes64, and the role of alternative protein DNMT3B3 in human oocytes remains controversial65,66, we found that overexpressing TCL1A reduced the interactions between DNMT3A and DNMT3B3 or DNMT3L in HEK293T cells (Fig. 5f). Notably, our simultaneous study revealed that TCL1A acted through competitive binding to DNMT3A, thereby inducing conformational rearrangements that consequently impair its interactions with DNA and S-adenosylmethionine67.

To further investigate the impact of NLRP7/TCL1A on the establishment of de novo DNA methylation in cells, we utilized a cellular differentiation system in which hypomethylated mESCs differentiate into hypermethylated formative pluripotent stem cells (fPSCs), with the methylation process mediated by DNMT3A and DNMT3B67,68. An mESC line with inducible co-expression of NLRP7 and TCL1A by doxycycline (Dox) and Shield1 (S1) were established (Fig. 5g, and Supplementary Fig. 10g)69. In fPSCs, we found that TCL1A overexpression severely disrupted methylation, reducing global levels from ~85.6% in control to 29.6% (Fig. 5h–j). Notably, NLRP7 coexpression counteracted TCL1A-driven hypomethylation, partially restoring methylation level to ~37.3% (Fig. 5h–j). All these results support a hypothesis that NLRP7 pathogenic variants impair the interaction with TCL1A and result in its accumulation in nuclear, where it binds to DNMT3A and inhibits de novo DNA methylation during human oogenesis (Fig. 5k).

To test it, we performed whole-exome sequencing on two patients with RHM and their families, and identified a homozygous L766R variant, as well as a previously reported homozygous R721W variant in NLRP7 (Fig. 6b). Detailed case reports for these two patients were provided in the Methods section. L766 and R721 were located within the LRR3-6 of NLRP7 (Fig. 6c). The mCSM Affinity and in silico analysis both predicted L766R and R721W to be reducing complex stability or potentially pathogenic, although the PolyPhen-2 prediction for R721W was an exception (Fig. 6d). To validate the accuracy of this prediction, we examined the effects of these two variants on their protein expression and their interaction with TCL1A. Our results showed that both variants, especially L766R, significantly reduced the expression of NLRP7 in HEK293T cells and severely disrupted their interaction with TCL1A, aligning with our predictions (Fig. 6e, f). Furthermore, we observed that L766R and R721W resulted in the nuclear accumulation of GFP-TCL1A in the injected mouse oocytes (Figs. 6g and 5c). In conclusion, these findings support the idea that assessing TCL1A binding affinity is a valuable approach for verifying and complementing traditional genetic diagnostic methods for NLRP7 pathogenic variants.

Although the core complex of SCMC and some of its functions are conserved in mice and humans, notable differences exist in SCMC-related proteins between these species45,50,55,72. For example, the SCMC component ZBED3/NLRP4F is highly abundant in mouse oocytes and early embryos, but shows minimal expression in human oocytes and early embryos52,53. Moreover, NLRP7 is exclusively present in humans49,52,53. Since the initial identification of NLRP7 variants as genetic causes of FRHM in 2006, extensive research over the past two decades has revealed more than 100 pathogenic NLRP7 variants in patients with female reproductive disorders1,9. Despite two decades of investigation, the pathophysiological cascade triggered by NLRP7 pathogenic variants persists as a major knowledge gap, with human-specific nature and phenotypic complexity posing significant barriers to mechanistic elucidation, thereby complicating clinical diagnosis.

In this study, we have identified TCL1A as an important binding protein of NLRP7. The direct and strong interaction between TCL1A and NLRP7 provides an opportunity to solve the protein structure of human-specific NLRP7, significantly contributing to the understanding of human reproduction-related NLRP proteins. Notably, NLRP7-TCL1A exists predominantly as dimers, with a minor monomeric population, similar to NLRP545. The oligomerization of NLRP7 may broaden its interaction network to store functional proteins (such as TCL1A), consistent with the model that SCMC/PADI6 assembles into cytoplasmic lattices (CPLs) that are required for mammalian preimplantation development73. Importantly, the impaired binding of TCL1A to NLRP7 variants is highly related with the disease, strongly supporting that TCL1A mediates the pathogenicity of human-specific NLRP7 variants. Building on these findings, we developed a method to predict the pathogenicity of NLRP7 variants. Therefore, our study demonstrates that the TCL1A-binding affinity of NLRP7 represents a valuable diagnostic tool for RHM and related human reproductive disorders.

Within the heterogeneous clinical spectrum of NLRP7-related disorders, RHM stands as the primary phenotype, marked by hypomethylation at maternal gDMRs17. The normal paternally imprinted gDMRs in the moles highlight this methylation deficit originates during human oogenesis17. Notably, NLRP7 is expressed from oocytes to post-implantation embryos, with re-expression after zygotic genome activation44. Although knockdown of NLRP7 causes altered methylation in CpG sites in human stem cells modes26, embryos with biallelic pathogenic variants in NLRP7 develop normally, and patients with RHM show normal blood genomic methylation patterns17, indicating pathogenic variants in maternal NLRP7 exert a more pronounced effect on de novo DNA methylation. The de novo establishment of genomic imprints is mediated by DNMT3A during oogenesis63,74. Thus, the identification of TCL1A functionally links pathogenic variants in NLRP7 to DNMT3A. While direct assessment of TCL1A subcellular localization in patient-derived oocytes remains unfeasible due to limited material availability, our biochemical evidence reveals that disease-causing NLRP7 variants, by affecting the protein instability and/or directly disrupting the binding interface, result in the impaired interaction with cytoplasmic TCL1A and accumulation of nuclear TCL1A in our cell models. The aberrant nuclear translocation of TCL1A binds to and inhibits DNMT3A methylation activity. Consequently, de novo DNA methylation fails to be established in human oocytes. Collectively, these findings suggest that NLRP7 safeguards the methylation function of DNMT3A by sequestering TCL1A in the cytoplasm. Disruption of this regulatory causes defective maternal imprinting in patients with NLRP7 variants, thereby elucidating the pathogenic cascade underlying the disease (Fig. 5k)67.

Notably, the different expression pattern of TCL1A and murine ortholog Tcl1 in oocytes underscores critical species-specific differences in NLRP7/TCL1A/DNMT3A regulatory mechanisms, suggesting evolutionary divergence in the regulation mechanisms of DNA methylation. Nevertheless, increasing evidence in mice underscores the relatively conserved mechanism of maternal cytoplasmic proteins in governing nuclear epigenetic modifications within mammalian oocytes and early embryos59–61,75. For example, the abnormal nuclear translocation of UHRF1/DNMT1, key regulators responsible for both de novo establishment and maintenance of DNA methylation, leads to genomic methylome dysregulation in GV-stage oocytes or 2-cell embryos with maternal disruption of STELLA, PADI6, or NLRP1459–61,75,76. Interestingly, beyond its role in DNA methylation maintenance, cytoplasm-localized UHRF1 also contributes to cytoplasmic quality regulation77. Similarly, although the function of TCL1A in human early embryos remains unclear, mouse studies indicate that TCL1 functions through AKT or PnPase to regulate early embryo development78,79. This further suggests that these maternal proteins distinctly function in different subdomains. In a related context, our recent study revealed that a histone reader protein, SPIN1, predominantly localizes to the cytoplasm in normal mouse oocytes and embryos through tight interaction with KHDC354. In KHDC3-deficient models, although no pronounced DNA methylation abnormalities are detected in oocytes, SPIN1 mislocalizes to the nucleus and disrupts H3K4me3 reprogramming in 2-cell embryos. Collectively, these findings support that the cytoplasmic localization of epigenetic regulators by binding to maternal proteins serve as a critical regulatory mechanism for safeguarding the precise “epigenetic landscape” in mammalian oocytes and early embryos. Elucidating the mechanisms that govern the nucleocytoplasmic shuttling of these regulators in mammalian oocyte and early embryo represents a major challenge for future research.

Although pathogenic variants of the hSCMC components impact DNA methylation, their underlying mechanisms appear to diverge. NLRP7 and KHDC3L variants disrupt the establishment of de novo DNA methylation in oocytes, leading to RHM, whereas NLRP5 and PADI6 impair the maintenance of DNA methylation in embryos, resulting in MLID1,11,12,19,22,24. Notably, although evidence from human samples shows their co-localization and cooperative involvement within the hSCMC80,81, the KHDC3L-NLRP7 interaction is notably weaker in vitro compared to other binding partners in our assays. We cannot rule out the possibility that additional factors are needed for their interaction. For example, NLRP5/TLE6/OOEP shows stronger association during tripartite co-expression than in pairwise interactions45,55. Therefore, directly examining protein behavior in patient-derived oocytes or early embryos may provide the most compelling insights into the relationship between KHDC3L and NLRP7. While our study implicates TCL1A in NLRP7-related RHM, UHRF1 may represent another key player in the pathogenesis of MLID. Total UHRF1 showed a decrease in both PADI6 and NLRP5 knockout mouse oocytes. However, its nuclear accumulation was promoted by PADI6 knockout but reduced by NLRP5 knockout59,82, indicating the complex regulatory mechanisms. Consistently, UHRF1 was also detected in our MS data for NLRP5 (Supplementary Table 1). Together, these findings highlight the value of systematically profiling the interactome of hSCMC subunits to fully elucidate its physiological functions and underlying mechanisms.

For genomic segment analysis, the genome was segmented into continuous intervals of 100 base pairs (bp), 1 kilobase (kb), or 1 megabase (Mb). On average, a minimum coverage criterion of three CpG sites per 100 bp was applied. The methylation level within each bin was determined as the ratio of methylated cytosines (C) to the sum of methylated and unmethylated cytosines (C + T) across all CpG sites within the interval. When comparing overexpressed samples to controls, only bins covered in both conditions were included in the analysis. The total methylation score for each interval was calculated as the sum of the methylation levels across all the CpG sites within that region.
