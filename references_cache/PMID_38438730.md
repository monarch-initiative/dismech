# Lipoprotein glomerulopathy: a rare cause of steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome in a child.
**Authors:** Vala K, Shah K, Kapadia S, Khandelwal M, Jojera A, Soni S, Prajapati A, Saha A
**Journal:** CEN Case Rep (2024)
**DOI:** [10.1007/s13730-024-00861-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13730-024-00861-7)

## Content

Lipoprotein glomerulopathy (LPG) is a rare condition of renal lipidosis characterized by lipoprotein thrombi in glomeruli, an abnormal plasma lipoprotein profile, and a marked increase in serum apolipoprotein E (apo E) levels. It is a monogenic disorder with autosomal dominant inheritance and the average age of presentation is 32 years (4-69 years). It is rare in children. The presentation can be nephrotic syndrome, hematuria, or progressive renal failure. Here we report the first described case of LPG in an Indian 7.5-year-old boy who presented with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome with normal renal function. A renal biopsy was suggestive of lipoprotein glomerulopathy. The detection of a pathogenic variant in apo E, Kyoto type, by exome sequencing, confirmed the diagnosis of lipoprotein glomerulopathy. Complete response was achieved with Angiotensin-converting Enzyme inhibitor and fenofibrates.

Lipoprotein Glomerulopathy (LPG) is rare and associated with heterozygous variants of apoE. It is characterized by abnormal lipid metabolism causing lipo-nephrotoxicity caused by variation in plasma apo E levels. The presence of massive eosinophilic lipoprotein thrombi with dilated capillary lumina showing lamella formation is a classic histopathology finding of LPG [2]. Most cases of LPG are asymptomatic; however, proteinuria and edema are the commonest presentations [2–4]. If left untreated, the disease usually progresses to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD). Since first described in 1989, there is still a lack of specific or targeted therapies. No beneficial effects have been observed for immunosuppressants, lipid-lowering agents, and transplantation [2].

LPG and other APOE-related disorders have been very well described in the literature for adults. LPG is rare in children, and literature is scarce. The unavailability of effective treatment and the aggressive nature of the disease make children with LPG more vulnerable and need great attention. Here we describe the youngest first 7.5-year-old Indian boy, who presented with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome, diagnosed as LPG, and responded to fenofibrate.

A 7.5-year-old male presented in April 2021 to a primary pediatric nephrologist with facial puffiness for 3 weeks, followed by abdominal distension and frothy urine for the last 2 days. There is no history of gross hematuria, arthritis, rash, or other systemic symptoms. Another important family history is that his father has been diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome since 2019, and his paternal aunt had end-stage kidney disease and died at 30 years of age (Fig. 1 Pedigree). On physical examination: weight: 17 kg (with edema) (Z score: − 3.18), height: 110 cm (Z score: − 3), blood pressure: 130/90 mmHg (> 99th centile for height and age, stage 2 hypertension), generalized enema with ascites, and pallor. No other extra-renal manifestations were noted. Urine analysis showed urine protein: + 3; microscopic hematuria: 8–10 cells/HPF; elevated urine protein creatinine ratio (UPCR): 6.22 g/g creatinine. Laboratory investigations showed low serum albumin: 2.39 g/dl; normal renal function-serum creatinine: 0.27 mg/dl. The lipid profile showed serum cholesterol of 359 mg/dL. Renal ultrasonography revealed normal-sized kidneys. These findings were in favor of the nephrotic syndrome. Secondary causes of nephrotic syndrome and infection, if any, were ruled out. The child received prednisolone (2 mg/kg/day) along with two antihypertensive medications, but there was no response after 6 weeks of prednisolone. And so, the child was labeled as having primary steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS). As per the international guideline for the management of SRNS [1], a renal biopsy was done. Light microscopy was suggestive of 30 glomeruli, 1 globally sclerosed; the remaining show a mild to moderate increase in mesangial matrix and cells; capillary lumina are extensively dilated and filled with pale-staining laminated material with mild irregular thickening of basement membrane. Immunofluorescence was negative for all. Electron microscopy was not done.

After a biopsy was done, the child was referred to our institute for further follow-up. A detailed biopsy report was discussed with the pathologist, and lipoprotein glomerulopathy was kept as a possibility. To confirm it on the biopsy report, an oil O red stain was done on paraffin blocks, which was suggestive of lipid droplets (Fig. 2 Histopathological diagnosis). To further confirm the diagnosis, next-generation sequencing was sent, which showed the presence of the heterozygous missense variant in APOE gene (NM_001302688.1) c.205C > T (p.Arg69Cys) [2] which is also known as APOE Kyoto after its first reported case in Japan (Fig. 3 Sanger’s sequencing). The same variant was also confirmed in the father. ApoE levels were not done. Serum levels of cholesterol-352 mg/dL, triglyceride-360 mg/dL, HDL cholesterol-50 mg/dL, LDL cholesterol-232 mg/dL, and VLDL cholesterol-72 mg/dL were noted. Prednisolone was tapered and stopped after confirmation. The child was started on an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI) at the maximum tolerable dose. Also, he received fenofibrate for his abnormal lipid profile.

LPG is a rare autosomal dominant disorder of renal lipidosis. Since being first described in 1989, more than 270 cases have been reported across the globe [3]. Most of these reported cases are from Asian countries, with the average age at presentation being 32 years [4]. LPG is very rare in children. There are 31 cases less than 18 years of age (8 cases < 10 years, 23 cases 11–18 years) reported worldwide [3, 5–8]. We describe a 7.5-year-old child who presented with nephrotic syndrome and hypertension with no response to steroids and a significant family history. As per the recent guidelines of SRNS [1], the child was evaluated further and diagnosed with LPG.

So far, there has been no reported Indian pediatric case of LPG, and we are reporting the youngest LPG case from the country. However, the majority of reported pediatric cases are from China [5]. Song et al. reviewed reported pediatric cases from China and described edema as the commonest presentation. Other important manifestations noted are hypertension, anemia, and hematuria. Similar to a Brazilian case report by Karla et al. [6], our child presented with nephrotic syndrome and did not respond to steroids.

ApoE, a major constituent of various plasma lipoproteins, is an essential apolipoprotein. This APOE gene is located on chromosome 19q13.2 and comprises 4 exons with 3603 base pairs. To date, 17 different variants in the APOE gene have been identified. Kyoto, APOE Tokyo-Maebashi, and APOE Sendai are the three major reported variants [3]. As described in the largest pediatric case literature review by Song et al., the Kyoto APOE variant has been the most common. As reported in a recent meta-analysis by Li et al., the age of onset for patients with APOE Tokyo-Maebashi seemed to be younger than that of other variants [3]. In our case, both father and son had the same APOE Kyoto R25C missense variants. ApoE isoforms were not studied in our case.

No specific or targeted therapy is available for LPG. Immunosuppression or transplantation has no role [9, 10]. Lipid-lowering agents have shown some role in targeting the causative pathology [3, 9]. However, none of these lipid-lowering agents have been approved for use in children. There is not enough literature to prove the role of Staphylococcal protein-A immuno-adsorption and double filtration plasmapheresis [5, 9]. Our child, however, was given fenofibrate along with ACEI, to which he showed a complete response.

We have described the first Indian pediatric case of LPG with the APOE Kyoto variant presented as SRNS. Thorough evaluation of SRNS cases with atypical features in the background of family history and good histopathology evaluation is crucial to diagnose rare diseases like LPG early and facilitate timely management.
