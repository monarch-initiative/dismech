# Causes of mortality in the congenital disorders of glycosylation.
**Authors:** Alharbi H, Horikoshi S, Jenkins SM, Scaglia F, Lam C, Morava E, Larson A, Edmondson AC
**Journal:** Mol Genet Metab (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.ymgme.2025.109052](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ymgme.2025.109052)

## Content

Congenital Disorders of Glycosylation (CDG) are a group of some 200 genetic disorders with PMM2-CDG being the most common disease. These disorders individually remain rare with poorly understood natural history (NH) and causes of mortality. We established a NH study for CDG and collected both prospective and retrospective data on CDG outcomes. In the current data set analysis on deceased patients, we describe the clinical phenotype and causes of death for thirty-seven individuals with various genetic causes of CDG. About a third of this cohort were affected with PMM2-CDG. All of the patients presented with multisystem features with involvement of the neurological system. The majority of patients involved in this study died during the first three years of life, and only four patients lived beyond ten years. The cause of death was unavailable for two patients, and about a third died secondary to cardiopulmonary failure. Progression of neurological involvement, sepsis and respiratory infection were also among the reported causes. Pericardial effusion was the primary cause of death for three infants affected with PMM2-CDG. This study emphasizes the importance of diagnosis and supportive care following the published monitoring and management guidelines for affected patients with CDG to optimize their health and development in the early stages of the disease.

Glycosylation of protein and lipids is an essential modification that is enabled by various enzymes, transporters, and structural proteins. Monosaccharide generation starts in the cytoplasm, with glycan synthesis occurring in the endoplasmic reticulum, and maturation of the glycan occurring in the Golgi apparatus. Genetic defects in any of these steps that result in clinical symptoms cause a heterogeneous group of disorders named Congenital Disorders of Glycosylation (CDG). These disorders have recently been classified into eight categories: 1) Disorders of monosaccharide synthesis and interconversion, 2) Disorders of nucleotide sugar synthesis and transport, 3) Disorders of N-linked protein glycosylation, 4) Disorders of O-linked protein glycosylation, 5) Disorders of lipid glycosylation, 6) Disorders of vesicular trafficking, 7) Disorders of multiple glycosylation pathways and 8) Disorders of glycoprotein/glycan degradation[1, 2]. Since the description of the first CDG by Jaeken et al. in 1980 in 2-year-old monozygotic twin sisters [1, 3, 4], which was later identified to be secondary to biallelic variants in PMM2 [5], this group of disorders expanded to more than 189 genetic causes of 200 CDG [2].

The majority of CDG have multisystem manifestations, although some CDG prominently affect single organ system such as EXT1/EXT2-CDG presenting with multiple osteochondromas [6, 7]. PMM2-CDG continues to be the most common N-linked CDG, with about 1000 reported patients [8] and an estimated prevalence of 1:20,000 [9]. A smaller number of patients are reported for other CDG with no reliably documented prevalence [10]. Developmental disability, hypotonia and failure to thrive as well as endocrine, hematological, gastrointestinal, and musculoskeletal involvement are among the most commonly shared multisystem manifestations of CDG [10–12]. The severity, age of onset and presentations are variable among CDG patients, even within a single genetic cause, and with reported intrafamilial variability. Therefore, considering this group of disorders in the differential diagnosis of patients with multisystem manifestations is recommended[10].

The natural history (NH) of various CDG disorders is not well established, given the rarity of these disorders. We recently reported a cross-sectional analysis of the Frontiers of CDG Consortium (FCDGC) NH study at year 5 of the study comprising 280 individuals with CDG[12]. Still, there remain knowledge gaps regarding the causes of mortality for individuals with CDG. In this report, we retrospectively review the longitudinal experience with CDG mortality over more than a decade of six United States-based CDG centers in FCDGC of the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Rare Disease Clinical Research Network (RDCRN), identifying thirty-seven deceased patients affected with different CDG. Understanding causes and patterns of mortality in CDG will better equip physicians to counsel newly diagnosed patients, guide prospective health monitoring, and identify opportunities for impactful health interventions.

The FCDGC NH study is an NIH-funded collaborative effort of twelve clinical sites in North America and Europe working in close collaboration with the CDG CARE and other patient advocacy groups. Since 2019, the clinical sites have recruited individuals with known and highly suspected CDG diagnoses into a NH study that collects demographic, clinical, biochemical, genetic, patient reported outcome and severity scoring data at initial and annual study visits.

Data is stored in a secure REDCap database. This NH protocol was approved by the NIH and the Mayo Institutional Review Board (IRB) that served as the central IRB with local context review at each FCDGC NH site (NCT04199000). All procedures were in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2013. To enroll, participants visited one of the site study teams either in person or virtually for a review of medical history, physical examination, and laboratory tests. They and/or their legally authorized representative provided written or electronically signed consent and/or assent using an IRB-approved consent/assent tool as indicated. The Mayo IRB also approved post-mortem subject enrollment for retrospective data and sample analysis without prospective collection of study questionnaires or procedures, but with historical data and samples used as they would for a living participant. These individuals were entered into a memorial arm of the NH study for retrospective data collection only.

The inclusion criteria for the memorial arm of the FCDGC NH study are: all individuals with an enzymatically or molecularly confirmed diagnosis of CDG or NGLY1 deficiency, or individuals whose laboratory values are highly suggestive of CDG, but who do not have a verified genetic diagnosis. This analysis includes the subset of individuals who were enrolled in the memorial arm of the study, or who were enrolled into the prospective arm of the NH study but subsequently died during the first 5 years of the NH study, and who had a CDG diagnosis clinically confirmed with consistent genetic data. Included individuals have at least one variant documented in a gene associated with a dominantly or X-linked inherited CDG, or two variants documented in a gene associated with a recessively inherited CDG.

Prospective NH study arm data collection is previously described[12]. For the memorial arm, medical records were retrospectively reviewed and clinical data from clinical visits, physical examinations, laboratory data, and any clinically obtained radiologic, laboratory, or other functional tests were collected and recorded into the REDcap database hosted in the NIH Cloud managed by the Data Management and Coordinating Center at the Children’s Hospital Medical Center and the University of Cincinnati. Causes of death as documented in clinical notes or from official death records were also collected. To address gaps in retrospective data collection, targeted queries for missing data relevant to cause of death were issued to each study site. Unfortunately, as some subjects died in non-FCDGC-affiliated hospitals or at home, some relevant data was unable to be obtained. Data cut for analysis was performed on May 31, 2023.

We analyzed data of thirty-seven individuals enrolled in the FCDGC NH study. Twenty-seven patients were retrospectively enrolled in the memorial arm of the study after their death, and another ten patients were enrolled in the prospective arm of the study and died after their enrollment. We used descriptive statistics to investigate the characteristics of the entire cohort, each CDG group and for PMM2-CDG, the diagnosis with the greatest number of individuals. Comparisons of prevalence of deceased patient genotypes and those represented in living patients in the prospective arm of the NH study were performed using Fisher’s Exact Test.

Data of deceased patients included in this study are summarized in supplemental table, including molecular variants, clinical manifestations and causes of the death.

We report thirty-seven deceased CDG patients: fourteen male patients and twenty-three female patients. The ten patients in the prospective arm of the NH study were enrolled at ages ranging from 3.5 months to 19 years. The most commonly reported genetic cause of CDG in this cohort is PMM2-CDG, affecting 12 patients, and reflecting the most common overall genetic cause of CDG. Four patients were affected with PIGN-CDG. PIGA-CDG and ALG6-CDG affected three patients each. Other less commonly reported types affecting two or fewer patients included ST3GAL5-CDG, ALG1-CDG, PIGQ-CDG, ALG3-CDG, MOGS-CDG, UGDH-CDG, SLC35A3-CDG, DPAGT1-CDG, DHDDS-CDG, ALG9-CDG and NGLY1-CDDG.

All deceased patients were reported to experience their first symptoms before one year of age. However, CDG diagnosis was delayed to the age of 17 years for a patient with ALG3-CDG, 16 years for a patient with ALG6-CDG, 8 years for a patient with ALG1-CDG and 4.5 years for a patient with PIGQ-CDG. The earliest diagnosis was made within the first month of life for patients with PIGN-CDG, PMM2-CDG, SLC35A3-CDG and PIGA-CDG. These early diagnoses largely reflect the severity of presenting clinical manifestations and more recent advances in the availability of rapid exome and genome based diagnostic testing. The diagnosis was established after death for three patients; two were affected with PIGN-CDG and they died at 7 months and 32 months of age and one with ALG1-CDG who died at 5 months of age. The age of diagnosis was not available even after chart review for six patients.

The patients in this study presented with multisystem involvement (Figure 1). Cardiac system involvement was reported in 54% of deceased patients. Cardiopulmonary failure is the most common documented cause of death in this study. The most common cardiac manifestation was pericardial effusion, a common manifestation in severely affected PMM2-CDG patients, with seven affected patients with PMM2-CDG, one patient with ALG1-CDG and one patient with ALG3-CDG. A PMM2-CDG patient (P6) in the memorial study arm died at 2 months of age due to cardiac tamponade, demonstrating the potential seriousness of this disease manifestation. Other reported features included cardiomyopathy, valvular involvement, arrythmia, and congenital heart defects such as patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), ventricular septal defect (VSD) and atrial septal defect (ASD).

The neurological system was the most commonly involved system in this cohort regardless of the genetic cause of CDG. It was the second most common cause of death, precipitated by seizures or progression of neurological manifestations. All the deceased patients were described to have a variable degree of neurological manifestations. Developmental disability (28/37), hypotonia (24/37) and epilepsy (23/37) were the most commonly reported neurological manifestations. Other features included structural brain anomalies, visual and hearing impairment and abnormal movement. A neural tube defect was reported for one patient affected with ALG3-CDG (P16). Neurological involvement is common in several types of CDG with seizure, developmental delay, peripheral neuropathy, ataxia and congenital brain malformation [13]. Movement disorder is thought to be underestimated in patients affected with CDG [14]. In this report, three patients manifested abnormal movements who were affected with PIGN-CDG (P24), PIGQ-CDG (P28), and NGLY1-CDDG (P37). Movement disorder is common in patients with NGLY1-CDDG, PIGQ-CDG [15, 16] and has been reported in some patients affected with PIGN-CDG [17].

Gastrointestinal (GI) features were the second most commonly reported manifestations in 84% of this cohort. Feeding difficulties and failure to thrive are among the most commonly shared features regardless of the genetic cause of CDG. Failure to thrive is a common GI feature in CDG patients and could be related to hypotonia, motor dysfunction or malabsorption [18]. Liver involvement was reported in ten patients in the form of elevated serum transaminases, hepatic failure, jaundice, fatty liver and hepatomegaly; one patient with PMM2-CDG (P4) had a hepatic hemangioma. Congenital defects of the GI system were primarily present in disorders of GPI -anchor biosynthesis: congenital diaphragmatic hernia (PIGN-, PIGA-, ALG3-CDG), imperforated anus (PIGN-CDG), congenital transesophageal fistula (PIGQ-CDG) and omphalocele (PIGA-CDG).

Genitourinary system involvement was reported in nearly 60% (22/37) of deceased patients. Nephrotic syndrome or proteinuria, hydronephrosis and renal dysfunction are among the reported abnormalities.

Involvement of musculoskeletal, endocrine, hematological, and respiratory systems are less common and reported in 30% or less of the cohort as detailed in supplemental table.

The youngest age of mortality in this cohort is 1 month for two patients with SLC35A3-CDG and PIGA-CDG, the oldest age was 21 years for a patient with ALG6-CDG. Over 2/3 of patients (27/37) included in this study died during the first three years of life with approximately 1/3 of those dying before 6 months of age, 1/3 dying between 6-12 months of age, and 1/3 dying between 1-3 years of age. Age of death was not reported for one patient affected with PMM2-CDG (Figure 2).

In the prospective natural history arm, the youngest age of mortality was 12 months for two siblings with PMM2-CDG who both carried the compound heterozygous variants p.Arg141His/p.Val231Met. However, in the memorial enrollment arm, three patients with the same PMM2 genotype were deceased at earlier ages (2, 5 and 11 months). The age of death for PMM2-CDG ranged from 2 months to 4.5 years. The three patients with ALG6-CDG had a wide age range and they survived to 12 months, 2 years and 21 years. While most CDG patients died in the first 3 years of life, relatively later deaths were reported for the two patients with PIGQ-CDG (3 years and 9 years) and the single patient with NGLY1-CDDG (12 years), a patient with ALG1-CDG (9 years) and a patient with ALG3-CDG (19 years). It should be noted that these ages do not define a limit of survival for each of these CDG, as older patients have been reported and there are older surviving patients that continue in the prospective arm of the FCDGC NH study.

The most commonly reported cause of the death in our study was cardiopulmonary failure followed by seizure and/or progression of neurological disease (Figure 3). Sepsis and respiratory infection were reported in 14% and 11%, respectively. The cause of death could not be determined in two patients of retrospective memorial subjects. Table 1 showed the different causes of death for the different CDG categories of patients involved in this study.

Only patients with PMM2-CDG were sufficiently represented in our cohort to draw any preliminary conclusions regarding the relationship between genotype and mortality (Table 2). Twelve patients of this cohort and ninety-four patients of the prospective natural history arm were affected with PMM2-CDG. The genotypes of all these patients are composed of compound heterozygous variants. The most common single variant in PMM2 is the p.Arg141His variant which present in 9/12 patients (75%) in the memorial arm and 63/94 (67%) in the prospective arm. This variant is a known as a severe pathogenic variant [19]. The most commonly reported single genotype in the deceased group was the compound heterozygous variants p.Arg141His and p.Val231Met in six patients (half of the deceased PMM2-CDG patients). In the FCDGC prospective natural history study, this genotype is reported in five patients who are currently alive at ages 3, 7 and 8 years and two patients at 11 years at the time of this report, out of 94 patients currently living with PMM2-CDG (Fisher’s Exact Test P-value <0.001). Thus, PMM2-CDG patients who are compound heterozygous for p.Arg141His and p.Val231Met appear to be at risk for early mortality. The PMM2 compound heterozygous variants p.Phe119Leu and p.Arg141His were found in only one patient of this study (P8) who died at the age of 15 months with respiratory failure, and this compound heterozygous genotype is present in ten living patients currently enrolled in the prospective NH study with current ages ranging from 12 months to 37 years (Fisher’s Exact Test P-value =1). While not statistically significant, so many patients living with the p.Phe119Leu variant suggests they may have a decreased risk of early mortality. The compound heterozygous p.Gly208Ala and p.Arg141His genotype was identified in two patients in the natural history study who were 5 and 13 years of age at the time of this report, and also has been found in another two patients of this cohort who were deceased at the ages of 7 months and 4.5 years (Fisher’s Exact Test P-value =0.06). The close to statistically significant P-value may suggest that this genotype is associated with an increased risk of mortality. Other compound heterozygous PMM2 genotypes with the p.Arg141His variant represented in more than two PMM2-CDG patients in the prospective natural history study and not present in our cohort of deceased patients include p.Pro113Leu (n=12) and p.Asn216Ile (n=5), suggesting these may be more mild genotypes protective against early mortality. The second most commonly reported genetic cause of CDG in this deceased cohort was PIGN-CDG, affecting four patients. However, none of these patients shared the same genotype.

Nijmegen pediatric CDG rating scale (NPCRS) was established in 2011 to objectively evaluate disease severity and progression in CDG. Initially, it was validated for the pediatric age group up to 18 years of age. The scale is based on three domains: current function, system involvement and current clinical status; and the scoring is categorized into mild (0-14), moderate (15-25) and severe (>26) [20]. In 2022, the NPCRS was updated to enable monitoring of disease progression into adulthood as well as to standardize the scoring across all age ranges [21].

In this study, the NPCRS was available for fourteen patients (38%). Most scores fell in the severe category ranging from 27 to 49. The median score of living CDG patients in the prospective arm is in the moderate range with a score of 20[12]. Only one deceased patient (P9) had a score of in the moderate category, score of 25, recorded at the age of 7 months shortly before death. His moderate score likely reflects a lack of achieved developmental progress by the time of his death and highlights a shortcoming of the scale in very young patients.

This study identified the cause of mortality for thirty-seven patients affected with different genetic causes of CDG who were enrolled in the FCDGC NH study. It is limited by the retrospective nature of the data collection, especially for patients enrolled in the memorial arm, where some important clinical and laboratory data as well as clear description of causes of death for some patients was missing, especially for patients diagnosed post-mortem or who died out of the hospital. We were also limited by IRB approval to retrospectively include only patient’s whose families who had previously expressed interest in enrolling their child with CDG into a research study prior to death or parents who actively reach out to our study team and offer their child’s retrospective data. We did not attempt a broader enrollment of deceased CDG individuals, such as through patient advocacy group contacts. Each of these limitations could introduce bias into our analysis.

The majority of patients in this study had genetic defects disrupting N-linked glycosylation, with PMM2-CDG remaining the most commonly reported type representing 32.4% of this cohort, followed by ALG6-CDG affecting three patients. GPI-anchor biosynthesis defects were the second most represented category with 24.3% of the cohort represented by these disorders. Many of the GPI-anchor biosynthesis defect patients had congenital anomalies. A high rate of early mortality has been reported in PIGA-CDG with respiratory failure as the most common cause [22].

Given that PMM2-CDG is the most commonly reported type, some genotype-phenotype correlations have been identified. Although the most common genotype reported is p.Arg141His/p.Phe119Leu [23], this combination was only found in one patient of this study (P8) who died at 15 months of age. In contrast, ten patients with this genotype are enrolled in the NH study currently living. These findings could support longer survival associated with this genotype. Both p.Val231Met and p.Val129 Met variants have been associated with a severe phenotype [24]. However, the p.Val129Met variant was found in only one patient in combination with p.Glu54fs (P4) in this study but in four currently living patients in the natural history study with the oldest patient alive at the age of 34 years; all these living patients carry the compound heterozygous genotype of p.Arg141His and p.Val129Met. The p.Val231Met is the second most commonly reported variant in this cohort in seven deceased patients (six of which carry the variant in combination with p.Arg141His), but six patients enrolled in the NH study are living with ages ranging from 12 months to 11 years, suggesting increased mortality risk.

Early diagnosis of CDG is important to start supportive care and initiate appropriate surveillance and targeted therapies if available. The patients in this study manifested their first related clinical features during infancy, but the age of diagnosis was variable and five patients were diagnosed after 2 years of age (2, 4.5, 8, 16 and 17 years of age). Establishing the diagnosis for these disorders can be challenging due to the multisystem nature of the disease [25]. Carbohydrate deficient transferrin (CDT) is widely available screening test for N-linked CDG, however, the types affecting O-glycosylation and GPI-anchor biosynthesis are missed using this method. With the continuous improvement of molecular technology at lower costs, diagnosing the majority of these disorders relies mainly on genetic testing and has been facilitated more recently due to advances in availability of exome and genome-based diagnostics. Therefore, molecular testing should be considered as a diagnostic test if CDG is suspected and biochemical studies are negative. Another challenge that could contribute to the delayed diagnosis is that newborn screening via biochemical markers or genomic testing is currently unavailable for CDG, unlike many intermediary metabolic and lysosomal storage diseases. Development and validation of therapeutics that alter CDG disease course are necessary before CDG can be considered for addition to NBS panels as a primary target.

While most CDG are not directly treatable, many of the medical manifestations of CDG may be. Given the variability in organ system manifestations among CDG patients, appropriate monitoring for health impacts of CDG may identify treatable manifestations. Clinical monitoring guidelines have been curated by international groups of CDG experts and published for several of the more common genetic causes of CDG: PMM2-CDG, MPI-CDG, PGM1-CDG, ALG13-CDG, ALG3-CDG, ALG8-CDG [26–31]. Our study suggests some simple screening interventions to implement following initial diagnosis, particularly during the first 3 years of age given the increased risk of mortality. Patients should be evaluated with the NPCRS, and patients scoring in the severe category be triaged for more frequent monitoring during the first 3 years of life. Special attention should also be paid to young patients (<6 months) with high moderate scores due to the NPCRS under-identifying severity in young patients. Patients particularly with PMM2-CDG, but also other type I N-linked disorders, should be screened with echocardiogram for pericardial effusions and monitored for potential development of cardiac tamponade. Patients with GPI-anchor biosynthesis defects should be screened with an echocardiogram and abdominal ultrasound at diagnosis to identify potential congenital anomalies. Given the frequency of cardiopulmonary failure as a cause of death, careful attention should be given to cardiac and pulmonary functioning in CDG patients, particularly in the setting of illness.
