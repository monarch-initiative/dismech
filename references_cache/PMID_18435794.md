# Severe mandibuloacral dysplasia caused by novel compound heterozygous ZMPSTE24 mutations in two Japanese siblings.
**Authors:** Miyoshi Y, Akagi M, Agarwal AK, Namba N, Kato-Nishimura K, Mohri I, Yamagata M, Nakajima S, Mushiake S, Shima M, Auchus RJ, Taniike M, Garg A, Ozono K
**Journal:** Clin Genet (2008)
**DOI:** [10.1111/j.1399-0004.2008.00992.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1399-0004.2008.00992.x)

## Content

Mandibuloacral dysplasia (MAD) is a rare autosomal recessive progeroid syndrome, characterized by mandibular hypoplasia, acroosteolysis affecting distal phalanges and clavicles, delayed closure of the cranial sutures, atrophic skin, and lipodystrophy. Recently, mutations in lamin A/C (LMNA) and zinc metalloprotease (ZMPSTE24), involved in post-translational processing of prelamin A to mature lamin A, have been identified in MAD kindreds. We now report novel compound heterozygous mutations in exon 1 (c.121C>T; p.Q41X) and exon 6 (c.743C>T; p.P248L) in ZMPSTE24 in two Japanese sisters, 7- and 3-year old, with severe MAD and characteristic facies and atrophic skin. The older sister had lipodystrophy affecting the chest and thighs but sparing abdomen. Their parents and a brother, who were healthy, had heterozygous mutations. The missense mutation, P248L, was not found in 100 normal subjects of Japanese origin. The mutant Q41X was inactive in a yeast halo assay; however, the mutant P248L retained near normal ZMPSTE24 activity. Immunoblots demonstrated accumulation of prelamin A in the patients' cell lysates from lymphoblasts. The lymphoblasts from the patients also revealed less intense staining for lamin A/C on immunofluorescence. We conclude that ZMPSTE24 deficiency results in accumulation of farnesylated prelamin A, which may be responsible for cellular toxicity and the MAD phenotype.

Keywords: lamin A, lipodystrophy, mandibuloacral dysplasia, progeroid syndrome, ZMPSTE24

Mandibuloacral dysplasia (MAD) is a rare autosomal recessive, heterogeneous progeroid syndrome. Prominent clinical features include craniofacial anomalies such as mandibular hypoplasia, dental overcrowding, bird-like facies with prominent eyes, and thin beaked nose (1, 2). Skeletal anomalies include delayed closure of the cranial sutures, as well as progressive osteolysis of the terminal phalanges and clavicles. Skin anomalies consist of atrophy, mottled hyperpigmentation, and lipodystrophy. Stiff joints and post-natal growth retardation have been reported in many cases. The patients have normal intelligence. Most of the symptoms first appear around the age of 4 years in many reported cases, and these clinical manifestations are progressive. Geographically, many cases were reported from Italy, suggesting the existence of a founder effect (3, 4); however, several cases outside Italy have also been reported (5–8).

Two distinct patterns of lipodystrophy have been reported in patients with MAD: type A (OMIM 248370) and type B (OMIM 608612) (5). Type A lipodystrophy is characterized by marked loss of subcutaneous (SC) fat from the extremities with normal or slight excess in the neck and truncal regions. Patients with MAD and type A lipodystrophy have been reported to harbor mutations in the LMNA gene (OMIM 150330) on chromosome 1q21, which encodes two nuclear envelope proteins, lamin A and C (6–10). Type B lipodystrophy is characterized by generalized loss of SC fat involving the face, trunk, and extremities. Patients withMAD and type B lipodystrophy have been reported to carry compound heterozygous mutations in the gene encoding an endoprotease, zinc metalloproteinase (ZMPSTE24) (OMIM 606480), on chromosome 1p34 (11). ZMPSTE24 is involved in posttranslational processing of prelamin A to mature lamin A (12); thus, accumulation of prelamin A is proposed to be responsible for the phenotype.

Mutations in LMNA gene are also associated with other progeroid syndromes such as Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome(HGPS), restrictive dermopathy (RD), progeria-associated arthropathy, and atypical progeroid syndrome (also referred to as atypical Werner’s syndrome) (13–16). Mutations in ZMPSTE24 gene are also associated with RD (17, 18). There are many overlapping features among patients with MAD, HGPS, RD and atypical progeroid syndrome, although each presents with some unique phenotypic features. So far, 21 MAD patients have been reported to have LMNA mutations (6–10), but only 3 MAD patients have been reported with ZMPSTE24 mutations (11, 19, 20). Here, we report two Japanese siblings with MAD resulting from novel compound heterozygous mutations in ZMPSTE24.

The studies were approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Osaka University Hospital and the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center at Dallas. Each participant or their legal guardian gave a written informed consent for all studies and photographs.

Genomic DNA was isolated from the subjects’ peripheral whole blood cells using Dr. GenTLE (Takara Bio, Otsu, Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Direct sequencing of the entire coding region and the flanking exon—intron boundaries of the LMNA and ZMPSTE24 was first conducted in the proband, and the mutations were later confirmed in the younger sister. The primer sets that amplify each exon of LMNA and ZMPSTE24 genes were designed from published sequence information and exons were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) as previously described (11, 17, 21, 22). ZMPSTE24 coding sequence is available at http://genome.ucsc.edu/. For segregation analyses, only selected exons were sequenced in the family members. The protein sequences of ZMPSTE24 in various species were obtained from the website of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (http://www. ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Genbank).

The generation of yeast expression vector containing the human ZMPSTE24 gene has been described (11, 19). The mutations Q41X and P248L were generated using the expression vector, pRS416GPD-hZMPSTE24, as a template using QuickChange Mutagenesis Kit (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The mutation Q41X was generated with primer pairs 5′-CCTAGCACAGCGGTAGAGAAGGATATAT-3′ (sense) and 5′-ATATATCCTTCTCTACCGCTGTGCTAGG-3′ (antisense) and mutation P248L was generated with primer pairs 5′-GAGTATTGACTTTCTTTTGACGAAGG-3′ and 5′-CCTTCGTCAAAAGAAAGTCAATACTCTTTGCC-3′. The resulting yeast expression vectors were again sequenced to ensure that no PCR errors had occurred.

To determine if the ZMPSTE24 mutation is active in complementing the yeast STE24 (22,24), the yeast expression plasmids pRS416GPD-hZMPSTE24-Q41X and pRS416GPD-hZMPSTE24-P248L were transformed into yeast strain SM3614 (MATa ste24Δrce1Δ) (a kind gift of Dr S. Michaelis) and grown on selective medium. Liquid cultures were concentrated, and 1 µl aliquots of serial dilutions was then spotted on to a lawn of mating yeast (strain XBH8-2C, MATα) on yeast peptone dextrose medium with 0.043% Triton X-100 (24, 25), then allowed to grow for 2 days at 30°C. The production of mature a-factor is proportional to the zone of growth inhibition (halo) formed.

Lymphocytes were isolated from the MAD patients (patients 1 and 2) and a healthy volunteer (control) using Ficoll-Paque PLUS (GE Healthcare Bio-Science, Piscataway, NJ) and were immortalized by infecting with Epstein–Barr virus. The cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (Nikken Bio Medical Laboratory, Kyoto, Japan) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin and maintained at 37°C and 5%CO2.

Henrietta-Lacks’ cervical cancer cells (HeLa) and lymphoblast cell extracts were prepared by harvesting cells in lysis buffer consisting of 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 20 mM NaF and protease inhibitors cocktail (Roche, Indianapolis, IN). The cell lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 g for 15 min at room temperature, and protein concentrations were determined using Bio-Rad DC protein assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Total proteins (50 µg) were denatured by heating the samples at 100°C for 10 min and resolved on 7.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) gel. Resolved proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Immobilon; Millipore, Billerica, MA) and blocked with 5% fat-free milk overnight at 4°C. The blots were probed with 1:5000 diluted goat anti-lamin A/C (N-18; Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc., Santa Cruz, CA). Proteins were detected with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (1:2500 dilution) and ECL Plus chemiluminescence system (Amersham Pharmacia, Buckinghamshire, UK). The same blot was stripped using the Restore stripping buffer (Pierce, Rockford, IL) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, reprobed with 1:1000 diluted anti-actin antibody (catalog number A 2066; Sigma, St. Louis, MO), and detected with goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) conjugated with horseradish peroxidase at a dilution of 1:2500.

Approximately 100,000 lymphoblast cells were seeded onto coverslips coated with poly-L-lysine (Sigma) placed in a six-well plate. Cells were allowed to adhere to the coverslip for 1 h and were then fixed with methanol (−20°C), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% fetal calf serum and 0.03% bovine serum albumin in phosphate-buffered saline. The permeabilized cells were incubated with an antilamin A/C antibody at 1:100 dilution (H-110; Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc., Santa Cruz, CA) followed by Alexa Fluor 488, goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody at 1:100 dilution (Molecular Probes/Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Primary and secondary antibodies were incubated for 1 h each at 37°C. The slides then were incubated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI at 1 µg/ml) for 1 min and mounted with Aqua Poly/Mount (Polysciences Inc., Warrington, PA). Cell imaging was performed by Zeiss Axiovert 100M at similar exposure times. The digital images were prepared using ImageJ.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed using a 1.5-Tesla imaging device (GE Health Care, Milwaukee, WI). The body was surveyed employing contiguous, axial 5-mm slices, a T1-weighted spin echo sequence [633/8, TR (repetition time)/TE (echo time)], a eight-channel body array coil, 256 × 192 matrix, 0.9 × 0.9 × 5 mm voxel, and one excitation. Fat was easily identified as areas of relatively high signal intensity on images.

This 7-year-old female was the first child of healthy and unrelated parents of Japanese origin. She was born at 37 weeks of gestation, with a length of 40 cm (−3.6 SD), a weight of 2230 g (−1.1 SD) and an occipitofrontal circumference (OFC) of 34 cm (+0.7 SD). She had a mild feeding problem and poor weight gain in the infantile period (Fig. 1). Atrophic skin, mandibular hypoplasia and prominent eyes were noted at birth. The right clavicle was accidentally fractured at the age of 1 month. She had a characteristic facial appearance, including mandibular hypoplasia, prominent eyes, and pointed nose. The skin was atrophic, shiny and taut with mottled hyperpigmentation. A contracture developed in the fifth fingers from age of 1 month, and hypoplasia of terminal phalanges was noticed at the age of 1 year. Radiographs of the head showed underdevelopment of the skull and wormian bones at the age of 3 months. Acroosteolysis was not obvious at 3 months but was clearly noticeable at the age of 15 months. These physical and radiological features led to the diagnosis of MAD.

In the affected subjects from this pedigree, no mutations were demonstrated in LMNA gene; however, we found novel compound heterozygous mutations in ZMPSTE24 gene (Fig. 3). In exon 1, the heterozygous c.121C>T substitution resulted in p.Q41X, and in exon 6, the heterozygous c.743C->T substitution caused p.P248L (Fig. 3 a,b). A synonymous heterozygous single nucleotide polymorphism in exon 6 (rs 2076697, c.651T>C; p.D217D) was noted in all family members except in the father. Each mutation segregated in Mendelian fashion, and the heterozygous carriers, parents and a brother had normal phenotype. These mutations were not detected in 200 chromosomes from unrelated subjects of Japanese ancestry. Partial alignment of ZMPSTE24 sequences from various species showed that the P248 is well conserved, indicating the important role of the residue in function of the enzyme (Fig. 3c). In addition, the flanking residues ‘F’ and ‘L’ were also conserved, suggesting that the tripeptide FPL may be important for the enzymatic activity.

Human ZMPSTE24 expressed in yeast also processes a-factor and complement the mating defect of the haploid MATα yeast strain SM3614 lacking the STE24 and RCE1 genes (27, 28). The secreted mature a-factor diffuses into the medium and arrests the growth of the MATα strain, leaving a ‘halo’ surrounding the MATα strain. The size of the halo is roughly proportional to the production of the mature a-factor; however, modest reductions in activity may produce a normal-sized halo as a-factor is normally secreted in excess. The ste24Δ rcelΔ yeast when transfected with an empty vector did not produce a zone of growth inhibition (halo). Expression of human ZMPSTE24 (wild type) produced a halo surrounding the yeast colony (Fig. 4a). The ZMPSTE24, Q41X mutation, was inactive in this assay; however, the mutant P248L in our assay appears to produce a halo of comparable size as the wild-type ZMPSTE24 (Fig. 4a), even when examined at reduced yeast concentration after dilution (data not shown).

We report that our patients had two novel heterozygous mutations in the ZMPSTE24 gene, one of them being a null mutation, Q41X, and the other a missense mutation, P248L. Previously, compound heterozygous ZMPSTE24 mutation F361fsX379 was found with N265S mutation in two patients, and with W340R mutation in one patient presenting with MAD phenotype (11, 19, 20). Another patient with an overlapping phenotype of HGPS and MAD had a homozygous V402SfsX403 ZMPSTE24 mutation (29). This patient also harbored a heterozygous LMNA R654X mutation, which was predicted to have ameliorated the phenotype somewhat due to inability of R645X mutant lamin A to be farnesylated (29). On the other hand, all patients with RD associated with ZMPSTE24 mutations have had homozygous null F361fsX379 mutation or have been compound heterozygotes with an F361fsX379 mutation in association with other ostensibly null mutations such as Q417X, P99fsX38 or L91-L209del mutations (17).

The residue proline 248 lies adjacent to the sixth membrane-spanning domain of ZMPSTE24, and mutation P248L was not detected in the 100 unrelated subjects of Japanese ancestry. Additionally, the proline 248 is well conserved across all the species examined, which supports that this mutation is a disease-causing mutation. The results of the yeast halo assay demonstrated that the ZMPSTE24 mutation, Q41X, was totally inactive; however, the mutant P248L was nearly as active as the wild-type ZMPSTE24. Previously, two heterozygous mutations, N265S and W340R, were shown to have slightly reduced ZMPSTE24 enzymatic activity compared with the wild-type construct in the same yeast halo assay. Nonetheless, documentation of prelamin A accumulation in the lysates from the patients’ lymphocytes clearly suggests ZMPSTE24 deficiency. These results suggest that haploinsufficiency combined with even a minor loss of enzyme activity in the remaining allele may be sufficient to give rise to clinical symptoms of MAD. Because the halo assay is not very sensitive, it is likely that the mutant P248L may have lost 20–25% activity compared with the wild type, which may not be observed in this assay. Furthermore, the structural requirements of ZMPSTE24 for yeast a-factor processing appear to be less stringent than those for prelamin A cleavage. Other sensitive assays to measure ZMPSTE24 activity in vitro will clarify the molecular pathogenesis of these cases. Immunostaining of lymphoblasts with lamin A/C antibodies shows slightly reduced staining in the affected patients when compared with the unrelated control lymphoblasts. However, the nuclear morphology remains unaltered. The significance of this observation requires further confirmation in the dermal fibroblasts from these patients.

Our patients’ phenotype is more severe than that of MAD patients with LMNA mutations, who usually have normal development during the first 4 years of life. Our patients developed skeletal abnormalities, skin atrophy and joint contractures earlier than age 2 years (1). Our patients, who presented with characteristic facies and atrophic skin at birth, represented severe expressions of MAD in early childhood. Skin atrophy and joint rigidity were more severe in the elder sister who also developed osteolysis of the right femoral head.

MAD patients with LMNA mutations have been reported to have lipodystrophy of the extremities with sparing of the head, neck and trunk. Previously, two MAD patients with ZMPSTE24 mutations were reported to have generalized lipodystrophy on the basis of physical examination, but systematic evaluation of their body fat distribution using anthropometric measurements, DXA or MRI had not been conducted (11, 20). The third patient with severe progeroid and MAD phenotype due to ZMPSTE24 mutations also died prematurely at the age of 2 years and 9 months due to respiratory failure. She was reported to have prominent calf muscles due to lipodystrophy (19). Given the lack of objective data related to lipodystrophy in MAD patients with ZMPSTE24 mutations, we attempted to characterize the lipodystrophy in our patients. Phenotypically, SC fat was nearly absent in patient 1 with the exception of the cheeks, arms and thigh, and this disproportionately distributed adipose tissue was also demonstrated by the DXA scan. MRI studies revealed that patient 1 had slight reduction of SC fat on the anterior and lateral regions of the thigh and gluteal region and trunk SC fat on the extremities, but SC fat in the abdomen was normal. Unfortunately, in our patients, due to tight skin, it was hard to evaluate skinfold thickness. However, our patients may be too young, at the age of 3 and 7 years, to show the full phenotype of lipodystrophy. In patients with MAD due to LMNA mutations, the onset of lipodystrophy occurs at puberty, and it is possible that our patients may also lose more SC fat during puberty. Thus, further SC fat loss in these two patients will illuminate the natural history of lipodystrophy in MAD, type B.

Previously, two MAD patients with ZMPSTE24 mutations were reported to have SC calcified nodules in the phalanges and developed focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, resulting in end-stage renal disease (20). Our patients have not developed any SC nodules in the phalanges. Even though both of them have normal serum creatinine values, both had microhematuria suggestive of renal involvement.

At present, our patients have normal glucose tolerance and lipid profiles. Previously, MAD patients with lipodystrophy and LMNA mutations have been reported to have insulin resistance, mild glucose intolerance, and hyperlipidemia (30). Recently, Simha et al. reported four MAD patients with partial lipodystrophy (6), who had normal glucose tolerance but had fasting and post-prandial hyper-insulinemia. Elevated serum triglycerides with low levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol were noted in three subjects (6). However, similar data have not been available from MAD patients with ZMPSTE24 mutations. It is likely that, if the lipodystrophy progresses with puberty, our patients may also develop insulin resistance and dyslipidemia.

Deficiency of Zmpste24 in mice causes accumulation of prelamin A and phenotypic features similar to those of MAD. Pendas et al. (31) reported partial lipodystrophy, muscular dystrophy, and cardiomyopathy in Zmpste24-deficient mice. Bergo et al. (32) reported that Zmpste24-deficient mice gained weight slowly and exhibited progressive hair loss, multiple spontaneous bone fractures, and muscle weakness. The toxicity is most likely due to accumulation of farnesylated prelamin A at the nuclear pore complexes, resulting in abnormal nuclear morphology and premature cell death (33). Therefore, Fong et al. (34) tested the efficacy of farnesyltransferase inhibitors (FTIs) in Zmpste24-deficient mice and reported improved body weight, grip strength, bone integrity, and percent survival at 20 weeks of age. These results suggest that FTIs decrease the accumulation of farnesylated prelamin A and may be potentially beneficial in patients with ZMPSTE24 deficiency.

In summary, we report two Japanese siblings with MAD who harbor novel compound heterozygous mutations in the ZMPSTE24 gene. In the 7-year-old girl, SC fat was almost totally absent with the exception of the cheeks, arms and thighs. Our patients had more severe phenotypes than those observed in MAD patients with LMNA mutations. Accumulation of farnesylated prelamin A may be responsible for toxicity in these patients.
