</div><div id="deafness-overview.Audiometric_and_Clinic"><h2 id="_deafness-overview_Audiometric_and_Clinic_">1.  Audiometric and Clinical Aspects of Hearing Loss</h2><p>Hearing loss can be characterized by type, onset, severity, and frequency.</p><p>
<b>Type</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Conductive hearing loss, due to abnormalities of the external ear and/or the ossicles of the middle ear</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Sensorineural hearing loss due to malfunction of the inner ear structures (i.e., cochlea or auditory nerve, with potential for vestibular dysfunction)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Mixed hearing loss, a combination of conductive and sensorineural hearing loss</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Central auditory dysfunction, due to damage or dysfunction at the level of the eighth cranial nerve (auditory nerve), the auditory brain stem (medulla, pons, and midbrain), or the cerebral cortex</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Onset</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Congenital (present at birth)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Prelingual (occurring before the acquisition of speech)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Postlingual (occurring after the acquisition of speech)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Adult (occurring after age 18 years)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Presbycusis (age-related hearing loss that typically occurs after middle age)</div></li></ul><p><b>Severity.</b> Hearing is measured in decibels (dB). The threshold or 0 dB mark for each frequency refers to the level at which normal young adults perceive a tone burst 50% of the time. Hearing is considered normal if an individual's thresholds are within 15 dB of normal thresholds. <b>Laterality</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Bilateral symmetric</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Bilateral asymmetric</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Unilateral</div></li></ul><p><b>Configuration or shape or audioprofile</b> (degree and pattern of hearing loss across frequencies)</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Low frequency, upsloping, or rising</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Mid-frequency, "cookie bite"</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>High frequency, downsloping</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Flat</div></li></ul><p><b>Assessment of the auditory system.</b> Formal hearing assessment is performed by audiologists who are trained to adapt their clinical evaluation to the ability of the individual being tested. The method of assessment of hearing depends on the individual's age or developmental age and neurologic status (i.e., ability to respond to queries from the audiologist).</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Audiometric measures</b> based on the individual's age or ability to respond to queries from the audiologist include:</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Visual response audiometry: age &#x0003c;2.5 years</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Conditioned play audiometry: age 2.5-5 years</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Conventional audiometry: age &#x0003e;5 years</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Objective measures,</b> which do not require participation of the individual, are typically used in young children or individuals who are unable to participate in other audiometric testing. They include:</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Auditory brain stem response (ABR, also called brain stem auditory evoked response, or BAER), which measures physiologic response of the auditory nerve, brain stem, and brain to varying auditory stimuli. Automated ABR may be used in newborn hearing screening. Diagnostic ABR is used for assessment of transmission of auditory stimuli from the ear to the brain.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Otoacoustic emissions (OAE), which measure the response of the cochlea to auditory stimulus. OAE may be used in newborn hearing screening.</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Conduction tests</b> compare sound conduction through air and through bone to differentiate middle ear dysfunction (conductive hearing loss) from inner ear dysfunction (sensorineural hearing loss).</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Air conduction tests measure responses from auditory stimuli presented through the external auditory canal.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Bone conduction tests measure responses from auditory stimuli transmitted through the bone near the inner ear.</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Tympanometry</b> assesses the function of the tympanic membrane and middle ear and is part of routine audiometric evaluation performed by an audiologist. The goal is to differentiate conductive hearing loss from sensorineural hearing loss.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Vestibular function</b> testing evaluates the integrity of the peripheral vestibular system. This testing is typically performed by audiologists with specialized training and evaluates the ability of the inner ear to maintain balance and coordinate the vestibular-ocular pathways that are integral in maintenance of head position in space. Imbalance, dizziness, and vertigo are common among individuals with hearing loss given that the cochlea (responsible for hearing) and the vestibular organs (the saccule, utricle, and semicircular canals) share much of the same physiology and developmental origin.</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Definitions of hearing loss and deafness</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Hearing loss.</b> Auditory <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> characterized by any degree of loss of the ability to hear</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>deaf (small "d").</b> In this <i>GeneReview</i>, "deaf" describes an auditory <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> characterized by a total or near total loss of the ability to hear.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Deaf (always a capital "D").</b> A community with a distinct culture and visual language shared by the experience of being deaf or hard of hearing. The Deaf can include persons who are deaf, hard of hearing, and hearing. In the United States, members of the Deaf community use American Sign Language (ASL). Similar communities and sign languages exist in other countries. Members of the Deaf community (i.e., the Deaf) do NOT consider themselves to be hearing "impaired," nor do they feel that they have a hearing "loss." Their deafness is not considered a pathology or disease to be treated or cured.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Hard of hearing.</b> Auditory <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> characterized by a partial loss of the ability to hear. It is sometimes used by the Deaf to signify that a person has some usable hearing &#x02013; anything from mild to severe hearing loss.</div></li></ul></div><div id="deafness-overview.Causes_of_Genetic_Hear"><h2 id="_deafness-overview_Causes_of_Genetic_Hear_">2.  Causes of Genetic Hearing Loss</h2><p>Hearing loss is not a diagnosis; rather, hearing loss is a symptom of an underlying pathologic change to the auditory system.</p><p>Eighty percent of prelingual hearing loss is attributed to genetic causes; likewise, a genetic cause can be identified in a signification proportion of individuals with adult-onset hearing loss (see <a class="figpopup" href="/books/NBK1434/figure/deafness-overview.F1/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-figpopup="figdeafnessoverviewF1" rid-ob="figobdeafnessoverviewF1">Figure 1</a>). Determining the genetic cause of hearing loss provides information for individuals and their families, including prognosis (e.g., whether hearing loss is static or progressive), whether hearing loss is nonsyndromic or <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/syndromic/">syndromic</a> (i.e., associated with other medical issues), habilitation options and other supportive care, availability of disease-specific therapies (e.g., <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene-therapy/">gene therapy</a> for <a href="/books/n/gene/dfnb9/"><i>OTOF</i>-related hearing loss</a>), and <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genetic-counseling/">genetic counseling</a> regarding chance of recurrence. Habilitation for hearing loss includes improved access to sound through hearing aids or cochlear implants and, when desired, exposure to and teaching of American Sign Language.</p><div class="iconblock whole_rhythm clearfix ten_col fig" id="figdeafnessoverviewF1" co-legend-rid="figlgnddeafnessoverviewF1"><a href="/books/NBK1434/figure/deafness-overview.F1/?report=objectonly" target="object" title="Figure 1. " class="img_link icnblk_img figpopup" rid-figpopup="figdeafnessoverviewF1" rid-ob="figobdeafnessoverviewF1"><img class="small-thumb" src="/books/NBK1434/bin/deafness-overview-Image001.gif" src-large="/books/NBK1434/bin/deafness-overview-Image001.jpg" alt="Figure 1. " /></a><div class="icnblk_cntnt" id="figlgnddeafnessoverviewF1"><h4 id="deafness-overview.F1"><a href="/books/NBK1434/figure/deafness-overview.F1/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobdeafnessoverviewF1">Figure 1. </a></h4><p class="float-caption no_bottom_margin">Causes of prelingual hearing loss in developed countries [Morton &#x00026; Nance 2006, Sheffield &#x00026; Smith 2019] </p></div></div><p><b>Nonsyndromic hearing loss</b> is not associated with visible abnormalities of the external ear or related medical findings; however, it can be associated with abnormalities of the middle ear and/or inner ear.</p><p><b>Syndromic hearing loss</b> is associated with any combination of malformations of the external ear or malformations or medical findings involving other organs or organ systems.</p><p><b>Nonsyndromic hearing loss "mimics"</b> refers to <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/syndromic/">syndromic</a> hearing loss that "mimics" nonsyndromic hearing loss in the early presentation period, when other organ system involvement may not be evident on medical history and/or physical examination. Of note, approximately 20% of children with hearing loss as their only initial clinical feature (i.e., who apparently have nonsyndromic hearing loss) will subsequently be diagnosed with syndromic hearing loss [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.sloanheggen.2016.441">Sloan Heggen et al 2016</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.downie.2020.587">Downie et al 2020</a>].</p><div id="deafness-overview.Nonsyndromic_Hearing_L"><h3>Nonsyndromic Hearing Loss</h3><p>As of this writing, more than 150 genes are associated with nonsyndromic hearing loss (a regularly updated, comprehensive list of identified nonsyndromic hearing loss genes is available at the <a href="https://hereditaryhearingloss.org/" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">Hereditary Hearing Loss Homepage</a>).</p><p>Approximately 70% of prelingual genetic hearing loss is nonsyndromic (see <a class="figpopup" href="/books/NBK1434/figure/deafness-overview.F1/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-figpopup="figdeafnessoverviewF1" rid-ob="figobdeafnessoverviewF1">Figure 1</a>) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.smith.2005.879">Smith et al 2005</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.sheffield.2019.a033258">Sheffield &#x00026; Smith 2019</a>] (<a href="https://perspectivesinmedicine.cshlp.org/content/9/9/a033258.full.pdf+html" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">full text</a>). In most individuals with nonsyndromic genetic hearing loss (80%), hearing loss is associated with <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> pathogenic variants and inherited in an <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-recessive/">autosomal recessive</a> manner. Nonsyndromic hearing loss may also be inherited in an <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-dominant/">autosomal dominant</a> manner (19%) or, rarely, associated with mitochondrial or <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/x-linked/">X-linked</a> inheritance (&#x0003c;1%). Although comparable data are not available for postlingual nonsyndromic genetic hearing loss, autosomal dominant inheritance is most common.</p><p><b>Note:</b> Nonsyndromic hearing impairment may be referred to by the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> involved (e.g., <i>OTOF</i>-related deafness) or by the genetic <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/locus/">locus</a> (e.g., DFNB9). Nonsyndromic deafness loci are designated DFN (for <b>D</b>ea<b>FN</b>ess) and further classified by <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/mode-of-inheritance/">mode of inheritance</a> (DFN<b>A</b>: <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-dominant/">autosomal dominant</a>; DFN<b>B</b>: <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-recessive/">autosomal recessive</a>; DFN<b>X</b>: <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/x-linked/">X-linked</a>) and a number indicating the numeric locus assigned in the course of gene mapping.</p><div id="deafness-overview.Autosomal_Recessive_No"><h4>Autosomal Recessive Nonsyndromic Hearing Loss</h4><p>As of this writing, more than 80 genes have been associated with <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-recessive/">autosomal recessive</a> nonsyndromic hearing loss.</p><p>In general, <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-recessive/">autosomal recessive</a> nonsyndromic hearing loss is prelingual and severe to profound. Differential Diagnosis of Genetic Hearing Loss</h2><p>In developed countries, approximately 65% of prelingual hearing loss is attributed to genetic causes and approximately 35% to acquired/environmental causes (see <a class="figpopup" href="/books/NBK1434/figure/deafness-overview.F1/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-figpopup="figdeafnessoverviewF1" rid-ob="figobdeafnessoverviewF1">Figure 1</a>).</p><p>The most common acquired/environmental cause of prelingual hearing loss is <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/congenital/">congenital</a> cytomegalovirus (cCMV) infection (the overall birth prevalence of cCMV is approximately 0.64% [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.kenneson.2007.253">Kenneson &#x00026; Cannon 2007</a>]). Testing for cCMV, typically performed by <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pcr/">PCR</a> from saliva or urine, is most specific for cCMV when performed in infants who are younger than three weeks of age.</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>About 10% of infants with cCMV are symptomatic with findings that can include a characteristic rash, hearing loss (in ~50%), neurologic deficits (e.g., seizures, spasticity), and/or hepatic insufficiency. Some infants succumb to their complications.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>About 90% of infants with cCMV are considered "asymptomatic." Of these up to 10% develop unilateral or bilateral hearing loss. This variability is characteristic of cCMV-related hearing loss.</div></li></ul><p>Acquired/environmental hearing loss in children may also result from:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Prenatal infections caused by "TORCH" organisms (i.e., <i>to</i>xoplasmosis, <i>r</i>ubella, <i>c</i>ytomegalovirus, and <i>h</i>erpes);</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Postnatal infections, particularly bacterial meningitis caused by <i>Neisseria meningitidis</i>, <i>Haemophilus influenzae</i>, or <i>Streptococcus pneumoniae</i>. Other causative organisms include <i>Escherichia coli</i>, <i>Listeria monocytogenes</i>, <i>Streptococcus agalactiae</i>, and <i>Enterobacter cloacae</i>.</div></li></ul><p>Acquired/environmental hearing loss in adults is most often attributed to factors such as noise and medications. It is likely that complex environmental-genetic interactions affect the age of onset and severity of age-related hearing loss (presbycusis) and noise-induced hearing loss [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.ahmadmehrabi.2021.401">Ahmadmehrabi et al 2021</a>].</p></div><div id="deafness-overview.Evaluation_Strategy"><h2 id="_deafness-overview_Evaluation_Strategy_">4.  Evaluation Strategy</h2><div id="deafness-overview.Identification_of_Chil"><h3>Identification of Children with Hearing Loss</h3><p><b>Newborns.</b> Universal newborn hearing screening (NBHS) using physiologic screening (either otoacoustic emissions [OAE] or automated ABR [AABR]), required by law or rule in all 50 states in the United States, is performed on &#x0003e;98% of children in the US typically within days after birth (see <a href="https://www.cdc.gov/ncbddd/hearingloss/ehdi-data.html" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">CDC Annual Data</a>). Universal NBHS in the US identifies about 6,000 children with hearing loss each year; note that NBHS, which is designed to detect moderate-to-profound hearing loss, may miss infants with mild hearing loss.</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>First, abnormal NBHS results must be followed by confirmatory audiometric testing, typically diagnostic auditory brain stem response (ABR).</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The next step is typically medical evaluation by an otolaryngologist, often the first point of contact for children with newly diagnosed hearing loss. The otolaryngologist will perform an evaluation to determine cause of hearing loss by differentiating between non-permanent causes like otitis media (fluid in the middle ear), outer and middle ear abnormalities causing conductive hearing loss, and sensorineural hearing loss. The examination will evaluate for <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/syndromic/">syndromic</a> features. The otolaryngologist will consider history, physical examination, audiometric testing, and frequently imaging studies or genetic tests to determine the underlying diagnosis.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>A genetic cause is identified in up to 60% of children with <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/congenital/">congenital</a> bilateral severe-to-profound sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.downie.2020.587">Downie et al 2020</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.corriolsnoval.2022.1125">Corriols-Noval et al 2022</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.seligman.2022.734">Seligman et al 2022</a>]. For older children and adults, the rates of genetic diagnosis are ~40% and ~20%, respectively [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.sloanheggen.2016.441">Sloan-Heggen et al 2016</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.corriolsnoval.2022.1125">Corriols-Noval et al 2022</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.seligman.2022.734">Seligman et al 2022</a>].</div></li></ul><p><b>Other children</b> referred for complete diagnostic assessment regardless of NBHS result often include:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Children considered to have a high chance of hearing loss for reasons such as a family history of hearing loss, a syndrome associated with hearing loss (e.g., trisomy 21 or cleft lip and palate), or exposure to specific environmental risk factors (e.g., care in a neonatal intensive care unit [NICU], exposure to aminoglycoside antibiotics);</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>School-age children who typically have milder hearing loss that was not detected on NBHS and are often identified following: (1) parental concern regarding hearing; (2) delayed speech or language development; and/or (3) hearing screening as part of a well-child examination or school program [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.wake.2006.1842">Wake et al 2006</a>].</div></li></ul></div><div id="deafness-overview.Identification_of_Adul"><h3>Identification of Adults with Hearing Loss</h3><p>Adults with hearing loss &#x02013; at the time of presentation &#x02013; typically are symptomatic with either diminished hearing or tinnitus (ringing in the ears), which may indicate hearing loss.</p></div><div id="deafness-overview.Initial_Diagnostic_Eva"><h3>Initial Diagnostic Evaluation</h3><p>Initial diagnostic evaluation of any individual with hearing loss (see <a class="figpopup" href="/books/NBK1434/figure/deafness-overview.F2/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-figpopup="figdeafnessoverviewF2" rid-ob="figobdeafnessoverviewF2">Figure 2</a>) should include genetic testing (see <a href="#deafness-overview.Molecular_Genetic_Test">Molecular Genetic Testing</a>) as well as the following:</p><div class="iconblock whole_rhythm clearfix ten_col fig" id="figdeafnessoverviewF2" co-legend-rid="figlgnddeafnessoverviewF2"><a href="/books/NBK1434/figure/deafness-overview.F2/?report=objectonly" target="object" title="Figure 2. " class="img_link icnblk_img figpopup" rid-figpopup="figdeafnessoverviewF2" rid-ob="figobdeafnessoverviewF2"><img class="small-thumb" src="/books/NBK1434/bin/deafness-overview-Image002.gif" src-large="/books/NBK1434/bin/deafness-overview-Image002.jpg" alt="Figure 2. " /></a><div class="icnblk_cntnt" id="figlgnddeafnessoverviewF2"><h4 id="deafness-overview.F2"><a href="/books/NBK1434/figure/deafness-overview.F2/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobdeafnessoverviewF2">Figure 2. </a></h4><p class="float-caption no_bottom_margin">Diagnostic algorithm for presumed hereditary nonsyndromic sensorineural hearing loss </p></div></div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Hearing loss history</b> with a focus on onset, severity, frequency, and laterality (See <a href="#deafness-overview.Audiometric_and_Clinic">Audiometric and Clinical Characteristics of Hearing Loss</a>.)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Family history of</b>
<b>hearing loss,</b> particularly early-onset hearing loss, and presence of features suggestive of the syndromes included in <a href="/books/NBK1434/table/deafness-overview.T.select_common_causes/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobdeafnessoverviewTselectcommoncauses">Tables 6a</a>, <a href="/books/NBK1434/table/deafness-overview.T.select_common_causes_1/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobdeafnessoverviewTselectcommoncauses1">6b</a>, and <a href="/books/NBK1434/table/deafness-overview.T.select_common_causes_2/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobdeafnessoverviewTselectcommoncauses2">6c</a> (select common syndromes with hearing loss organized by <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/mode-of-inheritance/">mode of inheritance</a>)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Physical examination</b> with particular attention to abnormalities of the auricle including shape and size (e.g., <a href="/books/n/gene/charge/"><i>CHD7</i> disorder</a>), pits or tags (e.g., <a href="/books/n/gene/bor/">branchiootorenal spectrum disorder</a>), thyroid function (e.g., <a href="/books/n/gene/pendred/">Pendred syndrome</a>), and pigmentary abnormalities (e.g., <a href="/books/n/gene/ws1/">Waardenburg syndrome type 1</a>)</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Audiometric testing.</b> Hearing status can be determined at any age and is tailored to the individual's ability to participate (see <a href="#deafness-overview.Audiometric_and_Clinic">Audiometric and Clinical Characteristics of Hearing Loss).</a></div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Evaluation of vestibular function</b> is indicated because about 50% of hearing loss is associated with peripheral vestibular dysfunction. The finding of vestibular dysfunction may guide physical therapy, particularly in children with delayed motor milestones.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Consideration of</b>
<b>imaging (CT and/or MRI)</b> if hearing loss is unilateral or asymmetric and/or sudden onset.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Other ancillary testing/evaluation.</b> Electrocardiogram (EKG) is often performed in children with severe-to-profound hearing loss to evaluate for prolonged QT interval, which may be life-threatening. An ophthalmology evaluation may be recommended, as individuals with hearing loss may depend more on vision for sensory input, as well as to evaluate for retinitis pigmentosa.</div></li></ul><p>Timing and order of diagnostic evaluation is individual specific and depends on the age of the individual and the anticipated habilitation options. For instance, a child with severe-to-profound SNHL will typically require imaging (CT or MRI) prior to consideration of cochlear implantation. In clinical practice, the diagnostic evaluation is often performed simultaneously with <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a> given the turnaround time (1-3 months) for genetic testing.</p></div><div id="deafness-overview.Molecular_Genetic_Test"><h3>Molecular Genetic Testing</h3><p>Molecular genetic testing is the standard of care in evaluation of individuals with hearing loss. See <a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.liming.2016.251">Liming et al [2016]</a> (<a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165587616303160?via%3Dihub" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">full text</a>) and <a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.li.2022.1392">Li et al [2022]</a> (<a href="https://www.gimjournal.org/article/S1098-3600(22)00713-4/fulltext" ref="pagearea=body&amp;targetsite=external&amp;targetcat=link&amp;targettype=uri">full text</a>).</p><p>The diagnosis of a specific genetic cause of hearing loss <b>is established</b> in a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> with suggestive findings and pathogenic (or <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/likely-pathogenic/">likely pathogenic</a>) variant(s) in the causative <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> identified by <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a>.</p><p>Note: (1) Per American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics&#x000a0;/ Association for Molecular Pathology variant interpretation guidelines, the terms "<a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>" and "<a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/likely-pathogenic/">likely pathogenic</a> variant" are synonymous in a clinical setting, meaning that both are considered diagnostic and can be used for clinical decision making [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.richards.2015.405">Richards et al 2015</a>]. Reference to "pathogenic variants" in this <i>GeneReview</i> is understood to include any likely pathogenic variants. (2) The identification of variant(s) of <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/uncertain-significance/">uncertain significance</a> cannot be used to confirm or rule out the diagnosis.</p><p><b>Recommended <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/molecular-genetic-testing/">molecular genetic testing</a> approaches</b> include use of a <b>multigene hearing loss panel</b> and/or <b><a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genomic/">genomic</a> testing</b>.</p><p>Note: Single-<a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> testing (<a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/sequence-analysis/">sequence analysis</a> of a given gene, followed by gene-targeted <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/deletion-duplication-analysis/">deletion/duplication analysis</a>) is rarely useful and typically NOT recommended. Isolated <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/chromosomal-microarray/">chromosomal microarray</a> testing in an individual with apparent nonsyndromic hearing loss also has a low diagnostic yield.</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm"><b>A multigene hearing loss panel</b> can often identify the cause of genetic hearing loss while limiting identification of pathogenic variants and variants of <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/uncertain-significance/">uncertain significance</a> in genes that are irrelevant to the underlying <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a>. Note: (1) The genes included in the panel and the diagnostic <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/sensitivity/">sensitivity</a> of the testing used for each <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> vary by laboratory and are likely to change over time. (2) Some multigene panels may include genes not associated with the condition discussed in this <i>GeneReview</i>. (3) In some laboratories, panel options may include a custom laboratory-designed panel and/or custom phenotype-focused <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/exome/">exome</a> or genome analysis that includes genes specified by the clinician. (4) Methods used in a panel may include <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/sequence-analysis/">sequence analysis</a>, <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/deletion-duplication-analysis/">deletion/duplication analysis</a>, and/or other non-sequencing-based tests.</div><div class="half_rhythm">For an introduction to multigene panels click <a href="/books/n/gene/app5/#app5.Multigene_Panels">here</a>. More detailed information for clinicians ordering genetic tests can be found <a href="/books/n/gene/app5/#app5.Multigene_Panels_FAQs">here</a>.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div class="half_rhythm"><b>Genomic testing</b> does not require the clinician to determine which <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> is likely involved. <b>Exome sequencing</b> is most commonly used. <b>Genome sequencing</b> is also increasingly being used in the clinical setting as the cost of testing decreases; diagnostic yield is higher, as noncoding regions are interrogated for pathogenic regulatory variants.</div><div class="half_rhythm">For an introduction to comprehensive <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genomic/">genomic</a> testing click <a href="/books/n/gene/app5/#app5.Comprehensive_Genomic_Testing">here</a>. More detailed information for clinicians ordering genomic testing can be found <a href="/books/n/gene/app5/#app5.Comprehensive_Genomic_Testing_1">here</a>.</div></li></ul></div></div><div id="deafness-overview.Management"><h2 id="_deafness-overview_Management_">5.  Management</h2><p><b>Management of hearing loss in children</b> is structured around the goals of the parents and/or care providers for speech and language. Families may want their child to attain oral speech and language and to participate in mainstream schooling. Members of the Deaf community may prefer to rely on visual communication (typically American Sign Language) to fulfill communication goals or may prefer to incorporate total communication. (Total communication is a holistic approach to communication that promotes the use of all modes of communication including sign language, spoken language, gestures, facial expressions, and environmental cues such as pictures and sounds.)</p><p>Discussion between health care providers and parents/caregivers to solicit the goals and preferences of the family is the first step in establishing a plan for management.</p><p><b>Management of hearing loss in adults</b> also takes into account the affected individual's preference for communication. Adults who communicate with spoken language most often seek to restore hearing using hearing aids, cochlear implants, <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene-therapy/">gene therapy</a>, or a combination of modalities. It is becoming increasingly clear that hearing loss in older adults leads to isolation and increases risk for dementia [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.thomson.2017.69">Thomson et al 2017</a>]. These risks may be mitigated by addressing hearing loss.</p><div id="deafness-overview.Multidisciplinary_Supp"><h3>Multidisciplinary Supportive Treatment of Hearing Loss</h3><p><b>Children.</b> Multidisciplinary supportive treatment recommended for hearing loss includes an otolaryngologist, an audiologist experienced in the assessment of hearing loss in children, a speech-language pathologist, a clinical geneticist, a genetic counselor, and a pediatrician. In some instances, other specialists such a neuropsychologist and/or an educator of the Deaf may be required. If a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/syndromic/">syndromic</a> diagnosis is made, then clinicians with expertise in other areas may be required (e.g., an ophthalmologist for a child diagnosed with Usher syndrome).</p><p><b>Adults.</b> Management of hearing loss in adults typically focuses on continued integration into a hearing society primarily by use of verbal communication and often other abilities, such as use of a telephone and enjoyment of music. Hearing loss in older adults is associated with increased risk of dementia [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.thomson.2017.69">Thomson et al 2017</a>]. Improved quality of life is possible with early treatment with hearing aids or cochlear implants [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.andries.2021.61">Andries et al 2021</a>].</p><div id="deafness-overview.Habilitation"><h4>Habilitation</h4><p>Early auditory intervention can include sound amplification with hearing aids, otologic surgery to implant bone conduction devices, cochlear implantation, and/or intracochlear <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene-therapy/">gene therapy</a> [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.smith.2005.879">Smith et al 2005</a>]. Early identification of hearing loss in children is critical to developing speech and language [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.yoshinagaitano.2018.1256">Yoshinaga-Itano et al 2018</a>]. Conversely, delayed identification of hearing loss and delayed exposure to language is associated with poor reading performance, poor communication skills, and poor speech production; subsequent intervention may be insufficient to completely remediate these deficiencies [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.tobey.2013.219">Tobey et al 2013</a>].</p><p>Habilitation options include the following:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Hearing aids (sound amplification),</b> customized by an audiologist to the degree and frequency of hearing loss, can be used in individuals with mild-to-severe hearing loss.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Bone conduction devices (bone-anchored hearing systems),</b> surgically implanted devices, are used for some types of conductive hearing loss or unilateral hearing loss.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>
<b>Cochlear implantation</b>
</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Cochlear implantation can be considered in children with severe-to-profound hearing loss who are older than age nine months. Children who undergo cochlear implantation early (before age two years) may achieve &#x02013; by school age &#x02013; oral speech and language that is indistinguishable from their normal-hearing peers [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.loy.2010.247">Loy et al 2010</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.langereis.2015.834">Langereis &#x00026; Vermeulen 2015</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>In adults, cochlear implant performance may be compromised when the auditory nerve itself is affected; however, further research is needed in this area [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.shearer.2017.138">Shearer et al 2017</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Although research has focused on cochlear implant performance based on the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene/">gene</a> involved, given the genetic heterogeneity of hearing loss, large sample sizes are difficult to obtain for performance on a per-gene basis. However, data are clear that individuals with <i>GJB2</i>-related hearing loss (see <a href="/books/n/gene/dfnb1/"><i>GJB2</i>-Related Autosomal Recessive Nonsyndromic Hearing Loss</a>) have excellent cochlear implant outcomes that are significantly better than those of individuals with hearing loss due to an environmental cause [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.yoshida.2013.435">Yoshida et al 2013</a>, <a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.abdurehim.2017.16">Abdurehim et al 2017</a>].</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div><b>Gene replacement therapy</b> is approved by the FDA for treatment of <i>OTOF-</i>related hearing loss. Otarmeni&#x02122; (lunsotogene parvec-cwha) is a single-dose intracochlear infusion shown to be effective at restoring hearing to children. Individuals cannot receive <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/gene-therapy/">gene therapy</a> in an ear that has already undergone cochlear implantation; therefore, early identification of individuals with <i>OTOF-</i>related hearing loss via genetic testing is critical. Further research is needed to assess long-term speech and language outcomes for children treated with gene therapy. (See <a href="/books/n/gene/dfnb9/"><i>OTOF</i>-Related Hearing Loss</a> for detailed information.)</div></li></ul></div><div id="deafness-overview.Communication_and_Iden"><h4>Communication and Identity Development</h4><p>On initial evaluation of individuals with hearing loss, the goals for communication must be established with a focus on equipping individuals with language and appropriate access to language.</p><p>Early access to sign language for children with hearing loss often provides a "head start" to auditory communication skills that are used later by such children who will proceed to communicate using spoken language [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.blancoelorrieta.2018.5492">Blanco-Elorrieta et al 2018</a>].</p><p>A speech-language therapist as well as a neuropsychologist/psychologist for children with hearing loss provides access to resources within the community to aid in speech and language and D/deaf and hard of hearing (DHH) identity development.</p></div><div id="deafness-overview.Education"><h4>Education</h4><p><b>Ages 0-3 years.</b> Referral to an early intervention program is recommended for access to occupational, physical, speech-language, and feeding therapy as well as specialized D/deaf and hard of hearing (DHH) services to support DHH identity development in the child and family. In the US, early intervention is a federally funded program available in all states that provides in-home services to target individual therapy needs.</p><p><b>Ages 3-5 years.</b> In the US, developmental preschool through the local public school district is recommended. Before placement, an evaluation is made to determine needed services and therapies and an individualized education program (IEP) is developed for those who qualify based on established motor, language, social, or cognitive delay. The early intervention program typically assists with this transition. Developmental preschool is center based; for children too medically unstable to attend, home-based services are provided.</p><p><b>All ages.</b> Consultation with a developmental pediatrician and/or neuropsychologist/psychologist is recommended to ensure the involvement of appropriate community, state, and federal educational agencies and to maximize quality of life for the individual with hearing loss and their family. Some issues to consider:</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>IEP services:</div><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>An IEP provides specially designed instruction and related services to children who qualify.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>IEP services will be reviewed annually to determine whether any changes are needed.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Special education law requires that children participating in an IEP be in the least restrictive environment feasible at school and included in general education as much as possible, when and where appropriate.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Vision and hearing consultants should be a part of the child's IEP team to support access to academic material.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Physical, occupational, and speech-language therapy services will be provided in the IEP to the extent that the need affects the child's access to academic material. Beyond that, private supportive therapies based on the affected individual's needs may be considered.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>When developmentally appropriate, self-advocacy skills for DHH children can be fostered in a safe and supportive school environment. Access to a DHH mentor and/or DHH adult as well as social-emotional learning are tools that support a student's self-expression, which can lead to positive social interactions with peers and educators.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>DHH specialty services should be provided, especially if a child is in a mainstream setting, to ensure appropriate hearing support and promote positive DHH identity development.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>As a child enters the teen years, a transition plan should be discussed and incorporated in the IEP. For some of those receiving IEP services, the public school district is required to provide services until age 21.</div></li></ul></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Should an individual not qualify for an IEP, a 504 plan (Section 504: a US federal statute that prohibits discrimination based on disability) can be considered for those who require accommodations or modifications such as front-of-class seating, assistive technology devices, classroom scribes, extra time, modified assignments, and enlarged text.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Developmental Disabilities Administration (DDA) enrollment is recommended. DDA is a US public agency that provides services and support to qualified individuals. Eligibility differs by state but is typically determined by diagnosis and/or associated cognitive/adaptive disabilities.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Families with limited income and resources may also qualify for supplemental security income (SSI) for their child with a disability.</div></li></ul></div></div><div id="deafness-overview.Targeted_Medical_Thera"><h3>Targeted Medical Therapy</h3><p>Specific medical therapy is available for select disorders (see <a href="/books/NBK1434/table/deafness-overview.T.specific_medical_the/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobdeafnessoverviewTspecificmedicalthe">Table 7</a>).</p><div id="deafness-overview.T.specific_medical_the" class="table"><h3><span class="label">Table 7. </span></h3><div class="caption"><p>Specific Medical Therapy for Select Genetic Disorders</p></div><p class="large-table-link" style="display:none"><span class="right"><a href="/books/NBK1434/table/deafness-overview.T.specific_medical_the/?report=objectonly" target="object">View in own window</a></span></p><div class="large_tbl" id="__deafness-overview.T.specific_medical_the_lrgtbl__"><table class="no_bottom_margin"><thead><tr><th id="hd_h_deafness-overview.T.specific_medical_the_1_1_1_1" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">Disorder Name</th><th id="hd_h_deafness-overview.T.specific_medical_the_1_1_1_2" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">Gene(s)</th><th id="hd_h_deafness-overview.T.specific_medical_the_1_1_1_3" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">Therapy</th><th id="hd_h_deafness-overview.T.specific_medical_the_1_1_1_4" scope="col" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">Comments</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td headers="hd_h_deafness-overview.T.specific_medical_the_1_1_1_1" scope="row" rowspan="1" colspan="1" style="text-align:left;vertical-align:middle;">

<b>Parents of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a></b>

<b>Sibs of a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a></b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If both parents are known to be <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for an <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-recessive/">autosomal recessive</a> hearing loss-related <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>, each sib of the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> has at conception a 25% chance of having hearing loss, a 50% chance of having no hearing loss and being a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/carrier/">carrier</a>, and a 25% chance of having no hearing loss and not being a carrier.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Individuals who are <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/heterozygous/">heterozygous</a> for a <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> associated with <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-recessive/">autosomal recessive</a> hearing loss do not have an increased chance of genetic hearing loss.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Depending on the specific diagnosis, clinical severity and <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/phenotype/">phenotype</a> may differ between sibs with the same <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/biallelic/">biallelic</a> pathogenic variants; thus, age of onset and/or progression of hearing loss may not be predictable. <b><a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>.</b> Females with <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/x-linked/">X-linked</a> hearing loss have a 50% chance of transmitting the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> to each child.</p><p><b>Other family members.</b> If a parent has the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a> identified in the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a>, the parent's href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genetic-counseling/">genetic counseling</a> information about maternal inheritance.</p></div><div id="deafness-overview.Empiric_Risk_to_Family"><h3>Empiric Risk to Family Members</h3><p>If a specific diagnosis cannot be established (and/or the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/mode-of-inheritance/">mode of inheritance</a> cannot be established in a person with a positive family history of deafness or hearing loss), the following empiric figures can be used for families of northern European ancestry.</p><p><b>The subsequent offspring of a hearing couple with one deaf child and an otherwise negative family history of deafness</b> have an 18% empiric probability of deafness in future children [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.green.1999.2211">Green et al 1999</a>].</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If the deaf child does not have <i>GJB2-</i>related <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-recessive/">autosomal recessive</a> nonsyndromic hearing loss, the chance of recurrence is 14% for deafness unrelated to connexin 26 [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.green.1999.2211">Green et al 1999</a>].</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If the hearing couple is <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/consanguineous/">consanguineous</a> or comes from a highly consanguineous community, the subsequent offspring have close to a 25% probability of deafness because of the high likelihood of <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-recessive/">autosomal recessive</a> inheritance.</div></li></ul><p><b>The offspring of a deaf person and a hearing person</b> have a 10% empiric probability of deafness [<a class="bk_pop" href="#deafness-overview.REF.green.1999.2211">Green et al 1999</a>].</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Most of the probability is attributed to <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-dominant/">autosomal dominant</a> <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/syndromic/">syndromic</a> deafness.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If both <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/syndromic/">syndromic</a> deafness and a family history of <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-dominant/">autosomal dominant</a> inheritance can be excluded, the probability of deafness is chiefly related to <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pseudodominant-inheritance/">pseudodominant inheritance</a> of recessive deafness. <i>GJB2</i> testing can identify much of this probability (see <a href="/books/n/gene/dfnb1/"><i>GJB2</i>-Related Autosomal Recessive Nonsyndromic Hearing Loss</a>).</div></li></ul><p><b>The child of a nonconsanguineous deaf couple in whom <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-dominant/">autosomal dominant</a> deafness has been excluded</b> has an approximately 15% empiric probability of deafness [<a class="bk_pop" 100%.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>If the couple has <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-recessive/">autosomal recessive</a> deafness known to be caused by class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/proband/">proband</a> (presumed to have <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/autosomal-recessive/">autosomal recessive</a> <b>The following points are noteworthy:</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Clear communication between individuals with hearing loss, families, and health care providers is key. D/deaf and hard of hearing (DHH) persons may use a variety of communication methods including spoken language, sign language, lip reading, and written notes. For DHH individuals and families who use sign language, a certified sign language interpreter must be used. Communication aids such as visual aids and verbal cues when changing topics can be helpful.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>It is important to ascertain and address the questions and concerns of the family/individual. DHH persons may be interested in obtaining information about the cause of their hearing loss, including information on medical, educational, and social services. Others may seek information about the chance of having children with hearing loss and information for family planning decisions.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The use of neutral or balanced terminology can enhance the provision of services; for example: use of the term "chance" instead of "risk"; "deaf" or "hearing" instead of "affected" or "unaffected"; and "deaf" or "hard of hearing" instead of "hearing impaired." Members of the Deaf community may view deafness as a distinguishing characteristic and not as a handicap, impairment, or medical condition requiring a "treatment" or "cure," or to be "prevented." Terms such as "handicap" should be avoided.</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Family planning</b>
</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The optimal time for determination of genetic status and discussion of the availability of prenatal/<a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a> is before pregnancy.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>It is appropriate to offer <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/genetic-counseling/">genetic counseling</a> (including discussion of the probability of hearing loss in offspring and reproductive options) to young adults who have hearing loss.</div></li></ul></div><div id="deafness-overview.Prenatal_Testing_and_P"><h3>Prenatal Testing and Preimplantation Genetic Testing</h3><p>Once the <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/pathogenic-variant/">pathogenic variant</a>(s) have been identified in the family, prenatal and <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a> for genetic hearing loss are possible.</p><p>Differences in perspective may exist among medical professionals and within families regarding the use of prenatal and <a class="def" href="/books/n/gene/glossary/def-item/preimplantation-genetic-testing/">preimplantation genetic testing</a>.
