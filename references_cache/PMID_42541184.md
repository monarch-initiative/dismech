# AAV9-mediated GAA gene therapy following enzyme replacement therapy discontinuation in children with infantile-onset Pompe disease.
**Authors:** Liu X, Mao Y, Hu L, He W, Wang X, Guo X, Li M, Wang Y, Wang Y, Ma W, Yuan Y, Wang Z, Yu S, Zhang S, Wu S, Dong X, Wu X, Yang G
**Journal:** EClinicalMedicine (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.104081](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2026.104081)

## Content

BACKGROUND: While our previous research showed that a single infusion of recombinant AAV9-mediated GAA gene therapy enabled infants (<1 year) with infantile-onset Pompe disease (IOPD) and minimal prior enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) to remain ventilator- and ERT-free for at least one year, it was unknown whether the same benefits could be sustained in older children with IOPD with prolonged, high-dose ERT exposure after treatment cessation.

METHODS: In this investigator-initiated, single-arm trial (ChiCTR2200065664), six children with IOPD aged 12.5-50.8 months-all having previously received 10-98 cycles of ERT-were enrolled and administered a single intravenous infusion of AAV9-mediated gene therapy. Over a 12-month period, the following outcomes were evaluated: primary efficacy endpoints (gross motor function, and cardiac structure and function); secondary efficacy endpoints (ventilator-free survival, and the proportion of patients who showed improvement at the end of the trial); exploratory endpoints (IOPD-related skeletal muscle pathological changes); safety and tolerability endpoints; and immunogenicity assessment endpoints.

FINDINGS: The intervention was generally well tolerated, with a low incidence of grade ≥3 adverse events (2.31 events per person-time). All six patients remained ventilator-free over the one-year observation period (one patient required transient ventilatory support for pneumonia). However, lung function and cardiac structure showed no further improvement after gene therapy. All children (patient 4 was not documented due to early withdrawal from the study) maintained sustained growth and development throughout follow-up, and overall quality of life showed a trend of improvement (PedsQL 3.0 NMM). During the study period, patient 1 achieved standing with assistance and crawling; patient 2 achieved standing and walking without assistance; and patient 3 achieved walking and sitting without assistance. Muscle biopsies from the three patients revealed relatively high GAA enzyme activity and reduced glycogen accumulation.

INTERPRETATION: These preliminary findings suggest that AAV9-mediated GAA gene therapy may reduce reliance on ERT and is associated with improvements in muscle histopathology among biopsied participants.

Pompe disease is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by variants in the GAA gene located on chromosome 17q25.2-q25.3, which encodes the lysosomal hydrolase enzyme, acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA).1, 2, 3 GAA is synthesized as an inactive proenzyme in the endoplasmic reticulum.4, 5, 6 Within the Golgi apparatus, it acquires a mannose 6-phosphate (M6P) lysosomal targeting signal, which facilitates its binding to the mannose 6-phosphate receptor (M6PR) for transport to lysosomes, where it functions to hydrolyze glycogen.7,8 Variants in the GAA gene can disrupt any stage of this finely regulated process—from synthesis and correct folding to lysosomal trafficking and proteolytic activation—resulting in varying degrees of enzyme deficiency that correlate with the clinical severity of the disease.3,9,10 The complete or partial loss of GAA function leads to the pathological accumulation of glycogen within lysosomes, causing lysosomal swelling, rupture, and cascading cellular damage that ultimately results in progressive tissue injury and organ dysfunction.11,12

Traditionally, Pompe disease is classified into two primary phenotypes based on the age of onset, organ involvement, and rate of progression: infantile-onset Pompe disease (IOPD) and late-onset Pompe disease (LOPD).13 IOPD is defined by symptom onset within the first 12 months of life and invariably features hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Without treatment, this form is typically fatal within the first year or two of life due to cardiorespiratory failure.14,15 LOPD includes patients with onset before 12 months without cardiomyopathy, and all individuals with onset after 12 months.16 It primarily presents with progressive proximal muscle weakness and respiratory insufficiency, while clinically significant cardiac involvement is uncommon.17,18

Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) with recombinant human GAA (rhGAA) has been the standard of care since its approval in 2006.19 While ERT provides undeniable benefits by prolonging survival and improving motor and respiratory function, it faces limitations. These challenges include: (1) the burden of lifelong, high-frequency intravenous infusions (administered every two weeks); (2) the requirement for high doses of rhGAA (20–40 mg/kg) to achieve therapeutic benefit; (3) prohibitively high costs; and (4) intrinsically low tissue uptake efficacy, particularly in CNS and skeletal muscle.20,21 Consequently, despite an initial favorable response to ERT, disease progression persists, and severe motor, cardiac, respiratory, and cognitive deficits remain prevalent among IOPD survivors.22

To assess the efficacy of an alternative therapy, a recombinant adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) vector carrying a codon-optimized human GAA gene expression cassette, designated GC301, was engineered to enhance tissue transduction efficiency.2 Preliminary pilot clinical studies have indicated that a single intravenous injection of GC301 enabled patients with IOPD under one year of age to remain free from ERT and ventilator support for at least a one-year observation period, while demonstrating a favorable safety profile. In the present study, we aimed to determine the safety and efficacy of GC301 in patients with IOPD who were older than one year and had received prolonged ERT.

This investigator-initiated trial (ChiCTR2200065664) evaluated the safety and efficacy of GC301 treatment in patients with IOPD aged 1–6 years who had received prior prolonged ERT (clinically defined in this study as a sufficient duration of prior ERT to establish a stable baseline without rapid disease progression). The protocol was approved by the Chinese PLA General Hospital Ethics Committee (S2022-596-04) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) guideline, and the Good Clinical Practice guideline of the National Medical Products Administration of China (NMPA-GCP). Prior to enrollment, written informed consent was obtained from all legal guardians.

Patients with confirmed IOPD were enrolled between April and October 2023. The key inclusion criteria were: (1) Aged 1–6 years, regardless of sex; (2) Onset of Pompe disease symptoms before 12 months of age, with IOPD diagnosis confirmed by GAA gene mutation analysis, deficient GAA enzyme activity, and classic clinical features of IOPD (specifically, a documented history of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and muscle weakness/hypotonia prior to 12 months of age)23; (3) Prior receipt of ERT with clinical stability, defined as the absence of rapid disease progression (e.g., no acute decline in cardiopulmonary function, such as a new requirement for respiratory support, and no rapid loss of motor milestones prior to screening); (4) Ability to complete motor function assessments; (5) Legal guardian(s) capable of understanding and adhering to the study protocol, and who voluntarily provided signed informed consent for the participant's enrollment. The exclusion criteria were: (1) Clinically significant organic diseases other than those attributable to IOPD, including significant cardiovascular, pulmonary, hepatic, renal, or neurological disorders, or other severe comorbidities; (2) Requirement of invasive respiratory support or non-invasive respiratory support for >16 h per day; (3) Severe non-respiratory illness requiring systemic therapy and/or hospitalization within 2 weeks before screening; (4) Upper or lower respiratory tract infection requiring antiviral/antibacterial therapy and/or hospitalization within 4 weeks before screening; (5) Use of immunomodulatory therapy (excluding inhaled corticosteroids) within 90 days prior to screening; (6) History of severe adverse reaction to corticosteroids; (7) Serum anti-AAV9 neutralizing antibody titer >1:100; (8) Previous or concurrent participation in another gene therapy clinical trial; (9) Any other condition deemed by the investigator to render the participant unsuitable for the trial.

Patients received a single intravenous infusion of GC301 (driven by a CMV enhancer/chicken β-actin promoter) at a dose of 1.2 × 1014 vg/kg following a 4–20 day washout period from ERT.2 Oral prednisolone (2 mg/kg/day starting Day −1) was tapered over 8 weeks to mitigate immune responses.

The safety was assessed by the incidence of adverse events (graded per Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events Version 5.0) and serious adverse events. Assessments of efficacy (defined as ventilator-free survival and independence from ERT at 52 weeks). Motor function will be evaluated using a multi-faceted approach, including the Gross Motor Function Measure-88 (GMFM-88) total score (%), the attainment of World Health Organization (WHO) motor milestones, and imaging studies such as muscle ultrasonography. Cardiac function will be monitored via echocardiography, with a primary focus on the change in the height-adjusted left ventricular mass Z-score and left ventricular ejection fraction over the 52-week period. Pulmonary function will be assessed using tPTEF/tE (%) and VT/kg (ml/kg). The health-related quality of life will be compared before and after treatment using the PedsQL 3.0 Neuromuscular Module (PedsQL 3.0 NMM). Growth metrics (height and weight) will be evaluated against age- and sex-specific normative growth charts for Chinese children and adolescents aged 0–18 years. The circulating biomarkers to be monitored include creatine kinase (CK), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), with measurements performed by the hospital's clinical laboratory. GC301 (rAAV9-coGAA) is a recombinant AAV9 vector carrying a codon-optimized human GAA gene and is produced using the baculovirus-insect cell system.24 The expression of the therapeutic transgene is driven by a hybrid promoter consisting of the cytomegalovirus (CMV) early enhancer element and the chicken beta-actin (CAG) promoter.

The vector genome copy number in whole blood samples was quantified using a ddPCR assay. Blood samples were collected in EDTA-K2 tubes, and genomic DNA was extracted from 100 μL of whole blood using a magnetic Universal Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN, China), according to the manufacturer's protocol. The ddPCR assay employed the same coGAA-specific primers and probe used for the quantification of the rAAV9-coGAA vector. The sequences were as follows: forward primer 5′-GGCACCTGGTCGTGGAACTC-3′, reverse primer 5′-CCAGCCTCTGATCGGCAAGG-3′, and a FAM-labeled probe 5′-TGGCCAGGCTCCACCGCCTT-3′ with a TAMRA quencher (synthesized by Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). Each sample was analyzed in duplicate. The results, expressed as the genome copy number per milliliter of blood, demonstrated a lower limit of detection for the assay of 1.76 × 103 copies/mL.

Patient peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were collected approximately 10 days after the most recent ERT session and prior to the GC301 infusion. These PBMCs were then immortalized by Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) transformation to generate lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs). The LCLs were cultured and passaged for a minimum of 10 passages before analysis. For Western blot analysis, LCLs were lysed using RIPA buffer, and 40 μg of total protein was loaded per lane. Protein detection was carried out using a mouse monoclonal antibody against human GAA capable of recognizing both the precursor and active forms of the protein. β-Actin served as the loading control, and Raji cell protein lysate was included as a positive control. Samples were classified as cross-reactive immunologic material (CRIM)-positive upon detection of any GAA precursor or mature protein fragments.25

A strictly validated cell-based microneutralization (MN) assay was employed to quantify anti-AAV9 neutralizing antibodies at the bioanalytical laboratory of Beijing Genecradle Therapeutics Inc. Serum or plasma samples were pre-treated at 56 °C for 30 min to inactivate complement. Starting from a 1:20 dilution, 50 μL of each sample was subjected to a series of two-fold dilutions. These dilutions were then incubated with 2 × 108 vector genomes (vg) of rAAV9-EGFP-2A-Gluc in 50 μL of DMEM supplemented with 0.1% BSA for 1 h at 37 °C. The virus-serum mixture was subsequently transferred onto HEK-293-C340 cells (a highly permissive subclone) seeded in 96-well plates containing D10 medium with 1 mM sodium butyrate, which enhances transduction. The plates were incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 48–72 h. After incubation, Gaussian luciferase activity was measured in relative light units (RLU). Wells with a coefficient of variation (CV%) for RLU exceeding 30% were excluded from the analysis. The percentage of transduction inhibition (TI) was calculated using the formula: TI% = [1 − (mean RLUtest − mean RLUcc)/(mean RLUvc − mean RLUcc)] × 100%, where “test” is the test sample, “vc” is the virus control, and “cc” is the cell control. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) titer, representing the serum dilution that causes a 50% reduction in transduction, was determined through curve-fit modeling.

An antigen-binding enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used to detect antibodies that bind to the AAV9 capsid. 96-well plates were coated with 50 ng per well of AAV9 vector particles in carbonate buffer (pH 9.6) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After coating, the plates were blocked with PBS containing 5% skim milk for 2 h at 37 °C. For system suitability control, low and high-quality positive controls were prepared using a mouse monoclonal anti-AAV9 antibody at concentrations of 0.12 μg/mL and 5 μg/mL, respectively, diluted in pooled negative serum. Test samples and controls were diluted to a minimum required dilution (MRD) of 1:100 in 1% skim milk. For the confirmatory assay, samples were pre-incubated with 50 ng of soluble AAV9 vector particles for 1 h to assess specificity through competitive inhibition. The samples were then added to the coated plates in duplicate and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Following washes, a mixture of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-human and goat anti-mouse IgG antibodies was added. The signal was developed using tetramethylbenzidine substrate, and the optical density (OD) was read at 450 nm. The signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio was calculated by dividing the OD value of the sample by the OD value of the negative control. The percentage inhibition in the confirmatory assay was defined as [1 − (S/N ratio with AAV9 particles/S/N ratio without AAV9 particles)] × 100%. A sample was considered positive if its inhibition percentage exceeded the confirmatory cut point of 43.84%. The binding antibody titer was reported as the reciprocal of the maximum serum dilution yielding a positive result.

The assay for detecting antibodies against the human GAA followed a similar ELISA protocol to the anti-AAV9 binding antibody assay, with the following modifications: (1) the coating antigen was full-length human GAA protein expressed in CHO cells; (2) the positive control was a rabbit monoclonal anti-GAA antibody used at 0.1 μg/mL and 3 μg/mL; (3) the detection antibody was a mixture of HRP-conjugated goat anti-human and goat anti-rabbit IgG. The confirmatory cut point for a positive result was set at an inhibition percentage of ≥38.512%. The assay's lower limit of detection (LLOD) was 29.67 ng/mL.

Cellular immune responses were evaluated using an enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISpot) assay to detect interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) secretion upon stimulation with peptide pools. Overlapping peptides (18–20 amino acids in length, with 10–12 amino acid overlaps) covering the full sequence of the AAV9 VP1 capsid protein and human acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA) were synthesized by ChinaPeptide Qyaobio (Shanghai, China) and Scilight-Peptide (Beijing, China), respectively.

Fresh PBMCs were isolated using BD Vacutainer® CPT™ mononuclear cell preparation tubes according to the manufacturer's instructions. The cells were washed and resuspended at a concentration of 2 × 106 cells/mL in culture medium. A total of 100 μL of the cell suspension was added to each well of a pre-coated 96-well plate and stimulated with 2.5 μg/mL of the respective peptide pools. Unstimulated cells (in medium alone) served as the negative control, and cells stimulated with an anti-CD3 monoclonal antibody were included as the positive control.

After a 36-h incubation period at 37 °C under 5% CO2, the cells were removed, and the plate was processed according to the kit protocol (Human IFN-γ [ALP] ELISpot kit; Mabtech, Sweden). This included incubation with a biotinylated detection antibody, followed by an enzyme conjugate, and development with BCIP/NBT substrate. Spot-forming units (SFUs) were counted manually. A response was considered positive if the number of SFUs per million cells exceeded 50 and was at least three times greater than the value observed in the unstimulated negative control wells.

Muscle ultrasound was performed on the quadriceps femoris, and echogenicity was graded using a modified Heckmatt scale,26 which classifies findings into the following three levels:

Grade 1 (Normal): Muscle echogenicity is not increased relative to the adjacent epimysium, and the perimysium remains sharply defined.

Grade 2 (Mild Increase): Less than 50% increase in muscle echogenicity compared to the superficial epimysium, which remains clearly visible.

Grade 3 (Marked Increase): Greater than 50% increase in muscle echogenicity compared to the superficial epimysium, accompanied by a loss of epimysial clarity.

Percutaneous muscle biopsies were performed on patients 3, 5, and 6 at baseline and 26 weeks post-treatment. Following local anesthesia with 2% lidocaine (without direct muscle infiltration to avoid altering tissue biochemistry), a 1-cm longitudinal incision was made. A cylindrical muscle sample was excised, immediately placed on saline-moistened gauze, and maintained on ice to preserve enzymatic reactivity and prevent macromolecule degradation.

Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) Staining: Paraffin sections (4 μm) were fixed in ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and rinsed three times in PBS. Slides were oxidized with 0.5% periodic acid for 10 min at room temperature, thoroughly washed in distilled water (three changes, 2 min each), and incubated in Schiff's reagent for 15–20 min in the dark. After a 5-min rinse under running water, nuclei were counterstained with modified Harris's hematoxylin for 2 min, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol for 30 s, and blued in running water for 5 min. Sections were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%–100%), cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. Glycogen content, visualized by PAS staining, was compared before and after GC301 injection using transmission electron microscopy for ultrastructural assessment.

Succinate Dehydrogenase (SDH) Staining: Sections were air-dried for 30 min at room temperature and then incubated at 37 °C for 45 min in a freshly prepared solution containing 0.2 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.6), 0.05 M sodium succinate, and 1 mM nitroblue tetrazolium, protected from light. After three 1-min rinses in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, slides were dehydrated in graded acetone (30%, 60%, 90%; 2 min each), cleared in xylene, and mounted with synthetic resin. Mitochondria-rich, oxidative Type I fibers stained dark blue-purple and were imaged at × 20 magnification for fiber-type quantification.

Oil Red O Staining: Following 10 min of fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde and three PBS washes, 4-μm cryosections were immersed in 60% isopropanol for 2 min and stained for 15 min in the dark with freshly prepared modified Oil Red O solution (stock solution to distilled water ratio of 3:2). Differentiation in 60% isopropanol (3–5 s) was stopped when lipid droplets appeared bright red against a colorless stroma. Sections were rinsed in running water for 5 min, counterstained with modified Harris's hematoxylin for 1 min, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol for 30 s, and blued under tap water. After dehydration through graded ethanol, clearing in xylene, and mounting in glycerol gelatin, slides were imaged at × 20 magnification. Lipid droplets were quantified as a percentage area using ImageJ software.

Desmin Immunostaining: Cryosections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and washed three times (5 min each) in PBS to remove excess fixative. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubation in 3% H2O2 in methanol containing 0.3% NaN3 for 15 min at room temperature. After three additional PBS rinses, sections were blocked for 1 h in 2% normal goat serum diluted in PBS. They were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a mouse anti-desmin monoclonal antibody (1:5,000, clone MA5-13259, Thermo Fisher) prepared in blocking buffer. Following three PBS washes, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:500, Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min, washed in PBS, and slides were mounted with ProLong Gold antifade medium. Images were acquired on a confocal microscope at ×20 magnification.

GAA Enzyme Activity Assay in Dried Blood Spots (DBS).27 A 3.2 mm punch from a DBS was placed into a 96-well plate. An assay cocktail containing the specific substrate and internal standard was added to each well. The plate was sealed with an aluminum film and incubated for 18 h at 37 °C with orbital shaking. Following incubation, the reaction was quenched by adding a quench solvent, and the solution was thoroughly mixed before being transferred to a deep-well plate. A liquid–liquid extraction by adding ethyl acetate and water to each well. After centrifugation, the upper organic layer (ethyl acetate phase) was carefully transferred to a new plate. The solvent was evaporated under a stream of nitrogen gas, and the residue was reconstituted in a mobile phase solution prior to analysis by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). The analysis was conducted using a Waters Acquity I-Class UPLC system (Waters, MA) coupled to a XevoQ-S mass spectrometer (Waters, MA), equipped with a C18 analytical column (ThermoFisher, Shanghai, China).

GAA Enzyme Activity Assay in Muscle Tissue.12 Muscle tissue samples, collected from the quadriceps, were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −70 °C until analysis. The frozen tissues were homogenized on ice using stainless-steel beads in a RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. The total protein concentration of the homogenate was quantified using the Bradford assay. To measure GAA activity, the tissue homogenates were incubated with a reaction mix containing a specific substrate (e.g., 4-MU-α-glucopyranoside) and an internal standard at 37 °C for 2 h. The reaction was terminated by adding methanol, followed by vigorous vortex mixing. The mixture was then centrifuged at 13,300 rpm at room temperature. The resulting supernatant was collected and transferred vials containing methanol for analysis. Chromatographic separation was achieved using an Agilent 1290 UPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Beijing, China) coupled to a 6475 triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Beijing, China). A C18 column was used, and the mass spectrometer was operated in positive electrospray ionization (ESI) mode with multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) for detection.

Muscle tissue samples were homogenized on ice in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors using stainless steel beads. The total protein concentration of the resulting lysate was determined using the Bradford assay, with bovine serum albumin serving as the standard. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg per lane) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) on 12.5% gels.

Following electrophoresis, the proteins were electrophoretically transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. In clinical research, adverse events (AEs) are defined as any unfavorable medical occurrence in a patient or clinical trial subject administered a medicinal product, which does not necessarily have a causal relationship with the treatment. The severity of AEs is systematically classified according to the National Cancer Institute's Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0 and above,28 into five grades: Grade 1 (Mild) includes asymptomatic or mild symptoms requiring only clinical observation and no treatment, with specific laboratory thresholds such as AST/ALT levels between the upper limit of normal (ULN) and 3 times the ULN; Grade 2 (Moderate) involves symptoms requiring minimal, local, or non-invasive intervention, and limits instrumental activities of daily living (ADL), with AST/ALT levels between 3 and 5 times the ULN; Grade 3 (Severe) includes medically significant events that require hospitalization or prolongation of existing hospitalization, are disabling, or limit self-care ADL, with AST/ALT levels between 5 and 20 times the ULN; Grade 4 (Life-threatening) entails urgent intervention for life-threatening consequences, with AST/ALT levels exceeding 20 times the ULN; and Grade 5 (Death) is fatal and directly related to the AE. Separately, a Serious Adverse Event (SAE) is any untoward medical occurrence that, at any dose, results in death, is life-threatening, requires inpatient hospitalization or prolongs existing hospitalization, results in persistent or significant disability/incapacity, constitutes a congenital anomaly/birth defect, or may jeopardize the subject and require medical or surgical intervention to prevent one of these outcomes.29

Pre-specified analyses: As per our study protocol (see Supplementary Material, “Statistical Analysis” section), descriptive statistical analyses were conducted for the primary efficacy endpoints (GMFM-88 scores, LVM Z-scores, and LVEF), safety, efficacy, pharmacokinetic, and immunogenicity data related to GC301.

Exploratory (post-hoc) analyses: Given the longitudinal, multi-timepoint data generated over the one-year follow-up, several post-hoc analyses were performed for further data exploration. These included: (1) Linear regression analyses to estimate individual longitudinal trends for all clinical continuous variables; (2) Trend analyses of concentration changes in clinical continuous variables over the follow-up period, with the slope and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI) reported as an indicator of potential treatment effect. In addition, the analysis of Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining in muscle biopsy tissues—using the Mann–Whitney U test with a two-sided significance level of p < 0.05 to compare pre- and post-treatment timepoints—was also performed as a post-hoc analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For all analyses, a nominal two-sided alpha level of 0.05 was applied.

A total of 14 patients with IOPD met the initial enrollment criteria. Of these, eight patients were excluded for the following reasons: four due to anti-AAV9 neutralizing antibody titers and/or anti-GAA antibody titers exceeding the threshold (>1:100); and the investigator assessed another four patients as unsuitable for participation in this clinical trial (poor overall functional status (n = 1), and motor function development comparable to that of age-matched typically developing children (n = 3)). Consequently, six patients were ultimately enrolled in gene therapy. However, patient 4 was withdrawn from the study after developing severe pneumonia in the ninth week post-enrollment, which necessitated resumption of ERT in accordance with clinical guidelines. Therefore, only five patients completed the full 52-week follow-up period. For this report, data from patient 4 collected over the first nine weeks were included in the analysis, including the adverse events statistics (Fig. 1A). This gene therapy strategy outlines a structured approach for monitoring safety and efficacy (Fig. 1B). The dosing regimen initiates with an ERT washout period. At baseline (Week 0), enrolled patients receive oral prednisolone prophylaxis (2 mg/kg/day) followed by a single intravenous infusion of GC301 at 1.2 × 1014 vg/kg. Post-infusion, the prednisolone regimen is designed to mitigate immune responses against the AAV vector and to maximize transduction efficiency (Fig. 1B).

Consistent with our previous findings,2 all six patients developed sustained AAV-binding IgG after GC301 administration (Supplementary Table S2). T-cell immune responses were transient and asymptomatic. Mild T-cell responses to AAV9 capsid were detected in two patients (P2 and P6), while responses to GAA-derived peptide pools were observed in three patients (P1, P2, and P5); none of these cellular responses were associated with elevated liver enzyme levels, or clinical deterioration (Supplementary Table S3).

Since ERT became the standard treatment for IOPD, most patients in real-world settings have required prolonged, bi-weekly infusions.31 Even with sustained, high-dose ERT, however, limited tissue distribution of the drug remains a recognized challenge.32,33 Our previous study showed that a single intravenous dose of GC301 enabled infants under 1 year of age with minimal prior ERT to remain ventilator- and ERT-free for at least one year.2 Whether this therapeutic strategy can also sustain survival, growth, and development in older children with IOPD after extended, high-dose ERT remains unknown. This trial enrolled six patients with IOPD with a median age of 25.5 months, a median of 30 prior ERT sessions, and a median ERT duration of 19.5 months. Patient selection criteria were meticulously balanced: individuals with end-stage cardiorespiratory failure were excluded to avoid ethical risks associated with rapid disease progression upon ERT withdrawal, as were those with normal motor development, to ensure post-treatment clinical improvements could be effectively assessed. Furthermore, although the pre-treatment ERT washout period varied (4–20 days), this variance in this phase is unlikely to meaningfully interfere with the 52-week long-term therapeutic outcomes, given the extremely short half-life of exogenous rhGAA.

A one-year observation period following a single infusion of GC301 at 1.2 × 1014 vg/kg revealed that: (1) High safety profile, with the incidence of grade ≥3 adverse events being 2.31 person-time, possibly drug-related adverse events were 5.58 person-time; (2) 100% ventilator-free survival rate, except for patient 4 who required ventilator support once on day 60 post-treatment due to pneumonia, while pulmonary function parameters (VT/kg and tPTEF/tE) generally showed no overall improvement in the six patients (except tPTEF/tE of patient 2); (3) Patients' cardiac function and structure remained at baseline levels. The overall trend of LVEF showed a slight declining trend and the overall trend of LVM Z-score showed a slight upward trend, however, the median trends for both indicators are very weak, with slopes of −0.013 and 0.004, respectively; (4) All documented children exhibited growth during the observation period, although the gene therapy did not improve their percentile distribution in growth parameters; (5) Patients 1, 2, and 3 achieved key milestones such as unassisted crawling, standing, and walking during the observation period (the independent standing and walking of patient 1 occurred in the 13th month); (6) Muscle biopsy findings from the subset of three treated participants suggested reduced skeletal muscle glycogen accumulation, alongside apparent improvements in muscle structure; (7) PedsQL 3.0 NMM scores indicated overall improvement in quality of life; and (8) Improvement in GMFM-88 scores was observed in patients 1 and 2.

The longitudinal clinical trajectories of the six patients were reviewed from the initial diagnosis through the ERT period to the 52-week observation period following gene therapy (Supplementary Figure S1). At initial diagnosis, these patients presented with myocardial hypertrophy. Following ERT, the hypertrophy was ameliorated to a certain extent in all patients. Upon enrollment, the LVM Z-score showed a slight increase in 4 of the 5 pediatric patients, while their LVEF was maintained within the normal range. Five out of six patients displayed no improvement in pulmonary function; however, all patients remained alive and ventilator-free over the 52 weeks (with the exception of patient 4, who developed severe pneumonia). Considering that the natural history of IOPD indicates a median age of death of only 8.7 months in untreated patients, predominantly due to cardiopulmonary failure,34 the outcomes following GC301 treatment provide preliminary evidence of clinical activity in this study population.

The gross motor function is influenced by multiple factors, such as the patient's baseline status at the time of treatment and cardiopulmonary function. For instance, the decline in motor function relative to baseline observed in patient 6 at 52 weeks post-treatment must be interpreted in light of the fact that this patient presented with severe motor impairment upon enrollment (a GMFM-88 score of only 15.8%). Such an advanced underlying disease state suggests that chronic glycogen accumulation had likely already caused irreversible muscle fiber damage, atrophy, and fibrofatty replacement. Despite histological evidence of reduced glycogen accumulation at the cellular level, residual muscle was insufficient to reverse the already established clinical motor deficits. Thus, improvement in gross motor function may require a longer observation period. Similarly, given that alteration of LVM is a slow pathophysiological process, a 52-week observation window may be insufficient to fully capture long-term structural changes. An ongoing long-term follow-up study is concurrently being conducted to evaluate these patients for up to five years post-treatment, and subsequent results will be reported in due course.

Although all three patients had received 19 to 85 rounds of ERT prior to GC301 gene therapy, baseline muscle histopathological analysis revealed substantial glycogen accumulation, numerous lipid vacuoles, compromised structural integrity, uneven mitochondrial distribution, and fatty infiltration. After GC301 gene therapy, histological data from muscle biopsies demonstrated marked changes: glycogen accumulation was reduced, muscle integrity was enhanced, mitochondrial density increased and became more uniformly distributed, and fatty infiltration was reduced. These findings suggest that GAA gene therapy using a recombinant AAV9 vector is associated with increased GAA expression in muscle tissue and subsequent alterations in muscle structure and function. In contrast, changes in respiratory muscle-related indicators were less apparent, which may be attributed to the distribution characteristics of the intravenously administered AAV9.35,36

GAA enzyme activity was monitored in peripheral blood for one year and in muscle tissue at 26/39 weeks post-gene therapy. Patient 1's baseline GAA activity was 82.8 μmol/L/hr, likely reflecting residual effects from prior ERT. The median blood GAA activity in the six patients gradually declined from 1.9 μmol/L/hr at week 1 to 0.9 μmol/L/hr by week 52. The peak in blood GAA activity observed during the initial phase (week 4–13) of gene therapy likely originates in part from AAV9-mediated GAA gene expression in circulating peripheral blood cells and in part from minor secretion by the transduced tissues. As peripheral blood cells have a relatively short lifespan and undergo rapid natural turnover and apoptosis, the subsequent decline in blood GAA levels is biologically expected. In contrast, the median GAA activity in muscle tissue at week 26/39 was 24.67 nmol/mg/hr. If the enzymatic activity of muscle tissue is simply converted to the same unit as that of serum enzyme activity, assuming 1 mg of muscle is equivalent to 1 mL in volume, the enzymatic activity in muscle tissue at 26/39 weeks after gene therapy was 24.67 μmol/L/hr, which is substantially higher than the corresponding serum enzyme activity at similar time points (1.0 μmol/L/hr at week 26 and 0.7 μmol/L/hr at week 39). While the standardization of GAA enzyme activity measurement and its sensitivity and specificity in diagnosing Pompe disease are still in the process of being improved,37,38 based on the patients' clinical manifestations over the one-year observation period, our gene therapy dosage appears to maintain effective GAA levels for at least one year.

Throughout the 52-week observation period, CK, LDH, AST, and ALT remained generally elevated, with CK showing an overall upward trend. These biomarkers were persistently elevated from diagnosis through the ERT period and generally remained near baseline levels after gene therapy (Supplementary Figure S1). The fluctuation and upward trend in CK levels may be driven by a transient decrease during initial prophylactic corticosteroid treatment, followed by a gradual rebound as steroids were tapered.39 In IOPD, massive glycogen accumulation causes lysosomal swelling and rupture, releasing hydrolytic enzymes into the cytoplasm and triggering cell necrosis, pyroptosis, and apoptosis, ultimately driving chronic sterile inflammation—a possible key mechanism underlying elevated CK, LDH, AST, and ALT.40 However, CK is also a key enzyme in creatine metabolism. It is possible that GAA deficiency disrupts cellular energy homeostasis, which prompts skeletal muscle to upregulate creatine metabolism for energy supply. Thus, metabolic stress and pathway switching, rather than active muscle destruction, are the likely core drivers of persistently elevated CK in Pompe disease.41, 42, 43, 44 Currently, no evidence links changes in CK levels to therapeutic efficacy.

Although next-generation ERT has been optimized, it still relies on lifelong, bi-weekly exogenous enzyme infusions.45,46 In contrast, AAV9-mediated gene therapy achieves sustained, multi-organ endogenous GAA expression with a single dose. Recent preclinical studies47, 48, 49 have shown that muscle-targeted promoters can lower vector doses while maintaining high expression in skeletal and cardiac muscles. However, IOPD is a severe multisystemic disorder requiring widespread and sustained functional improvement across multiple organs, including the myocardium, skeletal muscle, respiratory muscles, and the CNS.46 To meet this therapeutic goal, the GC301 expression cassette uses a ubiquitous CMV enhancer/chicken β-actin promoter to drive broad gene expression, achieving high GAA activity not only in muscles but also in the liver. It is theoretically possible that the liver could act as a systemic reservoir, secreting functional GAA into the circulation. However, the relatively low and declining blood GAA levels observed in our study suggest that the contribution of liver-derived GAA to muscle glycogen clearance is likely limited. Therefore, the observed changes in skeletal muscle are likely associated with direct transduction of muscle fibers by GC301, as supported by the higher GAA enzyme activity and protein expression detected in muscle biopsies.

Regarding safety, GC301 treatment in patients with IOPD compares favorably to high-dose AAV trials for other neuromuscular diseases, which have shown severe toxicity such as hepatotoxicity or thrombotic microangiopathy.50, 51, 52 In our cohort, post-dosing ALT and AST elevations were transient and self-limiting. This favorable safety profile is largely due to our prophylactic corticosteroid regimen and the baculovirus-insect cell system.24 This system utilizes biologically contained, species-specific expression cassettes and entirely lacks the AAV p5 promoter.53 These design features are intended to minimize the risk of generating replication-competent AAV (rcAAV) by limiting the homology sequences required for recombination, thereby providing a built-in safety mechanism at the point of delivery.53,54 In contrast, the HEK293 triple-transfection system has an inherent biological potential to generate rcAAV through recombination.55,56 In a reported case, metagenomic analysis of a liver biopsy from a patient with severe hepatitis identified residual sequences from manufacturing plasmids and complex vector genome rearrangements. The authors postulated that these contaminants, potentially in conjunction with helper viruses like HHV-6B, might contribute to hepatotoxicity, although a formal causal link remains to be established.57 The hypothesis is that in a patient with a high vector load and latent human herpesvirus 6B (which may provide helper functions), these rcAAV particles find conditions conducive to amplification.

Serious adverse events were mainly upper respiratory tract infections and pneumonia, which reflect the underlying disease characteristics due to inherent respiratory muscle weakness and impaired airway clearance in IOPD. Apart from expected steroid-induced dyslipidemia, drug-related adverse events were confined to mild post-dosing fever and transient transaminase elevations.

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size was necessitated by the rare and specific clinical progression of Pompe disease, which also precluded a randomized controlled trial design; therefore, all statistical results should be interpreted with caution. Second, as a single-center investigation, the study's representativeness and external validity are limited.58 Third, this study employed only a single dose and was unable to determine the optimal therapeutic dose. Furthermore, this study is subject to two key limitations regarding muscle biopsy analyses. First, the limited tissue yield was entirely allocated to pre-specified core assays, precluding quantification of glycogen content, autophagic flux, and the prevalence of central nuclei. Second, although three participants (Patients 1–3) achieved major motor milestones—including independent walking—paired pre- and post-treatment biopsies were available only for patient 3. Consequently, evidence correlating gene therapy with molecular histopathological improvement remains limited. Finally, interpretation of neurological outcomes is constrained by two factors: first, the age-dependent permeability of the blood–brain barrier to AAV9,59 which may have limited CNS transduction in our cohort (median age 25.5 months), although preclinical data in adult and aged mice suggest that partial CNS activity persists; and second, the 52-week observation period, which may be insufficient to detect slowly progressive neurological effects.

Recombinant AAV9-mediated GAA gene therapy shows promise in decreasing the substantial reliance of IOPD patients on ERT, while also inducing changes in both development and skeletal muscle pathology.
