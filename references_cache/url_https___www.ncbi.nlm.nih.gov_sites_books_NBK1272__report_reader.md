K.</p><p><a>Publication Details</a></p><p><em>Estimated reading time: 26 minutes</em></p></div></div><div><div><h2>Summary</h2><div><h4>Clinical characteristics.</h4><p><i>GJB2</i>-related autosomal recessive nonsyndromic hearing loss (<i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL) is the most common genetic cause of congenital (present at birth) severe-to-profound non-progressive sensorineural hearing loss in many world populations. In countries where available, newborn hearing screening (NBHS) typically identifies severe-to-profound hearing loss.</p><p><i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL can also be mild to moderate and is usually not progressive; however, it can progress. Congenital mild-to-moderate <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL is not detected by NBHS.</p><p><i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL has no related systemic findings.</p></div><div><h4>Diagnosis/testing.</h4><p>The diagnosis of <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL is established in a proband with suggestive findings and biallelic <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variants identified by molecular genetic testing. Of note: About 1% of individuals with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL are compound heterozygotes for one <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variant and one of several different deletions that include sequences upstream of <i>GJB2</i> (comprising either <i>GJB6</i> and portions of <i>CRYL1</i> or just portions of <i>CRYL1</i>) that delete regulatory regions of <i>GJB2</i> on the same allele, thereby abolishing <i>GJB2</i> expression. Occasionally, the deletion also includes <i>GJB2</i>.</p></div><div><h4>Management.</h4><p><i>Treatment of manifestations:</i> It is recommended that NBHS be completed by age one month, the genetic diagnosis be established by age three months, and early intervention begun by age six months. (This recommendation is also known as "the 1-3-6 benchmark.") In the United States, the states that recommend the 1-3-6 benchmark should actually strive for a "1-2-3" timeline.</p><p>Because children with severe-to-profound hearing loss who are candidates for cochlear implantation can attain levels of social functioning and education indistinguishable from those of normal-hearing peers, cochlear implantation should be performed as soon as possible.</p><p>Children with mild-to-moderate hearing loss can be treated with hearing aids customized to the child's age and severity of hearing loss.</p><p><i>Surveillance:</i> Most children with severe-to-profound <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL who are cochlear implant recipients initially require frequent follow-up visits with their cochlear implant team (otolaryngologist, audiologist, and speech-language pathologist) for assessment of speech recognition and equipment check. Once the cochlear implant recipient and their family become comfortable with the cochlear implant, many of these tasks can be performed by the family at home.</p><p>All children with mild-to-moderate <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL require follow-up audiograms annually to detect any progression of hearing loss. Children using hearing aids typically have an annual evaluation by an otolaryngologist, audiologist, and hearing aid specialist to examine the ears, obtain an audiogram, and check hearing aid function.</p><p><i>Agents/circumstances to avoid:</i> Individuals with mild-to-moderate hearing loss should avoid environmental exposures known to cause hearing loss. Most important for persons with <i>GJB2</i>-related mild-to-moderate hearing loss is avoidance of repeated overexposure to loud noises (i.e., &gt;75 decibels), particularly secondary to earbud use. Note that the headphone safety feature built into many smartphones can be set to a maximum limit of decibels.</p><p><i>Evaluation of relatives at risk:</i> With limited exceptions, it is appropriate to clarify the genetic status of sibs of a proband with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL; early identification of infants and children with hearing loss allows appropriate support and management to be provided to the child and family.</p></div><div><h4>Genetic counseling.</h4><p><i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner. The parents of a child with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL are typically heterozygous for a <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variant. In populations with a high carrier rate, it is possible that either one or both parents of a child with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL also have <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL. If both parents are known to be heterozygous for a pathogenic variant, each sib of an individual with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL has at conception a 25% chance of inheriting biallelic pathogenic variants and having hearing loss, a 50% chance of inheriting one pathogenic variant, and a 25% chance of inheriting neither of the familial <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variants. Once the <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variants have been identified in a family member with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL, prenatal and preimplantation genetic testing for <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL are possible.</p></div></div><div><h2>Diagnosis</h2><div><h3>Suggestive Findings</h3><p>The diagnosis of <i>GJB2</i>-related autosomal recessive nonsyndromic hearing loss (<i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL) should be considered in two scenarios: an <a>abnormal newborn hearing screening (NBHS) result</a> and a <a>symptomatic individual</a>.</p><div><h4>Scenario 1: Abnormal Newborn Hearing Screening (NBHS) Result</h4><p>Universal NBHS using physiologic screening (either otoacoustic emissions [OAE], which measure the response of cochlear outer hair cells to auditory stimuli, or automated auditory brain stem response [ABR], which measures the physiologic response of cochlear inner hair cells, auditory nerve, brain stem, and brain to auditory stimuli) is required by law or rule in all 50 states in the United States and is performed on &gt;98% of children in the US typically within days after birth (see <a>2020 Summary of National CDC EHDI Data</a>).</p><p><b>Note:</b> (1) NBHS is designed to detect moderate-to-profound hearing loss and may miss neonates with mild hearing loss, an important consideration, since data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) show that more than 25% of bilateral deafness in newborns is slight or mild (&lt;40 dB) (see <a>2020 Type and Severity Summary of Identified Cases of Hearing Loss</a>). An even larger percentage of newborns may be affected by this degree of hearing loss, as very mild hearing loss can go undetected by NBHS (depending on the hearing thresholds and testing methods used). Although implementing physiologic screening at lower hearing thresholds would detect more mild hearing loss, it would also increase the rate of false positive test results and burden confirmatory diagnostic audiology evaluations. (2) The exact hearing thresholds used in NBHS are difficult to define precisely because manufacturers of equipment used in auditory screening tests choose the stimulus levels for their equipment, and these cannot be adjusted by the operator [<a>Shearer et al 2019</a>].</p><p>The following evaluations need to begin immediately on receipt of an abnormal NBHS result:</p><ul><li><div><b>The first step</b> is to perform confirmatory audiometric testing, typically diagnostic ABR testing.</div></li><li><div><b>The next step</b> is a medical evaluation by an otolaryngologist, who is often the first point of contact for children with newly diagnosed hearing loss and will perform examinations including: (1) otomicroscopic evaluation for other causes of hearing loss such as conductive hearing loss resulting from otitis media (fluid in the middle ear) and outer and middle ear abnormalities; and (2) evaluate for features of a syndrome that may be associated with hearing loss.</div></li><li><div>The otolaryngologist will consider family history, gestational history, physical examination, audiometric testing, and molecular genetic testing to determine the underlying diagnosis (see <a>Establishing the Diagnosis</a>).</div></li></ul></div><div><h4>Scenario 2: Symptomatic Individual</h4><p><i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL <b>should be suspected</b> in a three- to five-year-old child with the following clinical findings and family history.</p><p>
<b>Clinical findings</b>
</p><ul><li><div><b>Mild-to-moderate typically non-progressive sensorineural hearing loss</b> as measured by ABR testing or pure tone audiometry</div></li><li><div><b>No related systemic findings</b> identified by medical history and physical examination</div></li></ul><p>Hearing is measured in <b>decibels</b> (dB). The threshold or 0 dB mark for each frequency refers to the level at which young adults with normal hearing perceive a tone burst 50% of the time. Hearing is considered normal if an individual's hearing thresholds are within 15 dB of normal thresholds. Severity of hearing loss is graded as shown in <a>Table 1</a>.</p><div><a><img/></a><div><h4><a>Table 1. </a></h4><p>Severity of Hearing Loss in Decibels (dB) </p></div></div><p><b>Family history</b> is consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance (e.g., affected sibs and/or parental consanguinity). Absence of a known family history does not preclude the diagnosis. Note: Pseudodominant inheritance (i.e., an autosomal recessive condition present in individuals in two or more generations) has been reported in some families (see <a>Genetic Counseling</a>).</p></div></div><div><h3>Establishing the Diagnosis</h3><p>The diagnosis of <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL <b>is established</b>
<b>in a proband</b> with <a>suggestive findings</a> who has EITHER of the following identified by molecular genetic testing (see <a>Table 2</a>):</p><ul><li><div>Biallelic <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic (or likely pathogenic) variants (99%);</div></li></ul><p>OR</p><ul><li><div>Compound heterozygosity for one <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic (or likely pathogenic) variant and one of the following findings:</div><ul><li><div>A deletion that is either (1) an intragenic deletion or (2) a whole-gene deletion (see <a>Molecular Genetics</a>, <b><i>GJB2</i>-specific laboratory technical considerations)</b>;</div></li><li><div>A noncoding pathogenic (or likely pathogenic) variant upstream or downstream of <i>GJB2</i>; or</div></li><li><div>Mosaic uniparental disomy of <i>GJB2</i>.</div></li></ul></li></ul><p>Note: (1) Per ACMG/AMP variant interpretation guidelines, the terms "pathogenic variant" and "likely pathogenic variant" are synonymous in a clinical setting, meaning that both are considered diagnostic and can be used for clinical decision making [<a>Richards et al 2015</a>]. For expert specification of the ACMG/AMP variant interpretation for genetic hearing loss, see <a>Oza et al [2018]</a> (<a>full text</a>). Reference to "pathogenic variants" in this section is understood to include likely pathogenic variants. (2) Identification of biallelic <i>GJB2</i> variants of uncertain significance (or of one known <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variant and one <i>GJB2</i> variant of uncertain significance) does not establish or rule out the diagnosis.</p><p>Molecular genetic testing approaches can include a combination of <b>gene-targeted testing</b> (multigene panel) and <b>comprehensive</b>
<b>genomic testing</b> (exome sequencing, genome sequencing). Gene-targeted testing requires that the clinician determine which gene(s) are likely involved (see <a>Option 1</a>), whereas comprehensive genomic testing does not (see <a>Option 2</a>).</p><p>Note: Single-gene testing (sequence analysis of <i>GJB2</i>, followed by gene-targeted deletion/duplication analysis) is rarely useful and typically NOT recommended.</p><div><h4>Option 1</h4><p><b>A hearing loss multigene panel</b> that includes all genes implicated in nonsyndromic hearing loss and disorders that mimic nonsyndromic hearing loss including <i>GJB2</i> and other genes of interest (see <a>Differential Diagnosis</a> and <a>Genetic Hearing Loss Overview</a>) is most likely to identify the genetic cause of the condition while limiting identification of variants of uncertain significance and pathogenic variants in genes that do not explain the underlying phenotype. Note: (1) The genes included in the panel and the diagnostic sensitivity of the testing used for each gene vary by laboratory and are likely to change over time. (2) Some multigene panels may include genes not associated with the condition discussed in this <i>GeneReview</i>. (3) In some laboratories, panel options may include a custom laboratory-designed panel and/or custom phenotype-focused exome analysis that includes genes specified by the clinician. (4) Analytic methods used for this panel must include the detection of deletions of <i>GJB2</i>, either intragenic or whole gene, and deletions that include sequences upstream of <i>GJB2</i> (comprising either <i>GJB6</i> and portions of <i>CRYL1</i> or just portions of <i>CRYL1</i>) that delete regulatory regions of <i>GJB2</i> on the same allele, thereby abolishing <i>GJB2</i> expression). See also <a>Molecular Genetics</a>, <b><i>GJB2</i>-specific laboratory technical considerations</b>.</p><p>For an introduction to multigene panels click <a>here</a>. More detailed information for clinicians ordering genetic tests can be found <a>here</a>.</p></div><div><h4>Option 2</h4><p><b>Comprehensive</b>
<b>genomic testing</b> does not require the clinician to determine which gene is likely involved. <b>Exome sequencing</b> is most commonly used; <b>genome sequencing</b> is also possible.</p><p>For an introduction to comprehensive genomic testing click <a>here</a>. More detailed information for clinicians ordering genomic testing can be found <a>here</a>.</p><div><a><img/></a><div><h4><a>Table 2. </a></h4><p>Molecular Genetic Testing Used in <i>GJB2</i>-Related Autosomal Recessive Nonsyndromic Hearing Loss </p></div></div></div></div></div><div><h2>Clinical Characteristics</h2><div><h3>Clinical Description</h3><p><i>GJB2</i>-related autosomal recessive nonsyndromic hearing loss (<i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL) is the most common genetic cause of congenital (present at birth) severe-to-profound non-progressive sensorineural hearing loss in many world populations. <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL can also be mild to moderate and is usually not progressive; however, there are exceptions.</p><p><b>Severe-to-profound <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL</b> is typically congenital and diagnosed on newborn hearing screening (NBHS); however, there are occasional exceptions.</p><p>In two examples, a newborn passed his NBHS auditory brain stem response (ABR)-based test, but was diagnosed as deaf at age 15 months when an ABR test obtained at the request of his parents showed no responses through 90 dB. Another child had a normal free-field audiogram performed by an experienced audiologist at age five months, but at age nine months had ABR-confirmed severe hearing loss [<a>Green et al 2000</a>].</p><p>Severe-to-profound <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL is most frequently associated with truncating <i>GJB2</i> variants (see <a>Genotype-Phenotype Correlations</a>).</p><p><b>Mild-to-moderate <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL</b> may escape detection on the NBHS, especially when the hearing loss is mild. Most mild-to-moderate <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL is not progressive; however, an important exception is the unique trajectory of biallelic p.Val37Ile pathogenic variants, which has been well documented by <a>Chen et al [2022]</a> (see <a>Genotype-Phenotype Correlations</a>).</p><p>Mild-to-moderate <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL is most often associated with either biallelic missense variants or a missense variant together in a compound heterozygous state with a truncating variant (see <a>Genotype-Phenotype Correlations</a>).</p><p><b>Intrafamilial variability in the degree of <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL.</b> The majority (67%) of sib pairs will have similar levels of hearing loss (within 20 dB); however, in a significant percentage (33%), there will be a prominent difference in hearing levels (&gt;30 dB) between sibs [<a>Fujioka et al 2020</a>].</p><p><b>All <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL.</b> Vestibular function is normal. Affected infants and young children do not experience balance problems and learn to sit and walk at age-appropriate times.</p><p>Except for the hearing impairment, individuals with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL are healthy; life span is normal.</p></div><div><h3>Genotype-Phenotype Correlations</h3><div><h4>Class of <i>GJB2</i> Pathogenic Variants</h4><p><b>Truncating vs nontruncating variants</b>. The strong genotype-phenotype correlations with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL can be recognized by classifying <i>GJB2</i> variants as truncating (T) or nontruncating (NT) and then defining three genotype classes: biallelic truncating (T/T), biallelic nontruncating (NT/NT), and compound heterozygous (T/NT) [<a>Snoeckx et al 2005</a>]. Hearing loss across these classes is nonrandomly distributed, with loss in the T/T class being more severe than in the T/NT class, which is more severe than in the NT/NT class. See <a>Figure 3</a> in <a>Snoeckx et al [2005]</a> (<a>full text</a>), which provides audiograms for various genotypes, showing the average hearing thresholds (50th centile) and the expected range (10th centile and 90th centile) for common genotypes.</p><ul><li><div>In the T/T class, hearing loss is most typically severe to profound (84%-92% of persons), although moderate (10%-12%) and mild (0%-3%) hearing losses are reported.</div></li><li><div>In the T/NT class, 34%-47% of persons have severe-to-profound hearing loss.</div></li><li><div>In the NT/NT class, only one person in five has severe-to-profound hearing loss, with more than half of persons having mild hearing loss.</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Large deletions involving <i>GJB2</i></b>
</p><ul><li><div>When a large <i>GJB2</i> deletion occurs in the compound heterozygous state with either a <i>GJB2</i> truncating variant or a missense variant, hearing loss is severe to profound.</div></li><li><div>Homozygosity for a large <i>GJB2</i> deletion, which is very rare, is associated with severe to profound sensorineural hearing loss [<a>Pandya et al 2020</a>].</div></li></ul></div><div><h4>Specific <i>GJB2</i> Variants</h4><p><a><b>p.Val37Ile</b></a><b>.</b>
<a>Chen et al [2022]</a> reported that more than 40% of newborns either homozygous for p.Val37Ile or compound heterozygous for p.Val37Ile and a truncating <i>GJB2</i> variant have hearing thresholds lower than 20 dB (i.e., normal hearing levels) on click-evoked ABR-based testing and pass their distortion-product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAE)-based NBHS. However, hearing thresholds decline with age so that ~60% of 40- to 60-year-olds and ~80% of 60- to 85-year-olds with this genotype have moderate hearing loss.</p></div></div><div><h3>Nomenclature</h3><p><i>GJB2</i>-related autosomal recessive nonsyndromic hearing loss (<i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL) may also be referred by its genetic locus, DFNB1 (DFN for DeaFNess, B to indicate autosomal recessive inheritance, and 1 to indicate the relative order of hearing loss-related gene mapping). However, locus-based naming can be problematic when more than one gene is mapped to a given locus, as is the case with <i>GJB2</i> and <i>GJB6</i>, which both map to the DFNB1 locus (13q12.11). For clarity, gene-based naming is used in this <i>GeneReview</i> rather than locus-based naming.</p></div><div><h3>Prevalence</h3><p>The prevalence of <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL and the carrier rate for pathogenic <i>GJB2</i> variants are population dependent (see <a>Figure 1</a>). Some of this variability may reflect ascertainment bias. For example, although <a>Adadey et al [2022]</a> present the most comprehensive data of the landscape of genetic hearing loss to date, they note that in Africa the causes of genetic hearing loss are still to be investigated in a large majority of African countries. Based on currently available data, the highest carrier rate (8.3%) is reported in the East Asian population, in which the most common pathogenic variant is p.Val37Ile.</p><div><a><img/></a><div><h4><a>Figure 1. </a></h4><p>The bar is the carrier frequency for a pathogenic variant in <i>GJB2</i>. Note that the carrier frequency for a pathogenic (or likely pathogenic) variant in <i>GJB2</i> is highest in the East Asian population, reflecting the exceptionally high carrier frequency for <a>(more...)</a></p></div></div></div></div><div><h2>Genetically Related (Allelic) Disorders</h2><p>Fewer than 30 <i>GJB2</i> variants are associated with autosomal dominant inheritance [<a>Azaiez et al 2018</a>].</p><p>In most of these case reports, the severity of the hearing loss is variable and is associated with a spectrum of skin manifestations such as keratitis-ichthyosis-deafness syndrome (OMIM <a>148210</a>), hystrix-like ichthyosis with deafness (OMIM <a>602540</a>), Vohwinkel syndrome (OMIM <a>124500</a>), Bart-Pumphrey syndrome (OMIM <a>149200</a>), and palmoplantar keratoderma with deafness (OMIM <a>148350</a>) [<a>Iossa et al 2011</a>].</p><p>Two variants at amino acid residue 75 – p.Arg75Gln and p.Arg75Trp – have been reported to cause autosomal dominant hearing loss. Both of these missense changes affect the same amino acid and are associated with hearing loss that is fully penetrant and a skin phenotype (palmoplantar keratoderma) that is not fully penetrant [<a>Birkenhäger et al 2010</a>]. This phenotypic variability occurs even within the same family [<a>Manzoli et al 2013</a>].</p></div><div><h2>Differential Diagnosis</h2><p>As of this writing, more than 70 genes have been associated with autosomal recessive nonsyndromic hearing loss. For a list of selected genes associated with distinctive clinical features, see Genetic Hearing Loss Overview, <a>Table 3</a>.</p><p>For a current, comprehensive list of all identified autosomal recessive nonsyndromic hearing loss genes, see <a>Hereditary Hearing Loss Homepage</a>.</p></div><div><h2>Management</h2><p>No clinical practice guidelines specific to <i>GJB2</i>-related autosomal recessive nonsyndromic hearing loss (<i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL) have been published.</p><p>Management ideally occurs in the context of a multidisciplinary clinic with specialists in otolaryngology, audiology, and genetic counseling. See Genetic Hearing Loss Overview, <a>Management</a>.</p><div><h3>Evaluations Following Initial Diagnosis</h3><p>To establish the extent of involvement and needs in an individual diagnosed with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL, the following evaluations by an otolaryngologist are recommended:</p><ul><li><div>Comprehensive medical history focused on gestational and perinatal events, newborn hearing screen (NBHS) results, and cytomegalic virus (CMV) test results (if performed)</div></li><li><div>Assessment of hearing using age-appropriate tests such as auditory brain stem response (ABR) testing, auditory steady-state response (ASSR) testing, and pure-tone audiometry to determine severity of hearing loss (see <a>Table 1</a>) and laterality (expected to be symmetric)</div></li><li><div>Physical examination including an evaluation for syndromic features (which should be absent) and a thorough examination of the head and neck, including an otomicroscopic evaluation of the ear to evaluate for otitis media</div></li><li><div>Ophthalmologic examination to identify refractive errors (unrelated to <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL) that require spectacle correction (glasses) to improve visual acuity</div></li><li><div>Consultation with a medical geneticist, certified genetic counselor, or certified advanced genetic nurse to inform affected individuals and their families about the nature, mode of inheritance, and implications of <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL in order to facilitate medical and personal decision making</div></li><li><div>Assessment of need for family support and resources including community or online <a>resources</a> such as <a>Parent to Parent</a>, social work involvement for parental support, and home nursing referral</div></li></ul></div><div><h3>Treatment of Manifestations</h3><p>Management of children with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL involves discussion of the following issues with the family.</p><p>Uncorrected hearing loss (regardless of etiology) has consistent sequelae. Auditory deprivation during the first two years of life is associated with poor reading performance, poor communication skills, and poor speech production, deficiencies that cannot be completely remediated even with educational intervention. Early auditory intervention is effective – whether through cochlear implantation or amplification [<a>Smith et al 2005</a>].</p><p>To optimize intervention for children with hearing loss, the <a>Joint Committee on Infant Hearing [2019]</a> recommends universal NBHS be completed by age one month, diagnosis established by age three months, and early intervention begun by age six months (also known as "the 1-3-6 benchmark"). In the United States, the states that recommend the 1-3-6 benchmark should actually strive for a "1-2-3" timeline.</p><p><b>Children with severe-to-profound hearing loss</b> who are candidates for cochlear implantation can attain levels of social functioning and education indistinguishable from those of normal-hearing peers [<a>Loy et al 2010</a>, <a>Langereis &amp; Vermeulen 2015</a>]. Cochlear implantation performed as soon as possible is associated with significantly better long-term auditory performance and speech intelligibility than later cochlear implantation [<a>Wu et al 2015</a>, <a>Eshraghi et al 2020</a>]. Nonetheless, there is no upper age limit for cochlear implantation; however, at older ages habilitation outcomes are less predictable.</p><p><b>Children with mild-to-moderate hearing loss</b> can be treated with hearing aids customized to the child's age and severity of hearing loss.</p></div><div><h3>Surveillance</h3><p>To monitor the individual's response to habituation and to identify any changes in severity of hearing loss, the following evaluations are recommended.</p><p><b>Most children with severe-to-profound <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL with a cochlear implant</b> will initially have frequent evaluations recommended by their cochlear implant team (otolaryngologist, audiologist, and speech and language pathologist).</p><p>At follow-up appointments, the following may be performed: speech recognition testing, equipment checks (including device adjustment and troubleshooting), and provision of replacement or upgraded equipment [<a>Cullington et al 2016</a>].</p><p>As the cochlear implant recipient becomes comfortable with the cochlear implant, many of the above tasks can be performed by the family at home, obviating the need for routinely scheduled appointments unless the need for a clinic visit arises.</p><p>
<b>Children with mild-to-moderate <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL</b>
</p><ul><li><div>An annual evaluation by an otolaryngologist, audiologist, and hearing aid specialist is recommended to examine the ears, obtain an audiogram, and check hearing aid function.</div></li><li><div>This annual follow up is essential, as progression of hearing loss can occur. For most <i>GJB2</i> genotypes associated with mild-to-moderate hearing loss, hearing is stable; however, the variant <a>p.Val37Ile</a> is associated with progressive hearing loss. In one study, one of eight persons homozygous for the p.Val37Ile pathogenic variant underwent cochlear implantation due to progression of mild-to-moderate hearing loss; six of eight used hearing aids; and one of eight was not treated [<a>Lee et al 2021</a>].</div></li></ul></div><div><h3>Agents/Circumstances to Avoid</h3><p>Individuals with hearing loss should avoid environmental exposures known to cause hearing loss.</p><p>Most important for persons with <i>GJB2</i>-related mild-to-moderate hearing loss is avoidance of repeated overexposure to loud noises, particularly secondary to earbud use. The headphone safety feature built into most smartphones can be set a maximum limit of 75 dB.</p><p>Headphone/earbud safety features can be found in the phone settings menu:</p><ul><li><div>In iPhones, under Settings &gt; Sounds &amp; Haptics &gt; Headphone Safety</div></li><li><div>In Android phones, under Settings &gt; Sounds &amp; Vibrations &gt; Volume &gt; Media volume limit</div></li></ul><p>Also see these general resources on noise reduction:</p><ul><li><div>

<a>How Do I Prevent Hearing Loss from Loud Noise?</a>
</div></li></ul></div><div><h3>Evaluation of Relatives at Risk</h3><p>It is appropriate to clarify the genetic status of sibs of a proband with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL if:</p><ul><li><div>A newborn sib has an abnormal result on universal NBHS;</div></li><li><div>A newborn sib has a <b>normal</b> result on NBHS and the <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variants identified in the proband are associated with mild hearing loss (as NBHS may miss newborns with mild hearing loss); or</div></li><li><div>A sib did not undergo NBHS and/or NBHS results are unknown.</div></li></ul><p>Early identification of infants and children with hearing loss allows appropriate support and management to be provided to the child and family.</p><p>See <a>Genetic Counseling</a> for issues related to testing of at-risk relatives for genetic counseling purposes.</p></div><div><h3>Therapies Under Investigation</h3><p>Search <a>ClinicalTrials.gov</a> in the US and <a>EU Clinical Trials Register</a> in Europe for information on clinical studies for a wide range of diseases and conditions. Note: There may not be clinical trials for this condition.</p></div></div><div><h2>Genetic Counseling</h2><p>
<i>Genetic counseling is the process of providing individuals and families with
information on the nature, mode(s) of inheritance, and implications of genetic disorders to help them
make informed medical and personal decisions. The following section deals with genetic
risk assessment and the use of family history and genetic testing to clarify genetic
status for family members; it is not meant to address all personal, cultural, or
ethical issues that may arise or to substitute for consultation with a genetics
professional</i>. —ED.</p><div><h3>Mode of Inheritance</h3><p>By definition, <i>GJB2</i>-related autosomal recessive nonsyndromic hearing loss (<i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL) is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner.</p><p>Note: Pseudodominant inheritance (i.e., an autosomal recessive condition present in individuals in two or more generations) is reported in some families [<a>Pampanos et al 2000</a>, <a>Tekin et al 2003</a>] and is more likely to occur in populations with a high carrier rate (see <a>Figure 1</a>).</p></div><div><h3>Risk to Family Members</h3><p>
<b>Parents of a proband</b>
</p><ul><li><div>The parents of a child with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL are typically heterozygous for a <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variant.</div></li><li><div>In populations with a high carrier rate (e.g., the East Asian population, in which the carrier frequency for p.Val37Ile is 8.3% [see <a>Figure 1</a>]), it is possible that either one or both parents of a child with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL also have <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL.</div></li><li><div>Molecular genetic testing of the parents is recommended to confirm that both parents are heterozygous for a <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variant and to allow reliable counseling regarding the likelihood of recurrence.</div></li><li><div>If a pathogenic variant is detected in only one parent and parental identity testing has confirmed biological maternity and paternity, it is possible that one of the pathogenic variants identified in the proband occurred as a <i>de novo</i> event in the proband or as a postzygotic <i>de novo</i> event in a mosaic parent [<a>Jónsson et al 2017</a>]. If the proband appears to have homozygous pathogenic variants (i.e., the same two pathogenic variants), additional possibilities to consider include:</div><ul><li><div>A deletion of a portion of <i>GJB2</i> in the proband that was not detected by sequence analysis and that resulted in the artifactual appearance of homozygosity [<a>Abe et al 2018</a>];</div></li><li><div>Uniparental isodisomy for the parental chromosome with the pathogenic variant that resulted in homozygosity for the pathogenic variant in the proband.</div></li></ul></li><li><div>Heterozygotes (carriers) of a <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variant associated with autosomal recessive nonsyndromic hearing loss are asymptomatic.</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Sibs of a proband</b>
</p><ul><li><div>If both parents are known to be heterozygous for a <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variant, each sib of an individual with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL has at conception a 25% chance of inheriting biallelic <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variants and having hearing loss, a 50% chance of inheriting one <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variant, and a 25% chance of inheriting neither of the familial <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variants.</div></li><li><div>Strong <a>genotype-phenotype correlations</a> are observed in <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL.</div></li><li><div>The majority of sibs (67%) who inherit biallelic <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variants have a hearing threshold similar (within 20 dB) to that of the proband; a prominent difference (&gt;30 dB) in hearing thresholds is seen in 33% of sib pairs [<a>Snoeckx et al 2005</a>, <a>Fujioka et al 2020</a>].</div></li><li><div>Heterozygotes (carriers) of a <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variant associated with autosomal recessive nonsyndromic hearing loss are asymptomatic.</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Offspring of a proband</b>
</p><ul><li><div>Unless an affected individual's reproductive partner also has <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL or is a carrier, offspring will be obligate heterozygotes (carriers) for a pathogenic variant in <i>GJB2.</i></div></li><li><div>In populations with a high carrier rate (see <a>Figure 1</a>), the reproductive partner of the proband may have two <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variants or be heterozygous. Thus, the likelihood of <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL recurrence in offspring is most accurately determined after <i>GJB2</i> molecular genetic testing of the proband's reproductive partner.</div></li></ul><p><b>Other family members.</b> Each sib of the proband's parents has a 50% chance of being a carrier of a <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variant.</p></div><div><h3>Carrier Detection</h3><p>Carrier testing for relatives of an individual with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL requires prior identification of the <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variants in the family.</p></div><div><h3>Related Genetic Counseling Issues</h3><p>See <a>Evaluation of Relatives at Risk</a> for information on evaluating at-risk relatives for the purpose of early diagnosis and treatment.</p><p>
<b>The following points are noteworthy:</b>
</p><ul><li><div>Clear communication between individuals with hearing loss, families, and health care providers is key. Deaf and hard-of-hearing persons may use a variety of communication methods, including spoken language, sign language, lip reading, and written notes. For deaf individuals and families who use sign language, a certified sign language interpreter must be used. Communication aids such as visual aids and verbal cues when changing topics can be helpful.</div></li><li><div>It is important to ascertain and address the questions and concerns of the family/individual. Deaf and hard-of-hearing persons may be interested in obtaining information about the cause of their own deafness, including information on medical, educational, and social services. Others may seek information about the chance of deaf/hard-of-hearing children and information for family planning decisions.</div></li><li><div>The use of neutral or balanced terminology can enhance the provision of services; for example: use of the term "chance" instead of "risk"; "deaf" or "hearing" instead of "affected" or "unaffected"; and "deaf" or "hard-of-hearing" instead of "hearing impaired." Members of the Deaf community may view deafness as a distinguishing characteristic and not as a handicap, impairment, or medical condition requiring a "treatment" or "cure," or to be "prevented." Terms such as "handicap" should be avoided.</div></li></ul><p>
<b>Family planning</b>
</p><ul><li><div>The optimal time for determination of genetic status and discussion of the availability of prenatal/preimplantation genetic testing is before pregnancy.</div></li><li><div>It is appropriate to offer genetic counseling (including discussion of the probability of hearing loss in offspring and reproductive options) to young adults with hearing loss.</div></li><li><div>Because <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL is associated with extensive allelic heterogeneity, reliable carrier testing for the reproductive partners of individuals with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL (and individuals who are carriers of <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL) requires <i>GJB2</i> sequence analysis. Note: Screening for the large deletions shown in <a>Figure 2</a> is not universally recommended because of the very low frequency of these deletions.</div></li></ul><div><a><img/></a><div><h4><a>Figure 2. </a></h4><p>Schematic of large (&gt;10 kilobase) pathogenic deletions associated with <i>GJB2</i>-related autosomal recessive nonsyndromic hearing loss Modified from Abe et al [2018]</p></div></div></div><div><h3>Prenatal Testing and Preimplantation Genetic Testing</h3><p>Once the <i>GJB2</i> pathogenic variants have been identified in a family member with <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL, prenatal and preimplantation genetic testing for <i>GJB2</i>-AR NSHL are possible.</p><p>Differences in perspective may exist among medical professionals and within families regarding the use of prenatal testing. While most centers would consider use of prenatal testing to be a personal decision, discussion of these issues may be helpful.</p></div></div><div><h2>Resources</h2><p>
