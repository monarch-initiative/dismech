1 Introduction
Cyanide poisoning is a serious public health and forensic concern because its toxicity is 
widespread and rapid-acting in both natural and industrial sources. It is therefore often 
associated with deliberate poisoning, industrial accidents, and chemical warfare, but the 
risk from certain plant-based foods is often underestimated and remains a significant 
concern for health practitioners as well as forensic investigators [ 1]. A variety of seeds 
and plants contain cyanogenic glycosides, which liberate hydrogen cyanide (HCN) dur -
ing the digestion process. These include apricot kernels, bitter almonds, cassava, cherry 
seeds, and apple seeds etc., which have been linked to cases of accidental poisoning [ 2].

Abstract
Cyanide poisoning is a significant challenge for forensic toxicology because it acts 
rapidly and can be difficult to identify due to its rapid metabolism and instability 
post-mortem. Characteristic signs such as pink discoloration of the skin (livor mortis) 
and cherry-red blood may be observed, aiding diagnosis. Apart from industrial 
accidents and suicide cases, cyanide poisoning from edible seeds remains an 
overlooked risk. Foods such as apricot kernels, bitter almonds, and cassava and 
cherry seeds contain cyanogenic glycosides that generate hydrogen cyanide (HCN) 
during digestion, causing serious health risks. Amygdalin, known for its traditional 
medicinal properties, is a major source of cyanide poisoning. This review highlights 
forensic aspects of cyanide poisoning, particularly the challenges of postmortem 
examination and identification. Forensic instruments such as gas chromatography-
mass spectrometry (GC–MS), electrochemical sensors and spectrophotometry that 
may be helpful in confirming poisoning are reviewed. Also, the role of biomarkers 
such as 2-aminothiazoline-4-carboxylic acid (ATCA) is highlighted, which can help in 
more accurate identification. To make forensic investigations effective, coordination 
between toxicologists, forensic pathologists and law enforcement agencies is 
essential. This review emphasizes the need for standardized forensic protocols 
and improved identification techniques to accurately detect and prevent cyanide 
poisoning from food sources.

spectrophotometry, as well as real case studies. It also examines public health concerns, 
including regulatory gaps in cyanogenic food safety, and proposes prevention strategies 
through education, safe food preparation, and policy recommendations.
2 Cyanide: chemistry and mechanisms of toxicity
2.1 Chemical forms of cyanide
One of the highly toxic chemical: Cyanide; occurs in a variety of forms, which include(s) 
hydrogen cyanide (HCN), cyanide salts [e.g. sodium cyanide (NaCN) and potassium 
cyanide (KCN)], and cyanogenic glycosides, which occur naturally in some plants [ 10, 
11]. Hydrogen cyanide (also represented as HCN) is a volatile, colourless gas at 26.8 °C,

having a bitter almond-like odour, although only about 20–40% of people can smell it 
due to genetic variation. It is highly soluble in water and can be rapidly absorbed via 
inhalation, ingestion, or dermal exposure, making it extremely dangerous [ 11–14]. Cya-
nide salt, that is potassium cyanide (KCN) and sodium cyanide (NaCN), are white crys -
talline solids that dissolve easily in water to release free cyanide ions (CN −), which can 
have immediate toxic effects [15]. Cyanogenic glycosides, on the other hand, are present 
in approximately 2,600 varieties of plants, seeds, and food items and can release hydro -
gen cyanide (HCN) through enzymatic hydrolysis. For example, amygdalin, found in 
apricot kernels, and linamarin, found in cassava, can cause cyanide toxicity after being 
broken down by β-glucosidase enzymes in the body (Table 1) [16–18].
The toxicological impact of cyanide is fundamentally governed by the lability of the 
metal-cyanide bond, necessitating a forensic classification beyond free ions and glyco -
sides. Cyanide forms complexes with various metals, categorized by their dissociation 
constants. Weak Acid-Dissociable (WAD) complexes (e.g., with Zn2+, Cu2+, Ni2+) release 
HCN under mild acidic conditions and are of significant toxicological concern due to 
their bioavailability. In contrast, Strong Acid-Dissociable (SAD) complexes, most nota -
bly ferrocyanides ([Fe(CN) 6]4−) and ferricyanides ([Fe(CN) 6]3−), possess remarkably 
stable coordination bonds. These complexes require rigorous conditions—strong acid, 
high temperature, and extended heating—to liberate HCN, rendering them virtually 
non-toxic under physiological conditions [ 11, 19]. This distinction is forensically criti -
cal; the intentional ingestion of a SAD complex, often mistaken for a potent cyanide salt, 
may not result in death, a fact that can drastically alter the legal classification of an inci -
dent from a completed suicide or homicide to an attempt [ 1, 20]. Consequently, ana -
lytical methods for total cyanide must employ conditions harsh enough to degrade these 
refractory SAD complexes to avoid false negatives in forensic investigations [20].
HCN gas and cyanide salts have a rapid toxic effect, whereas cyanogenic glycosides 
require time for metabolic breakdown, leading to delayed onset of toxicity [ 15]. The 
chances of cyanide poisoning depend on the amount of cyanide absorbed through inges-
tion, inhalation, or dermal absorption and how effective the body's detoxification path -
ways, such as rhodanese enzyme-mediated conversion to thiocyanate (SCN−). Therefore, 
proper food processing techniques and industrial safety measures are essential to mini -
mize the health risks from cyanide exposure [19].

2.2 Symptoms of toxicity
The clinical features of cyanide poisoning heavily rely on the duration of exposure 
and route of exposure. The severity of symptoms is affected by whether the cyanide is 
ingested, absorbed through inhalation, or absorbed dermal absorption [21, 22]. If cyano-
genic compounds are ingested orally, such as amygdalin-containing seeds, this can cause 
an acute reaction in the gastrointestinal system. Initial symptoms may include mucosal 
irritation, nausea, vomiting, dizziness, and confusion. In more severe cases, respiratory 
distress, convulsions, and loss of consciousness may occur [18, 19, 21–23].
Dermal exposure to cyanide can cause localized irritation, erythema (redness of the 
skin), and in severe cases, systemic absorption can develop toxic symptoms similar to 
those caused by oral ingestion [ 16–19, 21–24]. Neurological symptoms develop rap -
idly because cyanide inhibits cellular respiration by disrupting the mitochondrial elec -
tron transport system. This causes a critical shift from aerobic metabolism to anaerobic 
glycolysis, which leads to excessive accumulation of lactic acid and the development of 
metabolic acidosis [22, 25].
A distinctive diagnostic indicator of cyanide poisoning is a bitter almond smell in the 
breath or stomach contents. However, this symptom is rarely observed and is not pres -
ent in all cases [ 23, 25]. In addition, bright red venous blood is commonly observed in 
patients with cyanide poisoning as, cyanide blocks oxygen utilization by cells, causing 
tissues to be unable to consume oxygen even when sufficient oxygen is present in the 
blood [18, 21]. If medical intervention is not performed in time, cyanide poisoning can 
rapidly lead to neurological deterioration, coma, and death within minutes to hours [ 16, 
22].
2.3 Mechanism of toxicity
The human body has a limited natural ability to inactivate cyanide (CN −), which occurs 
primarily through enzymatic processes in the liver. This depends on the sulfur-trans -
ferase pathway, where cyanide reacts with sulfur, provided by three major enzymes: 
thiosulfate reductase, mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase, and cystathionase. The rho -
danese enzyme plays a key role in this process, converting cyanide (CN −) to thiocyanate 
(SCN−), which is relatively safer and is excreted from body by the kidneys through urine. 
This biological mechanism helps the body prevent the accumulation of cyanide acquired 
from food sources or environmental exposure. However, the body has a limited cyanide 
detoxification capacity, amounting to about 0.017 mg/kg per minute. If cyanide intake 
exceeds this limit, the body cannot effectively inactivate it, which can lead to increased 
cyanide levels in the blood and severe toxic effects [ 21, 22, 25, 26]. This biochemical 
reaction can be represented by the following equation:

and heart, are high oxygen consuming organs, are most affected by cyanide poisoning, 
which can lead to fatal conditions such as difficulty in breathing, unconsciousness and

ited amounts of cyanide, excessive intake or industrial exposure can cause serious neu -

3 Treatment protocols for cyanide poisoning
Treatment of cyanide poisoning requires prompt and effective action. If cyanide comes 
into contact with skin, remove contaminated clothes immediately and wash the area well 
with soap and water to prevent further absorption of the poison by your body. If cya -
nide has been orally consumed, gastric lavage is performed, in which large amounts of 
oxidizing agents are added to convert cyanide to relatively less toxic cyanate. Sodium 
thiosulfate (5%), potassium permanganate (0.1%), or hydrogen peroxide (3%) (diluted 
1:5) are used for gastric lavage [ 23]. Specific antidotes for cyanide poisoning include 
nitrites, hydroxocobalamin, and cobalt compounds. Nitrites, such as sodium nitrite and 
amyl nitrite, have long been used for the treatment of cyanide poisoning. They form 
methemoglobin (MetHb, Fe 3+), which reduces toxicity by converting cyanide to cyan -
methemoglobin. In this process, hemoglobin reacts with nitrite to further form methe -
moglobin (MetHb, Fe3+), which then binds with cyanide to develop cyanmethemoglobin 
[21, 27] (Fig. 1B):

In addition to antidotal therapy, supportive care plays a critical role in managing cyanide 
poisoning. This includes oxygen therapy to counteract hypoxia, intravenous fluids and 
vasopressors to stabilize cardiovascular function, and correction of metabolic acidosis 
using sodium bicarbonate. T ogether, these measures aim to restore physiological balance 
and improve patient outcomes [28–30].
Survivors of acute cyanide poisoning often face long-term complications that can 
significantly impact their quality of life. Neurological sequelae are common, including 
memory deficits, ataxia, and parkinsonian symptoms, which arise from mitochondrial 
damage in the brain caused by cyanide toxicity [ 1, 31, 32]. Cardiovascular complica -
tions, such as persistent hypotension and myocardial injury, are frequently observed in 
patients who suffered severe poisoning episodes. Additionally, psychiatric manifesta -
tions, including depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), are fre -
quent among survivors, particularly those who experienced cyanide-related attempted 
suicides or industrial accidents [33, 34].
4 Edible seeds as cyanide vectors
The cyanogenic composition in seeds shows outstanding diversity between species and is 
affected largely by the external environment. Among the rosaceae family, a distinct rank-
ing of amygdalin content is evident, with green plum seeds having the highest amount at 
17.5 mg/g, thereafter apricot (14.4 mg/g), black plum (10 mg/g), peach (6.8 mg/g), and 
red cherry (3.9 mg/g), with nectarine seeds having the lowest at a paltry 0.1 mg/g [ 18]. 
This is also seen in apple cultivars, in which "Golden Delicious" displays increased levels 
of 2.96 mg/g fresh weight (FW) over the rest of the varieties such as "Fuji" and "Cox". 
Environmental conditions control the biosynthesis of cyanogenic glycosides (CNG), 
with differences being noted across "Golden Delicious" apple seeds depending on region 
from 2.2 mg/g FW in Azerbaijan to 0.80 mg/g FW in Slovenia due to differences in tem -
perature, precipitation, and salinity of soil [ 35]. The difference between sweet and bitter 
types is especially noteworthy, with bitter almonds having 0.3–68.5 mg/g of amygdalin 
in contrast to sweet almonds' 0–11.6 mg/g [ 18], while bitter types in cassava have much 
higher amounts (75–1000 ppm) in contrast to sweet types (less than 100 ppm).
Different processing techniques have been effective in lowering cyanogenic glycoside 
levels, with 72-h soaking of cassava roots reducing levels by 90%, and fermentation low -
ers the levels by 52–63%. Cooking techniques like steaming cassava, lower total cyanide 
levels by 74–80%, while garification lowers levels by 90–93%. Storage conditions also 
affect cyanide contents, with four weeks storage at 35 ± 2 °C lowers the cyanide in cas -
sava products by 50–64% by volatilization. Public education regarding proper processing

The significance of adequate preparation is also reinforced by regulatory authorities. 
The Joint Food and Agriculture Organization/Worls Health Organisation (FAO/WHO)

5 Case studies
5.1 Cyanide poisoning from "natural" remedies and food consumption
A reported case study [37] on apricot kernel-induced toxicity: Acute cyanide toxicity due 
to ingestion of apricot kernels is a serious but often overlooked public health problem. 
This case study describes a case from the United States (Arizona), reported in the late 
1990s, which was the first case of cyanide toxicity from apricot kernel ingestion reported 
in the US since 1979. A 41-year-old woman ate approximately 30 apricot kernels (15 g) 
bought from a health food store. Within 20  min of ingestion, she experienced gener -
alized weakness, paresthesia (numbness), dyspnea (shortness of breath), and dysphagia 
(difficulty swallowing), following which Emergency Medical Services (EMS) was called. 
During pre-hospital management, she was given oxygen therapy, intravenous (IV) flu -
ids, epinephrine, methylprednisolone, dextrose, naloxone, and diphenhydramine, but 
her condition did not improve. On arrival at the hospital, his heart rate was recorded as 
118 bpm, respiratory rate 36 breaths/min, hypothermia (33.6 °C), and metabolic acidosis

5.2 Cyanide toxicity from improper cassava processing
As reported by the United States Centers for Disease Control (US CDC) [ 38], In Sep -
tember 2017, a severe outbreak of cyanide poisoning was reported in Uganda’s Kasese 
district, induced by the consumption of cassava flour contaminated with highly cyano -
genic compounds. The outbreak was printed in the US CDC Morbidity and Mortality 
Weekly Report (MMWR) in 2019, which mentioned 98 cases and 2 deaths. This incident 
highlights that if proper detoxification of cassava is not done, it can cause a serious pub -
lic health crisis. Cassava (Manihot esculenta) is a principal food source in Africa and is 
widely cultivated. However, it is rich in cyanogenic glycosides, which can be transformed 
into hydrogen cyanide (HCN) during the digestion process. Generally, it can be safely 
eaten after being peeled, soaked, dried, and cooked, but if this process is not done prop -
erly, the cyanide it contains can cause poisoning. Furthermore, environmental stress 
such as drought can lead to increased accumulation of cyanogenic glycosides like lina -
marin in cassava roots. During droughts, processing shortcuts are also more likely, fur -
ther increasing the risk of cyanide poisoning from finished products like flour. According 
to the World Health Organization (WHO), cassava flour ready for consumption should 
not contain more than 10 ppm (parts per million) of cyanide (expressed as total cyano -
genic potential), but flour from "wild" varieties can contain levels as high as 2,000 ppm, 
which is highly toxic [ 38, 39]. The flour used in this incident was contaminated with 
88 ppm cyanide, 8 times the safe limit. The outbreak began during a funeral ceremony 
on 5 September 2017, when attendees consumed food made from contaminated cassava 
flour. Within hours, attendees began experiencing symptoms such as vomiting, diarrhea, 
dizziness, and shortness of breath. Many others developed similar symptoms over the 
next 3 days. Between 5 and 8 September, more than 33 people were admitted to Bwera 
Hospital, following which the Ugandan Ministry of Health launched an investigation 
into the matter. Meanwhile, the affected flour was confiscated from the market, and the 
local community was educated on safe methods of cassava processing [38].
According to the epidemiological study, the outbreak impacted individuals across all 
age groups and genders, but the infection rate was highest in the 5–18 years age group 
(15.2 per 10,000 persons). Both females (12.8 per 10,000) and males (10.5 per 10,000) 
were affected by the poisoning, while geographically Ihandiro (14.0 per 10,000 persons) 
was the most affected area. The poisoning caused most patients to develop symptoms

such as vomiting (95%), diarrhea (87%), fatigue (60%), dizziness (48%), rapid breathing 
(27%), fainting (16%) and rapid heart rate (10%). Laboratory analysis revealed that the 
suspect cassava flour came from Tanzania and was distributed locally through a whole -

flour from a wild (bitter) variety of cassava, which has high inherent cyanogenic content, 
was used and likely underwent inadequate processing prior to sale and consumption 
[38]. Since hydroxocobalamin, the recommended treatment for cyanide poisoning, was 
not used in this incident, treatment was limited to intravenous antibiotics and oral rehy -
dration salts (ORS). This incident highlights that foods like cassava, which are a principal 
source of food security in many African countries, can pose serious health risks if not 
processed properly. Chronic cyanide exposure can lead to long-term neurological dis -
orders such as Konzo, which causes permanent paralysis. This incident leads to several 
important public health implications, such as launching a massive awareness campaign 
on safe processing of cassava, adopting Rapid Colorimetric Tests for food quality testing, 
and enforcing strict controls on the sale of products with high cyanogenic levels. Finally, 
this cyanide poisoning outbreak in Kasese district demonstrates the need for vigilance 
regarding food safety. Local governments and public health organizations should strictly 
enforce food processing standards to prevent similar incidents in the future [38, 39].
5.3 Autopsy findings: classic indicators of cyanide poisoning
Cyanide poisoning is a highly fatal condition, with death occurring very rapidly and a 
wide range of post-mortem findings, although none of these are completely pathogno -
monic, making toxicological testing essential for accurate diagnosis [ 40]. A prominent 
but non-definitive sign is cherry-red livor mortis, caused by cyanide blocking the a-a 3 
position of mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase. This blockage prevents oxidative phos -
phorylation, preventing tissues from using oxygen, and causing an arterial-like redness, 
even in venous blood, due to the accumulation of oxygenated blood [ 41]. However, this 
symptom is not exclusive to cyanide poisoning, as similar changes can be seen in carbon 
monoxide poisoning due to the accumulation of carboxyhemoglobin. Another tradi -
tional but unreliable sign is the bitter almond-like odor caused by the presence of hydro-
gen cyanide (HCN). However, this sign is not considered reliable because it is genetically 
variable and studies show that 40% to 60% of people are unable to recognize this smell 
[40]. Additionally, modern forensic laboratories have high-flow ventilation systems, 
making it even more difficult to identify this smell during an autopsy [41].
Cyanide poisoning causes pulmonary congestion and edema, which is a result of 
severe metabolic acidosis resulting from excessive accumulation of lactate due to anaer -
obic metabolism. Effects on the neurological system may include cerebral hypoxia, and if 
the victim survives long enough before death, ischemic changes in the basal ganglia such 
as pseudolaminar necrosis may develop [40].
Furthermore, the forensic examination of gastric contents can provide critical evi -
dence in cases of suspected oral cyanide ingestion. While direct forensic case reports 
documenting the recovery of seed fragments from gastric contents in cyanide poisoning 
are lacking, forensic botany studies indicate that seeds and their fragments can some -
times survive gastric digestion due to their protective seed coat. Partially digested seeds 
have been successfully identified in stomach contents in other forensic contexts using 
stereomicroscopy, histological examination, and DNA-based methods [ 42, 43]. This

Singh et al. Discover Toxicology            (2025) 2:21 
suggests that, although not yet explicitly reported for cyanide seed ingestions, the recov -
ery and identification of seed fragments is biologically and forensically plausible and 
should be considered a valuable part of the autopsy process.
6 Laboratory detection: confirming cyanide exposure
The rapid identification of cyanide is of utmost importance to forensic investigators, as it 
is a highly toxic substance that rapidly converts to thiocyanate (SCN −) after entering the 
body, making it difficult to accurately analyze [44]. Its volatility and instability in biologi-
cal samples can lead to underestimation of toxicity levels if testing is delayed, making it 
difficult to draw accurate conclusions [ 45]. To overcome this challenge, gas chromatog -
raphy-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) is used, which separates hydrogen cyanide (HCN) 
through acidification and thermal desorption, followed by accurate measurement by 
mass spectrometric quantification [45, 46]. This technique provides more reliable results 
by minimising the interference from other compounds, which is why it is considered the 
gold standard [47]. However, for rapid detection at the field level, the picrate paper test is 
used, which is based on the colour change from yellow to reddish-brown when cyanide 
reacts with picric acid [ 44]. In the König reaction, cyanogen chloride (CNCl) is formed 
and reacted with pyridine and barbituric acid to form coloured compounds, which are 
measured spectrophotometrically [ 48]. Cyanide-specific electrodes also allow rapid 
measurement of cyanide presence by potentiometric methods in emergency situations, 
although the presence of sulphide compounds can affect this test. Modern spectropho -
tometric and electrochemical sensors also allow rapid and portable detection of cyanide, 
with naphthalene dialdehyde (NDA)-based methods being particularly effective [44].
However, forensic toxicologists face challenges such as cyanide volatility, post-mortem 
redistribution, and biotransformation. Since cyanide is converted to thiocyanate (SCN −) 
with the help of the enzyme rhodanese, stable biomarkers such as 2-aminothiazoline-
4-carboxylic acid (ATCA) are relied upon instead of its direct measurement [ 31, 45]. In 
addition, it is necessary to strengthen analytical procedures to take into account factors 
such as endogenous thiocyanate levels, sample storage and degradation [ 49]. Thus, a 
combination of GC–MS, spectrophotometric methods and biological markers becomes 
necessary for accurate detection of cyanide toxicity to obtain more reliable and scientifi -
cally proven results.
6.1 Additional autopsy findings and toxicology testing
A variety of autopsy findings have been observed in deaths involving cyanide poisoning. 
According to Akhgari et al. [ 33], a four-year study (2009–2014) of 52 cases in Tehran, 
Iran, found that pink lividity was observed in 21 cases, while only two cases had a bitter 
almond odour, concluding that odour-based identification is not completely reliable. In 
addition, hemorrhagic mucosa was also found in some cases, especially when the toxin 
was consumed orally. Various analytical techniques are used to diagnose cyanide poi -
soning. The prussian blue test serves as the initial screening tool, whereas the voltam -
metry technique is utilized for verification. All samples of liver and stomach content in 
this study were found positive for cyanide [33]. Since there are no definitive gross patho-
logical findings for cyanide poisoning, post-mortem toxicological analysis is mandatory. 
It is determined by methods such as spectrophotometric testing, gas chromatography-
mass spectroscopy (GC–MS), and headspace gas chromatography (HS-GC), which are 

Page 12 of 20
Singh et al. Discover Toxicology            (2025) 2:21 
capable of detecting cyanide at levels as low as 0.02 µg/mL [ 40]. However, cyanide is an 
unstable compound and can degrade rapidly after death, so it is necessary to immedi -
ately store samples in refrigeration or add sodium fluoride as a preservative.
Regarding the characteristics and causes of cases, suicide was found to be the lead -
ing cause in cases of poisoning from cyanide, where the victims intentionally consumed 
potassium cyanide (KCN) or sodium cyanide (NaCN). Of the 52 cases in Akhgari's 
(2015) study, 33 were suicides, most of the victims were aged 21–40, and 76.9% were 
men [33]. When external symptoms are vague or completely absent, a comprehensive 
forensic investigation is necessary to establish cyanide poisoning as the cause of death. 
This includes case history, circumstantial evidence, and laboratory analysis to ensure 
that death was indeed caused by cyanide poisoning [41].
7 Challenges faced by forensic experts and toxicologists in cyanide poisoning 
cases
Cyanide poisoning poses many challenges to forensic experts and toxicologists because 
cyanide is rapidly metabolized and has complex identification requirements that quickly 
convert cyanide into thiocyanate by detoxification via rhodanese enzyme; found in many 
tissues but particularly abundant in the liver & muscles and is excreted in the urine mak-
ing it difficult to detect in both living and deceased individuals [ 19]. Post-mortem chem-
ical changes further complicate detection, especially in delayed or decomposed cases, 
requiring robust preservation methods and advanced analytical techniques such as 
tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry 
(GC–MS) have improved accuracy, yet they require prompt sample collection, special -
ized equipment and trained personnel, that are not available especially in resource-lim -
ited settings [50–52].
Legal and investigative challenges further more complicate matter when cyanide 
symptoms like as respiratory distress and cardiac arrest overlap with other conditions 
like carbon monoxide in smoke inhalation and the absence of classic signs like cherry-
red skin or bitter almond odor that require extensive toxicological analysis and corrobo -
rative evidence for accurate diagnosis. In high profile cases such as deliberate poising 
also require forensic expertise to trace its source and prove intent. The forensic commu -
nity is now exploring advance technologies such biosensors and portable diagnostic kits 
to provide rapid and field base solutions for cyanide detection but that are not always 
available [44, 53].
8 Advancing forensic detection and toxicological research
8.1 Electrochemical aptasensors
Cyanide is absorbed from the environment through food and air, and breathing in fire 
smoke can further increase levels in the body [ 54]. Hydrogen cyanide (HCN) is volatile 
and reactive, which can lead to errors in sample collection and separation processes. To 
obtain accurate results, testing for stable biomarkers is considered more reliable than 
directly detecting cyanide. Biomarkers such as 2-aminothiazoline-4-carboxylic acid 
(ATCA), thiocyanate (SCN–), and cyanide-protein adducts are found in tissues and 
biological fluids, which can help confirm cyanide exposure [ 55, 56]. Cyanide detoxifica-
tion in the body via cysteine   produces ATCA, which accounts for approx. 20% of cya -
nide metabolism. ATCA is stable at cold temperatures for long periods of time (up to 

Page 13 of 20
Singh et al. Discover Toxicology            (2025) 2:21 
months), making it a reliable biomarker. Given its utility in forensic investigations, Li 
et al. studied methods for detecting ATCA from biological samples and found it to be 
useful in forensic investigations. Testing is accurate immediately after a recent cyanide 
exposure, but testing for its metabolites may be more effective if more time has passed 
[57, 58].
In this direction, the study by Khan et al. [ 59] developed an advanced aptasensor 
based on graphene oxide (GO)-modified screen-printed carbon electrodes (SPCEs). It 
used an aptamer called Apt46, which was selected through the GO-SELEX technique. 
This aptamer specifically recognizes ATCA with high binding affinity while ensuring low 
interference from other compounds with similar structure. The electrochemical proper -
ties of this sensor were tested using techniques such as differential pulse voltammetry 
(DPV), cyclic voltammetry (CV), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), 
which recorded a detection limit of 0.05  µg/mL. The sensor proved to be effective in 
biological samples such as urine, blood and serum with a high recovery rate of 92.29%–
114.22% and remained stable for up to 50 days.
The electrochemical aptasensor is an effective technique for cyanide detection, but it 
has some limitations. These include interference (cross-reactivity) from compounds with 
similar structure, declining performance of graphene oxide (GO)-modified electrodes 
over time, and limited real-life validation beyond laboratory tests. In addition, the sta -
bility of the sensor after 50 days and the need for sample preparation may affect its field 
use. To overcome these drawbacks, accuracy can be increased by better aptamer design, 
electrode durability can be improved by protective coating, and more testing should 
be done in forensic cases. Also, the feature of long-term storage and integration with 
smartphone-based portable devices may make it more practical for field-forensic appli -
cations [59]. This technique is inexpensive, portable and highly selective, increasing the 
accuracy of forensic investigations and postmortem toxicology. It reduces false positive 
and negative results and helps in quick and reliable identification of cyanide poisoning.
8.2 Mass spectrometry
In forensic toxicology, the mass spectrometric analysis of cyanide and its metabolites is 
most frequently performed on whole blood, which is the primary specimen for confirm -
ing exposure. Urine is also routinely analyzed for metabolites such as thiocyanate, while 
organ tissues (e.g., liver, kidney) may be examined in research contexts to study distribu-
tion and metabolism. Mass spectrometry is used in this chemical analysis where it helps 
in the identification of isotopes and elements present in a sample, measurement of the 
mass of molecules and study of their structure [45, 51]. In advancements for forensic cya-
nide analysis Osak et al. [51] developed a gas chromatography-tandem mass spectrome -
try (GC–MS/MS) technique for accurate identification of cyanide in forensic toxicology 
using negative chemical ionization (NCI) and triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. The 
aim of this study was to attain high levels of selectivity and sensitivity for cyanide by 
stabilizing it by pentafluorobenzyl bromide (PFB-Br) derivatization technique and ana -
lyzing it through multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transition. The method was vali -
dated as per FDA bioanalytical guidelines, recording a limit of detection (LOD) of 24 ng/
mL & limit of quantitation (LOQ) of 80 ng/mL, with the calibration curve (R 2 = 0.9997) 
being highly linear from 0.1  µg/mL to 10  µg/mL. The extraction method achieved a 
98% average recovery rate, ensuring the reproducibility of the technique, and cyanide

Singh et al. Discover Toxicology            (2025) 2:21 
concentrations of more than 21.54 µg/mL were detected in postmortem blood samples, 
confirming lethal poisoning. However, this technique has limitations such as high cost, 
complicated sample preparation, and long analysis process, making it less suitable for 
urgent clinical settings and precise storage is necessary to prevent the rapid decomposi -
tion of cyanide. To make this technique more effective, it is necessary to simplify the 
sample preparation process, develop low-cost alternative methods, and conduct exten -
sive testing in actual forensic cases. This study confirms that the GC–MS/MS technique 
is a superior method for high-precision and sensitive detection of cyanide poisoning in 
forensic toxicology [51].
The critical challenge in forensic toxicology is the accurate quantification of total 
cyanide, which includes cyanide liberated from stable SAD complexes. Headspace Gas 
Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (HS–GC–MS) is exceptionally suited for this pur -
pose, as it can be coupled with a sample pretreatment designed to hydrolyze all cyano -
genic species into volatile HCN. A validated HS–GC–MS method utilizes strong acid 
(e.g., 1  M H 2SO4) and high headspace oven temperatures (e.g., 120  °C) to completely 
degrade even refractory complexes like hexacyanoferrate(II) ([Fe(CN) 6]4−), ensuring 
quantitative recovery of cyanide [ 20]. This approach is superior to methods that rely on 
vapor pressure equilibrium alone, which are insufficient for breaking robust metal-cya -
nide bonds and can lead to significant underestimation of total cyanide content in com -
plex matrices like blood. The selectivity of MS detection (e.g., using m/z 27 for HCN) 
minimizes interference from other volatiles, making HS–GC–MS a gold-standard tech -
nique for conclusive forensic confirmation of cyanide poisoning in post-mortem sam -
ples [20, 46].
Similarly, Tusiewicz et al. studied the post-mortem stability of cyanide in foren -
sic investigations, where cyanide levels in blood and urine were found to be 1900  ng/
mL and 500 ng/mL five years after the death of a 30-year-old man. The levels in blood 
samples stored six and seven years later were 1898.2 ng/mL and 1618.7 ng/mL, showing 
a gradual decline. The gas chromatography-triple quadrupole tandem mass spectrom -
etry (GC-QqQ-MS/MS) technique and chromatographic methods confirmed this trend, 
proving this technique to be more accurate and reliable for forensic cyanide analysis 
[52].
In contrast to these laboratory-intensive methodologies Moaddeli et al. developed 
Ion Mobility Spectrometry (IMS) technique for accurate and rapid detection of cya -
nide in blood after death in forensic toxicology. This method is more effective than 
conventional methods as it is fast, economical and portable. In this technique hydro -
gen cyanide (HCN) is transferred directly to the IMS injection port via headspace vapor 
transfer, eliminating the need for a complex derivatization process. The method was val-
idated according to EMA and FDA bioanalytical guidelines, recording a linear range of 
50–2000 µg/L (R 2 > 0.99), a minimum limit of detection (LOD) of 20.4 µg/L and a limit 
of quantitation (LOQ) of 68.1 µg/L. The technique was found to have a recovery rate of 
82%–94% and a CV < 15%, indicating high precision. IMS helped separate cyanide based 
on mass-to-charge (m/z) ratio using atmospheric pressure chemical ionization (APCI) 
in negative ion mode. The method proved to be faster, less expensive, portable, and had 
less matrix interference compared to GC–MS, spectrophotometry, and electrochemi -
cal techniques. However, the study also pointed out that it has some limitations such 
as possible interference from other ions, limited testing in real forensic cases, and low

sensitivity in urine samples. Moaddeli et al. suggested that integrating IMS with GC–MS 
or LC–MS could further enhance its accuracy, making it a promising technique for post-
death forensic cyanide investigations [60].
8.3 Fluorescent probes
Fluorescent probes have become an effective technique for sensitive and non-destructive 
detection of cyanide in biological fluids for forensic investigations. According to Khosh -
soroor et al., the uracil-based fluorescent probe UraCS-Cu 2+ emits strong fluorescence 
at 378 nm, which is quenched upon contact with cyanide, allowing accurate detection of 
cyanide [61]. Similarly, phenoxazine and BODIPY-based probes show ratiometric fluo -
rescence changes, which are useful for cell imaging and forensic samples [ 62–67]. These 
probes detect cyanide via nucleophilic addition or coordination mechanisms, allowing 
reliable detection at nanomolar levels. However, some fluorescent sensors may suffer 
from solvatochromism and cross-reactivity with other anions such as fluoride or acetate. 
Recent research has focused on developing mitochondria-targeting and reusable fluo -
rescent probes to make forensic cyanide detection more effective and cost-effective [68].
Expanding on these advancements Zhou et al. developed a spiropyran-based fluores -
cent probe called TPTC, which is capable of selective and sensitive detection of cya -
nide (CN−). It is based on ESIPT and ICT mechanisms and incorporates structures such 
as triphenylamine, benzothiazole, and semicarbocyanine. It gave a “turn-on” fluores -
cence response at 530 nm in DMSO:PBS (3:7), pH 7.4 conditions and displayed a LOD 
of 146.00 nM. Its detection was verified by HRMS and 1H NMR. This probe was used 
to detect cyanide in water, bitter almonds, serum, and living cells, where the recovery 
rate ranged from 101 to 116%. In addition, pH-sensitivity was also observed in the range 
of pH 10–12. This probe has demonstrated its effectiveness in detecting cyanide within 
both environmental and biomedical fields with rapidity, high reliability, and specificity 
[69].
Further contributing to the field, Li et al. [ 70] developed a phenothiazine-based fluo -
rescent sensor capable of sensitive and selective detection of free cyanide (CN −). The 
sensor works via a nucleophilic addition reaction where CN − reacts with the carbonyl 
group, activating the intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) effect and fluorescence 
quenching. The research synthesized the sensor using phenothiazine fluorophore and 
benzofuran units, which were analyzed using techniques such as ultraviolet–visible 
(UV–Vis) spectroscopy, fluorescence spectroscopy, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) 
spectroscopy, and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS). The sensor was tested to 
detect the presence of cyanide in water, blood, urine, and biological samples. The results 
showed that the sensor exhibits high selectivity with a lowest detection limit (LOD) of 
2.0 × 10−6 M and is able to effectively detect CN − while minimizing the interference of 
other ions. The sensor successfully detected cyanide in live-cell and zebrafish models, 
proving its biocompatibility. However, it has some limitations such as potential fluores -
cence overlap and more testing is needed in real-world complex samples. For the future, 
Li et al. recommend optimizing the sensor structure, increasing the response efficiency, 
and extending it to environmental monitoring and forensic toxicology [70].

8.4 Portable and on-site detection device
Musile et al. conducted a comprehensive review on the use of paper-based microfluidic 
analytical devices (µPADs) in clinical and forensic toxicology, finding these devices to 
be a rapid, cost-effective, and effective option for on-site toxic detection. In this study, 
µPADs were designed and tested for the detection of toxic substances such as arsenic, 
cyanide, benzodiazepines, fentanyl, and synthetic cannabinoids, where blood, urine, 
seized materials, and forensic samples were used for analysis. The research found that 
µPADs offer high sensitivity, cost effectiveness, and portability, making them useful for 
emergency toxicology investigations and rapid tests at crime scenes. However, they have 
certain limitations such as reproducibility, selectivity, and potential interference with 
real samples, which may make their accuracy slightly lower than traditional spectromet -
ric techniques. To overcome these challenges, Musile et al. have recommended adding 
multisensing capabilities, microfluidic paper-based separation, and wearable sensors to 
µPADs to improve their accuracy and practicality. In the future, these advanced µPADs 
could prove to be highly effective tools for forensic toxicology, diagnostic testing, and 
environmental monitoring [71].
Similarly, Norouzi et al. developed oxazolidine-based polymer nanoparticles (OX-
PNPs) as dual-mode fluorometric and colorimetric sensors capable of detecting cyanide 
ion (CN–) in biological and environmental samples. These nanoparticles were synthe -
sized via emulsion copolymerization of methyl methacrylate (MMA) and acrylamide 
(AAm) with the addition of oxazolidine derivatives to improve their optical properties. 
These sensors were tested in deionized water, human blood plasma, and paper-based 
test strips, where their color changed instantly from orange to pink in the presence of 
cyanide. Their detection limit ranged from 3.67 × 10−9 to 1.66 × 10 −8 M, which was 
found to be more sensitive than the safe limit of cyanide set by WHO. Moreover, these 
nanoparticles were able to show pH response in the range of 1–14, making them use -
ful in pH sensitivity and intelligent fluorescent artwork. However, some limitations were 
also found, such as photo-degradation of oxazolidine dye when exposed to UV rays and 
potential interference from other ions in complex biological samples. To overcome these 
challenges, Noroozi et al. recommended enhancing stability through polymer coat -
ings, improving selectivity by molecular imprinting technique and developing wearable 
paper-based sensors, which can provide highly effective solutions for forensic toxicology 
and environmental monitoring. This study indicates that OX-PNPs can serve as a quick 
and sensitive instrument for cyanide poisoning, forensic investigation and industrial 
safety monitoring [72].
Furthermore, along with polymer-based sensors, paper-based test kits have also 
emerged as a promising solution for cyanide detection. In this regard, Malahom et al. 
[73] developed a paper-based test kit based on Ag 3PO4/Ag nanocomposite for sensi -
tive and selective determination of cyanide in food samples. This nanocomposite was 
prepared through a one-step chemical bath method, which can effectively oxidize ABTS 
under acidic conditions, and does not require H 2O2. In this method, cyanide was con -
verted to hydrogen-cyanide (HCN) with the help of sulfuric acid, which then reacts 
with Ag+ ions to form silver cyanide complex. This reduces the amount of Ag + ion avail-
able for oxidation of ABTS, leading to a color change and the presence of cyanide can 
be measured. The test kit provides a detection range of 2.50–20.0 mg L −1, with a LOD 
(Limit of Detection) of 0.48 mg L −1 and a LOQ (Limit of Quantification) of 1.59 mg L −1.

The system demonstrated high selectivity towards cyanide and excellent tolerance 
towards interferents of other metal ions and anions. When validated with conventional 
precipitation titration, it provided accurate analysis of cyanide in actual samples such 
as fruit juices and food products. This paper-based test kit can be extremely useful in 
forensic science for post-mortem analysis, investigation of suspicious food or beverages, 
and toxicity testing at crime scenes [73].
8.5 Exploring alternative cyanide antidotes
Suzuki et al. and Mao et al. studied advanced techniques for the therapeutics of cya -
nide poisoning, where both adopted novel approaches to overcome the limitations of 
existing treatments. Currently available cyanide antidotes, namely hydroxocobalamin 
(Cyanokit®), sodium thiosulfate, and sodium nitrite, are not fully effective due to several 
drawbacks. The high binding capacity of hydroxocobalamin to serum proteins (~ 95%) 
reduces its actual bioavailability, and its clearance rate is slow, delaying the detoxification 
process [74]. Sodium nitrite, on the other hand, acts by forming methemoglobin, which 
decreases the blood’s oxygen carrying capacity and increases the risk of hypoxia. More -
over, these conventional treatments are not effective in cases of combined poisoning of 
carbon-monoxide (CO) and hydrogen cyanide (HCN) [75].
Suzuki et al. developed liposome-encapsulated methemoglobin (metHb@Lipo) to 
overcome these limitations, which mimics the nitrite-based detoxification system but 
without affecting the oxygen carrying capacity in the blood. Their research found that 
metHb@Lipo rapidly binds to cyanide, thereby preserving mitochondrial function and 
significantly reducing mortality from cyanide poisoning. Moreover, this nano-liposomal 
structure displayed better pharmacokinetic properties than conventional methemoglo -
bin treatments, making the detoxification process more effective and safer [ 74]. On the 
other hand, Mao et al. focused on synthetic porphyrin-based detoxification, particularly 
to tackle the combined toxicity of CO and HCN present in fire smoke. They developed 
a dual-function antidote called hemoCD-Twins, which consists of two different heme-
mimetic structures—one to remove CO and the other to neutralize cyanide toxicity. 
The most important feature of this antidote is its rapid detoxification activity and quick 
renal clearance, making it an effective solution for emergency situations such as fires and 
chemical accidents [75]. While Suzuki et al.'s approach focused on biomimetic liposomal 
structures, Mao et al. developed a more efficient and multifunctional detoxification sys -
tem using synthetic porphyrin derivatives. Both of these study findings make it clear that 
by combining biomimetic and synthetic sciences, more effective and safer antidotes can 
be developed, which could revolutionize emergency medicine in the future.
9 Conclusion & future directions
Cyanide poisoning is an important public health and forensic science topic. Cyano -
genic glycosides present in apricot kernels, bitter almonds, cassava, and cherry seeds 
can pose serious health risks despite their potential health benefits. Forensic investiga -
tion remains challenging because of the quick metabolism and postmortem instability of 
cyanide. This review highlights cyanide toxicity, detection methods, and forensic chal -
lenges. GC–MS, spectrophotometric assays, and electrochemical sensors have improved 
forensic diagnostics, but rapid and field-applicable testing methods are still lacking. In 
the future, the development of electrochemical aptasensors, µPADs, and AI-powered

toxicology models should be focused on. AI and machine learning (ML) can help in the 
fast and accurate identification of cyanide poisoning cases by analysing big data. AI-
Driven biosensors can further improve on-site toxicology screening. In addition, there 
is a need to increase consumer awareness, safe food processing techniques, and imple -
ment strict regulatory policies. Collaboration between forensic scientists, toxicologists, 
and public health officials can improve detection, prevention, and treatment strategies 
for cyanide poisoning to reduce the health impacts of this toxicological hazard.
