# Exome sequencing in severe non-syndromic specific learning and language disorders in a French cohort.
**Authors:** Viora-Dupont E, Delanne J, Garde A, Nambot S, Colin E, Bournez M, Fauconnier-Fatus C, Racine C, Simao De Souza C, Bernard C, Maurer A, Espitalier A, Binquet C, Bouctot M, Humbert ML, Briffaut AS, Darmency V, Plumet P, Cotinaud-Ricou A, Relin N, Callier P, Mosca-Boidron AL, Marle N, Tran Mau-Them F, Denommé-Pichon AS, Safraou H, Vitobello A, Philippe C, Duffourd Y, Bruel AL, Thauvin-Robinet C, Faivre L
**Journal:** Mol Autism (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1186/s13229-025-00688-8](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13229-025-00688-8)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Specific learning disorders (SLDs) affect approximately 5% of school-age children. In France, genetic investigations of complex non-syndromic SLD cases include chromosomal microarray analysis and fragile X syndrome testing. However, the examples of genes being described in intellectual disability or autism spectrum disorder and also reported in patients with complex and severe SLDs are multiplying. International efforts using exome sequencing have identified monogenic diseases that explain severe SLDs in some instances. The aim of our study was to investigate the value of exome sequencing in children with SLDs without intellectual disability and autism spectrum disorder. METHODS: We initiated a prospective study using exome sequencing in patients with well-documented, severe SLD. RESULTS: Analysis of 82 patients revealed pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in 11 (13.4%) patients (ADNP, BRAF, CREBBP, KCNN2, KIF1A, RERE, SCN8A, SET, SMARCC2 (x2), TRIO). In addition, 38 variants of uncertain significance in candidate genes for severe neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) or in genes of unknown significance that could contribute to the phenotype were identified in 30 patients. LIMITATIONS: The study of 82 patients does not provide sufficient statistical power to conclude that exome testing adds value over chromosomal microarray analysis, or to determine which patient profiles are more likely to benefit from genetic testing. Studies with larger patient numbers are needed to increase statistical power. CONCLUSIONS: This study confirms the involvement of NDD genes in milder phenotypes and suggests the potential of exome sequencing for diagnosing severe SLDs. Further research on larger samples is required to determine which SLDs are most likely to benefit from pangenomic explorations and to clarify the implication of candidate variants.

In the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) [1], specific learning disorders (SLDs) belong to the group of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), along with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), intellectual disability (ID), communication disorders, motor disorders, and attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). SLDs involve the impairment of one or several specific cognitive functions, unlike ID, which affects overall cognitive functioning. Therefore, an SLD diagnosis is made when there is no ID, sensory or neurological lesion, or psychological disorder present [1]. However, they can be associated with other NDDs (except ID), particularly ADHD. They affect 5% of school-age children, or one to two students per class [2]. These are, therefore, frequent pathologies with major repercussions for families and education policies. SLDs encompass several deficits, the list and names of which have varied over time. Currently, DSM-5 separates: (1) disorders linked to school learning (disorders with impairment in reading, disorders with impairment in written expression and disorders with impairment in mathematics), (2) developmental coordination disorder (DCD), which belongs to motor disorders, and (3) developmental language disorder (DLD), which belongs to communication disorders [1]. In France, these different disorders are known as ‘dys’ disorders (dyslexia, dysorthography, dyscalculia, dyspraxia, and dysphasia). The new French strategy for NDDs encourages the diagnosis and management of NDDs as a whole, whereas previously it focused on ASD, demonstrating a political will to better care for all patients. Research in this field is also encouraged [3].

As early as the 1970s, several researchers postulated the existence of genetic predispositions to SLD [4, 5]. Although the environmental component has been much emphasized, relative risk calculations have shown an increased risk of 40%-50% for siblings. Several twin studies have been conducted to evaluate the contribution of genetic and environmental components including the large Colorado and London studies on the origin of dyslexia [6]. Numerous linkage studies have been carried out to identify the genes that may contribute to the risk factors for SLD, particularly in children with dyslexia and DLD. A recent review identified 45 (candidate) genes for DLD, including 22 previously implicated in other NDDs [7]. Numerous examples from the literature demonstrate that the same gene may be responsible for ID of variable severity in some patients and only learning disabilities in others. This could be explained by variant heterogeneity, oligogenic inheritance, or genetic background, for example. The advent of exome sequencing (ES) in the 2010s marked a major paradigm shift in genetics. In 2015, the National Institute for Health Research stated that further research was required to determine the clinical and cost-effectiveness of using new generation sequencing technologies to investigate the cause of SLDs [8].

As a result, ES has been used to identify single-gene disorders in individuals with learning difficulties. After the identification of FOXP2 variants in childhood apraxia of speech (CAS) in 2001 [9], the first major publication on the subject was published in 2013 by Worthey and colleagues, who used an ES approach to search for candidate genes [10]. Since then, several groups have carried out studies integrating ES or genome sequencing in patients with DLD or CAS [11–18]. A recent review of the literature, based on three articles analyzing cohorts of patients with CAS, highlighted a diagnostic rate of around 25% for ES or GS and established a list of 32 genes of interest [19].

Thanks to the 2025 French Genomic Medicine Plan, France is accelerating the deployment of genome-wide sequencing in routine care, with an opportunity for new indications if justified, which could include SLDs if its clinical interest is demonstrated [20]. To participate in the international effort to identify the genetic basis of SLDs, our group has conducted a prospective study on the interest of ES in complex and severe non-syndromic SLDs in routine care.

Our study was conducted as a substudy within the DISCOVERY project, a French multicenter research project aimed at elucidating the molecular and/or pathophysiological mechanisms of developmental diseases (authorization n° ID-RCB 2016-A01347-44; NCT03287193). Biological samples were integrated into the INSERM UMR1231 GAD (Genetics of Developmental Anomalies) Biological Collection, approved by the local institutional review board under reference No. DC2011-1332.

Patients were identified through genetic consultations at Dijon University Hospital between February 2021 and July 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: a diagnosis of one or more severe documented SLDs - including dyslexia, dysorthography, dyscalculia, DCD and/or DLD - established by a physician specialized in NDD, within an expert center or professional network, based on assessments conducted by relevant specialists (speech therapists, occupational therapists, neuropsychologists, psychomotor therapists, psychologists) and being over three years of age. The caregivers or the patients themselves, if adults, signed the consent form for the study. The SLD was considered severe if it required significant and long-term care and/or impacted schooling, such as requiring human assistance or placement in a special needs class or school. In our study, SLDs are defined as complex if they are associated with each other or with behavioral disorders, particularly ADHD. Patients with a proven ID (defined by a homogeneous IQ of less than 70 and systematically investigated by psychometric assessment), a clear-cut ASD, or notable facial features or major malformations were excluded. All patients underwent IQ testing to exclude ID, but a clinical assessment to rule out ASD was not required. Mild epilepsy beginning in childhood or adolescence was not an exclusion criterion, given its high prevalence in the general population. Previous chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) was not an exclusion criterion, and CMA and fragile X syndrome testing were prescribed for all patients who had not undergone these analyses. Clinical data were entered into a clinical data management system (Cleanweb®, Telemedicine Technologies, Boulogne-Billancourt, France), which was overseen by CIC-EC1432 data managers. After the informed consent form was signed, blood samples were taken from the proband and, if available, the parents. Trio analysis was preferred, but the absence of a parent was not an exclusion criterion.

ES was performed according to previously published protocols using a SureSelect Human all exon V6 Agilent or TWIST kit from Integragen [21, 22]. The resulting libraries were sequenced on different platforms for paired-end reads with a target mean depth of 100X. Reads were aligned to the human genome reference sequence (GRCh37 or GRCh38 assembly) using BWA 0.6.2 (Burrows–Wheeler Aligner). Duplicate reads were marked using Picard 1.77, and BAM files were processed using GATK 3.5 (Genome Analysis Toolkit) to realign indels and recalibrate base quality scores. The XHMM software, which uses principal component analysis normalization and a hidden Markov model to identify copy number variation (CNV) from normalized read-depth information, was used to detect CNV, as previously described [23, 24].

Biologists analyzed the data from a table compiling different scores and information about genes or variants. Using MobiDetails [25], which centralizes several predictive scores, the biologists checked the impact of the variant on the protein. The laboratory uses the score ranges determined by the tools themselves. Allele frequencies were verified in gnomAD (allele frequency less than 0.1%) and dbSNP. Variant classification, according to the recommendation of American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) [26], and with the help of Intervar and the ClinVar database, was addressed during the clinical-biological meeting. Only pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants that explain the patient’s phenotype, and selected variants of uncertain significance (VUS) considered potentially relevant were included in the written report, while VUS deemed unlikely to be involved were not reported. Benign and likely benign variants (polymorphisms) were not reported. Variant segregation was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Family segregation studies, data sharing, functional assays and episignature analysis [27–29] were undertaken on a case-by-case basis to support the reclassification of class 3 variants. The results were given back to the family during a dedicated consultation, and additional analyses (imaging, metabolic analyses, etc.) proposed for subsequent reverse phenotyping in care settings if appropriate. Neuropsychological and/or speech therapy assessments were proposed to parents when a reported variant was inherited from a parent initially considered asymptomatic.

Logistic regression was carried out to investigate whether one SLD profile would be more likely than another to have a positive result (i.e. the identification of a class 4 or 5 variant). The following factors were considered: sex, family history, SLD type, presence of comorbidity, presence of ADHD, type of scholarship, and presence of morphological features. Additionally, we performed a targeted analysis of patients with no prior genetic testing to determine diagnostic rates and compare CMA and ES in this population, evaluating the potential superiority of ES over CMA.

A total of 82 patients (57 males and 25 females) were enrolled. A familial history of SLDs was reported in 68%. The mean age was 10 ± 5 years (range 4–36 years). The majority of patients (55/82) had more than one SLD: 46 had developmental language disorders (DLDs), 37 developmental coordination disorders (DCDs), 36 dyslexia/dysorthography, and 10 dyscalculia (dyscalculia was always associated with another SLD). The SLD diagnosis was associated with another neurological or psychiatric disorder in 67 patients: 48 had behavioral disorders (e.g., aggressiveness, agitation, oppositional defiant disorder, relationship difficulties or unspecified), 44 had ADHD, and 10 had epilepsy. Thirty-two patients were evaluated at a specialized center for specific language and developmental disorders, while the rest received multidisciplinary care in a private practice setting. Most of the patients were in a special needs class (73/82).

Previous genetic testing included CMA in 35 individuals, standard karyotype in three, targeted genetic testing in two, and fragile X syndrome testing in one. Sixty-six patients underwent trio analyses, 14 underwent solo analyses and two underwent duo analyses.

ES analysis identified a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant in 11 patients, resulting in a 13.4% diagnostic rate (Fig. 1). Class 4/5 variants were identified in genes associated with NDDs (ADNP, BRAF, CREBBP, KCNN2, KIF1A, RERE, SCN8A, SET, SMARCC2 (x2), and TRIO), known for their broad phenotypic spectrum. Four frameshift variants, one nonsense variant, and five missense variants were identified (Table 1). Seven variants were de novo, two were inherited from an affected mother, and DNA from the father was unavailable for one patient. Reverse phenotyping was completed for all patients at the time of the results consultation.

Patient 1 had a de novo missense variant in KIF1A and presented with several SLDs (DLD, DCD, and dyscalculia), ADHD, and anxiety. Retrophenotyping revealed difficulty walking, spasticity, hyperreflexia, extensor plantar responses, sphincter disorders and hyperlordosis. The brain MRI was normal at age 7, and no visual impairment was reported. These clinical features were consistent with the recently described mild form of NESCAV (Neurodegeneration and Spasticity with or without Cerebellar Atrophy or cortical Visual impairment) syndrome (MIM 614255).

Patient 2 had a nonsense variant in SMARCC2 inherited from the affected mother, as well as a 15q11.2 microdeletion. He was initially classified as a sporadic case, but further investigation of the mother following the genetic results revealed learning difficulties and epilepsy in childhood (Fig. 2A). The patient’s mild morphological features (anteverted nares and long eyelashes) and neurological manifestations (epilepsy and severe ADHD) were consistent with a mild form of Coffin–Siris syndrome (MIM 618362).

Patient 3 had a frameshift variant in the TRIO gene. The variant was absent from the mother, and no biological sample was available from the father. Both parents had learning disabilities and received speech therapy. In retrospect, the minor morphological features observed in the patient (long palpebral fissures, thin upper lip, pointed jaw and low set, slightly hemmed and posteriorly rotated ears, with normal head circumference) and abnormal neurological development (epilepsy, speech delay, and SLD) were compatible with TRIO-related neurodevelopmental disorder [30].

Patient 4 had a de novo missense variant in the ADNP gene, which is associated with Helsmoortel-van der Aa syndrome (MIM 615873). Although this variant had bioinformatic scores favoring pathogenicity, it was initially classified as a VUS because Helsmoortel-van der Aa syndrome is a syndromic form of moderate to severe ID and no missense variants have been published. To understand the clinical relevance of the NM_015339.2:c.335G >A, p.Cys112Tyr variant in ADNP, an EpiSign panel v4 DNA methylation profile analysis was performed by the Amsterdam UMC [28]. The result supported a diagnosis of Helsmoortel-van der As syndrome (ADNP-T signature) [31]. The DNA methylation profile was not consistent with any other syndromes in the EpiSign panel v4. Reverse phenotyping identified morphological features (e.g., prominent forehead, smooth philtrum, down-slanting palpebral fissures, broad nasal bridge, thin upper lip, and finger abnormalities), a severe dysexecutive syndrome, and ADHD, which were compatible with a mild form of Helsmoortel-van der Aa syndrome.

Patient 5 had a missense variant in CREBBP that was initially classified as a VUS because only patients with ID have been described in Menke-Hennekam syndrome (MIM 618332). This variant was reclassified as likely pathogenic thanks to the identification of the same variant in a half-brother with ID (Fig. 2B), and following discussions with a group of experts as part of an international collaboration from the European Reference Network Ithaca [32].

Patient 6 had a de novo frameshift variant in the SET gene. His minor morphological features (asymmetry of the ears, ogival palate, inward walking, long and thin fingers, hyperlaxity of the elbows and toes, skin elasticity, and white hair), combined with a severe SLD affecting schooling, were retrospectively found to be compatible with the clinical presentation of intellectual developmental disorder, autosomal dominant 58 (MIM 618106) (Fig. 3A).

Patient 7 had a de novo missense variant in the KCNN2 gene, which is associated with neurodevelopmental disorder with or without variable movement or behavioral abnormalities (MIM 619725). A likely pathogenic missense variant affecting the same residue was reported in a patient with similar characteristics (walking delay, language delay, abnormal movements, and anxiety) [33].

Patient 8 had a de novo frameshift variant in the SMARCC2 gene. Tactile hypersensitivity, hypersensoriality, feeding difficulties, failure to thrive, and subtle facial features (arched eyebrows, broad bridge of the nose, prognathism, and high palate), in addition to SLDs, were retrospectively compatible with a mild form of Coffin–Siris syndrome (MIM 618362) (Fig. 3B).

Patient 9 had a de novo missense variant in BRAF (MIM 164757). Retrospectively, her minor morphological features (low-set ears, macrocephaly, down-slanting palpebral fissures, short neck, short stature, sparse and curly hair, and nystagmus) were consistent with a mild form of RASopathy.

Patient 10 had a de novo frameshift variant in RERE, which was previously reported as pathogenic in the ClinVar database in an unrelated individual. His developmental history and behavioral disorders were consistent with neurodevelopmental disorder with or without anomalies of the brain, eye, or heart syndrome (MIM 616975), a disorder with known phenotypic variability [34], and complementary explorations have been planned.

Patient 11 had an SCN8A missense variant inherited from his affected father, who had left school early. Although this variant was initially classified as a VUS, SCN8A has recently been associated with NDDs without epilepsy and without ID [35]. The developmental history of the patient and loss-of-function prediction of the variant were consistent with this clinical presentation, prompting the reclassification of the variant as likely pathogenic (see Fig. 2C for the family tree). This patient also had a nonsense variant in a gene of unknown significance, YTHDF1, also inherited from his affected father.

Among the 47 patients who did not undergo CMA prior to ES, ES identified three recurrent CNV with incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity: a 16p11.2 microduplication, a 16p11.2 microdeletion, and a 15q11.2 microdeletion (the latter was found in patient 2, who also had a variant in SMARCC2). These CNVs were also detected by CMA. Overall, 15% of these patients (7/47) received a positive diagnosis through ES, including class 4 or 5 variants or CNVs with incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity, compared to 8.5% (4/47) by CMA alone. Although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.5), ES increased the positive diagnostic yield in SLDs by 5%. Additionally, one CNV with incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity (15q11.1q11.2 deletion) was identified only by CMA, and six CNVs of unknown significance were reported across both methods (only two were reported by ES).

Thirty-eight candidate variants, classified as VUS by the laboratory, were identified in 30 patients: 14 in genes associated with ID, 12 in genes associated with NDDs, three in genes associated with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy, three in genes associated with other neurological or neurosensory diseases and six variants in genes of unknown significance (GUS), as defined by the ACMG [26].

Six patients had two or three VUS each. Most of the VUS were missense variants, one was a synonymous variant, four were nonsense variants, and seven were frameshift variants (Table 2). The main reasons for the classification as VUS were discordant predictive scores, and uncertainty regarding the familial segregation of the variant (Table 2; Fig. 2).

To identify which SLD profiles would be more likely to have a positive result (i.e., the identification of a class 4 or 5 variant), we carried out a logistic regression analysis for several variables (see Methods). No variables were significantly associated with a positive result, likely due to the small size of our cohort. Only the presence of DCD (odds ratio [OR] = 3.29, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.92–11.76, p = 0.066), the absence of dyslexia (OR = 3.06, 95% CI = 0.77–12.07, p = 0.11), or the presence of multiple SLDs (OR = 3.20, 95% CI = 0.07–1.56, p = 0.159) showed marginally positive results.

Our ES study of 82 patients with severe SLDs confirms the clinical variability related to certain genes but also broadens the phenotypic spectrum associated with other genes, initially linked to more severe neurodevelopmental phenotypes.

Eleven pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were identified in genes associated with NDDs. These genes are known to exhibit a broad phenotypic spectrum, with prior reports describing affected individuals without ID. For some genes, the proportion of patients without ID has been investigated. For instance, one out of nine individuals with SET pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants does not present with ID [36], and 15% of individuals with SMARCC2 pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants do not display ID [37]. Detailed neuropsychological studies are lacking in the literature and should be encouraged to better describe the neurodevelopmental spectrum of rare diseases. Larger studies should be conducted to determine whether a more moderate phenotype could be secondary to variants with a milder effect or a more favorable genetic background [38]. Intrafamilial phenotypic variability, as seen in family 5, is striking: one child presented with SLD, another with ID, and the transmitting parent was paucisymptomatic. The genetic background or modifier genes may influence the phenotypic expression of the same variant in different family members.

One of the secondary aims of our study was to determine if certain patient profiles were more likely to receive a genetic diagnosis through ES. Although not significant, DCD and several SLDs appear to be most associated with a genetic diagnosis. As expected, many patients had comorbid conditions (e.g., ADHD, epilepsy, or behavioral disorders), though these conditions may not be determining factors in a genetic diagnosis. It is likely that the small number of patients included in our study resulted in a lack of power. Larger studies are needed to identify the SLDs that are most likely candidates for pangenomic explorations.

ES revealed numerous VUS in NDD genes in our cohort. Several recurrent reasons for classifying variants as VUS were identified. First, the type of variant identified may have contributed to its classification as a VUS. In some disorders, only truncating variants have been reported so far, with few or no missense variants. This was the case for patients 13, 16, 20, 22, 23, and 30, who had missense variants in the genes ACTG1, AHDC1, AP2M1, HIVEP2 or SHANK2 [39–43]. Milder phenotypes are beginning to be associated with some of these genes. For example, missense variants in the AHDC1 gene have been associated with milder phenotypes compared to truncating variants [41]. Conversely, patient 29 had a frameshift variant in the GRIA4 gene whereas only missense variants had been previously reported in the literature [44], suggesting a distinct pathogenic mechanism. These examples suggest that the nature of the variant —missense versus truncating— may influence both protein function and clinical severity, contributing to the uncertainty in variant classification. Second, in some cases, the molecular arguments were insufficient to conclude that the variant was pathogenic. In some cases, the scores of in silico predictors of variant impact were discordant (patients 13, 17, 18, 21, 22, 25, 27, 28, 31, and 32; genes AHDC1, BRWD3, CACNA1C, CREBBP, DYNC1H1, HIVEP2, JMJD1C, KDM5C, MED12, MED13L, SIN3A, SPTBN1, and TLK2). In addition, some variants were not in a protein domain of interest (patients 13, 18, 20, 22, 24, 28, 30, and 41; genes ACTG1, AHDC1, HIVEP2, KDM4B, KDM5C, SHANK2, SIN3A, TLK2, and UBR5) or had been previously reported in gnomAD (v4.1) (patients 20, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 30, 32, and 36; genes AHDC1, CACNA1C, GABBR2, KDM4B, MED13L, RBM12, SHANK2, SPTBN1, STX1B, TLK2, and TRRAP). In other cases, variants had previously been reported as VUS (patients 13, 26, and 30; genes GABBR2, MED12, and STX1B). Third, the presence of several VUS in an individual complicates the reclassification because it is difficult to determine the impact of each variant on the phenotype (patient 13: HIVEP2 + MED12, 14: TRRAP + GRIN2B, 16: AP2M1 + METTL4, 22: TLK2 + AHDC1, 28: CACNA1C + SIN3A, 30: ACTG1 + STX1B + XPO7). A recent article emphasized the importance of considering multiple molecular diagnoses, as each variant may contribute to explaining part of the patient’s phenotype [45]. Fourth, familial segregations were sometimes discordant with an affected child, and the variant was inherited from an asymptomatic parent or was present in an asymptomatic relative (patients 12, 15, 18, 19, 25, 27, and 35; genes HCN1, KDM5C, MED13L, OTUD7A, SEMA6B, SPTBN1, and TAOK2). Specific clinical investigations of carrier parents are needed to identify paucisymptomatic parents, but we cannot exclude risk factors for SLD with incomplete penetrance. Fifth, in cases involving a variant in a gene known to cause syndromic disease, retrophenotyping of the patient was requested. In some cases, retrophenotyping did not reveal any signs consistent with the syndrome, resulting in the variant remaining classified as VUS (patient 17 with a variant in CREBBP). Lastly, four loss-of-function variants were identified in genes initially associated with other neurological or neurosensory diseases (patients 33, 34, and 36; genes ATXN1 (MIM 164400), RBM12 (MIM 617629), USP48 (MIM 620227)), or in genes recently associated with NDDs, albeit with a different mode of inheritance (patient 35, gene OTUD7A, autosomal recessive, MIM 620790) [46]. The identification of variants in GUS leads us to expect the description of new disorders with NDDs. For example, KDM2A and UBR5 have recently been reported in association with NDDs, but the evidence is not yet substantial enough for them to be considered in diagnosis [47, 48]. It is still difficult to classify variants in these genes due to the paucity of literature. Accurate classification requires pursuing functional studies, developing animal models, and combining these with clinical cohort studies to better understand the pathophysiology of disorders associated with these new genes.

Several approaches have been proposed to assist in reclassifying these variants: extending familial segregation of variants, episignature studies, and sharing data with other groups via GeneMatcher or other sharing initiatives. For example, the heterozygous missense variant located in exon 31 of CREBBP in patient 5 was initially classified as a VUS because it was not possible to determine paternal segregation, and the patient’s phenotype was milder than those reported in the literature. However, identifying the same variant in a half-brother with ID, along with the evolution of the literature [49] and an international collaboration, made it possible to reclassify this variant. Another example is the ADNP variant in patient 4. Initially classified as a VUS, it was later reclassified as likely pathogenic after a compatible episignature. Extensive research has demonstrated that this approach is effective for reclassifying variants in genes with a known episignature [27–29, 31].

Among the 47 patients who had not undergone genetic testing, 15% (7/47) received a positive diagnosis (class 4 or 5 variants or CNVs with incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity) from ES, while the positive rate of CMA was 8.5% (4/47). ES and CMA identified three CNVs with incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity, and one CNV identified by CMA was not mentioned in the ES report. ES therefore increases the positive diagnosis rate in SLDs by 5%. Some CNVs with incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity identified by CMA were not identified in the exome data; however, improved bioinformatic pipelines for CNV detection should make ES a powerful genome-wide test. When this study was designed at the end of 2020, few data were available on the diagnostic rates of ES and CMA in NDD. Comparing the two strategies was therefore one of our objectives. Our rates are higher than those highlighted in the study by Martinez-Granero et al. [50], in which the exome and CMA rates in the NDD population without ID or ASD are 7.1% and 1.4%, respectively. Further research in this field is essential to refine the diagnostic rates of genetic examinations in NDD without ID.

First, recruitment bias affected this study. Some patients had previously undergone negative CMA testing, while others had CMA performed concurrently with ES, creating two study subpopulations with potentially different profiles. To address this issue, we conducted a subgroup analysis of the 47 patients who underwent CMA and ES simultaneously. However, this reduced the statistical power of our analyses. To obtain more robust data on the diagnostic yield of these two approaches, we are currently conducting a second study comparing CMA and ES results in 101 patients that had not undergone genetic investigations.

We also attempted to define a typical patient profile for genetic testing but none of the variables were statistically significant. This is likely due to limited statistical power related to the small sample size. Therefore, larger-scale studies are required to better identify which SLD cases are most likely to benefit from pangenomic investigation.

In conclusion, our study showed that ES is a useful tool for the etiological assessment of patients with severe or complex non-syndromic language disorders. ES established a genetic diagnosis in 13.5% of cases and provided leads in 37%. The large number of candidate variations clearly illustrates the difficulty of interpreting variations in patients with NDDs without ID. Although not statistically significant, our preliminary results suggest that genetic diagnoses are most strongly associated with the presence of DCD or multiple SLDs.
