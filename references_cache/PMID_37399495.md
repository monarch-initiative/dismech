Mutations in theSLC12A2gene, which encodes the Na-K-2Cl cotransporter-1 (NKCC1), are linked to various conditions such as neurodevelopmental deficits, deafness, and fluid secretion in different epithelia. Cases of complete NKCC1 deficiency in young patients are straightforward, leading to clinical presentations that overlap with the phenotypes observed in NKCC1 knockout mouse models. However, cases involving deleterious variants in one allele are more difficult, as the clinical presentation is variable, and the cause-effect relationship is not always clear. For instance, we worked on a single patient’s case from multiple angles and published six related papers to convince ourselves of the cause-and-effect relationship between her NKCC1 mutation and her clinical presentations. The cluster of mutations in a small portion of the carboxyl terminus and its association with deafness point to a cause-and-effect relationship, even if the molecular mechanism is unknown. Overall, the preponderance of evidence suggests that theSLC12A2gene is a human disease-causing and likely haploinsufficient gene that requires further investigation.

Keywords:deafness, intestinal obstruction, knockout mouse, loss of function, NKCC1

A gene is classified as “haploinsufficient” when one functional copy is insufficient to maintain normal function. When the complete absence of one allele’s function consistently results in a specific phenotype, the case is clear. However, the situation is often unclear because the penetrance of pathogenic mutations in haploinsufficient genes is variable, and the phenotype is observed in only a fraction of cases. This perspective seeks to answer the question of whether theSLC12A6gene, which encodes the Na-K-2Cl cotransporter-1 (NKCC1), is haploinsufficient.

The Na-K-2Cl cotransporter, as functionally characterized by Geck in 1980, mediates the electroneutral movement of Na+, K+, and Cl−ions into cells (1). In 1994, the cloning of the Na-K-2Cl cotransporter revealed the existence of two distinct genes encoding NKCC:SLC12A1(located on Chr. 15, encoding NKCC2) andSLC12A2(located on Chr. 5, encoding NKCC1) (2–4). NKCC2 is almost exclusively expressed in the thick ascending limb of Henle and macula densa in the kidney, where it participates in the direct reabsorption of Na+, Cl−, and indirect reabsorption of divalent cations (5). NKCC1, on the other hand, is expressed in many cells and tissues, such as the brain, skeletal muscle, stomach, intestine, kidney, inner ear, and pancreas, where it fulfills critical functions (6).

In this perspective, we will limit our discussion to the function of NKCC1 in the nervous system, inner ear, lung, and intestinal epithelia. The development of NKCC1 knockout mice at the end of the 1990s confirmed the importance of NKCC1 in the physiology of these tissues: Mice lacking NKCC1 function suffer from sensorineural deafness and imbalance (7), intestinal obstruction (8,9), lung dysfunction (during the neonatal period) (10), and a pain-perception phenotype (11,12). However, neurobehavioral tests assessing cognition have not been conducted. Given NKCC1’s function in so many tissues/organs, we assumed that the NKCC1 knockout mouse would not be viable. However, the NKCC1 knockout mice were viable but presented with multiple debilitating phenotypes, which led the field to believe that the gene would be embryonically lethal in humans since no human withSLC12A2mutation had been reported until recently.

In 2015, the mother of a 12-yr-old girl who had been admitted to the Undiagnosed Disease Program (UDP) at the National Institutes of Health (NIH) contacted us. The girl’s exome had recently been sequenced, and her geneticists had identified a mutation in exon 22 ofSLC12A2(5:127514355,ATGTCTGGTGGC> A leading to p.V1226Ffs2, seeFig. 1) that was likely pathogenic. However, understanding the disease and its connection to NKCC1 turned out to be much more difficult than anticipated, and is still an ongoing investigation.

The patient, referred to as “patient 1,” is the youngest of four children and first exhibited symptoms at 6 mo of age, including obstructive apnea, vomiting, dehydration, decreased energy and fatigue, exercise intolerance, left ventricular dilated myopathy, and seizure-like symptoms. As a toddler, she was often lethargic, in ketosis, and doctors suspected she had a metabolic disorder. By the age of 4, she required BiPAP support to aid with nighttime respiration, and at 7, a G-tube was placed to improve her nutritional status and prevent metabolic crises. As her stomach function declined, she transitioned to a jejunum tube, and at age 11, she began total parenteral nutrition. She was the first patient onphase 1of the NIH Epi-743 clinical trial, as her fibroblasts showed signs of mitochondrial disorder. She also required additional support for her adrenal gland, parathyroid, and thyroid issues. Following these gastrointestinal and endocrine problems, she began developing issues with the smooth muscle cells of her pancreatic ducts and bladder. In 2015, she required an intervention to place a pancreatic stent due to a duct blockage, and she lost complete bladder function. She also suffered from autonomic issues and is on orthostatic hypotension and other blood pressure medications. In 2017, she underwent a total colectomy to address chronic infection issues, followed 6 mo later by a resection of her duodenum and small intestine. She has also experienced multiple bone fractures (including a recent hip fracture), osteomyelitis, dry eye, and dry mouth, which are indicative of a lack of fluid secretion. Recently, she began experiencing renal issues such as some degree of renal failure and metabolic acidosis.

We created the cDNA that reproduced the patient’s 11-bp deletion in exon 22 of NKCC1, resulting in a protein that is 186 residues shorter. Heterologous expression inXenopus laevisoocytes revealed a total absence of function of the mutant transporter (15). Interestingly, coinjection of cRNAs encoding mutant and wild-type transporters did not show evidence of dominant negative effects. Similarly, functional analysis of NKCC1 in the patient’s fibroblasts revealed a slightly reduced bumetanide-sensitive K+flux but again did not indicate any dominant-negative effects (15). However, we started doubting the involvement of the transporter in the etiology of the patient’s disease. A breakthrough came when we expressed enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP)-tagged wild-type or mutant transporters in MDCK cells. We demonstrated that the wild-type NKCC1 transporter trafficked properly to the basolateral membrane as expected, but surprisingly, the mutant transporter mis-trafficked to the apical membrane. More intriguing was the discovery that the mutant transporter could, likely through dimerization, send some wild-type transporters to the apical membrane (16). Thus, this mistargeting constitutes a significant dominant negative effect in epithelial cells. Further research revealed that the mis-trafficking was due to the loss of a di-leucine-like motif at the extreme end of the protein (17). Using CRISPR/Cas9, we created a mouse model carrying the patient’s deletion. Staining of NKCC1 in the salivary gland and intestine showed expression of the mutant transporter at the apical membrane. We demonstrated that the deficient transporter led to abnormal mucus secretion associated with decreased fluid secretion, a leakier intestinal epithelium, and the consequent invasion of commensal bacteria (18). More recent experiments performed in HT-29 intestinal cells in culture revealed increased expression of claudin-2 and increased epithelial monolayer permeability in cells deficient in NKCC1 (17). The observation that the absence of NKCC1 affected other proteins, i.e., claudin-2, prompted us to confirm that the effect was genuine and not the result of clonal selection. We verified it by demonstrating that wild-type cells exposed to bumetanide, an inhibitor of NKCC1, or exposed to reduced external chloride concentrations for 24 h also responded by increasing claudin-2 expression (19). This finding further confirmed that it was the function of NKCC1 and not the mere presence of the protein that was increasing claudin-2 expression. As the claudin-2 transcript was increased, we proposed the hypothesis that the intracellular Cl−concentration was somehow affecting the promoter of theCLDN2gene. Interestingly, NKCC1 inhibition was shown to affect the expression of other major junctional proteins in blood-labyrinth barrier endothelial cells (20). Thus, the absence of NKCC1 expression is likely to have secondary effects through other proteins that also contribute to pathogenicity. The increase in claudin-2 expression, associated with leakier intestinal epithelium is likely to contribute to the gastrointestinal issues of individuals lacking NKCC1 function.

Note that the epithelial defect may not account for all phenotypes. Epithelial cells in the intestine, bladder, salivary gland, and exocrine pancreas are surrounded by smooth muscle cells that facilitate the movement of the fluids they produce. In addition, these tissues are under the control of the autonomic nervous system, which has feedback mechanisms through sensorineural fibers. Both smooth muscle cells and sensory neurons express NKCC1, and a defective transporter in these cells may also contribute to the disorders (15).

Three years after our first report, the case of a 5-yr-old boy from Illinois with a complete absence of NKCC1 was published (21). The patient carried two copies of chromosome 5 from his father and none from his mother (chromosome disomy). Unfortunately, for this young individual, there was a large 11 kb deletion of a portion of theSLC12A2gene affecting exons 2–7 (Fig. 1), resulting in the absence of NKCC1 expression. The patient suffered from complete hearing loss, mucus accumulation in the lung and intestine, dry mouth, and intellectual disability.

This case of total absence of NKCC1 function was followed by the publication of the case of two sisters from Sweden carrying defectiveSLC12A2alleles (compound heterozygotes) inherited from the mother and the father (Fig. 1), with also a complete absence of NKCC1 production. The clinical presentations were very similar to the patient reported in the study by Macnamara et al. (21): intellectual disability, deafness, lung, and intestinal phenotypes (22). These cases with similar clinical presentations overlap with the phenotype of the NKCC1 knockout mouse, thereby confirming cause and effect: NKCC1 is clearly a disease-causing gene (14). They also proved us wrong about the viability of individuals lacking NKCC1 expression.

Two families from Saudi Arabia were later described with young children (2 each, 3 girls, and 1 boy) carryingSLC12A2alleles with a single base substitution in the acceptor site of exon 18 (c.2617-2A > G, seeFig. 1). They all suffer from hypotonia, microcephaly, developmental delay, and some respiratory issues, but had no gastrointestinal issues (23). One individual only presented with hearing loss. Whether the splicing mutation allows the expression of some transporter is unknown, but the absence of systematic deafness suggests that some transporters might make it to the plasma membrane of thestria vascularisepithelium to participate in the production of the K+-rich endolymph.

Although deafness can be easily demonstrated, no effort was made to assess cognition in the NKCC1 knockout mouse. Cognition is not easily defined in mice, and tests of attention, learning, memory, and object recognition given to mice only provide incomplete answers. NKCC1 is clearly expressed in central neurons, with the highest expression when the neurons are born, migrate, and settle, and decreasing expression during neuronal maturation (24,25). In the immature brain, the cotransporter is responsible for the presence of giant depolarizing potentials (26,27). There is a consensus in the field regarding the existence of a developmental period where Cl−transporters, such as NKCC1 and KCC2, play a critical role in the maturation of the inhibitory GABAergic system. Disruption of this process during this delicate developmental period likely contributes to the development of neurodevelopmental disorders such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and schizophrenia. The fact that bumetanide might ameliorate symptoms in patients with ASD supports this proposition (28–30).

Returning to the haploinsufficiency of theSLC12A2gene, if individuals with genetic variants in NKCC1 suffered from sensorineural deafness and presented additional symptoms such as gastrointestinal deficits, developmental delays, or autism, the case for haploinsufficiency would be strengthened. Recently, we identified a series of such individuals and reported their clinical presentations (31). The case of a 15-yr-old girl with a neurodevelopmental disorder, autism, motor stereotypy, and hypotonia, as well as bilateral sensorineural hearing impairment, caught our attention. She also had initial feeding difficulties associated with reflux. Exome sequencing identified a de novo, heterozygous nonsense variant inSLC12A2(g.127503511G > A, p.Trp892*, seeFig. 1). Interestingly, this mutation also truncates the carboxyl terminus and eliminates the di-leucine motif situated at the extreme of the C-terminus. What could explain the difference between her clinical presentation and that ofpatient 1? The difference might be that a longer truncation of the carboxyl-terminal tail could prevent the mutant transporter from interacting with the wild-type and, therefore, prevent a dominant-negative effect in epithelia. On the other hand, haploinsufficiency, in this case, might be responsible for the neurological features that escapedpatient 1. The low penetrance of pathogenic mutations in this haploinsufficient gene might explain whypatient 1, fortunately, escaped severe neurodevelopmental issues. Remarkably, the father of the second patient, the father and mother of the Sweden sisters have only one functional allele ofSLC12A2, but none of them present any clinical phenotype. This indicates thatSLC12A2is not only haploinsufficient, but the penetrance and the presence of functionally dominant mutants, i.e., truncated proteins, play an important role in the development of diseases.

The cases of a 44-yr-old father and his 5-yr-old son from France were also highly interesting. Both family members carried a p.Glu979Lys mutation in NKCC1 (Fig. 1) and suffered from deafness (31). The residue is located within a structurally unresolved segment of the carboxyl-terminal tail of the transporter (13,32), within which is a fragment encoded by the only NKCC1 exon that is alternatively spliced (33). Neighboring residues Glu980 [case in the United States, (31)], Asp981, and Pro988 [cases in Japan, (34)] when mutated also lead to deafness. What is so particular about this acidic stretch of residues located in a highly disorganized portion of the carboxyl terminus such that one deficient copy has such a devastating effect on the endolymph-producing epithelium of the inner ear? Further research will be needed to answer this question.

In summary, our laboratory has contributed to establishing the link between NKCC1 and human disease, particularly in complex cases where the cause-effect relationship was not fully established and only a preponderance of evidence was used to conclude a likely association. Compelling overlaps exist between the absence of NKCC1 function in mice and humans (Fig. 2). Patients with different genetic variants such as patients with NKCC1-DFX (patient 1) and with NKCC1 variants display common phenotypes or symptoms. However, additional work and evidence will be necessary to fully understand the physiopathology of NKCC1 in all these important organs. Our work suggests thatSLC12A2is haploinsufficient since heterozygote mutations in this gene cause a collection of phenotypes and clinical symptoms, but like in many other genes, theSLC12A2mutations result in reduced or incomplete penetrance.
