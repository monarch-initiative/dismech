OBJECTIVE: Bone cement leakage is a major complication of percutaneous 
vertebroplasty (PVP) while treating Kümmell's disease and it is a focus of close 
attention during the surgical procedure. The study aimed to investigate whether 
pre-injecting a composite of bone cement and gelatine sponge (the "bone 
cement-gelatine sponge composite") before injecting bone cement during PVP aids 
in lowering the leakage rate in stage I and II Kümmell's disease.
METHODS: This prospective analysis evaluated 74 patients with stage I and II 
Kümmell's disease who underwent PVP treatment at our hospital from December 2019 
to December 2021. The participants were divided randomly into groups based on 
whether the bone cement-gelatine sponge composite was used during the surgery. 
The two groups were the bone cement-gelatine sponge composite group (GS group, 
comprising 37 patients) and the no bone cement-gelatine sponge composite group 
(N-GS group, comprising 37 patients). The independent samples t-test and 
chi-square test were employed to compare general information, operative time, 
cement injection volume, intraoperative bleeding, and bone cement leakage 
between the two groups. Additionally, the visual analogue scale (VAS) score, 
Oswestry disability index (ODI), anterior vertebral height ratio (AVHR), and the 
kyphotic Cobb angle were compared between the two groups at the preoperative, 
2 days postoperative, and 6 months postoperative stages using repeated measures 
analysis of variance.
RESULTS: All patients were followed up for more than 6 months, with an average 
of (11.19 ± 2.21) months. No significant differences were observed in terms of 
the operative time, cement injection volume, and intraoperative bleeding between 
the two groups (P > 0.05). The incidence of bone cement leakage in the N-GS 
group (32.43%) was significantly higher than that in the GS group (5.41%), and 
the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05). The VAS score and ODI 
of the two groups at postoperative 2 days and 6 months improved significantly (P 
< 0.05). The AVHR and kyphotic Cobb angle were corrected to a certain extent (P 
< 0.05); however, no significant difference was observed between the two groups 
(P > 0.05).
CONCLUSION: The bone cement-gelatine sponge composite intravertebral prefilling 
technique can lower bone cement leakage in stage I and II Kümmell's disease and 
can also relieve pain and improve vertebral body height.

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs) are a widespread and clinically prevalent complication of osteoporosis, and approximately 50% of women worldwide aged 80–90 years have experienced OVCF.
1
 Conservative treatment in such patients might result in complications such as severe kyphosis, reduced pulmonary function, prolonged bed rest, and acute and chronic pain.
2
, 
3
 Delayed treatment could result in Kümmell's disease, which can have a detrimental effect on the patient's quality of life.
4
 However, open surgery carries a risk of considerable trauma, heavy bleeding, and prolonged operative time, making it a dangerous option for frail elderly patients.
5



Percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) or percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) are minimally invasive procedures in spine surgery that cause minimal surgical trauma, have a shorter operative time, and are associated with rapid pain relief and prompt postoperative recovery.
6
, 
7
 These techniques have been extensively used to treat vertebral compression fractures and vertebral bone destruction caused by osteoporosis, bone metastases, and aggressive haemangiomas.
8
, 
9
, 
10



Kümmell's disease, first described by German physician Kümmell in 1895, is also known as ischaemic necrosis after an osteoporotic vertebral fracture. It refers to patients who experience back pain and progressive kyphotic deformity after several weeks or months of an asymptomatic period following minor vertebral trauma. Conservative treatment for Kümmell's disease is ineffective and might result in neurological dysfunction and worsening of spinal deformity.
11
 Therefore, spinal surgeons currently prefer surgical treatment.
12
 In Kümmell's disease, clinical management is largely based on its clinical staging. For patients with stage III Kümmell's disease and accompanying spinal cord compression and severe vertebral collapse, the recommended surgical intervention is bone cement‐augmented short‐segment fixation (BCASSF).
13
 Several studies have demonstrated favorable outcomes with BCASSF in terms of alleviating back pain symptoms, restoring nerve function, and maintaining vertebral height.
14
, 
15
, 
16
 On the other hand, PVP and PKP are advantageous for patients with stage I and II Kümmell's disease who lack neurological symptoms or are elderly and frail owing to their minimal invasiveness, high safety, and prompt postoperative recovery.
17



The application of PVP in the management of Kümmell's disease has been associated with a high incidence of leakage, up to 79%.
18
 Therefore, in clinical practice, the most significant potential hazard of PVP for Kümmell's disease is bone cement leakage.
19
, 
20
 Surgeons have employed Vessel‐X capsular bag fillers, nanomaterials, and biofilm bone cement during surgery, which have demonstrated moderate efficacy in decreasing cement leakage.
21
 Nevertheless, these methods might be expensive and have limited applicability in most clinical settings, and do not eliminate the possibility of cement leakage. Hence, it is imperative to develop cost‐effective and efficient methods for preventing or decreasing cement leakage.

Studies have revealed that bone cement‐gelatine sponge debris can effectively reduce the incidence of bone cement leakage by sealing the rupture gap around the vertebral body and decreasing the fluidity of bone cement.
22
 However, some studies have also indicated that gelatine sponges do not have a significant impact on the leakage of bone cement.
23
 At present, literature is scarce in this area. Furthermore, the gelatine sponge cannot be observed within the vertebral body, rendering it impossible to track the fluoroscopy of the gelatine sponge injected during surgery, which significantly affects the intraoperative sealing effect. Therefore, we conducted a prospective study on 92 patients with stage I and II Kümmell's disease who underwent PVP at our institution from December 2019 to December 2021, with the following objectives: (i) to explore the feasibility of the bone cement‐gelatine sponge composite intravertebral prefilling technique in reducing cement leakage in stage I and II Kümmell's disease; (ii) to assess the impact of the bone cement‐gelatine sponge composite on clinical outcomes after vertebroplasty; and (iii) to explore the technical considerations for using the bone cement‐gelatine sponge composite during vertebroplasty.

This was a prospective, randomized controlled trial conducted at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University from December 2019 to December 2021. Ninety‐two patients with stage I and II Kümmell's disease were included in the study based on specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) bone mineral density (BMD) T‐value ≤ −2.5; (ii) age ≥65 years; (iii) patients with low back pain symptoms that did not improve after more than 3 months of conservative treatment, affecting daily life; and (iv) radiographic examination revealed a clear single‐segment vertebral body compression fracture and computed tomography (CT) revealed irregular vacuum fissure signs in the vertebral body. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) patients with coagulation dysfunction or bleeding tendency; (ii) simple thoracolumbar osteoporotic vertebral fractures; (iii) patients with severe heart, liver, lung, and nervous system diseases who could not tolerate surgery; (iv) long‐term use of hormone therapy; (v) malignant tumors of the spine and spinal infection; and (vii) patients with hypersensitive constitutions and mental disorders.

All the patients underwent PVP surgery, and we randomly assigned the 74 eligible patients into two groups based on whether they received an intraoperative application of bone cement‐gelatine sponge composite using a random number table. The two groups were the bone cement‐gelatine sponge composite group (GS group, comprising 37 patients) and the no bone cement‐gelatine sponge composite group (N‐GS group, comprising 37 patients). The group allocation was concealed by placing the subgroups' assignments in sealed, opaque envelopes, which were managed by a nurse who was not involved in the study. The patient remained blinded to the treatment assignment. Once the patient was brought into the operating room, the surgeon opened the envelope to prepare for the appropriate procedure. The surgeon was not involved in the collection and analysis of subsequent study data. Statistical analysis was performed by a statistician who was also blinded to the group allocation. All procedures were performed by the same senior physician.

The study was registered on chictr.org.cn (chictr2300070797) and approved by our institution's ethics committee (approval number: 2019111401). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided informed consent for their participation in the study and the publication of their data.

For the GS group, the patient was placed in the prone position on a U‐shaped cushion on the operating table, with the head and tail of the operating table appropriately raised to create a “V” shape and hyperextended the injured vertebra. Under fluoroscopic guidance using a G‐arm machine, the injured vertebra was repositioned and observed. After routine disinfection and draping of the surgical field, local anesthesia was administered. A bilateral pedicle approach was employed, and targeted puncture technology was used to access the fractures or intravertebral fissures. A 3.0 mm‐diameter puncture needle was used for the lower thoracic and lumbar vertebrae, with the anterior and middle 1/3 of the vertebral body being targeted. The working sleeve, with a diameter of 4.2 mm, was inserted after satisfactory positioning was confirmed using fluoroscopy. The bone drill was then inserted into the anterior edge of the vertebral body, with strict fluoroscopic observation to avoid damage to the bone cortex at the anterior edge of the vertebral body. The gelatine sponge was trimmed into 0.2 × 0.5 × 0.25 cm pieces prior to the procedure. The preparation process for the bone cement‐gelatine sponge composite involved taking a complete absorbent gelatine sponge measuring 6 × 2 × 0.5 cm, which was divided into two equal parts with a blade. The 6 cm side was then cut into 0.2 cm, and the 2 cm side was cut into 0.5 cm, resulting in several gelatine sponges with a size of 0.2 × 0.5 × 0.25 cm. Bone cement was then prepared, and the gelatine sponge was infiltrated with bone cement when the cement was in a thin stage, forming the bone cement‐gelatine sponge composite. The prepared bone cement‐gelatine sponge composite was placed into the working sleeve, and a pusher was used to push the composite into the fracture defect or intravertebral fissure. The filling position was monitored closely with fluoroscopy, and a pusher with bone cement was inserted and slowly pushed after confirming that the filling position was satisfactory. The diffusion and leakage of the bone cement were closely observed during the surgery. After the bone cement hardened, the cement column was rotated and broken, and the working sleeve was withdrawn. Sterile gauze was then used to stop bleeding, and the incision was wrapped with a dressing.

In the N‐GS group, in the absence of the bone cement‐gelatine sponge composite, the bone cement was directly injected using conventional PVP surgery. The remaining surgical procedures were identical to those performed in the GS group.

Demographic information, such as sex, age, BMD, body mass index, Kümmell's disease stage, and fracture segment, were recorded for both groups. The operative time, which was measured from the time of puncture after local anesthesia to the time of removal of all surgical instruments, the amount of bone cement injected, and the amount of intraoperative bleeding were compared between the two groups.

Low back pain was evaluated using a visual analogue scale (VAS) score ranging from zero (no pain) to 10 (severe pain). Lumbar spine dysfunction was assessed using the Oswestry disability index (ODI), which comprises 10 questions with a total score of 50 points. The full score is 45 points to accommodate those who do not use the indicator of sexual function. A higher score indicated more serious dysfunction.

Anterior vertebral height ratio (AVHR) was calculated by dividing the height of the anterior margin of the injured vertebral body (a) by the average of the anterior margin heights of the adjacent upper and lower vertebral bodies (b + c/2) (Fig. 1A). The Cobb angle was measured on lateral radiographs by drawing horizontal lines at the upper edge of the upper cone of the injured vertebra and the lower edge of the lower cone of the injured vertebra, creating perpendicular lines for each. The intersection angle of these two perpendicular lines is the Cobb angle (Fig. 1B).

Postoperative bone cement leakage was observed in both groups and was categorized based on anatomical sites, including intervertebral disc leakage, intraspinal leakage, anterior vertebral wall leakage, pedicle leakage, and paravertebral vein leakage.

All patients were followed up for more than 6 months, and routine re‐examination or telephonic follow‐up was conducted at the outpatient clinic every 3 months for these patients.

The sample size for this study was determined based on the incidence of bone cement leakage in the GS and N‐GS groups. A preliminary study conducted at our hospital (unpublished) showed that the incidence of bone cement leakage was 5% in the GS group and 30% in the N‐GS group. Based on this, a sample size of 33 patients per group was calculated with a significance level of α = 0.05 and a power of 0.8. Considering a dropout rate of 10%, the final sample size was increased to 37 patients per group.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The normality and homogeneity of variance of the continuous data were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. Measurement data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Independent samples t‐test was used to compare the data between the two groups, and repeated measures analysis of variance was used to compare the data at different time points within groups. Count data were compared using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact probability method and expressed as rates. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Herein, 92 patients were assessed for eligibility. Among them, four patients had pain for less than 3 months, seven patients had more than two fractures, and two patients had coagulation dysfunction because of which they were excluded from the study. In addition, two patients in the GS group and three patients in the N‐GS group withdrew their informed consent for personal reasons during the trial. Ultimately, 74 patients were included in the study (Fig. 2), with 37 patients each in the GS and N‐GS groups, respectively. During the 6 month follow‐up period, there were no follow‐up losses or deaths in any of the patients. Moreover, no significant differences were observed in the general information of patients when comparing the two groups (Table 1).

In this study, bone cement was combined with gelatine sponge to form a bone cement‐gelatine sponge composite, which was pre‐injected into the vertebral body before injecting the bone cement. According to our study findings, a significant reduction was observed in the bone cement leakage rate, without affecting the clinical efficacy of PVP.

Bone cement leakage is a major complication during PVP surgery.
24
 Once it enters the blood vessels or spinal canal, it can cause pulmonary embolism or spinal cord compression, which is life‐threatening and makes clinical management extremely challenging.
25
, 
26
 Kümmell's disease is a severe vertebral fracture characterized by the presence of a vacuum cleft within the vertebral body. This cleft is thought to be caused by vertebral body collapse due to osteonecrosis or avascular necrosis.
27
 Due to the presence of the vacuum cleft, the risk of bone cement leakage increases during PVP surgery in Kümmell's disease compared with ordinary OVCF.
28
 Cortical rupture is a crucial pathway for the bone cement to leak out of the vertebral body and into the surrounding tissues. When the integrity of the vertebral wall is compromised, bone cement leakage increases significantly.
29
 OVCF with intravertebral vacuum cleft is a special type of elderly osteoporotic vertebral fracture, with an incidence ranging from 21.3% to 60%.
19
 Chen et al.
30
 reported that the incidence of bone cement leakage in PVP treatment for Kümmell's disease was 34.4%, with the leakage mainly located in the anterior or lateral part of the vertebral body or within the intervertebral disc. These findings underscore the importance of taking steps to reduce the risk of bone cement leakage during PVP surgery.

In the past, balloon expansionplasty and bone‐filled mesh bag vertebroplasty have been used to reduce bone cement leakage during PVP, resulting in satisfactory outcomes.
21
, 
31
, 
32
 However, these procedures are relatively costly, making them challenging to promote. On the other hand, gelatine sponges are widely used in clinical settings and are easily and economically sourced. Gelatine sponges primarily comprise collagen, which provides bulk and mechanical strength and enhances absorption, making it a common component in surgical procedures for hemostasis and vascular embolisation.
33
, 
34
, 
35
 Bhatia et al.
22
 suggested that the pre‐injection of gelatine sponge before performing PVP is a cost‐effective approach to reduce bone cement leakage. Biomechanical experiments conducted by Meng et al.
36
 demonstrated that gelatine sponge could decrease the incidence of leakage by increasing the viscosity and decreasing the fluidity of bone cement. Gelatine sponge is not visible under x‐ray fluoroscopy, making it challenging to track its precise location after injection into the vertebral body. Thus, gelatine sponge is usually injected blindly into the vertebral body, affecting the technique's accuracy and, ultimately, the treatment outcome. However, bone cement is visible under x‐ray fluoroscopy. Therefore, in this study, a bone cement‐gelatine sponge composite was developed by mixing the visible bone cement with the invisible gelatine sponge. The composite was then injected into the vertebral body, using the fluoroscopic visualization feature of bone cement to effectively track the gelatine sponge's location. This approach solves the problem of gelatine sponge's lack of fluoroscopic tracking in the vertebral body while taking advantage of its benefits for hemostasis and filling. In this study, the incidence of bone cement leakage in the GS group (5.41%) was significantly lower than that in the N‐GS group (32.43%). The reasons behind this finding might be attributed to: (i) gelatine sponge's composition, primarily composed of collagen, which can serve as a mechanical barrier to block vertebral cortex rupture; (ii) the bone cement‐gelatine sponge composite's fluoroscopic tracking capability to accurately seal bone defects; and (iii) the increased viscosity of the bone cement after mixing with gelatine sponge, reducing its fluidity in the vertebral body and subsequently decreasing the incidence of bone cement leakage.

The findings of this study revealed a significant improvement in the VAS score and ODI at 2 days and 6 months postoperatively compared with the preoperative values (P < 0.05). However, the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant (P > 0.05), indicating that the use of gelatine sponges did not adversely affect the recovery of analgesia and dysfunction in PVP surgery. Both groups showed significant improvement in the AVHR and Cobb angle at 2 days and 6 months postoperatively compared with the preoperative values, with some correction of the posterior convexity deformity. However, the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant (P > 0.05), suggesting that the gelatine sponge did not affect the vertebral body correction achieved by PVP. Furthermore, the technique did not increase the operative time, the amount of bone cement, or intraoperative bleeding. Chen et al.'s study demonstrated similar results, indicating that the use of gelatine sponges had no significant effect on the amount of bone cement injected.
37



During the surgical procedure, the following considerations must be taken into account. First, the quantity of gelatine sponge used should be carefully controlled, as an excessive amount can impact bone cement distribution and injection. Second, when drilling the vertebral body with a fine drill, the positioning must be confirmed with fluoroscopy, and care should be taken not to penetrate the cortical bone at the anterior edge of the vertebral body. Third, a CT examination of the injured vertebra should be performed before surgery to determine the extent of damage to the cortical bone surrounding the vertebral body and the location of the bone defect. This information will enable targeted puncture to precisely fill the bone defect with composite. Fourth, the gelatine sponge strips should be of an appropriate size to prevent excessive squeezing of the bone cement when pushing the composite through the working cannula, which might affect its effectiveness. Fifth, during the composite injection, it is important to control the injection pressure and speed to avoid excessive pressure or a fast injection speed that might cause bone cement leakage. Lastly, if fluid flows out after the puncture needle enters the vertebral cleft; the cavity's fluid must be aspirated before filling the composite and reinforcing the bone cement.

Our study has several advantages. First, the bone cement‐gelatine sponge composite, formed by mixing bone cement and gelatine sponge, has demonstrated efficacy in reducing the incidence of bone cement leakage in stage I and II Kümmell's disease. Additionally, the composite can be tracked intraoperatively with fluoroscopy, and its use does not affect clinical outcomes. However, this study also has some limitations. First, since Kümmell's disease is a relatively rare condition, the sample size of this study was limited, and further research is necessary to collect additional cases for a larger sample study. Second, the leakage rate might change with the increased use of gelatine sponges, but this possibility has not yet been explored. Additionally, the impact of pressure during bone cement injection on leakage was not considered in this study. Lastly, the follow‐up period of this study was relatively short, and additional observations are necessary to evaluate the long‐term impact of the bone cement‐gelatine sponge composite on the refracture of the injured vertebra or adjacent vertebral fractures.

In conclusion, the use of the bone cement‐gelatine sponge composite intravertebral prefilling technique can reduce bone cement leakage in stage I and II Kümmell's disease while also alleviating pain and improving the height of the vertebral body. The gelatine sponge, with its ease of availability and cost‐effectiveness, has wide clinical applicability and promising prospects for further promotion in clinical practice.
