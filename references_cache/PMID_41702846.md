# Efficient induction of motor neuron disease in transgenic G93A SOD1 mice by prion-like seeding.
**Authors:** Xu G, Lopez A, Brkic S, Fromholt S, Chakrabarty P, Borchelt DR
**Journal:** Prion (2026)
**DOI:** [10.1080/19336896.2026.2630484](https://doi.org/10.1080/19336896.2026.2630484)

## Content

Mutations in superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) cause paralysis in familial amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and promote its misfolding into neurotoxic aggregates. Previous studies have shown that mice expressing the ALS-causing G85R variant of SOD1 develop paralysis much faster after intraspinal injection of spinal homogenates from paralysed G85R SOD1 mice. These findings, and other studies in cell models, established the prionoid templating properties of misfolded mutant SOD1. Previously, however, we noted that the widely used Gur1-G93A SOD1 mice, which express at high levels and develop paralysis by 6 months of age, were resistant to seeding by homogenates from paralysed G93A mice. A line of G93A mice that expresses at very low levels (VLE-G93A) was responsive to seeding but at low efficiency. The poor susceptibility of G93A-SOD1 mice to seeding was not what we expected if prion-like propagation is essential to SOD1 ALS pathogenesis. In our prior studies, seeding homogenates from paralysed G93A-SOD1 mice were injected into the spine of newborn mice, leading us to question whether older G93A SOD1 mice might be more susceptible to seeding. Here, we establish that adult VLE G93A SOD1 mice (up to 12 months of age) injected intrathecally with seeding homogenates containing misfolded G93A or G85R SOD1 developed accelerated motor neuron disease efficiently. Thus, we demonstrate that both the route and age of inoculation can influence the efficiency of SOD1 seeding to induce motor neuron disease in VLE G93A-SOD1 mice. These data, together with our earlier reports, suggest that prion-like templating contributes to disease progression in SOD1-ALS.

A familial form of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is caused by mutations in the gene encoding the antioxidant enzyme Cu/Zn SOD1 [1]. Since the initial description of SOD1-ALS, more than 170 mutations at 80 different amino acid positions in the enzyme have been associated with the disease (https://alsod.uk). Most disease-associated mutations are missense point mutations, which cause conformational changes that induce the protein to misfold and aggregate, forming pathological inclusions [2–5]. About a dozen different ALS-causing mutations have been expressed in transgenic mice, causing phenotypes consistent with motor neuron disease (reviewed in [6]). In most of these studies, SOD1 was expressed from a 12-kb fragment of human genomic DNA, causing paralysis at variable ages depending upon the level of transgene expression [6–8]. One of the most studied models expresses the G93A mutant human SOD1 at about 16-fold over endogenous SOD1 and develops paralysis at ~4 months of age [8,9]. Over-expression of wild-type SOD1 at very high levels can also induce paralysis in mice [10], though some of the phenotypes of these over-expression models are confounded by disturbances in copper metabolism due to Cu sequestration by the over-abundant SOD1 [11]. All of these models exhibit similar neuropathological abnormalities at paralysis, including loss of motor neurons, astrocytosis, microglial activation, and accumulation of detergent-insoluble SOD1 aggregates (reviewed in [12–16]).

One of the defining features of ALS, familial or sporadic, is that the disease seems to spread from a point of origin [17,18]. Patients often first notice weakness in a hand or foot that slowly progresses along neuroanatomically connected pathways until respiratory failure occurs. The typical course of disease from onset to death is 2–5 years. This spreading nature of ALS has prompted comparisons to prion disease [19]. The first studies to suggest SOD1 might acquire prion characteristics involved cultured cell models where evidence of cell-to-cell propagation of misfolded and toxic conformations was described [20–22]. Subsequently, our laboratory and others found that injecting spinal tissue preparations containing misfolded and aggregated mutant SOD1 from paralysed mice or human ALS spinal tissues into the spinal cords of young mice expressing the G85R variant of human SOD1 accelerates the onset of pathology, leading to paralysis [23–25]. Further, we have shown that injecting these preparations into the sciatic nerve of mice expressing G85R-SOD1 induces a pathology that slowly spreads rostrally in the spinal cord, reaching anatomically connected nuclei in the brain, before causing paralysis [26].

The model system that we have used most extensively to evaluate the prion properties of mutant SOD1 in vivo is a line of mice in which G85R-SOD1 is fused in-frame to yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) [27]. Naïve heterozygous G85R-SOD1:YFP mice do not begin developing paralysis until after 18 months of age [27], whereas heterozygous mice injected with spinal tissue homogenates from paralysed G85R-SOD1 mice can become paralysed within 2 months post-injection [23,28]. Importantly, injection of numerous control homogenates from the spinal cords of asymptomatic wild-type mice, asymptomatic young mice expressing ALS-causing mutant SOD1, or from older paralysed mice that are transgenic for human MAPT (tau) or SNCA (α-synuclein), failed to induce early paralysis when injected into G85R-SOD1:YFP mice [26,29,30]. Collectively, these data demonstrated that tissue homogenates from paralysed ALS-mutant SOD1 mice specifically induce the misfolding of naïve G85R-SOD1 in recipient host mice.

Multiple studies in our laboratory, and others, have confirmed that mice that express human G85R-SOD1 are responsive to prion-like seeding [23–25,28–30]. The most efficient seeding of G85R-SOD1 mice occurs when seeding homogenates are prepared from older paralysed G85R-SOD1 mice [23,28]. Homogenates prepared from paralysed mice that express other ALS-causing SOD1 variants, such as G37R, G93A or L126Z, can also induce early paralysis, but at lower efficiency and with more variable incubation periods [23]. In these initial studies, we also noted that some ALS-SOD1 transgenic models appeared to be resistant to seeding. In particular, we had reported that motor neuron disease was not accelerated when Gur 1 G93A mice were injected intraspinally as newborns with seeding homogenates from older paralysed mice [30]. The Gur1 G93A-SOD1 strain of mice expresses mutant SOD1 at very high levels (16×) and will develop paralysis in 4–6 months in the absence of exogenous seeding [8,9]. To determine whether the high level of expression overwhelmed any seeding effect, we tested seeding in two lines of G93A-SOD1 mice that express mutant protein at levels that produce paralysis only at very old ages (>18 months). One of these lines of mice was a sub-strain of Gur1 G93A-SOD1 mice that had spontaneously lost transgene copies, reducing from 25 copies to 4, that is designated as the very low expressing (VLE G93A-SOD1) line [31,32]. The VLE G93A-SOD1 mice do not develop paralysis until after 18 months of age. The other line was a model in which human G93A-SOD1 cDNA is expressed by a vector with a Thy-1 promoter (Thy-1 G93A), with heterozygous mice developing paralysis beyond 20 months of age [33]. Injection of seeding-homogenates from paralysed Gur 1 G93A-SOD1 mice into the spinal cord of newborn Thy-1 G93A mice failed to induce paralysis [23]. However, injection of newborn VLE G93A-SOD1 mice was more promising, as 3 of 8 mice injected as newborns and 3 of 11 mice injected into the sciatic nerve at 2 months of age, developed paralysis early [23]. Although this latter result suggested that G93A-SOD1 mice can be seeded, the efficiency of isologous seeding of naïve G93A SOD1 mice with homogenates from paralysed G93A SOD1 mice was lower than expected.

Questions that arose from this earlier work were whether the G93A models may show greater susceptibility to seeding with a different method of inoculation or at older ages. To address these questions, we established a method for intrathecal injection of seeding homogenates that was applied to the longer-lived VLE G93A-SOD1 mice, which offered the opportunity to determine how older mice respond to seeding. VLE G93A-SOD1 mice aged 2, 6 or 12 months were injected intrathecally with homogenates from paralysed G93A-SOD1 mice or paralysed G85R-SOD1 mice. Our results demonstrate that it is possible to induce accelerated paralysis in the VLE G93A-SOD1 mice efficiently at all ages, indicating that G93A-SOD1 can sustain the propagation of an ALS-causing conformation associated with misfolded SOD1.

In the present study, we sought to determine whether injecting SOD1 seeding homogenates in older adult mice could prove more efficacious in triggering paralysis than what we had observed previously in neonatal mice injected intraspinally. One route for spinal cord delivery in older mice is intrathecal injection. We therefore sought to first establish the efficacy of intrathecal injection routes in mice that express G85R-SOD1, using a recently developed line of mice that is homozygous for a modified human-genomic DNA expressing G85R-SOD1:YFP [34]. These mice, termed Line 230, are similar to the original G85R-SOD1:YFP mice used previously [23], but express the transgene at slightly higher levels. When breeding to homozygosity, in the absence of seeding, approximately one quarter of the animals develop hindlimb paralysis spontaneously between 8 and 16 months of age [34]. To provide a reference for the efficacy of this new route, we compared the efficiency of intrathecal injection in adult 2-month-old Line 230 mice to the intraspinal route used in neonatal mice as we have used extensively. As the experimental control for seeding, we prepared a pooled homogenate from the spinal cords of four asymptomatic healthy young 2-month-old Line 230 mice (Y230-pool). None of the neonatal Line 230 mice injected intraspinally (ISP) with Y230 pool seeds developed paralysis by 10 months of age, when they were euthanized for neuropathological analysis (Figure 1A, Cohort 1).

To directly compare the efficacy of the neonatal ISP injections to adult intrathecal injections performed in adult 2-month-old mice, we used a seeding-homogenate that was a pool of four spinal cords from G85R-SOD1:YFP animals that became paralysed at 2.5–3.1 months after being seeded by a homogenate from a 10-month-old, unseeded, paralysed Line 230 mouse (inoculum 230-11; see Methods section). Consistent with previous studies [34], ISP injections of this 230 pool seeding-homogenate into neonatal Line 230 mice induced early hindlimb paralysis with a mean incubation period (the interval between injection of SOD1 seeds and paralysis) of 2.7 months (Figure 1A, cohort 2). Two mice (2/17 injected) in this cohort failed to develop paralysis early and were euthanized at 8 months of age (Figure 1A, open squares). Intrathecal injection of this same 230 Pool homogenate in 2-month-old Line 230 mice induced early paralysis in all injected mice with a mean incubation period of 1.8 months post-injection (Figure 1A, cohort 3). Interestingly, all 14 mice that received 230 pool through the intrathecal route were paralysed within the same time frame, indicating consistent disease onset. There was no statistical difference in incubation period between the neonatal ISP injected and adult intrathecal injected cohorts.

We confirmed that the paralysed mice had SOD1-related MND pathology by two methods. First, we used direct fluorescence to assess the level of G85R-SOD1:YFP inclusion pathology (Figure 1B-D). Second, we stained the same fluorescently imaged sections using Campbell–Switzer silver stain (Figure 1B’-D’), which provides a histochemical method of detecting SOD1 inclusions [23,35]. None of the asymptomatic mice injected with the asymptomatic young 230 seeding pool (Y230 pool displayed fluorescent or silver positive puncta inclusion pathology in spinal cord at 10 months of age (Figure 1B). By contrast, spinal cords of all the paralysed mice that were seeded by either route with homogenates from paralysed Line 230 mice (230 pool exhibited inclusion pathology visible by direct fluorescence and silver stains (Figure 1C–D, C’–D’). Although the inclusions in fluorescent images are more obvious, most fluorescently labelled G85R-SOD1:YFP inclusions were also stained by silver (Figure 1C, C’, arrows mark examples). Silver-stained sections, counterstained with haematoxylin, also revealed obvious changes in the organization of the spinal cord in paralysed mice, including loss of large motor neurons and increased silver-positive fibrils (compare Figure 1B’ to Figure 1C’,D’). Together, these findings indicated that intrathecal injections induced accelerated motor neuron disease in mice expressing G85R-SOD1 as efficiently as occurred when seeding homogenates were injected in neonatal mice.

Our findings in Line 230 mice demonstrated that direct intrathecal delivery of misfolded SOD1 seeds is an efficient experimental paradigm to examine its prion-like seeding properties. Following on our earlier observation that neonatal VLE G93A-SOD1 mice were less susceptible to ISP-delivered seeds, we then tested if intrathecal injection in adult mice would be more vulnerable to prion-like seeding. We first confirmed that naïve VLE G93A-SOD1 mice did not display signs of paralysis even when aged to 20 months (Figure 2A), thus allowing ample time to test different ages for seed inoculation. A previous study of VLE G93A-SOD1 mice reported that 74% of 18-month-old VLE G93A-SOD1 mice display motor symptoms in the form of impaired hindlimb splay reflex that coincides with 30% reductions in motor neuron numbers in lumbar spinal cord [31]. In a cohort of 11 VLE G93A-SOD1 mice that were aged to 18–23 months in our colony, none was observed to have overt paralysis. Initially, we elected to inject 12-month-old VLE G93A-SOD1 mice with two types of spinal cord homogenates. One was prepared from the spinal cord of a paralysed Gur1 G93A-SOD1 mouse (G93A), which was the same homogenate used previously to inject newborn VLE G93A-SOD1 mice ISP, where it produced early paralysis in only three of eight mice injected [23]. Following intrathecal delivery in 12-month-old VLE G93A-SOD1 mice, the efficiency of seeding improved as seven of the eight injected mice developed paralysis within 6–10 months post-injection (Figure 2B, column 1). We also challenged a second cohort of 12-month-old VLE G93A-SOD1 mice with a seeding-homogenate that was made from a pool of four spinal cords from paralysed G85R-SOD1:YFP mice that were the final recipients of serially propagating ALS conformations from G93A-SOD1 homogenates (Second Passage [P2 G93A Pool], see Methods). Seven of the eight mice in this second cohort developed paralysis early, and surprisingly the incubation period was shorter than observed in Cohort 1 that received first passage G93A inoculum (Figure 2B, column 2). To determine whether the advanced age of the VLE G93A-SOD1 mice influenced susceptibility to P2 G93A pool seeding homogenates, we injected three additional cohorts of VLE G93A-SOD1 mice with the P2 G93A pool. One cohort was injected ISP as newborns, and the other two cohorts were injected intrathecally at 2 months or 6 months of age. By 8 months post-injection, 90% of mice in these cohorts developed paralysis (range 3–8 months post-injection) (Figure 2B, columns 3, 4, and 5). Together, these findings demonstrate that VLE G93A-SOD1 mice are permissive to ALS prion conformers carried by both misfolded G93A and G85R SOD1 found in spinal cords of paralysed mice.

To determine whether misfolded G85R-SOD1 that had not been induced by G93A-SOD1 aggregates could also efficiently induce paralysis in VLE G93A-SOD1 mice, we injected cohorts of 12-month-old VLE G93A-SOD1 mice with 230 pool homogenate (same as used in Figure 1). Of the six mice injected, four developed paralysis within 3–4 months post-injection, with the other two developing paralysis at 7- and 10-month post-injection (Figure 2B, column 6). As a control for this experiment, we injected a small cohort of 12-month-old VLE G93A-SOD1 mice with the Y230 pool. By 8–10-month post-injection (cumulative age 20–22 months), three of the four injected animals were asymptomatic (Figure 2B, Column 7). One of these aged animals developed hindlimb paralysis 8.8 months post-injection (20.8 months cumulative) and could represent an example of spontaneous disease in an aged VLE G93A-SOD1 animal. These findings indicated that misfolded G85R-SOD1 that had never been exposed to G93A-SOD1 conformations could also efficiently seed VLE G93A-SOD1 mice.

For the statistical analysis of the incubation periods, we excluded mice that did not attain paralysis when harvested (Figure 2C, Table 1). We also excluded data from the mice injected with the control Y230 pool because only one animal developed paralysis, leaving six cohorts to analyse by log-normal ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test [36,37]. Among these comparisons, four groups showed statistically significant differences (Figure 2C, Table 1). The median incubation period to paralysis for 12-month-old VLE G93A-SOD1 mice injected with G93A spinal inoculum was ~6 months, whereas injection with P2-G93A pooled seeding-homogenate caused paralysis in 4 months (p < 0.0001; Figure 2C; Table 1). The mean incubation period for VLE G93A-SOD1 mice injected as newborns with P2-G93A inoculum was longer than when the same inoculum was injected in 2- or 12-month-old VLE G93A-SOD1 mice (p < 0.0001; Figure 2C; Table 1). We noted that the three cohorts of VLE G93A-SOD1 mice injected at 2, 6 or 12 months with the pooled P2-G93A seeding-homogenate all had similar incubation times of ~4–5 months post-injection. Importantly, the incubation period for VLE G93A-SOD1 mice injected with P2-G93A pooled homogenate was not significantly different from that of mice injected with the 230 pool homogenate. Together, these findings demonstrate that young adult and older VLE G93A-SOD1 mice are permissive to seeding by homogenates containing misfolded G93A-SOD1 or G85R-SOD1:YFP, with the latter having the potential to induce paralysis more quickly.

Neuropathological examination of the seeded VLE G93A-SOD1 mice confirmed the presence of SOD1 inclusion pathology using Campbell–Switzer silver stain [23,35] and ubiquitin immunostaining [4,38] (Figures 3 and 4). The Campbell–Switzer stain reveals SOD1-containing inclusions and provides a reliable histochemical stain that is less variable than immunostaining with SOD1 antibodies [23,35]. All VLE G93A-SOD1 mice that were asymptomatic at 18–23 months exhibited some level of sparse inclusion pathology revealed as black puncta by silver staining (Figure 3A, white arrowheads mark examples). Similar levels of inclusion pathology were detected in the 18- to 20-month-old asymptomatic VLE G93A-SOD1 mice that were injected with the Y230 pool (Figure 3B). By comparison, in the paralysed mice, the level of inclusion pathology was dramatically higher (compare panels A and B in Figure 3 to panels C–I). Increased inclusion pathology was evident in paralysed VLE G93A-SOD1 mice injected at 12 months of age with all three of the seeding homogenates; Line 230 pool, G93A, or P2-G93A pool (Figure 3C–E). The general appearance of the pathological inclusions by ubiquitin immunostaining was similar among all of the paralysed animals (Figure 4). Most of the inclusions were small puncta dispersed throughout the neuropil grey matter. In some sections, thread-like structures were visible, indicating dendrites or axons filled with G93A-SOD1 aggregates (Figure 4, yellow arrowheads mark examples). Most sections contained at least one large neuron that was filled with SOD1 aggregates (Figure 4, yellow arrows mark examples). These pathologic features were also seen, at similar levels, in paralysed VLE G93A-SOD1 mice injected with either G93A-SOD1 or P2 G93A-SOD1 pool homogenates at 2- or 6-months of age (Figure 4F–I). Overall, the pathological data confirmed the induction of MND in adult VLE G93A-SOD1 mice by injection of seeding-homogenates containing misfolded G93A-SOD1 or G85R-SOD1:YFP.

Findings from prior studies showed that misfolded SOD1 in spinal cord homogenates from paralysed G93A-SOD1 mice acquired a prion-like capacity to induce and accelerate paralysis in G85R-SOD1:YFP mice, but these same homogenates were ineffective or inefficient in mice expressing G93A-SOD1 [23,30]. In those prior studies, the primary route of introducing seeding-homogenates was ISP injection into neonatal mice or sciatic nerve injection in young adult mice. These findings raised questions as to whether the seeding susceptibility of mice expressing the G93A-SOD1 might be different in older mice or if the route of seed administration was modified. To address these questions, we sought an effective route to deliver seeding-homogenates into the spinal cord of adult mice. Initially, we compared the efficiency of our standard P0 ISP injection to intrathecal injection in 2-month-old G85R-SOD1:YFP mice (Line 230), finding that intrathecal injection of misfolded G85R-SOD1:YFP seeding-homogenates could efficiently accelerate the onset of paralysis. We then demonstrated that VLE G93A-SOD1 mice injected intrathecally at 12 months of age with seeding-homogenates from a paralysed Gur1 G93A-SOD1 mouse became paralysed ~6 months post-injection. VLE G93A-SOD1 mice were similarly susceptible to seeding by homogenates prepared from paralysed G85R-SOD1:YFP mice that had been propagating misfolded G93A-SOD1 seeds. These second passage G93A seeds efficiently induced paralysis when injected intrathecally into 2-, 6-, or 12-month-old VLE G93A-SOD1 mice. These seeds were also efficient when injected ISP into newborn VLE G93A-SOD1 mice. Lastly, we noted that seeding-homogenates from paralysed G85R-SOD1:YFP mice that had not been exposed to G93A-SOD1 seeds were also effective when injected intrathecally into 12-month-old VLE G93A-SOD1 mice. Pathological evaluations confirmed the induction of inclusion pathology in the spinal cords of all paralysed mice. Based on these data, we conclude that the G93A variant of human SOD1 is capable of efficiently initiating and sustaining the propagation of ALS-causing conformations that accumulate in paralysed mutant SOD1 mice.

In 12-month-old VLE G93A-SOD1 mice injected with seeding-homogenate from a paralysed Gur1 G93A-SOD1 mouse, we noted that the incubation period to paralysis was ~6 months. This incubation period was somewhat longer than expected, as we had observed G93A-SOD1 seeds could induce paralysis in G85R-SOD1:YFP mice as quickly as 2 months post-injection [23,30]. The longer incubation period for G93A-SOD1 ALS prions when propagated in VLE G93A-SOD1 mice may explain why our prior seeding studies in Gur1 G93A mice did not accelerate paralysis. Unseeded Gur1 G93A-SOD1 mice develop paralysis by 4–6 months of age due to high over-expression, leaving little margin for seeding to accelerate the age to paralysis.

When we initially began evaluating the susceptibility of VLE G93A-SOD1 mice to seeding, we first assessed seeding-homogenates from spinal cords of paralysed Gur1 G93A-SOD1 mice and paralysed G85R-SOD1:YFP mice that had themselves been seeded with homogenates originally derived from paralysed G93A-SOD1 mice. We were not sure initially whether there could be some strain characteristic of misfolded G93A-SOD1 variants that transferred to G85R-SOD1, facilitating seeding in VLE G93A-SOD1 mice. However, misfolded G85R-SOD:YFP in spinal cords of paralysed mice seeded with G85R-SOD1 aggregates
from mice that had never been exposed to G93A-SOD1 was equally efficient in seeding 12-month-old VLE G93A-SOD1 mice. Therefore, we think it likely that misfolded G93A-SOD1 and G85R-SOD1 in paralysed mice present ALS prion conformations that can readily cross-seed. Notably, Bergh and colleagues provided evidence that misfolded mutant SOD1 could adopt alternative strain conformations, designated A and B, based on differential antibody-binding properties of insoluble aggregates from paralysed transgenic SOD1 mice [39]. In their study, the insoluble aggregates of the G85R-SOD1 and G93A-SOD1 variants showed similar binding profiles and were designated as Strain A. Thus, conformational similarities in misfolded
G93A-SOD1 and G85R-SOD1 may enable efficient cross-seeding of these two variants. Further experiments that provide more structural details of the misfolded forms of these proteins that accumulate in spinal cords could provide more insight into the shared structural features of ALS prion conformations of these two variants.

We noted that the minimum incubation period for adult VLE G93A-SOD1 mice injected intrathecally at different ages with homogenates originating from G85R-SOD1:YFP mice was ~2.5 months. Additionally, there were no obvious differences in the location or morphology of pathologic inclusions between animals injected with the different seeding homogenates. However, because we observed that the minimum
incubation period for VLE G93A-SOD1 mice injected with Gur1 G93A-SOD1-derived seeds was ~6 months, we cannot rule out subtle strain differences between misfolded G93A-SOD1 and G85R-SOD1, depending upon which seed is paired with which recipient SOD1 host for the propagating ALS conformer. Overall, our data suggest that G93A and G85R variants are highly compatible and are capable of efficient cross-seeding.

Recipient SOD1 mouse models used in this study are capable of developing MND without seeding at old ages. Even though overt paralysis in older VLE G93A-SOD1 mice is rare in our colony, low-level inclusion pathology is common at old ages. Therefore, we cannot completely rule out that VLE G93A-SOD1 and G85R-SOD1:YFP animals that develop paralysis after very long incubation periods following seeding could have developed disease from the expressed transgene spontaneously. In the VLE G93A-SOD1 mice injected as 12-month-old adults with Gur1 G93A-SOD1 seeding-homogenates, the incubation period of 5.6–9.9 months pushed the cumulative age into the range at which there was a risk of some mice succumbing to the natural disease course for this model. The low frequency of paralysis in similarly aged uninjected VLE G93A-SOD1 mice or mice injected with asymptomatic Y230 pool homogenates leads us to conclude that the Gur1 G93A-SOD1 seeds were effective in inducing paralysis in the 12-month-old VLE G93A-SOD1 mice. In VLE G93A-SOD1 mice injected with G85R-SOD1 seeds, the cumulative age post-seeding for all cohorts was below 18 months, giving us high confidence that paralysis was indeed induced as a result of injecting seeding homogenates.

At the moment, we cannot offer a definitive explanation for the poor seeding efficiency we previously observed in our in-house colony of Gur1 G93A mice or the Thy1-G93A line of mice [23,30]. Our data indicate that the route and mode of injection, the composition of the seeding homogenate, as well as the ages at which these mice received the seeding homogenates were factors that influenced susceptibility.

This report is the first to introduce G85R-SOD1 strains of ALS prions into G93A-SOD1 mice, finding evidence of efficient cross-seeding. The present study demonstrates that the VLE sub-strain of Gur1 G93A-SOD1 mice, which expresses mutant SOD1 at low levels, is susceptible to prion seeding by spinal homogenates from paralysed Gur1 G93A-SOD1 mice and G85R-SOD1:YFP mice. Based on our current data, we conclude that the route of injection and age of introduction of ALS conformers can significantly impact the seeding efficiency of SOD1 ALS prions. G85R-SOD1 ALS prions seed efficiently by many routes, but other variants may show different efficiencies depending upon the route of delivery. Collectively, our data show that the G93A variant of human SOD1 can support both the initiation and propagation of ALS, causing prion conformations that arise in paralysed mice expressing mutant human SOD1.

A pooled seeding-homogenate of passaged G93A conformations was generated by first injecting newborn naïve G85R-SOD1:YFP mice intraspinally with spinal homogenates from a paralysed Gur1 G93A-SOD1 mouse (Figure 5C). G85R-SOD1:YFP mice that became paralysed following these injections (Passage 1 [P1]) were harvested, and spinal cord homogenate from one of these animals was injected a second time into naïve G85R-SOD1:YFP mice (Passage 2 [P2]). The flow of injections was as follows: Gur1G93A → G85R-SOD1:YFP (P1) → G85R-SOD1:YFP (P2, -pooled). Spinal cords from four P2 G85R-SOD1:YFP mice that became paralysed ~2.5 months post-injection were harvested, homogenized as described above and pooled to make a P2-G93A seeding-homogenate (referred to as the P2-G93A pool in Figure 2).

In neonatal injections, litters were randomly selected for each injection, but individual mice within litters were not randomized between control and experimental injections. Newborn mice were injected intraspinally as previously described [30]. Intrathecal injections were performed in adult mice under isoflurane anaesthesia (2% in oxygen), following previously described procedures [40]. The lumbar region was shaved, and the skin was disinfected with chlorhexidine solution applied using sterile cotton swabs, followed by sterile saline swabs and drying with gauze. Mice were positioned prone with the spine gently arched by applying light pressure to the flanks. A 30-gauge, 0.75-in-long needle attached to a Hamilton syringe was used to inject 4 µl of solution into the lumbar intervertebral space. The mixture contained 2 µl of spinal cord homogenate and 2 µl of 2% lidocaine (final lidocaine concentration: 1%). The needle was inserted at a 30° cranial angle, then gradually redirected towards a vertical or slightly caudal angle (~30° towards the tail) without withdrawing from the skin, until the needle correctly accessed to the intervertebral space. Tail flick or hindlimb movement indicated correct intrathecal placement. The solution was injected slowly, and the needle was withdrawn without delay. Mice were placed in a warm recovery cage and monitored until fully awake, then observed for signs of motor impairment or distress.

The primary goal of the study was to determine whether older VLE G93A-SOD1 mice may be more susceptible to prion-like seeding by intrathecal administration of seeding homogenates. In prelude to addressing this question, we first sought to compare the relative seeding efficacy of different injection routes using the highly permissive homozygous Line 230 model. The seeding-homogenates for these studies were either the control Y230 pool, derived from asymptomatic naïve 2-month-old Line 230 mice (Figure 5A) or the 230 pool, derived from paralysed G85R-SOD1:YFP mice after ‘230-11’ homogenate injection (Figure 5B). We compared the incubation time to paralysis following lumbar intraspinal (ISP) injection in P0 newborn mice and lumbar intrathecal injection in adult 2-month-old Line 230 mice (Figure 5D).

Once we established the efficacy of adult intrathecal injection, we tested VLE G93A mice with homogenates from paralysed G93A SOD1 mice or with the P2-G93A seeding pool described above (Figure 5C). Initially, we challenged 12-month-old mice with both seeding homogenates and then subsequently determined whether younger VLE G93A-SOD1 mice were also susceptible, using the P2-G93A pool. Controls for these experiments included uninjected VLE G93A-SOD1 mice and mice injected with the Y230 pool (Figure 5E).

For most of these injections, we sought to inject mice from two to three litters. The homozygous Line 230 mice naturally produced larger cohorts. In the VLE G93A-SOD1 study, each cohort receiving the G93A or P2-G93A pool was between 8 and 10 transgene-positive animals. Only one litter of VLE G93A-SOD1 mice was injected with the 230 pool and Y230 pool inoculums, producing smaller cohorts of transgene-positive animals.

The primary humane endpoint for all injections was overt paralysis of at least one hindlimb. In a subset of mice, other body condition factors, such as hydration or muscle atrophy, were considered in decisions to euthanize and harvest tissues from mice that were obviously weak but not yet paralysed. Laboratory staff were not blind to treatment or genotype, but veterinary staff that assisted in determining endpoint were blind. Mice that were recommended for euthanasia due to fighting wounds or tumour development were excluded from the study. No mice that received seeding injections for data in Figure 1 were excluded from the study. For the data in Figure 2, there were mice that had to be excluded post-injection: Group 1 – two mice and Group 3 – one mouse.

Mice that were identified as distressed and recommended for euthanasia but did not otherwise exhibit signs of paralysis were excluded from statistical analyses of incubation periods. For data in Figure 2B, there were the following exclusions: Group 1 – one animal, Group 2 – two animals, Group 3 – one animal, Group 5 – two animals, Group 7 – three animals.

The primary method of euthanasia was by exsanguination under anaesthesia. Animals were placed in an isoflurane inhalation chamber until an adequate level of anaesthesia was confirmed absence of response to toe pinch. The animal was transferred to a down draft table or BSC Class II Type B2 and secured. A nosecone (15 ml conical tube) containing a small amount of isoflurane was placed on the animal to ensure a constant level of anaesthesia. The chest cavity was opened, and a needle was inserted into the left ventricle, the right atrium is cut to induce exsanguination, and a solution of phosphate buffered saline (PBS) was forced through the circulatory system with a syringe under steady pressure.

A subset of animals were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation. Whenever possible, animals were euthanized within their home cages without co-mingling. One hundred per cent medical-grade carbon dioxide gas was introduced into the enclosure at a displacement rate of 30–70% of the chamber volume per minute. Gas flow is maintained for at least 1 min after respiration has stopped. Death was confirmed by decapitation or cervical dislocation.
