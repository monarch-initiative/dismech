# Clinical and molecular characteristics of a series of Chinese children with pseudohypoaldosteronism: a case series.
**Authors:** Wu Z, Li J, Sheng H, Li X, Huang Z, Liang C, Mei H, Lin Y, Li T, Zhang W, Xu A
**Journal:** Transl Pediatr (2026)
**DOI:** [10.21037/tp-2025-1-844](https://doi.org/10.21037/tp-2025-1-844)

## Content

BACKGROUND: Pseudohypoaldosteronism (PHA) is a rare disorder characterized by renal resistance to mineralocorticoids, leading to hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, and metabolic acidosis. It is primarily classified into type I (PHAI), with subtypes including renal (caused by NR3C2 mutation), systemic (caused by SCNN1A/B/G mutations), and secondary forms, and type II (PHAII), which is commonly associated with mutations in genes involved in the WNK signaling pathway (WNK1, WNK4, KLHL3, CUL3). However, comprehensive cohorts delineating the clinical and genetic spectrum of PHA in Chinese children are lacking.

CASE DESCRIPTION: We present six unrelated Chinese children with PHA. Genetic testing enabled precise etiological classification: two with autosomal recessive systemic PHAI (harboring SCNN1B mutations) presented with severe neonatal salt-wasting; one with autosomal dominant renal PHAI (a heterozygous NR3C2 mutation); three with PHAII (heterozygous KLHL3 or CUL3 mutations) exhibiting hyperkalemia and acidosis that were responsive to thiazides. Management was tailored to the subtype, including aggressive salt supplementation for systemic PHAI and thiazide diuretics for PHAII. Notably, one patient with systemic PHAI subsequently developed severe atopic dermatitis and extreme hyperimmunoglobulin E (4,080 IU/mL), a rarely documented comorbidity. Outcomes were generally favorable; however, one infant with systemic PHAI succumbed to refractory electrolyte imbalance, and another experienced persistent failure to thrive.

CONCLUSIONS: In this study, we present six patients with different types of PHA (PHAI and PHAII), and describe their unique phenotypic, genetic characteristics. Our findings compellingly demonstrate that genetic testing is indispensable for precise subtyping, which in turn directs etiology-specific management and enables accurate prognostication, thereby optimizing long-term outcomes for affected children.

• This series of six Chinese children with pseudohypoaldosteronism (PHA) demonstrates distinct clinical and genetic features: systemic PHAI (SCNN1B) was associated with severe salt-wasting, whereas PHAII (KLHL3/CUL3) presented with hyperkalemia was responsive to thiazides.

• A rare association of systemic PHAI with extreme hyperimmunoglobulin E (hyper-IgE) was identified in one case.

• New disease-causing variants in CUL3 and KLHL3 were reported.

• PHA is a rare disorder of mineralocorticoid resistance with known genetic causes affecting the MR/ENaC (PHAI).

• This study provides a comprehensive clinical/genetic spectrum in a Chinese cohort and reports a rare association between systemic PHAI and severe hyperimmunoglobulin E with atopic dermatitis.

• Genetic testing is essential for precise PHA subtyping, as it directly guides targeted therapy and prognosis. Our findings suggest that vigilance for immunological manifestations in patients with systemic PHAI may be warranted.

Pseudohypoaldosteronism I (PHAI) is a rare, heterogeneous disorder characterized by end-organ resistance to mineralocorticoids, manifesting biochemically as hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, and metabolic acidosis (1). First described by Cheek and Perry in 1958 (2), the inherited forms of PHA have an estimated incidence in 1:47,000 (3). Clinically, PHA is primarily categorized into type I (PHAI) and type II (PHAII).

PHAI results from impaired transepithelial sodium transport in the distal nephron (4). It is further subdivided into three forms: renal, systemic, and secondary (5). The renal form of PHAI is autosomal dominant and stems from dominant-negative missense mutations in the NR3C2 protein encoding the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR), disrupting aldosterone signaling and leading to renal salt wasting (6). In contrast, the systemic form of PHAI is autosomal recessive and is caused by mutations in the genes (SCNN1A, SCNN1B, SCNN1G) encoding the α, β, and γ subunits of the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) (7). Given the broad expression of ENaC in the kidney, colon, sweat glands, and respiratory epithelium, patients present with multi-organ involvement, including severe salt-wasting, recurrent pulmonary infections, and dermatological manifestations (8). While its management focuses on treating the underlying condition, the precise mechanisms of acquired mineralocorticoid resistance remain incompletely understood (9,10).

PHAII, or Gordon syndrome, presents a pathophysiological paradox, characterized by hypertension, hyperkalemia, and metabolic acidosis (11). It arises from dysregulated sodium reabsorption via the thiazide-sensitive Na-Cl cotransporter (NCC) in the distal convoluted tubule (12). Notably, hypertension may be absent or mild in early childhood. PHAII can be inherited in an autosomal dominant or recessive pattern and is caused by mutations in genes involved in the WNK kinase signaling pathway, including WNK1, WNK4, KLHL3, and CUL3 (13). Specifically, KLHL3 and CUL3 form a ubiquitin ligase complex that negatively regulates WNK kinases. Mutations in these components lead to WNK kinase accumulation, subsequent phosphorylation of the kinases SPAK and OSR1, and increased NCC activity, ultimately promoting salt retention and hypertension (14-17).

Despite these established genetic foundations, comprehensive cohort studies delineating the full clinical and molecular spectrum of PHA in Chinese children are scarce (18,19).

We report a case series of six unrelated Chinese pediatric patients with PHA, encompassing both PHAI and PHAII subtypes. By integrating detailed clinical characterization with genetic analysis, this study aims to enhance early diagnosis, guide tailored management, and improve long-term prognoses for affected children. We present this article in accordance with the AME Case Series reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2025-1-844/rc).

We conducted a retrospective case series of six pediatric patients with genetically confirmed or clinically definitive PHA, managed at our tertiary care center (Guangzhou Women and Children Medical Center) between January 2010 and December 2024. The diagnosis of PHA was based on the presence of hyperkalemia and metabolic acidosis with inappropriately elevated plasma aldosterone levels. For PHAI, plasma renin activity was typically elevated, whereas for PHAII, it was normal or suppressed. Other causes of adrenal insufficiency and renal tubular acidosis were rigorously excluded (20). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was conducted with approval from the ethical review board of Guangzhou Women and Children Medical Center (Ethics approval No. (2020) 49801]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants' guardians for publication of this case series and the accompanying image. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were systematically abstracted from medical records. Hormonal profiles, including plasma aldosterone, renin activity, cortisol, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), and 17-hydroxyprogesterone, were measured using standardized clinical protocols in the hospital’s central laboratory. Genetic analysis was pivotal for etiological classification and subtyping. For patient 1 and patient 2 direct sequencing were applied to analyze the entire coding exons and exon-intron boundaries of SCNN1A, SCNN1B, SCNN1G genes. For patients with suspected primary PHA (Patients 3-6), whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on proband-parent trios. Identified sequence variants were verified by Sanger sequencing and interpreted according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines.

Based on clinical and genetic evaluation, the cohort was categorized into three etiological groups: (I) systemic PHAI (due to SCNN1B mutations, n=2): Patients 1 and 2 presented with severe neonatal salt-wasting. Genetic testing revealed mutations in the SCNN1B gene, consistent with the autosomal recessive form of systemic PHAI; (II) renal PHAI (due to NR3C2 mutation, n=1): Patient 3 presented similarly but was found to carry a heterozygous mutation in the NR3C2 gene, confirming the autosomal dominant renal form of PHAI; (III) PHAII (n=3): Patients 4, 5, and 6 exhibited hyperkalemia and metabolic acidosis but with normal or suppressed plasma renin activity, consistent with PHAII (Table 1). Molecular diagnoses identified heterozygous mutations in the KLHL3 (Patients 4 and 5) or CUL3 (Patient 6) genes.

Management was tailored to the specific pathophysiology of each PHA type.

Systemic PHAI (Patients 1 & 2): both patients presented with life-threatening salt-wasting crises within the first month of life, characterized by severe hyperkalemia (potassium ≥10 mmol/L), metabolic acidosis, and circulatory shock. Patient 1 is a 7-day-old full-term girl presented with lethargy, oliguria, and feeding refusal. ECG showed elevated T-waves and widened QRS complexes, with heart rate 72 bpm and blood pressure 44/32 mmHg. Laboratory findings revealed severe hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, and metabolic acidosis. Despite volume resuscitation and medical management for hyperkalemia, peritoneal dialysis was required. Hormonal evaluation showed markedly elevated plasma renin and aldosterone with normal cortisol, ACTH, and 17-hydroxyprogesterone (17-OHP), leading to a diagnosis of PHAI. She was treated with potassium restriction, sodium supplementation, and Kayexalate, and discharged after 44 days. During follow-up, she developed recurrent rash with elevated IgE, managed with cyclosporine and topical steroids. A skin biopsy confirmed chronic dermatitis with a lymphocytic and eosinophilic infiltrate (Figure 1). At age 10 years, her IgE decreased to 327 IU/mL, and growth parameters were at the −1.4 SD. Patient 2 is a 13-day-old full-term male presented with vomiting, lethargy, and 14% weight loss (3.05 kg from 3.55 kg). He was pale with weak pulses; BP was 67/49 mmHg. Labs showed hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and metabolic acidosis. ECG demonstrated elevated T-waves and broad QRS complexes. Despite intravenous correction and initial steroid therapy, hormonal studies revealed extremely high aldosterone and renin with normal 17-OHP, confirming PHAI. Steroids were discontinued; he was managed with Kayexalate, sodium chloride, and bicarbonate. During follow-up, the patient developed papular eruptions on the face and neck, which received no specific treatment. He was discharged after 45 days but died at 4 months from severe electrolyte imbalance complicated by pneumonia.

Renal PHAI (Patient 3): the patient experienced respiratory distress immediately after birth, necessitating admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Phototherapy was initiated on the fourth day of life to manage hyperbilirubinemia. During the second week of life, she developed severe hyponatremia, hypochloremia, hyperkalemia, and metabolic acidosis (Table 1). Daily oral or intravenous sodium 3–10 mmol/kg can maintain serum sodium concentration. On the 15th day, aldosterone was greater than 4,000.00 pg/mL (supine normal value 10–160 pg/mL), renin activity was 28.26 ng/mL/h. She has non-pigmented skin and non-masculine external genitalia. 17-OHP concentrations was normal. The diagnosis of renal PHAI was confirmed by mutational investigation in the patient, which showed a heterozygous mutation c.1955G>A (p.R652Q) in NR3C2 (Table 2). During the 9-month follow-up period, sodium and potassium levels were effectively maintained within normal ranges through daily supplementation of 5–6 mmol/kg sodium while the patient was breastfeeding. By 9 months of age, the patient demonstrated normal developmental milestones but exhibited difficulty gaining weight, with a recorded weight of 6.7 kg (−2 SD).

PHAII (Patients 4, 5 & 6): these patients presented with hyperkalemia and metabolic acidosis. All were effectively managed with thiazide diuretics and a low-salt diet. Blood pressure at presentation was documented as normal for age in all three patients. Consistent longitudinal blood pressure measurements were not available in the retrospective records.

Molecular results corroborated and refined the clinical classification (summarized in Table 2).

PHAI cases: Patient 1 was a compound heterozygote for a novel SCNN1B variant: a splice-site mutation (c.776+1G>A) and a missense mutation (c.118C>T, p.Arg40Cys), inherited from her mother and father, respectively. Patient 2 was homozygous for a known SCNN1B splice-site mutation (c.1542+1G>A). In contrast, Patient 3 harbored a heterozygous missense mutation in the NR3C2 gene (c.1955G>A, p.Arg652Gln).

PHAII cases: Patient 4 carried a novel heterozygous KLHL3 missense variant (c.748G>T, p.Val250Phe), inherited from his mother. Patient 5 had a de novo heterozygous KLHL3 mutation (c.233T>C, p.Met78Thr). Patient 6 was found to have a de novo heterozygous frameshift deletion in CUL3 (c.1374_1377+1del, p.Lys459Leufs18), which was predicted to be pathogenic by ACMG criteria, likely leading to a truncated protein and impaired ubiquitin ligase complex formation.

This study describes the clinical courses, management, and genetic findings in six pediatric patients diagnosed with distinct disorders under the diagnostic umbrella of PHA. Our cohort underscores that PHA constitutes a spectrum of etiologically separate conditions, where a precise molecular or etiological diagnosis is critical for guiding fundamentally different management strategies.

Our PHAI patients, harboring mutations in SCNN1B or NR3C2, presented with neonatal salt-wasting crises, consistent with defective sodium handling. However, their severity varied. Two infant with SCNN1B mutations, one (Patient 1) heterozygous for a novel (c.776+1G>A) variant with a previously reported mutation on the other allele, and the second (Patient 2) homozygous for a previously reported splice variant, faced life-threatening hyperkalemia, with one requiring peritoneal dialysis and another culminating fatally. Notably, in one survivor we observed the subsequent development of severe atopic dermatitis with extremely hyper-IgE levels. While atopic dermatitis is a recognized comorbidity in systemic PHAI, the extreme hyper-IgE levels observed in our patient represent a notable and poorly documented feature (21). This observation may suggest a more profound role for ENaC dysfunction in skin barrier and immune regulation, as supported by literature on ENaC expression in relevant tissues (22). However, this association, based on a single case, requires validation in larger cohorts and further mechanistic studies. In contrast, the infant with a heterozygous NR3C2 mutation had a milder, purely renal course in our study, manageable with sodium supplements and aligning with the transient nature of renal PHAI described in the literature (23,24). Dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) sulfate levels found in three patients on admission, physical examination revealed no skin hyperpigmentation or genital abnormalities, and follow-up tests showed normalization of DHEA.

Conversely, our three PHAII patients, with mutations in KLHL3 or CUL3, were characterized by mineralocorticoid resistance and a hallmark biochemical paradox to therapy; exogenous mineralocorticoids did not demonstrate improvement in hyperkalemia, while thiazide diuretics induced rapid, sustained remission. This underscores the distinct pathophysiology from PHAI. We identified novel mutations in both KLHL3 (p.Met78Thr) and CUL3 (p.Lys459Leufs18), with the former located in critical functional domains, expanding the mutational spectrum. Although all our PHAII patients were normotensive in early childhood, their early onset underscores the need for lifelong monitoring for hypertension, particularly in those with CUL3 variants known for severe, early-onset disease (25,26).

The diagnostic differentiation among PHA can be challenging. As exemplified by one of our PHAII cases (cases 4–6) who had elevated aldosterone, biochemical overlap between PHAI and PHAII can occur, rendering renin and aldosterone levels insufficient for definitive subclassification. This ambiguity reinforces genetic testing as the gold standard for precise diagnosis, which is paramount since management is subtype-specific.

Furthermore, the co-occurrence of secondary PHA with urinary tract anomalies necessitates renal ultrasonography in all infants presenting with a PHA-like picture. A prompt genetic and anatomical workup enables the initiation of correct, potentially curative therapy, preventing ineffective or harmful treatments.

There are several limitations in this study. First, its retrospective design and small sample size, inherent to the rarity of PHA, limit the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. Second, the conclusions drawn from subgroups with very few patients, particularly renal PHAI (n=1), should be interpreted with caution. Third, the notable association between systemic PHAI and hyper-IgE is based on a single case observation; thus, it remains a preliminary finding that necessitates independent confirmation in future studies. Finally, as a tertiary referral center, our cohort might be subject to selection bias towards more severe or complex cases.

In conclusion, we report the detailed clinical and molecular characteristics of a Chinese pediatric cohort with different disorders diagnosed as PHA. Our findings reinforce that these are distinct conditions with separate etiologies requiring specific management. This report adds to the phenotypic description of systemic PHAI, including an association with extreme hyper-IgE, and expands the mutational spectrum of PHAII in the Chinese population. Above all, our cases highlight that early and precise etiological diagnosis—distinguishing between these separate entities—is the cornerstone for initiating correct, targeted therapy and improving patient prognosis.
